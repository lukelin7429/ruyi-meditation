Chapter 1 · 梵網經 · Title, Substance, and the Meaning of "Mind Ground"

One Chapter, and What It Actually Means

Before the sutra's own narrative even opens, the classical commentary tradition insists on answering two questions first: what is this text, and what is it actually about? This opening chapter tells the surprising textual history behind the single chapter that survives as "the Brahma Net Sutra," then works through the five-part method Chinese commentators have always used before touching a scripture's own words — its title, its underlying substance, and, above all, what "Mind Ground" actually means.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T24, no. 1484 Commentary guide: Elder Master Wei Sung, cross-checked against the BDK Tripiṭaka translation

1. Milestone: One Chapter Surviving From a Vast Original

The Brahma Net Sutra as we have it today is not a complete scripture in its own right — it is a single surviving chapter, the tenth, of a vast original said to have run to over a hundred fascicles and sixty-one chapters. Two short prefaces, copied down at the moment of its Chinese translation in 406 CE, explain exactly how this one chapter came to circulate as though it were a whole sutra unto itself.

Chinese Source — unsigned colophon

此經本有一百十二卷六十一品……唯《梵網經》最後誦出。時融、影三百人等，一時受菩薩十戒。豈唯當時之益，乃有累劫之津！故與道融別書，出此心地一品，當時有三百餘人誦此一品。故即書是品八十一部，流通於後代持誦相授。

Ru-Yi Translation

This sutra originally comprised one hundred and twelve fascicles in sixty-one chapters ... and the Brahma Net Sutra was recited last of all. At that time Daorong, Daoying, and three hundred others together received the ten bodhisattva precepts on the spot. How could such a benefit belong only to that moment — it forms a crossing-point reaching across kalpa after kalpa to come! [Kumārajīva] therefore wrote out this single chapter on the Mind Ground separately for Daorong; at the time, more than three hundred people recited this one chapter. Eighty-one copies of it were made, to be handed down and recited by later generations.

Chinese Source — Sengzhao's preface

唯梵網經一百二十卷六十一品，其中菩薩心地品第十專明菩薩行地。是時道融、道影三百人等即受菩薩戒，人各誦此品以為心首。師徒義合，敬寫一品八十一部，流通於世。

Ru-Yi Translation

Of these, the Brahma Net Sutra alone comprised one hundred and twenty fascicles in sixty-one chapters, among which the tenth chapter, on the Bodhisattva Mind Ground, deals exclusively with the bodhisattva's stages of practice. At that time Daorong, Daoying, and three hundred others received the bodhisattva precepts on the spot, each reciting this chapter as the very core of their own practice. Teacher and disciples being of one mind, they respectfully copied out this single chapter in eighty-one copies, to be transmitted through the world.

Reading note

The two prefaces even disagree on the original's exact size — a hundred and twelve fascicles in one account, a hundred and twenty in the other — a small discrepancy that itself tells us something: whatever the larger work actually was, it was already effectively lost by the time these two notes were written down, and only living memory of its rough scale remained. What both accounts agree on is the moment of transmission itself: in 406 CE, at Kumārajīva's translation assembly in Chang'an's Thatched Hall Temple, three hundred people received the bodhisattva precepts on the spot and asked to keep only this one chapter — the "Bodhisattva Mind Ground" — as their own core text. Eighty-one hand-copied sets went out from that single moment. Every copy of the Brahma Net Sutra that exists today, translated into every modern language, ultimately traces back to that one chapter, deliberately excerpted by request from a text otherwise already beyond recovery.

2. Milestone: Why a Title Is Explained Before the Text Itself

Chinese commentators traditionally refuse to move directly into a sutra's narrative. Before a single line of the story is read, the classical method insists on first explaining the title, then the text's underlying substance — because a title glimpsed but not understood is worse than useless, and because clinging to words after their purpose is served is its own kind of trap.

Chinese Source

梵網經盧舍那佛說菩薩心地戒品第十

Ru-Yi Translation

The Brahma Net Sutra: Chapter Ten, the Chapter on the Bodhisattva Mind Ground Precepts, Spoken by Vairocana Buddha.

Reading note — after Elder Master Wei Sung

Master Wei Sung frames the title itself as a kind of doorway rather than the destination: reaching for a title in order to be drawn toward the text behind it is like reaching for a net in order to catch a fish, but stopping at the net while never actually catching anything defeats the entire purpose. He warns that dwelling on a title while ignoring the substance it points to is genuinely dangerous — comparable, in his own vivid phrase, to grasping a snake by its tail: sooner or later it turns and bites the hand holding it. He recalls the Buddha's own raft simile here: a raft exists to ferry a traveler across a river, and once the far shore is reached, no one keeps carrying the raft on their back out of misplaced loyalty to it. A title, in exactly the same way, is explained thoroughly precisely so it can then be set down, its work already done, once the substance underneath it is genuinely grasped.

3. Milestone: "The Buddha Speaks" — Three Enlightenments, One Joy

Every sutra title beginning "the Buddha speaks" carries a specific claim: that a fully enlightened being chose, out of genuine joy rather than obligation, to put a realization into words for beings who do not yet see it themselves — a joy that only makes sense once "Buddha" itself is properly understood as a completed threefold enlightenment, not a solitary private attainment.

Chinese Source

佛

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddha

Reading note — after Elder Master Wei Sung

Master Wei Sung unpacks "Buddha" as the perfection of three enlightenments together, not one: awakening to one's own true nature, awakening others to that same nature, and bringing both of these to complete, perfected fulfillment — a bare private insight that stops at the first of these does not yet earn the title. He explains that a Buddha speaks specifically because enlightenment itself reveals, beyond any doubt, that every single being without exception already carries this identical potential — recognizing that shared potential in others is precisely what turns realization into compassion, and compassion into the wish to teach. This is why he reads the classical description of the Buddha "speaking joyously from the depths of his own heart" quite literally: the teaching that follows is offered specifically to bring about the swiftest possible transformation, moving an ordinary being directly toward the rank of Buddha through the simple act of receiving these very precepts.

4. Milestone: "Brahma's Net" — One Teaching, Infinite Worlds

The title's central image is deliberately drawn from an ordinary object: the god Brahma's ceremonial net, woven with countless knots and hung with a jewel at every intersection. Its purpose in the title is to make an otherwise abstract claim about infinite worlds immediately visible.

Chinese Source

梵網

Ru-Yi Translation

Brahma's Net

Reading note — after Elder Master Wei Sung

Master Wei Sung reads the net quite exactly: a single net, an uncountable number of holes, no two holes identical, yet every hole held within one and the same weave. He maps this directly onto the sutra's own cosmology, worked out fully in the chapter's later narrative — countless worlds, no two alike, each still held within a single underlying teaching that reaches every one of them in a form suited to its own beings. The image does real work precisely because it is homely rather than abstract: anyone who has actually seen a net immediately sees why "infinite variety within one underlying unity" is being claimed, without needing the philosophy spelled out first.

5. Milestone: "Sutra" and Its Five Classical Meanings

The final word of the title, "sutra," is itself given a fivefold gloss in the commentary tradition — not merely "scripture" in a generic sense, but a specific claim about what this kind of text does: it reaches upward to match the subtle intention of a Buddha and downward to match whatever a listener is actually capable of receiving.

Chinese Source

經

Ru-Yi Translation

Sutra

Reading note — after Elder Master Wei Sung

Master Wei Sung lists five classical senses bundled into this one word: a foundation from which all further teaching can be drawn, in the way a source text underlies its own commentaries; a revelation of a principle otherwise too subtle to see unaided; a spring that keeps bubbling up of its own accord, never running dry however long it is drawn from; a carpenter's inked measuring cord, snapped against a plank to mark a perfectly straight line — used here to distinguish genuinely correct practice from its many convincing counterfeits; and a flower garland, individual syllables strung one after another exactly as loose blossoms are threaded into a single wearable strand. He adds a practical observation about how differently a single teaching lands: a listener of genuinely sharp capacity recognizes its brilliance the moment it is heard; an average listener needs to sit with it in stillness before its truth settles in; and a listener of duller capacity may reject it outright on first hearing — yet even that flat rejection, he insists, still plants a seed of eventual buddhahood, since the encounter itself has now genuinely happened and cannot be undone.

6. Milestone: Precepts, Concentration, Wisdom — Melting the Ice

Before turning to the specific virtues a bodhisattva is meant to cultivate, the commentary pauses on why a precepts-text is needed at all: greed, hatred, and delusion are what freeze an otherwise fluid, responsive mind into something rigid and reactive, and precepts exist specifically to begin thawing that ice back into water.

Chinese Source

戒

Ru-Yi Translation

Precept, discipline

Reading note — after Elder Master Wei Sung

Master Wei Sung compares an afflicted mind to water that has frozen solid: ice cannot flow around an obstacle, take the shape of its container, or respond to changing circumstance the way liquid water does at once, without any effort at all. Greed, anger, and delusion, in exactly this way, freeze a mind that would otherwise move naturally and appropriately through the world. He places precepts, concentration, and wisdom in strict sequence for this reason: genuine precepts produce a settled concentration, and settled concentration in turn allows wisdom to actually arise — the three together forming what the tradition calls the three trainings free of further outflow, standing opposed to the three poisons item for item. Precepts alone, kept as bare external rule-following with nothing behind them, never quite reach this — it is only when discipline genuinely begins to melt the ice of habitual greed, anger, and delusion that water starts moving freely again, which is the entire point of undertaking any precept in the first place.

7. Milestone: The Six Identities With Buddha — Refuting Both Laziness and False Humility

A recurring worry about bodhisattva teaching is that Buddhahood sounds either automatic, requiring no real effort, or permanently out of reach for anyone but an already-realized sage. The classical framework of six identities is deployed specifically to refute both mistakes at once, by mapping out the real, gradual distance between an ordinary being and complete awakening without denying either end of that distance.

Chinese Source

六即佛

Ru-Yi Translation

The Six Identities With Buddha

Reading note — after Elder Master Wei Sung

Master Wei Sung walks the six in order: identity in principle, true of every being simply by virtue of already possessing buddha-nature, whether or not it is ever recognized; identity in understanding, reached the moment the teaching is genuinely grasped intellectually; identity in practice, deepened through actual contemplation and disciplined conduct rather than mere concept; identity in resemblance, where visible defilement measurably thins and the six sense faculties begin to purify — which he likens to scouring rust off iron, or to a sculptor roughing out a Buddha statue's outward shape well before any inner radiance is added; identity in partial realization, where layer after layer of fundamental ignorance is broken through in turn, each fracture opening onto a corresponding depth of buddha-nature already there to be revealed; and identity in ultimate realization, where every last trace of ignorance has dissolved and the underlying true suchness stands fully, permanently manifest.

He illustrates the scheme with a story of converting a practitioner from a rival folk sect using the plainest possible image: a long train journey, where each intermediate station corresponds to one of the six identities in turn, and only arrival at the final city corresponds to the sixth and last. He is equally pointed about what the six identities are for: against the lazy assumption that buddha-nature already possessed means no real effort is needed, he offers the image of a buried pearl — genuinely precious by nature, yet still requiring real polishing before its light actually shows; and against the opposite error — dismissing Buddhahood as categorically impossible for an ordinary being, or looking down on those still visibly unrealized — the same six-part ladder shows a real, walkable path connecting the two ends, with nothing on either side that deserves either despair or contempt.

8. Milestone: The Three Bodies, One Buddha

The sutra's own narrative, opening in the chapter to follow, names three seemingly separate figures — Vairocana, Śākyamuni, and an intermediate figure often rendered Niṣyanda — who turn out, on the commentary's reading, to be a single reality described from three different vantage points at once.

Chinese Source

法身、報身、應化身

Ru-Yi Translation

The Dharma Body, the Reward Body, and the Response-and-Transformation Body

Reading note — after Elder Master Wei Sung

Master Wei Sung glosses the three plainly: the Dharma Body as the underlying enlightened principle itself, without any particular shape or location; the Reward Body as the perfected wisdom a Buddha actually gains as the fruit of first breaking through to that principle; and the Response, or Transformation, Body as whatever compassionate form then actually appears to a given being, shaped by that being's own needs and capacity. He reads the three proper names in the sutra's opening as a direct match to these three: Vairocana, meaning something like "universal, all-pervading light," names the Dharma Body; Niṣyanda, meaning the perfected fruit of purity, names the Reward Body; and Śākyamuni, meaning "capable of humaneness" and "still and silent" at once, names the Response Body actually walking among beings in this world.

He is careful that this distinction never becomes three separate buddhas: the three are inseparable aspects of one and the same reality, and which aspect a given being actually perceives depends entirely on that being's own karmic conditions and present capacity, not on any real division in what is being perceived. His preferred image is a single pearl: its underlying substance corresponds to the Dharma Body, the light it naturally emanates corresponds to the Reward Body, and whatever image that light happens to cast when it falls on a given surface corresponds to a Response Body — three real, distinguishable things, and yet never, for a moment, three separate pearls.

9. Milestone: What "Mind Ground" Actually Means

Having worked through the title word by word, the commentary turns to what it calls "revealing the substance" — the actual thing the whole chapter is about, once the title's own words have done their job and can be set aside. "Mind Ground" names something the sutra itself will state directly once its narrative properly opens: a nature shared, without exception, by every being capable of thought at all.

Chinese Source

光明金剛寶戒是一切佛本源、一切菩薩本源、佛性種子。一切眾生皆有佛性，一切意識色心是情是心，皆入佛性戒中。

Ru-Yi Translation

This luminous, vajra-like, precious precept is the very source of every Buddha and the very source of every bodhisattva — the seed of buddha-nature itself. Every being without exception has buddha-nature; every form of consciousness, whether sentient or merely mental, enters into this same precept of buddha-nature.

Reading note — after Elder Master Wei Sung

Master Wei Sung insists this line is not a devotional flourish but the chapter's actual thesis, stated as plainly as the classical Chinese allows: buddha-nature is not something acquired partway through practice, nor a private possession of the already-enlightened — it is what every being already is, prior to any practice at all, in exactly the same way for a sage as for someone who has never once heard the word "dharma." He draws out the deliberately paradoxical phrase used elsewhere in the commentarial tradition to describe this same ground: it is not decreased in an ordinary person, and it does not increase in a sage — the entire distance separating the two lies in whether it has been recognized, never in whether it is actually present.

He also stresses what "Mind Ground" is not: not a location to travel to, not an object separate from the mind that would go looking for it, and not something expressible in any fixed set of words or marks, even though every single phenomenon that exists can be shown to arise from it. This is precisely why no demon and no rival, lesser teaching can ever actually destroy it — there is simply nothing external for an opponent to reach. Having now explained both the title, word by word, and the substance the title points to, the commentary's own traditional method is satisfied; the sutra's actual narrative — Śākyamuni's radiant light, the assembly's question, and the ascent to Vairocana's own Lotus Treasury World — opens in the chapter that follows.
