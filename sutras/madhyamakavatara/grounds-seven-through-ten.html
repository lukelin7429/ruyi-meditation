Reading Notes · Grounds 7–10

Far-Going, Immovable, Excellent, and Cloud of Dharma

How a bodhisattva becomes irreversible, why even the buddhas of the ten directions must intervene at the Eighth Ground, and the coronation-like empowerment that opens the Tenth.

Author: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: tenth entry Original commentary — not a translation

These reading notes are Ru-Yi's own composition. They draw on a study of Ven. Miao Jing's (妙境長老) 入中論頌講記 (Lecture Notes on the Madhyamakāvatāra), transcribed by Fayun Publishing (法雲出版社); the original Chinese lectures are available at fayun.org.

This is not a translation of that work — it is original commentary informed by it, listed here as a reference. Any quotations from the transcript below are brief excerpts cited for illustration. Root verses are translated directly from Candrakīrti's text as rendered into Chinese by Venerable Fazun (法尊法師).

Root Verses 七地至十地

Having closed the Sixth Ground's long philosophical core, the text moves quickly through the remaining four grounds — each one paired, like the first six, with one of the ten perfections (skillful means, power, aspiration, and wisdom), and each marking a further, less intuitive kind of mastery. Chinese source at left is Venerable Fazun's (法尊法師) translation; the English at right is Ru-Yi's own translation of that Chinese.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

第七菩提心遠行地：此遠行地於滅定，剎那剎那能起入。亦善熾然方便度。

Translation · Ru-Yi

The Seventh: Far-Going Ground. On this ground, one is able to enter and rise from the absorption of cessation moment after moment, and here the perfection of skillful means blazes forth in full strength.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

第八菩提心不動地：數求勝前善根故，大士當得不退轉，入於第八不動地，此地大願極清淨。

Translation · Ru-Yi

The Eighth: Immovable Ground. By repeatedly seeking roots of virtue surpassing all that came before, the great being attains irreversibility, entering the Eighth Ground, Immovable — where the great vow becomes utterly pure.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

第九菩提心善慧地：第九圓淨一切力，亦得淨德無礙解。

Translation · Ru-Yi

The Ninth: Excellent Intelligence Ground. On this ground, all powers become perfectly purified, and one attains the pure virtue of unhindered understanding.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

第十菩提心法雲地：十地從於十方佛，得妙灌頂智增上，佛子任運澍法雨，生長眾善如大雲。

Translation · Ru-Yi

The Tenth: Cloud of Dharma Ground. Here one receives a wondrous empowerment from the buddhas of the ten directions, wisdom made supreme; the bodhisattva then effortlessly showers down the rain of the Dharma, nurturing every form of goodness to grow, like a great cloud.

Reading Notes

The Seventh Ground: Mastery Without Landing

Far-Going takes its name from a capacity that sounds paradoxical stated plainly: a bodhisattva here can enter the deepest meditative cessation — the same absorption an arhat uses, in effect, to rest from engagement with the world — and immediately rise back out of it, moment after moment, without ever settling into it as a destination. The lecture frames this ground's practice as twelve skillful means, six aimed at deepening the bodhisattva's own realization (not abandoning beings even while realizing how little separates saṃsāra from nirvāṇa, maintaining diligence, delighting in wisdom) and six aimed outward, at guiding others (making small acts of virtue yield vast results through dedication, drawing in the hesitant, ripening and then liberating those ready for it). Mastery, on this ground, isn't escape — it's the ability to keep moving through the deepest possible stillness without ever mistaking it for the finish line.

The Eighth Ground: Why the Buddhas Themselves Must Intervene

The Eighth Ground contains one of the more startling details in the whole text. By this point, a bodhisattva's afflictive obstruction — the root of ordinary craving and aversion — is essentially exhausted, and they become "immovable," irreversible even without effort. And yet the text describes the buddhas of the ten directions actively rousing this bodhisattva from a state the tradition calls "resting content," urging them onward rather than stopping here. What remains at this stage isn't affliction in the ordinary sense; it's the far subtler cognitive obstruction — a residual pull toward premature quiescence, even after the coarser problem has been solved. The lecture reads this less as a moral failing and more as a structural one: having accomplished so much, there is a real gravitational pull toward treating the accomplishment itself as sufficient, which is exactly what the buddhas' intervention exists to correct. From here the bodhisattva also gains ten masteries — over lifespan, mind, resources, karma, birth, aspiration, resolve, miraculous power, wisdom, and Dharma — the practical toolkit for shaping how and where they continue to act in the world.

The Ninth and Tenth Grounds: Teaching Without Limit

The Ninth Ground, Excellent Intelligence, perfects the four unhindered understandings — of teachings, of their meaning, of language itself, and of eloquence in expressing them — the specific capacities that make effective teaching possible rather than merely correct understanding held privately. The Tenth Ground, Cloud of Dharma, is described almost like a coronation: the bodhisattva receives an empowerment from the buddhas of the ten directions, and from that point on, teaching happens the way rain falls from a cloud — not as a series of deliberate decisions, but as the natural, unforced expression of what has already been cultivated. The image matters: a cloud doesn't calculate where to rain: it simply does, according to conditions, and every seed within reach benefits according to its own capacity to grow.

A Note on the Lecture's Discussion

The text closes this stretch with a striking numerical escalation that the lecture doesn't try to soften into pure metaphor: a First Ground bodhisattva can see, shake, and illuminate a hundred world-systems in a single moment; by the Second Ground the figure has grown a thousandfold; by the time the text reaches the Tenth Ground, the stated number of world-systems affected in a single instant exceeds anything ordinary language can express — described as beyond the count of dust motes in more universes than can be spoken of. Whether this is meant with strict literalness or as a rhetorical device for conveying scale beyond ordinary comprehension was, by the lecturer's own account, not fully settled even among the classical commentators he consulted. What both readings agree on is the direction of the claim: whatever a practitioner imagines the ceiling of this path to be, the text keeps insisting the real ceiling is further out than imagination reaches.
