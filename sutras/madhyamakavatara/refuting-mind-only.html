Refuting "Mind-Only": Candrakīrti and the Dream Argument

Candrakīrti's running debate with Yogācāra over whether the mind can exist without any object at all — argued through dreams, cataracts, and a hungry ghost's river of pus.

Author: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: sixth entry Original commentary — not a translation

These reading notes are Ru-Yi's own composition. They draw on a study of Ven. Miao Jing's (妙境長老) 入中論頌講記 (Lecture Notes on the Madhyamakāvatāra), transcribed by Fayun Publishing (法雲出版社); the original Chinese lectures are available at fayun.org.

This is not a translation of that work — it is original commentary informed by it, listed here as a reference. Any quotations from the transcript below are brief excerpts cited for illustration. Root verses are translated directly from Candrakīrti's text as rendered into Chinese by Venerable Fazun (法尊法師).

A note on framing: the lecture itself is unusually candid that it does not fully agree with the text's reading of Yogācāra here — more on that at the end of this note.

Root Verses 破唯識無境

Having refuted the four ways an external cause could produce an external effect, the text turns to a rival explanation: the Yogācāra ("Consciousness-Only") position that there is no external object at all — only mind, appearing to itself as if there were a world outside it. Chinese source at left is Venerable Fazun's (法尊法師) translation; the English at right is Ru-Yi's own translation of that Chinese.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

無外境心有何喻？若答如夢當思擇，若時我說夢無心，爾時汝喻即非有。

Translation · Ru-Yi

What analogy is there for mind without an external object? If you answer "the dream," consider this carefully: the moment I say there is no mind in a dream either, your analogy no longer holds.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

如於夢中無眼根，有似青等意心生，無眼唯由自種熟，此間盲人何不生？

Translation · Ru-Yi

If, in a dream, mental images resembling blue and so forth arise with no [functioning] eye faculty at all — simply from one's own seed ripening — then why does a blind person, here in the waking world, not have the same experience?

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

如同有翳諸眼根，鬼見膿河心亦爾。總如所知非有故，應知內識亦非有。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Just as with eyes affected by cataracts, so too the mind of a hungry ghost that sees a river as pus: since the object known is, by this very reasoning, without existence, the inner consciousness [that supposedly knows it] should equally be understood to lack existence.

Reading Notes

What the Dream Is Supposed to Prove

Yogācāra's core claim is that everything we take to be an external world is actually just consciousness appearing to itself — vivid, structured, but with no object standing genuinely apart from the mind that perceives it. The dream is Yogācāra's standard evidence: a dreamer sees mountains, hears voices, feels rain, all without any of it existing outside the dreaming mind — so why assume the waking world is any different?

Candrakīrti's first move, in the verse above, is almost a trap: if a dream can furnish vivid content with no external object, why assume the dreaming mind itself is any more real than the dreamed mountains? If the object is conceded to be unreal on the strength of the dream, the same argument concedes the perceiving mind is unreal too — the dream analogy, followed all the way through, doesn't rescue "mind alone is real" any more than it rescues "the object alone is real." Something has to explain why the mind gets special treatment, and "it's like a dream" alone doesn't supply that.

The Blind Person Who Should, But Doesn't, See

The text's sharper argument targets the specific mechanism Yogācāra offers: dream-images arise purely from a "seed" in the storehouse consciousness ripening, with no need for the eye faculty to be doing anything at all — after all, the eyes are closed and inactive throughout a dream. But a blind person's eye faculty is equally non-functional, all day, awake — and their seeds, on this same theory, should be just as capable of ripening into visual experience. They don't. Something the theory treats as dispensable — a working sense faculty — turns out to matter after all, which is exactly what the theory was trying to deny.

One River, Three Ways of Seeing It

The third verse above brings in a classic image, used elsewhere in Mahāyāna literature to illustrate how differently karma shapes perception: a hungry ghost sees a river as a channel of pus and blood; an ordinary human sees the same location as clean, drinkable water; a being with sufficient purity might see it as something finer still. Yogācāra uses this to argue objects have no fixed nature outside the perceiving mind — reasonable enough as an argument against a naively solid "external world." But Candrakīrti pushes the same logic one step further than Yogācāra wants it to go: if the object's variability across observers shows the object has no independent, fixed nature, exactly the same reasoning applies to the perceiving mind itself, which likewise appears differently — calm, agitated, confused, clear — depending on conditions. Whatever forces "the object is empty of independent nature," by parity, should equally force "the perceiving mind is empty of independent nature." Candrakīrti isn't disputing that objects lack a fixed self-nature; he's disputing that this observation earns mind an exemption that phenomena as a whole don't get.

A Note on the Lecture's Discussion

This is one of the places the lecture is most openly independent of the text it is teaching. Ven. Miao Jing states plainly that, in his own reading, Yogācāra's actual scriptures — he names the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra, the Mahāyānasaṃgraha, and the Yogācārabhūmi specifically — never claim the storehouse consciousness or its objects possess inherent self-nature (自性) in the sense Candrakīrti is refuting here; he suggests the text may be arguing against a stronger claim than Yogācāra actually makes, and says so candidly rather than smoothing over the disagreement. His own proposed reconciliation is fairly simple: "manifestation by consciousness" and "producing a result through the force of karma" may just be two vocabularies for the same underlying process, and a Madhyamika need not feel obligated to throw out the storehouse-consciousness model altogether in order to hold that nothing — mind included — has a fixed, independent nature. Whether or not that fully resolves the two systems, it's a useful thing to know going in: this chapter reads less like a settled verdict and more like one side of a debate the lecturer himself doesn't consider fully closed.
