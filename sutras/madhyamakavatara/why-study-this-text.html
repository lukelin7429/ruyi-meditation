Why Study the Madhyamakāvatāra

Who Candrakīrti was, what "middle" means in Madhyamaka, and a close reading of the text's opening homage — the three causes of a bodhisattva, and why compassion is named first among them.

Author: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: pilot entry Original commentary — not a translation

These reading notes are Ru-Yi's own composition. They draw on a study of Ven. Miao Jing's (妙境長老) 入中論頌講記 (Lecture Notes on the Madhyamakāvatāra), transcribed by Fayun Publishing (法雲出版社); the original Chinese lectures are available at fayun.org.

This is not a translation of that work — it is original commentary informed by it, listed here as a reference. Any quotations from the transcript below are brief excerpts cited for illustration. Root verses are translated directly from Candrakīrti's text as rendered into Chinese by Venerable Fazun (法尊法師).

Root Verses 歸敬頌

The text opens, as Indian śāstras conventionally do, with verses of homage — but Candrakīrti uses the homage itself to state his entire argument in miniature. Chinese source at left is Venerable Fazun's (法尊法師) translation from the Sanskrit; the English at right is Ru-Yi's own translation of that Chinese, made fresh for this page.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

聲聞中佛能王生，諸佛復從菩薩生，大悲心與無二慧、菩提心是佛子因。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Śrāvakas, and the middling buddhas [pratyekabuddhas], are born from the King of Sages; buddhas, in turn, are born from bodhisattvas. The mind of great compassion, non-dual wisdom, and the mind of awakening — these are the causes that produce a child of the Victorious Ones.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

悲性於佛廣大果，初猶種子長如水，常時受用若成熟，故我先讚大悲心。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Compassion, toward the vast fruit that is buddhahood, is like the seed at the very start, like water in the growing, and, when the fruit has ripened, like what is enjoyed for a long time. Therefore I pay homage to compassion first.

Reading Notes

Why This Text

The Madhyamakāvatāra — "Entering the Middle Way" — is Candrakīrti's guide into Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, the foundational text of Madhyamaka philosophy. Candrakīrti wrote it because the Middle Way Treatise itself is notoriously hard to enter: it opens with the "eight negations" and gives the reader almost nothing to hold onto. The Madhyamakāvatāra exists to make that entry possible — not by simplifying Nāgārjuna's insight, but by walking the reader up to it through the ten bodhisattva grounds (十地), each ground illustrated by its own root verses.

Candrakīrti taught at Nālandā roughly a century after Nāgārjuna, and — as it happens — at almost the same moment Xuanzang was there studying under Śīlabhadra. The two may never have met; by the time Xuanzang arrived, Śīlabhadra was already said to be 120 years old, while Candrakīrti was probably not yet fifty. What did travel from Nālandā to later centuries was the text itself, which became one of the most heavily studied Madhyamaka works in Tibetan scholarship, particularly through Tsongkhapa's own commentary on it.

The Meaning of "Middle"

Before opening the verses, it's worth sitting with the word "middle" (中) itself, because the whole text hangs on it. "Middle" here does not mean a compromise between two views. It means refusing to fall into either of two extremes: treating things as truly, solidly existent, or treating them as simply nonexistent.

Ordinary experience defaults to the first extreme without our noticing. A wall is there; we don't walk into it because we take it to be real in an unqualified way. Fear, desire, and aversion all depend on this same unexamined assumption — that what confronts us is exactly as solid as it appears. Madhyamaka's claim is not that the wall is a hallucination, but that it is dependently arisen and therefore empty of the fixed, independent nature we project onto it — and that this emptiness is not a subtraction from the wall's existence, but what allows it to function as a wall (arise from causes, obstruct passage, be walked around) at all. Empty and dependently-existent are not two separate facts about the wall; they are one fact seen without the extra assumption. That is the "middle" — and it is also, on this account, what a bodhisattva's insight into emptiness actually looks like in practice, not merely a philosophical position held about the world.

Reading the Homage

The logic of the two verses above runs backward through a chain of dependence: śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas depend on a buddha having appeared and taught; a buddha depends on having first been a bodhisattva; and a bodhisattva depends on three things arising together — great compassion (大悲心), wisdom free of the two kinds of grasping (無二慧, freedom from clinging to a fixed self and to fixed dharmas), and the resolve for awakening (菩提心). None of the three is sufficient alone. Wisdom without compassion has no reason to stay engaged with difficult beings; the resolve for awakening without compassion has nothing sustaining it when the work gets hard; compassion without wisdom collapses into ordinary sentimentality that cannot actually free anyone.

The second verse explains why compassion gets named first among the three, using an image that repeats through the whole first chapter: compassion is the seed at the beginning, the water sustaining growth throughout, and — this is the less obvious part — still present at the end, as what is "enjoyed" once the fruit (buddhahood) has ripened. A buddha's continued activity in the world is not a leftover of the path but compassion's fruition. This is worth pausing on for readers coming from more renunciation-focused frameworks: the argument here is not that compassion is instrumentally useful for reaching some other goal, but that it is present at every stage, including the goal itself.

A Note on the Lecture's Discussion

The transcript's own strength, session to session, is less in dense argument and more in how readily an abstract point gets tied back to something recognizable. In this opening session, the illustration is a story from the Han dynasty: the general Li Guang mistakes a boulder for a tiger in the grass and shoots an arrow clean through it — then, told it's only rock, tries again and cannot pierce it a second time. The point being made is not about archery but about how much of what we call "solid" and "penetrable" tracks our own conviction rather than some fixed property sitting in the object. It's a plain, almost folkloric way into a claim that could otherwise stay purely abstract, and it's characteristic of how this whole lecture series works — doctrine explained by returning again and again to something the listener has already felt.

The question-and-answer portion of this session is substantial and worth summarizing rather than quoting at length: a listener pushes back on the claim that "the small vehicle came out of the great vehicle," pointing to the Āgamas' own instruction that awakened disciples should go teach. The exchange that follows doesn't fully resolve in either direction — it moves through what "final nirvāṇa without remainder" means for an arhat versus a bodhisattva who has reached the eighth ground, why compassion is what distinguishes the two, and a fairly candid aside on the state of contemporary Chinese Buddhism relative to the Tibetan and Theravāda traditions. It reads less like a settled doctrinal answer and more like watching an experienced teacher think in real time with an engaged room — which is itself a useful thing to preserve in notes on this text, even in summary.
