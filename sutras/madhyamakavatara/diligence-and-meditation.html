The Fourth and Fifth Grounds: Diligence and Meditative Concentration

The 37 factors of enlightenment, the four foundations of mindfulness read the Mahāyāna way, and why even advanced meditators remain vulnerable to subtle pride.

Author: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fourth entry Original commentary — not a translation

These reading notes are Ru-Yi's own composition. They draw on a study of Ven. Miao Jing's (妙境長老) 入中論頌講記 (Lecture Notes on the Madhyamakāvatāra), transcribed by Fayun Publishing (法雲出版社); the original Chinese lectures are available at fayun.org.

This is not a translation of that work — it is original commentary informed by it, listed here as a reference. Any quotations from the transcript below are brief excerpts cited for illustration. Root verses are translated directly from Candrakīrti's text as rendered into Chinese by Venerable Fazun (法尊法師).

Root Verses 四焰慧地 · 五難勝地

Diligence (精進) is the Fourth Ground's virtue, Arciṣmatī — "Blazing Light." Meditative concentration (靜慮) is the Fifth Ground's, Sudurjayā — "Difficult to Conquer." Chinese source at left is Venerable Fazun's (法尊法師) translation; the English at right is Ru-Yi's own translation of that Chinese.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

功德皆隨精進行，福慧二種資糧因，何地精進最熾盛，彼即第四焰慧地。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Every virtue follows upon diligence, the cause behind both provisions — of merit and of wisdom. Whichever ground has diligence blazing at its height, that is the Fourth, Blazing Light.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

此地佛子由勤修，菩提分法發慧焰，較前赤光尤超勝，自見所屬皆遍盡。

Translation · Ru-Yi

On this ground, a child of the Victorious Ones, through diligent cultivation of the factors of enlightenment, kindles a flame of wisdom surpassing the ground before it — and the view of a self, together with all that belongs to it, is fully exhausted.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

大士住於難勝地，一切諸魔莫能勝，靜慮增勝極善知，善慧諸諦微妙性。

Translation · Ru-Yi

A great being abiding on the ground Difficult to Conquer cannot be overcome by any māra; meditative concentration grows supreme, and its wisdom skillfully knows the subtle nature of the truths.

Reading Notes

Why Nothing Moves Without Diligence

The first verse above states the Fourth Ground's logic almost as a syllogism: every virtue "follows upon" diligence, so whichever ground has diligence at its most intense is, by definition, this one. The lecture treats this less as a poetic claim and more as an observation about how practice actually works — faith, discipline, learning, generosity, and wisdom don't sustain or deepen themselves; without sustained effort behind them, each one stalls. The text's illustration is blunt rather than devotional: an elderly couple, destitute, are said to have had the capacity to become wealthy nobility, or even to attain arhatship, "if only they had been diligent" — outcomes here are treated as earned, not allotted.

The Fourth Ground's cultivation centers on the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment (菩提分法), and specifically on the four foundations of mindfulness (四念住) — body, feeling, mind, and phenomena. What's distinctive about the Mahāyāna reading of this practice, as the lecture frames it, is the object of contemplation. The older, Hīnayāna version of the four foundations observes the body as impure, feeling as suffering, mind as impermanent, and phenomena as without a self — true observations, but observations about qualities of things that are still, implicitly, being treated as there. The Mahāyāna version pushes one step further: body, feeling, mind, and phenomena are themselves contemplated as empty, not merely as impure or impermanent. This is also where a subtler layer of self-grasping — one the text calls "innate" (俱生我執), as opposed to the more easily named "conceptually acquired" kind already cut at the First Ground — finally starts to give way, since it survives exposure to doctrine and only yields to sustained direct contemplation.

The Fifth Ground: Unconquerable, and Still Not Finished

"Difficult to Conquer" names two things at once. Positively, the text says a bodhisattva here becomes unassailable by māras — the lecture specifically has in mind the kind of subtle, seductive obstruction that targets advanced practitioners rather than crude temptation. But the name is also read, in an alternate strand the lecture mentions, as pointing to something harder: fusing the two truths — conventional and ultimate — into a single, coherent way of seeing, which is difficult even for a bodhisattva this far along the path.

The lecture is unusually direct about a risk here that a reader might assume was already resolved by the Third Ground's wisdom: even a meditator with real, deep concentration can still develop attachment, wrong view, or subtle pride toward the concentration-state itself — toward the accomplishment of stillness, rather than toward what the stillness was supposed to be used for. Meditative concentration alone, however refined, doesn't automatically uproot that. Only wisdom does. This ground is also where a bodhisattva first attains the four form-realm absorptions together with the ordinary superknowledges — but conspicuously not yet the deepest attainment, cessation of feeling and perception, which belongs to the Sixth Ground.

A Note on the Lecture's Discussion

The lecture is insistent, almost polemical, on one methodological point that recurs across both grounds: genuine contemplative insight (vipaśyanā, 毗缽舍那) has to be learned from the actual source texts — the transcript names the Yogācārabhūmi and Zhiyi's meditation manuals specifically — rather than picked up as a vague technique of "just watching the mind" detached from any doctrinal content. The concern isn't scholarly fussiness; it's that a meditator who mistakes stillness itself, or the bare fact of "knowing," for realization can spend a great deal of diligence — real, sustained effort — reinforcing exactly the subtle self-view this whole stretch of the path is meant to dismantle. Diligence and concentration, in other words, are necessary but not, on their own, sufficient; the text keeps insisting that wisdom has to be doing the actual work.
