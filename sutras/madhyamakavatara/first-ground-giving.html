The First Ground: Joy and the Perfection of Giving

What it takes to enter the First Ground of the bodhisattva path, why giving is its signature virtue, and the difference between giving that merely pays off and giving that liberates.

Author: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: second entry Original commentary — not a translation

These reading notes are Ru-Yi's own composition. They draw on a study of Ven. Miao Jing's (妙境長老) 入中論頌講記 (Lecture Notes on the Madhyamakāvatāra), transcribed by Fayun Publishing (法雲出版社); the original Chinese lectures are available at fayun.org.

This is not a translation of that work — it is original commentary informed by it, listed here as a reference. Any quotations from the transcript below are brief excerpts cited for illustration. Root verses are translated directly from Candrakīrti's text as rendered into Chinese by Venerable Fazun (法尊法師).

Root Verses 初極喜地 · 布施波羅蜜

After the homage, the text turns to its main structure: the ten grounds (十地) a bodhisattva passes through on the way to buddhahood, each named for a quality that comes to full flower there. The First Ground is Pramuditā — "Perfect Joy." Chinese source at left is Venerable Fazun's (法尊法師) translation; the English at right is Ru-Yi's own translation of that Chinese.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

佛子此心於眾生，為度彼故隨悲轉，由普賢願善回向，安住極喜此名初。

Translation · Ru-Yi

A child of the Victorious Ones' mind turns toward sentient beings, following compassion in order to free them; well dedicated through Samantabhadra's vow, it abides in Perfect Joy — this is named the First [ground].

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

施者受者施物空，施名出世波羅蜜，由於三輪生執著，名世間波羅蜜多。

Translation · Ru-Yi

When giver, receiver, and gift are seen as empty, the giving is called a world-transcending perfection; when attachment arises toward these three, it is called a worldly perfection.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

極喜猶如水晶月，安住佛子意空中，所依光明獲端嚴，破諸重暗得尊勝。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Perfect Joy is like a crystal moon, abiding in the sky of a bodhisattva's mind; its radiance attains beauty and dignity, dispelling layer upon layer of darkness to attain supremacy.

Reading Notes

Entering the First Ground

The text doesn't treat the First Ground as an early or minor attainment. Getting there is itself described as the work of a full "great eon" (一大阿僧祇劫) of practice — drawing near good teachers, hearing the true teaching, reflecting on it correctly, and practicing accordingly — moving through what the tradition calls the "outer" and "inner" stages of an ordinary being (外凡, 內凡) before reaching a fourfold threshold known as warmth, summit, forbearance, and supreme worldly dharma (煖、頂、忍、世第一法). What actually opens the door to the First Ground is the acceptance of non-arising (無生法忍) — not an intellectual conclusion that phenomena are empty, but a direct realization of it.

Something changes discretely at that threshold, not just gradually. The three fetters that keep a being circling helplessly through the six realms — the view of a fixed self (薩迦耶見), attachment to rites and vows as ends in themselves (戒禁取), and doubt (疑) — are permanently severed, the same three fetters a stream-enterer cuts in the Hīnayāna path. The practitioner becomes irreversible: never again falling to the three lower realms, except by their own compassionate choice. And only from this point does the text consider it accurate to actually call someone a "bodhisattva" — earlier aspiration and practice matter, but the name is now earned outright, not merely intended.

The ground takes its name — Pramuditā, Perfect Joy — from what this moment feels like from the inside: a joy the lecture transcript describes as overwhelming enough to bring tears and make "every pore of the body stand on end." That's not incidental color. It's evidence, in the text's own logic, that whatever has just happened is real and not merely conceptual.

Why Giving Comes First

Each of the ten grounds is paired with one of the ten perfections (波羅蜜), and the First Ground's signature virtue is giving (布施). The text's reasoning for the order is almost blunt: every being seeks happiness; happiness is impossible without the resources to sustain it; and resources, ultimately, come from giving. So giving is what the Buddha teaches first — not because it's the easiest perfection, but because it's the most immediately legible one, the perfection every listener already has some direct experience of wanting and receiving.

The text pushes the ground's generosity to an extreme case: a bodhisattva at this stage can give away flesh and body without inner reluctance, a capacity the lecture attributes specifically to the stability that comes with real meditative attainment — pain is still pain, but it no longer commands the mind the way it commands an untrained one.

Worldly and Transcendent Giving

The second verse above draws the line that matters most for practice. Giving that still holds giver, receiver, and gift as three separate, solidly real things is called "worldly" — it's genuinely good, and it genuinely produces future comfort, but that comfort runs out once its merit is spent, the way any finite deposit eventually runs dry. Giving contemplated as empty of that three-part fixation — not that giver, receiver, and gift don't conventionally exist, but that none of the three has the solid, independent nature we instinctively assign it — is what the text calls "transcendent" or "world-surpassing" (出世波羅蜜): it doesn't just pay off, it participates directly in the path toward liberation.

This distinction — the same action, but a different relationship to what one believes is really there while doing it — recurs for every one of the ten perfections as the text moves through the later grounds. Giving is simply the first and most concrete place it's introduced.

A Note on the Lecture's Discussion

One illustration from this part of the lecture is worth keeping: a doctor treats a king who shows no evident gratitude, and privately begins to doubt that goodness is ever rewarded — only to discover, much later, that flocks, land, and gold had already been quietly set aside for him all along, simply not yet delivered. The point isn't really about doctors or kings. It's aimed at the ordinary doubt that arises when good conduct doesn't appear to pay off on any visible timetable — the lecture's answer is that the fruit of genuine giving is not absent, only not yet ripened, and that doubting it on the evidence available so far is premature.
