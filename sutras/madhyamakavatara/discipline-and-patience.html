The Second and Third Grounds: Discipline and Patience

Why pure discipline requires the wisdom of no-self, and how a bodhisattva endures harm without anger — grounded in compassion and in seeing "the cutter, the cut, and the cutting" as illusion-like.

Author: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: third entry Original commentary — not a translation

These reading notes are Ru-Yi's own composition. They draw on a study of Ven. Miao Jing's (妙境長老) 入中論頌講記 (Lecture Notes on the Madhyamakāvatāra), transcribed by Fayun Publishing (法雲出版社); the original Chinese lectures are available at fayun.org.

This is not a translation of that work — it is original commentary informed by it, listed here as a reference. Any quotations from the transcript below are brief excerpts cited for illustration. Root verses are translated directly from Candrakīrti's text as rendered into Chinese by Venerable Fazun (法尊法師).

Root Verses 二離垢地 · 三發光地

Giving is the First Ground's virtue; discipline (持戒) is the Second Ground's, Vimalā — "Stainlessness." Patience (安忍) is the Third Ground's, Prabhākarī — "Radiance." Chinese source at left is Venerable Fazun's (法尊法師) translation; the English at right is Ru-Yi's own translation of that Chinese.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

若彼淨戒執有我，則彼尸羅不清淨，故彼恆於三輪中，二邊心行皆遠離。

Translation · Ru-Yi

If pure discipline still grasps at a self, then that discipline is not truly pure. So, amid the three wheels of discipline, [the bodhisattva] stays far from both extremes of mental engagement.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

佛子月放離垢光，非諸有攝有中祥，猶如秋季月光明，能除眾生意熱惱。

Translation · Ru-Yi

The bodhisattva's moon radiates a stainless light — unclaimed by any realm of existence, yet auspicious within it — like autumn moonlight, able to dispel the fevered turmoil of beings' minds.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

已見無我諸菩薩，能所何時何相割，彼見諸法如影像，由此亦能善安忍。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Bodhisattvas who have already seen selflessness ask: when, and in what form, is there ever a cutter and something cut? Seeing all phenomena as like reflections, they are able, by this very seeing, to remain patient with ease.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

若有瞋恚諸佛子，百劫所修施戒福，一剎那頃能頓壞，故無他罪勝不忍。

Translation · Ru-Yi

If a child of the Victorious Ones gives way to anger, a hundred eons' worth of merit from giving and discipline can be destroyed in a single instant. No other transgression, therefore, is worse than the failure to be patient.

Reading Notes

Purity That Doesn't Grasp

The Second Ground's discipline is described as pure even in dreams — the text's way of saying it has stopped being a matter of watchfulness and become simply how the mind is. But the first verse above adds a harder condition: discipline kept out of conscience or fear of consequence, while still holding onto a real, solid "I" who is being disciplined, is not yet the discipline this ground means. Attachment to a fixed self is exactly what eventually produces the craving and aversion that break a vow in the first place — so discipline grounded in the wisdom of no-self is, in a real sense, more stable than discipline grounded in willpower alone. The Second Ground names this quality "Stainlessness": expressed concretely through the ten wholesome courses of action, three of body, four of speech, three of mind.

The lecture is blunt about what happens when giving is practiced without discipline: generosity still ripens, but it can ripen in an unfortunate rebirth — a well-fed pet, or a wealthy hungry ghost — wasting its own fruit on a being unable to use it well. Discipline is what lets every other virtue actually land where it's meant to.

The Third Ground: Radiance and the Extreme Test of Patience

The Third Ground is called Prabhākarī, "Radiance," because sustained practice here is said to burn through what obstructs deeper knowledge the way fire burns dry firewood — the text describes a bodhisattva entering this ground as emitting a reddish-gold light, like a sunrise. Its virtue, patience, is pushed by the text to its most extreme case: enduring the body being cut apart, piece by piece, without anger increasing — in fact, with patience increasing instead.

What makes that plausible, according to the lecture, is two things held together, not one substituting for the other. Compassion supplies the reason not to retaliate: whoever is causing the harm is, in this view, acting out of their own confusion and deserves pity rather than anger. Wisdom supplies something more specific — under the kind of deep meditative concentration the text assumes at this stage, "the one who cuts," "the one who is cut," and the act of cutting itself are seen as no more solidly real than a reflection. Third verse above makes the logic explicit: it isn't that pain has stopped, but that the framework in which pain would normally trigger anger has already come apart under analysis.

Why Anger Is Uniquely Costly

The fourth verse is one of the sharper warnings in the text, and it's aimed specifically at practitioners, not at wrongdoing in general: a single outburst of anger can undo a hundred eons of accumulated merit from giving and discipline, instantly. Elsewhere the lecture clarifies this isn't quite annihilation — the merit's eventual fruition is delayed rather than permanently erased — but the practical force of the warning doesn't really depend on that footnote. The text's broader argument against retaliation is almost purely pragmatic: anger cannot undo harm already done, it plants a further cause for one's own future suffering, and — visibly, immediately — it disfigures the person feeling it and clouds the very judgment needed to tell a wise response from an unwise one.

A Note on the Lecture's Discussion

One image from this part of the lecture pairs well with the two grounds together: a goddess of fortune and her sister, misfortune, are inseparable twins — wherever one goes, the other follows, whether anyone invites her or not. The lecture uses this to make a point about discipline specifically: a pure community and a violation of its discipline cannot really coexist side by side any more than fortune can be kept while turning away its shadow. The same point, extended, applies to the patience taught on the Third Ground — a bodhisattva who wants the "radiance" this ground describes doesn't get to keep it while making an exception for the one provocation that seemed to deserve anger.
