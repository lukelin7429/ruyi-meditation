Refuting the Self: The Sevenfold Chariot Reasoning

The text's most famous argument — is a chariot its parts, apart from its parts, or something else entirely? — applied to dismantle a fixed, independently existing self.

Author: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: ninth entry Original commentary — not a translation

These reading notes are Ru-Yi's own composition. They draw on a study of Ven. Miao Jing's (妙境長老) 入中論頌講記 (Lecture Notes on the Madhyamakāvatāra), transcribed by Fayun Publishing (法雲出版社); the original Chinese lectures are available at fayun.org.

This is not a translation of that work — it is original commentary informed by it, listed here as a reference. Any quotations from the transcript below are brief excerpts cited for illustration. Root verses are translated directly from Candrakīrti's text as rendered into Chinese by Venerable Fazun (法尊法師).

Root Verses 破我 · 七相觀車

Everything so far — the refutation of wrong causation, the two truths, the sixteen emptinesses — has been building toward the text's central practical target: the belief in a fixed, independently existing self, the root the tradition calls satkāyadṛṣṭi (薩迦耶見). This entry covers the argument's best-known form: applying a sevenfold analysis first to an ordinary chariot, then to the person. Chinese source at left is Venerable Fazun's (法尊法師) translation; the English at right is Ru-Yi's own translation of that Chinese.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

慧見煩惱諸過患，皆從薩迦耶見生，由了知我是彼境，故瑜伽師先破我。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Wisdom sees that all the faults of the afflictions arise from the view of a perishable, aggregated self. Knowing that the self is the object of that view, a yogin therefore refutes the self first.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

如不許車異支分，亦非不異非有支，不依支分非支依，非唯積聚復非形。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Just as a chariot is not accepted to be different from its parts, nor identical to them, nor possessing them; it does not depend on its parts, nor do the parts depend on it; it is not the mere collection of the parts, and it is not their shape either.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

可為眾生說彼車，名為有支及有分，亦名作者與受者，莫壞世間許世俗。

Translation · Ru-Yi

For the sake of beings, that chariot may still be spoken of as possessing parts, as a whole made of parts, and as both agent and one carried — without disturbing what the world conventionally accepts.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

由無作者則無業，故離我時無我所，若見我我所皆空，諸瑜伽師得解脫。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Since there is no [inherently existing] agent, there is no [inherently existing] action; so apart from a self, there is nothing that is truly "mine." A yogin who sees both self and what belongs to self as empty attains liberation.

Reading Notes

Why the Self Comes First

The first verse above states the priority plainly: every affliction — craving, aversion, jealousy, pride — ultimately traces back to the instinctive sense of a fixed "I" that needs protecting, indulging, or defending. This isn't presented as a philosophers' problem. The lecture makes a point of noting that animals, with no doctrinal training at all, still grasp at self intensely enough to flee danger and cling to comfort — proof, in the text's logic, that self-grasping doesn't wait for or depend on a theory. What the philosophical self-views (a self separate from the aggregates, a self identical to them, an "inexpressible" self neither one nor the other) add is only a more explicit, more stubborn version of something already operating in every being, everywhere, without exception.

The Chariot, Examined Seven Ways

The second verse above lays out the full analysis, tested first on something with no emotional stakes: a chariot. Is a chariot the same thing as its wheels, axle, and frame? No — "chariot" and "its parts" aren't used interchangeably. Is it something different from its parts, existing apart from them? No — no one has ever found a chariot lurking separately from any of its components. Does the chariot possess its parts, the way an owner possesses property? Does it depend on them, or do they depend on it? Is it simply the parts heaped together? Is it their particular shape? Each answer, examined, fails — a heap of disassembled wheels and axles isn't a chariot, and assembling them in "chariot shape" doesn't locate some new thing called "chariot" hiding inside the arrangement.

The same seven questions, run against a person instead of a chariot, produce the same result. "Self" is not the five aggregates, not something apart from them, does not possess them, is not possessed by them, does not depend on them nor they on it, is not merely their collection, and is not any particular configuration of them. There is no eighth place left to look.

Not Destroying the World's Conventions

The third verse above heads off the obvious worry: if the chariot can't be found under analysis, doesn't that mean there's no chariot — and, by extension, no self, no agent, no one who acts or is responsible for anything? The text's answer is explicit and deliberately practical: without philosophical investigation, "chariot," "self," "agent," and "the one who experiences results" remain perfectly legitimate, functioning exactly as everyone already uses them. Nothing about the analysis is meant to disrupt ordinary language or ordinary responsibility. What the analysis removes is only the further, usually unnoticed assumption that behind the label there must also be some solid, independently findable thing that the label refers to. Take that assumption away, and dependent origination, mere designation, and emptiness turn out to be three ways of describing exactly the same fact, not three competing claims.

A Note on the Lecture's Discussion

One image from this stretch of the lecture is worth keeping in mind whenever the chariot argument starts to feel like a purely abstract exercise: a man notices what looks like a snake against his bedroom wall and is afraid — then someone helpfully reassures him, "don't worry, there's no elephant in this room." True, and completely beside the point; the fear was never about an elephant. The lecture uses this to make a sharp practical distinction: refuting some grand, philosopher's-only version of a permanent, separate self (the "elephant") does nothing at all about the ordinary, moment-to-moment grasping at "me" that actually runs through daily experience (the "snake," still very much on the wall). The sevenfold reasoning is aimed at the snake — the self ordinary people actually cling to, embedded in the aggregates themselves — not at a straw-man self no one seriously believes in to begin with.
