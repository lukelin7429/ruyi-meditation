The Two Truths and the Refutation of Wrong Arising

Why "I saw my mother give birth to me" isn't proof of anything, ultimately — the fourfold refutation of self-arising, other-arising, both, and causeless arising that opens the text's central philosophical argument.

Author: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fifth entry Original commentary — not a translation

These reading notes are Ru-Yi's own composition. They draw on a study of Ven. Miao Jing's (妙境長老) 入中論頌講記 (Lecture Notes on the Madhyamakāvatāra), transcribed by Fayun Publishing (法雲出版社); the original Chinese lectures are available at fayun.org.

This is not a translation of that work — it is original commentary informed by it, listed here as a reference. Any quotations from the transcript below are brief excerpts cited for illustration. Root verses are translated directly from Candrakīrti's text as rendered into Chinese by Venerable Fazun (法尊法師).

Root Verses 六現前地 · 觀甚深緣起真實義

The Sixth Ground, Abhimukhī — "Manifest" or "Face-to-Face" — is where the text's philosophical argument really opens up, and it occupies by far the largest portion of the whole work. This entry covers its opening move: showing that nothing arises in any of the four ways a cause could produce an effect, and drawing the line between two fundamentally different kinds of "seeing." Chinese source at left is Venerable Fazun's (法尊法師) translation; the English at right is Ru-Yi's own translation of that Chinese.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

彼非彼生豈從他？亦非共生寧無因？

Translation · Ru-Yi

It does not arise from itself — how, then, from another? Not from both together — how, then, without any cause at all?

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

因滅猶見異果故，世亦不許彼是一。故計諸法從自生，真實世間俱非理。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Since the cause is seen to cease while a different effect appears, even ordinary people do not take the two as one and the same. So the claim that things arise from themselves does not hold up — neither under ultimate analysis, nor even by the world's own standard.

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

若謂依他有他生，火焰亦應生黑暗，又應一切生一切，諸非能生他性同。

Translation · Ru-Yi

If "arising from another" held simply because cause and effect are other, then a flame should be able to produce darkness — indeed, anything should be able to produce anything, since every non-cause is equally "other."

Source · 法尊法師漢譯

由於諸法見真妄，故得諸法二種體，說見真境即真諦，所見虛妄名俗諦。

Translation · Ru-Yi

Because things are seen either truly or falsely, all things are said to have two natures: what is seen truly is called the ultimate truth; what is seen falsely is named the conventional.

Reading Notes

Whose Testimony Counts?

Ordinary experience seems to settle causal questions easily: a mother gives birth to a child, a seed gives rise to a sprout, and that certainty feels like enough. The lecture's answer is that this kind of confidence is real but limited in scope — it's valid for correcting other ordinary claims ("no, I planted that tree myself"), but it was never designed to answer, and can't answer, the harder question of whether cause and effect are related in a way that survives philosophical analysis. Before it even reaches the four alternatives, the text is making a point about method: "the world says so" is not, by itself, a closing argument.

The Fourfold Refutation

Any real cause-and-effect relationship, examined closely, has to fall into one of four categories: the effect arises from itself, from something genuinely other, from both together, or from nothing at all. The text works through each in turn and finds all four unworkable.

From itself fails on a simple timing problem: if the effect already exists, in its own right, within the cause, then "producing" it is redundant — and worse, there's no reason production should ever stop, since the same self-sufficient effect should keep re-arising indefinitely. The second verse above adds that even ordinary people don't actually believe cause and effect are strictly identical — nobody thinks the seed itself, unchanged, is the sprout — so the theory fails by the world's own standard, not only under deeper analysis.

From another fails for the opposite reason. If a cause and its effect are genuinely, independently separate things — real "others" in the strong sense — then nothing about their relationship explains why this cause produces this effect rather than some entirely unrelated thing. The third verse above pushes the point to its most vivid form: if being "other" were sufficient for causation, fire should be able to produce darkness, and — since everything is equally "other" than everything else — anything should be able to produce anything.

The remaining two options are dispatched quickly because they inherit the same problems: arising from both self and other combines the difficulties of each rather than solving them, and arising without any cause at all would mean effort is pointless — farmers would have no more reason to plant seeds than not to, since results would arrive (or fail to) regardless of what anyone did.

Two Kinds of Seeing

Having shown that nothing survives analysis as a genuinely, independently existing cause producing a genuinely, independently existing effect, the text still has to account for the fact that causes and effects plainly do appear to function. Its answer is the two truths, stated in the fourth verse above: what is seen with the wisdom that realizes emptiness is the ultimate truth; what is seen otherwise — which includes essentially all of ordinary experience — is conventional.

The lecture adds a further distinction worth keeping: conventional seeing itself splits into two kinds. There is the "healthy-organ" seeing that the world accepts as accurate — a vase, a road, a face — and there is the seeing of a diseased or impaired organ: a dream, a mirage, a magician's illusion. The world is right to distinguish between these two conventionally — a mirage is not water, even though neither is ultimately real in the way it appears. But relative to an ārya's undefiled wisdom, both the vase and the mirage are, in the end, equally "false" — appearing other than how they ultimately are. Nothing in this framework requires treating the vase and the mirage as somehow the same kind of nonexistence; it only asks that neither be mistaken for what an ārya actually sees.

A Note on the Lecture's Discussion

The lecture reaches back here to a story used earlier in the series — the Han-dynasty general Li Guang, who shoots an arrow clean through what he takes to be a tiger in the grass, then discovers it was only a boulder and, trying again with the same force, can no longer pierce it. The point made the first time was about how conviction shapes what seems penetrable or solid. Brought back here, it does a second job: neither "it's a tiger" nor "it's a rock" was ever the ultimate truth of the matter — both are just different conventional readings, each doing real causal work (the arrow really did go through, once), neither one entitled to the last word. That's roughly the position ordinary, unexamined seeing is always in, according to this stretch of the text — useful, functional, and still not the same thing as how an ārya actually sees.
