Sudden and Gradual

頓漸品第八

By this point the empire had come to speak of "Neng in the south, Xiu in the north" as though two rival schools were competing for the same prize. Huineng's own verdict opens this chapter: the Dharma has no sudden or gradual at all — only people's own faculties differ, sharp or dull. What follows tests that claim three times over: a spy sent to steal the teaching who ends up staying as a disciple, a would-be assassin whose blade cannot touch Huineng, and a brilliant thirteen-year-old whose quick, correct-sounding answers earn the sharpest rebuke in the entire sūtra.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T2008 · Zongbao's redaction, Yuan dynasty (1291 CE) Commentary guide: Hanshan Deqing's 六祖法寶壇經解義 (17th c.)
This part covers
Huineng's own verdict that the Dharma has no sudden or gradual, only faculties that are sharp or dull; Zhicheng, sent by Shenxiu's community to overhear Huineng's teaching in secret, who instead stays as a disciple; the would-be assassin Xingchang, whose blade cannot harm Huineng, and the paradox of permanence and impermanence that later brings him to genuine awakening; and the young novice Shenhui, whose quick, technically correct answers are met with the sūtra's bluntest correction — "you will only ever become a follower who deals in intellectual understanding."
Why it matters
This chapter directly addresses the sectarian rivalry that had grown up around Huineng's own lifetime, and answers it not by defending the southern school's superiority but by dissolving the premise of the dispute — the Dharma was never two things to begin with.

1. The Spy Who Stayed: Zhicheng

Chinese Source

時，祖師居曹溪寶林，神秀大師在荊南玉泉寺。于時兩宗盛化，人皆稱南能北秀，故有南北二宗頓漸之分，而學者莫知宗趣。師謂眾曰：「法本一宗，人有南北。法即一種，見有遲疾。何名頓漸？法無頓漸，人有利鈍，故名頓漸。」然秀之徒眾，往往譏南宗祖師，不識一字，有何所長。秀曰：「他得無師之智，深悟上乘。吾不如也。且吾師五祖，親傳衣法。豈徒然哉！吾恨不能遠去親近，虛受國恩。汝等諸人，毋滯於此，可往曹溪參決。」一日，命門人志誠曰：「汝聰明多智，可為吾到曹溪聽法。若有所聞，盡心記取，還為吾說。」志誠稟命至曹溪，隨眾參請，不言來處。時祖師告眾曰：「今有盜法之人，潛在此會。」志誠即出禮拜，具陳其事。師曰：「汝從玉泉來，應是細作。」對曰：「不是。」師曰：「何得不是？」對曰：「未說即是，說了不是。」師曰：「汝師若為示眾？」對曰：「常指誨大眾，住心觀靜，長坐不臥。」師曰：「住心觀靜，是病非禪；長坐拘身，於理何益？聽吾偈曰： 「生來坐不臥，　死去臥不坐， 一具臭骨頭，　何為立功課？」 志誠再拜曰：「弟子在秀大師處學道九年，不得契悟。今聞和尚一說，便契本心。弟子生死事大，和尚大慈，更為教示。」師云：「吾聞汝師教示學人戒定慧法，未審汝師說戒定慧行相如何？與吾說看。」誠曰：「秀大師說，諸惡莫作名為戒，諸善奉行名為慧，自淨其意名為定。彼說如此，未審和尚以何法誨人？」師曰：「吾若言有法與人，即為誑汝。但且隨方解縛，假名三昧。如汝師所說戒定慧，實不可思議。吾所見戒定慧又別。」志誠曰：「戒定慧只合一種，如何更別？」師曰：「汝師戒定慧接大乘人，吾戒定慧接最上乘人。悟解不同，見有遲疾。汝聽吾說，與彼同否？吾所說法，不離自性。離體說法，名為相說，自性常迷。須知一切萬法，皆從自性起用，是真戒定慧法。聽吾偈曰： 「心地無非自性戒，　心地無癡自性慧， 心地無亂自性定，　不增不減自金剛， 身去身來本三昧。」 誠聞偈，悔謝，乃呈一偈曰： 「五蘊幻身，　幻何究竟？　迴趣真如， 法還不淨。」 師然之，復語誠曰：「汝師戒定慧，勸小根智人；吾戒定慧，勸大根智人。若悟自性，亦不立菩提涅槃，亦不立解脫知見。無一法可得，方能建立萬法。若解此意，亦名佛身，亦名菩提涅槃，亦名解脫知見。見性之人，立亦得、不立亦得，去來自由，無滯無礙，應用隨作，應語隨答，普見化身，不離自性，即得自在神通游戲三昧，是名見性。」志誠再啟師曰：「如何是不立義？」師曰：「自性無非、無癡無亂，念念般若觀照，常離法相，自由自在，縱橫盡得，有何可立？自性自悟，頓悟頓修，亦無漸次，所以不立一切法。諸法寂滅，有何次第？」志誠禮拜，願為執侍，朝夕不懈(誠吉州太和人也)。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the Patriarch dwelt at Baolin Temple in Caoxi, while the great teacher Shenxiu was at Yuquan Temple in Jingnan. Both schools were flourishing greatly, and people everywhere spoke of "Neng in the south, Xiu in the north," so that there came to be a division between the northern and southern schools, sudden and gradual — yet students had no way of knowing which doctrine to follow. The Master said to the assembly: "The Dharma itself is fundamentally one single school; it is only people who have come to be divided into north and south. The Dharma itself is a single thing; it is only the seeing of it that comes slowly or swiftly. Why are they called sudden and gradual? The Dharma itself has no sudden or gradual — it is only that people are sharp or dull, and this is why they are called sudden and gradual." Yet Shenxiu's own disciples often mocked the patriarch of the southern school, saying that a man who could not read a single character could have nothing worth learning from him. Shenxiu said, "He has attained a wisdom that needs no teacher, and has deeply awakened to the highest vehicle — I myself am not his equal. Moreover, my own teacher, the Fifth Patriarch, personally transmitted the robe and Dharma to him — how could this have been for nothing? I only regret that I cannot go to him myself and draw near to him, and so idly receive the state's own support instead. All of you should not remain fixed here — go to Caoxi, and settle this question for yourselves." One day he instructed his disciple Zhicheng: "You are intelligent and full of ability — go to Caoxi on my behalf and listen to the teaching there. Whatever you hear, commit it carefully to memory, and come back and tell me." Having received this charge, Zhicheng went to Caoxi, and joined the assembly there to seek instruction, without saying where he had come from. At that time the Patriarch told the assembly, "There is now a person here who has come to steal the Dharma, hidden within this very gathering." Zhicheng at once came forward and bowed, and related the whole matter in full. The Master said, "You have come from Yuquan — you must be a spy." He answered, "No, I am not." The Master said, "How could you not be?" He answered, "Before I spoke, I was; now that I have spoken, I am not." The Master said, "How does your own teacher instruct his assembly?" He answered, "He constantly teaches everyone to fix the mind and contemplate stillness, sitting continuously without ever lying down." The Master said, "To fix the mind and contemplate stillness is a sickness, not chan. To sit continuously, confining the body — what benefit is there in this, by any reasoning? Hear my verse: "While alive, one sits without lying down; when dead, one lies without sitting up — a set of stinking bones either way, so why set up this as your practice?" Zhicheng bowed again and said, "Your disciple studied the Way at the great teacher Shenxiu's place for nine years, without ever reaching genuine understanding. Now, hearing the venerable monk's single teaching, I have reached my own fundamental mind at once. Your disciple regards the matter of birth and death as of great importance — I beg the venerable monk's great compassion, and ask you to instruct me further." The Master said, "I have heard that your teacher instructs students in the Dharma of precept, samādhi, and wisdom — tell me, how does your teacher describe the character of this practice? Speak, and let me hear it." Zhicheng said, "The great teacher Shenxiu says: 'To do no evil at all is called precept; to uphold every good deed is called wisdom; to purify one's own intention is called samādhi.' This is how he describes it — I do not know by what teaching the venerable monk instructs others." The Master said, "If I told you I had some fixed Dharma to give to another person, this would be deceiving you. I simply loosen whatever binds a person according to their particular situation, giving it the provisional name of samādhi. What your teacher describes as precept, samādhi, and wisdom is indeed inconceivable — but what I see as precept, samādhi, and wisdom is something different again." Zhicheng said, "Precept, samādhi, and wisdom should surely be only one single thing — how could there be any difference?" The Master said, "Your teacher's precept, samādhi, and wisdom are meant to lead those of the Great Vehicle; my own precept, samādhi, and wisdom are meant to lead those of the very highest vehicle. Understanding differs, and the seeing of it comes slowly or swiftly. Listen to what I say, and judge for yourself whether it is the same as his teaching. What I teach never departs from self-nature; to teach the Dharma apart from its own substance is what is called teaching through marks alone, and self-nature remains constantly deluded under such teaching. You must know that the ten thousand things all arise as the function of self-nature itself — this alone is the true Dharma of precept, samādhi, and wisdom. Hear my verse: "Where the ground of mind holds no wrong, this is the precept of self-nature; where the ground of mind holds no folly, this is the wisdom of self-nature; where the ground of mind holds no confusion, this is the samādhi of self-nature. Neither increasing nor decreasing, it is of itself like a diamond; the body may come and the body may go, but this is fundamentally what samādhi already is." Hearing this verse, Zhicheng, filled with shame, offered a verse of his own in apology: "This illusory body of the five aggregates — what final end could any illusion have? Turning back toward true suchness itself, even this very Dharma remains unclean." The Master approved of this, and said further to Zhicheng, "Your teacher's precept, samādhi, and wisdom are meant to encourage those of lesser faculties and wisdom; my own precept, samādhi, and wisdom are meant to encourage those of great faculties and wisdom. If one awakens to self-nature, one does not even set up bodhi or nirvāṇa, and does not even set up liberation or its knowledge and vision. There is not a single thing to be attained, and only in this way can the ten thousand things be established at all. If you understand this meaning, it may equally be called the buddha-body, or bodhi and nirvāṇa, or the knowledge and vision of liberation. For one who has seen their own nature, to set these up is fine, and not to set them up is equally fine; coming and going freely, without obstruction or hindrance, responding to whatever arises, answering whatever is asked, manifesting transformation-bodies everywhere without ever departing from self-nature — this is to attain the free and unobstructed spiritual power of playful samādhi. This alone is called seeing one's own nature." Zhicheng asked further, "What is meant by 'not setting up'?" The Master said, "Self-nature holds no wrong, no folly, and no confusion; contemplating with the light of prajñā thought after thought, constantly free of every mark of the Dharma, at ease and free in every direction, fully attained in every respect — what would there be left to set up? Self-nature awakens for itself, sudden awakening and sudden practice together, without any gradual stages at all — this is why nothing whatsoever is set up. If every phenomenon is already still and extinguished, what sequence of stages could there possibly be?" Zhicheng bowed in homage, and asked to remain in the Master's service, attending him morning and night without ever growing slack. (Zhicheng was a man of Taihe in Jizhou.)

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads a quiet irony running under this entire episode: Shenxiu's community sends a spy to overhear the southern teaching in secret, apparently unaware that a teacher who has genuinely realized self-nature has no need of ordinary information-gathering to know who has entered the room. Huineng names Zhicheng before Zhicheng has said a word, which Hanshan takes as demonstrating exactly the difference the rest of the chapter goes on to argue in words: years of disciplined effort at Shenxiu's community had not produced what Huineng shows here without any effort visible at all.

On the two competing accounts of precept, samādhi, and wisdom, Hanshan draws the distinction precisely: Shenxiu's formula — do no evil, uphold every good, purify one's intention — describes three separate acts of discipline performed upon the mind from outside, rules a person follows. Huineng's verse relocates all three inside self-nature's own condition: "where the ground of mind holds no wrong, this is the precept of self-nature" describes what an unobstructed mind already is, not an instruction for what a mind should do. Zhicheng's own confessional verse in response — "even this very Dharma remains unclean" — shows he has understood the deeper trap Hanshan flags throughout this sūtra: that even "turning back toward true suchness" can itself become one more thing to cling to, a subtler version of the very attachment it claims to escape.

2. The Assassin's Blade, and True Impermanence

Chinese Source

僧志徹，江西人，本姓張，名行昌，少任俠。自南北分化，二宗主雖亡彼我，而徒侶競起愛憎。時北宗門人，自立秀師為第六祖，而忌祖師傳衣為天下聞，乃囑行昌來刺師。師心通，預知其事，即置金十兩於座間。時夜暮，行昌入祖室，將欲加害。師舒頸就之，行昌揮刃者三，悉無所損。師曰：「正劍不邪，邪劍不正。只負汝金，不負汝命。」行昌驚仆，久而方蘇，求哀悔過，即願出家。師遂與金，言：「汝且去，恐徒眾翻害於汝。汝可他日易形而來，吾當攝受。」行昌稟旨宵遁。後投僧出家，具戒精進。一日，憶師之言，遠來禮覲。師曰：「吾久念汝，汝來何晚？」曰：「昨蒙和尚捨罪，今雖出家苦行，終難報德，其惟傳法度生乎？弟子常覽《涅槃經》，未曉常無常義。乞和尚慈悲，略為解說。」師曰：「無常者，即佛性也。有常者，即一切善惡諸法分別心也。」曰：「和尚所說，大違經文。」師曰：「吾傳佛心印，安敢違於佛經？」曰：「經說佛性是常；和尚却言無常。善惡之法乃至菩提心，皆是無常；和尚却言是常。此即相違，令學人轉加疑惑。」師曰：「《涅槃經》吾昔聽尼無盡藏讀誦一遍，便為講說，無一字一義不合經文。乃至為汝，終無二說。」曰：「學人識量淺昧，願和尚委曲開示。」師曰：「汝知否？佛性若常，更說什麼善惡諸法，乃至窮劫無有一人發菩提心者；故吾說無常，正是佛說真常之道也。又，一切諸法若無常者，即物物皆有自性，容受生死，而真常性有不遍之處。故吾說常者，正是佛說真無常義。佛比為凡夫、外道執於邪常，諸二乘人於常計無常，共成八倒，故於《涅槃》了義教中，破彼偏見，而顯說真常、真樂、真我、真淨。汝今依言背義，以斷滅無常及確定死常，而錯解佛之圓妙最後微言。縱覽千遍，有何所益？」行昌忽然大悟，說偈曰： 「因守無常心，　佛說有常性， 不知方便者，　猶春池拾礫。 我今不施功，　佛性而現前， 非師相授與，　我亦無所得。」 師曰：「汝今徹也，宜名志徹。」徹禮謝而退。

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk Zhiche was a man of Jiangxi, whose lay surname was Zhang and given name Xingchang; in his youth he had lived as a wandering swordsman. Although the leaders of the northern and southern schools no longer held any rivalry between themselves, their followers had come to compete in love and hatred all the same. At that time Shenxiu's own disciples had set themselves up, claiming Shenxiu as the Sixth Patriarch, and, resenting that the Patriarch's own transmission of the robe was becoming known throughout the world, charged Xingchang with the task of assassinating him. The Master, whose mind had penetrating insight, knew of this beforehand, and placed ten taels of gold on his own seat in advance. At nightfall Xingchang entered the Patriarch's room, intending to do him harm. The Master stretched out his own neck to receive the blow. Xingchang swung his blade three times, yet caused no injury at all. The Master said, "A true sword is never crooked; a crooked sword is never true. I owe you only this gold — I do not owe you my life." Xingchang, startled, fell to the ground, and after some time revived; begging in tears for forgiveness, he at once wished to go forth from home. The Master then gave him the gold, saying, "You should leave now — I fear the others might turn on you and do you harm instead. Come to me again another day, in a different form, and I will receive you then." Xingchang, following these instructions, fled that very night. He later took ordination under another monk, and practiced the precepts with diligence. One day, remembering the Master's words, he came from far away to pay his respects. The Master said, "I have long thought of you — why have you come so late?" He said, "Once, the venerable monk graciously forgave my offense; now, though I have gone forth from home and practice with hardship, I can hardly repay such kindness — is it not only by transmitting the Dharma and delivering beings that I ever could? Your disciple has often read the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, but has never understood the meaning of permanence and impermanence. I beg the venerable monk's compassion to explain this briefly for me." The Master said, "Impermanence is itself buddha-nature; permanence is itself the mind that distinguishes every good and evil phenomenon." He said, "What the venerable monk has just said greatly contradicts the sūtra text." The Master said, "I transmit the very seal of the Buddha's own mind — how would I dare contradict the Buddhist scriptures?" He said, "The sūtra says buddha-nature is permanent, yet the venerable monk says it is impermanent; it says every good and evil phenomenon, down to the very mind of bodhi, is impermanent, yet the venerable monk says these are permanent. This is a direct contradiction, and it leaves this student all the more confused." The Master said, "I once heard the nun Wujinzang recite the Nirvāṇa Sūtra straight through a single time, and from that alone I was able to explain it — there was not a single word or a single meaning that failed to accord with the sūtra's own text. Even now, explaining it for you, I say nothing different at all." He said, "This student's own understanding is shallow and unclear — I beg the venerable monk to explain this to me patiently, in full." The Master said, "Do you understand? If buddha-nature were simply permanent, what further need would there be to speak of any good or evil phenomenon at all — and in that case, not a single person, throughout the whole of time, could ever give rise to the mind of bodhi. This is exactly why I say it is impermanent — this is itself the Buddha's own teaching of true permanence. And again, if all things whatsoever were simply impermanent, then every single thing would possess its own fixed nature, capable of undergoing birth and death on its own, and the truly permanent nature would then fail to pervade everything completely. This is exactly why I say it is permanent — this is itself the Buddha's own teaching of true impermanence. The Buddha, seeing that ordinary people and the followers of outside teachings cling to a mistaken notion of permanence, and that followers of the two lesser vehicles mistake what is permanent for impermanent, together forming eight kinds of inversion, therefore, within the fully revealed teaching of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, broke through these one-sided views, and openly proclaimed true permanence, true bliss, a true self, and true purity. You, today, are following the words while turning your back on their meaning, taking impermanence to mean simple annihilation and permanence to mean a fixed, unchanging deadness, and thereby misreading the Buddha's own perfect and subtle final teaching. Even if you read it a thousand times over, what benefit could that possibly bring you?" At this Xingchang was suddenly and greatly awakened, and spoke a verse: "Because I clung to a mind of impermanence, the Buddha spoke of a nature that is permanent; not understanding this as a skillful means, I was like someone picking up pebbles from a spring pond, thinking them pearls. Now, without any effort of my own at all, buddha-nature has appeared right before me — not something handed to me by any teacher, nor anything I myself have gained." The Master said, "You have now truly penetrated it — you should be called Zhiche, 'one who has penetrated.'" Zhiche bowed in gratitude and withdrew.

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads Huineng's equanimity — stretching out his own neck to the blade rather than resisting — together with his verdict afterward — "I owe you only this gold, not my life" — as treating the whole episode as the resolution of an old debt rather than an act of persecution to be resented. Nothing in the text suggests fear or anger on Huineng's part at any point in the exchange; what governs his response instead is exact accounting, and once the debt is settled, so is the matter, cleanly and without residue.

The doctrinal exchange that follows is, on Hanshan's reading, not a genuine contradiction of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra but a corrective aimed at two opposite misreadings of it at once, named in the sūtra's own doctrine of the "eight inversions": ordinary people and outside teachers who cling to a naive, static permanence, and followers of the lesser vehicles who mistake what is genuinely permanent for something impermanent. Huineng's answer is deliberately calibrated to Xingchang's own error rather than stated as a fixed doctrine — call buddha-nature simply "permanent" and a listener concludes there is nothing left to cultivate; call it simply "impermanent" and a listener concludes it can be lost. Both corrections point at the same reality from different directions, which is why Huineng can say without contradiction that he never departs from the sūtra's own text even while reversing its plainest reading. The gold Xingchang once tried to kill for and the pearls he mistakes pebbles for in his own closing verse are, on Hanshan's account, the same image doing the same work twice: what looked valuable from outside was never where the real treasure actually was.

3. The Novice Shenhui, and the Nameless Thing

Chinese Source

有一童子，名神會，襄陽高氏子。年十三，自玉泉來參禮。師曰：「知識遠來艱辛，還將得本來否？若有本則合識主。試說看。」會曰：「以無住為本，見即是主。」師曰：「這沙彌爭合取次語？」會乃問曰：「和尚坐禪，還見不見？」師以柱杖打三下，云：「吾打汝痛不痛？」對曰：「亦痛亦不痛。」師曰：「吾亦見亦不見。」神會問：「如何是亦見亦不見？」師云：「吾之所見，常見自心過愆，不見他人是非好惡，是以亦見亦不見。汝言：『亦痛亦不痛。』如何？汝若不痛，同其木石；若痛，則同凡夫，即起恚恨。汝向前見、不見是二邊，痛、不痛是生滅。汝自性且不見，敢爾弄人！」神會禮拜悔謝。師又曰：「汝若心迷不見，問善知識覓路。汝若心悟，即自見性依法修行。汝自迷不見自心，却來問吾見與不見。吾見自知，豈代汝迷？汝若自見，亦不代吾迷。何不自知自見，乃問吾見與不見？」神會再禮百餘拜，求謝過愆。服勤給侍，不離左右。 一日，師告眾曰：「吾有一物，無頭無尾，無名無字，無背無面。諸人還識否？」神會出曰：「是諸佛之本源，神會之佛性。」師曰：「向汝道：『無名無字』，汝便喚作本源佛性。汝向去有把茆蓋頭，也只成箇知解宗徒。」祖師滅後，會入京洛，大弘曹溪頓教，著《顯宗記》，盛行于世(是為荷澤禪師)。 師見諸宗難問咸起惡心，多集座下愍而謂曰：「學道之人，一切善念惡念應當盡除。無名可名，名於自性，無二之性，是名實性。於實性上建立一切教門，言下便須自見。」諸人聞說，總皆作禮，請事為師。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a young novice named Shenhui, a son of the Gao family of Xiangyang. At the age of thirteen he came from Yuquan Temple to pay his respects. The Master said, "Good friend, you have come a hard distance from far away — have you managed to bring the root along with you? If you have this root, then you ought to recognize its master. Try telling me." Shenhui said, "No-dwelling is the root; seeing itself is the master." The Master said, "What kind of glib novice talks like that, so carelessly?" Shenhui then asked, "When the venerable monk sits in meditation, do you see, or do you not see?" The Master struck him three times with his staff and said, "When I strike you, does it hurt, or does it not hurt?" He answered, "It both hurts and does not hurt." The Master said, "I too both see and do not see." Shenhui asked, "What does it mean to both see and not see?" The Master said, "What I see is constantly my own mind's own faults; what I do not see is the rights and wrongs, the good and evil, of other people — this is why I both see and do not see. Now, as for what you said, that it both hurts and does not hurt — how is that? If you did not feel any hurt at all, you would be no different from a piece of wood or stone; but if you did feel the hurt, then you are no different from an ordinary person, giving rise to anger and resentment at once. What you said before — seeing and not seeing — are two extremes; hurting and not hurting are simply arising and ceasing. You have not even seen your own self-nature yet, and you dare to come here and toy with me?" Shenhui bowed in shame and apology. The Master said further, "If your mind is deluded and you do not see your own nature, you should go and ask a good friend to show you the way; but if your mind is awakened, you have already seen your own nature for yourself, and should simply practice according to the Dharma. You yourself are deluded, unable to see your own mind, and yet you come here asking me whether I see or do not see. My own seeing, I know for myself — how could that take the place of your own delusion? And if you were to see for yourself, this likewise could not take the place of my own delusion. Why do you not know and see for yourself, instead of asking me whether I see or not?" Shenhui bowed again, more than a hundred times, begging forgiveness for his fault, and from then on served the Master diligently, never leaving his side. One day the Master told the assembly, "I have a certain thing, without a head and without a tail, without a name and without any word for it, without a back and without a face — does anyone among you recognize it?" Shenhui came forward and said, "It is the very source of every buddha, and it is Shenhui's own buddha-nature." The Master said, "I told you it had no name and no word for it at all, and yet you go and call it 'the source of buddha-nature.' Even if you go on from here to build yourself a thatched hut to sit under, you will only ever become a follower who deals in intellectual understanding." After the Patriarch's death, Shenhui went to the capital, where he greatly spread the sudden teaching of Caoxi, and wrote the Record of Clarifying the Doctrine, which became widely known throughout the world. He is known as the Chan Master of Heze. Seeing that every school raised difficult questions out of ill will, the Master gathered many of them beneath his seat and, with compassion, said to them: "For a person studying the Way, every thought of good and every thought of evil alike should be entirely removed. There is no name that can truly name it; it is named only from within self-nature itself. This nature without duality is called the real nature. Upon this real nature, every teaching whatsoever is to be established — the moment these words are spoken, you must see it for yourself." Hearing this, everyone present bowed in homage, and asked to serve him as their teacher.

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads Shenhui's opening answer — "no-dwelling is the root, seeing itself is the master" — as technically fluent and doctrinally almost correct, using exactly the vocabulary this sūtra has spent seven chapters building, and precisely for that reason more dangerous than an obviously wrong answer would be. Huineng's blunt dismissal, "what kind of glib novice talks like that," names the gap the words themselves cannot reveal: a thirteen-year-old repeating the correct formula has not thereby seen his own nature, and fluent doctrine recited without realization is, on this account, worse than useless — it can pass for the real thing.

The staff-strike is Hanshan's favorite kind of teaching moment in this sūtra: rather than answering Shenhui's clever question about seeing and not-seeing with a further abstraction, Huineng creates an actual physical event and asks Shenhui to report his own immediate experience of it. "It both hurts and does not hurt" turns out to be the same evasive, ungrounded cleverness as his first answer, and Huineng's correction — pain is either felt or it isn't, seeing and not-seeing are extremes to be dissolved, not paired as a clever formula — exposes that Shenhui has learned to produce the shape of a Chan answer without the substance behind it. His later confidence, naming the nameless thing as "Shenhui's own buddha-nature," draws the sūtra's single sharpest rebuke — "you will only ever become a follower who deals in intellectual understanding" — a phrase Hanshan notes became, in the centuries after this sūtra, the standard criticism leveled against Shenhui's entire later career, brilliant and influential as it was, by those who felt his teaching remained more conceptual than realized.
