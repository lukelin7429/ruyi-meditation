Encounters: The Lineage Masters

機緣品第七（下）

The second half of this chapter's gallery of encounters turns from questions about scripture to questions about practice itself — a monk who has replaced one delusion with a subtler one, another troubled by what nirvāṇa's "bliss" could possibly mean, and two disciples, Xingsi and Huairang, who would each go on to found a line from which every later school of Chan descends. It closes with Yongjia Xuanjue's celebrated one-night exchange, a practitioner who spent twenty years perfecting the wrong kind of stillness, and a sculptor who could shape a lifelike likeness of Huineng's face but not yet see the nature underneath it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T2008 · Zongbao's redaction, Yuan dynasty (1291 CE) Commentary guide: Hanshan Deqing's 六祖法寶壇經解義 (17th c.)
This part covers
Zhichang, whose teacher's account of seeing-and-knowing turns out to be a subtler delusion rather than its cure; Zhidao's confusion over what "bliss" could mean if nirvāṇa is the cessation of all arising; Xingsi and Huairang, the two disciples from whom every later House of Chan traces its descent; Yongjia Xuanjue's rapid-fire exchange, remembered as "the one-night enlightenment"; Zhihuang, whose twenty years of motionless sitting turn out to have been the wrong kind of samādhi entirely; and the sculptor Fangbian, who can shape a lifelike image but not yet see buddha-nature.
Why it matters
Two of the encounters in this chapter — Xingsi and Huairang — are lineage-founding moments: nearly every later school of Chan, the Five Houses among them, traces its descent back through one or the other of these two brief exchanges.

1. Zhichang: Beyond Seeing and Knowing

Chinese Source

僧智常，信州貴溪人，髫年出家，志求見性。一日參禮，師問曰：「汝從何來？欲求何事？」曰：「學人近往洪州白峯山禮大通和尚，蒙示見性成佛之義。未決狐疑，遠來投禮，伏望和尚慈悲指示。」師曰：「彼有何言句？汝試舉看。」曰：「智常到彼，凡經三月，未蒙示誨。為法切故，一夕獨入丈室，請問：『如何是某甲本心本性？』大通乃曰：『汝見虛空否？』對曰：『見。』彼曰：『汝見虛空有相貌否？』對曰：『虛空無形，有何相貌？』彼曰：『汝之本性，猶如虛空，了無一物可見，是名正見；無一物可知，是名真知。無有青黃長短，但見本源清淨，覺體圓明，即名見性成佛，亦名如來知見。』學人雖聞此說，猶未決了，乞和尚開示。」師曰：「彼師所說，猶存見知，故令汝未了。吾今示汝一偈： 「不見一法存無見，　大似浮雲遮日面， 不知一法守空知，　還如太虛生閃電。 此之知見瞥然興，　錯認何曾解方便， 汝當一念自知非，　自己靈光常顯現。」 常聞偈已，心意豁然。乃述偈曰： 「無端起知見，　著相求菩提， 情存一念悟，　寧越昔時迷。 自性覺源體，　隨照枉遷流， 不入祖師室，　茫然趣兩頭。」 智常一日問師曰：「佛說三乘法，又言最上乘。弟子未解，願為教授。」師曰：「汝觀自本心，莫著外法相。法無四乘，人心自有等差。見聞轉誦是小乘；悟法解義是中乘；依法修行是大乘；萬法盡通，萬法俱備，一切不染，離諸法相，一無所得，名最上乘。乘是行義，不在口爭。汝須自修，莫問吾也。一切時中，自性自如。」常禮謝執侍，終師之世。

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk Zhichang, a man of Guixi in Xinzhou, went forth from home while still a child, and set his will on seeing his own nature. One day he came to pay his respects, and the Master asked him, "Where have you come from, and what are you seeking?" He said, "This student recently went to Mount Baifeng in Hongzhou to pay respects to the venerable monk Datong, and received his teaching on the meaning of seeing one's nature and becoming a buddha. But I have not yet resolved my remaining doubt, and have come from far away to pay my respects here, hoping the venerable monk will, out of compassion, instruct me." The Master said, "What did he say to you? Try telling me." He said, "When I arrived there, a full three months passed without my receiving any instruction. Because I was so earnest about the Dharma, one evening I went alone into his room and asked, 'What are my own fundamental mind and fundamental nature?' Datong said, 'Do you see empty space?' I answered, 'I see it.' He said, 'Do you see empty space as having any form or appearance?' I answered, 'Empty space is without shape — what form or appearance could it have?' He said, 'Your own fundamental nature is exactly like empty space: to see that there is not a single thing to be seen is called right seeing; to know that there is not a single thing to be known is called true knowing. It has no blue or yellow, no long or short — simply see that its original source is pure, its very substance of awareness perfectly bright, and this is what is called seeing one's own nature and becoming a buddha, also called the knowledge and vision of the Tathāgata.' Though this student heard this teaching, I still have not resolved it fully — I beg the venerable monk to instruct me." The Master said, "What that teacher said still retains seeing and knowing as something held onto, and this is exactly why it has left you unresolved. I will now show you a verse: "Not seeing a single thing, yet holding onto the notion of 'no seeing' — this is very much like a drifting cloud covering the face of the sun. Not knowing a single thing, yet guarding an empty 'knowing' — this is just like a flash of lightning bursting from the open sky. The moment this kind of seeing and knowing suddenly arises, how could it ever count as understanding skillful means? You should, in a single thought, recognize this for yourself as mistaken, and your own spiritual light will constantly appear of itself." Hearing this verse, Zhichang's mind opened wide, and he composed a verse of his own: "For no reason at all, I gave rise to seeing and knowing, becoming attached to marks in my search for bodhi. Holding onto the notion of a single thought of awakening, how could this ever surpass my former delusion? The very source and substance of self-nature's awareness, following each act of illumination, wrongly drifts and shifts about. Without entering the Patriarch's own room, I would have wandered on aimlessly, caught between these two extremes." One day Zhichang asked the Master, "The Buddha taught the Dharma of three vehicles, and also spoke of a highest vehicle — this disciple does not understand this, and I beg you to teach me." The Master said, "Look at your own fundamental mind, and do not become attached to any outward mark of the Dharma. The Dharma itself has no four vehicles — it is people's own minds that differ in rank. To see, hear, and merely recite is the small vehicle; to awaken to the teaching and understand its meaning is the middle vehicle; to practice in accordance with the Dharma is the great vehicle; and to penetrate the ten thousand things entirely, complete in the ten thousand things, stained by none of them, free of every mark of the Dharma, with not a single thing attained — this is called the highest vehicle. 'Vehicle' carries the meaning of practice, not of argument. You must cultivate this for yourself — do not ask me about it further. At every moment, let self-nature simply be as it is." Zhichang bowed in gratitude, and remained in the Master's service for the rest of the Master's life.

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads Datong's teaching as sounding almost identical to true doctrine while failing at one specific point: "no seeing" and "no knowing" have themselves been turned into something held onto, an object of attainment rather than the release of every object at once. The image of the cloud covering the sun captures exactly this failure — Datong has replaced one obstruction (ordinary seeing) with another, subtler obstruction (a fixed notion of "no seeing"), and the sun's light is covered either way. This is the same warning against mistaking blankness for true emptiness that runs throughout this sūtra, here shown working even on a teacher who is using entirely correct-sounding vocabulary.

Hanshan notes that Zhichang's own second verse — describing his prior state as "caught between these two extremes" — shows he has understood the deeper problem: not that Datong's specific words were wrong, but that any teaching still requiring a fixed position, even the position of having "no position," remains one more mark to be freed from. This is why Huineng's answer to Zhichang's later question about the vehicles refuses to define "highest vehicle" as a fourth category alongside the other three — the highest vehicle, on this account, is not a further rung on the ladder but the freedom from needing the ladder to have rungs at all.

2. Zhidao: The True Bliss of Nirvāṇa

Chinese Source

僧志道，廣州南海人也。請益曰：「學人自出家，覽《涅槃經》十載有餘，未明大意，願和尚垂誨。」師曰：「汝何處未明？」曰：「諸行無常，是生滅法；生滅滅已，寂滅為樂。於此疑惑。」師曰：「汝作麼生疑？」曰：「一切眾生皆有二身，謂色身、法身也。色身無常，有生有滅；法身有常，無知無覺。經云：『生滅滅已，寂滅為樂』者，不審何身寂滅？何身受樂？若色身者，色身滅時，四大分散，全然是苦，苦不可言樂。若法身寂滅，即同草木瓦石，誰當受樂？又法性是生滅之體，五蘊是生滅之用，一體五用，生滅是常。生則從體起用，滅則攝用歸體。若聽更生，即有情之類，不斷不滅；若不聽更生，則永歸寂滅，同於無情之物。如是，則一切諸法被涅槃之所禁伏，尚不得生，何樂之有？」師曰：「汝是釋子，何習外道斷常邪見，而議最上乘法？據汝所說，即色身外別有法身，離生滅求於寂滅。又推涅槃常樂，言有身受用。斯乃執悋生死，耽著世樂。汝今當知，佛為一切迷人，認五蘊和合為自體相，分別一切法為外塵相，好生惡死，念念遷流，不知夢幻虛假，枉受輪迴。以常樂涅槃翻為苦相，終日馳求。佛愍此故，乃示涅槃真樂。剎那無有生相，剎那無有滅相，更無生滅可滅，是則寂滅現前。當現前時，亦無現前之量，乃謂常樂。此樂無有受者，亦無不受者，豈有一體五用之名？何況更言涅槃禁伏諸法，令永不生。斯乃謗佛毀法。聽吾偈曰： 「無上大涅槃，　圓明常寂照， 凡愚謂之死，　外道執為斷， 諸求二乘人，　目以為無作， 盡屬情所計，　六十二見本。 妄立虛假名，　何為真實義， 惟有過量人，　通達無取捨。 以知五蘊法，　及以蘊中我， 外現眾色象，　一一音聲相， 平等如夢幻，　不起凡聖見， 不作涅槃解，　二邊三際斷。 常應諸根用，　而不起用想， 分別一切法，　不起分別想。 劫火燒海底，　風鼓山相擊， 真常寂滅樂，　涅槃相如是。 吾今彊言說，　令汝捨邪見， 汝勿隨言解，　許汝知少分。」 志道聞偈大悟，踊躍作禮而退。

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk Zhidao, a man of Nanhai in Guangzhou, came asking for instruction: "Since going forth from home, this student has studied the Nirvāṇa Sūtra for over ten years, without ever understanding its central meaning — I beg the venerable monk to instruct me." The Master said, "Where is it that you do not understand?" He said, "'All conditioned things are impermanent — this is the Dharma of arising and ceasing; when arising and ceasing have themselves ceased, this stillness of cessation is bliss.' It is this passage that troubles me with doubt." The Master said, "How does your doubt arise?" He said, "Every sentient being is said to possess two bodies, called the physical body and the Dharma-body. The physical body is impermanent, having both arising and ceasing; the Dharma-body is permanent, without any knowing or awareness. Now the sūtra says, 'when arising and ceasing have themselves ceased, this stillness of cessation is bliss' — but I do not understand which body it is that reaches this cessation, and which body it is that experiences this bliss. If it is the physical body, then when the physical body ceases, the four elements scatter apart, and this is entirely suffering — suffering cannot be called bliss. If it is the Dharma-body that reaches cessation, then it becomes no different from grass, trees, tiles, or stones — who, then, would be left to experience any bliss at all? Moreover, the Dharma-nature is the substance underlying arising and ceasing, while the five aggregates are its function; one substance with five functions, and arising and ceasing are constant to it. Arising is the substance giving rise to function; ceasing is the function being gathered back into the substance. If further arising is permitted, then this belongs to the class of sentient beings, neither cut off nor extinguished; but if further arising is not permitted, then it returns forever to cessation, becoming the same as an insentient thing. If this is so, then all things whatsoever are held bound and suppressed by nirvāṇa itself, unable even to arise at all — what bliss could there possibly be in that?" The Master said, "You are a disciple of Śākyamuni — why do you study the wrong views of permanence and annihilation from outside teachings, and use them to discuss the highest vehicle? By what you have said, there exists, apart from the physical body, some separate Dharma-body, seeking a cessation apart from arising and ceasing; and you further suppose that the constant bliss of nirvāṇa implies some body that experiences and enjoys it. This is nothing but clinging tightly to birth and death, indulging in worldly pleasure. You should now understand: the Buddha, for the sake of all deluded people, who mistake the coming-together of the five aggregates for the mark of their own body, and distinguish every phenomenon as an external, worldly mark, who love life and hate death, drifting on in thought after thought, not knowing that all of this is as insubstantial as a dream or an illusion, and thereby suffer needlessly through endless transmigration — the Buddha, seeing them mistake the constant bliss of nirvāṇa for a mark of suffering, and run about seeking release from it all day long, took pity on this, and so revealed the true bliss of nirvāṇa. In it, there is not, even for an instant, any mark of arising; there is not, even for an instant, any mark of ceasing; there is no further arising or ceasing left even to be extinguished — this is the very presence of the stillness of cessation itself. And at the moment this presence appears, there is not even any measure of 'presence' to speak of — this alone is called constant bliss. This bliss has no one who experiences it, and yet no one who fails to experience it either — how, then, could there be any name for 'one substance, five functions'? How much less could one say that nirvāṇa holds and suppresses every phenomenon, causing it never to arise again? This is nothing but slandering the Buddha and vilifying the Dharma. Hear my verse: "The unsurpassed great nirvāṇa, perfectly bright, constantly still, constantly illuminating — the foolish and ordinary call it death; those of outside teachings hold it to be annihilation. Those who seek the two lesser vehicles regard it as something wholly inactive — all of this belongs only to calculation by feeling, the very root of the sixty-two views. Falsely setting up empty, provisional names — how could these ever be the real, true meaning? Only a person who has gone beyond ordinary measure penetrates it fully, neither grasping nor rejecting anything at all. Knowing the Dharma of the five aggregates, and the notion of a self within those aggregates, and every outwardly appearing form of color, and every single mark of sound — all of it is equal, like a dream or an illusion; giving rise to no distinction between the ordinary and the sage, forming no interpretation of nirvāṇa at all, the two extremes and the three periods of time are alike cut off. Constantly responding through the function of every sense-faculty, and yet giving rise to no notion of 'function'; discerning every single phenomenon, and yet giving rise to no notion of 'discernment.' When the fire at the end of the kalpa burns the very floor of the ocean, and the wind stirs the mountains until they crash against one another — the true, constant bliss of the stillness of cessation is exactly like this, and no different. I now force myself to put this into words, only to cause you to abandon your wrong view; do not follow my words and take them as a fixed understanding, and I will allow that you have come to know a small portion of this truth." Zhidao, hearing this verse, was greatly awakened, and leapt for joy, bowing in homage before withdrawing.

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads Zhidao's entire confusion as generated by a single unexamined assumption: that bliss requires someone left over to experience it. Having correctly ruled out both the physical body (destroyed at death) and a reified Dharma-body (insentient, like a stone) as candidates for who feels this bliss, Zhidao concludes the whole doctrine must be incoherent — nirvāṇa's bliss must belong to someone, and no one is left to hold it. Huineng's answer removes the premise rather than supplying a third candidate: "this bliss has no one who experiences it, and yet no one who fails to experience it either." The framework of a subject possessing an experience is itself, on this reading, one more mark that dissolves along with arising and ceasing.

Zhidao's "one substance, five functions" model — treating Dharma-nature as a fixed substance underlying the five aggregates as its output — is, in Hanshan's reading, the same error already named elsewhere in this sūtra as taking self-nature as a thing that produces effects rather than as the very activity of seeing, hearing, and thinking itself. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra debate over icchantikas and buddha-nature, cited already in this sūtra's first chapter at the wind-and-flag temple, returns here from a different angle: both passages insist that buddha-nature admits no exceptions and no fixed categories, whether applied to the worst of offenders or to the metaphysics of bliss itself.

3. Xingsi and Huairang: Two Streams from One Source

Chinese Source

行思禪師，生吉州安城劉氏。聞曹溪法席盛化，徑來參禮，遂問曰：「當何所務，即不落階級？」師曰：「汝曾作什麼來？」曰：「聖諦亦不為。」師曰：「落何階級？」曰：「聖諦尚不為，何階級之有？」師深器之，令思首眾。一日，師謂曰：「汝當分化一方，無令斷絕。」思既得法，遂回吉州青原山，弘法紹化(諡弘濟禪師)。 懷讓禪師，金州杜氏子也。初謁嵩山安國師，安發之曹溪參扣。讓至禮拜，師曰：「甚處來？」曰：「嵩山。」師曰：「什麼物？恁麼來？」曰：「說似一物即不中。」師曰：「還可修證否？」曰：「修證即不無，污染即不得。」師曰：「只此不污染，諸佛之所護念。汝既如是，吾亦如是。西天般若多羅讖，汝足下出一馬駒，踏殺天下人。應在汝心，不須速說(一本無西天以下二十七字)。」讓豁然契會，遂執侍左右一十五載，日臻玄奧。後往南嶽，大闡禪宗(勅諡大慧禪師)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Chan Master Xingsi was born into the Liu family of Ancheng in Jizhou. Hearing how greatly the Dharma assembly at Caoxi was flourishing, he came directly to pay his respects, and asked, "In what should one apply oneself, so as not to fall into any graduated rank at all?" The Master said, "What have you done up until now?" He said, "I have not even undertaken the noble truths." The Master said, "Then what rank could you possibly have fallen into?" He said, "If I have not even undertaken the noble truths, what rank could there be to speak of at all?" The Master, recognizing his depth, held him in great regard, and made Xingsi the head of the assembly. One day the Master said to him, "You should go and transform one region of the world — do not let this teaching be cut off." Having received the Dharma, Xingsi returned to Mount Qingyuan in Jizhou, where he spread the teaching and carried on its transmission. (He was posthumously honored with the title Chan Master Hongji.) Chan Master Huairang was a son of the Du family of Jinzhou. He first went to study under National Teacher Hui'an of Mount Song, who then sent him on to Caoxi to seek instruction. When Huairang arrived and bowed in homage, the Master asked, "Where have you come from?" He said, "From Mount Song." The Master said, "What is this thing, that comes in this way?" He said, "To say it resembles any single thing would already miss the mark." The Master said, "Then can it be cultivated and realized?" He said, "Cultivation and realization are not without their place, but it cannot be stained or defiled." The Master said, "It is exactly this very freedom from staining and defilement that every buddha holds and guards in mind. You are already so, and I am exactly the same. Prajñātāra, in India, once prophesied: 'Beneath your feet a colt will emerge, and it will trample to death everyone under heaven.' This should be held within your own mind — there is no need to speak of it too quickly." At this Huairang understood completely, and remained in the Master's service for fifteen years, growing daily more accomplished in this profound teaching. He later went to Nanyue, where he greatly proclaimed the Chan teaching. (He was posthumously honored by imperial decree with the title Chan Master Dahui.)

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan hears a deliberate echo in Xingsi's answer, "I have not even undertaken the noble truths" — the same refusal to claim any attainment at all that Bodhidharma gave Emperor Wu, generations earlier, when asked what holy truth he stood for: "vast emptiness, nothing holy." Xingsi's question — how to avoid falling into any graduated rank — presupposes a ladder of stages between ordinary person and buddha; his own answer removes the ladder by removing the climber, refusing even the noble truths as something to have "done" at all. Huineng's recognition of his depth follows immediately, without further testing — a contrast, Hanshan notes, with how much longer other visitors in this chapter are made to work before Huineng is satisfied.

Huairang's exchange follows a more familiar pattern of testing: "what is this thing, that comes in this way?" is what Hanshan calls a 機鋒, a barbed question meant to catch a student naming or objectifying their own nature. Huairang's answer — "to say it resembles any single thing would already miss the mark" — passes precisely because it refuses the trap of naming. These two brief exchanges are also, historically, the two roots from which nearly every later school of Chan descends: Xingsi's line through Shitou Xiqian eventually produced the Caodong, Yunmen, and Fayan houses, while Huairang's line through Mazu Daoyi — the "colt" of Prajñātāra's prophecy — produced the Linji and Guiyang houses. Between them, these two brief conversations account for all but a fraction of the entire later Chan tradition.

4. Yongjia Xuanjue: The One-Night Enlightenment

Chinese Source

永嘉玄覺禪師，溫州戴氏子。少習經論，精天台止觀法門。因看《維摩經》發明心地。偶師弟子玄策相訪，與其劇談，出言暗合諸祖。策云：「仁者得法師誰？」曰：「我聽方等經論，各有師承。後於《維摩經》悟佛心宗，未有證明者。」策云：「威音王已前即得，威音王已後，無師自悟，盡是天然外道。」曰：「願仁者為我證據。」策云：「我言輕。曹溪有六祖大師，四方雲集，並是受法者。若去，則與偕行。」覺遂同策來參，繞師三匝，振錫而立。師曰：「夫沙門者，具三千威儀、八萬細行。大德自何方而來，生大我慢？」覺曰：「生死事大，無常迅速。」師曰：「何不體取無生，了無速乎？」曰：「體即無生，了本無速。」師曰：「如是，如是！」玄覺方具威儀禮拜，須臾告辭。師曰：「返太速乎？」曰：「本自非動，豈有速耶？」師曰：「誰知非動？」曰：「仁者自生分別。」師曰：「汝甚得無生之意。」曰：「無生豈有意耶？」師曰：「無意，誰當分別？」曰：「分別亦非意。」師曰：「善哉！少留一宿。」時謂一宿覺。後著《證道歌》，盛行于世(諡曰無相大師，時稱為真覺焉)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Chan Master Yongjia Xuanjue was a son of the Dai family of Wenzhou. In his youth he studied the scriptures and treatises, and became expert in the Tiantai method of śamatha and vipaśyanā. Later, reading the Vimalakīrti Sūtra, he had an awakening of the mind-ground. By chance the Master's disciple Xuance came to visit him, and in their vigorous discussion, Xuanjue's own words happened to agree closely with the teaching of the patriarchs. Xuance said, "From whom, sir, did you receive the Dharma?" He said, "I have listened to the teachings and treatises of the extended vaipulya sūtras, each with its own line of transmission; but later, through the Vimalakīrti Sūtra, I awakened to the buddha-mind school, without anyone yet having confirmed this for me." Xuance said, "Before the Buddha Bhīṣmagarjitasvararāja, this might have been possible; but after that buddha, to awaken by oneself without a teacher is nothing but a natural-born heretic." He said, "I hope you, sir, will confirm this for me." Xuance said, "My own word carries little weight. At Caoxi there is the Sixth Patriarch, to whom people gather from every direction, all of them receiving the Dharma there. If you go, I will go together with you." Xuanjue then came with Xuance to see the Master, circled around him three times, and stood there shaking his monk's staff. The Master said, "A śramaṇa ought to be complete with three thousand rules of deportment and eighty thousand fine points of conduct. Great Worthy, from what direction have you come, giving rise to such great pride?" Xuanjue said, "The matter of birth and death is great, and impermanence comes swiftly." The Master said, "Then why not embody the very fact of no-birth, and see for yourself that there is no swiftness at all?" He said, "To embody it is itself no-birth; to understand it is, from the start, without any swiftness." The Master said, "Just so! Just so!" Only then did Xuanjue arrange himself with proper deportment and bow, and a moment later took his leave. The Master said, "Are you returning so very quickly?" He said, "It was never moving to begin with — how could there be any swiftness in it?" The Master said, "Who is it that knows there is no movement?" He said, "You yourself, sir, are the one giving rise to this distinction." The Master said, "You have grasped the meaning of no-birth very deeply indeed." He said, "How could no-birth have any 'meaning' at all?" The Master said, "If there is no meaning, then who is it that could make any distinction?" He said, "Even to make a distinction is not itself a 'meaning.'" The Master said, "Excellent! Stay the night here a while." At that time people called this "the one-night enlightenment." He later wrote the Song of Enlightenment, which became widely known throughout the world. (He was posthumously given the title Master of No Mark, and was known in his own time as Zhenjue, "the truly awakened.")

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads Xuanjue's opening gesture — circling Huineng three times and standing with his staff, without bowing — as deliberately provocative, treating the Sixth Patriarch as an equal rather than a teacher to be venerated in the conventional way. Huineng's rebuke tests exactly this: is the gesture genuine confidence, or empty pride performing confidence? Xuanjue's answer, about the urgency of birth and death, shows real weight behind the gesture rather than theater, and Huineng's exchange shifts at once from rebuke to genuine testing.

What follows is, on Hanshan's reading, a sequence of increasingly narrow traps, each one closed the instant Xuanjue is asked to step into it. "Are you returning so quickly?" invites him to concede that leaving quickly implies some prior stillness now being disturbed; he refuses the premise entirely — nothing was ever moving. "Who knows there is no movement?" invites him to locate a subject doing the knowing, separate from what is known; he redirects the question back onto Huineng's own act of distinguishing. By the final exchange — "even to make a distinction is not itself a meaning" — Xuanjue has answered every attempt to reintroduce a subject, an object, or a fixed position, which is exactly why Huineng's response is not a further question but simple approval. The episode's name, "the one-night enlightenment," marks how briefly this entire testing took — a single evening's conversation, ending with an invitation to stay only because there was nothing further left to examine.

5. Zhihuang's Twenty Years, and a Question on Huangmei

Chinese Source

禪者智隍，初參五祖，自謂已得正受。菴居長坐，積二十年。師弟子玄策，游方至河朔，聞隍之名，造菴問云：「汝在此作什麼？」隍曰：「入定。」策云：「汝云入定，為有心入耶？無心入耶？若無心入者，一切無情草木瓦石，應合得定；若有心入者，一切有情含識之流，亦應得定。」隍曰：「我正入定時，不見有有無之心。」策云：「不見有有無之心，即是常定。何有出入？若有出入，即非大定。」隍無對，良久，問曰：「師嗣誰耶？」策云：「我師曹溪六祖。」隍云：「六祖以何為禪定？」策云：「我師所說，妙湛圓寂，體用如如。五陰本空，六塵非有，不出不入，不定不亂。禪性無住，離住禪寂；禪性無生，離生禪想。心如虛空，亦無虛空之量。」隍聞是說，徑來謁師。師問云：「仁者何來？」隍具述前緣。師云：「誠如所言。汝但心如虛空，不著空見，應用無礙，動靜無心，凡聖情忘，能所俱泯，性相如如，無不定時也(一本無汝但以下三十五字。止云：師憫其遠來，遂垂開決)。」隍於是大悟，二十年所得心，都無影響。其夜河北士庶，聞空中有聲云：「隍禪師今日得道。」隍後禮辭，復歸河北，開化四眾。一僧問師云：「黃梅意旨，甚麼人得？」師云：「會佛法人得。」僧云：「和尚還得否？」師云：「我不會佛法。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Chan practitioner Zhihuang had first studied under the Fifth Patriarch, and believed he had already attained true absorption; he dwelt alone in a hermitage, sitting continuously for twenty years. The Master's disciple Xuance, traveling about, arrived north of the Yellow River, and hearing of Zhihuang's reputation, went to his hermitage and asked him, "What are you doing here?" Zhihuang said, "Entering samādhi." Xuance said, "When you say you enter samādhi, do you enter it with a mind, or without a mind? If you enter it without a mind, then every insentient thing — grass, trees, tiles, and stones — should equally attain this samādhi; but if you enter it with a mind, then every sentient being possessed of consciousness should equally attain this samādhi as well." Zhihuang said, "At the very moment I am entering samādhi, I do not perceive the existence of any mind of 'existing' or 'not existing.'" Xuance said, "If you do not perceive the existence of any mind of existing or not existing, this is itself constant samādhi — how, then, could there be any entering or leaving it at all? If there is entering and leaving, this is not the great samādhi." Zhihuang had no answer. After a long silence, he asked, "Whom does your own teacher succeed?" Xuance said, "My teacher is the Sixth Patriarch of Caoxi." Zhihuang said, "What does the Sixth Patriarch take dhyāna-samādhi to be?" Xuance said, "What my teacher describes is a wondrous, profound stillness, its substance and function both just as they are. The five aggregates are fundamentally empty; the six kinds of sense-objects are not truly existent. There is no leaving and no entering, no settling and no confusion. The nature of dhyāna is without dwelling, free even of dwelling in the stillness of dhyāna itself; the nature of dhyāna is without arising, free even of the notion of a dhyāna that arises. The mind is like empty space, and yet is without even the measure of 'empty space.'" Hearing this, Zhihuang went directly to see the Master himself. The Master asked him, "Where have you come from, sir?" Zhihuang related everything that had happened. The Master said, "It is truly just as he described it to you. Simply let your mind be like empty space, without becoming attached even to the view of emptiness; let its function be unobstructed, its movement and stillness both without any fixed mind; forget the distinction between the ordinary and the sage; let subject and object both dissolve away; let nature and mark remain just as they are — and there will be no time at all that is not this very samādhi." At this Zhihuang was greatly awakened, and the mind he had held onto for twenty years vanished entirely, without a trace. That night the people north of the Yellow River heard a voice from the empty sky, saying, "Chan Master Zhihuang has attained the Way this very day." Zhihuang later took his leave and returned north of the river, where he taught and transformed the fourfold assembly. A monk once asked the Master, "The final instruction at Huangmei — who received it?" The Master said, "Whoever understands the Buddha-dharma receives it." The monk said, "Then has the venerable monk himself received it?" The Master said, "I myself do not understand the Buddha-dharma."

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads Xuance's opening question — entering samādhi with a mind, or without one — as a deliberately unanswerable dilemma, designed to expose that Zhihuang's twenty years of sitting had never actually resolved this question at all, only avoided asking it. His real diagnosis lands in the follow-up: "if there is entering and leaving, this is not the great samādhi." Zhihuang's practice had a beginning and an end each session — he entered it and later left it — which means, on this account, that whatever state he had spent twenty years perfecting was never the unbroken samādhi of self-nature described elsewhere in this sūtra, only a temporary and effortful suspension that necessarily lapsed the moment he stopped maintaining it.

The closing exchange with the anonymous monk — "who received Huangmei's final instruction?" "whoever understands the Buddha-dharma"; "has the venerable monk himself received it?" "I myself do not understand the Buddha-dharma" — reads, on Hanshan's account, as neither false modesty nor a riddle but the same logic this whole chapter has been demonstrating on visitor after visitor: to claim attainment is already to have turned attainment into an object held apart from oneself, exactly the error Zhichang's teacher made with "seeing" and "knowing," and exactly what Zhihuang's twenty years of entering and leaving samādhi never escaped.

6. The Sculptor Fangbian, and Wolun's Verse

Chinese Source

師一日欲濯所授之衣而無美泉，因至寺後五里許，見山林欝茂，瑞氣盤旋。師振錫卓地，泉應手而出，積以為池，乃跪膝浣衣石上。 忽有一僧來禮拜，云：「方辯是西蜀人，昨於南天竺國，見達磨大師，囑方辯速往唐土。吾傳大迦葉正法眼藏及僧伽梨，見傳六代，於韶州曹溪，汝去瞻禮。方辯遠來，願見我師傳來衣鉢。」師乃出示，次問：「上人攻何事業？」曰：「善塑。」師正色曰：「汝試塑看。」辯罔措。過數日，塑就真相，可高七寸，曲盡其妙。師笑曰：「汝只解塑性，不解佛性。」師舒手摩方辯頂，曰：「永為人天福田。」 (師仍以衣酬之。辯取衣分為三，一披塑像，一自留，一用椶裹瘞地中。誓曰：「後得此衣，乃吾出世，住持於此，重建殿宇。」宋嘉祐八年，有僧惟先，修殿掘地，得衣如新。像在高泉寺，祈禱輒應)。 有僧舉臥輪禪師偈曰： 「臥輪有伎倆，　能斷百思想， 對境心不起，　菩提日日長。」 師聞之，曰：「此偈未明心地，若依而行之，是加繫縛。」因示一偈曰： 「惠能沒伎倆，　不斷百思想， 對境心數起，　菩提作麼長。」

Ru-Yi Translation

One day the Master wished to wash the robe he had received, but there was no good spring nearby. He went about five li behind the temple, and there saw a place where the forest grew thick and lush, with an auspicious energy circling about it. The Master struck his staff into the ground, and a spring gushed forth at once, gathering into a pool; he then knelt on a rock beside it and washed the robe. Suddenly a monk came and bowed before him, saying, "My name is Fangbian, and I am a man of western Sichuan. Recently, in southern India, I saw the great teacher Bodhidharma, who charged me: 'Go quickly to the land of Tang. I transmitted Mahākāśyapa's true Dharma-eye treasury, together with the patched robe, and it has now passed down through six generations, to Caoxi in Shaozhou — go there and pay it homage.' Fangbian has come from far away, and wishes to see the robe and bowl transmitted to my teacher." The Master then brought them out to show him, and asked in turn, "What is your own occupation, sir?" He said, "I am skilled in sculpture." The Master said, with a serious expression, "Then try sculpting something for me to see." Fangbian, at a loss, did not know how to respond. Several days later, he completed a true likeness, about seven inches high, capturing every subtlety with wondrous skill. The Master laughed and said, "You understand only how to sculpt physical nature — you do not understand buddha-nature." The Master then stretched out his hand, placed it upon Fangbian's head, and said, "May you forever be a field of blessing for gods and humans." (The Master repaid him for this with the robe. Fangbian divided the robe into three parts: one he draped over the sculpted image, one he kept for himself, and one he wrapped in palm fiber and buried in the ground, vowing, "In a future age, whoever recovers this robe will be myself, born again, to serve as abbot here and rebuild these very halls." In the eighth year of the Jiayou era of the Song dynasty, a monk named Weixian, repairing the temple hall, dug into the ground and recovered the robe, still as fresh as new. The image itself remains at Gaoquan Temple, and prayers made before it are answered without fail.) A monk once cited a verse by Chan Master Wolun: "Wolun has a certain skill: he is able to cut off a hundred kinds of thought. Facing every circumstance, no mind stirs within him — and bodhi grows greater, day by day." Hearing this, the Master said, "This verse has not yet made clear the ground of the mind. To practice according to it would only add further bondage." And he offered a verse of his own: "Huineng has no particular skill at all: he has never cut off a hundred kinds of thought. Facing every circumstance, the mind stirs again and again — and how, then, could bodhi ever grow?"

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads Huineng's request — "try sculpting something for me to see" — as a deliberate test disguised as an ordinary request for a portrait. Fangbian succeeds completely at the craft he actually practices, producing a likeness Hanshan's commentary calls masterful, "capturing every subtlety with wondrous skill" — and Huineng's verdict, "you understand only how to sculpt physical nature, not buddha-nature," turns on the pun between 塑性 (shaping form) and 佛性 (buddha-nature): a perfect likeness of Huineng's face is still only a likeness, no closer to the nature underneath that face than any other outward form. The blessing Huineng gives him anyway — undiminished by the correction — treats the craft itself as worthy even though it was never going to be the thing being asked for.

Hanshan reads Wolun's verse as this chapter's final and clearest instance of a mistake several other visitors have already made in different forms: mistaking the successful suppression of thought for the growth of bodhi itself. "No mind stirs" sounds like accomplishment, but on the account this sūtra has built across several chapters now — the warning that stopping thought entirely is death, not attainment, and that no-thought means freedom within thought rather than its absence — Wolun has achieved exactly the wrong kind of stillness. Huineng's counter-verse does not claim a special skill either, but reports the plain opposite condition: thought continues to arise, again and again, and bodhi is not something that a quieter mind accumulates more of. The verse closes this long chapter by returning, one final time, to the same teaching on no-thought first stated in this sūtra's second chapter.
