Encounters: Fada and the Lotus Sūtra

機緣品第七（上）

The longest chapter in the sūtra turns from continuous discourse to a gallery of individual encounters — each disciple arriving with a specific confusion, and Huineng answering it exactly, never with the same teaching twice. This first half opens with an illiterate nun who cannot believe an unlettered man can explain her sūtra, then moves through Fahai's question on mind and buddha to its longest and richest encounter: a monk who has recited the Lotus Sūtra three thousand times without once understanding it, and the single line that changes what all three thousand recitations meant.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T2008 · Zongbao's redaction, Yuan dynasty (1291 CE) Commentary guide: Hanshan Deqing's 六祖法寶壇經解義 (17th c.)
This part covers
The nun Wujinzang, astonished that an illiterate man could explain a sūtra she had long studied; the Refuge Rock where Huineng hid from pursuers during his years in hiding; Fahai's question on "mind itself is buddha"; and Fada's encounter — his prideful bow rebuked, the pun on his own name, and Huineng's exposition of the Lotus Sūtra's "one great causal purpose," the Buddha's fourfold knowledge-and-vision, and the parable of the three carts and the one true vehicle.
Why it matters
This is where the sūtra's method changes from sustained teaching to individual diagnosis — each visitor's specific confusion met with an answer built for that confusion alone, and the Fada encounter shows this method at its fullest, working through an entire other sūtra one line at a time.

1. Wujinzang, and the Refuge Rock

Chinese Source

師自黃梅得法，回至韶州曹侯村，人無知者(他本云，師去時，至曹侯村，住九月餘。然師自言：「不經三十餘日便至黃梅。」此求道之切，豈有逗留？作去時者非是)。有儒士劉志略，禮遇甚厚。志略有姑為尼，名無盡藏，常誦《大涅槃經》。師暫聽，即知妙義，遂為解說。尼乃執卷問字，師曰：「字即不識，義即請問。」尼曰：「字尚不識，焉能會義？」師曰：「諸佛妙理，非關文字。」尼驚異之，遍告里中耆德云：「此是有道之士，宜請供養。」有魏(魏一作晉)武侯玄孫曹叔良及居民，競來瞻禮。時，寶林古寺，自隋末兵火已廢，遂於故基重建梵宇，延師居之。俄成寶坊，師住九月餘日，又為惡黨尋逐，師乃遯于前山。被其縱火焚草木，師隱身挨入石中得免。石今有師趺坐膝痕，及衣布之紋，因名避難石。師憶五祖懷會止藏之囑，遂行隱于二邑焉。

Ru-Yi Translation

After Huineng received the Dharma at Huangmei, he returned to Caohou Village in Shaozhou, where no one knew who he was. (Other editions say that when the Master left Huangmei, he stopped at Caohou Village and stayed there for over nine months. But the Master himself said, "In less than thirty days I reached Huangmei" — given how urgent his own search for the Way had been, how could he have lingered so long on the way back? The version claiming he stopped there on his way south is therefore mistaken.) There was a Confucian scholar named Liu Zhilüe, who treated the Master with great respect. Zhilüe had an aunt who had become a nun, named Wujinzang, who constantly recited the Great Nirvāṇa Sūtra. The Master, listening for a moment, at once understood its subtle meaning, and explained it for her. The nun then held up the scroll and asked him about a character in the text. The Master said, "I cannot read the character, but you may ask me its meaning." She said, "If you cannot even read the character, how could you possibly understand its meaning?" The Master said, "The subtle principles of the buddhas have nothing to do with written words." The nun, astonished at this, went throughout the village telling the local elders, "This is a person who has attained the Way — we ought to invite him and make offerings to him." A descendant of the Prince of Wei, Cao Shuliang, together with the local residents, came competing with one another to pay their respects. At that time the ancient temple of Baolin, which had lain in ruins since it was destroyed by war at the end of the Sui dynasty, was rebuilt on its old foundations, and the Master was invited to dwell there. It soon became a splendid monastery, and the Master lived there for more than nine months, until he was again pursued by a wicked faction, and fled into the mountain in front of the temple. They set fire to the grass and trees to drive him out, but the Master hid himself by pressing into a crevice in the rock, and so escaped. That rock still bears the marks of the Master's knees from sitting cross-legged there, and the pattern of his robe's cloth, and for this reason it is called the Refuge Rock. Remembering the Fifth Patriarch's charge to stop at Huai and hide at Hui, the Master then went into hiding in those two districts.

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads "the subtle principles of the buddhas have nothing to do with written words" as Huineng's own claim from the fourth part of this sūtra — that scriptures exist because of people, not the reverse — no longer stated as doctrine but demonstrated on an actual skeptic. Wujinzang's objection is entirely reasonable on its own terms: how could someone unable to read a character possibly explain its meaning? Hanshan's answer is that meaning was never stored in the characters themselves, only pointed to by them, and a person who has seen their own nature directly has no need of the pointer to reach what it points toward.

This editorial aside about which village Huineng stopped at — rare in a text otherwise narrated without interruption — is itself worth noting: it shows later compilers of the received text cross-checking the narrative against Huineng's own words elsewhere in the sūtra and correcting an inconsistency rather than silently preserving two contradictory accounts. The uncertainty is left visible rather than smoothed away.

2. Fahai: Mind Itself, Buddha Itself

Chinese Source

僧法海，韶州曲江人也。初參祖師，問曰：「即心即佛，願垂指諭。」師曰：「前念不生即心，後念不滅即佛；成一切相即心，離一切相即佛。吾若具說，窮劫不盡。聽吾偈曰： 「即心名慧，　即佛乃定，　定慧等持， 意中清淨。　悟此法門，　由汝習性， 用本無生，　雙修是正。」 法海言下大悟，以偈讚曰： 「即心元是佛，　不悟而自屈， 我知定慧因，　雙修離諸物。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk Fahai, a man of Qujiang in Shaozhou, first came to the Patriarch and asked, "Mind itself is buddha — I beg you to give me instruction on this." The Master said, "The former thought, not yet arisen, is mind; the latter thought, not yet ceased, is buddha. To form every mark is mind; to be free of every mark is buddha. If I were to explain this in full, I could not exhaust it in an entire kalpa. Hear my verse: "Mind itself is called wisdom; buddha itself is samādhi — holding samādhi and wisdom equally, the intention within becomes pure. Awaken to this teaching, and let it come from your own cultivated nature; its function is fundamentally without arising — cultivating both together is the correct way." At these words Fahai was greatly awakened, and praised the teaching with a verse of his own: "Mind itself was always the buddha; not to awaken to this is to wrong oneself for nothing. Now I know the cause of samādhi and wisdom together — cultivating both as one, free of every single thing."

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads Huineng's answer as built on the same substance-and-function structure already established for samādhi and prajñā two chapters earlier: mind and buddha are not two things needing to be equated, but one thing named twice, once from the angle of its ordinary functioning and once from the angle of what that functioning ultimately is. "Its function is fundamentally without arising" ties this teaching directly back to the no-birth doctrine central to Huineng's own awakening — mind's activity and buddha's stillness are not opposites to be reconciled but the same fact described from two directions.

Fahai went on to become the sūtra's own principal recorder — the disciple to whom this text's colophon, in its final chapter, attributes the writing down of Huineng's teachings. Hanshan does not dwell on this detail here, but it gives this opening encounter a particular weight: the first exchange in this chapter belongs to the very person whose transcription preserved the whole of what follows.

3. Fada's Pride, and the Pun on His Own Name

Chinese Source

僧法達，洪州人，七歲出家，常誦《法華經》。來禮祖師，頭不至地。師訶曰：「禮不投地，何如不禮？汝心中必有一物。蘊習何事耶？」曰：「念《法華經》已及三千部。」師曰：「汝若念至萬部，得其經意，不以為勝，則與吾偕行。汝今負此事業，都不知過。聽吾偈曰： 「禮本折慢幢，　頭奚不至地？ 有我罪即生，　亡功福無比。」 師又曰：「汝名什麼？」曰：「法達。」師曰：「汝名法達，何曾達法？」復說偈曰： 「汝今名法達，　勤誦未休歇， 空誦但循聲，　明心號菩薩。 汝今有緣故，　吾今為汝說， 但信佛無言，　蓮華從口發。」 達聞偈，悔謝曰：「而今而後，當謙恭一切。弟子誦《法華經》，未解經義，心常有疑。和尚智慧廣大，願略說經中義理。」師曰：「法達！法即甚達，汝心不達。經本無疑，汝心自疑。汝念此經，以何為宗？」達曰：「學人根性闇鈍，從來但依文誦念，豈知宗趣？」師曰：「吾不識文字，汝試取經誦一遍，吾當為汝解說。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk Fada, a man of Hongzhou, went forth from home at the age of seven and constantly recited the Lotus Sūtra. He came to bow before the Patriarch, but his head did not touch the ground. The Master rebuked him: "If you bow without your head touching the ground, how is that any different from not bowing at all? There must be something you are carrying in your mind. What have you been cultivating?" He said, "I have recited the Lotus Sūtra already, three thousand times over." The Master said, "If you recited it ten thousand times, and grasped the sūtra's meaning without taking this as some achievement to be proud of, then you would walk the same path as I do. But now you carry this accomplishment as a burden, and do not even recognize your own fault. Hear my verse: "Bowing is meant to break down the banner of pride — why does your head not touch the ground? Where there is still a self, offense arises at once; forget your own achievement, and merit becomes beyond compare." The Master asked further, "What is your name?" He said, "Fada." The Master said, "Your name is Fada — 'reaching the Dharma' — but have you ever actually reached it?" And he spoke a further verse: "Your name now is Fada, diligently reciting without ever resting; but reciting emptily, merely following the sound, is called a bodhisattva only once the mind itself is made clear. Since you now have this connection with me, I will now speak for your sake: simply trust that the Buddha never spoke a word at all, and a lotus flower will bloom straight from your own mouth." Hearing this verse, Fada, ashamed, said in apology, "From this moment on, I will be humble and reverent toward everyone. Your disciple has recited the Lotus Sūtra without ever understanding its meaning, and my mind has always held doubts about it. The venerable monk's wisdom is vast — I beg you to explain briefly the principles within this sūtra." The Master said, "Fada! The Dharma itself has indeed been reached — it is your own mind that has not reached it. The sūtra itself holds no doubt at all; it is your own mind that doubts it on its own. When you recite this sūtra, what do you take as its central doctrine?" Fada said, "This student's faculties are dull and unclear; I have only ever followed the text and recited it aloud — how could I know its central purport?" The Master said, "I myself cannot read — try taking up the sūtra and reciting a portion of it aloud, and I will explain it for you."

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan treats the detail of Fada's head not touching the ground as diagnostic rather than a minor lapse in etiquette: pride announces itself through the body before it announces itself in words, and Huineng's question — "what have you been cultivating?" — reads the posture correctly before Fada has said a single thing about his three thousand recitations. The verse's line "where there is still a self, offense arises at once" is, on Hanshan's account, the whole encounter's real subject: not whether Fada has recited enough, but whether the recitation has been quietly repurposed into something to be proud of.

The pun on Fada's own name — 法達, literally "reaching the Dharma" — is a device Hanshan reads as characteristic of how this sūtra teaches throughout: a name, a label, an accomplishment restated back to the person holding it, turned from a settled fact into an open question. Fada arrived certain of what his own name and his three thousand recitations already proved about him; Huineng's first move, before any doctrine is explained at all, is to unsettle that certainty completely.

4. One Vehicle, Not Three

Chinese Source

法達即高聲念經，至譬喻品，師曰：「止！此經元來以因緣出世為宗，縱說多種譬喻，亦無越於此。何者因緣？經云：『諸佛世尊，唯以一大事因緣出現於世。』一大事者，佛之知見也。世人外迷著相，內迷著空；若能於相離相、於空離空，即是內外不迷。若悟此法，一念心開，是為開佛知見。佛，猶覺也。分為四門，開覺知見、示覺知見、悟覺知見、入覺知見。若聞開示，便能悟入，即覺知見，本來真性而得出現。汝慎勿錯解經意，見他道：『開示悟入，自是佛之知見。我輩無分。』若作此解，乃是謗經毀佛也。彼既是佛，已具知見，何用更開？汝今當信，佛知見者，只汝自心，更無別佛。蓋為一切眾生，自蔽光明，貪愛塵境，外緣內擾，甘受驅馳。便勞他世尊，從三昧起，種種苦口，勸令寢息，莫向外求，與佛無二。故云：『開佛知見。』吾亦勸一切人，於自心中，常開佛之知見。世人心邪，愚迷造罪，口善心惡，貪瞋嫉妬，諂佞我慢，侵人害物，自開眾生知見。若能正心，常生智慧，觀照自心，止惡行善，是自開佛之知見。汝須念念開佛知見，勿開眾生知見。開佛知見，即是出世；開眾生知見，即是世間。汝若但勞勞執念，以為功課者，何異犛牛愛尾。」達曰：「若然者，但得解義，不勞誦經耶？」師曰：「經有何過，豈障汝念？只為迷悟在人，損益由己。口誦心行，即是轉經；口誦心不行，即是被經轉。聽吾偈曰： 「心迷法華轉，　心悟轉法華， 誦經久不明，　與義作讎家。 無念念即正，　有念念成邪， 有無俱不計，　長御白牛車。」 達聞偈，不覺悲泣，言下大悟，而告師曰：「法達從昔已來，實未曾轉法華，乃被法華轉。」再啟曰：「經云：『諸大聲聞乃至菩薩，皆盡思共度量，不能測佛智。』今令凡夫但悟自心，便名佛之知見。自非上根，未免疑謗。又經說三車，羊鹿牛車與白牛之車，如何區別？願和尚再垂開示。」師曰：「經意分明，汝自迷背。諸三乘人，不能測佛智者，患在度量也。饒伊盡思共推，轉加懸遠。佛本為凡夫說，不為佛說。此理若不肯信者，從他退席。殊不知，坐却白牛車，更於門外覓三車。況經文明向汝道：『唯一佛乘，無有餘乘若二若三。』乃至無數方便，種種因緣譬喻言詞，是法皆為一佛乘故。汝何不省，三車是假，為昔時故；一乘是實，為今時故。只教汝去假歸實，歸實之後，實亦無名。應知所有珍財，盡屬於汝，由汝受用，更不作父想，亦不作子想，亦無用想。是名持法華經，從劫至劫，手不釋卷，從晝至夜，無不念時也。」 達蒙啟發，踊躍歡喜，以偈讚曰： 「經誦三千部，　曹溪一句亡， 未明出世旨，　寧歇累生狂。 羊鹿牛權設，　初中後善揚， 誰知火宅內，　元是法中王。」 師曰：「汝今後方可名念經僧也。」達從此領玄旨，亦不輟誦經。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fada then recited the sūtra in a loud voice, and when he reached the Parable Chapter, the Master said, "Stop! This sūtra, from the very beginning, takes as its central doctrine the manifestation of the Buddha in the world for the sake of a single causal purpose. However many kinds of parables it goes on to tell, none of them go beyond this one point. What is this causal purpose? The sūtra says, 'The buddhas, the world-honored ones, appear in the world only for the sake of one great causal purpose.' This one great purpose is the Buddha's own knowledge and vision. People of this world are deluded outwardly, attached to marks, and deluded inwardly, attached to emptiness; if one is able to be free of marks within marks, and free of emptiness within emptiness, this is to be undeluded both within and without. If you awaken to this teaching, and your mind opens in a single thought, this is what is meant by opening the Buddha's knowledge and vision. "'Buddha' means, simply, awakening, divided into four gates: opening the knowledge and vision of awakening, showing the knowledge and vision of awakening, awakening to the knowledge and vision of awakening, and entering the knowledge and vision of awakening. If, on hearing it opened and shown, one is able to awaken and enter it, then this knowledge and vision of awakening — one's own original true nature — comes to manifest itself. Be careful not to misread the sūtra's meaning: on hearing others say that 'opening, showing, awakening, and entering' belongs to the Buddha's own knowledge and vision alone, and that we ourselves have no share in it — if you understand it in this way, this is itself slandering the sūtra and vilifying the Buddha. If he is already a buddha, already complete with this knowledge and vision, what further need would there be to open it at all? You should now believe that this knowledge and vision of the Buddha is simply your own mind, and that there is no other buddha apart from it. It is only because every sentient being obscures their own light, greedily clinging to the dust of worldly circumstances, disturbed without by conditions and within by their own mind, and willingly submits to being driven about by them, that the World-Honored One must trouble himself to rise from his samādhi, and through every kind of bitter, urgent word, persuade beings to let this all come to rest, and not to seek outside themselves — for this is no different from the Buddha himself. This is why it is called 'opening the Buddha's knowledge and vision.' I too urge everyone: within your own mind, constantly open the knowledge and vision of the Buddha. People of this world have crooked minds, foolish and deluded, committing offenses, speaking well with the mouth while the mind is evil, full of greed, anger, envy, flattery, deceit, and arrogance, encroaching on others and doing harm — this is opening, for oneself, the knowledge and vision of a sentient being. But if you can rectify your mind, constantly give rise to wisdom, contemplate your own mind, cease from evil and practice good — this is opening, for yourself, the knowledge and vision of the Buddha. You must, thought after thought, open the Buddha's knowledge and vision, and not open the knowledge and vision of a sentient being. To open the Buddha's knowledge and vision is itself to transcend the world; to open the knowledge and vision of a sentient being is itself to remain within the world. If you merely toil away, gripping tightly to your recitation, and call this your practice, how would this be any different from a yak that loves nothing so much as its own tail?" Fada said, "If that is so, then does one need only grasp the meaning, without troubling to recite the sūtra at all?" The Master said, "What fault does the sūtra itself have? How could it possibly obstruct your practice? It is only that delusion and awakening lie with the person, and loss and benefit come from oneself. To recite with the mouth and carry it out in the mind is to turn the sūtra; to recite with the mouth without carrying it out in the mind is to be turned by the sūtra. Hear my verse: "When the mind is deluded, the Lotus Sūtra turns you; when the mind is awakened, you turn the Lotus Sūtra. Reciting the sūtra for a long time without understanding it, you become an enemy to its very meaning. Thought without thought is itself right thought; thought with attachment becomes wrong thought. Reckoning neither existence nor non-existence, you ride forever in the great white ox cart." Hearing this verse, Fada could not help weeping, and at these very words was greatly awakened. He said to the Master, "From long ago until now, I have truly never once turned the Lotus Sūtra — I have only ever been turned by the Lotus Sūtra." He asked further, "The sūtra says, 'Even the great śrāvakas and bodhisattvas, exhausting their thought together in reckoning, cannot fathom the Buddha's wisdom.' Yet now you have caused an ordinary person simply to awaken to their own mind, and call this the Buddha's own knowledge and vision — unless one is of the very highest faculties, one could hardly avoid doubting and slandering this claim. And the sūtra also speaks of three carts — the goat cart, the deer cart, and the ox cart — set against the great white ox cart. How are they to be distinguished? I beg the venerable monk to instruct me further." The Master said, "The sūtra's meaning is stated plainly — it is you yourself who has become confused and turned away from it. Those of the three vehicles who cannot fathom the Buddha's wisdom are troubled precisely by their own reckoning. Though they exhaust every thought reckoning it together, they only grow further and further from it. The Buddha originally spoke this teaching for the sake of ordinary people, not for the sake of buddhas themselves; if there is anyone unwilling to believe this principle, let them withdraw from the assembly as they please. Little do they know that they are already seated in the great white ox cart, and yet still search outside the gate for the three carts. Moreover, the sūtra states plainly to you: 'There is only the one buddha vehicle — there is no second vehicle, and no third,' down to the countless skillful means, and every kind of causal parable and figure of speech — all of this teaching exists only for the sake of the one buddha vehicle. Why do you not realize this for yourself? The three carts are provisional, spoken for the sake of that earlier time; the one vehicle is real, spoken for the sake of the present time. All of this teaches you only to set aside the provisional and return to the real; and once you have returned to the real, even 'the real' has no name left to call it. You should know that every treasure that exists belongs entirely to you, to be used and enjoyed by you alone — no longer thinking of it as a father's, nor as a child's, nor even thinking of it as something to be used at all. This is what is meant by upholding the Lotus Sūtra: from kalpa to kalpa, never setting the scroll down from your hand, from day to night, never a moment without being mindful of it." Having received this instruction, Fada leapt with joy, and praised it with a verse of his own: "Reciting the sūtra three thousand times over, one single word at Caoxi has undone them all; without understanding the purpose of transcending the world, how could the frenzy of many lifetimes ever be put to rest? The goat, the deer, and the ox cart were only provisionally set up, proclaimed as good in the beginning, the middle, and the end. Who could have known that within the burning house itself, there was already the very king of the Dharma?" The Master said, "From now on you may truly be called a monk who recites the sūtra." From this time on Fada grasped its profound meaning, and yet he never once stopped reciting the sūtra.

Reading note — after Hanshan

Readers who have followed this site's Lotus Sūtra project will recognize the exchange Huineng is condensing here almost entirely: "the buddhas, the world-honored ones, appear in the world only for the sake of one great causal purpose" is the very line Zhiyi's commentary treats as the doctrinal hinge of the Lotus Sūtra's own second chapter, and Huineng's fourfold gate of opening, showing, awakening, and entering matches, gloss for gloss, how that commentary reads the same passage. What is distinctive here is not the doctrine but the method: rather than working through all twenty-eight chapters as this site's own Lotus Sūtra pages do, Huineng needs only Fada's mistaken assumption — that "opening, showing, awakening, and entering" belongs to the Buddha alone, and "we ourselves have no share in it" — to unlock the entire sūtra in a single exchange.

Hanshan reads the closing image, "you are already seated in the great white ox cart, and yet still search outside the gate for the three carts," as this whole encounter's real punchline: Fada has spent years treating the Lotus Sūtra's parable as a description of someone else's rescue, still standing outside a house he had, in fact, already been given the keys to. His own closing verse — "who could have known that within the burning house itself, there was already the very king of the Dharma?" — is, on Hanshan's reading, Fada's own recognition of exactly this, phrased in the same fire-and-house imagery the parable itself uses.

5. Zhitong: The Threefold Body and Fourfold Wisdom

Chinese Source

僧智通，壽州安豐人。初看《楞伽經》。約千餘遍，而不會三身四智。禮師求解其義，師曰：「三身者，清淨法身，汝之性也；圓滿報身，汝之智也；千百億化身，汝之行也。若離本性，別說三身，即名有身無智；若悟三身無有自性，即明四智菩提。聽吾偈曰： 「自性具三身，　發明成四智， 不離見聞緣，　超然登佛地。 吾今為汝說，　諦信永無迷， 莫學馳求者，　終日說菩提。」 通再啟曰：「四智之義，可得聞乎？」師曰：「既會三身，便明四智。何更問耶？若離三身，別談四智，此名有智無身。即此有智，還成無智。」復說偈曰： 「大圓鏡智性清淨，　平等性智心無病， 妙觀察智見非功，　成所作智同圓鏡。 五八六七果因轉，　但用名言無實性， 若於轉處不留情，　繁興永處那伽定。」 (如上轉識為智也。教中云，轉前五識為成所作智，轉第六識為妙觀察智，轉第七識為平等性智，轉第八識為大圓鏡智。雖六七因中轉，五八果上轉，但轉其名而不轉其體也)。 通頓悟性智，遂呈偈曰： 「三身元我體，　四智本心明， 身智融無礙，　應物任隨形。 起修皆妄動，　守住匪真精， 妙旨因師曉，　終亡染污名。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk Zhitong, a man of Anfeng in Shouzhou, had at first read the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra some thousand times over, without ever grasping the meaning of the threefold body and fourfold wisdom. He came to bow before the Master and asked him to explain their meaning. The Master said, "The three bodies are these: the pure Dharma-body is your own nature; the complete reward-body is your own wisdom; the hundred-thousand-koṭi transformation-body is your own conduct. If you speak of the three bodies apart from your own fundamental nature, this is called having the bodies without the wisdom; but if you awaken to the three bodies as having no separate nature of their own, this is what is meant by the bodhi of the fourfold wisdom. Hear my verse: "Self-nature is complete with the three bodies; brought into clarity, it becomes the fourfold wisdom. Never departing from the conditions of seeing and hearing, you transcend them and ascend directly to the ground of buddhahood. I now explain this for you — believe it truly, and you will never again be deluded; do not study like those who run about seeking outside themselves, speaking of bodhi all day long." Zhitong asked further, "May I hear the meaning of the fourfold wisdom?" The Master said, "Once you have grasped the three bodies, the fourfold wisdom is already clear — why ask further? If you speak of the fourfold wisdom apart from the three bodies, this is called having the wisdom without the bodies; and even this wisdom, spoken of in such a way, becomes itself no wisdom at all." And he spoke a further verse: "The great perfect-mirror wisdom is the nature itself, pure; the wisdom of universal equality is a mind free of sickness; the wisdom of wondrous contemplation sees without effort; the wisdom that accomplishes what is to be done is the same as the great mirror. The fifth and eighth, and the sixth and seventh, consciousnesses turn at the stage of fruition and at the stage of cause, respectively; only their names are used, and they have no fixed nature of their own — if, at the very point of this turning, you hold nothing back, then though the world's affairs arise thick and fast all around you, you dwell forever in nāga-like samādhi." (This describes the turning of consciousness into wisdom. The teaching says: the first five consciousnesses are turned into the wisdom that accomplishes what is to be done; the sixth consciousness is turned into the wisdom of wondrous contemplation; the seventh consciousness is turned into the wisdom of universal equality; the eighth consciousness is turned into the great perfect-mirror wisdom. Though the sixth and seventh turn at the stage of cause, and the fifth and eighth turn only at the stage of fruition, only their names are turned, never their underlying substance.) At this Zhitong suddenly awakened to the wisdom of his own nature, and offered a verse in response: "The three bodies were, from the start, my own substance; the fourfold wisdom is simply my own mind made bright. Body and wisdom fuse together without obstruction, responding to whatever comes, taking on whatever form is needed. To set out deliberately to cultivate is itself already a false stirring; to hold fast and remain unmoving is likewise not the true essence. This wondrous principle I have understood only through the Master's teaching, and at last I forget even the very name of defilement."

Reading note — after Hanshan

Zhitong's question and Huineng's answer both draw directly on the technical vocabulary of Yogācāra — the eight consciousnesses and their transformation into the four wisdoms is worked out at length in the treatises this site's own Yogācāra corpus translates, the Cheng Weishi Lun and the Yogâcārabhūmi-śāstra among them, and Hanshan's parenthetical gloss here is close to a compressed restatement of that same scheme. What distinguishes Huineng's answer is where he insists the account must start: not with the four wisdoms as an external classification to be memorized, but with the same threefold body already taught in this sūtra's own chapter on repentance — nature, wisdom, and conduct, each already possessed, not separately acquired.

Hanshan draws out the point behind "the fifth and eighth turn only at the stage of fruition, only their names are turned, never their underlying substance": the transformation from ordinary consciousness into wisdom is not a replacement of one thing by another but a renaming of a process already underway, discovered rather than manufactured. This is the same claim, in Yogācāra's own technical register, that the rest of this sūtra keeps making in plainer language — that awakening uncovers a capacity already present rather than installing a new one.
