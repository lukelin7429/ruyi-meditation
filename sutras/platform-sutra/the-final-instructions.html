The Final Instructions

付囑品第十

Huineng's closing chapter moves through four distinct acts: a practical method — the thirty-six paired opposites — for his ten senior disciples to carry the teaching forward without distorting it; the announcement of his own approaching death, met with weeping from everyone but Shenhui, and the naming of this sūtra for the first time within its own text; the return to his home village, a cryptic prophecy about his own head, and the full lineage of thirty-three patriarchs back through Bodhidharma to the Buddha himself; and, at the very end, a final verse and a death witnessed, the sūtra says, by fragrance filling the room and a white rainbow touching the earth.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T2008 · Zongbao's redaction, Yuan dynasty (1291 CE) Commentary guide: Hanshan Deqing's 六祖法寶壇經解義 (17th c.)
This part covers
The thirty-six paired opposites and a method for teaching through them, so that any question is answered in a way that dissolves both extremes rather than taking a fixed position; the announcement of Huineng's approaching death, the Verse of True and False, Moving and Still, and the naming of this text as "The Platform Sūtra of the Dharma Treasure" for the first time; the return to Xinzhou, a cryptic prophecy about a threat to his own remains after death, and the full lineage of thirty-three patriarchs from the Buddha through Bodhidharma to Huineng himself; and the Verse of the True Buddha of Self-Nature, the final instructions against conventional mourning, and the parinirvāṇa itself.
Why it matters
This closing chapter is where the sūtra becomes self-aware as a text — naming itself, securing its own lineage, and instructing its own transmission — while its final teaching circles back to the very first words Huineng ever spoke in this sūtra: that the mind already contains the buddha, and there is nowhere else to look for one.

1. The Thirty-Six Pairs

Chinese Source

師一日喚門人法海、志誠、法達、神會、智常、智通、志徹、志道、法珍、法如等，曰：「汝等不同餘人，吾滅度後，各為一方師。吾今教汝說法，不失本宗：先須舉三科法門，動用三十六對，出沒即離兩邊。說一切法，莫離自性。忽有人問汝法，出語盡雙，皆取對法，來去相因。究竟二法盡除，更無去處。三科法門者，陰、界、入也。陰是五陰——色、受、想、行、識是也。入是十二入，外六塵——色、聲、香、味、觸、法；內六門——眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、意是也。界是十八界，六塵、六門、六識是也。自性能含萬法，名含藏識。若起思量，即是轉識。生六識，出六門，見六塵。如是一十八界，皆從自性起用。自性若邪，起十八邪；自性若正，起十八正。若惡用即眾生用，善用即佛用。用由何等？由自性有，對法外境。無情五對：天與地對，日與月對，明與暗對，陰與陽對，水與火對；此是五對也。法相語言十二對：語與法對，有與無對，有色與無色對，有相與無相對，有漏與無漏對，色與空對，動與靜對，清與濁對，凡與聖對，僧與俗對，老與少對，大與小對；此是十二對也。自性起用十九對：長與短對，邪與正對，癡與慧對，愚與智對，亂與定對，慈與毒對，戒與非對，直與曲對，實與虛對，險與平對，煩惱與菩提對，常與無常對，悲與害對，喜與瞋對，捨與慳對，進與退對，生與滅對，法身與色身對，化身與報身對；此是十九對也。」師言：「此三十六對法，若解用即道，貫一切經法，出入即離兩邊。自性動用，共人言語，外於相離相，內於空離空。若全著相，即長邪見；若全執空，即長無明。執空之人有謗經，直言不用文字。既云不用文字，人亦不合語言。只此語言，便是文字之相。又云：『直道不立文字。』即此不立兩字，亦是文字。見人所說，便即謗他言著文字。汝等須知，自迷猶可，又謗佛經。不要謗經，罪障無數。若著相於外，而作法求真；或廣立道場，說有無之過患。如是之人，累劫不得見性。但聽依法修行，又莫百物不思，而於道性窒礙。若聽說不修，令人反生邪念。但依法修行，無住相法施。汝等若悟，依此說、依此用、依此行、依此作，即不失本宗。若有人問汝義，問有將無對，問無將有對，問凡以聖對，問聖以凡對。二道相因，生中道義。如一問一對，餘問一依此作，即不失理也。設有人問：『何名為闇？』答云：『明是因，闇是緣，明沒即闇。』以明顯闇，以闇顯明，來去相因，成中道義。餘問悉皆如此。汝等於後傳法，依此轉相教授，勿失宗旨。」

Ru-Yi Translation

One day the Master summoned his disciples Fahai, Zhicheng, Fada, Shenhui, Zhichang, Zhitong, Zhiche, Zhidao, Fazhen, and Faru, and said to them: "You are not the same as the rest — after I have passed away, each of you will become a teacher in your own region. I will now teach you how to expound the Dharma without ever losing the essence of our own school. You must first raise the teaching of the three categories, and put into motion the thirty-six pairs, so that in every movement and every stillness you remain free of both extremes. In expounding any teaching at all, never depart from self-nature. If someone should suddenly ask you about the Dharma, let your words always come in pairs, each drawn from a corresponding opposite, each arising in dependence on the other; and when this is understood completely, both terms of the pair are removed entirely, leaving nowhere further to go. "The three categories are the aggregates, the sense-fields, and the elements. The aggregates are the five aggregates — form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. The sense-fields are the twelve sense-fields: the six outer objects of dust — form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and mental objects — and the six inner gates — the eyes, ears, nose, tongue, body, and mind. The elements are the eighteen elements — the six objects of dust, the six gates, and the six consciousnesses. Self-nature is able to contain the ten thousand things, and for this reason is called the storehouse consciousness. The moment deliberate thought arises, this is called the turning consciousness; it gives rise to the six consciousnesses, passes out through the six gates, and perceives the six objects of dust. All eighteen of these elements arise as the function of self-nature itself. If self-nature is deviant, eighteen deviant functions arise; if self-nature is upright, eighteen upright functions arise. If the function is put to evil use, this is the function of a sentient being; if put to good use, this is the function of a buddha. And on what does this function depend? It depends on self-nature itself. "The pairs correspond to outer circumstances. There are five pairs among insentient things: heaven paired with earth, sun paired with moon, brightness paired with darkness, yin paired with yang, water paired with fire — these are the five pairs. There are twelve pairs of dharma-marks and language: speech paired with the Dharma, existence paired with non-existence, form paired with formlessness, marks paired with marklessness, the defiled paired with the undefiled, form paired with emptiness, movement paired with stillness, clarity paired with turbidity, the ordinary paired with the sagely, the monastic paired with the lay, the old paired with the young, the great paired with the small — these are the twelve pairs. There are nineteen pairs arising as the function of self-nature: long paired with short, deviant paired with upright, foolish paired with wise, ignorant paired with intelligent, confused paired with settled, compassionate paired with harmful, disciplined paired with undisciplined, straight paired with crooked, substantial paired with empty, perilous paired with level, affliction paired with bodhi, permanent paired with impermanent, sorrowful paired with harmful, joyful paired with angry, generous paired with stingy, advancing paired with retreating, arising paired with ceasing, Dharma-body paired with physical body, transformation-body paired with reward-body — these are the nineteen pairs." The Master said: "If you understand how to use these thirty-six pairs, this understanding runs through every scripture and every teaching, so that in every movement and every stillness you remain free of both extremes. When self-nature is set into motion, and you speak with other people, remain outwardly free of marks even while using marks, and remain inwardly free of emptiness even while speaking of emptiness. If you become entirely attached to marks, you only strengthen wrong views; if you become entirely attached to emptiness, you only strengthen ignorance. Those who cling to emptiness will even go so far as to slander the scriptures, saying outright that written words should not be used at all. But if written words are truly not to be used, then people should not use spoken language either — for spoken language is itself already a mark made of words. They will also say, 'the direct Way does not establish written words' — but even these two characters, 'not establish,' are themselves written words. On seeing someone else teach, they immediately accuse that person of clinging to the written word. You must understand: to be deluded yourself is one thing, but to go on and slander the Buddhist scriptures besides is another matter entirely. Do not slander the scriptures — the karmic hindrance from this is beyond counting. If, on the other hand, someone becomes attached to outward marks, performing rituals in search of what is real, or broadly establishing places of practice while arguing over the faults of existence and non-existence, such a person will fail to see their own nature for many kalpas to come. Simply listen, and practice in accordance with the Dharma; and do not, in reaction, think of nothing at all and thereby obstruct the very nature of the Way. To hear the teaching and not practice it only causes people to give rise to wrong views instead. Simply practice in accordance with the Dharma, giving the gift of teaching without dwelling on any mark. If you have awakened, and you speak, apply, practice, and act according to this, you will not lose the essence of our school. "If someone asks you about the meaning of the teaching, and asks about existence, answer with non-existence; if they ask about non-existence, answer with existence; if they ask about the ordinary person, answer with the sage; if they ask about the sage, answer with the ordinary person. The two paths depend on one another, and from this dependence the meaning of the middle way is born. If for a single question you give a single paired answer, and for every other question you act according to this same method, you will not lose the underlying principle. Suppose someone asks you, 'What is meant by darkness?' You would answer: 'Brightness is the direct cause, and darkness is the supporting condition; when brightness disappears, there is darkness. Brightness is used to reveal darkness, and darkness is used to reveal brightness, each depending on the other in turn, and from this the meaning of the middle way is accomplished.' Every other question should be answered in exactly this same way. As you go on to transmit the Dharma in the future, teach one another according to this method, and do not lose the essence of our school."

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads the thirty-six pairs not as abstract classification for its own sake but as a working method Huineng is handing his ten senior disciples for the specific situations they will face once he is gone: a questioner arrives already thinking in fixed, one-sided terms, and answering with the paired opposite — darkness answered through brightness, the ordinary person answered through the sage — does not supply a second fixed position but exposes that the two terms were always defined only in relation to one another. Once a questioner sees this mutual dependence, the middle way is not a third position to be argued for; it simply appears where the false choice between the two fixed positions used to be.

The passage's brief argument against those who "cling to emptiness" and claim writing itself should be discarded is, on Hanshan's reading, a small masterpiece of self-refutation built into the text itself: the claim that "the direct Way does not establish written words" is stated in four written characters, and objecting to someone else's use of scripture is itself an act of speech using marks to condemn marks. Hanshan reads this less as a defense of literacy for its own sake than as one more instance of this sūtra's recurring warning against attachment to emptiness — the same warning first given in the second chapter against mistaking a blank, thought-emptied mind for true realization, here applied specifically to teachers who might otherwise use "no reliance on words" as cover for having nothing substantive left to teach at all.

2. The Announcement, the Verse of True and False, and the Naming of This Sūtra

Chinese Source

師於太極元年壬子，延和七月(是年五月改延和，八月玄宗即位方改元先天，次年遂改開元。他本作先天者非)命門人往新州國恩寺建塔，仍令促工，次年夏末落成。七月一日，集徒眾曰：「吾至八月，欲離世間。汝等有疑，早須相問，為汝破疑，令汝迷盡。吾若去後，無人教汝。」法海等聞，悉皆涕泣。惟有神會，神情不動，亦無涕泣。 師云：「神會小師却得善不善等，毀譽不動，哀樂不生；餘者不得。數年山中竟修何道？汝今悲泣，為憂阿誰？若憂吾不知去處，吾自知去處。吾若不知去處，終不預報於汝。汝等悲泣，蓋為不知吾去處；若知吾去處，即不合悲泣。法性本無生滅去來，汝等盡坐，吾與汝說一偈，名曰真假動靜偈。汝等誦取此偈，與吾意同，依此修行，不失宗旨。」眾僧作禮，請師說偈。偈曰： 「一切無有真，　不以見於真， 若見於真者，　是見盡非真。 若能自有真，　離假即心真， 自心不離假，　無真何處真？ 有情即解動，　無情即不動， 若修不動行，　同無情不動。 若覓真不動，　動上有不動， 不動是不動，　無情無佛種。 能善分別相，　第一義不動， 但作如此見，　即是真如用。 報諸學道人，　努力須用意， 莫於大乘門，　却執生死智。 若言下相應，　即共論佛義； 若實不相應，　合掌令歡喜。 此宗本無諍，　諍即失道意， 執逆諍法門，　自性入生死。」 時，徒眾聞說偈已，普皆作禮，並體師意，各各攝心，依法修行，更不敢諍，乃知大師不久住世。法海上座，再拜問曰：「和尚入滅之後，衣法當付何人？」師曰：「吾於大梵寺說法，以至于今抄錄流行，目曰『法寶壇經』。汝等守護，遞相傳授。度諸群生，但依此說，是名正法。今為汝等說法，不付其衣。蓋為汝等信根淳熟，決定無疑，堪任大事。然據先祖達磨大師付授偈意，衣不合傳。偈曰： 「『吾本來茲土，　傳法救迷情， 一華開五葉，　結果自然成。』」 師復曰：「諸善知識！汝等各各淨心，聽吾說法。若欲成就種智，須達一相三昧、一行三昧。若於一切處而不住相，於彼相中不生憎愛，亦無取捨，不念利益成壞等事，安閒恬靜，虛融澹泊，此名一相三昧。若於一切處行住坐臥，純一直心，不動道場，真成淨土，此名一行三昧。若人具二三昧，如地有種，含藏長養，成熟其實。一相一行，亦復如是。我今說法，猶如時雨，普潤大地。汝等佛性，譬諸種子，遇茲霑洽，悉得發生。承吾旨者，決獲菩提。依吾行者，定證妙果。聽吾偈曰： 「心地含諸種，　普雨悉皆萌， 頓悟華情已，　菩提果自成。」 師說偈已，曰：「其法無二，其心亦然。其道清淨，亦無諸相，汝等慎勿觀靜及空其心。此心本淨，無可取捨。各自努力，隨緣好去。」爾時徒眾作禮而退。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the first year of the Taiji era, in the seventh month of the Yanhe era (that same year the era name was changed to Yanhe in the fifth month, and then, in the eighth month, when Emperor Xuanzong took the throne, it was changed again to Xiantian; the following year it was changed once more to Kaiyuan — other editions that give this as "Xiantian" are mistaken), the Master instructed his disciples to go to Guo'en Temple in Xinzhou and build a stūpa, and had the work hastened along; it was completed by the end of the following summer. On the first day of the seventh month he gathered his disciples and said, "By the time the eighth month comes, I intend to leave this world. If any of you have doubts, ask them now without delay, so that I may resolve your doubts for you and clear away all your confusion — for once I am gone, there will be no one left to teach you." Hearing this, Fahai and the others all wept; only Shenhui remained unmoved, his expression unchanged, without any tears at all. The Master said, "Young Master Shenhui alone has attained a state in which good and bad, praise and blame, are all the same to him, and neither sorrow nor joy arises within him. The rest of you have not attained this — after all these years in the mountains, what Way have you actually been cultivating? You weep now — for whose sake are you grieving? If you are grieving because you fear I do not know where I am going, you should know that I know perfectly well where I am going. If I did not know where I was going, I would never have been able to tell you of it in advance at all. You are weeping only because you do not know where I am going; if you knew where I was going, there would be no cause for grief. The nature of all things fundamentally has no arising or ceasing, no going or coming. Sit down, all of you, and I will speak a verse for you, called the Verse of True and False, Moving and Still. Learn to recite this verse — if it accords with my own meaning, and you practice according to it, you will not lose the essence of our school." The assembled monks bowed and asked the Master to speak the verse. The verse said: "In all things there is nothing true; do not, therefore, look for truth in what you see. If you do see something as true, this very seeing is itself never true at all. If you are able to have truth within yourself, being free of the false is itself the true mind. If your own mind is not free of the false, where else could any truth be found? Whatever has feeling knows how to move; whatever has no feeling does not move at all. If you cultivate the practice of not-moving, you become the same as something that does not move because it has no feeling at all. If you seek a 'true not-moving,' know that there is a not-moving within movement itself. But if you take not-moving to mean simple stillness, then something without feeling has no seed of buddhahood in it either. To be skilled at distinguishing every mark, while remaining unmoved with respect to the ultimate truth — simply to hold this view is itself the function of true suchness. I say this to every student of the Way: strive, and apply your intent carefully. Do not, within the gate of the Great Vehicle, go on clinging to the wisdom that still belongs to birth and death. If, at these very words, you find they accord with your own mind, then let us discuss the meaning of the Buddha together; but if they truly do not accord with you, simply join your palms and let the other person go their way content. This school of ours is fundamentally without argument — to argue is already to have lost the very meaning of the Way. To hold stubbornly to a position and argue over this teaching is to let self-nature fall back into birth and death." At that time, hearing this verse, the assembled disciples all bowed in homage, and, embodying the Master's own intention, each of them settled their minds and practiced in accordance with the Dharma, no longer daring to argue with one another; and they understood then that the Great Master would not remain in the world much longer. Fahai, the senior disciple, bowed again and asked, "After the venerable monk enters extinction, to whom should the robe and the Dharma be entrusted?" The Master said, "From the time I taught at Dafan Temple up to this very day, my teachings have been transcribed and are already in circulation, under the title 'The Platform Sūtra of the Dharma Treasure.' You are to guard this teaching, and hand it down from one to another, delivering all manner of beings by it — simply follow what is said in it, and this alone is the true Dharma. I am now teaching you the Dharma, but I will not confer the robe upon anyone. This is because your own roots of faith have already ripened fully, free of any doubt at all, and you are each capable of bearing this great responsibility yourselves. Moreover, according to the meaning of the verse our first patriarch Bodhidharma left at the time of his own transmission, the robe was never meant to be handed down forever. His verse said: 'I came originally to this land to transmit the Dharma and save those caught in delusion; one flower opens into five petals, and the fruit ripens of itself, in its own time.'" The Master said further: "Every one of you good friends, purify your own minds, and listen to what I now teach. If you wish to accomplish the seed-wisdom of buddhahood, you must reach the samādhi of a single mark and the samādhi of a single practice. If, in every place, you do not dwell on any mark, and within that mark give rise to neither love nor hatred, and neither grasp nor reject anything, and give no thought to matters of benefit, success, or failure, remaining at ease, tranquil, empty, and unburdened — this is called the samādhi of a single mark. If, in every place, whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, you maintain a single, direct mind throughout, never leaving the very site of awakening, so that a true pure land is genuinely accomplished — this is called the samādhi of a single practice. If a person is complete in both of these samādhis, it is like the earth already holding a seed within it, nurturing and sustaining it until the fruit finally ripens; the single mark and the single practice are exactly the same. My teaching of the Dharma now is like a timely rain, moistening the whole earth at once. Your own buddha-nature is like these very seeds; meeting with this moisture, every one of them will sprout and grow. Those who receive my teaching are certain to attain bodhi; those who practice according to it are certain to realize the wondrous fruit. Hear my verse: 'The ground of the mind holds every kind of seed; the universal rain causes every one of them to sprout. Once you have suddenly awakened to the very nature of the flower, the fruit of bodhi ripens of itself.'" Having spoken this verse, the Master said further, "This Dharma is not two, and this mind is exactly the same. This Way is pure, and free of every mark. Be careful, all of you, not to contemplate stillness, or to empty your own minds. This mind is fundamentally pure already, with nothing at all to be grasped or rejected. Each of you, strive on your own, and go well, according to your own conditions." At that time the assembled disciples bowed in homage and withdrew.

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan does not read Shenhui's composure here as coldness — it is, in fact, the very equanimity this sūtra has been teaching all along, the mind stained by neither praise nor blame, neither sorrow nor joy. What Huineng points out is not that Shenhui's stillness is wrong but that everyone else's grief reveals how far short of that same teaching they still remain: "after all these years in the mountains, what Way have you actually been cultivating?" His question — "are you grieving because you fear I do not know where I am going?" — reframes their tears as a failure of trust in something this whole sūtra has already established: that self-nature has no arising or ceasing, and Huineng's own death changes nothing about that fact.

This is the first point in the entire sūtra where the text names itself — "The Platform Sūtra of the Dharma Treasure" — and explains directly why the physical robe, unlike the teaching itself, will not be passed down any further: Bodhidharma's own transmission verse, quoted here, predicted "one flower opens into five petals," which Hanshan and the broader tradition read as a direct prophecy of the Five Houses of Chan that would eventually descend from Huineng's own disciples — including, as this sūtra has already shown two chapters earlier, through Xingsi and Huairang specifically. The robe's usefulness, on this account, was always tied to establishing an undisputed single line of transmission in the tradition's early, fragile generations; once the teaching had taken root widely enough to grow into five separate branches at once, a single physical object handed to a single named successor no longer served any purpose, and continuing to insist on it — as the rest of this sūtra has shown, in the assassination attempt three chapters earlier — had become actively dangerous.

3. The Return to Xinzhou, and the Lineage of Thirty-Three Patriarchs

Chinese Source

大師，七月八日忽謂門人曰：「吾欲歸新州，汝等速理舟楫。」大眾哀留甚堅。師曰：「諸佛出現，猶示涅槃。有來必去，理亦常然。吾此形骸，歸必有所。」眾曰：「師從此去，早晚可回。」師曰：「葉落歸根，來時無口。」又問曰：「正法眼藏，傳付何人？」師曰：「有道者得，無心者通。」又問：「後莫有難否？」師曰：「吾滅後五六年，當有一人來取吾首。聽吾記曰：『頭上養親，口裏須餐，遇滿之難，楊柳為官。』」又云：「吾去七十年，有二菩薩從東方來，一出家、一在家。同時興化，建立吾宗，締緝伽藍，昌隆法嗣。」問曰：「未知從上佛祖應現已來，傳授幾代？願垂開示。」師云：「古佛應世已無數量，不可計也。今以七佛為始，過去莊嚴劫，毘婆尸佛、尸棄佛、毘舍浮佛；今賢劫，拘留孫佛、拘那含牟尼佛、迦葉佛、釋迦文佛。是為七佛。 「已上七佛，今以釋迦文佛首傳。 「第一摩訶迦葉尊者、第二阿難尊者、第三商那和修尊者、第四優波毱多尊者、第五提多迦尊者、第六彌遮迦尊者、第七婆須蜜多尊者、第八佛馱難提尊者、第九伏馱蜜多尊者、第十脇尊者、十一富那夜奢尊者、十二馬鳴大士、十三迦毘摩羅尊者、十四龍樹大士、十五迦那提婆尊者、十六羅睺羅多尊者、十七僧伽難提尊者、十八伽耶舍多尊者、十九鳩摩羅多尊者、二十闍耶多尊者、二十一婆修盤頭尊者、二十二摩拏羅尊者、二十三鶴勒那尊者、二十四師子尊者、二十五婆舍斯多尊者、二十六不如蜜多尊者、二十七般若多羅尊者、二十八菩提達磨尊者(此土是為初祖)、二十九慧可大師、三十僧璨大師、三十一道信大師、三十二弘忍大師。 「惠能是為三十三祖。從上諸祖，各有稟承。汝等向後，遞代流傳毋令乖誤。」

Ru-Yi Translation

On the eighth day of the seventh month, the Great Master suddenly said to his disciples, "I wish to return to Xinzhou — make ready a boat for me quickly." The assembly begged him most earnestly to remain. The Master said, "Every buddha, on appearing in this world, still displays the entering of nirvāṇa in the end; whatever has come must go — this is the constant principle of things. This physical frame of mine must return somewhere in the end." The assembly said, "If the Master goes now, will you come back to us again, sooner or later?" The Master said, "When leaves fall, they return to the root — when I return this time, there will be no mouth to speak with." They asked further, "To whom will the treasury of the true Dharma-eye be entrusted?" The Master said, "Whoever has the Way will attain it; whoever has no mind will penetrate it." They asked, "Will there be any difficulty for you afterward?" The Master said, "Five or six years after I have passed away, someone will come to take my head. Hear my own prophecy on this: 'Nourishing a parent above the head, needing to eat with the mouth below — when the calamity of "Man" comes, the officials will be named Willow and Poplar.'" He said further, "Seventy years after I am gone, two bodhisattvas will come from the east, one who has left home and one still a layperson; together, at the same time, they will revive the teaching, establish my own school, found monasteries, and cause the Dharma-descendants to flourish." Someone asked, "We do not know how many generations the Dharma has been transmitted through, from the buddhas and patriarchs of old, manifesting themselves down to now — we beg you to instruct us on this." The Master said, "The buddhas who have appeared in this world since ancient times are beyond number, impossible to count in full. Let us begin now with the seven buddhas: in the past Glorious Kalpa, the buddhas Vipaśyin, Śikhin, and Viśvabhū; in the present Auspicious Kalpa, the buddhas Krakucchanda, Kanakamuni, Kāśyapa, and Śākyamuni — these are the seven buddhas. "From these seven buddhas, it was Śākyamuni who first transmitted the Dharma, to the following patriarchs in succession: first, the Venerable Mahākāśyapa; second, the Venerable Ānanda; third, the Venerable Śāṇakavāsa; fourth, the Venerable Upagupta; fifth, the Venerable Dhītika; sixth, the Venerable Miccaka; seventh, the Venerable Vasumitra; eighth, the Venerable Buddhanandi; ninth, the Venerable Buddhamitra; tenth, the Venerable Pārśva; eleventh, the Venerable Puṇyayaśas; twelfth, the great being Aśvaghoṣa; thirteenth, the Venerable Kapimala; fourteenth, the great being Nāgārjuna; fifteenth, the Venerable Kāṇadeva; sixteenth, the Venerable Rāhulata; seventeenth, the Venerable Saṃghanandi; eighteenth, the Venerable Gayaśāta; nineteenth, the Venerable Kumārata; twentieth, the Venerable Jayata; twenty-first, the Venerable Vasubandhu; twenty-second, the Venerable Manorhita; twenty-third, the Venerable Haklenayaśas; twenty-fourth, the Venerable Siṃha; twenty-fifth, the Venerable Basiasita; twenty-sixth, the Venerable Puṇyamitra; twenty-seventh, the Venerable Prajñātāra; twenty-eighth, the Venerable Bodhidharma (the first patriarch here in this land); twenty-ninth, the great teacher Huike; thirtieth, the great teacher Sengcan; thirty-first, the great teacher Daoxin; thirty-second, the great teacher Hongren. "Huineng is the thirty-third patriarch. Every patriarch from the very beginning has received this transmission in succession, each from the one before. From this day forward, you too must hand it down through the generations to come — let there be no error or confusion in it."

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads the cryptic prophecy about "someone who will come to take my head" as a riddle the sūtra itself expects a later reader to see resolved, not left hanging: the tradition records that within the predicted years, a would-be grave robber was in fact caught attempting to steal the Sixth Patriarch's mummified head, and that the local officials who handled the case were surnamed Yang and Liu — poplar and willow, exactly as the prophecy names them. Hanshan treats this less as a puzzle to be admired for its cleverness than as one more instance of the plain, matter-of-fact register this sūtra uses even for its most extraordinary claims: the same voice that has spent nine chapters redefining meditation and self-nature states a prophecy about its own posthumous body with no more drama than it used to describe a lamp and its light.

The thirty-three-patriarch lineage, running from the Buddha through Mahākāśyapa — who received the Buddha's own wordless transmission at Vulture Peak, sometimes told as the story of a single held-up flower — down through Bodhidharma and the five Chinese patriarchs before Huineng, is the last instance in this sūtra of a single, individually named chain of transmission. Hanshan reads its placement here as deliberate: immediately after Huineng has just explained why the physical robe stops with him, the sūtra recites, one final time, the unbroken personal chain the robe once symbolized — as if to preserve the lineage's memory in words precisely at the moment the practice of marking it with an object comes to an end.

4. The True Buddha of Self-Nature, and the Parinirvāṇa

Chinese Source

大師，先天二年癸丑歲八月初三日(是年十二月改元開元)，於國恩寺齋罷，謂諸徒眾曰：「汝等各依位坐，吾與汝別。」法海白言：「和尚！留何教法，令後代迷人得見佛性？」師言：「汝等諦聽！後代迷人，若識眾生，即是佛性；若不識眾生，萬劫覓佛難逢。吾今教汝。識自心眾生，見自心佛性。欲求見佛，但識眾生。只為眾生迷佛，非是佛迷眾生。自性若悟，眾生是佛；自性若迷，佛是眾生。 自性平等，眾生是佛；自性邪險，佛是眾生。汝等心若險曲，即佛在眾生中；一念平直。即是眾生成佛。我心自有佛，自佛是真佛。 自若無佛心，何處求真佛？汝等自心是佛，更莫狐疑。外無一物而能建立，皆是本心生萬種法。故經云：『心生種種法生，心滅種種法滅。』吾今留一偈與汝等別，名自性真佛偈。後代之人，識此偈意，自見本心，自成佛道。偈曰： 「真如自性是真佛，　邪見三毒是魔王， 邪迷之時魔在舍，　正見之時佛在堂。 性中邪見三毒生，　即是魔王來住舍， 正見自除三毒心，　魔變成佛真無假。 法身報身及化身，　三身本來是一身， 若向性中能自見，　即是成佛菩提因。 本從化身生淨性，　淨性常在化身中， 性使化身行正道，　當來圓滿真無窮。 婬性本是淨性因，　除婬即是淨性身， 性中各自離五欲，　見性剎那即是真。 今生若遇頓教門，　忽悟自性見世尊， 若欲修行覓作佛，　不知何處擬求真？ 若能心中自見真，　有真即是成佛因， 不見自性外覓佛，　起心總是大癡人。 頓教法門今已留，　救度世人須自修， 報汝當來學道者，　不作此見大悠悠。」 師說偈已，告曰：「汝等好住。吾滅度後，莫作世情悲泣雨淚，受人弔問、身著孝服，非吾弟子，亦非正法。但識自本心，見自本性，無動無靜，無生無滅，無去無來，無是無非，無住無往。恐汝等心迷，不會吾意，今再囑汝，令汝見性。吾滅度後，依此修行，如吾在日；若違吾教，縱吾在世，亦無有益。」復說偈曰： 「兀兀不修善，　騰騰不造惡， 寂寂斷見聞，　蕩蕩心無著。」 師說偈已，端坐至三更，忽謂門人曰：「吾行矣！」奄然遷化。于時異香滿室，白虹屬地，林木變白，禽獸哀鳴。十一月，廣韶新三郡官僚，洎門人僧俗，爭迎真身，莫決所之。乃焚香禱曰：「香煙指處，師所歸焉。」時香煙直貫曹溪。十一月十三日，遷神龕併所傳衣鉢而回。次年七月出龕，弟子方辯以香泥上之，門人憶念取首之記，仍以鐵葉漆布固護師頸入塔。忽於塔內白光出現，直上衝天，三日始散。韶州奏聞，奉勅立碑，紀師道行。 師春秋七十有六，年二十四傳衣，三十九祝髮，說法利生三十七載，嗣法四十三人，悟道超凡者莫知其數。達磨所傳信衣(西域屈眴布也)，中宗賜磨衲寶鉢，及方辯塑師真相，并道具，永鎮寶林道場。留傳《壇經》以顯宗旨，興隆三寶，普利群生者。

Ru-Yi Translation

On the third day of the eighth month of the second year of the Xiantian era, in the year guichou (that same year, in the twelfth month, the era name was changed to Kaiyuan), the Great Master, after the meal at Guo'en Temple, said to his assembled disciples, "Each of you, sit according to your place — I will now take my leave of you." Fahai said, "Venerable monk, what teaching will you leave behind, so that deluded people of later ages may come to see buddha-nature?" The Master said, "Listen carefully, all of you. Deluded people of later ages, if they recognize sentient beings, will thereby see buddha-nature; if they do not recognize sentient beings, they could search for the Buddha ten thousand kalpas and never find him. I will now teach you to recognize the sentient beings within your own mind, and see the buddha-nature within your own mind. If you wish to see the Buddha, simply recognize sentient beings — for it is only sentient beings who are deluded about the Buddha; the Buddha is never deluded about sentient beings. When self-nature is awakened, sentient beings are the Buddha; when self-nature is deluded, the Buddha is a sentient being. When self-nature is even and equal, sentient beings are the Buddha; when self-nature is deviant and perilous, the Buddha is a sentient being. If your own mind is deviant and crooked, then the Buddha is already hidden within a sentient being; the moment a single thought becomes even and upright, that sentient being becomes the Buddha. My own mind already has the Buddha within it of itself, and this buddha that is my own self is the true Buddha. If you yourself have no mind of buddhahood, where else could you ever go looking for a true buddha? Your own mind is itself already the Buddha — have no further doubt about this at all. Nothing whatsoever, outside your own mind, is able to establish anything; every kind of phenomenon is given birth to by the fundamental mind alone. This is why the sūtra says, 'When the mind is born, every kind of thing is born with it; when the mind ceases, every kind of thing ceases with it.' I now leave you a final verse, as I take my leave of you, called the Verse of the True Buddha of Self-Nature. Whoever, in later ages, recognizes the meaning of this verse will see their own fundamental mind for themselves, and accomplish the buddha-path for themselves." The verse said: "The self-nature of true suchness is the true buddha; wrong views and the three poisons are the demon-king. At the time of wrong views and delusion, the demon dwells within the house; at the time of right views, the buddha sits within the very same hall. When wrong views and the three poisons arise within self-nature, this is exactly the demon-king coming to dwell within the house; when right views remove the mind of the three poisons of themselves, the demon is transformed into the buddha, truly and without any falsehood at all. The Dharma-body, the reward-body, and the transformation-body — these three bodies were, from the very beginning, a single body. If you are able to see this for yourself within your own nature, this is itself the very cause of becoming a buddha, the seed of bodhi. The transformation-body originally gives rise to the pure nature, and this pure nature constantly dwells within the transformation-body; when this nature causes the transformation-body to walk the true path, the reward to come will be perfectly complete, true and without end. The nature of desire is, at its root, the very cause of the pure nature; remove desire, and this is itself the body of the pure nature. When self-nature, each for itself, is free of the five desires, the very instant one sees this nature is itself the truth. If, in this present life, you meet with the gate of the sudden teaching, you will suddenly awaken to your own self-nature and behold the World-Honored One directly. But if you wish to cultivate practice in search of becoming a buddha, and do not know where else to seek this truth — if you are able to see the truth within your own mind for yourself, having this truth is itself the cause of becoming a buddha. Not seeing your own nature, and searching for the Buddha somewhere outside instead — to give rise to such a thought at all is nothing but the mark of a very great fool. The gate of the sudden teaching, I have now left behind for you; to save and deliver the people of this world, one must cultivate for oneself. I report this now to those who will study the Way in ages yet to come: do not fail to hold this view, and drift on carelessly through your lives." Having spoken this verse, the Master said further, "Live well and stay in peace, all of you. After I have passed into extinction, do not act as ordinary people do, weeping and shedding tears out of worldly feeling, receiving condolences from others, and wearing the garments of mourning — this is not to be my disciple at all, nor is it the true Dharma. Simply recognize your own fundamental mind, and see your own fundamental nature, which has no movement and no stillness, no arising and no ceasing, no going and no coming, no right and no wrong, no dwelling and no departing. I fear your minds are still deluded, and have not fully grasped my meaning, and so I charge you once more, so that you may truly see your own nature. After I have passed into extinction, practice according to this teaching, exactly as though I were still in the world; but if you go against my teaching, then even if I remained in the world, it would be of no benefit to you at all." And he spoke a further verse: "Utterly still, cultivating no good at all; utterly at ease, creating no evil at all; in complete silence, cutting off all seeing and hearing; vast and empty, the mind attached to nothing at all." Having spoken this verse, the Master sat upright until the third watch of the night, when he suddenly said to his disciples, "I am going now," and passed away all at once, without any struggle at all. At that time an extraordinary fragrance filled the room, a white rainbow touched down to the earth, the trees of the forest turned white, and the birds and animals cried out in mourning. In the eleventh month, the officials of the three prefectures of Guangzhou, Shaozhou, and Xinzhou, together with the Master's monastic and lay disciples, all competed to receive his true body, unable to agree on where it should go. They then burned incense and prayed, saying, "Wherever the smoke of this incense points, there the Master shall return." At that time the smoke rose and drifted straight toward Caoxi. On the thirteenth day of the eleventh month, his spirit-shrine was moved there, together with the robe and bowl he had transmitted, and returned to Caoxi. The following year, on the twenty-fifth day of the seventh month, the shrine was opened, and his disciple Fangbian covered the body with fragrant clay to preserve it. His disciples, remembering the prophecy about someone coming to take his head, first wrapped his neck securely in iron sheets and lacquered cloth before placing him in the stūpa. Suddenly a white light appeared from within the stūpa, rising straight up into the sky, and did not disperse for three days. The prefecture of Shaozhou reported this to the throne, and by imperial decree a stele was erected, recording the Master's own life and conduct. The Master lived to the age of seventy-six. He received the robe at the age of twenty-four, and took full ordination at thirty-nine. He taught the Dharma and benefited beings for thirty-seven years. Forty-three disciples received his sanction as having inherited the Dharma, and those who awakened to the Way and transcended the ordinary state were beyond number. Bodhidharma's own transmitted robe of good faith (a length of fine kaṣāya cloth from the Western Regions), together with the ceremonial robe and the precious bowl bestowed by Emperor Zhongzong, and the true likeness sculpted by Fangbian, along with the Master's other ritual implements, remain permanently at Baolin Temple, guarded by those who tend the stūpa. The Platform Sūtra has been handed down to make this teaching's essential meaning clear, for the flourishing of the Three Treasures and the universal benefit of all beings.

Reading note — after Hanshan

Hanshan reads this closing teaching as the sūtra's deliberate return to its own opening words: "the self-nature of bodhi is fundamentally pure — simply use this mind, and you go straight to buddhahood," spoken at Dafan Temple in the very first chapter, and now, at the very end, restated as "my own mind already has the Buddha within it of itself, and this buddha that is my own self is the true Buddha." Nothing new is being introduced in this final teaching; the sūtra closes exactly where it began, which Hanshan takes as the clearest possible signal that everything between these two points — the biography, the doctrine, the dozens of individual encounters — has been elaboration of a single claim, never a departure from it.

On the verse's most startling line — "the nature of desire is, at its root, the very cause of the pure nature" — Hanshan reads this as the same non-dual move this sūtra has made repeatedly with affliction and bodhi, ordinary person and buddha: desire is not something to be eliminated from outside as a foreign contaminant, but something whose own nature, seen clearly, already is the purity being sought. This is not license, on Hanshan's reading, but precision — the verse still says plainly to "remove desire," only locating where that removal actually happens, within the nature of desire itself rather than through suppression imposed on it from elsewhere. As for the death scene's fragrance, white rainbow, and mourning animals, Hanshan treats these as the conventional furniture of a hagiography, worth noting but not worth dwelling on; what he returns to instead is the instruction given just before them — no mourning clothes, no ordinary grief — as the sūtra's genuinely final teaching, asking its readers to extend the doctrine of no-arising and no-ceasing to the one moment where it is hardest to hold: the death of the very teacher who taught it.
