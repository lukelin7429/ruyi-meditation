Desire and the Dharma

The Brahmin makes one final attempt, asking whether there is anything at all that is not worldly disputation — and the Buddha answers with something that is neither a position nor its denial, neither speech nor its absence. The Brahmin, unable to find any further foothold, falls silent and withdraws, privately admitting to himself that the Buddha's own teaching lies entirely outside the framework he had mastered. Mahāmati then asks the question this whole episode was building toward: what exactly distinguishes attachment that produces mere desire from attachment that produces the Dharma? The Buddha's answer turns not on what is grasped, but on why.

Is There Anything That Is Not Worldly Disputation?

Chinese Source

「復次，大慧！爾時世論婆羅門，復問我言：『頗有非世論者不？我是一切外道之宗，說種種句味、因緣、譬喻莊嚴。』我復報言：『婆羅門！有非汝有者。非為，非宗，非說，非不說種種句味，非不因譬莊嚴。』婆羅門言：『何等為非世論，非非宗，非非說？』我時報言：『婆羅門！有非世論，汝諸外道所不能知，以於外性不實妄想虛偽計著故。謂妄想不生，覺了有無自心現量。妄想不生，不受外塵。妄想永息，是名非世論。此是我法，非汝有也。婆羅門！略說彼識，若來若去，若死若生，若樂若苦，若溺若見，若觸若著，種種相。若和合相續，若受，若因計著。婆羅門！如是等比者，是汝等世論，非是我有。』大慧！世論婆羅門，作如是問，我如是答。彼即默然，不辭而退。思自通處，作是念言：『沙門釋子，出於通外，說無生無相無因，覺自妄想現，妄想不生。』大慧！此即是汝向所問我：『何故說習近世論種種辯說，攝受貪欲，不攝受法？』」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, Mahāmati, at that time the Brahmin steeped in worldly disputation asked me still further: 'Is there, then, anything at all that is not worldly disputation? I myself am the very foundation of every outsider school, expounding every kind of clever phrase, causal argument, and simile.' I answered him again: 'Brahmin, there is something you yourself do not have. It is neither a doing, nor a foundation, nor a teaching, nor is it the non-teaching of manifold clever phrases; nor is it the absence of causal argument and simile.' The Brahmin said: 'What, then, is this thing that is neither worldly disputation, nor not-a-foundation, nor not-a-teaching?' I answered him at once: 'Brahmin, there is something that is not worldly disputation, which none of you outsiders is able to know, precisely because you are attached, through unreal false thought, to what is external as though it were real. It is that false thought does not arise, that one is aware that existence and non-existence are both alike one's own mind's manifestation; false thought does not arise, and one does not take in what is external; false thought is forever put to rest — this is what is called that which is not worldly disputation. This is my own Dharma; it is not something you yourself have. Brahmin, to state it briefly: that consciousness — whether it comes or goes, whether it dies or is born, whether it is pleasant or painful, whether one sinks into it or merely observes it, whether one touches it or clings to it, in every one of its manifold marks; whether framed as combination and continuity, as sensation, or as attachment to a cause — Brahmin, all such things as these are your own worldly disputation; they are not what I myself have.' Mahāmati, the Brahmin steeped in worldly disputation asked in just this way, and I answered in just this way. He then fell silent, and withdrew without taking his leave, reflecting privately to himself, thinking: 'This śramaṇa, this child of the Śākyas, stands entirely outside what I have mastered — he speaks of the unborn, the markless, and the causeless, aware that this is his own false thought's manifestation, [and, aware of this,] false thought does not arise [for him].' Mahāmati, this is precisely what you were asking me just now: why I said that pursuing worldly disputation closely, with all its manifold argumentation, only leads one to take on desire, and not to take on the Dharma."

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

The commentators note that the Buddha's answer to the Brahmin's final, most pointed question is deliberately constructed to escape every category the Brahmin has just tried to force it into: "非為非宗，乃至譬喻莊嚴皆言非者，謂異其論，雖微啟之而終未與說實" — "'neither a doing, nor a foundation,' down through 'nor causal argument and simile' — every one of these is denied, showing that [what the Buddha teaches] differs entirely from [the Brahmin's own] system. Though this opens a small crack for him, the Buddha still has not, even here, told him the thing itself directly." Even the negations themselves are not offered as a rival position to be argued for; they simply refuse, one by one, every slot the Brahmin's own framework has prepared in advance for an answer to fill.

On the Brahmin's silent withdrawal, the commentators read his private reflection as a genuine, if incomplete, moment of insight rather than mere defeat: "彼論既屈，復慚而退，亦不暇辭，且言思自通處...此皆外道退而有省之言，以佛所說求諸己而不得，始知出於自通之外" — "his own doctrine having been thoroughly exhausted, he withdrew in shame, not even pausing to take proper leave, reflecting instead on what he had mastered himself... these are the words of an outsider who, in retreat, has genuinely begun to reflect: searching within his own system for what the Buddha had just taught, and finding nothing there, he came at last to realize that it lay entirely outside what he himself had mastered." The commentators add, of his final private words — "觀其所領，亦足以見其有所得矣" — "observing what he took away from the encounter, one can see that he did, in fact, gain something real from it" — the debate ends not in humiliation for its own sake, but in the beginning of the Brahmin's own genuine reflection.

Attachment That Produces Desire, and Attachment That Produces the Dharma

Chinese Source

大慧白佛言：「世尊！攝受貪欲及法，有何句義？」

佛告大慧：「善哉，善哉！汝乃能為未來眾生，思惟諮問如是句義。諦聽，諦聽！善思念之，當為汝說。」

大慧白佛言：「唯然受教。」

佛告大慧：「所謂貪者，若取、若捨、若觸、若味，繫著外塵，墮二邊見。復生苦陰，生老病死憂悲苦惱。如是諸患，皆從愛起，斯由習近世論，及世論者，我及諸佛說名為貪。是名攝受貪欲，不攝受法。大慧！云何攝受法？謂：善覺知自心現量，見人無我及法無我相，妄想不生。善知上下(丹上)地，離心意意識，一切諸佛智慧灌頂，具足攝受十無盡句，於一切法無開發自在，是名為法。所謂不墮一切見、一切虛偽、一切妄想、一切性、一切二邊。大慧！多有外道癡人，墮於二邊若常若斷，非黠慧者。受無因論，則起常見；外因壞因緣非性，則起斷見。大慧！我不見生住滅故，說名為法。大慧！是名貪欲及法。汝及餘菩薩摩訶薩，應當修學。」

爾時，世尊欲重宣此義而說偈言：
「一切世間論，　外道虛妄說，
妄見作所作，　彼則無自宗。

惟我一自宗，　離於作所作，
為諸弟子說，　遠離諸世論。

心量不可見，　不觀察二心，
攝所攝非性，　斷常二俱離，
乃至心流轉，　是則為世論。

妄想不轉者，　是人見自心，
來者謂事生，　去者事不現。

明了知去來，　妄想不復生，
有常及無常，　所作無所作，
此世他世等，　斯皆世論通。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Mahāmati said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what precise meaning distinguishes taking on desire from taking on the Dharma?"

The Buddha told Mahāmati: "Excellent, excellent! That you are able, for the sake of sentient beings in ages to come, to reflect on and inquire into a meaning such as this. Listen well! Listen well! Consider this carefully — I shall now explain it to you."

Mahāmati said to the Buddha: "I gladly receive your teaching."

The Buddha told Mahāmati: "As for desire: it is grasping, or rejecting, or touching, or savoring, bound in attachment to what is external, falling into the view of one of the two extremes. It further gives rise to the aggregate of suffering — birth, old age, sickness, death, sorrow, grief, suffering, and affliction. Every such affliction as this arises out of craving, and it is precisely through pursuing worldly disputation closely, and those steeped in it, that this comes about — this is what I and every buddha call desire. This is what is meant by taking on desire rather than taking on the Dharma. Mahāmati, what is meant by taking on the Dharma? It is rightly perceiving the very measure of one's own mind's manifestation, seeing the mark of the no-self of persons and of dharmas, with no false thought arising at all; rightly knowing every ground, from lowest to highest; being apart from mind, will, and consciousness; receiving the crown-anointing of every buddha's own wisdom; being fully complete in embracing the ten inexhaustible statements; and having, with respect to every dharma, a mastery that requires no deliberate exertion at all — this is what is called the Dharma. That is to say: not falling into any view whatsoever, into any falsehood whatsoever, into any false thought whatsoever, into [attachment to] any [fixed] nature whatsoever, or into either of the two extremes. Mahāmati, there are many outsiders, ignorant people, who fall into one of the two extremes, whether permanence or annihilation — they are simply not the wise. Accepting a theory of no cause gives rise to the view of permanence; [accepting] that an external cause is destroyed, and that cause and condition themselves have no fixed nature, gives rise to the view of annihilation. Mahāmati, it is because I do not see any arising, abiding, or ceasing at all that this is called the Dharma. Mahāmati, these are what is called desire and the Dharma. You, and the other bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, should diligently train in this distinction."

Then the World-Honored One, wishing to restate this meaning, spoke in verse: "Every worldly discourse is the outsiders' own false teaching;
falsely seeing a doer and a deed, they have, in truth, no genuine foundation of their own at all.

Only my own single foundation is apart from any doer and any deed;
it is this that I teach to every one of my disciples, far removed from every kind of worldly discourse.

The measure of mind cannot itself be seen; do not observe it through either of the two [kinds of grasping] mind;
the grasped and the grasper both alike have no fixed nature; permanence and annihilation are both alike left behind —
yet so long as mind still turns and flows on at all, this itself is still worldly discourse.

One in whom false thought no longer turns is the one who truly sees his own mind;
'coming,' [for such a one,] means merely that a matter arises; 'going' means merely that it no longer appears.

Clearly understanding coming and going in just this way, false thought no longer arises at all;
permanence, and impermanence; what is made, and what is not made;
this world, another world, and every such pairing besides — all of this is nothing but the domain of worldly discourse."

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

The commentators locate the precise dividing line between desire and Dharma not in what is grasped, but in what the grasping is grounded in and aimed toward: attachment "繫著外塵，墮二邊見" ("bound in attachment to what is external, falling into the view of one of the two extremes") is desire, while the very same energy directed toward "善覺知自心現量，見人無我及法無我相" ("rightly perceiving the very measure of one's own mind's manifestation, seeing the mark of the no-self of persons and of dharmas") is the Dharma. The commentators draw out the closing line's own definition as the cleanest possible statement of the distinction: "我不見生住滅故，說名為法" — "it is because I do not see any arising, abiding, or ceasing at all that this is called the Dharma" — the Dharma is defined not by a positive content opposed to the outsiders' own claims, but precisely by the absence of the fixed arising and ceasing that both desire and every outsider position alike are built upon.

On the closing verse's warning that "so long as mind still turns and flows on at all, this itself is still worldly discourse," the commentators tie this directly back to the criterion running through the entire episode: even a correct-sounding philosophical position, if it still involves mind actively turning toward and grasping at some object as fixed, has not yet escaped the category of worldly disputation — it is only "one in whom false thought no longer turns" who has genuinely stepped outside it. This is why the Buddha's own answers to the Brahmin, throughout the whole exchange, never once offered a competing thesis to be argued for: any thesis defended through argument, however correct its content, would still belong to the very mind-in-motion the sūtra identifies as worldly discourse's actual defining feature, rather than to the stillness that lies genuinely outside it.

Part 32 Complete

This closes Part 32: the Brahmin's final question met with an answer that fits no category his own system had prepared for it, his silent withdrawal and private admission that the Buddha's teaching lay entirely outside what he had mastered, and the precise line the Buddha draws between attachment that produces desire and attachment that produces the Dharma — not by what is grasped, but by whether mind is still turning toward something fixed at all.
