The Assembly at Mount Laṅkā, and Mahāmati's Hundred and Eight Questions

The sūtra opens on the summit of Mount Laṅkā, where the bodhisattva Mahāmati rises from the assembly, pays homage to the Buddha in verse, and then asks — in one unbroken chain of terse, five-character questions running to well over a hundred lines — after nearly everything a student of the Mahāyāna could want explained: the storehouse consciousness, the bodhisattva grounds, the nature of a buddha's body, the making of the world, food and desire, kingship, meat-eating, the icchantika, and dozens of questions besides. The list is not a random inventory. Sòng Lè and Rúqí's commentary reads it as the sūtra's own table of contents, organized beneath a single fourfold frame — the five dharmas, the three own-natures, the eight consciousnesses, and the two kinds of no-self — that the rest of the text will spend four fascicles unpacking.

The Assembly at Mount Laṅkā

Chinese Source

如是我聞：一時佛住南海濱楞伽山頂，種種寶華以為莊嚴，與大比丘僧及大菩薩眾俱，從彼種種異佛剎來。是諸菩薩摩訶薩，無量三昧自在之力，神通遊戲，大慧菩薩摩訶薩而為上首。一切諸佛手灌其頂，自心現境界，善解其義。種種眾生、種種心色，無量度門，隨類普現。於五法自性、識、二種無我，究竟通達。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard. At one time the Buddha was dwelling at the summit of Mount Laṅkā on the shore of the southern sea, adorned with every kind of jeweled flower, together with a great assembly of monks and a great assembly of bodhisattvas who had come there from many different buddha-lands. These bodhisattva-mahāsattvas sported freely in spiritual powers through the sovereign strength of immeasurable samādhis, with the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Mahāmati foremost among them. Every buddha had anointed his crown with their own hand; he understood well the meaning of every realm manifested by his own mind; he appeared everywhere, according to kind, through immeasurable gateways of salvation, for every kind of sentient being and every kind of mental disposition; and he had penetrated, to the very end, the five dharmas, own-nature, the consciousnesses, and the two kinds of no-self.

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

Before reaching a single word of the sūtra proper, Zongle and Ruqi's preface to the title glosses "Laṅkā" itself: 不可往, "cannot be reached" — a city on the summit of a mountain in the southern sea that "no one without spiritual powers can reach," so that the Buddha's speaking there is itself already a statement about the teaching's altitude: this is buddha-territory (佛境界), reached by what the place is, not merely what is said in it. Their gloss on the品 title that follows, "一切佛語心品" (the chapter of every buddha's mind-teaching), makes the same point about content rather than geography: the sūtra's actual subject matter, they write, comes down to four things — 五法、三自性、八識、二無我, the five dharmas, the three own-natures, the eight consciousnesses, and the two kinds of no-self — and it is called simply "mind" because these four are "一經之綱目、究心之精要," the outline of the whole sūtra and the essential point of investigating mind itself. They cite the *Ten-Fascicle Laṅkāvatāra*'s own description of these teachings directly: "是一切諸佛菩薩入自心境，離所行相，稱真實義，諸佛教心也" — this is where every buddha and bodhisattva enters the domain of their own mind, apart from any object of activity, matching ultimate meaning exactly: it is what every buddha teaches as mind.

On the opening description of Mahāmati himself, the commentary reads each phrase as marking a specific attainment rather than generic praise. "一切諸佛手灌其頂" (every buddha has anointed his crown with their own hand) they gloss as 明其位居等覺，當授佛位 — showing that his rank is equal to a buddha's own enlightenment, awaiting only the formal transmission of buddhahood itself, the same consecration a crown prince receives before taking the throne. "自心現境界，善解其義" is glossed as praise of Mahāmati's own understanding specifically — 大慧善解，其智超勝也, "Mahāmati understands this well; his wisdom surpasses [the rest of the assembly]" — which is why, of the entire gathering, it is Mahāmati and no one else who is about to ask the questions that structure everything that follows.

Mahāmati's Homage in Verse

Chinese Source

爾時大慧菩薩與摩帝菩薩，俱遊一切諸佛剎土，承佛神力，從坐而起偏袒右肩，右膝著地，合掌恭敬，以偈讚曰：

「世間離生滅，　猶如虛空華，
智不得有無，　而興大悲心。

一切法如幻，　遠離於心識，
智不得有無，　而興大悲心。

遠離於斷常，　世間恒如夢，
智不得有無，　而興大悲心。

知人法無我，　煩惱及爾炎，
常清淨無相，　而興大悲心。

一切無涅槃，　無有涅槃佛，
無有佛涅槃，　遠離覺所覺。

若有若無有，　是二悉俱離，
牟尼寂靜觀，　是則遠離生，
是名為不取，　今世後世淨。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the bodhisattva Mahāmati, together with the bodhisattva Matiḥ, was roaming through the lands of every buddha; and now, sustained by the Buddha's own spiritual power, he rose from his seat, bared his right shoulder, set his right knee upon the ground, joined his palms in reverence, and praised the Buddha in verse, saying:

"The world is free of arising and ceasing,
just like a flower conjured in the sky —
wisdom finds in it neither existence nor non-existence,
and yet gives rise to great compassion.

All dharmas are like an illusion,
far removed from mind and consciousness —
wisdom finds in them neither existence nor non-existence,
and yet gives rise to great compassion.

Far removed from annihilation and eternalism,
the world is forever like a dream —
wisdom finds in it neither existence nor non-existence,
and yet gives rise to great compassion.

Knowing the no-self of persons and of dharmas,
and the afflictions, and what is to be known —
forever pure and without any mark,
and yet [wisdom] gives rise to great compassion.

There is no nirvāṇa at all,
no buddha who attains nirvāṇa,
and no nirvāṇa that a buddha attains —
far removed from the knower and the known.

Whether it exists or does not exist,
both of these are equally left behind.
The sage's silent contemplation
is thus far removed from arising —
this is called taking hold of nothing,
pure in this life and in every life to come."

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

The commentary reads the refrain "wisdom finds in it neither existence nor non-existence, and yet gives rise to great compassion" as the whole verse's argument compressed into one clause, repeated three times because it needs to survive three separate objects — birth-and-death, all dharmas, and the world itself — without weakening: 一切世間眾生背覺合塵，流轉生死，而起妄想心識，復計斷常二見，無由出離. All worldly beings, they write, turn their backs on awakening and unite with the dust of objects, cycling through birth and death, giving rise to the discriminating mind and its false thoughts, and further conceiving the two views of annihilation and eternalism — with no way out. It is precisely because the Buddha's wisdom sees these things as neither existent nor non-existent that compassion for beings still caught between the two extremes can arise at all; a wisdom that had settled into either extreme would have nothing left to be compassionate about.

On "the afflictions, and what is to be known" (煩惱及爾焰), the commentary makes a small but load-bearing terminological point: 爾焰 is not a Chinese phrase but a transliterated Sanskrit term, and it names the hindrance to knowledge (智障) precisely as 煩惱 names the hindrance of affliction (惑障) — the verse is naming both members of Buddhism's standard two-hindrances pair, not one term followed by a vague gesture at "and so on." Their gloss adds a further turn that will recur across this sūtra: even wisdom itself, if a practitioner clings to it, becomes a hindrance — 若於智生著，智亦成障 — so that "常清淨無相" (forever pure and without any mark) has to describe the Buddha's insight into the emptiness of the wisdom that sees emptiness, not merely the emptiness of what it sees.

On the closing lines' apparent contradiction — no nirvāṇa at all, and yet a "silent contemplation" that is "far removed from arising," achieving a purity that spans every life to come — Zongle and Ruqi trace the logic precisely: 佛與眾生同具此理，尚無生死可斷，焉有涅槃可證. The Buddha and sentient beings equally possess this same principle [of intrinsic quiescence]; if there is, in the deepest sense, no birth-and-death to be cut off in the first place, how could there be any nirvāṇa left over to attain? "No nirvāṇa-buddha" (無有涅槃佛) denies that a person could exist apart from the truth he realizes; "no buddha-nirvāṇa" (無有佛涅槃) denies, symmetrically, that the truth could exist apart from the person who realizes it — person and principle are inseparable in both directions, which is exactly what "far removed from the knower and the known" then states directly.

The Hundred and Eight Questions

Chinese Source

爾時大慧菩薩偈讚佛已，自說姓名：「我名為大慧，通達於大乘，今以百八義，仰諮尊中上。」世間解之士，聞彼所說偈，觀察一切眾，告諸佛子言：「汝等諸佛子，今皆恣所問，我當為汝說，自覺之境界。」

爾時大慧菩薩摩訶薩，承佛所聽，頂禮佛足，合掌恭敬以偈問曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Having praised the Buddha in verse, Mahāmati then spoke his own name: "My name is Mahāmati; I have penetrated the Great Vehicle. Now, with a hundred and eight matters, I look up and ask you, most honored one." The Knower of the World, hearing the verse he had spoken, surveyed the whole assembly and said to all the buddhas' children: "All of you buddhas' children, ask now whatever you wish; I will explain to you the domain of self-realization."

Then the bodhisattva-mahāsattva Mahāmati, granted leave by the Buddha to speak, bowed his head to the Buddha's feet, joined his palms in reverence, and asked in verse:

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

The commentary glosses this entire chain almost line by line — nearly every five-character phrase gets its own short paraphrase — which is itself a claim about the passage: that it is not decoration but the sūtra's actual outline, stated in advance. Their running gloss on 楞伽 in the title already promised this ("the essential matter of the whole sūtra comes down to the five dharmas, the three own-natures, the eight consciousnesses, and the two kinds of no-self"), and reading through their line-by-line notes confirms it: "云何名為藏" (what is called the storehouse) is glossed simply 問第八藏識, a direct question about the eighth, storehouse consciousness; "云何建立相，及與非我義" is glossed 問有相及無我相, tying the pair straight to the two-no-self doctrine; the long run of questions about kings, gods, elephants, mountains, and world-shapes is glossed collectively as belonging to what the five-dharmas scheme calls "name and mark" (名相), the raw content any teaching has to discriminate before wisdom (正智) and suchness (如如) can be reached. The list reads like chaos only if one misses that it is being organized by a classification scheme not yet named.

The commentary also flags, without smoothing over, the list's own numerical mismatch: the questions plainly number in the hundreds when counted individually, yet Mahāmati has just announced "百八義" — a hundred and eight matters. Sòng Lè and Rúqí do not try to force an exact count; a note appended after the Buddha's answering verses states plainly that "所問所領皆無倫次，故不可以定數開合而論之" — what is asked and what is echoed back follow no fixed sequence, so the number cannot be settled by any particular way of splitting the questions apart. The number 108 is doing symbolic rather than arithmetic work here — the commentary elsewhere ties it explicitly to the 108 afflictions (煩惱) countered by 108 dharma-gates — and the questions themselves are the sūtra's full scope stated at the threshold, not a list meant to be counted out one by one. The Buddha's reply, beginning on the next page, will first echo this same list back nearly line for line before finally distilling everything into a fixed hundred-and-eight pairs of terms — where the arithmetic, this time, is meant to come out exact.
