The Four Kinds of Dhyāna

Having just spent a full page dismantling every fixed position, the Buddha now describes his own teaching in positive terms: apart from the four extremes, yet still built up carefully, statement by statement, the way a caravan-leader guides his merchants safely across dangerous country. From there the sūtra names four kinds of dhyāna — the meditation the ignorant practice, the meditation that observes meaning, the meditation that dwells in suchness itself, and the dhyāna of the Tathāgata — before Mahāmati asks the question the entire sūtra has been building toward from its very first page: what, exactly, is nirvāṇa?

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 15 of 50 complete Source: Taishō T0670, 楞伽阿跋多羅寶經 (tr. Guṇabhadra, 443 CE) Reading notes after Zongle and Ruqi's 楞伽阿跋多羅寶經註解 (T1789)

How the Tathāgata Teaches, and the Four Kinds of Dhyāna

Chinese Source

「復次，大慧！如來說法，離如是四句，謂：一異、俱不俱、有無非有非無、常無常。離於有無建立誹謗分別。結集真諦、緣起、道滅、解脫。如來說法，以是為首。非性、非自在、非無因、非微塵、非時、非自性相續而為說法。復次，大慧！為淨煩惱爾炎障故，譬如商主，次第建立百八句無所有，善分別諸乘及諸地相。

「復次，大慧！有四種禪。云何為四？謂：愚夫所行禪、觀察義禪、攀緣如禪、如來禪。云何愚夫所行禪？謂：聲聞、緣覺、外道修行者，觀人無我性，自相共相骨鎖，無常、苦、不淨相，計著為首。如是相不異觀，前後轉進，想不除滅，是名愚夫所行禪。云何觀察義禪？謂：人無我自相共相，外道自他俱無性已。觀法無我彼地相義，漸次增進，是名觀察義禪。云何攀緣如禪？謂：妄想二無我妄想，如實處不生妄想，是名攀緣如禪。云何如來禪？謂：入如來地，行自覺聖智相三種樂住，成辦眾生不思議事，是名如來禪。」

爾時，世尊欲重宣此義而說偈言：
「凡夫所行禪，　觀察相義禪，
攀緣如實禪，　如來清淨禪。

譬如日月形，　鉢頭摩深嶮，
如虛空火燼，　修行者觀察。

如是種種相，　外道道通禪，
亦復墮聲聞，　及緣覺境界，
捨離彼一切，　則是無所有。

一切剎諸佛，　以不思議手，
一時摩其頂，　隨順入如相。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, Mahāmati, when the Tathāgata teaches the Dharma, he is apart from just these four extremes — identity and difference, both together and neither, existence and non-existence, both together and neither, permanence and impermanence — apart from the false assertion and slanderous denial that arise from existence and non-existence. [Yet on this very basis] he gathers together the noble truths, dependent origination, the path, cessation, and liberation. This is what stands foremost in the Tathāgata's teaching. He does not teach by way of any [fixed] nature, nor by way of a sovereign lord, nor by way of no cause at all, nor by way of atoms, nor by way of time, nor by way of any own-nature that merely continues on. Furthermore, Mahāmati, it is for the sake of purifying the hindrance of affliction and the hindrance of the knowable that he builds up, step by step, like a caravan-leader [guiding his merchants safely across dangerous country], the Hundred and Eight Statements on what has nothing at all, skillfully distinguishing every vehicle and the marks of every ground.

"Furthermore, Mahāmati, there are four kinds of dhyāna. What are the four? The dhyāna practiced by the ignorant, the dhyāna that observes meaning, the dhyāna that dwells in suchness, and the dhyāna of the Tathāgata. What is the dhyāna practiced by the ignorant? It is that in which practitioners among the śrāvakas, the pratyekabuddhas, and the outsiders, observing the nature of the no-self of persons, [taking as their object] the own mark and shared mark of a skeleton's linked bones, the marks of impermanence, suffering, and impurity, become attached to this as foremost. Observing these marks without variation, advancing further and further through them, without ever eradicating the very notion [that clings to them] — this is called the dhyāna practiced by the ignorant. What is the dhyāna that observes meaning? It is that in which, [having passed beyond] the own mark and shared mark of the no-self of persons, and beyond the outsiders' notion that both self and other lack any [fixed] nature, one observes the meaning of the no-self of dharmas and the marks of the [bodhisattva] grounds, advancing further and further by stages — this is called the dhyāna that observes meaning. What is the dhyāna that dwells in suchness? It is that in which, [seeing through] the false thought bound up with the two kinds of no-self as themselves false thought, no further false thought arises within the very place of reality itself — this is called the dhyāna that dwells in suchness. What is the dhyāna of the Tathāgata? It is that in which, entering the ground of the Tathāgata, and practicing the three blissful abidings proper to the mark of self-realized noble wisdom, one accomplishes the inconceivable work of bringing sentient beings to maturity — this is called the dhyāna of the Tathāgata."

Then the World-Honored One, wishing to restate this meaning, spoke in verse: "The dhyāna the ignorant practice, the dhyāna that observes the mark of meaning,
the dhyāna that dwells in true reality, and the pure dhyāna of the Tathāgata —

like the shape of the sun and the moon, like a red lotus in the ocean's depths,
like empty space or a burnt-out ember: so does the practitioner observe [what arises in meditation].

Every such mark, and the meditation the outsiders' own path leads through,
falls, too, into the domain of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha alike —
leave all of it behind, and this itself is what has nothing at all.

Every buddha, in every land, with an inconceivable hand,
anoints, all at once, [the practitioner's] crown, and he enters, in accordance with it, the very mark of suchness."

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

The commentators resolve what looks like a direct contradiction — teaching apart from all four positions, and yet still gathering together specific doctrines such as the four noble truths — by locating each half in a different one of the two truths: "如云離句絕非，則真諦不可說也。又云離四句已，無妨四說，則俗諦可說也" — "when it is said [the Tathāgata's teaching is] apart from [fixed] statements and free of every wrong position, this shows that ultimate truth cannot, strictly, be spoken at all; yet it is also said that, having left the four extremes behind, there is nothing to prevent [teaching by way of] four specific doctrines — this shows that conventional truth can indeed be spoken." Ultimate truth resists every fixed formulation the four extremes could produce; conventional truth, precisely because it makes no claim to be that ultimate truth itself, remains fully available to teach — which is exactly why the same passage that has just spent an entire page demolishing fixed positions can, in the very next breath, proceed to build the Hundred and Eight Statements "like a caravan-leader," one careful step at a time.

On the four kinds of dhyāna, the commentators are precise that "the ignorant" in the first kind does not mean merely unlearned people but specifically śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and outsiders who never move past a fixed object of contemplation: "如是相不異觀，前後轉進，想不除滅" — "observing these marks without variation, advancing further and further through the very same fixed object, the notion [that clings to it] is never actually eradicated." Progress within this first kind of dhyāna is real but bounded — one advances through deeper and deeper stages of concentration while the underlying grasping at a fixed mark (a skeleton, impermanence, impurity) is never itself dissolved. Only the second kind, "the dhyāna that observes meaning," moves past this by turning from the no-self of persons to the no-self of dharmas — and the commentators note the third kind, dwelling in suchness, is reached only when even the deliberate contemplation of the two kinds of no-self is itself finally released, so that "no further false thought arises within the very place of reality itself" without any remaining effort of observation at all.

What Is Nirvāṇa?

Chinese Source

爾時，大慧菩薩摩訶薩復白佛言：「世尊！般涅槃者，說何等法，謂為涅槃？」

佛告大慧：「一切自性習氣，藏意識見習轉變，名為涅槃。諸佛及我涅槃，自性空事境界。

「復次，大慧！涅槃者，聖智自覺境界，離斷常妄想性非性。云何非常？謂自相共相妄想斷，故非常。云何非斷？謂一切聖去來現在得自覺，故非斷。大慧！涅槃不壞不死。若涅槃死者，復應受生相續。若壞者，應墮有為相。是故涅槃離壞離死。是故修行者之所歸依。

「復次，大慧！涅槃非捨非得，非斷非常，非一義非種種義。是名涅槃。

「復次，大慧！聲聞、緣覺涅槃者，覺自相共相，不習近境界，不顛倒見，妄想不生。彼等於彼，作涅槃覺。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Mahāmati Bodhisattva-Mahāsattva again said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what dharma does 'attaining nirvāṇa' actually refer to? What is it that is called nirvāṇa?"

The Buddha told Mahāmati: "It is the turning and transformation of every habituated force of own-nature, of the views habituated in the storehouse consciousness and the discriminating consciousness alike — this is what is called nirvāṇa. The nirvāṇa of every buddha, and my own, is the very domain of own-nature's emptiness [of all such habituated force].

"Furthermore, Mahāmati, nirvāṇa is the domain of noble wisdom's own self-realization, apart from the false thought of annihilation and permanence, apart from [fixed] nature and its absence. What does it mean that it is not permanent? It is that the false thought of own mark and shared mark is cut off — hence it is not permanent. What does it mean that it is not annihilated? It is that every noble one, past, future, and present, attains this same self-realization — hence it is not annihilated. Mahāmati, nirvāṇa is neither destroyed nor does it die. Were nirvāṇa to die, it would then have to undergo further rebirth in continued succession; were it to be destroyed, it would then have to fall into the mark of the conditioned. It is for this reason that nirvāṇa is apart from both destruction and death — and it is for this very reason that it is what every practitioner takes refuge in.

"Furthermore, Mahāmati, nirvāṇa is neither something relinquished nor something newly attained; it is neither annihilation nor permanence; it is neither a single meaning nor a manifold one. This is what is called nirvāṇa.

"Furthermore, Mahāmati, the nirvāṇa of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha is this: becoming aware of own mark and shared mark, no longer growing habituated to any [further] object-realm, free of any inverted view, with false thought no longer arising — it is with respect to just this much that they form the notion of nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

The commentators frame Mahāmati's question as necessary precisely because the word "nirvāṇa" is used so differently by three quite different parties: "涅槃有三：謂外道妄計涅槃、二乘取證涅槃、如來究竟涅槃。此三涅槃名雖同而實大異，苟不以法而正其名，則如來藏與凡小混" — "there are three [things called] nirvāṇa: the outsiders' falsely conceived nirvāṇa, the two vehicles' nirvāṇa attained through verification, and the Tathāgata's own ultimate nirvāṇa. Though these three share an identical name, they differ enormously in substance — and unless the name is corrected against the actual dharma it refers to, the tathāgatagarbha itself risks being confused with what ordinary people and the lesser vehicles mean by the same word." The question that opens this section is not idle curiosity but the same vocabulary-policing concern that opened Fascicle Two: a shared word, left unexamined, lets three incompatible doctrines masquerade as one.

On why nirvāṇa can be described as "the turning and transformation" of habituated force, the commentators add a caution against over-reading the word "transformation": "然一切眾生即涅槃相，何轉變之有？所謂名轉而體不轉也" — "yet every sentient being is, in truth, already the very mark of nirvāṇa — what transformation could there really be? What is meant is that the name turns while the essence itself does not turn at all." Nothing is actually converted from one substance into another; what changes is only whether the storehouse consciousness's own habituated views are recognized for what they already are. On the closing distinction between the Tathāgata's nirvāṇa and the two vehicles' own, the commentators flag the phrase "彼等於彼，作涅槃覺" ("it is with respect to just this much that they form the notion of nirvāṇa") as a quiet but pointed limitation — the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha have genuinely stopped false thought from arising, which is no small thing, but they have stopped at a boundary the passage is careful to mark as their own, not as nirvāṇa's final extent.

Part 15 Complete

This closes Part 15: the Tathāgata's own teaching described as apart from the four extremes yet still built up methodically, like a caravan-leader's careful guidance; the four kinds of dhyāna, from the ignorant's fixation on a single unchanging object up through the Tathāgata's own inconceivable activity; and the opening of the sūtra's direct teaching on nirvāṇa, distinguishing the śrāvaka's genuine but bounded attainment from something further still. Part 16 continues the teaching on nirvāṇa, then turns to the two kinds of self-nature-mark bound up with speech and with actual matter, and to the two forms of spiritual power by which every buddha sustains a bodhisattva's own realization.
