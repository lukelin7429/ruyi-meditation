The Brahmin of Worldly Disputation

Mahāmati asks why the Buddha once warned that worldly disputation, however skillfully argued, should never be pursued closely. The Buddha's answer opens with a story: a disciple of a worldly-disputation teacher once took the form of a nāga and challenged Śakra, king of the gods, to a formal debate, wagering his own many heads against Śakra's thousand-spoked wheel-chariot — and won. If even a nāga in disguise could defeat a god through mere clever argument, the Buddha asks, how much more easily could such argument mislead ordinary people? Mahāmati then presses further — does the Buddha's own extensive teaching not amount to the same thing? — and the page turns to the Buddha's own encounter with a Brahmin steeped in exactly this tradition, who fires off dozens of loaded philosophical questions in rapid succession, each one answered with the same single reply: "worldly disputation."

A Nāga's Challenge to the King of the Gods

Chinese Source

爾時，大慧菩薩白佛言：「世尊！如世尊一時說言：『世間諸論種種辯說，慎勿習近。若習近者，攝受貪欲，不攝受法。』世尊何故作如是說？」

佛告大慧：「世間言論，種種句味，因緣譬喻，採習莊嚴，誘引誑惑愚癡凡夫。不入真實自通，不覺一切法，妄想顛倒，墮於二邊。凡愚癡惑而自破壞，諸趣相續不得解脫，不能覺知自心現量，不離外性自性，妄想計著。是故世間言論種種辯說，不脫生老病死憂悲苦惱，誑惑迷亂。大慧！釋提桓因，廣解眾論，自造聲論。彼世論者有一弟子，持龍形像，詣釋天宮，建立論宗，要壞帝釋千輻之輪。隨我不如，斷一一頭，以謝所屈。作是要已，即以釋法，摧伏帝釋。釋墮負處，即壞其輪，還來人間。如是，大慧！世間言論，因譬莊嚴，乃至畜生，亦能以種種句味，惑彼諸天及阿修羅著生滅見，而況於人。是故，大慧！世間言論，應當遠離，以能招致苦生因故，慎勿習近。大慧！世論者，惟說身覺境界而已。大慧！彼世論者，乃有百千，但於後時後五十年，當破壞結集。惡覺因見盛故，惡弟子受。如是，大慧！世論破壞結集，種種句味，因譬莊嚴，說外道事，著自因緣，無有自通。大慧！彼諸外道，無自通論，於餘世論，廣說無量百千事門，無有自通，亦不自知愚癡世論。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Mahāmati Bodhisattva said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as the World-Honored One once taught: 'the manifold arguments and disputations of worldly discourse should never be pursued closely — one who does pursue them closely thereby takes on desire, and does not take on the Dharma.' World-Honored One, for what reason did you teach this?"

The Buddha told Mahāmati: "Worldly discourse, with its manifold clever phrases, its causal arguments and similes, gathered and adorned with skill, entices and deceives the ignorant and foolish. It does not lead into genuine self-realization, and, failing to perceive any dharma correctly, false thought becomes inverted, falling into one of the two extremes. Ordinary and ignorant people, confused by it, thereby destroy themselves, continuing on through the [six] destinies without attaining liberation, unable to perceive the very measure of their own mind's manifestation, and remaining attached to what is external as though it had a fixed own-nature. It is for this reason that worldly discourse, however manifold its disputations, never frees anyone from birth, old age, sickness, death, sorrow, grief, suffering, and affliction — it only deceives and confuses further. Mahāmati, Śakra, [lord of the gods,] had broadly mastered every kind of treatise, and had even composed a treatise on grammar of his own. Now, a certain teacher of worldly discourse had a disciple who took on the form of a nāga and went to Śakra's own heavenly palace, proposing a formal debate and wagering this: should Śakra be unable to match him, he would shatter Śakra's own thousand-spoked wheel-chariot; should he himself be unable to match Śakra, he would cut off his own heads, one after another, in acknowledgment of his defeat. Having made this wager, he then used Śakra's own method of argument against him, and thoroughly defeated Śakra. Śakra, having lost, had his own chariot destroyed, and [the nāga] returned to the human world. Just so, Mahāmati, worldly discourse, adorned with causal argument and simile, can, even coming from the mouth of an animal, mislead the very gods and asuras into the view of arising and ceasing — how much more easily, then, could it mislead a human being? It is for this reason, Mahāmati, that worldly discourse should be kept far away from, since it is able to bring about the very cause of suffering — never pursue it closely. Mahāmati, those who engage in worldly discourse speak of nothing but the domain of bodily sensation. Mahāmati, there are hundreds and thousands of such worldly disputers; but in a later age, in the final fifty years [of the Dharma's decline], they will corrupt the very compilation of the true teaching, since the causes and conditions for evil views will then flourish, and evil disciples will take up their teaching. Just so, Mahāmati, worldly discourse corrupts the compiled teaching with its manifold clever phrases, its causal arguments and similes, expounding the outsiders' own affairs, attached to their own [supposed] causes and conditions, with no genuine self-realization behind any of it. Mahāmati, none of these outsiders has any genuine self-realization within their own teaching; expounding, across every other kind of worldly discourse, boundless hundreds and thousands of specific topics, they have no self-realization at all, and do not even know their own worldly discourse to be as ignorant as it truly is."

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

The commentators supply the fuller wager the sūtra's own compressed prose leaves implicit, quoting the parallel version directly: "作是要言：『憍尸迦！我共汝論，汝若不如我，當破汝千輻之輪。我若不如，斷一一頭以謝所屈。』" — "he made this wager, saying: 'Kauśika! Let us debate. Should you be unable to match me, I will shatter your thousand-spoked wheel-chariot; should I be unable to match you, I will cut off my own heads, one by one, in acknowledgment of my defeat.'" Śakra, lord of the gods and master of his own treatise on grammar, nonetheless lost this debate to a nāga in disguise, and had his own chariot destroyed as the wager required. The commentators draw the moral the Buddha himself draws: "乃至畜生亦能以種種句味惑彼諸天及阿修羅著生滅見，而況於人" — "even coming from an animal, mere clever phrasing was able to mislead the very gods and asuras into the view of arising and ceasing — how much more easily, then, could ordinary people be misled by the very same means?" Rhetorical skill detached from genuine realization is dangerous precisely because it is not correlated with truth at all — a god can lose a formal debate to an animal, which is exactly why the sūtra treats mere argumentative cleverness as a hazard rather than a virtue.

On the prophecy that worldly discourse will "corrupt the compiled teaching" in "the final fifty years," the commentators flag both a chronological reading and a possible textual variant worth noting rather than silently resolving: "按《金剛功德施論》，謂人壽百齡開為二分，初分五十教力增強，後五十歲教力漸微，言正法欲滅時也。或當作後五百年，『十』字恐誤" — "according to the *Vajra Merit-Giving Treatise*, a human lifespan of a hundred years is divided into two halves: in the first fifty years the teaching's power grows strong, and in the last fifty years it gradually weakens — this refers to the time when the true Dharma is about to disappear. Alternatively, this may originally have read 'the final five hundred years,' and the character for 'ten' here may be a transcription error." The commentators preserve the ambiguity rather than silently picking one reading, treating it as an open textual question worth flagging for the reader rather than quietly resolving.

Is the Buddha's Own Teaching Not Also Worldly Disputation?

Chinese Source

爾時，大慧白佛言：「世尊！若外道世論，種種句味因譬莊嚴，無有自通，自事計著者；世尊亦說世論，為種種異方諸來會眾天人、阿修羅，廣說無量種種句味。亦非自通耶？亦入一切外道智慧言說數耶？」

佛告大慧：「我不說世論，亦無來去，惟說不來不去。大慧！來者，趣聚會生。去者，散壞。不來不去者，是不生不滅。我所說，不墮世論妄想數中。所以者何？謂：不計者外性非性，自心現處。二邊妄想所不能轉。相境非性，覺自心現，則自心現，妄想不生。妄想不生者，空無相無作，入三脫門，名為解脫。大慧！我念一時，於一處住。有世論婆羅門，來詣我所。不請空閑，便問我言：『瞿曇！一切所作耶？』我時答言：『婆羅門！一切所作，是初世論。』彼復問言：『一切非所作耶？』我復報言：『一切非所作，是第二世論。』彼復問言：『一切常耶？一切無常耶？一切生耶？一切不生耶？』我時報言：『是六世論。』大慧！彼復問我言：『一切一耶？一切異耶？一切俱耶？一切不俱耶？一切因種種受生現耶？』我時報言：『是十一世論。』大慧！彼復問言：『一切無記耶？一切有記耶？有我耶？無我耶？有此世耶？無此世耶？有他世耶？無他世耶？有解脫耶？無解脫耶？一切剎那耶？一切不剎那耶？虛空耶？非數滅耶？涅槃耶？瞿曇！作耶？非作耶？有中陰耶？無中陰耶？』大慧！我時報言：『婆羅門！如是說者，悉是世論。非我所說，是汝世論。我唯說無始虛偽妄想習氣種種諸惡，三有之因，不能覺知自心現量而生妄想，攀緣外性。如外道法，我諸根義，三合智生。我不如是，婆羅門！我不說因，不說無因。惟說妄想攝所攝性，施設緣起，非汝所及餘墮受我相續者，所能覺知。』大慧！涅槃、虛空、滅，非有三種，但數有三耳。

「復次，大慧！爾時世論婆羅門，復問我言：『癡愛業因故，有三有耶？為無因耶？』我時報言：『此二者，亦是世論耳。』彼復問言：『一切性皆入自共相耶？』我復報言：『此亦世論。婆羅門！乃至意流妄計外塵，皆是世論。』

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Mahāmati said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if the outsiders' own worldly disputation — every kind of clever phrase, adorned with causal argument and simile — has no genuine self-realization behind it, and is merely attachment to their own affairs, then does the World-Honored One not also teach a kind of worldly disputation, expounding boundless, manifold, clever phrases at length for the sake of the various assemblies of gods, humans, and asuras who gather from every different region? Is this, too, not lacking any genuine self-realization? Does it, too, not fall into the very same category as every outsider's own display of wisdom and clever speech?"

The Buddha told Mahāmati: "I do not teach worldly disputation, nor do I teach anything that comes or goes — I teach only what neither comes nor goes. Mahāmati, 'coming' is what arises through the coming-together of conditions; 'going' is what scatters and is destroyed. What neither comes nor goes is precisely the unborn and unceasing. What I teach does not fall into the category of worldly disputation's own false thought at all. Why is this? Because it is taught by one who does not treat what is external as having a fixed nature or lacking one, [teaching instead from] the very place of one's own mind's manifestation — a teaching the false thought of the two extremes is simply unable to shift at all. Once the mark of an object-realm is seen to have no fixed nature, and one is aware that this is one's own mind's manifestation, then, precisely because it is one's own mind's manifestation, false thought does not arise. Where false thought does not arise, there is emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness — entering thereby the three gates of liberation, which is what is called liberation. Mahāmati, I recall a time when, dwelling in a certain place, a Brahmin steeped in worldly disputation came to where I was. Without so much as asking whether I had a free moment, he immediately asked me: 'Gautama, is everything something made?' I answered him at once: 'Brahmin, [to say] everything is something made is the first worldly disputation.' He then asked again: 'Is everything, then, not something made?' I replied again: 'Everything is not something made — this is the second worldly disputation.' He then asked further: 'Is everything permanent? Is everything impermanent? Does everything arise? Does everything not arise?' I answered him: 'These are the third through sixth worldly disputations.' Mahāmati, he then asked me further: 'Is everything one? Is everything different? Is everything both together? Is everything neither? Does everything manifest, being born through every kind of cause?' I answered him: 'These are the seventh through eleventh worldly disputations.' Mahāmati, he then asked further: 'Is everything undetermined? Is everything determined? Is there a self? Is there no self? Is there this world? Is there no this world? Is there another world? Is there no other world? Is there liberation? Is there no liberation? Is everything momentary? Is everything not momentary? [What of] space? Cessation not through analysis? Nirvāṇa? Gautama, is it made, or not made? Is there an intermediate state, or is there not?' Mahāmati, I answered him at once: 'Brahmin, everything spoken of in this way is nothing but worldly disputation. This is not what I myself teach — it is your own worldly disputation. I teach only this: that the beginningless, false, habituated force of false thought, every kind of evil, and the causes of the three realms of existence, [all arise] because one fails to perceive that this is the very measure of one's own mind's manifestation, and so gives rise to false thought, grasping outward at what is external as though it had a fixed nature. As the outsiders' own teaching holds, a self, the sense-faculties, and their objects come together as three, and wisdom is thereby born — I do not teach anything of this kind. Brahmin, I do not teach that things arise through a cause, nor do I teach that they arise without one — I teach only the very nature of false thought's grasping and what is grasped, provisionally establishing dependent origination on that basis alone — something neither you nor any other who has fallen into holding onto a continuing "I" is able to perceive at all.' Mahāmati, nirvāṇa, space, and cessation are not, in truth, three separate things — it is only in number that they are spoken of as three.

"Furthermore, Mahāmati, at that time the Brahmin steeped in worldly disputation asked me further: 'Is it on account of the cause of ignorance, craving, and karma that the three realms of existence exist? Or is it without any cause at all?' I answered him at once: 'Both of these, too, are nothing but worldly disputation.' He then asked further: 'Does every [fixed] nature fall entirely within own mark and shared mark?' I answered him again: 'This, too, is worldly disputation. Brahmin, right down to the very drift of the mind falsely conceiving of external dust — every bit of this is worldly disputation.'"

Reading note — after Zongle and Ruqi

The commentators locate the precise pivot in the Buddha's answer to Mahāmati's challenge in what "coming and going" is actually being used to mean: "來言趣聚會生者，謂來則眾緣和合而生。去言散壞者，謂去則緣散而滅。佛說異是，故曰『不去不來即不生不滅』，乃我所通" — "'coming,' [as the outsiders use it,] means arising through the coming-together of manifold conditions; 'going' means ceasing through the scattering of those conditions. What the Buddha teaches is different from this: hence 'neither going nor coming' is precisely 'neither arising nor ceasing' — and this is what I myself have penetrated." Worldly disputation, on this reading, is not simply "discourse about things" in general — it specifically names any account of reality built on things genuinely coming into and passing out of existence; the Buddha's own teaching, resting on nothing coming or going in that sense at all, escapes the category structurally, not merely by being more sophisticated argument of the identical kind.

On the Buddha's method throughout the Brahmin's rapid-fire questions, the commentators note that he never once answers on the questions' own terms: "彼問雖多，概以一世論報之，所以拒之也...使彼不得措辭，所以杜之也" — "however many questions he asked, the Buddha answered every one of them alike, simply as 'worldly disputation' — this is precisely how he declined to engage. By doing so, he left the Brahmin with no further foothold for argument at all." Every one of the Brahmin's paired questions — made or unmade, permanent or impermanent, one or many, self or no-self — already presupposes exactly the fixed, independently existing terms this sūtra has spent three fascicles dismantling; answering any single one of them directly, in either direction, would have granted the very premise the Buddha refuses to grant. On the closing remark that nirvāṇa, space, and cessation are not truly three separate things, the commentators tie it directly back to Mahāmati's own challenge that the Buddha's teaching resembles the outsiders' numerological systems: these three unconditioned things "但數有三，而非有三" — "are spoken of as three only as a matter of counting, not because they are, in reality, three genuinely distinct things" — even the Buddha's own use of a number is a provisional convenience for speech, not a claim about how reality itself is carved.

Part 31 Complete

This closes Part 31: the nāga who defeated Śakra in debate as a warning about what mere rhetorical skill can accomplish apart from genuine realization, and the Buddha's own encounter with a Brahmin whose every loaded question — made or unmade, permanent or impermanent, self or no-self, this world or another, and finally the very cause of the three realms of existence themselves — receives the identical answer: worldly disputation, declined without engaging its premises at all. Part 32 turns to how the exchange actually ends — the Brahmin's silent retreat, and his own private admission of what he found — before the Buddha explains, in his own terms, the precise difference between attachment that produces desire and attachment that produces the Dharma.
