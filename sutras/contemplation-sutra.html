The Contemplation Sūtra

on the Buddha of Immeasurable Life《佛說觀無量壽佛經》

A queen imprisoned by her own son is taught sixteen ways to see Sukhāvatī in the mind's eye, and nine grades of rebirth stretching from the most disciplined practitioner to the most ordinary sinner saved by ten dying breaths — the third and final of the "three Pure Land sūtras," completing this site's Pure Land series.

Why This Sūtra, After the Other Two

The Amitābha Sūtra describes Sukhāvatī and instructs listeners to hold Amitābha's name in mind; the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra tells the causal story of how that land came to exist, through Dharmākara's forty-eight vows. This sūtra does something neither of the other two attempts: it opens with an ordinary, brutal family catastrophe — a prince imprisons his own parents to seize the throne — and only then, in response to one grieving woman's request, teaches sixteen specific, describable methods for holding that land in the mind's eye, followed by nine distinct grades of rebirth precise enough to name exactly what kind of person, and what kind of conduct, lands where.

It is also the only one of the three Pure Land sūtras built around a real narrative frame rather than a direct discourse. Where the other two open with Śākyamuni simply beginning to teach, this one spends its first part on a coup, an attempted matricide, and a woman stripped of her own ornaments in grief before the Buddha ever mentions Sukhāvatī at all — and everything that follows, all sixteen contemplations and all nine grades, is addressed first to her, a named and particular person in the middle of the worst days of her life, before it is addressed to anyone else.

The teaching in one sentence

A queen, imprisoned and grieving, is shown every buddha-land in the universe and chooses Sukhāvatī; she is then taught sixteen ways to build that land in her own mind, from a setting sun to the boundless body of Amitāyus Buddha himself, and finally nine distinct grades of rebirth — reaching from a lifetime of disciplined Mahāyāna practice down to a single dying person's ten desperate recitations of a name — so that, in the Buddha's own closing words to Ānanda, "to uphold this teaching is nothing other than to uphold the name of Amitāyus Buddha."

A new voice on this site: Shandao

The reading notes across all eight parts of this guide draw primarily on Shandao (善導, 613–681), whose 觀經四帖疏 ("Commentary on the Contemplation Sūtra in Four Fascicles") is the single most influential commentary this sūtra has ever received. Shandao is traditionally regarded as the patriarch who gave Chinese Pure Land Buddhism its distinct shape as a tradition centered on reliance on Amitābha's vow-power rather than on a practitioner's own accumulated merit; centuries later, the Japanese teacher Hōnen (法然, 1133–1212) read Shandao's commentary and, by his own account, found in it the passage that convinced him to found an entire school of Buddhism — Jōdo-shū — on exclusive recitation of Amitāyus's name. No commentator this site has used before carries quite this weight in his own tradition, and his voice — direct, occasionally polemical, given to vivid parables — is markedly different from the more measured, synthesizing tone of Jingying Huiyuan's commentary used on the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra pages.

An honest disagreement, not smoothed over

Where this guide declines to pick a side

This sūtra's nine grades of rebirth — translated across Part 5, Part 6, and Part 7 — have been read in two genuinely incompatible ways by this sūtra's own major commentators. Jingying Huiyuan holds that the upper grades describe bodhisattvas who have already reached specific advanced stages of the path through their own cultivation before ever reaching Sukhāvatī, and that even the lower grades carry hidden Mahāyāna roots from past lives beneath their present wrongdoing. Shandao reads every one of the nine grades, top to bottom, as describing ordinary unawakened beings — ordinary enough that the very worst of them, guilty of the five heinous transgressions, is saved by nothing more than ten sincere recitations of a name at the very last moment of a wasted life.

This is the same kind of divergence this site's guide to the Platform Sūtra found between its Dunhuang and received editions, and it is handled the same way here: both readings are given in full, in the commentators' own terms, without this guide declaring one of them correct. The disagreement is real, it is old, and it turns on a genuine and still-open question about how literally to read this sūtra's own stated outcomes.

How to actually use this text

  1. Read Part 1 before anything else. The imprisonment, the plea, and the Three Blessings set up everything that follows — the sixteen contemplations only make sense as an answer to a specific, named person's specific grief.
  2. Treat the sixteen contemplations as one continuous sequence. Parts 2 through 4 build a single mental scene in careful stages — sun, water, ground, trees, ponds, towers, throne, image, true body, the two bodhisattvas, the practitioner's own rehearsed arrival — and Shandao repeatedly stresses that skipping the order undermines the method.
  3. Read the nine grades of rebirth as a matched set. Parts 5 through 7 are meant to be read together — the upper, middle, and lower grades form a single graduated structure, and the most famous line in the whole sūtra, on the salvation of a dying evildoer, only carries its full weight once the eight grades above it have been read first.

One question Western students often ask

"If someone genuinely evil can be saved by ten words at the very end of life, what was the point of everything the sūtra teaches before that?" Shandao's own commentary treats this as a real question rather than a rhetorical one, and his answer is that the nine grades are not nine equally weighted options to choose from, but nine different starting conditions that different readers actually find themselves in. The sixteen contemplations and the upper grades of conduct remain exactly as demanding as they are stated to be for anyone able to undertake them; the lower-lower grade exists for the specific, narrower case of someone who reaches the end of life having done neither — and even there, on Shandao's reading, it is not that wrongdoing carries no weight, but that a particular kind of desperate sincerity, met by Amitāyus's own vow, is sufficient where nothing else remains. Jingying Huiyuan's alternative reading — that even this person carries hidden merit from an earlier life — is, in its own way, a different answer to the same worry: that grace this large needs some further explanation before it can be believed.

Key Terms

Vaidehī 韋提希 — the queen of Magadha whose imprisonment by her own son opens this sūtra, and whose request for "a place free of grief and affliction" is the occasion for everything the Buddha teaches in it.
十六觀 — the Sixteen Contemplations: a graduated sequence from the setting sun through water, ice, and a lapis lazuli ground, jeweled trees and ponds, the lotus throne, the image and true body of Amitāyus, the two bodhisattvas, the practitioner's own rehearsed rebirth, and finally an accommodated smaller image for anyone unable to hold the boundless true body in mind.
九品往生 — the Nine Grades of Rebirth: three grades each of upper, middle, and lower birth, open respectively to disciplined Mahāyāna practice, ordinary precept-keeping, and — at the very floor — a dying sinner's last sincere words.
定善 / 散善 — "meditative good" and "dispersed good," Shandao's own division of this sūtra's entire content: the sixteen contemplations, which require a stilled and single-pointed mind, and the Three Blessings and nine grades, which do not.
是心作佛，是心是佛 — "This mind makes the buddha; this mind is the buddha" — this sūtra's most quoted line, from the eighth contemplation, on the relationship between the visualizing mind and the buddha it visualizes.
攝取不捨 — "embracing and never abandoning": the ninth contemplation's description of Amitāyus's light reaching specifically those mindful of him, glossed at length by Shandao as the intimate, proximate, and empowering conditions binding such a being to that Buddha.
正行 / 雜行 / 正定業 — "correct practices," "sundry practices," and, within the correct practices, the single "rightly settled karma" of sustained mindfulness of Amitāyus's name — a distinction from Shandao's commentary that later became foundational to Japanese Pure Land Buddhism.
抑止門 — the "gate of restraint": Shandao's resolution of the apparent conflict between the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra's exclusion of the five heinous transgressions from its Eighteenth Vow and this sūtra's rescue of exactly such a person in its lowest grade.

The Eight Parts

The narrative's natural divisions, each with the Chinese–English text side by side and reading notes drawn from Shandao's 7th-century commentary. Read in order — the sixteen contemplations and the nine grades both build cumulatively — or jump to whichever part you need.

Part 1 · 序分

Vaidehī's Imprisonment, and the Three Blessings

A prince imprisons his own parents; Vaidehī begs the Buddha for a world without grief, is shown every buddha-land in the universe, and chooses Sukhāvatī.

如是我聞 至 云何得見極樂國土 Part 2 · 初觀－六觀

The First Six Contemplations

The sun, water hardening into ice and a lapis lazuli ground, the ground itself, jeweled trees, ponds of eightfold water, and the towers that gather it all into one scene.

日想觀 至 寶樓觀 Part 3 · 七觀－九觀

The Lotus Throne and the True Body

Amitāyus appears standing in the sky; the jeweled lotus throne; the image contemplation's "this mind is the buddha"; and the true body whose light embraces all who remember it.

華座觀 至 真身觀 Part 4 · 十觀－十三觀

Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta

Amitāyus's two great bodhisattvas in full bodily detail; the practitioner's own rehearsed rebirth; and a smaller image offered for anyone who cannot hold a boundless body in mind.

觀音觀 至 雜想觀 Part 5 · 十四觀

The Upper Grades of Rebirth

The Three Minds, the parable of the two rivers and the white path, and an honest look at where Shandao and Jingying Huiyuan sharply disagree about who these grades describe.

上品上生 至 上品下生 Part 6 · 十五觀

The Middle Grades of Rebirth

A single day and night of pure precept-keeping, or nothing more than ordinary filial devotion met by the right encounter at the moment of death.

中品上生 至 中品下生 Part 7 · 十六觀

The Lower Grades of Rebirth

This sūtra's most famous and most debated passage: ten deathbed recitations, Shandao's "gate of restraint," and a genuine disagreement about who is actually being saved.

下品上生 至 下品下生 Part 8 · 流通分

The Final Entrustment

Vaidehī and her five hundred attendants awaken; Ānanda asks the sūtra's name; and a closing instruction that later grounded an entire tradition of practice.

About the Source

The Chinese is Kālayaśas's translation (畺良耶舍, Taishō T365), sourced here from the CBETA electronic canon (Chinese Buddhist Electronic Text Association), which collates the Taishō edition against earlier printed canons and adds modern punctuation. The English translation was made for Ru-Yi Meditation Center directly from this Chinese, without reference to any existing English translation, following the same conventions as the Amitābha Sūtra and the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra pages on this site.

Reference, not a source translated

The reading notes across all eight parts draw primarily on Shandao's (善導, 613–681) 觀經四帖疏, sourced from the same CBETA electronic canon, and long in the public domain. Where the sūtra's nine grades of rebirth are concerned, this guide also draws on Jingying Huiyuan's (淨影寺慧遠, 523–592) 觀無量壽經義疏 — the same commentator used throughout this site's guide to the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra — specifically because his reading of who the nine grades describe diverges sharply from Shandao's own, and that divergence is presented directly rather than smoothed over.

The commentaries are listed here as references consulted, not reproduced. Each "Reading note — after Shandao" (or, where noted, "after Shandao, compared with Jingying Huiyuan") is Ru-Yi's own English synthesis of these commentaries' key points, images, and distinctions for that passage — not a translation of either commentary in full.
