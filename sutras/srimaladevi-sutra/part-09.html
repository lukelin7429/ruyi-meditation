A Pure Mind, and How It Comes to Be Defiled

Saṃsāra itself, Śrīmālā argues, depends on the tathāgatagarbha — and then she puts to the Buddha the single hardest sentence in the whole sūtra: that a mind pure by its own nature is nonetheless defiled by affliction. The Buddha calls it one of two things "difficult to understand" even for him to state plainly, and Śrīmālā closes by naming three kinds of disciples who can hold this teaching without slandering it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 9 of 10 complete Source: Taishō T0353 (tr. Guṇabhadra, 436 CE) Reading notes after Jizang's 勝鬘寶窟 (T1744)

Saṃsāra Depends on the Tathāgatagarbha

Chinese Source

「世尊！生死者依如來藏，以如來藏故，說本際不可知。世尊！有如來藏故說生死，是名善說。世尊！生死，生死者，諸受根沒，次第不受根起，是名生死。世尊！死生者，此二法是如來藏。世間言說故，有死有生，死者謂根壞，生者新諸根起，非如來藏有生有死。如來藏者離有為相，如來藏常住不變。是故如來藏，是依、是持、是建立。世尊！不離、不斷、不脫、不異、不思議佛法。世尊！斷脫異外有為法依持、建立者，是如來藏。

「世尊！若無如來藏者，不得厭苦樂求涅槃。何以故？於此六識及心法智，此七法剎那不住、不種眾苦，不得厭苦、樂求涅槃。世尊！如來藏者，無前際，不起不滅法，種諸苦，得厭苦、樂求涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One! Saṃsāra depends on the tathāgatagarbha, and it is because of the tathāgatagarbha that one can speak of an otherwise unknowable original limit. World-Honored One! It is only because there is a tathāgatagarbha that one can speak of saṃsāra at all — this is a well-spoken teaching. World-Honored One! Saṃsāra, saṃsāra — this means the sense-faculties that received their prior experience submerge, and in due order new faculties that have not yet received experience arise; this is called saṃsāra. World-Honored One! Death and rebirth, these two, are themselves grounded in the tathāgatagarbha. It is only by worldly convention of speech that there is said to be death and there is said to be birth: death means the faculties break down, birth means new faculties arise — it is not the tathāgatagarbha itself that is born or that dies. The tathāgatagarbha is apart from conditioned marks; the tathāgatagarbha is ever-abiding and unchanging. This is why the tathāgatagarbha is the ground, the support, and the foundation. World-Honored One! It is buddha-dharmas that are inseparable, unsevered, unreleased, no different, and inconceivable. World-Honored One! What serves as the ground, support, and foundation for conditioned dharmas outside itself — severed from it, released from it, different from it — is the tathāgatagarbha.

"World-Honored One! If there were no tathāgatagarbha, one could not weary of suffering, nor delight in seeking nirvāṇa. Why is this? Because these six consciousnesses, together with mind and its cognitive functions — these seven dharmas — do not abide even for a moment, and do not themselves plant the seeds of manifold suffering, and so could not, on their own, give rise to weariness of suffering or delight in seeking nirvāṇa. World-Honored One! It is the tathāgatagarbha — without any prior limit, a dharma neither arising nor ceasing — that plants the seeds of every suffering, and so it is the tathāgatagarbha that makes possible weariness of suffering and delight in seeking nirvāṇa."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang opens this chapter by naming, in advance, exactly what makes it hard: it continues the previous chapter's pairing of saṃsāra as the dependent, arising side of things and the tathāgatagarbha as the supporting, pure side, and he states the crux without softening it — "purity should not have defilement, and defilement should not have purity; this matter is hard to clarify, and only the Buddha can fully understand it" (淨不應有染、染不應有淨，此事難明，唯佛能了). He notices something telling in how Śrīmālā herself structures her own argument here: a brief statement, an extended explanation, and finally "looking up in deference to the Buddha" (仰推於佛) — the exposition itself ends not in a closed logical proof but in reverent deference, which Jizang reads as the text's own signal that this doctrine is built to exceed ordinary comprehension by design, not by accident.

Not a Self, Not a Being — and Five Names for One Thing

Chinese Source

「世尊！如來藏者，非我、非眾生、非命、非人。如來藏者，墮身見眾生、顛倒眾生、空亂意眾生，非其境界。

「世尊！如來藏者，是法界藏、法身藏、出世間上上藏、自性清淨藏。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One! The tathāgatagarbha is not a self, not a sentient being, not a life, not a person. The tathāgatagarbha is not the domain of beings who have fallen into the view of a body-self, of inverted beings, or of beings whose minds are confused about emptiness.

"World-Honored One! The tathāgatagarbha is the storehouse of the dharma-realm, the storehouse of the dharmakāya, the world-transcending, supreme storehouse, and the storehouse of inherent purity."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang glosses the tathāgatagarbha's five names — counting "tathāgatagarbha" itself alongside the four storehouses just listed — by drawing on the Treatise on Buddha Nature and the Mahāyānasaṃgraha, assigning each name its own governing sense: tathāgatagarbha itself carries the sense of own-nature — every dharma is subsumed within it, marked by selflessness; the storehouse of the dharma-realm carries the sense of cause — it is the very object that a sage's practice, such as the four foundations of mindfulness, takes as its field, bringing what has not yet arisen into being and what has already arisen to fulfillment; the storehouse of the dharmakāya carries the sense of attainment — through faith and delight in it, a sage comes to possess the Tathāgata's own virtues; the world-transcending, supreme storehouse carries the sense of reality, since it alone escapes the three faults that make something merely mundane; and the storehouse of inherent purity carries the sense of secrecy — "when all dharmas accord with this nature, they are called internal and correct, hence pure; when they run counter to it, they are called external and deviant, hence defiled" (若一切法隨順此性…違此理則名為外). He is careful to add that these five names track five aspects of meaning, "not five separate essences" (非體別有五也) — one reality, described five ways depending on which function is in view.

A Mind Never Touched, Yet Somehow Defiled

Chinese Source

「此性清淨如來藏，而客塵煩惱、上煩惱所染，不思議如來境界。何以故？剎那善心非煩惱所染；剎那不善心亦非煩惱所染。煩惱不觸心，心不觸煩惱，云何不觸法，而能得染心？世尊！然有煩惱，有煩惱染心，自性清淨心而有染者，難可了知。唯佛世尊！實眼實智，為法根本、為通達法、為正法依，如實知見。」

勝鬘夫人說是難解之法問於佛時，佛即隨喜：「如是如是！自性清淨心而有染污，難可了知。有二法難可了知，謂自性清淨心難可了知，彼心為煩惱所染亦難了知。如此二法，汝及成就大法菩薩摩訶薩乃能聽受，諸餘聲聞，唯信佛語。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"And yet this same tathāgatagarbha, pure by its own nature, is defiled by adventitious afflictions and higher afflictions — an inconceivable domain belonging to the Tathāgata alone. Why is this? A wholesome mind, moment by moment, is not defiled by affliction; an unwholesome mind, moment by moment, is likewise not defiled by affliction. Affliction does not touch mind, and mind does not touch affliction — so how, without ever touching, could affliction possibly come to defile mind at all? World-Honored One! And yet there is affliction, and there is a mind defiled by affliction — that a mind pure by its own nature could nonetheless be defiled is indeed difficult to understand. Only the Buddha, the World-Honored One, with his real eye and real wisdom — being the very root of the Dharma, the one who penetrates the Dharma, the ground the true Dharma rests on — knows and sees this exactly as it is."

When Śrīmālā put this difficult teaching to the Buddha as a question, the Buddha at once rejoiced in it, saying: "So it is, so it is! That a mind pure by its own nature is nonetheless defiled is indeed difficult to understand. There are two dharmas difficult to understand: that the mind pure by its own nature is itself difficult to understand, and that this same mind, defiled by affliction, is likewise difficult to understand. Only you, and bodhisattva-mahāsattvas who have accomplished the great Dharma, are able to hear and accept these two dharmas; every other śrāvaka can only take them on faith in the Buddha's own word."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang's central resolution turns on strict mind-moment analysis: at any single instant, the mind is either wholesome or unwholesome, never both at once, so there is never an actual moment where purity and affliction genuinely coexist to "touch." His key citation is the sūtra's own line — "affliction does not touch mind, mind does not touch affliction" (煩惱不觸心，心不觸煩惱) — and he reaches for the classic rope-and-snake image to make the logic vivid: a person sees a rope in dim light and mistakes it for a snake; the illusory snake arises purely from delusion and never actually touches the rope, and the rope, entirely unchanged, is never touched by the illusory snake either. Real "defilement," on this model, would require dye and cloth to actually be present together — but a pure mind-moment and a defiled mind-moment never co-occur, so no literal contact, no literal dyeing, ever takes place.

He backs the analysis with Nāgārjuna's own chapter on bondage and liberation in the Middle Treatise — "if one says the five aggregates can be bound, and some other affliction among the aggregates comes to bind them, this too does not hold" — and cites a commentary attributed to the translator Tanying: "when mind is pure, there is nothing that comes to bind it; when mind is defiled, there is nothing left that could be bound" (心淨時則無物來縛，心垢時則無可縛), tying Śrīmālā's paradox directly to the Madhyamaka analysis of bondage he inherits from his own lineage.

But Jizang refuses to let this analysis explain the phenomenal fact of affliction away entirely — his synthesis holds both truths at once: a prior mind-moment is pure, a later mind-moment is defiled, and the later moment's arising obscures the earlier one; this obscuring relation is what "defilement" names, even though nothing ever literally entered or touched the earlier, pure moment. He parallels this with Nāgārjuna's chapter analyzing defilement and the one defiled: neither can be found under analysis as a fixed, independent thing, and yet, conventionally, through dependent designation, an "agent" and an "object" of defilement are still provisionally and usefully spoken of. If even the coarse defilement of an ordinary mind is this hard to pin down, Jizang notes, the defilement of a mind inherently pure by nature is harder still — which is exactly why the passage ends where it does, at the Buddha's "real eye," rather than at a fully closed argument any listener could reconstruct alone.

Three Kinds of Good Sons and Daughters

Chinese Source

「若我弟子隨信增上者，依明信已隨順法智，而得究竟。隨順法智者，觀察施設根意解境界；觀察業報；觀察阿羅漢眼；觀察心自在樂禪樂；觀察阿羅漢、辟支佛、大力菩薩聖自在通。此五種巧便觀成就。於我滅後未來世中，我弟子隨信增上，依於明信隨順法智，自性清淨心彼為煩惱染污而得究竟，是究竟者入大乘道因。信如來者，有是大利益，不謗深義。」

爾時，勝鬘白佛言：「更有餘大利益，我當承佛威神復說斯義。」佛言：「更說。」勝鬘白佛言：「三種善男子善女人，於甚深義離自毀傷，生大功德、入大乘道。何等為三？謂：若善男子善女人自成就甚深法智。若善男子善女人成就隨順法智。若善男子善女人於諸深法不自了知，仰惟世尊，非我境界，唯佛所知。是名善男子善女人仰惟如來。

「除此諸善男子善女人已。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"If my disciples, following a faith that grows ever stronger, rely on that clear faith and thereby accord with the wisdom of the Dharma, they will reach its final completion. To accord with the wisdom of the Dharma means: contemplating the domain of faculties and understanding as they are established and designated; contemplating karmic retribution; contemplating the arhat's own eye; contemplating the mind's own mastery and the bliss of meditative absorption; and contemplating the noble, sovereign spiritual powers of arhats, pratyekabuddhas, and great-power bodhisattvas. Accomplishing these five skillful contemplations, my disciples of the future age, after I have passed away, following a faith that grows ever stronger, relying on clear faith and according with the wisdom of the Dharma, will reach the final completion of understanding that the mind pure by its own nature is defiled by affliction — and this final completion is itself the cause for entering the path of the Mahāyāna. To have faith in the Tathāgata carries this great benefit: one does not slander this profound teaching."

Then Śrīmālā said to the Buddha: "There is yet further great benefit to speak of; relying on the Buddha's own majestic power, I will explain this meaning further." The Buddha said: "Then explain it." Śrīmālā said to the Buddha: "There are three kinds of good sons and daughters who, with respect to this profound meaning, do not harm themselves, but instead generate great virtue and enter the path of the Mahāyāna. What are the three? A good son or daughter who has personally accomplished the profound wisdom of the Dharma; a good son or daughter who has accomplished the wisdom that accords with the Dharma; and a good son or daughter who, unable to understand these profound dharmas on their own, looks up in deference to the World-Honored One, saying, 'This is not within my own domain — only the Buddha knows it.' This last is called a good son or daughter who defers to the Tathāgata.

"Apart from these good sons and daughters just described..."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang reads "my disciples" across this passage as spanning four progressive grades of faith, not the three Śrīmālā names outright: following-faith, at the stage of the ten faiths, believing simply because it has been taught; faith-grown-strong, at the stage of the ten abodes, likened via a citation from the Vimalakīrti Sūtra to standing "firm as vajra"; according-with-correct-wisdom, cultivated on the approach to the first ground through the five contemplations named in this very passage; and final attainment itself — the forbearance of the unarisen, from the first ground onward. Only this last stage constitutes full, direct realization; the earlier stages involve progressively less self-generated insight and correspondingly greater reliance on trust in what has been taught.

On why deferring to the Buddha's authority, without personally penetrating the "pure yet defiled" paradox, still counts as a complete and legitimate relationship to the teaching rather than a mere placeholder for real understanding, Jizang ties it back to his opening framing of the whole chapter: "one who has faith in the Tathāgata… having faith in what the Buddha has said, is freed from the icchantika obstruction, and therefore does not slander this profound meaning" (信如來者…既信佛所說，離闡提障，故不謗深義). Trust in the Buddha's own endorsement of Śrīmālā's teaching is, by itself, sufficient to avoid slandering a doctrine that exceeds one's own comprehension — the third kind of good son or daughter, who says only "this is not my domain," is not a lesser or failed version of the first two, but a complete category in its own right.
