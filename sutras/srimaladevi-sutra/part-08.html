Empty, Not Empty, and the One Truth Beneath the Four

Four short chapters carry the sūtra's most quoted formula anywhere — the empty tathāgatagarbha, and the not-empty tathāgatagarbha — then argue that only one of the four noble truths is truly ultimate, and close with the sūtra's own reversal of ordinary experience: permanence, bliss, self, and purity, correctly seen only in the Tathāgata's dharmakāya.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 8 of 10 complete Source: Taishō T0353 (tr. Guṇabhadra, 436 CE) Reading notes after Jizang's 勝鬘寶窟 (T1744)

The Empty and Not-Empty Tathāgatagarbha

Chinese Source

「世尊！如來藏智是如來空智。世尊！如來藏者，一切阿羅漢、辟支佛、大力菩薩，本所不見，本所不得。

「世尊！有二種如來藏空智。世尊！空如來藏，若離、若脫、若異一切煩惱藏。世尊！不空如來藏，過於恒沙不離、不脫、不異、不思議佛法。世尊！此二空智，諸大聲聞能信如來。一切阿羅漢、辟支佛空智，於四不顛倒境界轉。是故一切阿羅漢、辟支佛，本所不見、本所不得。一切苦滅，唯佛得證，壞一切煩惱藏，修一切滅苦道。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One! The wisdom of the tathāgatagarbha is the Tathāgata's own wisdom of emptiness. World-Honored One! The tathāgatagarbha is something that no arhat, pratyekabuddha, or great-power bodhisattva has ever seen or ever attained.

"World-Honored One! There are two kinds of tathāgatagarbha wisdom of emptiness. World-Honored One! The empty tathāgatagarbha is separate from, freed from, and different from every store of affliction. World-Honored One! The not-empty tathāgatagarbha is, beyond the sands of the Ganges, inseparable from, unreleased from, no different from, and inconceivable buddha-dharmas. World-Honored One! Only great śrāvakas who can believe in the Tathāgata accept these two wisdoms of emptiness. Every arhat's and pratyekabuddha's own wisdom of emptiness operates only within the domain of the four non-inverted views. This is why no arhat or pratyekabuddha has ever seen or ever attained the tathāgatagarbha — the complete cessation of all suffering, destroying every store of affliction and cultivating the complete path to that cessation, is realized by the Buddha alone."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang's key resolution: "empty" describes the afflictions doing the concealing, never the tathāgatagarbha itself — the afflictions are like flowers conjured in empty space, unreal, and it is precisely their unreality that earns the name "empty": "this is false, not a really-existing dharma… hence the dharma that does the concealing is what is called empty" (此是虛妄、非實有法…故說能覆之法名之為空). He resolves a related ambiguity about "wisdom of emptiness" itself: it is not that the object known is bare of marks, but that the cognizing wisdom is without marks because it knows the store's two aspects as one: "knowing the two kinds of store is called wisdom; the substance of that wisdom is without marks, hence it is called the wisdom of emptiness" (知二種藏名智，智體無相故名空智). Having settled that, he glosses the two terms plainly: the empty tathāgatagarbha is the store viewed as utterly separate from all affliction; the not-empty tathāgatagarbha is the identical store viewed as endowed with every buddha-quality beyond the sands of the Ganges — hence "not empty," citing the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's parallel: "the wise see both emptiness and non-emptiness — emptiness is the twenty-five states of existence, non-emptiness is great parinirvāṇa itself."

This is where Jizang's own Sanlun training becomes explicit rather than implicit: he folds the sūtra's empty/not-empty pair directly into his school's non-being/non-nonbeing middle way. Since the empty aspect rules out real existence — the afflictions are utterly empty — and the not-empty aspect rules out real nonexistence — the buddha-qualities are truly and fully present — "neither existent nor nonexistent: that is the middle way," citing the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own line that "buddha-nature is the seed of the middle way within the three kinds of bodhi" and that "the dharma of the middle way is itself called the Buddha." To attain both wisdoms at once, on this reading, just is to attain the middle way itself.

He then takes on two named rival readings. Teachers "south of the Yangtze" read "empty tathāgatagarbha" as the dharmakāya once it has already manifested, and "not-empty tathāgatagarbha" as the tathāgatagarbha still hidden and mixed together with affliction. Jizang rejects this on two grounds: the passage is explicitly about wisdom cognizing the tathāgatagarbha, not about a dharmakāya/tathāgatagarbha split; and grammatically, "every store of affliction" belongs to what the empty store is separated from, not to what "not-empty" is somehow made of — "not-empty" names buddha-qualities, never a residue of affliction. A separate reading, attributed to a "Master Lin," takes the whole passage as meaning that the very notion of emptiness itself is what conceals the real teaching, since the two lesser vehicles mistake emptiness for the ultimate truth. Jizang records this reading without adopting it.

Why Only Cessation Is Ultimate

Chinese Source

「世尊！此四聖諦，三是無常，一是常。何以故？三諦入有為相，入有為相者是無常。無常者是虛妄法。虛妄法者，非諦、非常、非依。是故苦諦、集諦、道諦，非第一義諦，非常、非依。

「一苦滅諦，離有為相，離有為相者是常。常者非虛妄法。非虛妄法者，是諦、是常、是依。是故滅諦，是第一義。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One! Of these four noble truths, three are impermanent and one is permanent. Why is this? Three of the truths fall within conditioned marks, and whatever falls within conditioned marks is impermanent. What is impermanent is a false dharma. A false dharma is neither truth, nor permanence, nor a true basis. This is why the truth of suffering, the truth of origin, and the truth of the path are not the truth of the first principle, are not permanent, and are not a true basis.

"The single truth of the cessation of suffering alone is apart from conditioned marks, and whatever is apart from conditioned marks is permanent. What is permanent is not a false dharma. What is not a false dharma is truth, is permanence, is a true basis. This is why the truth of cessation alone is the first principle."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang's own summary is blunt: "of these four truths, three are impermanent and one is permanent" (此四諦三是無常一是常) — suffering and origin arise and cease, "already existing where there once was nothing, and returning to nothing once again," and are therefore merely provisionally real; cessation alone stands apart from conditioned marks, permanent and not false, and so alone can serve as a true refuge. But he immediately adds a qualifier that keeps this from becoming a naive metaphysical claim: from the standpoint of ultimate principle, all four truths equally "enter suchness," and strictly speaking none of them can even be predicated as "permanent" or "impermanent" at all — citing the Middle Treatise's chapter on the Tathāgata directly: "within the mark of quiescence there is no permanence, no impermanence, and so on for every pair." He backs this with the sūtra's own later warning against over-literalizing the doctrine — fixing on nirvāṇa as permanent and saṃsāra as impermanent is itself "falling into the two views" of eternalism and nihilism.

He resolves the apparent tension with a fourfold breakdown: suffering and origin are impermanent only; cessation is permanent only; the path is permanent or impermanent depending on whether one considers its causal stage or its resultant stage; and beyond all three distinctions lies a silence that predicates neither term of anything. The doctrine that "only cessation is ultimate" is, on Jizang's reading, a teaching pitched at a specific corrective purpose — showing why the cessation-truth alone can ground the other three — not a final metaphysical inventory of which things really exist forever and which do not.

Inverted Views, and a Newborn Who Cannot See the Sun

Chinese Source

「不思議是滅諦，過一切眾生心識所緣，亦非一切阿羅漢、辟支佛智慧境界。譬如生盲不見眾色，七日嬰兒不見日輪。苦滅諦者，亦復如是！非一切凡夫心識所緣，亦非二乘智慧境界。凡夫識者二見顛倒；一切阿羅漢、辟支佛智者，則是清淨。邊見者，凡夫於五受陰，我見、妄想、計著，生二見，是名邊見，所謂常見、斷見。見諸行無常，是斷見非正見；見涅槃常，是常見非正見。妄想見故，作如是見。於身諸根分別思惟，現法見壞，於有相續不見，起於斷見，妄想見故；於心相續愚闇，不解不知，剎那間意識境界，起於常見，妄想見故。此妄想見，於彼義若過若不及，作異想分別，若斷若常。顛倒眾生，於五受陰，無常常想、苦有樂想、無我我想、不淨淨想。一切阿羅漢、辟支佛淨智者，於一切智境界及如來法身，本所不見。

「或有眾生，信佛語故，起常想、樂想、我想、淨想，非顛倒見，是名正見。何以故？如來法身是常波羅蜜、樂波羅蜜、我波羅蜜、淨波羅蜜。於佛法身，作是見者，是名正見。正見者，是佛真子，從佛口生、從正法生、從法化生，得法餘財。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"The truth of cessation is inconceivable, beyond the reach of every sentient being's own mind and consciousness, and not the domain of even an arhat's or pratyekabuddha's wisdom. It is like how one born blind cannot see any colors at all, or how a seven-day-old infant cannot look upon the sun's own disk. The truth of the cessation of suffering is exactly the same — not the domain of any ordinary being's mind and consciousness, nor the domain of the two lesser vehicles' wisdom. An ordinary being's own consciousness holds two inverted views; the wisdom of every arhat and pratyekabuddha, by contrast, is pure. As for these extreme views: ordinary beings, with respect to the five aggregates of clinging, generate the view of a self, false imagining, and grasping, and from these arise two views, called extreme views — namely the view of permanence and the view of annihilation. Seeing all conditioned formations as impermanent is the view of annihilation, not right view; seeing nirvāṇa as permanent is the view of permanence, not right view. It is because of false imagining that such views arise. Discriminating and reflecting on the body's own faculties, seeing them break down in this present life and failing to see any continuity in their ongoing existence, one gives rise to the view of annihilation, through false imagining; being confused and in the dark about the mind's own continuity, neither understanding nor knowing that the field of consciousness changes moment by moment, one gives rise to the view of permanence, through false imagining. This false imagining, whether it overshoots or falls short of the actual meaning, produces a different, discriminated notion — whether of annihilation or of permanence. Inverted beings, with respect to the five aggregates of clinging, hold the notion of permanence where there is impermanence, the notion of bliss where there is suffering, the notion of self where there is no self, and the notion of purity where there is impurity. Every arhat's and pratyekabuddha's pure wisdom, with respect to the domain of all-knowledge and the Tathāgata's dharmakāya, has never once seen it at all.

"But there are beings who, through faith in the Buddha's own words, give rise to the notion of permanence, of bliss, of self, and of purity — this is not an inverted view, but is called right view. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata's dharmakāya is the pāramitā of permanence, the pāramitā of bliss, the pāramitā of self, and the pāramitā of purity. Whoever holds this view of the Buddha's dharmakāya holds right view. One who holds right view is a true child of the Buddha, born from the Buddha's own mouth, born from the true Dharma, born through the transformation of the Dharma, inheriting the Dharma's own remaining wealth."

Reading note — after Jizang

On the newborn-and-sun simile, Jizang draws a precise distinction: ordinary beings are like one born blind, with no perception whatsoever of the cessation-truth's own "colors"; arhats and pratyekabuddhas are like the seven-day-old infant, whose eyes function but are simply overwhelmed by the sun's own brightness — "the eyes are weak and the sun's disk too strong, hence there is no clear seeing" (眼弱、日輪強，故不明了). The two vehicles, on this reading, attain only the cessation of the phenomenal event of suffering, without ever realizing the dharmakāya's own inherent, fundamental quiescence — eradicating affliction is one achievement, recognizing intrinsic quiescence a different one entirely, and the two do not automatically come together.

Jizang records, and ultimately declines, an alternative chapter division here: an unnamed source argued the material on the "two views" and the infant simile actually belongs with the previous chapter on inversion and reality rather than starting a new one, since it already concerns the errors of both ordinary beings and the two vehicles. He allows this fits the text and the doctrine well — "this account, in both text and meaning, is quite good" (此釋於今文及義並好) — but declines to adopt it anyway, purely so as not to conflict with the sūtra's own received fourteen-chapter title sequence, an unusually candid moment of weighing doctrinal fit against inherited structure and choosing structure.

On eternalism and nihilism, he is precise about what each actually consists in: nihilism arises from seeing conditioned formations as impermanent and totally perishing with no continuation at all; eternalism arises from seeing nirvāṇa as permanent — but Jizang stresses that even this can be an extreme view, if nirvāṇa is grasped as a fixed, self-natured permanence rather than "a permanence arising from conditions, not a permanence of fixed nature" (因緣常，非定性常). He supports this with the Ratnagotravibhāga's own formula — "not annihilating conditioned formations, so as to be free of the extreme of nihilism; not grasping at unconditioned nirvāṇa, so as to be free of the extreme of eternalism" — glossed with the Madhyamaka identity of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa already familiar from the Middle Treatise: "the true limit of birth-and-death and the limit of nirvāṇa — these two limits are not different from each other by so much as a hair's breadth," and Sengzhao's own line, "is the path really so far away? Touch any matter, and there is the real."

The Four Bases, and the One Beneath Them

Chinese Source

「世尊！淨智者，一切阿羅漢、辟支佛智波羅蜜，此淨智者雖曰淨智，於彼滅諦，尚非境界，況四依智？何以故？三乘初業，不愚於法。於彼義當覺當得，為彼故，世尊說四依。世尊！此四依者，是世間法。世尊！一依者，一切依止，出世間上上第一義依，所謂滅諦。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One! As for pure wisdom — the perfected wisdom of every arhat and pratyekabuddha — although this is called pure wisdom, it does not even reach the domain of the truth of cessation itself, let alone the wisdom of the four bases. Why is this? Because beginners in any of the three vehicles are not utterly ignorant of the Dharma; with respect to that meaning, they will yet come to awaken to it and attain it — and it is for their sake that the World-Honored One teaches the four bases. World-Honored One! These four bases are worldly dharmas. World-Honored One! The one basis — the ground of every dependence, the supreme, supramundane basis of the first principle — is none other than the truth of cessation."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang closes this stretch of the sūtra by defending its claim that beginning practitioners of the three vehicles are "not utterly ignorant of the Dharma" against what looks like a flat contradiction with the Lotus Sūtra, which elsewhere treats śrāvakas as needing to be drawn out of real ignorance by expedient means. Jiangnan Satyasiddhi masters had tried to resolve this by saying Śrīmālā speaks from principle while the Lotus speaks from expedient teaching; Jizang rejects that move outright, since it would wrongly demote the Lotus to something less than a fully definitive teaching. His own resolution distinguishes non-ignorance at an initial stage of practice from non-ignorance at a later stage — the two texts are describing different points along the same path, not disagreeing about the path itself.

He backs this discussion with an anecdote about his own broader tradition: Zhizang of Kaishan Monastery (開善智藏法師) originally taught that real śrāvaka-vehicle attainment exists as such, until the eccentric monk Baozhi (志公) cryptically rebuked him in verse — after which Zhizang reversed his own position and denied any truly separate śrāvaka vehicle at all. Jizang also rejects two further readings that identify the mere feeling of "wearying of suffering and seeking joy" with buddha-nature's own "correct cause" directly; his own view is that a being can feel this weariness only because buddha-nature is already present within, not that the feeling itself constitutes that nature — keeping cause and its felt symptom carefully distinct.
