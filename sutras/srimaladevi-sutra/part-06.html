The Lion's Roar, and the One True Refuge

Chapter Five closes with its title's own promise fulfilled: the Tathāgata's unimpeded declaration that he will never again take birth, set against the arhat's more modest, more provisional version of the same claim — and the argument that refuge in Dharma and Saṅgha is, at bottom, never anything other than refuge in the Tathāgata himself.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 6 of 10 complete Source: Taishō T0353 (tr. Guṇabhadra, 436 CE) Reading notes after Jizang's 勝鬘寶窟 (T1744)

The Tathāgata's Own Lion's Roar

Chinese Source

法王法主，而得自在，登一切法自在之地。如來、應、等正覺正師子吼：『我生已盡，梵行已立，所作已辦，不受後有。』是故世尊以師子吼依於了義，一向記說。

「世尊！不受後有智有二種。謂：如來以無上調御降伏四魔，出一切世間，為一切眾生之所瞻仰，得不思議法身。於一切爾焰地得無礙法自在，於上更無所作。無所得地十力勇猛，昇於第一無上無畏之地，一切爾炎無礙智觀不由於他，不受後有智師子吼。」

Ru-Yi Translation

He becomes the Lord and Master of the Dharma, attains sovereign mastery, and ascends to the ground of sovereignty over every dharma. The Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, then truly roars the lion's roar: "My rebirth is exhausted, the holy life is established, what had to be done is done, there will be no further becoming." This is why the World-Honored One, through the lion's roar, speaks in reliance on the complete and final meaning, pronouncing it as a single, decisive record.

"World-Honored One! There are two kinds of the wisdom of 'no further becoming.' The Tathāgata, with unsurpassed mastery, subdues the four māras, transcends every world, is looked up to by every being, and attains the inconceivable dharmakāya. Within every field of the knowable he attains unobstructed mastery of the Dharma, and beyond this there is nothing further left for him to do. Standing on the ground of no attainment, valiant with the ten powers, he ascends to the foremost, unsurpassed, fearless ground; his unobstructed wisdom-vision of every field of the knowable relies on no other — this is the lion's roar of the wisdom of no further becoming."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang explains the name directly: when the Tathāgata's declaration of no further becoming is grounded in his own unimpeded wisdom-vision, it is called a lion's roar precisely because "the principle is settled beyond doubt" (道理決定，名師子吼) — a pronouncement with no hedge or qualification left in it anywhere. He contrasts this with the two vehicles' own four kinds of knowledge, which are, in his phrase, never truly self-attained: "the two vehicles' four kinds of knowledge are obtained by being taught by another; the Tathāgata realizes his own directly" (小乘四智由他教得，如來自證故). The same four-line proclamation — "my rebirth is exhausted," and so on — means something different depending entirely on who is speaking it.

The Arhat's Resting Place Is Not the Real Destination

Chinese Source

「世尊！阿羅漢、辟支佛度生死畏，次第得解脫樂，作是念：『我離生死恐怖，不受生死苦。』世尊！阿羅漢、辟支佛觀察時，得不受後有，觀第一蘇息處涅槃地。世尊！彼先所得地，不愚於法、不由於他，亦自知得有餘地，必當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。何以故？

聲聞、緣覺乘皆入大乘，大乘者即是佛乘，是故三乘即是一乘。得一乘者得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者即是涅槃界，涅槃界者即是如來法身，得究竟法身者則究竟一乘，無異如來、無異法身，如來即法身；得究竟法身者，則究竟一乘，究竟者即是無邊不斷。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One! Arhats and pratyekabuddhas, having crossed beyond the fear of birth-and-death, attain in stages the bliss of liberation, and think to themselves: 'I am free of the dread of birth-and-death; I will not undergo the suffering of birth-and-death again.' World-Honored One! When arhats and pratyekabuddhas examine their own attainment of no further becoming in this way, they regard it as the foremost resting place, the ground of nirvāṇa. World-Honored One! As for the ground they have already attained, they are not deluded about the Dharma there, and do not depend on another to know it — they know for themselves that what they have attained is only a partial ground, and that they must still go on to attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. Why is this?

Because the śrāvaka vehicle and the pratyekabuddha vehicle both enter into the Mahāyāna, and the Mahāyāna is itself the buddha-vehicle — hence the three vehicles are, in truth, the One Vehicle. Whoever attains the One Vehicle attains unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening; unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening is itself the realm of nirvāṇa; the realm of nirvāṇa is itself the Tathāgata's dharmakāya. Whoever attains the ultimate dharmakāya thereby attains the ultimate One Vehicle — there is no Tathāgata apart from this, and no dharmakāya apart from this; the Tathāgata is precisely the dharmakāya. Whoever attains the ultimate dharmakāya attains the ultimate One Vehicle, and this ultimacy is itself boundless and unceasing."

Reading note — after Jizang

On the "resting place" (蘇息處), Jizang surveys two prior readings before setting both aside: one takes the phrase as referring jointly to Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna nirvāṇa together, another as the two vehicles' own claim to a final nirvāṇa free of the death of inconceivable transformation. He treats the passage more simply, as one continuous error rather than two separate claims: the two vehicles wrongly regard their own extinction of afflictions as final — "they consider it nirvāṇa, and consider it ultimate" (自謂涅槃究竟) — the identical mistake, in a different key, as their earlier wrong claim that their four kinds of knowledge were already complete. A way station mistaken for the destination is still one mistake, however many places in the text it shows up.

Jizang also distinguishes two kinds of śrāvaka who are eventually converted to this teaching: those who never heard the Mahāyāna at all and had no bodhi-seed to begin with (本乘聲聞), and those who once heard the One Vehicle teaching, generated bodhicitta, and then retreated back into the lesser vehicle anyway (退菩提心聲聞). Both count as "dharma-deluded" the moment they mistake arhatship for something final — "if they realize arhatship, they consider it ultimate, and do not know there is still buddhahood to attain" (若證羅漢，則自謂究竟，不知作佛) — and both become undeluded only upon hearing the One Vehicle teaching and turning back toward buddhahood, which he supports with the Lotus Sūtra's own Parable of the Conjured City, where travelers who mistake a phantom resting place for their real destination eventually "attain entry into the Buddha's wisdom" (得入佛慧) once the illusion is dispelled.

Boundless Compassion, and Why Three Refuges Are One

Chinese Source

「世尊！如來無有限齊時住，如來、應、等正覺後際等住。如來無限齊大悲，亦無限齊安慰世間。無限大悲、無限安慰世間，作是說者是名善說如來。若復說言無盡法、常住法，一切世間之所歸依者，亦名善說如來。是故於未度世間、無依世間，與後際等，作無盡歸依、常住歸依者，謂如來、應、等正覺也。

「法者即是說一乘道，僧者是三乘眾。此二歸依非究竟歸依，名少分歸依。何以故？說一乘道法，得究竟法身，於上更無說一乘法身。三乘眾者有恐怖，歸依如來，求出修學向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是故二依非究竟依，是有限依。

「若有眾生，如來調伏，歸依如來得法津澤，生信樂心歸依法、僧，是二歸依；非此二歸依，是歸依如來。歸依第一義者是歸依如來。此二歸依第一義，是究竟歸依如來。何以故？無異如來，無異二歸依。如來即三歸依。何以故？說一乘道。如來四無畏成就師子吼說，若如來隨彼所欲而方便說，即是大乘，無有三乘。三乘者入於一乘，一乘者即第一義乘。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One! The Tathāgata dwells for no limited or fixed span of time; the Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, dwells equal to the very final limit of time itself. The Tathāgata's great compassion knows no limit or bound, nor does his comforting of the world know any limit or bound. To speak in this way — of boundless great compassion, of boundlessly comforting the world — is what it means to speak well of the Tathāgata. And to say further that he is an inexhaustible dharma, an ever-abiding dharma, the refuge of the whole world, is likewise to speak well of the Tathāgata. This is why, for a world not yet delivered, a world without shelter, one who remains equal to the very final limit of time, and who becomes an inexhaustible refuge, an ever-abiding refuge, is called the Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened.

"'Dharma' here means the teaching of the path of the One Vehicle; 'Saṅgha' means the assembly of the three vehicles. These two refuges are not the ultimate refuge — they are called partial refuges. Why is this? The Dharma that teaches the path of the One Vehicle itself attains the ultimate dharmakāya, and there is no further dharmakāya beyond it that some other teaching of the One Vehicle could point to. The assembly of the three vehicles still has fear, and takes refuge in the Tathāgata, seeking to be delivered and to train toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening — this is why these two, Dharma and Saṅgha, are not the ultimate refuge, but only a limited one.

"If there are beings whom the Tathāgata has tamed, who take refuge in the Tathāgata and receive the moisture of the Dharma, and who, with a mind of joyful faith, take refuge also in Dharma and Saṅgha — this taking refuge in the two is not, in the end, a refuge apart from taking refuge in the Tathāgata himself. To take refuge in the first principle is to take refuge in the Tathāgata. These two refuges, understood at the level of the first principle, are the ultimate refuge in the Tathāgata. Why is this? There is no Tathāgata apart from them, and no two refuges apart from him — the Tathāgata is himself the three refuges. Why is this? Because he teaches the path of the One Vehicle. The Tathāgata, having accomplished the four fearlessnesses, roars the lion's roar and teaches: whenever the Tathāgata teaches according to what a listener wishes, through skillful means, it is nonetheless the Mahāyāna — there are no truly separate three vehicles. The three vehicles all enter into the One Vehicle, and the One Vehicle is itself the vehicle of the first principle."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang parses the Tathāgata's fourfold description here as two pairs, not four independent claims: the first two lines concern permanence of time — "the first states the dharmakāya's ever-abiding nature, the second the response-body's ever-abiding nature" (前明法身常住，後明應身常住) — while the latter two concern boundless activity on others' behalf, compassion and comfort. He supports the response-body's endless activity with a citation from the Treatise on Buddha Nature: "since sentient beings are inexhaustible, the transformation-body's transforming of beings is inexhaustible as well" (眾生無盡，故化身常化物無盡).

The collapse of the three refuges into one is where Jizang's own Sanlun training shows most clearly. He reads "Dharma" here as standing for every provisional teaching — the various three- and five-vehicle doctrines are themselves only the causal path leading toward buddhahood, not the resultant fruit itself — and "Saṅgha" as śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and even bodhisattvas who have not yet merged with the Buddha's own substance. Understood this way, refuge in Dharma and Saṅgha is "merely a provisional refuge, and so is called partial" (但是權歸，故名少分) — not because Dharma and Saṅgha are somehow lesser in themselves, but because, described at the level of provisional teaching-content, they remain causal rather than resultant. Once understood instead at the level of the first principle — ultimate truth, resultant virtue — Dharma and Saṅgha are simply "not different from the Tathāgata" (無異如來), and refuge in them just is refuge in the Tathāgata.

He resolves the odd-sounding claim that "the Tathāgata is himself the three refuges" — rather than the Tathāgata being one refuge that somehow absorbs two others — by decomposing his single substance into three aspects at once: "as awakening, the Tathāgata's whole being is called Buddha; as norm, his whole being is called Dharma; as harmony, his whole being is called Saṅgha" (如來舉體覺義為佛、舉體軌義為法、舉體和義為僧). This is Jizang's signature move: not one thing subsuming two others, but one reality carrying three names at once — the same dissolution of apparent plurality into a single underlying substance that his school applies everywhere else. He also gives three concrete reasons the Buddha ever taught a threefold refuge to begin with, rather than only the one ultimate refuge from the start: to counter rival triads taught by other traditions; as a medical analogy, with the Buddha as physician, Dharma as medicine, and Saṅgha as nurse, all three needed together to treat the disease of affliction; and to answer three distinct fears, illustrated by a deer that escapes a hunter in three successive bounds — the same three-stage pattern, he notes, as the Lotus Sūtra's "three turnings of the Dharma-wheel," where the original, single teaching gives way to provisional branch teachings pitched at limited capacities, before those branches are finally gathered back into the one root they came from.

Part 6 Complete

This closes Part 6, and with it Chapter Five — the sūtra's longest chapter, spanning Parts 4 through 6: the One Vehicle as the source of every vehicle, the arhat's incomplete liberation explained through two kinds of death and the ignorance-dwelling-ground, and the Tathāgata's own lion's roar, in which every refuge collapses into one. Part 7 turns to Chapters Six through Eight, where the sūtra names the tathāgatagarbha for the first time — the term this whole sūtra is best known for.
