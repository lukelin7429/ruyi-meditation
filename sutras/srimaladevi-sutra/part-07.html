The Tathāgatagarbha, Named at Last

Three short chapters carry this sūtra's most consequential move: the noble truths themselves are shown to rest on something deeper, "the tathāgatagarbha" is spoken for the first time, and the dharmakāya already discussed in earlier chapters turns out to be the identical reality, seen from a different angle.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 7 of 10 complete Source: Taishō T0353 (tr. Guṇabhadra, 436 CE) Reading notes after Jizang's 勝鬘寶窟 (T1744)

Only Vajra-Like Wisdom Can Finish the Task

Chinese Source

「世尊！聲聞、緣覺初觀聖諦，以一智斷諸住地；以一智四斷、知、功德、作證，亦善知此四法義。世尊！無有出世間上上智、四智漸至及四緣漸至，無漸至法是出世間上上智。

「世尊！金剛喻者是第一義智。世尊！非聲聞、緣覺不斷無明住地，初聖諦智是第一義智。世尊！以無二聖諦智斷諸住地。世尊！如來、應、等正覺，非一切聲聞、緣覺境界。不思議空智，斷一切煩惱藏。世尊！若壞一切煩惱藏究竟智，是名第一義智。初聖諦智非究竟智，向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提智。

「世尊！聖義者，非一切聲聞、緣覺，聲聞、緣覺成就有量功德，聲聞、緣覺成就少分功德，故名之為聖。聖諦者，非聲聞、緣覺諦；亦非聲聞、緣覺功德。世尊！此諦如來、應、等正覺初始覺知，然後為無明㲉藏世間開現演說，是故名聖諦。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One! When śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas first contemplate the noble truths, they sever the dwelling-grounds with a single kind of wisdom; with this same single wisdom they sever, know, realize the virtue of, and bear witness to all four truths at once, and skillfully understand the meaning of these four dharmas as well. World-Honored One! There is no supramundane, supreme wisdom reached gradually through four kinds of knowledge or four kinds of condition — a dharma reached only gradually is not the supramundane, supreme wisdom.

"World-Honored One! Vajra-like wisdom is the wisdom of the first principle. World-Honored One! It is not the case that śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas fail to sever the ignorance-dwelling-ground, and yet the very first wisdom that contemplates the noble truths is already the wisdom of the first principle. World-Honored One! It is by a single, undivided wisdom of the noble truths that the dwelling-grounds are severed. World-Honored One! This is the domain of the Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, and not the domain of any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha at all. It is the inconceivable wisdom of emptiness that severs every store of affliction entirely. World-Honored One! Only the wisdom that utterly destroys every store of affliction may be called the wisdom of the first principle; the wisdom that first contemplates the noble truths is not yet this ultimate wisdom — it is only wisdom heading toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

"World-Honored One! What is meant by 'noble' does not belong to any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha whatsoever. Śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas accomplish only a measured virtue, only a fractional virtue — and it is only in this limited sense that they are called 'noble.' The noble truths are not the truths of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha, nor are they their virtue. World-Honored One! It is the Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, who first awakens to and knows this truth, and only afterward opens it up and expounds it for a world wrapped in the shell of ignorance — and it is for this reason that it is called the noble truth."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang's coming-intent analysis ties this chapter directly to what came before: practice cannot arise without a supporting object, so once Chapter Five has established the One Vehicle as practice's fruit, the text must next establish the principle that practice actually rests on — the noble truths. More specifically, since the One Vehicle depends on the tathāgatagarbha, and the tathāgatagarbha only becomes manifest through the four truths, "the One Vehicle depends on the tathāgatagarbha, and the tathāgatagarbha is made manifest through the four truths — hence the truths must be explained" (一乘依如來藏，如來藏由四諦而顯，故須明諦也). The chapter also has a polemical edge: it refutes śrāvakas who take the provisional four truths of the first turning of the wheel as the final word.

On "vajra-like wisdom," Jizang gives two complementary glosses: the Tathāgata's wisdom is permanent, "not destroyed by arising and ceasing," comparable to vajra's own indestructibility; and vajra destroys everything without residue, symbolizing that this wisdom alone can sever the ignorance-dwelling-ground completely, reaching all the way to the unconditioned truth rather than resting content with the conditioned four truths. He records, and sets aside, an alternate reading that takes "vajra" as naming the Buddha's own śūraṅgama-samādhi at the moment of his enlightenment, preferring instead the reading built on permanence and completeness.

The Tathāgatagarbha, Named

Chinese Source

「聖諦者，說甚深義，微細難知，非思量境界。是智者所知，一切世間所不能信。何以故？此說甚深如來之藏。如來藏者，是如來境界，非一切聲聞、緣覺所知。如來藏處，說聖諦義。如來藏處甚深故，說聖諦亦甚深，微細難知，非思量境界，是智者所知，一切世間所不能信。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"The noble truths speak of a meaning so profound, so subtle and hard to know, that it is not the domain of ordinary thought at all — it is known only by the wise, and cannot be believed by the whole world. Why is this? Because this speaks of the profound tathāgatagarbha itself. The tathāgatagarbha is the Tathāgata's own domain, not known by any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. It is at the very place of the tathāgatagarbha that the meaning of the noble truths is spoken. Because the place of the tathāgatagarbha is so profound, the noble truths spoken there are likewise profound, subtle and hard to know, not the domain of ordinary thought — known only by the wise, and unable to be believed by the whole world."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang reports a real, unresolved disagreement over exactly where this chapter — barely six lines in the sūtra itself — actually begins. A "more recent lecturer" (晚講人) places its start one clause earlier than the traditional reading; the older, traditional reading keeps that clause inside the noble-truths chapter, since its stated topic isn't finished yet, and warns that pulling tathāgatagarbha and dharmakāya material out early would leave the chapter "jumbled" (雜亂); and a third view, from a Jiangnan master named Min, ties a different clause to the theme of "emptiness concealing" borrowed from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's milk-and-cream simile — a reading Jizang rejects on textual grounds, since the relevant passage never actually mentions "emptiness concealing" and is really about the dharmakāya's inseparability, not about buddha-nature directly. His own resolution treats Chapters Six through Eight as one continuous unfolding of a single reality — the cessation-truth — described first as itself, then as dharmakāya, then as tathāgatagarbha: "one substance, different meanings" (體一義異).

On why this meaning is "not the domain of thought," Jizang correlates the sūtra's phrases with the classical scheme of wisdoms: what is "subtle" lies beyond the wisdom gained from hearing; what is "hard to know" lies beyond the wisdom gained from reflection; what is "not the domain of thought" lies beyond ordinary cultivated wisdom; and only "what the wise know" reaches the wisdom of direct realization. Hence, in his own summary, "those with the three [ordinary] wisdoms still cannot know it; those without any of the three cannot even believe it" (有三慧者不知、無三慧者不信) — only a buddha, "wise, awakened, and seeing," truly knows and sees it. He also gives three separate reasons this teaching counts as "deep": saṃsāra itself is the tathāgatagarbha, which he supports with the Avataṃsaka's "mind, buddha, and sentient beings — these three are without distinction"; a sentient being's own body is identical with the dharmakāya; and the noble truth is itself the one true truth, "neither suffering, nor origin, nor cessation, nor path" — depth's own extreme.

The Dharmakāya, and Two Kinds of Noble Truth

Chinese Source

「若於無量煩惱藏所纏如來藏不疑惑者，於出無量煩惱藏法身亦無疑惑。於說如來藏、如來法身、不思議佛境界及方便說，心得決定者，此則信解說二聖諦。如是難知難解者，謂說二聖諦義。何等為說二聖諦義？謂說作聖諦義、說無作聖諦義。說作聖諦義者，是說有量四聖諦。何以故？非因他能知一切苦、斷一切集、證一切滅、修一切道。是故世尊！有有為生死、無為生死。涅槃亦如是，有餘及無餘。說無作聖諦義者，說無量四聖諦義。何以故？能以自力知一切受苦、斷一切受集、證一切受滅、修一切受滅道。如是八聖諦，如來說四聖諦。如是四無作聖諦義，唯如來、應、等正覺事究竟，非阿羅漢、辟支佛事究竟。何以故？非下、中、上法得涅槃。

「何以故？如來、應、等正覺，於無作四聖諦義事究竟，以一切如來、應、等正覺，知一切未來苦、斷一切煩惱上煩惱所攝受一切集、滅一切意生身、除一切苦滅作證。

「世尊！非壞法故，名為苦滅。所言苦滅者，名無始無作、無起無盡、離盡常住、自性清淨，離一切煩惱藏。世尊！過於恒沙不離、不脫、不異、不思議佛法成就，說如來法身。世尊！如是如來法身不離煩惱藏，名如來藏。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Whoever has no doubt about the tathāgatagarbha wrapped within immeasurable stores of affliction likewise has no doubt about the dharmakāya freed from those immeasurable stores of affliction. Whoever attains a settled mind concerning the teaching of the tathāgatagarbha, the Tathāgata's dharmakāya, the inconceivable domain of buddhahood, and the skillful means by which they are taught, has thereby come to believe in and understand what is called the two noble truths. This difficult-to-know, difficult-to-understand matter is exactly what is meant by speaking of the two noble truths. What are these two noble truths? The conditioned noble truths, and the unconditioned noble truths. The conditioned noble truths are the four noble truths taught as limited. Why is this? Because those who are taught them cannot, through their own power alone, know all suffering, sever all its origin, realize all its cessation, and cultivate the complete path. This is why, World-Honored One, there is a conditioned birth-and-death, and an unconditioned birth-and-death; nirvāṇa is likewise of two kinds, with remainder and without remainder. The unconditioned noble truths are the four noble truths taught as measureless. Why is this? Because those who realize them are able, through their own power, to know every kind of suffering received, sever every kind of its origin received, realize every kind of its cessation received, and cultivate the path to the cessation of every kind received. These are, in this sense, eight noble truths, though the Tathāgata speaks of them as four. These unconditioned four noble truths are brought to their ultimate completion only by the Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened — not by arhats or pratyekabuddhas. Why is this? Because nirvāṇa is not attained by a lesser, middling, or merely superior dharma.

"Why is this? Because the Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, brings the meaning of the unconditioned four noble truths to their ultimate completion — since every tathāgata, worthy one, perfectly and fully awakened, knows all future suffering, severs every origin gathered in by every affliction and higher affliction, extinguishes every mind-made body, and bears witness to the cessation of all suffering.

"World-Honored One! It is not by destroying some existing dharma that this is called the cessation of suffering. What is called the cessation of suffering is named: without beginning, unmade, without arising, inexhaustible, apart from all exhaustion, ever-abiding, inherently pure by its own nature, and free of every store of affliction. World-Honored One! It is the accomplishment of buddha-dharmas beyond the sands of the Ganges, inseparable from, unreleased from, not different from, and inconceivable, that is called the Tathāgata's dharmakāya. World-Honored One! And it is this very dharmakāya of the Tathāgata, not yet separate from the store of affliction, that is called the tathāgatagarbha."

Reading note — after Jizang

Jizang offers four complementary lenses for the conditioned/unconditioned distinction: coarse phenomenal birth-death against subtle transformational birth-death; truths covering only ordinary saṃsāric causation against truths covering both ordinary and transformational causation; the shallower realization of schools that know only conditioned arising and the self's own emptiness against the Mahāyāna's full realization of the tathāgatagarbha; and knowing the four truths only in gross aggregate against knowing their infinitely differentiated particulars — "knowing a single instance of suffering, and thereby knowing suffering as numerous as the specks of dust in the whole dharma-realm" (知一苦中，具知法界微塵數苦). He locates the real dividing line in whether realization is self-attained: the conditioned truths are for those who still depend on being taught by another, while the unconditioned truths are known entirely "through the Tathāgata's own power" (如來自力故知).

On "cessation of suffering" as something positive rather than a mere subtraction, Jizang draws a sharp line against the śrāvaka's own "annihilative cessation" — what he elsewhere calls, bluntly, "ashes-and-extinguished-cognition" (灰身滅智) — which he treats as inferior and only provisional. The Mahāyāna cessation he glosses term by term: "without beginning," because principle-cessation was never newly produced by any cause; "without arising, inexhaustible," because what was never falsely brought into being cannot truly perish into nothing; "inherently pure by its own nature," an intrinsic purity present even while still concealed. He backs this with a citation from the Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra: "if a dharma existed before and does not exist after, the buddhas and bodhisattvas would themselves bear a fault" — true cessation, in other words, cannot be simple destruction, on pain of implicating the buddhas in a kind of cosmic loss.

Jizang's central move across all three chapters is the identity he draws between dharmakāya and tathāgatagarbha: "the tathāgatagarbha and the dharmakāya are not two things at all — they are merely names for what is concealed and what is manifest. Concealing the dharmakāya, it is called tathāgatagarbha; manifesting the tathāgatagarbha, it is called dharmakāya" (如來藏之與法身更無有二，只是隱顯之名，隱法身故名如來藏、顯如來藏故名法身). He immediately qualifies this against a subtler danger — reifying the very distinction he has just drawn: from the standpoint of true principle, there is never any actual concealing or manifesting going on at all; "concealment conceals nothing real, and manifestation manifests nothing real" (隱實無所隱，顯實無所顯) — these are only descriptions tracking a being's own delusion or awakening, never real changes undergone by the thing itself. He puts the whole three-chapter arc together as a single nested unfolding of the cessation-truth: first stated as itself, then, viewed as the aspect that has emerged from its wrapping, called dharmakāya; then, viewed as still wrapped in affliction, the identical reality is called tathāgatagarbha — one thing, three descriptions, each answering to how far a given listener has traveled toward seeing it clearly.

Part 7 Complete

This closes Part 7: the noble truths shown to rest on the tathāgatagarbha, the tathāgatagarbha named outright for the first time, and its identity with the dharmakāya under two different descriptions — concealed, or manifest.
