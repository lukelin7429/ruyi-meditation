Distinguishing the Characteristics — False Discrimination, the Three Natures, and the Sixteen Emptinesses

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of Part One of the Madhyāntavibhāga, "Distinguishing the Middle from the Extremes" (辯中邊論). Maitreya's opening chapter sets out the treatise's entire program in a single verse — characteristics, obstructions, reality, the cultivation of remedies, the stages of that cultivation, the fruit attained, and the unsurpassed vehicle — before turning to its own subject: false discrimination, the constructing activity of mind that appears as an object grasped and a subject that grasps, though neither grasped nor grasper truly exists. From this single insight the chapter derives the three natures, the middle way between "everything is empty" and "everything is real," a systematic account of emptiness's sixteen kinds, and the treatise's most quoted line — that the mind's own nature is originally pure, and only ever tainted by dust that was never its own.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 1 of 8 complete Source: Taishō T31, no. 1600, 辯中邊論 (Maitreya, comm. Vasubandhu, tr. Xuánzàng) Commentary guide: Kuījī's 辯中邊論述記 (T1835)

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuánzàng's seventh-century translation of the Madhyāntavibhāga (辯中邊論, T1600): root verses attributed to Maitreya, with a prose commentary (長行) by Vasubandhu carried directly beneath each verse — the same one-text, two-voice structure this site's Viṃśatikā already follows, though here the verses and the commentary belong to two different authors rather than one. For a second layer of reading, we follow Kuījī, Xuánzàng's direct disciple, whose commentary (辯中邊論述記, T1835) preserves the same "Xuánzàng system" of terminology already running through this site's Cheng Weishi Lun, Mahāyānasaṃgraha, and Viṃśatikā.

Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right. Reading notes drawn from Kuījī's commentary follow each passage. See also this site's note on translation method.

A Treatise with Three Authors

Kuījī opens his commentary with a lineage most readers would miss from the Chinese title alone. Nine hundred years after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, he explains, Asaṅga ascended to Maitreya's presence and asked him to teach a great treatise; Maitreya spoke the root verses translated here — "Distinguishing the Middle from the Extremes." Asaṅga, having received them, then entrusted his younger brother Vasubandhu with composing the prose commentary that gives the verses their fully worked-out sense: what this site translates, line by line, as "the treatise says" (論曰) beneath each verse.

Chinese Source

稽首造此論，　善逝體所生，

及教我等師，　當勤顯斯義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Homage to him who composed this treatise,

born of the Well-Gone One's own body,

and to our teacher, who taught it to us —

I shall now diligently make its meaning manifest.

Reading note — after Kuījī

Kuījī reads this homage as reaching two people at once. The first three phrases bow to Maitreya himself — called "born of the Well-Gone One's own body" because, poised to succeed Śākyamuni as the next buddha, he is the Buddha's true spiritual heir, not merely a disciple. The final phrase bows to Asaṅga, named simply "our teacher," who taught Vasubandhu (and, through him, Vasubandhu's own students — the "us" of "taught us") to compose this very commentary. Vasubandhu, writing in his own voice for the first time in the opening line, then promises to make the verses' meaning plain.

1. The Treatise's Whole Program in One Verse

Before any topic is taken up individually, the treatise states its entire seven-part structure in a single verse — the outline that gives this site's eight pages their shape, and that this chapter itself begins to unpack.

Chinese Source

唯相障真實，　及修諸對治，

即此修分位，　得果無上乘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Only this: characteristics, obstructions, reality,

and the cultivation of every remedy,

the stages of this very cultivation,

the fruit attained, and the unsurpassed vehicle.

論曰：此論唯說如是七義，一相、二障、三真實、四修諸對治、五即此修分位、六得果、七無上乘。今於此中先辯其相。

What does this mean? This treatise speaks only of these seven topics: first, characteristics; second, obstructions; third, reality; fourth, the cultivation of every remedy; fifth, the stages of this very cultivation; sixth, the fruit attained; seventh, the unsurpassed vehicle. Here, we first distinguish its characteristics.

2. False Discrimination: What Exists, and What Does Not

The chapter's opening move states, in four tight lines, the entire relationship between false discrimination — the constructing activity of an unenlightened mind — and the emptiness that is its true nature. This single verse is quoted directly by the Mahāyānasaṃgraha, where it anchors that treatise's own account of the dependent nature.

Chinese Source

虛妄分別有，　於此二都無，

此中唯有空，　於彼亦有此。

Ru-Yi Translation

False discrimination exists;

within it, the two are entirely absent;

within this, there is only emptiness;

and within that, too, this exists.

論曰：虛妄分別有者，謂有所取、能取分別。於此二都無者，謂即於此虛妄分別，永無所取、能取二性。此中唯有空者，謂虛妄分別中但有離所取及能取空性。於彼亦有此者，謂即於彼二空性中亦但有此虛妄分別。若於此非有，由彼觀為空；所餘非無故，如實知為有。若如是者，則能無倒顯示空相。

What does this mean? "False discrimination exists" means: the discrimination of the grasped and the grasper exists. "Within it, the two are entirely absent" means: within this very false discrimination, the two natures of the grasped and the grasper are forever absent. "Within this, there is only emptiness" means: within false discrimination there is only the emptiness that is free of the grasped and the grasper. "And within that, too, this exists" means: within that very emptiness, too, there is only this false discrimination. Where this — the grasped and the grasper — is absent, one observes it, by that very absence, as empty; and what remains is not itself non-existent, so one truly knows it as existent. If one understands it this way, one is then able to display the character of emptiness without any inversion.

Reading note — after Kuījī

Kuījī treats this verse as the formula behind the whole chapter: "where this is absent, by that absence it is empty; what remains is not non-existent, so one knows it truly as existent" — meaning the three natures are, in a single stroke, all accounted for. The discriminated, attached-to nature — a truly external grasped object and a truly separate grasping subject — is what is entirely absent, and so is known as non-existent. The dependent nature, false discrimination itself, and the perfectly accomplished nature, the emptiness that is its true nature, are both what "remains," and so both are truly known as existent — though, as the next section makes clear, existent in two quite different senses.

3. Neither Empty Nor Not Empty: The Middle Way

Having stated that both false discrimination and emptiness exist while the grasped and the grasper do not, the treatise draws out the consequence directly: no dharma is ever simply "empty" or simply "not empty" — and this, it claims, is exactly what the Middle Way, and the Prajñāpāramitā scriptures, have always meant.

Chinese Source

故說一切法，　非空非不空，

有無及有故，　是則契中道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus it is said: all dharmas

are neither empty nor not empty —

because of existence, non-existence, and again existence —

and this accords with the Middle Way.

論曰：一切法者，謂諸有為及無為法。虛妄分別名有為，二取空性名無為。依前理故，說此一切法非空非不空，由有空性虛妄分別故說非空，由無所取能取性故說非不空。有故者，謂有空性虛妄分別故。無故者，謂無所取能取二性故。及有故者，謂虛妄分別中有空性故，及空性中有虛妄分別故。是則契中道者，謂一切法非一向空亦非一向不空，如是理趣妙契中道，亦善符順般若等經說一切法非空非有。

What does this mean? "All dharmas" means all conditioned and unconditioned dharmas — false discrimination being the conditioned, and the emptiness of the two graspings being the unconditioned. By the reasoning just given, it is said that all these dharmas are neither empty nor not empty: because emptiness and false discrimination both exist, they are said to be not empty; because the natures of the grasped and the grasper do not exist, they are said to be not not-empty. "Existence" means: emptiness and false discrimination exist. "Non-existence" means: the two natures of the grasped and the grasper do not exist. "And again existence" means: within false discrimination there is emptiness, and within emptiness there is false discrimination. "This accords with the Middle Way" means: all dharmas are neither wholly empty nor wholly not empty — this principle marvelously accords with the Middle Way, and agrees well with the Prajñāpāramitā and other scriptures' teaching that all dharmas are neither empty nor existent.

4. The Own-Characteristic of False Discrimination

Having shown that false discrimination exists, the treatise now identifies what it actually is: consciousness itself, arising and transforming so that it appears as four things — an object, a sentient being, a self, and cognition — none of which, as they appear, are real.

Chinese Source

識生變似義、　有情、我及了，

此境實非有，　境無故識無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Consciousness arises and transforms, appearing as if it were an object,

a sentient being, a self, and cognition;

this apparent object is not, in truth, real;

and since the object does not exist, [such] consciousness does not exist.

論曰：變似義者，謂似色等諸境性現。變似有情者，謂似自他身五根性現。變似我者，謂染末那與我癡等恒相應故。變似了者，謂餘六識，了相麁故。此境實非有者，謂似義似根無行相故，似我似了非真現故，皆非實有。境無故識無者，謂所取義等四境無故，能取諸識亦非實有。

What does this mean? "Transforms and appears as if it were an object" means it appears in the nature of form and the other sense-objects. "Transforms and appears as if it were a sentient being" means it appears in the nature of the five sense-faculties of one's own body and others' bodies. "Transforms and appears as if it were a self" means the defiled manas, constantly conjoined with self-delusion and the rest. "Transforms and appears as if it were cognition" means the remaining six consciousnesses, whose aspect of cognizing is coarse. "This apparent object is not, in truth, real" means: the apparent object and the apparent faculties have no [genuinely cognizing] aspect at all, and the apparent self and apparent cognition do not truly appear as what they seem — so none of these four are, in truth, real. "Since the object does not exist, [such] consciousness does not exist" means: since the four objects grasped — the object, and the rest — do not exist, the consciousnesses that grasp them, [taken as truly external-object-grasping realities,] likewise are not truly real.

Reading note — after Kuījī

Kuījī is careful about which consciousness does which transforming. The eighth consciousness, the storehouse consciousness, transforms into the apparent "object" (the physical world and the bodies' sense-faculties) and the apparent "sentient being" — it is the subtlest and does not itself claim a self. The seventh consciousness, the defiled manas, transforms into the apparent "self," since it alone is constantly, moment to moment, conjoined with self-view and self-delusion. The remaining six consciousnesses transform into "cognition" in the ordinary sense — the coarse, episodic awareness of sights, sounds, and thoughts. He also flags a translation trap in the earlier Chinese versions of this treatise, which read the object of the eighth consciousness's transformation as including a self and consciousness directly — a reading Xuánzàng's version corrects, since the storehouse consciousness's own scope, elsewhere in the Yogācāra corpus, is defined as the body's sense-faculties, seeds, and the physical world, never a self.

Chinese Source

虛妄分別性，　由此義得成，

非實有全無，　許滅解脫故。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this reasoning, the nature of false discrimination

is established: it is neither truly real

nor utterly non-existent —

because its cessation is accepted as liberation.

論曰：虛妄分別由此義故成非實有，如所現起非真有故；亦非全無，於中少有亂識生故。如何不許此性全無？以許此滅得解脫故。若異此者，繫縛解脫則應皆無，如是便成撥無雜染及清淨失。

What does this mean? By this reasoning, false discrimination is established as not truly real — since what arises and appears within it does not truly exist as it appears. Yet it is also not utterly non-existent — since within it, a confused consciousness does, to some small extent, actually arise. Why is it not accepted that this nature is utterly non-existent? Because its cessation is accepted as liberation. Were it otherwise, bondage and liberation alike would have to be non-existent — and this would amount to the fault of denying both defilement and purity altogether.

5. The Three Natures Subsumed in False Discrimination

False discrimination alone, the treatise now shows, is enough to account for all three natures at once — each defined by a different relation to this single constructing activity of mind.

Chinese Source

唯所執、依他，　及圓成實性，

境故分別故，　及二空故說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Only the discriminated, attached-to nature, the dependent nature,

and the perfectly accomplished nature —

spoken of through its object, through discrimination itself,

and through the twofold emptiness.

論曰：依止虛妄分別境故，說有遍計所執自性。依止虛妄分別性故，說有依他起自性。依止所取能取空故，說有圓成實自性。

What does this mean? In dependence on false discrimination's object, the discriminated, attached-to nature is spoken of. In dependence on false discrimination's own nature, the dependent nature is spoken of. In dependence on the emptiness of the grasped and the grasper, the perfectly accomplished nature is spoken of.

6. The Gateway to Signlessness

Two further verses turn from what false discrimination is to how a practitioner actually enters, step by step, the direct realization that neither the grasped object nor the grasping consciousness truly exists.

Chinese Source

依識有所得，　境無所得生；

依境無所得，　識無所得生。

Ru-Yi Translation

In dependence on the apprehension that is in consciousness,

the non-apprehension of the object arises;

in dependence on the non-apprehension of the object,

the non-apprehension of consciousness [itself] arises.

論曰：依止唯識有所得故，先有於境無所得生；復依於境無所得故，後有於識無所得生。由是方便，得入所取能取無相。

What does this mean? In dependence on the apprehension there is in Consciousness-Only, there first arises the non-apprehension of the object; and in further dependence on this non-apprehension of the object, there afterward arises the non-apprehension of consciousness itself. By this skillful means, one is able to enter the signlessness of the grasped and the grasper.

Chinese Source

由識有得性，　亦成無所得，

故知二有得，　無得性平等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since consciousness's own nature of apprehending

likewise comes to be non-apprehended,

one thus knows: the two, [once thought] apprehended,

are equal in the nature of non-apprehension.

論曰：唯識生時現似種種虛妄境故，名有所得。以所得境無實性故，能得實性亦不得成。由能得識無所得故，所取能取二有所得，平等俱成無所得性。

What does this mean? When Consciousness-Only arises, it appears as various false objects — this is called "having apprehension." Since the apprehended object has no real nature, the real nature of the apprehending [consciousness] likewise cannot be established. And since the apprehending consciousness thereby also has no apprehension, the grasped and the grasper — both formerly "having apprehension" — equally come to share the same nature of non-apprehension.

Reading note — after Kuījī

Kuījī places these two verses at two distinct points on the path. The first describes the level of practice, roughly the stage of "heat" and "summit" in the path of preparation, where a practitioner has come to accept that consciousness alone exists while the external object does not — but has not yet directly realized that consciousness's own claim to "grasp" anything real is equally empty. The second describes the path of seeing itself, where that final equality is directly known: grasped and grasper, having both been objects of "apprehension," are now understood as equally without any apprehensible nature at all — the two-fold emptiness of the sixth chapter's very first verse, arrived at here by a different route.

7. Three More Aspects: Differentiated, Synonymous, Arising

Three brief verses round out false discrimination's description: how it differs across the three realms, the two names — "mind" and "mental factors" — that pick out the same reality from different angles, and how the eight consciousnesses actually arise, one conditioning the next.

Chinese Source

三界心心所，　是虛妄分別，

唯了境名心，　亦別名心所。

Ru-Yi Translation

The minds and mental factors of the three realms —

this is false discrimination.

That which cognizes an object as a whole is called "mind";

that which also cognizes it distinctly is called "mental factors."

論曰：虛妄分別差別相者，即是欲界色無色界諸心心所。異門相者，唯能了境總相名心，亦了差別名為受等諸心所法。

What does this mean? False discrimination's differentiated aspect is simply the minds and mental factors of the desire realm, the form realm, and the formless realm. Its synonym aspect: that which cognizes an object's general character alone is called "mind"; that which also cognizes its particulars is called the mental factors — feeling and the rest.

Chinese Source

一則名緣識，　第二名受者，

此中能受用、　分別推心所。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first is called the conditioning consciousness;

the second is called the experiencer.

Among these, feeling is what experiences,

perception discriminates, and volition and the rest impel.

論曰：緣識者，謂藏識，是餘識生緣故。藏識為緣所生轉識，受用主故名為受者。此諸識中受能受用、想能分別、思作意等諸相應行能推諸識，此三助心故名心所。

What does this mean? "The conditioning consciousness" means the storehouse consciousness, since it is the condition for the arising of the other consciousnesses. The evolving consciousnesses, arisen in dependence on the storehouse consciousness as their condition, are called "the experiencer," since they are the master of experiencing. Among these consciousnesses, feeling experiences, perception discriminates, and volition, attention, and the other conjoined formations impel the consciousnesses; these three assist the mind, and so are called mental factors.

8. The Defiled Aspect: The Twelve Links of Dependent Origination

The chapter's ninth and final aspect of false discrimination is the most sweeping: the entire twelve-linked chain of dependent origination, reread as eleven functions performed by a single defiling process, and organized into three, two, and seven kinds of defilement — none of it, the closing line insists, ever arising apart from false discrimination itself.

Chinese Source

覆障及安立、　將導、攝、圓滿、

三分別、受用、　引起并連縛、

現前、苦果故，　唯此惱世間，

三、二、七雜染，　由虛妄分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

By concealing, and by establishing;

by leading onward, by encompassing, by perfecting;

by threefold discrimination, by experiencing;

by giving rise and by binding together;

by becoming present; and by being the fruit of suffering —

by these alone is the world afflicted.

The threefold, twofold, and sevenfold defilements

all arise from false discrimination.

論曰：覆障故者，謂由無明覆如實理、障真見故。安立故者，謂由諸行植本識中，業熏習故。將導故者，謂有取識引諸有情至生處故。攝故者，謂名色攝有情自體故。圓滿故者，謂六內處令諸有情體具足故。三分別故者，謂觸能分別根境識三，順三受故。受用故者，謂由受支領納順違，非二境故。引起故者，謂由愛力令先業所引後有得起故。連縛故者，謂取令識緣順欲等連縛生故。現前故者，謂由有力令已作業所與後有諸異熟果得現前故。苦果故者，謂生老死性有逼迫，酬前因故。唯此所說十二有支，逼惱世間令不安隱。

What does this mean? "By concealing" means: ignorance conceals the truth of reality and obstructs the seeing of it as it truly is. "By establishing" means: the formations plant, within the root consciousness, the habituation of karma. "By leading onward" means: consciousness bound up with grasping leads sentient beings on to the place of their next birth. "By encompassing" means: name-and-form encompasses a sentient being's own body. "By perfecting" means: the six inner sense-fields bring a sentient being's body to its full completeness. "By threefold discrimination" means: contact discriminates the faculty, the object, and the consciousness — the three — and accords with the three kinds of feeling. "By experiencing" means: through the link of feeling, one takes in what is agreeable or disagreeable. "By giving rise" means: through the power of craving, the later existence already drawn by prior karma is caused to arise. "By binding together" means: grasping causes consciousness, conditioned by desire and its like as agreeable, to arise bound together [with what is to come]. "By becoming present" means: through the power of becoming, the karmically ripened fruits already bestowed by past deeds are made to become present. "By being the fruit of suffering" means: birth, old age, and death have, by their nature, an oppressive character — the recompense of prior causes. It is these twelve links alone, as described, that afflict and oppress the world, allowing it no peace.

論曰：三雜染者，一煩惱雜染，謂無明愛取；二業雜染，謂行有；三生雜染，謂餘支。二雜染者，一因雜染、謂煩惱業；二果雜染、謂所餘支。七雜染者，謂七種因：一顛倒因，謂無明；二牽引因，謂行；三將導因，謂識；四攝受因，謂名色、六處；五受用因，謂觸、受；六引起因，謂愛、取、有；七厭怖因，謂生、老死。此諸雜染無不皆由虛妄分別而得生長。

The threefold defilement: first, the defilement of affliction — ignorance, craving, and grasping; second, the defilement of karma — formations and becoming; third, the defilement of birth — the remaining links. The twofold defilement: first, the defilement of cause — affliction and karma; second, the defilement of effect — the remaining links. The sevenfold defilement means seven kinds of cause: first, the cause of inversion — ignorance; second, the cause of impelling — formations; third, the cause of leading onward — consciousness; fourth, the cause of encompassing — name-and-form and the six sense-fields; fifth, the cause of experiencing — contact and feeling; sixth, the cause of giving rise — craving, grasping, and becoming; seventh, the cause of dread — birth, old age, and death. None of these defilements ever arise and grow apart from false discrimination.

Reading note — after Kuījī

Kuījī notes that the treatise's elevenfold scheme — eleven functions for twelve links — comes from folding birth and old-age-and-death together under a single label, "the fruit of suffering," since both are simply the ripening of prior karma made manifest. He also traces the threefold and sevenfold divisions to a shared underlying logic with the Yogācārabhūmi and the Abhidharmasamuccaya, already on this site: the same twelve links can be sorted by which stage of the cycle they belong to (affliction, karma, or birth), by simple cause and effect, or by seven distinct functional roles — different lenses on one chain, not competing accounts of it. What all of them insist on, against any rival explanation, is the closing line: it is false discrimination, not a real external world, that keeps the entire chain of birth and suffering turning.

9. The Character of Emptiness

With false discrimination's nine aspects fully laid out, the treatise turns from what is empty to emptiness itself — beginning with its defining character: neither existent nor non-existent, and neither identical to nor different from the false discrimination it is the true nature of.

Chinese Source

無二有無故，　非有亦非無，

非異亦非一，　是說為空相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because the two are absent, and this absence exists,

it is neither existent nor non-existent;

because it is neither different nor identical [with false discrimination] —

this is said to be the character of emptiness.

論曰：無二，謂無所取能取。有無，謂有二取之無。此即顯空無性為性，故此空相非有非無。云何非有？無二有故。云何非無？有二無故。此顯空相非有非無。此空與彼虛妄分別非異非一，若異應成法性異法，便違正理，如苦等性；若一則應非淨智境亦非共相。此即顯空與妄分別離一異相。

What does this mean? "The two" [being absent] means the grasped and the grasper are absent. "This absence exists" means the non-existence of the two graspings exists. This shows that emptiness takes non-existence itself as its own nature — so the character of emptiness is neither existent nor non-existent. Why is it not existent? Because the two, in existing, are absent. Why is it not non-existent? Because the non-existence of the two exists. This shows the character of emptiness is neither existent nor non-existent. This emptiness and that false discrimination are neither different nor identical. Were they different, a dharma's nature would have to differ from the dharma itself — contradicting correct reasoning, as with the nature of suffering. Were they identical, emptiness could not be the object of pure wisdom, nor could it be a universal character. This shows that emptiness and false discrimination are free of both the character of identity and the character of difference.

10. The Synonyms of Emptiness

Emptiness carries several other names throughout the sūtras — each one, Vasubandhu shows, picking out the very same reality under a different aspect of its meaning.

Chinese Source

略說空異門，　謂真如、實際、

無相、勝義性、　法界等應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

In brief, the synonyms of emptiness

are: suchness, the reality-limit,

signlessness, the ultimate nature,

and the dharma-realm, and the rest — these should be known.

Chinese Source

由無變無倒，　相滅聖智境，

及諸聖法因，　異門義如次。

Ru-Yi Translation

By virtue of being without change, without inversion,

the cessation of characteristics, the object of noble wisdom,

and the cause of all noble dharmas —

these are the meanings of the synonyms, in order.

論曰：即此中說所知空性，由無變義說為真如。真性常如，無轉易故。由無倒義說為實際，非諸顛倒，依緣事故。由相滅義說為無相，此中永絕一切相故。由聖智境義說為勝義性，是最勝智所行義故。由聖法因義說為法界，以一切聖法緣此生故。此中界者，即是因義。

What does this mean? This object of knowledge, emptiness: by virtue of the meaning of being without change, it is called suchness (tathatā) — its true nature is constantly thus, without alteration. By virtue of the meaning of being without inversion, it is called the reality-limit — it is not the basis on which any of the inversions depend. By virtue of the meaning of the cessation of characteristics, it is called signlessness — within it, every characteristic is forever cut off. By virtue of the meaning of the object of noble wisdom, it is called the ultimate nature — it is the object practiced by the most excellent wisdom. By virtue of the meaning of the cause of all noble dharmas, it is called the dharma-realm — since every noble dharma arises in dependence on it. Here, "realm" (dhātu) carries the meaning of "cause."

11. The Distinctions of Emptiness: Defiled, Pure, and Sixteen Kinds

Emptiness itself never changes, but its relationship to a given mind-stream does: defiled while taint remains, pure once the taint is gone — like water, gold, or space, each called "pure" once cleared of what was never truly part of it. From here the chapter sets out the sixteen emptinesses by name, each one defined by exactly what is being contemplated as empty, and why.

Chinese Source

此雜染、清淨，　由有垢、無垢，

如水界金空，　淨故許為淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

This, as defiled and as pure,

is so through having taint and being without taint —

like water, gold, and space,

each accepted as pure once purified.

論曰：空性差別略有二種，一雜染、二清淨。此成染淨由分位別，謂有垢位說為雜染，出離垢時說為清淨。雖先雜染、後成清淨，而非轉變成無常失。如水界等出離客塵，空淨亦然，非性轉變。

What does this mean? The distinctions of emptiness are, in brief, of two kinds: defiled and pure. It becomes defiled or pure according to the difference in its stage — in the stage where taint is present, it is called defiled; at the time it is freed from taint, it is called pure. Although it is first defiled and afterward becomes pure, this does not amount to the fault of impermanent transformation. Just as water and the rest, once freed of adventitious dust, are called pure without their own nature having changed, emptiness's purity is likewise so: it is not that its nature itself transforms.

Chinese Source

能食及所食，　此依身所住，

能見此如理，　所求二淨空。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eater, and the eaten;

that on which [both] depend, and what one dwells within;

the seeing of this, what accords with reality,

and the two purities sought — [each] empty.

Chinese Source

為常益有情、　為不捨生死、

為善無窮盡，　故觀此為空。

Ru-Yi Translation

For the sake of constantly benefiting sentient beings,

for the sake of not abandoning saṃsāra,

for the sake of good roots never being exhausted —

for these reasons one contemplates this as empty.

Chinese Source

為種性清淨、　為得諸相好、

為淨諸佛法，　故菩薩觀空。

Ru-Yi Translation

For the sake of the purity of one's own spiritual lineage,

for the sake of attaining the marks and excellent signs,

for the sake of purifying all the buddha-dharmas —

for these reasons the bodhisattva contemplates emptiness.

論曰：能食空者，依內處說，即是內空。所食空者，依外處說，即是外空。此依身者，謂能所食所依止身，此身空故，名內外空。諸器世間說為所住，此相寬廣故名為大，所住空故名為大空。能見此者，謂智能見內處等空。空智空故，說名空空。如理者，謂勝義，即如實行。所觀真理，此即空故，名勝義空。菩薩修行為得二淨，即諸有為、無為善法。此二空故，名有為空及無為空。為於有情常作饒益，而觀空故，名畢竟空。生死長遠無初後際，觀此空故，名無際空。不觀為空便速厭捨，為不厭捨此生死故，觀此無際生死為空。為所修善至無餘依般涅槃位，亦無散捨而觀空故，名無散空。

What does this mean? The emptiness of the eater: spoken of with respect to the inner sense-fields — this is inner emptiness. The emptiness of the eaten: spoken of with respect to the outer sense-fields — this is outer emptiness. "That on which they depend": the body on which the eater and the eaten both depend; since this body is empty, it is called inner-and-outer emptiness. The various vessel-worlds are called "what one dwells within"; since this character is vast, it is called "great," and since what one dwells within is empty, it is called great emptiness. "The seeing of this" is the wisdom that sees the emptiness of the inner sense-fields and the rest; since this wisdom is itself empty, it is called the emptiness of emptiness. "What accords with reality" means the ultimate — practice as it truly is; the true principle contemplated is itself empty, hence the emptiness of the ultimate. The bodhisattva's practice aims at the two purities — the conditioned and unconditioned wholesome dharmas; since these two are empty, this is the emptiness of the conditioned and the emptiness of the unconditioned. For constantly benefiting sentient beings, one contemplates emptiness — hence ultimate emptiness. Saṃsāra is long and vast, without beginning or end; contemplating this as empty is the emptiness of no boundary. Without contemplating it as empty, one would swiftly grow weary and abandon sentient beings; so as not to abandon this saṃsāra, one contemplates this boundless saṃsāra as empty. For the good one cultivates, all the way to the state of nirvāṇa without remainder, one contemplates it as empty without scattering or abandoning it — hence the emptiness of no scattering.

論曰：諸聖種姓自體本有，非習所成，說名本性。菩薩為此速得清淨而觀空故，名本性空。菩薩為得大士相好而觀空故，名為相空。菩薩為令力無畏等一切佛法皆得清淨而觀此空，故名一切法空。是十四空，隨別安立。

The self-nature of the noble spiritual lineage is originally possessed, not brought about by habituation — this is called "original nature." The bodhisattva, in order to swiftly attain purity in this regard, contemplates it as empty — hence the emptiness of original nature. The bodhisattva, in order to attain the great being's marks and excellent signs, contemplates this as empty — hence the emptiness of the marks. The bodhisattva, in order that the [ten] powers, [four] fearlessnesses, and all the buddha-dharmas may attain complete purity, contemplates this as empty — hence the emptiness of all dharmas. These fourteen emptinesses are each severally established.

Chinese Source

補特伽羅法，　實性俱非有，

此無性、有性，　故別立二空。

Ru-Yi Translation

The person and dharmas alike

have no truly existent real nature;

this very non-existence of nature itself has an existent nature —

hence two further emptinesses are separately established.

論曰：補特伽羅及法實性俱非有故，名無性空。此無性空非無自性，空以無性為自性故，名無性自性空。於前所說能食空等，為顯空相別立二空；此為遮止補特伽羅法增益執、空損減執，如其次第立後二空。

Since neither the person nor dharmas possess a real, [ultimately] existent nature, this is called the emptiness of no nature. Yet this emptiness of no nature is not itself without any nature of its own — since this very emptiness takes no-nature as its own nature, it is called the emptiness of the intrinsic nature of no-nature. As for the fourteen emptinesses spoken of above, beginning with the emptiness of the eater — these are separately established in order to display the character of emptiness. These last two are established, in their proper order, in order to check the exaggerating misapprehension that clings to a real person or real dharmas, and the deprecating misapprehension that denies emptiness itself.

Reading note — after Kuījī

Kuījī reads the sixteen emptinesses as answering two different questions at once. The first fourteen each name what is being emptied and why a bodhisattva bothers to contemplate it — the eater and eaten (the six inner and six outer sense-fields), the body they both depend on, the vessel-world one dwells within, the very wisdom that sees all this as empty, the ultimate truth itself, the conditioned and unconditioned paths to purity, saṃsāra not abandoned out of compassion, the good roots never let go of, one's own spiritual lineage, the buddha's physical marks, and the buddha-dharmas themselves. The final two, no-nature emptiness and no-nature's-own-nature emptiness, work differently: they exist to block two opposite errors that could arise from all this list-making — mistaking "person" and "dharmas" for something real after all (checked by the first), and mistaking emptiness's own utter negation for total non-existence, so that even emptiness is denied (checked by the second). He is careful to note this list is not exhaustive — the Prajñāpāramitā sūtras count eighteen, twenty, or more — and that this treatise's own sixteen is one legitimate way of organizing the same underlying territory, not a rival total.

12. Establishing Emptiness: The Mind Is Originally Pure

The chapter's closing move proves that emptiness must be exactly what has just been described — neither wholly defiled nor wholly pure by its own nature — by showing what would follow if it were otherwise, arriving at the treatise's single most quoted line: the mind's own nature is pure from the start, and defilement is never more than adventitious dust.

Chinese Source

此若無雜染，　一切應自脫；

此若無清淨，　功用應無果。

Ru-Yi Translation

If this had no defilement,

all beings would be, of themselves, already liberated;

if this had no purity,

then [spiritual] effort would have to be fruitless.

論曰：若諸法空，未生對治無客雜染者，一切有情不由功用應自然解脫。若對治已生亦不清淨，則應求解脫勤勞無果。

What does this mean? If the dharmas, being empty, were before any remedy has arisen already free of adventitious defilement, then all sentient beings would, without any effort at all, already be spontaneously liberated. And if, even once the remedy had arisen, they still did not become pure, then the effort of seeking liberation would have to be fruitless.

Chinese Source

非染非不染，　非淨非不淨，

心性本淨故，　由客塵所染。

Ru-Yi Translation

Neither defiled nor undefiled,

neither pure nor impure —

because the mind's nature is originally pure,

[yet] it is tainted by adventitious dust.

論曰：云何非染非不染？以心性本淨故。云何非淨非不淨？由客塵所染故。是名成立空差別義。

Why is it neither defiled nor undefiled? Because the mind's nature is originally pure. Why is it neither pure nor impure? Because it is tainted by adventitious dust. This is what is meant by establishing the distinction of emptiness.

論曰：此前空義總有二種，謂相安立。相復有二，謂無及有。空性有相，離有離無、離異離一以為其相。應知安立即異門等。

The foregoing meaning of emptiness comprises, in total, two aspects: character and establishment. Character itself has two aspects: non-existence and existence. Emptiness's character — free of existence, free of non-existence, free of identity, and free of difference — constitutes its character. "Establishment" should be understood as comprising the synonyms and the rest already explained.

Reading note — after Kuījī

Kuījī reads this closing verse as a piece of careful logic disguised as a simple slogan. If emptiness — the mind's true nature — already excluded all defilement from the start, spiritual practice would be redundant: everyone would already be a buddha with no effort required. If it excluded all purity from the start, spiritual practice would be pointless: no amount of effort could ever produce a result it structurally cannot have. Only one position survives both objections: the mind's nature is originally pure, and what defiles it — the discriminated, attached-to nature clinging to a grasped and a grasper — was never intrinsic to it, only ever adventitious dust settled on top. This is the same insight later Chinese Buddhist tradition would call tathāgatagarbha-adjacent, but Kuījī keeps it firmly inside the three-natures framework: what is "originally pure" is the perfectly accomplished nature, never separate from false discrimination itself, and what is "adventitious" is nothing but the discriminated, attached-to nature that a mistaken mind projects on top of it.
