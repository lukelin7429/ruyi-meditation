The Five Evils

五惡五痛五燒（下）

Five evils, unpacked one at a time, each ending in the same refrain — and a single vow open to anyone, in the midst of any of them: to hold the mind to one point, and alone do good.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T360 · Saṃghavarman's translation, 3rd century CE Commentary guide: Jingying Huiyuan's 無量壽經義疏 (6th c.)
This part covers
The five evils named one at a time — violence, deceit and greed, lust, wrong speech, and idleness — each ending in the same refrain comparing the suffering to a great fire, and each answered by the same possibility: one person, in the midst of it, choosing otherwise. Closes with the Buddha's charge to Maitreya and the claim that a single day's sincere practice here outweighs a hundred years of ease in Sukhāvatī.

39. The First Evil

Chinese Source

其一惡者。諸天人民蠕動之類。欲為眾惡，莫不皆然。強者伏弱，轉相剋賊。殘害殺戮，迭相吞噬。不知修善，惡逆無道。後受殃罰，自然趣向。神明記識，犯者不赦。故有貧窮、下賤、乞丐、孤獨、聾盲、瘖啞、愚癡、憋惡。至有尫狂、不逮之屬。又有尊貴、豪富、髙才、明達。皆由宿世慈孝，修善積德所致。世有常道王法牢獄。不肯畏慎。為惡入罪受其殃罰。求望解脫難得免出。世間有此目前現事。壽終後世，尤深尤劇。入其幽冥，轉生受身。譬如王法，痛苦極刑。故有自然三塗無量苦惱。轉貿其身。改形易道。所受壽命，或長或短。魂神精識，自然趣之。當獨值向，相從共生，更相報復，無有止已。殃惡未盡，不得相離。展轉其中，無有出期。難得解脫，痛不可言。天地之間，自然有是。雖不即時，卒暴應至，善惡之道，會當歸之。是為一大惡，一痛，一燒。勤苦如是。譬如大火焚燒人身。人能於中一心制意，端身正行，獨作諸善，不為眾惡者，身獨度脫，獲其福德，度世上天泥洹之道。是為一大善也。

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for the first evil: among all the gods, humans, and every crawling, moving creature, there is none who does not wish to do evil of some kind. The strong overpower the weak, each preying upon the other in turn; they injure, kill, and slaughter one another, devouring each other by turns. Not knowing how to cultivate goodness, they do evil, going against all order and reason, and afterward receive calamity and punishment, drawn naturally toward it. The spirits record and remember it, and no offender is pardoned. And so there is poverty, low and humble station, begging, solitude, deafness, blindness, muteness, stupidity, and wickedness; there are even the crippled, the mad, and those otherwise incapable. And there is also honor, wealth, lofty talent, and understanding — all of this, too, comes from kindness and filial devotion, cultivating goodness and accumulating virtue, in a past life. There exists, in this world, the constant way of royal law and the prison — and yet people still refuse to fear or take care, do evil, fall into crime, and receive their calamity and punishment; and seeking release from it, they find it hard to obtain or escape. This is a plain matter, visible before one's own eyes in this present world. But when life ends and one passes into the next, the consequence is deeper and more severe still, entering into a dim and hidden realm, and taking on a new body there. It is like the law of a king, whose penalties reach the harshest punishment; and so there naturally exist the three lower paths, and their immeasurable suffering, in which one's very body is exchanged, one's form and destiny changed, the lifespan one then receives long or short according to it, and one's soul and consciousness carried, of itself, toward it. Each must go there alone and face it alone, yet those bound together are born together again, taking their revenge on one another in turn without end; while the calamity of their wrongdoing is not yet exhausted, they cannot be released from one another, turning within it again and again, with no time set for their release — hard indeed to gain freedom from it, a suffering beyond what words can say. Between heaven and earth, this exists of itself; though it may not come at once, it is bound, sooner or later, to arrive without warning, and the path of good and evil must, in the end, come home to roost. This is the first great evil, the first pain, the first burning, and the hardship is just as I have described — like a great fire burning a person's body. If a person can, in the midst of this, hold their mind to a single point, keep their body upright and their conduct correct, and alone do good, not joining in the many evils, then they alone are delivered, and obtain their own blessing and merit, the path beyond this world, up to the heavens, and to nirvāṇa. This is the first great good."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

The five evils that follow correspond, loosely but recognizably, to the five lay precepts against killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and intoxicants — this first evil concerns violence and killing, and the pattern becomes explicit by the fourth evil, which names the four traditional categories of wrong speech outright, and the fifth, which names drunkenness by name. Huiyuan reads the five evils as related in sequence rather than as five unconnected vices — his own summary phrase for this whole unit is that the five evils "generate one another in turn" (五惡展轉相生): violence breeds the conditions for deceit and theft, which breed the conditions for lust and family breakdown, and so on, each evil compounding the ground the next grows in.

Every one of the five sections that follow ends with the identical refrain: a comparison to a great fire consuming the body, and then the same offer — that a single person, in the midst of any of this, can hold their mind to one point, act rightly, and alone be delivered, regardless of what everyone around them is doing. Huiyuan reads this repeated final clause as the section's real center of gravity, easy to lose sight of amid the length and severity of everything that precedes it: the passage is not fatalistic. Its whole argument is that none of this is inevitable for any single person, however inevitable it may look at the scale of the world as a whole.

40. The Second Evil

Chinese Source

其二惡者。世間人民，父子、兄弟、室家、夫婦。都無義理，不順法度。奢婬憍縱，各欲快意。任心自恣，更相欺惑。心口各異，言念無實。佞諂不忠。巧言諛媚。嫉賢謗善。陷入怨枉。主上不明，任用臣下。臣下自在，機偽多端。踐度能行，知其形勢。在位不正，為其所欺。妄損忠良，不當天心。臣欺其君。子欺其父。兄弟、夫婦。中外知識。更相欺誑。各懷貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡。欲自厚己。欲貪多有。尊卑上下，心俱同然。破家亡身，不顧前後。親屬內外，坐之滅族。或時室家知識、鄉黨市里、愚民野人，轉共從事。更相剝害。忿成怨結。富有慳惜，不肯施與。愛保貪重，心勞身苦。如是至竟，無所恃怙。獨來獨去，無一隨者。善惡禍福，追命所生。或在樂處。或入苦毒。然後乃悔，當復何及。世間人民。心愚少智。見善憎謗，不思慕及。但欲為惡，妄作非法。常懷盜心，悕望他利。消散磨盡，而復求索。邪心不正，懼人有色。不豫思計，事至乃悔。今世現有王法牢獄。隨罪趣向受其殃罰。因其前世不信道德，不修善本。今復為惡天神剋識別其名籍。壽終神逝下入惡道。故有自然三塗無量苦惱。展轉其中。世世累劫，無有出期。難得解脫，痛不可言。是為二大惡，二痛，二燒。勤苦如是。譬如大火焚燒人身。人能於中一心制意，端身正行，獨作諸善，不為眾惡者，身獨度脫，獲其福德，度世上天泥洹之道。是為二大善也。

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for the second evil: among the people of this world — parents and children, brothers, members of one household, husbands and wives — there is no sense of right conduct at all, and no compliance with any proper measure. Extravagant, lascivious, arrogant, and unrestrained, each wants only their own pleasure, following their own mind self-indulgently, deceiving one another in turn. Their heart and their mouth say different things, and their words carry no truth; they are flattering and disloyal, glib and ingratiating, envious of the worthy and slanderous of the good, entrapping others in false accusation. A ruler who is not perceptive entrusts his ministers, and those ministers, left to their own devices, scheme in every possible way; they take the measure of what they can get away with, judge the true situation, and, when their lord holds his position without integrity, deceive him at will, wrongfully harming the loyal and the good, out of keeping with the true heart of heaven. Ministers deceive their lord; children deceive their parents; brothers, husbands and wives, relatives and associates near and far, all deceive one another in turn, each harboring greed, anger, and delusion, wanting only to enrich themselves, wanting only to possess more — honored and lowly, high and low, their hearts all just the same in this. They destroy their households and lose their own lives over it, giving no thought to what comes before or after, and their relatives, both near and distant, are brought down with them, their whole clan destroyed because of it. And sometimes members of a household, their associates, the people of a village or a marketplace, foolish commoners and rustic folk, all engage together in this same way, exploiting and harming one another in turn, until resentment knots itself into lasting enmity. Those who are rich are stingy and begrudging, unwilling to give anything away, clinging to what they love and hoarding what they prize, their mind wearied and their body worn out by it. And when it all comes, in the end, to nothing, there is nothing left to rely upon; a person comes alone and goes alone, with not a single thing to accompany them, while good and evil, calamity and blessing, pursue the life that gave rise to them, leading now to a place of joy, now to one of bitter suffering — and only then does regret come, and by then, what use is it? The people of this world, foolish at heart and lacking in wisdom, see goodness and meet it with envy and slander, giving it no thought or admiration, wanting only to do evil, recklessly acting against the law; they constantly harbor a thief's intent, coveting what belongs to another, and when what they have is spent and exhausted, they go seeking more again, their crooked mind never set right, fearful whenever another shows displeasure, never planning ahead, and only regretting it once the matter is already upon them. In this present world there exists royal law and the prison, and, according to the crime, its calamity and punishment are received; and this, too, follows from a person's failure, in a former life, to trust in virtue and the path, and their failure to cultivate roots of goodness. Now, doing evil once more, the spirits of heaven mark and record their name in the register, and when their life ends, their spirit departs and descends into an evil destiny. And so there naturally exist the three lower paths and their immeasurable suffering, turning within them, lifetime after lifetime, for aeons on end, with no time set for release — hard indeed to gain freedom from it, a suffering beyond what words can say. This is the second great evil, the second pain, the second burning, and the hardship is just as I have described — like a great fire burning a person's body. If a person can, in the midst of this, hold their mind to a single point, keep their body upright and their conduct correct, and alone do good, not joining in the many evils, then they alone are delivered, and obtain their own blessing and merit, the path beyond this world, up to the heavens, and to nirvāṇa. This is the second great good."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

This section widens from the individual thief or deceiver to an entire chain of mutual betrayal running from ministers deceiving their ruler down to ordinary neighbors exploiting one another — a structure Huiyuan reads as deliberate: greed and deceit are shown operating identically at every scale of society, from the court down to the marketplace, "honored and lowly, high and low, their hearts all just the same in this." No social position, however elevated, is treated as exempt from this particular evil.

41. The Third Evil

Chinese Source

其三惡者。世間人民。相因寄生。共居天地之間。處年壽命無能幾何。上有賢明、長者、尊貴、豪富。下有貧窮、斯賤、尫劣、愚夫。中有不善之人。常懷邪惡。但念婬泆。煩滿胷中。愛欲交亂，坐起不安。貪意守惜，但欲唐得。眄睞細色，邪態外逸。自妻厭憎，私妄出入。費損家財，事為非法。交結聚會，興師相伐。攻劫殺戮，強奪不道。惡心在外，不自修業。盜竊趣得，欲擊成事。恐勢迫脅，歸給妻子。恣心快意，極身作樂。或於親屬，不避尊卑。家室中外，患而苦之。亦復不畏王法禁令。如是之惡著於人鬼。日月照見神明記識。故有自然三塗無量苦惱。展轉其中。世世累劫，無有出期。難得解脫，痛不可言。是為三大惡，三痛，三燒。勤苦如是。譬如大火焚燒人身。人能於中一心制意，端身正行，獨作諸善，不為眾惡者，身獨度脫，獲其福德，度世上天泥洹之道。是為三大善也。

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for the third evil: the people of this world, dependent upon one another for their existence, dwell together between heaven and earth, their years and their lifespan never amounting to very much. Above are the worthy, the eminent, the honored, and the wealthy; below are the poor, the lowly, the weak, and the foolish. And in the midst of them are those not good at all, constantly harboring wicked and evil intent, thinking only of lust and excess, their chest filled with turmoil; craving and desire entangle and confuse them, restless whether sitting or rising, their mind greedy and grasping, wanting only to gain something for nothing. They cast furtive glances at another's beauty, their manner crooked and their conduct dissolute, growing weary and contemptuous of their own spouse, and secretly coming and going elsewhere, squandering the family's wealth, and taking up unlawful affairs. Gathering together in league with others, they raise armies and attack one another, assaulting, plundering, killing, and seizing without any regard for what is right; their evil intent is turned outward, and they do not cultivate their own proper work, stealing and seizing whatever they can get, wanting only to force the matter through. When threatened by fear or overpowering force, they turn and hand it all over to their own wife and children, indulging their heart's every whim, taking their pleasure to its utmost — and sometimes, even among their own relatives, they show no regard for who is senior or junior, so that the members of their own household, near and far, come to suffer and be troubled by them. Nor do they fear the laws and prohibitions of the king. Wrongdoing such as this becomes known to both the living and the spirits; the sun and moon shine upon it and see it, and the spirits mark and record it. And so there naturally exist the three lower paths and their immeasurable suffering, turning within them, lifetime after lifetime, for aeons on end, with no time set for release — hard indeed to gain freedom from it, a suffering beyond what words can say. This is the third great evil, the third pain, the third burning, and the hardship is just as I have described — like a great fire burning a person's body. If a person can, in the midst of this, hold their mind to a single point, keep their body upright and their conduct correct, and alone do good, not joining in the many evils, then they alone are delivered, and obtain their own blessing and merit, the path beyond this world, up to the heavens, and to nirvāṇa. This is the third great good."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Notice the passage's own escalation: it opens with private lust and marital betrayal, and moves, within the same section, to gathering armies and open warfare — "raise armies and attack one another, assaulting, plundering, killing, and seizing." Huiyuan reads this jump as intentional rather than a loss of focus: uncontrolled desire, on this account, does not stay contained to the individual or the household but scales up, given the chance, into organized violence between groups — the same evil the section opened with, working at a larger radius.

42. The Fourth Evil

Chinese Source

其四惡者。世間人民不念修善。轉相教令共為眾惡。兩舌、惡口、妄言、綺語。讒賊鬪亂，憎嫉善人，敗壞賢明，於傍快喜。不孝二親，輕慢師長。朋友無信，難得誠實。尊貴自大，謂己有道。橫行威勢，侵易於人。不能自知，為惡無耻。自以強健，欲人敬難。不畏天地、神明、日月。不肯作善。難可降化。自用偃蹇，謂可常爾。無所憂懼，常懷憍慢。如是眾惡天神記識。賴其前世頗作福德。小善扶接營護助之。今世為惡，福德盡滅。諸善神鬼各去離之。身獨空立，無所復依。壽命終盡，諸惡所歸。自然迫促，共趣奪之。又其名籍，記在神明。殃咎牽引。當往趣向。罪報自然無從捨離。但得前行，入於火鑊。身心摧碎，精神痛苦。當斯之時，悔復何及。天道自然不得蹉跌。故有自然三塗無量苦惱。展轉其中。世世累劫，無有出期。難得解脫，痛不可言。是為四大惡，四痛，四燒。勤苦如是。譬如大火焚燒人身。人能於中一心制意，端身正行，獨作諸善，不為眾惡，身獨度脫，獲其福德，度世上天泥洹之道。是為四大善也。

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for the fourth evil: the people of this world give no thought to cultivating goodness, and instead teach and incite one another to do every kind of evil together — speech that divides, harsh speech, lying speech, and idle speech; slander that harms and stirs up disorder, hatred and envy of good people, undermining and ruining the worthy and the wise while taking secret pleasure in it; disrespect toward their own parents, contempt toward their teachers and elders, no trust between friends, sincerity hard to find; arrogant and self-important, they consider themselves already possessed of the path, moving forward on sheer force and intimidation, encroaching upon and belittling others, unable even to see this in themselves, doing evil without any shame. Believing themselves strong and capable, they want others to hold them in fearful respect; they do not fear heaven and earth, the spirits, or the sun and moon, and refuse to do good, difficult indeed to subdue and transform, stubborn and self-assured, believing things can always remain just as they are, with no worry or fear at all, constantly harboring arrogance and conceit. Wrongdoing such as this the spirits of heaven mark and record. Depending on whatever merit they happened to accumulate in a former life, some small measure of goodness may still support, protect, and assist them for a time — but doing evil in this present life exhausts that merit entirely, and every good spirit and deity then departs and leaves them, until the person stands utterly alone, with nothing left to rely upon. When their life comes to its end, every evil converges upon them, pressing in naturally and swiftly, together seizing hold of them; and their name and record, already noted by the spirits, draw them onward by the calamity of their own guilt, toward the destiny they must enter — a retribution for wrongdoing from which there is naturally no escape. There is only the way forward, into the fiery cauldron, body and mind shattered, spirit in agony — and at that moment, what use is regret? The way of heaven follows its own course and never misses a step. And so there naturally exist the three lower paths and their immeasurable suffering, turning within them, lifetime after lifetime, for aeons on end, with no time set for release — hard indeed to gain freedom from it, a suffering beyond what words can say. This is the fourth great evil, the fourth pain, the fourth burning, and the hardship is just as I have described — like a great fire burning a person's body. If a person can, in the midst of this, hold their mind to a single point, keep their body upright and their conduct correct, and alone do good, not joining in the many evils, then they alone are delivered, and obtain their own blessing and merit, the path beyond this world, up to the heavens, and to nirvāṇa. This is the fourth great good."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

The four kinds of wrong speech named at the opening of this section — divisive speech, harsh speech, lying speech, and idle speech (兩舌、惡口、妄言、綺語) — are a standard doctrinal set found throughout the canon, the specific violations of right speech under the eightfold path. Huiyuan reads what follows this list — arrogance, contempt for teachers, self-assurance that "things can always remain just as they are" — as an extended portrait of exactly the kind of person such speech comes from: not merely careless with words, but someone who has stopped fearing any consequence at all, human or otherwise, and whose fall, when it comes, is described here as arriving with unusual suddenness for exactly that reason.

43. The Fifth Evil

Chinese Source

其五惡者。世間人民。徙倚懈惰，不肯作善治身修業。家室眷屬飢寒困苦。父母教誨。瞋目怒應。言令不和。違戾反逆，譬如怨家。不如無子。取與無節。眾共患厭。負恩違義。無有報償之心。貧窮困乏，不能復得。辜較縱奪，放恣遊散。串數唐得，用自賑給。躭酒嗜美。飲食無度。肆心蕩逸。魯扈抵突。不識人情。強欲抑制。見人有善。妬嫉惡之。無義無禮。無所顧錄。自用職當不可諫曉。六親眷屬所資有無。不能憂念。不惟父母之恩。不存師友之義。心常念惡。口常言惡。身常行惡。曾無一善。不信先聖諸佛經法。不信行道可得度世。不信死後神明更生。不信作善得善，為惡得惡。欲殺真人鬪亂眾僧。欲害父母兄弟眷屬。六親憎惡願令其死。如是世人心意俱然。愚癡曚昧。而自以智慧。不知生所從來死所趣向。不仁不順。逆惡天地。而於其中。悕望僥倖。欲求長生。會當歸死。慈心教誨令其念善。開示生死、善惡之趣，自然有是。而不肯信之。苦心與語，無益其人。心中閉塞，意不開解。大命將終，悔懼交至。不豫修善。臨窮方悔。悔之於後，將何及乎。天地之間，五道分明。恢廓窈冥，浩浩茫茫，善惡報應，禍福相承。身自當之，無誰代者。數之自然，應其所行。殃咎追命，無得縱捨。善人行善，從樂入樂，從明入明。惡人行惡，從苦入苦，從冥入冥。誰能知者？獨佛知耳。教語開示，信用者少。生死不休，惡道不絕。如是世人，難可具盡。故有自然三塗無量苦惱。展轉其中。世世累劫，無有出期。難得解脫，痛不可言。是為五大惡，五痛，五燒。勤苦如是。譬如大火焚燒人身。人能於中一心制意，端身正念，言行相副，所作至誠，所語如語，心口不轉，獨作諸善，不為眾惡，身獨度脫，獲其福德，度世上天泥洹之道。是為五大善也。

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for the fifth evil: the people of this world drift along in idleness, unwilling to do good, to discipline themselves, or to attend to their own proper work, so that their household and family go hungry, cold, and in hardship. When their parents try to instruct and admonish them, they glare back in anger, their words harsh and discordant, contrary and rebellious, as though their own parents were enemies — it would be better had they no children at all. They take and give without any restraint, and everyone around them comes to be troubled and weary of them; they betray kindness and violate what is right, with no thought of ever repaying anyone. Poor and destitute, unable to obtain what they want, they seize and take by force whatever they can, indulging themselves recklessly, gaining things again and again without effort and using them up on themselves. Addicted to wine, craving fine food, they eat and drink without limit, their heart given over entirely to excess and dissipation, coarse and reckless, giving no heed to others' feelings, wanting only to force their own way. Seeing another's goodness, they meet it with envy and resentment; without any sense of right conduct or propriety, they give no thought or consideration to anyone, so set in their own ways that they cannot be reasoned with or advised at all. Toward their six kinds of close relatives, and whatever support they do or do not provide, they feel no concern; they give no thought to their parents' kindness, and hold no regard for the duty owed to a teacher or a friend. Their mind constantly dwells on evil, their mouth constantly speaks evil, their body constantly does evil, without a single good deed among it all. They place no faith in the scriptures and teachings of the sages and buddhas of the past; they do not believe that walking the path can bring deliverance beyond this world; they do not believe that, after death, the spirit is born again; they do not believe that doing good brings good, and doing evil brings evil. They wish to kill a true sage, and stir up disorder among the assembled monks; they wish to harm their own parents, brothers, and relatives, and their six kinds of close relatives, in turn, hate them and wish for their death. People of this world, in mind and intent, are exactly this way — foolish, benighted, and in the dark, and yet they consider themselves wise; they do not know where birth comes from, or where death leads to. Neither humane nor compliant, going against heaven and earth itself, and yet, in the midst of it all, they still hope for some lucky escape, wishing to live long — though in the end, they must return to death regardless. Taught with a compassionate mind, and led to think of goodness, shown clearly the destiny of birth and death and of good and evil, exactly as it naturally is — still they refuse to believe it. Speaking to them with a troubled and earnest heart brings no benefit to such a person; their mind remains closed and blocked, their intent unopened. And when their great span of life is about to end, regret and dread arrive together; not having cultivated goodness beforehand, only as death draws near do they finally come to regret it — but regret, at that point, what use is it? Between heaven and earth, the five destinies are clearly distinct, vast and hidden, boundless and immeasurable; good and evil bring their own response in turn, calamity and blessing following one after the other, and each person must bear it entirely themselves, with no one to stand in for them. This reckoning follows its own natural course, answering exactly to one's own conduct; calamity and fault pursue the life that incurred them, and there is no way to be let off or excused. A good person, doing good, goes from joy to joy, from brightness to brightness; an evil person, doing evil, goes from suffering to suffering, from darkness to darkness. Who can know this fully? Only a buddha knows it completely. Though taught and shown this clearly, few come to believe and follow it; birth and death never cease, and the evil destinies are never severed. People of this world are like this — it is hard to tell it all. And so there naturally exist the three lower paths and their immeasurable suffering, turning within them, lifetime after lifetime, for aeons on end, with no time set for release — hard indeed to gain freedom from it, a suffering beyond what words can say. This is the fifth great evil, the fifth pain, the fifth burning, and the hardship is just as I have described — like a great fire burning a person's body. If a person can, in the midst of this, hold their mind to a single point, their mindfulness correct, their words and conduct matching each other, all that they do utterly sincere, saying exactly what they mean, their mind and their mouth never turning from one another, and alone do good, not joining in the many evils, then they alone are delivered, and obtain their own blessing and merit, the path beyond this world, up to the heavens, and to nirvāṇa. This is the fifth great good."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Naming wine by name — "addicted to wine, craving fine food" — completes the loose correspondence to the five lay precepts noted at the start of this part: killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, wrong speech, and intoxicants. Huiyuan does not read the fifth evil as being narrowly about drinking, however; the passage spends most of its length on idleness, filial disrespect, and outright disbelief in karmic cause and effect, with intoxication named as one symptom among several rather than the section's sole concern. His summary point for the unit as a whole, closing on this fifth evil: everything traced across all five evils — violence, deceit, lust, wrong speech, dissolution — ultimately funnels into the same root failure named here explicitly, disbelief that actions have consequences at all. Once that belief is gone, on this reading, nothing else in the list has anything left to restrain it.

The refrain closing this fifth evil is worded slightly differently from the other four — "their mindfulness correct, their words and conduct matching each other, all that they do utterly sincere... their mind and their mouth never turning from one another" — a small but real textual variation this translation preserves rather than smoothing into uniformity with the other four refrains.

44. The Buddha's Closing Charge to Maitreya

Chinese Source

佛告彌勒：吾語汝等，是世五惡勤苦若此。五痛五燒展轉相生。但作眾惡不修善本。皆悉自然入諸惡趣。或其今世先被殃病，求死不得，求生不得。罪惡所招，示眾見之。身死隨行，入三惡道。苦毒無量，自相燋然。至其久後，共作怨結。從小微起，遂成大惡。皆由貪著財色，不能施慧。癡欲所迫，隨心思想。煩惱結縛，無有解已。厚己諍利，無所省錄。富貴榮華，當時快意。不能忍辱，不務修善。威勢無幾，隨以磨滅。身生勞苦，久後大劇。天道施張，自然糺舉。綱紀羅網，上下相應。焭焭忪忪，當入其中。古今有是，痛哉可傷。佛語彌勒：世間如是，佛皆哀之。以威神力摧滅眾惡。悉令就善棄捐所思。奉持經戒，受行道法，無所違失。終得度世，泥洹之道。佛言。汝今諸天人民及後世人。得佛經語，當熟思之。能於其中端心正行。主上為善，率化其下。轉相勅令，各自端守。尊聖敬善，仁慈博愛。佛語教誨，無敢虧負。當求度世，拔斷生死眾惡之本。永離三塗無量憂畏苦痛之道。汝等於是廣殖德本，布恩施慧，勿犯道禁。忍辱精進，一心智慧。轉相教化，為德立善，正心正意，齋戒清淨一日一夜，勝在無量壽國為善百歲。所以者何？彼佛國土無為自然，皆積眾善，無毛髮之惡。於此修善十日十夜，勝於他方諸佛國中為善千歲。所以者何？他方佛國，為善者多，為惡者少，福德自然，無造惡之地。唯此間多惡，無有自然，勤苦求欲，轉相欺殆，心勞形困，飲苦食毒，如是怱務，未嘗寧息。吾哀汝等天人之類，苦心誨喻，教令修善，隨器開導，授與經法，莫不承用，在意所願，皆令得道。佛所遊履，國邑丘聚，靡不蒙化，天下和順，日月清明，風雨以時，災厲不起，國豐民安，兵戈無用，崇德興仁，務修禮讓。佛言：我哀愍汝等諸天人民，甚於父母念子。今吾於此世作佛，降化五惡，消除五痛，絕滅五燒。以善攻惡，拔生死之苦，令獲五德，昇無為之安。吾去世後，經道漸滅，人民諂偽，復為眾惡，五燒五痛，還如前法。久後轉劇，不可悉說。我但為汝略言之耳。佛告彌勒。汝等各善思之，轉相教誡，如佛經法，無得犯也。於是彌勒菩薩合掌白言。佛所說甚善，世人實爾。如來普慈哀愍，悉令度脫。受佛重誨，不敢違失。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Maitreya: "I have told you: the hardship of these five evils in this world is just as I have described. The five pains and the five burnings generate one another in turn; a person who does nothing but evil, and never cultivates any root of goodness, falls naturally into the evil destinies, every one of them. Some, even in this present life, are struck first by calamity and sickness, unable either to obtain death or to go on living, the consequence of their own wrongdoing shown openly for all to see; and when the body dies, it is carried along by that same conduct into the three evil paths, an immeasurable, bitter suffering that consumes them from within. And over a long time, such people form lasting enmities together, beginning from something small and trivial and growing, in the end, into a great evil — all of it arising from attachment to wealth and lust, and an inability to give freely with wisdom; driven by foolish desire, following wherever their own thoughts lead, they remain bound by affliction with no release. Enriching themselves, contending over gain, giving no thought or consideration to anything else, they take their pleasure for the moment in wealth, rank, and glory, unable to endure hardship, making no effort to cultivate goodness; and their power and influence, however great, in time wear away and are lost, their body left in toil and hardship, growing only more severe as time goes on. The way of heaven, laid out as it is, gathers up and calls each person to account of itself; its net and its ordering framework correspond exactly, above and below, and, utterly alone and afraid, a person must enter into it. This has been so since ancient times — how sorrowful, how truly to be pitied." The Buddha said to Maitreya: "This is how the world is, and the Buddha grieves for it entirely. By the power of his majestic authority, he crushes and dissolves every evil, and leads everyone toward goodness, setting aside their old ways of thinking; upholding the scriptures and precepts, receiving and practicing the teaching of the path, without transgressing it in any way, they at last attain deliverance beyond this world, the path to nirvāṇa." The Buddha said: "You who are here now — every god, every human, and everyone of later generations — on receiving the Buddha's own words in this scripture, should think them through with care, and, in the midst of them, keep your mind upright and your conduct correct. Let a ruler act well, and lead those beneath him by that same example; let each command be passed down and upheld in turn, each person keeping their own conduct correct, honoring the sage, revering the good, kind and broadly loving toward others. Never dare to fall short of the Buddha's own teaching and admonition. You should seek deliverance beyond this world, uprooting and severing the very source of birth, death, and every evil, forever leaving behind the three lower paths and their immeasurable path of dread and suffering. In this, plant broadly the roots of virtue, spread kindness and give wisdom freely, and do not transgress the path's own prohibitions. With patience and diligence, and a single-minded wisdom, teach and transform one another in turn, establish virtue and goodness, set your mind and intent aright — a single day and night of pure fasting and precepts, kept here, outweighs a hundred years of doing good in the land of Amitāyus. And why is this? Because that buddha-land is unconditioned and spontaneous, accumulating only goodness, without so much as a hair's breadth of evil in it. To cultivate goodness for ten days and ten nights here outweighs a thousand years of doing good in any other buddha-land elsewhere. And why is this? Because in other buddha-lands, those who do good are many and those who do evil are few, their blessing and merit arising spontaneously, with no ground there for evil to be created in the first place — only here, in this place, is evil so abundant, with nothing arising spontaneously at all, so that people toil and struggle after their desires, deceiving one another in turn, their mind wearied and their body worn out, drinking bitterness and eating poison, caught up in such busy, urgent affairs that they never once find rest. I grieve for all of you, gods and humans alike, and so, with a troubled and earnest heart, I teach and instruct you, leading you toward goodness, guiding each according to their own capacity, and giving you these scriptures and their teaching — and there is no one who, receiving them, fails to put them to use, each attaining the path exactly according to their own resolve. Wherever the Buddha himself has traveled, in every country, city, hill, and settlement, there is none that has not received this transforming influence; the whole world becomes harmonious and compliant, the sun and moon shine clear, wind and rain come in their proper season, no calamity or plague arises, the country is prosperous and the people at peace, weapons of war go unused, virtue is exalted and humaneness flourishes, and everyone attends to ritual courtesy and mutual deference." The Buddha said: "My grief and compassion for all of you, gods and humans, surpasses even a parent's love for a child. Now, in this present age, having become a buddha in this world, I subdue and transform the five evils, dissolve the five pains, and extinguish the five burnings; with goodness I attack evil, uproot the suffering of birth and death, and cause you to attain the five virtues, and to ascend to the peace of what is unconditioned. After I have left the world, the scriptures and the path will gradually fade away, and people, growing flattering and false again, will return to every evil, and the five burnings and five pains will return exactly as before. As time goes on further still, it will grow only more severe, more than I could ever tell in full — I have spoken of it for you here only in the briefest outline." The Buddha told Maitreya: "Each of you should reflect well upon this, and teach and admonish one another in turn, exactly according to the Buddha's own scripture and its teaching, never transgressing it." Then the bodhisattva Maitreya, with palms joined, said: "What the Buddha has spoken is excellent indeed, and the people of this world are truly as he has described. The Tathāgata, in his universal compassion and pity, brings deliverance to every one of them. Having received the Buddha's own solemn teaching, we shall not dare to transgress it."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

The comparison closing this passage is worth being precise about, because it is easy to read backward: a single day and night of sincere practice in this world outweighs a hundred years of ease in Sukhāvatī, and ten days here outweigh a thousand years of goodness in any other buddha-land. Huiyuan's explanation for this is structural, not a claim that suffering itself has special merit: other buddha-lands make goodness easy and evil nearly impossible to commit, so practice there costs little; this world makes goodness difficult and evil constantly available, so choosing goodness here demonstrates — and, on this reading, actually builds — a different order of resolve. The passage is not recommending that a practitioner prefer suffering to Sukhāvatī; it is explaining why practice undertaken against real resistance carries a weight that practice undertaken in an already-easy environment does not.

The Buddha's own closing prophecy — that after he leaves the world the Dharma will fade, and the five evils will return exactly as before, "more severe... than I could ever tell in full" — is a standard theme found throughout the wider Mahāyāna canon, the doctrine of the Dharma's own historical decline (末法); this sūtra does not treat itself as exempt from that decline, which is precisely why the next part of this translation returns to the specific claim that this sūtra itself will be deliberately preserved for a hundred years after every other teaching has faded. This whole extended unit on this degenerate world — spanning both this part and the previous one — now closes, and the sūtra returns to Sukhāvatī directly: Ānanda is about to see it with his own eyes.
