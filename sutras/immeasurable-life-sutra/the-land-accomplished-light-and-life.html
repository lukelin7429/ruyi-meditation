The Land Accomplished: Light, Life, and the Bodhi Tree

願行成就（上）

Dharmākara is revealed, at last, as Amitāyus Buddha — ten aeons ago, in a land with no mountain, no ocean, and no seasons, whose light and lifespan the sūtra insists are not metaphors but measurements without a ceiling.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T360 · Saṃghavarman's translation, 3rd century CE Commentary guide: Jingying Huiyuan's 無量壽經義疏 (6th c.)
This part covers
The reveal that Dharmākara is now Amitāyus Buddha; the land's geography without Sumeru, oceans, or seasons; the twelve names of his light; his immeasurable lifespan and the ocean-drop parable for his assembly's size; the jeweled trees; the towering bodhi tree and the three kinds of acceptance gained by encountering it.

15. Ten Aeons Ago, in the West

Chinese Source

阿難白佛：法藏菩薩，為已成佛而取滅度，為未成佛，為今現在？佛告阿難：法藏菩薩，今已成佛現在西方。去此十萬億剎，其佛世界名曰安樂。阿難又問：其佛成道已來為經幾時？佛言：成佛已來凡歷十劫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda said to the Buddha: "Has the bodhisattva Dharmākara already become a buddha and passed into extinction, or has he not yet become a buddha, or does he exist right now?" The Buddha said to Ānanda: "The bodhisattva Dharmākara has already become a buddha, and exists right now, in the west. A hundred thousand koṭis of buddha-lands from here, his buddha-world is named Peace and Bliss." Ānanda asked further: "How long has it been since that buddha attained the way?" The Buddha said: "It has now been ten full aeons since he became a buddha."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Fourteen sections into this sūtra, this is the first moment the text confirms outright that Dharmākara's vow succeeded — and it does so only because Ānanda asks a plain, almost administrative question: is he dead, not yet a buddha, or alive right now? Huiyuan reads the bluntness of the question as intentional: the sūtra has spent an enormous amount of narrative on the vow and the cultivation behind it, and only now supplies the one fact a listener actually needs in order to treat any of it as a live claim about the present moment, not a legend about someone long gone. The land's name given here, 安樂 ("Peace and Bliss"), is this sūtra's own name for it; "Utmost Bliss" (極樂), the name used throughout the Amitābha Sūtra, appears later in this translation as an alternate name the text itself supplies, not a discrepancy between the two sūtras.

16. A Land Without Sumeru, Without Seasons

Chinese Source

其佛國土自然七寶。金銀、瑠璃、珊瑚、琥珀、車磲、碼碯合成為地。恢廓曠蕩，不可限極。悉相雜廁，轉相間入。光赫焜耀，微妙奇麗。清淨莊嚴，超踰十方一切世界。眾寶中精，其寶猶如第六天寶。又其國土無須彌山，及金剛圍一切諸山。亦無大海、小海、溪渠、井谷。佛神力故，欲見則見。亦無地獄、餓鬼、畜生諸難之趣。亦無四時春秋冬夏。不寒不熱，常和調適。

Ru-Yi Translation

That buddha's land is naturally formed of the seven treasures. Gold, silver, lapis lazuli, coral, amber, and carnelian together compose its ground, vast and expansive beyond any limit, all mingled together, interpenetrating one another, blazing and brilliant, subtle and wondrous in its rare beauty — pure and adorned beyond every world of the ten directions. Foremost among treasures, its jewels are like the treasures of the sixth heaven. And that land has no Mount Sumeru, nor any of the ring of mountains that encircles it; nor does it have any great ocean, small sea, stream, canal, well, or ravine — yet by the Buddha's spiritual power, should anyone wish to see such a thing, it appears. Nor does it have hells, hungry ghosts, animals, or any of the difficult destinies; nor does it have the four seasons — spring, summer, autumn, winter. It is never cold and never hot, always harmonious and comfortable.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads a passage built this way — almost entirely as a list of absences — as doing real doctrinal work rather than merely painting scenery: naming what is missing (no Sumeru, no seasons, no lower rebirth destinations) makes an explicit claim about what ordinary suffering depends on, by removing each dependency one at a time. Seasons cause cold and heat to be endured; mountains and seas divide beings from one another and obstruct travel; the three lower destinies are the worst outcomes of unwholesome karma. A land built by explicitly subtracting each of these is, on this reading, not simply pleasant but structurally incapable of reproducing the specific mechanisms of hardship that define an ordinary world — this sūtra's version of what the Amitābha Sūtra states more briefly as "no suffering of any kind, only every kind of joy."

17. Where the Gods Live, Without a Mountain to Stand On

Chinese Source

爾時阿難白佛言：世尊，若彼國土無須彌山，其四天王及忉利天，依何而住？佛語阿難：第三炎天，乃至色究竟天，皆依何住？阿難白佛：行業果報不可思議。佛語阿難：行業果報不可思議，諸佛世界亦不可思議。其諸眾生功德善力，住行業之地，故能爾耳。阿難白佛：我不疑此法。但為將來眾生，欲除其疑惑，故問斯義。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if that land has no Mount Sumeru, then where do the Four Heavenly Kings and the Trāyastriṃśa heaven dwell?" The Buddha said to Ānanda: "The third heaven of flame, and on up to the Highest Form heaven — where do they dwell?" Ānanda said to the Buddha: "The results of karmic action are inconceivable." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "The results of karmic action are inconceivable, and the worlds of the buddhas are likewise inconceivable. It is by the power of merit and goodness belonging to those very beings, abiding on the ground of their own karmic action, that they are able to dwell as they do." Ānanda said to the Buddha: "I myself hold no doubt about this teaching. I ask only for the sake of beings of future times, so as to remove their uncertainty, that I raise this question."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Ānanda's own follow-up question is doing something sūtras rarely do explicitly: he supplies the counter-example himself. The heavens above the desire realm — the third heaven of flame and everything above it — have never depended on Mount Sumeru to hold them up in ordinary cosmology either; they simply exist without visible support, sustained by the beings' own karmic power. Once Ānanda names that precedent himself, the Buddha's answer barely needs to add anything. Huiyuan reads this small exchange as a piece of internal reasoning the sūtra offers so a skeptical reader does not have to take the missing mountain purely on faith — the text supplies its own analogy from within a cosmology any reader of the period would already accept.

Ānanda's closing line — that he asks not from his own doubt but to remove the doubt of beings not yet born — recurs across this sūtra whenever a genuinely hard question is raised, and Huiyuan reads it as more than a rhetorical courtesy: it marks this text, more explicitly than most sūtras, as being composed with an eye toward readers who were not present in the room.

18. Twelve Names for a Single Light

Chinese Source

佛告阿難：無量壽佛威神光明最尊第一。諸佛光明所不能及。或有佛光照百佛世界、或千佛世界。取要言之，乃照東方恒沙佛剎，南、西、北方四維，上、下亦復如是。或有佛光照于七尺，或照一由旬，二、三、四、五由旬，如是轉倍乃至照一佛剎。是故無量壽佛號無量光佛、無邊光佛、無礙光佛、無對光佛、炎王光佛、清淨光佛、歡喜光佛、智慧光佛、不斷光佛、難思光佛、無稱光佛、超日月光佛。其有眾生遇斯光者，三垢消滅，身意柔軟，歡喜踊躍，善心生焉。若在三塗勤苦之處，見此光明，皆得休息，無復苦惱。壽終之後，皆蒙解脫。無量壽佛光明顯赫照曜十方諸佛國土，莫不聞知。不但我今稱其光明，一切諸佛、聲聞、緣覺、諸菩薩眾，咸共歎譽，亦復如是。若有眾生。聞其光明威神功德，日夜稱說，至心不斷，隨意所願，得生其國。為諸菩薩聲聞大眾，所共歎譽稱其功德。至其然後得佛道時，普為十方諸佛菩薩，歎其光明亦如今也。佛言：我說無量壽佛光明威神巍巍殊妙，晝夜一劫尚不能盡。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Ānanda: "Amitāyus Buddha's majestic light is foremost and supreme — no other buddha's light can reach as far. Some buddhas' light illumines a hundred buddha-worlds, or a thousand buddha-worlds; to state it plainly, it illumines the buddha-lands of the east as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, and the same for south, west, north, the four intermediate directions, above and below. Some buddhas' light illumines only seven feet, or one yojana, two, three, four, or five yojanas, and so on by increasing multiples up to a single buddha-land. This is why Amitāyus Buddha is named: the Buddha of Immeasurable Light, the Buddha of Boundless Light, the Buddha of Unobstructed Light, the Buddha of Unrivaled Light, the Buddha of Blazing Kingly Light, the Buddha of Pure Light, the Buddha of Joyful Light, the Buddha of Wisdom's Light, the Buddha of Unceasing Light, the Buddha of Inconceivable Light, the Buddha of Indescribable Light, and the Buddha of Light Surpassing Sun and Moon. Any being who encounters this light finds the three defilements dissolved, body and mind made gentle and soft, and joy and delight leaping up, and a wholesome mind is born in them. And if a being dwells in the hard toil of the three lower destinies and sees this light, they all gain rest and are troubled no more; and when their life there ends, every one of them is granted liberation. The blazing radiance of Amitāyus Buddha's light shines upon the buddha-lands of the ten directions, and there is nowhere it is not known and heard of. It is not only I who praise this light today — every buddha, śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and host of bodhisattvas together praise it in just the same way. If there are beings who hear of the majesty and merit of this light and, day and night, proclaim it without ceasing, with a sincere mind, then according to their own wish they attain rebirth in his land, and are praised for their own merit by the assembled bodhisattvas and śrāvakas there; and when, later still, they themselves attain the path to buddhahood, they too will be praised for their own light by all the buddhas and bodhisattvas of the ten directions, just as is done now." The Buddha said: "Were I to speak of the majestic, towering, rare splendor of Amitāyus Buddha's light for a full aeon, day and night without pause, I still could not exhaust it."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the twelve light-names as twelve genuinely distinct claims rather than synonyms strung together for emphasis: unobstructed describes its reach through any barrier, unrivaled its lack of any equal, blazing kingly its intensity, pure its moral effect on whoever is touched by it, unceasing its constancy in time, inconceivable and indescribable the limits of any listener's ability to fully grasp it in words at all. Read this way, the passage is closer to a careful definition, worked from several angles, than to devotional repetition.

Worth being precise about what the light is said to do: it is not described here as forcibly saving anyone, nor as overriding a being's own will. The stated effects — the three defilements dissolving, a wholesome mind arising, rest granted to beings suffering in the lower destinies — are described as a kind of contact that makes wholesome response possible, not as an imposed transformation. This is consistent with the causal, rather than transactional, logic the Amitābha Sūtra's own reading notes describe for the practice of reciting the name: something is made available by contact with the light, not compelled by it.

19. A Lifespan Beyond Reckoning

Chinese Source

佛語阿難：又無量壽佛，壽命長久不可稱計。汝寧知乎？假使十方世界無量眾生皆得人身，悉令成就聲聞緣覺，都共集會禪思一心竭其智力，於百千萬劫悉共推算，計其壽命長遠劫數，不能窮盡知其限極。聲聞菩薩天人之眾，壽命長短亦復如是，非算數譬喻所能知也。又聲聞菩薩，其數難量，不可稱說。神智洞達，威力自在，能於掌中持一切世界。佛語阿難：彼佛初會，聲聞眾數不可稱計，菩薩亦然。能如大目揵連，百千萬億無量無數，於阿僧祇那由他劫，乃至滅度，悉共計挍，不能究了多少之數。譬如大海深廣無量，假使有人，析其一毛以為百分，以一分毛沾取一渧。於意云何，其所渧者於彼大海，何所為多？阿難白佛：彼所渧水，比於大海，多少之量，非巧歷算數，言辭譬類，所能知也。佛語阿難：如目連等，於百千萬億那由他劫，計彼初會聲聞菩薩，所知數者猶如一渧，其所不知如大海水。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Furthermore, Amitāyus Buddha's lifespan is so long that it cannot be reckoned or counted. Do you know how long? Suppose the immeasurable beings of the worlds of the ten directions all attained a human body, and all of them became śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, and all of them gathered together and, with a single, concentrated mind, exhausted the full power of their wisdom, and across a hundred thousand koṭis of aeons together reckoned and calculated the length of his life in aeons — they could not exhaust it or know its limit. The lifespans of his śrāvakas, bodhisattvas, gods, and humans, whether long or short, are just the same — beyond what any number or comparison could convey. And his śrāvakas and bodhisattvas are so numerous they cannot be measured or described; their spiritual understanding is thoroughly penetrating, their power free and unobstructed, able to hold every world within the palm of a hand." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "At that buddha's first assembly, the number of his śrāvakas could not be reckoned, and it was the same for his bodhisattvas. Even if beings like Mahāmaudgalyāyana — a hundred thousand koṭis of them, immeasurable and countless — spent asaṃkhyeya nayutas of aeons, all the way until their own extinction, calculating together, they could not fully know that number. It is like the great ocean, deep and vast beyond measure: suppose a person split a single hair into a hundred parts, and with one of those hair-fine parts dipped up a single drop of water — what do you think, would that drop amount to very much, set against that great ocean?" Ānanda said to the Buddha: "That drop of water, set against the ocean, is a quantity that no skilled reckoning, no words, and no comparison could ever convey." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "It is just the same with someone like Maudgalyāyana: even spending a hundred thousand koṭis of nayutas of aeons calculating the śrāvakas and bodhisattvas of that first assembly, what could be known of their number is like that single drop, and what remains unknown is like the water of the whole ocean."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan pairs this passage directly with the Twelfth and Thirteenth Vows from Part 3 — immeasurable light, immeasurable life — and reads this section as the sūtra making good on those two vows specifically, in the same order they were originally promised. The ocean-drop parable recurs from Part 2, where Lokeśvararāja used the same image — a single person measuring the ocean ladle by ladle — to encourage Dharmākara that any sufficiently patient effort eventually reaches its goal; here the same image is repurposed to describe not effort but scale, the gap between what even Mahāmaudgalyāyana, traditionally foremost among the Buddha's disciples in supernormal powers, could ever calculate and what the true number actually is. Huiyuan treats the echo as deliberate: the sūtra reuses its own images rather than reaching for new ones, so a careful reader notices the pattern building across parts written, in the story's own timeline, aeons apart.

20. The Jeweled Trees

Chinese Source

又其國土。七寶諸樹周滿世界。金樹、銀樹、瑠璃樹、頗梨樹、珊瑚樹、瑪瑙樹、車磲之樹。或有二寶、三寶、乃至七寶轉共合成。或有金樹，銀葉華果。或有銀樹，金葉華果。或瑠璃樹，玻梨為葉，華果亦然。或水精樹，瑠璃為葉，華果亦然。或珊瑚樹，瑪瑙為葉，華果亦然。或瑪瑙樹，瑠璃為葉，華果亦然。或車磲樹，眾寶為葉，華果亦然。或有寶樹，紫金為本，白銀為莖，瑠璃為枝，水精為條，珊瑚為葉，瑪瑙為華，車磲為實。（……如是七寶輾轉為本、莖、枝、條、葉、華、實，七重錯綜合成。）行行相值，莖莖相望，枝枝相准，葉葉相向，華華相順，實實相當。榮色光曜不可勝視。清風時發出五音聲，微妙宮商自然相和。

Ru-Yi Translation

And in that land, trees of the seven treasures fill the whole world: trees of gold, of silver, of lapis lazuli, of crystal, of coral, of carnelian, of mother-of-pearl. Some are formed together of two treasures, or three, and on up to all seven at once. Some are gold trees with silver leaves, flowers, and fruit; some are silver trees with golden leaves, flowers, and fruit. Some are lapis lazuli trees with crystal leaves, and their flowers and fruit likewise; some are crystal trees with lapis lazuli leaves, and likewise their flowers and fruit; some are coral trees with carnelian leaves, and likewise; some are carnelian trees with lapis lazuli leaves, and likewise; some are mother-of-pearl trees with leaves of every jewel, and likewise. And some are jeweled trees with a trunk of red-gold, a stem of white silver, branches of lapis lazuli, twigs of crystal, leaves of coral, flowers of carnelian, and fruit of mother-of-pearl — and so on, the seven treasures exchanging places in turn as trunk, stem, branch, twig, leaf, flower, and fruit, interwoven sevenfold in every combination. Row answers row, stem faces stem, branch matches branch, leaf turns toward leaf, flower corresponds to flower, and fruit answers fruit — their splendor and radiance more than the eye can take in. When a clear wind rises now and then, it draws out five musical tones, and their subtle notes harmonize together of themselves.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

The Chinese here runs through all seven possible rotations of the seven treasures as trunk, stem, branch, twig, leaf, flower, and fruit in full — a passage this translation has compressed with an editorial ellipsis rather than reproducing all seven combinatorial variations at equal length, since each simply cycles the same seven materials through the same seven positions. Nothing of doctrinal content is lost in the compression; what is lost is the sheer repetitive density of the original, which Huiyuan reads as itself part of the point — a deliberately exhaustive, almost combinatorial thoroughness standing in for a claim that literally nothing about this land's decoration was left arbitrary or incomplete.

21. The Bodhi Tree, and Three Kinds of Acceptance

Chinese Source

又無量壽佛，其道場樹髙，四百萬里。其本周圍五千由旬。枝葉四布二十萬里。一切眾寶自然合成。以月光摩尼持海輪寶眾寶之王。而莊嚴之。周匝條間垂寶瓔珞。百千萬色，種種異變。無量光炎照曜無極。珍妙寶網羅覆其上，一切莊嚴隨應而現。微風徐動，出妙法音，普流十方一切佛國。其聞音者，得深法忍，住不退轉，至成佛道，不遭苦患。目覩其色，耳聞其音，鼻知其香，舌甞其味。身觸其光，心以法緣。一切皆得甚深法忍，住不退轉至成佛道，六根清徹，無諸惱患。阿難，若彼人天見此樹者，得三法忍：一者、音響忍，二者、柔順忍，三者、無生法忍。此皆無量壽佛威神力故，本願力故，滿足願故，明了願故，堅固願故，究竟願故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And Amitāyus Buddha's own tree of awakening rises four million li in height; its trunk measures five thousand yojanas around, and its branches and leaves spread twenty myriad li on every side. It is formed of every kind of jewel, spontaneously joined together, and adorned with moonlight-maṇi jewels and the king of ocean-wheel treasures among all treasures. All around, hanging among its branches, are strings of jeweled ornaments in a hundred thousand colors, each shifting and changing; its immeasurable light and radiance shine without limit, and rare, wondrous jeweled nets cover it over, every kind of adornment appearing in response to whatever is fitting. When a gentle breeze stirs it, it sends out a wondrous sound of the Dharma, flowing out to pervade every buddha-land of the ten directions. Anyone who hears that sound attains a deep acceptance of the Dharma, abides in non-retrogression, and, all the way to buddhahood, meets with no affliction or harm. Whether one sees its color with the eye, hears its sound with the ear, smells its fragrance with the nose, tastes its flavor with the tongue, feels its light touch the body, or connects with it through the mind by way of the Dharma — everyone, in every one of these ways, attains a profound acceptance of the Dharma, abides in non-retrogression all the way to buddhahood, and has all six faculties made clear and pure, free of any affliction. Ānanda, if the humans and gods of that land behold this tree, they attain three kinds of acceptance: the acceptance that comes through sound and echo, the acceptance of pliant compliance, and the acceptance of the birthless nature of things. All of this comes about through the majestic power of Amitāyus Buddha, through the power of his original vow, through the vow now fulfilled, made clear, made firm, and brought to its very end.

Reading note — after Huiyuan

Huiyuan reads the three kinds of acceptance named here as a graded sequence rather than three unrelated gifts: acceptance through sound and echo (音響忍) is a first, still relatively coarse recognition of the Dharma's truth, prompted by something heard, the way an echo is recognized as sound without yet being fully understood; acceptance through pliant compliance (柔順忍) is a mind grown flexible enough to follow the teaching without resistance, no longer needing to be prompted by an external sound at all; and acceptance of the birthless nature of things (無生法忍) is the deepest of the three, a direct realization that nothing truly arises or ceases — the same attainment the Diamond Sūtra names, in different vocabulary, as central to a bodhisattva's progress. That this land's own bodhi tree can transmit all three simply by being seen, heard, or even touched by light is, on Huiyuan's reading, the clearest single illustration in this whole section of what the sūtra means when it insists these adornments are not scenery but a functioning teaching device.

The six-fold list closing this passage — the Buddha's majestic power, the power of his original vow, the vow fulfilled, made clear, made firm, brought to its end — recurs in the sūtra's own vocabulary whenever it wants to explain why some feature of the land works the way it does; Huiyuan reads it as a compact restatement of the same causal claim made at the end of Part 3: nothing here is magic without a cause. Every feature of the land traces back, by the text's own repeated insistence, to Dharmākara's vow and to the cultivation that fulfilled it.

22. Music Beyond the Six Heavens

Chinese Source

佛告阿難：世間帝王有百千音樂。自轉輪聖王。乃至第六天上伎樂音聲。展轉相勝千億萬倍。第六天上萬種樂音。不如無量壽國諸七寶樹一種音聲。千億倍也。亦有自然萬種伎樂。又其樂聲無非法音。清暢哀亮微妙和雅。十方世界音聲之中最為第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Ānanda: "The kings of the world have a hundred thousand kinds of music. From a wheel-turning sage-king on up to the music of the sixth heaven, each surpasses the one before by a thousand koṭis of times. Yet the ten thousand kinds of music of the sixth heaven do not equal even a single note of Amitāyus's land's seven-jeweled trees — that single note surpasses them by a thousand koṭis of times. And that land also has, spontaneously arising, ten thousand kinds of music of its own, and every sound of that music is nothing other than the sound of the Dharma — clear, resonant, moving, subtle, and harmonious, foremost among every sound in the worlds of the ten directions."

Reading note — after Huiyuan

The comparative structure here — human kings, then a wheel-turning sage-king, then each of the six heavens of the desire realm in ascending order, each surpassing the last by a fixed multiple — is a standard cosmological ladder used elsewhere in the canon to describe relative splendor; this same ladder returns, extended further, in the parable of the beggar beside the king in the next part of this translation. Huiyuan's point in citing music specifically, rather than any other sense, is that it makes the land's "teaching function" audible in the most ordinary possible way: even background sound in Sukhāvatī is, by the text's own account, never merely pleasant but always already Dharma.
