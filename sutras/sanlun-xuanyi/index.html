Jizang · 549–623 CE · 三論玄義

The School Explains Itself.

Jizang founded the Sanlun — Three Treatise, Chinese Madhyamaka — school on three works this site has already translated in full: the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, the Twelve Gate Treatise, and the Hundred Treatise. This treatise is his own apologia for that school, written by imperial command at the Sui court. It refutes four sects in turn — the outsiders of India and China, the Abhidharma, Satyasiddhi, and a mistaken grasp of the Mahāyāna itself — then reveals what is true: Nāgārjuna's own prophesied authority, the guiding doctrine proper to each of the four treatises this school defends, an entire history of how the Buddhist community first split into schools, and finally an exhaustive exegesis of the very name Zhonglun, "the Middle Treatise."

How to read this collection

Jizang's own opening paragraph gives the whole map: two gates, "refuting the false" and "revealing the true," with four sects to refute and a further extended account of what the three treatises actually teach once refutation is complete. This collection follows that same two-gate structure across fourteen parts, splitting at the natural topic breaks Jizang himself marks with his own recurring 次明, "next, making clear," rather than at even intervals — so some parts carry a single tightly focused argument and others carry an entire chapter of Indian sectarian history.

Jizang is the recognized founder of this school and its acknowledged authority on what the three treatises mean, so this collection does not manufacture a separate ancient commentator the way the site's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā and Hundred Treatise pages do with Piṅgala and Vasu. His own prose is the primary text throughout; the reading notes are Ru-Yi Meditation Center's own structural and cross-referential observations, offered honestly as such.

The Treatise's Own Structure

Jizang's opening paragraph names two gates. The first, refuting the false, rebukes four sects in ascending order of sophistication — outsiders, who posit both a self and real phenomena; the Abhidharma, which has seen through the self but still clings to real phenomena; Satyasiddhi, which sees the emptiness of both but does not carry the insight to completion; and a mistaken Mahāyāna, which can fall into a subtler version of the very same trap. The second gate, revealing the true, spans everything after — Nāgārjuna's authority, the treatise's own guiding doctrines, the schism history, and the final exegesis of the treatise's own title.
