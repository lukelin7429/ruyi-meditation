How Sūtra and Treatise Depend on Each Other

Jizang steps back from the doctrinal battles of the first seven parts to ask a structural question: what exactly is a treatise, and how does it relate to the scripture it comments on? The answer runs through a fourfold web of mutual dependency that finally dissolves into "neither sūtra nor treatise, neither Buddha nor bodhisattva" — after which the treatise turns to history, opening its long account of how the Buddhist community first split apart, from the First Council's peaceable division into two groups through Mahādeva's five contested theses to the first wave of doctrinal schism two centuries later.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T45, no. 1852, 三論玄義 Jizang (吉藏, 549–623) Reading notes: Ru-Yi, original

1. How Sūtra and Treatise Depend on Each Other

Jizang opens with the most radical possible framing — from the standpoint of ultimate emptiness, there is no birth-and-death, no nirvāṇa, no sentient being, and no buddha, so how could there even be such a thing as sūtra or treatise? The answer moves immediately from that ultimate vantage point back down to the conventional level where sūtra and treatise, and their own internal distinctions of shared and particular purpose, actually operate.

Chinese Source

次明經論相資。大品經云。雖生死道長眾生性多。菩薩應如是正憶念。生死邊如虛空。眾生性邊亦如虛空。此中無生死往來亦無解脫者。然既無生死亦無涅槃。則知亦無眾生及以於佛。寧有經之與論耶。故內外並冥緣觀俱寂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, making clear how sūtra and treatise depend on each other. The Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says: "Although the road of birth-and-death is long and the nature of sentient beings is manifold, the bodhisattva should correctly recollect thus: the bounds of birth-and-death are like empty space, and the bounds of sentient beings' own nature are likewise like empty space. Within this, there is no coming and going of birth-and-death, and no one who attains liberation either." Since, then, there is neither birth-and-death nor nirvāṇa, it may be known that there is likewise no sentient being and no buddha — how, then, could there possibly be any such thing as sūtra or treatise? For this reason, inside and outside are both stilled together, and the condition contemplated and the contemplating mind are alike quiesced.

然雖非生死涅槃。而於眾生成生死。故大品云。諸法無所有如是有。既有眾生故有諸佛。既有諸佛便有教門。既有諸佛教門則有菩薩之論。諸佛為眾生失道。是故說經。菩薩為眾生迷經。是故造論。

And yet, although it is truly neither birth-and-death nor nirvāṇa, it nonetheless comes to constitute birth-and-death for sentient beings. Hence the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says: "All dharmas, having nothing whatsoever of their own, exist in just this way." Since, then, there are sentient beings, there come to be buddhas; since there are buddhas, there comes to be a teaching-gate; and since there is the buddhas' own teaching-gate, there comes to be the bodhisattvas' own treatise-writing. The buddhas, on account of sentient beings having lost the way, therefore expound sūtras; the bodhisattvas, on account of sentient beings being confused about the sūtras, therefore compose treatises.

然經有通別。在論亦爾。所言經通者。通為息眾生顛倒。通為開顯道門。所言論通者。諸聖弟子造一切論。亦通為息迷教之病申明正道。所言經別者。赴大小二緣說大小兩教。所言論別者。為破大小兩迷申大小兩教。故有大小二論也。

Now, sūtra has both a shared and a particular aspect, and the same holds for treatise as well. What is called the shared aspect of sūtra is this: shared in that it brings the inverted views of sentient beings to rest; shared in that it opens up and reveals the gate of the way. What is called the shared aspect of treatise is this: whatever treatise any of the noble disciples composed, it too was shared in bringing to rest the sickness of confusion about the teaching, and in setting forth the correct path clearly. What is called the particular aspect of sūtra is this: responding to the two distinct kinds of receptivity, great and small, by teaching the two distinct teachings, great and small. What is called the particular aspect of treatise is this: in order to refute the two distinct kinds of confusion, great and small, setting forth the two distinct teachings, great and small — hence there come to be the two kinds of treatise, Hīnayāna and Mahāyāna.

然就經論之中具有能所之義。經以二智為能說。二諦為所說。論以二慧為能說。言教為所說。斯則經論各有能所也。

Now, within sūtra and treatise alike, there is fully present the sense of agent and patient. In sūtra, the two kinds of knowledge serve as the agent that speaks, and the two truths serve as the patient that is spoken of. In treatise, the two kinds of wisdom serve as the agent that speaks, and verbal teaching serves as the patient that is spoken of. In this way, sūtra and treatise each have their own agent and their own patient.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This passage supplies the vocabulary this collection has been building toward since the essential/functional distinction of Part 7: "the two kinds of knowledge" (二智) are a buddha's own knowledge of the conventional and the ultimate, "the two truths" (二諦) are what that knowledge knows, and "the two kinds of wisdom" (二慧) are what a bodhisattva-commentator like Nāgārjuna attains in coming to understand what the Buddha taught. The distinction between shared (通) and particular (別) purpose in both sūtra and treatise becomes the direct basis, later in this collection, for classifying which of the four treatises this school defends count as "general treatises" spanning all of Buddhist teaching and which count as "particular treatises" addressed to one specific confusion.

2. Agent and Patient, Interlaced Four Ways

Having established that sūtra and treatise each have their own agent and patient, Jizang shows that these four terms interlock with each other as well — the Buddha's own knowledge becomes what the treatise-author takes as his object of realization, and so on through all four possible combinations — before collapsing the whole fourfold scheme first into a single pairing, and finally into the wordless real character that the entire apparatus was only ever pointing toward.

Chinese Source

次明經論能所絞絡。有四句不同。一者經能為論所。二者經所為論能。三者論能為經所。四者論所為經能。經能為論所者。如來二智即是論主所悟。故法華明。今昔兩教為直往菩薩及迴小向大之人。並令悟入佛慧。故涌出品云。是諸眾生始見我身聞我所說。即便信受入如來慧。此明昔教為直往菩薩入佛慧也。次云。除先修習學小乘者。我今亦令得聞是經入於佛慧。此明今教迴小之人入於佛慧。故今昔兩教同明為入佛慧。則知佛慧是所悟也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, making clear how the agent and patient of sūtra and treatise interlace with one another. There are four distinct propositions. First, sūtra's agent becomes treatise's patient. Second, sūtra's patient becomes treatise's agent. Third, treatise's agent becomes sūtra's patient. Fourth, treatise's patient becomes sūtra's agent. As for sūtra's agent becoming treatise's patient: the Tathāgata's own two kinds of knowledge are precisely what the treatise-author takes as his object of realization. Hence the Lotus Sūtra makes clear that both the present and the past teachings alike lead direct-path bodhisattvas, as well as those who turn from the lesser toward the great, all alike to awaken and enter the Buddha's own wisdom. Hence the "Emerging from the Earth" chapter says: "These sentient beings, on first seeing my body and hearing what I teach, straightaway believe and accept it, and enter the Tathāgata's own wisdom." This makes clear that the past teaching leads the direct-path bodhisattva to enter the Buddha's wisdom. It then says: "Excepting those who had previously cultivated and studied the Hīnayāna, I now likewise cause them to hear this sūtra and enter the Buddha's wisdom." This makes clear that the present teaching leads those who turn from the lesser to enter the Buddha's wisdom. Since, then, both the present and past teachings alike make clear that their purpose is entry into the Buddha's wisdom, it may be known that the Buddha's wisdom is precisely what is realized.

次明經所為論能者。經所即是二諦。能發生論主二慧故。佛之二諦為能生。論主二慧為所生也。次明論能為經所者。論主二慧由經發生也。次明論所為經能者。論主言教能申佛二諦也。次會四句為二句。經若能若所並是能資。論若能若所皆是所資。又論若能若所悉為能申。經若能若所悉是為所申。故合成一能一所也。

Next, making clear how sūtra's patient becomes treatise's agent: sūtra's patient is precisely the two truths, which are able to give rise to the treatise-author's own two kinds of wisdom. On this account, the Buddha's two truths serve as what generates, and the treatise-author's two kinds of wisdom serve as what is generated. Next, making clear how treatise's agent becomes sūtra's patient: the treatise-author's two kinds of wisdom arise by being generated from sūtra. Next, making clear how treatise's patient becomes sūtra's agent: the treatise-author's own verbal teaching is able to set forth the Buddha's two truths. Next, reconciling these four propositions into two: whether agent or patient, sūtra as a whole serves as what supplies; whether agent or patient, treatise as a whole serves as what is supplied. Furthermore, whether agent or patient, treatise as a whole serves as what expounds; whether agent or patient, sūtra as a whole serves as what is expounded. Hence they are brought together into a single agent and a single patient.

次泯一句以歸無句。以能而為所則能非定能。以所而為能則所非定所。以能非定能。是則非能。所非定所。是則非所。故非能非所。非經非論。非佛非菩薩。不知何以目之。故稱正法強名中實也。

Next, dissolving even this single proposition to return to no proposition at all: since what is agent can become patient, agent is thereby not fixed as agent; and since what is patient can become agent, patient is thereby not fixed as patient. Since agent is not fixed as agent, it is thereby not truly agent at all. Since patient is not fixed as patient, it is thereby not truly patient at all. Hence it is neither agent nor patient, neither sūtra nor treatise, neither Buddha nor bodhisattva. Not knowing what else to call it, one names it the true Dharma, or forcibly designates it the reality within the middle.

問能非定能是則非能所非定所是則非所。出何文耶。答中論然可然品云。若法因待成。是法還成待。今則無因待。亦無所成法。即其證也。

Question: "Since agent is not fixed as agent, it is thereby not truly agent at all; since patient is not fixed as patient, it is thereby not truly patient at all" — from what text does this come? Answer: The chapter examining the burning and the burnable in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā says: "If a dharma is established through mutual dependence, that very dharma, in turn, comes to constitute the ground for a further dependence. But here there is no dependence to serve as cause, and likewise no dharma that is thereby established." This is exactly the proof of it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "burning and the burnable" chapter cited here is the tenth chapter of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, which uses fire and its fuel as a paradigm case for showing that neither term of a mutually dependent pair can be established as more fundamental than the other — exactly the logical structure Jizang has just applied to sūtra and treatise, agent and patient. The final destination of this whole fourfold analysis, "neither agent nor patient, neither sūtra nor treatise, neither Buddha nor bodhisattva," is the same "reality within the middle" (中實) that will receive its own extended, dedicated treatment in the title-exegesis chapters that close this entire collection, beginning with Part 13.

3. Why Treatises Get Written: The Root Loss and the Branch Loss

Jizang now asks directly why treatises need to exist at all, given that the Buddha's own sūtras already exist. His answer distinguishes two layers of "loss" — the Buddha's own hearers losing the way, which is the root loss that sūtra addresses, and later students losing the sūtra's own meaning, which is the branch loss, or "a loss within a loss," that treatise exists specifically to address.

Chinese Source

次別明造論緣起。然所以造論者。如上所明。如來為失道故說經。論主為迷經故造論。為失道故說經。此是根本失。論主為迷經故造論。此是枝末失。又佛為失道者說經。此失謂一往失。論主為迷經故造論。此失即失中更起失。所以然者。以其迷道此是一失。如來說經為令入道。而復迷經故是失中失也一往之失謂利根人。聞經即悟。失中之失謂鈍根人也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, making clear in particular the origin of treatise-composition. As to why treatises came to be composed, it is as already explained above: the Tathāgata, on account of beings losing the way, therefore expounded sūtras; the treatise-author, on account of beings being confused about the sūtra, therefore composed treatises. Expounding sūtras on account of the loss of the way — this is the root loss. Composing treatises on account of confusion about the sūtra — this is the branch loss. Moreover, the Buddha's expounding sūtras for those who have lost the way addresses a loss that may be called a single, straightforward loss. The treatise-author's composing treatises on account of confusion about the sūtra addresses a loss that is, in fact, a further loss arising within an original loss. The reason is this: losing the way is, in itself, a single loss; the Tathāgata expounds the sūtra so as to bring one into the way, and yet if one becomes confused about the sūtra as well, this is a loss within a loss. The single, straightforward loss describes those of sharp faculty, who awaken the very moment they hear the sūtra. The loss within a loss describes those of dull faculty.

問何等是迷經之人。答即是諸部異執。言諸部異執者。或二部或五部。或十八部。或二十部。或五百部。

Question: Who, exactly, are these people confused about the sūtra? Answer: They are precisely the various schools with their divergent doctrines. As for these various schools with divergent doctrines, they are counted variously as two schools, or five schools, or eighteen schools, or twenty schools, or five hundred schools.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This "loss within a loss" framework quietly reframes the entire purpose of everything this treatise has been doing since Part 1: the three treatises are not correcting the Buddha's own teaching, which was always sufficient for those of sharp faculty, but correcting a second-order confusion that arose only among those who, in trying to understand the sūtras, ended up misreading them instead. Every sect refuted in Parts 1 through 6 of this collection — outsiders excepted — falls under this category of "confused about the sūtra" rather than "having lost the way" outright, which is a notably more forgiving diagnosis than it might first appear.

4. The First Schism: Two Schools at the First Council

The great historical narrative begins at the very moment of the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, with the First Council convened at Rājagṛha under Mahākāśyapa's leadership — and a second, larger assembly of those left outside it, which Jizang identifies as the origin of the later Mahāsāṃghika school.

Chinese Source

言二部者。如來二月十五日入涅槃。諸聖弟子四月十五日於王舍城祇闍崛山中結集三藏。爾時即有二部名字。一上座部。謂迦葉為上座。迦葉上陳如一夏。為佛以法付屬迦葉名上座部也。迦葉所領但有五百人。依智度論則有千人。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for what is meant by "two schools": the Tathāgata entered parinirvāṇa on the fifteenth day of the second month, and the noble disciples compiled the Three Baskets at Gṛdhrakūṭa Mountain in Rājagṛha on the fifteenth day of the fourth month. At that time there arose the names of two schools. The first is the Sthavira, "the Elders." Kāśyapa is reckoned as the elder, ranking above Ājñāta-Kauṇḍinya by a single rains-retreat in seniority; the Buddha entrusted the Dharma specifically to Kāśyapa, and this is why it is named the Sthavira school. The group Kāśyapa led numbered only five hundred; according to the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, however, it numbered a thousand.

二大眾部。即界外大眾。乃有萬數。婆師波羅漢為主。此云淚出。常悲苦眾生而淚墮也。即五比丘中之一人而年大迦葉。教授界外大眾。所以有二眾。迦葉有五百羅漢。前入界內結集三藏。後多人來結集三藏。迦葉並不許之。有二因緣。一者五百皆聰明人故。二者已羯磨竟故。依智度論。阿闍世王但設千人食。故餘人來不得。從是以來至佛滅度後百一十六年。但有二部名字未有異執。

The second is the Mahāsāṃghika, "the Great Assembly" — that is, the great assembly gathered outside the boundary line, numbering in the tens of thousands, with the arhat Vāṣpa as its leader. His name translates as "one who sheds tears," since he was forever grieving in compassion for suffering sentient beings and shedding tears over them; he was one of the group of five original disciples, and being senior in age even to Kāśyapa, he took charge of instructing the great assembly gathered outside the boundary. This is why there came to be two separate assemblies. Kāśyapa had five hundred arhats who entered within the boundary first to compile the Three Baskets; afterward, a great many more people arrived wishing to join the compilation, but Kāśyapa did not permit them to do so, for two reasons: first, because the five hundred were all men of penetrating intelligence; second, because the formal proceeding had already been completed. According to the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, King Ajātaśatru had prepared food for only a thousand people, and this is why the remaining arrivals could not be accommodated. From that time onward, up to the hundred and sixteenth year after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, there existed only the two names of these two schools, with no divergent doctrines between them as yet.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the opening of an enormous historical undertaking that will occupy this and the following two parts of this collection in full — a genealogy of every named school of early Indian Buddhism, traced from this single point of origin. Notice that Jizang is careful to record a real discrepancy between sources (five hundred versus a thousand at the First Council) rather than silently picking one figure, a habit of honest source-citation that recurs throughout this entire historical section.

5. Mahādeva's Five Theses, and the Schism Made Doctrinal

For over a century the two groups remained only nominally distinct. That changed with Mahādeva, a figure of a genuinely troubled past who, after ordination, proposed a set of controversial theses about the nature of arhatship that split the community permanently — the first time, on this account, that the division between the two schools became a matter of real doctrinal disagreement rather than mere administrative history.

Chinese Source

百一十六年外有舶主兒名摩訶提婆。端正聰明作三逆罪。後入佛法。凡有二事。一者取諸大乘經內三藏中釋之。諸阿羅漢結集法藏時。已簡除此義。而大眾部用此義。上座部不用之。因爾起諍遂成二部。二者摩訶提婆自作偈言。餘人染污衣。無明疑他度。聖道言所顯。是諸佛正教。以此一偈安置戒後。布薩誦戒竟亦誦此一偈。

Ru-Yi Translation

Beyond the hundred and sixteenth year, there was the son of a ship's captain named Mahādeva — handsome and intelligent, who committed three of the five heinous crimes, and only afterward entered the Buddhist path. His activity within the tradition consisted, in all, of two things. First, he took the meaning found within various Mahāyāna sūtras and used it to interpret passages within the Three Baskets; this very meaning had already been deliberately excluded at the time the arhats compiled the Dharma treasury, yet the Mahāsāṃghika now adopted this meaning while the Sthavira did not, and it was on this account that a dispute arose which finally hardened into two separate schools. Second, Mahādeva himself composed a verse, saying: "Others may soil his robe; there is ignorance; there is doubt; one is led across by another; the noble path is manifest through speech — these are the true teachings of all the buddhas." He placed this single verse directly after the recitation of the precepts, so that whenever the precepts were chanted at the poṣadha ceremony, this verse too came to be recited immediately afterward.

此偈有五事。一餘人染污衣者。提婆不淨出污衣。而誑弟子言。我是阿羅漢。實無不淨。但是天魔女以不淨污羅漢衣。故云餘人染污衣。然此一語有虛有實。其實是凡夫。誑弟子說如上事。是故為虛。魔女實能以不淨污羅漢衣。是故為實。其眾諍其所說。或虛或實。故分二部。

This verse contains five separate matters. First, "others may soil his robe": Mahādeva, having experienced a nocturnal emission that soiled his robe, deceived his disciples by claiming, "I am an arhat; in reality there was no impurity at all — it was only that a heavenly demon-maiden soiled an arhat's robe with impurity," and this is why the verse says "others may soil his robe." Now, this one statement contains both falsehood and truth. In actual fact, Mahādeva was simply an ordinary, unenlightened person who deceived his disciples with the account just described — this much is false. Yet it is true that a demon-maiden really is capable of soiling an arhat's robe with impurity in just this way — this much is true. His followers disputed over which parts of his statement were false and which were true, and it was on this account that the school split into two.

二云無明者。然羅漢乃無三界受生無明。而有無知習氣無明。故云無明。時眾或言羅漢有無明。或言無無明。因此起諍故分二部。三云疑者。須陀洹果乃於三解脫門無疑。而於外事有疑。故云疑也。四他度者。鈍根初果而不自知得初果。問善知識。善知識為說。於三寶四諦無疑。是初果相。其自觀察方知得初果。故云他度。五聖道言所顯者。然得聖道時。亦有言所顯。如身子當口誦偈時即得初果。故云言所顯。時眾諍此五義。或是或非。故成二部也。

Second, "there is ignorance": an arhat is indeed without the ignorance that leads to rebirth within the three realms, and yet he still retains the ignorance of habitual tendencies born of not-knowing — hence the verse says "there is ignorance." At the time, some in the assembly said an arhat has this ignorance, and others said he has no ignorance at all; a dispute arose on this account, and the school split into two. Third, "there is doubt": one who has attained the fruit of stream-entry is indeed without doubt regarding the three gates of liberation, and yet he may still have doubt regarding external matters — hence the verse says "there is doubt." Fourth, "one is led across by another": a person of dull faculty who has attained the first fruit does not know by himself that he has attained it; he must ask a good spiritual friend, who then tells him — being without doubt regarding the Three Jewels and the four truths is the mark of the first fruit — and only through this process of self-examination does he come to know he has attained the first fruit; hence this is called "being led across by another." Fifth, "the noble path is manifest through speech": at the very moment one attains the noble path, this attainment may indeed be made manifest through an utterance of speech — as when Śāriputra attained the first fruit at the very moment a verse was being recited aloud; hence the verse says "manifest through speech." The assembly at the time disputed over these five points, some holding them right and others holding them wrong, and it was on this account that the two schools came to be fully formed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Mahādeva's "Five Points" (五事) are one of the most famous episodes in the whole history of Buddhist sectarianism, traditionally credited with precipitating the schism between the Sthavira and Mahāsāṃghika schools — though modern scholarship generally treats this narrative, preserved primarily in Sarvāstivādin sources, as a piece of sectarian polemic against the Mahāsāṃghika rather than neutral history. Jizang reports it here exactly as he received it, including its unflattering account of Mahādeva's own character, without editorial distance; readers should hold the account as this tradition's own origin story for the schism rather than as settled historical fact.

6. The Central Doctrinal Divide, and Three New Schools at Year Two Hundred

Jizang names, at last, the actual doctrinal content dividing Sthavira from Mahāsāṃghika — a disagreement over whether birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are merely provisional names or truly, really existent — and then opens the next wave of schism, a century later, when the Mahāsāṃghika itself splits into three further schools over how far Mahāyāna teaching may be read into the Hīnayāna canon.

Chinese Source

問此二部執何義異耶。答義異乃多。今略明其一。大眾部執生死涅槃皆是假名。上座部執生死涅槃皆是真實。至二百年中。從大眾部又出三部。于時大眾部因摩訶提婆私度住央崛多羅國。此國在王舍城北。此部將華嚴般若等大乘經雜三藏中說之。時人有信者。有不信者。故成二部。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: In what doctrine do these two schools actually differ from one another? Answer: The doctrinal differences are, in fact, numerous; let one be briefly set forth here. The Mahāsāṃghika holds that birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are both merely provisional names, while the Sthavira holds that birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are both truly, really existent. By the two-hundredth year, three further schools emerged out of the Mahāsāṃghika. At that time, the Mahāsāṃghika, through Mahādeva's own private ordaining activity, came to dwell in the country of Aṅkuḍa, located north of Rājagṛha. This group took sūtras such as the Avataṃsaka and the Perfection of Wisdom and expounded them intermixed with the Three Baskets. Some people of the time believed this was legitimate, and others did not — and on this account the group split into two.

不信者唯言阿難等三師所誦三藏此則可信。自三藏外諸大乘經皆不可信。復有信大乘者有三因緣。一者爾時猶有親聞佛說大乘法者。是故可信。二者自思量道理應有大乘。是故可信。三者信其師故。是故可信。

Those who did not believe held that only the Three Baskets as recited by Ānanda and the two other masters could be trusted, and that every Mahāyāna sūtra beyond the Three Baskets could not be trusted at all. Those who did believe in the Mahāyāna, on the other hand, did so for three reasons. First, because at that time there were still those living who had personally heard the Buddha teach the Mahāyāna Dharma — and so it could be trusted. Second, because, reasoning the matter through for themselves, they concluded that there ought, by principle, to exist a Mahāyāna — and so it could be trusted. Third, because they trusted their own teacher — and so it could be trusted.

言三部者。一一說部。此部執生死涅槃皆是假名。故云一說。二出世說部。此部言。世間法從顛倒生業。業生果。故是不實。出世法不從顛倒生。故是真實。三灰山住部。前二從執義受名。此因住處為目(此山有石。堪作灰。此部住彼山中修道。故以為名)。

As for these three schools: the first is the Ekavyavahārika, "the school of a single designation." This school holds that birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are both merely provisional names — hence it is called "a single designation." The second is the Lokottaravāda, "the school of world-transcending teaching." This school holds that worldly dharmas arise from inverted views, which give rise to karma, which in turn gives rise to its fruit — and are therefore not real; whereas world-transcending dharmas do not arise from inverted views, and are therefore truly real. The third is the Kaukkuṭika, "the school dwelling on Ash Mountain." The first two schools took their names from the doctrines they held; this third school, by contrast, takes its name from where it dwelt. (This mountain contains stone suitable for making into ash; this school dwelt on that mountain to practice the path, and so it was named accordingly.)

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The naming pattern Jizang flags here — schools named either for the doctrine they hold or for the place where they dwell — becomes an organizing thread across the whole historical narrative that continues through Part 9 and Part 10 of this collection, where dozens of further sub-schools are traced by exactly this same two-part logic of etymology. Worth flagging early: this passage's claim that "the Mahāsāṃghika holds birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are provisional names, the Sthavira that they are really existent" runs in the very same conceptual channel as this treatise's own central distinction between provisional, empty existence and fixed, self-natured existence — the ancient schism, on Jizang's account, was already fought along a fault line the three treatises are still defending one side of, centuries later.

7. The Ash Mountain School's Own Precept, and Two More Schools by Year Two Hundred

Jizang gives the Kaukkuṭika, "the Ash Mountain school," its own distinctive doctrine — a rule of flexible practice built around a scriptural verse permitting whatever fitting means most swiftly cuts off the afflictions — before recording two further splits from the Mahāsāṃghika within that same two-hundredth year, each associated with a figure of nearly legendary longevity.

Chinese Source

其執毘曇是實教。經律為權說故。彼引經偈云。隨宜覆身。隨宜飲食。隨宜住處。疾斷煩惱。隨宜覆身者。有三衣佛亦許。無三衣佛亦許。隨宜飲食者。時食佛亦許。非時食亦許。隨宜住處者。結界住亦許。不結界亦許。疾斷煩惱者。佛意但令疾斷煩惱。此部甚精進過餘人也。

Ru-Yi Translation

This school holds that the Abhidharma is the real, definitive teaching, while the sūtras and the Vinaya are merely provisional, expedient teaching. They cite a scriptural verse in support, saying: "Cover the body as is fitting; take food as is fitting; dwell as is fitting; swiftly cut off the afflictions." As for "cover the body as is fitting": the Buddha permits the three robes, and he permits going without the three robes as well. As for "take food as is fitting": he permits eating at the proper time, and he permits eating outside the proper time as well. As for "dwell as is fitting": he permits dwelling within a bounded monastic zone, and he permits dwelling without such a zone as well. As for "swiftly cut off the afflictions": the Buddha's own intent was simply that the afflictions be cut off swiftly, by whatever fitting means answers the case. This school is far more diligent in its practice than any of the others.

至二百年中。從大眾部內又出一部。名多聞部。大眾部唯弘淺義棄於深義。佛在世時有仙人。值佛得羅漢。恒隨佛往他方及天上聽法。佛涅槃時其人不見。在雪山坐禪。至佛滅度後二百年中。從雪山出覓諸同行。見大眾部唯弘淺義不知深法。其人具足誦淺深義。深義中有大乘義。成實論即從此部出。時人有信其所說者。故別成一部。名多聞部。

Within the same two-hundredth year, a further school split off from within the Mahāsāṃghika, named the Bahuśrutīya, "the widely learned school." The Mahāsāṃghika, by this point, propagated only the shallow meaning of the teaching and had set aside its profound meaning. Now, while the Buddha was still in the world, there was a sage who, on encountering the Buddha, attained arhatship, and who thereafter constantly followed the Buddha to other regions and to the heavens themselves to listen to the Dharma there. At the time of the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, this person was nowhere to be found, having gone instead to sit in meditation among the Himalayan snow mountains. It was only within the two-hundredth year after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa that he finally emerged from the snow mountains in search of his former fellow practitioners — and on finding that the Mahāsāṃghika now propagated only the shallow meaning and no longer knew the profound teaching, he, being able to recite both the shallow and the profound meaning in full, and with Mahāyāna meaning contained within that profound teaching — it is exactly from this same school that the Satyasiddhi-śāstra later emerged. People of the time who believed what he taught formed, on this account, a separate school, named the Bahuśrutīya.

於二百年中。從大眾部更出一部。名多聞分別部。佛在世時。大迦旃延造論解佛阿含經。至二百年。大迦旃延從阿耨達池出。更分別前多聞部中義。時人有信其所說者。故云多聞分別部。

Also within the two-hundredth year, yet another school split off from the Mahāsāṃghika, named the Prajñaptivāda, "the school of designation." While the Buddha was still in the world, the great Kātyāyana had composed a treatise explaining the Buddha's own Āgama sūtras. By the two-hundredth year, this same great Kātyāyana emerged from Lake Anavatapta and further distinguished the meaning contained within the Bahuśrutīya school just described. People of the time who believed what he taught named this school, accordingly, the Prajñaptivāda.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The claim that Kātyāyana — already named in Part 2 as the Sarvāstivādin author of the Eight Aggregations three centuries after the Buddha, and again in Part 9 as the final figure in the Sthavira's own teacher-to-teacher lineage — should also appear here, at year two hundred, "emerging from Lake Anavatapta" to found the Prajñaptivāda, is chronologically very difficult to reconcile at face value, and is best read as this tradition's own legendary elaboration rather than a claim about one continuously living historical person. Jizang records it here exactly as received, without editorial comment, in keeping with this treatise's general practice of transmitting its source material faithfully even where internal tensions remain unresolved. Note also that this passage is the source of the claim, already anticipated in Part 3's account of Harivarman's own teacher Kumāralāta, that the Satyasiddhi-śāstra's doctrinal roots trace back through the Bahuśrutīya school specifically.

Part Eight Complete

This closes Part Eight: the fourfold interlocking of agent and patient between sūtra and treatise, dissolving finally into "neither sūtra nor treatise, neither Buddha nor bodhisattva"; the root loss and branch loss that explain why treatises get written at all; and the opening chapters of the great schism narrative, from the First Council's peaceable division through Mahādeva's five contested theses to the three-way split within the Mahāsāṃghika at year two hundred, the Ash Mountain school's own precept, and the origins of the Bahuśrutīya and Prajñaptivāda.
