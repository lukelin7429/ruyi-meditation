Nine Proofs That Satyasiddhi Is Hīnayāna

Is the Satyasiddhi-śāstra a Hīnayāna work, a Mahāyāna work, or some blend of the two? Jizang answers with ten proofs that it belongs squarely to the Hīnayāna — a judgment that costs the treatise nothing in respect, since Satyasiddhi is credited throughout with having gone further than the Abhidharma even while falling short of the Mahāyāna it is sometimes mistaken for. This part carries the first nine of the ten proofs, opening with the direct testimony of Sengrui, who sat beside Kumārajīva as the treatise was first rendered into Chinese.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T45, no. 1852, 三論玄義 Jizang (吉藏, 549–623) Reading notes: Ru-Yi, original

1. Naming the Question, and the Method of Proof

Jizang states the three live possibilities and his own answer before a single proof is given: Satyasiddhi has real depth, comparable to fine jade rather than crude stone, which is exactly why the question of its rank cannot be settled by casual impression and needs to be argued out point by point.

Chinese Source

破斥第二。問成實為是小乘之論。為是大乘。為含大小。答有人言。是大乘也。有人言。是小乘。有人言。探大乘意以釋小乘。具含大小。夫珉玉精麁。蓋是耳目所覩。尚有昏明殊鏡。況妙道真偽言亡慮絕。豈易識哉。今以十義證。則明是小乘非大乘矣。一舊序證。二依論徵。三無大文。四有條例。五迷本宗。六分大小。七格優降。八無相即。九傷解行。十檢世人。

Ru-Yi Translation

Refuting it, second. Question: Is the Satyasiddhi-śāstra a Hīnayāna treatise, or a Mahāyāna one, or does it contain elements of both? Answer: Some say it is Mahāyāna; some say it is Hīnayāna; some say it draws on the intent of the Mahāyāna in order to expound the Hīnayāna, and so contains both together. Now, distinguishing fine jade from coarse stone is at least a matter that the eye and the ear can directly perceive, and even so there remain sharply differing verdicts, some clear-sighted and some clouded. How much more so, then, with the wondrous way, where truth and falsehood alike leave speech behind and cut off deliberation — how could this possibly be easy to discern? Here we prove the matter through ten considerations, and thereby make it clear that it is Hīnayāna and not Mahāyāna. First, the testimony of the old preface. Second, verification from the treatise's own text. Third, the absence of any Mahāyāna scripture cited. Fourth, the existence of a governing precedent. Fifth, losing sight of its own root tradition. Sixth, the division between great and small. Seventh, ranking superior against inferior. Eighth, the absence of mutual identity. Ninth, harming both understanding and practice. Tenth, examining what people of the time actually said.

2. Proof One: The Testimony of the Old Preface

The first and, in Jizang's presentation, the most decisive proof is a piece of direct historical testimony: Sengrui, who lectured on the newly translated Satyasiddhi-śāstra at Kumārajīva's own instruction, recorded in his preface both the treatise's own claim about itself and Kumārajīva's blunt assessment of Chinese readers who mistook it for equal to the Mahāyāna.

Chinese Source

舊序證第一。昔羅什法師翻成實論竟。命僧叡講之。什師沒後。叡公錄其遺言。製論序云。成實論者。佛滅度後八百九十年。罽賓小乘學者之匠鳩摩羅陀上足弟子訶梨跋摩之所造也。其論云。色香味觸實也。地水火風假也。精巧有餘。明實不足。推而究之。小乘內之實耳。比於大乘。雖復龍燭之於螢耀。未足喻其懸矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The testimony of the old preface, first. Long ago, when Master Kumārajīva had finished translating the Satyasiddhi-śāstra, he instructed Sengrui to lecture on it. After Kumārajīva passed away, Sengrui recorded his remaining words and composed a preface for the treatise, which says: "The Satyasiddhi-śāstra — within the eight hundred and ninetieth year after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, Harivarman, the foremost disciple of Kumāralāta, master of the Hīnayāna scholars of Kashmir, composed it. Its own text says: form, smell, flavor, and touch are real; earth, water, fire, and wind are provisional. It is more than sufficiently refined and skillful, yet what it makes clear of what is truly real falls short. Investigating it thoroughly, it is simply what is real within the Hīnayāna. Set beside the Mahāyāna, though one might compare a dragon-lantern to a firefly's glow, this would still not suffice to liken the distance between them."

或有人言。此論明於滅諦。與大乘均致。羅什聞而歎曰。秦人之無深識。何乃至此乎。吾每疑其普信大乘者。當知悟不由中。而迷可識矣。成實是羅什所翻。僧叡為講論之始。後學不應孤負前匠。

"There were those who said that this treatise's exposition of the truth of cessation reaches the very same conclusion as the Mahāyāna. On hearing this, Kumārajīva sighed and said: 'That the people of Qin should lack deep discernment — how could it come to this? I have always suspected that anyone who believes so indiscriminately in the Mahāyāna has, by that very fact, an awakening that does not come from the center — and now his confusion may be known for certain.'" The Satyasiddhi-śāstra was translated by Kumārajīva himself, and Sengrui was the very one who began the lecturing on it; later students ought not turn their backs on the master who came before them.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Sengrui (僧叡) here plays exactly the evidentiary role that Sengzhao's preface plays for the Hundred Treatise elsewhere on this site — a direct disciple's eyewitness record of how a text was received at the moment of its translation, treated as the single most authoritative kind of testimony available. Jizang's closing line, "later students ought not turn their backs on the master who came before them," is a pointed remark aimed at Chinese readers of his own era, roughly two centuries after Kumārajīva, who had elevated Satyasiddhi to a status its own translator and first lecturer had explicitly warned against.

3. Proofs Two and Three: The Treatise's Own Words, and the Absence of Mahāyāna Citation

The second proof turns to the Satyasiddhi-śāstra's own stated purpose — Harivarman himself claims only to be correcting the true meaning within the Three Baskets, the Hīnayāna canon, not to be expounding anything beyond it. The third proof is a simple matter of citation practice: treatises that expound the Mahāyāna quote Mahāyāna scripture; the Satyasiddhi-śāstra, in two hundred and two chapters, never does.

Chinese Source

依論徵第二。成實文云。諸比丘異論種種。佛皆聽故。我欲正論三藏內實義。訶梨自云正論三藏。故知成實理是小乘。若言斯論亦明大者。過在門人。非跋摩之咎。問何以知三藏是小乘耶。答法華云。亦不親近小乘三藏學者。恐大照未圓小法容染故。智形宜隔。行止勿共。誡於大士。勿親近小人。則知三藏非大乘矣。智度論云。迦葉阿難結集三藏。文殊彌勒集大乘藏。外人問云。何故不於三藏內集大乘耶。論主答云。小乘不受大。不應小內而集大。以此推之。但是小乘耳。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verification from the treatise's own text, second. The text of the Satyasiddhi-śāstra says: "Since the various monks have propounded all manner of differing theories, and the Buddha allowed for this, I wish to correctly discourse on the true meaning within the Three Baskets." Harivarman himself says that he means to correctly discourse on the Three Baskets — from which it may be known that Satyasiddhi's own doctrine is Hīnayāna. If it is claimed that this treatise also expounds the Mahāyāna, the fault lies with his later followers, not with Harivarman himself. Question: How is it known that the Three Baskets are Hīnayāna? Answer: The Lotus Sūtra says: "Nor should one draw near to those who study the Three Baskets of the Hīnayāna" — for fear that, before the great illumination is yet complete, room might be left for the lesser teaching to seep in and defile it; wisdom and its outward form ought properly to be kept apart, and one's conduct and repose ought not be shared with such people. This warns the great being not to draw near to lesser persons — from which it may be known that the Three Baskets are not the Mahāyāna. The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says: "Kāśyapa and Ānanda compiled the Three Baskets; Mañjuśrī and Maitreya compiled the Mahāyāna canon." An outsider asked: "Why was the Mahāyāna not compiled together within the Three Baskets?" The author of the treatise answered: "The Hīnayāna does not accept the Mahāyāna; it would not be fitting to compile the great together within the small." Reasoning from this, it can only be Hīnayāna.

無大文第三。原夫作論皆引佛言。如龍樹釋大。而還引大經。訶梨解小經。唯將小證。二百二品並探四阿含。十六卷文竟無方等。以此詳之。即可知矣。

The absence of any Mahāyāna scripture cited, third. Tracing the matter to its origin, whenever a treatise is composed it invariably cites the Buddha's own words as proof. So when Nāgārjuna expounds the Mahāyāna, he in turn cites the great sūtras. Harivarman, however, explains the lesser sūtras and draws proof solely from the lesser. His two hundred and two chapters all draw from the four Āgamas, and across sixteen fascicles of text there is, in the end, not a single citation from the broad Mahāyāna canon. Examining the matter in this detail, the answer can be known at once.

4. Proof Four: The Governing Precedent That Small Cannot Contain Great

The fourth proof anticipates an objection: if a disciple's treatise on the small vehicle is not allowed to also expound the great, should not the same restriction apply to the three treatises this school defends, which explicitly discuss both? Jizang's answer draws a structural asymmetry directly from scriptural precedent — the great can include the small, but the small cannot include the great — and applies it to treatises exactly as it already applies to sūtras.

Chinese Source

有條例第四。問若成實釋小不許兼明於大。亦應三論解大不應兼明於小。答義有條例。不應相濫。佛經有二。一者小乘。二者方等。若明大乘必兼辨小。若辨小乘不兼明大。故大乘經初有小乘眾。小乘經首無菩薩僧。示大能包小。小不含大。佛經既爾。在論例然。大乘之論兼明小乘。小乘之論不兼明大。若弟子之論探大釋小。如來之經義亦應然。則巨細互兼。何名大小。

Ru-Yi Translation

The existence of a governing precedent, fourth. Question: If Satyasiddhi's exposition of the small does not allow that it might also, at the same time, be making clear the great, then by the same reasoning the three treatises' exposition of the great ought likewise not to be permitted to also, at the same time, make clear the small. Answer: The principle here follows a governing precedent, and the two cases should not be confused with one another. Buddhist scripture is of two kinds: first, Hīnayāna; second, the broad Mahāyāna canon. Wherever the Mahāyāna is expounded, the Hīnayāna is necessarily discussed alongside it as well; but wherever the Hīnayāna is discussed, the Mahāyāna is not thereby also made clear. This is why Mahāyāna sūtras open with an assembly that includes Hīnayāna disciples, while Hīnayāna sūtras never open with an assembly of bodhisattva-monks — showing that the great is able to encompass the small, while the small does not contain the great. Since this holds for the Buddhist sūtras, the same precedent applies to treatises as well: a Mahāyāna treatise may, at the same time, make clear the Hīnayāna, while a Hīnayāna treatise does not, at the same time, make clear the Mahāyāna. If a disciple's own treatise could draw on the great in order to expound the small, then the Tathāgata's own scriptures ought to follow the very same pattern — and then the vast and the narrow would each include the other indiscriminately, and what, at that point, would even be meant by calling one great and the other small?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This same asymmetry — the great encompasses the small, but the small does not encompass the great — is exactly the principle Jizang will later use, in Part 11 of this collection, to classify the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā itself as a Mahāyāna treatise despite its middle chapters engaging Hīnayāna positions at length — because, on his own account there, the treatise's beginning and end both stay fixed on the Mahāyāna, with the Hīnayāna material folded in along the way rather than standing as the destination.

5. Proof Five: Losing Sight of Its Own Tradition, by the Same Argument the Abhidharma Failed

The fifth proof turns Satyasiddhi's most impressive-sounding claim — that it teaches the twin emptiness of both person and phenomena, not merely the emptiness of the person alone — back into evidence against it, by showing that the very same twin emptiness is already taught within the Hīnayāna Āgamas themselves, including within the Abhidharma tradition Satyasiddhi otherwise surpasses.

Chinese Source

迷本宗第五。問成實論文盛辨生法二空。與大品明四諦平等。義既無異。故知應是探大釋小。答四阿含教內有二空。論明二空。則還釋三藏。云何乃言探大解小。又身子毘曇亦辨二空。而是小非大。訶梨之論義亦應同。問身子毘曇亦探大釋小。與成實。例同。彼既探大。則此非專小。答身子所造還釋佛毘曇。佛說既是小乘。彼論寧言探大。

Ru-Yi Translation

Losing sight of its own root tradition, fifth. Question: The text of the Satyasiddhi-śāstra discusses at length the twin emptiness of person and phenomena, and this shows no difference in meaning from the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra's teaching of the equality of the four truths — from which it may be known that it must be drawing on the great in order to expound the small. Answer: The twin emptiness is already present within the teaching of the four Āgamas; when the treatise expounds this twin emptiness, it is simply expounding the Three Baskets once again — how, then, could this be called drawing on the great to explain the small? Furthermore, Śāriputra's own Abhidharma likewise discusses the twin emptiness, and yet it is Hīnayāna and not Mahāyāna — by this same reasoning, Harivarman's treatise ought to be judged the same way. Question: If Śāriputra's Abhidharma also draws on the great to expound the small, this is exactly the same precedent as Satyasiddhi — and since that text does draw on the great, this one is therefore not exclusively Hīnayāna either. Answer: What Śāriputra composed is, itself, simply an exposition of the Buddha's own Abhidharma. Since what the Buddha himself taught there is Hīnayāna, how could that treatise possibly be said to be drawing on the great?

6. Proof Six: Four Ways the Two Emptinesses Differ Between Great and Small

The sixth proof grants that both Hīnayāna and Mahāyāna teach "twin emptiness," and then draws out, in four precise distinctions, why the same two words do not mean the same thing at both levels — a distinction this treatise treats as decisive for ranking every school it examines.

Chinese Source

分大小第六。問小明一空。大辨二空。可有差別。既同其二空。大小何異。答雖同辨二空。二空不同。略明四種。一者小乘拆法明空。大乘本性空寂。二者小乘但明三界內人法二空。空義即短。大乘明三界內外人法並空。空義即長。三者小乘但明於空未說不空。大乘明空亦辨不空。故涅槃云。聲聞之人但見於空不見不空。智者見空及以不空。空者一切生死。不空者謂大涅槃。四者小乘名為但空。謂但住於空。菩薩名不可得空。空亦不可得也。故知雖明二空。空義有異。故分大小。

Ru-Yi Translation

The division between great and small, sixth. Question: If the small vehicle expounds a single emptiness while the great vehicle discriminates a twin emptiness, a real distinction could be drawn on that basis. But since both alike teach this same twin emptiness, what difference remains between great and small? Answer: Although both discuss a twin emptiness, the two emptinesses are not the same. Let four kinds of difference be briefly set out. First, the Hīnayāna analyzes phenomena apart, piece by piece, in order to arrive at emptiness, while the Mahāyāna holds that phenomena are, in their very own nature, empty and still from the outset. Second, the Hīnayāna makes clear only the twin emptiness of person and phenomena within the three realms, so its sense of emptiness is thereby narrow; the Mahāyāna makes clear that person and phenomena, both within and beyond the three realms, are alike empty, so its sense of emptiness is thereby vast. Third, the Hīnayāna expounds only emptiness and does not speak of the not-empty; the Mahāyāna expounds emptiness and also discriminates the not-empty. Hence the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Persons of the śrāvaka vehicle see only emptiness and do not see the not-empty; the wise see both emptiness and the not-empty. What is empty is all of birth-and-death; what is not empty is called great nirvāṇa." Fourth, the Hīnayāna's emptiness is called mere emptiness, meaning that one simply abides in emptiness; the bodhisattva's emptiness is called the emptiness that cannot be apprehended, meaning that even emptiness itself cannot be apprehended. Hence it is known that although both alike expound a twin emptiness, the sense of that emptiness differs between them — and on this basis great and small are divided.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The fourth distinction here — that Mahāyāna emptiness must itself not be seized as one more fixed thing, "the emptiness that cannot be apprehended, meaning that even emptiness itself cannot be apprehended" — is the same warning against reifying emptiness that runs throughout the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā's own chapter on examining views, and it will resurface as one of this treatise's central charges against the mistaken Mahāyāna teacher refuted in Part 6, who is shown clinging to a notion of "the great vehicle" exactly as if it were itself an ungraspable fixed thing.

7. Proofs Seven and Eight: Ranked by Nāgārjuna's Own Measure, and Lacking Mutual Identity

The seventh proof appeals directly to Nāgārjuna's own ranking of the śrāvaka's emptiness against the bodhisattva's, using his justly famous image of a pore's worth of space set against the space of the ten directions. The eighth turns to the Lotus Sūtra's account of its four great arhats describing their own past complacency, and finds in their self-description the exact fault Satyasiddhi has not overcome: seeing emptiness and existence as two separate things that can never be identical with one another.

Chinese Source

格優降第七。龍樹釋般若累教品云。善吉觀生法二空。欲比菩薩二空。譬如毛孔之空比十方空。即小空為淺。大空為深。成實所明但是聲聞空。非大士所得耳。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ranking superior against inferior, seventh. Nāgārjuna, in his commentary on the chapter on the transmission of the teaching in the Perfection of Wisdom, says: "Should Subhūti's contemplation of the twin emptiness of person and phenomena be compared with the bodhisattva's twin emptiness, it would be like comparing the emptiness within a single pore of the skin to the emptiness of the ten directions." The lesser emptiness is thus shallow, and the greater emptiness is deep. What Satyasiddhi expounds is simply the śrāvaka's own emptiness — it is not what the great being attains.

無相即第八。法華信解品云。四大聲聞自述所得空云。我等長夜修習空法。無生無滅。無小無大。無漏無為。於佛智慧不生貪著。成實所辨與此全同。故知非大也。問何以知然。答法華之文辨聲聞證空不能即空觀有即有觀空。故無相即。成實所說亦無相即。若明相即。應空有並觀。若空有並觀。與大乘何別。問何以知小乘義無相即耶。答釋論云。小乘內不明生死即畢竟空。唯大乘乃說。故知爾也。

The absence of mutual identity, eighth. The chapter on faith and understanding in the Lotus Sūtra records the four great śrāvakas describing, in their own words, the emptiness they had attained: "Throughout the long night, we cultivated the dharma of emptiness — without arising, without ceasing, without small, without great, without outflow, without conditioning — and toward the Buddha's own wisdom we gave rise to no craving or attachment." What Satyasiddhi discusses is entirely the same as this — from which it is known that it is not the Mahāyāna. Question: How is this known to be so? Answer: The text of the Lotus Sūtra makes clear that the śrāvaka's realization of emptiness is unable to see existence while contemplating emptiness itself, or to see emptiness while contemplating existence itself — hence there is no mutual identity between them. What Satyasiddhi teaches likewise has no mutual identity. Were mutual identity to be made clear, emptiness and existence would have to be contemplated together as one; and were emptiness and existence contemplated together as one, how would this differ from the Mahāyāna at all? Question: How is it known that the Hīnayāna's own doctrine lacks this mutual identity? Answer: The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says that within the Hīnayāna it is never made clear that birth-and-death is itself, identically, ultimate emptiness — only the Mahāyāna teaches this. Hence it may be known to be so.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Mutual identity" (相即) — that saṃsāra just is, without remainder, ultimate emptiness, rather than emptiness being something reached only after saṃsāra is left behind — is one of the sharpest technical distinctions this treatise draws anywhere, and it recurs as a live concern in the discussion of the two truths later in this collection. Nāgārjuna's pore-and-ten-directions image, cited in the seventh proof, is worth holding onto: it is not a claim that the śrāvaka's emptiness is false, only that it is real but drastically undersized next to what the same word names in the bodhisattva's realization — exactly the kind of graded ranking, rather than flat rejection, that this treatise has applied to every school so far.

8. Proof Nine: Harming Both Understanding and Practice

The ninth proof presses a specific consequence of Satyasiddhi's account of emptiness: if seeing that giving is ultimately empty of any real giver, gift, or recipient leads someone to conclude there is no such thing as giving at all, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra itself calls this a breach of precept and a wrong view — and Jizang finds exactly this danger already present in how Satyasiddhi frames its own central teaching.

Chinese Source

傷解行第九。涅槃經云。若以聲聞辟支佛心言無布施。是即名為破戒邪見。小乘人入於空觀不見布施。破大乘行。故云破戒。破大乘解。故云邪見。而成實明不見布施是實法空。以為宗極。欲為大乘。勿起小心也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Harming both understanding and practice, ninth. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "If, with the mind of a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, one says that there is no such thing as giving, this is precisely what is called breaking the precepts through wrong view." When a person of the Hīnayāna enters the contemplation of emptiness and thereby fails to see giving as real, this breaks the practice proper to the Mahāyāna, and is therefore called breaking the precepts; it breaks the understanding proper to the Mahāyāna, and is therefore called wrong view. Yet Satyasiddhi makes "not seeing giving," as the emptiness of a truly existing phenomenon, into its own ultimate doctrine. If one wishes to practice as the Mahāyāna does, one ought not give rise to this Hīnayāna cast of mind.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice that this ninth proof is not really a proof about classification at all — it is a warning about consequences, addressed directly to the reader rather than to Satyasiddhi's own text. That shift in register, from "here is evidence this treatise is Hīnayāna" to "here is why it matters practically that you know that," signals that the ten-proof structure is winding toward its close. The tenth and final proof, which opens Part 5, returns to historical testimony one last time — the account of an Indian kṣatriya who traveled to Chang'an specifically to test whether Kumārajīva's Mahāyāna could outshine what he had heard claimed for Harivarman back in India.

Part Four Complete

This closes Part Four: nine of the ten proofs that the Satyasiddhi-śāstra, for all its genuine sophistication and its clear advance over the Abhidharma, remains a Hīnayāna treatise — running from Sengrui's direct testimony and the treatise's own stated purpose, through the asymmetry between great and small, to Nāgārjuna's ranking of the śrāvaka's emptiness and the practical danger of mistaking "no giving" for a Mahāyāna teaching.

Part Five completes the tenth proof with a vivid historical anecdote from Kumārajīva's own translation bureau, closes out the treatise's assessment of Satyasiddhi against the Abhidharma, and then opens the fourth and final sect: a mistaken grasp of the Mahāyāna itself, built around the doctrine of five periods of the Buddha's teaching and a flawed account of the two truths.
