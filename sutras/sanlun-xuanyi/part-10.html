General and Particular Treatises, and How They Are Named

Jizang turns from history to taxonomy: which treatises count as "general," addressing every confusion across the whole tradition, and which are "particular," addressed to one school's error alone — and why the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā counts as a Mahāyāna treatise despite devoting two whole chapters to Hīnayāna material. He then explains the three ways any treatise gets its title, and opens the treatise's account of what guiding doctrine each of the four treatises defended by this school actually takes as its own, beginning with the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom's two kinds of wisdom.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T45, no. 1852, 三論玄義 Jizang (吉藏, 549–623) Reading notes: Ru-Yi, original

1. General Treatises and Particular Treatises

Jizang defines the distinction that will organize the rest of this treatise's account of Buddhist literature: a "general treatise" refutes confusion and reveals teaching across both Hīnayāna and Mahāyāna at once, while a "particular treatise" addresses only one side. He illustrates both categories with concrete examples on each side of the great/small divide, and adds a further layer — the same general/particular distinction applies again within how a single basket of scripture is expounded.

Chinese Source

次明諸部通別義。論有二種。一者通論。二者別論。若通破大小二迷。通申大小兩教。名為通論。即中論是也。故前二十五品破大迷申大教。後兩品破小迷申小教。二者別論。別破大小迷。別申大小教。名為別論。如攝大乘論地持論等。謂大乘通論。十地論智度論等。大乘別論。如成實論等。通申三藏。謂小乘通論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, making clear the sense of general and particular among the various treatises. Treatises are of two kinds: first, general treatises; second, particular treatises. Whatever refutes the confusions of both great and small vehicles together, and sets forth the teachings of both together, is named a general treatise — this describes the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā itself, whose first twenty-five chapters refute the great confusion and set forth the great teaching, and whose last two chapters refute the small confusion and set forth the small teaching. The second kind, particular treatises, refute the confusions of great and small separately, and set forth the teachings of great and small separately. Works such as the Mahāyānasaṃgraha and the Bodhisattvabhūmi-śāstra are Mahāyāna general treatises; works such as the Daśabhūmika-śāstra and the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom are Mahāyāna particular treatises. Works such as the Satyasiddhi-śāstra, which sets forth the Three Baskets as a whole, are Hīnayāna general treatises.

馬鳴菩薩師名脇比丘。造四阿含優婆提舍。別釋修多羅藏。善見毘婆沙別釋毘尼藏。智度論云。八十部律八十部毘婆沙釋之。善見律別釋師子國要。用十誦律。舍利弗別釋佛九分毘曇。如此別釋三藏故。是小乘別論。就三藏中復有通別。若具釋一藏名為通論。別釋一藏中一部名為別論也。

Aśvaghoṣa's own teacher, the monk Pārśva, composed the Upadeśa on the Four Āgamas, a work devoted specifically to explaining the sūtra basket alone. The Samantapāsādikā is devoted specifically to explaining the Vinaya basket alone; the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says that eighty divisions of Vinaya are explained by eighty divisions of Vibhāṣā commentary. The Samantapāsādikā Vinaya is devoted specifically to explaining the essentials transmitted in Sri Lanka, drawing on the Sarvāstivādin Daśabhāṇavāra Vinaya. Śāriputra's own work is devoted specifically to explaining the Buddha's ninefold Abhidharma. Since works of this kind each explain only one of the Three Baskets separately, they are Hīnayāna particular treatises. And within the explanation of the Three Baskets themselves, there is, again, a further general and particular distinction: whatever explains one whole basket in full is named a general treatise; whatever explains only one single school's own version within one basket is named a particular treatise.

問中論既通釋大小。應名大小通論。不得名為大乘論也。答雖釋大小。但為顯大故是大乘論。所以然者。以初分明大乘。中分明小乘。後分還明大乘故。以是義故名大乘論耳。

Question: Since the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā explains both great and small vehicles together, it ought properly to be named a great-and-small general treatise, and should not be called a Mahāyāna treatise at all. Answer: Although it explains both great and small, its purpose throughout is to reveal the great — and this is why it is a Mahāyāna treatise. The reason is this: its opening section makes the Mahāyāna clear; its middle section makes the Hīnayāna clear; and its final section returns once more to making the Mahāyāna clear. On this basis, it is properly named a Mahāyāna treatise.

問十二門論是何論耶。答是大乘通論。以始終破於大迷。通申大教。無破小迷別申於小教故。是大乘通論也。問百論復云何。答百論通破障大小之邪。通申如來大小兩正。故是大小通論。但始終為明大乘故。屬大乘通論耳。

Question: What kind of treatise is the Twelve Gate Treatise? Answer: It is a Mahāyāna general treatise. From beginning to end it refutes the great confusion and sets forth the great teaching as a whole, without ever refuting the small confusion or separately setting forth the small teaching — and so it is a Mahāyāna general treatise. Question: And what of the Hundred Treatise? Answer: The Hundred Treatise refutes as a whole the erroneous views that obstruct both great and small, and sets forth as a whole the correct teaching that the Tathāgata gave for both great and small alike — and so it is a great-and-small general treatise. Yet since its purpose from beginning to end is to make the Mahāyāna clear, it, too, belongs among the Mahāyāna general treatises.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This passage supplies the precise classificatory home for all three treatises already translated in full on this site: the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, the Twelve Gate Treatise, and the Hundred Treatise are all, by this treatise's own classification, Mahāyāna general treatises — differing from one another only in whether they also engage Hīnayāna material along the way (as the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā and Hundred Treatise both do) or stay fixed on Mahāyāna confusion throughout (as the Twelve Gate Treatise does). The governing principle — that a text's overall trajectory, not merely its subject matter, determines its classification — is the same "great encompasses small, small does not encompass great" asymmetry already established in Part 4.

2. Three Ways a Treatise Gets Its Name

Jizang next catalogs how Buddhist treatises acquire their titles in general — from the dharma they expound, from the person who composed or transmitted them, or from a simile — before turning to the four treatises central to this school's own project, all of which are named from the dharma itself, yet each by a different specific angle.

Chinese Source

次明眾論立名不同門。眾論立名凡有三種。一從法為名。如成實論等。實謂四諦之理。成謂能成之文。故云為成是法故造斯論。謂從法立名也。二從人立名。如舍利弗阿毘曇等。智度論云。犢子道人受持此毘曇。亦名犢子毘曇也。三從喻立名。如甘露味毘曇等。亦如訶梨跋摩師鳩摩羅陀造日出論等也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, making clear the different ways the various treatises are named. There are, in all, three ways a treatise is named. First, named from the dharma it expounds — as with the Satyasiddhi-śāstra and the like: "real" (實) refers to the principle of the four truths, and "establishing" (成) refers to the text that does the establishing — hence it is said that the treatise was composed in order to establish this very dharma, which is what is meant by naming it from the dharma. Second, named from a person — as with "Śāriputra's Abhidharma" and the like; the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says that a follower of the Vātsīputrīya upheld and transmitted a certain Abhidharma, which is accordingly also named "the Vātsīputrīya Abhidharma." Third, named from a simile — as with the Amṛtarasa Abhidharma, "the Abhidharma of the Flavor of Sweet Dew," and the like, or as with Harivarman's own teacher Kumāralāta's composition, the Sūryodaya-śāstra, "the Treatise of the Rising Sun," and other such works.

四論立名。並是從法。非人非喻。就中自開四種。大智度論從所釋之經立名。大謂摩訶。智謂般若。度謂波羅蜜。論釋經題。故從所釋為名。中論從理實立名。十二門從言教為目。百論從偈句為稱也。

The naming of the four treatises central to this school, however, is entirely from the dharma in every case — never from a person, and never from a simile — and within this single category, four further distinct approaches are opened up. The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom is named from the very sūtra it explains: "great" translates mahā; "wisdom" translates prajñā; "perfection" translates pāramitā — the treatise explains the sūtra's own title, and so it is named from what it explains. The Mūlamadhyamakakārikā is named from the underlying reality of its principle. The Twelve Gate Treatise is named from its own verbal teaching. The Hundred Treatise is named from its verse count.

若通而為言。四論通顯中道理實。並得就理立名。四論同有言教開通理實。並得以教為稱。同有偈句。通得從偈立名。今欲互相開避。故有四部差別。所以立名不同也。

Speaking of the matter in general terms, however, all four treatises alike reveal the underlying reality of the middle way as their principle, and so all four could equally well be named from their principle. All four alike possess verbal teaching that opens up and reveals this underlying reality, and so all four could equally well be named for their teaching. And all four alike contain verses, and so all four could equally well be named from their verses. It is only because each was meant to open up its own distinct approach, avoiding overlap with the others, that these four separate works came to differ from one another in this way — and this is why their naming turns out to differ as it does.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This closing observation — that all four treatises could, in principle, have been named by any of the four naming-conventions, since all four equally reveal the middle way, teach it in words, and contain verses — is a quietly important methodological point: the differences among the four treatises' titles are not differences in what they ultimately teach, but differences in which facet of one shared teaching each has chosen to foreground in its own name. The same logic will be applied again, at far greater length, when this treatise turns to why there are said to be "three treatises" specifically despite there being four such works in total, later in this collection.

3. What Each Treatise Actually Teaches: Opening with the Two Wisdoms

Jizang now opens the treatise's account of each of the four treatises' own individual "guiding doctrine" (宗) — the single thread each one takes as central. All four, he begins, share unattainable correct contemplation as their common aim, but differ in emphasis according to the specific confusion each was written to correct; the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom is taken up first, with its own guiding doctrine identified as the two kinds of wisdom — real wisdom and skillful wisdom — mirroring the two truths that will be shown, in the part following, to guide the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā itself.

Chinese Source

次明眾論旨歸門。通論大小乘經。同明一道。故以無得正觀為宗。但小乘教者正觀猶遠。故就四諦教為宗。大乘正明正觀。故諸大乘經同以不二正觀為宗。但約方便用異故有諸部差別。如明應說不應說。今昔開會名為法華。破斥八倒辨常無常用名為涅槃。至論不二正道更無別異。在經既爾。在論亦然。雖諸部有異。同用不二正觀為宗。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, making clear the guiding doctrine of the various treatises. Speaking generally of both Hīnayāna and Mahāyāna sūtras together, they alike make clear one single way, and so they share unattainable correct contemplation as their common guiding doctrine. It is only that, within the Hīnayāna teaching, correct contemplation still lies at a great remove, and so it takes the teaching of the four truths as its own guiding doctrine instead; the Mahāyāna, by contrast, directly makes correct contemplation clear, and so the various Mahāyāna sūtras alike take non-dual correct contemplation as their common guiding doctrine. It is only with respect to differing skillful applications that the various schools come to differ among themselves — as when clarifying what should and should not be taught, and reconciling the present teaching with the past, this is named the Lotus Sūtra's own approach; or when refuting the eightfold inversion and discriminating the functions of permanence and impermanence, this is named the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own approach. Yet when it comes to the non-dual correct way itself, there is, in the end, no difference between them whatsoever. As it stands among the sūtras, so too among the treatises: though the various schools differ from one another, they alike make use of non-dual correct contemplation as their shared guiding doctrine.

又經論同宗。佛說正觀為經。論申正觀為論。經論用異正觀無別。故無量義經云。如水洗穢義同。約井池為異。自昔及今。一切諸教。同治斷常之病。同開正道。但約今昔教用異耳。今四論約用不同故辨四宗差別。

Moreover, sūtra and treatise share this same guiding doctrine in common: the Buddha's own speaking of correct contemplation constitutes sūtra; a treatise-author's setting forth of correct contemplation constitutes treatise. Sūtra and treatise differ only in their function, while correct contemplation itself admits of no difference between them. Hence the Sūtra of Immeasurable Meaning says: "It is like water washing away filth — the meaning is the same, though it differs according to whether one draws from a well or from a pond." From ancient times down to the present, every single teaching alike remedies the sickness of annihilationism and eternalism, and alike opens up the correct way; they differ only according to the differing function of present teaching against past teaching. Now, since the four treatises differ from one another in their respective function, this treatise will accordingly discriminate the differing guiding doctrine proper to each of these four schools of thought.

智度論正釋大品。而龍樹開大品為二道。前明般若道。次明方便道。此之二道即是法身父母。故大品以實慧方便慧為宗。論申經二慧。還以二慧為宗。如中論申二諦。還以二諦為宗也。

The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom is devoted directly to explaining the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra, and Nāgārjuna there opens up the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra into two paths: the first makes clear the path of prajñā; the second makes clear the path of skillful means. These two paths are precisely the father and mother of the Dharma-body. Hence the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra takes real wisdom and skillful wisdom as its own guiding doctrine, and the treatise, in setting forth the sūtra's own two kinds of wisdom, in turn takes these same two kinds of wisdom as its own guiding doctrine as well — exactly as the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, in setting forth the two truths, in turn takes the two truths themselves as its own guiding doctrine.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the treatise's own explicit statement of the principle that governs everything from here through the rest of this part and into Part 11: a commentary's own guiding doctrine simply mirrors the guiding doctrine of the sūtra it explains. Because prajñā and skillful means are called here "the father and mother of the Dharma-body," this passage effectively places the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom's two wisdoms in the same structural role that the two truths will occupy for the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā — two accounts of the very same underlying insight, approached through two different texts.

4. Why Prajñā Is Taught Before Skillful Means

A natural question follows: if prajñā and skillful means are equally fundamental, why does the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra always teach prajñā first? Jizang answers with three separate and complementary explanations, before closing this part by distinguishing his own reading of the two wisdoms from an older interpretation he considers too narrow.

Chinese Source

問大品何故前明般若。後明方便耶。答般若方便實無前後。而作前後說者般若為體。方便為用。故智度論云。譬如金為體。金上精巧為用。故前明其體。後辨其用也。又非凡夫行。非賢聖行。是菩薩行。般若超凡。方便越聖。要前超凡後方越聖。故前明般若後辨方便。又眾生起見凡有二種。一者有見。二者無見。般若破其有見。方便斥其無見。故前明般若後辨方便。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Why does the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra make prajñā clear first, and skillful means clear only afterward? Answer: In reality, prajñā and skillful means have no such sequence of before and after at all; the reason they are spoken of in this order is that prajñā serves as the essence, and skillful means serves as the function. Hence the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says: "It is like gold serving as the essence, and fine workmanship upon the gold serving as the function" — this is why the essence is made clear first, and the function discriminated only afterward. Moreover, it is said that bodhisattva conduct is neither the conduct of the ordinary unenlightened person nor the conduct of the noble sage: prajñā transcends the ordinary person, while skillful means goes beyond even the sage — and one must first transcend the ordinary before one can go on to surpass the sage, which is why prajñā is made clear first and skillful means discriminated only afterward. Moreover, the views that arise among sentient beings are, in all, of two kinds: first, the view of existence; second, the view of non-existence. Prajñā breaks their view of existence; skillful means rebukes their view of non-existence — and this too is why prajñā is made clear first and skillful means discriminated only afterward.

若明次第者。三藏多說有教以破外道。而封執三藏之有故。般若次說空。惑者著般若之空故。次說方便令其離空。故智度論序云。知邪病之自起故。阿含為之作。以滯有之為患故。般若為之照。即斯意也。若約位而言。般若配於六地。故前明之。方便在於七地。故後說也。

As for accounting for this ordering directly: the Three Baskets speak mostly of the teaching of existence, in order to refute the outsiders — yet since people then become sealed within, and cling to, this very existence taught in the Three Baskets, the Perfection of Wisdom next teaches emptiness. And since those who are confused then become attached to this very emptiness taught in the Perfection of Wisdom, skillful means is taught next, in order to free them from this emptiness as well. Hence the preface to the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says: "Knowing that heterodox sickness arises of itself, the Āgamas were composed on that account; taking the affliction of being mired in existence as the concern, the Perfection of Wisdom was composed to illuminate it" — this is precisely the sense intended here. Speaking in terms of the bodhisattva stages, prajñā is assigned to the sixth ground, and so is made clear first; skillful means belongs to the seventh ground, and so is taught only afterward.

問舊亦明大品二慧為宗。與今何異。答今明。聖心未曾二。為眾生故無二說二。欲令因二悟於不二。故與舊不同。又雖明二慧。與舊亦異。舊義實慧但照空不達有。漚和但照有不達空。蓋是限局聖心便成二見。今明。至人體無礙之道故有無礙之用。般若既照空即能鑒有。

Question: The old interpretation likewise held that the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra takes the two kinds of wisdom as its guiding doctrine — how does the present account differ from it? Answer: The present account makes clear that the sage's own mind has never, in truth, actually been dual; it is solely for the sake of sentient beings that, without there being any real duality, two are spoken of at all — precisely so as to lead beings, by means of these two, to awaken to the non-dual. This is why the present account differs from the old. Moreover, although both alike speak of two kinds of wisdom, the present account differs from the old one in this respect as well: the old doctrine held that real wisdom illuminates emptiness alone and does not reach existence, while skillful means illuminates existence alone and does not reach emptiness — which simply confines and limits the sage's own mind, and ends up producing two separate views. The present account makes clear that the consummate person's own way is itself unobstructed, and therefore has an unobstructed function as well: prajñā, having already illuminated emptiness, is thereby also able to perceive existence in turn.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The three explanations offered above — essence before function, transcending the ordinary before surpassing the sage, and breaking the view of existence before rebuking the view of non-existence — are not competing theories but three complementary angles on the same underlying claim, consistent with this treatise's general habit, seen already in its reconciliation of variant historical counts across Part 8 and Part 9, of holding multiple true explanations together rather than forcing a single winner. The closing comparison between the "old" and "present" readings of the two wisdoms — a limited, one-sided pairing giving way to an unobstructed wisdom that illuminates both emptiness and existence at once — anticipates, in miniature, the same treatment Part 11 gives in full to the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā's own guiding doctrine of the two truths.
