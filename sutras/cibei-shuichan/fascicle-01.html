Fascicle 1

Refuge, the sevenfold mind, and the confession of affliction《慈悲水懺法》卷上

The liturgy opens with refuge in the Three Jewels, then sets out the seven attitudes of mind — shame, dread, revulsion, and more — that it argues must be cultivated before confession can mean anything at all, before turning to the first of the three obstructions: the mental poisons of greed, hatred, and delusion, confessed here through an exhaustive numerical accounting running from three categories of fault up to a hundred and eight.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1910 Obstruction 1 of 3: Affliction (煩惱障) Status: complete
Chinese
《慈悲水懺法》卷上 — the first of three fascicles of the Water Repentance.
Covers
Refuge in the Three Jewels; the sevenfold mind that prepares a practitioner for confession; and the full confession of the first of the three obstructions, the obstruction of affliction (煩惱障) — the karma of mind itself, greed, hatred, and delusion, enumerated through an ascending numerical framework.
Genre note
Continuous liturgical prose meant to be recited, not narrative. This translation divides it into four numbered sections for readability; the original carries no internal headings.
A recurring formula
A sixteen-name roll call of Buddhas and bodhisattvas closes each major unit of confession in this fascicle. It is translated in full every time it appears. "We disciples" translates the ritual placeholder 某甲 ("so-and-so"), spoken by whoever is performing the rite in place of their own name.
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — the fourth section in particular works through a long climbing sequence of Abhidharma-style numerical categories (three poisons, four floods, five hindrances, and onward to the hundred and eight afflictions) that reads more like a doctrinal reference list than continuous argument.

Opening the Rite

Every session of this liturgy begins the same way: a single line of homage to the Buddhas of the three times, followed by the seven Buddhas of remotest antiquity, before the confession proper begins.

1. Refuge in the Three Jewels歸依三寶

Chinese Source

一切諸佛愍念眾生，為說水懺道場總法。佛言：眾生垢重，何人無罪？何者無愆？凡夫愚行無明闇覆，親近惡友煩惱亂心，立性無知恣心自恃，不信十方諸佛、不信尊法聖僧，不孝父母六親眷屬，盛年放逸以自憍𭝟，貪一切財寶、貪一切歌樂、貪一切女色，心生貪戀意起煩惱，親近非聖媟狎惡友，不知懺悔。或殺害一切眾生、或飲酒昏迷，無智慧心，今日披誠一一懺悔。過去諸罪、現作眾惡，今日志誠悉皆懺悔，未作之罪不敢更作。弟子某甲等，今日志心歸依十方盡虛空界一切諸佛、諸大菩薩、辟支羅漢四果四向、梵王帝釋、天龍八部一切聖眾，願垂證鑒。 南無毘盧遮那佛　南無本師釋迦牟尼佛　南無阿彌陀佛　南無彌勒佛　南無龍種上尊王佛　南無龍自在王佛　南無寶勝佛　南無覺華定自在王佛　南無袈裟幢佛　南無師子吼佛　南無文殊師利菩薩　南無普賢菩薩　南無大勢至菩薩　南無地藏菩薩　南無大莊嚴菩薩　南無觀自在菩薩 禮諸佛已，次復懺悔。夫欲禮懺，必須先敬三寶。所以然者？三寶即是一切眾生良友福田，若能歸向者則滅無量罪、長無量福，能令行者離生死苦、得解脫樂。是故弟子某甲等，歸依十方盡虛空界一切諸佛　歸依十方盡虛空界一切尊法　歸依十方盡虛空界一切聖僧

Ru-Yi Translation

All the Buddhas, mindful of living beings with compassion, have spoken this comprehensive rite of the Water Repentance. The Buddha said: living beings are heavy with defilement — who among them is without fault? Who is without transgression? The ordinary person acts foolishly, covered in the darkness of ignorance; drawing close to bad companions, their afflictions throw the mind into confusion; fixed in their nature and without understanding, they indulge the mind and rely only on themselves. They have no faith in the Buddhas of the ten directions, no faith in the venerable Dharma or the noble Saṅgha; they are unfilial to their parents and their kin. In the prime of life they grow reckless and arrogant, greedy for every kind of wealth, greedy for every kind of song and music, greedy for the beauty of every woman; desire and attachment arise in the mind, afflictions stir, and they draw close to unworthy and dissolute companions, not knowing how to repent. Some kill and harm every kind of living being; some grow stupefied with wine, lacking any wisdom of mind. Today we bare our sincerity and confess these, one by one. Every fault of the past, every evil act committed now — today, with utmost sincerity, we confess them all, and we dare not commit again what we have not yet committed. We disciples, today with sincere mind, take refuge in all the Buddhas throughout the ten directions and the whole reach of empty space, in all the great bodhisattvas, in the pratyekabuddhas and arhats of the four fruits and four approaches, in Brahmā and Śakra, and in all the noble assembly of the eightfold host of gods and dragons — may they bear witness and look upon us. Homage to the Buddha Vairocana. Homage to our fundamental teacher, the Buddha Śākyamuni. Homage to the Buddha Amitābha. Homage to the Buddha Maitreya. Homage to the Buddha King of the Dragon Race, Most Honored. Homage to the Buddha King of Sovereign Dragons. Homage to the Buddha Ratnaśrī. Homage to the Buddha King Ever Still in the Samādhi of the Awakening-Flower. Homage to the Buddha Banner of the Kāṣāya Robe. Homage to the Buddha Lion's Roar. Homage to the Bodhisattva Mañjuśrī. Homage to the Bodhisattva Samantabhadra. Homage to the Bodhisattva Mahāsthāmaprāpta. Homage to the Bodhisattva Kṣitigarbha. Homage to the Bodhisattva of Great Adornment. Homage to the Bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara. Having paid homage to the Buddhas, we next continue with confession. Whoever wishes to bow in repentance must first revere the Three Jewels. Why is this so? The Three Jewels are the good friend and field of merit for every living being; whoever can turn and take refuge in them will extinguish immeasurable faults and grow immeasurable merit, and can bring the practitioner out of the suffering of birth and death into the joy of liberation. For this reason, we disciples take refuge in all the Buddhas throughout the ten directions and the whole reach of empty space; we take refuge in all the venerable Dharma throughout the ten directions and the whole reach of empty space; we take refuge in all the noble Saṅgha throughout the ten directions and the whole reach of empty space.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The sixteen-name roll call that appears here — opening with Vairocana and Śākyamuni and closing with Avalokiteśvara — recurs verbatim more than a dozen times across all three fascicles of this liturgy, always marking the boundary between one completed act of confession and the next. It is not a one-time invocation but a structural device, functioning the way a refrain functions in a hymn: a fixed point a chanting assembly returns to again and again. This translation gives the full list every time it occurs rather than shortening it after its first appearance, since the repetition itself is part of how the rite paces a practitioner through hours of sustained confession.

2. The Sevenfold Mind七種心

Chinese Source

弟子今日所以懺悔者，正言無始以來在凡夫地，莫問貴賤罪自無量，或因三業而生罪、或從六根而起過、或以內心自邪思惟、或藉外境起於染著，如是乃至十惡，增長八萬四千諸塵勞門。然其罪相雖復無量，大而為悟不出有三：一者煩惱、二者是業、三者是果報。此三種法能障聖道及以人天勝妙好事，是故經中目為三障，所以諸佛菩薩教作方便，懺悔除滅此三障者，則六根十惡乃至八萬四千諸塵勞門皆悉清淨。是故弟子某甲等，今日運此增上勝心懺悔三障。欲滅三障者，當用何等心可令此障滅除？先當興七種心以為方便，然後此障乃可得滅。何等為七？一者慚愧、二者恐怖、三者厭離、四者發菩提心、五者怨親平等、六者念報佛恩、七者觀罪性空。第一慚愧者，自惟我與釋迦如來同為凡夫，而今世尊成道以來已經爾所塵沙劫數，而我等相與耽染六塵輪轉生死永無出期，此實天下可慚可愧可羞可恥。第二恐怖者，既是凡夫身口意業常與罪相應，以是因緣命終之後，應墮地獄、畜生、餓鬼受無量苦，如此實為可驚可恐可怖可懼。第三厭離者，相與常觀生死之中，唯有無常苦空無我，不淨虛假如水上泡速起速滅，往來流轉猶如車輪，生老病死八苦交煎無時暫息。眾等相與但觀自身，從頭至足其中但有三十六物，髮毛爪齒、眵淚涕唾、垢汗二便、皮膚血肉、筋脈骨髓、肪膏腦膜、脾腎心肺、肝膽腸胃、赤白痰癊、生熟二藏，如是九孔常流。是故經言：此身眾苦所集，一切皆是不淨。何有智慧者而當樂此身？生死既有如此種種惡法，甚可患厭。第四發菩提心者，經言：當樂佛身。佛身者即法身也。從無量功德智慧生、從六波羅蜜生、從慈悲喜捨生、從三十七助菩提法生、從如是等種種功德智慧生如來身。欲得此身者，當發菩提心求一切種智常樂我淨薩婆若果，淨佛國土成就眾生，於身命財無所悋惜。第五怨親平等者，於一切眾生起慈悲心無彼我相。何以故爾？若見怨異於親即是分別，以分別故起諸相著，相著因緣生諸煩惱，煩惱因緣造諸惡業，惡業因緣故得苦果。第六念報佛恩者，如來往昔無量劫中，捨頭目髓腦支節手足、國城妻子、象馬七珍，為我等故修諸苦行。此恩此德實難酬報，是故經言：若以頂戴兩肩荷負，於恒沙劫亦不能報。我等欲報如是恩者，當於此世勇猛精進，捍勞忍苦不惜身命，建立三寶弘通大乘，廣化眾生同入正覺。第七觀罪性空者，罪無自性從因緣生，顛倒而有。既從因緣而生，則可從因緣而滅。從因緣而生者，狎近惡友造作無端。從因緣而滅者，即是今日洗心懺悔。是故經言：此罪性不在內、不在外、不在中間。故知此罪從本是空。生如是等七種心已，緣想十方諸佛賢聖，擎拳合掌，披陳至到慚愧改革，舒歷心肝洗蕩腸胃。如此懺悔，亦何罪而不滅，亦何福而不生。若復不爾，悠悠緩縱情慮躁動，徒自勞形於事何益？且復人命無常喻如轉燭，一息不還便同灰壤。三塗苦報即身膺受，不可以錢財寶貨囑託求脫，杳杳冥冥恩赦無期，獨嬰此苦無代受者。莫言我今生中無有此罪，所以不能懇禱懺悔。經中謂言：凡夫之人，舉足動步無非是罪。又復過去生中皆悉成就無量惡業，追逐行者如影隨形，若不懺悔罪惡日深。故知包藏瑕疵，佛不許可；說悔先罪，淨名所尚。故使長淪苦海，實由隱覆。是故弟子某甲，今日發露懺悔不復覆藏。

Ru-Yi Translation

The reason we disciples confess today is precisely this: since beginningless time, dwelling in the stage of the ordinary person, our faults — noble or humble makes no difference — have been without measure, whether arising from the three kinds of karma, or springing from the six sense-faculties, or born of the mind's own distorted thinking, or provoked by attachment to external conditions — extending in this way as far as the ten evils, and multiplying into the eighty-four thousand gates of worldly affliction. Yet although the marks of these faults are themselves without measure, in their broad outline they come down to no more than three: first, affliction; second, karma; and third, retribution. These three things are able to obstruct the noble path and every excellent, wondrous good in the human and heavenly realms, and for this reason the sūtras name them the three obstructions. This is why the Buddhas and bodhisattvas teach the skillful means of confession — for whoever confesses and eliminates these three obstructions, the six sense-faculties, the ten evils, and even the eighty-four thousand gates of worldly affliction are all made pure. For this reason, we disciples today set this exalted and excellent mind in motion to confess the three obstructions. If one wishes to eliminate the three obstructions, what state of mind must be used to make this elimination possible? One must first arouse a sevenfold mind as the skillful means, and only then can this obstruction be eliminated. What are the seven? First, shame; second, dread; third, revulsion; fourth, the arising of the awakening-mind; fifth, impartiality toward friend and enemy alike; sixth, remembering to repay the Buddha's kindness; and seventh, contemplating that the nature of fault is empty. First, shame: reflect that we and the Tathāgata Śākyamuni were once alike, both ordinary beings — and yet since the World-Honored One attained the Way, so many kalpas as numerous as motes of dust have already passed, while we ourselves remain steeped in attachment to the six sense-objects, turning through birth and death with never an end in sight. This is truly, in all the world, a thing to be ashamed of, a thing to feel shame and disgrace over. Second, dread: being ordinary beings, our deeds of body, speech, and mind are constantly bound up with fault, and because of this, when our lives come to an end, we are bound to fall into the hells, or among the animals, or among the hungry ghosts, to undergo immeasurable suffering. This is truly a thing to be alarmed at, a thing to fear and dread. Third, revulsion: let us together constantly contemplate that within birth and death there is only impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self — impure and unreal, like a bubble on the surface of water, arising and vanishing in an instant, coming and going in a cycle like a turning wheel, with the eight sufferings of birth, old age, sickness, and death pressing in together without a moment's rest. Let us together simply contemplate our own bodies: from head to foot there are only thirty-six substances within them — hair, body-hair, nails, and teeth; rheum, tears, mucus, and spittle; grime, sweat, and the two excretions; skin, flesh, and blood; sinews, veins, bones, and marrow; fat, grease, brain, and membrane; spleen, kidneys, heart, and lungs; liver, gall, intestines, and stomach; red and white phlegm; the raw and the ripe storehouses of digestion — flowing constantly from the nine orifices in this way. This is why the sūtra says: this body is a heap of many sufferings, and all of it is impure. What person of wisdom could take pleasure in this body? Since birth and death contain so many kinds of evil in this way, they are truly worth being weary of and turning away from. Fourth, the arising of the awakening-mind: the sūtra says, one should delight in the Buddha's body. The Buddha's body is precisely the Dharma-body — born from immeasurable merit and wisdom, born from the six perfections, born from loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, born from the thirty-seven factors that aid awakening, born, in this way, from every kind of merit and wisdom, this body of the Tathāgata comes to be. Whoever wishes to attain this body should arouse the awakening-mind, seeking the fruit of omniscience — all-knowing wisdom, eternal, blissful, true self, and pure — purifying buddha-lands and bringing living beings to fulfillment, begrudging nothing of body, life, or possessions. Fifth, impartiality toward friend and enemy: arouse a mind of loving-kindness and compassion toward all living beings, without the notion of "other" or "self." Why is this so? To regard an enemy as different from a friend is itself discrimination; because of this discrimination, various attachments to appearances arise; because of the attachment to appearances, various afflictions arise; because of the afflictions, various evil karma is created; and because of that evil karma, a bitter fruit is obtained. Sixth, remembering to repay the Buddha's kindness: throughout immeasurable kalpas in the past, the Tathāgata gave up his head, eyes, marrow, and brain, his limbs, hands, and feet, his kingdoms, cities, wife and children, his elephants, horses, and the seven treasures, and practiced every manner of austerity for our sake. This kindness and this virtue are truly hard to repay — the sūtra says that even if one carried him on the crown of one's head or bore him on both shoulders for kalpas as numerous as the sands of the river, one still could not repay it. If we wish to repay such kindness, we should, in this present life, advance with courage and diligence, enduring hardship and bearing suffering without begrudging body or life, establishing the Three Jewels and spreading the Great Vehicle widely, broadly transforming living beings so that they enter true awakening together. Seventh, contemplating that the nature of fault is empty: fault has no nature of its own — it arises from causes and conditions, existing only through inverted thinking. Since it arises from causes and conditions, it can also be extinguished through causes and conditions. What arises from causes and conditions is this: drawing close to bad companions and committing acts without end. What is extinguished through causes and conditions is this: washing the mind clean through confession, here and today. This is why the sūtra says: the nature of this fault is not within, not without, and not in between. Hence we know that this fault, from its very root, is empty. Having aroused these seven kinds of mind, we turn our thoughts toward the Buddhas and sages of the ten directions, raise our clasped hands together, and lay bare our utmost shame, resolving to change — opening wide our very heart and liver, washing clean our very entrails. Confessing in this way, what fault could fail to be extinguished, and what merit could fail to be born? But if instead we drift along carelessly, indulging our thoughts, restless and agitated, what benefit is there in merely exhausting the body over such things? Moreover, human life is impermanent, like a candle guttering in the wind — let one breath fail to return, and the body is at once the same as ash and dust. The bitter retribution of the three evil destinies is received by this very body, and it cannot be bought off with money, treasure, or bribery; in that dim and hidden place there is no fixed term for a pardon, and one alone must bear that suffering, with no one to bear it in one's place. Do not say: in this present life I have no such fault, and so I need not earnestly implore and confess. The sūtra says: for the ordinary person, there is not a single step taken, not a single movement made, that is without fault. And moreover, in past lives we have each already accomplished immeasurable evil karma, which pursues the one who bears it as a shadow follows a form — and if one does not confess it, the fault only deepens day by day. Hence we know that to conceal one's own flaws is something the Buddha does not permit; to speak of repenting one's past faults is what Vimalakīrti himself esteemed. That we should sink for so long in the sea of suffering is, in truth, entirely because we have kept them hidden. For this reason, we disciples today lay bare our faults in confession, and conceal them no longer.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The seven attitudes set out here are worth reading as a deliberate psychological sequence rather than a list of synonyms for remorse. Shame and dread are aversive — they make the practitioner unwilling to continue as before. Revulsion, built on the standard meditation on the body's thirty-six impure substances, undercuts the attachment that fuels wrongdoing in the first place. But the sequence does not stop at aversion: the arising of the awakening-mind and impartiality toward friend and enemy alike turn the practitioner outward, toward the good they are moving into rather than only the fault they are moving away from, before gratitude toward the Buddha and the contemplation of fault's emptiness close the sequence on doctrinal ground. A repentance built only on the first three of these seven would risk collapsing into scrupulous anxiety; the text's own architecture guards against that by insisting the mind move all the way through to emptiness before confession proper begins.

3. Confessing the Obstruction of Affliction懺悔煩惱障

Chinese Source

As for what is called the three obstructions: the first is named affliction, the second is named karma, and the third is retribution. These three things depend upon one another in turn — because of affliction, various evil karma arises; because of that evil karma, a bitter fruit is obtained. Why is this so? Because it is the karma of mind that arises first, and the body and speech move in accordance with it afterward. The karma of mind is threefold: first, stinginess and greed; second, hatred and anger; third, delusion and darkness. Because of delusion and darkness, various wrong views arise, and various unwholesome deeds are committed. Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The climbing numerical list in this section — three poisons, four floods, five hindrances, and onward through the categories up to the hundred and eight afflictions — is characteristic of Abhidharma-influenced Chinese Buddhist literature, where doctrine is organized into fixed numbered sets as a memory and teaching device rather than argued through in prose. Some of the individual terms here (the "four floods," the "eleven pervasive latent tendencies," the specific inventory behind "twenty-five forms of self") come from technical scholastic distinctions that would need a dedicated glossary to unpack fully; this translation renders each one as literally and consistently as English allows rather than either expanding each into an explanatory paraphrase or collapsing the list with "and so on," since the text's own rhetorical strategy is precisely to make the practitioner feel the sheer, exhausting comprehensiveness of what is being confessed.

4. The Nature of True Repentance, and Confessing the Karma of Mind論懺悔、懺意業

Ru-Yi Translation

Having paid homage to the Buddhas, we next continue with confession. As for confession itself: at its root it is simply this — to correct what is past and cultivate what is to come, to extinguish evil and raise up good. Living in this world, who among us can be without fault? Even a learner in training, losing mindfulness for a moment, still gives rise to affliction; even an arhat, through the lingering habits of past afflictions, still stirs deeds of body and speech — how much more, then, should an ordinary person expect to be without fault? Yet the wise, awakening to this early, are able to correct and repent, while the foolish conceal their faults and so let them spread and grow, so that the habits accumulate through a long night with no time of dawning ever in sight. If one can confess in shame and lay one's faults bare, this is not only enough to extinguish fault — it will also make immeasurable merit grow, and set up the wondrous fruit of the Tathāgata's nirvāṇa. Whoever wishes to practice this rite should first, outwardly, make solemn one's bearing and gaze reverently upon the sacred image, and inwardly arouse a reverent mind directed toward the Dharma, earnestly and sincerely giving rise to two kinds of thought. What are these two? First, reflect to oneself: this body and life of mine are hard to keep for long — once they scatter and are destroyed in a single morning, I do not know when this body may be regained. And if I should then fail to encounter the Buddhas and sages, and instead suddenly meet with bad companions and commit every kind of evil karma, I would again fall into a deep pit, into a perilous destiny. Second, reflect to oneself: although in this life I have been fortunate enough to encounter the Tathāgata's true Dharma, I have not, for the sake of the Buddha-dharma, continued the lineage of the sages, nor kept myself dwelling in wholesome ways with body, speech, and mind purified. And yet now we secretly commit evil and then conceal it, saying that others do not know, supposing that they do not see — hiding our wrongdoing in the heart, arrogant and without any shame. This is truly, in all the world, the very height of foolish confusion. For even now, the Buddhas of the ten directions, the great bodhisattvas, and all the gods and spirits — when have they ever failed to see, with their pure heavenly eye, every evil deed that we have done? And moreover, the hidden and manifest numinous spirits record fault and merit without the smallest discrepancy. Consider, then, the one who has committed a fault: when his life comes to an end, the ox-headed jailers of hell will seize his spirit, and bring it before King Yama's tribunal to be examined and judged, right and wrong. At that time, every wronged party will come forward as witness, each one saying: "You once slaughtered my body, roasted, boiled, steamed, and broiled it," or "you once stripped away all my wealth and separated me from my kin — today at last I have gotten hold of you." At the moment such evidence stands before him, how could he dare deny it? He can only resign himself and accept his share of that long-standing calamity. As the sūtra makes clear, within the hells no one is punished unjustly. And even if a person has, in ordinary life, forgotten in his own mind the many faults he has committed, then when his life is about to end, every scene and mark of the place where the evil was done will appear before him all at once, each one saying: "Long ago, beside me, you committed such-and-such a fault — how can you deny it now?" At that time, the one who committed the fault has nowhere left to hide. Thereupon King Yama, gnashing his teeth in reproach, hands him over to the hells, from which he seeks release through immeasurable kalpas and finds no way out. This matter is not far off, and it concerns no one else — it is precisely one's own self, acting and receiving the consequence oneself. Even between father and son, the closest of kin, once this reckoning arrives, there is no one to bear it in another's place. We who together have obtained this human body, our bodies free of every affliction, should each strive with all our strength while we still contend for our very life — for once the great terror arrives, regret will come too late. For this reason, with utmost sincerity, we beg for mercy in confession. We disciples, from beginningless time until this very day, have accumulated ignorance that has obstructed and darkened the eye of the mind, and, following the nature of affliction, have committed faults across the three periods of time — whether through the affliction of craving and greed, born of indulgent attachment; or the affliction of hatred and rage, harboring the intent to harm; or the affliction of a mind confused and befuddled, unable to understand; or the affliction of pride and arrogance, exalting oneself and despising others; or the affliction of hesitation, doubting the true path; or the affliction of wrong view, denying cause and effect; or the affliction of clinging to a self, not recognizing conditioned things as provisional; or the affliction of clinging to annihilationism or eternalism, confused about the three periods of time; or the affliction of the view of grasping at views, arising from consorting with evil teachings; or the affliction of the view of grasping at wrong precepts and observances, arising from wrongly following a false teacher — extending even to every kind of affliction born of the four false attachments and misguided reckoning. Today, with utmost sincerity, we confess all of these. Furthermore, from beginningless time until this very day, we have given rise to the affliction of miserliness, clinging tightly and holding fast; the affliction of dissipation, failing to restrain the six faculties; the affliction of cruelty, a mind and conduct too wicked to bear; the affliction of idleness, slack and unrestrained, without diligence; the affliction of restless deliberation, doubt-ridden and agitated; the affliction of ignorance, confused whenever meeting any object; the affliction of self-and-other, following after the eight worldly winds; the affliction of flattery, praising to the face without an upright heart; the affliction of obstinacy, harsh and unyielding, refusing to be reconciled; the affliction of easy anger and hard-won pleasure, quick to hold a grudge; the affliction of jealousy, cutting and contentious in nature; the affliction of cruelty and violence, savage and poisonous; the affliction of clinging to appearances, turning one's back on the noble truths; the affliction of inverted thinking regarding suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path; the affliction of turning through the twelve links of dependent origination in the cycle of birth and death; extending even to the afflictions, numerous as the sands of the river, of the beginningless ground of ignorance, and the afflictions born from the four abiding-grounds that construct the bitter fruit of the three realms — immeasurable and boundless, vexing and confusing the sages and the six destinies and four modes of birth alike. Today we lay them bare, and before the Buddhas, the venerable Dharma, and the noble assembly of the ten directions, we confess them all. We pray that, by the merit born of confessing every affliction arisen from the karma of mind — greed, hatred, delusion, and the rest — we disciples may, in life after life, break the banner of arrogance, exhaust the waters of craving and desire, extinguish the fire of hatred, and shatter the darkness of delusion; uproot the very root of doubt, and tear apart every net of wrong view; understand deeply that the three realms are like a prison, the four elements like poisonous serpents, the five aggregates like enemies and thieves, and the six sense-fields like an empty village feigning friendship and deceiving affection; cultivate the eightfold noble path, cutting off the source of ignorance, and turn straight toward nirvāṇa without ceasing and without rest, the thirty-seven factors of awakening arising moment after unbroken moment in the mind, and the ten perfections ever present before us. Having confessed, with utmost sincerity we take refuge in and pay homage to the ever-abiding Three Jewels.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The image of King Yama's underworld tribunal, with the ox-headed jailers and the wronged dead coming forward as witnesses, belongs to a distinctly Chinese bureaucratic vision of the afterlife layered onto Indian Buddhist karma theory — a court of law rather than an automatic natural process, complete with testimony, cross-examination, and a verdict that cannot be bought off. The text uses this vividly concrete imagery for a specific rhetorical purpose: karma theory stated abstractly ("actions have consequences") is easy to nod along with and forget; a courtroom scene where one's own victims appear to testify is much harder to file away. Read alongside the doctrinal enumeration of afflictions immediately before it, this section pairs Abhidharma's exhaustive analytical vocabulary with folk-religious narrative imagery in the same breath — one register for classifying the fault, another for making its consequence feel real.
