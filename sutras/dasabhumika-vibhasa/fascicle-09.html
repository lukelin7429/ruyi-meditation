The Four Dharmas, and the Beginning of Buddha-Recollection

An unbroken sequence of paired fours — what erodes wisdom and what builds it, four false friends against four true ones, four treasuries and what overcomes Māra — meant to purify the very first bodhisattva ground. Then Bhadrapāla and the pratyutpanna samādhi open a visualization practice: the thirty-two marks and eighty minor marks of a buddha's body, each one paired with what it signifies.

Four Dharmas That Lose Wisdom, Four That Gain It

Chinese Source

四法品第十九

如所說得三十二相諸業，菩薩應一心修習，修如此三十二相業以慧為本。

是故：退失慧四法，菩薩應遠離，得慧四種法，應常修習行。

有四法能退失慧，菩薩所應遠離。復有四得慧法，應常修習。

何等四法失慧？一、不敬法及說法者。二、於要法祕匿悋惜。三、樂法者，為作障礙壞其聽心。四、懷憍慢，自高卑人。

何等四法得慧？一、恭敬法及說法者。二、如所聞法及所讀誦，為他人說，其心清淨，不求利養。三、知從多聞得智慧故，勤求不息如救頭然。四、如所聞法，受持不忘，貴如說行，不貴言說。

是為四。

若人不壞諸善根者，是人能捨失慧四法，能行得慧四法。

是故求增益智慧者，如偈說：食善根四法，菩薩應遠離；增善根四法，菩薩應修習。

何等是侵食善根四法？一、懷憍慢，貪求世事。二、著利養，出入諸家。三、起憎嫉，謗諸菩薩。四、未聞經，聞不信受。

何等是增長善根四法？一、所未聞經，求之無厭，所謂六波羅蜜菩薩藏。二、於眾生，除憍慢心，謙遜下下。三、如法得財，趣足而已，離諸邪命，樂行四聖種行。四、於他罪，若實不實，無有刺譏，不求人短。

若於法中，有所不達，心不違逆，以佛為證，佛是一切智，其法無量，隨宜而說，非我所知。

如是增益善根四法，非諂曲者所能成就。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Four Dharmas Chapter

As for the karma that attains the thirty-two marks already explained, the bodhisattva ought to cultivate and study it with a single mind; the very karma that cultivates these thirty-two marks takes wisdom as its own root.

It is for this reason: "The four dharmas that cause the loss of wisdom, the bodhisattva ought to depart from; the four kinds of dharma that gain wisdom, one ought constantly to cultivate and practice."

There are four dharmas able to cause the loss of wisdom, which the bodhisattva ought to depart from. There are, further, four dharmas that gain wisdom, which one ought constantly to cultivate.

What are the four dharmas that lose wisdom? First, failing to revere the Dharma and one who expounds it. Second, concealing and begrudging the essential Dharma. Third, obstructing one who delights in the Dharma, destroying their own mind of listening. Fourth, harboring arrogance, exalting oneself and demeaning others.

What are the four dharmas that gain wisdom? First, revering the Dharma and one who expounds it. Second, expounding for others exactly what one has heard of the Dharma and what one has recited, one's own mind pure, seeking no gain or support. Third, knowing that wisdom is attained through vast learning, diligently seeking it without rest, as though rescuing one's own head from fire. Fourth, upholding exactly what one has heard of the Dharma without ever forgetting it, valuing practicing exactly as it has been taught, not merely valuing the speaking of it.

These are the four.

Should a person never destroy their own roots of goodness, this person is able to relinquish the four dharmas that lose wisdom, and able to practice the four dharmas that gain wisdom.

It is for this reason that one who seeks to increase their own wisdom, as the verse states: "The four dharmas that consume one's own roots of goodness, the bodhisattva ought to depart from; the four dharmas that increase one's own roots of goodness, the bodhisattva ought to cultivate and study."

What are the four dharmas that erode one's own roots of goodness? First, harboring arrogance, craving worldly affairs. Second, attached to gain and support, going in and out of every household. Third, giving rise to hatred and envy, slandering the bodhisattvas. Fourth, upon hearing a sūtra never heard before, hearing it yet failing to accept it with faith.

What are the four dharmas that increase one's own roots of goodness? First, toward a sūtra never heard before, seeking it without ever tiring, namely the six pāramitās and the bodhisattva basket. Second, toward sentient beings, removing every trace of arrogance, humble and yielding in every respect. Third, obtaining wealth in accordance with the Dharma, content with only what is sufficient, departing from every wrongful livelihood, delighting in practicing the conduct of the four noble lineages. Fourth, toward another's own offense, whether real or unreal, holding no mockery or reproach, never seeking out another's own faults.

Should there be something within the Dharma one has not yet penetrated, one's own mind holds no contrary resistance, taking the Buddha himself as witness — the Buddha is himself omniscient, and his own Dharma is immeasurable, expounded according to what is fitting, something I myself do not yet know.

These four dharmas that increase one's own roots of goodness are not something one who is flattering and crooked is able to accomplish.

A note on this fascicle's own structure

Fascicle 9 opens a chapter built almost entirely from paired lists of four — dozens of them in sequence, each contrasting what erodes a particular quality against what builds it. This compositional method, familiar from earlier fascicles, here becomes the chapter's sole organizing device from beginning to end, culminating in the explicit claim that walking through all of it purifies the very first bodhisattva ground.

Flattery and Crookedness, Corruption and Harmony

Chinese Source

是故：菩薩應遠離，諂曲相四法；應常修習行，直心相四法。

在家出家菩薩，應遠離四諂曲法，如曲木在稠林難可得出。如是世間有佛弟子，雖入佛法，不能得出生死深林。

何等為四？一、於佛法懷疑不信，無有定心。二、於眾生憍慢、瞋恨。三、於他利，心生貪嫉。四、毀謗菩薩，惡聲流布。是為四。

何等是四直心相？一者、有罪即時發露，無所隱藏，悔過除滅，行無悔道。二者、若以實語，失於王位及諸財寶，猶不妄語，口未曾說輕人之言。三者、若人惡口罵詈、輕賤、譏謗、繫閉、鞭杖、考掠等罪，但怨前身，不咎於他，信業果報，心無恚恨。四者、安住信功德中，諸佛妙法，甚難信解，心清淨故，皆能信受。

敗壞菩薩行四諂曲，調和菩薩有四直行，是故菩薩，欲不行諂曲相，欲行直心。

如說：應捨離四種，敗壞菩薩法；應修習四種，調和菩薩法。

云何名為四敗壞菩薩法？一、多聞而戲調，不隨法行。二、於教化而生戲論，不敬順和尚、阿闍梨。三者、不能消人信施，毀壞防制而受供養。四者、不敬柔善菩薩，心懷憍慢。是為四。

云何名為四調和菩薩法？一、常樂聞所未聞法，聞已能如所說行，依法、依義、依如說行。二、隨順義趣，不惑言辭，調和易化，於師事中用意施作。三、不失戒定，清淨活命。四、於調和菩薩，生恭敬心，隨順情重，破憍慢心，求其功德。

復次，菩薩有四種錯謬，常於此中求菩薩短，是名敗壞菩薩；若能親近四種善道，是名調和菩薩。

如偈說：菩薩應遠離，四種菩薩謬；菩薩應修習，四種菩薩道。

何謂菩薩四種錯謬？一、於非器眾生說甚深法，是名錯謬。二、樂深大法者，為說小乘，是名錯謬。三、於正行道者，持戒善心，輕慢不敬，是名錯謬。四、於未成就者，未可信而信，攝破戒惡人，以為親善，是名錯謬。

何等為四種菩薩道？一、於一切眾生行平等心。二、以善法教化一切。三、等為一切眾生說法。四、以正行行於一切眾生。

若常行菩薩四種錯謬，不樂思惟諸法，不勤修習善法，則是像菩薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is for this reason: "The bodhisattva ought to depart from the four dharmas that mark flattery and crookedness; one ought constantly to cultivate and practice the four dharmas that mark an upright mind."

The bodhisattva, whether householder or renunciant, ought to depart from these four dharmas of flattery and crookedness, just as a crooked piece of timber, lodged within a dense forest, is difficult indeed to extricate. So too, in this world, there are disciples of the Buddha who, though they have entered the Buddhadharma, are unable to escape the deep forest of birth and death.

What are these four? First, harboring doubt and lacking faith toward the Buddhadharma, without any resolute mind. Second, arrogance and resentment toward sentient beings. Third, giving rise, toward another's own gain, to a mind of craving and envy. Fourth, slandering the bodhisattvas, one's own evil reputation spreading abroad. These are the four.

What are the four marks of an upright mind? First, should an offense occur, disclosing it at once, concealing nothing, repenting of it and extinguishing it, practicing a path free of regret. Second, should truthful speech cost one one's own royal throne and every treasure, still never speaking falsely, one's own mouth never once uttering a word demeaning another. Third, should a person offend through harsh speech, reviling, contempt, mockery and slander, imprisonment, the whip and the rod, or torture, resenting only one's own past deeds, never blaming another for it, holding faith in the fruit and recompense of karma, one's own mind free of anger or resentment. Fourth, abiding securely within faith and merit, even though every buddha's own sublime Dharma is exceedingly difficult to believe and understand, one's own mind being pure, one is nonetheless able to accept it with faith in every case.

Four kinds of flattery and crookedness corrupt the bodhisattva's own practice; four kinds of upright conduct bring the bodhisattva into harmony. It is for this reason that the bodhisattva wishes never to practice the mark of flattery and crookedness, and wishes instead to practice an upright mind.

As the verse says: "One ought to relinquish and depart from these four kinds, which corrupt the bodhisattva's own dharma; one ought to cultivate and study these four kinds, which bring the bodhisattva's own dharma into harmony."

What, then, are called the four dharmas that corrupt the bodhisattva? First, being learned yet frivolous, failing to practice in accordance with the Dharma. Second, giving rise to idle argument toward those one teaches and transforms, failing to revere and accord with one's own preceptor and instructor. Third, being unable to properly digest another's own offering given in faith, breaking through every restraint while still receiving offerings. Fourth, failing to revere a gentle and virtuous bodhisattva, one's own mind harboring arrogance. These are the four.

What, then, are called the four dharmas that bring the bodhisattva into harmony? First, constantly delighting in hearing a dharma never heard before, and, having heard it, being able to practice exactly as it has been taught, relying upon the Dharma, relying upon its own meaning, practicing exactly as it has been taught. Second, according with its own true meaning, never being confused by mere words, harmonious and easily taught, applying one's own mind and effort in serving one's own teacher. Third, never losing the precepts or meditative concentration, maintaining a pure livelihood. Fourth, toward a harmonious bodhisattva, giving rise to a mind of reverence, according with them with deep feeling, breaking through one's own arrogance, seeking their own merit.

Furthermore, the bodhisattva has four kinds of error, constantly seeking out the bodhisattva's own faults within them — this is called corrupting the bodhisattva; should one be able to draw near to four kinds of wholesome path instead, this is called bringing the bodhisattva into harmony.

As the verse says: "The bodhisattva ought to depart from these four kinds of error belonging to the bodhisattva; the bodhisattva ought to cultivate these four kinds of path belonging to the bodhisattva."

What is meant by the bodhisattva's own four kinds of error? First, expounding a profoundly deep dharma to a sentient being unfit to receive it — this is called an error. Second, expounding the lesser vehicle to one who delights in a profound and great dharma — this is called an error. Third, toward one of right conduct upholding the precepts with a wholesome mind, showing contempt and a lack of reverence — this is called an error. Fourth, toward one not yet accomplished, trusting what ought not yet to be trusted, drawing in one who has broken the precepts and is evil, treating them as an intimate friend — this is called an error.

What are the four kinds of path belonging to the bodhisattva? First, practicing an equal mind toward every sentient being. Second, teaching and transforming all through the wholesome dharma. Third, expounding the Dharma equally for every sentient being. Fourth, practicing right conduct toward every sentient being.

Should one constantly practice the bodhisattva's own four kinds of error, taking no delight in reflecting upon every dharma, failing diligently to cultivate and study the wholesome dharma, this is then merely an image of a bodhisattva.

A note on the crooked timber

The image of crooked timber lodged within a dense forest, difficult to extricate, gives this section's own recurring theme its clearest shape: flattery and crookedness are not merely individual bad habits but structural entanglements, the very shape of the obstacle compounding with the density of the forest around it. This is why the chapter pairs each corrupting quality so precisely with its own harmonizing counterpart rather than simply listing faults to avoid — untangling crooked timber requires knowing exactly which straight path runs alongside it.

The Image of a Bodhisattva, and the First Four Merits

Chinese Source

是故：諸菩薩法中，四種像菩薩，佛說如是法，一一應遠離。

何等為四？一、貪重利養，不貴於法。二、但為名譽，不求功德。三、求欲自樂，不念眾生。四、貪樂眷屬，不樂遠離。是為四。

問曰：像菩薩法云何可捨？

答曰：若菩薩應修菩薩初行功德，是則能離像菩薩法，是故菩薩若欲離像菩薩法，如偈說：初行四功德，精勤令得生；生已令增長，增長已當護。

何等為四？一者，信解空法，亦信業果報。二者，樂無我法，而於一切眾生生大悲心。三者，心在涅槃而行在生死。四者，布施為欲成就眾生，而不求果報。

若人欲生菩薩初行四功德，增長守護者，當親近善知識。

如偈說：菩薩當親近，四種善知識；亦應當遠離，四種惡知識。

菩薩愛樂阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，應當親近恭敬供養四種善知識，當深遠離四種惡知識。

何等為四種善知識？一、於來求者生賢友想，以能助成無上道故。二、於說法者生善知識想，以能助成多聞智慧故。三、稱讚出家者生善知識想，以能助成一切善根故。四、於諸佛世尊生善知識想，以能助成一切佛法故。

何等為四種惡知識？一、求辟支佛乘心，樂少欲少事。二、求聲聞乘比丘，樂坐禪者。三、好讀外道路伽耶經，莊嚴文頌巧問答者。四、所親近者，得世間利不得法利。

是故菩薩應親近四善知識，遠離四惡知識。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is for this reason: "Within the bodhisattva's own dharma, there are four kinds of mere image of a bodhisattva — the Buddha has spoken of a dharma such as this, and each and every one of them ought to be departed from."

What are these four? First, craving and valuing gain and support, not valuing the Dharma. Second, seeking only fame and renown, not seeking merit. Third, seeking only one's own pleasure, giving no thought to sentient beings. Fourth, craving delight in one's own kin and retinue, taking no delight in dwelling apart. These are the four.

Question: How, then, can this mere image of a bodhisattva's own dharma be relinquished?

Answer: Should the bodhisattva cultivate the very merit of the bodhisattva's own initial practice, this alone is able to depart from this mere image of a bodhisattva's own dharma; it is for this reason that, should the bodhisattva wish to depart from this mere image of a bodhisattva's own dharma, as the verse says: "The four merits of the initial practice, diligently applying effort so that they arise; once arisen, causing them to increase; once increased, one ought then to guard and protect them."

What are these four? First, faith and understanding of the dharma of emptiness, and also faith in the fruit and recompense of karma. Second, delighting in the dharma of non-self, while giving rise to a mind of great compassion toward every sentient being. Third, one's own mind abiding in nirvāṇa while one's own conduct remains within birth and death. Fourth, giving for the sake of bringing sentient beings to accomplishment, without seeking any fruit in recompense.

Should a person wish to give rise to the bodhisattva's own four merits of initial practice, and to increase and guard and protect them, they ought to draw near to a good and virtuous friend.

As the verse says: "The bodhisattva ought to draw near to four kinds of good and virtuous friend; and ought likewise to depart far from four kinds of evil friend."

The bodhisattva who loves and delights in unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening ought to draw near to, revere, and make offerings to these four kinds of good and virtuous friend, and ought to depart far and deeply from these four kinds of evil friend.

What are these four kinds of good and virtuous friend? First, toward one who comes to make a request, giving rise to a thought of them as a worthy friend, since they are able to assist in accomplishing the unsurpassed path. Second, toward one who expounds the Dharma, giving rise to a thought of them as a good and virtuous friend, since they are able to assist in accomplishing vast learning and wisdom. Third, praising one who has gone forth from home, giving rise to a thought of them as a good and virtuous friend, since they are able to assist in accomplishing every root of goodness. Fourth, toward every buddha and world-honored one, giving rise to a thought of them as a good and virtuous friend, since they are able to assist in accomplishing the entire Buddhadharma.

What are these four kinds of evil friend? First, one who seeks the pratyekabuddha vehicle's own mind, delighting in few desires and few affairs. Second, a monk who seeks the śrāvaka vehicle, delighting only in sitting in meditation. Third, one who delights in reading the outside path's own Lokāyata scriptures, adorned with fine verse and clever question-and-answer. Fourth, one whom, in drawing near to them, one gains only worldly benefit and gains no benefit of the Dharma.

It is for this reason that the bodhisattva ought to draw near to these four good and virtuous friends, and depart far from these four evil friends.

A note on the "mere image" of a bodhisattva

The phrase "image of a bodhisattva" (像菩薩) names something specific and unsettling: not an outright fraud, but someone who has genuinely taken up the outward forms of bodhisattva practice while missing its actual substance, motivated instead by gain, reputation, private pleasure, or comfortable company. The remedy the text proposes is not stricter vigilance against impostors but a positive project — cultivating the four initial merits and deliberately choosing which friendships to cultivate — suggesting that the image and the reality are separated less by intention at any single moment than by the company one keeps and the habits one builds over time.

Four Treasuries, and What Overcomes Māra

Chinese Source

若菩薩能遠離四惡知識、親近四善知識者則得四廣大藏、過一切魔事法、能生無量福德、盡能攝取一切善法。

問曰：何等是菩薩大藏法？何等是能過一切魔事法？何等是能生無量福德法？何等是能攝取一切善法？

答曰：諸菩薩有四，廣大藏妙法，四攝諸善法，菩提心為先。

何等為四？一、得值佛。二、得聞六波羅蜜。三、於說法者心無瞋閡。四、以不放逸心樂住阿練若處。是為四大藏。

能過一切魔者，有四法。何等四？一、不捨菩提心。二、於一切眾生心無瞋礙。三、覺知一切諸見。四、於諸菩薩心無憍慢。是為四。

得無量福德法，復有四法。何等為四？一、於法施無所悕求。二、於破戒惡人生大悲心。三、於教眾生中發無上菩提。四、於下劣眾生而行忍辱。是為四。

攝一切善法者，有四法。何等為四？一、於空閑不現矯異常行。二、行四攝法不求恩報。三、不惜身命護持正法。四、種諸善根時以菩提心為先。是為四。

是一一四法皆應廣解，於文煩多故不廣，今如佛所說以偈略解。

若菩薩欲得諸菩薩藏，欲過一切魔事，欲攝一切善法者，皆當遠離：二空繫二縛，二障二垢法；二瘡及二坑，二燒二病法。

若菩薩欲得諸菩薩藏等功德者，應當遠離是諸二法。

何等為二空繫法？一、貪著應路伽耶等經，二、嚴飾衣鉢。

二縛者：一、著諸見縛，二、貪名利縛。

二障法者：一、親近白衣，二、疎遠善人。

二垢法者：一、忍受諸煩惱，二、樂諸檀越知識。

二瘡法者：一、見他人過，二、自藏其過。

二坑法者：一、毀壞正法，二、破戒受供。

二燒法者：一、以穢濁心而著袈裟，二、受淨戒者供給。

出家之人有二病難治：一、增上慢人自謂能降伏心，二、求大乘者沮壞其意。

若菩薩遠離如是等法，更有疾得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提法，則能疾得，又得諸佛、辟支佛、阿羅漢之所稱歎。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should the bodhisattva be able to depart from the four evil friends, and draw near to the four good and virtuous friends, they thereby attain the four vast treasuries, the dharma of overcoming every activity of māra, the ability to give rise to immeasurable merit, and the ability fully to gather in every wholesome dharma.

Question: What, then, is the bodhisattva's own dharma of the great treasuries? What is the dharma able to overcome every activity of māra? What is the dharma able to give rise to immeasurable merit? What is the dharma able to gather in every wholesome dharma?

Answer: "Every bodhisattva possesses these four vast treasuries of the sublime Dharma; these four gather in every wholesome dharma, with bodhicitta placed foremost."

What are these four? First, encountering the Buddha. Second, hearing the six pāramitās. Third, holding no mind of anger or obstruction toward one who expounds the Dharma. Fourth, delighting, with a mind free of heedlessness, in dwelling within a forest hermitage. These are the four great treasuries.

As for what is able to overcome every activity of māra, there are four dharmas. What are these four? First, never abandoning bodhicitta. Second, holding, toward every sentient being, a mind free of anger or obstruction. Third, being aware of every view. Fourth, holding, toward every bodhisattva, a mind free of arrogance. These are the four.

As for the dharma that attains immeasurable merit, there are, further, four dharmas. What are these four? First, holding no expectation whatsoever toward the giving of the Dharma. Second, giving rise to a mind of great compassion toward an evil person who has broken the precepts. Third, giving rise to the unsurpassed bodhi within the very act of teaching sentient beings. Fourth, practicing patient endurance toward the most degraded sentient being. These are the four.

As for gathering in every wholesome dharma, there are four dharmas. What are these four? First, never displaying, within solitude, any pretense or affectation, one's own conduct constant. Second, practicing the four means of gathering others while seeking no gratitude or repayment. Third, never sparing one's own body or life in guarding and protecting the true Dharma. Fourth, at the very moment of planting every root of goodness, placing bodhicitta foremost. These are the four.

Each and every one of these sets of four dharmas ought to be explained at great length, but, since this would make the text overly voluminous, I shall not explain them at length; I shall now, as the Buddha himself has spoken, explain them briefly through verse.

Should the bodhisattva wish to attain every bodhisattva treasury, wish to overcome every activity of māra, and wish to gather in every wholesome dharma, they ought, in every case, to depart from: "the two bonds of emptiness, the two fetters, the two obstructions, the two defilements; the two wounds and the two pits, the two burnings and the two sicknesses."

Should the bodhisattva wish to attain merit such as every bodhisattva treasury, they ought to depart from these very pairs of dharmas.

What are the two bonds of emptiness? First, craving attachment to the Lokāyata and similar scriptures; second, adorning and decorating one's own robe and bowl.

The two fetters are: first, the fetter of attachment to every view; second, the fetter of craving fame and gain.

The two obstructions are: first, drawing near to those in white robes; second, keeping far from good and virtuous persons.

The two defilements are: first, tolerating and enduring every affliction; second, delighting in every donor and acquaintance.

The two wounds are: first, beholding another's own fault; second, concealing one's own fault.

The two pits are: first, destroying the true Dharma; second, breaking the precepts while receiving offerings.

The two burnings are: first, wearing the kāṣāya robe with a mind that is impure and turbid; second, receiving the support offered to one who upholds pure precepts.

The renunciant possesses two sicknesses difficult to cure: first, one of excessive pride, who believes themselves already able to subdue their own mind; second, one who seeks the Great Vehicle, yet undermines and destroys their own resolve toward it.

Should the bodhisattva depart from dharmas such as these, there is, further, a dharma for swiftly attaining unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, which they are thereby able swiftly to attain, and further attain the praise of every buddha, pratyekabuddha, and arhat.

A note on the two sicknesses

The closing pair — excessive pride mistaking itself for genuine mastery, and undermining one's own resolve toward the Great Vehicle — is singled out as "difficult to cure" precisely because each disguises itself from the person suffering it: the proud practitioner believes they have already succeeded, while the self-undermining one may not even recognize their own doubt as sabotage. Every other paired fault in this section names an external behavior to avoid; these two name internal states that resist the very self-examination needed to notice them.

Question: What kind of dharma, then, is a dharma for swiftly attaining unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening? What kind is praised by every buddha, pratyekabuddha, and arhat?

Answer: "Being able to practice the marks of the four noble truths, one swiftly attains buddha-bodhi. And practicing these four dharmas, one is praised by the three kinds of noble one."

What are the marks of the four noble truths? First, applying diligent effort for the sake of seeking every wholesome dharma. Second, listening to, receiving, reciting, and studying the scriptural teaching, and practicing exactly as it has been taught. Third, holding a mind of weariness and renunciation toward the three realms, just as toward a place of execution, constantly seeking to escape from it. Fourth, benefiting one's own mind for the sake of benefiting and bringing peace to every sentient being.

"Truth" refers to what is genuine and real, free of deception; through attaining unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, it is called that which is never empty or false.

There are, further, four dharmas praised by the three kinds of noble one — what are these four? First, never committing an evil deed even at the cost of one's own life. Second, constantly practicing the giving of the Dharma. Third, receiving the Dharma with a constantly unified mind. Fourth, should a defiled mind arise, being immediately able rightly to contemplate this very defiled mind, and the very causes and conditions giving rise to this defilement. As for the root of this very defilement, what is truly meant by "defilement"? What exactly is this defilement? Through what matter does it arise? Who gives rise to this very defilement? Reflecting rightly in this way, one knows it to be illusory, without any true reality, without any real existence at all, resolutely believing and understanding that every dharma is empty, and that no dharma possesses any true existence whatsoever. Contemplating rightly, in this way, the very causes and conditions of defilement, one thereby fails to give rise to any evil karma; every remaining affliction ought likewise to be contemplated in just this same way.

The bodhisattva, having attained this very dharma praised by the great persons, departing from every evil karma of the afflictions, their own mind thereby complete with a mind of relinquishment, as the verse states: "Complete with a mind of relinquishment, one seeks both worldly and transcendent benefit. At the very moment of seeking these benefits, one's own mind knows no weariness or fatigue."

This bodhisattva, complete with this very dharma of relinquishment, wishing to practice the giving of the Dharma and the giving of material wealth for the benefit of sentient beings, whether seeking a worldly or a transcendent benefit, before it is yet attained, one's own mind knows no weariness or fatigue.

"Worldly benefit" refers to skillfully understanding worldly scriptures, skills, arts, and every clever expedient.

"Transcendent benefit" refers to every uncontaminated faculty, power, factor of awakening, and dharma of the path.

As the verse states: "Seeking these two kinds of benefit in this way, one's own mind knows no weariness or fatigue; through this very freedom from weariness and fatigue, one is able to attain every profound dharma. Through this very seeking of scriptures, one is able to attain wisdom, complete in knowing the very highest, foremost dharma of this world."

As for being "free of weariness and fatigue": weariness and fatigue refers to a sense of aversion; should whatever one studies be free of this very aversion, one's own mind knows no weariness or fatigue.

Should one be free of weariness and fatigue, then, in seeking every scripture, art, medical prescription, skill, ceremony, and rule, one experiences no weariness or fatigue whatsoever.

Through this very freedom from weariness and fatigue, one thereby attains wisdom, complete in a profound knowledge of what is fitting within this world.

"Worldly dharma" refers to what is fitting according to local custom, according with the very mind of this world, being able fully to know the very governance of this world — it is for this reason that one is able to know how to guide the superior, the middling, and the inferior sentient being, each according to what is fitting. Skillfully understanding every worldly matter, one holds a deep mind of conscience and shame.

"Guiding according to what is fitting" refers to the superior, the middling, and the inferior each possessing their own fitting approach.

As for "conscience and shame": being ashamed of one's own conduct by oneself is called conscience; feeling shame on account of another is called shame. There are those who feel embarrassed through their own action alone, and those who feel shame only upon being seen by another.

Within worldly dharma, shame is the very foremost of these to be applied. As the sūtra states: "Two pure, unblemished dharmas guard and protect this world — namely, conscience and shame."

As the verse says: "Wherever a person holds shame, they thereby know the Dharma, and know offense from merit; where shame is absent, the good depart far away, and there is no evil left undone."

A note on right contemplation of defilement

The four-question interrogation of a defiled mind the moment it arises — what is defilement, what exactly is this instance of it, through what does it arise, who gives rise to it — is a compact meditative technique rather than mere doctrinal assertion: turning toward the defilement with these specific questions, rather than either suppressing it or indulging it, is what dissolves its apparent solidity into the emptiness the passage then names directly. The chapter's turn afterward to worldly competence — scriptures, arts, medicine, local custom — as itself a form of benefit worth pursuing without weariness continues a theme first raised in Fascicle 6's account of a bodhisattva's education, treating skillful engagement with the world as inseparable from the transcendent path rather than a distraction from it.

Monks draw near to them, the devas make offering to them, surrounded on every side by every great assembly with reverence; with a single-pointed mind, being mindful of them, one takes up every buddha's own mark.

One is further mindful: every buddha is one of great vows, having accomplished great compassion, never once broken off. Complete with great loving-kindness, bringing deep peace to sentient beings. Practicing great sympathetic joy, fulfilling every vow. Practicing a mind of equanimity, relinquishing hatred and love alike, yet never abandoning sentient beings.

A note on Bhadrapāla

Bhadrapāla (跋陀婆羅, here rendered 跋陀婆) is the householder bodhisattva to whom the historical Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra (般舟三昧經) is addressed, one of the earliest Mahāyāna sūtras to survive in Chinese translation and a foundational text for buddha-visualization practice throughout East Asian Buddhism. That the recipient of this teaching is explicitly a layperson, not a monastic, matters for this treatise's own larger argument: the vision of every buddha this samādhi makes possible is available to a householder bodhisattva through disciplined practice, without first requiring ordination or the supernormal faculties of the heavenly eye and ear.

A note on the fourfold lion throne

The throne's own construction from four jeweled lions, each rendered in a different precious material for a different feature — amber eyes, coral tongue, diamond teeth, silver mane — extends the same symbolic density applied to the Buddha's own body onto the very furniture that supports him, a visualization detail meant to be held in the mind with the same precision as the eighty minor marks themselves. The passage's own attention then shifts, in its closing lines, from the Buddha's own form to the visible physical response of the listening assembly — hair standing on end, tears, warmth of feeling — treating the congregation's own bodily reaction as itself part of what the practitioner visualizes.

A note on the sequence from names to liberation

The nine-step chain traced here — mere names leading to understood meaning, meaning to delight, delight to joy, joy to concentration, concentration to true wisdom, true wisdom to weariness with saṃsāra, weariness to the extinguishing of fetters, and finally to liberation itself — describes teaching's own psychological mechanics with unusual precision, treating the sequence as a genuine causal process a listener passes through rather than a metaphor. This passage closes the descriptive account of what buddha-recollection contemplates; the fascicle's own final movement, beginning in the next section, turns to the verses a practitioner actually recites to train the mind toward this same visualization.

A note on this verse form

These verses revisit the same thirty-two marks named in prose earlier in this fascicle, now cast into a form meant for recitation rather than analysis — each stanza restating a mark's own karmic cause in a more compressed, memorable phrasing suited to the actual practice of chanting while visualizing. The final stanza's own dedication, wishing purity and peace for every person rather than merely for the reciting practitioner, marks this as devotional recitation with an outward-facing aim, not private contemplation alone.

A note on this closing warning

The final line's own stark claim — that failing to practice this very mindfulness permanently forfeits benefit in this life and beyond — is a striking note to end the fascicle on, given how much of the treatise elsewhere insists on skillful means fitted to each practitioner's own actual capacity. Read alongside Fascicle 5's own Chapter of Easy Practice, which recommended calling upon a buddha's own name precisely for those unable to sustain more difficult practices, this warning likely addresses a practitioner already capable of this contemplative visualization who neglects it, not one for whom an easier path remains genuinely available.
