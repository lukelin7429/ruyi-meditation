Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā Śāstra.

Nāgārjuna's own extended commentary on the bodhisattva's ten grounds — the fourth of his treatises on this site, joining the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, the Twelve Gate Treatise, and the Hundred Treatise. Seventeen fascicles, thirty-five chapters, unfolding the ten grounds first named in the Daśabhūmika Sūtra itself into a full account of vows, precepts, giving, and practice — including, at Chapter 9, the Chapter of Easy Practice, the earliest scriptural source for Pure Land Buddhism's own distinction between the difficult path of self-power and the easy path of relying on a buddha's vow.

How to read this collection

The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā Śāstra is a commentary on the ten bodhisattva grounds first named in the Daśabhūmika Sūtra, later absorbed into the Avataṃsaka Sūtra as its own "Ten Grounds" chapter. Kumārajīva's early fifth-century translation survives complete through the second ground and partway into further material touching the third; the received text, seventeen fascicles across thirty-five named chapters, is traditionally read as an incomplete commentary — vast even so, at roughly 128,000 Chinese characters. This site's translation follows the fascicle divisions of the Taishō text, one page per fascicle, each broken into several source-and-translation sections at natural argument boundaries.

This treatise already anchors a term used across this site's Pure Land pages: the Ten Doubts About the Pure Land and the Rebirth Treatise (through Tanluan's commentary) both quote its Chapter 9, the Chapter of Easy Practice, as the scriptural root of the distinction between the "difficult path" (難行道) of relying on one's own power alone and the "easy path" (易行道) of relying instead on a buddha's own vow — the very distinction Shandao's Commentary on the Contemplation Sūtra, already complete on this site, builds an entire soteriology upon. This collection translates that chapter, and the treatise surrounding it, directly and in full for the first time on this site.

The Treatise's Thirty-Five Chapters

Unlike a text organized strictly ground by ground, this treatise's thirty-five chapters range freely across vows, precepts, giving, and practice — Chapter 9, the Chapter of Easy Practice, is this treatise's own most historically consequential single chapter.
