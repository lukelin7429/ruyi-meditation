Entering the Temple, and Practices Shared by Householder and Renunciant

Why the householder bodhisattva must never judge a monk who has broken the precepts — only the Buddha's own omniscience can measure another's inner state. Not Clinging to What Is Requested

Chinese Source

入寺品第十七

如是在家菩薩，不應於諸事中生貪著心、我我所心，何以故？隨所貪著難捨之物、法應施與，若能施與，則除此過。菩薩如是無有貪著慳惜之心，可以處家。

問曰：在家菩薩，或有貪惜愛著之物，有來求者，此應云何？

答曰：於所貪著物，有來求索者，當自勸喻心，即施勿慳惜。

菩薩所貪惜物，若有乞人，急從求索：「汝以此物，施與我者，速得成佛菩薩。」即時，應自勸喻，而施與之。如是思惟：「若我今者，不捨此物，此物必當遠離於我。設至死時，不隨我去，此物則是遠離之相，今為阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，具足檀波羅蜜故施與，後至死時，心無有悔。」

經說：「不悔心死，必生善處，是得大利，云何不捨！」

如是自勸，猶貪惜者，應辭謝乞者言：「我今是新學，善根未成就，心未得自在，願後當相與。」

應辭謝乞者言：「勿生瞋恨，我新發意，善根未具，於菩薩行法，未得勢力，是以未能捨於此物，後得勢力，善根成就，心得堅固，當以相與。」

復次——若眾不和合，斷於經法事，菩薩應隨力，方便令不絕。

眾僧或以事緣，諍競乖散，法事有廢；在家菩薩應勤心方便，彼此之間，心無所偏；若以財物，若以言說，禮敬求請，令還和合。

或以乏少衣食因緣；或邪見者，橫作障礙；或說法者，欲求利養；或聽法者，心不恭敬。

在家菩薩，於此事中，隨宜方便——若以財物，若以言說；下意求請，使法事不廢。法事不廢者，是為然佛法燈，供養十方三世諸佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Entering the Temple Chapter

The householder bodhisattva, in this way, ought never, within any matter whatsoever, give rise to a mind of craving attachment, or a mind of "I" and "mine." And why is this so? Whatever object or dharma is craved and clinged to and difficult to relinquish ought to be given away; should one be able to give it away, this very fault is thereby removed. The bodhisattva, being in this way free of a mind of craving attachment and stinginess, is thereby fit to dwell within the household.

Question: Should the householder bodhisattva possess some object of craving, attachment, and fondness, and should one come to request it of them, how ought this be handled?

Answer: "Toward an object one craves and clings to, should one come to request and seek it, one ought to encourage and persuade one's own mind at once, and give it away without any stinginess."

Should there be an object the bodhisattva craves and clings to, and should a beggar come urgently requesting it, saying: "Should you give this object to me, you shall swiftly accomplish buddhahood" — one ought, at that very moment, to encourage and persuade oneself, and give it to them. One reflects thus: "Should I, at this moment, fail to relinquish this object, this object shall inevitably depart from me regardless. Should I reach the moment of death, it shall not follow me there either — this object bears, in any case, the very mark of departing from me. Now, for the sake of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, completing the pāramitā of giving, I give it away — so that, when I later reach the moment of death, my own mind shall hold no regret."

The sūtra states: "A mind free of regret at the moment of death necessarily leads to rebirth in a good place — this itself is a great benefit, so why would one not relinquish it!"

Should one, having encouraged oneself in this way, still remain unable to overcome this craving and stinginess, one ought to decline the beggar's request, saying: "I am now still a new learner, my own roots of goodness not yet accomplished, my own mind not yet having attained mastery — I hope that, later on, I may be able to give it to you."

One ought to decline the beggar's request, saying: "Give rise to no anger or resentment toward me — I have only newly given rise to this aspiration, my own roots of goodness not yet complete, and, within the bodhisattva's own practice, I have not yet attained sufficient strength; it is for this reason that I am not yet able to relinquish this object. Once I have attained sufficient strength, my roots of goodness accomplished, my mind firm and resolute, I shall certainly give it to you."

Furthermore: "Should the assembly fall into discord, severing the very affairs of the scriptural teaching, the bodhisattva ought, according to their own capacity, to apply skillful means so that it is not utterly severed."

Should the assembly of monks, through some matter or circumstance, fall into contention and scatter apart, so that the affairs of the Dharma fall into neglect, the householder bodhisattva ought diligently to apply skillful means, holding no partiality between either side; whether through material wealth or through speech, showing reverence and making a respectful request, so as to restore this very harmony.

This may arise through the cause and condition of a lack of clothing or food; or through one holding wrong views, who wrongly creates an obstruction; or through one who expounds the Dharma, who seeks gain and support; or through one who listens to the Dharma, whose own mind lacks reverence.

The householder bodhisattva, amid a matter such as this, applies whatever skillful means are fitting — whether through material wealth or through speech; lowering their own mind and making a respectful request, so that the affairs of the Dharma are not neglected. That the affairs of the Dharma are not neglected is itself the very kindling of the lamp of the Buddhadharma, making offering to every buddha of the ten directions and the three times.

A note on declining honestly

The permission to decline a request when genuinely unable to give without regret — offered here as an honest alternative to forcing a gift one would later resent — sits alongside the treatise's own repeated insistence elsewhere that giving ought to be immediate and unhesitating. The distinction is candor: a gift given through gritted teeth, later regretted, undermines the very purity of giving described in earlier fascicles, while an honest "not yet" that names one's own current limitation preserves the relationship and leaves room for genuine giving later.

The Fasting Day and the Eight Precepts

Chinese Source

復次——齋日受八戒，親近淨戒者，以戒善因緣，深心行愛敬。

齋日者：月八日，十四日，十五日，二十三日，二十九日，三十日，及遮三忌。三忌者，十五日為一忌，從冬至後四十五日。

此諸惡日，多有鬼神，侵剋縱暴；世人為守護日故，過中不食。

佛因教令受一日戒，既得福德，諸天來下，觀察世間，見之歡喜，則便護念。在家菩薩於諸小事，猶尚增益，何況先有此齋，而不隨順？是故應行一日齋法，既得自利，亦能利人。

問曰：齋法云何？

答曰：應作是言——如諸聖人，常離殺生，棄捨刀杖，常無瞋恚，有慚愧心，慈悲眾生。我某甲今一日一夜，遠離殺生，棄捨刀杖，無有瞋恚，有慚愧心，慈悲眾生，以如是法，隨學聖人。

如諸聖人，常離「不與取」，身行清淨，受而知足。我今一日一夜，遠離劫盜、不與取，求受清淨自活，以如是法，隨學聖人。

如諸聖人，常斷婬泆，遠離世樂。我今一日一夜，除斷婬泆，遠離世樂，淨修梵行，以如是法，隨學聖人。

如諸聖人，常離妄語。真實語，正直語。我今一日一夜遠離妄語。真實語，正直語，以如是法，隨學聖人。

如諸聖人，常遠離酒，酒是放逸處，我今一日一夜，遠離於酒，以如是法，隨學聖人。

如諸聖人，常遠離歌舞作樂，花香瓔珞，嚴身之具。我今一日一夜，遠離歌舞作樂，華香瓔珞，嚴身之具，以如是法，隨學聖人。

如諸聖人，常遠離高廣大床處，在小榻草蓐為座。我今一日一夜，遠離高廣大床，處在小榻草蓐為座，以如是法，隨學聖人。

如諸聖人，常過中不食，遠離非時行非時食。我今一日一夜，過中不食，遠離非時行非時食，以如是法，隨學聖人。

如說：「殺盜淫妄語，飲酒及華香、瓔珞歌舞等，高床過中食；聖人所捨離，我今亦如是，以此福因緣，一切共成佛。」

親近持淨戒比丘者，在家菩薩應親近諸比丘，盡能護持清淨禁戒、成就功德防遠眾惡者。

以戒善因緣者，又應親近持戒比丘身口業淨、心行直善無眾惡者。

深心愛敬者，於上直心、善行、持戒比丘、成就諸功德者，應生最上恭敬、深心愛樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: "On a fasting day, one receives the eight precepts, drawing near to those who uphold pure precepts, and, through the very causes and conditions of the goodness of the precepts, practicing love and reverence with a deep mind."

The "fasting days" are: the eighth day of the month, the fourteenth, the fifteenth, the twenty-third, the twenty-ninth, the thirtieth, and the three restraint days. The three restraint days are: the fifteenth day counted as one such day, and again forty-five days after the winter solstice.

On these very inauspicious days, ghosts and spirits abound, encroaching and running rampant; ordinary people, in order to guard against this day, refrain from eating past midday.

The Buddha, on account of this, taught people to receive the precepts for a single day; having thereby obtained this merit, the devas descend to observe this world, and, beholding it, feel delight, and thereupon extend their own protective mindfulness. The householder bodhisattva, even toward the smallest of matters, still seeks to increase their own benefit all the more — how much more so, then, when this very fasting observance already exists, should they fail to accord with it? It is for this reason that one ought to practice this one-day fasting observance, thereby obtaining both one's own benefit and the ability to benefit others as well.

Question: What, then, is the method of this fasting observance?

Answer: One ought to speak thus: "Just as the noble ones constantly depart from the taking of life, relinquishing blade and club, forever free of anger, possessing a mind of conscience and shame, showing loving-kindness and compassion to sentient beings — I, so-and-so, for this one day and one night, depart from the taking of life, relinquishing blade and club, free of anger, possessing a mind of conscience and shame, showing loving-kindness and compassion to sentient beings, and, through this very dharma, follow and study the noble ones."

"Just as the noble ones constantly depart from 'taking what is not given,' their own bodily conduct pure, receiving only what is needed and knowing contentment — I, for this one day and one night, depart far from robbery and theft, from taking what is not given, seeking only a pure livelihood, and, through this very dharma, follow and study the noble ones."

"Just as the noble ones constantly sever sexual excess, departing far from worldly pleasure — I, for this one day and one night, sever and cut off sexual excess, departing far from worldly pleasure, purely cultivating the holy life, and, through this very dharma, follow and study the noble ones."

"Just as the noble ones constantly depart from false speech, speaking only what is true, speaking only what is upright — I, for this one day and one night, depart far from false speech, speaking only what is true, speaking only what is upright, and, through this very dharma, follow and study the noble ones."

"Just as the noble ones constantly depart far from wine, since wine is itself the very locus of heedlessness — I, for this one day and one night, depart far from wine, and, through this very dharma, follow and study the noble ones."

"Just as the noble ones constantly depart far from song, dance, and music, from flowers, fragrance, and garlands, from every ornament that adorns the body — I, for this one day and one night, depart far from song, dance, and music, from flowers, fragrance, and garlands, from every ornament that adorns the body, and, through this very dharma, follow and study the noble ones."

"Just as the noble ones constantly depart far from high, broad, and grand beds, sitting instead upon a small couch or a mat of grass — I, for this one day and one night, depart far from high, broad, and grand beds, sitting instead upon a small couch or a mat of grass, and, through this very dharma, follow and study the noble ones."

"Just as the noble ones constantly refrain from eating past midday, departing far from eating at an improper time — I, for this one day and one night, refrain from eating past midday, departing far from eating at an improper time, and, through this very dharma, follow and study the noble ones."

As the verse says: "Killing, theft, sexual misconduct, false speech, drinking wine, and flowers and fragrance, garlands and dance and the like, a high bed, and eating past midday — these the noble ones have relinquished, and I, too, do likewise now; through the causes and conditions of this very merit, may every being together accomplish buddhahood."

"Drawing near to monks who uphold pure precepts" means the householder bodhisattva ought to draw near to every monk fully able to guard and uphold the pure precepts, accomplishing merit and keeping far from every evil.

"Through the causes and conditions of the goodness of the precepts" means further drawing near to precept-upholding monks whose own conduct of body and speech is pure, whose mind and conduct are upright and good, free of every evil.

"Practicing love and reverence with a deep mind" means, toward monks of upright mind, wholesome conduct, and precept-upholding, who have accomplished every merit as described above, giving rise to the very highest reverence, and love and delight with a deep mind.

A note on the eight precepts

This fasting observance (poṣadha, 齋) is the lay tradition's own periodic taste of monastic life: for a single day and night, a householder voluntarily takes on precepts that exceed the ordinary five — full celibacy rather than mere avoidance of misconduct, no eating past noon, no music or adornment, no high beds — before returning the next morning to ordinary lay conduct. The closing dedication verse frames this brief discipline explicitly as a shared path toward buddhahood rather than a private austerity.

Compassion, Not Contempt, for a Monk Who Has Broken the Precepts

Chinese Source

問曰：在家菩薩若於持戒比丘成就功德生愛敬心者，應於破戒比丘生輕恚心耶？

答曰：若見破戒者，不應起輕恚。

在家菩薩，若見破戒雜行比丘，威儀不具，所行穢濁，覆藏瑕疵，無有梵行自稱梵行；於此比丘，不應輕慢、有瞋恚心。

問曰：若不瞋恨，應生何心？

答曰：應生憐愍心，訶責諸煩惱。

在家菩薩，若見破戒比丘，不應生瞋恨、輕慢，應生憐愍、利益之心。

作是念：「咄哉！此人遇佛妙法，得離地獄、畜生、餓鬼、色無色界、邊地生處。諸根具足，不聾啞，不頑鈍，值佛妙法；別識好醜，心存正見，解知義理。

「人身難得，如大海中，有一眼鼈，頭入板孔；生在人中，倍難於此。既聞佛法，能滅諸惡，度諸苦惱，得至正智。

「捨諸資生，所有多少，永割親族，無所顧戀。若生凡庶，或在種姓，信佛語故，能捨出家。

「常聞破戒之罪，所謂自賤其身，智所訶責，惡名流布，常懷疑悔，死墮惡道；得聞此事，而猶破戒。

「行十善道，乃得人身，而不能如法善用，以自利益。

「咄哉！三毒其力甚惡，常陵眾生，難得捨離。諸佛種種呵罵煩惱惡賊、惡行，如實有理。」

如是思惟，不應輕賤破戒之人。

又作是念：若我不能都離瞋恚、輕慢心者，應自思惟：「佛法無量，猶如大海，或有開通而我不知。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Should the householder bodhisattva give rise to a mind of love and reverence toward a monk who upholds the precepts and has accomplished merit, ought they then give rise to a mind of contempt and anger toward a monk who has broken the precepts?

Answer: "Should one behold one who has broken the precepts, one ought never to give rise to contempt or anger."

Should the householder bodhisattva behold a monk of broken precepts and mixed conduct, their own bearing incomplete, their own conduct impure and turbid, concealing their own faults, lacking the holy life yet calling themselves one who lives it — toward this monk, one ought never to give rise to contempt or a mind of anger.

Question: Should one not give rise to anger and resentment, what mind ought one then give rise to?

Answer: "One ought to give rise to a mind of pity, reproaching only the afflictions themselves."

Should the householder bodhisattva behold a monk who has broken the precepts, they ought never to give rise to anger, resentment, or contempt, but ought to give rise to a mind of pity and benefit instead.

One reflects thus: "Alas! This person has encountered the Buddha's own sublime Dharma, able to depart from hell, the animal realm, the realm of hungry ghosts, the formless and form realms, and rebirth in a borderland. Their own faculties are complete, neither deaf nor mute, neither dull nor obtuse, having encountered the Buddha's own sublime Dharma; able to distinguish the fine from the foul, their own mind holding right view, understanding and knowing its true meaning.

"A human birth is difficult to obtain, just as, within the great ocean, a one-eyed turtle's head might chance to pass through the hole of a floating plank; being born among humans is more difficult still than even this. Having already heard the Buddhadharma, able to extinguish every evil, to cross beyond every suffering and affliction, and to reach right wisdom.

"Relinquishing every article of livelihood, whatever their quantity, forever severing themselves from their own kin, holding no attachment or longing for them. Whether born among the common people, or within a noble lineage, through faith in the Buddha's own words, able to relinquish it all and go forth from home.

"Having constantly heard of the offense of breaking the precepts — namely, debasing oneself, being reproached by the wise, one's own evil reputation spreading abroad, constantly harboring doubt and regret, and, at death, falling into an evil destiny — having heard this very matter, and yet still breaking the precepts nonetheless.

"Practicing the ten wholesome paths, one thereby attains a human birth, and yet is unable to make proper use of it in accordance with the Dharma, for their own benefit.

"Alas! The threefold poison's own power is exceedingly evil, constantly overpowering sentient beings, difficult indeed to relinquish and depart from. Every buddha reproaches, in every manner, this evil bandit of the afflictions, and this evil conduct — and this is genuinely, truly so."

Reflecting in this way, one ought never to hold this person, who has broken the precepts, in contempt.

One further reflects: should I be unable to depart entirely from a mind of anger and contempt, I ought to reflect for myself: "The Buddhadharma is immeasurable, just like the great ocean itself — there may be some opening or passage within it that I myself do not yet know."

A note on the blind turtle

The image of a one-eyed sea turtle chancing to surface with its head through the single hole of a drifting plank is one of the most widely repeated similes in the Buddhist canon for the rarity of human birth, appearing across multiple early sūtras in slightly different forms. Its function here is precise: the passage does not excuse breaking the precepts, but insists that the very rarity of the birth in which this failure occurred is itself grounds for pity rather than contempt — a person who received so improbable an opportunity and still fell short is tragic, not merely blameworthy.

Only the Buddha Can Measure Another's Mind

Chinese Source

如《大乘決定王經》中，佛告阿難：「或有比丘，根鈍闇塞，心不明了，不達諸法相，常念有想。無想法中，而取有想——生男女想、生罪礙想、生垢想、生淨想——生如是想者，名為鈍根，心不明了，則為有罪。

「阿難！若人一切法中，不能善解，名為不了一切諸法，從初以來，本體、性、相常不可得。是人不知如是之事，生是諸想，則與外道無有差別。

「阿難！我所說法，皆有開通，明了清淨，此中無罪，亦無罪者。阿難！罪，名疑悔、愚癡、闇冥；罪者，名生眾生想、我想、命想、人想；皆因身見，名為罪者。

「於我法中，無如此人。

「若我法中，定實有我、眾生、命、人、身見等者，不言：『我法有開』，非是不開；我法從本已來，常清淨明了。

「復次，阿難！若決定有罪、有受罪者，則身即是神，即墮常見，則無佛道；若身異於神，即墮斷見，亦無佛道。

「如是六十二見，皆可是菩提，但是事不然。

「是故，阿難！我於大眾中師子吼說而無所畏，言：我法有開，非不有開，從本已來，常清淨明了。

「阿難！若罪定有，則畢竟無涅槃，我則不言：我法有開。

「阿難！我法實從本已來，清淨明了，是故我弟子降心安隱，無有疑悔，無諸罪惡，清淨行道。」

菩薩應如是思惟，不應瞋恚破戒者。

又作是念：是戒必定得住阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。何以故？

曾聞必定菩薩有起罪者：如過去十萬劫，有菩薩誹謗漏盡阿羅漢名為阿羅漢；又聞必定菩薩於此劫前三十一劫，以矛刺須陀洹；又此賢劫中，聞有菩薩誹謗劬樓孫佛言：「何有禿人，而當得道？」

如是等眾生，難可得知，是故我於此事，何用知為？得失好惡，彼自作自受，何豫於我？我今若欲實知彼事，或自傷害，籌量眾生，佛所不許。如《經》中說：佛告阿難：「若人籌量於他，即自傷身，唯我可得籌量眾生，與我等者亦應籌量。」

如說：有瓶蓋亦空，無蓋亦復空，有瓶蓋亦滿，無蓋亦復滿。

當知諸世間，有此四種人。

威儀及功德，有無亦如是。

若非一切智，何能籌量人？

寧以見威儀，而便知其德？

正智有善心，名為賢人相，但見外威儀，何由知其內？

內有功德慧，外現無威儀，遊行無知者，如以灰覆火。

若以外量內，而生輕賤心，敗身及善根，命終墮惡道。

外詐現威儀，遊行似賢聖，但有口言說，如雷而無雨。

諸心所行處，錯謬難得知。

是故諸眾生，不可妄度量；唯有一切智，悉知諸心心。

微密所行處，是故量眾生；佛言與我等，乃能量眾生。

若佛如是說，誰能籌量人？

若見外威儀，稱量其內德。

自敗其善根，如水自崩岸，若於此錯謬，則起大業障。

是故於此人，不應起輕賤。

As, within the Sūtra of the Resolute King's Great Vehicle, the Buddha told Ānanda: "There may be a monk of dull faculties, obscured and blocked, whose own mind is not clear, unable to penetrate the very mark of any dharma, constantly holding a conception of existence. Within a dharma that is without any conception, they seize instead upon a conception of existence — giving rise to a conception of male and female, a conception of offense and obstruction, a conception of defilement, a conception of purity — one who gives rise to conceptions such as these is called one of dull faculties, whose own mind is unclear, and this itself constitutes an offense.

"Ānanda! Should a person, within every dharma, be unable to skillfully understand it, this is called failing to comprehend every dharma — that, from the very outset, their own essence, nature, and mark can never truly be apprehended at all. Such a person, not knowing this very matter, gives rise to conceptions such as these, and is thereby no different at all from an outside path.

"Ānanda! The Dharma I myself expound is, in every case, open and unobstructed, clear and pure — within it there is no offense, and no one who commits an offense either. Ānanda! "Within my own Dharma, there is no such person at all.

"Should there truly and definitely exist, within my own Dharma, a self, a sentient being, a life-force, a person, a view of self, and the like, I would not say: 'My own Dharma is open' — it is not that it fails to be open; my own Dharma, from the very outset, has forever been pure and clear.

"Ānanda! Should offense truly and definitely exist, there would, in the end, be no nirvāṇa at all, and I would then never say: my own Dharma is open.

The bodhisattva ought to reflect in this very way, never giving rise to anger toward one who has broken the precepts.

One further reflects: this very precept shall certainly lead, in the end, to abiding within unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. And why is this so?

It has been heard that even a bodhisattva certain of buddhahood has, at times, given rise to an offense: as, in a hundred thousand kalpas past, there was a bodhisattva who slandered an arhat free of every outflow, denying that they were truly an arhat at all; and it has further been heard that a bodhisattva certain of buddhahood, thirty-one kalpas before this present kalpa, once pierced a stream-enterer with a spear; and, within this present fortunate kalpa, it has been heard that there was a bodhisattva who slandered the Buddha Krakucchanda, saying: "How could such a bald-headed person possibly attain the path?"

Sentient beings such as this are difficult indeed to truly know — it is for this reason that, in this very matter, of what use would it be for me to seek such knowledge? Gain and loss, the fine and the foul, are what each being brings about for themselves and undergoes for themselves — what business is this of mine? Should I now wish to truly know such a matter, I might well harm myself in the process; the very weighing and measuring of other sentient beings is something the Buddha himself has not permitted. As the sūtra states: the Buddha told Ānanda: "Should a person weigh and measure another, they thereby harm themselves — only I myself am able to weigh and measure sentient beings, and those equal to me are likewise able to weigh and measure them."

As the verse says: "A vessel with a lid may still be empty; one without a lid may likewise be empty. A vessel with a lid may still be full; one without a lid may likewise be full.

"One should know that, within this world, there exist these very four kinds of person.

"Outward bearing and inward merit — their presence and absence are likewise so.

"Should one lack omniscience itself, how could one possibly weigh and measure another person?

"How could one, merely by beholding an outward bearing, thereby come to know their own inward merit?

"Right wisdom, possessed of a wholesome mind, is what is truly called the mark of a worthy person; yet, beholding only an outward bearing, how could one possibly know what lies within?

"One possessed within of merit and wisdom, yet outwardly showing no such bearing, wandering about unrecognized, is like fire covered over with ash.

"Should one measure what lies within by what appears outwardly, and thereby give rise to a mind of contempt, one destroys one's own body and one's own roots of goodness, and, at the end of life, falls into an evil destiny.

"One who outwardly feigns and displays a fine bearing, wandering about appearing as a worthy or a noble one, possesses only words spoken from the mouth, like thunder without any rain.

"The very place where every mind carries out its own activity is confused and mistaken, difficult indeed to truly know.

"It is for this reason that sentient beings cannot be wrongly measured and weighed; only omniscience itself is able fully to know every mind and every mental state.

"The subtle and hidden place where this activity occurs — it is for this reason that, in measuring sentient beings, the Buddha alone declares that those equal to himself alone are able to measure sentient beings.

"If the Buddha has spoken thus, who else could possibly weigh and measure another person?

"Should one behold only an outward bearing, and thereby weigh and measure their own inward merit —

"One destroys one's own roots of goodness by this very act, just as water erodes its own bank; should one fall into this very error, one thereby gives rise to a great karmic obstruction.

"It is for this reason that, toward this person, one ought never to give rise to contempt."

It is for this reason that the householder bodhisattva ought never to give rise to contempt and anger toward one who has broken the precepts.

And further, whether one upholds the precepts or has broken them, a person in white robes, not dwelling together with them, how could they possibly come to know this at all? Should I wish, in this very matter, to distinguish it with certainty, I would thereby give rise to a karmic obstruction of offense; and through the very causes and conditions of this obstruction of offense, I would undergo suffering across a thousand myriad kalpas, just as is stated within the Sūtra of No Activity.

And further, within a Great Vehicle sūtra, the Buddha told the Elder Ugra: "The householder bodhisattva, in this way, ought, toward a monk who has broken the precepts, give rise to a mind of pity: this person's own conduct is defiled, their own conduct evil and unwholesome. And why is this so? This person wears the very robe of the Thus Come One, the good, tranquil, and cessation-attaining sovereign teacher's own robe, and yet, themselves neither good nor gentle, is unable to tame their own faculties, carrying out conduct that is corrupt and destructive."

And further, the Buddha states within the sūtra: "Never hold in contempt one who has not yet studied — this is not that person's own offense, but the offense of the afflictions themselves; this person, through these very afflictions, gives rise to an unwholesome deed."

And further, the Buddhadharma possesses this very opening, and this person may yet be able to remove their own transgression and offense by themselves. Through the causes and conditions of right mindfulness, they may attain entry into the very station of the Dharma, and, having entered this certainty, abide within unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. And as the Buddha has said: "Only wisdom itself is able to break through the afflictions."

And it is further said: "One ought never wrongly to weigh and measure another person; should one weigh and measure them, one thereby harms oneself." Only the Buddha's own wisdom is able truly to comprehend a matter such as this — it is not something I myself am able to know.

Thus, toward one who has broken the precepts, one gives rise to no mind of anger or contempt whatsoever.

A note on the vessel with and without a lid

The four-part image — a lidded vessel that may be empty or full, an unlidded vessel that may likewise be empty or full — collapses the intuitive assumption that outward appearance reliably signals inward content, in either direction. A closed, formal bearing might conceal genuine attainment or nothing at all; an open, unimpressive bearing might likewise conceal either. This chapter's own repeated citation of the sixty-two views, the Sūtra of No Activity, and named historical lapses by bodhisattvas otherwise certain of buddhahood builds toward a single practical conclusion stated plainly at the end: judgment of another's inner state is categorically beyond a householder's competence, whatever their own certainty might feel like from the inside.

A note on this opening comparison

The very first pairing here — household life as clamorous and belonging to defilement, going forth as tranquil and belonging to nothing — opens what becomes, in the next section, one of the longest sustained parallel structures in the entire treatise. The scale of the claim in the preceding paragraph is worth pausing on: giving alone, however vast and sustained across inconceivable spans of time, is explicitly ranked below the mere aspiration to renounce, let alone renunciation itself — a striking statement given how much of this treatise, up through the previous fascicle, has been devoted to praising the bodhisattva's own practice of giving.

Reading it in bulk risks flattening what is, on closer attention, a carefully varied set of registers: some pairs are frankly practical (whip and rod, blade and spear), some psychological (regret, contentment, pride), some cosmic in scale (this shore and the far shore, māra's banner and the Buddha's banner), and a few startlingly intimate (a spouse as companion versus a resolute mind as companion). The cumulative effect is less a single argument than an exhaustive inventory meant to leave no possible angle of comparison unexamined.

A note on the salve and the grease

The simile of taking food only "as one applies salve to a wound, or grease to a cart" captures a specific attitude toward eating that recurs across monastic literature: food as bare functional maintenance of a vehicle, applied only enough to keep it moving, rather than as a source of pleasure to be pursued for its own sake. The six-faculties-as-six-animals image works the same way — a dog, deer, fish, snake, monkey, and bird each pulling toward its own favored terrain is a vivid picture of a mind scattered across six simultaneous cravings, each faculty tugging in its own direction, and the discipline required to bring all six to rest in one place at once.

A note on this chapter's close

The closing line of this section marks a formal transition within the treatise itself — everything specific to the householder bodhisattva's own path is now complete, and the text explicitly signals a shift toward material common to both lay and monastic practitioners alike. The final image of pure giving, defined by the giver holding no distinction whatsoever between a worthy and unworthy recipient, revisits a theme introduced in Fascicle 6's own extended treatment of the "purity of the giver, the gift, and the recipient" — the same standard reappearing here in its most condensed form.

「行忍得端嚴」者，能忍惡言、罵詈、呪誓、繫縛、刀杖、考掠、搒笞，心不動異，悉能堪受。如是忍辱，所獲果報，生於人天，常得端正；後成佛時，相好無比。

「法施知宿命」者，行法施者，能知過去無量劫事。「法施」名為種種分別聲聞乘、辟支佛乘、佛乘，解說義理。

法施果報雖有三十五，要者，知宿命。說法因緣，斷人所疑，是故得「知宿命」。

「法忍得總持」者，「法」名應「空、無相、無願」、應六波羅蜜。菩薩諸地、一切菩薩所行之法，曉了明解，心能忍持，名為「法忍」。行是忍者，則得總持。「總持」，名為如所聞經，如所讀誦，其中義趣乃至百千萬劫，終不忘失。

「思惟得智慧」者，「思惟」，名為籌量善法，分別義趣。是故，能得今世後世利益。

「不曲心得正念」者，「不曲」，名為質直無諂。修行此法，則於一切法中，得堅固念。

「重法法則堅」者，若人尊重、恭敬於法，法則堅固。「堅法」，名為所受持、習學之法，自然牢堅，不可動轉。後成佛時，多有菩薩、聲聞弟子，住是堅固法，無能障其所受法者。

又，「堅」名為法得久住。

「不障得守護」者，若人說法及人聽法，不橫與作障礙之事，後成佛時，諸天世人，共守護法；未得佛道，常能護持諸佛正法；諸佛滅後，守護遺法，乃能至於後佛出世。以是因緣，菩薩、聲聞皆應盡心善守護法。

「供養法值佛」者，「供養」，名為恭敬諸法。法施法會，敬心供養說法之人，施設法座，起立禪坊，莊挍嚴飾講法之處；如是深心愛樂，供養法因緣故，得值諸佛。

「以信解捨諸難」者，「信」，名於諸善法深生欲樂；以是法故，得離八難。「解」者，能滅諸罪，能於諸善法中，以心力故隨意而解。如十「一切入」，隨意所解。

若人多有信解力者，能滅無始生死已來無量罪惡。如先〈悔過品〉中說。

復次——修空不放逸，不貪得成利，隨說滅煩惱，燈施得天眼。

「修空不放逸」者，「修」有二種：得修、行修。

修空力故，信有為法皆是虛誑，亦不住空。諸法無定，是故常自攝撿，心不放逸。

「不貪得成利」者，「貪」，名於他物中，生貪取心。若除是事，所求皆成，所願皆滿。

「隨說滅煩惱」者，隨有所說，身即行之，則斷煩惱，於諸事中，皆如說行。世世已來，諸煩惱氣常熏其心，則皆除滅，轉諸煩惱、惡氣習性。

「燈施得天眼」者，若人然燈供養佛、聲聞、辟支佛及塔像、舍利，以是因緣，得天眼報。

復次——樂施天耳報，以正願淨土；乘施獲神足，攝法得具僧。

「樂施得天耳報」者，於大會作諸音樂供養於佛，得天耳報。

「乘施得神足」者，「乘」名輦輿、象馬等乘。復有人言：以履屣等施，亦得神足。

「以正願淨土」者，隨以所願，取清淨土。若金、銀、頗梨、珊瑚、琥珀、車璩、碼瑙無量眾寶，清淨國土。

「攝法得具僧」者，若菩薩具足行四攝法得具足僧，以布施、愛語、利益、同事，攝取眾生故；後成佛時，得清淨具足無量菩薩僧及聲聞僧，如阿彌陀佛有二種僧清淨具足。願具足者，如先十願中說。

復次——利益眾生故，一切所愛敬，平等心無二，得為最勝者。

若菩薩以身口意業，有所作，皆為利益、安樂眾生。是故眾生，皆悉敬愛。

若菩薩於諸眾生怨親中人，行平等心，不捨一切眾生，以是業報，得為「最勝」。

「勝」名能勝貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡、一切煩惱惡法，故名為佛。

Practices Shared in Common Chapter

Question: You have said that you would explain the dharma of the practice shared in common by both householder and renunciant bodhisattva — please explain this now.

Answer: "Patient endurance, the giving of the Dharma, forbearance with regard to the Dharma, contemplation, a dharma free of crookedness, reverence for the Dharma, a dharma that obstructs nothing, making offering to the Dharma, faith and understanding, cultivating emptiness, freedom from craving and envy, practicing exactly as one has spoken, the giving of lamplight, the giving of music, the giving of a vehicle, right vows, the dharma of gathering others, reflecting on benefiting and bringing peace to sentient beings, and an equal mind toward all. This is the essential dharma of the practice shared in common by householder and renunciant alike."

It is for this reason that the verse states: "Practicing patient endurance, the body becomes dignified and fine; giving the Dharma, one comes to know past lives; forbearance with the Dharma attains the dhāraṇī; contemplation obtains wisdom; toward every dharma, holding no crookedness, one constantly attains right mindfulness."

As for "practicing patient endurance, the body becomes dignified and fine": being able to endure harsh speech, reviling, cursing, bondage, blade and club, torture, and beating, one's own mind never wavering or changing, able to bear every one of these fully. Patient endurance such as this obtains, as its own fruit and recompense, rebirth among humans or devas, constantly possessing a dignified and fine bearing; and, upon later accomplishing buddhahood, one's own marks and signs become beyond compare.

As for "giving the Dharma, one comes to know past lives": one who practices the giving of the Dharma is able to know matters of immeasurable kalpas past. "The giving of the Dharma" refers to distinguishing, in every kind of way, the śrāvaka vehicle, the pratyekabuddha vehicle, and the buddha vehicle, and explaining their own true meaning.

Though the fruit and recompense of the giving of the Dharma is of thirty-five kinds, its essential one is knowing one's own past lives. Through the very cause and condition of expounding the Dharma, severing every doubt held by others, one thereby attains "knowledge of past lives."

As for "forbearance with the Dharma attains the dhāraṇī": "the Dharma" here refers to that which accords with emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, and that which accords with the six pāramitās. Fully comprehending and clearly understanding the bodhisattva's own grounds, and the very dharma practiced by every bodhisattva, one's own mind able to endure and uphold it, this is called "forbearance with the Dharma." One who practices this very forbearance thereby attains the dhāraṇī. "The dhāraṇī" refers to whatever sūtra one has heard, whatever one has recited, its own true meaning never forgotten even across a hundred thousand myriad kalpas.

As for "contemplation obtains wisdom": "contemplation" refers to weighing and considering wholesome dharmas, distinguishing their own true meaning. It is for this reason that one is able to obtain benefit in this present life and in lives to come.

As for "holding no crookedness of mind, one attains right mindfulness": "no crookedness" refers to being upright and honest, free of flattery. Cultivating and practicing this very dharma, one thereby attains, within every dharma, a firm and resolute mindfulness.

Furthermore: "Honoring the Dharma, the Dharma thereby becomes firm; obstructing nothing, one thereby attains its own protection; making offering to the Dharma, one thereby encounters the buddhas; through faith and understanding, one departs from every difficulty."

As for "honoring the Dharma, the Dharma thereby becomes firm": should a person honor and revere the Dharma, this very Dharma thereby becomes firm. "A firm dharma" refers to a dharma one has received, upheld, and studied that naturally becomes secure and firm, unable to be shaken or overturned. Upon later accomplishing buddhahood, there shall be many bodhisattva and śrāvaka disciples abiding within this very firm dharma, and none shall be able to obstruct the dharma they themselves have received.

And further, "firm" refers to this Dharma enduring for a long time.

As for "obstructing nothing, one thereby attains its own protection": should a person who expounds the Dharma, and a person who listens to the Dharma, never wrongly create any obstruction toward it, then, upon later accomplishing buddhahood, every deva and every person in this world shall together guard and protect this Dharma; and, not yet having attained buddhahood themselves, one shall constantly be able to guard and uphold the true Dharma of every buddha; and, after every buddha has passed into extinction, guarding and protecting the Dharma they have left behind, until the appearance of the next buddha in the world. Through this very cause and condition, both bodhisattva and śrāvaka alike ought, with their whole mind, skillfully to guard and protect the Dharma.

As for "making offering to the Dharma, one thereby encounters the buddhas": "offering" refers to showing reverence toward every dharma. Through the giving of the Dharma and Dharma assemblies, showing a reverent mind in making offering to one who expounds the Dharma, setting out a Dharma seat, erecting a meditation hall, adorning and decorating the very place where the Dharma is taught — through this deep mind of love and delight, and through the very cause and condition of this offering to the Dharma, one comes to encounter every buddha.

As for "through faith and understanding, one departs from every difficulty": "faith" refers to giving rise to a deep desire and delight toward every wholesome dharma; through this very dharma, one is able to depart from the eight difficulties. "Understanding" refers to being able to extinguish every offense, and, within every wholesome dharma, through the very power of one's own mind, understanding it according to one's own will — just as, with the ten totalities, one comes to understand each according to one's own will.

Should a person possess much power of faith and understanding, they are able to extinguish immeasurable offense and evil accumulated since beginningless birth and death, exactly as already explained within the earlier Chapter of Repentance.

Furthermore: "Cultivating emptiness, one is free of heedlessness; free of craving, one accomplishes benefit; practicing exactly as one has spoken, one extinguishes the afflictions; giving lamplight, one attains the heavenly eye."

As for "cultivating emptiness, one is free of heedlessness": "cultivation" is of two kinds — cultivation that attains, and cultivation that practices.

Through the very power of cultivating emptiness, one holds faith that every conditioned dharma is entirely illusory and false, and yet does not abide even within emptiness itself. Since no dharma is ever fixed or certain, one constantly restrains and examines oneself, one's own mind free of heedlessness.

As for "free of craving, one accomplishes benefit": "craving" refers to giving rise, toward another's own possessions, to a mind of craving and grasping. Should this very matter be removed, whatever one seeks is accomplished, and whatever one wishes is fulfilled.

As for "practicing exactly as one has spoken, one extinguishes the afflictions": whatever one has spoken, one's own body immediately carries it out, thereby severing the afflictions; within every matter, one practices exactly as one has spoken. Across every past lifetime, the very scent of the afflictions has constantly permeated one's own mind — through this practice, it is entirely extinguished and removed, transforming the afflictions and their own evil habitual nature.

As for "giving lamplight, one attains the heavenly eye": should a person kindle a lamp in offering to the Buddha, to the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, and to a stūpa, an image, or relics, through this very cause and condition, one attains, as its recompense, the heavenly eye.

Furthermore: "Delighting in giving music, one attains, as its recompense, the heavenly ear; through right vows, one attains a pure land; giving a vehicle, one attains supernormal locomotion; gathering others through the four means, one attains a complete assembly."

As for "delighting in giving music, one attains, as its recompense, the heavenly ear": within a great assembly, performing every kind of music in offering to the Buddha, one attains, as its recompense, the heavenly ear.

As for "giving a vehicle, one attains supernormal locomotion": "a vehicle" refers to a palanquin, a carriage, an elephant, a horse, and every such conveyance. There are, further, those who say: giving even sandals and the like likewise attains this same supernormal locomotion.

As for "through right vows, one attains a pure land": according to whatever vow one makes, one thereby obtains this pure land. Whether of gold, silver, crystal, coral, amber, mother-of-pearl, or agate, and every kind of immeasurable jewel besides, one attains a pure and undefiled land.

As for "gathering others through the four means, one attains a complete assembly": should the bodhisattva fully practice the four means of gathering others, they thereby attain a complete assembly — through giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and cooperative engagement, gathering in sentient beings; upon later accomplishing buddhahood, one attains an assembly, pure and complete, of immeasurable bodhisattvas and śrāvakas, just as Amitābha Buddha possesses these two kinds of assembly, pure and complete. As for the completeness of one's own vows, this is as already explained within the earlier chapter on the ten vows.

Furthermore: "For the sake of benefiting sentient beings, one is loved and revered by all; holding an equal mind free of any duality, one attains the status of the very foremost."

Should the bodhisattva, through the karma of body, speech, and mind, undertake any action whatsoever, it is, in every case, for the benefit and peace of sentient beings. It is for this reason that sentient beings, in every case, hold this bodhisattva in reverence and love.

Should the bodhisattva, toward every sentient being, whether hostile or intimate, practice an equal mind, never abandoning any sentient being whatsoever, through this very karmic recompense, they attain the status of "the very foremost."

"Foremost" refers to being able to overcome craving, anger, delusion, and every evil dharma of the afflictions — it is for this reason that this status is called buddhahood.

A note on this catalogue's own structure

This chapter's own opening list of nineteen shared practices, each subsequently unpacked one item at a time and paired with its own specific karmic fruit, follows a compositional pattern familiar from earlier fascicles of this treatise — name the full set first, then work through it systematically. What distinguishes this particular list is the specificity of its pairings: giving a lamp yields the heavenly eye, giving music yields the heavenly ear, giving a vehicle yields supernormal locomotion — a symbolic logic in which the nature of the gift itself prefigures the nature of its eventual fruit, rather than an arbitrary system of reward.

Firmly upholding, without ever relinquishing, every dharma one has received, one thereby attains the mark of a firmly planted foot.

Possessing this very mark, none are able to overturn or shake one.

Constantly cultivating the four means of gathering others — giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and cooperative engagement — one thereby attains the mark of webbed hands and feet.

Possessing this very mark, one swiftly gathers a great assembly of people.

Through fragrant, delicious, and soft food and drink, given in offering to others and to every venerable person, providing whatever they require, one thereby attains the mark of soft hands and feet, and the mark of seven full places.

Possessing this very mark, one obtains much fragrant, delicious, and soft food and drink.

Through rescuing one who was to die, and increasing another's own lifespan, and further receiving the precept against killing, one thereby attains the mark of long, slender fingers, the mark of a full heel, and the mark of a great, upright body.

Possessing this very mark, one's own lifespan is long and enduring.

Through every wholesome dharma one has received continuing to increase without ever being lost, one thereby attains the mark of a high instep, and the mark of hair curling upward to the right.

Possessing this very mark, every merit one attains never retrogresses or is lost.

Through teaching one's own skill and every scripture to others without any reluctance, and through giving sandals and the like, one thereby attains the mark of antelope calves.

Possessing this very mark, whatever one studies and cultivates is accomplished swiftly, exactly as one wishes.

Through, when one comes to make a request, holding back nothing whatsoever, one thereby attains the mark of long, even arms.

Possessing this very mark, one is able to attain great strength, and able to give greatly.

Through being skillfully able to reconcile others, never allowing sentient beings to become estranged from their own kin, and, should estrangement occur, restoring this very harmony, one thereby attains the mark of a concealed organ.

Possessing this very mark, one gains many disciples.

Through giving fine, pure and clean clothing, bedding, towers, and dwellings, one thereby attains the mark of golden color, and the mark of thin skin.

Possessing this very mark, one obtains fine, pure and clean clothing, bedding, towers, and dwellings.

Through, according to what is fitting, making offering to one's own preceptor, one's own instructor, one's own parents and siblings, and every venerable and worthy person, being able to protect and guard them, one thereby attains the mark of a single hair growing from each and every pore, the mark of hair curling to the right, and the mark of white hair adorning the face.

Possessing this very mark, none are equal to oneself.

Through speech marked by conscience and shame, speech that accords with others, and kind speech, one thereby attains the mark of an upper body like a lion's own, and the mark of round, full shoulders.

Possessing this very mark, whoever beholds one delights in the sight, never growing weary of it.

Through providing medicine and food and drink to the sick, personally attending to them, one thereby attains the mark of full underarms, and the mark of tasting every flavor to the fullest.

Possessing this very mark, one's own body suffers little illness.

Through giving gardens and groves, sweet fruit, bridges, flourishing trees, pools and wells, food and drink, flowers and fragrance, garlands, dwellings, and the erecting of stūpas and rest-houses of merit, and, in giving alongside the assembly, being able to bring forth much in the way of goods, one thereby attains the mark of a body like the nyagrodha tree, and the mark of the uṣṇīṣa.

Possessing this very mark, one attains nobility and mastery.

Through cultivating, across a vast span of time, truthful speech and gentle speech, one thereby attains the mark of a broad, long tongue, and the mark of a Brahmā-like voice.

Possessing this very mark, one attains the five meritorious qualities of voice. These five meritorious qualities of voice are: a voice easily understood, a voice that never wearies the listener, a voice deep and far-reaching, a voice pleasing to the ear, and a voice that never scatters.

Through, across a vast span of time, speaking truthfully and never speaking idle, frivolous words, one thereby attains the mark of a lion's own cheeks.

Possessing this very mark, whatever one says is certainly believed and accepted.

Through, having once made an offering, never afterward showing contempt, providing according to what is needed, one thereby attains the mark of white teeth, and the mark of even teeth.

Possessing this very mark, one attains a pure, harmonious, and like-minded following.

Through, across a vast span of time, speaking truthfully, never slandering or defaming, one thereby attains the mark of forty teeth, and the mark of close-set teeth.

Possessing this very mark, one's own following remains harmonious and united, unable to be broken apart.

Through a deep mind of love and affection, beholding sentient beings with a gentle countenance, free of craving, anger, and delusion, one thereby attains the mark of eyes the deep blue-black of collyrium, and the mark of eyelashes like a bull's own.

Possessing this very mark, everyone who beholds oneself feels nothing but love and reverence.

A note on this correspondence

The pairing of each mark with its own specific cause makes a claim that runs through much of this treatise: nothing about a buddha's appearance is arbitrary or merely decorative. A concealed, well-proportioned body results from reconciling estranged relatives; a golden radiance results from giving fine clothing and shelter; a voice with five distinct qualities results from a lifetime of truthful, gentle speech. The thirty-two marks, read this way, are less a checklist for recognizing a buddha than a complete portrait of an entire life of specific, nameable conduct, made visible on the body itself.
