Vajragarbha's Own Vow, and the Twelve Ascetic Practices

Each of the ten wholesome and unwholesome paths receives its own specific fruit, then the bodhisattva enters the second ground through an extraordinary act of compassion — the Ten Grounds Sūtra's own Vajragarbha quoted at length, taking up sentient beings sunk in every named affliction one by one. The precepts themselves are analyzed sixty-five ways down to a supreme precept beyond even the concept of a precept, and the fascicle closes with all twelve ascetic practices, each cited with its own ten specific benefits.

The General and Specific Fruits of the Ten Paths

Ru-Yi Translation

Guarding the Precepts Chapter

This bodhisattva, in this way, practices every one of the wholesome paths. Toward both the wholesome and unwholesome paths, in their own general aspect and particular aspect, they distinguish and know each of them individually, each possessing two kinds of fruit and recompense.

As for the ten wholesome karmic paths — the "general fruit and recompense" refers to being reborn among the devas, or being reborn among humans.

As for the "particular fruit and recompense": the wholesome conduct of departing from killing possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, a long life; second, little illness.

The wholesome conduct of departing from robbery and theft possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, great wealth; second, sole possession of one's own property.

The wholesome conduct of departing from sexual misconduct possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, a spouse who is chaste and virtuous; second, never being violated by an outsider.

The wholesome conduct of departing from false speech possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, never being slandered or defamed by others; second, never being deceived by others.

The wholesome conduct of departing from divisive speech possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, attaining a fine circle of kin; second, never having this circle broken apart by others.

The wholesome conduct of departing from harsh speech possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, hearing whatever sound one delights in exactly as one wishes; second, being free of contention and dispute.

The wholesome conduct of departing from idle speech possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, others accept one's own words with trust; second, whatever one says is taken as resolute and certain.

The wholesome conduct of freedom from craving and grasping possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, contentment; second, few desires.

The wholesome conduct of freedom from anger and affliction possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, constantly encountering another's own good fortune, wherever one is reborn; second, taking no delight in harming or afflicting sentient beings.

The wholesome conduct of right view possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, departing from flattery and crookedness; second, whatever one sees is pure.

The ten unwholesome paths are likewise so: as for the "general fruit and recompense," the superior degree of this conduct causes one to fall into hell, the middling degree causes one to fall among the animals, and the inferior degree causes one to fall among the hungry ghosts.

As for the "particular fruit and recompense": the unwholesome conduct of killing possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, a short life; second, much illness.

The unwholesome conduct of robbery and theft possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, poverty; second, the loss of one's own wealth.

The unwholesome conduct of sexual misconduct possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, obtaining an ugly, corrupt spouse, further lacking chastity and virtue; second, being violated by another.

The unwholesome conduct of false speech possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, being slandered and defamed by others; second, being deceived by others.

The unwholesome conduct of divisive speech possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, obtaining an evil circle of kin; second, this very circle being easily broken apart.

The unwholesome conduct of harsh speech possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, hearing only evil sounds; second, constant contention and dispute.

The unwholesome conduct of idle speech possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, one's own words are never accepted with trust; second, whatever one says has neither beginning nor end.

The unwholesome conduct of craving and grasping possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, a mind never content; second, abundant desire, never satiated.

The unwholesome conduct of anger and affliction possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, an evil nature; second, delighting in afflicting sentient beings.

The unwholesome conduct of wrong view possesses two kinds of fruit and recompense: first, a mind of flattery and crookedness; second, falling into wrong view.

A note on this paired symmetry

This section's own perfectly mirrored structure — every wholesome deed's own two-part fruit answered by its unwholesome counterpart's own two-part fruit, in the identical order — makes the causal logic of karma vivid through direct opposition rather than abstract assertion: chastity guarded produces a chaste spouse, while its violation produces one who is not; harsh speech heard becomes harsh speech spoken about oneself in turn. This closes the treatise's own extended technical treatment of the ten paths begun in Fascicle 14, and the chapter now turns to the compassion that carries the bodhisattva from the first ground into the second.

Loving the Dharma, and Compassion Surpassing the First Ground

Chinese Source

知已愛樂法，於法心不動；於諸眾生中，慈悲心轉勝。

「愛法」者：但愛於法，更無勝事。此中法者，先說十善業道。

「樂法」者：但樂於法，更無餘事。

「於法心不動」者：乃至失命，終不捨法。

菩薩行如是法，於眾生中慈悲轉勝。

初地中雖有慈悲，不及此地，以通達罪福業因緣故，眾生可愍，皆屬於業，不得自在，則無瞋恨憎恚之心。

如是行者慈悲轉勝。作是念：咄哉諸眾生，深墮於邪見；我應說正見，令得入正道。

菩薩通達罪、福業因緣，於諸眾生深行慈悲。

作是念：「眾生可愍，不知諸法實相故，多行妄想，生諸邪見；因邪見故，起諸煩惱；因煩惱故，而起諸業；起業因緣故，輪轉生死。我先發心求阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，為度眾生故，當說正見。是諸眾生，是我應度，今當為說正見，令入真道，使得度脫。」

如是念已，知諸眾生有種種煩惱，所謂：觀所起煩惱，及諸煩惱垢；種種黑惡業，受種種苦惱。

愍念諸眾生，多有所闕少；種種觀察已，是皆如我有。

即時以悲心，方便發大願；云何令眾生，得滅是諸苦。

「煩惱、煩惱垢」者：使所攝名為煩惱，纏所攝名為垢。

「使所攝煩惱」者：貪、瞋、慢、無明、身見、邊見、見取、戒取、邪見、疑；是十根本，隨三界見諦、思惟所斷分別故，名九十八使。

「非使所攝」者：不信、無慚、無愧、諂曲、戲、侮、堅、執、懈怠、退沒、睡眠、佷、戾、慳、嫉、憍、不忍食、不知足。亦以三界見諦、思惟所斷分別故，有一百九十六纏垢。

有人言：煩惱在深心，垢在淺心。

有人言：諸障蓋名為纏垢，餘皆名煩惱。

「黑惡業」者：即是七不善業道及貪取、瞋惱、邪見相應思，能生苦報。

「種種苦惱」者：身中種種惡事名為苦，心中種種惡事名為惱。

又今世苦名為苦，後墮惡道名為惱。

「多有所少」者：或諸根支體、或資生所須、或信戒等諸功德不具故名為少。

餘句易解，如偈中所說，不復須釋。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Knowing this, loving and delighting in the Dharma, this mind unmoving toward the Dharma; toward every sentient being, this mind of loving-kindness and compassion grows ever more supreme."

As for "loving the Dharma": this refers to loving only the Dharma, with nothing else more supreme. "The Dharma" here refers to the ten wholesome karmic paths already explained.

As for "delighting in the Dharma": this refers to delighting only in the Dharma, with nothing else besides.

As for "this mind unmoving toward the Dharma": even at the cost of one's own life, one never, under any circumstance, relinquishes this Dharma.

The bodhisattva, practicing this very dharma, thereby grows ever more supreme in loving-kindness and compassion toward sentient beings.

Though the first ground already possesses loving-kindness and compassion, it does not reach the level found within this very ground, since one now penetrates the very causes and conditions of offense and merit within karma — sentient beings, worthy of pity, belong entirely to their own karma, unable to govern themselves, and one thereby holds no mind of anger, resentment, or hatred toward them.

One who practices in this way thereby grows ever more supreme in loving-kindness and compassion. They reflect thus: "Alas, these sentient beings, sunk so deeply within wrong view — I ought to expound right view, causing them to enter the right path."

The bodhisattva, penetrating the very causes and conditions of offense and merit within karma, practices deep loving-kindness and compassion toward every sentient being.

They reflect thus: "Sentient beings, worthy of pity, not knowing the true mark of every dharma, thereby give rise to much false imagination, giving rise to every wrong view; through this very wrong view, every affliction arises; through this very affliction, every karma thereby arises; through the very cause and condition of this arising karma, they revolve endlessly through birth and death. I have already given rise, from the very outset, to this aspiration seeking unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, for the sake of liberating sentient beings — I ought therefore to expound right view. These very sentient beings are exactly those I myself ought to liberate; I shall now expound right view for their sake, causing them to enter the genuine path, and thereby attain liberation."

Having reflected in this way, knowing that sentient beings possess afflictions of every kind, namely: "Contemplating the very affliction that has arisen, and every defilement of the afflictions; every kind of dark, evil karma, undergoing every kind of suffering and affliction.

"Feeling pity for every sentient being, so abundantly lacking; having contemplated this in every way, I know that all of this is likewise present within myself.

"At that very moment, through this mind of compassion, giving rise to this great vow, through skillful means: how might I cause sentient beings to extinguish every one of these very sufferings?"

As for "affliction, and the defilement of the afflictions": what is subsumed within an underlying tendency is called affliction; what is subsumed within an active entanglement is called defilement.

As for "affliction subsumed within an underlying tendency": craving, anger, arrogance, ignorance, the view of self, the view of extremes, the grasping of views, the grasping of precepts and vows, wrong view, and doubt — these ten roots, distinguished according to what is severed through the vision of truth or through further contemplation across the three realms, are called the ninety-eight underlying tendencies.

As for "what is not subsumed within an underlying tendency": faithlessness, lack of conscience, lack of shame, flattery and crookedness, frivolity, contempt, stubbornness, attachment, indolence, retreat and sinking, drowsiness, obstinacy, harshness, stinginess, envy, arrogance, intolerance toward food, and discontent. These too, distinguished according to what is severed through the vision of truth or through further contemplation across the three realms, amount to one hundred ninety-six active entanglements and defilements.

There are those who say: affliction dwells within the deep mind, while defilement dwells within the shallow mind.

There are those who say: every obstruction and hindrance is called an active entanglement and defilement, while every remaining affliction is called simply affliction.

As for "dark, evil karma": this refers precisely to the seven unwholesome karmic paths, together with the volition associated with craving and grasping, anger and affliction, and wrong view — able to give rise to a fruit of suffering.

As for "every kind of suffering and affliction": every kind of evil matter within the body is called suffering; every kind of evil matter within the mind is called affliction.

And further, suffering in this present life is called suffering; falling into an evil destiny afterward is called affliction.

As for "abundantly lacking": whether lacking in one's own faculties and limbs, in one's own necessities of livelihood, or in one's own merit of faith, precepts, and the like, remaining incomplete — this is what is called lacking.

Every remaining phrase is easily understood, exactly as stated within the verse, and requires no further explanation.

A note on ninety-eight and one hundred ninety-six

The technical enumeration here — ten root afflictions distinguished across three realms and two modes of severing (through the vision of truth versus through further contemplation) into ninety-eight underlying tendencies, and eighteen further mental factors doubled into one hundred ninety-six active entanglements — belongs to the standard Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma classification of defilements, cited here as background the reader is assumed to recognize rather than derived from first principles. What distinguishes this section's own use of it is the pivot that follows immediately: this technical apparatus exists only to specify, with precision, exactly what the bodhisattva's own compassion must actually address in each sentient being it takes as its object.

The Great Vow for Suffering Sentient Beings

Chinese Source

如是思惟已，眾生甚可愍，墮在於二乘；我當為發願，令住於大乘。

是事如此，《十地經》中金剛藏菩薩自說：「是菩薩離十不善業道，亦令眾生住十善業道。為眾生深求——勝心、好心、樂心、憐愍心、慈悲心、利益心、守護心、我所有心、大師心、攝取心、受取心。」作是念：

此諸眾生，甚可憐愍，墮種種邪意、邪見，行邪險道；我今應令住在真實正見道中。

是諸眾生，種類不同，互相諍競。常懷忿恚，瞋惱熾盛；然我當令住無上大慈。

是諸眾生，無有厭足，貪求他利，邪命自活；我當令住清淨身、口、意業。

是諸眾生，在貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡因緣中，常起種種煩惱結使，而不方便求欲自出；我當滅其諸苦惱事，令住無苦惱處。

是諸眾生，為無明所翳，入黑闇稠林，不能自出。離智慧明，入在諸見險惡道中；我應救之，使得無礙智慧之眼；以是慧眼不隨他人，於一切法知如實相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having reflected in this way, sentient beings are exceedingly worthy of pity, falling into these two lesser vehicles — I ought therefore to give rise to this vow, causing them to abide within the Great Vehicle instead.

This very matter is so; within the Ten Grounds Sūtra, the Bodhisattva Vajragarbha himself declares: "This bodhisattva, having departed from the ten unwholesome karmic paths, further causes sentient beings to abide within the ten wholesome karmic paths. For the sake of sentient beings, deeply seeking — a supreme mind, a good mind, a delighted mind, a mind of pity, a mind of loving-kindness and compassion, a mind of benefit, a mind of protection, a mind that claims them as one's own, a mind of a great teacher, a mind that gathers them in, a mind that receives them." They reflect thus:

"These very sentient beings are exceedingly worthy of pity, falling into every kind of wrong intent and wrong view, walking a wrong and perilous road; I ought now to cause them to abide within the very road of genuine, right view instead.

"These very sentient beings, of every different kind, contend with one another. Constantly harboring resentment and anger, this very anger and affliction blazing fiercely; and yet I shall cause them to abide within the unsurpassed, great loving-kindness instead.

"These very sentient beings, never satiated, craving and seeking another's own gain, sustaining themselves through a wrongful livelihood; I shall cause them to abide within a pure karma of body, speech, and mind instead.

"These very sentient beings, dwelling within the very causes and conditions of craving, anger, and delusion, constantly giving rise to every kind of afflictive fetter, and yet, lacking any skillful means, never seeking to escape it themselves; I shall extinguish every one of these very sufferings and afflictions, causing them to abide within a place free of suffering and affliction instead.

"These very sentient beings, obscured by ignorance, entering a dense, dark forest, unable to escape it themselves. Departing from the very radiance of wisdom, entering instead into the perilous, evil road of every wrong view; I ought to rescue them, causing them to attain the very unobstructed eye of wisdom — through this very eye of wisdom, following no other, coming to know the true mark of every dharma."

A note on Vajragarbha's own eleven minds

The eleven distinct minds Vajragarbha names before taking up sentient beings' own suffering one image at a time — supreme, good, delighted, pitying, loving-compassionate, beneficial, protective, claiming, teacherly, gathering, and receiving — establish that compassion here is not a single undifferentiated feeling but a deliberately varied set of orientations, each suited to a different aspect of the relationship between bodhisattva and sentient being. This passage, quoted directly from the Ten Grounds Sūtra (Daśabhūmika Sūtra), marks this treatise's own explicit citation of the very scripture it takes as its subject — the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā Śāstra is, after all, a commentary on this sūtra's own account of the ten grounds.

Vajragarbha's Own Extended Vow, in Full

Chinese Source

是諸眾生，墮在生死長流，欲墮地獄、畜生、餓鬼、阿修羅坑，入邪曲網中。種種煩惱惡草所覆，無有導師，不生出心。道言非道，非道言道。魔民怨賊常共隨逐，無有善師。隨順魔意，遠離佛法；如是眾生，我應令度此諸生死險惡道，得住無畏、無衰一切智慧城。

是諸眾生，為欲流、有流、見流、無明流所漂，種種罪業濤波所覆。沒在愛河隨生死波浪，為洄澓所轉不能自出，為欲覺、瞋覺、惱覺鹹水淹爛，為身見羅剎之所執持。入五欲深林為喜染所著吹，在我慢陸地，甚可憐愍，無洲無救。於六入空聚落，不能動發，無善度者；如是眾生，我今應以大悲牢堅智慧之船，載至諸安隱、無怖畏一切智洲。

是諸眾生，多苦可愍，閉在生死憂悲苦惱牢獄。多懷貪恚、愛憎，墮四顛倒，為四大毒蛇所害，為五陰怨家所殘，喜染詐賊所陷，在六入空聚受無量苦惱；我應破其生死牢獄，令得自在無礙涅槃，安隱快樂。

是諸眾生，甚可憐愍，狹劣小心，樂於少利，縮沒無有一切智心。設求出者，則樂聲聞、辟支佛乘；我應令得大心，使樂佛廣大之法。

Ru-Yi Translation

"These very sentient beings, falling into this vast, flowing current of birth and death, about to fall into the pit of hell, the animal realm, the hungry ghosts, or the asuras, entering into a wrongful, crooked net. Covered over by every kind of evil weed of affliction, possessing no guide, giving rise to no mind of escape. Calling the path 'not the path,' and what is not the path 'the path.' Constantly pursued by māra's own troops and hostile bandits, possessing no good teacher. According with māra's own intent, departing far from the Buddhadharma; sentient beings such as this — I ought to bring them across this very perilous and evil road of birth and death, causing them to abide within the city of fearless, undiminished omniscience instead.

"These very sentient beings, swept along by the current of desire, the current of existence, the current of views, and the current of ignorance, covered over by the surging waves of every kind of offensive karma. Submerged within the very river of craving, following the waves of birth and death, spun about by its own whirlpools, unable to escape it themselves; scalded and corroded by the brackish water of desirous thought, angry thought, and afflictive thought; seized and held fast by the very rākṣasa of the view of self. Entering the deep forest of the five desires, blown about, attached to and defiled by delight; standing upon the dry land of arrogance, exceedingly worthy of pity, with no island and no rescue in sight. Within the empty village of the six sense-bases, unable to stir or set forth, with no one to genuinely liberate them; sentient beings such as this — I ought now, through the very firm and secure vessel of great compassion and wisdom, to carry them across to the island of peace, security, and fearless omniscience.

"These very sentient beings, abundant in suffering and worthy of pity, confined within the very prison of birth and death, sorrow, grief, suffering, and affliction. Harboring much craving and anger, love and hatred, falling into the four inversions, harmed by the four great venomous serpents, mangled by the hostile enemy of the five aggregates, ensnared by the deceptive bandit of delight and defilement, undergoing immeasurable suffering and affliction within the empty village of the six sense-bases; I ought to break open this very prison of birth and death, causing them to attain free and unobstructed nirvāṇa, peace, security, and genuine joy.

"These very sentient beings, exceedingly worthy of pity, their own mind narrow, inferior, and small, delighting in only the slightest benefit, shrinking and sinking, without any mind of omniscience whatsoever. Should they even seek to escape at all, they delight only in the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha vehicle; I ought to cause them to attain this great mind instead, so that they might delight in the Buddha's own vast and expansive dharma."

A note on this cascade of images

This extended passage compounds image upon image without pause — a net, a river with its own currents and whirlpools, brackish water, a rākṣasa, a forest, dry land, an empty village, a prison, four venomous serpents, a hostile enemy, a deceptive bandit — each drawn from this treatise's own established vocabulary (the five aggregates as bandit and the six sense-bases as empty village recur from Fascicle 14's own similes) and layered together into a single unbroken description of sentient beings' own predicament. The rhetorical accumulation itself performs the very scale of suffering the vow responds to: no single image could carry this weight alone, so the passage refuses to let any one metaphor stand by itself before piling on the next.

Entering the Second Ground

Chinese Source

菩薩如是行，則得持戒力，善知起善業，使令得增長，是則為佛子，深入離垢地。

「持戒力」者：一心清淨，具足十善道戒，則得修集福德力。

「能起善業」者：善知自生增長善道，亦令他眾生深入者，所行轉遠，盡其邊底。

「佛子」者：能隨法行，名為佛子。

於初地始生，至二地增長。

A note on openness and restriction

The claim that "the Buddhadharma possesses both openness and restriction, unlike the outside paths" identifies a specific quality this treatise values in its own tradition's approach to rules: rather than an absolute prohibition that would make attending a sick fellow monk a violation of one's own forest vow, the practice admits ten specific, named exceptions, while insisting that even these exceptions never actually abandon the underlying thought of emptiness the forest-dwelling was cultivating in the first place. The rule and its own exceptions are shown to serve the identical purpose rather than working against one another.

A note on the thirty-four matters told to Ugra

The extensive thirty-four-item list of what the forest-dwelling bodhisattva actually seeks — recalling this treatise's own earlier citation of the Buddha's teaching to the householder Ugra back in Fascicle 7 — reframes forest-dwelling as goal-directed rather than an end in itself: solitude is worthless if it merely produces a creature no better disciplined than "deer, monkeys, and birds" also found in that same forest, and the entire list exists to specify exactly what genuine practice, as opposed to mere physical isolation, must actually accomplish there. The closing reflection on birds and beasts inverts the comparison one might expect — rather than claiming superiority over animals, the passage uses their own evident fearlessness, despite practicing nothing wholesome at all, as a genuine reproach: a practitioner's own wisdom ought to achieve at least what mere unreflective animal courage already manages.

A note on dwelling like grass and trees

The passage's own final image — the bodhisattva dwelling within the forest hermitage exactly "as grass and trees do," giving rise to a thought of themselves as a mere reflection, echo, or illusion — reframes the entire practice of forest-dwelling one last time: physical solitude was never truly the point, since even a genuine wilderness dwelling remains "empty" if craving attachment to self persists there unaddressed, while the deepest sense of "forest hermitage" turns out to require no particular location at all — severing contention and holding no attachment to self is itself what the term ultimately names, closing the loop opened at the start of this section's own inquiry into what dwelling here actually means.

Ru-Yi Translation

One ought never to delight in dwelling within a clamorous place, whether among householders or renunciants. Every buddha forbids the monk dwelling within the forest hermitage from joining together with either householder or renunciant.

Question: Does the Buddha forbid joining together with every person whatsoever?

Answer: Not so! "The Buddha permits four kinds of joining together, and forbids every other kind — it is for this reason that one ought to draw near to these four, and depart far from every other."

The bodhisattva, dwelling within the forest hermitage, is permitted to join together with these four assemblies: namely, entering an assembly to listen to the Dharma, teaching and transforming sentient beings, making offering to the Buddha, and never departing from this very mind of omniscience while joining together with others. It is for this reason that only these four matters of joining together are permitted, and one ought never to draw near to any other.

Furthermore, the bodhisattva ought to reflect thus: "How, then, might I draw near to a very dwelling-place of the forest hermitage that every buddha permits? I may, perhaps, dwell somewhere that is not truly a forest hermitage at all, while believing it to be a genuine dwelling within the forest hermitage — some error may well be present."

Question: What, then, is this very dwelling-place of the forest hermitage that the bodhisattva ought to join together with?

Answer: The Buddha himself states within the sūtra: "The dwelling-place of the forest hermitage" refers to dwelling within no dharma whatsoever, taking refuge in no worldly dust, grasping at the mark of no dharma whatsoever, craving no form, sound, fragrance, taste, or touch. Since every dharma is equal in every respect, dwelling with nothing whatsoever relied upon is what is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Through one's own mind being wholesome, dwelling in a place contrary to nothing whatsoever, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Relinquishing every burden, dwelling at ease and delighted, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Liberated from every affliction, dwelling free of fear, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Crossing beyond every current, dwelling thus, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Dwelling within the noble lineage is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Dwelling in contentment, receiving whatever comes, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Dwelling easily satisfied, easily sustained, with few desires, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Dwelling complete with wisdom is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Dwelling in right conduct and vast learning is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

The very gateways of liberation — emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness — being directly present, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Severing every bond, dwelling in liberation, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Dwelling in accordance with the twelve links of dependent origination is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

Dwelling in ultimate tranquil cessation, whatever needed to be done already done, is called dwelling within the forest hermitage.

This very dwelling-place of the forest hermitage accords with the category of the precepts, assists the category of concentration, benefits the category of wisdom, easily attains the category of liberation, easily attains the category of the knowledge and vision of liberation, easily practices every dharma that assists bodhi, and is able to gather in every merit of the ascetic practices.

This dwelling-place of the forest hermitage penetrates every truth.

Within the forest hermitage, one beholds and knows every aggregate.

Within the forest hermitage, every nature is identical with the very nature of the Dharma.

Within the forest hermitage, one transcends the twelve sense-bases.

Within the forest hermitage, one never forgets or loses bodhicitta.

Within the forest hermitage, one contemplates emptiness without fear.

Within the forest hermitage, one is able to guard and protect the Buddhadharma.

Within the forest hermitage, one who seeks liberation never loses their own merit.

Within the forest hermitage, one able to attain omniscience is thereby able to increase it further.

Within the forest hermitage, the bodhisattva, practicing in this way, swiftly attains completeness in the six pāramitās.

And why is this so?

Should the bodhisattva dwell within the forest hermitage, never sparing their own body or life, this alone is called the practice of the pāramitā of giving.

These three kinds of wholesome karma, pure, entering into the very refined practice of the ascetic observances, this is called the pāramitā of moral discipline.

A mind free of anger and resentment, this very mind of loving-kindness extending universally toward every sentient being, patiently enduring, delighting only in the vehicle of omniscience, dwelling in no other vehicle whatsoever, this is called the pāramitā of patient endurance.

Establishing this very vow oneself, that, within the forest hermitage, should the forbearance of the true Dharma not yet be attained, one shall never, under any circumstance, relinquish this very place, this is called the pāramitā of diligent effort.

Having attained meditative concentration, never regarding one's own place of rebirth while cultivating this root of goodness, this is called the pāramitā of meditative concentration.

Just as the very body itself is the forest hermitage, so too is bodhi itself; within genuine reality, there exists no distinction between them whatsoever — this is called the pāramitā of wisdom.

The Buddha permits four dharmas for dwelling within the forest hermitage.

What are these four? As the Buddha told the elder: "First, vast learning. Second, skillfully knowing the resolute meaning. Third, delighting in cultivating right mindfulness. Fourth, practicing exactly as taught."

A person such as this ought to dwell within the forest hermitage.

There is, further, a bodhisattva whose own afflictions run deep and thick — should this person remain amid the clamor of the crowd, their own afflictions would thereby arise; they ought to dwell within the forest hermitage, subduing these very afflictions.

Furthermore, the bodhisattva who has attained the five spiritual powers, wishing to teach and transform and bring to maturity the devas, nāgas, yakṣas, and gandharvas, ought to dwell within the forest hermitage.

There is, further, a bodhisattva who reflects thus: "This very place every buddha praises and permits is itself the forest hermitage."

Furthermore, dwelling within the forest hermitage, assisting and fulfilling every wholesome dharma, increasing this very root of goodness, and only afterward entering a settlement to expound the Dharma for sentient beings —

Only having accomplished merit such as this may one genuinely dwell within the forest hermitage.

Furthermore: "Within the Sūtra of the Resolute King, the Buddha, for the sake of Ānanda, expounds that the monk dwelling within the forest hermitage ought to abide within four sets of four dharmas."

As for the bodhisattva dwelling within the forest hermitage: first, departing far from both householder and renunciant alike; second, wishing to recite the profound sūtras;

third, guiding sentient beings so that they too attain the very merit of the forest hermitage; fourth, never departing, day or night, from mindfulness of the Buddha.

There are, further, four dharmas: first, never giving rise to a mind of anger toward sentient beings, even for the mere span of a single snap of the fingers; second, never allowing drowsiness and sleep to cover the mind, even for a single moment; third, never giving rise to a thought of "sentient being" as such, even for the span of a single thought; fourth, never forgetting or relinquishing bodhicitta, even for the span of a single thought.

There are, further, four dharmas: first, constantly sitting in solitude, never gathering a crowd; second, constantly delighting in walking meditation; third, constantly contemplating that no dharma whatsoever is new or old; fourth, never departing from the profound dharma of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness.

There are, further, four dharmas: first, practicing the four dhyānas, never practicing a merely worldly form of absorption; practicing the four immeasurable states of mind, taking sentient beings as one's own object and giving rise to compassion toward them, and yet never grasping at the very mark of a sentient being; second, though practicing a mind of loving-kindness, never taking sentient beings as its own object; though practicing a mind of sympathetic joy, never craving or delighting in it; though practicing a mind of equanimity, never abandoning sentient beings; third, beholding for oneself that one's own body possesses the very conduct of the four noble lineages, and yet never exalting oneself while demeaning another; fourth, personally practicing vast learning exactly as one has heard it taught. These are the four.

A note on this final redefinition

The list of nearly twenty distinct phrases here, each ending in the identical refrain "is called dwelling within the forest hermitage," completes the term's own total transformation from a physical location into a description of inward attainment — the twelve links of dependent origination, the gateways of liberation, and completion of every task all receive this same name, so that by the section's own end, "forest hermitage" has become simply another name for liberation itself, wherever it occurs. The four qualifying conditions that follow — vast learning, skillfully knowing resolute meaning, right mindfulness, and practicing exactly as taught — then pull the discussion back to the practical question of who is actually ready to attempt this practice, closing the treatise's own extended meditation on forest-dwelling before turning to the remaining ten ascetic practices.

A note on this closing catalogue

These final ten practices, each cited with its own exact ten benefits, complete the chapter's own announced structure with striking consistency — one hundred distinct benefits in total across twelve ascetic practices, none of them merely repeating the last, ranging from the plainly physical (little illness, a body light and agile) to the deeply psychological (constant weariness toward rebirth, freedom from every fear). Read across the whole chapter, the twelve practices trace a single trajectory: shedding attachment first to clothing and food, then to comfort and shelter, and finally, in dwelling among the dead and in the open ground, confronting mortality and exposure directly — each renunciation stripping away one further layer of what an ordinary life depends on for its own sense of security.
