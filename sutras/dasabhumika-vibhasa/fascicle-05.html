The Chapter of Easy Practice

A disciple, terrified of the difficult path's own risk of falling into the lesser vehicles, asks for an easier way. Nāgārjuna answers with buddhas of the ten directions, Amitābha's own original vow quoted directly, and pages of buddhas and bodhisattvas to call upon by name — the earliest scriptural root of Pure Land Buddhism's own distinction between self-power and reliance upon a buddha's vow, closing with the fourfold practice of confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication.

The Question: Is There an Easier Way?

Chinese Source

易行品第九

問曰：是阿惟越致菩薩，初事如先說。至阿惟越致地者，行諸難行，久乃可得，或墮聲聞、辟支佛地；若爾者是大衰患！

如《助道法》中說：若墮聲聞地，　及辟支佛地，是名菩薩死，　則失一切利。若墮於地獄，　不生如是畏；若墮二乘地，　則為大怖畏。墮於地獄中，　畢竟得至佛，若墮二乘地，　畢竟遮佛道。佛自於經中，　解說如是事，如人貪壽者，　斬首則大畏；菩薩亦如是，　若於聲聞地，及辟支佛地，　應生大怖畏。

是故，若諸佛所說，有易行道疾得至阿惟越致地方便者，願為說之。

答曰：如汝所說，是儜弱怯劣，無有大心，非是丈夫志幹之言也！

何以故？若人發願欲求阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，未得阿惟越致，於其中間應不惜身命，晝夜精進如救頭燃。

如《助道》中說：菩薩未得至，　阿惟越致地，應常勤精進，　猶如救頭燃；荷負於重擔，　為求菩提故，常應勤精進，　不生懈怠心。若求聲聞乘，　辟支佛乘者，但為成己利，　常應勤精進；何況於菩薩，　自度亦度彼，於此二乘人，　億倍應精進。

行大乘者，佛如是說：「發願求佛道，重於舉三千大千世界。」汝言：「阿惟越致地，是法甚難，久乃可得，若有易行道，疾得至阿惟越致地」者，是乃怯弱下劣之言，非是大人志幹之說！

汝若必欲聞此方便，今當說之。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Chapter of Easy Practice

Question: The very first matter concerning this irreversible bodhisattva has already been explained. But reaching the stage of irreversibility requires practicing what is difficult to practice, attained only after a very long time — and one may, along the way, fall into the ground of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha. Should this be so, this is indeed a great calamity!

As the Treatise on Aids to the Path states: "Should one fall into the ground of the śrāvaka, or the ground of the pratyekabuddha, this is called the death of a bodhisattva, and every benefit is thereby lost. Falling into hell itself does not give rise to a fear such as this; but falling into the ground of the two lesser vehicles is indeed a source of great terror. Falling into hell, one is certain, in the end, to arrive at buddhahood; but falling into the ground of the two lesser vehicles permanently obstructs the path to buddhahood. The Buddha himself, within the sūtras, has explained this very matter — just as a person who cherishes their own life is filled with great terror at the thought of beheading; the bodhisattva is likewise so — toward the ground of the śrāvaka, and the ground of the pratyekabuddha, they ought to give rise to great terror."

It is for this reason that, should the buddhas themselves have spoken of some easy path, some skillful means for swiftly reaching the stage of irreversibility, I ask that you explain it to me.

Answer: What you have said here is feeble, timid, and inferior, lacking any great resolve — this is not the speech of one possessed of a great person's own strength of will!

And why is this so? Should a person give rise to the vow to seek unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, and not yet have attained irreversibility, throughout this very interval they ought to begrudge neither body nor life, exerting themselves diligently day and night, as though extinguishing a fire upon their own head.

As the Treatise on Aids to the Path states: "The bodhisattva who has not yet attained the stage of irreversibility ought constantly to exert diligent effort, just as though extinguishing a fire upon their own head; bearing this heavy burden for the sake of seeking bodhi, they ought constantly to exert diligent effort, never giving rise to a mind of indolence. Even one who seeks merely the vehicle of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha, seeking only their own benefit, ought constantly to exert diligent effort — how much more so, then, should the bodhisattva, who liberates both themselves and others, exert effort a hundred million times more diligently than these two vehicles' own followers!"

As for those who practice the Great Vehicle, the Buddha has spoken thus: "To give rise to the vow seeking the path to buddhahood is weightier than lifting the entire three-thousandfold great thousand-world system itself." What you have said — that the stage of irreversibility is so difficult a Dharma, attainable only after so long a time, and asking whether there might be some easy path to reach the stage of irreversibility swiftly instead — this is itself the speech of the feeble and the inferior, not the speech of a great person's own strength of will!

Yet, since you truly wish to hear of this skillful means, I shall now explain it.

A note on this opening rebuke

Nāgārjuna's own answer opens not with reassurance but with a direct rebuke — calling the very question "feeble and inferior" before agreeing to answer it at all. This rhetorical posture matters for how the easy path that follows should be read: it is offered not as an admission that the difficult path was somehow mistaken or unnecessary, but as a concession granted despite Nāgārjuna's own evident preference for the "extinguish a fire on one's own head" urgency of self-effort. The easy path arrives framed, from its very first sentence, as a response to weakness rather than a repudiation of strength.

The Ten Buddhas of the Directions

Chinese Source

佛法有無量門，如世間道，有難、有易；陸道步行則苦，水道乘船則樂。菩薩道亦如是：或有勤行精進，或有以信方便易行疾至阿惟越致者。

如偈說：東方善德佛，　南栴檀德佛，西無量明佛，　北方相德佛；東南無憂德，　西南寶施佛，西北華德佛，　東北三行佛；下方明德佛，　上方廣眾德，如是諸世尊，　今現在十方。

若人疾欲至，　不退轉地者，應以恭敬心，　執持稱名號。

若菩薩欲於此身，得至阿惟越致地、成就阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，應當念是十方諸佛，稱其名號。

如《寶月童子所問經．阿惟越致品》中說：佛告寶月：東方去此，過無量無邊不可思議恒河沙等佛土，有世界名無憂。其地平坦，七寶合成，紫磨金縷，交絡其界，寶樹羅列，以為莊嚴。無有地獄、畜生、餓鬼、阿修羅道，及諸難處。

清淨無穢，無有沙礫、瓦石、山陵、垖阜、深坑、幽壑。天常雨華，以布其地。

時世有佛，號曰善德如來、應供、正遍知、明行足、善逝、世間解、無上士、調御丈夫、天人師、佛、世尊。大菩薩眾恭敬圍繞，身相光色，如燃大金山，如大珍寶聚。為諸大眾，廣說正法。初、中、後善，有辭、有義，所說不雜。具足清淨，如實不失。何謂不失？不失地、水、火、風；不失欲界、色界、無色界；不失色、受、想、行、識。

寶月！是佛成道已來，過六十億劫。又其佛國，晝夜無異，但以此間閻浮提日月歲數，說彼劫壽。其佛光明，常照世界。於一說法，令無量無邊千萬億阿僧祇眾生，住無生法忍，倍此人數得住初忍、第二、第三忍。

寶月！其佛本願力故。若有他方眾生，於先佛所，種諸善根，是佛但以光明觸身，即得無生法忍。寶月！若善男子，善女人聞是佛名能信受者，即不退阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

餘九佛事，皆亦如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddhadharma possesses immeasurable gates, just as a worldly road may be either difficult or easy — traveling on foot by land is arduous, while riding a boat by water is pleasant. The bodhisattva path is likewise so: some diligently exert effort, while others, by means of faith and skillful means, follow an easy practice and swiftly arrive at irreversibility.

As the verse says: "In the east, the Buddha of Good Virtue; in the south, the Buddha of Sandalwood Virtue; in the west, the Buddha of Immeasurable Light; in the north, the Buddha of Formed Virtue; in the southeast, the Buddha of Sorrowlessness; in the southwest, the Buddha of Jeweled Giving; in the northwest, the Buddha of Flower Virtue; in the northeast, the Buddha of the Threefold Conduct; below, the Buddha of Radiant Virtue; above, the Buddha of Vast Assembled Virtue — these World-Honored Ones exist, even now, throughout the ten directions."

"Should a person wish swiftly to reach the stage of non-retrogression, they ought, with a mind of reverence, to hold fast to and call upon these very names."

Should a bodhisattva wish, within this very body, to reach the stage of irreversibility and accomplish unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, they ought to be mindful of these buddhas of the ten directions, calling upon their very names.

As the Sūtra Requested by Ratnacandra Kumāra states, in its Chapter on Irreversibility: The Buddha told Ratnacandra: "To the east of here, past immeasurable, boundless, inconceivable buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Sorrowless. Its ground is level and even, formed entirely of the seven treasures, threaded through with pure gold, its very boundaries interlaced with it; jeweled trees stand arrayed in rows, forming its own adornment. It possesses no hell, no animal realm, no hungry ghosts, no realm of asuras, and none of the other perilous states.

"It is pure and free of any impurity, without sand, gravel, broken stone, mountain ridges, mounds, deep pits, or dark ravines. The devas constantly rain down flowers, scattering them across its ground.

"At that time there was a buddha in this world, named Sudṛṣṭi Tathāgata, Worthy of Offerings, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, Complete in Wisdom and Conduct, Well-Gone, Knower of the World, Unsurpassed, Tamer of Beings, Teacher of Devas and Humans, Buddha, World-Honored One. A great assembly of bodhisattvas surrounded him in reverence; his own bodily form, radiant in color, was like a great mountain of blazing gold, like a great mass of precious treasure. For the sake of this great assembly, he expounded the true Dharma at length. Excellent in its beginning, its middle, and its end, possessed of both eloquent expression and true meaning, what he expounded was never mixed with anything extraneous. Complete in purity, it was exactly as it truly is, and never lost. What, then, is meant by 'never lost'? Never losing sight of earth, water, fire, and wind; never losing sight of the desire realm, the form realm, and the formless realm; never losing sight of form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness.

"Ratnacandra! It has now been sixty koṭis of kalpas since this buddha accomplished the path. And within this buddha's own land, day and night are indistinguishable; it is only by means of the sun, the moon, and the count of years within this Jambudvīpa of ours that the span of this kalpa is described at all. This buddha's own light constantly illuminates this entire world. Within a single exposition of the Dharma, he causes immeasurable, boundless thousands of ten thousands of koṭis of asaṃkhyeyas of sentient beings to abide within the acceptance of the non-arising of dharmas, and twice this number of beings to abide within the first, second, and third levels of acceptance.

"Ratnacandra! This is through the power of this buddha's own original vow. Should there be sentient beings from other realms who have already planted roots of goodness in the presence of former buddhas, this buddha, merely by touching their body with his own light, causes them to attain the acceptance of the non-arising of dharmas at once. Ratnacandra! Should a good son or good daughter hear this buddha's own name and be able to accept it with faith, they shall not retrogress from unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening."

The matters concerning each of the remaining nine buddhas are, in every case, likewise so.

A note on this citation

The extended quotation from the Sūtra Requested by Ratnacandra Kumāra, given in full for Sudṛṣṭi Buddha alone before the treatise abbreviates "the matters concerning each of the remaining nine buddhas are, in every case, likewise so," establishes the template the rest of this chapter will assume without repeating: an immeasurably distant pure land, a buddha whose own light or name alone is sufficient to grant the acceptance of non-arising, and a vow explicitly extended to beings who merely hear this name with faith. This is the exact scriptural shape that Amitābha's own vow, quoted directly further below, will follow as well — Sudṛṣṭi is not a digression before the chapter's real subject, but the pattern that subject fits into.

The Ten Directions Named, and Verses of Homage

今當解說「諸佛名號」及「國土名號」。

善德者：其德淳善，但有安樂。非如諸天、龍神福德惑惱眾生。

栴檀德者：南方去此無量無邊恒河沙等佛土，有世界名歡喜，佛號栴檀德，今現在說法。譬如栴檀香而清涼，彼佛名稱遠聞，如香流布。滅除眾生三毒火熱，令得清涼。

華德佛者：西北方去此無量無邊恒河沙等佛土，有世界名眾音，佛號華德，今現在說法。其佛色身猶如妙華，其德無量。

三乘行佛者：東北方去此無量無邊恒河沙等佛土，有世界名安隱，佛號三乘行，今現在說法。其佛常說：聲聞行、辟支佛行、諸菩薩行。有人言：說上、中、下精進故，號為三乘行。

明德佛者：下方去此無量無邊恒河沙等佛土，有世界名廣大，佛號明德，今現在說法。明，名身明、智慧明、寶樹光明，是三種明，常照世間。

廣眾德者：上方去此無量無邊恒河沙等佛土，有世界名眾月，佛號廣眾德，今現在說法。其佛弟子福德廣大故，號廣眾德。

今是十方佛，善德為初，廣眾德為後。若人一心稱其名號，即得不退於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

如偈說：若有人得聞，　說是諸佛名，即得無量德，　如為寶月說。

我禮是諸佛，　今現在十方，其有稱名者，　即得不退轉。

東方無憂界，　其佛號善德，色相如金山，　名聞無邊際；若人聞名者，　即得不退轉，我今合掌禮，　願悉除憂惱。

南方歡喜界，　佛號栴檀德，面淨如滿月，　光明無有量。能滅諸眾生，　三毒之熱惱，聞名得不退，　是故稽首禮。

西方善世界，　佛號無量明，身光智慧明，　所照無邊際；其有聞名者，　即得不退轉，我今稽首禮，　願盡生死際。

北方無動界，　佛號為相德，身具眾相好，　而以自莊嚴；摧破魔怨眾，　善化諸人天，聞名得不退，　是故稽首禮。

東南月明界，　有佛號無憂，光明踰日月，　遇者滅煩惱；常為眾說法，　除諸內外苦，十方佛稱讚，　是故稽首禮。

西南眾相界，　佛號為寶施，常以諸法寶，　廣施於一切；諸天頭面禮，　寶冠在足下，我今以五體，　歸命寶施尊。

西北眾音界，　佛號為華德，世界眾寶樹，　演出妙法音；常以七覺華，　莊嚴於眾生，白毫相如月，　我今頭面禮。

東北安隱界，　諸寶所合成，佛號三乘行，　無量相嚴身；智慧光無量，　能破無明闇，眾生無憂惱，　是故稽首禮。

上方眾月界，　眾寶所莊嚴，大德聲聞眾，　菩薩無有量；諸聖中師子，　號曰廣眾德，諸魔所怖畏，　是故稽首禮。

下方廣世界，　佛號為明德，身相妙超絕，　閻浮檀金山；常以智慧日，　開諸善根華，寶土甚廣大，　我遙稽首禮。

過去無數劫，　有佛號海德，是諸現在佛，　皆從彼發願；壽命無有量，　光明照無極，國土甚清淨，　聞名定作佛。

今現在十方，　具足成十力，是故稽首禮，　人天中最尊。

Ru-Yi Translation

I shall now explain the names of these buddhas, and the names of their own lands.

"Good Virtue": his own virtue is pure and wholesome, bringing only peace and joy — unlike the merit of devas, nāgas, and spirits, which can still confound and afflict sentient beings.

"Sandalwood Virtue": to the south of here, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Joy, whose buddha is named Sandalwood Virtue, presently expounding the Dharma. Just as sandalwood incense brings coolness, this buddha's own renown is heard from far away, spreading like the very fragrance of this incense, extinguishing the burning heat of the threefold poison within sentient beings, bringing them to coolness.

"Buddha of Immeasurable Light": to the west of here, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Goodness, whose buddha is named Immeasurable Light, presently expounding the Dharma. This buddha's own bodily light, and his own wisdom, shine forth immeasurable and boundless.

"Buddha of Formed Virtue": to the north of here, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Immovable, whose buddha is named Formed Virtue, presently expounding the Dharma. This buddha's own merit rises high and visible, just like a banner's own standard.

"Sorrowlessness Virtue": to the southeast of here, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Moon-Bright, whose buddha is named Sorrowlessness Virtue, presently expounding the Dharma. This buddha's own spiritual virtue causes every deva and human to be entirely free of sorrow.

"Buddha of Jeweled Giving": to the southwest of here, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Assembled Marks, whose buddha is named Jeweled Giving, presently expounding the Dharma. This buddha constantly bestows upon sentient beings the treasures of the uncontaminated faculties, powers, factors of awakening, and path.

"Buddha of Flower Virtue": to the northwest of here, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Assembled Sounds, whose buddha is named Flower Virtue, presently expounding the Dharma. This buddha's own bodily form is just like a wondrous flower, his own virtue immeasurable.

"Buddha of the Threefold Conduct": to the northeast of here, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Peace, whose buddha is named the Threefold Conduct, presently expounding the Dharma. This buddha constantly expounds the conduct of the śrāvaka, the conduct of the pratyekabuddha, and the conduct of the bodhisattvas. Some say he is named the Threefold Conduct because he expounds diligent effort of the highest, middling, and lowest degree.

"Buddha of Radiant Virtue": below, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Vast, whose buddha is named Radiant Virtue, presently expounding the Dharma. "Radiance" refers to the radiance of the body, the radiance of wisdom, and the radiance of the jeweled trees — these three kinds of radiance constantly illuminate this entire world.

"Vast Assembled Virtue": above, past immeasurable, boundless buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, there is a world named Assembled Moons, whose buddha is named Vast Assembled Virtue, presently expounding the Dharma. Since this buddha's own disciples possess merit vast and extensive, he is named Vast Assembled Virtue.

These, then, are the buddhas of the ten directions, from Good Virtue first through Vast Assembled Virtue last. Should a person, with a single mind, call upon these very names, they attain non-retrogression from unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening at once.

As the verse says: "Should there be a person who comes to hear these very names of these buddhas spoken, they attain immeasurable merit at once, just as was spoken for Ratnacandra himself.

"I bow in homage to these buddhas, presently existing throughout the ten directions; whoever calls upon their names attains non-retrogression at once.

"In the eastern world of Sorrowlessness, its buddha is named Good Virtue, his own form the color of a mountain of gold, his renown reaching without any bound; should a person hear this very name, they attain non-retrogression at once — I now join my palms and bow, praying that every sorrow and affliction be entirely removed.

"In the southern world of Joy, the buddha is named Sandalwood Virtue, his face pure as the full moon, his light beyond all measure. Able to extinguish the burning affliction of the threefold poison within every sentient being, hearing this name grants non-retrogression — it is for this reason that I bow my head in homage.

"In the western world of Goodness, the buddha is named Immeasurable Light, his bodily light and wisdom's own radiance shining without any bound; whoever hears this very name attains non-retrogression at once — I now bow my head in homage, praying to reach the very end of birth and death.

"In the northern world of Immovability, the buddha is named Formed Virtue, his own body complete with every excellent mark, adorning himself thereby; crushing and destroying the hosts of hostile māra, skillfully transforming every deva and human, hearing this name grants non-retrogression — it is for this reason that I bow my head in homage.

A note on this directional scheme

Naming ten buddhas across the eight compass directions plus above and below — rather than a single buddha in a single direction — establishes a structural principle this chapter will repeat at ever-larger scale: the easy path is not built around one uniquely privileged buddha, but around the sheer availability of many. Only after this full compass rose is completed does the chapter turn to Amitābha specifically, introduced not as a lone alternative but as one name among "Amitābha and the rest" — a phrasing worth noting given how singularly Amitābha's own name would come to dominate later Pure Land practice.

A note on this vow, quoted directly

This single sentence — "should a person be mindful of me, calling upon my name and taking refuge in me of their own accord, they shall enter the stage of certainty at once" — is the passage that later Pure Land tradition would treat as this treatise's own single most consequential line, and the earliest surviving scriptural articulation of what would become Amitābha's own eighteenth vow in the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra, already translated on this site. A note on the closed lotus

The verse on doubt preventing the lotus from opening — "should a person plant this root of goodness yet harbor doubt, their own lotus flower does not open; should their mind of faith be pure, this flower opens, and they behold the Buddha at once" — anticipates, in miniature, the entire narrative structure Shandao's Commentary on the Contemplation Sūtra develops at far greater length: unopened lotus flowers, sealed shut in proportion to the doubt or grave offense of the being reborn within them, appear repeatedly across that commentary's own account of the nine grades, already translated in full on this site. The image's own presence here, centuries before Shandao's own commentary, confirms how early this particular picture of Pure Land rebirth had already taken shape.

A note on the seven buddhas

The seven buddhas of the past named here — Vipaśyin through Śākyamuni — belong to a fixed lineage recognized across nearly every school of Buddhism, distinguishing this list from the immeasurably distant, otherwise unattested buddhas of the ten directions catalogued earlier in this chapter. By moving from those cosmic, barely nameable figures to this well-established sevenfold lineage, and then to Maitreya as the certain buddha of the future, the chapter closes its own account of "buddhas to call upon" with figures the entire tradition already recognized, grounding the easy path's own vast catalogue in names no reader could dispute.

Removing Karma Chapter

Question: Is holding these buddhas, Amitābha among them, and further bodhisattvas, in mindfulness sufficient by itself to attain irreversibility — or is there some further skillful means as well?

Answer: One who seeks the stage of irreversibility does not rely upon mindfulness, calling upon these names, and reverent homage alone — one ought, further, in the presence of these buddhas, to confess, to exhort, to rejoice, and to dedicate merit.

Question: What, then, is meant by this?

Answer: "Before the immeasurable buddhas of the ten directions, whose knowledge leaves nothing whatsoever unknown, I now confess and lay bare, in their very presence, every one of my own dark and evil deeds. Three groups of three make nine kinds, arising from three kinds of affliction; whether within this present body, or within a former one, I confess and repent of these very offenses in their entirety. Should there be karmic retribution owed within the three evil destinies, I pray that I might discharge it within this very body, and never enter these evil destinies to undergo it."

"The buddhas of the ten directions" refers to every buddha presently existing, their own life-force still intact, not yet entered into nirvāṇa. "The ten directions" refers to the four cardinal directions, the four intermediate directions, and above and below. "Buddha" refers to one who knows whatever ought to be known, in its entirety, without remainder.

"Lay bare" means, in the presence of these buddhas, to lay bare every offense without any concealment whatsoever, never to commit it again afterward — just as one builds a levee to hold back water.

"Dark and evil deeds" refers to committing manifold evils on account of the absence of wisdom, insight, and clarity — whether unwholesome dharmas, or those that are concealed and indeterminate.

"Three groups of three" refers to: evils arising through body, speech, and mind; retribution experienced in this present life, in the next life, or in a life thereafter; and committed by oneself, taught to another to commit, or rejoiced in when committed by another.

"Arising from three kinds of affliction": these three kinds of affliction refer to those bound to the desire realm, those bound to the form realm, and those bound to the formless realm; or those that assist craving, those that assist anger, and those that assist delusion; or those of the highest degree, the middling degree, and the lowest degree.

"Confessing entirely, whether within this present body or a former one" means to repent, without remainder, of every evil committed within this present life and within former lives.

"The hells" refers to the eight hot hells and the ten cold hells. "Animals" refers to those born of the earth, born of the water, without feet, with two feet, or with many feet. "Hungry ghosts" refers to those who feed on spittle, on vomit, on rinse-water, or on pus, blood, urine, and excrement, and the like.

Should my own karmic conduct be such that I ought to undergo retribution within these three evil destinies, I pray that this offense might instead be undergone directly within this very body; and should it be undergone within a later body, may it never be undergone within hell, among hungry ghosts, or among animals.

Furthermore, the Buddha himself has spoken this very method of confession. Should a bodhisattva wish to confess an offense, they ought to speak in this way: "Within the worlds of the ten directions presently existing, the buddhas have attained unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, turning the wheel of the Dharma, raining down the rain of the Dharma, beating the drum of the Dharma, blowing the conch of the Dharma, and raising up the banner of the Dharma. Through the giving of this Dharma, they satisfy sentient beings, bringing benefit far and wide, and peace and security far and wide, compassionate toward this world, bringing abundant benefit to devas and humans.

"I now, with body, speech, and mind, bow my head in homage at the feet of these presently existing buddhas — these buddhas who know, who see, the very eye of the world, the very lamp of the world.

"Since beginningless birth and death, whatever offense and karma I have given rise to, pressed upon by craving, anger, and delusion — whether failing to recognize the Buddha, failing to recognize the Dharma, failing to recognize the Saṅgha; or failing to recognize offense and merit; or committing manifold offenses through body, speech, and mind; or, with an evil mind, drawing blood from a buddha's own body; or destroying the true Dharma, causing schism within the Saṅgha, or killing a true arhat; or myself practicing the ten unwholesome courses, or teaching another to practice them, or further rejoicing in them; whatever unloving speech I have spoken toward sentient beings; whatever deception through dishonest measures or scales, encroaching upon others, or troubling and confounding sentient beings through wrongful conduct; or failing to be filial to my own parents; or stealing property belonging to a stūpa or property belonging to the Saṅgha of the four quarters; or breaking and destroying the scriptures and precepts spoken by the Buddha, defying my own preceptor or instructor; or, toward one who has given rise to the vehicle of the śrāvaka, the vehicle of the pratyekabuddha, or the Great Vehicle, speaking evil words, reviling and belittling them, holding them in contempt and resentment; or, my own mind covered over by stinginess and envy, giving rise to evil speech in the presence of these buddhas; or declaring what is Dharma to be not the Dharma, and what is not the Dharma to be the Dharma —

"I now, on account of these very offenses, in the presence of these presently existing buddhas who know, who see, who bear witness, lay them bare in their entirety, daring to conceal nothing, and from this moment onward daring never to commit them again. Should I possess any offense that ought to cause me to fall among hell-beings, animals, hungry ghosts, or asuras, never encountering the Three Jewels, born amid every calamity, I pray that this very offense might be undergone directly within this present life.

"Just as the bodhisattvas of ages past, seeking the path to buddhahood, confessed and repented of their own evil karma and offenses, I too, in just this same way, lay bare and confess them, daring to conceal nothing, never to commit them again afterward.

"Should the bodhisattvas of the present, seeking the path to buddhahood, confess and repent of their own evil karma and offenses, I too, in just this same way, lay bare and confess them, daring to conceal nothing, never to commit them again afterward.

"Just as the bodhisattvas of ages yet to come, seeking the path to buddhahood, shall confess and repent of their own evil karma and offenses, I too, in just this same way, lay bare and confess them, daring to conceal nothing, never to commit them again afterward.

"Just as the bodhisattvas of the past, the future, and the present, seeking the path to buddhahood, confess and repent of their own evil karma and offenses — having already confessed, now confessing, and yet to confess — I too, in just this same way, confess this evil karma and these offenses, daring to conceal nothing, never to commit them again afterward."

A note on this confession formula

The formula's own closing structure — repeating the identical confession four times, for bodhisattvas of the past, the present, the future, and then all three together — is not mere repetition for emphasis. It places the speaker's own act of confession inside a continuous, unbroken lineage of confession stretching across every possible moment of time, so that a single individual's private admission of fault becomes, by the formula's own logic, one instance within a practice every bodhisattva who has ever existed, exists now, or will ever exist has likewise undertaken.

Question: You have already explained the method of confession — what, then, is meant by exhortation?

Answer: "Every buddha of the ten directions who has now accomplished the path, I request that they turn the wheel of the Dharma, bringing peace and joy to every sentient being; every buddha of the ten directions who wishes to relinquish their own lifespan, I now bow my head in homage, exhorting them to abide long in this world."

"Turning the wheel of the Dharma" means expounding the meaning of the four noble truths through three turnings and twelve aspects: this is the truth of suffering, this is the arising of suffering, this is the cessation of suffering, this is the path leading to the cessation of suffering — this is called the first turning, with its four aspects.

This truth of suffering ought to be known, this arising of suffering ought to be severed, this cessation of suffering ought to be realized, this path leading to the cessation of suffering ought to be cultivated — this is called the second turning, with its four aspects.

This truth of suffering has already been known, this arising of suffering has already been severed, this cessation of suffering has already been realized, this path leading to the cessation of suffering has already been cultivated — this is called the third turning, with its four aspects.

"The four aspects" refers to the eye, the wisdom, the clarity, and the awakening that arise within these four noble truths.

Some say: the vehicle of the śrāvaka, the vehicle of the pratyekabuddha, and the Great Vehicle are together called the wheel of the Dharma; explaining the meaning of these three vehicles is called turning the wheel of the Dharma.

As for "bringing peace and joy to every sentient being": the pleasure of the five desires is not called peace and joy; rather, attaining pure peace and joy, within this present life and lives to come, and entering into one of the three vehicles — this is called peace and joy. This person exhorts the buddhas to turn the wheel of the Dharma, causing sentient beings to receive the joy of nirvāṇa, and, should nirvāṇa not yet be attained, causing them to receive worldly joy instead — it is for this reason that this is called peace and joy.

As for "lifespan": through the causes and conditions of undergoing karmic retribution, the life-faculty continues in unbroken sequence and thereby abides — just as with something magically conjured, it abides in accordance with the mind and karma that sustain it, and ceases the moment this mind and karma come to rest.

"Exhortation" means to seek this vow with utmost sincerity. Since the buddhas regard sentient beings, great and small alike, without any distinction, one seeks and requests, hoping that this vow might be granted: that they might never relinquish their own lifespan, but abide for immeasurable asaṃkhyeya kalpas, liberating sentient beings.

Furthermore, the Buddha himself has spoken this very method of exhortation; the bodhisattva ought to speak in this way: "I bow in homage to the buddhas of the ten directions presently existing. Should they have only just attained unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, and not yet turned the wheel of the Dharma, I now seek and request that they turn this wheel of the Dharma, beat the drum of the Dharma, blow the conch of the Dharma, raise up the banner of the Dharma, hold the great sacrifice of the Dharma, and kindle the great torch of the Dharma; through the giving of this Dharma, satisfying sentient beings, bringing benefit far and wide, and peace and joy far and wide, compassionate toward this world, bringing abundant benefit to devas and humans — it is for this reason that I now offer this exhortation." This is what is meant by exhorting the buddhas to turn the wheel of the Dharma.

As for "abiding long," one ought likewise to say: "To the buddhas of the ten directions presently existing, should any of these buddhas wish to relinquish their own lifespan, I request that they abide long instead, bringing benefit far and wide, and peace and joy far and wide, compassionate toward this world, bringing abundant benefit to devas and humans."

Question: You have already explained confession and exhortation — what, then, is meant by rejoicing?

Answer: "Whatever merit of giving, of upholding the precepts, or of cultivating meditative practice, arising through body, speech, or mind, whatever exists from the past, the future, and the present; whatever merit belongs to those who practice the three vehicles, and to those who have fully accomplished the three vehicles, and every root of merit belonging to ordinary beings as well — in all of this, I rejoice together with them."

"The merit of giving" arises from relinquishing the dharma of stinginess. "The merit of upholding the precepts" arises from subduing the conduct of body and speech. "Meditative practice" refers to the various meditative absorptions.

"Arising through body and speech" refers to giving, upholding the precepts, welcoming and sending off others, and the like, brought about through body and speech; "arising through mind" refers to meditative concentration, loving-kindness and compassion, and the like.

"Whatever exists from the past, the future, and the present" refers to the merit of every sentient being across the three times.

"Those who practice the three vehicles" refers to those seeking the vehicle of the śrāvaka, the vehicle of the pratyekabuddha, and the Great Vehicle. "Those who have fully accomplished the three vehicles" refers to those who have accomplished the vehicle of the arhat, the vehicle of the pratyekabuddha, and the vehicle of the buddha. "Every" means entirely, without remainder. "Ordinary beings" refers to those who have not yet attained the four noble truths. "Merit" refers to two kinds of karma: wholesome karma, and karma that is neither concealed nor indeterminate.

"Rejoicing" means that, upon another's own act of merit, one's own mind gives rise to joy, praising it as good.

A note on the fourfold practice

Confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication together form a structure that later East Asian Buddhist liturgy would develop into the standard "repentance ritual" (懺法) still performed in Chinese monasteries — this chapter's own account is among the earliest systematic treatments of what would become that entire genre. Notably, none of these four practices requires any particular meditative attainment: confessing a fault, wishing a living teacher a long life, and feeling genuine gladness at a stranger's own small act of kindness are each, in principle, available to any practitioner at all, regardless of how advanced their own cultivation may be — a democratizing feature entirely of a piece with this chapter's own governing theme of an easy path open to those unable to sustain the difficult one.

答曰：我所有福德，　一切皆和合，為諸眾生故，　正迴向佛道。

諸眾生者，三界眾生。

正者，如諸佛迴向，如真實迴向，迴向菩提。

迴向菩提者，迴諸福德向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

又，隨喜、迴向此二事，佛亦自說：有菩薩摩訶薩，欲隨喜、迴向，應念諸佛，斷三界相續道，滅諸戲論，乾煩惱淤泥，滅諸刺棘，除諸重擔，逮得己利，正智解脫，心得自在盡諸有結。無量無邊不可思議阿僧祇十方世界，一一世界中，亦無量無邊不可思議阿僧祇諸佛出已滅度；從初發心乃至得佛，入無餘涅槃，至遺法未盡，於其中間，是諸佛所有善根福德，應六波羅蜜及所受辟支佛記所有善根；又，聲聞人善根，若布施、持戒、修禪，及學、無學、無漏善根；及諸佛戒品、定品、慧品、解脫知見品，大慈大悲等無量功德；及諸佛有所說法，於此法中，有人信解、受學得此法利，是諸人等所有善根，於此法中，及凡夫所種善根；及諸天、龍、夜叉、乾闥婆、阿修羅、迦樓羅、緊那羅、摩睺羅伽，得聞法已生諸善心，乃至畜生聞法生諸善心；及諸佛欲入涅槃時，眾生所種善根；是諸善根福德，一切和合稱量使無遺餘，以最上、最妙、最勝，無上、無等、無等等隨喜。

隨喜已，以是隨喜所生福德，迴向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

未來、現在諸佛亦如是。是三世諸佛福德，及因諸佛所生福德，心皆隨喜，迴向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

是故偈說：罪應如是懺，　勸請隨喜福，迴向無上道，　皆亦應如是；如諸佛所說，　我悔罪勸請，隨喜及迴向，　皆亦復如是。

無始世界來，有無量遮佛道罪，應於十方諸佛前懺悔、勸請諸佛、隨喜、迴向亦應如是。如佛所知、所見、所許懺悔，我亦如是懺悔。勸請諸佛、隨喜、迴向，亦復如是。若如是懺悔、勸請、隨喜、迴向，是名正迴向。

問曰：云何名為諸佛所知、所見、所許「懺悔、勸請、隨喜、迴向」？

答曰：懺悔、勸請，如先說。

隨喜、迴向，如《大品經》中：須菩提白佛言：世尊！所說菩薩於過去、未來、現在一切諸佛及諸弟子，一切眾生所有福德善根，盡和合稱量，以最上隨喜。

世尊！云何名為最上隨喜？

佛告須菩提：若菩薩於過去、未來、現在諸法，不取、不念、不見、不得、不分別而能如是思惟：「是諸法皆從憶想分別，眾緣和合有。一切法實不生，無所從來。是中乃至無有一法已生、今生、當生，亦無已滅、今滅、當滅。諸法相如是，我順諸法相隨喜。」

隨喜已，亦隨諸法實相，迴向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

是名「最上隨喜、迴向」。

復次，須菩提！求佛道善男子、善女人，不欲謗佛者，應以善根如是迴向。應作是念：「如諸佛心、佛智、佛眼，知見是善根福德，本、末、體相，從何而有？我亦如是，隨諸佛知見隨喜，如諸佛所許，我亦如是以善根迴向，若菩薩如是迴向，則不謗諸佛亦無過咎。」深心信解如實迴向，是名「大迴向、具足迴向」。

復次，須菩提！善男子、善女人，以諸善根福德應如是迴向。如諸賢聖戒品、定品、慧品、解脫品、解脫知見品，不繫欲界、不繫色界、不繫無色界，不在過去、不在未來、不在現在，以不繫三界故，是迴向亦如是不繫，所迴向處亦不繫。若菩薩能如是得心，信解如實，是名「不失迴向、無毒迴向、法性迴向。」若菩薩於此迴向取相貪著，是名「邪迴向」。

是故諸菩薩摩訶薩，應如諸佛所知法相，以是法相迴向，能至阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是名「正迴向」。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: You have already explained confession, exhortation, and rejoicing — what, then, is meant by dedication?

Answer: "Whatever merit I myself possess, gathered together in its entirety, for the sake of every sentient being, I rightly dedicate toward the path to buddhahood."

"I" refers to one's own person.

"Whatever merit I possess" refers to whatever arises through body, through speech, or through mind; whatever arises through giving, through upholding the precepts, through cultivating meditative practice, through rejoicing, or through exhortation — all of this, together with every other form of goodness besides, is called one's own possessed merit.

"Gathered together in its entirety" means that, holding every one of these forms of merit in mind, one gathers them together and weighs them, coming to know their own vastness.

"Every sentient being" refers to the sentient beings of the three realms.

"Rightly" means dedicating it just as the buddhas themselves dedicate it, just as it is truly dedicated, dedicated toward bodhi.

"Dedicated toward bodhi" means turning this merit toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

Furthermore, concerning these two matters, rejoicing and dedication, the Buddha himself has likewise spoken: There is a bodhisattva-mahāsattva who, wishing to rejoice and to dedicate merit, ought to be mindful of the buddhas — of their severing the continuous path of the three realms, extinguishing every idle proliferation of thought, drying up the mire of the afflictions, eliminating every thorn and bramble, casting off every heavy burden, having attained their own true benefit, their right wisdom liberated, their mind having attained mastery, every fetter of existence exhausted. Throughout the immeasurable, boundless, inconceivable asaṃkhyeya worlds of the ten directions, within each and every one of these worlds, immeasurable, boundless, inconceivable asaṃkhyeya buddhas likewise have arisen and already passed into extinction; from the very first arising of the aspiration up until the attainment of buddhahood, entering nirvāṇa without remainder, up until the very moment their bequeathed teaching is not yet exhausted — throughout this entire span, whatever roots of goodness and merit these buddhas possess, corresponding to the six pāramitās, and whatever roots of goodness belong to those who received the prediction of pratyekabuddhahood; and further, the roots of goodness of the śrāvakas, whether through giving, upholding the precepts, or cultivating meditative practice, and the uncontaminated roots of goodness of both the trainee and the one beyond training; and the buddhas' own category of the precepts, of concentration, of wisdom, and of the knowledge and vision of liberation, together with great loving-kindness and great compassion and every other immeasurable quality of merit; and whatever Dharma the buddhas have spoken, within which some person has faith, understanding, receives it in study, and attains this very benefit of the Dharma — whatever roots of goodness such persons possess within this Dharma, together with whatever roots of goodness ordinary beings have planted; and whatever devas, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kiṃnaras, and mahoragas, having heard the Dharma, give rise to wholesome minds, even down to animals who, hearing the Dharma, give rise to wholesome minds; and whatever roots of goodness sentient beings plant at the very moment a buddha is about to enter nirvāṇa — all of these roots of goodness and merit, gathered together in their entirety and weighed without a single remainder omitted, are rejoiced in with the very highest, most sublime, most excellent, unsurpassed, unequaled, and unequaled-to-any-equal rejoicing.

Having rejoiced in this way, one dedicates whatever merit arises from this very rejoicing toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

It is likewise so for the buddhas of the future and the present. Toward the merit of the buddhas of these three times, and toward the merit arising through these buddhas, one's mind, in every case, rejoices, and dedicates it toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

It is for this reason that the verse says: "One's offenses ought to be confessed in this way, and the merit of exhortation and rejoicing likewise ought all to be dedicated toward the unsurpassed path in just this same manner; exactly as every buddha has spoken, I confess my own offenses, exhort the buddhas, rejoice, and dedicate this merit, in just this same way."

Since beginningless time, there have been immeasurable offenses obstructing the path to buddhahood; one ought, in just this same way, to confess them in the presence of the buddhas of the ten directions, to exhort these buddhas, to rejoice, and to dedicate this merit. Exactly as the buddhas themselves know, see, and sanction this confession, I too confess in just this same way. Exhorting the buddhas, rejoicing, and dedicating merit are likewise so. Confessing, exhorting, rejoicing, and dedicating merit in this way is called right dedication.

Question: What, then, is meant by "confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication" as known, seen, and sanctioned by the buddhas themselves?

Answer: Confession and exhortation are as already explained above.

As for rejoicing and dedication, as the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra states: Subhūti addressed the Buddha, saying: "World-Honored One! It is said that the bodhisattva, toward every root of goodness and merit belonging to every buddha of the past, the future, and the present, together with their disciples, and every sentient being, gathers it together in its entirety and weighs it, rejoicing in it with the very highest rejoicing.

"World-Honored One! What, then, is meant by this very highest rejoicing?"

The Buddha told Subhūti: "Should the bodhisattva, toward the dharmas of the past, the future, and the present, neither grasp at them, nor dwell upon them, nor perceive them, nor attain them, nor discriminate among them, and yet be able to reflect in just this way: 'Every one of these dharmas arises entirely from recollection and discriminating thought, existing through the coming together of manifold conditions. Every dharma, in truth, does not arise at all, coming from nowhere whatsoever. Within this, there is not even a single dharma that has arisen, is arising now, or shall arise; nor is there any that has ceased, is ceasing now, or shall cease. Such is the very mark of every dharma — I accord with this mark of every dharma, and rejoice in it accordingly.'

"Having rejoiced in this way, one likewise, according with the real mark of every dharma, dedicates this merit toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

"This is called 'the very highest rejoicing and dedication.'

"Furthermore, Subhūti! Should a good son or good daughter, seeking the path to buddhahood, wish never to slander the Buddha, they ought to dedicate their own roots of goodness in just this way. They ought to reflect: 'Just as the buddhas' own mind, the buddhas' own wisdom, and the buddhas' own eye know and perceive these very roots of goodness and merit — their origin, their end, their essence, their mark — from where do they actually arise? I too, in just this same way, rejoice according to the buddhas' own knowledge and vision; exactly as the buddhas themselves have sanctioned it, I too dedicate my own roots of goodness in just this same way. Should a bodhisattva dedicate merit in this manner, they thereby neither slander the buddhas, nor incur any fault.' Holding faith and understanding with a profound mind, dedicating merit exactly as it truly is — this is called 'the great dedication, the complete dedication.'

"Furthermore, Subhūti! A good son or good daughter ought to dedicate their own roots of goodness and merit in just this way. Just as the categories of precepts, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation belonging to every worthy and noble one are bound to neither the desire realm, the form realm, nor the formless realm, and exist within neither the past, the future, nor the present — because they are bound to none of these three realms — this very dedication is likewise unbound in just this same way, and the very destination toward which it is dedicated is likewise unbound. Should the bodhisattva be able to attain a mind such as this, with faith and understanding exactly as it truly is, this is called 'the dedication that is never lost, the dedication free of poison, the dedication according with the very nature of the Dharma.' Should the bodhisattva, within this very act of dedication, grasp at any mark or harbor any craving attachment to it, this is called 'wrongful dedication.'"

It is for this reason that every bodhisattva-mahāsattva ought, in accordance with the very mark of the Dharma as known by the buddhas themselves, to dedicate merit by means of this very mark of the Dharma, thereby able to arrive at unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening — this is called "right dedication."

A note on this closing move

The chapter's own final turn is worth pausing on: after two entire chapters spent naming names — over a hundred buddhas, more than a hundred bodhisattvas, each to be called upon, revered, and held in mindfulness — the closing teaching on dedication insists that the very highest form of this practice involves grasping at no mark whatsoever, not even the mark of the merit being dedicated. The easy path this fascicle has spent so long populating with countless nameable figures closes by folding back into the same signless, markless dialectic that closed the previous fascicle's own account of irreversibility. The easy path and the difficult path's own philosophical rigor, it turns out, are not actually two separate destinations.
