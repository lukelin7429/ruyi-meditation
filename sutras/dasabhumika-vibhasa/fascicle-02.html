Seven Marks, Seven Fears, Twenty-Seven Dharmas

Nāgārjuna turns from why the ten grounds are undertaken at all to what the first ground actually looks like: seven marks that identify a bodhisattva who has attained it, seven fears they are free of — grounded in a dialectical demonstration that no self exists to fear anything — twenty-seven dharmas that purify this ground once entered, and the opening six of ten vows a bodhisattva makes upon it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1521, 十住毘婆沙論, attributed to Nāgārjuna, tr. Kumārajīva 17 fascicles, 35 chapters

The Seven Marks of the First Ground

Chinese Source

問曰：得初地菩薩有何相貌？

答曰：菩薩在初地，多所能堪受，不好於諍訟，其心多喜悅，常樂於清淨，悲心愍眾生，無有瞋恚心，多行是七事。

菩薩若得初地，即有是七相：「能堪受」者，能為難事修集無量福德善根，於無量恒河沙劫往來生死，教堅心難化惡眾生，心不退沒，能堪受如是等事故，名為堪忍。

「無諍訟」者，雖能成大事而不與人諍競、共相違返。

「喜」者，能令身得柔軟，心得安隱。

「悅」者，於轉上法中，心得踊悅。

「清淨」者，離諸煩惱垢濁。有人言：信解名為清淨。有人言：堅固信名為清淨，是清淨心於佛法僧寶，於苦集滅道諦，於六波羅蜜，於菩薩十地，於空、無相、無作法。略而言之，一切深經，諸菩薩及其所行一切佛法，悉皆心信清淨。

「悲」者，於眾生憐愍救護，是悲漸漸增長而成大悲。有人言：在菩薩心名為悲，悲及眾生名為大悲，大悲以十因緣生，如第三地中廣說。

「不瞋」者，是菩薩結未斷故，名為行善心少於瞋恨。

如是菩薩在於初地，心不畏沒故，名為能有堪忍。樂寂滅故，名為不好諍訟。得順阿耨多羅三藐三菩提大悲故，名為心多喜。離諸煩惱垢濁故，於佛法僧寶諸菩薩所，心常清淨。心安隱無患故，名為心悅。深愍眾生故，名為悲。心常樂慈行故，名為不瞋。是名菩薩在初地相貌。

問曰：何故不說菩薩於初地中有此七事而言多？

答曰：是菩薩漏未盡故，或時懈怠於此事中暫有廢退，以其多行故說為多。於初地中已得是法，後諸地中轉轉增益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: What are the marks of a bodhisattva who has attained the first ground?

Answer: "The bodhisattva upon the first ground is able to bear and endure much; does not delight in dispute or contention; whose mind is filled with great joy and delight; who constantly delights in purity; whose compassionate mind pities sentient beings; who is free of any mind of anger — these seven matters are practiced extensively."

Should a bodhisattva attain the first ground, these very seven marks are present at once. "Able to bear and endure": able to undertake difficult tasks and cultivate immeasurable roots of merit and goodness, coming and going through birth and death across immeasurable kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, teaching with a firm mind even evil sentient beings difficult to transform, their mind never regressing or sinking — because they are able to bear and endure matters such as these, this is called forbearance.

"Free of dispute": though able to accomplish great undertakings, they do not contend or compete with others, nor turn against them.

"Joy": able to cause the body to become gentle and supple, and the mind to attain peace and security.

"Delight": within the dharmas that grow ever more sublime, the mind attains a leaping delight.

"Purity": departed from every affliction, defilement, and turbidity. Some say: faith and understanding are called purity. Some say: firm and unshakeable faith is called purity — this pure mind holds toward the Three Jewels of Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha, toward the noble truths of suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path, toward the six pāramitās, toward the bodhisattva's ten grounds, and toward the dharmas of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness. To speak in brief: toward every profound sūtra, toward the bodhisattvas, and toward every dharma of the Buddha that they practice, the mind's own faith is entirely pure throughout.

"Compassion": to pity and rescue sentient beings — this compassion gradually grows and increases until it becomes great compassion. Some say: when present within the bodhisattva's own mind, it is called compassion; when it extends to sentient beings, it is called great compassion. Great compassion arises from ten causes and conditions, as is expounded at length within the third ground.

"Free of anger": since this bodhisattva has not yet severed every fetter, it is said that, in practicing this wholesome mind, anger and resentment are merely diminished.

Thus, since this bodhisattva, upon the first ground, does not fear being overwhelmed, they are said to be able to bear and endure. Since they delight in tranquil cessation, they are said to be free of delight in dispute. Since they have attained great compassion in accordance with unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, their mind is said to be filled with great joy. Since they have departed from every affliction and defilement, their mind is constantly pure toward the Three Jewels and the bodhisattvas. Since their mind is at peace and free of any affliction, it is said to be filled with delight. Since they pity sentient beings profoundly, this is called compassion. Since their mind constantly delights in loving-kindness, they are said to be free of anger. This, then, is what is meant by the mark of a bodhisattva upon the first ground.

Question: Why does the verse not simply say that the bodhisattva upon the first ground possesses these seven matters, but instead says "extensively"?

Answer: Since this bodhisattva's own outflows are not yet exhausted, there may be times of indolence in which these matters are momentarily set aside or diminished; it is because they are practiced extensively, on the whole, that the verse says "extensively." Having already attained this Dharma upon the first ground, it grows and increases still further upon each of the grounds that follow.

A note on "extensively"

Nāgārjuna's own gloss on the verse's word "extensively" (多) rather than a flat claim of completeness is a small but telling admission: a bodhisattva who has just entered the first ground still has unexhausted outflows, and can still lapse into indolence. The seven marks describe a strong tendency, not an achieved perfection — the very same principle that will govern the next chapter's twenty-seven purifying dharmas, needed precisely because this ground, once entered, still requires refining.

Why Called the Ground of Joy

Chinese Source

問曰：初歡喜地菩薩在此地中名多歡喜，為得諸功德故歡喜為地，法應歡喜，以何而歡喜？

答曰：常念於諸佛，及諸佛大法，必定希有行，是故多歡喜。

如是等歡喜因緣故，菩薩在初地中心多歡喜。

「念諸佛」者，念然燈等過去諸佛、阿彌陀等現在諸佛、彌勒等將來諸佛，常念如是諸佛世尊如現在前，三界第一無能勝者，是故多歡喜。

「念諸佛大法」者，略說諸佛四十不共法：一、自在飛行隨意；二、自在變化無邊；三、自在所聞無礙；四、自在以無量種門，知一切眾生心。如是等法後當廣說。

「念必定諸菩薩」者，若菩薩得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提記入法位得無生法忍，千萬億數魔之軍眾不能壞亂，得大悲心成大人法，不惜身命，為得菩提勤行精進，是念必定菩薩。

「念希有行」者，念必定菩薩第一希有行，令心歡喜，一切凡夫所不能及，一切聲聞、辟支佛所不能行，開示佛法無礙解脫及薩婆若智。

又念十地諸所行法，名為心多歡喜。是故菩薩得入初地，名為歡喜。

問曰：有凡夫人未發無上道心，或有發心者未得歡喜地，是人念諸佛及諸佛大法，念必定菩薩及希有行亦得歡喜，得初地菩薩歡喜，與此人有何差別？

答曰：菩薩得初地，其心多歡喜；諸佛無量德，我亦定當得。

得初地必定菩薩念諸佛有無量功德，我當必得如是之事，何以故？我以得此初地入必定中，餘者無有是心，是故初地菩薩多生歡喜，餘者不爾，何以故？餘者雖念諸佛，不能作是念：「我必當作佛」。

譬如轉輪聖子生是轉輪王家，成就轉輪王相，念過去轉輪王功德尊貴，作是念：「我今亦有是相，亦當得是豪富尊貴，心大歡喜，若無轉輪王相者無如是喜。」

必定菩薩若念諸佛及諸佛大功德威儀尊貴，我有是相必當作佛，即大歡喜，餘者無有是事，定心者深入佛法心不可動。

復次，菩薩在初地念諸佛時，作是思惟：我亦不久，當作利益諸世間者及念佛法，我亦當得相好嚴身，成就佛不共法，隨諸眾生所種善根、心力大小而為說法。

又，我已得善法滋味，不久當如必定菩薩遊諸神通。

又，念必定菩薩所行之道，一切世間所不能信，我亦當行。如是念已心多歡喜，餘者不爾，何以故？是菩薩入初地故，其心決定願不移動，求所應求。譬如香象所作，唯有香象能作，餘獸不能。

是故汝所說者，是事不然。

復次，菩薩得初地無諸怖畏故，心多歡喜。若怖畏者，心則不喜。

問曰：菩薩無何等怖畏？

答曰：無有不活畏，死畏、惡道畏，大眾威德畏，惡名毀呰畏，繫閉桎梏畏，拷掠刑戮畏。無我我所故，何有是諸畏？

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: The bodhisattva upon the very first ground, the Ground of Joy, is said, while abiding upon this ground, to be filled with great joy — is this joy on account of the merit attained upon this ground itself, this ground's own dharma naturally giving rise to joy, or through what does this joy come about?

Answer: "Constantly mindful of the buddhas, and of the buddhas' own great Dharma, and of the certain and rare conduct of bodhisattvas — it is for this reason that this joy is so extensive."

It is through causes and conditions such as these that the bodhisattva's mind, upon the first ground, is filled with such extensive joy.

"Mindful of the buddhas": mindful of the buddhas of the past such as Dīpaṃkara, the buddhas of the present such as Amitābha, and the buddhas of the future such as Maitreya — constantly mindful of these World-Honored buddhas as though directly present before them, the very foremost throughout the three realms, unsurpassed by any — it is for this reason that this joy is so extensive.

"Mindful of the buddhas' own great Dharma": to speak briefly, this refers to the forty uncommon dharmas of the buddhas — first, mastery in flying through the air according to their own will; second, mastery in boundless transformation; third, mastery in unobstructed hearing; fourth, mastery in knowing the minds of every sentient being through immeasurable kinds of approach. Dharmas such as these will be expounded at length hereafter.

"Mindful of bodhisattvas of certainty": should a bodhisattva receive the prediction of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, entering the very station of the Dharma and attaining the acceptance of the non-arising of all dharmas, such that not even ten million legions of māra's own host can disturb or confound them, having attained a mind of great compassion and accomplished the dharma of a great person, begrudging neither body nor life, diligently exerting themselves to attain awakening — this is what is meant by mindfulness of bodhisattvas of certainty.

"Mindful of rare conduct": to be mindful of the very foremost and rarest conduct of these bodhisattvas of certainty causes the mind to rejoice — conduct that no ordinary being whatsoever can equal, that no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha is able to practice, opening and revealing the Buddhadharma's own unobstructed liberation and the wisdom of omniscience.

Further, being mindful of every dharma practiced throughout the ten grounds — this too is what is meant by the mind's own extensive joy. It is for this reason that the bodhisattva, upon entering the first ground, names it the Ground of Joy.

Question: There are ordinary beings who have not yet given rise to the aspiration for the unsurpassed path, or those who have given rise to it but have not yet attained the Ground of Joy; such a person, being mindful of the buddhas and the buddhas' own great Dharma, and mindful of bodhisattvas of certainty and their rare conduct, likewise attains joy — what, then, is the difference between this joy and the joy of a bodhisattva who has attained the first ground?

Answer: "The bodhisattva who has attained the first ground, their mind is filled with extensive joy; for the immeasurable merit of all buddhas, I too am certain to attain."

A bodhisattva of certainty who has attained the first ground, being mindful that the buddhas possess immeasurable merit, thinks: "I too am certain to attain matters such as these." And why is this so? Because, having attained this first ground, I have entered into the stage of certainty — no one else possesses this very mind. It is for this reason that the bodhisattva upon the first ground gives rise to such extensive joy, while others do not. And why is this so? Others, though mindful of the buddhas, are unable to give rise to this very thought: "I am certain to become a buddha."

It is just as the son of a wheel-turning sage-king, born into the household of such a king and possessing the very mark of a wheel-turning sage-king, is mindful of the merit and nobility of wheel-turning kings of the past, and thinks: "I too now possess this very mark, and I too shall attain this wealth and nobility" — his mind is filled with great joy; but one who lacks the mark of a wheel-turning sage-king possesses no such joy.

A bodhisattva of certainty, mindful of the buddhas and of the buddhas' own great merit, majestic bearing, and nobility, thinks: "I possess this very mark, and I am certain to become a buddha" — and their mind is filled with great joy; no one else possesses this. One whose mind has attained certainty has penetrated deeply into the Buddhadharma, a mind that cannot be shaken.

Furthermore, the bodhisattva upon the first ground, when mindful of the buddhas, reflects in this way: "I too, before long, shall become one who benefits every world, and, mindful of the Buddhadharma, I too shall attain a body adorned with the excellent marks, accomplishing the buddhas' own uncommon dharmas, and shall expound the Dharma according to the roots of goodness each sentient being has planted and the strength of their own mind.

"And, having already tasted the flavor of this wholesome Dharma, before long I too shall roam through every spiritual power, just as a bodhisattva of certainty does.

"And, mindful of the path a bodhisattva of certainty practices — a path the entire world cannot bring itself to believe — I too shall practice it." Having reflected in this way, their mind is filled with extensive joy; no one else possesses this. And why is this so? Because this bodhisattva, having entered the first ground, their mind's own vow is resolute and unmoving, seeking what ought to be sought. It is just as what a musk elephant alone can accomplish, only a musk elephant is able to accomplish — no other beast is capable of it.

This is why what you have said is simply not so.

Furthermore, the bodhisattva, having attained the first ground, is free of every fear — it is for this reason that their mind is filled with such extensive joy. Were fear still present, the mind could not be joyful.

Question: Of what fears, then, is the bodhisattva free?

Answer: "Free of the fear of not surviving; the fear of death, and the fear of the evil destinies; the fear of a majestic assembly; the fear of a bad reputation and reproach; the fear of imprisonment and shackles; the fear of torture and execution. Since there is no self and nothing belonging to a self, how could any of these fears remain?"

A note on the prince and the musk elephant

Both similes Nāgārjuna reaches for here — the wheel-turning king's son who alone can inherit the throne he already carries the mark of, and the musk elephant who alone can do what only a musk elephant does — locate the difference between ordinary joy and the bodhisattva's own joy not in the content of what is contemplated, but in the standing of the one contemplating it. Anyone can be mindful of the buddhas' own merit; only a bodhisattva of certainty can complete that thought with "and I too am certain to attain this," because only they have already entered the very lineage the thought presupposes.

Freedom From Fear: Not Surviving, Death, and the Evil Destinies

Chinese Source

問曰：菩薩何故住初地無不活畏？

答曰：有大威德故、能堪受故、大智慧故、知止足故。作是念：「我多修福德，有福之人衣服、飲食、所須之物自然即至。如昔劫初大人，群臣士民請以為王。若薄福德者，雖生王家以身力自營，衣、食尚不充足，何況國土！」菩薩作是念：「我多修福德，如劫初王自然登位。我亦如是，亦當復得如是事故，不應有不活畏。」

復次，人雖薄福有堪受力，勤修方便能生衣食。如經說：「以三因緣得有財物：一者、現世自作方便；二者、他力作與；三者、福德因緣。」我能堪受難成之事，現世亦有方便力故，不應有不活畏。

有智之人，少設方便能得自活。能求佛道智慧分，今已有之。是智慧利，能得自活也，不應有不活畏。

復次，菩薩作是念：「我住世間，世間有利、衰、毀、譽、稱、譏、苦、樂，如是八事。何得無也？」不應以不得故，有不活畏。

復次，是菩薩以知足故，好、醜、美、惡，隨得而安，不應有不活畏。若不知足者，設得滿世間財物，意猶不足。如說：若有貧窮者，但求於衣食；既得衣食已，復求美好者；既得美好者，復求於尊貴；既得尊貴已，求王一切地；設得盡王地，復求為天王。世間貪欲者，不可以財滿。

若知足之人，得少財物，今世後世能成其利。是菩薩樂布施故，具足智慧故，多能發起不貪善根。若不樂施、若多作眾惡，以慳貪、愚癡因緣故，增益慳貪不善根。無厭足法，屬於慳貪。是故菩薩多發不貪善根故知足；知足故，無不活畏。

復次，「無死畏」者：多作福德故，念念死故，不得免故，無始世界，習受死法故，多修習空故，菩薩作是念：「若人不修福德則畏於死，自恐後世墮惡道故。我多集諸福德，死便生於勝處，是故不應畏死。」如說：待死如愛客，去如至大會；多集福德故，捨命時無畏。

復作是念：「死名隨所受身，末後心滅為死。若心滅為死者，心念念滅故，皆應是死。若畏死者，心念念滅，皆應有畏。非但畏末後心滅，亦應當畏前心盡滅。何以故？前、後心滅，無有差別故。若謂：畏墮惡道故，畏末後心滅者，福德之人不應畏墮惡道，如先說。我當受念念滅故，於末後心滅，不應有死畏。」

復作是念：「我於無始世界往來生死，受無量無邊阿僧祇死法，無有處所能免死者。佛說：『生死無始。』若人於一劫中死，已積骨高於雪山，如是諸死不為自利不為利他，我今發無上道願，為欲自利亦為利他故，勤心行道有大利故，云何驚畏？」如是菩薩即捨死畏。

復次，作是念：「今此死法，必當應受，無有免者，何以故？「劫初諸大王：頂生、喜見、照明王等，有三十二大人相莊嚴其身，七寶導從，天人敬愛，王四天下，常行十善道，是諸大王皆歸於死。「復有蛇提羅諸小轉輪王，自以威力王閻浮提，身色端正猶如天人，於色、聲、香、味、觸，自恣無乏。所向皆伏，無有退却，善通射術。是諸王等，霸王天下，人民眷屬，皆不免死。「又，諸仙聖、迦葉、憍瞿摩等，行諸苦行、得五神通、造作經書，皆不免死。「又，諸佛、辟支佛、阿羅漢，心得自在，離垢得道，皆為死法之所磨滅，一切眾生無能過者。我發無上道心，不應畏死。「又，為破死畏故，發心精進，自除死畏，亦除於他。是故發心行道，云何於死而生驚畏？」菩薩如是思惟無常，即除死畏。

復次，菩薩常修習空法故，不應畏死，如說：離死者無死，離死無死者；因死有死者，因死者有死。死成成死者，死先未成時；無有決定相，無死無成者。離死有死者，死者應自成；而實離於死，無有死者成。而世間分別，是死是死者；不知死去來，是故終不免。以是等因緣，觀於諸法相；其心無有異，終不畏於死。

「無惡道畏」者：菩薩常修福德故，不畏墮惡道。作是念：「罪人墮惡道，非是福德者。我乃至一念中，不令諸惡得入，而於身、口、意常起清淨業，是故我得無量無邊功德成就。如是大功德聚，云何畏墮惡道？」

復次，菩薩一發心為利安一切眾生故，大慈悲所護故，住四功德處，得無量功德，度一切惡道，何以故？是心勝一切聲聞、辟支佛，如《淨毘尼經》中，迦葉白佛言：「希有！世尊善說！菩薩以是薩婆若多心，能勝一切聲聞、辟支佛。」我成就如是大功德，住如是大法，云何當畏墮於惡道？

復作是念：「我無始已來，往來生死，墮諸惡道，受無量苦，不為自利，亦不利他。我今發無上大願，為欲自利，亦為利他。先來墮惡道，無所利益，今為利益眾生故，設墮惡道不應有畏。」

復次，實行菩薩發如是心：「假令我於阿鼻地獄一劫受苦，然後得出，能令一人生一善心；積集如是無量善心，堪任受化，令發三乘；如是教恒河沙等眾生聲聞乘，恒河沙等眾生辟支佛乘，恒河沙等眾生發大乘，然後我當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，尚不應退沒，何況我今修集無量無邊功德，遠離惡道！」菩薩如是思惟，何得有惡道畏！

復次，如《叫喚地獄經》中說：「菩薩答魔言：「『我以布施故，墮在叫喚獄；所受我施者，皆生於天上。若爾猶尚應，常行於布施；眾生在天上，我受叫喚苦。』」菩薩如是等種種因緣，能遮惡道畏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Why does the bodhisattva, abiding upon the first ground, have no fear of not surviving?

Answer: Because of their great majestic virtue; because of their capacity to bear and endure; because of their great wisdom; and because they know how to be satisfied. They reflect: "I have cultivated much merit and virtue; for one possessed of merit, clothing, food, and every necessity arrive of their own accord. It is just as the great persons at the very beginning of the kalpa, whom the assembled ministers, officers, and people entreated to become their king. But one of scant merit, though born into a royal household, must labor by their own bodily strength — even clothing and food would not suffice for them, let alone an entire realm!" The bodhisattva reflects: "I have cultivated much merit; just as the king at the beginning of the kalpa ascended his throne of his own accord, I too am thus, and I too shall attain matters such as these — I ought not, then, to harbor any fear of not surviving."

Furthermore, even a person of scant merit, possessing the capacity to bear and endure, and diligently cultivating skillful means, is able to bring forth clothing and food. As the sūtra states: "Wealth is obtained through three causes and conditions: first, one's own effort within this present life; second, another's power giving it to one; third, the causes and conditions of one's own merit." Since I am able to bear and endure matters difficult to accomplish, and since I too possess the power of skillful means within this present life, I ought not to harbor any fear of not surviving.

A person of wisdom, exerting even the slightest skillful means, is able to sustain their own life. The very portion of wisdom capable of seeking the buddha-path I already possess now; it is through the keenness of this wisdom that I am able to sustain my own life — I ought not, then, to harbor any fear of not surviving.

Furthermore, the bodhisattva reflects: "I dwell within this world, and this world holds gain and loss, disgrace and honor, praise and blame, suffering and joy — these eight matters. How could I ever be entirely without them?" One ought not, then, to harbor a fear of not surviving simply because one might not obtain these things.

Furthermore, this bodhisattva, knowing contentment, remains at peace with whatever is received, be it fine or coarse, beautiful or ugly — they ought not, then, to harbor any fear of not surviving. But one who does not know contentment, even were they to obtain wealth sufficient to fill the entire world, their mind would still remain unsatisfied. As the verse says: "Should there be one who is poor and destitute, they seek only clothing and food; having obtained clothing and food, they then seek finer things still; having obtained these finer things, they then seek nobility; having obtained this nobility, they seek to rule over every land; and even were they to obtain rule over every land, they would then seek to become a king among the devas themselves. One who craves in this world can never be satisfied by wealth alone."

But one who knows contentment, obtaining even slight wealth, is able to accomplish their own benefit in this present life and in lives to come. This bodhisattva, delighting in giving and possessing wisdom in full measure, is thereby well able to give rise to the root of goodness that is freedom from greed. But should one take no delight in giving, or commit manifold evils, then, through the causes and conditions of stinginess, greed, and delusion, the unwholesome root of stinginess and greed is increased still further. The dharma of insatiability itself belongs to stinginess and greed. It is for this reason that the bodhisattva, giving rise extensively to the root of goodness that is freedom from greed, thereby knows contentment; and knowing contentment, is free of any fear of not surviving.

Furthermore, as for "free of the fear of death": because they have cultivated much merit; because they are mindful, moment by moment, of death; because death cannot be escaped; because, since beginningless time, they have grown accustomed to receiving this very fate of death; and because they have cultivated emptiness extensively — the bodhisattva reflects: "Should a person fail to cultivate merit, they fear death, dreading their own fall into an evil destiny in a life to come. But I have gathered much merit, and at death I shall be reborn instead into a superior place — I ought not, then, to fear death." As the verse says: "Await death as one would a beloved guest; depart as one would arrive at a great gathering; having gathered much merit, at the moment life is relinquished, there is no fear at all."

They further reflect: "'Death' names the cessation of the final moment of mind, according to whatever body has been received. If the cessation of mind is death, then, since the mind ceases moment by moment, every such moment ought equally to be death. Were one to fear death, then, since the mind ceases moment by moment, one ought equally to fear every one of these moments — not merely the final moment's cessation, but every prior moment's cessation as well, for there is no distinction between the cessation of an earlier moment and the cessation of the final one. And should it be said that the final moment's cessation alone is feared because it leads to falling into an evil destiny — a person of merit, as already explained, ought not to fear falling into any evil destiny at all. Since I shall in any case undergo this ceasing moment by moment, I ought not, then, to harbor any fear of death at the final moment's own cessation."

They further reflect: "Since beginningless time, I have come and gone through birth and death within this world, undergoing death immeasurable, boundless, and beyond counting times — there is no place anywhere that could ever escape death. The Buddha has said: 'Birth and death are without beginning.' Should a person die even once within a single kalpa, the bones thus accumulated would already tower higher than the Himalayas — yet none of these countless deaths served either their own benefit or the benefit of others. I have now given rise to the vow for the unsurpassed path, wishing both to benefit myself and to benefit others; diligently applying my mind to practicing this path brings great benefit indeed — why, then, should I be startled or afraid?" In this way, the bodhisattva at once relinquishes the fear of death.

Furthermore, they reflect: "This very fate of death must certainly be undergone, and none can escape it — and why is this so? The great kings at the beginning of the kalpa — Mūrdhaja, Priyadarśana, Dyutimat, and the rest — their bodies adorned with the thirty-two marks of a great person, escorted by the seven treasures, cherished by devas and humans alike, ruling over the four continents, constantly practicing the ten wholesome courses of action — every one of these great kings has nonetheless returned to death. There were, further, the lesser wheel-turning kings of the Sātirā lineage, ruling Jambudvīpa by their own might alone, their bodies as fine in form as those of the devas, indulging without restraint in forms, sounds, fragrances, tastes, and touches, subduing every direction they turned toward, never once retreating, thoroughly skilled in archery — yet every one of these kings, however they lorded over the world with their subjects and households, could not escape death. And the sages and seers, Kāśyapa, Kauśika, and the rest, who practiced every austerity, attained the five spiritual powers, and composed scriptures, could not escape death either. And the buddhas, pratyekabuddhas, and arhats, whose minds have attained mastery, departed from defilement, and attained the path, are every one of them likewise ground away by this very fate of death — no sentient being whatsoever can surpass it. I have given rise to the mind for the unsurpassed path — I ought not, then, to fear death. And it is precisely in order to break through the fear of death that I have given rise to this aspiration and diligently exert myself, removing this fear of death from myself, and removing it from others as well. Since I have given rise to this aspiration and taken up this path for just this reason, why, then, should I feel startled or afraid before death?" Reflecting thus upon impermanence, the bodhisattva at once removes the fear of death.

Furthermore, the bodhisattva, constantly cultivating and practicing the Dharma of emptiness, ought not to fear death. As the verse says: "Apart from death, there is no death; apart from death, there is no one who dies. It is because of death that there is one who dies; it is because of one who dies that there is death. Death is established, and one who dies is thereby established — yet before death itself is established, there can be no fixed mark of either at all: no death, and no one established. Were there one who dies apart from death, this one who dies would have to be established on its own; yet, truly apart from death, there is no such one who dies that could be established. Yet the world distinguishes 'this is death' and 'this is one who dies' — not knowing that death itself neither comes nor goes, and it is for this very reason that beings never escape it in the end. Contemplating the mark of every dharma by means of causes and conditions such as these, their mind remains free of any alteration, and they never fear death in the end."

As for "free of the fear of the evil destinies": the bodhisattva, constantly cultivating merit, does not fear falling into an evil destiny. They reflect: "It is the offender who falls into an evil destiny, not one possessed of merit. I do not allow any evil whatsoever to enter, even for a single thought-moment, and I constantly give rise instead to pure conduct of body, speech, and mind — it is for this reason that I have attained immeasurable, boundless merit already accomplished. Possessed of such a great mass of merit, how could I fear falling into an evil destiny?"

Furthermore, the bodhisattva, having once given rise to this aspiration for the sake of benefiting and bringing peace to every sentient being, is protected by great loving-kindness and compassion, abides within the four abodes of merit, and attains immeasurable merit, crossing beyond every evil destiny entirely. And why is this so? Because this very mind surpasses every śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha, just as in the Sūtra of Pure Discipline, Kāśyapa addresses the Buddha, saying: "How rare! How well the World-Honored One has spoken! The bodhisattva, through this mind so abundant in the aspiration for omniscience, is able to surpass every śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha." Having accomplished such great merit as this, abiding within such a great Dharma as this, how could I possibly fear falling into an evil destiny?

They further reflect: "Since beginningless time, coming and going through birth and death, I have fallen into every evil destiny, enduring immeasurable suffering that served neither my own benefit nor the benefit of others. I have now given rise to this great, unsurpassed vow, wishing both to benefit myself and to benefit others. My former falling into evil destinies brought no benefit at all — but now, should I fall into an evil destiny for the very sake of benefiting sentient beings, I ought not to harbor any fear of it."

Furthermore, a bodhisattva of true practice gives rise to this very thought: "Even were I to endure suffering within the Avīci hell for an entire kalpa, and only then be released, if this could cause even a single person to give rise to a single wholesome thought — and, gathering together immeasurable such wholesome thoughts, capable of receiving transformation and giving rise to the three vehicles — were I thereby to teach sentient beings as numerous as the sands of the Ganges to enter the vehicle of the śrāvaka, sentient beings as numerous as the sands of the Ganges to enter the vehicle of the pratyekabuddha, and sentient beings as numerous as the sands of the Ganges to give rise to the Great Vehicle, and only afterward were I myself to attain the mind of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening — even then, my own resolve ought not to waver or sink. How much more so, then, now that I am cultivating and gathering immeasurable, boundless merit, remaining far removed from the evil destinies!" Reflecting thus, how could the bodhisattva possibly harbor any fear of the evil destinies!

Furthermore, as the Hell of Wailing Sūtra states: "The bodhisattva answered māra, saying: 'Though it is on account of my own giving that I fall into the Hell of Wailing, all those who received what I gave are thereby reborn instead into the heavens. If this is truly so, I ought all the more to constantly practice giving — let sentient beings dwell in the heavens above, and let me myself endure the suffering of this wailing hell.'" It is through causes and conditions such as these that the bodhisattva is able to ward off the fear of the evil destinies.

A note on the wailing hell

The bodhisattva's answer to māra — that even the suffering incurred by his own giving is worth it, so long as the recipient benefits, and he would rather bear the wailing hell himself than see beings deprived of what generosity provides them — closes this section on a note distinct from the arguments preceding it. The earlier reasoning proceeds by demonstrating that fear is irrational (death is moment-to-moment already, merit forecloses evil destinies); this final citation instead simply accepts the suffering as a fair price, which is a different kind of fearlessness than the dialectical kind — one built on willingness rather than on argument.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "free of the fear of a majestic assembly": because they have accomplished the wisdom gained through hearing, the wisdom gained through reflection, and the wisdom gained through cultivation, and because they are free of every fault in argumentation, this bodhisattva establishes the very foundation of their own speech, and what they say is without error. They are able, through causal explanations, similes, and conclusions neither too many nor too few, to speak without any doubt or hesitation. Their words never depart from true meaning, and are free of any flattery or deception; they are straightforward and gentle, adorned in every way; easy to understand and easy to remember, their meaning and intent following in proper sequence. They are able to make their own position clear, and able to refute the arguments of others; departed from the four deviant causes, and possessed of the four great causes. Adorning their own speech with qualities such as these, they speak within any assembly whatsoever without the slightest fear.

As for "free of the fear of a bad reputation, and free of the fear of reproach and scolding": because they do not crave gain or support, and because their conduct of body, speech, and mind is pure.

As for "free of the fear of imprisonment, shackles, torture, and scourging": because they are free of any offense; because they hold compassion for every sentient being; because they endure every affliction and suffering; and because, relying upon the very fruit of karma, whatever I myself have done before, I now receive its recompense in turn.

It is through causes and conditions such as these that this bodhisattva is free of the fear of not surviving, and every fear like it.

Furthermore, delighting in contemplating that every dharma is without self, they are thereby free of every fear whatsoever. Every fear arises from the view of self; the view of self is itself the very root of every decline, sorrow, and suffering. This bodhisattva, through the keenness of their own wisdom, penetrates deeply and truly into the real mark of every dharma, and thereby finds no self present at all. Since there is no self, from where could any fear possibly arise?

Question: How, then, does this bodhisattva come to be free of any mind grasping at a self?

Answer: Because they delight in the Dharma of emptiness; because the bodhisattva contemplates the body and thereby departs from self and what belongs to self. As the verse says:

"The mind of self depends upon what belongs to self; what belongs to self arises depending upon self in turn — it is for this reason that self and what belongs to self, these two natures, are both alike empty.

"Self is itself the very meaning of a possessor; what belongs to self is the very thing possessed by that possessor. If there is no possessor at all, then the thing possessed by that possessor is likewise nonexistent.

"If there is no thing possessed by a possessor, then there is likewise no possessor at all. Self is itself merely the view of self; the thing of self is merely the view of what belongs to self — through true contemplation, there is no self at all; and since there is no self, there is likewise nothing that is not-self.

"It is because of grasping that there arises one who grasps; without grasping, there is no one who grasps. Apart from one who grasps, there is no grasping at all — how, then, could grasping be established because of one who grasps?

"If one who grasps is established because of grasping, then this very grasping would remain unestablished; and since this grasping remains unestablished, it cannot in turn establish one who grasps.

"Since one who grasps is itself empty, one cannot say that this is 'self'; and since grasping itself is empty, one cannot say that this is 'what belongs to self.'

"It is for this reason that to say 'self is not self,' or 'both self and not-self,' or 'neither self nor non-self' — every one of these is itself a mistaken view.

"Likewise, to say 'what belongs to self is not what belongs to self,' or 'both self and what belongs to self,' or 'neither self nor what belongs to self' — this too is a mistaken view."

The bodhisattva, constantly delighting in cultivating emptiness and no-self in this way, thereby departs from every fear. And why is this so? Because the Dharma of emptiness and no-self is itself able to depart from every fear whatsoever.

The bodhisattva upon the Ground of Joy possesses marks such as these.

A note on this dialectic

The verse's own tight regress — self depends on what belongs to self, which depends on self, so that neither can be established without first establishing the other — is a compact instance of Nāgārjuna's own signature method, mutual dependence collapsing into mutual emptiness, applied here to a specifically practical end rather than a purely metaphysical one: this is offered as the actual mechanism by which fearlessness becomes possible, not just as abstract philosophy. If nothing has an independent self to begin with, nothing remains available to be afraid for.

Question: The bodhisattva, having already attained the first ground, how then should they cultivate and refine it?

Answer: "The power of faith grows ever more supreme; profoundly practicing a mind of great compassion; with loving-kindness and pity toward every class of sentient being; cultivating a wholesome mind without weariness; delighting in every sublime dharma; constantly drawing near to good and virtuous friends; possessing a sense of shame and reverence; gentle and harmonious of mind; delighting in contemplating the Dharma without attachment; single-mindedly seeking vast learning; free of craving for gain or support; departed from fraud, deception, flattery, and falsehood; not defiling the household of the buddhas; not breaking the precepts or deceiving the buddhas; deeply delighting in the wisdom of omniscience; unmoving as a great mountain; constantly delighting in cultivating and practicing dharmas ever more sublime; delighting in the dharma that transcends the world, not delighting in the dharma of this world — it is precisely this that refines the Ground of Joy, difficult to refine, yet refined nonetheless; it is for this reason that one should constantly, with a single mind, diligently practice these very dharmas. "The power of faith grows supreme": "faith" means that, having heard and seen something, one accepts it without any doubt whatsoever. "Supreme" means surpassing and excellent.

Question: There are two kinds of "supreme": first, abundance; second, excellence. Which of these does the verse speak of here?

Answer: Both of these are spoken of together here. The bodhisattva, having entered the first ground and tasted the flavor of every quality of merit, their power of faith thereby grows ever greater; and it is by means of this very power of faith that they are able to weigh and accept, with full faith, the buddhas' own merit, immeasurable and profound — it is for this reason that this mind is both abundant and excellent at once.

"Profoundly practicing great compassion": because their pity for sentient beings penetrates through to the very marrow of their bones, it is called profound. Because they seek the buddha-path on behalf of every sentient being, it is called great.

"A mind of loving-kindness": constantly seeking to bring benefit and peace to sentient beings. Loving-kindness is of three kinds, to be expounded at length hereafter.

"Cultivating a wholesome mind without weariness": "wholesome dharma" refers to that which can be drawn near to and cultivated, capable of yielding a beloved fruit. While cultivating dharmas such as these, the mind never grows slack or falters. The causes and conditions of this wholesome dharma are named: the four means of gathering others, the ten wholesome courses of action, the six pāramitās, the bodhisattva's ten grounds, and every other quality of merit.

"Delighting in every sublime dharma": constantly reflecting upon and cultivating it, deeply tasting the flavor of this Dharma, until, over time, joy naturally arises — just as a person within a forest of flowers takes delight in beloved colors all around them.

"Constantly drawing near to good and virtuous friends": the bodhisattva has four kinds of good and virtuous friends, to be expounded at length hereafter. Here, "good and virtuous friend" refers to the buddhas and bodhisattvas themselves; constantly drawing near to them with a right mind is able to bring about great delight.

"A sense of shame and reverence": to delight in one's own sense of shame. "Reverence": to be mindful of another's own merit, and to honor that very person. "Gentleness": a mind that is harmonious and delighted, dwelling together with others in peace and joy.

"Delighting in contemplating the Dharma": "dharma" here refers to the five aggregates, the twelve sense-bases, the eighteen elements, emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, and the rest; with right mindfulness, one constantly contemplates these very dharmas.

"Without attachment": "attachment" refers to the mind's own inclination toward some destination; the three states of existence are what sentient beings turn toward. Some say: "The five desires and every wrong view are what beings turn toward. And why is this so? Because the minds of sentient beings are constantly bound and attached to them. The bodhisattva, through the keenness of their own wisdom, holds a mind free of craving and attachment."

"Single-minded": to hold the Buddhadharma in the highest regard, the mind entertaining no other thought at all.

"Seeking vast learning": able to thoroughly investigate the nine divisions of scripture spoken by the Buddha, cultivating and studying them until fully clear — should one's study be scant, it remains incomplete.

"Free of craving for gain or support": "gain" refers to obtaining food, drink, wealth, and the like; "support" refers to reverence, homage, the provision of a seat, and being welcomed and sent off. The bodhisattva ought to bestow such things upon sentient beings themselves, without craving or attachment to them personally.

"Fraud and deception": using dishonest measures and scales, or goods that are not what they claim to be. "Flattery": a mind that is not upright and straightforward. "Falsehood": the five wrong means of livelihood — first, feigning peculiarity; second, feigning intimacy; third, provoking; fourth, alternately praising and disparaging; fifth, seeking further gain through gain already obtained.

A note on this list

Twenty-seven dharmas grouped as a single memorable verse, then unpacked one at a time in the prose that follows, is this treatise's own recurring compositional method — the same shape as the seven marks and the ten powers already seen in Fascicle 1. What distinguishes this particular list is its practical register: unlike the ten powers or the profound-mind catalogue, several of these twenty-seven items (fraud, flattery, feigned intimacy) describe not lofty attainments but everyday temptations of the religious life, addressed at the same doctrinal altitude as compassion and wisdom.

The Five Wrong Livelihoods, and Not Defiling the Buddha's Household

「矯異」者：有人貪求利養故，若作阿練若著納衣、若常乞食、若一坐食、若常坐、若中後不飲漿，受如是等頭陀行，作是念：「他作是行，得供養恭敬，我作是行，或亦得之。」為利養故，改易威儀，名為矯異。

「激動」者：有人不計貪罪，欲得財物，作得物相，如是言：「是鉢好、若衣好、若戶鉤好、若尼師檀好，若我得者，則能受用」。又言：「隨意能施，此人難得。」又至檀越家作是言：「汝家羹飯餅肉香美、衣服復好，常供養我，我以親舊，必當見與。」如是示現貪相，是名激動。

「因利求利」者：有人以衣、若鉢、僧伽梨、若尼師檀等資生之物，持示人言：「若王、王等及餘貴人，與我是物。」作是念：「檀越或能生心：彼諸王、貴人尚能供養，況我不與是人。」因以此利，更求餘利，故名因利求利。是故應當遠離如此諂偽。

「不污諸佛家」者：何等為污諸佛家？

有人言：「若人發求無上道心已，後迴向聲聞、辟支佛道，不能住世繼三寶種，是名污諸佛家。」是義不然！何以故？是人能度生死，又得諸無漏根、力、覺、道，亦是佛子，云何言污諸佛家？如經說：「佛告比丘：汝是我子，從我心生、口生，得法分者。」

又！聲聞人言：「諦、捨、滅、慧處，名諸佛家。」何以故？從是四事，出生諸佛故。若污此四法，名污諸佛家。是故，若人虛妄、慳貪、狂亂、愚癡，是污佛家。若正行此四，則不污諸佛家。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Feigning peculiarity": there are those who, craving gain and support, undertake practices such as dwelling in a forest hermitage, wearing patched robes, always begging for food, eating only a single sitting's meal, always remaining seated, or never drinking any liquid after midday — taking up ascetic practices such as these, they think: "Others who undertake such practices receive offerings and reverence; perhaps I too, undertaking these same practices, shall receive them as well." Altering their own outward bearing for the sake of gain and support — this is called feigning peculiarity.

"Feigning intimacy": there are those who, craving gain, go to the household of a patron and say: "You are no different to me than my own parents, brothers, sisters, or relatives. Should you need anything at all, I am able to provide it; should there be anything to be done, I am able to do it for you; regardless of distance, I am able to come and inquire after you — my dwelling here is entirely for your own sake." Seeking offerings in this way, craving and clinging to their patron, drawing in another's own heart through their words alone — this and the like is called feigning intimacy.

"Provoking": there are those who, unconcerned with the offense of craving, wish to obtain material goods, and so display the appearance of desiring some object, saying: "This bowl is fine, this robe is fine, this door-hook is fine, this sitting mat is fine — were I to obtain it, I could put it to good use." Or they say: "One who is able to give freely, according to their own wish, is a person hard to find." Or, arriving at a patron's household, they say: "Your household's soups, rice, cakes, and meats are fragrant and fine, and your own clothing is fine as well; you have long made offerings to me, and since I am an old and close acquaintance, you are certain to give me this as well." Displaying this very appearance of craving in such ways is called provoking.

"Alternately praising and disparaging": there are those who, craving gain, say to a patron: "You are extremely stingy — you cannot even bring yourself to give to your own parents, brothers, sisters, wife, or relatives; who, then, could ever obtain anything from you?" — until the patron, ashamed, relents and gives something out of embarrassment. Or, arriving at another household, they say instead: "You possess such merit, your birth as a human being has not been in vain — arhats constantly come and go from your very household, sitting and rising and speaking with you," thinking to themselves: "Perhaps this patron will then think: 'No one else, surely, comes and goes from my household in this way — this must refer to me alone.'" This is called alternately praising and disparaging.

"Seeking further gain through gain already obtained": there are those who take robes, bowls, an outer robe, a sitting mat, or other such articles of daily use, and display them to others, saying: "It was the king himself, or a person of like rank, or some other noble person, who gave me this very thing." They think to themselves: "Perhaps this patron will then think: 'Since kings and noble persons themselves are able to make offerings to this one, how could I fail to give to such a person?'" Using gain already obtained in this way to seek out further gain still is called seeking further gain through gain already obtained. It is for this reason that one ought to depart far from flattery and deception such as this.

"Not defiling the household of the buddhas": what, then, constitutes defiling the household of the buddhas?

Some say: "Should a person, having given rise to the aspiration for the unsurpassed path, later turn instead toward the vehicle of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha, unable to remain in the world to carry forward the seed of the Three Jewels, this is called defiling the household of the buddhas." This meaning is not correct! And why is this so? Because such a person is nonetheless able to cross beyond birth and death, and attains the uncontaminated faculties, powers, factors of awakening, and path — such a person too remains a child of the buddhas; how, then, could this be called defiling the household of the buddhas? As the sūtra states: "The Buddha told the monks: You are my own children, born from my own mind, born from my own mouth, sharers in this very Dharma."

Furthermore, followers of the śrāvaka vehicle say: "The abodes of truth, generosity, cessation, and wisdom are called the household of the buddhas." And why is this so? Because it is from these four matters that every buddha comes forth. Should one defile these four dharmas, this is called defiling the household of the buddhas. It is for this reason that a person who is false, stingy and greedy, deranged, or foolish, defiles the household of the buddhas; while one who rightly practices these four does not defile the household of the buddhas.

Some say: "The six pāramitās are the household of the buddhas, since every buddha is born from them; should one transgress against these six matters, this is defiling the household of the buddhas." Some say: "The perfection of wisdom is the mother of all buddhas, and skillful means is their father — this is named the household of the buddhas. Since every buddha is born from these two dharmas, to transgress against this Dharma is to defile the household of the buddhas."

Furthermore, the verse itself states directly what constitutes defilement and non-defilement — namely, "not breaking the precepts" and "not deceiving the buddhas." Should one receive the Buddha's own precepts and yet fail to guard and uphold them, this is to deceive every buddha, and is itself defiling the household of the buddhas. And why is this so? Because, at the moment the precepts are received, one is born into the very household of the buddhas; breaking these precepts thereby deceives every buddha, and this is called defiling the household of the buddhas.

Question: Can a bodhisattva of certainty ever break the precepts?

Answer: Since the afflictions are not yet severed, this matter is indeed to be feared; a bodhisattva who has only recently entered the stage of certainty may, on occasion, break the precepts. Just as it is stated within the Dharma of the Great Victorious Buddha that "Nanda deliberately broke the precepts" — I speak of this matter still with a sense of trepidation. Yet, since the sūtra itself contains this statement, and since I have faith in the Buddha's own word, my own mind accepts it with faith nonetheless. Should one receive the precepts and never break them, thereby never deceiving any buddha, this is called not defiling the household of the buddhas.

Furthermore, "precepts" refers to the three trainings — the training in precepts, the training in mind, and the training in wisdom; to break any of these trainings is called defiling the household of the buddhas. To receive the precepts in accordance with the Dharma, and then afterward to break and destroy them, is called deceiving the buddhas. These two clauses of the verse each carry their own distinct meaning.

"Deceiving the buddhas": to give rise to a vow in vain, and then fail to practice as one has spoken, thereby deceiving sentient beings — this is called deceiving the buddhas. Furthermore, failing to practice in accordance with one's own words in any dharma whatsoever is likewise called deceiving the buddhas.

A note on Nanda's exception

Nāgārjuna's admission that even a bodhisattva of certainty may still break the precepts, citing Nanda's own case, is notable for the trepidation he attaches to it — he does not soften the doctrine to make it comfortable, but reports it "still with a sense of trepidation" while accepting it purely on the authority of the sūtra that states it. This candor mirrors the honest limit acknowledged back in the seven marks' own gloss on "extensively": certainty of eventual buddhahood is not the same as flawless conduct along the way.

從初發心乃至得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，於其中間所有諸佛，盡當供養、奉給、恭敬。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Firmly abiding in the wisdom of omniscience, unmoving as a great mountain": this bodhisattva, in every vow they give rise to, seeks this wisdom of omniscience alone; whatever causes and conditions may arise, even the suffering of the great hells itself, their mind remains unmoved and unshaken — just as Mount Sumeru, king of mountains, cannot be stirred by any wind whatsoever.

"Constantly cultivating dharmas ever more sublime": from the very first arising of the aspiration, constantly seeking out ever more excellent dharmas, and, having entered the first ground, cultivating still more sublime dharmas further still — proceeding in this way without end, the mind never grows satiated.

"Delighting in the dharma that transcends the world, not delighting in the dharma of this world": "the dharma of this world" refers to whatever accords with worldly matters and increases birth and death — the six destinies, the three states of existence, the five aggregates, the twelve sense-bases, the eighteen elements, the twelve links of dependent origination, and every affliction and contaminated karma. "The dharma that transcends the world" refers to whatever dharma, when applied, is able to lead beyond the three realms — namely, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of awakening, the eightfold path, the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of spiritual power, the three gates of liberation of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, the discipline of moral precepts, vast learning, the wholesome roots of freedom from greed, anger, and delusion, a mind of weariness and renunciation, and freedom from heedlessness. This bodhisattva, through the keenness of their own faculties, takes no delight in the false and empty dharma of this world, but delights only in the true and real dharma that transcends it.

"It is precisely this that refines the Ground of Joy, difficult to refine, yet refined nonetheless": "refine" means to penetrate without any obstruction. It is just as when a person splits bamboo — the very first joint is the most difficult, while every joint thereafter becomes easy. The first ground is difficult to refine; but once it has been refined, every ground thereafter becomes easy of its own accord. And why is this so? Because the bodhisattva, upon the first ground, does not yet possess sufficient strength, their roots of goodness are not yet deep, and they have not yet cultivated wholesome dharmas for very long — the eye and the other faculties still follow after their own sense-objects, and the mind is not yet fully tamed; it is for this reason that the afflictions are still able to cause them affliction. It is just as when a person's own strength is not yet sufficient, rowing against the current proves difficult indeed.

Furthermore, upon this ground, māra and his own subjects create many obstructions — it is by means of the power of skillful means that one must diligently exert effort here; it is for this reason that this ground is called difficult to refine.

Thus, beginning with "the power of faith grows ever more supreme" and ending with "not delighting in the dharma of this world," these twenty-seven dharmas are cultivated in order to refine the bodhisattva's own first ground, the Ground of Joy — it is for this reason that it is said that the bodhisattva should constantly practice these very dharmas.

"Practice" means to remain single-minded and free of heedlessness; constantly practicing and constantly contemplating, removing every fault and evil — this is why it is called refining, just as a road that a person travels is refined until it becomes clear and clean.

These very dharmas are not cultivated only to refine the first ground — every ground thereafter is likewise refined by means of this same Dharma.

Question: You have already explained the skillful means for attaining the first ground, and the dharmas that purify and refine it. How, then, does the bodhisattva abide securely upon it without regressing or losing it?

Answer: Constantly practicing and accomplishing dharmas such as "the power of faith grows ever more supreme" and the rest — this is what is meant by abiding securely upon the first ground.

"Bodhi" means the supreme path; "sattva" means a mind of full depth. Because they deeply delight in bodhi, they are called a bodhisattva. Furthermore, "sattva" also means sentient being; because they cultivate and gather bodhi for the sake of sentient beings, they are called a bodhisattva. As for "supreme dharma": faith and the dharmas like it are called supreme dharmas because they are able to cause a person to accomplish the path to buddhahood.

Having already explained the skillful means for entering the first ground, and the dharmas that purify and refine it: the bodhisattva, through the power of their own vow, comes to enter each of the grounds, and, through accomplishing the merit of the power of faith growing ever more supreme and the rest, abides securely upon that very ground. I shall now distinguish this vow in detail.

"I vow to make offerings and to attend upon, and to revere, every buddha; I vow to guard and uphold, in every respect, the Dharma of every buddha." This is the bodhisattva's own first vow.

From the very first arising of the aspiration up until the attainment of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, every buddha encountered within this span shall be made offerings to, attended upon, and revered in full.

"Offerings" refers to flowers, incense, garlands, banners and canopies, lamps, and the erection of stūpas and shrines, and the like. "Attendance" refers to robes, bedding, and every other necessity. "Reverence" refers to honoring, bowing in homage, welcoming and sending off, and personally attending with palms joined.

Furthermore, to teach and transform sentient beings by means of the Hīnayāna Dharma is called offerings; to teach and transform sentient beings by means of the pratyekabuddha's own Dharma is called attendance; to teach and transform sentient beings by means of the Mahāyāna Dharma is called reverence. This is the first vow.

As for "guarding and upholding the Dharma of every buddha": the bodhisattva reflects: "I ought to guard and uphold the Dharma of every buddha of the past, the future, and the present, throughout the ten directions and the three times."

Question: The buddhas of the past have already passed into extinction, and their Dharma has likewise passed away with them; the buddhas of the future have not yet come forth, and their Dharma does not yet exist either. There is not even a first turning of the Dharma-wheel yet to speak of, let alone any further dharma! How, then, could these possibly be guarded and upheld? Surely it is only the Dharma of the present buddhas that can rightly be guarded, since these buddhas alone are actually present.

Answer: The Dharma of the buddhas of the past, the future, and the present is, in every case, one single essence and one single mark. It is for this reason that, in guarding the Dharma of even a single buddha, one thereby guards the Dharma of every buddha throughout the three times, just as the sūtra states: "The Buddha told the monks: Vipaśyin Buddha's own Dharma — going forth from home, receiving the precepts, donning the robe, holding the bowl, meditative concentration, wisdom, expounding the Dharma, and teaching and transforming beings — was likewise just as my own is." It is for this reason that the objection you have raised does not hold! This is the second vow.

Furthermore: "The buddhas, descending from the Tuṣita heaven, come down into this world; until their teaching and transformation of beings is complete, they then enter forever into the realm without remainder. Dwelling within the womb, and at the moment of birth, going forth from home toward the very seat of awakening, subduing māra and accomplishing the path to buddhahood, first turning the wondrous wheel of the Dharma — welcoming these Thus Come Ones, and at every other time besides, I vow that I shall attain the opportunity, in every case, to make offerings to them with the whole of my own heart."

The buddhas, beginning from their descent down from the Tuṣita heaven into this world, continue on until they finally reach nirvāṇa without remainder. Throughout this span — at the moment of entering the womb, great offerings are set out, and likewise at the moment of birth, of going forth from home toward the very seat of awakening, of subduing the māra-king, of accomplishing the path to buddhahood, and of turning the Dharma-wheel — these Thus Come Ones are welcomed and honored at every one of these moments.

"Every other time" refers to those occasions when the buddhas manifest great spiritual powers, when a great assembly of humans and devas gathers together, and when sentient beings are extensively liberated; at such times, one ought to make offerings to the buddhas with flowers and incense, banners and canopies, music, songs of praise and acclaim, going forth from home to receive the Dharma, and practicing exactly as it has been taught — offering these very finest of all offerings. This is the third vow.

Furthermore: "I vow to teach and transform sentient beings, that every one of them might enter these very paths." "Teaching" means instructing another in the wholesome Dharma. "Transforming" means causing them to depart far from unwholesome dharmas. By means of these two, I shall cause immeasurable, incalculable sentient beings to abide within the path of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha. This is the fourth vow.

Furthermore: "I vow that every sentient being might accomplish the very awakening of a buddha." Should there be a person inclined toward the path of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha, this person, cultivating and gathering the dharma of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha, not yet having entered the very station of the Dharma, I shall teach and transform, causing them to turn instead toward the path to buddhahood. Should there be a person not yet inclined toward either the path of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha, I shall teach and transform them, causing them to turn toward the unsurpassed path to buddhahood. Should there be a person already inclined toward the unsurpassed path to buddhahood, I shall instruct, benefit, and delight them, causing their own merit to increase and grow further still. In this way, I shall teach and transform every sentient being. This is the fifth vow.

A note on the second vow's own argument

The second vow's objection — that guarding the Dharma of past buddhas already extinct, or future buddhas not yet arisen, seems meaningless — receives an answer that echoes the treatise's own repeated move: past, present, and future buddhas share "one single essence and one single mark," so guarding any one buddha's Dharma guards them all at once. This is the same non-differentiation principle that let a single hair's-worth of ocean stand for the whole of extinguished suffering in Fascicle 1 — a recurring willingness to treat apparent particulars as expressions of one underlying reality.

A note on this enumeration

The sixth vow's own method is unlike anything in either fascicle so far: rather than describing a quality or naming a fixed set, it exhausts the Abhidharma's entire technical vocabulary for classifying dharmas — nearly a hundred paired and tripled categories — precisely in order to declare that every last one of them, without exception, is to be brought into the same equality, the same emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness. The vow's own point depends on its own completeness: it is not that most dharmas dissolve into equality while a few technical exceptions remain outside it, but that the entire apparatus of Abhidharma classification itself, exhaustively listed, is what gets subsumed into "equal and without duality." Nothing is quietly exempted.
