What Distinguishes the Path to Buddhahood from Every Lesser Vehicle

Which practitioners the ten wholesome paths lead specifically to the pratyekabuddha ground — deep contemplation, love of solitude, becoming a genuine field of merit. Then, at far greater length, the treatise turns to the bodhisattva's own practice of these identical ten paths: roughly two dozen qualities of vow, compassion, skillful means, and valiant effort, each explained through its own fivefold structure, that mark the difference between walking toward liberation for oneself and walking toward buddhahood for the sake of every being.

分別聲聞辟支佛品之餘

問曰：十善道令何等人至辟支佛地？

答曰：於聲聞所行，十善道轉勝，深禪不隨他，常憙於遠離。

恒樂善修習，甚深因緣法，遠離方便力，及以大悲心。

少欲及少事，惡賤憒閙語，常樂遠離處，威德深重人。

喜為福田地，常觀於出性，成辦有理事，恭敬於諸主。

已成就繫心，知心在所緣，常樂於禪定，中人之勢力。

樂於出家法，善心不縮沒，得慧光明者，或從二勝處，

或三勝處來，十善之業道，能令如是人，至於緣覺地。

於聲聞所行，十善道轉勝者，過聲聞人所行十善道，而不及菩薩所行；作是念：「聲聞人應隨他聞而行道，然後得自證智慧。我則不然，不樂隨他人。以是故，我應令十善道轉勝。以是因緣故，我樂不隨他，十善道令我至辟支佛地。」如是思惟已，常樂於遠離。

作是念：「若我常樂憒閙，則為集諸惡不善法，以近可染、可瞋、可癡事故；於是遠離中，應修習甚深因緣法。」

復作是念：「若我不修習甚深因緣法者，則不得不隨他智。我今何故不常修習甚深因緣，然後可得不隨他智？」

甚深者，難得其底，不可通達。一切凡夫從無始生死中，所有經書及其技藝，皆可得其邊底，唯甚深因緣不可得底。如兔等小虫，不能得大海邊底。若人有方便、大悲心及修集甚深因緣，即得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。若離此二事，修集甚深因緣智，則成辟支佛。

方便名於成就教化眾生中，種種思惟而不錯謬，亦於甚深法不取相。

大悲名深憐愍眾生，勝聲聞、辟支佛，何況凡夫！

少欲、少事，惡賤憒鬧語，如是則得辟支佛地。

若大欲、大事、好聚眾人，為方便、大悲所護者，阿耨多羅三藐三菩提則為易得。

何以故？求辟支佛人，少欲者作是念：「但自度身」；少事者，「但自成就善根，不及餘人」。是人捨離教化眾生事故，不親近眾閙。

菩薩大欲、大事，作是念：「我應度一切眾生。」以此大欲因緣故，則為大事教化眾生。教化眾生，此非小事，若憎惡憒鬧語，則不成此事。是故菩薩入憒鬧中，亦用憒鬧之語，但無所著。

復次，覆真實功德故，是為少欲；少事務故，名為少事。

惡賤憒鬧，名少欲；樂獨處故，名為少事。如是之人，少欲、少事，不樂眾鬧語。

樂於親近遠離、可畏、深邃之處，其心深大。是人作是念：「若我住遠離、可畏、深邃之處，人則不來。」以遠離處住故，心亦深遠。

若人自不深遠，喜戲調者，外人往來，則不為難。

如是人不與眾生和合，雖捨眾生，亦欲令眾生種諸善根，為大利益；作是念：「我云何不與眾生和合，亦能利益眾生？」如是思惟知已，我當為眾生作福田之利，受其供養；如是雖不與眾生和合，而能作大利益。

是人復思惟：「我云何當得福田地？」即自見知：「若我深樂為福田地，常觀出性，然後福田地法，自然而來；乃至出性之法，亦自然而來——所謂：持戒、禪定、智慧等。」

復作是念：「我當云何疾至福田地及出性法？我當為正觀者，於諸現有理趣事中，皆悉成辦，供養、恭敬諸主——如是福田地及出性法，不久疾得。

「何以故？我當成辦有理之事，正觀諸法，能得不隨他智。

「又供養、恭敬諸主故，令善根增厚；善根增厚故，智慧深厚；智慧深厚故，能通達實事；能通達實事故，能生厭；從厭則生離，從離得解脫，得解脫故，前後所集善根得為福田，然後得證出性之法。

「諸主者，諸佛世尊；是種諸善根時，是最大因緣。」

是人復思惟：「我云何能疾成有理趣事？」是人即自知見：「若我集繫心一處，知其所緣，常樂禪定。」

是人能繫心一處，則能得三昧。得三昧故，有理事皆能成辦。如《經》中說：「得禪定能如實知、如實見。若人已行繫心，則疾入三昧。疾入三昧故，名禪定者、常定者。」

若能如是修集諸法，則為供養、恭敬諸佛。若人以香華、四事供養佛，不名供養佛。若能一心不放逸，親近、修集聖道，是名供養、恭敬諸佛。如《經》說：「般涅槃時，佛告阿難：天雨文陀羅華及栴檀末香，作天伎樂，不名供養、恭敬如來也。阿難！若比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷，一心不放逸，親近修集聖法，是名真供養佛。是故阿難！汝當修學真供養佛。」

如是眾功德，皆是中勢力人。

樂出家、善心不縮沒者，最上勢力，能得成佛；下勢力者，作聲聞；以是故，中勢力人作辟支佛。樂出家故，能成眾功德。

何以故？若在居家，則不能少欲、少事，不能身心遠離，亦不能禪定。若心縮沒，不清淨者，不能成辦眾事，不能知甚深因緣法，不能證出性，不能如法真供養、恭敬諸佛。

如是眾生是中勢力，作是念：「我中勢力人，常樂出家，心不縮沒。諸所願功德事，皆自然來。」復作是思惟：「是中勢力樂為得何果？」即知當得智慧果。何以故？智慧能為照明。如《經》中說：「諸比丘！一切光明中，智慧光為勝。」

復作是念：「我所樂慧光，云何當得？」即知若從二勝處來、若三勝處來。

二勝處者，先已說。三勝處者，所謂諦、捨、寂滅；或諦、捨、慧；或諦、寂滅、慧。

以是故，我當修集如是諸勝處。我修集是已，得智慧光明。所願智慧，自然而至。如是相、如是修集助道法者，十善道能令至辟支佛地。

Distinguishing the Śrāvaka and the Pratyekabuddha Chapter, Continued

Question: What kind of person do the ten wholesome paths bring to the ground of the pratyekabuddha?

Answer: "One whose own practice of the ten wholesome paths grows ever more supreme beyond the śrāvaka's own practice, whose meditation runs deep and follows no other, constantly delighting in dwelling apart.

"Constantly delighting in skillfully cultivating and studying the exceedingly profound dharma of causal origination, the power of skillful means in dwelling apart, together with a mind of great compassion.

"Few desires and few affairs, holding in contempt every clamorous, idle word, constantly delighting in a place apart, a person of deep and weighty majesty.

"Delighting in becoming a field of merit, constantly contemplating the very nature of transcendence, accomplishing every reasonable matter, showing reverence toward every lord.

"Already accomplished in binding the mind, knowing where this mind rests upon its object, constantly delighting in meditative concentration — this is the power of the middling person.

"Delighting in the dharma of going forth from home, a wholesome mind never sinking or shrinking, one who attains the very radiance of wisdom, whether coming from two of the excellent places,

"Or coming from three of the excellent places — this very karmic path of the ten wholesome deeds is able to bring a person such as this to the ground of the pratyekabuddha."

As for "one whose own practice of the ten wholesome paths grows ever more supreme beyond the śrāvaka's own practice": this surpasses the śrāvaka's own practice of the ten wholesome paths, and yet does not reach the bodhisattva's own practice; reflecting thus: "The śrāvaka ought to follow what they hear from another in practicing the path, and only afterward attain their own personally realized wisdom. I myself am not so — I take no delight in following another. It is for this reason that I ought to cause my own practice of the ten wholesome paths to grow ever more supreme. Through this very cause and condition, delighting in following no other, these ten wholesome paths shall bring me to the ground of the pratyekabuddha." Having reflected in this way, they constantly delight in dwelling apart.

They reflect thus: "Should I constantly delight in clamor and crowds, this would only gather every evil, unwholesome dharma, since I would draw near to matters capable of defiling, provoking anger, and provoking delusion; it is within this very dwelling apart, then, that I ought to cultivate and study the exceedingly profound dharma of causal origination."

They further reflect: "Should I fail to cultivate and study this exceedingly profound dharma of causal origination, I would then be unable to attain wisdom that follows no other. Why, then, ought I not constantly cultivate and study this exceedingly profound causal origination, so that I might afterward attain this very wisdom that follows no other?"

As for "exceedingly profound": this refers to something whose own depth is difficult to fathom, unable to be fully penetrated. Every ordinary being, throughout beginningless birth and death, is able to reach the very outer limit of whatever scripture or skill they study — only this exceedingly profound causal origination admits of no such limit whatsoever. Just as a rabbit or some other small creature is unable to reach the very edge of the great ocean. Should a person possess skillful means, a mind of great compassion, and the cultivation of this exceedingly profound causal origination together, they thereby attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. Should they depart from these first two matters, cultivating and studying only this exceedingly profound wisdom of causal origination alone, they thereby become a pratyekabuddha instead.

"Skillful means" refers to, within accomplishing the teaching and transformation of sentient beings, reflecting upon every matter without error, and further, toward this exceedingly profound dharma, never grasping at its own mark.

"Great compassion" refers to a deep pity for sentient beings, surpassing even the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha — how much more so, then, the ordinary being!

Few desires and few affairs, holding in contempt every clamorous, idle word — through this alone, one attains the ground of the pratyekabuddha.

Should one possess great desire, great affairs, delighting in gathering many people, and be protected by skillful means and great compassion, unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening then becomes easy to attain.

And why is this so? One who seeks the ground of the pratyekabuddha, being of few desires, reflects thus: "I need only liberate myself alone"; being of few affairs, they reflect: "I need only accomplish my own roots of goodness, and need not concern myself with others." This person, having relinquished and departed from the very matter of teaching and transforming sentient beings, does not draw near to the clamor of the crowd.

The bodhisattva's own great desire and great affairs consist in reflecting thus: "I ought to liberate every sentient being." Through the very cause and condition of this great desire, they thereby take up the great affair of teaching and transforming sentient beings. Teaching and transforming sentient beings is no trifling matter — should one hold contempt for clamorous, idle speech, this very matter could never be accomplished. It is for this reason that the bodhisattva enters into the very clamor of the crowd, and further makes use of clamorous speech themselves, while remaining attached to none of it.

Furthermore, concealing one's own genuine merit is what is called "few desires"; being free of excessive activity is what is called "few affairs."

Holding clamor and crowds in contempt is called "few desires"; delighting in solitude is called "few affairs." A person such as this, of few desires and few affairs, takes no delight in the clamorous speech of the crowd.

Delighting in drawing near to a place apart, fearsome and deep, their own mind becomes profound and vast. This person reflects thus: "Should I dwell within a place apart, fearsome and deep, others shall not come there." Through dwelling within this very place apart, their own mind likewise becomes profound and far-reaching.

Should a person themselves lack this profundity, delighting instead in frivolous jesting, others coming and going presents no difficulty for them at all.

A person such as this, though not joining together with sentient beings, still wishes, even while relinquishing them, to cause sentient beings to plant every root of goodness, for their own great benefit; reflecting thus: "How might I, without joining together with sentient beings, still be able to benefit them?" Having reflected and known this, they resolve: "I shall become, for the benefit of sentient beings, a field of merit, receiving their own offerings" — in this way, though not joining together with sentient beings, they are nonetheless able to bring about great benefit.

This person further reflects: "How, then, might I attain this very ground of a field of merit?" They thereupon behold and know for themselves: "Should I deeply delight in becoming this very field of merit, constantly contemplating the nature of transcendence, this very dharma of a field of merit shall then naturally arrive of its own accord; and even the very dharma of transcendence itself shall likewise naturally arrive of its own accord — namely, upholding the precepts, meditative concentration, wisdom, and the rest."

They further reflect: "How, then, might I swiftly attain this very ground of a field of merit, and this very dharma of transcendence? I ought to become one of right contemplation, accomplishing every reasonable matter presently at hand in full, making offering and showing reverence to every lord — this very ground of a field of merit, and this dharma of transcendence, shall thereby soon be swiftly attained.

"And why is this so? I shall accomplish every reasonable matter, rightly contemplating every dharma, and thereby attain wisdom that follows no other.

"And further, through making offering and showing reverence to every lord, my own roots of goodness thereby grow ever thicker; through these roots of goodness growing thicker, my own wisdom thereby grows ever deeper; through this wisdom growing deeper, I am thereby able to penetrate genuine reality; through this ability to penetrate genuine reality, I am thereby able to give rise to weariness; from this weariness arises detachment, and from this detachment I attain liberation; through attaining this liberation, every root of goodness gathered before and after this attainment becomes a genuine field of merit, and only then do I attain realization of this very dharma of transcendence.

"'Every lord' refers to every buddha, every world-honored one — at the very moment of planting every root of goodness, this is the very greatest cause and condition of all."

This person further reflects: "How, then, might I swiftly accomplish every reasonable matter?" This person thereupon knows and beholds for themselves: "Should I gather and bind my own mind to a single place, knowing precisely where it rests upon its object, constantly delighting in meditative concentration."

This person, being able to bind their own mind to a single place, is thereby able to attain samādhi. Through attaining this samādhi, every reasonable matter is thereby able to be accomplished. As the sūtra states: "One who attains meditative concentration is able to know truly, and see truly. Should a person already practice binding their own mind in this way, they thereby swiftly enter samādhi. Through swiftly entering samādhi, they are called one of meditative concentration, one of constant concentration."

Should one be able to cultivate and gather every dharma in this way, this itself constitutes making offering and showing reverence to every buddha. Should a person make offering to the Buddha merely through incense, flowers, and the fourfold material offering, this cannot be called genuine offering to the Buddha. Should one be able, with a single-pointed mind, free of heedlessness, to draw near to and cultivate the very noble path, this alone is called making offering and showing reverence to every buddha. As the sūtra states: "At the very moment of entering parinirvāṇa, the Buddha told Ānanda: though the devas rain down mandārava flowers and powdered sandalwood incense, and perform heavenly music, this cannot be called making offering and showing reverence to the Thus Come One. Ānanda! Should a monk, a nun, a layman, or a laywoman, with a single-pointed mind, free of heedlessness, draw near to and cultivate the very noble dharma, this alone is called genuine offering to the Buddha. It is for this reason, Ānanda, that you ought to cultivate and study this genuine offering to the Buddha."

Every merit such as this belongs entirely to the person of middling power.

As for "delighting in going forth from home, a wholesome mind never sinking or shrinking": this is the very highest power, able to attain buddhahood; the very lowest power becomes a śrāvaka; it is for this reason that the person of middling power becomes a pratyekabuddha. Delighting in going forth from home, one is thereby able to accomplish every merit.

And why is this so? Should one remain within the household, one is unable to attain few desires and few affairs, unable to remain apart in both body and mind, and further unable to attain meditative concentration. Should one's own mind sink and shrink, remaining impure, one is unable to accomplish every matter, unable to know this exceedingly profound dharma of causal origination, unable to realize transcendence, and unable to make a genuine offering, in accordance with the Dharma, to every buddha.

A sentient being such as this, of middling power, reflects thus: "I, a person of middling power, constantly delight in going forth from home, my own mind never sinking or shrinking. Every meritorious matter I wish for shall come to me naturally, of its own accord." They further reflect: "What fruit does this very middling power delight in attaining?" They thereupon know that they shall attain the very fruit of wisdom. And why is this so? Because wisdom is able to serve as this very illumination. As the sūtra states: "Monks! Among every radiance whatsoever, the very radiance of wisdom is supreme."

They further reflect: "This very radiance of wisdom I delight in — how, then, shall I attain it?" They thereupon know that they shall come either from two of the excellent places, or from three of the excellent places.

"The two excellent places" have already been explained above. "The three excellent places" refers to truth, relinquishment, and tranquil cessation; or truth, relinquishment, and wisdom; or truth, tranquil cessation, and wisdom.

It is for this reason: "I ought now to cultivate and gather excellent places such as these. Having cultivated and gathered them, I shall attain this very radiance of wisdom. The very wisdom I have wished for shall naturally arrive of its own accord." A mark such as this, and a cultivation of the dharmas that assist the path such as this — these very ten wholesome paths are able to bring one to the ground of the pratyekabuddha.

A note on the field of merit's own paradox

The pratyekabuddha's own reasoning here — withdrawing from ordinary engagement with sentient beings while still wishing to benefit them, and resolving this tension by becoming "a field of merit" that others may draw near to and give to — offers a genuinely coherent account of how solitary practice can remain oriented toward others without direct social engagement: benefit flows not through teaching or companionship but through the very presence of a purified practitioner worth making offerings to. This differs sharply from the bodhisattva's own resolution described just before it, entering the clamor of the crowd directly rather than working around it — the same underlying wish to benefit sentient beings, expressed through two structurally opposite strategies.

大乘品第三十

問曰：如仁已說，十善道能令人至聲聞、辟支佛地，十善業道復令何等眾生至於佛地？

答曰：所行十善道，勝於二種人，無量希有修，勝一切世間。

發堅善二願，成大悲無礙，善受行方便，忍辱諸苦惱。

不捨諸眾生，深愛諸佛慧，於佛力自在，樂盡遍行者。

能破邪見意，愛護佛正法，健堪受精進，堅心化眾生。

不貪著自樂，及無量身命，一切事中上，所作無過咎。

一切種清淨，一切勝處來，善道令此人，至十力世尊。

所修十善道勝二種人者，菩薩修十善道，於求聲聞、辟支佛者為轉勝。

轉勝者，一心修行、常修行，為自利故修行，為他利故修行，清淨修行。

一心行者，用意修行；常修行者，不中休息。

為自利故修行者，生天人因緣、泥洹因緣。

為他利修行者，菩薩修行十善道，迴向利安一切眾生故。以是因緣故，能度過算數眾生。

清淨修行者，不壞行、無雜行、不濁行、自在行、具足行、不貪著行，智者所讚行。

壞者，有行有不行，與此相違，名不壞行。

雜者，自不作令他作，與此相違，名不雜行。

濁者，與煩惱罪業合行，與此相違，名為不濁行。

自在者，破戒人為田業、妻子、財物所繫，不得自在；持戒者，無如是事，隨意自在，無所繫屬。

具足者，盡行一切大小戒，遮止諸煩惱，常憶念守護，為禪定作因緣，迴向佛道，能令同真際法性，是名具足。

不貪著者，不向世間不取戒相自高卑他。

智者所讚者，聲聞法中不隨生死，但為涅槃故，名智者所讚；此大乘法中，尚不迴向聲聞、辟支佛乘，何況生死！但向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是名智者所讚十善道。

The Great Vehicle Chapter

Question: As you yourself have already explained, the ten wholesome paths are able to bring a person to the ground of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha — what kind of sentient being, then, do these very ten wholesome karmic paths bring to the very ground of buddhahood?

Answer: "One's own practice of the ten wholesome paths surpasses both of these two kinds of person, this immeasurable, rare and marvelous cultivation surpassing every world.

"Giving rise to a firm vow and a good vow together, accomplishing great compassion unobstructed, skillfully receiving and practicing skillful means, patiently enduring every suffering and affliction.

"Never abandoning any sentient being, deeply loving the wisdom of every buddha, delighting in exhaustively practicing every buddha's own power and mastery.

"Able to break through every wrong intent, cherishing and protecting the Buddha's own true Dharma, valiantly bearing diligent effort, a firm mind in teaching and transforming sentient beings.

"Never craving one's own pleasure, nor one's own immeasurable body and life, foremost in every matter, whatever is done free of any fault.

"Pure in every respect, coming from every excellent place — this wholesome path brings a person such as this to the World-Honored One of the ten powers."

As for "one's own practice of the ten wholesome paths surpassing both of these two kinds of person": the bodhisattva's own practice of the ten wholesome paths grows ever more supreme beyond that of one who seeks the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha vehicle.

As for "growing ever more supreme": practicing with a single-pointed mind, constantly practicing, practicing for the sake of one's own benefit, practicing for the sake of another's benefit, and practicing in purity.

"Practicing with a single-pointed mind" refers to practicing with one's own intent fully applied; "constantly practicing" refers to never resting midway.

"Practicing for the sake of one's own benefit" refers to the very cause and condition of rebirth among humans and devas, and the very cause and condition of nirvāṇa.

"Practicing for the sake of another's benefit" refers to the bodhisattva's own practice of the ten wholesome paths, dedicating it toward the benefit and peace of every sentient being. Through this very cause and condition, they are able to liberate sentient beings beyond any count.

"Practicing in purity" refers to conduct that is never broken, never mixed, never turbid, free and unhindered, complete and thorough, and free of craving attachment, conduct praised by the wise.

"Broken" refers to practicing at times and not practicing at other times; contrary to this is called unbroken conduct.

"Mixed" refers to never doing something oneself, yet causing another to do it; contrary to this is called unmixed conduct.

"Turbid" refers to conduct joined together with the afflictions and offensive karma; contrary to this is called untarnished conduct.

"Free and unhindered" refers to how one who has broken the precepts is bound by their own field and estate, their own spouse and children, and their own wealth, unable to attain this very freedom; one who upholds the precepts has no such entanglement, remaining free and unhindered exactly as they wish, bound to nothing whatsoever.

"Complete and thorough" refers to fully practicing every major and minor precept, restraining every affliction, constantly holding it in mind and guarding it, making it the very cause and condition of meditative concentration, dedicating it toward the path to buddhahood, able to cause it to accord with the very true nature of reality itself — this is called complete and thorough.

"Free of craving attachment" refers to never turning toward this world, and never grasping at the very mark of the precepts to exalt oneself while demeaning another.

"Praised by the wise" refers to, within the śrāvaka's own dharma, never following birth and death, seeking only nirvāṇa — this alone is called praised by the wise; within this Great Vehicle's own dharma, one does not even dedicate this practice toward the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha vehicle, let alone toward birth and death itself! Directing it only toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening — this alone is called the ten wholesome paths praised by the wise.

A note on this chapter's own scale

This chapter's own opening verse names roughly two dozen distinct qualities in rapid succession — vow, compassion, skillful means, patience, non-abandonment, love of wisdom, mastery, breaking wrong views, guarding the Dharma, valor, capacity, diligence, firm resolve, freedom from craving, foremost conduct, faultlessness, and purity — each of which the prose that follows will unpack one at a time, typically through its own fivefold analysis. The scale of this undertaking mirrors the treatise's own earlier account of the forty unshared dharmas: rather than claim the bodhisattva's superiority through a single sweeping statement, the text builds it up through an exhaustive accumulation of specific, nameable distinctions.

A note on the twenty-eight named hells

A note on lifting a trichiliocosm

The comparison drawn here — an act of supernormal power so vast it could suspend an entire trichiliocosm in space for hundreds of thousands of kalpas, dismissed as "not sufficiently difficult to be remarkable" against the mere vow to become a buddha — makes a deliberate point about where this treatise locates genuine difficulty. Physical feats, however cosmically scaled, remain within the category of things that can simply be accomplished through sufficient power; a vow that commits an entire future across immeasurable time to the benefit of others is a different order of undertaking altogether, one that power alone, without this specific resolve, could never produce.

A note on the five obstructions named

Naming the five specific things capable of obstructing compassion — the sheer horror of hell, the degradation of the animal and ghost realms, an ingrate's own failure to reciprocate kindness, and the general futility of birth and death itself — before declaring compassion genuinely unobstructed only when none of these five succeed, gives this quality a testable content rather than leaving it as an unqualified ideal: a compassion that would flinch before witnessing hell's own suffering, or curdle when a rescued person shows no gratitude, has not yet met this treatise's own bar for "great compassion, unobstructed."

This section opens the treatise's own account of skillful means specifically as a teacher's craft, distinct from the compassion and firm vow discussed in the sections before it: knowing the right place and time, reading another's own genuine inclination, and knowing how to redirect a mind already moving in some direction rather than simply opposing it. The next section continues into the fourth and fifth of these five — the proper sequence for teaching each vehicle in turn, laid out at considerable length — and the specific means of guiding different kinds of listener.

A note on manifesting a woman's own body

The passage on guiding "according to whatever gateway sentient beings themselves delight in" — including manifesting a woman's own body to guide men, or a man's own body to guide women — reflects a well-attested strand of Mahāyāna skillful-means literature in which a bodhisattva's own form is understood as itself provisional, adopted according to what will actually reach a given listener rather than fixed by any inherent necessity. Read alongside the treatise's own earlier account of Ugra's household teaching in Fascicle 7, and the graduated sequence for śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and bodhisattva teaching laid out immediately before it here, this closes the fivefold account of skillful means with its own most concrete illustration: a teacher's own outward form, no less than their own words, is itself an instrument shaped entirely by what the listener in front of them actually needs.

A note on debt to the Buddha's own austerities

The argument against abandoning even the worst person turns, in its own closing lines, on an unusual ground: not merely that the person might still be reformed, but that abandoning them would itself constitute an act of ingratitude toward the Buddha, whose own kalpas of austerity were undertaken precisely for beings such as this one. The logic runs backward from the Buddha's own sacrifice to the present obligation it creates — refusing a single difficult being is reframed not as a private failure of patience but as a betrayal of a debt every bodhisattva already owes.

A note on the four times eight and six times three

The verse's own compressed reference to "four times eight, and further six times three kinds" of the Buddha's own disciples points toward the traditional enumeration of the noble Saṅgha — the four pairs and eight kinds of noble one this treatise has referenced since Fascicle 6, here counted in a slightly different arrangement — used as shorthand within verse to gesture at a body of teaching too extensive to spell out again in full. This is a recurring feature of this treatise's own verse sections: technical enumerations already explained at length elsewhere are compressed into brief numerical tags, trusting the reader to recall the underlying content from earlier fascicles.

A note on the fivefold refutation of self

The fivefold analysis of a possible relationship between self and the five aggregates — identical with them, possessing them, contained within them, containing them, or existing apart from them — is one of the classic Madhyamaka arguments against a substantial self, exhaustively covering every logically available position and refuting each in turn, a technique this treatise's own attributed author, Nāgārjuna, is famous for deploying at length elsewhere. Compressed here into a few brief verses rather than developed at full argumentative length, it functions as a condensed reminder of reasoning this treatise assumes its reader already knows from the broader Madhyamaka tradition.

The striking claim that a well-tended body, however long attended to, "nonetheless breaks this very debt of gratitude owed it" personifies the body as a debtor who ultimately defaults regardless of how much care is invested in it — every meal, every comfort given to it across a lifetime is a kind of loan the body's own impermanence guarantees it can never fully repay. This sits alongside the treatise's own repeated insistence, since Fascicle 6's account of giving, that the body is not something to be indulged, yet the emphasis here falls less on renunciation for its own sake than on simple realism about what any amount of care can actually secure.

"Immeasurable, great merit, its own fruit and benefit swiftly attained — one of wisdom, knowing this, incurs no fault whatsoever afterward.

As for "pure in every respect, coming from every excellent place": through five causes and conditions, every one of these excellent places is pure in every respect: first, a deep mind that is pure; second, a dedication that is pure; third, personally practicing exactly as taught within this very excellent place; fourth, causing others likewise to practice it; fifth, departing from every dharma contrary to these excellent places — namely, false speech, stinginess and craving, frivolity, and delusion. As it is said: "The bodhisattva's own deep, pure mind, departing far from flattery and crookedness, dedicates all four of these excellent places toward the path to buddhahood.

"First cultivating this wholesome dharma oneself, and only afterward causing others to practice it — the bodhisattva who is so is pure within these very four excellent places."

A note on this section's own closing symmetry

The final two qualities in this list — faultless action and purity flowing from the four excellent places — return explicitly to the fourfold framework of truth, relinquishment, cessation, and wisdom this treatise has used as an organizing structure since Fascicle 13's own road simile, closing this long catalogue of roughly two dozen bodhisattva qualities with the same foundational categories it opened toward earlier in this very chapter. The list has now covered vow, firmness, depth of mind, purity, skillful means, compassion, endurance, non-abandonment, wisdom, mastery, valor, and faultless conduct — the remaining section closes the chapter by naming exactly what all of this together produces.

The Ten Powers, and What "World-Honored One" Means

Chinese Source

十善道能令至十力世尊者，如是修習十善業道，能令人至十力。

十力者，名為正遍知，正遍知者則是佛。

以五因緣故名世尊：一、斷過去世疑，二、斷未來世疑，三、斷現在世疑，四、斷過三世法疑，五、斷不可說法疑。如說：無始過去世，通達無有疑，無邊未來世，智通達無疑。

十方無有邊，現在一切世，出過於三世，無為微妙法。

十四不可說，亦通無有疑，是故功德藏，諸佛名世尊。

如是功德成就者，十善業道能令菩薩至阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。是故求佛道者，應如是修十善業。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "these ten wholesome paths are able to bring one to the World-Honored One of the ten powers": cultivating and studying the ten wholesome karmic paths in this very way, one is thereby able to bring a person to these very ten powers.

"The ten powers" refers to perfect, universal knowledge; and one who possesses this perfect, universal knowledge is precisely the Buddha himself.

Through five causes and conditions, one is called "world-honored one": first, severing every doubt regarding the past; second, severing every doubt regarding the future; third, severing every doubt regarding the present; fourth, severing every doubt regarding the dharma that transcends the three times; fifth, severing every doubt regarding the dharma that cannot be expressed. As it is said: "Throughout beginningless ages past, penetrating them fully, without any doubt whatsoever; throughout the boundless ages yet to come, this very wisdom penetrates them fully, without any doubt whatsoever.

"Throughout the ten directions, without any boundary whatsoever, throughout every world presently existing, and transcending even these three times altogether, this unconditioned, subtle and sublime dharma —

"And these fourteen matters that cannot be expressed — this too is penetrated fully, without any doubt whatsoever; it is for this very reason that this treasury of merit, every buddha, is named the world-honored one."

Merit such as this, once accomplished, these ten wholesome karmic paths are thereby able to bring the bodhisattva to unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. It is for this reason that one who seeks the path to buddhahood ought, in just this way, to cultivate these ten wholesome karmic deeds.

A note on this fascicle's own closing arc

This closing definition of "world-honored one" as freedom from doubt across every temporal and even atemporal category — past, future, present, what transcends the three times, and what cannot be expressed at all — brings the fascicle full circle back to the five treasuries of dharma this treatise first named in Fascicle 10's own account of the vajra samādhi, confirming that the entire roughly two-dozen-quality portrait of the bodhisattva just completed converges on precisely the same omniscience the treatise spent its own most sustained debate defending. The chapter's closing line states plainly what every section since the beginning of this fascicle has been building toward: this same practice of the ten wholesome paths, walked by three different practitioners with three different underlying dispositions, produces three entirely different destinations — the śrāvaka's own liberation, the pratyekabuddha's own solitary realization, or buddhahood itself.
