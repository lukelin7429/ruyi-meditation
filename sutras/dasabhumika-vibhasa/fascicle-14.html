The Ten Karmic Paths Analyzed in Full, and Every Destination They Produce

A rigorous Abhidharma-style analysis of the ten unwholesome and wholesome paths — twenty distinctions applied to each, then a further twelve tracing origin, cause, and fruit — followed by every specific destination they produce, from the eight great hells through every heaven up to the peak of existence. The fascicle closes by asking a sharper question: of those who walk this same ten-path road, which ones arrive at the śrāvaka path specifically, and why.

Which Acts Are Bodily, Verbal, or Mental

Chinese Source

分別二地業道品之餘

菩薩住此地，自然不行惡，深樂善法故，自然行善道。

問曰：十不善道自然不作，自然行十善道。此二種道，幾是身行？幾是口行？幾是意行？

答曰：身意二三種，口四善亦爾，略說則如是，此應當分別。

不善身行有三種：所謂奪他命、劫盜、邪婬。

不善口行四種：妄語、兩舌、惡口、散亂語。

不善意行三種：貪取、瞋惱、邪見。

善身行亦有三種：離奪命、劫盜、邪婬。

善口行亦四種：離妄語、兩舌、惡口、散亂語。

善意行有三種：不貪取、不瞋惱、正見。身口意業道，是善不善應須論議，令人得解。

初奪命不善道者：所謂有他眾生，知是眾生，故行惱害，因是惱害則失壽命；起此身業，是名初奪命不善道。

離此事故，名為離奪命善行。

劫盜者：所謂屬他之物，知是物屬他，生劫盜心，手捉此物，舉離此處，若劫若盜，計是我物，生我所心，是名劫盜行。

離此事者，名為離劫盜善行。

邪婬者：所有女人，若為父母所護、親族所護、為姓所護、世法所護、戒法所護；若他人婦，知有鞭杖惱害等障礙，於此事中生貪欲心，起於身業；或於自所有妻妾，若受戒、若懷妊、若乳兒、若非道，是名邪婬。

遠離此事，名為善身行。

妄語者：覆相、覆心、覆見、覆忍、覆欲；知如是相而更異說，是名妄語。

遠離此事，名為遠離妄語善行。

兩舌者：欲離別他，以此事向彼說，以彼事向此說；為離別他故，和合者令別離，別離者則隨順樂為別離、憙別離、好別離，是名兩舌。

離如此事，名為遠離兩舌善行。

惡口者：世間所有惡語、害語、苦語、麁語、弊語，令他瞋惱，是名惡口。

遠離此事，名為離惡口善行。

散亂語者：非時語、無利益語、非法語、無本末語、無因緣語，是名散亂語。

遠離此事，名為離散亂善行。

貪取者：屬他之物，他所欲，他田塢、他財物，心貪取，願欲得。

於此事中，不貪、不妬、不願欲得，是名不貪善行。

瞋惱者：於他眾生瞋恨心、礙心，發瞋恚作是念：「何不打縛殺害！」是名瞋惱。

離如此事，名為無瞋惱善行。

邪見者：言無布施、無有恩報、善惡業無果報、無今世無後世、無父母、無沙門無婆羅門能知此世後世了了通達自身作證，是名邪見。

正見者：為有施者、有恩報、有善惡業報、有今世後世、世間有沙門婆羅門知此世後世了了通達自身作證，是名正見善行，是菩薩如是入正見道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Distinguishing the Karmic Path of the Second Ground Chapter, Continued

"The bodhisattva, dwelling within this ground, naturally never practices evil, since they deeply delight in the wholesome dharma; they thereby naturally practice the wholesome path."

Question: One naturally never commits the ten unwholesome paths, and naturally practices the ten wholesome paths. Of these two kinds of path, how many belong to bodily conduct? How many to verbal conduct? How many to mental conduct?

Answer: "The body and mind each have three kinds, speech four for the wholesome likewise — briefly stated, this is so; this ought now to be distinguished in full."

Unwholesome bodily conduct is of three kinds: namely, taking another's life, robbery and theft, and sexual misconduct.

Unwholesome verbal conduct is of four kinds: false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle speech.

Unwholesome mental conduct is of three kinds: craving and grasping, anger and affliction, and wrong view.

Wholesome bodily conduct is likewise of three kinds: departing from taking life, robbery and theft, and sexual misconduct.

Wholesome verbal conduct is likewise of four kinds: departing from false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle speech.

Wholesome mental conduct is of three kinds: freedom from craving and grasping, freedom from anger and affliction, and right view. This karmic path of body, speech, and mind, whether wholesome or unwholesome, ought now to be discussed in full, so that it may be genuinely understood.

As for the very first unwholesome path, "taking another's life": namely, there exists another sentient being; knowing them to be a sentient being, one deliberately inflicts harm upon them, and, through this very harm, they lose their own life — giving rise to this bodily karma is what is called the unwholesome path of taking life.

Departing from this very matter is what is called the wholesome conduct of departing from taking life.

As for "robbery and theft": namely, an object belonging to another; knowing this object belongs to another, giving rise to a mind of robbery and theft, seizing this object with one's own hand, and removing it from its own place — whether through robbery or theft, reckoning it now as one's own object, giving rise to a thought of "mine" — this is called the conduct of robbery and theft.

Departing from this very matter is what is called the wholesome conduct of departing from robbery and theft.

As for "sexual misconduct": whatever woman is protected by her own parents, protected by her own kin, protected on account of her caste, protected by the law of this world, or protected by the very law of the precepts; or another man's own wife, knowing that there exists the obstruction of the whip, the rod, and every such affliction — giving rise, toward this very matter, to a mind of craving desire, and giving rise to this bodily karma; or, toward one's own wife or concubine, whether she has received the precepts, is pregnant, is nursing a child, or through an improper orifice — this is called sexual misconduct.

Departing far from this very matter is what is called wholesome bodily conduct.

As for "false speech": concealing what is genuinely the case, concealing one's own mind, concealing what one has seen, concealing what one accepts, concealing what one desires — knowing this very matter to be so, and yet speaking of it otherwise, is what is called false speech.

Departing far from this very matter is what is called the wholesome conduct of departing far from false speech.

As for "divisive speech": wishing to separate another from someone else, speaking of this very matter to that person, and speaking of that very matter to this person; for the sake of separating them from one another, causing those in harmony to become separated, and, once separated, according with and delighting in this very separation, taking joy in this separation, favoring this separation — this is called divisive speech.

Departing from this very matter is what is called the wholesome conduct of departing far from divisive speech.

As for "harsh speech": every evil word, harmful word, painful word, coarse word, and corrupt word found within this world, causing another to become angry and afflicted — this is called harsh speech.

Departing far from this very matter is what is called the wholesome conduct of departing from harsh speech.

As for "idle speech": speech at an improper time, speech bringing no benefit whatsoever, speech contrary to the Dharma, speech without any beginning or end, speech without any cause or condition — this is called idle speech.

Departing far from this very matter is what is called the wholesome conduct of departing from idle speech.

As for "craving and grasping": toward an object belonging to another, what another desires, another's own field or dwelling, another's own wealth — the mind craves and grasps at it, wishing and desiring to obtain it.

Toward this very matter, being free of craving, free of envy, and free of any wish or desire to obtain it, is what is called the wholesome conduct of freedom from craving.

As for "anger and affliction": toward another sentient being, a mind of anger and resentment, a mind of obstruction, giving rise to anger, reflecting thus: "Why should I not strike, bind, and slay them!" — this is called anger and affliction.

Departing far from this very matter is what is called the wholesome conduct of freedom from anger and affliction.

As for "wrong view": claiming there exists no giving, no recompense for kindness, that wholesome and unwholesome karma bear no fruit in recompense, that there exists no present life and no life to come, no parents, no ascetic and no brahmin able to know this present world and the world to come, thoroughly and directly, and personally bear witness to it — this is called wrong view.

As for "right view": holding that there does exist giving, that there does exist recompense for kindness, that there does exist a fruit in recompense for wholesome and unwholesome karma, that there does exist a present life and a life to come, that there does exist within this world an ascetic and a brahmin able to know this present world and the world to come, thoroughly and directly, and personally bear witness to it — this is called the wholesome conduct of right view; the bodhisattva, in this way, enters the very path of right view.

A note on this careful catalogue

The precision here — protection by parents, by kin, by caste, by secular law, and by monastic precept named as five distinct grounds making a woman off-limits, or five separate ways speech can conceal what is genuinely the case — reflects this chapter's own stated purpose: not merely to list the ten paths as familiar categories, but to make each one "genuinely understood" through careful definition before subjecting all ten to the far more technical twentyfold and twelvefold analyses that follow.

The Twenty-Fold Analysis, Applied First to Killing

Chinese Source

善道不善道，各二十分別；知何處起等，十二種分別。

菩薩於十不善道、十善道等種種別相，知二十種分別。

又於是二十種分別，善知「從何處起」等十二種分別。

於此十不善道中，有二十種分別：所謂不離奪他命罪：一、是不善。二、欲界繫。三、有漏。四、非心數法。五、心不相應。六、不隨心行。

七、或共心生、或不共心生。何等共心生？實有眾生，知是眾生，以身業故奪其命，是名共心生。云何名不共心生？若人欲殺眾生，捉持牽挽撲著地已，然後能死，是名不共心生。又！身不動，口不言，但生心「我從今日當作殺眾生」者，如是奪他命罪，是名不共心生。又！是不離奪他命者，若睡、若覺，常積習增長，亦名不共心生。

八、或色、或非色：初共心生殺罪是色，第二殺罪、第三、第四非是色。

九、或作、或非作：有色是作，餘者無作。

十、或有緣、或無緣：色是有緣，餘者是無緣。

問曰：是心為有緣，為無緣？

答曰：非有緣。

問曰：若心非有緣，身不動、口不言時，但心生念「我從今日當作殺眾生」者，如是罪業云何名為非緣？

答曰：若殺罪是心，則應有緣；今實殺罪，非是心。若心是殺罪，即是身業；而心實非身業，是故殺生罪不名有緣。但殺生罪共心在身中生，以是無作，故言非緣。

十一、是業。十二、非業相應。十三、不隨業行。十四、或共業生、或不共業生：如共心生無異，但除心與思共生為異。

十五、非先世業報。十六、不可修。十七、應善知。十八、應以慧證，不以身證。十九、可斷。二十、可知見。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The wholesome path and the unwholesome path each receive a twenty-fold analysis; knowing 'from what place a given deed arises' and the rest is a further twelve-fold analysis."

The bodhisattva, toward every particular mark distinguishing the ten unwholesome and ten wholesome paths, knows this twenty-fold analysis.

And, further, toward this very twenty-fold analysis, skillfully knows "from what place a given deed arises" and the rest, a further twelve-fold analysis.

Within these ten unwholesome paths, there exists a twenty-fold analysis: namely, regarding the offense of never departing from taking another's life: first, it is unwholesome. Second, it is bound to the desire realm. Third, it is accompanied by outflows. Fourth, it is not a mental factor. Fifth, it is dissociated from mind. Sixth, it does not follow the conduct of mind.

Seventh, it may either arise together with mind, or arise apart from mind. What is meant by "arising together with mind"? A sentient being genuinely exists; knowing them to be a sentient being, one takes their own life through this very bodily karma — this is called arising together with mind. What is meant by "arising apart from mind"? Should a person, wishing to kill a sentient being, seize, drag, and throw them down upon the ground, so that they die only afterward — this is called arising apart from mind. And further: the body remaining motionless, the mouth remaining silent, and yet merely giving rise to this very thought — "from this day forward, I shall kill sentient beings" — this very offense of taking another's life is likewise called arising apart from mind. And further: one who has never departed from taking life, whether asleep or awake, constantly accumulates and increases this very tendency — this too is called arising apart from mind.

Eighth, it may either possess form, or be without form: the very first instance of this offense of killing, arising together with mind, possesses form; the second, third, and fourth instances of this offense of killing are without form.

Ninth, it may either be an active deed, or not an active deed: whatever possesses form is an active deed; every remaining instance is not an active deed.

Tenth, it may either possess an object, or be without an object: what possesses form has an object; every remaining instance is without an object.

Question: Is this very mind itself something that possesses an object, or is it without one?

Answer: It does not possess an object.

Question: Should the mind be without any object, and, at the very moment the body remains motionless and the mouth remains silent, merely give rise to this thought — "from this day forward, I shall kill sentient beings" — how, then, can this very karmic offense be called "without an object"?

Answer: Should this offense of killing be identical with the mind itself, it ought then to possess an object; yet this offense of killing, in truth, is not identical with the mind. Should the mind itself be this offense of killing, it would then be bodily karma; yet the mind, in truth, is not bodily karma at all — it is for this reason that the offense of killing cannot be called something that possesses an object. It is only that this offense of killing arises together with the mind, present within the body — since this is a non-active deed, it is therefore said to be without an object.

Eleventh, it is karma. Twelfth, it is not associated with karma. Thirteenth, it does not follow the conduct of karma. Fourteenth, it may either arise together with karma, or arise apart from karma: this is no different from arising together with or apart from mind, except that mind and volition arising together is what makes the distinction.

Fifteenth, it is not the fruit and recompense of a past-life karma. Sixteenth, it cannot be cultivated. Seventeenth, it ought to be skillfully known. Eighteenth, it ought to be realized through wisdom, not realized through the body directly. Nineteenth, it can be severed. Twentieth, it can be known and seen.

A note on the offense apart from mind

The distinction drawn here between an offense "arising together with mind" and one "arising apart from mind" addresses a genuinely difficult question in Buddhist ethics: does the wrongness of killing require the killer's own conscious intention present at the exact moment of death, or does merely setting a lethal chain of events in motion — dragging a victim toward their death, or even privately resolving in advance to become a killer — already constitute the same offense? The treatise's own answer treats both as equally real instances of "taking another's life" as a karmic path, even though only the first involves mind and object in the technical sense this analysis requires.

The Same Analysis Applied to Every Remaining Unwholesome Path

Chinese Source

不離劫盜罪、不離邪婬罪、不離妄語罪中：但一、共心生，二、不共心生。一、有色，二、無色。一、作，二、無作。一、有緣，二、無緣。

餘如殺中說。不離兩舌、不離惡口，亦如是。

不離散亂語：或不善、或無記：從不善心生是不善，從無記心生是無記。

或欲界繫、或色界繫：欲界繫者，以欲界身心散亂語，是欲界繫；色界繫亦如是。餘如妄語中說。

貪取：欲界繫。是有漏。心數法。非心相應。非隨心行。心共生。無色。無作。有緣。非業相應。非隨業行。非共業生。非先世業報，除因報。非可修。應善知。應以慧證、身證。可斷。可見知。

瞋惱：或心相應、或心不相應：纏所攝名心相應，使所攝名心不相應。隨心行、不隨心行，亦如是。

共心生、不共心生：有覺眾生，與心共生；無覺眾生，不與心共生。

如心相應、隨心行、共心生，業相應、隨業行、共業生亦如是。

如心不相應、不隨心行、不共心生，業不相應、不隨業行、不與業共生亦如是。餘分別，如貪取中說。

如瞋惱，邪見亦如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the offense of never departing from robbery and theft, the offense of never departing from sexual misconduct, and the offense of never departing from false speech: only first, arising together with mind; second, arising apart from mind; first, possessing form; second, without form; first, being an active deed; second, not an active deed; first, possessing an object; second, without an object.

Every remaining point is as already explained under killing. The offense of never departing from divisive speech, and the offense of never departing from harsh speech, are likewise so.

As for the offense of never departing from idle speech: it may be either unwholesome or indeterminate — arising from an unwholesome mind, it is unwholesome; arising from an indeterminate mind, it is indeterminate.

It may be either bound to the desire realm, or bound to the form realm: as for being bound to the desire realm, this refers to idle speech spoken through a body and mind belonging to the desire realm — this is bound to the desire realm; being bound to the form realm is likewise so. Every remaining point is as already explained under false speech.

As for craving and grasping: it is bound to the desire realm. It is accompanied by outflows. It is a mental factor. It is not dissociated from mind. It does not fail to follow the conduct of mind. It arises together with mind. It is without form. It is not an active deed. It possesses an object. It is not associated with karma. It does not follow the conduct of karma. It does not arise together with karma. It is not the fruit and recompense of a past-life karma, apart from being its own causal recompense. It cannot be cultivated. It ought to be skillfully known. It ought to be realized through wisdom, and realized through the body as well. It can be severed. It can be known and seen.

As for anger and affliction: it may be either associated with mind, or dissociated from mind — what is subsumed within an active entanglement is called associated with mind; what is subsumed within an underlying tendency is called dissociated from mind. Following, or not following, the conduct of mind is likewise so.

As for arising together with mind, or apart from mind: toward a sentient being one is conscious of, it arises together with mind; toward a sentient being one is not conscious of, it does not arise together with mind.

Being associated with mind, following the conduct of mind, and arising together with mind — being associated with karma, following the conduct of karma, and arising together with karma are likewise so.

Being dissociated from mind, not following the conduct of mind, and not arising together with mind — being dissociated from karma, not following the conduct of karma, and not arising together with karma are likewise so. Every remaining distinction is as already explained under craving and grasping.

As with anger and affliction, wrong view is likewise so.

A note on this compressed method

This section's own repeated "as already explained" and "likewise so" is not a shortcut around thoroughness but the source text's own deliberate compositional method: having established the full twenty-fold framework in exhaustive detail for the offense of killing, every subsequent unwholesome path is analyzed only where it genuinely diverges from that established pattern — a technique this treatise has used throughout its own technical passages, letting the reader hold the full structure in mind while the text itself economizes on repetition.

The Twenty-Fold Analysis of the Wholesome Paths

A note on delusion-motivated killing

The examples given for killing motivated by delusion are pointed rather than abstract — naming a specific outside people's own sacrificial practice, and a specific practice of temple killing among Indian devotees seeking heavenly rebirth — treating sincerely religious motivation for killing as no less an instance of delusion than crude ignorance of karmic consequence. The brahmin argument that all worldly wealth already belongs to brahmins by right, so taking it back is no theft, receives the same classification: a rationalized doctrine functions here exactly like simple ignorance, since both equally obscure the genuine fruit and recompense the act will bring.

What is meant by "never departing from killing, and genuinely the offense of killing"?

Should there exist a sentient being, and, knowing them to be a sentient being, one deliberately kills them, taking their own life through this bodily karma — this is called never departing from killing, and likewise the offense of killing.

What is meant by "never departing from killing, yet not the offense of killing"?

This refers to a person who, though they have already created the very cause and condition of killing, the sentient being nonetheless does not die.

And further: the body remains motionless, the mouth remains silent, and yet the mind merely holds the thought, "from this day forward, I shall kill sentient beings" — this is called never departing from killing, yet not the offense of killing.

This twofold distinction becomes a fourfold analysis, namely, the wholesome and the unwholesome each of two kinds.

"Not only the wholesome and unwholesome, twofold karma of body and mind — one ought further to know that there exists yet another distinction besides."

Apart from bodily killing, robbery and theft, and sexual misconduct, every remaining act — striking, binding, confining, restraining, whipping, beating with a rod, dragging, pulling — merely fails to cause death, and this kind of unwholesome bodily karma is not subsumed within taking life and the rest.

Among wholesome deeds, welcoming and seeing off, joining the palms, prostrating, showing reverence, offering greeting, bathing, massaging, giving, and every such wholesome bodily karma is not subsumed within not killing and the rest.

Within mental karma, apart from craving and grasping, anger and affliction, and wrong view, there remain every other unwholesome dharma of failing to guard and restrain the mind, and every underlying tendency and the like.

And further: within mental karma, apart from freedom from craving and grasping, freedom from anger and affliction, and right view, there remain every other wholesome dharma of skillfully guarding and restraining the mind — faith, the precepts, learning, concentration, relinquishment, wisdom, and the like.

"Seven of these are both karma and the path of karma; the remaining three are the path of karma, but not karma itself."

Killing, robbery and theft, sexual misconduct, false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle speech — these seven are karma, and are likewise the path of karma.

Craving and grasping, anger and affliction, and wrong view are the path of karma, but are not karma itself. The very volition associated with these three matters is itself the karma.

Question: Why, then, are these first seven matters both karma and the path of karma?

Answer: Through cultivating and practicing these seven matters, this very tendency grows and increases, leading to hell, the animal realm, and the realm of hungry ghosts — it is for this reason that they are called the path of karma; and, since these seven are themselves capable of being enacted, they are called karma.

As for the three that are the path of karma, but not karma itself: these are themselves the very root of unwholesome karma — it is for this reason that these three are called the path of karma, but not karma itself.

Among the wholesome, this is likewise so — namely, departing from killing, robbery and theft, sexual misconduct, false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle speech: these are both karma, and the path of karma.

The remaining three — freedom from craving and grasping, freedom from anger and affliction, and right view — are the path of karma, but not karma itself. The very volition associated with these three is itself the karma.

Question: Why, then, are these first seven matters both karma and the path of karma?

Answer: Through constantly cultivating and practicing these matters, one is able to reach a good place among humans and devas — this is why they are called the path. Since these seven are themselves capable of being enacted, they are called karma.

Question: Why, then, are the remaining three merely the path of karma, and not karma itself?

Answer: These three are themselves the very root of every wholesome karma — since every wholesome karma proceeds from within them, they are called the path of karma, but not karma itself.

Furthermore: "The dharma of the precepts is itself karma; karma may be either the precepts, or not the precepts."

Karma and the path of karma receive a fourfold analysis.

Bodily and verbal karma are themselves the precepts. Mental karma is karma, but not the precepts.

As for the fourfold analysis of karma and the path of karma: there is karma that is not the path of karma; there is the path of karma that is not karma; there is what is both karma and the path of karma; and there is what is neither karma nor the path of karma.

As for "karma, but not the path of karma": the three kinds of unwholesome bodily karma not subsumed within the path of karma — namely, a mere blow of the fist, a stroke of the rod, and the like; and the three kinds of wholesome bodily karma not subsumed within the path of karma — namely, welcoming, showing reverence, and the like — these two, wholesome and unwholesome karma, are not subsumed within the path of karma.

There are those who say: "These too are the path of karma — and why is this so? Because these two kinds of karma sometimes lead to a good or evil place, they are thereby called the path of karma; yet, since this is not fixed or certain, the text does not name them the path of karma."

As for "the path of karma, but not karma": the latter three unwholesome matters, and the three wholesome ones.

Since these are of the very nature of the afflictions, they are not karma; since they are able to give rise to karma, they are called the path of karma.

These three wholesome matters, being of the very nature of a root of goodness, are not karma; since they are able to give rise to wholesome karma, they are called the path of karma.

As for "both karma, and the path of karma": namely, these seven matters — killing, not killing, and the rest.

As for "neither karma, nor the path of karma": every remaining dharma.

A note on this fourfold logical grid

The distinction between "karma" (the actual enacted deed) and "the path of karma" (the tendency or route that either produces or results from such deeds) resolves an ambiguity easy to overlook in ordinary speech about the ten paths: craving, anger, and wrong view are themselves purely mental states, never literally "enacted" the way killing or theft are, so the treatise locates the actual karma in the volition (cetanā) associated with them, while the states themselves remain merely the path leading toward or away from this karma. The four-cell logical grid this section builds — karma without path, path without karma, both together, neither — is a recognizable Abhidharma technique for exhaustively mapping a conceptual distinction rather than leaving it as a loose intuitive pairing.

Answer: "One who practices unwholesome karma finds it no easy matter to cause another to become wholesome; since they themselves fail to practice what is wholesome, another will not accept their own words with trust."

Should an evil person, who does not themselves practice what is wholesome, wish to cause another to practice what is wholesome, this becomes exceedingly difficult. And why is this so? Because this person themselves fails to practice what is wholesome, another person will not accept their own words with trust. "It is for this reason that you ought first to practice wholesomeness and tranquil cessation yourself, and only afterward teach another person, causing them to practice wholesomeness and tranquil cessation."

This bodhisattva ought, in just this way, to practice the wholesome dharma.

A note on no fixed master

The observation that karmic causes and conditions "truly exist, yet possess no fixed master over it" makes a precise point within this treatise's own Madhyamaka commitments: rebirth in a good or evil place is genuinely determined by the ten paths, real cause producing real effect, and yet this causal chain operates without any controlling agent standing behind it and directing outcomes — neither a cosmic judge nor an unchanging self accumulating merit, only the deeds themselves and their own consequences. This closes the fascicle's own extended technical analysis of the ten paths; the next section turns from analyzing the deeds themselves to mapping every specific destination they produce.

A note on this graded cosmology

This section's own exhaustive mapping — hells scaled downward by "slightly less," human rebirths scaled upward through caste and rank, heavens scaled through the four dhyānas and the formless absorptions up to the peak of existence itself — makes explicit the answer to a question raised and set aside in Fascicle 9's own account of the ten wholesome paths: not just that these deeds bear fruit, but precisely where along this entire cosmological ladder each particular degree of practice lands. The dialogue's own resolution near the middle — that even rebirth in the formless realm, technically achieved through meditative absorption, still depends on the ten wholesome paths as its own necessary foundation — closes off any reading in which meditation alone, detached from ethical conduct, could substitute for this foundation.

若人能取諸佛功德、遍學智慧，十善道必令此人徑至佛道。

隨他聞聲、怖畏三界，取功德少分，是人有二種：十善道能令至聲聞地者、至辟支佛地者。

問曰：是人云何俱從他聞，怖畏三界，取功德少分，十善道能令至聲聞地，至辟支佛地？

答曰：志劣弱者，作阿羅漢；小堅固者，作辟支佛。

問曰：十善道令一切志劣弱者至聲聞地？

答曰：不然！何以故？所謂志弱、樂厭離生死者；非但志劣、無厭離者。

問曰：觀何事，得知樂厭離心？

答曰：觀「有為法無常、一切法無我」，當知是必樂於厭離。

問曰：已知樂厭離，菩薩亦如是觀「有為無常、一切法無我」，是十善道何得不令此人墮聲聞地耶？

答曰：是人深厭離，離大悲故，乃至一念中不樂受生，不信世間有安隱相。

如《經》中佛告諸比丘：「譬如少糞尚臭穢不淨，何況多也？如是一念中受生尚苦，何況多也？諸比丘！當學斷生，莫令更受。」聲聞人信受是語故，乃至一念中不樂受生。

是人復作是念：「世間無常，於所作事及受命，都無安隱相。死常逐人，誰能知死時節？不知死時為受何業果報？為生何心？如是事中不安隱故，不可信故，當疾求盡苦。」

菩薩則不爾——於恒河沙無量阿僧祇劫受生，為得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，度諸眾生。

是故〈偈〉中說：「乃至一念頃，不樂於受生」——善道令是人能至聲聞地。

Distinguishing the Śrāvaka and the Pratyekabuddha Chapter

Question: Are these very ten wholesome karmic paths merely the cause and condition for rebirth among humans and devas, or is there some further benefit besides?

Answer: There is. "Every one of the śrāvaka vehicle, the pratyekabuddha vehicle, and the Great Vehicle takes the ten wholesome paths as their own great benefit."

Every cause and condition for escaping birth and death consists solely in these three vehicles: the śrāvaka, the pratyekabuddha, and the Great Vehicle. All three of these vehicles take the ten wholesome paths as their own great benefit. And why is this so? These very ten wholesome paths are able to bring the practitioner to the ground of the śrāvaka, and are likewise able to bring them to the ground of the pratyekabuddha, and are likewise able to bring a person to the very ground of buddhahood.

Question: What kind of sentient being do these ten wholesome paths bring to the ground of the śrāvaka?

Answer: "One who follows another, lacking great compassion, dreading the three realms, delighting only in a small portion of merit, their own resolve exceedingly weak and feeble.

"Their own mind delighting in weariness and renunciation, constantly contemplating this world's own impermanence, and knowing that every dharma whatsoever is without self.

"Even for the mere span of a single thought, taking no delight in undergoing rebirth, constantly disbelieving that this world contains any dharma of genuine peace and security.

"Contemplating the four great elements as a venomous serpent, the aggregates as a bandit with a drawn blade, the six sense-bases as an empty village, taking no delight in the wealth and pleasure of this world.

"Valuing firmly upholding the precepts for the sake of meditative concentration, constantly delighting in peaceful meditative absorption, cultivating and studying every wholesome dharma.

"Contemplating nirvāṇa alone as the very foremost refuge, constantly seeking the wisdom that exhausts suffering, delighting in gathering and practicing liberation.

"Valuing only their own benefit, having come from each of these excellent places in turn — the wholesome path causes this very person to be able to reach the ground of the śrāvaka."

As for "one who follows another's own voice": this refers to hearing what another has taught, and, according with it, practicing accordingly, without being able to give rise to wisdom of their own.

Question: Do these ten wholesome paths cause every single person who hears the teaching from another to become a śrāvaka?

Answer: Not so! Should one lack great compassion, these ten wholesome paths are able to bring this person to the ground of the śrāvaka; should there exist a bodhisattva who hears the Dharma from every buddha, since they possess great compassion, these ten wholesome paths are unable to bring them to the ground of the śrāvaka.

Question: Do these ten wholesome paths bring every single person who lacks great compassion to the ground of the śrāvaka?

Answer: Not so! One who dreads the three realms — these ten wholesome paths are able to bring this person to the śrāvaka path. Every remaining person who does not dread them is caused to be reborn in a good place among humans and devas, since they still delight in the three realms.

Question: Do these ten wholesome paths bring every single person who dreads the three realms to the ground of the śrāvaka? If this is so, the bodhisattva too dreads the three realms, and yet, for the sake of their own body, and further for the sake of sentient beings, diligently applies effort, seeking nirvāṇa — ought not these very ten wholesome paths likewise bring them to the ground of the śrāvaka?

Answer: It is not necessarily so that every single person who dreads the three realms entirely falls into the ground of the śrāvaka. What, then, is meant by "falling into" it? One who delights in cultivating and practicing only a small portion of merit, receiving and practicing only a small portion of the six pāramitās the Buddha himself teaches and transforms others through — a person such as this falls into the ground of the śrāvaka.

Should a person be able to take up every buddha's own merit, universally studying wisdom, these ten wholesome paths shall necessarily bring this very person directly to the path to buddhahood.

As for "following another, hearing their own voice, dreading the three realms, taking up only a small portion of merit" — this person is of two kinds: one whom the ten wholesome paths are able to bring to the ground of the śrāvaka, and one whom they are able to bring to the ground of the pratyekabuddha.

Question: How, then, can this same person, alike in following another, dreading the three realms, and taking up only a small portion of merit, be brought by these ten wholesome paths to the ground of the śrāvaka in one case, and to the ground of the pratyekabuddha in another?

Answer: "One whose own resolve is weak and feeble becomes an arhat; one whose own resolve is slightly more firm becomes a pratyekabuddha."

Question: Do these ten wholesome paths bring every single person of weak and feeble resolve to the ground of the śrāvaka?

Answer: Not so! And why is this so? This refers specifically to one whose own resolve is weak, and who delights in weariness and renunciation toward birth and death — not merely to one whose own resolve is weak, without any weariness or renunciation at all.

Question: By contemplating what matter does one come to know a mind that delights in weariness and renunciation?

Answer: Contemplating that "every conditioned dharma is impermanent, and every dharma whatsoever is without self" — one should thereby know that this person necessarily delights in weariness and renunciation.

Question: Having already known this delight in weariness and renunciation — the bodhisattva likewise contemplates in just this same way, that "every conditioned dharma is impermanent, and every dharma whatsoever is without self" — how, then, do these ten wholesome paths fail to cause this very person to fall into the ground of the śrāvaka as well?

Answer: This person's own weariness and renunciation is deep, yet departs entirely from great compassion, so that, even for the mere span of a single thought, they take no delight in undergoing rebirth, disbelieving that this world contains any mark of genuine peace and security whatsoever.

As the sūtra states, the Buddha told the monks: "Suppose even a small quantity of excrement is already foul, defiled, and impure — how much more so, then, a great quantity! In just this same way, undergoing rebirth even for a single thought is already suffering — how much more so, then, undergoing it repeatedly! Monks! You ought to study the very severing of rebirth, so that you never undergo it again." The śrāvaka, accepting these very words with faith, thereby takes no delight in undergoing rebirth even for the span of a single thought.

This person further reflects thus: "This world is impermanent, and, within whatever is undertaken and whatever life is received, there exists no mark of genuine peace and security whatsoever. Death constantly pursues every person — who could possibly know the very moment of their own death? Not knowing this moment of death, what fruit and recompense of karma shall then be undergone? What mind shall then arise? Since there exists no peace or security within matters such as these, and since they cannot be trusted, one ought swiftly to seek the very exhaustion of suffering."

The bodhisattva, however, is not so — undergoing rebirth across immeasurable asaṃkhyeya kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, for the sake of attaining unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, liberating every sentient being.

It is for this reason that the verse states: "Even for the mere span of a single thought, taking no delight in undergoing rebirth" — the wholesome path causes this very person to be able to reach the ground of the śrāvaka.

A note on the same contemplation, different fruit

This chapter's own central puzzle — that both the śrāvaka and the bodhisattva contemplate the identical truths of impermanence and non-self, and yet arrive at entirely different destinations — is resolved not through any difference in what is seen, but through what accompanies the seeing: the śrāvaka's own weariness with rebirth is unaccompanied by great compassion, so that even a single further thought's worth of rebirth becomes intolerable, while the bodhisattva's own identical clarity about impermanence and non-self is held together with a compassion vast enough to embrace immeasurable further kalpas of rebirth willingly. The following section names exactly what this weariness contemplates in specific, image-rich detail.

Question: Through delighting in cultivating and gathering what very matter does this person come to take no delight in undergoing rebirth?

Answer: This person contemplates the four great elements — earth, water, fire, and wind — since these readily give rise to anger and resentment, are impure and foul, and know no gratitude, giving rise to a thought of them as a venomous serpent.

The five aggregates of form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness are able to seize away the very life of wisdom, giving rise to a thought of them as a hostile bandit.

The eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind, together with their own sense-bases, depart from permanence, depart from being "unmoving, unchanging, and indestructible," being without self and without anything belonging to self, giving rise to a thought of them as an empty village.

Should a person, toward every experience of rebirth throughout this world, and every article of livelihood and pleasure, recognize them as impermanent, illusory and false, never remaining even for the briefest moment, they thereby give rise to no mind of delight toward them whatsoever.

A person such as this, toward every place of rebirth, gives rise to a thought of it as free of any peace or security whatsoever, with nirvāṇa alone, this single dharma, able to serve as a genuine refuge.

As the sūtra states: "Monks! This entire world is ablaze. Namely, the eye is ablaze, form is ablaze, eye-consciousness is ablaze, eye-contact is ablaze, and the very feeling that arises through the cause and condition of eye-contact is likewise ablaze. Through what matter is it ablaze? Namely, ablaze with the fire of craving, the fire of anger, the fire of delusion, and the fire of birth, old age, sickness, death, sorrow, grief, suffering, and affliction. The ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind are likewise so. Contemplating every conditioned dharma as entirely ablaze in this way, only the dharma of nirvāṇa, tranquil cessation itself, is able to serve as a genuine refuge." Valuing this single dharma of nirvāṇa, one relinquishes every other matter, and diligently practices sitting in meditation.

A note on the fire sermon

The sūtra quoted here on the world's own blaze is among the most celebrated discourses in the entire early canon, traditionally understood as delivered to a thousand fire-worshipping ascetics whose own prior devotion to literal fire made its central image especially pointed — every sense faculty, its object, and the contact and feeling arising between them are themselves burning, not with the sacred fire these ascetics once tended, but with craving, anger, and delusion. The four similes preceding it — elements as venomous serpent, aggregates as bandit, sense-bases as empty village — build toward this same image cumulatively, each contemplation stripping away a different form of false comfort before the fire sermon names what all of them are actually burning with.

Question: Should one merely "contemplate every conditioned dharma as entirely ablaze, with nirvāṇa, tranquil cessation itself, alone able to serve as a genuine refuge," do the ten wholesome paths thereby bring every such person to the ground of the śrāvaka?

Answer: Not so! The very precepts the Buddha established are valued and honored for the sake of meditative concentration. Possessing a resolute mind, never once transgressing them, relinquishing every other matter, delighting only in sitting in meditation. Seeking the very wisdom that exhausts suffering, constantly and diligently cultivating and practicing the causes and conditions of liberation. Should this person, in a former life, have come from one of the excellent places, or from two of them, these ten wholesome paths are able to bring this very person to the ground of the śrāvaka. And why is this so? Through upholding the precepts in purity, the mind thereby holds no regret; through this freedom from regret, one attains delight; through this delight, the body becomes light and gentle; through this lightness of body, the mind becomes joyful; through this joy of mind, one gathers the mind and attains concentration; through this concentration, one gives rise to wisdom that knows things as they truly are; through this true wisdom, one thereby gives rise to weariness and renunciation; from this weariness arises detachment, and from this detachment one attains liberation.

As for those who have come from one, or from two, of these excellent places: just as the venerable Rāhula came from the excellent place of truth; just as the venerable Śroṇa came from the excellent place of relinquishment; just as the venerable Revata came from the excellent place of tranquil cessation; just as the venerable Śāriputra came from the excellent place of wisdom.

There are, further, those who have come from the two excellent places of truth and relinquishment together; or from truth and tranquil cessation together; or from truth and wisdom together; or from relinquishment and tranquil cessation together; or from relinquishment and wisdom together; or from tranquil cessation and wisdom together.

These ten wholesome paths, in this way, are able to bring one to the ground of the śrāvaka.

A note on the four excellent places

The "four excellent places" — truth, relinquishment, tranquil cessation, and wisdom — are the same fourfold framework this treatise first introduced in Fascicle 13's own simile of grass sustaining a traveler's horse along the good road, here applied to explain why different arhats, each named individually, arrived at liberation through recognizably different emphases: Rāhula through truthfulness, Śroṇa through relinquishment, Revata through tranquil cessation, and Śāriputra, unsurprisingly given his own reputation, through wisdom. That a given arhat might combine two of these four excellent places rather than embodying only one closes this fascicle's own extended technical account of exactly what conditions bring a practitioner to the śrāvaka path specifically.
