Dharma-Giving, Refuge, and the Faults of Household Life

Four monks each claim personal authority for what they teach; Nāgārjuna's own answer is to test every one of them against the sūtras and the Vinaya alone, never against the speaker's own standing. From there: the qualities of a true Dharma-teacher, an apophatic account of mindfulness of the Buddha that negates every possible mark, the five precepts, and an extended sūtra cataloguing exactly why household life is itself an affliction to be seen through.

Four Tests Against False Authority

Chinese Source

分別法施品第十三

菩薩於財施，應如是修學，又應修學法施，如說：眾施法施最，　智者應修行。

一切布施中第一、最上、最妙，所謂法施。是施，智者所應行。

問曰：何故但言智者應行法施？

答曰：不智者，若行法施，即說異論。說異論故，自失利，亦失他利。

問曰：何謂異論？

答曰：佛欲滅度時告阿難：「從今日後，依修多羅，莫依人。」阿難云：「何名依修多羅，不依人？」

有比丘來作是言：「我現從佛聞，現從佛受，是法是善，是佛所教。」是比丘語，莫受、莫捨，審諦聽已，應以經律撿其所說。

若不入修多羅，不入毘尼，又復違逆諸法相義，應報是比丘言：「是法或非佛所說，或長老謬受。何以故？是法不入修多羅、不入毘尼，又復違逆諸法相義；是則非法，非善、非佛所教。如是知已，即應除却。」

復有比丘來作是言：「彼住處有大眾，有明經上座，善說戒律。我現從彼聞，現從彼受。是法是善，是佛所教。」

是比丘語，莫受、莫捨，審諦聽已，應以經律撿其所說。若不入修多羅，不入毘尼，又復違逆諸法相義，應報是比丘言：「長老！彼比丘僧法相、善相，或作非法、非善說；或長老謬受。何以故？是法不入修多羅，不入毘尼，又復違逆諸法相義；是則非法，非善、非佛所教；如是知已，即應除却。」

復有比丘來作是言：「彼住處多諸比丘，持修多羅、持毘尼、持摩多羅迦。我現從彼聞、現從彼受，是法是善，是佛所教。」

是比丘語，莫受、莫捨，審諦聽已，應以經律撿其所說。若不入修多羅，不入毘尼，又復違逆諸法相義，應報是比丘言：「長老！彼比丘僧法相善相，或作非法、非善說，或長老謬受。何以故？是法不入修多羅，不入毘尼，又復違逆諸法相義；是則非法、非善、非佛所教；如是知已，即應除却。」

復有比丘來作是言：「彼住處中有長老比丘，多知、多識，人所尊重，我現從彼聞，現從彼受，是法是善，是佛所教」。

是比丘語，莫受、莫捨，審諦聽已，應以經律撿其所說。若不入修多羅，不入毘尼，又復違逆諸法相義，應報是比丘言：「長老，彼諸比丘法相善相，或作非法、非善說，或長老謬受，何以故？是法不入修多羅，不入毘尼，又復違逆諸法相義；是則非法、非善、非佛所教；如是知已，即應除却。」

是四名異論，是故言：「智者不依異論，而行清白法施。」

問曰：云何知諸施中，法施第一？

答曰：經說有二施：財施、法施。二施之中，法施為上。

Distinguishing Dharma-Giving Chapter

The bodhisattva ought to cultivate and study the giving of material wealth in this way, and ought further to cultivate and study the giving of the Dharma, as the verse says: "Among every kind of giving, the giving of the Dharma is foremost — this is what the wise ought to practice."

Among every kind of giving, the very foremost, the highest, the most sublime, is precisely the giving of the Dharma. This giving is what the wise ought to practice.

Question: Why does the verse say only that the wise ought to practice the giving of the Dharma?

Answer: Should one lacking in wisdom practice the giving of the Dharma, they thereby expound deviant teachings. Through expounding deviant teachings, they lose their own benefit, and cause others to lose their own benefit as well.

Question: What, then, is meant by "deviant teachings"?

Answer: When the Buddha was about to pass into extinction, he told Ānanda: "From this day forward, rely upon the sūtras — do not rely upon any person." Ānanda asked: "What is meant by relying upon the sūtras, and not relying upon any person?"

Should a monk come and say: "I myself have directly heard this from the Buddha, and directly received it from the Buddha — this Dharma is wholesome, and is what the Buddha himself taught" — do not accept this monk's own words, yet do not reject them either; having listened carefully and attentively, one ought to examine what they have said against the sūtras and the Vinaya.

Should it fail to accord with the sūtras, fail to accord with the Vinaya, and further contradict the very mark and meaning of every dharma, one ought to reply to this monk: "This Dharma may not have been spoken by the Buddha at all, or the venerable one may have received it mistakenly. And why is this so? Because this Dharma fails to accord with the sūtras, fails to accord with the Vinaya, and further contradicts the very mark and meaning of every dharma — it is therefore not the Dharma, not wholesome, and not what the Buddha himself taught. Having known this, one ought to set it aside at once."

Should a further monk come and say: "In that dwelling place there is a great assembly, with elders learned in the sūtras, well able to expound the precepts and the Vinaya. I myself have directly heard this from them, and directly received it from them — this Dharma is wholesome, and is what the Buddha himself taught" —

Do not accept this monk's own words, yet do not reject them either; having listened carefully and attentively, one ought to examine what they have said against the sūtras and the Vinaya. Do not accept this monk's own words, yet do not reject them either; having listened carefully and attentively, one ought to examine what they have said against the sūtras and the Vinaya. These four are named "deviant teachings" — it is for this reason that it is said: "The wise do not rely upon deviant teachings, but instead practice the pure, unblemished giving of the Dharma."

Question: How, then, does one know that the giving of the Dharma is foremost among every kind of giving?

Answer: The sūtra speaks of two kinds of giving: the giving of material wealth, and the giving of the Dharma. Between these two kinds of giving, the giving of the Dharma is supreme.

A note on these four tests

The four monks in this passage each escalate the authority they claim in turn — a personal report of hearing it from the Buddha, then a great assembly's own senior elders, then monks learned in the full canon, then a single widely honored elder — and the treatise's own answer refuses to be moved by any of these escalating credentials whatsoever. The identical formula is applied to all four without variation: examine the content against the sūtras and the Vinaya alone, and never treat the speaker's own reputation, however exalted, as itself a form of evidence. This is one of the clearest statements in the entire Buddhist canon of what would later be formalized as the "four reliances" (四依), a principle still invoked whenever competing claims about what counts as authentic teaching arise.

The Qualities of a True Dharma-Teacher

Chinese Source

復次——《決定王經》中，　讚說法功德，及說法儀式，　應常修習行。

若菩薩欲以法施眾生者，應如《決定王大乘》經中：稱讚法師功德，及說法儀式，隨順修學。

謂說法者，應行四法。何等為四？一者、廣博多學，能持一切言辭章句。二者、決定善知世間、出世間，諸法生滅相。三者、得禪定慧，於諸經法隨順無諍。四者、不增不損，如所說行。

說法者處師子座，復有四法。何等為四？一者、欲昇高座，先應恭敬禮拜大眾然後昇座。二者、眾有女人，應觀不淨。三者、威儀視瞻，有大人相，敷演法音，顏色和悅，人皆信受，不說外道經書，心無怯畏。四者、於惡言問難，當行忍辱。

處師子座，復有四法。何等為四？一者、於諸眾生，生饒益想。二者、於諸眾生，不生我想。三者、於諸文字，不生法想。四者、願諸眾生，從我聞法者，於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提而不退轉。

處師子座，復有四法。何等為四？一者、善能安住陀羅尼門，深信樂法。二者、善得般舟三昧，勤行精進，持戒清淨。三者、不樂一切生處，不貪利養，不求果報。四者、於三解脫，心無有疑，又能善起諸深三昧，具足威儀。憶念堅固，有念安慧，不調戲，不輕躁，不無羞，不癡亂。言無錯謬，守護諸根，不貪美味，善攝手足，所念不忘，樂行頭陀，分別世間、出世間法。心無疑悔，言辭章句不可窮盡。為諸聽者，求安隱利，不求他過。

有如是法，應處師子座。

復有四法？一、不自輕身，二、不輕聽者，三、不輕所說，四、不為利養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: "Within the Sūtra of the Resolute King, the merit of expounding the Dharma is praised, together with its proper ceremony — one ought constantly to cultivate and practice this."

Should a bodhisattva wish to give the Dharma to sentient beings, they ought to cultivate and study it exactly as within the Sūtra of the Resolute King's Great Vehicle — praising the merit of a Dharma-teacher, and the very ceremony of expounding the Dharma, according with it in practice.

As for one who expounds the Dharma, they ought to practice four dharmas. What are these four? First, vast and extensive learning, able to hold fast to every phrase and expression. Second, resolutely and skillfully knowing the marks of the arising and ceasing of every dharma, whether worldly or transcendent. Third, attaining meditative concentration and wisdom, according with every scriptural teaching without contention. Fourth, neither adding to nor subtracting from it, practicing exactly as it has been taught.

One who expounds the Dharma, ascending the lion's throne, further possesses four dharmas. What are these four? First, wishing to ascend this high seat, one first reveres and bows to the assembly, and only then ascends. Second, should there be women within the assembly, one contemplates them as impure. Third, one's own bearing and gaze possess the very mark of a great person, expounding the sound of the Dharma with a countenance gentle and delighted, so that others accept it with trust; one does not expound the scriptures of an outside path, and one's own mind is free of timidity or fear. Fourth, toward hostile words and challenging questions, one practices patient endurance.

Ascending the lion's throne, there are, further, four dharmas. What are these four? First, giving rise to a thought of benefiting every sentient being. Second, giving rise to no thought of self toward any sentient being. Third, giving rise to no thought that the Dharma is contained within mere written words. Fourth, vowing that every sentient being who hears the Dharma from oneself might never retrogress from unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

Ascending the lion's throne, there are, further, four dharmas. What are these four? First, being well able to abide securely within the gate of dhāraṇī, with deep faith and delight in the Dharma. Second, having well attained the pratyutpanna samādhi, diligently exerting effort, upholding the precepts purely. Third, taking no delight in any place of rebirth whatsoever, craving neither gain nor support, seeking no fruit in recompense. Fourth, holding no doubt whatsoever toward the three liberations, and further being well able to give rise to every profound samādhi, complete in one's own bearing; one's own mindfulness firm, possessing mindfulness and tranquil wisdom, never restless, never frivolous, never shameless, never confused; one's speech free of error, guarding one's own faculties, craving no fine flavor, well restraining one's own hands and feet, never forgetting what one holds in mind, delighting in ascetic practice, distinguishing worldly dharmas from those that transcend the world. One's mind free of doubt or regret, one's own phrases and expressions inexhaustible. For the sake of every listener, one seeks their own peace and benefit, never seeking out their own faults.

Possessing dharmas such as these, one ought to ascend the lion's throne.

There are, further, four dharmas: first, never demeaning oneself; second, never demeaning the listener; third, never demeaning what one expounds; fourth, never acting for the sake of gain or support.

A note on this repeated form

Four separate lists of "four dharmas," each attached to the same image of ascending the lion's throne, is this chapter's own way of building a composite portrait one facet at a time rather than in a single comprehensive statement — outward bearing, inward attitude toward the audience, meditative and moral foundation, and finally a fourth list circling back to guard against the pride that mastering the first three lists might itself invite. The final four items — never demeaning oneself, the listener, the teaching, or acting from mixed motives — function as a check on everything that came before them.

The Bowl of Oil: How the Buddha Himself Taught

Chinese Source

佛告阿難：「說法者，應說何法？阿難！所可說法，不可示，不可說，無相無為。」

「世尊！法若爾者，云何可說？」

「阿難！是法甚深，如來以四相方便而為演說：一、以音聲，二、以名字，三、以語言，四、以義理。

「又以四因緣而為說法：一者、為度應度眾生。二者、但說色受想行識名字。三者、以種種文辭章句利益眾生。四者、雖說名字而亦不得，譬如鉢油，清淨無垢，於中觀者，自見面相。」

「阿難！汝若見若聞智慧男子、若持戒女人、若聖弟子能作是說：『我於鉢油，見實人不？』」

「世尊！我不聞、不見智慧男子、持戒女人、若聖弟子能作是言：『我於鉢油，見真實人。』何以故？智者先知鉢油非有，何況有人！但以假名，說言鉢油而見人相。」

「阿難！如來亦復如是，但以名字假有所說。阿難！如來以四因緣而為說法，眾生聞者，心得安樂，種涅槃因。

「如來說法，音聲遍滿十方世界，眾生聞者，心得歡喜，離諸惡趣生兜術天。如來聲中，無男無女，男不取女相，女不取男相。

如來音者，不惱眾生，不壞諸法，但為示現音聲之性。」

說法者應習行是事，應隨所行而為法施。施者、受者所得果報，後當廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Ānanda: "What dharma ought one who expounds the Dharma actually expound? Ānanda! Whatever dharma can genuinely be expounded cannot be shown, cannot be spoken of at all — it is signless, unconditioned."

"World-Honored One! If the Dharma is truly so, how, then, can it be expounded at all?"

"Ānanda! This Dharma is exceedingly profound; the Thus Come One, through four skillful marks, expounds it nonetheless: first, through sound; second, through names; third, through language; fourth, through meaning and principle.

"And I expound the Dharma through four further causes and conditions as well: first, for the sake of liberating sentient beings who ought to be liberated; second, speaking only of the names of form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness; third, benefiting sentient beings through phrases and expressions of every kind; fourth, though these very names are spoken, nothing whatsoever is actually attained through them — just as a bowl of oil, pure and unclouded, allows one who gazes into it to behold their own reflected face."

"Ānanda! Have you ever seen or heard of a wise man, a woman who upholds the precepts, or a noble disciple, able to say: 'Within this bowl of oil, have I beheld an actual, real person'?"

"World-Honored One! I have neither heard nor seen a wise man, a woman who upholds the precepts, or a noble disciple, able to say: 'Within this bowl of oil, I have beheld a true, real person.' And why is this so? Because the wise already know that this bowl of oil itself does not truly exist as such — how much less, then, could there be an actual person within it! It is merely by means of a provisional name that one speaks of 'the bowl of oil' while beholding the mark of a person reflected within it."

"Ānanda! The Thus Come One is likewise so — expounding only by means of provisional names that are themselves merely provisional. Ānanda! The Thus Come One, through these four causes and conditions, expounds the Dharma, so that sentient beings who hear it attain peace and joy of mind, and plant the very cause of nirvāṇa.

"When the Thus Come One expounds the Dharma, this very sound fills the worlds of the ten directions entirely; sentient beings who hear it attain joy of mind, departing from every evil destiny and being reborn in the Tuṣita heaven. Within the Thus Come One's own voice, there is neither male nor female — a man does not perceive within it the mark of a woman, nor does a woman perceive within it the mark of a man.

"The Thus Come One's own voice troubles no sentient being, and destroys no dharma whatsoever — it serves only to reveal and manifest the very nature of sound itself."

One who expounds the Dharma ought to cultivate and practice this very matter, and ought, according to what they themselves practice, to carry out the giving of the Dharma. The fruit and recompense obtained by both the giver and the recipient shall be explained at length hereafter.

A note on the bowl of oil

The bowl-of-oil simile resolves what could otherwise read as a flat contradiction — a dharma that "cannot be shown, cannot be spoken of" is nonetheless spoken of, at great length, throughout this entire treatise. The reflection seen in a bowl of oil is genuinely visible and genuinely useful for seeing one's own face, and yet no wise person mistakes it for an actual person contained within the oil itself. Every word the Buddha speaks functions the same way: real enough to be heard, understood, and acted upon, while never itself containing the signless reality it merely reflects back to the listener.

Wealth for the Householder, Dharma for the Renunciant

Chinese Source

歸命相品第十四

上已解說財施法施，今更分別。白衣在家者，　應多行財施，餘諸善行法，　今當復解說。

是二施中，在家之人當行財施，出家之人當行法施。何以故？

在家法施不及出家，以聽受法者，於在家人信心淺薄故。

又在家之人多有財物；出家之人，於諸經法讀誦通達，為人解說，在眾無畏，非在家人之所能及。

又使聽者起恭敬心，不及出家。又若欲說法降伏人心，不及出家。

如說：先自修行法，　然後教餘人，乃可作是言：　汝隨我所行。

是事出家者所宜，非在家者所行。

又說：身自行不善，　安能令彼善？自不得寂滅，　何能令人寂？是故身自善，　能令彼行善，自身得寂滅，　能令人得寂。

善法寂滅，是出家者之所應行。又出家之人，於聽法者恭敬心勝。

又出家之人，若行財施，則妨餘善；又妨行遠離阿練若處、空閑林澤。

出家之人若樂財施，悉妨修行如是等事。

若行財施，必至聚落與白衣從事，多有言說；若不從事，無由得財；若出入聚落，見聞聲色，諸根難攝，發起三毒；又於持戒、忍辱、精進、禪定、智慧心薄。

又與白衣從事，利養垢染，發起愛恚、慳嫉煩惱，惟心思惟力而自抑制，心志弱者或不自制，或乃致死，或得死等諸惱苦患。

貪著五欲，捨戒還俗故，名為「死」。

或能反戒，多起重罪，是名「死等諸惱苦患」。

以是因緣故，於出家者稱歎法施，於在家者稱歎財施。

如是廣說在家菩薩所行財施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Taking Refuge Chapter

Having already explained the giving of material wealth and the giving of the Dharma above, I shall now distinguish them further: "Those in white robes, dwelling as householders, ought chiefly to practice the giving of material wealth; the remaining wholesome practices I shall now further explain."

Between these two kinds of giving, the householder ought to practice the giving of material wealth, while the renunciant ought to practice the giving of the Dharma. And why is this so?

The householder's own giving of the Dharma falls short of the renunciant's, since those who hear and receive this teaching hold only shallow faith toward a householder to begin with.

And further, the householder possesses much material wealth; whereas the renunciant, having thoroughly studied and recited the scriptural teaching, is able to explain it to others, entering any assembly without fear — something no householder is capable of matching.

And further, in causing the listener to give rise to a mind of reverence, the householder falls short of the renunciant. And further, should one wish to expound the Dharma so as to subdue another's own mind, the householder falls short of the renunciant.

As the verse says: "First practice the Dharma oneself, and only afterward teach others; only then can one truly say, 'follow what I myself practice.'"

This is fitting for the renunciant, not something the householder is able to practice.

And it further says: "How could one whose own conduct is unwholesome cause another to become wholesome? Should one not have attained tranquil cessation oneself, how could one cause another to attain it? This closes the extended explanation of the giving of material wealth practiced by the householder bodhisattva.

A note on this division of labor

The chapter's own reasoning is practical rather than a claim about intrinsic worth — nothing here suggests dharma-giving is unavailable to a householder in principle, or that wealth-giving is somehow beneath a renunciant. The argument turns entirely on structural risk: a monastic drawn repeatedly into commerce faces predictable exposure to exactly the temptations their own vows exist to guard against, while a householder attempting to teach faces an uphill credibility problem no amount of sincerity easily overcomes. Each vocation is assigned the form of giving least likely to undermine the very discipline that vocation depends on.

A note on this reframing

Each of the three refuges receives a definition that quietly redirects the reader's own attention away from a passive, one-time vow and toward an ongoing standard of conduct: refuge in the Buddha becomes never abandoning bodhicitta, refuge in the Dharma becomes actively drawing near a living teacher and then teaching others in turn, and refuge in the Saṅgha becomes seeking its own merit for the sake of a noble assembly's example rather than for one's own private liberation alone. The formal act of "taking refuge" performed once at ordination is here reinterpreted as a description of an entire way of living.

A note on this apophatic method

The sheer accumulation of negations here — "not by means of" repeated across dozens of categories, from form and the five aggregates down to the buddhas' own ten powers and four fearlessnesses — performs the very point it argues: any single positive description of the Buddha that mindfulness might settle on, however exalted, becomes itself an obstruction if grasped as the Buddha's own true nature. This is the same signless dialectic that closed Fascicle 4's own account of irreversibility, here applied specifically to the devotional practice of buddha-recollection (念佛) that this same treatise's Chapter of Easy Practice, translated in Fascicle 5, spent an entire chapter recommending through calling upon names and visualizing forms. The two teachings are not in tension so much as addressed to different moments: the easy path meets a practitioner where ordinary devotion can actually take hold; this passage describes what that devotion is ultimately pointing toward.

A note on this objection

The objection Nāgārjuna raises against his own earlier teaching — quoting a recognizable proverb about relinquishing the smaller for the sake of the larger, ending with one's own person relinquished for the Dharma itself — argues from a hierarchy of nested loyalties that ultimately still centers the self at its own foundation. The answer simply refuses that hierarchy's own premise: for a bodhisattva, another's benefit is not a rung on a ladder leading back to one's own, but identical with one's own benefit from the start, collapsing the entire structure the objection depends on.

A note on the wine exception

The exception permitting a householder bodhisattva to give wine, when it is genuinely what is requested, stands out precisely because it appears immediately after the treatise's own strongest condemnation of alcohol as "the very gateway to heedlessness and every evil." The exception is not a loosening of the precept itself — the giver still does not drink, and still intends afterward to guide the recipient away from it — but a recognition that the pāramitā of giving, to remain genuinely responsive to what is actually asked for, cannot simply refuse whatever a strict reading of the precepts might discourage; it works instead through relationship and gradual teaching rather than blanket denial.

A note on the general and the soldiers

The closing simile — a king holding a general responsible for casualties among troops he commands, while never blaming the ordinary soldiers themselves, who lack the general's own knowledge — locates moral responsibility precisely at the level of capacity and awareness. The bodhisattva is reproached by the buddhas not simply for the existence of sentient beings who fall into evil destinies, an outcome no single being controls, but specifically for possessing the power to prevent this and failing to exert it — the same standard, scaled up, that any competent leader is held to for the outcomes within their own actual reach.

"'The ordinary being, dwelling within this very household, does what ought not to be done, speaks what ought not to be spoken, and practices what ought not to be practiced. Furthermore: "The bodhisattva ought to know these very faults and evils of household life!"

"Drawing near to giving, to upholding the precepts, to goodness, and to shared delight — should one behold a beggar, one ought to give rise to five sets of three thoughts."

The householder bodhisattva, having thus known the faults of household life, ought to practice giving, upholding the precepts, goodness, and shared delight.

"Giving" refers to relinquishing a mind of craving. "Upholding the precepts" refers to purity of conduct in body and speech. "Goodness" refers to skillfully restraining one's own faculties. "Shared delight" refers to a mind shared together in joy. "Five sets of three thoughts" refers to: upon beholding a beggar, one ought to give rise to these five sets of three thoughts.

The first three: a thought of a good and virtuous friend; a thought of great wealth in a future life; a thought of assisting bodhi.

There are, further, three thoughts: a thought of subduing stinginess and craving; a thought of relinquishing everything; a thought of craving and seeking omniscient wisdom.

There are, further, three thoughts: a thought of according with the Thus Come One's own teaching; a thought of seeking no fruit in recompense; a thought of subduing māra.

There are, further, three thoughts: a thought, upon seeing one who comes to request something, of them as one's own kin; a thought of never abandoning the dharma of gathering others; a thought of relinquishing any wrongful reception of what is given.

There are, further, three thoughts: a thought of freedom from desire; a thought of cultivating loving-kindness; a thought of freedom from delusion.

I shall now explain this fifth set of "three thoughts."

The bodhisattva, on account of one who comes to request something, causes the threefold poison to be weakened and thinned, and, relinquishing the object given, gives rise to a thought of freedom from desire.

Through the causes and conditions of bringing joy to the one who requests something, one's own mind of anger and resentment grows thin — this is called a thought of cultivating loving-kindness.

Through dedicating this very giving toward the unsurpassed path, one's own mind of delusion grows thin — this is called a thought of freedom from delusion.

The meaning of every remaining thought should be understood in just this same way!

A note on this sūtra's own recipient

This sūtra, addressed to a lay elder named Ugra, belongs to a well-attested genre in early Mahāyāna literature aimed specifically at householders who wish to take the bodhisattva path seriously without necessarily renouncing lay life — the Ugraparipṛcchā Sūtra, one of the tradition's earliest surviving texts to grapple directly with what a lay bodhisattva's own practice should look like. Its own unflinching catalogue of similes — a thicket of thorns, a stinging insect, a single drop of honey — is characteristically severe even by the standards of renunciant literature, suggesting the text's own underlying aim is less to condemn household life outright than to strip away any illusion that it could ever be a comfortable, final resting place.

Furthermore: "The bodhisattva, on account of one who comes to request something, thereby completes the six pāramitās; it is through this very cause and condition that, upon seeing such a person, they ought to feel great joy."

The six pāramitās are: giving, upholding the precepts, patient endurance, diligent effort, meditative concentration, and wisdom. It is on account of one who comes to request something that these can be brought to completion. It is on account of this very benefit that the bodhisattva, beholding one who comes to request something even from afar, feels great joy of mind, reflecting: "A field of merit naturally arrives before me; through this very person, I am able to bring the six pāramitās to completion."

And why is this so?

Should one's own mind hold no craving or stinginess toward the object given, this is called the pāramitā of giving.

Giving for the sake of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, this is called the pāramitā of moral discipline.

Should one hold no anger toward the one who requests it, this is called the pāramitā of patient endurance.

At the very moment of giving, having no concern for one's own future scarcity, one's own mind never sinking or retreating, this is called the pāramitā of diligent effort.

Should one, whether at the moment of giving to the one who requests it or at the moment of giving of one's own accord, hold a resolute mind free of any regret, this is called the pāramitā of meditative concentration.

A note on this paired list

The rhythmic structure of this closing comparison — fifteen consecutive pairings, each turning on the same "once given away... remaining in the household" contrast — builds its own argument through sheer repetition rather than variety, in a way this treatise has used before with its enumerated dharma-lists. What makes this particular list distinctive is its own claim about possession itself: an object one keeps is repeatedly described as never having truly become one's own at all, while the very same object, once given away, is what genuinely belongs to the giver — a deliberate inversion of the ordinary intuition that giving something away means losing it.

A note on this cascade

This unbroken cascade of triads — well over thirty sets of "three thoughts" and "three marks" applied to a single relationship — is the most extreme instance in this treatise so far of a compositional method that has recurred throughout: exhaustive enumeration as its own form of argument, wearing down any residual attachment through sheer accumulated weight rather than through any single decisive point. The final line's own careful qualification is easy to miss amid this flood of hostile imagery: even here, the conclusion is not to abandon one's spouse through cruelty, but, failing actual renunciation, simply never to act toward them with any ill will at all — a notably restrained landing point after so relentless a buildup.

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: "Should one hold a partial love toward one's own child, one ought, through the power of wisdom, to relinquish it at once.

"On account of this very child, one practices equanimity, extending loving-kindness universally to every sentient being."

Should the householder bodhisattva know for themselves that their own love toward their own child is unduly partial, they ought, through the power of wisdom, to reflect upon it and relinquish this very partiality. As for "the power of wisdom," one ought to reflect thus: "It is only through a mind of equanimity that the bodhisattva possesses unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening at all; a mind that ranks higher and lower possesses no bodhi whatsoever. This very unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening is attained through a single mark, the mark of signlessness, and is never attained through any mark of distinction and difference. I now seek unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening — should my own love toward my child be unduly partial, this itself constitutes a ranking of higher and lower, and cannot be called equanimity at all; this is itself a mark of distinction, not the single mark. Should this be so, I would then remain exceedingly far from unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. It is for this reason that I ought never to give rise to a partial love toward my own child."

At that very moment, toward this child, one ought to give rise to three marks: first, that, toward myself, this is itself a kind of thief — the Buddha teaches equanimous loving-kindness, and this partiality breaks it, rendering it unequal; since this love is unduly partial, both this love and this inequality together become a kind of theft and harm.

Second, on account of this very child, one's own roots of goodness are destroyed, and the very life of right wisdom is obstructed.

Third, on account of this very child, I proceed along a path contrary to the true path, and fail to walk the path that accords with it.

At that very moment, on account of this child, one practices a mind of loving-kindness equally toward every sentient being.

One ought to reflect thus: "This child has come from some other place, and I too have come from some other place; this child shall go to some other place, and I too shall go to some other place; I do not know where they shall go, nor do they know where I shall go; they do not know from where I have come, nor do I know from where they have come. This child is not truly my own possession at all — why, then, for no real reason, should I give rise to this misplaced bond of attachment!" As the verse says: "Neither of us truly knows whence the other has come, nor whither the other shall go; how, then, could we be truly close to one another, that I should give rise to this very thought of 'mine'!"

Furthermore, throughout beginningless birth and death, every sentient being has, at some time, been my own child, and I too have, at some time, been their own child. Within conditioned dharmas, there is nothing fixed or certain that this one is my own child and that one is another's child. And why is this so? Because sentient beings, revolving through the six destinies, become parent and child to one another in turn. As the verse says: "Ignorance obscures the very eye of wisdom, and, through birth and death again and again, coming and going, accomplishing much, we become parent and child to one another in turn.

"Craving and clinging to worldly pleasure, not knowing that anything more excellent exists — an enemy may often become a friend, and a friend may often become an enemy!"

It is for this reason that I employ this very skillful means, never giving rise to a mind of hatred or of love! And why is this so? Because should there be a good and virtuous friend who constantly seeks one's own benefit in every way, and should there be a hostile enemy who constantly gives rise to marks of harm in every way — holding this very mind of hatred and love, one is unable to penetrate the equal mark of every dharma. One whose mind ranks higher and lower is reborn, after death, into a wrongful place; one who practices rightly is reborn into a place of right conduct — it is for this reason that I ought never to practice wrongful conduct.

Practicing equanimity toward every sentient being, one thereby attains equanimous omniscience.

Fascicle 7 of the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā Śāstra

A note on this closing turn

After the unrelenting hostility of the preceding section's own catalogue of marks applied to a spouse, this fascicle's own final movement pivots to something gentler: the argument against partiality toward one's own child rests not on the child being somehow contemptible, but on the sheer contingency of the relationship itself — child and parent alike arriving from and departing to places neither can know, having occupied this same role toward countless other beings across beginningless lifetimes. The conclusion drawn is not indifference but its own kind of expansion: the very love that might otherwise narrow around one child becomes, once its object is seen as arbitrary, the ground for extending that same warmth toward every sentient being equally.
