The Forty Unshared Dharmas, and the Challenge to Omniscience

Forty qualities belonging to a buddha alone, from flying at will and transformation without limit to the vajra samādhi that nothing can break. Then the treatise stages its most sustained philosophical debate: an opponent argues, point by point and episode by episode from the Buddha's own recorded life, that no being can truly be omniscient — and the text answers every single charge in turn.

The Forty Unshared Dharmas, Named

Chinese Source

四十不共法品第二十一

菩薩如是以三十二相、八十種好念佛生身已，今應念佛諸功德法，所謂：又應以四十，不共法念佛，諸佛是法身，非但肉身故。

諸佛雖有無量諸法，不與餘人共者，有四十法。若人念者，則得歡喜，何以故？諸佛非是色身，是法身故。如《經》說：「汝不應但以色身觀佛，當以法觀。」

四十不共法者：一者、飛行自在，二者、變化無量，三者、聖如意無邊，四、聞聲自在，五、無量智力知他心，六、心得自在，七、常在安慧處，八、常不忘誤，九、得金剛三昧力，十、善知不定事，十一、善知無色定事，十二、具足通達諸永滅事，十三、善知心不相應、無色法，十四、大勢波羅蜜，十五、無礙波羅蜜，十六、一切問答及記具足答波羅蜜，十七、具足三轉說法，十八、所說不空，十九、所說無謬失，二十、無能害者，二十一、諸賢聖中大將，二十五、四不守護，二十九、四無所畏，三十九、佛十種力，四十、無礙解脫。是為四十不共之法。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Forty Unshared Dharmas Chapter

The bodhisattva, having thus been mindful of the Buddha's own physical body through the thirty-two marks and the eighty minor marks, ought now to be mindful of every buddha's own dharma of merit as well, namely: "One ought further to be mindful of the Buddha through these forty unshared dharmas, since every buddha is the very Dharma body, not merely a body of flesh."

Though every buddha possesses immeasurable dharmas, there are forty dharmas not shared with any other being. Should a person be mindful of them, they thereby attain joy — and why is this so? Because every buddha is not truly a body of physical form, but is the very Dharma body itself. As the sūtra states: "You ought not to behold the Buddha merely through his physical body — you ought to behold him through the Dharma."

The forty unshared dharmas are: first, mastery of flying at will; second, transformation without limit; third, noble wish-fulfillment without bound; fourth, mastery over hearing every sound; fifth, the immeasurable power of wisdom to know another's mind; sixth, attaining mastery of one's own mind; seventh, constantly abiding within tranquil wisdom; eighth, never once forgetting or erring; ninth, attaining the power of the vajra samādhi; tenth, skillfully knowing every uncertain matter; eleventh, skillfully knowing every matter of the formless absorptions; twelfth, being complete in penetrating every matter of ultimate cessation; thirteenth, skillfully knowing every dharma dissociated from mind, and every formless dharma; fourteenth, the pāramitā of great power; fifteenth, the pāramitā of freedom from obstruction; sixteenth, the pāramitā of answering every question and giving complete replies to every prediction; seventeenth, being complete in the threefold turning of expounding the Dharma; eighteenth, whatever is spoken is never empty; nineteenth, whatever is spoken is free of any error; twentieth, none are able to harm him; twenty-first, the very general among every worthy and noble one; twenty-fifth, the four things requiring no guarding; twenty-ninth, the four fearlessnesses; thirty-ninth, the Buddha's own ten powers; fortieth, unobstructed liberation. These are the forty unshared dharmas.

A note on the Dharma body

The forty items are numbered non-consecutively in the source text itself — jumping from twenty-one to twenty-five to twenty-nine to thirty-nine — because several items name entire numbered sets (the four things requiring no guarding, the four fearlessnesses, the ten powers) whose own internal members are counted individually within the total of forty, a convention preserved here exactly as it appears in the base text. The chapter's own opening distinction between the physical body just finished being visualized in Fascicle 9 and the Dharma body now being contemplated marks a shift in the very object of buddha-recollection, from bodily form to functional quality.

Flying at Will

Chinese Source

今當廣說：飛行自在者：諸佛飛行如意自在，如意、滿足，速疾、無量、無礙。所以者何？

佛若欲於虛空，先舉一足，次舉一足，即能如意。

若欲舉足躡虛空而去、若欲住立不動而去，即能得去。

若結跏趺安坐而去，亦能得去。

若欲安臥而去，亦復能去。

若欲於青琉璃莖、真珊瑚葉、黃金為鬚如意珠臺，無量圍繞如日初出，是寶蓮花遍於空中，蹈上而去。

若欲如日月宮殿、帝釋勝殿、夜摩天、兜率陀天、化樂天、他化自在天、諸梵王等宮殿，隨意化作如彼宮殿坐中而去，即能成辦。

若更以餘種種因緣，隨意能去，是故說言：「隨諸所願，皆能滿足」。

是故諸佛能以一步，過恒河沙等三千大千世界。

有人言：「佛能一念頃，過若干百千國土。」有人言：「若知佛一步一念能如是去，即可得量。」《經》中說：「諸佛力無量」。是故當知，諸佛虛空飛行自在無量無邊。

何以故？若大聲聞弟子神通自在，以一念頃能過百億閻浮提、瞿陀尼、弗婆提、欝多羅，越四大王天、忉利天、夜摩天、兜率陀天、化樂天、他化自在天、梵天，一瞬中過若干念，積此諸念，以成一日、七日、一月、一歲，乃至百歲。一日過五十三億二百九十六萬六千三千大千世界，如是聲聞人百歲所過，佛一念能過。

復次，假令恒河中沙，一沙為一劫，有大聲聞神通第一，壽命如是諸恒河沙大劫，於一念中過若干世界，積如是念以為日月歲數，以自在力盡是諸大劫數所過國土，佛能一念中過。諸佛飛行自在，如是速疾。

於一切鐵圍山、十寶山，四天王處、忉利天處、夜摩、兜率陀、化樂、他化自在、梵世、梵眾、大梵、少光、無量光、光音、少淨、無量淨、遍淨、廣果、無相、不廣、不惱、喜見、妙見、阿迦尼吒天，如是諸處；大風、大水、劫盡火等，及諸天、龍、夜叉、乾闥婆、阿修羅、緊那羅、摩睺羅伽、諸天、魔及梵、沙門、婆羅門及得諸神通者，不能為礙，是故說飛行無礙。

又，飛行自在，如意所作，出沒於地，能過石壁、諸山障礙等，佛於此事勝諸聖人。

又，佛能以常身立至梵天，聲聞人所不能及，有如是等差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

I shall now explain these at length. As for "mastery of flying at will": every buddha's own flight is exactly as they wish, at will, complete, swift, immeasurable, unobstructed. And why is this so?

Should the Buddha wish to travel through empty space, raising one foot, then the other in turn, he is thereby able to do so exactly as he wishes.

Whether wishing to raise his own foot and tread upon empty space as he departs, or wishing to stand motionless as he departs, he is thereby able to depart.

Should he wish to sit cross-legged in meditation as he departs, he is likewise able to depart.

Should he wish to lie down at ease as he departs, he is likewise able to depart.

Should he wish to travel upon a jeweled lotus flower with a stem of blue lapis lazuli, petals of genuine coral, and stamens of yellow gold forming a wish-fulfilling jeweled dais, surrounded on every side without measure like the sun at its very first rising, filling all of empty space, he treads upon it as he departs.

Should he wish to travel seated within a palace such as that of the sun and moon, the supreme palace of Śakra, or the palaces of the Yāma heaven, the Tuṣita heaven, the Nirmāṇarati heaven, the Paranirmitavaśavartin heaven, or the various lords of Brahmā, he transforms himself at will into a form seated within such a palace, and this is thereby accomplished.

Should he wish to travel through any further cause and condition whatsoever, he is able to do so exactly as he wishes — it is for this reason that it is said: "According with every wish, he is able to fulfill it completely."

It is for this reason that every buddha is able, with a single step, to traverse a great trichiliocosm as numerous as the sands of the Ganges.

There are those who say: "The Buddha is able, within the span of a single thought, to traverse several hundred thousand lands." There are those who say: "Should one know that the Buddha's own single step, within a single thought, is able to travel thus, one is thereby able to measure it." The sūtra states: "Every buddha's own power is immeasurable." It is for this reason that one should know that every buddha's own mastery of flying through empty space is immeasurable and boundless.

And why is this so? Should even a great śrāvaka disciple's own mastery of spiritual power be such that, within the span of a single thought, they are able to traverse a hundred koṭis of Jambudvīpas, Godānīyas, Pūrvavidehas, and Uttarakurus, crossing beyond the heaven of the Four Great Kings, the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, the Yāma heaven, the Tuṣita heaven, the Nirmāṇarati heaven, the Paranirmitavaśavartin heaven, and the Brahmā heaven, traversing several such thoughts within a single instant, and accumulating these very thoughts to form a single day, seven days, a single month, a single year, up to a hundred years — in a single day traversing five billion three hundred twenty-nine million six hundred sixty thousand great trichiliocosms — such a śrāvaka's own traversal across a hundred years, the Buddha is able to traverse within a single thought.

Furthermore, suppose the sand within the Ganges river, each single grain counted as one kalpa; there is a great śrāvaka of the very foremost spiritual power, whose own lifespan spans as many great kalpas as there are grains of sand in the Ganges, traversing several worlds within a single thought, accumulating these very thoughts to form days, months, and years, and, through this very mastery, traversing every land across the full span of all these great kalpas — the Buddha is able to traverse this within a single thought. Every buddha's own mastery of flight is swift in just this way.

Throughout every iron ring-mountain and the ten jeweled mountains, the abode of the Four Heavenly Kings, the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, the Yāma, Tuṣita, Nirmāṇarati, and Paranirmitavaśavartin heavens, the Brahmā world, the Brahmā-attendant heaven, the Great Brahmā heaven, the heaven of lesser light, immeasurable light, and radiant sound, the heaven of lesser purity, immeasurable purity, and universal purity, the heaven of vast fruit, the heaven without conception, and the heavens of no vastness, no affliction, fair vision, sublime vision, and Akaniṣṭha — through every such place; through great winds, great floods, and the fire that ends a kalpa, and every deva, nāga, yakṣa, gandharva, asura, kiṃnara, mahoraga, deva, māra, Brahmā, ascetic, brahmin, and every being who has attained spiritual powers — none of these are able to obstruct him; it is for this reason that his own flight is said to be unobstructed.

And further, this very mastery of flight, accomplished exactly as he wishes, emerging and vanishing through the very earth, able to pass through stone walls, mountains, and every such obstruction — in this very matter, the Buddha surpasses every noble one.

And further, the Buddha is able, with his own ordinary body, to stand and reach the Brahmā heaven directly, something no śrāvaka is able to attain — there exist differences such as this between them.

A note on this cosmological scale

The precise numerical comparisons here — a great śrāvaka's own century of travel across five billion three hundred twenty-nine million six hundred sixty thousand trichiliocosms per day, equaled by the Buddha in a single thought, and further exceeded when even that śrāvaka's own lifespan is stretched across as many kalpas as grains of sand in the Ganges — belong to a distinctive genre within Buddhist cosmological literature that uses escalating, almost absurdist arithmetic to gesture at a difference in kind rather than merely a difference in degree between a buddha's own powers and even the greatest disciple's own attainment.

Transformation Without Limit, and Noble Wish-Fulfillment

Chinese Source

變化自在者：變化事中有無量力。

餘聖變化有量有邊，諸佛變化無量無邊。

餘聖於一念中變化一身，佛以一念隨意變化，有無量事。如《大神通經》中說：「佛從臍中出蓮花，上有化佛，次第遍滿，上至阿迦尼吒天。諸佛變化所作眾事，種種色、種種形，皆以一念。」

又，聲聞人能於千國土內變化，諸佛能於無量無邊國土，變化自在，又能倍是。諸佛得堅固變化三昧。

又，諸佛變化能過恒沙世界，皆從一身出。

復次，佛能普於十方無量無邊世界，現生受身墮地行七步、出家、學道、破魔軍眾、得道、轉法輪；如是等事，皆以一念作之。是諸化佛皆亦復能施作佛事，如是等諸佛所變化事，無量無邊。

又於聖如意中，有無量力。

聖如意者：所謂從身放光，猶如猛火，又出諸雨，變化壽命，隨意長短。於一念頃能至梵天，能變諸物，隨意自在，能動大地。

光明能照無量世界而不斷絕。

聖如意者，不與凡夫等故，無有量故，過諸量故；諸凡夫等，雖變化諸物，少不足言。

聲聞人能裂千國土，還使令合，能令壽命若至一劫，若減一劫。

還能令短，短已不能令長，於一念中能至千國土、梵世界。能於千國土隨意變化。能動千國土，能身出光明，相續不絕照千國土。設使身滅，能留神力，變化如本於千國土。

小辟支佛，能於萬國土，萬種變化。

中辟支佛，能於百萬國土，百萬種變化。

大辟支佛，能於三千大千國土，變化如上。

諸佛世尊：能過諸恒河沙世界算數變化。

身出水火。

能末恒河沙等世界，令如微塵，又能還合能住。

壽命無量劫數，還能令少，少已還能令長。

能於無量時住，變化隨意。

能以一念至無量無邊恒河沙等世界。

能以常身，立至梵世。

又能變化無量無邊阿僧祇世界皆令作金，或令作銀、琉璃、珊瑚、車璩、馬瑙。取要言之，能令作無量寶物，隨意所作。

又復能變恒河沙等世界大海水，皆使為乳、酥、油、酪、蜜，隨意而成。

又能以一念變化諸山皆是真金，過諸算數不可稱計。

又能震動無量無邊世界，一切欲界、色界、諸天宮殿。

又以一念能令若干金色光明遍照，如是無量世界，日月光明及欲、色界諸天宮殿光明，皆令不現。

雖滅度後，能於如是諸世界中，隨意久近，流布神力常不斷絕。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "transformation without limit": within the very matter of transformation, there exists an immeasurable power.

Every other noble one's own transformation is bounded and finite; every buddha's own transformation is immeasurable and boundless.

Every other noble one, within a single thought, transforms a single body; the Buddha, within a single thought, transforms exactly as he wishes, accomplishing immeasurable matters. As the Sūtra of Great Spiritual Powers states: "The Buddha, from his own navel, brings forth a lotus flower, upon which sits a manifested buddha; these fill the space in due succession, reaching upward as far as the Akaniṣṭha heaven. Every buddha's own transformative works, of every kind of color and every kind of form, are all accomplished within a single thought."

And further, a śrāvaka is able to transform within a thousand lands; every buddha is able to transform freely within immeasurable, boundless lands, and further able to exceed even this. Every buddha attains the firm and unshakeable samādhi of transformation.

And further, every buddha's own transformation is able to exceed a world as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, all emanating from a single body.

Furthermore, the Buddha is able, universally throughout the immeasurable, boundless worlds of the ten directions, to manifest a birth, receive a body, descend to earth and take seven steps, go forth from home, study the path, break the army of māra, attain the path, and turn the wheel of the Dharma; matters such as these are, in every case, accomplished within a single thought. Every one of these manifested buddhas is likewise able to carry out the very work of a buddha — the transformative works of every buddha, in this way, are immeasurable and boundless.

And further, within noble wish-fulfillment too, there exists an immeasurable power.

"Noble wish-fulfillment" refers to emitting light from one's own body, just like a fierce blaze, and further sending down rain, transforming one's own lifespan, longer or shorter exactly as one wishes. Within the span of a single thought, reaching the Brahmā heaven, able to transform every object exactly as one wishes, able to shake the great earth itself.

This radiance is able to illuminate immeasurable worlds without ever being interrupted.

"Noble wish-fulfillment" is unlike that possessed by the ordinary being, since it is without measure, exceeding every measure; the ordinary being, though able to transform certain objects, accomplishes so little it is scarcely worth mentioning.

A śrāvaka is able to split apart a thousand lands, and cause them to reunite once more, able to cause their own lifespan to reach a full kalpa, or to fall short of a kalpa.

They are further able to shorten it, though, once shortened, unable to lengthen it again; within a single thought, able to reach a thousand lands, or the Brahmā world. Able to transform, exactly as they wish, within a thousand lands. Able to shake a thousand lands, able to emit light from their own body, continuously illuminating a thousand lands without interruption. Should their own body pass away, they are able to retain this spiritual power, continuing to transform exactly as before, within a thousand lands.

A lesser pratyekabuddha is able to transform, in ten thousand ways, within ten thousand lands.

A middling pratyekabuddha is able to transform, in a million ways, within a million lands.

A great pratyekabuddha is able to transform, as described above, within a great trichiliocosm.

Every buddha, every world-honored one, is able to transform beyond any count, exceeding a number of worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges.

Able to send forth water and fire from his own body.

Able to pulverize a world as numerous as the sands of the Ganges into fine dust, and further able to reunite it and cause it to remain stable.

His own lifespan spanning immeasurable kalpas, able to shorten it, and, once shortened, able to lengthen it once more.

Able to remain for an immeasurable span of time, transforming exactly as he wishes.

Able, within a single thought, to reach immeasurable, boundless worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges.

Able, with his own ordinary body, to stand and reach the Brahmā world directly.

Able further to transform immeasurable, boundless, incalculable worlds, causing them all to become gold, or causing them to become silver, lapis lazuli, coral, mother-of-pearl, or agate. To speak of the essential point, able to cause them to become immeasurable jeweled objects, exactly as he wishes.

Further able to transform the great ocean waters of worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, causing them all to become milk, ghee, oil, curds, or honey, accomplished exactly as he wishes.

Further able, within a single thought, to transform every mountain into genuine gold, beyond every count, incalculable.

Further able to shake immeasurable, boundless worlds, and every palace of every deva throughout the desire realm and form realm.

Further able, within a single thought, to cause a certain golden radiance to illuminate universally, so that, throughout these immeasurable worlds, the radiance of the sun and moon, and of every deva's own palace throughout the desire and form realms, is entirely made to disappear.

Though he has already passed into extinction, he is able, within these very worlds, according to what is fitting, whether for a long or short span, to continue spreading his own spiritual power without ever being interrupted.

A note on the graded scale of transformation

The explicit ranking here — a śrāvaka limited to a thousand lands, a lesser pratyekabuddha reaching ten thousand, a middling one a million, a great one a full trichiliocosm, and a buddha exceeding all count entirely — extends the same graded hierarchy used throughout this treatise for merit and wisdom into the domain of supernormal power specifically, making explicit that even the greatest pratyekabuddha's own attainment remains a bounded quantity next to a buddha's own boundless one.

Mastery Over Every Sound Heard, and Knowing Every Mind

Chinese Source

聞聲自在者：諸佛所聞聲中，隨意自在。

若無量百千萬億技樂同時俱作，若無量百千萬億眾生一時發言，若遠若近，隨意所聞。假令恒河沙等三千大千世界所有眾生，同時俱作若干百千萬種伎樂，遍滿世界，復有恒河沙等世界眾生，同時以梵音，遍滿一切世界。諸佛若欲於中聞一音聲，隨意得聞，餘者不聞。

聲聞所應聞者若有大神力障者，不能得聞。諸佛所聞音聲，雖有大神力障，亦能得聞。

聲聞能聞千國土內音聲，諸佛世尊所聞音聲，過無量無邊世界最細音聲，皆亦得聞。

大神力聲聞住梵世界，發大音聲能滿千國土內；諸佛世尊若住於此，若住梵世，若住餘處，音聲能滿無量無邊世界。若欲令眾生聞，過無量無邊世界最細音聲，能令得聞；欲令不聞，即便不聞。

是故，但有諸佛於聞聲中得自在力。

知他心無量自在力者：諸佛世尊，於無量無邊世界現在眾生，悉知其心。

餘人但隨名相故知，諸佛以名相義故知。

又餘人不能知無色界眾生諸心，諸佛能知。

餘人雖有知他心智，大力者障則不能知。假使一切眾生成就心通皆如舍利弗、目犍連、辟支佛等，以其神力障一人心，不令他知，而佛能壞彼神力，得知其心。

復次，佛以神力悉知眾生上中下心、垢心、淨心。

又知諸心各有所緣，從是緣至是緣，次第遍知一切諸緣。

又以實相知眾生心。

是故，諸佛以無量力悉知他心。

A note on knowing through meaning, not mere marks

The distinction drawn between ordinary beings who "know only through following names and marks" and buddhas who "know through the very meaning of names and marks themselves" recalls the bowl-of-oil teaching from Fascicle 7 — names and marks function as a genuine but provisional surface, useful for communication yet never identical with what they point toward, and only a buddha's own knowledge penetrates all the way through to that underlying reality rather than stopping at its outward sign.

A note on this chain of questions

The unbroken chain of eight consecutive questions, each asking "why" of the answer just given, is a distinctive argumentative technique this treatise uses sparingly — most often for its own most contested claims — walking backward through causes until it reaches bedrock: the vajra samādhi is unbreakable because it penetrates everything, penetrates everything because it opens every obstruction, opens every obstruction because it is foremost among concentrations, foremost because built from boundless roots of goodness, and finally because it belongs to the omniscient one alone. That final claim — that only the omniscient one possesses this samādhi — is precisely the premise the following chapter subjects to its most sustained challenge in the entire treatise.

Challenging the Omniscient One Among the Forty Unshared Dharmas Chapter

Question: You have said that "the vajra samādhi is possessed by the omniscient one alone, and by no other being" — should this samādhi be possessed by the omniscient one alone, and by no other being at all, then this samādhi does not truly exist. And why is this so? Because no omniscient being exists at all.

And why is this so? Whatever is knowable is immeasurable and boundless, while wisdom itself is bounded and finite; through this bounded and finite wisdom, one ought not to be able to know immeasurable matters. Consider even now, throughout Jambudvīpa alone: the aquatic and terrestrial sentient beings there already exceed every count. These sentient beings are of three kinds — male, female, and neither male nor female. In the womb, as a child, in youthful vigor, in declining age, and every dharma of suffering and joy. Every mind and mental state of the past, the future, and the present, and every wholesome and unwholesome karma, already accumulated, now accumulating, or yet to be accumulated; whose fruit has already been undergone, is now being undergone, or has not yet been undergone. Every phenomenon's own arising and cessation, and, throughout Jambudvīpa, every mountain and river, spring and pool, grass and tree, forest and thicket, root and stem, branch and leaf, flower and fruit — whatever might be known of these has no boundary whatsoever; and the remaining three continents are likewise so. Just as with these four continents, so too with every object throughout the great trichiliocosm. Just as with every object throughout the great trichiliocosm, so too with whatever might be known throughout every world whatsoever. If the mere count of this world alone is already immeasurable and boundless, difficult indeed to know, how much more so, then, every object, whether sentient being or non-sentient thing, throughout every Jambudvīpa, throughout every world? Through this very cause and condition, one should know: since whatever might be known is immeasurable and boundless, there exists no omniscient being.

Should one claim: "Wisdom possesses great power, holding no obstruction whatsoever toward whatever is knowable, thereby knowing everything universally. Whatever is knowable pervades every dharma just as empty space itself does — it is for this reason that there ought to exist an omniscient being" — this matter is simply not so! Wisdom's own great power might well be granted. Yet great wisdom is unable to know itself, just as the very tip of a finger cannot touch itself — it is for this reason that no omniscient being exists.

Should one claim: "There exists some further wisdom, able to know this very wisdom" — this too is not so! And why is this so? Because this would give rise to an infinite regress.

Whether this wisdom knows itself, or is known through some other wisdom, in neither case does this hold. Should this very wisdom possess immeasurable power, yet be unable to know itself, one cannot then say it "possesses immeasurable power." It is for this reason that there exists no wisdom able to know every dharma; since there exists no wisdom able to know every dharma, there exists no omniscient being. And why is this so? Because "omniscient" means knowing every dharma through wisdom.

Furthermore, whatever is knowable is immeasurable and boundless; even should hundreds of thousands of myriads of koṭis of wise persons combine their own knowledge together, they would still be unable to know it fully — how much less, then, a single person alone? It is for this reason that there exists no single person able to know every dharma, and no omniscience exists at all.

A note on this staged debate

This chapter opens the treatise's single most sustained philosophical debate, structured as a formal challenge-and-response spanning the rest of the fascicle: an opponent's objection runs uninterrupted for well over a hundred lines of source text before the text answers a single point of it. The finger-that-cannot-touch-its-own-tip argument against self-knowing wisdom is a genuinely difficult epistemological puzzle, one the treatise's own answer, still to come, must address directly rather than dismiss.

And further, the four Vedāṅga scriptures alone, bounded and finite as they are, remain, even now, something no one is able fully to know — how much less, then, could anyone fully know every scripture whatsoever? It is for this reason that no omniscient being exists.

Furthermore, there exist scriptures able to increase craving — songs, dances, and music, and the like — should the omniscient one know this very matter, he would thereby possess craving himself. These very scriptures are themselves the cause and condition of craving; where there exists a cause, there must exist a fruit. Should the omniscient one fail to know this very matter, he could not be called omniscient at all.

Furthermore, there exist scriptures able to assist anger, delighting in deceiving others — namely, treatises on statecraft, and the like. Should he know this very matter, he would thereby possess anger himself. And why is this so? Because where there exists a cause, there must exist a fruit. Should he fail to know it, he could not be called omniscient — it is for this reason that one knows no omniscient being exists.

Furthermore, the Buddha does not necessarily know every matter of the future in full. Consider, for example, my own present challenge to the omniscient one — the Buddha has no scripture predicting in advance that a certain person, of such a family, of such a household, in such a place, would challenge the omniscient one with a matter such as this. Should one claim the Buddha knows this fully, why, then, has he never spoken of this very matter? Were it truly recorded in a sūtra, this sūtra ought to contain it; since it never speaks of this very matter, one thereby knows he is not omniscient.

Furthermore, should the Buddha fully know every matter of the future, he ought to have known in advance that Devadatta, once gone forth from home, would break apart the saṅgha; had he known this, he ought not to have permitted his going forth.

Furthermore, the Buddha did not know of the wooden device and the flung stone. Had the Buddha known this in advance, he ought not to have walked in meditation at that very place.

Furthermore, the Buddha did not know that the brahmin woman Cañcā would slander him with the charge of sexual misconduct. Had the Buddha known this beforehand, he ought to have told the monks that this would occur in the future.

Furthermore, there was a brahmin ascetic who, out of envy toward the Buddha, murdered the brahmin girl Sundarī elsewhere, and buried her within a trench at Jetavana. The Buddha did not know of this very matter; had he known of it, he ought to have rescued this girl's own life from those very brahmin ascetics.

When the stone was rolled down upon him at the place where Devadatta lay in wait, he spoke nothing of either the brahmin woman's or the brahmin girl's own matter, since he did not know of them; one should thereby know that the Buddha does not fully know the future, and is therefore not omniscient.

Furthermore, when the Buddha entered a brahmin settlement to beg for food and emerged with an empty bowl, he was unable to know in advance that māra would, at that very time, turn the minds of every person there, so that he obtained not even a single meal; had the Buddha known this, he ought not to have entered that brahmin settlement. It is for this reason that one knows "the Buddha does not fully know matters of the future."

Furthermore, King Ajātaśatru, wishing to harm the Buddha, released an intoxicated elephant kept for guarding treasure. The Buddha, not knowing of this, entered the city of Rājagṛha to beg for food; had he known this in advance, he ought not to have entered the city. It is for this reason that he does not know matters of the future, and, not knowing matters of the future, he is not omniscient.

Furthermore, the Buddha did not know the true circumstances of the wicked brahmin Verañjā's invitation, and thereupon accepted this invitation, leading the assembled monks to the country of Verañjā. This brahmin, having forgotten his own earlier invitation, caused the Buddha to eat horse fodder for three months. Had the Buddha known this in advance, he ought not to have accepted this invitation, and eaten horse fodder for three months. It is for this reason that one knows the Buddha does not know matters of the future, and, not knowing matters of the future, he is not omniscient.

Furthermore, the Buddha, in accepting Sunakṣatra as his own disciple, thereby did not know matters of the future. This person's own evil mind was firm and unyielding, difficult indeed to transform, never believing the Buddha's own words. Had the Buddha known this, how could he have accepted him as a disciple? Through accepting him as a disciple, he thereby did not know matters of the future; not knowing matters of the future, he is not omniscient. Furthermore, should the Buddha be truly omniscient, he ought to have guarded in advance against one who had not yet committed any offense, establishing a precept for them beforehand. Since he did not know in advance the very cause and condition requiring a precept, and only established the precept after an offense had already been committed, he thereby did not know matters of the future; not knowing matters of the future, he is not omniscient.

A note on these named episodes

Every incident the objector cites here is drawn from the Buddha's own recorded biography as preserved across the canon — Devadatta's schism, the assassination attempt by a rolled stone, Cañcā's and Sundarī's slanders, the empty almsbowl, the drunken elephant Nālāgiri, the three months of horse fodder at Verañjā, and the incorrigible disciple Sunakṣatra — rather than invented hypotheticals. The rhetorical force of the objection depends entirely on this: these are not speculative counterexamples but events the tradition itself records as having actually happened, each apparently showing the Buddha caught by surprise. The treatise's own answer, beginning in the sections that follow, must therefore reckon with its own scriptural record rather than merely assert omniscience by definition.

Answer: Speak no further of this — the Buddha is genuinely omniscient. And why is this so? Every dharma whatsoever falls entirely within five treasuries of dharma — namely, the dharma of the past, the dharma of the future, the dharma of the present, the dharma that transcends the three times, and the dharma that cannot be expressed — and the Buddha alone truly and universally knows this entire dharma.

As for your earlier challenge, that "whatever is knowable is immeasurable and boundless, and therefore no omniscient being exists," I shall now answer it: should whatever is knowable be immeasurable and boundless, wisdom itself is likewise immeasurable and boundless. Through this very "immeasurable, boundless wisdom" knowing this "immeasurable, boundless dharma," there is no fault in this at all.

Should one claim, "this very knowing itself ought also to be known through some further wisdom, and this would then continue without end," I shall now answer it: a dharma ought to be known through wisdom, and wisdom, just as an ordinary person in this world might say: "I am wise, I am unwise; I am of coarse wisdom, I am of fine wisdom." Through this very cause and condition, since wisdom is able to know wisdom itself, there is no fault of infinite regress at all. Just as present wisdom is able to know past wisdom, one thereby comes to know every dharma entirely, with nothing left over.

Furthermore, just as a person who counts another's own body parts as ten fingers thereby also knows their own, knowing works in just this same way. Knowing oneself and knowing another both occur together, with no fault in this whatsoever — just as a lamp illuminates itself while also illuminating what is around it.

As for what you have said, that "even should hundreds of thousands of myriads of koṭis of wise persons combine their own knowledge, they would still be unable to know every dharma fully — how much less, then, a single person alone" — this matter is simply not so! And why is this so? One who is truly and fully wise is able to know every matter; though there may be a great many people, if they lack this very wisdom, they remain unable to know anything at all. Just as a hundred thousand blind persons are unfit to serve as a guide, while a single person possessing sight is fit to serve as this very guide. It is for this reason that your own challenge, framed around a single person, though there may be many who possess partial wisdom, mistakes what the Buddha's own wisdom truly is. This matter is simply not so!

A note on the lamp that illuminates itself

The lamp simile directly answers the finger-that-cannot-touch-its-own-tip objection from the previous section by supplying a genuine counterexample: a lamp's own light does illuminate the lamp itself along with everything else in the room, so self-reference is not universally impossible, only impossible for certain kinds of things (a finger's tactile contact) and not others (light's own illumination). The blind-guides analogy then reframes the "many minds combined still can't know everything" objection — the problem was never insufficient quantity of ordinary wisdom, but the qualitative absence of the one thing (sight, or true omniscience) that quantity can never substitute for.

Answer: No such thing exists at all! And why is this so? Within the Vedic scriptures there exist "four inversions": this world is impermanent, and yet they hold that there separately exists some permanent world. As it is said: perform the fire-sacrifice to the devas once, and one still falls; perform it twice more, and one still falls; only upon the third performance does one no longer fall — this is the very "inversion of permanence" within what is genuinely impermanent.

This world is suffering, and yet they speak of some place of constant joy — this is the very "inversion of joy" within what is genuinely suffering.

They further state that one's own soul is reborn as one's own son, wishing that this son might live to a hundred years. Yet this son is another's own body entirely — how could this possibly be "myself"? This is the very "inversion of self" within what is genuinely without self.

They state that the body is pure, the very foremost, beyond compare. Gold, silver, and every precious jewel do not equal the body itself — this is called the very "inversion of purity" within what is genuinely without purity.

Where there is inversion, there is no reality whatsoever; and where there is no reality, how could there possibly be tranquil cessation? It is for this reason that the Vedas contain no dharma of true tranquil cessation.

Question: The Vedas state that "whoever is able to know the Vedas attains purity and peace" — how, then, can you say they contain "no dharma of true tranquil cessation"?

Answer: One who knows the Vedas, though it speaks of attaining peace, does not thereby attain ultimate liberation. Within some other body, one gives rise to a mere thought of liberation — this speaks only of a long-lived heavenly rebirth attained through this very cause, and calls this "liberation." It is for this reason that the Vedas, in truth, contain no genuine liberation whatsoever.

Furthermore, the Vedas, to speak briefly, contain three kinds of content: first, incantations and prayers; second, praise and eulogy; third, rules and regulations.

"Incantations and prayers" refers to seeking to obtain a wife and children, cattle and horses, gold, silver, and precious jewels.

"Praise and eulogy" refers to praising the fire-deity — his own head black, his own neck red, his own body yellow, constantly present within the five elements of every sentient being.

"Rules and regulations" refers to what ought to be done, and what ought not to be done.

Just as one first receives the fire-ritual from the star Kṛttikā, in truth these incantations and prayers, this praise and eulogy, and these rules and regulations contain no tranquil cessation or liberation whatsoever. And why is this so? Craving and clinging to worldly pleasure, burning ghee while reciting incantations and prayers — lacking genuine wisdom, never severing the afflictions — how could there possibly be liberation in this?

A note on the four inversions

The "four inversions" (顛倒) named here — mistaking the impermanent for permanent, suffering for joy, the selfless for self, and the impure for pure — form one of the most widely used doctrinal frameworks across the entire Buddhist canon for diagnosing wrong view, applied elsewhere throughout this treatise to ordinary ignorance and here turned specifically against the Vedic tradition's own claims to liberation. The argument's structure is worth noting: it does not deny that the Vedas promise peace and reward, only that what they promise, examined closely, turns out to be another form of the very inversions Buddhist practice exists to see through.

A note on mud before the lotus

The mud-before-the-lotus and illness-before-medicine images dismantle the objector's appeal to seniority directly: something appearing earlier in time carries no inherent claim to greater truth or value, and may in fact be exactly what the later thing exists to remedy. The farmer's harvest simile that follows makes a complementary point about incidental knowledge — a farmer who sets out to grow grain and finds weeds alongside it has not thereby failed at farming, just as the Buddha's own comprehensive knowledge of wrong paths, acquired in the course of walking the right one, is not itself evidence that he endorses or teaches them.

Should you claim that "there exist scriptures able to give rise to craving and anger," I shall now answer it: should a person wish to live long, they ought to depart from the very cause and condition of death; the Buddha is likewise so — wishing to sever every sentient being's own craving and anger, he ought first to know the causes and conditions of craving and anger.

The Buddha, though he knows this, neither makes use of it nor practices it, and there is therefore no fault in this at all. It is for this reason that this has already been stated in full, in general terms. Every ordinary being of the future, including yourself, is already included within this very statement. Since there would be no benefit in it, of what use would it be to distinguish and name each and every such person individually?

Should one claim that "the Buddha knew there would be this challenge, and yet did not answer it in advance," this too is unnecessary! Even now, within the fourfold assembly, there exist those skillfully able to resolve doubts and difficulties, and there exist those able to refute every difficult question raised — of what use, then, would it be to answer them all in advance? Just as, even today, you yourself can see, among the monks, those able to refute a brahmin's own challenge — it is for this very reason that there is no need to answer every such challenge beforehand.

And further, an answer to this very matter has already been given at an earlier time, scattered throughout the various sūtras — it is only that people, being unable to fully know the entire Buddhadharma, do not know exactly where to find it.

A note on knowing without producing

The distinction drawn here — between knowing a cause and thereby producing its fruit — is the same logical move the treatise will use repeatedly through the rest of this debate: knowledge of the Vedas, knowledge of what gives rise to craving, even knowledge that an assassination attempt or a false accusation is coming, does not by itself compel any particular action in response. This separates omniscience (a state of knowing) from omnipotence or predetermined intervention (a compulsion to act on every piece of knowledge), which becomes essential to the specific biographical episodes the text answers next.

A note on demonstrating karma cannot be discarded

The treatise's own answer to the flung stone is striking for what it concedes rather than denies: it does not claim the Buddha was unaware of the danger, but that he deliberately walked into it, to demonstrate to sentient beings that the fruit of karma, once planted, cannot simply be waved away even by a buddha's own supernormal power. This reframes apparent vulnerability as itself a teaching, and the Cañcā episode extends the same logic in reverse — had the Buddha announced the slander in advance, the very karmic debt requiring it to occur would have gone unpaid, since the accuser would never have come at all.

A note on the elephant lowering its head

The mad elephant Nālāgiri's own submission before the Buddha is one of the most frequently depicted scenes in early Buddhist art precisely because of the reversal it stages: an animal driven to violence by intoxication and royal malice instead kneels, and the crowd's own reaction — reverence spreading through the witnesses rather than mere relief at danger averted — is presented here as the actual point of the Buddha's approach, not a fortunate side effect of it. Each answer in this section follows the same shape established with the flung stone: apparent vulnerability turns out, on examination, to conceal a deliberate act of teaching or a karmic debt being paid, never a genuine gap in the Buddha's own foreknowledge.

如王者立制：「不應作惡！後有犯者，隨事輕重，作如是罪，如是治之。」佛亦如是：先總說戒，後有犯者，說其罪相；如有作惡者，教令懺悔：作如是罪，應如是懺——不見擯、滅擯、不共住等；成如是事故，後乃結戒。

《十住毘婆沙論》卷第十

Ru-Yi Translation

As for your claim concerning "keeping Sunakṣatra as a disciple," I shall now explain it: the Buddha's own karma of body, speech, mind, and life requires no guarding whatsoever, being free of every fear — it was for this reason that he permitted him as a disciple.

Furthermore, this person, through constantly remaining near the Buddha, was able to witness spiritual power of every kind, beholding every deva, nāga, yakṣa, gandharva, and asura king coming to make offering to the Buddha, and inquiring after every kind of profound and essential dharma — his own mind thereby attained purity; through this very purity of mind, he obtained the causes and conditions of genuine benefit. It was for this reason that, though he was evil, the Buddha permitted him as a disciple.

Question: This person held, toward the Buddha, much evil intent — it was therefore improper to permit him as a disciple.

Answer: Had he not been permitted as a disciple, he would likewise still have held this very evil intent — it was for this reason that permitting him as a disciple carried no fault whatsoever.

As for your claim, "before any offense had yet been committed, why did he not establish the precept beforehand," I shall now answer it: the Buddha had already, from the very outset, established the precepts, in expounding the eightfold noble path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. In expounding this very path leading to nirvāṇa, he had already expounded every precept in essence.

Furthermore, the Buddha expounded the threefold training — skillfully training in the precepts, skillfully training the mind, and skillfully training in wisdom — one should thereby know that he had already expounded every precept in essence.

Furthermore, the Buddha told the monks: "Every evil deed whatsoever ought never, under any circumstance, to be done." Is this not itself called "establishing the precepts beforehand"?

Furthermore, the Buddha expounded the ten wholesome paths: departing from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, divisive speech, harsh speech, false speech, idle speech, craving and envy, anger, and wrong view — is this not itself called "establishing the precepts beforehand"?

The Buddha, during his own first twelve years, spoke a single verse as the very dharma of the poṣadha observance — namely: "Refrain from every evil deed; undertake every wholesome deed; purify one's own intent and mind — this alone is the teaching of every buddha." It is for this reason that one should know he had already established the precepts beforehand.

Furthermore, the Buddha stated: "Every cause and condition of even the smallest evil ought, in every case, to be departed from." As it is said: "Depart from every evil deed of the body, and further depart from every evil of speech; depart from every evil deed of the mind, and depart far from every other evil besides." Having spoken thus, one should know he had already established the precepts beforehand.

Furthermore, the Buddha had already, from the outset, spoken of "every dharma of guarding and protection." As it is said: "Guarding the body is itself excellent; being able to guard speech is likewise excellent; guarding the mind is itself excellent; guarding everything is likewise excellent; the monk who guards everything thereby departs far from every evil." Having spoken thus, one should know he had already established the precepts beforehand.

Furthermore, the Buddha had already spoken of the very marks of what is wholesome, as it is said: "Never wrongfully transgress with hand or foot; measure your own speech, and be careful in your own conduct; constantly delight in guarding a concentrated mind — this alone is called a genuine monk." Having spoken thus, one should know he had already established the precepts beforehand.

Furthermore, in expounding the very dharma of the ascetic, one should know he had already established the precepts beforehand — the ascetic possesses four dharmas: first, never repaying anger with anger; second, remaining silent when reviled; third, being able to bear the rod and the whip; fourth, patiently enduring one who does harm.

Furthermore, the Buddha expounded the four foundations of mindfulness — contemplating the body, contemplating feeling, contemplating the mind, and contemplating mental phenomena — since this is itself the very dwelling-place of the path to nirvāṇa, one should know he had already established the precepts beforehand.

Should even the smallest evil already be forbidden, how much more so, then, an evil karma of body or speech! Through causes and conditions such as these, one should know he had already established the precepts beforehand.

Just as a king establishes a decree: "One ought never to commit evil! Should there afterward be an offender, according to the gravity or lightness of the matter, this offense shall be committed, and shall be dealt with in such a way" — the Buddha is likewise so: he first expounded the precepts in their general essence, and only afterward, should there be an offender, spoke of the very mark of that particular offense; should there be one who has done evil, he taught them to repent — having committed such an offense, one ought to repent in such a way, whether through suspension without acknowledgment, suspension through extinction, or exclusion from communal life, and the like — it was only through matters such as these coming to pass that the specific precepts were established afterward.

Fascicle 10 of the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā Śāstra

A note on the king's own decree

The closing analogy resolves the entire "why establish precepts only after an offense occurs" objection with a single, everyday observation: a wise ruler's own general decree against wrongdoing precedes any specific statute detailing penalties, and the later, more particular rule does not indicate the ruler failed to anticipate wrongdoing — it indicates that general principles were already sufficient, and specific case law develops as specific cases actually arise. Paired with the earlier citation of the eightfold path, the threefold training, and the ten wholesome paths as the precepts "already established in essence," this closes the treatise's own defense of the Buddha's omniscience by relocating where that omniscience was actually expressed — not in a list of hypothetical future violations, but in principles comprehensive enough to have already covered them.
