Taming the Mind, and the Marks of Non-Retrogression

Nāgārjuna catalogues, in unsparing detail, what actually causes the mind of awakening to be lost — arrogance, false speech, envy of another's teaching, and an extended survey of māra's own tactics for disrupting a Dharma talk from the inside. Then, distinguishing the bodhisattva doomed to fail from the one advancing toward irreversibility, the fascicle turns to a Madhyamaka dialectic on grasping at nothing whatsoever — not self, not sentient beings, not even the Buddha himself.

Four Dharmas That Cause Bodhicitta to Be Lost

Chinese Source

調伏心品第七

問曰：如上品說：「三發心必成，餘四不必成。」云何為成？云何不成？

答曰：若菩薩發菩提心，行失菩提心法，是則不成；若行不失菩提心法，是則必成。

是故偈說：菩薩應遠離，　失菩提心法；應一心修行，　不失菩提法。

「遠離」，名除滅惡法，不令入心；若入，疾滅。「失」，名若今世、若後世忘菩提心，不復隨順修行——應遠離如是法。若不失菩提法、不忘菩提心，應常一心勤行。

問曰：何等法失菩提心？

答曰：一、不敬重法，　二、有憍慢心，三、妄語無實，　四、不敬知識。

有是四法者，若於今世死時、若次後世，則忘失菩提心，不能自知我是菩薩，不復發願，菩薩行法不復在前。

不恭敬法者：法，名諸佛所說上、中、下乘。取要言之，是諸佛如來所用教法。於此法中不恭敬、供養、尊重、讚歎；不生希有想、難得想、寶物想、滿願想，是法能失菩提心。

慢心者：自高其心，未得謂得、未證謂證空、無相、無願，若無生忍法，若六波羅蜜，若菩薩十地，如是等及諸餘從修生者，於此法中未得謂得。

妄語者：有屬突吉羅，有屬波夜提，有屬偷蘭遮，有屬僧伽婆尸沙，有屬波羅夷。或有人言：有第六妄語，是妄語心生懺悔。上五妄語，初輕後重；第六者最輕。

屬波羅夷者：自無過人法，若口言，若形示，趣以方便，現有此德。

屬僧伽婆尸沙者：若口言，若形示，於彼比丘四事中，以一一有根、無根事謗。

屬偷蘭遮者：欲以有根、無根事謗而說不成。

屬波夜提者：以無根僧伽婆尸沙事謗。

屬突吉羅者：除入四種罪，餘妄語是。

自心除滅者：若說戒時，自知有小罪，不得向他說，即自心悔。

問曰：是妄語者但在比丘，不在白衣。而此論通在家、出家？

答曰：凡知事實爾，而異知說者，此論中說是總相妄語，以有眾生分別故、事分別故、時分別故、五眾罪分別故、住處分別故，則有輕重。雖輕妄語，習久則重，能失菩提心。

眾生分別者：斷善根、邪見者，及餘深煩惱者，是則為重。事分別者：若說過人法、破僧是。時分別者：出家人妄語，則重。五眾罪分別者：如波羅夷、僧伽婆尸沙罪則重。住處分別者：僧中妄語，若證時則重。

不恭敬善知識者：不生恭敬、畏難想。

多行此四法，則失菩提心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Taming the Mind Chapter

Question: As the preceding chapter has explained, "three of these arisings of the aspiration are certain to reach accomplishment, while the remaining four are not necessarily accomplished." What, then, constitutes accomplishment, and what constitutes its failure?

Answer: Should a bodhisattva give rise to bodhicitta and then act in a manner that causes bodhicitta to be lost, this then fails to reach accomplishment; should they act in a manner that does not cause bodhicitta to be lost, this then is certain to reach accomplishment.

It is for this reason that the verse says: "The bodhisattva ought to depart far from whatever dharma causes bodhicitta to be lost, and ought to practice, with a single mind, whatever dharma does not cause bodhicitta to be lost."

"Depart far from" means to eliminate unwholesome dharmas, never allowing them to enter the mind; and should they enter, to swiftly extinguish them. "Lost" means that, whether within this present life or within a life to come, one forgets bodhicitta and no longer accords with it in practice — one ought to depart far from any such dharma. Should one never lose this bodhi-dharma, never forgetting bodhicitta, one ought constantly, with a single mind, to diligently practice it.

Question: What dharmas, then, cause bodhicitta to be lost?

Answer: "First, failing to revere the Dharma; second, harboring a mind of arrogance; third, false speech devoid of truth; fourth, failing to revere one's own good and virtuous friends."

Should one possess these four dharmas, then, whether at the moment of death within this present life, or within a life to come thereafter, bodhicitta is forgotten and lost, and one is no longer able to know for oneself that one is a bodhisattva; the vow is no longer given rise to, and the bodhisattva's own practice no longer stands before them.

"Failing to revere the Dharma": "the Dharma" refers to the higher, middle, and lower vehicles spoken by every buddha; to speak of its essential meaning, this is the very teaching employed by every Thus Come One. Within this Dharma, failing to revere it, to make offerings to it, to honor it, and to praise it; failing to give rise to a sense of its rarity, its difficulty to obtain, its value as a treasure, and its power to fulfill every wish — this dharma of failing to revere is itself able to cause bodhicitta to be lost.

"A mind of arrogance": exalting one's own mind, claiming to have attained what one has not attained, claiming to have realized emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness when one has not; whether the acceptance of the non-arising of dharmas, the six pāramitās, or the bodhisattva's ten grounds, and every other such dharma arising from cultivation — within these very dharmas, claiming to have attained what one has not attained.

"False speech": this is variously classified as a duṣkṛta offense, a pāyantika offense, a sthūlātyaya offense, a saṃghāvaśeṣa offense, or a pārājika offense. Some say there is a sixth kind of false speech as well, one for which repentance arises spontaneously within one's own mind. Among the first five kinds of false speech, the earlier are lighter and the later graver; the sixth is the very lightest of all.

"Classified as pārājika": lacking any superhuman dharma oneself, yet, whether through speech or through outward display, employing skillful means to give the appearance of possessing such a virtue.

"Classified as saṃghāvaśeṣa": whether through speech or through outward display, slandering a fellow monk regarding any one of the four grounds, whether with or without foundation.

"Classified as sthūlātyaya": intending to slander with or without foundation, yet the statement fails to be completed.

"Classified as pāyantika": slandering with a groundless charge of a saṃghāvaśeṣa offense.

"Classified as duṣkṛta": every instance of false speech other than these four classes of offense.

"Extinguished spontaneously within one's own mind": at the time of reciting the precepts, knowing for oneself that one has committed some minor offense, yet unable to declare it to another — one's own mind repents of it directly.

Question: Is this false speech confined to monks alone, and not to laypeople? Yet this treatise applies it broadly to both householders and renunciants alike?

Answer: Whatever the actual matter, its manner of recognition differs from how it is spoken of; this treatise speaks here of false speech in its general aspect, and, since it is further distinguished according to the sentient being involved, the matter involved, the time involved, the class of the five assemblies' offenses involved, and the setting involved, there arise differences of lightness and gravity. Though a given instance of false speech may be light, should it become habitual over a long time, it grows grave, and is thereby able to cause bodhicitta to be lost.

"Distinguished according to the sentient being": deceiving one whose roots of goodness are severed, who holds wrong views, or who is beset by other profound afflictions, is graver. "Distinguished according to the matter": speaking falsely of a superhuman dharma, or causing a schism within the Saṅgha, are matters of this kind. "Distinguished according to the time": false speech committed by a renunciant is graver. "Distinguished according to the class of the five assemblies' offenses": offenses such as pārājika and saṃghāvaśeṣa are graver. "Distinguished according to the setting": false speech spoken within the Saṅgha, or spoken at the time of formal testimony, is graver.

"Failing to revere one's own good and virtuous friends": failing to give rise to reverence, and to a sense of the difficulty and gravity owed to them.

Extensively practicing these four dharmas causes bodhicitta to be lost.

A note on this Vinaya classification

Nāgārjuna's decision to treat false speech through the full Vinaya taxonomy of monastic offenses — pārājika through duṣkṛta — while explicitly answering the objection that this framework was designed for monks, not laypeople, reveals something about this treatise's own intended readership: a text addressed to both householders and renunciants alike, borrowing the monastic community's own precise vocabulary for grading an offense's severity, because no comparably fine-grained vocabulary existed anywhere else in the tradition for weighing exactly how grave a given falsehood actually is.

Further Faults, and the Four Black Dharmas

問曰：但是四法能失菩提心？更有餘法？

答曰：悋惜最要法，　貪樂於小乘，謗毀諸菩薩，　輕賤坐禪者。

悋惜要法者：師所知甚深難得之義，多所利者貪著利養，恐與己等故，祕惜不說。

貪樂小乘者：不得大乘滋味故，貪樂二乘。

謗諸菩薩者：無罪而言有罪，名為謗。「菩薩」義先已說。此人無過而妄加其罪，若實有罪而論說者，此雖有罪，比前為輕。何以故？《經》說：「諸菩薩若實有罪，若無有罪，皆不應說。」

輕賤坐禪者：若在家、出家，為斷諸煩惱故，勤行精進，為遮一切煩惱，集助佛道法。此人或不善論議、或無才辯、或無重威德，無智之人而輕賤之，則得重罪。

復次，若於善知識，其心懷結恨，亦有諂曲心，貪諸利養等。善知識義：先已說。於此教化說法者生嫌恨心，如嫌父母，得重罪。諂者，心佞媚。曲者，身、口業現有所作。

貪利養等者，貪著利、樂、稱譽。以此法壞質直心故，不能深起善根；如惡色染衣，更不受好色。

復次——不覺諸魔事，　菩提心劣弱；業障及法障，　亦失菩提心。

不覺魔事者：若不知諸魔事，則不能制伏；若不制伏，則失菩提心。

問曰：何等是諸魔事？

答曰：說應布施、持戒、忍辱、精進、禪定、智慧波羅蜜時，及說大乘所攝深義時，不疾樂說。若樂說，於其中間，餘緣散亂。若書讀、解說、論議、聽受等，慠慢自大，其心散亂，緣想餘事，妄念戲笑。

互相譏論，兩不和合，不能通達實義，從座而去，作是念：「我於此中，無有受記，心不清淨。亦不說我城邑、聚落、居家生處。」是故不欲聞法，不得滋味，從座而去。

捨大乘所說諸波羅蜜，及於聲聞、辟支佛自調度經中求薩婆若。若書讀、解說、聽受等時，欲樂說餘種種事，破散般若波羅蜜。

所謂說方國聚落、城邑園林、帥事賊事、兵甲器仗、憎愛苦樂、父母兄弟、男女妻子、衣服飲食、臥具醫藥、資生之物，心則散亂，失般若波羅蜜。

又說：貪恚癡、怨家親屬、好時惡時、歌舞伎樂、憂愁戲笑、經書文頌、往世古事、國主帝王、地水火風、五欲富貴、及利養等世間諸事，令心喜悅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Are these four dharmas alone able to cause bodhicitta to be lost? Or are there further dharmas as well?

Answer: "Stinginess with the most essential Dharma; delighting in craving the lesser vehicle; slandering the bodhisattvas; and belittling those who sit in meditation."

"Stinginess with the essential Dharma": some profound and rare meaning a teacher knows, of great benefit to many, is instead craved and hoarded as their own gain, kept secret and unspoken out of fear that others might become their equal.

"Delighting in craving the lesser vehicle": failing to attain the flavor of the Great Vehicle, one instead delights in craving the two lesser vehicles.

"Slandering the bodhisattvas": to declare an offense where none exists is called slander. The meaning of "bodhisattva" has already been explained above. Such a person, without any actual fault, falsely adds an offense to them; should the offense actually exist and merely be discussed, this, though still an offense, is lighter than the former. And why is this so? Because the sūtra states: "Whether or not a bodhisattva actually has committed an offense, one ought never to speak of it at all."

"Belittling those who sit in meditation": whether householder or renunciant, diligently exerting effort for the sake of severing every affliction, gathering the dharmas that assist the path to buddhahood in order to obstruct every affliction — should such a person be unskilled in debate, lack eloquence, or lack an imposing majestic virtue, and an unwise person then belittles them for it, a grave offense is thereby incurred.

Furthermore, should one's mind harbor resentment toward a good and virtuous friend, and further hold a mind of flattery and crookedness, craving gain and the like. The meaning of "good and virtuous friend" has already been explained above. To give rise to a mind of resentment toward one who teaches and transforms in this way, expounding the Dharma, is like resenting one's own parents, and a grave offense is thereby incurred. "Flattery" means a mind that is ingratiating and fawning. "Crookedness" means that one's conduct of body and speech visibly betrays some hidden intent.

"Craving gain and the like": craving gain, pleasure, and praise. Through this very dharma, one's own straightforward mind is corrupted, and one becomes unable to give rise to any deep root of goodness — just as a robe already dyed a foul color can no longer take on a fine one.

Furthermore: "Failing to perceive māra's own works, bodhicitta grows weak and feeble; the obstruction of karma, and the obstruction of the Dharma, likewise cause bodhicitta to be lost."

"Failing to perceive māra's own works": should one fail to recognize māra's own works, one is then unable to subdue them; and should one fail to subdue them, bodhicitta is thereby lost.

Question: What, then, are these works of māra?

Answer: At the very moment one ought to expound giving, upholding the precepts, patient endurance, diligent effort, meditative concentration, or the pāramitā of wisdom, and at the moment one ought to expound the profound meaning subsumed within the Great Vehicle, one takes no swift delight in expounding it. And even should one delight in expounding it, in the very midst of doing so, other distractions cause the mind to scatter. Whether reading, explaining, discussing, or listening and receiving it, one becomes arrogant and self-important, one's mind scattered, drawn toward other objects, given to idle thought and frivolous laughter.

Mutually mocking one another's arguments, the two parties fail to reach harmony, unable to penetrate the true meaning, and rise from their seats and depart, thinking: "Within this teaching, I have received no prediction of awakening, and my own mind remains impure. Nor has anything been said of my own city, town, or household of birth." It is for this reason that they no longer wish to hear the Dharma, fail to taste its flavor, and rise from their seats and depart.

Abandoning the pāramitās expounded within the Great Vehicle, and instead seeking omniscience within the śrāvaka's or the pratyekabuddha's own self-directed scriptures. Whether reading, explaining, or listening and receiving the teaching, one desires instead to speak of every manner of other matter, breaking apart and scattering the perfection of wisdom.

Namely, speaking of distant lands, towns and settlements, cities and pleasure-gardens, military campaigns and banditry, weapons and armor, hatred and love, suffering and joy, parents and siblings, husbands, wives, and children, clothing and food, bedding and medicine, and every article of daily livelihood — the mind thereby scattered, and the perfection of wisdom lost.

And further speaking of: greed, anger, and delusion; enemies and relatives; auspicious times and inauspicious times; song, dance, and music; sorrow and frivolous laughter; scriptures and verses; ancient events of ages past; kings and rulers of nations; earth, water, fire, and wind; the five desires and worldly wealth; and gain and support, and every other worldly matter — causing the mind to delight in them.

A note on slandering a bodhisattva

The distinction drawn here — that falsely accusing a bodhisattva of an offense they never committed is graver than merely discussing an offense they actually did commit, though both are still faulted — sets a real evidentiary standard even within condemnation itself. The sūtra Nāgārjuna cites goes further still, forbidding either kind of speech regardless of truth, which the treatise treats as the ideal even while distinguishing degrees of fault for those who fall short of it.

Ru-Yi Translation

Should māra transform himself into the appearance of a monk or a nun, and, by means of the causes and conditions of a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha scripture, cause a person to attain something, saying to them: "You ought to study and learn this scripture instead, abandoning what you originally studied" — the one listening to the Dharma no longer delights in receiving it, and the one expounding it grows indolent of mind, each drawn away by some other distraction.

The listener requires this teaching, yet the one expounding it wishes instead to go elsewhere. The one expounding it delights in expounding it, yet the listener wishes to go elsewhere.

The one expounding it is full of desire, craving gain; the listener has no mind to give anything at all.

The listener holds a mind of faith, delighting in wishing to hear the Dharma, yet the one expounding it takes no delight in expounding it. The one expounding it delights in expounding it, yet the listener takes no delight in it.

Or, at times, one is told: "The sufferings of hell are such that it is far better to exhaust every suffering within this very body and swiftly attain nirvāṇa — this is the greatest benefit." One is told: "The immeasurable afflictions of the animal realm, and the manifold transgressions of hungry ghosts and asuras" — and told: "Birth and death hold much sorrow and affliction; you should, within this very body, swiftly attain nirvāṇa — this is the greatest benefit." Or one is told, in praise, of the nobility and wealth of this world, and in praise of the merit and pleasure of the form and formless realms, that to be reborn among them is itself a great benefit.

Or one is told, in praise, of the benefit of the merit of stream-entry up through the fruit of arhatship: "Should you, within this very body, realize these very fruits, this is your own great benefit." Or the one expounding the Dharma delights in their own retinue, while the one listening has no wish to follow along.

Or the one expounding the Dharma wishes to travel to some land beset by famine and unrest, saying to the listener: "What use is it now for you to follow me to lands such as these?" — and the listener, at once growing weary and reluctant, no longer follows along.

Or the one expounding the Dharma prizes and reveres a certain patron, repeatedly going to pay them visits, causing the one listening to be unable to receive the teaching.

Or, within this profound Dharma, doubt is caused to arise: "This is not the very scriptural teaching spoken by the buddhas — what I myself expound is the buddhas' own scriptural teaching. Should a bodhisattva be able to practice this very teaching, they shall realize the ultimate truth." Through causes and conditions such as these, the two parties fail to reach harmony. One should know that all such matters are, in every case, the works of māra.

To speak of the essential meaning: whatever creates an obstruction to any wholesome dharma whatsoever is, in every case, the work of māra.

"Bodhicitta grows weak and feeble": since the afflictions grow strong in power, the mind of the path grows weak and feeble, without any force remaining, and the resolve for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening is forever severed.

"The obstruction of karma": though there are many kinds of karmic obstruction, what is spoken of here refers to that which causes one seeking the Great Vehicle to regress and retrogress.

"The obstruction of the Dharma": delighting in practicing unwholesome dharmas, and detesting emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, together with the pāramitās and every other profound and sublime dharma.

These four dharmas are likewise able to cause bodhicitta to be lost.

A note on this catalogue

What is striking about this extended survey of māra's own works is how ordinary nearly all of it is: not cosmic battles or terrifying apparitions, but a teacher and a listener simply out of sync — one wanting to travel while the other wants to stay, one bored while the other is eager, one distracted by gain while the other withholds attention. Nāgārjuna's own closing definition, "whatever creates an obstruction to any wholesome dharma whatsoever," generalizes the entire catalogue into a single principle: māra's own work does not require any supernatural intervention at all, only the mundane friction that already derails most human conversations.

The Four Black Dharmas, and What Reverses Them

無有疑悔，令生疑悔者：此人實不破戒，有少罪相而言大罪；若破正命威儀、若破正見，皆令生疑悔。

瞋大乘人者：有人乘大乘、無上乘、如來乘、大人乘、一切智人乘，乃至初發心者，於此人中，深生瞋恚，呵罵譏論，說其惡名，令廣流布。

共事諂曲心者：於和上、阿闍梨、諸善知識所，不以直心親近，習行曲心故。乃至未曾所識，亦行諂曲。

四黑法者：「黑」，名垢穢不淨，能失菩提心。如說：轉此五四法，　世世修善行；如是則不失，　無上菩提心。

五四合為二十法，是失菩提心。轉此法，修習行，世世不忘阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。

轉者：轉上五四法，所謂恭敬法、破慢心、遠離妄語、深尊重善知識。餘應如是知。

問曰：以何等法世世增長菩提願？又後復能更發大願？

"Promising a gift to one's teacher and then not giving it": whether one has already promised some object to one's own teacher, or not yet promised it, and afterward fails to give it. Or, in giving it, one gives it at the wrong time, in the wrong place, or in a manner not in accordance with the Dharma — this is itself the way of an outside path within this world. Within the Buddhadharma, having received the scriptural teaching from one's own teacher, should one possess any material wealth, one gives it to this very teacher for the sake of honoring the Dharma; and should one possess none, there is no fault in this.

"Causing one who has no doubt or regret to be forced into doubt and regret": this person has, in truth, not broken any precept, yet, over some trifling appearance of fault, one declares it a grave offense; whether it concerns a breach of right livelihood or proper bearing, or a breach of right view, in every case forcing doubt and regret to arise.

"Harboring anger toward those of the Great Vehicle": there are those who ride the Great Vehicle, the unsurpassed vehicle, the vehicle of the Thus Come One, the vehicle of the great person, the vehicle of the one of omniscience — down even to one who has only just given rise to the aspiration — and, toward such a person, giving rise to deep and grave anger, scolding and mocking them in argument, spreading a bad name for them far and wide.

"A mind of flattery and crookedness in matters shared with others": toward one's own preceptor, one's own instructor, and every other good and virtuous friend, failing to draw near to them with a straightforward mind, habitually practicing instead a crooked mind; and even toward those never before acquainted, likewise practicing this very flattery and crookedness.

As for "the four black dharmas": "black" refers to defilement, filth, and impurity, itself able to cause bodhicitta to be lost. As the verse says: "Reverse these five and these four dharmas, cultivating wholesome conduct life after life; and thus this unsurpassed bodhicitta will never be lost."

These five and these four together make twenty dharmas that cause bodhicitta to be lost. Reversing these very dharmas, cultivating and practicing their opposite, one never forgets the mind of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, life after life.

"Reverse" means to reverse the five and the four dharmas explained above — namely, revering the Dharma, breaking down the mind of arrogance, departing far from false speech, and deeply revering one's own good and virtuous friends. The rest should be understood in just this same way.

Question: By means of what dharma does this vow for awakening grow and increase life after life? And how, afterward, is one further able to give rise to this great vow once more?

Answer: "Even were one to lose one's own body and life, or the very throne of a wheel-turning sage-king, still one ought never, even then, to resort to false speech or to flattery and crookedness. This is able to cause every class of sentient being throughout the world to give rise to a mind of reverence toward the very assembly of bodhisattvas. Should there be a person able to practice a wholesome dharma such as this, their own vow for unsurpassed bodhi grows and increases, life after life."

The bodhisattva, by means of this very dharma, causes this vow for bodhi to grow and increase life after life, and is further able to give rise to this pure, great vow once more. Should it be for the sake of speaking truthfully that one loses even the throne of a wheel-turning sage-king, or the throne of a heavenly king, one ought still to speak truthfully, and never resort to false speech — how much more so, then, in matters of trifling consequence, should one fail to speak truthfully! Furthermore, toward one's own retinue, and toward every outside person as well, one departs from flattery and crookedness. Furthermore, from the very first arising of the aspiration onward, one gives rise to a mind of reverence toward every bodhisattva, honoring and praising them exactly as one would a buddha, without any difference. Furthermore, one causes them, according to one's own ability, to abide within the Great Vehicle.

A note on twenty dharmas

The chapter's own arithmetic — "these five and these four together make twenty dharmas" — refers back not to nine items but to two sets of dharmas each treated as a group: the earlier four causes of loss plus the "stinginess with essential Dharma" cluster of four, and this final "four black dharmas" cluster, apparently each counted as containing five constituent elements in the treatise's own internal reckoning. The precise arithmetic is less important than the structure it enacts one final time: name the failure mode in a tight verse, then declare that its exact reversal is the cure — the same shape that has organized nearly every list in this fascicle so far.

The Marks of Non-Retrogression Chapter

Question: These bodhisattvas are of two kinds: first, those still subject to retrogression; second, those irreversible, no longer subject to retrogression. Please explain the mark of each — what is the mark of one still subject to retrogression, and what is the mark of the irreversible?

Answer: "A mind that regards sentient beings equally; free of envy toward another's gain and support; never, even at the cost of one's own body and life, speaking of a Dharma-teacher's own faults; possessing faith and delight in the profound, sublime Dharma; and free of craving for reverence — possessed of these five dharmas, one is irreversible."

"A mind that regards sentient beings equally": since sentient beings are subsumed within the six destinies, whether of superior, middling, or inferior standing, the mind holds no distinction among them — this is called irreversible.

Question: As it is elsewhere stated: "Toward the buddhas and bodhisattvas, one ought to give rise to the very foremost mind of reverence — this does not hold for others!" And it is likewise stated: "Drawing near to the buddhas and bodhisattvas, revering and making offerings to them — this too does not hold for others!" How, then, can it be said that "toward every sentient being, the mind is equal, without any duality"?

Answer: Each of these statements carries its own distinct meaning, and this ought not to be raised as an objection. As for "a mind that regards sentient beings equally": should there be sentient beings who regard the bodhisattva as a hostile enemy, others who regard them as their own parents, and others still who regard them with indifference — among these three kinds of sentient beings, the mind of benefit remains equal, wishing to liberate every one of them without any distinction. It is for this reason that you ought not to raise this as an objection.

"Free of envy toward another's gain and support": should another obtain clothing, food, bedding, medicine, dwelling, property, gold and silver, treasure, villages, towns, cities, or attendants and the like, within such an act of giving, one gives rise to no envy, nor harbors any resentment, but delights in it with a joyful mind.

"Never speaking of a Dharma-teacher's own faults": should there be one who expounds the Great Vehicle's own dharma of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, or the six pāramitās, or the four abodes of merit, or the bodhisattva's ten grounds, and every other such dharma of the Great Vehicle — even at the cost of one's own life, one still does not utter their faults, how much more so, then, refraining from adding to them any evil charge at all!

"Possessing faith and delight in the profound, sublime Dharma": "the profound Dharma" refers to emptiness, signlessness, wishlessness, and every profound sūtra — such as the perfection of wisdom, the bodhisattva canon, and the rest. Within this Dharma, holding a single mind of faith and delight, free of any doubt whatsoever; in no other matter does one hold delight such as this, for it is within these profound sūtras alone that one tastes their true flavor.

"Free of craving for reverence": having penetrated the real mark of every dharma, one holds fame and disgrace, gain and loss, as equal, without any difference between them.

Possessing these five dharmas, as explained above, one never retrogresses from, and never grows indolent toward, unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening — this is called irreversible. Whatever runs contrary to this is called still subject to retrogression.

These bodhisattvas still subject to retrogression are themselves of two kinds: some are degenerate; others gradually advance further and attain irreversibility.

Question: As for what is spoken of as "degenerate," what is its mark?

Answer: "Lacking in resolve and capability; delighting in inferior dharmas; deeply attached to fame and gain; a mind that is not straightforward; stingy and possessive toward another household; without faith or delight in the Dharma of emptiness; and prizing mere speech alone — this is called the degenerate mark."

"Lacking in resolve and capability": their countenance without luster, their own majestic virtue shallow and thin.

Question: Is it not the case that bodily appearance and majestic virtue are not themselves the mark of irreversibility — why, then, is this statement made here?

Answer: This statement has its own meaning, and ought not to be doubted. What I mean is: "because inner merit is present, the body possesses majestic virtue" — I do not mean merely that the body's own color and countenance are fine and upright. "Resolve and capability" refers to majestic virtue and inner strength. Should a person be able to cultivate and gather wholesome dharmas and eliminate unwholesome ones, possessing genuine force within this very matter, this is called "resolve and capability." Though one's body might resemble that of a heavenly king, radiant as the sun and moon, should one be unable to cultivate wholesome dharmas and eliminate unwholesome ones, this is called "lacking in resolve and capability"; though one's body and countenance might be ugly and coarse, in form like a hungry ghost, should one be able to cultivate wholesomeness and eliminate what is unwholesome, this alone is called "resolve and capability" — it is for this reason that your objection does not hold.

"Delighting in inferior dharmas": aside from the buddha-vehicle itself, every other vehicle, compared to the buddha-vehicle, is slight and inferior, falling short of it — it is for this reason called "inferior," not because it is itself evil; and every other evil matter besides is likewise called inferior.

What the two lesser vehicles attain is inferior only in comparison to the buddha-vehicle. Since both alike transcend the world and enter nirvāṇa without remainder, they are not themselves called evil. It is for this reason that, should a person depart far from the buddha-vehicle and instead have faith and delight in the two lesser vehicles, this is called delighting in an inferior dharma. Though such a person may delight in what is otherwise a superior matter, since they have faith and delight in the two lesser vehicles, departing far from the Great Vehicle, this too is called delighting in an inferior dharma.

Furthermore, "inferior" also refers to an evil matter — namely, the five desires. And further, the sixty-two views such as eternalism and annihilationism, every argument of an outside path, and everything that increases birth and death — these too are inferior dharmas. Practicing dharmas such as these is likewise called delighting in an inferior dharma.

"Deeply attached to fame and gain": within matters of giving, material gain, offerings, and praise, one's mind clings deeply and skillfully devises means toward them, and, failing to attain the pure flavor of the Dharma, craves and delights in these matters instead.

"A mind that is not straightforward": their own nature is flattering and crooked, delighting in deception.

"Stingy and possessive toward another household": such a person, whatever household they enter, upon seeing another obtain gain, reverence, or praise, at once gives rise to envy, sorrow, and displeasure. Their mind impure, their own reckoning of self running deep, they crave and cling to gain and support, giving rise to a mind of envy, and resenting the very patron involved.

"Without faith or delight in the Dharma of emptiness": every buddha expounds three kinds of this dharma of emptiness — namely, the three gates of liberation. Toward this dharma of emptiness, having no faith, no delight, and holding it in no esteem, since their own mind fails to penetrate it.

"Prizing mere speech alone": delighting only in fine words, unable to practice in accordance with what is spoken; possessing only speech from the mouth, unable to have faith in and understand these very dharmas, and unable to attain their true flavor — this is called "the degenerate mark."

Should a person give rise to bodhicitta and yet possess marks such as these, one should know that this is a degenerate bodhisattva. "Degenerate" means untamed and unyielding. It is just as the very worst and most vicious horse is called degenerate — possessing the name of a horse alone, without any of a horse's actual use. The degenerate bodhisattva is likewise so — possessing only an empty name, without any genuine practice. Should a person not wish to become a degenerate bodhisattva, they ought to eliminate this unwholesome dharma, and receive their own name in accordance with the Dharma they actually practice.

A note on the horse

The simile of the vicious horse that carries "the name of a horse alone, without any of a horse's actual use" gives this chapter's own closing warning its sharpest edge: the danger named here is not failing to become a bodhisattva at all, but occupying the title while none of what the title actually names is present. This is the same concern that opened the entire treatise back in Fascicle 1 — a "bodhisattva in name only" who has not yet turned that name into "the true dharma itself" — now returned in its negative form, as a warning against never making that turn at all.

Question: You have stated: "Among bodhisattvas still subject to retrogression, there are two kinds — first, the degenerate bodhisattva; second, one who, through gradual diligent effort, later attains irreversibility." You have already explained the degenerate bodhisattva; please now explain the one who, through gradual diligent effort, later attains irreversibility.

Answer: "The bodhisattva grasps at no self, and grasps at no sentient being either; they make no distinction in expounding the Dharma, grasp at no bodhi, and do not behold the Buddha through any mark. Through these five qualities of merit, one comes to be called a great bodhisattva, and accomplishes irreversibility."

The bodhisattva, practicing these five qualities of merit, arrives directly at irreversibility.

As for "grasping at no self": because they have departed from attachment to self. This bodhisattva, seeking a self within what is internal, what is external, the five aggregates, the twelve sense-bases, and the eighteen elements, finds it nowhere to be obtained. They reflect:

"Were the aggregates themselves the self, then this self would bear the very mark of arising and ceasing; how, then, could one, by means of grasping, become at once the very one who grasps?

"Were the self to exist apart from the aggregates, it ought then to be obtainable outside the aggregates; how, then, could one, by means of grasping, become distinct from the one who grasps?

"Were the self to possess the five aggregates, this self would then be distinct from these five aggregates — just as the world commonly says that an ox is distinct from the owner of that ox; because two distinct things are joined together, this matter is then said to exist. It is for this reason that, were the self to possess the aggregates, this self would then be distinct from these very aggregates.

"Were the self present within the aggregates, as a person is present within a room, or as a listener is present upon a couch, this self ought then to be distinct from the aggregates.

"Were the aggregates present within the self, as fruit is present within a vessel, or as a fly is present within milk, these aggregates would then be distinct from the self.

"Just as what can be kindled is not itself the kindling, nor is this kindling apart from what can be kindled; this kindling does not itself possess what can be kindled, and this kindling is not present within what can be kindled — so too, the self is neither the aggregates, nor apart from the aggregates; the self does not itself possess the aggregates, and the aggregates are not present within the self, nor is the self present within the five aggregates. Just so should one understand every instance of a defiling agent and what it defiles, of the afflictions and the one afflicted, and every jar, garment, and the like.

"Should one claim that the self possesses a fixed nature, and that every dharma possesses its own distinct mark, one should know that such a person has not attained the true flavor of the Buddhadharma."

The bodhisattva, reflecting in this way, thereby departs from the view of self; having departed far from this view of self, they thereby grasp at no self at all.

As for "grasping at no sentient being": "sentient being" is named for what is distinct from the bodhisattva themselves; having departed from craving and the view of self, they reflect: "Were another person truly to possess a self, that person could then be regarded as 'other'; it is because of the existence of a self that this other person is regarded as 'other' — yet, in truth, seeking this self, it cannot be found at all. And since that other person likewise cannot be found, there being no 'other,' there is likewise no self — it is for this reason that the bodhisattva grasps at no sentient being either."

As for "making no distinction in expounding the Dharma": this bodhisattva, having faith in and understanding that every dharma is without duality, without any distinction, and of a single mark. They reflect: "Every dharma arises entirely from wrong recollection and discriminating thought, false and deceptive." This bodhisattva extinguishes every such discrimination, free of every decline and affliction. They thereby enter directly into the unsurpassed, ultimate causal dharma of the highest meaning, following no other's own wisdom.

A note on the fire simile

The verse comparing self and aggregates to "what can be kindled" and "the kindling" is Nāgārjuna's own signature analysis, developed at far greater length in his Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (chapter ten, already translated on this site): fire and fuel can neither be identical nor wholly separate without generating contradiction, so neither self and its own aggregates can be either. Its compressed appearance here, offered almost in passing as a settled conclusion rather than argued fresh, suggests this treatise assumes a reader already familiar with the fuller demonstration elsewhere in Nāgārjuna's own corpus.

And though, in truth, there is nothing at all, this too is not to say there is nothing whatsoever — this is what it means to relinquish entirely every discrimination that could be held. Every dharma, whether a cause or arisen from a cause, is alike a mark of tranquil cessation, neither to be grasped nor to be relinquished. It is just as ash renders a garment impure, and this very ash then further soils the garment — so too, without speech, this truth cannot be conveyed, and yet speech itself carries its own fault."

The bodhisattva, contemplating in this way, holds faith, understanding, and penetration; within the expounding of the Dharma, they make no distinction whatsoever.

As for "grasping at no bodhi": this bodhisattva, through faith in and understanding of the Dharma of emptiness, does not attain bodhi in the manner an ordinary being imagines it to be attained. They reflect:

"The Buddha does not attain bodhi, nor does one who is not a buddha attain it; every fruit, and every other dharma, is likewise so. Were there a buddha and a bodhi such that the buddha attains it, this would itself become a permanent thing; were there no buddha and no bodhi, this failure to attain would itself become a case of annihilation. Apart from the buddha, there is no bodhi; apart from bodhi, there is no buddha — and should neither their unity nor their difference be established, how could there be any union between them at all? In every dharma whatsoever, it is precisely because of difference that union can occur; yet bodhi is not different from the buddha — it is for this very reason that no union between these two can be established. The buddha and bodhi together, then, are established neither as different nor as united; and apart from these two possibilities, there is no third — how, then, could this ever be established? It is for this very reason that the buddha is himself tranquil cessation, and bodhi too is tranquil cessation; and because these two are alike tranquil cessation, everything whatsoever is alike tranquil cessation."

As for "not beholding the Buddha through any mark": this bodhisattva holds faith in, understands, and penetrates the signless Dharma. They reflect:

"Should everything whatsoever be without any mark, then everything whatsoever would thereby possess a mark; for tranquil cessation is itself signlessness, and this would then itself become a mark. Should one contemplate the signless dharma in this way, signlessness itself becomes a sign; and should one say that one cultivates signlessness, this is then no longer the cultivation of signlessness at all. Should the relinquishing of every craving attachment be called signlessness, then grasping at this very mark of relinquishing craving is itself the absence of any true liberation. Whenever there is grasping, it is because of this grasping that relinquishing then exists — who, then, grasps, and what matter is grasped, that could be called relinquishing? The one who grasps, the very act of grasping employed, and the dharma that can be grasped — taken together or apart, neither exists at all; every one of these is called tranquil cessation. Should any dharma be established through causal dependence upon a mark, this is itself the very absence of any inherent nature; and should there be no inherent nature present, this is itself the very absence of any mark. Should a dharma lack any inherent nature, and this itself be signlessness, how, then, could one say that the absence of inherent nature is itself signlessness? Should one make use of both existence and non-existence, at once negating and at once permitting them, though one speaks of them, so long as the mind remains unattached, then there is no fault in this at all. Where, then, was there ever first a dharma that did not afterward cease? Where, then, was there ever first a flame that did not afterward have its own extinguishing? This very tranquil cessation of what is marked is identical to the tranquil cessation of what is signless — it is for this reason that both this very speech of tranquil cessation, and the one who speaks of this tranquil cessation, and what preceded them, are themselves neither tranquil cessation, nor not tranquil cessation, neither both tranquil and not tranquil, nor neither tranquil nor not tranquil."

This bodhisattva, having thus penetrated the wisdom of signlessness, is free of any doubt or regret. They do not behold the Buddha through the mark of form, nor through the mark of feeling, perception, mental formations, or consciousness.

Question: How is it that one does not behold the Buddha through the mark of form, nor through feeling, perception, mental formations, or consciousness?

Answer: Form is not itself the Buddha, nor are feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness themselves the Buddha. Nor is there a buddha apart from form, nor a buddha apart from feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. Nor does the Buddha possess form, nor does the Buddha possess feeling, perception, mental formations, or consciousness. Nor is the Buddha present within form, nor is the Buddha present within feeling, perception, mental formations, or consciousness. Nor is form present within the Buddha, nor are feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness present within the Buddha.

The bodhisattva, grasping at no mark among these five, thereby attains to the very stage of irreversibility.

A note on this fivefold negation

The closing analysis of the Buddha and the five aggregates — neither identical to them, nor separate from them, nor possessing them, nor contained within them, nor containing them — is the same fivefold structure Nāgārjuna applies to fire and fuel, and to self and the aggregates, earlier in this same fascicle, now turned upon the single most consequential object a Buddhist practitioner could imagine grasping at: the Buddha himself. That the treatise applies its own strictest dialectical tool here, refusing the devotee even the comfort of a graspable Buddha to venerate, is a deliberate culmination — the marks of irreversibility close not with a positive vision to hold onto, but with the systematic removal of every last thing that could be held.

A note on this closing catalogue

This long list of marks moves fluidly between the metaphysical and the mundane — one line denies duality between the buddha-ground and the ordinary-being-ground, the next simply notes that the irreversible bodhisattva "sleeps but little" and keeps a body "free of parasites." Read together with the chapter's own closing dialectic on grasping at nothing whatsoever, the effect is instructive: the treatise never suggests that emptying every philosophical category of its own graspable content excuses a practitioner from equally ordinary, checkable standards of conduct — clean bedding, sparing speech, no gossip about kings and wars. The most rarefied philosophy and the most everyday habits are offered here as marks of the very same attainment.
