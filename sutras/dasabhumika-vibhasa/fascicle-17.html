Praising the Precepts, and the Wheel-Turning King's Seven Treasures

The treatise's final fascicle traces what purifies moral discipline down to its own root — six sets of four dharmas culminating in the very emptiness of mark and marked — then distinguishes four kinds of ascetic by what genuinely motivates each one, before a hundred consecutive similes praise the precepts without pause. It closes with the second ground's own fruit: a wheel-turning king, his seven treasures, and his four spontaneous virtues, described in the fullest physical detail this treatise offers anywhere.

Five Motivations for Forest-Dwelling, Only Two Genuine

Chinese Source

解頭陀品之餘

如五空閑說，餘功德亦爾；自讀誦教他，得捨空閑處。

阿練若比丘有五種分別：一、以惡意欲求利養。二、愚癡鈍根故，行阿練若。三、狂癡失意，作阿練若。四、為行頭陀行故，作阿練若。五、以諸佛菩薩賢聖所稱讚故，作阿練若。

於此五阿練若中，為行頭陀行故作阿練若；以諸佛菩薩賢聖所稱讚故作阿練若，是二為善，餘三可呵。

如五種分別阿練若法，餘十一頭陀行，亦應如是分別知。

問曰：佛說：「若已受阿練若法，終不應捨。」若有因緣得捨去不？

答曰：讀誦經因緣，可捨阿練若。

若比丘：欲從他受讀誦經法、若欲教他讀誦，應從阿練若處，來入塔寺。以是因緣可得捨離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the Ascetic Practices Chapter, Continued

"Just as explained for the five aspects of dwelling within solitude, every remaining merit is likewise so; oneself reciting the sūtras and teaching others to recite them, one may thereby relinquish this dwelling within solitude for a time."

The monk dwelling within the forest hermitage is distinguished into five kinds: first, dwelling there through an evil intent, wishing to seek gain and support; second, practicing forest-dwelling on account of foolish, dull faculties; third, taking up forest-dwelling through derangement and confusion of mind; fourth, taking up forest-dwelling for the sake of practicing the ascetic observances; fifth, taking up forest-dwelling because it is praised by every buddha, bodhisattva, and noble one.

Among these five kinds of forest-dwelling, taking it up for the sake of the ascetic observances, and taking it up because it is praised by every buddha, bodhisattva, and noble one — these two are wholesome, while the remaining three deserve reproach.

Just as this fivefold distinction applies to the dharma of forest-dwelling, every one of the remaining eleven ascetic practices ought likewise to be distinguished and understood in just this same way.

Question: The Buddha has stated: "Should one have already undertaken this dharma of forest-dwelling, one ought never, under any circumstance, to relinquish it." Should some cause and condition arise, is one able to relinquish it and depart?

Answer: "Through the very cause and condition of reciting the sūtras, one may relinquish this forest-dwelling."

Should a monk wish to receive the dharma of reciting the sūtras from another, or wish to teach another to recite them, they ought to come from the forest hermitage into the stūpa or temple. Through this very cause and condition, one is permitted to relinquish and depart from it.

A note on this fivefold motivation

The identical outward practice of forest-dwelling receives five entirely different moral evaluations depending solely on what motivates it — gain, dullness, derangement, genuine ascetic discipline, or alignment with what the tradition itself has praised — a distinction this treatise has applied consistently to nearly every practice discussed across all seventeen fascicles: the deed's own outward form never settles its worth, only the intent carrying it out.

Serving a Teacher Without Seeking Reward

Chinese Source

教他讀誦時，不應望供給，即時應念佛，佛常有所作。

阿練若從空閑處來，教他讀誦，不應求敬心供給。應當念：「佛尚自有所作，何況於我！念佛者：佛是多陀阿伽陀、三藐三佛陀，諸天、龍神、乾闥婆、阿修羅、迦樓羅、緊那羅、摩睺羅伽、釋提桓因、四天王、人、非人所供養；一切眾生無上福田，尚不求他供給，身自執事。我今未有所知，始欲求學，云何受他供給？」

復應作是念：我應善供給，一切諸眾生，不望彼供給，自利利他故。

云何為自利？若貴供給，則失法施功德；若不貴供給者，則得法施功德。

云何為利他？若貴彼供給而教令讀誦者。彼則生念：「師直以世利故，而教誨我，不以法故。」是人若以是心供給師者，則不得大利。

若但恭敬法故，尊重師者，則得大利，是名利他。

從他求智慧，應不惜身命。

若行者欲從他求智慧，應捨身命。「捨」者：為智慧故，勤心精進，恭敬於師，不惜身命。

問曰：何以故為智慧恭敬師而不惜身命？

答曰：若一字一心，以此為劫數，恭敬於師所，能說此論者。

離諸諂曲心，深愛而恭敬，晝夜不休息，盡於爾所劫。

隨師所教論義字數及爾所心念，若受法者心無諂曲、不惜身命，晝夜恭敬，始終無異。雖能如是，猶不報師所益論議智慧之恩，是故弟子應離諂曲心，捨貪惜身命，破於憍慢。

若師輕蔑及以敬愛，心無有異。當生深愛心、第一恭敬心，應生父母心，應生大師心，應生善知識想，應生能為難事想，應生難報心。

若師聽，則受。所常行事，不須師勅；餘事則相望師意，隨事而行。

師所愛重，隨而愛重，不應因師，求於世利。

莫求師讚歎，莫求名聞，但求智慧法寶。

師有謬失，常應隱藏；若師過釁，若彰露者，當方便覆之。

師有功德，稱揚流布，深心愛樂，聽受持解，思惟義趣，如所說行。

求自利、利他者，莫為秸弟子，莫為大弟子，莫為垢弟子，莫為衰弟子，莫為無益弟子。無如是等過，但住善弟子法中，供給於師。

Ru-Yi Translation

"At the very moment of teaching another to recite the sūtras, one ought never to hope for material support in return; at that very moment, one ought to be mindful of the Buddha, who himself constantly has much to accomplish."

The forest-dwelling monk, coming from this solitary place to teach another to recite the sūtras, ought never to seek reverent support in return. One ought to reflect thus: "The Buddha himself still has much to accomplish — how much more so, then, myself! As for being mindful of the Buddha: the Buddha is himself the Tathāgata, the Fully Awakened One, receiving offerings from every deva, nāga, gandharva, asura, garuḍa, kiṃnara, mahoraga, from Śakra, from the Four Heavenly Kings, and from every human and non-human being; the very unsurpassed field of merit for every sentient being, and yet he still never seeks another's own support, attending to every task himself. I, at this very moment, possess no true knowledge whatsoever, only now beginning to seek learning — how, then, could I possibly accept another's own support?"

One further ought to reflect thus: "I ought skillfully to provide for every sentient being myself, never hoping for their own support in return, for the sake of benefiting both myself and others."

How, then, is this benefiting oneself? Should one value receiving support, one thereby loses the very merit of giving the Dharma; should one never value receiving support, one thereby attains this very merit of giving the Dharma.

How, then, is this benefiting others? Should one, valuing another's own support, thereby teach them to recite the sūtras, this very person then gives rise to this thought: "My teacher instructs me solely for the sake of worldly gain, not for the sake of the Dharma itself." Should this person provide for their own teacher with a mind such as this, they thereby fail to attain any great benefit.

Should one revere their own teacher solely out of reverence for the Dharma itself, they thereby attain great benefit — this alone is what is called benefiting others.

"Seeking wisdom from another, one ought never to spare one's own body or life."

Should the practitioner wish to seek wisdom from another, they ought to relinquish their own body and life. "Relinquish" refers to, for the sake of wisdom, diligently applying effort, revering one's own teacher, never sparing one's own body or life.

Question: Why, then, for the sake of wisdom, ought one to revere one's own teacher without sparing one's own body or life?

Answer: "Should one, for even a single word, held in mind for even a single moment, treat this as though it were an entire kalpa, revering the very place where one's own teacher, able to expound this treatise, resides.

"Departing from every flattering, crooked mind, holding this deep love and reverence, never resting day or night, throughout this entire span of kalpas."

According to whatever number of words and phrases of this treatise one's own teacher has taught, and this same number of mindful moments, should the one receiving this very dharma hold a mind free of flattery and crookedness, never sparing their own body or life, revering them day and night, never once wavering from beginning to end — even were one able to do this, one would still be unable fully to repay this very kindness of the wisdom of this treatise's own meaning that one's teacher has bestowed. It is for this reason that the disciple ought to depart from a flattering, crooked mind, relinquish craving and reluctance toward their own body and life, and break through their own arrogance.

Whether one's own teacher shows contempt or reverent affection, one's own mind ought to hold no difference whatsoever toward them. One ought to give rise to a deep mind of love, the very foremost mind of reverence, ought to give rise to a mind toward them as one's own parent, ought to give rise to a mind toward them as a great teacher, ought to give rise to a thought of them as a good and virtuous friend, ought to give rise to a thought of them as one able to accomplish what is difficult, and ought to give rise to a mind of a debt difficult to repay.

Should one's own teacher permit something, one then accepts it. Whatever is constantly practiced requires no command from one's own teacher; every other matter, however, ought to be weighed against one's own teacher's own intent, acting accordingly.

Whatever one's own teacher loves and values, one ought likewise to love and value it, never seeking worldly gain on account of one's own teacher.

Never seek one's own teacher's own praise, never seek renown, but seek only the very treasure of wisdom itself.

Should one's own teacher commit some error or mistake, one ought constantly to conceal it; should this very fault of one's own teacher become exposed, one ought, through skillful means, to cover it over.

Should one's own teacher possess genuine merit, one ought to praise and spread word of it, with a deep mind of love and delight, listening to, receiving, upholding, and understanding it, reflecting upon its own true meaning, and practicing exactly as it has been taught.

One who seeks to benefit both themselves and others ought never to become a mere husk of a disciple, nor a merely great disciple, nor a defiled disciple, nor a declining disciple, nor a useless disciple. Free of faults such as these, one ought only to abide within the very dharma of a good disciple, providing for one's own teacher accordingly.

A note on the four unhealthy disciples

The five failed modes of discipleship named here — mere husk, merely great, defiled, declining, and useless — each name a way the student-teacher relationship can go wrong even while outwardly persisting, a caution that pairs naturally with the extensive instruction on concealing a teacher's own faults rather than exposing them: loyalty here is measured not by uncritical praise but by the sustained, humble reverence this passage describes at length, protective of the teacher's own reputation without ever excusing genuine harm.

Bhadrapāla and the Reverence Owed a True Teacher

Chinese Source

如《般舟經》說：佛告颰陀婆羅：「若菩薩欲得是三昧者，應勤精進，於諸師所，生尊重心、難遭心。若從口聞，若得經卷處，於是師所，應深心恭敬，生父母心、善知識心、大師心，以能說如是法，助菩提故。

「颰陀婆羅！若求菩薩道者，若求聲聞者，所從師讀誦是法處，不生深恭敬心、父母心、善知識心、大師心，能得誦利是法，令不忘失，久住不滅者，無有是處。何以故？颰陀婆羅以不恭敬因緣故，佛法則滅。

「是故颰陀婆羅！若求菩薩道者，若求聲聞者，於所從聞、讀誦、書寫是法處，生恭敬心、父母心、善知識心、大師心者，於所讀誦、書寫，未得者令得，已得久住，則有是處。何以故？以恭敬心故，佛法不滅。

「是故颰陀婆羅！我今告汝：於是師所，應生深恭敬心、父母心、善知識心、大師心，是則隨我所教。」

Ru-Yi Translation

As the Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra states: the Buddha told Bhadrapāla: "Should the bodhisattva wish to attain this very samādhi, they ought diligently to apply effort, giving rise, toward every teacher, to a mind of reverence, a mind that regards them as difficult indeed to encounter. Whether heard directly from their own mouth, or obtained from a scriptural volume, toward this very teacher, one ought to hold a deep mind of reverence, giving rise to a mind toward them as one's own parent, a mind of a good and virtuous friend, a mind of a great teacher, since they are able to expound this very dharma, assisting one's own bodhi.

"Bhadrapāla! Should one who seeks the bodhisattva path, or one who seeks the śrāvaka path, at the very place where they receive and recite this dharma from their own teacher, fail to give rise to this deep mind of reverence, this mind of a parent, this mind of a good and virtuous friend, this mind of a great teacher — that they should still be able to recite this dharma fluently, causing it never to be forgotten or lost, enduring long without ever being extinguished, there exists no possibility of this whatsoever. And why is this so? Bhadrapāla, through the very cause and condition of this lack of reverence, the Buddhadharma is thereby extinguished.

"It is for this reason, Bhadrapāla, that should one who seeks the bodhisattva path, or one who seeks the śrāvaka path, at the very place where they hear, recite, or copy out this dharma, give rise to this mind of reverence, this mind of a parent, this mind of a good and virtuous friend, this mind of a great teacher — that whatever is recited or copied out, whatever has not yet been attained shall thereby be attained, and whatever has already been attained shall endure long — this is indeed possible. And why is this so? Through this very mind of reverence, the Buddhadharma is never extinguished.

"It is for this reason, Bhadrapāla, that I now declare to you: toward this very teacher, you ought to give rise to this deep mind of reverence, this mind of a parent, this mind of a good and virtuous friend, this mind of a great teacher — this alone is what accords with my own teaching."

A note on this closing citation

This second quotation from the Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra addressed to Bhadrapāla — the same sūtra and the same householder bodhisattva this treatise first introduced back in Fascicle 9's own account of buddha-recollection — closes the entire chapter on the ascetic practices by tying reverence toward a teacher directly to the survival of the Dharma itself: the sūtra's own repeated claim is not merely that disrespect is impolite, but that the transmission of the teaching across generations depends structurally on this very quality of regard, since a text recited without reverence is a text destined to be forgotten.

The First Four Dharmas That Purify the Precepts

Chinese Source

助尸羅果品第三十三

如是菩薩為求多聞，知多聞義，已隨說行，故能令尸羅清淨。

清淨尸羅法，應當修行。

問曰：何等法能令尸羅清淨？

答曰：護身口意業，亦不得護法，終不令我見，及以餘見雜，迴向薩婆若，此四淨尸羅。

行者修此四法，尸羅自然清淨。

護身口意業者，常應正念身口意業，乃至小罪不令錯謬。譬如龜、鼈常護頭足。

此人深樂空故，於第一義中而亦不得護三業法。

有人雖見法空，謂知空者在，是故說不雜我見、眾生見、人見、壽者見、知者見。

迴向薩婆若者，持戒果報不求餘福，但為度一切眾生以求佛道。

是為四。

Ru-Yi Translation

Assisting the Fruit of Moral Discipline Chapter

This bodhisattva, seeking vast learning for the sake of knowing its own true meaning, having already practiced accordingly, is thereby able to cause moral discipline to become pure.

This very dharma of purifying moral discipline ought to be cultivated and practiced.

Question: What dharma is able to cause moral discipline to become pure?

Answer: "Guarding the karma of body, speech, and mind, and yet not even attaining this very dharma of guarding; never allowing the view of self, nor any other view besides, to become mixed in; dedicating it toward omniscience — these four purify moral discipline."

The practitioner who cultivates these very four dharmas thereby causes moral discipline to become naturally pure.

As for "guarding the karma of body, speech, and mind": one ought constantly to hold right mindfulness toward the karma of body, speech, and mind, never allowing even the smallest offense to occur through error. Just as a tortoise or turtle constantly guards its own head and limbs.

This very person, deeply delighting in emptiness, within the very ultimate meaning, does not even attain this dharma of guarding these three karmas either.

There are those who, though they behold the emptiness of every dharma, still believe there remains some knower of this emptiness — it is for this reason that the text states one must never mix in the view of self, the view of a sentient being, the view of a person, the view of a life-force, or the view of a knower.

As for "dedicating it toward omniscience": the fruit and recompense of upholding the precepts is never sought for any other merit whatsoever, but only for the sake of liberating every sentient being, seeking the path to buddhahood alone.

These are the four.

A note on guarding what cannot be attained

This first set of four dharmas builds its own internal tension deliberately: the practitioner must guard the three karmas with the vigilance of a tortoise withdrawing its limbs, and yet, within ultimate meaning, must not even attain this very dharma of guarding as some fixed possession. The apparent contradiction resolves through the same distinction this treatise drew in Fascicle 16 between the conventional precept and the supreme precept beyond self — conventional vigilance and ultimate non-attachment operate simultaneously, at different levels, rather than one canceling the other.

The Second and Third Sets: The Middle Way, and the Noble Lineages

Chinese Source

復有四法，能令尸羅清淨。所謂：無我我所心，亦無斷、常見，入於眾緣法，則能淨尸羅。

無我我所心者，不貪著我、我所心，但知此心虛妄顛倒而無我法。

無斷、常見者，以斷、常見多過故。

入眾緣法者，知諸法從眾緣生，無有定性，行於中道。

如是四法能淨尸羅。

復有四法能淨尸羅。所謂：行四聖種行，及十二頭陀，亦不樂眾鬧，念何故出家。

四聖種者，所謂：趣得衣服而足，趣得飲食而足，趣得坐臥具而足，樂斷樂修行。

十二頭陀者，所謂：受阿練若法，受乞食法，糞掃衣，一坐，常坐，食後不受非時飲食，但有三衣，毛毳衣，隨敷坐，樹下住，空地住，死人間住。

亦不樂眾鬧者，不與在家、出家者和合。有人雖行阿練若法，多知、多識故，多人往來，是故說不樂眾鬧；若至餘處，若心不與和合。

何故出家者，行尸羅者作是念：「我何故而出家？」念已，隨出家事欲成就故，如所說行。

是為四。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, four dharmas able to cause moral discipline to become pure. Namely: "A mind free of self and what belongs to self; further free of the view of annihilationism or eternalism; entering into the very dharma of dependent origination — through this, one is able to purify moral discipline."

As for "a mind free of self and what belongs to self": never craving or clinging to a mind of self or what belongs to self, but knowing only that this very mind is illusory and inverted, without any dharma of self whatsoever.

As for "free of the view of annihilationism or eternalism": since the view of annihilationism and the view of eternalism each bring many faults.

As for "entering into the very dharma of dependent origination": knowing that every dharma arises from a convergence of conditions, possessing no fixed nature of its own, and practicing the very middle way.

These four dharmas, in this way, purify moral discipline.

There are, further, four dharmas able to purify moral discipline. Namely: "Practicing the conduct of the four noble lineages, and further the twelve ascetic practices; never delighting in the clamor of the crowd; and being mindful of why one has gone forth from home."

As for "the four noble lineages": namely, contentment with whatever robe one obtains; contentment with whatever food and drink one obtains; contentment with whatever bedding one obtains; and delighting in severing and delighting in cultivation.

As for "the twelve ascetic practices": namely, receiving the dharma of forest-dwelling, receiving the dharma of begging for food, wearing the rag robe, a single sitting, always sitting, never receiving any drink or food at an improper time after this meal, possessing only three robes, the woolen robe, sitting wherever a seat is provided, dwelling beneath a tree, dwelling in the open ground, and dwelling among the dead.

As for "never delighting in the clamor of the crowd": never joining together with either householder or renunciant. There are those who, though they practice this dharma of forest-dwelling, since they possess much learning and are widely known, receive many visitors — it is for this reason that the text states one never delights in this very clamor; should one go to some other place, one's own mind still never joins together with the crowd there.

As for "being mindful of why one has gone forth from home": one who practices moral discipline reflects thus: "Why, then, have I gone forth from home?" Having reflected thus, one, according with this very matter of going forth from home, wishing to accomplish it, thereupon practices exactly as taught.

These are the four.

A note on these two consecutive sets

The second set of four grounds moral discipline in the same Madhyamaka reasoning that runs throughout this treatise — freedom from self, freedom from the twin extremes of annihilationism and eternalism, and entry into dependent origination as the middle way between them — while the third set turns immediately practical, naming the specific external disciplines (the four noble lineages of contentment, the twelve ascetic practices) that give this philosophical orientation its own concrete daily shape. The chapter's own structure alternates in exactly this way throughout: an abstract purifying principle followed by its practical enactment.

The Fourth Set: Emptiness of the Aggregates, and the Refutation of Mark and Marked

Chinese Source

復有四法能淨尸羅，所謂：五陰無生滅，六性如法性，見六情亦空，不著世俗語。

如是之四法，亦能淨尸羅。

五陰無生滅者，思惟五陰本末故，見五陰無生滅者。

見地等六性如法性，如法性不可得，六性亦不可得。

知六情雖是苦樂等心心數法因緣，以正智推求，亦知是空。

了達三種皆知是空，有行者貪著於空則還妨道，是故說：「莫貪著空」，隨於世俗說空名字。

如是法者能淨尸羅。

問曰：若爾者，云何言五陰諸法？

答曰：以空故，五陰諸法空。最後言「莫著於空」者，空亦應捨，如是無有邪疑法妨礙尸羅。

問曰：五陰諸法，以有相可相故，決定有。如說：「色是苦惱相，覺苦樂是受相。」現有如是等諸相，云何言非空非不空？

答曰：惱壞是色相，何等為是色？

若惱是色相，離相無可相。

此相在何處，無相無可相，世界終無有，無相有可相。

相與及可相，非合非不合，其來無所從，去亦無所至。

若有合非合，成於相可相，如是則為失，相及可相相。

以相成可相，相亦不自成，相自不能成，云何成可相？

世界甚可愍，分別相可相，迷惑諸邪徑，邪師所欺誑。

相可相則是，無相無可相，如是眼見事，如何不能知？

These four dharmas, in this way, likewise purify moral discipline.

As for "the five aggregates are without arising or ceasing": through reflecting upon the very origin and full extent of the five aggregates, one beholds that the five aggregates are without arising or ceasing.

As for "beholding the six natures — earth, and the rest — as identical with the very nature of the Dharma": just as the very nature of the Dharma can never be apprehended, these six natures likewise can never be apprehended.

Knowing that, though the six sense-faculties are the very cause and condition of suffering, joy, and every mental factor, through the very pursuit of right wisdom, one likewise knows them to be empty.

Having fully penetrated all three of these, knowing them to be empty — should a practitioner nonetheless crave and cling even to this very emptiness, this would itself once more obstruct the path; it is for this reason that the text states: "Never crave or cling even to emptiness itself," speaking of the very name "emptiness" only according to conventional usage.

A dharma such as this is able to purify moral discipline.

Question: If this is so, why, then, does the text still speak of "the five aggregates" and every such dharma at all?

Answer: Because of emptiness, the five aggregates and every such dharma are themselves empty. The very last statement, "never cling even to emptiness itself," means that emptiness too ought to be relinquished — in this way, there exists no wrongful doubt or dharma whatsoever able to obstruct moral discipline.

Question: The five aggregates and every such dharma, since they possess a mark and something marked by it, definitely exist. "Where, then, does this very mark reside? Without any mark, and without anything marked by it, throughout this entire world there could never exist a mark without anything genuinely marked by it.

"The mark and what is marked by it — neither joined together, nor not joined together; this mark comes from nowhere whatsoever, and departs to nowhere whatsoever either.

"Should there exist a joining, or a non-joining, that accomplishes this mark and what is marked by it, this itself would then lose the very mark of both the mark and what is marked by it.

"Through this mark, one accomplishes what is marked by it — and yet this very mark cannot even accomplish itself; since this mark is itself unable to be accomplished, how, then, could it accomplish what is marked by it?

"The mark and what is marked by it are themselves without any mark whatsoever, and without anything genuinely marked at all — this very matter, evident even to the ordinary eye, how could it possibly go unrecognized?

"Wherever one calculates a mark and what is marked by it, there exists this very idle proliferation; and wherever this idle proliferation arises, one thereby follows the very domain of the afflictions."

A note on the mark that cannot mark itself

This dense argument against the independent reality of "mark" (相) and "what is marked" (可相) recapitulates a classical Madhyamaka move: a mark cannot exist apart from what it marks, and what it marks cannot exist apart from the mark that identifies it, so neither can serve as the other's own independent foundation — and since the mark cannot even establish itself, it certainly cannot establish anything else. This argument grounds the fourth set of purifying dharmas in the deepest layer of analysis this treatise offers, moving from the aggregates' own emptiness through the very concept of "having a mark at all" being shown empty.

A note on this string of similes

Five similes in unbroken succession — fire from drilled wood, eye-consciousness, a dragon's rain cloud, a painted figure on a wall, and a lamp's own flame — each illustrate the identical point from a different angle: something genuinely functions, genuinely appears, genuinely produces effects, without originating from any single locatable source or departing to any locatable destination when it ceases. This is dependent origination's own positive content stated through image rather than argument, complementing the preceding section's negative argument against mark and marked with a constructive account of how phenomena actually do arise and pass away.

The verse's own rigorous, near-mathematical treatment of "superior," "middling," and "inferior" applies the same dependent-origination logic used against mark and marked in the previous section to hierarchical ranking itself: none of the three terms can produce the other two, since a truly independent inferior would need the middling and superior to already exist as its own contrast, while a genuinely self-sufficient inferior producing only itself leaves the other two terms unaccounted for either way. The passage's own sharpest turn is its warning against signlessness becoming its own subtle trap: to declare something "signless" is already to have applied a mark to it, so genuine freedom from marks cannot rest even in the concept of "no mark" without falling back into exactly the grasping it was meant to escape. A note on grass and gold in the fire

The image of a blade of grass consumed by fire, set against genuine gold that emerges from the same fire unharmed, gives concrete shape to why the doctrine of non-self, taught identically to every listener, produces such different effects: what determines whether a mind is destroyed or refined by this teaching is not the teaching itself but the accumulated preparation the listener already brings to it. This closes the sixth and final set of four dharmas purifying moral discipline, and with it, the treatise's own extended sequence tracing this purification from guarding the three karmas all the way down to bearing the most demanding doctrine the tradition offers.

The Kāśyapa-parivarta's own point mirrors this treatise's own account of the supreme precept from Fascicle 16: flawless outward conduct alone, without the corresponding view of emptiness, is precisely what makes these four cases counterfeit rather than genuine.

A note on affected bearing's own fear of emptiness

The second type's own most telling feature is not its outward discipline, which is genuinely impressive, but its specific fear: dreading emptiness "as though falling into a pit" and treating anyone who teaches it as a hostile enemy. This detail links this passage directly back to the sixth precept-purifying dharma explained earlier in this fascicle — "never startled by signlessness" — showing that even rigorous, well-regarded ascetic conduct fails this treatise's own test if it cannot tolerate the very teaching moral discipline was ultimately meant to purify itself toward.

There exists an ascetic who does not even crave or spare their own body — how much less, then, fame and gain! Hearing that every dharma is empty, possessing nothing whatsoever, their own mind fills with great joy, and they practice exactly as it has been taught.

They do not even crave or spare nirvāṇa itself while practicing the holy life — how much less, then, could they crave or spare the three realms!

They do not even cling to the very view of emptiness — how much less, then, cling to the view of self, of person, of sentient being, of life-force, of a living being, of a knower, or of a seer!

A note on this closing paradox

The final description's own repeated structure — "not even X, how much less Y" — builds a genuine ascetic identity out of successive relinquishments that go further than any ordinary renunciation would think to reach: not craving nirvāṇa itself, not clinging even to emptiness, not discriminating even among the noble assembly. This fourth type is defined less by what it does than by what it has stopped grasping at, including the very distinction between bondage and liberation — a portrait that closes the loop opened by the second type's own fear of emptiness, since true practice turns out to be precisely the fearlessness that affected bearing could never sustain.

A note on the fear of a fixed station

Among the ten fears a bodhisattva transcends through pure precepts, the seventh — "the fixed station of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha" — stands out against the more expected dangers surrounding it (hell, poverty, warfare, slander): this treatise has argued since Fascicle 13 that permanent attainment of either lesser vehicle forecloses the path to buddhahood more severely than even hell itself does, and its inclusion here, alongside genuinely mortal dangers, signals how seriously this fear is meant to be taken. This closes the chapter's own extended treatment of what purifies moral discipline and what it protects against; the treatise now turns to a sustained hymn praising the precepts themselves.

A note on this unbroken physical inventory

The description of the jade woman, running from her own fingernails through every feature of her face and body to her own character and skill, is by far the most physically detailed portrait this treatise offers anywhere across all seventeen fascicles, matched only by the earlier accounts of the Buddha's own thirty-two marks and eighty minor marks in Fascicles 9 and 10. This is a deliberate structural echo: the seven treasures of a wheel-turning king's reign are themselves a worldly reflection of buddhahood's own attributes, the same descriptive method — exhaustive, feature-by-feature enumeration — applied here to temporal sovereignty as it was there applied to the Buddha's own body, confirming this treatise's own recurring claim that the two forms of accomplishment share a common root even while remaining categorically distinct.

A note on this treatise's own close

The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā Śāstra ends here, in the middle of its own account of the second bodhisattva ground, having completed only two of the ten grounds the Ten Grounds Sūtra itself describes — a closing this abrupt is characteristic of the work's own transmission history, and traditional scholarship has long understood the received text as incomplete relative to whatever fuller commentary, covering all ten grounds, may once have existed or been originally planned. The final line's own comparison — hundreds of samādhis and visions of buddhas in the first ground, thousands in the second — is the treatise's own last word before it ends, a quiet reaffirmation of the graded, ever-expanding structure of attainment this entire seventeen-fascicle work has traced from its very first fascicle onward.

Fascicle 17 Complete — The Treatise Concludes

This closes Fascicle 17, and with it the entire Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā Śāstra as it has come down to us: the reverence owed a teacher, six sets of four dharmas purifying moral discipline down to its own root in the emptiness of mark and marked, five similes for how phenomena arise without coming or going, the equality of buddhas, sentient beings, and every dharma, four false precepts that merely resemble true ones, four kinds of ascetic distinguished by what genuinely motivates each, keeping the precepts for no worldly reason whatsoever, a hundred consecutive similes praising moral discipline without pause, and, closing the entire work, the second ground's own fruit — a wheel-turning king and his seven treasures, described in the fullest physical detail this treatise offers anywhere, ending mid-course in its own account of the ten grounds exactly as it has survived to the present day.
