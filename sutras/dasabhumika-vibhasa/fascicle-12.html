Verses of Praise, and What Generates the Samādhi of Seeing Every Buddha

A long devotional hymn recapitulates every one of the forty unshared dharmas and the bodhisattva's own past-life giving in verse, meant to be recited as praise. Then the treatise turns practical: from visualizing the physical body toward the Dharma body and beyond, the exact practices — twenty for the householder, sixty for the renunciant — that generate the pratyutpanna samādhi, its vast fruit, and, closing the fascicle, the marks that purify the very first bodhisattva ground.

Verses Praising the Forty Unshared Dharmas

Chinese Source

讚偈品第二十四

已如是解四十不共法竟，應取是四十不共法相念佛，又應以諸偈讚佛，如現在前對面共語，如是則成念佛三昧。

如偈說：聖主大精進，四十獨有法，我今於佛前，敬心以稱讚：如意及飛行。其力無邊限。

於聖如意中，無有與等者。聲聞中自在。他心智無量。善能調伏心，隨意而應適。其念如大海，湛然在安隱。世間無有法，而能擾亂者。

諸佛所稱歎，金剛三昧寶，得之在胸中；如賢懷直心。善知不定法。四無色定事，微細難分別，盡知無有餘。眾生若已滅、今滅及當滅，唯獨有世尊，智慧能通達。善知不相應、非色法中事；一切諸世間，悉皆不能知。

世尊大威力，功德不可量。智慧無邊際，皆無與等者。於四問答中，超絕無倫匹，眾生諸問難，一切皆易答。

若諸世間中，欲有害佛者，是事皆不成，以成不殺法。若於三時中，諸有所說者，言必不虛設，常有大果報。凡有所說法，無非是希有。義趣尚不謬，何況於言辭。於三聖弟子，上中下差別，四雙八輩等，第一大導師。

身口意業命，畢竟常清淨，是故於此中，不復須防護。

自說一切智，心無有疑畏；若人來難我，恐有所不知。自說漏盡相，盡到無漏邊，心無有疑畏，餘漏有不盡。自說障礙法，於中無疑難；雖有用此法，不能為障礙。所說八聖道，心無有疑畏；有言是八道，不能至解脫。

如實知是因、是果及與非；故號一切智，名聞流無量。三世所有業，是諸業定報，及非定果報，種種皆悉知。

諸禪三昧中，麁細深淺事，皆悉能了知，禪中無等者。先知眾生根，上中下差別。種種樂及性，隨宜而說法。行道得諸利，兼以化導人，是以弟子眾，如實得善利。

宿命知無量、天眼見無邊；一切人天中，無能知其限。住金剛三昧，滅煩惱及氣，又知人漏盡，故名漏盡力。煩惱諸禪障、一切法障礙，三礙得解脫，號無礙解脫。

四十不共法，功德不可量，無能廣說者，我已略說竟。世尊若一劫，稱說此佛法，猶尚不可盡，況我無此智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Verses of Praise Chapter

Having now explained the forty unshared dharmas in full, one ought to take up the very marks of these forty unshared dharmas and be mindful of the Buddha through them, and further ought to praise the Buddha through verse, exactly as though he stood directly before oneself, speaking face to face — practiced in this way, this becomes the very samādhi of buddha-recollection.

As this verse says: "O sovereign lord of great diligence, possessing these forty dharmas unique to yourself alone — I now, before the Buddha, with a reverent mind, offer this very praise: mastery of wish-fulfillment, and flight itself — this very power is without any bound or limit.

"Within this very noble wish-fulfillment, none whatsoever are equal to you. Mastery among every śrāvaka. Immeasurable wisdom in knowing another's mind. Skillfully able to subdue the mind, according with it exactly as fitting. This very mindfulness, like the great ocean itself, tranquil and abiding in peace. There exists no dharma in this world whatsoever able to disturb it.

"The very treasure of the vajra samādhi, praised by every buddha, is attained and held within your own breast — like a worthy one who holds an upright mind. Skillfully knowing what is undetermined. The very matter of the four formless absorptions, subtle and difficult to distinguish, entirely known without any remainder. Sentient beings, whether already extinguished, presently being extinguished, or yet to be extinguished — you alone, World-Honored One, are able, through wisdom, to penetrate this fully. Skillfully knowing what is dissociated from mind, and every formless matter — every world without exception is entirely unable to know this.

"World-Honored One, your own great majestic power, this merit beyond all measure. Your own wisdom without any boundary whatsoever, with none whatsoever equal to it. Within these four kinds of question and answer, transcending all, beyond any comparison — every question and challenge raised by sentient beings, every one of them easily answered.

"Should there exist, throughout every world, one who wishes to harm the Buddha, this very matter can never be accomplished, since he has attained the very dharma of never being killed. Whatever is spoken, throughout the three times, these very words are never spoken in vain, constantly bringing a great fruit in recompense. Whatever dharma is spoken, none of it is anything but rare and marvelous. Even its own true meaning is never mistaken — how much less, then, its mere words! Among the three kinds of noble disciple, distinguished as superior, middling, and inferior, the four pairs and eight kinds of noble one — you are the very foremost, the great guide among them all.

"Your own karma of body, speech, mind, and life, ultimately and constantly pure — it is for this reason that, within this very domain, no further guarding is required at all.

"You yourself declare your own omniscience, your own mind holding no doubt or fear whatsoever — should any person come to challenge me, fearing that there might exist something I do not know. You yourself declare the very mark of exhausted outflows, having reached entirely the very limit free of outflows, your own mind holding no doubt or fear whatsoever — fearing that some remaining outflow might not yet be exhausted. You yourself speak of the dharmas that obstruct the path, within this holding no doubt or difficulty whatsoever — fearing that, though these very dharmas are made use of, they might fail to genuinely obstruct the path. You speak of the eightfold noble path, your own mind holding no doubt or fear whatsoever — fearing that someone might say this eightfold path is unable to reach liberation.

"Truly and accurately knowing what is, and what is not, a cause, and what is, and what is not, its own fruit — it is for this reason that you are named the omniscient one, your own renown spreading without measure. Every karmic deed belonging to the three times, whether its own recompense is fixed, or its own fruit and recompense is not fixed — every kind of this, entirely known.

"Within every meditative absorption and samādhi, whether coarse or fine, whether deep or shallow — all of this entirely understood, with none equal to you within meditative absorption. First knowing every sentient being's own faculties, distinguished as superior, middling, and inferior. Every kind of inclination and nature, according to what is fitting, expounding the Dharma accordingly. Practicing the path, attaining every benefit, and further teaching and guiding others — it is through this that the assembly of disciples truly attains genuine benefit.

"Knowledge of past lives immeasurable, the heavenly eye's own vision boundless — among every human and every deva, none whatsoever are able to know its own limit. Abiding within the vajra samādhi, extinguishing every affliction together with its own habitual residue, and further knowing a person's own exhaustion of outflows — it is for this reason that this is called the power of the exhaustion of outflows. The obstruction of the afflictions, the obstruction of every meditative absorption, and the obstruction of every dharma whatsoever — liberated from these very three obstructions, this is named unobstructed liberation.

"These forty unshared dharmas, their own merit beyond all measure — none whatsoever are able to expound them at full length, and I myself have now only briefly explained them. Should the World-Honored One himself, across an entire kalpa, expound this very Buddhadharma, it still could not be exhausted in full — how much more so, then, for myself, who lacks even this very wisdom!"

A note on verse as its own form of practice

These opening verses recapitulate, in condensed poetic form, every unshared dharma explained in prose across Fascicles 10 and 11, and the chapter's own opening instruction frames this recitation as itself constituting the samādhi of buddha-recollection — not a mere summary for the reader's convenience, but the actual devotional practice this entire section of the treatise has been building toward. The final stanza's own humility, acknowledging that even a full kalpa of the Buddha's own speech could not exhaust these forty dharmas, and that the verses just recited are only the barest sketch, is itself a familiar closing gesture in Buddhist hymnic literature.

The Immeasurable Merit of the Marks, and the Founding Vow

Chinese Source

世尊大慈蔭，無量業善集，四功德處故，得佛無量法。世尊所稱說，四功德勝處，我今還以此，稱讚於如來。

三十二相具，相有百福德，八十種妙好，三界誰能有。三千大千界，眾生所有福，果報為百倍，相有如是德。如此諸福德，并及其果報，復以為百倍，成一白毫相。三十相一一，福德及果報，復以為千倍，成一肉髻相。世尊諸功德，不可得度量，如人以尺寸，量空不可盡。

從初發大心，為度眾生故，堅心無量劫，是故成佛道。精勤欲成滿，如此之大願，無量劫數中，行諸難苦行。如諸往古佛，說四功德處，無量劫乃成，今得安住中。本為護實諦，捨身及親愛，財寶諸富樂，是故得具足。

無量劫數中，見聞覺知法，每先善思惟，而後為人說。若於不見等，及於中有疑，而能如實說，所益無有量。不說他匿事，嫌譏而拒逆，念常在安慧，順化令安隱。第一真妙諦，涅槃實為最，餘者皆虛妄，世尊德具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One, your own great compassion shelters all, immeasurable karma and merit gathered together; through the four abodes of merit, you have attained the Buddha's own immeasurable dharma. World-Honored One, what you yourself have praised — these four supreme abodes of merit — I now, in turn, use this very praise to extol the Thus Come One.

"Complete with the thirty-two marks, each mark possessing the merit of a hundred kinds of goodness, and the eighty sublime minor marks — who, throughout the three realms, could possibly possess such a thing? The merit possessed by every sentient being throughout a great trichiliocosm, its own fruit and recompense multiplied a hundredfold, still only equals the merit possessed by a single one of these marks. This very merit, together with its own fruit and recompense, multiplied a further hundredfold, becomes a single one of the white-tuft mark. Each and every one of these thirty such marks, their own merit and fruit and recompense, multiplied a further thousandfold, becomes a single one of the uṣṇīṣa mark. World-Honored One, your every merit can never be measured, just as a person using a mere ruler could never exhaust the very measure of empty space.

"From the very first giving rise to this great mind, for the sake of liberating sentient beings, holding this resolute mind across immeasurable kalpas — it is for this very reason that buddhahood was accomplished. Diligently applying effort, wishing to fulfill this great vow completely, across immeasurable numbers of kalpas, practicing every difficult and painful austerity. Just as every past buddha has spoken of these four abodes of merit, accomplished only after immeasurable kalpas, you have now come to abide securely within them. Originally, for the sake of guarding this very true reality, you relinquished your own body and every intimate affection, every treasure and every worldly pleasure — it is for this very reason that you have attained this completeness.

"Across immeasurable numbers of kalpas, whatever dharma was seen, heard, sensed, or known, you always first reflected upon it carefully and skillfully, and only afterward expounded it for others. Should there be something not yet seen, or something concerning which doubt remains, you are nonetheless able to speak of it truly and accurately, and the benefit of this is beyond all measure. Never speaking of another's own hidden matters, never showing contempt or mockery or contrary resistance, this very mindfulness constantly abiding within tranquil wisdom, according with and transforming others so that they attain peace and security. The very foremost, genuine, sublime truth — nirvāṇa itself is truly the supreme — every other thing is illusory and false; World-Honored One, your own merit is entirely complete."

A note on this escalating arithmetic

The multiplier chain here — a great trichiliocosm's own total merit, multiplied a hundredfold, equals a single major mark; that same figure multiplied a further hundredfold produces the white tuft between the brows; and each of thirty such marks, multiplied a thousandfold again, produces the uṣṇīṣa alone — continues this treatise's own recurring rhetorical technique of escalating, near-vertiginous arithmetic, here applied to merit itself rather than supernormal power. The immeasurability these numbers gesture toward is made explicit in the closing line of this passage: attempting to measure the Buddha's own merit with any finite unit is like measuring empty space with a ruler, a category error rather than merely a very large sum.

The Bodhisattva's Universal Giving, Catalogued in Verse

Chinese Source

飲食臥具等，堂閣妙樓觀，名好象馬車，端嚴諸婇女；金銀珍寶等，聚落諸城邑，國土及榮位，并以四天下；愛子所親婦，支節及頭目，割肉出骨髓，及以舉身施。

憐愍諸眾生，悉施無所惜，為求出生死，不以求自樂。虛空諸星宿，地上所有沙，世尊菩薩時，布施數過是。

終不以非法，求財而布施。無有不知施。無侵惱人施。不貪惜好物，而以惡者施。無諂曲心施。無惜而強施。無恚無疑心、無邪無輕笑、無厭無不信、䫌面等布施。無有分別心，此應彼不應；但以悲心故，平等而行施。不輕於眾生，以為非福田；見聖心恭敬；破戒者憐愍。不自高其身、卑下於他人。亦不為稱讚。不求報等施。無悔無憂愁、無惡賤心施。無待急恨心。無法應當施。無不敬心施。無棄著地施。無求惱者施。無垢競勝施。無戲弄求者。無不自手施。不輕於少物，以多自高施。不以聲聞乘、辟支佛乘施。不限一世施。無有非時施。

世尊無數劫，行諸希有施，皆為無上道，不為求自樂。於諸佛法中，出家行遠離，修習諸佛法，為諸人天說。說如是施法，於諸施中上，猶如日光明，星月中殊勝。如是勝捨處，超越諸天人，猶亦如世尊，一切世間上。是故能具足，如是勝捨處，名聞無量劫，流布無窮已。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Food and drink, bedding and every article of livelihood; halls and towers, sublime pavilions and lookouts; renowned and fine elephants, horses, and carriages; dignified and beautiful court attendants; gold, silver, and every precious jewel; settlements and every walled city; a kingdom and every position of honor, together with all four continents; a beloved son, an intimate wife, one's own limbs and joints, one's own head and eyes, cutting one's own flesh and drawing out one's own marrow, and giving one's entire body besides.

"Feeling pity for every sentient being, giving all of this away entirely, holding nothing back, seeking only to escape birth and death, never seeking one's own private pleasure. The very stars filling empty space, every grain of sand covering the earth — the World-Honored One, in his own bodhisattva lifetimes, gave in a number surpassing even these.

"Never, through any wrongful means, seeking wealth in order to give it away. Never giving without full knowledge of what one gives. Never giving in a way that encroaches upon or troubles another. Never craving and clinging to a fine object while giving away only what is inferior. Never giving with a flattering, crooked mind. Never giving with reluctance, forced against one's own will. Giving free of anger, free of doubt, free of wrong intent, free of contemptuous mockery, free of weariness, free of distrust, and free of any change in one's own countenance. Holding no discriminating mind whatsoever, thinking 'this one deserves it, that one does not' — giving solely through a mind of compassion, practicing this giving equally toward all. Never holding sentient beings in contempt, as though they were not a genuine field of merit; beholding a noble one with a reverent mind; feeling pity toward one who has broken the precepts. Never exalting oneself, nor demeaning another. Never giving for the sake of praise. Never giving while seeking any recompense in return. Giving free of regret, free of sorrow, free of a mind of contempt or disdain. Free of any anxious or resentful mind while waiting to give. Never withholding any dharma that ought to be given. Never giving without a reverent mind. Never giving carelessly, as though discarding an object upon the ground. Never giving in a way that troubles the one who requests it. Never giving with a defiled mind that seeks to outdo another. Never mocking or toying with one who requests something. Never giving without using one's own hand directly. Never showing contempt toward a small object, while exalting oneself for giving something greater. Never giving merely for the sake of the śrāvaka vehicle or the pratyekabuddha vehicle. Never limiting one's own giving to a single lifetime. Never giving at an improper time.

"The World-Honored One, across countless kalpas, practiced every rare and marvelous form of giving, all of it for the sake of the unsurpassed path, never for the sake of seeking his own pleasure. Within the Buddhadharma of every buddha, going forth from home and practicing dwelling apart, cultivating and studying the Buddhadharma, expounding it for every human and every deva. Expounding this very dharma of giving, the very foremost among every form of giving, just as sunlight itself is the very foremost among the light of the stars and the moon. This very supreme place of relinquishment surpasses every deva and every human, just as the World-Honored One himself is supreme above every world. It is for this very reason that, having become complete in this supreme place of relinquishment, his own renown spreads across immeasurable kalpas, proclaimed without end."

A note on this catalogue's own recapitulation

This extended verse inventory of every form of giving — external possessions escalating to one's own body, then thirty-odd distinct qualities the giving itself must never lack — condenses and reprises the extensive prose treatment of pure giving this treatise gave across Fascicles 4 through 8, now recast as praise rather than instruction. The shift in register matters: where the earlier fascicles explained why each quality mattered, this hymn simply names them in sequence as accomplished facts of the Buddha's own past-life conduct, treating the entire earlier teaching on giving as itself a portrait of what the object of this recitation's devotion actually did.

The Precepts and Meditative Attainments

Chinese Source

世尊無量劫，護持清淨戒，開諸禪定門，為得深寂處。先離於五相，後行八解脫，入淨三三昧，亦住三解脫。世尊善分別，六十五種禪，無有一禪定，先來不生者。於此諸定中，亦不受其味。世尊因諸定，得三種神通，以此度眾生，是故一切勝。

世尊無量劫，等心弘慈化。阿僧祇眾生，令住於梵世。能以巧方便，善說禪定故。世尊菩薩時，常於無量世，無貪煩惱纏，而往來世間。過去得值者，無量生天上。過去諸菩薩，所可行寂滅，世尊菩薩時，亦等無有異。是故於寂滅，勝處悉充滿。

世尊菩薩時，所有諸智慧，以慧求菩提，今成是慧報。一切所資食，如人依地生。世尊於世世，捨十闇惡道，常行十善道，斯由慧氣分。捨五欲五蓋，得種種禪定，無量劫數世，不從他人受，善哉大聖尊，悉是慧勢力。

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One, across immeasurable kalpas, guarding and upholding the pure precepts, opening the very gateway of every meditative absorption, for the sake of attaining a place of profound tranquility. First departing from the five obstacles, and afterward practicing the eight liberations, entering the pure threefold samādhi, and further abiding within the threefold liberation. World-Honored One, skillfully distinguishing sixty-five kinds of meditative absorption, there exists not a single such absorption you have not already given rise to. And yet, within every one of these absorptions, you never once grasp at their own flavor. World-Honored One, through these very absorptions, you attained the threefold spiritual power, and, through this, liberated sentient beings — it is for this reason that you are supreme in every respect.

"World-Honored One, across immeasurable kalpas, with an equal mind, extending vast loving-kindness to transform all. Causing asaṃkhyeya sentient beings to abide within the Brahmā world. Being able, through skillful means, to expound meditative absorption well. World-Honored One, in your own bodhisattva lifetimes, constantly, across immeasurable ages, free of the entanglement of craving and every affliction, coming and going within this world. Those who encountered you in ages past were reborn, immeasurably, among the devas. Every past bodhisattva's own practice of tranquil cessation, in your own bodhisattva lifetimes, was likewise equally so, without any difference whatsoever. It is for this reason that, within this very tranquil cessation, every supreme place is entirely filled.

"World-Honored One, in your own bodhisattva lifetimes, whatever wisdom you possessed, through this wisdom you sought bodhi, and now this very wisdom has become its own fruit and recompense. Every provision and sustenance is like a person relying upon the earth to arise. World-Honored One, across every lifetime, relinquishing the ten dark, evil paths, constantly practicing the ten wholesome paths — this comes from the very portion of wisdom you possess. Relinquishing the five desires and the five hindrances, attaining meditative absorption of every kind, across immeasurable numbers of kalpas, never receiving this from any other — how excellent, O great and noble sovereign, that all of this is the very power of wisdom itself."

A note on wisdom as the root

The final stanza's own repeated refrain — "this comes from the very portion of wisdom," "this is the very power of wisdom itself" — begins a deliberate reframing that the following section extends much further: the giving, precepts, and meditative attainment just praised at length are here credited back to wisdom as their own underlying cause, setting up the closing hymn's own extended argument that wisdom is not simply one virtue among the six pāramitās but the very condition that makes every other virtue genuinely effective.

Wisdom as the Root of Every Virtue — Closing Hymn

Chinese Source

眾生因世尊，無量生六天，亦令至梵世，斯皆由慧力。世尊於生死，苦樂所迷悶，不失菩提心，斯皆是慧力。世尊於生死，不樂而常在，樂涅槃不取，斯皆是慧力。安坐道場時，降魔及軍眾，度脫諸群生，斯皆是慧力。本求菩提時，集無量助法，聞者常迷悶，何況能受行，世尊能堪忍，斯皆是慧力。經書諸技術，世世生自知，亦能兼教人，斯皆是慧力。

親近無量佛，悉飲甘露教，種種諮請問，亦隨而分別。經法智慧中，未曾有悋惜，乃至僕僮奴，亦諮受善語，世尊以是故，慧勝處流布。

世尊於前世，求是菩提時，於一切眾生，行大慈悲心。以第一智慧，常出大勢力，悉作無量種，希有諸難事。一切諸世間，盡共無量劫，說之不可盡，亦非算數及，如是等諸事，超越於人天，一切世間中，奇特無有比。大業所獲果，具足一切智，能破生死王，安住法王處。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Sentient beings, through the World-Honored One, were reborn, immeasurably, among the six heavens, and further caused to reach the Brahmā world — this too comes entirely from the very power of wisdom. World-Honored One, within birth and death, though confounded by suffering and joy, you never lost your own bodhicitta — this too is entirely the power of wisdom. World-Honored One, within birth and death, finding no delight in it, and yet constantly remaining within it, delighting in nirvāṇa and yet never grasping at it — this too is entirely the power of wisdom. Seated at the very seat of awakening, subduing māra and his own army, liberating every multitude of beings — this too is entirely the power of wisdom. Originally, at the very moment of seeking bodhi, gathering immeasurable assisting dharmas, dharmas that confound even the mere listener, let alone one who must practice them — the World-Honored One was able to bear this fully — this too is entirely the power of wisdom. Assisting the Samādhi of Buddha-Recollection Chapter

"The bodhisattva ought, through these forty unshared dharmas, to be mindful of every buddha's own Dharma body, since a buddha is not merely a body of physical form."

This very verse, in due sequence, briefly explains the meaning found across the six chapters treating the forty unshared dharmas — it is for this reason that the practitioner first becomes mindful of the Buddha's own physical body, and only afterward becomes mindful of the Dharma body. And why is this so? The newly resolved bodhisattva ought to be mindful of the Buddha through the thirty-two marks and the eighty minor marks, exactly as already explained.

As one's own progress deepens further, attaining a middling power, one ought to be mindful of the Buddha through the Dharma body.

As one's own mind deepens further still, attaining the very highest power, one ought to be mindful of the Buddha through his true mark, without any craving attachment whatsoever.

"Never clinging to attachment toward the physical body, and never clinging to the Dharma body either; skillfully knowing every dharma, forever tranquil, just like empty space itself."

This bodhisattva, having attained this very highest power, no longer holds any deep craving attachment toward the Buddha through either the physical body or the Dharma body. And why is this so? Because he holds faith and delight in the dharma of emptiness, knowing that every dharma is just like empty space itself. As for "empty space," this refers to being free of any obstruction whatsoever. As for "the causes and conditions of obstruction," these are every such mountain — Mount Sumeru, Yugandhara and the other nine jeweled mountains, the iron ring-mountain, the black mountains, the stone mountains, and the like — obstructions such as these, immeasurable in their own causes and conditions. It is for this reason that the newly resolved bodhisattva ought to be mindful of the Buddha through the ten sublime titles. As it is said: "The newly resolved bodhisattva, through the ten sublime titles, is mindful of the Buddha without any loss or error — just as a reflection appears within a mirror."

The ten sublime titles are: Thus Come One, Worthy of Offerings, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, Complete in Knowledge and Conduct, Well-Gone, Knower of the World, Unsurpassed, Tamer of Persons, Teacher of Gods and Humans, Buddha, and World-Honored One.

As for "without any loss or error": whatever matter is contemplated is empty, just as empty space itself, and within this very dharma, nothing whatsoever is lost. And why is this so? Because every dharma, from the very outset, is unborn, tranquil cessation itself — every dharma whatsoever is likewise so. This person, taking this very name and title as their own object, increases this meditative practice, and thereby becomes able to take up its own mark as well. At that very moment, this person attains a genuine mark within this very meditative practice, namely, the body attaining an extraordinary and blissful joy — one should know that this is the very attainment of the pratyutpanna samādhi, and, this samādhi being accomplished, one thereby attains the vision of every buddha.

As for "just as a reflection appears within a mirror": should the bodhisattva accomplish this very samādhi, it is just as a bright, clean mirror reveals one's own reflected face, or just as clear, still water reveals one's own bodily form.

At the very first moment, according with whichever buddha one was previously mindful of, one beholds this very buddha's own form; having beheld this very form, should one afterward wish to behold every buddha in every other world, according with whatever one is mindful of, one is thereby able to behold every buddha, without any obstruction whatsoever. It is for this reason that this person: "though not yet possessing any spiritual power to fly through the air and travel there directly, is nonetheless able to behold every buddha, and to hear the Dharma without any obstruction whatsoever."

This newly resolved bodhisattva, toward Mount Sumeru and every such mountain, finds none of them able to serve as any obstruction whatsoever, and, though not yet having attained any spiritual power, the heavenly eye, or the heavenly ear, and not yet able to fly from this land to that land, through the very power of this samādhi, remains within this very land and yet attains the vision of every buddha, every world-honored one, in every other world, hearing whatever Dharma is expounded there; and, through constantly cultivating and practicing this very samādhi, attains the vision of the true and genuine buddhas throughout the ten directions.

A note on the mirror and still water

The progression traced here — physical body, then Dharma body, then the ten titles as a middle rung, then the signless real mark beyond both — gives this treatise's own account of buddha-recollection a structured ladder rather than a single static practice, matching capacity to stage exactly as Fascicle 5's Chapter of Easy Practice matched devotional calling-upon-names to practitioners for whom more demanding contemplation remained out of reach. The mirror and still-water similes make a precise claim about what the pratyutpanna samādhi actually delivers: not travel to some other place, but the direct, unmediated appearance of what is contemplated, exactly as a reflection requires no journey to the object it reflects.

A note on this sixtyfold list

This sixty-item enumeration is the longest single numbered list this treatise has offered for a specific practitioner's own conduct, and its own internal organization moves through recognizable clusters — nine consecutive items on the precepts alone (first through ninth), a run on gratitude and reverence toward teachers (thirtieth through thirty-sixth), a cluster of ascetic observances recognizable from the earlier twelve dhūtaṅga practices (forty-sixth through forty-ninth), and a closing sequence of the four immeasurables paired with their own opposing faults (fifty-third through fifty-eighth). Read as a whole, the list functions less as sixty independent requirements than as an exhaustive description of an entire monastic character remade around this single devotional practice.

A note on sympathetic rejoicing exceeding giving

The comparison this passage builds toward is genuinely startling on first read: an act as vast as filling a distance no one but the Buddha, Śāriputra, or an irreversible bodhisattva could even measure with pure gold, given away entirely, is still exceeded — by a margin the text says cannot even be reckoned — by the mere act of sympathetic rejoicing (anumodanā) in this samādhi upon merely hearing of it. This is not a claim that giving is worthless, but a specific statement about what sympathetic rejoicing accomplishes that material giving alone cannot: aligning one's own mind directly with the buddhas' own rejoicing across all three times, a mental act this treatise elsewhere treats as capable of generating merit disproportionate to its apparent effort.

A note on this scholastic classification

The technical Abhidharma-style analysis here — sorting the samādhi by dhyāna level, by association with initial and sustained thought, by realm-binding, by whether it involves outflows — situates this devotional practice within the same rigorous doctrinal apparatus the tradition applies to every other meditative state, refusing to treat buddha-recollection as somehow exempt from ordinary analysis simply because its object is a buddha's own vision. The closing image of gold refined by fire until it becomes fit for use gives this classification its own practical payoff: cultivating this samādhi is not an end in itself but a process that tempers roots of goodness into a form actually usable for the extended work of teaching and transforming others.

Simile Chapter

"This bodhisattva ought to hear of the mark, the attainment, the cultivation, and the fruit of each ground; for the sake of attaining every portion of these grounds, one diligently applies effort."

As for "mark": this refers to its own outward appearance, through which it can be recognized.

As for "attainment": this refers to accomplishment. Through this very dharma, one is said to have accomplished this dharma.

As for "cultivation": this refers to cultivation that attains, and cultivation that practices.

As for "constantly being mindful of the fruit": from a cause, some matter comes into being — this is called its own fruit.

This bodhisattva, wishing to attain the practice of the ten grounds, ought skillfully to hear of the mark, the attainment, the cultivation, and the fruit of each.

As for "hearing": this refers to hearing from every buddha and bodhisattva, and from one who surpasses oneself.

As for "attaining every portion of these grounds": for the sake of attaining this very portion of a given ground, one diligently applies effort.

Within this very matter, as for the mark of the first ground, as already explained above: "The bodhisattva, dwelling within the first ground, is able to bear a great deal, taking no delight in contention or dispute, their own mind holding much delight. Constantly delighting in purity, a mind of compassion showing pity toward sentient beings, holding no mind of anger whatsoever, practicing these very seven matters extensively."

It is for this reason that forbearance, freedom from contention, delight, joy, purity, a mind of compassion, and freedom from anger — these seven dharmas — are the very mark of the first ground.

Accomplishing these very seven dharmas of forbearance and the rest is what is called "attainment."

Furthermore, the very mark of these seven dharmas of forbearance and the rest is itself the attainment of the first ground. As the verse says: "Should one plant deep roots of goodness, practicing every conduct well, well gathering every provision of life, well making offering to every buddha; protected by a good and virtuous friend, complete with a deep mind, a mind of compassion mindful of sentient beings, faith and understanding of the unsurpassed dharma —

"Complete with these very eight dharmas, one ought then to give rise to this very vow oneself: 'I have already attained my own liberation — I shall now further liberate sentient beings.'

"For the sake of attaining the ten powers, entering the very group of those certain of buddhahood; one is thereby born into the Thus Come One's own family, free of every fault and error.

"At once transforming the worldly path into the very supreme path that transcends this world; through this, one attains the first ground, this ground named the Ground of Joy."

It is for this reason that one should know: the very resolute mind attained for the sake of bodhi is what is called the attainment of the first ground.

A note on this chapter's own title

This chapter's own title, "Simile Chapter," sits somewhat loosely against its actual content here at the outset, which is a systematic fourfold framework — mark, attainment, cultivation, fruit — applied methodically to the first ground rather than an extended simile in the ordinary sense; the treatise appears to be signaling that similes will illustrate this framework as the chapter continues through the remaining grounds in later fascicles. The eight-dharma verse marking genuine entry into the first ground closes with the same vow structure this treatise has returned to since its earliest fascicles: liberation for oneself is never treated as sufficient on its own, but immediately generates the further vow to liberate others.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "cultivation": from "first giving rise to this aspiration" up through "accomplishing the samādhi of every buddha appearing directly present" — within this very interval, the merit of every ground is explained in full. Being able to give rise to this merit, and, having given rise to it, cultivating, gathering, and increasing it — this is what is called the cultivation of the first ground.

As for "fruit": it has already been explained at length, at various points, that one "attains a certain amount of merit, which is never dedicated toward the ground of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha." I shall now further explain that the bodhisattva, having attained the first ground, is able to attain several hundred kinds of concentration belonging to the bodhisattva, and the like.

As for "the portions of the first ground": every dharma that combines together to form the first ground is what is called its own portions. Just as yeast, rice, and the like, combined together, are able to become wine, and are thereby called the very cause and condition of wine — every dharma able to form the first ground is what is called the portion of the first ground.

Namely: "The power of faith growing ever more supreme, accomplishing a mind of great compassion; showing loving-kindness and pity toward every kind of sentient being, cultivating wholesomeness with a mind never weary; delighting joyfully in the sublime Dharma, constantly drawing near to a good and virtuous friend; conscience and shame, together with reverence, gently and harmoniously settling one's own mind; delighting in contemplating the Dharma without attachment, with a single mind seeking vast learning; never craving gain or support, departing from deceit, fraud, flattery, and falsehood; never defiling any buddha's own family, never breaking the precepts or deceiving the Buddha; deeply delighting in omniscience, unmoving as a great mountain; constantly delighting in cultivating and practicing this ever-more-supreme, sublime Dharma; delighting in what transcends this world, taking no delight in worldly dharma; thereby cultivating the very Ground of Joy, difficult to cultivate and yet able to be cultivated nonetheless; it is for this reason that, with a constantly single mind, one diligently practices these very dharmas; the bodhisattva, able to accomplish this very supreme, sublime Dharma, thereby abides securely within the bodhisattva's own first ground."

Question: Of what use is it for the bodhisattva to hear of this very mark of the first ground, and the rest?

Answer: This bodhisattva, within this very dharma of the mark of the first ground and the rest, ought to skillfully know the appropriate skillful means — it is for this reason that they ought to hear of it.

Question: Ought the bodhisattva to skillfully know the appropriate skillful means only within this very dharma, or ought they further skillfully know the appropriate skillful means within every other dharma as well?

Answer: They ought to skillfully know the appropriate skillful means within these very dharmas, and further ought to skillfully know it within every other dharma as well.

Question: If this is so, please explain it briefly.

Answer: "There exists a dharma able to assist a given ground; there exists a dharma that opposes a given ground; there exists a dharma able to generate a given ground; there exists a dharma able to destroy a given ground; there exist the mark and fruit belonging to every ground; there exists the very attainment within every ground; there exists the portion of purification belonging to every ground; there exists passage from one ground to the next; there exists the very increase attained while abiding within a given ground; there exists none able to cause one to retrogress; there exists passage from the bodhisattva's own pure ground all the way to the immeasurable ground of buddhahood — within every one of these very matters, one ought skillfully to know the appropriate skillful means, inquiring respectfully of every good and virtuous person, and thereby removing and breaking through one's own arrogance."

A note on yeast, rice, and wine

The image of yeast and rice combining to produce wine gives a concrete, everyday model for how this treatise understands "portions" of a bodhisattva ground: not a single decisive cause but multiple distinct ingredients, none sufficient alone, that together produce something none of them individually resembles — the eleven-item verse that follows names exactly this kind of combination, faith alongside compassion alongside conscience alongside vast learning, none of which by itself would constitute the Ground of Joy. The closing question-and-answer exchange then opens the framework the next section develops in full: nine distinct categories of "skillful means" governing how a bodhisattva moves through the grounds at all.

As for "the dharma that assists the first ground": namely, faith, the precepts, learning, relinquishment, diligent effort, mindfulness, wisdom, and the like — these and every other dharma that accords with the first ground are what is called the assisting dharma.

Passing from the first ground to the second, one attains freedom from flattery and crookedness, and the other ten minds.

Passing from the second ground to the third, one attains faith and delight, and the other ten minds.

Through attaining various minds and various dharmas such as these, one is able to pass from one ground to the next.

Within the third ground, vast learning predominates, and further, giving, upholding the precepts, faith, and the rest attain an ever-greater power.

Every remaining ground is likewise so.

A note on this nine-part framework

The nine categories enumerated here — what assists a ground, what opposes it, what destroys it, what generates it, its mark and fruit, its attainment, its purification, passage between grounds, and the immovable security once a ground is genuinely secured — together form a complete taxonomy for understanding progress through the entire bodhisattva path, applied here specifically to the first ground but announced as the template for every ground that follows. The chapter's own closing claim is a striking one to end on: mere knowledge of this framework, skillfully applied, already secures permanent non-retrogression from the first ground, even before buddhahood itself is reached — understanding the structure of the path is treated here as itself a form of genuine attainment, not merely a map of attainment yet to come.
