The Merit of the Fourfold Practice, and Pure Giving

How, exactly, is confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication practiced day and night, and what is its merit measured against? Nāgārjuna answers with sūtra after sūtra weighing this merit against oceans of world-systems' worth of grasping giving, then confronts head-on whether confession actually dissolves karma at all, before opening an extended catalogue of what makes an act of giving genuinely pure.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1521, 十住毘婆沙論, attributed to Nāgārjuna, tr. Kumārajīva 17 fascicles, 35 chapters

Six Times a Day and Night

Chinese Source

分別功德品第十一

問曰：懺悔、勸請、隨喜、迴向應云何作？於晝夜中幾時行？

答曰：以右膝著地、　偏袒於右肩、合掌恭敬心、　晝夜各三時。

以恭敬相故，右膝著地、偏袒右肩、合掌，是事應初夜一時禮一切佛。

懺悔、勸請、隨喜、迴向，中夜、後夜皆亦如是。於日初分、日中分、日後分亦如是。一日一夜合為六時，一心念諸佛，如現在前。

問曰：作是行已，得何果報？

答曰：若於一時行，　福德有形者，恒河沙世界，　乃自不容受。

若於一時中，行此事者所得福德，若有形者，恒河沙等無量無邊不可思議三千大千世界所不容受。

如《三支經》〈除罪業品〉中說：「佛告舍利弗：若善男子、善女人，以滿恒河沙等三千大千世界七寶布施諸佛；若復有人勸請諸佛轉法輪，此福為勝。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Distinguishing Merit Chapter

Question: How, exactly, ought confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication be practiced? At how many times within a day and a night ought this be carried out?

Answer: "With the right knee touching the ground, the right shoulder bared, palms joined, and a mind of reverence — three times each within the day, and three times each within the night."

With this very mark of reverence — the right knee touching the ground, the right shoulder bared, palms joined — this practice ought to be carried out once during the first watch of the night, bowing to every buddha.

Confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication are likewise practiced once during the middle watch of the night, and once during the final watch. And likewise once during the first portion of the day, once during the midday portion, and once during the final portion of the day. Across a single day and a single night, this makes six occasions in all, mindful of every buddha with a single mind, as though they stood directly before one.

Question: Having carried out this practice, what fruit and recompense does one obtain?

Answer: "Should this be practiced even once, and this merit possess a physical form, worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges could not contain it."

Should this practice be carried out even a single time, the merit thereby obtained, were it to possess a physical form, could not be contained even by three-thousandfold great thousand-world systems as numerous, immeasurable, boundless, and inconceivable as the sands of the Ganges.

As the Chapter on Removing the Karma of Offenses within the Triskandhaka Sūtra states: "The Buddha told Śāriputra: 'Should a good son or good daughter fill three-thousandfold great thousand-world systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges with the seven treasures and give them to the buddhas — should there then be a further person who exhorts the buddhas to turn the wheel of the Dharma, this latter merit is superior.'"

A note on this schedule

Six sessions across a single day and night — three by day, three by night — matches the schedule this site's own translation of the Amitābha Sūtra and its commentaries already describes as the standard rhythm of formal Pure Land recitation practice; this passage is among that schedule's own earliest textual sources. The physical posture specified — right knee to the ground, right shoulder bared — is the same gesture of reverence prescribed throughout Vinaya literature for formal requests made of a teacher or preceptor, applied here to the buddhas of the ten directions themselves.

Merit Weighed Against Oceans of Giving

Chinese Source

又佛於《般若波羅蜜》〈隨喜迴向品〉中說：「善哉！善哉！須菩提！汝能作佛事，與諸菩薩說迴向法。若菩薩作是念：『如諸佛知見是善根福德本末體相何因緣故有？我亦如是隨佛所知所見迴向。』是人得福多。

「譬如恒河沙等三千大千世界中眾生皆成就十善道；菩薩迴向福德最上、最妙、最勝無比、無等、無等等。

「須菩提！置是恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生，成就十善道；若恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生皆得四禪，其福比此亦復最上、最妙、最勝。四無量心、四無色定、五神通、得須陀洹果、斯陀含果、阿那含果、阿羅漢果、辟支佛道亦如是。如法迴向福德最上、最妙、最勝。

「須菩提！置此恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生皆作辟支佛；若有恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生，皆發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。復有恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生，其一菩薩以取相心，供養是諸眾生衣服、飲食、臥具、醫藥；於恒河沙等劫，以一切樂具供養、恭敬、尊重、讚歎，一一菩薩皆亦如是。須菩提！於意云何？是諸菩薩以此因緣，得福多不？」

「甚多，世尊！如是福德算數譬喻所不能及。若是福德有形，恒河沙等世界所不能受。」

佛告須菩提：「善哉！善哉！須菩提！是菩薩為般若波羅蜜守護，以善根隨法性迴向所得福德，先諸菩薩取相布施福德百分不及一，千分、萬分、百千萬億分，乃至算數譬喻所不能及。何以故？先諸菩薩取相分別布施，皆是有量有數。」

又《般若波羅蜜》〈迴向品〉中說：佛告淨居天子：「置此恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生皆發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，餘恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生，一一菩薩以取相心供養是諸眾生衣服、飲食、臥具、醫藥資生之物，隨意供養於恒河沙等劫。

「諸天子！若是恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，餘恒河沙等三千大千世界中眾生皆亦發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，其一菩薩供養是諸菩薩衣服、飲食、臥具、醫藥資生之物於恒河沙等劫，是布施取相分別。如是諸菩薩各於恒河沙等劫，供養是諸菩薩衣服、飲食、臥具、醫藥資生之物，隨意供養恭敬、尊重、讚歎，皆是取相布施。

「若菩薩為般若波羅蜜所護，過去、未來、現在諸佛戒品、定品、慧品、解脫品、解脫知見品及聲聞五品，及諸凡夫人於中種善根，已種、今種、當種，盡和合稱量，使無遺餘，最上、最妙、最勝、無等、無等等不可思議隨喜福德，迴向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。作是念：『我是福德能至佛道』，是福德於先取相福德百分不及一，千分、萬分、億分，乃至算數譬喻所不能及。何以故？是諸菩薩取相分別布施故。

「復有恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生，皆發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，身行善業、口行善業、意行善業。

「復有恒河沙等三千大千世界眾生，皆發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心；若人於恒河沙等劫惡口罵詈皆能忍受。

「於恒河沙等劫身心精進，除諸懈怠；攝心禪定無諸亂想，而皆取相，不如菩薩如法性迴向其福為勝。」

是故如汝先說「作如是事得何等利」者，得如是大福德聚。是故若人欲得如是無量無邊不可思議福德聚者，應行是懺悔、勸請、隨喜、迴向，不惜身命、利養、名聞，於晝夜中常應勤行。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, the Buddha, within the Chapter on Rejoicing and Dedication in the Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra, states: "Excellent, excellent, Subhūti! You are able to accomplish the very deeds of a buddha, expounding this dharma of dedication for the bodhisattvas. Should a bodhisattva reflect thus: 'Exactly as the buddhas themselves know and perceive this very root of goodness and merit — its origin, its end, its essence, its mark, from what cause and condition it arises — I too, in just this same way, dedicate it according to what the Buddha himself knows and sees,' such a person obtains abundant merit.

"It is just as though sentient beings throughout three-thousandfold great thousand-world systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges were all to accomplish the ten wholesome courses of action; the merit of a bodhisattva's own dedication is the very highest, most sublime, and most excellent, beyond compare, unequaled, and unequaled to any equal.

"Subhūti! Setting aside sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges accomplishing the ten wholesome courses of action — should sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges all attain the four dhyānas, this bodhisattva's own merit, compared even to this, remains the very highest, most sublime, and most excellent still. It is likewise so compared to the four immeasurables, the four formless absorptions, the five spiritual powers, and the attainment of the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, arhatship, and the path of the pratyekabuddha. Merit dedicated in accordance with the Dharma is the very highest, most sublime, and most excellent.

"Subhūti! Setting aside sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges all becoming pratyekabuddhas — should sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges all give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, and should there be, further, sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous again as the sands of the Ganges, with a single bodhisattva, grasping at marks in their own mind, making offerings of clothing, food, bedding, and medicine to every one of these sentient beings — across kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, making offerings with every implement of pleasure, revering, honoring, and praising them, and every single one of these bodhisattvas doing likewise — Subhūti, what do you think? Through these very causes and conditions, do these bodhisattvas obtain abundant merit or not?"

"Exceedingly abundant, World-Honored One! Merit such as this cannot be reckoned by any number or comparison. Were this merit to possess a physical form, worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges could not contain it."

The Buddha told Subhūti: "Excellent, excellent, Subhūti! The merit obtained by a bodhisattva who is protected by the perfection of wisdom, dedicating their own roots of goodness in accordance with the very nature of the Dharma, does not equal even one hundredth part of the merit of those earlier bodhisattvas who gave while grasping at marks — not one thousandth part, not one ten-thousandth part, not one part in a hundred thousand million, and cannot be reckoned by any number or comparison whatsoever. And why is this so? Because the giving of those earlier bodhisattvas, grasping at marks and discriminating as they gave, was, in every case, limited and countable."

And further, the Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra, within its own Chapter on Dedication, states: The Buddha told the deva-sons of the Pure Abodes: "Setting aside sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges all giving rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening — should there be, throughout world-systems as numerous again as the sands of the Ganges, a single bodhisattva, grasping at marks in their own mind, making offerings of clothing, food, bedding, medicine, and every article of livelihood to these sentient beings, offering according to their own will across kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges —

"Deva-sons! Should sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, and sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous again as the sands of the Ganges likewise give rise to this very mind, and a single bodhisattva makes offerings of clothing, food, bedding, medicine, and every article of livelihood to these very bodhisattvas across kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges — this giving is still one that grasps at marks and discriminates. Should every one of these bodhisattvas, each across kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, make offerings of clothing, food, bedding, medicine, and every article of livelihood to these very bodhisattvas, offering according to their own will, revering, honoring, and praising them — this too is, in every case, giving that grasps at marks.

"Should a bodhisattva, protected by the perfection of wisdom, gather together the roots of goodness planted, already planted, being planted now, and yet to be planted — by the buddhas of the past, the future, and the present within their own categories of precepts, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation, and within the śrāvakas' own five categories, and by every ordinary being as well — weighing them together without a single remainder omitted, rejoicing in this merit with the very highest, most sublime, most excellent, unequaled, and inconceivable rejoicing, and dedicating it toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, reflecting: 'This merit of mine is able to reach the path to buddhahood' — this merit does not equal even one hundredth part of the earlier merit gained through giving that grasped at marks — not one thousandth part, not one ten-thousandth part, not one part in a hundred million, and cannot be reckoned by any number or comparison whatsoever. And why is this so? Because these earlier bodhisattvas gave while grasping at marks and discriminating as they did so.

"There are, further, sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges who all give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, practicing wholesome deeds through body, through speech, and through mind.

"There are, further, sentient beings throughout world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges who all give rise to this very mind; and should a person, across kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, endure every manner of evil speech and abuse without fail —

"Should one, across kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, exert diligent effort of body and mind, eliminating every indolence, and gather the mind in meditative concentration free of every distracting thought — even this, so long as it still grasps at marks, does not equal the merit of a bodhisattva who dedicates merit in accordance with the very nature of the Dharma."

It is for this reason that, as you have asked earlier what benefit is obtained by carrying out this very practice, one obtains a mass of merit as great as this. It is for this reason that, should a person wish to obtain a mass of merit immeasurable, boundless, and inconceivable such as this, they ought to practice this very confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication, begrudging neither body nor life, neither gain nor renown, constantly and diligently practicing it throughout the day and the night.

A note on this comparison's own structure

Each successive comparison in this passage does not simply add another impressive number to the pile — it escalates the very object being outdone: first sentient beings practicing the ten wholesome courses, then meditative attainments, then arhatship and pratyekabuddhahood, then bodhisattvas giving vast material gifts across inconceivable kalpas, then bodhisattvas giving to other bodhisattvas specifically. At every single stage, dedication free of grasping at any mark still surpasses it, and by the same margin each time — not one part in a hundred, a thousand, or a hundred million. The point is not that grasping giving is worthless, but that it belongs to an entirely different order of magnitude from giving joined to emptiness, no matter how impressive its own object becomes.

Why Confession's Own Merit Is Greatest

Chinese Source

問曰：汝但說勸請、隨喜、迴向中福德，何故不說懺悔中福德耶？

答曰：於諸福德中，懺悔福德最大，除業障罪故，得善行菩薩道，行勸請、隨喜、迴向，與空、無相、無願和合無異。

復次，懺悔如如意珠，隨願皆得。如佛說：「若人欲於婆羅門大姓中生、剎利大姓中生、居士大家中生，應如是懺悔罪業，無所覆藏，後不更作。

「若有人欲生四天王天上、忉利天上、夜摩天上、兜率陀天上、化樂天上、他化自在天上，亦應如是懺悔罪業，無所覆藏後不更作。

「若人欲生梵世，乃至非想非非想處，是人亦應如是懺悔罪業，無所覆藏後不更作。

「若人欲得須陀洹果、斯陀含果、阿那含果、阿羅漢果，亦應如是懺悔罪業。

「若人欲得三明六神通，聲聞道中自在力，盡聲聞功德彼岸，亦應如是懺悔罪業。

「若人欲得辟支佛道，亦應如是懺悔罪業。

「若人欲得一切智慧、不可思議智慧、無礙智慧、無上智慧，亦應如是懺悔罪業，無所覆藏，後不更作。」

是故當知懺悔有大果報。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: You have explained the merit within exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication — why have you not explained the merit within confession?

Answer: Among these forms of merit, the merit of confession is the very greatest, for it removes the very obstruction of karma and offense, thereby enabling one to skillfully practice the bodhisattva path; and practicing exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication is itself no different from being joined together with emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness.

Furthermore, confession is like a wish-fulfilling jewel, granting whatever is wished for. As the Buddha has said: "Should a person wish to be born into a great brahmin lineage, a great kṣatriya lineage, or a great householder's family, they ought, in just this way, to confess their own karmic offense, concealing nothing, never to commit it again afterward.

"Should a person wish to be reborn among the devas of the Four Heavenly Kings, of Trāyastriṃśa, of Yāma, of Tuṣita, of Nirmāṇarati, or of Paranirmita-vaśavartin, they likewise ought, in just this way, to confess their own karmic offense, concealing nothing, never to commit it again afterward.

"Should a person wish to be reborn within the Brahmā world, up through the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception, this person likewise ought, in just this way, to confess their own karmic offense, concealing nothing, never to commit it again afterward.

"Should a person wish to attain the fruit of stream-entry, of once-returning, of non-returning, or of arhatship, they likewise ought, in just this way, to confess their own karmic offense.

"Should a person wish to attain the three insights and the six spiritual powers, mastery within the śrāvaka's own path, and the farther shore of every śrāvaka merit, they likewise ought, in just this way, to confess their own karmic offense.

"Should a person wish to attain the path of the pratyekabuddha, they likewise ought, in just this way, to confess their own karmic offense.

"Should a person wish to attain omniscient wisdom, inconceivable wisdom, unobstructed wisdom, and unsurpassed wisdom, they likewise ought, in just this way, to confess their own karmic offense, concealing nothing, never to commit it again afterward."

It is for this reason that one should know that confession carries a great fruit and recompense.

A note on this ranking

Confession's own primacy here is explained functionally rather than through further scriptural weighing: it is not that confession itself outweighs exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication on some further comparative scale, but that it is the very precondition that makes the other three practices possible in genuine, unobstructed form at all — removing the karmic obstruction that would otherwise interfere with practicing them well. The wish-fulfilling jewel simile that follows, applicable to every possible rebirth from a fine human family up through the very attainment of a buddha's own omniscience, sets up the sharper question the chapter turns to next: if confession is this powerful, does it actually erase karma, or only soften it?

The Objection: Does Karma Truly Vanish?

Chinese Source

問曰：汝言「懺悔除業障罪」。

餘經中說：「佛告阿難，故作業必當受報」又《阿毘曇》中說：「諸業因緣不空，果報不失不滅。」又經說：「眾生皆屬業，皆從業有，依止於業。眾生隨業各自受報，若現報、若生報、若後報。」又《業報經》中閻羅王為眾生說言：「咄！眾生！汝此罪非父母作、天作、沙門、婆羅門作，汝自作自應受報。」

又賢聖偈中說：「實法如金剛，　業力將無勝，今我已得道，　而受惡業報。」

又佛自說：「大海諸名山，　丘陵樹林木，地水火風等，　日月諸星宿；若至劫燒時，　皆盡無有餘；業於無量劫，　常在而不失。汝遇具相者，　一切智人師，先所造罪業，　已償其果報。今雖得值佛，　垢盡證聖果，以餘因緣故，　木刺猶在身。」

是故不應言懺悔除業罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: You have stated that confession removes the very obstruction of karma and offense.

Yet other sūtras state: "The Buddha told Ānanda: karma deliberately committed must certainly be undergone in retribution." And the Abhidharma states: "The causes and conditions of every karmic act are never empty; their fruit and recompense are never lost, never extinguished." And a sūtra states: "Every sentient being belongs to their own karma, arises entirely from their own karma, and relies upon their own karma; sentient beings, following their own karma, each undergo their own retribution, whether in this present life, in the next life, or in a life thereafter." And in the Sūtra on the Recompense of Karma, King Yama says to sentient beings: "Alas, sentient beings! This offense of yours was committed by neither your own parents, nor by the devas, nor by any ascetic or brahmin — you yourselves committed it, and you yourselves must undergo its retribution."

And a verse of the worthy and the noble states: "True dharma is like vajra, and the power of karma has no equal; even now that I have attained the path myself, I still undergo the retribution of my own evil karma."

And the Buddha himself has said: "The great ocean, every renowned mountain, every hill, forest, and tree, earth, water, fire, and wind, the sun, the moon, and every constellation — when the fire that consumes a kalpa arrives, all of these are exhausted, with nothing remaining; yet karma, across immeasurable kalpas, constantly persists, and is never lost. You have encountered one complete with every mark, the teacher of all who possess omniscience — yet even he, for offenses committed in the past, has already repaid their fruit and recompense. Though he has now come to meet the Buddha, every defilement exhausted, having realized the fruit of a noble one, still, through some remaining cause and condition, a splinter of wood yet remains lodged within his own body."

It is for this reason that one ought not to say that confession removes the very karma of an offense.

A note on this objection's own force

The objection's own closing example — that even the Buddha himself, defilements entirely exhausted, still bore a splinter of wood lodged in his body from some remaining karmic cause — is drawn from a well-known episode in Buddhist biographical literature, and lands with real weight precisely because it names the single figure whose own liberation is beyond any possible doubt. If even complete awakening does not erase the last trace of karma's own working, the objection asks, on what basis can an ordinary bodhisattva's mere confession be said to remove it?

The Answer: Lightened, Not Erased

Offenses such as these, through extensively practicing merit, holding a vast and expansive resolve, and gathering every quality of merit, do not cause a fall into an evil destiny.

It is for this reason that your earlier objection — that "should confession cause karmic offense to be entirely extinguished, there would then be no fruit or recompense remaining at all" — this statement does not hold.

Furthermore, should it be said that "offense can never be extinguished at all," then the Buddha's own statement within the Vinaya, that "confession removes offense," could not be believed. This is simply not so — it is for this reason that the obstruction of karma and offense ought to be confessed.

A note on this resolution

The salt-in-water simile resolves the objection without contradicting it: karma is never denied its own reality or its own inevitable ripening — the treatise agrees entirely with the objection's own premise that karmic causes and conditions are never empty. What confession changes is not whether the offense ripens, but the volume of merit it ripens within: the identical measure of salt tastes overwhelming in a cup and imperceptible in an ocean. Aṅgulimāla, Ajātaśatru, and the venomous serpent that becomes a pratyekabuddha are offered not as exceptions to the law of karma, but as demonstrations of exactly how large a "great pond" genuine confession and accumulated merit can actually build.

Distinguishing Giving Chapter

The bodhisattva, able to practice confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication in this way —

"the power of their own merit grows ever greater, and their mind, too, grows ever more gentle; they thereby attain faith in the merit of the buddhas, and in the great conduct of the bodhisattvas."

This bodhisattva, through confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication, has the power of their own merit grow ever greater, their mind tamed and made gentle; toward the immeasurable merit of the buddhas, pure and supreme, which ordinary beings cannot believe, they are able to accept it with faith; and toward the pure and great conduct of the great bodhisattvas, rare and difficult though it is, they are likewise able to accept it with faith.

Furthermore: "Toward suffering, afflicted sentient beings who lack this profound and pure Dharma, they give rise, in this regard, to grief and sorrow, and thereby give rise to a mind of profound compassion."

The bodhisattva, having already come to have faith in the immeasurable, profound, pure, and supreme merit of the buddhas and bodhisattvas, grieves and sorrows that sentient beings lack this very merit, and, on account of every manner of wrong view, undergo suffering and affliction of every kind — it is for this reason that they give rise to a profound mind of compassion.

"Mindful that these sentient beings have sunk into the mire of suffering and affliction, I shall rescue and pull them free, causing them to abide in a place of peace and security."

This bodhisattva, having attained this mind of compassion, reflects: "These sentient beings are constantly afflicted by the sickness of craving, anger, and delusion, undergoing every suffering and affliction in body and mind — I shall rescue and deliver them, causing them to depart from this deep mire of bodily and mental suffering, ultimately freeing them from the afflictions of birth, old age, sickness, and death, and causing them to abide within the peaceful and secure place of the joy of nirvāṇa."

It is for this reason that, toward these suffering, afflicted sentient beings, a profound mind of compassion is given rise to; and it is through this very mind of compassion, seeking to fulfill their own wishes and cause them to attain peace and joy, that this is called a mind of loving-kindness.

"Should the bodhisattva, in this way, deeply follow this mind of loving-kindness and compassion, they sever every craving and stinginess they possess, and diligently exert effort for the sake of giving."

"Bodhisattva" refers here to one who is mindful of seeking the path to buddhahood and liberating suffering, afflicted sentient beings. "Follow" means to follow in accordance with loving-kindness and compassion, following no other mind. "Deep loving-kindness" means that it extends universally to every sentient being, this mindfulness penetrating through to the very marrow of one's bones. "Whatever they possess" refers to everything, whether internal or external, whatever gold, silver, treasure, city, spouse, or child one possesses. "Craving" means desiring to obtain without ever being satiated. "Stinginess" means clinging attachment, unwilling to relinquish it to another — departing from these three evils is what is meant here.

In this way, the very gate of the pāramitā of giving is thereby opened; it is for this reason that one ought constantly, with a single mind, to diligently practice it, never allowing heedlessness to arise.

A note on this sequence

The chain of causation laid out here is worth tracing explicitly: confession, exhortation, rejoicing, and dedication strengthen merit and soften the mind; a softened, strengthened mind becomes capable of genuine faith in qualities of merit an ordinary mind would find unbelievable; and this faith, once established, becomes the ground from which compassion for beings who lack this very faith naturally arises. Giving itself, the subject this new chapter is named for, is presented not as a standalone virtue to be cultivated in isolation, but as the practical consequence of a mind already transformed by everything the previous two chapters have built.

A note on this correspondence system

The correspondence is consistently metaphorical rather than simply proportional in scale, suggesting the underlying principle at work: what one gives becomes, through the very act of giving it away, a kind of rehearsal or symbolic enactment of the specific spiritual capacity its counterpart represents.

A note on the bodhisattva's own exception

The question that opens this section deserves its own weight: if the bodhisattva genuinely never engages in any of the sixty-plus impure forms of giving just catalogued, this seems to conflict with ordinary human relationships entirely — repaying a debt of gratitude, honoring family custom. Nāgārjuna's own answer is precise rather than evasive: the bodhisattva may well do these things at times, but such acts simply do not count toward the pāramitā of giving specifically — a technical, not a moral, disqualification. Only giving joined with emptiness accomplishes this particular perfection; everything else may still be ordinary decency, just not this.

Question: Among these four kinds of giving you have explained, which giving ought the bodhisattva to practice?

Answer: "Among these four kinds of giving, one practices two kinds of pure giving, seeking neither renown nor gain, nor seeking any fruit in recompense."

This giving is of four kinds — with three aspects of purity and three aspects of impurity — and one never practices what is impure at all.

Among the pure, one practices two kinds of purity: first, pure with respect to the giver, though not pure with respect to the recipient; second, pure with respect to both together. Within these two kinds of pure giving, one ought constantly to exert diligent effort.

And why is this so? Because this bodhisattva does not anticipate any fruit in recompense — were one to anticipate such a fruit, one would then seek out only a pure recipient.

"Pure" refers to the giver and the recipient both being adorned with merit, their own mind pure. "Impure" refers to the giver possessing a mind of stinginess. As the Buddha has said: "Stinginess is the very defilement of giving." Though every other affliction likewise constitutes impurity, stinginess is the very gravest of all.

Question: Should the bodhisattva diligently practice these two kinds of giving — pure with respect to the giver alone, and pure with respect to both together — yet stinginess is itself the very defilement of the giver, and indeed the greatest defilement of giving; should the bodhisattva not yet have departed from desire, not yet able to sever this very stinginess, how, then, are they able to practice these two kinds of pure giving?

Answer: "Should any object be capable of giving rise to stinginess, one simply does not keep this very object."

Should the bodhisattva know that some animate or inanimate object gives rise to a mind of stinginess, they simply do not keep this very object — it is for this reason that, whatever they give, they give without any stinginess at all.

Question: External objects can indeed simply not be kept — but what, then, of one's own body?

Answer: "Constantly, for the benefit of sentient beings, one understands one's own body to be like a medicinal tree."

For the sake of benefiting sentient beings, one has faith in and understands one's own body to be like a medicinal tree. Just as a medicinal tree is of use to sentient beings — its roots, trunk, branches, leaves, flowers, and fruit each able to cure some particular illness, taken freely according to need, without any restriction or protection withheld — the bodhisattva is likewise so: for the benefit of sentient beings, they are able to relinquish their own body freely. They reflect: "Should sentient beings take my own head, eyes, hands, feet, limbs, joints, spine, belly, thighs, shoulders, ears, nose, teeth, tongue, blood, flesh, bone, or marrow, whatever they may require, I am able to give it to them in every case, even offering my entire body all at once." In this way, taming their own mind, cultivating and gathering roots of goodness, protected by skillful means, they practice the pāramitā of giving.

"Both giving in its general aspect, and giving in its particular aspect, are each, in every case, able to be dedicated."

This bodhisattva, through these two kinds of pure giving, is able to know two kinds of dedication: first, dedication in its general aspect; second, dedication in its particular aspect.

"Dedication in its general aspect": whatever is given is, in every case, dedicated toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

"Giving in its particular aspect": as explained within the section on the fruit and recompense of giving.

Furthermore, "dedication in its general aspect" is for the sake of bringing peace, joy, and benefit to every sentient being.

"Dedication in its particular aspect": for sentient beings without faith, that they might attain faith; for those who have broken the precepts, that they might attain the upholding of the precepts; for those of little learning, that they might attain vast learning; for the indolent, that they might attain diligent effort; for those of scattered mind, that they might attain meditative concentration; for foolish sentient beings, that they might attain wisdom; for the stingy, that they might attain a mind of relinquishment — particular aspects of every kind, such as these.

Furthermore, "dedication in its general aspect" dedicates the six pāramitās toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

"Dedication in its particular aspect": at the moment of giving an external object, one vows that every sentient being might attain the very greatest joy; at the moment of giving one's own limbs and joints, one vows that every sentient being might attain a buddha's own body in full.

A note on the medicinal tree

The image of one's own body as a medicinal tree — every part available to whoever needs it, without restriction — reframes the extreme gifts of flesh and bone described earlier in this fascicle not as heroic exceptions performed by rare, advanced bodhisattvas, but as the natural extension of a settled disposition: just as a tree does not deliberate before offering its own fruit to whoever is hungry, a bodhisattva who has genuinely tamed their own mind in this way simply is available in the same unreflective manner. The extremity of the gift is not the point; the absence of any remaining grasping to overcome first is.

Question: How many kinds of dedication does giving have? And how many kinds of non-dedication?

Answer: "There is one kind that purifies through four dedications, and three kinds of non-dedication."

The bodhisattva's own giving is dedicated for the sake of purifying four matters.

"Three kinds of non-dedication": one does not dedicate it for the sake of becoming a king; one does not dedicate it for the sake of attaining sensual pleasure; one does not dedicate it for the sake of attaining the ground of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha.

"Not dedicating it for the sake of becoming a king": this restraint against becoming a king likewise restrains against becoming any noble person, or one of power and mastery.

"Not dedicating it for the sake of attaining sensual pleasure": aside from the noble persons mentioned above, this refers to all others who enjoy wealth, pleasure, and the five desires for their own amusement.

"Not dedicating it for the sake of attaining the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha": this restrains one from entering nirvāṇa without remainder by means of the lesser vehicle, causing one instead to abide securely within the Great Vehicle, entering nirvāṇa without remainder only after a very long time.

As for "the four dedications that purify": the bodhisattva's own giving is dedicated for the sake of purifying a buddha-land; dedicated for the sake of purifying bodhisattvas; dedicated for the sake of purifying the teaching and transformation of sentient beings; and dedicated for the sake of purifying omniscience itself.

The bodhisattva ought to dedicate merit through skillful means in just this way, never allowing this giving to be diminished, but causing it instead to gain its own true force.

Question: By what dharma is giving diminished? And by what dharma is giving increased?

Answer: "Should this giving not be dedicated, and be without any skillful means, seeking rebirth in some lowly place, and drawing near to an evil friend — giving of this kind is then diminished."

Should this giving: fail to be dedicated toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, following instead after worldly pleasure; seek rebirth in some lowly place; lack any skillful means able to transcend the mere fruit and recompense of giving and meditative concentration, born of mastery over one's own circumstance; or draw near to a friend who obstructs the Great Vehicle — through these four dharmas, giving is thereby diminished.

"Departing from these four kinds of giving, one attains increase instead; and one ought further to give with three kinds of mind — the bodhisattva, according with the Buddha's own word, likewise never seeks a fruit in recompense."

Departing from these four dharmas in giving, one thereby attains increase: first, dedicating it toward unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening; second, dedicating it through skillful means; third, seeking the very station of a Dharma-king; fourth, drawing near to a good and virtuous friend.

One further ought to practice giving with these three states of mind: first, giving with the mind of bodhi, out of pity for every sentient being; second, giving without departing far from the Buddhadharma; third, giving without seeking any fruit in recompense.

Furthermore: "For the sake of attaining three dharmas, one practices giving; and, wishing to seek two further dharmas, one likewise ought to practice giving."

The bodhisattva practices giving for the sake of attaining three dharmas: first, the Buddhadharma itself; second, the expounding of this Dharma; third, causing every sentient being to abide within unsurpassed joy.

And, wishing to seek two further dharmas, one practices giving for: first, great wealth; second, the pāramitā of giving brought to completion. And why is this so?

A note on non-dedication

The three explicit "non-dedications" — never dedicating giving's merit toward kingship, toward sensual pleasure, or toward the lesser vehicles — read as a direct rebuttal to the most common motivations for religious giving across the wider Indian devotional landscape this treatise emerged within: prosperity, worldly power, and personal liberation alone. Naming these three specifically, rather than leaving them as implied contrasts, signals how live and tempting each option actually was to the treatise's own intended audience.

A note on this catalogue of comfort

This passage's own sustained, almost luxuriating attention to the concrete pleasures great wealth brings — fine couches, fragrant baths, physicians arriving unbidden, enemies growing scarce — sits in real tension with the sixty-plus impurities of giving catalogued earlier in this same fascicle, several of which explicitly forbid giving that seeks worldly comfort or reputation in return. The tension is resolved, not ignored, in the exchange that follows: wealth sought for one's own private enjoyment is condemned; wealth sought as the necessary precondition for a lifetime of further giving is not only permitted but actively recommended as one of two things "wished for" through giving itself.

A note on cause within the fruit

Nāgārjuna's own resolution — "this is speaking of the cause within the very fruit itself" — distinguishes wealth as an end from wealth as a means with a precision worth restating plainly: the earlier prohibition targets the intention behind the giving act (do not give in order to become wealthy for your own sake), while the later recommendation targets a separate, later choice (having wealth, use it to keep giving). The same object, wealth, is condemned as a goal and endorsed as an instrument, depending entirely on what it is subsequently deployed toward — the same distinction that runs through this entire chapter's treatment of every apparently self-interested motive.
