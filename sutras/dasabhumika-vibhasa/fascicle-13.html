The Simile of the Good Road, and the Thirty-Two Marks of a True Bodhisattva

A skilled guide's own knowledge of a good road — its grass and kindling, its freedom from bandits and wild beasts, the very ascetic extremes it need never resort to — is mapped, feature by feature, onto the ten bodhisattva grounds. Then numbered sets of dharmas, one through ten, encompass the entire path, thirty-two marks distinguish a genuine bodhisattva from one in name only, and ten minds carry a bodhisattva from the first ground into the second.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1521, 十住毘婆沙論, attributed to Nāgārjuna, tr. Kumārajīva 17 fascicles, 35 chapters

What Makes a Road Good: The Guide's Own Knowledge

Chinese Source

譬喻品之餘

問曰：菩薩善知是諸法，未得佛道，終不退者，其喻云何？

答曰：如大力導師，善知好道相，此處與彼處，轉道之所宜。

資糧及行具，皆悉令備足，於彼險道中，令眾得安隱。

得至大城邑，能令眾無患，由是大導師，善能知道故。

善知諸地轉，具足助道法，菩薩善知道，好惡此彼處。

自度生死險，兼導多眾生，令至安隱處、無為涅槃城。

悉令於惡道，不遇眾苦患；菩薩方便力，善能知道故。

好道相者，多有薪、草、水；無有寇賊、師子、狼虎及諸惡獸、毒虫之屬；不寒、不熱，無有惡山、溝坑、絕㵎、險隘、深榛、叢林、隈障；亦無高下，平直夷通，少於岐道，寬博多容，多人行處；行無厭惓，多有華、果、可食之物。

如是等事，名為好道相；與此相違，名為惡道相。

「此處」名人眾止宿、食息之處。

「彼處」名從是處至異處；若二宿中間，亦名異處。

「轉道」名見有岐道至大城者，是道應行，餘者應捨。

「資糧」名麨蜜摶等，道路所食。

「大力」名大勢力，多有財物、善解治法。

「備足」名多有飲食無所乏少。

「安」名無有賊寇恐怖之事。

「隱」名無有疾病、苦痛、衰患。

「大城」名多容人眾，能令多人眾得至大城。

「導師」善解道相，自無患難，亦令人眾無有患難。善諳道故，無有寒熱、飢渴、怨賊、惡獸、毒虫、惡山、惡水、深坑坎等如是過患。何以故？善知道路好、惡相故。以此喻歡喜等十地，如人行路，去不休息，能至大城，菩薩如是行是十地，得至佛法，入涅槃大城。如彼好道多有薪、草、水等，行者無乏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Simile Chapter, Continued

Question: The bodhisattva who skillfully knows these very dharmas, even before attaining buddhahood, never retrogresses — what simile illustrates this?

Answer: "Just as a guide of great strength skillfully knows the very mark of a good road, this place and that place, and which turn in the road is fitting.

"Provisions and every article for the journey, all made complete and sufficient, so that, amid this very perilous road, the multitude attains peace and security.

"Reaching the great city, causing the multitude to remain free of affliction — all of this comes from this very guide of great strength, since he skillfully knows the road itself.

"Skillfully knowing every turning of the ground, complete with every dharma that assists the path, the bodhisattva skillfully knows the road, whether good or bad, this place or that.

"Liberating themselves from the very peril of birth and death, and further guiding a great multitude of beings, causing them to reach a place of peace and security — the unconditioned city of nirvāṇa.

"Causing all to avoid every evil road, encountering no manifold suffering or affliction whatsoever — this is the bodhisattva's own power of skillful means, since he skillfully knows the road itself."

As for the very mark of a good road: it possesses abundant kindling, grass, and water; it is free of bandits and enemies, lions, wolves and tigers, and every kind of evil beast or venomous creature; it is neither too cold nor too hot; it is free of evil mountains, ditches and pits, sheer ravines, perilous narrows, deep thickets, dense forest, or hidden ambush; it is likewise free of steep rises and falls, level, straight, and smooth throughout; it has few forking paths, is broad and spacious, accommodating many travelers, a place where many people journey; traveling it brings no weariness whatsoever, and it possesses abundant flowers, fruit, and edible things.

Matters such as these are what is called the very mark of a good road; whatever contradicts this is called the mark of an evil road.

"This place" refers to wherever the multitude of travelers stops to lodge, eat, and rest.

"That place" refers to going from this place to a different place; even the interval between two nights' lodging is likewise called a different place.

"Turning in the road" refers to, upon beholding a fork in the road, recognizing which path leads to the great city — this very path ought to be taken, and every other ought to be abandoned.

"Provisions" refers to parched grain, honey-cakes, and the like, eaten along the road.

"Great strength" refers to great power, possessing abundant wealth, and skillfully understanding how to manage matters.

"Complete and sufficient" refers to possessing abundant food and drink, lacking nothing whatsoever.

"Peace" refers to being free of any matter of banditry or fearful threat.

"Security" refers to being free of illness, pain, and affliction.

"The great city" refers to a place able to accommodate a great multitude of people, able to cause a great multitude of people to reach this very great city.

As for "the guide": skillfully understanding the very mark of the road, he himself remains free of any affliction or difficulty, and further causes the multitude to remain free of any affliction or difficulty. Because he is thoroughly familiar with this road, there exists no cold, no heat, no hunger or thirst, no hostile bandit, no evil beast, no venomous creature, no evil mountain, no evil water, no deep pit or ravine, and no fault or affliction of any such kind whatsoever. And why is this so? Because he skillfully knows the good and evil marks of the road itself. This simile illustrates the Ground of Joy and the other nine grounds — just as a person traveling this road, journeying without ever ceasing, is able to reach the great city, the bodhisattva, practicing these ten grounds in just this same way, is able to reach the Buddhadharma, entering the great city of nirvāṇa. Just as this good road possesses abundant kindling, grass, water, and the like, the traveler lacks nothing whatsoever.

A note on this extended simile's own method

This chapter's own title, "Simile Chapter," is fully earned here: rather than a single brief comparison, the guide-and-road image is unpacked term by term — "this place," "that place," "provisions," "great strength," even the very city itself — each definition then mapped precisely onto a corresponding feature of the ten-ground path in the sections that follow. The technique gives the entire path a concrete, navigable shape: not an abstract sequence of attainments, but a road whose every hazard and resource has a specific, nameable counterpart in the bodhisattva's own actual practice.

Grass, Kindling, and Water — the Path's Own Sustenance

Chinese Source

「草」名如人乘馬，路多好草，馬力強盛；十地道功德亦如是——諦、捨、滅、慧四勝處助諸功德故，名為草。何以故？若人貴於實事，樂隨諦語，常親近實語者，見實有利樂隨實事，深惡妄語，遠離妄語，見妄語過，不欲樂聞，如是等因緣得諦勝處；捨等三處，亦應如是知。如彼好道，須諸象、馬、牛驢等得至大城，草助成其力。如是諦、捨、滅、慧處，能令至佛法，入涅槃大城。

「薪」名多聞、思、修慧，能至大智慧業。如薪能令火然，亦令猛盛。如是聞、思、修慧能生大慧，能令增長。如火能燒、能煮、能照，智慧火亦如是：燒諸煩惱、成熟諸善根、照四聖諦。

如火是智慧，薪是能生智慧等諸法。

「多水」名多有諸流河渠，隨意取用，充足大眾；泉井及池所不能爾。

復次，「多水」者，如人乘船隨水至大城，井泉、陂池水則不能得爾。如《經》說：「信為大河，福德為岸。如河除熱、除渴、除垢，能生勢力；善法中，信亦如是，能滅三毒熱、除三惡行垢、除三有渴；為涅槃故，於善法中得勢力。」如彼好道，多有諸根、藥草，則行者無乏；十地道亦如是。

「根」名深心所愛，如有根故，則生芽、莖、枝、葉等及諸果實；深心愛道，生正憶念、大願等諸功德。

「藥草」名諸波羅蜜。如藥草能滅諸毒；諸波羅蜜藥草滅貪恚癡毒、諸煩惱病，亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "grass": just as a person riding a horse, upon a road abundant with good grass, finds the horse's own strength vigorous and full — the merit of the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, since the four supreme places of truth, relinquishment, cessation, and wisdom assist every merit, and are thereby called "grass." And why is this so? Should a person value what is genuinely real, delighting in according with truthful speech, constantly drawing near to one who speaks truthfully, beholding this very truth as beneficial and delighting in according with what is real, deeply detesting false speech, departing far from false speech, beholding the fault of false speech and taking no delight in even hearing it — through causes and conditions such as these, one attains the supreme place of truth; the remaining three places of relinquishment, cessation, and wisdom ought likewise to be understood in just this same way. Just as this good road requires elephants, horses, oxen, and donkeys to reach the great city, with grass assisting and completing their own strength, so too do these places of truth, relinquishment, cessation, and wisdom enable one to reach the Buddhadharma, entering the great city of nirvāṇa.

As for "kindling": this refers to the wisdom born of learning, of reflection, and of cultivation, able to reach the very activity of great wisdom. Just as kindling causes a fire to burn, and further causes it to blaze fiercely — the wisdom born of learning, reflection, and cultivation is likewise so, able to give rise to great wisdom, and able to cause it to increase. Just as fire is able to burn, able to cook, and able to illuminate, the very fire of wisdom is likewise so: burning away every affliction, ripening every root of goodness, and illuminating the four noble truths.

Just as fire is itself wisdom, kindling is every dharma able to give rise to this wisdom.

As for "abundant water": this refers to possessing many flowing rivers and channels, drawn upon exactly as needed, fully sufficient for the great multitude — something a mere spring, well, or pond is unable to accomplish.

Furthermore, as for "abundant water": just as a person riding upon a boat, following the water, reaches the great city, while the water of a mere well, spring, or pond is unable to bring this about — as the sūtra states: "Faith is the great river, merit its own bank. Just as a river removes heat, removes thirst, and removes defilement, and is able to generate this very force — within the wholesome dharma, faith is likewise so, able to extinguish the very heat of the threefold poison, remove the defilement of the three evil conducts, and remove the thirst of the three states of existence; for the sake of nirvāṇa, within the wholesome dharma, one thereby attains this very force." Just as this good road possesses abundant roots and medicinal herbs, so that the traveler lacks nothing whatsoever, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so.

As for "root": this refers to what is loved with a deep mind — just as, because a root exists, a sprout, stem, branch, leaf, and every fruit thereby arises, loving the path with a deep mind gives rise to right mindfulness, the great vow, and every other such merit.

As for "medicinal herb": this refers to every pāramitā. Just as a medicinal herb is able to counteract every poison, the medicinal herb of every pāramitā counteracts the poison of craving, anger, and delusion, and every illness of the afflictions, in just this same way.

A note on faith as a great river

The image of faith as a great river, with merit as its own bank, gives a physically grounded picture to what could otherwise remain an abstract virtue: a river's own power to remove heat and thirst and carry heavy loads that a well or pond cannot is presented as the precise analogue for what faith accomplishes against the threefold poison and the three states of existence — not a static reservoir but a moving force capable of carrying a traveler all the way to the destination.

The Talisman, and Every Danger the Road Lacks

Chinese Source

如彼好道，不失韋婆陀，則行道安隱。

韋陀秦言無對義，是符檄。如行者不失符檄，則在所欲至，無有障礙；十地道亦如是，不失韋婆陀，則在所過諸地、所集善根，則能隨意助成、增長現在善根。彼又能教化聲聞道、辟支佛道、欲界、色界諸天道眾生，令住佛道。若魔、若外道，不能干亂，是名不失韋婆陀。

如彼好道無有蚊、虻、毒虫之屬；十地道亦如是，無有憂愁、啼哭之聲。

如彼好道無有賊難；十地道亦如是，無有五蓋諸惡賊眾。

如佛告比丘：「聚落賊者，所謂五蓋，如賊先奪人物，後乃害命；五蓋賊亦如是，先奪善根，後斷慧命，則墮放逸而死」。

如道中無師子、虎狼、諸惡獸等；十地道亦如是，無有瞋恚、鬪諍。

如師子等惡獸好惱害他；瞋恚等為惱他故生，亦復如是。

如惡獸等噉肉、飲血；瞋恨等食多聞慧肉、飲修慧等血，亦復如是。

如彼好道，無有寒、熱過惡；十地道亦如是，不墮寒氷地獄，故無有寒過惡；不墮熱地獄故，無有熱過惡。

如彼好道無深坑等諸難；十地道亦如是，無有外道苦行等諸難，所謂：灰身、入氷、拔髮、日三洗、翹一足、日一食、二日一食…乃至一月一食、默然至死、常舉一臂，常行忍辱、五熱炙身、臥刺蕀上、入火、入水、自投高巖、深爐中立、牛屎燒身、直趣一方不避諸難、常著濕衣裳、水中臥等，身苦心苦不至正智；無如是等故，名為無難。

如道無邪徑；十地道亦如是，無身、口、意惡業故，名為無邪徑。

如道無刺蕀者；十地道亦如是，無諸業障刺蕀故，名為無刺蕀。

如刺刺脚則廢行路，業障刺蕀障行佛法、入涅槃。

如道正直；十地道亦如是，無一切諂曲、欺誑故，名為正直。

如道少岐道；十地道亦如是，少於異道，何以故？發大乘者，少行聲聞、辟支佛道，是故少於異道。或有菩薩行二乘道者，當知未到菩薩地、未入正位，行於邊行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as, upon this good road, never losing this very talisman brings peace and security to one's own journey —

"Vaidya," in Chinese, means "matchless meaning" — this refers to a pass or a proclamation. Just as the traveler who never loses this very pass or proclamation reaches wherever they wish, without any obstruction whatsoever, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so — never losing this very talisman, one is thereby able, within every ground passed through and every root of goodness gathered, to freely assist, complete, and increase one's own present roots of goodness exactly as one wishes. This person is further able to teach and transform the śrāvaka path, the pratyekabuddha path, and every sentient being belonging to the heavenly paths of the desire and form realms, causing them to abide within the path to buddhahood. Neither māra nor any outside path is able to disturb or confound them — this is what is called never losing this very talisman.

Just as this good road is free of mosquitoes, gadflies, and every kind of venomous creature, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, free of the very sound of sorrow and weeping.

Just as this good road is free of the danger of bandits, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, free of the five hindrances and every such evil, bandit-like host.

As the Buddha told the monks: "The bandits of a settlement are the five hindrances — just as a bandit first seizes a person's own possessions, and only afterward takes their life, the bandits of the five hindrances are likewise so, first seizing away one's own roots of goodness, and only afterward severing the very life of wisdom, so that one falls into heedlessness and dies."

Just as this road is free of lions, tigers, wolves, and every evil beast, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, free of anger and contention.

Just as lions and every such evil beast delight in afflicting and harming others, anger and the like arise for the very sake of afflicting others, in just this same way.

Just as an evil beast devours flesh and drinks blood, anger and resentment devour the very flesh of vast-learning wisdom, and drink the very blood of cultivated wisdom, in just this same way.

Just as this good road is free of the affliction of cold and heat, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so — never falling into the icy hell, it is thereby free of the affliction of cold; never falling into the fiery hell, it is thereby free of the affliction of heat.

Just as this good road is free of the danger of deep pits and the like, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, free of the danger of every ascetic austerity practiced by the outside paths — namely: smearing the body with ash, sitting in ice, tearing out one's own hair, washing three times a day, standing on a single leg, eating once a day, once every two days, up to once a month, remaining silent until death, constantly holding one arm aloft, constantly practicing patient endurance amid the "five fires" scorching the body, lying upon a bed of thorns, entering fire, entering water, throwing oneself from a high cliff, standing within a deep furnace, burning the body with cow dung, walking straight in a single direction without avoiding any obstacle, constantly wearing wet clothing, lying within water, and the like — suffering in body and suffering in mind, and yet never reaching right wisdom. Being free of every such practice, this is what is called being free of danger.

Just as this road is free of wrong turns, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, free of any evil karma of body, speech, or mind, and is thereby called free of wrong turns.

Just as this road is free of thorns and brambles, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, free of every thorn and bramble of karmic obstruction, and is thereby called free of thorns and brambles.

Just as a thorn piercing the foot causes one to abandon the journey, the very thorns and brambles of karmic obstruction obstruct one's own practice of the Buddhadharma, and entry into nirvāṇa.

Just as this road is straight and true, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, free of every flattery, crookedness, and deception, and is thereby called straight and true.

Just as this road has few forking paths, the path of the ten grounds is likewise so, having few divergent paths. And why is this so? One who has given rise to the Great Vehicle's own aspiration rarely practices the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha path — it is for this reason that there are few divergent paths. Should there exist a bodhisattva who practices the path of these two lesser vehicles, one should know that they have not yet reached the bodhisattva's own ground, have not yet entered the very station of certainty, and are practicing a peripheral conduct.

A note on the catalogue of austerities

The extended list of ascetic extremes — ice-sitting, hair-pulling, the "five fires," standing in a furnace, lying in water — draws on a well-documented catalogue of non-Buddhist austerities found across early Buddhist literature, cited here not to condemn effort itself but to make a specific point: a genuinely good road requires none of these desperate measures, since "suffering in body and suffering in mind, yet never reaching right wisdom" is precisely the outcome such practices produce when severed from wisdom. The talisman opening this section functions as a memorable image for whatever protects safe passage through the grounds — likely referring to the very mindfulness or vow discussed in earlier fascicles — described here through its own effect: freedom from confusion by māra or any outside path, wherever one currently stands on the road.

A Wide, Well-Traveled Road, and the Great City Itself

Chinese Source

如彼好道無諸叢林妨礙；十住道亦如是，無有五欲諸惡叢林。

問曰：何故不言「都無五欲叢林」，但言「無惡林」耶？

答曰：發大乘者，福德因緣，有第一五欲，是故不得言無，但無惡耳。

復次，如深叢林，難入難過，多諸難礙；菩薩五欲則不然，不如凡夫於五欲生諸過惡。如是故，但說無叢林。

如道寬博多容，不相妨礙；十住道亦如是，多所容受，無量百千萬億眾生共發無上道心，而不相妨礙，是百千萬億眾生，若一切眾生，俱發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，同行此道，不相妨礙。

如道多人所行；十住道亦如是，恒河沙等過去、現在諸佛行菩薩道時，皆行此道。

如彼好道，行不疲厭；十住道亦如是，多有因果諸樂，所謂：多生人天中受果報，樂離欲故，受歡喜樂、禪定樂、無喜樂、現在樂；得是諸樂，故無有疲厭。

如道多有華、果、根；十住道亦如是，多根、華、果。

「根」者三善根。

「華」者七覺華是。如《經》說：「七華者七覺意是」。

「果」者四沙門果是。

無如是等違好道功德過故，名為離惡。

如導師知道中，是中應食、是應宿，彼處亦應宿；菩薩行十地亦如是，知何處可宿、何處可食。

可宿名有諸現在佛處。

可食名可得修習善法處。如食能利益諸根，亦助壽命；諸善法亦如是，能益「信」等諸根，助成慧命。

「異處宿」名從彼佛所，至餘佛所。

復次，此佛國土、彼佛國土中間，亦名異處。

善知道轉者，如彼導師知道不安隱則轉；菩薩亦如是，善知是道至聲聞、是道至辟支佛、是道至佛。如是知已，捨聲聞道、辟支佛道，但行至佛道。

如彼好道多有飲食；十住道亦如是，多行布施、持戒、修禪。

如彼導師以多財物，善能治法，有大勢力；菩薩亦如是，有財物、治法故，有大勢力。

財者七財，所謂信、戒、慚、愧、捨、聞、慧。

治法者，一切魔、種種沙門、婆羅門、外道論師，悉能摧伏，是為威勢。

如彼大城無有怨賊、疫病、暴死、種種衰惱故，名為安隱；涅槃大城亦如是，無有諸魔、外道諸流、貪欲、瞋恚、放逸、死、憂悲、苦惱、啼哭故，名為安隱。

如彼大城多有飲食，故名為豐饒；涅槃城亦如是，多有諸深禪定、解脫、三昧故，名為豐饒。

如彼大城多所容受，故名為大城；涅槃城亦如是，多受眾生，故名為大。假令一切眾生不受諸法故，皆入無餘涅槃，而涅槃性無增無減。

A note on this ascending numerical scaffold

This section's own escalating structure — a single dharma sufficient on its own, then two, then progressively larger sets up through ten — is not merely a mnemonic device but an argument in itself: each larger set does not replace the smaller ones before it but incorporates and elaborates them, so that "freedom from heedlessness" alone at the base and the ten wholesome paths at the summit are shown to be different resolutions of the very same underlying practice rather than competing systems. The technique lets a reader daunted by the treatise's own accumulating detail enter the path at whatever scale of commitment currently feels manageable, with the assurance that each smaller formulation genuinely does encompass the whole.

A note on this parallel structure

This section mirrors the preceding one exactly in scale and organization — one fault through ten — but inverts its logic: rather than dharmas that build cumulatively toward buddhahood, these are hazards to be recognized and refused at every stage. The second item's own severity is worth pausing on: falling into hell is explicitly ranked as less dangerous to a bodhisattva than falling into either lesser vehicle, since hell does not permanently foreclose the path to buddhahood while genuine attainment of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha fruit does — the sharpest statement in this treatise of just how seriously it treats the distinction between the vehicles.

The image of an earthen wall bearing the name "jeweled city" gives sharp, memorable shape to this section's own central distinction: a title claimed without the substance behind it deceives not only outside observers but the very person who claims it, promising a defense the wall cannot actually provide. Distinguishing the Karmic Path of the Second Ground Chapter

"Every bodhisattva who has already attained completeness within the first ground, wishing to attain the second ground, ought then to give rise to ten kinds of mind."

Every bodhisattva who has already attained the first ground, the Ground of Joy, for the sake of attaining the second ground, gives rise to these ten kinds of mind — through these very ten minds, one is thereby able to attain the second ground, just as a person wishing to ascend to an upper story must necessarily climb by means of a stairway.

Question: What are these ten minds, the skillful means for attaining the second ground?

Answer: "An upright mind, a fit and usable mind, a gentle mind, a subdued mind, a tranquil, ceased mind, a genuine and sublime mind, an unmixed mind, a mind free of craving, and a vast, joyful mind, together with a great mind — these are the ten."

Every bodhisattva already complete within the first ground, wishing to attain the second ground, gives rise to these ten minds as skillful means: first, an upright mind; second, a fit and usable mind; third, a gentle mind; fourth, a subdued mind; fifth, a tranquil, ceased mind; sixth, a genuine and sublime mind; seventh, an unmixed mind; eighth, a mind free of craving; ninth, a vast and joyful mind; tenth, a great mind.

As for "an upright mind": this refers to departing from flattery and crookedness.

Through departing from flattery and crookedness, the mind thereby becomes gentle. "Gentle" refers to being free of rigidity, harshness, and coarseness.

The bodhisattva, having attained this very gentle mind, gives rise to meditative absorption of every kind, and further cultivates and studies every wholesome dharma, contemplating the true mark of every dharma — the mind thereby becomes fit and usable.

Through this very mind becoming fit and usable, one gives rise to a subdued mind. "Subdued" refers to being skillfully able to subdue the eye and every other faculty; as the sūtra states: "What is the wholesome path? Namely, the monk who subdues the very faculty of the eye, right up through the faculty of the mind." Through subduing these very six faculties, this is what is called a subdued mind.

Once the mind has already been subdued, it thereby easily gives rise to a tranquil, ceased mind.

"Tranquil, ceased mind" refers to being able to extinguish craving, anger, delusion, and every other affliction. Having first restrained these through the very "subdued mind," one thereby causes them to become tranquil and ceased.

There are, further, those who say: "Attaining meditative absorption of every kind is itself what is called the tranquil, ceased mind; as the sūtra states: 'Should a person skillfully know the very mark of meditative absorption, and yet never grasp at its own flavor, this is called the tranquil, ceased mind.'"

Having attained this very tranquil, ceased mind, one necessarily gives rise to the genuine and sublime mind. "Genuine and sublime mind" refers to, within every matter concerning meditative absorption, spiritual power, and one's own vow, attaining mastery over them exactly as one wishes, just as genuine gold can be used exactly as one wishes.

The practitioner, having already attained the upright mind up through the genuine and sublime mind, for the sake of guarding and protecting this very mind, delights in giving rise to the unmixed mind. "Unmixed mind" refers to never engaging in worldly affairs together with either householder or renunciant. This person reflects thus: "I have attained minds such as these entirely through the power of meditative absorption; through these very minds, I shall attain the second ground and every other immeasurable benefit. Should I engage together with the multitude, I would thereby lose this very benefit. And why is this so? Should a person engage in worldly affairs together with the multitude, the eye and the other five faculties may, at times, once more give rise to some unwholesome dharma. And why is this so? Because, drawing near to a dharma capable of defiling, capable of provoking anger, capable of provoking delusion, every faculty is thereby stirred into motion, and the very fire of the afflictions is kindled; through this very fire of the afflictions being kindled, this benefit is thereby lost." Beholding faults such as these, one gives rise to the unmixed mind, and ought never to engage in worldly affairs together with either householder or renunciant.

This person, having already attained this very unmixed mind, next gives rise to the mind free of craving. "Mind free of craving" refers to, toward both householder and renunciant alike — namely, one's own parents, siblings, preceptor, and teacher — never giving rise to any craving attachment whatsoever, reflecting thus: "Should I give rise to craving attachment toward either householder or renunciant, I would necessarily come and go, offering greeting to them, and how, then, could I possibly maintain this very unmixed mind? It is for this very reason that, wishing to cause every benefit of meditative absorption and the rest to abide within this unmixed mind, I ought to relinquish, toward both householder and renunciant, this very mind of craving attachment."

A note on this chapter's own opening

The ten minds unfold here as a genuine causal sequence, each explicitly generating the next — uprightness producing gentleness, gentleness producing fitness for use, fitness producing subdual of the faculties, subdual producing tranquility, tranquility producing the genuine and sublime mind capable of using its own attainments freely, and only then, as a deliberate act of guarding what has been built, the unmixed mind that withdraws from ordinary social entanglement. The very next section raises the obvious objection this withdrawal invites — has the bodhisattva not just been warned, at length, never to abandon any sentient being? — before answering it and completing the remaining minds.

"Should there exist sentient beings who fail to accept the bodhisattva's own benefit, toward these one ought never to abandon them, but ought instead to give rise to great loving-kindness and compassion."

How, then, can you say that "the bodhisattva, having attained the unmixed mind, gives rise to the mind free of craving"? Should the bodhisattva hold no craving whatsoever toward sentient beings, would this not itself be a form of abandoning them — how, then, could they possibly liberate them?

Answer: One ought to practice this very mind of relinquishment in accordance with the bodhisattva's own path. And why is this so? This person, through this very mind of relinquishment, gives rise to the vast and joyful mind, reflecting thus: "Should I relinquish this very clamor of the crowd, I shall thereby attain meditative absorption; through this meditative absorption, I shall give rise to a sublime, vast, and joyful dharma; having attained this very dharma, I shall afterward be able to benefit sentient beings a thousand myriad times more than I could now." It is for this reason that, for the sake of benefiting sentient beings all the more, one relinquishes, for a brief time, this very clamor of the crowd through this mind of relinquishment, as a skillful expedient, so as to attain meditative absorption, the five spiritual powers, and every other means of benefiting sentient beings.

Why, then, does the bodhisattva employ this very skillful means? The bodhisattva, for the sake of attaining the great mind, reflects thus: "A great person delights in great benefit, and does not settle for a lesser benefit." It is for this reason that I ought now to seek the very dharma of a great person, and cultivate and study it accordingly; I ought, in just this way, diligently apply further effort, for the sake of this great benefit — namely, meditative absorption of every kind, spiritual power, the extinguishing of suffering, liberation, and the rest.

It is for this reason that what you have said does not hold.

A note on relinquishment as skillful means

The resolution here turns on timing rather than intention: the unmixed mind's own temporary withdrawal from ordinary social entanglement is reframed not as abandoning sentient beings but as investing in a far greater future capacity to benefit them, the very same logic this treatise has applied elsewhere to giving that appears, on its surface, to withhold something. The great person's own preference for great benefit over lesser benefit gives this deferral its own ethical justification — a bodhisattva who declines an immediate, modest engagement is not thereby less committed to sentient beings, but calculating toward a return "a thousand myriad times" greater once meditative absorption and its own powers are secured.

Question: The first ground already possesses this very dharma of "an upright mind" and the rest — why, then, does the text speak once more of "the bodhisattva, wishing to attain the second ground, giving rise to these ten minds"?

Answer: Though the first ground already possesses this very dharma, one has not yet attained a deep delight in it, and it is not yet firm and secure; within this very ground, the mind constantly delights in it, growing ever deeper and more firm, until it becomes fit and usable — it is for this reason that your own challenge here does not hold.

Question: Should one attain a deep delight in this dharma, firm and secure, what different matter is thereby attained?

Answer: "Should one attain, all at once, this deep delight and this firm, secure mind, one no longer requires any further effort to sustain it, just as one who has been sent on an errand constantly continues to follow it through."

Just as an emissary, once dispatched, constantly continues to follow the person they were sent to attend — the bodhisattva, having attained, all at once, this deep delight and this firm, secure mind, likewise constantly continues in just this way; it no longer requires any further effort to arise, and, given even the slightest cause and condition, arises of its own accord. And why is this so? Because its own root has already penetrated deeply, so that its own stem and every joint continue on in unbroken succession.

Question: Should the bodhisattva attain these very ten kinds of mind, what fruit is thereby attained?

Answer: "Should one attain these very minds, one rightly abides within the second ground, complete with the threefold freedom from defilement — freedom from evil karma, and freedom from the afflictions."

Should the bodhisattva attain this very "upright mind" and the other nine, this is what is called abiding within the bodhisattva's own second ground.

The first freedom from defilement is the very name of this ground itself; the second freedom from defilement refers to, within this very ground, departing from the defilement of offense and karma belonging to the ten unwholesome paths; the third freedom from defilement refers to departing from the defilement of craving, anger, and every other affliction — it is for this reason that this ground is called freedom from defilement.
