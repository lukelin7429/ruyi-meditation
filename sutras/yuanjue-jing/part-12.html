The Sūtra's Own Transmission

The sūtra's twelfth and final questioner asks what no one has asked yet: what is this teaching actually called, and how is it to be carried forward once the assembly disperses? The Buddha answers with five names for one sūtra, a measure of the merit in upholding it, and a promise of protection — after which vajra spirits, heavenly kings, and a ghost king each rise, unprompted, to vow that whoever keeps this teaching will be guarded as carefully as one's own eyes.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 12 of 12 complete Source: Taishō T0842, 圓覺經 (tr. Buddhatrāta) Reading notes after Zongmi's 圓覺經略疏 (T1795)

Worthy and Wholesome Foremost's Question

Chinese Source

於是賢善首菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右遶三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！廣為我等及末世眾生，開悟如是不思議事。世尊！此大乘教名字何等？云何奉持？眾生修習得何功德？云何使我護持經人？流布此教至於何地？」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告賢善首菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能為諸菩薩及末世眾生，問於如來如是經教功德名字。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，賢善首菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Worthy and Wholesome Foremost rose from his seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — you have broadly opened, for our sake and for sentient beings of the final age, this inconceivable matter. World-Honored One: what is this Mahāyāna teaching called? How should it be upheld and carried forward? What merit does a sentient being gain through practicing it? How should I go about protecting those who uphold this sūtra? And to what level does this teaching, once spread abroad, actually bring a person?" Having said this, he prostrated himself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Worthy and Wholesome Foremost: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata about the merit and name of this sūtra's own teaching. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Worthy and Wholesome Foremost, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads this fivefold question as the sūtra's own hinge from doctrine into transmission: the first eleven chapters gave the teaching itself, and this final chapter turns to how that teaching survives the assembly's dispersal — under what name it will be recognized, how it should be studied once the Buddha is no longer present to explain it directly, and what protects it from being lost or corrupted along the way. He glosses this bodhisattva's own name precisely for the role: "worthy" names one who accords with principle, "wholesome" names one who follows it, and "foremost" names one who stands at the head of every wholesome thing — since it is exactly this teaching's own transmission, carried forward by those who follow it faithfully, that lets every wholesome practice described across the preceding eleven chapters continue at all.

Five Names for One Sūtra

Chinese Source

「善男子！是經百千萬億恒河沙諸佛所說，三世如來之所守護，十方菩薩之所歸依，十二部經清淨眼目。是經名大方廣圓覺陀羅尼，亦名修多羅了義，亦名祕密王三昧，亦名如來決定境界，亦名如來藏自性差別，汝當奉持。善男子！是經唯顯如來境界，唯佛如來能盡宣說；若諸菩薩及末世眾生依此修行，漸次增進，至於佛地。善男子！是經名為頓教大乘，頓機眾生從此開悟，亦攝漸修一切群品。譬如大海不讓小流，乃至蚊虻及阿修羅飲其水者，皆得充滿。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: this sūtra has been spoken by buddhas as numerous as the sands of hundreds of thousands of myriads of koṭis of Ganges rivers, is guarded by the tathāgatas of all three times, is where bodhisattvas of the ten directions take refuge, and is the pure eye of all twelve divisions of the scriptures. This sūtra is named the Great and Expansive Dhāraṇī of Perfect Enlightenment; it is also named the Decisive Meaning of the Sūtras; it is also named the King of Secret Samādhis; it is also named the Tathāgata's Own Decisive Realm; and it is also named the Differentiated Nature of the Tathāgatagarbha. You should uphold it by these names. Good sons: this sūtra discloses only the Tathāgata's own realm, and only a buddha, a tathāgata, can fully proclaim it — yet if bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age practice in reliance on it, they will gradually advance further and further, until they reach the very ground of buddhahood. Good sons: this sūtra is named the Sudden Teaching of the Mahāyāna. Beings of sudden capacity awaken through it directly, and yet it also fully embraces every kind of gradual cultivation, for beings of every capacity. It is like the great ocean, which turns away no small stream that flows into it — even a mosquito, or a gnat, or an asura, drinking from it, is equally filled."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi walks through all five names as five different angles on the same single reality, not five separate teachings: "the Dhāraṇī of Perfect Enlightenment" names it by its substance, the total-holding gate Mañjuśrī's opening chapter already described; "the Decisive Meaning of the Sūtras" names it by its authority, a definitive rather than a provisional teaching; "the King of Secret Samādhis" names it by its depth, reserved for those genuinely capable of receiving it rather than proclaimed indiscriminately; "the Tathāgata's Own Decisive Realm" names it by what it discloses, the buddha's own realm rather than a mere description of it from outside; and "the Differentiated Nature of the Tathāgatagarbha" names it by how that single reality appears differently across the sūtra's twelve chapters, one underlying tathāgatagarbha manifesting as illusion, as the four elements, as the twenty-five wheels, and as every other topic the sūtra has taken up along the way — the same relationship this site's Awakening of Faith collection already established between mind-as-suchness and mind-as-arising-and-ceasing, one substance read two ways.

On "sudden teaching that fully embraces gradual cultivation," Zongmi is precise about how the two are not actually in tension: a being of sudden capacity awakens the moment the teaching is heard, requiring no staged sequence at all, while a being of gradual capacity uses the very same teaching to work, stage by stage, through exactly the kind of graduated practice Pure Wisdom's chapter and Perfect Enlightenment's own retreat instructions already laid out — the ocean simile making the point precisely: the ocean does not have a shallow section reserved for small streams and a deep section reserved for large ones; every stream that enters is filled by the identical water, whatever its own prior size. Sudden and gradual, on this reading, are differences in the drinker's own capacity, never differences in what is actually being offered.

Measuring the Merit of This Teaching

Chinese Source

「善男子！假使有人純以七寶積滿三千大千世界以用布施，不如有人聞此經名及一句義。善男子！假使有人教百恒河沙眾生得阿羅漢果，不如有人宣說此經分別半偈。善男子！若復有人聞此經名，信心不惑，當知是人非於一佛二佛種諸福慧，如是乃至盡恒河沙一切佛所種諸善根，聞此經教。汝善男子！當護末世是修行者，無令惡魔及諸外道惱其身心，令生退屈。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: suppose there were a person who filled the whole of a great trichiliocosm entirely with the seven treasures and gave it all away in generosity — this would still not equal a person who merely hears the name of this sūtra and understands a single phrase of its meaning. Good sons: suppose there were a person who brought beings as numerous as the sands of a hundred Ganges rivers to the fruit of arhatship through his teaching — this would still not equal a person who explains and expounds even half a verse of this sūtra. Good sons: if there is a person who hears the name of this sūtra and whose faith is not shaken by any doubt, one should know that such a person has not planted roots of merit and wisdom under only one buddha or two, but has, across as many buddhas as there are grains of sand in the Ganges, already planted every root of goodness needed to hear this sūtra's teaching now. You, good sons, should protect those who practice this teaching in the final age, and never let evil demons or outsiders of wrong view trouble their body and mind, causing them to lose heart and retreat."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads these three comparisons — treasure against a single phrase, converting arhats against half a verse, and settled faith itself against the sheer breadth of merit it implies — as measuring the same distinction the sūtra has drawn since Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery's chapter, depth against breadth: material generosity and successful conversion to arhatship each produce real but bounded merit, however vast in scale, while genuine contact with even a fragment of this teaching touches something the sūtra has insisted, from its opening chapter onward, is without boundary. He is careful about the third comparison in particular: unshaken faith on merely hearing the sūtra's name is not treated as a small or easy thing simply because no formal study is described — it is offered as proof of an already deep and long-cultivated capacity, the same point his earlier reading notes already made about the sudden, stageless accord closing Pure Wisdom's chapter. The closing instruction to protect practitioners from demonic and heterodox interference he treats as setting up, quite directly, the vows that follow in the sūtra's final scene — the Buddha names the responsibility here, and the assembled protectors take it up themselves in the section immediately after.

The Vows of the Protectors

Chinese Source

爾時，會中有火首金剛、摧碎金剛、尼藍婆金剛等八萬金剛，并其眷屬，即從座起，頂禮佛足，右繞三匝而白佛言：「世尊！若後末世，一切眾生有能持此決定大乘，我當守護如護眼目，乃至道場所修行處，我等金剛自領徒眾，晨夕守護，令不退轉。其家乃至永無災障，疫病銷滅，財寶豐足，常不乏少。」

爾時，大梵天王、二十八天王，并須彌山王、護國天王等，即從座起，頂禮佛足，右繞三匝而白佛言：「世尊！我亦守護是持經者，常令安隱，心不退轉。」

爾時，有大力鬼王名吉槃茶，與十萬鬼王，即從座起，頂禮佛足，右繞三匝而白佛言：「世尊！我亦守護是持經人，朝夕侍衛，令不退屈。其人所居一由旬內，若有鬼神侵其境界，我當使其碎如微塵。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, within the assembly, Fire-Crowned Vajra, Crushing Vajra, Nīlāmbara Vajra, and eighty thousand vajra-spirits like them, together with their retinues, rose from their seats, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One — if, in the later final age, any sentient being is able to uphold this decisive Mahāyāna teaching, we will guard that person as carefully as we would guard our own eyes. As for the place of practice itself, wherever this practice hall may be, we vajra-spirits, leading our own retinues, will guard it morning and night without exception, so that it never falls into decline. That person's household, moreover, will forever be free of calamity and misfortune, epidemic disease will be dispelled, and wealth and provisions will remain abundant, never lacking."

At that time, the Great Brahmā King, the kings of the twenty-eight heavens, together with Mount Sumeru's own king and the guardian kings of the four directions, rose from their seats, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One — we too will guard whoever upholds this sūtra, always keeping them at peace and their mind free from retreat."

At that time, a mighty ghost king named Kumbhāṇḍa, together with a hundred thousand ghost kings, rose from their seats, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One — we too will guard whoever upholds this sūtra, attending them morning and night so that they never lose heart and retreat. Within one yojana of wherever that person dwells, should any spirit or ghost trespass upon their surroundings, we will grind that spirit into fine dust."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads the three groups of protectors — vajra-wielding spirits, heavenly kings, and ghost kings — as together spanning the full range of beings that traditionally guard the Dharma, offered here entirely unprompted rather than solicited by the Buddha, which he takes as itself significant: the Buddha only just finished instructing the bodhisattvas to protect practitioners in the previous section, and these three assemblies immediately volunteer their own protection in turn, without being asked, showing the teaching's own weight is recognized even by beings outside the human and bodhisattva assembly. He notes each vow is pitched to a different kind of danger: the vajra-spirits guard against outright calamity and material want, the heavenly kings against the practitioner's own loss of inner peace and resolve, and the ghost king specifically against subtler spiritual trespass within the immediate vicinity of practice — together covering the practical, the psychological, and the unseen threats a practitioner might otherwise face in isolation.

The Assembly Rejoices

Chinese Source

佛說此經已，一切菩薩、天龍、鬼神八部眷屬，及諸天王、梵王等一切大眾，聞佛所說，皆大歡喜，信受奉行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once the Buddha had finished speaking this sūtra, every bodhisattva, every being of the eightfold retinue of gods, dragons, and spirits, and every heavenly king, Brahmā king, and member of the whole great assembly, having heard what the Buddha had spoken, rejoiced greatly, and, believing and accepting it, undertook to uphold and practice it.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi glosses the sūtra's closing formula by a threefold purity he draws from the Sūtra Requested by Mañjuśrī: rejoicing of exactly this kind arises where the one who spoke the teaching is himself untainted by any desire for fame or gain, where what was spoken is itself an accurate account of reality, and where the result of hearing it is itself genuine benefit for the hearer — all three conditions, on his reading, fully satisfied by everything the preceding eleven chapters have already shown. He notes this is the standard closing formula shared by nearly every Mahāyāna sūtra, and offers no further gloss beyond confirming that this sūtra, like the others, earns it.

In Verse

This is the only one of the sūtra's twelve chapters that closes without a verse — Worthy and Wholesome Foremost's question, unlike the eleven before it, receives its answer in prose alone, followed directly by the protectors' spontaneous vows and the assembly's closing rejoicing.
