The Practice Hall

Every prior chapter answered a doctrinal or diagnostic question; Perfect Enlightenment now asks the sūtra's most practical one — for someone who has not yet awakened, how should retreat itself actually be structured? The answer sets out three lengths of retreat, a rite of worship and repentance, a bodhisattva's own variant of the traditional summer retreat, and a first approach to each of the three methods, closing every single instruction with the same standing warning: whatever unusual experience arises, if it was not already named in this teaching, it is never to be taken as genuine attainment.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 11 of 12 complete Source: Taishō T0842, 圓覺經 (tr. Buddhatrāta) Reading notes after Zongmi's 圓覺經略疏 (T1795)

Perfect Enlightenment's Question

Chinese Source

於是圓覺菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右遶三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！為我等輩廣說淨覺種種方便，令末世眾生有大增益。世尊！我等今者已得開悟，若佛滅後末世眾生未得悟者，云何安居修此圓覺清淨境界？此圓覺中三種淨觀以何為首？唯願大悲為諸大眾及末世眾生施大饒益。」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告圓覺菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能問於如來如是方便，以大饒益施諸眾生。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，圓覺菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Perfect Enlightenment rose from his seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — you have broadly set out, for our sake, every kind of skillful means for pure awakening, bringing great benefit to sentient beings of the final age. World-Honored One: we here have already attained awakening — but after the Buddha's own passing, for sentient beings of the final age who have not yet attained it, how should they retreat into practice to cultivate this pure state of Perfect Enlightenment? Among the three pure contemplations within Perfect Enlightenment, which should come first? I ask only, out of great compassion, that you bestow great benefit upon this assembly and upon sentient beings of the final age." Having said this, he prostrated himself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Perfect Enlightenment: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata about skillful means of exactly this kind, bestowing great benefit upon sentient beings. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Perfect Enlightenment, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi glosses "already attained awakening" carefully: the bodhisattvas of this assembly have understood the teaching directly from the Buddha himself, but sentient beings of the future age will lack that immediate presence, hence the question shifts from doctrine already covered to the concrete structure needed to help an as-yet-unawakened practitioner actually get there. He reads Perfect Enlightenment's own name, given last among the twelve questioners, as marking exactly this transitional role: earlier chapters unfolded theory and diagnosis one piece at a time, but by this point "theory is already perfect and practice already complete," so this bodhisattva's task is to gather everything into a single, workable structure a practitioner can actually follow — hence a chapter built almost entirely of concrete instructions rather than further doctrine.

Building the Practice Hall

Chinese Source

「善男子！一切眾生，若佛住世，若佛滅後、若法末時，有諸眾生具大乘性，信佛祕密大圓覺心，欲修行者，若在伽藍安處徒眾，有緣事故隨分思察，如我已說；若復無有他事因緣，即建道場，當立期限，若立長期百二十日，中期百日，下期八十日，安置淨居。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: whether the Buddha is present in the world, or has already passed away, or the age of the Dharma is nearing its end, there are sentient beings possessing the nature of the Mahāyāna who have faith in the Buddha's secret, great mind of Perfect Enlightenment, and who wish to practice. If such a person lives within a monastery among a community of fellow practitioners, then, given the occasions and demands of that shared life, one should reflect on and contemplate this teaching as circumstances allow, exactly as I have already explained. But if a person has no other pressing occasion or circumstance, then one should establish a practice hall and set a fixed period for it: a long period of a hundred and twenty days, a middle period of a hundred days, or a lesser period of eighty days — and settle oneself into a pure dwelling for that time."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi treats the three retreat lengths as calibrated to a practitioner's own capacity, though he notes two ways this calibration can be read, and does not decide firmly between them: on one reading, the longer period suits a practitioner with heavier obstruction, who needs more time to work through it, while the shorter period suits one already closer to readiness; on the other, the longer period suits a practitioner of greater diligence, able to sustain sincere effort across the fuller span, while the shorter period suits one whose diligence is more easily exhausted. Either way, he stresses the lengths are not arbitrary numerology but a practical accommodation to real differences among practitioners — the same instinct behind the twenty-five wheels of Eloquent Voice's chapter, applied here to duration rather than to method.

Worship and Repentance, Then Settling Into Recollection

Chinese Source

「若佛現在，當正思惟；若佛滅後，施設形像，心存目想，生正憶念，還同如來常住之日。懸諸幡花，經三七日，稽首十方諸佛名字，求哀懺悔，遇善境界，得心輕安。過三七日，一向攝念。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"If the Buddha is present in the world, one should reflect on him directly; if the Buddha has already passed away, one should set up an image of him, holding it in mind and fixing the eye on it, giving rise to right recollection, exactly as though the Tathāgata's living presence still continued unbroken. Hang banners and flowers for a period of three weeks of seven days each, bow the head to the names of the buddhas of the ten directions, and seek forgiveness through sincere repentance. On encountering favorable signs, one attains ease of mind. Once the three weeks have passed, turn wholly and singly to sustained recollection."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes the rite described here is unusually brief in the sūtra's own telling — worship and repentance are named in a single line — but he reconstructs the fuller eightfold ritual pattern standard to Mahāyāna liturgy that this passage compresses: making offerings, praising the Buddha, prostration, repentance, entreating the Buddha to remain and teach, rejoicing in others' merit, dedicating merit, and making vows. Only worship and repentance are stated outright here, he says, but the other six are implicitly carried along with them, since the sūtra elsewhere insists such practice must be genuinely complete rather than perfunctory. He is careful about "on encountering favorable signs" — some vision or experience arising during the three weeks of repentance — reading it strictly as evidence that obstruction is being cleared, never as a goal to chase for its own sake, which is precisely the caution the chapter will repeat, with mounting insistence, in every section that follows.

A Bodhisattva's Own Summer Retreat

Chinese Source

「若經夏首，三月安居，當為清淨菩薩止住，心離聲聞，不假徒眾。至安居日，即於佛前作如是言：『我比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷某甲，踞菩薩乘，修寂滅行，同入清淨實相住持，以大圓覺為我伽藍，身心安居平等性智，涅槃自性無繫屬故。今我敬請不依聲聞，當與十方如來及大菩薩三月安居，為修菩薩無上妙覺大因緣故，不繫徒眾。』善男子！此名菩薩示現安居，過三期日隨往無礙。善男子！若彼末世修行眾生求菩薩道入三期者，非彼所聞一切境界，終不可取。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"If the beginning of summer arrives during this retreat, and the traditional three-month summer retreat falls due, one should keep it as a pure bodhisattva dwelling apart, the mind set apart from the śrāvaka's own manner of retreat, not bound by dependence on a fixed community. On the day the retreat begins, one should say, before the Buddha, these very words: 'I, bhikṣu, bhikṣuṇī, upāsaka, or upāsikā, named so-and-so, taking my stand on the bodhisattva vehicle, cultivate the practice of quiescent extinction, entering together into the abiding maintenance of pure true reality — taking great Perfect Enlightenment itself as my own monastery, my body and mind resting in the wisdom of the nature of equality, since the very nature of nirvāṇa is bound to nothing at all. I now respectfully request, without relying on the śrāvaka's own manner of retreat, to keep this three-month retreat together with the tathāgatas and great bodhisattvas of the ten directions, for the sake of the great causes and conditions of cultivating a bodhisattva's unsurpassed, wondrous awakening — and so I am not bound to any fixed community.' Good sons: this is called a bodhisattva's manner of manifesting a retreat. Once the three set periods have passed, one may go about freely, without hindrance. Good sons: if practitioners of the final age who seek the bodhisattva path enter one of these three periods, then whatever experience arises that was not already named in this teaching must, without exception, never be taken as a genuine attainment."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi lists eight specific differences between this bodhisattva retreat and the traditional monastic summer retreat it deliberately departs from, most turning on the same axis: the śrāvaka's retreat depends on a fixed physical boundary and a fixed community, requiring formal acknowledgment if a monk must leave that boundary even briefly, while the bodhisattva's retreat here is grounded instead in Perfect Enlightenment itself as its true monastery, so leaving a physical location does not break it the way leaving a monastic boundary would break the traditional observance. He is careful to frame this as a genuine departure permitted specifically to practitioners already engaged in the sūtra's own three-period retreat, not a general license for any Mahāyāna practitioner to disregard the traditional summer retreat at will — the departure is warranted here because it is this deeper, mind-grounded retreat, already underway, that the traditional boundary-based one would otherwise interrupt.

On the closing warning — "whatever experience arises that was not already named in this teaching must never be taken as genuine attainment" — Zongmi reads this as the single most important safeguard in the entire chapter, repeated, as it will be, after every one of the three methods described next. Intensive retreat practice reliably produces unusual states, visions, and sensations of every kind, and a practitioner eager for confirmation of progress is exactly the practitioner most vulnerable to mistaking one of these for a genuine sign of realization. The instruction is not a claim that such experiences never occur, but a strict rule for how to relate to them: only what this sūtra itself has already named as a genuine mark of progress may be trusted as one — everything else, however striking, is simply noted and set aside.

A First Approach to Each of the Three Methods

Chinese Source

「善男子！若諸眾生修奢摩他，先取至靜，不起思念，靜極便覺。如是初靜，從於一身至一世界，覺亦如是。善男子！若覺遍滿一世界者，一世界中有一眾生起一念者皆悉能知，百千世界，亦復如是；非彼所聞一切境界，終不可取。

「善男子！若諸眾生修三摩鉢提，先當憶想十方如來、十方世界一切菩薩，依種種門漸次修行，勤苦三昧，廣發大願，自熏成種；非彼所聞一切境界，終不可取。

「善男子！若諸眾生修於禪那，先取數門，心中了知生住滅念，分齊頭數，如是周遍四威儀中，分別念數，無不了知，漸次增進，乃至得知百千世界一滴之雨，猶如目覩所受用物；非彼所聞一切境界，終不可取。是名三觀初首方便。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: if sentient beings cultivate śamatha, they should first take up utmost stillness, giving rise to no discursive thought — and once stillness is brought to its fullest, awareness itself appears. This initial stillness spreads from one's own single body outward to encompass a whole world, and awareness spreads in exactly the same way. Good sons: once this awareness fully pervades one whole world, then, within that world, whenever even a single sentient being gives rise to a single thought, one is able to know it entirely; and this holds equally for a hundred thousand worlds as well — yet whatever experience arises that was not already named in this teaching must, without exception, never be taken as a genuine attainment.

"Good sons: if sentient beings cultivate samāpatti, they should first call to mind the tathāgatas of the ten directions and every bodhisattva throughout the worlds of the ten directions, and, relying on every kind of gate, gradually cultivate practice, exerting themselves diligently in samādhi, broadly giving rise to great vows, so that these become, through their own repeated habituation, a settled seed within the mind — yet whatever experience arises that was not already named in this teaching must, without exception, never be taken as a genuine attainment.

"Good sons: if sentient beings cultivate dhyāna, they should first take up the practice of counting, clearly knowing in the mind the thoughts of arising, abiding, and ceasing, distinguishing their exact number one by one, and, continuing this in just the same way through all four postures of daily life, discerning and knowing the count of every thought without exception, one gradually advances further and further, until one comes to know even a single drop of rain falling somewhere among a hundred thousand worlds, as clearly as an object seen directly with one's own eyes — yet whatever experience arises that was not already named in this teaching must, without exception, never be taken as a genuine attainment. These, then, are the first approaches to the three contemplations."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads all three of these opening instructions as deliberately basic, meant for a practitioner taking up a given method for the very first time rather than as the full unfolding of any of the three gates described in Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery's chapter — this is, in his words, simply "the first approach," the initial doorway rather than the completed practice. For śamatha, he notes the progression from stillness within one's own body outward to a whole world, and then to countless worlds, is not meant as a literal, sequential achievement to strive after, but describes what naturally happens as stillness itself deepens — the scope of awareness widening in step with how thoroughly discursive thought has actually settled, not as a separate goal pursued for its own sake. For samāpatti, he notes the instruction is comparatively brief precisely because engagement with the world through illusion-based compassionate activity, fully worked out, is inherently open-ended and cannot be reduced to a fixed sequence of stages the way stillness or counting can — here the "first approach" is simply establishing the habit of vow and visualization that later, fuller practice will build on. For dhyāna, the breath-and-thought counting method he treats as the most concretely trainable of the three, tracking not the breath alone but the arising, abiding, and ceasing of thought itself moment by moment — a discipline of attention that, carried far enough, is said to yield the sūtra's most vivid image of expanded perception, a single raindrop known clearly among a hundred thousand worlds.

He treats the fourfold repetition of "whatever experience arises that was not already named in this teaching must never be taken as a genuine attainment" — once after each of the three methods and again in the closing summary — as entirely deliberate rather than redundant: a beginning practitioner is at their most vulnerable to over-interpreting an early, striking experience precisely because they have the least accumulated judgment to weigh it against, which is exactly why the warning needed restating after every single method rather than being stated once at the chapter's opening and trusted to carry through.

When a Method Doesn't Take: Repent, and Try Another

Chinese Source

「若諸眾生遍修三種，勤行精進，即名如來出現于世。若後末世鈍根眾生，心欲求道，不得成就，由昔業障，當勤懺悔，常起希望，先斷憎、愛、嫉妬、諂曲，求勝上心，三種淨觀隨學一事，此觀不得，復習彼觀，心不放捨，漸次求證。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"If sentient beings cultivate all three methods fully and completely, diligently exerting themselves with vigor, this alone is what is called the Tathāgata's own appearance in the world. But if, in the later final age, a dull-natured sentient being sincerely wishes to seek the path and yet cannot succeed, this is because of karmic obstruction from the past — such a person should diligently repent, constantly giving rise to genuine hope, first cutting off hatred, craving, envy, and flattery, and seeking a mind set on what is truly excellent. Taking up the three pure contemplations, one should study just one of them at a time; if this particular contemplation does not take hold, one should then practice a different one instead — never abandoning the mind in discouragement, but seeking realization gradually, step by step."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads "if this particular contemplation does not take hold, practice a different one instead" as this chapter's own practical concession to exactly the situation Eloquent Voice's twenty-five wheels were designed for in principle but which a single practitioner, working alone without the ritual drawing of lots, must now navigate on their own: not every method suits every temperament, and a practitioner of duller capacity in a difficult period should not treat repeated failure with one method as a verdict on their own capacity for practice altogether, but as information about fit, prompting a considered switch rather than either forced persistence or discouraged abandonment. "Never abandoning the mind in discouragement" he reads as the chapter's genuinely final instruction, closing a section built almost entirely of concrete practical structure with the one thing no structure can supply on its own — the sustained will to keep going.

In Verse

Chinese Source

圓覺汝當知，　一切諸眾生，
欲求無上道，　先當結三期，
懺悔無始業。

經於三七日，　然後正思惟，
非彼所聞境，　畢竟不可取。

奢摩他至靜，　三摩正憶持，
禪那明數門，　是名三淨觀。

若能勤修習，　是名佛出世。

鈍根未成者，　常當勤心懺，
無始一切罪，　諸障若銷滅，
佛境便現前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Perfect Enlightenment, you should know:
every sentient being
who wishes to seek the unsurpassed path
should first set a fixed period of retreat,
repenting every beginningless karmic act.

Once the three weeks of seven days have passed,
only then should one turn to right reflection —
whatever state is not already found in this teaching
must, in the end, never be taken up.

Śamatha is utmost stillness;
samāpatti is right, sustained recollection;
dhyāna is the clear practice of the counting gate —
these, together, are called the three pure contemplations.

If one is able to diligently cultivate them,
this alone is called the Buddha's own appearance in the world.

One of dull capacity who has not yet succeeded
should constantly, diligently repent
every beginningless offense —
and once every obstruction is thereby dissolved,
the Buddha's own realm appears directly before them.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes the verse omits the bodhisattva's own summer-retreat instructions entirely, along with the practical advice to switch methods when one fails to take hold — both, he says, being matters of concrete procedure rather than doctrine, and therefore judged unnecessary to restate in verse form once the prose has already set them out in full. He treats the verse's closing image — the Buddha's own realm appearing directly before a dull-natured practitioner once obstruction has genuinely dissolved — as this chapter's own quiet reminder that the entire elaborate structure of retreat periods, ritual repentance, and graduated method exists for exactly this purpose, and no other: not accumulating unusual experience, but clearing away what has been obstructing a reality that, per every chapter before this one, was never actually absent to begin with.

Part 11 Complete
