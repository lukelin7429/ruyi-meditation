The Sky Never Had a Flower In It

This page opens Ru-Yi's translation of the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment (大方廣圓覺修多羅了義經), translated into Chinese by Buddhatrāta at the White Horse Monastery around 693. It carries the sūtra's opening assembly and Mañjuśrī's question — the first of twelve bodhisattvas who will each ask one question of the Buddha in turn — answered with the dhāraṇī-gate called Perfect Enlightenment, from which all buddhas arise, and the sūtra's central image for what ignorance actually is: a flower seen only by a diseased eye, in a sky that never held one.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 1 of 12 complete Source: Taishō T0842, 圓覺經 (tr. Buddhatrāta) Reading notes after Zongmi's 圓覺經略疏 (T1795)

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Buddhatrāta's translation, traditionally dated to the Tang dynasty (circa 693 CE) though its exact date went unrecorded even by the earliest catalogues. No Sanskrit or Tibetan version of this sūtra has ever surfaced, and most modern scholarship treats it as very likely composed in China rather than translated from an Indic original — a judgment that has never dimmed its influence: it became one of the most heavily studied scriptures in Chinese Buddhism, central to Chan, Huayan, and Tiantai alike. This collection reads it with the commentary of Guifeng Zongmi (圭峰宗密, 780–841), fifth patriarch of the Huayan school and, at the same time, an heir of Heze Shenhui's line of Southern Chan — a double inheritance that shapes the commentary throughout. Zongmi wrote several commentaries on this sūtra at different lengths; this collection follows his shorter commentary, the Yuanjue Jing Lüeshu (圓覺經略疏, T1795), the version he himself calls the more accessible one, written to be paired directly with the sūtra rather than studied as an independent treatise. Zongmi's own preface to the sūtra records that he was converted by this very text as a young monk, weeping partway through a single reading of it at a lay believer's house — his teacher told him afterward, "you must broadly propagate the teaching of the perfect and sudden; this sūtra itself entrusted it to you." Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right. See also this site's note on translation method.

The Assembly Gathers

Chinese Source

如是我聞：一時，婆伽婆入於神通大光明藏，三昧正受，一切如來光嚴住持，是諸眾生清淨覺地；身心寂滅平等本際，圓滿十方不二隨順，於不二境現諸淨土。與大菩薩摩訶薩十萬人俱，其名曰文殊師利菩薩、普賢菩薩、普眼菩薩、金剛藏菩薩、彌勒菩薩、清淨慧菩薩、威德自在菩薩、辯音菩薩、淨諸業障菩薩、普覺菩薩、圓覺菩薩、賢善首菩薩等，而為上首；與諸眷屬皆入三昧，同住如來平等法會。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard. At one time the Bhagavān entered the great treasury of light of spiritual power, abiding in samādhi. This is the ground of pure awakening shared by every sentient being, in which all tathāgatas dwell, upheld in radiant splendor; where body and mind, utterly still, rest in one and the same ultimate limit; where the ten directions stand fulfilled in a single, non-dual accord — and where, within that non-dual state, every pure land appears. He was together with a great assembly of one hundred thousand bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, foremost among them Mañjuśrī, Samantabhadra, Universal Eye, Vajragarbha, Maitreya, Pure Wisdom, Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery, Eloquent Voice, Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances, Universal Enlightenment, Perfect Enlightenment, and Worthy and Wholesome Foremost. Together with their whole retinues, all had entered samādhi, dwelling as one within the Tathāgata's assembly of equality.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads the "great treasury of light of spiritual power" (神通大光明藏) as another name for the same reality the Awakening of Faith calls mind-as-suchness: "the treasury is the treasury of the dharma-nature of the Ratnagotra — it is the source of both buddhas and sentient beings, the very substance of spiritual power and light." He adds that this is also called "the land of dharma-nature" and "the land of eternal quiescent light," and that the Buddha's "entering" it names nothing more than the cessation of discrimination — wisdom and principle fused into one, mind and object no longer two. The sūtra opens in a sambhogakāya-style setting, not the usual grove or city where most sūtras are set, because — on Zongmi's reading — this whole scripture is spoken from within that fused state rather than about it from outside.

On the twelve names in the roster, Zongmi supplies an etymology for each, and read together they work as a table of contents for the whole sūtra: Mañjuśrī ("wonderful and auspicious") asks the question that establishes right faith and understanding, so he is given the first question; Samantabhadra ("pervasive in essence, virtuous by according with conditions") asks about practice, matching action to that understanding; Universal Eye's chapter yields a direct, all-pervading vision of purity, "great wisdom's universal eye" fused with "great compassion's universal eye"; Vajragarbha is named for the vajra's twin qualities of hardness (nothing can destroy it) and sharpness (it can cut through anything) — fitting a chapter that raises three formidable doubts and dissolves every one; Maitreya digs out the root of desire, the subtlest affliction, matching a bodhisattva one lifetime from buddhahood; Pure Wisdom's chapter concerns the stages of realization, and her wisdom "does not dwell in or cling to" any of them; Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery corresponds to a chapter where the three methods of practice are perfected and demons can no longer disturb the practitioner; Eloquent Voice — "one Buddha-voice reaches every kind of being," and this bodhisattva can discern the single sound as it lands, unified or combined, in twenty-five different ways; Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances asks the question that dissolves the four subtle marks of self; Universal Enlightenment addresses the four remaining sicknesses of practice once those marks are gone; Perfect Enlightenment is named last among the twelve questioners because, Zongmi says, by that point in the sūtra "the theory is already perfect and the practice already complete" — the name itself marks the return of every partial teaching back into the whole; and Worthy and Wholesome Foremost, whose name means one who accords with principle and stands at the head of every wholesome thing, asks the sūtra's closing question about its own transmission, fittingly presiding over the section on how the teaching is to be spread. See this collection's index for how each bodhisattva's chapter maps onto this site's twelve parts.

Mañjuśrī's Question, and the Gate All Buddhas Flow From

Chinese Source

於是文殊師利菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右遶三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！願為此會諸來法眾，說於如來本起清淨因地法行，及說菩薩於大乘中發清淨心，遠離諸病，能使未來末世眾生求大乘者不墮邪見。」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告文殊師利菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能為諸菩薩諮詢如來因地法行，及為末世一切眾生求大乘者，得正住持不墮邪見。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，文殊師利菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

「善男子！無上法王有大陀羅尼門，名為圓覺，流出一切清淨真如菩提涅槃及波羅蜜教授菩薩。一切如來本起因地，皆依圓照清淨覺相，永斷無明方成佛道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Mañjuśrī rose from his seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — I ask that you explain, for the whole assembly of the Dharma gathered here, the pure practice of the causal ground from which the Tathāgata originally arose, and explain how bodhisattvas give rise to a pure mind within the Mahāyāna and leave every sickness far behind, so that sentient beings of the future, of the final age, who seek the Mahāyāna, may not fall into wrong views." Having said this, he prostrated himself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Mañjuśrī: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata, on behalf of the bodhisattvas, about the practice of the causal ground, and, for the sake of every sentient being of the final age who seeks the Mahāyāna, to secure right abiding so that they do not fall into wrong views. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Mañjuśrī, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

"Good sons: the unsurpassed Dharma-king has a great dhāraṇī-gate named Perfect Enlightenment, from which flow all pure suchness, bodhi, nirvāṇa, and the pāramitās, teaching the bodhisattvas. Every tathāgata's original arising, in the causal ground, depends on the complete, luminous illumination of the pure mark of awakening, and on permanently cutting off ignorance — only in this way is the path to buddhahood accomplished.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi glosses dhāraṇī (陀羅尼) as "total holding" — traditionally divided into three kinds, holding by many syllables, by one syllable, or by no syllable at all, and this one, he says, is the wordless kind: the substance of Perfect Enlightenment already holds within it every quality there is, "from the very beginning, holding them without loss." On "gate" (門) he insists this is not an ordinary threshold with a shallow room on one side and a deep one on the other — quoting the Heze teaching that "the single word knowing is the gate of every wonder" — but a gate with two simultaneous senses: outflow, since every defiled and pure dharma issues from it, and inflow, since to fully awaken to its substance and function is to awaken into all of them at once. To trace things from root to branch is outflow; to gather branch back into root is inflow. To be deluded is, in this sense, already an outflow from the gate; to awaken is to pass back through it.

On "flow out" itself, Zongmi is careful to block a possible misreading: this does not mean some separate thing named suchness, bodhi, nirvāṇa, or the pāramitās travels outward from Perfect Enlightenment the way water flows from a spring. He quotes the Awakening of Faith directly — already translated in full on this site — for the parallel structure: "if the mind has movement, then there are defiled qualities as numerous as the sands of the Ganges; against this same fact, when the mind's nature is unmoved, there are equally countless pure qualities displayed." Suchness, bodhi, nirvāṇa, and the six perfections are not exported from Perfect Enlightenment to somewhere else — they are what its own display looks like, described from the near side. See this site's Awakening of Faith collection for the treatise this note draws from.

What Is Ignorance? A Flower Seen Only by a Sick Eye

Chinese Source

「云何無明？善男子！一切眾生從無始來種種顛倒，猶如迷人四方易處，妄認四大為自身相，六塵緣影為自心相；譬彼病目見空中花及第二月。善男子！空實無花，病者妄執。

由妄執故，非唯惑此虛空自性，亦復迷彼實花生處，由此妄有輪轉生死，故名無明。善男子！此無明者非實有體，如夢中人夢時非無，及至於醒了無所得；如眾空花滅於虛空，不可說言有定滅處，何以故？無生處故。一切眾生於無生中，妄見生滅，是故說名輪轉生死。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is ignorance? Good sons — all sentient beings, from beginningless time, have been caught in every kind of inverted view, like a person who has lost his bearings and taken one direction for another: they falsely take the four great elements to be the marks of their own body, and the conditioned shadow of the six sense-objects to be the marks of their own mind — just as a person with diseased eyes sees flowers in the empty sky, or sees a second moon. Good sons: the sky in truth has no flowers in it at all; it is the sick man who falsely clings to them.

Because of this false clinging, he is confused not only about the true nature of empty space itself, but is also deluded about the actual place from which the flower arose. Because of this, he falsely comes to have the cycle of birth-and-death — and this is what is called ignorance. Good sons: this ignorance has no real substance at all. It is like a person in a dream — while the dream lasts, its people are not absent, but on waking, nothing at all can be found. Or it is like the flowers of empty space vanishing back into empty space: one cannot say there is any fixed place they vanished to. Why is this? Because they had no place of arising to begin with. All sentient beings, within what was never born, falsely perceive birth and cessation — and it is for this reason that it is called the cycle of birth-and-death."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi parses "falsely taking the four elements as self, and the six dusts' shadow as mind" into four paired inversions, each pairing a delusion about the body with a delusion about the mind: taking the non-self four elements to be a self is inversion one, while the true dharma-body — which actually is the self — goes unrecognized; taking the illusory, borrowed four elements to be real is inversion two, while the truly real dharma-body is taken for nothing; taking the arising-and-ceasing conditioned mind (緣念) to be the true mind is inversion three, while the true mind, though perfectly evident, goes unacknowledged; and taking the black shape that appears when a pearl reflects a dark object to be something real is inversion four, while the pearl's own bright nature — which is actually present — is taken for absent. Two of the four are the self-grasping of the "person" (我執), two are the grasping at "dharmas" (法執); ordinary beings fall into all four without any teacher's instruction, purely by habit, which is why the sūtra calls them "every kind" of inversion.

On the sky-flower simile, Zongmi flags a detail easy to read past: the text says the deluded man "is also confused about the actual place the flower arose from" (亦復迷彼實花生處) — and he insists this does not mean the flower has some real birthplace after all. The man has already assumed the flower grew out of the sky itself; what he fails to notice is that it is the disease in his own eye, not the sky, that is the flower's actual origin. The sky supplies no flowers, ever — a diseased eye merely makes it look as though it does. This distinction matters beyond the metaphor: it blocks a subtle wrong reading in which awakening or emptiness could itself be said to give rise to ignorance, a doubt the sūtra will confront directly, and by name, in Vajragarbha's chapter a few pages ahead — where the very question "if sentient beings are originally buddhas, why does ignorance ever arise" gets asked, and answered with this same distinction.

The Fourfold Release, and Why the Tathāgatagarbha Is Never Moved

Chinese Source

「善男子！如來因地修圓覺者，知是空花，即無輪轉，亦無身心受彼生死，非作故無，本性無故。彼知覺者猶如虛空，知虛空者即空花相，亦不可說無知覺性。有、無俱遣，是則名為淨覺隨順。

何以故？虛空性故，常不動故，如來藏中無起滅故，無知見故，如法界性究竟圓滿遍十方故；是則名為因地法行。菩薩因此於大乘中發清淨心，末世眾生依此修行不墮邪見。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: a tathāgata who, in the causal ground, cultivates Perfect Enlightenment knows that this is a flower in the sky — and so there is, for him, no cycle of birth-and-death at all, nor any body or mind that undergoes that birth-and-death. This is not because he has, through some act, made it not exist; it is because, by its very nature, it was never there to begin with.

That which knows this awareness is itself just like empty space; and that which knows empty space is itself only the mark of a sky-flower — and yet one also cannot say there is no nature of awareness at all. Both existence and non-existence are set aside together, and this is what is called being in accord with pure awakening.

Why is this? Because it has the nature of empty space; because it is forever unmoving; because within the tathāgatagarbha there is no arising and no ceasing; because it is without dualistic knowledge and views; because, like the very nature of the dharma-realm, it is ultimately complete and pervades the ten directions. This, then, is what is called the practice of the causal ground. It is by this that bodhisattvas give rise to a pure mind within the Mahāyāna, and by this that sentient beings of the final age may practice it and not fall into wrong views."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads the run of four short clauses here — "that which knows this awareness is itself like empty space… one also cannot say there is no nature of awareness" — as a deliberate fourfold retreat, each clause dismantling the position the previous one might tempt a reader to settle into. First, the wisdom that knows body and mind are empty is itself dismissed — do not reify even that knowing into a possession. Second, the very act of dismissing it is dismissed in turn — "that which knows empty space is itself only a flower-mark" — pull back the one doing the pulling-back. Third, the retreat is stopped from sliding into nihilism — "one cannot say there is no nature of awareness" — this is not a claim that nothing is there. Fourth, existence and non-existence are set aside together, and only then does the text call this "accord with pure awakening." Zongmi's own gloss: "once existence and non-existence no longer apply to the mind, the path of language itself is cut off, and the substance of pure awakening appears of itself from this" — not as a further achievement layered on top, but simply as what remains once the four moves are complete.

On the closing string of reasons — space-nature, unmoving, no arising-and-ceasing in the tathāgatagarbha, no dualistic knowing, and complete like the dharma-realm — Zongmi maps the first two onto what the Śrīmālā Sūtra calls the "empty tathāgatagarbha" (空如來藏, empty of every defilement) and the last three onto its "not-empty tathāgatagarbha" (不空如來藏, replete with qualities beyond the sands of the Ganges) — the identical empty/not-empty structure already translated in this site's Awakening of Faith collection, where the "greatness of attributes" names this same not-empty aspect. He also glosses tathāgatagarbha (如來藏) itself by its three classical senses — concealment, since it hides the tathāgata within the ordinary being who has not yet found it; inclusion, since the dharma-body's qualities, and all sentient beings besides, are held within it; and generation, since realizing what is already held within it is what allows those qualities to be brought forth. All three senses, he notes, describe a single reality seen from three angles — a mark of continuity this collection will keep returning to as later chapters build on it.

In Verse

Chinese Source

文殊汝當知，　一切諸如來，
從於本因地，　皆以智慧覺，
了達於無明。　知彼如空花，
即能免流轉，　又如夢中人，
醒時不可得。　覺者如虛空，
平等不動轉，　覺遍十方界，
即得成佛道。　眾幻滅無處，
成道亦無得，　本性圓滿故。

菩薩於此中，　能發菩提心，
末世諸眾生，　修此免邪見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Mañjuśrī, you should know:
every tathāgata,
from the original causal ground,
through the awakening of wisdom
fully penetrated ignorance.
Knowing it to be like a flower in the sky,
they were thereby freed from the turning wheel —
and, like a person in a dream,
on waking, nothing at all is found.
The awakened is like empty space,
equal, unmoving;
awakening pervades the ten directions —
this is what it means to accomplish the path to buddhahood.
The host of illusions vanishes, with no place to vanish to;
accomplishing the path is likewise without any attainment,
because the fundamental nature was already complete and full.

Bodhisattvas, within this,
are able to give rise to the mind of awakening;
sentient beings of the final age,
cultivating this, are freed from wrong views.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Every one of the sūtra's twelve chapters closes this same way, with the Buddha "wishing to state this meaning again" in verse — a pattern Zongmi identifies by its formal name, gāthā of the geya type, verse that restates a preceding prose passage rather than introducing new content on its own. He tracks, chapter by chapter, exactly which prose clauses a given verse compresses, expands, or silently drops; here he notes only that the verse's closing couplet — bodhisattvas giving rise to bodhicitta, and beings of the final age thereby escaping wrong views — restates the chapter's own closing line without adding anything further. Later chapters, particularly Vajragarbha's and Mañjuśrī's counterparts among the middle nine, use the verse to compress material the prose treats at much greater length; this collection's reading notes flag it wherever Zongmi finds something worth remarking on in the compression itself.

Part 1 Complete

This closes Part 1: the opening assembly, Mañjuśrī's question, and the Buddha's answer — Perfect Enlightenment as the dhāraṇī-gate from which all buddhas arise, ignorance as a sky-flower with no real birthplace, and the fourfold release that ends in "neither existence nor non-existence," identified with the tathāgatagarbha itself.
