The Three Gates of Practice

Six chapters have established what Perfect Enlightenment is and why every being already has it. Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery now asks the question the sūtra has so far only gestured toward: concretely, what are the actual methods of practice, and how many are there? The answer names exactly three — śamatha, samāpatti, and dhyāna — each anchored to its own governing image, and closes with the claim that a single instant according with this teaching outweighs converting a hundred thousand myriad beings to arhatship.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 7 of 12 complete Source: Taishō T0842, 圓覺經 (tr. Buddhatrāta) Reading notes after Zongmi's 圓覺經略疏 (T1795)

Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery's Question

Chinese Source

於是威德自在菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右遶三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！廣為我等分別如是隨順覺性，令諸菩薩覺心光明承佛圓音，不因修習而得善利。世尊！譬如大城外有四門，隨方來者非止一路，一切菩薩莊嚴佛國及成菩提非一方便。唯願世尊！廣為我等宣說一切方便漸次，并修行人總有幾種？令此會菩薩及末世眾生求大乘者，速得開悟，遊戲如來大寂滅海。」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告威德自在菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能為諸菩薩及末世眾生，問於如來如是方便。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，威德自在菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery rose from his seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — you have broadly distinguished for us this accord with the nature of awakening, so that the bodhisattvas' minds of awakening, receiving the Buddha's own perfect voice, are made luminous, gaining good benefit even apart from cultivation itself. World-Honored One: it is like a great city with four gates on its outside — those who approach from different directions do not all take a single road. In just the same way, no single skillful means alone lets every bodhisattva adorn a buddha-land and accomplish bodhi. I ask only, World-Honored One, that you broadly proclaim for us every skillful means and its gradual steps — and altogether, how many kinds of practitioner are there? — so that the bodhisattvas of this assembly, and sentient beings of the final age who seek the Mahāyāna, may swiftly attain awakening, and roam freely upon the Tathāgata's great ocean of ultimate quiescence." Having said this, he prostrated himself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata, on behalf of the bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age, about skillful means of exactly this kind. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads the four-gated city as making a precise, limited claim, not a vague gesture toward pluralism: someone approaching from the east cannot simply walk in through the south gate, yet all four gates open onto the identical city inside. The differing methods of practice, in the same way, are genuinely differently suited to differing practitioners — a method fit for one person's temperament is not interchangeable with a method fit for another's — and yet all of them, without exception, terminate in the identical Perfect Enlightenment. This chapter, he notes, is where the sūtra turns from establishing what awakening is to actually supplying the promised methods, and Majestic Virtue Self-Mastery's own name suits the transition: this bodhisattva corresponds to the stage at which the three methods about to be taught have already been perfected and demonic obstruction can no longer disturb the practitioner — majestic virtue describes the perfected power itself, self-mastery describes what it is like to no longer be swayed by what would have disturbed an earlier stage.

Śamatha — Taking Stillness As the Practice

Chinese Source

「善男子！無上妙覺遍諸十方，出生如來，與一切法同體平等，於諸修行實無有二。方便隨順其數無量，圓攝所歸，循性差別，當有三種。

「善男子！若諸菩薩悟淨圓覺，以淨覺心，取靜為行；由澄諸念，覺識煩動，靜慧發生，身心客塵從此永滅，便能內發寂靜輕安；由寂靜故，十方世界諸如來心於中顯現，如鏡中像。此方便者名奢摩他。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: the unsurpassed, wondrous awakening pervades every direction, giving rise to the tathāgatas, sharing one and the same substance with every dharma, entirely equal — with respect to actual practice, there is truly no duality in it at all. Yet the skillful means that accord with it are without number; gathered together and drawn to their single source, and sorted according to differing dispositions, they come to exactly three kinds.

"Good sons: if a bodhisattva, having awakened to pure Perfect Enlightenment, uses that pure awakened mind and takes stillness as the practice — then, by settling every thought, one becomes aware of the agitated movement of consciousness itself; a stillness-born wisdom arises, and the guest-like dust of body and mind is, from this point on, permanently extinguished. One is thereby able to bring forth an inward stillness and ease — and because of this stillness, the minds of the tathāgatas of every world in the ten directions appear within it, exactly like reflections appearing in a mirror. This skillful means is called śamatha."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi treats this chapter's three methods as corresponding closely, though not identically, to the Awakening of Faith's own single practice of "true suchness samādhi" — the difference being that the treatise does not divide that one samādhi into three the way this sūtra does. He identifies each method's structure as running through three stages every time: undertaking the practice, the practice bearing fruit, and a corresponding response — and for śamatha, the fruit is a "stillness-born wisdom," the response is the inward feeling of ease that follows genuine stillness, and the mirror simile names what happens once that stillness is fully settled: the practitioner's own mind, quieted, becomes capable of reflecting the tathāgatas the way a still lake reflects the moon, not because the tathāgatas move toward the practitioner, but because a mind no longer agitated by its own waves can, for the first time, actually show what was always there to be shown.

Samāpatti — Illusion Rising to Open Illusory Beings

Chinese Source

「善男子！若諸菩薩悟淨圓覺，以淨覺心，知覺心性及與根塵皆因幻化，即起諸幻以除幻者，變化諸幻而開幻眾；由起幻故便能內發大悲輕安，一切菩薩從此起行，漸次增進。彼觀幻者非同幻故，非同幻觀皆是幻故，幻相永離；是諸菩薩所圓妙行，如土長苗。此方便者名三摩鉢提。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: if a bodhisattva, having awakened to pure Perfect Enlightenment, uses that pure awakened mind, and, knowing that the very nature of the discerning mind, together with the sense-faculties and sense-objects, are all alike caused by illusion — thereupon gives rise to illusion in order to remove illusion, transforming and manifesting every kind of illusion in order to open illusory beings to the truth. Because illusion has been given rise to in this way, one is able to bring forth an inward great compassion and ease, and every bodhisattva, starting from this, takes up this practice and gradually advances further. That which observes illusion is not itself identical to illusion — and since it is not identical to illusion, the observing itself is likewise still illusion — so illusory marks are permanently left behind. This is the wondrous, perfected practice of these bodhisattvas, like a sprout growing from soil. This skillful means is called samāpatti."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi unpacks the doubled "illusion" logic here as answering a question this same chapter's method necessarily raises: if the discerning mind, the sense-faculties, and sense-objects are all illusion, how can any of them be trusted to act at all? His answer: precisely because they are illusion rather than real, they can be used without contradiction — one illusion (the practitioner's cultivated skillful means) removing another illusion (the affliction obstructing beings), the way a wooden puppet can still be used to strike another wooden puppet without either being anything more than wood. He reads "that which observes illusion is not itself identical to illusion" as itself immediately undercut by the clause that follows — "since it is not identical to illusion, the observing itself is likewise still illusion" — a compact, two-step move that prevents the observing wisdom from being mistaken for something genuinely real standing outside illusion altogether, matching the same fourfold-release logic already used in Mañjuśrī's and Samantabhadra's earlier chapters. On the growing-seedling simile, he glosses each element: the awakened mind is the seed, the illusion-based method is the soil, the compassionate action that results is the sprout — and once the harvest is gathered, both seed and soil, like the illusion-wisdom and the illusion-actions built on it, are together set aside.

Dhyāna — Neither Illusion Nor Stillness, The Absolute Mind

Chinese Source

「善男子！若諸菩薩悟淨圓覺，以淨覺心不取幻化及諸靜相，了知身心皆為罣礙；無知覺明不依諸礙，永得超過礙無礙境，受用世界及與身心，相在塵域；如器中鍠，聲出於外，煩惱涅槃不相留礙，便能內發寂滅輕安、妙覺隨順寂滅境界，自他身心所不能及，眾生壽命皆為浮想。此方便者名為禪那。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: if a bodhisattva, having awakened to pure Perfect Enlightenment, uses that pure awakened mind, and takes neither illusory transformation nor any mark of stillness, but clearly knows that both body and mind are alike obstructions — then, with a knowing awareness that is unconditioned by any of these obstructions, one need no longer depend on any of them at all, and permanently transcends both the realm of obstruction and the realm of no-obstruction. One's resources, one's world, and one's very body and mind all remain exactly where they are, situated within the same dusty domain as before — like the ringing of a bell inside its own casing, its sound still carrying outward — and affliction and nirvāṇa no longer obstruct one another in the slightest. One is thereby able to bring forth an inward stillness-and-extinction, ease, and the wondrous, awakened accord of quiescence itself — a state that neither one's own nor another's body and mind can reach, in which the very lifespan of sentient beings is seen to be nothing but a floating thought. This skillful means is called dhyāna."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads dhyāna as neither śamatha's stillness nor samāpatti's engagement with illusion, but a third position taking neither pole: not clinging to illusory activity, as the second method's own practitioner still does in a refined way, and not clinging to stillness either, as the first method's own practitioner still does. What remains once both are set down is a "knowing awareness" (無知覺明) that he takes pains to distinguish from mere blankness — this is not an absence of knowing, but a knowing that no longer depends on anything obstructive to know by, which is why body, mind, and world can all remain exactly as they were without the practitioner being caught by any of them.

On the bell-in-its-casing simile, Zongmi records some uncertainty about the exact character the source text intends — he considers three possible readings of a single ambiguous character, settling on the sense of a large bell's ringing tone — but is confident about what the simile as a whole illustrates regardless: a bell's casing and its ringing tone are two different things, the casing staying fixed in place while the sound travels freely outward, unconfined by the metal that produced it. In the same way, the practitioner's ordinary body, mind, and world remain visibly unchanged, exactly where they always were, while the observing wisdom itself moves freely, unconfined by any of them — which is why "affliction and nirvāṇa no longer obstruct one another": both terms name states of the casing, so to speak, while the wisdom itself has already passed beyond needing either term to apply.

Three Gates, One Perfect Enlightenment

Chinese Source

「善男子！此三法門皆是圓覺，親近隨順，十方如來因此成佛，十方菩薩種種方便，一切同異皆依如是三種事業，若得圓證即成圓覺。

「善男子！假使有人修於聖道，教化成就百千萬億阿羅漢、辟支佛果，不如有人聞此圓覺無礙法門，一剎那頃隨順修習。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: these three methods are all alike Perfect Enlightenment itself. Drawing near to it and according with it, the tathāgatas of the ten directions accomplish buddhahood by this very means; and every kind of skillful means used by bodhisattvas throughout the ten directions, however alike or different they may appear, all rely on these same three undertakings. If one attains perfect realization through them, this is itself the accomplishment of Perfect Enlightenment.

"Good sons: suppose there were a person who cultivated the sagely path and brought hundreds of thousands of myriads of koṭis of beings to the fruit of arhatship or pratyekabuddhahood through his teaching — this would still not equal a person who, hearing this unobstructed teaching of Perfect Enlightenment, accords with it and cultivates it for even the space of a single instant."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes that a bodhisattva need not practice all three methods in equal measure, or even practice each one in isolation — the very next chapter, Eloquent Voice's, exists precisely to work out how they can be combined, one at a time, two at a time, or all three together, into twenty-five distinct configurations. What matters, he stresses here, is only that whichever combination a given practitioner takes up, all three "undertakings" trace back to this same single Perfect Enlightenment, so genuine attainment through any configuration counts as genuine attainment of the whole. On the closing comparison — a single instant's accord with this teaching outweighing the conversion of countless beings to arhatship — he reads it, as in similar comparisons elsewhere in Mahāyāna literature, as measuring depth against breadth rather than devaluing the two-vehicle path itself: bringing beings to a bounded liberation, however vast in scale, remains bounded, while even a single moment's genuine accord with Perfect Enlightenment touches something the sūtra has insisted, from its opening chapter onward, has no boundary at all.

In Verse

Chinese Source

威德汝當知，　無上大覺心，
本際無二相，　隨於諸方便，
其數即無量。　如來總開示，
便有三種類：　寂靜奢摩他，
如鏡照諸像；

如幻三摩提，　如苗漸增長；

禪那唯寂滅，　如彼器中鍠。

三種妙法門，　皆是覺隨順，
十方諸如來，　及諸大菩薩，
因此得成道，　三事圓證故，
名究竟涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Majestic Virtue, you should know:
the unsurpassed mind of great awakening
has, at its very source, no mark of duality at all;
yet, according with every kind of skillful means,
its number is without measure.
The Tathāgata, opening this teaching in full,
thereby gives three kinds:
the stillness of śamatha,
like a mirror reflecting every image;

the illusion of samāpatti,
like a sprout gradually growing;

and dhyāna, which is stillness-and-extinction alone,
like the sound of that bell within its casing.

These three wondrous methods
are all alike accord with awakening.
The tathāgatas of the ten directions,
and every great bodhisattva,
accomplish the path by means of this;
and because all three undertakings are perfectly realized,
this is called ultimate nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes that the verse restates the three methods purely by their governing similes — mirror, sprout, bell — omitting the fuller three-stage structure (undertaking, fruit, response) the prose spelled out for each, and drops the closing merit-comparison entirely, ending instead on "ultimate nirvāṇa" as the plainest possible name for what all three methods, taken together, accomplish. He treats this chapter's verse as unusually tightly matched to its prose counterpart compared to earlier chapters, precisely because the three similes themselves are already about as compressed as the teaching can get without losing its content.
