The Four Marks of Self

The previous two chapters supplied methods; this one supplies a diagnostic. Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances asks why a mind whose nature is originally pure still feels obstructed even in practitioners who have made genuine progress — and the Buddha answers with four subtle marks of self, each subtler than the last, that can survive intact underneath even a sincere and well-developed practice, tested not by what a practitioner claims to believe but by how praise and blame still land.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 9 of 12 complete Source: Taishō T0842, 圓覺經 (tr. Buddhatrāta) Reading notes after Zongmi's 圓覺經略疏 (T1795)

Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances' Question

Chinese Source

於是淨諸業障菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右繞三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！為我等輩廣說如是不思議事一切如來因地行相，令諸大眾得未曾有，覩見調御歷恒沙劫勤苦境界，一切功用猶如一念，我等菩薩深自慶慰。世尊！若此覺心本性清淨，因何染污，使諸眾生迷悶不入？唯願如來廣為我等開悟法性，令此大眾及末世眾生作將來眼。」說是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告淨諸業障菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能為諸大眾及末世眾生，諮問如來如是方便。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，淨諸業障菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances rose from her seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — you have broadly explained, for our sake, this inconceivable matter, the very manner of every tathāgata's practice in the causal ground, letting this great assembly gain what it never had before, witnessing the Guide's own arduous striving across kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges compressed into what feels like a single thought — we bodhisattvas find ourselves deeply consoled by this. World-Honored One: if this awakened mind's original nature is pure, then because of what does it become defiled, leaving sentient beings confused and unable to enter it? I ask only that the Tathāgata broadly open up the nature of dharmas for our sake, so that this assembly, and sentient beings of the final age, may have an eye for what lies ahead." Having said this, she prostrated herself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata, on behalf of the great assembly and sentient beings of the final age, about skillful means of exactly this kind. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads this question as arising from a real gap the previous chapters have left open: a practitioner may already accept, intellectually, that awakening is originally pure and that the three methods are available in twenty-five configurations, and yet still feel, in practice, unable to enter it — a mismatch between doctrine held and awakening actually attained. This chapter's whole purpose, he says, is to dissolve every last trace of self hiding beneath that gap, which is why every subsection of the four marks that follow, however differently worded, carries the character "self" (我) running through it: the four marks are not four separate afflictions, but one single root of self-clinging examined at four successively subtler depths.

Why Purity Still Feels Blocked

Chinese Source

「善男子！一切眾生從無始來，妄想執有我、人、眾生及與壽命，認四顛倒為實我體，由此便生憎愛二境。於虛妄體重執虛妄，二妄相依生妄業道，有妄業故妄見流轉，厭流轉者妄見涅槃，由此不能入清淨覺。非覺違拒諸能入者，有諸能入，非覺入故。是故動念及與息念皆歸迷悶。何以故？由有無始本起無明為己主宰，一切眾生生無慧目，身心等性皆是無明，譬如有人不自斷命。是故當知！有愛我者我與隨順，非隨順者便生憎怨，為憎愛心養無明故，相續求道皆不成就。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: all sentient beings, from beginningless time, have falsely imagined and clung to the existence of a self, a person, sentient beings, and a lifespan, taking these four inverted views as the real substance of a self — and because of this, the two realms of hatred and craving arise. On top of an already unreal substance, they cling once more to what is unreal, and these two layers of falsehood, depending on one another, give rise to a false karmic path; because of this false karma, they falsely perceive themselves caught in the turning wheel, and, wearying of that turning, they falsely perceive nirvāṇa as its opposite. Because of all this, they are unable to enter pure awakening. It is not that awakening itself refuses or obstructs whoever would enter it — it is that whatever 'enters,' it is never awakening itself that does the entering. This is why both giving rise to thought and stilling thought alike still end in confusion. Why is this? Because a beginningless, fundamental ignorance still governs as one's own master, all sentient beings are born without the eye of wisdom, and the very nature of body and mind is itself, entirely, ignorance — just as a person, however miserable, will never bring himself to end his own life. You should therefore know: whoever accords with this sense of self is loved, and whoever does not is resented — and because this very mind of hatred and craving continually nourishes ignorance, seeking the path in reliance on it will, without exception, never succeed."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes that "it is not awakening that refuses whoever would enter it — it is that whatever enters is never awakening itself" directly answers this chapter's opening question: purity was never actually blocked from outside; the entering subject, so long as it is still a self trying to enter, is itself the obstruction, the way a dreaming person cannot literally walk from within the dream into the waking world no matter how hard the dream-body tries, because it is precisely the dream-body's own persistence that constitutes still being asleep. On "a person will never bring himself to end his own life," he reads this less literally than it first appears: it names the structural impossibility of self using itself to eliminate self, since ignorance's entire operation depends on there being a self it can govern as "its own master" — asking that self to dissolve itself is asking the very instrument of the problem to serve as its own solution, which is why the chapter's later sections will not ask the practitioner to try harder at eliminating self directly, but instead teach a diagnostic for noticing where it still, subtly, persists.

The Mark of Self, and the Mark of a Person

Chinese Source

「善男子！云何我相？謂諸眾生心所證者。善男子！譬如有人百骸調適，忽忘我身；四支弦緩，攝養乖方，微加鍼艾，則知有我。是故證取方現我體。善男子！其心乃至證於如來，畢竟了知清淨涅槃，皆是我相。

「善男子！云何人相？謂諸眾生心悟證者。善男子！悟有我者，不復認我，所悟非我，悟亦如是。悟已超過一切證者，悉為人相。善男子！其心乃至圓悟涅槃俱是我者，心存少悟，備殫證理，皆名人相。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: what is the mark of self? It is whatever a sentient being's mind takes as something realized. Good sons: it is like a person whose whole body is perfectly at ease, so much so that he forgets he has a body at all — but the moment his limbs grow slack, his care of himself goes even slightly out of balance, and a needle or a touch of moxa is applied, he immediately knows he has a self. It is precisely at the moment of taking hold of something as realized that the substance of self appears at all. Good sons: even a mind that has come to realize something as exalted as the Tathāgata himself, and thoroughly understands pure nirvāṇa itself — even this is still the mark of self.

"Good sons: what is the mark of a person? It is whatever a sentient being's mind awakens to as having realized something. Good sons: one who awakens to having a self no longer identifies with that self — what was awakened to is no longer taken as self, and the awakening itself works exactly the same way. Once this awakening has surpassed every mark of having realized something, this surpassing itself is entirely the mark of a person. Good sons: even a mind that takes perfectly awakening to nirvāṇa itself as still being a self — as long as the mind retains even the slightest trace of this awakening, fully exhausting every point of realization it can find — all of this is still called the mark of a person."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads the needle-and-moxa simile as making a precise diagnostic point: a self that goes entirely unnoticed under comfortable conditions is not thereby proven absent — it is simply undisturbed. Apply even a slight provocation and its presence becomes immediately obvious, "so it is only at the moment of taking hold of something as realized that the substance of self appears at all" — meaning the mark of self is not a feeling a practitioner carries around at all times, but something that surfaces specifically whenever there is something to grasp as an accomplishment, however subtle. This is why "even a mind that has come to realize something as exalted as the Tathāgata himself… is still the mark of self": the mark does not depend on the grandeur of what is realized, only on there still being a someone who did the realizing.

On the mark of a person, Zongmi notes the text is deliberately harder to parse than the mark of self, because it describes a subtler, second-order version of the identical problem: having already seen through the crude mistake of clinging to a realized self, a practitioner may then take quiet pride in that very seeing-through — "I am the one who is no longer fooled by self" — which is itself, unnoticed, a new and subtler self, formed around the very act of transcending the first one. He calls this "surpassing" itself the mark of a person precisely because it is built from having gotten somewhere past the previous mark, which is exactly what makes it so easy to miss: a practitioner who has genuinely progressed past the mark of self can mistake that very progress for having arrived at no-self at all.

The Mark of Sentient Beings, and the Mark of a Lifespan

Chinese Source

「善男子！云何眾生相？謂諸眾生心自證悟所不及者。善男子！譬如有人作如是言：『我是眾生。』則知彼人說眾生者非我非彼。云何非我？我是眾生，則非是我。云何非彼？我是眾生，非彼我故。善男子！但諸眾生了證了悟皆為我、人，而我、人相所不及者，存有所了，名眾生相。

「善男子！云何壽命相？謂諸眾生心照清淨覺所了者，一切業智所不自見，猶如命根。善男子！若心照見一切覺者，皆為塵垢；覺、所覺者不離塵故，如湯銷氷，無別有氷，知氷銷者，存我、覺我，亦復如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: what is the mark of sentient beings? It is whatever lies beyond the reach of a sentient being's own mind, even where that mind has already realized and awakened to a great deal. Good sons: it is like a person who says: 'I am a sentient being.' From this, one knows that what that person calls 'a sentient being' is neither the same as himself nor entirely other than himself. Why is it not himself? Because he himself is a sentient being, and so cannot simply be the 'I' making the statement. Why is it not entirely other? Because he says 'I am a sentient being' — not 'that other one is a sentient being.' Good sons: for as long as sentient beings are still fully realizing and fully awakening — which is precisely what the marks of self and of a person are — then whatever still lies beyond the reach of those marks of self and person, so long as there remains something still retained as having been understood, is called the mark of sentient beings.

"Good sons: what is the mark of a lifespan? It is whatever the mind's own clear awareness of pure awakening has understood, in a way that all karmic wisdom itself still fails to see, the way one cannot see one's own life-faculty directly, only its ongoing continuity. Good sons: if the mind clearly sees that every instance of awareness is itself still dust and defilement — since both the awareness and what that awareness is aware of never actually leave that dust behind — then it is exactly like hot water melting ice: there is no separate ice left over once it has melted, and yet the very awareness that knows 'the ice has melted' still, in just the same way, retains a self, an awareness of self."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads the "I am a sentient being" wordplay as illustrating an infinite regress that never quite closes: whatever residual sense of self a practitioner locates and names always turns out to be neither identical with the "I" doing the locating nor cleanly separable from it, because the very act of locating it creates a new vantage point standing just outside what was located — which is exactly the structure of the mark of sentient beings, "whatever still lies beyond the reach of the marks of self and person." He treats this mark as naming the residue that remains once the first two, cruder marks have both been seen through, and stresses it is defined purely relationally — it just is whatever the current level of realization has not yet caught, whatever that turns out to be — which is why it cannot be pinned down the way the first two marks could.

On the mark of a lifespan, Zongmi's gloss on the ice-and-water simile is the chapter's most precise formulation of how subtle this final mark actually is: melting eliminates the ice completely, with no residue of "ice-ness" left in the resulting water at all — and yet the very awareness that registers "the ice has melted" is itself still an act of a self observing an event, still implicitly assuming an "I" for whom the melting was noteworthy. This is the mark of a lifespan precisely because it functions the way a life-faculty does: never seen directly, only inferred from its own uninterrupted continuity, present in the very fact that awareness keeps arising, moment after moment, still framed around someone for whom it arises. Even the wisdom that has correctly diagnosed the first three marks, Zongmi notes, is not automatically exempt from carrying this fourth — which is exactly the difficulty the sūtra now turns to solving.

Mistaking a Thief for One's Own Son

Chinese Source

「善男子！末世眾生不了四相，雖經多劫勤苦修道，但名有為，終不能成一切聖果，是故名為正法末世。何以故？認一切我為涅槃故，有證有悟名成就故。譬如有人以賊為子，其家財寶終不成就。何以故？有我愛者亦愛涅槃，伏我愛根為涅槃相；有憎我者亦憎生死，不知愛者真生死故，別憎生死，名不解脫。

「云何當知法不解脫？善男子！彼末世眾生習菩提者，以己微證為自清淨，猶未能盡我相根本。若復有人讚歎彼法，即生歡喜，便欲濟度；若復誹謗彼所得者，便生瞋恨。則知我相堅固執持，潛伏藏識，遊戲諸根，曾不間斷。善男子！彼修道者不除我相，是故不能入清淨覺。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: sentient beings of the final age who fail to understand these four marks, though they may cultivate the path with strenuous effort across many kalpas, only ever produce what is conditioned — they will never, in the end, accomplish any sagely fruit at all. This is why it is called the final age of the true Dharma. Why is this? Because they take every trace of self and call it nirvāṇa, and call whatever is realized and awakened to a genuine accomplishment. It is like a family that has mistaken a thief for its own son — the household's wealth will, in the end, never be safeguarded. Why is this? Whoever cherishes a self also cherishes nirvāṇa, subduing the very root of that self-cherishing and mistaking the result for the mark of nirvāṇa; whoever resents a self also resents birth-and-death, not realizing that this very cherishing is itself the true birth-and-death, and, resenting birth-and-death as something separate from it, this is called failing to be liberated.

"How, then, can one know that a teaching has failed to bring liberation? Good sons: sentient beings of the final age who study bodhi take their own slight realization as evidence of their own purity, still without having exhausted the very root of the mark of self. If someone then praises the teaching they hold to, they immediately feel delight and wish to go save that person; if someone else criticizes what they have attained, they immediately feel resentment and anger. From this alone, one may know that the mark of self is still firmly grasped and held, lying hidden within the storehouse consciousness, at play among every sense-faculty, never once actually interrupted. Good sons: for a practitioner of the path who has not removed the mark of self, it is for this very reason that pure awakening remains impossible to enter."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi glosses the thief-as-son simile precisely: a stranger recognized as a thief can at least be guarded against, but a thief welcomed into the family and raised as its own child has free run of the household's wealth, and the family, believing they are safe, never thinks to protect against him — self-clinging welcomed in as though it were nirvāṇa itself operates exactly this way, quietly depleting whatever merit and insight a practitioner accumulates, precisely because it goes unrecognized as the thief it actually is. He treats the diagnostic that follows — delight at praise, resentment at criticism — as this chapter's single most useful practical tool, because it does not require a practitioner to introspect abstractly on whether self-clinging remains; it only requires noticing an involuntary reaction that self-clinging alone could produce. A person with no remaining self-mark left to protect, he notes, has nothing at stake in whether their own teaching is praised or criticized — the very presence of a strong emotional charge either way is itself sufficient evidence that "the mark of self is still firmly grasped and held," regardless of how sophisticated the practitioner's doctrine has otherwise become.

The Cure: A Vow With No Self Left Standing

Chinese Source

「善男子！末世眾生希望成道，無令求悟，唯益多聞，增長我見。但當精勤降伏煩惱，起大勇猛，未得令得，未斷令斷，貪、瞋、愛、慢、諂曲、嫉妬，對境不生，彼我恩愛，一切寂滅。佛說是人漸次成就。求善知識，不墮邪見，若於所求別生憎愛，則不能入清淨覺海。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: sentient beings of the final age who hope to accomplish the path should not seek mere awakening in a way that only benefits from wide learning and thereby increases the view of self. They should only diligently subdue affliction, arousing great courage, bringing about what has not yet been attained and cutting off what has not yet been cut off, so that greed, aversion, craving, arrogance, flattery, and envy no longer arise when met with any circumstance, and every affection between self and other, entirely, falls silent. The Buddha says of such a person that they achieve success gradually. Seeking a good spiritual friend in this way does not fall into wrong views — but if one gives rise to hatred or craving over whatever one is seeking, then one is unable to enter the pure ocean of awakening."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads "should not seek mere awakening in a way that only benefits from wide learning" as a direct correction of a mistake this chapter has been building toward all along: a practitioner who reads the four marks of self merely as more doctrine to memorize and discuss will only end up with a more sophisticated self, better equipped to feel proud of understanding self-clinging, which is precisely the mark-of-a-person problem in a fresh disguise. The remedy the sūtra actually prescribes, he notes, is not further analysis but a direct, sustained effort against affliction as it actually arises — greed, aversion, craving, arrogance, flattery, and envy named specifically because these are exactly the reactions the praise-and-blame diagnostic of the previous section was designed to catch in the act. "Every affection between self and other, entirely, falls silent" closes the chapter by returning to where it opened: not a claim that self has been eliminated by force, but a description of what remains once nothing is left standing that still needs defending.

In Verse

Chinese Source

淨業汝當知，　一切諸眾生，
皆由執我愛，　無始妄流轉，
未除四種相，　不得成菩提。

愛憎生於心，　諂曲存諸念，
是故多迷悶，　不能入覺城。

若能歸悟剎，　先去貪瞋癡，
法愛不存心，　漸次可成就。

我身本不有，　憎愛何由生？
此人求善友，　終不墮邪見。
所求別生心，　究竟非成就。

Ru-Yi Translation

Purifier of Karmic Hindrances, you should know:
every sentient being,
entirely because of grasping self-love,
has, since beginningless time, falsely turned in the wheel —
and until these four marks are removed,
bodhi cannot be accomplished.

Craving and hatred arise in the mind,
flattery and crookedness persist in every thought —
this is why beings are so often confused and troubled,
unable to enter the city of awakening.

If one can turn toward the land of awakening,
first cast off greed, aversion, and delusion —
with no attachment even to the Dharma left in the mind,
gradual accomplishment becomes possible.

This body of mine was never truly there —
from what, then, could hatred or craving ever arise?
A person who seeks a good friend in this spirit
will never fall into wrong views.
But if one gives rise to a separate mind over whatever one seeks,
this can, in the end, never be true accomplishment.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes the verse's third stanza — "with no attachment even to the Dharma left in the mind" — names something the long prose passage only implied throughout: that even a refined attachment to the teaching itself, dharma-craving in the technical sense already distinguished back in Maitreya's chapter, is still a residue of the same self-clinging this whole chapter has been diagnosing, and must be released along with the coarser afflictions named just before it. He reads this as the verse's own quiet warning against a reader who, having just carefully studied all four marks of self, now takes pride specifically in understanding this teaching better than others do — which the chapter has already shown, twice over, to be exactly how the mark of a person renews itself.

Part 9 Complete

This closes Part 9: Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances' question about why purity still feels obstructed, and the Buddha's answer — the four marks of self, of a person, of sentient beings, and of a lifespan, each subtler than the last, tested not by doctrine held but by how praise and blame still land, and diagnosed with the image of a thief mistaken, and welcomed in, as a family's own son.
