The Four Sicknesses of Practice

Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances' chapter diagnosed the self hiding beneath genuine realization; Universal Enlightenment now asks a more practical pair of questions — how does one find a teacher capable of helping, and what remaining sicknesses can still afflict a practitioner's own understanding? The answer names four: forcing enlightenment through effort, drifting passively and calling it acceptance, suppressing thought by force, and annihilating everything down to blankness — each one, Zongmi shows, a subtle misreading of a method the sūtra itself has already taught.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 10 of 12 complete Source: Taishō T0842, 圓覺經 (tr. Buddhatrāta) Reading notes after Zongmi's 圓覺經略疏 (T1795)

Universal Enlightenment's Question

Chinese Source

於是普覺菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右遶三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！快說禪病，令諸大眾得未曾有，心意蕩然，獲大安隱。世尊！末世眾生去佛漸遠，賢聖隱伏，邪法增熾，使諸眾生求何等人？依何等法？行何等行？除去何病？云何發心，令彼群盲不墮邪見？」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告普覺菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能諮問如來如是修行，能施末世一切眾生無畏道眼，令彼眾生得成聖道。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，普覺菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Universal Enlightenment rose from his seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — you have spoken so clearly of the sicknesses of meditation practice, letting this great assembly gain what it never had before, its mind and thought thoroughly cleared, gaining great peace and ease. World-Honored One: sentient beings of the final age are ever further removed from the Buddha's own time; the worthy and sagely go into hiding, while wrong teachings grow ever more numerous and fierce. What kind of person should such sentient beings seek out? On what kind of teaching should they rely? What kind of practice should they carry out? What sicknesses should they remove? How should they arouse the mind for practice, so that these many blind ones do not fall into wrong views?" Having said this, he prostrated himself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Universal Enlightenment: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata about practice of exactly this kind, able to give every sentient being of the final age a fearless eye for the path, letting them accomplish the sagely path. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Universal Enlightenment, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads Universal Enlightenment's five-part question as answered, in turn, by five sections of the chapter that follows: what kind of person to seek (a teacher of right knowledge and view), what teaching to rely on (the teacher's own instruction, not a fixed curriculum), what practice to carry out (how to serve that teacher once found), what sicknesses to remove (the four named directly), and how to arouse the mind (the vow the chapter closes with). He glosses this bodhisattva's own name to fit the moment precisely: earlier chapters removed the coarser afflictions and, in Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances' chapter, even the four subtle marks of self, but a residual difficulty remains at the level of how a practitioner actually applies effort in daily practice — the four sicknesses about to be named — so that awakening, though genuine progress has already been made, is "not yet universal." This chapter's work is to make it so, hence Universal Enlightenment.

Finding a Teacher of Right Knowledge and View

Chinese Source

「善男子！末世眾生將發大心，求善知識，欲修行者，當求一切正知見人，心不住相，不著聲聞、緣覺境界，雖現塵勞，心恆清淨，示有諸過，讚歎梵行，不令眾生入不律儀。求如是人，即得成就阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。末世眾生見如是人，應當供養，不惜身命。彼善知識，四威儀中常現清淨，乃至示現種種過患，心無憍慢；況復摶財、妻子、眷屬？若善男子於彼善友不起惡念，即能究竟成就正覺，心華發明，照十方剎。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: sentient beings of the final age who are about to arouse the great mind, and who wish to seek a good spiritual friend in order to practice, should seek out a person of entirely right knowledge and view — whose mind does not dwell on any mark, who is not attached to the realm of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, who, though outwardly appearing amid the world's dust and toil, remains inwardly forever pure, and who, even where he shows apparent faults, still praises and upholds pure conduct, never letting sentient beings fall into undisciplined ways. One who seeks a person of this kind will thereby attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. Sentient beings of the final age who encounter a person of this kind should make offerings to him and not spare even their own body or life for his sake. Such a good spiritual friend, in every one of the four postures of daily life, constantly manifests purity, and even where he displays what appear to be faults, no arrogance arises in the beholder's mind — how much more, then, should this be true of any attachment to wealth, spouse, or family? If a good son gives rise to no ill will toward such a good friend, he will thereby be able to accomplish, to the very end, right and complete awakening, the flower of his mind opening into full bloom, illuminating every land in the ten directions."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads "showing apparent faults, yet still praising pure conduct" as marking exactly what distinguishes an authentic teacher's skillful means from an actual moral failure to be avoided: such a teacher may, for pedagogical reasons — to draw close to a student mired in worldly attachment, or to demonstrate compassion rather than judgment — appear to share in ordinary faults, but never abandons praising and upholding genuine discipline in doing so, and never uses the appearance of fault as an excuse to lead a student into real transgression. He is careful to distinguish this from the false teacher a student should actually avoid: one who genuinely breaks precepts and then justifies the breaking as itself a teaching, leading others astray under cover of "skillful means." The test he offers is behavioral rather than doctrinal — does the apparent fault come paired with an undiminished praise of real discipline, or does it come paired with a rationalization for further transgression?

On "no arrogance arises in the beholder's mind" even at apparent faults, and the escalation to "how much more so any attachment to wealth, spouse, or family," Zongmi reads this as forestalling a natural but mistaken response: a student who sees a teacher display something that looks like a flaw might feel emboldened to judge, growing careless or superior — precisely the wrong reaction, since it reintroduces exactly the self-regard Purifier of All Karmic Hindrances' chapter just finished diagnosing. The instruction to offer one's very body and life without reservation is not, he stresses, a demand for blind literal sacrifice, but a deliberate escalation meant to establish, as forcefully as language allows, that ordinary attachments — to comfort, reputation, wealth, or family — are the much smaller thing a genuine seeker should have already set aside.

Four Sicknesses of Practice

Chinese Source

「善男子！彼善知識所證妙法，應離四病。云何四病？一者作病，若復有人作如是言：『我於本心作種種行，欲求圓覺。』彼圓覺性非作得故，說名為病。二者任病，若復有人作如是言：『我等今者不斷生死、不求涅槃，涅槃、生死無起滅念，任彼一切，隨諸法性，欲求圓覺。』彼圓覺性非任有故，說名為病。三者止病，若復有人作如是言：『我今自心永息諸念，得一切性寂然平等，欲求圓覺。』彼圓覺性非止合故，說名為病。四者滅病，若復有人作如是言：『我今永斷一切煩惱，身心畢竟空無所有，何況根塵虛妄境界，一切永寂，欲求圓覺。』彼圓覺性非寂相故，說名為病。離四病者，則知清淨；作是觀者，名為正觀；若他觀者，名為邪觀。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: the wondrous Dharma realized by a genuine good spiritual friend should be free of four sicknesses. What are the four? The first is called the sickness of forcing. Suppose someone says: 'Within my own original mind, I carry out every kind of deliberate practice, seeking thereby to attain Perfect Enlightenment.' Because the nature of Perfect Enlightenment is never obtained through forced action, this is called a sickness. The second is called the sickness of drifting. Suppose someone says: 'We, now, neither cut off birth-and-death nor seek nirvāṇa; we hold no thought of nirvāṇa or of birth-and-death either arising or ceasing, but simply let everything be, according with whatever the nature of each dharma happens to be, and thereby seek Perfect Enlightenment.' Because the nature of Perfect Enlightenment is never present through mere drifting, this too is called a sickness. The third is called the sickness of suppressing. Suppose someone says: 'I now permanently still every thought within my own mind, attaining a state in which the nature of everything is quiescent and equal, and thereby seek Perfect Enlightenment.' Because the nature of Perfect Enlightenment does not accord with mere suppression, this too is called a sickness. The fourth is called the sickness of annihilating. Suppose someone says: 'I now permanently cut off every affliction; body and mind, in the end, are entirely empty, with nothing there at all — how much more so the sense-faculties, sense-objects, and every unreal state — everything, permanently still, and thereby I seek Perfect Enlightenment.' Because the nature of Perfect Enlightenment is not itself a mark of quiescence, this too is called a sickness. One who has left all four sicknesses behind thereby knows purity itself; to observe in this way is called right contemplation, while to observe in any other way is called wrong contemplation."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi traces each of the four sicknesses back to a specific earlier teaching, misapplied by a practitioner who took a single method as the whole of the path rather than one gate among several: the sickness of forcing arises from misreading Samantabhadra's chapter, where "all bodhisattvas take up this practice from here" was meant to describe compassionate activity arising naturally from illusion-insight, not effortful striving toward a goal; the sickness of drifting arises from misreading Eloquent Voice's own opening line, "Perfect Enlightenment is, at root, without any cultivation and without anyone who cultivates it," taken by a careless reader as license for passive indifference rather than as a description of awakening's own ultimate nature; the sickness of suppressing arises from misreading śamatha itself, "taking stillness as the practice," where actively silencing every thought is mistaken for the settling that stillness actually names; and the sickness of annihilating arises from misreading dhyāna's own quiescence, mistaking a forced emptying-out of body, mind, and every sense-object for the effortless "stillness-and-extinction" the third gate of practice actually describes.

Zongmi is explicit that none of these four methods — deliberate effort, letting-be, stilling thought, or emptying out — is wrong in itself; each is, in fact, a genuine teaching found elsewhere in this very sūtra. What makes each one a sickness is treating it as sufficient on its own, seized on and clung to in isolation rather than held loosely as one gate among several, deployed as the moment calls for it. His closing formula for the difference: "if one is able to fully penetrate all four without becoming fixed on any single one, then even engaging in these very four practices is, in every case, already entering the path" — the identical four activities, done with nothing clung to, cease to be sicknesses at all.

How to Serve a Teacher, and What Old Habit Still Requires

Chinese Source

「善男子！末世眾生欲修行者，應當盡命供養善友、事善知識。彼善知識欲來親近，應斷憍慢；若復遠離，應斷瞋恨。現逆順境，猶如虛空，了知身心畢竟平等，與諸眾生同體無異，如此修行，方入圓覺。善男子！末世眾生不得成道，由有無始自他憎愛一切種子，故未解脫。若復有人觀彼怨家如己父母，心無有二，即除諸病；於諸法中自他憎愛，亦復如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: sentient beings of the final age who wish to practice should make offerings to a good friend and serve a genuine spiritual guide with their whole life. When that good friend draws close, one should cut off any arrogance; when he instead grows distant, one should cut off any resentment. Whatever adverse or favorable circumstance one encounters should be met exactly as one meets empty space — clearly knowing that self and other, in the end, are entirely equal, sharing one and the same substance with every sentient being without any difference at all. Only by practicing in this way does one truly enter Perfect Enlightenment. Good sons: the reason sentient beings of the final age fail to accomplish the path is that every seed of hatred and craving between self and other, sown since beginningless time, still remains, and it is because of this that they remain unliberated. But if a person looks upon an enemy exactly as he would look upon his own father or mother, his mind holding no distinction between the two, this alone removes every sickness — and the very same holds for hatred and craving between self and other with respect to every dharma as well."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads "cut off arrogance when the teacher draws close, cut off resentment when he grows distant" as addressing a specific, common failure mode in the student-teacher relationship that a purely doctrinal understanding of the four sicknesses would miss entirely: closeness to a respected teacher can breed a subtle pride at being favored, while distance can breed a resentment at being neglected, and both, however different they feel from the inside, are simply hatred and craving wearing the costume of a spiritual relationship. He treats the closing instruction — regarding an enemy exactly as one regards one's own parents — as this chapter's most demanding practical test, precisely because it targets the oldest and most deeply worn habit-seeds, "sown since beginningless time," that the more abstract four-sickness diagnosis, taken alone, would leave completely untouched. Doctrine correctly held is not, on Zongmi's reading, the same thing as habit actually worn down — which is exactly why the sūtra insists on this concrete relational practice rather than resting content with the conceptual clarity the four sicknesses alone provide.

The Vow That Arouses the Mind

Chinese Source

「善男子！末世眾生欲求圓覺，應當發心，作如是言：『盡於虛空一切眾生，我皆令入究竟圓覺，於圓覺中無取覺者，除彼我人一切諸相。』如是發心，不墮邪見。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: sentient beings of the final age who wish to seek Perfect Enlightenment should arouse the mind, speaking these very words: 'I vow to bring every sentient being throughout the whole of empty space into ultimate Perfect Enlightenment — yet within Perfect Enlightenment itself, there is no one who grasps at awakening as something attained, and every mark of a self, a person, and an other is thereby removed.' Arousing the mind in this way does not fall into wrong views."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads this closing vow as deliberately holding together two clauses that would, taken separately, each collapse into error: the first half, "bring every sentient being into Perfect Enlightenment," is boundless, positive, and active — exactly the kind of vow that, standing alone, risks reintroducing a subtle self as the one doing the bringing; the second half, "there is no one who grasps at awakening as something attained," corrects this immediately, but if stated alone and first would risk collapsing into passivity, since a vow that begins by denying any grasper might never actually get around to vowing anything at all. Held together, in this order, the vow generates real compassionate activity while simultaneously dissolving, in the same breath, the self that activity would otherwise have accumulated around — which is precisely why the sūtra can say that arousing the mind in exactly this way "does not fall into wrong views," closing a chapter that has spent its whole length identifying the many subtler ways it otherwise could.

In Verse

Chinese Source

普覺汝當知，　末世諸眾生，
欲求善知識，　應當求正見，
心遠二乘者。

法中除四病，　謂作止任滅；
親近無憍慢，　遠離無瞋恨。

見種種境界，　心當生希有，
還如佛出世。

不犯非律儀，　戒根永清淨；
度一切眾生，　究竟入圓覺。

無彼我人相，　常依止智慧，
便得超邪見，　證覺般涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Universal Enlightenment, you should know:
sentient beings of the final age
who wish to seek a good spiritual friend
should seek one of right view,
whose mind is far removed from the two vehicles.

Within the Dharma, remove the four sicknesses —
namely, forcing, suppressing, drifting, and annihilating.
When such a teacher draws close, have no arrogance;
when he grows distant, have no resentment.

Seeing every kind of circumstance,
the mind should give rise to a sense of wonder,
as though a buddha had appeared in the world once more.

Never transgressing right conduct,
the root of discipline forever pure —
bringing every sentient being across,
one enters, in the end, Perfect Enlightenment.

With no mark of a self, a person, or an other,
always relying on wisdom alone,
one is thereby able to transcend wrong views,
and realize awakening's own final nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes the verse's ordering of the four sicknesses — forcing, suppressing, drifting, annihilating — differs from the prose, which gave forcing, drifting, suppressing, annihilating, and takes this simply as evidence that the verse is compressing the list for meter rather than tracking a meaningful sequence among the four. He reads "as though a buddha had appeared in the world once more" as the verse's own compact restatement of a fuller instruction the prose gave at greater length: treating even a teacher's apparent faults with the same wonder one would feel encountering the Buddha directly, rather than with the arrogance the prose warned against just before it.
