The Stages of No Attainment

If Perfect Enlightenment admits of no bodhisattva and no sentient being at all, Pure Wisdom asks, then why does the sūtra keep speaking of buddhas, bodhisattvas, and ordinary beings as though they differed? The answer gives a four-stage account of "according with awakening" — ordinary beings, bodhisattvas before and after the grounds, and buddhas — that Zongmi reads as the same four stages the Awakening of Faith, already translated on this site, builds around its four marks of mind: generation, continuation, change, and cessation.

Pure Wisdom's Question

Chinese Source

於是清淨慧菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右繞三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！為我等輩廣說如是不思議事，本所不見本所不聞，我等今者蒙佛善誘，身心泰然得大饒益，願為一切諸來法眾重宣法王圓滿覺性，一切眾生及諸菩薩、如來世尊所證所得云何差別？令末世眾生聞此聖教，隨順開悟漸次能入。」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告清淨慧菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能為諸菩薩及末世眾生，請問如來漸次差別。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，清淨慧菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Pure Wisdom rose from her seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — you have broadly explained, for our sake, matters we had never before seen or heard. We, having now received your skillful guidance, find body and mind at ease and greatly benefited. I ask that you again proclaim, for every member of this Dharma-assembly, the Dharma-king's own perfectly complete nature of awakening — how do the realizations and attainments of ordinary sentient beings, of bodhisattvas, and of the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, actually differ? Let sentient beings of the final age, hearing this sacred teaching, be brought into accord with it, awakening and gradually entering in." Having said this, she prostrated herself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Pure Wisdom: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata, on behalf of the bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age, about the gradual differences among the stages. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Pure Wisdom, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes the tension this question is built to surface: the sūtra has just spent five chapters insisting that awakening is single, undivided, and equally present in every being — yet plainly speaks, throughout, of ordinary beings, bodhisattvas not yet on the grounds, bodhisattvas already on the grounds, and buddhas as though these were genuinely different attainments. Pure Wisdom's own name fits the question precisely: her wisdom, Zongmi says, "does not dwell in or cling to" any stage, which is exactly the quality of mind needed to hold both halves of the coming answer together — that the stages are real enough to be named and taught, and that, in the deepest sense the sūtra means, nothing has actually been attained at any of them.

No Bodhisattva, No Sentient Being, in Reality

Chinese Source

「善男子！圓覺自性，非性性有，循諸性起，無取無證，於實相中實無菩薩及諸眾生。何以故？菩薩、眾生皆是幻化，幻化滅故無取證者。譬如眼根不自見眼，性自平等，無平等者。眾生迷倒，未能除滅一切幻化，於滅未滅，妄功用中便顯差別；若得如來寂滅隨順，實無寂滅及寂滅者。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: the self-nature of Perfect Enlightenment is not itself a fixed nature that exists among other fixed natures — it accords with and arises through every differing nature, without grasping and without attainment; and within true reality itself, there is, in fact, neither bodhisattva nor sentient being at all. Why is this? Because both bodhisattva and sentient being are alike illusory transformation — and once illusory transformation has dissolved, there is no one left who grasps or attains anything. It is like the eye itself, which cannot see itself; its own nature is simply equal, without anyone there to make it equal. Sentient beings, deluded and inverted, have not yet been able to eliminate every illusory transformation — and it is precisely within this space between not-yet-eliminated and eliminated, within this still-deluded exertion of effort, that differences come to appear at all. Once one attains accord with the Tathāgata's own quiescence, there is, in truth, neither quiescence nor anyone who is quiescent."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads "the self-nature of Perfect Enlightenment is not itself a fixed nature" as directly recalling the five natures of the previous chapter: awakening itself does not belong to any one of the five as a fixed possession, the way a being's karmic history might belong to them — it simply accords with and manifests through whichever nature a given being currently has, the way water takes the shape of whatever vessel holds it without becoming any one vessel's permanent property. On the eye-cannot-see-itself simile, he draws out the force of the comparison precisely: an eye sees every other thing without partiality, yet cannot turn around and see itself — in just the same way, awakening illuminates every differing stage, ordinary being and buddha alike, without favoring any of them, all while itself remaining beyond the kind of before-and-after comparison that would let anyone call it "equal" to something else, since equality in that sense requires two things being compared, and here there is, in the end, only the one.

He locates the actual source of apparent difference precisely: not awakening itself, which never varies, but "the still-deluded exertion of effort" (妄功用) — the interval, still within delusion, between having not yet removed illusory transformation and having fully removed it. This is the technical hinge on which the whole chapter turns, since it is this same interval, subdivided further, that the next two sections will use to distinguish ordinary beings from bodhisattvas not yet on the grounds, and bodhisattvas on the grounds from a buddha's own final state — differences entirely internal to that effort, never differences in awakening's own single, unvarying nature.

Four Stages, I — Ordinary Beings and Bodhisattvas Before the Grounds

Chinese Source

「善男子！一切眾生從無始來，由妄想我及愛我者，曾不自知念念生滅，故起憎愛，耽著五欲；若遇善友教令開悟淨圓覺性發明起滅，即知此生性自勞慮。若復有人勞慮永斷得法界淨，即彼淨解為自障礙，故於圓覺而不自在。此名凡夫隨順覺性。

「善男子！一切菩薩，見解為礙，雖斷解礙，猶住見覺，覺礙為礙而不自在；此名菩薩未入地者隨順覺性。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: all sentient beings, from beginningless time, have taken a self to exist falsely and cherished it — never knowing, on their own, that this very self arises and ceases thought after thought. Because of this they give rise to hatred and craving, indulging in the five desires. If they meet a good friend who teaches them to awaken to the pure nature of Perfect Enlightenment, and thereby come to discern this very arising and ceasing, they then know that this life is, of its own nature, nothing but toil and worry. And if a person then permanently cuts off this toil and worry, attaining a pure state within the dharma-realm, that very understanding of purity itself becomes an obstruction, and so, with respect to Perfect Enlightenment itself, he is not yet free. This is called an ordinary being's accord with the nature of awakening.

"Good sons: bodhisattvas, in general, are still obstructed by view and understanding — and though they have cut off the obstruction of understanding, they still dwell within a view of awakening, and this obstruction-of-awakening is itself still an obstruction, so they are not yet free. This is called the accord with the nature of awakening belonging to bodhisattvas who have not yet entered the grounds."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi ties this four-stage structure directly to the Awakening of Faith's own four marks of mind — generation, continuation, change, and cessation, already translated in full in this site's Awakening of Faith collection — mapping each of the sūtra's four stages onto that treatise's account of "actualized enlightenment" ceasing one mark at a time, from coarsest to subtlest. The ordinary being's stage corresponds to ceasing the mark of continuation: an ordinary person, on meeting a teacher, first notices that thoughts arise and cease moment to moment — a real advance, since beforehand this went entirely unnoticed — but then makes a second mistake, taking the resulting calm itself, the "pure understanding," as something to cling to, which becomes a new, subtler obstruction in its own right. This corresponds, in the Awakening of Faith's own words, to "awakening to the change of thought, the thought having no mark of change" — a partial, not-yet-complete unbinding.

The bodhisattva-before-the-grounds stage corresponds to ceasing the mark of change: this practitioner has already gone further, seeing through the very "pure understanding" that trapped the ordinary being — Zongmi notes the text says "though they have cut off the obstruction of understanding," marking real progress beyond stage one — but a subtler obstruction remains: still dwelling within "a view of awakening" itself, treating awakening as something seen and held onto rather than simply being what one is. Both stages, Zongmi stresses, show real advancement over the one before, and both remain, in the sūtra's own words, "not yet free" — the difference between them is a matter of exactly which residual clinging has yet to be seen through, not a difference in kind.

Four Stages, II — Bodhisattvas on the Grounds, and the Tathāgata

Chinese Source

「善男子！有照有覺俱名障礙，是故菩薩常覺不住，照與照者同時寂滅。譬如有人自斷其首，首已斷故，無能斷者；則以礙心自滅諸礙，礙已斷滅，無滅礙者。修多羅教如標月指，若復見月，了知所標畢竟非月；一切如來種種言說開示菩薩，亦復如是。此名菩薩已入地者隨順覺性。

「善男子！一切障礙即究竟覺，得念、失念無非解脫，成法、破法皆名涅槃，智慧、愚癡通為般若，菩薩、外道所成就法同是菩提，無明、真如無異境界，諸戒、定、慧及婬、怒、癡俱是梵行，眾生、國土同一法性，地獄、天宮皆為淨土，有性、無性齊成佛道；一切煩惱畢竟解脫，法界海慧照了諸相，猶如虛空。此名如來隨順覺性。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: to have illumination, and to have a knower of that illumination, are both alike still obstructions. This is why a bodhisattva at this stage stays constantly aware without dwelling anywhere, and the illumination and the one who illuminates dissolve together, at the very same time. It is like a person cutting off his own head — once the head has already been cut off, there is no one left to have done the cutting. So too, using an obstructed mind to dissolve every obstruction, once the obstructions have all been dissolved, there is no one left who dissolved them. The teaching of the sūtras is like a finger pointing at the moon — once one has actually seen the moon, one clearly understands that the finger being pointed was, in the end, never the moon itself; and every kind of teaching that the tathāgatas use to instruct bodhisattvas is, in just the same way, not itself the thing it points to. This is called the accord with the nature of awakening belonging to bodhisattvas who have already entered the grounds.

"Good sons: every obstruction is itself, in the end, ultimate awakening. Whether mindfulness is retained or lost, both are equally liberation. Whether the Dharma is accomplished or broken down, both alike are called nirvāṇa. Wisdom and delusion both together are prajñā. Whatever bodhisattvas accomplish and whatever outsiders accomplish are the very same bodhi. Ignorance and suchness are not two different realms. The precepts, meditative absorption, and wisdom, together with lust, anger, and delusion, are all equally pure conduct. Sentient beings and their lands share one and the same dharma-nature. The hell realms and the palaces of heaven are both alike pure lands. Those who have the nature for it and those who do not, alike, accomplish the path to buddhahood. Every affliction, in the end, is itself liberation, and the ocean-like wisdom of the dharma-realm illuminates every mark as clearly as it illuminates empty space. This is called the Tathāgata's own accord with the nature of awakening."

Reading note — after Zongmi

The bodhisattva-on-the-grounds stage, Zongmi says, corresponds to ceasing the mark of continuation — subtler still than change, since here even "having an illumination" and "having a knower of that illumination" are recognized as a residual pair, subject and object still implicitly opposed, and both must dissolve together rather than one being used to eliminate the other. He reads the self-beheading simile with care: it does not mean the practitioner survives to reflect on having succeeded, the way a swordsman might reflect on a completed stroke — the point is that once the obstruction is actually gone, there is, by definition, no separate "one who removed it" left over to take credit, any more than a beheaded man remains to confirm the beheading. On the finger-pointing-at-the-moon simile, he stresses this is not a devaluation of the sūtras themselves — a finger is still how one is actually shown where to look — but a caution against mistaking the teaching, however precisely it points, for the reality it only ever pointed toward.

The Tathāgata's own stage corresponds to ceasing the mark of generation — the subtlest mark of all, "mind's very first stirring," in the Awakening of Faith's phrase, and its cessation is what that treatise calls ultimate enlightenment itself. Zongmi reads the sūtra's tenfold list of paired opposites — mindfulness and its loss, the Dharma made or unmade, wisdom and delusion, and so on down through hell-realms and heaven's palaces — not as ten separate claims requiring ten separate justifications, but as ten instances of one single point, restated because a reader needs to see it apply across every domain before it truly sinks in: once obstruction itself is recognized as ultimate awakening, no term on either side of any of these pairs can any longer function as an obstruction, because there is, at this final stage, no longer anyone left standing outside the pair for whom the distinction could matter. This is why, and only why, the sūtra can say that even hell and heaven are alike pure lands — not because suffering has been redescribed as pleasant, but because the entire framework in which "pure" and "impure" stood opposed to one another has itself, at this stage, been seen through.

Sudden Accord, Without Any Stages At All

Chinese Source

「善男子！但諸菩薩及末世眾生，居一切時不起妄念，於諸妄心亦不息滅，住妄想境不加了知，於無了知不辨真實。彼諸眾生聞是法門，信解受持不生驚畏，是則名為隨順覺性。善男子！汝等當知，如是眾生已曾供養百千萬億恒河沙諸佛及大菩薩，植眾德本，佛說是人名為成就一切種智。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: it is only that bodhisattvas, and sentient beings of the final age, at all times give rise to no false thought, and yet do not, on the other hand, extinguish false thoughts either; abiding within a state of false imagining, they do not add further discernment to it, and, in the absence of that added discernment, they do not go on to distinguish out some separate 'true reality' either. Such sentient beings, hearing this teaching, believe, understand, receive, and uphold it without giving rise to alarm or fear — and this alone is what is called according with the nature of awakening. Good sons, you should know: sentient beings such as these have already, in the past, made offerings to buddhas and great bodhisattvas as numerous as the sands of hundreds of thousands of myriads of koṭis of Ganges rivers, and have planted the roots of every virtue. The Buddha says of such a person that they have accomplished the wisdom that knows all modes."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi treats this final passage as a genuinely different register from everything that came before it — not a fifth stage added to the previous four, but a direct, sudden accord that bypasses the whole staged sequence entirely. Where the four-stage account describes gradual practice matched to differing levels of realization, this closing teaching describes a single, immediate posture of mind available in principle to anyone: neither generating false thought nor forcibly suppressing it, neither adding analysis to delusion nor going on to manufacture a separate "true reality" standing apart from it to grasp instead. He is explicit that this is not a lesser teaching offered as a consolation to those unable to manage the staged approach, but the sūtra's own most direct route — which is exactly why simply hearing it "without giving rise to alarm or fear" is treated as evidence of an already deep, long-cultivated capacity, not as a low bar. A mind that can hear "there is, in the end, no true reality standing apart from this very confusion to be found" and not recoil from it in fear, he says, is a mind that has already, across many lifetimes, been prepared for exactly this teaching.

In Verse

Chinese Source

清淨慧當知，　圓滿菩提性，
無取亦無證，　無菩薩眾生。

覺與未覺時，　漸次有差別，
眾生為解礙，　菩薩未離覺，
入地永寂滅，　不住一切相，
大覺悉圓滿，　名為遍隨順。

末世諸眾生，　心不生虛妄，
佛說如是人，　現世即菩薩，
供養恒沙佛，　功德已圓滿。
雖有多方便，　皆名隨順智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pure Wisdom, you should know:
the nature of perfectly complete bodhi
admits neither grasping nor attainment,
and admits neither bodhisattva nor sentient being.

Between the time of awakening and not-yet-awakening,
there are gradual, differing stages:
sentient beings are obstructed by their own understanding,
bodhisattvas have not yet left awakening itself behind,
and once one enters the grounds, quiescence is forever complete,
dwelling in no mark of anything at all —
great awakening, entirely perfect and full,
is what is called all-pervading accord.

Sentient beings of the final age
whose minds give rise to no falsehood —
the Buddha says that such people
are, in this very life, already bodhisattvas.
Having made offerings to buddhas numerous as the sands of the Ganges,
their merit is already complete.
Though there are many skillful means,
all of them alike are called the wisdom of accord.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes the verse compresses the two bodhisattva stages of the long prose passage — before and after entering the grounds — into a single line each, and drops the tenfold list of paired opposites belonging to the Tathāgata's stage down to the four words "dwelling in no mark of anything." He treats the verse's final couplet, "though there are many skillful means, all of them alike are called the wisdom of accord," as the chapter's own closing gloss on itself: the four-stage account and the sudden, stageless accord of the previous section are not two competing teachings, but different skillful means pointing at one and the same underlying wisdom, offered to practitioners who find themselves, at any given moment, more suited to one approach or the other.
