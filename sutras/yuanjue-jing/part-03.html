The Practice Itself

Mañjuśrī asked what enlightenment is, and Samantabhadra asked how illusion can cure illusion. Now Universal Eye asks the practical question both of them left open: concretely, what does a practitioner actually do? This is the sūtra's longest chapter by far — a full method, from a plain reflection on the body's four elements through to the three-tiered vision the Huayan school called the dharmadhatu, ending in the claim that a practitioner who has carried it through finds nothing left standing between their own mind and a buddha's.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 3 of 12 complete Source: Taishō T0842, 圓覺經 (tr. Buddhatrāta) Reading notes after Zongmi's 圓覺經略疏 (T1795)

Universal Eye's Question

Chinese Source

於是普眼菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右遶三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！願為此會諸菩薩眾及為末世一切眾生，演說菩薩修行漸次，云何思惟？云何住持？眾生未悟作何方便普令開悟？世尊！若彼眾生無正方便及正思惟，聞佛如來說此三昧心生迷悶，則於圓覺不能悟入。願興慈悲，為我等輩及末世眾生假說方便。」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告普眼菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能為諸菩薩及末世眾生，問於如來修行漸次、思惟、住持乃至假說種種方便，汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，普眼菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Universal Eye rose from his seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — I ask that you set out, for the bodhisattvas of this assembly and for every sentient being of the final age, the gradual stages of a bodhisattva's practice. How should one reflect? How should one abide? Sentient beings who have not yet awakened — by what skillful means can they be brought to full awakening? World-Honored One, if those sentient beings lack the right skillful means and right reflection, then on hearing the Tathāgata teach this samādhi their minds will only grow bewildered, and they will be unable to awaken into Perfect Enlightenment. I ask, out of compassion, that you set out provisional skillful means for the sake of us here and sentient beings of the final age." Having said this, he prostrated himself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Universal Eye: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata, on behalf of the bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age, about the gradual stages of practice, reflection, and abiding, and even about provisional skillful means of every kind. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Universal Eye, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi treats this question as a direct response to a gap the previous chapter left open: Samantabhadra's answer closed with "knowing illusion is already leaving it, with no further method needed" — which some listeners, he says, might take to mean nothing further needs doing at all. Universal Eye asks for the actual practice precisely so the teaching will not be mistaken for an excuse not to practice. The chapter that follows he divides into three movements matched to the bodhisattva's name itself, which he glosses as holding both wisdom and compassion at once: "seeing all dharmas as purely clean is the great wisdom of the universal eye; seeing all sentient beings as already buddhas is the great compassion of the universal eye." Universal Eye's own name is thus a preview of where the chapter is going to end up.

Beginning the Practice: Precepts, Stillness, and Good Company

Chinese Source

「善男子，彼新學菩薩及末世眾生，欲求如來淨圓覺心，應當正念，遠離諸幻。先依如來奢摩他行，堅持禁戒，安處徒眾，宴坐靜室，恒作是念：『我今此身四大和合，所謂髮毛、爪齒、皮肉、筋骨、髓腦、垢色皆歸於地，唾涕、膿血、津液、涎沫、痰淚、精氣、大小便利皆歸於水，暖氣歸火，動轉歸風。四大各離，今者妄身當在何處？』即知此身畢竟無體，和合為相，實同幻化。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: newly-trained bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age who wish to seek the Tathāgata's pure mind of Perfect Enlightenment should hold to right mindfulness and leave every illusion behind. First rely on the Tathāgata's practice of śamatha: firmly keep the precepts, settle among a community of fellow practitioners, and sit quietly in a still room, constantly holding this reflection in mind: 'This body of mine now is a coming-together of the four great elements. What is called hair, body-hair, nails, teeth, skin, flesh, sinew, bone, marrow, and brain, along with every impure color, all belong to earth. Saliva, mucus, pus, blood, bodily fluid, spittle, phlegm, tears, seminal fluid, urine, and feces all belong to water. Warmth belongs to fire. Movement belongs to wind. When these four elements each go their own way, where, then, is this false body of mine to be found?' In this way one comes to know that this body, in the end, has no substance of its own — it is only a coming-together that takes on the appearance of a body, in truth no different from illusion and transformation."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads "rely first on śamatha" as a deliberate ordering: precepts kept by an already-agitated mind, he says, are not yet fit ground for this contemplation, so stillness is placed first even though the text lists precepts right alongside it — practice and sequence, he adds, need not match exactly. On the "community of fellow practitioners" sitting oddly next to "a still room," he offers two readings: different temperaments need different settings (a dull, sinking mind benefits from a group's energy; a scattered, restless one needs solitude to settle), and, more fully, that calm and insight need each other — a practitioner may live among peers for the sake of discussing the Dharma and still keep a private hour, morning, midday, and night, to sit in silence, so the two settings are not actually in tension.

On the four-elements reflection itself, Zongmi calls this the first of a two-part sequence — first a probing, investigative contemplation (尋伺觀) that questions where the self could possibly be located among earth, water, fire, and wind, given that these four are mutually exclusive (earth is obstructive and sinks, wind is unobstructed and rises; water and fire likewise contradict each other) so no single self could coherently belong to all four; only after this questioning does the "as-it-really-is" contemplation (如實觀) that follows conclude outright that the body has no fixed substance at all. He is explicit that "each element going its own way" does not mean the moment of physical death — the four elements are already, right now, distinguishable component by component; recognizing this at any moment already counts as their having "each gone their own way."

The Mind Has No Self Either

Chinese Source

「四緣假合，妄有六根；六根、四大中外合成，妄有緣氣，於中積聚，似有緣相假名為心。善男子！此虛妄心若無六塵則不能有，四大分解無塵可得，於中緣塵各歸散滅，畢竟無有緣心可見。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"The four conditions falsely come together and the six sense-faculties falsely appear; the six faculties and the four elements combine, inner and outer, and a conditioned energy falsely arises. Gathering and accumulating within this, there seems to be a conditioning mark, which is falsely called 'mind.' Good sons: this false mind, if there were no six sense-objects, could not exist at all. Once the four elements are separated out, there is no sense-object left to be found; and once the conditions and objects within it each return to dispersal, in the end there is no conditioning mind left to be seen anywhere."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Having emptied the body, Zongmi notes, the sūtra turns to the mind by the same two-stage method: a probing contemplation, then an as-it-really-is conclusion. The mind has no fixed mark of its own — it depends on an object to arise at all, and the object's own nature is empty, arising only because the mind projects it. Root and object turn out to be mutually dependent, so pressed for a location, neither one can be found. He glosses "conditioning energy" (緣氣) carefully: this false, conditioned mind, though arising in dependence on causes and conditions, is not simply cut off from the true mind — it retains some trace, some "flavor," of it, the way a scent lingers faintly in a robe that once held incense, which is why the text uses the word "energy" (氣) rather than describing the false mind as wholly other than the true one. Once the six dusts are seen to scatter — because the four elements they depend on have already been shown to have no fixed substance — the conditioning mind that depended on those dusts has, at that point, nothing left to condition it either.

Both Dissolve Together, Like a Mirror Wiped Clean

Chinese Source

「善男子！彼之眾生幻身滅故幻心亦滅，幻心滅故幻塵亦滅，幻塵滅故幻滅亦滅，幻滅滅故非幻不滅；譬如磨鏡垢盡明現。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: because that illusory body of sentient beings dissolves, the illusory mind dissolves as well; because the illusory mind dissolves, the illusory dust dissolves as well; because the illusory dust dissolves, even the illusion of dissolving itself dissolves; and because the illusion of dissolving dissolves, that which is not illusion does not dissolve at all. It is like polishing a mirror: once the grime is gone, its own brightness appears of itself."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi marks a shift in register here: the previous two sections emptied self out of body and mind individually — what he calls emptying the person (我空) — while this passage empties the body, mind, and every dust-object together as one, which he calls emptying the dharmas themselves (法空). He is careful to head off a misreading: none of this is newly caused to vanish just now. "Form is emptiness — not that form is destroyed to make emptiness," he writes, quoting the Heart Sūtra directly; the body and mind were never anything other than empty. What ends is only the mistaken clinging that took them to be otherwise — hence "dissolve" is the word used, not "destroy."

On the mirror simile, Zongmi's gloss deliberately corrects its own most natural first reading: "though it says 'polishing the mirror,' what is actually being polished is the grime." Cultivating the path, he says, is nothing more than clearing away what was false — the mirror's own brightness was never absent, only covered over; polishing does not add light, it removes what hid it. He maps the simile onto the two contemplations directly: attachment to person and to dharmas is the grime, the probing-then-as-it-really-is contemplations are the polishing, the mind's own original, unbuilt brightness is the light, and the emptying of both person and dharmas is what that appearing looks like from the outside.

The Mani Jewel: How Purity Spreads Through Every Faculty

Chinese Source

「善男子！當知身心皆為幻垢，垢相永滅，十方清淨。善男子！譬如清淨摩尼寶珠映於五色，隨方各現。諸愚癡者見彼摩尼實有五色。善男子！圓覺淨性現於身心，隨類各應，彼愚癡者說淨圓覺實有如是身心自相，亦復如是，由此不能遠於幻化，是故我說身心幻垢，對離幻垢，說名菩薩；垢盡對除，即無對垢及說名者。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: you should know that both body and mind are illusory dust — and once every trace of that dust has entirely dissolved, the ten directions are pure. Good sons: it is like a pure mani jewel reflecting the five colors, showing whichever color stands before it. Foolish people, seeing this, believe the mani jewel itself truly has five colors. Good sons: the pure nature of Perfect Enlightenment appears within body and mind, responding to whatever kind it meets — and those same foolish people, seeing this, say that pure Perfect Enlightenment itself truly has such marks of body and mind. This is exactly the same error, and it is exactly what keeps them from ever getting free of illusion and transformation. This is why I say body and mind are illusory dust: it is only in contrast to the leaving-behind of that illusory dust that one is called a bodhisattva at all. Once the dust is entirely gone and the very contrast with it is removed, then there is no longer any dust to contrast with, nor anyone left to be called by that name."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads the mani-jewel simile as doing two jobs at once: it confirms everything the sūtra has argued so far (a fool who mistakes the jewel's reflected colors for the jewel's own nature is exactly like a being who mistakes illusory body-and-mind for something real, the five colors mapping onto the five destinies of rebirth), and it also opens what comes next, since — unlike the earlier mirror, which is bright only once wiped clean and needs polishing to show it — the mani jewel is bright and pure to begin with, ten-directionally radiant without any polishing at all. The mirror suits emptying by contrast; the jewel suits what follows once nothing is left to contrast against.

On "once the dust is gone and the contrast with it is removed, there is no one left to be called a bodhisattva" — Zongmi notes this quietly dismantles the very vocabulary the sūtra has just spent three sections building: sentient being and bodhisattva were both provisional labels, applied only for as long as there was dust to be contrasted against and a leaving-behind to be named. Once genuinely nothing is left to leave behind, calling someone a "bodhisattva who has left illusion behind" makes no more sense than continuing to call clean water "unmuddied" once no one remembers it was ever muddy to begin with.

Three Ways of Seeing the Same Reality

Chinese Source

「善男子！此菩薩及末世眾生，證得諸幻滅影像故，爾時便得無方清淨，無邊虛空覺所顯發。覺圓明故顯心清淨，心清淨故見塵清淨。見清淨故，眼根清淨。根清淨故，眼識清淨。識清淨故，聞塵清淨。聞清淨故，耳根清淨。根清淨故，耳識清淨。識清淨故，覺塵清淨。如是乃至鼻、舌、身、意，亦復如是。善男子！根清淨故，色塵清淨。色清淨故，聲塵清淨。香、味、觸、法，亦復如是。善男子！六塵清淨故，地大清淨。地清淨故，水大清淨。火大、風大，亦復如是。善男子！四大清淨故，十二處、十八界、二十五有清淨。彼清淨故，十力、四無所畏、四無礙智、佛十八不共法、三十七助道品清淨，如是乃至八萬四千陀羅尼門一切清淨。善男子！一切實相性清淨故一身清淨，一身清淨故多身清淨，多身清淨故如是乃至十方眾生圓覺清淨。善男子！一世界清淨故多世界清淨，多世界清淨故如是乃至盡於虛空圓裹三世一切平等清淨不動。

「善男子！虛空如是平等不動，當知覺性平等不動；四大不動故，當知覺性平等不動；如是乃至八萬四千陀羅尼門平等不動，當知覺性平等不動。

「善男子！覺性遍滿清淨不動，圓無際故，當知六根遍滿法界。根遍滿故，當知六塵遍滿法界。塵遍滿故，當知四大遍滿法界。如是乃至陀羅尼門遍滿法界。善男子！由彼妙覺性遍滿故，根性、塵性無壞無雜。根、塵無壞故，如是乃至陀羅尼門無壞無雜；如百千燈光照一室，其光遍滿，無壞無雜。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: once these bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age realize the dissolving of every illusory reflected image, they thereupon attain a purity with no location at all, disclosed by boundless empty space itself as awakening. Because awakening is perfectly luminous, the mind it discloses is pure; because the mind is pure, the sense-object it sees is pure. Because seeing is pure, the eye-faculty is pure. Because the faculty is pure, eye-consciousness is pure. Because consciousness is pure, the object of hearing is pure. Because hearing is pure, the ear-faculty is pure. Because the faculty is pure, ear-consciousness is pure. Because consciousness is pure, the object of awareness is pure — and so on for the nose, tongue, body, and mind, each exactly the same. Good sons: because the faculties are pure, the object of form is pure. Because form is pure, the object of sound is pure — and so on for smell, taste, touch, and mental objects, each exactly the same. Good sons: because the six sense-objects are pure, the element of earth is pure. Because earth is pure, the element of water is pure — and so on for fire and wind, each exactly the same. Good sons: because the four great elements are pure, the twelve sense-fields, the eighteen sense-realms, and the twenty-five states of existence are pure. Because these are pure, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the four unhindered forms of wisdom, the buddha's eighteen unique qualities, and the thirty-seven limbs of enlightenment are pure — and so on, until the eighty-four thousand dhāraṇī-gates are, every one of them, pure. Good sons: because the nature of true reality itself is pure, one body is pure; because one body is pure, every body is pure — and so on, until the Perfect Enlightenment of sentient beings throughout the ten directions is pure. Good sons: because one world is pure, every world is pure — and so on, until the whole of empty space, encompassing the three times, is entirely equal, pure, and unmoving.

"Good sons: just as empty space is equal and unmoving in this way, know that the nature of awakening is equally unmoving; because the four great elements are unmoving, know that the nature of awakening is equally unmoving; and so on, until the eighty-four thousand dhāraṇī-gates are equally unmoving — know that the nature of awakening is equally unmoving in just the same way.

"Good sons: because the nature of awakening is all-pervading, pure, and unmoving, complete without any boundary, know that the six sense-faculties pervade the entire dharma-realm. Because the faculties pervade it, know that the six sense-objects pervade the entire dharma-realm as well. Because the sense-objects pervade it, know that the four great elements pervade the entire dharma-realm — and so on, until the dhāraṇī-gates themselves pervade the entire dharma-realm. Good sons: because that wondrous nature of awakening is all-pervading, the nature of the faculties and the nature of the objects are neither destroyed nor confused with one another. Because the faculties and objects are undestroyed, so too, and so on, are the dhāraṇī-gates undestroyed and unconfused — it is like a hundred thousand lamps lighting a single room: their light fills the whole room completely, and yet is neither destroyed nor confused, each with each."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads these three passages as one continuous unit built directly on the framework of his own teacher's teacher, Dushun (杜順), founder of the Huayan school's Discernment of the Dharmadhatu (華嚴法界觀門) — the same three-tiered vision already at work across this site's Treatise on the Sūtra of the Ten Grounds collection. First comes the contemplation of true emptiness (真空觀): the long chain running from the mind's own purity out through the six faculties, six sense-objects, four elements, and every category of worldly and transcendent dharma up to the eighty-four thousand dhāraṇī-gates, each said to be "pure" because each, seen clearly, dissolves without remainder into awakening itself — followed by the shorter chain confirming the same list is likewise "unmoving," since awakening never moves. Second comes the unimpeded interpenetration of principle and phenomena (理事無礙觀): rather than dissolving each phenomenon into principle, this stage shows that each phenomenon, precisely because it is not separate from that principle, is itself as boundless and pervasive as the principle it embodies — hence the faculties and sense-objects are said to fill the whole dharma-realm, not merely to vanish into it. Third comes the unimpeded interpenetration of phenomenon and phenomenon (事事無礙觀), illustrated by a hundred lamps lit in a single room: each lamp's light fills the entire room, yet no lamp's light displaces or is confused with any other's — every phenomenon, on this view, both pervades and is contained within every other, without contradiction.

Zongmi treats these three as strictly cumulative, not interchangeable options: true emptiness alone can collapse into a bare negation that dissolves the world without accounting for how phenomena still function; adding unimpeded principle-and-phenomena shows how what is empty can still act, without collapsing into confusion; and only the third stage, unimpeded phenomenon-and-phenomenon, delivers what he calls the sūtra's own promise — that purity is not achieved by escaping the world of things, but is already, right now, how each thing already stands in relation to every other. This same threefold vision, worked out at far greater length, is what this site's Avataṃsaka-related pages — the Ten Grounds treatise among them — return to again and again as the Huayan school's central contribution to Chinese Buddhist thought.

Sentient Beings Are, Right Now, Already Buddhas

Chinese Source

「善男子！覺成就故，當知菩薩不與法縛，不求法脫；不厭生死，不愛涅槃；不敬持戒，不憎毀禁；不重久習，不輕初學。何以故？一切覺故。譬如眼光曉了前境，其光圓滿，得無憎愛，何以故？光體無二，無憎愛故。

「善男子！此菩薩及末世眾生，修習此心得成就者，於此無修亦無成就。圓覺普照，寂滅無二，於中百千萬億不可說阿僧祇恒河沙諸佛世界，猶如空華，亂起亂滅，不即不離，無縛無脫，始知眾生本來成佛，生死、涅槃猶如昨夢。

「善男子！如昨夢故，當知生死及與涅槃無起無滅、無來無去。其所證者無得無失，無取無捨；其能證者無作無止，無任無滅。於此證中無能無所，畢竟無證，亦無證者，一切法性平等不壞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: because awakening has been accomplished, know that such a bodhisattva is neither bound by any dharma nor seeking release from any dharma; neither wearies of birth-and-death nor cherishes nirvāṇa; neither reveres the keeping of precepts nor resents their being broken; neither values long practice nor disdains the beginner. Why is this? Because everything, without exception, is awakening. It is like the light of the eye, illuminating what lies before it — that light, being complete and whole, has no hatred and no fondness. Why is this? Because the light itself, having no second thing set against it, has nothing to hate or to love.

"Good sons: these bodhisattvas, and sentient beings of the final age, who cultivate this mind and bring it to completion, find, within this, neither cultivation nor completion at all. Perfect Enlightenment shines everywhere; quiescence admits no second thing. Within it, the hundred thousand myriad koṭis of ineffable, asaṃkhyeya buddha-worlds, countless as the sands of the Ganges, are exactly like flowers in the sky, randomly arising and randomly vanishing — neither identical with awakening nor separate from it, neither bound nor released. Only now does one truly know: sentient beings have, from the very beginning, already been buddhas; birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are both alike a dream from last night.

"Good sons: because it is like a dream from last night, know that birth-and-death and nirvāṇa alike have no arising and no ceasing, no coming and no going. What is realized has no gain and no loss, no grasping and no rejecting; what does the realizing has no acting and no stopping, no letting-be and no annihilating. Within this realization there is no subject and no object; in the end there is no realization at all, and no one who realizes it. The nature of every dharma is, throughout, equal and unbroken."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads this closing movement as the chapter's payoff: "becoming identical to the Buddha's own state" (頓同佛境), arrived at through matching mental attitude, matching vision, and matching accord with reality itself. On the eightfold list of things a bodhisattva no longer favors or resists — bound versus released, weary of birth-and-death versus cherishing nirvāṇa, and so on — he notes these are four pairs, not eight separate points, and that the underlying principle is stated plainly in the text's own reasoning: "because everything, without exception, is awakening" — quoting the Awakening of Faith's definition of awakening itself as "the mind's substance being free of thought." A bodhisattva at this stage has, in Zongmi's phrase, simply stopped generating the eight biased attitudes named here, the way light has no bias for or against what it illuminates.

On "sentient beings have, from the very beginning, already been buddhas" — the single most quoted line in this sūtra — Zongmi is careful about how literally to take it: this is not a claim that ordinary confused beings, unaltered, count as buddhas in the sense a Buddhist would ordinarily mean by the word; it is a claim about what is disclosed once "cultivation" and "completion" are themselves seen through, the way the previous paragraph has just shown neither survives close examination. He cross-references six competing accounts of exactly when a being becomes a buddha, current in the schools of his own day — from the once-only awakening of Śākyamuni in early Buddhist doctrine, through the "three uncountable eons" of the gradual path, to Huayan's own teaching that the very first arousal of bodhicitta already counts as full awakening — and places this sūtra's claim in the sixth and final position, the one belonging distinctively to the perfect teaching: not that beings gradually become buddhas, and not even that awakening happens in a single instant, but that being a buddha was never, at any point, something still to be arrived at.

In Verse

Chinese Source

普眼汝當知：　一切諸眾生，
身心皆如幻，　身相屬四大，
心性歸六塵。　四大體各離，
誰為和合者？　如是漸修行，
一切悉清淨，　不動遍法界。

無作止任滅，　亦無能證者。

一切佛世界，　猶如虛空華；
三世悉平等，　畢竟無來去。

初發心菩薩，　及末世眾生，
欲求入佛道，　應如是修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Universal Eye, you should know:
every sentient being's
body and mind are alike illusion —
the body's marks belong to the four elements,
the mind's nature returns to the six sense-objects.
Once the substance of the four elements has each gone its own way,
who, then, is the one they came together as?
Practicing gradually in just this way,
everything, entirely, becomes pure,
unmoving, pervading the whole dharma-realm.

No acting, no stopping, no letting-be, no annihilating —
and no one there to do the realizing.

Every buddha-world
is exactly like a flower in empty space;
the three times are, throughout, equal —
in the end, with no coming and no going at all.

Bodhisattvas who have newly given rise to the mind,
and sentient beings of the final age,
who wish to enter the path to buddhahood,
should practice exactly in this way.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes that this chapter's verse compresses an unusually long prose passage into just three short stanzas — the two-emptiness contemplation of body and mind rendered in full, but the entire threefold dharmadhatu of true emptiness, unimpeded principle-and-phenomena, and unimpeded phenomenon-and-phenomenon reduced to the single line "unmoving, pervading the whole dharma-realm." He treats this as the clearest example in the sūtra of the verse working at a wholly different altitude than its prose counterpart — a reader who has just worked through the mani jewel, the sevenfold purity chain, and the hundred lamps in one room is being handed, in the verse, only the barest outline to hold the whole architecture together in memory.

Part 3 Complete

This closes Part 3, the sūtra's longest chapter: Universal Eye's request for an actual method, answered with the four-elements reflection on the body, the dissolving of the false conditioned mind, the mirror wiped clean of grime, the mani jewel radiant without polishing, the Huayan threefold dharmadhatu of true emptiness and mutual interpenetration, and the claim — carefully qualified by Zongmi — that sentient beings have, from the very beginning, already been buddhas. Part 4 turns to Vajragarbha's three formidable doubts about exactly this claim: if beings are originally buddhas, why does ignorance ever arise at all?
