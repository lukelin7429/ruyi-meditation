Curing Illusion With Illusion

This page carries Samantabhadra's question to the Buddha: if a practitioner's own body and mind are illusion, how can illusion be used to remove illusion — and if all illusion is finally extinguished, who is left to practice at all? The Buddha's answer supplies the sūtra's second great image, the fire-drill whose two rubbed sticks burn away to nothing, leaving only the fire they made, and the claim that runs through the rest of the sūtra: awakening is never harmed either by illusion's arising or by its ending.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 2 of 12 complete Source: Taishō T0842, 圓覺經 (tr. Buddhatrāta) Reading notes after Zongmi's 圓覺經略疏 (T1795)

Samantabhadra's Question

Chinese Source

於是普賢菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右遶三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！願為此會諸菩薩眾，及為末世一切眾生修大乘者，聞此圓覺清淨境界，云何修行？世尊！若彼眾生，知如幻者，身心亦幻，云何以幻還修於幻？若諸幻性，一切盡滅，則無有心，誰為修行？云何復說修行如幻？若諸眾生本不修行，於生死中常居幻化，曾不了知如幻境界，令妄想心云何解脫？願為末世一切眾生，作何方便漸次修習，令諸眾生永離諸幻。」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告普賢菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能為諸菩薩及末世眾生，修習菩薩如幻三昧，方便漸次，令諸眾生得離諸幻。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，普賢菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Samantabhadra rose from his seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — I ask that you explain, for the bodhisattvas of this assembly and for every sentient being of the final age who cultivates the Mahāyāna, having heard of this pure realm of Perfect Enlightenment, how one is to practice it. World-Honored One: if those sentient beings know that whoever is illusion, their own body and mind are illusion as well — then how can illusion be used to cultivate illusion? And if the nature of all illusion is entirely extinguished, then there is no mind left at all — who, then, does the practicing? And why does the teaching still speak of 'practicing as illusion'? Yet if sentient beings simply do not practice at all, remaining forever in illusion and transformation within birth-and-death, never coming to understand this illusion-like state, then how can their deluded, thought-bound minds ever be liberated? I ask that you show every sentient being of the final age what skillful means, and what gradual steps of cultivation, will let them leave every illusion behind for good." Having said this, he prostrated himself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Samantabhadra: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able, on behalf of the bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age, to cultivate the bodhisattva's illusion-like samādhi, its skillful means and its gradual steps, so that sentient beings may come to leave every illusion behind. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Samantabhadra, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads Samantabhadra's question as two distinct traps, not one. The first — "how can illusion cure illusion?" — follows directly from Mañjuśrī's chapter, where knowing body and mind are like a sky-flower already counted as the whole of practice; if so, what is there left to do? The second — "if all illusion is finally extinguished, there is no mind left; who practices?" — he traces to a different worry, that the sūtra's own logic of dissolving every trace, carried to its conclusion, seems to leave no one standing to do the dissolving. He glosses the "illusion" language itself with a stock five-part analysis he says applies to every illusion-simile the sūtra will use from here on: on the simile side, a silk cloth, a magician's skill, the conjured horse, the horse's being-then-not-being, and a fool who takes it for real; on the doctrinal side these correspond to true nature, the discriminating mind, dependent arising, the emptiness of self and dharmas, and the deluded clinging to self and dharmas as real. A third worry Zongmi draws out from the question's own momentum — if not practicing just leaves beings endlessly circling in illusion, is refusing to practice also wrong? — sets up the fourfold answer that follows: not a contradiction to resolve piece by piece, but a single teaching that dissolves all three worries by showing that neither acting nor not-acting was ever quite the right frame.

Illusion Arises From the Awakened Mind — and Awakening Is Never Broken

Chinese Source

「善男子！一切眾生種種幻化，皆生如來圓覺妙心，猶如空花從空而有，幻花雖滅空性不壞；眾生幻心還依幻滅，諸幻盡滅覺心不動。依幻說覺亦名為幻，若說有覺猶未離幻，說無覺者亦復如是。

是故，幻滅名為不動。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: the manifold illusory transformations of all sentient beings all arise from the tathāgata's wondrous mind of Perfect Enlightenment — just as a flower in the sky arises from the sky itself. Though the illusory flower vanishes, the nature of the sky is not thereby harmed. Just so, the illusory mind of sentient beings comes, in the end, to be dissolved by illusion itself, and once every illusion is entirely dissolved, the awakened mind remains unmoved.

To speak of awakening in dependence on illusion is itself still to name something illusory; to say that awakening exists is likewise not yet free of illusion; and to say that no awakening exists is exactly the same. This, then, is why the ending of illusion is called 'unmoved.'"

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi treats "all illusion arises from the wondrous mind of Perfect Enlightenment" as this section's foundation and expects a natural objection to follow immediately: if truth genuinely gives rise to falsehood, is truth not itself the source of falsehood — contradicting the earlier teaching that ignorance has no real basis at all? His answer turns on how "arise" is meant: falsehood relies on truth in order to appear, so truth is spoken of as its source, but this is exactly like a second moon appearing when one presses a finger against the eye — the second moon depends on the real moon to appear, yet the real moon itself never at any point becomes two. Present delusion sees falsehood; truth itself was never false. "The sūtra says it is illusion that arises from the awakened mind," he writes, "not that the awakened mind gives rise to illusion" — a distinction on which the whole answer rests.

On the threefold dismissal that closes this passage — awakening spoken of in dependence on illusion is itself illusion; saying awakening exists is not yet free of illusion; saying it does not exist is exactly the same — Zongmi reads each clause as retracting the position just offered, exactly as in Mañjuśrī's earlier fourfold release. A practitioner who has just been told illusion dissolves back into an unmoved awakened mind might now reach for "awakening" as something solid to hold on to; the text immediately withdraws it, then withdraws the withdrawal, so that no resting place, positive or negative, is left standing. Only then does it say the ending of illusion is "unmoved" — not because a thing called awakening was left untouched somewhere off to the side, but because there was never, at any point, anything there to move.

The Fire-Drill, and How Far "Leaving Illusion Behind" Goes

Chinese Source

「善男子！一切菩薩及末世眾生，應當遠離一切幻化虛妄境界，由堅執持遠離心故，心如幻者亦復遠離，遠離為幻亦復遠離，離遠離幻亦復遠離，得無所離即除諸幻。譬如鑽火兩木相因，火出木盡灰飛煙滅；以幻修幻亦復如是，諸幻雖盡不入斷滅。善男子！知幻即離，不作方便；離幻即覺，亦無漸次。一切菩薩及末世眾生依此修行，如是乃能永離諸幻。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: all bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age should leave behind every illusory, unreal state entirely. Because they firmly hold to this mind that leaves things behind, the mind that does the leaving — itself still illusion — must also be left behind. This leaving-behind, which is itself illusion, must also be left behind. And this leaving behind of the leaving-behind of illusion must also, in turn, be left behind. Only when there is nothing left to leave behind is every illusion truly removed. It is like drilling for fire with two pieces of wood rubbed against each other: the fire appears, the wood is consumed, the smoke disperses and the ashes scatter. Curing illusion with illusion is exactly the same — every illusion comes to an end, yet this does not fall into annihilation.

Good sons: to know illusion is already to have left it — no further skillful means is needed for this; and to leave illusion behind is already to be awakened — no further gradual stages are needed for this either. All bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age, practicing by relying on this, are thereby able to leave every illusion behind for good."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads the fourfold "leaving behind" as answering, point by point, the very worry Samantabhadra raised about an infinite regress: first one leaves behind illusory objects; then the mind that does the leaving, itself still illusion-like, is also left behind; then this act of leaving-behind, which is itself a kind of illusion, is left behind in turn; then even the leaving-behind of that leaving-behind is left behind. He offers a second, equally valid way of counting the same four steps — leave the delusion, leave the awakening, discard the leaving, discard the discarding — and notes that the process is not truly infinite, because it terminates: "when there is nothing further left to leave behind, all illusions are thereby removed." He glosses this with the Heze line: "delusion arising is itself awareness; when delusion ceases, awareness ceases too; when both delusion and awareness have ceased together, this is true suchness."

On the fire-drill, Zongmi maps its four elements onto the four stages: the two pieces of wood are the illusory delusion being cultivated and the illusion-like wisdom doing the cultivating; the smoke is the leaving-behind; the ash is the final relinquishing. He also catches what he takes to be a translation error — the source text says "the ashes fly, the smoke disperses" (灰飛煙滅), but by the natural order of a fire actually burning out, the smoke ought to disperse first and only then would ash be left to scatter; he judges this an artifact of the translators reversing the phrase, not a point of doctrine. On the closing claim — knowing illusion already is leaving it, with no further method needed — he supplies the sūtra's own later image for why: it is like a person who dreams he is wounded and, within the dream, seeks a doctor and medicine for it; on waking and realizing it was only a dream, what further remedy could there possibly be left to seek?

In Verse

Chinese Source

普賢汝當知，　一切諸眾生，
無始幻無明，　皆從諸如來，
圓覺心建立。　猶如虛空花，
依空而有相，　空花若復滅，
虛空本不動。　幻從諸覺生，
幻滅覺圓滿，　覺心不動故。

若彼諸菩薩，　及末世眾生，
常應遠離幻，　諸幻悉皆離。

如木中生火，　木盡火還滅，
覺則無漸次，　方便亦如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Samantabhadra, you should know:
every sentient being's
beginningless illusory ignorance
is entirely established
upon the tathāgatas' mind of Perfect Enlightenment —
just like a flower in empty space,
which has its mark only in dependence on that space;
and when the sky-flower vanishes again,
empty space was never moved to begin with.
Illusion arises from awakening itself;
when illusion ends, awakening is complete —
because the awakened mind was never moved at all.

If those bodhisattvas,
and sentient beings of the final age,
constantly leave illusion behind,
every illusion is thereby entirely left behind.

As fire arises from within wood,
the wood consumed, the fire itself then dies away —
awakening admits no gradual stages,
and its skillful means are exactly the same.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes that the verse compresses the prose's threefold dismissal of awakening — spoken in dependence on illusion, said to exist, said not to exist — into a single line, and drops the fire-drill's fuller unpacking (the leaving-behind of the leaving-behind, the smoke and the ash) down to its bare two-line image. He treats this as typical of how these closing verses relate to their prose counterparts throughout the sūtra: restating the chapter's structure at a much higher altitude, for readers who have already worked through the argument once and now want it held in a single, memorable shape.
