Three Doubts About Original Buddhahood

Universal Eye's chapter closed with the sūtra's boldest claim: sentient beings have, from the very beginning, already been buddhas. Vajragarbha now presses on it with three doubts that fold into one — if beings are originally buddhas, why does ignorance ever arise; if ignorance is original, why does the sūtra say beings are originally buddhas; and if beings become buddhas, could a buddha's own awakening ever unravel back into ignorance again? The answer supplies three of the sūtra's most durable images: a spinning eye that mistakes still water for moving water, a sky-flower that never returns once the disease that produced it is gone, and gold refined from ore that never reverts to ore.

Vajragarbha's Three Doubts

Chinese Source

於是金剛藏菩薩在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足右遶三匝，長跪叉手而白佛言：「大悲世尊！善為一切諸菩薩眾宣揚如來圓覺清淨大陀羅尼因地法行漸次方便，與諸眾生開發蒙昧；在會法眾承佛慈誨，幻翳朗然，慧目清淨。世尊！若諸眾生本來成佛，何故復有一切無明？若諸無明眾生本有，何因緣故，如來復說本來成佛？十方異生本成佛道，後起無明；一切如來，何時復生一切煩惱？唯願不捨無遮大慈，為諸菩薩開祕密藏，及為末世一切眾生，得聞如是修多羅教了義法門永斷疑悔。」作是語已五體投地，如是三請終而復始。

爾時，世尊告金剛藏菩薩言：「善哉！善哉！善男子！汝等乃能為諸菩薩及末世眾生，問於如來甚深祕密究竟方便，是諸菩薩最上教誨了義大乘，能使十方修學菩薩及諸末世一切眾生，得決定信，永斷疑悔。汝今諦聽！當為汝說。」時，金剛藏菩薩奉教歡喜，及諸大眾默然而聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then Vajragarbha rose from his seat in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled him three times to the right, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Greatly Compassionate World-Honored One — you have skillfully proclaimed, for the sake of every bodhisattva, the practice of the causal ground of the Tathāgata's pure, great dhāraṇī of Perfect Enlightenment, its gradual steps and skillful means, opening what was clouded and dim in sentient beings. The Dharma-assembly gathered here, receiving your compassionate instruction, finds its illusory cataracts now clear and its wisdom-eye pure. World-Honored One: if sentient beings are originally buddhas, why does ignorance of every kind still arise? And if that ignorance is itself something beings have had from the beginning, for what reason does the Tathāgata still say that they are originally buddhas? If beings of every kind throughout the ten directions originally accomplished the path to buddhahood and only afterward gave rise to ignorance, then might not any tathāgata, at some time, likewise give rise to every affliction all over again? I ask only that you, not withholding your unobstructed great compassion, open this secret treasury for the bodhisattvas and for every sentient being of the final age, so that, hearing this decisive teaching of the sūtras, they may forever cut off doubt and regret." Having said this, he prostrated himself, all five limbs to the ground, and repeated this same request three times, beginning again each time it ended.

At that time the World-Honored One said to Vajragarbha: "Excellent, excellent! Good sons — you are able to ask the Tathāgata about this most profound, secret, and ultimate skillful means, the supreme instruction given to the bodhisattvas, the decisive teaching of the Mahāyāna, able to bring bodhisattvas training throughout the ten directions, and every sentient being of the final age, to settled and decisive faith, forever cutting off doubt and regret. Now listen closely — I will explain it for you." Then Vajragarbha, rejoicing to receive this teaching, fell silent together with the whole assembly, and listened.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads Vajragarbha's question as one difficulty pressed from three directions rather than three independent objections: put logically, if beings are originally buddhas, ignorance ought never to have arisen in the first place; if it did arise despite that, then buddhahood and ignorance must be able to co-occur on equal footing, in which case a fully realized buddha ought, by the same logic, to be equally able to relapse into affliction at some future point. Caught between those two horns — nothing can start where there was originally nothing to start it, and nothing already accomplished should be able to unravel — the questioner is left with no way forward and no way back. Zongmi notes that no earlier sūtra addresses this precise difficulty directly; it is knowledge, he says, reserved for those who have already reached the grounds, now being opened for the first time to an ordinary audience — which is exactly why Vajragarbha's own name, hard and sharp as a vajra, fits a chapter built to dissolve a doubt this formidable.

A Spinning Eye Cannot See Water Stand Still

Chinese Source

「善男子！一切世界始終生滅，前後有無，聚散起止，念念相續，循環往復，種種取捨，皆是輪迴。未出輪迴而辨圓覺，彼圓覺性即同流轉；若免輪迴，無有是處。譬如動目能搖湛水；又如定眼猶迴轉火；雲駛月運，舟行岸移，亦復如是。善男子！諸旋未息，彼物先住尚不可得；何況輪轉生死垢心，曾未清淨，觀佛圓覺而不旋復？是故汝等便生三惑。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: every world's beginning and ending, arising and ceasing, before and after, being and non-being, gathering and dispersing, starting and stopping, thought after thought in unbroken succession, circling round and returning again, and every kind of grasping and rejecting — all of this is the turning wheel of saṃsāra. To try to discern Perfect Enlightenment without first having left this turning wheel is for that very nature of Perfect Enlightenment to be dragged along in the same turning — there is no way it could possibly escape the wheel under those conditions. It is like a moving eye that makes a body of still water appear to sway; or like fixing the eye on a whirling firebrand until it seems to trace a solid ring of flame; and it is exactly the same as clouds racing past so that the moon itself seems to be moving, or a boat moving forward so that the shore itself seems to be sliding past. Good sons: as long as the spinning has not yet stopped, one cannot even establish that the thing being watched was ever at rest to begin with — how much less, then, could a mind still stained by the turning of birth-and-death, never yet made pure, gaze upon the Buddha's Perfect Enlightenment without that very gazing itself becoming another turning? This, then, is exactly why you have given rise to these three doubts."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi is explicit that this passage does not yet answer Vajragarbha's question — it only diagnoses where the question itself went wrong, identifying it as the root of the doubt rather than a rebuttal of it. A questioner still caught inside the turning wheel of saṃsāra and using that very turning mind to size up Perfect Enlightenment cannot help but see Perfect Enlightenment itself as something caught in turning too — not because Perfect Enlightenment is actually moving, but because the instrument doing the looking is. The four similes work in pairs: the moving eye and the fixed eye both mistake something still (water, a single ember) for something in motion (waves, a ring of fire) because of a defect in the seeing itself; the racing clouds and the moving boat both mistake something still (the moon, the shoreline) for something in motion because of a mismatch between the observer's own frame of reference and what is actually being observed. All four converge on a single point: nothing has to be wrong with the water, the ember, the moon, or the shore for them to appear to move — it is enough that the eye, or the clouds, or the boat, are themselves in motion. Only once this misattribution is seen through — in the sections that follow — does the sūtra go on to actually answer the three doubts themselves.

The Sky-Flower Never Returns

Chinese Source

「善男子！譬如幻翳，妄見空花；幻翳若除，不可說言：『此翳已滅，何時更起一切諸翳？』何以故？翳、花二法，非相待故。亦如空花滅於空時，不可說言虛空何時更起空花？何以故？空本無花，非起滅故。生死、涅槃同於起滅，妙覺圓照離於花翳。善男子！當知虛空非是暫有，亦非暫無，況復如來圓覺隨順，而為虛空平等本性。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: it is like an illusory cataract that falsely produces the sight of flowers in the sky. Once that illusory cataract is removed, one cannot then say: 'now that this cataract is gone, when will it come back to produce flowers again?' Why is this? Because the cataract and the flower are not two things that depend on one another to occur. Likewise, when a sky-flower vanishes back into the sky, one cannot say: 'when will empty space produce a sky-flower again?' Why is this? Because the sky never had any flower in it to begin with, so there was never any real arising or ceasing of one. Birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are exactly the same in this respect, as an arising and a ceasing; the wondrous, perfectly luminous awakening is itself entirely apart from either the flower or the cataract. Good sons: know that empty space itself was never, even briefly, something that came to be, nor was it ever, even briefly, something that ceased to be — how much more so, then, is the Tathāgata's Perfect Enlightenment, in perfect accord with it, itself the very ground and equal nature of empty space."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi treats these two similes as answering the second and third doubts together — will ignorance recur once removed, and could a buddha ever relapse into affliction — by attacking the shared premise underneath both: that the cataract and the flower, or the sky and the flower, were ever a pair of things capable of causing or depending on one another at all. Because the flower never actually arose from anything real — the eye disease, not the sky, was always the true cause, as Mañjuśrī's chapter already established — asking "when will it come back" mistakes an illusion for a genuine occurrence with its own conditions for recurring. He anticipates the objection that eye diseases do, as a matter of ordinary fact, sometimes return, and answers that similes are never meant to be pressed past the single point they illustrate: "one merely takes the events of a single day — a person who sees flowers at dawn and whose eyes clear by breakfast; one does not press the question of what happens for the rest of that person's life." Vajragarbha's doubt, read this way, is really only the same mistake in disguise a second time — treating something that never actually happened as though it were an event with a history capable of repeating.

Refined Gold Never Reverts to Ore

Chinese Source

「善男子！如銷金鑛，金非銷有；既已成金，不重為鑛，經無窮時，金性不壞。不應說言本非成就，如來圓覺亦復如是。善男子！一切如來妙圓覺心本無菩提及與涅槃，亦無成佛及不成佛，無妄輪迴及非輪迴。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: it is like smelting gold ore. The gold does not come into being because of the smelting — but once it has become gold, it does not turn back into ore again, and though limitless time should pass, the nature of gold is never destroyed. One should not, on that account, say that the gold's being gold was not something already accomplished from the start. The Tathāgata's Perfect Enlightenment is exactly the same. Good sons: the wondrous mind of Perfect Enlightenment possessed by every tathāgata is fundamentally without bodhi and without nirvāṇa; it is likewise without becoming a buddha and without not becoming a buddha; without any false turning wheel of saṃsāra, and without any escape from that turning wheel either."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi treats the gold-ore simile as necessary precisely because the sky-flower simile, taken alone, proves too much: if birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are both nothing but arising-and-ceasing, equally illusory, then it seems the distinction between ordinary being and buddha collapses entirely, and there would be no reason a being couldn't just as easily un-become a buddha as become one. The gold simile restores what the flower simile could not supply on its own: gold does not exist because it was smelted — its nature as gold was already there, latent in the ore, before any fire touched it — yet once brought out, it never regresses to ore again, however long the passage of time. He is careful that the two similes divide the labor rather than compete: "the flower, from start to finish, was never really there at all; the ore only becomes gold once smelted" — one simile guards against a naive view that beings were never really buddhas, the other guards against a naive view that buddhahood, once attained, could be undone. Used alone, either simile risks a wrong view; used together, each corrects what the other, by itself, leaves open. Only after both are in place does the text draw its final, most radical conclusion — that even "attaining buddhahood" and "not attaining buddhahood," even "saṃsāra" and "escape from saṃsāra," are themselves still a pair of opposed terms, and Perfect Enlightenment stands prior to that opposition altogether, the way gold's nature stood prior to being asked whether it was, or was not yet, gold.

Why a Discriminating Mind Cannot Reach This at All

Chinese Source

「善男子！但諸聲聞所圓境界，身心語言皆悉斷滅，終不能至彼之親證所現涅槃，何況能以有思惟心測度如來圓覺境界？如取螢火燒須彌山，終不能著。以輪迴心生輪迴見，入於如來大寂滅海，終不能至。是故我說一切菩薩及末世眾生，先斷無始輪迴根本。善男子！有作思惟從有心起，皆是六塵妄想緣氣，非實心體，已如空花。用此思惟辨於佛境，猶如空花復結空果，展轉妄想，無有是處。善男子！虛妄浮心多諸巧見，不能成就圓覺方便。如是分別，非為正問。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good sons: even the realm the śrāvakas bring to completion — where body, mind, and speech are all entirely cut off — never actually reaches the nirvāṇa disclosed by that direct personal realization; how much less, then, could a mind still bound by deliberate thought ever gauge the Tathāgata's realm of Perfect Enlightenment? It is like trying to burn down Mount Sumeru with a firefly's light — it can never catch. To bring a mind still caught in the turning wheel, giving rise to views still caught in that same turning wheel, into the Tathāgata's great ocean of ultimate quiescence — this, too, can never be reached. This is why I say that all bodhisattvas and sentient beings of the final age must first cut off the root of beginningless saṃsāra. Good sons: deliberate thought arises from a mind that already grasps something as existing — it is entirely the false, conditioned energy of the six sense-objects, not the substance of the true mind at all, and is itself already just like a sky-flower. To use this kind of thought to try to gauge the Buddha's realm is like a sky-flower going on to bear fruit made of sky as well — turning delusion upon delusion, with no possibility of success. Good sons: a false and unsettled mind, however clever its views may seem, can never bring about the skillful means of Perfect Enlightenment. Discrimination of this kind is not, in the end, a right question at all."

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi reads this closing passage as circling back, deliberately, to indict the very act of asking the question in the way it was asked — not to scold Vajragarbha, whose asking he elsewhere praises as an act of great service to the assembly, but to make a structural point: even a śrāvaka's own hard-won realization, achieved by cutting off body, speech, and mind entirely, does not reach the buddha's own directly-realized nirvāṇa, so an ordinary discriminating mind — still generating deliberate thought, still implicitly assuming there is something that exists to be thought about — is even less equipped to size up a question this deep. He notes the apparent tension with the sūtra's earlier praise ("excellent, excellent!") and resolves it: the praise was for raising a question this consequential at all, since without it the doubt would fester unaddressed for beings of the final age; the rebuke that closes the chapter is for the specific manner of raising it — via calculating, comparing thought — which was never going to be capable of grasping the answer regardless of how the question was framed. Praise for the service, correction of the method: on Zongmi's reading, both are true at once, and neither cancels the other.

In Verse

Chinese Source

金剛藏當知，　如來寂滅性，
未曾有終始。　若以輪迴心，
思惟即旋復，　但至輪迴際，
不能入佛海。

譬如銷金鑛，　金非銷故有，
雖復本來金，　終以銷成就，
一成真金體，　不復重為鑛。

生死與涅槃，　凡夫及諸佛，
同為空華相。

思惟猶幻化，　何況詰虛妄？

若能了此心，　然後求圓覺。

Ru-Yi Translation

Vajragarbha, you should know:
the Tathāgata's nature of ultimate quiescence
never had any ending or beginning.
If one thinks with a mind still caught in the turning wheel,
that very thinking is itself another turning —
it only reaches the far edge of the turning wheel,
and can never enter the buddha's ocean.

It is like smelting gold ore:
the gold does not exist because of the smelting —
yet though it was gold all along,
it is only through the smelting that this is finally accomplished;
once it has become the true substance of gold,
it never again turns back into ore.

Birth-and-death and nirvāṇa,
the ordinary being and every buddha alike,
are equally the mark of a flower in the sky.

Thinking itself is already illusion and transformation —
how much more so, then, is interrogating what is already false?

If one can fully understand this mind,
only then should one go on to seek Perfect Enlightenment.

Reading note — after Zongmi

Zongmi notes that the verse omits the sky-flower-and-cataract simile of the chapter's middle section entirely, folding its conclusion straight into the closing line "birth-and-death and nirvāṇa … are equally the mark of a flower in the sky" — since, by that point in the sūtra, the image itself no longer needs restating in full. The verse's final couplet, he adds, has no prose counterpart at all: "if one can fully understand this mind, only then should one go on to seek Perfect Enlightenment" is the sūtra's own bridge into the next chapter, where Maitreya asks not about doubts concerning buddhahood already accomplished, but about the very different, much more practical question of how the ordinary, still-afflicted mind is to be understood and worked with in the first place.

Part 4 Complete

This closes Part 4: Vajragarbha's three doubts about original buddhahood, and the Buddha's answer in three matched similes — a spinning eye that cannot see water stand still, a sky-flower that never returns because it was never really there, and refined gold that never reverts to ore because its nature as gold was never in question.
