One Taxonomy, Three Depths.

Yogācāra's foundational classroom exercise is the five-position, hundred-dharma system (五位百法): every conditioned and unconditioned thing there is, sorted into mind, mental factors, form, formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned. This site's Yogācāra corpus already covers the system at treatise length — the hundred-fascicle Stages of Yogic Practice, the twenty-fascicle Illuminating the Sacred Teaching, the Abhidharmasamuccaya — but never at the entry level, where students traditionally begin. This collection supplies that beginning: the same taxonomy read three times, each time at a different depth, in the sequence students have studied it in for over a thousand years.

Why three texts, not one

These are three separate treatises, not three parts of a single work — which is why this collection reads differently from this site's other multi-part pages. The Hundred Dharmas Treatise (百法明門論, 578 characters) gives the bare skeleton: five categories, a hundred names, and not one definition, in the sole service of proving that a self is nowhere on the list. Vasubandhu's own Five Aggregates Treatise (五蘊論, 3,126 characters) supplies every definition the skeleton withheld, reorganized under the older frame of the five aggregates. And Sthiramati's Extended Five Aggregates Treatise (廣五蘊論, 5,726 characters) takes up that same text clause by clause and expands nearly every definition with an etymology, a worked example, or the reasoning behind it — CBETA cross-references the two five-aggregates texts against each other directly. Read start to finish, one doctrine is examined at three successively finer grains.

The traditional teaching sequence places the hundred-dharmas list first and the five-aggregates material second, which this collection follows. A reader in a hurry can stop after Part One and already have the complete taxonomy; a reader who wants the reasoning behind every term should continue through Parts Two and Three.

Read by Part

The traditional teaching sequence: the bare list first, its definitions second, and the definitions' own reasoning last.

Part 1 · T1614

The Hundred Dharmas Treatise

Vasubandhu's complete 578-character text: five categories, a hundred dharmas, numbered and named without a single definition — because the treatise's only real argument is the two selflessnesses its exhaustive list is built to prove.

Part 2 · T1612

The Five Aggregates Treatise

The same author's ninety-five "what is it?" questions, each answered in one clause — every one of the fifty-one mental factors defined, the ālayavijñāna proven, and the same material re-sorted twice more as sense fields and elements.

Part 3 · T1613

The Extended Five Aggregates Treatise

Sthiramati's expansion of Part Two, adding a function clause to nearly every definition, plus etymologies for "faculty" and "element," a philosophical aside on why an effect can borrow its cause's name, and a citation of the Buddha's own words.

Translation Notes

Recurring terms and cross-text divergences, kept consistent across all three parts and matched to the site's other Yogācāra pages where the same term appears. See also this site's note on translation method.

天親 / 世親 — one author, two Chinese namesBoth render Vasubandhu; 天親 is the older transliteration used in Part 1's title line, 世親 the newer one Xuánzàng preferred in Part 2.
五位百法 — the five categories, hundred dharmasMind (8), mental factors (51), form (11), formations dissociated from mind (24), and the unconditioned (6) — the taxonomy all three texts share.
安慧 — SthiramatiAuthor of Part 3's expansion; also the commentator behind this site's Abhidharmasamuccaya pages (T1606).
謂……為性 / ……為業 — nature and functionEvery definition's core grammar: what a thing is (Parts 1–2), and, added in Part 3, what it does.
無想報 / 無想所有 / 無想天 — three names, one referentThe karmic fruit of mistaking the no-thought attainment for liberation, named differently in each of the three texts.
Term order across the three textsThe wholesome eleven, the root six, and the secondary twenty are each listed in a different internal order in Part 1 versus Parts 2–3 — preserved as each text's own, not harmonized.
