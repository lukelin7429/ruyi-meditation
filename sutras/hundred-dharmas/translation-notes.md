# The Hundred Dharmas — Translation Notes

These notes record recurring translation decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of the *Hundred Dharmas* collection: the *Mahāyāna Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas That Clarify the Gate to the Teaching* (大乘百法明門論, Taishō T1614), the *Mahāyāna Five Aggregates Treatise* (大乘五蘊論, Taishō T1612), and the *Mahāyāna Extended Five Aggregates Treatise* (大乘廣五蘊論, Taishō T1613). They exist so that vocabulary stays consistent across all three parts, and matches the site's larger Yogācāra corpus — above all *Illuminating the Sacred Teaching* (顯揚聖教論), whose own translation notes supplied most of this collection's terminology before a single sentence of these three texts was translated.

## The texts, and why they are read together

- T1614, traditionally attributed to Vasubandhu, translated by Xuánzàng: 578 characters, a bare inventory of five categories and one hundred dharmas with no definitions, closing on the two selflessnesses the list exists to prove.
- T1612, by Vasubandhu, translated by Xuánzàng: 3,126 characters, the same material reorganized under the older five-aggregates frame, with every term now defined in one clause.
- T1613, by Sthiramati (安慧), translated by Divākara (地婆訶羅): 5,726 characters, an expansion of T1612 that keeps its ninety-five questions nearly verbatim and adds, to nearly every answer, an etymology, a function clause, or a worked example. CBETA marks T1612 and T1613 as cross-references of one another (T1612's header carries `[cf. No. 1613]` and vice versa).
- Directory slug: `hundred-dharmas`, chosen over `five-aggregates-treatises` because the taxonomy common to all three texts — the five categories, hundred dharmas — is what the collection is actually about; the second and third texts organize the identical material by aggregate rather than by category, but do not change what is being catalogued. No Sanskrit title is used as the collection's own name: T1614's Sanskrit original does not survive, and Pañcaskandhaka (the Sanskrit title of T1612 and its shared root with T1613) covers only two of the three texts.

## One author, two Chinese names

T1614's colophon reads 天親菩薩造 ("composed by Bodhisattva Tiānqīn"); T1612's reads 世親菩薩造 ("composed by Bodhisattva Shìqīn"). Both are Vasubandhu — 天親 is the older transliteration, 世親 the newer rendering Xuánzàng generally preferred and which this site uses elsewhere (*Viṃśatikā*, *Madhyāntavibhāga*, the *Abhidharmasamuccaya*'s root author's brother). The same Taishō volume carries both names for the identical historical person. We translate both as "Vasubandhu" in running English prose and note the Chinese variant where the colophon itself is quoted.

## Reading-note sourcing

Per this site's commentator-vetting convention (see the note on `sutra-translation-page-conventions` for the copyright-clearance rule behind it), reading notes should be grounded in a named commentator wherever one exists, rather than presented as unattributed analysis. This collection uses three different sources, one per part, because the three texts sit at three different points in the commentarial chain:

- **Part 1** (T1614): "Reading note — after Kuījī," drawing on 窺基's 大乘百法明門論解 (T1836, annotated by Kuījī, 632–682, with a Ming-dynasty revision by 普泰) — a dedicated commentary on exactly this text, matching the precedent already set for Kuījī on this site's *Viṃśatikā* pages. Both authors are many centuries past any copyright concern.
- **Part 2** (T1612): "Reading note — after Sthiramati," treating T1613 itself as T1612's commentary — which is what it actually is: Sthiramati's own clause-by-clause expansion of Vasubandhu's shorter text, adding etymologies, function clauses, and citations T1612 omits. No further external commentary was needed or used.
- **Part 3** (T1613): "Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center." T1613 is already commentary-tier writing; no dedicated sub-commentary on it was found in the source library, so its own reading notes are the translator's own analysis, not attributed to a named patriarch. This matches the labeling already used on this site's *Ālambanaparīkṣā* (T1624) page, which carries the same honest attribution for the same reason.

## Where the three texts genuinely disagree

Luke's brief for this collection asked that divergences in term order, wording, and count be surfaced rather than smoothed over. The most consequential:

- **不正見 vs. 見** (T1614 vs. *Illuminating the Sacred Teaching*): T1614 names the sixth root affliction with the fuller term 不正見, "incorrect view"; Asaṅga's treatise abbreviates to plain 見. Both are translated "wrong view." T1612/T1613 write plain 見 as well, agreeing with *Illuminating the Sacred Teaching* against T1614.
- **Six kinds of the unconditioned vs. eight vs. four**: T1614 counts six (splitting off a single, unified suchness); *Illuminating the Sacred Teaching* fascicle 1 counts eight (splitting suchness three ways, by wholesome/unwholesome/indeterminate object); T1612 and T1613 count only four (omitting the immovable and the cessation of perception and feeling, since those meditative-attainment states are already covered under formations dissociated from mind). All four counts describe the same underlying scheme at different resolutions.
- **Internal ordering of the fifty-one mental factors**: T1614's wholesome eleven run 信精進慚愧無貪無嗔無癡輕安不放逸行捨不害 (diligence in second place); T1612 and T1613 both run 信慚愧無貪無瞋無癡精進輕安不放逸捨不害 (diligence in seventh place, matching *Illuminating the Sacred Teaching*'s own order). T1614's root six end in 不正見; T1612/T1613 end in 疑. T1614's twenty secondary afflictions and four indeterminate factors are also sequenced differently from T1612/T1613 (忿恨惱覆… and 睡眠惡作… in T1614, against 忿恨覆惱… and 惡作睡眠… in T1612/T1613). We have preserved each text's own order throughout rather than harmonizing them.
- **慢過慢 vs. 過過慢**: T1612 and T1613 name the third of the seven conceits differently — "conceit exceeding conceit" versus "excessive conceit exceeding excessive conceit" — describing the identical mental state. Both are translated "conceit exceeding excessive conceit."
- **忘念 vs. 失念**: T1612 uses 忘念 where T1613 and *Illuminating the Sacred Teaching* use 失念 for the same secondary affliction, "forgetfulness." Same English both times.
- **惛沈 vs. 昏沈**: graphic variants of the same word ("torpor") across T1612 and T1613.

## What T1613 adds beyond T1612

Sthiramati's expansion is not a paraphrase; it follows T1612's own ninety-five questions almost verbatim and then, for nearly every answer, adds material T1612 omits:

- A function clause (……為業) after nearly every mental-factor definition, stating what the factor *does* in addition to what it *is*.
- Etymologies for technical terms T1612 simply uses without explanation — 根 ("faculty," glossed as "ruling master") and 界 ("element," glossed as "what sustains its own nature and the form it produces").
- A philosophical aside on why an effect can borrow its cause's name (冷 "cold" really means the desire for warmth; 惡作 "regret" really means remorse over a prior deed), invoked twice, with a direct cross-reference between the two occurrences.
- A citation of the Buddha's own words on the meaning of "aggregate" (the sevenfold classification of form: past/future/present, internal/external, coarse/fine, superior/inferior, far/near).
- The etymology of 薩迦耶 (satkāya), explaining how the compound's two halves individually refute permanence and unity.
- An explicit tally closing the discussion of consciousness — 此八名識蘊, "these eight are named the aggregate of consciousness" — that T1612 leaves implicit.

The one substantial passage T1613 does *not* expand is the closing classification of the eighteen elements (幾有色、幾無色…), which it reproduces almost clause for clause from T1612 — the clearest sign in the whole text that this is commentary on a source, not an independent composition.

## A note on 日親

T1613's definition of resolve (勝解) cites "日親" — literally "Kinsman of the Sun" — for the five similes of the aggregates (foam, a bubble, a mirage, a plantain tree, an illusion). This is an epithet for the Buddha himself, referencing the Śākya clan's traditional solar lineage, not a reference to any human commentator; it is not another name for Vasubandhu, despite the superficial resemblance to 世親 ("kinsman of the world").
