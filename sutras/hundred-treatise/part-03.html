Giving, Precepts, Wisdom, and Letting Merit Go

Discarding Sin and Merit, concluded捨罪福品第一

The chapter now delivers what its title promised. First the Buddha's threefold division of persons — giving for the low, precepts for the middling, wisdom for the high — and then, immediately, the division of each of those three into pure and impure: a merchant's calculation found inside almsgiving, lust inside celibacy, and inside knowledge an enemy who arrives as a friend. Even the Buddha's own two teachers are named and set aside. And then the chapter asks for the thing nobody expects to be asked for. Not the sins. The merit.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T30, no. 1569, 百論 Āryadeva (提婆), with Vasu's commentary (婆藪開士釋) Kumārajīva, revised 404 CE Reading notes: Ru-Yi, original

1. The Man Who Is Thinking About Nothing

Part 2 ended with the Outsider's last retreat: stopping evil is a species of good dharma, so naming the wider term should be enough. The answer is not an argument from definitions. It is a case — a specific person in a specific state, for whom the two terms come apart.

Chinese Source · 捨罪福品第一

內曰：應說惡止善行。何以故？惡止名受戒時息諸惡，善行名修習善法。若但說善行福不說惡止者，有人受戒惡止，若心不善、若心無記，是時不行善故不應有福，是時惡止故亦有福。是故應說惡止，亦應說善行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Insider: Both "evil stopped" and "good practiced" must be said. Why? "Stopping evil" names the bringing to rest of all evils at the moment one takes the precepts; "practicing good" names the cultivation of good dharmas. Suppose one spoke only of the merit of practicing good and did not speak of stopping evil. There is a man who has taken the precepts, so that his evil is stopped, but whose mind at a given moment is not good, or is neutral. At that time he is not practicing good, and so on your account he ought to have no merit — yet at that time his evil is stopped, and so he does have merit. Therefore stopping evil must be spoken of, and practicing good must be spoken of too.

是惡止善行法，隨眾生意故，佛三種分別：下中上人施戒智(修妬路)。行者有三種：下智人教布施，中智人教持戒，上智人教智慧。布施名利益他捨財相應思。及起身口業持戒名，若口語、若心生、若受戒，從今日不復作三種身邪行、四種口邪行。智慧名諸法相中心定不動。

This dharma of stopping evil and practicing good, the Buddha divided in three according to the dispositions of beings: for persons low, middling and high — giving, precepts, wisdom.sūtra Practitioners are of three kinds: those of low wisdom he taught giving; those of middling wisdom he taught the keeping of precepts; those of high wisdom he taught wisdom. "Giving" names the intention associated with relinquishing wealth so as to benefit another, together with the acts of body and speech that arise from it. "Keeping precepts" names this: whether by word spoken, or by a movement of mind, or by formally taking the precepts — "from this day I shall not again commit the three kinds of wrong bodily conduct or the four kinds of wrong verbal conduct." "Wisdom" names the mind settled and unmoving amid the marks of things.

何以說下中上？利益差降故。布施者少利益，是名下智；持戒者中利益，是名中智；智慧者上利益，是名上智。復次施報下，戒報中，智報上，是故說下中上智。

Why are they called low, middling and high? Because the benefit descends by degrees. Giving has the least benefit, and is therefore called low wisdom; keeping precepts has middling benefit, and is called middling wisdom; wisdom has the highest benefit, and is called high wisdom. Furthermore the fruit of giving is low, the fruit of precepts middling, the fruit of wisdom high — and so they are spoken of as low, middling and high wisdom.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The argument turns on a technical category that does a great deal of quiet work in Buddhist psychology: 無記, the indeterminate or morally neutral state of mind — neither wholesome nor unwholesome. Most of anyone's waking life is spent in it.

So picture the man. He has undertaken the precepts. He is not at this moment cultivating anything; he is walking down a road thinking about his dinner. Is he accumulating merit? On the Outsider's account, no: nothing good is being practiced. But his evil is stopped, and it is stopped continuously, whether or not he is attending to it — that is what taking a precept accomplishes. So merit is accruing to a man who is doing nothing in particular. The two terms have come apart, and the case that separates them is not an exotic one. It is the ordinary condition of a practitioner most of the time.

One point of punctuation to flag. The received text runs 布施名利益他捨財相應思。及起身口業持戒名，若口語……, which puts 及起身口業 with the definition of giving, as we have translated it. It can also be read as beginning the definition of precepts — "and as for the arising of acts of body and speech, 'keeping precepts' names…" The sentence is awkward either way, and the phrase sits on the seam between two definitions; we follow the received punctuation and note the alternative rather than tidying it.

2. Almsgiving as Trade

The Outsider now asks the obvious question about the three-tier scheme: does it really consign every giver to the bottom rank? The answer introduces the move that governs the rest of the chapter. Each of the three practices splits in two, and the split is not by how much is done but by what is wanted.

Chinese Source

外曰：布施者皆是下智不？

內曰：不然。何以故？施有二種：一者不淨，二者淨行。不淨施是名下智人。

外曰：何等名不淨施？

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: Are all who give of low wisdom?

Insider: Not so. Why? Because giving is of two kinds: first the impure, second pure conduct. It is impure giving that marks a person of low wisdom.

Outsider: What is called impure giving?

內曰：為報施是不淨，如市易故(修妬路)。報有二種：現報、後報。現報者，名稱敬愛等。後報者，後世富貴等。是名不淨施。所以者何？還欲得故。譬如賈客遠到他方，雖持雜物多所饒益，然非憐愍眾生，以自求利故，是業不淨。布施求報亦復如是。

Insider: Giving for a return is impure, because it is trade.sūtra A return is of two kinds: the present return and the later return. The present return is reputation, respect, affection and the like; the later return is wealth and rank in a future life and the like. This is what is called impure giving. Why? Because one is after getting something back. It is like a merchant who travels far into another country: though he carries goods of many kinds and does a great deal of good with them, it is not out of pity for beings — he is seeking his own profit, and so the business is impure. Giving in search of a return is just the same.

外曰：何等名淨施？內曰：若人愛敬利益他故，不求今世後世報。如眾菩薩及諸上人行清淨施，是名淨施。

Outsider: What is called pure giving?

Insider: When a person gives out of love and respect, in order to benefit another, seeking no return either in this life or in a later one — as the bodhisattvas and those of high attainment practice pure giving — this is what is called pure giving.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The merchant is a carefully fair image, and its fairness is what makes it bite. Vasu grants everything favorable that can be granted: the trader carries goods that people need, into places that lack them, and 多所饒益 — he does a great deal of good. The benefit is real and it is not being denied. The one thing withheld is the motive: 然非憐愍眾生, it is not out of pity for beings.

So the criticism is not that gift-with-expectation fails to help anyone. It is that it belongs to a different category of act — commerce rather than generosity — and that the giver has, without noticing, entered into an exchange. And the two returns named cover the whole territory. The crude version is obvious enough: giving in order to be reborn wealthy. The subtle version is the one that catches actual practitioners, and it is listed first: 名稱敬愛, reputation, and being respected and liked. A person who would be embarrassed to admit hoping for a mansion in the next life may be entirely unembarrassed about hoping to be thought generous in this one, and the treatise puts both on the same side of the line.

3. The Ram Backing Up

The same test is applied to the middling practice, and here the impure version has a name and a story attached. The image is coarse and it is meant to be.

Chinese Source

外曰：持戒皆是中智人不？

內曰：不然。何以故？持戒有二種：一者不淨，二者淨。不淨持戒者，名中智人。

外曰：何等不淨持戒？

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: Are all who keep the precepts of middling wisdom?

Insider: Not so. Why? Because keeping precepts is of two kinds: first the impure, second the pure. It is one who keeps precepts impurely that is called a person of middling wisdom.

Outsider: What is impure keeping of precepts?

內曰：持戒求樂報，為婬欲故，如覆相(修妬路)。樂報有二種：一者生天，二者人中富貴。若持戒求天上，與天女娛樂，若人中受五欲樂。所以者何？為婬欲故。如覆相者，內欲他色、外詐親善，是名不淨持戒。如阿難語難陀：

Ru-Yi Translation

Insider: Keeping precepts in search of a pleasant fruit, and for the sake of lust — as with a covered appearance.sūtra A pleasant fruit is of two kinds: first, birth among the gods; second, wealth and rank among men. One may keep the precepts seeking the heavens, to take pleasure there with the goddesses, or seeking the enjoyment of the five sense-pleasures among men. And why is this so? Because it is for the sake of lust. As for "a covered appearance": inwardly one desires another's body while outwardly feigning friendly goodness. This is what is called impure keeping of precepts. As Ānanda said to Nanda:

Quoted Verse · 阿難語難陀

「如羝羊相觸，　將前而更却；

汝為欲持戒，　其事亦如是。

身雖能持戒，　心為欲所牽，

斯業不清淨，　何用是戒為？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Like rams that butt against each other,

drawing back the better to drive forward:

you keep the precepts for desire's sake,

and the matter stands just so.

 

Though the body can keep the precepts,

the mind is dragged along by desire;

such work as this is not pure —

what use is a discipline like that?

外曰：何等名淨持戒？內曰：行者作是念，一切善法戒為根本，持戒之人則心不悔，不悔則歡喜，歡喜則心樂，心樂得一心，一心則生實智，實智生則得厭，得厭則離欲，離欲得解脫，解脫得涅槃。是名淨持戒。

Outsider: What is called pure keeping of precepts?

Insider: The practitioner reflects thus: of all good dharmas, discipline is the root. One who keeps the precepts has a mind without regret; without regret, there is gladness; being glad, the mind is at ease; at ease, it attains one-pointedness; one-pointed, true knowledge arises in it; true knowledge arisen, there is disenchantment; disenchanted, one is freed of desire; freed of desire, one attains liberation; liberated, one attains nirvāṇa. This is what is called pure keeping of precepts.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ram is the sharpest image in the chapter and it needs its mechanics spelled out: fighting rams back away from each other in order to charge harder. The retreat is not a retreat. It is part of the attack, and it exists for the sake of the impact.

That is the accusation against a celibacy undertaken for the sake of pleasure later. Such abstinence is not the opposite of indulgence; it is a run-up to it. And the last line lands with real force precisely because it does not say the discipline is being broken. It grants that the body is keeping it perfectly — 身雖能持戒 — and asks what such an intact and well-kept discipline is actually for.

Nanda is the Buddha's half-brother, and the story behind the rebuke is one of the best known in the canon. Ordained reluctantly and still pining for his wife, he was shown the nymphs of the heavens and told he could have them by practicing; he practiced with tremendous zeal, and his fellow monks would not speak to him. Ānanda's verse is the moment the strategy is named out loud. The story ends well — Nanda eventually understands and attains arhatship — but the treatise cites only the rebuke, because what it needs is the diagnosis, not the cure.

The definition of pure precepts that follows is not Āryadeva's invention. It is a chain of conditions found in the early discourses in almost exactly this sequence, running from non-regret through gladness and concentration to liberation, and the point of quoting it whole is its shape: each link is the natural consequence of the one before, with nothing willed or extracted. Discipline undertaken this way is not a price paid for a reward. It is the first link of something that unrolls on its own.

4. An Enemy Who Arrives as a Friend

The third practice gets the same treatment, and the Outsider spots his opening. If wisdom is what makes a person high, then the great contemplatives outside the Buddhist fold must be high — and he names two of them who are impossible to dismiss lightly.

Chinese Source

外曰：若上智者，欝陀羅伽等為上(修妬路)。若行智人，是名上智。今欝陀羅伽、阿羅邏外道等應為上智人。

內曰：不然。何以故？智亦有二種：一者不淨，二者淨。

外曰：何等名不淨智？

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: If wisdom makes one high, then Udraka and the rest are high.sūtra Whoever practices wisdom is called of high wisdom. Then Udraka, Ārāḍa and the other outsiders must be persons of high wisdom.

Insider: Not so. Why? Because wisdom too is of two kinds: first the impure, second the pure.

Outsider: What is called impure wisdom?

內曰：為世界繫縛故不淨，如怨來親(修妬路)。世界智能增長生死。所以者何？此智還繫縛故。譬如怨家，初詐親附，久則生害。世界智亦如是。

Insider: Because it binds one to the world, it is impure — like an enemy who comes as a friend.sūtra Worldly knowledge increases birth and death. Why? Because such knowledge binds one back again. It is like an enemy who at first feigns intimacy and attachment, and in the long run works harm. Worldly knowledge is just so.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The two names are not chosen at random, and a reader who does not recognize them will miss how pointed the objection is. Ārāḍa Kālāma and Udraka Rāmaputra were the Buddha's own teachers before his awakening — the two masters under whom Siddhārtha studied, reaching the sphere of nothingness with the first and the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception with the second, and leaving each because what he had attained did not lead to the end of suffering. They are the most formidable contemplatives the tradition itself acknowledges, and it acknowledges them by name because the Buddha's own biography requires it.

So the Outsider is not scraping. He is saying: your own scriptures concede that these men achieved states you cannot deny; by your own criterion they are of the highest rank. The answer does not dispute the attainment for a moment. It applies the same split as before — impure and pure — and locates the impurity not in any deficiency of the knowledge but in what it does. 此智還繫縛故: this knowledge binds one back again.

And the simile is unusually cold. Not a false friend, not a disappointment — an enemy, one who feigns closeness from the first meeting and does harm in the long run. What is being described is a genuine attainment that functions as a trap precisely because it is genuine: it satisfies, and what satisfies does not release. The refined states are more dangerous than the coarse ones for exactly the reason they are better.

5. Taking Merit Up, and Setting It Down

The Outsider immediately asks the right question. If refined knowledge keeps a person circling, do giving and precepts do the same? The answer is yes — and with that the chapter arrives at the business its title announced.

Chinese Source

外曰：但是智能增長生死？施戒亦爾耶？內曰：取福捨惡是行法(修妬路)。福名福報。

外曰：若福名福報者，何以修妬路中但言福？

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: Is it only knowledge that increases birth and death? Do giving and precepts do so as well?

Insider: Taking up merit and discarding evil is a dharma of faring on.sūtra "Merit" here names the fruit of merit.

Outsider: If "merit" names the fruit of merit, why does the sūtra line say only "merit"?

內曰：福名因，福報名果。或說因為果、或說果為因，此中說因為果。譬如食千兩金，金不可食，因金得食，故名食金。又如見畫，言是好手，因手得畫，故名好手。取名著，著福報惡先已說。行名將人常行生死中。

Insider: "Merit" names the cause and "fruit of merit" names the effect. Sometimes the cause is spoken of under the name of the effect, and sometimes the effect under the name of the cause; and here the cause is spoken of under the name of the effect. It is like saying that a man eats a thousand ounces of gold. Gold cannot be eaten; but it is by means of the gold that he gets his food, and so it is called eating gold. Or like looking at a painting and saying "that is a fine hand": it is by means of the hand that the painting was got, and so it is called a fine hand. "Taking up" names attachment — and attachment to the fruit of merit, and to evil, has been spoken of already. "Faring" names what leads a person to fare on always within birth and death.

外曰：何等是不行法？內曰：俱捨(修妬路)俱名福報罪報，捨名心不著。心不著福，不復往來五道，是名不行法。

Outsider: What then is the dharma of not faring on?

Insider: Discard both.sūtra "Both" names the fruit of merit and the fruit of offense. "Discard" names the mind's not being attached. When the mind is not attached to merit, one no longer comes and goes among the five destinies. This is what is called the dharma of not faring on.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two idioms are being explained here, and both are ordinary Chinese speech pressed into service as a lesson in reading. A man "eats a thousand ounces of gold" — nobody eats metal; the gold buys the meal, and the cause has borrowed the name of its effect. A connoisseur looks at a painting and says "a fine hand" — the hand is not on the wall; the hand made what is on the wall. Vasu is teaching the reader a habit that this treatise will demand constantly: when a word seems to be pointing at the wrong thing, ask whether cause and effect have swapped names.

But the load-bearing sentence is the gloss on 取: "Taking up" names attachment. Everything turns on this. The instruction is not to stop giving, or to abandon the precepts, or to stop doing good. It is to stop taking hold of what the good produces. And 捨名心不著 makes the same point from the other side: discarding is not disposal, it is the mind's not clinging. Nothing is thrown away. What ends is the grip.

Notice too the deliberate pairing of 行法 and 不行法, which we render as the dharma of faring on and of not faring on. 行 here is not "practice" but travel — Vasu says so outright: what leads a person to fare on always within birth and death. Merit and offense are both modes of transport. One is a comfortable carriage and one is a cart to the lower realms, and the chapter's proposal is to get out of the vehicle.

6. A Pleasant Feeling Is Painful When It Ends

The Outsider objects on the best possible authority. The fruits of merit are excellent, everyone wants them, and the Buddha himself told the monks not to be afraid of merit. On top of which — a fair procedural complaint — no reason has yet been given for discarding it.

Chinese Source

外曰：福不應捨，以果報妙故，亦不說因緣故(修妬路)。諸福果報妙，一切眾生常求妙果，云何可捨？又如佛言：「諸比丘！於福莫畏。」汝今又不說因緣，是故不應捨福。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: Merit should not be discarded, because its fruit is excellent, and because you give no reason.sūtra The fruits of merit are excellent, and all beings are forever seeking an excellent fruit — how could it be discarded? Moreover the Buddha said: "Monks, do not be afraid of merit." And you have given no reason for it. Therefore merit should not be discarded.

內曰：福滅時苦(修妬路)。福名福報，滅名失壞。福報滅時，離所樂事，生大憂苦。如佛說：「樂受生時樂、住時樂，滅時苦。」是故應捨福。又如佛言於福莫畏者，助道應行故。如佛說：「福尚應捨，何況罪。」

Insider: When merit ceases, there is suffering.sūtra "Merit" names the fruit of merit, and "cease" names its being lost and broken up. When the fruit of merit ceases, one is parted from what one enjoyed, and great grief and suffering arise. As the Buddha said: "A pleasant feeling is pleasant when it arises, pleasant while it lasts, and painful when it ceases." Therefore merit should be discarded. And as for the Buddha's saying "do not be afraid of merit" — that was because what assists the path should be practiced. As the Buddha said: "Even merit is to be discarded; how much more offense."

外曰：罪福相違故。汝言福滅時苦者，罪生住時應樂。

內曰：罪住時苦(修妬路)。罪名罪報，罪報生時苦，何況住時。如佛說：「苦受生時苦、住時苦，滅時樂。」汝言罪福相違故，罪生時應樂者，今當答汝，何不言福罪相違故，罪滅時樂、生住時苦？

Outsider: Offense and merit are opposites. If, as you say, there is suffering when merit ceases, then there ought to be pleasure when an offense arises and while it lasts.

Insider: While an offense lasts, there is suffering.sūtra "Offense" names the fruit of offense. The fruit of offense is painful when it arises — how much more so while it lasts. As the Buddha said: "A painful feeling is painful when it arises, painful while it lasts, and pleasant when it ceases." You say that since offense and merit are opposites there ought to be pleasure when an offense arises; and here is my answer to you. Why not say instead that since merit and offense are opposites, there is pleasure when an offense ceases, and suffering when it arises and while it lasts?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reason finally given is a single clause, and it is the whole argument of the chapter: when merit ceases, there is suffering. Not "merit is bad." Not "merit is unreal." Merit is exactly as good as advertised, and that is the problem — a good thing that ends leaves a hole precisely the size of how good it was. The scriptural formula quoted is a standard analysis of feeling, and reading the two versions side by side shows why it is quoted: a pleasant feeling is pleasant, pleasant, and then painful; a painful feeling is painful, painful, and then pleasant. Each has its sting at the opposite end. The merit is not being denied its two-thirds of pleasantness. It is being shown its final third.

The handling of the Buddha's own words is worth watching, since the Outsider has quoted scripture and scripture must be answered. The reply does not deny the quotation or explain it away. It supplies the context — merit is not to be feared because what assists the path should be practiced — and then produces a second saying that settles the order between them. That second line, 福尚應捨，何況罪, is the one Zhiyi cites from this treatise in the Great Calming and Contemplation, six centuries later and already in this compressed form. It travelled well.

The Outsider's symmetry objection is a good one and the reply is better. He reasons: opposites should behave oppositely, so if merit stings at the end, offense should please at the beginning. The answer does not break the symmetry — it points out that the Outsider has applied it to the wrong end. Run it correctly and offense is pleasant when it ceases, which is precisely what the canonical formula says and precisely what anyone who has waited out a punishment knows.

7. The Horse Sacrifice

The Outsider has one card left, and it is a strong one within his own system. Grant that ordinary merit ends and stings when it ends. But his scriptures describe a merit that does not end — and he names the rite that produces it.

Chinese Source

外曰：常福無捨因緣故不應捨(修妬路)。汝說捨福，因緣滅時苦。今常福報中無滅苦，故不應捨。如經說：能作馬祀，是人度衰老死，福報常、生處常。是福不應捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: Since permanent merit affords no ground for discarding, it should not be discarded.sūtra Your reason for discarding merit was the suffering at its ceasing. But in the fruit of permanent merit there is no suffering of ceasing, and so it should not be discarded. As our scripture says: whoever can perform the horse sacrifice passes beyond decay and death, his fruit of merit is permanent and his place of birth is permanent. Such merit is not to be discarded.

內曰：福應捨，二相故(修妬路)。是福有二相：能與樂、能與苦。如雜毒飯，食時美、欲消時苦。福亦如是。復次有福報是樂因，多受則苦因。譬如近火，止寒則樂，轉近燒身則苦。是故福二相，二相故無常，是以應捨。

Insider: Merit should be discarded, because it has two marks.sūtra This merit has two marks: it can give pleasure and it can give pain. It is like food mixed with poison, delicious in the eating and painful when it comes to be digested; merit is just so. Furthermore, a fruit of merit is a cause of pleasure, but taken in quantity it becomes a cause of pain. It is like drawing near a fire: while it stops the cold it is pleasant, but as one goes closer it burns the body and is painful. So merit has two marks; having two marks it is impermanent; and being impermanent, it should be discarded.

又汝言馬祀福報常者，但有言說，無因緣故(修妬路)。馬祀福報實無常。何以故？馬祀業因緣有量故。世間因若有量，果亦有量。如泥團小，瓶亦小。是故馬祀，業有量故無常。復次聞汝天有瞋恚，共鬪相惱，故不應常。又汝馬祀等業，從因緣生故皆無常。

And as for your saying that the fruit of merit of the horse sacrifice is permanent — that is words only, since you give no reason.sūtra The fruit of merit of the horse sacrifice is in truth impermanent. Why? Because the causes and conditions of the act of horse sacrifice are of limited measure. In the world, if the cause is of limited measure, the fruit is of limited measure too — as when the lump of clay is small, the pot is small also. Therefore the horse sacrifice, its act being of limited measure, is impermanent. Furthermore, one hears that your gods have anger, and fight and vex one another; so they cannot be permanent. And your acts of horse sacrifice and the rest all arise from causes and conditions, and are therefore all impermanent.

復次有漏淨福無常故尚應捨，何況雜罪福(修妬路)。如馬祀業中有殺等罪故。復次如僧佉經言，祀法不淨、無常、勝負相故，是以應捨。

Furthermore, since even pure merit that is still with outflows is impermanent and therefore to be discarded, how much more merit mixed with offense.sūtra For in the act of horse sacrifice there is the offense of killing and the rest. Furthermore, as the Sāṃkhya text itself says, the rite of sacrifice is impure, is impermanent, and is marked by winning and losing — and therefore should be discarded.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The aśvamedha, the Vedic horse sacrifice, was the most expensive and prestigious rite in the Brahmanical repertoire — a year-long royal ceremony that only a king could mount, and one whose promised fruit was, as the Outsider says, permanent. It is the strongest available counter-example, and the reply comes at it from four directions in quick succession.

The poisoned meal is the argument in its purest form, and it is the sentence to carry away from this chapter. A poisoned meal is not a bad meal. It is delicious — that is the whole trouble. 食時美、欲消時苦: sweet in the eating, bitter when it comes to be digested. Nothing about the pleasure is illusory. The fire says the same thing in another key: the same fire that is comfort at one distance is injury at another, and nothing has changed but the amount. Anything with two effects like that is not a stable thing; it is a variable, and a variable is impermanent by definition.

Then the measurement argument, which is the technically decisive one. An act of sacrifice is a bounded event — so many days, so many priests, so much wealth. A bounded cause has a bounded fruit; a small lump of clay makes a small pot. An unlimited result cannot be squeezed out of a limited outlay. This is not a Buddhist premise smuggled in; it is the ordinary logic of production that the ritualist himself relies on when he insists the rite must be performed exactly and completely.

The last two strokes are debater's craft of a high order. First, an argument from the opponent's own mythology: your gods quarrel and are angry with each other, and whatever quarrels is not permanent. Second, and sharper, a citation from the Sāṃkhya text — a rival non-Buddhist school — which already says the sacrifice is impure and impermanent. The ritualist is not merely wrong; he is outvoted at home. It is also a small piece of evidence about how this treatise was meant to be used: whoever carried it into a debate hall was expected to know his opponents' scriptures well enough to quote one against another.

8. Then Why Do It at All?

The obvious complaint, and the treatise gives it its full force: if the merit has to go in the end, the sensible course is not to accumulate it in the first place. Why work hard for something you will be told to put down?

Chinese Source

外曰：若捨福，不應作(修妬路)。若福必捨，本不應作，何有智人空為苦事？譬如陶家，作器還破。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: If merit is to be discarded, it should not be made.sūtra If merit must in the end be discarded, it should never have been made at all. What wise man goes to painful trouble for nothing? It is like a potter making a vessel only to break it again.

內曰：生道次第法，如垢衣浣染(修妬路)。如垢衣先浣後淨乃染，浣淨不虛也。所以者何？染法次第故，以垢衣不受染故。如是先除罪垢，次以福德熏心，然後受涅槃道染。

Insider: It is the orderly way by which the path is brought to birth — as a dirty cloth is washed and then dyed.sūtra A dirty cloth is first washed, and only when clean is it dyed; and the washing is not wasted. Why? Because dyeing goes in order, and a dirty cloth will not take the dye. Just so: first the dirt of offense is removed; next the mind is perfumed with merit; and then it can take the dye of the path to nirvāṇa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The cloth returns, and it is now doing heavier work than it did in Part 2. There it had two stages, washing and dyeing, and answered a question about sequence. Here it has three — the dirt removed, the cloth perfumed with merit, the dye of the path taken on — and answers a question about waste.

The Outsider's potter is a genuinely good image and the reply meets it exactly. A potter who makes a vessel in order to smash it has produced nothing; the making and the breaking cancel. But the washing of a cloth is not cancelled by the dyeing. 浣淨不虛也 — the washing is not wasted. It is the condition of the dyeing, and it is used up in the dyeing rather than undone by it. Merit is not destroyed when it is set down. It is spent.

Which is why the chapter can ask for something that would otherwise sound like nihilism, and not be asking for that at all. Nobody is being told that giving and precepts were a mistake. They are being told that the merit is a solvent, not a possession — and that a man who has washed his cloth and then refuses to let go of the washing will never get any color into it.

9. Marklessness Is Highest

One question remains, and it is exactly the right one. Merit was the leverage by which evil was let go. What is the leverage by which merit is let go?

Chinese Source

外曰：捨福依何等(修妬路)？依福捨惡，依何捨福？

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: On what does one rely in discarding merit?sūtra One relies on merit to discard evil. On what does one rely to discard merit?

內曰：無相最上(修妬路)。取福人天中生，取罪三惡道生，是故無相智慧最第一。無相名一切相不憶念，離一切受，過去未來現在法心無所著。一切法自性無故則無所依，是名無相。以是方便故能捨福。何以故？除三種解脫門，第一利不可得。如佛語諸比丘：「若有人言我不用空無相無作，欲得若知若見無增上慢者，是人空言無實。」

Insider: Marklessness is highest.sūtra One who takes up merit is born among men and gods; one who takes up offense is born in the three evil destinies. Therefore the wisdom of marklessness is first of all. "Marklessness" names the not calling to mind of any mark whatever, the being free of all feeling, the mind's being unattached to things past, future, or present. Since all things have no nature of their own, there is nothing to rely on — and this is what is called marklessness. It is by this skillful means that one is able to discard merit. Why? Because apart from the three gates of liberation, the highest benefit is not to be got. As the Buddha said to the monks: "If anyone should say, 'I have no use for emptiness, marklessness, and wishlessness, yet I would attain knowing and seeing free of overweening pride' — that man speaks emptily and without truth."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The answer is a refusal to give the answer the question asked for, and the refusal is the point. The Outsider wants a third thing to stand on, one rung higher than merit — and if there were one, the same question would arise about it, and the regress would never stop. What he is given instead is 無所依: nothing to rely on. Merit was let go by standing on something better. Merit is let go by ceasing to stand on anything.

And the ground for that is stated in a single clause which is the hinge of the entire book: 一切法自性無故則無所依 — since all things have no nature of their own, there is nothing to rely on. This is the first appearance of 自性 in the treatise as the load-bearing term it will be for the next nine chapters, and everything from Part 4 to the end is the demonstration of it. Chapter One has argued its way, entirely within ethics, to the place where the metaphysics has to begin.

The three gates of liberation — emptiness, marklessness, wishlessness (空・無相・無作) — are not three techniques but three faces of one seeing: nothing has a nature of its own; therefore there is no mark to fix on; therefore there is nothing to lean toward. The closing quotation makes the exclusivity explicit and rather severe. A person who wants the attainment while declining the three gates is not making a smaller claim or taking a longer road. He is 空言無實 — talking, with nothing in it.

And with that the first chapter has done exactly what its title said: 捨罪福, discarding sin and merit. It began with an ethics anyone would recognize, tested each of its three practices for what was really wanted underneath, and then removed the last thing the practitioner was holding — not by disparaging it, but by pointing out that a hand full of merit is still a hand that is full.

· · ·

Part Three Complete

This closes Part Three, and with it the first chapter: the man of neutral mind whose evil is stopped though he practices nothing; the threefold division of persons and the splitting of each practice into pure and impure; almsgiving as trade, celibacy as a ram's backward step, refined knowledge as an enemy who arrives in friendship; the poisoned meal and the fire that comforts and then burns; the horse sacrifice refuted four ways over, including out of the Sāṃkhya's own book; the dirty cloth whose washing is spent rather than wasted; and marklessness, which is not a better thing to stand on but the end of standing on things.

Chapter One closed on the claim that all things have no nature of their own. Chapter Two opens with the Outsider refusing it flatly — and he refuses it by producing the one thing he is most sure of. Not a pot, not a god: the self. Sāṃkhya and Vaiśeṣika both step forward with their accounts of it, and what follows is the longest sustained argument in the treatise.
