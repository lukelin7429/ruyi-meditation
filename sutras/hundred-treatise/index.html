Āryadeva · Vasu · Kumārajīva · 百論

A Book Written to Be Argued With.

The Hundred Treatise is not a teacher explaining his thought to a pupil. It is a debate manual. On one side stands 外, the outsider — a Sāṃkhya philosopher, a Vaiśeṣika, a Jain ascetic — who states his case fully, in his own vocabulary, and is quoted without distortion. On the other stands 內, the insider, who almost never asserts anything, and instead lets each position collide with its own premises. Ten chapters, roughly a hundred and ninety exchanges, and by the end the self, the pot, the senses, causation, space, time and nirvāṇa have all been let go of — including, in the last chapter, the argument that let them go.

How to read this collection

The treatise runs to two fascicles and about twenty thousand characters of Chinese, organized in ten chapters of very uneven length. We have split it into fourteen parts by the shape of the argument rather than by counting chapters: seven chapters fit one page each, while the first two — on discarding merit, and on refuting the self — are long enough to need three pages apiece.

Two features of the text govern how these pages are laid out, and both are explained in full on Part 1. Āryadeva's root lines do not appear as verses — Kumārajīva rendered them as terse prose aphorisms inside Vasu's commentary, each closed by the marker (修妬路) — so the root text is marked inline rather than set in verse blocks. And the speakers are not "question" and "answer" but 外 and 內, the outsider and the insider, which is why they appear here as Outsider and Insider throughout.

Read by Part

From an argument about whether a book may open with the word "evil," through the longest sustained refutation of the self in Chinese Madhyamaka, to a last chapter in which the treatise turns on itself and asks what its own refutation is made of.

Start here

The Preface and the Homage

Sengzhao's preface, which is nearly everything we know about how the text reached China — including the fact that half of it did not. Then the question that starts the argument: which teacher deserves the name World-Honored One?

The longest argument

Refuting the Self

Three parts on Sāṃkhya's self, whose mark is awareness, and Vaiśeṣika's self, which merely possesses knowledge. An ox, a horse, a black cloth, a walking staff — and a knife that must cut whatever it can hurt.

The turning point

You Are the Destroyer

The final chapter, in which the opponent asks what an argument that empties everything is itself made of. The answer brings the two truths, a fourth position found in the opponent's own texts, and a mirage.

Translation Notes

The Chinese source is Kumārajīva's translation, revised at Chang'an in 404 CE — five years before the same workshop produced this site's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā and Twelve Gate Treatise. Terminology is matched across all three, so that a reader moving among them meets the same English for the same Chinese. See also this site's note on translation method.

自性 — a nature of its ownSvabhāva. What is unmade and depends on nothing else. Named first at the close of Chapter One and load-bearing for the nine chapters after it.
外曰／內曰 — the Outsider, the InsiderNot "question" and "answer" but 外道 and 內道: those outside the Buddha's way and those within it. The labels tell you this is a manual for public debate.
修妬路 — the root-line markerTransliterates sūtra. Marks the end of one of Āryadeva's own lines, which Kumārajīva rendered as prose rather than verse.
相・可相 — mark and what is markedA mark is what a thing is recognized by. The treatise's most-used distinction is between a mark and a mere act: a hand is a hand while it rests.
神 — the selfKumārajīva's word for puruṣa in Sāṃkhya and ātman in Vaiśeṣika. A Chinese word for spirit, pressed into service; here it means simply the abiding subject.
陀羅驃・求那 — substance and qualityDravya and guṇa, two of Vaiśeṣika's six categories. The treatise repeatedly beats its opponent with the rule that the two never convert.
作因・了因 — productive and revealing causeA potter makes the pot; a lamp only shows what was already there. Used in Chapters Four and Nine, and matched to the Twelve Gate pages.
世諦・第一義諦 — conventional and ultimate truthNamed in the last chapter. Not one true and one false, but two levels each of which can be got right or wrong — as a crab-apple is truly large beside a jujube.

On the missing half

Sengzhao's preface states that the treatise had twenty chapters of five verses each — a hundred verses, which is where the title comes from. Only the first ten chapters were brought into Chinese: the last ten, he writes, "he judged to be of no benefit to this land, and so they are wanting and were not transmitted." The Hundred Treatise, as it exists in Chinese and therefore as it exists at all, holds roughly fifty of its hundred verses. There is no Sanskrit and no Tibetan witness to tell us what was in the other half.

There is a second and smaller hole. In the middle of Chapter Three, the received text carries an in-line editorial note — 此中四紙辯名字無可傳譯, "four sheets of argument concerning names occur at this point, which cannot be carried over in translation." Four sheets of the argument are simply not there, and whoever prepared the Chinese said so rather than closing the gap silently. Both losses are flagged where they occur, in Part 1 and Part 7.

On the author, the commentator, and the title

Āryadeva (提婆) was Nāgārjuna's pupil and, by the tradition's account, the sharpest debater of his generation; Sengzhao places him some eight centuries after the Buddha. The commentary is by one 婆藪開士, "Vasu the Illumining Being," about whom essentially nothing is known beyond Sengzhao's few lines of praise. His exposition is translated here as primary text, exactly as Piṅgala's commentary is on this site's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā pages — this treatise, unlike the Twelve Gate Treatise, is not a self-commentary, and there are genuinely two voices in it.

We title the work in English rather than in Sanskrit. The form Śataśāstra sometimes given is a back-formation from the Chinese; the text survives only in Chinese, with no Sanskrit or Tibetan witness. "Hundred Treatise" also follows the rendering already used on this site's Great Calming and Contemplation pages, where Zhiyi quotes this treatise by name. Note that Āryadeva's Catuḥśataka, the "Four Hundred," is a different and separate work.
