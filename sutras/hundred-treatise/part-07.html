Being, Oneness, and the Pot

Refuting Oneness破一品第三

Having lost the self, the Outsider falls back on its possessions. Surely there is one pot — and if there is something owned, there is an owner. But the Chinese phrase he uses, 有一瓶, has three words in it: being, one, pot. The chapter asks how those three are related, and finds that if they are one thing, then either everything is a pot, or nothing is, or the two results arrive together. This page also carries the treatise's single confessed hole: four sheets of the argument that Kumārajīva judged untranslatable and left out.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T30, no. 1569, 百論 Āryadeva (提婆), with Vasu's commentary (婆藪開士釋) Kumārajīva, revised 404 CE Contains an acknowledged lacuna

1. "There Is One Pot"

The bridge from Chapter Two is an argument from ownership: things belong to somebody, so somebody exists. And the example chosen to stand for "things" is the humblest sentence available.

Chinese Source · 破一品第三

外曰：應有神。有一瓶等，神所有故(修妬路)。若有神則有神所有，若無神則無神所有。有一瓶等是神所有，故有神。

內曰：不然。何以故？神已不可得故。今思惟有一瓶等，若以一有？若以異有？二俱有過。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: There must be a self, because there is one pot and the like, and these are the self's possessions.sūtra If there is a self, there is what belongs to a self; if there is no self, there is nothing belonging to a self. "There is one pot" and the like are what belong to a self — and therefore there is a self.

Insider: Not so. Why? Because the self has already been shown to be unobtainable. Let us now consider "there is one pot" and the like. Do they exist as one, or do they exist as different? Both alternatives are at fault.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Everything in this chapter and the next depends on seeing what the Chinese phrase contains, so it is worth pausing on the words. 有一瓶 is three characters: 有, being or existence; 一, one; 瓶, pot. English flattens this into "there is one pot," where "there is" and "one" feel like grammatical scaffolding rather than parts of the world. For the schools being argued with, they are not scaffolding. Vaiśeṣika holds existence to be a real universal, inhering in everything that exists, and number likewise to be a real quality. So the sentence names three items, and the question of how they hang together is a serious metaphysical question with a serious answer expected.

The Insider's reply sets the frame for both chapters at once. Are these three one, or are they different? Chapter Three takes the first horn and Chapter Four takes the second, and their titles say so: Refuting Oneness and Refuting Difference. This is the same dilemma that was run against the elephant and its trunk in Part 2, where the treatise pointed forward to exactly these two chapters. It is now being run at the scale of everything there is.

2. If the Three Are One

The Outsider takes the first horn, and the consequences arrive in three waves. If being, oneness and pot are strictly one thing, then everything is established, nothing is established, and everything is turned upside down — and the treatise means each of the three literally.

Chinese Source

外曰：有一瓶等，若以一有，有何過？內曰：若有一瓶一，如一一切成若不成、若顛倒(修妬路)。若有一瓶一者，如因陀羅釋迦憍尸迦，其有因陀羅處則有釋迦憍尸迦。如是隨有處則有一瓶，隨一處則有有瓶，隨瓶處則有有一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: If "there is one pot" and the like exist as one, what fault is there in that?

Insider: If being, one and pot are one, then as with the one, so with everything — all is established, or nothing is established, or everything is inverted.sūtra If being, one and pot are one, then it is as with Indra, Śakra and Kauśika: wherever Indra is, there Śakra and Kauśika are. Just so, wherever being is, there are one and pot; wherever one is, there are being and pot; wherever pot is, there are being and one.

若爾衣等諸物亦應是瓶，有一瓶一故，如是其有一物皆應是瓶，今瓶衣等物悉應是一。復次有常故，一瓶亦應常。復次若說有則說一瓶。復次一是數，有瓶亦應是數。復次若瓶五身，有一亦應五身。若瓶有形有對，有一亦應有形有對。若瓶無常，有一亦應無常。是名如一一切成。

If so, cloth and all other things must be pots as well, since being, one and pot are one; and so whatever thing has being and oneness must be a pot, and pot and cloth and the rest must all be one. Furthermore, since being is permanent, one and pot must be permanent too. Furthermore, to say "being" would be to say "one pot." Furthermore, one is a number, so being and pot must be numbers as well. Furthermore, if the pot has the five constituents, being and one must have the five constituents. If the pot has shape and resistance, being and one must have shape and resistance. If the pot is impermanent, being and one must be impermanent. This is what is called: as with the one, so all is established.

若處處有是中無瓶，今處處瓶是亦無瓶，有不異故。復次事事有不是瓶，今瓶則非瓶，有不異故。復次若說有不攝一瓶，今說一瓶亦不應攝一瓶，有不異故。復次若有非瓶。瓶亦非瓶。有不異故。是名如一一切不成。

If in place after place there is being and yet no pot in them, then wherever there is a pot there is likewise no pot, since being is not different from it. Furthermore, since in case after case there is being that is not a pot, the pot is now not a pot, since being is not different from it. Furthermore, if saying "being" does not include "one pot," then saying "one pot" should likewise not include "one pot," since being is not different from it. Furthermore, if being is not a pot, then the pot too is not a pot, since being is not different from it. This is what is called: as with the one, so nothing is established.

若欲說瓶應說有，欲說有應說瓶。復次汝瓶成故，有一亦成。若有一成故，瓶亦應成，以一故。是名如一，一切顛倒(此中四紙辯名字無可傳譯)。

If you wish to say "pot" you should say "being," and if you wish to say "being" you should say "pot." Furthermore, since your pot is established, being and one are established too; and since being and one are established, the pot must be established, on account of their being one. This is what is called: as with the one, so all is inverted. (Four sheets of argument concerning names occur at this point, which cannot be carried over in translation.)

Reading note — Ru-Yi · the missing four sheets

That parenthesis is not a modern editorial insertion. It stands in the received Chinese text — 此中四紙辯名字無可傳譯 — and it is the most candid sentence in the whole treatise. Somewhere in the middle of this argument there were four sheets of further reasoning about names, and whoever prepared the Chinese decided they could not be brought across and said so in the text rather than closing the gap silently.

Why untranslatable? Almost certainly because the argument turned on features of Sanskrit that Chinese does not have. An argument "about names" at this point in the chapter would very likely have been working with grammatical machinery — case endings, gender, the way a Sanskrit adjective agrees with its noun, the formation of abstract nouns like sattva from sat. Chinese has none of that apparatus. The reasoning would not have been difficult to translate so much as impossible to exhibit: rendered word by word into Chinese it would have ceased to be an argument at all.

This is worth sitting with, because it is a second and quite different kind of loss from the one Sengzhao reported in Part 1. There, ten whole chapters were left out on a judgment about what China needed. Here, a passage the translator evidently wanted was defeated by the languages. We know the length — four sheets — and the subject, and nothing else, and no Sanskrit or Tibetan witness survives to tell us more. Readers should know that the argument they are reading has a hole in it at this point, and that the hole is honestly marked.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Indra, Śakra and Kauśika are three names for one god, and they are the perfect illustration because the identity is uncontroversial: no one thinks a room might contain Indra but not Śakra. That is what strict identity implies — total co-presence, with no gap anywhere. So if being, oneness and pot are one in that way, then wherever any of the three turns up, all three do.

Follow it out and the results are the three named. All is established: cloth exists, so cloth has being, so cloth is a pot; and every property of one member transfers to the others, so being becomes impermanent and breakable, and the pot becomes eternal. Nothing is established: being also turns up in a thousand places where no pot is, and since being is not different from pot, the absence of a pot in those places is the absence of a pot everywhere. All is inverted: the two results hold at once, so the pot is established and unestablished together, and every sentence can be exchanged for any other.

It is worth noticing how patient the treatise is here. It does not stop when the absurdity first appears; it works through shape, resistance, number, permanence and the five constituents one at a time. The reason is that the identity thesis is not silly — it is the natural thing to say if you want to avoid multiplying entities, and a reader has to be shown how much comes with it before the cost is visible.

3. Being Is Everywhere, and Pots Are Not

The Outsider's repair is to make the containment one-directional. Every pot carries being and oneness with it, but not everything carrying being and oneness is a pot. Say "pot" and you have said "being"; say "being" and you have not said "pot."

Chinese Source

外曰：物有一故無過(修妬路)。物是有，亦是一，是故若有瓶處必有有一，非有一處皆是瓶。復次若說瓶，當知已攝有一，非說有一必攝瓶。

內曰：瓶有二。何故二？無瓶(修妬路)。若有一瓶一，何故有一處無瓶？復次云何說有一不攝瓶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: There is no fault, because a thing is both being and one.sūtra A thing is being and is also one; and so wherever there is a pot there are certainly being and one, while it is not the case that everywhere being and one are, there is a pot. Furthermore, if one says "pot," one should know that being and one are already included; but saying "being and one" does not necessarily include pot.

Insider: Then the pot is two. Why two? Because there is no pot.sūtra If being, one and pot are one, why is there any place where being and one are and a pot is not? And further, how can saying "being and one" fail to include pot?

外曰：瓶中瓶有定故(修妬路)。瓶中瓶有與瓶不異，而異於衣物等，是故在在處瓶是中有瓶有，亦在在處瓶有是中有瓶，非在在有處有瓶。

Outsider: Because the being-of-a-pot is fixed within the pot.sūtra The being-of-a-pot within the pot is not different from the pot, while it is different from cloth and other things. Therefore wherever there is a pot, there the being-of-a-pot is; and wherever the being-of-a-pot is, there a pot is; but it is not the case that wherever being is, there a pot is.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The second move is more sophisticated than the first and deserves credit. The Outsider is inventing a restricted existence — not being at large, but pot-being, an existence proper to pots and found nowhere else. That secures the one-way implication he needs: pot-being and pot travel together, while being in general does not.

A modern reader will recognize the manoeuvre. It is what happens whenever a general term proves too general and is quietly indexed to save a thesis. The cost is that the general term stops doing the work it was introduced to do — if what pots have is pot-being, and cloths have cloth-being, then "existence" has become a name for a heap of unrelated properties, and the unity it was supposed to explain has been given up.

One note on the root line. The received text reads 瓶有二。何故二？無瓶, punctuated as we have translated it. The clauses are compressed even by this treatise's standards and can be parsed otherwise — for instance taking 瓶、有 as the two items ("pot and being are two") and reading the question as asking why, being two, a pot is anywhere absent. The argument that follows is the same either way, since it turns on the gap between being and pot; we follow the received punctuation and flag the alternative.

4. General and Particular, and the Father Who Needed a Son

The reply names what has gone wrong in a vocabulary both sides share: 總相 and 別相, the general mark and the particular. And the test given for telling them apart is beautifully simple — it is a test about what you have conceded when you say a word.

Chinese Source

內曰：不然。瓶有不異故(修妬路)。有是總相。何以故？若說有，則信瓶等諸物。若說瓶，不信衣等諸物。是故瓶是別相，有是總相，云何為一？

外曰：如父子(修妬路)。譬如一人亦子亦父，如是總相亦是別相，別相亦是總相。

內曰：不然。子故父(修妬路)。若未生子不名為父，子生然後為父。復次是喻同我，汝則非也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Insider: Not so. Because the pot and being are not different.sūtra Being is a general mark. Why? Because if one says "being," one grants pots and all the other things; but if one says "pot," one does not grant cloth and the other things. Therefore the pot is a particular mark and being is a general mark — how then should they be one?

Outsider: As with father and son.sūtra One and the same man is both a son and a father. Just so, the general mark is also a particular mark, and the particular mark is also a general mark.

Insider: Not so. It is because of the son that there is a father.sūtra Before a son is born the man is not called a father; only once the son is born is he a father. Furthermore, this simile agrees with my own position, and so tells against you.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The test for generality is worth having: say "being" and you have committed yourself to pots, cloths, carts and everything else; say "pot" and you have committed yourself to nothing about cloth. The scope of what a word obliges you to grant is the measure of how general it is — and two terms with different scopes are not one term.

The father-and-son reply is the finest short exchange in this chapter, and it is worth reading twice. The Outsider's point is that generality is relative: a man is a son looking upward and a father looking downward, so "general" and "particular" are positions rather than natures, and nothing prevents being from occupying both.

The answer accepts the example and then reads it more carefully than the man who offered it. Before the son is born he is not a father. Fatherhood is not a property the man was carrying about in himself, waiting to be noticed; it arrives with the son and could not have arrived otherwise. So the example does not show that one thing has two natures. It shows that both terms are relational — that neither "father" nor "son" names anything a person is on their own.

Hence 是喻同我, this simile agrees with my position, which is the treatise doing what it does best: not refuting the example but accepting it and pointing out whose case it makes. Everything the Insider wants is in it. Being and pot are not two fixed items that might be identified or distinguished; they are terms whose sense depends on each other, and things constituted by dependence have no nature of their own. The Outsider handed over the demonstration while trying to escape it.

5. Everyone Believes in Pots

The appeal to common sense, which every argument of this kind eventually meets. People see pots. People use pots. Whatever the analysis says, the pots are there.

Chinese Source

外曰：應有瓶，皆信故(修妬路)。世人眼見信有瓶用，是故應有瓶。

內曰：有不異故一切無(修妬路)。若瓶與有不異者，瓶應是總相非別相。別相無故總相亦無，因有別相故有總相，若無別相則無總相。是二無故，一切皆無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: There must be a pot, because everyone grants it.sūtra Ordinary people see with their eyes and grant that there is a pot to be used; and so there must be a pot.

Insider: Since being is not different, everything is absent.sūtra If the pot is not different from being, then the pot must be a general mark and not a particular one. Since the particular mark is absent, the general mark is absent too; for it is in dependence on particular marks that there is a general mark, and where there are no particular marks there is no general mark. And these two being absent, everything is absent.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reply looks nihilistic and is not, but the appearance is worth taking seriously rather than waving away.

What is being refuted is a particular account of what a pot is: a thing whose existence is a real universal inhering in it and not different from it. Run that account and the pot is swallowed by the universal — it becomes a general mark rather than a particular one. But general marks live on particulars; strip the particulars away and there is nothing for generality to be generality of. So the account destroys both of its own terms.

This is not the claim that nobody carries water. The treatise will say so explicitly in Part 14, when the Outsider finally accuses it of contradicting the world outright and gets the answer that dependently arisen things are exactly what ordinary people accept, and that it is the theories of fixed natures which cannot account for the pots we actually use. Here, mid-argument, that reply has not been earned yet, and the Insider simply lets the opponent's own theory finish itself off.

6. The Foot That Would Have to Be the Head

A new model, and a much better one: whole and part. The foot is not different from the body, yet nobody says the foot is the body. If pot and being stand like that, identity is preserved without absurdity.

Chinese Source

外曰：如足分等名身(修妬路)。如頭足分等雖不異身，非但足為身。如是瓶與有雖不異，而瓶非總相。

內曰：若足與身不異，何故足不為頭(修妬路)。若頭足分等與身不異者，足應是頭，是二與身不異故。如因陀羅釋迦不異故，因陀羅即釋迦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: As the foot and the other parts are called the body.sūtra Head and feet and the other parts, though not different from the body, are not such that the foot alone is the body. Just so, though the pot and being are not different, the pot is not a general mark.

Insider: If the foot is not different from the body, why is the foot not the head?sūtra If head and feet and the other parts are not different from the body, then the foot must be the head, since the two are alike not different from the body — as Indra and Śakra are not different, and so Indra just is Śakra.

外曰：諸分異故無過(修妬路)。分有分不異，非分分不異，是故頭足不一。

內曰：若爾無身(修妬路)。若足與頭異，頭與足分等異，如是但有諸分，更無有分名之為身。

Outsider: There is no fault, because the parts differ from one another.sūtra The part is not different from the whole that has the parts, but the parts are not undifferentiated among themselves; and so head and foot are not one.

Insider: If so, there is no body.sūtra If the foot is different from the head, and the head is different from the foot and the other parts, then there are only the parts, and there is no further whole-that-has-parts to be called the body.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the elephant-and-trunk argument from Part 2 arriving at the chapter it was promised to, and it is now given properly. Identity is transitive: if the foot is not different from the body and the head is not different from the body, then the foot is not different from the head. Indra returns to make the point, as he did at the top of the chapter.

The Outsider's repair is precise and worth stating in his own terms: identity holds vertically, between each part and the whole, but not horizontally, between the parts. That is exactly the position, and the reply exposes what it costs. Once the parts are genuinely different from each other, the "whole" they are each identical with has to be different from itself in as many ways as there are parts — which is to say there is no single whole there at all. 但有諸分: only the parts, and no body over and above them.

This dilemma — the whole is either identical with its parts and therefore not one, or distinct from them and therefore never found — is one of the two hinges of the entire treatise, and it will be applied to atoms in Part 8, to the perceived object in Part 10, and to space in Part 13. What is being established here is a tool.

7. The Army and the Forest

Two more attempts, both appealing to collective nouns — things everyone agrees are one thing and many things at once. If an army can be one army made of thousands, a pot can be one pot made of its five sensory constituents.

Chinese Source

外曰：不然。多因一果現故，如色等是瓶(修妬路)。如色分等多因現一瓶果，此中非但色為瓶，亦不離色為瓶，是故色分等不為一。足分等與身亦如是。

內曰：如色等瓶亦不一(修妬路)。若瓶與色聲香味觸五分不異者，不應言一瓶。若言一瓶，色分等亦應一，色等與瓶不異故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: Not so. Because many causes manifest one fruit, as color and the rest are the pot.sūtra Many causes — the part that is color and the others — manifest one fruit, the pot; and here it is not that color alone is the pot, nor is the pot apart from color. Therefore the part that is color and the others are not one. And it is the same with the foot and the other parts in relation to the body.

Insider: As with color and the rest, so the pot too is not one.sūtra If the pot is not different from the five parts — color, sound, smell, taste and touch — then one should not speak of one pot. And if one speaks of one pot, then the part that is color and the others must be one too, since color and the rest are not different from the pot.

外曰：如軍林(修妬路)。若象馬車步多眾合故名為軍，又松柏等多樹合故名為林，非獨松為林，亦不離松為林；軍亦爾。如是非一色名為瓶，亦不離色為瓶。

內曰：眾亦如瓶(修妬路)。若松柏等與林不異者，不應言一林。若言一林者，松柏等亦應一，與林不異故。如松樹根莖枝節華葉，亦應如是破。如軍等一切物，盡應如是破。

Outsider: As with an army, or a forest.sūtra Elephants, horses, chariots and foot-soldiers, many of them combined, are called an army; and pines and cypresses, many trees combined, are called a forest. The pine alone is not the forest, and yet the forest is not apart from the pine; and so with the army. Just so, color alone is not called the pot, and yet the pot is not apart from color.

Insider: The multitude too is as the pot.sūtra If pines and cypresses are not different from the forest, one should not speak of one forest. And if one speaks of one forest, then pines and cypresses must be one too, since they are not different from the forest. The pine tree's root, trunk, branches, joints, flowers and leaves are to be broken in the same way; and the army and all other things are to be broken in this same way entirely.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The forest is the best example the Outsider produces in this chapter, and the reply is correspondingly important. What the Insider does is not deny that we say "one forest." It is to point out that the same dilemma reappears one level down, and then again below that.

Watch the descent. Is the forest one? Then the pines are one, being not different from it. Is the forest many? Then it is not one forest. Very well — take a single pine and ask the same question of its root, trunk, branches, joints, flowers and leaves. And of a leaf, and of the leaf's parts. 盡應如是破: every thing whatever is to be broken in this same way. There is no level at which the analysis bottoms out in something simply and unproblematically one.

This is why the chapter can spend its length on a pot without being about pots. The pot was chosen because it is the least interesting object anyone could name; and the argument that dissolves it dissolves the forest, the army, the tree, the body, and — as Part 8 will insist — the atom that was supposed to be the floor beneath them all.

8. "So You Accept Many Pots"

A counter-attack, and a fair one: if the pot dissolves into its many constituents, then the Insider has replaced one pot with many, which is hardly a refutation. And behind it a deeper move — the constituents were never refuted, and a surviving cause guarantees a fruit.

Chinese Source

外曰：受多瓶故(修妬路)。汝說色分等多(修妬路)。瓶亦應多，是故欲破一瓶而受多瓶。

內曰：非色等多故瓶多(修妬路)。我說汝過，非受多瓶。汝自言色分等多，無別瓶法為色等果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: Because you accept many pots.sūtra You say that the part that is color and the others are many; sūtra then the pot too must be many. So in wishing to refute one pot, you have accepted many pots.

Insider: It is not that the pot is many because color and the rest are many.sūtra I am stating your fault; I do not accept many pots. It is you who say that the part that is color and the others are many, and that there is no separate pot-dharma as the fruit of color and the rest.

外曰：有果，以不破因。有因故果成(修妬路)。汝破瓶果，不破色等瓶因。若有因必有果，無無果因。復次色等瓶因是微塵果，汝受色等故，因果俱成。

Outsider: There is a fruit, since you have not refuted the cause; and because there is a cause, the fruit is established.sūtra You refute the pot as fruit, but you do not refute color and the rest, which are the pot's cause. If there is a cause there must be a fruit; there is no cause without a fruit. Furthermore, color and the rest, which are the pot's cause, are themselves the fruit of atoms; and since you accept color and the rest, cause and fruit are both established.

內曰：如果無，因亦無(修妬路)。如瓶與色等多分不異故，瓶不應一。今色等多分與瓶不異故，色等不應多。又如汝言無無果因，今果破故因亦自破，汝法因果一故。復次三世為一(修妬路)。泥團時現在，瓶時未來，土時過去。若因果一，泥團中應有瓶土，是故三世時為一。已作、今作、當作者，如是語壞。

Insider: As the fruit is absent, so the cause is absent too.sūtra Just as, the pot not being different from the many parts of color and the rest, the pot cannot be one — so now, the many parts of color and the rest not being different from the pot, color and the rest cannot be many. And as you say there is no cause without a fruit: now that the fruit is refuted, the cause too is refuted of itself, since in your doctrine cause and fruit are one. Furthermore, the three times become one.sūtra At the time of the lump of clay it is present; at the time of the pot it is future; at the time of the earth it is past. If cause and fruit are one, then pot and earth must both be present in the lump of clay — and so the three times become one time, and the expressions "already made," "now being made," and "to be made" are destroyed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Insider's disclaimer is the important sentence and it recurs throughout the treatise in various forms: 我說汝過，非受多瓶 — I am stating your fault; I do not accept many pots. The many pots were never his thesis. They are what follows from the Outsider's premises, produced in order to be shown unacceptable. Mistaking a consequence drawn for reductio for a position held is the commonest misreading of Madhyamaka, and it is the misreading that Part 14 exists to answer.

The symmetry that follows is elegant. The Outsider had used the many parts to break up the one pot; the Insider runs the same "not different" in reverse — if the parts are not different from the pot, then the pot's oneness infects the parts, and they cannot be many either. The premise was the Outsider's and it cuts both ways.

Then the three times, which is the passage this chapter is best remembered for. Clay, pot, earth: past, present and future strung along a single causal line. The ritualist's own principle is that cause and fruit are one substance. But if the pot in the lump of clay is really the same thing, it is there now, and so is the earth it will crumble to — and past, present and future have collapsed into one another. The consequence is drawn in the plainest possible terms: 已作、今作、當作者，如是語壞, the words "already made," "being made" and "to be made" are destroyed. A theory that makes tense unintelligible has not explained production; it has abolished it. Chapters Seven and Eight will take this up as their main business.

9. Long and Short

The chapter closes with the Outsider reaching for the one relation that seems immune to all this — mutual dependence. Nothing is long in itself; it is long compared to something shorter. Let cause and fruit be like that, and the collapse of the three times is avoided.

Chinese Source

外曰：不然。因果相待成故，如長短(修妬路)。如因長見短、因短見長，如是泥團，觀瓶則是因、觀土則是果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: Not so. Because cause and fruit are established in mutual dependence, as long and short are.sūtra In dependence on the long one sees the short, and in dependence on the short one sees the long. Just so with the lump of clay: regarded from the pot it is the cause, and regarded from the earth it is the fruit.

內曰：因他相違共過故，非長中長相，亦非短中及共中(修妬路)。若實有長相，若長中有？若短中有？若共中有？是不可得。何以故？長中無長相，以因他故，因短故為長。短中亦無長，性相違故。若短中有長，不名為短。長短共中亦無長，二俱過故。若長中有、若短中有，先說有過。短相亦如是。若無長短，云何相待？◎

Insider: Because of the faults of depending on another, of opposition, and of the two together — the mark of length is not in the long, nor in the short, nor in the two together.sūtra If there really were a mark of length, would it be in the long? in the short? or in the two together? It is not to be got. Why? In the long there is no mark of length, since it depends on another: it is long because of the short. Nor is length in the short, their natures being opposed — for if length were in the short, the short would not be called short. Nor is length in the long and the short together, since the faults of both attach to it. And whether it be in the long or in the short, the faults stated above apply. The mark of shortness is just the same. And if there is neither long nor short, how can there be mutual dependence?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing question is the one that matters, and it should be read slowly, because it looks like a defeat for the Insider's own position and is not: if there is neither long nor short, how can there be mutual dependence?

The Outsider offered mutual dependence as a way of having cause and fruit without either being fixed. The Insider agrees that nothing is long in itself — that is his own doctrine — and then presses one step further than the Outsider wanted to go. Mutual dependence was being used as a foundation, a stable relation on which two unstable terms could rest. But a relation needs relata. If neither term has any nature of its own, there are not two things standing in the relation, and "mutual dependence" cannot be the bedrock either.

This is the treatise refusing to let its own favorite notion harden into a thesis, and it is worth noticing early, because the same refusal is what Part 14 will make explicit when the Outsider argues that emptiness must at least depend on non-emptiness. The answer there is this answer: 若無一，則無相待. Dependence is not a floor. It is the name for there being no floor.

The mark ◎ at the end of the Chinese closes the first fascicle. 百論卷上 ends here, and 卷下 opens with Chapter Four, which takes the other horn of the dilemma set in this chapter's first exchange: if being, oneness and pot are not one, are they different?

· · ·

Part Seven Complete · End of Fascicle One

This closes Part Seven and the first of the treatise's two fascicles: the sentence "there is one pot" pulled apart into three items, and the consequence of identifying them run out in three directions — everything established, nothing established, everything inverted; the invention of a pot-being that goes only where pots go; the general and the particular, and the father who was not a father until his son was born; the foot that would have to be the head, and the body that turns out to be only its parts; the forest that cannot be one and cannot be many, and every other thing broken in the same way; the three times collapsed into the lump of clay; and mutual dependence declined as a resting place.

Fascicle Two opens with the other horn. If being, oneness and pot are different — each with its own nature, standing apart from the others — then Chapter Four asks what is left of the pot. Along the way the Outsider loses his temper for the first time and calls his opponent what he has plainly been thinking for three chapters: a destroyer of dharma.
