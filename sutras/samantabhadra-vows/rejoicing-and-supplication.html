Rejoicing, and Two Supplications

隨喜功德、請轉法輪、請佛住世

Samantabhadra rejoices in every scrap of merit in existence — a buddha's eons of self-sacrifice and an ordinary being's smallest good deed counted the same way — and then makes two acts of entreaty: asking every buddha to teach, and asking every buddha, bodhisattva, and true teacher not to leave the world.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T293 · Prajñā's translation, completed 798 CE Commentary guide: Chengguan's 普賢行願品別行疏 (8th–9th c.), with Zongmi's 疏鈔
This part covers
The fifth, sixth, and seventh of the ten vows: rejoicing in the merit of buddhas, ordinary beings, and the two vehicles alike; requesting every buddha to turn the wheel of the Dharma; and requesting every buddha, bodhisattva, and true teacher to remain in the world rather than pass into final nirvana.
Why it matters
These three vows are this chapter's clearest statement that a bodhisattva's inner life is not only about one's own effort — it is built just as much out of gladness at what others have already done, and out of a refusal to let good teaching or good teachers ever go quiet.

1. Fifth Vow — Rejoicing in Merit

Chinese Source

「復次，善男子！言隨喜功德者：所有盡法界、虛空界十方三世一切佛剎極微塵數諸佛如來，從初發心為一切智，勤修福聚，不惜身命，經不可說不可說佛剎極微塵數劫，一一劫中捨不可說不可說佛剎極微塵數頭、目、手、足，如是一切難行、苦行，圓滿種種波羅蜜門，證入種種菩薩智地，成就諸佛無上菩提及般涅槃，分布舍利，所有善根，我皆隨喜。及彼十方一切世界，六趣、四生一切種類所有功德，乃至一塵我皆隨喜。十方三世一切聲聞及辟支佛、有學、無學所有功德，我皆隨喜。一切菩薩所修無量難行、苦行，志求無上正等菩提廣大功德，我皆隨喜。如是虛空界盡、眾生界盡、眾生業盡、眾生煩惱盡，我此隨喜無有窮盡，念念相續，無有間斷，身、語、意業無有疲厭。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, as for rejoicing in merit: toward all the buddhas and tathāgatas, as many as there are fine motes of dust in all buddha-lands of the ten directions and three time periods, filling the whole Dharma realm and the realm of space — who, from their very first arousing of the wish for all-knowledge, have diligently cultivated stores of merit without sparing body or life, for as many eons as there are fine motes of dust in buddha-lands beyond number, and beyond number again, in each and every one of those eons giving up heads, eyes, hands, and feet beyond number, and beyond number again, undertaking every kind of difficult and painful practice, perfecting every gate of the perfections, entering every ground of a bodhisattva's wisdom, accomplishing every buddha's unsurpassed awakening, and, upon their final nirvana, distributing their relics — toward every root of goodness they possess, I rejoice completely. And toward the merit of every kind of being in the six destinies and four modes of birth, throughout all the worlds of the ten directions, down to a single mote of dust, I rejoice completely. Toward the merit of every śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha of the ten directions and three time periods, whether still learning or beyond learning, I rejoice completely. Toward the vast merit of every bodhisattva who cultivates immeasurable difficult and painful practices, aspiring to unsurpassed, perfect awakening, I rejoice completely. And so it continues until the realm of space is exhausted, the realm of sentient beings is exhausted, their karma is exhausted, and their afflictions are exhausted — this rejoicing of mine can never be exhausted, thought after thought continuing without a break, my deeds of body, speech, and mind never growing weary."

Reading note — after Chengguan

Chengguan reads this vow as four concentric circles, deliberately ordered from the least to the most exalted: first the whole of every buddha's own path, from the first arousing of the aspiration through eons of literally giving away heads, eyes, hands, and feet, down to the distribution of their relics after death; then the ordinary merit of every being reborn anywhere across the six destinies; then the attainment of śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas; then the vast, difficult cultivation of bodhisattvas. He explains the ordering as pedagogical rather than a claim that a buddha's merit is somehow last in importance — the text moves "from inferior to superior" precisely so that the vow ends on the note of greatest weight, having already established, at the bottom of the scale, that no good deed is too small to be included.

To convey how much is generated by rejoicing alone, without any deed of one's own, Chengguan cites two comparisons this vow's tradition inherited from elsewhere. The Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra asks the reader to imagine splitting a single hair into a hundred strands, dipping one strand into the entire ocean, and drawing out one drop — the number of such drops in that ocean can, at least in principle, be counted; the merit of rejoicing in others' virtue cannot. And the Lotus Sūtra's "fiftieth person" parable is more extreme still: someone fifty people removed from ever hearing the sermon itself — who merely rejoices on catching a fragment of it, secondhand, from someone who heard it from someone who heard it — still generates merit that outstrips eighty years of giving every sentient being in four hundred myriad koṭis of world-systems every imaginable luxury. Chengguan's point in citing both is the same: rejoicing costs nothing but a change of heart toward what someone else has already done, and this vow treats that change of heart as inexhaustible merit in its own right.

2. Sixth Vow — Requesting the Turning of the Dharma Wheel

Chinese Source

「復次，善男子！言請轉法輪者：所有盡法界、虛空界十方三世一切佛剎極微塵中，一一各有不可說不可說佛剎極微塵數廣大佛剎，一一剎中念念有不可說不可說佛剎極微塵數一切諸佛成等正覺，一切菩薩海會圍遶，而我悉以身、口、意業種種方便，慇懃勸請轉妙法輪。如是虛空界盡、眾生界盡、眾生業盡、眾生煩惱盡，我常勸請一切諸佛轉正法輪無有窮盡，念念相續，無有間斷，身、語、意業無有疲厭。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, as for requesting the turning of the Dharma wheel: within every fine mote of dust of every buddha-land that fills the whole Dharma realm and the realm of space, throughout the ten directions and three time periods, there are, in each and every one, vast buddha-lands beyond number, and beyond number again, and within each and every land, in thought after thought, there are buddhas beyond number, and beyond number again, each attaining perfect awakening, each surrounded by a vast, ocean-like assembly of bodhisattvas — and I, with every skillful means of body, speech, and mind, earnestly entreat them all to turn the wondrous wheel of the Dharma. And so it continues until the realm of space is exhausted, the realm of sentient beings is exhausted, their karma is exhausted, and their afflictions are exhausted — my constant entreaty to every buddha to turn the wheel of the true Dharma can never be exhausted, thought after thought continuing without a break, my deeds of body, speech, and mind never growing weary."

Reading note — after Chengguan

Chengguan raises the objection this vow invites directly: in the perfect teaching, every sound in the universe is already the Dharma wheel turning, ceaselessly, whether anyone asks for it or not — so what could a "request" possibly add? His answer turns on where the obstruction actually lies. It is not that the Buddha's teaching needs to be started; it is that an ordinary mind, fixed on particular sounds and forms, is like an ear made deaf by too much music — unable to hear the single, all-pervading resonance beneath the noise. He quotes the Avataṃsaka's own chapter on the Buddha's manifestation: "knowing sound to be like an echo, one requests the turning of the Dharma wheel" — the request is not a summons addressed outward to a Buddha who might otherwise stay silent, but the very act of a mind clearing itself enough to finally hear what was never not being taught. He grounds this in the historical scene as well: at Deer Park, tradition holds it was Brahmā's own request that occasioned the first turning of the wheel for the five ascetics — not because the Buddha's realization required an audience's permission, but because the request itself is what makes a teaching land in the world it is spoken into.

3. Seventh Vow — Requesting the Buddhas to Remain in the World

Chinese Source

「復次，善男子！言請佛住世者：所有盡法界、虛空界十方三世一切佛剎極微塵數諸佛如來將欲示現般涅槃者，及諸菩薩、聲聞、緣覺、有學、無學，乃至一切諸善知識，我悉勸請莫入涅槃，經於一切佛剎極微塵數劫，為欲利樂一切眾生。如是虛空界盡、眾生界盡、眾生業盡、眾生煩惱盡，我此勸請無有窮盡，念念相續，無有間斷，身、語、意業無有疲厭。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, as for requesting the buddhas to remain in the world: toward all the buddhas and tathāgatas, as many as there are fine motes of dust in all buddha-lands of the ten directions and three time periods, filling the whole Dharma realm and the realm of space, who are about to manifest their entry into final nirvana, and toward all bodhisattvas, śrāvakas, and pratyekabuddhas, whether still learning or beyond learning, and toward every good and wise teacher besides, I entreat them all not to enter nirvana, but to remain for as many eons as there are fine motes of dust in every buddha-land, for the sake of benefiting and bringing joy to all sentient beings. And so it continues until the realm of space is exhausted, the realm of sentient beings is exhausted, their karma is exhausted, and their afflictions are exhausted — this entreaty of mine can never be exhausted, thought after thought continuing without a break, my deeds of body, speech, and mind never growing weary."

Reading note — after Chengguan

Chengguan poses this vow's own hardest question before answering it: if a buddha's dharma-body is, by nature, always and everywhere present, what could it possibly mean to "request" that a buddha remain, as though presence were something a buddha might otherwise withhold? His answer draws on an image from the Avataṃsaka's chapter on the Buddha's manifestation: the sun rises and its reflection appears in every clean vessel of water at once, without exception; if the reflection fails to appear in one particular vessel, the fault lies with that vessel — cracked, or clouded — never with the sun, which has done nothing different. In exactly the same way, the Buddha's wisdom, like a sun, shines into the entire Dharma realm without gap or interruption; a being who cannot see the Buddha has not been abandoned by the Buddha, but is looking out through a mind too clouded or too broken to hold the reflection. On this reading, "requesting the buddhas to remain" is not a plea addressed outward at all — it is the vow to keep one's own mind clean enough, moment by moment, to go on seeing what was never actually absent. Chengguan draws the explicit parallel to the Vimalakīrti Sūtra's own teaching that a pure land is nothing other than a mind made pure.

He also cites, at length, the scene from the Nirvana Sūtra that stands behind this vow's language: light streaming from the Buddha's face at Kuśinagara, and the assembled beings' grief-stricken entreaty in verse — "like a calf that has lost its mother," "like a country with no king, its people starving" — begging him not to enter nirvana. Read alongside the vessel-and-reflection teaching, the two together give this vow its full weight: the grief is real and the language of loss is not dismissed, but the deeper teaching underneath it is that what looks like a buddha's departure is, from the buddha's own side, no departure at all — only a change in whether the world in front of him still has a clean enough vessel to catch the light.
