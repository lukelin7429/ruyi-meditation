The Buddha of Lapis Lazuli Light, and His Twelve Vows

This page opens Ru-Yi's translation of the Sūtra of the Fundamental Vows of Bhaiṣajyaguru (藥師琉璃光如來本願功德經), translated into Chinese by Xuanzang in 650 CE. It carries the sūtra's opening assembly at Vaiśālī, Mañjuśrī's request that the Buddha proclaim a name capable of dissolving karmic hindrance, and the Buddha's answer: an eastern buddha-land called Pure Lapis Lazuli, ruled by a buddha named for the medicine he offers every suffering being, together with the twelve great vows he made while he was still only a bodhisattva walking toward that name.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 1 of 5 complete Source: Taishō T0450, 藥師琉璃光如來本願功德經 (tr. Xuanzang) Reading notes after Taehyeon's 本願藥師經古迹 (T1770)

Translation Method

The Chinese source is Xuanzang's translation (玄奘, 602–664 CE), completed in 650 CE at the Hongfu Monastery in Chang'an, four years after his return from India. It is the fourth of five Chinese translations of this sūtra to survive — following Bo Shilizhu's lost 4th-century version, Huijian's 457 CE translation (T0449, still extant), and Dharmagupta's 615 CE translation (T0451, folded into a larger compilation) — and it is Xuanzang's version, shorter and plainer than the others, that has been chanted and studied in East Asia ever since. True to his reputation as the most literal of the great Chinese translators, Xuanzang keeps the Sanskrit term Bhagavān transliterated as 薄伽梵 throughout this sūtra's narration, reserving 世尊 ("World-Honored One") for direct address — a distinction this translation preserves in English rather than collapsing both into a single term.

This collection reads it with the commentary of Taehyeon (太賢, fl. 8th century), a monk of the Korean kingdom of Silla and one of the most prolific commentators in the entire East Asian Yogācāra tradition — traditionally credited with over fifty works, of which roughly a dozen survive, spanning Yogācāra, Madhyamaka, Vinaya, and Pure Land literature alike. His commentary on this sūtra, the Bonwon Yaksa-gyeong Gojeok (本願藥師經古迹, "Investigating the Traces of the Sūtra of the Fundamental Vows," T1770), runs to two fascicles and opens with the classical three-part exegetical structure — title, doctrinal classification, and text — before working through Xuanzang's sūtra clause by clause. Taehyeon explicitly rejects one contemporary scheme that would file this sūtra under a "gradual" or "situational" teaching, arguing instead that a sūtra which addresses Mañjuśrī directly on the causes and fruits of Mahāyāna practice, and describes a pure land free of any trace of concealment, belongs among the fully revealed teachings of the Great Vehicle. Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right. See also this site's note on translation method.

The Assembly at Vaiśālī, and Mañjuśrī's Request

Chinese Source

如是我聞：一時，薄伽梵遊化諸國，至廣嚴城，住樂音樹下。與大苾芻眾八千人俱，菩薩摩訶薩三萬六千，及國王、大臣、婆羅門、居士、天、龍、藥叉、人、非人等，無量大眾，恭敬圍繞，而為說法。

爾時，曼殊室利法王子，承佛威神，從座而起，偏袒一肩，右膝著地，向薄伽梵，曲躬合掌，白言：「世尊！惟願演說如是相類諸佛名號，及本大願殊勝功德，令諸聞者業障銷除，為欲利樂像法轉時諸有情故。」

爾時，世尊讚曼殊室利童子言：「善哉！善哉！曼殊室利！汝以大悲，勸請我說諸佛名號、本願功德，為拔業障所纏有情，利益安樂像法轉時諸有情故。汝今諦聽，極善思惟，當為汝說。」曼殊室利言：「唯然！願說，我等樂聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard. At one time the Bhagavān was journeying and teaching among various lands, and came to the city of Vaiśālī, where he dwelt beneath a Tree of Music. He was accompanied by a great assembly of eight thousand bhikṣus, and thirty-six thousand bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, together with kings, great ministers, brahmins, householders, gods, nāgas, yakṣas, humans, and non-humans — a measureless great assembly, who reverently surrounded him as he taught the Dharma.

At that time Mañjuśrī, Dharma Prince, sustained by the Buddha's majestic power, rose from his seat, bared one shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, bowed toward the Bhagavān with joined palms, and said: "World-Honored One, I beg only that you proclaim the names of buddhas of this same kind, together with the excellent merit of their fundamental vows, so that all who hear may have their karmic hindrances dissolved, and so that beings in the age of the semblance Dharma may gain benefit and ease thereby."

At that time the World-Honored One praised the youth Mañjuśrī, saying: "Excellent, excellent, Mañjuśrī! Moved by great compassion, you urge me to speak of the names of buddhas and the merit of their fundamental vows, to rescue beings bound by karmic hindrance, and to benefit and give ease to beings in the age of the semblance Dharma. Listen carefully now, and reflect well, and I shall speak it for you." Mañjuśrī said: "So be it — we wish gladly to hear it."

Reading note — after Taehyeon

Taehyeon glosses Bhagavān as carrying six meanings at once — sovereign (never bound by any affliction), blazing (burned clean by the fire of wisdom), magnificent (adorned with every excellent mark), renowned (his merit fully known), auspicious (whoever draws near him gains benefit), and honored (tireless in benefiting beings) — citing the Yogācārabhūmi's own verse on the term to justify Xuanzang's choice to transliterate rather than translate it. He glosses Vaiśālī itself as meaning "fullness of every virtue," and the Tree of Music by its cause: a light breeze moving through its branches produces, of itself, the correct court modes — hence its name. He also identifies three distinct groups within the "measureless great assembly": śrāvakas, bodhisattvas, and worldly beings (kings, brahmins, gods, and spirits) — a threefold division he will return to at the sūtra's close, when this same assembly rises together to depart.

On Mañjuśrī's request, Taehyeon reads each physical gesture as carrying its own meaning: rising from his seat signals a movement out from ultimate reality into engaged teaching; baring one shoulder signals readiness to labor for others' benefit; the right knee on the ground signals dwelling within the world; bowing toward the Bhagavān signals inclining toward what transcends it; and joined palms signal a mind made single-pointed. He notes that Mañjuśrī's request itself compresses three questions into one sentence — the buddha's name, the cause behind his vow, and the fruit that vow produced — which the sūtra will answer in that same order over the pages that follow. He also draws out the technical weight of "the age of the semblance Dharma" (像法轉時): citing the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra's six kinds of steadfastness — in dharma-body, in liberation, in meditation, in learning, in merit, and, last, only in contention — he maps these onto three five-hundred-year ages: the True Dharma (the first two steadfastnesses), the Semblance Dharma (the next two), and the Final Dharma (the last, when teaching alone survives without realization or its fruit). Mañjuśrī's question, on this reading, is asked on behalf of beings a thousand years removed from the Buddha's own lifetime — which is to say, on behalf of exactly the kind of reader who is encountering this sūtra now.

The Naming of Bhaiṣajyaguru, Buddha of Lapis Lazuli Light

Chinese Source

佛告曼殊室利：「東方去此過十殑伽沙等佛土，有世界名淨琉璃，佛號藥師琉璃光如來、應、正等覺、明行圓滿、善逝、世間解、無上丈夫、調御士、天人師、佛、薄伽梵。曼殊室利！彼佛世尊藥師琉璃光如來，本行菩薩道時，發十二大願，令諸有情，所求皆得。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Mañjuśrī: "To the east of this world, past buddha-lands as many as the sands of ten Ganges rivers, there is a world called Pure Lapis Lazuli, and its buddha is named the Tathāgata Bhaiṣajyaguru, Vaiḍūryaprabha — Worthy of Offerings, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, Complete in Understanding and Conduct, Well-Gone, Knower of the World, Unsurpassed, Tamer of Beings, Teacher of Gods and Humans, Buddha, Bhagavān. Mañjuśrī, when that World-Honored One, the Tathāgata of Lapis Lazuli Light, was practicing the bodhisattva path, he made twelve great vows, causing every being to obtain whatever they sought.

Reading note — after Taehyeon

Taehyeon notes that this is the first of three answers the Buddha owes Mañjuśrī — name, then vow-cause, then vow-fruit — and treats the ten epithets that follow the name as needing no fresh gloss here, since the Yogācārabhūmi and other texts already explain each one at length. He glosses the name itself in two parts joined by their shared function: "able to draw out every suffering is what earns the title Medicine (藥師); reaching everywhere without exception is what earns the title Lapis Lazuli Light (琉璃光)." What is cultivated, he adds, is called the fundamental vow (本願); what is realized by that cultivation is called merit (功德) — so that the sūtra's very title states, in miniature, a causal claim: this buddha's effect follows directly and only from a describable cause, which the sūtra is now about to describe.

On the twelve vows themselves, Taehyeon cites two other sources rarely brought into the same discussion of this sūtra: the (now-lost) Asura Sūtra, which states that "the Bodhisattva of Lapis Lazuli Light first made a general vow upon meeting the Buddha Jñānottara, and made his specific vows only later, at the place of the Buddha Ratnaśikhin"; and the Sūtra on the Present Appearance of the Buddhas of the Ten Directions, which names the buddha before whom these twelve vows were spoken as Nīlanāgaprabha. Taehyeon does not attempt to reconcile the two accounts or settle which is correct — he simply records that other scriptures locate this same vow-making in more than one place and before more than one buddha, a plurality he treats as unremarkable given how many kalpas separate the vow from its fruit.

The Twelve Great Vows

Chinese Source

「第一大願：願我來世得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提時，自身光明，熾然照曜無量無數無邊世界，以三十二大丈夫相、八十隨好，莊嚴其身；令一切有情，如我無異。

「第二大願：願我來世得菩提時，身如琉璃，內外明徹，淨無瑕穢，光明廣大，功德巍巍，身善安住，焰網莊嚴，過於日月；幽冥眾生，悉蒙開曉，隨意所趣，作諸事業。

「第三大願：願我來世得菩提時，以無量無邊智慧方便，令諸有情，皆得無盡所受用物，莫令眾生有所乏少。

「第四大願：願我來世得菩提時，若諸有情行邪道者，悉令安住菩提道中；若行聲聞獨覺乘者，皆以大乘而安立之。

「第五大願：願我來世得菩提時，若有無量無邊有情，於我法中修行梵行，一切皆令得不缺戒，具三聚戒。設有毀犯，聞我名已，還得清淨，不墮惡趣。

「第六大願：願我來世得菩提時，若諸有情，其身下劣，諸根不具，醜陋、頑愚、盲、聾、瘖、瘂、攣、躄、背僂、白癩、癲狂、種種病苦；聞我名已，一切皆得端正黠慧。諸根完具，無諸疾苦。

「第七大願：願我來世得菩提時，若諸有情，眾病逼切，無救無歸，無醫無藥，無親無家，貧窮多苦，我之名號，一經其耳，病悉得除，身心安樂，家屬資具，悉皆豐足，乃至證得無上菩提。

「第八大願：願我來世得菩提時，若有女人，為女百惡之所逼惱，極生厭離，願捨女身；聞我名已，一切皆得轉女成男，具丈夫相，乃至證得無上菩提。

「第九大願：願我來世得菩提時，令諸有情，出魔羂網，解脫一切外道纏縛；若墮種種惡見稠林，皆當引攝置於正見，漸令修習諸菩薩行，速證無上正等菩提。

「第十大願：願我來世得菩提時，若諸有情，王法所錄，縲縛鞭撻，繫閉牢獄，或當刑戮，及餘無量災難凌辱，悲愁煎迫，身心受苦；若聞我名，以我福德威神力故，皆得解脫一切憂苦。

「第十一大願：願我來世得菩提時，若諸有情，飢渴所惱，為求食故造諸惡業；得聞我名，專念受持，我當先以上妙飲食，飽足其身；後以法味，畢竟安樂而建立之。

「第十二大願：願我來世得菩提時，若諸有情，貧無衣服，蚊虻寒熱，晝夜逼惱；若聞我名，專念受持，如其所好，即得種種上妙衣服，亦得一切寶莊嚴具，華鬘塗香，鼓樂眾伎，隨心所翫，皆令滿足。

「曼殊室利！是為彼世尊藥師琉璃光如來、應、正等覺，行菩薩道時，所發十二微妙上願。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The first great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain Anuttarā-samyak-saṃbodhi, my own body's light will blaze forth, illuminating measureless, countless, boundless world-systems, my body adorned with the thirty-two marks of a great being and the eighty subsidiary excellent features; and I will cause every being to become no different from myself.

"The second great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, my body will be like lapis lazuli, clear and radiant within and without, pure and without blemish, its light vast and great, its merit towering, its form well and firmly established, adorned with a net of blazing light surpassing the sun and moon; and beings dwelling in darkness will all be opened to understanding, and will freely go about and carry out whatever work they wish.

"The third great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, by means of measureless, boundless wisdom and skillful means, I will cause every being to obtain an inexhaustible store of things to use and enjoy, and never let any being lack for anything.

"The fourth great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, should any beings walk a wrong path, I will cause them all to abide securely on the path of bodhi; and should any walk the vehicle of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha, I will establish them all firmly in the Great Vehicle.

"The fifth great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, should measureless, boundless beings practice the holy life within my teaching, I will cause every one of them to keep their precepts unbroken, complete in the threefold pure precepts; and should any of them transgress, then, on hearing my name, they will be restored to purity, and will not fall into an evil destiny.

"The sixth great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, should any beings have bodies that are inferior, their faculties incomplete — ugly, dull-witted, blind, deaf, mute, crippled, lame, hunchbacked, afflicted with leprosy, deranged, or suffering every kind of illness — then, on hearing my name, they will all become well-formed and clever, their faculties whole and complete, free of every affliction.

"The seventh great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, should any beings be pressed hard by every kind of illness, with no one to save them and nowhere to turn, without physician or medicine, without kin or home, poor and beset with suffering — should my name once pass through their ears, their illness will be entirely removed, their body and mind at ease, their family and possessions abundant and sufficient, until they at last realize unsurpassed bodhi.

"The eighth great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, should there be a woman pressed and troubled by the hundred afflictions of being a woman, so that she comes to feel the deepest revulsion and wishes to cast off her woman's body — then, on hearing my name, she will be transformed from a woman into a man, complete with a man's form, until she at last realizes unsurpassed bodhi.

"The ninth great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, I will cause every being to come forth from Māra's net and snare, and be freed from every bond of heterodox teachings; and should they fall into the dense thicket of various wrong views, I will draw them all out and set them upon right view, gradually leading them to cultivate the practices of the bodhisattvas, so that they may swiftly realize unsurpassed, perfect, complete bodhi.

"The tenth great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, should any beings be seized under the king's law, bound and whipped, imprisoned in fetters, or facing execution, or beset by every kind of measureless calamity and humiliation, tormented by grief and sorrow, suffering in body and mind — should they hear my name, then, through the power of my merit and majestic might, they will all be freed from every sorrow and suffering.

"The eleventh great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, should any beings be tormented by hunger and thirst, and commit every kind of evil deed in search of food — should they hear my name and hold it single-mindedly in mind, I will first satisfy their bodies with food of the finest kind, and afterward establish them in ultimate ease through the flavor of the Dharma.

"The twelfth great vow: I vow that when, in a future life, I attain bodhi, should any beings be poor and without clothing, tormented day and night by mosquitoes and gnats, by cold and heat — should they hear my name and hold it single-mindedly in mind, then, according to their own liking, they will obtain every kind of fine clothing, and also every kind of precious ornament, garlands and fragrant unguents, music and drums and every kind of entertainment, and all that their hearts desire will be entirely satisfied.

"Mañjuśrī, these are the twelve subtle and supreme vows that the World-Honored One, the Tathāgata Bhaiṣajyaguru Vaiḍūryaprabha, Worthy of Offerings, Perfectly and Fully Awakened, made while practicing the bodhisattva path.

Reading note — after Taehyeon

Taehyeon does not read the twelve vows as a loose list but as six deliberate pairs, each pair answering a single concern from two directions. The first pair — light filling boundless worlds, a body like lapis lazuli reaching even the darkest beings — he calls the "gate of benefiting both the pure and the impure," since the first vow perfects the Buddha's own pure land while the second reaches beings still trapped in darkness elsewhere. The third and fourth vows form the "gate of the worldly and the transcendent": inexhaustible material provision answers worldly lack, while the redirection of wrong paths and lesser vehicles into the Great Vehicle answers a properly transcendent concern. The fifth and sixth form the "gate of preserving both precept and body": unbroken precepts for those who keep them, and whole, uncrippled faculties for those born without them — and Taehyeon is careful to note that "ugly" glosses only bodily inferiority while "dull-witted" glosses mental inferiority, with blindness, deafness, and muteness covering the sense-faculties specifically and derangement covering the mental faculty, so that the vow's list of afflictions is not miscellaneous but organized.

The seventh and eighth vows form the "gate of removing inner and outer suffering" — the seventh removing external suffering (illness, poverty, isolation), the eighth removing what Taehyeon, citing the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own harsh line that "every woman is a dwelling-place for every evil," treats as an internally-borne suffering specific to a woman's embodied condition in that text's own cosmology, addressed by the vow's promise of rebirth in a man's body. The ninth and tenth form the "gate of untying bonds both inner and outer": right view untying the snare of wrong views, majestic power untying the literal bonds of imprisonment and punishment. The eleventh and twelfth form the "gate of aspiration toward food and clothing": establishing beings first in the taste of the Dharma, then in the plainest physical comforts of clothing and adornment. Six pairs, Taehyeon concludes, is not an arbitrary count — it is the vow answering every register of suffering he can find in the sūtra's own list, worldly and transcendent, bodily and mental, inner and outer, alike.

Part 1 Complete

This closes Part 1: the assembly gathered beneath the Tree of Music at Vaiśālī, Mañjuśrī's threefold question about a name, a cause, and a fruit, and the Buddha's answer — an eastern pure land named for lapis lazuli, and the twelve vows its buddha made long before he held that name at all. Part 2 turns to the land those vows produced: a buddha-field said to rival Sukhāvatī itself, watched over by two attendant bodhisattvas of light, and the sūtra's first account of what beings weighed down by greed, broken precepts, or wrong view actually gain simply by hearing this buddha's name.
