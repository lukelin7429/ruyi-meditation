Vows, Precepts, and the Placement of Letters

Part 8 gathers six shorter chapters. A vision of the maṇḍala built entirely from the practitioner's own body; eight further secret seals; a six-month practice schedule moving through the five elements in turn; the letter A as the heart of every mantra; a complete map of Sanskrit letters onto every part of the body, from throat to genitals; and, closing the Part, this sūtra's most sustained treatment of ethics — the ten precepts, each paired with its own bodhisattva exception.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 8 of 11 complete Source: Taishō T0848, 大毘盧遮那成佛神變加持經 (tr. Śubhakarasiṃha and Yixing) Reading notes after Yixing's 大毘盧遮那成佛經疏 (T1796)

The Inner Heart Maṇḍala

Chinese Source (abridged)

爾時大日世尊入於等至三昧，觀未來世諸眾生故，住於定中。即時諸佛國土地平如掌，五寶間錯……無量菩薩隨福所感宮室殿堂意生之座，如來信解願力所生。法界幖幟大蓮華王出現，如來法界性身安住其中，隨諸眾生種種性欲令得歡喜。……說如是音聲已，還入如來不思議法身。

爾時世尊復告執金剛祕密主言：「善男子！諦聽內心漫荼羅。祕密主！彼身地即是法界自性，真言密印加持而加持之，以本性清淨故、羯磨金剛所護持故，淨除一切塵垢，我人、眾生、壽者、意生、儒童、造立者等株杌過患。方壇四門，西向通達，周旋界道。內現意生八葉大蓮華王，抽莖敷蕊綵絢端妙。其中如來一切世間最尊特身，超越身語意地至於心地逮得殊勝悅意之果。於彼東方寶幢如來、南方開敷華王如來、北方鼓音如來、西方無量壽如來，東南方普賢菩薩、東北方觀自在菩薩、西南方妙吉祥童子、西北方慈氏菩薩。一切蕊中，佛菩薩母六波羅蜜三昧眷屬而自莊嚴。下列持明諸忿怒眾、持金剛主菩薩以為其莖，處于無盡大海。一切地居天等，其數無量而環繞之。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the World-Honored One Mahāvairocana entered the samādhi of equal attainment, and, so as to survey the beings of ages yet to come, abided in that absorption. At once every buddha-land's own ground became level as the palm of a hand, inlaid throughout with the five jewels…measureless bodhisattvas, according to the merit each had accrued, appeared upon palaces, halls, and thrones born purely of mind, born of the Tathāgata's own confident understanding and the power of his vow. The emblem of the dharma-realm, a great lotus-king, then appeared, and the Tathāgata's own dharma-realm-natured body abode within it, bringing joy to beings according to each of their own natures and desires…Having given voice to this sound, he returned once more into the Tathāgata's own inconceivable dharma-body.

At that time the World-Honored One said again to Vajrapāṇi, Lord of Secrets: "Good son, listen closely to the inner heart maṇḍala. Lord of Secrets, that ground of the body is itself the dharma-realm's own nature; empowered by the secret seal of mantra, and, because its fundamental nature is pure and protected by the vajra of ritual action, it is purified of every speck of defilement — the faults, stump and root alike, of self, person, being, life, mind-born notion, the young scholar, and the maker. The square altar has four gates, opening toward the west, with a boundary-path circling all around. Within, an eight-petal great lotus-king born of mind appears, its stem rising, its filament and pistil unfolding, gloriously and finely adorned. Within it dwells the Tathāgata's own body, most honored throughout the whole world, passing beyond the grounds of body, speech, and mind to reach the very ground of the heart, and attaining there the fruit of surpassing delight. To the east, the Tathāgata Ratnaketu; to the south, the Tathāgata Saṃkusumitarāja; to the north, the Tathāgata of the Drum's Sound; to the west, the Tathāgata Amitāyus; to the southeast, Samantabhadra Bodhisattva; to the northeast, Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva; to the southwest, the youth Mañjuśrī; to the northwest, Maitreya Bodhisattva. Within every filament, the mother of every buddha and bodhisattva, the six pāramitās, adorns herself with her own retinue in samādhi. Below, arrayed as the very stem, are every wrathful vidyā-holder and Vajrapāṇi, lord of the vajra family, standing amid a boundless great ocean, encircled by every earth-dwelling deity beyond number."

Reading note

This chapter's "inner heart maṇḍala" restates the central eight-petal court of Part 2 with one significant substitution: where the outer maṇḍala names the north buddha as Akṣobhya, this inner version names him "the Tathāgata of the Drum's Sound" (鼓音如來, Divyadundubhimeghanirghoṣa) — the more standard identification in the fully developed Womb Maṇḍala tradition, and a small, precise example of how this sūtra itself is not perfectly uniform in every deity name from chapter to chapter, a variation worth noting honestly rather than silently harmonizing. The chapter's deeper claim is structural: this is the same maṇḍala already given in full iconographic, letter, and mudrā form across Parts 2, 5, and 6, now relocated entirely inside the practitioner's own body — "that ground of the body is itself the dharma-realm's own nature" — collapsing the distance between an externally painted maṇḍala and the practitioner contemplating it that this sūtra has been narrowing since Part 1's claim that mind, space, and bodhi are three names for one thing.

Eight Secret Seals

Eight further mudrās follow, each paired with the shape its own contemplated maṇḍala should take — a triangle, the letter VA, a moon-disc, a half-moon, a rainbow, and further forms — closing with the Buddha's own instruction that these particular eight should be given only to a disciple already initiated, "gentle and pliant in nature, diligent and firm, who has made a surpassing vow, reveres their teacher, remembers kindness received, is pure within and without, and would relinquish their own body and life in seeking the Dharma."

Chinese Source (selected)

謂以智慧三昧手作空心合掌，而散風輪地輪如放光焰，是世尊本威德生印。其漫荼羅三角而具光明。彼真言曰：「南麼三曼多勃馱喃　㘕落　莎訶」
……即以此印，屈其火輪，餘相如前，是世尊陀羅尼印。其漫荼羅猶如彩虹，而遍圍之垂金剛幡。彼真言曰：「南麼三曼多勃馱喃　勃馱陀羅尼　娑沒㗚底沫羅馱那羯履　馱囉也薩鑁　薄伽嚩底　阿迦囉嚩底　三麼曳　莎訶」

Ru-Yi Translation

Forming a hollow-centered joined palm with both hands, and spreading the wind- and earth-fingers as though releasing flames of light — this is the seal of the World-Honored One's own original majestic power. Its contemplated maṇḍala is a triangle, radiant with light. Namaḥ samanta-buddhānāṃ raṃ raḥ svāhā.
…With this same seal, bending the fire-finger, the rest as before — this is the World-Honored One's own dhāraṇī-seal. Its contemplated maṇḍala is like a rainbow, hung all around with vajra banners. Namaḥ samanta-buddhānāṃ buddha-dhāraṇī smṛti-bala-dhāraṇa-karī dhāraye svāhā svāhā svāhā samaye svāhā.

Reading note

The eight seals of this chapter each pair a specific hand-position with a specific visualized shape — triangle, crescent, rainbow, moon-disc, half-moon — extending the correspondence already established in Part 5's five-syllable body meditation (square, circle, triangle, half-moon, jewel) to a further, more restricted set of eight secret forms. The Buddha's closing qualification for who may receive them repeats, almost verbatim, the qualities Part 2 already required of a worthy disciple before initiation — reverence, firmness, remembered gratitude, inner and outer purity — a consistency this collection's translation notes track as one of this sūtra's recurring structural habits: ethical fitness, stated once early on, is assumed and reapplied rather than re-derived each time a more advanced teaching is introduced.

Six Months of Vidyā-Holder Practice

Chinese Source

「最初金輪觀，　住大因陀羅，當結金剛印，　飲乳以資身。行者一月滿，　能調出入息，次於第二月，　嚴正水輪中，應以蓮華印，　而服醇淨水。次於第三月，　勝妙火輪觀，噉不求之食，　即以大慧力，燒滅一切罪，　而生身意語。第四月風輪，　行者常服風，結轉法輪印，　攝心以持誦。金剛水輪觀，　依住於瑜伽，是為第五月，　遠離得非得。行者無所著，　等同三菩提，和合風火輪，　出過眾過患。復一月持誦，　亦捨利非利，梵釋等天眾，　摩睺毘舍遮，遠住而敬禮，　一切為守護，皆悉奉教命。彼常得如是，　人天藥叉神，持明諸靈仙，　翊侍其左右，隨所命當作。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"In the very first month, contemplating the golden earth-wheel, abiding in the great vajra seat, one should form the vajra seal, and nourish the body with milk alone. Once the practitioner has completed this first month, able to regulate the breath moving in and out, then, in the second month, dwelling reverently within the water wheel, one should form the lotus seal, and drink only pure, unmixed water. Next, in the third month, contemplating the supreme and wondrous fire wheel, one eats only food that has not been asked for, and, through the power of great wisdom, burns away every offense, giving rise thereby to a purified body, mind, and speech. In the fourth month, the wind wheel: the practitioner constantly subsists on wind alone, forming the dharma-wheel seal, gathering the mind for recitation. Contemplating the vajra water-wheel, abiding in yoga, this is the fifth month, transcending both attainment and its absence. The practitioner, attached to nothing, becomes equal to the threefold bodhi, uniting the wind and fire wheels, passing beyond every fault. A further month of recitation follows, relinquishing both benefit and its absence — Brahmā, Indra, and the host of gods, mahoragas and piśācas, stand at a distance and bow in reverence, all offering their protection, every one submitting to this command. Such a one constantly gains this: humans, gods, yakṣas, spirits, vidyā-holders, and every numinous sage attend at their left and right, ready to act at their bidding."

Reading note

This six-month schedule maps the same five elements already given bodily placement in Part 5 onto a sustained practice regimen, one element per month — earth, water, fire, wind, and a fifth month uniting water and vajra — each paired with an increasingly severe ascetic discipline: milk alone, then only water, then only unrequested food, then subsisting on breath itself. The sixth and final month drops the elemental sequence entirely and asks instead for "relinquishing both benefit and its absence" — a deliberate loosening of the whole preceding structure, so that six months of increasingly demanding physical austerity resolve not in a still more extreme practice but in the release of the very distinction between gain and loss the ascetic months might otherwise have reinforced. That the chapter's title names this a vidyā-holder's "禁戒" (precepts or restraint) rather than merely a meditation schedule marks the fasting itself as a form of moral discipline in its own right, continuous with the ethical precepts given fuller treatment later in this same Part.

The Master's True Wisdom, and the Letter A

Chinese Source

「所謂阿字者，　一切真言心，從此遍流出，　無量諸真言，一切戲論息，　能生巧智慧。……由彼本初字，　遍在增加字，眾字以成音，　支體由是生，故此遍一切，　身生種種德，今說所分布，　佛子一心聽。以心而作心，　餘以布支分，一切如是作，　即同於我體。安住瑜伽座，　尋念諸如來，若於此教法，　解斯廣大智，正覺大功德，　說為阿闍梨，是即為如來，　亦即名為佛。……阿字第一命，　嚩字名為水，囉字名為火，　[合*牛]字名忿怒，佉字同虛空，　所謂極空點，知此最真實，　說名阿闍梨。故應具方便，　了知佛所說，常作精勤修，　當得不死句。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"The letter A is the very heart of every mantra: from it flow forth measureless mantras without exception; every idle discourse is stilled by it, and it is able to give rise to skillful wisdom…From this fundamental, primordial letter, present throughout every added letter, every syllable is formed into sound, and every limb of the body is born from it — thus, pervading everything, it gives rise to every quality within the body. I shall now explain how it is distributed; children of the Buddha, listen with a single mind. With the mind, one makes the mind itself; the rest is distributed among the body's own limbs — carrying this out in just this way, one becomes identical with my own body. Abiding in the yogic seat, holding every Tathāgata in mind, whoever, within this teaching, understands this vast wisdom, this great merit of perfect awakening, is called a master — and this very one is the Tathāgata, and is called, too, a buddha…The letter A is the very first life-force; the letter VA is called water; the letter RA is called fire; the letter HŪṂ is called wrath; the letter KHA is equal to space — namely, the point of utmost emptiness. Whoever knows this as the truest reality is called a master. One should therefore be complete in skillful means, fully understanding what the Buddha has taught, and, constantly practicing with diligence, will attain the phrase of deathlessness."

Reading note

This chapter's closing five-syllable list (A as life-force, VA as water, RA as fire, HŪṂ as wrath, KHA as space) is a variant of the A-VA-RA-HA-KHA system given full bodily placement in Part 5, substituting the wrathful HŪṂ for the more common HA at the fourth position — a reminder that this sūtra's own five-element formula is not perfectly fixed even within a single text, and that later systematized traditions harmonized variants such as this one into the single canonical sequence now most commonly taught. The chapter's more striking claim comes earlier: whoever genuinely understands the letter A as the heart of every mantra "is called a master — and this very one is the Tathāgata, and is called, too, a buddha." This is among the most direct statements in the sūtra of what later Shingon doctrine would call sokushin jōbutsu stated as a claim about the master's title itself, not only about a state of attainment: on this chapter's own logic, "ācārya" and "buddha" name the same thing once this single letter is truly known.

Placing the Letters on the Body

Chapter Seventeen carries out, in complete and systematic form, what Part 5 and the previous chapter each gestured toward more briefly: a full map of Sanskrit consonants and vowels onto every part of the practitioner's own body, from the throat down through the torso and limbs to the eyes, ears, and cheeks, closing with the same two syllables — AṂ for attained bodhi, AḤ for parinirvāṇa — that closed the four-transformation teaching of Part 4.

Chinese Source

「復次祕密主！　諸佛所宣說，　安布諸字門，佛子一心聽。迦字在咽下，　佉字在齶上，誐字以為頸，　伽字在喉中，遮字為舌根，　車字在舌中，若字為舌端，　社字舌生處，吒字以為脛，　咤字應知髀，拏字說為腰，　荼字以安坐，多字最後分，　他字應知腹，娜字為二手，　馱字名為脇，波字以為背，　頗字應知胸，麼字為二肘，　婆字次臂下，莽字住於心，　耶字陰藏相，囉字名為眼，　[口*邏]字為廣額，縊伊在二眥，　塢烏為二脣，翳藹為二耳，　污奧為二頰，暗字菩提句，　噁字般涅槃，知是一切法，　行者成正覺，一切智資財，　常在於其心，世號一切智，　是謂薩婆若。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, Lord of Secrets, listen with a single mind to the placement of these letter-gates, as every buddha has taught it. The letter KA is below the throat; the letter KHA is at the roof of the mouth; the letter GA forms the neck; the letter GHA is within the throat; the letter CA is the root of the tongue; the letter CHA is the middle of the tongue; the letter JA is the tip of the tongue; the letter JHA is where the tongue arises; the letter ṬA forms the shin; the letter ṬHA should be known as the thigh; the letter ḌA is spoken of as the waist; the letter ḌHA is the seat on which one sits; the letter TA is the very lowest part; the letter THA should be known as the belly; the letter DA is the two hands; the letter DHA is called the ribs; the letter PA forms the back; the letter PHA should be known as the chest; the letter MA is the two elbows; the letter BA, next, the underarms; the letter BHA dwells at the heart; the letter YA is the mark of the hidden organ; the letter RA is called the eyes; the letter LA is the broad forehead; short and long I are the two corners of the eyes; short and long U are the two lips; short and long E are the two ears; short and long O are the two cheeks; the letter AṂ is the phrase of bodhi; the letter AḤ is parinirvāṇa. Knowing this to be the truth of every dharma, the practitioner accomplishes perfect awakening; the very substance of all-wisdom ever dwells within their heart — this the world calls all-wisdom, this is what is meant by sarvajña."

Reading note

This chapter's letter-by-letter map follows, with only minor variation, the same alphabetical order as the forty-two gateways of Part 3 — but where that earlier chapter assigned each letter a specific doctrinal meaning (KA for freedom from action, DA for the ungraspability of giving, and so on), this one assigns each letter a specific bodily location instead, so that a practitioner who has learned both chapters holds, for any given syllable, both what it means and where on the body it belongs. The sequence deliberately runs from the throat downward through the trunk and limbs before returning to the face — throat and mouth, then torso, then hands and arms, then the lowest points of the body, then finally the eyes, ears, and cheeks — closing, as Part 4's four-transformation teaching also closed, on AṂ and AḤ: attained bodhi and parinirvāṇa, the map's own final destination after every other letter has found its place.

Skillful Means, and the Ten Wholesome Ways

Chapter Eighteen turns from ritual and visualization to the sūtra's most sustained treatment of ethics: the classical ten wholesome courses of action (十善業道) — already familiar from Part 1's account of a practitioner's very first steps — reframed here specifically for a bodhisattva equipped with skillful means. Each precept is stated first as an ordinary prohibition, then given its own exception: a specific circumstance in which the same act, performed from compassion rather than self-interest, remains within the precept rather than breaking it.

PreceptThe basic prohibitionThe bodhisattva's skillful-means exception
Not taking lifeSet aside weapons; hold no intent to kill; guard another's life as one's own.May act among beings, without a mind of enmity, so as to free them from the retribution their own evil karma would otherwise bring.
Not taking what is not givenGive rise to no thought of taking what belongs to another.Seeing a being hoarding through miserliness, refusing to cultivate the merit of giving, may act — without thought of self or other — to break that miserliness, and the being later comes to praise the gift and gain excellent qualities from it.
Not sexual misconductGive rise to no desire toward another's spouse or protected kin.May act to gather and protect beings fit to be liberated, as their circumstances require.
Not false speechNever lie, even to save one's own life — to do so would deceive the very bodhi of every buddha.(No exception given: this precept alone, the text states, admits none.)
Not harsh speechSpeak to every being with a gentle mind, in words suited to their kind.May, on seeing a being fixed on a cause leading to an evil destiny, use harsh words deliberately, in order to break that course before it ripens.
Not divisive speechNever speak so as to sow discord or cause harm between beings.May, for a being attached to one fixed view, speak words that seem divisive so as to draw them away from it and onto the single path — the path of the wisdom of all-wisdom.
Not idle or frivolous speechSpeak words fitted to time and place, bringing about real benefit and gladdening the ear.May lead with playfulness or laughter to arouse a being's own desire and joy, and thereby settle them in the Dharma — words that appear to carry no benefit, yet do not entangle the bodhisattva in birth-and-death.
Not covetousnessGive rise to no defiled intent toward what another possesses or enjoys.Should rejoice at another's own generosity, thinking "how truly excellent" — repeatedly encouraging it, rather than causing beings to lose what wealth they have.
Not ill willCultivate patience everywhere, unmoved by anger, regarding friend and enemy with an equal mind.(No exception given: a bodhisattva's own fundamental purity, the text states, admits no genuine ill will at all.)
Not wrong viewAbandon wrong view and practice right view; fear the consequences of one's acts in future lives; hold no harm, no crookedness, no flattery; keep a heart upright and settled in the Three Jewels.(No exception given: wrong view is named "the mother of every unwholesome dharma," able to sever every one of a bodhisattva's own good roots — not to be indulged even in jest.)
Chinese Source (framing verse)

「過去諸正覺，　及與未來世，現在仁中尊，　具足智方便，修行無上覺，　得無漏悉地，亦說餘學處，　離於方便智。當知大勤勇，　誘進諸聲聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"The Perfectly Awakened Ones of the past, and those of the age to come, and the honored ones of the present, all complete in wisdom and skillful means, cultivate unsurpassed awakening, and attain undefiled siddhi; they also teach a further set of precepts, apart from this wisdom of skillful means — know that the Greatly Diligent One teaches this second set only to draw śrāvakas gradually onward."

Reading note

The four precepts this chapter gives no exception for — false speech, ill will, and wrong view, together with the four root offenses named at the chapter's close (slandering the Dharma, abandoning bodhicitta, miserliness, and harming beings) — mark the outer limit of skillful means as this sūtra understands it: every other precept can, in the right hands and for the right reason, be set aside for a being's own benefit, but these remain fixed, because what they protect (truthfulness itself, a heart free of genuine malice, and right view as the root of every wholesome quality) is not the kind of thing skillful means can compromise without undermining the very compassion it claims to serve. The chapter is explicit that this reading of the ten precepts is not the only one the Buddha teaches — a plainer, exception-free version exists as well, offered specifically to śrāvakas "so as to draw them gradually onward" — making clear that the skillful-means framework given here is calibrated to a bodhisattva's own more demanding standard of accountability (self and other both weighed, in every exception, before the precept is set aside) rather than a general loosening of ethical discipline for anyone who might invoke it.

The chapter also distinguishes lay from monastic bodhisattvas explicitly, in a passage this page has not translated at length for space: a lay bodhisattva may hold worldly rank, preside over a household, and even employ the arts of dance, drama, and public performance as skillful means of gathering beings through the four means of attraction — provided the same five basic precepts (against killing, stealing, false speech, sexual misconduct, and wrong view) remain intact underneath. This sūtra's ethics, in other words, were never framed as requiring monastic withdrawal from ordinary life; a king, a parent, or a performer is given, in this same chapter, exactly the same path toward buddhahood already described for the ascetic vidyā-holder fasting through six months of elemental practice.

Part 8 Complete

This closes Part 8: the maṇḍala rebuilt entirely inside the practitioner's own body, eight further secret seals, a six-month elemental fast, the letter A named as identical with the master's own title of "buddha," a complete map of the alphabet onto the body from throat to genitals, and the sūtra's fullest treatment of precepts — most of them subject to compassionate exception, four of them held absolutely fixed. Part 9 gathers the remaining thirteen chapters of this sūtra's main teaching — the hundred-syllable mantra sequence, further doctrinal chapters, the fire-offering rite in full, and the closing entrustment that ends the text's core thirty-one chapters.
