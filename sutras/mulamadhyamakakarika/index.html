Why This Text

If you read one work of Buddhist philosophy, it is usually this one. Almost every later Mahāyāna school — Chan, Tiantai, Huayan, Pure Land's doctrinal defenders, the whole of Tibetan scholasticism — traces some part of its thinking back to these 446 verses.

What makes the Middle Treatise singular is not its conclusion but its method. Nāgārjuna almost never asserts a position of his own. He takes a claim his opponent already holds, follows it out with complete seriousness, and shows that it does not survive contact with itself. Chapter after chapter proceeds this way: motion, the senses, the aggregates, fire and fuel, time, karma, the Buddha, the Four Noble Truths, nirvāṇa. Each is examined; each turns out to be unfindable as a fixed, self-contained thing; and each, crucially, keeps working perfectly well afterward.

That last point is the one most readers miss, so it is worth stating at the front door. Emptiness is not nothingness. To say a thing is empty (空, śūnya) is to say it has no svabhāva — no nature it possesses by and of itself, independently of causes, parts, and conditions. It is not to say the thing is absent, illusory, or unimportant. Nāgārjuna is explicit about this, and he becomes almost impatient about it in Chapter 24, when an opponent accuses him of destroying the entire Buddhist path.

眾因緣生法，　我說即是無，
亦為是假名，　亦是中道義。

Whatever arises from a host of causes and conditions,
that, I declare, is without being;
it is also a conventional designation,
and it is also the meaning of the middle way.

Chapter 24, verse 18 — the most quoted verse in Madhyamaka, and the hinge of the whole treatise. Dependent arising, emptiness, conventional naming, and the middle way are not four doctrines; they are one situation, described four times. A note on the wording: Kumārajīva's verse reads 無, "without being," where Piṅgala's commentary one line later restates the same line with 空, "empty" — the form in which later Chinese tradition almost always quotes it. The sense is the same either way, and the commentary fixes it: without inherent nature, never without existence.

Read that verse slowly and much of the difficulty dissolves. Things are empty because they arise dependently — the emptiness and the arising are the same fact, not competing claims. And because they arise dependently, they can be named, used, cultivated, and transformed. Nāgārjuna's counter-charge against his opponent is that it is fixed things which would make the Buddhist path impossible: something with a permanent nature of its own could never change, never be purified, never be liberated. His summary line is 以有空義故，一切法得成 — because emptiness works, everything works.

Nāgārjuna, and the treatise's afterlife

Little about Nāgārjuna's life is certain beyond the approximate dates — somewhere in the second or third century, in southern India. What is certain is the scale of his influence. The Middle Treatise was carried to China by Kumārajīva, the great Kuchean translator, who rendered it into Chinese in 409 CE at Chang'an; it became one of the three foundational texts of the Chinese Sanlun ("Three Treatises") school, and its arguments run underneath a great deal of Chan. Carried instead into Tibet, it became the object of a commentarial tradition that has not stopped since.

Two other texts on this site sit directly alongside it. Candrakīrti's Madhyamakāvatāra — "Entering the Middle Way" — was written precisely because this treatise is so hard to enter, and walks the reader up to Nāgārjuna's insight through the ten bodhisattva grounds. And the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra, the vast commentary on the Perfection of Wisdom also attributed to Nāgārjuna and also translated by Kumārajīva, is the expansive counterpart to this compressed one.

How to actually read it

  1. Start with Part 1 and do not skip it. Chapter 1 sets up the machinery — the eight negations, the four-cornered refutation of arising, the dismantling of the four conditions — that every later chapter reuses. If the tetralemma is familiar to you by the end of Part 1, the rest is much easier going.
  2. Read the verses and the commentary as one text. Nāgārjuna's verses often state only a conclusion; Piṅgala supplies the argument that got there. On these pages both are translated, side by side with the Chinese.
  3. If you only have an hour, read Chapter 24. It is in Part 8, and it is the chapter the other twenty-six exist to make possible — the objection that emptiness destroys Buddhism, and the reply that reverses the charge completely.
  4. Expect repetition, and treat it as method. The same argument returns in a dozen vocabularies because grasping has a dozen hiding places. Piṅgala says so directly in Chapter 1: the extra negations exist not because the logic requires them but because people will not accept the first one.

Three questions Western readers ask

"If nothing has inherent existence, isn't this just nihilism dressed up?" It is the opposite, and Nāgārjuna anticipates the charge by name. Nihilism (ucchedavāda) says things do not exist; Madhyamaka says things exist dependently, which is the only way anything could exist at all. Chapter 15 puts both errors side by side — 定有則著常，定無則著斷, fixed existence is eternalism and fixed non-existence is annihilationism — and refuses both. The "middle" in the title is the refusal.

"Doesn't refuting causation make morality meaningless?" No, and the treatise is unusually blunt here. Chapter 1 argues that a genuinely causeless world would be monstrous: generosity would land you in hell and atrocity in heaven, since nothing would follow from anything. What is denied is not that acts have consequences but that causal power is a substance sitting inside a cause, findable there. Chapter 17 then spends thirty-three verses on exactly how karma works without a permanent self to carry it.

"Chapter 22 says the Buddha cannot be found. Is that an attack on the Buddha?" It is the reverse — it is the highest compliment the system can pay. The Tathāgata is empty in precisely the way everything else is empty, which is why Chapter 22 concludes that the nature of the Tathāgata and the nature of the world are one nature. A Buddha exempted from emptiness would be a Buddha exempted from dependent arising, and so from the very insight that makes him a Buddha.

Key Terms

śūnyatā 空 — emptiness. The absence of inherent, self-standing nature. Not the absence of the thing. The openness that makes change, causation, and liberation possible.
svabhāva 自性 — inherent nature, own-being. A nature a thing would possess by itself, independently of causes and parts. What every chapter of the treatise looks for and fails to find.
pratītyasamutpāda 因緣 · 緣起 — dependent arising. That things come to be in dependence on conditions. For Nāgārjuna this is not the opposite of emptiness but its other name.
prapañca 戲論 — conceptual elaboration; the mind's manufacture of ever-finer categories, then mistaking them for the world's own joints. What the opening homage praises the Buddha for quieting.
catuṣkoṭi 四句 — the four corners: from itself, from another, from both, from neither. Indian logic's exhaustive options, all four denied in Chapter 1's third verse.
八不 — the eight negations: neither arising nor ceasing, neither permanent nor annihilated, neither identical nor different, neither coming nor going. The treatise's opening homage, and its thesis in miniature.
二諦 (saṃvṛti / paramārtha) — the two truths, conventional and ultimate. Chapter 24 insists that whoever cannot distinguish them will not understand the deep meaning — and that the ultimate cannot be taught without relying on the conventional.
Piṅgala 青目 — "Blue Eyes," the Indian commentator whose prose Kumārajīva translated together with the verses in 409 CE. Nothing else of his work survives, in any language.

The Nine Parts

All twenty-seven chapters, grouped by argument rather than by scroll — each part carries the Chinese and English side by side, Piṅgala's commentary in full, and reading notes. Read straight through, or start wherever your question starts.

Part 1 · Chapter 1 · 16 verses

The Eight Negations, and the Four Conditions

The opening homage, the fourfold refutation of arising, and the dismantling of the Abhidharma's four conditions. The machinery every later chapter reuses.

觀因緣品第一 Part 2 · Chapters 2–5 · 50 verses

Motion, Seeing, and What a Person Is Made Of

The famous analysis of motion — the gone, the not-yet-gone, the going — then the emptying of the senses, the aggregates, and the elements.

觀去來品第二 至 觀六種品第五 Part 3 · Chapters 6–8 · 57 verses

Defilement, the Three Marks, and the Agent

One argument in three keys: an attribute and its bearer, an act and its agent, cannot be established as either identical or separate. Includes the treatise's longest chapter.

觀染染者品第六 至 觀作作者品第八 Part 4 · Chapters 9–12 · 46 verses

Fire, Fuel, and the Self That Was Never There

Is there someone who exists prior to experience? The classic fire-and-fuel model for the self and the aggregates, the beginninglessness of saṃsāra, and the question of who suffers.

觀本住品第九 至 觀苦品第十二 Part 5 · Chapters 13–16 · 38 verses

Emptiness, and the Danger of Making It a View

Where emptiness is named and immediately guarded: those who take emptiness itself as a view, the buddhas cannot cure. Being, non-being, bondage, and release.

觀行品第十三 至 觀縛解品第十六 Part 6 · Chapters 17–19 · 51 verses

Karma, the Reality of Things, and Time

How a deed ripens without a self to carry it — and then Chapter 18, the philosophical summit of the work, on the reality of things beyond the reach of speech.

觀業品第十七 至 觀時品第十九 Part 7 · Chapters 20–22 · 60 verses

Cause and Fruit, Dissolution, and the Tathāgata

Causation revisited through the assembly of conditions, the absence of any findable beginning or end, and the sevenfold search for the Buddha that comes up empty.

觀因果品第二十 至 觀如來品第二十二 Part 8 · Chapters 23–25 · 88 verses

The Inversions, the Four Truths, and Nirvāṇa

The climax. The charge that emptiness destroys Buddhism, the reply that reverses it, the treatise's most quoted verse — and nirvāṇa found to differ in no way from the world.

觀顛倒品第二十三 至 觀涅槃品第二十五 Part 9 · Chapters 26–27 · 40 verses

The Twelve Links, and the End of All Views

The treatise turns back to the śrāvaka teaching, sets out the twelve links plainly, sweeps away the speculative views about past and future selves, and closes in homage.

About the Source

The Chinese on these pages is Kumārajīva's translation of 409 CE (鳩摩羅什, Taishō T1564), made at Chang'an and in the public domain for sixteen centuries. It has a feature the surviving Sanskrit does not: after each root verse it carries a prose commentary by an Indian teacher the Chinese sources call Piṅgala (青目, "Blue Eyes"), which Kumārajīva translated in the same undertaking as the verses themselves.

These pages translate both layers as the text proper — the root verses and the ancient commentary — rather than rendering the bare verses and leaving the reader to reconstruct the arguments compressed inside them. Nāgārjuna's verses frequently state only a conclusion; Piṅgala supplies the premises. He argues with the Abhidharma scholastics of his own day, in their vocabulary, about their problems — and he is what Chinese Buddhism actually read, for sixteen hundred years, whenever it read the Middle Treatise.

On the reading notes

The reading notes throughout these nine parts are Ru-Yi's own, written fresh from the primary text, and are deliberately not attributed to any modern teacher. This is a considered choice. The most authoritative modern Chinese commentary on this treatise, Venerable Yin Shun's (印順導師, 1906–2005) 中觀論頌講記, remains in copyright — in Taiwan, protection runs for the author's life plus fifty years — and unlike the Heart Sūtra or Diamond Sūtra pages on this site, no comparably authoritative public-domain modern lecture series on the Middle Treatise was available to work from. Rather than lean on a source we cannot lawfully reproduce, the notes here follow the same approach as our Madhyamakāvatāra reading notes: original commentary on a public-domain primary text, written in our own words.
