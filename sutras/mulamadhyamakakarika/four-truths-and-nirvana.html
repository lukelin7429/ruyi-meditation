The Inversions, the Four Truths, and Nirvāṇa

Examining the Inversions — Examining Nirvāṇa觀顛倒品第二十三 — 觀涅槃品第二十五

Here the opponent finally says the thing that has been building since the first page: if everything is empty, there is no suffering to see, no origin to cut off, no path to walk, no fruit to attain — emptiness has destroyed the Four Noble Truths, the Three Jewels, and morality along with them. Nāgārjuna gives the objection six verses in its own voice, and then reverses it completely: it is the doctrine of fixed natures, not emptiness, that makes a path impossible, because whatever has a nature of its own can never change and never be freed. Because emptiness works, everything works. Chapter 25 then turns the same analysis on nirvāṇa itself, and finds it is not somewhere else.

The Text

Chapter 23 · Examining the Inversions 觀顛倒品第二十三

Having taken apart the person, the Tathāgata, and the workings of karma, Nāgārjuna turns to what drives a person: greed, hatred, and delusion, and the four inversions (顛倒, viparyāsa) that feed them — taking the impermanent for permanent, the painful for pleasant, the selfless for a self, the foul for the beautiful. The opponent's opening move is unusually strong: he grants that the afflictions arise from conditions and thinks this proves they exist. Nāgārjuna answers that it proves the opposite. The chapter then asks a question with no comfortable answer — whose afflictions are these? — and ends, unusually, on a dilemma left standing rather than a conclusion.

The Afflictions Have No Nature and No Owner

Commentary · 青目釋

問曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Question:

Root Verse 1 · 頌

從憶想分別，　生於貪恚癡，

淨不淨顛倒，　皆從眾緣生。

Ru-Yi Translation

From imagining and discriminating

greed, hatred, and delusion are born;

the inversions of pure and impure

all arise from a host of conditions.

Commentary · 青目釋

經說因淨不淨顛倒，憶想分別生貪恚癡。是故當知有貪恚癡。答曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

The scriptures say that on the basis of the inversions of pure and impure, imagining and discriminating give rise to greed, hatred, and delusion. Therefore one should know that greed, hatred, and delusion exist. Answer:

Root Verse 2 · 頌

若因淨不淨，　顛倒生三毒，

三毒即無性，　故煩惱無實。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, on the basis of pure and impure,

inversion gives rise to the three poisons,

then the three poisons have no nature of their own,

and so the afflictions are not real.

若諸煩惱因淨不淨顛倒憶想分別生，即無自性，是故諸煩惱無實。復次：

If the afflictions arise on the basis of the inversions of pure and impure and of imagining and discriminating, then they have no inherent nature (svabhāva); and therefore the afflictions are not real. Furthermore:

Root Verse 3 · 頌

我法有以無，　是事終不成；

無我諸煩惱，　有無亦不成。

Ru-Yi Translation

That there is a self, or that there is not —

this can never be established;

and where there is no self, the afflictions

cannot be established as existent or non-existent either.

我無有因緣若有若無而可成，今無我諸煩惱云何以有無而可成？何以故？

There is no ground on which a self can be established either as existent or as non-existent; and now, with no self, how could the afflictions be established as existent or non-existent? Why?

Root Verse 4 · 頌

誰有此煩惱，　是即為不成；

若離是而有，　煩惱則無屬。

Ru-Yi Translation

Who is it that has these afflictions? —

that cannot be established;

and if they exist apart from anyone,

then the afflictions belong to no one.

煩惱名為能惱他，惱他者應是眾生，是眾生於一切處推求不可得。若謂離眾生但有煩惱，是煩惱則無所屬。若謂雖無我而煩惱屬心，是事亦不然。何以故？

"Affliction" means what is able to afflict someone; the one afflicted would have to be a sentient being, and such a being cannot be found by searching anywhere at all. If you say that apart from sentient beings there are simply afflictions, then those afflictions belong to nothing. And if you say that although there is no self the afflictions belong to the mind, that will not do either. Why?

Root Verse 5 · 頌

如身見五種，　求之不可得，

煩惱於垢心，　五求亦不得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as the view of a self in the body,

searched for in five ways, cannot be found,

so the afflictions in a defiled mind,

searched for in five ways, are not found either.

如身見，五陰中五種求不可得；諸煩惱亦於垢心中，五種求亦不可得。又垢心於煩惱中，五種求亦不可得。復次：

Just as the view of a self in the body, searched for in five ways among the five aggregates, cannot be found, so the afflictions cannot be found by a fivefold search within a defiled mind; and the defiled mind cannot be found by a fivefold search within the afflictions either. Furthermore:

Root Verse 6 · 頌

淨不淨顛倒，　是則無自性；

云何因此二，　而生諸煩惱？

Ru-Yi Translation

The inversions of pure and impure

have no inherent nature of their own;

how then, on the basis of these two,

could the afflictions arise?

淨不淨顛倒者，顛倒名虛妄。若虛妄即無性，無性則無顛倒。若無顛倒，云何因顛倒起諸煩惱？問曰：

As to the inversions of pure and impure: "inversion" names what is false and hollow. If a thing is false it has no nature; and having no nature, there is no inversion. And if there is no inversion, how could the afflictions arise on the basis of inversion? Question:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The opening exchange is worth slowing down for, because the opponent is not being careless. He quotes scripture correctly: anger and craving do not fall out of the sky, they are produced — by taking something as lovely or as loathsome, and then turning that appraisal over in the mind until it hardens. His inference looks obvious. If they are produced, they are there. Nāgārjuna's reply takes the same sentence and reads it the other way: if they are produced, then they are not there in the way you think. A thing assembled out of conditions is a thing with nothing of its own.

Then verse 4 asks the question that gives the chapter its teeth: whose anger is it? Everyone answers "mine" without hesitating, and the answer will not survive being looked at. Search for the one who is angry and you find a body, a set of sensations, a history of appraisals — you never find the possessor standing behind them holding the anger like a tool. Piṅgala presses both horns: cut the owner loose and you have afflictions belonging to nothing, which is incoherent; keep the owner and you must produce him, which cannot be done.

The "five ways" of verse 5 is a compressed reference to the standard Madhyamaka search, used earlier in the treatise on the self and the aggregates: is A the same as B, is A different from B, does A possess B, is A in B, is B in A? Five relations, and the point is that they exhaust the possibilities. Run the search on anger and a mind: the anger is not identical to the mind (the mind persists when the anger goes), not separate from it (there is no free-floating anger), not owned by it, not located in it, and does not contain it. Piṅgala then does something quietly devastating — he runs the search a second time in the other direction. The defiled mind cannot be found inside the affliction either. Neither side of the relation can be pinned down first, which means neither can be used to anchor the other.

Nothing here says that anger does not occur. It says that when you go looking for anger as a possession of a possessor, both the possession and the possessor dissolve under inspection. Anyone who has tried to sit still with a strong emotion and locate it precisely — where is it, what is it made of, who exactly is having it — has already run the experiment. The finding is not that nothing was happening. The finding is that there was less somebody there than the feeling insisted.

The Six Objects, and Pure and Impure

Root Verse 7 · 頌

色聲香味觸，　及法為六種，

如是之六種，　是三毒根本。

Ru-Yi Translation

Form, sound, smell, taste, touch,

and mental objects — these six;

and it is these six

that are the root of the three poisons.

Commentary · 青目釋

是六入，三毒根本，因此六入生淨不淨顛倒，因淨不淨顛倒生貪恚癡。答曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

These six sense-fields are the root of the three poisons: on the basis of these six sense-fields the inversions of pure and impure arise, and on the basis of the inversions of pure and impure, greed, hatred, and delusion arise. Answer:

Root Verses 8–9 · 頌

色聲香味觸，　及法體六種，

皆空如炎夢，　如乾闥婆城。

如是六種中，　何有淨不淨？

猶如幻化人，　亦如鏡中像。

Ru-Yi Translation

Form, sound, smell, taste, touch,

and mental objects — these six in their own substance

are all empty, like a mirage, like a dream,

like the city of the gandharvas.

 

Within these six,

what could be pure or impure?

They are like a conjured man,

like an image in a mirror.

色聲香味觸法自體，未與心和合時空無所有，如炎如夢，如化如鏡中像，但誑惑於心，無有定相。如是六入中，何有淨不淨？復次：

Form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and mental objects, in their own substance, before they have combined with a mind, are empty and without anything at all — like a mirage, like a dream, like a conjuring, like an image in a mirror. They only deceive and confuse the mind; they have no fixed characteristic. Within these six sense-fields, then, what could be pure or impure? Furthermore:

Root Verse 10 · 頌

不因於淨相，　則無有不淨；

因淨有不淨，　是故無不淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Without depending on the mark of the pure

there is nothing impure;

the impure depends upon the pure —

therefore there is no impure.

若不因於淨，先無有不淨，因何而說不淨？是故無不淨。復次：

If it did not depend on the pure, there would be no impure to begin with — on what basis would anything be called impure? Therefore there is no impure. Furthermore:

Root Verse 11 · 頌

不因於不淨，　則亦無有淨；

因不淨有淨，　是故無有淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Without depending on the impure

there is nothing pure either;

the pure depends upon the impure —

therefore there is no pure.

若不因不淨，先無有淨，因何而說淨？是故無有淨。復次：

If it did not depend on the impure, there would be no pure to begin with — on what basis would anything be called pure? Therefore there is no pure. Furthermore:

Root Verse 12 · 頌

若無有淨者，　何由而有貪？

若無有不淨，　何由而有恚？

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is nothing pure,

by what would greed come about?

If there is nothing impure,

by what would hatred come about?

無淨不淨故，則不生貪恚。

問曰：經說常等四顛倒。若無常中見常，是名顛倒；若無常中見無常，此非顛倒。餘三顛倒亦如是。有顛倒故，顛倒者亦應有，何故言都無？

答曰：

Since there is neither pure nor impure, greed and hatred do not arise.

Question: The scriptures speak of the four inversions, permanence and the rest. To see permanence in what is impermanent is called an inversion; to see impermanence in what is impermanent is not an inversion. It is the same with the other three inversions. Since there are inversions, there must also be someone who is inverted — why do you say there is nothing at all?

Answer:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The heart of this passage is verses 10 and 11, and they are best read as a single hinged argument. Nothing is impure except by contrast with something taken as pure; nothing is pure except by contrast with something taken as impure. Neither can be established first, because each is defined by the other. This is not word-play. Take any object you find disgusting and ask what makes it so — the answer always turns out to be a comparison, and the comparison always turns out to require a standard that is itself only a comparison. The pair spins in place with nothing underneath it.

The four images in verse 8 are chosen with care, and they are not interchangeable. A mirage shimmers on the road and there is water there for as long as you are far away; the appearance is perfectly reliable and there is nothing to drink. A dream is fully convincing from inside and has no location at all. The city of the gandharvas is the cloud-castle that Indian literature puts on the horizon at dusk — an architecture with no builder and no bricks. An image in a mirror is the sharpest of the four, because it is genuinely there, it is not a hallucination, you can use it to shave; and yet nothing whatever is behind the glass. Objects are like that. Not absent. Not findable where you are looking.

Piṅgala adds a phrase that no summary should skip: these objects, before they have combined with a mind, are without anything at all. This is not idealism — he is not saying the mind manufactures the world. He is saying that "lovely" and "revolting" are not sitting in the object waiting to be discovered. They arise where an object and a mind meet, and both parties supply half. Which is why two people can look at the same face, the same food, the same body, and one is drawn and one repelled, and neither is making a factual error.

Here is where an English-speaking reader should be careful, because the next step is tempting and wrong. "So nothing is really disgusting, and nothing is really beautiful — therefore it does not matter what I do." That does not follow, and Nāgārjuna will spend the whole of the next chapter demolishing exactly that inference. What has been dismantled is a metaphysical claim: that attractiveness and repulsiveness are intrinsic properties of things, so that craving and revulsion are simply accurate perception. Once that claim goes, craving and revulsion lose their alibi. They stop being reports about the world and become recognizable as something the mind is doing — which is precisely the condition under which they can be worked with rather than merely obeyed.

Who Is Inverted?

Root Verse 13 · 頌

於無常著常，　是則名顛倒；

空中無有常，　何處有常倒？

Ru-Yi Translation

To cling to permanence in the impermanent

is what is called an inversion;

but in emptiness there is no permanence —

where then is the inversion of permanence?

Commentary · 青目釋

若於無常中著常，名為顛倒。諸法性空中無有常，是中何處有常顛倒？餘三亦如是。復次：

Ru-Yi Translation

To cling to permanence within the impermanent is called an inversion. But within the natural emptiness of things there is no permanence at all; where in that is there any inversion of permanence? It is the same with the other three. Furthermore:

Root Verse 14 · 頌

若於無常中，　著無常非倒；

空中無無常，　何有非顛倒？

Ru-Yi Translation

If clinging to impermanence

within the impermanent is no inversion —

in emptiness there is no impermanence either,

so what could be free of inversion?

若著無常言是無常，不名為顛倒者，諸法性空中無無常，無常無故，誰為非顛倒？餘三亦如是。復次：

If clinging to the impermanent and calling it impermanent is not to be called an inversion — still, within the natural emptiness of things there is no impermanence; and since there is no impermanence, who is it that is free of inversion? It is the same with the other three. Furthermore:

Root Verse 15 · 頌

可著著者著，　及所用著法，

是皆寂滅相，　云何而有著？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is clung to, the one who clings, the clinging,

and the means by which one clings —

all of these bear the mark of quiescence;

how then could there be any clinging?

可著名物，著者名作者，著名業，所用法名所用事，是皆性空寂滅相。如〈如來品〉中所說，是故無有著。復次：

"What is clung to" means the object; "the one who clings" means the agent; "clinging" means the act; "the means by which one clings" means the instrument employed. All of these are empty by nature and bear the mark of quiescence, as was said in the chapter on the Tathāgata. Therefore there is no clinging. Furthermore:

Root Verse 16 · 頌

若無有著法，　言邪是顛倒，

言正不顛倒，　誰有如是事？

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is no such thing as clinging,

then calling this wrong and an inversion,

calling that right and no inversion —

who has anything of the sort?

著名憶想分別此彼有無等。若無此著者，誰為邪顛倒？誰為正不顛倒？復次：

"Clinging" means imagining and discriminating — this and that, existent and non-existent, and the rest. If there is no such clinging, who is it that is wrong and inverted? Who is it that is right and free of inversion? Furthermore:

Root Verses 17–18 · 頌

有倒不生倒，　無倒不生倒，

倒者不生倒，　不倒亦不生。

若於顛倒時，　亦不生顛倒，

汝可自觀察，　誰生於顛倒？

Ru-Yi Translation

Where inversion is, inversion is not produced;

where inversion is not, inversion is not produced;

the inverted does not produce inversion,

nor does the uninverted produce it.

 

Nor, at the moment of inverting,

is inversion produced.

Examine the matter for yourself:

who is it that produces inversion?

已顛倒者，則更不生顛倒，已顛倒故。不顛倒者亦不顛倒，無有顛倒故。顛倒時亦不顛倒，有二過故。汝今除憍慢心，善自觀察，誰為顛倒者？復次：

One who is already inverted does not further produce inversion, being already inverted. One who is not inverted is not inverted either, since there is no inversion there. And there is no inverting at the moment of inverting, since that carries both faults at once. Set your arrogance aside now and examine the matter well for yourself: who is it that is inverted? Furthermore:

Root Verse 19 · 頌

諸顛倒不生，　云何有此義？

無有顛倒故，　何有顛倒者？

Ru-Yi Translation

The inversions are unproduced —

but how can that be made into a doctrine?

And since there is no inversion,

how could there be one who is inverted?

顛倒種種因緣破故，墮在不生。彼貪著不生，謂不生是顛倒實相。是故偈說，云何名不生為顛倒？乃至無漏法尚不名為不生相，何況顛倒是不生相。顛倒無故，何有顛倒者？因顛倒有顛倒者。復次：

Since inversion has been broken by argument after argument, it falls into non-arising. But then the opponent craves and clings to that non-arising, saying that non-arising is the true character of inversion. And so the verse asks: how can non-arising be called inversion? Even things free of outflows are not said to have the mark of non-arising — how much less could inversion be the mark of non-arising. And since there is no inversion, how could there be one who is inverted? For it is on the basis of inversion that there is anyone inverted. Furthermore:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Verses 13 and 14 are one of the most elegant traps in the treatise, and the second half is the half that matters. Everyone expects the first move: if there is no permanence anywhere, then "seeing permanence in the impermanent" has no target, and the classic definition of inversion has nothing to bite on. Fine — the reader assumes the correct view survives. Then verse 14 removes it. If things are empty, there is no impermanence standing there as a findable property either, so "seeing impermanence in the impermanent" has no target and there is no such thing as the uninverted view. Both the mistake and its correction lose their object at the same moment.

This should be read as a warning about a very ordinary spiritual failure mode. Impermanence begins as medicine and turns into a possession: I see impermanence, unlike those people who do not. At that point "impermanent" has become a property one imagines oneself to be perceiving correctly in things that really have it — which is the same grasping, wearing better clothes. Verse 14 takes the clothes off. Impermanence is not a thing found in things; it is the failure of things to be the fixed items we took them for.

Verse 15 then dismantles the grammar of the whole enterprise. Clinging looks like a transaction with four parts — an object clung to, someone clinging, the act, and the means. Piṅgala labels them exactly as one would label a sentence: object, agent, act, instrument. And every one of the four has already been shown, chapter by chapter, to be unfindable on its own. So the sentence has no nouns left in it. What we call clinging is not a thing an agent does to an object; it is a pattern we describe afterward in a grammar that requires actors.

Then verse 19, which is where the chapter turns on its own reader. Having heard that inversion is unproduced, the opponent immediately takes that up as his new position — non-arising is what inversion really is. Piṅgala catches him in the act: "he craves and clings to that non-arising." This is the treatise's recurring alarm, and it will sound again in Chapter 24 when Nāgārjuna says emptiness is itself empty. Every conclusion here is a solvent, not a deposit. The moment "unproduced" becomes something you have, you are holding a view again, and the view will do what views do.

What Could Possibly Be Cut Off?

Root Verse 20 · 頌

若常我樂淨，　而是實有者，

是常我樂淨，　則非是顛倒。

Ru-Yi Translation

If permanence, self, pleasure, and purity

were really existent,

then permanence, self, pleasure, and purity

would not be inversions at all.

Commentary · 青目釋

若常我樂淨是四實有性者，是常我樂淨則非顛倒。何以故？定有實事故。云何言顛倒？若謂常我樂淨倒是四無者，無常苦無我不淨是四應實有，不名顛倒。顛倒相違故名不顛倒。是事不然。何以故？

Ru-Yi Translation

If these four — permanence, self, pleasure, and purity — really had existent natures, then permanence, self, pleasure, and purity would not be inversions. Why? Because there would then be a fixed and real thing there. How could that be called an inversion? And if you say that the four inversions of permanence, self, pleasure, and purity do not exist, then the four — impermanence, suffering, no-self, and impurity — must really exist and would not be called inversions, being called uninverted precisely because they are the opposite of the inversions. But this will not do. Why?

Root Verse 21 · 頌

若常我樂淨，　而實無有者；

無常苦不淨，　是則亦應無。

Ru-Yi Translation

If permanence, self, pleasure, and purity

really do not exist,

then impermanence, suffering, and impurity

must equally not exist.

若常我樂淨是四實無，無故無常等四事亦不應有。何以故？無相因待故。復次：

If these four — permanence, self, pleasure, and purity — really do not exist, then since they do not, the four beginning with impermanence cannot exist either. Why? Because there is then nothing left for them to stand in dependence upon. Furthermore:

Root Verse 22 · 頌

如是顛倒滅，　無明則亦滅，

以無明滅故，　諸行等亦滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

When the inversions cease in this way,

ignorance ceases as well;

and because ignorance ceases,

the formations and the rest cease too.

如是者，如其義。滅諸顛倒故，十二因緣根本無明亦滅，無明滅故三種行業乃至老死等皆滅。復次：

"In this way" means: in accordance with the meaning just given. Because the inversions cease, ignorance — the root of the twelve links — ceases as well; and because ignorance ceases, the three kinds of formative action, and so on down to old age and death, all cease. Furthermore:

Root Verse 23 · 頌

若煩惱性實，　而有所屬者，

云何當可斷？　誰能斷其性？

Ru-Yi Translation

If the afflictions had a real nature

and belonged to someone,

how could they ever be cut off?

Who could cut off a nature?

若諸煩惱即是顛倒而實有性者，云何可斷？誰能斷其性？若謂諸煩惱皆虛妄無性而可斷者，是亦不然。何以故？

If the afflictions just are the inversions and really have a nature, how could they be cut off? Who could cut off their nature? And if you say that the afflictions are all false and without nature and can be cut off for that reason, that will not do either. Why?

Root Verse 24 · 頌

若煩惱虛妄，　無性無屬者，

云何當可斷？　誰能斷無性？

Ru-Yi Translation

If the afflictions are false,

without nature and belonging to no one,

how could they ever be cut off?

Who could cut off what has no nature?

若諸煩惱虛妄無性，則無所屬，云何可斷？誰能斷無性法？

If the afflictions are false and without nature, then they belong to nothing — so how could they be cut off? Who could cut off a thing that has no nature?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice how the chapter ends. It does not end. The last two verses set out a dilemma with both horns intact and then simply stop: if the afflictions are real, nothing can remove them, because a nature is not the sort of thing that can be taken away; if the afflictions are unreal, there is nothing there to remove. Piṅgala does not resolve it. He restates the second horn and the chapter closes on a question mark.

This is deliberate, and it is the doorway into what follows. The dilemma is genuinely unanswerable as posed — and it is posed inside the opponent's framework, in which a thing either has a fixed nature or is nothing whatever. Both branches of that fork lead to a world with no possible path: the first because nothing could ever change, the second because there would be nothing to change. Chapter 24 will not pick a horn. It will reject the fork.

Verse 22 deserves separate notice, because it is the one constructive sentence in a chapter of demolitions, and it is placed exactly where the twelve links begin. Ignorance is the first link, and this verse says what ignorance actually consists of: not a lack of information, but the four inversions — the settled habit of taking what passes as lasting, what wounds as satisfying, what is assembled as a someone, what is merely a body as a treasure. Stop the inversions and the first link goes; and because it goes, the formative actions built on it go, and so on down the whole chain to old age and death. The chain is not cut by force in the middle. It is cut by no longer being mistaken at the start.

Chapter 24 · Examining the Four Noble Truths 觀四諦品第二十四

This is the chapter the treatise exists for. Twenty-three chapters of demolition have raised one objection above all others, and here Nāgārjuna finally lets it be spoken at full strength — six verses in the opponent's own voice, unhurried and undiluted: emptiness abolishes the Four Noble Truths, and with them the fruits, the community, the Dharma, the Buddha, and the difference between a good act and an evil one. The reply does not soften the charge or negotiate with it. It hands the charge back. What follows contains the two truths, the most famous verse in Buddhist philosophy, and the sentence that everything else in Madhyamaka is a footnote to: 以有空義故，一切法得成 — because emptiness works, everything works.

The Objection: Emptiness Destroys Everything

Commentary · 青目釋

問曰：破四顛倒，通達四諦，得四沙門果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: By breaking the four inversions one penetrates the Four Noble Truths and attains the four fruits of the renunciant.

Root Verses 1–5 · 頌

若一切皆空，　無生亦無滅，

如是則無有，　四聖諦之法。

以無四諦故，　見苦與斷集，

證滅及修道，　如是事皆無。

以是事無故，　則無四道果；

無有四果故，　得向者亦無。

若無八賢聖，　則無有僧寶；

以無四諦故，　亦無有法寶；

以無法僧寶，　亦無有佛寶。

如是說空者，　是則破三寶。

Ru-Yi Translation

If everything is empty,

with neither arising nor ceasing,

then it follows that there is no such thing

as the Dharma of the Four Noble Truths.

 

And because there are no Four Truths,

seeing suffering and cutting off its origin,

realizing cessation and cultivating the path —

none of these things exist.

 

And because these do not exist,

there are no four fruits of the path;

and because there are no four fruits,

there is no one who attains them or approaches them.

 

If there are no eight noble persons,

there is no Jewel of the Saṅgha;

and because there are no Four Truths,

there is no Jewel of the Dharma;

 

and with no Jewel of the Dharma or the Saṅgha,

there is no Jewel of the Buddha either.

To speak of emptiness in this way

is to destroy the Three Jewels.

Commentary · 青目釋

若一切世間皆空無所有者，即應無生無滅。以無生無滅故，則無四聖諦。何以故？從集諦生苦諦，集諦是因、苦諦是果。滅苦集諦名為滅諦，能至滅諦名為道諦。道諦是因、滅諦是果。如是四諦有因有果，若無生無滅則無四諦。四諦無故，則無見苦、斷集、證滅、修道。見苦斷集證滅修道無故，則無四沙門果。四沙門果無故，則無四向四得者。若無此八賢聖，則無僧寶。又四聖諦無故，法寶亦無。若無法寶、僧寶者，云何有佛？得法名為佛，無法何有佛？汝說諸法皆空，則壞三寶。復次：

Ru-Yi Translation

If all the world were empty and without anything at all, then there would be neither arising nor ceasing; and since there is neither arising nor ceasing, there are no Four Noble Truths. Why? The truth of suffering arises out of the truth of origination — origination being the cause and suffering the fruit. The extinguishing of suffering and its origination is called the truth of cessation, and what is able to reach cessation is called the truth of the path — the path being the cause and cessation the fruit. In this way the Four Truths have causes and they have fruits; and if there is no arising and no ceasing, there are no Four Truths. Since there are no Four Truths, there is no seeing of suffering, no cutting off of origination, no realizing of cessation, no cultivating of the path. And since there is none of that, there are no four fruits of the renunciant. And since there are no four fruits, there is no one who has attained a fruit and no one approaching one. And if there are none of these eight noble persons, there is no Jewel of the Saṅgha. Further, since there are no Four Noble Truths, the Jewel of the Dharma is gone as well. And if there is no Jewel of the Dharma and no Jewel of the Saṅgha, how could there be a Buddha? It is by attaining the Dharma that one is called a Buddha; with no Dharma, how could there be a Buddha? You say that all things are empty — then you destroy the Three Jewels. Furthermore:

Root Verse 6 · 頌

空法壞因果，　亦壞於罪福，

亦復悉毀壞，　一切世俗法。

Ru-Yi Translation

The doctrine of emptiness destroys cause and fruit,

it destroys wrongdoing and merit,

and it demolishes utterly

every convention of the world.

若受空法者，則破罪福及罪福果報，亦破世俗法。有如是等諸過故，諸法不應空。

答曰：

Whoever accepts the doctrine of emptiness breaks wrongdoing and merit and the ripening of wrongdoing and merit, and breaks the conventions of the world as well. Because of faults such as these, things ought not to be empty.

Answer:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Read the objection slowly and give it everything it deserves, because Nāgārjuna does. Six verses is a great deal of space to hand an opponent, and the chain of reasoning is not clumsy at any link. Emptiness means no arising and no ceasing. But the Four Truths are through and through a matter of arising and ceasing — craving arises and suffering follows, the path is walked and suffering stops. Take away arising and ceasing and the Four Truths have nothing to describe. And the Four Truths are load-bearing: the fruits of practice are defined as stages of seeing them, the Saṅgha is defined as the people at those stages, the Dharma is the teaching of them, and the Buddha is whoever has attained that Dharma. Pull the first stone and the whole building comes down, Buddha included.

Then verse 6 adds the charge that is not academic at all. If everything is empty, wrongdoing and merit are empty, and if wrongdoing and merit are empty then cruelty and kindness ripen into nothing in particular and it makes no difference which you choose. This is the accusation of nihilism, stated plainly, and it has followed Madhyamaka for eighteen centuries. It is worth admitting that it is a reasonable thing to worry about. Anyone who has watched "it's all empty anyway" used to excuse something has watched this objection come true in a particular life.

Two things about the shape of the reply are worth knowing before it starts. First, Nāgārjuna does not answer by weakening emptiness — there is no retreat here to "well, things are empty in a manner of speaking." Second, he does not answer by defending himself at all. He turns the argument around and runs it against the opponent's own position, and the reversal is total: every consequence the opponent has just listed does in fact follow — from fixed natures. If suffering had a nature of its own it could not arise, could not cease, and could not be seen through; the path could not be walked, because walking a path means becoming something you were not. The objector has been describing his own house on fire and pointing at his neighbor.

The Two Truths

Root Verse 7 · 頌

汝今實不能，　知空空因緣，

及知於空義，　是故自生惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

You truly do not know

what emptiness is, or why it is taught,

or what emptiness means —

and so you torment yourself.

Commentary · 青目釋

汝不解云何是空相？以何因緣說空？亦不解空義。不能如實知故，生如是疑難。復次：

Ru-Yi Translation

You do not understand what the mark of emptiness is, or for what reason emptiness is taught, nor do you understand the meaning of emptiness. Because you cannot know it as it is, you raise objections of this kind. Furthermore:

Root Verses 8–9 · 頌

諸佛依二諦，　為眾生說法：

一以世俗諦，　二第一義諦。

若人不能知，　分別於二諦，

則於深佛法，　不知真實義。

Ru-Yi Translation

The buddhas rely on two truths

in teaching the Dharma to sentient beings:

the first is conventional truth,

the second is the truth of the ultimate.

 

Whoever is unable to know

how to distinguish these two truths

does not know the real meaning

of the deep teaching of the Buddha.

世俗諦者，一切法性空，而世間顛倒，故生虛妄法，於世間是實。諸賢聖真知顛倒性，故知一切法皆空無生，於聖人是第一義諦名為實。諸佛依是二諦，而為眾生說法。若人不能如實分別二諦，則於甚深佛法不知實義。若謂一切法不生是第一義諦，不須第二俗諦者，是亦不然。何以故？

As to conventional truth: all things are empty by nature, but the world is inverted and so brings forth false things, which for the world are real. The noble ones truly know the nature of that inversion, and so know that all things are empty and unarisen; for the noble ones this is the ultimate truth and is what is real. The buddhas rely on these two truths in teaching the Dharma to sentient beings. If someone cannot distinguish the two truths as they are, he will not know the real meaning of the profound Buddha-dharma. And if you say that "all things do not arise" is the ultimate truth and that the second, conventional truth is unnecessary, that will not do either. Why?

Root Verse 10 · 頌

若不依俗諦，　不得第一義；

不得第一義，　則不得涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Without relying on conventional truth

the ultimate is not attained;

and without attaining the ultimate

nirvāṇa is not attained.

第一義皆因言說，言說是世俗，是故若不依世俗，第一義則不可說。若不得第一義，云何得至涅槃？是故諸法雖無生，而有二諦。復次：

The ultimate is in every case conveyed by means of speech, and speech is conventional; therefore, if one does not rely on the conventional, the ultimate cannot be spoken at all. And if the ultimate is not attained, how could one arrive at nirvāṇa? Therefore, although things do not arise, there are still two truths. Furthermore:

Root Verse 11 · 頌

不能正觀空，　鈍根則自害，

如不善呪術，　不善捉毒蛇。

Ru-Yi Translation

One of dull faculties, unable

to contemplate emptiness rightly, harms himself,

like a spell badly cast,

like a poisonous snake badly caught.

若人鈍根不善解空法，於空有失而生邪見。如為利捉毒蛇，不能善捉反為所害。又如呪術，欲有所作不能善成則還自害。鈍根觀空法亦如是。復次：

If a person of dull faculties does not understand the teaching of emptiness well, he goes wrong about emptiness and gives rise to wrong views. It is like catching a poisonous snake for profit: not catching it well, one is harmed by it instead. Or like a spell: wanting to accomplish something, one fails to work it properly, and the harm comes back on oneself. So it is when a dull person contemplates the teaching of emptiness. Furthermore:

Root Verse 12 · 頌

世尊知是法，　甚深微妙相，

非鈍根所及，　是故不欲說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The World-Honored One, knowing this Dharma

to bear a mark deep and subtle,

beyond the reach of dull faculties,

was for that reason unwilling to teach it.

世尊以法甚深微妙，非鈍根所解，是故不欲說。復次：

Because the Dharma is deep and subtle and not to be understood by dull faculties, the World-Honored One was unwilling to teach it. Furthermore:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Verse 7 is one of the great put-downs in philosophy, and it is also a precise diagnosis. It names three distinct things the objector has failed to grasp, and they are not the same failure: he does not know what emptiness is (its mark), he does not know why it is taught (its purpose), and he does not know what the word means (its sense). Most of the trouble people have with this doctrine can be sorted into those three bins. And then the closing line, which is the humane part: 是故自生惱 — and so you torment yourself. The objector is not being told he is stupid. He is being told that his distress is real, is his own manufacture, and is unnecessary.

The two truths follow, and they are the instrument that dissolves the objection. Conventional truth (世俗諦) is not a polite fiction or a lower grade of falsehood. It is how things function — cause and effect, wrongdoing and merit, teacher and student, illness and medicine — and Piṅgala says of it exactly the right thing: for the world it is real. Ultimate truth (第一義諦) is that none of it has a nature of its own. The objector's mistake, and it is the standard one, is to hear the second and assume it cancels the first. It does not, any more than knowing a rainbow has no substance stops it from being visible from here and not from there.

Verse 10 is where this stops being a tidy scheme and becomes something stranger and more interesting. The ultimate cannot be reached except through the conventional — and Piṅgala's reason is disarmingly concrete: the ultimate has to be said, and all saying is conventional. There is no direct channel. Every sūtra, every argument on this page, every word for emptiness is conventional through and through, and there is no other vehicle available. So the two truths are not two floors of a building with the wise living upstairs. The upper truth is only ever accessible by way of the lower, which means the conventional is not something the practitioner graduates out of. This is also the answer to anyone who imagines that realizing emptiness means having less to do with ordinary life.

Then the snake. It is the most vivid image in the chapter and it should be read as a genuine warning rather than a rhetorical flourish. Snake-catchers in India caught venomous snakes on purpose — for venom, for display, for a living. The snake is not the problem; the grip is. Grasp it behind the head and you have your livelihood. Grasp it in the middle and it turns and kills you. Emptiness is like that: correctly held it releases everything, incorrectly held it is the most dangerous idea in Buddhism, because it can be used to dissolve every restraint a person has while feeling like wisdom the whole time. Verse 12 then says something remarkable — that this danger is why the Buddha hesitated to teach at all. The famous reluctance under the tree is here given a specific content. He was not worried that no one would listen. He was worried about what would happen to those who listened badly.

Emptiness Is Itself Empty

Root Verse 13 · 頌

汝謂我著空，　而為我生過；

汝今所說過，　於空則無有。

Ru-Yi Translation

You say that I cling to emptiness

and on that ground charge me with a fault;

but the fault you have just stated

does not apply to emptiness.

Commentary · 青目釋

汝謂我著空故，為我生過。我所說性空，空亦復空，無如是過。復次：

Ru-Yi Translation

You say that because I cling to emptiness you can charge me with a fault. But the emptiness of nature that I teach is itself empty as well; there is no such fault. Furthermore:

Root Verse 14 · 頌

以有空義故，　一切法得成；

若無空義者，　一切則不成。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because emptiness holds,

everything can be established;

if emptiness did not hold,

nothing could be established at all.

以有空義故，一切世間、出世間法皆悉成就。若無空義，則皆不成就。復次：

Because emptiness holds, everything worldly and everything beyond the world is brought to completion. If emptiness did not hold, none of it would be completed. Furthermore:

Root Verse 15 · 頌

汝今自有過，　而以迴向我，

如人乘馬者，　自忘於所乘。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fault is your own,

and you turn it back upon me,

like a man riding a horse

who has forgotten what he rides.

汝於有法中有過不能自覺，而於空中見過，如人乘馬而忘其所乘。何以故？

You have a fault within your own doctrine of existent things and cannot notice it, while you see a fault in emptiness — like a man on horseback who has forgotten what he is riding. Why?

Root Verse 16 · 頌

若汝見諸法，　決定有性者，

即為見諸法，　無因亦無緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

If you see things

as definitely having a nature,

then you are seeing things

as having no cause and no condition.

汝說諸法有定性。若爾者，則見諸法無因無緣。何以故？若法決定有性，則應不生不滅，如是法何用因緣？若諸法從因緣生，則無有性。是故諸法決定有性，則無因緣。若謂諸法決定住自性，是則不然。何以故？

You say that things have a fixed nature. If so, you are seeing things as having no cause and no condition. Why? If a thing definitely has a nature, it should neither arise nor cease — and what use would causes and conditions be to such a thing? Whereas if things arise from causes and conditions, they have no nature. Therefore, if things definitely have a nature, there are no causes and conditions at all. And if you say that things definitely abide in a nature of their own, that will not do. Why?

Root Verse 17 · 頌

即為破因果，　作作者作法，

亦復壞一切，　萬物之生滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then you break cause and fruit,

the act, the agent, and the doing,

and you demolish as well

the arising and ceasing of the ten thousand things.

諸法有定性，則無因果等諸事。如偈說：

If things had a fixed nature there would be no cause and fruit and none of the rest. As the verse says:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Verse 13 answers the charge of nihilism in a single stroke, and the stroke is easy to miss because it is so short. The objector's complaint has a hidden premise: that Nāgārjuna holds emptiness as a doctrine — a description of how things ultimately are, in place of the opponent's description. If that were so, the complaint would land. Piṅgala's reply removes the premise: 空亦復空, emptiness too is empty. Emptiness is not the true nature of things replacing their false natures. It is the absence of any such nature, including its own. A medicine that flushed out every impurity and then stayed in the body would be a poison; the point of this one is that it goes out with what it removes.

Then verse 14, which is the load-bearing sentence of Madhyamaka and possibly of Mahāyāna philosophy: because emptiness holds, everything can be established. The word order in the Chinese matters — emptiness is not being defended as harmless, it is being made the condition of everything else. This inverts the objection completely. The opponent thinks emptiness is the solvent that dissolves the path; Nāgārjuna says emptiness is what makes a path possible at all. Only what lacks a fixed nature can change; only what can change can be cultivated; only what can be cultivated can be liberated. A person with a fixed nature could not be improved by anything. A suffering with a fixed nature could not be relieved by anything.

Verse 15's image is worth savoring for a moment before moving on. A man rides a horse from one town to the next and spends the journey wondering aloud how anyone gets anywhere. He is not lying and he is not stupid; he has simply stopped noticing what is carrying him. The opponent uses dependent arising every hour of his life — he practices, he expects results, he believes his teacher's instruction can change him — and dependent arising means emptiness. He is arguing against the animal underneath him.

Verses 16 and 17 then make the reversal explicit, and it is important to see that the argument is not rhetorical. It is a straight entailment, and it runs in both directions. If a thing has a nature of its own, it does not depend on causes — that is what "of its own" means. Therefore anyone who asserts fixed natures has, without intending to, asserted a world with no causation: no seed becoming sprout, no act ripening into consequence, no agent doing anything, nothing arising and nothing ceasing. The accusation the opponent brought in verse 6 — that emptiness wrecks cause and fruit, merit and wrongdoing, and all the world's conventions — has just been returned to sender, word for word.

Dependent Arising, Emptiness, Designation, the Middle Way

Root Verses 18–19 · 頌

眾因緣生法，　我說即是無，

亦為是假名，　亦是中道義。

未曾有一法，　不從因緣生，

是故一切法，　無不是空者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever arises from a host of causes and conditions,

that, I declare, is without being;

it is also a conventional designation,

and it is also the meaning of the middle way.

 

There has never been a single thing

that did not arise from causes and conditions;

therefore there is nothing whatever

that is not empty.

Commentary · 青目釋

眾因緣生法，我說即是空。何以故？眾緣具足和合而物生，是物屬眾因緣故無自性，無自性故空，空亦復空。但為引導眾生故，以假名說，離有無二邊，故名為中道。是法無性故不得言有，亦無空故不得言無。若法有性相，則不待眾緣而有，若不待眾緣則無法，是故無有不空法。汝上所說空法有過者，此過今還在汝。何以故？

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever arises from a host of causes and conditions, I declare to be empty. Why? Because a thing arises when the host of conditions is complete and has come together; and because that thing belongs to the host of causes and conditions, it has no inherent nature; and because it has no inherent nature, it is empty — and emptiness too is empty. But in order to guide sentient beings it is spoken of by way of a conventional designation; and because this leaves behind both extremes, existence and non-existence, it is called the middle way. Since this thing has no nature, one cannot call it existent; and since there is no emptiness either, one cannot call it non-existent. If a thing had a nature and a mark of its own, it would exist without waiting upon a host of conditions; and if it did not wait upon a host of conditions, there would be no thing at all. Therefore there is nothing that is not empty. And the fault you stated above — that the doctrine of emptiness is at fault — that fault now returns to you. Why?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the verse. If a reader comes to the Middle Treatise having heard one thing about it, this is usually the thing, and it is the passage East Asian Buddhism has quoted, argued over, and built entire schools upon for fifteen hundred years — the Tiantai tradition's threefold contemplation of emptiness, provisional designation, and the middle takes its architecture directly from these twenty characters. What it does is deceptively simple. It names four things and says they are one thing: dependent arising, emptiness, conventional designation, and the middle way. Not four doctrines that agree. Four names for a single state of affairs, seen from four sides.

Take them in the order the verse gives them. Something arises because conditions came together — that is dependent arising, and it is a plain observation about how anything gets here. Because it arose that way, it has nothing of its own: strip away the conditions and there is no residue, no core that was going to be that thing regardless. That is emptiness, and note that it is not an additional fact about the thing. It is the same fact, restated. Third: nevertheless the thing has a name, and the name works — you can call for the chariot and it comes, you can point at the fire and be warned. That is 假名, conventional designation, borrowed naming, and Piṅgala says exactly why it is used: in order to guide sentient beings. Names are not errors; they are how anything gets taught. Fourth: to hold all three together — really there, and empty; empty, and functioning — is to stand where neither extreme can be occupied. That is the middle way, and it is not a compromise between existence and non-existence. It is the position that neither of those words was ever describing what is in front of us.

A textual note that honest reading requires. Kumārajīva's transmitted text reads 我說即是無 — "I declare to be without being" — where the manuscript tradition also records the reading 空, "empty," and where Piṅgala, commenting one line later, writes 我說即是空 without hesitation. The two readings are one thought, and the commentary settles the sense: the "not" here is the absence of inherent nature, never the absence of the thing. That is why Piṅgala immediately adds the sentence that the whole chapter turns on — since this thing has no nature, one cannot call it existent; and since there is no emptiness either, one cannot call it non-existent. Both words are refused in the same breath. Anyone who leaves this verse holding "so nothing exists" has taken the first half and dropped the second.

Then the argument compressed into the last line of the commentary, which is the shortest complete statement of Madhyamaka available anywhere: if a thing had a nature of its own, it would exist without waiting upon conditions; and if it did not wait upon conditions, there would be no thing at all. Follow it slowly. Having a nature of one's own means not needing anything else in order to be what one is. But nothing whatever is like that — nothing arrives without conditions, nothing persists without support, nothing means anything in isolation. So the only things that could be non-empty are things that do not exist. Emptiness is not opposed to existence. Emptiness is the fine print on it.

And the practical upshot, which is why this verse matters to someone who has no interest in Indian logic. The two moves people make when they hear "empty" are to reach for permanence (surely something is really there underneath) or to reach for nothing (so none of it is real, and none of it counts). Verse 18 blocks both by refusing to let the four terms come apart. If you find yourself with emptiness but no conventional designation, you have nihilism and you have lost the world. If you find yourself with designation but no emptiness, you have the ordinary condition and you have lost the path. Hold all four at once and there is nothing left to hold — which was the point, and is what the fourth name means.

The Reversal: A Fixed Nature Destroys the Four Truths

Root Verse 20 · 頌

若一切不空，　則無有生滅，

如是則無有，　四聖諦之法。

Ru-Yi Translation

If everything were not empty,

there would be no arising and no ceasing;

and then there would be no such thing

as the Dharma of the Four Noble Truths.

Commentary · 青目釋

若一切法各各有性不空者，則無有生滅。無生滅故，則無四聖諦法。何以故？

Ru-Yi Translation

If each and every thing had a nature of its own and were not empty, there would be no arising and no ceasing. And since there would be no arising and ceasing, there would be no Dharma of the Four Noble Truths. Why?

Root Verse 21 · 頌

苦不從緣生，　云何當有苦？

無常是苦義，　定性無無常。

Ru-Yi Translation

If suffering did not arise from conditions,

how could there be any suffering?

Impermanence is the meaning of suffering,

and in a fixed nature there is no impermanence.

苦不從緣生故則無苦。何以故？經說無常是苦義。若苦有定性，云何有無常？以不捨自性故。復次：

If suffering did not arise from conditions there would be no suffering. Why? The scriptures say that impermanence is the meaning of suffering. If suffering had a fixed nature, how could there be impermanence in it — since it would never let go of its own nature? Furthermore:

Root Verse 22 · 頌

若苦有定性，　何故從集生？

是故無有集，　以破空義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If suffering had a fixed nature,

why would it arise from origination?

Therefore there is no origination,

since you have broken the meaning of emptiness.

若苦有定性者，則不應更生，先已有故。若爾者，則無集諦，以壞空義故。復次：

If suffering had a fixed nature it should not arise again, since it was already there beforehand. And if so, there is no truth of origination — because you have destroyed the meaning of emptiness. Furthermore:

Root Verse 23 · 頌

苦若有定性，　則不應有滅。

汝著定性故，　即破於滅諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

If suffering had a fixed nature,

there could be no cessation of it;

and because you cling to a fixed nature,

you break the truth of cessation.

苦若有定性者，則不應滅。何以故？性則無滅故。復次：

If suffering had a fixed nature it should not cease. Why? Because a nature does not cease. Furthermore:

Root Verse 24 · 頌

苦若有定性，　則無有修道；

若道可修習，　即無有定性。

Ru-Yi Translation

If suffering had a fixed nature,

there would be no cultivating of a path;

and if the path can be cultivated,

then there is no fixed nature.

法若定有，則無有修道。何以故？若法實者則是常，常則不可增益。若道可修，道則無有定性。復次：

If a thing definitely exists, there is no cultivating of a path. Why? If a thing is real it is permanent, and what is permanent cannot be added to. And if the path can be cultivated, then the path has no fixed nature. Furthermore:

Root Verse 25 · 頌

若無有苦諦，　及無集滅諦，

所可滅苦道，　竟為何所至？

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is no truth of suffering,

and no truth of origination or cessation,

then the path that extinguishes suffering —

where in the end does it arrive?

諸法若先定有性，則無苦集滅諦。今滅苦道，竟為至何滅苦處？復次：

If things had a fixed nature from the start, there would be no truths of suffering, origination, or cessation. Then the path that extinguishes suffering — at what place of extinguished suffering does it finally arrive? Furthermore:

Root Verse 26 · 頌

若苦定有性，　先來所不見，

於今云何見？　其性不異故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If suffering definitely had a nature,

then what was not seen before,

how could it be seen now?

For its nature does not alter.

若先凡夫時不能見苦性，今亦不應見。何以故？不見性定故。復次：

If, back when one was an ordinary person, one could not see the nature of suffering, then one should not see it now either. Why? Because the not-seeing would be fixed by nature. Furthermore:

Root Verse 27 · 頌

如見苦不然，　斷集及證滅，

修道及四果，　是亦皆不然。

Ru-Yi Translation

As with seeing suffering, which will not hold,

so cutting off origination and realizing cessation,

cultivating the path and the four fruits —

none of these will hold either.

如苦諦性，先不見者後亦不應見。如是亦不應有斷集、證滅、修道。何以故？是集性先來不斷，今亦不應斷，性不可斷故。滅先來不證，今亦不應證，先來不證故。道先來不修，今亦不應修，先來不修故。是故四聖諦見斷證修四種行，皆不應有。四種行無故，四道果亦無。何以故？

As with the nature of the truth of suffering — what was not seen before should not be seen afterward either. In the same way there should be no cutting off of origination, no realizing of cessation, no cultivating of the path. Why? The nature of origination was not cut off before, and should not be cut off now, since a nature cannot be cut off. Cessation was not realized before, and should not be realized now, since it was not realized before. The path was not cultivated before, and should not be cultivated now, since it was not cultivated before. Therefore the four activities of the Four Noble Truths — seeing, cutting off, realizing, and cultivating — should none of them exist. And since these four activities do not exist, the four fruits of the path do not exist either. Why?

Root Verse 28 · 頌

是四道果性，　先來不可得；

諸法性若定，　今云何可得？

Ru-Yi Translation

The nature of these four fruits of the path

could not be obtained before;

and if the natures of things are fixed,

how could they be obtained now?

諸法若有定性，四沙門果先來未得，今云何可得？若可得者，性則無定。復次：

If things had a fixed nature, then the four fruits of the renunciant, not having been obtained before, how could they be obtained now? And if they can be obtained, then the nature is not fixed. Furthermore:

Root Verse 29 · 頌

若無有四果，　則無得向者，

以無八聖故，　則無有僧寶。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there are no four fruits,

there is no one who attains them or approaches them;

and since there are no eight noble ones,

there is no Jewel of the Saṅgha.

無四沙門果故，則無得果、向果者。無八賢聖故，則無有僧寶。而經說八賢聖，名為僧寶。復次：

Since there are no four fruits of the renunciant, there is no one who has attained a fruit and no one approaching one. And since there are no eight noble persons, there is no Jewel of the Saṅgha — yet the scriptures say that the eight noble persons are what is called the Jewel of the Saṅgha. Furthermore:

Root Verse 30 · 頌

無四聖諦故，　亦無有法寶，

無法寶僧寶，　云何有佛寶？

Ru-Yi Translation

And since there are no Four Noble Truths,

there is no Jewel of the Dharma;

and with no Jewel of the Dharma or the Saṅgha,

how could there be a Jewel of the Buddha?

行四聖諦得涅槃法，若無四諦則無法寶，若無二寶云何當有佛寶？汝以如是因緣，說諸法定性，則壞三寶。

問曰：汝雖破諸法，究竟道阿耨多羅三藐三菩提應有，因是道故名為佛。

答曰：

It is by practicing the Four Noble Truths that one attains the Dharma of nirvāṇa; and if there are no Four Truths there is no Jewel of the Dharma; and if those two Jewels are lacking, how could there be a Jewel of the Buddha? By reasoning of just this kind: to say that things have a fixed nature is to destroy the Three Jewels.

Question: Even granting that you break all things, there must still be the ultimate path, anuttarā-samyak-saṃbodhi; it is on account of that path that one is called a Buddha.

Answer:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Eleven verses, and they do one thing with great deliberateness: they take the opponent's indictment from verses 1 to 5 and read it back to him line by line, in the same order, with a single word changed. Where he said "if everything is empty," Nāgārjuna says "if everything is not empty." Everything else stays. No Four Truths, no seeing and cutting off and realizing and cultivating, no fruits, no eight noble persons, no Saṅgha, no Dharma, no Buddha. It is one of the most exact pieces of construction in Buddhist literature, and it should be read as the answer to the question the opponent never thought to ask: what would a world of fixed natures actually be like to live in?

The answer is that it would be a world in which nothing could ever get better. Watch it applied to each truth in turn. Suffering could not arise, because what has a nature is already what it is and does not need to come about — and it could not even be suffering, since the scriptures define suffering by impermanence, and a fixed nature is precisely what never changes. Origination would have nothing to originate. Cessation could not occur, because a nature does not stop; that is what makes it a nature. And the path could not be walked, for the reason given in verse 24, which is the cleanest sentence in the sequence: 若法實者則是常，常則不可增益 — what is real in that sense is permanent, and what is permanent cannot be added to. Cultivation means becoming what you were not. A fixed thing has nothing to become.

Verse 26 turns the same knife on the one thing the opponent was surest of: the moment of insight. He was an ordinary person; then he saw suffering as it is; now he is not what he was. But if the nature of suffering is fixed, and it was not seen before, then the not-seeing is fixed too, and there is no mechanism by which the seeing could ever happen. Piṅgala's phrase is exact and slightly chilling — 不見性定故, the not-seeing would itself be fixed by nature. A doctrine of real, findable essences cannot account for the single experience the whole tradition is organized around.

And so the last verses simply walk the opponent's own chain of consequences to the same terminus he threatened Nāgārjuna with: no fruits, therefore no noble ones, therefore no Saṅgha; no Four Truths, therefore no Dharma; and with those gone, no Buddha. The Three Jewels are destroyed. By him. This is the moment the chapter has been building toward since verse 1, and it is worth stating in the plainest possible terms, because it is the reverse of what almost every newcomer assumes. Emptiness is not a threat to Buddhist practice that has to be carefully managed. Emptiness is the only thing that makes Buddhist practice coherent. Take it away and there is no illness, no medicine, and no recovery — only fixed things being fixedly themselves forever.

No Buddha, No Merit, No Path

Root Verse 31 · 頌

汝說則不因，　菩提而有佛；

亦復不因佛，　而有於菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

On your account there is no Buddha

who comes to be in dependence on awakening,

nor is there any awakening

that comes to be in dependence on a Buddha.

Commentary · 青目釋

汝說諸法有定性者，則不應因菩提有佛、因佛有菩提，是二性常定故。復次：

Ru-Yi Translation

If you say that things have a fixed nature, then there should be no Buddha in dependence on awakening and no awakening in dependence on a Buddha, since the natures of the two would be permanently fixed. Furthermore:

Root Verse 32 · 頌

雖復勤精進，　修行菩提道，

若先非佛性，　不應得成佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Though one strive with diligence

and practice the path of awakening,

if one is not of buddha-nature beforehand

one can never become a buddha.

以先無性故，如鐵無金性，雖復種種鍛煉終不成金。復次：

Because the nature was not there beforehand — as iron has no nature of gold, so that however it is hammered and refined it never becomes gold. Furthermore:

Root Verse 33 · 頌

若諸法不空，　無作罪福者，

不空何所作？　以其性定故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If things were not empty,

no one would do wrong or do merit;

what could the non-empty do,

its nature being fixed?

若諸法不空，終無有人作罪福者。何以故？罪福性先已定故，又無作作者故。復次：

If things were not empty, in the end there would be no one who does wrong or does merit. Why? Because the natures of wrongdoing and merit would already be fixed beforehand; and because there would be no act and no agent. Furthermore:

Root Verse 34 · 頌

汝於罪福中，　不生果報者，

是則離罪福，　而有諸果報。

Ru-Yi Translation

If on your view no ripening

arises out of wrongdoing and merit,

then there would be ripenings

apart from wrongdoing and merit.

汝於罪福因緣中皆無果報者，則應離罪福因緣而有果報。何以故？果報不待因出故。

問曰：離罪福可無善惡果報，但從罪福有善惡果報。

答曰：

If on your view no ripening comes from the causes and conditions of wrongdoing and merit, then there must be ripenings apart from the causes and conditions of wrongdoing and merit. Why? Because the ripening would come forth without waiting on a cause.

Question: Apart from wrongdoing and merit there may indeed be no good or bad ripening; but from wrongdoing and merit good and bad ripenings do come.

Answer:

Root Verse 35 · 頌

若謂從罪福，　而生果報者，

果從罪福生，　云何言不空？

Ru-Yi Translation

If you say that ripening

arises out of wrongdoing and merit,

then, the fruit arising from wrongdoing and merit,

how can you call it not empty?

若離罪福無善惡果，云何言果不空？若爾，離作者則無罪福。汝先說諸法不空，是事不然。復次：

If apart from wrongdoing and merit there is no good or bad fruit, how can you say the fruit is not empty? And if that is so, then apart from an agent there is no wrongdoing or merit either. What you said earlier, that things are not empty, will not do. Furthermore:

Root Verse 36 · 頌

汝破一切法，　諸因緣空義，

則破於世俗，　諸餘所有法。

Ru-Yi Translation

In breaking the emptiness of all things,

which is their arising from conditions,

you break the conventional

and everything else there is.

汝若破眾因緣法第一空義者，則破一切世俗法。何以故？

If you break the foremost meaning of emptiness — that things arise from a host of causes and conditions — then you break every convention of the world. Why?

Root Verse 37 · 頌

若破於空義，　即應無所作；

無作而有作，　不作名作者。

Ru-Yi Translation

If you break the meaning of emptiness,

then nothing can be done;

there would be doing with nothing done,

and one would be called an agent without acting.

若破空義，則一切果皆無作無因，又不作而作，又一切作者不應有所作，又離作者應有業、有果報、有受者。但是事皆不然，是故不應破空。復次：

If you break the meaning of emptiness, then every fruit is without a maker and without a cause; and there is doing without any doing; and no agent could ever do anything; and apart from any agent there would still be actions, ripenings, and someone who undergoes them. None of this will do. Therefore emptiness ought not to be broken. Furthermore:

Root Verse 38 · 頌

若有決定性，　世間種種相，

則不生不滅，　常住而不壞。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there were a fixed nature,

the world's manifold appearances

would neither arise nor cease,

abiding forever without decay.

若諸法有定性，則世間種種相，天、人、畜生、萬物，皆應不生不滅常住不壞。何以故？有實性不可變異故。而現見萬物各有變異相生滅變易，是故不應有定性。復次：

If things had a fixed nature, then the world's manifold appearances — gods, humans, animals, the ten thousand things — should all neither arise nor cease, but abide forever without decay. Why? Because what has a real nature cannot alter. And yet we plainly see that the ten thousand things each bear marks of alteration, arising and ceasing and changing about. Therefore there ought not to be any fixed nature. Furthermore:

Root Verse 39 · 頌

若無有空者，　未得不應得，

亦無斷煩惱，　亦無苦盡事。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there were no emptiness,

what has not been attained could not be attained,

there would be no cutting off of the afflictions,

and no ending of suffering.

若無有空法者，則世間、出世間所有功德，未得者皆不應得，亦不應有斷煩惱者，亦無苦盡。何以故？以性定故。

If there were no emptiness, then of all the good qualities in the world and beyond the world, whatever had not been attained could never be attained; there could be no one who cuts off the afflictions; and there would be no ending of suffering. Why? Because natures would be fixed.

Root Verse 40 · 頌

是故經中說，　若見因緣法，

則為能見佛，　見苦集滅道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore the scriptures say:

whoever sees dependent arising

is able to see the Buddha,

and sees suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path.

若人見一切法從眾緣生，是人即能見佛法身，增益智慧，能見四聖諦苦集滅道，見四聖諦得四果滅諸苦惱，是故不應破空義。若破空義則破因緣法，破因緣法則破三寶，若破三寶則為自破。

If a person sees that all things arise from a host of conditions, that person is able to see the Buddha's Dharma-body; his wisdom increases, and he is able to see the Four Noble Truths — suffering, origination, cessation, and the path. Seeing the Four Noble Truths he attains the four fruits and extinguishes every suffering and affliction. Therefore the meaning of emptiness ought not to be broken. To break the meaning of emptiness is to break dependent arising; to break dependent arising is to break the Three Jewels; and to break the Three Jewels is to break oneself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The iron and the gold is the image to carry away from this stretch, and it is Piṅgala's, not Nāgārjuna's — one of the places where the old commentator earns his keep. If a thing's nature is fixed, then a person who is not already a buddha cannot become one, and no amount of effort will change that, because effort does not alter natures. Hammer iron as long as you like, refine it, heat it, fold it: it will be excellent iron and it will never be gold. The verse and image together dispose of a mistake that recurs in every generation of practitioners — the quiet suspicion that awakening is for people who were built for it. If that were true it would be true because natures are fixed, and if natures were fixed nothing could be cultivated by anyone, including the people who look like they were built for it.

Verses 33 to 35 then take up the moral charge from verse 6, the one that actually worries people, and answer it head-on. The opponent said emptiness abolishes the difference between wrongdoing and merit. Nāgārjuna's reply is that fixed natures abolish it, and abolish it more completely. If an act had a nature of its own it would need no agent and could have no consequence — consequences require change, and natures do not change. Worse, if merit did not produce its ripening, ripenings would have to arrive from somewhere else, and a world where fortune and misfortune arrive without regard to what anyone did is exactly the moral chaos the opponent was afraid of. Then verse 35 closes the trap with a question the opponent cannot answer: you say the fruit comes from the act — so how can you call the fruit non-empty? Anything that comes from something else is, by that very fact, empty. The opponent has been describing dependent arising all along, in his own defense.

Verse 38 is the one place in the chapter where the argument leaves the seminar room and points out the window, and it is the same appeal that opened the treatise back in Chapter 1 with the grain and the sprout: 而現見萬物各有變異 — we plainly see that the ten thousand things alter. Gods, humans, animals, everything. Fixed natures are not merely inconvenient for soteriology; they are contradicted by the most ordinary observation available to anybody. Things change. That is not a minor detail to be accounted for later. It is the whole disagreement, and it is settled by looking.

And then verse 40, which closes the chapter by quoting the Buddha and turning the entire long argument into a single practical instruction: whoever sees dependent arising sees the Buddha, and sees the Four Truths. This is the reunion of everything the chapter separated. Dependent arising, emptiness, the Four Truths, and the Buddha are not four subjects. Seeing how anything comes to be — that it comes to be through conditions, and therefore has nothing of its own, and therefore can change, and therefore can be freed — is simultaneously seeing what suffering is, why it arises, that it can stop, and what road leads there. Piṅgala's last sentence is the treatise's answer to the accusation with which the chapter began, and it does not hedge: to break emptiness is to break dependent arising; to break dependent arising is to break the Three Jewels; and whoever breaks the Three Jewels breaks himself.

Chapter 25 · Examining Nirvāṇa 觀涅槃品第二十五

The same objection returns in miniature, now aimed at the goal itself: if nothing arises or ceases, what exactly is extinguished when someone attains nirvāṇa? Nāgārjuna answers with the identical reversal — a nirvāṇa that had a nature of its own could not be attained either — and then runs the four alternatives one by one. Nirvāṇa is not an existent thing, not a non-existent thing, not both, not neither. What is left when all four are refused is not a blank; it is verse 19, the sentence that stops every first-time reader, and the passage on the sixty-two views that brings twenty-five chapters of argument to rest.

Neither Existent nor Non-existent

Commentary · 青目釋

問曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Question:

Root Verse 1 · 頌

若一切法空，　無生無滅者，

何斷何所滅，　而稱為涅槃？

Ru-Yi Translation

If all things are empty,

with neither arising nor ceasing,

what is cut off, what extinguished,

that one should call it nirvāṇa?

Commentary · 青目釋

若一切法空，則無生無滅。無生無滅者，何所斷何所滅，而名為涅槃？是故一切法不應空。以諸法不空故，斷諸煩惱滅五陰，名為涅槃。答曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

If all things were empty there would be neither arising nor ceasing. And with neither arising nor ceasing, what would be cut off and what extinguished, that one should call it nirvāṇa? Therefore all things ought not to be empty. It is because things are not empty that cutting off the afflictions and extinguishing the five aggregates is called nirvāṇa. Answer:

Root Verse 2 · 頌

若諸法不空，　則無生無滅；

何斷何所滅，　而稱為涅槃？

Ru-Yi Translation

If things were not empty,

there would be neither arising nor ceasing;

what then is cut off, what extinguished,

that one should call it nirvāṇa?

若一切世間不空，則無生無滅，何所斷何所滅而名為涅槃？是故有無二門，則非至涅槃。所名涅槃者：

If all the world were not empty, there would be neither arising nor ceasing; what then would be cut off, what extinguished, that one should call it nirvāṇa? Therefore neither of the two gates, existence and non-existence, arrives at nirvāṇa. As for what is called nirvāṇa:

Root Verse 3 · 頌

無得亦無至，　不斷亦不常，

不生亦不滅，　是說名涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not obtained and not arrived at,

not cut off and not permanent,

not arising and not ceasing:

this is what is called nirvāṇa.

無得者，於行於果無所得。無至者，無處可至。不斷者，五陰先來畢竟空故，得道入無餘涅槃時亦無所斷。不常者，若有法可得分別者則名為常，涅槃寂滅無法可分別故，不名為常。生滅亦爾，如是相者名為涅槃。復次經說，涅槃非有、非無、非有無、非非有非非無，一切法不受，內寂滅名涅槃。何以故？

"Not obtained": there is nothing obtained, whether in the practice or in the fruit. "Not arrived at": there is no place to arrive at. "Not cut off": because the five aggregates have from the beginning been ultimately empty, so that when one attains the path and enters nirvāṇa without remainder there is nothing cut off either. "Not permanent": if there were something obtainable that could be marked off, that would be called permanent; but nirvāṇa is quiescent and there is nothing in it to mark off, so it is not called permanent. It is the same with arising and ceasing. What bears such marks is called nirvāṇa. Further, the scriptures say: nirvāṇa is not existent, not non-existent, not both existent and non-existent, not neither existent nor non-existent; it takes up nothing at all, and being inwardly quiescent it is called nirvāṇa. Why?

Root Verse 4 · 頌

涅槃不名有，　有則老死相；

終無有有法，　離於老死相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nirvāṇa is not called existent,

for the existent bears the mark of aging and death;

there has never been an existent thing

apart from the mark of aging and death.

眼見一切萬物皆生滅故，是老死相。涅槃若是有，則應有老死相。但是事不然，是故涅槃不名有。又不見離生滅老死別有定法而名涅槃。若涅槃是有，即應有生滅老死相；以離老死相故，名為涅槃。復次：

The eye sees that all the ten thousand things arise and cease, and this is the mark of aging and death. If nirvāṇa were existent it would have to bear the mark of aging and death. But that will not do, and so nirvāṇa is not called existent. Nor does one find, apart from arising and ceasing and aging and death, some separate fixed thing to be called nirvāṇa. If nirvāṇa were existent it would have the marks of arising, ceasing, aging, and death; it is because it is free of the mark of aging and death that it is called nirvāṇa. Furthermore:

Root Verse 5 · 頌

若涅槃是有，　涅槃即有為；

終無有一法，　而是無為者。

Ru-Yi Translation

If nirvāṇa were existent,

nirvāṇa would be conditioned;

for there has never been a single thing

that was unconditioned.

涅槃非是有。何以故？一切萬物從眾緣生，皆是有為。無有一法名為無為者，雖常法假名無為，以理推之，無常法尚無有，何況常法不可見不可得者。復次：

Nirvāṇa is not existent. Why? All the ten thousand things arise from a host of conditions and are all conditioned. There is not a single thing that can be called unconditioned. Permanent things are conventionally named "unconditioned," but when the matter is pressed by reason: even impermanent things do not exist — how much less permanent ones, which can neither be seen nor found. Furthermore:

Root Verse 6 · 頌

若涅槃是有，　云何名無受？

無有不從受，　而名為有法。

Ru-Yi Translation

If nirvāṇa were existent,

how could it be called free of grasping?

There is nothing that does not come from grasping

and is yet called an existent thing.

若謂涅槃是有法者，經則不應說無受是涅槃。何以故？無有有法不受而有，是故涅槃非有。

問曰：若有非涅槃者，無應是涅槃耶？

答曰：

If nirvāṇa were an existent thing, the scriptures should not have said that what is free of grasping is nirvāṇa. Why? Because there is no existent thing that exists without grasping. Therefore nirvāṇa is not existent.

Question: If the existent is not nirvāṇa, then should the non-existent be nirvāṇa?

Answer:

Root Verse 7 · 頌

有尚非涅槃，　何況於無耶！

涅槃無有有，　何處當有無？

Ru-Yi Translation

If the existent is not nirvāṇa,

how much less the non-existent!

In nirvāṇa there is no existence —

where then would non-existence be?

若有非涅槃，無云何是涅槃？何以故？因有故有無；若無有，何有無？如經說，先有今無則名無。涅槃則不爾。何以故？非有法變為無故。是故無亦不作涅槃。復次：

If the existent is not nirvāṇa, how could the non-existent be nirvāṇa? Why? Because it is on the basis of existence that there is non-existence; and if there is no existence, where is non-existence? As the scriptures say: what was there before and is not there now is called non-existent. But nirvāṇa is not like that. Why? Because it is not an existent thing that has turned into a non-existent one. Therefore the non-existent does not make nirvāṇa either. Furthermore:

Root Verse 8 · 頌

若無是涅槃，　云何名不受？

未曾有不受，　而名為無法。

Ru-Yi Translation

If the non-existent were nirvāṇa,

how could it be called free of grasping?

There has never been anything free of grasping

that was called a non-existent thing.

若謂無是涅槃，經則不應說不受名涅槃。何以故？無有不受而名無法，是故知涅槃非無。

問曰：若涅槃非有非無者，何等是涅槃？

答曰：

If the non-existent were nirvāṇa, the scriptures should not have said that being free of grasping is what nirvāṇa is called. Why? Because nothing free of grasping is called a non-existent thing. Therefore one knows that nirvāṇa is not non-existent.

Question: If nirvāṇa is neither existent nor non-existent, then what is nirvāṇa?

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chapter opens with the same trade as Chapter 24, compressed into two verses, and it is worth watching the exchange closely because it is over so fast. The opponent: if everything is empty, nothing arises and nothing ceases, so nothing is extinguished and nirvāṇa is impossible. Nāgārjuna, verse 2, changing one word: if things were not empty, nothing would arise or cease, so nothing could be extinguished and nirvāṇa would be impossible. Piṅgala's summary is the cleanest statement in the chapter — 有無二門，則非至涅槃, neither of the two gates, existence and non-existence, arrives at nirvāṇa. The opponent thought he was standing on solid ground and pointing at a hole. He was standing in the hole.

Verse 3 then gives the definition, and every clause of it removes something a reader wants. Not obtained: there is no acquisition, in the practice or in the fruit. Not arrived at: there is no place to go. This is the plainest possible refusal of the picture nearly everyone carries — nirvāṇa as a destination, a state entered, a something one comes to have. Not cut off is the strangest and most important: since the aggregates were from the beginning empty, even at final nirvāṇa nothing is actually severed. What would you cut? The chain was never a chain.

Then verses 4 through 8 run the argument that nirvāṇa is neither an existent thing nor a non-existent one. The reasoning is not mystical at all; it is almost pedantic, and that is its strength. If nirvāṇa existed as a thing, it would be a conditioned thing — everything that exists arose from conditions, and everything conditioned ages and ends. A nirvāṇa that ages is not nirvāṇa. But neither can it be sheer non-existence, and the reason given is the sharpest in the chapter: "non-existent" only ever means what was here and is now gone. Non-existence is parasitic on existence; it is a report of a loss. And nirvāṇa is not the loss of anything, because nothing was ever there in the fixed way that would make its absence a loss.

The word doing quiet work throughout is 受 — grasping, taking up, the appropriation by which a person makes a bundle of processes into mine. The scriptural phrase Piṅgala keeps returning to is that nirvāṇa is 無受, free of grasping. Notice what follows from that. Nirvāṇa is not being characterized as an object with unusual properties; it is being characterized by the absence of a certain activity. Nothing new appears. Something long-running stops. That is why it can be neither an existent thing nor the destruction of one — no thing was added and no thing was taken away.

Not Both, and Not Neither

Root Verse 9 · 頌

受諸因緣故，　輪轉生死中；

不受諸因緣，　是名為涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because one grasps at causes and conditions

one turns in the round of birth and death;

not grasping at causes and conditions —

this is what is called nirvāṇa.

Commentary · 青目釋

不如實知顛倒故，因五受陰往來生死。如實知顛倒故，則不復因五受陰往來生死。無性五陰不復相續故，說名涅槃。復次：

Ru-Yi Translation

Because one does not know inversion as it is, one comes and goes in birth and death by way of the five grasped aggregates. Because one does know inversion as it is, one no longer comes and goes in birth and death by way of the five grasped aggregates. And since the aggregates, having no nature, no longer continue on, this is called nirvāṇa. Furthermore:

Root Verse 10 · 頌

如佛經中說，　斷有斷非有；

是故知涅槃，　非有亦非無。

Ru-Yi Translation

As is said in the Buddha's scriptures,

cut off existence, cut off non-existence;

therefore know that nirvāṇa

is neither existent nor non-existent.

有名三有，非有名三有斷滅。佛說斷此二事故，當知涅槃非有亦非無。

問曰：若有若無非涅槃者，今有無共合是涅槃耶？

答曰：

"Existence" means the three realms of existence; "non-existence" means the annihilation of the three realms of existence. Because the Buddha taught the cutting off of both of these, one should know that nirvāṇa is neither existent nor non-existent.

Question: If neither the existent nor the non-existent is nirvāṇa, then are the two combined nirvāṇa?

Answer:

Root Verse 11 · 頌

若謂於有無，　合為涅槃者，

有無即解脫，　是事則不然。

Ru-Yi Translation

If you say that existence and non-existence

combined are nirvāṇa,

then existence and non-existence would be liberation —

and that will not do.

若謂於有無合為涅槃者，即有無二事合為解脫。是事不然。何以故？有無二事相違故，云何一處有？復次：

If you say that existence and non-existence combined are nirvāṇa, then the two together would be liberation. That will not do. Why? Because the two contradict each other — how could they be in one place? Furthermore:

Root Verse 12 · 頌

若謂於有無，　合為涅槃者；

涅槃非無受，　是二從受生。

Ru-Yi Translation

If you say that existence and non-existence

combined are nirvāṇa,

then nirvāṇa is not free of grasping,

since these two arise from grasping.

若謂有無合為涅槃者，經不應說涅槃名無受。何以故？有無二事從受生，相因而有。是故有無二事，不得合為涅槃。復次：

If existence and non-existence combined were nirvāṇa, the scriptures should not have said that nirvāṇa is called free of grasping. Why? Because the two arise from grasping and exist by depending on each other. Therefore existence and non-existence cannot be combined into nirvāṇa. Furthermore:

Root Verse 13 · 頌

有無共合成，　云何名涅槃？

涅槃名無為，　有無是有為。

Ru-Yi Translation

How could existence and non-existence,

combined, be called nirvāṇa?

Nirvāṇa is called the unconditioned,

and existence and non-existence are conditioned.

有無二事共合，不得名涅槃。涅槃名無為，有無是有為，是故有無非是涅槃。復次：

Existence and non-existence combined cannot be called nirvāṇa. Nirvāṇa is called the unconditioned, and existence and non-existence are conditioned; therefore they are not nirvāṇa. Furthermore:

Root Verse 14 · 頌

有無二事共，　云何是涅槃？

是二不同處，　如明暗不俱。

Ru-Yi Translation

How could the two, existence and non-existence,

together be nirvāṇa?

The two do not share one place,

as light and dark are not together.

有無二事不得名涅槃。何以故？有無相違，一處不可得，如明暗不俱。是故有時無無、無時無有，云何有無共合而名為涅槃？

問曰：若有無共合非涅槃者，今非有非無應是涅槃。

答曰：

Existence and non-existence cannot be called nirvāṇa. Why? Because they contradict each other and cannot be found in one place, as light and dark are not together. Therefore when there is existence there is no non-existence, and when there is non-existence there is no existence. How then could existence and non-existence be combined and called nirvāṇa?

Question: If existence and non-existence combined are not nirvāṇa, then "neither existent nor non-existent" must be nirvāṇa.

Answer:

Root Verse 15 · 頌

若非有非無，　名之為涅槃；

此非有非無，　以何而分別？

Ru-Yi Translation

If "neither existent nor non-existent"

is what is called nirvāṇa,

then this "neither existent nor non-existent" —

by what is it marked off?

若涅槃非有非無者，此非有非無因何而分別？是故非有非無是涅槃者，是事不然。復次：

If nirvāṇa were neither existent nor non-existent, on what basis would this "neither existent nor non-existent" be marked off? Therefore to say that "neither existent nor non-existent" is nirvāṇa will not do. Furthermore:

Root Verse 16 · 頌

分別非有無，　如是名涅槃；

若有無成者，　非有非無成。

Ru-Yi Translation

To mark off "neither existent nor non-existent"

and call this nirvāṇa —

only if existence and non-existence were established

would "neither existent nor non-existent" be established.

汝分別非有非無是涅槃者，是事不然。何以故？若有無成者，然後非有非無成。有相違名無、無相違名有，是有無第三句中已破。有無無故，云何有非有非無？是故涅槃非非有非非無。復次：

Your marking off of "neither existent nor non-existent" as nirvāṇa will not do. Why? Only if existence and non-existence were established would "neither existent nor non-existent" then be established. What contradicts the existent is called non-existent and what contradicts the non-existent is called existent — and existence and non-existence were already broken in the third alternative. Since there is neither existence nor non-existence, how could there be "neither existent nor non-existent"? Therefore nirvāṇa is not "neither existent nor non-existent" either. Furthermore:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Verse 9 is the chapter's one positive statement and it is easy to read past, so it is worth stopping on. Grasping at conditions, one revolves; not grasping at them, that is nirvāṇa. Note what is not said. Not: the conditions stop. Not: the world goes away. Not: one departs for elsewhere. The same conditions, the same aggregates, the same street and weather and body — and one thing subtracted, the appropriating that turns all of it into a self and its possessions. Piṅgala's phrase for the ordinary condition is exact: 五受陰, the five grasped aggregates. It is the grasping that makes them a life someone is having.

The rest of the section completes the catuṣkoṭi, the four-cornered exhaustion of a question that has structured the whole treatise since Chapter 1. Existent, refused. Non-existent, refused. Both, refused — and here the argument is not merely formal: existence and non-existence are contradictories, and Piṅgala reaches for the plainest image available, that light and dark do not occupy one room. Then neither, refused, and this is the corner that catches thoughtful readers, because "neither existent nor non-existent" sounds like exactly the sophisticated thing a Buddhist ought to say about nirvāṇa. Verse 15 asks the flat question: by what is that negation marked off? A double negation is not free-floating. It is defined entirely by the two terms it negates, and those two have already been dismantled. Deny both legs and the "neither" has nothing left to stand on.

What this rules out, in practice, is the most refined version of the mistake — the one made not by beginners but by people some distance along. Having learned not to say nirvāṇa is a thing, and not to say it is nothing, one settles comfortably into "it is beyond existence and non-existence" and feels one has arrived at the subtle view. Nāgārjuna will not let that be a resting place either. It is still a description, still marking something off, still an act of the mind that pins a nature onto what is being described. There is no fourth position that survives.

And so the reader arrives where the chapter needed them: with all four corners gone and nothing to hold. That is not a failure of the inquiry. It is the inquiry succeeding. What was being looked for all along was a thing called nirvāṇa, and the finding is that the search itself was the last piece of grasping. The next four verses say what that leaves.

Nirvāṇa and the World

Root Verses 17–18 · 頌

如來滅度後，　不言有與無，

亦不言有無，　非有及非無。

如來現在時，　不言有與無，

亦不言有無，　非有及非無。

Ru-Yi Translation

After the Tathāgata's final passing

one does not say he exists or does not exist,

nor that he both exists and does not,

nor that he neither exists nor does not.

 

While the Tathāgata is present

one does not say he exists or does not exist,

nor that he both exists and does not,

nor that he neither exists nor does not.

Commentary · 青目釋

若如來滅後、若現在，有如來亦不受，無如來亦不受，亦有如來亦無如來亦不受，非有如來非無如來亦不受。以不受故，不應分別涅槃有無等。離如來，誰當得涅槃？何時何處以何法說涅槃？是故一切時一切種、求涅槃相不可得。復次：

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether after the Tathāgata's passing or while he is present: that the Tathāgata exists is not taken up; that the Tathāgata does not exist is not taken up; that he both exists and does not exist is not taken up; that he neither exists nor does not exist is not taken up. And since nothing is taken up, one should not divide nirvāṇa into existent, non-existent, and the rest. Apart from the Tathāgata, who is it that would attain nirvāṇa? At what time, in what place, by what means would nirvāṇa be spoken of? Therefore at every time and in every way, the mark of nirvāṇa cannot be found by searching. Furthermore:

Root Verse 19 · 頌

涅槃與世間，　無有少分別；

世間與涅槃，　亦無少分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Between nirvāṇa and the world

there is not the slightest difference;

between the world and nirvāṇa

there is not the slightest difference either.

五陰相續往來因緣故，說名世間。五陰性畢竟空無受寂滅，此義先已說。以一切法不生不滅故，世間與涅槃無有分別，涅槃與世間亦無分別。復次：

Because the five aggregates continue in succession, coming and going by way of causes and conditions, this is called the world. The nature of the five aggregates is ultimately empty, free of grasping and quiescent — this meaning has already been stated. And since all things neither arise nor cease, there is no difference between the world and nirvāṇa, and no difference between nirvāṇa and the world. Furthermore:

Root Verse 20 · 頌

涅槃之實際，　及與世間際，

如是二際者，　無毫釐差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The true limit of nirvāṇa

and the limit of the world:

between these two limits

there is not a hair's breadth of difference.

究竟推求世間、涅槃、實際、無生際，以平等不可得故，無毫釐差別。復次：

Pressing the search to the very end — the world, nirvāṇa, the true limit, the limit of non-arising — since they are equal and cannot be found, there is not a hair's breadth of difference. Furthermore:

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Here is the sentence that stops every first-time reader, and it deserves to be sat with rather than explained away: 涅槃與世間，無有少分別 — between nirvāṇa and the world, not the slightest difference. Nāgārjuna then says it a second time with the terms reversed, so that no one can take it as a one-way claim, and verse 20 says it a third time with a unit of measurement: not a hair's breadth. Whatever else this is, it is not an incidental remark.

The argument behind it is short and has been earned across twenty-five chapters. The world (世間, saṃsāra) is not a location. It is the five aggregates continuing in succession, grasped at, one condition giving way to the next. Nirvāṇa is not a location either; verse 3 already said there is no place to arrive at. And when either is searched for as a thing with a nature of its own, neither can be found — so there is nothing there to differ. Two things can differ only if there are two things. The reasoning is not poetic. It is the same analysis applied twice with the same result.

Now the caution, and it matters more than anything else on this page. This verse does not say that suffering is unreal, that it makes no difference whether a person is in anguish or at peace, or that a bombed city and a quiet room are the same to look at. Nothing in the treatise licenses that, and Chapter 24 has just spent forty verses establishing the opposite: emptiness is precisely what makes suffering real enough to arise and dependent enough to end. To hear verse 19 as "so it doesn't matter" is to make exactly the mistake the previous chapter was written to prevent — mistaking the ultimate for a replacement of the conventional rather than its fine print. On the conventional level, which is where every being actually lives and every act lands, the difference between torment and freedom is the most important difference there is.

What the verse denies is a metaphysical gap: that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are two realms, one to be escaped and one to be reached, made of different stuff, with a border between them. There is no such border, and the practical consequence is enormous. It means liberation is not migration. There is no elsewhere to get to, no better world that this one is a waiting room for, and nothing about your circumstances that has to change first. The same aggregates that are the world are the aggregates that are quiescent when nothing appropriates them. This is the single verse from which the whole East Asian conviction descends — that awakening is found here, in this life, in ordinary conditions, or nowhere. Chan's insistence on the everyday and Pure Land's insistence that this defiled world is not other than the pure one are both, in the end, commentaries on these twenty characters.

One more thing worth noticing, because it explains why verses 17 and 18 come immediately before. If there is no findable Tathāgata even while he is standing in front of you — the point Chapter 22 established and this passage repeats for both times, present and after the passing — then there is no one to be moved from one realm to the other. The question where does the Buddha go? was always the wrong question, and Piṅgala asks it in its most deflating form: apart from the Tathāgata, who is it that would attain nirvāṇa? No traveler, so no journey. No journey, so no two places.

The End of All Views

Root Verse 21 · 頌

滅後有無等，　有邊等常等，

諸見依涅槃，　未來過去世。

Ru-Yi Translation

Existence and non-existence after the passing,

having a limit and the rest, permanence and the rest —

these views rest upon nirvāṇa,

upon time to come and time gone by.

Commentary · 青目釋

如來滅後有如來、無如來、亦有如來亦無如來、非有如來非無如來。世間有邊、世間無邊、世間亦有邊亦無邊、世間非有邊非無邊。世間常、世間無常、世間亦常亦無常、世間非有常非無常。此三種十二見。如來滅後有無等四見，依涅槃起。世間有邊無邊等四見，依未來世起。世間常無常等四見，依過去世起。如來滅後有無等不可得，涅槃亦如是。如世間前際後際、有邊無邊、有常無常等不可得，涅槃亦如是。是故說世間、涅槃等無有異。復次：

Ru-Yi Translation

After the Tathāgata's passing: the Tathāgata exists; the Tathāgata does not exist; the Tathāgata both exists and does not exist; the Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist. The world has a limit; the world has no limit; the world both has and has not a limit; the world neither has nor has not a limit. The world is permanent; the world is impermanent; the world is both permanent and impermanent; the world is neither permanent nor impermanent. These are the three sets, twelve views in all. The four views about existence and non-existence after the Tathāgata's passing arise in dependence on nirvāṇa. The four views about the world's having or not having a limit arise in dependence on time to come. The four views about the world's permanence or impermanence arise in dependence on time gone by. Existence and non-existence after the Tathāgata's passing cannot be found — and it is the same with nirvāṇa. Just as the world's earlier limit and later limit, its having or not having a limit, its permanence or impermanence, cannot be found — so it is with nirvāṇa. This is why it is said that the world and nirvāṇa are equal and not different. Furthermore:

Root Verses 22–24 · 頌

一切法空故，　何有邊無邊，

亦邊亦無邊，　非有非無邊？

何者為一異，　何有常無常，

亦常亦無常，　非常非無常？

諸法不可得，　滅一切戲論，

無人亦無處，　佛亦無所說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since all things are empty,

how could there be a limit or no limit,

both a limit and no limit,

neither having nor not having a limit?

 

What would be identical or different?

How could there be permanence or impermanence,

both permanence and impermanence,

neither permanence nor impermanence?

 

Things cannot be found;

all conceptual elaboration is quieted;

there is no person and no place —

and the Buddha taught nothing at all.

一切法、一切時、一切種，從眾緣生故，畢竟空故無自性。如是法中，何者是有邊，誰為有邊？何者是無邊，亦有邊亦無邊，非有邊非無邊。誰為非有邊非無邊？何者是常，誰為是常？何者是無常，常無常非常非無常，誰為非常非無常？何者身即是神，何者身異於神？如是等六十二邪見，於畢竟空中皆不可得。諸有所得皆息、戲論皆滅。戲論滅故，通達諸法實相得安隱道。從〈因緣品〉來，分別推求諸法，有亦無、無亦無、有無亦無、非有非無亦無，是名諸法實相，亦名如，法性，實際，涅槃。是故如來無時無處為人說涅槃定相，是故說諸有所得皆息、戲論皆滅。

All things, at all times, in every way, arise from a host of conditions, and being ultimately empty they have no inherent nature. Among things of that kind: what is it that has a limit, and who is it that has a limit? What is it that has no limit, that both has and has not a limit, that neither has nor has not a limit? Who is it that neither has nor has not a limit? What is it that is permanent, and who is it that is permanent? What is it that is impermanent — permanent, impermanent, neither permanent nor impermanent — and who is it that is neither permanent nor impermanent? What is it whose body is the very same as its soul, and what is it whose body is other than its soul? None of the sixty-two wrong views of this kind can be found within ultimate emptiness. Everything that might be obtained comes to rest; all conceptual elaboration is extinguished. And because elaboration is extinguished, one penetrates the real character of things and attains the path of peace. From the chapter on Conditions onward, things have been distinguished and pressed with questions: the existent is not, the non-existent is not, both existent and non-existent is not, neither existent nor non-existent is not. This is called the real character of things; it is also called suchness, the nature of things, the true limit, nirvāṇa. Therefore at no time and in no place has the Tathāgata taught anyone a fixed character of nirvāṇa. And that is why it is said: everything that might be obtained comes to rest, and all conceptual elaboration is extinguished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The chapter ends by naming the questions the Buddha had famously refused to answer — the ones the early texts call the undeclared points, where a monk demands to know whether the world is eternal, whether it has an edge, whether the Tathāgata exists after death, and receives silence. Nāgārjuna does something with them that no one had done before: he explains why the silence was the right answer, and the explanation is not that the questions are unprofitable or that the Buddha was being pastoral. It is that the questions are malformed. Each of them asks for a property of something with a fixed nature, and there is no such something. "Does it have an edge?" presupposes an it.

Piṅgala's sorting of the twelve views is worth pausing on because it shows how these questions actually work. The four about the Tathāgata after death lean on nirvāṇa; the four about the world's limits lean on the future; the four about permanence lean on the past. Every one of them is an attempt to fix a boundary — where does it stop, when did it start, what happens at the end. And the boundary is always being drawn around something that was never a unit in the first place. Then the throwaway line at the end of the list, which is the oldest question of all: what is it whose body is the very same as its soul, and what is it whose body is other than its soul? Sixteen centuries on, that is still the question people bring to a meditation hall, and it is still shaped the same wrong way.

Then the final verse, and the last line of it should be read twice: 佛亦無所說 — and the Buddha taught nothing at all. Coming at the end of twenty-five chapters of extremely determined argument, this can look like a paradox or a piece of showmanship. It is neither. Piṅgala's gloss is precise and deflationary: at no time and in no place did the Tathāgata teach anyone a fixed character of nirvāṇa. Nothing was ever handed over as a thing to hold. The teaching was always a set of removals, and when the removals are complete there is no residue to point at and say that is what he taught me.

Notice what Piṅgala says the outcome is, because it is not a blank and it is not despair. 諸有所得皆息、戲論皆滅 — everything that might be obtained comes to rest, all elaboration is extinguished — and then, immediately, 得安隱道, one attains the path of peace. The word is quiet and domestic: security, ease, the state of not being under threat. The end of the treatise is not vertigo. It is the relief of putting something down that had been carried a very long way for no reason, and finding that the world is still there, unchanged, and no longer requires defending.

And one last summing-up that Piṅgala offers, which functions as the treatise's own table of contents read backward. From the chapter on Conditions onward — that is, from the very first page, from the grain and the sprout and the four conditions — the method has been the same: the existent is not, the non-existent is not, both is not, neither is not. Four corners, chapter after chapter, on arising, motion, the senses, the aggregates, fire and fuel, the self, time, karma, the Tathāgata, the inversions, the truths, and now nirvāṇa. And what that relentlessness arrives at is given four names in a single breath — the real character of things, suchness, the nature of things, the true limit — and then a fifth, which is where this page ends: nirvāṇa. Not a place attained at the end of the road. The road, seen without anything added to it.
