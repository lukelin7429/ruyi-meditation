The Sūtra on the Profound Kindness of Parents

A Debt as Boundless as High Heaven《佛說父母恩重經》

A short, emotionally direct Chinese sūtra about a mother's exhausting devotion and a grown child's neglect — and one of the clearest cases on this site of a text the canon itself doubts: catalogued by the Taishō's own editors among the scriptures suspected of being composed in China rather than translated from India, and denounced outright as a forgery by the Ming-dynasty Pure Land master Yunqi Zhuhong.

A Text the Canon Itself Doubts

This page states plainly, before anything else, what the Taishō canon's own editors already concluded: this sūtra is not confidently a translation of an Indian original. It is catalogued in volume 85 of the Taishō canon, in a section reserved for texts suspected — on textual and historical grounds — of having been composed in China itself and given the outward form of a translated scripture. No translator's name is attached to it, itself a small warning sign, since genuine translations from any period of Chinese Buddhist history are almost always tied to a named monk and a documented date.

What "Doubtful and Dubious" Means

The Taishō canon organizes its Chinese-language volumes largely by genre and attribution. Volume 85 gathers texts the compilers could not confidently place among the general sūtra collections — including the 疑似部, "doubtful and dubious" section, for scriptures that carry every outward mark of a translated sūtra — an opening "Thus have I heard," a named location, an assembled audience, a closing formula of rejoicing — while lacking the textual pedigree (a named translator, an attested date, agreement with other recensions) that would place them among genuine Buddhavacana. This sūtra sits there, not among the site's other, confidently canonical texts.

The Internal Evidence

Several features of the text itself point the same direction, independent of its catalogue placement. The phrase translated below as "vast and boundless as high heaven" (昊天罔極) is quoted almost word for word from the Confucian Classic of Poetry (詩經), centuries older than Chinese Buddhism itself — a striking thing to find inside a purported Indian sūtra. The text gives itself, mid-passage, an inflated title borrowing the name of the Perfection of Wisdom corpus, which two lines later does not even match the short title the Buddha is made to give the sūtra directly. Its first answer to Ānanda's question openly borrows the seventh-month offering practice from a different, genuinely canonical text — the Ullambana Sūtra, already on this site. And its narrative register — a mother interrupted at the well, the stove, the mill; a child dragging itself along the ground to greet her; a bit of cake carried home tucked away — reads far closer to Chinese vernacular moral storytelling than to the terse, formulaic prose typical of sūtras actually translated from Sanskrit. Each point is taken up again, at the passage where it occurs, in the reading notes below.

Yunqi Zhuhong's Verdict: "A Forged Sūtra"

The Ming-dynasty Pure Land master Yunqi Zhuhong (雲棲祩宏, 1535–1615), one of the most influential monks of his generation, addressed this text directly in a short essay in his collection Zhuchuang Sanbi (竹窗三筆), titled "On the Forged Sūtra on the Weighty Kindness of Parents" (偽造父母恩重經). It is a brief note of rebuke, not a line-by-line commentary — Zhuhong is warning readers against mistaking a Chinese composition for scripture, not explaining its teaching passage by passage. We record his judgment here as the tradition's own strongest internal critique, not as a neutral outside verdict: readers within Chinese Buddhism have continued to value and circulate texts under this title for devotional reasons for centuries after Zhuhong wrote, precisely because its portrait of parental love asks nothing of the reader by way of scriptural authentication.

Not to Be Confused With Its Longer, More Famous Cousin

A separate, much longer and far more widely known apocryphal text — often illustrated with woodblock scenes of "the ten graces" (十恩) of a mother's care, and still recited today in some Chinese folk-Buddhist and filial-piety settings — commonly circulates under the extended title 父母恩重難報經, "the Sūtra on the Weighty Kindness of Parents, and the Difficulty of Repaying It." That text is a separate, later elaboration on the same basic theme and a closely related title, and is not the text translated on this page. What follows is specifically CBETA/Taishō no. T2887: a shorter, single-fascicle composition preserved in the canon's own 疑似部. Guifeng Zongmi and Lingzhi Yuanzhao each quote a few lines from a text called 父母恩重經 in passing, within their own commentaries on the Ullambana Sūtra — but those brief citations are commentary on that other text's tradition, not on this fascicle, and are not treated here as evidence for or against this specific sūtra.

Why Read It Anyway

Devotional value and canonical authenticity are two different questions, and this page tries to hold both honestly rather than collapsing one into the other. Whatever its origin, this sūtra is a window into how Chinese Buddhist writers absorbed the inherited Confucian language of filial debt — including, quite literally, a line from the Classic of Poetry — and reforged it into specifically Buddhist categories of merit, offering, and recitation. Texts exactly like this one, questioned or not, are part of what made filial piety a central practice of Chinese Buddhism, regardless of where any single fascicle's authority actually traces back to.

No Surviving Commentary — Reading Notes Are Ru-Yi's Own

A search of the standard collections of Chinese Buddhist exegesis turns up no dedicated, line-by-line commentary on this specific text — unsurprising for a scripture the tradition itself, at its most careful, doubted the authority of. The reading notes below are Ru-Yi's own, reading the text on its own terms: as a piece of devotional literature about a debt that, whatever its date or authorship, can never quite be settled.

Key Terms

the doubtful and dubious 疑似部 — the section of Taishō volume 85 reserved for texts suspected of Chinese, rather than Indian, composition; this sūtra's catalogue placement.
boundless as high heaven 昊天罔極 — quoted almost verbatim from the Confucian Classic of Poetry (詩經·小雅·蓼莪): "wishing to repay their virtue, I find it boundless as high heaven." One of the clearest single pieces of internal evidence for this sūtra's Chinese authorship.
the five heinous crimes 五逆 — the gravest category of offense in Buddhist ethics: patricide, matricide, killing an arhat, wounding a buddha, and causing a schism in the monastic sangha. The sūtra claims its own recitation can dissolve every one of them.
the woven cradle-cart 蘭車 — a small woven cart or pram in which an infant was carried or laid to rest; named twice in the sūtra's opening description of a mother's care.
the Ullambana offering 盂蘭盆 — the bowl-offering made on the fifteenth day of the seventh lunar month, the entire subject of the canonical Ullambana Sūtra (佛說盂蘭盆經), already on this site — and the practice this sūtra borrows directly for its first answer to Ānanda.

The Translation

The Chinese below follows the CBETA electronic edition of Taishō no. T2887, in the public domain. The English is Ru-Yi's own translation, kept close to the Chinese and set out passage by passage — the complete sūtra, without abridgment. No classical commentary on this text survives; the reading notes below are Ru-Yi's own, and where the source text itself is textually uncertain or grammatically irregular, the notes say so directly rather than smoothing it over.

1. The Setting

Chinese Source

如是我聞。一時佛在王舍城耆闍崛山中。與大菩薩摩訶薩及聲眷屬俱。亦與比丘比丘尼優婆塞優婆夷。一切諸天人民及天龍鬼神。皆來集會。一心聽佛說法。瞻仰尊顏。目不暫捨。佛言。人生在世。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard: at one time the Buddha was at Rājagṛha, on Vulture Peak, together with great bodhisattva-mahāsattvas and their retinue, and also together with monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen — with all the gods and people of every realm, and with the dragons, spirits, and demons of the heavens. All had gathered together, listening to the Buddha's teaching with one mind, gazing up at his honored countenance without turning their eyes away for a moment. The Buddha said: In this life, in this world —

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is a fairly standard Mahāyāna opening formula. Vulture Peak (耆闍崛山, Gṛdhrakūṭa) near Rājagṛha is one of the most common settings for Mahāyāna sūtras, and an assembled crowd of bodhisattvas, monastics, laypeople, and non-human beings is a stock feature of the genre. One small irregularity: the source text names great bodhisattva-mahāsattvas together with their "聲 retinue" — literally a "sound" or "voice" retinue, where the expected term is 聲聞, "śrāvaka." This may be simple scribal contraction, but it is exactly the kind of loose, slightly irregular phrasing that distinguishes this text's grammar from the more disciplined, formulaic Chinese of sūtras genuinely translated from a Sanskrit or Prakrit original — a point taken up at more length in the essay above.

2. A Mother's Labor, Measured

Chinese Source

父母為親。非父不生。非母不育。是以寄託母胎懷身十月。歲滿月充。母子俱顯生墮草上。父母養育。臥則蘭車。父母懷抱。和和弄聲。含笑未語。飢時須食。非母不哺。渴時須飲。非母不乳。母中飢時吞苦吐甘推乾就濕。非義不親。非母不養。慈母養兒。去離蘭車。十指甲中食子不淨。應各有八斛四㪷。計論母恩。昊天罔極。

Ru-Yi Translation

Parents are our nearest kin: without a father one is not born, without a mother one is not raised. And so we are entrusted to a mother's womb, carried there through ten full months; when the months are complete and the years fulfilled, mother and child both come into the open, and the child falls onto the birthing straw. The parents then raise and nurture it: at rest, laid in a woven cradle-cart; held in a parent's arms, soothed with gentle, playful sounds, before it can yet smile or speak. When hungry, it must be fed — without the mother, it cannot be fed. When thirsty, it must drink — without the mother, it cannot be nursed. Even hungry herself, the mother swallows what is bitter and spits out what is sweet for the child; she moves it from the wet place to the dry and lies herself in the wet — not from formal duty does she cling so close, not because it is owed does she nurture so. The loving mother, raising her child — moving it out of the woven cradle-cart, the filth of the child cleaned from beneath her own ten fingernails — reckoned altogether, comes to eight hu and four dou. Counting up a mother's kindness in this way: it is as vast and boundless as high heaven.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The phrase translated here as "vast and boundless as high heaven," 昊天罔極, is not original to this sūtra at all — it is quoted almost word for word from the Confucian Classic of Poetry (詩經), from the poem 蓼莪 in the "Lesser Odes": 欲報之德，昊天罔極, "wishing to repay their virtue, I find it boundless as high heaven." That poem is itself one of the oldest and most famous expressions of grief over parents in the entire Chinese literary tradition, predating Buddhism's arrival in China by centuries. Finding it quoted directly inside a purported translation of an Indian sūtra is one of the clearest single pieces of internal evidence that this text was composed in Chinese, drawing on Chinese literary resources, rather than translated from an Indic original.

The figure "eight hu and four dou" (八斛四㪷) — roughly a hundred gallons in traditional measure — is a folk numerological device: quantifying a mother's labor or care in exact units of grain-measure recurs across a whole later genre of Chinese parental-kindness literature, though the specific figures vary by text and telling. It is a rhetorical gesture, not a historical claim, meant to make an otherwise unmeasurable debt feel countable.

3. Ānanda's Question

Chinese Source

嗚呼慈母云何可報。阿難白佛言。世尊云何可報其恩。唯願說之。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Alas — such a loving mother's kindness, how can it ever be repaid?" Ānanda addressed the Buddha, saying: "World-Honored One, how can her kindness be repaid? We beg you to explain it."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

A short, functional bridge: Ānanda posing exactly the question the whole sūtra exists to answer. The device — a disciple asking precisely what the audience is meant to be wondering — is common to both genuine and apocryphal sūtras alike, so it carries no particular evidentiary weight on its own. What matters more is how the Buddha's answer is then constructed, taken up in the next two sections.

4. Repayment Through Merit and Scripture

Chinese Source

佛告阿難。汝諦聽善思念之。吾當為汝分別解說。父母之恩昊天罔極。云何若有孝順慈孝之子。能為父母作福造經。或以七月十五日能造佛槃盂蘭盆。獻佛及僧得果無量。能報父母之恩。若復有人。書寫此經。流布世人。受持讀誦。當知此人報父母恩。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Ānanda: "Listen closely, and reflect well upon it — I shall explain it for you in full. A parent's kindness is as vast and boundless as high heaven. How, then, may it be repaid? If there is a filial and loving child who is able, for the sake of their parents, to make merit and produce scriptures — or on the fifteenth day of the seventh month is able to prepare an Ullambana bowl-offering, presenting it to the Buddha and the sangha and thereby gaining boundless fruit — such a one is able to repay a parent's kindness. And if there is furthermore a person who copies out this sūtra, spreads it among the people of the world, upholds it, reads it, and recites it — know that this person, too, has repaid a parent's kindness."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Buddha's first answer borrows directly from a different, canonical sūtra already on this site: the practice of preparing an offering on the fifteenth day of the seventh month (七月十五日) as an Ullambana bowl-offering (盂蘭盆) is the entire subject of the Ullambana Sūtra (佛說盂蘭盆經), translated by Dharmarakṣa in the late third century. Here it appears folded into a different text's general argument about repaying parents, detached from the Ullambana Sūtra's specific narrative of rescuing a parent already reborn in suffering — a sign of borrowing rather than independent composition.

The second half of the answer — that copying, spreading, upholding, reading, and reciting this very sūtra also counts as repaying one's parents — is a self-referential merit claim: the text asserting that engaging with the text itself is spiritually sufficient. Claims like this appear occasionally in genuinely translated Mahāyāna sūtras too, so this alone does not prove forgery — but it is a recurring, almost obligatory feature of the wider genre of Chinese devotional and merit-generating apocrypha that this text belongs to.

5. A Mother Watching for Her Child

Chinese Source

父母云何可報。但父母至於行來。東西隣里井竈碓磨。不時還家。我兒家中啼哭。憶我即來還家。其兒遙見我來。或在蘭車。搖頭弄腦。或復曳腹隨行。嗚呼向母。母為其子曲身下就長。舒兩手拂拭塵土。嗚和其口開懷出乳。以乳與之。母見兒歡。兒見母喜。二情恩悲親愛。慈重莫復。

Ru-Yi Translation

But how else may a parent's kindness be repaid? Consider: when a parent goes out and about — east and west among the neighbors, to the well, the stove, the mortar, the mill — and does not come home at the expected hour, she thinks, "My child is at home crying," and remembering this, hurries straight back. Her child, catching sight of her from afar, whether still in the cradle-cart shaking its head and tossing about, or dragging its own body along the ground to follow her, cries out toward its mother. The mother, for her child's sake, bends her body low and reaches out her arms, brushing the dust from it, cooing softly and opening her robe to bring out her breast, giving it to the child. The mother, seeing her child, rejoices; the child, seeing its mother, is glad — between the two of them, love and sorrow, tenderness and affection, all at once. Such is the depth of this love — nothing surpasses it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This passage and the one following it shift register noticeably away from doctrinal statement and into a small, granular domestic story — a mother interrupted at the well, the stove, the mill; a child dragging itself along the ground to greet her. This kind of closely observed, almost novelistic household detail has few real precedents in the terse, formulaic prose of sūtras translated from Sanskrit, which tend to stay at the level of doctrinal statement and stock epithet rather than lingering on scenes like this one. It reads far more like the Chinese genre of moral exempla (勸孝故事, stories written expressly to instruct readers in filial devotion) fitted into sūtra form — part of the internal evidence discussed in the essay above.

6. Growing Up, Growing Distant

Chinese Source

二歲三歲弄意始行。於其食時非母不知父。母行來值他座席。或得餅肉。不噉輟味懷挾來歸。向其與子。十來九得恒常歡喜。一過不得憍啼佯哭。憍子不孝。必有五㰅。孝子不懷。必有慈順。遂至長大。朋友相隨。梳頭摩髮。欲得好衣。覆蓋身體。弊衣破故。父母自著新好綿帛。先與其子。至於行來。官私急疾。傾心南北。逐子東西。橫上其頭。既索妻婦得他子女。父母轉疏。私房屋室共相語樂。父母年高氣力衰老。終朝至暮不來借問。惑復父孤母寡。獨守空房。猶如客人。寄止他舍。常無恩愛。復無濡被寒。

Ru-Yi Translation

At two or three years old, the child begins, in its own way, to walk. At mealtimes, it looks first to its mother, not its father. If the mother, out and about, happens to sit at another's table and is given a cake or some meat, she will not eat it herself, but sets the taste aside and carries it home tucked away, to give to her child. Nine times in ten the child gets what it wants and is glad; the one time it does not, it turns petulant, crying and pretending to weep. A spoiled child who is unfilial will surely fall into the five transgressions; a filial child who bears no grudge will surely keep tender obedience.

So the child grows to adulthood, going about with friends, combing and dressing its hair, wanting fine clothes to cover its body — while its own clothes grow shabby and worn, the parents wear the old, coarse cotton themselves and give the new, fine silk to their child first. In every coming and going, on public duty or private urgent business, the parents' hearts lean south and north, chasing after their child east and west, watching over its very head. But once the child takes a wife and she in turn bears children of her own, the parents are gradually pushed aside; in their own private room, husband and wife speak and laugh only with each other. The parents, growing old, their strength failing, receive no visit and no inquiry after their welfare from morning to evening. Or else the father is left alone, or the mother widowed, guarding an empty room by herself like a guest lodging in someone else's house — always without love, and without even a quilt against the cold.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The character rendered here as "the five transgressions" (五㰅) is textually uncertain — 㰅 is a rare or nonstandard graph, and since the sūtra later uses the ordinary term 五逆, "the five heinous crimes," twice in almost identical phrasing, this is very likely a variant or corrupted writing of 逆 rather than a distinct term. CBETA's transcription preserves the character as it appears in the printed canon, and this translation follows suit rather than silently correcting it.

The scene that follows — parents favoring a growing child with new clothes while wearing the old themselves, then finding themselves pushed aside once the child marries — is the sūtra's central argument in narrative form: physical devotion flows overwhelmingly in one direction, from parent to child, across an entire lifetime, with no structural guarantee of return.

7. The Parents' Grief

Chinese Source

苦辛厄難遭之。甚年老色衰。多饒蟣虱。夙夜不臥。長呼歎息。何罪宿愆生此不孝之子。或時喚呼。瞋目驚怒。歸兒罵詈。低頭含笑。妻復不孝子。復五㰅夫妻和合同作五逆。彼時喚呼。急疾取使。十喚九違。盡不從順。罵詈瞋恚。不如早死。強在地上。父母聞之。悲哭懊惱。流淚雙下。啼哭目腫。汝初小時非吾不長。但吾生汝。不如本無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bitterness, hardship, and misfortune befall them all the more as they grow old and their looks fade — plagued with lice, unable to sleep through the night, sighing long sighs: "What crime, what fault from a past life, gave me such an unfilial child?" Sometimes, when the parent calls out, the child's eyes flare with anger; returning home, it curses and reviles them, all the while lowering its head and smiling in front of others. The wife, too, is unfilial; the child falls again into the five transgressions, husband and wife together committing the five heinous offenses. When the parent calls out urgently for some errand, nine times in ten the child refuses, disobedient in everything, cursing and raging: "Better you had died long ago than go on stubbornly clinging to this earth!" Hearing this, the parents weep in grief and anguish, tears streaming down both cheeks, crying until their eyes swell: "When you were small, without me you would never have grown — yet if I had simply never given birth to you at all, it would have been better than this."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The parents' complaint here is sharply, almost uncomfortably vivid for a Buddhist scripture — cursing, and a wish spoken aloud that the child had never been born. Genuine early Buddhist discourse literature rarely stages this kind of raw domestic grievance directly in a parent's own voice; the emotional register belongs more to vernacular storytelling than to doctrinal exposition, and is part of what makes the text so effective devotionally, whatever its origin. The "five heinous crimes" (五逆) named here — patricide, matricide, killing an arhat, wounding a buddha, and causing a schism in the monastic sangha — are the single gravest category of offense in Buddhist ethics; invoking them at exactly this point sets up the extraordinary claim made in the section that follows.

8. The Sūtra's Power, and Its Name

Chinese Source

佛告阿難。若善男子善女人。能為父母受持讀誦書寫父母恩重大乘摩訶般若波羅蜜經一句一偈。一逕耳目者所有五逆重罪悉得消滅。永盡無餘。常得見佛聞法。速得解脫。阿難從座而起。偏袒右肩。長跪合掌。前白佛言。世尊此經云何名之。云何奉持。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Ānanda: "If a good son or good daughter is able, for the sake of their parents, to uphold, read, recite, and copy out even a single phrase or a single verse of this Mahāyāna Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra on the Weighty Kindness of Parents, so that it passes but once through their ears and eyes, then every grave offense among the five heinous crimes they carry will be entirely dissolved, exhausted forever without remainder. They will constantly behold the Buddha and hear the Dharma, and swiftly attain liberation." Then Ānanda rose from his seat, bared his right shoulder, knelt with palms joined, and, coming before the Buddha, said: "World-Honored One, what is this sūtra to be called? How are we to uphold it?"

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Two details here are worth pausing on. First, the sūtra gives itself, mid-text, an extraordinarily inflated title: 父母恩重大乘摩訶般若波羅蜜經, "the Mahāyāna Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra on the Weighty Kindness of Parents" — folding in the name of the Perfection of Wisdom corpus, even though nothing else in this text touches emptiness, non-abiding, or any Prajñāpāramitā teaching at all. Two lines later, when Ānanda asks the sūtra's name directly, the Buddha instead gives the plain, short title actually used for this text ever since: 父母恩重經. The mismatch between these two names, within the space of a few lines of the same text, is exactly the kind of internal inconsistency scholars point to when arguing this sūtra was assembled from borrowed pieces rather than translated whole from a single Indic original.

Second, the claim itself is extraordinarily strong: that hearing even a single phrase of this text dissolves every one of the five heinous crimes — the gravest category of karmic offense recognized anywhere in Buddhist ethics — "entirely, without remainder." No canonical sūtra already on this site makes a claim of quite this scale for its own recitation; hyperbolic promises of merit like this one are a recognizable feature of the wider genre of Chinese devotional apocrypha discussed at more length in the essay above.

9. The Assembly's Response

Chinese Source

佛告阿難。此經名父母恩重經。若有一切眾生。能為父母作福造經燒香請佛禮拜供養三寶。或飲食眾僧。當知是人能報父母其恩。帝釋梵王諸天人民一切眾生聞經歡喜。發菩薩心。㘁哭動地。淚下如雨五體投地。信受頂禮佛足。歡喜奉行。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Ānanda: "This sūtra is called the Sūtra on the Weighty Kindness of Parents. If there is any sentient being at all who is able, for the sake of their parents, to make merit, produce scriptures, burn incense, invite the Buddha, bow in worship, and make offerings to the Three Jewels, or to feed the assembly of monks — know that this person is able to repay a parent's kindness." Śakra, Brahmā, all the gods and people, and every sentient being, hearing the sūtra, rejoiced and gave rise to the bodhisattva mind; they wailed until the earth shook, tears falling like rain, and prostrated their five limbs to the ground. With faith they received the teaching, bowed their heads at the Buddha's feet, and joyfully undertook to carry it into practice.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"This sūtra is called the Sūtra on the Weighty Kindness of Parents" — the short title actually used for this text ever since, in contrast to the inflated self-title given only two lines earlier. The closing scene otherwise follows the standard shape of a sūtra's ending: the assembly rejoices, generates the bodhisattva resolve, and receives the teaching with reverence. One detail still stands out: the assembly does not merely rejoice, but "[wails] until the earth shook, tears falling like rain" — an intensity of collective grief with no real parallel in the calmer, more composed closings of the canonical sūtras already on this site. It is a fitting final image for a text that argues, from its opening line to its last, that a parent's kindness is a debt too large to discharge quietly — whatever the truth of who actually wrote it down.

What does the Taishō canon's own classification of this sūtra tell us?

Question 2 of 8
What specific phrase in this sūtra is quoted almost verbatim from the Confucian Classic of Poetry (詩經)?
Correct: B. 昊天罔極 comes almost word for word from the poem 蓼莪 in the 詩經's "Lesser Odes" — a text centuries older than Chinese Buddhism, and one of the clearest pieces of internal evidence for this sūtra's Chinese authorship.
Question 3 of 8
Who was Yunqi Zhuhong, and what did he argue about this sūtra?
Correct: A. Zhuhong (1535–1615) addressed the text directly in a short essay titled 偽造父母恩重經, "On the Forged Sūtra on the Weighty Kindness of Parents" — a rebuke, not a line-by-line commentary.
Question 4 of 8
According to the sūtra, what does a spoiled, unfilial child fall into?
Correct: D. The rare character 㰅 is textually uncertain, but given the sūtra's later, plain use of 五逆 in nearly identical phrasing, it is almost certainly the same term.

The sūtra's first answer to Ānanda's question directly borrows a practice from which other, genuinely canonical text on this site?
