What a Layperson Should Never Do

This gate gathers five loosely related, thoroughly practical questions about where a layperson's proper conduct toward the Sangha actually stops: whether to invite monastics by personal preference or by impartial rotation, how far a layperson may go in using or dispensing monastic food and property, whether the laity may ever discipline a wayward monastic, how the Mahāyāna tradition closed a door the early Vinaya had left only provisionally open on eating meat, and why keeping a cat or dog to kill mice counts, in Vinaya terms, as cultivating a genuine "evil convention."

1. Milestone: Singling-Out Invitation versus Sangha-Order Invitation

This gate opens with a question that sounds procedural but is really about the layperson's own heart: when inviting monastics to a meal or a ceremony, should one invite by impartial rotation through the Sangha roster, or personally select which individuals to invite? The Procedural Commentary takes up both the definition of proper "Sangha-order invitation" and the Mahāyāna scripture that condemns its opposite.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『五分。僧次請者。凡夫、聖人、坐禪、誦經、勸佐眾事、並為解脫出家者、得入僧次。唯除惡戒人。 若言次第上座者是僧次攝、又不知齊幾為上座。佛言、上無人者皆名上座。以法取人、不稱名字、猶名別請、為佛所訶。』

資持釋云『初簡能受人。文列五種、並堪預數、不可揀擇。 若下二示僧次法又二。初正示。以下顯非以法取者雖不定名、簡其所學即非僧次。此謂雖通而別、非平等故。 十誦下三引文證。彼云。有人請佛五百羅漢。佛言。不名請僧福田。若能於僧中請一似像極惡比丘、猶得無量果報。與下增一意同。』見事鈔記卷二十三

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary (Xingshi Chao) states: "The Five-Part Vinaya says: those invited by Sangha-order invitation — ordinary worldlings, sages, those who sit in meditation, those who recite the sutras, those who assist with the assembly's affairs — all who have left the household life for liberation are admitted into the Sangha-order roster, with the sole exception of those of evil discipline. If it is said that inviting in seniority order still counts as Sangha-order invitation, one must also ask: up to what point does someone actually count as a 'senior'? The Buddha said: wherever there is no one more senior present, that person is called the senior. Selecting a person by a fixed standard, without even naming him by name, is nevertheless still called singling-out invitation, and is reproved by the Buddha."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "First, this distinguishes who is capable of receiving [the invitation]. The text lists five kinds, all of whom are entitled to be counted among those invited, and none of whom may be selectively excluded. What follows forms a second part, showing the method of Sangha-order invitation, itself in two parts: first, a direct demonstration; then, beginning 'selecting by a fixed standard...,' it shows what is false about it — even though such a person is not named individually, singling him out by his learning already fails to be Sangha-order invitation. This is what is meant by 'though it passes through everyone in general, it is still particular' — it fails to be truly equal. What follows, citing the Ten Recitations Vinaya, forms a third part, citing a further text as proof: it says that someone once invited the Buddha together with five hundred arhats, and the Buddha said this could not be called inviting the field of merit that is the Sangha; but if one is able to invite, from within the Sangha, even a single monk who merely resembles [a real monastic] and is utterly evil, one still obtains immeasurable reward. This agrees in meaning with the passage below from the Ekottarika [Āgama]." Seen in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 23.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary states further: "The Brahma Net Sutra says: property [given through] singling-out invitation is itself theft of property belonging to the Sangha of the ten directions. The Sutra for Humane Kings likewise reproves the fault of singling-out invitation. Since Sangha-order invitation carries the greater merit, whoever has occasion to invite [monastics] should explain the merit of Sangha-order invitation, so as to open and awaken the minds of the laity, and not allow singling-out invitation to occur."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "First, this cites the text to demonstrate the point. As for 'the Sutra for Humane Kings likewise reproves it' — that sutra says: those evil bhikṣus who accept singling-out invitations are practicing a non-Buddhist teaching, utterly not my own doctrine, and so on. What follows, beginning 'since...,' forms a second part, urging that Sangha-order invitation be praised and encouraged." Seen in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 38.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide draws out the exact shape of the distinction: Sangha-order invitation makes no selection at all — worldling or sage, someone who sits in meditation or someone who simply recites sutras or helps with communal chores, anyone who has genuinely left the household life for liberation is eligible, with the single exception of those of thoroughly evil discipline. Singling-out invitation, by contrast, is any invitation that deliberately picks out a particular kind of person — inviting only meditation masters, say, or only well-known Dharma teachers — even if no individual is ever named by name. The moment any selectivity enters in, it is no longer Sangha-order invitation, however it is dressed up.

The guide also notes how sharply the Brahma Net Sutra and the Sutra for Humane Kings condemn the alternative: singling-out invitation is treated as tantamount to stealing property that belongs to the whole Sangha of the ten directions, and accepting such an invitation is called, bluntly, a non-Buddhist practice. Because Sangha-order invitation is understood to carry the far greater store of merit, anyone in a position to arrange an invitation is urged to actively explain why — to open the ordinary lay mind to the logic of impartial giving, rather than let habit default to singling out whichever monastic is most familiar or most admired.

2. Milestone: The Lion Elder's Turn to Equal Giving

The Procedural Commentary illustrates the principle with a story about an elder named Siṃha, or "Lion," who learns the hard way that even generosity can be quietly self-defeating — and the section closes by resolving an apparent tension with the Five-Part Vinaya's own exclusion of monastics of evil discipline.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『增一云。師子長者別請五百羅漢。佛言、不如僧次一人、福不可量。因說如飲大海、則飲眾流。師子言、自今已後、當不別請。佛言、我亦不令別施、以無有福。師子便平等施、亦不言此持戒犯戒。佛讚善哉、平等之施獲福無量、平等施者施中第一。

賢愚經。以毯施佛、佛讓與僧、義意同此。正使將來法垂滅盡、比丘畜妻挾子、四人已上名字眾僧漫請供養、應當敬視如舍利弗等。』

資持釋云『前引增一。彼云、佛在羅閱城、長者請舍利弗目連等五百人。佛訶如鈔。飲大海者由心通僧寶、無所簡擇、雖得一人則為供養十方凡聖故。 賢愚中。姨母自紡績作一端金色毯上佛。佛言、可以施僧、得福無量。挾抱也。名字僧無實德者。』

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary states further: "The Ekottarika [Āgama] says: the elder Siṃha singled out five hundred arhats to invite personally. The Buddha said: this does not compare to inviting a single person by Sangha-order, whose merit cannot be measured. He then illustrated this with the image of drinking from the great ocean, which is thereby to drink every stream that flows into it. Siṃha said: from now on, I will no longer invite by singling out. The Buddha said: nor do I ask you to give without any discrimination at all among individuals, for that too would be without merit. Siṃha then gave with true equality, no longer asking whether this monk kept the precepts or that one had broken them. The Buddha praised him: excellent — a gift given with equality obtains immeasurable merit; whoever gives equally is foremost among all givers.

"In the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, [a woman] offered a blanket to the Buddha, and the Buddha in turn passed it on to the Sangha — the meaning here is the same. Even if, in a future age when the Dharma is on the verge of utter extinction, bhikṣus should keep wives and embrace children, so long as four or more of them still bear the name of 'Sangha' and are invited generally [by Sangha-order], one should still regard them with the same reverence as Śāriputra and the rest."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first part cites the Ekottarika. It says: the Buddha was in Rājagṛha, and the elder invited Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, and five hundred others. The Buddha's rebuke is as given in the Commentary. 'Drinking from the great ocean' means that because the mind opens through to the Jewel of the Sangha without any discrimination, even obtaining a single person is thereby to make offering to all the sages and worldlings of the ten directions. In the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish: [the Buddha's] maternal aunt spun the thread herself and wove a single length of golden-colored blanket to offer to the Buddha. The Buddha said: this may be given to the Sangha, and immeasurable merit will be obtained. 'Embrace' here means to hold in one's arms. 'Name-only Sangha' means those who bear the name but lack any real virtue."

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary says: "Question: the Five-Part Vinaya cited earlier excludes those of evil discipline — how does this not contradict [the passage just given]?

"Answer: the Commentary [on the Precept-Text, Jieshu] says: the Five-Part Vinaya's selection of persons is fine-grained; the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish's admission of persons is coarse-grained. Giving to one who has broken the precepts still takes hold of an outsider's arising faith, allowing him, within the great sea of the Sangha, to bring about for himself the merit of having given — it is not that a deficiency in conduct can somehow consume merit given in faith. This is the meaning within the Commentary. My own view is this: the Five-Part Vinaya's exclusion of those of evil discipline and the like is entirely a matter of the strictest admonition directed at the Sangha's own internal members, meant to spur them to discipline themselves. The Ekottarika, the Sūtra of the Wise and the Foolish, the Ten Recitations Vinaya, and the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts [also called the Sutra of Shansheng] are all, by contrast, teachings directed at guiding the laity, out of fear that they might carelessly show contempt toward the Sangha and thereby bring ruin upon themselves. From this we know: whether what one receives is pure or not is a matter the recipient must undoubtedly answer for himself; but giving must be universal and complete — if it is not complete, then what is given yields no merit at all. Judging the matter by this standard, there is truly no contradiction here." All of the above is found in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 23.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide highlights the pivot in Siṃha's own story: he first tries to fix his error of singling-out invitation by singling out differently — inviting only the five hundred arhats he most admired — and is corrected a second time. The Buddha's actual instruction is not to discriminate more carefully but to stop discriminating at all: give without even asking whether the recipient keeps the precepts or has broken them. What earns the praise "foremost among all givers" is precisely that indifference to credentials. The guide also flags the passage's most startling claim: even in an imagined future age so degenerate that "monks" openly keep wives and children, a group of four or more who still nominally form a Sangha, invited by rotation rather than singled out, should still be treated with the reverence due Śāriputra — a teaching the guide connects to a companion gate's instruction not to go looking for monastics' faults.

On the apparent tension with the Five-Part Vinaya's own exclusion of monastics of evil discipline, the guide's resolution is that the two texts are simply answering different questions for different audiences: the Five-Part Vinaya's exclusion is aimed inward, as a severe internal discipline meant to spur the Sangha's own members toward self-cultivation, while the other texts are aimed outward, at laypeople who might otherwise use a monastic's visible faults as an excuse to slight the Sangha as a whole and thereby harm themselves. Whether what a monastic receives is offered purely is that monastic's own karmic burden to carry; whether a donor's giving is truly universal, unrestricted by private judgment, is the donor's.

3. Milestone: The Proper Use of Sangha Food

Master Hongyi notes at the outset that this chapter's teachings are mostly addressed to monastics, warning them against giving Sangha food away carelessly — but he adds that laypeople, too, can learn from it exactly where the boundary lies on asking for or receiving such food themselves. The first section states the basic principle.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『佛法中無貴賤親疏。唯以有法平等、應同護之。人來乞索、一無與法。若隨情輒與、即壞法也。』

資持釋云『初勸依法。一無與者謂不非用也。心能依法、如下則聽。』

△事鈔續云『俗人本非應齋食者。然須借問。能齋、與食。不能齋者、示語因果、使信罪福、知非為吝、懷歡而退。此中非生人好處、非生人惡處。不得一向瞋人、一向任人不齋者而食。必須去情存道、善知處量也。

是以謹守佛教、慎護僧法、是第一慈悲人。現在未來一切眾生離苦得樂故。』

資持釋云『二示損益二、初守教之益中、初明善用。註云非生好者以任情故、非生惡者以依法故。 是下結益。』

上云齋者、如宗體篇八戒中委明。 白衣能受齋者得食、出五分律。

△事鈔續云『若不守佛教、隨情壞法。謂聽俗人不齋而食。有來乞請、隨請輒與。 令諸眾生不知道俗之分、而破壞僧法、毀損三歸。既無三歸、遠離三寶。令諸眾生沈沒罪河、流入苦海、失於利樂。皆由壞法。 是以不守佛教、不閑律藏、缺示群生、自昏時網、名第一無慈悲人也。』

資持釋云『二違教之損中、初明非用。 令下二示過、初明失利。後令諸下明墮苦。道俗分者道修智分為俗福田、俗修福分當供道眾。今則反亂故云不知。毀三歸者失彼信心、侵陵三寶故。 是下三結損。』已上皆見事鈔記卷七

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary states: "Within the Buddha Dharma there is no such thing as noble or base, kin or stranger. Only insofar as the Dharma is present should equal protection be extended accordingly. When someone comes begging or asking for something, there is no single fixed rule for giving to them. If one simply gives according to one's own feelings on the matter, this is precisely to destroy the Dharma."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "This first part urges reliance on the Dharma. 'There is no single rule for giving' means that one must not act wrongly through mere habit of use. If the mind is able to rely on the Dharma, then what follows will permit certain exceptions."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Laypeople are not, by their basic status, entitled to receive the food of a fast. Even so, one must first ask them: those able to keep the fast should be given food; those unable to keep it should be told of cause and effect, so that they come to trust in the reality of offense and blessing, understand that this is no mere stinginess, and withdraw with a glad heart. In none of this should the person be made to feel especially favored, nor especially disliked. One must not simply grow angry at people on the one hand, nor simply let everyone who is not fasting eat on the other. One must set personal feeling aside and hold to the Way, skillfully understanding how to weigh each situation.

"For this reason, one who carefully upholds the Buddha's teaching and prudently guards the Sangha's Dharma is the very foremost person of compassion — because it is for the sake of all sentient beings, present and future, that they should be freed from suffering and attain happiness."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "This second part shows the loss and the benefit, in two parts; the first, on the benefit of upholding the teaching, first clarifies its proper use. The note 'not especially favored' means one has not simply followed personal feeling; 'not especially disliked' means one has acted according to the Dharma. What follows draws the conclusion as to the benefit."

[The term] "fast" mentioned above is treated in full within the section on the eight precepts, in the Root and Essence Fascicle. That a layperson able to keep the fast may receive food comes from the Five-Part Vinaya.

The Procedural Commentary continues: "If one fails to uphold the Buddha's teaching and, following personal feeling, destroys the Dharma — meaning that one permits laypeople who are not fasting to eat anyway, and whenever anyone comes begging, simply gives to them as asked — this causes all sentient beings to lose their sense of the distinction between the monastic and the lay, thereby ruining the Sangha's Dharma and damaging the three refuges. Once the three refuges are lost, one grows distant from the Three Jewels. This causes all sentient beings to sink into the river of offense and flow out into the sea of suffering, forfeiting benefit and happiness — all of this arising from the destruction of the Dharma. For this reason, one who fails to uphold the Buddha's teaching, is not versed in the Vinaya-piṭaka, fails in his duty to instruct all beings, and darkens his own understanding within the net of the age, is called the very foremost person without compassion."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "This second part, on the harm of violating the teaching, first clarifies its improper use. What follows, beginning 'causes...,' forms a second part, showing the fault: it first clarifies the loss of benefit, and what follows, beginning 'causes all... [to sink],' clarifies the falling into suffering. 'The distinction between the monastic and the lay' means: the monastic cultivates wisdom and thereby serves as a field of merit for the laity, while the laity cultivates merit and thereby ought to make offerings to the monastic assembly. Here the two are reversed and thrown into confusion, hence it speaks of 'not knowing' [the distinction]. 'Damaging the three refuges' means the loss of that person's own faith, and an encroachment upon the Three Jewels. What follows, beginning 'for this reason...,' forms a third part, drawing the conclusion as to the loss." All of the above is found in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 7.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide frames Master Hongyi's opening remark as the key to reading this entire chapter: although every passage in it is technically addressed to monastics — warning them not to give Sangha food away carelessly — a layperson reading over their shoulder learns exactly the same boundary from the other side, namely that Sangha food may not simply be asked for, or received, without regard for whether one is actually entitled to it.

On the core principle itself, the guide underscores that a layperson who cannot keep the fast should not simply be turned away in irritation, nor simply be handed food regardless — both extremes are named as failures. The correct response is to explain honestly, with reference to cause and effect, that this is a matter of principle rather than stinginess, so the person can withdraw without resentment. The guide reads the chapter's closing contrast as its real payload: whoever upholds this distinction carefully is called, without exaggeration, "the foremost person of compassion," precisely because getting this boundary right protects both the Sangha's own integrity and the layperson's genuine understanding of the difference between monastic and lay roles — while whoever collapses the distinction by handing out Sangha food indiscriminately is called, just as bluntly, "the foremost person without compassion," since doing so quietly destroys the very framework of the three refuges for everyone involved.

4. Milestone: Circumstantial Exceptions

Having stated the basic principle, the Commentary immediately turns to concrete situations where the ordinary rule bends — an uninformed visitor's resentment, the ordinary costs of provisioning officials, and the sharper question of whether "usefulness" to the Sangha ever justifies giving away its property.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『五分。若白衣入寺、僧不與食、便起嫌心。佛言、應與。便持惡器、盛食與之、又生嫌心。佛言、以好器與之。 此謂悠悠俗人見僧過者。若在家二眾及識達俗士、須說福食難消、非為慳吝。』

資持釋云『初引文。 此下義決、初指前文且據無信。悠悠謂遠離三寶、無所歸者。若下次明有信。福食謂檀越求福、施眾僧故。』

△事鈔續云『十誦。供給國王大臣薪火燈燭、聽輒用十九錢不須白僧、若更索者白僧給之。惡賊來至、隨時將擬、不限多少。 僧祇、若惡賊檀越工匠乃至國王大臣、有力能損益者、應與飲食。多論云、能損者與之、有益者不合即是汙家。若彼此知法、如律亦得。』

資持釋云『初引十誦明用分齊、初給王臣。十九錢者彼土大銅錢、一當十六、當今三百也。下明賊難。不可約數。 次僧祇中。通列五人、上三可解。下示王臣須論勢力、必無力者應非所開。多下引決。由上律云損益皆與、既是有益理不當與、在文有了故續決之。若下通上律。俗知僧物難消必無虛受、僧知汙家非法必無妄與。但有緣須給、微亦通之十誦通開、諒在於此。』已上皆見事鈔記卷十七

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary states: "The Five-Part Vinaya says: if a layman enters the monastery and the Sangha does not give him food, he grows resentful. The Buddha said: they should give to him. But if the food is then given to him in a shabby vessel, he again grows resentful. The Buddha said: give it to him in a fine vessel.

"This refers to the case of an unattached, ordinary layperson who sees only the Sangha's apparent fault. But if the person is one of the two kinds of lay disciples in the household life, or a discerning and cultivated layperson, one must explain that food obtained through blessing is difficult to digest [without corresponding virtue] — this is not a matter of stinginess."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "First, this cites the text. What follows resolves its meaning: it first points out that the preceding text concerns specifically one without faith. 'Unattached' means one who is distant from the Three Jewels and has nowhere to take refuge. What follows next clarifies the case of one who does have faith. 'Food obtained through blessing' means food given by a donor in search of merit, offered to the assembled Sangha."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The Ten Recitations Vinaya: in providing kings and great ministers firewood, fire, lamps, and candles, one is permitted to use up to nineteen coins on one's own authority without needing to inform the Sangha; if more is required than this, the Sangha should be informed and then provide it. Should fierce bandits arrive, one may respond as the moment demands, without any fixed limit.

"The Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya: as for fierce bandits, donors, craftsmen, and even kings and great ministers — anyone with the power to either harm or benefit [the Sangha] — food and drink should be given to them. The Sarvāstivāda Treatise (Duo Lun) says: one who has the power to cause harm should be given to; one who has already brought benefit should not additionally be given to, for this would itself be 'polluting the household.' But where both parties understand the Dharma, this too may be permitted, as in the Vinaya."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "First, this cites the Ten Recitations Vinaya to clarify the proper limit of use — first, providing for kings and ministers. 'Nineteen coins' refers to the large bronze coins of that land, where one was equal to sixteen [smaller units], amounting to roughly three hundred in coins of the present day. What follows clarifies the danger of bandits, where no fixed number can be given. Next, in the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, five kinds of persons are listed together; the first three are readily understood. What follows shows that, in the case of kings and ministers, their actual power must be taken into account — where someone genuinely lacks such power, this permission should not apply to him. What follows, citing the Sarvāstivāda Treatise, settles the matter: since the Vinaya passage above speaks of giving in either case, whether harmful or beneficial, and it stands to reason that one who has already brought benefit ought not to be given to, the text was not yet complete, and so [the Sarvāstivāda Treatise] goes on to settle it further. What follows, beginning 'but where...,' reconciles this with the Vinaya passage above: a layperson who understands that the Sangha's property is difficult to properly digest will certainly not receive it without real cause, and a monastic who understands that 'polluting the household' is improper will certainly not give it away carelessly — but wherever there is genuine occasion requiring provision, even a small amount may still be permitted through it; this is surely the reasoning behind the Ten Recitations Vinaya's general allowance." All of the above is found in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 17.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide organizes these exceptions around a single distinction the Commentary keeps returning to: an uninformed, unattached layperson who reacts to refusal with plain resentment should simply be given food, and in a decent vessel, precisely because he does not yet understand what is being protected — but a knowledgeable lay Buddhist should instead be told the truth honestly, that food obtained through blessing is genuinely hard to "digest" without matching virtue, which is not stinginess but simple accuracy.

For provisioning officials, the guide notes the Commentary sets a workable small daily allowance that needs no special sign-off, while emergencies such as a bandit raid override any fixed limit altogether. The sharper case is the "harm versus benefit" principle: give to whoever currently has the power to harm the Sangha, since that gift may forestall real damage, but do not give anything extra to whoever has already brought benefit, since that crosses over into currying favor — precisely the fault of "polluting the household" taken up next. The guide reads the final qualification, that mutual understanding of the Dharma can still permit an exception either way, as the Commentary's own acknowledgment that no rule this practical can be applied by rote; it still requires judgment.

5. Milestone: "Polluting the Household"

The chapter's sharpest warning is reserved for monastics who take property given to them in faith and pass it along to laypeople — currying favor with the very people who are supposed to be seeking merit from the Sangha, not the reverse. Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide identifies two distinct harms in "polluting the household." First, it wrecks the original donor's equal-mindedness: the person who happens to receive the redirected gift feels warmly toward the monastic, while everyone else — even someone genuinely virtuous — feels overlooked, so the monastic's own careless generosity ends up costing that other person a field of merit that was rightfully theirs. Second, and more fundamentally, it inverts the whole relationship the Dharma depends on: laypeople are supposed to seek merit from the Sangha, sacrificing their own resources to plant good roots, not the other way around; a monastic who instead showers gifts on laypeople teaches them, in effect, to expect favors from monastics rather than to give to them.

The guide also stresses the passage's closing comparison as its real point: even redirecting Sangha property toward visibly pious ends — building a stūpa, funding a monastery, supplying every sage in the world — still falls short of simply sitting quietly and keeping the precepts purely, which alone counts as authentic offering to the Dharma-body. On the named exceptions, the guide notes Master Hongyi's own careful qualification on giving to parents: it is permitted only where the parents are genuinely poor and have already taken the refuges and precepts themselves — not a blanket allowance for family generosity.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide walks through the Sūtra of the Ten Wheels' four vivid analogies for why a precept-breaking monastic still deserves protection rather than punishment: an ox's bezoar is still harvested after the ox is dead; musk remains useful after the musk-deer dies; a merchant's story tells of medicine compounded, gruesomely, from a slaughtered creature's eyes, yet still able to restore a blind person's sight; and incense, even as its physical substance is consumed by burning, still perfumes everything around it. The point in each case is the same: some residual efficacy survives even after the thing that produced it is, in effect, spent — and a monastic's "residual power" of the precepts he once kept works the same way, which is why the sūtra concludes that laypeople should protect, never punish, such a person.

Against this, the guide sets the Nirvāṇa and Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtras' opposite instruction: kings and officials are told they must strictly correct anyone who grows lax, breaks the precepts, and actively destroys the true Dharma, and that protecting a single Dharma-abiding monastic outweighs protecting any number of evil ones. The starkest warning is reserved for a ruler who lets a Buddhist disciple's own wealth and power shield him from correction — this, the sūtra says, amounts to severing the very lineage of the Three Jewels and blinding sentient beings to the truth, a loss so severe it cancels out even a lifetime's accumulated merit from precepts and giving.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide summarizes the resolution as a distinction of kind rather than degree: the Ten Wheels Sutra's ban on lay correction applies to a monastic who is inwardly flawed but still outwardly credible — someone broadly learned enough that leaving him alone still draws people toward faith — while the Nirvāṇa and Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtras' call for strict correction applies to someone hopelessly confused, unable to tell right from wrong, whose visible misconduct actively opens what the text calls the "three evil gateways" for monastic and lay alike. The first kind of person is worth protecting for the good he still does; the second is doing active harm that protecting him would only extend.

The guide also draws attention to the secondary argument offered almost as a fallback: since the Nirvāṇa Sūtra is traditionally regarded as the Buddha's final and most authoritative teaching, the settled definitive statement to which his teaching-life as a whole returns, its instruction can simply be taken as controlling wherever an earlier text disagrees — with the Commentary adding, almost as an aside, that readers are free to hold both interpretations at once, or to set the earlier text aside outright, since either approach reaches the same practical conclusion. The guide treats this brief passage as something of a template for how to read the Vinaya literature generally: an apparent contradiction between scriptures is not automatically evidence that one side is simply wrong.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra says: there are immeasurable causes and conditions on account of which one should not eat meat. Briefly stated, there are ten. First, all sentient beings have, since beginningless time, constantly been one's own six classes of kin; because one should regard them with this thought of kinship, one should not eat meat. Second, [the meat of] foxes, dogs, humans, and horses is mixed together and sold indiscriminately by butchers. Third, it is generated and grows from impure emanations. Fourth, sentient beings who smell its odor are all filled with fear. Fifth, it prevents the practitioner from giving rise to a compassionate mind. Sixth, it is what the foolish and ignorant habitually practice, foul-smelling and impure, without any good reputation attached to it. Seventh, it prevents the successful accomplishment of mantra practice. Eighth, through eating meat, seeing its form gives rise to consciousness, becoming attached through defiled taste. Ninth, it is what the gods themselves reject, it brings on many evil dreams, and it draws tigers and wolves by its scent. Tenth, because of eating one kind of meat after another, one eventually comes to devour human flesh, as told in the Story of King Kalmāṣapāda."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The second part cites the Laṅkāvatāra to show the faults [of meat-eating]. The ten faults within the Laṅkāvatāra most require careful contemplation. The first fears that one might come to devour one's own parents, thereby committing a grave transgression against them; the Brahma Net Sutra says: all men and women without exception have, at some time, been my own father or mother, since I have, life after life, without exception, received birth through them — so to kill and eat them is precisely to kill one's own parents. The second fears eating one's own kind, which is contrary to a humane mind. The third means that the meat is formed from the mingled reproductive fluids and blood of mating birds and beasts — a foul, rank, and impure substance, taken into the mouth from outside oneself — the act of one deeply impure; hence, though it may be eaten, it earns no good reputation. The seventh means that one who holds and practices mantras must be pure and undefiled; if even pungent [root vegetables] are prohibited to such a person, how much more so meat. The eighth means that whenever one encounters the form of an animal, one's thoughts immediately turn to devouring its flavor. The ninth contains three faults together: the gods' own reward is pure, and this is why they reject it; one who does not practice wholesome dharmas accordingly has many evil dreams; and one whose body carries the same odor as an animal's is accordingly preyed upon by tigers and wolves. The tenth means that, through this mutual causation, one eventually comes to devour one's own kind. As for King Kalmāṣapāda: his father, while hunting in the mountains, mated with a lion and thereby fathered him, so that he was born with a human form but spotted feet. He later succeeded to the throne. One day, when the palace cook lacked meat, he obtained the flesh of a small child to make up the shortfall. The king noticed the unusual flavor and thereupon decreed that it be regularly supplied. As the killing grew ever more extensive, the people sought to kill the king in turn; the king then transformed into a flying rākṣasa, and for twelve years continually devoured human flesh."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "There are now many foolish and ignorant people who greatly relish meat of every kind. Among all offenses, none is graver than this. For it is precisely because those who butcher and sell meat find that meat-eaters would otherwise certainly have none to eat that they refrain from butchering at all. Hence one should understand: the eater shares equally in the very karma of the butcher, and is implicated together with the one who takes life. How could this fail to be a warning?"

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The third part, in delivering this warning, first clarifies the gravity of the offense, as already set out above. What follows, beginning 'for it is precisely because...,' next clarifies the depth of the karma involved — because it is identical to the karma of the one who does the actual killing." All of the above is found in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 33.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's parable — a starving couple forced to eat their own child, choking down the food through tears, unable to taste it — as the emotional center of the whole section: if one can genuinely learn to see every being's flesh through that same lens, the craving to eat it should simply stop on its own. The guide is careful to note what the Commentary is doing structurally here, which is a distinctively Nanshan move: treating the earlier Vinaya's allowance of "the three kinds of clean meat" not as a mistake to be embarrassed about, but as a provisional, compassionate expedient that the Mahāyāna's final teaching was always meant to supersede.

On the Laṅkāvatāra's ten reasons, the guide highlights the culminating story of King Kalmāṣapāda — born from his father's union with a lion, later developing an uncontrollable taste for human flesh after a single accidental meal, and ultimately transforming into a flesh-eating demon — as a deliberately extreme cautionary tale about where an unchecked appetite for meat can lead if indulged rather than examined. The guide's own summary line lands hard: once a habit of eating flesh is fully formed, its hunger recognizes no natural stopping point at all.

9. Milestone: Even the Hinayana Vinaya Tightens the Rule

Having established the Mahāyāna position, the Commentary turns back to the Hinayana Vinaya texts themselves and shows that, read carefully, even they leave almost no room left for licitly eating meat at all — while reserving its harshest words for those who invoke "Mahāyāna freedom" as cover for indulgence.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『僧祇云。若為比丘殺者、一切七眾不應食。乃至為優婆夷殺、七眾不食亦爾。』

資持釋云『初引僧祇急制。為一眾殺而制七眾者、以同沾佛戒、意所通故。彼律得食三種淨肉、謂不見不聞不疑為我故殺者。是知雖云得食、還同禁斷。下引四分、其意益明。』

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary states: "The Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya says: if an animal is killed for the sake of a bhikṣu, none of the sevenfold assembly should eat of it; and likewise, if it is killed for the sake of even a single upāsikā, none of the sevenfold assembly should eat of it either."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "First, this cites the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya's strict prohibition. That killing done for the sake of a single individual should result in a prohibition binding the entire sevenfold assembly is because all equally share in the same precepts of the Buddha, and the meaning extends to all alike. That Vinaya does permit the eating of the three kinds of pure meat — meaning meat from an animal one has not seen, not heard, and has no reason to suspect was killed specifically for oneself. From this we can see: although it is said that such meat may be eaten, this in practice amounts to the same thing as an outright prohibition. What follows, citing the Four-Part Vinaya, makes this meaning still clearer."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Those who study the Vinaya today, for the most part, do not eat meat, matching the same practice as the Mahāyāna monastics of the Middle Land [i.e., India].

"There are those who, while claiming to study the Mahāyāna, make use of wine and meat as though this were itself spiritual practice and understanding. Such persons are received by neither the Mahāyāna nor the Hīnayāna teaching, and have of themselves entered into the ranks of butchers. Even heavenly demons and non-Buddhist ascetics do not drink wine or eat meat — such a person is nothing more than an underling of King Yama."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The second part rebukes those who study but disregard the times, first clarifying the case of those who study the Vinaya. Those in the Middle Land who study the Mahāyāna all follow the regulations of the Brahma Net Sutra, the Laṅkāvatāra, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, and the like. Having already undertaken the great practice, with compassionate deliverance placed first, how could there be a 'Mahāyāna' that then goes on to commit killing? What follows, beginning 'there are...,' singles out such people for censure. 'Practice' here means doing such a thing without any shame; 'understanding' means clinging to it without any doubt. That 'neither teaching receives' them means that both the Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna equally prohibit this, and that, on the contrary, they fail to follow either teaching's actual practice, so that neither teaching's protection extends to them. Since neither teaching's protection extends to them, they are not disciples of the Buddha at all — being without compassion and fond of killing, it is fitting that they enter the ranks of butchers. Heavenly demons enjoy a superior reward, brought about by pure causes; non-Buddhist ascetics practice austerities such as subsisting on wind or starving themselves. Hence we know: meat-eaters do not even rise to the level of demons or non-Buddhist ascetics — they are truly of the same kind as underlings of King Yama. 'Underlings' refers to yakṣas, ghost-soldiers, and the like."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The Four-Part Vinaya says: if the one doing the killing thereby carries out one of the ten evil deeds, killing for one's own sake, even including meat from a great sacrificial rite, one is not permitted to eat it — because the mind of the one who prepared and brought it has no fixed, settled object [i.e., one cannot be certain it was not killed for oneself]. Since butchers nowadays kill indiscriminately for the general market, it follows, according to this teaching, that there is no meat at all that may be eaten — this is precisely to cut off meat-eating outright.

"The Vinaya says: if a person upholds the ten wholesome deeds, that person would never, in the end, take the life of any being for one's own sake — meat from such a person alone could be eaten. Judging by this standard, how could one ever actually obtain meat to eat at all?"

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The third part reveals the Four-Part Vinaya's hidden prohibition, first clarifying the prohibition itself: both 'for one's own sake' and 'a great sacrificial rite' refer to meat that is impure in either case. What follows, beginning 'since...,' reveals the intended meaning. What follows next clarifies the permitted case of eating: the one who gives the meat must be a person who practices the ten wholesome deeds — but how could there ever actually be such a person, who practices the ten wholesome deeds and yet still has meat to give? Hence it is said, 'how could one ever actually obtain meat to eat,' and so on." All of the above is found in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 33.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide walks through the logical trap the Commentary sets here: the Vinaya nominally still permits "the three kinds of pure meat" — from an animal the eater did not see, did not hear, and has no reason to suspect was killed specifically for him — yet extending a prohibition meant for one person to the entire sevenfold assembly already shows the rule tightening toward an effective ban. The Four-Part Vinaya then closes the last gap: since ordinary butchers kill indiscriminately for the open market rather than for any one identifiable buyer, no one can ever actually verify that a given piece of meat meets even the narrow "three kinds of pure meat" standard — which means, as the Commentary bluntly concludes, there is in practice no meat left that could licitly be eaten at all.

The guide reads the passage's sharpest language as aimed at a specific, familiar target: people who invoke "Mahāyāna" open-mindedness to justify drinking and eating meat as though doing so were itself some kind of advanced spiritual attainment. The Commentary's verdict is unsparing — such a person is rejected by both Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna teaching alike, ranks below even non-Buddhist ascetics and heavenly demons who at least abstain from wine and meat, and belongs, in effect, among the underlings of King Yama. The guide notes this remains a pointed rebuke of any modern version of the same self-serving logic.

10. Milestone: Other Prohibited Foods and Materials

The chapter on eating meat closes by widening its scope slightly, to the five pungent roots the Laṅkāvatāra pairs with wine and meat, and then, in Master Hongyi's own added note, to the Nanshan tradition's further restrictions on silk, milk, and honey.

The Zichi Commentary explains: "Wine and meat here are paired together with the five pungent roots, though the text is missing [the fifth, called] hing. Moreover, within the Nanshan Vinaya tradition, silk garments [made from cultivated silkworm cocoons] are strictly forbidden, and milk and honey are permitted only in cases of serious illness, never to be drunk merely at will. Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide notes that the five pungent roots — scallion, garlic, leek, shallot, and a fifth (hing, or asafoetida) missing from this particular text — are prohibited alongside wine and meat for reasons that are physiological as much as spiritual: the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's claim that they arouse lust when cooked and anger when eaten raw, and its striking image of heavenly beings recoiling from the smell while hungry ghosts are drawn to lick the eater's own lips, regardless of how eloquently that same person can expound the sūtras.

On Master Hongyi's own addition, the guide reads his vivid phrasing — taking milk compared to imprisoning a calf to keep its mother's milk for oneself, taking honey compared to robbing a hive — as extending the same underlying logic one step further: these are still cases of taking something produced by another creature's labor or body, even though the guide is careful to note Hongyi's own scaling of the offense, ranking it nowhere near as grave as outright hunting or slaughter, and reserving milk and honey's use strictly for cases of serious illness rather than everyday consumption.

11. Milestone: Keeping Cats and Dogs as an "Evil Convention"

The gate's final sub-topic turns to something closer to home for most laypeople than robes or almsfood: whether keeping a cat or dog specifically to kill mice or rats is compatible with the precepts at all — and, if one already keeps such an animal, what to actually do about it.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『或畜貓狗、專擬殺鼠。是惡律儀。 雜心云、惡律儀者、流注相續成也。善生成論、若受惡律儀、則失善戒。』

資持釋云『初顯過。 雜下引示。流注者惡業遍也。相續者念念增也。所以然者、由彼害心非止一境、復無時限故。 善生成論失善戒者惡勢強也。惡業順惑、勢力猛盛、一切善戒並絕相續故。有云、且望一類鼠上、善戒不續、非謂餘戒俱無。又云、此乃誡勒之切耳。』見事鈔記卷七

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary states: "Some people keep cats and dogs specifically in order to have them kill mice and rats — this constitutes an 'evil convention.'

"The Miscellaneous Abhidharma Treatise on the Heart says: what is called an 'evil convention' is formed through a continuous, unbroken outflow [of killing-intent]. The Sutra on Upasaka Precepts [also called the Sutra of Shansheng] and the *Tattvasiddhi Śāstra both say: whoever takes on an evil convention thereby loses all wholesome precepts."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first part reveals the fault. What follows, citing the Miscellaneous [Abhidharma Treatise on the] Heart, explains it further: 'outflow' means that the unwholesome karma pervades everything; 'continuous' means that it increases with every single thought, moment after moment. The reason this is so is that such a killing intent is not confined to a single object, and moreover has no limit in time. As for the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts and the *Tattvasiddhi's statement that one thereby loses wholesome precepts: this is because the force of the evil intention is so strong — unwholesome karma, following and reinforcing the underlying affliction, grows so fierce and overpowering that every wholesome precept is thereby cut off from continuing at all. Some say this refers only to the wholesome precept as it specifically concerns this one class of being, namely mice, and not that every other precept is thereby lost as well; others say that this is simply the sharp edge of an admonition meant to warn and restrain." Seen in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 7.

The Treatise for Weighing Light and Heavy Offenses states: "Keeping and raising cats and dogs specifically in order to carry out killing — the sūtras and treatises judge this to be an 'evil convention'; simply keeping such an animal already causes one to lose one's wholesome precepts. Selling it on to someone else still involves a living creature, and only makes the resulting karmic obstruction deeper still. Giving it away to another likewise only continues its killing intent, and in the end only ties the knot of entanglement more tightly. It is best to release it into the deep wilds, and let it come and go as it will. Should one feel compelled to keep it tied at a visible post, this only increases its burden of forced labor. Simply following the judgment given above, both parties — oneself and the animal — are set at peace with one another: this thereby extinguishes any house of mutual enmity built on killing, and newly establishes, in its place, a sacred abode of compassion." Seen in the Treatise for Weighing Light and Heavy Offenses, fascicle 1.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide dwells on the phrase "continuous, unbroken outflow" as the technical heart of why keeping a mouse-hunting cat or dog counts as an "evil convention" rather than an ordinary lapse: unlike a single act of killing, the intent behind keeping such an animal is not confined to any one occasion and has no built-in stopping point, so it keeps compounding, thought after thought, for as long as the arrangement continues. The guide notes some disagreement among commentators over how far the resulting loss of "wholesome precepts" actually extends — whether it is limited specifically to precepts touching that one class of victim, mice, or something broader — but reads both readings as agreeing that the warning's real purpose is simply to be strict enough to actually change behavior.

On the Treatise for Weighing Light and Heavy Offenses' practical guidance, the guide is direct about what it rules out: selling the animal on only deepens one's own karmic entanglement, since a living being is still being traded away, and giving it to someone else does not resolve anything either, since the same killing intent simply continues under new ownership. The only real resolution the text offers is release into the wild, or, where that is not possible, simply not adding to the animal's burden of enforced "hunting duty" — a small, concrete step the guide frames as turning what would otherwise remain a lasting enmity between hunter and hunted into something closer to a household actually built on compassion.
