Where the Precepts Actually Live

Every one of the fourteen gates in this compendium ultimately serves one question, but this gate asks it directly: at the moment of ordination, what actually happens? What is the thing — invisible, untouchable, yet as real as anything else a person carries — that gets generated in the mind and thereafter constitutes "having precepts" at all? Dao Xuan works through three rival Buddhist schools in turn, the Sarvāstivādin Substantialists, the Dharmaguptaka/Tattvasiddhi Nominalists whose lineage this very text belongs to, and finally a Mahayana-inflected Consummate Teaching — before Master Hongyi tells us, in the margin, which of the three he actually believes.

1. Milestone: The Gate's Four Chapters, and Why It Matters

Before any doctrine is stated, Dao Xuan lays out how this gate is organized — four chapters moving from what the precept-essence actually is, to how its "receiving" moment differs from day-to-day "ongoing practice," to how wide a field of beings it must reach, to how many individual precepts it actually generates. Master Hongyi's own compendium then explains, in its first line of commentary, why a chapter on something this abstract is worth the reader's patience at all: almost nobody who receives the precepts actually understands what they received.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Precept-Essence divides into four chapters: (1) the marks of the precept-essence; (2) sameness and difference between reception and ongoing practice; (3) the breadth of the object conditioned; (4) the quantity of precepts generated.

The Zichi Commentary states: "The precept-essence [section comprises] four chapters. The first two chapters discuss the essence itself, though conduct is already implicated within the second. The third and fourth belong to its application, and the fourth also touches on the precept-marks. This is stated roughly in passing; the fuller elaboration follows below."

Although this section is headed "the precept-essence," it in fact touches on the precept-Dharma, precept-conduct, and precept-marks all together, precisely to show that the four gates mutually include one another.

The Procedural Commentary states: "The reason the prātimokṣa precepts, though received by everyone alike, are actually understood clearly by only three or five people in a hundred is entirely that they had no broad grounding beforehand, and did not consult widely. As a result, at the very moment of formal reception, most people remain confused about the essence and its marks, and the whole event is completed as though blind, or as in a dream. And when it comes to whether they actually obtained the precepts or not, they are as lost as if gazing across the Milky Way. This is exactly why, at the very head of the chapters on the precept-marks, these several gates are set forth — so that one may determine for oneself whether one has actually obtained the precepts, and only then take up the practice of keeping and transgressing them." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "'Blind' is a metaphor for not knowing; 'dream' is a metaphor for what is unreal. 'The Milky Way' is a metaphor for being utterly lost, unable to see any shore." [Xingshi Chao Ji, fasc. 16]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide frames the whole gate around one image: the precept-essence is what gets "received into the heart" at ordination — invisible and untouchable, yet real enough to keep functioning afterward, restraining wrongdoing every day that follows. Before receiving it, the mind is the active party, taking the Dharma in; after receiving it, the relationship flips — the Dharma becomes what the mind now depends on, and conduct proceeds from there. This gate's whole business is pinning down what, exactly, that "it" is, how it arises, and how it keeps working once it has.

The guide also underlines Dao Xuan's blunt diagnosis for why this gate exists at all: precept ordination is nearly universal among Buddhists, but genuine understanding of what was received is rare — most people leave the ordination platform not actually knowing whether anything happened to them at all. That diagnosis is the entire reason this gate — by far the most demanding in the whole compendium — earns its size.

2. Milestone: The Mind That Receives, Before the Essence Itself

Chapter One opens with a deliberate choice: rather than first defining what the precept-essence objectively is, Dao Xuan first describes what kind of mind is capable of receiving it at all — because, the Zichi Commentary insists, whether ordination actually succeeds turns entirely on this "mark of the receiving mind," and nothing else in the literature explains it as fully.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Chapter One: The Marks of the Precept-Essence. This divides into two sections: (1) the mark of the receiving mind; (2) the generated karma-essence. Section One: the mark of the receiving mind.

The Procedural Commentary states: "As for the precept-essence — if one speaks of it in general terms, one explains the karma-essence that is thereby generated; but here, addressing it directly, I state plainly the mark of the mind that is able to receive it." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The opening sentence points to both sides at once. What is 'generated' is precisely the unmanifest [karma]. 'Mark' means the mind's own configuration."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "What is meant is this: with respect to the dust-mote-and-sand-grain-like multiplicity of the Dharma-realm and its various teachings such as the two truths, one takes one's own vow and undertakes the expedient means [of the ordination ritual]; one makes the mind's own vessel wholesome and pure, resolved never to do evil, weighing one's intention with clear wisdom; and one's mind silently merges with the Dharma set before it. This vowing mind then corresponds with that sublime Dharma, and with respect to that Dharma there occurs the sense of a condition arising — and this being received and held within the mind is called the precept-essence." The Zichi Commentary explains at length: the opening lines establish the "measure" of the precepts — the Dharma-realm names the ten realms as environment and inhabitant together, "dust-motes and sand-grains" is a metaphor for their sheer multiplicity, and "the two truths" names what the Buddha himself established as teaching. From "with one's own" onward, the mind's actual configuration is explained: the first clause is the vow taken for the whole of one's remaining life; the next clause covers the bodily and verbal karma of reverent bowing and reciting the words [of the ordination formula]; "wholesome" refers to banishing false thoughts; "weighing" refers to the correspondence between mind and Dharma — this "weighing intention" is the very root from which karma is accomplished and the cause by which the precepts are obtained, whichever of the three grades of mind happens to arise; "clear wisdom" means reflecting on mind and object exactly as principle and teaching require, rather than inverted thinking that falsely clings to the object before one. The passage above concerns how the mind is used; what follows concerns how it unites with the Dharma. Because the mind that arises must range universally over dust-mote-and-sand-grain-like objects, the Dharma too, being regulated by its object, is universal in measure; the mind arises following the Dharma, the Dharma being vast, the mind too is all-pervading — mind and Dharma correspond, fitting together exactly like a lid on its vessel, which is why this is called "silently merging." The Dharma, properly speaking, still resides in its object; because the mind faces toward it, it is called "the Dharma set before it" — the same holds, below, for "that Dharma." From "with this" onward, a third point shows how the essence is received, itself in three stages: the first two clauses pick up the "silent merging" just mentioned; from "with respect to" onward it shows how the Dharma arises following the mind — the Dharma, being non-sentient, only arises through being conditioned by mind, and so in turn follows the mind; hence, at the moment the essence is received, the Dharma first stirs in its object, next gathers in space, and only then enters the mind — and because the Dharma depends on mind, it is called "the Dharma-essence." The final clause shows where the essence actually resides: strictly by category, the essence itself is "not mind," and does not disclose what it depends on — the essence and the mind [are distinct]. But to say here that it "resides in the mind" is to adopt the Consummate meaning — namely, that the store-consciousness itself is pointed to as its very locus.

The Zichi Commentary asks: "Why not simply point directly to the unmanifest [karma], instead of explaining the mind's mark?" And answers: "The receiving mind is precisely what is essential to the generation of the essence — this alone is explained at length here, since nowhere else is it addressed. For it is only by examining the original source from which the Dharma is obtained that one can determine whether one's own reception has actually succeeded. Should one remain confused on this very point, what more is there left to say? Some receive it with an indeterminate mind wandering at random, some with a merely ordinary scattering of virtue; a whole lifetime passes in a fog, and one remains hesitant to the very end [about whether one has any precepts at all] — yet the sage's own words carry real intent on this point. This is exactly why it is pointed out here — the meaning is not idle." [Xingshi Chao Ji, fasc. 3]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide singles out three conditions bundled into this one dense definition: a resolved, life-long vow (要期之心) whose scope must cover every possible object in the Dharma-realm, not some narrower slice of it; a wholesome, purified mind (善淨心) that has set aside distraction for the moment of reception; and clear-eyed reflection (明慧) that actually matches the intention to the teaching rather than drifting through the ritual on autopilot. Miss any of the three, and — in the commentary's own blunt phrase — the whole event happens "as though blind, or in a dream," producing no real essence at all.

The guide flags one line as the single most consequential in the whole section: Zicheng's aside that, strictly analyzed, the essence itself is "not mind" and does not disclose where it resides — yet Dao Xuan chooses to say it "resides in the mind" anyway, deliberately adopting what the commentary calls "the Consummate meaning," pointing to the eighth, store-consciousness as its actual locus. This single throwaway clause, the guide notes, is already planting the seed for the entire Consummate Teaching's doctrine that appears many pages later — the whole gate's real destination is visible in outline this early.

3. Milestone: How Many Kinds of Essence Are There, and Why Exactly Two

Section Two turns from the receiving mind to the "generated karma-essence" itself — and opens with the most basic possible question: given that the objects a person might transgress against are limitless, are there limitless kinds of precept-essence to match? Dao Xuan's answer collapses the entire question down to exactly two categories, and the two supporting items that follow explain why it could not have been one, and could not have been three.

Ru-Yi Translation

Section Two: the generated karma-essence. This divides into six items: (1) distinguishing how many kinds of essence there are; (2) establishing the two names; (3) deriving the essence according to the treatises; (4) establishing the true doctrine; (5) sequential generation; (6) the number of kinds of unmanifest karma. Item One: distinguishing how many kinds of essence there are.

The Procedural Commentary states: "Question: how many kinds can the prātimokṣa precepts actually have? Answer: discussing the essence strictly by its objects, it is truly limitless — the precepts exist fundamentally to guard against wrongdoing, and since the conditions for wrongdoing are many, the precepts they give rise to are correspondingly many. Hence the Sutra on Upāsaka Precepts says, 'sentient beings are limitless, and so the precepts are likewise limitless,' and so on. But reasoning it through now, in essentials there are only two kinds: active karma and unmanifest karma. These two precepts together gather in everything; there is no object they fail to exhaust." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The answer first indicates the measure by way of the object, then, from 'but' onward, gathers up the essentials. Since we are now clarifying the essence directly, these two are what is essential, and so they alone are singled out. 'Gathering in everything' means that, since these two precepts hold universal guard, they are already included within the totality of the generated essence. That this gathers in the Dharma-essence yet speaks in terms of 'object' is meant to show that the Dharma reaching every possible object all reduces to these two precepts."

Item Two: establishing the two names. This divides into two supports: (1) why exactly two are established; (2) explaining the meaning of the two names together. Support One: why exactly two are established.

The Procedural Commentary states: "Question: why not establish just one, or else three?" The Zichi Commentary glosses: "'Not one' is meant to show that the two mutually require each other, hence the question is framed in terms of their mutual dependence. 'Or else three' — the Commentary on Karman Procedures raises the question of why not combine them into three, on the grounds that action-concurrent unmanifest karma is itself a distinct third thing occurring simultaneously with the [other] two, and so, it might seem, ought to be set up as one further category alongside the previous two, making three altogether."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Answer. If active karma alone were established, then, once the act itself has ceased and passed away, it could not go on guarding against transgression, nor can one keep acting continuously forever — hence unmanifest karma is required, to guard against transgression over the long term. If unmanifest karma alone were established, then it would have nothing from which to arise, and could not spring up on its own — it necessarily depends on active karma to generate it. The two thus mutually depend on each other, and neither can be established alone." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The first answer, on why not just one, first explains that active karma requires unmanifest karma. 'Once ceased, it cannot guard' is because the act itself is brief. 'Cannot act continuously' is because the mind must also attend to other things. Next, from 'if unmanifest alone' onward, it explains that unmanifest karma requires active karma. The final sentence draws the conclusion."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Why not three? Simply because the very principle of their essence and marks contradicts [a third category]. First, active and unmanifest karma are themselves distinct; second, mind and not-mind are themselves distinct. Their natures cannot be merged, and so only two can be established. As for what is being guarded against, that varies limitlessly according to its object." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The second answer, on why not three: 'active and unmanifest are distinct' is because motion and stillness differ; 'mind and not-mind are distinct' is because their essences themselves contradict each other. From 'as for' onward resolves a further doubt — since three are not permitted, one might suppose the precept-Dharma is confined to only these two [narrow categories]; this is specifically clarified to show that the point above is made strictly with respect to the capacity to guard, and it is for this reason alone that there are just two." [Xingshi Chao Ji, fasc. 16]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide notes the deliberate contrast set up here: in principle, since beings and their possible objects of transgression are limitless, the precepts they generate could in theory be limitless too — but Dao Xuan collapses that infinity down to a working pair, active karma and unmanifest karma, because between them these two categories already cover everything that matters. Neither can stand alone: active karma is too brief to guard anything once the ritual act itself is over, while unmanifest karma has no way to arise except by being generated through an active karma in the first place — so they are, in the guide's phrase, mutually load-bearing rather than independently sufficient.

On why not three, the guide underscores that Dao Xuan is not merely being tidy — he is refusing to let action-concurrent unmanifest karma (the momentary kind arising alongside the ordination act itself) count as some genuinely separate third essence, because at bottom it still reduces to the same active/unmanifest, mind/not-mind distinction that already produces exactly two categories, no more.

4. Milestone: Naming Active and Unmanifest Karma

Having established that there are exactly two categories, Support Two turns to their names. Dao Xuan reaches for two homely images — a potter's wheel, and something that quietly "keeps arising" on its own — to make an otherwise abstract distinction between a momentary act and its lasting residue immediately graspable.

Support Two: explaining the meaning of the two names together.

The Procedural Commentary states: "Question: now that we know there are two precepts, please explain their names." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "This concludes the preceding and opens what follows, asking generally about the three names."

The three names are these: "active" and "unmanifest" are the individual names, while "karma" is the shared, general name — together they make three.

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The answer is this. What is called 'active' [karma] is named by analogy with the moment a potter's wheel is turning — it is called 'active.' Hence the Abhidharma-hṛdaya-śāstra Miscellany says: 'Action is bodily; it is the expedient means that moves the body.'" The Zichi Commentary glosses: "'Active' carries the meaning of expedient means, construction. 'Potter' means one who works clay. 'The wheel' is the cart-like device that shapes clay into a vessel — set in motion, it turns; hence it is used as the analogy. The substance made of the four great elements is called 'retribution-matter'; the movement arising from conditions is called 'expedient means.' Retribution gives rise to the expedient means, and the expedient means depends on retribution — the two mutually borrow from each other, neither identical nor wholly different. But to speak of 'retribution' does not necessarily mean 'expedient means,' whereas to speak of 'expedient means' necessarily implies retribution is present. Here the turning wheel-wood is the analogy for the substance of retribution, and the wheel's turning is the analogy for the expedient means — the motion itself is what is named 'active.' What follows cites proof; 'the body, and what moves the body' can be understood by the correspondence."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "As for what is called 'unmanifest' [karma]: once arisen it goes on continuously manifesting, present without interruption from beginning to end, [accompanying] the four aggregates of mind across the three moral qualities, requiring no further condition to bring it about. Hence the Abhidharma-hṛdaya-śāstra Miscellany says: 'Once the bodily action has ceased, [it continues] together with the remaining consciousnesses; this dharma arises following after — hence it is called unmanifest.' Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide draws special attention to what it calls the single most decisive argument in this entire "not-matter, not-mind" case: the reasoning from the formless realm. Beings in the formless realm have no matter of any kind whatsoever — no body, no physical form — and yet they can still possess unmanifest karma. If unmanifest karma required matter to exist, this would be flatly impossible; since it plainly is not impossible, unmanifest karma cannot, after all, be a form of matter. The guide treats this as the Nominalist school's decisive rebuttal of the Substantialist position surveyed earlier.

It also singles out the Tattvasiddhi citation about diligence, long life, and heavenly bliss as a genuinely elegant piece of reasoning in its own right: no one actually sustains a single unbroken wholesome mental state for an entire lifetime, and yet merit from a past wholesome deed clearly keeps accumulating regardless — which is exactly what a merely mental account of the precept-essence cannot explain, and exactly what a "neither matter nor mind" essence, functioning independently of the mind's own moment-to-moment fluctuations, was invented to explain.

9. Milestone: Three Schools — Dao Xuan's Real Doctrine at Last

Item Four opens the section Master Hongyi himself flags, in his own marginal note, as the single most important passage to study in this entire gate. The guide underscores just how deliberately Master Hongyi flags this transition: everything the gate has covered up to now — the receiving mind, the two categories of active and unmanifest karma, the borrowed six-point Sarvāstivādin analysis — was preparatory. The guide also flags the three-school framework itself as the section's real organizing device: rather than simply adjudicating between the Sarvāstivādin and Tattvasiddhi positions already surveyed, Dao Xuan sets up a genuine three-way contest — Substantialist, Nominalist, and a third, Mahayana-inflected Consummate Teaching that will turn out, by the section's end, to be where his own sympathies actually lie.

10. Milestone: The Substantialist School — The Precept-Essence Is Subtle Matter

Support One states the Sarvāstivāda position in Dao Xuan's own final formulation, and it is more precise than the earlier borrowed summary: both active and unmanifest karma are matter, but a peculiarly subtle kind — comparable to the matter that makes up an intermediate-state being between death and rebirth, visible only to a Buddha's heavenly eye, never to an ordinary one.

The guide's table format captures the Substantialist position at a glance: both active and unmanifest karma are matter — active karma is the visible sense-objects of form and sound themselves, while unmanifest karma is a subtler "imputed matter," gathered under the sense-object of dharmas. The guide flags Yuan Zhao's own aside as worth lingering on: he calls this reading of karma-as-matter genuinely close to "the Buddha's original intent," even while ultimately siding with a different school overall — a reminder that Dao Xuan's three-school analysis is not simply "two wrong answers and one right one," but a real attempt to credit what each position gets right before showing where it falls short. 11. Milestone: The Nominalist School — Neither Matter Nor Mind

Support Two turns to the Tattvasiddhi school — the very tradition this whole compendium's base Vinaya, the Four-Part Vinaya, technically belongs to. Its account already "partially opens onto the Mahayana," in the commentary's own phrase, because it grounds active karma explicitly in mind rather than matter alone. But when the commentary pushes further and asks what unmanifest karma actually is, even this school's own analysis starts straining at its limits — coming remarkably close, as the commentary itself notices, to the Consummate Teaching's position still to come.

Support Two: the Nominalist School.

The Commentary on Karman Procedures states: "Second, following the Tattvasiddhi Śāstra, our own school, which distinguishes active and unmanifest karma as having different essences." Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide is emphatic about why this school matters more than the others structurally: the Four-Part Vinaya, the base text underlying this entire compendium, technically belongs to the Dharmaguptaka lineage, and Dao Xuan interprets its doctrine through Tattvasiddhi categories — so the Nominalist reading given here is, in an important sense, "our own school's" own account, as the Commentary on Karman Procedures itself says outright. Its real advance over the Substantialists is grounding active karma explicitly in mind rather than treating everything as matter, which the commentary credits as "partially opening onto the Mahayana."

But the guide zeroes in on the section's most quietly remarkable moment: when the commentary tries to pin down what the unmanifest karma-essence actually is, it describes something generated by mind that then "perfumes" — 恵熏, the very same verb the Consummate Teaching will later use for its own doctrine of habituating the store-consciousness — and yet, held back by its own school's more limited categories, it can only conclude "we don't know what to call it, so we provisionally label it with two negations." The guide reads Jiyuan's own aside — "this is precisely the point at which the essence is being indicated, though the commentary declines to point it out directly, fearing to disrupt the school's stated doctrine" — as close to an admission that the Nominalist school is reaching for the Consummate Teaching's own answer without quite being able to say it in its own vocabulary.

12. Milestone: The Consummate Teaching — Why a Third School at All

Support Three opens the Consummate Teaching — Dao Xuan's own synthesis, drawing explicitly on Mahayana sources rather than the abhidharma treatises alone. Support Three: the Consummate Teaching.

The Commentary on Karman Procedures states: "Finally, explaining the precept-essence according to the Consummate Teaching." Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide unpacks the character "圓" (consummate) as carrying three distinct senses at once, all of them load-bearing: consummate-and-sudden, because this teaching decides the lesser schools by Mahayana authority without requiring anyone to separately re-ordain under a Mahayana ritual; consummate-and-all-pervading, because it recognizes that "matter" and "non-matter" were never really opposed positions to begin with, just different names the earlier schools reached for under provisional teaching; and consummate-and-complete, because where the first two schools each addressed one particular audience's needs, this teaching claims to state the matter exhaustively and finally. Jiyuan's own answer to "why need a third school" is unusually direct about the stakes: relying on either lesser school alone leaves the essence genuinely unclear, but abandoning them both without a replacement leaves the whole tradition in disarray — hence a specifically Mahayana teaching, grounded in the Lotus and Nirvāṇa sutras, has to be built to resolve the matter for good.

On the Tiantai comparison, the guide is careful to preserve Yuan Zhao's precise, almost lawyerly distinction: the underlying principle Dao Xuan reaches is genuinely the same one Tiantai's own Consummate Teaching reaches — both ultimately draw on the Lotus Sūtra's own vision — but the scope is entirely different. Tiantai's Consummate Teaching is a comprehensive account of the entire path; Dao Xuan borrows only enough of it to resolve one specific technical question, the precept-essence, which would count, inside Tiantai's own larger system, as merely one item within its account of "sublime conduct." The overlap in name, Yuan Zhao insists, should not be mistaken for Dao Xuan simply importing Tiantai's system wholesale — he reaches his own conclusion independently, using the same root scriptures.

13. Milestone: Precepts as the Rein on a Deluded Mind

Before stating the Consummate Teaching's actual definition of the essence, the commentary explains why precepts are necessary at all — an ordinary being's mind has been entangled with delusion since beginningless time, unable on its own to turn back toward its own true source. It then distinguishes two kinds of practitioner: the dull, who mistake the precept-essence for either matter or a mixture of matter-and-mind, and the sharp, who see through to the fact that everything is, at root, simply mind.

Examining this passage closely, it directly resolves the essence as it is actually received today: the earlier explanation of subtle matter has already refuted the Sarvāstivāda school; the present passage decides, specifically, the Dharmaguptaka school's own account of active and unmanifest karma. Active karma is constructed by mind, [yet that school] also includes matter [in its account]; unmanifest karma is a mental seed, [yet that school] holds it to be neither matter nor mind. This difference arises because, in the Buddha's own lifetime, [his listeners'] capacities for awakening differed, causing [his followers], after his passing, to divide into schools, each holding its own calculation. Hence the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: 'In my sutras, sometimes I spoke [of the precept-essence] as matter, and the bhikṣus accordingly say it is matter; sometimes I spoke of it as not matter, and the bhikṣus accordingly say it is not matter — in every case this is because they fail to understand my true intent,' meaning they do not recognize that the Buddha spoke by way of expedient means. From 'the wise' onward, the passage next recounts the sharp-facultied practitioner's easy awakening, in two parts: first, clarifying awakened understanding brought about through the teaching. 'Object' refers to both sentient and non-sentient objects such as the two truths; 'condition' refers to the dust-mote-and-sand-grain-like dharmas regulated according to their object — both of these are [ultimately] mind. 'Constructed' means all of it, without exception, is simply mind. From 'without' onward, the passage next clarifies reflective contemplation: having already realized that everything is simply mind, then, whatever stirs and functions, one no longer conditions upon external objects at all; gathering the mind and reflecting inward, one sees only the one consciousness. This consciousness is precisely the essence of mind — not fixed to any single nature of its own, it follows defiled or pure conditions to construct dark or bright karma, forming wholesome or unwholesome retribution — hence there arise the differentiated ten realms of both sentient beings and Buddhas, as environment and inhabitant alike; hence it is said to have 'this and that.' From beginningless time, failing to understand this, [beings] construct false karma pervading the entire Dharma-realm as their object, appearing and disappearing throughout birth-and-death. It is for this very reason that the Tathāgata, matching the Dharma-realm as object, establishes precepts without limit. The precepts have no separate essence of their own — they are simply this same false karma; just as with lust, theft, and so on — how could there be some separate 'precept' apart from these? Precisely by turning false [conduct] into karma, restraining that karma is what is called 'precept.' Hence the Procedural Commentary says: before receiving the precepts, unwholesomeness pervades the entire Dharma-realm; now, wishing to proceed to reception, one reverses those former unwholesome objects and gives rise, in their place, to a wholesome mind — hence the very cause from which the precept arises likewise pervades the entire Dharma-realm. And the Sutra on Upāsaka Precepts says: sentient beings are limitless, the great earth is limitless, plants and trees are limitless, the ocean's waters are immeasurable, empty space is without boundary — and the precepts are equally so. All of this carries the very same meaning."

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads this passage as the Consummate Teaching's own foundation myth: precepts exist, on this account, purely to "rouse the intention" of a mind that has been running on autopilot in delusion since beginningless time — there is, strictly speaking, no such thing as "a precept" floating free of one's own mind's karma; the precept simply is that karma, turned back against itself and restrained. The guide finds real pathos in this framing: an ordinary being's mind keeps meeting its own delusion again and again, sinking further through birth-and-death, with no thought at all of turning back — until a Buddha's teaching supplies, for the first time, a reason and a method to do so.

On the dull-versus-sharp distinction, the guide is careful to note what is actually being claimed: this passage is not simply saying "some people are smarter than others," but specifically diagnosing why the Substantialist and Nominalist schools ended up disagreeing at all — both are dull-facultied readings that mistake a purely mental phenomenon for some kind of matter, or a mixture of matter and mind, because they have not yet seen that "object" and "condition" alike are, at the deepest level, simply mind's own constructions. The guide cites the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own striking admission here — that even the Buddha's own listeners heard "matter" or "not matter" depending on their own prior capacity, and mistook this expedient variation in his teaching for real disagreement about the facts.

14. Milestone: Wholesome Seed in the Store-Consciousness — Master Hongyi's Own Verdict

This is the single most important passage in the entire gate — the sentence Master Hongyi himself ultimately endorses as his own final answer to what the precept-essence actually is. The Commentary on Karman Procedures states it in one dense, four-clause line; Yuan Zhao then supplies two separate glosses, one comparing it precisely against Tiantai's own terminology, the other carefully distinguishing the precept-essence from the store-consciousness itself, so the two are never mistakenly collapsed into one.

The Jiyuan Commentary glosses at length: "The third part, on the received essence, has three sections. First, showing the essence and its marks according to the Consummate meaning. What was explained above concerned the sharp-facultied practitioner giving rise to great understanding before formal reception; what is explained here concerns the fact that cultivation and realization must first begin with receiving the precepts. The passage itself has three parts. The first clause expresses the wish to be liberated — 'bring to an end' means exhaust completely; the words 'deluded feeling' broadly include the afflictions of views, thought, and fundamental ignorance. The second clause looks back on one's own accumulated karma: from beginningless time, habituated over and over, unwholesomeness has accumulated deeply over a long span; though one may already understand that everything is simply mind, it remains genuinely difficult to actually tame and subdue in practice. Apart from the precept-Dharma, there is no way to settle this karma. To set this aside and still seek to cultivate the path is to waste one's efforts in vain; to step backward while seeking to go forward can never arrive anywhere at all. From 'hence' onward, the third section explains how the Dharma is received according to this same condition, itself in two parts. The first two clauses show the active karma: 'the formal act' broadly covers everything from the initial expedient preparations through to the moment of formal reception itself. 'Perfuming the deluded mind' means that, borrowing the sublime object set before one, a sublime mind is roused into activity; this mind, turning back against delusion, is itself the true mind — and it is by means of this true [mind] perfuming the deluded [mind] that delusion is kept from arising. This is exactly what the Procedural Commentary means when it says: taking one's own vow, undertaking the expedient means, making the mind's own vessel wholesome and pure, resolved never to do evil, weighing one's intention with clear wisdom, one's mind silently merges with the Dharma set before it. This is precisely the perfuming subject, with delusion as what is perfumed. This is the 'active' perfuming — comparable to burning incense to perfume away every foul odor. The next three clauses directly explain the unmanifest: through this perfuming, karma is accomplished, and once karma is perfected, it becomes a seed. This seed possesses its own force and function; without needing any further construction, it perfumes on its own, spontaneously and continuously — the deluded seed lies dormant and hidden, and deluded thoughts no longer arise. This is the 'unmanifest' perfuming — comparable to how, even once the incense itself has burned away, its lingering fragrance still remains. The first clause indicates the locus depended upon, which is also what is perfumed; the next clause reveals the essence that depends upon it, which is also the perfuming subject itself. 'Wholesome' distinguishes it from the unwholesome. 'Seed' is a metaphor: just as, in the world, every grain and fruit possesses its own seed, so too, stated briefly, ten features apply — (1) it arises from a coming-together of manifold conditions; (2) its essential nature differs from case to case; (3) its generative nature persists continuously; (4) it grows and matures spontaneously, without further effort; (5) it already contains within it root, stalk, flower, leaf, and every such feature; (6) though it already contains all this, none of it can actually be perceived or grasped; (7) when the proper time comes, it opens and unfolds; (8) seed and resulting fruit never fail to correspond; (9) it continues on, one generation flowing into the next; (10) what it produces multiplies many times over. The unmanifest possesses all these same features, and so is compared to a seed in this way. Yet the term 'seed' is used broadly, and its precise meaning requires careful distinction. First, with respect to the ten realms: the four evil destinies are unwholesome, the remaining six are wholesome. Next, within the wholesome: the human and heavenly realms are [merely] contaminated, while the four sagely realms are uncontaminated. Third, within the sagely realms themselves: the three vehicles' own scriptures are one-sided and provisional, while only the Buddha-vehicle is consummate and real. Now, this precept-seed under discussion here — the text only distinguishes it from the unwholesome — but, measured against the human and heavenly realms, it is an uncontaminated seed, and measured against the one-sided, provisional [teachings], it is a consummate, real seed. If one need not receive them separately, should keeping and transgressing then simply be judged directly according to the Mahayana standard? The Zhiyuan Yibian states: "The Tiantai commentary says: '[the nature] simply has not yet arisen; once it arises, it is the nature's own unmanifest, imputed form.' The Nanshan [tradition, i.e. Dao Xuan] says: 'it perfumes the fundamental store-consciousness, forming a wholesome seed — this is the precept-essence.' In Tiantai's account, the single word 'nature' names the very cause capable of arising, while 'unmanifest, imputed form' names the essence thereby generated. In Nanshan's account, 'the store-consciousness' names the very locus depended upon, while 'the wholesome seed' names the essence that depends upon it. What is capable of arising, and what it depends upon, is a nature already inherently present; what is thereby generated, and what depends upon it, is the essence [actually constituted] through today's reception. If the essence is derived in this way, the textual basis is entirely clear — subject and object are each distinctly accounted for, and essence and nature are never carelessly conflated. Only then can reception be free of doubt, and cultivation have something genuine to rely upon." [Zhiyuan Yibian, fasc. 2]

The Zhiyuan Yibian further states: "What the sutras and treatises describe as the bodhisattva precepts actually divide, of themselves, into two essences. The first is 'the essence proper,' which is precisely the precept-essence, no different from the wholesome seed discussed here. The second is 'the depended-upon essence,' which is the mind-nature itself. This being so, the mind-nature is merely what the precept-essence depends upon — it is, in reality, not itself the precept-essence." [Zhiyuan Yibian, fasc. 1]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide singles this out as the single most important sentence in the entire gate, and gives it its own full translation as a standalone quotation: "Through the formal act of reception, one perfumes the deluded mind in turn; and within the fundamental store-consciousness, a wholesome seed is thereby formed — this is the precept-essence." Jiyuan's commentary supplies the governing metaphor for how this happens in two stages: the ordination ritual itself is like burning incense to drive out a foul smell — an active, one-time act of "perfuming" the deluded mind with a sublime object and a sublime resolve; and what remains afterward, the unmanifest karma itself, is like the fragrance that lingers on in a room long after the incense itself has finished burning — no longer requiring any further active effort, simply continuing to work on its own.

The guide walks through the ten traditional qualities of a seed that this metaphor is built to carry, and stresses that this is not merely decorative imagery — each of the ten features (arising from many conditions together, growing spontaneously without further effort, remaining hidden and imperceptible even while fully present, eventually producing fruit that exactly matches its own cause, and so on) does real argumentative work explaining how something planted once at ordination can go on quietly functioning, unseen, for an entire lifetime.

Finally, the guide flags Yuan Zhao's careful two-part clarification as essential to getting this right: first, his side-by-side comparison with Tiantai vocabulary — where Tiantai speaks of "nature" as the capacity and "unmanifest imputed form" as what is generated, Nanshan speaks of "the store-consciousness" as the locus and "the wholesome seed" as what depends on it — the two systems map onto each other precisely, term for term, without actually being identical vocabularies. Second, and more important for practice: Yuan Zhao insists the precept-essence must never simply be equated with "mind-nature" or Buddha-nature itself. The mind-nature is only ever what the wholesome seed depends on — the seed itself, freshly planted at one's own ordination, is the real precept-essence. Collapsing the two, the guide notes, would blur this teaching into a vague, generic "seeing one's own nature" that loses the whole point of actually receiving precepts at all.

15. Milestone: How the Precept-Essence Goes On Working — Remember, Uphold, Guard

Having stated what the wholesome seed is, the commentary now explains what it actually does once planted — the exact mechanism by which a single ordination moment keeps functioning throughout a whole lifetime of ongoing practice. This passage supplies the fixed phrase "the capacity to remember, to uphold, and to guard against transgression" that the very next gate, on precept-conduct, will directly build on.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats this passage as the mechanical heart of the whole Consummate Teaching doctrine: the wholesome seed does not simply sit inertly in the store-consciousness after ordination — it functions actively as "one mind, three functions," in Jiyuan's own phrase, remembering an object of potential transgression, upholding one's grip on the precept in response, and thereby guarding against the transgression actually occurring, with no real sequence of before-and-after among the three since they operate as a single continuous capacity.

Equally important, the guide stresses, is the feedback loop the passage describes: every act of genuine ongoing practice "perfumes the fundamental consciousness" once again, reinforcing and nourishing the very seed from which it arose — which is exactly why the commentary warns, in its closing lines, that even a fleeting lapse into a deluded state of mind does the same thing in reverse, perfuming and deepening one's own underlying delusion instead. The precept-essence, on this account, is never a fixed, static possession; it is continuously being either strengthened or eroded by how one actually lives day to day — which is precisely the link this gate hands forward to the next, on precept-conduct itself.

16. Milestone: Three Vows, Three Bodies, Three Virtues

The Consummate Teaching's account does not stop at explaining the essence and its ongoing function — it opens out into a full soteriological framework, mapping the receiving of precepts onto the Three Collections of Pure Precepts, and those in turn onto the Three Gates of Liberation, the three bodies of Buddhahood, and the three virtues a Buddha ultimately possesses. Receiving precepts, on this reading, is already the seed of becoming a Buddha.

The guide lays out the full correspondence as a single connected chain: the mind's three original, pure qualities — being naturally clean, naturally free, and naturally equal — each get corrupted by delusion into a corresponding affliction (affliction proper, karmic bondage, and the cycle of birth-and-death), and the Three Collections of Pure Precepts exist specifically to reverse each corruption in turn. Now, knowing that everything is simply consciousness, with no external sense-object at all, at the very moment of formal reception one conditions oneself universally upon the entire Dharma-realm, boldly gives rise to the three vows, reverses one's three former obstructions, and, through the power of mind and karma, binds together a seed, which becomes, of itself, the precept-essence. Since mind is boundless, the essence too is boundless; since mind is inexhaustible, the precepts too are inexhaustible. One should know that this is precisely giving rise to the bodhi-mind, cultivating the practice of great compassion, and seeking the unsurpassed fruit. This is called the real path; this is precisely the Mahayana. The Tathāgatas of the three times, the Buddhas of the ten directions, appearing in birth and proclaiming their passing, opening [the teaching] suddenly for some and guiding others gradually, through a hundred, a thousand expedient means, through limitless Dharma-gates, through every manner of skillful activity — none of it is anything other than this. Hence it is said: 'though I speak of every kind of path, in truth it is all for the sake of the one Buddha-vehicle.' This is precisely the place where practice itself fixes the mind. And yet, in this turbid age, obstructions run deep, habituated patterns are difficult to break, and the beginner's mind is often timid, liable to retreat from bodhi. Hence one must, in addition, set one's aim on rebirth in Amitābha's Pure Land. Moreover, the Consummate school's own Three Collections are themselves precisely the three minds of the highest grade [of rebirth in that Pure Land]: cutting off evil through the Restraint Collection is precisely the utterly sincere mind; gathering wholesomeness and cultivating wisdom through the Wholesome Dharmas Collection is precisely the profound mind; and gathering beings and benefiting them through the Benefiting Sentient Beings Collection is precisely the mind that dedicates merit and makes the vow. Once these three minds are complete, one is certain to attain the highest grade [of rebirth] — one obtains the forbearance of the unborn without needing many further lifetimes, and one's own attainment of bodhi suffers no retreat or discouragement at all. This, too, is the ultimate place where the practitioner's own mind is fixed. What follows cites proof, namely the Lotus Sūtra's own text on revealing the [one true teaching]. The sutra says: 'these nine divisions of my own teaching, spoken according to what beings can accept, all take entry into the Mahayana as their root' — of the twelve divisions [of scripture], nine belong to the lesser vehicle, since these too ultimately return to the one Buddha-vehicle. The Zhiyuan Yibian states: "As for the precept-essence: it is the very hinge of the entire Vinaya canon, the very foundation of keeping and transgressing, the very fountainhead of turning the current back, the very guide that leads all practice forward. But because the various teachings [on this point] have remained submerged and obscure, and the underlying principle runs so deep, across nine dynasties of transmitted teaching, eminent worthies have appeared from time to time, and though each pursued his own separate path, none was ever heard to reach a final, settled resolution. It was only when the Tang dynasty came that our own patriarch alone exhaustively investigated the hidden depths and fully realized [our own true] nature, turning over the ancients' teachings again and again, dismissing whatever was superficial or false, dissecting and adjudicating the schools' own doctrines, carefully weighing their meaning and principle, and firmly establishing the three schools as they now stand. This may truly be called gathering together the very wellspring of the Buddha's teaching throughout its transformations, and kindling the sun of wisdom for the multitude still lost in confusion. First, the Sarvāstivāda school: both the active and unmanifest essences are matter — this much is already understood. Second, the Tattvasiddhi school: active karma takes matter-and-mind as its essence — this, too, is already understood. As for the unmanifest karma, said to take neither matter nor mind as its essence: 'neither matter nor mind' is, however, merely the name of the aggregate under which it is classified, and is not, in reality, a description of its actual configuration — and this has led, across the generations, to no small amount of mistaken commentary on the point. Now, following the Commentary's own text, one may derive its true name directly: it is, in secret, simply speaking of the wholesome seed all along. It is only that, being confined to the lesser vehicle's own teaching, [that school] could not yet point to this directly, and so, externally, gave it the name 'neither matter nor mind.' Hence the Commentary says: 'examining this karma-essence, it is originally generated by mind, and in turn perfumes the original mind, possessing both capacity and function' — down to where it says, 'not knowing what else to call it, it is provisionally labeled with the two negations.' Examining this passage closely, though it does not yet say 'wholesome seed' outright, it already says the essence 'perfumes the mind' and 'possesses function' — the secret meaning here is plainly visible for anyone who looks. One should recognize that this is already the very essence that will later be derived as 'neither matter nor mind.' Third, the Consummate Teaching: this fuses together the preceding schools, pointing directly to the real meaning itself. What the preceding two schools each call their essence is, in fact, already the wholesome seed — grasping the root by way of the branch, this same wholesome seed is precisely what the preceding two essences actually were all along; gathering the branch back into the root is precisely what is meant by 'the Consummate account' — the name used makes no real difference either way. Hence the Commentary says: 'here we discuss this one single dharma according to three schools' — hence we know the distinctions are three, while the essence itself is, in reality, not two. Question: if it is said that the essence is only one, while the distinctions are three, does this mean the preceding two schools possess only an empty name, with no real essence behind it at all? Answer: though the schools each hold their own calculation, how could the essence itself actually differ or diverge? It is simply that, taking it to be different, they forcibly constructed their own separate names for it. One should know that the Sarvāstivāda school calculates the seed as matter, the Tattvasiddhi school calculates the seed as neither matter nor mind — it is only the later Consummate Teaching that points out what the preceding two really were, all along. Hence the Commentary says: 'the foolish take it to be different, and, clinging to their own position, become attached to it,' and so on. Let me offer a further analogy: it is like a fine piece of jade found in the world. One person, out of ignorance, calls the jade a mere stone; another calls it 'not-stone,' without yet being able to reveal its true substance; only a later person, grasping the real truth, can point out the error in both earlier accounts at once. If there were no jade-substance there to begin with, how could there be any failure to recognize it? This is the analogy for how the present three schools resemble one another in this same respect. Question: for what reason is it called 'wholesome seed'? Answer: 'wholesome' is the Dharma-essence itself; 'seed' is the metaphor. What is meant is this: the precept-Dharma, countless as dust-motes and sand-grains, is received into the fundamental store-consciousness, and thereafter gives rise to ongoing conduct; this conduct is able to draw forth a future fruit — just as a grain of rice, sown into a field, sprouts, grows into a stalk, and eventually bears fruit as a full head of grain. The correspondence holds without any real difference — hence the name is given."

The Zhiyuan Yibian further states: "As for the essence of the received Dharma, following the Commentary on Karman Procedures's own three-school analysis: first, the Sarvāstivāda school. Active and unmanifest karma are both, in essence, matter. The continuous wholesome form-and-sound produced by bodily and verbal expedient means is the essence of active karma; matter gathered under the sense-object of dharmas, [i.e.] imputed matter, is the essence of the unmanifest. Now, our own patriarch, in investigating the essence, holds that this is, in fact, the very nature of wholesome and unwholesome karma itself — perceptible to the heavenly eye, distinctly and clearly, the same in kind as an intermediate-state being, subtle and difficult to know, differing from the coarse matter perceived by the ordinary fleshly eye; hence it is called 'subtle matter.' Second, the Tattvasiddhi school. The essences of active and unmanifest karma differ from each other: body-and-speech-karma-as-intention, which is able to produce both matter and mind, is the essence of active karma; 'neither matter nor mind' — sought by each of the five defining marks in turn, and in every case unattainable — is the essence of the unmanifest. Our patriarch, investigating the essence, [holds that] it is precisely mind constructing karma, perfuming through habituation and thereby possessing function, able to give rise to later habituation; since mind itself cannot be given any fixed shape, it is provisionally described by way of matter, in order to make it manifest. The measure of the karma thereby generated differs from that of active karma — possessing both mind and matter together, and yet corresponding to neither exactly — and so it is provisionally named with the two negations, taken together as the precept-essence. Third, according to the Consummate Teaching school, clarifying the essence: it is simply that the two preceding schools, each following their own respective calculation, describe the matter in terms of motion or stillness, but in the end neither reaches the ultimate truth. Hence [Dao Xuan] reaches across and takes up the Mahayana's own consummate, real meaning, pointing directly to that same essence as nothing other than the ālaya store-consciousness itself, flowing and transforming according to conditions, and thereby constructing a karma-seed. What actually constructs it is the sixth consciousness — this is precisely the active karma. The karma thus constructed, held and preserved by the ālaya, is what is called the unmanifest; the karmic cause thereby stored up is called the wholesome seed. Though karma is constructed by mind, once it is accomplished, it stands together with the remaining consciousnesses, its nature no longer subject to opposition or obstruction; and it is, moreover, constructed of the four great elements, its essence subject to increase and decrease, perceptible to the heavenly eye as distinctly wholesome or unwholesome. This is precisely why the Tathāgata, according to differing capacities, meeting differing beings, sometimes speaks of it as neither matter nor mind, and sometimes speaks of it as matter. Lesser capacities, failing to grasp this fully, calculate it as matter, thereby rejecting the emptiness-school's own view; or cling to 'neither matter nor mind,' thereby denouncing the Sarvāstivāda school as mistaken. As in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, [where the Buddha says] this is, in every case, simply because [his listeners] fail to understand his own true intent — causing contending calculations to diverge along separate paths. But the school we follow here takes, throughout, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own final, ultimate teaching, gathering together these divergent extremes and bringing them back to a single, unified conclusion. This may truly be called uniting the essence from beginning to end throughout the whole transformative teaching, and cutting through the net of doubt for later generations. Hence the Commentary on Karman Procedures says: 'it ultimately returns to the Mahayana, hence one's mind must be fixed on that very place' — this same clear instruction is elaborated at length in that text. What people today receive corresponds precisely to the Tattvasiddhi Nominalist school: 'neither matter nor mind' is its own stated Dharma-essence; taking the Consummate view to gain full access to it, this is nothing other than the wholesome seed itself. What has been set forth here gives only a rough outline of the matter's own thread; when it comes to the deep intricacies of karmic theory, it runs extremely deep and subtle, and without sustained study one is genuinely liable to be left at a loss — as when the blind men each grasp a different part of the elephant, the confusion remains widespread even among people today." [Zhiyuan Yibian, fasc. 1]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide leans heavily on Yuan Zhao's own closing image for how the three schools actually relate to one another: a piece of fine jade, still embedded in rough stone. One person, not knowing any better, calls the whole thing a mere stone — that is the Sarvāstivāda position, unable to see past ordinary matter. A second person recognizes it is not simply a stone, but still cannot say what it actually is — that is the Tattvasiddhi position, correctly ruling out matter without yet being able to name what remains. Only a third person, who actually knows what jade is, can look at the very same object and identify it directly — that is the Consummate Teaching, and its "seed" is not a fourth, competing account, but simply the correct name for the very thing the other two schools were already, however imperfectly, pointing at all along. As the guide puts it, this reframes the whole three-school debate as three progressively deeper levels of recognizing one and the same reality, not three genuinely incompatible theories.

The guide also flags Master Hongyi's own extraordinary personal aside embedded in this section — not part of Dao Xuan's original text at all, but the modern compendium's own compiler stepping forward to say that this stretch of the Commentary on Karman Procedures, running from "what, then, is the precept-essence?" all the way to this closing passage, is the single most refined piece of writing Dao Xuan ever produced, and to recommend that serious students copy it out separately as one continuous scroll for regular recitation. The guide reads this as a rare moment of the compiler's own voice breaking directly into the text, underscoring just how much weight this section is meant to carry for the reader.

Finally, the guide draws out the practical stakes of the closing exhortation: guarding the precepts is compared to guarding one's own life, or a life-raft in open ocean — vivid classical images (a goose that swallowed a pearl, a monk who let himself be bound rather than harm blades of grass, a monk clinging to a single plank at sea) all illustrating monks who chose to risk death itself rather than transgress. The guide reads this not as literal instruction to court danger, but as a deliberately extreme illustration of just how seriously the tradition asks a practitioner to weigh what was actually received at ordination.

Item Five: sequential generation.

The Zichi Commentary states: "As for active karma, there is no question of before-and-after at all [since it occupies only the one span of ritual activity]. It is unmanifest karma alone that has attracted multiple explanations, and so requires careful resolution."

The Procedural Commentary states: "The first explanation holds that, like the two horns of an ox, the two arise at exactly the same moment. Hence the Sarvāstivāda treatise says: in the very first instant, the precept possesses both the manifest and unmanifest teaching together; in the second instant, only the unmanifest teaching remains." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The first, 'simultaneous,' explanation has two parts: the first two clauses offer the analogy; from 'hence' onward, proof is cited. That both are present together in the first instant can be verified as arising at exactly the same time."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The second explanation holds that the two arise in sequence, one after the other. Hence the Sutra on Upāsaka Precepts says: among worldly dharmas, wherever there is a cause, there is also a fruit — just as, because of water or a mirror, a reflected face appears. Hence we know that active karma arises first, and unmanifest karma arises only afterward. As for the treatise's statement that, 'at the moment of the [ordination] act, both manifest and unmanifest are already present together' — this refers to action-concurrent unmanifest karma, which, together with the manifest act, both belong to the causal stage; only once the three-part ritual concludes and the karma reaches its full completion are both precepts brought to their consummation together — hence it is said that 'the manifest act already possesses the unmanifest.' This does not, however, rule out that lifespan-coextensive unmanifest karma still arises only afterward — that is to say, at the very moment that first single thought concludes, once both precepts have [momentarily] ceased, the [lasting] unmanifest then arises." The Zichi Commentary glosses at length: "The second, 'sequential,' explanation has two parts. First, establishing the position, in four parts: the first clause states the position; from 'hence' onward, proof is cited — 'worldly dharmas' means whatever is constructed through conditions; 'cause' refers to active karma, 'fruit' refers to the unmanifest; from 'just as' onward, an analogy illustrates the point; from 'hence' onward, the position is confirmed. From 'the treatise says' onward is the second part, resolving an apparent objection — since a sequential order has just been established, yet the treatise's own text, cited above, states both are present together in the first instant, this apparent conflict requires resolution. The passage divides into two: first, restating the objection; from 'this' onward, resolving it, in two parts — first clarifying that the action-concurrent [unmanifest] arises at exactly the same time [as the act]; from 'this does not' onward, showing that the lifespan-coextensive [unmanifest] arises only afterward. Second, unmanifest karma at the fruit stage, of which there are two kinds: one still arises together with the manifest act, exactly as explained above; the other is the lifespan-coextensive kind, which only now becomes the essence proper, at the very moment the three-part ritual concludes and it is outwardly manifested. The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "The second part, showing the marks according to the three time-stages, first states them generally. 'At the fruit stage' refers to the very first instant at which both of the above two kinds reach their completion. 'After the fruit stage' refers to the second instant onward, at which point the action-concurrent [unmanifest] has already ceased following the act, and only the lifespan-coextensive [unmanifest] remains." Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide lays this timing question out as a simple three-row table running through three moments: right after ascending the platform, while the three-part ritual is still incomplete, the manifest act is "arising" while the lifespan-coextensive unmanifest is merely "caused but not yet manifest"; at the very first instant after the ritual formally concludes, both are "perfected" together, along with the momentary action-concurrent unmanifest; and from the second instant onward, the manifest act itself has already "ceased," the action-concurrent unmanifest has ceased along with it, and only the lifespan-coextensive unmanifest survives, persisting alone all the way to the end of one's life.

The guide's real point is that Dao Xuan's "present explanation" is not a brand-new third position invented from scratch, but a refinement of the older "simultaneous" explanation — he keeps its basic claim that manifest and unmanifest arise together, while adding the missing precision about exactly which unmanifest karma (the fleeting, action-concurrent kind, or the long-abiding, lifespan-coextensive kind) is doing the arising at each of the three distinct moments. This is exactly the technical machinery, the guide notes, that lets the tradition say both that "the unmanifest arises with the act" and that "it goes on functioning for an entire lifetime afterward," without actually contradicting itself.

20. Milestone: Eight Kinds of Unmanifest Karma

Item Six closes Chapter One with a broader survey: beyond the two kinds already introduced — action-concurrent and lifespan-coextensive — the Sarvāstivāda treatise's own eight-fold taxonomy shows just how many distinct ways unmanifest karma can arise, from a formal vow to something as ordinary as a standing well or bridge quietly generating merit for as long as it remains usable. The item closes by showing how a single ordinary act can trigger several of these eight all at once.

The guide's own closing example is the most vivid part of this item: a person who habitually keeps a whip, always intending to use it to hurt someone, turns out to be generating all eight kinds of unmanifest karma from that single ongoing situation at once — vow-bound karma from the standing intention to keep using it, cross-conditioned karma if he verbally orders someone else to strike with it, assisted karma in whoever actually carries out the order, action-concurrent karma the instant it is actually swung, object-persisting karma for as long as the whip itself remains intact, karma-from-use every time it is actually put to use, and mind-following karma from the unwholesome intention simmering in the background the whole time. It also flags the reception of the precepts itself as a nice real-world illustration of the same overlap: ordination already involves action-concurrent and lifespan-coextensive karma arising together at the ritual's climax, plus vow-bound karma from the resolve taken, plus assisted karma from the preceptor administering the formula, plus mind-following karma in the capacity to keep drawing forth later conduct — several of the eight kinds, layered on top of one another, in the very same moment this whole gate has spent so long analyzing.

21. Milestone: What "Receiving" and "Ongoing Practice" Actually Mean

上來戒體中第一章戒體相狀竟

Chapter One closes here, having established what the precept-essence is; Chapter Two turns to a different question — how the moment of "receiving" the precepts differs from the "ongoing practice" of actually keeping them afterward, day after day. The two terms sound close enough to be interchangeable, but Dao Xuan draws a careful line: reception can only ever be pure, while ongoing practice, precisely because it spans a whole lifetime of real conduct, must be able to accommodate both keeping and transgressing alike.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide draws out the sharpest single distinction this section establishes: reception (受) is "confined strictly to what is pure" — any defilement mixed into the ordination moment itself and reception simply fails to occur — while ongoing practice (隨) "encompasses both keeping and transgressing alike," since both are equally possible only after a genuine reception has already taken place. The guide reads this as clarifying something practically important: transgressing a precept is categorically different from never having received it in the first place. A person who breaks a precept they hold is still, in a real sense, practicing "ongoing practice" — badly — whereas someone who never received the precept-essence at all has no ongoing practice, good or bad, to speak of.

It also highlights the final exchange's own resolution of why ongoing conduct still deserves to be called "precept" rather than mere generic "wholesomeness": the guide's answer is scope — an ordinary wholesome act addresses whatever particular situation happens to arise, but ongoing precept-conduct is watchfulness of mind stretched across the entire Dharma-realm, inherited directly from a vow that was, from the very start, already universal in scope. The comprehensiveness is what earns it the stronger name.

The guide highlights how the "same essence" category quietly folds all three schools into a single comparative framework at once: whichever essence a given school assigns to the precept-essence — subtle matter for the Substantialists, neither matter nor mind for the Nominalists, mind-seed for the Consummate Teaching — received and ongoing unmanifest karma share that very same essence within that school's own account. It is ongoing conduct that actually "grips the weapon" moment to moment, and the guide reads this as reinforcing the very same point already made back in Milestone 15 about the essence needing to be continuously reinforced through practice: a precept-essence received once and then never actively drawn on again is, functionally, closer to an unused weapon than a functioning guard.

There is, in this precise sense, no such thing as guarding against a present transgression — only preventing a future one, or repairing a past one.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats this section as the practical payoff of the whole "breadth of the object conditioned" chapter: the received precept-essence, on its own, can guard neither the present moment (there is nothing there yet to guard against) nor anything at all, strictly speaking, without help — it is, in the guide's own phrase, merely "equipment" (防具), not itself the act of defense. Actually preventing a future transgression, or repairing a past one through repentance, requires ongoing conduct to pick up that equipment and use it.

The guide's closing image is the section's most memorable: a city wall, moat, bow, and blade are all genuinely capable of repelling an attacker — nobody doubts their real defensive power — and yet none of them do anything at all until someone actually takes hold of them and stands ready to fight. The received precept-essence, the guide notes, is exactly this kind of capacity: entirely real, entirely necessary, and still, on its own, entirely inert without a practitioner's ongoing, active engagement to put it to use, day after day, for the rest of one's life.

26. Milestone: The Object Must Be Universal — No Exceptions Allowed

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide's sharpest point in this section is the asymmetry it draws out: the number of precept-branches one receives varies by school and by which precepts one takes, and even the mental cause behind reception can vary — but the object, meaning the totality of sentient beings the vow reaches, is the one thing that cannot vary at all, on pain of the whole reception simply failing. "Not obtained from all sentient beings" does not mean a smaller, weaker precept results — it means, flatly, no precept at all.

The guide dwells especially on the "five kinds of mental reservation" as a genuinely practical warning against a very human temptation: quietly carving out an exception while going through the ordination ritual — "I'll keep this precept except toward that one person," "except in a war," "except after this particular date." Every one of these silent reservations, however small or seemingly reasonable, the text says, produces only ordinary wholesome merit, never the actual precept-essence itself. The guide reads the closing line — "a heart that cannot tolerate even the slightest trace of evil" — as the section's real thesis: genuine reception of the precepts is an all-or-nothing act, and even a single unstated exception is enough to disqualify it entirely.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide is careful to frame this closing section correctly: it is a technical demonstration of dharma-analysis method, not a doctrinal claim readers need to memorize or feel obligated to reconcile. The underlying question is genuinely narrow — given every possible object a precept could apply to (every category of sentient being, down to hell-beings and up to the highest heavens, plus non-sentient objects like wine itself) and every possible combination of the three afflictive poisons that might motivate a transgression, how many technically distinct "precepts" does a single act of receiving the five or eight lay precepts actually generate? The answer varies enormously — from fifteen up to sixty-three — purely as a function of which counting convention (the Sarvāstivādin treatise's own more restrictive method, or the broader inferential method) is being applied.

The guide highlights Master Hongyi's own closing caveat as the single most important takeaway from this entire technical section: every number in every table assumes the Sarvāstivāda school's premise that the five and eight lay precepts have only four branches each; the Tattvasiddhi school that this whole compendium actually follows holds that they have seven branches each, which would push every figure higher still. Readers, the guide suggests, should take away not the specific numbers but the underlying principle already established in the previous milestone: "a heart that cannot tolerate even the slightest trace of evil" — the arithmetic is simply a rigorous, almost obsessive way of making sure that comprehensiveness is never taken for granted.
