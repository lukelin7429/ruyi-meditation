The Visible Shape of a Kept Precept

Precept-Dharma names what was received, precept-essence names what took root at the moment of receiving it, and precept-conduct names the daily labor of living up to it — this fourth and last gate of the Part on the Tradition and Its Essence asks what all of that labor finally looks like from the outside, once it has actually taken hold. The answer, in Dao Xuan's own words, takes only a few lines: dignified deportment perfected, every movement in accord with the Dharma. This gate is the shortest in the entire book, and deliberately so — it names precept-marks' two senses and then, having named them, hands the far larger of the two whole to the Part that follows.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: 弘一律師《南山律在家備覽略編》(bfnn.org public-domain text)

1. Milestone: What Precept-Marks Are — Deportment That Speaks for Itself

Dao Xuan's own definition, quoted here in full, says everything this gate has to say about precept-marks in their first sense: not a rule recited, but a way of moving through the world that has become so thoroughly shaped by the precept-essence within that it is visible to anyone watching. Yuan Zhao's commentary then pauses on a single word — "accords" — to show exactly how this definition connects back to the conduct discussed in the gate just before it.

第四門
戒相
▲事鈔云『戒相者。威儀行成。隨所施造、動則稱法、美德光顯、故名戒相。』

Ru-Yi Translation

Gate Four
Precept-Marks
[Fresh citation] The Procedural Commentary says: "As for precept-marks: when dignified deportment has been perfected in practice, then in whatever one undertakes, one's every movement accords with the Dharma, and its beauty and virtue shine forth visibly — this is why it is called precept-marks."

Chinese Source

資持釋云『初即承前。隨下正示。 問、事鈔隨戒釋相篇中以戒本為相、與此異者。答。此約行明、彼就法辨。然行必循法、法必軌行。文云動則稱法、豈不明乎。』見事鈔記卷三

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The opening clause carries on from what came before; what follows it states the matter directly. Question: In the 'Explaining the Marks Precept by Precept' chapter [of the Procedural Commentary], the precept-text itself is treated as the mark — how does this differ from the present passage? Answer: this passage speaks in terms of conduct; that one distinguishes in terms of the rule. Yet conduct must always follow the rule, and the rule must always govern conduct — does not the text's own phrase 'one's every movement accords with the Dharma' already make this plain?" [Zichi Commentary, fascicle 3]

2. Milestone: Two Senses of Precept-Marks, and Where the Second One Lives

Having given the definition, the compendium immediately names the fork in the road: precept-marks can mean the outward shape of kept conduct, or it can mean the precept-rules themselves, laid out one by one. The first sense is what this gate has just shown in full. The second sense — the one most readers actually picture when they hear "precept-marks," a itemized ledger of what is and is not allowed — gets only a single pointing sentence here, because Dao Xuan's own root text already sends it elsewhere.

Chinese Source

戒相有二義、一約行為相、二以法為相。事鈔標宗顯德篇中、約行為相、即此略文也。事鈔隨戒釋相篇中、以法為相、即今編持犯篇所引據也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Precept-marks carry two senses: first, in terms of conduct as the mark; second, in terms of the rule itself as the mark. In the "Establishing the Tradition" chapter [of the Procedural Commentary], the marks are treated in terms of conduct as the mark — this is precisely the brief passage given above. In the "Explaining the Marks Precept by Precept" chapter [of the Procedural Commentary], the marks are treated in terms of the rule itself as the mark — this is precisely what the present compilation's Part on Upholding and Violation draws upon and cites.

Chinese Source

上來宗體篇中第四門戒相竟

Ru-Yi Translation

With this, the fourth gate of the Part on the Tradition and Its Essence — Precept-Marks — is concluded.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

It is worth pausing on just how little text this gate actually contains, because the brevity is not an accident of transmission — it is the book's own design. The companion study guide traces the logic of the whole sequence: the precept-Dharma gate asked what was received, the precept-essence gate asked what receiving it actually produced within the practitioner, the precept-conduct gate asked how that essence gets lived out day to day, and this gate now asks the natural last question — what does all of that finally look like, seen from outside? Dao Xuan answers in one sentence: dignified deportment perfected, every act in accord with the Dharma, its beauty and virtue shining forth of itself. That sentence is precept-marks in what the tradition calls its first sense, "conduct as the mark" — and it is genuinely all that needs saying about that sense, because it is describing an outcome, not a procedure.

The second sense is a different matter entirely, and the compendium is candid about why it gets no more than a pointer here. "As the rule itself as the mark" means precept-marks understood as an itemized code — each precept named, its boundary of violation drawn precisely, its gradations of severity worked out one by one. Dao Xuan's own Procedural Commentary keeps this material in a wholly separate chapter, and Hongyi's compendium follows that same division: rather than compress an enormous body of case-by-case detail into a few crowded lines under the Part on the Tradition and Its Essence, he sets it aside whole and opens an entirely new Part for it — the Part on Upholding and Violation, which is exactly where the book turns next. So the shortness of this gate is not a gap to be explained away; it is a signpost. The four gates of the Part on the Tradition and Its Essence — precept-Dharma, precept-essence, precept-conduct, precept-marks — have now been walked in full, each one shorter than the last as the compendium moves from the abstract toward the concrete, and this final gate's one-sentence definition, plus its one-sentence table of contents for what comes next, is the hinge on which the entire book now turns from theory toward the long, detailed practice of telling upheld precepts from broken ones.
