The Point of Leaving Home

Every gate before this one has addressed the layperson as a layperson — what to keep, what to avoid, how to act at the temple gate or the sickbed. This last gate turns the book's whole compass needle elsewhere: Master Hongyi explains, in his own opening words, that he places it here precisely because some readers of a book for laypeople may themselves be weighing whether to stop being laypeople at all. What follows is Dao Xuan's own account of what renunciation is actually for — three paths open to anyone, the hardships that should be known before, not after, and five qualities meant to outlast the first vow that named them.

1. Milestone: Why This Gate Comes Last

Master Hongyi opens the gate himself, in his own voice rather than through quotation, with a question-and-answer explaining an editorial choice: why should a compendium written for laypeople end with a gate about leaving lay life behind? His answer reframes the entire gate as a form of informed consent, offered before an irreversible step rather than after it. Dao Xuan's own citations then supply the two qualities — faith and wisdom — without which the step should not be taken at all.

問今輯在家備覽、何以最後列出家宗致門耶。答。凡俗士尚未出家、而欲出家者。須先了知出家之後、如何發心、如何苦行、自量己力、以定可否。若其力有未能、應即知難而退、不須率爾出家、免致將來憂悔。以是之故、出家宗致、為在家者所應預知。因以此門、殿於卷末焉。

Question: Now that this Compendium for Laypeople has been compiled, why place the gate on the point of leaving home last of all? Answer: An ordinary layperson who has not yet left the household life, but wishes to, must first come to understand what state of mind and what austerities await afterward, and must weigh his own capacity against them in order to decide whether he can actually go through with it. If his capacity should prove unequal to it, he ought to recognize the difficulty at once and step back, rather than rashly leaving home only to bring on future regret. For this reason, the point of leaving home is something a layperson ought to know in advance — and it is for this reason that this gate is placed at the very end of the fascicle.

The gate on the point of leaving home is divided into three chapters: Chapter One, the Original Intent of Renunciation; Chapter Two, Explaining the Hardships First; and Chapter Three, the Five Qualities One Should Know.

The Procedural Commentary states: "The rank of śrāmaṇera, once established, marks the very beginning of leaving the ordinary world. From the moment one is first touched by the profound scriptures, the mind is set toward what lies far off. From here one may rightly take up the teaching and properly apply it in carrying forward one's practice. Yet cases claimed in name only, propped up one by another, are common enough as one goes through the world." The Zichi Commentary explains: "Of the four pairs of phrases in this opening passage, which sets out the basic purpose while exposing spurious cases: the first two phrases show rank. Here one must distinguish two kinds of 'sameness' — sameness in form and sameness in Dharma: one who has merely shaved the head is called 'the same in form' as a śrāmaṇera, while one who has received the ten precepts is called 'the same in Dharma.' The next two phrases clarify the original aspiration: the upper phrase speaks of its beginning, the lower of its culmination. 'The profound scriptures' refers generally to the Buddha's teaching; 'what lies far off' points directly to the fruit of Buddhahood. The next two phrases show that the vinaya can be relied upon. The final two rebuke the world for lacking proper training."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Now, faith is the wellspring of the path and the mother of merit; wisdom is the cause of transcending the world and attaining liberation. One who leaves the household life must have these two before all else. If one does not understand this, one merely shaves the head and dons the robe in vain — inwardly the mind holds to no path, outwardly one's conduct follows no standard, and giving free rein to foolish feelings, one remains no different from the unclean and the worldly. This is why some enter the Dharma and yet, by the time their hair has turned white, still stand as if facing a blank wall at every turn: it is simply because, lacking faith of their own, sagely wisdom has no cause from which to arise. They only attend to nourishing the body — how could they ever know the surpassing karma that is the essential point of leaving home?" The Zichi Commentary explains: "Of the two parts of this passage clarifying the two gateways of faith and wisdom, the first sets out their essential point. The path is established by faith, hence it is called the wellspring of the path; merit is born from faith, hence the mother of merit. Cultivating karma toward liberation depends on the power of wisdom, and breaking through delusion lies in wisdom's illumination — hence it is the cause of liberation. Without faith, the path has nothing from which to arise; without wisdom, delusion has nothing by which to be removed. The passage beginning 'if one does not understand this' sets out the fault of failing to understand, first showing how form and mind alike become mixed with the worldly. 'Hair turned white' means having grown old; 'facing a wall' means seeing nothing at all. Without faith, wisdom does not arise; without wisdom, one does not long for the path; eating one's fill and staying warmly clothed, one idles away an entire lifetime."

[Both passages above are found in the Zichi Commentary, fasc. 41.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide reads Master Hongyi's own question-and-answer as the key to the entire gate's placement: this is not a book urging laypeople toward ordination, but a document of informed consent. Someone genuinely weighing renunciation deserves to know, in advance, what mindset and what hardships await — so that a mismatch between aspiration and actual capacity can be recognized honestly, before it becomes years of quiet regret rather than after.

It also singles out the faith-and-wisdom passage as the gate's real opening test: without these two conditions, shaving the head and donning robes changes nothing essential — the person underneath remains exactly as worldly as before, indistinguishable from anyone still living an ordinary lay life, however different the outward form. The guide reads "facing a wall" quite literally, as the image of someone who has spent an entire lifetime in robes and arrived at old age having still seen nothing.

2. Milestone: A Sevenfold Map of the Renunciate's Course

Before working through any of it, Dao Xuan's commentators name the shape of the whole first chapter: seven sections that trace a renunciant's course from initial motive through to a final, explicit comparison between the Hinayana and Mahayana paths. The commentary's own framing note — that a student who merely repeats these words without self-examination gains nothing from them — sets the tone for everything that follows.

The chapter on the original intent of renunciation is divided into seven sections: One, the Origin of Leaving Home; Two, the Benefit of Enabling Renunciation; Three, the Harm of Obstructing Renunciation; Four, Ordinary Wrongdoing Committed After Leaving Home; Five, Ordinary Merit-Making Committed After Leaving Home; Six, the Conduct of the Noble Path After Leaving Home; and Seven, Comparing Hinayana and Mahayana.

The Zichi Commentary states: "In setting out and listing these seven sections: the first three clarify merit, the next three clarify conduct, and the last resolves a doubt." [Zichi Commentary, fasc. 41]

The Jiyuan Commentary states: "Next, in listing these seven sections, the text comprehensively clarifies the marks of conduct running from the beginning to the end of leaving the household life. A student who has reached this point ought to examine himself closely. If he merely repeats the words without more, what benefit is there to himself? Cases of describing food without eating, of counting another's treasure without possessing it, are seen constantly before our very eyes." [Jiyuan Commentary, fasc. 11]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide does not linger long on this outline itself, but it does draw out the Jiyuan Commentary's warning as a thread running through the whole chapter: doctrine repeated without self-examination is worthless to the person repeating it. "Describing food without eating, counting another's treasure without possessing it" becomes, in effect, this gate's own standard for how it wants to be read — not as information to be filed away, but as a series of questions a genuine aspirant is expected to turn back on himself.

3. Milestone: Textual Proofs for Leaving Home

The first of the seven sections gathers scriptural testimony for why anyone would leave home at all — an Avataṃsaka verse on the bodhisattva's own example, a stark comparison between household and homelessness drawn from the Ugra-Householder Sūtra and the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, and a striking eighteen-item catalogue, drawn from the Jingzhuzi, of exactly what a renunciant does differently from everyone else.

▲業疏云『如華嚴偈。若有不識出家法、樂著生死不求脫。是故菩薩捨國財、為之出家求寂靜。五欲所縛不離家。欲令眾生解脫故。以此文證。眾生無始纏著家屬、無思解脫。故大士引出於世。』
濟緣釋云『華嚴二偈、前偈為不識故立法示之、後偈為著欲故方便引之。初偈、上二句示不識之過。下二句明立法之相。云菩薩者即指釋迦因行為言。捨國財者菩薩在家為王太子、次紹王位、國城財寶一切自在、欲明難捨猶須捨之、況餘凡庶不足戀矣。寂靜即涅槃果。後偈、初句示著欲。下三句明方便。文略下二句、具云、示現不樂處五欲、是故出家求解脫。以下疏家結顯推功歸佛。』

△業疏續云『廣如郁伽長者、涅槃經中。家及非家相比顯過、方起欣厭、得預法門。』
濟緣釋云『郁伽長者經、穢居品云。居家菩薩當知在家穢污之事常念作故名為居家、斷諸善根本是名居家。乃至居家如羅網如毒蛇如火燒身等。涅槃云。居家迫窄猶如牢獄一切煩惱由之而生、出家寬曠猶如虛空一切善法由之增長。若在居家不得盡壽淨修梵行、我今應當剃除鬚髮出家學道。』

The Commentary on Karman Procedures states: "As in the Avataṃsaka Sūtra's verse: 'If there are those who do not recognize the Dharma of leaving home, who delight in and cling to birth-and-death and do not seek liberation — for this reason the bodhisattva relinquishes his kingdom and his wealth, and for their sake leaves home in search of tranquility. Bound by the five desires, they do not leave their households; the bodhisattva does this in order to bring about the liberation of sentient beings.' This passage serves as proof: sentient beings, entangled in their households since beginningless time, give no thought to liberation — and this is why the great being draws them forth from the world." The Jiyuan Commentary explains: "Of these two Avataṃsaka verses, the first is set up to show the Dharma to those who fail to recognize it; the second draws in, through skillful means, those attached to desire. In the first, the upper two lines show the fault of not recognizing it; the lower two clarify the mark of setting up the Dharma. 'Bodhisattva' refers to Śākyamuni's own causal practice. 'Relinquishes his kingdom and wealth' refers to how, while still a householder, he was a crown prince destined to inherit the throne, with the kingdom's treasures entirely at his command — the point being that even what is hardest to relinquish must still be relinquished, how much more should ordinary people feel no attachment to lesser things. 'Tranquility' is the fruit of nirvāṇa. In the second verse, the first line shows attachment to desire, the lower three clarify skillful means; the quoted text abbreviates the last two lines, which in full read: 'manifesting displeasure at dwelling among the five desires, for this reason he leaves home in search of liberation.' The commentary's concluding words trace this merit back to the Buddha as its source."

The Commentary on Karman Procedures continues: "This is set out at length in the Ugra-Householder Sūtra and the Nirvāṇa Sūtra: household life and the state of no longer having a household are set side by side to reveal the fault of the former, so that a sense of longing and revulsion first arises, and one becomes fit to enter the Dharma-gate." The Jiyuan Commentary explains: "The Ugra-Householder Sūtra, in its chapter on the defilements of household life, says: 'A bodhisattva living at home should know that household life means constantly thinking of and doing defiled things — this is precisely why it is called household life; it is the very uprooting of every wholesome root, and this too is called household life,' down to: 'household life is like a net, like a poisonous snake, like a fire burning the body,' and so forth. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: 'Household life is cramped and narrow, like a prison — every affliction is born from it; leaving home is spacious and open, like empty space — every wholesome dharma grows from it. If, while remaining a householder, I am unable to practice pure conduct to the end of my life, I ought now to shave off my hair and beard and leave home to study the path.'"

The Zichi Commentary further states: "The Jingzhuzi says that leaving home involves eighteen things difficult to do, which the renunciant is nonetheless able to do. Filial love for one's parents is hard to set aside — yet he takes leave of his kin. Attachment to wife and children is hard to relinquish — yet he cuts off this love. Power and rank are what worldly sentiment competes for — yet he casts aside such glory. Hunger and hardship are what people find hard to endure — yet he moderates his food. Rich flavors are what people crave — yet he is content with coarse vegetable fare. Diligent exertion is what people grow weary of — yet he remains diligent and austere. The seven kinds of treasure are what people are loath to part with — yet he gives them up entirely. Coin and cloth are what people hoard — yet he casts them away. Servants are what people rely on — yet he provides for himself and employs no one. The five colors are what people delight to look upon — yet he abandons them without a backward glance. The eight kinds of music are what people vie to hear — yet he cuts himself off from listening at all. Fine ornaments are what people cherish — yet whether things are fine or coarse makes no difference to him. Caring for the body is what everyone alike does — yet he is able to forget his own form and give up his life. Sleep is something no one can avoid — yet he does not lie down to sleep, day or night. Indulging the appetite and roaming with companions is what people habitually do — yet he dwells in stillness and examines himself. Householders know no limit to eating and drinking — yet to him such things, brought near the mouth, are like poison. Householders find something to their liking at every hour — yet he restricts himself to fixed watches, keeping his stomach empty. Householders match themselves to ornate halls and to a spouse — yet he takes to dwelling among the graves, cut off from all attachment. This was composed by Xiao Ziliang, Prince Wenxuan of the Qi dynasty; being of such importance, it is recorded here."

[The three passages above are found in the Jiyuan Commentary, fasc. 11, and the Zichi Commentary, fasc. 41, respectively.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide highlights the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's prison-versus-open-sky image as the section's emotional center: household life as a cramped cell from which every affliction is born, renunciation as open space in which every wholesome quality can actually grow. It treats the Jingzhuzi's eighteen-item list as the section's practical center — not abstract doctrine but a concrete, repeatedly uncomfortable inventory of exactly what ordinary attachment looks like, item by item, so that anyone reading it can honestly ask which single item they personally find hardest to imagine letting go of.

The guide is careful to note that this list is not meant as a checklist to complete once, but as a mirror for ongoing self-examination — renunciation, on this reading, is not simply a change of clothing or title, but a sustained willingness to move against the grain of ordinary human wanting in every one of these very ordinary places at once.

4. Milestone: Exhortations to Practice

Having established why renunciation is worth pursuing, the second part of this section turns to why it so often fails in practice — beginning with a blunt diagnosis of a degenerate age, moving through Nāgārjuna's famously severe comparison of the unpracticing human being to an animal, and arriving at a definition of "conduct of the path" that turns out to require no special activity at all, only a mind unstained by whatever it happens to encounter.

▲業疏云『然世濁惑深、厭苦求樂。初雖欣出終墜欲海。不修行業。故徒行也。』
濟緣釋云『初斥濁世不修二、初示時機。謂厭在家營趁之苦、求出家供給之樂。即涅槃云為衣食而出家者。初下次明退墮。詩云、靡不有初、鮮克有終。即斯類矣。徒行謂出家無益也。』

△業疏續云『如智論云。六情根完具、智鑒亦明利。而不求道法、唐受身智慧。禽獸皆亦知、欲樂以自恣。而不知方便、為道修善事。既已得人身、宜勉自利益。不知修道行、與彼亦何異。龍樹引誡、為極言也。聞而不行、猶是不聞。今重引告、何得不用。』
濟緣釋云『二引聖論極誡二、初論文三偈、初偈。上二句明報勝、下二句嗟不學。六情根者情即是識、謂根識無缺也。智能鑒物故云智鑒。唐虛也。身對初句、智慧對次句。次一偈舉況。畜是癡報但知欲樂。即經所謂但念水草餘無所知也。後五偈中。上半合初偈、下半合次偈。龍下結告勸依。必應此偈出在他經故云引誡。以畜況人、為誡深切、故云極言。』

△業疏續云『道行何耶。一切無染者是也。良由眾生無始封著、是此是彼是得是失。因之起染、纏縛有獄。故世鈍者多著財色、少有利者多貪名見。四科束之、尟不收盡。終歸死去何事迷乎。』
濟緣釋云『三示所修行相二、初示道行。以偈文未顯、故特徵示用開心路。一切之言通收善惡塵境、染惡則增業、染善則成障。能於日用所行所學觸境無染、無染之智即是般若、背塵合覺絕縛入道必始於此。深可體究、慎勿誦文。良下反釋所以又三、初示染本。封著是貪愛、此彼得失即分別。由斯二種輪迴不息、能離此者即名道行、非別有道、故下云道在虛通達累為本是也。故下次舉世情。財收八穢、色乃荒婬、名謂虛聲、見即妄執。上二常流所著故為鈍、下二學者所求故為利。此且一往分之、然有具四或復互輕不必一定。尟猶無也。終下重誡。凡在同徒、用斯自照、有一於是、未脫輪迴。積財荒色、諍見沽名、跡混世塵、不思出要、形出心沒、何所利乎。』

The Commentary on Karman Procedures states: "Yet in this degenerate world delusion runs deep, and people weary of suffering while seeking pleasure. Though they may at first delight in leaving home, they end by sinking back into the sea of desire, never cultivating the practice — and so their going forth comes to nothing." The Jiyuan Commentary explains: "Of the two parts of this rebuke of the degenerate world's failure to cultivate: the first shows the disposition of the age — wearying of the toil of household life, and seeking instead the ease of being provided for. This is exactly what the Nirvāṇa Sūtra means by 'leaving home for the sake of food and clothing.' The passage beginning 'though they may at first' then clarifies backsliding — as the Book of Odes says, 'none fail to have a beginning; few manage to have an end.' 'Their going forth comes to nothing' means that leaving home brings them no benefit."

The Commentary on Karman Procedures continues: "As the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says: 'The six sense faculties are complete, and the mirror of one's understanding is bright and keen — and yet, if one does not seek the Dharma of the path, one has received this body and this understanding in vain. Even birds and beasts know how to indulge themselves in pleasure; yet they do not know the skillful means of cultivating wholesome deeds for the sake of the path. Having already obtained a human body, one ought to strive for one's own true benefit — for if one does not know how to cultivate the path, how is one any different from the beasts?' Nāgārjuna's admonition here is meant as the most direct and severe kind of warning. To hear it and not put it into practice is no different from never having heard it at all — now that it is cited here once more, how can it not be taken to heart?" The Jiyuan Commentary explains: "Of the two parts of this passage citing a noble treatise as the most severe admonition — the treatise text itself comprises what corresponds to three verses. In the first, the upper two lines clarify the superiority of one's karmic reward, the lower two lament the failure to cultivate. 'The six sense faculties' means the faculties and their consciousness lack nothing. That understanding can illuminate things is why it is called 'the mirror of understanding.' 'In vain' means empty, wasted. The next verse offers an analogy: an animal, being the fruit of delusion, knows only pleasure — exactly what the sutra means by 'thinking only of water and grass, and knowing nothing beyond that.' The passage beginning 'Nāgārjuna' concludes the exhortation. As this verse must come from a different scripture than the one just named, it is called 'an admonition here cited'; and because likening a human being to an animal makes for a deeply pointed warning, it is called 'the most direct and severe kind of statement.'"

The Commentary on Karman Procedures continues: "What, then, is 'conduct of the path'? It is simply being unstained by anything at all. This is because sentient beings, since beginningless time, have clung fast to distinctions — this versus that, gain versus loss — and from this clinging, defilement arises, binding them fast within the prison of saṃsāra. Hence those of dull faculties are mostly attached to wealth and sensual form, while those of somewhat sharper faculties are mostly greedy for fame and for views taken as correct. Bind all this together under these four categories, and scarcely anything is left uncovered. In the end all return to death — over what, then, should one remain so deluded?" The Jiyuan Commentary explains: "Of the two parts of this passage, showing the mark of the practice to be cultivated — the first shows 'conduct of the path.' Since the verse itself had not made this explicit, the text specially points it out, to open the way for the mind. The word 'all' comprehensively includes both wholesome and unwholesome objects: being stained by evil increases karma, while being stained even by good becomes an obstruction. To remain unstained no matter what one encounters, amid one's daily conduct and study — this unstained understanding is itself prajñā; turning one's back on the dust of the senses and entering the path must necessarily begin right here. This deserves deep investigation — one must be careful not to merely recite the words. The passage beginning 'this is because,' explaining the reason, has three further parts. The first shows the root of defilement: 'clinging fast' is craving; 'this versus that, gain versus loss' is discriminating thought — because of these two, the wheel of rebirth turns without cease; one who leaves them behind is precisely what is meant by 'conduct of the path,' there being no separate path apart from this. The passage beginning 'hence' next raises worldly sentiment: wealth is the eight kinds of defilement, sensual form is dissolute lust, fame is empty reputation, and wrong views are mistaken clinging — the first two being what ordinary people are attached to, hence 'dull'; the latter two being what students of the path tend to seek, hence 'sharp' — though this division is only rough, since a person may hold all four at once in varying degrees. The passage beginning 'in the end' repeats the admonition: all who belong to the same company should use this to examine themselves — if even one of these is present, one has not yet escaped the wheel of rebirth. Amassing wealth, indulging in lust, contending over views, currying fame, mixing one's tracks with the dust of the world, giving no thought to what is essential — one's form has gone forth, yet one's mind has sunk back into the world: of what benefit is that?"

[The three passages above are all found in the Jiyuan Commentary, fasc. 11.]

"One's form has gone forth, yet one's mind has sunk back": the Commentary on Karman Procedures glosses this as meaning that although one's outward form has attached itself to the path, one's mind has sunk back into the world.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide calls Nāgārjuna's verse "the most direct and severe kind of statement" for good reason, and lingers on why the animal comparison lands as hard as it does: a being with complete faculties and a clear mind who still fails to seek the path has, in effect, wasted precisely what an animal never had the chance to use in the first place — even beasts know how to pursue pleasure, so a human who does no more than that has gained nothing his human birth was actually for.

It reads the "unstained mind" passage as this section's real payoff, and stresses the Jiyuan Commentary's own caution against misreading it: staying unstained applies just as much to good objects as to bad ones — becoming attached even to something wholesome still counts as an obstruction. The guide frames "conduct of the path," on this account, as needing no special activity at all: it is available in the middle of ordinary daily life, the instant attachment of any kind, positive or negative, stops taking hold.

5. Milestone: The Benefit of Enabling Renunciation, and the Harm of Blocking It

Two short sections form a matched pair: the vast merit of helping someone leave home for the sake of liberation, set directly against the correspondingly vast harm of standing in the way of it — whether as a reluctant family member or an interfering official.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide draws out why the merit of enabling renunciation is said to exceed even a century of feeding arhats: an offering, however generous, remains karma of the desire realm, and desire-realm karma is eventually exhausted no matter how large it starts out; enabling someone's genuine bid for liberation is not bound to any of the three realms at all, and so admits of no such ceiling. It reads this as a deliberately practical point aimed at readers of exactly this book — whatever one's own path turns out to be, supporting someone else's authentic renunciation is itself a way of taking part in that same unbounded merit.

On the matching warning about obstruction, the guide notes how ordinary the two examples are: a family that will not give consent, an official who forbids it by decree — neither villain, just people exercising ordinary authority over someone else's life-choice. The severity of the stated consequence is the section's real point: obstructing another's path to liberation is treated as serious in direct proportion to how serious the merit of enabling it is.

The guide gives its own extended retelling of the sixty-bhikṣu story as the section's emotional centerpiece: hearing the very same teaching on how hard material gain is to digest, one group of sixty listeners — already pure — heard it and immediately attained arhatship; a second group of sixty, having already transgressed, felt such shame and fear that blood poured visibly from their faces; and a third group of sixty, newly ordained and simply afraid of what they had just heard, chose to return to lay life outright. One teaching, three utterly different outcomes, each depending entirely on the state the listener already carried into the room.

It also draws a direct line from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's prophecy — corrupt renunciants driving out or even harming those who genuinely keep the precepts — to a pattern the guide calls timelessly recognizable: people who lack real virtue themselves, threatened by the visible contrast a genuinely disciplined person creates, respond not with admiration but with jealousy and active hostility. Bad currency driving out good, the guide notes, is exactly as old as this text.

7. Milestone: Ordinary Merit-Making After Leaving Home

Even sincere religious activity, this section warns, can remain merely "ordinary merit" rather than the noble path — precept-keeping pursued as an end in itself, worldly meditative absorption, learning pursued for reputation, or pious donation tangled up with self-regard. The commentary is careful, though, to close this section by insisting the problem is never the activity itself, only the mind behind it.

Yet since the mind behind the act is not genuinely evil, one does not actually take rebirth in that evil destiny; and since the good deed was in fact accomplished, one still receives a favorable station." The Jiyuan Commentary explains: "The fourth, on busying oneself with affairs, has two parts: the first clarifies the conduct and its fruit; the passage beginning 'because' traces this back to its mental cause — wishing to prevail over others, one loves oneself and resents others; wishing to gain recognition, one engages in flattery and deception. Since these accumulate in such quantity, they are likened to a tree. 'The evil destiny' is the general retribution; 'a favorable station' is the particular retribution."

The Jiyuan Commentary states: "Foolish people, seeing this, at once conclude that precept-keeping and extensive learning are simply not worth doing at all — not realizing that it is only aimless practice, lacking any real attainment, that constitutes mere worldly merit; if one is genuinely seeking liberation, there is nothing here that is not the noble path. It is only a matter of whether the mind is open or obstructed — how could the practice itself ever be abandoned on that account?"

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide singles out the Jiyuan Commentary's closing clarification as the section's most important corrective, precisely because the preceding material is so easy to misread. Someone could come away from the warnings about precept-keeping, meditation, learning, and donation concluding that none of these practices are worth doing — but the guide insists the text says the opposite: the deeds themselves are never the problem, only whether the mind behind them is "open" or "obstructed." The very same act of keeping a precept, sitting in meditation, studying a sutra, or making an offering can be either ordinary worldly merit or the noble path itself, depending entirely on that one variable.

It reads the warning about mistaking precept-keeping for the whole of the path as a pointed one, still relevant to any community of practitioners: looking down on other approaches — study, contemplation, other forms of practice — while treating one's own discipline as uniquely correct is itself named here as a subtle form of clinging, no less an obstruction than the very laxity it feels superior to.

8. Milestone: The Noble Path After Leaving Home — Three Modes of Seeing

Against the two preceding sections' warnings, this section finally describes what actually counts as the noble path: three progressively subtler modes of contemplation, shared by the Hinayana practitioner, the lesser bodhisattva, and the great bodhisattva, each seeing the same phenomena through a different depth of understanding. A closing summary then steps back to show how wrongdoing, worldly merit, and the noble path together account for the causes of every one of the ten dharma-realms.

The third is the conduct of the great bodhisattva: contemplating phenomena as mind itself, one perceives them as discriminated thought expressed in mental speech. Hence the Compendium of the Great Vehicle says: from the stage of aspiration and delight up through the ultimate stage, this is called 'contemplation of the middle,' taking as its object the discrimination of mental speech." The Jiyuan Commentary explains: "In all three modes of contemplation, 'phenomena' refers to the object of contemplation, comprehensively including all dharmas; the text cites self, person, good, and evil merely as examples. In the first, one contemplates arising and ceasing, and upon cessation sees emptiness. In the second, one contemplates illusory transformation, and sees emptiness at the very point of arising. In the third, one contemplates mind-only, seeing that arising, ceasing, and illusory transformation are nothing but the transformations of mind — the lesser sages of the two vehicles see this and call it merely 'empty,' without knowing that arising and ceasing are fundamentally the tathāgatagarbha itself. Hence the great bodhisattva alone fully understands mind-only, perfectly cultivating all three contemplations at once, favoring neither nature nor mark — hence 'the middle way.' 'Discrimination expressed in mental speech' means that, in contemplating all dharmas, it is entirely through the mind's reflection that one discriminates what lies before one; the ten thousand dharmas all return to thought, which never departs from one's own mind — gathering the branch back into the root, this is called 'mind-only.' 'The stage of aspiration and delight' comprehensively includes the stage of expedient practice and the three worthy stages; 'the ultimate stage' refers to the final stage of sublime enlightenment. Though the depth of practice varies, all alike contemplate consciousness-only, which is itself the middle way." The Zichi Commentary explains: "In all three contemplations, 'phenomena' is precisely the object contemplated — meaning form, mind, the aggregates, the sense-bases, the sense-realms, sentient and insentient things, and what is wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral, and so on. If speaking in terms of intellectual understanding, one must penetrate all dharmas; but in terms of actual practice at any given moment, contemplating the mind matters most — whatever the mind gives rise to, wholesome or unwholesome, is itself the 'phenomenon.' Illuminating it with these three modes of principle, one knows it as distortion: there is only false discrimination, with nothing actually there; following the mind's activity, everything is empty. Some contemplate by tracing marks back to their nature, called 'emptiness of nature'; some by recognizing the mark itself as illusory, called 'emptiness of mark'; some by penetrating that the mark is mind itself, called 'consciousness-only' — like a dream, whether one traces where the dream-thought arises and ceases, or knows the dream's own mark to be unreal even while dreaming, or knows it is mind-only that has transformed, with no dream-event apart from mind. These serve as analogies for the shallower or deeper senses of the three contemplations. Now, the stages of practice are three, yet the object contemplated is but one — 'phenomena.' But the principle seen is of two kinds: in the first two, though nature and mark differ, both take emptiness as their principle; in the last, mind itself is the principle. The first two are provisional, the last is ultimate. Yet since leaving home transcends the world and its study spans all three vehicles, the present text, following the Commentary's intent to reconcile them, specifically singles out the Buddha-vehicle as the true basis of leaving home. In the passage on emptiness of nature: since the nature of self, person, good, and evil is fundamentally without existence, arising when conditions come together and ceasing the moment they disperse — the very point where arising and ceasing cease is called the principle of emptiness, the utmost that the two vehicles can reach. In the passage on the great bodhisattva: since all dharmas are fundamentally but a single consciousness, and apart from this consciousness there is no separate dharma at all, false movement of mind since beginningless time has conceived of an inner and an outer, this and that. Investigated to its root, this difference is entirely the false arising of grasping through mental intention, and it is because of this grasping that names and words are falsely constructed. This is why the wise, wishing to contemplate consciousness-only, must take mental-speech-discrimination as their object, since this discrimination is itself nothing but the one consciousness — one does not depart from thought, and yet, right within difference, one penetrates to no-difference."

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide presents the three contemplations as a single ladder rather than three unrelated methods: the Hinayana practitioner sees phenomena arise and cease and infers, from that impermanence, an underlying emptiness; the lesser bodhisattva skips the inference and sees emptiness directly in the very appearance of things, like recognizing a mirage the instant it appears rather than reasoning one's way to the conclusion; and the great bodhisattva goes further still, recognizing that arising, ceasing, and apparent emptiness are all themselves nothing but the mind's own activity — not three separate stages of getting closer to truth, but three different depths of seeing the very same thing.

It treats the closing summary — wrongdoing, worldly merit, and the noble path together spanning the causes of all ten dharma-realms — as this gate's real thesis statement, tying back directly to the three-part framework named at the very start of the chapter: renunciation, properly understood, is specifically oriented toward the third of these three paths, never the second, however much ordinary merit-making might resemble it on the surface.

10. Milestone: The Mahayana Three Trainings

The Mahāyāna's own three trainings expand the same basic structure: precepts divided into three groupings rather than one, a famous exchange from the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom on whether true insight into reality could ever coexist with broken precepts, a carefully fenced-off teaching on skillful transgression reserved for advanced bodhisattvas alone, and the well-known simile of a drowning traveler refusing even the smallest concession to a demon begging for his life-raft.

The Zichi Commentary explains: "Of the two parts of this passage — the first marks out similarity and difference. 'Three groups' refers to the three collections of Mahāyāna precepts. Since they do not cultivate skillful means to teach sentient beings, they lack the collection of embracing wholesome dharmas; and since they only liberate themselves, they lack the collection of benefiting sentient beings — this is why the present text compares similarity and difference only with respect to the restraint-precepts. It says: the precepts divide into three groups in the sense that, considered in terms of underlying meaning, they do not differ from the vinaya rules — since this is not explicitly legislated, it is said to hold 'in terms of meaning.' If this holds for one precept, the rest follow by the same pattern; this applies equally to every precept against a naturally wrong act. As for the restraining precepts, such as against alcohol or jewels: abstaining from the transgression of hoarding or drinking is the restraint-precepts; constantly applying the antidote is embracing wholesome dharmas; and putting to rest worldly reproach is benefiting sentient beings. If one divides according to the Lesser and Greater Vehicles' own root precept-texts, the Hinayana has four defeats while the Mahāyāna has ten grave precepts; the four defeats are largely the same in both, while the remaining six Mahāyāna grave precepts all differ — extending to matters such as hoarding jewels or self-immolation, the differing particulars grow numerous. As with false speech: abstaining from every condition of falsehood is itself constantly speaking truthfully, and so on."

[Jiyuan Commentary, fasc. 11]

△事鈔續云『智論。問云。菩薩住於實相、不得一法。得破戒否。答曰。以住於實相故、尚不作福、何況作罪。雖種種因緣、不破戒人。』
資持釋云『二廣示異。檅三、初智論住實相者心冥妙理、空無所有故不得一法。既無所得則無善惡、既無善惡則無持破、既無持破則無有戒、既無有戒則應任意施為不須守戒。世多邪見、故問決之。答中、以福況罪。不作福者不取福相故云不作。種種因緣謂方便化導、隨所動用皆離過故。』

△事鈔又云『攝論云。菩薩得無分別智、一切塵不顯現、由有勝智方便、具行殺生等十惡。由前有利益故、自無染濁過失。縱有利益、有過失不應行。準此初地已上方得用此無分別智、故地前不合。』
資持釋云『二攝論中。勝智即無分別。方便謂誘化眾生。準下判位。初地已上者故知十聖方許行之。地前三賢猶制不合、況餘凡愚安可僭濫。』
濟緣云『無分別智謂觀諸法如實平等、故無分別。唯一真體、外塵本無、故塵不顯現。方便即權巧。有利益者謂利他也、自無染者謂自利也。具此二利雖行無犯、互有所缺亦不行之。故云縱有等。今時愚人不量位地、不知權行。作惡無恥、妄引為例。自誤誤他、難可救也。』

見業疏記卷十一

△事鈔續云『涅槃。持息世譏嫌戒、與性重戒無別。因說菩薩持戒相、羅剎乞浮囊喻。』
資持釋云『三涅槃中、初明持相。息世譏嫌即目遮戒。遮性等持故云無別。次以喻顯。渡海人喻菩薩、羅剎喻三毒、浮囊喻具戒。』

見事鈔記卷四十一

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The Treatise asks: does a bodhisattva who abides in ultimate reality, apprehending not a single dharma, thereby break the precepts? It answers: precisely because he abides in ultimate reality, he does not even make merit — how much less does he commit an offense? Though he may act through all manner of causes and conditions, he does not become a person who has broken the precepts." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The second broadly shows the differences, through three citations. The first, from the Treatise: 'abiding in ultimate reality' means the mind is merged with the subtle principle, empty of anything at all, and so apprehends not a single dharma. Since there is nothing apprehended, there is neither good nor evil; since there is neither, there is neither keeping nor breaking precepts; since there is neither keeping nor breaking, there is, in that sense, no precept at all — and one might conclude one should act however one pleases, with no need to keep any precept. Since the world holds many such wrong views, the question is raised to resolve the matter. In the answer, the point about merit is used to illustrate the point about offense: 'does not make merit' means he does not grasp at the mark of merit. 'All manner of causes and conditions' refers to skillful means used to guide beings — whatever he does remains free of transgression."

The Procedural Commentary also states: "The Compendium of the Great Vehicle says: a bodhisattva who has attained non-discriminating wisdom, for whom no object of the senses any longer appears as fixed and real, may, by virtue of this excellent wisdom and its skillful means, actually carry out all ten evil acts, including killing. Because there is benefit to others behind this, he himself incurs no defiling fault. But even where there is benefit, if there is also fault, he should not act. On this basis, only a bodhisattva who has reached the first ground and above may make use of this non-discriminating wisdom — one who has not yet reached the first ground is not permitted to do so." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The second, on the Compendium: 'excellent wisdom' is non-discriminating wisdom itself; 'skillful means' refers to drawing in and transforming sentient beings. 'The first ground and above' — from which we know this is permitted only to the ten grounds of the sages. Even the three worthy stages prior to the first ground are still forbidden from it — how much more, then, could ordinary, foolish people ever presume to overstep in this way?" The Jiyuan Commentary states: "Non-discriminating wisdom means seeing all dharmas as they really are, equal and undifferentiated, and so being free of discrimination. There being only the single true essence, external sense-objects are fundamentally without existence, and so 'no object appears.' 'There being benefit' refers to benefiting others; 'himself incurring no defilement' refers to benefiting himself. When both these benefits are present together, such action, though carried out, incurs no transgression; but where either is lacking, it must not be done. Foolish people today, without gauging their own stage or ground, and without understanding what genuine skillful action actually requires, commit evil shamelessly and wrongly cite this passage as their precedent — deceiving both themselves and others, in a way that is difficult indeed to remedy."

[Jiyuan Commentary, fasc. 11]

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The Nirvāṇa Sūtra: keeping the precepts that put to rest worldly reproach and suspicion is no different from keeping the grave precepts against what is naturally wrong. On this occasion it goes on to explain the mark of a bodhisattva's precept-keeping, through the simile of the rākṣasa demon begging for the float-bag." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The third, on the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, first clarifies the mark of precept-keeping: 'putting to rest worldly reproach and suspicion' refers to the restraining precepts; that these are kept on a par with the naturally-wrong precepts is why it says 'no different.' Next, the text illustrates this through a simile: the person crossing the sea is likened to the bodhisattva, the rākṣasa is likened to the three poisons, and the float-bag is likened to the precepts kept in their entirety."

[Zichi Commentary, fasc. 41]

▲事鈔云『若論定慧。小觀相空、深觀唯識。鈍見空時不分別色、利知唯識不分別空。』
資持釋云『次定慧中。由小菩薩涉於大小、小據觀智大約志求、小大雖異並菩薩乘故。且一往通收大中。初示觀別。如上所明。次校淺深。鈍即小菩薩、在大為鈍望小則利、不分別色異上二乘析色故。利即大菩薩、不分別空超過菩薩故。由觀唯識住於中道、了一切法無非心識、識非色空非不色空、尚不分別識何況分別空、若知唯識則住實相無分別故。』

見事鈔記卷四十一

The Procedural Commentary states: "As for concentration and wisdom: the lesser bodhisattva contemplates the emptiness of characteristic-marks, while the great bodhisattva contemplates consciousness-only. The dull, at the moment of seeing emptiness, no longer discriminate form; the sharp, in knowing consciousness-only, no longer discriminate even emptiness itself." The Zichi Commentary explains: "Next, on concentration and wisdom: since the 'lesser bodhisattva' here straddles both Lesser and Greater — lesser in terms of the contemplating wisdom used, yet greater in terms of what is aspired to — though the two differ, both alike belong to the bodhisattva vehicle, and so both are here comprehensively included within 'the Mahāyāna.' Next the text compares their relative depth: 'the dull' is the lesser bodhisattva — dull when set against the great bodhisattva, yet sharp when set against the two Hinayana vehicles, since he does not discriminate form the way they do through analytical dissection. 'The sharp' is the great bodhisattva, who does not discriminate even emptiness, thereby surpassing the lesser bodhisattva. By contemplating consciousness-only, one abides in the middle way, understanding that all dharmas are nothing but mind and consciousness; one does not even discriminate consciousness itself, how much less would one discriminate emptiness — for to know consciousness-only is precisely to abide in ultimate reality, free of discrimination."

[Zichi Commentary, fasc. 41]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats the exchange on whether a bodhisattva abiding in ultimate reality could break the precepts as clearing up a real misunderstanding of Mahāyāna emptiness teaching: if someone who has genuinely realized emptiness no longer even grasps at the notion of "making merit," it would be absurd to think such a person could turn around and commit deliberate wrongdoing — the same realization that empties out attachment to good removes any basis for attachment to evil as well.

On the more delicate teaching that permits an advanced bodhisattva to carry out an ordinarily forbidden act for the sake of benefiting others, the guide is emphatic about the fence built around it: this is explicitly restricted to bodhisattvas who have already reached the first ground and beyond — not even the three worthy stages just prior to that threshold are permitted it, let alone an ordinary person. It reads the commentary's closing rebuke of those who "wrongly cite this as a precedent" as a warning that stays exactly as relevant now as when it was written: invoking "skillful means" or "compassionate expedient action" to excuse one's own misconduct, without having remotely reached the level of realization the teaching actually requires, deceives both oneself and others at once.

The guide reads the closing simile — a person crossing the sea refusing even the smallest concession to a demon begging piece by piece for the life-raft that keeps him afloat — as this section's most memorable image: even a single, seemingly minor precept, the "restraining" rather than the gravest kind, deserves the same uncompromising defense as any of the great root precepts, because a raft holed anywhere at all still sinks its passenger just the same.

上來出家宗致中第一章出俗本意竟

11. Milestone: Explaining the Hardships First

Chapter Two turns from doctrine to plain practical counsel: before anyone is accepted for renunciation, the hardships involved should be spelled out honestly in advance, precisely so that willingness tested this early does not collapse later, once the reality actually sets in.

▲羯磨註云『僧祇云。若初欲出家者、為說苦事一食、一住、一眠、多學問。答能者、度。』

見隨機羯磨第三　次科亦爾

業疏釋云『為說苦事者以世網苦辛、多厭求樂、初雖慈許、終有退敗。一食者佛教之中、一食為本。託緣開二、不是長途。至今西域、統五天竺、常行一食。希見東華寺別兩頓。一住者一坐跏趺、周時方起、自非味重何以致斯。一眠者中夜之時、暫爾倚臥、分星月次、尋起緣念。多學者慧心常運、不許浮散也。』
濟緣釋云『世網苦辛謂工商士農經營求趁不容自安、人多厭之求安閑樂、故反說苦事知其志願。初慈許者謂師輒受也、終退敗者謂資疲厭也。一食中、初二句明本制。託下示緣開、因病開粥故。至下引據希下斥非。今時見便進噉、豈止兩頓。律崩法壞、一至於此。悲夫。周時謂盡一日。味重謂禪定樂。倚臥謂倚身而已。分星月次不使長久故。慧心常運即遺教云常自省察不令有失是也。』

The Commentary on the Karman Procedures states: "The Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya says: when someone first wishes to leave home, one should explain to him the hardships involved — eating only once a day, remaining seated in one posture, sleeping only briefly, and studying extensively. Only if he answers that he is able to bear these is he given leave to be ordained."

[Suiji Jiemo, fasc. 3; the parallel section elsewhere agrees.]

The Commentary on Karman Procedures explains: "The reason for explaining the hardships beforehand is that, since the toil of worldly entanglement is bitter, people mostly weary of it and seek ease instead; though a teacher may at first compassionately grant ordination out of sympathy, this in the end leads to backsliding and failure. As for 'eating only once a day': within the Buddha's teaching, a single daily meal is the basic rule; relying on certain circumstances a second meal may be permitted, but this is not meant as the ordinary practice. Even today in the Western Regions, throughout the five parts of India, a single daily meal remains the constant practice; it is rare to see, as in the monasteries of China, two separate meals taken. As for 'remaining seated in one posture': sitting once in full lotus until a full day-and-night has passed before rising — apart from someone who deeply savors meditative absorption, how could anyone manage this? As for 'sleeping only briefly': in the middle watch of the night, one lies down only for a short while, marking the movement of the stars and moon, and rises again shortly after to take up one's practice. As for 'studying extensively': the wisdom-mind is kept constantly active, and idle distraction is not permitted." The Jiyuan Commentary explains: "The toil of worldly entanglement refers to how artisans, merchants, scholars, and farmers, in pursuit of a livelihood, are never permitted a moment's ease; most people, wearying of this, seek instead a life of leisure — hence the vinaya deliberately explains the hardships of monastic life first, in order to test the sincerity of one's aspiration. 'At first compassionately grants' means the preceptor simply accepts him without more; 'in the end leads to backsliding' means the disciple grows weary once the true hardship sets in. In the passage on eating once a day, the first two sentences clarify the original rule; the passage beginning 'relying on' shows how circumstances allow an exception, since gruel is permitted on account of illness; the passage beginning 'even today' cites evidence, and the passage beginning 'it is rare' rebukes the departure from it. People nowadays eat whenever convenient — how could this stop at merely two meals? That the vinaya has crumbled and the Dharma decayed to such a degree is truly lamentable. 'A full day-and-night' means an entire day; 'savors deeply' refers to the bliss of meditative absorption. 'The wisdom-mind kept constantly active' is exactly what the Bequeathed Teaching Sūtra means by 'constantly examining oneself, and not permitting any lapse.'"

△資持云『僧祇、先說苦事。欲令知歎、免後悔故。四分、則有十種。謂能耐風、雨、寒、熱、飢、渴、毒蟲、惡言。一食。持戒。』

見事鈔記卷四十一

The Zichi Commentary states: "The Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya explains the hardships first, in order to let the aspirant know and be warned of them beforehand, so as to avoid later regret. The Four-Part Vinaya, for its part, lists ten such things: being able to endure wind, rain, cold, heat, hunger, thirst, venomous insects, and harsh speech; eating only once a day; and keeping the precepts."

[Zichi Commentary, fasc. 41]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads the requirement to explain hardship before granting ordination as a deliberate safeguard against precisely the pattern named earlier in the chapter — a teacher's compassionate but premature acceptance, followed by an aspirant's later exhaustion and retreat. Naming the difficulty in advance, rather than letting someone discover it only after committing, is itself framed as an act of genuine care, not discouragement.

It also notes the guide's own observation about how far actual practice in China had already drifted from the described standard even in Dao Xuan's own era — two daily meals where one was the ancient norm — and reads the commentator's exclamation of grief over this drift as a reminder that ideals stated in a text and practice as actually lived have always had some gap between them, one every generation of practitioners has to reckon with honestly rather than pretend away.

上來出家宗致中第二章先說苦事竟

12. Milestone: The Five Qualities Every Renunciant Should Carry — And the Book's Own Closing Word

The book's final chapter, and this page's final section, names five qualities a renunciant should know and hold for life — not a one-time vow recited at ordination, but a standing description of what renunciation actually is meant to look like, from the newest novice to the most senior monastic. In naming the fourth of these five as reverent devotion to the three trainings — precepts, concentration, and wisdom — this closing chapter quietly returns to the very framework with which the whole compendium first opened.

▲羯磨註云『如請僧福田經。沙彌應知五德。一者發心出家、懷佩道故。二者。毀其形好、應法服故。三者。永割親愛、無適莫故。四者委棄身命、遵崇道故。五者志求大乘、為度人故。』
業疏釋云『初德發心懷道者創發蒙俗、情厭諸有。唯欣出要、常懷佩也。佩謂帶持之者也。二德毀形應法者道俗路乖、反形易性。故割其所重、服彼所輕。明志絕奢靡、與世違也。三德割愛懷捨者以無始所親、慈戀難斷、終歸死別、然不早悟。今近割俗緣、遠成濟度、且從道業兩捨親疏也。故善見云、瓦缽貫肩、以四海為家居。知誰可愛憎也。四德委命遵道者明崇奉三學、死而有已也。五德志大度人者奉行極教、兼濟於他。』
濟緣釋疏云『初德中、初釋上句。唯下釋下句。二中、初通示。故下別釋。上二句釋反形、下二句釋易性。奢則華侈、靡謂美麗。三中、初敘情愛。世俗貪生不知有死故不早悟。今下釋永割。兩捨親疏。謂無適莫、適字音嫡。故下引證。四中。奉崇三學釋下遵道、死而有已釋上委命。五中。大乘窮理盡性謂之極教。』

見業疏記卷十一

▲資持云『此之五德、出家大要。五眾齊奉、不唯小眾。終身行之、不唯初受。疏云、斯德始終通於五眾、俱堪物養、人天師範、故使誦持、無輕受體及形服也。』

見事鈔記卷四十一

The Commentary on the Karman Procedures states: "As the Sutra on the Field of Merit in Inviting the Sangha says: a śrāmaṇera should know five qualities. First, generating the aspiration to leave home, cherishing and carrying the path within himself. Second, altering his appearance, so as to accord with the Dharma-robe. Third, permanently cutting off attachment to kin, with no partiality toward any one over another. Fourth, giving up body and life itself, in reverence for and devotion to the path. Fifth, aspiring to seek the Mahāyāna, for the sake of saving others." The Commentary on Karman Procedures explains: "The first quality, generating the aspiration and cherishing the path, means that, newly awakened from worldly ignorance, one's feelings weary of all conditioned existence; delighting solely in what is essential to liberation, one constantly cherishes and carries this aspiration. The second, altering one's appearance to accord with the Dharma, means that, since the path of the renunciant and that of the layperson diverge, one alters one's outward form and changes one's very disposition — relinquishing what is generally prized and instead wearing what is generally scorned, making plain that one's resolve has cut off all extravagance, at odds with the world. The third, cutting off attachment and embracing renunciation, means that, though affection for those one has been close to since beginningless time is hard to sever, one will in the end be parted from them by death regardless — yet most people fail to awaken to this early enough. Now, by cutting off worldly ties close at hand, one accomplishes, in the more distant future, the salvation of all beings, thereby relinquishing both intimate and distant relations alike for the sake of the path. Hence the Samantapāsādikā says: 'carrying an earthen bowl slung over the shoulder, making the four seas one's home' — who, then, is there left to love or resent? The fourth, giving up one's life in devotion to the path, means revering and upholding the three trainings even unto death, and no further. The fifth, aspiring to the Greater Vehicle and saving others, means upholding the ultimate teaching while also working for the salvation of others." The Jiyuan Commentary, glossing the Commentary, explains: "In the first quality, the first part explains the upper phrase, the passage beginning 'delighting solely' explains the lower. In the second, the first part gives a general account, and the passage beginning 'hence' the detailed explanation: the upper two phrases explain 'altering one's form,' the lower two explain 'changing one's disposition.' 'Extravagance' means excessive luxury; 'scorned' means what is admired for its beauty by the world, but which the renunciant relinquishes. In the third, the first part sets out worldly affection: ordinary people, clinging to life, do not reckon with death, and therefore fail to awaken to this early enough; the passage beginning 'now' explains 'permanently cutting off' — relinquishing both intimate and distant relations alike, meaning 'no partiality.' In the fourth, 'revering and upholding the three trainings' glosses 'devotion to the path,' and 'unto death, and no further' glosses 'giving up body and life.' In the fifth, the Mahāyāna's thorough investigation of principle and full realization of one's own true nature is what is meant by 'the ultimate teaching.'"

[Jiyuan Commentary, fasc. 11]

The Zichi Commentary states: "These five qualities are the essential outline of leaving home. All five assemblies of the ordained uphold them equally — they are not for novices alone. They are to be practiced throughout one's entire life, not merely at the time of first ordination. The Commentary says: these qualities apply, from beginning to end, to all five assemblies alike; all are thereby made worthy of receiving offerings from others and of serving as teachers to gods and humans — and it is for this reason that they are to be recited and upheld, with no one permitted to treat lightly either the precept-essence received or the outward form and robe that mark it."

[Zichi Commentary, fasc. 41]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide dwells on the third quality — cutting off attachment to kin — as the one it expects readers to find hardest, and reads its logic carefully: the point is not that family bonds do not matter, but that death will sever them regardless of anyone's wishes, so choosing to loosen that attachment deliberately, in service of a larger effort to help all beings, is simply facing an inevitability early and putting it to use, rather than being blindsided by it later with nothing gained. The image of an alms bowl slung over one shoulder, the whole world now equally home, is offered as what that loosened attachment actually looks like in practice — not coldness, but an evenness that no longer sorts people into those to love and those to resent.

It also stresses the Zichi Commentary's insistence that these five qualities are lifelong, not a formula recited once at ordination and then set aside — binding on every rank of the ordained sangha equally, from the newest novice to the most senior elder, for as long as that person remains a renunciant at all.

Reading note — the book's own full circle

This closing chapter's fourth quality — "giving up body and life itself, in reverence for and devotion to the path," glossed by the commentary as revering and upholding the three trainings unto death — is not an isolated flourish. Precepts, concentration, and wisdom are exactly the same three trainings this gate has been tracing throughout its seventh section, in both their Hinayana and Mahāyāna forms; and precepts specifically — 戒, the very first of the three — are what the entire compendium opened with, in its very first gate on the precept-Dharma, unpacking what precepts are in essence, in received substance, in ongoing conduct, and in outward mark.

Fourteen gates later, a book written to help laypeople keep those same precepts closes by turning to explain the deeper reason precepts exist at all: not as an end in themselves, but as the first of three trainings meant to carry a person, householder or renunciant alike, from ordinary wrongdoing, past ordinary worldly merit, and onto the noble path itself. A layperson who reads this book from its first gate to its last does not arrive at a different subject by the end — only at a fuller view of the same one, seen now from the vantage point of the very choice, leaving the household life, that the whole compendium was written to help a reader weigh honestly, with eyes open, before making it.
