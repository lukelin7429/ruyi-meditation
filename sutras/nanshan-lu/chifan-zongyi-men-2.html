The Grammar of Keeping and Breaking, Part Two: From the Anatomy of an Offense to the Rebuke of Its Despisers

Where Part One laid down the four-fold grammar of upholding and violating a precept, Part Two dissects the machinery by which a violation actually completes itself — the three stages of preparation on the way to a consummated offense, the seven ways an act can fall short and stall short of "root offense," the object-perception formulas that fix a violation's exact weight, and the difference focused versus diffuse intent makes across the four gravest precepts. It closes with Dao Xuan's own pastoral teaching that a single lapse never shatters every other precept at once, and his sustained rebuke of those who dismiss the Vinaya itself as a lesser, merely Hīnayāna concern — bringing the fifth gate, and the general theory of observance and violation, to its close.

1. Milestone: The Anatomy of an Offense — Prior, Root, and Subsequent Preparation

This gate's second half opens directly into the fifth chapter of the general theory, "Preparatory Stage and the Consummated Fruit" (方便趣果). Every act that ends in a violation passes through three periods: a preparatory stage before the act is complete, the root-stage at which it actually completes, and — a distinctively Nanshan refinement — a subsequent stage afterward, in which lingering satisfaction with the deed already done becomes a small offense in its own right. This section works through all three in turn, using the precept against unchastity as its running example.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary says: "Let us explain this now by reference to the precept against unchastity. When lustful intent has arisen within the mind but body and speech have not yet manifested it, this is called the distant preparatory stage; this incurs a lower-grade offense. When body and speech have already moved but the object has not yet been reached, this is called the next preparatory stage; this incurs a middle-grade offense. When one has actually arrived at the site of the object and bodily contact has begun, but the point of consummation has not yet been reached, this is called the near preparatory stage; this is a middle-grade offense of the graver sort."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Below this point, although the relative weights differ somewhat from case to case, the general pattern can be inferred from this model." [Both passages from Zichi Commentary, fascicle 27]

All three preparatory stages apply to the upper-grade offenses; the pattern may be inferred from the case just given. For offenses that stand alone at the middle or lower grade, following the various commentaries, only a distant and a near preparatory stage are distinguished — the distant stage being the same as above, while the next and near stages are merged together under the single name "near preparatory stage," both uniformly resulting in a lower-grade offense, with no further distinction drawn between them.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide reads this chapter as Nanshan working out, in fine grain, exactly what it means for an intention to "become" an offense rather than simply flickering and dying. Using unchastity as the test case, the guide walks the same three-stage ladder: a distant preparatory stage where lustful intent has arisen but body and speech have not yet moved at all (a lower-grade offense on its own); a next stage where body and speech have already set out but have not yet reached the object (a middle-grade offense); and a near preparatory stage, where the parties have physically arrived at the point of contact but consummation itself has not yet occurred (already a middle-grade offense of the graver sort, one step short of the full root-offense). The guide stresses that this is not legalistic hair-splitting for its own sake — it reflects a considered view that moral responsibility accumulates in real, measurable increments well before any "final" act, and that the precepts are meant to intervene at the earliest possible point.

2. Milestone: The Root-Stage, and What Comes After It

Having laid out the three sub-stages of "prior" preparation, the chapter turns to the root-stage itself — the exact instant at which each of the four grave offenses actually consummates — and then, in a section distinctive to this compendium, to what happens in the mind immediately afterward.

Section Two: The Root-Stage. The Procedural Commentary says: "What is the mark of the root-stage itself? Penetration to the depth of a single hair's breadth is called consummated unchastity; lifting the object clear of its original place is called consummated theft; severing the life-faculty is called consummated killing; and speech fully understood by the hearer is called consummated false speech. At the moment the offense is settled, our school gathers up the prior cause together into the single resulting fruit; it is not like other schools, in which, once the cause has already become the fruit, there remains some further, separate preparatory stage belonging to the original moment." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first part clarifies the mark of the root-stage under two heads: the first sentence poses the question generally; what follows answers it point by point. What follows that clarifies how the prior cause is gathered up to form the fruit, distinguishing this from other schools so that no confusion arises." [Zichi Commentary, fascicle 27]

Ru-Yi Translation

Section Three: Subsequent Preparation. The Procedural Commentary says: "What is meant by 'subsequent preparatory stage'? It means that once the deed accomplished has fully satisfied one's intent, and one's earlier mind gives rise to gladness over it without any thought of repentance — this itself constitutes a further offense, and, uniformly, only a lower-grade offense results." The Zichi Commentary explains: "Regardless of whether the original offense was heavy or light, this subsequent stage is uniformly assigned only a lower-grade offense — hence the word 'uniformly.' This is the reverse of the prior preparatory stage, where two or three grades of severity differ, since there middle-grade and lower-grade offenses are distinguished." [Zichi Commentary, fascicle 27]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide singles out "subsequent preparation" as a genuinely distinctive move: most legal thinking about an offense stops at the moment of consummation, but Nanshan's system reaches forward into the psychological aftermath as well. Savoring a completed wrong without any impulse toward regret is treated as its own small but real offense — a lower-grade offense regardless of how grave the original act was — precisely because it signals that the karmic momentum behind the deed has not actually stopped. The guide also draws out the definitional core of the root-stage itself: penetration "to the depth of a single hair" for unchastity, lifting an object clear of its place for theft, severing the life-faculty for killing, and a statement actually understood by the hearer for false speech — deliberately concrete, physical thresholds chosen so that a practitioner always has a clear, checkable answer to "has this already become the full offense, or not yet."

3. Milestone: Three Lesser-Known Deficiencies — General Deficiency, an Overpowering Object, and Circumstantial Divergence

The gate now turns to its sixth chapter, "Deficient Conditions and What Fails to Complete an Offense" (闕緣不成) — the core mechanics of why a violation can stall at the preparatory stage and never reach the root-offense. Dao Xuan identifies seven distinct ways a violation can fall short; this section covers the first three, which are comparatively brief.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Commentary continues: "Third, 'both general and specific at once' — what does this mean? Whoever commits any violation must, at that moment, still possess the essential status of an upāsaka. Suppose the offense has been undertaken but not yet completed as a fruit: perhaps the person's own life comes to an end, or the precepts are relinquished, or a wrong view arises, or the person's sex physically changes, and so on; or perhaps illness, madness, or derangement intervenes. Whenever either kind of condition is present, the act cannot be called a completed violation — both nonetheless still count as contributing to the cause, since it has not yet reached the fruit; hence this is called 'deficiency in the condition of being an upāsaka.' Item Two: An Object Too Strong to Overcome. The Commentary on the Precept-Text says: "Second is 'an object too strong to overcome': as when, wishing to carry out a killing, the intended object instead proves too strong, and turns to attack and harm the assailant instead. The object's being too strong is not itself the middle-grade offense; rather, the middle-grade offense arises on account of that strength. Item Three: Circumstantial Divergence. The Commentary on the Precept-Text says: "Third is the preparatory-stage offense of circumstantial divergence: as when, intending to kill or to steal, one goes to the place and encounters some other, unexpected person; or fears that the matter will be discovered; or one's knife or weapon is damaged or lost; or the appointed occasion simply fails to come about. All of these together are called 'circumstantial divergence.' In its general sense, this name could apply to all seven conditions alike; but taken according to its own specific mark, it belongs properly only to this third category." The Xingzong Commentary explains: "The first part lists four marks: encountering someone other than expected; fearing discovery; the killing implement being deficient; and failing to actually see the intended object. As a name it applies broadly to all seven conditions, but its specific mark is confined to these four situations." [Xingzong Commentary, fascicle 4]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide frames this whole chapter around a single, load-bearing idea: the complete "conditions constituting a violation" for any precept — five conditions for killing, for instance — function like a checklist, and if any single item on that checklist is missing, the act cannot reach the root-offense and instead remains stalled at the preparatory stage. The seven items catalogued here are simply the seven distinct ways such a checklist item can go missing. "Deficient conditions" in the narrow sense covers cases where the perpetrator's own basic eligibility to violate the precept at all — being, at the moment of the act, a person who actually holds the precept — drops out partway through, whether through death, apostasy, a change of sex, or sudden madness. "An object too strong" and "circumstantial divergence" cover the more mundane ways an attempt can simply fail in execution — a would-be victim who turns out to be able to fight back, or a plan derailed by some unexpected turn of events. None of these, the guide stresses, make the original intent any less real or any less blameworthy in itself; they simply mean that, as a formal matter, the full offense was never actually completed.

4. Milestone: Object Divergence I — Killing "Zhang" but Striking "Wang"

The fourth of the seven deficient conditions, object divergence (境差), is by far the largest topic in this chapter — Dao Xuan devotes to it a running case study that will occupy several sections of this page. The example is deliberately concrete: a killer fixes targeted intent on one person, "Zhang," but a different person, "Wang," comes to take his place at the critical moment.

Item Four: Object Divergence. The Commentary on the Precept-Text says: "Fourth is object divergence, which occurs, in principle, with every precept. The Commentary on the Precept-Text says: "For instance, wishing to kill a person, one fixes focused intent on 'Zhang'; but 'Wang,' a different human object, comes to take Zhang's place. But because a divergent object intervened, Zhang the intended person does not die, and the intention to kill him has itself now ceased — this remains lodged at the preparatory stage; hence it is called 'object divergence.'" The Xingzong Commentary explains: "The two conclusions: with respect to the divergent object [Wang] — a completed grave offense; with respect to the original object [Zhang] — a middle-grade offense, termed the preparatory-stage offense of deficient conditions." [Xingzong Commentary, fascicle 4]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide calls this the chapter's single largest set-piece: the Zhang-and-Wang case. The core intuition it is built to test is simple — does killing the wrong person "count" as murdering the person you meant to kill? Nanshan's answer splits the question in two. Toward Wang, the actual victim, the offense is fully consummated and graded as the gravest offense, because "human being" is the operative category the precept actually protects, and Wang unquestionably belongs to it. But toward Zhang, the originally intended victim, something real still happened — a fully formed intent to kill, carried into action — even though Zhang never came to any harm at all. Rather than simply letting that intent evaporate as though nothing occurred, Nanshan insists it must be accounted for as its own, separate middle-grade preparatory offense: "object divergence." The guide underscores why this matters pastorally as much as legally — it refuses to let a would-be killer off the hook merely because circumstance intervened to save the intended victim.

5. Milestone: Object Divergence II — Two Hard Questions About Intent and Cause

Two objections immediately press on the Zhang-and-Wang ruling. First: if there was never any intent at all toward Wang specifically, how can killing Wang possibly count as the graver offense? Second, and more technical: if the cause originally aimed at Zhang is simply absorbed into the fruit actually consummated against Wang, does "object divergence" even remain a coherent, separately existing category at all?

Ru-Yi Translation

Group Two: Question and Answer. This divides into two threads: (1) does divergence toward the object mean no intent at all?, (2) a difficulty about the relation between cause and effect.

Thread One. The Commentary on the Precept-Text says: "Question: at the moment one actually kills Wang, one is only construing him as Zhang, with no intent at all toward Wang — how can this consummate as the graver offense? Answer: 'Zhang' and 'Wang' as such are not what the offense actually turns on; it is 'human being' that is the object of the killing. Although there is no intent specifically toward Wang, there is nonetheless the perception 'a human being' — and since the conditions constituting the offense of killing are thereby all present, how could this fail to be the graver offense? This is exactly why the Vinaya states: perceiving a male, one kills a female — the Buddha declared this too an upper-grade offense. The present case may be understood on the same pattern." The Xingzong Commentary explains: "'Zhang' and 'Wang' are particular individuals, but 'human being' is the shared category; here the offense is settled according to the shared category, and so it consummates as the graver offense." [Xingzong Commentary, fascicle 4]

Following the Commentary's text above, it would appear that, for the precept against killing, focused intent is never exempted where a divergent human object is involved. But according to the later chapter distinguishing nature-offenses in detail, in the section on misapprehension-in-the-moment and mistaken-belief, misapprehension-in-the-moment is exempted while mistaken-belief still constitutes a violation.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats this back-and-forth as Nanshan actively working out — and correcting — an older generation's answer, not just reporting settled doctrine. The older interpretation tried to solve the "no intent toward Wang" problem by simply merging Zhang's cause straight into Wang's fruit, the way a theft aimed at "more than five coins" can be credited toward an actual haul of exactly five, since both cases involve harming the very same owner throughout. The guide reads this as the chapter's real payoff: it is precisely because Nanshan insists on this separation that "object divergence" survives as its own coherent category at all, rather than dissolving the instant the actual victim is struck.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats this eighteen-formula grid mainly as evidence of how systematically the vinaya tradition worked out the logic already established for the Zhang-and-Wang case, rather than introducing any new principle. The pattern holds steady across every row: whatever actually happens to the substitute object — whether it lives, dies, or is never even the right kind of being to generate an offense on its own — the original object always keeps its own middle-grade "object divergence" offense, undisturbed. What changes, row by row, is only what happens on the substitute's own side: a human substitute who is actually killed completes the gravest offense in full; a non-human or animal substitute who dies generates no offense at all when perception was settled (since there was never any intent toward "a non-human" or "an animal" as such), but does generate a small, separate lower-grade offense of its own once doubt was present; and an inanimate stump, lacking any capacity to be a proper object of killing whatsoever, never generates any offense no matter what.

What differs, case by case, is only the substitute's own side of the ledger. Where the substitute is human, matters are already settled from the earlier Zhang-and-Wang case. Where it is a non-human or animal, Nanshan rules that settled perception generates no separate offense at all with respect to the substitute — since the killer never actually held any intent toward "a non-human" as such — while doubt does generate a small, independent lower-grade offense, precisely because doubt, unlike settled perception, never fully lets go of either object. Where the substitute is a stump, there is simply nothing there capable of being wronged, and no offense results under any circumstance.

For theft under focused intent, the guide highlights a genuinely practical result: if one meant to steal specifically from a human being, and the item taken turns out to belong to a non-human or animal instead, neither error counts as a violation — but only up to the point of actually recognizing the mistake. The same logic, even more strikingly, exempts taking the wrong item from the very same owner — gold mistaken for silver — provided it too is simply put back once the error is noticed.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats the killing-versus-theft contrast here as the single most important payoff of this whole section, and builds one of its discussion questions directly around it: under focused intent, mistaken-belief toward theft is exempted entirely, while the exact same structure of error — Zhang for Wang — still incurs the graver offense under killing. Why the difference? The guide's answer is that the underlying logic tracks what can and cannot actually be undone. The guide reads this asymmetry as expressing something the whole compendium takes for granted but rarely states outright: the four grave precepts are not simply four equally severe items on a list, but are themselves internally ranked by how irreversible the harm they prohibit actually is.

The guide treats this section as the pastoral center of gravity for the entire gate, and it is worth quoting the *Sarvāstivāda-vinaya*'s own formula in full, since the guide does: "it is acceptable to give rise to every precept at a single moment, but it is never acceptable to violate every precept at a single moment." Reception, the guide explains, is anticipatory and can leap forward all at once — a woman's body alone carries twenty-one distinct instances of the precept against unchastity, obtained together in a single moment of ordination — but violation can only ever touch the one specific instance actually confronted. Break faith with one woman, through one act, and only that one twenty-first of the precept is defiled; the other twenty stand exactly as bright and untouched as the moment they were received, and every other precept entirely is likewise untouched.

The guide singles out Dao Xuan's own real-world observation as the section's emotional core: people who, having genuinely violated once, talk themselves into believing the precepts are now simply "lost" altogether, and thereupon abandon all further restraint — "is this not simply falling, through sheer ignorance of the precept's true structure, into the worst of worldly habit?" The guide reads this as a direct, explicit refutation of the all-or-nothing thinking that turns one lapse into a permanent excuse for many more, and closes by noting how the chapter itself ends: not with triumphant certainty, but with the sober admission that even a genuinely disciplined practitioner, once actually confronted with temptation, may simply be unable to avoid violation — and that what matters most, at that point, is neither self-congratulation nor self-abandonment, but confessing without concealment and starting again.

19. Milestone: Refuting the Contempt for the Vinaya as "Merely a Hīnayāna Teaching"

With the technical machinery of observance and violation now fully laid out, the gate's final chapter, "Broadly Rebuking a Foolish Teaching" (廣斥愚教), turns polemical. Dao Xuan names and directly answers a real current of opinion in his own day — self-styled Mahāyāna practitioners who dismissed the entire Vinaya as a lesser, merely Hīnayāna concern, no longer binding on anyone pursuing the greater path.

Chapter Nine: Broadly Rebuking a Foolish Teaching. The Procedural Commentary says: "In these times, those who do not understand the teaching are, in large part, guilty of self-inflicted harm. This shows the Tathāgata retrospectively explaining his own earlier intent in teaching the lesser vehicle provisionally. By the time of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra itself, the provisional teaching's remaining doubts are all fully resolved, and everything alike returns to the single, ever-abiding reality — how, then, could there remain any genuinely separate 'lesser vehicle' at all? Studying the Vinaya carefully is, on this logic, no obstacle to the Great Vehicle at all.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads the three-trainings argument as Dao Xuan's structural, rather than merely rhetorical, answer to his opponents: precepts are placed first among precepts, concentration, and wisdom not as some lesser, preliminary formality to be outgrown, but because the three poisons are, at root, driven by the mind, and it is precisely the training in precepts that first "seizes hold" of the karmic acts of body and speech through which those poisons would otherwise run riot — concentration then "binds fast" what remains, and only then can wisdom actually "cut through" to the root. The guide singles out the two proof-texts Dao Xuan marshals as deliberately chosen to outflank his opponents on their own ground: the Śrīmālā Sūtra and the Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra are unimpeachably Mahāyāna texts, yet both state plainly that the Vinaya itself is a Great Vehicle study.

The guide reads the parable of the two quarreling disciples as the chapter's emotional climax: mutual contempt between the two vehicles does not raise either one at the other's expense — it breaks both legs, and the whole teaching is left, in Dao Xuan's own words, "destroyed together." The guide's closing note is that Dao Xuan does not end on a note of confidence that his own argument has settled the matter — his final image, "plain white silk is easily dyed, and once dyed, red and purple are difficult to tell apart again," is offered as a sober acknowledgment that clear argument alone rarely undoes an already-formed prejudice, even as he still commits the effort to making it as fully and carefully as he can. This closing chapter brings the fifth gate to its end, and the compendium now moves on to its sixth gate, the specific particulars of the four gravest offenses themselves.
