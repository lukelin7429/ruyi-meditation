The Law That Comes Before the Vow

Before a single vow can be taken, something has to already exist for the vow to lay hold of. This opening gate asks what that something is — not yet how it is kept, only what it fundamentally is — and only then turns to the living liturgies through which it is actually received: the Three Refuges, the Five Precepts, and the Eight Precepts of the Poṣadha Fast.

1. Milestone: Before the Gate — Why Exactly Four Categories

The whole of the Doctrinal Fascicle — all four of its gates — rests on a single four-part scheme that Master Hongyi lays out before Gate One even begins: the precept-Dharma, the precept-essence, precept-conduct, and precept-marks. This gate is the first of the four. Before showing what the precept-Dharma specifically is, the source text first explains why the larger scheme needs exactly these four terms, no more and no fewer, and how they relate to one another.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Doctrinal Fascicle
The Doctrinal Fascicle divides into four gates: first, the precept-Dharma; second, the precept-essence; third, precept-conduct; fourth, precept-marks.

The Procedural Commentary (Xingshi Chao) says: "I here briefly point out the doctrine, essence, conduct, and marks, so that those coming after may have something to lean on in setting out and building up their practice." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "'Setting out' means generating the mind [of aspiration]; 'building up' means establishing practice. Once one recognizes the essence and advances in conduct, cause is accomplished and fruit obtained — hence it is said there is 'something to lean on.'"

The Procedural Commentary continues: "But the marks of the precepts run in many directions, not along a single track. The mind, too, has its own limits, and takes them up in differing measure. Were one to name every mark according to its object, the number would be limitless. Let me instead grasp the pivot and briefly set out four categories: first, the precept-Dharma; second, the precept-essence; third, precept-conduct; fourth, precept-marks." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "This passage explains the breadth of the subject. The first two lines show its breadth in terms of the Dharma itself — the Five Precepts, Eight Precepts, Ten Precepts, and full ordination make four distinct grades. The next two lines show its breadth in terms of the mind — each of the above four grades itself divides into three levels. The lines after that show its breadth in terms of objects — sentient and insentient objects alike being beyond counting. The final lines show what is essential: 'pivot' means the pivot of a door, taken here also in the sense of what is essential."

[The "three levels" mentioned here are set out later, under the Five Precepts in the second chapter, Ordination Liturgies, in the section on generating the aspiration in accordance with the object-field.]

The Zichi Commentary says: "To grasp the four categories, one must first have a brief outline. What the sage institutes as regulation is called the Dharma; what is received and becomes karmic action is called the essence; what arises relying on that essence, as guarded conduct, is called conduct; and what gives conduct its outward form is called marks. Some say that before ordination it is called 'the Dharma,' and only after ordination is it called 'the essence.' This is not so, I hold: the Dharma, in its meaning, runs through from beginning to end — how could there be a case where, once ordination has occurred, it is no longer proper to call it 'the Dharma'? One should know that the latter three all take their name from the very first, which is why each of the four may properly be called 'precept' — or, put another way, all four may be strung together under the single word 'Dharma,' so as to show that essence, conduct, and marks are not some separate, merely ordinary kind of good."

[Regarding "what gives conduct its outward form is called marks": precept-marks carry two senses. One is marks taken in terms of conduct, as stated here. The other is marks taken in terms of the Dharma itself, as shown later in the Gate of Keeping and Violating.]

The Zichi Commentary says: "Question: why exactly four, no more and no fewer? Answer: because these four take in the whole of practice from beginning to end, without deficiency or excess. Whenever any single act of practice is accomplished, all four are already fully present within it — there is an order only in the speaking of them; in actual practice they do not come one before another."

The Zichi Commentary says: "Question: how are the Dharma and the essence the same, and how do they differ? Answer: the Commentary on Karman Procedures (Yeshu) says, 'The essence is the very basis the precept-Dharma depends upon.' This makes the Dharma the dependent and the essence the depended-upon, so the two cannot be called the same. It also says, 'The precept-essence is what is meant by receiving the sagely Dharma into the very core of one's heart' — which makes the Dharma exactly what is received as the precept-essence. On this basis the two cannot be called different, either. One should understand: to speak of 'the Dharma' does not necessarily mean 'the essence,' but to speak of 'the essence' necessarily means 'the Dharma.' Neither identical nor separate, neither the same nor different." The Jiyuan Commentary says: "Question: if the Dharma just is the essence, how can Dharma and essence be distinguished at all? Answer: looked at before ordination, it is merely called 'the Dharma,' and as such is without sentience. Once the aspirational vow is added, and the conditions come together to be received, karmic action is accomplished depending on the mind — it is only once this Dharma has this efficacy that it is called 'the essence.' It is like the analogy of pills of medicine: the individual ingredients, taken separately, are like the precept-Dharma; combined together into a pill, they are like the precept-essence. The pill is nothing other than the ingredients themselves, now combined — different, and yet the same; the same, and yet distinct. One should understand it in just this way." [Cited from the Jiyuan Commentary on the Yeshu, fascicle 16.]

The Zichi Commentary says: "Question: how do conduct and marks differ? Answer: they are distinguished by the three types of karma." [The above is all found in the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 3.]

["Distinguished by the three types of karma" means precept-conduct belongs to the mental karma, while precept-marks belong to bodily and verbal karma. That conduct belongs to the mind is said from the standpoint of the faculty that observes — see the later passages in the Gate of the Precept-Essence, on the Consummate-Teaching school's "being able to remember, able to uphold, able to guard," and the opening passage of the Gate of Precept-Conduct. That marks belong to body and speech is seen in the later passage on the four kinds of conduct explained in terms of karma, in the Chapter on Consummation within the Gate of Keeping and Violating. If this is so, why does the Zichi Commentary elsewhere say "the three types of karma, when carried out in cultivation, are called conduct"? Answer: that statement there also takes into account the faculty that observes.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide places this whole gate on a map before it opens: the Doctrinal Fascicle's four gates move from precept-Dharma (what the rule itself is) to precept-essence (the karmic imprint left in the mind when that rule is formally received) to precept-conduct (living out of that imprint) to precept-marks (the concrete, checkable shape that conduct takes moment to moment). None of the four is optional and none is redundant — Dao Xuan's commentators insist the scheme needs exactly these four because between them they cover practice completely, from the sage's original legislation through to the single instant of upholding or breaking a precept, with nothing left over and nothing missing.

The guide also flags a subtlety worth carrying forward: "Dharma" and "essence" are neither simply identical nor simply separate. The essence depends on the Dharma the way a pill depends on its ingredients — genuinely different, since loose ingredients are not yet a pill, and yet genuinely the same substance throughout. This pill-and-ingredients image, borrowed from the Jiyuan Commentary, is worth holding onto: the rest of this gate is devoted entirely to the "ingredients" — what the precept-Dharma is before anyone has taken a vow — while the next gate, on the precept-essence, will take up what happens the moment those ingredients are actually combined.

2. Milestone: The Precept-Dharma, Defined — Constant Across Ordinary Beings and Sages

Chapter One, A General Account of the Precept-Dharma, opens with the plainest possible question: what exactly is being received when someone "takes precepts"? Dao Xuan's answer refuses to make the precept-Dharma a private possession of the already-enlightened — it is one and the same law whether the person receiving it is an ordinary being or a sage. Yuan Zhao then explains how an ordinary recipient can still be said to receive something properly called "sage-Dharma."

Chapter One: A General Account of the Precept-Dharma
This chapter divides into three sections: first, Showing Its Marks and Making Its Name Clear; second, A Brief Account of the Teaching-Substance; third, Making Plain the Path by Which It Is Known.

Section One: Showing Its Marks and Making Its Name Clear
This section divides into two sub-sections: first, A Direct Showing; second, Sundry Distinctions.

Sub-section One: A Direct Showing

The Procedural Commentary (Xingshi Chao) says: "As for what is called the precept-Dharma — speaking of it purely as Dharma, it is not confined to the ordinary or the sagely. It directly shows that this Dharma alone is able to serve as the very rule by which the path of liberation is accomplished. It is essential that the recipient believe and know that this exists." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "Within the passage on showing its marks, the opening sentence gives the heading, and what follows the word 'directly' gives the direct explanation. Although the Dharma pertains to both [ordinary and sage] alike, the text cannot spell this out in full, so it speaks of it only from the sage's side — hence the word 'directly.' 'Serve as the rule by which it is accomplished' shows the meaning of the Dharma; 'the path of liberation' is what the sage has realized. The clause beginning 'it is essential' gives the reason this is spoken of from the sage's side. Yet this passage only shows the power the Dharma has — the text does not point out explicitly what, exactly, this Dharma is; the intent is to have the student think it through and arrive at it for himself."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Although it is a Dharma that ordinary beings and sages alike possess in common, what is received here — spoken of in terms of what it will one day become — is called 'sage-Dharma.'" The Zichi Commentary glosses: "Within the passage on making its name clear, the first two lines pick up the thread from before; what follows the word 'here' gives the direct showing. 'What it will one day become' means the three trainings cultivated from the first fruit [of stream-entry] onward, which are called the sage's path. Although the recipient is, here and now, still an ordinary being, this is nonetheless called 'sage-Dharma' — a case of borrowing the name of the future fruit while still in the causal stage." [The above is all found in the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 3.]

The Commentary on Karman Procedures (Yeshu) says: "Question: everyone knows about 'receiving' precepts, but what exactly is it that is received? Answer, according to the interpretation handed down: what is received is called 'sage-Dharma.' Because of this Dharma, one honors and guards it, keeping it pure as a bright pearl, since it is able to serve as the very foundation on which the sage's path is built." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "Although those who receive precepts are many, they do not, of themselves, know the essence of what they have received. Wishing to alert students to this, [the Commentary] therefore raises this question. In the answer, 'handed down' means received from the interpretation of the ancients: it names the cause by the fruit, since this Dharma is able to lead through to the sage's path, and also so as to keep the recipient from holding himself too lightly." [Cited from the Jiyuan Commentary on the Yeshu, fascicle 15.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide singles out Dao Xuan's phrase "not confined to the ordinary or the sagely" as the entire section's real claim: the precept-Dharma itself does not change depending on who receives it. What changes is only whether the recipient has yet realized it. Yuan Zhao then closes the obvious gap this leaves open — if the Dharma is constant, how can an admittedly still-ordinary recipient be said to receive something called "sage-Dharma"? — with the phrase the guide treats as the single most important formula in this whole opening section: 因中果號, "naming the cause by the fruit." What is received at ordination is the cause that reliably leads to the sage's fruit, so tradition borrows the name of that future fruit and applies it, in advance, to the cause. Receiving precepts does not make someone a sage on the spot; it puts them, for the first time, formally onto the road that leads there.

The guide adds a small but pointed observation about the passage's own restraint: the text says a great deal about what the precept-Dharma does — it functions as the rule by which liberation is accomplished — without ever directly stating what, in itself, it actually is. That omission, Yuan Zhao suggests, is deliberate: the reader is meant to work it out through reflection rather than be handed a definition outright, which is exactly the work the rest of this chapter goes on to do.

3. Milestone: Sorting the Teachings — Transformation Versus Regulation, and Where the Five and Eight Precepts Actually Sit

Sub-section Two, Sundry Distinctions, works through three successive tests for pinning down exactly what kind of thing the precept-Dharma is. The first test sorts the Buddha's teaching as a whole into two broad streams — the teachings of transformation, open to monastics and laity alike, and the regulatory teaching, technically legislation for monastics only — and then places the Five Precepts and Eight Precepts in a genuinely ambiguous spot between the two.

Ru-Yi Translation

Sub-section Two: Sundry Distinctions
This sub-section divides into three parts: first, Transformation and Regulation; second, Wholesome Action and Precept; third, Nature-Offense and Proscribed-Offense.

Part One: Transformation and Regulation

"Transformation and regulation" refers to the teachings of transformation and the regulatory teaching. The regulatory teaching is also called "the practice-teaching." Although the Commentary on the Precept-Text (Jieshu), in its section on the boundaries comprehended by the teachings, lists "the practice-teaching" and "the regulatory teaching" as separate items, elsewhere in the Procedural Commentary, the Jieshu, and their sub-commentaries the two terms are used interchangeably; the meaning can evidently be reconciled. Likewise, although the Jieshu confines itself to speaking of "the three wheels," elsewhere the Procedural Commentary, the Jieshu, and their sub-commentaries all divide the teaching in two, using either "transformation and practice" or "transformation and regulation." The Zichi Commentary notes: in the Jieshu the division is sometimes by "the three wheels," sometimes by "transformation and practice," sometimes by "transformation and regulation," and sometimes by "regulation and permission" — the Jieshu itself adopts "the three wheels," while the present text uses "transformation and practice"; this is simply usage suited to the occasion, and there is no need to force the terms into agreement.

The Commentary on Karman Procedures (Yeshu) says: "From ancient times, on close examination the teaching has always divided into two paths. The teachings of transformation extend to monastics and laity alike, and are devoted entirely to opening the door of faith and understanding. The regulatory teaching is confined strictly to monastics, and does nothing but clarify the business of upholding and observing conduct." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "This passage sets the two teachings side by side to bring out their two differences. 'Monastic and lay' against 'monastics alone' marks a difference in whom each reaches; 'faith and understanding' against 'upholding and observing' marks a difference in how each law is established. The Jieshu says: what is meant by 'the teachings of transformation'? They expound and instruct so as to let beings tell wrong from right; the teaching's basic purpose is to transform people by opening their understanding, and it is not, in its origin, established in response to any particular offense. As for 'the regulatory teaching': it necessarily arises in response to an offense and is legislated according to that offense; it speaks only of keeping and violating, and its business covers both what is to be stopped and what is to be done — the single Vinaya tradition of precepts and discipline is confined to this teaching alone."

The Jiyuan Commentary says: "The Ten Wholesome Actions, the Five Methods for Stilling the Mind, the Four Universal Vows, the six pāramitās, and every practice of contemplation belong to the karmic domain of the teachings of transformation. Everything the Vinaya expounds — permission and prohibition, light and grave — belongs to the karmic domain of the regulatory teaching. Transformation is grounded in principle, and principle admits of being followed or violated; regulation is set in terms of legislated conduct, and legislated conduct admits of being kept or broken." [The above is all found in the Jiyuan Commentary on the Yeshu, fascicle 1.]

The Zichi Commentary says: "The Buddha's teaching, across his whole lifetime, reduces in the end to transformation and practice. What opens up faith and understanding, leaving whether it is taken up or set aside to the recipient's own conditions, is called the teachings of transformation. What legislates observance, such that violating it constitutes an offense, is called the practice-teaching."

[Passages distinguishing the teachings of transformation from the regulatory teaching recur throughout the Procedural Commentary, the Jieshu, and their sub-commentaries, all largely to the same effect; not all can be reproduced here. Based on the passages above, a table is given below to show the two apart.]

Teachings of transformation — reach: monastics and laity alike; aim: open faith and understanding; basis: meant to open wisdom, not established in response to any offense; examples: the Ten Wholesome Actions, the Five Methods for Stilling the Mind, and the like; character: taking up or setting aside left to one's own conditions.
The regulatory teaching (practice-teaching) — reach: monastics only; aim: legislates observance; basis: keeping and violating are fixed and regulated according to the offense; examples: permission and prohibition, light and grave, and the like; character: violation constitutes an offense.

The Zichi Commentary says: "Question: since the Five Precepts and Eight Precepts are themselves precept-legislation, should they not be classed under the practice-teaching? Answer: they are classed under the teachings of transformation." [The above is all found in the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 2.]

[In the Nanshan Vinaya, the Ten Precepts of the novice and full ordination are classed under the regulatory teaching, while the Five Precepts and Eight Precepts are classed under the teachings of transformation. On examination, however, the Five Precepts and Eight Precepts differ from the teachings of transformation in the ordinary sense: name-wise they are classed with the teachings of transformation, but in substance they amount to the regulatory teaching. Although their sense reaches into regulation, the teaching itself remains, in the end, confined to transformation — exactly as the Four-Part Vinaya's own tradition is, name-wise, classed as Hīnayāna, but in substance amounts to Mahāyāna: although its sense reaches into the Great Vehicle, the teaching itself remains, in the end, confined to the lesser. Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats "name-wise classed with the teachings of transformation, but in substance amounting to the regulatory teaching" as the single most important interpretive move in this whole part of the gate. On the surface, the Five and Eight Precepts look like they belong with the teachings of transformation — open to monastics and laity, framed as guidance rather than legislation. But look at how they actually function: they carry a genuine keeping-or-violating structure, screen candidates for disqualifying impediments before ordination, and generate an actual precept-essence at the moment of reception, exactly the way monastic legislation does. Nanshan's solution is to let a single item carry two different labels at two different levels — "name" and "substance" — without forcing a single flat classification onto it.

The guide draws out the parallel Master Hongyi's own source text supplies: the Four-Part Vinaya is, by name, a Hīnayāna text, yet the doctrine it actually contains reaches — "partially opens onto" — the Mahayana. In both cases, a text or precept can carry one classification by name and a different, deeper one by substance, without any contradiction, so long as the two levels are kept clearly apart. The guide notes that readers will meet this same "name versus substance" move again and again through the rest of the Doctrinal Fascicle, so it is worth fixing firmly in mind here, at its first appearance.

Part Two: Wholesome Action and Precept

The Commentary on Karman Procedures (Yeshu) says: "Question: is every wholesome act a precept? The Procedural Commentary (Xingshi Chao) says: "One should draw a fourfold analysis. Second, precept but not wholesome — namely, the wrongful vocational precepts. Third, both wholesome and precept — namely, the first seven of the ten wholesome courses of action: carried out without any prior vow, they are simply called 'wholesome'; taken up instead with a vow and sustained effort, they are called 'precept.' [Cited from the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 16.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads this fourfold analysis as pinning down exactly two conditions that separate a bare wholesome habit from a genuine precept: an advance vow undertaken with a definite term (本期誓), and a scope that reaches every sentient being without exception (遍該生境). Absent either condition, an act stays merely "wholesome" — good in itself, but not the structured thing tradition calls "precept." This is why the same seven bodily-and-verbal wholesome actions can appear in two of the four categories at once: done casually, without a vow, they are simply good; taken up under a formal vow, the very same actions become precept.

The guide dwells longest on the second category — "precept but not wholesome," meaning the wrongful vocational precepts of the professional butcher or hunter. The point is structural, not moral: such a person has made a standing commitment to a fixed course of harmful conduct, sustained across time and reaffirmed in practice, which gives the disposition the same shape as a precept — vow, continuity, a settled scope of application — with only its moral polarity reversed. Recognizing this mirror-image case, the guide suggests, actually sharpens what "precept" itself means: it is defined by having this settled, vow-like structure at all, independent of whether the content committed to is wholesome or unwholesome.

5. Milestone: Nature-Offense and Proscribed-Offense — Why Killing Is Always Wrong but Drinking Only Became So

The third and final test in Sundry Distinctions separates precepts into two kinds by asking a single question: would this act be wrong even if the Buddha had never prohibited it? Killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and lying pass this test — they are nature-offense precepts. The precept against alcohol fails it — it is a proscribed-offense precept, wrong only because, and only after, it was legislated.

The Commentary on the Precept-Text (Jieshu) says: "In clarifying proscribed-offense and nature-offense: because the conditions that give rise to evil cannot simply be enumerated one by one, they are gathered, by their underlying meaning, into two broad categories."

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reduces the whole distinction to a single diagnostic question, worth keeping in mind through the rest of the book: would this act still be wrong if the Buddha had never said a word about it? Killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and lying pass the test — they carry all three faults the Jieshu lists (violating reason itself, violating the Buddha's prohibition, and obstructing the path), and were already generating bitter karmic fruit long before any precept singled them out. Drinking alcohol fails the test: before it was prohibited, drinking generated no offense and did no harm to one's stock of merit. It became a fault only because, and only after, the Buddha legislated against it — not because the act was inherently corrupt in the way killing is.

The guide also draws out why the Kośa passage on drunkenness belongs here, rather than looking like a digression: it shows exactly how a merely proscribed act becomes dangerous in practice — not through any evil in the substance of alcohol itself, but through the cascade of judgment it destroys, so that a single lapse in one precept becomes the gateway to breaking several others at once. This is also why the guide flags the alcohol precept as the sole proscribed-offense item among the Five Precepts: every other item on that list — and the first four items of the Eight Precepts as well — belongs to the nature-offense category the Jieshu opens with.

7. Milestone: Why Precepts Are Instituted at All — For the Path, Not for Worldly Merit

Section Three, Making Plain the Path by Which It Is Known, opens by asking the most basic practical question of all: why did the Buddha legislate precepts in the first place? The answer refuses a common misreading — precepts were never instituted to accumulate worldly merit, but strictly to serve the path to liberation, and the "three trainings" of precept, concentration, and wisdom are shown to depend on one another in a fixed, necessary order, using an image — a thief seized, bound, then executed — that Master Hongyi's readers would have found immediately memorable.

Section Three: Making Plain the Path by Which It Is Known
This section divides into five parts: first, The Very Root of the Sage's Path; second, The Great Function of Precept; third, A Brief Explanation of Its Names; fourth, Its Superiority Over Other Kinds; fifth, Whether One May Re-Take What Has Already Been Received.

Part One: The Very Root of the Sage's Path

The Commentary on the Precept-Text (Jieshu) says: "This is exactly why the great sage descended into the world, first opening the very root of transformation. Wishing to rescue every mode of existence and bring them together to the far shore, he legislated precepts for the sake of the path — not, in their origin, for worldly merit." The Xingzong Commentary glosses: "'For the sake of the path,' spoken broadly, refers to the three vehicles. Tracing the Buddha's original intent — reaching all the way down to the Refuges that Turn One from Wrong Views — it is, from beginning to end, a leading-in toward the one real vehicle. Moreover, the sutras' own reconciling of the vehicles shows that different paths return to the same place, and the repeated support Nirvāṇa lends to this teaching is nothing other than the revealing of buddha-nature. What is explained here as 'for the sake of the path' points specifically to the Buddha-vehicle; resting content in a mere conjured way-station was never the original intent. Hence one knows that the very root of transformation is not even the two lesser vehicles — how much less could it be a matter of precept kept merely for worldly merit."

[The "Refuges that Turn One from Wrong Views" here means the Three Refuges — simply receiving the threefold refuge alone.]

The Jieshu continues: "Yet affliction and delusion are hard to clear away, and require skillful means; hence the three trainings were established as the means for curing their root. This is why the Treatise on Establishing Reality says: precept is like seizing the thief, concentration like binding him, wisdom like putting him to death — the three acts of practice depend on one another, and not one of them can be spared even for a moment. This is why the beginning practitioner must first of all learn the precepts, examining and restraining every wrongdoing; once the three types of karma are purified, right concentration and right wisdom establish themselves naturally." The Xingzong Commentary glosses: "Wishing to explain why the three trainings were set up, I give here a brief account, so the text will not seem obstructed. All sentient beings, from the very beginning, are fully endowed with a true, subtle, wondrous nature, and this nature, in its very substance, is nothing but stillness and illumination. The moment this nature is obscured, it becomes clouded and scattered — reversed and renamed 'ignorance' — accumulating into affliction and delusion, conceiving a self and another, and, following whatever object it meets, stirring up the poison and beating the drum of body, speech, and mind, thereby creating the karma of birth-and-death. That precepts are legislated first is exactly for this reason — it is like turbid water: while the wind still stirs its waves, there is no possibility at all of its becoming clear and settled. The sequence of the three trainings is a matter of necessity in the nature of things; to depart from this ordinary pattern is to move entirely away from the path. 'Affliction' means clouded turbidity; 'delusion' means disordered agitation. The three poisons and their fetters plunder one's stock of wholesome wealth, hence the analogy of a thief. 'The three acts of practice depend on one another' means they arise in sequence; 'not one of them can be spared even for a moment' means they cannot be separated from one another." How could one abandon precept and seek the sage's path by some other way? Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide highlights "for the sake of the path, not, in their origin, for worldly merit" as the sentence that reorients everything else this gate says about precept: precepts are not a transaction for a better rebirth, they are technology for liberation, and any merit they happen to produce along the way is a side effect rather than the point. The guide reads the seize-bind-execute image — precept as capturing the thief, concentration as binding him, wisdom as finally putting him to death — as an unusually vivid way of insisting that all three trainings are necessary and none can be skipped or reordered: without first seizing the thief of affliction through precept, there is nothing left to bind through concentration, and nothing bound is left to be finally eliminated through wisdom.

The guide also draws attention to the image of turbid water settling only once the wind that stirs it has stopped: precepts come first in the sequence not because they matter more than concentration or wisdom, but because a mind still agitated by unrestrained conduct has no chance of settling into either. This is also why the guide treats "the very root of transformation is not even the two lesser vehicles" as worth pausing on — precepts are shown here to serve not merely the goal of personal liberation shared with the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha paths, but ultimately the single, real vehicle the whole of the Buddha's teaching is said to lead toward.

Part Three: A Brief Explanation of Its Names

The Procedural Commentary says: "According to the original Sanskrit, three names are set out in full. First, 'Vinaya,' translated as 'law.' Second, 'śīla,' translated as 'precept.' Third, 'prātimokṣa,' rendered here as 'liberation at every point.' This shows the three in their proper order, spanning the whole course of a single transformation from beginning to end. Law is grounded in teaching; teaching does not arise on its own, but must explain the marks of conduct, and precept is thereby established on its basis. Precept is not an empty cause — it must bear a fruit that is won through it — hence liberation, the cutting-off of every bond, comes properly last of all." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "'The whole course of a single transformation from beginning to end' — this threefold sequence of teaching, practice, and fruit is not confined to precept and discipline alone; every gate of the teaching follows this same order."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Next, their meaning is clarified. First, 'law' means 'the Dharma' — that is, the laws governing what is and is not an offense, light and grave, and so forth, all of which the Vinaya expounds; this is what the teaching explains. On the three Sanskrit names, the guide underscores that Dao Xuan is not offering three interchangeable synonyms but a single sequence read off the process of transformation itself: vinaya (rendered "law") names the teaching in its capacity as instruction — what may and may not be done, and how gravely; śīla (rendered "precept") names that teaching once it is actually taken up and practiced, a word whose own root sense runs through good and evil alike, which is precisely why the butcher's wrongful vocational precepts can share the same name as the layperson's Five Precepts; and prātimokṣa (rendered "liberation at every point") names the fruit that practice bears — a liberation won bit by bit, offense by offense, in its near sense, and, in its far sense, the sage's own final liberation from every bond, won specifically by keeping to precept as its root. Teaching, practice, fruit: the three names trace exactly the same arc.

Fourth: the mokṣa precepts reach across all seven assemblies, carrying forward the three vehicles, the Three Jewels, and the three paths — their power to sustain is strong, a power the other two lack, and so they are inferior. Fifth: the mokṣa precepts belong only to disciples of the Buddha; the meditative precepts, by contrast, are also possessed by non-Buddhist practitioners." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The Vibhāṣā gives five points: first, time; second, object; third, mind; fourth, function; fifth, person. In the first, 'rare against constant' means the mokṣa precepts require a Buddha's presence in the world to be legislated at all, while the other two — even where no Buddha has appeared — can still be attained by those who reach concentration or realize the path, and so exist at all times. In the second, on object: the mokṣa precepts forbid both proscribed-offenses and nature-offenses across the board; the other two only restrain nature-evil. Third, on mind: loving-kindness is precisely the great mind, and is therefore the Buddha's own cause. The treatise says the meditative and undefiled precepts are not obtained through a mind of loving-kindness, but through wisdom alone — being wholly self-benefiting, the mind of the two lesser vehicles, their inferiority can readily be seen. Fourth, function has two aspects: first, breadth in taking in beings, since the mokṣa precepts reach all seven assemblies; second, superiority in sustaining, since they carry forward the three vehicles, Three Jewels, and three paths. Within 'sustaining,' all three items are strung together by this one word 'carrying forward': the first is the Dharma thereby carried, the second the very ground on which it rests, the third the fruit thereby accomplished. Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide presents the five points as five different angles on a single conclusion: formal ordination beats spontaneous discipline, every time. Rarity favors the formal precepts, since they exist only in the narrow window a Buddha is actually present in the world, while meditative and undefiled discipline can arise at any time whatsoever, with or without a Buddha's teaching. Scope favors the formal precepts too, since they cover both sentient and insentient objects, while the other two guard only against harm to sentient beings. Motivation favors the formal precepts because they arise specifically from loving-kindness — a mind capable of becoming the cause of full Buddhahood — while meditative and undefiled discipline arise from wisdom alone, a self-benefiting orientation the guide identifies with the two lesser vehicles.

The fourth and fifth points, the guide notes, are really about reach: prātimokṣa precepts extend across all seven assemblies of Buddhist practitioners — monks, nuns, novices, and laypeople of every stripe — and actively sustain the continuity of the three vehicles, the Three Jewels, and the three paths across time, a sustaining function the other two simply lack; and, finally, they belong strictly to the Buddha's own disciples, whereas meditative discipline can arise even in non-Buddhist practitioners who happen to reach deep states of concentration. Taken together, the guide reads this five-point comparison as the doctrinal grounds for something the rest of the gate will take almost for granted: that the Three Refuges, Five Precepts, and Eight Precepts about to be described are worth going out of one's way to formally receive, rather than simply hoping the equivalent discipline arises on its own.

Part Five: Whether One May Re-Take What Has Already Been Received

The Zichi Commentary says: "Within 're-taking': the intent is to have the practitioner examine carefully what he has already received, and seek to further increase and strengthen it."

The Procedural Commentary also says: "The Treatise on Establishing Reality says: someone asks, can the prātimokṣa be generated again? Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide frames the Sarvāstivāda-versus-Tattvasiddhi disagreement in the plainest possible terms: can the aspiration behind a vow already taken actually be upgraded later, or is a person's first reception simply final? The Sarvāstivāda school says no — once received, the precept-essence is fixed for good, and later re-ordination adds nothing to what was already obtained. The Tattvasiddhi school says yes — later reception, taken with a stronger resolve, genuinely increases the earlier essence into a greater one, and the whole is simply named after whichever grade is now superior. Nanshan sides with the more permissive Tattvasiddhi reading here, which is what makes the practice of "increasing the precept" — receiving again, better than the first time — a live option at all.

The guide treats the three grades of aspiration introduced here as the single idea worth carrying forward through the rest of this gate, since it resurfaces, essentially unchanged, in the Five Precepts' own section on generating the aspiration later on: a lower grade that seeks only its own escape from suffering, matching the śrāvaka's and pratyekabuddha's own orientation; a middle grade that seeks to benefit both oneself and others, matching a minor bodhisattva's resolve; and an upper grade that forgets the self almost entirely in benefiting beings, cultivating merit and wisdom together and aiming at the Buddha's own enlightenment, matching the great bodhisattva's resolve. The guide notes a candid, almost consoling admission buried in this teaching: most people, receiving precepts for the very first time, manage only a middle- or lower-grade aspiration — which is exactly why the tradition builds in a second chance to receive again and consciously aim higher, rather than treating the first vow as the last word on how seriously it was meant.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads the opening critique of earlier Vinaya compilers as this whole second chapter's mission statement: previous works, following the letter of "the Vinaya legislates for the inner assembly," simply cut the laity out of the picture, while Master Hongyi's own source text goes back to the Vinaya itself to justify including them fully. The empty-space analogy is doing real work here — because it is boundless, it reaches everyone; because it is all-containing, it takes in every capacity — and the guide singles out the resulting formula as the key move: the Five Precepts are, in outward form, a law extended specifically to laypeople, yet in underlying essence they are the very same rule that structures the whole monastic path, so that keeping and violating, major and minor offense, and even the pre-ordination screening for disqualifying impediments, run in exact parallel between the two.

On why refuge is taken at all, the guide walks through the concrete logic of the criminal-and-foreign-king analogy: a person who has wronged their own sovereign and fled to another country receives that new king's protection only on strict condition — stay within his territory, obey his law. Sentient beings, bound to Māra in exactly the four forms the commentary lists — the heavenly tempter, the five aggregates themselves, affliction, and death — gain the same kind of protection by taking refuge in the Three Jewels, on the same condition of staying within that refuge. The guide treats this as more than a decorative simile: it explains why refuge, ordinary and formulaic as it can start to feel through repetition, is understood in this tradition as an actual change of allegiance with real consequences, not a mere verbal gesture.

Part Two: Showing Its Marks

The Commentary on the Precept-Text (Jieshu) says: "There are four kinds of Three Jewels. First, the noumenal-essence Three Jewels: the Five Aggregates of the Dharma-body serve as the Buddha-jewel; the unconditioned principle of cessation serves as the Dharma-jewel; the merit of the śrāvaka in training and beyond training serves as the Saṅgha-jewel. Second, the transformation-marks Three Jewels: Śākyamuni, sovereign of the three-thousandfold world through the path, serves as the Buddha-jewel; the teaching of the truths, expounded and spread abroad, serves as the Dharma-jewel; Kauṇḍinya and the other four of the first five disciples serve as the Saṅgha-jewel. Third, the sustaining Three Jewels: images and stūpas serve as the Buddha-jewel; what is handed down on paper and silk serves as the Dharma-jewel; the precept-Dharma and its outward marks serve as the Saṅgha-jewel. Fourth, the single-essence Three Jewels: as is discussed in the ordinary way — divided into three aspects purely in terms of the essence of mind, as when the Nirvāṇa Sūtra speaks of the Three Jewels sharing one and the same nature, and so on." The Xingzong Commentary glosses: "Other schools all lack the noumenal-essence Three Jewels — this fourfold distinction is unique to the present Jieshu. First, on the noumenal-essence: of the Five Aggregates, precept, concentration, and wisdom take their name from the cause, while liberation and the knowledge-and-vision of liberation take their name from the fruit; 'the principle of cessation' is the truth of cessation among the Four Noble Truths, that is, Nirvāṇa; 'in training and beyond training' means, from the first fruit onward, those who together see the real truth are called the Saṅgha united in principle — note that this noumenal-essence is itself of a single essence throughout, the distinction being made only in how Buddha and Saṅgha each realize that principle. Within the transformation-marks: 'three-thousandfold' is the realm of beings transformed; 'the teaching of the truths' is the Four Noble Truths — though the Dharma-gates are many, this speaks of them by their foremost representative, and the Four Noble Truths comprehend cause and fruit for ordinary being and sage alike, however broadly or narrowly the Great and Lesser Vehicles frame it. Within the sustaining Three Jewels, the precept-Dharma is the very substance of the Saṅgha, and its outward marks are the shaving of the head and dyeing of the robe. 'As is discussed in the ordinary way,' within the single-essence, refers to the position taken generally across the sutras and treatises." The Zhiyuan Yibian says: "The deluded thought of sentient beings, and the heaven-given, spontaneously perfect nature originally possessed within it — this is the single-essence Three Jewels. The fruit-virtues of every Buddha, pure and undefiled — this is the noumenal-essence Three Jewels. Appearing at the right moment for beings to see, opening and instructing the multitude — this is the transformation-marks Three Jewels. Bequeathing abundance to later ages, flowing on without end — this is the sustaining Three Jewels." [Cited from the Zhiyuan Yibian, appendix.]

The Jieshu continues: "These Three Jewels benefit the world: in their near effect they pull beings out of the three realms of existence, and in their far effect they cleanse the two kinds of birth-and-death. Rare in the world, and alone able to reach all the way through — hence they are worthy of honor, and are called 'jewels.' The Ratnagotravibhāga (Treatise on the Jewel-Lineage) accordingly uses the analogy of a jewel to distinguish six meanings. First, the meaning of rarity: a worldly jewel is what the poor and destitute lack; the Three Jewels are what those of scant merit fail to encounter. Second, the meaning of freedom from stain: a worldly jewel's substance is free of blemish; the Three Jewels are entirely free of every defiling stain. Third, the meaning of power: a worldly jewel dispels poverty and removes poison; the Three Jewels grant the six supernormal powers, inconceivable in their reach. Fourth, the meaning of adornment: a worldly jewel adorns the body and makes it beautiful; the Three Jewels are able to adorn the Dharma-body. Fifth, the meaning of supremacy: a worldly jewel is supreme among ordinary things; the Three Jewels, vast beyond measure, are unsurpassed. Sixth, the meaning of changelessness: a worldly jewel, refined and polished, does not alter; the Three Jewels are unmoved by the eight worldly winds." The Xingzong Commentary glosses: "The opening briefly explains the sense: the first three lines show the benefit; the words 'near' and 'far' necessarily correspond to cause and fruit. 'The two kinds of birth-and-death': the first is birth-and-death in fixed spans, ended in common by all three vehicles; the second is birth-and-death by transformation, brought to a final end by the Buddha alone. The line following 'rare' draws the conclusion: 'rare in the world' means the world lacks it; 'alone able to reach all the way through' means it transcends every mode of existence. What follows next matches the four kinds of Three Jewels against these six meanings. The first meaning applies across all four: without faith one fails to encounter the sustaining Three Jewels; without the right conditions one fails to encounter the transformation-marks Three Jewels — the case of the 'three hundred million' at Śrāvastī [who never once perceived the Buddha though he dwelt among them] illustrates this; the noumenal-essence is confined to sages, and the ordinary and the foolish never encounter it; the single-essence is not encountered even by the two lesser vehicles in its fullness. Within the second meaning, this applies only to the noumenal-essence jewel: the Homage Liturgy says the substance of the sustaining Three Jewels is itself conditioned and fully subject to outflow and defilement, and that the substance of the transformation-marks is impermanent, shifted by the four marks of arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing, its extinction now more than a thousand years past, so one can only look back on it from a distance and use this to increase one's own reverence; and as for the single-essence, taken from the standpoint of delusion, it too follows along with defilement — hence none of these three other kinds carries this second meaning, only the noumenal-essence does. Within the third analogy, the six supernormal powers belong to persons, and so correspond only to the transformation-marks jewel; 'poison' means suffering and affliction. The fourth meaning applies across all four: the sustaining and transformation-marks Three Jewels are able to give rise to faith and reverence, while the noumenal-essence and single-essence can be cultivated and realized — all of these count as 'adorning the body.' The fifth meaning likewise applies across all four, and the remaining three are easy to understand: the sustaining Buddha-Dharma, though made of worldly materials, is never without expressing the Dharma-body, its function without limit; the Homage Liturgy says gold, wood, stone, and clay are, in their substance, insentient, yet because an image has been fashioned from them, a person who honors or defaces it earns karmic merit or fault accordingly, hence one knows this jewel deserves the name 'unsurpassed' — the Dharma-jewel follows the same principle, and a monastic with shaved head and dyed robe is a field of merit for the world, foremost among gods and humans, such that even one who breaks the precepts and acts wrongly still surpasses an ordinary outsider in this respect, how much more, then, one who genuinely upholds and practices it. The sixth meaning is confined to the noumenal-essence jewel alone: the Homage Liturgy says these Three Jewels abide constantly in the world, never overridden or belittled by worldly dharmas; though the single-essence too is constant, from the standpoint of delusion it still shifts and changes along with conditions, and the remaining two are impermanent, so are not under discussion here. As for 'the eight dharmas': the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says decline, gain, disgrace, honor, praise, censure, suffering, and pleasure — four adverse and four favorable — are able to stir the feelings of beings, and are together called the eight winds; the noumenal-essence jewel, in both its person and Dharma aspects, belongs entirely to the undefiled, transmundane path of the sage, and so is not moved by these eight dharmas."

The Jieshu also says: "Speaking of the four kinds of jewel, the noumenal-essence is supreme. Because it alone abides constantly, it is what the world truly takes refuge in; the other three are established only provisionally, their substance being conditioned dharmas." The Xingzong Commentary glosses: "That the noumenal-essence jewel is supreme is because principle reaches through both Great and Lesser Vehicles alike: the emptiness of the person realized in the Hīnayāna's own principle, and the pure, fundamental mind of true suchness in the Mahayana's own principle — here one must reckon superiority and inferiority specifically from the Mahayana standpoint. If so, how does this differ from the single-essence jewel? Answer: the single-essence is spoken of strictly from the standpoint of delusion, concerning the ordinary being alone; the noumenal-essence is spoken of from the standpoint of realization, concerning the sage alone — hence the two are kept distinct. This is why the noumenal-essence jewel alone surpasses the other three." [The above is all found in the Xingzong Commentary on the Jieshu, fascicle 2.]

The Homage Liturgy (Guijing Yi) says: "As for taking refuge in the noumenal-essence Three Jewels in their aspect of dependent principle: 'principle' here means the ultimate principle itself, the heaven-given nature that abides constantly, which is none other than the very essence of mind. Spoken of from the standpoint of defilement, it is without a discernible beginning yet does have an end — bound only by the net of delusion, unable to break free of its own obstruction. Now, by means of the three trainings, this entanglement is cut through and cleared away; once affliction and karma have been overturned, the nature of mind shines forth in its own light, pure from beginning to end — a purity that is, in truth, without beginning and without end at all. Established by means of the Dharma, and divided into aspects according to its objects, this aspect is named the Five Aggregates of the Dharma-body: precept, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge-and-vision of liberation. The first three trainings take their name from the cause: because precept protects and assists, the fruit becomes the Dharma-body, hence called 'the precept-aggregate'; concentration and wisdom may be understood by the same pattern. The latter two take their name, in sequence, from the fruit: 'the liberation-aggregate' is so called because, through wisdom overcoming affliction, the place where affliction is no more is named liberation; 'the aggregate of the knowledge-and-vision of liberation' is so called because, having broken free of entanglement and shattered obstruction, one turns the light of contemplation back upon the observing mind itself. Only the sages of the three vehicles within the Buddha-Dharma possess these Five Aggregates in full, and are thereby able to serve as the great refuge for beings of all six destinies. Hence a treatise says: taking refuge in the Buddha means taking refuge in the Five Aggregates of the Dharma-body of all-knowing wisdom; taking refuge in the Dharma means taking refuge in the truth of cessation, Nirvāṇa itself; taking refuge in the Saṅgha means taking refuge in the merit of every worthy and sage, in training and beyond training, at the point where both their own and others' afflictions are utterly exhausted — that is, the very point where self and other alike have nothing further of affliction left to extinguish, hence called 'the point of exhaustion.' Just as a worldly treasure is prized by the living, so these Three Jewels are looked to with reverence by every being, by the three vehicles and the seven assemblies alike — hence they are called 'the true refuge.'" The Homage Liturgy continues: "This shows that the noumenal-essence jewel is the very object refuge itself is grounded in — hence it is explained here once more in careful detail, so the mind may have something firm to rest upon." [The above is all found in the Homage Liturgy for the Buddhist Community, fascicle 2.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide organizes the four Three Jewels as four different vantage points on the same underlying reality, moving from the most abstract to the most concrete: the noumenal-essence Three Jewels are the Buddha's own realized qualities — the Five Aggregates of the Dharma-body, the principle of Nirvāṇa itself, the śrāvaka's realized merit — accessible only to sages; the transformation-marks Three Jewels are the historical, one-time appearance of Śākyamuni and his first five disciples in this world; the sustaining Three Jewels are what actually keeps the tradition alive in the ordinary practitioner's daily life across the centuries — statues, scriptures on paper, and the precept forms that shape a monastic's very appearance; and the single-essence Three Jewels locates all three within the practitioner's own mind, present in seed form even amid ordinary deluded thought. The guide highlights that this fourfold scheme, uniquely including the noumenal-essence category, is original to Dao Xuan's own Jieshu and not found in other Vinaya traditions.

On the six meanings that justify calling any of this a "jewel," the guide reads the whole passage as an argument against treating refuge as merely sentimental: a real jewel is rare, unstained, powerful, adorning, supreme, and unchanging, and the Three Jewels are shown to satisfy every one of these tests more completely than any worldly treasure could — up to and including remaining wholly unmoved by the "eight winds" of gain and loss, praise and blame, that buffet ordinary life. The guide also flags that the noumenal-essence jewel alone is ultimately named supreme among the four, precisely because it alone is genuinely constant — the other three, however precious, remain in some sense conditioned or provisional.

13. Milestone: The Benefit of Refuge — and Why It Must Be Preceded by Repentance

Parts Three and Four turn from what refuge is to what it actually does, and what must happen before it can be taken sincerely. The benefit is described in almost extravagant terms — a single sincere refuge outweighs a lifetime of support given to countless enlightened beings — while the repentance that must precede it addresses a specific problem: a mind still carrying old wrong views cannot simply add refuge on top without first turning away from what came before.

Part Three: Its Benefit

The Homage Liturgy (Guijing Yi) says: "A sutra says: if a person once hears the two words 'eternally abiding,' that person will not fall into the evil destinies in any future birth. Why is this so? Because he thereby knows that the fundamental nature of the Dharma and the Buddha is eternal. Hearing and understanding it just once already perfumes the fundamental consciousness-mind; once the karmic seed is formed, pure faith is never lost again. How much more, then, when one is actually able to establish the vow of taking refuge, and honor the Three Jewels as one's own teacher and model — this will certainly remain pure and excellent across kalpa after kalpa, with no possibility, in principle, of ever being submerged. As the sutra says, there was one who, having received the Three Refuges, will be liberated from birth-and-death at the very first assembly of Maitreya. This is exactly the good ford by which one leaves the sea of suffering, the very first step by which one enters the ranks of the Buddha-Dharma. Yet because offenses are many and evil grave, those who take it lightly and treat it with contempt, though they meet the occasion of receiving refuge, still lose it again as conditions shift. This is exactly why the wise person, at the moment of first receiving refuge, fixes the mind single-mindedly on this very act — this alone is what deserves the name 'taking refuge.' It is this that moves good spirits to follow along and offer their protection." [Cited from the Homage Liturgy for the Buddhist Community, fascicle 2.]

The Homage Liturgy continues: "Every discerning person, monastic or lay, must take refuge in the Three Jewels and seek their protection — the merit thereby obtained is without limit. The seven assemblies, reckoned by precept, have already received refuge beforehand; this is no obstacle to receiving it again, and receiving again generates the non-act precept afresh. Since this holds for both wholesome and unwholesome action alike, it holds equally for precept. If one has not yet received precepts, one may still simply receive the Refuges that Turn One from Wrong Views alone — at any of the six periods of the day, taking refuge whenever one wishes, openly professing refuge in the Three Jewels, vowing for oneself never to turn back."

The Homage Liturgy also says: "As the Sūtra on the Upāsaka Precepts says: if a person receives the three self-professed refuges, the resulting fruit cannot be exhausted — like the four great treasure-vaults, which the entire population of a kingdom could not empty even carrying from them for seven years straight; the merit of one who receives the Three Refuges surpasses even that, beyond all reckoning. The Sūtra on Comparing Merits also says: were the whole of the four great continents filled with beings who had attained the fruit of the two lesser vehicles, and someone were to make offerings to every one of them for a whole lifetime, even to the point of raising stūpas for them — this would still not equal a man or woman simply saying, 'I, so-and-so, take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha.' The merit thereby obtained is inconceivable, because among all forms of merit, the Three Jewels alone are supreme. The Saṃyukta Āgama also says: whoever associated with Sudatta was made to receive the Three Refuges, and in the end was born in the heavens. Those who were pregnant had the unborn child receive the three self-professed refuges on its behalf; once born, the child received them again; and once old enough to understand, was taught the Three Refuges once more. Even if a servant or a traveler in one's household became pregnant and bore a child, the same was taught to them. When purchasing a servant, one first had them receive the Three Refuges and the Five Precepts, and only then completed the purchase; if this could not be done, the purchase was not made. Even in matters of borrowing, lending, and charging interest, one required the other party to first receive the Three Refuges before proceeding. Whoever donated an offering to the Three Jewels, having heard it from the World-Honored One himself, invoked his name and made a vow, and thereby attained birth in the heavens. The Buddha said: 'Excellent — the Tathāgata possesses unsurpassed knowledge and vision, and knows precisely the skillful means by which all may attain birth in the heavens.'" [The above is all found in the Homage Liturgy for the Buddhist Community, fascicle 9.]

The Procedural Commentary (Xingshi Chao) says: "The Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: a pregnant woman, fearing for the safety of her unborn child, should first receive the Three Refuges; once she has done so, no harm will come to the child, and once it is born, it will be complete in body and mind, and protected by good spirits." Part Four: Repentance

The Procedural Commentary says: "Because one has believed in wrong views for so long, one has heedlessly committed acts against the Dharma. Now, taking refuge for the first time, one must certainly turn away from wrongful karma. The Āgamas and other sutras all direct that repentance come first. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: 'confess openly every evil deed, disclosing in full every evil committed since the boundary of birth-and-death, all the way to the point where there is no further point to reach.' If one wishes to set out a formal repentance, it may be recited at whatever time suits, and this may likewise be applied in common to the refuges." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The opening explains the intent: anyone who has not yet taken refuge in the Three Jewels is, by that very fact, called one who 'believes in wrong views'; anyone who follows along with birth-and-death is, by that fact, said to carry out 'wrongful karma.' What follows cites textual support. In the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, 'the boundary of birth-and-death' means beginningless time; 'the point where there is no further point to reach' means the utmost future limit — that is, attaining Buddhahood and realizing great Nirvāṇa is itself called 'the point where there is no further point,' meaning that once this utmost limit is reached, there is nowhere further to go. What follows points to the actual method: 'at whatever time suits' means each person recites it in his own way; 'applied in common' means, as in the various sutras, simply repenting the ten unwholesome deeds across the three times, and the like." [Cited from the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 39.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide points to a single line as the practical heart of the whole section on benefit: refuge taken casually — "lightly and with contempt" — can be lost again as conditions shift, while refuge taken with the mind genuinely, single-mindedly fixed on the act itself is what actually earns the name "taking refuge," and draws the protection of good spirits along with it. The extravagant comparisons that follow — outweighing offerings to countless arhats and pratyekabuddhas, outweighing the wealth of four great treasure-vaults — are not there to inflate the ego of the newly converted, the guide suggests, but to make vivid just how much is actually at stake in a moment that can otherwise feel like a formality.

On repentance, the guide reads Dao Xuan's diagnosis as exact rather than merely pious: everyone who has not yet taken refuge counts, by that very fact, as having "believed in wrong views," and everything done under that condition counts as "wrongful karma" — not necessarily through malice, but simply through not yet having turned toward the truth. This is why repentance has to come before refuge rather than after it: one cannot simply add a new allegiance on top of an old one without first, deliberately, setting the old one down. The guide also notes the striking scope of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own phrase for this — confessing everything "since the boundary of birth-and-death," meaning since beginningless time itself, all the way to the point, at final Buddhahood, "where there is no further point to reach."

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats the timing question as the single most important technical point in this whole section, one that recurs, unchanged, at every later ordination in the book: the precept-essence — or, at the refuge stage, its non-act counterpart — is generated the instant the first threefold recitation is finished, not at the second, closing threefold repetition. The closing repetition is a "charge," not a fresh act of reception; its whole purpose is to fix what has already been received firmly in memory, so it will not later be forgotten. The guide draws out why this matters practically: it means the decisive moment of any ordination in this tradition is earlier, and quieter, than most people assume — already accomplished before the ceremony's more emphatic-sounding closing words are even spoken.

On the sundry clarifications, the guide reads all four questions as testing the same underlying principle from different angles: the refuge has to be complete, unified, and sincerely meant to actually take hold. Omitting even one of the Three Jewels invalidates the whole act, since the Three are received as a single indivisible unit; splitting the vow across three different preceptors fails for the same reason, since person and Dharma would differ with every fresh recitation; and, unlike the Five and Eight Precepts, which are locked to fixed terms, the Three Refuges may be taken for any length of time the practitioner chooses. The guide highlights the final ruling — that refuge is, in principle, open to beings across all five destinies, barring only those under the very heaviest karmic retribution or incapable of understanding what is being said — as one more expression of this gate's recurring theme: the precept-Dharma is, at its root, "not confined to the ordinary or the sagely," and here, not even confined to the human realm.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide unpacks the "outranks a hundred years of vast almsgiving" claim by pointing to the three kinds of giving Yuan Zhao finds packed into precept-keeping at once: giving of wealth (not stealing), giving of fearlessness (not killing), and giving of the Dharma itself (living the precept and thereby modeling it for others) — three gifts that ordinary material giving can never combine, since wealth is always limited in supply and inevitably tangled up with the giver's own attachment. The guide also flags a second, related teaching: giving done by someone who does not keep precept is itself corrupted by greed and yields a correspondingly degraded result, while giving grounded in precept stays pure all the way through to the fruit of Buddhahood — precept, in other words, is not merely one good deed among others, but the condition that makes every other good deed actually work as intended.

On screening candidates, the guide is careful to separate two very different categories the source text keeps distinct: ordinary disqualifying impediments — theft from the Saṅgha, sexual misconduct with a close relative or monastic, abandoning an ill parent or teacher, killing a bodhisattva-aspirant — which bar someone only until they have genuinely repented, after which the Five Precepts remain fully open to them; and a small set of graver offenses, above all defiling a monastic, which permanently close off full monastic ordination even after repentance, though lay precepts and lay practice remain available. The guide reads this two-tier structure as characteristically compassionate rather than punitive: the tradition draws a hard line only where a specific higher status is at stake, while keeping the door to lay precept, and to eventual liberation, open to almost everyone.

Part Three: Preparing in Advance to Generate the Precept

The Commentary on Karman Procedures (Yeshu) says: "When one is about to receive precept, one must first be told the breadth of the objects involved, so that the recipient's resolve reaches far and his grasp of the object is entirely clear." [Cited from the Commentary on Karman Procedures, fascicle 10.]

[Among the first four precepts, the precepts against stealing and lying also involve insentient objects; this is explained below, in the section on the scope of objects that generate the precept.]

The Procedural Commentary (Xingshi Chao) says: "The Sarvāstivāda tradition says: whenever precept is received, one must first be given teaching to lead and open the understanding, so that a mind of compassion is aroused toward every single object — only then is an ever-increasing precept obtained." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "This citation shows the need for the preceptor's own instruction. 'Opening the understanding' — understanding is precisely wisdom, and the precept-Dharma is so deep and broad that only wisdom can master it. 'Every single object' means sentient and insentient alike. All too often one sees people who merely recite the words, and both object and mind end up falling short — how can one not feel some dread at this?" [Cited from the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 8.]

[Preparing in Advance to Generate the Precept divides into four components: first, What Is Received as the Dharma; second, The Scope of the Object-Field That Generates the Precept; third, Generating the Aspiration in Accordance with the Object-Field; fourth, Applying the Mind in Reverent Reception.]

Component One: What Is Received as the Dharma

The Zhiyuan Yibian says: "Setting the mind on receiving precept, one must recognize just what this Dharma is: it is what a Buddha, appearing in the world, established as the precept-Dharma, restraining and guarding body and speech, and taming conduct of mind. Because the Buddhas of the ten directions, and the worthies and sages of all three vehicles, cultivate this same Dharma in common, it is called sage-Dharma. Now, generating the mind and vowing to take up this Dharma, one receives it by performing the formula. When causes and conditions come together in union, mind and object merge as one, and the non-act precept is thereby generated and received into the mind — this is called the precept-essence. One should therefore know that, at the moment of receiving, one must apply the mind with the utmost care — this is the great matter of an entire lifetime, a matter that can hardly be treated lightly." [Cited from the Zhiyuan Yibian, fascicle 3.]

Component Two: The Scope of the Object-Field That Generates the Precept

The Zhiyuan Yibian says: "The object relied upon is nothing less than every dharma of every sentient being's dependent and direct retribution throughout the Dharma-realm. Precept is legislated in relation to its object, and the essence is generated from that object; since the object is immeasurable, the essence too is boundless." [Cited from the Zhiyuan Yibian, fascicle 2.]

The Zhiyuan Yibian continues: "Though the objects relied upon are many, they do not go beyond two kinds. First, sentient objects: the Buddhas of the ten directions and three times, as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, together with the bodhisattvas, pratyekabuddhas, and śrāvakas; the gods, humans, asuras, hell-beings, hungry ghosts, and animals; down to the tiniest crawling things — mosquitoes, gnats, fleas, and lice — and even the intermediate-state beings outside the six destinies. All such beings, spread throughout the ten directions and reaching across the three times, immeasurable, boundless, beyond all counting, generate precept when one is ordained. Second, insentient objects: every mote of dust in every land throughout the world, every mountain, river, and expanse of earth, every tree, plant, flower, and fruit, down to a single flower, a single leaf, a single object, a single speck of dust — each and every one, in its full number, likewise generates precept. This extends even to the two truths of emptiness and existence, the principle of cessation and Nirvāṇa, the sacred teachings and their scriptural scrolls, and every image and stūpa; and to earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness, and the like." [Cited from the Zhiyuan Yibian, fascicle 3. This passage follows the Procedural Commentary; the Zichi Commentary's gloss on it is cited below.] The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The four clauses beginning with 'emptiness and existence' specifically name the two remaining jewels: since sentient and insentient objects between them already take in every object without remainder, this special showing is given only to forestall any confused doubt. The first line shows the transformation-marks: the Buddha's teaching of the Four Noble Truths takes in cause and fruit for the worldly and transmundane, ordinary being and sage alike; suffering, its origin, and the path are together named 'the truth of existence,' while cessation is 'the truth of emptiness' — also called the two truths of the ultimate and the conventional. Answer: for fear that one might suppose sacred objects do not generate precept. Question: how do these two Dharma-aspects generate precept at all? [Cited from the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 8.]

The Zhiyuan Yibian continues: "Now, the body and mind presently before us have, across immeasurable kalpas and within this present life alike, created karma of birth-and-death beyond all counting, an evil mind spread out and filling the entire Dharma-realm. If one obtains precept, then all this immeasurable evil karma is turned into a pure precept-essence, becomes a wholesome seed, and forms the very root of eventual Buddhahood. The patriarch of Nanshan says: before receiving precept, one's evil already fills the whole Dharma-realm; now, wishing to go forward and receive it, one turns every one of those former evil objects around and gives rise to a wholesome mind in its place — hence the very cause from which precept is generated likewise fills the whole Dharma-realm." [Cited from the Zhiyuan Yibian, fascicle 3.] The Zichi Commentary says: "As for 'Dharma-realm': reckoned by the ordinary limit of the teaching, this is confined to a single three-thousandfold great-thousand world-system; taken here in the Consummate Teaching's own sense, however, it must be discussed as the Dharma-realm of the ten directions. The essence of the non-act precept arises exactly matching its object, equal in measure to the whole Dharma-realm itself." [Cited from the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 8.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide treats "Preparing in Advance to Generate the Precept" as the theoretical heart of the whole gate — the part that explains, in real practical terms, how a formula recited aloud actually produces something as consequential as a precept-essence. The definition given for "what is received" is deliberately precise: it is not the words of the vow themselves, but what results when causes and conditions come together, mind and object fuse as one, and the non-act precept arises and settles into the recipient's own mind. The guide underscores Yuan Zhao's closing warning not to let familiarity dull the seriousness of this moment — it is called, without exaggeration, "the great matter of an entire lifetime."

On the object-field, the guide highlights the logic behind an otherwise startling claim: since precept is legislated in relation to its object, and the object named here is nothing less than every sentient being and every insentient thing throughout the entire Dharma-realm, the resulting precept-essence must itself be boundless — there is no smaller object available for it to be generated in relation to. The guide also draws attention to the passage's most striking single image: the ordinary person's accumulated evil across countless past lives is described as already filling the whole Dharma-realm, and ordination does not shrink or erase this vastness — it reverses its polarity, turning that same vast field, object for object, into an equally vast field of wholesome seed. The scale of the sin one starts with becomes, quite literally, the scale of the good one receives.

The Procedural Commentary (Xingshi Chao) says: "Next, the recipient is made to generate the precept. The Procedural Commentary continues: "The remaining two are explained in terms of their sense. What is the middle grade? The Zichi Commentary glosses: "In the middle grade, the opening shows the fruit aimed at must still be reckoned in terms of the Buddha-vehicle; what follows shows the practice aimed at, benefiting both sides at once. 'Ford and bridge' is an analogy: sentient beings, fallen into doubt, thereby undergo birth-and-death, and this practitioner is able to open their understanding and bring them across. Birth-and-death is like the river-crossing, and one's own person is like the bridge — the analogy is self-evident."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "What is the upper grade? One who says: 'I now generate the mind and receive precept, so as to accomplish the three collections of pure precepts, and to enter the three gates of liberation, directly seeking the fruit of Nirvāṇa — and, further, using this same Dharma to guide sentient beings to Nirvāṇa, causing the Dharma to abide long in the world.'" The Zichi Commentary glosses: "The upper grade has three parts: the opening shows the practice that benefits oneself; what follows shows the practice that benefits others; and 'causing the Dharma to abide long' shows the practice of protecting the Dharma. Within the first, what follows shows the far-reaching aim: the first two lines are the Mahayana's three trainings, that is, causal practice; the following line, seeking great Nirvāṇa, is the consummate fruit. 'The three collections of pure precepts' comes from the Sūtra of the Jeweled Necklace: first, the precept of the collection of formal discipline, which forbids evil, definitively cutting off karmic bonds and affliction — the practice of stopping; second, the precept of the collection of wholesome dharmas, which cultivates worldly and transmundane practice and realization to their ultimate completion — the practice of doing; third, the precept of the collection of benefiting sentient beings, which brings every single being to ultimate liberation — the practice of the four means of attraction, also called the precept of comprehensively benefiting sentient beings. 'The three gates of liberation,' though a matter of contemplative wisdom, cannot arise without concentration — together they are the two trainings of concentration and wisdom, through which the cutting-off of every bond and the realization of true reality are entered into. Though the name is shared with the Hīnayāna teaching, here, set against the three collections, it must be confined to the Mahayana sense: first, the gate of liberation through emptiness, the emptiness of self-nature; second, the gate of liberation through signlessness, the emptiness of characteristics; third, the gate of liberation through wishlessness, that is, mind-only. This shows that great conduct must be reckoned in terms of consummate cultivation. 'The fruit of Nirvāṇa,' though this name too is shared with the Hīnayāna, that the Mahayana sense is meant here should be clear. Question: is what is received here identical with the three collections, or not? If one says it is identical, must one then still separately receive the bodhisattva precepts afterward — and would this not confuse Great and Lesser Vehicles beyond distinction? If one says it is not identical, then, since precept arises from the mind, and this very mind has already been generated, how could this not already be that same precept? Either way one risks serious misunderstanding — hence this point is specially raised here, so the reader may work it out for himself. Next, on benefiting others: 'this Dharma' is the causal practice named above, and 'Nirvāṇa' is the fruit-virtue named above. Within protecting the Dharma: benefiting oneself and benefiting others, transmitted on from one to the next without break, keeps the Buddha-seed from ever being cut off, and this is how the Dharma comes to abide long in the world. Fearing the discussion has already grown too lengthy, only the outline is sketched here; the rest is treated fully in the Jieshu."

The Procedural Commentary says: "Once one thus knows for oneself the exact measure of one's own mind, the pure precept of the Buddha one obtains will likewise carry that same measure." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "This is precisely because the non-act precept is generated by means of the act itself; being neither physical matter nor mind, it has no way to be shown directly, and its superiority or inferiority can only be shown through the capacity of the one who receives it. As explained above, the upper grade aims far and wide, so the essence thereby received is certainly known to be all the more excellent."

The Procedural Commentary says: "Question: this school's own teaching — what vehicle does it belong to, that it should give rise to a Mahayana aspiration? Answer: this Four-Part Vinaya school, in substance, amounts to the Mahayana. Master Guang, too, classed it as belonging to Mahayana Vinaya." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "Since the upper grade explained above seems to overstep the teaching's own tradition, this question and its answer are necessary. In the answer, the first two lines give the heading: to say 'in substance amounts to' shows that the teaching-tradition is not, in itself, originally the Mahayana — its sense merely 'amounts to' it, which the Jieshu calls 'partially opening onto' the Mahayana, the sense here being the same. What follows invokes an ancient precedent, but that classification is far too loose and sweeping — though it draws on textual evidence, its conclusion cannot be wholly accepted: if the Four-Part Vinaya simply were the Mahayana, what then would count as Hīnayāna? It would then have to follow that its substance and conduct are entirely identical with the Brahma Net Sūtra, with no distinction at all between the bodhisattva precepts and the śrāvaka precepts — this is certainly not so. This is exactly why our own patriarch's own formulation proceeds with such deliberate restraint: 'in substance amounts to a partial opening onto' the Mahayana matches the teaching's true intent far more deeply. Question: is the upper-grade mind, then, wholly Mahayana, or only a partial opening onto it? Answer: it supports and completes the fundamental tradition itself, and is a matter of partial opening only. Question: on whose authority is this notion of 'partial opening' established? Answer: the Tathāgata, in establishing the teaching, shaped it to fit this very capacity; the founder of this branch, understanding this deeply, established the Tattvasiddhi school precisely to match the Buddha's own intent. This is exactly the sense repeatedly expressed throughout the prefaces to the Vinaya, both earlier and later, and in every position the Dharma-master himself settled on — how could this not be clear?" [The above is all found in the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 8.]

["Partially opening onto," as the Jieshu uses the term, is treated further later, in the section on the Nominal-Designation school within the Gate of the Precept-Essence.]

The Zhiyuan Yibian says: "Question: is this the same essence as the bodhisattva precept-essence, or different? Answer: the essence is the same, though the conditions differ. As for the conditions differing: in the Mahayana ordination, the threefold refuge, the four universal vows, requesting a preceptor, screening for impediments, the threefold karman procedure, and all the remaining formal elements are worlds apart from the present, lesser tradition's own two-part procedure. As for the essence being the same: the outward conditions are only a supporting aid, while the mind is the true, direct cause; the conditions are distant, the cause is intimate, and the essence is fundamentally generated from the cause — since the mind, as already shown, can be generated at the upper grade, one knows that the essence obtained here is itself already Mahayana. Question: if the essence is the same, how, then, are keeping and violating, and the setting up of repentance, and all the other marks of conduct, to be distinguished at all? Answer: though the essence is reckoned in terms of the Consummate Teaching, conduct must necessarily follow from what was actually received; since what was received follows the tradition proper to it, the marks of conduct show no discrepancy. If so, since keeping and violating are taken strictly according to the tradition received, why must the essence itself be reckoned in terms of the Consummate Teaching at all? Answer: because the essence runs through many different paths at once, it must be reconciled through the Consummate Teaching; because the discipline itself does not differ, it can still be treated according to its own tradition. Moreover, upholding it in accordance with the tradition received keeps reception and subsequent conduct in exact correspondence, while understanding it in terms of the Consummate Teaching lets the mind settle wherever it is applied. Once the great understanding has been opened up in this way, upholding the lesser discipline itself becomes great conduct — why, then, would one need to cast the lesser aside before it could be called 'great'? Hence the Jieshu says: 'constantly reflecting in this way, this very conduct is itself already the collection of formal discipline,' and so on." [Cited from the Zhiyuan Yibian, fascicle 1.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide walks the three grades through their exact wording at ordination: the lower grade vows only to save beings by not taking their lives, matching the śrāvaka's own more limited aim; the middle grade adds actually guiding others through the Dharma, becoming a "ford and bridge" across birth-and-death, matching a minor bodhisattva's resolve; and the upper grade names its aim explicitly as the three collections of pure precepts, the three gates of liberation, and Nirvāṇa itself — while also vowing to guide others there and to keep the Dharma itself alive for future generations. The guide notes that the source text deliberately teaches only the upper-grade wording to the candidate at the moment of ordination, precisely so no one settles for less than they might have reached.

On the delicate question of how a Hīnayāna-classified ordination can generate a genuinely Mahayana aspiration, the guide highlights Zhanran's careful formula as the resolution: "in substance amounts to a partial opening onto" the Great Vehicle — neither fully Mahayana outright, which the Zichi Commentary explicitly rejects as too sweeping a claim, nor confined to the Lesser Vehicle with no opening at all. The Zhiyuan Yibian's own image, "the essence is the same, though the conditions differ," makes this concrete: the outward ordination procedure genuinely differs from the full Mahayana bodhisattva ordination, but the mind generated — since it can, in principle, reach the upper grade — is already of the same essence. This is why, the guide notes, someone who has received only the Five Precepts, with a sincerely upper-grade aspiration, is not thereby locked out of the Mahayana path; the small vessel already carries the great cargo.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads the warning about timing as a corrective aimed squarely at how ordinations can actually go wrong in practice: if a candidate hears about the upper-grade aspiration for the very first time only as the ceremony itself is already underway, there simply is not enough time for the teaching to settle, and the result is described bluntly as "hazy and unsettled" — an ordination that may generate little or nothing of real weight, however correctly the words themselves are pronounced. The guide treats this as the source text's own explanation for why so many ordinations, technically valid, still fail to produce the upper-grade precept the tradition actually recommends: not bad intentions, but simply insufficient preparation beforehand.

On the visualization itself, the guide singles out the instruction to picture one's own body as "encompassing the whole of empty space" as the practical technique that makes the vast object-field described earlier actually receivable — a finite human body cannot literally hold a precept-Dharma equal in scope to the entire Dharma-realm, so the mind must first be trained to imagine a vessel large enough to hold it. The guide reads the passage's closing warning — that even a hair's-breadth of distraction at this exact moment reduces the whole event to a lifetime spent in vain — as continuous with everything the gate has said since its opening lines about naming the cause by its future fruit: what is received here is called "sage-Dharma" precisely because it is treated with a seriousness that matches the sage's own path, not because it is easy to receive correctly.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide highlights the contrast this section draws with the Three Refuges: refuge is conferred to anyone straightaway, precisely because the newly-turned mind is fragile and needs to be secured at once, while the Five Precepts are deliberately screened and offered by portion, because by this stage the candidate's mind has already settled and can bear real reflection on capacity. Asking "which portion do you wish to receive" is not a hedge, the guide stresses, but a safeguard — better an upāsaka of one honestly-kept precept than one who nominally receives all five and immediately breaks several.

The guide reads the preceptor's final pre-ordination speech as this whole gate's most vivid single passage: the claim that the incoming precept-Dharma, were it to have physical form, would enter the body with a sound "like heaven splitting apart and earth breaking open," and that it goes unnoticed only because it has no such form. The instruction to envision one's own body as "a vessel the size of empty space itself" is offered as the practical answer to an obvious problem — an ordinary, karmically-conditioned body plainly cannot contain something that vast, so the mind must be deliberately expanded to meet it. The guide also draws out the three-stage structure mapped onto the threefold recitation still to come: stirring the Dharma into motion, gathering it overhead like a canopy of cloud, and finally pouring it down into body and mind — a structure that will recur, essentially unchanged, in both the Five Precepts' and the Eight Precepts' own ordination formulas.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide flags the built-in announcement — "what was just conferred in the threefold refuge is precisely the precept-essence itself" — as Nanshan closing a real historical dispute rather than merely stating a preference: some earlier authorities held the essence arrives only once the fifth and final precept has been individually named, or even that a single precept named right after the refuge already fixes the whole set. The Vibhāṣā's own "settled, correct position," which this tradition adopts, is unambiguous: the threefold refuge alone already does the work. Everything that follows — naming each precept individually and asking whether it can be upheld — is instruction for living out what has already been received, not a further step in receiving it.

The guide also lingers on the practical detail folded into the closing exhortation: the "three months, six days" fasting calendar tied to when a heavenly king, or his emissaries, are said to survey the human world for good and evil conduct. Rather than treating this as mere folk astrology, the guide reads it as this gate's characteristic move once again — taking a widely shared cultural framework and refilling it with Buddhist content, in exactly the way the six-directions bowing custom or the wheel-turning king's worldly virtue were handled elsewhere in this tradition. The guide also notes Yuan Zhao's own gentle correction of a folk practice still current in his day — drinking only water all day and calling it a "pure fast" — as a reminder that Buddhist fasting was never meant as an extreme austerity, but simply as not eating past noon.

The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "'Not received in full but only in part' means not receiving all five but only one or two, so that the precept-essence is not generated at all. This is resolved just below in the Sūtra on the Upāsaka Precepts' own account of receiving the five by portion: that sutra holds that at the moment of reception all five precepts are generated at once, and it is only in how many one subsequently keeps that the terms 'one portion,' 'few portions,' and so on arise. Next, in the Tattvasiddhi treatise: 'all at once' means receiving all five together, 'gradual' means receiving by portion. Since the Three Refuges earlier do not permit any of the three to be left out, and separate reception from different preceptors is likewise ruled invalid, this question is specially settled here, for fear one might suppose the Five Precepts work the same way. 'Bakkula' translates as 'good countenance,' since he was known for his fine bearing. He was of the brahmin caste, a native of India. In the time of the ancient Buddha Vipaśyin, he had once, as a poor man, given a single myrobalan fruit to a sick monk, who recovered upon eating it; through this single cause, he enjoyed happiness and merit among gods and humans for ninety-one kalpas. He later sought ordination under the Buddha and attained arhatship, living to the age of one hundred and sixty without ever once falling ill — all this on account of having once kept, in that single past life, only the one precept against killing. The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "As the Vibhāṣā and the *Saṃyuktābhidharmahṛdaya both explain: the Five Precepts, received once, are fixed by the Buddha's own legislation and must necessarily be kept for the whole of bodily life; the Eight Precepts must necessarily be kept for one day and night only — in neither case may this be departed from. In the Treatise on Establishing Reality, however, either may be received for however many days one wishes, up to and including the whole of bodily life." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "That the Five Precepts, kept for a whole lifetime, cannot be shortened, and the Eight Precepts, kept for a day and night, cannot be extended, is because they are fixed by the Buddha's own legislation. What follows cites the Tattvasiddhi treatise: both precepts may be extended or shortened freely." [The above is all found in the Commentary on Karman Procedures, fascicle 10.]

The Procedural Commentary (Xingshi Chao) says: "The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom asks: of the four verbal faults, why does only false speech appear among the Five Precepts? Answer: naming false speech alone already takes in the other three as well; moreover, the Buddha-Dharma prizes truthful speech above all, and so it is given priority." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "According to the Sarvāstivāda school, only four branches are generated; the Tattvasiddhi school generates all seven, which agrees rather closely with this treatise, hence it is cited here to show this. [Cited from the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 39.]

The Zhiyuan Yibian says: "In that Sarvāstivāda tradition, the Five Precepts, Eight Precepts, and Ten Precepts generate only four branches — it is full ordination alone that generates all seven. In this tradition, all seven assemblies alike are bound equally by all seven branches." [Cited from the Zhiyuan Yibian, fascicle 1.]

The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "As the Vinaya text itself allows self-ordination in every case, while the Vibhāṣā's own text permits the five assemblies to receive from a preceptor — both are said to be valid." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "The root Vinaya and the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom both permit self-ordination; the Vibhāṣā and the Kośa both require reception from a preceptor. Though both are said to be valid, this is settled below, under the Eight Precepts: self-ordination is permitted only where no preceptor is available; where one is available, self-ordination is not permitted."

The Commentary continues: "Reckoned by this question and answer, reception from a preceptor is the rule. Yet the Vinaya text itself gives: 'World-Honored One, I now take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha; for the whole of this bodily life I will not kill... up to... not drink alcohol' — this is precisely a self-administered vow. Since the Eight Precepts already permit this, it should be permitted here as well. Whether to apply this depends on the occasion — the point is not meant to be rigidly restrictive. In reaching out to convert ordinary people, one should raise whichever approach fits the capacity at hand." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "The opening points to reception from a preceptor above; what follows shows self-ordination. The Tattvasiddhi treatise likewise allows self-ordination for the Eight Precepts, as cited later."

The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "According to the Vibhāṣā, one may not receive precept again. According to the Tattvasiddhi treatise and the Four-Part Vinaya, both allow re-reception — Queen Mallikā receiving the Five Precepts a second and even a third time is exactly the proof of this." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "In the Multi school, the Five Precepts must necessarily be kept for the whole of life, and so are received only once, with no possibility of adding to them again. The Tattvasiddhi school is not like this: whatever term one receives them for, one may receive and add to them again and again. The case of receiving two or three times over is recorded in the Four-Part Vinaya." [The above is all found in the Commentary on Karman Procedures, fascicle 10.]

First, applicability across destinies — Multi school: beings of other destinies do not obtain precept; Tattvasiddhi school: beings of other destinies do obtain precept.
Second, gradual versus sudden — Multi school: receiving less than the full set obtains no precept at all; Tattvasiddhi school: receiving less than the full set still obtains precept.
Third, extending or shortening the term — Multi school: the Five Precepts must be kept for life, the Eight Precepts for one day and night only; Tattvasiddhi school: the term may be freely extended or shortened.
Fourth, how many branches are generated — Multi school: only four branches are generated; Tattvasiddhi school: all seven branches are generated.
Fifth, self-ordination — Multi school: reception must be from a preceptor; Tattvasiddhi school: self-ordination is permitted.
Sixth, re-reception — Multi school: re-reception is not permitted; Tattvasiddhi school: re-reception is permitted.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads all six points of this table as expressions of a single underlying temperament: the Multi (Sarvāstivāda) school reads the Vinaya strictly and narrowly, while the Tattvasiddhi school reads it more generously, extending precept to a wider range of beings, allowing partial and repeated reception, and permitting flexible terms. The guide flags Nanshan's own working practice, already noted earlier in this gate: although the Four-Part Vinaya is formally classed as belonging to the stricter Multi-adjacent Nominal-Designation school, when it comes to the actual lived details of lay practice, Nanshan repeatedly follows the more accommodating Tattvasiddhi reading instead, precisely because it is better suited to drawing ordinary laypeople into practice without excessive rigidity.

The guide also highlights the two illustrative stories tucked into this otherwise technical table: Bakkula, who kept only the single precept against killing across one entire past lifetime, and was rewarded across ninety-one kalpas and, in his final life, with freedom from illness for a hundred and sixty years — offered as living proof that partial reception genuinely works, not merely as a theoretical concession; and Queen Mallikā, who received the Five Precepts two and three times over, offered as proof that re-reception is not merely permitted in the abstract but was actually practiced by respected historical figures. The guide reads both examples as doing the same work the whole Sundry Clarifications section does throughout the gate: turning an abstract doctrinal ruling into something a layperson can recognize as directly relevant to their own actual situation.

22. Milestone: The Eight Precepts — Three Names for One Practice, and Why One Day Can Match a Lifetime

Section Three turns to the Eight Precepts of the Poṣadha Fast, the last of the ordination liturgies this gate covers. It opens by unpacking three overlapping names for the very same practice, then makes an even bolder merit-claim than the Five Precepts received: a single day and night of the Eight Precepts, kept in full purity, can outweigh a lifetime of the Five — because where the Five Precepts merely restrain illicit sexual conduct, the Eight cut off sexual conduct entirely.

Section Three: The Eight Precepts
This section divides into six parts: first, Explaining the Name; second, Its Merit; third, Screening Candidates; fourth, Repentance; fifth, The Formula; sixth, Sundry Clarifications.

Part One: Explaining the Name

The Commentary on Karman Procedures (Yeshu) says: "As for why it is called 'precept,' called 'fast,' and called 'closure' — however many the names, all without exception mean gathering purity and turning the mind back to itself. As for 'the Eight Precepts': 'eight' names the objects being guarded against, 'precept' names the karmic action able to counter them. As for 'the eightfold fast': 'fast' means 'to make level,' bringing the mind to a single, unified point — it is also said to mean 'to purify,' stilling and gathering one's thoughts, just as a quiet room in ordinary life is likewise called a 'fasting-hall.' As for 'the closure-fast': this means shutting off wrongdoing and idleness, stilling and settling body and mind." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "The opening gives an overall showing of the three names — sometimes each is used singly, sometimes strung together as 'the Eight Precepts of the Poṣadha Fast'; the sutras and Vinaya call it by different names in different places. Ceasing distraction and departing from fault, wholesome dharma increasing from within — this is exactly what is meant by 'gathering purity and turning the mind back to itself.' What follows explains the three separately: first, the meaning of 'precept' — what is guarded against is the fault, what counters it is the practice. Part Two: Its Merit

The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "The Vibhāṣā says: a sutra says that even becoming a wheel-turning king of Jambudvīpa, possessed of every human treasure and complete mastery over it all, does not equal a sixteenth part of the merit of the Eight Precepts." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "'Jambudvīpa king' means the wheel-turning king. In that land a single coin was divided into sixteen parts, and sutras and treatises frequently use this as a standard of comparison."

The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "The Sūtra on the Upāsaka Precepts says: excepting the five heinous offenses, every other offense is thereby extinguished."

The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says: it is like a weak, timid general commanding troops — he never accomplishes anything in the end; a valiant general, by contrast, breaks the enemy in a single day, his achievement covering the whole realm. The Five Precepts and Eight Precepts stand in exactly this same relation. This is because the Five Precepts only depart from illicit sexual conduct, without yet cutting it off entirely, while the Eight Precepts' own conduct is wholly pure, matching the undefiled state itself. Reckoned by term, the Eight Precepts' period is short; reckoned by conduct, what they accomplish is greater — hence their merit surpasses that of the Five Precepts." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "Desire is the very root of birth-and-death, and cutting off desire entirely transcends birth itself, hence the comparison is drawn specifically in terms of sexual conduct. Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads the three-name explanation as building toward a single practical picture: "precept" names the discipline itself, "fast" names the concentrated, single-pointed state of mind that discipline is meant to produce, and "closure" names the protective function of shutting the door against wrongdoing and dissipation — three angles on one and the same undertaking, which is why the full formal name strings all three together as "the Eight Precepts of the Poṣadha Fast." The guide's own military image for the merit comparison — a timid general who accomplishes nothing across a long campaign, against a valiant general who wins everything in a single day — is offered as making vivid exactly why duration is not the same as merit: what makes the Eight Precepts outweigh the Five is not that they are kept longer, but that within their short term they achieve something the Five Precepts alone never reach, namely the complete cutting-off of sexual conduct rather than its mere restraint.

The guide draws particular attention to the Śakra story as a small, pointed teaching about humility: the king of the gods himself claims his own accumulated merit equals that of a layperson keeping the Eight Precepts for a single day, and the Buddha corrects him — such a claim could only rightly be made by someone whose defilements are already exhausted, a sage, not even by the ruler of the Thirty-Three Heavens. The guide reads this as reinforcing, from an unexpected angle, the same claim already made about the Five Precepts: real precept-keeping, however brief, can outrank even the most exalted worldly position, and even a great deity is shown here explicitly overreaching in claiming otherwise.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide points to a small but telling omission in the Eight Precepts formula, compared to the Five Precepts: the closing title "the Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly and Fully Enlightened One, is my World-Honored One" is dropped. The reason given is structural rather than casual — that title was added to the Five Precepts specifically to mark off correct refuge from the many rival claimants to the name "Three Jewels," a job already done once a candidate has advanced to the Eight Precepts, which assume the Five have already corrected that ground. The guide also flags Yuan Zhao's own careful hedge on this point: for someone receiving the Eight Precepts directly, without first taking the Five, there is a reasonable argument the title should be restored, since the omission's logic no longer strictly applies — a small but real example of how much interpretive judgment survives even within a fixed liturgical formula.

On the "eight items, nine supports" puzzle, the guide walks through the structure directly: nine distinct commitments are actually listed, yet the whole practice is still called "the Eight Precepts," because "not eating past noon" is the fast's own substance (齋體), while the other eight function only as its supporting conditions (助齋) — guarding against the wrongdoing and idleness that would otherwise undermine that one central fast. The guide also highlights the vow's own final aspiration, sarvajñā, "all-knowing wisdom," as tying this brief one-day-and-night practice directly back to the upper-grade aspiration introduced earlier in the gate: even so short and modest a precept, taken with the right resolve, is still explicitly aimed at nothing less than complete Buddhahood.

Component Two: Whether Delayed Reception Still Succeeds

The Karman Commentary says: "The Kośa says: if one has already resolved beforehand to receive the fast on this particular day, then even if one has already eaten before actually receiving it, reception still succeeds." The Commentary on Karman Procedures glosses: "This is because, observing that the delay arose from outward circumstances beyond one's own control, no mind of violating the precept has actually arisen."

The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "If, just as one is about to receive it, some difficulty arises preventing this, then once the difficulty has passed and one receives it afterward, this too succeeds." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "Such as being detained by a king's officers or by bandits — the mind remains open and willing even though outward circumstances obstruct it, so reception is permitted to succeed even at midday or afterward." [The above is all found in the Commentary on Karman Procedures, fascicle 10.]

Component Three: Receiving Alone to Avoid Fault

The Procedural Commentary says: "The Sūtra on the Upāsaka Precepts: in receiving the Eight Precepts, one should not do so in a large group — one should receive it alone." The Zichi Commentary glosses: "That the Sūtra on the Upāsaka Precepts forbids doing this in a large group is out of fear that people would become mixed together and the mind would fail to stay single-pointed. Speaking generally of refuge and precept, receiving it alone is best, since the mind then has nothing else to attend to, and the Dharma cannot become diffused or confused. Receiving it today in large assemblies, though not incorrect in principle, still ends up being less than ideal in practice." [Cited from the Zichi Commentary on the Xingshi Chao, fascicle 39.]

Component Four: Cutting Off Evil to Attain Purity

The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "The Sūtra on the Upāsaka Precepts says: if persons of high rank habitually order others to commit evil acts, then, should they wish to receive the fast, they must first cut off and renounce this practice — only afterward does reception succeed. If they do not renounce it, reception does not succeed at all. The Treatise on Establishing Reality says: there are those who, relying on established official procedure, or compelled by superior authority, cause harm to sentient beings, and suppose themselves free of any offense — yet they still incur the offense of killing, since every condition for it is fully present." The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "The first citation clarifies whether reception succeeds. 'Persons of high rank' means kings and great ministers. 'Habitually order others to commit evil acts' means carrying out killing, flogging, and the like. The next citation forestalls a possible doubt. 'Relying on established official procedure' means, as when an official administering government carries out the code of the state, or is ordered by a superior to execute or punish someone — a foolish person often supposes himself thereby free of any offense."

The Commentary on Karman Procedures says: "If one has already received the fast, and orders that a being be flogged — even if it is not carried out that very day, but only intended for the next morning — the fast is already impure in either case. To put it succinctly: if body or speech carries out any act unbecoming of proper deportment, this alone is already called 'impure.' Or if the mind gives rise to a thought of greed, hatred, or the intent to harm, though this does not technically break the fast, the fast is nonetheless not pure. And if one fails to cultivate the six recollections and the like, this too is called 'impure.'" The Jiyuan Commentary glosses: "The first shows a violation of body and speech; 'intended for the next morning' means an act already resolved upon in advance. What follows shows a violation of mind: resentment at what goes against one's own wishes is hatred, and wishing harm upon another is malice — both of the above are violations of commission. What follows that shows a violation of omission: one who has received precept must, day after day, recollect the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, the heavens, precept, and giving." [The above is all found in the Commentary on Karman Procedures, fascicle 10.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads the "in-between person" ruling as a small but telling illustration of how seriously this tradition takes precise classification: the Multi school genuinely cannot fit someone who has taken only a one-day precept into any of the seven standard assemblies, and rather than force an awkward answer, it simply names a new, honest category for the case — neither inside the seven assemblies nor entirely outside the tradition's structure. The more accommodating Tattvasiddhi school dissolves the puzzle differently, by defining its assemblies broadly enough to include lay practitioners generally, so the awkward gap never opens in the first place. The guide treats this as one more instance of the Multi-versus-Tattvasiddhi contrast already seen throughout the Five Precepts' own comparative table.

On the final component, cutting off evil to attain purity, the guide singles it out as the gate's own closing word on what "keeping" a precept actually demands: purity is not satisfied merely by not physically breaking a rule. A person of high rank who routinely orders violence must renounce that standing practice before the fast can even be validly received at all; someone who has already received it but still merely intends a future act of violence has already compromised its purity, even without having yet carried the act out; and a mind that harbors greed, hatred, or the wish to harm — or that simply neglects the six recollections that are supposed to accompany the fast — likewise falls short of purity, even where no outward rule has technically been broken. The guide reads this graduated standard, moving from action to intention to the unguarded background state of the mind itself, as the fitting note on which the whole gate closes: precept, from its very first page, was defined as reaching toward the sage's own path, and only a correspondingly searching standard of purity could actually carry a practitioner there.
