The Grammar of Keeping and Breaking, Part One: Naming the Four, and Why the Same Fault Can Weigh Heavy or Light

Every gate that follows this one — every precept-by-precept ruling in the rest of the book — depends on a shared vocabulary laid down right here. This gate opens the "observance and violation" (持犯) portion of the book: the fourfold grammar of upholding-by-restraint, upholding-by-action, violation-by-commission, and violation-by-omission; where these four are actually fulfilled, in the mind rather than merely in outward act; and how the very same broken precept can draw a heavier or lighter offense depending entirely on the state of mind behind it — a psychology of guilt built from the three moral natures, a granular eight-way analysis of intention, and one of this book's most searching passages on why habitual wrongdoing is worth taking seriously at all.

1. Milestone: The Nine Chapters of "Observance and Violation"

Master Hongyi opens this gate the way he opens every gate in this book — with an outline of everything to come, so the reader knows the shape of the whole before working through any one part of it. "Observance and violation" (持犯) divides into nine chapters in all; this first half of the gate works through the first four, ending exactly where the source text itself marks a chapter-boundary. The remaining five chapters — the preparatory stage and the fruit it leads to, deficient conditions, object-perception formulas, special points on the grave precepts, and a refutation of a certain fashionable disparagement of the Vinaya — are the business of Part Two.

Gate One: The General Theory of Observance and Violation.

The topic of observance and violation divides into nine chapters: (1) Naming Observance and Violation; (2) The Essence of Observance and Violation; (3) Where Fulfillment Actually Occurs; (4) Weighing How Serious a Violation Is; (5) The Preparatory Stage and the Fruit It Leads To; (6) When a Deficient Condition Prevents Full Consummation; (7) Distinguishing by Object-Perception; (8) Special Points on the Grave Precepts; and (9) A Broad Refutation of a Foolish Teaching.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide points to a single sentence, quoted further on in this same gate, that supplies the whole framework's coordinates: "the two terms 'observance' and 'violation' are both named with reference to whether one accords with or goes against the precept-essence received [at ordination]." Everything else in this gate is an elaboration of that one relation. The Procedural Commentary then splits both "observance" and "violation" along a second axis — restraint (not doing something) versus action (actively doing something) — producing a 2×2 grid: according with the precept-essence through restraint is upholding-by-restraint, according with it through action is upholding-by-action; going against it through restraint (failing to do a required positive act) is violation-by-omission, going against it through action (actively doing a forbidden act) is violation-by-commission.

The guide flags one point as especially important for lay readers: a layperson's five or eight precepts are entirely prohibitive in structure — none of them command a positive act the way certain monastic precepts do. In the strict, formal sense worked out later in this gate, this means lay practice really only ever engages two of the four categories, upholding-by-restraint and violation-by-commission. The other two categories remain conceptually necessary — the grammar has to be complete — but a layperson's own precept-keeping practice, examined honestly, is a matter of not doing wrong, and of not being the one who actively does wrong; it is not, in this technical sense, a matter of neglecting some commanded action.

2. Milestone: Chapter One — Naming the Four

The Procedural Commentary works through the four terms one at a time, each definition built from the same three-part pattern: first a verb describing what the practitioner actually does (restrain, or act), then a statement of what happens to the precept-essence as a result, and finally the compound name itself, explained as the fusion of the two.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary says: "A 'name' is simply a 'word' — the two run together as a single expression, and there is no need to force them apart. The two terms 'observance' and 'violation' are both named with reference to whether one accords with or goes against the precept-essence received [at ordination]. This can be seen directly from the text."

The Procedural Commentary says: "First we explain the two kinds of upholding. As for upholding-by-restraint: bringing forth right mindfulness as a countermeasure, guarding what one originally received, holding body and mouth in check, and committing no evil at all — this is called 'restraint.' Where this restraint is unbroken, the precept-essence remains bright and pure, according with what one originally received — this is called 'upholding.' Because the upholding is accomplished through restraint, it is named upholding-by-restraint." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first line cites the name. From 'bringing forth' onward explains the meaning — first the meaning of restraint: 'bringing forth' means arousing the countermeasure, 'right mindfulness' means being free of deviant defilement; 'body and mouth' refers here to the seven branches [of act], but necessarily extends to all three types of karma. From 'where this restraint' onward explains 'upholding.' From 'because the upholding' onward binds the two together."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Second, we clarify upholding-by-action. Urging on the three types of karma and cultivating precept-conduct, so that a wholesome intention arises and gives rise to guarding — this is called 'action.' 'Upholding' is explained as before." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first phrase cites the name. From 'urging on' onward explains 'action.' From 'upholding' onward the text simply points back for brevity — the three clauses run exactly as before; continue with the prior passage, only substituting 'action' for 'restraint.'"

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Next we explain the two kinds of violation. As for violation-by-commission: harboring the three poisons within, cherishing the inverted view of self, one thereby stirs body and mouth into motion, acting against principle and constructing an object of transgression — this is called 'commission.' Where this commission involves a transgression, defiling what one originally received — this is called 'violation.' Because the violation is accomplished through commission, it is named violation-by-commission." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first line cites the name. From 'harboring' onward explains 'commission': the first two phrases are the root from which karma arises, and from 'stirs' onward is the karma thereby constructed. From 'where this commission' onward explains 'violation.' From 'because the violation' onward binds the two together."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "As for violation-by-omission: precisely because of a deluded, indolent mind, one's conduct goes against what one originally received, wearying of the superior practices and failing to cultivate them — this is called 'restraint' [in the sense of ceasing effort]. Where this restraint involves a transgression, running contrary to the very aspiration made at ordination — this is called 'violation.'" The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first phrase cites the name. From 'precisely because' onward explains 'restraint.' From 'where this restraint' onward explains 'violation.'" [All the above from the Zichi Commentary, fasc. 26.] The Xingzong Commentary says: "Violation-by-commission, the active construction of evil, draws on all three poisons together; violation-by-omission, negligence toward the Dharma, pertains specifically to the poison of delusion." [Jieshu commentary, fasc. 4.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide lays the four definitions out as a simple grid: restraint that accords with the precept-essence is upholding-by-restraint ("not doing evil, guarding what was received"); action that accords with it is upholding-by-action ("urging on the three karmas, cultivating precept-conduct"); action that goes against it is violation-by-commission ("stirring body and mouth, constructing an object against principle"); restraint that goes against it — that is, failing to act when action was required — is violation-by-omission ("wearying of the higher practices, failing to cultivate and study them"). The Xingzong Commentary's closing line matters for how these four actually feel from the inside: active wrongdoing typically involves all three poisons together, while neglecting a duty tends to trace back to delusion and complacency specifically rather than to a sharp burst of greed or anger.

3. Milestone: Single and Double Observance-Violation — Why Lay Precepts Are Different

Having defined the four terms, Master Hongyi pauses to explain a distinction the Commentary makes elsewhere: the difference between how these four categories work psychologically, in the mind of any practitioner, and how they work formally, in the technical classification the Vinaya actually applies to a given precept — and why, on the formal reckoning, a layperson's five or eight precepts turn out to involve only half of the fourfold grammar just explained.

Ru-Yi Translation

What the Commentary explains concerning single and double observance-and-violation divides roughly into two approaches: the first considers how the mind is actually used in practice; the second considers formal teaching-and-conduct.

Under the first approach — considering how the mind is used — this concerns the mind actually brought to bear in upholding a precept, not a formal classification. Hence every single precept, considered this way, involves both kinds of upholding and both kinds of violation at once. The five precepts and eight precepts received by lay people fully possess this sense: whenever one upholds any one precept, a protecting intention necessarily arises — considered from the angle of departing from evil, this is upholding-by-restraint, and considered from the angle of that same intention arising, it is at once upholding-by-action too. The same holds for the two violations: going against the teaching to commit evil is violation-by-commission, and failing even to consider a countermeasure is violation-by-omission. This means the two kinds of upholding and the two kinds of violation each interpenetrate one another — not that violation is somehow present within upholding. It is simply that wholesome and unwholesome conduct are distinct, and the mind that accords or opposes differs accordingly.

Under the second approach — considering formal teaching-and-conduct — when it comes to actually classifying and adjudicating the precepts, it is precisely this second sense that properly applies. Only where a precept's teaching actually mandates some positive action does that precept possess both kinds of upholding and both kinds of violation together; all the remaining precepts — such as the prohibitions against killing or stealing, for which the teaching prescribes no positive action — belong only to a single kind of upholding and a single kind of violation. Hence "double upholding and double violation," in the sense of formal teaching-and-conduct, exists only among the precepts of those who have left home: among the two hundred and fifty root precepts of a bhikṣu, twenty-six precepts possess this double structure. For example, where the teaching mandates that some matter actually be carried out: acting in accordance with the teaching is upholding-by-action, and, considered from the angle of there being no transgression, it is also upholding-by-restraint; failing to act in accordance with the teaching is violation-by-omission, and, considered from the angle of there being a transgression, it is also violation-by-commission. As for the five precepts and eight precepts received by lay people, these involve only a single kind of upholding and a single kind of violation, and have no bearing at all on this "double upholding and violation" of formal teaching-and-conduct.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide calls this out as a point worth lay readers dwelling on directly: psychologically, holding any single precept always involves the full fourfold structure at once, since a protecting intention that keeps one from wrongdoing is, looked at from a slightly different angle, itself already a kind of active cultivation. But the formal classification the Vinaya actually applies to a given precept is stricter, and depends entirely on whether that precept's own wording commands a positive act. Only twenty-six of a bhikṣu's two hundred and fifty root precepts have that structure; none of the lay five or eight precepts do, since they are worded purely as prohibitions.

The practical upshot, as the guide puts it, is that a layperson's precept practice is centered on two tasks and two only: not committing wrong (upholding-by-restraint) and not being the active agent of wrong (avoiding violation-by-commission). The worry about "neglecting a commanded positive duty" — violation-by-omission in the strict, formal sense — simply does not arise for the five or eight precepts as such, however much it matters for a monastic's fuller code.

4. Milestone: Chapter Two — The Essence of Observance and Violation: Mind as the Governing Essence

Having named the four categories, the Commentary asks a more basic question: what, exactly, is the "stuff" out of which observance and violation are made? The answer comes in two halves — the essence of the upholding subject (what actually does the upholding or violating) and the essence of the upheld object (what it is upheld or violated with respect to) — and the first half turns out to reach straight back to the precept-essence and precept-conduct already established in earlier gates of this book.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary says: "Second, we clarify the essence. We set aside other, secondary explanations, and directly present the correct one: presenting the essence involves two aspects — one in terms of the upholding subject, the other in terms of the upheld object." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first sentence dismisses the old explanations. The Commentary on the Precept-Text cites an earlier view: some hold that the ten wholesome actions constitute the essence of upholding-by-restraint, the ten unwholesome actions the essence of violation-by-commission, and practices such as giving, worship, chanting, austerities, and the four great vows the essence of upholding-by-action, with their opposites the essence of violation-by-omission. This fails to distinguish precept-essence proper from transformative conduct in general, and is rather sloppy in principle — refuted in detail elsewhere. From 'directly' onward marks out the present, correct explanation: both the upholding and upheld essences are established strictly on the basis of this school's own regulatory teaching. Where the text speaks of 'upholding' and 'upheld,' the term 'violation' is, for brevity, simply omitted, but its sense must be understood as included." The Xingzong Commentary says: "This does not mean that practices such as giving and compassion are simply not to be cultivated at all — only that they do not constitute the essence of observance-and-violation in this strict, technical sense." [Jieshu commentary, fasc. 4.]

The Procedural Commentary continues: "As for the upholding subject: the mind functioning with intention is its essence; body and mouth are merely its instruments. Hence a treatise says, 'these three types of karma are, in every case, simply the mind'; and the Vinaya says, 'being fully equipped with the three types of karma, one must carefully examine one's own intention,' and so forth, as will be further explained below." The Zichi Commentary explains: "The first line cites the name. From 'the mind' onward presents the essence — the first clause directly indicates it: the Precept-Text Commentary says, if there is no deliberation, no observance or violation is constituted, hence intention is taken as the essence of the upholding-and-violating subject. The second clause rules out what is not the essence: bodily and vocal form are merely the condition through which karma is completed, not karma's true root — elsewhere the Commentary says, 'the bodily category is merely the instrument, and is not to be called the karma itself.' From 'hence' onward cites supporting evidence: the first is from the Treatise on Establishing Reality, tracing karma to its root, which further says, 'apart from the mind there is no intention, and no karma of body or mouth.' The next citation is from the root Vinaya itself: mental karma is the sovereign, and the karmas of body and mouth are completed by means of it — hence it alone is singled out for careful examination, to determine whether a violation is constituted or not. 'And so forth' refers to how the Vinaya, in adjudicating a violation, always asks with what mind it was done, and how, among the cases of non-violation, forgetfulness and mistaken-belief are uniformly exempted — 'as will be explained below' points ahead to Chapter Three.

"Question: the treatise says the three karmas are, in every case, simply the mind — this refers to the sovereign mind itself; how, then, can intention specifically be fixed on as the essence, as stated above? Answer: the sovereign mind and intention are simply essence and function, divided. The treatise traces the three karmas to their root, and so speaks in terms of essence; here we are determining the actual capacity that constitutes karma, and so speak in terms of function. If so, why not likewise clarify it from the root, as the treatise does? Answer: essence extends generally across the four aggregates, while function is confined specifically to the cultivating-mind. Setting aside the general in favor of the specific makes the discussion of karma far clearer. Furthermore, mind need not always be intention, but intention is necessarily mind; essence does not necessarily include function, but function necessarily attains essence. Speaking now of 'intention,' therefore, gathers essence and function together at once, with no discrepancy in meaning." [Zichi Commentary, fasc. 26.] The Xingzong Commentary says: "The Treatise on Establishing Reality is cited here because it belongs to this very school, and so serves as evidence. 'In every case simply the mind' refers to consciousness in the sense that still extends generally across the four aggregates; but what actually constitutes karma must reach the cultivating-mind — 'cultivating' meaning intention. Because intention arises from mind, and the two karmas of body and mouth are, in turn, completed by means of intention, the present passage speaks in terms of karma's root, and so simply says 'mind.'" The Xingzong Commentary also says: "Question: in the precept received at ordination, both form and mind together constitute its essence; here, in the case of the 'upholding subject' — that is, ongoing observance following ordination — only intention-mind is spoken of. Since receiving the precept and ongoingly observing it ought to correspond, why are they not the same? Answer: what is received depends on conditions coming together, and so must necessarily include form as well; what is ongoingly observed constitutes karma strictly in terms of mind. Students should reflect on this deeply before its far-reaching point becomes clear." [Jieshu commentary, fasc. 4.]

The Zhiyuan Commentary says: "All matters and dharmas [every concrete object of conduct] constitute the upheld essence — this is the correct doctrine. By 'matters and dharmas' is meant that each such object possesses both a wholesome and an unwholesome aspect. With respect to a wholesome or unwholesome matter or dharma, the mind arises in accordance or in opposition, and it is from this that the two kinds of upholding and the two kinds of violation are generated. The mind of accordance or opposition is itself the upholding essence; the wholesome or unwholesome matter or dharma is itself the upheld essence. Since observance and violation both arise dependent on some matter or dharma, we therefore take all matters and dharmas as the upheld essence. How could it be otherwise?" [Zhiyuan's Posthumous Compilation, fasc. 1.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide underlines a single governing idea running through this entire chapter: "mind is the essence, body and mouth are merely the instrument." Whether a given act constitutes observance or violation is never settled by outward appearance alone — it is settled by whether an intending mind was actually behind it, which is exactly why the Vinaya, in judging a case, always asks first "with what mind was it done." This "mind-first" premise, the guide notes, is not a new idea introduced here for the first time — it is the same governing logic already established for precept-essence and precept-conduct in earlier gates of this book, simply now applied specifically to the question of upholding and violating.

On the "upheld" side, the Zhiyuan Commentary's formula is deliberately all-encompassing: since every concrete "matter or dharma" a practitioner might encounter carries both a wholesome and an unwholesome possibility within it, the entire field of experience becomes, in principle, the object with respect to which the mind's accordance or opposition generates the four categories already defined.

5. Milestone: Chapter Three, Section One — Where Restraint Is Fulfilled: The Three Prior Minds

Chapter Three asks a still more exacting question: at exactly what point, within the flow of mental activity, does observance or violation actually come to be fulfilled? The answer borrows a four-stage psychological model — cognition, perception, sensation, and cultivation — and argues that the first three of these four are, strictly speaking, not yet karmically active at all, which creates an interesting puzzle specifically for upholding-by-restraint.

The guide walks through the borrowed four-mind model plainly: cognition discerns an object, perception takes hold of its image, sensation receives it as pleasant, painful, or neutral, and only cultivation — the "thinking," karma-constituting mind — actually generates karma. Strictly speaking, the first three minds are described as "neither karma nor recorded": they have not yet ripened into a determinate wholesome or unwholesome act at all. This creates a genuine puzzle for upholding-by-restraint specifically, since restraint, almost by definition, can look like simple absence of activity rather than any positive cultivating-mind event.

The Commentary's resolution, as the guide frames it, is a kind of courtesy extension rather than a strict technical claim: because the precept-essence received at ordination is itself already a determinate, "recorded" wholesome entity, one can loosely say that "upholding" is present even during the three prior minds, simply in virtue of that essence remaining undefiled — what the ancients called, memorably, "upholding-by-restraint in folded hands," accomplished spontaneously rather than through any deliberate act. The guide also notes the Commentary's own caveat: even someone who has broken one precept still keeps every other precept in this same loose, folded-hands sense — which is exactly the seed of the reassurance about "one failure is not total ruin" that this gate's companion section develops much further in Chapter Four.

6. Milestone: Chapter Three, Section Two — The Cultivating Mind, and Why Violation Reaches Further Than Upholding

Strictly speaking — once the loose, folded-hands sense from the previous section is set aside — none of the four practices are actually fulfilled until the cultivating-mind itself is reached. The manuscript itself shows visible wear at exactly the point distinguishing the three prior minds from the cultivating-mind in the case of violation — a gap in the transmitted text where a character has evidently dropped out. On mere mental karma, the guide is precise about the threshold: an isolated intention that never gets past pure thought constitutes nothing at all — but the moment that thought hardens into an actual, deliberate calculation of how body or mouth will carry the act out, it already counts as "belonging to body and mouth" even though nothing has physically moved yet. 9. Milestone: Chapter Four — Where Karma Really Begins, and Why the Same Broken Precept Can Weigh Heavy or Light

Chapter Four turns from where observance and violation are fulfilled to how serious a given violation actually is — and opens by distinguishing two entirely different ways of measuring that seriousness. The regulatory teaching (制教) judges mechanically: once the formal conditions for an offense are present, the fixed penalty simply applies. The transformative teaching (化教) judges by the concentration of the underlying state of mind instead — and it is this second, psychological register that occupies the rest of the chapter, tracing every offense back to its ultimate root in self-grasping.

The regulatory teaching alone sets a fixed standard: once the required conditions are all present, it firmly imposes its stipulated penalty, without regard to anything else. But the mind is never constant — once it stirs into activity, one must distinguish its essence and nature. Since cause and effect differ under the two teachings, transformation and regulation are accordingly distinguished. One must not remain in the dark as to this basic approach, nor fail to reach the very root of karma — hence what follows first sorts the matter out carefully, laid out in detail for later students." [Zichi Commentary, fasc. 14.]

Chapter Four contains four sections. The first two address both the transformative and regulatory teachings together; the third pertains to the transformative teaching alone; the fourth to the regulatory teaching alone. If one violates one of the "nature" precepts [acts inherently wrong], one incurs offenses under both the transformative and regulatory teachings at once; if one violates a merely "prohibitory" precept [wrong only because prohibited], one incurs an offense under the regulatory teaching alone.

The Procedural Commentary says: "The arising of karma necessarily depends on the three poisons for its generation; and what gives rise to the poisons in turn is rooted in the mind of self. This principle unfolds broadly, and practitioners must understand it — it is explained in full detail in the texts on repentance-practice."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "This first shows karma's root. Karma has no nature of its own — it necessarily arises by borrowing conditions. Though the conditions are many, they never go beyond mind and object: it is the object that provokes the poison, which then constructs and completes the karma. The object is the external condition, the poison arises internally — hence, in clarifying how karma arises, the text traces it back to nothing but the three poisons alone. The poisons arise from self, and self is itself a false, mistaken reckoning — this very false reckoning is karma's root, and so is called 'false karma.' A sutra says: 'the whole ocean of karmic hindrance arises entirely from false thinking.' Truly seeking this false root, one finds in the end nothing at all to rely on — it is simply the one mind, following conditions, unaware of itself; and it is precisely because of this unawareness that one obstinately reckons a 'self.' From this self, the poisons arise; from the poisons, karma is generated; karma, once complete, draws forth its fruit, and that fruit is entirely suffering — this suffering is precisely birth-and-death, wandering and appearing and disappearing, constructing and receiving, feeding on itself again and again, on and on through kalpa after kalpa, with no knowledge of where it will ever stop. From the root all the way to the branch-tip, reasoning from the fruit back to the cause — anyone who, on reading this, comes to recognize even a little of this false root and roughly grasps this root of suffering, how could they fail to reflect on themselves? How sorrowful indeed! What follows below points to a fuller treatment; the reader is asked to seek it in the later chapters, which need not be troublesomely cited here."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Now, briefly stated: the arising of an offense necessarily occurs in dependence on one of the three moral natures, and how deep or shallow the resulting retribution is always traces back, in every case, to mental karma as its root. Hence the Mingliao Treatise explains: the gravity of the offense incurred by breaking a precept is not fixed. There are cases where breaking a lighter precept, with a certain aggravated state of mind, incurs a heavier offense: where there is no sense of shame, and the act is done fearlessly and unrestrained; or where it arises from wrong views, holding that there is no cause and effect at all; or where it arises from lack of faith — holding either that this precept was not actually established by the Buddha, or that breaking it does not actually bring about this retribution; or where it arises from doubt — being uncertain whether the Buddha actually established it, or uncertain whether the retribution will actually be incurred. If the breaking of the precept arises from any such state of mind, the offense incurred is thereby made heavier. If it does not arise from any such state of mind, but the precept is broken merely by chance, then what would be a heavier offense is instead made light."

The Zichi Commentary explains: "Next the text properly sets out the matter of graver and lighter offenses, in two parts: first it shows the distinct gradations of offense and retribution. The guide names the tension that structures this entire chapter right at the outset: the regulatory teaching judges "from the formal teaching," mechanically, once the stipulated conditions are met; the transformative teaching judges "from the mind," and requires exactly the kind of careful psychological analysis this chapter goes on to provide. The Zichi Commentary's chain of causation — self-grasping generates the three poisons, the poisons generate karma, karma generates its fruit, and the fruit is suffering — is, the guide notes, this chapter's philosophical foundation: the whole subsequent discussion of "how heavy is this violation" is really an extended meditation on how self-grasping shows up, more or less starkly, in a given act.

The table drawn from the Mingliao Treatise gives that meditation concrete shape: the very same broken precept can be judged more severely if committed shamelessly, from wrong view, from unbelief, or from doubt — and, strikingly, can be judged less severely, "reversed to become light," when a graver precept is broken by someone who nonetheless retains shame, right view, faith, and freedom from doubt. The guide reads this as this book's own version of a broader Buddhist principle: what one did matters, but the state of mind one did it with matters at least as much, and sometimes more.

10. Milestone: The Wholesome Mind — Why Good Intentions Do Not Excuse a Violation

The chapter's psychological analysis proceeds through the three moral natures one at a time, beginning with a genuinely uncomfortable case: violations committed with a wholesome — even a compassionate — state of mind. The verdict is unambiguous, and rests on a single governing principle already familiar from earlier gates on precept-marks: ignorance of what one is doing is never, by itself, a valid excuse, because the precept-marks are something every practitioner is expected to have learned.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide highlights the two illustrative cases as deliberately uncomfortable: an administrator who genuinely did not know the property rules and mixed up the belongings of the Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha; and someone who, moved by real compassion, helps end a suffering being's life or urges a layperson to kill an animal quickly rather than let it suffer longer. In both cases the offense is committed by someone acting in obvious good faith — and in both cases the Commentary still records a full root offense.

The guide is explicit that the governing principle here — "ignorance is not permitted to serve as grounds for exemption; since the precept-marks are learnable, a root offense is incurred" — is not a claim that good intentions count for nothing at all. It carries forward directly from the general theory already established: a wholesome mind does genuinely make the resulting karma lighter than the same act done with hatred or greed. What it does not do is erase the formal violation itself. The precept-marks exist precisely so that a sincere practitioner has no excuse for not knowing them, and the responsibility to actually learn them is treated as inseparable from the responsibility to keep them.

11. Milestone: The Unwholesome Mind — Four Marks of a Genuinely Evil Intention

Where the wholesome mind still leaves a residue of good faith, the unwholesome mind is characterized by its total absence — a mind driven openly by greed, hatred, and delusion, and marked, the Mingliao Treatise specifies, by four distinguishing features that together define what counts as a genuinely "coarse, evil-minded" violation.

12. Milestone: The Indeterminate Mind — Unrestrained Drift, and the Boar Who Killed the Monk Who Never Meant to Kill

The third of the three moral natures is the hardest to pin down, since "indeterminate" ordinarily means neither wholesome nor unwholesome — yet the Commentary insists that even an indeterminate mind can, under the right conditions, still generate a genuine violation. Two kinds are distinguished: an ordinary, unremarkable drift of attention with no real protecting intention behind it, and a benighted state such as sleep, intoxication, or delirium. The section closes by carving out the one narrow case where an indeterminate lapse is genuinely exempted — and it turns out to depend entirely on who the practitioner already was before the lapse occurred.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide highlights the two kinds of indeterminate violation as genuinely distinct failures: "unrestrained abandon" is simply never having formed any protecting intention in the first place — an ordinary, unremarkable drift through the day with no real guard up at all — while "sleep or delirium" is a benighted state built on top of a prior, deliberate preparatory stage, such as setting up the means of a killing beforehand and then letting the act complete itself while unconscious. Both still count as violation, because both trace back to a real preparatory choice or a real absence of the protecting intention a practitioner is expected to maintain.

The dhutanga monk and the wild boar is, in the guide's reading, the chapter's most striking illustration of how far-reaching even indeterminate karma can be: an entirely unnoticed killing still ripens into a very concrete, personal consequence later on, even though it never rises to the level of a full rebirth-determining offense. The guide draws out the sentence that actually does the exempting work in this whole section: "the sole exception is one who constantly cherishes a protecting intention, yet acts under a mistaken, deluded lapse" — genuine exemption is reserved for the practitioner who has been vigilant all along and simply slips, not for anyone who happens to be unaware. The appended question-and-answer at the end, the guide notes, is Dao Xuan quietly correcting his own earlier, stricter statement — acknowledging that even an unlearned person can, by analogy, receive the same exemption in the right circumstance, since confusion about a matter does not actually differ depending on how much precept-study someone has done.

The guide singles this passage out as the most literary in the entire gate — a deliberate change of register, stepping back from the technical apparatus to describe the three evil destinies in genuinely visceral terms: the hells' scorching flame and unbearable cold, the animal realm's horns and fur bound up in the hunter's snare and the butcher's block, the hungry ghosts' torch-mouths and needle-throats subsisting on filth. The guide also notes the passage's closing gesture of humility — "these words run long and elaborate, [yet] how could the meaning they point to ever be exhausted" — as characteristic of Dao Xuan's own voice throughout the Zichi Commentary: even after such an extended and vivid description, he treats the underlying reality as still exceeding what any amount of prose could actually convey.

16. Milestone: Presence or Absence of Mind — The Four Grave Precepts across the Same Three Stages

The chapter's final section runs the same three-stage framework once more, but now asks a cruder, binary question at each stage — was any violating mind present at all, or not? — and applies the resulting eight combinations specifically to the four grave "nature" precepts: sexual misconduct, theft, killing, and false speech. Unlike the previous section's psychological, transformative-teaching register, this one is explicitly framed in terms of the regulatory teaching, and closes the chapter — and this half of the gate — on a note about why even a combination that incurs no offense at all still counts, in a meaningful sense, as violation.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Procedural Commentary says: "Setting presence-of-mind against absence-of-mind across the three time-periods likewise yields eight combinations, sorted into the same four ranks as before." The Zichi Commentary explains: "Presence-of-mind here generally covers both the grave and the light cases discussed above; absence-of-mind stands opposite to what came before. Running through the individual combinations, the pattern is entirely the same as the single-mind scheme."

What follows displays the pattern strictly in terms of the four grave "nature" precepts; the rest can be inferred by analogy.

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The first combination: mind present throughout all three time-periods."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The next three combinations. First: mind present at the preparatory and root stages, absent at the subsequent stage — all four grave precepts are violated as grave. Second: mind absent at the preparatory stage, present at the root and subsequent stages — likewise all four grave precepts are violated as grave. Third: mind present at the preparatory and subsequent stages, absent at the root-stage — the first precept is violated as grave, while the remaining three precepts incur a middle-grade or lower-grade offense."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The last three combinations. First: mind present only at the root-stage, absent at the preparatory and subsequent stages — all four grave precepts are violated as grave. Second: mind present only at the preparatory stage, absent at the root and subsequent stages — the first precept is violated as grave, the remaining three incur either a middle-grade or a lower-grade offense. Third: mind present only at the subsequent stage, absent at the preceding two — the precept against sexual misconduct is still violated as grave, because pleasure was felt at the moment of withdrawal; the remaining three precepts incur a lower-grade offense."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "The final combination: mind absent throughout all three time-periods — no violation is incurred."

The Zichi Commentary says: "... The sense of gravity and lightness here is likewise as explained above. If the eight combinations are compared against each other, one need only place the third combination of the second rank at the bottom, and the first combination of the third rank at the top, for the sequence to run in proper order. Question: if the lowest combination incurs no offense at all, how can it still be called violation, however light? Answer: it is simply that, with reference to the exemption granted by the regulatory teaching, there happens to be no offense in that particular case — it is not that no act was actually committed, and so it is still included within the category of violation."

The Procedural Commentary says: "In this latter set of eight combinations, whether a violation occurs or not is determined by the presence or absence of mind — unlike the earlier set of eight, where mind was, in every case, invariably present." The Zichi Commentary explains: "In this analytical passage, the first point distinguishes this scheme from the earlier single-mind scheme, which can be readily understood. One should recognize that the earlier scheme was framed in terms of transformative karma, while this present scheme is based on the regulatory teaching."

The Procedural Commentary continues: "Next, clarifying the case of absence-of-mind: this refers either to feeling no pleasure at all [in a case of sexual misconduct], or, in the case of killing, theft, and the like, to the mind being deranged or altogether unaware." The Zichi Commentary explains: "A second, more detailed presentation of 'absence of mind' follows, in two parts. The first cites what came before, since it stands opposite the grave-and-light single-mind scheme discussed above — hence 'next, clarifying.' (This may instead specifically mark out the fourth, wholly-absent combination.) From 'or' onward the pattern is shown: the first line specifically singles out the precept against sexual misconduct. This section, in clarifying that precept, is discussed entirely in terms of a case of coercion [by another party], with presence or absence of mind considered across the three time-periods; if the act is instead self-initiated, a violation is incurred as soon as the object is engaged regardless — the three time-periods are not even considered, and no exemption is granted by way of object-perception, so absence of mind is still judged grave in that case, and is therefore not under discussion here. What follows jointly displays the pattern for the remaining three precepts, considered generally in terms of a confused mind failing to recognize the object correctly. Returning to the precept against sexual misconduct: across the three time-periods, if mind is present at even a single one of them, the offense is, in every case, grave without exception — only where it is absent throughout all three is an exemption granted. For the remaining three precepts, gravity and lightness are not fixed: where mind is present at the first stage and absent at the rest, the offense is either middle-grade or lower-grade, since this pertains generally to the preparatory stage; where mind is present at the middle stage and absent at the rest, the offense is grave throughout, since this pertains to the root-stage; where mind is present only at the last stage and absent at the rest, the offense is lower-grade throughout, since this pertains merely to delighted approval after the fact." Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide is careful to distinguish this final section's register from the previous one: the single-mind scheme of the previous section belonged to the transformative teaching, grading intensity of intention on a sliding scale; this presence-or-absence scheme belongs instead to the regulatory teaching, asking a cruder yes-or-no question at each of the three stages, and it is explicitly restricted to the four grave "nature" precepts. The sexual-misconduct case gets its own special rule throughout, framed here strictly around coercion: if mind is present at even one of the three stages, the offense is grave without exception, and — strikingly — even a case where mind is absent at the preparatory and root stages still counts as grave "because pleasure was felt at the moment of withdrawal." Only total absence of mind across all three stages earns the exemption, and, the guide notes, that rule holds only for the coercion scenario being discussed; a self-initiated act of sexual misconduct is grave the instant the object is engaged, with no three-stage analysis or object-perception exemption available at all.

The guide singles out the closing question-and-answer as this gate's own quiet clarification of what "violation" actually means as a category: even the eighth combination, which the regulatory teaching formally exempts from any penalty, is still called a "violation" in the broader sense, because an act was genuinely committed — the exemption concerns only whether a penalty is imposed, not whether wrongdoing, in the fullest sense, actually occurred. This closes Chapter Four, and with it, the first half of this gate; Part Two takes up the preparatory stage and its fruit, deficient conditions, the object-perception formulas, and the special points reserved for the four grave precepts.
