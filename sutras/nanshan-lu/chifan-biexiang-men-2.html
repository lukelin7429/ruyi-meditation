The Four Grave Wrongs, Part Two — and the Five Lesser Rules

The first half of this gate worked through killing and stealing in exhaustive casuistic detail; this second half finishes the four grave nature-offenses with sexual misconduct and false speech, then turns to a second, shorter class of rule altogether — the five further prohibitions that distinguish the Eight Precepts of the Poṣadha Fast from the ordinary Five: drinking alcohol, eating after noon, sleeping on high or luxurious beds, adorning the body, and attending or performing music and entertainment. With the last of these, the Part on Upholding and Violation — the whole long middle section of this compendium — comes to its close, and the book turns next to its third and final part, on repentance.

1. Milestone: Two Senses of "Sexual Misconduct" — Restricted and Unrestricted

Before the casuistry proper begins, the compendium flags a terminological trap that runs through this entire section. Under the Five Precepts, "sexual misconduct" names a restricted class of act — essentially, adultery. Under the Eight Precepts, the same two characters expand to cover all sexual activity whatsoever, making the lay practitioner's rule for that one day identical to a monastic's. Since the source texts being quoted mostly speak of "sexual conduct" in the broad sense, and only occasionally narrow to the restricted sense, Master Hongyi warns the reader up front to keep the two apart.

Chinese Source

第三節　婬

五戒唯制邪婬。八戒正邪俱制、全同道眾。今文唯於犯相料簡中、略示邪婬之義。餘皆汎明婬相、不簡邪正。學者宜分別觀之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Section Three: Sexual Misconduct

The Five Precepts prohibit only sexual misconduct in its restricted sense [i.e., adultery]. The Eight Precepts prohibit both the restricted and the unrestricted sense at once, becoming on this point entirely identical to the monastic community's own rule. In the present text, only within the clarification of violation-marks does it briefly indicate what "sexual misconduct" means in the restricted sense; everywhere else it speaks broadly of the marks of sexual conduct as such, without distinguishing the restricted sense from the unrestricted. Students should take care to distinguish the two as they read.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide treats this restricted/unrestricted distinction as the doorway into everything that follows, not a mere translator's footnote. Under the Five Precepts, only adultery — relations with another's spouse — is forbidden, while relations within one's own marriage remain licit; the Eight Precepts erase that carve-out entirely, so that for the duration of the fast a lay practitioner renounces sexual activity of any kind, exactly as a monastic does. The guide reads Master Hongyi's warning here as more than philological housekeeping: because the casuistry that fills the rest of this section speaks broadly of "the marks of sexual conduct" without specifying which sense is meant, a reader who loses track of which set of precepts is actually in force on a given day risks either underestimating or overestimating what the text is actually asking of them.

2. Milestone: The Peril of Women

The compendium opens its treatment of the object of this offense not with a legal definition but with an extended, almost homiletic warning — quoting first the Saṃghika Vinaya's blunt verdict on the danger of women, then the Zichi Commentary's much longer meditation on why even a glance or an overheard voice already counts as a defiled act.

Chinese Source — 犯境, first citation

▲事鈔云『初明犯境者。僧祇云。可畏之甚、無過女人。敗正毀德、莫不由之。染心看者下罪、聞聲起染亦爾。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Fresh citation] The Procedural Commentary says: "First, clarifying the object of the offense: the Saṃghika Vinaya says, 'Among all things to be feared, none surpasses woman. The ruin of rectitude and the destruction of virtue both trace back to her. To look on her with a defiled mind is a lower-grade offense; to be defiled merely by hearing her voice is the same.'"

Chinese Source

資持釋云『初示過相。上二句示來報。次二句彰現損。言可畏者訶欲經云。女色者世間之枷鎖、凡夫戀著不能自拔。女解者世間之重患、凡夫因之至死不免。女色者世間之衰禍、凡夫遭之無厄不至。行者既得離之、若復顧念、是為從地獄出還復思入。又云。女人之相、其言如蜜、其心如毒。譬如清淵澄鏡而蛟龍居之、金山寶窟而師子處之。當知此害、不可近也。

敗正者立事公正、苟荒女色則無所成。即彼經云。室家不和、婦人之由。毀宗敗族、婦人之罪。毀德者修身立行、或著女色則皆喪失。即經云、凡夫重色、甘為之僕、終身馳驟為之辛苦。淨心觀云、貪色者憍憍、貪財者吝、既憍且吝、雖有餘德亦有足觀。

染下明制急。然心行微細、粗情不覺。縱知違戒制御猶難、豈況悠悠終無清脫。請臨現境、自審狂心。或宛轉迴頭、或殷勤舉眼、或聞聲對語、或吸氣緣根、雖未交身、已成穢業。大聖深制、信不徒然。諒是眾苦之源、障道之本。是以托腥臊而為體、全欲染以為心。漂流於生死海能知返、交結於根塵網裏實謂難逃。當自悲嗟、深須勉強。或觀身不淨即是屎囊、或諦彼婬根實唯便道。或緣聖像、或念佛名、或誦真經、或持神咒。或專憶受體、或攝念在心。或見起滅無常、或知唯識所變。隨心所到著力治之、任性隨流難可救也。』已上皆見事鈔記卷十七

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "First it shows the marks of this fault. The first two lines show the retribution to come; the next two make plain the harm already present. As for 'fearsome' — the Sūtra on Reproving Desire says: 'Woman's beauty is the world's fetters and shackles; the ordinary person, once infatuated, cannot free himself. Woman's charm is the world's grave affliction; the ordinary person, on her account, cannot escape it even unto death. Woman's beauty is the world's calamity and misfortune; the ordinary person, once caught by it, meets with every disaster.' Once a practitioner has actually managed to leave her behind, to turn back and dwell on her again is exactly like climbing out of hell only to think of climbing back in. It also says: 'A woman's manner — her speech is like honey, but her heart is like poison. She is like a clear, mirror-still pool in which a flood-dragon dwells, or a mountain of gold and a jeweled cave in which a lion makes its den.' One should know that this danger cannot be approached.

"'The ruin of rectitude' means that establishing affairs with public rectitude accomplishes nothing once a person has been laid waste by women. That same sūtra says: 'Discord within the household is caused by women; the ruin of clan and lineage is women's fault.' 'The destruction of virtue' means that cultivating oneself and establishing right conduct is entirely lost the moment one becomes attached to women. That sūtra says: 'The ordinary person prizes beauty and is glad to be its servant, driven and worn out by it for his entire life.' The Contemplation for Purifying the Mind says: 'One who craves beauty grows arrogant; one who craves wealth grows stingy. Being both arrogant and stingy, even one who still has other virtues to his name has, by that very fact, something to be watched closely.'

"The word 'defiled' below makes plain how urgent the prohibition is. The workings of the mind are subtle and fine, and coarse feeling does not notice them. Even one who knows the precept is being broken still finds it hard to master himself — how much less could a person drifting carelessly through life ever attain purity at all? Examine your own reckless mind closely when you actually stand before such an object. Whether it is a lingering, turned-back glance, an eager raising of the eyes, listening to her voice and speaking with her, or drawing in her scent through the breath — even though the bodies have not yet joined, a defiled deed has already been done. The great sage's prohibition goes this deep for good reason, and it is certainly not without cause. Truly this is the wellspring of every suffering, the root that obstructs the path. It takes what reeks of flesh as its very substance, and takes desire and defilement entire as its mind. One drifts upon the sea of birth-and-death [the source text appears to be missing a negating character here — the sense clearly requires something like "unable to find the way back"], bound up within the net of the senses and their objects, and truly it is hard to escape. One ought to grieve over this and make every effort to master it. Either contemplate the body as impure — it is nothing but a sack of filth — or examine that organ of desire closely: it is, in truth, nothing but an excretory channel. Or fix the mind on a sacred image, or recite the Buddha's name, or chant a true sūtra, or hold a spirit-mantra. Or focus the mind wholly on the precept-body one has received, or gather one's thoughts and hold them fast. Or observe how all things arise and perish, impermanent; or know that all is merely a transformation of consciousness. Wherever the mind is inclined, apply effort there to treat it — for if one simply follows one's nature and drifts with the current, there is no saving it." [The preceding passages are all found in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 17.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide singles out this passage as the compendium's most rhetorically intense, and reads its purpose as practical rather than merely admonitory: the extended imagery of woman as fetters, affliction, and calamity — and the biting image of a clear pool concealing a flood-dragon, or a jeweled cave concealing a lion — is meant to jolt a practitioner out of treating attraction as harmless. Its real teaching point, in the guide's reading, is that defilement is completed well before any bodily contact: a lingering glance, an eager upward look, or simply catching a scent already constitutes a stained deed, "though the bodies have not yet joined." The remedies the text offers are correspondingly mental rather than physical — contemplating the body as nothing more than a sack of filth, fixing attention on a sacred image or a recitation, or simply naming the arising and passing of the impulse as impermanent — because, as the guide frames it, the danger lives in how the mind meets its object, not in the object itself.

3. Milestone: The Object Defined — Realms, Bodies, Orifices

Having warned of the danger in general terms, the Procedural Commentary turns to a precise definition of what counts as the object of this offense: which classes of being, which bodily forms, and which orifices are at issue, together with the rule that even the barest penetration — "as little as the width of a hair" — already completes the upper-grade offense.

Chinese Source

△事鈔續云『四分中。犯境。謂人非人天子鬼神等畜趣。據報則男女二形。據處則女人三道謂大小便道及口、男子二道。此等婬處、若覺、若眠。若死未壞、少壞。但使入婬處如毛頭、皆上罪。律云。牛馬豬狗雁雞之屬。莫問心懷想、但是下道皆重。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Continuation] The Procedural Commentary continues: "In the Four-Part Vinaya: as for the object of the offense — it means a human, a non-human [such as a deity, a spirit, or a ghost], or any being of the animal destiny. In terms of physical form, this covers both the male and the female body. In terms of location, a woman has three orifices — the anus, the urethra, and the mouth — and a man has two. As for these sites of sexual union, it makes no difference whether the partner is conscious or asleep, or whether, in the case of a corpse, it is not yet decomposed or only slightly decomposed — so long as penetration of the site reaches as little as the width of a hair, it is in every case the upper-grade offense. The Vinaya says: with cattle, horses, pigs, dogs, geese, chickens, and the like, it makes no difference what thought or intention is held — so long as it is the lower orifice, it is in every case a grave offense."

Chinese Source

資持釋云『二列諸境中三、初列境相又三、一以趣攝。註云等者即修羅地獄、四合為一。二約報有三、該上六趣。三約處亦三、須通三報。此下二定犯分齊。文列四相、二生二死、相別可見。如毛頭者斷犯處也。

律下三明不開疑想。律云、若道、作道想若疑若非道想、並上罪。文舉畜趣、餘趣同然。』

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The second part, listing the various objects, has three points. First, listing the marks of the object, itself in three parts: first, subsuming by destiny — the note's 'and the like' refers to the asuras and to hell-beings, the four being combined as one. Second, in terms of physical form there are three cases, covering all six destinies above. Third, in terms of location there are likewise three cases, which must be understood across all three kinds of retribution-body. What follows fixes the exact boundary of what completes the offense: the text lists four cases — two while living, two after death — and each is clearly distinguishable. 'As little as the width of a hair' marks the precise cutoff for completing the offense.

"The third point, beginning with 'the Vinaya,' makes clear that no allowance is made for a mistaken or doubtful perception. The Vinaya says: whether it is truly the [wrong] orifice, or one perceives it as the [wrong] orifice, or one is merely in doubt, or one perceives it as not the [wrong] orifice — in every case it is the upper-grade offense. The text cites the case of animals, but the other destinies are exactly the same."

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide reduces this technical definition to its practical bottom line: the object of this offense is drawn as widely as possible — spanning humans, non-humans, and animals alike, and covering three orifices for a woman and two for a man — with no allowance made for whether the partner is conscious, asleep, freshly dead, or only slightly decomposed. The guide stresses the "width of a hair" standard in particular: it is not a figure of speech for near-total penetration but a literal minimum, meaning the upper-grade offense is complete at the very first instant of contact rather than only once an act is carried through to its end. This fits a pattern the guide traces across the whole gate — the tradition cares as much about locating the exact threshold of an offense as about condemning the offense itself, because a precise threshold is what a practitioner actually needs when examining their own conduct honestly.

4. Milestone: Against Mockery, and Why the Vinaya Speaks So Plainly

Two further passages anticipate an objection any reader of this material might raise: why does a monastic code speak this explicitly about something so indecent? The Procedural Commentary first names four faults in the reader's own reaction — distaste, mockery, doubt, and disbelief — and then quotes the Sudarśana Commentary's own defense of Master Upāli, who compiled this material, against exactly that reaction.

Chinese Source

△事鈔續云『然婬過粗現、人並知非。及論問犯、犯皆結正。約相示過。耳不欲聞、或致輕笑、生疑、生怪。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Continuation] The Procedural Commentary continues: "Now, the fault of sexual misconduct is coarse and plainly evident, and everyone already knows it is wrong. Yet when it comes to examining an actual case of violation, the violation must still be established as such in every particular. Setting out the marks of the offense in order to show its fault, the ear may be reluctant to hear it, and this can provoke a scornful laugh, or give rise to doubt, or give rise to bewilderment."

Chinese Source

資持釋云『三誡輕二、初敘情見、上二句縱其所知。及下示其制重。問犯謂究問教法也。約下述其愚暗。此有四過。一生厭惡、不欲聞故。二無尊重、生輕笑故。三無深信、疑非佛說故。四不正見、怪作是說故。』

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The third point, warning against taking this lightly, has two parts. First, it lays out the ordinary reaction: the first two lines concede what the reader already knows. What follows shows why the prohibition must nonetheless be so exacting. 'Examining a violation' means investigating according to the teaching's own method. What follows that describes the reader's own dim-wittedness — there are four faults here. First, a feeling of disgust arises, because one does not wish to hear it. Second, a lack of due respect arises, because it provokes a scornful laugh. Third, a lack of deep faith arises, because one doubts this was really spoken by the Buddha. Fourth, an incorrect view arises, because one finds it strange that such things would even be said."

Chinese Source

△事鈔續云『故善見云。法師曰。此不淨法語、諸聞說者勿驚怪。生慚愧心。志心於佛。何以故。如來慈愍我等。佛是世間王、離於愛染得清淨處、為愍我等、說此惡言、為結戒故。又觀如來功德、便無嫌心。若佛不說此事、我等云何得知上罪。有笑者驅出。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Continuation] The Procedural Commentary continues: "Hence the Sudarśana Commentary says: the Dharma Master [Upāli] said, 'As for this language describing an impure matter — let none of you who hear it spoken be startled or find it strange. Instead, give rise to a mind of shame, and fix your intention wholly on the Buddha. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata, out of compassion for us, [spoke it]. The Buddha is the king of the world; he has left desire and defilement behind and attained a place of purity — and it was out of compassion for us that he spoke this coarse language, in order to establish the precept. Reflect further on the Tathāgata's own merit, and no resentment will remain. If the Buddha had not spoken of this matter, how could we ourselves ever come to know an upper-grade offense?' Anyone who laughed was driven out."

Chinese Source

資持釋云『二引誡。法師即論主、將集此戒故先約勒。文中三、初遮眾情。生下教觀察。生慚愧者克己自責也。世間愚人、誰能反照。身行鄙穢殊不省非、及聞教說反生驚怪。汝必惡聞、何如不作。汝既自作、何得惡聞。此由不知於大慈門、說毗尼藏、全是指出眾生惡業。若能知業、豈復有教。嗚呼凡愚、迷倒至此。志下令觀勝境。初觀佛慈、又下次觀佛德、若下後觀佛教。有笑下明立制。』已上皆見事鈔記卷十七

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "Second, citing this admonition. 'The Dharma Master' is the author of that treatise, who, being about to compile this precept, first lays down this constraint. The passage has three parts: first, it forestalls the assembly's ordinary reaction. What follows, beginning with 'give rise to,' teaches self-examination. 'Giving rise to a mind of shame' means holding oneself to account. Among the foolish people of this world, who is actually capable of turning the light of attention back on himself? One's own body commits filthy, unclean acts without the least recognition of the wrong in it — yet on hearing the teaching state this plainly, one instead grows startled and finds it strange. If you truly hate to hear of it, why not simply not do it? And since you yourself in fact do it, how can you then hate to hear it spoken of? This is because one does not understand that, out of the gate of great compassion, expounding the Vinaya-piṭaka is entirely a matter of pointing out sentient beings' own unwholesome karma. If one could already fully know one's own karma, what further need would there be for any teaching at all? Alas — how far the ordinary fool's confusion and inversion can go. What follows 'fix your intention,' beginning there, directs contemplation toward the superior object: first contemplating the Buddha's compassion, then, beginning with 'reflect further,' contemplating the Buddha's merit, then, beginning with 'if,' contemplating the Buddha's own teaching. What follows 'anyone who laughed' makes clear how the rule was established." [The preceding passages are all found in the Zichi Commentary, fascicle 17.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide doesn't quote this exchange directly, but it fits a pattern the guide traces across the whole gate: South Mountain vinaya's commentators are keenly aware that laying out sexual conduct in this much explicit detail can itself provoke discomfort, and they treat that discomfort as something to examine rather than indulge. The four faults named here — distaste, mockery, doubt, disbelief — read less as a scolding of the reader than as a diagnostic: a practitioner's own resistance to hearing the material stated plainly is itself worth noticing, since squeamishness about naming an act honestly is a poor guide to whether the act itself has actually been avoided. Master Upāli's defense — that the Buddha spoke this way purely out of compassion, so that violations could actually be recognized and named — sets the tone for how the rest of the section should be read: not as prurience, but as the precision a workable ethical standard requires.

5. Milestone: Four Conditions for a Self-Initiated Violation

The compendium now turns from the object of the offense to the conditions that actually constitute a violation — and immediately splits the analysis in two, because the Vinaya treats a person who initiates the act quite differently from one who is coerced into it. This milestone gives the first of the two: the four conditions for an offense one brings about oneself.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『次成犯相有二緣。一自有婬心向前境、縱有裏隔互障、但入如毛頭、結成大重。具四緣成。一是正境、男則二道女則三處二興染心、謂非餘睡眠等三起方便、四與境合便犯。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Fresh citation] The Procedural Commentary says: "Next, the conditions that complete the mark of the offense fall into two sets. The first: one who, of his own accord, holds a lustful mind toward the object before him — even if some barrier or covering intervenes, so long as penetration reaches as little as the width of a hair, the grave upper-grade offense is completed. This is constituted by four conditions: (1) a valid object — for a male, either of the two orifices; for a female, any of the three; (2) defiled intent arises — meaning something other than, say, being asleep; (3) the means is undertaken; (4) union with the object occurs, at which point the offense is committed."

Chinese Source

資持釋云『以自造他逼、犯相有異、故列兩緣。前約趣境論緣、由本有心、故就身明犯。後約境合辨緣、既開身交、故就心明犯。初自造。第二註云等者者即不犯中一切無婬意、癡狂心亂等。』

行宗云『自造緣四種、不出心境。前二離明。後二合辨。三即心為境發、四謂境與心會。』見戒疏記卷五

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "Because a self-initiated offense and a coerced one have different marks, two separate sets of conditions are given. The first set discusses the conditions in terms of approaching the object, since intent is already present from the start, and so the offense is defined in terms of the body. The second set discusses the conditions in terms of union with the object, since bodily contact has already been allowed for, and so the offense is defined in terms of the mind. This first set is the self-initiated case. As for the note's 'and the like' in the second condition — this refers to what falls under 'not an offense' below: cases with no lustful intent whatsoever, such as insanity or a deranged mind."

The Xingzong Commentary says: "The four conditions for a self-initiated offense come down to nothing but mind and object. The first two are treated separately; the last two are treated together. The third is where the mind is aroused by the object; the fourth is where the object and the mind actually meet." [Xingzong Commentary, fascicle 5]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide sets this list of four conditions side by side with its coerced counterpart in a single comparison table, and the contrast between the two is really the point. Because intent is already present at the outset in the self-initiated case, the offense is defined here by what the body actually does: a valid object, defiled intent arising, an undertaken means, and union with the object. The guide flags the fourth condition as unusually strict in practice — since the standard for "union" is set at the width of a hair, and the rule applies "even if some barrier or covering intervenes," there is very little room left for a practitioner to argue that an act fell short of a full violation on some technicality.

6. Milestone: Four Conditions Under Coercion

The second set of four conditions covers the opposite case — a person seized or assaulted by another, where the Vinaya's concern shifts entirely from bodily contact, which is excused outright, to the single remaining question of whether pleasure was experienced.

Chinese Source

△事鈔續云『二者若為怨逼、或將至前境或就其身、佛開身會、制令不染。亦具四緣。一是正境、不問自他二為怨逼、三與境合、四受樂便犯。善見云。婬不受樂者、如以男根內毒蛇口中及以火中、是不染之之相。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Continuation] The Procedural Commentary continues: "Second: if one is coerced by an assailant — whether dragged to the object or the object brought to one's own body — the Buddha permits the bodily union itself, but requires that the mind not become defiled by it. This too is constituted by four conditions: (1) a valid object, regardless of whether the aggressor is oneself or the other party; (2) coercion by an assailant; (3) union with the object occurs; (4) experiencing pleasure constitutes the offense. The Sudarśana Commentary says: 'Not experiencing pleasure in the sexual act is like inserting one's own member into the mouth of a venomous snake, or into fire — that is the mark of remaining undefiled.'"

Chinese Source

資持釋云『二怨逼中、初示相有二。一逼己婬他、二為他婬己。開身免難、制心護體。遭婬尚易、持往尤難。自非久習淨心、直恐未逃刑網。善下示護心法。本謂女根故生染著、今為蛇火乃生厭懼。以心隨境轉、其必然乎。』已上皆見事鈔記卷十七

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "Second, on the case of coercion. First, it shows that this mark has two forms: one, being forced to violate another; two, being made the object of another's violation. Permitting the body to avoid harm while still requiring the mind to guard its own integrity — enduring the act itself is comparatively easy, but holding the mind steady throughout is far harder. Unless one has long cultivated a pure mind, one may well fail to escape the net of this offense. What follows, beginning with 'the Sudarśana Commentary,' shows a method for guarding the mind: what ordinarily gives rise to attachment is precisely the woman's organ — yet by treating it instead as a snake or as fire, aversion and dread arise instead. Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

Where the previous section covered a violation someone brings about themselves, this one covers the opposite case, and the guide's comparison table makes the shift explicit: once coercion is established, bodily contact itself is no longer the issue — the Buddha excuses it outright — and the entire question becomes whether pleasure was experienced. The guide highlights the Sudarśana Commentary's stark image for how to hold the mind in that moment, comparing the assailant's approach to one's own body being forced into a venomous snake's mouth or into fire. In the guide's reading that comparison is doing real ethical work: it reframes an experience the victim has no power to prevent as one where responsibility narrows down to a single, genuinely controllable question of inner attitude, rather than extending blame to contact that was never chosen.

On teaching another to commit the act, the guide draws out what it treats as one of the section's most important structural principles: instigating sexual misconduct or false speech is judged more lightly than instigating killing or theft, because the benefit — pleasure, or fame and profit — accrues to whoever actually carries out the act, whereas killing and stealing harm both the person acting and the person acted upon at once, so instigator and instigated are held equally liable.

The companion guide's own severity table places false speech in sharp contrast with the three offenses already covered: where killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct each admit of upper-, middle-, and lower-grade offenses along a single continuous scale, false speech splits instead into one grave offense — a false claim of spiritual attainment — set apart from an entire cluster of lesser faults: the lesser falsehood, harsh speech, slander, and idle chatter, all four of which are graded no higher than the lower grade. The guide reads this asymmetry as the section's real organizing principle: ordinary lying, however common and however habitual, is treated as categorically different from claiming a sanctity one does not possess, precisely because the latter corrupts other people's trust in the entire teaching, not merely in the speaker.

The guide notes that Master Hongyi's editorial restraint here — declining to redraw the full lattice of transferred perception and doubt a second time — is itself instructive: once the tiered framework has been demonstrated exhaustively for one precept, the tradition trusts the reader to carry it forward rather than repeating dozens of near-identical cases. Both point to the same underlying test that has run through this entire gate: it is the presence or absence of an actual intent to deceive, not the objective truth or falsity of what was said, that ultimately determines guilt.

[The preceding passages are all found in the Procedural Commentary, fascicle 22.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide highlights the Zichi Commentary's psychological observation here as one of the most striking passages in the whole section: false speech is treated as the offense practitioners violate most habitually, precisely because the underlying tendency has been "perfumed" into the mind through long repetition, so that fabrication arises almost automatically whenever circumstances present an opening. Its more technical point is equally important — the guide's own summary states it plainly: "犯無定境、起必依心、但使違內想心、不論外緣虛實、一切皆犯" (the offense is judged purely by whether speech contradicts one's own actual perception, not by whether the underlying fact happens to be objectively true). This means a technically true statement, spoken to convey a false impression the speaker themselves does not actually hold, is still false speech in the tradition's own terms — sincerity toward one's own mind, not correspondence to outward fact, is what the precept actually protects.

20. Milestone: Harsh Speech

The second of the three lesser faults is harsh speech — words spoken to wound. The compendium opens with a striking causal claim about how such speech shapes the very landscape a community inhabits, then tells a vivid animal fable before listing the circumstances, chiefly involving teachers and close friends, under which blunt or unflattering speech is exempted.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『智論。一人生國中、皆共作因緣。謂內法與外法為因緣。如惡口業故、地生荊棘等。不作上諸惡者、地則平正。如彌勒佛時、人行十善、地多珍寶。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Fresh citation] The Procedural Commentary says: "The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says: when a person is born within a given land, they and that land together form a single causal nexus — meaning that inward karma and outward circumstance act as cause and condition for one another. Thus, on account of the karma of harsh speech, the very earth itself brings forth thorns and brambles. Where none of the evils described above are committed, the earth instead lies smooth and level — as in the age of Maitreya Buddha, when people practice the ten wholesome deeds and the earth abounds with treasures."

資持釋云『內法是業即善不善、外法是報即所依器界、報由業感故云與也。引此文者、意彰惡口過重、感報不淨。令自勉也。』

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "'Inward karma' is the deed itself, wholesome or unwholesome; 'outward circumstance' is the retribution, namely the material world one comes to depend on, since retribution is called forth by karma — hence the text says the two act 'for one another.' Following the ox's own advice, the owner won the contest this time. Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide reads the ox fable as more than a colorful aside: it is offered as a concrete demonstration that harsh words carry a genuine causal force even when addressed to an animal, and that this force is entirely reversible — the same ox that quit under public mockery went on to win when the very same owner praised it instead. On the exemptions, the guide's own summary distills the Zichi Commentary's key warning into a single line worth sitting with: "然瞋心難狀、非智莫曉，彌須審悉、不可自欺" (an angry mind is genuinely hard to detect, and only real wisdom can discern it — one must examine oneself all the more closely, and cannot simply take one's own innocence for granted). In the guide's reading, this is the whole section's real caution: a teacher, parent, or close friend can always claim their harsh words were spoken purely out of concern, but the exemption only actually applies where that claim is true, and self-deception on this exact point is, in the tradition's own estimation, disturbingly easy.

21. Milestone: Slander and Idle Chatter

The third and fourth lesser faults close out the treatment of false speech: slander, defined as speech meant to break two parties apart, and idle chatter, defined by the Tattvasiddhi Śāstra's own threefold test for speech that fails even when every word in it happens to be true. With these, the entire first chapter of this gate — the four nature-offenses — comes to its close.

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The companion guide is candid that the Chinese rendering "兩舌" (literally "two tongues") is a somewhat awkward calque, and sides with the Commentary on the Precept-Text's own preference for "divisive speech" as the clearer sense — a preference this translation follows throughout. On idle chatter, the guide draws out the Tattvasiddhi Śāstra's threefold test as its most useful takeaway: speech can be entirely factual and still be idle chatter, if it is spoken at the wrong moment, spoken only to unsettle or wound without benefit, or spoken with no real grounding even where it happens to help. The guide reads this as a genuinely high bar — truthfulness alone was never sufficient in this tradition's own accounting of right speech, which asks equally about timing, effect, and whether the speaker actually has standing to say what they are saying.

22. Milestone: The Five Proscribed Offenses, and Why Drinking Heads the List

Chapter Two turns from the four nature-offenses — wrongs that would be evil in themselves even without any precept forbidding them — to a different class of rule altogether: the five proscribed offenses, forbidden not because they are inherently evil but because the Buddha judged them harmful to practice and to the community's standing in the world. All five are graded no higher than the lower offense. The first and most extensively treated is the prohibition on alcohol, which the Vinaya introduces with an unusually severe warning before listing its ten distinct faults.

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『律云。若以我為師者。乃至不得以草木內酒、滴口。因說酒有十過。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Fresh citation] The Procedural Commentary says: "The Vinaya says: 'Whoever takes me as their teacher' — down to 'must not so much as dip a blade of grass or a twig in wine and touch it to the mouth.' The text then goes on to explain the ten faults of alcohol."

Chinese Source

資持釋云『初示嚴敕。以為資者必稟師教、不可違故。乃下明急制也。今時學法、率多嗜酒、臨此慈訓、那不自慚。因下顯過。一顏色惡、二少力、三眼視不明、此三及六損色四現瞋恚相、此一與九亂神五壞業資生、破家六增疾病、七益鬥訟、長業八無名稱、喪德九智慧少、十命終墮三惡道。上九現惡十即來苦觀斯十過、現事灼然。世愚反云益力治病者、不亦謬哉。』見事鈔記卷二十四

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "The opening shows how solemn this injunction is: whoever relies on the Buddha as their teacher must receive and follow the teacher's own instruction, and cannot go against it. What follows, beginning with 'must not,' makes clear how strict and urgent the prohibition is. People studying the Dharma nowadays are, for the most part, all too fond of drink — confronted with this compassionate instruction, how could they not feel some shame? What follows, beginning with 'the text then,' sets out the faults themselves: (1) an unsightly complexion; (2) diminished strength; (3) dimmed eyesight — these three, together with the sixth, harm one's physical appearance; (4) a display of anger and ill temper — this one, together with the ninth, disturbs the spirit; (5) ruin of one's livelihood and undertakings, and the destruction of one's household; (6) an increase in illness; (7) a growing tendency toward quarrels and litigation, which further compounds one's karma; (8) loss of reputation and destruction of one's virtue; (9) diminished wisdom; (10) after death, rebirth in one of the three lower destinies. The first nine of these are evils manifest already in this present life; the tenth is the suffering retribution yet to come. Contemplating these ten faults, the plain facts are self-evident — how mistaken, then, are the foolish people of this world who instead claim that drink builds strength and cures illness!" [Procedural Commentary, fascicle 24.]

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide's own overview frames the shift from Chapter One to Chapter Two as the single most important structural distinction in the whole gate: nature-offenses would remain wrong even in a world with no Buddhist teaching at all, while proscribed offenses like drinking are wrong specifically because the Buddha forbade them, for reasons of practical harm and the community's public standing rather than any evil intrinsic to the act itself. The guide reproduces the ten faults essentially unchanged, and highlights the commentary's closing rebuke of the folk belief that alcohol "builds strength and cures illness" as a live issue even now — the text's own confidence that "觀斯十過、現事灼然" (contemplating these ten faults, the plain facts are self-evident) is offered not as blind deference to authority but as an invitation to verify the claim against ordinary observation.

23. Milestone: Drinking — Conditions, Rulings, and Exemptions

Having explained why alcohol is forbidden, the compendium turns to the mechanics of the offense itself: the three conditions that constitute a violation, a wide-ranging ruling on exactly what counts as "wine" — extending even to alcohol-preserved foods unknown in ancient India — and finally the single, narrowly bounded exemption for medicinal use.

▲戒疏云『三緣一是酒、二無重病緣、三飲咽便犯。』

The guide's own summary of this material is unusually concrete: anything possessing wine's color, aroma, and taste counts as wine regardless of how it was actually produced, so that sweet-and-sour wine, malted grain used for brewing, and wine-lees are all violations in their own right, not merely borderline cases. On the medicinal exemption, the guide is equally blunt: illness alone is not sufficient grounds for drinking; every other treatment must first have genuinely failed, a much higher bar than casual excuse-making usually assumes.

24. Milestone: Eating After Noon — Real Time and Nominal Time

The second proscribed offense, eating after noon, opens not with a simple rule but with an extended philosophical excursus borrowed from the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom, addressing an objection any careful reader might raise: if ultimate reality itself admits no fixed "time," why does the Buddha legislate around a specific clock at all?

Chinese Source

▲事鈔云『智論。問曰。若法無時。云何聽時食、遮非時食為戒。答曰。我已說世界名字法有非實、汝不應難。亦是毗尼中結戒法、是世界中實有、為眾人訶責故。亦欲護佛法使久存、定佛弟子禮法故。佛世尊結諸戒、不應求有何名字相應不相應等。』

Ru-Yi Translation

[Fresh citation] The Procedural Commentary says: "The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom raises a question: 'If ultimate reality itself admits of no fixed time, then why does the Buddha permit eating at the proper time and prohibit eating at an improper time, and establish this as a precept?' It answers: 'I have already explained that worldly, conventional designations have no ultimate reality of their own, and so you ought not to press this objection. This too belongs to the class of precepts established within the Vinaya: the distinction between proper and improper time genuinely exists within the conventional world, and this rule was established on account of the criticism it draws from ordinary people. It is also meant to protect the Buddha's own teaching, so that it may endure long in the world, and to fix a settled etiquette proper to the Buddha's own disciples. When the World-Honored One established the various precepts, one ought not to go searching for whether this or that name and its designated referent do or do not, in the abstract, actually correspond.'"

Ru-Yi Translation

The Zichi Commentary explains: "This passage belongs to the Treatise's exposition of the six factors of accomplishment, specifically the factor of time. There it explains that India recognizes two kinds of time: the first is called kāla, rendered here as 'real time' — meaning years, months, days, hours, the four seasons, and the solar terms, all of which the ordinary world reckons to be objectively real. The second is called samaya, rendered here as 'nominal time' — meaning whatever varies in duration according to the particular matter and circumstance at hand, having no fixed reality of its own. The Buddha, following conventional truth, speaks of samaya rather than kāla, precisely in order to remove the wrong views of non-Buddhist ascetics and ordinary laypeople alike: laypeople cling to real existence, while non-Buddhist ascetics posit permanence, and were the Buddha to speak of 'real time' as such, it would only reinforce these very views. This, too, is spoken broadly in general terms, not meant to exclude every mention of real time whatsoever. The treatise first refutes the notion that 'real time,' as ordinarily conceived, has any ultimate reality at all. And yet the Vinaya's own rules of conduct mostly rely on real time in just this ordinary sense — which shows that the Tathāgata does, after all, also speak in terms of real time. How, then, could it be said there is no time at all? Hence the question is raised, exactly as the Procedural Commentary cites it here. The answer comes in two parts: the first explains the underlying principle, resolving the apparent tension; the second, beginning with 'the Buddha,' forestalls the further objection. The first part itself has three lines of reasoning. The first explains the matter in terms of nominal designation: having refuted 'real time' in the ordinary sense, the treatise then says that the arising and perishing of the aggregates, sense-fields, and sense-bases is itself what is nominally designated 'time,' with nothing further beyond this — meaning that even where certain sūtras speak of 'real time,' this too is simply a designation adopted in accordance with worldly convention, and hence called 'nominal,' though this technical sense of 'nominal' differs from the word's ordinary worldly meaning. Further, according to what the Buddha himself actually teaches, both kāla and samaya are, at bottom, nominal designations; it is simply called 'real' insofar as it follows conventional usage. Where the text says 'I have already explained,' it points back to the passage above: because worldly, conventional things genuinely exist as such, there is no harm in speaking of 'proper time' and 'improper time'; and because they lack ultimate reality, there is equally no harm in saying that, in the final analysis, there is no time at all. Since the argument has already been made plain, and the objector presses the same question again without having examined it closely, the treatise turns the objection back on the questioner, saying, 'you ought not to press this.' What follows, beginning with 'this too,' explains the matter a second way, in terms of conventional worldly usage: 'genuinely exists within the conventional world' refers to what ordinary attachment takes to be real; 'the criticism it draws from ordinary people' refers directly to the actual circumstance that gave rise to the rule, since it was precisely people's attachment to this as something real that provoked criticism and mockery, and the Vinaya accordingly attaches itself to worldly convention in order to forestall exactly this kind of criticism. What follows, beginning with 'it is also meant,' explains the matter a third way, in terms of protecting the Dharma: meaning that fixing the time for taking the precepts into upper, middle, and lower periods allows practitioners to show mutual respect for one another and causes the teaching to endure without being lost — hence the text speaks of making it 'endure long' and so on. The first line of reasoning explains the matter from the standpoint of nominal designation, tracing it back to the ultimate path; the latter two explain it from the standpoint of the real, tracing it forward into worldly convention. As for forestalling the objection that follows: because the Vinaya's own rules of conduct are established provisionally, in accordance with conventional truth, one cannot simply take ultimate truth and use it to challenge a matter of worldly convention — the teaching's own transformative purpose is precisely to establish rules suited to dissolving worldly attachment, and hence the text says one 'ought not to go searching' for this kind of literal correspondence."

Reading note — after the Ru-Yi Study Group companion guide

The guide condenses this dense Abhidharma excursus into a single practical point worth keeping in view through the more technical rulings that follow: the Vinaya deliberately speaks of "real time" — the ordinary clock of years, months, and hours — rather than the philosophically more sophisticated "nominal time" found in general teaching, precisely because laypeople and outsiders needed a rule they could actually observe and be held to, not a metaphysical subtlety. The guide singles out the final rebuke of "those who falsely claim to study the Great Vehicle" as directly relevant to any tradition capable of citing high doctrine to excuse itself from disciplined observance — the text's answer is not to deny that ultimate reality transcends time, but to insist that this very fact is no license for casual indulgence.

With the underlying rationale established, the compendium turns to the offense's own four conditions, an unusually strict definition of exactly where noon falls, and a short list of exemptions — foods whose composition places them outside the ordinary category of "mealtime food" altogether.

The guide flags the noon deadline as one of the most operationally strict rules anywhere in the compendium: even the exact instant of true noon itself already counts as a violation if food is taken then, and passing the deadline by "so much as the blink of an eye or the breadth of a single hair" is enough to complete the offense outright — a meal must be fully received before noon, with no margin whatsoever. The guide is equally precise about the exemptions, since they turn entirely on the composition and preparation of the food rather than on the eater's intention: hardened rock-sugar candy escapes the category of "mealtime food" by its own nature, and strained liquid from beans, grains, or barley is permitted for the ill only so long as the husk has not split open during boiling — the moment it does, the resulting liquid is reclassified as genuine food and the exemption no longer applies.

The guide notes a small but telling detail in the bed-height ruling: the eight-finger-width limit is measured by the Buddha's own finger, while the narrow exemption for a propping block on damp ground is measured by an ordinary person's finger instead — two different rulers used deliberately, so that the exempted allowance can never accidentally be stretched up to the Buddha's own more generous standard. The guide reads all five of these final rules together as sharing one underlying logic distinct from the four nature-offenses covered earlier in this gate: none of them is inherently evil the way killing or lying is, and every one of them targets a specific, ordinarily innocent comfort or pleasure — a drink, a late meal, a soft bed, an attractive appearance, a pleasant performance — precisely because, in the tradition's own accounting, it is exactly these small, repeatable comforts, far more than any single dramatic transgression, that quietly erode a day of genuine renunciation from the inside.
