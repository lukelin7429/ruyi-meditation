Opening, and Giving Without Dwelling

法會因由分第一 至 妙行無住分第四

An ordinary morning at Jetavana, Subhūti's opening question, the bodhisattva's vow to liberate every being, and the sūtra's first concrete instruction: give without dwelling on anything at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T235 · Kumārajīva's translation, c. 402 CE Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's 金剛般若波羅蜜經講錄 (1927)

1. The Occasion

Chinese Source · 法會因由分第一

如是我聞。一時，佛在舍衛國祇樹給孤獨園，與大比丘眾千二百五十人俱。爾時，世尊食時，著衣持缽，入舍衛大城乞食。於其城中，次第乞已，還至本處。飯食訖，收衣缽，洗足已，敷座而坐。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard. At one time the Buddha was staying at Jetavana, in Anāthapiṇḍada's Park near Śrāvastī, together with a great assembly of monks, twelve hundred and fifty in all. When it came time to eat, the World-Honored One put on his robe, took up his bowl, and went into the great city of Śrāvastī to beg for food. Going from door to door in order, he returned to his own place when he had finished. He ate the food, put away his robe and bowl, washed his feet, and sat down on the seat prepared for him.

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu reads the opening line's six short elements — 如是 (this very teaching), 我聞 (heard directly, by Ānanda), 一時 (a time), 佛 (the teacher), the named place, and the assembly of 1,250 — as the "six certainties" that authenticate every sūtra: what was heard, who heard it, when, by whom it was taught, where, and before whom. Even 我聞 gets a caveat: strictly speaking there is no fixed self doing the hearing, only causes and conditions coming together — "hearing that does not really hear, a hearing with nothing heard" — but the word is kept for everyday, workable speech.

What Taixu draws attention to, though, is what this sūtra's occasion is not. Other Mahāyāna sūtras open with the earth shaking or light streaming from the Buddha's brow. This one opens with him getting dressed, taking his bowl, walking door to door without favoring the rich over the poor, eating, washing his feet, and sitting down. "Ordinary daily conduct is itself the Buddha-dharma," he writes — walking, standing, sitting, lying down, the ordinary mind itself, none of it separate from the teaching. Before Subhūti asks a single question, the sūtra has already shown its answer once, silently, in the shape of an unremarkable morning.

2. Subhūti Asks the Question

Chinese Source · 善現啟請分第二

時，長老須菩提，在大眾中，即從座起，偏袒右肩，右膝著地，合掌恭敬。而白佛言：「希有世尊！如來善護念諸菩薩，善付囑諸菩薩。世尊！善男子、善女人，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，應云何住？云何降伏其心？」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the elder Subhūti rose from his seat in the midst of the assembly, bared his right shoulder, knelt on his right knee, and, joining his palms together in reverence, said to the Buddha: "How rare, World-Honored One! The Tathāgata watches over and cares for all bodhisattvas, and entrusts all bodhisattvas with his teaching. World-Honored One — when a good son or good daughter sets out for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, how should they stand, and how should they subdue their own mind?"

佛言：「善哉，善哉。須菩提！如汝所說：如來善護念諸菩薩，善付囑諸菩薩。汝今諦聽，當為汝說：善男子、善女人，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，應如是住，如是降伏其心。」「唯然，世尊！願樂欲聞。」

The Buddha said: "Well asked, well asked, Subhūti. It is as you say — the Tathāgata watches over and cares for all bodhisattvas, and entrusts all bodhisattvas with his teaching. Listen carefully now; I will tell you. When a good son or good daughter sets out for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, they should stand like this, and subdue their mind like this." "Yes, World-Honored One — we would gladly hear it."

Reading note — after Taixu

Subhūti's own name, Taixu notes, already carries the theme: his family's storehouse was found mysteriously empty the day he was born, so he was named "Born Into Emptiness" — and he grew up to be the disciple most at home in emptiness of all. His question borrows a mother's and a father's two ways of caring for a child: 護念, "watching over," is like a mother who keeps her child in mind — think of the child and it thrives, forget it and it withers; 付囑, "entrusting," is like a father handing over the family estate to a grown son, charging him to carry it well. Both describe how the Buddha holds his bodhisattvas: minding their inner growth, and handing them the outer work.

Taixu's sharpest observation is about the answer the Buddha promises but has not yet given: "abide like this, subdue the mind like this" points forward to what the next two sections will actually say — there is no separate technique for subduing the mind on top of learning how to abide; grasping after one more method to control the mind is itself the disturbance. He compares it to Bodhidharma and his student Huike: Huike begged him, "my mind is not at peace — pacify it for me." Bodhidharma answered, "bring me this mind, and I will pacify it." Huike searched, and said, "I have looked for it, and it cannot be found." "Then," said Bodhidharma, "I have already pacified it for you." The Buddha's promised answer works the same way — not a new mind laid over the old one, but seeing that there was never a fixed thing there to subdue.

3. The True Doctrine of the Great Vehicle

Chinese Source · 大乘正宗分第三

佛告須菩提：「諸菩薩摩訶薩，應如是降伏其心：所有一切眾生之類，若卵生、若胎生、若濕生、若化生；若有色、若無色；若有想、若無想、若非有想非無想，我皆令入無餘涅槃而滅度之。如是滅度無量無數無邊眾生，實無眾生得滅度者。何以故？須菩提！若菩薩有我相、人相、眾生相、壽者相，即非菩薩。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Subhūti: "All bodhisattva-mahāsattvas should subdue their mind like this: of every kind of sentient being there is — born from an egg, born from a womb, born from moisture, born by transformation; possessing form or without form; possessing perception, without perception, or neither possessing nor lacking perception — I will lead every one of them to final nirvāṇa, and there liberate them all. And when I have liberated these countless, immeasurable, boundless beings, in truth no being at all has been liberated. Why is this? Subhūti, if a bodhisattva still holds the notion of a self, a person, a being, or a lifespan, that bodhisattva is no bodhisattva at all."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu walks through the nine categories one by one — egg-born, womb-born, moisture-born, transformation-born; possessing form or formless; possessing perception, without perception, or neither — as the tradition's complete inventory of every possible kind of being in the three realms, leaving nothing outside the bodhisattva's vow. He pauses on the beings "without perception" to tell a cautionary story: a meditator, deep in the highest formless absorption, found the cries of birds and the splashing of fish unbearably distracting and privately resolved that he would one day eat every last one of them. He held that meditative state — the very summit of what samsaric concentration can reach — for tens of thousands of aeons, and still, on emerging, fell back into the round of rebirth: first as a wildcat that ate fish and birds, then into hell. "The three realms are one great prison," Taixu writes; "the world is one great sea of suffering." No depth of concentration alone gets a being out, only wisdom does.

The turn in the passage — liberating countless beings, and yet "in truth no being at all has been liberated" — is where that wisdom gets named directly. Taixu explains that clinging to just one of the four notions pulls in all four at once: call yourself "I," and there is already a self; grant that self a human shape among others, and there is a person; place it among the nine kinds of beings, and there is a being; grant it a lifespan, and there is a lifespan. A bodhisattva who privately thinks "I am the one liberating them" has, in that very thought, reintroduced a fixed self, a fixed other, and every affliction that follows from the split between the two — falling, Taixu says pointedly, short of even the emptiness of self an arhat already sees, let alone the emptiness of both self and things a true bodhisattva must see.

4. Wondrous Practice, Without Dwelling

Chinese Source · 妙行無住分第四

「復次，須菩提！菩薩於法，應無所住，行於布施。所謂不住色布施，不住聲、香、味、觸、法布施。須菩提！菩薩應如是布施，不住於相。何以故？若菩薩不住相布施。其福德不可思量。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, Subhūti, when a bodhisattva practises giving, they should give without dwelling on anything at all — giving without dwelling on form, and without dwelling on sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental object. Subhūti, a bodhisattva should give like this, not dwelling on any mark. Why is this? If a bodhisattva gives without dwelling on marks, their merit cannot be measured."

「須菩提！於意云何？東方虛空，可思量不？」「不也，世尊！」「須菩提！南、西、北方，四維上下虛空，可思量不？」「不也，世尊！」「須菩提！菩薩無住相布施，福德亦復如是不可思量。須菩提！菩薩但應如所教住。」

"Subhūti, what do you think — can the empty space to the east be measured?" "No, World-Honored One." "Subhūti, can the empty space to the south, west, north, above, and below be measured?" "No, World-Honored One." "Subhūti, the merit of a bodhisattva who gives without dwelling on marks is just as unmeasurable as that. Subhūti, a bodhisattva should stand only in the way I have taught."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu takes this section as the Buddha's first concrete answer to Subhūti's question — not a further explanation, but an instruction to actually do something. Giving, he notes, traditionally comes in three kinds: material giving, giving of the teaching, and giving of fearlessness or protection — and because giving heads the list of the six pāramitās, practising it fully is said to already contain the other five within it. The six objects named here — form, sound, smell, taste, touch, mental object — stand in for the six senses and the six kinds of consciousness built on them; to give "without dwelling" on any of the six is simply to give without the mind fastening onto anything at all: not the giver, not the gift, not the receiver, not even the merit accumulating from the act.

His gloss on the space-in-ten-directions comparison is direct: giving that still dwells on a mark nets only the finite, countable rewards of gods and humans — a return that can, in principle, be measured. Giving that dwells on nothing is unmeasurable for the plainest of reasons — it was never gathered up as a possession belonging to anyone in the first place, so there is nothing there to add up. This is, Taixu says, exactly how a bodhisattva "abides": not by fixing the mind on some higher object, but by giving in a way that leaves the mind nowhere to land.
