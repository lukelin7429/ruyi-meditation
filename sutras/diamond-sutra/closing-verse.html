Like a Dream, a Bubble, a Shadow

威儀寂靜分第二十九 至 應化非真分第三十二

Why the Tathāgata comes from nowhere and goes nowhere, one world ground to dust and reassembled, and the six similes of the closing verse — probably the single most chanted four lines in Chinese Buddhism.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T235 · Kumārajīva's translation, c. 402 CE Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's 金剛般若波羅蜜經講錄 (1927) Status: complete

29. Still, in Every Posture

Chinese Source · 威儀寂靜分第二十九

「須菩提！若有人言：如來若來若去，若坐若臥，是人不解我所說義。何以故？如來者，無所從來，亦無所去，故名如來。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, if someone says that the Tathāgata comes, or goes, or sits, or lies down, that person has not understood the meaning of what I teach. Why is this? 'Tathāgata' means one who comes from nowhere and goes nowhere — that is why he is called the Tathāgata, the Thus-Come One."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu reads this brief section as answering a worry the previous one leaves hanging: if a bodhisattva does not even receive the merit they create, how can the Buddha still be said to sit on the seat of awakening, walk his alms round, or eventually enter final nirvāṇa — all ordinary comings and goings? His answer draws the same line drawn back in Section 20, between the dharma-body and the bodies that manifest for beings' sake. The dharma-body has always been present, everywhere, needing no arrival and permitting no departure; walking, sitting, and lying down belong entirely to the response-body, taken up only as a skillful means for teaching beings who need a visible teacher in front of them.

To take those ordinary postures as the whole of what the Tathāgata is, Taixu says, is to mistake the convenient vehicle for the passenger it carries. "Coming from nowhere, going nowhere" is not a riddle about physics; it is the plainest possible description of something that was never bound by arrival or departure in the first place — which is exactly what the word "Tathāgata," the Thus-Come One, is built to name.

30. The Principle of the Single Whole

Chinese Source · 一合理相分第三十

「須菩提！若善男子、善女人，以三千大千世界碎為微塵，於意云何？是微塵眾寧為多不？」須菩提言「甚多，世尊！何以故？若是微塵眾實有者，佛則不說是微塵眾。所以者何？佛說微塵眾，則非微塵眾，是名微塵眾。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, if a good son or good daughter ground a billion-world universe into fine dust — what do you think, would that mass of dust be great?" Subhūti said: "Very great, World-Honored One. Why is this? If that mass of dust truly existed as such, the Buddha would not call it a mass of dust. And why is that? What the Buddha calls a mass of dust is no mass of dust at all — that is why it is called a mass of dust."

「世尊！如來所說三千大千世界，則非世界，是名世界。何以故？若世界實有者，則是一合相。如來說一合相，則非一合相，是名一合相。」「須菩提！一合相者，則是不可說；但凡夫之人貪著其事。」

"World-Honored One, the billion-world universe that the Tathāgata speaks of is no universe at all — that is why it is called a universe. Why is this? If a universe truly existed as such, it would be a single, indivisible whole. The Tathāgata speaks of a single, indivisible whole, yet it is no single whole at all — that is why it is called a single, indivisible whole." "Subhūti, a single, indivisible whole is, in truth, beyond words — it is only ordinary people who crave and cling to it as a thing."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu walks the division through to its end: grind a world down far enough, and the resulting motes can, in principle, be divided again and again until they border on empty space itself — the point past which no further division is even possible. If a mote of dust is finally empty in this way, then a world, being nothing but countless motes gathered together, is equally empty. World and dust, he says, cannot properly be called "one," and cannot properly be called "many" either — the whole is nothing apart from its parts, and the parts have nothing solid to be parts of.

He then extends the same logic outward, from one world to the dharma-body shared by every buddha of the ten directions: it cannot be said that there are many separate dharma-bodies, one per buddha, and it cannot be said there is only a single dharma-body either, as though every buddha's realization were literally the same numerical thing. His image is light meeting light — countless lamps in one room, each casting its own light, each light interpenetrating every other without one ever blocking or diminishing another. Many and one are not different, and many and one do not obstruct each other. The section's closing line is a caution as much as a conclusion: it is only ordinary, unawakened craving that insists on forcing a "one" or a "many" onto something that was never built to hold that question.

31. Knowing and Seeing, Without Grasping

Chinese Source · 知見不生分第三十一

「須菩提！若人言：佛說我見、人見、眾生見、壽者見。須菩提！於意云何？是人解我所說義不？」「不也，世尊！是人不解如來所說義。何以故？世尊說我見、人見、眾生見、壽者見，即非我見、人見、眾生見、壽者見，是名我見、人見、眾生見、壽者見。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, if someone says that the Buddha teaches the view of a self, the view of a person, the view of a being, the view of a lifespan — Subhūti, what do you think, has that person understood the meaning of what I teach?" "No, World-Honored One, that person has not understood what the Tathāgata teaches. Why is this? What the World-Honored One calls the view of a self, a person, a being, a lifespan, are no such views at all — that is why they are called the view of a self, a person, a being, a lifespan."

「須菩提！發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心者，於一切法，應如是知，如是見，如是信解，不生法相。須菩提！所言法相者，如來說即非法相，是名法相。」

"Subhūti, one who sets out for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening should, with regard to all things, know them, see them, and believe and understand them exactly like this — without giving rise to any notion of a thing. Subhūti, what is called the notion of a thing, the Tathāgata says, is no notion of a thing at all — that is why it is called the notion of a thing."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu ties craving directly back to the view of a self: ordinary beings cling to things because there is, for them, an "I" they take to be doing the clinging; take that view away, and there is no subject left standing to crave anything, nor any object solid enough to be worth craving — the whole apparatus, he says, is the mind's own projection, as insubstantial as a flower reflected in a mirror or the moon reflected on water. But he catches a possible confusion here: does the Buddha's own repeated talk of "the view of a self" mean such views genuinely exist as fixed things after all? No — naming them this precisely is what proves they are empty, not what proves they are real. To speak the words is not to grant the views a fixed nature; it is only to use ordinary language to point at their absence.

The section's final move applies this same discipline to the six senses, six objects, and six kinds of consciousness all at once: see the entire net of experience as empty in just this way, and do not let even the subtlest "notion of a thing" (法相) arise in its place — because a bare, wordless intuition that "everything is empty" can itself quietly become one more fixed thing to hold onto, no different in kind from any other grasping the sūtra has already dismantled. Taixu's point is exact: the freedom this teaching offers is not exchanging one set of fixed views for another, subtler set, but no longer needing a fixed view to stand on at all.

32. A Manifestation Is Not the Real

Chinese Source · 應化非真分第三十二

「須菩提！若有人以滿無量阿僧祇世界七寶，持用布施；若有善男子、善女人，發菩提心者，持於此經，乃至四句偈等，受持讀誦，為人演說，其福勝彼。云何為人演說，不取於相，如如不動。何以故？」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, if someone filled countless, incalculable universes with the seven treasures and gave it all away; and if a good son or good daughter who has set out for awakening took up even a single four-line verse of this sūtra, upheld it, recited it, and explained it to others — their merit would surpass the first's. And how should they explain it to others? Without grasping at any mark, unmoved, just as things are. Why is this?

一切有為法，如夢幻泡影，如露亦如電，應作如是觀。
All conditioned things are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow; like dew, and like a flash of lightning — see them all like this.
Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu takes each of the six similes in the sūtra's most chanted verse individually, rather than as one blurred image. A dream: vivid and entirely convincing while it lasts, entirely gone the instant one wakes. An illusion: a conjurer's trick — the audience sees objects appear that were never actually there. A bubble: sunlight catches its surface and, for a moment, it gleams like a jewel; reach for it, and there was never a jewel at all. A shadow: it exists only exactly as long as the object casting it exists, and has no substance of its own even then. Dew: it gathers in the clear stillness of early morning and is gone within the hour — never something that was going to last. And lightning: here in one instant, gone in the next, striking one place and then another, neither constant nor evenly spread. Taixu's point in listing all six rather than resting on one image is that no single simile exhausts the point — each catches conditioned existence from a slightly different angle, and together they close off any last place the mind might try to smuggle in a "but this part, at least, must be solid."

He closes his commentary on the entire sūtra with a four-line verse of his own, meant to be carried away the way the sūtra's own closing verse is: "Abiding the mind and subduing the mind; the selflessness of the person and the selflessness of things — this is called prajñā, the very cause and fruit of the Great Vehicle." Everything asked in Section 2 — how should one stand, how should one subdue the mind — and everything answered across thirty-two sections since, comes down, in his own final word, to exactly that.

Question 1 of 12
What does "Diamond" (金剛, vajra) mean in the title of this sūtra?
Correct: C. Vajra's older sense is a thunderbolt or indestructible weapon. The full title, Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā, means "the diamond that cuts through to the Perfection of Wisdom" — a blade applied, calmly and repeatedly, to every place the mind wants to stand still.
Question 2 of 12
Who is the Buddha's dialogue partner throughout almost the entire sūtra?
Correct: B. Subhūti was known in the early tradition as foremost in understanding emptiness, and for a compassion so thorough he practised loving-kindness even while begging for alms — the ideal partner for a text about seeing through fixed notions without losing compassion.
Question 3 of 12
The sūtra constantly repeats a pattern like "merit is not merit, that is why it is called merit." What is this three-beat formula actually doing?
Correct: D. Name it, negate it, rename it. The word is granted ordinary function, denied a solid essence behind the label, then handed back for use — training the reader to use language without being captured by it.
Question 4 of 12
Section 3 says a bodhisattva vows to liberate every being, "and yet in truth no being at all has been liberated." How do these two claims fit together?
Correct: A. The vow stands; what's denied is a solid self on either end of the transaction. If a bodhisattva held onto "I liberated them" as a real, fixed accomplishment, that grasping would recreate the very self-notion the practice is meant to dissolve.
Question 5 of 12
Section 4 teaches that a bodhisattva should give (布施) "without dwelling on anything." What does "dwelling" mean here?
Correct: C. Giving without dwelling means giving without the subtle bookkeeping — I gave, they received, I am now generous — that quietly turns generosity back into a transaction for the self. Its merit is called unmeasurable, like space in every direction, because it was never collected as a possession.
Question 6 of 12
"應無所住而生其心" — "give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere at all" (Section 10) — is traditionally associated with which story?
Correct: B. This is the sūtra's single most famous line in East Asian Buddhism. Taixu recalls that when the Fifth Patriarch first expounded exactly this line to Huineng, Huineng was awakened on the spot and cried out five times, "How could I have known that self-nature is originally pure in itself!"
Question 7 of 12
In Section 14, King Kalī cuts the Buddha's body apart limb by limb, and the Buddha feels no anger. Why not, according to the text?
Correct: D. This is not suppression through discipline — it's the absence of anything the anger would be defending. Taixu adds that in that life as the patient sage, the bodhisattva privately vowed toward his tormentor: "when I attain the path, I will be the first to liberate you."
Question 8 of 12
Section 22: "In unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, I did not attain the least thing whatsoever." What is this saying about the Buddha's enlightenment?
Correct: B. Taixu is blunt about this: if there were even a hair's breadth of something actually gained, that gain itself would disqualify the attainment from being called unsurpassed. It is precisely the absence of anything held onto that lets it be called complete.
Question 9 of 12
The question Subhūti asks in Section 2 — "how should one stand, how should one subdue the mind?" — returns almost word for word in Section 17. What is the effect of this repetition?
Correct: A. The doubled question is a deliberate structural device. Taixu reads Section 17 as the sūtra folding back on its own opening: the vow to liberate every being still stands, but now understood as beings liberating themselves by their own nature.
Question 10 of 12
Section 18 lists five kinds of "eyes" a Tathāgata possesses, from the physical eye up to the buddha eye. What is the point of this list?
Correct: C. Taixu describes each as a widening circle folded into the next — physical, heavenly, wisdom, dharma, and buddha eyes — with the buddha eye alone able to see every realm simultaneously, without gap or excess.
Question 11 of 12
The closing verse (Section 32) — "all conditioned things are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow; like dew, and like a flash of lightning" — is making what point?
Correct: D. Six similes for one point, each catching it from a different angle: a dream real until waking, an illusion with nothing behind it, a bubble that only looked like a jewel, a shadow with no substance of its own, dew gone within the hour, lightning here and then elsewhere.
Question 12 of 12
"This sūtra keeps saying words don't refer to real things — isn't that just skepticism about language?" What is the accurate response?
Correct: B. The three-beat formula always restores the word to use. Words still work perfectly well for teaching, giving, and living once the extra claim — a fixed essence behind the label — is removed.
