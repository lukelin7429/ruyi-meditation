No Fixed Fruit to Hold

一相無相分第九 至 尊重正教分第十二

Why none of the four fruits of the path can be privately claimed as "attained," the line that is said to have awakened the Sixth Patriarch on the spot, and why teaching four lines of this sūtra outweighs a gift too vast to count.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T235 · Kumārajīva's translation, c. 402 CE Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's 金剛般若波羅蜜經講錄 (1927)

9. One Mark Is No Mark

Chinese Source · 一相無相分第九

「須菩提！於意云何？須陀洹能作是念：『我得須陀洹果』不？」須菩提言：「不也，世尊！何以故？須陀洹名為入流，而無所入；不入色、聲、香、味、觸、法，是名須陀洹。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, what do you think — can a stream-enterer have the thought, 'I have attained the fruit of stream-entry'?" Subhūti said: "No, World-Honored One. Why is this? 'Stream-enterer' means one who has entered the stream, yet there is nothing entered — not entering form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental object. That is why they are called a stream-enterer."

「世尊！佛說我得無諍三昧，人中最為第一，是第一離欲阿羅漢。世尊！我不作是念：『我是離欲阿羅漢。』世尊！我若作是念：『我得阿羅漢道。』世尊則不說須菩提是樂阿蘭那行者。以須菩提實無所行，而名須菩提，是樂阿蘭那行。」

"World-Honored One, the Buddha has said that I have attained the state free of contention and am foremost among people — the foremost arhat free of desire. But World-Honored One, I do not have the thought, 'I am an arhat free of desire.' World-Honored One, if I had the thought, 'I have attained the path of arhatship,' the World-Honored One would not say that Subhūti is one who delights in the practice of stillness. It is only because Subhūti in truth practices nothing at all that he is called Subhūti, one who delights in the practice of stillness."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu glosses each of the four fruits by its plain meaning before showing how the sūtra empties it: a stream-enterer has only "entered the stream" of the noble path in name, since there is, in truth, no fixed person who enters and no fixed sense-realm entered; a once-returner is named for "going once to the heavens and returning once to the human world" before their outflows are exhausted, though no such coming-and-going has a solid ground; a non-returner "no longer comes" to be reborn in the realm of desire, though there was never a fixed realm to come to in the first place; an arhat has ended rebirth, slain the thieves of affliction, and become worthy of offerings — yet Taixu stresses "there is in truth no fixed thing called arhat" at all. The moment any of the four privately thinks "I have attained this," that thought alone reintroduces a self to be congratulated — and self, person, being, and lifespan all reappear together.

Subhūti then turns the teaching on himself, in the sūtra's most personal moment so far. The Buddha has publicly named him foremost among arhats in the "concentration of non-contention" (無諍三昧) — the deepest freedom from inner and outer friction — and foremost in delighting in the quiet, secluded practice named 阿蘭那. Yet Subhūti insists he holds no private thought "I am this foremost one." Taixu's summary line for the whole section is exact: every dharma is simply as it is; acting without a fixed actor, abiding without a fixed abode, and attaining without a fixed attainer, is itself the supreme teaching the Tathāgata calls "buddha-dharma, no buddha-dharma." The instant a thought of "I have gotten something" stirs, it falls back into a bounded, dualistic frame — and what was boundless is bounded once again.

10. Adorning the Buddha-Land

Chinese Source · 莊嚴淨土分第十

佛告須菩提：「於意云何？如來昔在燃燈佛所，於法有所得不？」「不也，世尊！如來在燃燈佛所，於法實無所得。」「須菩提！於意云何？菩薩莊嚴佛土不？」「不也，世尊！何以故？莊嚴佛土者，即非莊嚴，是名莊嚴。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha told Subhūti: "What do you think — long ago, in the presence of Dīpaṃkara Buddha, did the Tathāgata attain anything in the teaching?" "No, World-Honored One. In the presence of Dīpaṃkara Buddha, the Tathāgata in truth attained nothing in the teaching." "Subhūti, what do you think — does a bodhisattva adorn a buddha-land?" "No, World-Honored One. Why is this? To adorn a buddha-land is no adorning at all — that is why it is called adorning."

「是故，須菩提！諸菩薩摩訶薩，應如是生清淨心，不應住色生心，不應住聲、香、味、觸、法生心，應無所住而生其心。」「須菩提！譬如有人，身如須彌山王，於意云何？是身為大不？」須菩提言：「甚大，世尊！何以故？佛說非身，是名大身。」

"For this reason, Subhūti, all bodhisattva-mahāsattvas should give rise to a pure mind like this: they should not give rise to a mind dwelling on form, nor give rise to a mind dwelling on sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental object. They should give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere at all." "Subhūti, suppose there were a person whose body was as great as Mount Sumeru, king of mountains — what do you think, would that body be large?" Subhūti said: "Very large, World-Honored One. Why is this? The Buddha speaks of no-body — that is why it is called a great body."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu heads off an obvious worry: elsewhere the scriptures describe the Buddha, in a past life before the buddha Dīpaṃkara, receiving the prediction of his future buddhahood after ages of practice — so did he not attain something there? What Dīpaṃkara spoke and what the future Buddha heard, Taixu answers, were both only language, with no fixed essence behind either; giving and receiving a prediction is itself just more of the "no giver, no gift" pattern already taught. He briefly names the tradition's four kinds of pure land — one shared by ordinary and noble beings together, one for those still using skillful means, one adorned without limit by undefiled wisdom, and the formless "land of dharma-nature" that is simply reality itself — to make the point that "adorning a buddha-land" was never a matter of decorating scenery; a bodhisattva's own nature is originally pure land already, and any visible adornment undertaken to guide others is offered without being clung to as a fixed accomplishment.

The section's most famous line — "give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere at all" — gets Taixu's most personal aside in this fascicle. He recalls that when the Fifth Patriarch, Hongren, first expounded exactly this line to Huineng in the middle of the night, Huineng was awakened on the spot and cried out five times in a row: "How could I have known that self-nature is originally pure in itself! How could I have known that self-nature is originally beyond birth and death! How could I have known that self-nature is originally complete in itself! How could I have known that self-nature is originally beyond all wavering! How could I have known that self-nature can give rise to the ten thousand things!" The closing image, of a body as vast as Mount Sumeru, king of mountains, makes the same point in miniature: the mountain never regards its own height as height — and it is exactly this not-clinging that lets Subhūti call it, and the Buddha's own dharma-body, truly great.

11. The Merit of the Unconditioned Surpasses

Chinese Source · 無為福勝分第十一

「須菩提！如恆河中所有沙數，如是沙等恆河，於意云何？是諸恆河沙，寧為多不？」須菩提言：「甚多，世尊！但諸恆河，尚多無數，何況其沙。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, if there were as many Ganges Rivers as there are grains of sand in the Ganges, what do you think — would the grains of sand in all those Ganges Rivers be many?" Subhūti said: "Very many, World-Honored One. Just the Ganges Rivers themselves would be beyond counting, how much more so their grains of sand."

「須菩提！我今實言告汝：若有善男子、善女人，以七寶滿爾所恆河沙數三千大千世界，以用布施，得福多不？」須菩提言：「甚多，世尊！」佛告須菩提：「若善男子、善女人，於此經中，乃至受持四句偈等，為他人說，而此福德，勝前福德。」

"Subhūti, I tell you truly now: if a good son or good daughter filled as many billion-world universes as there are grains of sand in all those Ganges Rivers with the seven treasures, and gave it all away, would the merit they gained be great?" Subhūti said: "Very great, World-Honored One." The Buddha told Subhūti: "But if a good son or good daughter took up even a single four-line verse of this sūtra, upheld it, and explained it to others, this merit would surpass that merit."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu lets the arithmetic build in stages, the way the text itself does: first, the grains of sand in a single Ganges — already uncountable; then as many Ganges Rivers as there are grains in that one river; then the grains of sand in every one of those rivers combined. The number is not meant to be grasped, only to dwarf the imagination — and then the sūtra asks Subhūti to imagine that same incomprehensible number of entire billion-world universes, each filled edge to edge with the seven treasures and given away. Even that, Taixu says, remains merit with an edge to it, because material giving — however vast — is still finite by nature; it can only ever purchase the bounded, worldly rewards of gods and humans.

Passing on even four lines of this sūtra outweighs it, he explains, because the two kinds of giving aim at entirely different things. Material giving, however immeasurable, ends in the giver and receiver both remaining exactly what they were. Someone who takes this teaching to heart sees their own nature and becomes a buddha; someone who then hears it from them and takes it to heart does the same — and every being brought to that seeing is freed without limit. Merit measured against something boundless is itself boundless, the way merit measured against a finite gift, however large, can never be more than finite.

12. Honoring the True Teaching

Chinese Source · 尊重正教分第十二

「復次，須菩提！隨說是經，乃至四句偈等，當知此處，一切世間，天、人、阿修羅，皆應供養，如佛塔廟。何況有人，盡能受持讀誦。須菩提！當知是人成就最上第一希有之法，若是經典所在之處，即為有佛，若尊重弟子。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, Subhūti, wherever this sūtra is spoken, even a single four-line verse of it, know that this place should be venerated by every being in every world — gods, humans, and asuras alike — as they would venerate a buddha's stūpa or shrine. How much more so where someone can uphold, recite, and fully embody it. Subhūti, know that such a person accomplishes the highest, foremost, rarest teaching. And wherever this sūtra is found, there the Buddha is present, honored among his disciples."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu names the wider assembly the verse only gestures at with "gods, humans, and asuras": the eight kinds of non-human beings said to protect the dharma — gods of the desire, form, and formless realms; nāga water-spirits; yakṣa sky-spirits; gandharva fragrance-eating musicians; asura the "not-quite-gods" whose merit resembles a god's without a god's virtue; garuḍa golden-winged birds who feed on nāgas; kiṃnara the "not-quite-humans" with a single horn; and mahoraga, the great serpent-spirits of the earth. Every one of them, he says, is drawn to venerate wherever even four lines of this teaching are spoken — the same reverence owed to a stūpa housing the Buddha's own relics, or a shrine holding his image.

The reasoning, for Taixu, is simple and direct: this sūtra is what every buddha of the ten directions and three times comes forth from, so to revere the Buddha genuinely is to revere this teaching. Wherever it is held, read, and recited, that place stands exactly as if the Buddha himself were present there, together with his foremost disciples such as Mañjuśrī and Śāriputra — not as a figure of speech, Taixu insists, but because the teaching itself is what makes a buddha a buddha. The sūtra outranks any relic; the person who carries it is, in that carrying, made rare and venerable too.
