Seeing Truly, and Rare Faith

如理實見分第五 至 依法出生分第八

Why the Tathāgata cannot be seen through any mark, why real faith in this teaching is rare even among the devout, the raft that must eventually be let go of, and why this sūtra is called the mother of every buddha.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T235 · Kumārajīva's translation, c. 402 CE Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's 金剛般若波羅蜜經講錄 (1927)

5. Seeing Reality As It Truly Is

Chinese Source · 如理實見分第五

「須菩提。於意云何？可以身相見如來不？」「不也，世尊，不可以身相得見如來。何以故？如來所說身相，即非身相。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, what do you think — can the Tathāgata be seen by his bodily marks?" "No, World-Honored One, the Tathāgata cannot be seen by his bodily marks. Why is this? What the Tathāgata calls bodily marks are no bodily marks at all."

佛告須菩提：「凡所有相，皆是虛妄。若見諸相非相，即見如來。」

The Buddha told Subhūti: "Wherever there is a mark, it is empty and false. See that all marks are no marks, and you see the Tathāgata."

Reading note — after Taixu

The question follows naturally from Section 4, Taixu notes: if a bodhisattva gives without dwelling on any mark at all, how then does a buddha come to possess the thirty-two auspicious marks and eighty fine characteristics that scripture describes so carefully? Subhūti — who by this point has already grasped that every appearance of Buddha, bodhisattva, or being is just an image cast by his own discriminating mind — answers without hesitation: those bodily marks are themselves only the temporary, composite appearance of the four elements and five aggregates come together; they are no fixed "bodily mark" at all.

Taixu presses on what it means to really see this, not merely recite it, with the story of the poet-official Su Dongpo, who once wrote a verse boasting that his mind stood "unmoved by the eight winds" — praise and blame, gain and loss — and sent it across the river to his friend, the Chan master Foyin. Foyin wrote back a single dismissive word. Furious, Su Dongpo rushed across the river to confront him — at which Foyin only smiled: "unmoved by the eight winds, and blown across the river by one word?" Reciting "all marks are empty and false" is easy; seeing it "as principle demands" (如理) is what the section's own title names, and it is a different thing entirely. Only when marks are seen through this way — not merely denied in words — does the Tathāgata's true dharma-body, which no physical mark could ever picture, actually appear.

6. True Faith Is Rare

Chinese Source · 正信希有分第六

須菩提白佛言：「世尊！頗有眾生，得聞如是言說章句，生實信不？」佛告須菩提：「莫作是說。如來滅後，後五百歲，有持戒修福者，於此章句能生信心，以此為實，當知是人不於一佛、二佛、三四五佛而種善根，已於無量千萬佛所種諸善根，聞是章句，乃至一念生淨信者，須菩提！如來悉知悉見，是諸眾生得如是無量福德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Subhūti said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, will there really be beings who, hearing words and passages like these, give rise to true faith?" The Buddha told Subhūti: "Do not speak like that. Five hundred years after the Tathāgata's final passing, there will be those who keep the precepts and cultivate merit, who are able to give rise to faith in these very words, and take them as true. Know that such people have not planted good roots under just one buddha, or two, or three, four, five buddhas — they have planted good roots under countless thousands of buddhas. Whoever hears these words and gives rise to even a single thought of pure faith — Subhūti, the Tathāgata knows them completely, sees them completely, and they obtain immeasurable merit of just this kind.

何以故？是諸眾生無復我相、人相、眾生相、壽者相。」「無法相，亦無非法相。何以故？是諸眾生若心取相，則為著我、人、眾生、壽者。」「若取法相，即著我、人、眾生、壽者。何以故？若取非法相，即著我、人、眾生、壽者。是故不應取法，不應取非法。以是義故，如來常說：汝等比丘，知我說法，如筏喻者，法尚應捨，何況非法。」

Why is this? Because these beings no longer hold the notion of a self, a person, a being, or a lifespan." "They hold no notion of a teaching, and no notion of a non-teaching either. Why is this? If these beings' minds grasp at a mark, they thereby cling to a self, a person, a being, a lifespan." "If they grasp at the mark of a teaching, they thereby cling to a self, a person, a being, a lifespan. Why is this? If they grasp at the mark of a non-teaching, they thereby cling to a self, a person, a being, a lifespan. For this reason you should not grasp at a teaching, and you should not grasp at a non-teaching either. This is why the Tathāgata always says: monks, know that my teaching is like a raft — even the teaching itself should be let go of, how much more so what is not the teaching."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu reads "five hundred years after" against the traditional three ages of the teaching — five hundred years of the true dharma, five hundred of the semblance dharma — placing real faith in this sūtra deep into the harder, later age, when it would seem least likely to arise. That someone could, in that age, hear even a fragment and trust it completely is proof, he says, of good roots planted across countless past buddhas, not just this one lifetime's practice. His example is the Sixth Chan Patriarch, Huineng: an illiterate woodcutter who overheard a single line of this very sūtra recited in a marketplace and felt his own "vajra seed" awaken on the spot. Someone without roots already laid down could hear the Buddha teach this in person and still not truly understand it; someone with those roots can wake from a single overheard phrase, lifetimes later.

The second half turns to the danger on the far side of faith: clinging to the teaching itself as a fixed possession is still clinging. Taixu explains this with the classic raft: a man fleeing bandits builds a raft from wooden planks, crosses the river on it, and — reaching the other shore — leaves the raft behind rather than carrying it on his back. The teaching gets you across; hauling it around afterward as a trophy misses the entire point of having crossed. And "letting go of the raft" doesn't mean holding onto its opposite instead — the sūtra is equally firm that clinging to non-teaching, treating negation itself as the prize, is the same error wearing a different mask. Huineng put the destination plainly: "every teaching flows through, every teaching is complete, and yet not one single teaching is grasped — this is called the supreme vehicle."

7. Nothing Attained, Nothing Taught

Chinese Source · 無得無說分第七

「須菩提！於意云何？如來得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提耶？如來有所說法耶？」須菩提言：「如我解佛所說義，無有定法，名『阿耨多羅三藐三菩提』，亦無有定法，如來可說。何以故？如來所說法。皆不可取、不可說；非法，非非法。所以者何？一切賢聖，皆以無為法而有差別。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, what do you think — has the Tathāgata attained unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening? Has the Tathāgata taught any fixed teaching?" Subhūti said: "As I understand what the Buddha has taught, there is no fixed teaching called 'unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening,' and there is no fixed teaching that the Tathāgata could teach. Why is this? What the Tathāgata teaches can none of it be grasped, and none of it be spoken; it is neither a teaching nor a non-teaching. And why is that? Because all the sages and worthies are distinguished by the unconditioned."

Reading note — after Taixu

If bodily marks cannot show the Tathāgata, Taixu asks, then what of the awakening the Buddha attained beneath the tree, and the teaching he has been giving ever since — are those not fixed, real things? Subhūti's answer holds the line: awakening itself has no shape language could pin down, and neither does anything the Buddha says about it. Taixu's reasoning is precise — if the Tathāgata's teaching could be said and grasped as a fixed object, the listener's mind would have somewhere to land and would keep spinning thought around it; a teaching that lands nowhere is the only kind that actually points at what has no fixed nature to begin with. So the teaching is not a thing (非法) and not simply nothing either (非非法) — it stands clear of both.

The closing line — sages and worthies differ, though all rest in the same unconditioned reality — gets Taixu's simile of three birds escaping one net, or three animals fording one river: a sparrow, a crane, and a great roc all fly free of the same net, but not to the same height; a rabbit, a horse, and an elephant all cross the same river, but the rabbit paddles the surface, the horse wades through the middle, and the elephant's feet reach the riverbed. Stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arhat, the ten bodhisattva grounds, buddhahood — all differ not because they cultivate different truths, but because each goes only as deep into the same single truth as their own crossing allows.

8. Born From This Teaching

Chinese Source · 依法出生分第八

「須菩提！於意云何？若人滿三千大千世界七寶以用布施，是人所得福德，寧為多不？」須菩提言：「甚多，世尊！何以故？是福德即非福德性，是故如來說福德多。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, what do you think — if someone filled a billion-world universe with the seven treasures and gave it all away, would the merit that person gained be great?" Subhūti said: "Very great, World-Honored One. Why is this? Because this merit has no fixed nature as merit — that is exactly why the Tathāgata calls it great merit."

「若復有人，於此經中受持，乃至四句偈等，為他人說，其福勝彼。何以故？須菩提！一切諸佛，及諸佛阿耨多羅三藐三菩提法，皆從此經出。須菩提！所謂佛法者，即非佛法，是名佛法。」

"But if someone else took up even a single four-line verse of this sūtra, upheld it, and explained it to others, their merit would surpass the first person's. Why is this? Subhūti, all buddhas, and the teaching of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening that all buddhas hold, come forth from this very sūtra. Subhūti, what is called the buddha-dharma is no buddha-dharma at all — that is why it is called the buddha-dharma."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu unpacks the scale of the comparison before its point: a "billion-world universe" (三千大千世界) is a small-thousand of world-systems multiplied a thousandfold into a medium-thousand, multiplied a thousandfold again into a great-thousand — a single Sahā world, our entire cosmos, several times over — filled edge to edge with the seven treasures: gold, silver, lapis lazuli, giant clam shell, agate, amber, coral. Even giving away wealth on that scale, he notes, still only plants the finite, measurable merit of a worldly cause, because it is still gathered around a giver, a gift, and a fixed idea of "merit" as a thing possessed.

Taking up even four lines of this sūtra and explaining them to one other person outweighs that entire cosmos of treasure, because — as Taixu puts it plainly — "this sūtra is the mother of all buddhas": every buddha of the ten directions and three times, and the very awakening each of them attained, comes forth from exactly this teaching on non-abiding. Passing it on doesn't just transfer information; it plants the seed a listener's own buddhahood eventually grows from. And true to the section's own logic, the last line refuses to let even "buddha-dharma" become one more fixed thing to grasp: "what is called the buddha-dharma is no buddha-dharma at all — that is why it is called the buddha-dharma" is the sūtra's first full statement of the name it, negate it, rename it pattern that will run through everything still to come.
