Saving No One, Owning Nothing

化無所化分第二十五 至 不受不貪分第二十八

Why "the Tathāgata saves beings" cannot be taken at face value, the verse warning against seeking the Buddha through form or sound, why denying fixed marks is not the same as denying cause and effect, and why refusing to hold onto merit is what makes a bodhisattva's merit boundless.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T235 · Kumārajīva's translation, c. 402 CE Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's 金剛般若波羅蜜經講錄 (1927)

25. Saving No One At All

Chinese Source · 化無所化分第二十五

「須菩提！於意云何？汝等勿謂如來作是念：『我當度眾生。』須菩提！莫作是念。何以故？實無有眾生如來度者。若有眾生如來度者，如來則有我、人、眾生、壽者。須菩提！如來說：『有我者，則非有我，而凡夫之人以為有我。』須菩提！凡夫者，如來說則非凡夫，是名凡夫。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, what do you think — do not say that the Tathāgata has the thought, 'I should save all beings.' Subhūti, do not think this way. Why is this? There is in truth no being that the Tathāgata saves. If there were a being that the Tathāgata saves, the Tathāgata would hold a self, a person, a being, a lifespan. Subhūti, the Tathāgata says, 'a self exists' is no existing self at all — it is only ordinary people who take there to be a self. Subhūti, what the Tathāgata calls an 'ordinary person' is no ordinary person at all — that is why they are called an ordinary person."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu draws out the logic hiding inside this denial: the sūtra has already established, back in Section 17, that the Buddha and beings stand level, with no rank of higher or lower between them. If that is true, then positing a fixed being genuinely "saved" by the Tathāgata would, absurdly, let the relation run both directions — a being could just as well be said to save the Tathāgata. Taking the notion of a saving self seriously, even in this most compassionate-sounding form, would give the Tathāgata exactly the self, person, being, and lifespan the whole sūtra has worked to clear away. "The Tathāgata has a self" is therefore only ever conventional speech, offered because worldly language needs a subject for its verbs — never a claim about something fixed found upon examination.

The section closes on "ordinary person" (凡夫) as one more name without a fixed thing behind it: there is no separate "ordinary-person nature" some beings simply have. To be confused about this very teaching, Taixu says, is what the word "ordinary person" names; to see through it is what the word "sage" names. The label describes a temporary condition of not-yet-having-seen, not a permanent category of being — which is exactly why the Tathāgata can say "ordinary person, no ordinary person, that is why it is called ordinary person," and mean it as an invitation rather than a judgment.

26. The Dharma-Body Has No Mark

Chinese Source · 法身非相分第二十六

「須菩提！於意云何？可以三十二相觀如來不？」須菩提言：「如是！如是！以三十二相觀如來。」佛言：「須菩提！若以三十二相觀如來者，轉輪聖王則是如來。」須菩提白佛言：「世尊！如我解佛所說義。不應以三十二相觀如來。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, what do you think — can the Tathāgata be seen through the thirty-two marks?" Subhūti said: "So it is, so it is — the Tathāgata is seen through the thirty-two marks." The Buddha said: "Subhūti, if the Tathāgata could be seen through the thirty-two marks, then a wheel-turning king would be a Tathāgata too." Subhūti said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, as I understand what the Buddha has taught, the Tathāgata should not be seen through the thirty-two marks."

爾時，世尊而說偈言：「若以色見我，以音聲求我，是人行邪道，不能見如來。」

At that time the World-Honored One spoke this verse: "Whoever sees me through form, whoever seeks me through sound, that person walks a mistaken path and cannot see the Tathāgata."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu catches something easy to miss on a fast read: Subhūti, asked this same question he had already answered correctly back in Section 5, momentarily slips and answers "yes." The Buddha's rebuttal is immediate and concrete — a wheel-turning sage king, a legendary righteous monarch of Indian tradition, was also said to bear these same auspicious marks, without being a buddha at all. If bodily marks alone were sufficient, that king would qualify equally. Caught out, Subhūti corrects himself at once — Taixu reads the moment as a small, honest demonstration of exactly how easily even an advanced disciple can slide back into taking a mark for the reality behind it, useful precisely because it shows the error happening in real time rather than only being warned against in the abstract.

For the closing verse's second line — seeking the Buddha through sound — Taixu supplies the matching image the text leaves implicit: the kalaviṅka, a bird from Indian legend prized for a voice so beautiful it was said to outsing every other creature. A glorious voice, no more than a glorious appearance, is not the Tathāgata either. His fuller statement of the principle runs through all four possible positions at once: the Tathāgata is not seen by clinging to form or sound, and is not seen by rejecting form and sound as worthless either — not through them, and not apart from them, and not through their absence, and not apart from their absence. Only a mind that has stopped searching in any fixed direction — toward marks, or away from marks — is positioned to see the Tathāgata at all.

27. Neither Cut Off Nor Annihilated

Chinese Source · 無斷無滅分第二十七

「須菩提！汝若作是念：『如來不以具足相故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』須菩提！莫作是念：『如來不以具足相故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。』」「須菩提！汝若作是念，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心者，說諸法斷滅。莫作是念！何以故？發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心者。於法不說斷滅相。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, if you have the thought, 'The Tathāgata did not attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening because he lacked perfectly formed marks' — Subhūti, do not think this way, that the Tathāgata did not attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening because he lacked perfectly formed marks." "Subhūti, if you have the thought that one who sets out for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening teaches the annihilation of all things — do not think this way. Why is this? One who sets out for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening does not teach that anything is cut off or annihilated."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu treats this short section as closing off the one misreading the whole sūtra risks inviting after twenty-six sections of negation: if bodily marks are not what makes the Tathāgata the Tathāgata, has the whole doctrine of merit and cause-and-effect just been declared void? He calls a mind that draws this conclusion "wild wisdom" (狂慧) — clever enough to spot the negations, but reckless enough to conclude from them that nothing whatsoever functions, which the tradition names outright as a wrong view: denying cause and effect entirely.

His summary is the cleanest formulation in the entire fascicle of the middle way this sūtra has been walking all along: from the first resolve for awakening to buddhahood itself, the sages never teach permanence — so they do not fall into the fixed view that something solid persists unchanged — and they never teach annihilation either — so they do not fall into the fixed view that nothing continues at all. Every mark is empty of fixed self-nature, and every mark still functions, still causes, still ripens into consequence. Holding both without collapsing into either is, Taixu says, exactly what "unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening" names.

28. Neither Received Nor Craved

Chinese Source · 不受不貪分第二十八

「須菩提！若菩薩以滿恆河沙等世界七寶持用布施；若復有人，知一切法無我，得成於忍，此菩薩勝前菩薩所得功德。何以故？須菩提！以諸菩薩不受福德故。」須菩提白佛言：「世尊！云何菩薩不受福德？」「須菩提！菩薩所作福德，不應貪著。是故說不受福德。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Subhūti, if a bodhisattva filled as many worlds as there are grains of sand in the Ganges with the seven treasures and gave it all away; and if another bodhisattva came to know that all things are without self, and thereby attained forbearance — this second bodhisattva's merit would surpass the first's. Why is this? Subhūti, because bodhisattvas do not receive merit." Subhūti said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, what does it mean that a bodhisattva does not receive merit?" "Subhūti, a bodhisattva should not crave or cling to the merit they create. This is why it is said that they do not receive merit."

Reading note — after Taixu

Taixu marks a deliberate shift in depth here: earlier sections established the selflessness of the person — that no fixed "I" runs through the five aggregates. This section moves to the harder companion realization, the selflessness of dharmas themselves — that not even the elements and categories used to analyze the person have a fixed nature of their own. A bodhisattva who genuinely absorbs this and holds steady in it, without being shaken or drawn back, is said to have "attained forbearance" (忍) — not mere patience under provocation, as in Section 14, but the settled capacity to rest in this deeper emptiness without flinching from it.

What makes this bodhisattva's merit exceed even worlds' worth of treasure given away is not a bigger gift but a different relationship to the gift already given: "not receiving merit" means holding no inner tally of what has been accumulated, no quiet pride collecting behind the generous act. Taixu's phrase for it is direct — no mind that receives, and no mind that craves. Because nothing is gathered up and held onto as a possession, there is, in principle, no ceiling on how far the resulting benefit can reach; a merit hoarded, however large the hoard, is bounded by the hoarding itself.
