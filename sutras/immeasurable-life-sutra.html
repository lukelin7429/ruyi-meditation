Why This Sūtra, After the Amitābha Sūtra

If the Amitābha Sūtra is a description of Sukhāvatī and an instruction for reaching it, this sūtra is the account of how that land came to exist at all — a king who hears one sermon, renounces his throne, and spends five aeons studying two hundred and ten koṭis of other buddha-lands before staking his own future buddhahood on forty-eight specific promises. Where the shorter sūtra opens directly with Śākyamuni describing a finished place, this one insists on the causal story first: a vow, a name (Dharmākara, not yet Amitābha), and an enormous span of disciplined practice, before the land itself is ever shown.

It is also, by a wide margin, the longest and most structurally complex text on this site. Where the Diamond Sūtra runs about 5,180 characters across thirty-two traditional sections, this sūtra runs to more than 26,000 characters across two fascicles — long enough that this guide follows the traditional shape of the text (序分, the introduction; 正宗分, the main body; 流通分, the closing transmission) only loosely, breaking the main body into the largest natural units the narrative itself provides, from Dharmākara's vow through the land's full description, the three grades of rebirth, an extended and unflinching account of ordinary human suffering, and the sūtra's own account of who arrives in Sukhāvatī instantly and who arrives delayed.

The teaching in one sentence

Long ago, a king renounced his throne, studied the accomplishments and failures of two hundred and ten koṭis of other buddha-lands, and staked his own future buddhahood on forty-eight specific vows for the land he would build and the beings who would be reborn there; having fulfilled every one of those vows, he is now Amitāyus Buddha, presiding over that land ten aeons later, and anyone anywhere who hears his name with sincere faith — even amid the worst possible circumstances for practice — has access to it.

An honest note on authorship: who was Saṃghavarman?

This translation is traditionally attributed to Saṃghavarman (康僧鎧), a monk said to have translated it in the Kingdom of Wei in 252 CE — the attribution used by Chinese Wikisource's source text and reproduced in this guide's citation. It is worth being direct about something modern Buddhist scholarship raises and this site's other translation pages have not needed to: the historical figure of "Saṃghavarman" and the date of 252 CE are contested. A number of modern scholars, working from the translation's vocabulary and style, have argued the text as it survives was more likely produced later, in the early-to-mid fifth century, and some attribute it instead to translators such as Buddhabhadra and Baoyun working roughly a century and a half after the traditional date. This is a live question in the field, not a settled one, and this guide takes no position on it beyond stating plainly that the traditional attribution carried by the received Taishō text — which every printed and online edition of this sūtra still uses as its heading — is not beyond scholarly dispute. Nothing about the sūtra's content or its centuries of use in East Asian Buddhist practice depends on resolving this question.

Five translations, and a warning about "compiled" editions

Read before trusting any copy of this sūtra you find online

This sūtra survives in five extant Chinese translations, made across roughly six centuries by different translators, and they do not agree with one another in every detail — not the number of vows (twenty-four in one early translation, thirty-six in another, forty-eight in this one and later ones), not their order, and not every point of content. Saṃghavarman's forty-eight-vow version, translated here, became the standard in later practice, but it is one translation among five, not the only one that has ever circulated.

Separately, and more importantly for anyone searching for this sūtra today: because the five translations differ from one another, several later editors — most consequentially the Qing-dynasty layman Wang Longshu (王龍舒, 12th c.) and, in the 20th century, the layman Xiawulian, known as Hsia Lien-chü (夏蓮居, 1884–1965) — produced "compiled editions" (會集本), splicing passages from multiple translations together into a single new text, intending to preserve the fullest possible content from each. These compiled editions circulate very widely today, and a great deal of what appears online under the title "無量壽經" is one of them, not any one of the five original translations. This guide translates only Saṃghavarman's original translation, Taishō T360, and does not draw on or reproduce any compiled edition.

This is not a minor bibliographic footnote. Yin Guang (印光大師, 1861–1940), one of the most influential Pure Land teachers of the modern era and later counted among its patriarchs, explicitly and repeatedly opposed the circulation of compiled editions, holding that the five original translations should each be read and transmitted on their own terms, and that splicing them together — however well-intentioned — risked distorting a text whose translators had each made their own deliberate choices. Readers encountering other editions of this sūtra elsewhere may be reading a compiled text; what follows in this guide's eleven parts is Saṃghavarman's translation alone.

The forty-eight vows, and where the tradition later placed its weight

The forty-eight vows, translated in full in Part 3 of this guide, range from vows shaping Amitāyus's own future body and powers, to vows shaping the physical land, to — the majority of them — vows shaping any being who is born there or who simply hears his name from anywhere in the universe. One vow among the forty-eight, the Eighteenth, later became the single most consequential clause in East Asian and Japanese Pure Land thought: the promise that sincere recollection of the name, as few as ten times, secures rebirth, with an exception carved out only for the five heinous transgressions and slander of the true Dharma. Centuries after this sūtra was translated, the Japanese teacher Hōnen (法然, 1133–1212) singled out this vow as "the King of Vows," the doctrinal center of an entire later tradition — a development this sūtra itself does not make explicit, and one this guide notes as a later reading laid over the text rather than a ranking the sūtra states for itself.

A degenerate world, deliberately chosen

Roughly a third of the way through the second fascicle, the sūtra makes an unusual turn: after an extended description of Sukhāvatī's beauty, it addresses the bodhisattva Maitreya directly and spends several thousand characters describing, in unflinching and often bleak detail, the ordinary anxieties, family resentments, inherited disbelief, and the five evils, five pains, and five burnings of the very world its own listeners inhabit — translated in full across Parts 8 and 9 of this guide. Worth noting honestly: some modern scholars have questioned whether this entire unit belongs to the sūtra's earliest transmitted form at all, observing that its moralizing tone, its concern with filial duty and social harmony, and its style read to some readers as closer to indigenous Chinese ethical writing than to translated Indian material. This is a genuine, unresolved scholarly question, not a settled fact in either direction, and this guide notes it rather than passing over it — while translating the passage in full regardless, since it is part of the received, canonical text used in East Asian Buddhist practice for over a millennium.

How to actually use this text

  1. Read the vow-story first. Parts 1 through 5 — the assembly, Dharmākara's resolve, the forty-eight vows, and the land they produced — form a complete narrative arc on their own, and reward being read in sequence before jumping elsewhere.
  2. Take the five evils as a unit. Parts 8 and 9 are the sūtra's longest sustained stretch of prose without a narrative break. They are also its most demanding reading, morally and emotionally; consider reading them in one sitting rather than piecemeal, since their cumulative weight is part of their argument.
  3. Sit with the golden-cage parable on its own. Part 10's account of womb-birth — a palace lacking nothing except access to the Three Jewels — is easy to misread quickly and worth returning to slowly; it is this sūtra's most psychologically precise image, and one of its least literally understood.

Three questions Western students often ask

"Are the forty-eight vows a Buddhist bill of rights, a legal contract, or something else?" Neither, exactly. Each vow has the grammatical shape of a conditional oath — "if such-and-such is not so, may I not attain awakening" — but their function in the tradition is closer to a description of what a fully realized compassion looks like when made specific and falsifiable, staked against the vower's own future attainment, rather than a legal instrument granting rights to a petitioner.

"If karma is so precisely tracked in the five evils section, why does the sūtra also promise rebirth for something as simple as ten recollections of a name?" The sūtra does not treat these as contradictory paths but as different scales of the same causal logic: ordinary karmic consequence, worked out in exhaustive and often severe detail in Parts 8 and 9, governs this world's ordinary unfolding; the Eighteenth Vow describes a different, specific cause — sincere recollection — set in motion by Dharmākara's own accumulated merit, which produces a different effect. The sūtra holds both as true without treating either as canceling the other out.

"Is 'womb-birth' actual literal gestation, the way the term suggests?" No — the sūtra uses the term by analogy to an ordinary womb's confinement and eventual emergence, not to describe biological birth. As Part 10 makes explicit through its own parable, it describes a temporary, materially comfortable confinement caused by residual doubt about the scope of a buddha's wisdom, ending automatically after five hundred years or sooner through sincere recognition — not a punishment, and not a biological process.

Key Terms

Dharmākara 法藏 — "Dharma-Treasury," the name of the king-turned-monk whose forty-eight vows, once fulfilled, made him Amitāyus Buddha. The sūtra does not call him Amitāyus until his vows are complete.
Lokeśvararāja 世自在王 — the buddha under whom Dharmākara studied, who showed him two hundred and ten koṭis of buddha-lands before Dharmākara chose the features of his own.
四十八願 — the Forty-Eight Vows, Saṃghavarman's count; other surviving translations of this sūtra give twenty-four or thirty-six.
十念必生 — the Eighteenth Vow: sincere recollection of the name, as few as ten times, assures rebirth. Later Japanese Pure Land teachers called it "the King of Vows," a ranking this sūtra does not itself state.
安樂 / 極樂 — Peace and Bliss / Utmost Bliss — both names for Amitāyus's land, each supplied by this sūtra itself at different points; the second is the name used throughout the Amitābha Sūtra.
正定聚 — the assembly of the rightly settled: everyone born in Sukhāvatī belongs to it, guaranteeing non-retrogression.
三輩往生 — the three grades of rebirth — upper, middle, and lower — open respectively to the full renunciant, the ordinary lay practitioner, and anyone capable of as little as ten sincere recollections.
五惡・五痛・五燒 — the five evils, five pains, and five burnings: this sūtra's extended, unsparing account of ordinary human suffering in the present world, loosely corresponding to violations of the five lay precepts.
胎生 / 化生 — womb-birth and transformation-birth: two modes of arriving in Sukhāvatī, the first a temporary delay caused by doubt about the scope of a buddha's wisdom, the second immediate and complete.
邊地 — the borderland: where those born by womb-birth dwell, comfortable in every material way but cut off from the Three Jewels for up to five hundred years.
觀世音 / 大勢至 — Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta, Amitāyus's two foremost bodhisattvas, both of whom this sūtra says practiced originally in our own sahā world.

About the Source

The Chinese is Saṃghavarman's translation (康僧鎧, Taishō T360), sourced here from a public-domain critical edition hosted at Chinese Wikisource, collated against the Qianlong-era Longzang and Taishō editions — the version that became standard in East Asian Pure Land practice, one of five surviving Chinese translations of this sūtra. The English translation was made for Ru-Yi Meditation Center directly from this Chinese, without reference to any existing English translation, following the same conventions as the Amitābha Sūtra and Diamond Sūtra pages on this site.

Reference, not a source translated

The reading notes across all eleven parts draw primarily on Jingying Huiyuan's (淨影寺慧遠, 523–592) 無量壽經義疏, the earliest and most systematically complete of the surviving commentaries on this sūtra, written in the sixth century and long in the public domain. Where noted, this guide also draws on Jizang's (吉藏, 549–623) commentary of the same title and on Gyeongheung's (憬興, 7th c., Silla) 無量壽經連義述文贊 — both roughly contemporary with Huiyuan's own work — and, where their readings genuinely diverge from his, that divergence is noted rather than smoothed over into false agreement.

The commentaries are listed here as references consulted, not reproduced. Each "Reading note — after Huiyuan" is Ru-Yi's own English synthesis of the commentarial tradition's key points, images, and distinctions for that passage — not a translation of any commentary in full. See also this site's note on translation method.
