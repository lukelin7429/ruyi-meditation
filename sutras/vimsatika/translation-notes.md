# Viṃśatikā Translation Notes

These notes record recurring translation decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of the *Viṃśatikā* (唯識二十論, Taishō T31, no. 1590), composed by Vasubandhu and translated into Chinese by Xuánzàng. They exist so that the vocabulary of this treatise stays consistent with the site's larger Yogācāra corpus, especially the *Cheng Weishi Lun* and the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*.

## Source and independence from prior translations

- Root text: Taishō T1590, 唯識二十論, Vasubandhu, translated by Xuánzàng — unlike the *Triṃśikā* (translated on this site as part of the *Cheng Weishi Lun*), this treatise carries Vasubandhu's own prose commentary (長行) directly beneath each verse, so no separate root-commentary pairing is needed.
- Commentary guide: Taishō T1834, 唯識二十論述記, Kuījī (窺基), Xuánzàng's direct disciple and the actual founder of the Faxiang (法相) school — chosen for the same reason as on this site's other Xuánzàng-lineage pages (*Cheng Weishi Lun*, *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*, *Treatise on the Stages of Yogic Practice*): a single consistent "Xuánzàng system" of terminology, running from the root translation itself into the exegesis. Kuījī's commentary is unusual even among his own body of work in that it records notes taken during the very week Xuánzàng translated the root text (Kuījī served as amanuensis), not composed independently afterward.
- This page translates directly from Xuánzàng's Chinese. Existing English translations and scholarly studies may be consulted privately to check difficult passages, but the English wording, sentence structure, and section framing on this site must be Ru-Yi's own.
- The title is given as *Viṃśatikā*, "the Twenty-Verse [Treatise]" — named to distinguish it from Vasubandhu's own, later *Triṃśikā*, the "Thirty Verses."

## Core terminology

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 唯識二十論 | *Viṃśatikā* | Use the full Sanskrit title on first appearance per page; "the treatise" thereafter. |
| 三界 | the three realms | Desire, form, and formless (欲界, 色界, 無色界) — matches the site's standard Yogācāra usage. |
| 心、意、識、了 | mind, intent, consciousness, cognizing | Four names Kuījī reads as distinguished by function, not by referring to different things: 心 for the accumulating function (especially the ālayavijñāna), 意 for deliberation (especially the seventh consciousness), 識 for discernment in general, 了 as a catch-all. Part 1, section 1. |
| 心所 | mental factors | What necessarily accompanies every moment of mind; "consciousness-only" excludes an external object, never the mental factors. |
| 眩瞖 / 眩翳 | a clouded eye | The treatise's controlling simile: someone with this eye condition sees hairs, flies, a second moon, or floating spots where nothing real exists — introduced in the opening thesis (Part 1, section 1) and echoed throughout Part 1's answer to the fourfold challenge. |
| 尋香城 | a gandharva's phantom city | 健達縛城; a mirage-like city conjured and inhabited only by wandering "scent-seeker" musician-spirits (gandharvas), cited in Part 1's fourfold challenge as an image with no real city's function. Kept transliterated rather than rendered "mirage," since the treatise's own point depends on there being an apparent inhabitant (the gandharva) as well as an apparent place. |
| 異熟 | karmic ripening | vipāka; the process by which karma matures into its result — used in Part 1 of the hungry ghosts' and hell-beings' shared perception, all arising from a shared karmic ripening rather than a shared external object. |
| 熏習 | habituation | vāsanā; matches the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* glossary — the process by which an action leaves behind, in the consciousness-stream, a functional capacity to reproduce something like it later, not a stored copy of the action itself. Central to Part 1's refutation of the Sautrāntika school. |
| 那落迦 / 捺落迦 | hell / hell-beings | naraka; rendered simply "hell" in running prose, since the Chinese itself glosses the Sanskrit loanword as "the vessel of suffering" (苦器) rather than treating it as a proper name requiring transliteration. |
| 獄卒 | hell-guardians | The tormentors that the condemned in hell perceive — the treatise's central test case for whether a shared, vivid, harmful appearance requires a real external referent. Proven, across Part 1, to be neither real sentient beings (against Mahāsāṃghika and Sāṃmitīya), nor karma-made external matter (against Sarvāstivāda), nor a habituation that ripens somewhere outside consciousness (against Sautrāntika). |
| 大種 | great elements | mahābhūta; the four (or, in some contexts, more) fundamental physical elements Sarvāstivāda held karma could directly produce as non-sentient matter, given the provisional label "hell-guardian" — refuted in Part 1, section 6. |
| 密意 (趣) | hidden intent | A scriptural statement's real, compassionate purpose, distinct from its literal surface meaning. Kuījī's four kinds (Part 2, section 8): equality, a different time, a different meaning, and accommodating a listener's disposition — the last is what explains the Buddha's teaching that the twelve sense-processes exist. |
| 數取趣 | the person who repeatedly takes rebirth | pudgala; the conventional "self" refuted by the twelve-process teaching (Part 2, section 10) — one who "repeatedly (數) takes hold of (取) a destiny/rebirth (趣)," not an ultimately real self. |
| 人無我 / 法無我 (有情無我 / 諸法無我) | the selflessness of the person / the selflessness of dharmas | Matches the site's standard Yogācāra pairing. The twelve-process teaching enables the first; the Consciousness-Only teaching enables the second (Part 2, section 10). |
| 遍計所執(自性) / 依他起(自性) / 圓成實(自性) | the discriminated, attached-to nature / the dependent nature / the perfectly accomplished nature | Kept identical to the *Cheng Weishi Lun* and *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* glossaries. Central to Part 2, section 10's defense against the charge that Consciousness-Only is itself "empty of everything": only the first of the three is what "selflessness of dharmas" denies. |
| 極微 | atom(s) | paramāṇu; the indivisible, partless physical particle whose combination, or failure to combine, and whose possession or lack of spatial parts, are both shown impossible in Part 2, sections 12–13 — the treatise's central argument. |
| 有分色 | a whole-possessing form | The Vaiśeṣika school's single, unified sense-object, standing over and above its own parts — the first of the three candidates for an external object ruled out in Part 2, section 11, and returned to directly in section 14. |
| 和合 / 和集 | combination / aggregation | Two rival accounts (Sautrāntika / the later Sarvāstivāda's Nyāya-influenced Naiyāyika school) of how discrete atoms might jointly serve as a single perceived object — both refuted in Part 2, sections 11–12. |
| 現量 | direct perception | pratyakṣa; one of the classical valid means of knowing (量), alongside inference (比量) and authoritative testimony (至教量) — argued in Part 3, section 15 to be no proof of an external object, since the reflective awareness "I now perceive this" always arises after the perceiving it reports on has already ceased. |
| 剎那(論者) | momentary, moment-by-moment (the momentariness schools) | kṣaṇa; the Sarvāstivāda-lineage doctrine that all conditioned things arise and perish moment to moment — cited in Part 3, section 15 as a second reason direct perception cannot prove an external object, since the object itself has already ceased by the time it is reflected upon. |
| 展轉增上力 | reciprocal, supporting influence | How one mind-stream conditions changes within a separate mind-stream without ever directly touching it as an object — Part 3, section 17's answer to how teaching and learning are possible with no shared external world. |
| 眾同分 | the grouping [a being belongs to] | nikāya-sabhāga; the conventional basis on which beings of the same type of rebirth are grouped together — "death," in Part 3, section 19, is defined as this grouping's continuity breaking off through a transformation in consciousness, not the destruction of an external body. |
| 他心智 | cognition of another's mind | The classical Buddhist claim that some minds (advanced practitioners', and above all a buddha's) can know another's mental states — examined in Part 3, section 21 as genuine but, short of a buddha's, never entirely free of the subtle subject-object structure the treatise as a whole sets out to dismantle. |
