Why the Heart Sūtra, First

Of the thousands of texts in the Mahāyāna canon, this is the one a monk in Kyoto, a nun in Taipei, and a layperson in San Francisco are most likely to know by heart. In 260 characters it compresses the entire Perfection of Wisdom — a body of scripture that runs, in its longest form, to a hundred thousand verses — into something you can chant in two minutes.

That compression is the point. The Prajñāpāramitā literature is vast, repetitive, and deliberately exhausting: it says the same radical thing about emptiness in ten thousand ways until the mind gives up trying to hold onto anything. The Heart Sūtra is the distillate. It keeps the one insight and throws away the scaffolding. This is why it is called a heart — hṛdaya, the pith, the essence, the core that everything else was protecting.

It is also the most misunderstood text in the tradition, because its surface is a wall of negations — no form, no eye, no suffering, no path, no wisdom, no attainment — that sounds, to a first-time Western reader, either nihilistic or like word-games. It is neither. This guide is written to get you past the wall, so that when you read the text itself, below, the negations land as what they are: not a denial of the world, but a description of what the world is like when nothing in it is grasped.

The teaching in one sentence

When wisdom looks deeply enough, it finds that nothing whatsoever — not the body, not the mind, not even the Buddhist path itself — has a fixed, separate, self-standing existence; and seeing this, the heart is set free from fear.

What "Heart" means

The title is often heard as "the sūtra about the heart," as though the text were about love or feeling. It is not. Hṛdaya here means essence or core — the way we say "the heart of the matter." The Heart Sūtra is the extracted centre of the enormous Prajñāpāramitā collection. Think of it as the single paragraph you would keep if you had to burn every other page and preserve the teaching. It is short not because it is introductory, but because it is concentrated.

Where it comes from: the Perfection of Wisdom

Beginning around the first century BCE, a new stream of Mahāyāna scriptures appeared, organized around a single theme: prajñāpāramitā, the "perfection of wisdom." These texts came in many sizes — the Perfection of Wisdom in 8,000 lines, in 25,000, in 100,000. All of them circle one claim: that liberation depends on seeing the emptiness (śūnyatā) of all phenomena, and that a bodhisattva — one bound for full buddhahood — practises by working without grasping at anything as real. The Heart Sūtra takes this whole world of scripture and gives you its conclusion, up front, without the argument.

The cast: Avalokiteśvara and Śāriputra

Two figures carry the text, and the choice of both is deliberate.

  • Avalokiteśvara (觀自在 / 觀世音, Guanyin) is the bodhisattva of compassion. That the deepest statement of wisdom is spoken by the embodiment of compassion is the whole Mahāyāna in miniature: wisdom and compassion are not two projects. The name itself, 觀自在, means "the one who observes with sovereign freedom" — the seeing that this sūtra describes.
  • Śāriputra is, in the older tradition, the disciple foremost in wisdom — the sharpest analytical mind among the Buddha's students. The Heart Sūtra has the bodhisattva of compassion teach the master of analysis. The point is pointed: even the finest scholastic understanding of the Buddha's lists must, in the end, be emptied.

The hinge: "Form is emptiness, emptiness is form"

The most famous sentence in Mahāyāna Buddhism is 色即是空，空即是色 — form is emptiness, emptiness is form. It is easy to quote and easy to get exactly backwards. Here is what it does not say: it does not say the world is an illusion, or that nothing exists, or that matter is secretly made of a mystical substance called emptiness.

"Form" (rūpa) means physical stuff — everything material. "Emptiness" (śūnyatā) means the absence of independent, intrinsic existence. A wave is real; it is also not a separate thing standing apart from the ocean, the wind, and the shore — it has no wave-essence you could isolate. To say "form is emptiness" is to say: everything that appears is real as appearance, and at the same time has no self-contained core. And crucially the sūtra runs the equation both ways. Emptiness is not a void behind things; it is not more real than form. Emptiness is form. The two are one situation seen truly. The text then extends the equation to the other four aggregates — feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — so that mind is included with matter. Nothing is exempt.

What emptiness is — and is not

This is where Western readers most often go wrong, so it is worth stating plainly. Emptiness is not nothingness. The Sanskrit śūnyatā is built on śūnya, "zero" or "hollow" — but the tradition spent centuries insisting it does not mean non-existence. To read "emptiness" as "nothing exists" is a specific error the Buddha's followers named and warned against: nihilism (ucchedavāda), which is as false as its opposite, the belief in permanent fixed essences (eternalism).

Emptiness is the middle between these. It says: things function, arise, and matter — and they do so precisely because they are not frozen, not self-made, not independent. A seed can become a tree only because it has no fixed unchanging seed-nature. Emptiness is the openness that makes change, relationship, and freedom possible. It is the reason liberation is available at all. Nāgārjuna, the great philosopher of this school, put it exactly: "For whom emptiness is possible, everything is possible."

The great negation: "no… no… no…"

The central movement of the sūtra is a systematic dismantling. In emptiness, it says, there is no form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness; no eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; no sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, object; and so on. What is being negated here is not the existence of your eyes. What is being negated is that any of these categories names a truly separate, self-existing thing. Notice which lists are being crossed out — they are the core teaching-lists of early Buddhism itself:

This is startling, and meant to be. The Heart Sūtra takes the Buddha's own most sacred lists — the Four Noble Truths above all — and declares them empty. It is not rejecting them. It is completing them. Those teachings were medicines; a medicine you cling to becomes a new disease. Even the path must not be turned into one more thing to grasp. The final and most audacious negation, 無智亦無得, "no wisdom and no attainment," pulls the ground out from under spiritual ambition itself. There is no prize to seize, no self to seize it, no seizing.

Why this sets you free: "no obstruction, no fear"

Everything in the sūtra bends toward one line: 心無罣礙，無罣礙故，無有恐怖 — the mind is without obstruction; and because it is unobstructed, there is no fear. This is the sūtra's emotional core and the reason it is chanted at funerals, before surgery, in prison cells, and at the edge of death.

The logic is precise. Fear needs two things: a self that can be threatened, and a threat treated as solid and real. When wisdom sees that neither the self nor the threat has the fixed independent existence we assign to it, the machinery of fear loses its grip. The mind stops snagging on things (罣礙 literally means "hanging up, getting caught"). Free of catching, it is free of dread; it leaves behind "upside-down views and dreamlike thinking" and comes to rest. This is not numbness. It is the fearlessness of someone who has stopped clutching.

The mantra: gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā

The sūtra ends by becoming a sound. After all the negations, it offers a mantra and calls the perfection of wisdom itself "the great radiant mantra, the unsurpassed mantra." The words are left untranslated in the chant, but they do mean something:

gate gate — gone, gone · pāragate — gone beyond · pārasaṃgate — gone completely beyond · bodhi svāhā — awakening, so be it!

It is a going-across: from this shore of grasping and fear to the far shore of freedom. Ending in a mantra is not a decorative flourish. The text has spent 250 characters showing that concepts cannot finally grasp reality; it closes by handing you something that is not a concept at all — a sound to be practised, not a proposition to be believed.

How to actually use this text

The Heart Sūtra is not primarily something to understand once; it is something to return to. Three ways in, from lightest to deepest:

  1. Read it slowly, with the guide. On a first pass, let this guide carry you through the negations so they stop sounding like denial and start sounding like release.
  2. Chant it. Across East Asia this text is recited, daily, out loud, often before meditation. The chanting is the practice — the meaning soaks in below the level of analysis, the way a song does. Try the Chinese, or the English; the point is the repetition.
  3. Contemplate one line. Take a single sentence — "form is emptiness," or "no obstruction, no fear" — and hold it in sitting meditation, not solving it, just letting it work on you. The sūtra is built for this. It rewards a lifetime of returns.

Three questions Western students often ask

"If everything is empty, why get out of bed? Doesn't this make life meaningless?" It does the opposite. Emptiness does not say things don't matter; it says things aren't fixed. The person you love, the work you do, the pain you feel — all real, all vivid, none of it frozen or separate from everything else. Meaning does not require permanence; in fact grasping for permanence is what makes us suffer. Seeing emptiness is what lets you love and act without the desperation of trying to make things last forever.

"Isn't 'no suffering, no path, no attainment' just a denial of Buddhism itself?" No — it is Buddhism refusing to let you turn Buddhism into an idol. The Four Noble Truths and the path are indispensable; the sūtra assumes you have taken them seriously. What it forbids is clinging to them as solid final things, which quietly recreates the very self-grasping they were meant to dissolve. A raft carries you across the river; you don't strap it to your back and carry it up the mountain.

"Do I have to believe in bodhisattvas and mantras to get something from this?" No. You can read Avalokiteśvara as a personification of awakened compassion and the mantra as a focusing practice rather than a magic spell, and the core teaching stands untouched: what you take to be solid, separate, and permanent — including yourself — is none of those things, and seeing that clearly loosens fear. The devotional frame is a door; the emptiness is the room. Enter by whichever door you can.

Key Terms

prajñāpāramitā 般若波羅蜜多 — the "perfection of wisdom." Prajñā is the deep, liberating insight into how things actually are; pāramitā is a perfection carried to its far shore. The name of both the wisdom and the whole body of scripture the sūtra distills.
hṛdaya 心 — heart, core, essence. Not "heart" in the emotional sense but "the heart of the matter." The title says: this is the extracted centre of the Perfection of Wisdom.
śūnyatā 空 — emptiness. The absence of independent, intrinsic, self-standing existence. Not nothingness; the openness that makes change and freedom possible.
rūpa 色 — form, material stuff. The first of the five aggregates, and the subject of the famous line "form is emptiness."
pañca-skandha 五蘊 — the five aggregates: form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness. The complete inventory of what a "person" is made of — all of it declared empty.
Avalokiteśvara 觀自在 · 觀世音 — "the one who observes with sovereign freedom" (Guanyin), speaker of the sūtra. That wisdom's deepest word is spoken by compassion is the teaching's whole shape.
Śāriputra 舍利子 — the disciple foremost in analytical wisdom, whom the sūtra addresses. Even the sharpest understanding of the lists must be emptied.
bodhisattva 菩薩 — one bound for full buddhahood who practises for the sake of all beings, working "with nothing to attain."
gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā 揭諦揭諦，波羅揭諦，波羅僧揭諦，菩提薩婆訶 — "Gone, gone, gone beyond, gone completely beyond — awakening, so be it!" The closing mantra: a crossing to the far shore.

The Translation

The Chinese below is Xuanzang's translation of 649 CE (玄奘, Taishō T251), the version chanted across East Asia and in the public domain. The English is Ru-Yi's own translation, kept plain and close to the Chinese, set out clause by clause. The reading note after each passage draws on Grand Master Yuanying's (圓瑛大師, 1878–1953) 般若心經講義 — a commentary delivered at Meifeng Monastery, Putian, and first printed in 1953. Grand Master Yuanying was president of the Chinese Buddhist Association for seven terms and one of the most widely heard teachers of his generation; he died in 1953, so this commentary is now in the public domain. It is quoted and paraphrased here as a reference, in Ru-Yi's own words — not reproduced in full.

1. Who Is Observing

Chinese Source

觀自在菩薩，

Ru-Yi Translation

Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva —

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying reads the first character, 觀 ("guān"), as pointed away from the ordinary sense of "watching with the eyes": this is the mind observing with the wisdom it already possesses, turned on the world of body and mind to break the two kinds of grasping — grasping at a self, and grasping at things as real. 自在, "sovereign freedom," is what that observing produces: watching existence without clinging to it as existence, watching emptiness without clinging to it as emptiness — the mind turns things, rather than being turned by them.

He traces the epithet to the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's account of how this bodhisattva perfected the ear faculty by listening inward until hearing itself dissolved, gaining both a personal mastery (self-cultivation freedom) and a boundless capacity to answer any being's cry for help by taking whatever form is needed (freedom in saving others) — hence the second name, Avalokiteśvara, "the one who hears the world's sounds." As for 菩薩 — bodhi-sattva, "awakened being" — Yuanying takes it three ways at once: one who has realized the goal for their own sake, one who labours to awaken every being still lost, and, most fully, one doing both together. He is pointed that this is not otherworldly: a bodhisattva "does not shrink from the donkey's womb or the horse's belly" — willing to be reborn anywhere, in any form, if it serves living beings.

2. What Is Practised

Chinese Source

行深般若波羅蜜多時，

Ru-Yi Translation

while practising the profound Perfection of Wisdom,

Reading note — after Yuanying

般若 is one of five categories of Sanskrit term the old translators agreed never to render into Chinese — here specifically "kept untranslated out of reverence," because "wisdom" (智慧) alone undersells it. Worldly cleverness, Yuanying says — even the sharpest scientific or philosophical kind — can advance material life without ever touching the root of suffering; prajñā is the different order of knowing that does. 波羅蜜多 he glosses as "arrived at the far shore," and — departing from most other commentators — reads the final syllable, 多, not as mere transliteration filler but as samādhi, concentration: wisdom and stillness held in balance, neither collapsing into the other, "settled and radiant at once."

His governing image for the whole sūtra appears here: the enlightened mind is like a bronze mirror, obscuring afflictions like the dust that dulls it, and the work of observing-wisdom is like polishing — the mirror's own brightness was never lost, only covered over, and steady polishing is all it takes for the light to show through on its own.

3. What Is Seen

Chinese Source

照見五蘊皆空，

Ru-Yi Translation

[Avalokiteśvara] clearly saw that all five aggregates are empty,

Reading note — after Yuanying

The five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — are Yuanying's inventory of everything a "person" is: one physical category (form, defined by taking up space and eventually breaking down) and four mental ones (receiving an experience as pleasant or not, forming an image of it, acting on it, and finally knowing it as an object). He walks through how form itself is composite — earth, water, fire, and wind combining, the way clay, water, fire, and air-pressure together make a vase — and how at death these same four elements separate out again in order, breath first, then warmth, then fluid, then flesh, until nothing is left standing.

His central image for why this counts as empty rather than merely temporary: it is like a dream. Not because the world is unreal exactly the way a dream is, but because within a dream, the dream-world feels every bit as solid and continuous as this one does — until waking shows it never had the substance it seemed to. "A single night's dream is the small dream; an entire lifetime is the great dream" — and most people, he says, are simply still asleep inside the larger one, mistaking its intensity for proof of its solidity.

4. The Result

Chinese Source

度一切苦厄。

Ru-Yi Translation

and so crossed beyond all suffering and distress.

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying reads "all" here as deliberately covering two tiers at once, not just the sufferings anyone would recognize. Within ordinary existence there is the suffering born of mistaken views and habitual craving, and the specific hardship of being born again and again in a body that ages and dies. Beyond that, even a bodhisattva who has left ordinary rebirth behind still carries a subtler suffering — the last residue of ignorance, and a finer kind of change-and-renewal that has not yet fully settled. The sūtra's "all" is built to reach both: it is not only a path out of an unhappy life, but out of every remaining trace of not-yet-fully-awake, however fine.

5. Two Doubts Answered

Chinese Source

舍利子，色不異空，空不異色；色即是空，空即是色。

Ru-Yi Translation

Śāriputra, form is not different from emptiness, emptiness is not different from form; form itself is emptiness, emptiness itself is form.

受想行識，亦復如是。

Feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are just the same.

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying reads these four short clauses as answering five specific doubts, not as a single poetic flourish. Two belong to the narrower path: a practitioner who has only understood that the self is empty may still quietly treat the five aggregates themselves as real building-blocks — hence "not different from emptiness." Another may treat emptiness as something reached only once form is extinguished, a peace found by shutting the world out — hence "form itself is emptiness," true right now, with nothing needing to end first. Three further doubts, which he traces to the Ratnagotravibhāga, belong to more advanced students of emptiness: mistaking emptiness for a separate space apart from form, mistaking it for form's annihilation, or — most subtly — quietly turning "emptiness" itself into a thing to be grasped. The fourfold formula closes each door in turn.

His two images for how this actually works: form is emptiness the way digging a hole is not importing space from somewhere else — the emptiness was already there, coextensive with the earth, before any digging began. And emptiness is form the way water is waves: constant change of surface, one same wetness underneath, so that naming the waves "not water" or the water "absent from the waves" would both be simply wrong.

6. The Nature of Emptiness Itself

Chinese Source

舍利子，是諸法空相，不生不滅，不垢不淨，不增不減。

Ru-Yi Translation

Śāriputra, all things bear the mark of emptiness: they neither arise nor cease, are neither defiled nor pure, neither increase nor decrease.

Reading note — after Yuanying

A worry Yuanying anticipates directly: does this "empty mark" only come into being because one meditates — as though contemplation manufactures an emptiness that wasn't there before, replacing the five aggregates with something new? No, he says: the aggregates were never anything else to begin with. Meditation uncovers what was already, always the case; it does not produce a fresh substitute for it.

His image: a wish-fulfilling jewel can throw off five colours of light, but the jewel's own nature stays exactly as it is throughout — the colours' coming and going is not the jewel arising or ceasing. Reflected in mud it is not stained; reflected in clean water it gains nothing extra we'd call "purity." Show it to a crowd and it does not multiply; show it to one person alone and it does not diminish. Whatever changes on the surface, the jewel itself never does — and this, for Yuanying, is exactly the relationship between the ever-shifting aggregates and the unmoving emptiness they never actually depart from.

7. Emptying the Aggregates, the Senses, and Their Fields

Chinese Source

是故空中無色，無受想行識，無眼耳鼻舌身意，無色聲香味觸法，無眼界，乃至無意識界。

Ru-Yi Translation

And so, within emptiness there is no form, no feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness; no eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind; no sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, or object of mind; no field of vision — and so on, up to no field of mind-consciousness.

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying distinguishes the coarse sense organs we can see — the fleshly eye, the outer ear — from what he calls their "subtle faculty": a refined, imperceptible working-part seated within the coarse organ, the actual site where seeing or hearing happens, invisible even to ordinary sight. The trickiest of the six sense-objects to place, he notes, are touch and mental objects. Touch only registers where something with awareness meets something without it — cloth resting unworn on a hanger is not "touch," but the moment it is worn and felt as comfortable or not, it becomes so. And mental objects, he says, are like photographs: each of the other five senses leaves an impression behind as it registers its object, and that afterimage — no longer the live sense-data itself, but the trace of it — is what the mind continues to hold, recall, and think about.

For why naming eighteen elements — six senses, six objects, six resulting consciousnesses — collapses once the first twelve are seen as empty, his image is a house with three connected rooms: knock down the outer wall and the inner wall, and the middle room, whose entire identity was "the space between the other two," no longer has anything to be a middle of. Inner faculties and outer objects both empty, the eighteen elements — the entire textbook of early Buddhist analysis — do not need separately emptying; they were never anything apart from those two in the first place.

8. Emptying Dependent Origination

Chinese Source

無無明，亦無無明盡，乃至無老死，亦無老死盡。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no ignorance and no end of ignorance; and so on, up to no old-age-and-death and no end of old-age-and-death.

Reading note — after Yuanying

The twelve links run in two directions at once here: forward, from ignorance through craving to old age and death, tracing how one life's causes ripen into the next; and in reverse, showing that if ignorance is uprooted, every link chained to it collapses in turn. On the forward chain, Yuanying repeats a piece of folk diagnostic teaching he clearly expected his audience to recognize: at the moment of death, where the body cools last is said to reveal where the departing consciousness is headed — the crown of the head for a realized sage, the eyes for rebirth in a heaven, the heart for rebirth as a human, the belly for a hungry ghost, the knees for an animal, the soles of the feet for hell. Vivid, and meant to be unsettling.

The turn he insists on is in the second half of the clause: it is not enough to see that the chain of suffering is empty. The sūtra also says there is no ending of it to attain — "emptying the extinguishing, too." A narrower path can still quietly treat nirvāṇa itself as a real destination to be grasped once the chain is broken; this line closes that door as well. The bodhisattva does not merely escape the wheel — the wheel, and the escaping, are seen through together.

9. Emptying the Four Truths, Knowledge, and Attainment

Chinese Source

無苦集滅道。無智亦無得，以無所得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no suffering, no origin, no cessation, no path; no wisdom, and no attainment — because there is nothing to attain.

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying keeps the Four Truths' ordinary meaning intact — suffering as fact, craving as its cause, cessation as the resulting peace, the path as the way there — before showing what the Mahāyāna does to them: "here, birth-and-death just is nirvāṇa, affliction just is awakening; there is no origin to cut off, no suffering to abandon, no path to walk, no cessation to attain." Not a rejection of the four truths, but the claim that seen clearly enough, there was never a gap between the sickness and the cure to begin with.

For "no attainment," his image is a family's own buried treasure, forgotten for years and then suddenly remembered: nothing was newly acquired in the remembering — the wealth had been there, theirs, all along. What awakening finds, on this account, was never absent; it only looks like a gain because it had, for so long, been mistaken for a loss.

10. The Bodhisattva's Fearlessness

Chinese Source

菩提薩埵，依般若波羅蜜多故，心無罣礙。無罣礙故，無有恐怖，遠離顛倒夢想，究竟涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva, relying on the Perfection of Wisdom, has a mind without obstruction. Because it is unobstructed, there is no fear; far removed from all upside-down views and dreamlike thinking, he comes to final nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying frames the obstruction two ways, and shows the bodhisattva clear of both at once: an ordinary person, lacking insight into emptiness, clings to things as real and so is hemmed in by existence; a practitioner of the narrower path, lacking a feel for how form still functions, clings instead to stillness and so is hemmed in by emptiness. Only holding neither extreme — seeing both the everyday and the ultimate truth without being caught by either — leaves the mind genuinely without a snag to catch on.

For the "upside-down, dreamlike thinking" that falls away, he describes four layers of delusion, coarse to subtle, arising and dissolving in mirrored order — like dressing, which proceeds from the innermost layer outward, and undressing, which reverses it, from the outermost layer in. The point: liberation is not one clean break but a peeling-back, the subtlest confusions going last, in the reverse order they first took hold.

11. The Buddhas' Awakening

Chinese Source

三世諸佛，依般若波羅蜜多故，得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

All the buddhas of the three times, relying on the Perfection of Wisdom, attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

Reading note — after Yuanying

Every buddha of past, present, and future, Yuanying stresses, reaches awakening by this same wisdom — there is no other route, for any of them. He unpacks the title itself in three parts: unsurpassed, because nothing stands higher than what is realized; perfect (or "equal"), because the realizing mind holds no favourites, and because what it sees is one same nature running through everything, however different things look on the surface; complete awakening, full realization with nothing left over — a height even advanced bodhisattvas, one rung short of buddhahood, have not yet fully reached.

His image for "perfect," or equality-without-favouritism: a goldsmith's cups, hairpins, and bracelets look entirely different if you attend only to their shapes — but attend to what they're made of, and every one of them, without exception, is simply gold. Things stay as different as they ever were on the surface; it is the shared nature beneath the surface that is, without qualification, one and the same.

12. Praising the Mantra's Power

Chinese Source

故知般若波羅蜜多，是大神咒，是大明咒，是無上咒，是無等等咒，能除一切苦，真實不虛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore know that the Perfection of Wisdom is the great sacred mantra, the great radiant mantra, the unsurpassed mantra, the mantra beyond all compare — able to remove all suffering, true and not false.

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying takes each epithet as earned, not decorative: "great sacred," because it can drive off the demon of samsaric suffering itself; "great radiant," because its light reaches everywhere, breaking the long night of ignorance; "unsurpassed," because — like a blazing mass of fire that cannot be touched from any side without burning — nothing in or beyond the world stands higher than it; "beyond all compare," because it is what every buddha, without exception, is born from, so nothing else could be placed beside it as an equal. Four titles, one claim repeated from four angles: this wisdom has no ceiling above it and no rival beside it.

13. The Mantra

Chinese Source

故說般若波羅蜜多咒，即說咒曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

So he proclaimed the mantra of the Perfection of Wisdom, saying:

揭諦揭諦，波羅揭諦，波羅僧揭諦，菩提薩婆訶。
gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā
Gone, gone, gone beyond, gone completely beyond — awakening, so be it!
Reading note — after Yuanying

Mantras, Yuanying explains, belong to the same "kept untranslated" category as 般若 in Section 2 — this time for secrecy rather than reverence: rendered into Chinese meaning, a mantra's force is thought to dissipate, so only the sound is carried across. He offers a practical case for why this still counts as genuine practice, not superstition: while reciting, the body sits or walks still and so commits no wrongdoing; the mouth repeats the syllables without interruption and so speaks no wrongdoing; the mind holds the mantra without scattering and so intends no wrongdoing — three karmic actions purified together, whatever the syllables mean.

He then tells a story to make the point that understanding the words is not the mechanism. A penniless drifter once passed himself off as a prince in a foreign land and was married to its princess; well-fed and comfortable, he grew short-tempered over trifles. A merchant who knew his real background — but wished to spare him public shame — taught the princess a short verse to recite, in sound only, whenever his temper flared: "No kin in this land, deceiver of everyone, coarse food your daily fare — why trouble yourself to rage?" Not understanding a word of it, she recited it anyway the next time he grew angry — and he, recognizing only that his secret had somehow been found out, was overcome with dread and never lost his temper again. The princess never learned what she had said; it worked all the same. Yuanying closes by quoting Hanshan Deqing: a mantra is not meant to be intellectually parsed but "recited straight through, in silence" — its speed of effect lying exactly in that surrender of the need to understand. "In the surging ocean of suffering, prajñā is the vessel; in the long, dark night, prajñā is the lamp."

Self-Check

Ten questions. Click an answer to see immediate feedback. No score is recorded — this is for your own checking.

Question 1 of 10
What does "Heart" (心, hṛdaya) mean in the title of this sūtra?
Correct: C. Hṛdaya means the pith, the essence — "the heart of the matter." The Heart Sūtra compresses the enormous Prajñāpāramitā collection (up to 100,000 verses) into ~260 characters. It is short because it is concentrated, not because it is introductory.
Question 2 of 10
Who speaks the Heart Sūtra, and to whom?
Correct: B. That the deepest statement of wisdom is spoken by the embodiment of compassion — and taught to the master of analysis, Śāriputra — is the whole Mahāyāna in miniature: wisdom and compassion are not two separate projects.
Question 3 of 10
"Form is emptiness, emptiness is form." What does emptiness (空, śūnyatā) actually mean here?
Correct: D. Emptiness is not nothingness. A wave is real, yet has no separate "wave-essence" apart from ocean, wind, and shore. Emptiness is the openness that makes change, relationship, and freedom possible. Reading it as "nothing exists" is the error of nihilism, which the tradition explicitly rejects.
Question 4 of 10
The sūtra runs the equation both ways — "form is emptiness" and "emptiness is form." Why does the second half matter?
Correct: A. If the sūtra only said "form is emptiness," a reader might imagine emptiness as a truer reality lurking behind the world. Running it the other way — "emptiness is form" — blocks that. There is no void behind appearances. The two are one situation seen truly.
Question 5 of 10
What are the five aggregates (五蘊) that Avalokiteśvara "clearly saw to be empty"?
Correct: C. The five aggregates (pañca-skandha) are the complete inventory of what a "person" is made of — one part material (form) and four parts mental (feeling, perception, formations, consciousness). The sūtra declares all five empty, so that mind is included with matter. Nothing is exempt.
Question 6 of 10
The sūtra says there is "no suffering, no origin, no cessation, no path" — negating the Four Noble Truths. What is it doing?
Correct: B. The sūtra is not rejecting the Four Noble Truths; it assumes you have taken them seriously. It forbids clinging to them as fixed final things, which quietly recreates the self-grasping they were meant to dissolve. A raft carries you across the river — you don't then strap it to your back and carry it up the mountain.
Question 7 of 10
"No wisdom and no attainment" (無智亦無得) is the most audacious negation. What does it pull the ground out from under?
Correct: D. Even the goal of the whole enterprise — wisdom, attainment — is emptied. This dismantles the subtle grasping that turns the spiritual path into one more acquisition. "Because there is nothing to attain," the bodhisattva can practise freely, without the grasping that recreates the very self it seeks to release.
Question 8 of 10
The sūtra's emotional core: "the mind is without obstruction; because it is unobstructed, there is no fear" (心無罣礙…無有恐怖). What is the logic linking emptiness to fearlessness?
Correct: A. 罣礙 literally means "getting hung up, caught." Fear requires a self to be threatened and a threat held as solid. When wisdom sees neither has the fixed, separate existence we assign it, the mind stops snagging. This is not numbness — it is the fearlessness of someone who has stopped clutching. It is why the sūtra is chanted before surgery and at the edge of death.
Question 9 of 10
The mantra gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā literally means:
Correct: C. The mantra is a going-across: from this shore of grasping and fear to the far shore of freedom. Ending in a mantra is not decoration — having shown that concepts cannot finally grasp reality, the sūtra closes by handing you something that is not a concept at all: a sound to be practised, not a proposition to be believed.
Question 10 of 10
"If everything is empty, why get out of bed? Doesn't emptiness make life meaningless?"
Correct: B. Emptiness does the opposite of draining meaning. The people you love, the work you do, the pain you feel are all real and vivid — none of it frozen or separate from everything else. Seeing emptiness is precisely what lets you love and act without the desperation of trying to make things last forever.
