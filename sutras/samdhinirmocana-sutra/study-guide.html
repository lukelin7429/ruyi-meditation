How to Read the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra

This guide gives students a map of the completed Ru-Yi English translation: how the eight chapters unfold, what Yogācāra questions they answer, and which terms carry the doctrinal weight of the sūtra.

The Sūtra's Movement

The Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra does not begin with abstract doctrine. It begins with a purified Buddha-field and a gathering of bodhisattvas, then lets each major teaching arise through a question. That structure matters: the sūtra is not simply listing Yogācāra ideas, but showing why each teaching is needed to remove a particular confusion.

Reality

Chapters 2 through 5 explain how the ultimate truth is beyond language, why the Three Natures are needed, and how "all dharmas have no own-nature" should be understood without falling into denial.

Practice

Chapter 6 turns doctrine into yoga: śamatha, vipaśyanā, meditative images, consciousness-only, emptiness, and the path of direct realization.

Completion

Chapters 7 and 8 map the bodhisattva grounds, the perfections, buddhahood, and the Tathāgata's accomplished work.

Chapter Path

1. Introductory Chapter

The stage is set in a purified Buddha-field. The assembly shows that what follows is a Great Vehicle teaching for those able to ask deep questions.

Read chapter 1

2. Ultimate Truth

The ultimate truth is apart from language, beyond ordinary thought, neither simply the same as nor different from conditioned phenomena, and one taste throughout all dharmas.

Read chapter 2

3. Mind and Consciousness

The Buddha introduces the ādāna-consciousness and ālayavijñāna as the subtle continuity that holds seeds, appropriates the body, and supports the six active consciousnesses.

Read chapter 3

4. The Three Natures

The discriminated, dependent, and perfectly accomplished characteristics explain how language, dependent arising, and realized purity relate without being collapsed into one another.

Read chapter 4

5. Naturelessness

The sūtra explains the three naturelessnesses and why the teaching of no own-nature is definitive only when its hidden intent is understood.

Read chapter 5

6. Yogic Practice

Maitreya asks how bodhisattvas cultivate śamatha and vipaśyanā, recognize meditative images as consciousness-only, and use emptiness to remove characteristics.

Read chapter 6

7. Grounds and Perfections

Avalokiteśvara asks about the bodhisattva grounds, the six and ten perfections, their purities, latent tendencies, and the hidden intent of one vehicle.

Read chapter 7

8. Tathāgata's Work

Mañjuśrī asks about the dharma-body, transformation body, speech, mind, pure lands, and how the Tathāgata's accomplished work benefits beings.

Read chapter 8

Core Doctrinal Thread

The sūtra's center is not the slogan "everything is mind" or "everything is empty." Its center is the disciplined clarification of what the Buddha means when different teachings seem to point in different directions. It protects emptiness from nihilism, consciousness-only from reification, and the one vehicle from flattening the differences among capacities.

Hidden IntentThe Buddha sometimes speaks in a compressed or indirect way. This sūtra "unravels" that intent so students do not cling to words while missing the meaning.
The Three NaturesThe discriminated nature is the projected structure of naming and grasping; the dependent nature is conditioned arising; the perfectly accomplished nature is the absence of the imagined in the dependent.
Three NaturelessnessesNo own-nature is not a single blanket denial. The sūtra distinguishes naturelessness of characteristic, naturelessness of arising, and naturelessness in the ultimate sense.
Consciousness-OnlyIn meditation, the image that appears is not apart from the mind that knows it. The sūtra uses this to train practitioners away from grasping objects as independently established.
YogaYoga here means disciplined integration: hearing and thinking the Dharma, cultivating śamatha and vipaśyanā, directly seeing suchness, and removing even subtle attachment to characteristics.

Suggested Reading Order

For first reading, move through the chapters in order. For study groups, read chapters 2, 4, and 5 together as the doctrinal core, then chapter 6 as the practice manual, and chapters 7 and 8 as the path and fruition. Students already familiar with the Cheng Weishi Lun may want to keep its terminology nearby, because this sūtra supplies the scriptural foundation for many later Yogācāra formulations.
