A Name Used to Erase Names

Part 2 opens the treatise's "part of explanation" (解釋分), the longest of its five parts, and with it the section Fazang calls "revealing the true meaning" (顯示正義). The One Mind named in Part 1 is now said to open onto two gates — mind as suchness and mind as arising-and-ceasing — and the first of the two is taken up here: what it is not, in a chain of negations pushed further than most readers expect, and then, more surprisingly, what it positively is.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 2 of 10 complete Source: Taishō T1666, 大乘起信論 (Aśvaghoṣa, tr. Paramārtha) Reading notes after Fazang's 大乘起信論義記 (T1846)

Two Gates, Each Holding Everything

Chinese Source

次說解釋分。

解釋分有三種。云何為三？一者、顯示正義，二者、對治邪執，三者、分別發趣道相。

顯示正義者，依一心法，有二種門。云何為二？一者、心真如門，二者、心生滅門。是二種門，皆各總攝一切法。此義云何？以是二門不相離故。

心真如者，即是一法界大總相法門體。所謂心性不生不滅，一切諸法唯依妄念而有差別，若離妄念則無一切境界之相。

是故一切法從本已來，離言說相、離名字相、離心緣相，畢竟平等、無有變異、不可破壞。唯是一心，故名真如，以一切言說假名無實，但隨妄念不可得故。

言真如者，亦無有相。謂言說之極因言遣言，此真如體無有可遣，以一切法悉皆真故；亦無可立，以一切法皆同如故。當知一切法不可說、不可念故，名為真如。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the part of explanation.

The part of explanation has three sections. What are the three? First, revealing the true meaning. Second, countering wrong attachments. Third, distinguishing the marks of the path bodhisattvas advance toward.

As for revealing the true meaning: in dependence on the one dharma of the mind, there are two gates. What are the two? First, the gate of mind as suchness. Second, the gate of mind as arising-and-ceasing. Each of these two gates includes within itself the whole of all dharmas. Why is this? Because these two gates are not separate from one another.

Mind as suchness: this is precisely the single dharma-realm, the essence of the great all-inclusive dharma-gate. It is the nature of mind, neither arising nor ceasing. All dharmas have distinctions only in dependence on false thought; apart from false thought there is no mark of any object-realm at all.

Therefore all dharmas, from the very outset, are apart from the mark of verbal expression, apart from the mark of names and words, and apart from the mark of mental objects — ultimately equal, without change, indestructible. They are only the one mind; hence it is called suchness. For all verbal expression is a provisional name without reality, following only false thought, and cannot be apprehended.

Yet even to speak of "suchness" is itself without a mark. It is the outermost limit of verbal expression, where words are used to dispel words. This essence of suchness has nothing that can be dispelled, since all dharmas are, every one, true; nor does it have anything that can be established, since all dharmas are, every one, identical with suchness. One should understand that, because all dharmas can neither be spoken of nor thought, they are named suchness.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang's gloss on "one mind, two gates" makes explicit what Part 1's reading note already anticipated: 一心者，謂一如來藏心含於二義 — the one mind is the one tathāgatagarbha-mind, containing two aspects within it. One is 約體絕相義 — the aspect of essence cut off from all marks — which is the suchness gate: "非染非淨，非生非滅，不動不轉，平等一味，性無差別，眾生即涅槃，不待滅也，凡夫彌勒同一際也" (not defiled, not pure, not arising, not ceasing, unmoving, unchanging, one uniform taste, a nature without distinction — sentient beings already are nirvāṇa, with no further cessation to wait for; an ordinary person and Maitreya himself share the identical limit). The other is 隨緣起滅義 — the aspect of arising and ceasing in dependence on conditions — which is the arising-and-ceasing gate. Fazang then makes a move worth sitting with: even the unmoving aspect, he says, belongs within the arising-and-ceasing gate too, "只由不動能成染淨，是故不動亦在動門" — it is only because it does not move that it is able to bring about both defilement and purity, and for that very reason the unmoving belongs inside the moving gate as well. The two gates are not a wall between the unconditioned and the conditioned; the unconditioned is what makes the conditioned possible from the inside.

On why each gate can "include the whole of all dharmas," Fazang reaches for the same simile twice, once per gate: the suchness gate is "染淨通相" (the shared, generic mark running through both defilement and purity), so that everything defiled or pure is already an instance of it — "如微塵是瓦器通相，通相之外無別瓦器，瓦器皆為微塵所攝" (just as dust is the generic substance of every clay vessel; apart from that generic substance there is no separate vessel, so every vessel is already included within the dust). The arising-and-ceasing gate, conversely, is 染淨別相 — the particular, differentiated mark — but since it is nothing other than suchness combined with conditions, "諸法既無異體，還攝真如門也，以瓦器收微塵等" (since the resulting dharmas have no separate essence of their own, this gate in turn includes the suchness gate — as the vessel, in its own way, gathers up the dust). Dust and vessel include each other completely, from opposite directions; that, for Fazang, is what "not separate" (不相離) actually means.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang treats the closing move — naming "suchness" only to say the name itself has no mark — as a real problem the treatise is aware it is creating, not a rhetorical flourish. He first forestalls an obvious objection: a careless reader might think the treatise has just contradicted itself, having said a moment earlier that all dharmas are apart from "the mark of names and words," only to introduce the name 真如 in the very next breath. His answer is that a name is only a name — 假名非實，不相違也 (a provisional label is not a real thing, so there is no contradiction). He then explains why this particular name was chosen rather than some other placeholder: citing the Mahāyānasaṃgraha's scheme of ten names for ultimate reality, he notes that 真如 is listed tenth and last — 真如名是第十究竟名 — the final name in the series, the one beyond which no further name is offered. And he supplies an image for what "using words to dispel words" (因言遣言) is actually doing: striking a bell to call for silence. "如打靜聲，若無此聲則不止餘聲，若為存此聲數數打靜，即自喧故，亦非止聲" — without striking the bell at all, no other noise would ever stop; but if the bell-sound itself is clung to and struck over and over, it becomes just one more noise, and silence is never actually reached. 真如 is that single, necessary strike — spoken once, to bring speech itself to rest, not to be mistaken for the silence it points toward.

How to Accord with What Cannot Be Grasped

Chinese Source

問曰：「若如是義者，諸眾生等云何隨順而能得入？」

答曰：「若知一切法雖說，無有能說可說，雖念，亦無能念可念，是名隨順。若離於念，名為得入。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: "If the meaning is as stated, how can sentient beings accord with it and gain entry?"

Answer: "To know that, although all dharmas are spoken of, there is nothing that speaks and nothing spoken of, and that although they are thought of, there is nothing that thinks and nothing thought of — this is called according with it. To be apart from thought altogether is called gaining entry."

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang reads this short exchange as marking the boundary between a preliminary practice and the real thing. "Knowing" that speech and thought have no real speaker or thinker, while one is still, in fact, speaking and thinking — 雖在於彼言念等中，觀此念等常無能所，雖未能離念，而順於無念 (still caught up in speech and thought of this kind, yet observing that this very speech and thought never really has a subject or object; not yet able to leave thought behind, but already according with no-thought) — this he calls 方便觀, the expedient contemplation. Only when this observation, sustained long enough, actually leaves thought behind and touches the principle of no-thought itself does it become 正觀, the correct contemplation proper — which is what the answer means by "gaining entry" (得入). He is careful to keep the two from collapsing into each other: according-with is not yet entry, but neither is it nothing; it is the only door entry has.

Fazang also flags the danger this passage is built to avoid on both sides at once. To think "there is no speaker, no thinker" and stop there risks nihilism — the extinction of speech and thought altogether, 斷見. To go on speaking and thinking exactly as before, unexamined, risks the opposite error, 常見, mistaking the ordinary flow of discursive mind for something solid and continuous. "於一念間離此二見，見此無二之法，故能稱順中道，隨順法性也" — in a single moment of mind, apart from both these views, to see this dharma of non-duality: that alone is what properly accords with the middle way, and with the nature of dharmas itself. The instruction is not to stop thinking, but to keep thinking while no longer believing in the thinker.

Empty, and Not Empty

Chinese Source

復次，真如者，依言說分別有二種義。云何為二？一者、如實空，以能究竟顯實故。二者、如實不空，以有自體，具足無漏性功德故。

所言空者，從本已來一切染法不相應故，謂離一切法差別之相，以無虛妄心念故。

當知真如自性，非有相、非無相、非非有相、非非無相、非有無俱相，非一相、非異相、非非一相、非非異相、非一異俱相。

乃至總說，依一切眾生以有妄心念念分別，皆不相應故說為空，若離妄心實無可空故。

所言不空者，已顯法體空無妄故，即是真心常恒不變淨法滿足，故名不空，亦無有相可取，以離念境界唯證相應故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, suchness, considered by way of verbal distinction, has two aspects. What are the two? First, suchness as empty in reality, because it is able to reveal reality to the utmost. Second, suchness as not empty in reality, because it has its own essence, complete with the immaculate qualities of its own nature.

By "empty" is meant that, from the very outset, it is not conjoined with any defiled dharma at all — that is, it is apart from the marks of distinction among all dharmas, because it is without any false thought of mind.

One should understand that the own-nature of suchness is: not the mark of existence, not the mark of non-existence, not the mark of neither existence nor non-existence, not the mark of both existence and non-existence together; not the mark of oneness, not the mark of difference, not the mark of neither oneness nor difference, not the mark of both oneness and difference together.

In sum: because all sentient beings, having false minds, discriminate thought after thought, none of these marks apply, and so suchness is spoken of as empty; but were false mind itself set aside, there would in reality be nothing left to call empty.

By "not empty" is meant that, the essence of the dharma having already been shown to be empty of falsehood, it is precisely the true mind, constant and unchanging, complete with pure dharmas — and so it is called not empty. Yet it likewise has no mark that can be grasped, since it is an object-realm apart from thought, accessible only to realization itself.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang catches a grammatical trap in the phrase 如實空 before a reader can fall into it: "此以如實之中空無妄染故，云如實空，非謂如實自空" — this means empty within reality, of false defilement; it does not mean that reality itself is empty. He marks it explicitly as a 依主釋, a "belonging-to" compound: not "the real is empty" but "the emptiness that belongs to the real" — emptiness of defilement, not emptiness of suchness itself. For "not empty," he distinguishes two senses in turn: 異妄無體故云有自體 (unlike falsehood, which has no essence of its own, suchness does have its own essence) and 異恒沙有漏煩惱故云具足無漏性功德 (unlike the afflictions numerous as the sands of the Ganges, it is complete with immaculate qualities). He backs both with citation — the Mahāyānasaṃgraha on the four inherent virtues (常樂我淨, permanence, bliss, self, and purity, already possessed rather than newly acquired), and a verse from the Buddha-Nature Treatise: "由客塵空故，與法界相離；無上法不空，與法界相隨" — because the guest-dust of affliction is empty, it stands apart from the dharma-realm; because the unsurpassed dharma is not empty, it accompanies the dharma-realm wherever it goes. Emptiness removes what was never really there; non-emptiness names what was never really absent.

The cascading tetralemma — not existence, not non-existence, not neither, not both; not oneness, not difference, not neither, not both — Fazang reads as a step-by-step disarming of an imagined interlocutor who keeps trying to relocate the same error at a higher level. 惑者云: hearing "not existence," the interlocutor assumes this means non-existence — corrected by "not non-existence" either. Hearing both denials, he next assumes the treatise is asserting some third, intermediate position, "both are being denied together" — corrected by "not both together" as well. Each new negation is aimed at foreclosing the specific way the previous negation was just about to be turned back into another positive claim; the pattern, on Fazang's reading, is not decoration but a trap deliberately re-set at every level a subtle reification could reappear. He cross-references the same structure in the Śataśāstravaipulya, whose own verse states the method directly: 有非有俱非，一非一雙泯，隨次而配屬，智者達非真 — existence, non-existence, both, neither; oneness, non-oneness, both together erased; matched in sequence, term by term — the wise understand that not one of these is ultimately true.

Part 2 Complete

This closes Part 2: the two gates of the One Mind are named, and the first of them, mind as suchness, has been described both negatively — apart from every verbal mark a mind could fasten onto — and positively, as empty of defilement yet not empty of its own immaculate qualities. Part 3 turns to the second gate, mind as arising-and-ceasing, where suchness first takes on a name of its own: the ālayavijñāna, "neither arising nor ceasing, conjoined with arising and ceasing" — and, within that same gate, the doctrine of original enlightenment and actualized enlightenment that will occupy Chan, Tiantai, and Huayan thought for the next thousand years.
