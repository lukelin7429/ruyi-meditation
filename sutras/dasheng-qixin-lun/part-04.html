How One Flicker of Delusion Becomes a World

Part 4 completes the treatise's account of the ālayavijñāna's two aspects. Enlightenment, taken up on the previous page as original and actualized enlightenment, is now described a second way, through four mirrors. Non-enlightenment is then introduced through the image of a traveler lost only because he trusts a direction, and unfolded into the treatise's second most influential passage: the three subtle marks and six coarse marks by which a single stirring of mind grows, stage by stage, into an entire deluded world.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 4 of 10 complete Source: Taishō T1666, 大乘起信論 (Aśvaghoṣa, tr. Paramārtha) Reading notes after Fazang's 大乘起信論義記 (T1846)

Four Mirrors

Chinese Source

復次，覺體相者，有四種大義，與虛空等，猶如淨鏡。云何為四？

一者、如實空鏡。遠離一切心境界相，無法可現，非覺照義故。二者、因熏習鏡。謂如實不空，一切世間境界悉於中現，不出不入、不失不壞，常住一心，以一切法即真實性故；又一切染法所不能染，智體不動，具足無漏熏眾生故。三者、法出離鏡。謂不空法，出煩惱礙、智礙，離和合相，淳淨明故。四者、緣熏習鏡。謂依法出離故，遍照眾生之心，令修善根，隨念示現故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, as for the essence and attributes of enlightenment, there are four great aspects, equal to space, like a bright mirror. What are the four?

First, the mirror of true emptiness: it is far removed from every mark of mind and mental object, with nothing at all appearing within it — because it is not the illuminating function of enlightenment as such. Second, the mirror of the cause of habituation: this is true non-emptiness. All worldly objects appear within it in their entirety — neither going out nor coming in, neither lost nor destroyed, forever abiding as the one mind, because all dharmas are, in themselves, its very reality; and no defiled dharma is able to defile it, the essence of wisdom remaining unmoved, complete with the immaculate power to habituate sentient beings. Third, the mirror of the dharma's liberation: this is the non-empty dharma, gone forth from the hindrance of affliction and the hindrance to knowledge, apart from the mark of conjoining — wholly pure and bright. Fourth, the mirror of habituation as condition: in dependence on the dharma's liberation, it universally illumines the minds of sentient beings, causing them to cultivate roots of goodness, manifesting in accordance with their thought.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang sorts the four mirrors along two crossing axes rather than treating them as an unordered list. The first pair are 自性淨 (intrinsically pure) and describe the stage while the cause is still hidden; the second pair are 離垢淨 (pure through the removal of defilement) and describe the stage once the fruit has become manifest. Independently, the first and third share an emphasis on emptiness, while the second and fourth share an emphasis on the mirror's active function — reflecting worldly appearances, or radiating outward to sentient beings. On the first mirror, he gives two distinct reasons nothing appears in it, one from each direction: 一以妄念望於真智，無覺照之功，以情執違理故 — looking from delusion toward true wisdom, delusion has no power to illuminate anything, because clinging to feeling directly contradicts principle, the way a mirror is not identical with the external object it reflects; 二以本覺望於妄法，亦無覺照功能，以妄本無故 — looking from original enlightenment toward deluded phenomena, there is likewise nothing to illuminate, because the deluded phenomena were never really there to begin with, like a healthy eye looking for a floater in clear space and finding nothing to see.

He also catches an apparent contradiction between the first mirror and the second worth noting: if nothing at all can appear in true emptiness, how can the very next mirror say that "all worldly objects appear within it in their entirety"? His answer distinguishes two different senses of appearing — 彼約依他似法，是此真心隨熏所作，無自體故，不異真如 (the second mirror concerns dependent, apparent phenomena, which are what this true mind produces under habituation, having no essence of their own and so not different from suchness) versus 此約遍計所執實法故，無可現也 (the first mirror concerns purely imagined, falsely-grasped-as-real phenomena — a self, above all — which have nowhere to appear because they were never dependently arisen at all). The distinction matters practically: the treatise is not claiming the phenomenal world disappears once delusion is seen through, only that a fictional self superimposed on that world does. On why a mirror's purity is proven, not compromised, by reflecting something dirty, Fazang adds one more line worth keeping: 如鏡明淨能現穢物，穢物現時反顯鏡淨，豈此穢物能污鏡耶 — a bright, clean mirror is able to reflect a filthy object; and the very appearance of that filth, far from soiling the mirror, is what proves the mirror clean in the first place. How could the filth it merely reflects possibly stain it?

The Lost Traveler, and Nine Marks of Delusion

Chinese Source

所言不覺義者，謂不如實知真如法一故，不覺心起而有其念，念無自相不離本覺，猶如迷人依方故迷，若離於方則無有迷。眾生亦爾，依覺故迷，若離覺性則無不覺，以有不覺妄想心故，能知名義為說真覺。若離不覺之心，則無真覺自相可說。

復次，依不覺故生三種相，與彼不覺相應不離。云何為三？一者、無明業相。以依不覺故心動，說名為業；覺則不動。動則有苦，果不離因故。二者、能見相。以依動故能見；不動則無見。三者、境界相。以依能見故境界妄現；離見則無境界。

以有境界緣故，復生六種相。云何為六？一者、智相。依於境界，心起分別愛與不愛故。二者相續相。依於智故生其苦樂，覺心起念相應不斷故。三者、執取相。依於相續緣念境界，住持苦樂，心起著故。四者、計名字相。依於妄執，分別假名言相故。五者、起業相。依於名字，尋名取著，造種種業故。六者、業繫苦相。以依業受果不自在故。

當知無明能生一切染法，以一切染法皆是不覺相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

By "the aspect of non-enlightenment" is meant that, because one does not truly know the single dharma of suchness as it really is, unenlightened mind stirs and thought arises within it. This thought has no mark of its own, and is never apart from original enlightenment — just as a lost person is lost only in dependence on direction; apart from direction, there would be no being lost at all. Sentient beings are just the same: it is in dependence on enlightenment that they are deluded; apart from the nature of enlightenment, there would be no non-enlightenment. And it is precisely because there is the deluded, discriminating mind of non-enlightenment that one is able to know the meaning of names and speak of true enlightenment. Apart from the mind of non-enlightenment, there would be no mark of true enlightenment left to speak of, either.

Furthermore, in dependence on non-enlightenment, three kinds of mark arise, corresponding to that non-enlightenment and never apart from it. What are the three? First, the karmic mark of ignorance. Because, in dependence on non-enlightenment, mind stirs, this is called karma; were there enlightenment, there would be no stirring. Stirring brings suffering, since the effect is never apart from its cause. Second, the mark of the perceiving subject. In dependence on stirring, there is the capacity to perceive; without stirring, there would be no perceiving. Third, the mark of the perceived realm. In dependence on the perceiving subject, an object-realm falsely appears; apart from perceiving, there would be no object-realm.

Because there is the condition of an object-realm, six further kinds of mark are then produced. What are the six? First, the mark of discernment. In dependence on the object-realm, mind gives rise to discrimination between what is liked and what is not liked. Second, the mark of continuity. In dependence on discernment, pain and pleasure are produced, and the aware mind gives rise to thought corresponding to them without break. Third, the mark of grasping. In dependence on continuity, thought clings to the object-realm, sustaining pain and pleasure, and mind gives rise to attachment. Fourth, the mark of conceptualizing names. In dependence on false grasping, mind discriminates the marks of provisional names and words. Fifth, the mark of initiating karma. In dependence on names, mind pursues names and clings to them, creating all manner of karma. Sixth, the mark of suffering bound by karma. In dependence on karma, one undergoes its effects, without freedom.

One should understand that ignorance is able to give rise to every defiled dharma, since every defiled dharma is, without exception, a mark of non-enlightenment.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang glosses the whole nine-mark sequence — the three subtle marks plus the six coarse marks — through a single extended allegory of an eye disease, mapping each mark to one stage of the same unfolding hallucination: 本覺真如其猶淨眼，熱翳之氣如根本無明，翳與眼合動彼淨眼，業識亦爾 (original enlightenment and suchness are like a clear, healthy eye; the heat and blur of disease are like fundamental ignorance; the blur joining the eye and disturbing its clarity is exactly the karmic mark, the first of the three subtle marks). 由淨眼動故有病眼起，能見相亦爾 (the healthy eye's disturbance gives rise to the diseased eye's activity — this is the mark of the perceiving subject). 以有病眼向外觀故，即有空華妄境界現，境界相亦爾 (because the diseased eye looks outward, there falsely appears a floater in empty space — this is the mark of the perceived realm). From there the six coarse marks follow the same floater outward: 以有空華境故，令其起心分別好華惡華等，智相亦爾 (the floater's mere appearance provokes the mind to judge it a good floater or a bad one — the mark of discernment); 由此分別堅執不改，相續相亦爾 (that judgment, held onto without change, is the mark of continuity); 由執定故於違順境取捨追遣，執取相亦爾 (fixed in that judgment, one pursues what pleases and pushes away what does not — the mark of grasping); 由取相故於上復立名字…計名字相亦爾 (grasping at the mark, one then assigns it a name — the mark of conceptualizing names); 既計名取相，發動身口，攀此空華造善惡業受苦樂報，長眠生死而不能脫，皆由根本無明力也 (having conceived the name and grasped the mark, body and speech are set in motion, and one goes on creating wholesome and unwholesome karma over this same floater, sleeping long in birth-and-death, unable to wake — all of it powered by nothing more than the original force of fundamental ignorance). A single blur in a healthy eye, followed step by step, becomes an entire lifetime's worth of craving and aversion — and, Fazang's allegory insists, there was never a real floater there to begin with.

Fazang also organizes the six coarse marks into three functional pairs rather than leaving them as a flat list of six. The first pair, discernment and continuity, is the subtlest form of affliction still operating within the "differentiating" consciousness, clinging to the object and the dharma as though each were real — the level a bodhisattva already on the grounds is working to eliminate. The second pair, grasping and conceptualizing names, is the coarser affliction built on top of the first — clinging further, in a self-and-object structure — which the two vehicles' own practice is aimed at cutting through. The third pair, initiating karma and suffering bound by karma, is not a new kind of affliction at all but simply what the first two pairs produce once acted upon: karma created, and its fruit endured without freedom. Three subtle marks belong entirely to the ālayavijñāna, before subject and object have even separated out — as the *Treatise Without Signs* puts it of the karmic mark alone, 相及境界不可分別，一體無異 (mark and object-realm cannot yet be distinguished; they are one and the same, without difference). The six coarse marks belong to the "consciousness that discriminates objects" one level further out — which is why, Fazang notes, the treatise does not mention the seventh consciousness, manas, by name anywhere in this ninefold scheme: manas is already necessarily implied wherever the ālaya and the discriminating consciousness are both present, so naming it separately here would add nothing the structure does not already require.

Where Enlightenment and Non-Enlightenment Meet, and Where They Part

Chinese Source

復次，覺與不覺有二種相。云何為二？一者、同相，二者、異相。

同相者，譬如種種瓦器皆同微塵性相，如是無漏無明種種業幻皆同真如性相。是故修多羅中依於此真如義故，說一切眾生本來常住入於涅槃，菩提之法非可修相、非可作相，畢竟無得，亦無色相可見；而有見色相者，唯是隨染業幻所作，非是智色不空之性，以智相無可見故。異相者，如種種瓦器各各不同，如是無漏無明隨染幻差別，性染幻差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, enlightenment and non-enlightenment have two aspects. What are the two? First, the aspect of identity. Second, the aspect of difference.

The aspect of identity: it is like how every kind of clay vessel shares the same nature of dust. Just so, the undefiled and ignorance, in all their manifold karmic apparitions, share the same nature of suchness. This is why the sūtras, on the basis of this meaning of suchness, say that all sentient beings have, from the very outset, already entered constantly-abiding nirvāṇa; that the dharma of bodhi has no mark that can be cultivated, no mark that can be produced, and is, in the end, nothing to be attained, with no form or mark to be seen at all. And where a form or mark is seen, this is nothing but what the apparitions of defiled karma have produced — it is not the not-empty nature of wisdom's own form, since the mark of wisdom itself has nothing that can be seen. The aspect of difference: it is like how every kind of clay vessel is, each one, different from the rest. Just so, the undefiled and ignorance follow the distinctions of their defiled apparitions, since their natures, as defiled apparitions, are distinct.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang reaches back for the identical clay-and-dust simile Part 2 already used for the two gates of the One Mind, applying it here one level further in: 染淨二法同以真如為體，真如以此二法為相 — the two dharmas, defiled and pure, share suchness as their single essence, and suchness, in turn, takes these same two dharmas as its own attributes. He is careful to note that "identity" and "difference" are not competing claims about the same thing but two separate lenses, applied to two separate targets: the identity aspect concerns 本末不覺 — original enlightenment and non-enlightenment traced back to their shared root — while a moment later he clarifies the difference aspect concerns something else again, 隨染幻差別 — the specific, varied way delusion happens to manifest in any given case. A given sentient being's particular confusion is never identical to another's in its texture, even though, traced to the root, both are exactly the same suchness wearing a different apparition.

On why seeing any color or form at all is, by this logic, already evidence of delusion rather than of realization, Fazang connects this passage directly back to the third mirror of the previous section: true wisdom's own nature — what the third mirror called 法出離鏡, the mirror of the dharma's liberation — is formless by definition, so any visible shape or color that does appear to an observer is necessarily the product of that observer's own residual defiled karma projecting an image, never wisdom's own substance showing itself directly. This is a thread the treatise will pick up explicitly several pages ahead, when it distinguishes the buddha-body ordinary beings and the two vehicles are capable of seeing from the one only advanced bodhisattvas can perceive — the same principle, that a body seen in form is already a concession to the seer's own remaining delusion, applied there to the doctrine of the buddha-bodies directly.

Part 4 Complete

This closes Part 4, and with it the treatise's full account of the ālayavijñāna's twofold nature — enlightenment, described through original and actualized enlightenment and now through four mirrors, and non-enlightenment, unfolded from a single stirring of mind into three subtle marks, six coarse marks, and the entire deluded world those nine marks jointly produce. Part 5 turns from what the ālayavijñāna is to how it operates: the causes and conditions of mind, will, and consciousness, culminating in the treatise's demonstration that the three realms of existence are nothing but mind.
