Five Ways to Misread Everything Just Said

Part 8 is the treatise's shortest major section: "countering wrong attachments" (對治邪執), the second of the three parts of explanation. Having spent seven pages describing the tathāgatagarbha in positive terms — complete with qualities, capable of manifesting form, never separate from suchness — the treatise now anticipates five specific ways a careful reader might misread exactly that language, corrects a sixth error proper to practitioners of the two vehicles, and closes by dissolving even the positive vocabulary it has just spent so much effort defending.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 8 of 10 complete Source: Taishō T1666, 大乘起信論 (Aśvaghoṣa, tr. Paramārtha) Reading notes after Fazang's 大乘起信論義記 (T1846)

Five Ways to Misread the Tathāgatagarbha

Chinese Source

對治邪執者，一切邪執皆依我見，若離於我則無邪執。是我見有二種。云何為二？一者、人我見，二者、法我見。人我見者，依諸凡夫說有五種。云何為五？

一者、聞修多羅說如來法身畢竟寂寞猶如虛空，以不知為破著故，即謂虛空是如來性。云何對治？明虛空相是其妄法、體無不實，以對色故有，是可見相令心生滅。以一切色法本來是心，實無外色。若無色者，則無虛空之相。所謂一切境界唯心妄起故有，若心離於妄動則一切境界滅，唯一真心無所不遍。此謂如來廣大性智究竟之義，非如虛空相故。

二者、聞修多羅說世間諸法畢竟體空，乃至涅槃真如之法亦畢竟空，從本已來自空離一切相。以不知為破著故，即謂真如、涅槃之性唯是其空。云何對治？明真如法身自體不空，具足無量性功德故。

三者、聞修多羅說如來之藏無有增減，體備一切功德之法。以不解故，即謂如來之藏有色心法自相差別。云何對治？以唯依真如義說故，因生滅染義示現說差別故。

四者、聞修多羅說一切世間生死染法皆依如來藏而有，一切諸法不離真如。以不解故，謂如來藏自體具有一切世間生死等法。云何對治？以如來藏從本已來唯有過恒沙等諸淨功德，不離、不斷、不異真如義故。以過恒沙等煩惱染法，唯是妄有，性自本無，從無始世來未曾與如來藏相應故。若如來藏體有妄法，而使證會永息妄者，則無是處故。

五者、聞修多羅說依如來藏故有生死，依如來藏故得涅槃。以不解故，謂眾生有始。以見始故，復謂如來所得涅槃，有其終盡，還作眾生。云何對治？以如來藏無前際故，無明之相亦無有始。若說三界外更有眾生始起者，即是外道經說。又如來藏無有後際，諸佛所得涅槃與之相應則無後際故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for countering wrong attachments: all wrong attachments depend on the view of a self; apart from a self, there would be no wrong attachment. This view of a self is of two kinds. What are the two? First, the view of a self of persons. Second, the view of a self of dharmas. As for the view of a self of persons: this is spoken of, in dependence on ordinary people, as being of five kinds. What are the five?

First, hearing the sūtras say that the Tathāgata's dharma-body is, in the end, quiescent, like space itself, and not understanding that this is said in order to break attachment, one takes space itself to be the Tathāgata's own nature. How is this countered? By making clear that the mark of space is itself a false dharma, its essence having no reality — it exists only in relation to form, and is a mark that can be seen, causing mind to arise and cease. Because all form is, in reality, from the very outset, mind itself; there is, in truth, no form external to it. And if there is no form, then there is no mark of space either. That is: every object-realm exists only because mind falsely stirs and gives rise to it; if mind is apart from false stirring, then every object-realm ceases, and only the single true mind remains, pervading everywhere without exception. This is what is meant by the ultimate meaning of the Tathāgata's vast, intrinsic wisdom — it is not at all like the mark of space.

Second, hearing the sūtras say that all worldly dharmas are, in the end, empty in their essence, and that even the dharma of nirvāṇa and suchness itself is likewise, in the end, empty — empty of itself, apart from every mark, from the very outset — and not understanding that this is said in order to break attachment, one takes the very nature of suchness and nirvāṇa to be nothing but emptiness. How is this countered? By making clear that suchness, the dharma-body, is not empty in its own essence, being complete with immeasurable qualities of its own nature.

Third, hearing the sūtras say that the tathāgatagarbha neither increases nor decreases, its essence complete with the dharma of every quality, and not understanding this, one takes the tathāgatagarbha to have, within its own nature, the distinct marks of both form and mind as separate dharmas. How is this countered? Because this is spoken only in terms of the meaning of suchness itself — differentiation is shown and spoken of only by way of the meaning of defiled arising-and-ceasing.

Fourth, hearing the sūtras say that all the defiled dharmas of birth-and-death in the world exist entirely in dependence on the tathāgatagarbha, and that all dharmas are never apart from suchness, and not understanding this, one supposes that the tathāgatagarbha's own essence itself possesses, within it, all the dharmas of worldly birth-and-death. How is this countered? Because the tathāgatagarbha, from the very outset, has only pure qualities as numerous as the sands of the Ganges — never separate, never interrupted, never different from the meaning of suchness itself. The afflictions and defiled dharmas, numerous as the sands of the Ganges, are merely falsely existent; their nature is, from the very beginning, itself nonexistent, and from beginningless time they have never once corresponded with the tathāgatagarbha. Were the essence of the tathāgatagarbha itself to contain false dharmas, then it would be impossible for realization ever to bring about the eternal cessation of falsehood.

Fifth, hearing the sūtras say that it is in dependence on the tathāgatagarbha that there is birth-and-death, and it is in dependence on the tathāgatagarbha that nirvāṇa is attained, and not understanding this, one supposes that sentient beings had a beginning. And, having supposed a beginning, one further supposes that the nirvāṇa a tathāgata attains must likewise come to an end, becoming a sentient being once again. How is this countered? Because the tathāgatagarbha has no prior limit, the mark of ignorance likewise has no beginning. Should anyone claim that sentient beings first arose somewhere outside the three realms, this would be nothing but the teaching of a non-Buddhist scripture. And because the tathāgatagarbha likewise has no later limit, the nirvāṇa the buddhas attain, corresponding to it, has no later limit either.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang anticipates the objection a careful reader is likely to have with this whole section's label before proceeding: these five errors are all mistakes about suchness and the tathāgatagarbha, not obviously about "a self" at all — so why call them "the view of a self of persons"? He offers two resolutions rather than one. First, simply that it is beginning Mahāyāna practitioners who still retain some residual sense of self who commit these particular errors — the label names who is doing the misreading, not what the misreading is about. Second, more precisely: 由如來藏中有二義，一是本覺義，即是當人，於上妄計，故云人執；二是理實義，當所觀之法，今據初義故說人執 — the tathāgatagarbha itself has two aspects, one being original enlightenment, which corresponds to "the person" doing the (mis)speculating, the other being principle-as-it-actually-is, which corresponds to the dharma being observed; this section's label follows the first aspect, tracking the deluded subject rather than the object misunderstood.

Fazang also sorts the five errors into a pattern worth noticing: the first two are opposite overcorrections about emptiness — the first mistakes the dharma-body's quiescence for literal empty space, the second mistakes suchness's teaching of emptiness for suchness itself being nothing at all — while the remaining three are all, in different ways, errors about "having": the third mistakes the tathāgatagarbha's own qualities for ordinary form-and-mind phenomena; the fourth mistakes it as literally containing samsaric defilement as part of its own substance; the fifth mistakes it as having a temporal beginning and, consequently, nirvāṇa as having a temporal end. Read in sequence, the five errors trace a single reader's likely progression: first mistaking too little content for the tathāgatagarbha (bare space), then, correcting that, mistaking too little content again in the opposite direction (bare emptiness), then, correcting that in turn, overcorrecting into mistaking too much and too literal a content three separate ways.

Reading note — after Fazang

On the fourth error's refutation, Fazang notes the treatise deploys two distinct argumentative moves rather than one — 云何對治，先奪破，後縱破 (how is this countered: first by a direct denial, then by a conceded refutation). The direct denial simply states the tathāgatagarbha's qualities are pure, and defilement merely apparent, never really conjoined with it at all. The conceded refutation then grants the opponent's premise hypothetically, only to show it self-destructs anyway: 若如來藏體有妄法，而使證會永息妄者，則無是處故 — even supposing, for argument's sake, that the tathāgatagarbha's essence really did contain false dharmas as part of its own substance, it would then be strictly impossible for any realization ever to bring about their permanent cessation — since one cannot permanently remove what is, by hypothesis, one's own essential nature. The doctrine that buddha-nature is pure is not simply asserted here; it is defended as the only position under which liberation remains logically possible at all.

On the fifth error, Fazang identifies the specific rival teaching this passage has in view rather than leaving "the teaching of a non-Buddhist scripture" unspecified: citing the *Humane Kings Sūtra* directly, 我說三界外別有一眾生界藏者，是外道大有經中說，非七佛說也 — the claim that a separate realm of sentient beings exists outside the three realms, later entering them for the first time, is explicitly attributed there to a rival non-Buddhist scripture, not to the teaching of any of the seven buddhas. The error under correction here is not a vague or hypothetical confusion; it names, and rejects, a specific competing cosmology the treatise's own Chinese Buddhist audience would have recognized by name.

The View Proper to the Two Vehicles, and the Final Dissolution

Chinese Source

法我見者，依二乘鈍根故，如來但為說人無我。以說不究竟，見有五陰生滅之法，怖畏生死、妄取涅槃。云何對治？以五陰法自性不生則無有滅，本來涅槃故。

復次，究竟離妄執者，當知染法、淨法皆悉相待，無有自相可說，是故一切法從本已來，非色非心、非智非識、非有非無，畢竟不可說相。而有言說者，當知如來善巧方便，假以言說引導眾生，其旨趣者皆為離念歸於真如，以念一切法令心生滅不入實智故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the view of a self of dharmas: because of the dull faculties of the two vehicles, the Tathāgata taught them only the selflessness of persons. Because this teaching was not carried to its ultimate conclusion, they still perceive the dharmas of the five aggregates as really arising and ceasing, and, terrified of birth-and-death, falsely grasp at nirvāṇa. How is this countered? Because the dharmas of the five aggregates, in their own nature, never truly arise, and so, having never arisen, they never truly cease either — they are, from the very outset, nirvāṇa itself.

Furthermore, as for leaving false attachment behind altogether: one should understand that defiled dharmas and pure dharmas are both entirely mutually dependent, having no mark of their own that can be spoken of. Therefore all dharmas, from the very outset, are not form, not mind; not wisdom, not consciousness; not existent, not nonexistent — a mark that can, in the end, never be spoken of at all. And where there is, nonetheless, verbal teaching, one should understand that the Tathāgata, through skillful means, provisionally makes use of words in order to guide sentient beings — and that the aim of all such teaching is only to lead one apart from thought and back to suchness, since to think of all dharmas as real only causes mind to arise and cease, and prevents entry into true wisdom.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang marks the self-of-dharmas view as a categorically different diagnosis from the five errors just corrected — not a misreading a careless Mahāyāna student makes, but the predictable result of a teaching deliberately withheld: 依二乘鈍根故，如來但為說人無我 — the Buddha taught practitioners of the two vehicles only the selflessness of persons because of their own dull faculties, not because that teaching was complete on its own terms. The five aggregates' apparent arising and ceasing is, on this treatise's own account, exactly as unreal as the "self" the two vehicles have already successfully seen through — but seeing through the person while still believing in the reality of the aggregates that compose it leaves one, in Fazang's phrase, still afraid of birth-and-death and grasping at nirvāṇa as an escape from something one has not yet recognized was never really happening in the first place. The two vehicles' own path, on this account, is not wrong — it is simply not finished.

On the closing paragraph's return to Madhyamaka-style dialectic, Fazang traces its logic directly to Nāgārjuna: 中論云，若法因待成，緣是法還成待，今則無因待，亦無所成法等 — the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* states that if a dharma is established only by depending on something else, then that dependence, in turn, only exists in relation to the dharma it establishes; take away the dependence, and there is no established dharma left standing either. Applied here, this closes off a door the preceding five corrections might otherwise have left ajar: having spent this entire section insisting that the tathāgatagarbha really does possess positive qualities, not mere emptiness, the treatise now clarifies that even this affirmed content was never a freestanding assertion — defiled and pure dharmas define each other only by mutual contrast, "empty" only ever meaning "not this specific defilement" and "not-empty" only ever meaning "not that specific absence." Every word used across this entire treatise, including "tathāgatagarbha" itself, is, on Fazang's reading of this closing line, exactly this kind of provisional, load-bearing scaffolding — necessary for guiding a reader out of confusion, and dispensable the moment that guidance succeeds.

Part 8 Complete

This closes Part 8 and the second of the "part of explanation"'s three sections: five misreadings of the tathāgatagarbha doctrine corrected, one further misreading proper to the two vehicles corrected, and even the treatise's own positive vocabulary dissolved back into skillful means. Part 9 turns to the third and final section, "distinguishing the marks of the path bodhisattvas advance toward" — the treatise's account of how faith actually develops, stage by stage, from a beginner's first resolve through to the bodhisattva grounds this collection's reading notes have already been quietly cross-referencing for the last several pages.
