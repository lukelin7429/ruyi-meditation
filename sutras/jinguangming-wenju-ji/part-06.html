The Golden Drum, Repentance, and the Marks of the Three Bodies

This part covers the second half of the third fascicle of Zhili's Notes, following Zhiyi's Textual Commentary through the rest of the "Repentance" chapter and the "Praise" chapter of the Golden Light Sūtra. Zhili reads Faith-Mark's dream as contemplative wisdom at the stage of resemblance: the drum, its light, and the buddhas in the light signify the Dharma-, reward-, and response-bodies, each containing all three, so that the whole sūtra is "the dharma Golden Light." He then works through the verses of repentance (refuge in the Dharma-body, the repentance of the three obstructions, offerings of goods and of Dharma, the eight difficulties of evil and of good, praise, and vows), adding lists the Commentary leaves out and stories behind its allusions (the white elephant, King Wondrous Adornment, Mencius's mother). In the "Praise" chapter he explains the four siddhāntas of the title and the earth goddess as witness, then gives his longest discussion: why the thirty-two marks praised by Golden Dragon Honored are called "specially honored." Using the Awakening of Faith's two consciousnesses and the real and the middle, he argues that the birth-body, the specially honored body, and the dharma-nature body are distinguished by how capacities see them, not by the number of marks, and that for the perfect person the three bodies are interfused as the secret treasury.

Ru-Yi Translation

First, explaining "dream." The Commentary earlier explained that Faith-Mark Bodhisattva is in the stage of resemblance, either the resemblance bordering on the ultimate or the resemblance bordering on the ultimate. Seeing the golden drum in a dream in this stage — how could it be the dream of the benighted delusion of ordinary beings of the broad earth? Hence [the Commentary] explains the word "dream" with two meanings. First, it says that the samādhi is called dream-like: because one penetrates inherent inclusion, cultivated virtue is without effort, and the cause accomplishes the work of the fruit, like diligent exertion in a dream; therefore it is called the dream-like samādhi. Next, from "Further, entering contemplation" on, it takes ordinary people's having dreaming and waking as a simile for the contemplation of the dharma-nature having entering and leaving; here speech and silence are taken as leaving and entering. Both explanations show that the dream is contemplative wisdom; the first explanation is somewhat closer. Question: Dreaming is an inverted dharma deluded about the true; contemplation is a dharma of awakened wisdom realizing principle. The sūtra text says "dream"; how can the Commentary explain it as contemplative wisdom? Delusion and understanding differ as heaven [from earth]; how can they be harmonized? Answer: At the buddha-ground delusion is exhausted, and ordinary beings have not yet understood; in neither case is contemplation discussed. Understanding within delusion may be called contemplative wisdom. Hence the Awakening of Faith says, "Because of depending on the karmic consciousness, seeing the Buddha is spoken of. If one departs from the karmic consciousness, there is nothing seen." Now, clarifying resembling understanding: because it illuminates the dharma-nature, it is called entering contemplation; because it depends on the karmic consciousness, it is called a dream. While wisdom and understanding are not yet ultimate, how could it not be a dream? A dream that illuminates the dharma-nature — how is it not contemplative wisdom? Without grasping this intent, this text cannot be interpreted.

Since each of the three buddhas contains three meanings, it reveals that dharma after dharma contains three: briefly, the ten sets of three dharmas; broadly, everything is threefold. These sets of three dharmas, explained by following the simile, are the three dharmas illustrated by worldly gold, light, and brightness. If by explanation by attaching to the text or by the thing itself, all are the dharma Golden Light, because each one contains the three: the meaning of being precious and weighty, the meaning of tranquil yet ever illuminating, and the meaning of being able to bring much benefit. Those who see and hear it should cast off emotional attachment and reflect on and cultivate it.

Ru-Yi Translation

First, dividing the text. Everywhere it says "lines": in ancient times seventeen characters of prose were taken as a line. Verses are of two kinds: for four- and five-character [verses], four phrases make a line; for seven-character verses, two phrases make a line. Therefore the ancient masters, in dividing sūtras, all took the number of lines as the standard. In recent times the system has been changed and confused, and characters are added and subtracted, damaging the original rule. Not only does this make the ancient commentaries' divisions of the sūtras uneven; it also leaves the paper counts in the catalogs without a standard. I say to those with understanding: you should follow the ancient system.

Third, from "For each mark its function is made clear" on, making clear the wondrous function. The three marks and three karmas, though identical according to the perfect, become different in reaching beings; distinguished through the teachings, though interfused, they are distinct. "Māras" means the delusion of craving; "heretics" are the delusion of views; since it dwells in the one-sided real, these two cannot move it.
