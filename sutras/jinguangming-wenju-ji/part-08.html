The Emptiness Chapter: The Twofold Emptiness of Beings and Dharmas

This part covers the second half of the fourth fascicle of Zhili's Notes, following Zhiyi's Textual Commentary through the rest of the "Emptiness" chapter of the Golden Light Sūtra. It opens with the contemplation of impurity as an aid to the correct path: the nine contemplations, the great impurity that extends to the dependent reward, and the light of eight colors from the bones. Zhili then explains why the four truths and twelve links, usually assigned to the two vehicles, are also the bodhisattva's object, and sets analytic emptiness (the fist taken apart finger by finger) against embodying emptiness (the fist seen in a mirror). Here he makes a characteristic move: the Commentary's "bodhisattva of the distinct [teaching]" is read as the person of the perfect teaching, and the "embodying" that counts is embodying the three truths, not emptiness alone. The bulk of the part works through the verse "where is there a person," the twenty and forty kinds of view of the body, the four elements that cannot keep their own natures, and the twelve links taken over three times, as retribution, and in a single thought. The last section reads the verses on the fruit — cutting the net of afflictions, the gate of sweet dew, the conch, drum, torch, and rain, and the four universal vows — as the stages of the four teachings, ending with the Great Treatise's account of how giving contains all six perfections.

Ru-Yi Translation

First, citing the Great Treatise to make clear its aid to the correct. "The three liberations" means emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness; realizing the fruit through these three, they are called the gates of liberation. Liberation and nirvāṇa are two names for one essence. "The factors of the path are the method of opening the gate": the Small Vehicle takes the contemplation of the sixteen aspects as the gates of the three liberations; the present chapter takes identity with the empty, the provisional, and the middle as the gates of the three liberations. If one wishes this gate to open, one must cultivate it with the factors of the path, adjusting and testing: as one cultivates identity with the empty, the provisional, and the middle by the four kinds of foundations of mindfulness, so too one adjusts, tests, and cultivates it by the four kinds of right effort and so on up to the eightfold right [path]. For the three liberations of the Small Vehicle, adjusting and testing by the seven groups, the meaning is the same: both take the factors of the path as the method of opening the gate. "The nine contemplations": first, the contemplation of bloating; second, the contemplation of rupture; third, smeared with blood; fourth, pus and rot; fifth, blue-black discoloration; sixth, being devoured; seventh, scattering; eighth, bones; ninth, burning. In terms of the Small Vehicle there are two kinds of people. First, the person who destroys dharmas: seeking only to cut off suffering, he goes straight to the contemplation of burning and becomes a person liberated by wisdom. Second, the person who does not destroy dharmas: he comes to rest in the contemplation of bones and does not advance to the contemplation of burning; he obtains the flowing light and other merits in full and becomes a person liberated both ways. Now it is made clear that the bodhisattva sees the true mark of dhyāna; this is called cultivating penetrating dhyāna, forever different from the small. Though great and small differ, both use impurity to aid the opening of the three liberations. Within this aid to opening, the nine contemplations of impurity are the vanguard for breaking desire; hence they are called the first gate.

As for what is called "great": contemplating only the principal reward of oneself and others is called the small impurity, namely the contemplation of the nine contemplations. If one also contemplates the dependent reward, it is called the great impurity: that is, houses are like grave mounds, money like dead snakes, broth like liquid excrement, rice like white worms, clothes like stinking hides, mountains like heaps of flesh, ponds like rivers of pus, gardens and groves like dried bones, rivers and seas like pouring filth; this is called the great impurity. It is also called the great liberation (背捨, "turning away and abandoning"): 背 means turning away from these clean and pure five desires; 捨 means abandoning this attached mind.

"The ordinary being has suffering but no truth": though he meets great suffering, he does not take it as an affliction, because he does not examine and truly know that it is suffering. The Great Treatise, fascicle twelve, explaining the perfection of giving, says: "If a bodhisattva practicing the perfection of giving can produce the six perfections, then it is called the fulfillment of the perfection of giving. How does giving produce the perfection of giving? How does it produce the perfection of patience? When the bodhisattva gives, the recipient reviles him, or demands a great deal, or demands at the wrong time, or demands what should not be demanded. At this time the bodhisattva reflects: 'I am now giving in order to seek the buddha-path. No one makes me give; I do it for my own sake. Why should I become angry?' Having reflected thus, he practices patience. How does it produce the perfection of vigor? When the bodhisattva gives, he always practices vigor, wishing to practice the two kinds of giving, diligently seeking wealth and Dharma in order to supply them. How does it produce the perfection of dhyāna? When the bodhisattva gives, he can remove stinginess and greed and practice one-mindedness, gradually removing the five hindrances; this is called the perfection of dhyāna. Further, the mind relying on giving enters the first dhyāna and so on up to the concentration of cessation. How does it produce the perfection of wisdom? When the bodhisattva gives, he knows that there is a fruit of retribution and has no doubt, and can break wrong views and ignorance. Further, he distinguishes pure and impure giving and the different retributions obtained; this is called producing wisdom. Hence it says that the meaning of giving gathers in the six." The other five perfections also gather in one another, but that is not the intent of the present text; hence it discusses only giving, because it is the first of the six and because it is convenient for gathering in beings.

Body, life, and wealth — these three belong to phenomena. When one contemplates these three, and the giving mind and the object given to, the three wheels right where they are are identical to the empty, the provisional, and the middle; hence they can be equal to the nirvāṇa of the three virtues. This is attaining the permanent fruit by the permanent cause. If it were not so, how could one obtain the indestructible and eternally abiding? Practicing giving in this way is called pāramitā. Because the three virtues are very deep, giving is vertically high; because the three virtues are immeasurable, giving is horizontally broad. Because these capacities delight in brevity, the Tathāgata wondrously teaches the one [perfection of] giving, [in which one] follows and cultivates the practices. One must further understand: since it speaks of practicing giving to benefit beings after the fruit, how could the practitioner not cultivate it as soon as he hears it?

Zhili's readings that differ from our Commentary English.
