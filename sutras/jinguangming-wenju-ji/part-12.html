Praising the Buddha

This last part of the Notes covers the second half of fascicle 6, the comments on "Praising the Buddha," the final chapter of the Golden Light Sūtra in Dharmakṣema's version. According to the colophon at its end, Zhili died after finishing seventeen chapters, and one of his disciples compiled this chapter from what he had heard in Zhili's lectures. The Commentary leaves the sixty-five and a half lines of verse almost undivided, so the compiler supplies a full outline. The bodhisattvas' verses are read through the major and minor marks and the specially honored body. Faith-Mark's verses recall his past life as Dragon-Honored. The tree spirit's verses praise the Dharma-, reward-, and response-bodies and then ask the Buddha to come out of concentration. A long excursus explains the twofold emptiness of nature and marks: Zhiyi frames it as the conventional truth breaking nature and the real truth breaking marks, and Jingxi links it to the emptiness of beings and of dharmas. The compiler argues that it is a single contemplation. He also addresses why Dharmakṣema's version has the Buddha rise from samādhi only at the end, while Yijing's begins with it. The part closes with Zhang'an's explanation, the contemplation of mind (the three robes and six requisites), and the colophon.

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, from "As for the sequence" on, making clear the sequence of the chapter, in two: first, making clear what the chapters return to. This is also the intent of the present chapter's coming. "How so?" on explains the sequence of the chapters. "'Introduction' narrates the great essence" and so on: earlier, the Commentary, in explaining the sūtra, narrated the five sections; that now it narrates only the essence is because it points to what the ultimate fruit roams in and accords with, high and broad. The dharma-nature, realized at the buddha-ground, is the great essence of the present sūtra. Below, "Lifespan" and the merits of the various chapters all do not go beyond the great wisdom and the great function. "Exhausting its source" and so on: in wondrous awakening the source of the principle of the middle has been exhausted, and the matters at its limit have also been reached; hence the wisdom knowing all modes ceases illuminating and the myriad practices come to rest. This is only the one nature, with no other path; hence it is called the great essence of the sūtra. In "Lifespan" the two wisdoms of the fruit-ground are already perfect; hence from the essence, profound, deep, and broad, it gives rise to the function of long and short. "Repentance," "Praise," and "Emptiness" guiding — how do they go beyond the great function? The merits of the chapters all accord with the essence without deviation; hence below it says, "all the power of the Golden Light." "Such benefits appearing in the world" on is the second, showing the intent of the praise in the present text. It makes clear that because of the benefits of the seventeen chapters above, there are the words of praise of the sixty-two lines below. "It begins well and ends well": since the three parts, the introductory and the rest, have all obtained benefit, the three [sets of] bodhisattvas, bearing the Buddha's deep kindness, raise praise; hence there is the chapter "Praising the Buddha," making the end good as well.

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, explaining the text, in two: first, setting out the sections. Second, explaining the meaning, in two: first, the compilers' narration. The Commentary's text can be seen. The sūtra says "from this world to the land of Golden Jewel Canopy": it wishes to magnify the assembly, so that the bodhisattvas filling [the space] in between all praise Śākyamuni, the lord of the teaching. Recently I have seen someone who did not understand this purport and said that they praise that buddha. The sūtra and the Commentary are not obscure; how benighted that is! Fearing that later students may follow his understanding and explain the sūtra text wrongly, I now briefly cite the Commentary to show it. Tracing the origin of the coming of the "Praising [the Buddha]" chapter: the three sets of bodhisattvas all obtained benefit from hearing the sūtra above, and so they praise the Buddha and the teaching; what would that have to do with another buddha? Moreover, the Commentary says, "the one Buddha is the praiser"; if one says they praise that buddha, then whom does "the one Buddha, the praiser" point to, if not Śākyamuni rising from concentration? The sūtra and the Commentary are plain, so I do not cite more.

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, from "his voice is clear and penetrating" on, two lines, again praising the one major mark of the Brahmā voice, and also praising together the ocean of minor marks, the deep and far-reaching sound of his speech. "The voice of Brahmā" likens the Buddha's voice to what is deep and far-reaching. "The voice of the lion's roar" likens the Buddha's voice to fearlessness. "The voice of great thunder" likens the Buddha's voice to breaking delusion. As for saying "the six kinds of … voice": all the texts say eight voices: first, utmost good; second, soft; third, harmonious; fourth, honored and wise; fifth, not feminine; sixth, unerring; seventh, deep and far-reaching; eighth, inexhaustible. The translators added or reduced; there is no need to reconcile them. One may also [say that] "wondrous like the voice of Brahmā," raised first, is the deep and far-reaching voice among the eight; "the lion's roar" and "great thunder" raise the not-feminine voice. Because the Buddha dwells in the Śūraṅgama concentration, he always has the virtue of a hero of the world and has long left the mind of feminine softness; hence the sounds he utters can make all who hear them revere and fear, and none of the heavenly māras and non-Buddhists fail to submit. Since two kinds of voice have been listed above, that below it says only "the six kinds of … voice" raises the six kinds of voice, the kalaviṅka, the peacock, and so on; for kalaviṅka is rendered "utmost good voice."

Recently there are lecturers who do not know this and say that later people wrongly added the character "mind" (心). This too comes from their earlier delusion of taking mind as what is contemplated, which gave rise to confusion. Such careless error is beyond words. Just as the Sixteen Contemplations Sūtra originally makes clear the contemplation of the buddha, and the Commentary [on it] says it "takes the contemplation of mind as its cardinal doctrine," some, not seeing this intent, falsely make forced interpretations. Present students should earnestly think about it. "Contemplating the body, one does not find the body": first it makes clear the contemplation of the body. Examining the four great elements, they originally have no real nature; this is called the emptiness of the body's nature. There are only names, which are the body's marks. The body, up to the body practicing the eighty-four thousand Dharma-gates [as many as] dust and sand: the practitioner receives names accordingly; penetrating names as no-names is called the emptiness of the body's marks.
