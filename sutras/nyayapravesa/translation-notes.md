# Nyāyapraveśa Translation Notes

These notes record terminology decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of the *Nyāyapraveśa* (因明入正理論, *Yin Ming Ru Zheng Li Lun*, Taishō T32, no. 1630), composed by Śaṅkarasvāmin (商羯羅主) and translated into Chinese by Xuanzang. They exist so that the vocabulary of this new field — hetuvidyā, Buddhist logic — stays consistent across the whole page, since the treatise builds its technical apparatus in a tight, cumulative sequence and relies on it holding steady from one section to the next.

This is a separate glossary from the one used for the *[Cheng Weishi Lun](../cheng-weishi-lun/translation-notes.md)*. The two treatises share almost no vocabulary in practice — hetuvidyā is a distinct discipline (logic and epistemology) from Yogācāra's analysis of consciousness — so terms are not assumed to carry over between the two pages.

## Source and independence from prior translations

- Base text: Taishō T1630, via the CBETA digital edition (public domain, CBETA punctuated text). We translate from this Chinese text directly.
- English-language scholarship on this treatise and on Dignāga's logic exists (for example, work by Richard S. Y. Chi, Katsura Shōryū, and others on hetuvidyā and the *Nyāyamukha*/*Nyāyapraveśa* tradition). Where such scholarship is under copyright, we do not reproduce or paraphrase it. We may consult it privately to check that we have not misread a difficult technical passage, but the English wording and structure on this page are our own, made directly from Xuanzang's Chinese.
- This treatise has no surviving Sanskrit original; only Xuanzang's Chinese translation survives complete. "Nyāyapraveśa" is the standard scholarly reconstruction of the Sanskrit title (lit. "entering right reasoning" — Xuanzang's own title, 因明入正理論, literally *A Treatise on Entering Right Reasoning [in] Hetuvidyā*).

## Core terminology

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 因明 | hetuvidyā (the science of reasons) | One of the "five sciences" (五明) of classical Indian learning; Buddhist logic and epistemology as a discipline. Gloss on first use, then may stand alone. |
| 能立 | proof | sādhana; a correctly-formed argument (thesis + reason + example) that establishes a thesis for an opponent — one of the treatise's "eight approaches" (八門), for the benefit of awakening others (悟他) |
| 能破 | refutation | dūṣaṇa; correctly pointing out a genuine flaw in an opponent's proof |
| 似能立 | fallacious proof | an argument that only appears to be a valid proof but fails through a flawed thesis, reason, or example |
| 似能破 | fallacious refutation | an objection that only appears to refute a proof, but is actually aimed at a proof with no real flaw |
| 現量 | direct perception | pratyakṣa; cognition free of conceptual construction (離分別), taking an object's own particular characteristic (自相) directly — one of only two valid means of knowledge, for self-awakening (自悟) |
| 比量 | inference | anumāna; cognition of an object arrived at through a reason possessing the three characteristics — the other of the two valid means of knowledge |
| 似現量 | fallacious [direct] perception | perceptual cognition mixed with conceptual construction (e.g. recognizing "a pot," "a cloth"), which therefore fails to grasp the object's own particular characteristic directly |
| 似比量 | fallacious inference | an inference built on a fallacious reason, producing only an apparent, not a genuine, understanding of the object |
| 宗 | thesis | pratijñā; the proposition to be established, combining a subject (有法) both parties accept exists with a predicate (能別) both parties accept as meaningful, in whatever combination the proponent wishes to establish. Example: "Sound is impermanent." |
| 因 | reason | hetu; the ground offered for the thesis, e.g. "because it is produced." Must satisfy the three characteristics (因三相) to be valid |
| 喻 | example | dṛṣṭānta; a case, external to the dispute, that shows the reason and the thesis's property going together. Two kinds: the example by agreement (同法喻) and the example by difference (異法喻) |
| 宗、因、喻 | thesis, reason, example | 三支, "the three parts" or "the three-part proof" — Dignāga's streamlined syllogism, replacing the older five-part form (五分) of earlier Nyāya logic. See the treatise's own closing description of itself as having "pruned away the five parts to fix on the three" |
| 有法 | the subject | dharmin; the "thing that holds a property" — the logical subject of the thesis, e.g. "sound" in "Sound is impermanent" |
| 能別 | the predicate | the "distinguishing [property]" — what the thesis asserts of the subject, e.g. "impermanent." Paired with 所別, "that which is distinguished" (i.e. the subject, viewed from the predicate's side) |
| 極成 | established (for both parties) | a term or an existence-claim that both the proponent and the opponent already accept without dispute — the precondition for a workable subject or predicate. A thesis fails if this precondition is missing (see the fallacious theses below) |
| 因三相 | the three characteristics of a valid reason | trairūpya — the treatise's central test for a reason: (1) 遍是宗法性, that it pervades (holds of) the subject of the thesis; (2) 同品定有性, that it is definitely present among the homologous instances; (3) 異品遍無性, that it is entirely absent from the heterologous instances |
| 同品 | the homologous class | sapakṣa; instances, other than the subject itself, that already share the property the thesis is trying to establish. In "Sound is impermanent," pots are of the homologous class |
| 異品 | the heterologous class | vipakṣa; instances that lack the property the thesis is trying to establish. In "Sound is impermanent," space (held to be permanent) is of the heterologous class |
| 九句因 | the nine forms of the reason | the Hetucakra ("wheel of reasons"), Dignāga's exhaustive grid of nine ways a reason can be distributed across the homologous and heterologous classes (present in all / some / none of each), of which only two are valid, two are contradictory, and five are inconclusive. This treatise's *four kinds of contradiction* (相違四種) and *six kinds of inconclusiveness* (不定六種) below work through seven of those nine cases without naming the chart directly; see the reading note at "Inconclusiveness, Six Kinds" |
| 似宗 | fallacious thesis | a proposition that cannot function as a genuine thesis, for any of nine reasons (似宗九過, see below) — even though the proponent may sincerely wish to establish it |
| 似因 | fallacious reason | a reason that fails one or more of the three characteristics; subdivided into 不成 (non-establishment), 不定 (inconclusiveness), and 相違 (contradiction) |
| 似喻 | fallacious example | an example that fails to actually show the reason and the thesis's property going together; subdivided into 似同法喻 (five kinds, for the example by agreement) and 似異法喻 (five kinds, for the example by difference) |
| 不成 | non-establishment | one of the three families of fallacious reason: the reason itself is not accepted as holding of the subject, in one of four ways (兩俱, 隨一, 猶豫, 所依 — see the page section) |
| 不定 | inconclusiveness | one of the three families of fallacious reason: the reason holds of the subject, but is compatible with more than one thesis, in one of six ways |
| 相違 | contradiction | one of the three families of fallacious reason: the reason, if taken to its logical end, actually establishes the *opposite* of what was intended, in one of four ways |
| 悟他 | awakening others | the function of proof and refutation (and their fallacious counterparts) — logic used in debate, to bring another person to understanding |
| 自悟 | self-awakening | the function of direct perception and inference (and their fallacious counterparts) — logic used privately, to arrive at one's own correct understanding |
| 商羯羅主 | Śaṅkarasvāmin | the treatise's author, a disciple of Dignāga (陳那); the postface's honorific "天主菩薩" ("Bodhisattva Deva-lord") refers to him by a Chinese gloss on his name |
| 陳那 | Dignāga | Śaṅkarasvāmin's teacher; author of the much larger *Pramāṇasamuccaya* and *Nyāyamukha* (因明正理門論), of which this treatise is traditionally read as a simplified, introductory digest |

Keep this table growing if later edits expand the page — add a row rather than improvising a fresh translation of a term that already has an entry.

## Structure of this page

Unlike the *Cheng Weishi Lun*, CBETA's edition of this text carries no inline 科判 (traditional outline markers) beyond a single top-level 卷 marker and one 後序 marker for the postface. The treatise is short enough (one fascicle, c. 3,400 characters) that it was organized here by its own argument instead: the eight-topic structure named in its opening verse (proof, refutation, and their fallacious counterparts, for awakening others; direct perception, inference, and their fallacious counterparts, for awakening oneself), unpacked in the order the text itself takes them up. Section headings on the page are Ru-Yi's own, written to make that structure legible to a modern reader — they are not a traditional 科判 and should not be cited as one.

## Register

- Plain English, close to the Chinese, not a loose paraphrase — matching the method already used for the *Cheng Weishi Lun* and the other treatises on this site.
- This text is far denser, line for line, than a narrative sūtra: nearly every sentence is a technical definition or a worked example. Reading notes carry more of the explanatory weight here than in less technical texts, since the Chinese itself allows almost no slack for the English to unpack a term in place without breaking the clean, formulaic structure the treatise depends on.
- The postface (後序), by contrast, is ornate Tang parallel prose (駢文), written to be read for its own literary and historical interest rather than as logical argument. Its translation stays closer to a readable, flowing English register than to the terse, technical register of the treatise proper.
