Eliminating Affliction I

The Initial Teaching's Anatomy of Affliction斷惑分齊・始教

Gate Nine's sixth sub-topic opens with the treatise's most exacting piece of abhidharma bookkeeping: ten root afflictions, sorted into the discriminated and the innate, distributed across five sense-consciousnesses, the mental consciousness, manas, and the storehouse consciousness, then multiplied out across the three realms and the Four Noble Truths until the count reaches one hundred and twenty-eight. Fazang gives this apparatus in full — not as decoration, but because the entire next four parts, on how the Final, Sudden, and Perfect Teachings handle affliction differently, only make sense against this baseline of exactly what the Initial Teaching thinks there is to cut.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1866, vol. 45, 華嚴一乘教義分齊章 Author: Fazang 法藏, 643–712, Tang Reading notes: Ru-Yi, original

The Text

Two Ways to Speak of Cutting Affliction

Chinese Source

第六斷惑分齊者。有云。若依小乘若依三乘有二種義。一約位滅惑相。二寄惑顯位相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, on the boundaries of eliminating affliction: some say that, whether one takes the Hīnayāna or the three vehicles, there are two approaches. First, cutting the mark of affliction with reference to position; second, using affliction to reveal position.

初義者。若依始教具足三乘斷惑差別。由此是其三乘教故。障有二種。謂煩惱所知。先辨二乘斷煩惱障。於中有二。先障名數後斷惑得果。

As for the first approach: taken according to the Initial Teaching, the distinctions in how the three vehicles cut affliction are given in full, since this is precisely a teaching of the three vehicles. The hindrances are of two kinds — the afflictive and the cognitive. We first discuss how the two vehicles cut the afflictive hindrance. Within this there are two topics: first, the hindrance's name and number; second, cutting affliction and attaining the fruit.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The "two approaches" named here structure this entire sub-topic. Parts 17–18 give the first approach — cutting affliction position by position, school by school. Part 19 and the opening of Part 20 give the second — 寄惑顯位, "using affliction to reveal position," an exegetical technique for reading scripture's own talk of affliction being cut as coded language for a bodhisattva's stage, rather than as literal abhidharma. The two are not rival theories; they are two different jobs the concept of "cutting affliction" is asked to do.

The afflictive hindrance (煩惱障) and the cognitive hindrance (所知障) are the two obstacles Yogācāra distinguishes throughout: the first is what keeps a being bound to saṃsāra — greed, hatred, and their kin — and an arhat can be free of it; the second is the subtler not-knowing that obstructs full, unobstructed knowledge of all things, and only a buddha is entirely free of it. Much of what follows in Parts 17–20 turns on exactly who cuts which of these two, and how much.

The Ten Root Afflictions, Sorted by Consciousness

Chinese Source

初中煩惱有二。謂分別俱生。總有十種。一貪二瞋三無明四慢五疑六身見七邊見八邪見九見取十戒禁取。於中四種唯分別起。謂疑．邪見．見取．戒禁取。餘六通二種。五識得起初三。亦通分別及俱生。由用意識中邪師等三因引故。得有分別起也。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the first: affliction is of two kinds, the discriminated and the innate. There are ten in all: first, greed; second, hatred; third, ignorance; fourth, conceit; fifth, doubt; sixth, the view of a self; seventh, extreme views; eighth, wrong views; ninth, view-grasping; tenth, attachment to precepts and observances. Among these, four arise only as discriminated afflictions — doubt, wrong views, view-grasping, and attachment to precepts and observances. The remaining six run through both kinds. The five sense-consciousnesses can give rise to the first three of these [greed, hatred, and ignorance], and these too run through both the discriminated and the innate, since a discriminated arising becomes possible even there, by drawing on three causes belonging to the mental consciousness — a false teacher and the rest.

意識具十種。四分別六通二。末那唯四俱生。六中除瞋及邊見。以瞋唯不善。此識有覆無記故。又以一類相續緣第八識剎那剎那執我故無邊見。又以後三見及疑籍三因生。此識無彼故不起也。又由恒相續緣故。非第六所引不同五識。是故唯四。其第八識總不起。唯是異熟。無覆無記性故。

The mental consciousness possesses all ten of these — four exclusively discriminated, and six running through both kinds. Manas has only four, and these are exclusively innate; of the six that elsewhere run through both kinds, it lacks hatred and extreme views. This is because hatred is exclusively unwholesome, whereas manas is of an obscured yet indeterminate nature. And because it is a single continuous stream that clings, moment after moment, to the eighth consciousness as a self, it has no extreme views. Further, the latter three views together with doubt are generated only by drawing on three further causes, and since manas has none of those causes, they do not arise in it. And because it takes its object through an ever-continuing, uninterrupted stream, rather than being induced by the sixth consciousness as the five sense-consciousnesses are, it therefore has only these four. The eighth consciousness gives rise to none of the ten at all, being solely of the nature of ripening — of an unobscured, indeterminate nature.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The ten root afflictions are a standard Yogācāra inventory, but Fazang's real interest is in where each one can arise. The system distinguishes eight consciousnesses — the five sense-consciousnesses, the sixth (the ordinary discursive mind), the seventh (manas, which clings to the eighth as "I"), and the eighth (ālayavijñāna, the storehouse consciousness). Only the sixth consciousness, capable of deliberate conceptual judgment, can generate a fully "discriminated" affliction — a view arrived at through bad teaching, bad doctrine, or bad reasoning. The five sense-consciousnesses and manas can only host the cruder, "innate" varieties that need no argument to arise; the storehouse consciousness, being pure undifferentiated ripening, hosts none of them at all. This is why manas is left with exactly the four innate afflictions that make sense for a consciousness whose entire business is clinging wordlessly to a self: greed for that self, ignorance about it, conceit in it, and the view that it exists — but never hatred, which needs an object to turn against, and never the discursive views built out of doctrine and reasoning.

The Arithmetic of Affliction — One Hundred and Twenty-Eight

Chinese Source

此分別俱生皆通三界。瞋唯欲界。以上二界煩惱。皆有覆無記性故。其分別起者。欲界四諦各有十使。即為四十。上二界除瞋諦別各有九。即有七十二。并欲界合有一百一十二也。其俱生者欲界具六。上二界除瞋各唯有五。合成十六。通前分別總有一百二十八也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Both the discriminated and the innate afflictions run through all three realms; hatred alone belongs only to the desire realm, since the afflictions of the two higher realms are entirely of an obscured, indeterminate nature. As for those that arise as discriminated: in the desire realm, each of the Four Noble Truths has all ten root afflictions attached to it, giving forty. In the two higher realms, hatred being excluded, each truth has nine, giving seventy-two; combined with the desire realm's forty, this makes one hundred and twelve in all. As for the innate afflictions: the desire realm has the full six, while the two higher realms, hatred again excluded, have only five each — sixteen in all. Combined with the hundred and twelve discriminated afflictions above, there are one hundred and twenty-eight in all.

問何故。前愚法小乘中。十使不通迷四諦。此中即通耶。答此有二義。一以三乘中煩惱功力漸漸寬廣故。障一切也。二又由迷等義通一切境也。

Question: Why is it that, in the naive Hīnayāna spoken of earlier, the ten root afflictions do not each obscure all Four Noble Truths, while here they do? Answer: There are two reasons. First, because within the three vehicles the power of affliction grows broader by degrees, so that it obstructs everything at once. Second, because the very meaning of confusion runs equally through every object.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The number 128 is arrived at by simple multiplication, but the logic behind it is worth unpacking. In the desire realm, all ten root afflictions can in principle attach to a wrong view of any of the Four Noble Truths, giving 10 × 4 = 40. In the form and formless realms, hatred cannot arise at all (these realms are too refined for aversion), so only nine afflictions attach to each truth: 9 × 4 = 36 per realm, or 72 for both. That gives 112 discriminated afflictions. The sixteen innate afflictions are simpler: six per realm-instance in the desire realm (the full roster minus nothing, since innate afflictions are not indexed to a specific truth), five each in the two higher realms (again hatred excluded) — 6 + 5 + 5 = 16. Total: 128. The exercise matters less for the number itself than for what it demonstrates: that the Initial Teaching treats affliction as something with an exact, countable structure, cut piece by piece — which is exactly the picture the Final Teaching (Part 18) and the Perfect Teaching (Part 20) will each dismantle in their own way.

Cutting the Discriminated Afflictions — Three Fruits at the Path of Seeing

Chinese Source

第二斷惑得果者。先斷分別有其三人。一若從具縛入真見道。剎那頓斷三界四諦分別煩惱得預流果。二若倍離欲人。入真見道兼斷倍離欲。得一來果。言倍離欲者。謂凡夫時欲界修惑九品之中。伏斷前六故云倍離欲。入見道時即永斷前所伏故。是以得彼果也。三若已離欲人。入真見道兼斷九品得不還果。如瑜伽說。入見道果者有其三種。隨其所應證三果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, on cutting affliction and attaining the fruit: cutting the discriminated afflictions belongs to three kinds of persons. First, one who, still fully bound, enters the true path of seeing: in a single instant he cuts off, all at once, the discriminated afflictions attached to the Four Noble Truths across the three realms, and attains the fruit of stream-entry. Second, one who has already halved his desire: entering the true path of seeing, he also cuts off the desire he had halved, and attains the fruit of once-returning. "Halved his desire" means someone who, while still an ordinary person, has among the desire realm's nine grades of cultivation-affliction already suppressed and cut off the first six — hence "halved" — and who, upon entering the path of seeing, permanently cuts off what he had earlier only suppressed, and so attains that fruit. Third, one who has already left desire behind entirely: entering the true path of seeing, he also cuts off all nine grades, and attains the fruit of non-returning. As the Yogācārabhūmi says, those who enter the path of seeing and its fruit are of three kinds, each attaining, as is fitting, one of the three fruits.

Cutting the Innate Afflictions — Gradual and Sudden Liberation

Chinese Source

次斷俱生者。第六識俱生。九地各有九品。又進修道人有其二種。一漸出離。斷欲界九品中前六品盡得一來果。斷九品盡得不還果。斷上二界盡得阿羅漢果。二頓出離者。謂得初果已。即頓斷三界。漸除九品即得阿羅漢果。更無餘果。何者。以彼欲界九品修惑及上二界。總三種九品各初一品。一時頓斷故云頓斷三界。竪論九品一一別斷故云漸除也。若爾何故有漸斷者。以於三界法不能頓緣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, cutting the innate afflictions: those belonging to the sixth consciousness extend through nine grades in each of the nine stages of the three realms. Those advancing along the path of cultivation are of two kinds. First, those liberated gradually: cutting off the first six of the nine grades of the desire realm exhausts them into the fruit of once-returning; cutting off all nine grades exhausts them into the fruit of non-returning; and cutting off both higher realms exhausts them into the fruit of arhatship. Second, those liberated suddenly: once they have attained the first fruit, they at once cut off, all together, [the first grade of each of the three realms,] then gradually remove the remaining grades until they attain the fruit of arhatship, with no further fruit in between. Why is this? Because, of the desire realm's nine grades of cultivation-affliction and the corresponding nine grades in each of the two higher realms — three sets of nine in all — the very first grade of each set is cut off all at once, in a single moment; hence "suddenly cutting off the three realms." Yet reckoned vertically, the nine grades within each realm are still cut off one by one, individually — hence "gradual removal." If this is so, why does gradual cutting exist at all? Because one is unable to take the dharmas of all three realms as a single object all at once beyond that shared first grade.

對法論云。頓出離者。謂入諦現觀已。依止未至定發出世道。頓斷三界一切煩惱。品品別斷唯立二果。謂預流果及阿羅漢果。乃至廣引指端經等。如彼說。其末那煩惱行相微細。前漸頓二人。皆與非想地惑一時頓斷故。瑜伽云。末那相應任運煩惱。唯與非想處共斷故。一時頓斷。非如餘惑漸次而斷故也。

The Abhidharmasamuccaya says: "Sudden liberation means that, having entered the direct realization of the truths, one relies on the 'not-yet-arrived' concentration to generate the path transcending the world, and thereby suddenly cuts off every affliction of the three realms all at once — yet grade by grade they are still cut off individually, and only two fruits are established, the fruit of stream-entry and the fruit of arhatship" — going on to cite at length the Fingertip Sūtra and the rest. It is as that text says. The afflictions belonging to manas are subtle in their mode of activity; both the gradual and the sudden practitioner alike cut them off all at once, together with the afflictions of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. The Yogācārabhūmi says: "The spontaneously arisen afflictions associated with manas are cut off only together with those of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception" — all at once, in a single moment, unlike the other afflictions, which are cut off gradually, grade by grade.

問何故前愚法二乘無頓出離。此中有耶。答為顯前劣故此超過。愚法二乘無此勝智。顯彼教劣。方便漸引起彼勝欲。令捨小從大故作此說。其論末那惑滅。小乘無者唯準此知。

Question: Why does the naive Hīnayāna spoken of earlier have no sudden liberation, while it exists here? Answer: This is precisely in order to show that what came before is inferior, by surpassing it. The naive two vehicles lack this superior wisdom, which shows that teaching to be inferior; and it is taught here as a skillful means of gradually drawing them on, arousing in them a superior aspiration, so that they will discard the Lesser and turn to the Greater. As for the treatise's statement that the affliction of manas is extinguished, and that the Hīnayāna lacks this — this too should be understood by the same reasoning.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, and arhat are the four classical fruits of the path (srotaāpanna, sakṛdāgāmin, anāgāmin, and arhat). What the Initial Teaching adds to this old scheme is a claim that Fazang flags explicitly as a mark of superiority: a "sudden liberation" path unavailable to the "naive Hīnayāna" described earlier in this treatise, by which cultivation-afflictions across all three realms begin to be cut simultaneously rather than realm by realm. The rhetorical move recurs throughout the Five Teachings: each higher teaching does not simply add doctrine, it demonstrates its superiority by claiming a capacity the lower teaching lacked, then explains the lower teaching's limitation as a deliberate pedagogical restraint rather than an error.

The Cognitive Hindrance Among the Two Vehicles

Chinese Source

此謂二乘斷煩惱障。其所知障。諸趣寂者入無餘時一時皆斷。唯此非擇滅也。其餘一切有斷不斷。慧解脫人不斷。俱解脫人分有所斷。謂八解脫障不染無知。修八勝解所對治故。如瑜伽說。又諸解脫由所知障解脫所顯。由聲聞及緣覺等於所知障心得解脫故。當知此始教為引愚法漸向大故。安立此教。深勝於彼。故所知障亦許分斷。然上所斷不同愚法。以彼唯斷煩惱得故。此即不爾。斷種子故直進菩薩斷惑者二障俱斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

This concerns how the two vehicles cut the afflictive hindrance. As for the cognitive hindrance: for those bent solely on quiescence, it is all cut off at once, entirely, upon entering nirvāṇa without remainder — and this alone is a cessation without deliberate discernment. As for everything else, some cut it and some do not: those liberated through wisdom alone do not cut it; those liberated in both ways cut a portion of it — namely, the undefiled ignorance obstructing the eight liberations, which the cultivation of the eight bases of mastery counteracts. So the Yogācārabhūmi says. Moreover, these liberations are made manifest through liberation from the cognitive hindrance, since śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas too attain, in mind, a liberation with respect to it. One should understand that the Initial Teaching, in order to draw the naive gradually toward the Greater Vehicle, establishes this teaching as deeper and superior to what came before, and so allows that a portion of the cognitive hindrance too is cut off. Yet what is cut here differs from the naive Hīnayāna, since that teaching only cuts affliction's manifest activity, whereas here the very seeds are cut. As for bodhisattvas advancing directly, once they cut affliction, both hindrances are cut together.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Wisdom-liberated" and "both-liberated" name two kinds of arhat recognized across Buddhist scholasticism: the former attains liberation through insight alone, without mastering the meditative absorptions; the latter combines insight with the eight liberations and the eight bases of mastery, advanced concentrative attainments that let one manipulate and transcend perception of form. Only the latter kind, on this reckoning, touches even a sliver of the cognitive hindrance — the deeper unknowing that a buddha alone extinguishes completely. What follows now is the Initial Teaching's own account of how a bodhisattva goes further still — an account Part 18 will show the Final Teaching redescribing from the ground up.

The Bodhisattva Retains the Afflictive Hindrance, and the Poison Simile

Chinese Source

又煩惱障中。不同二乘約界分品。但於二障分別起者地前伏現行。初地真見道時。一剎那中頓斷彼種。其俱生中煩惱障。初地已去自在能斷。留故不斷。何以故。潤生攝化故。不墮二乘地故。為斷所知障故。為得大菩提故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, within the afflictive hindrance, the bodhisattva's cutting does not, as with the two vehicles, divide into grades by realm. Rather, as for the discriminated afflictions within the two hindrances, before the first ground their manifest activity is suppressed, and upon entering the true path of seeing at the first ground, their seeds are cut off all at once, in a single instant. As for the afflictive hindrance among the innate afflictions, from the first ground onward the bodhisattva is fully capable of cutting it — but deliberately retains it, and does not cut it. Why is this? In order to sustain rebirth and gather beings in for their transformation; in order not to fall into the stage of the two vehicles; in order to cut the cognitive hindrance; and in order to attain great bodhi.

是故攝論云。由留惑至惑盡證佛一切智。解云。惑盡者。是所知障盡。即由留煩惱障起勝行故。得至此位證佛果也。又梁攝論既云留種子。是故當知。煩惱障種至金剛位。其所知障行相細故。正障菩薩道。是故地地分斷。要至佛地方得總盡。由此即說二障修惑俱至佛地故。對法論云。又諸菩薩。於十地修道位中。唯修所知障對治道。非斷煩惱障。得菩提時頓斷煩惱障及所知障。頓成羅漢及如來故。其末那俱生行相細故。亦同前至佛地盡也。

Hence the Mahāyānasaṃgraha says: "By retaining affliction, one arrives at the exhaustion of affliction and realizes the buddha's omniscience." Explanation: "the exhaustion of affliction" means the exhaustion of the cognitive hindrance — for it is precisely by retaining the afflictive hindrance that superior practice is aroused, and so one is able to arrive at this stage and realize buddhahood. Moreover, since the Liang translation of the Mahāyānasaṃgraha speaks of "retaining the seed," one should understand that the seed of the afflictive hindrance persists all the way to the vajra ground. As for the cognitive hindrance, its mode of activity is subtle, and it directly obstructs the bodhisattva path; hence it is cut off ground by ground, and only at the ground of buddhahood is it exhausted altogether. This is the basis for saying that the cultivation-afflictions of both hindrances alike persist all the way to the ground of buddhahood. The Abhidharmasamuccaya says: "Moreover, bodhisattvas, throughout the path of cultivation across the ten grounds, cultivate only the path that counteracts the cognitive hindrance, and do not cut the afflictive hindrance; only upon attaining bodhi do they suddenly cut off both the afflictive hindrance and the cognitive hindrance together, thereby suddenly becoming both arhat and tathāgata at once." As for the innate afflictions of manas, their mode of activity being subtle, they too, just as before, are exhausted only upon reaching the ground of buddhahood.

問其二障修惑諸識相應。地上現行有何同異。答其煩惱障內第六識惑既盡。盡故留彼。是故現種皆以智御用成勝行不起過患。猶如毒蛇以呪力御不令死。不起過患而成餘用。菩薩善巧留惑亦爾。故攝論云。如毒呪所害等。是故當知。於彼煩惱或現或種皆得自在。其第七識煩惱性非潤生故非所留。行相細故。七地已還有時暫現。以觀智有間故。其所知障皆後地惑於前地起。或現或種以地地分斷故。

Question: Among the cultivation-afflictions of the two hindrances, associated with the various consciousnesses, what similarities and differences are there in their manifest activity once a bodhisattva has reached the grounds? Answer: Within the afflictive hindrance, the affliction belonging to the sixth consciousness has already been exhausted — and it is precisely because it has been exhausted that it can be retained. Hence both its manifest activity and its seed are entirely governed by wisdom in their function, so that they accomplish superior practice without giving rise to any fault — just as a venomous snake, governed by the power of a spell, is kept from killing, and, without giving rise to any harm, is put instead to some other use. The bodhisattva's skillful retention of affliction is exactly the same. Hence the Mahāyānasaṃgraha says, "like one harmed by a poison rendered harmless through a spell," and the rest. One should therefore understand that with respect to that affliction — whether as manifest activity or as seed — the bodhisattva has attained complete mastery. As for the affliction-nature belonging to the seventh consciousness, since it is not what sustains rebirth, it is not something to be retained. Because its mode of activity is subtle, up through the seventh ground it still sometimes manifests briefly, since the contemplative wisdom that would otherwise suppress it is at times interrupted. As for the cognitive hindrance, the affliction proper to each later ground arises already within the ground before it, whether as manifest activity or as seed, since it is cut off ground by ground.

問若爾何故。有處說七地已還起有漏心等耶。答若約第六識中煩惱障為有漏者。彼既留惑故。即似有漏。若約所知障為有漏。即實有漏。此二通十地。若末那煩惱即實有漏。至於七地有無不定。以有時暫起有漏心故。餘義準知。

Question: If this is so, why do certain passages say that, up through the seventh ground, a defiled mind still arises? Answer: If one speaks of the afflictive hindrance within the sixth consciousness as "defiled," then, since the bodhisattva has deliberately retained this affliction, it only resembles being defiled. If one speaks of the cognitive hindrance as "defiled," then it is genuinely defiled — and this holds true across all ten grounds. If one speaks of the affliction of manas, it is likewise genuinely defiled, though whether it is present or absent at the seventh ground is not fixed, since a defiled mind sometimes arises there only briefly. The remaining points should be understood by the same reasoning.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The poison-and-spell simile is the Initial Teaching's own image for what Part 18 will call, more bluntly, "keeping what arhats cut." A retained affliction is not an affliction left unaddressed — it is an affliction whose destructive power has been entirely neutralized by wisdom while its function is redirected to useful ends, exactly as a controlled venom stops killing without stopping being venom. This is also the point in the text where this treatise's own vocabulary — direct affliction, seed, manifest activity — starts running in parallel with the ground-by-ground schedule that Vasubandhu's Daśabhūmikasūtra-śāstra gives from the opposite direction: what each ground positively accomplishes, rather than what in it is suppressed, retained, or finally released.

Simultaneity of Cutting and Wisdom, and the Emptiness at the Root of Hindrance

Chinese Source

又此教中惑滅智起分齊者。惑種在滅相時。智即在生相同時相返。如昂即低低即昂等。廣如對法論說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, as to the precise boundary, within this teaching, between an affliction's ceasing and wisdom's arising: at the very moment the seed of affliction is in its mark of ceasing, wisdom is, at that same moment, in its mark of arising — the two turning against each other, as when the rising of one end of a scale is precisely the falling of the other. This is set out at length in the Abhidharmasamuccaya.

又此障法。以依識無性故。即空無分別。是其障義。如維摩經云。五受陰洞達空無所起。是苦義。今此障義亦準彼知之。

Further, as for the dharma of this hindrance: because it depends upon consciousness and has no nature of its own, it is empty and beyond discrimination — and this very emptiness is what is meant by "hindrance." As the Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "To penetrate the five clinging-aggregates through and through as empty, as having nothing whatsoever that arises — this is what 'suffering' means." The meaning of "hindrance" spoken of here should be understood in just the same way.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The closing citation quietly reframes everything counted so exactly earlier in this part. If a hindrance, like the five aggregates in the Vimalakīrti Sūtra's own definition of suffering, is at bottom nothing but consciousness's own empty, non-arising activity, then the whole careful apparatus of 128 afflictions, three fruits, and gradual-versus-sudden liberation was never a report about substantial things being destroyed — it was a report about a confusion being seen through. The seesaw image just above makes the same point about timing: cutting and wisdom are not two events in sequence, cause and effect, but a single turning seen from two sides.
