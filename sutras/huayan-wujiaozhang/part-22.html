Is Buddhahood Permanent?

And How Many Marks Does a Buddha Bear佛果義相

Gate Nine's eighth sub-topic asks two questions about the fruit of buddhahood itself. First: is the dharma-body permanent or impermanent — a question the Five Teachings answer with steadily increasing sophistication, until the Perfect Teaching declares it both, without contradiction. Second: how many physical marks does a buddha actually bear — thirty-two, eighty-four thousand, or, on Huayan's own reckoning, an ocean of marks too vast to count at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1866, vol. 45, 華嚴一乘教義分齊章 Author: Fazang 法藏, 643–712, Tang Reading notes: Ru-Yi, original

The Text

Two Questions, and the Hīnayāna's Bare Impermanence

Chinese Source

第八佛果義相者於中有二。先明常無常義。後明相好差別。前中若小乘佛果唯是無常。以不說本性功德故。如佛性論云。小乘以無性德佛性。但有修德也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Eighth, on buddhahood's own nature and marks: within this there are two topics. First, explaining the meaning of permanence and impermanence; second, explaining the distinctions among its marks. Within the first: according to the Hīnayāna, the fruit of buddhahood is solely impermanent, since it does not speak of an intrinsic-nature virtue. As the Treatise on Buddha-Nature says: "The Hīnayāna, having no doctrine of an intrinsic-nature virtue as buddha-nature, holds only to a cultivated virtue."

The Initial Teaching — Permanent Nature, Impermanent Function

Chinese Source

若三乘始教法身是常。以自性故。亦無常。以離不離故。修生功德是無常。以從因緣生故。是有為無漏故。亦得是常。以無間斷故。相續起故。莊嚴論云。自性無間相續三。佛俱常住等。

Ru-Yi Translation

According to the Initial Teaching within the three vehicles, the dharma-body is permanent, since it is the buddha's own essential nature; yet it is also impermanent, on account of the difference between being apart from conditioned things and not being apart from them. Cultivated-and-arisen virtue is impermanent, since it arises from causes and conditions, being conditioned though undefiled; yet it too can be called permanent, since it is without interruption, arising in unbroken continuity. The Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra says: "The buddha's own-nature body, the body without interruption, and the body of unbroken continuity — the buddha abides permanently in all three," and so on.

The Final Teaching — Flowing From and Returning to Suchness

Chinese Source

若依終教有二義。先別明後總說。別中修生功德是無常。以修生故。亦即是常。一得已後同真如故。何以故。本從真流故。無明已盡還歸真體故。梁攝論云。無不從此法身流。無不還證此法身等。寶信論起信論等盛立此義。如彼應知。又智論云。薩婆若不與三世合。何以故。過去世等是虛妄是生滅。薩婆若是實法。非生滅故。解云。薩婆若此云一切智。即知佛地圓智同真如故。非生滅也。又攝論云。猶如虛空遍滿一切色際無生住滅變異等。如來智亦爾。遍一切所知無倒無變異等。是故當知。非直無間斷故以為常。亦即同真如不變異常也。

Ru-Yi Translation

According to the Final Teaching there are two aspects: first, explaining them separately; second, a general statement. Within the separate explanation: cultivated-and-arisen virtue is impermanent, since it is cultivated and newly arisen; yet it is also precisely permanent, since, once attained, it is thereafter identical with suchness. Why is this? Because it originally flowed forth from suchness itself, and, ignorance now exhausted, returns again to that very body of suchness. The Liang Mahāyānasaṃgraha says: "There is nothing that does not flow from this dharma-body; there is nothing that does not, in turn, come to realize this same dharma-body," and so on. The Ratnagotravibhāga, the Awakening of Faith, and the like all establish this meaning at length, and it should be understood as they explain it. Further, the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra says: "Sarvajñā does not conjoin with the three times. Why is this? Because the past and the other times are illusory, subject to arising and ceasing, whereas sarvajñā is a real dharma, not subject to arising and ceasing." Explanation: sarvajñā here means omniscience — one thereby knows that the perfect wisdom of the ground of buddhahood is identical with suchness, and is therefore not subject to arising and ceasing. Further, the Mahāyānasaṃgraha says: "Just as space pervades and fills the entire boundary of form, without arising, abiding, ceasing, or change, so too is the tathāgata's wisdom: it pervades everything knowable, without inversion and without change," and so on. One should therefore understand that it is not merely because it is without interruption that it is called permanent — it is also permanent in being identical with suchness's own unchanging permanence.

Permanent in Substance, Impermanent in Function

Chinese Source

法身是常。以隨緣時不變自性故。亦是無常。以隨染緣赴機故。何以故。以諸功德既並同是真。是故起用唯是真作故。起信論中。釋報化二身唯屬真如用大攝。又論云。眾生心淨法身影現等。又云。復次本覺隨染分別生二種相。與彼本覺不相捨離。謂一者智淨相。二者不思議業相。乃至廣說等。

Ru-Yi Translation

The dharma-body is permanent, since, even while according with conditions, it does not change its own essential nature; yet it is also impermanent, since it accords with defiled conditions in order to meet the capacities of beings. Why is this? Because all these virtues alike are, equally, identical with suchness — hence whatever function arises is, in every case, suchness's own doing. Within the Awakening of Faith, the reward-body and the transformation-body are explained as belonging entirely to the great function subsumed under suchness. That treatise also says: "When sentient beings' minds are pure, the dharma-body's own image appears therein," and so on. It also says: "Further, the original enlightenment, according with defilement and discriminating thought, gives rise to two kinds of marks, which are never separate from that original enlightenment itself: the first is the mark of wisdom's own purity; the second is the mark of inconceivable activity," and goes on to explain this at length.

二總說者。由此法身隨緣義故。是故功德差別得成。由不變義故。是故功德無不即真。如舉體隨緣全相不變。二義鎔融無障礙故。是故佛果即常即無常。具足四句或非四句。隨義應知。

Second, the general statement: because the dharma-body has the meaning of according with conditions, the differentiation of virtues is thereby established; because it has the meaning of being unchanging, there is no virtue whatsoever that is not identical with suchness. It is as though the entire substance accords with conditions while its whole character remains unchanged — the two meanings fusing together without any obstruction. For this reason, the fruit of buddhahood is precisely permanent and precisely impermanent, complete with all four positions of the tetralemma, or else beyond all four — this should be understood according to the particular meaning intended.

Neither One nor Different

Chinese Source

問若爾何故得說非一非異耶。答若始教中。以真如遍故智證真如故非異也。有為無為不同故非一也。若終教中。功德有二義。一緣起現前義。以三無數劫功德不虛故。二無自性義。以離真如無自體故。此中初義與法身隨緣。後義與法身不變。是非異門。以舉體全收故。又此初義與不變。後義與隨緣。是非一門。以義差別故。是即不動非異。明非一也。思之可見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: If this is so, then why can one say that it is neither one nor different? Answer: Within the Initial Teaching, since suchness pervades everywhere and wisdom directly realizes suchness, they are not different; yet since the conditioned and the unconditioned are not the same, they are not one either. Within the Final Teaching, virtue has two aspects: first, the aspect of dependently arisen manifestation, since the virtue of the three countless kalpas is not in vain; second, the aspect of having no nature of its own, since apart from suchness it has no substance of its own. Here, the first aspect corresponds to the dharma-body's according with conditions, and the latter aspect corresponds to the dharma-body's unchangingness — this is the gate of "not different," since the entire substance is thereby wholly gathered in. And this same first aspect, taken together with unchangingness, and the latter aspect, taken together with according with conditions, form the gate of "not one," since their meanings are distinct. It is precisely in remaining unmoved that they are not different — and this makes clear why they are not one either. Reflect on this, and it becomes evident.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Final Teaching's account rests on a single move, restated from several angles: whatever is cultivated over the three asaṃkhyeya kalpas of practice is real and not wasted, yet, since it has no substance of its own apart from suchness, it was never really a second thing standing beside suchness to begin with. "Not different" and "not one" are not a compromise between permanence and impermanence; they are the same relationship — virtue's total identity with an unmoving suchness — described from two directions at once, exactly as this treatise's own account of root and branch fusion, at the close of the entire first gate, described the relationship between the One Vehicle and the three vehicles.

The Sudden Teaching — One Undifferentiated Body

Chinese Source

若依頓教。以相盡離念故。唯一實性身。平等平等不可說有功德差別。亦不可說常與無常。若寄言顯者。如經云。吾今此身即是法身。又經云。一切諸佛身唯是一法身等。如是準之。

Ru-Yi Translation

According to the Sudden Teaching, since every mark is exhausted and every thought departed, there is only the single body of real nature, equal and equal alike — one cannot speak of any differentiation of virtue, nor can one speak of permanence and impermanence at all. If one borrows language to indicate it, then, as scripture says: "This very body of mine is precisely the dharma-body," and, as scripture also says: "The bodies of all buddhas are but a single dharma-body," and so on. It should be understood accordingly.

The Perfect Teaching — Three Accounts, All Four Positions

Chinese Source

若依圓教。佛果常等義有三說。一約用。佛果既通三世間等一切法。是故具有常等四句。二約德。佛果即具四義。謂一修生。二本有。三本有修生。四修生本有。圓融無礙備無邊德。是故亦通常等四句。上二句義思之可見。三約體亦通四句。謂此經中以不說為顯故是常。與阿含相應故是無常。二義無礙故俱有。隨緣起際故俱非。此上三義。若體即俱體。乃至用即俱用。以體攝無礙故。皆有常等無礙思之。

Ru-Yi Translation

According to the Perfect Teaching, the meaning of buddhahood's permanence and the rest has three accounts. First, with reference to function: since the fruit of buddhahood pervades all dharmas of the three kinds of world and the rest, it is complete with all four positions of the tetralemma concerning permanence. Second, with reference to virtue: the fruit of buddhahood is complete with four aspects — first, cultivated and newly arisen; second, originally possessed; third, originally possessed yet cultivated and newly arisen; fourth, cultivated and newly arisen yet originally possessed — perfectly fused and unobstructed, replete with boundless virtues; hence it too runs through all four positions concerning permanence. The meaning of these first two accounts may be seen upon reflection. Third, with reference to substance, it likewise runs through all four positions: since this sūtra makes itself known by not being explicitly spoken of, it is permanent; since it corresponds to the āgamas, it is impermanent; since the two meanings are unobstructed, it is both; and since it arises according to conditions at their very limit, it is neither. As for these three accounts above: where substance is meant, all are substance; and, going further, where function is meant, all are function — since substance and function are gathered in without any obstruction between them, all of them possess permanence and the rest without obstruction. Reflect on this.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The tetralemma — permanent, impermanent, both, neither — is a standard Indian logical device for exhausting a question's possible answers; most teachings pick one branch and defend it. The Perfect Teaching's distinctive move is to claim all four branches at once, three separate times over (by function, by virtue, by substance), and then to declare that even the distinction between these three approaches collapses once substance and function are seen as mutually inclusive. This is the same interpenetrating logic already met in Part 20's "cut one affliction, cut all," now applied to the question of permanence itself: a fourfold answer is not indecision, but the shape a genuinely unobstructed reality takes when described honestly.

How Many Marks — From Thirty-Two to Eighty-Four Thousand

Chinese Source

二明相好差別者。若依小乘。有三十二相八十種好。是實法也。若三乘中。或亦但說三十二相八十種好。是化身之相。仍即空是相義。如金剛般若經對法論等說。此約始教引小乘說也。或約報身說八萬四千相。並是實德。此約直進及終教等說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, explaining the distinctions among the marks: according to the Hīnayāna, there are the thirty-two marks and eighty minor marks, and these are real dharmas. Within the three vehicles, some likewise speak only of the thirty-two marks and eighty minor marks, holding these to be marks of the transformation-body — yet holding, too, that being empty is itself precisely what being a mark means. So the Diamond Sūtra, the Abhidharmasamuccaya, and the like say. This is spoken according to the Initial Teaching, in order to draw the Hīnayāna onward. Others, speaking with reference to the reward-body, speak of eighty-four thousand marks, all of them real virtues. This is spoken according to those who advance directly and according to the Final Teaching and the like.

Why Even a Buddha's Marks Have a Karmic Origin Story

Chinese Source

問何故智論等。於此化身辯金鏘馬麥等往業所致。三十二相等亦各出因耶。答為引二乘因下而說。現業果不亡故。聖道斷惑非滅報故。如羅漢飱沙。金鏘等亦爾。小乘以為實。始教即空說。以是方便故。如大乘方便經說。其相好出因有二義。一亦是方便為引二乘。即於此身示勝因果。以實報身非彼所見故。二此等亦即是實報相。垂在化中顯示現故。得出因也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Why do the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra and the like, with respect to this transformation-body, discuss such things as the golden-cymbal wound and the eating of horse-fodder as brought about by past karma, and give an originating cause for each of the thirty-two marks as well? Answer: This is done in order to draw the two vehicles onward, speaking to them at their own lower level, since present karmic fruits are never annulled, and since the noble path's cutting of affliction does not annihilate karmic retribution. Just as an arhat may still choke on sand, the golden-cymbal wound and the rest are exactly the same. The Hīnayāna takes these as real; the Initial Teaching speaks of them as empty, since this is a skillful means — as the Sūtra on Mahāyāna Skillful Means explains. The giving of an originating cause for the marks has two meanings. First, it too is a skillful means for drawing the two vehicles onward, displaying, within this very body, a superior cause and effect, since the real reward-body is not something they are able to see. Second, these are also, in truth, marks of the real reward-body, manifested and displayed within the transformation-body — and it is for this reason that an originating cause can be given for them.

問何故攝論中說三十二相等。入法身功德攝耶。答此亦有二義。一為迴二乘方便漸說。真實法身恐彼難信故。以此功德說為法身令易信受。以觀見故。二彼以功德為法身故。攝在彼中也。此上並約始教說。又三十二相等。即無生無性故。亦即是真如法身。此約終教說。

Question: Why does the Mahāyānasaṃgraha say that the thirty-two marks and the rest are subsumed under the virtue of the dharma-body? Answer: This too has two meanings. First, as a skillful means for gradually turning the two vehicles, since they might find the real dharma-body difficult to believe in; by speaking of this visible virtue as the dharma-body, belief becomes easier for them, since it is something they can actually observe and see. Second, that text takes virtue itself as the dharma-body, and so subsumes the marks within it. Both of these are spoken according to the Initial Teaching. Further, the thirty-two marks and the rest, being without arising and without any nature of their own, are for that very reason precisely suchness's own dharma-body. This is spoken according to the Final Teaching.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The golden-cymbal wound and the eating of horse-fodder are two of the standard "ten karmic retributions" traditionally said to have still afflicted the historical Buddha's own body — a splinter of gold-tinged wood driving into his foot, and a period spent, with his monks, eating fodder meant for horses during a famine, each traced back to a specific unwholesome act in a distant past life. Their appearance here is doing real philosophical work: if karmic retribution can still ripen in a buddha's physical body, appearances notwithstanding, then that body — the transformation-body — cannot itself be the ultimate reality being pointed to. The very marks that impress an audience as signs of buddhahood are, on the Initial Teaching's own account, exactly as conditioned and exactly as empty as everything else that ripens from karma; the real reward-body they gesture toward lies one level further in, beyond what any but a bodhisattva can see.

The One Vehicle's Ocean of Marks, in Three Tiers

Chinese Source

若依一乘。有十蓮華藏世界海微塵數相。彼二相皆遍法界。業用亦爾。所以說十者。欲顯無盡故。如相海品說。又觀佛三昧經中。約此三乘宗。分佛相好以為三段。故彼經云。略中略者。我今為此時會大眾及淨飯王。略說相好。佛生人間示同人事。同人相故說三十二相。勝諸天故說八十種好。為諸菩薩說八萬四千諸妙相好。佛實相好。我初成道摩伽陀國寂滅道場。為普賢賢首等諸大菩薩。於雜華經已廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

According to the One Vehicle, there are marks numbering ten times the motes of dust in the Lotus Womb World-Ocean. Both of these kinds of marks pervade the entire dharma-realm, and their karmic function does likewise. The reason ten is spoken of is to make manifest their inexhaustibility. So the "Ocean of Marks" chapter explains. Further, the Sūtra on the Samādhi of Contemplating the Buddha, with reference to these teachings of the three vehicles, divides the buddha's marks into three sections. Hence that sūtra says: "The briefest of the brief: I now, for this present assembly and for King Śuddhodana, briefly speak of the marks. The buddha, born among human beings and displaying deeds shared in common with human beings, on account of these marks shared with human beings, speaks of the thirty-two marks; surpassing all the gods, he speaks of the eighty minor marks. For the bodhisattvas, he speaks of eighty-four thousand wondrous marks. As for the buddha's own real marks — when I first attained the way, at the site of quiescent enlightenment in Magadha, for Samantabhadra, Bhadraśrī, and the other great bodhisattvas, I have already explained them at length within the Miscellaneous Flower Sūtra."

解云。此中三十二相等。當略中之略。為人天二乘等。即當初也。八萬四千等。義當但是略。為三乘菩薩等。當次也。佛實相好如雜華說者。義當廣說。即是指此華嚴相海品說。是一乘別教相。即當終也。以雜華即是華嚴故。餘義可知。

Explanation: Here, the thirty-two marks and the rest correspond to "the briefest of the brief," spoken for gods, humans, and the two vehicles — this is the first tier. The eighty-four thousand marks and the rest correspond, in meaning, to merely "the brief," spoken for the bodhisattvas of the three vehicles — this is the second tier. "The buddha's own real marks, as explained in the Miscellaneous Flower Sūtra" correspond, in meaning, to "the extensive teaching" — this refers precisely to the account given in this very Avataṃsaka Sūtra's "Ocean of Marks" chapter, the marks of the One Vehicle's own Separate Teaching — this is the final tier. For the Miscellaneous Flower Sūtra is precisely the Avataṃsaka Sūtra itself. The remaining points may be known accordingly.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"Ten times the motes of dust in the Lotus Womb World-Ocean" is not offered as an actual count. Ten functions throughout Huayan literature, and throughout this treatise's own Ten Mysterious Gates later on, as the number that stands for totality itself rather than for any specific quantity — nine plus one rounding upward into completeness. An ocean of marks "numbering ten times" an already uncountable number of dust motes is Fazang's way of saying that the question "how many marks does a buddha have" stops being answerable in the currency of counting at all once the One Vehicle's own vantage is reached. The three-tier reading of the Contemplation of Buddha Samādhi Sūtra makes the same point historically: thirty-two marks, eighty-four thousand marks, and an inexhaustible ocean of marks are not three rival head-counts but three depths of the very same disclosure, each pitched to what its particular audience — humans and gods, bodhisattvas of the three vehicles, or Samantabhadra and Bhadraśrī themselves — was capable of receiving.
