The Six Characteristics I — The Rafter and the House

Total, Particular, and Identical, Illustrated by a Single House六相圓融義

Fazang closes the treatise's doctrinal core with a second signature teaching, promised already at the end of Part 32 as the key to the Ten Mysterious Gates: the Six Characteristics. He names all six, explains in a few sentences why the teaching matters at all, and then reaches for an illustration so ordinary it could be pointed at from where he sat writing — a house, and the rafter that holds it up — to work out, in full dialectical detail, what it means to call a single part of something both wholly distinct from the whole and, in a real sense, identical with it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1866, vol. 45, 華嚴一乘教義分齊章 Author: Fazang 法藏, 643–712, Tang Reading notes: Ru-Yi, original

The Six Characteristics, Named

Fazang opens with a table of contents for the whole remaining discussion: the six names, each given a one-line gloss so brief that the illustration to come will be doing almost all of the real explanatory work.

Chinese Source

第四六相圓融義。六相緣起三門分別。初列名略釋。二明教興意。三問答解釋。初列名者。謂總相．別相．同相．異相．成相．壞相。總相者。一舍多德故。別相者。多德非一故。別依比總滿彼總故。同相者。多義不相違。同成一總故。異相者。多義相望各各異故。成相者。由此諸緣起成故。壞相者。諸義各住自法不移動故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth is the meaning of the Six Characteristics in perfect fusion 六相圓融義. The dependent origination of the Six Characteristics is distinguished under three approaches: first, listing their names and briefly explaining them; second, clarifying why this teaching is taught; third, resolving them through questions and answers.

As for listing the names: they are the Total characteristic, the Particular characteristic, the Identical characteristic, the Different characteristic, the Formative characteristic, and the Disintegrating characteristic. The Total characteristic is this: a single house comprises many distinct qualities within it. The Particular characteristic is this: the many qualities are not simply one; the particular is set against, and depends upon, the total, filling out that total. The Identical characteristic is this: the many senses do not contradict one another, and together constitute a single totality. The Different characteristic is this: the many senses, considered against one another, are each distinct. The Formative characteristic is this: it is by means of these very conditions that dependent origination is accomplished. The Disintegrating characteristic is this: each of the several senses abides in its own dharma, unmoved and unshifted.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Six words, defined here almost too tersely to grasp on a first pass — which is exactly why Fazang moves straight to a concrete case. Read them once as a bare checklist before the house arrives: a whole is Total; its parts, as parts, are Particular; the parts agree with each other in what they're all doing, which makes them Identical; the parts differ from each other in what they individually are, which makes them Different; the whole comes to exist because the parts cooperate, which is Formative; and each part stays exactly itself throughout, never actually becoming the whole, which is Disintegrating. Total and Particular are one pair, Identical and Different a second, Formative and Disintegrating a third — three complementary lenses on a single situation, not six separate facts piled on top of one another. Hold that structure loosely; the rafter and the house will make it concrete very shortly.

Why This Teaching Is Taught

Before working through the six in detail, Fazang states, in a single dense passage, the practical stakes: this is not an exercise in classification for its own sake, but the formal grounding for some of Huayan's largest claims already met earlier in this section.

Chinese Source

第二教興意者。此教為顯一乘圓教法界緣起無盡圓融自在相即無礙鎔融乃至因陀羅無窮理事等。此義現前一切惑障。一斷一切斷。得九世十世惑滅。行德即一成一切成。理性即一顯一切顯。並普別具足始終皆齊。初發心時便成正覺。良由如是法界緣起六相鎔融。因果同時相即自在具足逆順。因即普賢解行。及以證入果即十佛境界所顯無窮。廣如華嚴經說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, as for why this teaching is taught: this teaching exists in order to manifest that the dependent origination of the dharma-realm, within the One Vehicle's Perfect Teaching, is inexhaustible, perfectly fused, autonomous, mutually identical, unobstructed, and thoroughly interfused, extending even to Indra's Net's boundless principle and phenomena, and so forth. Once this meaning is present, every obstruction of affliction is such that cutting one is cutting all; the afflictions of the nine times and the ten times are extinguished; the virtue of practice, being one, is the accomplishment of all; the nature of principle, being one, is the disclosure of all — universal and particular alike are fully replete, beginning and end entirely level, so that at the very moment of first resolving upon awakening, one straightaway accomplishes true awakening. This is truly because the dependent origination of the dharma-realm is, in this way, perfectly fused through the Six Characteristics: cause and fruit, simultaneous, mutually identical and autonomous, replete with counter-flow and with-flow alike. The cause is exactly Samantabhadra's own understanding and practice, and realizing and entering the fruit is exactly the inexhaustibility disclosed within the ten buddhas' own realm. This is explained at length in the Avataṃsaka Sūtra.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Notice what this paragraph is doing: every large claim from the Ten Mysterious Gates section — one affliction cut is all afflictions cut, one practice completed is all practice completed, a bodhisattva's first thought of awakening is already buddhahood — is here traced back to the Six Characteristics as its actual mechanism. Fazang is not introducing a new doctrine so much as finally showing his work: the "why" behind every "one is exactly all" claim made across Parts 28 through 32 turns out to be this same total/particular, identical/different, formative/disintegrating structure, applied at every scale from a single coin to the whole dharma-realm.

The Total Characteristic — The Rafter Is the House

Fazang now turns to the illustration the entire Chinese Buddhist tradition remembers this treatise for: a house, built of rafters and tiles, in which — worked through carefully — the rafter turns out to be, in a very exact sense, the house itself.

Chinese Source

第三問答解釋者。然緣起法一切處通。今且略就緣成舍辨。問何者是總相。答舍是。問此但椽等諸緣。何者是舍耶。答椽即是舍。何以故。為椽全自獨能作舍故。若離於椽舍即不成。若得椽時即得舍矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, as for resolving them through questions and answers: the dharma of dependent origination applies everywhere without exception; here, for brevity, we will discuss it in terms of conditions completing a house.

Question: What is the Total characteristic? Answer: The house is it. Question: But this is only the rafter and the other conditions — which of them is the house? Answer: The rafter itself is the house. Why? Because the rafter, entirely on its own, is able to make the house. If the rafter is removed, the house cannot be constituted; when the rafter is obtained, the house is obtained.

問若椽全自獨作舍者。未有瓦等亦應作舍。答未有瓦等時不是椽故不作。非謂是椽而不能作。今言能作者。但論椽能作。不說非椽作。何以故。椽是因緣。由未成舍時無因緣故。非是緣也。若是椽者其畢全成。若不全成不名為椽。

Question: If the rafter, entirely on its own, makes the house, then even before there are tiles and the rest, it should already make the house. Answer: At a time when there are not yet tiles and the rest, it is not yet a rafter, and so it does not make the house — it is not that it is a rafter and yet unable to make the house. What is here called "able to make" speaks only of the rafter's own ability to make; it does not say that something which is not a rafter makes it. Why? Because the rafter is itself a condition — and at a time when the house is not yet completed, there is as yet no condition, and so it is not yet a condition at all. Whatever is truly a rafter accomplishes the house completely; whatever does not accomplish it completely cannot be called a rafter.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

"The rafter itself is the house" sounds, on first hearing, like an obvious overstatement — a rafter is plainly a small wooden beam, and a house is plainly a whole structure of walls, roof, and floor. Fazang's argument is not that the two are the same size, or the same shape, but that the rafter, correctly considered, is not really a self-contained object that happens to sit inside a larger one; it is only a "rafter" at all because it is already doing the work of house-making. Remove it and there is no house — but notice the sharper point in the second exchange: there is also, strictly, no rafter either, only a stray length of wood that has not yet entered into the relation that would make it one. "Rafter" is not the name of a shape; it is the name of a role a piece of wood plays only once the whole cooperative structure is in place. That is why Fazang can say the rafter "entirely on its own" makes the house, while also insisting this same rafter cannot exist as a rafter before the tiles and everything else are also present — the seeming contradiction dissolves once "rafter" is understood as a relational term rather than a freestanding thing.

What If Each Part Only Contributes a Little?

A natural rival theory — that the rafter and the other parts each supply some fraction of the house, jointly adding up to the whole — is tested and found to collapse into one of two classic philosophical errors.

Chinese Source

問若椽等諸緣。各出少力共作不全作者。有何過失。答有斷常過。若不全成但少力者。諸緣各少力。此但多箇少力。不成一全舍故是斷也。諸緣並少力皆無全成。執有全舍者。無因有故是其常也。若不全成者。去却一椽時舍應猶在。舍既不全成。故知非少力並全成也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: If the rafter and the other conditions each contributed only a partial force, working together without any single one of them making the whole, what fault would follow? Answer: There would be the faults of both severance and permanence. If it were not wholly accomplished but only partially so, then each condition would have only a partial force; and many partial forces of this kind could never constitute a single, complete house — hence severance. Yet if all the conditions have only partial force, and none accomplishes the whole, and one still insists that a complete house exists, this is having an effect without a cause — hence permanence. Moreover, if it were not wholly accomplished by each, then removing a single rafter should leave the house still standing; but the house is, in fact, not left standing — and so we know that it is not a matter of partial forces jointly accomplishing the whole.

問無一椽時豈非舍耶。答但是破舍無好舍也。故知好舍全屬一椽。既屬一椽故。知椽即是舍也。

Question: When a single rafter is missing, is it then not a house at all? Answer: It is merely a broken house, not a good, sound house. Hence we know that a sound house belongs, entirely, to a single rafter; and since it belongs to that single rafter, we know that the rafter is exactly the house.

問舍既即是椽者。餘板瓦等應即是椽耶。答總並是椽。何以故。去却椽即無舍故。所以然者。若無椽即舍壞。舍壞故不名板瓦等。是故板瓦等即是椽也。若不即椽者。舍即不成。椽瓦等並皆不成。今既並成。故知相即耳。一椽既爾。餘椽例然。是故一切緣起法不成則已。成則相即。鎔融無礙自在圓極。難思出過情量。法性緣起一切處準知。

Question: If the house is exactly the rafter, then should not the other boards, tiles, and the rest also be exactly the rafter? Answer: All of them, entirely, are the rafter. Why? Because remove the rafter, and there is no house. The reason is this: if there is no rafter, the house collapses — and once the house has collapsed, it can no longer be called boards, tiles, and the rest at all. This is exactly why the boards and tiles are themselves exactly the rafter. If they were not exactly the rafter, the house could not be constituted, and the rafter, tiles, and all the rest would, none of them, be constituted either. Since they are, in fact, all constituted together, we know that they are mutually identical.

As the single rafter is, so every other rafter follows the same pattern. This is why, in every dharma of dependent origination, either it is not accomplished at all, or, once accomplished, it is mutually identical throughout — thoroughly interfused, unobstructed, autonomous, and perfectly consummate, beyond the reach of ordinary thought and beyond the measure of ordinary feeling. This is the dependent origination of the nature of dharmas, to be understood accordingly, everywhere without exception.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The pair of faults named here — "severance" 斷 and "permanence" 常 — are two classic wrong extremes this treatise, like most Madhyamaka-influenced Buddhist argument, treats as the two ditches on either side of the middle path. "Severance" is the error of a total that never actually comes together — a heap of partial contributions that adds up to nothing complete, since no single piece of wood was ever wholly responsible for anything. "Permanence" is the opposite error: insisting a whole exists anyway, floating free of any real cause that could account for it. Fazang's own position steers between both: the rafter does not contribute a fraction of the house — it contributes the whole house, entirely, and so, by the same reasoning, does every other single part. This is why removing even one rafter does not leave a slightly-diminished house standing, but a "broken house" — proof, on Fazang's reading, that the sound house was never a sum of fractional contributions in the first place, but something each single part was wholly responsible for producing.

The final move — tiles, too, "are exactly the rafter" — is the payoff of the whole illustration, and it generalizes without limit: not just the rafter, but literally every part of the house turns out to be identical with every other part, and with the whole, once "identical" is understood the way Parts 28 through 30 already defined mutual identity for the ten coins. A house is not a rafter plus tiles plus walls, added together; it is one single event of mutual identity distributed, without residue, across everything that makes it up.

The Particular Characteristic — Distinct, and Yet Constituting the Whole

Having shown that the rafter is, in the fullest sense, the house, Fazang turns immediately to the opposite-seeming claim — that the rafter is also genuinely particular, distinct from the total — and shows the two claims support rather than contradict each other.

Chinese Source

第二別相者。椽等諸緣別於總故。若不別者總義不成。由無別時即無總故。此義云何。本以別成總。由無別故總不成也。是故別者。即以總成別也。問若總即別者。應不成總耶。答由總即別故。是故得成總。如椽即是舍故名總相。即是椽故名別相。若不即舍不是椽。若不即椽不是舍。總別相即此可思之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the Particular characteristic: the rafter and the other conditions are particular, distinguished from the total. Were they not distinguished as particulars, the meaning of the total could not be constituted — for when there is no particular, there is no total either. What does this mean? The total is, from the outset, constituted by means of the particulars; because there is no particular, the total is not constituted. This is exactly why the particular is what constitutes the total.

Question: If the total is exactly the particular, should not the total then fail to be constituted? Answer: It is precisely because the total is exactly the particular that the total can be constituted at all. Just as the rafter is exactly the house, and is therefore called the Total characteristic; and is exactly the rafter, and is therefore called the Particular characteristic. If it were not exactly the house, it would not be the rafter; if it were not exactly the rafter, it would not be the house. That the total and the particular are mutually identical should be considered in this light.

問若相即者。云何說別。答秖由相即是故成別。若不相即者。總在別外故非總也。別在總外故非別也。思之可解。問若不別者有何過耶。答有斷常過。若無別者。即無別椽瓦。無別椽瓦故。即不成總舍故此斷也。若無別椽瓦等。而有總舍者。無因有舍是常過也。

Question: If they are mutually identical, how can "particular" still be spoken of at all? Answer: It is precisely because they are mutually identical that "particular" can be established. Were they not mutually identical, the total would stand outside the particular, and so would not be a genuine total; and the particular would stand outside the total, and so would not be a genuine particular. Think this through and it can be understood.

Question: If there were no particular, what fault would follow? Answer: There would be the faults of severance and permanence. If there were no particular, there would be no particular rafters and tiles; and without particular rafters and tiles, the total house could not be constituted — hence severance. And if, without particular rafters, tiles, and the rest, there were nonetheless said to be a total house, this would be having an effect without a cause — hence permanence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The apparent tension — "the total is exactly the particular, so how is 'particular' still a meaningful word?" — gets the same treatment the ten coins already received back in Part 28: identity does not erase distinction, it depends on it. A total that stood entirely apart from its particulars, needing nothing from them, could never actually be constituted by them — it would be a total "outside" the particular, cut off from what was supposed to build it. Only because the total and the particular are mutually identical can the particular still do real, particular work: being distinctly a rafter, and not a tile, is exactly what allows this rafter to be identical with the whole house it helps constitute. Distinctness and identity, in other words, are not in competition here — one is the condition of the other.

The Identical Characteristic — Rafter and Tile Agree in Function

The third characteristic shifts the angle of vision again: not the relation of a part to the whole, as with Total and Particular, but the relation among the parts themselves — all pulling, despite their differences, toward the very same job.

Chinese Source

第三同相者。椽等諸緣和同作舍。不相違故皆名舍緣。非作餘物故名同相也。問此與總相何別耶。答總相唯望一舍說。今此同相約椽等諸緣。雖體各別成力義齊故名同相也。問若不同者有何過耶。答若不同者有斷常過也。何者。若不同者。椽等諸義互相違背不同作舍。舍不得有故是斷也。若相違不作舍。而執有舍者。無因有舍故是常也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, the Identical characteristic: the rafter and the other conditions harmoniously and identically make the house; because they do not contradict one another, they are all called conditions of the house — and because they do not make anything else, this is called the Identical characteristic.

Question: How does this differ from the Total characteristic? Answer: The Total characteristic speaks only with respect to the single house. This Identical characteristic, by contrast, speaks with respect to the rafter and the other conditions themselves: although each is distinct in substance, the force by which they accomplish the house is equal among them all, and so it is called the Identical characteristic.

Question: If they were not identical, what fault would follow? Answer: If they were not identical, there would be the faults of severance and permanence. Why? If they were not identical, the rafter and the other conditions would contradict one another and would not, together, make the house — and so the house could not exist at all; hence severance. And if, contradicting one another and not making the house, one nonetheless insisted a house existed, this would be having an effect without a cause; hence permanence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The Total/Particular pair and the Identical/Different pair are easy to blur together, and Fazang's own Q&A here draws the line precisely: Total names the house considered as one single thing — a claim about the whole. Identical, by contrast, is a claim about the parts, considered against each other: the rafter and the tile remain two entirely distinct pieces of substance — nobody is claiming a tile is made of the same wood as a rafter — but they agree perfectly in what they are both doing, namely making this one house and nothing else. A rafter and a tile are "identical" not because they resemble each other, but because their causal contribution converges on a single shared outcome. Parts 33 closes here, with three of the six characteristics — Total, Particular, and Identical — now fully worked through the rafter and the house; Part 34 completes the remaining three, and the illustration finally shows its other half: not just what makes rafter and tile agree, but what makes them nonetheless, and just as truly, different.
