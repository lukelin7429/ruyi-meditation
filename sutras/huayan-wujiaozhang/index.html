The One Vehicle's Distinctions in Teaching and Meaning.

通稱「五教章」 — commonly called the Wujiao Zhang, the "Essay on the Five Teachings"

Every other Huayan text on this site — the Gaṇḍavyūha, the Chapter of Pure Conduct, the Vows of Samantabhadra, Vasubandhu's Treatise on the Ten Grounds — is the Avataṃsaka Sūtra itself, or a commentary on one of its chapters. This treatise is different: it is Fazang (法藏, 643–712), the effective systematizer of the Huayan school, standing outside the sūtra and explaining, in his own voice, what kind of teaching it is, how it ranks against every other teaching the Buddha gave, and what makes it cohere. Here is the "Five Teachings" doctrinal classification that ranks all Buddhism from Hīnayāna up to Huayan's own Perfect Teaching; here are the Ten Mysterious Gates and the Six Characteristics — the formal statement of how one thing can contain everything and everything can be found in one thing, dependent origination of the dharma-realm, worked out with the patience of a philosopher rather than the wonder of a storyteller.

How to read this collection

Fazang organizes the whole treatise around his own Ten Gates 十門 — ten successive angles of approach, opened one after another, each building on what came before. This site's 34 parts follow those same ten divisions rather than the source's own four fascicles, since Fazang's own topic boundaries rarely land on a fascicle break. The table below groups all 34 parts by gate; a "Read by Part" list further down links every part in sequence.

Fazang himself is the authoritative voice defining this doctrine — no separate ancient commentator stands between the reader and his prose the way Jingying Huiyuan or Chengguan might elsewhere on this site. Every reading note here is Ru-Yi Meditation Center's own, stated plainly as such, written to make a genuinely difficult, densely cross-referential scholastic text approachable without softening its actual argument.

Fazang's Own Ten Gates

The treatise's own outline, stated at the very opening of Part 1: "I will now unlock the meaning of the Tathāgata's Ocean-Seal Samādhi, the One Vehicle's teaching and meaning, briefly making ten gates." Every part below sits under exactly one of these ten.

Gate 1 of 10
建立一乘 Establishing the One Vehicle
Two gates — the Separate Teaching and the Common Teaching — and ten proofs, anchored in the Lotus Sūtra's parable of the three carts, that the One Vehicle both differs from and was never other than the three vehicles.
Parts 1–3
Gate 2 of 10
教義攝益 Teaching, Meaning, and Benefit
What each vehicle actually delivers — not just its doctrine, but the benefit a person walks away with, and whether that benefit reaches only within the world or beyond it.
Part 3
Gate 3 of 10
古今立教 Old and New Classifications
Ten Chinese predecessors surveyed on their own terms — Bodhiruci's one teaching, Guangtong's gradual/sudden/perfect, Tiantai's four teachings, Xuanzang's three Dharma-wheels — before Fazang proposes his own.
Part 4
Gate 4 of 10
分教開宗 The Five Teachings and Ten Doctrines
The treatise's single most cited passage — Hīnayāna, Initial, Final, Sudden, and Perfect Teaching, reaching back through ten rival doctrines of early Buddhist scholasticism.
Part 5
Gate 5 of 10
乘教開合 Combining Vehicle and Teaching
How the vehicles counted in Gate 1 and the teachings named in Gate 4 fold into and out of one another — root within branch, branch within root.
Part 6
Gate 6 of 10
起教前後 When the Teachings Were First Taught
A precise chronology, in seven-day weeks after the Buddha's awakening, for when each teaching was first spoken — and how the "root" teaching and the "branch" teachings can be simultaneous and sequential at once.
Part 6
Gate 7 of 10
決擇前後意 Discerning the Order's Intent
Ten kinds of disciple, each hearing the Buddha teach in a different order — because the sequence lives in the listener's own capacity, not in the Buddha's single, simultaneous utterance.
Part 7
Gate 8 of 10
施設異相 Ten Marks That Set It Apart
Ten concrete marks distinguishing the One Vehicle from the three — its time, place, teacher, audience, and how a single grain of dust becomes a complete Dharma-gate.
Part 8
Gate 9 of 10
所詮差別 Differences in What Is Taught
The treatise's longest gate, run through ten sub-topics in turn: consciousness, spiritual lineage, path-stages, practice-duration, the practicing body, eliminating affliction, the two vehicles turning to Mahāyāna, buddhahood's own nature, a buddha's teaching-domain, and the buddha-bodies — each analyzed across all Five Teachings.
Parts 9–23
Gate 10 of 10
義理分齊 The Principle Itself
The treatise's climax: the Three Natures and the Six Meanings of a Cause, then Huayan's own signature teachings — the Ten Mysterious Gates and the Six Characteristics, worked out through the simile of ten coins and Fazang's famous illustration of a house built from rafters.
Parts 24–34

Translation Notes

Recurring terms, kept consistent across all 34 parts. See the full translation notes for the complete glossary and structural breakdown, and also this site's note on translation method.

判教 — panjiao, doctrinal classificationThe whole genre this treatise belongs to: ranking every Buddhist teaching by depth. Gate 3 surveys ten predecessors' attempts before Fazang proposes his own in Gate 4.
別教 / 同教 — the Separate Teaching / the Common TeachingFazang's own two-way division of the One Vehicle — deliberately rendered differently from Tiantai's unrelated "distinct teaching" / "shared teaching" already used elsewhere on this site, so the two schemes are never confused.
五教 — the Five TeachingsHīnayāna, Initial, Final, Sudden, and Perfect — Fazang's ranking of all Buddhist doctrine, with the Avataṃsaka alone occupying the Perfect Teaching.
十玄(緣起無礙法)門 — the Ten Mysterious GatesTen formal statements of how any one thing fully contains, and is contained by, everything else — dependent origination of the dharma-realm, worked out gate by gate in Parts 28–32.
六相圓融 — the Six CharacteristicsTotal, Particular, Identical, Different, Formative, and Disintegrating — worked out through Fazang's own illustration of a house built from rafters in Parts 33–34.
法界緣起 — dependent origination of the dharma-realmHuayan's own name for how all things arise together, mutually conditioning and mutually containing one another without obstruction.
