The Six Characteristics II — Difference, Formation, Disintegration, and the Closing Verse

The Rafter and the House, Concluded — and Fazang's Own Final Words六相圓融義・卷第四終

The rafter and the house, having already shown how a part can be both wholly Total and wholly Particular, wholly Identical with its neighbors, now show the other half of the same picture: how those same parts remain, at the very same time, genuinely Different, and how the house comes to exist precisely because no single part ever tries to become it. Fazang closes with a summary line for all six characteristics at once, and then, for the only time since the treatise's opening homage, breaks into verse — eight lines that are the very last words of the entire 34-part Essay on the Five Teachings.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1866, vol. 45, 華嚴一乘教義分齊章 Author: Fazang 法藏, 643–712, Tang Reading notes: Ru-Yi, original

The Different Characteristic — Rafter and Tile Disagree in Kind

Having just shown that rafter and tile are, in one sense, Identical, Fazang immediately shows they are, in another sense, and just as truly, Different — and that neither claim can stand without the other.

Chinese Source

第四異相者。椽等諸緣隨自形類相望差別故。問若異者應不同耶。答秖由異故。所以同耳。若不異者椽既丈二。瓦亦應爾。壞本緣法故。失前齊同成舍義也。今既舍成。同名緣者當知異也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, the Different characteristic: the rafter and the other conditions, considered against one another according to their own respective shapes and kinds, differ.

Question: If they differ, should they not then fail to be identical? Answer: It is precisely because they differ that they can be identical at all. If they did not differ — if, the rafter being twelve feet long, the tile too had to be exactly the same — this would destroy the original nature of the conditions, and the previous meaning of their equally cooperating to make the house would be lost. Since the house is, in fact, now accomplished, and they are all called "conditions" under the same name, we should know that they are, at the same time, different.

問此與別相有何異耶。答前別相者。但椽等諸緣。別於一舍故說別相。今異相者。椽等諸緣迭互相望。各各異相也。問若不異者。有何過失耶。答有斷常過。何者。若不異者。瓦即同椽丈二。壞本緣法不共成舍故是斷。若壞緣不成舍而執有舍者。無因有舍故是常也。

Question: How does this differ from the Particular characteristic? Answer: The Particular characteristic discussed earlier speaks only of the rafter and the other conditions being distinguished from the single house. This Different characteristic, by contrast, speaks of the rafter and the other conditions considered against one another, each and every one distinct in its own way.

Question: If they were not different, what fault would follow? Answer: There would be the faults of severance and permanence. Why? If they were not different, the tile would be exactly the same as a rafter twelve feet long — destroying the original nature of the conditions, so that they could not, together, make the house at all; hence severance. And if, the conditions being thus destroyed and unable to make the house, one nonetheless insisted a house existed, this would be having an effect without a cause; hence permanence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Fazang's own example is deliberately concrete: imagine a rafter twelve feet long, and imagine, absurdly, that a tile had to match it exactly in shape and size to count as a genuine "condition" of the house. Nothing could be built that way — a house needs its rafters long and its tiles small and curved, each part contributing exactly what the others cannot. This is the necessary complement to Part 33's Identical characteristic: rafter and tile agree perfectly in the single function they both serve, and disagree completely in the shape and substance by which they serve it. Sameness of purpose and difference of kind are not in tension; the second is what makes the first possible. A house built of parts that were all, in every respect, identical to one another would not be a more unified house — it would not be a house at all.

The Formative Characteristic — The House Arises Because No Part Tries to Be It

The fifth characteristic turns to how the house actually comes into being — and offers Fazang's most paradoxical-sounding claim in the whole illustration: that the house is accomplished precisely because the rafter does not act to become it.

Chinese Source

第五成相者。由此諸緣舍義成故。由成舍故。椽等名緣。若不爾者二俱不成。今現得成故知成相互成之耳。問現見椽等諸緣。各住自法本不作舍。何因得有舍義成耶。答秖由椽等諸緣不作故。舍義得成。所以然者。若椽作舍去。即失本椽法故。舍義不得成。今由不作故。椽等諸緣現前故。由此現前故。舍義得成矣。又若不作舍椽等不名多緣。今既得緣名。明知定作舍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, the Formative characteristic: it is because of these very conditions that the meaning of the house is accomplished; and it is because the house is accomplished that the rafter and the rest are called "conditions" at all. Were it not so, neither could be constituted; since, in fact, both are presently accomplished, we know that the Formative characteristic is precisely this mutual accomplishing of one another.

Question: We can plainly see that the rafter and the other conditions each abide in their own dharma, and fundamentally do not act to make the house — by what cause, then, can the meaning of the house be accomplished? Answer: It is precisely because the rafter and the other conditions do not act that the meaning of the house is accomplished. The reason is this: if the rafter went and acted to make the house, it would lose its own, original nature as a rafter, and the meaning of the house could not be accomplished. It is precisely because it does not act that the rafter and the other conditions remain present before us; and it is because they remain present in this way that the meaning of the house is accomplished. Moreover, if they did not act to make the house, the rafter and the rest could not be called "many conditions" at all; and since they have, in fact, obtained the name "condition," it is clear that they do indeed, in this sense, act to make the house.

問若不成者。何過失耶。答有斷常過。何者。舍本依椽等諸緣成。今既並不作。不得有舍故。是斷也。本以緣成舍名為椽。今既不作舍故無椽是斷。若不成者舍無因有故是常也。又椽不作舍得椽名者亦是常也。

Question: If the house were not accomplished, what fault would follow? Answer: There would be the faults of severance and permanence. Why? The house is, from the outset, accomplished in dependence on the rafter and the other conditions; if, now, none of them acted at all, there could be no house — hence severance. It is precisely because a condition makes the house that it is called a "rafter" in the first place; if, now, it did not make the house, there would be no rafter at all — this too is severance. And if, the house not being accomplished, one nonetheless insisted a house existed without any cause, this would be permanence. And likewise, if a rafter that did not make the house could still obtain the name "rafter," this too would be permanence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

This is the hardest turn in the whole illustration, because Fazang appears to say two opposite things in the same breath: the rafter "acts to make the house" (the closing line of the Q&A) and the rafter "does not act" (the answer that opens it). The two claims use "act" 作 in two different senses, and separating them is the whole solution. In the sense Fazang denies, "acting" would mean the rafter transforming itself into something other than a rafter — dissolving its own identity to become, directly, "house." That kind of acting, he insists, would be self-defeating: a rafter that stopped being a rafter in order to become the house would leave no rafter behind to have been a condition for anything, and the house could never be accomplished by a vanishing act like that. In the sense Fazang affirms, "acting to make the house" means something much simpler — remaining exactly, fully, unmistakably a rafter, and thereby doing the one thing a rafter alone can do to let a house arise around it. The house comes to be, in other words, precisely because every part refuses to try to be anything other than itself. This is the same insight Part 33's Total and Particular characteristics were already circling, now stated as a claim about causation rather than about identity: the whole is never built by its parts abandoning their distinctness, only by their fully occupying it.

The Disintegrating Characteristic — Each Part Remains Exactly Itself

The sixth and final characteristic restates the same insight from one more angle, making explicit what "not acting" really protects: the permanent, unshifting integrity of each single part, which is itself the very thing that lets the whole cohere.

Chinese Source

第六壞相者。椽等諸緣各住自法本不作故。問現見椽等諸緣作舍成就。何故乃說本不作耶。答秖由不作故。舍法得成。若作舍去不住自法有舍義即不成。何以故。作去失法舍不成故。今既舍成。明知不作也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, the Disintegrating characteristic: the rafter and the other conditions each abide in their own dharma, and fundamentally do not act.

Question: We can plainly see that the rafter and the other conditions act, and the house is thereby accomplished — why, then, is it said that they fundamentally do not act? Answer: It is precisely because they do not act that the dharma of the house can be accomplished. If they went and acted to make the house, not abiding in their own dharma, the meaning of the house could not be accomplished at all. Why? Because, having gone and acted, they would have lost their own dharma, and the house could not be accomplished. Since the house is, in fact, now accomplished, it is clear that they do not act.

問若作去有何失。答有斷常二失。若言椽作舍去。即失椽法。失椽法故。舍即無椽不得有。是斷也。若失椽法而有舍者。無椽有舍是常也。

Question: If they went and acted, what fault would follow? Answer: There would be the two faults of severance and permanence. If it is said that the rafter went and acted to make the house, it would thereby lose its own nature as a rafter; and having lost its own nature as a rafter, the house, being without a rafter, could not exist at all — this is severance. And if, having lost its own nature as a rafter, there were nonetheless said to be a house, this — a house without a rafter — would be permanence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Formative and Disintegrating describe the same fact from two directions, which is why their reasoning echoes so closely. Formative asked how the house comes to be, and answered: because the rafter never stops being a rafter. Disintegrating asks a slightly different question — why does the rafter, so evidently busy holding up a roof, count as something that "does not act" and "disintegrates" at all — and answers with the same fact turned around: because the rafter, for its entire useful life, never once becomes house, remaining permanently, unchangeably itself. "Disintegrating" is a deliberately unsettling name for what is, on inspection, a kind of fidelity: each part holds its own ground without wavering, and it is exactly this refusal to dissolve into the whole that keeps the whole from collapsing. A rafter that actually did dissolve into "house-ness" would not make the house more unified — it would simply leave no rafter behind, and, as the Formative characteristic already showed, no house behind it either.

The Six Characteristics, Restated, and the Treatise's Closing Verse

Fazang closes the entire doctrinal exposition of the treatise — everything since the Three Natures in Part 24 — with a single compressed restatement of all six characteristics, and then, for the first time since the opening homage that no longer survives in this recension, a verse of his own: eight lines distilling the whole teaching one final time.

Chinese Source

又總即一舍。別即諸緣。同即互不相違。異即諸緣各別。成即諸緣辦果。壞即各住自法。別為頌曰。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: the Total is exactly the single house; the Particular is exactly the several conditions; the Identical is exactly their not contradicting one another; the Different is exactly the several conditions each being distinct; the Formative is exactly the several conditions bringing about the result; the Disintegrating is exactly each one abiding in its own dharma. A verse is composed separately, saying:

Root Verse · 頌

一即具多名總相　多即非一是別相

多類自同成於總　各體別異現於同

一多緣起理妙成　壞住自法常不作

唯智境界非事識　以此方便會一乘

Ru-Yi Translation

The one, containing the many within it, is named the Total characteristic;

the many, not being one, are the Particular characteristic.

Many kinds, of themselves the same, accomplish the total;

each substance, distinct and different, manifests as the identical.

The dependent origination of the one and the many is wondrously accomplished;

disintegrating, each abides in its own dharma, forever unacting.

This is the domain of wisdom alone, not of discriminating consciousness —

by this skillful means, the One Vehicle is understood.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Read the eight lines as a compressed answer key to everything from Part 28 onward. Lines one and two are Total and Particular — the rafter that is the house, and the rafter that is only ever a distinct part of it. Lines three and four are Identical and Different, in the reverse order from how Fazang first presented them: "many kinds, of themselves the same" is the rafter and tile agreeing in function; "each substance, distinct and different" is the same rafter and tile disagreeing in shape and kind. Line five names the Formative characteristic directly — dependent origination "wondrously accomplished." Line six restates the Disintegrating characteristic in the verse's own words, "forever unacting" 常不作, the same refusal-to-dissolve met throughout this part.

The last two lines are not a seventh characteristic; they are Fazang's own final judgment on the entire treatise, placed deliberately at its very close. "The domain of wisdom alone, not of discriminating consciousness" returns, one last time, to a distinction drawn as far back as Part 31's Gate 4: none of this — not the coins, not Indra's Net, not the rafter and the house — is a picture assembled by ordinary, discriminating thought for its own convenience. It is offered as how things actually are, available only to a wisdom that sees without dividing. And the treatise's very last clause, "by this skillful means, the One Vehicle is understood" 以此方便會一乘, folds the entire Six Characteristics section back into a single word already carrying the whole weight of the work: 方便, skillful means — not a lesser, provisional teaching to be discarded once the truth is grasped, but the actual vehicle, in the fullest Huayan sense, by which the "One Vehicle" named in this treatise's own title is finally, truly met.

The Treatise Complete

Here Fazang's own text ends, at the close of what the Taishō recension marks as the fourth and final fascicle — and here, correspondingly, this thirty-four-part translation ends with it.

It has been a long road to this rafter. The treatise opened, thirty-three parts ago, with the most basic possible question — is there really a "One Vehicle" distinct from the three, or is the Lotus Sūtra's parable of the three carts just a manner of speaking? — and answered it by building, gate by gate, doxographer by doxographer, teaching by teaching, an entire architecture of classification: the Five Teachings and Ten Doctrines, the sequence in which each was first preached, ten marks that set the One Vehicle apart from everything the three vehicles could offer. Then, across the treatise's long middle stretch, that architecture was tested against nearly every major topic Buddhist scholasticism had ever organized — consciousness, lineage, the stages of the path, the length of practice, the elimination of affliction, the bodies of a buddha — showing, again and again, how the same phenomena look different once seen through a teaching built on interpenetration rather than sequence.

Everything in that long middle stretch was preparation for what Parts 24 through this final part actually deliver: not another classification, but the thing being classified. The three natures that are, at bottom, not two. The six meanings by which a cause is genuinely a cause. And then, at last, the two teachings this treatise is remembered for above all others — the Ten Mysterious Gates, opened with nothing more than ten coins on a table and closed with the Avataṃsaka's own confession that almost no one can believe any of it; and the Six Characteristics, opened with a list of six terse definitions and closed with a rafter that turns out, worked through carefully enough, to already be the house it merely appears to hold up. Every reader who has followed these seven parts from the first coin to this last verse has watched the same single claim get made in argument after argument, image after image, until it can no longer be mistaken for a rhetorical flourish: one is exactly all, and all is exactly one — not as a mystical blur where distinctions dissolve, but as a structure precise enough to survive being questioned from every angle Fazang's own imagined interlocutor could devise.

Sudhana's fifty-three teachers, Maitreya's tower, Indra's net of jewels, the coin that is both wholly one and wholly ten — this site's Gaṇḍavyūha tells in narrative what Fazang has spent this treatise proving in argument. A reader who has walked both roads now holds the same teaching twice: once as a story that can be entered, and once as a doctrine that can be defended. Fazang's own final words ask for nothing further — only that the reader recognize what has just been shown as "the domain of wisdom alone." Whatever that recognition amounts to for any one reader, the treatise itself has nothing left to add. 華嚴一乘教義分齊章卷第四終 — The Essay on the Distinctions in Teaching and Meaning of the One Vehicle of the Avataṃsaka, Fascicle Four, is complete.
