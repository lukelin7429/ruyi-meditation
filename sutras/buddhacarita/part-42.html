The Distribution of the Relics (I)

Word of the Buddha's death reaches seven neighboring kings, each of whom sends an envoy to Kuśinagara demanding a portion of the relics — and the Malla people, proud and protective, refuse them all, provoking a siege. As armies gather at the city walls, the brahmin Droṇa, respected on both sides for his learning and restraint, appeals directly to the kings: that killing one another over the remains of a teacher whose entire life argued against violence, greed, and pride would itself be the most complete possible failure to have understood anything he taught.

Seven Kings, and a Siege

Chinese Source

佛所行讚分舍利品第二十八

彼諸力士眾，　奉事於舍利，
以勝妙香花，　興無上供養。

時七國諸王，　承佛已滅度，
遣使詣力士，　請求佛舍利。

彼諸力士眾，　敬重如來身，
兼恃其勇健，　而起憍慢心，
寧捨自身命，　不捨佛舍利。

彼使悉空還，　七王大忿恨，
興軍如雲雨，　來詣鳩夷城。

人民出城者，　悉皆驚怖還，
告諸力士眾：　「諸國軍馬來，
象馬車步眾，　圍遶鳩夷城。

城外諸園林，　泉池花果樹，
軍眾悉踐蹈，　榮觀悉摧碎。」

力士登城觀，　生業悉破壞，
嚴備戰鬪具，　以擬於外敵，
弓弩挽石車，　飛炬獨發來。

七王圍遶城，　軍眾各精銳，
羽儀盛明顯，　猶如七耀光，
鍾鼓如雷霆，　勇氣盛雲霧。

力士大奮怒，　開門而命敵，
長宿諸士女，　心信佛法者，
驚怖發誠願，　伏彼而不害，
隨親相勸諫，　不欲令鬪戰。

勇士被重鉀，　揮戈舞長劍，
鍾鼓而亂鳴，　執仗鋒未交。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhacarita, Chapter Twenty-Eight: The Distribution of the Relics

The Malla people, honoring the relics with reverent service, made offerings of the most excellent fragrant flowers, the highest form of veneration they could give.

At that time the kings of seven neighboring realms, hearing that the Buddha had already entered final nirvāṇa, each sent an envoy to the Malla people, requesting a share of the Buddha's relics.

The Malla people, holding the Tathāgata's own body in the deepest reverence, and, besides, relying on their own strength and valor, gave rise to a heart of pride and arrogance, declaring that they would sooner give up their own lives than give up the Buddha's relics.

The envoys returned empty-handed, and the seven kings, filled with great fury, raised armies like gathering storm clouds and marched on the city of Kuśinagara.

The people who had gone out from the city returned in terror, telling the Malla people: "Armies and cavalry from every kingdom are approaching — elephants, horses, chariots, and foot soldiers surrounding the city of Kuśinagara.

Every garden and grove outside the city, every spring, pond, flower, and fruit tree, has already been trampled by the advancing troops, every beautiful sight there utterly destroyed."

The Malla people climbed the city walls to look, and, seeing their own livelihood already ruined, prepared their own weapons of war to meet the enemy — bows, crossbows, catapults hurling stones, and flaming torches launched one after another.

The seven kings surrounded the city, their armies each elite and well-trained, their banners and insignia shining brilliantly, like the light of the seven luminaries themselves, their bells and drums crashing like thunder, their martial spirit rising thick as gathering clouds.

The Malla people, roused to great fury, threw open their gates and challenged the enemy to battle; yet the elder men and women among them, those whose hearts held faith in the Buddha's own Dharma, were struck with dread, and made a sincere plea that the enemy be subdued rather than harmed, urging their own kinsmen not to let the battle actually be fought.

The warriors on both sides stood clad in heavy armor, brandishing spears and swords, bells and drums clashing in confused uproar — weapons already raised, though the blades themselves had not yet met.

Droṇa's Appeal to the Kings

Chinese Source

有一婆羅門，　名曰獨樓那，
多聞智略勝，　謙虛眾所宗。

慈心樂正法，　告彼諸王言：
「觀彼城形勢，　一人亦足當。

況復齊心力，　而不能伏彼？

正使相摧滅，　復有何德稱？

利鋒刃既交，　勢無有兩全，
困此而害彼，　二俱有所傷。

鬪戰多機變，　形勢難測量，
或有強勝弱，　或弱而勝強。

健夫輕毒蛇，　豈不傷其身？

有人性柔弱，　群女子所獎。

臨陣成戰士，　如火得膏油，
鬪莫輕弱敵，　謂彼無所堪，
身力不足恃，　不如法力強。

古昔有勝王，　名迦蘭陀摩，
端坐起慈心，　能伏大怨敵。

雖王四天下，　名稱財利豐，
終歸亦皆盡，　如牛飲飽歸。

應以法以義，　應以和方便，
戰勝增其怨，　和勝後無患。

今結飲血讐，　此事甚不可，
為欲供養佛，　應隨佛忍辱。」

如是婆羅門，　決定吐誠實，
方宜義和理，　而作無畏說。

爾時彼諸王，　告婆羅門言：
「汝今善應時，　黠慧義饒益，
親密至誠言，　順法依強理。

且聽我所說，　為王者之法，
或因五欲諍，　嫌恨競強力；
或因其嬉戲，　不急致戰爭；
吾等今為法，　戰爭復何怪？

憍慢而違義，　世人尚伏從，
況佛離憍慢，　化人令謙下，
我等而不能，　亡身而供養？

昔諸大地主，　弼瑟阿難陀，
為一端正女，　戰爭相摧滅；
況今為供養，　清淨離欲師，
愛身而惜命，　不以力爭求，
先王驕羅婆，　與般那婆戰，
展轉更相破，　正為貪利故；
況為無貪師，　而復貪其生？

羅摩仙人子，　瞋恨千臂王，
破國殺人民，　正為瞋恚故；
況為無恚師，　而惜於身命？

羅摩為私陀，　殺害諸鬼國；
況無攝受師，　不為其沒命？

阿利及婆俱，　二鬼常結怨，
正為愚癡故，　廣害於眾生；
況為智慧師，　而復惜身命？

如是比眾多，　無義而自喪，
況今天人師，　普世所恭敬，
計身而惜命，　不勤求供養？

汝若欲止爭，　為吾等入城，
勸彼令開解，　使我願得滿。

以汝法言故，　令我心小息，
猶如盛毒蛇，　呪力故暫止。」

爾時婆羅門，　受彼諸王教，
入城詣力士，　問訊以告誠：
「外諸人中王，　手執利器仗，
身被於重鉀，　精銳耀日光，
奮師子勇氣，　咸欲滅此城。

然其為法故，　猶畏非法行，
是故遣我來，　旨欲有所白。

我不為土地，　亦不求錢財，
不以憍慢心，　亦無懷恨心，
恭敬大仙故，　而來至於此；
汝當知我意，　何為苦相違？

尊奉彼我同，　則為法兄弟，
世尊之遺靈，　一心共供養。

慳惜於錢財，　此則非大過，
法慳過最甚，　普世之所薄。

決定不通者，　當修待賓法，
無有剎利法，　閉門而自防。

彼等悉如是，　告此吉凶法。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a brahmin named Droṇa, of wide learning and excellent judgment, humble and modest, revered by everyone.

With a heart of compassion and a delight in the true Dharma, he said to the kings: "Observing the layout and defenses of this city, a single man alone could hold it against attack.

How much more so, then, when its people have joined their hearts and their strength together — could you truly hope to subdue them at all?

And even should you succeed in destroying them utterly, what honor or renown would there be in that?

Once sharp blades have crossed, there is no possibility that both sides emerge unscathed — even in overpowering the other, both sides will surely suffer injury.

Battle is full of sudden reversals, its outcome exceedingly hard to predict in advance — sometimes the strong overcome the weak, and sometimes the weak overcome the strong instead.

A strong man who treats a venomous serpent lightly — will he not still be bitten and harmed by it regardless?

There are those who, though seemingly soft and weak by nature, are urged on and emboldened by a whole crowd of women behind them.

Facing battle, even such a person may become a fierce warrior, like a fire fed with oil — never underestimate a seemingly weak enemy, nor assume he can accomplish nothing at all; for one's own bodily strength alone is not something to be relied upon — it is nowhere near as strong as the power of the Dharma itself.

In ages past there was an excellent king named Kalandaka, who, sitting calmly and giving rise to a heart of compassion, was able to subdue even his greatest enemy by that alone.

Even a king who rules over all four continents, whose renown and wealth are vast beyond measure, must in the end see it all come to nothing, like an ox that drinks its fill and then simply wanders off.

One ought to resolve matters through the Dharma, through righteousness, through peaceful and skillful means — for victory won through battle only deepens the enmity between two sides, while victory won through reconciliation leaves no further trouble behind it at all.

To bind yourselves now in a blood-feud of this kind is a thing that ought never to be permitted — for if it is truly the Buddha you wish to honor, you ought instead to follow his own teaching of patient forbearance."

Thus did the brahmin speak his sincere and settled convictions in full, offering fearless counsel grounded in righteousness and reconciliation.

At this the kings said to the brahmin: "You have spoken well and at exactly the right moment, with wisdom and benefit both, sincere and heartfelt words that accord with the Dharma and rest on solid reasoning.

Yet hear now what I have to say in turn, concerning the very way of kings: sometimes kings go to war over the five desires, harboring grudges and contending for supremacy; sometimes they go to war merely for sport, with no urgent cause behind it at all — but we ourselves now go to war for the sake of the Dharma itself; why, then, should this seem strange at all?

Even ordinary people of this world submit and yield when confronted with mere pride that runs contrary to righteousness — how much more so, then, should we, when the Buddha himself was entirely free of pride, and taught others to humble themselves in just this way, fail to give even our own lives in offering to him?

In ages past, the great lords Bhīṣma and Ānanda once went to war and destroyed one another over a single beautiful woman — how much more so, then, should we, seeking now only to honor a teacher pure and free of desire, cling instead to our own bodies and lives, and refuse to contend for this with all our strength? The former kings Kaurava and Pāṇḍava once warred against one another, destroying each other in turn, purely out of greed for gain — how much more so, then, should we, honoring a teacher entirely free of greed, still cling greedily to our own lives?

The sage Rāma's own son, in his fury at the thousand-armed king, destroyed his whole kingdom and slaughtered its people, purely out of anger and hatred — how much more so, then, should we, honoring a teacher entirely free of anger and hatred, still cling to our own bodies and lives?

Rāma, for Sītā's sake, slaughtered whole kingdoms of demons — how much more so, then, should we, lacking even so gracious a teacher as this, fail to give up our own lives for his sake?

The two demons Ari and Bhoga remained locked in perpetual enmity, causing great harm to sentient beings purely out of their own foolishness and delusion — how much more so, then, should we, honoring a teacher of true wisdom, still cling to our own bodies and lives?

So many such cases as these exist, of people destroying themselves for causes with no true righteousness behind them at all — how much more so, then, should we, faced with the teacher of gods and humans, revered throughout the whole world, calculate the worth of our own lives and fail to strive diligently to honor him?

If you truly wish to put an end to this conflict, then go, on our behalf, into the city, and persuade them to relent and come to understanding, so that our own wish might be fulfilled.

Because of your own words, spoken in accordance with the Dharma, our hearts have already grown a little calmer — like a fierce and venomous serpent temporarily subdued by the power of a spell."

The brahmin, receiving the kings' own instruction, entered the city and went to the Malla people, greeting them and speaking to them earnestly: "The kings gathered outside, foremost among men, hold sharp weapons in their hands, their bodies clad in heavy armor, their elite troops shining bright as the sun, roused with the very courage of lions, all intent on destroying this city.

Yet it is for the sake of the Dharma that they still fear to act against the Dharma — and it is for this very reason that they have sent me here, wishing to convey something to you.

I myself seek neither land nor wealth, and I come with no thought of pride or arrogance, nor any grudge or resentment in my heart — I have come here solely out of reverence for this great sage. You ought to understand my true intent — why, then, should we remain so bitterly opposed to one another?

Since you and I both hold him in the same reverence, we are, in truth, brothers in the Dharma — let us honor the World-Honored One's own remaining spirit together, with a single, united heart.

To be miserly with one's own wealth is no great fault — but to be miserly with the Dharma itself is the very gravest of faults, one the whole world holds in contempt.

Even one who is firmly resolved not to negotiate at all ought still to observe the proper courtesy due a guest — for there is no principle among kṣatriyas that says one should simply shut one's gates and defend oneself alone."

In this way did Droṇa explain to them fully this policy, and what fortune or misfortune it might bring.
