Ārāḍa and Udraka (II)

Asked how his system is actually practiced, Ārāḍa lays out a recognizable path — renunciation, moral discipline, solitude, and the four meditative absorptions — that climbs past the fourth dhyāna into three formless attainments and rests at last in "the base of nothing whatsoever," which he calls true liberation. The prince's answer is exact rather than dismissive: if a self still exists there to know that nothingness, that self can still meet new conditions and be bound all over again, the way a seed set aside from soil and water still sprouts the moment they meet again. He and his teacher part on these terms, and the prince goes on to Udraka Rāmaputra, whose still subtler attainment fails for exactly the same reason.

The Path to Nothing Whatsoever

Chinese Source

太子聞斯說，　復問阿羅藍：
「云何為方便？　究竟至何所？

行何等梵行？　復應齊幾時？

何故修梵行？　法應至何所？

如是諸要義，　為我具足說。」

時彼阿羅藍，　如其經論說，
自以慧方便，　更為略分別：
「初離俗出家，　依倚於乞食，
廣集諸威儀，　奉持於正戒。

少欲知足止，　精麤任所得，
樂獨修閑居，　勤習諸經論。

見貪欲怖畏，　及離欲清涼，
攝諸根聚落，　安心於寂默。

離欲惡不善，　欲界諸煩惱，
遠離生喜樂，　得初覺觀禪。

既得初禪樂，　及與覺觀心，
而生奇特想，　愚癡心樂著，
心依遠離樂，　命終生梵天。

慧者能自知，　方便止覺觀，
精勤求上進，　第二禪相應。

味著彼喜樂，　得生光音天；
方便離喜樂，　增修第三禪。

安樂不求勝，　生於遍淨天；
捨彼意樂者，　逮得第四禪。

苦樂已俱息，　或生解脫想。

任彼四禪報，　得生廣果天，
以彼久壽故，　名之為廣果。

於彼禪定起，　見有身為過，
增進修智慧，　厭離第四禪。

決定增進求，　方便除色欲，
始自身諸竅，　漸次修虛解。

終則堅固分，　悉成於空觀，
略空觀境界，　進觀無量識。

善於內寂靜，　離我及我所，
觀察無所有，　是無所有處。

文闇皮骨離，　野鳥離樊籠，
遠離於境界，　解脫亦復然。

是上婆羅門，　離形常不盡，
慧者應當知，　是為真解脫。

汝所問方便，　及求解脫者，
如我上所說，　深信者當學。

林祇沙仙人，　及與闍那伽，
毘陀波羅沙，　及餘求道者，
悉從於此道，　而得真解脫。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The prince, hearing this teaching, asked Ārāḍa further: "What, then, is the actual practice? And where, in the end, does it lead?

What kind of holy conduct is to be undertaken, and for how long must it be sustained?

Why cultivate this holy conduct at all, and to what point ought the Dharma be carried?

I ask you to explain to me every one of these essential points, in full."

Ārāḍa, drawing again upon his own scriptures and treatises, now made a further, more detailed distinction, according to his own wisdom and skillful means: "First one leaves the ordinary household life and goes forth, relying on alms begged from others, and gathers together a full range of proper deportment, upholding the precepts correctly.

With few desires and knowing contentment, taking whatever one receives, whether fine or coarse, one delights in solitude and cultivates a life of quiet withdrawal, diligently studying the scriptures and treatises.

Seeing craving and desire as a source of dread, and the coolness that comes from being free of desire, one gathers in one's faculties as one would gather in a village's own boundaries, and settles the mind in quiet stillness.

Free of desire, free of evil and unwholesome states, free of every affliction belonging to the realm of desire, and with this freedom giving rise to joy and bliss, one attains the first dhyāna, accompanied still by examination and sustained reflection.

Having attained the bliss of this first dhyāna, together with its examining and reflecting mind, one may give rise to a strange and mistaken notion — the foolish mind clings to this very state with delight, and, its heart resting on the bliss of this seclusion, is reborn, at the end of that life, in the Brahmā heavens.

But the wise, knowing this for themselves, apply skillful means to still even this examination and reflection, and, striving diligently to advance further, attain accord with the second dhyāna.

Should one become attached instead to the joy and bliss found there, one is reborn in the Heaven of Radiant Sound; but applying skillful means to become free even of this joy and bliss, one cultivates further and advances to the third dhyāna.

Content in its ease and seeking nothing more excellent, one is reborn in the Heaven of Universal Purity; but relinquishing even this contentment of mind, one attains the fourth dhyāna.

Both suffering and pleasure now equally stilled, one may at this point give rise to the notion that this itself is liberation.

Yet resting merely in the fruit of this fourth dhyāna, one is reborn instead in the Heaven of Great Reward, so called because of the great length of life enjoyed there.

Rising up from within this very absorption, one comes to see that having a body at all is itself a fault, and, advancing further in the cultivation of wisdom, grows weary of even this fourth dhyāna.

Resolved to press on still further, one applies skillful means to remove all attachment to form, beginning with the very apertures of one's own body, and gradually cultivates an understanding of emptiness.

In the end, this understanding becomes firm and complete, resolving entirely into the contemplation of empty space; and, setting aside even this object of the contemplation of space, one advances to the contemplation of infinite consciousness.

Skilled now in inward stillness, free of both self and what belongs to the self, one contemplates the utter absence of anything at all — and this is called the base of nothing whatsoever.

As a caged bird set free into the wild sky is free of its confinement, or as the pith is separated at last from the bark and bone of the body — liberation, too, is exactly like this: a being's freedom from every object of the senses.

This is the supreme attainment of the brahmins, in which the eternal is separated from the physical form entirely; the wise ought to understand that this alone is true liberation.

As for the practice you have asked me about, and the pursuit of liberation itself — it is exactly as I have explained above, and whoever has deep faith in it ought to take up this very practice.

The seer Vaiśika, and Jaigīṣavya, Bhāradvāja, and every other seeker of the path besides — every one of them has followed this very same path, and thereby attained true liberation."

The Prince's Critique

Chinese Source

太子聞彼說，　思惟其義趣，
發其先宿緣，　而復重請問：
「聞汝勝智慧，　微妙深細義，
於知因不捨，　則非究竟道。

性轉變知因，　說言解脫者，
我觀是生法，　亦為種子法。

汝謂我清淨，　則是真解脫；
若遇因緣會，　則應還復縛。

猶如彼種子，　時地水火風，
離散生理乖，　遇緣種復生。

無知業因愛，　捨則名解者，
存我諸眾生，　無畢竟解脫。

處處捨三種，　而復得三勝，
以我常有故，　彼則微細隨，
微細過隨故，　心則離方便。

壽命得長久，　汝謂真解脫，
汝言離我所，　離者則無有。

眾數既不離，　云何離求那？

是故有求那，　當知非解脫。

求尼與求那，　義異而體一，
若言相離者，　終無有是處。

暖色離於火，　別火不可得，
譬如身之前，　則無有身者。

如是求那前，　亦無有求尼，
是故先解脫，　然後為身縛。

又知因離身，　或知或無知，
若言有知者，　則應有所知；
若有所知者，　則非為解脫。

若言無知者，　我則無所用；
離我而有知，　我即同木石。

具知其精麤，　背麤而崇微。

若能一切捨，　所作則畢竟。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The prince, hearing this teaching, reflected carefully on its actual meaning, and, drawing on causes and conditions from a former life, questioned him further still: "I have heard of your excellent wisdom, and the subtle and deeply refined meaning you have taught — yet as long as one does not set aside even the very knowing of the cause itself, this cannot be the ultimate path.

You speak of liberation as the knowing of the cause behind the transformation of this primal nature — but as I examine it, this very cause remains itself a conditioned thing, no different from a seed still capable of germinating.

You hold that once the self is purified, this alone is true liberation — yet should it ever again meet with the right causes and conditions, it would surely be bound all over again.

It is just like that very seed: when earth, water, fire, and wind are withheld from it, its own capacity to sprout lies dormant and separated from what it needs — but the moment it meets those same conditions again, the seed sprouts once more.

To call it liberation simply because ignorance, karmic action, and craving have been set aside, while a self is still held to remain — this cannot, for any of these sentient beings, be a truly final liberation at all.

In every stage of the path you describe, one sets aside three things and attains, in turn, three more excellent ones — yet because the self itself is held to exist permanently throughout, these three subtle qualities merely follow along after it in ever finer form; and it is precisely because these subtle faults continue to follow the self in this way that the mind, in the end, remains without any true and final means of escape.

You call it true liberation simply because life is thereby prolonged to a great length — you say this is "being apart from what belongs to the self," yet if this "apart" is genuine, then nothing at all would remain to be apart from anything else.

Since the very sum of these qualities themselves has never actually been left behind, how could one claim to be apart from the qualities (guṇa) they belong to?

And so, wherever such qualities still exist, one ought to understand that this is not liberation at all.

The knower (puruṣa) and the qualities (guṇa) known differ in meaning, and yet share one and the same underlying reality — to claim that they can be truly separated from one another is, in the end, simply not possible.

Just as the warmth of a form cannot be separated from its fire — a fire distinct from that very warmth cannot be found at all — so, too, prior to the existence of a body, there is no such thing as "a person" apart from it.

In just this same way, prior to the existence of these qualities, there is no such thing as a knower of them apart from them either — and so, were one to claim liberation comes first, and only afterward the body's own bondage, this too cannot be right.

Furthermore, as for this very "knowing of the cause" apart from the body — is it a knowing, or is it no knowing at all? If you say it is indeed a form of knowing, then there must be something that is thereby known; and if there is indeed something known, then this cannot be liberation at all.

But if you say instead that it involves no knowing whatsoever, then this "self" would have no function to serve at all — for a knowing that exists apart from any self would make that self no different from a mere piece of wood or stone.

[Your system] fully acknowledges both the coarse and the subtle forms of this predicament, and simply turns its back on the coarse in order to exalt the subtle instead.

Only if one is able to set aside every one of these entirely can what one has undertaken finally be brought to its true completion."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

No commentator's exegesis survives for this poem, so this note offers only context the poem's own history and content make visible. This chapter's basic shape — the bodhisattva studying under two teachers, mastering each one's stated attainment, and rejecting both as short of full liberation — matches the account the Pali canon itself gives in the Buddha's own words, most fully in the Ariyapariyesana Sutta (MN 26): there the Buddha describes training under Āḷāra Kālāma to the "base of nothingness" (ākiñcaññāyatana) and under Uddaka Rāmaputta to "neither-perception-nor-non-perception" (nevasaññānāsaññāyatana), and leaving both because neither one led, in his own words, to disenchantment, dispassion, cessation, calm, direct knowledge, awakening, or nirvāṇa. Aśvaghoṣa's version tracks this outline closely — Ārāḍa's system culminates in exactly this "base of nothing whatsoever" (無所有處), and the poem states directly, a few lines after this chapter closes, that Udraka's own attainment goes one step further to a state beyond perception and non-perception, which the prince also declines for the same reason.

What is distinctive to Aśvaghoṣa is the philosophical content he gives Ārāḍa to teach, and it is worth naming directly: the scheme of a permanent, unchanging witness-self (called 我, corresponding to puruṣa) entangled with a transforming primal nature (性, prakṛti) that unfolds into the elements, sense-faculties, and sense-objects, together with the eightfold enumeration of delusion and the citation of the sage Kapila as its founder, corresponds closely to what later Sanskrit philosophical tradition systematized as Sāṃkhya. Composing in the second century CE, well before Sāṃkhya's classical texts were codified, Aśvaghoṣa gives one of the earliest literary witnesses to this system's core commitments — and puts into the prince's mouth a critique that targets its single most vulnerable point with real precision: if a self persists as something that "knows" its own purified state, that very self remains a conditioned thing capable of encountering new causes and conditions again, the way a seed lies dormant without water but sprouts the instant water returns. The rejection is not a dismissal of meditative attainment as such — the prince has just mastered the very same absorptions Ārāḍa describes — but a demonstration that no depth of concentration alone can substitute for seeing through the self that concentration is still, however subtly, being performed on behalf of.
