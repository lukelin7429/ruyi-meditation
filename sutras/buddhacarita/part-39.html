The Great Final Nirvāṇa (III)

The last teaching closes with diligence and wisdom named as the boat, lamp, medicine, axe, and bridge across every form of suffering — and then the Buddha opens the floor one final time for any remaining question about the four noble truths. Aniruddha answers for the whole silent assembly: not a single doubt remains. The Buddha's own last words follow, gentle and unsentimental at once, before he passes through the meditative absorptions in careful sequence and enters final nirvāṇa — an event so significant that the earth quakes, fire fills the sky, and every being still bound by desire, in every realm, is plunged into grief, save for Māra alone, who marks the occasion with music of his own.

Diligence, and Wisdom

Chinese Source

若人勤精進，　無利而不獲，
是故當晝夜，　精勤不懈怠。

山谷微流水，　常流故決石，
鑽火不精進，　徒勞而不獲，
是故當精進，　如壯夫鑽火。

善友雖為良，　不及於正念，
正念存於心，　眾惡悉不入。

是故修行者，　常當念其身，
於身若失念，　一切善則忘。

譬如勇猛將，　被鉀御強敵，
正念為重鎧，　能制六境賊，
正定撿覺心，　觀世間生滅，
是故修行者，　當習三摩提。

三昧已寂靜，　能滅一切苦，
智慧能照明，　遠離於攝受。

等觀內思惟，　隨順趣正法，
在家及出家，　斯應由此路。

生老死大海，　智慧為輕舟，
無明大闇冥，　智慧為明燈，
諸纏結垢病，　智慧為良藥，
煩惱棘刺林，　智慧為利斧，
癡愛駃水流，　智慧為橋梁，
是故當勤習，　聞思修生慧。

成就三種慧，　雖盲慧眼通，
無慧心虛偽，　是則非出家。

是故當覺知，　離諸虛偽法，
逮得微妙樂，　寂靜安隱處。

遵崇不放逸，　放逸為善怨，
若人不放逸，　得生帝釋處，
縱心放逸者，　則墮阿修羅。

安慰慈悲業，　所應我已畢，
汝等當精勤，　善自修其業，
山林空閑處，　增長寂靜心。

當自勤勸勉，　勿令後悔恨。

猶如世良醫，　應病說方藥，
抱病而不服，　是非良醫過。

我已說真實，　顯示平等路，
聞而不奉用，　此非說者咎。

於四真諦義，　有所不了者，
汝今悉應問，　勿復隱所懷。」

世尊哀愍教，　眾會默然住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a person apply diligent effort, there is nothing at all he could fail to gain by it — and so you must apply yourselves diligently, day and night, without ever growing slack or lazy.

Even the faint trickle of a mountain stream, flowing on without pause, is able in time to cut through solid stone; while friction applied without diligence in trying to start a fire only wastes one's own labor for nothing — and so you must be diligent, as a vigorous man is diligent when working the fire-drill.

However excellent a good friend may be, none surpasses right mindfulness itself — for as long as right mindfulness abides in the heart, no evil at all can find its way in.

And so a true practitioner ought always to keep this body of his own in mind — for should mindfulness of the body ever be lost, every goodness is lost right along with it.

It is like a brave and valiant general, clad in armor, holding off a powerful enemy — right mindfulness is itself just such heavy armor, able to hold in check the very thieves that are the six sense-objects; and right concentration restrains the wandering mind, allowing one to observe clearly the arising and ceasing of all things in this world — and so a true practitioner ought to cultivate this very samādhi.

Once samādhi has brought the mind to quiescent stillness, it is able to extinguish every kind of suffering; and wisdom itself is then able to shine forth in full clarity, entirely free of any further clinging or attachment.

To contemplate all things with perfect equanimity, reflecting inwardly and moving in accordance with the true Dharma — this is the very path that both the householder and the one who has gone forth from home alike ought to follow.

Wisdom is the very light boat that crosses the great ocean of birth, old age, and death; wisdom is the very bright lamp that dispels the great darkness of ignorance; wisdom is the very medicine that cures the sickness of every entangling defilement; wisdom is the very sharp axe that clears the thorny forest of the afflictions; and wisdom is the very bridge across the swift and turbulent current of ignorance and craving — and so you must diligently cultivate wisdom, generated through hearing the teaching, reflecting upon it, and putting it into actual practice.

One who has accomplished these three kinds of wisdom sees clearly with the eye of wisdom even if physically blind; while one without wisdom, his heart empty and false, is no true renunciate at all, whatever robe he wears.

And so you must awaken to this, and depart from every kind of empty and false teaching, that you might attain the most subtle and wondrous bliss, the place of true quiescent peace and security.

Honor and uphold vigilance always, for negligence is itself a true enemy to your own good — one who remains vigilant and free of negligence attains rebirth even in the very realm of Śakra, lord of the gods, while one who gives free rein to his own heart in careless negligence falls instead among the asuras.

The work of compassion and consolation that I was meant to accomplish has now been fully accomplished — you, then, must apply yourselves diligently, cultivating well your own practice, and, in the mountains and forests, in places of quiet solitude, deepen further your own quiescent and peaceful heart.

Encourage and exert yourselves diligently, so that you might never come, later, to feel any regret over this.

It is like a skilled physician of this world, who prescribes the proper medicine according to the illness before him — should the patient hold that medicine and yet fail to take it, this is no fault of the skilled physician at all.

I have already spoken the truth, and made clear this path of perfect equanimity — should you hear it and yet fail to put it into practice, this, too, is no fault belonging to the one who taught it.

Should there be anything at all in the meaning of the four noble truths that remains unclear to you, you ought now to ask me about it fully — do not hold back whatever is on your minds."

The World-Honored One, having taught with such deep compassion, was met only with silence from the whole assembly.

The Last Question, and the Final Words

Chinese Source

時阿那律陀，　觀察諸大眾，
默然無所疑，　合掌而白佛：
「月溫日光冷，　風靜地性動，
如是四種惑，　世間悉已無。

苦集滅道諦，　真實未曾違，
如世尊所說，　眾會悉無疑。

唯世尊涅槃，　一切悉悲感，
不於世尊說，　起不究竟想。

正使新出家，　情未深解者，
聞今慇懃教，　疑惑悉已除。

已度生死海，　無欲無所求，
今皆生悲戀，　歎佛滅何速？」

佛以阿那律，　種種憂悲說，
復以慈愍心，　安慰而告言：
「正使經劫住，　終歸當別離，
異體而和合，　理自不常俱。

自他利已畢，　空住何所為？

天人應度者，　悉已得解脫。

汝等諸弟子，　展轉維正法，
知有必磨滅，　勿復生憂悲，
當自勤方便，　到不別離處。

我已燃智燈，　照除世闇冥，
世皆不牢固，　汝等當隨喜。

如親遭重病，　療治脫苦患，
已捨於苦器，　逆生死海流，
永離眾苦患，　是亦應隨喜。

汝等善自護，　勿生於放逸，
有者悉歸滅，　我今入涅槃。」

言語從是斷，　此則最後教。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Aniruddha, surveying the whole assembly and finding it entirely silent, with not a single doubt remaining anywhere, joined his palms and said to the Buddha: "Sooner would the moon grow warm, the sun's light turn cold, the wind fall utterly still, and the very nature of the earth itself begin to move — sooner would these four impossible things happen than any doubt of this kind remain anywhere in this world.

Concerning the truths of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path — their truth has never once been contradicted, exactly as the World-Honored One has taught them, and the whole assembly holds not the slightest doubt about any of it.

It is only the World-Honored One's own passing into nirvāṇa that fills everyone with grief — no one here has given rise to any doubt at all about the ultimate truth of what the World-Honored One has taught.

Even those newly gone forth from home, whose own understanding has not yet grown deep, have had every trace of doubt and confusion removed entirely, hearing this earnest teaching today.

Even those who have already crossed the ocean of birth and death, free of desire and seeking nothing further at all, now find grief and longing rising in themselves regardless, lamenting: how swiftly the Buddha now passes from us!"

The Buddha, hearing Aniruddha speak of this manifold grief and sorrow, once again, moved by a heart of compassionate concern, comforted them, saying: "Even should I remain for a full age of the world, in the end there would still have to be a parting, for whatever is made of distinct, combined elements can never, by its very nature, remain together forever.

The benefit that both I and others were meant to accomplish has already been fully accomplished — what purpose, then, would there be in my remaining any longer, uselessly, in this world?

Every god and human being who was meant to be delivered has already attained liberation.

You, my disciples, will carry the true Dharma onward from one to the next — know that whatever exists must, of necessity, come to dissolution, and give rise to no further grief and sorrow over it; instead, apply yourselves diligently, through your own skillful effort, to reach that place where no further parting exists at all.

I have already kindled the lamp of wisdom, and, by its light, dispelled the darkness of this world — nothing in this world is ever truly secure or lasting, and so you ought, instead, to rejoice together with me now.

It is like a kinsman who, once gravely ill, is now cured and freed from his own suffering — having now cast aside this vessel of suffering, having gone against the very current of the sea of birth and death, and having permanently departed from every affliction and suffering, this too is a cause for shared rejoicing.

Guard yourselves well, and never fall into carelessness and negligence — whatever exists returns, in the end, to dissolution; and I myself, now, enter final nirvāṇa."

From this point on, he spoke no further words at all — these were his very last teaching.

The Moment of Parinirvāṇa

Chinese Source

入初禪三昧，　次第九正受，
逆次第正受，　還入於初禪，
復從初禪起，　入於第四禪，
出定心無寄，　便入於涅槃。

以佛涅槃故，　大地普震動，
空中普雨火，　無薪而自焰。

又復從地起，　八方俱熾燃，
乃至諸天宮，　熾燃亦如是。

雷霆動天地，　霹靂震山川，
猶天阿修羅，　擊鼓戰鬪聲。

狂風四激起，　山崩雨灰塵，
日月無光暉，　清流悉沸涌，
堅固林萎悴，　華葉非時零。

飛龍乘黑雲，　垂五首淚流，
四王及眷屬，　含悲興供養。

淨居天來下，　虛空中列侍，
觀察無常變，　無憂亦無喜。

歎世違天師，　眼滅一何速？

八部諸天神，　遍滿虛空中，
散華以供養，　慼慼心不歡；
唯有魔王喜，　奏樂以自娛。

閻浮提失榮，　猶山頺巔崩，
大象素牙折，　牛王雙角摧。

虛空無日月，　蓮花遭嚴霜，
如來般涅槃，　世間悴亦然。

Ru-Yi Translation

He entered the first dhyāna in samādhi, and passed in due order through all nine successive absorptions; then, reversing the order, he passed back down through them and returned once more to the first dhyāna; and rising again from the first dhyāna, he entered the fourth dhyāna — and rising from that very absorption, his mind now resting nowhere at all, he entered final nirvāṇa.

At the Buddha's own entry into nirvāṇa, the great earth shook and trembled everywhere at once, and a rain of fire fell throughout the sky, blazing of itself with no fuel to feed it.

And fire, too, rose up from the earth itself, blazing in all eight directions at once, reaching even to the palaces of the gods, which blazed in exactly this same way.

Thunder shook heaven and earth, and lightning cracked across mountains and rivers, like the very sound of drums beaten by the asuras of heaven in battle.

A wild wind rose up violently on every side, mountains collapsed and rained down ash and dust, the sun and moon lost their own light, clear streams boiled and surged, the great forest itself withered and drooped, and flowers and leaves fell out of their proper season.

Flying dragons, riding upon dark clouds, wept tears streaming from all five of their heads at once; and the Four Heavenly Kings and their whole retinue, filled with grief, raised up offerings in mourning.

The gods of the Pure Abodes descended and arrayed themselves in attendance throughout the open sky, observing this impermanent transformation with neither grief nor joy.

They lamented only that the world had lost its own teacher of gods, sighing: how swiftly has this very eye of the world been extinguished!

The gods and spirits of the eight classes filled the whole of the open sky, scattering flowers in offering, their hearts heavy with sorrow and finding no joy in it at all — only Māra, king of the heavens, rejoiced, playing his own music in private celebration.

Jambudvīpa itself lost all its former glory, as though a mountain peak had suddenly crumbled and collapsed, as though a great elephant's own pure white tusks had been broken off, or a king among oxen had had both his horns shattered.

As the open sky is without sun or moon, or as a lotus flower is struck down by a harsh and bitter frost — so, too, did the whole world wither and fade at the Tathāgata's own entry into final nirvāṇa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

No commentator's exegesis survives for this poem, so this note draws only on comparison with the earlier canonical account. The sequence of absorptions entered just before the parinirvāṇa itself — ascending through the nine successive states, descending back down to the first dhyāna, then rising again only as far as the fourth before the final passing — matches precisely what the Pali Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN 16) describes as the Buddha's own last meditative movement, and later Buddhist tradition reads this specific pattern as itself significant: the Buddha does not simply cease from within the most exalted formless attainment, but returns deliberately to the fourth dhyāna, the same level of absorption from which, in some accounts, buddhas are said to perform their greatest feats, before passing from existence entirely. The poem does not explain this choice, but its careful, verse-by-verse tracking of the sequence — first ascending, then explicitly reversing, then rising again to a named, specific level — shows Aśvaghoṣa treating the manner of the Buddha's death with the same structural precision applied to the manner of his awakening two chapters earlier, as though the two events were meant to mirror each other formally even as one begins a teaching career and the other ends it.

The chapter's closing image — every realm still bound by desire thrown into mourning, with the single exception of Māra, who marks the Buddha's death with his own private music — completes a thread running through this entire biography since the temptation scene at the Bodhi tree. There, Māra's whole army broke uselessly against the Buddha's stillness because nothing it threatened or offered had any actual hold on a mind no longer susceptible to fear or desire; here, at the very end, Māra's isolated joy performs the same function in reverse, marking by sheer contrast just how completely the Buddha's own departure is mourned by every being still caught within the very desire realm Māra rules. The poem does not need to say that Māra has, in the end, gained nothing at all from this long contest — the placement of his lone celebration amid a universal grief he alone cannot share says as much on its own.
