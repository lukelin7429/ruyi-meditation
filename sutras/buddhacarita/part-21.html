Perfect Awakening (I)

With Māra's army dispersed, the bodhisattva's meditation deepens through the three watches of the night: in the first, he recalls his own countless past lives one by one; in the second, a purified divine eye shows him the same law of karma playing out across every being in all six realms of rebirth, rendered here in some of the poem's most unflinching imagery of hell, animal suffering, hungry ghosts, and the impermanence even of heaven; and in the third, he traces the twelvefold chain of dependent origination forward and backward until, with ignorance itself extinguished, perfect and complete awakening arises.

The First Watch: Recollection of Past Lives

Chinese Source

佛所行讚阿惟三菩提品第十四

菩薩降魔已，　志固心安隱，
求盡第一義，　入於深妙禪。

自在諸三昧，　次第現在前，
初夜入正受，　憶念過去生。

從某處某名，　而來生於此，
如是百千萬，　死生悉了知。

受生死無量，　一切眾生類，
悉曾為親屬，　而起大悲心。

大悲心念已，　又觀彼眾生，
輪迴六趣中，　生死無窮極。

虛偽無堅固，　如芭蕉夢幻，
即於中夜時，　逮得淨天眼。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhacarita, Chapter Fourteen: Perfect Awakening

Having subdued Māra, the bodhisattva's resolve grew firm and his heart entirely at peace; seeking to fathom the ultimate truth fully, he entered into a deep and wondrous meditative absorption.

Every mastery of samādhi presented itself before him in due order; in the first watch of the night, he entered true absorption and recollected his own past lives.

From such-and-such a place, under such-and-such a name, he had come to be born in this one — in just this way, across hundreds of thousands and tens of thousands of lives, he came to know fully every one of his own deaths and births.

Having undergone birth and death in this way, without measure, across every kind of sentient being, he saw that every one of them had, at some time, been his own kin — and a heart of great compassion rose up within him.

Having given rise to this great compassion, he then contemplated how these same beings, circling endlessly through the six realms of rebirth, suffer birth and death without any end at all —

empty and false, without any true solidity, like a plantain tree's hollow trunk, or like a dream, or an illusion. And then, in the middle watch of the night, he attained the pure and heavenly eye.

The Second Watch: The Divine Eye

Chinese Source

見一切眾生，　如觀鏡中像，
眾生生生死，　貴賤與貧富。

清淨不淨業，　隨受苦樂報，
觀察惡業者，　當生惡趣中。

修習善業者，　生於人天中，
若生地獄者，　受無量種苦。

吞飲於洋銅，　鐵槍貫其體，
投之沸鑊湯，　駈入盛火聚。

長牙群犬食，　利[口*(隹/乃)]鳥啄腦，
畏火赴叢林，　劍葉截其體。

利刀解其身，　或利斧斫剉，
受斯極苦毒，　業行不令死。

樂修不淨業，　極苦受其報，
味著須臾頃，　苦報甚久長。

戲笑種禍因，　號泣而受罪，
惡業諸眾生，　若見自報者。

氣脉則應斷，　恐怖崩血死，
造諸畜生業，　業種種各異。

死墮畜生道，　種種各異身，
或為皮肉死，　毛角骨尾羽。

更互相殘殺，　親戚還相噉，
負重而抱軛，　鞭策鉤錐刺。

傷體膿血流，　飢渴莫能解，
展轉相殘殺，　無有自在力，
虛空水陸中，　逃死亦無處。

慳貪增上者，　生於餓鬼趣，
巨身如大山，　咽孔猶針鼻，
飢渴火毒然，　還自燒其身。

求者慳不與，　或遮人惠施，
生彼餓鬼中，　求食不能得，
不淨人所棄，　欲食而變失。

若人聞慳貪，　苦報如是者，
割肉以施人，　如彼尸毘王。

或生人道中，　身處於行廁。

動轉極大苦，　出胎生恐怖，
軟身觸外物，　猶如刀劍截。

任彼宿業分，　無時不有死，
勤苦而求生，　得生長受苦。

乘福生天者，　渴愛常燒身，
福盡命終時，　衰死五相至。

猶如樹華萎，　枯悴失光澤，
眷屬存亡分，　悲苦莫能留。

宮殿廓然空，　玉女悉遠離，
坐臥塵土中，　悲泣相戀慕。

生者哀墮落，　死者戀生悲，
精勤修苦行，　貪求生天樂，
既有如此苦，　鄙哉何可貪？

大方便所得，　不免別離苦，
嗚呼諸天人，　脩短無差別。

積劫修苦行，　永離於愛欲，
謂決定長存，　而今悉墮落。

地獄受眾苦，　畜生相殘殺，
餓鬼飢渴逼，　人間疲渴愛，
雖云諸天樂，　別離最大苦。

迷惑生世間，　無一蘇息處，
嗚呼生死海，　輪轉無窮已，
眾生沒長流，　漂泊無所依。

Ru-Yi Translation

He beheld every sentient being as clearly as one sees a reflection in a mirror — beings born and dying again and again, whether noble or base, rich or poor.

Following their pure or impure karma, they received in turn a painful or pleasant reward; and he saw that those who had done evil deeds were bound to be born into the evil destinies,

while those who had cultivated good deeds were born among humans or gods. As for those born into the hells, they underwent suffering of countless kinds:

forced to swallow molten copper, their bodies pierced through with iron spears, hurled into cauldrons of boiling water, driven into blazing masses of fire.

Devoured by packs of long-fanged dogs, their very brains pecked out by sharp-taloned birds; fleeing in terror of the flames into a forest, only to have their bodies sliced apart by leaves sharp as swords.

Their bodies cut apart with sharp knives, or hacked and chopped with sharp axes — enduring such extreme suffering and torment, and yet their own karmic actions would not permit them even to die.

Those who had taken pleasure in impure deeds now received this extreme suffering in return; the pleasure they had tasted lasted but a fleeting moment, while the suffering that repaid it stretched on, exceedingly long.

Sowing the seeds of calamity amid laughter and play, they now wailed aloud as they received their punishment — such were the sentient beings of evil karma, when they beheld their own retribution.

Their very breath and pulse would seem to fail them, and, seized with terror, they would collapse and die in a flood of their own blood; and as for those who had created the various karma of the animal realm — this karma, too, differed each in its own particular kind.

At death they fell into the animal realm, taking on every manner of different body, some dying for the sake of their own skin and flesh, their fur, horns, bones, tails, or feathers.

Preying upon and slaughtering one another in turn, even devouring their own kin; bearing heavy loads beneath the yoke, driven on by whip, hook, and goad.

Their wounded bodies streaming with pus and blood, hunger and thirst never once relieved; destroying one another in endless succession, with no freedom of their own at all — whether in the open sky, in the water, or on dry land, there was nowhere at all to flee from death.

Those in whom miserliness and greed had grown to the utmost degree were born instead into the realm of hungry ghosts, their bodies enormous as great mountains, yet their throats no wider than the eye of a needle; hunger and thirst blazed within them like a burning poison, consuming their own bodies from within.

Those who had been miserly and refused to give when asked, or who had obstructed others from giving in charity, were born among these hungry ghosts, where, seeking food, they could never obtain it — and whatever unclean scraps others cast away, the very moment they sought to eat it, would vanish and turn to nothing before them.

Should anyone hear of the bitter retribution that miserliness and greed bring about in this way, let him instead cut his own flesh to give to others, as King Śibi once did.

Or [consider] one born instead into the human realm, whose very body must first dwell within a moving latrine —

every movement there an extreme torment, and the emergence from the womb itself a terror, his soft, tender body touched by the outside world as though slashed by knife or sword.

Bound entirely to the portion allotted by his own past karma, there is no moment at all free from the presence of death; and though one struggles painfully merely to be born, once born, one goes on to endure suffering at length regardless.

Even those reborn in heaven through the power of their own merit find their bodies forever scorched by thirst and craving; and once that merit is exhausted and their life comes to its end, the five signs of decline and death appear before them —

like a tree's blossoms withering, growing dry and lifeless, losing all their former luster; and as they part from their own kin, some remaining and some perishing, no grief or sorrow can hold them together any longer.

Their palace halls stand suddenly empty and hollow, every jeweled attendant having withdrawn far away; they sit or lie upon the bare dust, weeping in longing for what they have lost.

Those left behind grieve at seeing them fall; those who die grieve, in turn, at leaving the living behind. To strive diligently in ascetic practice only in order to obtain the pleasures of heaven — given that such suffering exists there as well, how base and unworthy a thing this craving truly is!

Even what is gained through the greatest of skillful means cannot escape the suffering of parting — alas, whether the life of gods or of men, in the end, there is no real difference between them at all in this.

Cultivating ascetic practice across accumulated ages, forever apart from craving and desire, believing themselves certain to endure forever — and yet even they, in the end, all fall away in just this same manner.

In the hells, beings undergo every manner of suffering; among animals, they prey upon and destroy one another; among hungry ghosts, they are driven by the torment of hunger and thirst; among humans, they are worn down by craving and desire — and though it is said that the gods enjoy their own pleasures, the suffering of parting remains, even there, the very greatest suffering of all.

Born into this world in utter confusion and delusion, there is not a single place of true rest or respite to be found anywhere — alas, this ocean of birth and death, circling on without end, in which sentient beings sink beneath its ceaseless current, adrift, with nothing at all to rely upon!

The Third Watch: Dependent Origination, and Awakening

Chinese Source

如是淨天眼，　觀察於五道，
虛偽不堅固，　如芭蕉泡沫，
即彼第三夜，　入於深正受。

觀察諸世間，　輪轉苦自性，
數數生老死，　其數無有量，
貪欲癡闇障，　莫知所由出。

正念內思惟，　生死何從起？

決定知老死，　必由生所致，
如人有身故，　則有身痛隨。

又觀生何因？　見從諸有業，
天眼觀有業，　非自在天生。

非自性非我，　亦復非無因，
如破竹初節，　餘節則無難。

既見生死因，　漸次見真實，
有業從取生，　猶如火得薪。

取以愛為因，　如小火焚山，
知愛從受生，　覺苦樂求安。

飢渴求飲食，　受生愛亦然，
諸受觸為因，　三等苦樂生。

鑽燧加人功，　則得火為用，
觸從六入生，　盲無明覺故。

六入名色起，　如芽長莖葉，
名色由識生，　如種芽葉生。

識還從名色，　展轉更無餘，
緣識生名色，　緣名色生識。

猶人船俱進，　水陸更相運，
如識生名色，　名色生諸根。

諸根生於觸，　觸復生於受，
受生於愛欲，　愛欲生於取。

取生於業有，　有則生於生，
生生於老死，　輪迴周無窮。

眾生因緣起，　正覺悉覺知，
決定正覺已，　生盡老死滅。

有滅則生滅，　取滅則有滅，
愛滅則取滅，　受滅則愛滅。

觸滅則受滅，　六入滅觸滅，
一切入滅盡，　由於名色滅。

識滅名色滅，　行滅則識滅，
癡滅則行滅，　大仙正覺成。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having, with this pure heavenly eye, observed the five destinies in this way — empty and false, without any true solidity, like the hollow trunk of a plantain tree, or like foam upon water — he then, in that third watch of the night, entered into a still deeper true absorption.

He contemplated the very nature of the world's own suffering, circling on endlessly, born and grown old and dying again and again, in numbers beyond all counting, obstructed always by the darkness of craving, desire, and delusion, with no knowledge at all of how to find the way out.

With right mindfulness he reflected inwardly: from what does birth and death actually arise?

He determined with certainty that old age and death must be brought about by birth itself — just as, because a person has a body at all, bodily pain necessarily follows after it.

He then contemplated further: what is the cause of birth? And he saw that it comes from the karma of conditioned existence — and with his heavenly eye he observed that this karma of existence is not brought about by Īśvara, the Sovereign God.

It is neither an inherent nature, nor a self, nor yet is it without any cause at all — it is like splitting bamboo: once the first joint is broken open, the rest present no difficulty at all.

Having seen the cause of birth and death, he came, step by step, to see the truth of the matter further still: this karma of existence arises from grasping, just as fire is sustained by fuel.

Grasping has craving as its cause, spreading like a small fire that burns down a whole mountain; and he came to know that craving arises from feeling, which perceives suffering and pleasure and seeks after ease.

Just as hunger and thirst give rise to the seeking of food and drink, so feeling gives rise to craving in just this same way; and every feeling has contact as its cause, from which the three kinds — pain, pleasure, and neutral sensation — all arise.

Just as friction, applied through human effort, produces fire fit for use, so contact arises from the six sense-bases, which exist because of the obstruction of blind ignorance.

The six sense-bases arise from name-and-form, as a sprout grows into stem and leaf; and name-and-form arises from consciousness, as a seed gives rise to sprout and leaf.

Consciousness, in turn, arises from name-and-form, each turning back upon the other with nothing left over — conditioned by consciousness, name-and-form arises; conditioned by name-and-form, consciousness arises.

Just as a person and a boat move forward together, each carrying the other across water and land in turn, so consciousness gives rise to name-and-form, and name-and-form gives rise to the [six] faculties.

The faculties give rise to contact; contact, in turn, gives rise to feeling; feeling gives rise to craving; craving gives rise to grasping.

Grasping gives rise to the karma of existence; existence, in turn, gives rise to birth; birth gives rise to old age and death — and so this wheel turns on, endlessly, without any final end.

Perfect and complete awakening consists precisely in fully realizing this dependent arising of sentient beings — and once this perfect and complete awakening is fixed with certainty, birth is exhausted and old age and death are extinguished.

When existence is extinguished, birth is extinguished; when grasping is extinguished, existence is extinguished; when craving is extinguished, grasping is extinguished; when feeling is extinguished, craving is extinguished.

When contact is extinguished, feeling is extinguished; when the six sense-bases are extinguished, contact is extinguished; and the complete extinguishing of all these sense-bases comes about through the extinguishing of name-and-form.

When consciousness is extinguished, name-and-form is extinguished; when formations are extinguished, consciousness is extinguished; and when ignorance itself is extinguished, formations are extinguished — and with this, the great sage's perfect and complete awakening was accomplished.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

No commentator's exegesis survives for this poem, so this note offers only what a comparison with the earlier canonical sources makes visible. The three-watches structure translated here — recollection of past lives, the divine eye surveying karma across the six destinies, and the direct realization of dependent origination — matches closely what the Pali canon itself gives as the content of the night of awakening, most fully in the Bhayabherava Sutta (MN 4) and the Mahāsaccaka Sutta (MN 36), where the Buddha describes attaining these same "three knowledges" (tevijjā) in this same order during the first, middle, and last watches of the night respectively. Aśvaghoṣa is not inventing new content here so much as giving the received structure of the awakening its fullest poetic expression, and the twelvefold chain traced forward and backward in the third watch (無明, ignorance, through 老死, old age and death, and back again through cessation) is the same twelvefold formula (dvādaśāṅga pratītyasamutpāda) found throughout the earliest sūtra literature, rendered here as verse rather than the more formulaic prose of the nikāyas and āgamas.

What is distinctive to this poem is the extended, unflinching detail of the divine-eye section's survey of the six realms — the molten copper, the sword-leaved forest, the hungry ghosts with throats "no wider than the eye of a needle," and, most strikingly, the observation that even heaven's own pleasures end in exactly the same grief of parting suffered everywhere else. This last point is the section's real argument: the passage does not exist merely to catalogue horror, but to make the case, through accumulated and comprehensive evidence across every possible destiny, that no rearrangement of one's circumstances within saṃsāra — better karma, a longer life, rebirth even among the gods — solves the problem that the whole of Chapter 14 exists to solve. Only the bodhisattva's third-watch insight into the causal chain that generates every one of the six realms alike, and its complete unwinding through the extinguishing of ignorance at the root, actually stops the wheel described in such exhaustive detail in the watch before it.
