Answering King Bimbisāra (II)

The prince's argument closes in on its target: even the "necessities" people take for granted — food against hunger, clothing against cold, sleep against fatigue — are only ever relief from one suffering or another, never a positive freedom in themselves, and a king's own eight worldly winds of praise and blame, gain and loss, leave him no better off than the servant he rules. He rejects animal sacrifice as no true path to merit, declines Bimbisāra's offer a final time, and sets out for the hermitage of Ārāḍa Kālāma, leaving the king to see him off with a request that he return once he has found what he seeks.

More Examples, and the Emptiness of Possessions

Chinese Source

束身投水火，　或投於高巖，
而求於天樂，　徒苦不獲利。

孫陶鉢孫陶，　阿修輪兄弟，
同生相愛念，　為欲相殘殺，
身死名俱滅，　皆由貪欲故。

貪愛令人賤，　鞭杖駈策苦，
愛欲卑希望，　長夜形神疲。

麋鹿貪聲死，　飛鳥隨色貪，
淵魚貪鉤餌，　悉為欲所困。

觀察資生具，　非為自在法，
食以療飢患，　除渴故飲水。

衣被却風寒，　臥以治睡眠，
行疲故求乘，　立惓求床座。

除垢故沐浴，　皆為息苦故，
是故應當知，　五欲非自在。

如人得熱病，　求諸冷治藥，
貪求止苦患，　愚夫謂自在。

而彼資生具，　亦非定止苦，
又令苦法增，　故非自在法。

溫衣非常樂，　時過亦生苦，
月光夏則涼，　冬則增寒苦。

乃至世八法，　悉非決定相，
苦樂相不定，　奴王豈有間？

教令眾奉用，　以王為勝者，
教令即是苦，　猶擔能任重。

普銓世輕重，　眾苦集其身，
為王多怨憎，　雖親或成患，
無親而獨立，　此復有何歡？

雖王四天下，　用皆不過一，
營求於萬事，　唐苦何益身？

未若止貪求，　息事為大安。

居王五欲樂，　不王閑寂歡，
歡樂既同等，　何用王位為？

Ru-Yi Translation

[Even those who] bind their own bodies and cast themselves into water or fire, or hurl themselves from high cliffs, seeking thereby the pleasures of heaven — all this labor is in vain, and gains them nothing at all.

Sunda and Upasunda, the two Asura brothers, born together and once devoted to one another in love, ended up destroying each other over desire; their bodies perished and their names were wiped out together, and all of this came about entirely on account of craving and desire.

Craving and attachment reduce a person to the lowest baseness, driven on as though by whip and lash; the base hopes born of craving and desire wear down body and spirit alike through one long night without end.

The musk deer dies for its craving of sound, the bird dies for its craving of color, and the fish in the depths is caught by its craving for the baited hook — every one of them is trapped, in the end, by desire alone.

Examining closely the very necessities of life, one finds that none of them constitutes any true freedom at all: one eats only to cure the affliction of hunger, one drinks water only to relieve thirst.

One wears clothing only to ward off wind and cold, one lies down only to remedy the need for sleep; one seeks a carriage only because walking has grown tiresome, one seeks a bed or a seat only because standing has grown wearisome.

One bathes only to remove dirt and grime — every one of these acts, in every case, exists only to bring some particular suffering to rest; and so one ought to understand from this that the five desires themselves grant no true freedom at all.

It is like a man suffering from a fever, seeking out every kind of cooling remedy; he seeks it only to bring his own suffering and affliction to an end, yet the foolish call this "freedom."

And these very necessities of life do not, in fact, bring suffering to any lasting rest at all — they may even increase the very conditions of suffering further, and so they cannot be called any true freedom either.

Warm clothing is no constant source of pleasure — once its proper season has passed, it too becomes a source of suffering; even moonlight, cool and pleasant in summer, only deepens the misery of the cold in winter.

Even the eight worldly conditions themselves hold no fixed or certain mark at all; the very marks of suffering and pleasure are never fixed — what real difference, then, is there between a servant and a king?

[It may be said that] when a king issues a command, the multitude obeys it, and it is for this that a king is accounted the more excellent of the two — yet the very issuing of such commands is itself a form of suffering, no different from a burden one must still bear the weight of.

Weighing and measuring the whole world's affairs in every direction, every kind of suffering gathers upon a king's own person: as a ruler, he draws much resentment and hatred; even those close to him may in time become a source of trouble; and to stand entirely alone, without a single true intimate — what joy could there possibly be in that?

Even one who rules as king over all four continents makes use, in the end, of no more than a single measure of anything at himself; to labor and strive after ten thousand different things — what real benefit does this bring to the one who bears all that useless toil?

It is far better simply to put an end to craving and grasping altogether, and find true peace in setting all such striving to rest.

Whether one dwells as a king amid the pleasures of the five desires, or dwells instead, without any kingdom at all, in the joy of quiet stillness — since the joy in either case amounts to exactly the same thing, what use, then, is the throne of a king at all?

Declining the Kingdom, and the Case Against Sacrifice

Chinese Source

汝勿作方便，　導我於五欲，
我情之所期，　清涼虛通道。

汝欲相饒益，　助成我所求，
我不畏怨家，　不求生天樂。

心不懷俗利，　而捨於天冠，
是故違汝情，　不從於來旨。

如免毒蛇口，　豈復還執持？

執炬而自燒，　何能不速捨？

有目羨盲人，　已解復求縛，
富者願貧窮，　智者習愚癡，
世有如此人，　則我應樂國。

欲度生老死，　節身行乞食，
寡欲守空閑，　後世免惡道，
是則二世安，　汝今勿哀我。

當哀為王者，　其心常虛渴，
今世不獲安，　後世受苦報。

汝以名勝族，　大丈夫禮儀，
厚懷處於我，　樂同世歡娛。

我亦應報德，　勸汝同我利，
若習三品樂，　是名世丈夫。

此亦為非義，　常求無足故，
若無生老死，　乃名大丈夫。

汝言少輕躁，　老則應出家，
我見年耆者，　力劣無所堪，
不如盛壯時，　志猛心決定。

死賊執劍隨，　常伺求其便，
豈聽至年老，　遂志而出家？

無常為獵師，　老弓病利箭，
於生死曠野，　常伺眾生鹿，
得便斷其命，　孰聽終年壽？

夫人之所為，　若生若滅事，
少長及中年，　悉應勤方便。

祠祀修大會，　是皆愚癡故，
應當崇正法，　反殺以祠天，
害生而求福，　此則無慈人。

害生果有常，　猶尚不應殺，
況復求無常，　而害生祠祀？

若無戒聞慧，　修禪寂靜者，
不應從世間，　祠祀設大會。

殺生得現樂，　慧者不應殺，
況復殺眾生，　而求後世福？

三界有為果，　悉非我所樂，
諸趣流動法，　如風水㵱草，
是故我遠來，　為求真解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Do not, then, devise some skillful scheme to lead me back into the five desires — what my own heart truly aspires to is the cool, clear, and open path of liberation.

I know that you wish only to benefit me, and to help bring about what I myself seek — yet I do not fear any enemy, and I do not seek the bliss of rebirth in heaven either.

My heart holds no thought at all for worldly gain — it was for this very reason that I cast aside even the heavenly diadem itself, and it is for this same reason that I must now, in going against your own generous intent, decline to follow the purpose of your offer.

Having just escaped the very mouth of a venomous serpent, how could I possibly turn around and take hold of it again?

Holding a burning torch that scorches one's own hand — how could one not cast it aside at once?

A man with sight who envies the blind, one already freed who seeks bondage again, the wealthy man who wishes for poverty, the wise man who takes up folly — should such people as these ever truly exist in this world, then perhaps I too might come to delight in ruling a kingdom.

Wishing to cross beyond birth, old age, and death, I discipline my own body and go about begging for alms; keeping my desires few and dwelling in solitude, I seek thereby to escape the evil destinies in the life to come — this alone brings true peace in both this life and the next, so you need not, then, grieve on my account.

You ought instead to grieve for those who rule as kings, whose hearts remain forever parched and unsatisfied — finding no true peace even in this present life, and destined to receive a bitter reward in the life to come besides.

You yourself, of so renowned a lineage, observing every courtesy proper to a truly great man, have shown me the depth of your own generous heart, wishing me to share equally in the joys and pleasures of this world.

Let me, in turn, repay your kindness by urging upon you the very benefit I myself have found: it is only in cultivating the three excellent kinds of joy that one may rightly be called a truly great man of this world.

Yet even this remains something less than the highest good, since such joys are pursued endlessly and never reach any true satisfaction; only one who has gone beyond birth, old age, and death entirely deserves, in the fullest sense, to be called a truly great man.

You have said that the young are restless and impulsive, and that one ought to go forth only in old age — yet I observe that those advanced in years find their own strength diminished, unequal to the task, and no match at all for the fierce resolve and settled determination found in the full vigor of youth.

The thief of death carries his blade always at the ready, forever watching for his opportunity to strike — how, then, could he possibly be counted on to wait until old age, allowing one to fulfill this resolve and go forth only then?

Impermanence is itself a hunter, his bow old and worn yet his arrows still sharp and deadly, forever watching, out in the wilderness of birth and death, for the deer that are all sentient beings; the instant he finds his opening, he cuts short their very lives — who, then, could ever be assured of living out his full span of years?

Whatever a person undertakes, whether it concerns what arises or what passes away, ought to be pursued with diligent effort at every stage of life alike — in youth, in old age, and in one's middle years equally.

As for holding great sacrificial assemblies and rites — these, too, arise entirely from ignorance and delusion; one ought instead to honor the true Dharma, rather than take life in order to offer sacrifice to the gods — for to harm living beings in the pursuit of one's own merit is the mark of a person entirely without compassion.

Even should the taking of life bring about some fixed and certain benefit, one still ought not to kill for it at all — how much less, then, should one harm living beings in pursuit of a benefit as uncertain as this, through mere sacrificial rites?

If one lacks moral discipline, learning, and wisdom, and does not cultivate meditative absorption and quiet stillness, one ought not, then, to follow the world's own custom of holding grand sacrificial assemblies at all.

Even killing for the sake of some pleasure in this present life is something the wise ought never to do — how much more so, then, killing living beings in the pursuit of merit in some life yet to come?

The conditioned fruits found throughout the three realms of existence are none of them anything I myself take delight in; every realm of rebirth is itself a thing in constant flux, like wind, like water, like drifting grass — and it is for this very reason that I have come this great distance, seeking nothing less than true liberation itself.

Farewell to King Bimbisāra

Chinese Source

聞有阿羅灆，　善說解脫道，
今當往詣彼，　大仙牟尼所。

誠言苦抑斷，　我今誨謝汝，
願汝國安隱，　善護如帝釋。

慧明照天下，　猶如盛日光。

殊勝大地主，　端心護其命，
正化護其子，　以法王天下。

氷雪火為怨，　緣火烟幢起，
烟幢成浮雲，　浮雲興大雨，
有鳥於空中，　飲雨不雨身。

殺重怨為宅，　居宅怨重殺，
有殺重怨者，　汝今應伏彼，
令其得解脫，　如飲不雨身。」

時王即叉手，　敬德心歡喜。

「如汝之所求，　願令果速成，
汝速成果已，　當還攝受我。」

菩薩心內許，　要令隨汝願，
交辭而隨路，　往詣阿羅藍。

王與諸群屬，　合掌自隨送，
咸起奇特想，　而還王舍城。

Ru-Yi Translation

I have heard that there is one named Ārāḍa, skilled in explaining the path of liberation, and I mean now to go to him — to the dwelling of that great sage and seer.

Having spoken these earnest words, cutting short further discussion of my own suffering, let me now offer you my thanks and take my leave; may your own kingdom remain forever secure and at peace, well guarded as Śakra's own realm is guarded.

May the light of your own wisdom shine out over the whole world, bright as the fullness of the sun's own light.

As the most excellent lord of this earth, guard your own life with an upright heart, and, ruling your people through true transformation, protect your own subjects as your own children, governing the whole world by the true Dharma.

Ice and snow hold fire as their natural enemy, yet fire itself gives rise to a column of smoke, and this column of smoke gathers into drifting clouds, and these drifting clouds, in turn, give rise to great rains — and there is a certain bird that drinks the rain while flying in the open sky, yet lets not a single drop fall upon its own body.

To take life is to build for oneself a house of grievous enmity, and to dwell in that house is only to invite still graver enmity through further killing — should there be one who has already committed the grievous enmity of taking life, you ought now to subdue him with compassion, and grant him thereby a true liberation — just as that bird drinks the rain and yet remains untouched by it."

At this the king joined his palms in reverence, his heart filled with joyful admiration for the prince's own virtue.

"May you swiftly accomplish exactly what you now seek — and once you have swiftly accomplished it, may you then return and receive me as your own disciple."

The bodhisattva silently assented in his own heart, resolving to grant the king his wish in due course; and with these words of parting spoken, he set out along the road toward Ārāḍa's own dwelling.

The king and his whole retinue, joining their palms, escorted him for a while along the way, every one of them filled with a sense of wonder, before finally turning back to return to Rājagṛha.
