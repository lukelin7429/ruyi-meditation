Nirvāṇa (I)

Vaiśālī falls silent and desolate the moment the Buddha leaves it, compared here to a girl who has just lost her father, and the Licchavi noble Siṃha voices an extended lament comparing the Buddha to a boat that has crossed but left everyone else stranded on the wrong shore. Before continuing his journey, the Buddha stops at Bhoganagara to give the monks a practical safeguard against future confusion: the "four great authorities," a method for testing any reported teaching — even one claimed to come from the Buddha's own mouth — against what the Dharma and discipline actually say, rather than accepting or rejecting it merely because of who claims to have heard it.

Vaiśālī's Grief

Chinese Source

佛所行讚涅槃品第二十五

佛至涅槃處，　鞞舍離空虛，
猶如夜雲冥，　星月失光明。

國土先安樂，　而今頓凋悴，
猶如喪慈父，　孤女常獨悲。

如端正無聞，　聰明而薄德，
心辯而口吃，　明慧而乏才。

神通無威儀，　慈悲心虛偽，
高勝而無力，　威儀而無法。

鞞舍離亦然，　素榮而今悴，
猶如秋田苗，　失水悉枯萎。

或斷火滅烟，　或對食忘飡，
悉廢公私業，　不修諸俗緣。

念佛感恩深，　默默各不言。

時師子離車，　強忍其憂悲，
垂泣發哀聲，　以表眷戀心：
「破壞諸邪徑，　顯示於正法，
已降諸外道，　遂往不復還。

世絕離世道，　無常為大病，
世尊入大寂，　無依無有救。

方便最勝尊，　潛光究竟處，
我等失強志，　如火絕其薪。

世尊捨世蔭，　群生甚可悲，
如人失神力，　舉世共哀之。

逃暑投涼池，　遭寒以憑火，
一旦悉廓然，　群生何所歸？

通達殊勝法，　為世陶鑄師，
世間失宰正，　人喪道則亡，
老病死自在，　道喪非道通。

能壞大苦機，　世間何有雙？

猛熱極焰盛，　大雲雨令消，
貪欲火熾然，　其誰能令滅？

堅固能擔者，　已捨世重任，
復何智慧力，　能為不請友。

如彼臨刑囚，　為死而醉酒，
眾生迷惑識，　惟為死受生，
利鋸以解材，　無常解世間。

癡闇為深水，　愛欲為巨浪，
煩惱為浮沫，　邪見摩竭魚，
唯有智慧船，　能度斯大海。

眾病為樹花，　衰老為纖條，
死為樹深根，　有業為其芽，
智慧剛利刀，　能斷三有樹。

無明為鑽燧，　貪欲為熾焰，
五欲境界薪，　滅之以智水，
具足殊勝法，　已壞於癡冥。

見安隱正路，　究竟諸煩惱，
慈悲化眾生，　怨親無異相，
一切智通達，　而今悉棄捨。

軟美清淨音，　方身纖長臂，
大仙而有邊，　何人得無窮？

當覺時遷速，　應勤求正法，
如嶮道遇水，　時飲速進路。

非常甚暴逆，　普壞無貴賤，
正觀存於心，　雖眠亦常覺。」

時離車師子，　常念佛智慧，
厭離於生死，　歎慕人師子。

不存世恩愛，　深崇離欲德，
折伏輕躁意，　栖心寂靜處。

勤修行惠施，　遠離於憍慢，
樂獨脩閑居，　思惟真實法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhacarita, Chapter Twenty-Five: Nirvāṇa

The moment the Buddha departed toward the place of his final nirvāṇa, Vaiśālī fell into utter emptiness, like a night sky covered in cloud, its stars and moon deprived of every light.

A land once so peaceful and content now suddenly withered away, like a motherless girl left alone, endlessly grieving the loss of her own beloved father.

It is like a person of fine appearance yet without learning; one intelligent yet meager in virtue; one clever at heart yet stammering in speech; one bright with wisdom yet lacking any real talent —

or one possessed of spiritual powers yet without dignity of bearing; one with a compassionate heart yet false and insincere; one exalted in rank yet without any real power; one dignified in manner yet without any true principle behind it.

Vaiśālī, in just this same way, its former glory now suddenly turned to ruin, was like a seedling in an autumn field, deprived of water and left entirely withered.

Some let their own cooking fires and hearths go cold and dark; some sat before their own food and forgot even to eat; every public and private duty was set aside, and no one attended to the ordinary business of life at all.

Thinking of the Buddha and the depth of the kindness he had shown them, they fell silent, each one unable to speak a word.

At that time, the Licchavi noble Siṃha, forcing himself to bear his own grief and sorrow, wept and cried out in mournful lament, giving voice to his own deep longing: "You who broke apart every crooked path and revealed the true Dharma in its place, who subdued every heterodox teacher — and now you go, never to return again.

Once the world's own path away from the world is cut off, impermanence itself becomes the greatest sickness — and now that the World-Honored One has entered great quiescence, we are left with nothing to rely upon, no one left to save us.

This most excellent one, so skilled in every means, has now concealed his own light in the ultimate place beyond all further seeking — and we, having lost our own firm resolve, are left like a fire suddenly deprived of any fuel to sustain it.

Now that the World-Honored One has cast aside the very shade he once gave the world, how deeply sentient beings are to be pitied — it is as though a man had suddenly lost his own spiritual powers, and the whole world grieved together over it.

Those fleeing the summer heat took refuge in his cool, refreshing pool; those caught in winter's cold relied on his warming fire — and now, in a single day, all of this has vanished into emptiness. Where, then, will sentient beings turn for refuge now?

He who had penetrated this most excellent Dharma was himself the world's own master craftsman, molding and shaping it — and now that the world has lost its rightful governor, humanity has lost the very path itself along with him; old age, sickness, and death now hold free rein once more, for the path is lost, and what is not the path now runs unobstructed.

He who alone could destroy the very mechanism of great suffering — where else in this whole world could his equal ever be found?

A fierce and towering blaze can be quenched by a great rain-cloud — but the fire of craving and desire, once it blazes up, who else could possibly extinguish it now?

He who was firm and steady enough to bear this heavy burden has now set down the weight of the world entirely — what power of wisdom remains now, capable of being a friend to those who never even thought to ask for one?

It is like a condemned prisoner facing execution, who drinks himself into a stupor rather than face death directly — sentient beings, their own consciousness clouded in confusion, are born only, in the end, to die; and it is impermanence, like a sharp saw, that cuts this whole world apart in just this same way.

Ignorant darkness is itself a deep and vast water, craving and desire its towering waves, the afflictions its scattered foam, and false views the great capricious fish lurking within it — only the boat of wisdom is able to carry one across this great ocean.

Every sickness is like the tree's own blossom, old age its slender branches, death its very deep root, and conditioned existence its sprouting seed — and it is the sharp, unyielding blade of wisdom alone that can cut down this tree of the three realms of existence.

Ignorance is the very fire-drill, craving and desire its blazing flame, and the realm of the five desires its fuel — and it is only by the water of wisdom that this fire can be extinguished; he, complete in this most excellent Dharma, had already destroyed this ignorant darkness entirely.

He who could see the safe and true path, who had brought every affliction to its ultimate end, who taught and transformed sentient beings with compassion, making no distinction at all between enemy and kin, whose penetration of all-encompassing wisdom was complete — and now, all of this, he has entirely set aside.

That gentle and pure voice, that broad body and slender, long arms — if even this greatest of sages has his own limit, then who could ever hope to attain what is truly without end?

One ought to awaken now to how swiftly time slips away, and apply oneself diligently to seeking the true Dharma — like a traveler on a perilous road who, upon finding water, drinks quickly and presses onward at once.

Impermanence is a thing of the utmost violence and cruelty, destroying everything everywhere without regard for rank or station — one ought to hold true contemplation always in one's heart, remaining ever watchful even while asleep."

From that time on, the Licchavi noble Siṃha kept the Buddha's own wisdom constantly in mind, grew weary of and renounced birth and death, and gave himself over to admiration and longing for this Lion among Men.

No longer holding to any worldly bond of love and affection, he honored deeply instead the virtue of freedom from desire, subduing his own restless and frivolous impulses, and settling his heart in a place of true quiescent stillness.

He devoted himself diligently to the practice of generosity, departed far from pride and arrogance, delighted in solitude and quiet withdrawal, and reflected always on the true Dharma.

The Four Great Authorities

Chinese Source

爾時一切智，　圓身師子顧，
瞻彼鞞舍離，　而說長辭偈：
「是吾之最後，　遊此鞞舍離，
往力士生地，　當入於涅槃。」

漸次第遊行，　至彼蒲加城，
安住堅固林，　教誡諸比丘：
「吾今以中夜，　當入於涅槃，
汝等當依法，　是則尊勝處。

不入脩多羅，　亦不慎律儀，
真實義相違，　則不應攝受。

非法亦非律，　又非我所說，
是則為闇說，　汝等應速捨。

執受於明說，　是則非顛倒，
是則我所說，　如法如律教。

如我法律受，　是則為可信，
言我法律非，　是則不可信。

不解微細義，　謬隨於文字，
是則為愚夫，　非法而妄說。

不別其真偽，　無見而闇受，
猶鍮金共肆，　誑惑於世間。

愚夫習淺智，　不解真實義，
受於相似法，　而作真法受。

是故當審諦，　觀察真法律，
猶如鍊金師，　燒打而取真。

不知諸經論，　是則非黠慧，
不應說所應，　應作不應見。

當作平等受，　句義如說行，
執劍無方便，　則反傷其手。

辭句不巧便，　其義難了知，
如夜行求室，　宅曠莫知處。

失義則忘法，　忘法心馳亂，
是故智慧士，　不違真實義。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the all-knowing one, turning his whole body around with the very gaze of a lion, looked back upon Vaiśālī, and spoke this verse of final farewell: "This is the last time I shall ever wander through Vaiśālī; I now go on to the birthplace of the Malla people, where I shall enter final nirvāṇa."

Traveling gradually onward, he arrived at the city of Bhoganagara, and, settling in the Ānanda Shrine grove, instructed the assembled monks: "This very night, at its middle watch, I shall enter final nirvāṇa — you must rely, then, on the Dharma itself, for this alone is the most honored and excellent refuge.

Should any teaching fail to accord with the sūtras, or fail to conform properly to the discipline, running contrary to the true and ultimate meaning of things, it ought never to be accepted.

Whatever is neither the Dharma nor the discipline, and is not, besides, something I myself have taught — this is nothing but a false and misleading claim, and you ought to reject it at once.

But to hold fast to what is clearly and correctly taught, without any distortion at all — this is what I myself have actually taught, in accordance with both the Dharma and the discipline.

Whatever accords with my own Dharma and discipline as I have taught them may rightly be trusted; whatever is said to contradict my own Dharma and discipline ought never to be trusted at all.

One who fails to grasp the subtle and precise meaning of a teaching, and mistakenly clings only to its literal words, is nothing but a foolish man, speaking falsely of what is not truly the Dharma at all.

One who cannot distinguish the true from the false, and accepts a teaching blindly, without any real insight into it, is no different from a shop that mixes brass in among its gold, deceiving and misleading the whole world.

The foolish, trained only in shallow understanding, fail to grasp the true and ultimate meaning, and mistake a mere semblance of the Dharma for the genuine Dharma itself.

And so one must examine such things with the greatest care, observing carefully whether a teaching is truly the Dharma and the discipline or not — just as a skilled goldsmith heats and hammers the metal to extract only what is genuinely pure gold.

One who does not know the scriptures and treatises cannot rightly be called wise or discerning at all; he ought not to speak of what he does not truly understand, nor should he act on what he has not truly seen for himself to be correct.

One ought instead to receive a teaching with even and impartial judgment, putting its words and meaning into practice exactly as they are taught — for one who grasps a sword without the proper skill only ends up wounding his own hand instead.

Should the words of a teaching be poorly and clumsily rendered, its true meaning becomes difficult to grasp at all — like searching for a room at night, in a vast and empty house, with no way of knowing where it might be found.

To lose the true meaning is to lose the Dharma itself, and to lose the Dharma is to leave the mind scattered and confused — and it is for this very reason that the wise never depart from the true and ultimate meaning of things."
