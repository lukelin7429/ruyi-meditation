In Praise of Nirvāṇa (II)

Aniruddha's eulogy closes with a catalogue of everything the world has lost — a king without his kingdom, a caravan without its guide, a night sky without its moon — before grief spreads to the Malla people themselves, who carry the body in procession to the cremation ground. The pyre, built and lit, refuses to catch flame three times over, and only when Mahākāśyapa arrives from Rājagṛha and pays his own final homage does the fire finally take — leaving, once the flesh has burned away, an indestructible core of relics that the poem reads as the Buddha's own vajra-like resolve made physically permanent.

Aniruddha's Eulogy Concludes

Chinese Source

天人普雲集，　充滿虛空中，
畏無窮生死，　心生大憂怖。

世間無遠近，　天眼悉照見，
業報諦明了，　如觀鏡中像。

天耳勝聰達，　無遠而不聞，
昇虛教諸天，　遊步化人境。

分身而合體，　涉水而不濡，
憶念過去生，　彌劫而不忘。

諸根遊境界，　彼彼各異念，
知他心通智，　一切皆悉知，
神通淨妙智，　平等觀一切，
悉盡一切漏，　一切事已畢，
智捨有餘界，　息智而長眠。

眾生剛強心，　見則得柔軟，
鈍根諸眾生，　見則慧明利，
無量惡業過，　見各得通塗，
一旦忽長眠，　誰復顯斯德？

世間無救護，　望斷氣息絕，
誰以清涼水，　灑之令蘇息，
所作自事畢，　大悲已長息。

世間愚癡網，　誰當為壞裂？

向生死迅流，　誰當說令反？

群生癡惑心，　誰說寂靜道？

誰示安隱處？　誰顯真實義？

眾生受大苦，　誰為慈父救？

猶多訟志忘，　馬易土失威，
王者亡失國，　世無佛亦然。

多聞無辭辯，　為醫而無慧，
人王失光相，　佛滅俗失榮。

良駟失善御，　乘舟失船師，
三軍失英將，　商人失其導，
疾病失良醫，　聖王失七寶，
眾星失明月，　愛壽而失命，
世間亦如是，　佛滅失大明。

如是阿羅漢，　所作皆已畢，
諸漏悉已盡，　知恩報恩故，
纏綿悲戀說，　歎德陳世苦。

諸未離欲者，　悲泣不自勝，
其諸漏盡者，　唯歎生滅苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Gods and humans gathered together everywhere, like clouds filling the whole of the open sky, seized with the greatest dread and anxiety, fearing this now unbounded cycle of birth and death.

No place in this world, whether near or far, once escaped the illumination of his own heavenly eye; every workings of karma and its retribution were plain and clear to him, as though he were watching a reflection in a mirror.

His heavenly ear, of surpassing keenness, missed nothing however distant; ascending to the sky, he taught the gods, and walking freely on earth, he transformed the realm of humans.

He could divide his own body into many, or merge them back into one; he could walk across water without being wetted by it at all; and he could recall his own past lives across ages beyond counting, without ever once forgetting them.

He knew the objects that each of the faculties moves through, and each being's own particular thoughts within them; he possessed the penetrating wisdom to know the very minds of others, knowing everything there was to know; his spiritual powers, born of pure and subtle wisdom, observed all things with perfect equanimity; having exhausted every defiling outflow entirely, and every task of his now complete, his wisdom set aside even the remaining trace of conditioned existence, and, resting that very wisdom, he entered his own long sleep.

Sentient beings of the most stubborn and unyielding heart grew gentle and pliant the moment they beheld him; sentient beings of the dullest faculties found their own wisdom sharpened and made keen the moment they beheld him; those burdened with immeasurable faults of evil karma each found their own way through, the moment they beheld him — and now that he has suddenly entered this long sleep, who will ever again make such virtues manifest?

The world is left with no one to protect it, its hope cut off and its very breath extinguished — who now will sprinkle it with the cool, refreshing water that revives it? His own work now complete, his great compassion, too, has come to its long and final rest.

The net of ignorance and delusion that binds this world — who now will tear it apart?

Facing the swift current that carries all toward birth and death — who now will teach a way to turn it back?

For the confused and deluded hearts of sentient beings — who now will teach the path of quiescent stillness?

Who now will show the place of true peace and security? Who now will reveal the ultimate meaning of things?

For sentient beings enduring such great suffering — who now will be their compassionate father and save them?

It is like a great litigant who has lost his own resolve to argue his case, or a fine horse that has lost its own spirited bearing, or a land that has lost its own majesty, or a king who has lost his own kingdom — the world without a buddha is exactly the same as all of these.

Like a scholar of wide learning left without eloquence, or a physician left without wisdom, or a human king who has lost the very radiance of his own royal countenance — so too, with the Buddha's passing, has the whole world lost its own glory.

Like a team of fine steeds that has lost its skilled charioteer, or a boat that has lost its own helmsman, or an army of three divisions that has lost its own valiant general, or a company of merchants that has lost its own guide, or a sick man who has lost his own good physician, or a sage-king who has lost his own seven treasures, or the multitude of stars that has lost the bright moon among them, or one who cherishes his own life and yet has lost it all the same — the world, in exactly this same way, has lost its own great light with the Buddha's passing.

In this way did the arahants, their own work fully accomplished and every defiling outflow entirely exhausted, still, knowing the kindness they owed him and wishing to repay it, pour out this tangled, sorrowful lament, praising his virtues and setting forth the world's own suffering.

Those not yet free of desire wept, unable to master their own grief; while those who had already exhausted every defiling outflow lamented only the suffering inherent in arising and ceasing itself.

The Mallas' Grief, and the Funeral Pyre

Chinese Source

時諸力士眾，　聞佛已涅槃，
亂聲慟悲泣，　如群鵠遇鷹，
悉來詣雙樹，　覩如來長眠，
無復覺悟容，　椎胸而呼天，
猶師子搏犢，　群牛亂呼聲。

中有一力士，　心已樂正法，
諦觀聖法王，　已入於大寂。

言：「眾生悉眠，　佛開發令覺，
今入於大寂，　畢竟而長眠。

為眾建法幢，　而今一旦崩，
如來智慧日，　大覺為照明。

精進為炎熱，　智慧耀千光，
滅除一切闇，　如何復長冥？

一慧照三世，　普為眾生眼，
而今忽然盲，　舉世莫知路。

生死大河流，　貪恚癡巨浪，
法橋一旦崩，　眾生長沒溺。」

彼諸力士眾，　或悲泣號咷，
或密感無聲，　或投身躃地，
或寂默禪思，　或煩冤長吟。

辦金銀寶輿，　香花具莊嚴，
安置如來身，　寶帳覆其上，
具幢幡華蓋，　種種諸伎樂，
諸力士男女，　導從修供養。

諸天散香花，　空中鼓天樂，
人天一悲歎，　聲合而同哀。

入城見士女，　長幼供養畢，
出於龍象門，　度凞連河表，
到諸過去佛，　滅度支提所。

積牛頭栴檀，　及諸名香木，
置佛身於上，　灌以眾香油，
以火燒其下，　三燒而不燃。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the Malla people, hearing that the Buddha had already entered nirvāṇa, wept and wailed in confused uproar, like a flock of swans set upon by a hawk, and came together to the twin sal trees, beholding the Tathāgata now in his long sleep, his countenance no longer showing any sign of waking; they beat their own breasts and cried out to heaven, their confused clamor like a herd of cattle bellowing at the sight of a lion seizing one of their own calves.

Among them was one Malla who had already come to delight in the true Dharma; gazing steadily at this holy king of the Dharma, he saw that he had now entered this great quiescence.

He said: "Sentient beings lie everywhere asleep, and it was the Buddha who once woke them to awareness — yet now he himself has entered this great quiescence, his own long sleep now final and complete.

He raised up, for the sake of all, the very banner of the Dharma — and now, in but a single day, it has collapsed; the Tathāgata was himself the very sun of wisdom, his great awakening the source of its light.

His diligent effort was its very heat, his wisdom radiant with a thousand rays, dispelling every darkness entirely — how, then, could such a long darkness return again now?

This single wisdom illuminated past, present, and future at once, serving as the very eye of every sentient being — and now that it has suddenly gone blind, the whole world is left with no way of knowing which path to take.

In the great river of birth and death, its towering waves formed of greed, hatred, and delusion, the very bridge of the Dharma has now, in a single day, collapsed — and sentient beings will now sink and drown in it forever."

Among the Malla people, some wept and wailed aloud; some grieved silently, without a sound; some threw themselves to the ground; some sat in silent meditation; some moaned on and on in their own bewildered anguish.

They prepared a bier of gold and silver, adorned with fragrant flowers, and placed the Tathāgata's own body upon it, covering it with a jeweled canopy above, complete with banners, streamers, and parasols, and every kind of musical instrument; and the Malla men and women, in solemn procession, made their offerings as they escorted him.

The gods scattered fragrant flowers, and heavenly music sounded from the sky; humans and gods together grieved as one, their voices joined together in shared sorrow.

Entering the city, the men and women, young and old alike, completed their own offerings; and the procession then went out through the Dragon-Elephant Gate, crossed over the Hiraṇyavatī River, and arrived at the very shrine where the buddhas of ages past had entered their own final nirvāṇa.

They piled up gośīrṣa sandalwood and every other kind of fine fragrant wood, and placed the Buddha's own body upon it, anointing it with fragrant oils; and when they set fire beneath it, the pyre would not catch flame, though they tried three times over.

Mahākāśyapa's Homage

Chinese Source

時彼大迦葉，　先住王舍城，
知佛欲涅槃，　眷屬從彼來。

淨心發妙願，　願見世尊身。

以彼誠願故，　火滅而不燃。

迦葉眷屬至，　悲歎俱瞻顏，
敬禮於雙足，　然後火乃燃。

內絕煩惱火，　外火不能燒，
雖燒外皮肉，　金剛真骨存。

香油悉燒盡，　盛骨以金瓶，
如法界不盡，　骨不盡亦然。

金剛智慧果，　難動如須彌，
大力金翅鳥，　所不能傾移，
而處於寶瓶，　應世而流遷。

奇哉世間力，　能轉寂滅法，
德稱廣流布，　周滿於十方。

隨世長寂滅，　唯有餘骨存，
大光耀天下，　群生悉蒙照。

一旦而潛暉，　遺骨於瓶中，
金剛利智慧，　壞煩惱苦山。

眾苦集其身，　金剛志能安，
受大苦眾生，　悉令得除滅。

如是金剛體，　今為火所焚。

彼諸力士眾，　勇健世無雙，
摧伏怨家苦，　能救苦歸依。

親愛遭苦難，　志強能無憂，
今見如來滅，　悉懷憂悲泣。

壯身氣強盛，　憍慢虛天步，
憂苦迫其心，　入城猶曠澤，
持舍利入城，　巷路普供養，
置於高樓閣，　天人悉奉事。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Mahākāśyapa, who had been dwelling in Rājagṛha, learned that the Buddha was about to enter nirvāṇa, and set out from there with his own followers.

With a pure heart he made a solemn vow: that he might yet be granted the chance to see the World-Honored One's own body one last time.

It was on account of this very sincere vow that the fire had gone out and would not catch flame.

Once Kāśyapa and his followers arrived, grieving as they gazed upon that sacred countenance, and paid reverent homage at the Buddha's own two feet, only then did the fire finally catch and burn.

The inner fire of the afflictions having already been extinguished within him, the outer fire could not truly burn him at all — though it burned away his outer skin and flesh, his true, vajra-like bones remained.

Once the fragrant oil had been entirely consumed, his bones were gathered into a golden urn — and just as the very realm of the Dharma itself is inexhaustible, so too were these bones themselves inexhaustible.

This vajra-like fruit of his own wisdom, as immovable as Mount Sumeru itself, could not be shifted even by the great and mighty garuḍa bird — and yet now it rests within a jeweled urn, moving on in response to the needs of the world.

How wondrous, this power found even in this world — able to turn even the Dharma of quiescent extinction into something that continues on; his renowned virtue spreads abroad widely, filling every one of the ten directions.

Having followed the way of the world into his own long quiescent extinction, only these remaining bones are left behind — yet their own great light still illuminates the whole world, and every sentient being continues to be lit by it.

In a single day his own radiance was hidden away, leaving only these bones behind in an urn — yet the sharp, vajra-like wisdom they represent still destroys the very mountain of the afflictions and their suffering.

Though every kind of suffering gathered upon his own body, his vajra-like resolve remained at perfect peace throughout — and it is this same resolve that brings about the removal of suffering for every being who endures such great suffering.

And now this very vajra-like body has been consumed by fire.

These same Malla people, valiant and mighty beyond compare in this world, capable of subduing every enemy's own suffering and offering refuge to those in need —

even they, whose own resolve remained unshaken when their own loved ones met with hardship, now, seeing the Tathāgata's own passing, were overcome entirely with grief and wept.

Their bodies once vigorous and full of spirited pride, walking once with an arrogant, heaven-treading gait — now, their hearts pressed down by grief and suffering, they entered their own city as though entering an empty wilderness, carrying the relics into the city, making offerings throughout every street and lane, and placing them at last within a high tower, where gods and humans alike came to honor and serve them.
