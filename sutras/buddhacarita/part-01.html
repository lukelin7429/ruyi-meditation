The Birth at Lumbinī

Aśvaghoṣa's epic opens not with argument but with genealogy and omen: the Śākya line traced back to the sugarcane kings, Queen Māyā's conception, and then the birth itself at Lumbinī — the bodhisattva stepping from his mother's right side already walking, already speaking, while the whole of creation answers with music, unscheduled blossoms, and a tremor that reaches even Māra's throne. The chapter closes at court, where a court brahmin reads the marks on the infant's body and tells King Śuddhodana plainly that lineage decides nothing: greatness of this kind is made, not inherited.

The Birth at Lumbinī

Chinese Source

生品第一

甘蔗之苗裔，　釋迦無勝王，
淨財德純備，　故名曰淨飯，
群生樂瞻仰，　猶如初生月。

王如天帝釋，　夫人猶舍脂，
執志安如地，　心淨若蓮花，
假譬名摩耶，　其實無倫比。

於彼象天后，　降神而處胎。

母悉離憂患，　不生幻偽心，
厭惡彼諠俗，　樂處空閑林。

藍毘尼勝園，　流泉花果茂，
寂靜順禪思，　啟王請遊彼。

王知其志願，　而生奇特想，
勅內外眷屬，　俱詣彼園林。

爾時摩耶后，　自知產時至，
偃寢安勝床，　百千婇女侍。

時四月八日，　清和氣調適，
齋戒修淨德，　菩薩右脇生，
大悲救世間，　不令母苦惱。

優留王股生，　卑偷王手生，
曼陀王頂生，　伽叉王腋生。

菩薩亦如是，　誕從右脇生，
漸漸從胎出，　光明普照耀，
如從虛空墮，　不由於生門。

修德無量劫，　自知生不死，
安諦不傾動，　明顯妙端嚴。

晃然後胎現，　猶如日初昇，
觀察極明耀，　而不害眼根。

縱視而不耀，　如觀空中月，
自身光照耀，　如日奪燈明，
菩薩真金身，　普照亦如是。

正真心不亂，　安庠行七步，
足下安平趾，　炳徹猶七星。

獸王師子步，　觀察於四方，
通達真實義，　堪能如是說。

此生為佛生，　則為後邊生，
我唯此一生，　當度於一切。

應時虛空中，　淨水雙流下，
一溫一清涼，　灌頂令身樂。

安處寶宮殿，　臥於琉璃床，
天王金華手，　奉持床四足。

諸天於空中，　執持寶蓋侍，
承威神讚歎，　勸發成佛道。

諸龍王歡喜，　渴仰殊勝法，
曾奉過去佛，　今得值菩薩。

散曼陀羅花，　專心樂供養，
如來出興世，　淨居天歡喜。

已除愛欲歡，　為法而欣悅，
眾生沒苦海，　令得解脫故。

須彌寶山王，　堅持此大地，
菩薩出興世，　功德風所飄，
普皆大震動，　如風鼓浪舟。

栴檀細末香，　眾寶蓮花藏，
風吹隨空流，　繽紛而亂墜，
天衣從空下，　觸身生妙樂。

日月如常度，　光耀倍增明，
世界諸火光，　無薪自炎熾，
淨水清涼井，　前後自然生。

中宮婇女眾，　怪歎未曾有，
競赴而飲浴，　皆起安樂想。

無量部多天，　樂法悉雲集，
於藍毘尼園，　遍滿林樹間。

奇特眾妙花，　非時而敷榮，
凶暴眾生類，　一時生慈心，
世間諸疾病，　不療自然除。

亂鳴諸禽獸，　恬默寂無聲，
萬川皆停流，　濁水悉澄清，
空中無雲翳，　天鼓自然鳴。

一切諸世間，　悉得安隱樂，
猶如荒難國，　忽得賢明主。

菩薩所以生，　為濟世眾苦，
唯彼魔天王，　震動大憂惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Chapter One: The Birth

Descended from the line of the sugarcane kings was Śuddhodana, unsurpassed among the Śākyas — a king whose wealth was pure and whose virtue was whole, and so he was named "Pure Rice." All living beings looked up to him with joy, the way they look up at the moon on the night of its first rising.

The king was like Indra, lord of the gods, and his queen was like Śacī, Indra's own consort — her resolve steady as the earth, her mind as clean as a lotus. "Māyā" was only a borrowed likeness for her name; in truth she had no equal at all.

Into that queen, radiant as an elephant among queens, the spirit descended and took up residence in the womb.

The mother was entirely free of sorrow and care; no thought of illusion or falsehood arose in her. She wearied of the clamor of the crowded world and loved instead to dwell in the quiet of the forest.

Lumbinī, that finest of gardens, had flowing springs and flowers and fruit in abundance — a stillness suited to meditation — and so she asked the king's leave to go and wander there.

The king, understanding what she wished for, felt in himself a wonder he could not quite name, and ordered the whole household, within and without, to make its way together to that garden grove.

At that time Queen Māyā, knowing of herself that the time of birth had come, lay down at ease upon a magnificent couch, attended by a hundred thousand serving women.

It was then the eighth day of the fourth month, the air clear and mild and perfectly tempered. Having kept the fast and cultivated purity of conduct, she brought forth the bodhisattva from her right side — for his great compassion meant to save the world, and so he would not let his mother suffer in bearing him.

King Urubilva was born from his mother's thigh; King Kapila, from her hand; King Mandara, from the crown of her head; King Kakṣīvat, from her armpit.

So too was it with the bodhisattva: he was born issuing from the right side. Little by little he emerged from the womb, his radiance shining out on every side, as though he had simply fallen from open space rather than come by the ordinary gate of birth.

Through immeasurable ages he had cultivated virtue, and he knew of himself that this birth was undying. Composed and unshaken, he shone forth already, wondrous and dignified in bearing.

Radiant, he appeared out of the womb like the sun at its first rising — dazzling to behold in its brilliance, and yet doing no harm at all to the eye that beheld it.

One could gaze at him directly and never be dazzled, the way one gazes at the moon in open sky. His own body cast its own light, outshining even the sun the way the sun outshines a lamp — and the bodhisattva's body, truly of gold, shone out over everything in just this way.

With a mind wholly settled and unconfused, he walked seven measured steps in perfect composure. Beneath his feet the ground lay level and smooth, marked with soles as luminous as the seven stars.

Walking with the gait of the lord of beasts, the lion, he surveyed the four directions, and — having already penetrated the truth of things — was able to speak these very words:

"This birth is a buddha's birth, and so it is my final birth. This alone — this one birth — shall I use to carry every being across."

At that very moment, in open space, two streams of pure water poured down together, one warm and one cool, bathing his crown and bringing ease to his body.

He was laid to rest in a jeweled palace hall, set upon a couch of lapis lazuli, while the heavenly kings, with hands like golden flowers, held up the couch's four legs.

The gods in the sky above held jeweled canopies in attendance, and, sustained by his majestic power, sang his praises, urging him on toward the attainment of buddhahood.

The nāga kings rejoiced, thirsting after this supreme dharma; having once served the buddhas of ages past, they now had the good fortune to encounter this bodhisattva as well.

Scattering mandārava blossoms, they gave themselves wholly to joyful offering, for the Tathāgata had arisen in the world, and the gods of the Pure Abodes rejoiced.

Having already cast off the pleasures of craving, they took their delight in the Dharma alone — for sentient beings, sunk in the sea of suffering, would now be granted liberation because of him.

Sumeru, king of jeweled mountains, holds this great earth firm and steady; yet when the bodhisattva arose in the world, the wind of his merit stirred everything, and the whole earth quaked and trembled, as wind churns up waves beneath a boat.

Fine powdered sandalwood incense and the hidden treasuries of lotus blossoms among every kind of jewel were carried streaming through the sky on the wind, falling in glorious profusion; and robes of heaven drifted down from above, bringing a wondrous bliss to whatever they touched.

The sun and moon kept their ordinary courses, yet their brightness doubled and grew more brilliant still. Every fire in the world blazed up of itself with no fuel to feed it, and clear, cool wells sprang up of their own accord, one after another.

The serving women of the inner palace marveled at what they had never seen before, and rushed together to drink and bathe in the water, every one of them filled with a sense of ease and well-being.

Countless bhūta spirits, delighting in the Dharma, gathered like clouds, filling every grove and every tree throughout the garden of Lumbinī.

Extraordinary and wondrous flowers bloomed out of their season; every manner of violent and savage creature gave rise, all at once, to a heart of loving-kindness; and every illness in the world, untreated, cured itself.

The clamoring cries of birds and beasts fell hushed into stillness; every river ceased its flowing, and every muddied water grew clear; no cloud veiled the sky, and the drums of heaven sounded of themselves.

Every realm, everywhere in the world, attained peace and ease and comfort — as when a country ravaged by calamity suddenly gains a wise and worthy ruler.

The very reason the bodhisattva was born was to deliver the world from its manifold sufferings — and it was for this reason alone that Māra, king of the heavens, was shaken with great anguish and dread.

The Portents Read at Court

Chinese Source

父王見生子，　奇特未曾有，
素性雖安重，　驚駭改常容，
二息交胸起，　一喜復一懼。

夫人見其子，　不由常道生，
女人性怯弱，　怵惕懷氷炭，
不別吉凶相，　反更生憂怖。

長宿諸母人，　互亂祈神明，
各請常所事，　願令太子安。

時彼林中有，　知相婆羅門，
威儀具多聞，　才辯高名稱，
見相心歡喜，　踊躍未曾有。

知王心驚怖，　白王以真實：
「人生於世間，　唯求殊勝子。

王今如滿月，　應生大歡喜，
今生奇特子，　必光顯宗族。

安心自欣慶，　莫生餘疑慮，
靈祥集家國，　從今轉休盛。

所生殊勝子，　必為世間救，
惟此上士身，　金色妙光明，
如是殊勝相，　必成等正覺；

若習樂世間，　必作轉輪王，
普為大地主，　勇猛正法治，
王領四天下，　統御一切王。

猶如世光明，　日光為最勝，
若處於山林，　專心求解脫。

成就實智慧，　普照於世間，
譬如須彌山，　普為諸山王。

眾寶金為最，　眾流海為最，
諸宿月為最，　諸明日為最，
如來處世間，　兩足中為最。

淨目脩且廣，　上下瞬長睫，
瞪矚紺青色，　明煥半月形，
此相云何非，　平等殊勝目？」

時王告二生：　「若如汝所說，
如此奇特相，　以何因緣故，
不應於先王，　乃現於我世？」

婆羅門白王：　「不應如是說。

多聞與智慧，　名稱及事業，
如是四事者，　不應顧先後。

物性之所生，　各從因緣起，
今當說諸譬，　王今且諦聽。

毘求央耆羅，　此二仙人族，
經歷久遠世，　各生殊異子。

毘利訶鉢低，　及與儵迦羅，
能造帝王論，　不從先族來。

薩羅薩仙人，　經論久斷絕，
而生婆羅婆，　續復明經論。

現在知見生，　不必由先胄，
毘耶娑仙人，　多造諸經論，
末後胤跋彌，　廣集偈章句。

阿低利仙人，　不解醫方論，
後生阿低離，　善能治百病。

二生駒尸仙，　不閑外道論，
後伽提那王，　悉解外道法。

甘蔗王始族，　不能制海潮，
至娑伽羅王，　生育千王子，
能制大海潮，　使不越常限。

闍那駒仙人，　無師得禪道。

凡得名稱者，　皆生於自力，
或先勝後劣，　或先劣後勝。

帝王諸神仙，　不必承本族，
是故諸世間，　不應顧先後。

大王今如是，　應生歡喜心，
以心歡喜故，　永離於疑惑。」

王聞仙人說，　歡喜增供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

The father king, seeing the son who had been born to him — a wonder such as he had never before beheld — found even his usual steadiness of temperament give way; his very countenance changed. Two breaths rose together, contending in his chest: one of joy, and, close behind it, one of dread.

The queen, seeing her son born by no ordinary way, felt all a woman's native timidity rise up in her; she trembled, her heart holding both ice and fire at once, unable to tell whether the sign portended fortune or misfortune — and so it was that dread, rather than joy, grew stronger in her still.

The elder matrons of the household, in their confusion, prayed all at once to whatever gods each one served, each invoking the deity she was accustomed to, that the young prince might be kept safe.

Now there lived, in that same forest, a brahmin learned in the reading of bodily marks — dignified in bearing, wide in learning, renowned for his eloquence — and when he saw the marks upon the child, his heart filled with a joy such as he had never known, and he could not contain his own delight.

Knowing that the king's heart was full of dread, he addressed the king truthfully: "Whoever is born into this world seeks, above all, an extraordinary son.

You, great king, are now like the moon at its fullness; you ought to feel the greatest joy. The extraordinary son born to you this day is certain to bring glory and honor to your whole line.

Set your heart at ease and rejoice; give rise to no further doubt or misgiving. Auspicious signs have gathered upon your household and your kingdom both — from this day forward, all shall turn toward prosperity and rest.

This extraordinary son who has been born to you is certain to become the world's own savior. This body alone, among the highest of beings, bears a radiance of gold, wondrous and shining — and marks as fine as these are certain, in the end, to bring him to unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Or, should he instead take his pleasure in the ways of the world, he is certain to become a wheel-turning king, universal lord of this whole great earth, ruling with courage under the true Dharma, master of all four continents, sovereign over every other king who lives.

Just as, of all the lights of the world, the light of the sun is supreme — so too, should he dwell instead in mountain forests, devoting his whole mind to the search for liberation,

he will accomplish a true and complete wisdom that shines out over the entire world — the way Mount Sumeru stands as sovereign over every other mountain there is.

Of every kind of treasure, gold is supreme; of every kind of flowing water, the ocean is supreme; of every constellation, the moon is supreme; of every source of light, the sun is supreme — and should a Tathāgata dwell in this world, he is supreme among all beings who walk on two feet.

His clear eyes are long and wide, their upper and lower lashes fine and long; his steady gaze holds the deep blue-black color of a radiant half-moon shape — how could a mark such as this be anything other than the mark of a perfectly balanced and supreme eye?"

At this the king said to the two brahmins: "If it is truly as you say — if these are marks as extraordinary as this — then for what reason, by what chain of causes, did they never once appear upon any king before me, and appear only now, in my own time?"

The brahmin answered the king: "You ought not to speak in such a way.

Learning and wisdom, renown and accomplishment — these four things do not depend on what came before them.

Whatever is born of the nature of things arises from its own causes and conditions, each in its own turn. Let me offer you some examples now; may the great king listen closely.

Bhṛgu and Aṅgiras — these two lineages of seers — across ages of great antiquity, each in turn produced sons who differed entirely from what had come before.

Bṛhaspati and Śukra were able to compose treatises on statecraft, and this gift did not descend to them from any ancestral line.

Among the seer Sarasvat's own descendants, the transmission of scripture and treatise had long since broken off — and yet from him was born Bhāradvāja, who took up and clarified scripture and treatise once again.

The understanding that appears in the present moment need not come by way of any ancestor at all: the seer Vyāsa composed a great many scriptures and treatises, and yet his final heir, Śuka, went on to gather together a vast body of verses and chapters of his own.

The seer Atri had no knowledge of medical treatises at all, and yet the Ātreya born after him proved skilled at curing every kind of illness.

The seer Kauśika, twice-born though he was, had no familiarity whatsoever with the doctrines of the outsiders — and yet the King Kātyāyana who came after him understood every one of the outsiders' teachings completely.

The founding line of the sugarcane kings could not hold back the ocean's tide — yet by the time of King Sagara, who fathered a thousand princely sons, they were able to master the great ocean's very tides and keep them from ever exceeding their appointed bounds.

The seer Jñānaka attained the way of meditation with no teacher at all.

Whoever attains renown of any kind attains it, in every case, from his own strength alone — sometimes surpassing what came before and later falling short of it, sometimes falling short at first and later surpassing it.

Kings and sages of every sort need not inherit their gifts from their own ancestral line — and this is exactly why, in every realm of the world, one ought never to weigh what came before against what comes after.

Great king, this being so, you ought to give rise to a joyful heart — and because your heart is joyful, be forever free of doubt and confusion."

Hearing what the sage had told him, the king rejoiced, and increased his offerings all the more.
