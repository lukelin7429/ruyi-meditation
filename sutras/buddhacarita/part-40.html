In Praise of Nirvāṇa (I)

Above the Buddha's body, a chain of eulogies unfolds. A heavenly being circling in a chariot of white swans speaks first, distilling the whole event into a verse on impermanence and the peace found only in cessation. A Brahmā-seer follows, marveling that even the one who penetrated ultimate truth and safeguarded the whole world could not escape the very law he taught. Then Aniruddha begins his own extended reflection — impermanence as a thunderbolt capable of shattering even Mount Meru, as a lion able to bring down even the greatest of dragons and elephants — surveying everything the Buddha's death has taken from a world that had grown to depend on him.

Two Laments from the Heavens

Chinese Source

佛所行讚歎涅槃品第二十七

時有一天子，　乘千白鵠宮，
於上虛空中，　觀佛般涅槃。

普為諸天眾，　廣說無常偈：
「一切性無常，　速生而速滅。

生則與苦俱，　唯寂滅為樂。

行業薪積聚，　智慧火熾燃。

名稱烟衝天，　時雨雨令滅，
猶如劫火起，　水災之所滅。」

復有梵仙天，　猶第一義仙，
處天勝妙樂，　而不染天報，
歎如來寂滅，　心定而口言：
「觀察三世法，　始終無不壞。

第一義通達，　世間無比士，
慧知見之士，　救護世間者，
悉為無常壞，　何人得長存？

哀哉舉世間，　群生墮邪徑。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhacarita, Chapter Twenty-Seven: In Praise of Nirvāṇa

At that time a heavenly being, riding in a palace-chariot drawn by a thousand white swans, looked down from the open sky above and beheld the Buddha's own parinirvāṇa.

He spoke then, for the sake of all the gods gathered there, this extended verse on impermanence: "Every nature is impermanent, swift to arise and swift to pass away.

Wherever there is birth, suffering accompanies it — only in quiescent extinction is there any true bliss at all.

The accumulated fuel of karmic action blazes up as the fire of wisdom.

One's own renown rises like smoke into the very sky — until the rain of time falls and extinguishes it, just as the fire that ends an age is itself, in the end, put out by the flood that follows it."

There was also a Brahmā-seer among the heavenly beings, himself a sage of ultimate truth, who, dwelling in the most excellent bliss of the heavens yet untainted by any attachment to its reward, praised the Tathāgata's own quiescent extinction, his heart settled and his voice steady, saying: "Observing the dharmas of past, present, and future alike, there is not one among them, from beginning to end, that does not eventually come apart.

He who had penetrated ultimate truth, unmatched by anyone else in this world, a man of wisdom and true insight, the very protector and savior of the world — even he has now been undone by impermanence. Who, then, could ever hope to endure forever?

How pitiable, that throughout this whole world, sentient beings should fall, again and again, onto such a crooked and mistaken path."

Aniruddha's Eulogy

Chinese Source

時阿那律陀，　於世不律陀，
已滅不律陀，　生死尼律陀，
歎如來寂滅，　群生悉盲冥，
諸行聚無常，　猶若輕雲浮。

速起而速滅，　慧者不保持，
無常金剛杵，　壞牟尼山王。

鄙哉世輕躁，　破壞不堅固，
無常暴師子，　害龍象大仙。

如來金剛幢，　猶為非常壞，
何況未離欲，　而不生怖畏？

六種子一芽，　一水之所雨，
四引之深根，　二觚五種菓，
三際同一體，　煩惱之大樹，
牟尼大象拔，　而不免無常。

猶如飾棄鳥，　樂水吞毒蛇，
忽遇天大旱，　失水而身亡。

駿馬勇於戰，　戰畢純熟還，
猶火緣薪熾，　薪盡則自滅。

如來亦如是，　事畢歸涅槃，
猶如明月光，　普為世除冥。

眾生悉蒙照，　而復隱須彌，
如來亦如是，　慧光照幽冥。

為眾生除冥，　而隱涅槃山，
名稱勝光明，　普照於世間。

滅除一切冥，　不停若迅流，
善御七駿馬，　軍眾羽從遊。

光光日天子，　猶入於崦嵫，
日月五障翳，　眾生失光明。

奉火祠天畢，　唯有燋黑烟，
如來已潛輝，　世失榮亦然。

絕恩愛希望，　普應眾生望，
眾生望已滿，　事畢絕希望。

離煩惱身縛，　而得真實道，
離群聚憒亂，　入於寂靜處。

神通騰虛遊，　苦器故棄捨，
癡冥之重闇，　智慧光照除。

煩惱之埃塵，　智水洗令淨，
不復數數還，　永之寂靜處。

滅一切生死，　一切悉宗敬，
令一切樂法，　以慧充一切。

悉安慰一切，　一切德普流，
名聞遍一切，　重照迄於今。

諸有競德者，　於彼哀愍心，
四利不為欣，　四衰不以慼。

善攝於諸情，　諸根悉明徹，
澄心平等觀，　六境不染著。

所得未曾得，　得人所不得，
以諸出要水，　虛渴令飽滿。

施人所不施，　亦不望其報，
寂靜妙相身，　悉知一切念。

好惡不傾動，　力勝一切怨，
一切病良藥，　而為無常壞。

一切眾生類，　樂法各異端，
普應其所求，　悉滿其所願。

聖慧大施主，　一往不復還，
猶若世猛火，　薪盡不復燃。

八法所不染，　降五難調群，
以三而見三，　離三而成三。

藏一以得一，　超七而長眠，
究竟寂滅道，　賢聖之所宗。

已斷煩惱障，　宗奉者已度，
飢虛渴乏者，　飲之以甘露。

被忍辱重鎧，　降伏諸恚怒，
勝法微妙義，　以悅於眾心。

修世界善者，　植以聖種子，
習正不正者，　等攝而不捨。

轉無上法輪，　普世歡喜受，
宿殖樂法因，　斯皆得解脫。

遊行於人間，　度諸未度者，
未見真實者，　悉令見真實。

諸習外道者，　授之以深法，
說生死無常，　無主無有樂。

建大名稱幢，　破壞眾魔軍，
進却無欣慼，　薄生歎寂滅。

未度者令度，　未脫者令脫，
未寂者令寂，　未覺者令覺。

牟尼寂靜道，　以攝於眾生，
眾生違聖道，　習諸不正業。

猶若大劫盡，　持法者長眠，
密雲震霹靂，　摧林雨甘澤。

少象摧棘林，　識養能利人，
雲離象老悴，　斯皆無所堪。

破見能成見，　於世度而度，
已壞諸邪論，　而得自在道。

今入於大寂，　世間無救護。

魔王大軍眾，　奮武震天地，
欲害牟尼尊，　不能令傾動，
如何忽一朝，　非常魔所壞？

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Aniruddha — Aniruddha who, in this world, was once "Unbridled" (Anuruddha), and who, having now extinguished that former unbridled nature, was truly "Beyond Restraint" (Nirodha) with respect to birth and death itself — praised the Tathāgata's own quiescent extinction, lamenting that sentient beings remain blind and in darkness, and that every aggregate of conditioned formations is impermanent, drifting like a light and insubstantial cloud.

Swift to arise and swift to pass away, nothing the wise could ever hold on to — impermanence is itself a vajra club, capable of shattering even Meru, king of mountains, itself.

How base and unworthy, this restless and fickle world, forever breaking apart, never once truly solid — impermanence is itself a fierce and violent lion, capable of destroying even the greatest dragons, elephants, and sages.

Even the Tathāgata's own vajra-like banner has now been undone by impermanence — how much more so, then, should one not yet free of desire fail to feel dread at this?

The single sprout of the six seeds, watered by a single rain; the deep root drawn by its four channels; the two-forked stem with its five kinds of fruit; the great tree of the afflictions, one and the same substance across past, present, and future — even the great elephant that is the sage, having uprooted this tree entirely, still could not escape impermanence itself.

It is like the cakravāka bird, delighting in water, that swallows down even a venomous serpent with ease, only to die the moment it meets a great and sudden drought, deprived of the very water it depended on.

Or like a fine warhorse, valiant in battle, that returns home safely once the battle is won, and yet, like a fire that blazes only as long as its fuel lasts, is extinguished of itself the moment that fuel is spent.

The Tathāgata, in just this same way, returned to nirvāṇa once his own work was accomplished — like the bright light of the full moon, which had once dispelled darkness everywhere for the sake of the whole world.

Every sentient being had once been illuminated by that very light, until it was hidden again behind Mount Sumeru — the Tathāgata, in just this same way, had once illuminated the deepest darkness with the light of his own wisdom.

Having dispelled darkness for the sake of all sentient beings, he has now hidden himself behind the very mountain of nirvāṇa — yet his renown, like a light more excellent still, continues to illuminate the whole world regardless.

Extinguishing every darkness without ever once pausing, like a swift-flowing current, skillfully driving his own seven swift steeds, with his whole army and retinue accompanying him in procession —

this radiant sun-god has now entered the very Mount of Sunset itself; the sun and moon both now obscured by the five kinds of eclipse, sentient beings have lost their own source of light.

It is like the moment after a fire-offering to the gods has been completed, leaving behind only scorched, black smoke — the Tathāgata has now likewise concealed his own radiance, and the world has, in just this same way, lost its own glory.

Having cut off every hope born of love and attachment, he had once answered fully the hopes of every sentient being — and now that their hopes have been fully satisfied and his own work is complete, every such hope has come, at last, to its end.

Freed from the very bondage of a body caught in the afflictions, he has attained the true path itself, departing from the confusion of the crowded multitude to enter a place of true quiescent stillness.

Roaming freely through the sky by his own spiritual power, he has now cast aside this very vessel of suffering; the heavy darkness of ignorance has been entirely dispelled by the light of his own wisdom.

The very dust of the afflictions has been washed clean by the water of his wisdom, and he shall never again return, having reached forever the place of true quiescent stillness.

Having extinguished birth and death entirely, he was revered by all; he brought delight in the Dharma to all, and filled all with his own wisdom.

He brought comfort to all, and his virtue flowed forth to all; his renown reached all, its light still shining even to this very day.

Toward all those who vied for excellence and virtue, he held a heart of deep compassion; he felt no elation at the four kinds of worldly gain, nor any sorrow at the four kinds of worldly loss.

Skillfully mastering his own senses, every one of his faculties remained perfectly clear and unclouded; his mind settled and impartial in its contemplation, he remained untainted by any of the six sense-objects at all.

What he attained was something never before attained, gaining what no other person could ever gain — and with the very water of true renunciation, he satisfied the parched thirst of all.

He gave what no one else could give, and expected nothing in return for it; his body, quiescent and wondrous in every mark, knew fully the thoughts and intentions of all beings.

Unmoved by either favor or disfavor, his own strength surpassing every possible enemy — he who was the very medicine for every illness has now, himself, been undone by impermanence.

Every kind of sentient being delights in the Dharma in its own different way, and he answered every one of their needs fully, satisfying every one of their wishes completely.

This great and holy donor of wisdom has now departed, never to return, like a fierce worldly fire that, once its fuel is spent, blazes up no more.

Untainted by the eight worldly conditions, he subdued the five difficult-to-tame classes of being; through the three he saw the three, and, departing from the three, accomplished the three.

Concealing the one, he attained the one; transcending the seven, he entered his own long sleep — this is the ultimate path of quiescent extinction, revered by every sage and worthy one.

Having already cut off the very obstruction of the afflictions, those who revered him have already crossed beyond suffering; those parched and famished with thirst, he gave to drink the very dew of immortality.

Clad in the heavy armor of patient forbearance, he subdued every form of anger and rage; the subtle and wondrous meaning of this most excellent Dharma brought delight to the hearts of all.

To those who cultivated goodness in this world, he planted within them the very seed of holiness; those who practiced rightly and those who did not, he embraced with perfect equanimity, never once abandoning either.

Turning the unsurpassed wheel of the Dharma, joyfully received throughout the whole world, those who had planted, in ages past, the very cause of delighting in the Dharma, all attained liberation through it.

Wandering freely among human beings, he delivered every being not yet delivered; those who had not yet seen the truth, he caused, every one of them, to see the truth at last.

To those who followed the paths of other traditions, he gave this profound teaching: that birth and death are impermanent, without any true master over them, and holding no genuine happiness at all.

Raising up the great banner of his own renown, he shattered the whole army of Māra's demons; advancing or withdrawing with neither elation nor sorrow, he set little store by mere birth, and praised instead quiescent extinction.

Those not yet delivered, he delivered; those not yet liberated, he liberated; those not yet at peace, he brought to peace; those not yet awakened, he awakened.

The sage's own path of quiescent stillness was meant to draw in every sentient being — yet sentient beings turned instead against this holy path, taking up every kind of wrong and improper conduct.

It is like the very ending of a great age of the world, when the one who upholds the Dharma falls into his own long sleep, while dense clouds crack with thunder, and trees are torn down by a sudden, drenching rain.

A young elephant may trample down a thorny thicket and, once trained, prove of great benefit to people — yet once the clouds disperse and the elephant grows old and worn, it can no longer accomplish anything at all.

Breaking apart false views and establishing true ones, he delivered the world, crossing over those who could be crossed, having already destroyed every heterodox theory, and attaining thereby the very path of true sovereignty and freedom.

Now that he has entered this great quiescence, the world is left with no one to protect it.

Māra's own great army of demons, brandishing their weapons and shaking heaven and earth, once sought to harm this great sage, and yet could never so much as shake him in the least — how, then, could he suddenly, in but a single morning, be undone instead by mere impermanence?
