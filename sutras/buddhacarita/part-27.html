Converting Anāthapiṇḍada (I)

Anāthapiṇḍada, a great merchant of Kosala famous for his charity to the poor, hears the word "Buddha" spoken in his host's house at night and cannot wait for morning to go and see for himself. The Buddha's teaching carries him to the first fruit of the path, and, recognizing that even this leaves subtler bonds intact, the Buddha begins one of the poem's most sustained pieces of philosophical argument: a point-by-point case against the idea that a sovereign creator god (Īśvara) is the true cause of the world, opened here and carried through to its conclusion in the next part.

A Merchant's Night Visit

Chinese Source

佛所行讚化給孤獨品第十八

時有大長者，　名曰給孤獨，
巨富財無量，　廣施濟貧乏。

遠從於北方，　憍薩羅國來，
止一知識舍，　主人名首羅。

聞佛興於世，　近住於竹園，
承名重其德，　即夜詣彼林。

如來已知彼，　根熟淨信生，
隨宜稱其實，　而為說法言。

「汝已樂正法，　淨信心虛渴，
能減於睡眠，　而來敬禮我。

今日當為汝，　具設初賓儀。

汝宿殖德本，　堅固淨其望，
聞佛名歡喜，　堪為正法器。

虛懷廣行惠，　周給於貧窮，
名德普流聞，　果成由宿因。

今當行法施，　至心精誠施，
時施寂靜施，　兼受持淨戒。

戒為莊嚴具，　能轉於惡趣，
令人上昇天，　報以天五樂。

諸求為大苦，　愛欲集諸過，
當脩遠離惡，　離欲寂靜樂。

知老病死苦，　世間之大患，
正觀察世間，　離生老病死。

既見於人間，　有老病死苦，
生天亦復然，　無有常存者。

無常則是苦，　苦則無有我，
無常苦非我，　何有我我所？

知苦即是苦，　集者則為集，
苦滅即寂靜，　道即安隱處。

群生流動性，　當知是苦本，
厭末塞其源，　不願有非有。

生老死盛火，　世間普熾然，
見生死動搖，　當習於無想，
三摩提究竟，　甘露寂靜處，
空無我我所，　世間悉如幻，
當觀於此身，　諸大眾行聚。」

長者聞說法，　即得於初果，
生死海消滅，　唯有一滴餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhacarita, Chapter Eighteen: Converting Anāthapiṇḍada

At that time there was a great elder named Anāthapiṇḍada, immensely wealthy beyond measure, who gave generously and widely to relieve the poor and destitute.

Traveling from a distant place in the north, from the country of Kosala, he came to lodge in the house of an acquaintance, whose master was named Śūra.

Hearing that a buddha had arisen in the world, and that he was staying nearby at the Bamboo Grove, and being moved by the renown of his very name and the weight of his virtue, he set out for that grove that very night.

The Tathāgata, already knowing that his faculties were ripe and that a pure faith had arisen in him, spoke to him in truthful terms suited to the moment, and taught him the Dharma.

"You already delight in the true Dharma, your heart parched with a pure faith and thirsting for it — you have been able to forgo your own sleep, and have come here to pay me reverence.

Today, then, I shall extend to you the full courtesy due a first guest.

You have planted, in ages past, the roots of virtue, firm and pure in what you hope for; hearing the Buddha's very name has filled you with joy, and you are a fit vessel for the true Dharma.

With an open heart you have given generously and widely, providing for the poor and destitute everywhere; your renown and virtue have spread far and wide, and this fruit has ripened through causes planted long ago.

Now you ought to practice the giving of the Dharma itself — giving with a wholly sincere and earnest heart, giving at the proper time, giving in a spirit of quiet stillness, and, together with this, upholding the pure precepts as well.

Moral discipline is itself an adornment, capable of turning one away from the evil destinies, and of raising a person up to rebirth in heaven, rewarded there with the five heavenly pleasures.

Every form of seeking is itself a great suffering, and craving and desire gather every kind of fault to themselves — one ought to cultivate instead a distancing from evil, and the peaceful bliss found in freedom from desire.

Knowing that old age, sickness, and death are themselves the great affliction of this world, one should rightly observe the world in this light, and depart from birth, old age, sickness, and death entirely.

Having seen that the human realm holds the suffering of old age, sickness, and death, one sees that it is exactly the same even in the heavens — there is nowhere at all where anything remains eternally.

What is impermanent is itself suffering, and what is suffering can have no true self within it — since the impermanent and the suffering are without self, how could there be any self, or anything belonging to a self, at all?

To know suffering is simply to know suffering as it is; to know its origin is simply to know that origin as it is; the cessation of suffering is itself quiescent stillness; and the path is itself the place of true peace and security.

The restless, ever-flowing nature of sentient beings ought to be recognized as the very root of suffering — one who grows weary of its downstream effects while leaving its very source unblocked can never truly wish to be rid of what has already come to exist.

The blazing fire of birth, old age, and death burns throughout the whole world without exception; seeing the restless turmoil of birth and death, one ought to cultivate the state beyond all conceptual perception, bringing samādhi to its very completion — the quiescent place of true immortality, empty of self and of anything belonging to a self, in which the whole world is seen to be like an illusion. One ought to contemplate this very body in just this way: nothing more than an aggregation formed by the great elements in their ceaseless activity."

Hearing this teaching, the elder attained at once the first fruit of the path; the great ocean of birth and death was dissolved for him, with only a single drop of it now remaining.

Against a Creator God

Chinese Source

空閑修離欲，　第一有無身，
不如今俗人，　見諦真解脫。

不離諸苦行，　種種異見網，
雖至第一有，　不見真實義，
邪想著天福，　有愛縛轉深。

長者聞說法，　陰蓋煥然開，
逮得於正見，　諸邪見永除，
猶如秋厲風，　飄散於重雲。

不計自在因，　亦非邪因生，
亦復非無因，　而生於世間。

若自在天生，　無長幼先後，
亦無五道輪，　生者不應滅。

亦不應災患，　為惡亦非過，
淨與不淨業，　斯由自在天。

若自在天生，　世間不應疑，
如子從父生，　孰不識其尊。

人遭窮苦時，　不應反怨天，
悉應宗自在，　不應奉餘神。

自在是作者，　不應名自在，
以其是作故，　彼則應常作。

常作則自勞，　何名為自在？

若無心而作，　如嬰兒所為，
若有心而作，　有心非自在，
苦樂由眾生，　則非自在作。

自在生苦樂，　彼應有愛憎，
已有愛憎故，　不應稱自在。

若復自在作，　眾生應默然，
任彼自在力，　何用修善為？

正復修善惡，　不應有業報，
自在若業生，　一切則共業。

若是共業者，　皆應稱自在，
自在若無因，　一切亦應無。

若因餘自在，　自在應無窮，
是故諸眾生，　悉無有作者。

當知自在義，　於此論則壞，
一切義相違，　無說則有過。

若復自性生，　其過亦如是，
諸明因論者，　未曾如是說。

無所依無因，　而能有所作，
彼彼皆由因，　猶如依種子。

是故知一切，　則非自性生，
一切諸所作，　非唯一因生，
而說一自性，　是故則非因。

若言彼自性，　周滿一切處，
若周滿一切，　亦無能所作，
既無能所作，　是則非為因。

若遍一切處，　一切有作者，
是則一切時，　常應有所作。

若言常作者，　無待時生物，
是故應當知，　非自性為因。

又說彼自性，　離一切求那，
一切所作事，　亦應離求那。

一切諸世間，　悉見有求那，
是故知自性，　亦復非為因。

若說彼自性，　異於求那者，
以常為因故，　其性不應異，
眾生求那異，　故自性非因。

自性若常者，　事亦不應壞，
以自性為因，　因果理應同，
世間見壞故，　當知別有因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Yet cultivating freedom from desire in quiet solitude, and even attaining the highest, formless mode of existence, still falls short of what an ordinary layman attains today, the moment he sees the truth directly: true liberation itself.

Those who fail to abandon their attachment to various ascetic practices, caught instead in the net of one heterodox view or another, may attain even that highest formless state and still fail to see the ultimate truth of the matter — their false thinking still clings to the pleasures of heaven, and their bondage to conditioned existence and craving only deepens further still.

The elder, hearing this teaching, felt the shroud of his own obscuration break open in radiant clarity; he attained right view, and every one of his false views was permanently removed — like a fierce autumn wind scattering apart even the heaviest banks of cloud.

The world does not arise through the causation of some sovereign lord, nor does it arise from some other mistaken cause, nor yet does it arise without any cause at all.

Were the world truly created by Īśvara, the Sovereign God, there could be no distinction at all between old and young, no proper order of before and after, and no wheel of the five destinies either — for whatever is born by such a cause ought never to perish at all.

Nor, in such a case, ought there to be any calamity or suffering, nor could doing evil be any fault at all — for both pure and impure karma alike would then be entirely the doing of Īśvara himself.

Were the world truly created by Īśvara, there ought to be no doubt about it anywhere in the world at all — just as a child, born of his own father, could hardly fail to recognize that father's own honored place.

When a person meets with poverty and hardship, he ought never, on this view, to turn around and resent heaven for it — everyone ought instead simply to revere Īśvara alone, and worship no other deity at all.

If Īśvara is himself the one who acts, then he ought not to be called "sovereign" or "free" at all — for since he is, by this account, always acting, he would have to be acting constantly and without pause.

But to act constantly without pause is itself to labor endlessly — how, then, could such a being possibly be called "sovereign" or "free"?

If he acts without any intention at all, this would be no different from the actions of a mere infant; and if he acts with intention, then having intention at all is itself incompatible with true sovereignty and freedom — and since suffering and pleasure clearly arise from the deeds of sentient beings themselves, they cannot, in that case, be the doing of Īśvara at all.

Should Īśvara himself be the one who produces both suffering and pleasure, then he himself must hold likes and dislikes of his own — and having such likes and dislikes, he could hardly still be called "sovereign" or entirely "free."

And if it is Īśvara alone who truly acts in every case, then sentient beings themselves ought simply to remain passive and silent, entrusting everything entirely to his sovereign power — for what use, then, would there be in cultivating goodness at all?

Indeed, if this were so, then whether one cultivated good or evil, no karmic retribution ought to follow from it at all; and if Īśvara himself is the source of all karmic action, then every action, in every case, would have to be his action alone, shared in common by all.

And if all karma were thus held in common in this way, then everyone alike would have to be called "Īśvara" as well; while if Īśvara himself has no cause behind his own existence, then, by the same reasoning, nothing at all ought to have any cause either.

And should Īśvara himself depend on some other, prior sovereign cause, then this chain of sovereign causes would extend without end — and so, in either case, it follows that no sentient being anywhere truly has any single, ultimate maker or doer behind it at all.

One ought to understand, then, that this very notion of a sovereign creator collapses entirely once examined in this way — for every possible meaning one could give it runs into contradiction, and to assert it without addressing these faults is itself already a serious error.

And should one instead hold that the world arises from some impersonal, inherent nature (svabhāva) as its cause, this view falls into exactly the same faults as the one before it — indeed, none of the great teachers of causal doctrine has ever actually taught such a thing.

For a thing to act at all requires some prior support and some prior cause; every particular thing depends, in every case, on its own particular cause, just as a sprout depends upon its own seed.

And so one ought to know that nothing at all arises from some single, universal, inherent nature in this way — for every action and every effect throughout the world arises from a great multiplicity of specific causes, never from one single, undifferentiated nature alone; and to claim a single universal nature as the cause of everything is, for this very reason, no true cause at all.

Should one claim that this inherent nature pervades every place without exception, then, being everywhere at once and undifferentiated, it could have no capacity to act upon anything nor be acted upon by anything at all — and having no such capacity to act or be acted upon, it could not, in that case, serve as any true cause at all.

And were it truly pervading everywhere without exception, with everything, in every place, already possessing its own capacity to act, then it would follow that action ought to be occurring absolutely everywhere, at every single moment, without exception.

Yet if one says instead that it is constantly acting in this way, without needing to wait for any specific occasion to produce a specific thing, then one ought to know from this very fact that such an inherent nature cannot, after all, be the true cause of anything.

Furthermore, it is said that this inherent nature stands apart from every one of the specific qualities (guṇa) it gives rise to — yet if this is so, then everything it produces ought likewise to stand entirely apart from those same qualities as well.

Yet throughout the whole world, we plainly observe that everything does, in fact, possess these very qualities — and so one must conclude from this that such an inherent nature, too, cannot be the true cause of anything at all.

And should one instead claim that this inherent nature is itself different from the qualities it produces, then, since it is held to be the constant and unchanging cause behind them, its own essential nature ought never to differ or vary at all — yet since the qualities found among sentient beings plainly do differ from one another, it follows that this inherent nature, too, cannot be their true cause.

And if this inherent nature were truly permanent and unchanging, then the things it produces ought never to break apart or perish either — for if a cause is truly permanent, its effect, by the very logic of cause and effect, ought to share that same permanence. Yet since we plainly observe things in this world coming apart and perishing all the time, one ought to know from this that there must be some other, different cause behind them instead.

This closes Part 27: Anāthapiṇḍada's night visit and his attainment of the first fruit of the path, and the opening movement of a sustained critique of both a sovereign creator god and an impersonal, unchanging cosmic nature as the true cause of the world. Part 28 continues the same chapter with the conclusion of this argument, the elder's full realization, his purchase of Prince Jeta's grove at an extravagant price, and the construction of what would become the Jetavana monastery.
