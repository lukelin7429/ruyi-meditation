The Forest of Ascetic Practice

Alone now, the bodhisattva walks into a community of forest ascetics, who receive him with the same wonder the deer and birds already show him. He asks each in turn what their austerities are for, and hears a whole catalogue of practices — fasting on roots and water, bird-like foraging, fire-worship, ritual bathing — all of it aimed at rebirth in heaven rather than an end to rebirth itself. His private reflection on why this falls short is the chapter's real center. When the ascetics beg him to stay, he refuses gently, and an old ascetic, recognizing that his aim exceeds what this community can offer, sends him onward to the teacher Ārāḍa Kālāma.

譬如師子王，　入于群獸中，
俗容悉已捨，　唯見道真形。

懍然心驚喜，　合掌端目矚，
男女隨執事，　即視不改儀。

諸仙不移足，　瞪視亦復然。

任重手執作，　瞻敬不釋事，
如牛在轅軛，　形來而心依。

俱學神仙者，　咸說未曾見，

能感群乳牛，　增出甜香乳。

彼諸梵志等，　驚喜傳相告，
為八婆藪天，　為二阿濕波，
為第六魔王，　為梵迦夷天，
為日月天子，　而來下此耶？

要是所應敬，　奔競來供養，
太子亦謙下，　敬辭以問訊。

種種修福業，　悉求生天樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhacarita, Chapter Seven: The Forest of Ascetic Practice

Having sent Chandaka away, the prince made his way alone into the dwelling of the seers, his dignified body shining with its own light, illuminating the whole forest of ascetic practice.

Complete now in every meaning he sought, he went forward according to that very meaning — like a lion-king entering the midst of a herd of lesser beasts — every worldly appearance now entirely set aside, showing only the true form of one upon the path.

The ascetics there, suddenly beholding a sight they had never before seen, were struck with a mixture of awe and delight; they joined their palms and fixed their eyes upon him — the men and women alike, though still occupied with whatever task was at hand, kept their gaze on him without ever breaking their composed bearing.

As the gods gaze upon Indra, staring without so much as a blink, so these seers, without a single step, stared at him in exactly the same way.

Even those whose hands were occupied with heavy labor kept their reverent gaze fixed on him without ever setting down their task — like an ox yoked to its harness, whose body remains at its work while its heart follows elsewhere.

All the students of the way of seers and sages declared with one voice that they had never before seen such a thing; peacocks and every other kind of bird cried out in confused clamor as they wheeled through the air.

Brahmins who kept the deer-vow, wandering the mountain forests in the company of deer, whose wild and skittish creatures would ordinarily startle at anything — even these deer stared fixedly at the prince, and the brahmins who followed after them stared in exactly the same fixed way.

Like a sugarcane lamp doubling its own light, or like the sun at its very first rising, his presence moved the herds of milk-cows there to yield forth milk sweeter and more fragrant than before.

The brahmins, astonished and delighted, spoke to one another in wonder: "Is this one of the eight Vasu gods? One of the twin Aśvins? Is it the sixth heaven's own Māra? One of the Brahmakāyika gods? Could it be the very son of the sun or the moon, come down to us here?"

Certain that he was one deserving reverence, they rushed forward eagerly to offer him homage; and the prince, in turn, humbled himself as well, greeting them with respectful words.

The bodhisattva surveyed the whole assembly, all the brahmins there in the forest, each cultivating some meritorious practice of his own, every one of them seeking the bliss of rebirth in heaven.

He asked the eldest and most senior of the brahmins about the true path they followed there: "Having only just arrived, I do not yet know what practice is followed here.

Let me ask you about it point by point — I beg you to explain it to me."

At this, the two twice-born elders explained to him, one by one and point by point, the full range of their austerities and the fruits each was meant to bring.

The Bodhisattva's Critique of Austerities

Chinese Source

非聚落所出，　清淨水生物，
或食根莖葉，　或復食華果。

種種各異道，　服食亦不同，
或習於鳥生，　兩足鉗取食。

或常水沐頭，　或復奉事火，
水居習魚仙，　如是等種種。

梵志修苦行，　壽終得生天，
以因苦行故，　當得安樂果。

不見真實義，　內心不欣悅，
思惟哀念彼，　心口自相告。

輪迴向生死，　苦多而果少。

違親捨勝境，　決定求天樂，
雖免於小苦，　終為大苦縛。

一切眾生類，　心常畏於死。

雖復畏於苦，　而長沒苦海。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some ate only pure, water-grown things that had not come from any village; some ate only roots, stems, and leaves; others ate only flowers and fruit.

Each followed its own particular path, and their manner of eating differed accordingly; some took up the way of birds, gathering food with the two "pincers" of their mouth alone.

Some followed the deer and ate only grass; some, like serpent-sages, subsisted only on air; some ground wood or stone between their teeth without truly eating at all, leaving only tooth-marks in it.

Some begged for alms and gave them to others, taking only the leftovers for themselves; some bathed their heads constantly in water; some devoted themselves to the service of fire; some, like fish-sages, dwelt entirely in the water — and so on, in every manner of variation.

These brahmins cultivated their austerities so that, at the end of their lives, they might be reborn in heaven; through the cause of ascetic hardship, they hoped to obtain the fruit of ease and bliss.

The Two-Footed Honored One, hearing of these many austerities, found in them no true or ultimate meaning, and his heart took no delight in them; reflecting on this, he felt only pity for them, and spoke silently to himself:

How pitiable — this great asceticism, seeking only the reward of rebirth as a human or a god, still circling on through birth and death, its suffering great and its fruit so slight.

To turn one's back on kin and abandon a life of ease, resolved only to seek the bliss of heaven — even should one thereby escape some lesser suffering, one remains bound, in the end, by a far greater one.

To waste and wither one's own body in the practice of every kind of austerity, and yet still seek further rebirth — this only strengthens the very cause of the five desires all over again.

Because they fail to see through birth and death itself, they use suffering to seek yet more suffering; and every kind of sentient being, in its heart, forever fears death.

Striving diligently to seek further birth, only to die once that birth has come — even while dreading suffering so greatly, they remain sunk forever in this sea of suffering.

This present life is already exhausting enough, and yet, in seeking a future birth, they find no rest even in that; enduring hardship now for the sake of some present pleasure, and laboring also to seek future birth in heaven — their very wish for happiness is itself base and inferior, and both paths alike fall into what is without true meaning.

Compared to what is most base and inferior, mere diligent effort might indeed count as superior — but this is nothing next to the cultivation of true wisdom, which sets both extremes aside entirely and rests in what is forever free of contrivance.

To hold that afflicting the body is itself the true Dharma, and that ease and comfort are contrary to it, is to think that only after practicing hardship does happiness follow — as though hardship were the true cause, and ease were somehow not its rightful fruit.

Yet whatever the body does, its arising and its ceasing alike, all of it comes from the power of the mind and its intention; apart from mind and intention, this body is no different from a withered log — and so one ought, rather, to bring the mind itself into proper order, for once the mind is ordered, the body naturally follows into rightness of its own accord.

If eating only pure things were itself the true cause of merit, then the birds and beasts, and the destitute and the poor, who live always on nothing but fruit and leaves, ought by this logic to possess the greatest merit of all.

And if it is said that a good heart gives rise to ascetic hardship as the true cause of merit — then why should those who dwell in ease and comfort not also give rise to that very same good heart?

Happiness does not arise from an evil heart, nor is goodness itself caused by suffering.

And as for those outsiders who hold that water itself makes one pure — the creatures who happily dwell in water all their lives would, by that logic, have their evil deeds forever washed clean.

[As for reverence for a particular place —] it was, originally, only because sages of true merit and virtue once dwelt there that such a place came to be honored at all.

It is because sages of merit and virtue once dwelt in a place that the whole world comes to hold it in reverence — one ought, then, to revere their merit and virtue itself, and not simply revere the place where they happened to dwell.

Farewell, and the Way to Ārāḍa

Chinese Source

如是廣說法，　遂至日云暮，
見有事火者，　或鑽或吹然。

或有酥油灑，　或舉聲呪願，
如是竟日夜，　觀察彼所行，
不見真實義，　則便欲捨去。

時彼諸梵志，　悉來請留住，
眷仰菩薩德，　無不勤勸請。

汝從非法處，　來至正法林，
而復欲棄捨，　是故勸請留。

諸長宿梵志，　蓬髮服草衣，
追隨菩薩後，　願請小留神。

菩薩見諸老，　隨逐身疲勞，
止住一樹下，　安慰遣令還。

梵志諸長幼，　圍繞合掌請：
「汝忽來至此，　園林妙充滿，
而今棄捨去，　遂成丘曠野。

如人愛壽命，　不欲捨其身，
我等亦如是，　唯願小留住。

此處諸梵志，　王仙及天仙，
皆依於此處，　又隣雪山側。

增長人苦行，　其處莫過此，
眾多諸學士，　由此路生天。

求福學仙者，　皆從此已北，
攝受於正法，　慧者不遊南。

若汝見我等，　懈怠不精進，
行諸不淨法，　而不樂住者。

我等悉應去，　汝可留止此，
此諸梵志等，　常求苦行伴。

汝為苦行長，　云何相棄捨？

若能止住此，　奉事如帝釋，
亦如天奉事，　毘梨訶鉢低。」

菩薩向梵志，　說己心所期：
「我修正方便，　唯欲滅諸有。

汝等心質直，　行法亦寂默，
親念於來賓，　我心實愛樂。

美說感人懷，　聞者皆沐浴，
聞汝等所說，　增我樂法情，
汝等悉歸我，　以為法良朋。

而今棄捨汝，　其心甚悵然，
先違本親屬，　今與汝等乖，
合會別離苦，　其苦等無異。

非我心不樂，　亦不見他過，
但汝等苦行，　悉求生天樂，
我求滅三有，　形背而心乖。

汝等所行法，　自習先師業，
我為滅諸集，　以求無集法，
是故於此林，　永無久停理。」

爾時諸梵志，　聞菩薩所說，
真實有義言，　辭辯理高勝，
其心大歡喜，　倍深加宗敬。

時有一梵志，　常臥塵土中，
縈髮衣樹皮，　黃眼脩高鼻。

而白菩薩言：　「志固智慧明，
決定了生過，　善知離生安。

祠祀祈天神，　及種種苦行，
悉求生天樂，　未離貪欲境。

能與貪欲爭，　志求真解脫，
此則為丈夫，　決定正覺士。

斯處不足留，　當至頻陀山，
彼有大牟尼，　名曰阿羅藍，
唯彼得究竟，　第一增勝眼。

汝當往詣彼，　得聞真實道，
能使心悅者，　必當行其法。

我觀汝志樂，　恐亦非所安，
當復捨彼遊，　更求餘多聞。

隆鼻廣長目，　丹脣素利齒，
薄膚面光澤，　朱舌長軟薄，
如是眾妙相，　悉飲爾炎水。

當度不測深，　世間無有比，
耆舊諸仙人，　不得者當得。」

菩薩領其言，　與諸仙人別，
彼諸仙人眾，　右繞各辭還。

Ru-Yi Translation

As he expounded on all this at length, the day drew on toward dusk, and he saw those who served the sacred fire, some kindling it by friction, some by blowing upon it.

Some sprinkled it with ghee, some chanted invocations and blessings aloud; and having observed their practices in this way throughout the whole day and night, and finding in them no true or ultimate meaning, he resolved to take his leave.

At this, all the brahmins came and begged him to stay, cherishing and admiring the bodhisattva's own virtue, every one of them urging him earnestly to remain.

"You have come from a place without the true Dharma to this forest of the true Dharma — why, then, would you wish to abandon it now? It is for this reason that we urge you to stay."

The eldest brahmins, their hair matted and their bodies clothed in bark, followed after the bodhisattva, begging him to linger a little longer.

The bodhisattva, seeing these elders grown weary from following after him, stopped beneath a tree, and comforted them, sending them back to their own places.

The brahmins, both old and young, gathered around him and, joining their palms, entreated him: "You have only just come to us, and this whole grove is filled with your wondrous presence — and now, if you abandon and leave us, it will become nothing more than a barren, empty waste again.

Just as a person cherishes his own life and does not wish to give up his body, so do we feel toward you — we ask only that you stay with us a little while longer.

The brahmins here — royal seers and celestial seers alike — all rely upon this very place, which lies, besides, close by the snowy mountains.

For fostering human ascetic practice, no place surpasses this one; it is by this very path that so many students of the way have gone on to be reborn in heaven.

Those who seek merit and study the way of the seers all come from this region northward; those of wisdom do not wander south of here, embracing instead the true Dharma found in this place.

If you find that we are indolent and lacking in diligence, that we practice things that are impure, and that this is why you take no pleasure in staying with us —

then let all of us leave instead, and you remain here in our place — for these brahmins here are forever in search of companions in ascetic practice.

You yourself have become the very foremost among us in ascetic practice — how, then, could you abandon us now?

Should you be willing to remain here, we would serve you as the gods themselves serve Indra, or as Indra himself once served the sage Bṛhaspati."

The bodhisattva then explained to the brahmins the true aim of his own heart: "What I cultivate is the correct means to an end — I seek only to extinguish every mode of conditioned existence entirely.

Your hearts are honest and upright, and your practice of the Dharma is quiet and still; you have shown genuine warmth toward a mere visitor such as myself, and my own heart has taken true delight in this.

Your fine words have moved my very heart, the way one is refreshed by bathing in clear water; hearing what you have said has only deepened my own love for the Dharma, and I have come to regard every one of you as my own good companions on the path.

And so, to abandon you now leaves my own heart filled with real regret; having already gone against my own kin, I now find myself parting from you as well — the suffering of parting after having once come together is, in either case, exactly the same.

It is not that my heart takes no pleasure in your company, nor that I have found any fault in you; it is only that your austerities are all aimed at the bliss of rebirth in heaven, while what I seek is the extinguishing of the three realms of existence entirely — our bodies may turn the same direction for now, but our hearts run contrary to one another.

What you practice, you have each inherited from the work of your own former teachers; but I seek to extinguish every accumulation of craving entirely, seeking instead the very Dharma that is free of all such accumulation — and it is for this reason that there is, in the end, no reason at all for me to remain long in this forest."

At this, all the brahmins, hearing what the bodhisattva had said — words both genuinely true and skillfully reasoned, their logic of the highest order — felt their hearts fill with great joy, and their reverence for him deepened all the more.

At that time there was one brahmin who always lay upon the bare dust, his hair matted and his body clothed in tree bark, his eyes a tawny yellow and his nose long and prominent.

He said to the bodhisattva: "Your resolve is firm and your wisdom is clear; you have determined for yourself the very fault of birth, and understand well the peace that lies beyond it.

Sacrificial rites offered in prayer to the gods, and every kind of ascetic austerity — all of these seek only the bliss of rebirth in heaven, and none of them has yet departed from the realm of craving and desire.

But one who is able to contend against craving and desire itself, whose resolve seeks true liberation — such a one alone is a true man, certain to become one of perfect and complete awakening.

This place is not one where you ought to remain. You should go instead to Mount Vindhya, where there dwells a great sage named Ārāḍa, who alone has attained the very ultimate — the supreme and most excellent eye of insight.

You should go and visit him, and hear the true path from him directly; and should he bring true joy to your heart, you should then take up and practice whatever he teaches.

Yet, observing your own aim and aspiration, I suspect that even his teaching may not, in the end, bring you full peace, and that you will go on to leave him as well, seeking still further learning beyond him.

Your nose stands high, your eyes are wide and long, your lips are red and your teeth white and sharp, your skin is fine and your face lustrous, your tongue is red, long, soft, and thin — every one of these wondrous marks shows that you will drink deep of that very radiant water of wisdom.

You are certain to cross over depths that cannot otherwise be fathomed, unmatched by anything in this world — and what even the seers of old, for all their age and experience, were never able to attain, you yourself are certain to attain."

The bodhisattva took these words to heart, and took his leave of all the seers there; and the whole company of seers circled him respectfully to his right before each returning to his own place.
