Ārāḍa and Udraka (I)

Ārāḍa Kālāma welcomes the prince as a student of exceptional promise and, seeing his resolve already fixed, lays out his entire system without holding anything back: a pure self (puruṣa) entangled by an eightfold delusion arising from the transformations of a primal, unmanifest nature (prakṛti) into the elements, the faculties, and the objects of sense — a scheme close kin to what later tradition would call classical Sāṃkhya philosophy, rendered here through the Buddhist poet's own eyes.

Meeting Ārāḍa

Chinese Source

佛所行讚阿羅藍欝頭藍品第十二

甘蔗月光胄，　到彼寂靜林，
敬詣於牟尼，　大仙阿羅藍。

迦藍玄族子，　遠見菩薩來，
高聲遙讚歎，　安慰言善來。

合掌交恭敬，　相問安吉不？

相勞問畢已，　庠序而就坐。

梵志見太子，　容貌審諦儀，
沐浴伏其德，　如渴飲甘露。

舉手告太子：　「久知汝出家，
斷親愛纏鎖，　猶如象脫羈，
深智覺慧明，　能免斯毒果。

古昔明勝王，　捨位付其子，
如人佩花鬘，　朽故而棄捨；
未若汝盛年，　不受聖王位。

觀汝深固志，　堪為正法器，
當乘智慧舟，　超度生死海。

凡人誘來學，　審才而後教，
我今已知汝，　堅固決定志，
但當任意學，　終無隱於子。」

太子聞其教，　歡喜而報言：
「汝以平等心，　善誨無愛憎，
但當虛心受，　所願便已獲。

夜行得炬火，　迷方者蒙導，
度海得輕舟，　我今亦如是。

今已蒙哀許，　敢問心所疑，
生老病死患，　云何而可免？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhacarita, Chapter Twelve: Ārāḍa and Udraka

The heir of the sugarcane and moonlight lineage arrived at that place of quiet stillness, and paid reverent visit to the great sage and seer Ārāḍa.

Kālāma's own noble descendant, seeing the bodhisattva approach from a distance, called out to him in a loud voice of praise, welcoming him warmly: "You are most welcome here."

They joined their palms in mutual reverence, each asking the other after his own peace and well-being.

Once these courteous greetings were finished, the prince was seated with all due order and ceremony.

The brahmin, beholding the prince's own dignified and composed bearing, felt himself washed over and won by his virtue, as a thirsty man is by a drink of ambrosia.

Raising his hand, he said to the prince: "I have long known of your going forth from home, cutting the very chains of kinship and affection, like an elephant slipping free of its tether — for your profound wisdom and clear insight are enough to let you escape this poisonous fruit entirely.

The wise and excellent kings of ages past set aside their own thrones and handed them to their sons, the way a man discards a garland of flowers once it has withered and grown old — yet you have surpassed even them, refusing the throne of a sacred king while still in the very fullness of your youth.

Observing the depth and firmness of your own resolve, I see that you are a fit vessel for the true Dharma, one certain to ride the very boat of wisdom across the sea of birth and death.

An ordinary teacher first draws a student in and only afterward examines his talent before deciding what to teach him — but I already know your resolve is firm and settled beyond question, and so you need only take up your studies exactly as you wish; I shall hold nothing back from you at all."

The prince, hearing his teaching, answered him with joy: "You have taught me so generously, with an impartial heart free of favor or aversion — I need only receive it with an open mind, and what I wish for will already be as good as attained.

It is like one traveling by night who obtains a torch, or one lost finding a guide, or one crossing the sea who finds a swift boat — I feel exactly the same way now.

Now that you have so graciously granted me your teaching, let me venture to ask what still troubles my own heart: how can the afflictions of birth, old age, sickness, and death possibly be escaped?"

Ārāḍa's System

Chinese Source

爾時阿羅藍，　聞太子所問，
自以諸經論，　略為其解說：
「汝是機悟士，　聰中之第一，
今當聽我說，　生死起滅義。

性變生老死，　此五為眾生，
性者為純淨，　轉變者五大。

我覺及與見，　隨境根名變，
色聲香味觸，　是等名境界。

手足語二道，　是五名業根，
眼耳鼻舌身，　是名為覺根。

意根兼二義，　亦業亦名覺，
性轉變為因，　知因者為我。

迦毘羅仙人，　及弟子眷屬，
於此我要義，　修學得解脫。

彼迦毘羅者，　今波闍波提，
覺知生老死，　是說名為見。

與上相違者，　說名為不見，
愚癡業愛欲，　是說為轉輪。

若住此三種，　是眾生不離，
不信我疑濫，　不別無方便。

境界深計著，　纏綿於我所，
不信顛倒轉，　異作亦異解。

我說我知覺，　我去來我住，
如是等計我，　是名我作轉。

於諸性猶豫，　是非不得實，
如是不決定，　是說名為疑。

若說法是我，　說彼即是意，
亦說覺與業，　諸數復說我。

如是不分別，　是說名總攬，
愚黠性變等，　不了名不別。

禮拜誦諸典，　殺生祀天祠，
水火等為淨，　而作解脫想，
如是種種見，　是名無方便。

愚癡所計著，　意言語覺業，
及境界計着，　是說名為著。

諸物悉我所，　是名為攝受。

如此八種惑，　彌淪於生死，
諸世間愚夫，　攝受於五節。

闇癡與大癡，　瞋恚與恐怖，
嬾惰名為闇，　生死名為癡。

愛欲名大癡，　大人生惑故，
懷恨名瞋恚，　心懼名恐怖。

此愚癡凡夫，　計著於五欲，
生死大苦本，　輪轉五道生。

轉生我見聞，　我知我所作，
緣斯計我故，　隨順生死流。

此因非性者，　果亦非有性，
謂彼正思惟，　四法向解脫。

黠慧與愚闇，　顯現不顯現，
若知此四法，　能離生老死。

生老死既盡，　逮得無盡處，
世間婆羅門，　皆悉依此義，
修行於梵行，　亦為人廣說。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At this, Ārāḍa, hearing what the prince asked, drew on his own scriptures and treatises to give him, in summary, this explanation: "You are a man of keen understanding, foremost among the intelligent — now listen closely as I explain to you the meaning of the arising and cessation of birth and death.

The transformation of a primal nature gives rise to birth, old age, and death — these five things together constitute a sentient being: the nature itself is pure and undefiled, while what undergoes transformation are the five great elements.

Awareness, and sight itself, change according to their objects and their faculties and names; form, sound, scent, taste, and touch — these are called the sense-objects.

The hands, the feet, speech, and the two lower passages — these five are called the faculties of action; the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body — these are called the faculties of awareness.

The faculty of mind holds a double meaning, being both a faculty of action and a faculty of awareness at once; the transformation of this primal nature serves as the cause, and that which knows this cause is called the self.

The seer Kapila, together with his own disciples and followers, cultivated this very essential teaching of mine, and thereby attained liberation.

That very Kapila is now known as Prajāpati; awareness of birth, old age, and death is what is called "seeing."

Whatever runs contrary to this is called "not-seeing" — ignorance, karmic action, and craving desire together are called "the turning wheel."

Should a being remain fixed within these three — a lack of faith, a doubting confusion, and an inability to discriminate correctly without any skillful means — such a being cannot escape this cycle at all.

Deep attachment to sense-objects, entangled with what belongs to the self; a lack of faith, and a perverse and inverted turning of the mind, acting one way and understanding another —

saying "I know," "I am aware," "I go," "I come," "I abide" — all such conceptions of a self as this are called "the self's own turning."

Wavering uncertainty over the various natures of things, unable to grasp with any certainty what is true and what is false — this state of irresolution is what is called "doubt."

Should one say that a given dharma is the self, or that it is mind, or that it is awareness, or action, or one of the various categories, and yet again call it the self —

this failure to discriminate properly is called "confused aggregation"; failing to understand the transformations of the foolish and the wise, and of nature itself, is called "the failure to discriminate."

Bowing in reverence, reciting scriptures, taking life in sacrifice to the gods, purifying oneself with water and fire and the like, and conceiving of liberation through such practices — every such view as this is called "having no skillful means."

The attachment the ignorant hold to mind, speech, awareness, action, and to the objects of sense — this is called simply "attachment."

Regarding every possession as belonging to one's own self — this is called "appropriation."

These eight kinds of delusion together submerge a being deep in birth and death, and the ignorant of this world are bound fast by these five afflicted conditions.

Dullness and great delusion, anger and dread — laziness is called "dullness," and the state of birth-and-death itself is called "delusion."

Craving desire is called "great delusion," for it is precisely this that gives rise to confusion even in great men; harboring resentment is called "anger," and a fearful heart is called "dread."

These foolish and ignorant ordinary beings, attached to the five desires, root themselves in the great suffering of birth and death, circling on through birth in the five destinies.

Turning through birth after birth, [holding to] my own seeing and hearing, my own knowing, my own doing — it is precisely because of clinging to such a notion of self that one is swept along, following the very current of birth and death.

This cause itself has no fixed nature of its own, and so its effect, too, has no fixed nature — and it is said that through right reflection on this, there are four dharmas that lead toward liberation.

Cleverness and ignorant darkness, the manifest and the unmanifest — should one come to know these four dharmas, one is able to escape birth, old age, and death entirely.

Once birth, old age, and death have been exhausted, one attains the inexhaustible state itself — and it is upon this very meaning that every brahmin in this world relies, cultivating the holy life accordingly, and it is this, too, that they teach widely to others."

Part 17 Complete

This closes Part 17: the prince's arrival at Ārāḍa's hermitage, his warm reception as a student of exceptional promise, and Ārāḍa's full system laid out — a pure self entangled by an eightfold delusion arising from the transformations of a primal nature, structured around the classical enumeration of faculties, sense-objects, and stages of confusion that later tradition would recognize as Sāṃkhya philosophy. Part 18 continues the same chapter: the prince's request for the actual practice this system requires, Ārāḍa's account of the path through the four meditative absorptions and the formless attainments up to "the base of nothing whatsoever," and the prince's own sharp critique of exactly where this system falls short.
