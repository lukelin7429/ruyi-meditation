The Great Final Nirvāṇa (II)

The Buddha's last teaching turns from guarding the senses to guarding the body and heart directly: eating as one takes medicine, without craving or aversion; treating sleep as a thief that afflictions exploit while the mind lies unguarded; and holding shame and conscience as a monk's true adornment, since without them a person is, in the poem's own blunt phrase, no different from an animal. From there the teaching moves to patience under the cruelest possible provocation, and a warning against pride — whether the pride of status abandoned at ordination, or the subtler pride still available to someone who owns nothing at all.

Moderation in Eating, and Wakefulness

Chinese Source

飯食知節量，　當如服藥法，
勿因於飯食，　而生貪恚心。

飲食止飢渴，　如膏朽敗車，
譬如蜂採花，　不壞其色香，
比丘行乞食，　勿傷彼信心。

若人開心施，　當推彼所堪，
不籌量牛力，　重載令其傷。

朝中晡三時，　次第修正業，
初後二夜分，　亦莫著睡眠，
中夜端心臥，　係念在明相。

勿終夜睡眠，　令身命空過，
時火常燒身，　云何長睡眠？

煩惱眾怨家，　乘虛而隨害，
心惛於睡寐，　死至孰能覺？

毒蛇藏於宅，　善呪能令出，
黑虺居其心，　明覺善呪除，
無術而長眠，　是則無慚人。

慚愧為嚴服，　慚為制象鈎，
慚愧令心定，　無慚喪善根，
慚愧世稱賢，　無慚禽獸倫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Know the proper measure in your own eating, treating it as one would take medicine — never let food become an occasion for craving or aversion to arise.

Food and drink exist only to relieve hunger and thirst, the way grease is applied merely to keep a worn-out cart running; and just as a bee gathers nectar from a flower without ever harming its color or fragrance, so too should a monk, going out to beg for alms, never do anything to damage the donor's own faith.

Should a person give with an open heart, one ought to accept only according to what one truly needs — never overloading an ox beyond its own strength, heedless of the injury such a burden would cause it.

Through the three watches of morning, midday, and evening, apply yourself in due order to right conduct; and during the first and last portions of the night as well, do not give yourself over to sleep — only in the middle of the night should you lie down, your mind composed, your thoughts fixed on the coming of dawn.

Do not sleep away the entire night, letting your body and this precious life pass by for nothing — the fire of impermanence is forever burning this very body; how, then, could you possibly justify such prolonged sleep?

The afflictions are themselves a whole host of enemies, waiting to strike the moment they find an opening — and when the mind lies dulled in sleep, who is there to notice death's own approach?

A venomous serpent hidden within one's own house can be driven out by a skillful spell; and the black viper that dwells within the heart can, in just the same way, be dispelled by clear awareness, itself a kind of skillful spell — but one who sleeps on and on with no such skillful means at all is a person entirely without shame.

Shame and conscience are themselves a fine and dignified garment; shame is itself the very goad that restrains a wild elephant; shame and conscience settle and steady the mind, while their absence destroys every root of goodness — one who has a sense of shame and conscience is honored by the world as truly wise, while one without it belongs among the very beasts of the field.

Patience, and Freedom from Pride

Chinese Source

若人以利刀，　節節解其身，
不應懷恚恨，　口不加惡言，
惡念而惡言，　自傷不害彼。

節身修苦行，　無過忍辱勝，
唯有行忍辱，　難伏堅固力，
是故勿懷恨，　惡言以加人。

瞋恚壞正法，　亦壞端正色，
喪失美名稱，　瞋火自燒心，
瞋為功德怨，　愛德勿懷恨。

在家多諸惱，　瞋恚故非怪，
出家而懷瞋，　是則與理乖，
猶如冷水中，　而有盛火燃。

憍慢心若生，　當自手摩頂，
剃髮服染衣，　手持乞食器，
邊生裁自活，　何為生憍慢？

俗人衣色族，　憍慢亦為過，
何況出家人，　志求解脫道，
而生憍慢心？　此則大不可。

曲直性相違，　不俱猶霜炎，
出家脩直道，　諂曲非所應，
諂偽幻虛詐，　唯法不欺誑。

多求則為苦，　少欲則安隱，
為安應少欲，　況求真解脫？

慳悋畏多求，　恐損其財寶，
好施者亦畏，　愧財不供足，
是故當小欲，　施彼無畏心。

由此少欲心，　則得解脫道，
若欲求解脫，　亦應習知足。

知足常歡喜，　歡喜即是法，
資生具雖陋，　知足故常安。

不知足之人，　雖得生天樂，
以不知足故，　苦火常燒心。

富而不知足，　是亦為貧苦，
雖貧而知足，　是則第一富。

其不知足者，　五欲境彌廣，
猶更求無厭，　長夜馳騁苦。

汲汲懷憂慮，　反為知足哀，
不多受眷屬，　其心常安隱。

安隱寂靜故，　人天悉奉事，
是故當捨離，　親踈二眷屬。

如曠澤孤樹，　眾鳥多集栖，
多畜眾亦然，　長夜受眾苦，
多眾多纏累，　如老象溺泥。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Even should a person cut your very body apart, joint by joint, with a sharp blade, you ought not to harbor anger or resentment, nor let a single harsh word pass your lips — for a mind full of ill will and a mouth full of harsh speech only injure yourself, and never the other person at all.

Of every kind of austerity one might undertake in disciplining the body, none surpasses the excellence of patient forbearance — for it alone possesses a firm and unyielding power that is exceedingly hard to overcome; and so you must never harbor resentment, nor direct harsh words at another.

Anger destroys the true Dharma, and destroys as well one's own fine and dignified appearance; it causes the loss of a good reputation, and its very fire burns one's own heart from within — anger is itself the enemy of every virtue, and so, cherishing virtue, you ought never to harbor resentment at all.

It is no great wonder that a layman, beset by so many worldly troubles, should feel anger arise in him — but for one who has gone forth from home to harbor anger runs directly against reason itself, like a raging fire somehow burning in the midst of cold water.

Should a thought of pride or arrogance arise in you, reach up and touch your own shaved head with your own hand — remember that you now wear dyed robes, carry a begging bowl in your hand, and sustain your own life on the margins of society, relying on others for your very food — why, then, should any thought of pride arise in you at all?

Even an ordinary layman, proud of his own fine clothes or noble lineage, is already at fault for such pride — how much more so, then, one who has gone forth from home, whose whole resolve is set on seeking the path of liberation, that he should give rise to a heart of pride and arrogance? This is something that must never be permitted at all.

Crookedness and straightness are, by their very nature, opposed to one another, no more able to coexist than frost and flame — one who has gone forth from home cultivates the straight and direct path, and flattery and deceit have no place in such a life at all; for flattery, falsehood, illusion, and deceit have no place where only the Dharma, which never deceives, is meant to reign.

To seek much is itself to suffer; to want little is itself to find peace — if peace alone is what one seeks, one ought already to have few desires; how much more so, then, one who seeks true liberation itself?

The miserly fear excessive demands, worried that their own wealth might be depleted; even the generous fear that their own means might prove insufficient to satisfy what is asked of them — and so you ought to keep your own desires few, and thereby grant others a heart free of any such anxiety.

It is through this very state of having few desires that one attains the path of liberation — and so, should you wish to seek liberation, you ought also to cultivate contentment.

One who is content is forever joyful, and this very joy is itself the Dharma; even should one's own material provisions remain humble and meager, contentment alone brings a lasting peace regardless.

One who lacks contentment, even should he attain the very pleasures of heaven, still finds his own heart forever scorched by the fire of dissatisfaction, precisely because he lacks contentment.

To be wealthy and yet without contentment is itself a kind of poverty and suffering; while to be poor and yet content is itself the truest wealth there is.

One who lacks contentment finds the realm of the five desires only growing ever wider before him, seeking still further without ever reaching satisfaction, driven on through the long night of existence by this very suffering.

Anxious and full of care, he is, in truth, an object of pity compared to the one who is content, and who, without gathering too large a following of his own, keeps his own heart forever at peace.

And it is precisely because of this peace and quiescent stillness that gods and humans alike come to serve and honor such a person — and so you ought to set aside entirely any attachment to followers, whether close or distant.

It is like a lone tree standing in a vast marshland, where every kind of bird gathers to roost — gathering too large a following works in exactly the same way, bringing suffering upon oneself through the long night of existence; for a great many followers bring with them a great many entanglements, like an old elephant sinking helplessly into the mire."
