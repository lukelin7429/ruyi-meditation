The Distribution of the Relics (II)

Droṇa's appeal succeeds: the Malla people agree to share what they had been ready to die defending, and the relics are divided into eight equal portions, one for each of the seven kings and one for the Mallas themselves, with Droṇa keeping the measuring vessel and a group of latecomers claiming the leftover ash — ten stupas in all, the first ever raised in the world. The poem then leaps forward through Buddhist history: the five hundred arahants convening the First Council to fix the scriptures in Ānanda's own voice, and King Aśoka centuries later reopening the old stupas to redistribute the relics into eighty-four thousand new ones across the land — before Aśvaghoṣa closes his epic with his own verses of praise.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 43 — final part Source: Taishō T0192, 佛所行讚 (tr. Dharmakṣema, early 5th c.)

The Division of the Relics

Chinese Source

我今私所懷，　亦告其誠實，
莫彼此相違，　理應共和合。

世尊在於世，　常以忍辱教，
不順於聖教，　云何名供養？

世人以五欲，　財利田宅諍，
若為正法者，　應隨順聖理，
為法而結怨，　此則理相違。

佛寂靜慈悲，　常欲安一切，
供養於大悲，　而興於大害？

應等分舍利，　普令得供養，
順法名稱流，　義通理則宣。

若彼非法行，　當以法和之，
是則為樂法，　令法得久住。

佛說一切施，　法施為最勝，
人斯行財施，　行法施者難。」

力士聞彼說，　內愧互相視，
報彼梵志言：　「深感汝來意，
親善順法言，　和理雅正說，
梵志之所應，　隨順自功德。

善和於彼此，　示我以要道，
如制迷塗馬，　還得於正路。

今當用和理，　從汝之所說，
誠言而不顧，　後必生悔恨。」

即開佛舍利，　等分為八分，
自供養一分，　七分付梵志。

七王得舍利，　歡喜而頂受，
持歸還自國，　起塔加供養。

梵志求力士，　得分舍利瓶，
又從彼七王，　求分第八分，
持歸起支提，　號名金瓶塔。

俱夷那竭人，　聚集餘灰炭，
而起一支提，　名曰灰炭塔。

八王起八塔，　金瓶及灰炭，
如是閻浮提，　始起於十塔。

舉國諸士女，　悉持寶花蓋，
隨塔而供養，　莊嚴若金山，
種種諸伎樂，　晝夜長讚嘆。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Let me now share with you as well what is truly in my own heart, speaking with the same sincerity — you and they ought not to remain opposed to one another; reason itself demands that you come together in reconciliation instead.

While the World-Honored One lived in this world, he taught always the way of patient forbearance — how, then, could failing to follow this very teaching of his ever be called honoring him at all?

Ordinary people of this world contend over the five desires, over wealth and profit, over fields and houses — but if you truly act for the sake of the true Dharma, you ought instead to follow its own sacred principles; to bind yourselves in enmity for the sake of the Dharma runs directly against reason itself.

The Buddha was himself quiescent and compassionate, forever wishing to bring peace to all beings — how, then, could honoring this very compassion give rise instead to such great harm?

You ought instead to divide the relics equally, so that all might be able to make offering to them — this alone accords with the Dharma, and your own renown will spread accordingly; its righteousness will be understood, and its reasoning will speak for itself.

And should there be any conduct here that runs contrary to the Dharma, it ought to be reconciled through the Dharma itself — for this alone is what it truly means to delight in the Dharma, and to let the Dharma endure long in the world.

The Buddha himself taught that, of every form of giving, the giving of the Dharma is the most excellent of all — for while people readily practice the giving of wealth, the giving of the Dharma is a far harder thing to practice."

The Malla people, hearing this, felt shame rise within them, and looked at one another; then they answered the brahmin: "We are deeply moved by the reason for your coming, your warm and sincere words in accordance with the Dharma, your reconciling and elegantly reasoned counsel — this is exactly what befits a true brahmin, acting in accordance with his own virtue.

You have skillfully reconciled both sides, and shown us the very essence of the true path — like a lost horse brought back, at last, onto the right road.

We shall now act in accordance with this reconciling counsel, following exactly what you have proposed — for to disregard such sincere and truthful words would surely bring us nothing but regret hereafter."

They then opened the Buddha's own relics, and divided them equally into eight portions — keeping one portion for their own offering, and entrusting the other seven to the brahmin for distribution.

The seven kings received their own shares of the relics, receiving them with joy and bowing their heads in reverence, and carried them back to their own kingdoms, where they raised stupas and made further offerings to them.

The brahmin, having requested and received from the Malla people the very vessel that had measured out the relics, and having further requested from the seven kings an eighth portion for himself, carried it back and raised his own shrine, called the Stupa of the Golden Vessel.

The people of Kuśinagara, meanwhile, gathered up the remaining ash and cinders, and raised a shrine of their own, called the Stupa of the Ashes and Cinders.

In this way, eight kings raised eight stupas, together with the Stupa of the Golden Vessel and the Stupa of the Ashes and Cinders — and so it was that, throughout Jambudvīpa, ten stupas were raised for the very first time.

The men and women of every kingdom, carrying jeweled canopies of flowers, made offerings at these stupas, adorning them until they shone like mountains of gold, with every kind of music sounding, and praises sung on, day and night without end.

The First Council, and King Aśoka

Chinese Source

時五百羅漢，　永失大師蔭，
恇然無所恃，　還耆闍崛山。

集彼帝釋巖，　結集諸經藏，
一切皆共推，　長老阿難陀，
如來前後說，　巨細汝悉聞，
鞞提醯牟尼，　當為大眾說。

阿難大眾中，　昇於師子座，
如佛說而說，　稱如是我聞。

合坐悉涕流，　感此我聞聲，
如法如其時，　如處如其人。

隨說而筆受，　究竟成經藏，
勤方便修學，　悉已得涅槃，
今得及當得，　涅槃亦復然。

無憂王出世，　強者能令憂，
劣者為除憂，　如無憂花樹。

王於閻浮提，　心常無所憂，
深信於正法，　故號無憂王。

孔雀之苗裔，　稟正性而生，
普濟於天下，　兼起諸塔廟，
本字強無憂，　今名法無憂。

開彼七王塔，　以取於舍利，
分布一旦起，　八萬四千塔。

唯有第八塔，　在於摩羅村，
神龍所守護，　王取不能得。

雖不得舍利，　知佛有遺骼，
神龍所供養，　增其信敬心。

雖王領國土，　逮得初聖果，
能令普天下，　供養如來塔，
去來今現在，　悉皆得解脫。

如來現在世，　涅槃及舍利，
恭敬供養者，　其福等無異。

明慧增上心，　深察如來德，
懷道興供養，　其福亦俱勝。

佛得尊勝法，　應受一切供，
已到不死處，　信者亦隨安，
是故諸天人，　悉應常供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the five hundred arahants, having lost forever the shade and shelter of their great teacher, felt lost and had nothing left to rely upon, and returned to Vulture Peak.

Gathering together at Indra's own cave there, they undertook to compile the scriptures into a fixed collection; and all of them together urged the elder Ānanda, saying: "You have heard everything the Tathāgata ever taught, from beginning to end, both great and small in detail — you, Vaidehī-putra, ought now to recite it all for the whole assembly."

Ānanda, ascending the lion's seat before the whole assembly, recited it exactly as the Buddha himself had spoken it, declaring at the start of each: "Thus have I heard."

The whole assembly wept together at this, moved by the very sound of "thus have I heard," recited exactly as the Dharma itself required, in its proper time, its proper place, and by the proper person.

Following his recitation, it was set down in writing, and thus was the collection of the scriptures at last completed; and those who applied themselves diligently, through skillful effort, to studying it have all already attained nirvāṇa, are attaining it now, or will attain it in the future — for nirvāṇa remains exactly the same regardless.

In time, King Aśoka arose in the world — one who could bring sorrow to the powerful and yet remove sorrow from the humble, like the very aśoka tree, whose name means "sorrowless."

This king, ruling over Jambudvīpa, held a heart forever free of sorrow himself, holding deep faith in the true Dharma — and it was for this reason that he was called "the Sorrowless King."

A descendant of the Maurya lineage, born already possessed of a righteous nature, he brought aid to the whole world, and, among his many deeds, raised stupas and shrines everywhere — originally named "Aśoka the Fierce," he came to be known instead as "Aśoka of the Dharma."

He opened the seven kings' own stupas to retrieve the relics within them, and, distributing them anew, raised in a single day eighty-four thousand stupas throughout the land.

Only the eighth stupa, in the village of the Mallas, guarded by a spirit-dragon, could not be opened, and the king was unable to obtain its relics.

Yet though he could not obtain these particular relics, knowing that the Buddha's own remains were there, protected and honored by a spirit-dragon, his own faith and reverence only grew the deeper for it.

Though this king ruled over his own kingdom, he had already attained the first of the noble fruits himself, and was able to cause the whole world, in every direction, to make offering at the Tathāgata's own stupas — so that beings of the past, the future, and the present alike all attained liberation through it.

Whether the Tathāgata still lived in the world, or had already entered nirvāṇa leaving only his relics behind, those who paid him reverent homage and made offering to him received an equal measure of merit in either case.

And for those of clear wisdom and a mind set on what is highest, who examine deeply the Tathāgata's own virtues, cherishing the path itself as they make their offerings, the merit gained is greater still.

The Buddha attained the most honored and excellent Dharma, and is fit to receive every kind of offering; having reached the place beyond death itself, those with faith in him find their own peace as well — and it is for this reason that every god and every human being ought forever to make offering to him.

Closing Verses

Chinese Source

第一大慈悲，　通達第一義，
度一切眾生，　孰聞而不感。

生老病死苦，　世間苦無過，
死苦苦之大，　諸天之所畏。

永離二種苦，　云何不供養？

不受後有樂，　世間樂無上，
增生苦之大，　世間苦無比；
佛得離生苦，　不受後有樂，
為世廣顯示，　如何不供養？

讚諸牟尼尊，　始終之所行，
不自顯知見，　亦不求名利，
隨順佛經說，　以濟諸世間。

Ru-Yi Translation

Foremost in great compassion, penetrating the ultimate truth entirely, delivering every sentient being — who could hear of this and remain unmoved?

Of all the suffering in this world, none surpasses the suffering of birth, old age, sickness, and death; and of all these, the suffering of death is the greatest, feared even by the gods themselves.

He who has forever departed from both these kinds of suffering — how could one possibly fail to make offering to him?

To receive no further existence is itself the highest bliss this world can know; while the suffering of further birth is itself the greatest suffering this world can know, beyond all comparison — and the Buddha attained freedom from the suffering of birth, and the very bliss of receiving no further existence, and showed this openly and fully to the whole world — how, then, could one possibly fail to make offering to him?

I have praised this most honored of sages, and all that he did, from beginning to end — not to display any knowledge or insight of my own, nor to seek any fame or gain for myself, but only in accordance with what the Buddha's own scriptures themselves teach, that I might, through this, bring benefit to all the world.

The Buddhacarita, Complete

This closes Part 43 and the entire Buddhacarita: Droṇa's successful mediation, the division of the relics into eight shares and the ten stupas raised to house them, the First Council's fixing of the scriptures in Ānanda's own voice, King Aśoka's later redistribution into eighty-four thousand new stupas across the land, and Aśvaghoṣa's own closing verses explaining why he composed this epic at all — not for his own renown, but in accordance with the Buddha's own teaching, for the benefit of the whole world. The poem's own final line names its purpose plainly: 以濟諸世間, "that I might, through this, bring benefit to all the world" — the same aim, translated once more into English, that this collection has tried to serve.
