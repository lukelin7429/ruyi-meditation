Accepting the Jetavana

Anāthapiṇḍada formally offers the completed monastery, and King Prasenajit of Kosala comes in person to pay homage, moved to reflect that even a small, undistinguished kingdom can become a place of great fortune. The Buddha's answering teaching addresses him directly as a ruler — how to govern with compassion rather than force, why every worldly possession and even the mightiest of past kings dissolve in time, and the three realms of existence as an ocean of ignorance crossed only by the light boat of wisdom. The chapter closes with the Buddha's three-month journey to the Trāyastriṃśa heaven to repay his mother's kindness by teaching her the Dharma, and his return to earth by a staircase of jewels witnessed by gods and humans together.

The Jetavana Dedication, and King Prasenajit

Chinese Source

佛所行讚受祇桓精舍品第二十

世尊已開化，　迦維羅衛人，
隨緣度已畢，　與大眾俱行，
往憍薩羅國，　詣波斯匿王。

祇桓已莊嚴，　堂舍悉周備，
流泉相灌注，　花果悉敷榮，
水陸眾奇鳥，　隨類群和鳴，
眾美世無比，　若稽羅山宮。

給孤獨長者，　眷屬尋路迎，
散花燒名香，　奉請入祇桓。

手執金龍瓶，　躬跪注長水，
以祇桓精舍，　奉施十方僧。

世尊呪願受，　鎮國令久安，
給孤獨長者，　福慶流無窮。

時波斯匿王，　聞世尊已至，
嚴駕出祇桓，　敬禮世尊足，
却坐於一面，　合掌白佛言：
「不圖卑小國，　忽成大吉祥，
惡逆多殃災，　豈能感大人？

今得覩聖顏，　沐浴飲清化。

鄙雖處凡品，　蒙聖入勝流，
如風拂香林，　氣合成薰飈，
眾鳥集須彌，　異色齊金光。

得與明人會，　蒙蔭而同榮，
野夫供仙人，　生為三足星。

世利皆有盡，　聖利永無窮，
人王多愆咎，　遇聖利常安。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhacarita, Chapter Twenty: Accepting the Jetavana

The World-Honored One, having now finished teaching and converting the people of Kapilavastu according to their own causes and conditions, set out together with his great assembly for the country of Kosala, to visit King Prasenajit.

The Jetavana had already been magnificently adorned, its halls and buildings all complete, flowing springs pouring one into another, flowers and fruit blooming in profusion, and rare birds of water and land calling out in harmonious chorus, each according to its own kind — a beauty unmatched anywhere in the world, like the very palace of Mount Kailāsa itself.

Elder Anāthapiṇḍada and his whole household came out along the road to welcome him, scattering flowers and burning fine incense as they invited him respectfully into the Jetavana.

Holding a golden dragon-shaped vessel in his own hands, he knelt and poured out a long stream of water, formally offering the Jetavana monastery to the whole community of monks throughout the ten directions.

The World-Honored One pronounced a blessing and accepted the gift, that it might secure the kingdom in lasting peace; and Elder Anāthapiṇḍada's own merit and good fortune flowed on, without end, from that day forward.

At that time King Prasenajit, hearing that the World-Honored One had already arrived, had his own carriage made ready and went out to the Jetavana, bowing reverently at the World-Honored One's own feet; withdrawing to sit to one side, he joined his palms and said to the Buddha: "I never imagined that so small and humble a kingdom as mine could suddenly become the site of so great and auspicious an event — how could a land beset by so much wickedness and misfortune possibly attract so great a being as you?

Yet today I have been granted the sight of your own sacred countenance, and, as though bathed in it, I drink in this pure transformation you bring.

Though I myself remain but an ordinary man, I have now been graced to enter, through you, into this most excellent stream — as when a wind passing through a fragrant forest carries that very fragrance abroad on its own current, or as when every bird throughout the world gathers upon Mount Sumeru, their every different color made equally golden in its light.

To be granted the chance to meet with a person of such clarity, and to share, sheltered beneath him, in his own glory — this is like a mere rustic who makes an offering to a sage, and is thereby reborn as one of the three-legged stars in heaven.

Every worldly benefit comes eventually to its end, while the benefit a sage bestows is forever inexhaustible; a human king accumulates fault upon fault, while one who encounters a sage finds lasting peace instead."

A Teaching on Righteous Kingship

Chinese Source

佛知王心至，　樂法如帝釋，
唯有二種著，　不能忘財色。

知時知心行，　而為王說法：
「惡業卑下士，　見善猶知敬。

況復自在王，　積德乘宿因，
遇佛加恭敬，　此乃非為難。

國素靜民安，　非見佛所增。

今當略說法，　大王且諦聽，
受持我所說，　見我功果成。

命終形神乖，　親戚悉別離，
唯有善惡業，　始終而影隨。

當崇法王業，　子養於萬民，
現世名稱流，　命終上昇天；
縱情不順法，　今苦後無歡。

古昔羸馬王，　順法受天福，
金步王行惡，　壽終生惡道。

我今為大王，　略說善惡法，
大要當慈心，　觀民猶一子。

不迫亦不害，　善攝持諸根，
捨邪就正路，　不自舉下人。

結友於苦行，　勿習邪見朋，
勿恃王威勢，　勿聽邪佞言，
勿惱諸苦行，　莫踰王正典，
念佛維正法，　調伏非法者。

現為人中上，　德將隆道中，
深思無常想，　身命念念遷。

栖心高勝境，　志求清涼津，
保慈自在樂，　來世增其歡，
傳名於曠劫，　必報如來恩。

如人愛甜果，　必種其良栽。

有從明入暗，　有從闇入明，
有闇闇相續，　有明明相因；
智者捨三品，　當學始終明。

言惡群嚮應，　善唱隨者難，
無有不作果，　作者不敗亡，
創業不勤習，　至竟莫能為。

素不修善因，　後致樂無斯，
既往無息期，　是故當修善。

自省不為惡，　自作自受故，
猶四石山合，　眾生無逃處。

生老病死山，　群生脫無由，
唯有行正法，　出斯苦重山。

世間悉無常，　五欲境如電，
老死錐鋒端，　何應習非法？

古昔諸勝王，　猶若自在天，
勇健志騰虛，　暫顯已磨滅。

劫火鎔須彌，　海水悉枯竭，
況身如泡沫，　而望久存世？

猛風止隨藍，　日光翳須彌，
盛火水所消，　有物悉歸滅。

此身無常器，　長夜苦守護，
廣資以財色，　放逸生憍慢。

死時忽然至，　挺直如枯木，
明人見斯變，　勤修豈睡眠？

生死獨搖機，　不止會墮落，
不習不續樂，　苦報者不為。

不近不勝友，　不學不斷智，
學不受有智，　受必令無身。

有身不染境，　染境為大過，
雖生無色天，　不免時遷變。

當學不變身，　不變則無過；
以有此身故，　為眾苦之本；
是故諸智者，　息本於無身。

一切眾生類，　斯由欲生苦，
是故於欲有，　當生厭離心，
厭離於欲有，　則不受眾苦。

雖生色無色，　變易為大患，
以不寂靜故，　況不離於欲。

如是觀三界，　無常無有主，
眾苦常熾然，　智者豈願樂？

如樹盛火然，　眾鳥豈群集？

覺者為明士，　離此則無明；
此則開覺士，　離此則非覺；
此則應所作，　離此則不應；
此則為近宗，　離此與理乖。

言此殊勝法，　非在家所應，
此則為非說，　法唯在人弘。

患熱入冷水，　一切得清涼，
冥室燈火明，　悉覩於五色。

修道亦如是，　道俗無異方，
或山居墮罪，　或在家昇仙。

癡冥為巨海，　邪見為濤波，
群生隨愛流，　漂轉莫能度。

智慧為輕舟，　堅持三昧正，
方便鼓念檝，　能濟無知海。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha, knowing that the king's own sincerity in delighting in the Dharma matched even Śakra's own, yet seeing that two attachments still remained in him — an inability to set aside wealth and sensual pleasure entirely —

knowing both the right moment and the true state of his heart, taught him the Dharma: "Even a lowly man of evil karma knows enough to show reverence when he sees goodness before him.

How much more so, then, for a sovereign king, who has accumulated virtue through causes planted in former lives — that he should show reverence upon encountering a buddha is no difficult thing at all.

Your kingdom has long been peaceful and its people at ease already, and this owes nothing to your having merely seen a buddha now.

Let me now explain the Dharma to you in brief; listen closely, great king, and take to heart what I now say, and you will see the fruit of this merit come to its own completion.

When life comes to its end, body and spirit part ways, and every one of your kinsmen will be parted from you as well — only your good and evil karma will follow after you, like a shadow, from beginning to end.

You ought to honor the true work of a righteous king, caring for the myriad people as your own children — in this present life your renown will spread abroad, and when your life ends you will be reborn in heaven; but should you instead indulge your own passions and go against the Dharma, you will suffer now, and find no joy at all hereafter.

In ages past, King Ashvamedha followed the Dharma and received the blessings of heaven for it, while King Suvarṇapāda did evil, and at the end of his life was reborn into an evil destiny.

Let me now explain to you, great king, in brief, the difference between good and evil conduct: the essential point is to hold a heart of loving-kindness, regarding your own people exactly as you would your own children.

Neither oppress nor harm them; skillfully master your own faculties; abandon what is crooked and take up the correct path; and never exalt yourself while demeaning those beneath you.

Form your friendships among those who practice diligent effort, and do not associate with companions of false and heterodox views; do not rely upon your own royal power and authority; do not heed flattering and deceitful speech; do not trouble those engaged in ascetic practice; do not overstep the proper laws of kingship; keep the Buddha and the true Dharma always in mind; and subdue those who act against the Dharma.

You now stand foremost among men, your virtue soon to flourish upon the very path itself — reflect deeply, then, on the truth of impermanence, and how body and life itself shift and change with every passing moment.

Let your heart dwell in a realm both high and excellent, your resolve set on seeking the very ford of coolness and peace; preserve this compassion and this sovereign bliss, and in the life to come your joy will only increase, your renown carried on across boundless ages — and you will surely, in this way, repay the Tathāgata's own kindness to you.

It is like a man who loves a sweet fruit: he is certain to plant its finest seedling for himself.

Some pass from brightness into darkness, some from darkness into brightness; some remain in darkness following upon darkness, and some in brightness following upon brightness — the wise set aside the other three paths, and take up only the one that leads from brightness to brightness, beginning to end.

When one speaks of evil, the crowd readily responds and follows along; when one proclaims goodness, followers are hard to find — yet there is no action at all that fails to bear its own fruit, and one who acts diligently is never, in the end, truly ruined by it; but if one fails to apply oneself diligently from the very start of an undertaking, one will never, in the end, be able to accomplish it at all.

One who has never cultivated the causes of goodness will, afterward, find no such joy awaiting him; and since what is past can never be recalled or paused, one ought, then, to cultivate goodness now, without delay.

Examine yourself, and do no evil — for whatever one does, one alone must bear its consequence; it is like the four great mountains of stone closing in together, from which no sentient being has anywhere at all to flee.

The mountains of birth, old age, sickness, and death leave sentient beings with no way of escape at all — only by practicing the true Dharma can one emerge from this heavy mountain of suffering.

Everything in this world is entirely impermanent, and the realm of the five desires is like a flash of lightning; with old age and death already resting at the very point of the blade against your neck, why would you still take up what is contrary to the Dharma?

The great and excellent kings of ages past, mighty as Īśvara himself, valiant and soaring in their own ambition, shone forth for but a moment before being ground away to nothing.

Even the fire that ends an age can melt Mount Sumeru itself, and dry up the very waters of the ocean — how much more so, then, a body as insubstantial as foam upon water, that it should ever hope to endure long in this world?

A raging wind is stilled only by an even fiercer one; the sun's own light is eclipsed by Mount Sumeru's shadow; a blazing fire is quenched by water — everything that exists returns, in the end, to its own extinction.

This body is itself an impermanent vessel, and yet one guards it painstakingly through the long night of existence, lavishing it with wealth and sensual pleasure, growing reckless and arrogant because of it.

Yet the moment of death arrives all at once, and the body lies rigid as a withered log — seeing this transformation so clearly, how could a person of true clarity possibly remain asleep, rather than apply himself diligently while there is still time?

Birth and death turn alone upon their own hidden mechanism, and one who does not bring it to a halt is certain, in the end, to fall; one who does not cultivate this practice will find no lasting joy, and one destined for a bitter reward will not escape it by inaction alone.

One who does not draw near to superior friends will never learn to cut off attachment through wisdom; and even learning it, if one does not put it into actual practice, one will not attain true wisdom — yet to truly attain it is, in the end, to bring an end to further embodied existence altogether.

To have a body and yet remain untainted by its objects of desire is possible, yet to be tainted by them is itself a grave fault; even one reborn in the formless heavens still cannot escape the ceaseless shifting and changing of time.

One ought, then, to aspire to a body beyond all change — for only what is beyond change is beyond fault; and it is precisely because one has this body at all that it becomes the very root of every kind of suffering — and so it is that the wise seek to bring this very root to rest in the state beyond embodiment altogether.

Every kind of sentient being suffers precisely because of desire — and so, with respect to desire and conditioned existence, one ought to give rise to a heart of weariness and renunciation; and once one is weary of and renounces desire and existence, one no longer receives the suffering that follows from them.

Even rebirth in the realms of form or formlessness still carries the great affliction of ceaseless change, since these states are not truly quiescent at all — how much more so, then, one who has not even departed from desire in the first place?

Contemplating the three realms of existence in this way — impermanent, and with no true master over them at all, forever ablaze with every kind of suffering — how could the wise possibly wish to take their pleasure in them?

It is like a tree engulfed in raging flame — would any flock of birds ever gather there?

To be awakened is to be a person of true clarity; apart from this, there is only ignorance. This alone is what opens a person to awakening; apart from this, there is no true awakening at all. This alone is what ought rightly to be done; apart from this, nothing else ought to be done at all. This alone brings one close to the true source; apart from this, one runs contrary to the very principle of things.

And should anyone say that this most excellent Dharma is not fitting for one who remains at home in ordinary life, this itself is a false statement — for the Dharma is spread abroad precisely through the very people who take it up, wherever they may dwell.

One suffering from fever finds coolness the moment he enters cold water, from wherever he happens to be; a lamp lit within a darkened room reveals every one of the five colors at once, regardless of the room itself.

Cultivating the path is exactly the same: household life and renunciate life are not, in themselves, different paths at all — for some, dwelling in the mountains, still fall into wrongdoing, while others, remaining at home, still rise to the rank of a sage.

Ignorance and delusion form a vast ocean, and false views are its rolling waves; sentient beings, swept along by the current of their own craving, drift and turn without ever managing to cross to the other side.

Wisdom alone is the light boat that can carry one across; holding fast to correct samādhi, and rowing forward with the oar of right mindfulness applied through skillful means, one is able to cross this very ocean of ignorance."

The Descent from Trāyastriṃśa Heaven

Chinese Source

時王專心聽，　一切智所說，
厭薄於俗榮，　知王者無歡，
如逸醉犴象，　醉醒純熟還。

時有諸外道，　見王信敬佛，
咸求於大王，　與佛決神通。

時王白世尊，　願從彼所求。

佛即默然許。　種種諸異見，
五通神仙士，　悉來詣佛所，
佛即現神力，　正基坐空中。

普放大光明，　如日耀朝陽，
外道悉降伏，　國民普歸宗。

為母說法故，　即昇忉利天，
三月處天宮，　普化諸天人。

度母報恩畢，　安居時過還，
諸天眾羽從，　乘於七寶階，
下至閻浮提，　諸佛常下處。

無量諸天人，　乘宮殿隨送，
閻浮提君民，　合掌而仰瞻。

Ru-Yi Translation

The king listened with his whole heart to this teaching of the all-knowing one, and grew weary of and indifferent to worldly glory, understanding at last that a king finds no true joy in it — like a wild, drunken elephant who, once its intoxication has fully passed, returns at last to its own true and settled nature.

At that time there were various followers of heterodox paths who, seeing the king's own faith and reverence for the Buddha, all urged the great king to arrange a contest of spiritual powers between the Buddha and themselves.

The king then relayed this request to the World-Honored One, asking that he grant what they sought.

The Buddha assented in silence. Every manner of practitioner of heterodox views, sages possessed of the five superknowledges, all came then to where the Buddha was; and the Buddha at once manifested his own spiritual power, sitting upright and firmly settled there in the open sky.

He radiated a great light everywhere, brilliant as the morning sun at its rising, and every one of the heterodox practitioners was subdued at once, and the whole people of the kingdom turned to take refuge in him.

For the sake of teaching the Dharma to his own mother, he then ascended to the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, dwelling three months in that heavenly palace, teaching and transforming the gods and their people there widely.

Having delivered his mother and repaid her kindness in full, and the time of his heavenly retreat having now passed, he descended, escorted by a great host of gods, upon a staircase of the seven treasures, down to Jambudvīpa, at the very place where every buddha is accustomed to descend.

Countless gods and heavenly beings, riding their own palace-chariots, escorted him in procession; and the rulers and people of Jambudvīpa joined their palms and gazed up at him in reverence.
