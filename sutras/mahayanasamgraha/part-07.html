The Higher Training in Morality

Part Six described the ten grounds a bodhisattva cultivates; Parts Seven through Nine turn to the three "higher trainings" — morality, concentration, and wisdom — practiced throughout them. This is the treatise's shortest part: a compact, four-part case for why a bodhisattva's moral discipline is not simply the śrāvaka precepts practiced with a bigger heart, but something structurally different — in its threefold form, in what it shares and doesn't share with the śrāvaka code, in its sheer scope, and in a few startling exceptions where an action that looks like a precept violation is, in fact, its deepest expression.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 7 of 11 complete Source: Taishō T31, no. 1594, 增上戒學分第七 Commentary guide: Vasubandhu's 攝大乘論釋 (T1597)

Translation Method

As in Parts 1 through 6, the Chinese source is Xuánzàng's translation of Asaṅga's root text (T1594), read with Vasubandhu's commentary (T1597), also translated by Xuánzàng. The three kinds of bodhisattva precepts named here match the site's Cheng Weishi Lun glossary exactly. See also this site's note on translation method.

1. Four Kinds of Superiority, Named at the Outset

Chinese Source

如是已說因果修差別，此中增上戒殊勝，云何可見？如菩薩地正受菩薩律儀中說。復次，應知略由四種殊勝故此殊勝：一、由差別殊勝，二、由共不共學處殊勝，三、由廣大殊勝，四、由甚深殊勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus spoken of the distinctions in cultivating that, how may the excellence of the higher training in morality be seen? It is as spoken of in the Bodhisattva Grounds, in the section on formally undertaking the bodhisattva's moral code. Furthermore, it should be understood that, briefly, this excellence consists of four kinds: first, excellence of distinction; second, excellence in shared and unshared training-points; third, excellence of vastness; fourth, excellence of profundity.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu pauses here to answer an obvious question: moral discipline is already one of the six perfections covered in Part 5, so why does it need a separate treatment as a "higher training"? His answer is that the three higher trainings — morality, concentration, and wisdom, covered in this and the next two parts — are being examined here specifically for how each causes the next: discipline gives rise to concentration, and concentration gives rise to wisdom, a causal sequence worth its own dedicated treatment even though the same three qualities were already named among the six perfections. The treatise also points its reader directly to Asaṅga's companion work, the Bodhisattva Grounds (Bodhisattvabhūmi), for the full ceremony and detailed rules of bodhisattva precepts — this part covers only what makes that code distinctively excellent, not its full content.

2. Excellence of Distinction: Three Kinds of Precepts

Chinese Source

差別殊勝者，謂菩薩戒有三品別：一、律儀戒，二、攝善法戒，三、饒益有情戒。此中律儀戒，應知二戒建立義故；攝善法戒，應知修集一切佛法建立義故；饒益有情戒，應知成熟一切有情建立義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Excellence of distinction: the bodhisattva's moral code has three distinct grades. First, the discipline of restraint; second, the discipline of gathering wholesome dharmas; third, the discipline of benefiting sentient beings. Here, the discipline of restraint should be understood as establishing the basis for the other two disciplines; the discipline of gathering wholesome dharmas should be understood as establishing the cultivation and accumulation of every buddha-dharma; and the discipline of benefiting sentient beings should be understood as establishing the bringing of all sentient beings to maturity.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu states the contrast plainly: śrāvakas and others have only the discipline of restraint — abstaining from wrongdoing — with no equivalent to the other two. A bodhisattva has all three at once, and Vasubandhu describes them as building on one another: the discipline of restraint is what makes the other two possible in the first place, since without basic ethical stability neither sustained spiritual practice nor genuine service to others can be reliably carried out; the discipline of gathering wholesome dharmas then channels that stability directly into cultivating every quality that leads to buddhahood; and the discipline of benefiting sentient beings channels it outward, into the actual, active work of helping others mature. Restraint alone, on this account, is only the foundation — necessary, but not yet the whole structure a bodhisattva's precepts are meant to support.

3. Excellence in Shared and Unshared Training-Points

Chinese Source

共不共學處殊勝者，謂諸菩薩一切性罪不現行故，與聲聞共；相似遮罪有現行故，與彼不共。於此學處，有聲聞犯菩薩不犯，有菩薩犯聲聞不犯。菩薩具有身語心戒，聲聞唯有身語二戒，是故菩薩心亦有犯非諸聲聞。以要言之，一切饒益有情無罪身語意業，菩薩一切皆應現行，皆應修學。如是應知說名為共不共殊勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Excellence in shared and unshared training-points: bodhisattvas, in never engaging in any inherently wrong action, share this with śrāvakas; but in sometimes engaging in what merely resembles a prohibited, though not inherently wrong, action, they do not share this with them. Within these training-points, there are cases where a śrāvaka would transgress but a bodhisattva does not, and cases where a bodhisattva would transgress but a śrāvaka does not. A bodhisattva holds discipline of body, speech, and mind together; a śrāvaka holds only the discipline of body and speech — and so a bodhisattva can transgress even in mind alone, which is not true of śrāvakas. In short: whatever action of body, speech, or mind genuinely benefits sentient beings without fault, a bodhisattva should actually carry out, and should train in, in every case. This is what should be understood as excellence in shared and unshared training-points.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu clarifies the categories with concrete cases. "Inherently wrong actions" — killing, stealing, and the like — are wrong regardless of any code; both vows equally forbid them, which is the "shared" portion. "Merely prohibited" actions are wrong only because a specific rule forbids them, not because they are inherently harmful, and here the two codes can diverge in both directions: a monastic rule against staying overnight in a certain place might be broken by a bodhisattva who judges that staying will genuinely benefit someone, while the very same bodhisattva might be considered to have transgressed by declining to help someone in need even where a śrāvaka's narrower rule would permit inaction. The sharpest difference, though, is that a bodhisattva's precepts extend to the mind itself — a śrāvaka's code governs only bodily and verbal action, so purely internal states of craving or ill will, however unwholesome, are not technically transgressions there, whereas for a bodhisattva they are. The underlying principle Vasubandhu draws out is simple: the real measure is whether an act genuinely, faultlessly benefits beings, not whether it happens to match a rule written for a narrower purpose.

4. Excellence of Vastness

Chinese Source

廣大殊勝者，復由四種廣大故：一、由種種無量學處廣大故，二、由攝受無量福德廣大故，三、由攝受一切有情利益安樂意樂廣大故，四、由建立無上正等菩提廣大故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Excellence of vastness: this, further, has four kinds of vastness. First, vastness through manifold, immeasurable training-points. Second, vastness through gathering in immeasurable merit. Third, vastness through the resolve to embrace the benefit and happiness of all sentient beings. Fourth, vastness through establishing unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Each of the four names a dimension along which the bodhisattva's precepts simply exceed the śrāvaka code's scope. The training-points themselves are more numerous and varied, since they cover restraint, positive cultivation, and active benefit to others all at once, rather than restraint alone. The merit they gather is of a different order of magnitude, since a śrāvaka's discipline is not aimed at accumulating the vast resources needed for full buddhahood. The resolve behind them reaches every single sentient being, not a bounded circle of concern. And their ultimate aim is nothing less than unsurpassed, perfect awakening itself, rather than personal liberation. Vasubandhu treats these four together as showing that "vastness" here is not merely a difference of degree, but a difference in what the discipline is actually oriented toward at every level — how many rules, how much merit, how wide a concern, and how high a goal.

5. Excellence of Profundity

The fourth and most striking kind of excellence describes cases where a bodhisattva's skillful action looks, on the surface, exactly like breaking a precept — yet is, in truth, its deepest fulfillment.

Chinese Source

甚深殊勝者，謂諸菩薩由是品類方便善巧行殺生等十種作業，而無有罪，生無量福，速證無上正等菩提。又諸菩薩現行變化身語兩業，應知亦是甚深尸羅。由此因緣，或作國王示行種種惱有情事，安立有情毘柰耶中。又現種種諸本生事，示行逼惱諸餘有情，真實攝受諸餘有情，先令他心深生淨信，後轉成熟。是名菩薩所學尸羅甚深殊勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Excellence of profundity: bodhisattvas, through this kind of skillful means, may carry out even the ten courses of unwholesome action — such as taking life — without incurring any fault, generating instead immeasurable merit and swiftly realizing unsurpassed, perfect awakening. It should further be understood that a bodhisattva's manifesting transformation bodies to carry out bodily and verbal actions is likewise a profound form of moral discipline. By this cause and condition, a bodhisattva may become a king and, appearing to trouble sentient beings in various ways, thereby establish them in proper discipline. Or a bodhisattva may manifest various former-life episodes, appearing to distress other beings, while in truth genuinely embracing them — first causing deep, pure faith to arise in their minds, and afterward bringing them to full maturity. This is named the profound excellence of the moral discipline a bodhisattva trains in.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu is careful to specify exactly what licenses this: a bodhisattva able to read another being's mind directly, who sees with certainty that a particular person is about to commit an act — say, killing a great many others — that will condemn them to a terrible rebirth, and who has no other way to stop it, may choose to end that person's life first, taking on the karmic consequence personally, purely out of compassion for both the would-be victims and the perpetrator, who is thereby spared a far worse fate. Such a bodhisattva, Vasubandhu says, is like a skilled physician who must sometimes cause pain to heal — the act incurs no fault at all, because it arises entirely from a benefiting mind free of anger or self-interest, and it generates immense merit precisely because of the compassion behind it. He also glosses "manifesting transformation bodies" with two illustrative examples: a bodhisattva appearing as a stern king to discipline unruly subjects into good conduct, and the well-known jātaka tales in which a bodhisattva appears to give away his own wife and children, causing apparent distress, while no real being is actually harmed by an illusory, transformation-body event, and the deeper purpose is always to plant and ripen genuine faith. This "excellence of profundity" is explicitly bounded, not a general license: it applies only to a bodhisattva with the wisdom to know such things with certainty and the compassion for it to be entirely selfless — not a permission ordinary practitioners can invoke for themselves.

Part Seven Complete

This closes Part Seven of Ru-Yi's translation — the treatise's shortest part, and its case for why a bodhisattva's moral discipline is not the śrāvaka precepts merely broadened, but a structurally distinct code: threefold rather than singular, extending even to the mind itself, vast in scope and aim, and, in the rarest cases, profound enough to make compassionate action look, on the surface, like its own violation.
