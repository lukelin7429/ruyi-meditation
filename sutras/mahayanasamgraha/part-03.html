The Character of the Knowable: The Three Natures

This page carries Ru-Yi's translation of Part Three — the treatise's second-longest part, and its account of the three natures (三自性): the dependent nature that actually arises from the ālayavijñāna, the discriminated nature we mistakenly project onto it, and the perfectly accomplished nature that is simply the dependent nature's permanent freedom from that projection. Along the way, Asaṅga proves that experience is "consciousness-only," resolves several puzzling scriptural sayings, and closes by laying out his own method for reading and composing Mahāyāna commentary — including a full catalogue of a buddha's twenty-one qualities and a bodhisattva's thirty-two defining practices.

1. The Three Natures Introduced

Chinese Source

已說所知依，所知相復云何應觀？此略有三種：一、依他起相，二、遍計所執相，三、圓成實相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having spoken of the basis of the knowable, how should the character of the knowable now be observed? Briefly stated, it is of three kinds: first, the dependent character; second, the discriminated, attached-to character; third, the perfectly accomplished character.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

This one sentence names the doctrine that gives Yogācāra philosophy its other common name, "the three-natures teaching." Everything in Part 3 is an elaboration of these three terms — how each is defined, why each is named as it is, how they relate to one another, and how the scriptures speak of them under other guises. On this site, the same three terms appear as "the dependent nature," "the discriminated, attached-to nature," and "the perfectly accomplished nature" throughout the Cheng Weishi Lun pages; the vocabulary is kept identical here.

2. The Dependent Nature: Ten Kinds of Consciousness

The treatise defines the dependent nature first, and does so by listing ten specific kinds of consciousness — each an aspect of ordinary experience — that together make it up.

Chinese Source

此中何者依他起相？謂阿賴耶識為種子，虛妄分別所攝諸識。此復云何？謂身，身者，受者識，彼所受識，彼能受識，世識，數識，處識，言說識，自他差別識，善趣惡趣死生識。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, what is the dependent character? It is the consciousnesses, having the ālayavijñāna as their seed, that are included within false discrimination. What, further, are these? They are: the body-consciousness, the body-owner-consciousness, the experiencer-consciousness, the experienced-consciousness, the experiencing-consciousness, the world-consciousness, the number-consciousness, the place-consciousness, the language-consciousness, the self-and-other-distinguishing consciousness, and the consciousness of the fortunate and unfortunate destinies, of death and rebirth.

此中若身，身者，受者識，彼所受識，彼能受識，世識，數識，處識，言說識，此由名言熏習種子。若自他差別識，此由我見熏習種子。若善趣惡趣死生識，此由有支熏習種子。由此諸識，一切界趣雜染所攝依他起相虛妄分別皆得顯現。如此諸識，皆是虛妄分別所攝，唯識為性，是無所有非真實義顯現所依；如是名為依他起相。

Here, the body-consciousness, the body-owner-consciousness, the experiencer-consciousness, the experienced-consciousness, the experiencing-consciousness, the world-consciousness, the number-consciousness, the place-consciousness, and the language-consciousness — these arise from the seed of name-habituation. The self-and-other-distinguishing consciousness arises from the seed of self-view-habituation. The consciousness of the fortunate and unfortunate destinies, of death and rebirth, arises from the seed of existence-link-habituation. It is through these consciousnesses that the dependent character — false discrimination, included within the defilement of every realm and destiny — is able to appear at all. As these consciousnesses are all included within false discrimination, having consciousness alone as their nature, they are without any real substance, and are the very basis on which the appearance of what is not truly real depends; this is what is named the dependent character.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

This list of ten looks strange at first, but Vasubandhu reads it as simply naming, one by one, the different roles a single stream of experience plays: the "body-consciousness" is the five physical sense-faculties themselves, treated as objects of consciousness; the "body-owner-consciousness" is the defiled manas that claims them as "mine"; the "experiencer-consciousness" is the sixth, mental consciousness that actually receives experience; the "experienced-consciousness" and "experiencing-consciousness" are the six kinds of sense-objects and the six sense-consciousnesses that engage them; "world-consciousness" is the sense of unbroken continuity running through birth after birth; "number-consciousness" is the mind's basic capacity to count and calculate; "place-consciousness" is the perceived external world itself; and "language-consciousness" is the fourfold structure of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing that underlies ordinary speech. All nine of these trace back to the seed of "name-habituation" — the deep habit of experiencing the world through fixed linguistic categories. The tenth item, "self-and-other-distinguishing consciousness," traces instead to the self-view-habituation planted by the defiled manas, and the final item, the consciousness of specific destinies and of death and rebirth, traces to the karmic seed of existence-link-habituation. Ten different faces, in other words, of the very same seed-bearing stream discussed at length in Part 2 — which is exactly why the dependent nature is defined here as having the ālayavijñāna itself as its seed.

3. The Other Two Natures Defined; Identifying the Ten Consciousnesses

Chinese Source

此中何者遍計所執相？謂於無義唯有識中似義顯現。此中何者圓成實相？謂即於彼依他起相，由似義相永無有性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, what is the discriminated, attached-to character? It is the appearance of what seems to be an object within what is, in truth, nothing but consciousness, devoid of any real object. Here, what is the perfectly accomplished character? It is simply this: within that very dependent character, the seeming-object aspect is permanently absent, without any nature of its own at all.

此中身，身者，受者識，應知即是眼等六內界。彼所受識，應知即是色等六外界。彼能受識，應知即是眼等六識界。其餘諸識，應知是此諸識差別。

Here, the body-consciousness, the body-owner-consciousness, and the experiencer-consciousness should be understood as precisely the six inner elements — the eye and the rest. The experienced-consciousness should be understood as precisely the six outer elements — form and the rest. The experiencing-consciousness should be understood as precisely the six consciousness-elements — the eye-consciousness and the rest. The remaining consciousnesses should be understood as further distinctions among these.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

The relationship between the three natures could hardly be stated more economically than it is here. The dependent nature is simply what is actually happening — a real, conditioned flow of consciousness, arising from seeds, exactly as Part 2 described. The discriminated nature is not some further thing added on top of that flow; it is the illusory sense of a fixed "object out there," projected onto the dependent flow by discrimination, where in truth there is only consciousness appearing as if it had an object. And the perfectly accomplished nature is not a third, separate thing either — it is simply the dependent nature's own permanent freedom from that projected illusion, the way a rope's real, permanent freedom from being a snake is not some additional feature of the rope, over and above the rope itself once the illusion of "snake" is seen through. Vasubandhu's identification of the ten consciousnesses with the eighteen elements (dhātus) of classical Abhidharma is his way of showing that this teaching is not inventing new categories, but reorganizing familiar Buddhist ones around a single insight.

4. The Dream Simile, and Scriptural Proof of Consciousness-Only

Having defined the three natures, the treatise now proves that experience really is consciousness-only — starting with the most memorable of its similes.

Chinese Source

又此諸識皆唯有識，都無義故。此中以何為喻顯示？應知夢等為喻顯示。謂如夢中都無其義獨唯有識，雖種種色聲香味觸，舍林地山似義影現，而於此中都無有義。由此喻顯，應隨了知一切時處皆唯有識。由此等言，應知復有幻誑，鹿愛，翳眩等喻。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, all these consciousnesses are nothing but consciousness itself, entirely without any real object. By what analogy may this be illustrated? It should be illustrated by the analogy of a dream, among others. Just as, in a dream, there is no real object at all, yet consciousness alone exists — although the various forms of color, sound, smell, taste, and touch, houses, forests, land, and mountains, appear as if they were real objects, there is, in fact, no real object present in any of this. By this analogy it should be understood that, at every time and in every place, there is nothing but consciousness alone. In this same vein, it should be understood that there are also the analogies of illusion, of delusion, of the mirage a thirsty deer takes for water, and of the floating spots seen by one whose eyes are clouded.

若於覺時一切時處皆如夢等唯有識者，如從夢覺便覺夢中皆唯有識，覺時何故不如是轉？真智覺時亦如是轉：如在夢中此覺不轉，從夢覺時此覺乃轉，如是未得真智覺時，此覺不轉，得真智覺此覺乃轉。

If, even in the waking state, at every time and place, experience is just like a dream, being consciousness alone, then — just as, upon waking from a dream, one comes to realize that everything within it was consciousness alone — why does the same realization not occur in the waking state? The answer is that it does occur, in just the same way, at the moment of awakening to true wisdom: just as, while still within the dream, this realization does not occur, and occurs only upon waking from the dream, so too, before attaining the awakening of true wisdom, this realization does not occur, and occurs only once the awakening of true wisdom is attained.

其有未得真智覺者，於唯識中云何比知？由教及理應可比知。此中教者，如《十地經》薄伽梵說：「如是三界，皆唯有心。」又薄伽梵《解深密經》亦如是說，謂彼經中慈氏菩薩問世尊言：「諸三摩地所行影像，彼與此心當言有異？當言無異？」佛告慈氏：「當言無異，何以故？由彼影像唯是識故；我說識所緣，唯識所現故。」「世尊！若三摩地所行影像，即與此心無有異者，云何此心還取此心？」「慈氏！無有少法能取少法，然即此心如是生時，即有如是影像顯現。如質為緣還見本質，而謂我今見於影像，及謂離質別有所見影像顯現。此心亦爾，如是生時，相似有異所見影現。」

For one who has not yet attained the awakening of true wisdom, how can Consciousness-Only be known by inference? It can be known by inference through both scripture and reasoning. As for scripture: the Bhagavat says, in the Daśabhūmika Sūtra, "These three realms are nothing but mind." The Bhagavat also says the same in the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra, where Maitreya Bodhisattva asks the World-Honored One: "The images that appear as the objects of meditative absorption — should they be said to differ from this mind, or should they be said not to differ from it?" The Buddha tells Maitreya: "They should be said not to differ from it. Why is this? Because those images are nothing but consciousness itself; I have taught that the object a consciousness takes is nothing but what consciousness itself manifests." "World-Honored One, if the images that appear as the objects of meditative absorption do not differ at all from this mind, then how does this mind come to grasp itself?" "Maitreya, no dharma whatsoever is able to grasp any other dharma. Rather, it is simply that, at the very moment this mind arises in such a manner, precisely such an image appears — just as, taking a physical object as condition, one sees that very object, and yet says, 'I now see an image,' and imagines that, apart from the object itself, there is some separate image being seen. This mind is just the same: at the moment it arises in this manner, what appears to be seen is something seemingly other, though it is not."

即由此教理亦顯現。所以者何？於定心中隨所觀見諸青瘀等所知影像，一切無別青瘀等事，但見自心。由此道理，菩薩於其一切識中，應可比知皆唯有識，無有境界。又於如是青瘀等中，非憶持識見所緣境，現前住故。聞思所成二憶持識，亦以過去為所緣故，所現影像得成唯識。由此比量，菩薩雖未得真智覺，於唯識中應可比知。

This is also shown by reasoning as well as scripture. Why is this? Because, within a meditatively concentrated mind, whatever image is contemplated and seen — a corpse turning livid-blue, for instance — there is, in fact, no separate livid-blue object present at all; one sees only one's own mind. By this reasoning, a bodhisattva should be able to infer, with regard to every consciousness, that there is nothing but consciousness, with no external object whatsoever. Moreover, with regard to such livid-blue meditation-images, it is not the memory-retaining consciousness that sees an object actually present before it, since the object is by definition not physically present; and the two memory-retaining consciousnesses produced by hearing and by reflection likewise take only something past as their object — so the image that in fact appears is thereby established as consciousness-only. By this inference, even a bodhisattva who has not yet attained the awakening of true wisdom should be able to infer Consciousness-Only.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

The dream simile does exactly one piece of work, and Vasubandhu is careful not to overclaim: it shows that a mind can produce vivid, structured, seemingly external experience with no real external object behind it at all — a bare possibility-proof, not yet a proof that waking experience actually works this way. The waking-versus-dreaming puzzle that follows — why don't we already see through the illusion the way a dream is seen through on waking? — is answered by treating full awakening itself as the analogous "waking up": ordinary beings are, in effect, still asleep with respect to consciousness-only, and only a buddha's or advanced bodhisattva's direct realization functions as the parallel moment of waking. For readers who haven't yet reached that realization, Asaṅga offers the meditator's blue-corpse visualization as a concrete, checkable case: everyone agrees such a meditation-image is not literally a corpse present in the room, yet it is seen vividly, in full color — which is only possible if an image can appear to consciousness without any matching external object, exactly the mechanism consciousness-only claims is happening in ordinary perception as well, just less obviously so.

Reading note — after Asvabhāva

The Maitreya dialogue quoted here is drawn from the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra's own chapter on meditation, which this site translates in full as part of its Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra pages — readers who want the fuller context for "no dharma can grasp any other dharma" can find the whole conversation there. Asvabhāva's own gloss stresses the everyday analogy the passage leans on: seeing a reflection and mistakenly imagining "the reflection" as something separate from "the object reflected," when in fact both are simply appearances of the one same underlying thing — mind, in the case of a meditation-image; the mirror's surface, in the case of an ordinary reflection.

5. Even Physical-Sense Consciousnesses Are Consciousness-Only

Chinese Source

如是已說種種諸識，如夢等喻，即於此中眼識等識可成唯識，眼等諸識既是有色，亦唯有識云何可見？此亦如前由教及理。若此諸識亦體是識，何故乃似色性顯現，一類堅住，相續而轉？與顛倒等諸雜染法為依處故；若不爾者，於非義中起義顛倒，應不得有。此若無者，煩惱、所知二障雜染應不得有。此若無者，諸清淨法亦應無有。是故諸識應如是轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far, various consciousnesses have been shown, by analogies such as the dream, to be consciousness-only. Within this, the eye-consciousness and the rest can likewise be established as consciousness-only — but since the eye and the other sense-consciousnesses appear to possess form, how can it be seen that they, too, are nothing but consciousness? This, too, is shown, as before, by both scripture and reasoning. If these consciousnesses, too, are in substance nothing but consciousness, why do they appear as if having the nature of physical form, remaining uniform and stable, continuing on in an unbroken stream? Because they serve as the basis for delusion and the other defiled dharmas. Were this not so, the delusion of taking what is not a real object to be a real object could not occur; and were this delusion not to occur, the defilements of the two obstructions — afflictive and cognitive — could not occur either; and were these not to occur, the pure dharmas that counteract them could not exist either. For this reason, these consciousnesses must function in just this way.

此中有頌：

「亂相及亂體，　應許為色識，

及與非色識，　若無餘亦無。」

Here there is a verse:

"The confused appearance, and the confused substance underlying it,

should be accepted as the form-consciousnesses and the non-form-consciousnesses, respectively;

without the one, the other, too, would not exist."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

This section closes a possible loophole: someone might grant that abstract mental experience is consciousness-only while insisting that seeing an actual, solid, colored object through the eye must be different, since color and shape seem so obviously physical. Asaṅga's answer is that the form-consciousnesses need to appear stable and physical-seeming precisely because that stability is what makes delusion — and therefore the whole edifice of affliction, obstruction, and the practices that counteract them — possible in the first place. A consciousness that visibly flickered and dissolved the way a dream-image does on waking could never be mistaken for solid matter; its very steadiness is functionally necessary for the illusion to work convincingly enough to trap ordinary beings. The verse's "confused appearance" and "confused substance" name two sides of the same coin: the vivid, form-like appearance itself, and the underlying consciousness that gives rise to it — remove either one and the other cannot exist either, since they are not really two separate things to begin with.

6. Why These Functions Co-Arise, and Why They Are So Numerous

Chinese Source

何故身，身者，受者識，所受識，能受識，於一切身中俱有和合轉，能圓滿生受用所顯故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it that the body-consciousness, the body-owner-consciousness, the experiencer-consciousness, the experienced-consciousness, and the experiencing-consciousness all operate together, united within every single body? Because it is only in this way that a complete life of arising and experienced enjoyment can be manifested.

何故如說世等諸識差別而轉？無始時來生死流轉無斷絕故；諸有情界無數量故；諸器世界無數量故；諸所作事展轉言說無數量故；各別攝取受用差別無數量故；諸愛非愛業果異熟受用差別無數量故，所受死生種種差別無數量故。

Why is it that the world-consciousness and the other consciousnesses just spoken of operate with such differentiation? Because saṃsāra's flow, since beginningless time, has never once been interrupted; because sentient beings' own realms are innumerable; because the physical worlds they inhabit are innumerable; because the deeds each performs, and the language used to describe them, are innumerable; because what each individually appropriates and enjoys differs innumerably; because the desirable and undesirable karmic results each ripens into, and enjoys, differ innumerably; and because the manifold circumstances of the death and rebirth each undergoes are innumerable.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

The first question explains why the ten kinds of consciousness listed earlier always show up together rather than one at a time: a complete moment of lived experience needs a faculty, an owner who claims it, an experiencer, an object, and the act of experiencing all present at once — remove any one and there is no complete experience to speak of. The second question then explains their sheer variety by pointing outward, to the simple scale of saṃsāra: there is no single fixed "amount" of experience because there is no limit to how many beings, worlds, actions, and life-circumstances there are to be differentiated. Multiplicity, on this reading, is not a mystery requiring some further object "out there" to explain it — an unbounded number of consciousness-streams, each running its own beginningless continuum, is already enough.

7. Three Marks That Establish Consciousness-Only

Chinese Source

復次，云何安立如是諸識成唯識性？略由三相：一、由唯識，無有義故；二、由二性，有相有見二識別故；三、由種種，種種行相而生起故。所以者何？此一切識無有義故，得成唯識。有相見故，得成二種：若眼等識，以色等識為相，以眼識識為見；乃至以身識識為見；若意識，以一切眼為最初，法為最後諸識為相，以意識識為見。由此意識有分別故，似一切識而生起故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, by what means is it established that these consciousnesses have the nature of consciousness-only? Briefly, by three marks: first, by being consciousness-only, since there is no real object; second, by having a twofold nature, since a distinction can be found between the consciousness that appears as an image and the consciousness that perceives it; third, by manifold-ness, since consciousness arises with manifold appearances. Why is this? Because all these consciousnesses have no real object, consciousness-only is established. Because they have an image-aspect and a perceiving-aspect, a twofold nature is established: in the case of the eye-consciousness and the rest, the consciousness that appears as form and the rest serves as the image, while the eye-consciousness that perceives it serves as the perceiver — and so on, up to the body-consciousness serving as the perceiver. In the case of the mental consciousness, the consciousnesses ranging from all that pertains to the eye at the beginning through to dharmas at the end serve as the image, while the mental consciousness itself serves as the perceiver. It is because this mental consciousness discriminates that it arises appearing as every other consciousness.

此中有頌：

「唯識二種種，　觀者意能入，

由悟入唯心，　彼亦能伏離。」

Here there is a verse:

"By entering consciousness-only, and by its twofold and manifold aspects,

the contemplative's mind is able to enter in;

and by awakening to mind-only,

that very mind is also able to subdue and set aside what is grasped."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

These three marks give a working structure to what "consciousness-only" actually means, rather than leaving it as a bare slogan. First, there is simply no object outside consciousness — this is the basic claim. Second, every act of consciousness nonetheless already has an internal image-aspect (what appears) and a perceiving-aspect (the awareness of it) built into a single event, which is why experience doesn't collapse into a single undifferentiated blank the moment an external object is denied. Third, this same structure repeats at every level and takes on endless specific appearances — the eye-consciousness's image is color-form, the mental consciousness's image is every other consciousness in turn — accounting for the sheer variety of what actually appears. The closing verse describes this as something to be entered progressively: first grasping that there is no object, then grasping the built-in image/perceiver structure, then grasping its endless variety — and, having entered all three, a practitioner is finally able to let go of grasping at an object altogether, since there was never a separate "thing grasped" to hold onto in the first place.

8. The One-Consciousness View; the Ālayavijñāna as Image and Seer

Chinese Source

又於此中，有一類師說一意識，彼彼依轉得彼彼名，如意思業名身語業。又於一切所依轉時，似種種相二影像轉：謂唯義影像，及分別影像。又一切處亦似所觸影像而轉，有色界中，即此意識依止身故，如餘色根依止於身。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, on this point, there is a group of teachers who hold that there is only a single mental consciousness, which, depending on whichever faculty it operates through, takes on that faculty's corresponding name — just as a single volitional mental action, operating through the body or through speech, is called bodily action or verbal action. Moreover, whenever it operates through any of its supports, it takes on two kinds of image at once: the image that seems to be an object, and the image that discriminates it. And in every case, it also operates as if taking on the image of what is physically touched — for, in the realm of form, this same mental consciousness depends on the body, just as the other, physical sense-faculties depend on the body.

此中有頌：

「若遠行，獨行，　無身寐於窟，

調此難調心，　我說真梵志。」

又如經言：「如是五根所行境界，意各能受，意為彼依。」

又如所說十二處中，說六識身皆名意處。

Here there is a verse:

"One who travels far, travels alone, is without a body, and lies down in a cave —

whoever tames this hard-to-tame mind, that one I call a true brāhmaṇa."

And as the sūtra says: "The objects that these five faculties operate on are each, in turn, received by the mind — the mind is their support."

And as is said among the twelve sense-bases, the six consciousness-bodies are all named the mental sense-base.

若處安立阿賴耶識識為義識，應知此中餘一切識是其相識，若意識識及所依止是其見識，由彼相識是此見識生緣相故，似義現時能作見識生依止事。如是名為安立諸識成唯識性。

Wherever the ālayavijñāna is posited as the object-consciousness, it should be understood that, in this scheme, every other consciousness is its image-consciousness, while the mental consciousness together with its support is its perceiving-consciousness — since that image-consciousness serves as the condition giving rise to this perceiving-consciousness, and, at the very moment it appears as an object, it thereby serves as the support for the perceiving-consciousness's own arising. This is what is meant by establishing that these consciousnesses have the nature of consciousness-only.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu presents the "one mental consciousness" view as a genuine alternative some teachers held: rather than eight distinct consciousnesses, there is really only one mental consciousness that simply takes on different names depending on which sense-faculty channel it happens to be operating through at the moment — much as a single intention, expressed physically, is called "bodily action," and the same intention, expressed verbally, is called "verbal action," without there being two separate intentions. The quoted verse and scriptural citations are marshaled in support of this single-consciousness reading: a "mind" that travels everywhere alone, without even needing a body, and that is what all five physical senses ultimately report back to. The final paragraph then offers a second, complementary way of mapping the same one-consciousness insight onto the vocabulary already developed for the ālayavijñāna in Part 2: the ālayavijñāna itself can be treated as the "object-consciousness," everything else as its "image," and the mental consciousness as the "perceiver" of that image — three roles played out within a single underlying continuum, whichever way of carving it up one prefers.

9. Four Kinds of Wisdom That Confirm There Is No Object

Chinese Source

諸義現前分明顯現而非是有，云何可知？如世尊言：「若諸菩薩成就四法，能隨悟入一切唯識都無有義。一者、成就相違識相智，如餓鬼傍生及諸天人，同於一事，見彼所識有差別故。二者、成就無所緣識現可得智，如過去未來夢影緣中有所得故。三者、成就應離功用無顛倒智，如有義中能緣義識應無顛倒，不由功用智真實故。

Ru-Yi Translation

How can it be known that objects, though they appear vividly and distinctly present, are not in fact real? As the World-Honored One has said: "If bodhisattvas accomplish four things, they are able to enter into the understanding that all is consciousness-only, entirely without any real object. First, accomplishing the wisdom of contradictory perceived appearances — as when hungry ghosts, animals, gods, and humans, encountering one and the same thing, each perceive it differently. Second, accomplishing the wisdom that a consciousness without any object can still actually be found to occur — as when something is vividly found within the objects of a dream, or of memory concerning the past or future. Third, accomplishing the undistorted wisdom that should be free of effort — for if a real object were what a cognizing consciousness actually took hold of, that consciousness ought to be undistorted without any need for effort, since its being true would not then depend on effort at all.

四者、成就三種勝智隨轉妙智。何等為三？一、得心自在一切菩薩，得靜慮者，隨勝解力諸義顯現。二、得奢摩他修法觀者，纔作意時諸義顯現。三、已得無分別智者無分別智現在前時，一切諸義皆不顯現。」由此所說三種勝智隨轉妙智，及前所說三種因緣，諸義無義道理成就。

Fourth, accomplishing the sublime wisdom that follows three kinds of superior wisdom. What are these three? First, all bodhisattvas who have mastered mind and attained meditative absorption find that objects appear according to the power of their own resolve. Second, those who have attained calm-abiding and cultivate the contemplation of dharmas find that objects appear the very instant attention is directed toward them. Third, those who have already attained non-discriminating wisdom find that, when non-discriminating wisdom is actually present, no object whatsoever appears at all." By these three kinds of superior wisdom and their sublime consequence, together with the three causal reasons stated previously, the principle that objects are without any real existence is established.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Each of the four proofs targets a different intuitive objection to consciousness-only. The first — contradictory perceived appearances — is the classic Yogācāra observation that a single river-spot looks like pus and blood to a hungry ghost, like a home to a fish, like nectar to a god, and like ordinary water to a human being; if there were one real, fixed object out there, it could not simultaneously have all these mutually exclusive natures. The second appeals to dreams and memory, where vivid content plainly appears without any present external object to match it. The third is more subtle: if perception simply registered a real object as it actually is, correct perception would require no training or effort at all — but in fact freedom from distortion is something that must be cultivated, which only makes sense if ordinary perception is not simply passive registration of a ready-made external world. The fourth points to advanced meditative states where objects visibly change according to the meditator's own resolve, appear or vanish with attention alone, or disappear altogether in the presence of non-discriminating wisdom — behavior no fixed external object could ever display.

10. Why Each Nature Is Named As It Is

Chinese Source

若依他起自性，實唯有識，似義顯現之所依止，云何成依他起？何因緣故名依他起？從自熏習種子所生，依他緣起故名依他起；生剎那後無有功能自然住故，名依他起。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the dependent nature — truly nothing but consciousness, and the very basis for the appearance of a seeming object — how does it come to be "dependent"? For what reason is it so named? Because it arises from its own habituated seeds, and arises in dependence on other conditions, it is named "dependent"; and because, the instant after it arises, it has no capacity to remain of its own accord, it is, for this reason too, named "dependent."

若遍計所執自性，依依他起實無所有似義顯現，云何成遍計所執？何因緣故名遍計所執？無量行相意識遍計顛倒生相，故名遍計所執；自相實無，唯有遍計所執可得，是故說名遍計所執。

As for the discriminated, attached-to nature — depending on the dependent nature, though truly nonexistent, yet appearing as a seeming object — how does it come to be "discriminated, attached-to"? For what reason is it so named? Because it is the characteristic, born of distortion, universally discriminated by the mental consciousness across boundless modes of activity, it is named "discriminated, attached-to"; and since its own specific nature is truly nonexistent, with only the act of being discriminated and attached to actually to be found, it is for this reason named "discriminated, attached-to."

若圓成實自性，是遍計所執永無有相，云何成圓成實？何因緣故名圓成實？由無變異性故名圓成實；又由清淨所緣性故，一切善法最勝性故，由最勝義名圓成實。

As for the perfectly accomplished nature — the permanent absence of any characteristic of the discriminated, attached-to nature — how does it come to be "perfectly accomplished"? For what reason is it so named? Because it has the nature of being without change, it is named "perfectly accomplished"; and because it has the nature of being a pure object, and the nature of being the most excellent of all wholesome dharmas, by this most excellent meaning it is named "perfectly accomplished."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Each name, Vasubandhu shows, is doing precise technical work rather than simply labeling. "Dependent" carries two senses at once: dependent in the sense of arising from conditions (rather than being self-caused or eternal), and dependent in the sense of being unable to sustain itself for even a second moment without those same conditions recurring — momentariness is built into the very meaning of the word. "Discriminated, attached-to" names something with, strictly speaking, no nature of its own at all — it exists only as an act of discrimination and the attachment that follows it, never as an independent thing that could be found apart from that act. And "perfectly accomplished" is named for two reasons at once: it never changes (unlike the dependent nature, which is constantly arising and ceasing), and it is the purest possible object of contemplation, the very thing that makes every wholesome practice ultimately wholesome. None of the three names is arbitrary; each encodes exactly what distinguishes that nature from the other two.

11. The Universal Imagining: Who Imagines, What Is Imagined, and How

Chinese Source

復次，有能遍計，有所遍計，遍計所執自性乃成。此中何者能遍計？何者所遍計？何者遍計所執自性？當知意識是能遍計，有分別故。所以者何？由此意識用自名言熏習為種子，及用一切識名言熏習為種子，是故意識無邊行相分別而轉，普於一切分別計度，故名遍計。又依他起自性，名所遍計。又若由此相令依他起自性成所遍計，此中是名遍計所執自性。由此相者，是如此義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, there is a universally-imagining subject, and a universally-imagined object, and it is through these that the discriminated, attached-to nature comes about. Here, what is the universally-imagining subject? What is the universally-imagined object? What is the discriminated, attached-to nature itself? It should be understood that the mental consciousness is the universally-imagining subject, since it possesses discrimination. Why is this? Because this mental consciousness uses its own name-habituation as seed, and also uses the name-habituation of every consciousness as seed, the mental consciousness therefore discriminates in boundless modes of activity, universally imagining and calculating with regard to everything — for this reason it is named "universal imagining." The dependent nature, in turn, is named "the universally imagined." And wherever this very process causes the dependent nature to become what is universally imagined, this is what is named the discriminated, attached-to nature.

復次，云何遍計能遍計度？緣何境界？取何相貌？由何執著？由何起語？由何言說？何所增益？謂緣名為境，於依他起自性中取彼相貌，由見執著，由尋起語，由見聞等四種言說而起言說，於無義中增益為有，由此遍計能遍計度。

Furthermore, how does the universally-imagining subject actually imagine and calculate? What sphere does it take as its object? What appearance does it grasp? Through what does it become attached? Through what does it give rise to speech? Through what does it come to use language? What does it superimpose? The answer: it takes names as its sphere; within the dependent nature it grasps a corresponding appearance; through seeing, it becomes attached; through inquiry, it gives rise to speech; through the four kinds of language — seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing — it comes to use language; and, upon what is without any real object, it superimposes existence. It is by this process that the universally-imagining subject actually imagines and calculates.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

This section names names, so to speak: the "imagining subject" is not some abstract force but the ordinary sixth, mental consciousness, doing what it always does — discriminating, using the linguistic and conceptual habits stored as seeds in the ālayavijñāna. The "imagined object" is not some external world but the dependent nature itself, the real, conditioned flow of consciousness described in Part 2 — the mental consciousness imagines a fixed object onto this flow. And the step-by-step mechanism Asaṅga traces — taking a name as one's sphere, grasping an apparent shape within the dependent flow, becoming attached through seeing it, generating speech through inquiry, and finally using full-blown language built from seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing — describes, in effect, how a name first gets attached to experience and then quietly convinces the mind that the named thing was there all along, independent of the naming.

12. Are the Three Natures the Same, or Different? How Many Kinds Does Each Have?

Chinese Source

復次，此三自性為異為不異？應言非異非不異。謂依他起自性，由異門故成依他起；即此自性由異門故成遍計所執，即此自性由異門故成圓成實。由何異門此依他起成依他起？依他熏習種子起故。由何異門即此自性成遍計所執？由是遍計所緣相故，又是遍計所遍計故。由何異門即此自性成圓成實？如所遍計畢竟不如是有故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, are these three natures the same, or are they different? It should be said that they are neither the same nor different. That is: the dependent nature, viewed under one aspect, is the dependent nature; this very same nature, viewed under another aspect, is the discriminated, attached-to nature; and this very same nature, viewed under yet another aspect, is the perfectly accomplished nature. Under what aspect does this dependent nature become the dependent nature? Because it arises from habituated seeds, in dependence on other conditions. Under what aspect does this very same nature become the discriminated, attached-to nature? Because it serves as the object universally imagined, and because it is what universal imagining actually imagines. Under what aspect does this very same nature become the perfectly accomplished nature? Because what is universally imagined ultimately does not exist in the way it is imagined to.

此三自性各有幾種？謂依他起略有二種：一者、依他熏習種子而生起故，二者、依他雜染清淨性不成故，由此二種依他別故，名依他起。遍計所執亦有二種：一者、自性遍計執故，二者、差別遍計執故，由此故名遍計所執。圓成實性亦有二種：一者、自性圓成實故，二者、清淨圓成實故，由此故成圓成實性。

How many kinds does each of these three natures have? The dependent nature, briefly, has two kinds: first, because it arises from habituated seeds, in dependence on other conditions; second, because, in dependence on other conditions, its nature as defiled or pure is not yet fixed. Because of these two aspects, it is distinctly named "dependent." The discriminated, attached-to nature likewise has two kinds: first, the imagining and attachment to something's own specific nature; second, the imagining and attachment to its differentiating qualities — and it is for this reason that it is named "discriminated, attached-to." The perfectly accomplished nature likewise has two kinds: first, the perfectly accomplished nature in its own essential aspect; second, the perfectly accomplished nature in its purified aspect — and it is for this reason that it is established as the perfectly accomplished nature.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

"Neither the same nor different" is the treatise's own careful middle path between two unwanted extremes: if the three natures were simply identical, there would be no point distinguishing them at all, and delusion and liberation would collapse into being the same thing; if they were simply different, unrelated things, then removing the discriminated nature could never reveal the perfectly accomplished nature, since the two would have nothing to do with each other. Instead, they are three aspects of one and the same underlying flow, seen under three different descriptions — the way the very same rope can, without contradiction, be correctly described as "made of fiber" (its dependent, conditioned reality), mistakenly described as "a snake" (the discriminated illusion projected onto it), and truthfully described as "not actually a snake" (its perfectly accomplished freedom from that illusion). The two-kind breakdown for each nature then adds a further layer of precision: the dependent nature is two-sided because it is capable of appearing either defiled or purified depending on what discrimination does with it; the discriminated nature splits into imagining a thing's basic identity versus imagining its further qualities; and the perfectly accomplished nature splits into its own timeless essence versus its condition once actually realized and purified through practice.

13. Four, and Then Five, Kinds of Universal Imagining

Chinese Source

復次遍計有四種：一、自性遍計，二、差別遍計，三、有覺遍計，四、無覺遍計。有覺者，謂善名言；無覺者，謂不善名言。如是遍計復有五種：一、依名遍計義自性，謂如是名有如是義；二、依義遍計名自性，謂如是義有如是名；三、依名遍計名自性，謂遍計度未了義名；四、依義遍計義自性，謂遍計度未了名義；五、依二遍計二自性，謂遍計度此名此義如是體性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, universal imagining is of four kinds: first, imagining a thing's own specific nature; second, imagining its differentiating qualities; third, imagining accompanied by awareness; fourth, imagining without awareness. "Accompanied by awareness" means one skilled in language; "without awareness" means one unskilled in language. Universal imagining, again, has five further kinds: first, imagining an object's own nature in dependence on a name — thinking that such-and-such a name has such-and-such a meaning; second, imagining a name's own nature in dependence on an object — thinking that such-and-such an object has such-and-such a name; third, imagining a name's own nature in dependence on a name — imagining a name whose meaning is not yet understood; fourth, imagining an object's own nature in dependence on an object — imagining an object whose name is not yet understood; fifth, imagining both natures in dependence on both — imagining that this very name and this very object have just such an essential nature.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

The fourfold list distinguishes imagining a thing's basic identity from imagining its further properties, and separately distinguishes imagining done by a language-user (a human being who can attach and manipulate names) from imagining done without that awareness — Vasubandhu gives the classic example of an ox or a sheep, which plainly does discriminate and react to things without possessing anything like conceptual language. The fivefold list then works through every possible direction the mind can move between a name and its object: starting from a name and imagining what it must mean; starting from an object and imagining what it must be called; puzzling over an unfamiliar name; puzzling over an unfamiliar thing; and, finally, taking a specific name-and-object pairing together and imagining that this exact combination reflects some fixed, real essence — which is, for Asaṅga, the single most consequential mistake of the five, since it is the one that actually produces the discriminated, attached-to nature in its fullest form.

14. Ten Kinds of Discrimination, and Their Single Antidote

The treatise now catalogues every kind of discrimination there is — a comprehensive taxonomy that culminates in the ten specific distractions bodhisattvas must overcome, and names the one wisdom that overcomes all of them at once.

Chinese Source

復次，總攝一切分別略有十種：一、根本分別，謂阿賴耶識。二、緣相分別，謂色等識。三、顯相分別，謂眼識等并所依識。四、緣相變異分別，謂老等變異，樂受等變異，貪等變異，逼害時節代謝等變異，㮈落迦等諸趣變異，及欲界等諸界變異。五、顯相變異分別，謂即如前所說變異所有變異。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, all discrimination, gathered together, is briefly of ten kinds: first, root discrimination — the ālayavijñāna. Second, discrimination of the perceived-object aspect — the consciousness of form and the rest. Third, discrimination of the manifesting aspect — the eye-consciousness and the rest, together with their supporting consciousness. Fourth, discrimination of change within the perceived-object aspect — such changes as aging, changes of pleasant feeling and the rest, changes of craving and the rest, changes brought by harm and by the passage of the seasons, changes among the hell-beings and the other destinies, and changes among the desire realm and the other realms. Fifth, discrimination of change within the manifesting aspect — namely, the changes just described, as they occur within the manifesting aspect itself.

六、他引分別，謂聞非正法類及聞正法類分別。七、不如理分別，謂諸外道聞非正法類分別。八、如理分別，謂正法中聞正法類分別。九、執著分別，謂不如理作意類，薩迦耶見為本六十二見趣相應分別。十、散動分別，謂諸菩薩十種分別：一、無相散動，二、有相散動，三、增益散動，四、損減散動，五、一性散動，六、異性散動，七、自性散動，八、差別散動，九、如名取義散動，十、如義取名散動。

Sixth, discrimination induced by another — discrimination arising from hearing a category that is not the true Dharma, or from hearing a category that is the true Dharma. Seventh, unwise discrimination — the discrimination of non-Buddhist teachers, arising from hearing what is not the true Dharma. Eighth, wise discrimination — discrimination, within the true Dharma, arising from hearing what is the true Dharma. Ninth, discrimination through attachment — discrimination associated with the sixty-two mistaken views rooted in the view of a substantial self, belonging to the category of unwise attention. Tenth, discrimination through distraction — the ten kinds of distraction found among bodhisattvas: first, distraction toward the characteristic-less; second, distraction toward the characteristic-bearing; third, distraction through superimposition; fourth, distraction through denial; fifth, distraction toward identity; sixth, distraction toward difference; seventh, distraction toward own-nature; eighth, distraction toward differentiating qualities; ninth, distraction that grasps the meaning as the name would suggest; tenth, distraction that grasps the name as the meaning would suggest.

為對治此十種散動，一切般若波羅蜜多中說無分別智。如是所治能治，應知具攝般若波羅蜜多義。

It is in order to counteract these ten kinds of distraction that non-discriminating wisdom is taught throughout the Perfection of Wisdom scriptures. This being what is counteracted and what counteracts it, it should be understood that the entire meaning of the Perfection of Wisdom is thereby encompassed.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu reads this list as moving from the most fundamental discrimination outward to the most refined: the ālayavijñāna itself is "root discrimination" because every other kind ultimately depends on it; the next four kinds simply track ordinary perceptual experience and its constant change; the following four sort discriminations by their source — imposed by an outside teacher, wrong or right depending on whether that teacher speaks the true Dharma, and finally hardened into fixed philosophical views. The tenth kind, reserved specifically for bodhisattvas, is the most demanding: ten specific ways even an advanced practitioner's mind can still wobble while contemplating emptiness — swinging between denying all characteristics and clinging to some characteristic, between exaggerating what's there and erasing what's there, between claiming things are identical and claiming they're utterly separate, and so on. Vasubandhu ties each of these ten directly to specific lines of the Perfection of Wisdom scriptures, which state and then immediately correct exactly this kind of imbalance — which is why he says the whole of the Prajñāpāramitā literature can be understood as one sustained treatment for these ten specific ailments, with non-discriminating wisdom as the single remedy for all of them at once.

15. Why the Discriminated Nature Only Seems to Match a Real Thing

Chinese Source

復次，云何得知如依他起自性，遍計所執自性顯現而非稱體？由名前覺無，稱體相違故；由名有眾多，多體相違故；由名不決定，雜體相違故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, how may it be known that, just as with the dependent nature, the discriminated, attached-to nature merely appears, without actually matching any real substance? Because, prior to the assignment of a name, there is no corresponding cognition — which contradicts its matching a real substance; because a single thing can have many names — which contradicts its matching a single, real substance; and because a name's meaning is not fixed — which contradicts its matching some single, mixed-together real substance.

此中有二頌：

「由名前覺無，　多名，不決定；

成稱體，多體，　雜體相違故。

法無而可得，　無染而有淨，

應知如幻等，　亦復似虛空。」

Here there are two verses:

"Because, prior to the name, there is no cognition; because there are many names; because meaning is not fixed —

for these reasons, its matching a real substance, matching a single substance, and matching a mixed-together substance are all contradicted.

Though the dharma that is imagined does not exist, it can still be found apparently present;

though there is no defilement in ultimate reality, there is still apparent purification —

this should be understood as being like an illusion, and also like empty space."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu's three arguments each show that a name cannot simply be labeling some pre-existing, matching real thing. If a name and the thing it names shared one and the same real substance, then simply encountering that substance — before ever hearing its name — should already produce the corresponding recognition, but it plainly does not, the way a small child sees a vase without yet having any "vase" cognition until taught the word. If name and thing shared one real substance, one thing could not have several different names (as many things do), since one real substance cannot equal several different substances at once. And if name and thing shared a real substance, a single ambiguous word (Vasubandhu's example is a term that can mean several unrelated things depending on context) could not correspond to several unrelated real substances simultaneously. Each of these plain, checkable facts about how naming actually works shows that a name never simply mirrors a fixed reality it points to — which is exactly what "discriminated, attached-to" means: something existing only as an act of naming and attaching, not as a thing already there to be named. The closing verse's two similes — illusion, and empty space — capture the resulting paradox from two directions: an illusion shows how something nonexistent can still be vividly "found" appearing; empty space shows how something that was never actually stained can still meaningfully be said to become "pure," once the illusion of a stain has been seen through.

16. Why the Dependent Nature Isn't Simply Nonexistent Either

Chinese Source

復次，何故如所顯現實無所有，而依他起自性非一切一切都無所有？此若無者，圓成實自性亦無所有；此若無者，則一切皆無。若依他起及圓成實自性無有，應成無有染淨過失。既現可得雜染清淨，是故不應一切皆無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, why is it that, although what appears has no real existence at all, the dependent nature is not therefore utterly nonexistent in every respect? Because, were this not so, the perfectly accomplished nature, too, would be nonexistent; and were this not so, then everything whatsoever would be nonexistent. If the dependent nature and the perfectly accomplished nature did not exist, this would entail the fault that there could be no defilement and no purification at all. But since defilement and purification are, in fact, actually found to occur, it must therefore not be the case that everything whatsoever is nonexistent.

此中有頌：

「若無依他起，　圓成實亦無，

一切種若無，　恒時無染淨。」

Here there is a verse:

"If there were no dependent nature, the perfectly accomplished, too, would not exist;

if every kind of nature did not exist, there would, at all times, be neither defilement nor purification."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

This short section guards against a real risk in Yogācāra teaching: having just shown that the discriminated, attached-to nature is entirely unreal, a careless reader might conclude that nothing at all is real — a nihilism the tradition treats as far more dangerous than the naive realism it is correcting. Vasubandhu's argument is simple and practical: defilement and purification are undeniable, everyday facts of experience — beings suffer, and beings are freed from suffering through practice. If the dependent nature (the real, conditioned flow of experience) and the perfectly accomplished nature (that flow's real freedom from illusion) did not genuinely exist, there would be nothing for defilement to happen to and nothing for purification to consist in. The doctrine of emptiness taught throughout this part, in other words, denies only the discriminated, attached-to nature's imagined objects — never the dependent flow of conditioned experience itself, and never the perfectly accomplished reality uncovered once illusion is removed.

17. Scripture's Own Similes: Eight Images for the Dependent Nature, Four Purities for the Perfectly Accomplished

The treatise now turns to how the Great Vehicle's own extended scriptures already speak of the three natures, quoting a passage that supplies the classic eightfold list of similes for the dependent nature and a fourfold analysis of purity for the perfectly accomplished.

Chinese Source

諸佛世尊於大乘中說方廣教，彼教中言：「云何應知遍計所執自性？應知異門說無所有。云何應知依他起自性？應知譬如幻、炎、夢、像、光、影、谷響、水月、變化。云何應知圓成實自性？應知宣說四清淨法。何等名為四清淨法？一者、自性清淨，謂真如空、實際、無相、勝義、法界。二者、離垢清淨，謂即此離一切障垢。

Ru-Yi Translation

The buddhas, the world-honored ones, in the Great Vehicle's own extended scriptures, have said: "How should the discriminated, attached-to nature be understood? It should be understood, under a different designation, as utterly nonexistent. How should the dependent nature be understood? It should be understood as being like an illusion, a mirage, a dream, a reflected image, light and shadow, an echo in a valley, the moon's reflection in water, and a magical transformation. How should the perfectly accomplished nature be understood? It should be understood through the teaching of the four pure dharmas. What are these four pure dharmas? First, intrinsic purity — that is, suchness, emptiness, the ultimate limit, the signless, the ultimate truth, and the dharma-realm. Second, purity gained by leaving defilement — that is, this very intrinsic purity, once free of every obstructing stain.

三者、得此道清淨，謂一切菩提分法波羅蜜多等。四者、生此境清淨，謂諸大乘妙正法教。由此法教，清淨緣故，非遍計所執自性；最淨法界等流性故，非依他起自性。如是四法，總攝一切清淨法盡。」

Third, purity of the path by which this is attained — that is, all the factors of enlightenment, the perfections, and the like. Fourth, purity of the object that gives rise to this — that is, the wondrous, true teaching of the Great Vehicle. Because this teaching serves as a pure condition, it is not the discriminated, attached-to nature; and because it has the nature of what flows forth from the purest dharma-realm, it is not the dependent nature either. These four dharmas, together, entirely encompass every pure dharma there is."

此中有二頌：

「幻等說於生：　說無計所執，

若說四清淨，　是謂圓成實。

自性與離垢，　清淨道所緣，

一切清淨法，　皆四相所攝。」

Here there are two verses:

"Illusion and the rest speak of arising; speaking of nonexistence speaks of the discriminated, attached-to;

speaking of the four pure dharmas — this is what is meant by the perfectly accomplished.

Intrinsic purity, and purity gained by leaving defilement, the object of the path, and the path itself —

every pure dharma is, without exception, gathered within these four aspects."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu takes care to note that the four "pure dharmas" are not four separate things but four stages of one and the same purity, seen from different angles: intrinsic purity is suchness itself, always already present, the shared nature every being's mind has whether recognized or not — the seed of what the tradition elsewhere calls the tathāgatagarbha, the buddha-embryo present in all beings; purity gained by leaving defilement is that same suchness once the afflictive and cognitive obstructions covering it have actually been removed, which is what makes a buddha a buddha; the purity of the path is simply the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment and the perfections that accomplish that removal; and the purity of the object is the Mahāyāna scriptures themselves, which supply the very teaching that sets the path in motion. He is also careful to specify why this teaching itself counts as neither discriminated nor dependent: unlike the discriminated nature, the Mahāyāna teaching genuinely serves as a real, effective cause of purification, not a mere false projection; and unlike the ordinary dependent nature, it "flows forth" directly from the purest dharma-realm rather than from ordinary defiled seeds, giving it a different origin from ordinary conditioned experience even while it still, in one sense, appears within it.

18. Why Each Simile Answers One Specific Doubt

Having listed the eight similes, the treatise now explains precisely which objection each one is designed to answer — showing that the list is not decorative but a carefully engineered set of responses to eight distinct, natural doubts about how a world with no real object could still function the way it does.

Chinese Source

復次，何緣如經所說於依他起自性說幻等喻？於依他起自性為除他虛妄疑故。他復云何於依他起自性有虛妄疑？由他於此有如是疑：云何實無有義而成所行境界？為除此疑說幻事喻。云何無義心心法轉？為除此疑說陽炎喻。云何無義有愛非愛受用差別？為除此疑說所夢喻。云何無義淨不淨業愛非愛果差別而生？為除此疑說影像喻。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, for what reason does the sūtra, as quoted, speak of illusion and the other similes with regard to the dependent nature? It is in order to remove others' mistaken doubts concerning the dependent nature. What mistaken doubts, then, do others hold? They hold doubts of this kind: How can there be an object actually experienced, when in truth there is no object at all? To remove this doubt, the simile of an illusory object is spoken. How can mind and mental factors operate, when there is no object at all? To remove this doubt, the simile of a mirage is spoken. How can there be experienced differences of the desirable and undesirable, when there is no object at all? To remove this doubt, the simile of a dream is spoken. How can the differentiated results — desirable and undesirable — of pure and impure karma arise, when there is no object at all? To remove this doubt, the simile of a reflected image is spoken.

云何無義種種識轉？為除此疑說光影喻。云何無義種種戲論言說而轉？為除此疑說谷響喻。云何無義而有實取諸三摩地所行境轉？為除此疑說水月喻。云何無義有諸菩薩無顛倒心，為辦有情諸利樂事，故思受生？為除此疑說變化喻。

How can the manifold consciousnesses operate, when there is no object at all? To remove this doubt, the simile of light and shadow is spoken. How can manifold discursive speech operate, when there is no object at all? To remove this doubt, the simile of an echo in a valley is spoken. How can there be an object genuinely grasped as the sphere of various meditative absorptions, when there is no object at all? To remove this doubt, the simile of the moon in water is spoken. How can bodhisattvas, whose minds are free of distortion, deliberately choose to take rebirth for the sake of accomplishing benefit and happiness for sentient beings, when there is no object — no being — at all? To remove this doubt, the simile of a magical transformation is spoken.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Read end to end, the eight doubts trace a path from the most basic worry (how can objectless experience feel like experiencing anything at all?) through increasingly sophisticated ones (how can objectless minds produce reliable karmic results, or coherent language, or genuinely effective meditation?) to the most practically urgent for a Mahāyāna reader: if there is truly no independently existing being to help, how can a bodhisattva's compassionate choice to be reborn for the sake of others mean anything at all? Vasubandhu treats the magical-transformation simile as the deliberate capstone answer to that last, highest-stakes doubt: a magically created person can act, help, and accomplish real things in the world with total effectiveness, without there needing to be, underneath the magic, some separately, independently existing self performing the action — which is exactly the model the treatise offers for how bodhisattva activity is both fully real in its effects and empty of any fixed self performing it.

19. Resolving Two Puzzling Sayings: The Brahma-Questions Sūtra, and the Gold-Ore Simile

Chinese Source

世尊依何密意於《梵問經》中說：「如來不得生死，不得涅槃？」於依他起自性中，依遍計所執自性及圓成實自性，生死涅槃無差別密意。何以故？即此依他起自性，由遍計所執分成生死，由圓成實分成涅槃故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With what hidden intent did the World-Honored One say, in the Brahma-Questions Sūtra, that "the Tathāgata neither attains birth-and-death nor attains nirvāṇa"? The answer: within the dependent nature, in dependence on the discriminated, attached-to nature and the perfectly accomplished nature, there is a hidden intent that birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are without difference. Why is this? Because this very dependent nature, through its discriminated, attached-to portion, constitutes birth-and-death, and, through its perfectly accomplished portion, constitutes nirvāṇa.

《阿毘達磨大乘經》中薄伽梵說：「法有三種：一、雜染分，二、清淨分，三、彼二分。」依何密意作如是說？於依他起自性中，遍計所執自性是雜染分，圓成實自性是清淨分，即依他起是彼二分；依此密意作如是說。於此義中以何喻顯？以金土藏為喻顯示。譬如世間金土藏中三法可得：一地界，二土，三金。於地界中土非實有而現可得，金是實有而不可得；火燒鍊時，土相不現，金相顯現。

In the Abhidharma-mahāyāna-sūtra, the Bhagavat says: "Dharma is of three kinds: first, the defiled portion; second, the pure portion; third, that which is both." With what hidden intent was this said? Within the dependent nature, the discriminated, attached-to nature is the defiled portion, the perfectly accomplished nature is the pure portion, and the dependent nature itself is that which is both; it is with this hidden intent that this was said. By what analogy may this meaning be illustrated? It is illustrated by the analogy of a gold-bearing ore-vein. Just as, in the ordinary world, three things can be found within such a vein — the earth-element, the soil, and the gold — within that earth-element, the soil, though not truly real, actually appears, while the gold, though truly real, does not yet appear; but once it has been smelted by fire, the appearance of soil no longer shows, and the appearance of gold shows instead.

又此地界，土顯現時虛妄顯現，金顯現時真實顯現，是故地界是彼二分。識亦如是，無分別智火未燒時，於此識中所有虛妄遍計所執自性顯現，所有真實圓成實自性不顯現。此識若為無分別智火所燒時，於此識中所有真實圓成實自性顯現，所有虛妄遍計所執自性不顯現；是故此虛妄分別識依他起自性有彼二分，如金土藏中所有地界。

Moreover, this earth-element, when soil appears, appears falsely; when gold appears, appears truly — and it is for this reason that the earth-element is that which is both. Consciousness is just the same: before the fire of non-discriminating wisdom has smelted it, the false, discriminated, attached-to nature present within this consciousness appears, while the true, perfectly accomplished nature present within it does not appear. Once this consciousness has been smelted by the fire of non-discriminating wisdom, the true, perfectly accomplished nature present within it appears, while the false, discriminated, attached-to nature present within it no longer appears. It is for this reason that this consciousness of false discrimination — the dependent nature — is that which is both, just as the earth-element is, within a gold-bearing ore-vein.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Both puzzles get the same solution: a saying that sounds paradoxical on its face turns out to be a precise statement about the dependent nature's two-sidedness. "The Tathāgata neither attains birth-and-death nor nirvāṇa" is not denying that ordinary beings suffer, or that buddhas are liberated — it is pointing out that both birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are simply two different ways the very same dependent flow of experience can appear, depending on whether the discriminated or the perfectly accomplished portion is uncovered, so there is, in that sense, nothing separate called "birth-and-death" to escape from and nothing separate called "nirvāṇa" to arrive at. The gold-ore simile then makes this vivid: a vein of ore genuinely contains gold from the very start, though what actually appears, before smelting, is only worthless-looking soil; smelting doesn't add gold that wasn't there — it simply burns away what was obscuring it. Applied to the mind, non-discriminating wisdom works the same way: the perfectly accomplished nature was never absent from the dependent flow of consciousness, only obscured by the discriminated, attached-to nature layered over it, and practice is the "smelting fire" that removes the obscuring layer rather than manufacturing something new.

20. Resolving "Permanent or Impermanent?" — and Every Paired Non-Duality

The treatise applies the same three-natures key to the largest remaining puzzle: why the Buddha, in different scriptures, seems to say flatly contradictory things about whether dharmas are permanent, impermanent, or neither — and extends the solution to a long list of similarly paired terms.

Chinese Source

世尊有處說一切法常，有處說一切法無常，有處說一切法非常非無常，依何密意作如是說？謂依他起自性，由圓成實性分是常，由遍計所執性分是無常，由彼二分非常非無常；依此密意作如是說。如常無常無二，如是苦，樂，無二；淨，不淨，無二；空，不空，無二；我，無我，無二；寂靜，不寂靜，無二；有自性，無自性，無二；生，不生，無二；滅，不滅，無二；本來寂靜，非本來寂靜，無二；自性涅槃，非自性涅槃，無二；生死，涅槃，無二亦爾。如是等差別，一切諸佛密意語言，由三自性應隨決了，如前說常無常等門。

Ru-Yi Translation

In some places the World-Honored One has said that all dharmas are permanent; in other places, that all dharmas are impermanent; in still other places, that all dharmas are neither permanent nor impermanent. With what hidden intent was this said? The dependent nature, through its perfectly accomplished portion, is permanent; through its discriminated, attached-to portion, is impermanent; and through both portions together, is neither permanent nor impermanent. It is with this hidden intent that this was said. Just as there is this apparent non-duality of permanent and impermanent, so too is there non-duality of suffering and pleasure; of pure and impure; of empty and not empty; of self and non-self; of quiescent and non-quiescent; of having own-nature and being without own-nature; of arising and not arising; of ceasing and not ceasing; of being originally quiescent and not being originally quiescent; of being intrinsically in nirvāṇa and not being intrinsically in nirvāṇa; and, likewise, of birth-and-death and nirvāṇa. All such differentiated sayings, spoken with hidden intent by all the buddhas, should be resolved and understood through the three natures, following the same pattern just shown for "permanent" and "impermanent."

Chinese Source

此中有多頌：

「如法實不有，　如現非一種；

非法非非法，　故說無二義。

依一分開顯，　或有或非有；

依二分說言，　非有非非有。

如顯現非有，　是故說為無。

由如是顯現，　是故說為有。

自然，自體無，　自性不堅住，

如執取不有，　故許無自性。

由無性故成，　後後所依止，

無生滅，本寂，　自性般涅槃。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Here there are several verses:

"As a dharma that is imagined is not truly real, yet as it appears it is not merely of one kind —

being neither simply a dharma nor simply not a dharma, the teaching therefore speaks of the meaning of non-duality.

Relying on one portion, it is disclosed as either existent or nonexistent;

relying on both portions, it is spoken of as neither existent nor nonexistent.

As it appears, it is not truly existent — therefore it is said to be nonexistent.

Because it does appear in just this way — therefore it is said to be existent.

Spontaneously, it has no own substance; its own-nature does not remain fixed —

just as what is grasped does not exist, so it is accepted that all dharmas are without own-nature.

Because they are without fixed nature, they are thereby established, one supporting the next in turn —

without arising or ceasing, originally quiescent, all dharmas are, by their own nature, in nirvāṇa."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu treats this entire list — permanent/impermanent, pure/impure, self/non-self, arising/not-arising, and the rest — as instances of exactly one pattern, repeated: whenever the Buddha's teaching seems to affirm and deny the very same predicate of "all dharmas," look at which portion of the dependent nature is being described. Describe only the perfectly accomplished portion, and the positive term (permanent, pure, self-abiding suchness) applies; describe only the discriminated, attached-to portion, and the negative term (impermanent, impure, without fixed self) applies; describe the dependent nature as a whole, encompassing both portions at once, and neither term straightforwardly applies, since it is genuinely both at different levels simultaneously. The final verses give the underlying reason all of this works: nothing described here has a fixed, independent own-nature to begin with — not even the "existence" or "nonexistence" being debated — so nothing here is actually caught in contradiction; it is only the assumption that "permanent" and "impermanent" must refer to one single, fixed thing that generates the apparent paradox. Once that assumption is dropped, and each remark is read against the specific portion of the dependent nature it is actually describing, all the pairs resolve into consistency — closing, fittingly, with a verse suggesting that dharmas, being without arising or ceasing to begin with, are in a certain sense already in nirvāṇa by their own nature, prior to and beneath any particular attainment.

21. Four Intentions Behind the Buddha's Words

The treatise now turns from the three natures to a related interpretive tool: four reasons the Buddha's own recorded sayings sometimes need to be read with a hidden intent behind their literal surface, rather than taken at face value.

Chinese Source

復有四種意趣，四種祕密，一切佛言應隨決了。四意趣者：一、平等意趣，謂如說言：「我昔曾於彼時彼分，即名勝觀正等覺者。」二、別時意趣，謂如說言：「若誦多寶如來名者，便於無上正等菩提已得決定。」又如說言：「由唯發願，便得往生極樂世界。」

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, four kinds of intention and four kinds of hidden meaning, by which all the Buddha's sayings should be resolved and understood. The four intentions are: first, the intention of equality — as when it is said, "I myself, long ago, at that time and in that portion of the eon, was already the Fully Awakened One Vipaśyin." Second, the intention of a different time — as when it is said, "Whoever recites the name of the Tathāgata Many Jewels thereby attains certainty of unsurpassed, perfect awakening"; or as when it is said, "By merely making the vow, one attains rebirth in the Land of Bliss."

三、別義意趣，謂如說言：「若已逢事爾所殑伽河沙等佛，於大乘法方能解義。」四、補特伽羅意樂意趣，謂如為一補特伽羅先讚布施，後還毀訾；如於布施，如是尸羅及一分修，當知亦爾。如是名為四種意趣。

Third, the intention of a different meaning — as when it is said, "Only one who has already served as many buddhas as there are grains of sand in the Ganges is able to understand the meaning of the Great Vehicle." Fourth, the intention accommodating an individual's own disposition — as when, for a single individual, generosity is first praised and then afterward criticized; and it should be understood that the same applies to moral discipline and to a portion of meditative cultivation as well. These are what are named the four kinds of intention.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Each intention answers a different kind of "how could the Buddha possibly have meant that literally?" puzzle. The intention of equality lets the Buddha speak of himself as a much earlier buddha because both share the same undifferentiated dharma-body — not a claim of literal reincarnation, but of shared identity at the level of ultimate truth. The intention of a different time is Vasubandhu's own favorite kind of example: someone who hears "recite this name and awakening is assured" might otherwise take it as an instant, magical guarantee, when the actual point is that the practice sets a process in motion toward a distant result — Vasubandhu compares it to the saying "one gold coin makes a thousand," which nobody takes to mean overnight profit, only that the first coin is the necessary seed of the later thousand. The intention of a different meaning uses an extreme number (buddhas as numerous as grains of sand in the Ganges) to signal that real understanding of emptiness requires accumulated merit and practice, not mere verbal comprehension of the words. And the intention accommodating disposition explains why the same teacher can praise generosity to a stingy student and later criticize excessive attachment to the very same practice in someone who has taken the first lesson too far — the apparent contradiction reflects a change in the student's needs, not a change in the truth being taught.

22. Four Kinds of Hidden Meaning

Chinese Source

四祕密者：一、令入祕密，謂聲聞乘中或大乘中，依世俗諦理說有補特伽羅及有諸法自性差別。二、相祕密，謂於是處說諸法相顯三自性。三、對治祕密，謂於是處說行對治八萬四千。

Ru-Yi Translation

The four hidden meanings are: first, the hidden meaning that leads one to enter the teaching — as when, in either the Śrāvaka Vehicle or the Great Vehicle, it is said, according to conventional truth, that there is a person, and that dharmas have their own differentiated natures. Second, the hidden meaning of characteristics — as when, in a given passage, the characteristics of dharmas are spoken of in a way that actually discloses the three natures. Third, the hidden meaning of counteraction — as when, in a given passage, the eighty-four thousand practices that serve as antidotes to the afflictions are spoken of.

四、轉變祕密，謂於是處以其別義，諸言諸字即顯別義。如有頌言：

「覺不堅為堅，　善住於顛倒，

極煩惱所惱，　得最上菩提。」

Fourth, the hidden meaning of transformation — as when, in a given passage, particular words and syllables, taken in a different sense, actually disclose a different meaning altogether. As there is a verse that says:

"Taking the unstable as stable, one dwells well within what is actually a distortion;

utterly wearied and afflicted by affliction, one attains the highest awakening."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

The four hidden meanings sort scriptural sayings by the specific interpretive move required to unlock them, rather than by content. The first, "leading one to enter," covers the entire everyday vocabulary of persons and things — a genuine concession to conventional truth, needed simply to get a listener into the teaching at all, since no one could follow a discourse conducted purely in terms of emptiness and no-self from the very first sentence. The second maps ordinary talk of "characteristics" directly onto the three natures already explained in this part. The third explains the famous, symbolically vast number "eighty-four thousand" teachings as referring to specific antidotes matched to specific afflictions, not a literal, exhaustive catalogue. The fourth and strangest is illustrated by the closing verse, which reads, on its surface, like nonsense or even praise of delusion — "taking the unstable as stable," "dwelling well in distortion" — until it is understood as deliberately coded language: taken in its intended sense, it actually describes the bodhisattva's own difficult, long labor through affliction toward awakening, using ordinary words in a transformed, technical sense that only makes sense once the code is recognized.

23. How to Compose a Mahāyāna Commentary: Dependent Origination and Characteristics

The treatise closes Part Three by turning reflexive: having spent the whole part explaining Mahāyāna doctrine, it now states its own method — the three things any commentary on Mahāyāna scripture ought to explain — and begins working through them.

Chinese Source

若有欲造大乘法釋，略由三相應造其釋：一者、由說緣起，二者、由說從緣所生法相，三者、由說語義。此中說緣起者，如說：

Ru-Yi Translation

If one wishes to compose a commentary on Mahāyāna scripture, one should, briefly, compose it by means of three approaches: first, by explaining dependent origination; second, by explaining the character of the dharmas arising from those conditions; third, by explaining the meaning of the words. As for explaining dependent origination, it is as this verse says:

「言熏習所生，　諸法，此從彼，

異熟與轉識，　更互為緣生。」

"Dharmas produced by the habituation of language — these arise from that;

the ripening consciousness and the actively-evolving consciousnesses generate one another, each in turn, as the other's condition."

復次，彼轉識相法，有相，有見，識為自性。又彼以依處為相，遍計所執為相，法性為相，由此顯示三自性相。如說：

Furthermore, the characteristic-bearing dharmas of those actively-evolving consciousnesses have both an image-aspect and a perceiving-aspect, consciousness alone being their nature. And they have, as their characteristic, the basis they depend on, what is discriminated and attached to, and the dharma-nature itself — and it is through this that the characteristics of the three natures are disclosed. As it is said:

「從有相，有見，　應知彼三相。」

"From having an image-aspect, and having a perceiving-aspect, those three characteristics should be understood."

復次，云何應釋彼相？謂遍計所執相，於依他起相中實無所有；圓成實相於中實有。由此二種非有及有，非得及得，未見已見真者同時。謂於依他起自性中，無遍計所執故，有圓成實故。於此轉時，若得彼即不得此，若得此即不得彼。如說：

Furthermore, how should these characteristics be explained? The discriminated, attached-to characteristic is, in truth, entirely absent within the dependent characteristic; the perfectly accomplished characteristic is, in truth, genuinely present within it. Because of this twofold absence and presence, this twofold not-attaining and attaining, the one who has not yet seen the truth and the one who has already seen it occur, in a sense, together at once. That is: within the dependent nature, the discriminated, attached-to is absent, while the perfectly accomplished is present. When this shift actually occurs, attaining the one means not attaining the other, and attaining the other means not attaining the one. As it is said:

「依他所執無，　成實於中有，

故得及不得，　其中二平等。」

"What is attached to, within the dependent, does not exist; the perfectly accomplished, within it, does exist —

therefore not-attaining and attaining are, in this, the two equally at once."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Asaṅga is here handing future commentators — including, in effect, everyone who will ever try to explain a Mahāyāna text after him — a working method built entirely from what this part has already established. First, ground the text in dependent origination: trace how the phenomena it describes arise from habituated seeds, in the constant mutual causality between the ripening consciousness and the active consciousnesses already proven in Part 2. Second, explain the characteristics involved using the three-natures framework just developed — the image-and-perceiver structure of consciousness, and how the discriminated, attached-to, and perfectly accomplished natures each show up within it. The closing point about "not-attaining and attaining occurring together" captures something genuinely subtle about the moment of insight itself: at the very instant a practitioner directly realizes the perfectly accomplished nature within the dependent flow, the discriminated, attached-to nature that used to be superimposed on that very same flow simultaneously drops away — gain and loss, in other words, are not two separate events in sequence, but two descriptions of one and the same moment of seeing clearly.

24. Explaining the Meaning of Words: A Buddha's Twenty-One Qualities

The third of the three approaches — explaining the meaning of words — is illustrated with two worked examples. The first takes a single phrase praising a buddha's "utterly pure awakening" and shows how an entire catalogue of twenty-one qualities is packed into that one opening phrase.

Chinese Source

說語義者，謂先說初句，後以餘句分別顯示。或由德處，或由義處。由德處者，謂說佛功德：最清淨覺，不二現行，趣無相法，住於佛住，逮得一切佛平等性，到無障處，不可轉法，所行無礙，其所安立不可思議，遊於三世平等法性，其身流布一切世界，於一切法智無疑滯，於一切行成就大覺，於諸法智無有疑惑，凡所現身不可分別，一切菩薩等所求智，得佛無二住勝彼岸，不相間雜如來解脫妙智究竟，證無中邊佛地平等，極於法界，盡虛空性，窮未來際。最清淨覺者，應知此句由所餘句分別顯示，如是乃成善說法性。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for explaining the meaning of words: one first states an opening phrase, and then uses the remaining phrases to differentiate and disclose its meaning — whether by way of qualities, or by way of practices. By way of qualities: this means speaking of the Buddha's own qualities, as in this passage: "the utterly pure awakening; manifesting without duality; proceeding toward the signless dharma; abiding in the abode of a buddha; having attained the equality of all buddhas; having reached the place beyond all obstruction; the dharma that cannot be turned back; operating without any impediment; establishing what is inconceivable; roaming through the equal dharma-nature of the three times; a body pervading every world-system; without any doubt regarding the wisdom of all dharmas; having accomplished great awakening with regard to every activity; without any uncertainty regarding the wisdom of all dharmas; manifesting bodies that cannot be differentiated one from another; the wisdom sought by all bodhisattvas alike; having attained the non-dual abiding of a buddha, reaching the further shore; the ultimate, unmingled, wondrous wisdom of the Tathāgata's liberation; realizing the equality of the buddha-ground, without center or edge; extending to the very limit of the dharma-realm, to the utter extent of space, to the farthest reach of future time." "The utterly pure awakening" — it should be understood that this opening phrase is differentiated and disclosed by all the remaining phrases; it is in just this way that the dharma-nature comes to be well explained.

最清淨覺者，謂佛世尊最清淨覺，應知是佛二十一種功德所攝。謂於所知一向無障轉功德，於有無無二相真如最勝清淨能入功德，無功用佛事不休息住功德，於法身中所依意樂作業無差別功德，修一切障對治功德，降伏一切外道功德，生在世間不為世法所礙功德，安立正法功德，授記功德，於一切世界示現受用變化身功德，斷疑功德，令入種種行功德，當來法生妙智功德，如其勝解示現功德，無量所依調伏有情加行功德，平等法身波羅蜜多成滿功德，隨其勝解示現差別佛土功德，三種佛身方處無分限功德，窮生死際常現利益安樂一切有情功德，無盡功德等。

"The utterly pure awakening" refers to the Buddha, the World-Honored One's own utterly pure awakening, and it should be understood as encompassed by the Buddha's twenty-one kinds of qualities: the quality of operating, with regard to the knowable, unfailingly without obstruction; the quality of entering the utterly excellent purity of suchness — the characteristic of neither existence nor nonexistence; the quality of abiding, without effort, unceasingly in buddha-activity; the quality of there being, within the dharma-body, no difference among its basis, its resolve, and its activity; the quality of having cultivated the antidote to every obstruction; the quality of subduing every non-Buddhist teacher; the quality of being born into the world yet not being hindered by worldly things; the quality of establishing the true Dharma; the quality of bestowing predictions of future buddhahood; the quality of manifesting the enjoyment body and the transformation body throughout every world; the quality of severing doubt; the quality of causing beings to enter manifold practices; the quality of the sublime wisdom that will arise regarding future dharmas; the quality of manifesting in accordance with each being's own resolve; the quality of taming sentient beings through countless supports; the quality of having brought the equal dharma-body's perfections to full completion; the quality of manifesting differentiated buddha-lands in accordance with each being's own resolve; the quality of the three buddha-bodies being without any limit of place; the quality of constantly manifesting benefit and happiness for all sentient beings to the very end of saṃsāra; and the quality of being inexhaustible, and so on.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu treats this as a worked demonstration of the method just announced: take a single dense phrase from a sūtra's opening praise of the Buddha, and show that it is not empty flattery but a compressed summary that a careful commentator can and should unpack, term by term. He glosses each of the twenty-one individually — for instance, "operating without obstruction with regard to the knowable" as omniscience with respect to every object of knowledge; "entering the purity of suchness, characterized by neither existence nor nonexistence" as direct realization of the very middle way between the two extremes explained throughout this part; "the dharma-body's basis, resolve, and activity being without difference" as the perfect unity, in a buddha, between what supports awakened action, what motivates it, and the action itself, where in an ordinary being these three often pull in different directions. Rather than reproduce each of the twenty-one glosses in full here, the pattern to notice is Asaṅga's underlying claim: praise of the Buddha in Mahāyāna scripture is not vague devotional language, but a technical map of exactly what full awakening consists of, and a properly trained commentator's task is to show precisely how each phrase of praise cashes out into a specific, definable quality.

25. Explaining the Meaning of Words: A Bodhisattva's Thirty-Two Practices and Sixteen Activities

The second worked example does the same for a definition of the bodhisattva: a single opening phrase — "arousing superior resolve for the benefit and happiness of all beings" — unpacked first into thirty-two defining practices, and then into sixteen underlying kinds of activity. This closes both the method and Part Three itself.

Chinese Source

復次，由義處者，如說：若諸菩薩成就三十二法，乃名菩薩。謂於一切有情起利益安樂增上意樂故：令入一切智智故，自知我今何假智故，摧伏慢故，堅牢勝意樂故，非假憐愍故，於親非親平等心故，永作善友乃至涅槃為後邊故，應量而語故，含笑先言故，無限大悲故，於所受事無退弱故，無厭倦意故，聞義無厭故，於自作罪深見過故，於他作罪不瞋而誨故，於一切威儀中恒修治菩提心故，不悕異熟而行施故，不依一切有趣受持戒故，於諸有情無有恚礙而行忍故，為欲攝受一切善法勤精進故，捨無色界修靜慮故，方便相應修般若故，由四攝事攝方便故，於持戒破戒善友無二故，以殷重心聽聞正法故，以殷重心住阿練若故，於世雜事不愛樂故，於下劣乘曾不欣樂故、於大乘中深見功德故，遠離惡友故，親近善友故，恒修治四梵住故，常遊戲五神通故、依趣智故，於住正行不住正行諸有情類不棄捨故，言決定故，重諦實故，大菩提心恒為首故。如是諸句，應知皆是初句差別；謂於一切有情起利益安樂增上意樂。

These are: arousing superior resolve for the benefit and happiness of all sentient beings; causing beings to enter the wisdom that knows all things in every mode; knowing oneself — "what wisdom, then, do I now truly possess?"; subduing conceit; steadfast, superior resolve; compassion that is never feigned; an even mind toward the intimate and the unintimate alike; remaining a true friend permanently, even to the very limit of nirvāṇa; speaking only what is fitting; always speaking first, with a smile; boundless great compassion; never faltering in what one has undertaken; never feeling weariness or fatigue; never tiring of hearing the meaning of the teaching; seeing deeply into one's own faults when one has done wrong; instructing, without anger, one who has done wrong; constantly cultivating and refining the mind of awakening in every posture and activity; All these phrases, it should be understood, are differentiations of the opening phrase — arousing superior resolve for the benefit and happiness of all sentient beings.

This phrase, "superior resolve for benefit and happiness," has, it should be understood, sixteen distinct kinds of activity: first, the activity of gradually advancing effort; second, the activity free of distortion; third, the activity of spontaneous effort, not waiting to be asked by another; fourth, the activity that is never shaken or destroyed; fifth, the activity free of desire and defilement, with three further distinctions — being free of the bondage of attachment, being without love or hatred toward those who have shown kindness or unkindness, and continuing on, unwaveringly, through birth after birth; sixth, the activity of speech and body that fit the occasion, with two further distinctions; seventh, the activity of equanimity toward pleasure, toward pain, and toward what is neither; eighth, the activity free of anything base; ninth, the activity free of any retreat or reversal; tenth, the activity of gathering in through skillful means; eleventh, the activity of being wearied by what is to be counteracted, with two further distinctions; twelfth, the activity of uninterrupted attention; thirteenth, the activity of superior, advancing practice, with seven further distinctions — the actual practice of the six perfections and of the four means of attraction; fourteenth, the activity of effort brought to full completion, with six further distinctions — drawing near to good teachers, listening to the true Dharma, dwelling in the forest, abandoning unwholesome thoughts, attending to good qualities (itself further twofold), and the good qualities of one's companions (itself further twofold); fifteenth, the activity of accomplishment brought to completion, with three further distinctions — immeasurable purity, the attainment of great power, and the realization of buddha-qualities; sixteenth, the activity of establishing that accomplishment in others, with four further distinctions — skillfully guiding an assembly, instructing and admonishing with unwavering certainty, unifying the gathering-in of wealth and of Dharma, and a mind free of any defiled admixture. All these phrases, it should be understood, are differentiations of the opening phrase.

如說：

「由最初句故，　句別德種類；

由最初句故，　句別義差別。」

As it is said:

"Because of the very first phrase, the remaining phrases differentiate its kinds of virtue;

because of the very first phrase, the remaining phrases differentiate its distinctions of meaning."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

The structure mirrors the previous example exactly, now applied to the bodhisattva rather than the buddha: one opening phrase — the arousing of superior resolve for the benefit and happiness of all beings — is treated as containing the whole of bodhisattva practice in seed form, and the remaining thirty-one items are Asaṅga's own demonstration that every other bodhisattva quality (patience, generosity, freedom from a lesser vehicle's aims, constant placing of bodhicitta first, and so on) can be shown to be a specific expression of that one root resolve, not an independent virtue alongside it. The sixteen-activity breakdown that follows goes one level deeper still, analyzing the psychological and practical mechanics of how that resolve actually operates moment to moment — gradually, without distortion, spontaneously, unshakably, free of any subtle self-interest, and so on through to the final activity of establishing the same accomplishment in others. Vasubandhu treats the closing couplet as the whole method's own one-sentence summary: a single well-chosen opening phrase, properly unpacked, discloses both every specific virtue it contains and every specific distinction of meaning within it — which is exactly the reading practice this entire third part of the treatise has just modeled, phrase by phrase, from the ten kinds of consciousness at its start to the thirty-two bodhisattva dharmas at its close.
