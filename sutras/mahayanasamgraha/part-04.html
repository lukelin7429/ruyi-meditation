Entering the Character of the Knowable

Part Three explained the three natures; Part Four explains how a bodhisattva actually comes to realize them directly. This is the treatise's account of the path itself in its decisive early stages: who is ready, what tempers the mind against discouragement, the four investigations and four kinds of thorough knowledge that dismantle the sense of a fixed object, the famous rope-mistaken-for-a-snake simile, and the moment of breakthrough into the path of seeing — the first of the ten bodhisattva grounds.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 4 of 11 complete Source: Taishō T31, no. 1594, 入所知相分第四 Commentary guide: Vasubandhu's 攝大乘論釋 (T1597)

Translation Method

As in Parts 1 through 3, the Chinese source is Xuánzàng's translation of Asaṅga's root text (T1594), read with Vasubandhu's commentary (T1597), also translated by Xuánzàng. This part's core vocabulary is shared with the site's Cheng Weishi Lun pages, particularly around the path structure (the stage of preparatory effort, the path of seeing, the path of cultivation) and the four concentrations of the aids to penetration. See also this site's note on translation method.

1. What Is "Entering the Character of the Knowable"?

Chinese Source

如是已說所知相，入所知相云何應見？多聞熏習所依，非阿賴耶識所攝，如阿賴耶識成種子；如理作意所攝似法似義而生似所取事有見意言。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus spoken of the character of the knowable, how should entering that character be seen? It is the support of hearing-habituation, not included within the ālayavijñāna, functioning as its seed in the same way the ālayavijñāna functions as a seed for defiled dharmas; included within correct attention, it gives rise to what seems to be dharmas and seems to be their meaning, and produces mental discourse that has the aspect of an image, seeming to take hold of an object.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

This opens by drawing directly on the "hearing-habituation" (聞熏習) introduced back in Part 2 as the seed of the dharma-body, distinct from the ālayavijñāna's own defiled continuity. Just as the ālayavijñāna serves as the seed from which defiled experience continually arises, hearing-habituation — built up by listening to and reflecting on the Great Vehicle's teaching — serves as the seed from which correct mental discourse about the Dharma arises, complete with its own image-aspect that seems, at this stage, still to be grasping an object. "Mental discourse" (意言, roughly "inner speech") names the conceptual, verbal-feeling activity of thinking through the teaching before it deepens into direct, wordless realization — the entire process this part goes on to trace.

2. Who Is Ready to Enter?

Chinese Source

此中誰能悟入所應知相？大乘多聞熏習相續，已得逢事無量諸佛出現於世，已得一向決定勝解，已善積集諸善根故，善備福智資糧菩薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, who is able to awaken to and enter the character of the knowable? A bodhisattva who has sustained a continuum of Great Vehicle hearing-habituation, who has already had the good fortune to serve countless buddhas appearing in the world, who has already gained wholly resolute conviction, and who, having well accumulated wholesome roots, has thereby well provisioned the twin resources of merit and wisdom.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu is careful to distinguish this from any single dramatic qualification: it is a sustained history — a long continuum of hearing and reflecting on Mahāyāna teaching specifically (not just any teaching), long service to many buddhas, and conviction so settled that "bad companions" cannot shake it. He also traces a causal chain behind the phrase "well provisioned with merit and wisdom": the power of one's causes, the power of good friends, the power of one's own resolve, and the power of sustained support each build on the last, so that readiness to enter this path is itself the fruit of a long, gradual process rather than a sudden qualification appearing from nowhere.

3. Where Does Entry Actually Happen?

Chinese Source

何處能入？謂即於彼有見似法似義意言，大乘法相等所生起；勝解行地，見道，修道，究竟道中，於一切法唯有識性，隨聞勝解故，如理通達故，治一切障故，離一切障故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Where does entry happen? It happens precisely within that mental discourse — bearing the image-aspect, seeming to be dharmas, seeming to be their meaning — which arises from the character of the Great Vehicle's own teaching. It happens in the stage of resolute conduct, in the path of seeing, in the path of cultivation, and in the ultimate path, with regard to the nature of consciousness-only in all dharmas: through resolute conviction upon hearing it, through correctly penetrating it, through counteracting every obstruction, and through being free of every obstruction.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu maps the four clauses directly onto four successive stages of the path: at the stage of resolute conduct — the long preparatory stage before direct realization — one enters simply through deep, settled conviction upon hearing that all is consciousness-only, without yet directly seeing it; at the path of seeing, one enters by correctly and directly penetrating this mental discourse, seeing that it is neither dharma nor object, neither what grasps nor what is grasped; at the path of cultivation, one enters by using that same seeing to counteract every remaining obstruction over repeated practice; and at the ultimate path — full buddhahood — one enters completely, since even the subtlest obstruction is by then entirely gone. The same single teaching, in other words, is engaged with differently at each stage of the path, deepening from conviction to direct insight to complete freedom.

4. Tempering the Mind Against Discouragement

Before describing the actual mechanics of realization, the treatise pauses on something practical: the sheer scale of the bodhisattva path can be discouraging, and three specific reflections are offered to temper the mind against giving up.

Chinese Source

由何能入？由善根力所住持故，謂三種相練磨心故，斷四處故，緣法義境止觀恒常殷重加行無放逸故。

Ru-Yi Translation

By what power does entry happen? By being sustained through the power of wholesome roots — that is, by tempering the mind through three reflections, by cutting off four obstacles, and by diligent, unfailingly earnest, ceaseless practice of calming and insight directed toward the meaning of the Dharma.

無量諸世界，無量人有情，剎那剎那證覺無上正等菩提，是為第一練磨其心。由此意樂，能行施等波羅蜜多；我已獲得如是意樂，我由此故少用功力修習施等波羅蜜多，當得圓滿，是為第二練磨其心。若有成就諸有障善，於命終時即便可愛一切自體圓滿而生；我有妙善無障礙善，云何爾時不當獲得一切圓滿，是名第三練磨其心。

In countless world-systems, countless human beings, moment after moment, are realizing unsurpassed, perfect awakening — this is the first tempering of the mind. Through this same resolve, one is able to practice generosity and the other perfections; since I myself have already gained this very resolve, I need only exert modest effort in cultivating generosity and the rest to bring them to full completion — this is the second tempering of the mind. If those who have accomplished only an obstructed goodness are, at the moment of death, reborn with every desirable bodily perfection they could wish for, then, having a wondrous, unobstructed goodness of my own, how could I fail, at that time, to attain complete fulfillment? — this is the third tempering of the mind.

此中有頌：

「人趣諸有情，　處數皆無量，

念念證等覺，　故不應退屈。

諸淨心意樂，　能修行施等，

此勝者已得，　故能修施等。

善者於死時，　得隨樂自滿，

勝善由永斷，　圓滿云何無？」

Here there is a verse:

"Among the beings in the human realm, the places and numbers are both immeasurable;

moment after moment, awakening is realized — therefore one should not be discouraged.

It is by pure resolve that generosity and the rest can be practiced;

this the excellent ones have already attained, and so they are able to practice generosity and the rest.

The virtuous, at the moment of death, attain fulfillment according to their wish;

how, then, could there fail to be fulfillment through the excellent goodness that comes from permanently cutting off the obstructions?"

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu names the discouragement each reflection specifically counters. The first answers despair at the sheer scale of full awakening: if it seems too vast and distant a goal, remember that it is, in fact, being achieved constantly, by countless beings, this very moment — so there is nothing uniquely impossible about it. The second answers despair at the perfections themselves — generosity, patience, and the rest — seeming too difficult to perfect: a bodhisattva who has already gained genuine confidence and aspiration toward them needs comparatively little further effort to bring them to completion, the hard psychological work being the aspiration itself. The third addresses a subtler worry: if even ordinary, "obstructed" goodness (worldly virtue still mixed with attachment) reliably produces excellent rebirths, then goodness that has been made genuinely unobstructed — free of the very afflictions that limit ordinary virtue — could hardly fail to produce the ultimate fulfillment of buddhahood.

5. Cutting Off Four Obstacles

Chinese Source

由離聲聞獨覺作意，斷作意故；由於大乘諸疑離疑，以能永斷異慧疑故；由離所聞所思法中我我所執，斷法執故；由於現前現住安立一切相中無所作意無所分別，斷分別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

By setting aside the intentions of the Śrāvaka and Solitary-Buddha vehicles, the obstacle of intention is cut off. By setting aside doubts concerning the Great Vehicle, permanently cutting off distorted views and doubt, the obstacle of doubt is cut off. By setting aside the grasping at a self and what belongs to a self within dharmas that have been heard and reflected upon, the obstacle of grasping at dharmas is cut off. By having no intention and no discrimination whatsoever with regard to whatever characteristics presently and continuously appear, the obstacle of discrimination is cut off.

此中有頌：

「現前自然住，　安立一切相，

智者不分別，　得最上菩提。」

Here there is a verse:

"Abiding of its own accord in what is presently established — whatever characteristics appear —

the wise one does not discriminate, and thereby attains the highest awakening."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

The four obstacles form a rough sequence, from coarse to subtle. Setting aside the Śrāvaka and Solitary-Buddha vehicles' own aims means genuinely committing to full buddhahood rather than merely personal liberation. Cutting off doubt about the Great Vehicle means being able to hold the seemingly contradictory language explored throughout Part 3 — dharmas described as without any self-nature yet also as illusion-like, dependent, and ultimately real — without wavering, once it is understood through the three natures. Cutting off grasping at dharmas addresses a subtler trap: even a practitioner who has abandoned self-grasping can still unconsciously treat the teachings themselves, or the dharmas described in scripture, as fixed, independently real things — which this obstacle removes. And the fourth, cutting off discrimination itself, is the most refined: even correct conceptual understanding still involves subtle mental labeling and categorizing, and full entry requires letting go of that activity as well, resting instead in bare, undistorted presence with whatever characteristics appear.

6. By What Means, and How? The Four Investigations and Four Kinds of Thorough Knowledge

Having described what tempers and clears the mind, the treatise now gives the actual technique: two paired sets of four, first investigating whether names and objects are real, then thoroughly knowing that they are not.

Chinese Source

由何云何而得悟入？由聞熏習種類如理作意所攝似法似義有見意言；由四尋思，謂由名、義、自性、差別假立尋思；及由四種如實遍智，謂由名、事、自性、差別假立如實遍智，如是皆同不可得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

By what means, and how, is entry actually accomplished? By the kind of mental discourse — included within correct attention, seeming to be dharmas, seeming to be their meaning — that arises from hearing-habituation; by the four investigations, namely the investigation of the provisional positing of a name, a meaning, an own-nature, and a differentiating quality; and by the four kinds of thorough knowledge of things as they really are, namely the thorough knowledge that the provisional positing of a name, a basis, an own-nature, and a differentiating quality are, in every case, equally unfindable.

以諸菩薩如是如實為入唯識勤修加行，即於似文似義意言，推求文名唯是意言，推求依此文名之義亦唯意言，推求名義自性差別唯是假立。若時證得唯有意言，爾時證知若名，若義，自性，差別皆是假立，自性差別義相無故，同不可得。由四尋思及由四種如實遍智，於此似文似義意言，便能悟入唯有識性。

Bodhisattvas, diligently practicing in just this way, genuinely and truthfully, in order to enter Consciousness-Only, investigate this mental discourse that seems to be text and seems to be meaning: they investigate that the text and the name are nothing but mental discourse; they investigate that the meaning depending on this text and name is likewise nothing but mental discourse; and they investigate that the own-nature and differentiating quality of the name and its meaning are merely provisionally posited. When mental discourse alone is realized as all there is, it is thereupon realized that the name, the meaning, the own-nature, and the differentiating quality are all merely provisionally posited, since their own-nature and differentiating quality, and the meaning of that quality, have no [real] mark and are, in every case, equally unfindable. By the four investigations and the four kinds of thorough knowledge of things as they really are, one is thereby able to enter, through this mental discourse that seems to be text and seems to be meaning, into the nature of consciousness-only alone.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

The four investigations and four kinds of thorough knowledge form a deliberate two-stage process, not four separate techniques. First, a practitioner investigates — questions and probes, without yet a settled conclusion — whether a name genuinely matches a real object's essential nature, whether a name genuinely matches its qualities, and so on across all four combinations. Investigation deepens into thorough knowledge once the answer is not merely suspected but directly, confidently realized: in every one of the four cases, nothing real underlies the name at all — it is entirely a provisional label. Vasubandhu is careful to specify the actual sequence: first one sees that the text (the word itself) is nothing but mental discourse; then that the meaning attached to that text is likewise nothing but mental discourse; only once both of these are seen does the deeper realization follow — that this "mental discourse" itself, the very medium of the investigation, is all there ultimately is. This is the technical machinery underlying what the site's Cheng Weishi Lun pages describe more briefly as realizing that "what is grasped is nothing but consciousness."

7. What Is Entered, and How? The Rope Mistaken for a Snake

The treatise now offers what has become one of Yogācāra's most famous images: a rope glimpsed in the dark and mistaken for a snake, used to show precisely how the three natures are entered one after another.

Chinese Source

於此悟入唯識性中，何所悟入？如何悟入？入唯識性，相見二性，及種種性：若名，若義，自性，差別假，自性差別義，如是六種義皆無故；所取能取性現前故；一時現似種種相義而生起故。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this entry into the nature of consciousness-only, what is entered? And how is it entered? What is entered into is the nature of consciousness-only — the twofold nature of image and perceiver, and its manifold nature — since these six things, name, meaning, own-nature, differentiating quality, the provisional positing itself, and the meaning of own-nature and differentiating quality, are all nonexistent; since the nature of what is grasped and what grasps is nonetheless [still] present before one; and since, all at once, manifold characteristics and meanings arise, appearing as if real.

如闇中繩顯現似蛇，譬如繩上蛇非真實，以無有故。若已了知彼義無者，蛇覺雖滅，繩覺猶在。若以微細品類分析，此又虛妄，色香味觸為其相故，此覺為依繩覺當滅。如是於彼似文似義六相意言，伏除非實六相義時，唯識性覺猶如蛇覺亦當除遣，由圓成實自性覺故。

It is like a rope in the dark, appearing as if it were a snake. Just as the snake seen upon the rope is not real, since it does not actually exist, once one has understood that this snake is nonexistent, the cognition of the snake is dispelled, though the cognition of the rope still remains. But if the rope, too, is analyzed down into its most subtle constituents, it is likewise found to be an unreal construction, since it is really nothing but color, smell, taste, and touch serving as its own characteristics — and once this is seen, the cognition of the rope, having lost its basis, is dispelled as well. In just this way, once the mental discourse bearing the six unreal characteristics — seeming to be text and seeming to be meaning — has had those six unreal characteristics subdued and removed, the very cognition of "consciousness-only," like the cognition of the rope, must also, in the end, be set aside — for the cognition of the perfectly accomplished nature itself then arises in its place.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu reads the simile as a precise three-stage map of the three natures being entered one after another. Seeing that the "snake" was never there at all corresponds to seeing through the discriminated, attached-to nature: an object that seemed vividly present turns out to have no reality whatsoever. But something still remains once the illusion is dispelled — the rope itself, still cognized as a rope — and this corresponds to the dependent nature: real, conditioned, but itself still a construction built from more basic elements (its color, texture, and so on), just as "consciousness-only" is itself still a conceptual formulation, useful but not yet the final resting point. Only when even the rope is analyzed down to its ultimate constituents, and the cognition of "rope" itself is let go of, does something remain that need not itself be dissolved further — and this, Vasubandhu says, corresponds to the perfectly accomplished nature: even the thought "there is only consciousness" must eventually be released, not because it is false, but because it is still a conceptual formulation standing between the practitioner and the direct, non-conceptual realization the whole path is aimed at.

8. Entering the Perfectly Accomplished Nature

Chinese Source

如是菩薩悟入意言似義相故悟入遍計所執性，悟入唯識故悟入依他起性，云何悟入圓成實性？若已滅除意言聞法熏習種類唯識之想，爾時菩薩已遣義想，一切似義無容得生，故似唯識亦不得生；由是因緣，住一切義無分別名，於法界中便得現見相應而住。爾時，菩薩平等平等所緣能緣無分別智已得生起，由此菩薩名已悟入圓成實性。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, a bodhisattva, by awakening to the fact that mental discourse merely seems to have meaning, thereby awakens to and enters the discriminated, attached-to nature; and by awakening to consciousness-only, thereby awakens to and enters the dependent nature. How, then, does one awaken to and enter the perfectly accomplished nature? Once the very notion of "consciousness-only," born from the hearing-habituation underlying mental discourse, has itself been extinguished, the bodhisattva has, at that point, relinquished even the notion of an object — no seeming object whatsoever can any longer arise, and so the seeming of "consciousness-only" cannot arise either. By this cause and condition, abiding without any discriminating name for any object at all, one comes, within the dharma-realm, to dwell in direct, corresponding sight of it. At that moment, the bodhisattva's non-discriminating wisdom — in which the object known and the knowing itself are perfectly, equally alike — has arisen; and it is by this that the bodhisattva is named one who has awakened to and entered the perfectly accomplished nature.

此中有頌：

「法，補特伽羅，　法，義，略，廣，性，

不淨，淨，究竟，　名所行差別。」

Here there is a verse:

"Dharma, person, dharma again, meaning, the concise, the extended, essential nature,

the impure, the pure, and the ultimate — these are the distinctions among the names a bodhisattva engages."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

This passage completes the sequence begun with the rope-and-snake simile: even the correct, sophisticated insight "there is only consciousness" must ultimately be relinquished, not retained as a final resting formula, since it remains a name, a discrimination, and the goal is freedom from discrimination as such. What replaces it is direct, non-conceptual contact with the dharma-realm itself — described here as a state in which "the object known and the knowing are perfectly, equally alike," meaning that the usual gap between a knower and a known object has genuinely closed, rather than one side simply becoming more accurate about the other. The verse's ten-item list of "names" — Vasubandhu explains it fully — surveys every kind of naming a bodhisattva must eventually see through: individual dharmas, persons, categories of scripture, their meanings, concise and extended teachings, and finally the ultimate object shared by both fundamental and post-attainment wisdom — the true nature common to all dharmas alike.

9. This Is the Path of Seeing

Chinese Source

如是菩薩悟入唯識性故，悟入所知相；悟入此故，入極喜地，善達法界，生如來家，得一切有情平等心性，得一切菩薩平等心性，得一切佛平等心性，此即名為菩薩見道。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, by awakening to and entering the nature of consciousness-only, a bodhisattva awakens to and enters the character of the knowable; and by entering this, one enters the Ground of Utmost Joy, thoroughly penetrates the dharma-realm, is born into the family of the Tathāgatas, attains a mind equal to that of all sentient beings, attains a mind equal to that of all bodhisattvas, and attains a mind equal to that of all buddhas. This is what is named a bodhisattva's path of seeing.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

This is the treatise's own account of the single most decisive moment on the entire bodhisattva path: the transition from an ordinary being to a noble one, entering the first of the ten grounds. "Born into the family of the Tathāgatas" means the lineage of buddhahood can never again be broken in this practitioner. The three kinds of "equal mind" that follow are, for Vasubandhu, three aspects of one realization: equality with all sentient beings, because one now genuinely wishes to be free of suffering exactly as much for others as for oneself; equality with all bodhisattvas, because one now shares their basic resolve and practice; and equality with all buddhas, because one has, in this moment, directly touched the same dharma-body every buddha has realized — different only in degree of completeness, not in kind.

10. Why Enter the Nature of Consciousness-Only? The Illusionist Simile

Chinese Source

復次，為何義故入唯識性？由緣總法出世止觀智故，由此後得種種相識智故。為斷及相阿賴耶識諸相種子，為長能觸法身種子，為轉所依，為欲證得一切佛法，為欲證得一切智智入唯識性。又後得智於一切阿賴耶識所生一切了別相中，見如幻等性無倒轉，是故菩薩譬如幻師，於所幻事，於諸相中及說因果，常無顛倒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, for what purpose does one enter the nature of consciousness-only? Because of the transcendent wisdom of calming and insight taking the general characteristic of all dharmas as its object; and because, through this, the subsequently-attained wisdom of manifold discerning characteristics [also arises]. One enters the nature of consciousness-only in order to cut off the seeds of the ālayavijñāna's characteristics, together with their cause; in order to nurture the seed capable of touching the dharma-body; in order to bring about the transformation of the basis; in order to realize every buddha-dharma; and in order to realize the wisdom that knows all things in every mode. Moreover, the subsequently-attained wisdom, with regard to every discerned characteristic arising from the ālayavijñāna, sees its nature as being like an illusion and the rest, without any distortion. For this reason, a bodhisattva, engaging with illusion-created things, their characteristics, and the explanation of their causes and effects, is, like a skilled illusionist, never once distorted in doing so.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu treats this passage as clarifying why entering consciousness-only matters practically, beyond the moment of insight itself: fundamental, non-discriminating wisdom alone cannot describe or teach anything, since it involves no discrimination whatsoever — it simply rests in direct seeing. A second kind of wisdom, "subsequently attained" once the meditator returns to active engagement with the world, is therefore also needed, and it is this second wisdom that lets a bodhisattva teach, reason about causes and effects, and act skillfully in the world without being fooled by appearances. The illusionist simile makes the point vivid: a skilled magician who has created an illusory elephant is never confused by his own illusion — he can describe it, explain how he produced it, and interact with an audience about it, all without mistaking it for a real elephant. A bodhisattva's post-attainment wisdom works the same way toward every appearance the ālayavijñāna produces: fully engaged, accurately described, never mistaken for independently real.

11. Four Concentrations at the Threshold of Breakthrough

Chinese Source

於此悟入唯識性時，有四種三摩地，是四種順決擇分依止。云何應知？應知由四尋思，於下品無義忍中，有明得三摩地，是暖順決擇分依止。於上品無義忍中，有明增三摩地，是頂順決擇分依止。復由四種如實遍智，已入唯識，於無義中已得決定，有入真義一分三摩地，是諦順忍依止。從此無間伏唯識想，有無間三摩地，是世第一法依止。應知如是諸三摩地，是現觀邊。

Ru-Yi Translation

At this moment of entering the nature of consciousness-only, there are four kinds of meditative absorption, which serve as the support for the four aids to penetration. How should this be understood? Through the four investigations, within a lesser degree of acceptance that there is no object, there is the absorption of "attained radiance," the support for the aid called "heat." Within a greater degree of acceptance that there is no object, there is the absorption of "increased radiance," the support for the aid called "summit." Further, through the four kinds of thorough knowledge of things as they really are, having already entered consciousness-only and gained certainty that there is no object, there is the absorption of "a portion of entry into the real meaning," the support for the aid called "acceptance in conformity." Immediately following this, subduing even the notion of consciousness-only itself, there is the "uninterrupted" absorption, the support for the aid called "the highest worldly dharma." These absorptions, it should be understood, stand at the very threshold of direct realization.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

These four — heat, summit, acceptance, and the highest worldly dharma — are the classical "four aids to penetration" (順決擇分, nirvedhabhāgīya) shared across Buddhist Abhidharma traditions, here mapped precisely onto the four investigations and four kinds of thorough knowledge just described. "Heat" and "summit" correspond to weaker and stronger degrees of provisional acceptance that no object really exists, gained through investigation alone; "acceptance in conformity" marks the point where this is no longer merely accepted but genuinely, confidently known, through the completed four kinds of thorough knowledge; and "the highest worldly dharma" is the final instant before breakthrough, in which even the residual notion of "consciousness-only" is itself relinquished — the very last worldly moment before the path of seeing (described in section 9 above) actually opens. This four-stage scheme, shared with the site's Cheng Weishi Lun pages, names the precise psychological terrain a practitioner crosses in the moments immediately preceding the first genuine breakthrough into direct insight.

12. The Path of Cultivation

Chinese Source

如是菩薩已入於地，已得見道，已入唯識，於修道中云何修行？於如所說安立十地，攝一切經皆現前中，由緣總法出世後得止觀智故，經於無量百千俱胝那庾多劫數修習故，而得轉依。為欲證得三種佛身，精勤修行。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bodhisattva who has thus entered the grounds, attained the path of seeing, and entered consciousness-only — how does one then practice within the path of cultivation? Within the ten grounds as they have been laid out, in which every scripture's meaning becomes directly present, one attains the transformation of the basis through the transcendent and subsequently-attained wisdom of calming and insight, taking the general characteristic of dharmas as its object, cultivated over countless hundreds of thousands of koṭis of nayutas of eons. One diligently practices in order to realize the three bodies of a buddha.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Having described the breakthrough moment in detail, the treatise moves quickly over what comes after it — the vast span of the path of cultivation, carried out across the ten bodhisattva grounds over an almost inconceivable length of time (a koṭi is traditionally ten million; a nayuta, a hundred thousand koṭis — the text is deliberately naming a number too large to hold in mind). Vasubandhu's gloss stays practical: the same two kinds of wisdom already introduced — non-discriminating wisdom directly realizing the general nature shared by all dharmas, and subsequently-attained wisdom skillfully engaging particular things — are simply exercised over and over, ground after ground, until the transformation of the basis (first explained in Part 2) is finally complete and the three bodies of buddhahood, the subject of Part 11, are fully realized.

13. Eleven Ways a Bodhisattva's Realization Differs from a Śrāvaka's

Chinese Source

聲聞現觀，菩薩現觀，有何差別？謂菩薩現觀與聲聞異，由十一種差別應知：一、由所緣差別，以大乘法為所緣故。二、由資持差別，以大福智二種資糧為資持故。三、由通達差別，以能通達補特伽羅法無我故。四、由涅槃差別，攝受無住大涅槃故。五、由地差別，依於十地而出離故。六、七、由清淨差別，斷煩惱習，淨佛土故。八、由於自他得平等心差別，成熟有情加行無休息故。九、由生差別，生如來家故。十、由受生差別，常於諸佛大集會中攝受生故。十一、由果差別，十力，無畏，不共佛法無量功德果成滿故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What difference is there between a śrāvaka's direct realization and a bodhisattva's? A bodhisattva's direct realization differs from a śrāvaka's in eleven ways: first, in its object, taking Great Vehicle teaching as its object; second, in its support, being supported by the twin resources of great merit and wisdom; third, in its penetration, being able to penetrate the selflessness of both persons and dharmas; fourth, in nirvāṇa, embracing the great, non-abiding nirvāṇa; fifth, in ground, going forth in dependence on the ten grounds; sixth and seventh, in purification, severing the afflictions' habitual traces and purifying buddha-lands; eighth, in gaining an equal mind toward self and other, tirelessly engaging in bringing sentient beings to maturity; ninth, in birth, being born into the family of the Tathāgatas; tenth, in the manner of receiving rebirth, constantly being received into rebirth within the great assemblies of all buddhas; and eleventh, in fruit, bringing to full completion the immeasurable fruit of the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, and the unique buddha-qualities.

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu treats this list as a direct answer to a natural objection: if śrāvakas also directly realize truth and attain liberation, in what sense is a bodhisattva's realization actually different, rather than merely differently motivated? His answer, item by item, is that the difference runs through nearly every dimension of the realization itself, not just the practitioner's stated aim — a wider object of realization (both kinds of selflessness, not only personal selflessness), a different destination (non-abiding nirvāṇa rather than final cessation), a structured ground-by-ground path with no real śrāvaka parallel, and a fruit — the ten powers, fearlessnesses, and unique qualities of a buddha — that a śrāvaka's own attainment simply does not include. The bodhisattva path, on this reading, is not simply the śrāvaka path pursued with a bigger heart, but a structurally different realization from the very object it takes as its starting point.

14. Two More Verses on Entering the Three Natures

Chinese Source

此中有二頌：

「名事互為客，　其性應尋思，

於二亦當推，　唯量及唯假。

實智觀無義，　唯有分別三。

彼無故此無，　是即入三性。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Here there are two verses:

"Name and thing are each, in turn, incidental to the other — their own-nature should be investigated;

with regard to both, one should further probe: there is only measure, and only provisional positing.

True wisdom observes that there is no object — there are only these three kinds of discrimination.

Because that is absent, this too is absent — this, precisely, is entry into the three natures."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu reads these two verses as a compact restatement of the entire path just described. "Name and thing are each incidental to the other" restates why neither can be the other's fixed essence, the same argument given in Part 3 for why the discriminated nature only seems to match a real substance. "There is only measure, and only provisional positing" names the dependent nature — real only as a functioning process of cognition ("measure"), with own-nature and qualities that are merely provisionally assigned labels. And "true wisdom observes that there is no object — there are only three kinds of discrimination" (discrimination of the name, of the own-nature, and of the differentiating quality) restates the entry into the perfectly accomplished nature: once even that threefold discrimination is seen to have nothing behind it, all three natures have been entered in turn — seeing through the discriminated (there is no object), understanding the dependent (there is only functioning discrimination), and finally not even holding onto that understanding as itself a further discrimination (the perfectly accomplished).

15. Two Instructional Verses from the Madhyānta-vibhāga

Chinese Source

復有教授二頌，如《分別瑜伽論》說：

「菩薩於定位，　觀影唯是心。

義相既滅除，　審觀唯自想。

如是住內心，　知所取非有，

次能取亦無，　後觸無所得。」

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, two instructional verses, as the Madhyānta-vibhāga says:

"A bodhisattva, in meditative absorption, contemplates that the reflected image is nothing but mind.

Once the appearance of an object has been extinguished, one carefully observes that it was only one's own conception all along.

Abiding thus within the mind itself, one knows that what is grasped does not exist;

next, that what grasps, too, is absent; and afterward, one touches [reality] and finds nothing further to attain."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

These two verses compress the whole path into four brief steps, drawn from a companion Yogācāra treatise the site translates elsewhere as the Madhyānta-vibhāga. First, a meditator recognizes that whatever image appears in meditation is simply mind itself, not some external object being reflected. Second, once the sense of an external object has genuinely dropped away, what remains is recognized as one's own conceiving activity, nothing more. Third — the step matching the rope-and-snake simile's second stage — the grasped object is seen to be absent, and then, further, the grasping mind itself is seen to be equally without independent existence, since "grasper" and "grasped" only ever made sense as a pair. And fourth, once both sides of that pair have dissolved, what is left is direct, non-conceptual contact with reality — described here, tersely, as "finding nothing further to attain," since there is no longer any gap between a knower and a known object left to close.

16. Five Verses on the Five Stages of Realization

Part Four closes with five further verses, drawn from the Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra, that Vasubandhu reads as mapping the entire path — from the earliest accumulation of merit to the final attainment of buddhahood — onto five successive stages.

Chinese Source

復有別五現觀伽他，如《大乘經莊嚴論》說：

「福德智慧二資糧，　菩薩善備無邊際，

於法思量善決已，　故了義趣唯言類。

若知諸義唯是言，　即住似彼唯心理，

便能現證真法界，　是故二相悉蠲除。

體知離心無別物，　由此即會心非有，

智者了達二皆無，　等住二無真法界。

慧者無分別智力，　周遍平等常順行，

滅依榛梗過失聚，　如大良藥銷眾毒。

佛說妙法善成立，　安慧并根法界中，

了知念趣唯分別，　勇猛疾歸德海岸。」

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, five distinct verses on direct realization, as the Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra says:

"The two provisions of merit and wisdom, a bodhisattva well provisions without end;

having well determined the Dharma through deliberation, one thereby understands that its meaning proceeds only from mental discourse.

Once one knows that all meanings are nothing but mental discourse, one abides in the principle that all is, in this way, mind alone;

one thereby becomes able to directly realize the true dharma-realm — and so the two characteristics are entirely swept away.

Understanding directly that, apart from mind, there is no separate thing, one thereby comes to know that mind, too, does not exist;

the wise, having penetrated that both are absent, abide equally within the true dharma-realm devoid of both.

The wise one's power of non-discriminating wisdom proceeds everywhere, constantly, in accord with equality;

it eliminates the whole thicket-like mass of faults it depends upon, like a great medicine dissolving every poison.

The Buddha's wondrous Dharma, well established, settles wisdom together with its root within the dharma-realm;

knowing that engaged mindfulness is itself nothing but discrimination, one swiftly and courageously returns to the shore of the ocean of virtue."

Reading note — after Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu reads these five verses as tracing the whole path in miniature, one verse per stage: the first describes the path of accumulation, where merit and wisdom are gathered without yet any direct breakthrough; the first half of the second verse describes the path of preparatory effort, where deliberate reflection brings the practitioner to the edge of realizing that meaning itself is only mental discourse; the second half of that verse, together with the moment both characteristics — the grasped and the grasper — are "swept away," describes the path of seeing already detailed earlier in this part; the fourth verse, with its image of non-discriminating wisdom flowing everywhere like medicine dissolving poison, describes the long path of cultivation across the ten grounds; and the fifth and final verse describes the ultimate path — buddhahood itself, wisdom finally settled at its very root within the dharma-realm. Read together with the rest of Part Four, these verses offer a compact aide-mémoire for the entire process this part has just described in full: from provisioning, through investigation, through breakthrough, through cultivation, to final arrival.

Part Four Complete

This closes Part Four of Ru-Yi's translation: the mechanics of entering the character of the knowable directly — who is ready, what tempers the mind, the four investigations and four kinds of thorough knowledge, the rope-and-snake simile's three-stage entry into the three natures, and the moment of breakthrough into the path of seeing, with a brief look ahead to the long path of cultivation that follows.
