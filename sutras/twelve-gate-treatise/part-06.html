No Nature, the Two Truths, and Cause and Fruit

Gate Eight is the doctrinal center of the treatise. Everything so far has been demolition; here, for the first time, an opponent lands a serious blow — if all things are empty, then suffering is not real, the path leads nowhere, there are no noble ones, and the Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha are gone with them. Emptiness, on this charge, does not purify Buddhism; it abolishes it. Nāgārjuna's reply is the doctrine of the two truths, and it does not merely defend emptiness — it turns the objection completely around and hands it back. Gate Nine follows as a brief coda, closing off the last place a fruit might have come from.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 6 of 8 complete Source: Taishō T30, no. 1568, 十二門論 (attrib. Nāgārjuna, tr. Kumārajīva, 409 CE)
Gate Eight · 觀性門第八

Examining Nature

1. What Changes Has No Nature

The gate opens with the shortest and most direct argument in the whole treatise, and it rests on what the word "nature" was always taken to mean. A nature is what a thing is in itself, unmade and not borrowed. Two consequences follow immediately: what has a nature cannot change, and what has a nature cannot be produced. Everything we encounter does both.

Chinese Source · 觀性門第八

復次，一切法空。何以故？諸法無性故。如說：

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, all things are empty. Why? Because things have no nature. As it is said:

Root Verse · 頌

見有變異相，　諸法無有性；

無性法亦無，　諸法皆空故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seeing that they bear the mark of alteration,

things have no nature of their own;

and what has no nature is not there either,

for all things alike are empty.

諸法若有性，則不應變異；而見一切法皆變異，是故當知諸法無性。復次，若諸法有定性，則不應從眾緣生；若性從眾緣生者，性即是作法；不作法不因待他名為性，是故一切法空。

If things had a nature of their own, they ought not to alter; and we see that all things alter — so one should know that things have no nature of their own. Furthermore, if things had a fixed nature, they ought not to arise from a gathering of conditions. And if a nature arose from a gathering of conditions, then that nature would be something made; whereas what is not made and does not depend on another is precisely what is called a nature. Therefore all things are empty.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The definition is the argument. What is not made and does not depend on another is what is called a nature — this is not Nāgārjuna's stipulation, it is what his opponents themselves mean by the word. A nature is supposed to be the thing's own, underived, and unalterable; that is why it can serve as the bedrock of an ontology. But then anything produced by conditions has, by definition, no nature, since it was made and does depend. The doctrine of emptiness is not asserting a mysterious extra fact about things. It is pointing out that "arisen from conditions" and "having a nature of its own" are contradictory descriptions, and that everything we ever meet answers to the first.

The verse's third line guards the exit. Having found that things have no nature, one might treat naturelessness as the truth about them — a hidden essence of emptiness underneath the appearances. The line closes that off before it opens: what has no nature is not there either. Emptiness is not a substance that things secretly are.

2. The Objection: Emptiness Destroys Everything

Now the strongest objection in the treatise, and one of the most famous in Buddhist philosophy. The opponent does not quibble about pots or marks. He follows emptiness through to what he takes to be its consequences, and the chain is relentless: no arising means no impermanence, no impermanence means no suffering, no suffering means no origin, no cessation, no path — and with the Four Noble Truths gone, so are the stages of attainment, the noble community, and finally the Three Jewels themselves.

Chinese Source

問曰：「若一切法空，則無生無滅；若無生滅，則無苦諦；若無苦諦，則無集諦；若無苦、集諦，則無滅諦；若無苦滅，則無至苦滅道。若諸法空無性，則無四聖諦；無四聖諦故，亦無四沙門果；無四沙門果故，則無賢聖。是事無故，佛、法、僧亦無，世間法皆亦無。是事不然，是故諸法不應盡空。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: If all things are empty, there is no arising and no ceasing. If there is no arising and ceasing, there is no noble truth of suffering. If there is no truth of suffering, there is no truth of its origin. If there is neither the truth of suffering nor of its origin, there is no truth of cessation. And if there is no cessation of suffering, there is no path leading to the cessation of suffering. If things are empty and without nature, there are no Four Noble Truths; and lacking the Four Noble Truths, there are no four fruits of the recluse; and lacking the four fruits, there are no noble ones. And these being absent, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha are absent too, and all worldly things along with them. This cannot be — and therefore things should not be entirely empty.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

It is important not to hear this as an outsider's attack. The speaker is a Buddhist, and everything he is defending is worth defending — that suffering is real, that it has a cause that can be cut, that the path actually leads somewhere, and that there really are people further along it. His fear is one that Madhyamaka has provoked in every century since: that this looks less like a deeper Buddhism than like the end of Buddhism.

The same objection is put at greater length in the twenty-fourth chapter of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, and receives the same answer. Reading the two together is the best way to see how central this exchange was to Nāgārjuna's project — it is the one place where he stops refuting and explains himself.

3. The Answer: Two Truths

The reply begins by naming a distinction the objection had collapsed. There are two truths, and they are not rivals; the ultimate is reached only by way of the conventional, and neither can be had without the other. The opponent's error is then diagnosed precisely: he heard something said at one level and took it for the other.

Chinese Source

答曰：「有二諦：一、世諦，二、第一義諦。因世諦得說第一義諦，若不因世諦，則不得說第一義諦；若不得第一義諦，則不得涅槃。若人不知二諦，則不知自利、他利、共利。如是若知世諦，則知第一義諦；知第一義諦，則知世諦。汝今聞說世諦，謂是第一義諦，是故墮在失處。諸佛因緣法，名為甚深第一義，是因緣法無自性，故我說是空。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: There are two truths: first, conventional truth; second, ultimate truth. It is in dependence on conventional truth that ultimate truth can be spoken of; without conventional truth, ultimate truth cannot be spoken of at all; and without reaching ultimate truth, nirvāṇa is not reached. If a person does not know the two truths, they do not know their own benefit, nor another's, nor the benefit of both. And so: knowing conventional truth, one knows ultimate truth; and knowing ultimate truth, one knows conventional truth. You have now heard what is spoken at the level of conventional truth and supposed it to be ultimate truth — and that is why you have fallen into error. The buddhas' teaching of dependent arising is what is called the profound ultimate; and because this dependent arising has no nature of its own, I call it empty.

"Knowing conventional truth, one knows ultimate truth; and knowing ultimate truth, one knows conventional truth."

Reading note — Ru-Yi

Three things are being said here, and it is worth separating them.

First, the two truths are not two worlds, one real and one fake. They are two ways of speaking truly about the same thing, and the relation between them runs in both directions. The ultimate is not reachable except through the conventional, because there is no other language to reach it in — one has to say "suffering," "path," "cessation" before one can say anything about their emptiness. And equally, understanding the ultimate is what lets one hold the conventional properly, as functioning description rather than as a report on self-standing entities.

Second, the diagnosis of the objection is exact. The opponent hears "there is suffering, there is a path, there are noble ones" and takes these to be claims about things with natures of their own. That is what he thinks emptiness threatens, and he is right that emptiness denies it. What he has missed is that those statements were never operating at that level. To deny that suffering has a nature of its own is not to deny that there is suffering; it is to say what kind of truth "there is suffering" is.

Third — and this is the sentence that carries the most weight — the buddhas' teaching of dependent arising is what is called the profound ultimate; and because this dependent arising has no nature of its own, I call it empty. Emptiness and dependent arising are not two doctrines, one negative and one positive. They are the same fact stated twice. Whatever arises dependently thereby lacks a nature of its own, and whatever lacks a nature of its own is precisely what can arise dependently. This is the identification on which all of Madhyamaka turns, and everything in the next paragraph follows from it.

4. The Objection Reversed

Now the counterattack, and it is total. Nāgārjuna takes the opponent's entire chain of consequences and runs it against the opponent's own position — showing that it is fixed natures, not emptiness, that abolish suffering, the path, the noble ones, and the Three Jewels.

Chinese Source

「若諸法不從眾緣生，則應各有定性，五陰不應有生滅相；五陰不生不滅，即無無常；若無無常，則無苦聖諦。若無苦聖諦，則無因緣生法集聖諦。諸法若有定性，則無苦滅聖諦。何以故？性無變異故。若無苦滅聖諦，則無至苦滅道。

Ru-Yi Translation

If things did not arise from a gathering of conditions, then each would have a fixed nature, and the five aggregates would bear no marks of arising and ceasing. If the five aggregates neither arose nor ceased, there would be no impermanence; and if no impermanence, no noble truth of suffering. And if no noble truth of suffering, then no noble truth of origination, which is the arising of things from causes and conditions. And if things had a fixed nature, there would be no noble truth of the cessation of suffering. Why? Because a nature does not alter. And if no noble truth of the cessation of suffering, then no path leading to the cessation of suffering.

是故若人不受空，則無四聖諦；若無四聖諦，則無得四聖諦；若無得四聖諦，則無知苦、斷集、證滅、修道，是事無故則無四沙門果；無四沙門果故，則無得、向者。若無得、向者，則無佛；破因緣法故，則無法；以無果故，則無僧；若無佛、法、僧，則無三寶。若無三寶，則壞世俗法。此則不然，是故一切法空。

Therefore if a person does not accept emptiness, there are no Four Noble Truths; and if no Four Noble Truths, no attainment of them; and if no attainment of them, then no knowing of suffering, no cutting off of its origin, no realizing of cessation, no cultivating of the path. These being absent, there are no four fruits of the recluse; and lacking the four fruits, there is no one who has attained them and no one approaching them. And if there is none attaining and none approaching, there is no Buddha. Since the teaching of dependent arising has been destroyed, there is no Dharma. Since there are no fruits, there is no Saṅgha. And if there is no Buddha, no Dharma, and no Saṅgha, there are no Three Jewels. And if there are no Three Jewels, then conventional truth itself is ruined. This cannot be — and therefore all things are empty.

「復次，若諸法有定性，則無生無滅、無罪無福；無罪福果報，世間常是一相。是故當知諸法無性。

Furthermore, if things had a fixed nature there would be no arising and no ceasing, no wrongdoing and no merit, and no ripening of the fruits of wrongdoing and merit — and the world would forever wear one single aspect. So one should know that things have no nature.

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The reversal is exact, clause for clause, and it is worth following slowly, because it is the single most important argument in Madhyamaka. Take the opponent's demand seriously: let things have fixed natures of their own. Then the five aggregates cannot arise or cease, because a nature is by definition unmade and unalterable. No arising and ceasing means no impermanence. No impermanence means no suffering — for the whole Buddhist analysis of suffering runs through impermanence. And a fixed nature is even worse for the third truth than for the first: if suffering had a nature of its own, it could never stop, because natures do not alter. Cessation becomes impossible in principle, and a path to an impossible destination is no path.

So the objection was aimed in the wrong direction. Emptiness does not make change and liberation impossible; it is what makes them possible. Only what lacks a fixed nature can arise, alter, and come to an end. A world of things with natures of their own would be a world where nothing could ever be different from what it is — the world would forever wear one single aspect — and in such a world there would be no wrongdoing, no merit, no ripening, no growth, and above all no awakening. The person who cannot accept emptiness is the one who has destroyed the Four Noble Truths.

The last clause is quietly the boldest: if there are no Three Jewels, then conventional truth itself is ruined. Emptiness is not the enemy of ordinary reality. Fixed natures are. It is the doctrine of self-standing things that cannot account for the world we live in, and emptiness that lets it be exactly what it appears to be — arising, changing, and passing.

5. Not by Another's Nature Either

The gate closes by blocking the last rescue, the same one attempted at the end of Gate One and now answered at the level of nature itself: if a thing has no nature of its own, let it have another's.

Chinese Source

「若謂諸法無自性、從他性有者，是亦不然。何以故？若無自性，云何從他性有？因自性有他性故。又他性即亦是自性。何以故？他性即是他自性故。若自性不成，他性亦不成；若自性、他性不成，離自性、他性何處更有法？若有不成，無亦不成。是故今推求無自性、無他性，無有、無無故，一切有為法空。有為法空故，無為法亦空；有為、無為尚空，何況我耶？」

Ru-Yi Translation

And if you say that things have no nature of their own but exist by another's nature, this too is not so. Why? If there is no nature of its own, how should it exist by another's nature? — since it is in dependence on a nature of one's own that there is such a thing as another's nature at all. Moreover, another's nature is itself a nature of one's own; for another's nature is just that other's own nature. If a nature of one's own is not established, another's nature is not established either; and if neither a nature of one's own nor another's nature is established, then apart from these two, where else is there any thing? And if existence is not established, non-existence is not established either. Therefore, pressing the search now: there is no nature of one's own and no other-nature; and since there is neither existence nor non-existence, all conditioned things are empty. Conditioned things being empty, unconditioned things are empty too; and if the conditioned and the unconditioned are alike empty, how much more the self!

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The decisive line is the simplest: another's nature is just that other's own nature. "Other-nature" is not a second kind of nature with different rules; it is exactly the same thing viewed from a different seat. So an argument that abolishes natures of one's own has already abolished other-natures, in the same stroke, everywhere. There is no borrowing from a neighbor who has nothing either — the same point made with rushes and a mat at the end of Gate One, here reaching its most general form.

Gate Nine · 觀因果門第九

Examining Cause and Fruit

The shortest gate in the treatise, and deliberately so. Gates Two and Three searched the conditions for the fruit and did not find it; the only remaining possibility is that the fruit came from somewhere outside altogether. Gate Nine closes that door in a single paragraph and moves on.

Chinese Source · 觀因果門第九

復次，一切法空。何以故？諸法自無性，亦不從餘處來。如說：

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, all things are empty. Why? Because things have no nature of their own, and do not come from anywhere else. As it is said:

Root Verse · 頌

果於眾緣中，　畢竟不可得，

亦不餘處來，　云何而有果？

Ru-Yi Translation

In the gathering of conditions

the fruit is finally not to be got;

nor does it come from anywhere else —

how, then, is there any fruit at all?

眾緣若一一中、若和合中，俱無果，如先說。又是果不從餘處來，若餘處來者則不從因緣生，亦無眾緣和合功。若果眾緣中無，亦不從餘處來者，是即為空。

果空故，一切有為法空；有為法空故，無為法亦空；有為、無為尚空，何況我耶？

Whether in the conditions taken one by one, or in their combination, there is no fruit — as was said above. Nor does this fruit come from anywhere else; for if it came from elsewhere, it would not arise from causes and conditions at all, and the gathering of the conditions would have accomplished nothing. And if the fruit is not in the gathering of conditions and does not come from anywhere else, then it is empty.

Since the fruit is empty, all conditioned things are empty; conditioned things being empty, the unconditioned is empty too; and if the conditioned and the unconditioned are alike empty, how much more the self!

Reading note — Ru-Yi

The gate is brief because it only needs to close one exit, and the closing argument is neat. Suppose the fruit did arrive from somewhere else. Then it did not come from its conditions — and the entire gathering of clay, wheel, cord, and potter turns out to have contributed nothing whatever to the pot. That is a price no one is willing to pay, including the opponent, since the whole reason for positing causes was to explain why the pot appears when they are assembled and not otherwise.

Placed where it is, immediately after Gate Eight, the brevity reads as confidence. Having just shown that emptiness is what makes causation possible rather than what destroys it, Nāgārjuna can dispose of the last alternative in a paragraph and turn to the three gates that remain — on the agent, on time, and on arising itself.

Part Six Complete

This closes Gates Eight and Nine: the argument that what alters and what is produced can have no nature of its own, since a nature is by definition unmade and unchanging; the gravest objection in the treatise, that emptiness abolishes the Four Noble Truths and the Three Jewels with them; the two truths, and the identification of emptiness with dependent arising that answers it; the reversal, showing that fixed natures are what would make suffering permanent and liberation impossible; and Gate Nine's brief closing of the last route by which a fruit might have arrived. Part Seven takes up Gate Ten alone — the treatise's longest sustained argument, on who or what makes suffering, and its fourteen-argument refutation of a creator god.
