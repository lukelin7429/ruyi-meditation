Nine Short Works on a Life Spent in the Vinaya

弘一大師文集—律學 gathers nine short pieces by Master Hongyi — the same monk whose Nanshan Vinaya for Laypeople is translated in full elsewhere on this site — spanning three decades: an autobiographical account of how he came to devote himself to Vinaya study, three vows, a fragment of lay instruction broken off by a lost page, ten questions and answers, eight points of clarification on a genuinely difficult problem of his own age, practical notes on a rite he compiled himself, a preface to an unrelated sutra, and finally a full lecture on the whole structure of the precepts, delivered late in his life to monastics receiving ordination. No English translation of this anthology has previously existed.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all nine pieces translated Source: 弘一大師文集—律學 (bfnn.org public-domain text)

1. 余弘律之因緣 — The Causes and Conditions of My Devotion to Vinaya Studies

The collection opens with Hongyi's own brief, plainly-stated account of how his Vinaya study began and grew — from an early reading of Ouyi Zhixu's works, through a decade of collecting old commentaries and drafting his own Tabular Record, to a documented moment of self-correction: an early draft that criticized Dao Xuan, quietly struck out once he judged the criticism unbecoming.

Chinese Source

初出家時，即讀梵網合注。續讀靈峰宗論，乃發起學律之願。

受戒時，隨時參讀傳戒正範及毘尼事義集要。

庚申之春，自日本請得古版南山靈芝三大部；計八十餘冊。

辛酉之春，始編戒相表記。六月，第一次草稿乃訖。以後屢經修改，手抄數次。

是年閱藏，得見義淨三藏所譯有部律及南海寄歸內法傳；深為讚歎。謂較舊律為善；故四分律戒相表記第一二次草稿中，屢引義淨之說，以糾正南山。其後自悟輕謗古德，有所未可，遂塗抹之。經多次刪改，乃成最後之定本。

以後雖未敢謗毀南山，但於南山三大部仍未用心窮研；故印專習有部律。二年之中，編有部犯相摘記一卷、自行抄一卷。

其時徐霨如居士創刻經處於天津，專刻南山宗律書，費資數萬金，歷時十餘年。

Ru-Yi Translation

When I first left the household life, I read the Combined Commentary on the Brahma Net [Sutra]. Going on to read the Lingfeng Discourses, I thereupon gave rise to the vow to study the Vinaya.

At the time of receiving the precepts, I read alongside them, as opportunity allowed, the Correct Model for Transmitting the Precepts and the Collected Essentials of Vinaya Matters.

In the spring of the gengshen year [1920], I sent to Japan and obtained an old printing of the Three Great Compendia of the Nanshan and Lingzhi [masters] — in all, more than eighty volumes.

In the spring of the xinyou year [1921], I began compiling the Tabular Record of Precept-Marks. By the sixth month, the first draft was complete. After that it went through repeated revision, and I copied it out by hand several times.

That same year, in reading through the canon, I came upon the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya translated by the Tripiṭaka Master Yijing, together with his Record of the Inner Law Sent Home from the Southern Seas, and was deeply moved to admiration. I judged it superior to the older Vinaya; and so, in the first and second drafts of the Tabular Record of Precept-Marks in the Four-Part Vinaya, I repeatedly cited Yijing's views in order to correct [the tradition of] Nanshan. Afterward I came to realize myself that lightly disparaging the ancient worthies was not something to be done, and so I struck those passages out. After many rounds of deletion and revision, the final settled text was completed.

After that, although I no longer dared to disparage Nanshan, I still had not applied myself to a thorough study of the Three Great Compendia; and so for a time I devoted myself exclusively to the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya. Over two years I compiled one fascicle of Gleanings on the Marks of Transgression in the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya and one fascicle of my own personal notes.

At that time the layman Xu Weiru founded a sutra-carving press in Tianjin devoted exclusively to carving the Vinaya texts of the Nanshan school, spending tens of thousands in silver and continuing the work for more than ten years.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This short autobiographical note is worth reading against the Nanshan Lu series translated elsewhere on this site: it is Hongyi's own account of the decade of study that eventually produced that compilation. Two details stand out. First, the timeline — a reading of Ouyi Zhixu that sparks the vow, a decade collecting the old Nanshan and Lingzhi commentaries from Japan, and a first draft of his own Tabular Record of Precept-Marks completed within roughly a year of receiving that material — shows how quickly and deliberately he moved once he committed himself. Second, and more striking, is the small act of self-correction he records without excuse or elaboration: an early draft used the newly-rediscovered Sarvāstivāda Vinaya to criticize Dao Xuan's own Nanshan tradition, and Hongyi later struck those passages out himself, judging it improper to disparage an ancient worthy even when a textual case could be made. The layman Xu Weiru, mentioned in the closing line, was the founder of the Tianjin sutra-carving press that printed the Nanshan-school Vinaya texts Hongyi relied on throughout his later career — the same press credited at the close of the seventh piece in this collection below.

2. 弘律願文 — A Vow to Propagate the Vinaya

A short, formal vow in Hongyi's own voice, composed under his ordination name Yanyin. It is built on the bodhisattva pattern of vicarious suffering — vowing to bear the karmic retribution of all sentient beings in their place — closing with four short, memorable vow-lines.

Chinese Source

如是戒品，我今誓願受持、修學，盡未來際，不復捨離。以此功德，願我及眾生，無始已來所作眾罪，盡得消滅。若一切眾生所有定業，當受報者，我皆代受。遍微塵國，歷諸惡道，經微塵劫，備嘗眾苦，歡喜忍受，終無厭悔；令彼眾生先成佛道。我所發願，真實不虛，伏惟三寶證知者。

演音自撰發願句三種，行住坐臥，常常憶念，我所修持一切功德，悉以迴施法界眾生；眾生所造無量惡業，願我一身代受眾苦。

誓捨身命，護持三世一切佛法！
誓捨身命，救度法界一切眾生！
願代法界一切眾生，備受眾苦！
願護南山四分律宗弘傳世間！

Ru-Yi Translation

Such precepts as these, I now vow to receive, uphold, and study, to the very end of future time, never again to abandon or depart from them. By this merit, may I and all sentient beings — for all the manifold offenses committed since beginningless time — obtain their complete extinction. Should any sentient being have fixed karma for which retribution must be received, I vow to receive it in their place: throughout every land as numerous as motes of dust, passing through every evil destiny, over kalpas as numerous as motes of dust, undergoing every kind of suffering in full — enduring it joyfully, never with weariness or regret — so that those sentient beings may first accomplish the path to buddhahood. This vow that I have made is true and not false; I humbly submit it for the witness and certification of the Three Jewels.

I, Yanyin — Master Hongyi's own ordination name — have composed for myself three phrases of vow, to be called to mind constantly whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down: whatever merit I cultivate and uphold, I dedicate entirely to the sentient beings of the Dharma-realm; and for the immeasurable evil karma that sentient beings have created, I vow that this one body of mine will receive all their suffering in their place.

I vow to give up my body and life to protect and uphold the Buddha-dharma of the three time-periods!
I vow to give up my body and life to save and deliver all sentient beings of the Dharma-realm!
I vow to receive, in the place of all sentient beings of the Dharma-realm, the whole of their suffering!
I vow to protect the Nanshan Four-Part Vinaya school, that it may spread and be transmitted throughout the world!

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The vow to "receive in their place" the fixed karmic retribution of all sentient beings is a standard bodhisattva form, but Hongyi's phrasing makes the cost concrete rather than abstract — every dust-mote land, every evil destiny, every dust-mote kalpa — before turning, in the closing four lines, from the general to the specific: the fourth and final vow-line names the Nanshan Four-Part Vinaya school by name, tying this personal vow directly to the decades of textual labor described in the piece just before it.

3. 南山律苑住眾學律發願文 — A Vow of the Resident Vinaya Students of the Nanshan Vinaya Cloister

A collective vow made by Hongyi together with his fellow Vinaya students, dated to a specific ceremony held on Ouyi Zhixu's traditional birthday in 1933, and signed by name. Where the previous vow was personal, this one is corporate — four shared aspirations for the community of students, offered before a named list of buddhas, bodhisattvas, and patriarchs.

Chinese Source

中華民國二十二年，歲次癸酉五月二十六日。即舊曆五月初三日。恭值靈峰蕅益大師聖誕。學律弟子等，敬於諸佛菩薩祖師之前，同發四弘誓願已；并別發四願：一願學律弟子等，生生世世，永為善友，互相提攜，常不捨離。同學毘尼，同宣大法，紹隆僧種，普利眾生；一願弟子等學律及以弘法之時，身心安寧，無諸魔障，境緣順遂，資生充足；一願當來建立南山律院，普集多眾，廣為弘傳。不為名聞，不求利養；一願發大菩提心，護持佛法。誓盡心力，宣揚七百餘年湮沒不傳之南山律教，流布世間。冀正法再興，佛日重耀；并願以此發宏誓願，及以別發四願功德、乃至當來學律一切功德，悉以迴向法界眾生；惟願諸眾生等，共發大心，速消業障，往往極樂，早證菩提！伏乞十方一切諸佛、本師釋迦牟尼佛、極樂世界阿彌陀佛、觀世音菩薩摩訶薩、地藏菩薩摩訶薩、南山道宣律師、靈芝元照律師、靈峰蕅益大師，慈念哀愍，證明攝受！

學律弟子演音弘一　性常宗凝　照融廣洽　傳淨了識　傳正心燦　廣演本妙　寂聲誰具　寂明瑞曦　寂德瑞澄　騰觀妙慧　寂護瑞衛　廣信平願

Ru-Yi Translation

In the twenty-second year of the Republic of China, in the year guiyou, on the twenty-sixth day of the fifth month — that is, the third day of the fifth month by the old lunar calendar — we reverently mark the sacred birthday of the Lingfeng Ouyi Great Master. We, the disciples studying the Vinaya, respectfully before all Buddhas, bodhisattvas, and patriarchal teachers, have together given rise to the Four Universal Vows; and in addition we make four further vows: first, we vow that we, the disciples studying the Vinaya, life after life, will forever be good companions to one another, supporting and drawing each other forward, never abandoning or departing from one another — studying the Vinaya together, proclaiming the great Dharma together, carrying forward the lineage of the Sangha, and universally benefiting sentient beings; second, we vow that, while we disciples are studying the Vinaya and propagating the Dharma, body and mind will be at peace, free of every demonic obstruction, with circumstances running smoothly and the necessities of life sufficiently provided; third, we vow that in time to come a Nanshan Vinaya cloister will be established, gathering together a great assembly, and broadly propagating [the Vinaya] — not for the sake of fame, nor seeking profit or support; fourth, we vow to give rise to the great mind of bodhi and to protect and uphold the Buddha-dharma, vowing to exhaust our hearts' strength in proclaiming the teaching of the Nanshan Vinaya school, submerged and untransmitted for more than seven hundred years, and spreading it throughout the world — hoping that the True Dharma may flourish anew and the sun of the Buddha shine forth once more. And we vow that the merit of this great vow, together with the merit of these four further vows, and all the merit of studying the Vinaya from now on, be entirely dedicated to the sentient beings of the Dharma-realm; may all sentient beings together give rise to this great resolve, swiftly dissolve their karmic obstructions, be reborn in the Land of Ultimate Bliss (reading 往往 in the source as 往生, a likely transcription slip — see note below), and speedily realize bodhi! We humbly beseech all Buddhas of the ten directions, our fundamental teacher Śākyamuni Buddha, Amitābha Buddha of the Land of Ultimate Bliss, the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattva Avalokiteśvara, the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattva Kṣitigarbha, the Vinaya Master Dao Xuan of Nanshan, the Vinaya Master Yuan Zhao of Lingzhi, and the Lingfeng Ouyi Great Master, to hold us in compassionate regard and pity, and to bear witness and receive us!

The disciples studying the Vinaya: Yanyin Hongyi, Xingchang Zongning, Zhaorong Guangqia, Chuanjing Liaoshi, Chuanzheng Xincan, Guangyan Benmiao, Jisheng Shuiju, Jiming Ruixi, Jide Ruicheng, Tengguan Miaohui, Jihu Ruiwei, Guangxin Pingyuan.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The date given — the third day of the fifth lunar month, 1933 — is offered because it is Ouyi Zhixu's traditional birthday: the entire vow is timed to fall on that anniversary, underlining how central Ouyi's example is to everything in this collection. The list of twelve signers, Hongyi first among them under his ordination name Yanyin, gives this piece a documentary character the other vow lacks — it records an actual community of named students at a particular Vinaya cloister on a particular day, not merely an individual's private aspiration.

The source text reads 往往極樂 ("[be reborn] again and again in the Land of Ultimate Bliss"), where the surrounding grammar and the parallel construction with 早證菩提 ("speedily realize bodhi") both suggest the intended reading is 往生極樂 ("be reborn in the Land of Ultimate Bliss") — an easy transcription slip between two visually similar characters. The translation above follows the sense of 往生, with this note flagging the discrepancy rather than silently altering the Chinese source shown alongside it.

4. 在家律要之開示 — An Exposition on the Essentials of Precept-Keeping for Laypeople

This piece survives only as a fragment: the bfnn.org source text itself breaks off mid-argument, in the middle of Hongyi's discussion of how difficult the precept against stealing is to keep for anyone doing business in society, followed by an editorial note acknowledging the loss. The translation below covers everything present in the source, in full, and stops exactly where the source stops.

Chinese Source

凡初發心人，既受三皈依，應續受五戒。倘自審一時不能全受者，即先受四戒三戒，乃至僅受一二戒，都可。在家居士，既聞法有素，知自行檢點，嚴自約束，不蹈非禮，不敢輕率妄行。則殺生、邪婬、大妄語、飲酒之四戒，或可不犯；惟有在社會上辦事之人，欲不破盜戒，為最不容易事。例如與人合買地皮房產，與人合做生意，報稅納捐時，未免有以多數報少數之事；因數人合夥，欲實報則人以為愚。或為股東所反對者有之。又不知而犯，與明知違背法律而故犯之事；如信中夾附鈔票，與手寫函件取巧掩藏，當印刷物寄，均犯盜稅之罪。凡非與而取，及法律所不許，而取巧不納，皆有盜取之心跡，及盜取之行為，皆結盜罪。非但銀錢出入上，當嚴淨其心；即微而至於一草一木、寸紙尺線，必須先向物主明白請求，得彼允許，而後可以使用。不待許可而取用、不曾問明而擅動；皆有不與而取之心跡，皆犯盜取盜用之行為，皆結盜罪。......（按：下文佚失，俟得全文，再續載之。）

Ru-Yi Translation

For anyone newly resolved on the path, having already received the Three Refuges, one should go on to receive the Five Precepts. If, upon honest self-examination, one finds oneself unable for the time being to receive them all, then receiving first four precepts, or three, or even only one or two, is entirely permissible. A lay Buddhist who has heard the Dharma for some time and knows how to examine his own conduct, strictly restraining himself, not treading into impropriety, not daring to act rashly or carelessly, may well manage not to transgress the four precepts against killing, sexual misconduct, great false speech, and drinking alcohol. But for a person engaged in worldly business, avoiding a breach of the precept against stealing is the most difficult thing of all.

For example: when jointly purchasing land or a building with others, or jointly running a business with others, at the time of reporting and paying taxes it is hard to avoid reporting less than the true amount held by the majority — because when several people are in partnership, if one insists on reporting honestly, the others will regard him as a fool, or he may even meet with the shareholders' opposition. There are also cases of transgressing without realizing it, and cases of knowingly violating the law and transgressing on purpose — as when banknotes are tucked inside a letter, or a handwritten letter is cunningly disguised and sent under cover of printed matter, both of which commit the offense of evading duty, which is a form of theft. In every case of taking what is not given, and of cunningly evading what the law does not permit to go unpaid, there is both the mental trace of a thieving intention and the actual act of theft, and in every such case the offense of stealing is incurred.

It is not only in matters of money coming and going that the mind must be kept strictly pure; even down to a single blade of grass or twig, an inch of paper or a foot of thread, one must first clearly ask the owner and obtain his permission before one may make use of it. To take and use something without waiting for permission, or to help oneself to something without first inquiring, both carry the mental trace of taking what is not given, both constitute the act of stealing or of wrongfully using what belongs to another, and in every such case the offense of stealing is incurred. ...

(Editor's note, present in the original source: the remainder of this text has been lost; it will be added when the complete version is obtained.)

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This is the one piece in the collection that cannot be completed, and the translation above stops exactly where the bfnn.org source stops — nothing after the editorial note has been supplied or reconstructed. What survives is nonetheless a coherent argument in miniature: Hongyi's larger point, developed at much greater length in the closing lecture below (see §9, "On the Five Precepts"), is that the precept against stealing is by far the hardest of the five for a working layperson to keep cleanly, precisely because ordinary commercial life — partnership accounting, tax reporting, postal customs — creates constant small occasions for "taking what is not given" that a person may not even register as theft. The fragment breaks off just as Hongyi turns from the graver case of tax evasion to the more exacting standard he is evidently building toward: that even borrowing "a single blade of grass" without asking first counts as the same offense.

5. 問答十章 — Ten Questions and Answers

A set of ten short exchanges, evidently written down quickly for circulation, on recurring points of confusion in the Vinaya practice of Hongyi's own day — from the propriety of the mantra-recitations used at modern ordination ceremonies to whether Chan monastics who plow their own fields are actually violating the precepts. Nearly every answer leans on Ouyi Zhixu as the controlling authority. Hongyi appended his own brief outline notes on the divination-ritual mentioned in Question 8, which are translated at the end of this section exactly as abbreviated as they appear in the source.

Chinese Source — Q1

問：近世諸叢林傳戒之時，皆令熟讀毘尼日用切要（俗稱為五十三咒），未審可否？

答：蕅益大師曾解釋此義，今略錄之。文云：「既預比丘之列，當以律學為先。今之願偈（即當願眾生等），本出華嚴。種種真言，皆屬密部。論法門雖不可思議，約修證則各有本宗。收之則全是，若一偈、若一句、若一字，皆為道種。撿之則全非，律不律、顯不顯、密不密、僅成散善；此正法所以漸衰，而末運所以不振。有志之士，不若專精戒律，辦比丘之本職也」。

（十誦：諸比丘廢學毘尼，便讀誦修多羅、阿毘曇，世尊種種訶責。乃至由有毘尼佛法住世等。多有上座長老比丘學律。）

Ru-Yi Translation — Q1

Question: In recent generations, when the various monastic communities transmit the precepts, they all have candidates thoroughly memorize the Essential Vinaya Verses for Daily Use (popularly called the "Fifty-Three Mantras"). Is this proper or not?

Answer: The Ouyi Great Master once explained the meaning of this; I here briefly record it. His text says: "Once one has taken one's place among the ranks of bhikṣus, one ought to make the study of the Vinaya one's first priority. The aspiration-verses of the present day [such as 'may all sentient beings...'] originate from the Avataṃsaka [Sutra]; the various mantras all belong to the esoteric division. Though the Dharma-gates involved are inconceivable, when it comes to actual cultivation and realization, each has its own proper tradition. Gathered together without discrimination, all of it may be said to be 'complete' — a single verse, a single phrase, even a single syllable, each becomes a seed of the path. Examined with discrimination, none of it is truly complete — neither properly Vinaya nor not, neither exoteric nor not, neither esoteric nor not, amounting to nothing more than scattered goodness; this is precisely why the True Dharma gradually declines, and why the fortunes of this final age fail to be revived. Those with real aspiration would do better to concentrate wholly on the precepts and the Vinaya, and attend to the fundamental duty of a bhikṣu."

(The Ten Recitations Vinaya: when bhikṣus neglected the study of the Vinaya and instead recited the sutras and the Abhidharma, the World-Honored One rebuked them in various ways — down to the teaching that it is by the Vinaya that the Buddha-dharma abides in the world; many were the elder and senior bhikṣus who studied the Vinaya.)

Chinese Source — Q2

問：百丈清規，頗與戒律相似；今學律者，亦宜參閱否？

答：百丈於唐時編纂此書，其後屢經他人增刪。至元朝改變尤多，本來面目，殆不可見；故蓮池、蕅益大師力詆斥之。蓮池大師之說，今未及檢錄。唯錄蕅益大師之說如下。文云：「正法滅壞，全由律學不明。百丈清規，久失原作本意；並是元朝流俗僧官住持，杜撰增飾，文理不通。今人有奉行者，皆因未諳律學故也。」又云：「非佛所制，便名非法；如元朝附會百丈清規等」。又云：「百丈清規。元朝世諦住持穿鑿，尤為可恥。」按律宗諸書，浩如煙海。吾人盡形學之，尚苦力有未及。即百丈原本今仍存在，亦可不須閱覽；況偽本乎？今宜以蓮池、蕅益諸大師之言，傳示道侶可也。

Ru-Yi Translation — Q2

Question: The Baizhang Rules of Purity closely resemble the Vinaya precepts; should a student of the Vinaya today also consult it?

Answer: Baizhang compiled this text in the Tang dynasty, and afterward it went through repeated additions and deletions by others. By the Yuan dynasty it had been altered even more extensively, so that its original character can hardly be seen at all; this is why the Lianchi and Ouyi Great Masters both forcefully denounced it. I have not yet had occasion to look up and record Lianchi's remarks; I here record only those of the Ouyi Great Master, as follows: "The ruin and destruction of the True Dharma is entirely due to the Vinaya's not being understood. The Baizhang Rules of Purity long ago lost the original author's intent; it is entirely the fabricated embellishment of vulgar clerical officials who held the position of abbot in the Yuan dynasty — its text and reasoning do not even hold together. That people today follow it is entirely because they are unfamiliar with the study of the Vinaya." He also says: "What was not instituted by the Buddha is called 'not the Dharma' — such as the Yuan-dynasty concoction falsely attributed to Baizhang's Rules of Purity." He also says: "As for the Baizhang Rules of Purity — that a worldly abbot of the Yuan dynasty tampered with and distorted it is especially shameful." Considering that the various texts of the Vinaya school are as vast as a sea of mist, even if we were to devote our whole lives to studying them, our strength would still fall short; even the original Baizhang text, supposing it still existed today, need not be read — how much less this spurious version! It is fitting now to pass on the words of the Lianchi and Ouyi Great Masters to fellow companions of the path.

Chinese Source — Q3

問：今世俗眾，乞師證明受皈依者，輒稱皈依某師，未知是否？

Question: In transmitting the precepts today, communities always gather together several hundred people and set a period of a full month; is this the Buddha's own institution?

Answer: In the Buddha's own time, whenever someone received the precepts, the tonsure-preceptor would simply invite nine monastics, and the precepts could then be conferred — this was conferral to a single individual, separately. Even in the Tang dynasty in this land, though there were cases of several people receiving together, the number never exceeded ten or twenty. Only in recent times, purely for the sake of a bustling and impressive occasion, have communities taken to gathering great multitudes together; hence the Ouyi Great Master once denounced this, saying: "One may enter the path at any time — why must it wait for the eighth day of the twelfth month or the eighth day of the fourth month? Only under conditions of genuine difficulty is a group of three permitted [to confer ordination together] — how could this permit a multitude running to hundreds or thousands?" As for the actual conferral of the precepts, it can be accomplished in less than half a day — why should it require many days? Moreover, in recent times, those who gather great multitudes for a month do so only in order to have the ordinands assist with water-and-land repentance rites and other Buddhist services, so that they are hurried and occupied all day long, with scarcely any leisure — while the actual matter of receiving the precepts is treated as of no real consequence. This is even harder to bear speaking of. Hence the receiving of precepts should certainly not require many days at all; what matters most is that the preceptor, both before and after the conferral, should shoulder the responsibility of instruction. The Tang Tripiṭaka Master Yijing says: "How can it be that, at the very moment of wishing to receive the precepts, one is utterly exhausted and worn out — and once it is obtained, the precepts are given no further thought, the precept-sutra is not recited, the Vinaya texts are not opened. Occupying a place in the ranks of the Dharma in vain, harming both oneself and others — people of this sort become those by whom the Dharma is destroyed!" The Ouyi Great Master says: "The precepts of a bhikṣu are the great regulation for transcending the world, and it is by means of them that the Jewel of the Sangha is established. What matters is study, cultivation, and practice after reception — it cannot simply be a matter of ascending the platform to go through the motions and fulfill an obligation. For this reason, guidance and encouragement ought to come beforehand, and thorough investigation and clarification requires a full five years' study. But teachers of later generations have become preoccupied with outward show, so that the method of ordinary instruction has become mixed together with the actual sequence of ascending the platform and conferring the precepts; moreover, though the moment of reception may seem solemn and weighty, once it is over people consider the whole matter finished. There is no error more upside-down than this." (The bodhisattva precepts are received separately, and this discussion does not concern them.)

Ru-Yi Translation — Q8

Question: In this Final Dharma Age, the conferral of precepts can no longer be carried out as the Dharma prescribes, so that the precepts are certainly not obtained. I do not know by what further expedient means one might still obtain the full monastic precepts.

Answer: The Ouyi Great Master says: "In this Final Age, if one wishes to obtain genuinely pure precepts, apart from this method of the sign-wheels of the divination [ritual], there is no other way." This refers to the divination-repentance ritual established according to the Sūtra on Divination of the Effects of Good and Evil Karma, spoken by Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva. The Sūtra on Divination says (first briefly showing the outline of the repentance method): "Sentient beings of future ages who wish to go forth from the household life, and those who have already gone forth, if they are unable to find a good and virtuous precept-master and a pure assembly of monastics, and their minds are full of doubt, unable to receive the precepts as the Dharma prescribes — they need only learn to give rise to the mind set upon the unsurpassed path, and thereby cause body, speech, and mind to become pure" (after seven days of performing the repentance, each morning casting the three wheels for body, speech, and mind three times; when all show purely wholesome signs, this is called obtaining the sign of purity). "One who has not yet gone forth should shave the head and don the Dharma robes, and, looking up, address the Buddhas and bodhisattvas of the ten directions, requesting them to serve as preceptor and witness. With single-minded resolve one establishes the vow and recites the marks of the precepts — first speaking the ten root grave precepts of the bodhisattva, and next comprehensively raising the three collections of the bodhisattva's moral discipline, namely the collection of the moral codes (the five, the eight, the ten, the full precepts, and so on), the collection of wholesome dharmas, and the collection of benefiting sentient beings. Receiving these by one's own vow alone, one is then said to have fully obtained the prātimokṣa precepts of one who has gone forth, and is called a bhikṣu or bhikṣuṇī." It was for this reason that the Ouyi Great Master, at the age of thirty-five, stepped back to the status of a novice, and thereafter devoted himself wholly to performing the divination-repentance ritual, until, on the first day of the first month at the age of forty-seven, he obtained the sign-wheels of purity and thereby obtained the full monastic precepts.

Prior to this: considered in terms of having precepts, he stepped back to become a gone-forth upāsaka; his disciples Chengshi and Xingdan likewise received the long-term eight precepts. Considered in terms of having no precepts, he received by self-administered vow the Three Refuges and the Five Precepts, the long-term Eight Precepts, and a partial portion of the bodhisattva precepts. As for the fourth condition for conferring the full monastic precepts — the correspondence of mind and object — either the mind fails to match the object, or the object fails to correspond to the mind, or mind and object both fail to correspond with one another; in every such case, the conferral is not in accordance with the Dharma.

Question: The great masters of the Chan school in ancient times, when dwelling in the mountains, sustained themselves purely on three strips of bamboo and a single hoe; and when leading their communities, made "a day without work is a day without food" their rule of purity — all of which runs contrary to the Vinaya's own regulations. Ru-Yi Translation — Appendix to Q8

The following appears in the source as a set of terse outline notes and a rough diagram of the wooden divination blocks, evidently Hongyi's own working notes appended after Question 8 above; it is translated here as literally as its abbreviated, diagram-like form allows.

The Divination Method. The wooden sign-wheels — marked, for example, "not-killing" on one face — nineteen wheels in all.

The sign-wheels admit of three kinds of distinction: (1) they can show the distinctions among the seeds of good and evil karma created in past lives (simply observing whether the seed of good or evil is present or not); (2) they show the relative strength or weakness of the force of good or evil karma; (3) they comprehensively show the distinctions in the retribution received across the three time-periods.

First: ten wheels in all, inscribed with the names of the ten good and ten evil courses of action — one face inscribed with the good, the opposite face with the evil, so that the two are set against each other, leaving the remaining two faces of each wheel blank; this is what allows the good or the evil to appear or not appear when cast. Second: a wheel marked with the character for "body." Third: a wheel marked with the numeral "one."

When divining, the first two sets of wheels are used: with the first wheel one considers whether the two casts correspond (if both show the same result, one does not cast again; otherwise one casts again); with the second wheel, one takes up only the question that corresponded on the first wheel — if it does not match, one casts again.

The bodhisattva precepts: received by self-administered vow, following the Yogâcāra karman-procedure (the formal karman-procedure first, the marks of the precepts after). The precepts of a bhikṣu or bhikṣuṇī: the karman-procedure is the same as above (one procedure for the bodhisattva precepts, two for the bhikṣu precepts). Question 8 is the hinge on which the whole collection turns, and Hongyi returns to it twice more below — once at length in the next piece, and again near the close of the final lecture. His premise is blunt: by his own account, no ordination performed in his lifetime met the Vinaya's own formal conditions for validly conferring the bhikṣu precepts (a claim he spells out in full in §6 below), so that a genuinely "pure" monastic precept, in the strict canonical sense, could no longer be obtained through ordinary ordination. The alternative he points to — the "sign-wheels of the divination ritual," 占察輪相 — is a physical divination tool from the Sūtra on Divination of the Effects of Good and Evil Karma: a set of small wooden blocks, each face inscribed with a good or evil sign, cast repeatedly over a period of repentance until the results show consistent purity. Ouyi Zhixu himself is the proof case cited throughout this collection: having stepped back from full ordination to novice status at thirty-five out of exactly this concern, he spent twelve years performing the repentance before obtaining, at forty-seven, the "clean sign" he took as confirmation that his precepts were genuinely pure.

The appended notes on the ritual itself are Hongyi's own working outline, not finished prose, and the translation preserves that terse, list-like character rather than smoothing it into continuous narrative. Read together with the closing lecture's treatment of the bodhisattva precepts (§9 below), the method described here — "self-administered reception," 自誓受 — is significant precisely because it requires no preceptor at all: the divination result itself, rather than a living ordained sangha, becomes the guarantee of a validly received precept, which is exactly the workaround Hongyi's generation needed if his diagnosis in the next piece is correct.

6. 徵辨學律義八則 — Eight Points Clarifying and Debating the Meaning of Vinaya Study

Hongyi's most systematic and most startling argument in the collection, laid out as eight linked questions and answers: given that no ordination of his day meets the Vinaya's own six conditions for validly conferring the bhikṣu precepts, is it even proper for the unordained — or the invalidly ordained — to study the bhikṣu Vinaya at all? His answer builds a careful, textually-supported case for a stopgap policy: pursue the divination-repentance path as the real solution, while permitting a defined class of people to keep studying the Vinaya in the meantime, so that the tradition does not go extinct before anyone can genuinely restore it.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The six conditions listed in Point 1 — a properly established boundary, a genuine ordaining monastic, a sufficient quorum, a fully gathered and harmonious assembly, a correctly performed karman-procedure, and complete provisions — are the standard formal requirements for a valid bhikṣu ordination across the Vinaya tradition; Hongyi's claim is simply that, in his assessment, every ordination he had witnessed in his own lifetime failed at least one of them, most likely the second condition, given how directly this connects to the argument of the previous piece that a "true bhikṣu" capable of validly conferring ordination could hardly still be found.

The genuinely delicate move in this piece is the two-track policy of Point 2: pursue the divination-repentance path as the real remedy, while in the meantime permitting a carefully bounded class of people — specifically, those who received precepts in good faith but did not validly obtain them — to keep studying the bhikṣu Vinaya, so the tradition survives long enough for anyone to restore it properly. Hongyi is explicit, in Point 8, that this is a temporary and self-limiting concession: once genuine ordinations become possible again, the whole workaround should simply lapse, and no one lacking the precepts at all — as opposed to someone who received them invalidly — should be encouraged to study the Vinaya under this reasoning. The technical term 賊住 ("thief-dweller"), at stake through much of the middle points, denotes someone who fraudulently poses as ordained without ever having received ordination at all; Hongyi's argument throughout is that a person in his own community's situation, who received the precepts sincerely though invalidly, is a fundamentally different case from that offense, however similar the two might look from the outside.

Measured against this clear textual precedent: unless there is a genuine difficulty of this kind, group reception should not be adopted.

Fifth: when receiving the Refuges and precepts, the one conferring them speaks the words, and the one receiving them repeats them after him. This was the sole method in the Western lands [India]. (This compilation follows exclusively the Sarvāstivāda school, and differs somewhat in places from the accounts given in other Vinaya traditions and treatises; students should not, on this account, doubt or disparage those other traditions, since each is suited to a different capacity, and all alike accord with the sacred teaching.)

Sixth: various other sutras and treatises state that one who is unable to receive the five precepts in full may receive one portion or two portions of them. Yet according to the Sarvāstivāda-vinaya-vibhāṣā: "One who does not receive the precepts in full does not obtain the precept at all. It states: Question — when one generally receives the upāsaka precepts, if one is unable to receive all five precepts, but receives one precept, or up to four, does one thereby obtain the precept? Answer: One does not. If this is so, then how is one to understand the sutras that speak of a 'minor-portion upāsaka,' a 'major-portion upāsaka,' and a 'full-portion upāsaka'? Answer: The reason such things are said is to clarify the relative degree of merit in upholding the precepts — it does not mean that there exists such a thing as a mode of receiving the precepts in fractional portions." Ouyi likewise says: "As for receiving four-fifths, three-fifths, or some other fraction — since this is not a full reception, it can only be counted among the merit-generating deeds that transcend the world, but cannot be called the study of the precepts." Weighing this consideration: a person today who wishes to receive the precepts should honestly assess his own capacity. If he truly judges his strength weak and his resolve timid, unable to sustain the distance, there is no harm in his first receiving one portion, or up to four; but otherwise, he should receive and uphold them in full, so that this may rightly be called the study of the precepts. How could one properly, out of fear of difficulty, forfeit this superior benefit?

Seventh: people today, when asking a master to certify their reception of the Refuges, habitually say they have "taken refuge in" such-and-such a master. This popular custom has been handed down and imitated without correction. Checking the name against the reality, it is considerably unsatisfactory, since what one takes refuge in is the Sangha, not merely a single master. Ouyi says: "To take refuge in the Sangha means that all monastics are one's teachers. Yet worldly people today select one renowned and virtuous bhikṣu to revere and serve, and privately boast, 'I am the disciple of such-and-such a spiritual guide, such-and-such a Dharma master!' And that spiritual guide or Dharma master likewise privately boasts, 'Such-and-such a layman, such-and-such an official, has taken refuge with me!' Alas! If it truly comes to this, then one ought simply to say, 'I take refuge in the Buddha, I take refuge in the Dharma, and I have made the acquaintance of one great worthy' — how could this be called taking refuge in the Sangha at all!" Hence one who has already received the Refuges ought, toward the whole assembly of monastics, whether worthy or foolish, to revere and honor every one of them as a teacher, and call oneself their disciple — one must not, out of arrogance, make arbitrary distinctions among them.

Eighth: people today, having just received the five precepts, at once take to wearing the five-panel robe and carrying a sitting-cloth, corrupting and confusing the proper regulations, and incurring an offense whose extent is hard to measure. According to the Buddha's own Vinaya regulation, only after one has gone forth from the household and had the head shaved is one given the plain, unpatterned robe. As for the five-panel robe, only a fully-ordained monastic is permitted to wear it. For laypeople today to confuse themselves with fully-ordained monastics in this way is deeply improper. (The Vaipulya-dhāraṇī Sutra says: "When the two lay assemblies enter the platform to carry out the rite, they wear the seamless three robes" — "seamless" meaning precisely the plain, unpatterned robe, not the five-panel robe. The Satyasiddhi-śāstra also says: "It is permitted to keep one repentance-robe, called a bota" — the bota being precisely the plain, unpatterned robe. Judging by the sutras and treatises, wearing the plain, unpatterned robe may indeed be permitted; but there is no explicit textual warrant for this within the Vinaya canon itself, so it is better still not to wear it.) As for the sitting-cloth: in Sanskrit it is called niṣīdana, formerly transliterated nishitan, and rendered in Chinese as "sitting-cloth" or "lying-cloth." It is used solely by fully-ordained monastics, spread beneath a rug or mat to guard it against soiling. That in this land it has come to be spread out for prostration is a matter of mistaken transmission. Even a fully-ordained monastic who uses it this way already departs from the sacred teaching — how much more disordered a matter it is for ordinary laypeople to do so! (The Tripiṭaka Master Yijing says: "The niṣīdana was originally intended to protect one's bedding, for fear of its being damaged, and was never meant for any other use. There is no textual warrant at all for spreading it on the ground for prostration; whoever violates the sacred word in this way must bear the offense himself.")

Ninth: once the precepts have been received, if one commits an offense of the upper grade, it cannot be repented away at all. If one commits an offense of the middle or lower grade, all of these fall within what can be repented. One should, in accordance with the Vinaya's regulation, disclose and confess the offense before the assembly of monastics, and only then can the offense be dissolved. How could one idly discourse on ultimate reality while making light of the formal procedure of confession? Ouyi says: "To confess an offense without contemplating the mind can still, at least, cut off the stream of that offense; but to discourse on principle without ever actually disclosing it makes it truly difficult to purify the offense at its source. If one insists on feeling ashamed of the formal procedure and refuses to carry it out, this is simply cherishing one's own reputation, concealing and covering things up out of endurance, and utterly failing to understand that the nature of an offense is fundamentally empty — how could this be called the sun of wisdom!" He also says: "When people are in the very act of committing an offense, it is truly a great evil, yet they feel no shame at all; but when it comes to disclosing it before others — the very best among good deeds — they instead feel ashamed of that. To be content with evil and yet pained by goodness is to become the very worst kind of evil, with never any prospect of release — there is no greater confusion or folly than this." I have now, at the end of this compilation, following the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, settled upon a fixed text for the disclosure of offenses. When this is used, one may not embellish it with additional ceremonial phrases, nor add to or subtract from its wording. This is the sacred regulation, and it must not be presumptuously altered.

Tenth: since the Final Dharma Age began, those receiving the Refuges and precepts have mostly followed the Correct Model for the Three Refuges and Five Precepts from Huashan [Baohua Mountain]. Skillfully accommodating the capacities of the age is that text's own strength; unfortunately, its ceremonial text tends toward excessive elaboration. Ouyi's own rite for receiving the Three Refuges and Five Precepts is generally recognized as concise and essential, yet those who actually follow it are few — this is truly a matter for regret! (Chen Xiyuan holds that this method is intended only for the receiver to speak the vow himself, and so gives only an abbreviated record of it — while, if received before a preceptor, one should still follow Huashan. Examining this claim carefully, it is in fact a matter of mere conjecture; the formal rite for the precepts should follow the sacred teaching. To take one's own abbreviated record and set it in opposition to a different tradition with its own different regulation, favoring elaborate ceremony instead — how could this possibly be acceptable, right and wrong so thoroughly confounded! Fearing that others might retain this earlier confusion, I venture once more to clarify and set the matter straight.) This present compilation follows exclusively the Sarvāstivāda tradition (in full, the Mūlasarvāstivāda. The Tang Tripiṭaka Dharma Master Yijing studied in India for more than twenty years, devoting himself exclusively to this school; since his return to China he translated and transmitted the Vinaya texts of this school, nineteen works in all, close to two hundred fascicles — precise, detailed, and clear, and known to the world as the "New Vinaya"). Taking that Vinaya text as authority, the reception-rite given here is simple, direct, and clear, running to no more than a few lines. This was the manner handed down in the Western lands, and all followed this same regulation; I do not say this out of mere attachment to antiquity, but simply to present one example — whether it is to be used or not, I leave entirely to worthy successors!

Point 2's quotation from Yuan Zhao — "entering the path must have a beginning... the resolve of the mind must have an end" — is the same passage translated in full, from the same source, in the Nanshan Vinaya for Laypeople series elsewhere on this site (Gate 3, "Keeping What Was Received"); its appearance here, prefacing a working ordination manual rather than a doctrinal commentary, shows how directly Hongyi's practical instructions for laypeople were built on the same architecture as his larger scholarly project.

8. 佛說無常經敘 — Preface to the Sūtra on Impermanence

A change of register: this is not a Vinaya text at all, but a preface Hongyi wrote in 1920 for a different sutra entirely — a short, largely forgotten scripture on impermanence that he encountered while studying the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya and pressed a friend to have reprinted. The preface itself sets out seven distinct benefits of the sutra, each supported by a footnote of textual citation; those seven footnotes are translated in full below the main preface.

Ru-Yi Translation — Preface

In the summer of the gengshen year [1920], I was dwelling at the Yuquan hermitage in Qiantang, studying the Vinaya of the Mūlasarvāstivāda school, and there found several passages concerning the practice of chanting the Threefold Sūtra on Impermanence. The Mūlasarvāstivāda Compendium, fascicle seven, says: "The Buddha said: 'If a bhikṣu attends at any of the five occasions, he should be given a share of the benefit. What are the five? First, the striking of the wooden gong; second, the chanting of the Threefold Sūtra on Impermanence; third, the veneration of a caitya; fourth, the passing of counting-sticks; fifth, the reading of a formal announcement.'" Several further passages are noted separately below. I also read Yijing's Record of the Inner Law Sent Home from the Southern Seas, which records the rite for chanting the Threefold Sūtra on Impermanence in the greatest detail (note 1). From this I came to know that this sutra was one regularly recited by the great disciples in the Buddha's own time, and was perhaps even used as a daily practice. The sutra was translated in the Tang dynasty, but at that time it had not yet spread widely, and those who recited it were few indeed (note 2). From the Song and Yuan dynasties onward, no one at all has spoken of it. Fearing that it might sink into obscurity and cease to be transmitted, I wrote to my good friend the layman Ding, urging and requesting that it be circulated. The layman received this with delight and asked me to write a preface for it. I have humbly considered that, were this sutra to circulate in the world, its benefit would be most far-reaching, and I wish briefly to set this out.

The sutra repeatedly speaks of the three conditions of old age, sickness, and death as unlovable, without lustre, not to be dwelt upon, and unsatisfying. One who recites this sutra will feel impermanence to the very core and press forward diligently toward the path — this is its first benefit. The main text of the sutra runs to no more than three hundred characters or so, and with the added verses comes to little more than a thousand — concise in wording yet rich in meaning, and easy to hold in mind and recite — this is its second benefit. This preface stands apart from the rest of the collection — it concerns not Hongyi's Vinaya scholarship directly, but a small act of textual rescue that grew out of it. While studying the Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya he kept running across incidental references to a short, largely forgotten sutra on impermanence, traditionally associated with Aśvaghoṣa's threefold recitation format described at length in note 1; concerned that it might disappear from circulation entirely, he wrote to the layman Ding to have it reprinted, and supplied this preface. The date given at the close, the second day of the seventh month of 1920, falls the day after he chanted the sutra himself, on the eve of felling trees to build the retreat dwelling he mentions — which is also why the seventh benefit he lists concerns precisely the ritual proper to tree-felling, translated in full in note 7 above.

The elephant story in note 4, concerning the bhikṣu Shanhe, is offered as textual evidence for the third benefit — that the Buddha permitted this one sutra alone to be chanted in an actual singing tone, rather than the flat monotone otherwise prescribed for recitation — and Hongyi clearly intends its vividness to make the case for the sutra's beauty more persuasively than argument could.

Anyone who would be a bhikṣu should first know the causes and conditions by which the Vinaya was transmitted into this land, and the general outline of the rise and decline of the Vinaya school here, from ancient times to now. From the Eastern Han through the beginning of the Cao-Wei period, monastics had no rite of refuge and precepts at all — they merely shaved the head, nothing more. In the Jiaping era of Wei, the Indian monk Dharmakāla [Fashi] arrived in this land, and thereupon established the karman-procedure for conferring the precepts — this was the beginning of the Vinaya here. That moment may be reckoned the true beginning of the conferral of the bhikṣu precepts, and from it things gradually reached a flourishing period.

Among the great, extensive Vinaya-texts, the first to be transmitted was the Daśabhāṇavāra Vinaya [Ten Recitations Vinaya], translated by Dharma Master Kumārajīva in the Yao Qin period; the first patriarch of the Pure Land tradition at Mount Lu, Master Huiyuan, also exerted himself to urge and praise it. In the Six Dynasties period this Vinaya flourished most in the south. Next translated was the Four-Part Vinaya, at a time not far removed from the Ten Recitations Vinaya, though it was not until the Sui dynasty that anyone propagated and promoted it, and it did not truly flourish until the early Tang. The third was the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya, translated in the Eastern Jin, with some propagation in the north during the Six Dynasties period. Following the Mahāsāṃghika Vinaya in the Liu Song came the Mahīśāsaka Vinaya, translated by the very same person who translated the sixty-fascicle Avataṃsaka Sutra — refined and concise in its wording, and greatly praised by Vinaya Master Dao Xuan, though unfortunately few propagated it. After this came the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, the translation-work of Dharma Master Yijing in the time of Empress Wu Zetian of the Tang, and the very Vinaya most current in the Tibetan regions. Dharma Master Yijing had spent more than twenty years in India; broadly learned and possessed of a powerful memory, he penetrated the subtleties of Vinaya study in a way no other monastic who reached India could match — truly the greatest Chinese Vinaya master before or since. Once Yijing returned to China and completed his translation work, he was already advanced in years, and passed into nirvāṇa not long after; thereafter no one propagated this Vinaya — what a pity, what a pity! Besides these there are a great many other Vinaya texts and treatises, too numerous to list here.

As for the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, I myself, upon first encountering it, was greatly delighted, and studied it for many years; afterward, on the advice of friends, I changed to studying the Nanshan Vinaya instead, the reason being that the Nanshan Vinaya is based on the Four-Part Vinaya, with certain further adaptations, and is able to suit the capacities of monastics in our own country. I will now speak briefly, specifically, about the history of the Four-Part Vinaya.

The Tang dynasty was the period when the Four-Part Vinaya flourished most. Before that, what was propagated was the Ten Recitations Vinaya, and few propagated the Four-Part Vinaya; by the early Tang, scholars of the Four-Part Vinaya had come to flourish, forming three great schools in all: first, the Xiangbu school, centered on Vinaya Master Faji; second, the Nanshan school, centered on Vinaya Master Dao Xuan; third, the Dongta school, centered on Vinaya Master Huaisu. Vinaya Master Faji preceded Dao Xuan, and Dao Xuan once studied under him; Vinaya Master Huaisu came after Dao Xuan, and also studied closely with both Faji and Dao Xuan. Although the Vinaya was thus divided into three great schools, the one that flourished most widely in the world may be reckoned the Nanshan school. The writings of the Nanshan Vinaya master are as vast as an ocean; among them the Procedural Commentary is the most renowned of all — at that time, scholars of every school without exception had to study the Procedural Commentary. From the Tang to the Song, more than sixty scholars wrote commentaries upon it, among whom Vinaya Master Yuan Zhao of Lingzhi was the finest; Yuan Zhao also left many other commentaries on sutras and Vinaya texts. After Yuan Zhao, Vinaya studies gradually sank into decline, and few gave rise to the resolve to propagate them.

After the Southern Song, as the Chan school flourished all the more, still fewer people paid any attention to Vinaya studies at all, and the several thousand fascicles of Vinaya writings by the various Tang and Song masters were entirely scattered and lost; by the early Qing, only a single fascicle — the Nanshan Karman-Procedures for Various Occasions — still survived. Considering this, one cannot help but sigh at great length! At the end of the Ming and the beginning of the Qing, great masters such as Ouyi and Jianyue wished to revive the Vinaya school anew, but the most regrettable thing was that the ancient books of the Tang and Song could no longer be seen. At that time the Ouyi Great Master wrote the Collected Essentials of Vinaya Matters; at his first lectures the audience was already small, and afterward smaller still — in the end the results came to little. Vinaya Master Jianyue achieved rather more in propagating the Vinaya, and wrote a great deal, including a commentary on the Karman-Procedures for Various Occasions and the Vinaya Practices [Piniさ Zuochi], which differ considerably from the Nanshan tradition, precisely because he was unable to see the Nanshan writings! Besides this there is also the most renowned work of all, the Correct Model for Transmitting the Precepts — from the end of the Ming down to today, this remains the sole work on transmitting the precepts, the one last ray of dawn on which the transmission of the precepts still depends; although it does not entirely agree with the Nanshan writings, its merit is very great, and it should not be regarded lightly. But the ordination rites of recent times have again been somewhat added to and subtracted from on the basis of this book, so that they are no longer even the original face of Vinaya Master Jianyue's own Correct Model for Transmitting the Precepts.

From the Southern Song to the Qing, over more than seven hundred years, one may say that virtually nothing survived of the Vinaya writings of the various Tang and Song masters; only at the very end of the Guangxu era of the Qing were a portion of these Tang and Song Vinaya texts sent back from Japan, and over the past decade or so, several hundred fascicles have already been printed in Tianjin. Besides this, several hundred more fascicles, collected in the Supplementary Canon, still remain unprinted separately.

If, from now on, there are those who give rise to the resolve to devote themselves single-mindedly to studying and propagating this, it will be possible to restore the ancient manner of the Tang dynasty — everything that Ouyi, Jianyue, and the others wished to see is now entirely available to us. We who live in this present age are, in truth, far more fortunate than the great masters Ouyi and Jianyue ever were.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This history is worth reading as Hongyi's own explanation for why the Nanshan Vinaya for Laypeople exists at all: the Tang and Song Vinaya commentaries he draws on throughout that later compilation had been lost in China for roughly seven centuries, surviving only in Japan, and were sent back and printed in Tianjin only in the decade or so before this lecture was delivered — the very printing project credited to the layman Xu Weiru in the opening piece of this collection. Hongyi's closing claim that his own generation is "more fortunate than Ouyi and Jianyue" is not false modesty about those two Ming-Qing revivalists; it is a precise historical judgment — they wanted to restore the Nanshan tradition but could not physically access its central texts, while Hongyi's generation, for the first time in centuries, finally could.

The names introduced here recur throughout this collection and the wider Nanshan Lu series translated elsewhere on this site: Dao Xuan and Yuan Zhao of Lingzhi are the two patriarchs whose commentaries form the core of that translated work; Ouyi Zhixu is quoted more often than any other authority across this entire anthology; and the Correct Model for Transmitting the Precepts, compiled by the Vinaya Master Jianyue, is identified here as the single surviving liturgical basis for nearly all ordinations performed in China from the Ming dynasty down to Hongyi's own day — which is precisely the institution whose formal adequacy Hongyi goes on to question later in this same lecture.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Hongyi never uses the paired terms 化教 ("the teachings of transformation") and 制教 ("the regulatory teaching") in this lecture by name, but the distinction he is drawing here is exactly that pairing at work: a precept's Vinaya classification (which formal category it belongs to, how it is technically conferred) is one axis, while the inward resolve with which someone receives it — whether merely to restrain oneself, or with the full altruistic bodhisattva aspiration — is an entirely different axis, and the two do not automatically track each other. His example is pointed: nothing in the formal content of the Five Precepts is "Hīnayāna," yet nothing prevents a person from receiving them with a narrowly self-regarding intention rather than the "great Mahāyāna mind" he says the Nanshan tradition requires of every reception, of any precept, without exception.

The examples he reaches for — Ouyi Zhixu himself stepping back from full ordination to become "merely a person of the Three Refuges," his disciples Chengshi and Xingdan styling themselves "gone-forth upāsakas" despite never advancing past the Five Precepts — are not incidental biography. They are Hongyi building, piece by piece, toward the argument he makes explicitly later in this same lecture (see "The Full Monastic Precepts" below): that in an age when the bhikṣu precepts may not be validly obtainable at all, the lower rungs of this same ladder — refuge, and the five lay precepts — remain fully available, fully dignified, and, in his own words elsewhere in this collection, perhaps the only precepts any of his listeners could actually expect to obtain.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Three Refuges: these do not belong to the category of "precept" at all — they are simply called the Three Refuges. The Three Refuges are: taking refuge in the Buddha, taking refuge in the Dharma, and taking refuge in the Sangha. Before receiving them, one absolutely must understand the reasoning behind the Three Refuges — it is by no means a matter of blindly and confusedly following along without understanding; if it is done that way, the Three Refuges are simply not obtained at all.

The "Three Jewels" are said to admit of four kinds of distinction: first, the Three Jewels of principle-substance; second, the Three Jewels of manifest form; third, the Three Jewels as sustained in the world; fourth, the Three Jewels as a single essence. To explain all of this in full would be very deep and complicated, so for now let me speak specifically of the Three Jewels as sustained in the world. What is the meaning of the Three Jewels — Buddha, Dharma, Sangha? The Buddha means an image — such as an image of Śākyamuni, of Bhaiṣajyaguru, of Amitābha, and so on; the Dharma means the sutras spoken by the Buddha — such as the Lotus Sutra, the Śūraṅgama Sutra, and so on, all of it Dharma that flowed out from the Buddha's own golden mouth; the Sangha means those who have gone forth from the household, shaved the head, received the precepts, and possess proper deportment. What has been said above about the meaning of Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha may be called the most basic level, and I trust all of you can understand it clearly enough.

"Taking refuge" carries the meaning of "turning back" — because one previously turned away from and abandoned the Three Jewels, and now turns back toward the Three Jewels, this is called "taking refuge." But whether a person is gone-forth or lay, when receiving the Three Refuges, there are two most important points. First, one must pay attention to what exactly it means to take refuge in the Three Jewels. Second, at the time of receiving the Three Refuges, what the preceptor says should be entirely clear — if what the preceptor speaks is all in classical, literary language that cannot be understood, then in that case the Three Refuges are certainly not obtained; or if one is too far away to hear clearly, the Three Refuges are likewise not obtained; or even if one can hear and generally understand, but there remain one or two points of doubt, the Three Refuges are likewise not obtained. Moreover, at the moment of actual conferral, it is precisely the three utterances "I take refuge in the Buddha," "I take refuge in the Dharma," "I take refuge in the Sangha" that matter most, and to which the closest attention must be paid; what follows afterward — "refuge in the Buddha is complete," "refuge in the Dharma is complete," "refuge in the Sangha is complete" — is called the "three concluding statements," and is of no great consequence. Hence, when all of you give rise to the resolve to receive the precepts, you should first come to understand the meaning of the Three Refuges; and at the moment of actual conferral, your attention should be fixed above all on those first three utterances, "I take refuge in the Buddha" and so on — only then can the Three Refuges truly be obtained.

The Eight Precepts — in full, the Eight Precepts of the Fast. "Fast" means to close off and restrain wrongdoing, shutting the door on every unwholesome act; "purity" means purity itself, cutting off every kind of scattered, extraneous thought. The Eight Precepts of the Fast originally comprised nine items, but because the seventh item actually combines two, the total comes to eight. The first five are the same as the Five Precepts; the remaining three are additional. The additional three are: sixth, refraining from flower-garlands, necklaces, and perfumed oil applied to the body — this was originally an Indian custom of beautiful adornment; in our own country there are only flowers and fragrance, no necklaces and the like, but "fragrance" here should be understood to include our own country's scented powders, perfumes, scented tooth-powder, toothpaste, soap, and so on — none of these may be used either.

Seventh, refraining from sitting on a high, luxurious seat, and from going purposely to watch or listen to song and performance — this is actually two items combined into one; let me briefly analyze it. "High" refers to the Buddha's own regulation that the legs of a seat or bed must never exceed one chi six cun [roughly half a meter] in height; "luxurious" refers to ornamentation in gold, silver, ivory, horn, and the like — none of this is permitted either. But where, in some other place, it truly cannot be avoided, a brief sitting may be allowed as an exception: the Buddha's regulation treats making such a seat oneself as the properly graded offense, whereas merely using one already made by someone else, not made by oneself, is a somewhat lighter offense. As for going purposely to watch or listen to song and performance, this covers music, film, and the like; the key word here is "purposely" — encountering such things unintentionally, by chance, is not a transgression. The above — sitting on a high, luxurious seat, and going purposely to watch or listen to performance — together form a single item, and one who has received the Eight Precepts of the Fast must not do either.

Eighth, not eating after midday. The Buddha's regulation is that, after receiving the Eight Precepts of the Fast, one may eat from dawn until noon; should one eat past that time, this is called "eating at the wrong time" — what is ordinarily called "not eating after midday." But after noon, it is not merely rice and the like that may not be eaten — milk, fruit, and similar items likewise may not be taken. In the case of serious illness, when truly unavoidable, one may eat gruel and the like in a place where others cannot see.

The Eight Precepts of the Fast are ordinarily received on the six fast-days: the eighth, fourteenth, fifteenth, and twenty-third days of the lunar month, and the last two days of the month; should one be able to give rise to the resolve to receive them every single day, that would be best of all. They should be received at the moment of rising each morning, for a period of one day and one night — from dawn until night, and from night until the next dawn. Once the Eight Precepts of the Fast are received, the single item of not eating after midday should be observed without exception from noon of that day until dawn of the next. There is a further distinction between the Eight Precepts and the bodhisattva precepts on one hand, and the other precepts on the other, in that both the Eight Precepts and the bodhisattva precepts are "precepts established all at once" (the bodhisattva precepts spoken of just above are confined specifically to the account given in the Brahma Net Sutra and the Sūtra of the [Bodhisattva's] Original Acts Adorned with Necklaces; according to the Yogâcārabhūmi's own precept-text, they would instead belong to the category of "precepts established gradually"). What is the reason for this? One who has not received the Five Precepts, the novice precepts, or the bhikṣu precepts may nonetheless receive the bodhisattva precepts or the Eight Precepts directly — hence they are called "established all at once"; whereas precepts "established gradually" must necessarily follow a fixed order — first the Five Precepts, next the novice precepts, next the bhikṣu precepts, advancing rung by rung. As for the Eight Precepts of the Fast described above, lay Buddhists elsewhere in the country receive them in very great numbers; I think this practice ought in future to be promoted here in southern Fujian as well! If six days a month seems too many, there is no harm at all in reducing this to one or two days — for even receiving them a single day already carries very great merit, how much more so if all six days are kept!

This is the argument the whole collection has been building toward, stated here in its most direct and personal form: no valid ordination is possible without a genuine bhikṣu to confer it, Hongyi judges that no such person can reliably be found anywhere in China, and therefore the precepts conferred at ordinations like the one his audience is attending — including, by his own frank admission earlier in this lecture, his own — are not actually obtained in the strict canonical sense. His "impersonating a military officer" analogy is doing real work here, not just rhetorical color: the danger he is warning against is not merely disappointment but the specific, serious offense of falsely claiming a status one does not hold, which is exactly the technical concern (賊住, "thief-dweller") examined at length in the Eight Points piece above.

What makes this passage remarkable is that Hongyi does not conclude from any of this that the ceremony is pointless. His counsel is genuinely two-layered: attend and receive the precepts anyway, honestly understanding that this plants good roots, builds proper monastic deportment, and secures the nominal standing needed to study the Vinaya without reproach — while, for anyone who wants the real thing, pointing to the one path he believes actually works, the divination-repentance rite that Ouyi Zhixu himself completed. The phrase "bodhisattva-bhikṣu" at the end of this passage names the specific outcome: since the sign-wheels of purity open the way to self-administered reception of the bodhisattva precepts, and those precepts already subsume the novice and bhikṣu precepts within their own three collections, a successful practitioner of this method obtains monastic precepts through the bodhisattva vow rather than through ordinary ordination at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Bodhisattva Precepts: because of time, I cannot explain these in detail either. Let me raise just three points briefly. First: one must possess the bodhisattva lineage-nature, and must also be able to give rise to the bodhi-mind, before one can receive the bodhisattva precepts. What is this "lineage-nature"? Simply put, it is the qualification one has already accomplished over many past lifetimes. Hence, at the time of reception, the precept-master asks: "Are you a bodhisattva?" — and one must answer, "I am a bodhisattva!" This is the bodhisattva lineage-nature. The precept-master then asks further: "Since you are a bodhisattva, have you already given rise to the bodhi-mind?" — and one must answer, "I have already given rise to the bodhi-mind." This is the giving rise of the bodhi-mind. Only in this way can the bodhisattva precepts be received. Second: ordinarily, those who receive the bodhisattva precepts receive them all in full; but according to the Sūtra of the [Bodhisattva's] Original Acts Adorned with Necklaces, one may instead receive them according to one's own capacity, one item or several — the same as what was said above concerning the reception of the Five Precepts. Third: the relative gravity of transgression differs considerably between the old and new sub-commentaries, and one should act according to one's own actual capacity; to give an example — regarding the precept against false speech, the old sub-commentary holds that only "great" false speech constitutes a pārājika offense, while the new sub-commentary holds that even "minor" false speech already constitutes a pārājika offense. As for the mere arising of the intention to kill, steal, engage in sexual misconduct, or speak falsely already constituting a pārājika offense — this applies specifically to bodhisattvas who have reached the [bodhisattva] grounds; we ordinary, unenlightened beings are simply incapable of meeting that standard.

Although the bodhisattva precepts are said to be difficult to obtain, if one has a truly sincere mind, this is in fact not so hard a thing; and moreover they may be received by self-administered vow alone, unlike the novice and bhikṣu precepts, which absolutely require someone else to confer them — because the bodhisattva precepts, like the Five Precepts and the Eight Precepts, may all be received by self-administered vow. So we do, in fact, have real hope of obtaining the bodhisattva precepts!

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The "lineage-nature" Hongyi describes is not something a person can acquire in the present moment by effort alone — it is presented as a pre-existing capacity built up over many past lifetimes, which reception merely recognizes and formalizes through the precept-master's ritual question and answer. Combined with the bodhi-mind, an intention actively aimed at the awakening of all beings rather than oneself alone, these are the two gates through which the bodhisattva precepts specifically become available.

The final paragraph explains why this lecture, after two sections of genuinely discouraging news about the novice, bhikṣu, and bhikṣuṇī precepts, ends on real hope rather than resignation: because the bodhisattva precepts can be received by self-administered vow — no living preceptor required at all — they sidestep exactly the structural problem, the absence of a genuine ordained sangha, that Hongyi has just spent two sections explaining. Read together with the divination-ritual method described in the previous section, this is the same door twice: the sign-wheels of purity lead to self-administered bodhisattva ordination, and the bodhisattva precepts can also, independently, simply be vowed outright by anyone with a sincere mind — which is why Hongyi can close this otherwise sobering survey by telling his audience they have "real hope" after all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Hongyi does not name the year of this lecture in the text itself, but the internal references situate it precisely: he mentions "this year's" repeated natural disasters affecting Quanzhou, and refers back to the Great Kaiyuan Temple's own precept-period lectures as having taken place "a few years" earlier. Read alongside the dated 1933 group vow translated above (§3) and Hongyi's own remark elsewhere in this lecture that he has by then studied the Vinaya for "close to twenty years," this places the lecture in Hongyi's later years in southern Fujian, among the monastic communities — Kaiyuan Temple and its neighbors — where he spent much of the final decade of his life.

The warmth of these closing lines is worth reading against the difficulty of everything just before them: having told his audience, as gently as he could manage, that the precepts they were about to receive likely could not be validly obtained in the strict canonical sense, Hongyi closes not with further qualification but with unreserved praise for the sincerity and thoroughness of the community hosting the ceremony — a reminder that, throughout this entire lecture, his target was never the good faith of the people in front of him, but the state of the institution they had inherited.
