The Eight Realizations of Great Beings

The shortest of the three sūtras traditionally handed to anyone beginning to study Buddhism in China — barely more than a page, meant to be recited whole every morning and evening. In eight short paragraphs it moves from a first, unflinching look at impermanence and no-self through desire, contentment, and diligence, to a final vow no earlier chapter prepares a reader for: not just to be free of suffering oneself, but to take on other beings' suffering in their place. Traditionally attributed to the 2nd-century Parthian translator An Shigao, and read here with the Ming-dynasty monk Ouyi Zhixu's own commentary.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete Source: Taishō T0779, 佛說八大人覺經 (attrib. An Shigao, 2nd c. CE) Reading notes after Ouyi Zhixu's 八大人覺經略解 (X0672)

Translation Method

The Chinese source is traditionally attributed to An Shigao (安世高), a Parthian prince turned monk who reached the Han capital of Luoyang around 148 CE and became one of the very first translators of Buddhist texts into Chinese — his name appears on this sūtra's own title line as "an Arsacid (Parthian) monk of the Later Han." As with the Sūtra of Forty-Two Chapters, modern scholarship treats this early attribution with some caution, and no Sanskrit or other Indic original has surfaced; the text's own opening line — "for a disciple of the Buddha, day and night, with a wholehearted mind, to recite and hold in mind the eight realizations of great beings" — reads less like the record of a single sermon than a distilled summary meant from the start for memorization and daily use, a genre distinct from, though related to, the forty-two-chapter digest translated on this site's companion page.

Reading notes throughout draw on Ouyi Zhixu (蕅益智旭, 1599–1655), one of the "Four Great Masters" of the late Ming dynasty and already a commentator this site has drawn on elsewhere, for the Amitābha Sūtra's own reading notes. His short commentary on this text, the Ba Daren Jue Jing Lüe Jie (八大人覺經略解, "A Brief Explanation of the Sūtra of the Eight Realizations of Great Beings," X0672), matches this sūtra's own extreme concision with an equally economical gloss — never more than a sentence or two per realization, in keeping with a text meant to be held whole in the mind rather than studied at length. Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right. See also this site's note on translation method.

Impermanence, Desire, and the Root of Suffering

Chinese Source

為佛弟子，常於晝夜，至心誦念，八大人覺。

第一覺悟：世間無常，國土危脆；四大苦空，五陰無我；生滅變異，虛偽無主；心是惡源，形為罪藪。如是觀察，漸離生死。

第二覺知：多欲為苦，生死疲勞，從貪欲起；少欲無為，身心自在。

第三覺知：心無厭足，唯得多求，增長罪惡；菩薩不爾，常念知足，安貧守道，唯慧是業。

Ru-Yi Translation

For a disciple of the Buddha, day and night, with a wholehearted mind, to recite and hold in mind the eight realizations of great beings:

The first realization: the world is impermanent; every land within it is fragile and precarious; the four elements are suffering and empty; the five aggregates are without any self; all things arise, cease, and change, false and without any true master governing them; the mind is the very wellspring of every evil, and the body the very den where every offense gathers. Observing this in just this way, one gradually leaves birth and death behind.

The second realization: many desires bring suffering; the weariness of birth and death arises from craving and desire alone. Having few desires, free of any contrived striving, brings ease of body and mind alike.

The third realization: a mind never satisfied only draws more and more to itself, and thereby increases its own offenses further still. A bodhisattva is not so — always holding contentment in mind, at peace even in poverty, upholding the way, taking wisdom alone as their true work.

Reading note — after Ouyi Zhixu

Ouyi reads the first realization as "the very first gate of entry to the path, the front line against the grasping at self and grasping at phenomena both at once" — contemplating first the impermanence of the outer world (nations rising and falling like mountain peaks becoming valleys), then the four elements composing the body (earth, water, fire, and wind constantly at odds with one another, the source of the body's four hundred and four named ailments), and finally the five aggregates composing the mind itself, in which "no self, and nothing belonging to a self, is genuinely to be found anywhere." His gloss on "the mind is the very wellspring of every evil" is unsparing: merely refraining from clinging to this body and mind does no good on its own, since "mistaking the six sense-objects' own fleeting shadow for one's real mind makes the mind itself the wellspring of every evil; mistaking the four elements for one's real body makes the body itself the den where every offense gathers" — the ordinary, unexamined sense of having a mind and a body is not neutral raw material waiting to be used well, but already the specific mechanism generating harm, until seen through directly.

Diligence, Learning, and Generosity

Chinese Source

第四覺知：懈怠墜落；常行精進，破煩惱惡，摧伏四魔，出陰界獄。

第五覺悟：愚癡生死。菩薩常念，廣學多聞，增長智慧，成就辯才，教化一切，悉以大樂。

第六覺知：貧苦多怨，橫結惡緣。菩薩布施，等念冤親，不念舊惡，不憎惡人。

第七覺悟：五欲過患。雖為俗人，不染世樂；常念三衣、瓦鉢、法器；志願出家，守道清白；梵行高遠，慈悲一切。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fourth realization: laziness leads only to downfall. Constantly practicing diligence, one breaks through the evil of affliction, subdues the four Māras, and escapes the prison of the five aggregates and the eighteen elements.

The fifth realization: ignorance itself is what gives rise to birth and death. A bodhisattva constantly holds in mind broad learning and extensive study, thereby increasing wisdom and perfecting eloquence, so as to teach and transform every being, bringing them all to great joy.

The sixth realization: poverty and hardship breed much resentment, tying beings by chance into evil bonds with one another. A bodhisattva therefore practices giving, regarding friend and foe with an equal mind, holding no memory of old wrongs, and hating no one, however evil they may be.

The seventh realization: the five sense-pleasures bring only trouble and harm. Though remaining, for now, an ordinary layperson, one is not stained by the world's own pleasures; constantly holding in mind the three robes and clay begging bowl of a monastic, one aspires and vows to leave the household life, to uphold the way in purity, one's pure conduct reaching far and wide, with compassion extended toward every being.

Reading note — after Ouyi Zhixu

Ouyi is direct about how the four Māras named in the fourth realization work in practice: "the affliction-Māra, once broken, causes the aggregate-Māra, the death-Māra, and the heavenly Māra all to be subdued together" — one root cause standing behind what look like three separate obstacles. On the fifth realization's pairing of "broad learning" with wisdom, he adds a caution worth taking seriously on its own: "learning without wisdom is like a person setting themselves alight while carrying a torch; wisdom without learning is like cutting oneself while gripping a blade — only when learning and wisdom are both complete can one genuinely benefit both oneself and others" — neither term is presented as sufficient without the other. The sixth realization's giving, he notes, completes what the fifth's wisdom alone could not accomplish: "possessing wisdom but lacking material means, one still cannot benefit oneself or others, and so the practice of the three kinds of giving becomes necessary here" — a bodhisattva's path, on this reading, requires wisdom and generosity together, neither one standing in for the other.

The Bodhisattva's Vow

Chinese Source

第八覺知：生死熾然，苦惱無量。發大乘心，普濟一切；願代眾生，受無量苦；令諸眾生，畢竟大樂。

如此八事，乃是諸佛菩薩大人之所覺悟。精進行道，慈悲修慧，乘法身船，至涅槃岸；復還生死，度脫眾生。以前八事，開導一切，令諸眾生，覺生死苦，捨離五欲，修心聖道。

若佛弟子，誦此八事，於念念中，滅無量罪；進趣菩提，速登正覺；永斷生死，常住快樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eighth realization: birth and death blaze on like a fire, and the suffering and distress they bring are without measure. One therefore arouses the mind of the Great Vehicle, vowing to save every being everywhere, wishing to take upon oneself, in the place of every being, their own measureless suffering, so that every being may come, at last, to great and lasting joy.

These eight matters are exactly what every buddha and bodhisattva, every great being, has come to realize. Through diligent practice of the path, through compassion and the cultivation of wisdom, they board the vessel of the Dharma-body and reach the shore of nirvāṇa — and then return once more into birth and death, to liberate every being there. By means of these same eight matters, they guide and open the way for all, causing every being to awaken to the suffering of birth and death, to set aside the five sense-pleasures, and to cultivate the mind on the holy path.

Should a disciple of the Buddha recite these eight matters, in thought after thought, measureless offenses are extinguished; advancing toward bodhi, one swiftly ascends to true awakening, permanently cutting off birth and death, and abiding forever after in true happiness.

Reading note — after Ouyi Zhixu

Ouyi treats the eighth realization as the point at which the whole preceding sequence turns decisively outward: "though one has already cultivated wisdom and merit both, if the mind of universal rescue through the Great Vehicle has not yet been aroused, then loving-kindness remains incomplete; and if the mind willing to bear suffering in another's place has not yet been aroused, then compassion remains shallow — only when kindness and compassion are both full and thoroughgoing does one become the true heir carrying on the buddhas' own household work." He reads the closing summary's image of "boarding the vessel of the Dharma-body" and "returning once more into birth and death" as making explicit what was implicit throughout: none of the first seven realizations — not impermanence, not contentment, not even generosity — is presented here as a private attainment a practitioner keeps for themselves once reached; each is instead one further piece of "the same eight matters" a great being uses afterward to open the same path for someone else. On the closing promise that mere recitation extinguishes "measureless offenses," he is careful to specify the actual mechanism at work, rather than leave it as bare assertion: "to recite the text is necessarily to reflect carefully on its meaning; to reflect carefully on its meaning is necessarily to use it to awaken oneself and then awaken others" — the recitation's power lying in what it is expected to set in motion, not in the syllables alone.
