The Rite Performed in Chinese Temples Today

This is the working script of the Great Compassion Repentance (大悲懺) — not the doctrinal treatise Siming Zhili wrote to explain the Great Compassion Dhāraṇī Sūtra, but the pared-down, practical liturgy that grew out of it: the version a Qing-dynasty Vinaya master abridged for actual performance, and which Chinese monasteries still recite today, often on a fixed weekly or monthly schedule. It opens with incense and prostrations, works through ten vows and a set of vows against each of the six destinies, arrives at the eighty-four clauses of the Great Compassion Mantra itself — each one, in this particular edition, annotated with the specific bodhisattva or guardian spirit it is traditionally said to embody — and closes with a formal confession and threefold refuge. Appended to the printed edition this translation follows is something rarer still: nearly four centuries of devotional postscripts, added one after another by named monks and laypeople who each, in turn, received, copied, and passed on the same treasured scroll.

Translation Method

This page translates Qianshouyan Dabei Xin Zhou Xingfa (千手眼大悲心咒行法, Xuzangjing X74, no. 1480), the printed liturgy attributed to "Siming Zhili, first compiler; Duti of Huashan, abridged and re-edited; Jixuan of Jiahe, image restored and blocks cut." This is a genuinely different text from Zhili's own Qianshouyan Dabei Xin Zhou Xingfa preserved in the Taishō canon as T1950 — the eleventh-century doctrinal manual already translated in paraphrase on this site's Great Compassion Dhāraṇī Sūtra page, which maps the sūtra's content onto Tiantai's technical categories for a reader already trained in that system. The text on this page is what that manual eventually became in practice: a working script, stripped of Zhili's doctrinal apparatus and organized instead as something an assembly can actually perform together, complete with stage directions (the bell struck once here, the leader chanting there) still printed into the text itself. This is the version of the "Great Compassion Repentance" that gives it its everyday name in Chinese Buddhism today.

As with this site's Great Compassion Dhāraṇī Sūtra page and its Śūraṅgama Sūtra pages, the Great Compassion Mantra itself is left in its classical Chinese phonetic transliteration rather than translated for meaning — a mantra's phonetic shape, not a paraphrased meaning, is what practice actually transmits. What is new on this page is the layer of annotation this particular edition preserves alongside the mantra: a centuries-old tradition, printed directly beneath each of the eighty-four clauses, identifying which bodhisattva, deva, or guardian spirit that clause is traditionally held to embody. That annotation layer is not phonetic Sanskrit — it is exegetical Chinese, and this page translates it in full, presented as a companion table beneath the mantra text.

The postscripts that close this page are translated from the same printed edition, preserved exactly as later hands appended them: a chain of colophons running from the Yuan dynasty (the earliest dated 1319) through the Qing (the latest dated 1699), each writer explaining how a single copy of this text and its accompanying image came into their hands, and passing it on to the next. See also this site's general note on translation method.

Offering Incense and Flowers燒香散華

Chinese Source

懺法：南無大悲觀世音菩薩（三稱）。一切恭謹。一心頂禮十方常住三寶（一禮畢）。燒香散華，首者唱：是諸眾等，各各胡跪，嚴持香華，如法供養。願此香華雲，徧滿十方界，一一諸佛土，無量香莊嚴，具足菩薩道，成就如來香。想云：我此香華徧十方，以為微妙光明臺，諸天音樂天寶香，諸天肴膳天寶衣，不可思議妙法塵，一一塵出一切塵，一一塵出一切法，旋轉無礙互莊嚴，遍至十方三寶前，十方法界三寶前，悉有我身修供養，一一皆悉遍法界。彼彼無雜無障礙，盡未來際作佛事，普熏法界諸眾生，蒙熏皆發菩提心。同入無生證佛智。供養已，一切恭謹。唱已，一禮：

Ru-Yi Translation

The rite: Homage to the Greatly Compassionate Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva (chanted three times). Let all be reverent and solemn.

With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the Three Jewels forever abiding throughout the ten directions. (Bow once. Burning incense, scattering flowers, the leader chants:) Let this whole assembly, each and every one, kneel with one knee raised, holding incense and flowers reverently, and make offering as the Dharma directs. May this very cloud of incense and flowers fill the ten directions to their limit, adorning every buddha-land with fragrance beyond measure, fully endowed with the bodhisattva path, and becoming the very fragrance of the Tathāgata.

Visualize, saying: May this incense and these flowers of mine fill the ten directions, becoming a subtle and wondrous tower of light, the music of the heavens, the incense of heavenly treasures, the delicacies of the heavens, the garments of heavenly treasures, an inconceivable, wondrous mote of Dharma — each mote giving rise to every mote, each mote giving rise to every dharma, turning without hindrance and adorning one another, reaching everywhere before the Three Jewels of the ten directions, before the Three Jewels of the Dharma-realm of the ten directions, my own body present everywhere making this offering, each and every one of them filling the whole Dharma-realm. Each and every one unmixed and unobstructed, performing the Buddha's own work to the very limit of the future, universally perfuming every living being of the Dharma-realm, so that, touched by this perfume, they all arouse the awakening-mind, together entering the non-arising and realizing the Buddha's wisdom. Having made this offering, let all be reverent and solemn. Having chanted this, bow once, saying:

Homage to Avalokiteśvara and the Buddhas頂禮三寶

Chinese Source

南無過去正法明如來，現前觀世音菩薩。成妙功德，具大慈悲。於一身心，現千手眼。照見法界，護持眾生。令發廣大道心，教持圓滿神呪。永離惡道，得生佛前。無間重愆，纏身惡疾。莫能救濟，悉使消除。三昧辯才，現生求願。皆令果遂，決定無疑。能使速獲三乘，早登佛地。威神之力，歎莫能窮。故我一心，歸命頂禮。一心頂禮本師釋迦牟尼世尊。想云：能禮所禮性空寂，感應道交難思議。我此道場如帝珠，釋迦如來影現中，我身影現釋迦前，頭面接足歸命禮。（如禮阿彌陀佛，即云阿彌陀佛影現中云云，禮僧但改為菩薩等，下隨位倣此。）一心頂禮西方極樂世界阿彌陀世尊。一心頂禮過去無量億劫千光王靜住世尊。一心頂禮過去九十九億殑伽沙諸佛世尊。一心頂禮過去無量劫正法明世尊。一心頂禮十方一切諸佛世尊。一心頂禮賢劫千佛三世一切諸佛世尊。（大眾聞罄上香。）一心頂禮廣大圓滿無閡大悲心大陀羅尼神妙章句（三稱三禮）。想云：真空法性如虗空，常住法寶難思議，我身影現法寶前，一心如法歸命禮。一心頂禮觀音所說諸陀羅尼及十方三世一切尊法（大眾上香）。一心頂禮千手千眼大慈大悲觀世音自在菩薩摩訶薩（三稱三禮）。想云：能禮所禮性空寂，感應道交難思議。我此道場如帝珠，大悲菩薩影現中，我身影現大悲前，為求滅障接足禮。一心頂禮大勢至菩薩摩訶薩。一心頂禮總持王菩薩摩訶薩。一心頂禮日光菩薩月光菩薩摩訶薩。一心頂禮寶王菩薩藥王菩薩藥上菩薩摩訶薩。一心頂禮華嚴菩薩大莊嚴菩薩寶藏菩薩摩訶薩。一心頂禮德藏菩薩金剛藏菩薩虗空藏菩薩摩訶薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Homage to the Tathāgata of the past, Right Dharma Illumination, present now as Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva. Accomplished in wondrous merit, endowed with great loving-kindness and great compassion, manifesting a thousand hands and a thousand eyes upon a single body and mind, illuminating and beholding the Dharma-realm, sheltering and upholding living beings, causing them to arouse the vast mind set upon the Way, and teaching them to uphold this perfect and complete spiritual mantra. Those who leave the evil destinies forever behind attain birth in the presence of the Buddha; the gravest offenses of unbroken retribution, and every terrible illness that binds the body, which nothing else could rescue or relieve, are all entirely dissolved. Samādhi and eloquence, and whatever is sought and wished for in this very life, are all brought to fruition, resolved beyond any doubt. He is able to cause swift attainment of the three vehicles, and swift ascent to the Buddha-ground. The power of his majestic spirit — praise could never exhaust it. And so, with sincere mind, we take refuge and bow our heads to the ground in homage.

With sincere mind, we bow in homage to our fundamental teacher, the World-Honored Śākyamuni. Visualize, saying: the nature of the one who bows and the one bowed to are both empty and still, yet the meeting of response and resonance is inconceivable; this very site of practice is like Indra's own jewel, the Tathāgata Śākyamuni reflected within it, my own body reflected before Śākyamuni, and, head and face touching his very feet, I take refuge and bow in homage. (When bowing to Amitābha, say instead "Amitābha reflected within it," and so on; when bowing to a monk rather than a buddha, change the wording to "bodhisattva"; every further instance below follows this same pattern.)

With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the World-Honored Amitābha of the Western Land of Ultimate Bliss. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the World-Honored King of a Thousand Lights, Dwelling in Stillness, of measureless hundred millions of kalpas past. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the World-Honored Buddhas of the past, as numerous as the sands of ninety-nine hundred million Ganges rivers. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the World-Honored Right Dharma Illumination, of measureless kalpas past. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to every World-Honored Buddha of the ten directions. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the thousand Buddhas of the Fortunate Kalpa, and every World-Honored Buddha of the three periods of time. (The assembly, hearing the bell, offers incense.)

With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the wondrous verses of the Vast, Perfect, Unimpeded Great Compassion Heart Great Dhāraṇī. (Recite and bow three times.) Visualize, saying: the true emptiness of the Dharma-nature is like empty space itself, and this ever-abiding jewel of the Dharma is beyond all conceiving; my own body reflected before this very jewel of the Dharma, with sincere mind, I take refuge and bow in homage as the Dharma directs. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to every dhāraṇī spoken by Avalokiteśvara, and to every venerable Dharma of the ten directions and the three periods of time. (The assembly offers incense.)

With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattva of the Thousand Hands and Thousand Eyes, of Great Kindness and Great Compassion, Avalokiteśvara, Sovereign and Free. (Recite and bow three times.) Visualize, saying: the nature of the one who bows and the one bowed to are both empty and still, yet the meeting of response and resonance is inconceivable; this very site of practice is like Indra's own jewel, the Bodhisattva of Great Compassion reflected within it, my own body reflected before the Bodhisattva of Great Compassion, and, seeking the dissolving of every obstruction, I touch his very feet in homage.

With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattva Mahāsthāmaprāpta. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattva Dhāraṇī-King. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattvas Sun Light and Moon Light. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattvas Jeweled King, Medicine King, and Medicine Superior. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattvas Flower Adornment, Great Adornment, and Jewel Treasury. With sincere mind, we bow in homage to the Bodhisattva-Mahāsattvas Virtue Treasury, Vajra Treasury, and Ākāśagarbha.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The visualization instructions repeated at each major bow — "the nature of the one who bows and the one bowed to are both empty and still, yet the meeting of response and resonance is inconceivable" — are doing real philosophical work, not simply decorating the gesture: they hold together the claim that ultimate reality has no fixed self on either side of the bow, and the claim that a real, efficacious response nonetheless occurs when the bow is made with sincerity. The image used to reconcile the two, Indra's net jewel reflecting the whole assembly within a single facet, appears throughout Huayan literature for exactly this purpose — showing interpenetration without requiring any of the reflected things to possess a substantial, independent nature of their own.

If I face a mountain of blades, that mountain of blades shall of itself break and crumble. If I face a cauldron of fire, that cauldron of fire shall of itself run dry. The Great Compassion Dhāraṇī大悲咒

Chinese Source

觀世音菩薩白佛言：「世尊！若諸眾生誦持大悲神呪墮三惡道者，我誓不成正覺；誦持大悲神呪若不生諸佛國者，我誓不成正覺；誦持大悲神呪若不得無量三昧辯才者，我誓不成正覺；誦持大悲神呪於現在生中一切所求若不果遂者，不得為大悲心陀羅尼也。」乃至說是語已，於眾會前合掌正住，於諸眾生起大悲心開顏含笑，即說如是廣大圓滿無閡大悲心大陀羅尼神妙章句陀羅尼曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if there are beings who recite and hold the Great Compassion Mantra and yet fall into one of the three evil destinies, then I vow I shall not attain true awakening. If there are those who recite and hold it and yet are not born in a buddha-land, then I vow I shall not attain true awakening. If there are those who recite and hold it and yet do not attain measureless samādhi and eloquence, then I vow I shall not attain true awakening. For those who recite and hold it: whatever they seek in this present life, if it is not fulfilled, then it cannot rightly be called the Great Compassion Heart Dhāraṇī." Having spoken these words, he stood before the whole assembly with palms joined, gave rise to a mind of great compassion toward every being, and, smiling, spoke the wondrous verses of the Vast, Perfect, Unimpeded Great Compassion Heart Great Dhāraṇī as follows:

Translator's note

The eighty-four clauses above are the same phonetic transliteration of the Nīlakaṇṭha ("blue-throated") dhāraṇī found in the Great Compassion Dhāraṇī Sūtra itself, here divided at eighty-four rather than the sūtra's own eighty-two — a difference of enumeration between transmission lineages rather than a difference of wording. As on this site's sūtra page, the mantra is left as the Chinese tradition itself has always transmitted it: sound to be recited, not text to be parsed for discursive meaning.

What this particular edition preserves that the bare sūtra text does not is a centuries-old layer of interlinear annotation, printed beneath most of the eighty-four clauses, identifying which bodhisattva, buddha, deva, or guardian spirit that specific clause is traditionally held to embody or invoke. This is not a claim about etymology or Sanskrit meaning — several entries note only a sound (a sheep's bleat, a sword being drawn, the rustle of falling leaves) or a role in the ritual (where the reciter should slow down, where every ghost and spirit is said to fold its hands and listen) — but it is a real, translatable layer of the tradition's own commentary, and this page renders it in full below.

What Each Clause Is Traditionally Said to Embody

1. 南無喝囉怛娜哆囉夜㖿
Avalokiteśvara's own body. One must be especially compassionate here, reciting with full attention and not too loudly — this deity's nature is quick to anger.
2. 南無阿唎㖿
Cintāmaṇicakra Bodhisattva's own body. Upon reaching this point one must hold the mind steady.

Ru-Yi Translation

Bow once. Silently reflect: I myself and every living being, since beginningless time, have constantly been obstructed by the grave faults of the three karmas and six faculties, unable to see the Buddhas, not knowing the essential way out, merely following along with birth and death, not understanding the profound principle. Though I now understand it, I remain, together with every living being, obstructed by every grave fault alike. Now, before Avalokiteśvara and the Buddhas of the ten directions, I take refuge and confess on behalf of every living being, and pray only that they add their protection, so that every obstruction may be dissolved.

(Having reflected on this, kneel and chant:) Universally, on behalf of the four kindnesses, the three states of existence, and every living being of the Dharma-realm, I vow that all may cut off the three obstructions, and I take refuge and confess.

(Having chanted this, prostrate fully to the ground, and reflect once more in the mind, saying:) I myself and every living being, from beginningless time until now, because of craving and false views, have reckoned within an "I" and an "other," and, drawing close to evil companions without, have failed to rejoice in even the smallest goodness of another, but instead, throughout the three karmas, have broadly created every kind of fault. Though the matter itself may not be vast, this evil mind has spread everywhere, continuing day and night without a single interruption. Concealing and denying our own faults, unwilling to let others know of them, unafraid of the evil destinies, without shame before ourselves or others, we have denied cause and effect altogether.

And so, today, believing deeply in cause and effect, giving rise to a heavy sense of shame, giving rise to great dread, we lay our faults bare in confession, cutting off the mind that would let them continue, arousing the awakening-mind, cutting off evil and cultivating good, diligently disciplining the three karmas, reversing our past grave faults, and rejoicing in even the smallest goodness of the ordinary and the sage alike. We call to mind the Buddhas of the ten directions, whose great blessedness and wisdom are able to rescue and protect me and every living being, carrying us across the sea of the two kinds of death to the shore of the three virtues. From beginningless time until now, not knowing that the true nature of every dharma is empty and still, we have broadly created every evil; now, knowing this emptiness and stillness, and for the sake of seeking awakening, and for the sake of every living being, we broadly cultivate every good and thoroughly cut off every evil. We pray only that Avalokiteśvara, with compassion, receive us.

(Having so reflected, kneel and chant, saying:) With sincere mind, we confess. We disciples, together with every living being of the Dharma-realm, in this very single thought before us, are originally endowed with a thousand dharmas, every one possessed of spiritual power and the brightness of wisdom, equal above to the mind of the Buddha, and the same below as every being possessed of consciousness. Yet, since beginningless time, moved by darkness, we have obstructed this very stillness and clarity, confused and deluded upon meeting any object, our minds arising only to bind themselves in attachment — and, within a Dharma that is in truth equal throughout, we give rise to the very notion of self and other. Craving and false views are their root, and body and speech their condition, so that, throughout every state of existence, there is no fault we have not committed: the ten evils, the five heinous crimes, slandering the Dharma and slandering others, breaking the precepts and breaking the fast, destroying stūpas and ruining monasteries, stealing the property of the monastic assembly, defiling pure conduct, and encroaching upon and damaging the food and wealth of the permanent assembly — a sin that a thousand buddhas coming into the world could not absolve through repentance.

Faults such as these are immeasurable and boundless; discarding this very body and life, we deserve by rights to fall into the three evil paths, undergoing every form of suffering there in full; and even in this present life, every kind of affliction presses in upon us together, whether a terrible illness binds the body, or other conditions press us hard, obstructing the way of the Dharma so that we cannot cultivate it. Now, encountering this very rite and bowing before the perfect and complete spiritual mantra of great compassion, we are able to swiftly dissolve every such obstruction of fault. And so, today, with sincere mind, we recite and uphold it, turning in refuge and bowing before Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva and the great teachers of the ten directions, arousing the awakening-mind and cultivating the practice of this true mantra, and, together with every living being, laying every fault bare, begging for confession, that it may be dissolved once and for all.

We pray only that, bowing before the great and compassionate Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva-Mahāsattva, his thousand hands may uphold and protect us, his thousand eyes may behold us, causing every obstructing condition, within and without, to be dissolved and stilled, our own practice and the practice of others alike brought to perfect completion, our original insight and understanding opened, every demon and heretic restrained, the three karmas made diligent, the cause of the pure land cultivated — so that, when at last we give up this body, with no other destiny remaining, we may resolve, without doubt, to be born, bowing before the Buddha Amitābha, in the Land of Ultimate Bliss, there to receive and serve him in person.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The confession's own logic runs on a single hinge stated early and returned to at the very end: everyone present is "originally endowed" with the same faculties as a buddha, "equal above to the mind of the Buddha," and what obstructs this is not a lack of any capacity but a beginningless darkness that has covered it over. Confession, on this account, is not the acquisition of something new but the removal of an obstruction from something already fully present — which is also why the text can move, within a single breath, from naming genuinely grave transgressions (the five heinous crimes, destroying stūpas) to the promise that this same mantra dissolves what "a thousand buddhas coming into the world could not absolve," a claim this liturgy inherits directly from the underlying sūtra itself.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The rite closes with two verses in noticeably different registers — the first devotional and image-rich (a thousand hands and eyes, a hair's tip containing all of empty space), the second in the terse, riddling idiom of Chan discourse, asking what could possibly be "called" anything at a point prior to name and form. Placed back to back, they make the same closing move from two directions: the first shows that the dhāraṇī's cosmic scope collapses into a single point (the hair's tip); the second insists that even that point is already too much to name. Neither verse is attributed to Zhili or Duti by name in this printed edition, and both read as liturgical convention rather than personal composition — closing gestures meant to leave the assembly, quite deliberately, without a final doctrinal statement to hold onto.

Seven Centuries of Postscripts歷代題跋

The printed edition this translation follows appends, after the rite itself, a chain of colophons attached over nearly four hundred years to a single treasured image of Avalokiteśvara paired with the text of the mantra — each new owner explaining how the scroll came into their hands, adding a verse of their own, and passing it on. The final entry, by a layman surnamed Jin, explicitly counts "seven people in the past, from the State Preceptor Zhongfeng onward," each of whom "appended a verse of praise at its end" — Zhongfeng being another name for the Yuan-dynasty Chan master Zhongfeng Mingben (中峰明本, 1263–1323), whose own brief inscription opens the chain translated below. What follows is that chain in full, translated in the order the source prints it, each entry given its own attribution and, where the source supplies one, its date.

The monk Ziru's own daily vow, as later inscribed for Jingming Daozhe — 1310

Avalokiteśvara, of great wisdom and land, endowed with a heart of great compassion, skillfully speaks this dhāraṇī, and with great compassion rescues all. I now arouse this vast vow: to uphold this Great Compassion Heart, this unhindered dhāraṇī, its light issuing from the crown of the head, shining everywhere across this land of mine and throughout the ten directions — down to the very rim of the wheel of wind, up to the highest heaven of existence, across Mount Meru and the Iron Ring Mountains, the great seas, the rivers and streams — that every being within them, touched by this light, may all receive its benefit, leave suffering behind and attain liberation, all arousing the awakening-mind, and, at the end of this one present body, together be born in the Land of Ultimate Bliss.

May the light of this Great Compassion Heart shine throughout the ten directions, throughout every realm of empty space, throughout every buddha-land numerous as motes of dust. May every Tathāgata of those lands give rise to great joy, and, by the power of their skillful means, their vow, and their wisdom, extend their compassionate pity to me as well. May each, from the crown of the head no one can see, universally emit the light of great compassion, and, from every one of those dust-mote lands, come together to pour it upon my own crown, dissolving every fault of mine and fulfilling every vow of mine, causing me swiftly to be endowed with this unhindered Great Compassion Heart, so that, like every Buddha, every World-Honored One, I may broadly deliver every being.

Our fundamental teacher, the honored Śākyamuni, spoke this Great Compassion Sūtra, and within this very sūtra I hold a resolute faith, free of doubt. I vow that, in the future, I shall accomplish omniscience, and, by means of this Great Compassion Heart, broadly perform the Buddha's own work — making offerings to Buddhas numerous as the sands of the Ganges, delivering every living being, and, like Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, being able to rescue the world from its suffering.

The verses above are the private vow-text that the monk Ziru recited daily alongside his own recitation of the mantra. The Way-person Jingming, having greatly aroused this compassionate vow, upheld these very verses, chanting them so constantly he lost track of the years. In the spring of the gengxu year of the Zhida era [1310], he obtained this very sūtra scroll, which matched his own vow exactly — this being precisely the intent of the great bodhisattva's own entrustment for its circulation. One day, holding the sūtra, he asked that the preceding verse be inscribed upon it, and so it was said to him:

"The great bodhisattva has no body, yet manifests a body of a thousand hands and a thousand eyes; he has no speech, yet manifests speech in the sound of these very verses. If one is able, beyond both form and language, to believe, accept, and put this into practice, then it will not be Avalokiteśvara alone who bears witness to it — I too shall attain realization through it." — Ziru of Jian'an

Yuanzhao, Nine Grades Contemplation Hall, Tianzhu

A single thought of great compassion, and every one of the ten realms is fully complete; originally without any mark at all, yet let it be tested against every form of argument. Its transformative response cannot be measured or grasped, and so, as an expedient, it is given a thousand hands and a thousand eyes, shielding against every evil everywhere, holding within it every goodness entire. When water is muddied, the moon seems to vanish from it; when water is clear, the moon seems to appear in it — yet grasp only the true nature of this very moment, and wherever it meets circumstance, its efficacy manifests at once. A clear-eyed teacher, possessed of this very insight, can make even ink and dust give off a blazing light. Penetrating deeply to the very substance of great compassion, one is able to be free of every view. Beneath the blue sky and the bright sun, there yet remain many eddies and whirlpools — we bow our heads to the Lord of Great Compassion, who forever raises up this vast skillful means.

The Way-person Ming (明道人)

We bow our heads to the one fully endowed with the heart of great compassion, whose body, for the benefit of living beings, manifests a thousand hands and a thousand eyes; from this one body, further manifesting bodies as numerous as the motes of every buddha-land, his wondrous wisdom and spiritual power broadly spreading and expounded. Giving and drawing out reach everywhere to me, though I remain unconditioned myself, since, from the very beginning, endless kalpas have perfumed him with every kind of goodness. When the midday sun shines bright, its brilliance may still at times be veiled — yet when spring returns to the great earth, what limit is there to what it brings forth? If you truly wish to enter the gateway of upholding this mantra, do not try to reason apart its secret words and its strange forms. Understand and hold fast to the very first phrase on its own, and the King of Right Dharma Illumination shall reveal himself even within the darkness.

The layman Jin — closing account, two entries

At the age of twenty, I recited and upheld the Great Compassion Heart Dhāraṇī. I heard the great master Wengu say: "Worldly affliction and karmic fault — neither the wise nor the foolish are spared from them, and the noble and the base alike possess them. Should you be able to recite this dhāraṇī five full times in a single night, every goodness will gather together in competition, and every evil will vie to be dissolved — this is beyond words, beyond words." Now, having reached the age of fifty, I have done nothing but murmur it day after day, this one thought never once moving from it.

On the anniversary of my mother's death, Master Cangxue brought this very scroll to show me. Kneeling with one knee raised, I unrolled and studied it, and had a fine-line copy made — the paper and ink as fresh as if new — and it felt as though the great earth itself had shaken, as though heaven had rained down jeweled flowers, sorrow and joy mingling together within me. And so I bowed my head and said: "The Lord of Great Compassion, Avalokiteśvara — originally without a body, yet manifesting a body of a thousand hands and a thousand eyes; originally without a single word, yet proclaiming these wondrous verses. And how many further ages, and how many further people, conjured up this body of forty-nine excellent marks, causing seven people across the past, from the State Preceptor Zhongfeng onward, each in turn to append a verse of praise at its end — is this not truly the very banner of light, the seal of spiritual power, the treasury itself?" The master closed the scroll, smiled, and said: "Seek it beyond both form and language." — respectfully recorded by Jin Qianzai

I have now upheld this Sanskrit-bound scroll for a further ten years. Monastics and laypeople alike have sought it out from me, and those who rejoice in it are as numerous as clouds. And so, struck with sudden clarity, I said: "Ziru of Jian'an praised the Way-person Jingming for chanting this so constantly he lost track of the years — this matches exactly the intent of the great bodhisattva's own entrustment for its circulation." And so I had it printed and distributed to the far corners of the earth, in grateful reverence for the profound kindness of the venerable Cangxue in bringing and giving it to me. Written at the age of sixty-two. — respectfully recorded by Jin Zai

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Read end to end, this chain of postscripts is less a set of independent literary compositions than a record of transmission — a scroll changing hands roughly once a generation across nearly four centuries (Yuan-dynasty entries dated 1310, 1319, and 1320; a final Qing-dynasty pair from Jixuan's own compilation in 1675 and 1699), each new custodian describing the same emotional beat: obtaining the scroll, being moved by it "as though the great earth itself had shaken," and passing it on with a verse attached so the chain would not end with them. Zhongfeng Mingben's own brief inscription — "it is not Chinese words, nor is it Sanskrit either" — anticipates by three centuries the exact reasoning this page's own translation method gives for leaving the mantra untranslated: the layman Jin's teacher closes the whole account the same way, telling him to "seek it beyond both form and language," which is as much a description of how this entire tradition has always handled the dhāraṇī's own sound as it is spiritual advice.
