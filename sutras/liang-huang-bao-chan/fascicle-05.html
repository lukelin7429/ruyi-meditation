Fascicle 5

Dissolving grudges and enmities《慈悲道場懺法》卷第五・解怨釋結

The chapter this liturgy is most remembered for: an argument that every enmity is born out of closeness itself, that our own parents, teachers, and kin across countless lifetimes are the very source of the grudges we carry, and a sustained, direct appeal asking them — and every being we have ever wronged or been wronged by — to let it all go.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1909 Chapter: 解怨釋結第三 Status: complete
Chinese
《慈悲道場懺法》卷第五 — the fifth of ten fascicles, consisting entirely of a single chapter, Dissolving Grudges and Enmities (解怨釋結).
Covers
Why every living being carries grudges and enmities from countless past lives; the argument that closeness itself, not distance, is the root of enmity; a direct appeal to one's own parents and kin across the six destinies to release every grudge; an extended treatment of enmity specifically toward one's teachers and masters, including the story of a disciple who fell into the hell of Avīci for a single disrespectful word; and a closing meditation on urgency and the "two kinds of shame" that make genuine confession possible.
Why it matters
This is, by most accounts, the single most quoted and most performed chapter of the entire Liang Huang Bao Chan — the passage most likely to be excerpted on its own in a shorter devotional service, and the one most associated with the liturgy's popular reputation as a rite for resolving karmic enmity between the living and the dead.
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — more personal and direct in address than the doctrinal catalogs of the preceding fascicles, though the central argument (that family relation is itself the root of enmity) rewards careful, non-literal reading.

今日道場同業大眾！一切眾生皆有怨對。何以知之？若無怨對則無惡道。今惡道不休、三途長沸，是知怨對無有窮已。經言：一切眾生悉皆有心，有心者皆得作佛。而諸眾生心想顛倒，長寢生死不能覺悟，貪著世間不知出要，建立苦本長養怨根，所以輪迴三有、往來六道，捨身受身無暫停息。何以故爾？一切眾生無始已來，闇識相傳，無明所覆、愛水所溺，起三毒根、起四顛倒。從三毒根起十煩惱，依於身見起於五見，依於五見起六十二見，依身口意起十惡行，身殺盜婬、口妄言綺語兩舌惡罵、意貪瞋癡，自行十惡、教他行十惡，讚歎十惡法、讚歎行十惡法者，如是依身口意起四十種惡。復依六情貪著六塵，乃至廣開八萬四千塵勞門。一念之間起六十二見，一念之頃行四十種惡，一念之間開八萬四千塵勞門。況復一日所起眾罪，一月一年終身歷劫所起眾罪，如是罪惡無量無邊，怨對相尋無有窮已。而諸眾生與愚癡俱，無明覆慧、煩惱覆心，不自覺知。心想顛倒，不信經說、不依佛語，不知解怨、不望解脫，怨怨相報何時解脫？自投惡道如蛾赴火，歷劫長夜受無量苦。假使業極有終得還人道，如是惡人終不改革。是以眾聖起大慈悲，正為如是怨對眾生。我等相與發菩提心行菩薩道，菩薩摩訶薩救苦為資糧、解怨為要行、不捨眾生為本，我等今日亦復如是，起勇猛心、起慈悲心、等如來心，承諸佛力，建道場幡、擊甘露鼓，秉智慧弓、執堅固箭，普為四生六道三世眾怨、父母師長六親眷屬解怨釋結，已作之罪一切捨施，未起之怨畢竟不造。仰願諸佛諸大菩薩，以慈悲力、以本願力、以神通力同加覆護折伏攝受，令三世無量眾怨，從今日去乃至菩提，解怨釋結無復怨對，一切眾苦畢竟斷除。相與至心等一痛切，五體投地，奉為天人六道三世眾怨、父母師長一切眷屬，歸依世間大慈悲父。 Ru-Yi Translation

Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — every living being carries grudges and enmities. How do we know this? If there were no grudges and enmities, there would be no evil destinies. Since the evil destinies never cease, and the three evil paths boil on without end, we know that grudges and enmities are truly without limit. The sūtra says: every living being possesses a mind, and whoever possesses a mind can attain buddhahood. And yet the thoughts of living beings are inverted; sunk for so long in the sleep of birth and death, they cannot awaken, clinging to the world and not knowing the essential way out, establishing the very root of suffering and nourishing the root of enmity — and this is why they turn through the three realms of existence, passing back and forth through the six destinies, discarding one body and taking up another without a moment's rest. Why is this so? Every living being, since beginningless time, has passed down a darkened consciousness from one life to the next, covered by ignorance and drowned in the waters of craving, giving rise to the root of the three poisons and the four inverted views. From the root of the three poisons arise the ten afflictions; relying on the view of a self, the five views arise; relying on the five views, the sixty-two views arise; relying on body, speech, and mind, the ten evil deeds arise — in body, killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct; in speech, false speech, flowery speech, divisive speech, and harsh speech; in mind, greed, hatred, and delusion — committing the ten evils oneself, instructing others to commit the ten evils, praising the ten evil deeds, and praising those who commit the ten evil deeds, so that, relying on body, speech, and mind in this way, forty kinds of evil arise. Relying further on the six faculties, one craves the six sense-objects, extending even to the broad opening of the eighty-four thousand gates of worldly affliction. Within a single thought, the sixty-two views arise; in the space of a single thought, the forty kinds of evil are committed; within a single thought, the eighty-four thousand gates of worldly affliction are opened. How much more, then, the many faults committed in a single day, a single month, a single year, a whole lifetime, across many kalpas! Faults and evils of this kind are immeasurable and boundless, and grudges and enmities follow one after another without end. And yet living beings, bound up with their own folly, their wisdom covered by ignorance and their minds obstructed by affliction, are unaware of this themselves. Their thoughts inverted, they do not believe the sūtras' teaching, do not rely on the Buddha's own word, do not know how to dissolve enmity, and do not hope for liberation — and if enmity is repaid with enmity in this way, when will liberation ever come? They cast themselves into the evil destinies like a moth flying into a flame, undergoing immeasurable suffering through the long night of many kalpas. And even should their karma at last run its course and they return to the human realm, such evil people never once reform themselves. This is precisely why the sages arouse great compassion — for the sake of living beings caught in enmity such as this. We ourselves, together, having aroused the awakening-mind and walking the bodhisattva path — for the great bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, rescuing beings from suffering is their very provision, dissolving enmity is their essential practice, and never abandoning living beings is their very foundation — we ourselves, today, do just the same: arousing a courageous mind, arousing a compassionate mind, a mind equal to the Tathāgatas', relying on the power of the Buddhas, we raise the banner of this rite and strike the drum of ambrosia, take up the bow of wisdom and grasp the firm arrow, and, on behalf of every enmity of the three periods of time throughout the four modes of birth and six destinies, our parents, teachers, and every degree of kin, we dissolve every grudge and enmity, entirely giving up whatever fault has already been committed, and vowing never to create the enmity that has not yet arisen. We look up and pray that all the Buddhas and great bodhisattvas, by the power of compassion, the power of their original vows, and their supernormal power, may together extend their shelter, subdue, and receive us, so that every immeasurable enmity of the three periods of time, from this day forward until we reach awakening, may have its grudges dissolved and its enmities resolved, never to give rise to enmity again, and every suffering may be cut off once and for all. Let us together, with sincere mind, in this same anguish of resolve, prostrate ourselves fully to the ground, on behalf of the enmities of the three periods of time among gods, humans, and the six destinies, and our parents, teachers, and every kin, and take refuge in the great and compassionate father of the world. Should any being of the six destinies be owed such requital, or not yet have received it, may they never again enter the evil destinies, never again turn a hateful mind toward one another, and never again inflict cruelty and poison upon each other; may all be given up entirely, with no notion of enemy or friend remaining; may every fault and transgression be dissolved, and every enmity attain liberation, all minds joined in harmony as water blends with milk, every joy like that of the first ground; may their lives be without end, their bodies and minds forever at peace, born at will into heavenly palaces and pure lands, their clothing arriving the moment they think of it, their food arriving the moment they wish for it, with no more sound of enmity or weeping; may their four limbs never be assailed by change, and their five faculties never be defiled by the dust of confusion; may every goodness gather together and every evil be dissolved in competition with it, arousing the Great Vehicle and cultivating the bodhisattva practice, the four immeasurables and six perfections all fully complete, discarding the retribution of birth and death and together accomplishing true and equal awakening. Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — what, then, is the root of enmity, the very root of suffering? The eye craves form, the ear craves sound, the nose craves fragrance, the tongue craves flavor, the body craves smoothness and softness, and one is constantly bound by these five sense-objects — this is why, through the long night of many kalpas, one cannot attain liberation. And moreover, our six degrees of kin and every one of our family are themselves the very root of our enmity across the three periods of time. Every enmity arises out of closeness — where there is no closeness, there is no enmity, and to be free of closeness is precisely to be free of enmity. Why is this so? If two people each dwelt in different places, far apart in distant lands, such two people would never give rise to a hateful, resentful mind. That resentment arises at all is entirely because of closeness — through the root of the three poisons, people irritate one another, and because of this irritation, a resentful mind frequently arises, so that family and kin quickly come to hold expectations of one another — a parent expecting something of a child, or a child expecting something of a parent, and brothers and sisters are all just the same, holding expectations of one another, resenting one another, giving rise to anger over the smallest displeasure. Should there be wealth or treasure that kin compete for, on the day of poverty there was at first no such worry — and yet the more one obtains, the more one feels it is too little, the more one gains, the more one feels unsatisfied; a hundred requests granted a hundred times bring no gratitude, while a single disappointment increases resentment. And so a person harbors an evil thought and gives rise to an estranged mind, binding grudges and connecting calamities life after life without end. Reasoning in this way, the enmities of the three periods of time are, in truth, no strangers at all — they are entirely our own kin and family. We should know that our very family is itself a gathering of enmity — how, then, could each of us fail to repent earnestly? Let each of us, with sincere mind, prostrate ourselves fully to the ground, on behalf of every spirit possessed of consciousness from time immemorial down to this present day — our parents through every life, our kin across every kalpa, wherever we have bound enmity within the six destinies, whether the debt has been repaid or not, whether light or grave — today, whether they dwell in the hell-destiny, the animal-destiny, the hungry-ghost destiny, the asura-destiny, the human destiny, the heavenly destiny, or the destiny of immortals, or whether they now stand present among our own family — for every such enmity of the three periods of time, and each of their kin, we disciples, today, with a compassionate mind and no notion of enemy or friend, equal to the mind of all the Buddhas, one with the vow of all the Buddhas, universally dedicate this on their behalf, and take refuge in the great and compassionate father of the world. Furthermore, from beginningless time until this very day, whether over farmland, over a dwelling, or over money and wealth, we have given rise to the karma of enmity, even inflicting killing upon our own kin because of it. Matters of this kind, in every variety, cannot all be told, and the enmity thus arisen has known no end. Today, in shame, we lay it bare and confess it, praying that our parents, our six degrees of kin, and every family member, with a compassionate mind, accept our confession, giving up everything entirely, with no trace of resentment remaining — extending even to theft, sexual misconduct, false speech, the five heinous crimes, and the ten evils, none left uncommitted, our deluded and inverted thoughts clinging to every object and creating every kind of fault. Faults of this kind are immeasurable and boundless. Whether toward our parents, our brothers and sisters, or our aunts and uncles and their kin, extending from the time we first possessed consciousness until this very day, whatever fault we have committed toward our own kin, and its bitter fruit, and however many kalpas of enmity and grudge we have incurred — only the Buddhas and great bodhisattvas of the ten directions know and see this in its fullness. Whatever measure of fault and however many kalpas of enmity the Buddhas and bodhisattvas know and see in us, yet to be received as requital in the ages to come, we disciples, today, our faces flushed with shame, choking with grief, reproach ourselves in sorrow, vowing to correct what is past and cultivate what is to come, and dare not commit it again. We pray only that our parents and kin, with a gentle mind, a harmonious mind, a mind that delights in goodness, a mind of joy, a mind of protection, a mind equal to the Tathāgatas', accept our confession today, giving up everything entirely, with no notion of enemy or friend remaining. We further pray that, should our parents and kin bear enmity anywhere among the six destinies, every being of the six destinies may likewise together give it up entirely, so that every enmity of the three periods of time may be exhausted at once. From this day forward, even to the very seat of awakening, may they forever leave behind the three evil paths and be free of the suffering of the four evil destinies, all joined in harmony as water blends with milk, all unhindered as empty space itself, forever becoming kin in the Dharma and compassionate family, each cultivating boundless wisdom, fully accomplishing every merit, courageous and diligent without rest, walking the bodhisattva path without ever growing weary, equal to the mind of the Buddhas, one with the vow of the Buddhas, attaining the Buddha's three mysteries and the fivefold Dharma-body, and, ultimately, unsurpassed awakening, accomplishing true and equal awakening.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The claim at the center of this section is easy to read past too quickly: not that closeness sometimes turns into enmity, but that closeness is enmity's very root, so that where there is no relationship at all, no grudge could ever have formed in the first place. This is a genuinely uncomfortable argument, and the text does not soften it into something more palatable — it follows it all the way to the conclusion that one's own family, precisely because they are family, is "a gathering of enmity." What keeps this from becoming a case against relationship altogether is what follows: rather than urging distance, the liturgy urges direct address, calling on parents and kin by name across every past life and every destiny, and asking them, and asking the reader in turn, to let it go.

Ru-Yi Translation

Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — together we have now dissolved the enmity toward our parents; next we should dissolve the grudges and enmities toward our teachers and masters. Even the great sages, since their attainment is not yet perfectly complete, and even in reaching the acceptance of the non-arising of dharmas, still undergo the passing away of the three marks — even the Tathāgata himself still made use of stern words, so that evil beings might be awakened through them. And if one whose virtue and clarity transform beings still made use of such words, how much more should the ordinary and foolish, whose reasoning is far from a pure realm? Now, good and evil lie mixed together, not yet made clear and distinct — how, then, could one suddenly be free of the faults of the three kinds of karma? If, upon hearing what is said, one truly feels shame, and reproaches oneself deeply for the kindness and virtue of one's teachers and masters, one need not be alarmed or suspect that ill will is meant. The sūtra says: even one who has left home is not free of every affliction and has not attained liberation, and one should not therefore say there is no evil left in him; and a lay person should not therefore say there is no good in him at all. But let us set this matter aside, and turn to what the sūtra itself says. The Buddha told the assembly: "You should reflect on the kindness of your teachers and masters. Though your parents gave birth to you, raised you, and instructed you, they could not free you from the three evil paths. Your teachers and masters, out of great compassion, guide the ignorant child forward, enabling him to leave home and receive the full precepts — this is to carry the very embryo of an arhat and give birth to the fruit of an arhat, leaving behind the suffering of birth and death and attaining the joy of nirvāṇa. Since teachers and masters possess kindness and virtue of this world-transcending kind, who could ever repay it in full? Even should one practice the Way to the end of one's life, this would only benefit oneself — it would not be enough to repay a teacher's kindness." The Buddha said: "Among all the good friends in the world, none surpasses one's own teacher and master." Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — as the Buddha has taught, our teachers and masters possess kindness and virtue such as this. And yet we have never once given rise to the thought of repaying their kindness; or, when instructed, we fail to believe and accept it, going so far as to slander them with coarse words, wrongly stirring up controversy and causing the Buddha-dharma itself to decline. How could faults of this kind ever be escaped? The bitter retribution of the three evil paths has no one to bear it in our place, and when we at last give up this life, longing to leave suffering behind and return to joy, our spirit will be stricken with anguish, our mind confused and dim, our six consciousnesses dulled and our five faculties failing — wishing to walk, the feet will not move; wishing to sit, the body cannot support itself; even wishing to hear the Dharma spoken, the ears will hear nothing more; wishing to behold some excellent sight, the eyes will see nothing more. At such a time, even if we then think to perform this very rite of confession, how could it possibly be done? There will be nothing left but the immeasurable suffering of the hells, and such bitter retribution is always one's own doing, to be borne by oneself alone. This is why the sūtra says: the foolish, relying on themselves, disbelieve in calamity, slandering their teacher, defaming their teacher, hating their teacher, and envying their teacher — people such as this are the great demons within the Dharma, the very seed of the hells, binding their own enmity and receiving its retribution without end. Consider the monk Huaguang, skilled in expounding the essentials of the Dharma, who had a disciple forever filled with arrogance and pride; whenever his teacher taught him, he never once believed or accepted it, and said outright: "My great teacher has no wisdom at all — he can only praise matters as empty as the sky. I hope never again to have the misfortune of seeing him in my next life." And so this disciple called the Dharma not the Dharma, and called what was not the Dharma the Dharma; though he kept the precepts without transgression, because of this very misunderstanding, when his life ended, in the time it takes an arrow to fly, he fell into the hell of Avīci, and for eighty koṭis of kalpas underwent great suffering there without ceasing. Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — as the sūtra describes, how could each of us fail to arouse great dread? Merely uttering one evil word against one's own preceptor brought a fall into the hell of Avīci for eighty koṭis of kalpas — how much more, then, for the evil karma we have created toward our own preceptors from the time we left home until this very day, its fault immeasurable, so that giving up this body and form to the same fate is beyond any doubt. Why is this so? Our preceptors and instructors constantly add to our instruction, and yet we have never once practiced as the Dharma teaches; toward our various teachers and masters we have given rise to disobedience time and again; or we have received their generosity without ever being satisfied; the teacher grows angry at the disciple, or the disciple resents the teacher, joy and anger without measure across the three periods of time — faults of this kind cannot be counted or reckoned. The sūtra says: give rise to a single thought of hatred, and the enmity it produces is without measure. Enmity of this kind is not confined to one's own family alone — the resentment between teacher and disciple, master and student, can be just as severe. Furthermore, those who share the same quarters and dwell together, of high, middle, and low station, fail to believe deeply that leaving home is itself a means of departing far from the world, do not know that patience is a practice of peace and joy, do not know that equanimity is the path of awakening, and do not know that departing from clinging to appearances is the mind that transcends the world. Teacher and disciple, dwelling in the same quarters together, their accumulated karma not yet exhausted, oppose one another, and a mind of anger and contention arises confusedly between them — this is why, life after life, they cannot achieve harmony. Moreover, among those who have left home, whether fellow students of the same subject or disciples under the same teacher, when their understanding differs and the day of advancement comes, poison and hatred are harbored, without ever saying to oneself that it is only because of accumulated wisdom from past habituation that another has such merit while I lack such a wholesome root. Hearing or seeing another's glory, an unsettled nature stirs, and a defiled mind quickly gives rise to feelings of superiority or inferiority, provoking contention more often than achieving harmony, unable to think generously of others while dwelling humbly oneself, resenting one another instead; failing to examine one's own faults, one speaks only of another's shortcomings. Or, through the three poisons, they slander one another, with no mind of loyalty and trust, no thought of reverence — how, then, could there be any room left to remember that this violates the Buddha's own precept? Extending even to raising the voice, shouting, cursing, and using foul language, the instruction of teachers and masters is met with no belief or acceptance at all. Those of high, middle, and low station each harbor resentment, and because of this resentment, dispute one another further. Among the evil destinies, much of the enmity found there belongs to precisely this kind — teacher and disciple, fellow students dwelling together, high, middle, and low station — a single thought of resentment giving rise to enmity without measure. This is why the sūtra says: in this present life, a slight thought of resentment and mutual hatred grows, in the life to come, ever more severe, until it becomes a great enmity — how much more so, then, the evil karma accumulated over a whole lifetime? Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — none of us knows in which destiny we ourselves have already given rise to such grudges and enmities toward our teachers and masters of high, middle, and low station; enmity of this kind knows no end, no fixed term of years, and no measure of kalpas, and when the time comes to undergo its suffering, it will be unbearable. Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The story of the monk Huaguang's disciple is the sharpest illustration this liturgy offers of a point it makes elsewhere in more abstract terms: the disciple kept every precept without a single outward transgression, and was condemned anyway, purely for an inward contempt expressed in one remark. The liturgy's argument is not that outward conduct is worthless, but that it is not sufficient on its own — the specific target here is the arrogance of a student who is technically faultless yet privately dismissive of a teacher's worth, a fault that leaves no external trace and is therefore easy to overlook in oneself. Read alongside the extended passage on rivalry between fellow students that follows it, this section reads as pointed, practical instruction aimed at a monastic community living and training in close quarters, not an abstract meditation on gratitude in general.

3. Urgency, and the Two Kinds of Shame誡無常、明二種心

Chinese Source

今日道場同業大眾！前是總相為他三世眾怨解諸怨結，此下自淨宜督其心。相與今日何故受生死身不得解脫，進不覩面前授記、退不聞一音演說，良由罪業深厚、怨結牢固，非唯不見前佛後佛菩薩賢聖，而亦將恐十二分教聞聲傳響永隔心路，惡道怨對無從得免。捨此形命方沈沸海，輪轉三途備歷惡趣，何當復得見此人身。發如是意，實有切情之悲；運如是想，不覺痛心之苦。相與已得仰飡風化，割愛辭親捨榮棄俗，更無異緣，豈得不與時競各求所安。若不堅強其志、捍勞忍苦銜悲惻愴者，忽使身被篤疾，中陰相現，獄卒羅剎牛頭阿旁殊形異狀一朝而至，風刀解身心懷怖亂，眷屬號泣無所覺知。當此之時，欲求今日起一善心，五體投地禮佛懺悔，豈可復得？但有三途無量眾苦。今日大眾各自努力與時馳競，若任情適意則進趣理遲，捍勞忍苦則趣向心疾。故經言：悲是道場，忍疲苦故。發行是道場，能辦事故。故知萬善莊嚴不勤無託，欲度巨海非舟何寄。若有願樂之心，而不行願樂之事，其願必虛，未見其果。如絕糧之人心存百味，於其飢惱終無濟益。當知欲求勝妙果報，必須心事俱行相與，及時生增上心、懷慚愧意，懺悔滅罪解諸怨結。脫更處闇開了未期，人皆解脫，莫追後悔。各各至心等一痛切，五體投地，歸依世間大慈悲父。 南無彌勒佛　南無釋迦牟尼佛　南無定義佛　南無施願佛　南無寶眾佛　南無眾王佛　南無遊步佛　南無安隱佛　南無法差別佛　南無上尊佛　南無極高德佛　南無上師子音佛　南無樂戲佛　南無龍明佛　南無華山佛　南無龍喜佛　南無香自在佛　南無大名佛　南無天力佛　南無德鬘佛　南無龍手佛　南無善行意佛　南無因莊嚴佛　南無智勝佛　南無量月佛　南無寶語佛　南無日明佛　南無藥王菩薩　南無藥上菩薩　南無無邊身菩薩　南無觀世音菩薩 又復歸依如是十方盡虛空界一切三寶。 某甲等積集罪障深於大地，無明覆蔽長夜不曉，常隨三毒造怨對因，致使迷淪三有永無出期。今日以諸佛菩薩大慈悲力，始蒙覺悟心生慚愧，至誠求哀發露懺悔。願諸佛菩薩慈悲攝受，以大智慧力、不可思議力、無量自在力、降伏四魔力、滅諸煩惱力、滅諸怨結力、度脫眾生力、安隱眾生力、解脫地獄力、濟度餓鬼力、救拔畜生力、攝化阿脩羅力、攝受人道力、盡諸天諸仙漏力、無量無邊功德力、無量無盡智慧力，令四生六道一切眾怨同到道場，受某甲等今日懺悔，一切捨施無怨親想，所結怨業同得解脫，永離八難無四趣苦，常值諸佛聞法悟道，發菩提心行出世業，四等六度深心修習，一切行願等階十地，入金剛心俱成正覺。 今日道場同業大眾！夫怨對相尋皆由三業，莊嚴行人嬰諸苦報。相與既知是眾苦之本，宜應勇猛挫而滅之。滅苦之要唯有懺悔，故經稱歎世二健兒，一不作罪、二能懺悔。大眾今日將欲懺悔，當潔其心整肅其容，內懷慚愧悲暢於外，起二種心則無罪不滅。何者二種心？一慚、二愧。慚者慚天，愧者愧人。慚者自能懺悔滅諸怨對，愧者能教他人解諸結縛。慚者能作眾善，愧者能見隨喜。慚者內自羞恥，愧者發露向人。以是二法，能令行人得無礙樂。相與今日起大慚愧、作大懺悔，至心求哀四生六道。何以故爾？經言：一切眾生皆是親緣，或經為父母、或經為師長，乃至兄弟姊妹，一切皆然。良由墮無明網不復相知，既不相知多起觸惱，以觸惱故怨對無窮。大眾今日覺悟此意，至誠懇惻苦切用心，必令一念感十方佛，一拜斷除無量怨對。等一痛切五體投地，重復歸依世間大慈悲父。 南無彌勒佛　南無釋迦牟尼佛　南無定意佛　南無量形佛　南無照明佛　南無寶相佛　南無斷疑佛　南無善明佛　南無不虛步佛　南無覺悟佛　南無華相佛　南無山主王佛　南無大威德佛　南無遍見佛　南無量名佛　南無寶天佛　南無住義佛　南無滿意佛　南無上讚佛　南無無憂佛　南無無垢佛　南無梵天佛　南無華明佛　南無身差別佛　南無法明佛　南無盡見佛　南無德淨佛 南無文殊師利菩薩　南無普賢菩薩　南無無邊身菩薩　南無觀世音菩薩 又復歸依如是十方盡虛空界一切三寶。 仰願三寶同加攝受，令某甲等所懺除滅、所悔清淨。又願今日同懺悔者，從今已去乃至菩提，一切怨對皆得消滅，一切眾苦畢竟解脫，結習煩惱永得清淨，長辭四趣自在受生，親侍諸佛面奉尊記，六度四等無不備行，具四辯才得佛十力，相好嚴身神通無礙，入金剛心成等正覺。

Ru-Yi Translation

Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — up to now we have, in general terms, dissolved every grudge and enmity toward others across the three periods of time; from this point on, let us turn to purifying ourselves and govern our own minds. Together, today, why have we received this body of birth and death and not attained liberation, unable, looking forward, to behold a prediction of awakening given to our face, and unable, looking back, to hear the Buddha's own voice expounding the Dharma? This is truly because our karmic fault runs deep and our grudges and enmities lie firm and fast — not only do we fail to see the Buddhas past and future, the bodhisattvas and sages, but I fear we may even find the twelve divisions of the teachings, heard only as an echo of a sound, forever cut off from the path of our own mind, with no way to escape the enmity of the evil destinies. Once we give up this body and life, we shall sink into the boiling sea, turning through the three evil paths and passing through every evil destiny in full — when shall we ever obtain this human body again? To give rise to a thought such as this brings a truly urgent sorrow; to hold a reflection such as this, one cannot help but feel the pain of a stricken heart. We, together, have already been fortunate enough to receive this transforming influence, cutting off attachment, taking leave of our kin, giving up glory and abandoning the world — with no other condition remaining, how could we fail to race against time and each seek our own peace? If we do not make our resolve firm, endure hardship, bear suffering, and carry this grief and sorrow with us, then, should our body suddenly be struck with a grave illness, and the signs of the intermediate state appear, the jailers, rākṣasas, and ox-headed, horse-faced demons, in strange and monstrous forms, will arrive in a single morning, the wind-blades dismembering the body, the mind thrown into terror and confusion, while our own family wails around us, unaware of any of it. At such a time, should we wish to arouse a single wholesome thought, to prostrate ourselves fully to the ground and bow to the Buddha in confession — how could this possibly be achieved? There will be nothing left but the immeasurable suffering of the three evil paths. This assembly, today, should each strive on our own, racing against time. If we indulge our own feelings and seek only comfort, our advance toward the truth will be slow; if we endure hardship and bear suffering, our progress toward it will be swift. This is why the sūtra says: sorrow is itself the training-ground, because it enables one to endure weariness and suffering; setting out into practice is itself the training-ground, because it enables one to accomplish the task. Hence we know that the adornment of ten thousand goodnesses has no foundation without diligence, and that to cross the vast ocean, without a boat, what could one possibly rely on? If one has a mind that wishes for and delights in something, but does not carry out the deeds that wish and delight require, that wish will surely remain empty, and its fruit will never be seen. It is like a man cut off from food, whose mind dwells on a hundred flavors — such thoughts bring no relief at all to his hunger and distress. We should know that whoever wishes to seek an excellent, wondrous fruit must have mind and deed proceed together; let us, together, in time, give rise to a mind of exalted resolve, harbor a sense of shame, confess and dissolve our fault, and resolve every grudge and enmity. Should we instead remain in darkness with no fixed date for our own awakening, while everyone else attains liberation, do not pursue regret only afterward. Let each of us, with sincere mind, in this same anguish of resolve, prostrate ourselves fully to the ground, and take refuge in the great and compassionate father of the world. We disciples have accumulated fault and obstruction deeper than the great earth itself, covered by ignorance through a long night without dawning, constantly following the three poisons to create the causes of enmity, bringing about our own confusion and immersion in the three realms of existence, with no time of escape ever in sight. Today, through the power of the great compassion of the Buddhas and bodhisattvas, we have at last begun to awaken, and a sense of shame has arisen in us; with utmost sincerity we beg for mercy, laying our faults bare in confession. We pray that the Buddhas and bodhisattvas, with a compassionate mind, may receive us, by their power of great wisdom, their inconceivable power, their boundless power of mastery, their power to subdue the four demons, their power to extinguish every affliction, their power to dissolve every grudge and enmity, their power to deliver living beings, their power to bring living beings to peace, their power to liberate beings from the hells, their power to relieve the hungry ghosts, their power to rescue the animals, their power to gather and transform the asuras, their power to receive and gather the human realm, their power to exhaust the defilements of the gods and immortals, their immeasurable and boundless power of merit, and their immeasurable and inexhaustible power of wisdom, so that every enmity throughout the four modes of birth and six destinies may together arrive at this very rite, and receive our confession today, we disciples, giving it all up entirely with no notion of enemy or friend, so that the karma of enmity thus bound may together attain liberation, forever leaving behind the eight adverse conditions and free of the suffering of the four evil destinies, constantly encountering the Buddhas, hearing the Dharma, and awakening to the Way, arousing the awakening-mind and practicing the deeds that transcend the world, cultivating the four immeasurables and six perfections with a profound mind, every practice and vow equal in rank to the ten grounds, entering the vajra-mind and together accomplishing true and equal awakening. Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — enmity follows one after another entirely because of the three kinds of karma, entangling the practitioner in every kind of bitter retribution. We, together, already knowing that this is the very root of every suffering, should courageously crush and extinguish it. The essential means for extinguishing suffering is confession alone, and this is why the sūtra praises two brave men in the world: the first, one who commits no fault to begin with; the second, one who is able to confess. This assembly, today, about to undertake confession, should purify the mind and make solemn one's bearing, harboring shame within and expressing sorrow openly without — and by arousing two kinds of mind, there is no fault that cannot be dissolved. What are these two kinds of mind? The first is called cán (shame before oneself); the second is called kuì (shame before others). Cán is shame before heaven; kuì is shame before others. One who has cán is able to confess and dissolve every enmity by oneself; one who has kuì is able to teach others to resolve their own bonds. One who has cán is able to perform every kind of goodness; one who has kuì is able, upon seeing it, to rejoice in it. One who has cán feels disgrace within; one who has kuì lays it bare before others. By these two dharmas, a practitioner is able to attain unhindered joy. Let us, together, today, arouse this great sense of shame and undertake this great confession, with sincere mind begging for mercy on behalf of every being of the four modes of birth and six destinies. Why is this so? The sūtra says: every living being is our own kin — at some time each has been our parent, at some time our teacher, extending even to brothers and sisters, all just the same. It is truly because we have fallen into the net of ignorance that we no longer recognize one another, and, not recognizing one another, we frequently give rise to irritation, and because of this irritation, enmity is without end. This assembly, today, awakening to this very thought, with utmost sincerity, earnest and sorrowful, applying the mind fully, must let a single thought move the Buddhas of the ten directions, and let a single bow cut off immeasurable enmity. With this same anguish of resolve, let us prostrate ourselves fully to the ground, and once again take refuge in the great and compassionate father of the world. Homage to the Buddha Maitreya. Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The distinction the liturgy draws between the two Chinese terms usually paired together as "shame" is worth preserving rather than collapsing into a single English word: 慚 (cán) is defined here as shame felt before oneself or before heaven, and is what makes a person able to confess and reform on their own initiative; 愧 (kuì) is shame felt before others, and is what makes a person able to acknowledge fault openly and encourage others toward the same honesty. The fascicle's closing move — declaring that every being has, across countless lifetimes, already been our own parent or teacher — is the doctrinal payoff of the whole chapter: if this is literally true, then the grudges named throughout this fascicle were never against strangers to begin with, only against family whose past connection to us has simply been forgotten.
