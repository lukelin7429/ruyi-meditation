Fascicle 9

Hell by hell, then impermanence, prisoners, and the dedication opens《慈悲道場懺法》卷第九・為地獄餓鬼畜生道禮佛・警念無常・發迴向第三（初）

This fascicle completes the realm-by-realm homage with the hardest material in the liturgy: five rounds of homage naming dozens of specific hells by name, followed by the hungry ghosts and the animals, and a single sweeping vow for every being across all six destinies. It then turns inward with an extended, unflinching meditation on death and impermanence quoting the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, pays homage on behalf of the laborers who built the rite and, remarkably, the prisoners held in the era's actual penal institutions, and closes by opening the liturgy's dedication chapter with a verse recited in the assembly's own voice.

Chinese
《慈悲道場懺法》卷第九 — the ninth of ten fascicles. Completes the chapter Homage to the Buddhas on Behalf of the Six Destinies (為六道禮佛第二) with five rounds of hell-by-hell homage, the hungry ghosts, the animals, and a comprehensive vow (為六道發願第八); adds a meditation on impermanence (警念無常) and homage for laborers and prisoners; then opens the chapter Dedication (發迴向第三).
Covers
Homage to named hells — Avīci, the river of ashes, the forest of swords, the hell of drinking molten copper, the mountain of blades, and dozens more — each round following the liturgy's now-familiar formula; homage to the hungry ghosts and the animals; a single comprehensive vow asking that every being of the six destinies receive the very mind-qualities of a bodhisattva; an extended, graphic meditation on the process of dying, quoting the Nirvāṇa Sūtra on the loneliness of the road after death; homage paid on behalf of everyone who performed physical labor to stage the rite, extended remarkably to prisoners held at the era's actual Eastern and Western Foundries; and the opening of the dedication chapter, including a gāthā (verse) recited by the assembly in its own voice.
Why it matters
The homage to prisoners is one of the liturgy's most concrete gestures of compassion toward the living: named alongside the rite's own laborers, incarcerated people are prayed for by the actual names of sixth-century penal institutions, not left in the abstract language used for otherworldly beings. The meditation on death that precedes it is the liturgy's most sustained and least consoling passage — it does not soften the vividness of dying in order to make its point about urgency.
Difficulty
★★★☆☆ — the hell-by-hell sections are formulaic and can be read quickly in sequence, but the meditation on impermanence is dense, unrelenting, and worth reading slowly rather than skimmed alongside the more repetitive homage rounds.

1. Homage on Behalf of Avīci Hell為阿鼻地獄禮佛第一

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Having now finished the homage for the human destiny, we next turn to homage on behalf of the three evil destinies. Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — from taking refuge until reaching this very chapter, we have said again and again that though the ten thousand dharmas differ and their functions are not the same, when it comes to light contrasted with darkness, it is simply good set against evil: what is good is called the excellent path of gods and humans, what is evil is called the different track of the three evil paths; cultivating benevolence and righteousness returns one to the excellent path, while giving rise to cruelty and harm casts one down into the inferior one. Those who dwell on the excellent path do so truly through excellent karma, needing no contention to secure it, receiving natural and wondrous joy and awaiting the free wandering of liberation; those cast down into the inferior paths do so truly through inferior karma, dwelling amid walls of fire and nets of iron, eating iron pellets and hot metal, drinking molten stone and boiling copper, their years surpassing the very span of creation, their kalpas equal to the infinite. And the suffering of the hells cannot be endured in person even once: the spirit leaves this very body and its consciousness is cast into that fortress, repaid by the wheel of blades laid upon the body, requited by the millstone of fire that destroys the form — and yet life there will not shorten itself, but embraces this suffering across a long span of years. Even should one at last be freed from it, one falls again among the hungry ghosts, fire issuing from the very mouth, life there never whole. And once dead from that state, one falls yet again among the animals, undergoing every suffering once more — carrying heavy loads over great distances, driven through dangerous and difficult places, or laid out for the cauldron and the cutting-board, one's very flesh serving as another's meal, one's life never counted as one's own. Truly, the heavy suffering of the three evil paths, the sorrow of a long night with no dawn in sight — and though the difference between excellent and inferior is perfectly clear, no one seems able to believe it, for out of the very sense of "I," doubt readily arises, and because of this doubt, one rarely turns toward the good. This is why the Buddha says: there are ten matters in the world that, at death, lead one into the evil destinies — a mind not devoted to goodness and failing to cultivate merit; craving food and drink like a starving tiger; addicted to the taste of wine and delighting in harboring the poison of anger; constantly practicing folly and refusing to accept another's admonition; relying entirely on one's own strength to carry out every evil deed; delighting in killing living beings and trampling upon the solitary and the weak; forever siding with evil people to invade and oppress another's territory; speaking, whenever one does speak, words that are never true or essential; and showing no compassion toward anyone, giving rise to every evil deed instead. Should a person be such as this, he will not remain long in this world, and at death will enter the evil destinies. Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — as the Buddha has said, who among us could truly claim to be free of all this? And since none of us can claim to be entirely free of it, each of us still bears some portion of fault destined for the hells. Let the assembly each awaken to this very meaning, and race against time to walk the bodhisattva path, diligently seeking every dharma and benefiting living beings — first dissolving our own fault, and second, generating blessing for others — this is precisely to benefit both oneself and others, self and other both gaining together. Together, today, let us arouse a courageous mind, a firm mind, a compassionate mind, a mind that delivers all beings, and a mind that rescues living beings, and never forget this vow even once we reach the very seat of awakening. We look up and rely upon all the Buddhas and great bodhisattvas throughout the ten directions and the whole reach of empty space, their great spiritual power, their great power of compassion, their power to liberate the hells, their power to deliver the hungry ghosts, their power to rescue the animals, their great power of sacred spells, and their great and mighty power, so that whatever benefit we ourselves work toward and whatever we ourselves vow may be accomplished. With this same anguish of resolve, let us prostrate ourselves fully to the ground, on behalf of every living being suffering in the great hell of Avīci, and in the eighteen hells of freezing ice, the hell of darkness, the eighteen hot hells, the eighteen hells of the wheel of blades, the hell of the forest of swords, the hell of the fire chariot, the hell of boiling excrement, the hell of the boiling cauldron, and all the further eighty-four thousand subsidiary hells belonging to these — for every living being suffering within them, we disciples, by the awakening-mind, by the conduct of awakening, and by the vow of awakening, stand in on their behalf, taking refuge, every one of us, in the great and compassionate father of the world. We pray that, by the power of compassion, they rescue and gather in every living being suffering in the hell of Avīci, the hell of darkness, the hell of the wheel of blades, and every subsidiary hell of the fire chariot and boiling excrement, and that, by the power of the Buddha, the power of the Dharma, the power of the bodhisattvas, and the power of the sages, every such living being may at once attain liberation, never again to fall into the hells, every obstruction of fault dissolved, never again to create the karma that leads to the hells. Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The ten conditions the Buddha lists here as leading to an evil rebirth are notably unspectacular — a mind not devoted to goodness, gluttony, addiction to drink, refusing correction, stubbornness, cruelty toward the weak, keeping bad company, careless speech, and a general absence of compassion. None of these require the theatrical villainy the hell-names that follow might suggest; the liturgy's own argument, made explicitly in this section, is that no one in the assembly can honestly claim to be entirely free of this list, which is precisely why the homage is framed as standing in on behalf of hell-beings rather than observing them from a safe distance.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

From this section on, each round of hell-by-hell homage follows an identical shape: a list of specifically named hells, a set of twenty-some buddha-names unique to that round, and a dedication asking for the same handful of outcomes — liberation, the vajra-body, the joy of nirvāṇa. The repetition is deliberate rather than perfunctory: a rite meant to be performed aloud gains its force from steady accumulation across many names, not from novelty in any single round.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The names in this round — wailing, great wailing, heat, great heat — are drawn from the classical eight hot hells common across Buddhist cosmology, escalating pairs where the second member of each pair simply intensifies the first. The liturgy does not pause to explain this structure; it assumes an audience already fluent in this cosmology and moves straight to the work of praying each named suffering out of existence.

Ru-Yi Translation

Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — together we have now finished this homage and confession on behalf of the six destinies; next we should awaken to impermanence. Fault and blessing across the three periods of time arise from one another as cause and effect, a matter that should press upon the mind with real feeling, not something to be held at a careless distance; we have always said that cause and effect answer one another as surely as echo answers sound, so that good and evil, in their consequences, could never be confused with one another. We ask only that the assembly awaken to impermanence, diligently cultivating practice to sustain ourselves, and not give way to laziness and fail to make the effort. The wise have always lamented: even should one enjoy the pleasures of the five desires for a thousand myriad koṭis of years, one still could not in the end escape the suffering of the three evil paths — how much less, then, can we, whose own lives do not even reach a hundred years, let alone half of that, afford to remain at ease within so brief a span? Moreover, the illusions of this world return, in the end, to dissolution: what seems permanent is entirely exhausted, what stands lofty also falls, every gathering ends in separation, and whatever is born must surely die — high office and generous emolument, who has ever been spared from this? Parents, brothers, sisters, and every degree of kin, however deep the love that reaches to the very marrow, cannot take one's place when the moment comes to give up this life. Glory, rank, wealth, and every treasured possession likewise cannot extend a person's own span of years, nor can they be bought off with fine words or offerings of food and drink — this formless reckoning, who could ever hold it back? The sūtra says: to die is to be utterly finished — the breath cut off, the spirit departed, the body and its frame left desolate. Every person and every creature comes to this one end; nothing that is born fails to end. And at the very moment of giving up this life, one undergoes great anguish, surrounded by wailing kin both near and distant, the dying one terrified and not knowing where to turn, the body already hollow and cold, the breath about to fail, beholding now the very signs of the good and evil one has done: for the one who has cultivated good, heavenly spirits come to support and guard; for the one who has done evil, the ox-headed demon stands already at his side, and the jailers and rākṣasas grant no mercy at all. Loving parents and filial children cannot save one another then; husband and wife, however deep their affection, can only watch each other reach this very end. The wind-blades dismember the body in agony beyond words, the dying one's very liver and gall torn apart bit by bit, immeasurable pain and anguish gathering all at once, the spirit-consciousness thrown into confusion as though mad or drunk — and though one may resolve, at last, to arouse a single thought of good or perform even the smallest act of merit, harboring only regret in the heart, it can no longer be done. Suffering and anguish such as this, no one else can bear in one's place. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: the dying have no provisions for the perilous road ahead, the destination impossibly far and no companion to travel with, walking on day and night without any limit, through depths of darkness with no light at all, entering a place with no barrier to stop them, yet arriving nowhere they can ever escape from. Living without cultivating merit, one returns at death to a place of suffering, its sorrow and bitterness beyond any cure — and it is not some fearsome, evil form that inspires this dread, but this very reckoning itself. Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — the fruits of birth and death turn like a ring without end, the solitary spirit departing utterly alone with no one to witness it, impossible to search out, impossible to entrust anything to; we can only each strive to endure hardship and bear suffering, diligently cultivating the four immeasurables and six perfections, to serve as our own provision for that solitary journey through whatever destiny awaits — let none of us take comfort in our own present health and strength. Let each of us, with sincere mind, in this same anguish of resolve, prostrate ourselves fully to the ground, and take refuge in the great and compassionate father of the world. Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This is the least consoling passage in the liturgy so far, and deliberately so: it does not move quickly past the physical description of dying to reach a hopeful conclusion, but lingers on it at length — the cold body, the surrounding wails, the "wind-blades" of traditional Buddhist death-imagery, the moment when a dying person wants to summon one last good thought and simply cannot. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra citation that follows sharpens the point further: it is not any monstrous shape that makes death terrifying, but the sheer solitude of the road afterward, with "no provisions" and "no companion." Read against the confident, formulaic vows that surround it on both sides, this section functions as the liturgy's own reminder that its recitations are not automatic guarantees, and that the urgency behind them is meant to be felt, not merely recited.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The "Eastern and Western Foundries" named here were real Six Dynasties penal institutions — forced-labor smelting works where convicts served their sentences — not a cosmological category like the hells and hungry ghosts prayed for elsewhere in this fascicle. This is homage extended to the living and the currently incarcerated, addressed by the actual names of the era's own prisons, and it makes no distinction between those wrongly convicted and those who "bear so grave a fault that they face execution": the liturgy prays for release and reform without first sorting the deserving from the undeserving.

We ask only that every god, immortal, dragon spirit of the eightfold division, and every living being of the ten directions, from this day forward until reaching awakening, may forever dwell in the markless and never again cling to anything at all.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The gāthā recited here in the assembly's own voice — rather than the third-person invocation used everywhere else in this fascicle — states the liturgy's underlying logic of dedication as plainly as it appears anywhere in the entire text: merit clung to for oneself keeps one bound to the very reward it produces, while merit given away dissolves that attachment and thereby, paradoxically, becomes the more direct path to liberation. The vow embedded in the verse — "until every living being has attained buddhahood, I will not abandon the vow of awakening" — is the classical bodhisattva vow in its starkest form, promising to hold back one's own final awakening indefinitely rather than treating it as a race to be won individually.
