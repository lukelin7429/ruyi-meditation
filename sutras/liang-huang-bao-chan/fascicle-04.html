Fascicle 4

Concluding the fruits of retribution, and deliverance from the hells《慈悲道場懺法》卷第四

This fascicle first concludes the previous chapter with Maudgalyāyana's encounters with hungry ghosts and the Buddha's own narrations of cause and effect, then opens the chapter on Deliverance from the Hells with a full architectural description of the hell of Avīci, an extended narrative of its cycles of illusion and torment, and the origin and court of King Yama.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T1909 Chapters: 顯果報之餘・出地獄第二 Status: complete
Chinese
《慈悲道場懺法》卷第四 — the fourth of ten fascicles, opening with the remainder of "Explaining the Fruits of Retribution" (顯果報之餘) before beginning "Deliverance from the Hells" (出地獄第二).
Covers
Maudgalyāyana's own encounters with hungry ghosts by a riverbank, and the Buddha's narration of the karmic history behind a giant worm, a monk with a nailed tongue, a many-headed fish, and other figures; then the opening of the hell chapter proper: the physical architecture of the hells, the etymology of the name Avīci, an extended narrative of the illusions and cycles of torment that await the gravest offenders, homage to sacred images and stūpas, and the origin story and court of King Yama.
Content note
The description of the hell of Avīci and the narrative of its cycles of illusion and torment in this fascicle are among the most sustained passages of graphic suffering in the entire liturgy. This reflects the genre and purpose of the source material — a vivid warning, not narrative embellishment.
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ — the architectural description of Avīci in particular uses a dense, specific vocabulary of hell-geography (chambers, gates, encircling walls) that rewards a slow, careful read rather than skimming.

復有一鬼問目連言：「我一生來肩上有大銅瓶，盛滿烊銅，以杓取之還自灌頭，痛苦難忍。何罪所致？」目連答言：「汝為人時，作寺維那知大眾事。有一瓶酥藏著屏處，不依時行，待客去後乃行舊住。酥是招提之物，一切有分，緣汝無道慳惜眾物，以是因緣故獲斯罪。汝今華報，果在地獄。」 復有一鬼問目連言：「我一生來常吞熱鐵丸。何罪所致？」目連答言：「汝為人時作沙彌子，取清淨水作石蜜漿。石蜜堅大，汝起盜心打取少許，大眾未飲汝盜一口。以是因緣故獲斯罪。此是華報，果在地獄。」 Ru-Yi Translation

Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — once again, with utmost sincerity, listen with a single mind. The Buddha was at the Bamboo Grove of Kalandaka near Rājagṛha. At that time Maudgalyāyana, rising from meditative concentration, wandered along the banks of the Ganges, and saw hungry ghosts undergoing punishments of every different kind. The hungry ghosts, each arousing a reverent mind, came to ask Maudgalyāyana about the causes and conditions of their former lives. One ghost asked: "Since my birth in this form, I have constantly borne hunger and thirst, and wished to go to the latrine to eat the filth there — but a mighty ghost stationed at the latrine strikes me with a rod, and I can never draw near. What fault brought this about?" Maudgalyāyana answered: "When you were a person, you were the head of a monastery. A visiting monk came to the monastery to beg for food, but you, stingy, would not give him any, waiting until the guest had left before serving the resident monks as usual. Because you were without regard for the Way and stingy with the community's goods, you incurred this very fault because of this cause and condition. What you receive now is only the flowering retribution; the true fruit still awaits you in the hells." There was another ghost who asked Maudgalyāyana: "Since my birth in this form, I have carried a great bronze vessel on my shoulder, filled with molten bronze, and must ladle it out to pour over my own head, the pain unbearable. What fault brought this about?" Maudgalyāyana answered: "When you were a person, you served as the monastery's overseer, in charge of the community's affairs. There was a jar of ghee that you hid away in a private place, and did not distribute it at the proper time, waiting until the guests had left before serving it to the resident monks as usual. That ghee belonged to the wandering community, and everyone had a share in it — because you were without regard for the Way and stingy with the community's goods, you incurred this very fault because of this cause and condition. What you receive now is only the flowering retribution; the true fruit still awaits you in the hells." There was another ghost who asked Maudgalyāyana: "Since my birth in this form, I must constantly swallow red-hot iron pellets. What fault brought this about?" Maudgalyāyana answered: "When you were a person, you were a novice monk, and took pure water to make a sugar-cane syrup drink. The block of sugar was hard and large, and a thought of theft arose in you, so you broke off a small piece for yourself — before the community had even had their share, you stole a mouthful for yourself. Because of this cause and condition, you incurred this very fault. This too is only the flowering retribution; the true fruit still awaits you in the hells." Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — as Maudgalyāyana himself witnessed, this is truly a thing to be feared. We ourselves may well have committed faults such as these, covered by ignorance and unable to recall or recognize it. Since such immeasurable evil karma already exists, to be received as bitter retribution in the ages to come, today, with sincere mind, in this same anguish of resolve, let us prostrate ourselves fully to the ground in shame and confession, praying that it be dissolved. We further, on behalf of every hungry ghost throughout the ten directions and the whole reach of empty space, beg for mercy in confession; and on behalf of our parents and teachers, beg for mercy in confession; and on behalf of the venerable witnesses of high, middle, and low station who share this very platform with us, beg for mercy in confession; and on behalf of our good and evil companions, extending it out endlessly to every living being of the four modes of birth and six destinies throughout the ten directions, beg for mercy in confession — so that whatever fault we have already committed may, through this, be dissolved, and whatever fault we have not yet committed, we dare not commit. Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — once again, with utmost sincerity, listen with a single mind. At that time the Buddha was at Rājagṛha, where southeast of the city there was a pool of water into which all manner of filth and excrement flowed, its stench too foul to approach. A great worm was born in this water, its body many fathoms long, without hands or feet, writhing and rearing so that thousands came to watch it. Ānanda went to see it, and reported the whole matter fully to the Buddha. The Buddha, together with the assembly, went to the pool, and the assembly thought to themselves: "Today the Tathāgata will surely explain the origin of this worm to the assembled company." The Buddha told the assembly: "After the Buddha Vipaśyin entered nirvāṇa, there was a stūpa-monastery there, and five hundred monks, passing through the monastery, were joyfully invited to stay and receive offerings by the monastery's abbot, who provided them with meals with his whole heart, sparing nothing. Afterward, five hundred merchants, returning from gathering treasures at sea, passed by the same monastery, and, seeing the five hundred monks diligently practicing the Way, each of them was moved and, discussing it happily together, said that a field of merit such as this was hard to encounter, and that they should provide a modest offering. Each person contributed one pearl, and five hundred maṇi-jewels were gathered and entrusted to the monastery's abbot. The abbot, afterward, gave rise to an unwholesome mind, and, plotting to take them all for himself alone, refused to prepare any offering for the monks. The assembly asked him: 'The merchants have donated pearls, and an offering should be made.' The abbot answered: 'These pearls were given to me. If you wish to seize them from me, I will give you dung instead. And if you do not leave at once, I will cut off your hands and feet and throw you into the dung-pit.' The assembly, reckoning him a fool, said nothing and each departed. Because of this evil deed, he received this very worm's body, and will afterward enter the hells and undergo further suffering there." While the Buddha was at Rājagṛha, he further saw a being whose tongue was long and huge, pierced through with iron nails, ablaze with fire, undergoing bitter pain day and night without rest. Maudgalyāyana asked the Buddha: "What fault brought this retribution, that he now suffers so?" The Buddha answered Maudgalyāyana: "This person, in a former time, once served as the abbot of a monastery, and scolded and drove away visiting monks, giving them neither food nor drink nor any share of the offerings. Because of this cause and condition, he incurred this very fault." There was further a being whose body was of great size, upon whose head sat a cauldron ablaze with fire, filled with molten bronze that poured out on every side over his body, as he moved through the air without rest. Maudgalyāyana asked the Buddha: "What fault brought this about, that he now suffers so?" The Buddha answered Maudgalyāyana: "This person, in a former time, served as the manager of a monastery's affairs; when a donor sent oil, he did not share it with the visiting monks, waiting until the guests had left before distributing it to the resident monks as usual. Because of this cause and condition, he incurred this very fault." There was further a being through whom red-hot iron pellets entered at the top of the body and emerged at the bottom, moving through the air in pain hard to bear. Maudgalyāyana asked the Buddha: "What fault brought this about, that he now suffers so?" The Buddha answered Maudgalyāyana: "This person, in a former time, was a novice monk who stole seven pieces of fruit from the community's orchard; at death he entered the hells and underwent immeasurable suffering there, and, his remaining karma not yet exhausted, he incurred this very fault as well." He further saw a great fish, one body with a hundred heads, each head different from the others, fallen into another's net. Upon seeing this, the World-Honored One entered the samādhi of loving-kindness and called out to the fish, and the fish answered at once. The World-Honored One asked: "Where is your mother?" It answered: "My mother is a worm in a latrine." The Buddha said to the monks: "This great fish was, in the time of the Buddha Kāśyapa, a monk versed in the Tripiṭaka, and, because of harsh speech, received this retribution of many heads. His mother, at that time, accepted the offerings made to him, and because of this cause and condition became a worm in a latrine." The Buddha said: "Whoever receives this retribution does so entirely because of harsh and coarse speech, spreading rumors between one party and another to stir up strife between two households. At death, they enter the hells, where the jailers heat an iron probe until it glows red through and through, and sear the tongue with it; they further heat an iron hook with three blades, sharp as a razor's edge, and use it to cut off the tongue; they further plow and split the tongue with an ox and plow; and they further heat an iron pestle and drive it into the throat. Only after thousands upon thousands of kalpas is this fault exhausted, and one is then born among birds and beasts." The Buddha said: "Should any living being speak ill of their ruler, their parents, or their teachers, their fault exceeds even this." Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — hearing the Buddha speak in this way, this is truly a thing to be feared. The two paths of good and evil are now plainly visible, the fruits of fault and merit clearly understood without any doubt, and we should only strive diligently to confess. Let us together, having opened the sūtra and seen this matter in full, take care not to slacken or retreat even slightly — for if we do, by what means shall what we now attempt ever be accomplished? It is like a starving man whose mind dwells on a hundred flavors — such thoughts bring no relief at all to his hunger and distress. Hence we know that whoever wishes to seek the excellent, wondrous Dharma, and wishes to deliver living beings, cannot let it remain a matter of thought alone. Since it is a matter of the mind, we should apply our own effort and diligently put it into practice. 2. Deliverance from the Hells: Doubt, and the Architecture of Avīci出地獄第二・阿鼻地獄

今日道場同業大眾！雖復萬法差品功用不一，至於明闇相形唯善與惡，語善則人天勝果，述惡則三途劇報，二事列世皎然非虛。而愚惑之者多起疑異，或言人天是妄造，地獄非真說，不知推因驗果，不知驗果尋因。既因果不分，各執世解，非但言空談有，乃亦題篇造論，心乖勝善未曾云謬，設使示誨執固益堅。如是等人自投惡道，如射箭頃墮在地獄，慈親孝子不能相救。唯得前行入於火鑊，身心摧碎精神痛苦，當此之時悔復何及。 劫欲盡時東門即開，見東門外清泉流水華果林樹一切俱現。是諸罪人從下鬲見，眼火暫歇。從下鬲起，宛轉腹行，𢫫身上走到上鬲中，手攀刀輪，時虛空中雨熱鐵丸。走趣東門，既至門閫。獄卒羅剎手捉鐵叉逆刺其眼，銅狗齧心悶絕而死，死已復生。見南門開如前不異，如是西門北門亦皆如此，如此時間經歷半劫。阿鼻獄死復生寒氷獄中，寒氷獄死生黑闇處，八千萬歲目無所見。受大虫身宛轉腹行，諸情闇塞無所解知，百千狐狼牽掣食之。命終之後生畜生中，五千萬身受鳥獸形。如是罪畢還生人中，聾盲瘖瘂疥癩癰疽，貧窮下賤一切諸衰以自莊嚴。受此賤形經五百身，後復還生餓鬼道中。餓鬼道中遇善知識諸大菩薩，呵責其言：『汝於前身無量世時作無限罪，誹謗不信墮阿鼻獄，受諸苦惱不可具說。汝今應當發慈悲心。』時諸餓鬼聞是語已，稱南無佛。稱佛恩力，尋即命終生四天處。生彼天已悔過自責，發菩提心。諸佛心光不捨是等，攝受是輩，慈哀是等如羅睺羅，教避地獄如愛眼目。」佛告大王：「欲知佛心光明所照，常照如此無間無救諸苦眾生。佛心所緣，常緣此等極惡眾生。以佛心力自莊嚴故，過算數劫，令彼惡人發菩提心。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — though the ten thousand dharmas differ in kind and their functions are not uniform, when it comes to light and dark set against one another, there is only good and evil: speak of good, and it yields the excellent fruit of humans and gods; describe evil, and it yields the severe retribution of the three evil paths — these two things stand plainly displayed in the world, and are no empty claim. Yet the foolish and deluded often give rise to doubt and difference, some saying that the realms of humans and gods are fabrications, that the hells are no true teaching, not knowing how to trace effect back to cause, nor how to trace cause forward to effect. Since they cannot distinguish cause from effect, each clings to his own worldly interpretation — not content merely to argue in speech that emptiness is existence, they even compose essays and treatises on it, their minds turned against excellent goodness, never once admitting their own error, and the more they are instructed, the more stubbornly they cling to their view. People such as this cast themselves into the evil destinies, falling into the hells in the time it takes an arrow to fly, and no loving parent or filial child can come to their rescue. They can only go forward into the cauldron of fire, body and mind shattered, spirit in agony — and at such a time, what good can regret possibly do? Today, the assembly gathered together in this common practice — good and evil depend upon one another exactly as a shadow depends on a form, or an echo on a sound; fault and merit dwell in different places, prepared and awaiting us with severity from long before. May each of us, fortunately, hold this clear belief and set aside every trace of doubt. What is meant by the hells? The sūtra says: in the space of darkness between the two ranges of the Encircling Iron Mountains of a great trichiliocosm, this is called the hells. The iron city measures sixteen million li in length and breadth, and within the city are eighty-four thousand chambers; below, the ground is made of iron, and above, a net of iron covers it; fire burns this city so that it glows red through and through, inside and out, the fire reaching from above all the way through to below and from below all the way through to above. Among its names are: Crushing-Together and Darkness, the Wheel of Blades and Forest of Swords, the Iron Frame and Forest of Thorns, the Iron Net and Iron Cave, Iron Pellets and Sharp Stones, the Charcoal Pit and Burning Forest, the Howling of Tigers and Wolves, the Boiling Cauldron and Burning Coals, the Mountain of Blades and Forest of Swords, the Fire-Mill and City of Fire, the Bronze Pillar and Iron Bed, the Fire-Chariot and Wheel of Fire, Drinking Bronze and Vomiting Fire, Great Heat and Great Cold, Tongue-Pulling and Body-Nailing, the Plow and the Axe, Blade and Weapon and Butchery, the River of Ash and Boiling Filth, Freezing Ice and Mire, Foolish Wailing, Deafness, Blindness, and Muteness, and the Iron Hook and Iron Beak. There are also the greater and lesser Naraka hells, and the hell of Avīci. The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Why is it called the hell of Avīci? 'A' means without, and 'vīci' means shelter; 'a' means without, and 'vīci' means rescue — together meaning without shelter and without rescue. Also, 'a' means uninterrupted, and 'vīci' means unmoving. 'A' means extreme heat, and 'vīci' means extreme torment. 'A' means without respite, and 'vīci' means without cessation — without respite and without cessation is why it is called the hell of Avīci. Also, 'a' means great flame, and 'vīci' means fierce heat — a fierce fire that enters the very heart is why it is called the hell of Avīci." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "The hell of Avīci measures three hundred and twenty thousand li exactly in length and breadth, with seven layers of iron walls and seven levels of iron netting; below are eighteen chambers, and all around, in seven layers, stand forests of blades, with a further forest of swords within the seven-layered wall. Below these eighteen chambers lie eighty-four thousand further chambers in layers. At its four corners stand four great bronze dogs, their bodies sixteen thousand li in length, their eyes like flashing lightning, their fangs like a forest of swords, their teeth like a mountain of blades, their tongues like iron thorns, every hair on their bodies pouring forth fierce flame, their smoke so foul that nothing foul in this world could serve as its comparison. There are also eighteen jailers, their heads like the heads of rākṣasas, their mouths like the mouths of yakṣas, with sixty-four eyes, from which iron pellets burst forth the size of a cart wheel ten li across. Their hooked fangs jut upward a hundred and sixty li, and fire streams from the tips of these fangs, burning the iron chariots before them, so that every rim of every iron wheel is transformed into a hundred million flames, and blades, edges, swords, and halberds all emerge from within these flames. Fire streaming in this way burns the city of Avīci, until the whole city of Avīci glows red as molten bronze. Upon the jailers' heads are eight ox-heads, each ox-head bearing eighteen horns, and from the tip of each horn bursts forth a mass of fire, which then transforms into eighteen fiery rims, and these fiery rims in turn transform into great wheels of blades the size of cart wheels, wheel following upon wheel amid the flames, filling the hell of Avīci entirely. The bronze dogs open their mouths and let their tongues hang to the ground, tongues like iron thorns, and when these tongues emerge, they transform into countless tongues that fill the city of Avīci. Within the seven-layered wall stand seven iron banners, from whose tips fire gushes forth like a boiling spring, this molten iron flowing and filling the city of Avīci. At the four gates of Avīci, above the thresholds, stand eighteen cauldrons, from which boiling bronze wells up and flows out through the gates, filling the city of Avīci. Between each and every chamber are eighty-four thousand great iron pythons and serpents, spitting poison and fire, their bodies filling the whole city; these serpents roar like heaven's own thunder, raining down great iron pellets that fill the city of Avīci. Within this city there are eighty billion, eighty thousand torments, and the very worst of all torments are gathered together in this one city. There are further fifty billion insects, each insect with eighty-four thousand beaks, from the tips of which fire streams down like rain, filling the city of Avīci. When these insects descend, the fierce fire of Avīci blazes still more fiercely, its red glow and flame illuminating three million, three hundred and sixty thousand li, rising up from the hell of Avīci to strike the great ocean and the mountain that consumes it beneath, so that the great ocean's very water, drop by drop the size of a cart-axle, is transformed into great iron spikes filling the city of Avīci." The Buddha told Ānanda: "Should any living being kill their father, harm their mother, or curse and revile their six degrees of kin — for such a fault, when their life comes to an end, the bronze dogs open their mouths and transform into eighteen chariots, each in the form of a golden chariot with a jeweled canopy above it, and every flame transforms into a jade maiden. Seeing this from afar, the guilty person's heart fills with joy: 'I wish to go there, I wish to go there!' As the wind-blades dismember the body, and the cold grows urgent, he loses his voice and cries: 'If only I could have that good fire!' Sitting upon the chariot, the fire ignites of itself. The moment this thought is formed, his life ends at once. In the flash of an instant, he finds himself seated in the golden chariot, gazing at the jade maidens — and they all seize iron axes and hack his body to pieces. Fire rises up from beneath his body like a spinning wheel of flame, and, in the time it takes a strong man to bend and stretch his arm, he falls straight down into the great hell of Avīci. From the uppermost chamber, spinning like a wheel of fire, down to the very lowest chamber, his body fills every chamber entirely. The bronze dogs roar loudly, gnawing his bones and sucking his marrow; the rākṣasa-jailers seize great iron forks and spear his head to make him rise, flames covering his whole body and filling the city of Avīci, while iron nets rain down blades that pierce through every pore of his skin. A manifestation of King Yama proclaims in a great voice: 'Foolish man, seed of the hells! In the world you were unfilial to your parents, perverse, arrogant, and without the Way — the place where you are now born is called the hell of Avīci. You knew no gratitude and felt no shame — do you find this suffering and affliction a pleasure, or not?' Having spoken these words, the vision vanishes and appears no more. At that time the jailers again drive the guilty one, from the lowest chamber up to the highest, passing through all eighty-four thousand chambers, his body dragged along until he reaches the very edge of the iron net — a single day and night pass in completing this whole circuit. But a single day and night in the hell of Avīci equals, by the reckoning of this Jambudvīpa's sun and moon, sixty small kalpas, and his lifespan there is not exhausted until a whole great kalpa has passed. One who has committed the five heinous crimes, without shame and without remorse, having committed these five crimes, at the moment his life comes to an end, the eighteen wind-blades, like an iron chariot of fire, dismember his body. Pressed by the heat, he says to himself: 'If only I could find a fine, cool, shady great tree beneath which to play — would that not be joyful!' The moment this thought arises, the eighty-four thousand evil forests of swords in the hell of Avīci transform into jeweled trees, their flowers and fruit lush and arrayed before him, and the great scorching flames transform into lotus flowers beneath that tree. Seeing this, the guilty man thinks: 'What I have wished for, I have now obtained.' The instant he says this, faster than a sudden downpour, he finds himself seated upon the lotus flower. No sooner has he sat down than iron-beaked insects rise up from the fiery flowers, boring through bone, entering the marrow, piercing the heart, and drilling through the brain. Climbing up the tree, every branch of blades shaves away his flesh down to the bone, and countless forests of blades descend upon him from above. The fire-chariot, the burning coals, and all eighteen kinds of torment arrive at once to greet him. The moment these signs appear, he plunges down into the ground below. Rising from the lowest chamber, his body, spread out like an unfolding flower, fills the lowest chamber entirely, and, rising from the lowest chamber, the fierce blaze of fire reaches all the way up to the highest chamber. Once at the highest chamber, his body fills it entirely, and, driven frantic by the urgency of the heat, he opens his eyes wide and thrusts out his tongue. Because of this person's fault, a hundred million measures of molten bronze and a hundred thousand wheels of blades fall from the sky, entering through the head and emerging through the feet. Every torment here exceeds those described above by a hundred thousand myriad times. Whoever has committed all five heinous crimes in full undergoes this punishment for a full five kalpas. There are further living beings who break the Buddha's own precepts while emptily consuming the offerings given in faith, who slander the Dharma, hold wrong views, do not recognize cause and effect, cut off their own study of the Perfection of Wisdom, defame the Buddhas of the ten directions, steal the property of the Buddha-dharma, engage in every kind of defiled and impure conduct, feel no shame, insult and defile their own kin, and commit every kind of evil deed. For such a person's fault, when their life comes to an end, the wind-blades dismember the body, and they lie tossing restlessly, as though being flogged. Their mind grows wild and deranged, giving rise to mad and foolish visions, and they see their own household, men and women, young and old, as nothing but impure things, filth and stench overflowing everywhere. At that time the guilty one says to himself: 'Why is there no fine walled city or fine mountain forest here for me to enjoy myself in — why must I dwell among such filthy things?' The moment he says this, the rākṣasa-jailers, holding up the hell of Avīci and its forests of blades on great iron forks, transform them into jeweled trees and a cool, clear pool, the flames transform into golden-leaved lotus flowers, the iron-beaked insects transform into ducks and wild geese, and the cries of pain from the hells sound like singing and music. Hearing this, the guilty one thinks: 'What a fine place — I shall go and enjoy myself there.' The moment he thinks this, he finds himself seated upon a fiery lotus, and the iron-beaked insects bore through every pore of his skin and devour his flesh, a hundred thousand iron wheels enter through the crown of his head, and iron forks as numerous as the sands of the river gouge out his eyes. The bronze dogs of hell transform into a hundred million iron dogs, competing to tear apart his body and eat his heart. In an instant, his body becomes like an iron flower filling all eighteen chambers, each flower with eighty-four thousand petals, and on the tip of each petal lies a body, a hand, or a limb within a single chamber. The hell is not large, and this body is not small, yet it fills this great hell entirely. Guilty ones such as these, having fallen into this hell, pass through eighty-four thousand great kalpas there. When this hell is finally exhausted, they enter the eighteen chambers to the east and undergo suffering there just as before. This hell of Avīci likewise has eighteen chambers to the south, eighteen to the west, and eighteen to the north. Whoever slanders the Vaipulya sūtras, commits the five heinous crimes in full, destroys the sages and worthies, and cuts off every wholesome root — a guilty one who has committed every one of these faults in full has a body that fills the hell of Avīci, its four limbs also filling the eighteen chambers. This hell of Avīci burns only beings who are the very seed of such hells. When the kalpa is about to end, the eastern gate opens, and beyond it a clear spring, flowing water, and flowering trees and fruit all appear together. These guilty ones, seeing this from the lowest chamber, find the fire momentarily fading from their eyes. Dying in the hell of Avīci, they are reborn in the hell of freezing ice; dying in the hell of freezing ice, they are born in a place of darkness, their eyes seeing nothing for eighty million years. When that life ends, they are born among the animals, receiving the forms of birds and beasts across fifty million bodies. When this fault, too, is exhausted, they are born again among humans, deaf, blind, mute, afflicted with scabies and leprosy and abscesses, poor and lowly, every kind of misfortune adorning their very person. Among the hungry ghosts, they encounter a good companion, one of the great bodhisattvas, who rebukes them, saying: 'In your former bodies, across immeasurable lifetimes, you committed boundless fault, slandering and disbelieving, and fell into the hell of Avīci, undergoing every affliction beyond full description. What the Buddha's mind takes as its object is constantly these very beings of the utmost evil. By the power of the Buddha's own mind, adorning itself in this way, across kalpas beyond counting, he causes even such evil people to arouse the awakening-mind."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The mechanism running through both extended narratives here — the golden chariot that turns out to be an execution, the cool tree and lotus pond that turn out to be a fresh torment — is deliberate and repeated: desire itself is what delivers the guilty into their own punishment, since it is the condemned person's own craving for comfort that conjures the illusion and carries them straight into it. This is a sharper and more specific claim than "desire causes suffering" in the abstract — it is staged here as a mechanism, where wanting something pleasant is the very trigger that produces its opposite. The numerical scale-up in the final long paragraph — a single life's punishment stretching to a lifespan measured in kalpas, then multiplied across an escape-and-recapture cycle at all four gates — is characteristic of this genre's rhetorical method: overwhelming scale standing in for argument.

How, then, could each of us fail to practice the Way while there is still time? Let us together, today, with this same anguish of resolve, prostrate ourselves fully to the ground, on behalf of every hell-lord and minister of the hell-destiny throughout the ten directions, the ox-headed and horse-faced jailers, and each of their kin; on behalf of every hungry ghost and hungry-ghost spirit, and each of their kin; on behalf of every animal and animal-spirit, and each of their kin; and, extending it out endlessly to the ten directions, on behalf of every living being, beg for mercy in confession, correcting what is past and cultivating what is to come, no longer doing evil — praying that whatever fault we have already committed be dissolved, and that we dare not commit again what we have not yet committed. We pray only that all the Buddhas of the ten directions, by their inconceivable, sovereign, supernormal power, may together extend their rescue, their compassionate regard, and their reception, so that all living beings may attain liberation in due time. By their sovereign, supernormal power, may they rescue the hell-lords and ministers of the hell-destiny, and every dependent of the hells, the eighteen lesser cell-hells — and each of these eighteen lesser cell-hells, in turn, has its own dependent hells — so that every hell throughout the hell-destiny, the ox-headed and horse-faced jailers, and every suffering being there, may today all attain liberation together, their fault and its bitter fruit dissolved together. Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The jailer's own self-justifying speech is one of the more striking rhetorical devices in this fascicle: rather than simply describing punishment from the outside, the text gives the jailer a voice and lets him argue his own case — that his cruelty is, from his perspective, a form of failed compassion, since kindness and warning have already been tried and repeatedly ignored by beings who return to hell again and again. Whether or not this argument is meant to be endorsed, it functions as a mirror held up to the listener: the jailer's frustration at incorrigible repeat offenders is a caricature of exactly the response this liturgy hopes its own audience will not need to hear directed at them.
