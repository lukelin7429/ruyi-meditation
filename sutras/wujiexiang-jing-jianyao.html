The Marks of the Five Precepts for Upāsakas, Annotated

When King Śuddhodana asked the Buddha to set out the five precepts for lay disciples, he was asking a genuinely practical question: not what the precepts are, but exactly where the lines fall — which failures can still be repaired, and which cannot. Guṇavarman's fifth-century translation answers case by case, precept by precept, in the manner of a casebook: modes of killing, kinds of theft, whom one may not touch, what counts as a lie, how wine itself is defined. Ouyi Zhixu's Ming-dynasty gloss (箋要) explains the technical apparatus as it goes, and a later editor, Tan Fang, appends his own three-chapter supplement — citing the fuller Vinaya where the abridged text runs thin, filling in a table of offense-grades, and closing with a set of miscellaneous notes.

1. Prologue: Why King Śuddhodana Asked

The sūtra opens with a frame story: King Śuddhodana, the Buddha's own father, asks for a lay counterpart to the monastic disciplinary code — a way of knowing, precept by precept, which violations can be repaired and which cannot. Ouyi's gloss makes the stakes explicit before a single precept is even stated: the five precepts are the gate through which every buddha of every age has passed.

Chinese Source

聞如是。一時佛在迦維羅衛國。爾時淨飯王來詣佛所。頭面禮足。合掌恭敬。而白佛言。欲所請求。以自濟度。惟願世尊。哀詶我志。佛言。可得之願。隨王所求。王白佛言。世尊已為比丘。比丘尼。沙彌。沙彌尼。制戒輕重。唯願如來。亦為我等優婆塞。分別五戒可悔不可悔者。令識戒相。使無疑惑。

迦維羅衛。中天竺國之名。即世尊生處也。淨飯王。即世尊之父。以父王為當機而請五戒法相。正表此五戒法。乃是三世諸佛之父。依於五戒出生十方三世一切諸佛。詎可忽哉。

佛言。善哉。善哉。憍曇。我本心念。久欲與優婆塞分別五戒。若有善男子受持不犯者。以是因緣。當成佛道。若有犯而不悔。常在三塗故。

上契佛意。下契群機。故再歎善哉也。憍曇。即瞿曇。是王之姓。西國以稱姓為敬故。受持不犯。則當成佛。犯而不悔。則墮三塗。五戒為法界。十法界皆趣五戒。皆趣不過也。

問。受持不犯。當成佛道。受而犯者。亦當成佛否。犯而不悔。常在三塗。犯而悔者。亦墮三塗否。答。受而犯者亦當成佛。惟不受戒。則永無成佛因緣。犯而悔者。不墮三塗。但犯分上中下三種差別。悔亦有作法。取相。無生三種不同。理須各就當戒委明。未可一言盡也。

爾時佛為淨飯王種種說已。王聞法竟。前禮佛足。遶佛而去。

佛以是因緣。告諸比丘。我今欲為諸優婆塞。說犯戒輕重。可悔不可悔者。諸比丘僉曰。唯然。願樂欲聞。

問。比丘律儀是大僧法。所以不許俗聞。今五戒相是優婆塞所學。何故不向王說。乃待王去之後。以是因緣告比丘耶。答。七眾戒法。如來皆於比丘僧中結者。正以比丘為七眾中尊。佛法藉僧寶而立。故云。佛滅度後。諸尼應從大僧而學戒法。夫尼戒尚屬比丘。況五戒而不屬比丘耶。故今向比丘僧說此五戒。正欲令優婆塞轉從比丘學也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard. At one time the Buddha was in the country of Kapilavastu. At that time King Śuddhodana came to where the Buddha was, bowed his head to the Buddha's feet, and with palms joined in reverence said to the Buddha: "I wish to make a request, that I may thereby save and deliver myself. I only pray that the World-Honored One will take pity and grant my wish." The Buddha said: "What can be granted, I grant, according to what the king seeks." The king said to the Buddha: "The World-Honored One has already established the precepts, light and grave, for monks, nuns, novice monks, and novice nuns. I only pray that the Tathāgata will also, for us upāsakas, distinguish the five precepts as to what is repentable and what is unrepentable, so that we may recognize the marks of the precepts and be free of doubt."

Kapilavastu is the name of a country in central India — it is precisely the place where the World-Honored One was born. King Śuddhodana is precisely the World-Honored One's own father. That the father-king should be the one whose capacity prompts this request for the marks of the five precepts precisely shows that this Dharma of the five precepts is itself the father of the buddhas of the three periods of time: it is in dependence on the five precepts that all buddhas of the ten directions and three periods of time are brought forth. How, then, could this be treated lightly?

The Buddha said: "Good, good, Gautama. It has long been my own intention to distinguish the five precepts for the upāsakas. If there is a good son who receives and upholds them without transgressing, then by this cause and condition he shall accomplish the path to buddhahood; if there is one who transgresses and does not repent, he shall remain constantly in the three evil destinies."

Above, this accords with the Buddha's own intention; below, it accords with the capacities of the assembly — hence he praises it twice with "good, good." Gautama is Gotama, the king's clan-name; in the lands of the West, addressing someone by clan-name was a mark of respect. To receive and uphold without transgressing is to accomplish buddhahood; to transgress without repenting is to fall into the three evil destinies. The five precepts constitute the Dharma-realm — all ten Dharma-realms converge upon the five precepts; none goes beyond this convergence.

Question: to receive and uphold without transgressing is to accomplish the path to buddhahood — but does one who receives and then transgresses also accomplish buddhahood? To transgress without repenting is to remain constantly in the three evil destinies — but does one who transgresses and repents also fall into the three evil destinies? Answer: one who receives and then transgresses shall also accomplish buddhahood; it is only one who never receives the precepts at all who has, forever, no cause or condition for accomplishing buddhahood. One who transgresses and repents does not fall into the three evil destinies — but transgression itself divides into the three distinct grades of upper, middle, and lower, and repentance likewise has the three distinct kinds of confession by formal act, confession through a sign, and confession through non-arising. In principle these must each be explained in full detail under the particular precept concerned; they cannot be exhausted in a single statement.

At that time, when the Buddha had finished expounding various teachings for King Śuddhodana, the king, having heard the Dharma to its end, came forward, bowed at the Buddha's feet, circled the Buddha, and departed.

By this cause and condition, the Buddha addressed the monks: "I now wish, for the sake of the upāsakas, to explain the light and grave transgressions of the precepts, what is repentable and what is unrepentable." The monks all together said: "So be it, World-Honored One — we wish and delight to hear it."

Question: the disciplinary code of monks is the Dharma of the great sangha, and for this reason lay people are not permitted to hear it. But the marks of the five precepts here are precisely what the upāsakas themselves are to study — why, then, did the Buddha not explain them directly to the king, but instead wait until after the king had departed, and only then, by this cause and condition, address the monks? Answer: the precept-Dharma of the seven assemblies is, in every case, established by the Tathāgata within the assembly of monks — precisely because monks are honored above the seven assemblies, and the Buddha-dharma is established in dependence on the Jewel of the Sangha. Hence it is said: after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, the nuns should study the precept-Dharma following the great monks. If even the precepts of nuns belong under the monks in this way, how much more should the five precepts belong under the monks? This is why the five precepts are now explained to the assembly of monks — precisely so that the upāsakas will, in turn, study them from the monks.

Reading note

The dialogue Ouyi appends to the prologue is doing real doctrinal work before the casebook even starts: receiving the precepts and later transgressing them still leads toward buddhahood, because reception itself plants the cause; only never receiving them at all forecloses that path completely. This reframes everything that follows — the elaborate grading of offenses below is not a scorecard for excluding people, it is a repair manual. Even the gravest, "unrepentable" offense only closes the door on repairing this particular reception; it does not close the door on the person.

Worth noticing too is why the Buddha waits until the king leaves before addressing the monks at all. The explanation Ouyi gives — that every tier of precept-Dharma, even the lay tier, is formally transmitted through the sangha — sets up something the rest of the text assumes throughout: an upāsaka does not simply read these rules off the page and self-administer them. The marks of the precepts are handed down through a living line of teachers, which is exactly the apparatus that later chapters of Tan Fang's supplement will lean on when they explain how an offense is confessed, or how a precept is formally set down.

2. Precept One, Killing (I): The Three and Five Modes of Taking Life, and the Smokeless Fire-Pit

The killing precept opens with the mechanics of intent and agency — acting oneself, instructing another, or dispatching an agent — then works through what "the means" can consist of: the body alone, a weapon alone, both together, or poison. Ouyi's gloss states the five conditions that make an unrepentable offense explicit at the outset, since every subsequent case in this precept is really just a variation worked against that same five-part test. The section closes with the sūtra's most elaborate single case-study, the smokeless fire-pit, used to establish how the grade of offense shifts depending on who actually falls in.

殺戒第一

佛告諸比丘。犯殺。有三種奪人命。一者自作。二者教人。三者遣使。△自作者。自身作。奪他命。

△

教人者。教語他人言。捉是人。繫縛奪命。

△

遣使者。語他人言。汝識某甲不。汝捉是人。繫縛奪命。是使隨語奪彼命時。優婆塞犯不可悔罪。

殺戒以五緣成不可悔。一是人。（謂所殺者人。非畜生等。）二人想。（謂意在殺人。）三殺心。四興方便。五前人命斷。今之自作。教人。遣使。皆是以殺心而興方便。故奪彼命時。犯不可悔罪也。不可悔者。初受優婆塞戒之時。說三歸竟。即得無作戒體。今犯殺人之罪。則失無作戒體。不復成優婆塞。故不可作法懺悔也。既不可悔。則永棄佛海邊外。名為邊罪。不可更受五戒。亦不得受一日一夜八關齋戒。亦不得受沙彌戒及比丘戒。亦不得受菩薩大戒。惟得依大乘法修取相懺。見好相已。方許受菩薩戒。亦許重受具戒。十戒。八戒及五戒等。爾時破戒之罪。雖由取相懺滅。不墮三塗。然其世間性罪仍在。故至因緣會遇之時。仍須酬償夙債。除入涅槃或生西方。乃能脫之。不受報耳。可不戒乎。

內色者。優婆塞用手打他。若用足及餘身分。作如是念。令彼因死。彼因死者。是犯不可悔罪。若不即死。後因是死。亦犯不可悔。若不即死後不因死。是中罪可悔。

△

用內非內色者。若以手捉木瓦石刀矟弓箭白鑞段鉛錫段木段。打他。作如是念。令彼因死。彼因死者。是罪不可悔。若不即死。後因是死。亦犯不可悔。若不即死。後不因死。是中罪可悔。

此三種亦皆殺法所謂興方便也。手足身分。是凡情之所執受。故名內色。木瓦石等。是凡情所不執受。名非內色。有處亦名外色。用彼內色。捉彼外色。故為雙用內非內色也。因此方便而死。不論即死後死。總是遂其殺心。故從前人命斷之時。結成不可悔罪。後不因死。則但有興殺方便之罪。未遂彼之殺心。故戒體尚未曾失。猶可殷勤悔除。名為中可悔罪也。

△

若為畜生作坑。畜生死者。是下罪可悔。（畜生。較諸天鬼神更劣。故殺之者。罪又稍輕。）若人墮死。若非人墮死。皆犯下罪可悔。（還從畜生邊得方便罪也）。

若優婆塞不定為一事作坑。諸有來者皆令墮死。人死者。犯不可悔。非人死者。中罪可悔。畜生死者。下罪可悔。都無死者。犯三方便可悔罪。是名無煙火坑殺也。

此廣釋無煙火坑殺他。以例核殺。弶殺。作穽殺。撥殺無不爾也。

問。一切有命。不得故殺。殺者非佛弟子。何故今殺天龍鬼神僅結中罪。殺畜生僅結下罪。猶不失戒。不至墮落耶。答。凡論失戒。須破根本四重。所謂殺人。盜五錢。邪婬。大妄語。此四重中。隨犯一種。決非作法之所能懺。至如殺非人畜生等。性罪雖重。而於違無作罪猶為稍輕。今云中罪可悔。下罪可悔。乃是悔除違無作罪。免墮三塗。非謂并除性罪也。殺一命者。必償一命。故殺者固當故償。誤殺者亦須誤償。縱令不受戒者。亦必有罪。故大佛頂經云。如於中間殺彼身命。或食其肉。如是乃至經微塵劫相食相誅。猶如轉輪。互為高下。無有休息。佛制。殺戒良由於此。受持不犯。便可永斷輪迴。設復偶犯至心懺悔。永不復造。亦可免墮三塗。故名中可悔。下可悔耳。設不念佛。求生淨土。何由永脫酬償之苦哉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Precept One: Killing

The Buddha told the monks: "In committing the offense of killing, there are three ways of taking a person's life: first, doing it oneself; second, instructing another; third, dispatching an agent." △ "Doing it oneself" means: one acts with one's own body, taking another's life.

△

"Instructing another" means: one instructs and tells another person, saying, "Seize this person, bind him, and take his life."

△

"Dispatching an agent" means: one says to another person, "Do you know so-and-so? Seize this person, bind him, and take his life." When this agent, following those words, takes that person's life, the upāsaka commits an unrepentable offense.

The precept against killing is constituted as an unrepentable offense by five conditions: first, that it is a human being (meaning that the one killed is a human being, not an animal or the like); second, the perception of it as a human being (meaning that one's intention is set on killing a human being); third, the intent to kill; fourth, the setting in motion of the means; fifth, that the other person's life is actually cut off. In the present cases of doing it oneself, instructing another, and dispatching an agent, in every case it is by means of the intent to kill that the means is set in motion — hence, at the moment that person's life is taken, one commits an unrepentable offense. As for "unrepentable": at the moment one first receives the upāsaka precepts, once the Threefold Refuge has been spoken, one obtains the unconditioned precept-essence; now, in committing the offense of killing a human being, one loses that unconditioned precept-essence and is no longer constituted an upāsaka — hence this cannot be repented through a formal act of confession. Since it cannot be repented, one is cast forever to the margins outside the ocean of the Buddha-dharma; this is called a border offense. One may not again receive the five precepts, nor may one receive the one-day-one-night eight-precept fast, nor may one receive the novice precepts or the monastic precepts, nor may one receive the great precepts of the bodhisattva. One may only, in dependence on the Mahāyāna Dharma, cultivate confession through a sign; only after beholding an auspicious sign is one permitted to receive the bodhisattva precepts, and also permitted to receive again the full precepts, the ten precepts, the eight precepts, the five precepts, and so on. At that point, although the offense of breaking the precepts is extinguished through confession by sign, so that one does not fall into the three evil destinies, the worldly offense of its own [karmic] nature still remains — hence, when the causes and conditions come together, one must still repay this old debt; only by entering nirvāṇa, or being born in the Western [Pure Land], can one be freed of it and escape its retribution. How, then, could one fail to guard this precept?

There are, again, three [further] ways of taking a person's life: first, using an internal form; second, using a non-internal form; third, using both an internal and a non-internal form.

△

"Internal form" means: the upāsaka strikes another with his hand, or uses his foot or another part of his body, thinking, "Let this cause him to die." If that person dies as a result, this is an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once but later dies as a result of it, this is likewise an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once and does not later die as a result of it, this is a middle-grade repentable offense.

△

"Using a non-internal form" means: one throws wood, tile, stone, a knife, a spear, a bow and arrow, a lump of pewter, or a lump of lead or tin at another person from a distance, thinking, "Let this cause him to die." If that person dies as a result, this is an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once but later dies as a result, this is likewise an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once and does not later die as a result, this is a middle-grade repentable offense.

△

"Using both an internal and a non-internal form" means: one takes hold of wood, tile, stone, a knife, a spear, a bow and arrow, a lump of pewter, or a lump of lead, tin, or wood with one's hand, and strikes another with it, thinking, "Let this cause him to die." If that person dies as a result, this offense is unrepentable. If he does not die at once but later dies as a result, this is likewise an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once and does not later die as a result, this is a middle-grade repentable offense.

These three kinds are likewise all methods of killing, what is called "setting the means in motion." The hand, foot, and other bodily parts are what the ordinary mind grasps and appropriates as its own — hence they are called an internal form. Wood, tile, stone, and the like are what the ordinary mind does not grasp and appropriate — hence they are called a non-internal form; in some places this is also called an external form. Using that internal form to take hold of that external form is thus called using both internal and non-internal form together. Whether death results from this means at once or later, in either case it fulfills the intent to kill — hence, from the moment the other person's life is cut off, this is settled as an unrepentable offense. If death does not occur as a result, then there is only the offense of having set the killing-means in motion, without having fulfilled the intent to kill; hence the precept-essence has not yet been lost, and one may still earnestly repent and remove it — this is called a middle-grade repentable offense.

There is, again, a way that uses neither an internal form, nor a non-internal form, nor both internal and non-internal form together: for the sake of killing a person, one compounds various poisons and places them in the eyes, ears, nose, or a wound on the body, or places them in food or drink, or in bedding, or in a cart or carriage, thinking, "Let this cause him to die." If that person dies as a result, this is an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once but later dies as a result, this is likewise an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once and does not later die as a result, this is a middle-grade repentable offense.

This uses poison as the means of killing. Since it uses neither hand, foot, and the like, nor wood, stone, knife, staff, and the like, it is said to use neither internal nor non-internal form. Yet since the other person's life is cut off just the same, the unrepentable offense is likewise the same.

There are, further, these methods: killing by means of a smokeless fire-pit; killing by a pit-trap; killing by a snare; killing by digging a pitfall; killing by a spring-trap; killing by vetāla-sorcery; killing by abortion; killing by pressing the belly; pushing someone into water or into fire; pushing someone into a pit to kill him; sending someone off so that he dies on the road; and even, within the womb, at the moment the two faculties of body and life are first received, setting a means of killing in motion there. (A "snare" is a wooden trap that seizes by deception; a "spring-trap" is a crossbow-and-stone device.)

This further sets out, at large, the various means of killing. The pit-trap, the snare, and the spring-trap are all killing-implements. Vetāla is precisely the sorcery of raising a corpse, explained in what follows below; the rest may be understood accordingly.

"Killing by a smokeless fire-pit" means: knowing that a certain person will come by this road, the upāsaka first digs a smokeless fire-pit along it and covers it over with sand and earth, or says aloud, "Because this person will come by this road, I am making this pit." If that person dies as a result, this is an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once but later dies as a result, it is an unrepentable offense. If he does not die at once and does not later die as a result, this is a middle-grade repentable offense.

△

If one digs a smokeless fire-pit for a human being, and a human being dies, this is unrepentable. If a non-human dies, this is a middle-grade repentable offense (the offense is obtained from the human side, as the means-offense; it is not obtained as a killing-offense from the non-human side, since there was no intent to kill the non-human). If an animal dies, this is a lower-grade repentable offense (the word "lower" is likely an error — measured against the Vinaya texts generally, this too should be a middle-grade offense, again obtained from the human side as the means-offense, not obtained as a killing-offense from the animal's side, since there was no intent to kill the animal).

△

If a human dies, this is a lower-grade repentable offense; if an animal dies, this is a lower-grade repentable offense (again, in both cases the offense is obtained from the non-human side as the means-offense; it is not obtained as a killing-offense from the human or the animal side, since there was originally no intent to kill either the human or the animal).

△

△

If the upāsaka does not dig the pit with any one particular target in mind, so that whoever comes is made to fall in and die — if a human dies, this is unrepentable; if a non-human dies, this is a middle-grade repentable offense; if an animal dies, this is a lower-grade repentable offense; if no one at all dies, this is a repentable offense at all three levels of means. This is called killing by a smokeless fire-pit.

This explains at large the killing of another by a smokeless fire-pit; by this example, killing by pit-trap, by snare, by digging a pitfall, and by spring-trap are all to be understood in exactly the same way.

Question: no living thing, of whatever kind, should be intentionally killed — one who kills is not a disciple of the Buddha. Why is it, then, that killing gods, dragons, ghosts, or spirits is here settled only as a middle-grade offense, and killing an animal only as a lower-grade offense, such that the precept is still not lost and one does not go so far as to fall? Answer: whenever the loss of the precept is under discussion, it must involve breaking one of the four root, grave offenses — namely, killing a human being, stealing to the value of five coins, sexual misconduct, and the great false speech [of falsely claiming spiritual attainment]. Committing any one of these four grave offenses can absolutely never be repented through a formal act of confession. As for killing a non-human, an animal, and the like, though the offense of its own [karmic] nature is grave, it is still somewhat lighter with respect to the offense of violating the unconditioned [precept-essence]. What is here called a middle-grade repentable offense or a lower-grade repentable offense is precisely the repenting and removal of the offense of violating the unconditioned [precept-essence], so as to avoid falling into the three evil destinies — it does not mean that the offense of [karmic] nature is thereby removed as well. One who kills a single life must inevitably repay a life in return; hence one who kills deliberately must certainly repay deliberately, and even one who kills by mistake must repay by a corresponding mistake. Even someone who has never received the precepts at all still inevitably incurs this offense. Hence the Great Śūraṅgama Sūtra says: "As, in the interval, one kills that being's body and life, or eats its flesh — thus, even through kalpas as numerous as fine dust, they devour and slaughter one another, turning like a wheel, alternately high and low, without ever coming to rest." It is precisely because of this that the Buddha established the precept against killing. Receiving and upholding it without transgression allows one to cut off the cycle of rebirth forever; and even should one happen to transgress, if one confesses with utmost sincerity and never commits the offense again, one may still avoid falling into the three evil destinies — hence it is called a middle-grade repentable or lower-grade repentable offense. But if one does not, in addition, recollect the Buddha and seek rebirth in the Pure Land, by what means could one ever be permanently freed from the suffering of repayment and retribution?

Reading note

The five-condition test Ouyi states up front — human victim, perceived as human, intent to kill, a means actually set in motion, and death actually resulting — is the engine behind every ruling that follows, in this precept and in the others. Once it is in hand, the sūtra's long enumerations of methods (bare hands, thrown weapons, poison, fire-pits, traps) stop looking like a grim inventory for its own sake and start looking like stress-tests of the same five-part formula: change one condition and watch the grade of the offense move. That is also why the fire-pit case is worked in such obsessive detail — dig it for a person and a non-human falls in, dig it with no target in mind at all, dig it and no one falls in — because the formula's behavior at the edges is exactly what a real casebook needs to establish, once and for all, so that every later case can simply be measured against it instead of restating it.

The closing question-and-answer is worth sitting with on its own terms. It does not soften the precept — killing a non-human or an animal is still called an offense "grave" in its own karmic nature — it only clarifies that losing the unconditioned precept-essence and incurring karmic retribution are two different kinds of consequence, tracked separately. A lower-grade repentable offense can still be fully repented as a violation of the precept, while the debt of having taken a life remains outstanding until it is actually repaid or until one is permanently freed of it through liberation or rebirth in the Pure Land. Ouyi's closing appeal to Amitābha practice is not a stray devotional flourish tacked onto a legal text — it is the only exit this passage actually offers from what it calls a wheel of mutual devouring "without ever coming to rest."

Reading note

The abortion cases repeat a pattern already established by the fire-pit in the previous section: the offense is always settled on the side of the person actually targeted by intent, never on the side of whoever happens to die instead. Kill the mother while aiming at the fetus, or the fetus while aiming at the mother, and the grave offense still attaches only to the intended target; the other death is absorbed as a mere means-offense. This is the same five-condition logic doing the same work in a much more emotionally difficult case, and Ouyi does not flinch from spelling it out twice, once for each direction of mistake.

Praise-inducing killing is the strangest entry in the whole precept, and worth pausing on for what it reveals about how deliberately this text tracks intent through a chain of events rather than judging the final outcome alone. The offense is not fixed the moment the words are spoken — it tracks forward through the listener's own response (does the evil person defiantly refuse, does the good person independently resist the suggestion) and then tracks the speaker's own change of heart, so that sincere regret and retraction can downgrade what would otherwise have been a completed, unrepentable act of incitement into something considerably lighter. The text is, in effect, building a moral ledger that stays open and revisable for as long as the chain of causation has not yet closed with an actual death.

Reading note

Ouyi's correction of the sūtra's own stated rule on object-perception is one of the more striking moments in the whole text — a Ming-dynasty annotator quietly overruling the letter of the sūtra by appeal to the wider logic of the Vinaya. The sūtra as transmitted settles the grave offense in three of six possible cases; Ouyi argues, by consistent application of the same intent-based reasoning used everywhere else in this precept, that only two should count. This is not treated as a crisis of authority — it is simply what "measuring by principle" (以理酌之) looks like when a careful reader takes a text's own internal logic more seriously than a possibly corrupted transmission of its surface wording.

The run of accident cases at the end draws a genuinely fine, and genuinely useful, line: falling off a roof and killing someone is no offense at all, because there was no way to foresee or intend it, but rolling a rock down a slope without shouting a warning first crosses into something that at least calls for the reminder "call out first, so people may know." The precept is not asking the reader to achieve omniscience about consequences; it is asking for exactly the amount of foresight that an ordinarily careful person could have exercised — no less, and, notably, no more. The tickled child and the twice-called sleeper make the same point from the opposite direction: a first call that could not have been foreseen to kill is blameless, but a second call, made with the knowledge of what happened the first time, is not.

5. Precept Two, Stealing (I): The Six Conditions and What Counts as "Leaving Its Place"

The stealing precept opens with its own version of the five-condition test — now six conditions, since stealing additionally requires a minimum value and the physical removal of the object from where it was. Much of what follows is a patient survey of what "removal from its original place" actually means for every kind of property imaginable: cloth, bedding, a cart, a boat, a house, water, birds carrying jewels in flight, even gambling pieces switched mid-game.

The Buddha told the monks: "The upāsaka who takes another's valuable property by three means commits an unrepentable offense: first, by means of the mind; second, by means of the body; third, by removing it from its original place."

△

"By means of the mind" means: giving rise to the thought, reflecting, and intending to steal.

△

"By means of the body" means: using a part of the body, or the like, to take another's property.

△

The precept against stealing is constituted as an unrepentable offense by six conditions: first, that it is another's property; second, the perception of it as another's property; third, the intent to steal; fourth, the setting in motion of the means of taking; fifth, that its value reaches five coins (in the Western Region, one large coin was worth sixteen small coins of this land, so that five coins amounts to eighty small coins. Wagering money as a game is called chupu; the game of backgammon is called liubo. In gambling houses, the pieces used — game-tokens, go-stones, chess pieces, dice, and the like — are all called "teeth." "Switching the teeth" means secretly exchanging pieces or moving them, or even using loaded dice. According to the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts and the Brahma Net Sutra, gambling and the like are also offenses of light defilement; but one who has received only the five precepts may be permitted not to count it an offense. Switching the pieces to beat an opponent, however, is entirely a thieving mind, and so constitutes the grave offense.

Reading note

What is genuinely useful about the long survey of "places" here — cloth, hides, wool mats, shoulders, carts, boats, houses, water — is that it is not really about property law for its own sake. It is a repeated demonstration of a single idea: an offense is only complete once the owner would, if they looked, actually perceive the object as missing from where they left it. Moving a bird from one shoulder to the other while a person is watching is nothing; moving stolen goods from the second layer of a mat to the first, so that a glance no longer finds them where they should be, completes the crime. The variety of examples is the text training the reader's intuition on a single abstract criterion by throwing case after case at it until the pattern becomes automatic.

The bird-and-jewel sequence is a small masterpiece of exhaustive casuistry — owned bird takes it, wild bird takes it, owned bird steals it back from wild bird, wild bird steals it back from owned bird — precisely because ownership of the object at the moment of the layman's own theft changes the grade of what he commits, even though his own thieving mind and his own action are identical in every version. It is a pointed illustration that, for this text, the offense is not simply a function of the thief's intention alone; it is a relation between that intention and the object's actual status in the world at the exact moment it is seized.

If one gives rise to the intent to steal but has not yet taken the object, this is a lower-grade repentable offense. If one takes it, and it is worth less than five coins, this is a middle-grade repentable offense. If one takes it, and it is worth a full five coins, this is unrepentable.

Intending to steal but not yet taking the object is a lower-grade repentable offense — this is the distant preparatory-stage offense. Taking it but not yet removing it from its place is a middle-grade repentable offense — this is the proximate preparatory-stage offense, though the text abbreviates this point. Taking something worth less than five coins is a middle-grade repentable offense — the precept is not yet lost. Taking something worth a full five coins is unrepentable — the precept is already lost. Once the precept is lost, one must undertake confession through a sign, following the same pattern as explained under the precept against killing. As for the worldly offense of its own karmic nature, it comes to rest of itself once repayment is complete — this is somewhat lighter than the karma of killing.

Reading note

The radish story and the field-robe story are placed back to back for a reason: they are mirror images. The radish-grower is entirely within his rights to refuse an unpaid request, and the visitor's magical retaliation is simply judged by ordinary property valuation, root to leaf. The robe-taker, by contrast, genuinely believed the object was unowned, looked carefully before acting, and is cleared — but the Buddha's closing instruction, "weigh the matter carefully, for an object may have an owner even with no one guarding it," refuses to let the ruling become a loophole. Innocent belief clears the offense this time; it is not offered as a permanently reliable method for the future.

The animal-theft ladder — legless, two- and three-legged, four-legged, many-legged, each with its own precise trigger for "removal from place" (a container, a cage, a rope's length, a specific number of double-paces past the herd) — reads at first like taxonomic overkill, but it is solving a real problem: without a physical object's edges to mark "its place," a living creature that can walk away on its own needs some other objective threshold, or the whole precept collapses into unanswerable disputes about exactly when an animal became "taken." Fixing the threshold at a specific distance is not arbitrary legalism; it is the only way to make the same six-condition test administrable once the stolen property can move by itself.

Precept Three: Sexual Misconduct

The Buddha told the monks: "The upāsaka should not give rise to lustful thoughts or lustful notions — he should not even let such a thought arise in the mind, how much less give rise to the impure and evil karma that is the root of the fetters of desire, hatred, and delusion."

Fixing a name or concept upon an object of desire is called a "lustful thought." Suddenly giving rise to a seeking after an object of desire is called a "lustful notion." When desire is not fulfilled, hatred arises from it; and both desire and hatred equally rest upon delusion. Once the three poisons are all present, this becomes the root of every fetter, violating pure conduct and bringing about suffering and retribution both in this life and in lives to come — hence it is called impure and evil karma.

Within this precept, the offense of sexual misconduct concerns four kinds of persons: males, females, paṇḍakas, and hermaphrodites. "Females" means human females, non-human females, and animal females. "Males" means human males, non-human males, and animal males. Paṇḍakas and hermaphrodites are likewise classified along the same lines as above.

If the upāsaka commits sexual misconduct with a human female, a non-human female, or an animal female, in any of the three proper orifices, this is unrepentable.

△

If he commits sexual misconduct with a human male, non-human male, animal male, paṇḍaka, or hermaphrodite, in either of the two available orifices, this is unrepentable.

If one gives rise to the intent to commit sexual misconduct but the two bodies have not yet joined, this is a lower-grade repentable offense (the distant preparatory-stage offense). If the two bodies have joined but one stops without completing the act, this is a middle-grade repentable offense (the proximate preparatory-stage offense).

The precept against sexual misconduct is constituted as an unrepentable offense by three conditions: first, a mind of lust — meaning that one experiences it as one who is hungry experiences food, or as one who is thirsty experiences drink, unlike an involuntary contact experienced as a burning iron entering the body or a stinking corpse tied about the neck; second, that it is one of the proper bodily orifices — meaning the three sites explained in the text below; third, that the act is completed — meaning that entry occurs even to the depth of a sesame seed; at that point the precept is lost.

If the upāsaka commits sexual misconduct with a female servant who has already been given in marriage and has an owner, this is unrepentable; the lesser offenses are as explained above. The three proper orifices are the mouth, the anus, and the urethral opening; apart from these three sites, engaging in the sexual act elsewhere is in every case merely repentable.

△

If the upāsaka, with a female servant not yet given in marriage, engages in sexual misconduct at a site other than the proper orifice, this is a repentable offense, though the retribution to be received in a future life is grave.

A female servant not yet given in marriage has as yet no other owner; if one wishes to take her in, one should, in accordance with proper ritual, formally establish her status — whether as concubine or as wife, either is permissible. But engaging in sexual misconduct at an improper site destroys her chastity, causing this woman to lose her virtue and her purity. Hence, although the precept-essence is not thereby lost, the retribution to be received afterward is grave — this is what is meant by saying that one who diminishes hidden virtue is deeply abhorred by the unseen powers.

If the upāsaka has a male servant or bondsman and engages in sexual misconduct with him in either of the two available orifices, this is unrepentable; the lesser offenses are as explained above.

If the upāsaka has intercourse with a prostitute but does not pay her fee, this is unrepentable sexual misconduct; if he pays the fee, there is no offense.

If a human being, or even an animal, has died, but the body has not yet decomposed, and one engages in sexual misconduct with it — in the case of a female, in any of the three proper orifices — this is unrepentable; the lesser offense is as explained above.

If the upāsaka, having himself received the eight branches (that is, the one-day-one-night eight-precept fast), engages in the sexual act, this is unrepentable. Under the eight branches there is no longer any distinction of proper or improper — every instance is an offense.

If an upāsaka, though he has not received any precepts at all, violates the chastity of one who is a pure-precept disciple of the Buddha, then although he does not commit the offense of breaking a precept he himself does not hold, he shall thereafter forever be barred from receiving the five precepts, or even from leaving the household life to receive the full precepts.

A "pure-precept disciple of the Buddha" means a monk, a nun, a probationary nun, a novice monk, a novice nun, an upāsaka, or an upāsikā. Even one's own wife, on a day when she has received the eight-precept fast, may not be violated; to do so is likewise called violating another's pure conduct.

Question: violating another's pure conduct is indeed called a grave impediment. But suppose one is instead seduced by the very person who holds the precepts — is this also a barring impediment? Or suppose one commits the offense unknowingly, by mistake, and afterward feels regret and remorse, confessing it with a sincere mind — is one then permitted to receive the five precepts, or to leave the household life? Answer: if one knew that the other person had already received the precepts, one ought not to have recklessly followed their seduction. But since one was in fact seduced, the offense should certainly be somewhat lessened; the case of an unknowing, mistaken offense should reasonably be treated the same way. But the method of confession in such a case is by no means a simple or trivial matter — one must seek out someone of weighty virtue and authority, deeply versed in the study of the Vinaya, who alone will be able to extinguish this offense.

The Buddha told the monks: "I have two bodies: the birth-body, and the precept-body. If a good son were to erect, for my birth-body, a stūpa of the seven treasures reaching up to the Brahmā heaven, and someone were to damage it, that offense would still be repentable. But to damage my precept-body is an offense beyond measure — one would receive retribution like that of the dragon-king Erāvaṇa."

This passage sums up and shows that the pure precepts must not be damaged or transgressed. The "precept-body" is precisely the Dharma-body, since the Buddha takes precepts, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation as his Dharma-body. It is by means of this precept-Dharma, transmitted master to master, that the Tathāgata's Dharma-body abides forever and is never extinguished. Whether one breaks one's own pure conduct, or breaks another's pure conduct, in either case one thereby destroys the Tathāgata's Dharma-body — hence this offense is graver still than the offense of destroying a relic-stūpa built for his birth-body. As for the dragon-king Erāvaṇa: in full, Airāvaṇa, also called Airāvata. Aira is the name of a tree, translated "foul odor"; vaṇa is translated "extreme." This dragon-king had, in a past life, been a monk in the time of Kāśyapa Buddha, and, through nothing more than a mind of anger, had transgressed the precept against breaking grass and trees, and, not knowing how to repent it, came to have this foul-smelling tree growing on his head, suffering immeasurable torment. How much more, then, should the root grave precepts against killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech never be transgressed? Yet, of the precepts against killing and stealing, one who has even a little compassion left, though his practice may have declined, is still generally unwilling to transgress; it is this precept against sexual misconduct alone that people find easiest of all to transgress — hence the passage singles this one out to sum up and show its gravity. But if the offense of breaking this precept is graver than destroying a stūpa, then surely the merit of upholding it must be graver still than erecting one. May the Buddha's disciples take this to heart.

Reading note

The two-bodies passage that closes this precept is doing something the killing and stealing precepts never quite do: it explains, in a single image, why the four root precepts are called root precepts at all. The Buddha's "precept-body" is identified outright with the Dharma-body itself — the same body constituted by precepts, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation — transmitted, Ouyi notes, "master to master," so that an individual's kept or broken precept is never merely a private matter. Breaking pure conduct, one's own or another's, is described as damaging something considerably more serious than a relic-stūpa built to the Buddha's physical remains, because it damages the living transmission of the thing relics can only commemorate.

Ouyi's closing observation — that people who would still hesitate over killing or stealing, out of ordinary residual compassion, find this precept the easiest of all four to break — reads as a piece of blunt pastoral honesty rather than moralizing. It also explains the precept's own internal architecture: notice how much of this section is given over not to the paradigm case but to edge cases involving consent, status, and retribution that outlasts the act itself (the unmarried servant, the wife on a fast day, the corpse) — precisely because, as Ouyi implies, this is the precept where people most need the edges spelled out, not the center.

Precept Four: False Speech

The Buddha told the monks: "I have, in many ways, censured false speech, and praised those who do not speak falsely. Even in jest or play one should not speak falsely — how much less speak falsely with deliberate intent."

Within this precept, the offense is this: if the upāsaka, without truly knowing or seeing the supra-human, noble Dharma, says of himself, "I am an arhat" (one who has entirely cut off the afflictions of the three realms), or claims to be nearing arhatship (one who has nearly cut off the deluded views of the formless realm), this is unrepentable. If he says, "I am an anāgāmin" (one who has entirely cut off the afflictions of the desire realm), or a sakṛdāgāmin (one who has cut off the six grades of deluded views of the desire realm), or a srotāpanna (one who has entirely cut off the deluded views), or even that he is nearing stream-entry (at the final mind-moment of the "highest worldly dharma," possessing all eight forms of forbearance, lacking only a small portion of full understanding); or that he has attained the first dhyāna (accompanied by the five factors of merit: detachment, joy, and bliss born of seclusion), the second dhyāna (accompanied by the four factors of merit: joy and bliss born of concentration), the third dhyāna (accompanied by the five factors of merit: detachment from joy and subtle bliss), the fourth dhyāna (accompanied by the four factors of merit: equanimity, mindfulness, and purity); or that he has attained the immeasurable minds of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity; or that he has attained the four formless absorptions — namely, the absorption of infinite space, the absorption of infinite consciousness, the absorption of nothingness, and the absorption of neither-perception-nor-non-perception; or that he has attained the contemplation of impurity, or mindfulness of ānāpāna (translated "sending out and bringing in," that is, the out-breath and in-breath — these two contemplations are the two ambrosia-gates of the Buddha-dharma, which one should only ever say one is cultivating, never say one has "attained"); or that gods come to where he is, or dragons, yakṣas (swift demons), pretas (also called "pi-li-duo," translated "ancestor-demons"), piśācas (spirit-devourers), kumbhāṇḍas (jar-shaped incubus-demons), or rākṣasas (fearsome demons) come to where he is, and that they question him and he answers them, or he questions them and they answer him — in every such case, this is unrepentable.

This great false speech is constituted as an unrepentable offense by five conditions: first, that there is a person one addresses; second, the perception of that person as such; third, a mind of deceit; fourth, that one speaks of a grave matter — that is, claiming to be an arhat, or that a rākṣasa has come to where one is, and so on; fifth, that the other person comprehends what is said. If one speaks to a deaf person, a simple-minded person, or someone who does not understand language, or speaks to a non-human, an animal, and so on, this belongs instead to the category of a middle-grade repentable offense.

If one originally intended to say "arhat" but mistakenly says "anāgāmin," this is a middle-grade repentable offense; the remaining cases are offenses in the same way (since the original intention was not fulfilled).

△

If someone asks the upāsaka, "Have you attained the path?" and he remains silent, or indicates it by a gesture, this is in every case a middle-grade repentable offense (since it is not yet fully articulated).

Even saying that a whirlwind earth-spirit has come to where he is is a middle-grade repentable offense (according to the Sarvāstivāda Vinaya, falsely claiming to have attained the specific or general contemplations of an ordinary practitioner not yet attained, falsely claiming that one's precepts, not yet pure, are pure, or falsely claiming to have read and recited sutras one has never read or recited, and the like, are all middle-grade repentable offenses).

If the upāsaka, having actually heard something, says he did not hear it, or having actually seen something, says he did not see it, or, being uncertain whether something exists, says it does not, or says it does when it does not — false statements of this kind are all repentable offenses (there are, further, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle chatter, which are likewise all offenses; but since the precept is not thereby lost, they are called repentable — this does not mean there is no offense of karmic nature involved).

If one gives rise to the intent to speak falsely but has not yet spoken, this is a lower-grade repentable offense (the distant preparatory-stage offense). If one speaks but does not fully convey the intended meaning, this is a middle-grade repentable offense (whether through a mistaken statement or an unclear one — this is only called the proximate preparatory-stage offense). If one declares to another that one has attained the path, this is at once unrepentable.

If one is insane, or of a deranged mind, and speaks without awareness, there is no offense.

Reading note

It is easy to misread this precept as narrowly about spiritual fraud, but the fifth condition — that the listener actually comprehends what is said — quietly generalizes it into something closer to a theory of what a lie even is. A false claim spoken to someone who cannot understand it (a young child, someone who does not share the language, an animal) never becomes the completed grave offense, because nothing has actually been communicated; the deceit has misfired rather than landed. This is the same intent-plus-completion architecture used throughout the whole sūtra, applied here to speech instead of to a physical act.

The parenthetical aside on the two breathing contemplations is a small but telling piece of Ouyi's own caution: one may say "I am cultivating mindfulness of the breath," but should never say "I have attained it," precisely because these two practices are singled out as the Buddha-dharma's own "ambrosia gates." Even a technically true claim of a genuinely advanced attainment would, if spoken to impress someone, risk sliding into exactly the pattern this precept exists to prevent — which is presumably why the note is there at all, attached to the two contemplations most likely to tempt a sincere practitioner into boasting.

9. Precept Five, Alcohol: The Elder Sāgata and the Dragon

Unlike the first four precepts, the alcohol precept opens with a full narrative rather than a legal enumeration: the elder Sāgata subdues a violent dragon through spiritual power, is invited to a meal out of gratitude, is unknowingly given wine, and collapses in the road — unable, once drunk, to subdue so much as a toad. Only after the story does the sūtra turn to defining exactly what counts as wine.

Ru-Yi Translation

Precept Five: Alcohol

The Buddha was in the country of Ceti, in the town of Bhadravatī (untranslated). In that place there was a fierce dragon named Ambara-tittha (untranslated), savage and violent, so that no one could go near its dwelling; elephants, horses, cattle, sheep, donkeys, mules, and camels — none could approach it, and not even birds could fly over it. When the autumn grain ripened, it would destroy the crops. The elder Sāgata (also rendered Panthaka, or Pantha, translated "born by the side of the small path," or also "continuing the way" — in a past life he had been stingy with the Dharma, and also fond of drinking wine; in this life he was born dull-witted, unable to recite a single verse in a hundred days, until the Buddha taught him to regulate his breath, whereby he attained arhatship) was traveling through the country of Ceti and gradually made his way to Bhadravatī. When that night had passed, in the early morning he put on his robe, took his bowl, and entered the village to beg for food. While begging, he heard that this town had a fierce dragon named Ambara-tittha, savage and violent, so that the people, birds, and beasts could not reach its dwelling-place, and that it destroyed the crops when the autumn grain ripened. Having heard this, and having finished his alms-round, he went to the dragon Ambara-tittha's dwelling-place, by a spring beneath a tree, spread out his sitting-mat, and sat down in full posture. The dragon, sensing the smell of his robe, at once grew enraged, and smoke poured from its body. The elder Sāgata then entered samādhi, and by his spiritual power smoke poured from his own body as well. The dragon grew still more enraged, and fire blazed from its body; Sāgata then entered the fire-radiance samādhi, and fire blazed from his own body as well. The dragon then rained down hail; Sāgata transformed the hail into various kinds of sweet cakes and confections. The dragon then hurled thunderbolts; Sāgata transformed them into various delightful sweet-rice balls. The dragon then rained down bows, arrows, knives, and spears; Sāgata transformed them into utpala flowers (translated "blue lotus"), padma flowers (translated "red lotus"), and kumuda flowers (translated "yellow lotus"). The dragon then rained down poisonous snakes, centipedes, earth-vipers, and millipedes; Sāgata transformed them into garlands of utpala flowers, campaka flowers, vārṣika flowers, and atimuktaka flowers (campaka is translated "yellow flower"; vārṣika is translated "summer-born flower," also translated "rain-flower," since it blooms just as the rains come; atimuktaka was formerly translated "flower of good intent," or also translated "sweet dragon-flower"). All the power that the dragon possessed was thus displayed against Sāgata, and having displayed its full power in this way and still failed to overcome him, it lost its might and its radiance. The elder Sāgata, knowing that the dragon's power was now exhausted and it could act no further, transformed himself into a tiny body and entered through the dragon's two ears, emerging from its two eyes; having emerged from its eyes, he entered through its nose and emerged from its mouth, walking back and forth upon the dragon's head without injuring the dragon's body at all. The dragon, seeing this, was struck with great astonishment, its hair standing on end with fear, and, joining its palms, said to the elder Sāgata, "I take refuge in you." Sāgata answered, "Do not take refuge in me — you should take refuge in my teacher, take refuge in the Buddha." The dragon said, "From now on I take refuge in the Three Jewels," and vowed that for the rest of its life it would be a Buddhist upāsaka. Having received the threefold refuge and become a disciple of the Buddha, this dragon never again did such fierce and violent deeds as before, and all the people, birds, and beasts were able to reach its dwelling-place; when the autumn grain ripened, it no longer destroyed the crops. This report spread throughout the various lands: the elder Sāgata had been able to subdue the fierce dragon and bring it to goodness, so that people, birds, and beasts could now reach the dragon's palace, and the crops, when they ripened in autumn, were no longer destroyed. Because of the elder Sāgata's spreading renown, people everywhere prepared food and invited him in turn. Among them was one poor woman who, with faith and reverence, invited the elder Sāgata, and Sāgata silently accepted her invitation.

The woman prepared a fine porridge of milk and cream for him; he accepted it and ate. The woman then thought to herself, "This monk, having eaten this fine milk-and-cream porridge, might well catch a chill." So she took what looked like a clear, water-colored wine and offered it to him. Sāgata, without looking closely, drank it; having drunk it, he expounded the Dharma for her and then departed, making his way back toward the monastery. But by then the strength of the wine had taken effect, and near the monastery gate he collapsed to the ground. His outer robe and other garments, his water-strainer, his bowl, his staff, his oil-pouch, his leather sandals, and his needle-case all lay scattered in different places, while his own body lay in yet another place, so drunk that he was aware of nothing at all. At that time the Buddha, together with Ānanda (translated "joy," the Buddha's cousin, born on the day the Buddha attained enlightenment, who served as the Buddha's attendant — also translated "rejoicing-in-celebration," or "undefiled"), was traveling through and came to that place. The Buddha saw this monk, and, though he already knew, asked all the same: "Ānanda, who is this?" Ānanda answered, "World-Honored One, this is the elder Sāgata." The Buddha then said to Ānanda, "Spread out a seat for me here, prepare water, and gather the sangha." Ānanda, receiving this instruction, spread out the seat, prepared the water, and gathered the sangha; he then went and reported to the Buddha, "World-Honored One, I have spread out the seat, prepared the water, and gathered the sangha." The Buddha, knowing the time was right, washed his feet and sat down, and asked the monks: "Have you ever heard or seen a dragon named Ambara-tittha, savage and violent, whose dwelling-place no one could formerly reach — elephants, horses, cattle, sheep, donkeys, mules, and camels, none able to reach it, and not even birds daring to fly over it — which, when the autumn grain ripened, would destroy the crops? This good son Sāgata was able to subdue it and bring it to goodness, so that now people, birds, and beasts can reach the spring there." At that time, those in the assembly who had seen this said, "We have seen it, World-Honored One," and those who had heard of it said, "We have heard of it, World-Honored One." The Buddha said to the monks: "What do you think — can this good son Sāgata now subdue even a toad?" They answered, "He cannot, World-Honored One." The Buddha said: "Even a noble sage, through drinking wine, suffers such a loss as this — how much more an ordinary worldling! Faults and offenses such as this — indeed, faults even beyond this — all come about because of drinking wine. From this day forward, whoever says, 'I am a disciple of the Buddha,' must not drink wine — not even a single drop no larger than the tip of a blade of grass may be drunk."

When the Buddha had, in various ways, censured the fault of drinking wine, he told the monks: "That the upāsaka must not drink wine applies to two kinds: grain-wine and wood-wine (grain-wine needs no explanation). Wood-wine means wine made using roots, stems, leaves, flowers, or fruit, or using various kinds of seeds, or various medicinal herbs mixed together — having the color of wine, the fragrance of wine, and the taste of wine, and able, when drunk, to intoxicate a person: this is called wine. If the upāsaka so much as tastes and swallows it, this is likewise called drinking, and is an offense. If he drinks grain-wine, each swallow is an offense; if he drinks sour wine, each swallow is an offense; if he drinks sweet wine, each swallow is an offense; if he eats leaven capable of intoxicating, each swallow is an offense; if he eats dregs from distillation, each swallow is an offense; if he drinks the lees settled from wine, each swallow is an offense; if he drinks something resembling wine — having the color, fragrance, and taste of wine — capable of intoxicating a person, each swallow is an offense."

"But if something merely has the color of wine, without the fragrance or taste of wine, and is not capable of intoxicating a person, and the like — drinking it is in every case no offense."

"Sour" means having a sour taste. So long as drinking it can intoxicate, it does not matter whether the taste is sour or sweet — in every case it is an offense. "Leaven" is the ferment used in making wine. "Dregs from distillation" is what is now called burnt liquor. "Lees settled from wine" — "lees" means the sediment and dregs of wine. "Something resembling wine" means that fruit juice and the like, once it has fermented and matured, can likewise intoxicate a person. This precept against alcohol is merely a preventive offense, established to guard against fault, set forth in parallel with the preceding four root precepts. It is constituted as an offense by three conditions: first, that it is wine — meaning that drinking it intoxicates; second, the perception of it as wine — meaning that one knows it to be wine, or mixed with wine; third, that it enters the mouth — each swallow settles a repentable offense. But if one does not know that a food contains wine, or if the wine used in cooking a dish has already lost its intoxicating property and cannot intoxicate, in either case there is no offense at all.

Here ends Ouyi Zhixu's Marks of the Five Precepts for Upāsakas.

Reading note

The alcohol precept is unique among the five for reasoning entirely from a single narrative rather than a taxonomy of cases, and the story is built to make one specific point unmissable: Sāgata's power against the dragon was real and formidable — displayed across seven successive escalations of a battle he clearly wins — and wine takes it away completely, reducing an arhat who had just out-fought a fierce nāga to someone who cannot "subdue even a toad." The joke has teeth. The precept is not arguing that wine is intrinsically unclean the way killing or stealing is; it is arguing, through demonstration, that intoxication dissolves exactly the discernment and self-command that every other precept depends on being intact.

The technical definition that follows the story is worth noticing for how it refuses easy loopholes: color alone is not enough to make something wine (a drink can look like wine and be fine), but the actual capacity to intoxicate is enough regardless of taste, source, or preparation — sour or sweet, grain or fruit, fermented or distilled, even the dregs and lees left over from making it. The rule is functional rather than categorical: whatever actually produces the state the story just illustrated is what the precept reaches, and nothing else.

Ru-Yi Translation

This supplementary explanation of the sūtra's meaning is divided into three chapters: first, citing the fuller Vinaya to clarify the text; second, setting up tables to distinguish the marks of offenses; third, separately recording supplementary points. As to the first, citing the fuller Vinaya: wherever the sūtra's text is abbreviated or its translated wording not fully worked out, I have, in outline, raised the essential points and cited passages from the fuller Vinaya as a supplementary explanation, so as to make things clearly evident and free of confusion for anyone reading through it. As to the second, setting up tables: the marks of offenses, the object-perception formulas, and the exempting circumstances are, in both the sūtra text and Ouyi's annotation, left with gaps and omissions, leaving later students hesitant, with nothing to go by — hence I have gathered and supplemented this material, laying it out as charts and tables, which may perhaps be of some small benefit to beginning students. As to the third, separately recording: I have sometimes cited the views of earlier scholars, and sometimes ventured my own private opinion, briefly supplementing what was left out, so as to make things easier to understand — though I would hardly claim this exhausts the subject; it may perhaps offer a rough outline of what is upheld and what is transgressed.

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, Clarifying the Exempting Circumstances

The Tang-dynasty Nanshan Vinaya Master [Dao Xuan] says: "Speaking in jest and the like, although it does not constitute the grave offense, still constitutes a light offense, since it does not accord with the proper manner of speech."

The Bodhisattva Precept Text says: "Again, for the sake of many sentient beings, to free them from mortal danger, from imprisonment and bondage, from having their hands and feet cut off, their noses severed, their ears cut off, or their eyes gouged out, and the like — even facing danger to their own lives, bodhisattvas do not, without proper mindfulness and clear comprehension, speak falsely; yet, for the sake of rescuing and freeing those sentient beings, knowing what they do, they deliberately choose to speak falsely. In short, bodhisattvas consider only the benefit or harm to sentient beings, having no defiled intent of their own, and, purely for the sake of benefiting sentient beings, they knowingly and with clear comprehension speak otherwise than the truth. In speaking such words, they commit no violation of the bodhisattva precepts, and in fact generate much merit." (End of quotation.) Although the Hīnayāna Vinaya contains no such passage, in cases of rescuing someone from mortal danger and the like, it too should reasonably be permitted the same exemption.

The Song-dynasty Lingzhi Vinaya Master [Yuan Zhao] says: "As for using wine as medicine when no other medicine can treat the illness — this does not mean that as soon as one is ill one may drink it. One must first thoroughly try treating it with every other medicine; only if it fails to cure the illness may one then take wine."

Reading note

Laid out this way, the table makes visible a structural symmetry the sūtra's own case-by-case presentation tends to obscure: all four root precepts run through exactly the same five-step ladder — completed grave act, proximate preparatory stage, distant preparatory stage, and then two "derivative-type" grades for lesser objects or lesser modes of the same offense. Killing a deity and taking under five coins are, in this scheme, structurally the same move: the offense keeps its shape but drops a grade because what it lands on is lesser than the paradigm case (a human life, a full five-coin theft). Once the pattern is visible as a grid, the individual rulings scattered across dozens of case-law paragraphs in the sūtra proper stop looking like a pile of unrelated verdicts and start looking like five parallel applications of one underlying scale.

The honest gap at "next, the object-perception formulas" is worth taking at face value rather than explaining away: Tan Fang's own introduction to this chapter already warned that "the sūtra text and Ouyi's annotation both have gaps and omissions" in exactly this area, and this is presumably one of them — a planned second table (whether the victim was correctly perceived, misperceived, or doubtfully perceived, mapped against the actual facts, the same six-case logic worked out explicitly under the killing precept above) that was never actually filled in before the text moves on. The exempting circumstances that follow, by contrast, are complete: a bodhisattva may knowingly speak falsely to save a life, and wine may be used medicinally only once every other remedy has been exhausted — both framed as narrow, deliberately hard-to-invoke exceptions rather than general permissions.

Chapter Three: Miscellaneous Notes

If one has already received the five precepts and then transgresses them, in every case this settles into a duṣkṛta offense (newly translated as duṣkṛta — "an ill deed"), divided by its weight into the three grades of upper, middle, and lower. The Sūtra on the Light and Grave Offenses of Breaking the Precepts says: "If one is without shame or remorse, and treats the Buddha's word with contempt, then whoever commits this offense falls into the hell realms for a span equal to five hundred years of the Four Heavenly Kings' lifespan, or nine hundred thousand years counted in human time." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "If it is claimed that the Tathāgata taught that a duṣkṛta offense leads to hell for the span of years given above, this is only the Tathāgata's skillful means of instilling fear so as to lead beings in; whoever asserts this literally should be known with certainty to be speaking from a demonic sūtra or Vinaya, not from what the Buddha actually taught."

Folio 3, line 4, explains: "The precept against killing is constituted as an unrepentable offense by five conditions," and so on — this is what is meant by "an offense complete with all its constituent conditions." In settling the offense of any precept, all its constituent conditions must be fully present for the offense to be complete; where every condition is present, this becomes an upper-grade unrepentable offense; where one or two conditions are lacking, this is a middle- or lower-grade repentable offense.

One who feels joy upon seeing another person kill likewise commits a lower-grade repentable offense. If one sees another about to kill, and has the power to intervene, one should rescue the victim; if one's power is insufficient to rescue him, one should give rise to a compassionate mind, recollect the Buddha, and hold a mantra, dedicating the merit to dissolve the enmity and release the entanglement, so as to permanently sever the evil condition between them.

Among the five precepts, the point on which the Hīnayāna and the Mahāyāna differ most is precisely the precept against killing. As for killing an animal under the Mahāyāna: although the Tiantai commentarial tradition allows for the offense to be settled in two possible ways, the Compendium of Vinaya Principles and Meanings by Master Lingfeng [Ouyi] uses only one of them, holding that a great being [bodhisattva] who kills an animal likewise commits the grave offense — because one who has received the bodhisattva precepts must already have given rise to the mind of enlightenment, and so should of himself understand that all sentient beings equally possess the buddha-nature, to be loved and cherished with compassion as one's own child, as one's own body; how, then, could one look down upon an animal and wantonly do it violence? Hence [the Compendium] uses only the single position that settles this as the grave offense. (End of quotation.) People today who have received only the five precepts do not settle this as the grave offense, but should still give rise to a compassionate mind and actively practice rescue and protection.

If one borrows another's property and, after a long time, fails to return it, appropriating it as one's own instead, this constitutes the offense of stealing.

The Vinaya's treatment of the offense of stealing is the most elaborate of all, running to several fascicles — from which one can see that this precept is by no means easy to keep. (People today, when sending a letter through the postal service, sometimes enclose paper currency inside it, or, when mailing printed matter, enclose a letter inside it — both of these are offenses of evading postal duty, and so are offenses of stealing.)

Folio 21, line 17, states: "If one has intercourse with a prostitute and pays her fee, this is no offense" — this should be understood as meaning no offense of the upper-grade, unrepentable kind. Yet how could destroying one's own body and ruining one's own virtue in this way really be called faultless? This deserves reflection.

If one's own wife or concubine is pregnant, or nursing a child, or if one engages in intercourse at a site other than the proper organ, this is likewise called sexual misconduct, as explained at length in the Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra. (Reasoned by analogy, this should be a repentable offense.)

A Vinaya treatise says: "If, O elders, you hear of these impure acts, take care not to be shocked or astonished by them. Why is this? Because the Tathāgata, out of compassion for us, spoke these coarse words specifically in order to establish the precepts; had he not spoken them, how could the weight or lightness of an offense ever be known? And if a Dharma-master is lecturing on this for others, the listeners should take care not to bare their teeth and laugh; whoever laughs should be sent out. Why is this? Because these are words the Buddha spoke, out of compassion for sentient beings, from his own golden mouth — you should give rise to a mind of shame and remorse as you listen. How could one possibly laugh?"

The precept against false speech is chiefly directed at the great false speech [of falsely claiming attainment], but it also governs lesser false speech. Lesser false speech is as the sūtra states: actually having heard something and saying one did not hear it, and so on — this should be a middle-grade repentable offense.

Divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle chatter likewise all constitute middle-grade repentable offenses. Divisive speech means telling this person what was said about them by that one, and telling that person what was said about them by this one, stirring up discord and estranging friends and kin. Harsh speech means cursing, reviling, and cursing others so that they cannot bear it. Idle chatter means speech without purpose or benefit, mere worldly frivolity, which increases heedlessness and causes one to lose right mindfulness.

Speaking of the faults and transgressions of monastic or lay bodhisattvas, monks, or nuns — the Brahma Net Sūtra and the Sūtra on Upāsaka Precepts both settle this as the grave offense, regardless of whether what is said is true or false; in either case it is an offense. Although the present five precepts do not settle this as the grave offense, one must all the more carefully guard against it, giving rise to great apprehension in the mind.

If, for the sake of gain, one praises in various ways another's accomplishment in precepts, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation, while secretly using this to extol oneself; or if, for the sake of gain, one sits, rises, walks, and stands, speaking with deliberate composure, so as to display thereby the appearance of having attained the path, wishing others to take notice — in every such case, this is a middle-grade repentable offense of the means.

If one causes another person to drink wine, each of that person's swallows and each of one's own instructing settle an offense together — "settling an offense for each swallow" means that a single swallow settles a single offense, and many swallows settle many offenses.

The Song-dynasty Lingzhi Vinaya Master [Yuan Zhao] says: "In this land there are many foods preserved in wine-lees, in which the flavor and potency remain entirely intact, still capable of intoxicating a person; people commonly indulge in eating them, and this is the hardest habit of all to restrain. It seems the Western regions originally had no such foods, and so the teaching does not explicitly prohibit them. Measured against the earlier cases of lees and leaven, this is a clear enough example — those of virtue and refinement ought urgently to attend to this."

Reading note

The note on killing animals under the Mahāyāna is the sharpest doctrinal moment in this whole closing chapter, precisely because it does not pretend the two traditions agree. Ouyi's own Compendium is cited as deliberately choosing the harder of two available positions — that a bodhisattva who kills even an animal commits the grave offense — not because the Hīnayāna ruling is wrong on its own terms, but because someone who has taken the bodhisattva vow has already committed to seeing every sentient being as bearing the same buddha-nature "as one's own child, as one's own body." Tan Fang's closing remark, that those holding only the five precepts are not bound to this stricter grading but "should still give rise to a compassionate mind," quietly declines to collapse the two standards into one — it lets the lay precepts stay lay precepts while still pointing toward something higher.

Several of the other notes function as small acts of updating a fifth-century casebook for a much later reader: the aside on mailing currency or letters inside printed matter to dodge postal duty is Tan Fang applying the sūtra's own logic about customs evasion to an institution that did not exist when Guṇavarman translated the text, and the note on wine-preserved foods does the same for the alcohol precept, extending "dregs and lees" to a category of food the translator explicitly says did not even exist in the Buddha's own country. Read together, these notes model something the sūtra itself never states outright but constantly assumes: that a precept properly understood is not a fixed list of forbidden objects, but a stable principle that each generation has to keep re-applying to whatever new form the same old temptation happens to take.
