Classifying the Teachings: What to Accept, the Great Outline, and Zhang'an's Private Notes

The last fascicle comments on the second half of the Profound Meaning's fifth profound meaning, classifying the teachings (the later part of fascicle-010 on this site). Zhanran first finishes the refutation of the "three of the south and seven of the north" — the six doctrines, the perfect doctrine, the Mahāyāna of marks and of no marks, and the single voice — and the "research in detail" of what to keep from each, with a long excursus on the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's "two birds roaming together." He then comments on the Tiantai classification proper: the great outline of sudden, gradual, and indeterminate in both teaching and contemplation (with the chapter list of the Avataṃsaka, old and new); the three proof-texts — the "Skillful Means" chapter (with the debate over the "three times seven days"), the Sūtra of Immeasurable Meanings, and the "Faith and Understanding" parable of the poor son; how the five flavors and the half-and-full complete each other; combination and non-combination; the common and particular reach of each period; benefiting and not benefiting; and the teaching through increasing numbers. The fascicle closes with Zhanran's notes on Zhang'an's appended "private record" — the prajñā and the Lotus, the two to eight treasuries, the Candrapradīpa's seventy-seven sets of four, the refutation of the five periods and seven stages, and the two treasuries of Dharmottara matched with the four teachings.

Again three: first a difficulty from the mutual identity of the two truths; next, from "Though the Avataṃsaka" on, a difficulty from the overall substance of the sūtra-divisions; third, from "Within the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra" on, citing the Larger [Prajñā] again to conclude the difficulty. The first passage, two: first raising the two truths, in which "mutual identity" means: the conventional is marks, the true is no marks, and they should not be separated from each other. Next, from "the person of complete wisdom" on, citing proof: the person of complete wisdom realizes the true; the true is the unconditioned; distinction is the conventional; "by means of" (以) means "using." Using this unconditioned, he can distinguish, so the true is discussed in terms of the conventional. The next passage again two: first, according to what the divisions make clear, the meaning should not be separated; next, from "If one uses marks alone, and purely so" on, making clear that setting them up separately is a hindrance, and lodging here a judgment that the Laṅkāvatāra belongs to the vaipulya division. Others say the Laṅkāvatāra is not the third period. In the sixth fascicle of the seven-fascicle sūtra: "Mahāmati asked: Even the non-Buddhists forbid and do not allow eating meat; how much more should the Tathāgata, embracing and nurturing all with great compassion, not allow oneself and others to eat meat?" Since the Brahmā's Net in the great vehicle had already forbidden it and yet this is still said, one should know that the Laṅkāvatāra, after the four Āgamas, is the beginning of the gradual prohibition. In the Buddha's answer below it still says "bodhisattvas should not eat meat"; so one knows that the allowance in the small teaching still remained.

○Fourth, researching in detail, two: first explaining the name of the section; next, from "As for the five-period classification" on, researching properly in detail; it examines in order the doctrines and teachings used by the three of the south and the seven of the north above; following it, it can be seen, and is not divided further into sections. "Entirely losing the other three gates, and likewise losing the seven gates": because within the prajñā there is shared and unshared; though the unshared has the difference of distinct and perfect, speaking of it generally for now it is called "non-empty," and set against the four of the shared it makes eight gates; since only one is grasped, the three of the shared and the four of the unshared are entirely lost, so it says "losing seven." "This grasps only the single kind of censuring the lesser śrāvaka, entirely losing the other seven kinds of śrāvaka": each of the eight gates of the Tripiṭaka and shared has śrāvakas; if one censures [only] the marked, one censures only the one kind, the gate of existence of the Tripiṭaka, and entirely loses the other three and the four of the shared, so it says "losing seven." "The gathering of the five": the five means humans, gods, and the three [vehicles]. "Nor the gathering of the seven": "seven," because the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas each have two. "Entirely losing the other seven [techniques]": permanence, bliss, and the other two, and neither permanent nor impermanent and the other three, make eight techniques in all; so one knows that permanence alone obtains only one of the eight techniques. The "eight techniques" are also as in my Record on the eighth fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. "The two birds roaming together": the Great Sūtra, fascicle 8, the "Bird Simile" chapter, says: "Good man, birds are of two kinds, one called kalaviṅka and the other called mandarin duck; they roam and rest together and do not leave each other." This chapter answers the earlier question, how is the holy practice shared? "Sārasa-kalaviṅka": an old explanation says "sārasa" means pair and "kalaviṅka" means bird; but "sāla" is rendered "firm," so it should not be said to mean "pair." Some say the sārasa is one pair and the kalaviṅka one pair. Some say the sārasa is one and the kalaviṅka one; the text cited says "birds are of two kinds." Some render "sārasa" as "mandarin duck," citing as proof, "In the question it says sārasa, in the answer it says mandarin duck; the kind differs but the meaning is the same," and so substitute mandarin duck for sārasa. Some say "sārasa" is rendered "heavenly crane," citing the six-fascicle Nihuan, which says "geese, cranes, and śāri birds." Zhang'an says: the Han [translators] were not good at Sanskrit sounds, and this only adds to dispute; the intent lies in the comparison, taking the male and female roaming and resting together to illustrate the immeasurable in the one and the one in the immeasurable. Question: Is it the ordinary together with the sage, the sage together with the ordinary, or the ordinary together with the ordinary? Others say: if one contemplates the permanent, one does not recognize the impermanence of birth and death; if one contemplates the impermanence of birth and death, one does not recognize the permanent abiding of nirvāṇa; if the two understandings are not distinguished, it is like recognizing gold but not brass, or brass but not gold; to recognize both things precisely is called the double contemplation; the other three are also so. Ordinary and sage are both so; so it says "roaming together." This explanation goes against the simile: one bird goes to the bottom, birth and death; one bird goes to the top, nirvāṇa; rising and sinking are forever apart; where is roaming together? Again, someone makes clear roaming together in terms of half and full. Roaming together means that birth and death and nirvāṇa each have permanence and impermanence; taking the pair of permanences in birth and death and nirvāṇa as one pair is full, taking the pair of impermanences in birth and death and nirvāṇa as one pair is half. Now it is made clear that this is not so: the two permanences arising together are two males; the two impermanences arising together are two females; this too goes against the simile, so it is not used. Now, roaming together means: in birth and death and nirvāṇa there are both permanence and impermanence; below and above they roam together and rest together; phenomena and principle are identical; the two are the middle, the middle is the two; not two and middle, yet two and middle; the meaning of phenomena and principle, male and female, is accomplished together. So this roaming together must be in terms of the six identities; here there are fully the ordinary together with the sage and so on, as in the three propositions above.

Ru-Yi Translation

Using the single voice: "wisdom together with skillful means is liberation" and so on: wisdom is the real, skillful means the provisional; the two meanings are identical and cannot be separated. Others use the five periods and obtain only skillful means without real wisdom; so the Vimalakīrti says "skillful means without wisdom is bondage"; so using skillful means without real wisdom is called compassion of craving and views, that is, the Tripiṭaka bodhisattva. Speaking generally, apart from [compassion] without object, all are called bondage by skillful means. Thus the distinct teaching before the grounds and the shared teaching going out into the provisional are all called craving and views. If there is only the single voice without provisional wisdom, not only is skillful means bondage, but wisdom too is not liberation; so the Vimalakīrti says "wisdom without skillful means is bondage," that is, the empty wisdom of the two assemblies. Speaking generally, the bodhisattvas of the shared and distinct entering emptiness are all called wisdom without skillful means. Now this meaning is used to reject their use of the teachings: if all the flavors used the real, though called the single voice, without meeting conditions, censuring, rebuking, washing and sifting to form the fresh and ripened butter, so that on reaching the Lotus they could enter the one real, it is called without skillful means. If one uses the benefit of the five periods gradually drawing in, one loses the equal single voice of all the flavors, and it is called without wisdom. So now they are rejected; the meaning is the same as bound wisdom and bound skillful means.

○Fifth, in classifying the teachings: listing the sections and explaining. The explanation has six of its own. First, explaining the great outline, again three: marking, listing, and explaining. The explanation again has two kinds, teaching and contemplation. Within it again two: first the meaning of the two gates, then the explanation proper. The explanation, two: first explaining the two kinds; next, from "Now, distinguishing" on, sifting, showing, and praising. The first passage, two. First, in terms of the teaching, first explaining the sudden teaching: beginning from the Avataṃsaka and ending at the Lotus, all have the meaning of the sudden; so among the openly revealed it excludes only the Deer Park, because the other divisions all have the sudden; it is called the sudden mark of teaching, but not the sudden division. The indeterminate below is likewise. Here it also cites the first attainment of the path, for now borrowing the secret to help enter this, making clear that even the first attainment in the Deer Park has the meaning of the sudden — how much more the sūtras? "Seven places and eight assemblies": this is the old sūtra, in fifty or sixty fascicles. In the fourteenth year of Yixi of the Jin, the Tripiṭaka master Buddhabhadra of northern India, here rendered "Awakened Worthy," translated it at the Sikong Monastery in Yangzhou. Later, the Tripiṭaka master of the great Tang, who came in the first year of Zhengsheng, together with Śikṣānanda of Khotan, here rendered "Joyful Awakening," translated it at the Aijing Monastery in eighty fascicles. But of the three texts in the dragon palace, the upper text has verses as many as the dust motes of thirteen worlds, the middle text four hundred ninety-eight thousand eight hundred verses, and the lower text a hundred thousand verses in forty-eight chapters; now there are only thirty-nine chapters, so the sūtra is still not complete. The old sūtra has seven places and eight assemblies; the new translation adds one more assembly in the Hall of Universal Light. The first, at the araṇya of Magadha, has six chapters: first, the Wondrous Adornments of the Lords of the World; second, the Appearance of the Tathāgata; third, the Samādhi of Samantabhadra; fourth, the Formation of the Worlds; fifth, the Flower-Treasury World; sixth, Vairocana. The second assembly, in the Hall of Universal Light at the bend of the Nairañjanā in Magadha, has six chapters: first, the Names of the Tathāgata; second, the Four Truths; third, Awakening by Light; fourth, the Bodhisattvas' Questions; fifth, Pure Conduct; sixth, Chief in Goodness. The third assembly, in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, teaches the ten abidings, in six chapters: first, Ascending to the Summit of Sumeru; second, Praises on the Summit of Sumeru; third, the Ten Abidings; fourth, Brahmā Conduct; fifth, the Merit of First Arousing the Mind; sixth, the Clarity of the Dharma — taught to make the ten abidings advance to later stages.
