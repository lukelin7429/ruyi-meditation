The Wondrous of Stage: From the Small Herb to the Most Real Stage

Fascicle ten comments on the wondrous of stage, the fourth of the ten wondrous aspects (the end of fascicle-004 and the start of fascicle-005 on this site). The Profound Meaning reads the stages of all four teachings out of the Lotus's parable of the medicinal herbs: the small herb (humans and gods), the middle herb (the two vehicles), the superior herb (the Tripiṭaka bodhisattva), the small tree (the shared teaching), the great tree (the distinct teaching), and "the most real matter" (the perfect teaching). Zhanran's comments here are largely technical supplements: he fills in the Kośa and Vibhāṣā lists of non-returners and arhats (including the reckoning that yields "twelve thousand nine hundred sixty kinds" of non-returner), explains the ten grounds of the shared teaching, and defends the Profound Meaning's use of three sūtras — the Yingluo, the Larger Prajñā, and the Nirvāṇa — to order the distinct stages, with a digression on the translation history of the prajñā sūtras. In the perfect teaching he comments on the ten headings of the "most real stage," the refutation of those who say sudden awakening needs no stages, the five ranks of the practitioner before the ten faiths (matched with the five methods for stilling the mind), the ten faiths and ten abidings, and the forty-two syllable gates of the Larger Prajñā. He closes by recalling Zhiyi's first meeting with Huisi and the wheelwright of the Zhuangzi, who could not put his skill into words.

The Wondrous of Stage

Chinese Source

◎第四位妙為四：初明來意，次「但位」下略敘諸位權實，三「今經」下略述今經，四「小草」下依經廣解。初文中言「體宗用足」者，境，體也；行，宗也；智，用也。次文中言「地攝等論別敘一途」者，此等多是別教一門，義不兼於大小，方等諸經明位委悉不及《瓔珞》，般若諸經明位委悉不及《仁王》，然《瓔珞》偏存別門，《仁王》多在圓別及含通意，而並不明辨位之意，尚不辨麁妙，何況論開？故知但是當味明義而不辨於始終位意。所言「意」者，諸教何故權實不同？諸味何故增減差異？謂施開廢等即其意也。三今經者，先略敘意，次略屬對。初中今經明位雖即不及彼之二經明位似細，而今經義兼大小及開判等，則諸經未明。何者？如〈序〉天雨四華，〈方便〉開示悟入，〈譬喻〉遊於四方，〈化城〉五百由旬，並迹門實位也；草菴、化城、三草二木並迹門權位也。分別功德始從無生即初住也，乃至一生即等覺也，此亦本實位也。菴城被廢唯存渚宅，草木並依一地一雨，此是會諸權位，咸歸一實，故云麁判權實。「文不盡度」者，相傳云：西方《法華》布一由旬。次屬對中二：初引經，次屬對。四廣釋者，文中自分三草二木及以一實以為六位。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, the wondrous of stage, in four: first making clear the purpose of its coming; next, from "Yet 'stage'" on, briefly stating the provisional and the real of the various stages; third, from "The present sūtra" on, briefly stating the present sūtra; fourth, from "the small herb" on, explaining at length according to the sūtra. The first says "the Essence, the Cardinal Doctrine, and the Function are thereby already sufficient": the object is the essence, practice the cardinal doctrine, and wisdom the function. The next says "the Bodhisattva-bhūmi, the Mahāyānasaṃgraha, and similar treatises … each sets out only its own particular path": these are mostly one gate of the distinct teaching, whose meaning does not include great and small. The Vaipulya sūtras' clarification of the stages is not as detailed as the Yingluo, and the Prajñā sūtras' not as detailed as the Humane Kings; yet the Yingluo keeps one-sidedly to the distinct gate, and the Humane Kings is mostly perfect and distinct, also containing the shared meaning; but neither makes clear the purpose of distinguishing the stages; they do not even distinguish coarse and wondrous, how much less discuss opening? So one knows they only make clear the meaning within their own flavor and do not distinguish the purpose of the stages from beginning to end. "Purpose" means: why do the teachings differ in provisional and real? Why do the flavors increase and decrease differently? Establishing, opening, setting aside, and the rest are that purpose. Third, the present sūtra: first briefly stating the meaning, next briefly matching. In the first: though the present sūtra's clarification of the stages falls short of those two sūtras, whose clarification of the stages seems detailed, the present sūtra's meaning includes great and small, opening and judging, and so on, which the other sūtras have not made clear. How so? As in the "Introduction," the heavens raining down the four flowers; in "Skillful Means," opening, showing, awakening to, and entering; in the "Parable," roaming the four directions; in the "Phantom City," the five hundred yojanas — all are the real stages of the trace gate; the thatched hut, the phantom city, and the three herbs and two trees are all provisional stages of the trace gate. "Discerning Merits," beginning from non-arising, which is the first abiding, up to "one [more] birth," which is virtual awakening — these too are real stages of the origin. The hut and the city are set aside, leaving only the island and the house; the herbs and trees all rely on the one ground and the one rain — this merges all provisional stages, all returning to the one real; hence "roughly judging the provisional and the real." "The Sanskrit text was not translated here in its entirety": it is handed down that the Lotus in the West, spread out, covers a yojana. Next, the matching has two: first citing the sūtra, then matching. Fourth, the broad explanation: the text divides by itself the three herbs, the two trees, and the one real into six stages.

The Small Herb: Humans and Gods

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the small herb, in three: first briefly citing the sūtra to establish it; next distinguishing its cause and fruit; third, from "the human stage" on, the explanation proper. The explanation has two: humans and gods. The humans have three: first distinguishing the cause; next, from "the lowest grade" on, briefly making clear the fruit; third, from "All of these" on, making clear again the lord among humans. The first is as the text says. In the next, the four wheels of the human stage: the Abhidharmakośa says, "Gold, silver, copper, and iron wheels — one, two, three, four continents": the iron-wheel king has one continent, and so on to the golden-wheel king with four. A wheel-turning king first practices seven dharmas: first, giving to the poor; second, respecting the people and supporting parents; third, at the four seasons and eight festivals, sacrificing to the four seas; fourth, cultivating forbearance in the four seasons; fifth, sixth, and seventh, removing the three poisons; then bathing, receiving the fast, making vows, and so on; then the sacred treasures respond of themselves, and so on. The lord among humans is only the highest of merit; being the highest of merit, his retribution is to be lord among humans.

Next, making clear the heavenly stage, divided by itself into the different heavens of the three realms. Within this, first briefly stating the causes of the heavens of the three realms, then the explanation proper. In the explanation, first, the Four Kings have forty abodes, the Four Kings' abodes being made clear briefly according to the [Sūtra of the] Establishment of Mindfulness of the True Dharma. "Garland-bearing gods": the Abhidharmakośa says, "Holding-firm, garland-bearing, ever-proud, and the great kings' host dwell in order on the four tiers, and also dwell on the other seven mountains"; body size and lifespan are fully as in the Kośa's "World" chapter. "The Four Heavenly Kings": the Great Treatise says, "In the east is Dhṛtarāṣṭra, in the language of Qin 'Lord Who Holds the Country'; in the south Virūḍhaka, in Qin 'Lord of Growth'; in the west Virūpākṣa, in Qin 'Lord of Various [Eyes]'; in the north Vaiśravaṇa, in Qin 'Lord of Much Hearing.'" These four, as kings, rule the four continents; the four tiers each have ten abodes; the names of these forty heavens are all set up from their causal practices.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The three refuges": according to the Sūtra of the Rare, which compares at length the merit of the three refuges: "Teaching [beings of] the four continents and the six desire heavens to attain the four fruits does not equal the merit of the three refuges." Again, as in the Ekottara, there was a son of the gods of Trāyastriṃśa who, when the five signs of decay appeared, was to be reborn among pigs; the sound of his sorrow reached the lord of the gods. Hearing it, the lord of the gods called him and said: You should take the three refuges. At once he did as taught and escaped rebirth as a pig. The Buddha spoke a verse: "All who take refuge in the Buddha do not fall into the three evil destinies; their taints exhausted, dwelling among humans and gods, they will then reach nirvāṇa." Having taken the three refuges, he was born in an elder's house, again left home, and became one beyond training. The text says "ten claps of the hands": perhaps he first held the natural precepts and took the three refuges for that length of time, then died at once, and so gained this reward; because the mind of refuge was unmixed, he got the name "White"; that the gods surpass the wheel-turning king is a matter of course. "Sixteen times" is because the refuge was superior. "Flows forth of itself from its own mountains and rivers": in the sūtra all are said to be flowing rivers, even rivers of wine and so on; for these forty heavens one must explain the names of the results in detail by their causal practices, as will be explained. "Steep Cliff": because the river crossing was hard to cross. "Fruit-of-Life": pure precepts are like fruit. Again, planting fruit trees; the merit of stūpas is the greatest; white is the root of colors; like flowers, which all who see them rejoice at; extinguishing fire with water practices the path of compassion; reconciling anger and disputes, hence "Craving Desire"; teaching the Dharma gathers good objects; seeing a pure field moves the mind of faith; making others rejoice is like enjoying play — explaining the names by comparing the causes can be known by this. "Non-Buddhist paths speak of these as the sun and the constellations" and so on: the outsiders also reckon that the northern stars do not set. Again, in the Lokaprajñapti Abhidharma, there are outsiders who reckon that the earth constantly moves without stopping; the Buddha refutes them: then a thing thrown forward should go backward. Others reckon that the earth is constantly falling; the Buddha says: then a thing thrown upward should not reach the ground. Others reckon that the stars do not move and the earth itself turns; the Buddha says: then an arrow shot should not reach the target. The cause of the six desire heavens should be all ten goods; it says here "not killing" up to "seven precepts" because, receiving all and keeping part, the name is given from what is kept most. "Mundane precepts" are the precepts against [conduct that draws] suspicion and blame. The other matters of the heavens — lifespan, body size, and so on — are fully as in the Kośa and the Vibhāṣā; this is to know the order of stages briefly, not to discuss them broadly.

The Middle Herb: The Two Vehicles

Ru-Yi Translation

The middle herb stage, in two: first fixing the persons to judge cause and fruit, then the explanation proper. The explanation has three: first refuting the ancients; next briefly showing the two treatises; third explaining the gate of existence at length. "The twenty-seven worthies and sages": in the thirtieth [fascicle] of the Madhyama Āgama, the Sūtra of the Fields of Merit: "An elder asked the Buddha: How many fields of merit are there? The Buddha said: There are eighteen in training and nine beyond training. The eighteen in training are: the faith-follower, the dharma-follower, the one released by faith, the one attaining by view, the bodily witness, the one from family to family, the one with a single seed, the one bound for the first fruit, the one who has attained the first fruit, bound for the second fruit, the second fruit, bound for the third fruit, the third fruit, and the five kinds of non-returner, namely [attaining nirvāṇa] in the interval, at birth, with effort, without effort, and going upstream. The nine beyond training are: the thinking, the advancing, the regressing, the non-regressing, the unshakable, the abiding, the guarding, the one released by wisdom, and the one released both ways." The Kośa's text agrees; the Satyasiddhi should be checked further. The explanation proper has two: first marking, then explaining. The explanation is the two vehicles. First the śrāvakas: first the worthies, then the sages. First the worthies, in two: first listing, then explaining. The explanation has three: first explaining the name, next the capability, third the explanation proper. In the capability, the seven worthy stages were briefly distinguished in the wondrous of wisdom. Here it says "the four truths of craving and of views": these are just what the two delusions of craving and views rely on; though there are craving and views, all belong to views, fully as said in the eighty-eight fixations. The explanation proper falls into seven by itself: first, the five methods cure the five obstructions, fully as in the remedies in the seventh [fascicle] of Calming and Contemplation. "The truth of suffering as the initial gate": the later text takes the truth of origin as the pratyekabuddha's initial gate; the śrāvaka's truth of suffering comes first because he first contemplates the four applications of mindfulness; for the pratyekabuddha, ignorance stands at the head of the links, and he first breaks craving, grasping, and becoming. "The object general and the contemplation general": contemplating the four objects generally with the four contemplations. "The object particular and the contemplation general": contemplating each object with the four contemplations. "The object general and the contemplation particular": contemplating the four objects generally with each contemplation. The three [kinds of] two can be understood by this. "Warmth" and the other passages were distinguished under [the wondrous of] wisdom. Here the order of stages is made clear so as to know their depth briefly, still more briefly than there; to know them broadly, one should seek the treatises.

Ru-Yi Translation

The seven sages, again three: listing the stages, explaining the names, the explanation proper. Opened broadly, they are fully as in the Vibhāṣā and the Kośa; the present text is extremely brief, enough to judge their depth. In explaining the names, "with the forbearance regarding suffering, illumination arises, leaving the ordinary and entering the sagely": once at the forbearance regarding suffering, in order and without interval one surely enters the first fruit; here, speaking from what comes later, it calls them generally "sages." The three fetters, the eighty-eight fixations, and the seven births are as in my Record on the sixth fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. "[Attaining nirvāṇa] in the interval is of three kinds": namely swift, not swift, and long-abiding; according to the Kośa, there are nine kinds in all, each of three divided into three, namely in the interval, at birth, and going upstream. With effort and without effort, attaining parinirvāṇa only after being born in the form realm, are both included in "at birth." "The three in the interval": swift, not swift, and taking long, likened to sparks flying from a fire; the three meanings can be understood by reflection. All are discussed as swift, not swift, and so on, in the intermediate state. "At birth has three": first, [attaining] at birth, set up by swiftness; second, with effort, set up by not being swift; third, without effort, set up by taking long. All are born in the form realm, and then swift and the rest are discussed.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Going upstream has three": first, fully leaping, set up by swiftness; second, half leaping, set up by not being swift; third, dying everywhere, set up by taking long. From the first form [heaven] to the last there are these three different persons; these three nines differ by karma, delusion, and faculty. Differing by karma: creating karma to be experienced now forms [nirvāṇa] in the interval; creating karma to be experienced in the next birth forms [nirvāṇa] at birth; creating karma to be experienced later forms [nirvāṇa] going upstream. By the three [grades of] delusion: lower-grade delusion forms in the interval, middle-grade at birth, upper-grade going upstream. Differing by faculty: upper faculties in the interval, middle at birth, lower going upstream. Severing the higher and lower fetters: the five higher fetters are restlessness, pride, ignorance, attachment to form, and attachment to the formless. Translator's notes

The forty abodes of the Four Kings. The Profound Meaning's long list of heavenly abodes is drawn from the Saddharmasmṛtyupasthāna Sūtra (正法念處經, T721); Zhanran adds the Kośa's verse on the four tiers of Sumeru and the Great Treatise's names of the Four Heavenly Kings.

The pig-destined god. The story of the god of Trāyastriṃśa saved from rebirth as a pig by taking the three refuges is from the Ekottara Āgama (T125); "the Sūtra of the Rare" (希有經) is the 希有校量功德經 (T690).

The twenty-seven worthies and sages. Zhanran cites the Madhyama Āgama's Sūtra of the Fields of Merit (福田經, in fascicle 30); the list of eighteen in training and nine beyond training is quoted closely.

12,960 kinds of non-returner. The calculation is quoted from the Saṃyuktābhidharmahṛdaya (雜阿毘曇心論, T1552); Zhanran notes that the text is hard to come by.

"Did anyone see me fall?" In paragraph 12, 「四顧遠望不有他人見我倒不」 is read with the final 不 as a question particle.

The translation history of the prajñā sūtras. Zhanran's dates and attributions in paragraph 19 (for example, that Dharmarakṣa translated the Guangzan in the first year of Taikang, and the repeated mention of the Damingdu) do not all agree with the catalogs; they are translated as written. His point is only that the Guangzan is part of the Larger Prajñā and not a separate period of teaching.

The Humane Kings on the Mahāprajñā. "Twenty-nine years after attaining the path" is Zhanran's paraphrase of the Renwang jing's opening.

The five ranks and the five methods. Paragraph 34 explains Zhang'an's "private" matching of the five ranks with the five methods for stilling the mind; "the text being the vital breath of the Dharma-body" is explained by analogy with the breath of the physical body.

The ten faiths have a hundred. Paragraph 35 shows how each of the ten dharmas of contemplation becomes one of the ten faiths, so that each faith contains all ten.

The forty-two syllable gates. The explanation of "syllables equal" and "speech equal" is attributed to Nanyue (Huisi), apparently from a lost two-fascicle commentary on the syllable gates.

The wheelwright. The story of Wheelwright Bian is quoted, with small differences, from the Zhuangzi, "The Way of Heaven" (天道).

Lemmas in paragraphs 35–46. The quoted phrases in these paragraphs were matched against the site's fascicle-005 where they could be found; a few short sūtra phrases quoted only by Zhanran are translated directly.
