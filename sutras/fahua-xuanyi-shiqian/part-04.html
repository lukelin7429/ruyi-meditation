Broad, High, and Long; the Ten Suchnesses; Relative and Absolute Wondrous

Fascicle four of the Explanatory Notes covers the end of the Profound Meaning's first fascicle and most of its second (fascicle-001 to fascicle-002 on this site). After closing the refutation of Guangzhai — counting how the "twenty-four" objections are reckoned — Zhanran follows Zhiyi's own account of "wondrous": the cause and fruit that are "broad, high, and long" in essence, stage, and function, read four ways (the ten realms, the five flavors, the contemplation of mind, the six identities) and matched to the four onenesses. He then comments on the three dharmas of Nanyue — of sentient beings, of the Buddha, and of mind — and on the ten suchnesses at length: the three readings of the sūtra passage, the hundred realms and thousand suchnesses, the ten suchnesses of each group of realms, the three kinds of birth in the land of change, why the Buddha's retribution is "unsurpassed," and the three closing verses. The fascicle ends with the general explanation of "wondrous" as relative and absolute, including the four absolutes of the four teachings, the simile of the government ferries, and the warning against chasing words "like a foolish dog chasing a clod." Two remarks stand out: that the ordinary practitioner should "constantly contemplate a single thought, the mind arising in the slightest way," and that citing far and near without contemplating mind is "like a poor man counting another's treasure."

The End of the Refutation of Guangzhai

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, in refuting the spiritual powers: "why does it extend only the lifespan, and not extend the eye?" — this strips it of principle. "Why does it not extend the tongue?" — this strips it of teaching.

Where the text lacks these, it is abbreviated. The one phrase "the eye does not see nature" takes up "lacking principle" briefly; fully stated, it should take up all four phrases.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the first: the meanings refuted in the twenty-four objections are all in Guangzhai's commentary; here they are cited directly as objections without going into detail. "Twenty-four" is reckoned, for now, by pairing: present and past discussed together make twenty-four. If present and past are discussed separately, the objections to the past cause and fruit are three each, making six objections, and the objections to the present cause and fruit also make six, twelve objections in all; each with four rounds makes forty-eight. Again, in the present text, of the three senses for the fruit only the present stage and function are challenged, and the past is left out, so two places of four rounds are missing, and the text as it stands makes forty. "Spear and shield" is as in my Record on the fifth fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. Here his own four onenesses are turned back to challenge his "wondrous" and make it coarse, and his own three [categories] for the past are turned back to challenge his "coarse" and make it wondrous; hence "neither more nor less."

Zhiyi's Own Account: Broad, High, and Long

Chinese Source

四正論今意，又二：先借光宅義以顯今妙，次正釋。初文為三：先標、次釋、三結。釋中二：先釋，次「初約」下判。初文自四：初約十界釋中二：先釋、次結。初釋中二：先因、次果。先因中二：先釋、次融通。初釋中三：體、位、用也。於一念中一法攝九故名「廣」，一念中九無非佛法故名「高」。十法無非三諦，並有破三惑、現三身之用。次融通中意者，前廣義似於橫，次高義似於縱，用中似獨具三，故須合前二義共成一意，秖是三諦一心有體位用耳。次果三義中言「體遍」者，本法身也，即本地已成法身也。非但高廣等殊，指本迹二門因果永異。「是為」下結，可知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, discussing the present meaning itself, again in two: first, borrowing Guangzhai's terms to reveal the present wondrous; next, the explanation proper. The first has three: first marking, then explaining, third concluding. The explanation has two: first explaining; then, from "The first account" on, judging. The first falls into four by itself. First, the explanation in terms of the ten realms, in two: first explaining, then concluding. The first explanation has two: first the cause, then the fruit. The cause has two: first explaining, then fusing them together. The first explanation has three: essence, stage, function. In one thought one dharma includes the nine, hence "broad"; in one thought the nine are none other than the Buddha-dharma, hence "high." The ten dharmas are none other than the three truths, and all have the function of breaking the three delusions and manifesting the three bodies. Next, the meaning of fusing them together: the preceding sense, "broad," seems horizontal; the next sense, "high," seems vertical; and "function" seems to possess the three alone; so the first two senses must be combined to form one meaning together — it is just the essence, stage, and function of the three truths in one mind. Next, among the three senses of the fruit, "its essence pervades" is the original Dharma-body, the Dharma-body already accomplished in the fundamental ground. Not only do broad, high, and the rest differ; it points out that the causes and fruits of the two gates of origin and trace are forever different. From "These are" on is the conclusion, which can be understood.

Next, judging coarse and wondrous by the five flavors, in two: first listing the five flavors; next, from "Moreover, the ghee sūtra" on, distinguishing sameness and difference again. In the first: both gates, cause and fruit, are spoken of in terms of the trace, so even in the milk there is one wondrous cause and fruit, and the other flavors likewise; so below it concludes that they are "no different from the wondrous cause and wondrous fruit" of the ghee.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, in terms of the contemplation of mind: to make clear the ten objects and the ten vehicles in detail — as for discussing the objects of contemplation, this does not match the detail of Calming and Contemplation; but for judging coarse and wondrous by the three, essence, stage, and function, this passage is clear and essential. If one's essence and the rest are not broad, high, and long, it is not called wondrous, and it is not the contemplation of the present sūtra. If one grasps this meaning, the Lotus samādhi appears right here; the pivot of the whole sūtra is established here; the purport of a lifetime's teaching is gathered here; the Buddha's purpose in appearing in the world is accomplished here. It is the marrow of the ten dharmas that form contemplation, and the guide of the ten inconceivables. Thus the two earlier explanations, by the ten realms and by the five flavors, are both included in it; and the later one, by the six identities, is the stage of the contemplation of mind; so the preceding two senses are established only by relying on this. Therefore the practitioner constantly contemplates a single thought, the mind arising in the slightest way: because it possesses all minds, it is equal to the Buddha's mind; because it is equal to the Buddha's mind, all six are called identity; once ultimate identity is attained, one can skillfully set up the five flavors. Reflect on it, reflect on it!

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the explanation by the six identities: each is "likewise a broad essence," since each possesses the ten realms; "likewise a high stage," since all are the Buddha-realm; "likewise a long function," since all are ultimate. All lie in one thought; hence each says "likewise." Next, from "The first account" on, it judges that the meanings of the preceding four explanations are the same as the four onenesses. How so? The present sūtra's essence possesses the ten dharma-realms, as it says, "the true mark: such a mark," and so on; because of this the sūtra's function includes the earlier four flavors; by contemplating this sūtra wondrous practice is formed; from beginning to end there are the six identities. The meaning of the four onenesses is evident; therefore the four explanations form the present four onenesses — and all the more since broad, high, and long pervade all four explanations. Hence the present teaching fully possesses the three, the broad essence and the rest, all forming the four onenesses; only then does one know that Guangzhai had not even a breath of this. Next, from "This briefly indicates" on, concluding what precedes. From "Given at length" on, introducing what follows.

The Three Dharmas of Nanyue

Chinese Source

次釋中二：先舉南嶽分三，次今師順南嶽意廣解。初文中二：初標列，次釋。釋中自三：初眾生法妙中六：先明妙有所憑。次「又經」下引今經相似之文，以驗眾生法成妙也。三引《大經》證眾生法位麁而行妙，初學大乘位居肉眼，從行而說故云佛眼。四「央掘」下舉果以勸脩妙行。五引《大品》二文明行妙之相。六「此即」下結。初文可解。次文者，既云「父母所生」，即是眾生清淨若此，故即名妙。第三文者既云「肉眼」，故名眾生，名為佛眼，故即是妙。四引《央掘經》，具如《止觀》第七記。五引《大品》二文中，初言「六自在王」者，凡夫為六所使，不名為王亦非自在，純行染污又非清淨。今六根得理，理無過上，故名為「王」。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the explanation, in two: first raising Nanyue's division into three; next, our master, following Nanyue's meaning, explains it at length. The first has two: first heading and list, then explanation. The explanation falls into three by itself. First, the wondrous dharma of sentient beings, in six parts: first, making clear what the wondrous rests on. Next, from "Again, the sūtra says" on, citing similar texts of the present sūtra to verify that the dharma of sentient beings forms the wondrous. Third, citing the Great Sūtra to prove that the dharma of sentient beings is coarse in stage but wondrous in practice: those first studying the Mahāyāna are at the stage of the physical eye, but speaking from practice it is called the buddha-eye. Fourth, from "Aṅgulimālīya" on, raising the fruit to encourage the cultivation of wondrous practice. Fifth, citing two texts of the Larger Prajñā to make clear the marks of wondrous practice. Sixth, from "This is how" on, the conclusion. The first passage can be understood. In the next: since it says "given by his father and mother," the sentient being is pure in this way, and so is called wondrous. In the third: since it says "physical eye," it is called a sentient being; since it is named the buddha-eye, it is wondrous. Fourth, the citation of the Aṅgulimālīya Sūtra is as in full in my Record on the seventh fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. Fifth, in citing the two texts of the Larger Prajñā, first, "the six sovereign kings": ordinary people are driven by the six [senses]; they are not called kings, nor are they sovereign, and acting purely in defilement they are not pure either. Now the six faculties attain principle, and principle has nothing above it; hence "kings."

遍一切處，故名「自在」。自性無染不為惑拘，故云「清淨」。別在行成，通具六即。又云「一切法趣」等者，即是六根皆具三諦故妙。「趣」義具如《止觀》第二記具引本文委釋，當知諸經並有眾生妙文，但部兼帶不受妙名耳。六「此即」下結，可知。次「佛法妙者」，先釋，次「如是」下結。釋中意者，攝前四味並是今經權智所攝，而與實理相即故妙。次心法妙意者，《大論》云：「眾生心性猶如利刀，唯用割泥，泥無所成，刀日就損。」理體常妙，眾生自麁。初引《安樂行》中既云「觀一切法，不為生死所動，不為煩惱所退」，以煩惱生死是法界故。又「一念心隨喜」等者，即觀行位。初秖於貪瞋一念心起，體即權實，諸皆例然，隨順三諦，故云「隨喜」。是故隨喜名心法妙。《普賢觀》意者，心體即理故云「自空」，誰執罪福故云「無主」，應遍十界以明罪福，在一念心方成妙觀。「觀心無心」等者，能緣之心既無，所緣之法安在？能所不二，故云「純是」，以心體本妙，故可於心行而求解脫。破心微塵如《止觀》第三記。

Ru-Yi Translation

It pervades every place, hence "sovereign." Its own nature is undefiled and not bound by delusion, hence "pure." In the particular sense this lies in the completion of practice; in the general sense it includes all six identities. Again, "All dharmas converge upon" and so on: the six faculties all possess the three truths, hence wondrous. The meaning of "converge" is as in full in my Record on the second fascicle of Calming and Contemplation, which cites the original text in full and explains it in detail. One should know that all sūtras have passages on the wondrousness of sentient beings, but since their collections combine and accompany, they do not receive the name "wondrous." Sixth, from "This is how" on, the conclusion, can be understood. Next, "the wondrous dharma of the Buddha": first explaining, then, from "these two dharmas" on, concluding. The meaning of the explanation: it includes the preceding four flavors, all of which are included in the provisional wisdom of the present sūtra and are identical with the real principle; hence wondrous. Next, the meaning of the wondrous dharma of mind: the Great Treatise says, "The mind-nature of sentient beings is like a sharp knife used only to cut mud: the mud becomes nothing, and the knife is damaged day by day." The essence of principle is always wondrous; sentient beings make themselves coarse. First, the citation from "Peaceful Practices" says, "contemplating all dharmas, not moved by birth-and-death, not turned back by afflictions," since afflictions and birth-and-death are the dharma-realm. Again, "a single thought-moment of rejoicing" and so on is the stage of contemplative practice. At first, just in a single thought of greed or anger arising, its essence is the provisional and the real; all others are likewise, according with the three truths; hence "rejoicing." Therefore rejoicing is called the wondrous dharma of mind. The meaning of the Contemplation of Samantabhadra: the mind's essence is principle, hence "itself empty"; who would cling to offense and merit? hence "no master"; it should pervade the ten realms to make clear offense and merit, and when it lies in a single thought of mind it forms wondrous contemplation. "Contemplating mind, there is no mind" and so on: since the mind that takes objects does not exist, where is the dharma taken as object? Subject and object are nondual, hence "purely." Because the mind's essence is originally wondrous, liberation can be sought within mental activity. "Breaking open a mote of dust of the mind" is as in my Record on the third fascicle of Calming and Contemplation.

The explanation has two: first, briefly making clear what the names rest on. "In expounding the dharma of sentient beings at length, this generally discusses (一往通論) every cause and fruit and all dharmas" and so on: the meaning of "sentient beings" is general, hence "generally discusses." Discussed generally, the meaning is not final, hence "at first glance" (一往). At first glance it is general; on a second look it is restricted: it does not extend to the Buddha and lies only in the cause.

Ru-Yi Translation

The dharma of the Buddha and of mind are not said to be "at first glance" because the dharma of the Buddha is definitely in the fruit and the dharma of mind definitely in the cause; so these three dharmas get distinct names. Why? Within a sentient being's body, the dharma of the Buddha and the dharma of mind still extend to cause and fruit; how much more does the name "sentient being" extend to ordinary and sagely alike?

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Buddha's body, the dharma of sentient beings and the dharma of mind are also definitely in the fruit; within the dharma of mind, the dharma of the Buddha and the dharma of sentient beings — these two are in the cause. If so, why does the sūtra say, "these three are without distinction"?

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: The essence of principle has no distinction; distinction is in terms of phenomenal function. This meaning is made clear at length, fully as in the ten dharmas forming the vehicle in Calming and Contemplation.

That is, the dharma of mind and the dharma of sentient beings; the Buddha-dharma-realm there also includes the fruit, and is not solely the fruit, because that text uses the fruit to make clear the nature of principle.

The Dharma of Sentient Beings: The Ten Suchnesses

Chinese Source

次正廣釋中自三：初廣眾生中文又自標二：謂列數，次釋。初列數中二：初通論諸經增數，次正約今經。初通諸經者，此中釋法唯立三門釋妙則本迹各十，且如下文通釋妙數中云「本迹三中一一各十，十中復各待絕不同，故有一百二十重妙」。當知諸經所列法數，一往且從增數而說，據其道理須論四教，四教之中皆有迹中三十麁法、三十妙法。更約五味以論麁妙，兼但對帶，依此等法而生增數，始從一法乃至百千，不出心佛眾生法故。增數之相略如《止觀》第六記。故宜樂不同令增減異。於中又二：初略至三，次廣例百千。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the broad explanation proper falls into three by itself. First, expounding the dharma of sentient beings at length; this text again marks two by itself: listing the numbers, then explaining. The first, listing the numbers, has two: first, discussing in general the increasing numbers in the sūtras; then, the present sūtra itself. First, in general for all sūtras: here, in explaining "dharma," only three gates are set up, while in explaining "wondrous," origin and trace have ten each; as the text below says in explaining the numbers of "wondrous" in general: "Within origin and trace, each of the three has ten; within the ten, each has the relative and the absolute, which are not the same; hence there are one hundred twenty levels of wondrous." One should know that the dharma-numbers listed in the sūtras are spoken, at first glance, by increasing numbers; by principle, the four teachings must be discussed, and within the four teachings there are, in the trace, thirty coarse dharmas and thirty wondrous dharmas. Further discussing coarse and wondrous by the five flavors — combined, exclusive, contrasted, accompanied — the increasing numbers arise from these dharmas, from one dharma up to hundreds and thousands, never going beyond the dharmas of mind, Buddha, and sentient beings. The marks of increasing numbers are roughly as in my Record on the sixth fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. So because preferences differ, they are made to increase or decrease differently. Within this again two: first, briefly up to three; then, broadly giving examples up to hundreds and thousands.

Explanatory Notes on the Profound Meaning of the Lotus Sūtra, Fascicle Four [ends]

Reading Notes for Fascicle 4

Translator's notes

Counting the objections. Zhanran explains that "twenty-four" comes from pairing past and present (six categories × four onenesses); taken separately they would be forty-eight, and the text as it stands, which omits the past in two places, gives forty.

"Constantly contemplate a single thought." Paragraph 8 (「行者常觀一念介爾起心……」) is one of the passages in this work most often quoted in later Tiantai writing on the contemplation of mind; it is translated closely.

"Generally discusses" (一往通論). The site's Profound Meaning renders 「若廣眾生法，一往通論諸因果及一切法」 without the word 一往 ("at first glance"), which Zhanran makes the point of his comment.

The Puchao Sūtra. The passage on not "appraising" others is from the last fascicle of the Puchao sanmei jing (T627), the sūtra on Ajātaśatru's remorse; Zhanran quotes it in condensed form.

Aṅgulimāla's "good nature." Zhanran's lemma reads 「善性相熟」 where the Taishō Profound Meaning (and the site) reads 「實相性熟」; the variant is kept as he wrote it.

Inherent and cultivated evil. In paragraph 27 Zhanran says that the "evil nature" of the four destinies, illustrated by "wood containing fire," is here cultivated evil (修惡), cultivation taken as nature, while in principle "a dharma inherently present cannot be changed." This anticipates the doctrine of inherent evil (性惡) developed in the Profound Meaning of Guanyin and the Pointing to the Essentials; the sentence 「不同今此明染中之惡，即脩惡也」 is terse and is translated literally.

"Though one regrets, one can still catch up." The phrase 雖悔可追 is from the Book of Documents ("Song of the Five Sons").

Thirty births, not forty. Zhanran corrects copies reading "forty births" or "forty-one births": the Humane Kings Sūtra divides each of the ten grounds into three grades, so thirty births bring one to wondrous awakening.

"Precisely" (即) and "some" (或). In paragraph 38 Zhanran comments on 「或入即假，或入即中」 in the milk teaching, reading 即假 / 即中 as "precisely the provisional / the middle." The site's Profound Meaning renders the same passage "Some enter equality through the provisional," taking 等 as "equality."

"Having something as a possession" (大有所有). Zhanran reads 大 here as "greater": the reckoning becomes "greater than" what it claims to cut off. The site renders the phrase "having something as a possession," which is kept in the lemma.

"Relative and absolute both wondrous" (持絕俱妙). In paragraph 57 持 is probably an error for 待; translated by the sense.

The seven buddhas' verse. The exchange between Kāśyapa and Ānanda is from the first fascicle of the Ekottara Āgama (T125), condensed.
