The End of the Wondrous of Wisdom and the Wondrous of Practice: Precepts and Concentration

Fascicle eight comments on the end of the Profound Meaning's third fascicle and the first part of its fourth (fascicle-003 and fascicle-004 on this site). It opens by finishing the wondrous of wisdom: the judgment of the remaining five kinds of threefold wisdom, the opening of the coarse, wisdom in relation to the one truth and to "no truth," and the "successive illumination" in which every kind of wisdom lights up every object; without it, Zhanran says, no one can see the Lotus's cardinal doctrine. He then turns to the wondrous of practice. He explains why object, wisdom, and practice are "one considered as three," why Zhiyi says "increasing enumeration" rather than "increasing by one," and he fills in the sources behind the practices of the four teachings, retelling two of Sudhana's visits. The five practices of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra follow. Under the precept holy practice Zhanran rebukes contemporaries who "talk Mahāyāna" while breaking the basic rules, sets out his own matching of the Nirvāṇa's ten precepts with the Great Treatise's ten, and argues that the Tripiṭaka and shared teachings have no separate bodhisattva precepts. The fascicle closes with the concentration holy practice, from the root dhyānas and the six wondrous gates to the "ground of forbearance" and the nine great dhyānas.

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the successive [illumination], again in two: first making clear the purpose of its coming; next, from "conceivable" (思議) on, the successive mutual illumination proper. Since the six objects have been opened and combined in succession, the six wisdoms must, following their opening and combining, also turn with the objects to illuminate them. If wisdom takes its name from the object, then the various wisdoms too are the same in meaning and different in name: because the names differ, each illuminates [its own]; because the meaning is the same, they illuminate in succession. This, then, is the inconceivable object and wisdom: one object is every object, one wisdom is every wisdom. Just as in the conceivable the six realms and the ten suchnesses still conclude by gathering up all the objects of different names — all the objects are so, and all the wisdoms likewise — how much more do the great and the small, the partial and the perfect of the inconceivable include one another! Hence below it concludes that when the truths are fused the wisdom is fused, completing the opening and revealing, the wondrous purport of the Lotus. If one does not see the wondrous meaning from beginning to end, what good is it to go on talking idly? Within this again four: first, the successive illumination proper. Second, from "Kinds of wisdom such as these" on, in meaning corresponding to judging and opening: illuminating in succession is like judging, and both being fused is like opening. Third, from "All such talk as this" on, in meaning corresponding to opening the teaching and seeing principle: when principle is seen, everything is opened. Fourth, briefly treating wisdom on the example of the objects. As for the first passage: it only uses the later wisdoms to illuminate in succession the earlier objects. Since the ten suchnesses stand first, no wisdom is discussed [for them]; but by principle one should also use the wisdom of the ten suchnesses to illuminate the [other] five objects, so that each of the six wisdoms can illuminate the five objects. This can be seen in the text and need not be discussed in detail. If one does not understand this — that the various objects and wisdoms, whether great or small, enter in among one another, and whether judged or opened are each the dharma-realm — how could one recognize what the Lotus takes as its cardinal doctrine? How could one understand the wondrous object of the one mind, in which object and wisdom match each other? How could one see what all the wondrous passages below rely on and issue from, up to the inconceivable function of transforming others? Wondrous appeal and wondrous response, up to the retinue, all arise from this, and even the original realization does not go beyond it. People who do not see it call it tangled overgrowth.

The Wondrous of Practice: Coarse and Wondrous Practice

Increasing Enumeration: The General Approach

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the explanation proper, in two: first stating the purpose of its coming; next, from "But if one were to discuss practice by increasing enumeration in relation to only a single kind of wisdom" on, the explanation proper. In the explanation, first explaining the general approach, then explaining according to the teachings. As for "general approach": it still relies on the wondrous just described to establish a general approach, because it has not yet been judged as belonging to any one teaching. Following each teaching, it separately makes clear its own increasing numbers; hence it says "according to the teachings." This is the same thing as the shared teachings within the various bodies of scripture: the Avataṃsaka has two [teachings] together, up to the Prajñā [sūtras] having three together; and within the various bodies there is moreover hearing together but understanding differently, which by itself forms [the explanation] according to the teachings. Hence there must be these two kinds of explanation. The reason these practices by increasing enumeration are listed is to make known that the wondrous of practice gathers in all practices universally, embracing the practices made clear by every teaching. Practice that depends [on object and wisdom] belongs properly to the distinct teaching; the Tripiṭaka and shared teachings also have this meaning, but their principle is shallow and near, so it is set aside and not discussed. Now it makes clear that for the person of the perfect [teaching], object, wisdom, and practice each possess three, and only so is it called the wondrous of practice. Thus practice possesses the three — complete, deep, exhaustive; understanding arising from principle is called the three of wisdom; and the substance of principle is the three of principle-nature. These three threes do not depend on one another. Again, the three of object, wisdom, and practice are matched with the three virtues, neither vertical nor horizontal.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Why is it not called "increasing by one," as in the Ekottara Āgama, the Vinaya, and so on, but instead "increasing enumeration"?

Chinese Source

答：其義實通。若言增一者，恐濫增法，謂以一法增而加前法。今言增數，但是數增，法體無定，或是一法離開增數，或以異法數增前故亦名增數。通途、約教二義咸然，是故唯著增數之言，總論秖是法界大體離出萬行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: In meaning they are actually interchangeable. If one said "increasing by one," it might be confused with increasing dharmas, that is, taking one dharma and adding it to the preceding dharma. "Increasing enumeration," as said here, is only an increase in number, and the substance of the dharma is not fixed: either one dharma is opened up to increase the number, or a different dharma increases the number of the preceding, and this too is called increasing enumeration. Both the general approach and [the explanation] according to the teachings are so; therefore only the words "increasing enumeration" are used. Spoken of in general, it is only the great substance of the dharma-realm separating out into the myriad practices.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Guard the mind's own practice of non-heedlessness": in the fourth [fascicle] of the Ekottara, the Buddha, at Anāthapiṇḍada's [park], told the monks: "You should cultivate one dharma and broadly spread one dharma; having cultivated it and broadly spread it, you will attain the supernormal powers, all activities will be stilled, and you will attain the fruit of the śramaṇa and reach the fruit of nirvāṇa. What is the one dharma? The practice of non-heedlessness. What is non-heedlessness? Guarding the mind. How does one guard the mind? Here a monk should guard the mind with respect to the tainted and tainted dharmas, and [so] gain delight; the outflow of desire not yet arisen will not arise, and that already arisen will cease; ignorance likewise. Dwelling alone in a quiet place, he becomes aware by himself, and roaming at ease by himself he attains liberation, complete with the four knowledges." "What is to be done" and so on: every arhat possesses the four knowledges, namely, "My births are ended, the pure life has been established, what is to be done has been done, I will not receive further existence." Zhanran places "needle … thread" in fascicle 33, "drives the ox" in fascicle 20, and the sentence about Maitreya and Mañjuśrī sitting among the śrāvakas in fascicle 38. In the CBETA text of T1509 the needle-and-thread simile (如針導綖縫衣) is in fascicle 30, the ox-and-driver simile (牛力雖能挽車，要須御者能有所至) in fascicle 7 (fascicle 20 has a charioteer testing a horse), and the seating passage in fascicle 34. The line "using poison to cure poison" (para. 49, attributed to fascicle 21) was not found verbatim in T1509. The fascicle numbers are translated as written.

The Ekottara Āgama. The "guarding the mind" passage (para. 15) is in T125 fascicle 4 (護心品), as Zhanran says; his quotation is condensed. The calming-and-contemplation sūtra for the forest monk is in fascicle 11 (善知識品), where the Tathāgata is transcribed 多薩阿竭. Zhanran's translator "僧伽婆" is presumably Saṅghadeva (僧伽提婆), who translated the Ekottara in the Long'an era; one character seems to have dropped out.

The Avataṃsaka stories. The fire-ascetic Jayoṣmāyatana (勝熱) is in the eighty-fascicle version (T279, fasc. 64, "Entering the Dharma-Realm"); Zhanran's retelling is condensed. For the "old sūtra" (T278) the text reads 文殊師利教我相黶子法、算數法、印法; Zhanran's 如黶子法門 is probably a slip for 相黶子. Bodhiruci's Treatise on the Nature of the Dharma-Realm (法界性論) is not in the Taishō canon.

The five gates of the Vimalakīrti. The quotations in para. 23 are from Kātyāyana's section of the "Disciples" chapter (T475): impermanence, suffering, emptiness, non-self, quiescent extinction. The double negations ("neither permanent nor impermanent," etc.) are Zhanran's reading, not the sūtra's words. "Also called the five practices" (亦名五行) is not in the CBETA text of the Profound Meaning at this point.

The seven good qualities. "The term 'seven good qualities' applies to both the great and the small" is from the Textual Commentary (T1718, fasc. 3), which reads 夫七善之語乃通大小.

The Vibhāṣā on breath-counting. In T1545 fasc. 26 the five kinds of counting are full, decreasing, increasing, confused (亂數), and pure, and "decreasing" means counting two breaths as one, "increasing" one breath as two. Zhanran's list has "gathered" (聚數) for "confused" and defines decreasing and increasing as "from three to one" and "from one to three." Translated as written.

"Using the many to break the one." In para. 17 Zhanran writes 以多破一名為一相, while the Profound Meaning says the single mark breaks the different marks (以一相破異相). The sentence may be compressed or garbled; it is translated literally.

Ṛṣabha and the twenty-five truths (para. 30). The twenty-five tattvas are normally ascribed to Kapila's Sāṃkhya school, and Ṛṣabha is the founder of the Jains; Zhanran's attribution is translated as written. The "butcher 伽叱" (para. 31) is not identified; the name is transliterated Jiachi.

The rebuke of "Mahāyāna talkers" (para. 26). The list follows the vinaya's categories in order: the great lie and theft (pārājika), matchmaking and building dwellings (saṅghāvaśeṣa), robes and bowls (naiḥsargika), food and medicine, sitting with women in screened or open places (the two aniyata rules), preaching to women without a knowledgeable man present, and lodging or traveling with women. 詎思益方 ("never consider the proper rule") is uncertain.

"Soft" (軟, para. 28). Probably the "soft" (mṛdu) stage of the good roots before the path of seeing, i.e. the resembling stages; the sentence says that the precept "accompanying the path" is named from these stages onward even before the real is brought forth.

The two lists of ten precepts (paras. 32–34). The Profound Meaning matches the Great Treatise's "precept of freedom" (自在戒) with a "precept of freedom" in the Great Sūtra, which has no such item. Zhanran gives his own matching and says "the erroneous text need not be changed." Note that 不缺 in the Treatise (site: "unlapsed") and 不缺 in the Great Sūtra (site: "unbroken") are the same word; Zhanran says they share a name but differ in meaning, and the Chinese is given in brackets.

式又 (para. 37). The CBETA text reads 式又; the Profound Meaning has 式叉 ("discipline," śikṣā), and 式又 is almost certainly a copying error. "Adds the six pārājikas" (加制六夷) for the renunciant bodhisattva is translated as written; the source is not identified.

Cross-references. Para. 1 continues directly from the end of the seventh fascicle, which judged the first two kinds of threefold wisdom (those of the Tripiṭaka and shared buddhas) as unusable. "Will be explained further in the sixth fascicle" (para. 23) refers to the sixth fascicle of the Profound Meaning. "Cultivation and Realization" (修證) refers to the chapter of that name in the Dhyāna Gate (T1916).
