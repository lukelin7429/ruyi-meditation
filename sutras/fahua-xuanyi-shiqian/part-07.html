The Wondrous of Wisdom: The Twenty Wisdoms, the Four Good Roots, and Wisdom in Relation to Its Objects

Fascicle seven comments on the wondrous of wisdom, the second of the ten wondrous aspects of the trace gate (fascicle-003 on this site). Zhanran follows the Profound Meaning's six steps — enumerating the twenty kinds of wisdom, classifying them, characterizing them, showing how they illuminate the objects, judging them coarse and wondrous, and opening them — and then turns to wisdom in relation to each object. His comments on the characterization are the most technical in the work so far: he fills in, from the Vibhāṣā and the Abhidharmakośa, the questions the Profound Meaning answers about the four good roots (warmth, summit, forbearance, and the supreme worldly dharma), explains the thirty-two "rounds" by which forbearance reduces its objects and aspects to a single moment, and corrects copies reading "forty-two." In the second half he comments on the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's four contemplations of the twelve links, insists that "the object must turn" with wondrous wisdom for either to be called wondrous, and defends the Profound Meaning's "twenty-one kinds of provisional and real wisdom" against both a mistaken reading of the Avataṃsaka ("the buddhas do not know the wisdom of the first abiding") and the suggestion that Tiantai should simply follow Nāgārjuna's Middle Treatise. The fascicle breaks off in the middle of the section on wisdom and the three truths.

The Wondrous of Wisdom: Enumeration and Classification

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, making clear the wondrous of wisdom, in two: first making clear the purpose of its coming, then the explanation proper. The first passage can be seen. The explanation proper falls into two by itself. In the first, the text falls into six by itself: first listing the six sections, then explaining according to the sections. In the enumeration: first six of the Tripiṭaka, next four of the shared, next three of the distinct, next the three buddhas, next four of the perfect. The three buddhas are at the fruit; those three fruits have no [real] people, so they are listed separately here. The perfect fruit has people, so it stands by itself after the cause. Next, "classification" (類) means example; again two: first listing, then concluding. The first passage states the meaning of the numbering: why each of the five methods, and so on to the ten grounds, counts as one number — hence this gate is set up. Since by class and example they are the same, for now each counts as one number; if each stage were counted separately, it would be far too complicated, so for now it speaks in general. The Tripiṭaka bodhisattvas should also be said to study all four gates, but to bring out how their own meaning differs, for now they are classed by the strength and weakness of the wisdom regarding phenomena and principle. The three buddhas should be said to be all at the teacher's stage, but to distinguish the three marks according to the teaching, each is made clear by its meaning.

Characterization: Worldly Wisdom

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, distinguishing the marks: going by number and class, they are truly not many; but within a class there are many marks, and from the marks further distinctions of more and fewer are made; hence so. First, in distinguishing the mark of worldly wisdom, "in India … reaches … only up to … neither-perception-nor-non-perception": speaking of what the wisdom depends on, it goes as far as the first dhyāna [and beyond]; here for now it speaks from the highest. "Loyalty and filial devotion" and so on are roughly as in my Record on Calming and Contemplation. For knowing many names of birds, beasts, plants, and trees, nothing surpasses the Erya. "Anointing one side while cutting the other": what this depends on is only worldly dhyāna. "Attaining the fundamental concentrations" and so on: using the supernormal powers necessarily depends on form-realm concentration, so stopping a river and the like must depend on the fundamental [concentrations].

Ru-Yi Translation

"Stopping a river's flow within one's own ear" and so on: as in the thirty-fifth [fascicle] of the Great Sūtra: "The outsiders came and said to King Prasenajit: Great King, you should not slight such a great man. Great King, who makes the moon wax and wane? Who made the salty taste of the great ocean and the Malaya mountains? Was it not we brahmins? Great King, have you not heard that the seer Agastya kept the waters of the Ganges in his ear for twelve years? The seer Gautama performed a great spiritual transformation: for twelve years he changed himself into Śakra's body, made Śakra's body into a ram's body, and put a thousand female organs on Śakra's body. Did not the seer Jahnu drink up the waters of the great ocean in a single day and make the earth dry? Did not the seer Vasu make the third eye for Maheśvara? Did not the seer Rāhula turn the city of Kapila into salt land? Among the brahmins there are seers of great power like these, who can be verified now; how can you slight such great seers?" Likewise among the ancients of this land, Zhang Kai could make fog, Luan Ba was good at spitting out clouds, and Ge Hong, Tao Yuanming, and others all had some slight arts — but these are small indeed; compared with the West, they are as far apart as heaven and earth. "Its dharma is a worldly dharma" and so on: the concentration is not taintless and cannot sever delusion, hence "unmoving"; it is always within the three existences, hence "not departing." Wrong wisdom cannot move delusion or leave the realms.

Ru-Yi Translation

The fourth, sifting, has two: first sifting the dharma, then sifting the grades. The first says, "That giving and upholding the precepts are all directed toward liberation," and so on. The Vibhāṣā says, "People of the West make this discussion"; the present text briefly follows its questions: What is the intent of the good root of warmth? What is its basis? What cause and condition? What dharma? What fruit? What retribution? What benefit? How many aspects does it practice? Is it conditioned arising? Conditioned origination? The wisdom of hearing, of reflection, or of cultivation? Of the desire, form, or formless realm? With initial and sustained thought, down to without them? Associated with which faculty? One mind or many? Regressing or not? And so on — the same questions are asked down to the supreme worldly dharma. The present text omits the words "question" and "answer" and gives the answers directly. The treatise's answers are all as in the present text, down to "the good root of forbearance is wholly dedicated to liberation; this is its intent," and so on; only the answer to "What dharma?" is missing. It should say: it is a tainted dharma; summit is the functional fruit of warmth; the determinate cause of nirvāṇa is its benefit; and some say not severing good roots is its benefit. Again it asks: How many kinds of warmth are there? The answer is as in the text. Again it asks: How many persons are there, from the desire realm to the sphere of nothingness? Answer: each has nine grades, together with the one fully bound person, seventy-three persons in all.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, sifting the grades of the good roots, set against the three lower good roots for sifting. The second master's text seems to have four grades, but the meaning lies only in three. That it takes them skipping order follows the nine grades above, which also skip order. "The meaning lies only in three": lower-lower and lower-middle are just lower, middle and upper are just middle, and upper-upper is just upper. Warmth and summit, among the lower three grades, are lower-lower and lower-middle; though two grades, they are just lower; forbearance takes the top of the middle three grades, and the supreme worldly dharma the top of the upper three. Ghoṣaka says that among the nine grades the upper can include the lower, so summit has six and forbearance eight. The supreme worldly dharma is near the real, so it cannot include many; hence only upper-upper. Since among the nine grades the upper includes the lower, the three grades are likewise; so warmth has one, and so on to forbearance with three, and the supreme worldly dharma also only one kind, the meaning as above. In the fifth, capability, "two kinds of forsaking": "leaving the realm and stage" means rising from lower to higher; "regression" means losing the higher and falling back to the lower; the time of losing what was gained in dhyāna is called "the time of loss."

Here the assessor is followed; hence "of the form realm." These only judge the four good roots: because of the differences of moving and the rest, they are matched to the four differently. From moving down to regressing is divided into two; from not moving down to not regressing is divided into two; though all are in the form realm, because the good roots differ in depth, their grading differs.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Moving" means still being moved by outer conditions; "abiding" means abiding long at warmth and summit; "difficulty" means that warmth and summit have difficulties; "cut off" means severing good roots; "regressing" means falling back to the five heinous deeds and the like; "not moving" and the rest are the latter two good roots, which can be understood by this. Next, in explaining the name: summit, compared with warmth, gets the name "summit" in moving and the rest; and there are other masters who say it should be "lower." Again, above, in moving and the rest it was called abiding; other masters call it not abiding long, or without difficulty, and so on; on reaching forbearance it is no longer called regressing. Third, making clear the contemplation of the dharma of summit, again in two: first explaining, then sifting. The first first makes clear the contemplation that is able to believe; next, from "This faith" on, makes clear its object. Next, from "Question" on, sifting: first the question, then the answer.

From "If the World-Honored One" on, explaining the meaning of faith again: because suffering and its origin cannot be believed, one believes the path and cessation. From "this affliction" on raises in general the features of suffering and origin that cannot give rise to faith. From "Those who are to be converted" on raises again that the path and cessation are believable. "There is also another account": one should believe all four, because by knowing suffering and origin one can believe the path and cessation. The treatise asks why, since one also believes the truths, it speaks only of believing the Jewels: because above the four truths were set up matched with the Three Jewels, this question is asked. The treatise says: "Some say: that young brahmin did not fail to believe the truths of suffering and origin, but did not believe the Three Jewels; because he did not believe, the Buddha taught him" — this is the first passage. "There is also another account": according to the practitioner's delight — this is the text from "It is simply according to" on. Fourth, explaining why this dharma is called regressing: first the question, as in the text. The answer: while practicing at the summit, the afflictions in the body keep giving rise to this thought, fearing that once he reaches forbearance there will be no place left for them to arise, and so they stir up trouble at the practitioner's gate of summit contemplation; so the text has two meanings: first, distress because of regressing through afflictions; second, distress again because of fearing to lose it. From "If one is able" on makes clear the two conditions for not regressing: first, outer — good friends and the like; second, inner — correct contemplation. The rest of the text can be seen.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, explaining the dharma of forbearance: the present text says in general "thirty-two mind-moments." Some copies say "forty-two mind-moments," which is a mistake: the four truths of upper and lower each have sixteen aspects, hence thirty-two. The present text is brief. It discusses only the general mark of reducing the objects all at once; next, reducing one object at a time, down to two aspects and one object remaining, all are called middle forbearance; only when one aspect and one object remain is it called upper forbearance. Translator's notes

The seers' feats. The list of brahmin seers (Agastya holding the Ganges in his ear, Gautama turning Śakra into a ram, and so on) is quoted from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (northern edition, fascicle 35). Zhanran compares them with Chinese adepts: Zhang Kai (張楷, written 張揩) who made fog, Luan Ba who spat clouds, Ge Hong, and "Tao Yuanming" — perhaps a slip for the Daoist Tao Hongjing (陶弘景); translated as written.

"Unmoving … not departing." Zhanran glosses 「定是不動定，慧是不動出」 as concentration that does not move delusion and wisdom that does not leave the three existences. The site's Profound Meaning renders the second phrase "an unmoving departure."

The four good roots. Paragraphs 3–15 summarize, and partly quote, the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā (T1545) and the Abhidharmakośa (T1558, "Worthies and Sages" chapter). The verse lines quoted ("Thus the two good roots are first the dharma, later the four," "Sages forsake them by losing the stage …") are from the Kośa's verses. Zhanran's correction of "forty-two" to "thirty-two" mind-moments follows from the sixteen aspects of the four truths in the upper and lower realms.

Seventy-three persons. Nine grades of attainment at each of eight stages from the desire realm through the sphere of nothingness, plus the one "fully bound" person, give seventy-three.

A garbled sentence. In paragraph 15, 「依欲界身，天六人三，除北洲，前三善根，三洲死生六天亦續生故」 is compressed and probably corrupt; it is translated as literally as possible.

"Breaking the obstruction to penetration." Zhanran glosses 破障通無知 with 通 as "spiritual penetration" (神通), the obstruction being the ignorance of dust-and-sand. The site's Profound Meaning renders it "breaking through the obstruction so as to penetrate what was formerly unknown."

The four contemplations. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra passage on lower, middle, upper, and highest wisdom contemplating the twelve links is from the northern edition, fascicle 25; Zhanran notes that the Vibhāṣā's three grades all count as "lower" wisdom for the Profound Meaning.

"The object must turn." Paragraph 39's argument — that the four kinds of practitioner all begin from the same arising-and-ceasing object, which becomes wondrous only when it "turns" with wondrous wisdom — is one of Zhanran's most characteristic statements on object and wisdom.

The Avataṃsaka on the first abiding. "The buddhas of the three times do not know the wisdom of the first abiding" is a loose quotation of the Avataṃsaka's praise of the first arousal of the mind; the Great Treatise passage on complete and incomplete wisdom of thusness-as-real is from fascicle 86.

Nāgārjuna as "our original doctrine." In paragraphs 49–50 Zhanran explains that Tiantai inherits Nāgārjuna's method of contemplation (the four-line verse) but not the Middle Treatise's manner of refutation; the closing line alludes to Zhiyi gaining dhāraṇī in the Lotus samādhi under Huisi.

無作 again. The site's Profound Meaning uses "uncreated" for 無作 in this fascicle; that word is kept in the quoted lemmas, while Zhanran's own sentences use "without fabrication" (decision 7).
