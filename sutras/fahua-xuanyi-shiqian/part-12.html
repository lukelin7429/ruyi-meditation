The Three Dharmas Judged and Correlated; the Wondrous of Sympathetic Response

Fascicle twelve finishes Zhanran's comments on the wondrous of the three dharmas (the end of fascicle-005 on this site) and then covers the wondrous of sympathetic response (the opening of fascicle-006). On the three dharmas — real nature, contemplative illumination, and resource-and-accomplishment — he follows the Profound Meaning in judging the three provisional teachings coarse and the perfect teaching wondrous, explains why emptiness and the tathāgatagarbha are neither "vertical" nor "horizontal," tells the story of the dream at the Wa-guan Monastery, traces the three dharmas from beginning to end, and correlates them with the ten sets of three (three paths, three consciousnesses, three buddha-natures, and so on). On sympathetic response he explains capacity (機) as subtle, as trigger, and as fitting; capacity as single good, single evil, and good and evil bound together, with the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's story of the drunken elephant tamed by the power of loving-kindness; capacity and response in the three times and the four right efforts; the four propositions and thirty-six phrases of hidden and manifest benefit, illustrated by Sudatta's monastery and Candracchattra's bow; the interpenetration of the ten realms; the matching of distinct and perfect; and the judgment of coarse and wondrous, closing with the parable of the medicinal herbs and the contemplation of mind.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the opening of the second phrase, again three similes reveal the marks of opening. "Determinate and indeterminate": the determinate is conditioned cultivation, reckoning that the determinate can reveal principle; the indeterminate means true cultivation, which can break the determinate reckoning. True cultivation compared with conditioned cultivation also has two meanings. The simile says "as the wheel-turning king can break and pacify": the majesty of the wheel-treasure subduing is like breaking; the ten goods transforming the world is like pacifying. Now likewise: pervading all dharmas is breaking, and there being no name of breaking is pacifying; again, removing darkness is like breaking, and giving life to beings like pacifying. Removing the disease and nourishing the pearl can be seen by this. In the third phrase, next, in the fault, it says "the three, five, and other vehicles": since the second phrase already opened the distinct, one knows that here only the Tripiṭaka and the shared are opened; so "three, five, and the rest" concern only the two teachings. Next, the concluding passage can be understood. So one knows that the vehicles of skillful means all fail to know the beginningless principle of the treasury, the three dharmas of one mind; so each reckons a small portion of one dharma and calls it ultimate. Now the Tathāgata, with skillful means, tames and ripens them in many ways and shows beings their originally present treasury of awakening, so that great and small all know it; covered before and revealed now, this is called opening. Though the words "great and small" in the present text are placed in the first phrase, as in Zhang'an's explanation their meaning extends to the later phrases; that is the intent.

The Three Dharmas from Beginning to End

The text above made clear the beginning and end of the stages: in terms of the ordinary stages, one beginning and end; in terms of the sagely stages, one beginning and end. The reason this gate is set up: though opened and revealed above, lest those who do not understand take opening and arousing as the beginning of the three dharmas, it must be made clear again. Only because the beginning lies in the ordinary can the ordinary stage be opened; if the ordinary had no three dharmas, what opening would there be to discuss? So, without moving the ordinary person's three dharmas, one forms the sage's ultimate three dharmas; for this reason this gate is set up again, urging people not to despise themselves.

Sixth, making clear the correlation, in two: marking, explaining. The explanation has five: first the purpose of its coming; next listing; third, from "the various three dharmas" on, the meaning of the ten items; fourth, the arising in sequence of the ten items; fifth, explaining the ten items properly. The first two: first the matter itself; next, from "Why is this?" on, explaining. One set of three dharmas from beginning to end is called vertical; one set of three dharmas correlated with all the threes is called horizontal; the other threes, compared with the three, each become horizontal. If one says that in meeting conditions the names differ but are one, then every three can by itself be horizontal and vertical. Third, the meaning of the ten items is as the text says. Fourth, from "the three paths" on, again giving the arising of the ten items: compared as different in name but the same in meaning, each reaches the ultimate, and by the perfect principle the essence of principle is no different; by their present names they are not without differences; so the ten items together form a beginning and an end, starting from those to be transformed in extreme delusion and ending with the one who transforms entering extinction. Again, each item is in terms of the six identities, so whether separate or combined, horizontal or vertical, all is included deeply. Fifth, the explanation proper falls into ten items by itself. First, the three paths, two: first the explanation proper, then concluding the stage. In the explanation, first matching in general, then explaining separately. The explanation falls into three; each passage first explains, then cites proof and so on; the nine threes below are largely the same in text and phrasing, and though there are slight differences, the main meaning can be seen. "Wondrous phrase": from "All dharmas from their origin" on makes clear the wondrous of principle; from "among the buddhas of the past" on, the wondrous of hearing the name. In the explanation by matching the three consciousnesses, two: first matching in general, then explaining. The first: the three consciousnesses all lie in the mind of principle; the teaching gates speak provisionally and for now set up far and near. The amala is the ninth: the original principle without defilement, matched with real nature. The ālaya is the eighth, the not-lost ignorance; the nature of ignorance is the nature of wisdom, so it is matched with prajñā. Manas is the seventh, holding the dharmas held by the store-consciousness; this very holding is called resource-and-accomplishment, because it assists the store-consciousness in holding the dharmas. The sixth can only discriminate dharmas, so together with the seventh it is resource-and-accomplishment; therefore the present text does not discuss the sixth. If, according to the Cheng weishi lun, the eight consciousnesses are turned to form the four wisdoms, and the four wisdoms bundled to form the three bodies: turning the eighth forms the great mirror wisdom; turning the seventh forms the wisdom of equality; turning the sixth forms the wondrous observing wisdom; turning the five consciousnesses forms the wisdom that accomplishes what is to be done. The great mirror wisdom forms the Dharma-body, the wisdom of equality the reward-body, the wisdom that accomplishes what is to be done the transformation body, and the wondrous observing wisdom pervades all three bodies; this does not take the ninth, and is one line of matching on the teaching-path, not the same as the present. Why? There, at the stage of the fruit, the three bodies are still distinct; here, at the stage of the cause, the three bodies fuse with one another; these three bodies are just the three virtues — the three virtues spoken of inwardly, the three bodies outwardly; now, from the initial mind one constantly contemplates the three virtues; so the meaning cannot be equated with theirs.

Reading Notes for Fascicle 12

Translator's notes

"Half-word" and "vaipulya." In paragraph 1 Zhanran quotes the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 5, on the elder who teaches his son only the half-word; he warns that 方等 there means the canon of equal principle, not the Vaipulya period that rebukes the two vehicles.

The drunken elephant. Paragraph 23 quotes the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's "Pure Practice" chapter (fascicle 14 in Zhanran's numbering) fairly closely; the five lions "did not really come from my fingers" — it was the power of the good root of loving-kindness.

Bodhiruci's treatise. The simile of quicksilver mixed with gold is attributed to a Treatise on the Nature of the Dharma-Realm (法界性論) by Bodhiruci; this work is not extant in the canon, and the attribution is translated as written.

The five expedients. In paragraph 24, "five expedients" counts the four applications of mindfulness as one, plus the four good roots.

Sudatta and Candracchattra. The story of Sudatta inviting the Buddha to the Jeta grove is quoted from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 27 (northern text); Candracchattra (月蓋) is referred to Zhanran's own Record on fascicle 2 of Calming and Contemplation.

Thirty-six or forty phrases. Paragraph 34 explains why the count is thirty-six and not forty: the four root propositions are counted once, under capacity.

The higher having the lower's capacity. Paragraphs 35–38 answer how the Buddha-realm can have the capacities of the nine realms; the answer turns on one who once aroused the mind for buddhahood and later fell into hell.

Nine times and ten times. The Avataṃsaka's ten kinds of three times (nine plus the one in which they are equal) and the Great Yingluo are cited in paragraph 40.

The great donor. The quotation in paragraph 45 ("not drinking wine … one gives wine") is cited as from the Great Sūtra; the exact source has not been located and is translated as written.

King Wonderful Adornment. The three bodhisattvas who appeared as his wife and two sons are named from the Lotus Sūtra, chapter 27; the reference is to "the tenth fascicle of the commentary" (the Fahua wenju).

The medicinal herbs. Paragraph 47 matches the three herbs and two trees with humans and gods, the two vehicles, and the three bodhisattvas, and notes that the matching changes when they are counted as the seven expedients.

Lemmas. Quoted phrases from the Profound Meaning were matched against the site's fascicle-005 and fascicle-006 where they could be found.
