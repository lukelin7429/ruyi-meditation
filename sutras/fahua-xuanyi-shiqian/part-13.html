The Wondrous of Spiritual Powers, of Teaching the Dharma, and of the Retinue

Fascicle thirteen covers three of the ten wondrous aspects in the Profound Meaning's fascicle-006 on this site: spiritual powers (the seventh), teaching the Dharma (the eighth), and the retinue (the ninth). On spiritual powers Zhanran explains the "Universal Gate" chapter's three wheels of body, speech, and mind; the medicine-tree and wish-fulfilling-jewel bodies and the poison and heavenly drums; the kinds of spiritual power obtained by birth, retribution, and cultivation; the six faculties as powers in the perfect teaching, including why the Lotus alone describes the power of the nose; and the lands manifested by spiritual power, with the four propositions on urgency and laxity of vehicle and precepts and the simile of the mirror and its images. On teaching the Dharma he works through Dharmottara's sevenfold analysis of the twelve divisions of scripture, explains the Kośa's numbers behind the "akṣobhya verses," quotes the Āgamas and the Great Treatise for the stories behind the parables, past events, and birth-stories, and discusses how great and small vehicles possess nine, eleven, or twelve divisions, the two fourfold schemes of teaching, what each teaching expresses, and Kātyāyana's five meanings rebuked by Vimalakīrti. On the retinue he explains the five kinds — principle, karma, vow, spiritual power, and response — the Dharma-body bodhisattvas who play the part of enemies, the Ratnagotravibhāga's parents of the buddha's child, and the seven names of retinue in common and particular.

"It asks: How does Bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara wander in this sahā world?" — this asks about body. "How does he teach the Dharma for beings?" — this asks about speech. "What is the matter of the power of his skillful means?" — this asks about mind. In the answer, each phrase raises only body and speech, as including mind: "Those who should, by the body of a śrāvaka" answers about body; "and teaches the Dharma for them" answers about speech. Since there are body and speech, mind is necessarily included; or the two words "should, by" are mind itself — because he thoroughly understands capacities and conditions, it says "should, by." The next passage again two: first in terms of body and speech showing mind; next, from "Moreover, in transforming others" on, discussing it by more and less. The first again two: dharma and simile. The simile again three: simile, application, and conclusion. The next passage again three: first discussing more and less properly; next citing proof; third distinguishing properly that body and speech are mostly used — because all the sage's teachings mostly have these two marks. Again four: first explaining the two bodies and the two drums; next, from "This is" on, making clear the source of body and speech; third, from "As for showing forth" on, making clear the mind that is included; fourth, from "this, too" on, raising the two practices to explain the two bodies and two drums. In the first passage, "the body of the king of medicine trees" shows a fearsome form that destroys evil, as the Great Sūtra, fascicle 29, the "Bodhisattva" chapter, says: "It is like a medicine tree named the king of trees, the most excellent among all medicines, able to remove all illnesses. The tree does not think, 'If someone takes my branches, leaves, bark, or trunk.' Though it does not think so, it can heal all illnesses. Nirvāṇa is also like this (and so on)." "The body of the king of wish-fulfilling jewels" shows a lovable form that gives rise to good, as the Larger [Prajñā], fascicle 10, says: "Wherever a maṇi jewel abides, no nonhuman can get the advantage; when the jewel is worn on the body, in darkness there is light, in heat there is coolness, in cold there is warmth; if it is in water, it shows colors according to things." "The poison drum": the Great Sūtra says: "It is like someone who smears a great drum with poisons and beats it in a great assembly to make it sound; all who hear it die." This likens teaching the Dharma that destroys evil, as in my Record on the fifth fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. "The heavenly drum": like the wondrous drum of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, which gives forth all kinds of sounds according to the thoughts of the gods; this likens teaching the Dharma that gives rise to good. The second sense: though body and speech appear, all come from the kindness of mind. The third sense: seeing the marks of body and speech, one knows the karma of mind. Here, for now, the Great Sūtra is cited; the sūtra speaks from the side of teaching, and so calls them "speech."

The present text briefly uses "speech" to prove mind, because "according with one's own intent" and the like, as speech, necessarily depend on mind. According with one's own intent is like the jewel and the heavenly drum; according with another's intent is like the medicine tree and the poison drum; the two practices matched with the above can also be seen. Next, making clear the sequence properly, two: first the dharma statement, then the comparison. First a small simile is raised; next, from "if even such small things" on, the small is used to infer the great, namely the wondrous of sympathetic response. "A magpie's cry": 鳱鵲 is a small magpie. The Miscellaneous Records of the Western Capital says: "When the dry-magpie (乾鵲) cries, a traveler arrives; it can also be written 䧲; most present copies write 乾." Next, in names and number, four: listing the number, explaining the names, sequence, and real and empty; the latter two are only distinguished within names and number. The first is as the text says. In explaining the names, though three texts differ, the meaning does not differ. "The Bodhisattvabhūmi says 'Spiritual' means what is difficult to fathom and know": the Book of Changes says "What yin and yang cannot fathom is called spirit"; this is not the Buddhist meaning. "The non-sameness of spiritual power" is fully as the text lists: ordinary people, non-Buddhists, and the four teachings. The three teachings are as the text says. A brief explanation of the six powers is as in my Record on the seventh fascicle of Calming and Contemplation; that text is entirely about powers arising from past habituation. There are also powers obtained at birth, as ghosts and animals know things; powers obtained by retribution, as with the gods; and powers obtained by cultivation, as with the śrāvakas, the non-Buddhists, the Tripiṭaka bodhisattva, and the shared teaching's going out into the provisional. Those obtained by cultivation are again four: the two vehicles of the Tripiṭaka rely on the root dhyānas with a taintless mind; non-Buddhists rely on the root dhyānas with a tainted mind; the two vehicles of the shared teaching, like the shared bodhisattva, also rely on the root dhyānas with a taintless mind; the distinct bodhisattva first relies on the root dhyānas, using a taintless mind, next relies on the samādhis as numerous as the Ganges sands beyond the realms, and finally relies on the true mark, bringing forth wondrous powers; in the perfect teaching, at the first mind of the first abiding, though different, the meaning of bringing them forth is the same. Again, in terms of the six identities: at the purification of the six faculties they are brought forth in resemblance; from the first abiding up, they are brought forth in partial truth; only at the single stage of wondrous awakening are they brought forth ultimately. The present text is about bringing them forth in partial truth; since it is from the perfect stages onward that powers arise to respond and appear, one knows it is properly powers that are brought forth. So the Great Sūtra, fascicle 22, says: "Powers are of two kinds: inner and outer. Outer means shared with non-Buddhists. Inner is again of two: first, the two vehicles; second, bodhisattvas." The spiritual powers obtained by bodhisattvas who cultivate the great parinirvāṇa are not shared with the small; so one knows these are the distinct and perfect bodhisattvas obtaining powers as above. "The fourteen kinds of transformation" mentioned earlier: the first dhyāna has two, namely transformations of the first dhyāna in the first dhyāna, and transformations of the desire realm in the first dhyāna.

The second dhyāna has three, the third four, the fourth five; they can be understood by analogy with the first dhyāna, and are explained in detail in the Graded Explanation of the Dharma-Realm. The perfect teaching's text is somewhat broader because it uses the meaning of the present sūtra, with other texts to help explain it. Within it again two: first the broad explanation, then the sifting. The first passage, two: first giving the differences; next, from "And yet" on, setting out the present sūtra's meaning of taking the six faculties as powers; the other sūtras do not make it clear, while the present sūtra is broadest, so one knows the six faculties should be used as powers. Within it, two: first making clear the meaning; next, from "Here … spoken of now" on, explaining. In the first passage, "And yet the sūtra's own text explains 'the power of the nose' in the greatest detail": no sūtra makes clear the power of the nose, which is not ultimate teaching — how could all the faculties attain spiritual powers, and the nose and tongue alone lack them? Only the present sūtra, on the purification of the six faculties, gives the most detailed text on the nose faculty. When the Avataṃsaka makes clear the ten kinds of six faculties, it too means that all six faculties have spiritual powers; but that sūtra is about the fruit, namely from the first abiding on; one should know that before the abidings there is also bringing forth in resemblance. The Avataṃsaka also makes clear ten kinds of spiritual transformation, namely the ten powers: first, beings' bodies; second, buddha-lands; third, offerings; fourth, sounds; fifth, practice and vows; sixth, taming; seventh, accomplishing; eighth, bodhi; ninth, teaching the Dharma; tenth, sustaining. Next, the explanation, two: first making clear what is relied on; next, from "The text says" on, giving the marks of the powers. First, "the dhyāna of unrecorded transformation upon transformation" is as in my Record on the first fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. Next, in explaining the marks of the powers, two: first, for now, explaining briefly in terms of the eye faculty; next, from "Seeing is" on, judging true and resembling. The first passage, three: first briefly citing this sūtra; next borrowing the Abhidharma's words "simply so of itself." That treatise is in the small, this is in the great; the name "simply so of itself" is the same but the meaning differs; "simply so of itself" is only another name for "naturally." "The Vibhāṣā says the six sense-gates' own excellence": all have the use of powers, so it says "excellence." The name "simply so of itself" extends to both great and small; it is cited for now to prove the great. In the small vehicle, after setting up causes and conditions for all dharmas, they always say "simply so of itself," as green leaves and red flowers are not made so by dyeing, so it says "simply so of itself." Powers are also like this: only by attaining dhyāna, powers are simply so of themselves; though deliberate, it is the power of the dhyānas simply so of themselves. If even the small vehicle is so, how much more the great vehicle? So here the small is borrowed to prove the great. Third, citing the Aṅgulimālīya, fully as in my Record on the seventh fascicle of Calming and Contemplation and the seventh text. Next, in judging true and resembling, the meaning of the Avataṃsaka is borrowed to distinguish them. "Bodily spiritual power manifested inwardly and outwardly": this is the mirror of suchness, pure, illuminating spontaneously; because it can illuminate outward things, it shows the images of the ten realms, so outer images appear within. Because it has these two functions, inner and outer are divided; in terms of its real substance, inner and outer are not two. Next, in the sifting, "the merit differs in increase and decrease" of the three faculties: this cites the Great Treatise, fascicle 40. "The nose, tongue, and body, three": it only cites that treatise as saying there is increase and decrease; if matched with the six faculties, it differs from the present. The meaning is: since the treatise allows increase and decrease, why should it not be that in the present sūtra the three, eye, nose, and body, are lesser in number than the other three? Therefore it cites the treatise as an example for the present sūtra, so it says "Following this same sense"; the rest is as the commentary says. Within it, three: first the example of difference; next citing the Zheng fahua as proof, and so on; third, again citing the present sūtra to prove interchangeable use.
