The Lotus Illustration Concluded; the Common Name "Sūtra"; Clarifying the Essence

Fascicle sixteen spans the end of fascicle-007 and the first part of fascicle-008 on this site. Zhanran first finishes the "correct explanation" of the lotus flower: why the Lotus uses the lotus rather than the udumbara, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's eight kinds of illustration, the partial and the extensive illustration (with the Great Treatise's lion and the Nirvāṇa's pārijāta tree quoted at length), and the lotus seed as an image of each of the ten suchnesses. He then comments on the common name "sūtra": the five untranslatable senses (dharma-source, subtle arising, gushing spring, marking-line, binding a garland), the translatable senses, the reconciliation of the two camps, "sūtra" traced through the six sense-objects — above all the single stroke of black ink — and "sūtra" in the contemplation of mind. Finally he begins the second of the five profound meanings, clarifying the essence: the refutation of earlier views that the essence is the one vehicle, the real truth, or the cause and fruit of the vehicle; the three dharma-seals and the one seal of the real mark; the house and its empty space; and the long sifting of the false — ordinary worldly arts, Confucian and Daoist teachings, non-Buddhist debaters, the small vehicle, and the one-sided teachings — down to the one real truth.

Ru-Yi Translation

Yet the sūtra's text speaks of the udumbara flower in two places: "like the udumbara flower, which appears only once in a while," and "just as all love and delight in the udumbara flower"; here it says only "appearing only once in a great while," concluding for now from one text. Explanations ancient and modern say: this resembles the lotus flower, so it is used as an illustration. Therefore one should properly use the lotus flower, because in the lotus flower there is a complete illustration. Third, making clear the meaning of using illustrations, two: first relating the eight kinds of illustration discussed generally in the Nirvāṇa; next making clear that the partial illustrations of sūtras and treatises are not ultimate. "The eight illustrations of the Nirvāṇa": sūtras and treatises take illustrations differently, partial and pervasive; as the Great Sūtra says, illustrations are of eight kinds, namely the illustration in order, the illustration in reverse, the present illustration, the illustration of the impossible, the prior illustration, the posterior illustration, the prior-and-posterior illustration, and the pervasive illustration. Partial illustration is fully as the sūtra says; so the fifth [fascicle] says: "A face handsome as the full moon; a white elephant spotless as the snow mountains." The full moon cannot be the same as the face, nor can the snow mountains be the white elephant; true liberation cannot be illustrated by illustration, but for the sake of beings this illustration is made. Here it is also so: the wondrous dharma cannot be illustrated by illustration, but for the sake of beings the lotus flower is used as illustration.

Ru-Yi Translation

The first again two: first concluding with the ten suchnesses, then applying by example to the other objects and other wondrous aspects. Next, the origin text can be seen. Next, from "Beginning and end" on, the conclusion: the trace gate takes establishing as the beginning and opening as the end; the origin gate takes letting down [the trace] as the beginning and brushing it away as the end; each takes beginning as opening and end as combining, and when combining is complete it is called "perfectly complete." Next, from "this is what is meant" on, the general conclusion can be seen. Next, the extensive illustration is the complete illustration. That it does not say "complete" but only "extensive": though this flower illustrates the ten meanings of each of origin and trace fully, yet cause and fruit, self and other, long and near, timeliness, and so on to the wondrous essence of the true mark, taking the essence as the wondrous cardinal doctrine, the function that belongs to the essence, and the marks of sifting essence, cardinal doctrine, and function — how could this flower illustrate these? The principle is sufficient but the phenomena are lacking, so it says "extensive." Within it again two: first stating the meaning; next, the broad explanation. The first passage briefly states the marks of pervasive illustration in the two texts: the Dharma-gate of the lion illustrates pervasively the great function of the Tathāgata, the one of fruit; next, the Dharma-gate of the pārijāta tree illustrates pervasively the practitioner from cause to fruit. The present lotus flower alone can illustrate both preceding meanings: in terms of the origin gate, the boundless great function of the one of fruit, never resting section after section, never ceasing time after time; in terms of the trace gate, it likens the practitioner from cause to fruit, tamed and ripened flavor after flavor, entering the perfect stage after stage. Within it two: first citing the two similes; next, from "Here, too" on, concluding the meaning to show they are the same. The sameness is as just said and can be grasped. "The Dharma-gate of the lion's roar": the Great Treatise, fascicle 23, first lists the kinds of lions, their bodily marks and roar, and then applies it, saying: "The Tathāgata as lion is also like this. He is born from the pure lineage of the six pāramitās and the four noble truths and dwells in the great mountain valley of quiescent cessation; the wisdom knowing all modes is his head; gathering all good is his cheeks; taintless right view is his long, wide eyes; concentration and wisdom equal in radiance are his gait; [the rest] his high, broad brows; the four fearlessnesses his white, sharp fangs; the four right efforts his firm, full jaws; the thirty-seven factors his even, close teeth; the contemplation of impurity his red tongue thrust out; mindfulness and wisdom his high ears; the eighteen exclusive qualities his glossy mane; the three gates of liberation the firm flesh of his body; the three kinds of display his long, level back; knowledge and conduct complete his belly not showing; patience his slender waist; detachment his long tail; the four bases of spiritual power his firm stance; the four immeasurables his stretching and yawning; unobstructed liberation his mouth, subduing the non-Buddhists and those of wrong views, awakening beings from the sleep of the four truths," and so on. "The Great Sūtra's pārijāta tree": in the pervasive illustration of the twenty-seventh [fascicle] of the sūtra, from the yellowing of the leaves to the opening of the blossoms, it pervasively illustrates the Buddha's disciples from first going forth to attaining the fruit. The sūtra says: "In the Heaven of the Thirty-Three there is a pārijāta tree, whose roots enter the earth five yojanas deep and whose branches and leaves spread fifty yojanas around. When the leaves are ripe they turn yellow. The gods, seeing this, rejoice: these leaves will surely fall before long. When the leaves have fallen they rejoice again: these branches will surely change color before long. When the branches have changed color they rejoice again: this color will surely form buds before long. Seeing this they rejoice again: these buds will surely form beaks before long. Seeing this they rejoice again: these beaks will surely open before long. When they open, their fragrance spreads fifty yojanas around and their light shines eighty yojanas far; then, for the three summer months, the gods enjoy themselves beneath it.

Next, the Sūtra of the Head-Adornment of Flowers is cited to prove beginning and end. Next, in explaining suchness of cause, the application of the simile matches each illustration; the text of the application is complete. Next, the sūtra cited only proves that the preceding true cause is like the flower dwelling in open space. Next, in likening the twelve links of dependent origination, it should also be explained generally, so it says "all as explained generally above"; now, briefly, the general follows the specific, pointing to the above eight characters as ignorance. From "using the lotus flower to illustrate the Four Noble Truths" on, the marks of the text are only general; examining in detail, they can be seen. The two udumbara passages. Paragraph 3 quotes two Lotus verses (「如優曇鉢華時一現耳」 and 「譬如優曇華一切皆愛樂」); they are translated directly, since the site's Profound Meaning gives only a paraphrase.

The Great Treatise's lion. The long quotation in paragraph 8 is close to fascicle 25 of the Great Treatise (Zhanran says 23); "[the rest]" marks a phrase that appears to be missing before 「高廣眉」.

The pārijāta tree. Paragraphs 8–9 quote the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (northern text, fascicle 27 in Zhanran's numbering) closely. "White-four karman" (白四羯磨) is the formal ordination procedure.

Square and round. In paragraph 11 Zhanran's 「乘其句勢」 is read as "following the momentum of the phrase": the Profound Meaning applies "neither square nor round" only because it follows the rhythm of the simile.

Cang Jie's terrace. The Record of Spiritual Responses (感通傳) quoted in paragraph 12 is Daoxuan's work; the legend linking the terrace to Kāśyapa Buddha is translated as written.

The Avataṃsaka quotations. The passages in paragraph 15 come from the sixty-fascicle Avataṃsaka; the fascicle numbers are Zhanran's.

The names of Śakra and of the five aggregates. The lists in paragraph 16 follow the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 31 (Zhanran's numbering); the Sanskrit equivalents of Śakra's names are the usual ones.

Dao'an's "five losses." Paragraph 19 refers to Dao'an's famous "five losses and three things not easy" in translation; the simile of chewed food is attributed to Kumārajīva in other sources but is translated as written.

Brahmā script. "Eleven dots added to the base sound to form twelve letters" (paragraph 20) refers to the vowel signs of Indian script; the count is Zhanran's.

The smell of the classics. The anecdote of the Tripiṭaka master who smelled blood in the Spring and Autumn Annals (paragraph 22) is "handed down"; no written source has been located.

The hundred liberations. "The fifth [fascicle] of the Great Sūtra, among the hundred [phrases on] liberation" (paragraph 27) refers to the long list of epithets of liberation in that fascicle.

Zhang'an's judgment. Paragraph 33 attributes the "private" judgment in the Profound Meaning to Zhang'an (Guanding), not Zhiyi.

The three dharma-seals. Paragraphs 34–40 quote the Great Treatise, fascicle 22 (Zhanran says 20), at length.

The five ranks. The glosses in paragraph 46 are standard Han-dynasty puns (公＝正, 侯＝候, 伯＝長, 子＝慈, 男＝任); the English renders the puns by meaning.

Zhang Ling and Ji Kang. Paragraph 52 dismisses the stories that Zhang Ling (founder of the Celestial Masters) and Ji Kang became immortals; Zhanran's reasons are translated as written.

Lunli and the Licchavis. In the Great Treatise story (paragraphs 54–55), 梨昌 / 離昌 are the Licchavis of Vaiśālī, who hired the debater.
