The Four Siddhāntas and the Refutation of Guangzhai

The third fascicle of the Explanatory Notes comments on the last part of the Profound Meaning's first fascicle (site page fascicle-001), from "Next, the four siddhāntas are explained under ten headings" to the end of the twenty-four objections against Guangzhai. Zhanran first shows the plan of the ten headings: seven that hold for all the sūtras and three that belong to the Lotus alone. He then walks through each heading — the name, the marks and Zhiyi's fifteen formulations, the sūtra terms that confirm the treatise's, the matching with the truths, how the siddhāntas give rise to contemplation, teaching, and treatises, speaking and silence, attaining and using, the provisional and the real, opening, and the sūtra's own text — filling in sources (the Bodhisattva-bhūmi, the Mahāsaṃnipāta, the Vibhāṣā, the Nirvāṇa) and settling small textual points. In the opening section he attacks those who rank the Lotus below the Avataṃsaka as a "gradual" teaching. On the start of the detailed exposition of the Name, he develops the pair "within a single stage" (當分) and "spanning across stages" (跨節), links it to the relative and absolute senses of "wondrous," and then glosses the earlier masters and the refutation of Guangzhai line by line, ending where the refutation of "spiritual power" begins.

The Four Siddhāntas: The Ten Headings

Chinese Source

天台沙門湛然述

Ru-Yi Translation

Composed by Zhanran, a monk of Tiantai.

Chinese Source

次解四悉中列章別釋，初標列十章通亘一代，於中為二：前七章通約諸經，後之三章獨今經意。前七又四：初二正明悉檀，次二名義同異，次二所起功能，次一自證與利他得失。初二者，前總名、次別相，次二名義同異中，初一名有同異，義一向同，次一名一向，異而義同。次二初凡夫起觀，聖人起教，次唯聖人說默，次一凡聖得失。次後三中初二明今經意，後一明今經文，於前二中初一判權實，次一開權實。得此意已，方知一代悉檀不同。雖前七門義及今經妙未獨顯，別明當部故有後三。思之思之！

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, in explaining the four siddhāntas, the headings are listed and then explained one by one. First, the ten headings are marked and listed; they extend across the whole of the Buddha's lifetime of teaching. They fall into two groups: the first seven headings deal with all the sūtras in general, and the last three concern the meaning of the present sūtra alone. The first seven again fall into four: the first two directly clarify the siddhāntas; the next two concern sameness and difference of name and meaning; the next two concern the power and effect of what the siddhāntas give rise to; and the next one concerns gain and loss in one's own realization and in benefiting others. Of the first two, the first gives the general name and the next the specific marks. Of the next two, on sameness and difference of name and meaning, in the first the names are partly the same and partly different, while the meaning is entirely the same; in the next the names are entirely different, but the meaning is the same. Of the next two, in the first ordinary beings give rise to contemplation and sages give rise to teaching; in the next, only sages speak and keep silent. The next one concerns the gain and loss of ordinary beings and sages. Of the last three, the first two clarify the meaning of the present sūtra, and the last clarifies the text of the present sūtra; of those first two, the first judges the provisional and the real, and the next opens the provisional and the real. Only when one has grasped this sense does one know how the siddhāntas differ throughout the Buddha's lifetime of teaching. Although the meaning of the first seven headings reaches the present sūtra, its wondrousness is not yet singled out; it is to clarify this scripture separately that the last three are added. Think on it, think on it!

Explaining the Name "Siddhānta"

Ru-Yi Translation

The first heading, explaining the name, has three parts: first, it cites the old masters in general; second, it cites the treatise to refute them; third, it cites Nanyue's correct explanation. The first is as in the text. In the second, it first cites the Bodhisattva-bhūmi to prove the error, and then directly refutes the error. First, on its citing the four udānas of the Bodhisattva-bhūmi: the seventeenth chapter of that work, on the factors of awakening, says: "All the other things that are practiced and learned enter the gates of the three samādhis. That is to say, among what śrāvakas practice and learn there are four udāna dharmas, and buddhas and bodhisattvas also teach these four for the sake of sentient beings: that all conditioned things are suffering is an udāna dharma, and so on up to: that nirvāṇa is quiescent extinction is an udāna dharma. Buddhas and bodhisattvas possess these dharmas in full and further pass these dharmas on to sentient beings; this is called udāna. The silent sages of the past handed them down one after another; this is called udāna. Surpassing, welling forth, and so on up to completely going beyond the peak of existence; this is called udāna." The treatise's text has no word "sūtra," but its title is given as Dichi jing, so here it is cited as "the sūtra." As for the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's calling these four "seals": this differs from the dharma-seals explained in the Great Treatise, cited below, where the Great Vehicle has one seal and the Lesser Vehicle three. Although this passage of the Bodhisattva-bhūmi is Mahāyāna, it wishes to clarify the dharmas it includes, and for the sake of beginner bodhisattvas it has them cultivate these four for the time being. The four are really just three; setting up the seal of suffering in addition makes four. Two seals seal birth and death, and one seal seals nirvāṇa; for birth and death, impermanence is the first seal and no-self the last. They are also called "doctrine." Hence we know that these are entirely different, in name and in meaning, from the four siddhāntas. From "'Seal' means" on, it explains the meaning of "seal"; the two passages, on teaching and on practice, differ. Teaching belongs to worldly wisdom; hence "What worldly wisdom teaches."

Answer: The teaching is the dharma that is transmitted; hence it says that buddhas and bodhisattvas "possess this dharma." Practice is the work of transmitting; hence it says "passing down through the silent, quiescent sages of the past" — it is simply that buddha after buddha all have the practice of transforming others and transmitting the teaching. "Surpassing" and so on: these all praise and explain the meaning of "doctrine." As for "the peak of existence" (第一有, literally "the first existence"): it means the summit of the three realms. This existence is hard to escape, hence "first"; and it sits at the very summit, hence again "first." Next, from "Yet that passage is explaining, clearly and definitively" on, it directly refutes.

Differentiating Their Marks

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, differentiating their marks, has two parts: first, it cites the great master's explanation; next, its own explanation. In the first passage, the four siddhāntas form four sections of their own. The first has two parts: first explanation, then resolution of doubts. In the first, "the mundane" means the distinctions of the five aggregates; the elements and sense-fields are likewise. In the second, from "Yet if there were no person at all" on is a question; from "'Person,' then, exists only in terms of the mundane" on is the answer; from "only ultimate meaning can be real" on is a question; and from "Answer: Each is real in its own way" on is the answer. From "Those outside the Buddha's teaching" on, it explains on that occasion. Outsiders make perverse suppositions: they do not call it the mundane and do not penetrate its distinctions, but simply suppose a self in general. "Without any cause" means [they say it comes about] spontaneously; "false cause" means Brahmā and the like. This text on the four siddhāntas comes entirely from the Great Treatise. The individual siddhānta has three parts: marking, explaining, and concluding. The explanation has two: first, the direct explanation; next, from "For instance, because of mixed karma" on, it sets up the marks of secondary and principal to resolve a doubt. In the first, where it says "one person may accept the teaching while another does not" (或聽不聽): 聽 means "permitting." Although both are raised, the intent lies in permitting; permitting is what is fitting, and what is fitting gives rise to good. Next, resolving the doubt: it is feared that someone would cite this to raise a doubt: "In a passage on giving rise to good, why is there also breaking evil?" Within this, it first presents the situation and then directly judges it. In the first, where it says "For instance, because of mixed karma" and so on: the text cites two sūtras, saying: "As a sūtra says, because of mixed karma one is born mixed in the world," and further there is the Sūtra to Bhagunna.

Question: How are these two sūtras to be reconciled?

Chinese Source

答：有人不信罪福墮於斷見，斷彼疑故說雜業等。若計有神我墮於常見，如破群那來問佛言誰受？若佛說言某受。更增其邪，是故不說受者觸者，但說無觸無受。此之二人雖有此疑，機在生善。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: Some people do not believe in sin and merit and fall into the annihilationist view; to cut off their doubt, [the Buddha] taught "mixed karma" and so on. Some suppose there is a spiritual self and fall into the eternalist view — as when Bhagunna came and asked the Buddha, "Who experiences?" Had the Buddha said, "So-and-so experiences," it would have further increased his error. Therefore he did not speak of one who experiences or one who contacts, but only said that there is no [one who] contacts and no [one who] experiences. Though these two people had these doubts, their capacity lay in giving rise to good.

Hence within the individual siddhānta there is no lack of breaking evil; there are only secondary and principal. Third, the therapeutic siddhānta likewise has marking, explaining, and concluding. The explanation has two parts: first, it distinguishes existence and nonexistence; next, from "As an antidote" on, it directly explains the mark. In the first, where it says "a dharma taught as an antidote exists as such an antidote" and so on: there is what treats and what is treated, hence it is called "existing." In ultimate meaning there is neither agent nor object, hence it says "does not exist." Here "inherent nature" is merely brought in as a contrast, to show that the antidote has agent and object. Next, in the conclusion, where it says "he teaches these dharma-medicines" and so on: speaking generally, none of the four siddhāntas is anything but dharma-medicine. Here it speaks in the specific sense: since it is convenient to set up the name from the side of the antidote, it speaks this way for the time being.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the siddhānta of ultimate meaning likewise has marking, explaining, and concluding. The explanation has two parts: first, it sets up the difference between two kinds; next, from "What cannot be spoken" on, it explains the marks of the two kinds. In the first, where it says "this has two kinds": the intent is that the ultimate meaning spoken of in the present text belongs, from the side of the teaching, to what can be spoken; what cannot be spoken belongs to the side of inner realization and is not the intent of the present text — hence the difference must be distinguished. In first explaining what cannot be spoken, it first directly presents the mark and then cites the treatise as proof. In the first, the realized dharma has nothing to rest on and must be distinguished by way of persons; hence it says "the true dharma attained by all buddhas, pratyekabuddhas, and arhats." The verse it cites is likewise cited in the treatise's passage on ultimate meaning. Its first two lines clarify the power of the realized dharma, for it can leave behind verbal expression and deluded thought. Its next two lines clarify the essence of the realized dharma: the upper line directly clarifies the essence of the dharma, and the next line brings in an example by way of comparison — just as the unarising, unceasing dharma of nirvāṇa attained by the buddhas of the past is within my own body. The verse from "Where activity is spoken of" on distinguishes [ultimate meaning] in contrast with the mundane; earlier, in clarifying the mundane, [the treatise] also distinguished it in contrast with ultimate meaning, so here it does the same. Next, what can be spoken directly clarifies the present intent: all four propositions are real, hence none of them is anything but ultimate meaning. From "The Buddha, in sūtra after sūtra of this kind" on, it cites proof.

Where all of the above are "named" and so on, this is entering the real. From "Hence the Middle Treatise says" on, it proves the intent of entering the real: it is for those of sharp faculties. "A swift horse … at the very sight of the shadow of the whip" is as in my Record on the second fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. The Fifteen Formulations

Chinese Source

十五番者，又二：初正釋，次「問」下料簡。初文者，總十五番，謂一事理，二假實，三善惡人，四善惡陰，五善惡法，六三世，七內外凡，八見脩聖，九凡及聖，非學非無學即凡位也。十至十三四悉各為一番，十四以四悉通為一番，十五別約四諦。此十五番為欲令人了四悉義遍一切法，若得此意觸境皆成自行化他法皆具足，然須細釋此十五番，皆使順於歡喜生善破惡入真。又此十五番雖趣舉一法，不無次第。於中為四：初之六番約所觀人法，次內外凡下三番約能觀凡聖，三五番約所施方法，四一番約所觀諦，非所觀法無以成能觀人，非能觀人無以用所施法，非上三故不成於諦，諦是所觀，義通能所人法迷悟故也。今略出相狀，不可具記。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fifteen formulations again have two parts: first, the direct explanation; next, from "Question" on, the resolution of doubts. In the first, there are fifteen formulations in all: first, phenomena and principle; second, the provisional and the real; third, good and evil persons; fourth, good and evil aggregates; fifth, good and evil dharmas; sixth, the three time-periods; seventh, ordinary practitioners within and outside the path; eighth, the sages of vision and of cultivation; ninth, ordinary beings and sages — "neither in training nor beyond training" is the ordinary rank. The tenth through the thirteenth each take one of the four siddhāntas as a formulation; the fourteenth takes the four siddhāntas in general as one formulation; the fifteenth deals specifically with the Four Noble Truths. These fifteen formulations aim to make people understand that the meaning of the four siddhāntas pervades all dharmas. If one grasps this sense, every object one touches becomes [an occasion for them], and the dharmas of one's own practice and of transforming others are all complete. But these fifteen formulations must be explained in detail, so that every one of them accords with gladness, giving rise to good, breaking evil, and entering the real. Further, although these fifteen formulations raise one dharma at a time as it comes, they are not without an order. They fall into four: the first six concern the persons and dharmas contemplated; the next three, from the ordinary practitioners within and outside on, concern the ordinary beings and sages who contemplate; third, five formulations concern the methods applied; fourth, one formulation concerns the truths contemplated. Without the dharmas contemplated, there is no way to form persons who contemplate; without persons who contemplate, there is no way to use the methods applied; without the above three, the truths are not formed. The truths are what is contemplated, and their meaning extends to subject and object, persons and dharmas, delusion and awakening. Here I briefly present their general features; they cannot be recorded in full.

Ru-Yi Translation

First, phenomena and principle: these names extend to dharmas of delusion and awakening, cause and fruit, and so on. Here, for now, it is within delusion: for the deluded it teaches that within phenomena there is principle, yet principle differs from phenomena. To hear this dharma simply and have gladness arise in the mind belongs to the mundane; to hear it and be able to give rise to good, break evil, and enter the real belongs to the latter three. Since the provisional and the real differ, hearing it and again being glad belongs to the mundane. "Wheel" and so on are similes for the real dharmas; "chariot" is a simile for the provisional name; the five aggregates and the person together illustrate that there is a person through combination. To speak from the side of the person is the individual, since the treatise says, "At times it is said there is a person; at times it is said there is no person," and the person is able to give rise to good. To speak of the real dharmas alone: real dharmas are empty and illusory and can be treated by contemplation. From "Negating both the provisional and the real together" on, it clarifies ultimate meaning; since the treatise says, "The path of speech is cut off," it says that negating both is ultimate meaning. Thus for each siddhānta it cites the treatise as proof. This pairing of the provisional and the real makes one formulation. The next deals solely with the provisional person, and the next solely with the real dharmas. From the aggregates of the outer ordinary practitioner arise the aggregates of the inner ordinary practitioner; with the aggregates of the inner and outer ordinary practitioners one breaks the aggregates of the three realms; the taintless means the first fruit and so on. Although the aggregates differ as inner and outer and so on, the aggregates are in the end the object contemplated.

Ru-Yi Translation

The sense of the answer: evil is what is broken, not what [the teaching] is for; hence the name "person" is not set up for it. The person is able to give rise to good — not that there are no evil persons. The next question: if giving rise to good lies only in the individual siddhānta, why does it make people break evil? Guangzhai and Baozhi. The stories of Fayun's rain-making lecture and the commentary found in the water are told in the Fozu tongji (T2035) and elsewhere. 「志公尚云嚙蚤」 is translated "Master Zhi said they were mere flea-bites"; the exact sense of this remark is uncertain.

Cross-references. The "wondrous of the origin fruit," where the four kinds of bodies are explained, is in the seventh fascicle of the Profound Meaning. Zhanran's repeated "my Record on fascicle N of Calming and Contemplation" refers to his Zhiguan fuxing (T1912).
