The Wondrous of Stage Concluded; the Three Dharmas by the Four Teachings

Fascicle eleven finishes Zhanran's comments on the wondrous of stage and begins the wondrous of the three tracks, all within fascicle-005 on this site. It picks up the "most real stage" at its third heading, severing and subduing, and follows the Profound Meaning through function, the coarse and the wondrous, the rise of the stages, their abolition, the opening of the coarse stages, the five similes of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, and the beginning and end of the wondrous stage. Zhanran's comments are mostly structural outlines, filled in with sources: he quotes the Nirvāṇa Sūtra on the physical eye, on "cause and cause of cause," on the crooked and the straight river, and on the mother who feeds her child too much butter; the Great Treatise's tree "Excellent and Firm"; and Bodhiruci on the Buddha's age when he taught the Lotus. He works through the sixteen four-phrase cases of abolishing and not abolishing, and glosses the parable of the minister from a small country. In the three tracks he explains the order of the seven headings and the role of Zhang'an's "In my own view." He also explains why the Tripiṭaka and distinct teachings take wisdom as the vehicle's substance while the shared and perfect take real nature. The fascicle ends inside the three tracks of the perfect teaching.

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called "both cause and the cause of a cause" and so on: the Great Sūtra, fascicle twenty-five, the "Lion's Roar" chapter, says, "Buddha-nature is both cause and cause of cause, both fruit and fruit of fruit." First, the twelve links are taken as the cause; the cause of cause is called wisdom; the fruit is anuttara bodhi; the fruit of fruit is the great parinirvāṇa. So one knows that the sūtra's meaning takes the twelve links of conditioning as the three causes of principle-nature; hence they are called cause. The wisdom that contemplates the conditions, measured against the fruit, is cause; a cause arising upon a cause, hence "cause of cause." Bodhi, measured against the cause, is called fruit; upon the fruit of bodhi, nirvāṇa is added and called fruit of fruit. Next, it uses the twelve links as a simile: just as ignorance is cause and formations fruit, formations cause and consciousness fruit; for this reason the substance of ignorance is both cause and cause of cause, both fruit and fruit of fruit. Ignorance measured against formations is called cause; measured against consciousness, cause of cause; measured against a past cause, fruit; measured against the cause of that past cause, fruit of fruit. Here it can be spoken of from both dharma and simile; hence "both cause and the cause of a cause" and so on. Still, it is in terms of the path of realization, so it runs from the initial abiding to wondrous enlightenment. So equal enlightenment is only cause and not cause of cause; it is only fruit when measured against the ten grounds alone; measured against the lower grounds it is also called fruit of fruit; hence "From this point downward." Thus the initial abiding is only cause and not fruit, wondrous enlightenment is fruit and not cause, and the stages in between receive each other's names.
