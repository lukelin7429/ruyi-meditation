Judging the Gates; the True Mark as the Essence of All; Clarifying the Cardinal Doctrine and the Function

Fascicle eighteen covers the end of fascicle-009 and the first part of fascicle-010 on this site. Zhanran first finishes the "gates that enter the true mark": judging the gates coarse and wondrous by agent and object, the "single narrow gate" of the Lotus read four ways, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's simile of the kālakā and tinduka fruits (quoted in full), the dispute between the northern and southern Dilun schools and the Mahāyānasaṃgraha masters, and the opening of the coarse gates. He then comments on the true mark as the essence of every sūtra, every practice, and every dharma. The second half takes up the third and fourth profound meanings. On the cardinal doctrine he explains why it is neither identical with nor different from the essence, refutes a dozen earlier views (Huiyuan, Long, Huiguan, Yin, Guangzhai, the Dilun masters, and others), and comments on the cause and fruit of trace and origin, the four propositions of gradual and perfect, and the four of worthy and sage. On the function he explains "power and function," tells the story of Cao Cao and Yang Xiu at the stele of Cao E, comments on the ten functions of the trace and the ten of the origin, and matches them with the four siddhāntas.

Ru-Yi Translation

◎Third, making clear the judgment, has two of its own: listing the sections is finished; next, explaining. The first section, two: first distinguishing coarse and wondrous in agent and object; next, resolving doubts by question and answer. The first passage, two: first setting up agent and object briefly; next distinguishing by two kinds of four propositions, first by the teachings, then by the flavors. The first four propositions: listing and explaining. If speaking in terms of principle, there is one coarse and one wondrous; the teachings that express the principle are skillful or clumsy, so that the distinct teaching, though it leads through to the wondrous principle, is still coarse. The next four propositions are all judged by the flavors, judging the coarse and wondrous of agent and object within those divisions. "A case of neither accompanied by coarseness — this is the milk teaching" is said at first glance in order to complete the four propositions; so in this proposition it only says "without the agent and object of the real truth." There is still the coarseness of the distinct teaching's agent that leads through. "A case where the object alone is accompanied by coarseness while the agent is not": for now it says that the prajñā is originally the gate of fusing; the two vehicles lodge in it to enter the real truth; though there is the coarseness of the object led through, the mere real, there is no Tripiṭaka agent that expresses the real and leads through to it; this too is said by the broad division of the period; if according to what the two vehicles see within their own division, it is not without the coarse teaching that expresses the empty-as-such. "A case where the agent alone is accompanied by coarseness" and so on: the agent that leads through comes from the shared teaching, so it is called coarse; what is led through is the middle way of the distinct and perfect, so it is called wondrous. "Likewise every gate discussed within the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra": in the Nirvāṇa division the gates of the first three teachings that lead through are called coarse; none of them stays in the real or in the teaching-path, and all enter the three virtues; so what is led through is wondrous.

In each teaching it makes three pairs of four and one, and judges the meaning of the gates. "Three" means the marks of the common and particular in teaching, practice, and principle, fully as the common and particular of each division at the beginning of the second fascicle; this is its meaning. All are called particular because of the four, and common because of the one. Now this common and particular is used to judge "narrow and small": in the two teachings, Tripiṭaka and distinct, teaching, practice, and principle are all narrow; in the two teachings, shared and perfect, teaching and practice are not narrow, but teaching and practice find it hard to reach principle, so principle is called narrow. Therefore when the sūtra says "a single gate," the one is the gate of the four, so it says "a single gate."

Translator's notes

The kālakā and tinduka fruits. Paragraphs 8–10 quote the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (fascicle 6 in Zhanran's numbering) closely; Zhanran turns the simile of monks into a simile of the ten modes of contemplation.

The northern and southern Dilun paths. Paragraph 11 gives Zhanran's account of the two Dilun lineages of Xiangzhou (the northern taking the ālaya, the southern suchness, as the support) and of the old (Paramārtha) and new (Xuanzang) translations of the Mahāyānasaṃgraha; it is translated as written.

Jātiśroṇa. The debate in paragraph 12 is from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's "Kauṇḍinya" chapter; 闍提首那 is rendered by its usual Sanskrit form.

The light and the throat. In paragraph 30 Zhanran discusses the variant 蹊 / 系 in the Profound Meaning's opening sentence on the cardinal doctrine; he prefers 蹊, as the site does.

Sadāprarudita. Paragraph 39 refers to the Larger Prajñā story of Sadāprarudita and Dharmodgata; the phrase is not in the site's Profound Meaning English and is translated directly.
