Total Inclusion, by Cause

Fascicle Three opens with the treatise's longest single chapter, the fifth mark: total inclusion. This page covers its first half — buddha-nature's causal aspect, likened to the ocean gathering treasure, virtue, and compassion into a single body of water; its resultant aspect, likened to the sun's own disc, light, and brightness; and the eight dharmas, from the inconceivable to freedom from desire, that uphold what the treatise calls the "transformed basis" (轉依), its term for the dharmakāya considered as the endpoint of a long process of purification.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 13 of 22 complete Source: Taishō T1610, 佛性論 (attributed to Vasubandhu, tr. Paramārtha)

Inclusion by Cause: The Ocean

Chinese Source

復次總攝義應知。攝有二種：一者由因、二者由果。由因攝者，是如來性清淨。有四種因、三種法與三譬相似，故取海為喻。三法者，一法身清淨因、二佛智德生因、三佛恩德因。法身清淨因者，修習信樂大乘應知。佛智德生因者，修習般若及禪定應知。恩德因者，修習菩薩大悲應知。修習信樂大乘者，與器相似，此中有無量定慧大寶所遍滿故，故說與器相似。修習般若禪定與寶相似者，般若無分別故；禪定不可思惟，功德所依止故。般若如淨寶，禪定如如意寶。修習菩薩大悲如清淨水，於一切世間眾生潤滑一味故，譬如大海唯一醎味。菩薩大悲潤諸眾生亦復如是，故此三法於因地中為所依能依，故說總攝名如來法海。是名因攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the meaning of total inclusion should be understood. Inclusion is of two kinds: by cause, and by fruit. As to inclusion by cause: this concerns the purity of the Tathāgata's nature. Among the four causes, three dharmas correspond to three similes, and so the ocean is taken as an analogy. The three dharmas are: the cause of the dharmakāya's purity, the cause from which the Buddha's wisdom and virtue arise, and the cause of the Buddha's grace and virtue. The cause of the dharmakāya's purity should be understood as the cultivation of faith and delight in the Mahāyāna. The cause from which the Buddha's wisdom and virtue arise should be understood as the cultivation of prajñā and meditative concentration. The cause of grace and virtue should be understood as the cultivation of bodhisattva great compassion. Cultivating faith and delight in the Mahāyāna resembles the vessel that is the ocean itself — because it is filled throughout with the immeasurable great treasures of concentration and wisdom, it is said to resemble the vessel. Cultivating prajñā and meditative concentration resembles the treasures held within: prajñā, being non-discriminating; meditative concentration, being inconceivable and the basis on which all virtue depends. Prajñā is like a pure jewel; meditative concentration is like a wish-fulfilling jewel. Cultivating bodhisattva great compassion is like pure water — smoothing and moistening all sentient beings throughout the world with a single taste, just as the great ocean has but a single, salty taste. Bodhisattva great compassion, moistening all sentient beings, is exactly the same. It is because these three dharmas, at the causal stage, together form the relation of what is supported and what supports, that this total inclusion is called the Tathāgata's ocean of Dharma. This is called inclusion by cause.

Inclusion by Fruit: The Sun

Chinese Source

次由果攝者，明如來法身有三種法，與三譬相似故，能總攝三譬。相似者，如日有三：一體、二光、三明。此與三身相似故。三法者：一神通、二流滅、三顯淨。一神通者，譬日有明，能除障自境界無明之暗，以為事用故，與日明相似。二流滅者，謂盡、無生智能燒除業煩惱令無餘，以為事用故，與日光相似。所言滅者，即是真智，正能除惑故。與滅名盡者，即惑無時名為解脫，故與盡稱。三顯淨者，謂盡、無生境名轉依，極清淨故、無垢故、澄靜故，與日輪相似。清淨者，解脫障滅故。無垢者，一切智障滅故。澄靜者，客塵所不能染，以本性清淨故。轉依者，勝聲聞、獨覺、菩薩三人所依止法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, as to inclusion by fruit: this shows that the Tathāgata's dharmakāya has three dharmas that resemble three similes, and are thereby able to be totally included within them. The resemblance is this: as the sun has three aspects — its own body, its light, and its brightness — these correspond to the three bodies of a buddha. The three dharmas are: supernatural power, the extinguishing of outflow, and manifest purity. As to the first, supernatural power: this is comparable to the sun's brightness, able to dispel the darkness of ignorance that obstructs its own domain, and, by way of this function, resembles the sun's brightness. As to the second, the extinguishing of outflow: this refers to the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising, able to burn away karmic affliction without remainder, and, by way of this function, resembles the sun's light. As for "extinguishing": this is precisely true wisdom, which is what actually removes delusion — and it is precisely when delusion is no longer present at all that this is called liberation, and so it is designated "exhaustion." As to the third, manifest purity: this refers to how the object of the wisdom of exhaustion and non-arising is called "the transformed basis" — being utterly pure, being without defilement, being clear and still — and thereby resembles the sun's own disc. "Pure" means that the obstruction to liberation has been extinguished. "Without defilement" means that the obstruction to omniscience has been extinguished. "Clear and still" means that it cannot be stained by adventitious defilement, on account of its inherent purity. "The transformed basis" means that it is the dharma on which śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas — all three kinds of persons — depend, each surpassing the last.

Four Correspondences

Chinese Source

又有四種相應知：一者為生依、二滅依、三善熟思量果、四法界清淨相。一生依者，佛無分別道相續依止，若不緣此法，無分別道即不得生，以依緣此故，名此法為道生依。二滅依者，一切諸惑及習氣究竟滅不生，無所依止故。若不依此轉依法究竟滅惑者，則聲聞獨覺與佛滅惑不異。由不同故，故知此法為究竟滅惑依止。三善熟思量果者，善正通達長時恭敬無間無餘等修習所知真如，是轉依果。若在道中，轉依為因；若在道後，即名為果。若轉依非是善熟思量果者，則諸佛自性應更熟思量、更滅、更淨。而不然者，故知轉依為善熟思量之果。四法界清淨相者，一切妄想於中滅盡故。此法界過思量、過言說所顯現故，故以法界清淨為相。此即心行處滅、言語道斷，不可詮詺，方是得無所得真如理故。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, four correspondences that should be understood: as the basis for arising, as the basis for cessation, as the fruit of well-ripened contemplation, and as the mark of the dharma-realm's purity. As to the first, the basis for arising: this is the continuous support of the buddhas' non-discriminating path — if one did not take this dharma as condition, the non-discriminating path could not arise at all. As to the second, the basis for cessation: this means the utter and final extinction, without further arising, of every affliction and its habitual residue. If one did not depend on this dharma of the transformed basis for the utter extinction of affliction, then the way śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas extinguish affliction would be no different from the way a buddha does — since they are, in fact, different, we know that this dharma is the basis for the utter extinction of affliction. As to the third, the fruit of well-ripened contemplation: this refers to the fully correct penetration of suchness as known through long, reverent, uninterrupted, and complete cultivation. While still on the path, the transformed basis serves as cause; once beyond the path, it is called the fruit. If the transformed basis were not the fruit of well-ripened contemplation, then the buddhas' own nature would have to be further ripened, further extinguished, and further purified — but since this is not so, we know that the transformed basis is the fruit of well-ripened contemplation. As to the fourth, the mark of the dharma-realm's purity: this is because every delusive thought is, within it, utterly extinguished. This dharma-realm is revealed as beyond contemplation and beyond speech altogether, and so purity of the dharma-realm is taken as its mark — this is precisely where the workings of mind cease and the path of language is cut off, where nothing at all can be designated, and this alone is what it means to attain the principle of suchness, a principle attained by attaining nothing at all.

Eight Dharmas: Freedom from Desire

Chinese Source

復次如來轉依有八種法攝持應知。八法者，一不可思量、二無二、三無分別、四清淨、五照了因、六對治、七離欲、八離欲因。此八合有二意：一離欲，是滅諦；二離欲因，即是道諦。前不可思量等三句屬滅諦攝，次清淨等三句屬道諦攝。

初離欲有三句：一者云何不可思量於有無等四句，覺觀思量不能通達故、一切眾生言語名句味等不能詮辯故、唯聖人無分別智所證知故，故名不可思量。二無二者，如經中說：舍利弗！諸佛法身無二法、無分別法。所言二者，煩惱及業是名為二。如來法身無此二法，故名無二。三無分別，分別者，煩惱業家習不正思惟，由不正思惟故起二。由通達自性故滅二，是二及分別不應不行。不應者，上心煩惱不相應共行；不行者，隨眠煩惱不共隨行。既不應不行此二處故，故說如來法身苦滅究竟永無生起。云何如此？非為除滅一法故名為滅，以本來不生故名為滅。如《無上依經》中說：阿難！於無生無滅法中，心意及識決定不生故。

釋曰：心者即六識心，意者阿陀那識，識者阿梨耶識，於此三中不得生故。此中若無三識則無分別，分別既無亦無不正思惟等。既無三識則不得起無明，是以如來法身離不正思惟故則不起無明。若不起無明，十二有分不為生緣，故名不生。

又《勝鬘經》說：是苦滅者，非滅壞法名為苦滅。壞者，破三界見諦，得有餘涅槃。滅者，除四種生死思惟煩惱，得無餘涅槃，故言滅壞。由苦滅名無始時節非作非生、無滅離盡、常住恒寂、湛然自性清淨、解脫一切煩惱㲉、功德過恒沙數，相攝非相離，不捨離智、不可思惟，與如來法相應。如來法身諸佛所說，是如來法身，說名離欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, it should be understood that the Tathāgata's transformed basis is upheld by eight dharmas: being inconceivable, being without duality, being without discrimination, being pure, being the cause of illumination, being the antidote, being freedom from desire, and being the cause of freedom from desire. These eight, together, carry two overall senses: freedom from desire corresponds to the truth of cessation, and the cause of freedom from desire corresponds to the truth of the path. The first three belong to the truth of cessation; the next three belong to the truth of the path.

As to freedom from desire, there are three phrases. First, why is it called "inconceivable"? Because it cannot be penetrated by any of the four alternatives of existence, non-existence, and the rest, through discursive reflection; because it cannot be captured through the words, names, and phrases of any sentient being's speech; and because it can only be realized by the sages' own non-discriminating wisdom. Second, as to "without duality": as the sūtra says, "Śāriputra! The dharmakāya of all buddhas has no dual dharma, no discriminated dharma." What is meant by "duality" here is affliction and karma. The Tathāgata's dharmakāya has neither of these — hence it is called without duality. Third, "without discrimination": discrimination refers to the habitual, incorrect thinking born of affliction and karma, from which duality arises. By penetrating one's own nature, this duality is extinguished, and both this duality and this discrimination are made neither to correspond nor to operate — "not to correspond" meaning that the afflictions arising in active thought no longer co-arise with it, "not to operate" meaning that the latent, dormant afflictions no longer accompany it either. Since neither of these operates or corresponds any longer, the Tathāgata's dharmakāya's cessation of suffering is utterly final, with no further arising at all. How is this so? It is not that one dharma is eliminated that it is called "cessation" — it is because it was, from the very start, never arisen at all. As the Anūnatvāpūrṇatvanirdeśa Sūtra says: "Ānanda! Within the unborn, unceasing dharma, mind, mentation, and consciousness decisively do not arise."

Explanation: "mind" refers to the mind of the six consciousnesses; "mentation" refers to the manas-consciousness; "consciousness" refers to the ālaya-consciousness — none of these three arise within the dharmakāya. If the three consciousnesses do not exist, there is no discrimination; and once discrimination is absent, there is likewise no incorrect thinking. And since the three consciousnesses do not exist, ignorance cannot arise either — this is why the Tathāgata's dharmakāya, being free from incorrect thinking, does not give rise to ignorance. If ignorance does not arise, the twelve links of dependent origination have no condition for arising — hence it is called "unborn."

Further, the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra says: "This cessation of suffering is not the cessation of some destructible dharma. 'Destruction' refers to breaking through the view-level afflictions within the three realms, attaining nirvāṇa with residue. 'Cessation' refers to removing the four kinds of afflictions of contemplative thought pertaining to saṃsāra, attaining nirvāṇa without residue — hence it is spoken of as 'cessation' and 'destruction' together." By virtue of this cessation of suffering, it is called: without beginning or temporal marker, neither made nor arisen, without cessation and free of exhaustion, permanently abiding and eternally still, limpidly, inherently pure, liberated from the shell of every affliction, its virtues surpassing the sands of the Ganges, mutually inclusive rather than mutually separate, never departing from wisdom, inconceivable, and in accord with the Tathāgata's own dharma. This — the Tathāgata's dharmakāya, spoken of by all buddhas — is what is called freedom from desire.

Eight Dharmas: The Cause of Freedom from Desire, and Two Cultivations

Chinese Source

二離欲因者，為得此法身，見諦道、修道所攝。由境界故，說無分別智有三義與日相似：無流清淨故，與日輪相似；能照了一切境界故，與日明相似；能對治一切真見暗障故，與日光相似。

釋曰：真見暗障者，謂具足想、煩惱難、業難、果報難。具足想者，以隨眠煩惱為因、五塵欲為緣、不正思惟為俱起因，具此三故，名為具足想。是暗障如實不見不知離欲法身一界故，即得生起如此應見應知如來法身離欲。云何見知？謂如實思量不見想及不見境。境者名分別性、想名依他，不見分別、依他二性故，名為真實見知一界。又想者人，境者法，不見此人法想境，故名二空。如是一切諸法，如來悉見悉知，由平等平等已通達如真實故。境智等無增減，是名平等觀。此觀能除真實見暗障，是如來法身至得家因，見修二道所攝故。

以是義故，此離欲因不離二修而得成就。二修者，一如理修、二如量修。故世間所知唯有二種：一人、二法。若能通達此二空者，則為永得應如實際，是故名為如理。如量際者，窮源達性究法界源，故名為際。如理修者，不壞人法。何以故？如此人法，本來妙極寂靜為性故，無增無減、離有離無。寂靜相者，自性清淨、諸惑本來無生，見此二空，名寂靜相。自性清淨心名為道諦。惑本無生、淨心不執名為滅諦。是心有自性清淨及有煩惱惑障，如此兩法無流界中善心惡心獨自行故、於一念中。兩心不相應故，此兩法難可通達。如《勝鬘經》說：世尊！善心念念滅不住，諸惑不能染；惡心念念滅，諸惑亦不染。世尊！煩惱不觸心、心不觸煩惱。云何無觸法而能得染心？如此而知，名如理智。

如量智者，究竟窮知一切境，名如量智。若見一切眾生乖如境智則成生死，若扶從境智則得涅槃。一切如來法以是義故名為如量。至初地菩薩得此二智，以通達遍滿法界理故，生死、涅槃二法俱知。又此兩智是自證智見，由自得解不從他得，但自得證知、不令他知故，名自證知見。又此二智有二種相：一者無著、二者無礙。言無著者，見眾生界自性清淨，名為無著，是如理智相。無礙者，能通達觀無量無邊界故，是名無礙，是如量智相。又此二智有二義，如理智為因、如量智為果。言如理為因者，能作生死及涅槃因。如量為果者，由此理故，知於如來真俗等法具足成就。又如理智者是清淨因，如量智者是圓滿因。清淨因者，由如理智三惑滅盡。圓滿因者，由如量智三德圓滿故。前不可思量、無二、無分別等三，名為離欲；以清淨、照了、對治等三，名離欲因。是名如來轉依攝持八種功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

As to the second, the cause of freedom from desire: this refers to what is comprehended by the path of insight and the path of cultivation, by which this dharmakāya is attained. In terms of its object, non-discriminating wisdom has three aspects that resemble the sun: because it is outflow-free and pure, it resembles the sun's own disc; because it is able to illuminate every object, it resembles the sun's brightness; because it is able to counteract every obstruction that darkens true seeing, it resembles the sun's light.

Explanation: the obstructions that darken true seeing are the fully-formed conception, the difficulty of affliction, the difficulty of karma, and the difficulty of karmic retribution. The fully-formed conception has latent affliction as its cause, the desires of the five sense-objects as its condition, and incorrect thinking as its co-arising cause. This obstruction prevents one from truly seeing and knowing the single realm of the dharmakāya free from desire — and it is precisely by removing it that one comes to see and know the Tathāgata's dharmakāya's freedom from desire. How does one see and know it? Through thinking that truly accords with reality, not seeing conception and not seeing object — "object" referring to the imagined nature, and "conception" to the dependent nature — and by not seeing these two natures, one is said to truly see and know this single realm. Furthermore, "conception" corresponds to person, and "object" to dharma; and by not seeing this person, dharma, conception, or object, this is called the twofold emptiness. It is by seeing and knowing every dharma in this way, through utter and complete equanimity, that one penetrates suchness in its very reality — object and wisdom being equally without increase or decrease. This is called the contemplation of equanimity, able to remove the obstruction that darkens true seeing, and is the cause belonging to the household of what is fully attained by the Tathāgata's dharmakāya, being comprehended by both the path of insight and the path of cultivation.

For this reason, this cause of freedom from desire is accomplished only inseparably from two forms of cultivation: cultivation in accordance with principle, and cultivation in accordance with measure. All that is known in the world is of only two kinds: person and dharma. If one is able to penetrate this twofold emptiness, one permanently attains the true limit as it really is — hence this is called "in accordance with principle." As for the limit reached "in accordance with measure": this means exhausting the source and reaching the very nature, plumbing the origin of the dharma-realm to its limit. Cultivation in accordance with principle does not destroy person or dharma — because this person and dharma are, by nature, from the very start, supremely subtle and still, without increase or decrease, apart from both existence and non-existence. This inherently pure mind is called the truth of the path; that afflictions never truly arise, and the pure mind grasps at nothing, is called the truth of cessation. This mind possesses both inherent purity and the obstruction of afflictive delusion — these two dharmas, within the outflow-free realm, operating as a wholesome mind and an unwholesome mind each entirely on their own, within a single moment of thought, the two minds never corresponding to one another — and for this reason these two dharmas are difficult to penetrate. As the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra says: "World-Honored One! The wholesome mind perishes moment by moment and does not abide, and yet no affliction can stain it; the unwholesome mind likewise perishes moment by moment, and yet no affliction stains it either. World-Honored One! Affliction does not touch the mind, and the mind does not touch affliction. How, then, without any contact at all, can the mind come to be stained?" To know this is called wisdom in accordance with principle.

Wisdom in accordance with measure means fully and exhaustively knowing every object. If one sees all sentient beings as departing from the object and the wisdom that accords with reality, this constitutes saṃsāra; if one follows and supports it, this attains nirvāṇa. From the first ground onward, bodhisattvas attain these two wisdoms, and, by penetrating the principle that pervades the whole dharma-realm, know both saṃsāra and nirvāṇa together. These two wisdoms, moreover, are the insight of self-realization: understood entirely by oneself, not received from another. They have two further aspects: non-attachment — to see the realm of sentient beings as inherently pure, the aspect of wisdom in accordance with principle — and unobstructedness — to be able to penetrate and contemplate the immeasurable, boundless realm, the aspect of wisdom in accordance with measure. And they have two further senses: wisdom in accordance with principle serves as cause, able to serve as the cause of both saṃsāra and nirvāṇa; wisdom in accordance with measure serves as fruit, knowing, on this basis, the Tathāgata's ultimate and conventional dharmas as fully accomplished. Wisdom in accordance with principle is the cause of purification, since through it the three afflictions are extinguished; wisdom in accordance with measure is the cause of fulfillment, since through it the three virtues are fulfilled. The former three — inconceivable, without duality, without discrimination — are called freedom from desire; the latter three — purity, illumination, and the antidote — are called the cause of freedom from desire. This is called the eight virtues by which the Tathāgata's transformed basis is upheld.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The two cultivations named here — "in accordance with principle" (如理修) and "in accordance with measure" (如量修) — map onto a distinction worth holding in mind for the rest of the treatise: knowing that all things are empty of a fixed self or dharma, and knowing all things in their full, particular extent. A practitioner could mistake the first for the whole of the path — settle into emptiness and stop there — which is exactly the "gate of liberation through emptiness" attachment the treatise flagged as a danger back in the cause mark chapter. The pairing here rules that out structurally: wisdom in accordance with principle is named the cause of purification, but wisdom in accordance with measure is named the cause of fulfillment, and the treatise is explicit that the transformed basis needs both. Realizing emptiness clears the ground; it is knowing the boundless particular realm of beings and dharmas, in all its extent, that the treatise credits with completing the Tathāgata's actual dharma.
