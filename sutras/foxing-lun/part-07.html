The Three Causes and Three Natures

Fascicle Two opens the treatise's "Part Revealing the Essence" (顯體分) — its direct, positive account of what buddha-nature actually is, now that Fascicle One has cleared away every wrong view about it. This page covers two chapters: the three causes that make buddhahood possible for any sentient being, and a systematic, ten-point analysis of the three natures — imagined, dependent, and perfected — the basic Yogācāra framework the rest of the treatise will keep returning to.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 7 of 22 complete Source: Taishō T1610, 佛性論 (attributed to Vasubandhu, tr. Paramārtha)

The Three Causes

Chinese Source

復次佛性體有三種，三性所攝義應知。三種者，所謂三因、三種佛性。三因者，一應得因、二加行因、三圓滿因。應得因者，二空所現真如，由此空故，應得菩提心及加行等，乃至道後法身，故稱應得。加行因者，謂菩提心，由此心故，能得三十七品十地十波羅蜜助道之法，乃至道後法身，是名加行因。圓滿因者，即是加行，由加行故得因圓滿及果圓滿。因圓滿者，謂福慧行。果圓滿者，謂智斷恩德。此三因，前一則以無為如理為體，後二則以有為願行為體。三種佛性者，應得因中具有三性：一住自性性、二引出性、三至得性。記曰：住自性者，謂道前凡夫位。引出性者，從發心以上，窮有學聖位。至得性者，無學聖位。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the essence of buddha-nature is of three kinds, comprehended within the three natures — this should be understood. The three kinds are: the three causes, and the three phases of buddha-nature. The three causes are: first, the cause of what is to be attained; second, the cause of practice; third, the cause of fulfillment. The cause of what is to be attained is the suchness manifested by the twofold emptiness — it is on account of this emptiness that bodhicitta and its subsequent practice, up through the dharmakāya attained at the end of the path, come to be attained; hence it is called "the cause of what is to be attained." The cause of practice is bodhicitta itself — it is by means of this mind that the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, the ten grounds, the ten pāramitās, and the other practices that assist the path are attained, up through the dharmakāya attained at the end of the path; this is called "the cause of practice." The cause of fulfillment is precisely this same practice — it is by means of practice that the fulfillment of the cause and the fulfillment of the fruit are both attained. The fulfillment of the cause refers to the conduct of merit and wisdom; the fulfillment of the fruit refers to the virtues of wisdom, severance, and grace. Of these three causes, the first has the unconditioned, true principle as its essence, while the latter two have the conditioned vow and practice as their essence. As for the three phases of buddha-nature: within the cause of what is to be attained, three natures are present — first, the nature abiding in itself; second, the nature drawn forth; third, the nature fully attained. A note explains: the nature abiding in itself refers to the stage of the ordinary person, prior to the path. The nature drawn forth refers to the range from the arising of bodhicitta up through the entirety of the stages still in training. The nature fully attained refers to the stage beyond training.

The Three Natures: Naming, Manifestation, and Inclusion

Chinese Source

復次三性所攝者，所謂三無性及三自性。三無性者，一無相性、二無生性、三無真性。此三性攝如來性盡。何以故？以此三性通為體故。無相性者，一切諸法但名言所顯，自性無相貌故，名無相性。無生性者，一切諸法由因緣生故，不由自能生、自他並不成就故，名無生性。無真性者，一切諸法離真相故、無更別有實性可得故，名無真實性。復次三種性者，一分別、二依他、三真實。別有十種義應知。何等為十？一分別名、二緣成、三攝持、四體相、五應知、六因事說、七依境、八通達、九若無等、十依止。一分別名者，為隨名言假說故，立分別性。若無此名言則分別性不成，故知此性但是名言所顯實無體相，是名分別性。依他性者，是十二因緣所顯道理，為分別性作依止故，故立依他性。真實性者，一切諸法真如、聖人無分別智境，為清淨二性、為解脫三，或為引出一切諸德故，立真實性，是名分別名。

二緣成者，問曰：分別性緣何因故而得顯現？答曰：由緣相名相應故得顯現。問曰：依他性緣何因故得成耶？答曰：緣執分別性故得顯現。問曰：真實性緣何因得成？答曰：由分別、依他二性極無所有故得顯現。故名緣成。

三攝持者，性有三種，法有五分。言三性者，所謂分別、依他、真實。五法者，一相、二名、三分別思惟、四聖智、五如如。前三是世間智，聖智是出世智，如如是無為境。為明此五法攝前三性故。問曰：於五法中，幾法攝第一性？答曰：五法並不可攝。何以故？為無體故。問曰：第二性幾法能攝？答曰：有四法攝。問曰：第三性幾法能攝？答曰：唯如如一法能攝。問曰：若依他性為聖智所攝者，云何說依他性緣分別性得成？答曰：依他有二種，一染濁依他、二清淨依他。染濁依他，緣分別得成；清淨依他，緣如如得成故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, as for what is comprehended within the three natures — this refers to the three non-natures and the three self-natures. The three non-natures are: first, the nature of no characteristic; second, the nature of no arising; third, the nature of no reality. These three natures comprehend the whole of the Tathāgata's nature, because they together serve as its essence. The nature of no characteristic: all dharmas are revealed only through names and words, and their self-nature has no characteristic mark of its own. The nature of no arising: all dharmas arise dependent on causes and conditions, and are not able to arise from themselves, since arising from self and from another can neither one be established. The nature of no reality: all dharmas are apart from any true characteristic, and there is no further, separate real nature to be found in them.

Next, as for the three natures in the more familiar sense: first, the imagined; second, the dependent; third, the perfected. Ten distinct topics concerning them should be understood: first, their naming; second, their manner of manifestation; third, their inclusion within five categories; fourth, their essence and characteristics; fifth, whether they should be known; sixth, their role in teachings given for a purpose; seventh, the cognition to which each belongs; eighth, penetration; ninth, the consequences of their absence; tenth, their basis. First, as to naming: the imagined nature is established on account of provisional designation through names and words — if there were no such names and words, it could not be established, so we know this nature is revealed only through names and words, and truly has no essence of its own. The dependent nature is the principle revealed by the twelve links of dependent origination, established as the basis on which the imagined nature depends. The perfected nature is the suchness of all dharmas, the object of the sages' non-discriminating wisdom, established in order to purify the other two natures, to bring about the three kinds of liberation, and to draw forth every virtue.

Second, as to their manner of manifestation: Question — "On what condition does the imagined nature become manifest?" Answer — "It becomes manifest by depending on the correspondence of characteristic and name." Question — "On what condition is the dependent nature established?" Answer — "It becomes manifest by depending on attachment to the imagined nature." Question — "On what condition is the perfected nature established?" Answer — "It becomes manifest precisely because the imagined and dependent natures are, in the final analysis, utterly without existence."

Third, as to their inclusion: there are three natures, and there are five categories of dharma — characteristic, name, discriminating thought, sagely wisdom, and suchness. The first three belong to mundane cognition, sagely wisdom belongs to supramundane cognition, and suchness is the unconditioned object. Question: "Among the five categories, how many comprehend the first nature?" Answer: "None of the five, because it has no real essence." Question: "How many comprehend the second nature?" Answer: "Four categories comprehend it." Question: "How many comprehend the third?" Answer: "Suchness alone." Question: "If the dependent nature is comprehended by sagely wisdom, how can it also be said to depend on the imagined nature?" Answer: "The dependent nature is of two kinds — the defiled dependent nature, established by depending on imagination, and the pure dependent nature, established by depending on suchness."

Essence, True Characteristics, and Whether They Should Be Known

Chinese Source

四體相者，有二：一通、二別。通者，由此三性通能成就一切諸餘真諦，或二三四七諦等法，故諸真諦不出三性，是以三性為諸真諦通體。二別體者，於三性中各有實義。何者實義？一者分別性體恒無所有，而此義於分別性中非不為實。何以故？名言無倒故。二者依他性體有而不實，由亂識根境故是有，以非真如故不實。何以故？因緣義無倒故。是以對分別性故名為有，對後真性故非實有，是名有不真實。三者真實性體，有無皆真，如如之體非有非無故。

問曰：是三性實相云何？答曰：分別性實相者，人法增益及損減，由解此性故，此執不生。是分別相人法者，是分別所作。若依真諦觀，此人法為有，名增益執。若依俗諦觀，此人法是無，名損減執。若通達此分別性，則增益、減損二執不生，是名分別實性相。

復次依他實性相者，能執所執增益及損減，由解此性故，故此執不生，是名依他性相。此能執所執，若見真為有，則是增益，名為常見。若見俗定無，則是損減，名為斷見。若通此二性，斷常二執並不得生，是名依他實性相。唯有似塵識故則無能所，無能所故無增益執，由有似塵識故無損減執。復次真實性相者，有無及增益損減執，由解此性故，執不得生。所以者何？若執空為有，名增益謗。若執空為無，名損減謗。若通達此性，則二執不生，是名真實性相。

五應知等者，問曰，是三性幾應知？幾不應知？答曰：一切應知。何以故？由知三性，能通達三解脫門，能除三障故。知分別性，能通達空解脫門，能除肉煩惱。知依他性，通達無願解脫門，能除皮煩惱。知真實性，能通達無相解脫門，能除心煩惱。又初解脫障，次禪定障，後一切智障故。問曰：三性中，幾性不可滅？幾性可滅耶？答曰：二性不可滅，一性可得滅。何以故？分別性本來是無，故不可滅。真實性本來是真，故不可滅。依他性雖有不真實，是故可滅。以是義故，說應知等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, as to their essence and characteristics: there are two aspects, the general and the particular. As to the general: these three natures, taken together, are able to establish every other truth spoken of elsewhere — the two, three, four, or seven kinds of truth, and so on — so no system of truths falls outside the three natures. As to the particular essence: within each of the three natures there is its own real point. First, the imagined nature's essence is, in every case, utterly without existence — yet this very fact is, within its own domain, not itself unreal, since there is no error in the mere designation by names and words as such. Second, the dependent nature's essence exists, yet is not ultimately real — it exists because it arises from confused consciousness meeting sense-faculty and sense-object, yet it is not real because it is not identical with suchness, there being no error in the principle of dependent arising as such. Set against the imagined nature, it is called "existent"; but set against the true nature that follows it, it is not truly existent — hence "existent but not truly real." Third, the perfected nature's essence is true whether spoken of as existent or as non-existent, since the essence of suchness is neither existent nor non-existent.

Question: "What is the true characteristic of each of the three natures?" Answer: "The true characteristic of the imagined nature concerns the attachments of superimposition and denial with respect to person and dharma — by understanding this nature, these attachments do not arise. This 'person and dharma' is precisely what imagination itself constructs. Viewed from the ultimate, to take this person and dharma to exist is superimposition; viewed from the conventional, to take them as non-existent is denial. To penetrate this nature is for neither attachment to arise — this is the true characteristic of the imagined nature."

Next, the true characteristic of the dependent nature concerns the attachments of superimposition and denial with respect to the grasping subject and the grasped object. Viewed from the ultimate as truly existing, this is superimposition, the view of permanence; viewed from the conventional as definitely non-existent, this is denial, the view of annihilation. Penetrating both natures, neither view arises. Since there is only consciousness appearing as though there were sense-objects, there is, in truth, no ultimately real subject or object, and so no superimposition; yet since consciousness does appear as though there were sense-objects, there is no denial either. Next, the true characteristic of the perfected nature concerns the attachments of existence and non-existence, of superimposition and denial. To attach to emptiness as though it were a positive existent is the slander of superimposition; to attach to it as mere non-existence is the slander of denial. To penetrate this nature is for neither attachment to arise.

Fifth, as to whether they should be known: Question — "How many of the three natures should be known?" Answer — "All three, because knowing them lets one penetrate the three gates of liberation and remove three obstructions. Knowing the imagined nature, one penetrates the gate of liberation through emptiness, removing the coarsest, 'flesh' level of affliction. Knowing the dependent nature, one penetrates the gate of liberation through wishlessness, removing the 'skin' level. Knowing the perfected nature, one penetrates the gate of liberation through signlessness, removing the 'mind' level — corresponding, in sequence, to the obstruction to liberation, then to meditative concentration, then to omniscience." Question: "How many of the three natures cannot be eliminated, and how many can?" Answer: "Two cannot be eliminated, one can. The imagined nature is, from the start, non-existent, and so cannot be eliminated. The perfected nature is, from the start, true, and so cannot be eliminated. The dependent nature, though it exists, is not truly real, and so can be eliminated."

Teachings of Provisional and Definitive Meaning

Chinese Source

六因事說，諸佛說法有二種：一了義經、二不了義經。不了義經者，由此三性，是故佛說不了義經。如緣有燈故知物在暗中，後時因燈能得了現暗中之物。如來亦爾，由有著三性者故說不了義經。達三性者，自然顯了，名了義經。如經中說：若人已得無生法忍則不退墮。問曰：此言云何成立？答曰：由有三性故則得成立。如來約分別性故說本來無生忍，約依他性故說自性無生忍，約真實性故說惑垢苦本性無生忍。問曰：如來約何性說如此義，言：一切諸法無生無滅、本來寂靜、自性涅槃耶？答曰：約無相性說如是言。問曰：如來約何法說一切諸法譬如幻化耶？答曰：約無生性說。問曰：如來約何法說如是言：一切諸法譬如虛空。答曰：約真實性說。是故佛因三性說故，有了不了義經。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, as to their role in teachings given for a purpose: the Buddha's teachings are of two kinds — sūtras of definitive meaning, and sūtras of provisional meaning. It is precisely on account of the three natures that the Buddha taught sūtras of provisional meaning. It is like knowing there is an object in the dark by depending on a lamp — only afterward, by means of the lamp, is the object hidden in the darkness clearly revealed. The Tathāgata is exactly the same: because there are those still attached to a misunderstanding of the three natures, he taught sūtras of provisional meaning. For one who has already penetrated the three natures, the truth is naturally revealed of itself — this is what is called a sūtra of definitive meaning. As the sūtra says: "One who has already attained the forbearance of the non-arising of dharmas will never retreat." Question: "How is this established?" Answer: "Precisely by means of the three natures. The Tathāgata, with reference to the imagined nature, speaks of 'the forbearance of fundamental non-arising'; with reference to the dependent nature, 'the forbearance of self-natured non-arising'; and with reference to the perfected nature, 'the forbearance of the fundamental non-arising of afflictive defilement and suffering.'" Question: "With reference to which nature does the Tathāgata teach that all dharmas are without arising and ceasing, originally still, and inherently in nirvāṇa?" Answer: "The nature of no characteristic." Question: "With reference to which does he teach that all dharmas are like magical illusions?" Answer: "The nature of no arising." Question: "With reference to which does he teach that all dharmas are like empty space?" Answer: "The perfected nature." It is for this reason that, on account of the three natures, the Buddha's teachings include both those of definitive and those of provisional meaning.

Cognition, Penetration, and What Follows from Absence

Chinese Source

七依境者，問曰：此三性為何智境？答曰：分別性者，唯是凡惑境非聖智境。何以故？無體相故。依他性者。為聖凡俗智境，是俗有故。真實性者，唯為無分別聖智境，如量如理故。如量則攝一切，如理則無顛倒，是名依境。

八通達者，問曰：修觀行人若通達分別性者，為當可說行執相中、為不可說行執相中耶？答曰：若由世俗智分別，可說行執相中；若由出世無分別智通達者，可說不行於執相中。是故依他與分別同一無相。如分別依他，真實亦如是。問曰：修觀行人能如真實理入分別性，照了何性耶？答曰：了真實性。問曰：修觀行人如真實理入真實性，照了何性？答曰：了依他性故然後得真實性，是名通達。

九若無等者，問曰：若分別性無，有何過失？答曰：若無分別性則名言不立，名言不立故則依他性不得成就，乃至淨不淨品並皆不立。問曰：若無依他性，有何過失？答曰：若無依他性，一切煩惱不由功用應自能滅。若爾，淨品亦不得成。問曰：若真實性無，有何過失？答曰：若無真實性，則一切一切種清淨境不得成故。一切者，別攝真俗盡。一切種者，通攝真俗故。問曰：是真實性者，為可立淨、為立不淨？答曰：不可得說定淨不淨。若定淨者，則一切眾生不勞修行，自得解脫故。若定不淨者，一切眾生修道即無果報。若定淨者，則無凡夫法；若定不淨者，則無聖人法。何以故？淨不淨品皆以如為本故。若其定淨，不即無明；若其不淨，不即般若。此兩處如性不異，故此真如非淨非不淨。何以故？欲顯真如異眼等諸根、異禪定心等故。異眼等諸根者，諸根既不被染，亦應得同如理清淨。而不然者，以有漏業為因故從本不淨。真如不爾，在於佛地本性清淨，無有從本是不淨義，故異諸根。異定心等者，定體本性自淨，可得同真，而為四惑所噉故，轉成不淨。真如之理本來清淨則不如是，雖復在無明㲉中，終不為彼所污。問曰：此三性，幾性無體能生有體？答曰：唯分別一性無體，能生依他性體。問曰：此幾性有體能生有體？答曰：唯是依他一性有不實體，還能生依他體，猶如無明生諸行等。問曰：此三性幾性有體，能生無體？答曰：真實一性能滅依他令其無體故，是名若無等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, as to the cognition to which each belongs: Question — "Of what wisdom is each nature the object?" Answer — "The imagined nature is the object only of the ordinary, deluded mind, not of sagely wisdom, because it has no real essence at all. The dependent nature is the object of both sagely and ordinary conventional cognition, since it exists conventionally. The perfected nature is the object only of non-discriminating sagely wisdom, being both fully comprehensive and free from error."

Eighth, as to penetration: Question — "When a practitioner penetrates the imagined nature, should this be described as still operating within the mark of attachment, or not?" Answer — "Through conventional, discriminating cognition, it may be said to still operate within that mark; through supramundane, non-discriminating wisdom, it may be said to no longer operate within it. For this reason the dependent and imagined natures share, in the end, the same mark of no characteristic — and the perfected is related to them in just the same way." Question: "When a practitioner enters the imagined nature by way of true principle, what nature does this illuminate?" Answer: "The perfected nature." Question: "When a practitioner enters the perfected nature by way of true principle, what does this illuminate?" Answer: "It is by illuminating the dependent nature that one then attains the perfected nature."

Ninth, as to the consequences of their absence: Question — "If the imagined nature did not exist, what fault would follow?" Answer — "Names and words could not be established, and so neither could the dependent nature, nor, indeed, either the pure or impure aspect of anything at all." Question: "If the dependent nature did not exist, what fault?" Answer: "All afflictions would have to cease of themselves without any effort — in which case the pure aspect could not be established either." Question: "If the perfected nature did not exist, what fault?" Answer: "Neither the 'universal' nor the 'every kind' of pure object could be established — 'universal' comprehending the true and conventional taken separately, 'every kind' comprehending them together." Question: "Is the perfected nature to be established as pure, or impure?" Answer: "It cannot be said to be fixedly either. If fixedly pure, all sentient beings would attain liberation of themselves, without practice; if fixedly impure, no one's practice could ever bear fruit. If fixedly pure, there could be no ordinary person; if fixedly impure, no sage — because both pure and impure aspects are rooted in suchness itself, which does not differ between the two states, and so is neither pure nor impure. This is meant to show that suchness differs both from the sense-faculties, which are impure from the start because their cause is karma subject to outflow, and from a state such as meditative concentration, which is inherently pure yet turns impure once consumed by the four afflictions. Suchness, being inherently pure from the start, is never sullied even while dwelling, as it were, within the shell of ignorance." Question: "Among the three natures, how many, lacking substance, are able to give rise to something that has substance?" Answer: "Only the imagined nature, giving rise to the substance of the dependent nature." Question: "How many, having substance, give rise to something with substance?" Answer: "Only the dependent nature, its unreal substance giving rise to further dependent substance, just as ignorance gives rise to karmic formations." Question: "How many, having substance, give rise to something without substance?" Answer: "Only the perfected nature, which is able to eliminate the dependent nature, causing it to become without substance."

Their Basis

Chinese Source

十依止者，問曰：分別性依何法得成？答曰：依三法故成。何者三？一相、二名、三思惟。依此三故，分別性立。問曰：依他性依何得成？答曰：依四法成。四法者，謂相、名、分別、聖智等。依此四法故，依他性成。問曰：真實性依何法得成？答曰：此性無住無著、無有依處，境無分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tenth, as to their basis: Question — "On what does the imagined nature depend for its establishment?" Answer — "On three factors — characteristic, name, and discriminating thought." Question: "On what does the dependent nature depend?" Answer: "On four factors — characteristic, name, discrimination, and sagely wisdom." Question: "On what does the perfected nature depend?" Answer: "This nature has no abode and no attachment; it has no basis at all to depend on, and its object involves no discrimination whatsoever."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The three-natures framework laid out here — imagined (parikalpita), dependent (paratantra), and perfected (pariniṣpanna) — is the treatise's inherited Yogācāra vocabulary, the same doctrine found in Vasubandhu's Triṃśikā and the broader Yogācāra corpus this collection also carries. What is distinctive is how the treatise puts it to work here: rather than treating the three natures purely as an account of ordinary experience and its correction, it uses them to explain why the Buddha sometimes speaks in ways that seem to contradict each other — teaching non-arising with reference to one nature, illusion with reference to another, emptiness like space with reference to a third. The "provisional meaning" sūtras are not lesser or false; they are simply keyed to a particular one of the three natures, useful until the whole structure is seen at once. This is the same move the treatise will keep making about buddha-nature itself in the fascicle ahead: not one flat claim, but a claim that only makes sense once you see which of several angles is being taken at any given moment.
