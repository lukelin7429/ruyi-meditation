Fourteen Deeds of a Buddha

This page completes the treatise's account of the dharmakāya's five marks and five virtues — immeasurable, uncountable, inconceivable, without equal, and utterly pure — including Śāriputra's own account of what it feels like to have faith in virtues too vast to fully know. It then turns to the sambhogakāya's three virtues, and closes with the nirmāṇakāya's fourteen deeds across a buddha's life, from birth in Tuṣita heaven through defeating Māra's army to entering final nirvāṇa.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 20 of 22 complete Source: Taishō T1610, 佛性論 (attributed to Vasubandhu, tr. Paramārtha)

The Fourth and Fifth Marks, and Five Virtues

Chinese Source

四離障相者，障有三種：一煩惱障，能離此障，得慧解脫阿羅漢。二禪定障，由離此障，故得俱解脫阿羅漢及獨覺等。三一切智障，是菩薩道所破，離此障故得成正覺。如來法身在三位中，雖有三障，非所染污。五法身界清淨相者，凡有四譬，譬各四義。初四義者，法身不改如金，如如清淨如水，第一義諦無相如空，大般涅槃顯了如覺。二四義者，神通轉變如金，慈悲潤澤如水，自性不捨眾生如空，般若解淨如覺。三四義者，因本清淨無染如金，勝道洗浣如水，解脫無累如空，果體顯現如覺。四四義者，樂性利益如金，淨體清潔如水，常德無壞如空，我義無著如覺。

問曰：此五相各顯何義？答曰：初無為相者，為顯種類義。何以故？如來法身以無為為種類相故。二無別異者，為顯相義。明如來相者應知不一不二為相故。三離二邊相者，為顯足趺義。足者即菩薩一切聖道，趺者聖道所依止處。捨離二邊，能依中道之理，得至法身故。四離一切障相者，為顯法身功德無諸染污，智障永度故。五清淨法身相者，顯法身果無垢澄寂故。復次五相次第義應知，初無為相者顯常住，二無別異相者顯真實義，三離二邊相者顯對治義，四離一切障相者顯解脫義，五法界清淨者顯自性清淨義。如是相生，亦得從前向後、從後向前故。復次五義次第者，一自性故說無為，二無分別故說不一不二，三聖智境界故說離二邊，四自性清淨故說離一切障，五究竟成就故說法界清淨。是名五相。

復次五德者，一不可量、二不可數、三不可思、四無與等、五究竟清淨。一不可量者，有四義：一由時節久故不可量；二功用大故不可量；三無餘不足故不可量；四無中間故不可量，如佛問舍利弗：汝能如量通達如來功德不？舍利弗言：不能。世尊！又問：汝云何得信如來功德？舍利弗言：我今依聲聞能觀見如來戒等功德，無處不生希有。譬如有人行天園，路見寶莊嚴樹，生希有心。我今亦爾。世尊！譬如有人在大城外，見彼諸人出入，無不可怜。作如是計：此城中人，皆應可怜。我亦如是，依聲聞故，能觀如來戒等功德，無非希有，以信知故。

二不可數者，是不可量功德為一為多？其數無窮，過恒沙數。如《馬先行經》中說偈：「若人有千頭，頭頭有百口，口口百言舌，舌舌百言聲，十力等一分，窮劫說不盡。」三不可思者，非覺觀境界故。四無與等者，不與聲聞獨覺菩薩三乘等共得故。五究竟清淨者，無明住地永滅無餘故。是名法身五德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, the mark of freedom from obstruction: obstruction is of three kinds. The obstruction of affliction — freed from this, one attains the arhat liberated through wisdom. The obstruction to meditative concentration — freed from this, one attains the arhat liberated in both respects, and likewise the pratyekabuddha. The obstruction to omniscience — this is broken by the bodhisattva path, and, freed from it, one attains perfect enlightenment. Though these three obstructions are present across the three stages, the Tathāgata's dharmakāya is never stained by them. Fifth, the mark of the purity of the dharmakāya's own realm: there are four similes, each carrying four meanings. As to the first set: the dharmakāya's unchangingness is like gold; thusness's purity is like water; the ultimate truth's signlessness is like space; and mahāparinirvāṇa's manifest clarity is like a sunbeam. As to the second set: supernatural transformation is like gold; compassion's moistening is like water; the self-nature's never abandoning sentient beings is like space; and prajñā's liberating purity is like a sunbeam. As to the third set: the cause's original, unstained purity is like gold; the supreme path's cleansing is like water; liberation's freedom from entanglement is like space; and the fruit's own essence made manifest is like a sunbeam. As to the fourth set: bliss's own beneficial nature is like gold; the pure essence's cleanness is like water; the virtue of permanence's indestructibility is like space; and the meaning of self's freedom from attachment is like a sunbeam.

Question: "What does each of these five marks reveal?" Answer: "The first, the unconditioned, reveals the sense of category, since the Tathāgata's dharmakāya takes the unconditioned as its categorial mark. The second, neither one nor different, reveals the sense of characteristic, showing that the Tathāgata's own characteristic is neither one nor two. The third, freedom from the two extremes, reveals the sense of foothold — 'foot' referring to the whole of the bodhisattva's noble path, 'instep' to what that path depends upon; by relinquishing both extremes, one can rely on the middle way and reach the dharmakāya. The fourth, freedom from every obstruction, reveals that the dharmakāya's virtue is free of all defilement. The fifth, the dharmakāya's own purity, reveals that its fruit is stainless, limpid, and still." Furthermore, the sequence of these five marks may be understood proceeding either from first to last or from last to first: the first reveals permanence, the second the truly real, the third the antidote, the fourth liberation, and the fifth inherent purity. And their sequence of meanings runs: on account of self-nature, the unconditioned; on account of non-discrimination, neither one nor different; on account of the domain of sagely wisdom, freedom from the two extremes; on account of inherent purity, freedom from every obstruction; and on account of ultimate accomplishment, the dharma-realm's purity. These are the five marks.

Next, the five virtues are: immeasurability, uncountability, inconceivability, having no equal, and ultimate purity. As to immeasurability, there are four senses: immeasurable because of the vastness of time involved; immeasurable because of the greatness of its function; immeasurable because there is neither excess nor deficiency; and immeasurable because it has no middle point. As when the Buddha asked Śāriputra: "Are you able to fully comprehend the Tathāgata's virtues in their true measure?" Śāriputra answered: "I am not, World-Honored One." The Buddha asked further: "How, then, do you come to have faith in the Tathāgata's virtues?" Śāriputra answered: "I now, relying on my status as a śrāvaka, am able to observe the Tathāgata's virtues of discipline and the rest, and everywhere I look, a sense of wonder arises. It is like a person walking through a heavenly garden who, along the way, sees a jewel-adorned tree, and a sense of wonder arises in him — I am exactly the same, World-Honored One. Or it is like a person standing outside a great city, seeing the people going in and out, every one of them admirable, and so reasoning: everyone within this city must be admirable as well. I am exactly the same: relying on my status as a śrāvaka, I observe the Tathāgata's virtues and find nothing there but wonder, knowing it through faith."

As to uncountability: are these immeasurable virtues one, or many? Their number is inexhaustible, surpassing even the sands of the Ganges. As the Aśvavatī Sūtra says in verse: "If a person had a thousand heads, and each head a hundred mouths, and each mouth a hundred tongues, and each tongue a hundred voices — even then, describing but one portion of the ten powers, an entire eon would not suffice to exhaust it." As to inconceivability: because it lies beyond the domain of ordinary reflective cognition. As to having no equal: because it is not shared in common attainment even with the three vehicles of śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas. As to ultimate purity: because the ground of ignorance is permanently extinguished without remainder. These are the dharmakāya's five virtues.

The Sambhogakāya's Three Virtues

Chinese Source

復次應身者，勢用廣大故。此身本有三德：一大般若、二大禪定、三大慈悲。大般若者，無分別聖智是其體相。大禪定者，無作意是其體相，已離出入意故。大慈悲者，能拔能救是其體相。如眾生意令得圓滿，故須此三。一為法樂、二為六通、三為拔濟，是故大悲為拔三惡道苦，安置人天，大定能顯六通，令生信樂。般若為受法樂，能成熟解脫。是名應身。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, as to the sambhogakāya: its power and function are vast and great. This body has, at its root, three virtues: great prajñā, great meditative concentration, and great compassion. Great prajñā has non-discriminating sagely wisdom as its essence. Great meditative concentration has effortlessness as its essence, already free of any thought of entering or emerging from concentration. Great compassion has the ability to extract and to rescue as its essence. It is in order to bring sentient beings' own aspirations to full completion that these three are needed: for the joy of the Dharma, for the six supernatural powers, and for extraction and rescue. Hence great compassion extracts beings from the suffering of the three evil destinies and establishes them among humans and gods; great concentration manifests the six supernatural powers, giving rise to faith and delight; and prajñā allows one to receive the joy of the Dharma, bringing liberation to maturity. This is called the sambhogakāya.

Fourteen Deeds of the Nirmāṇakāya

Chinese Source

復次化身者，大悲為本，禪定為變現，般若能令有五種能：一令生厭怖、二令入聖道、三令捨昔執、四令信樂大法、五令受大菩提記。此三大法在因地中，熏修如如，安立本願。由此本願，至道後時，隨於三身能作利益眾生之事。是故出現五濁世中，事有十四：一現本生事、二現生兜率天、三從天下處中陰、四入胎、五出胎、六學技能、七童子遊戲、八出家、九苦行、十詣菩提樹、十一破魔軍、十二成佛、十三轉法輪、十四般涅槃。此十四事現五濁世，至眾生盡。此十四事為五因緣：一為說無常苦無我空涅槃寂靜，由此正說音聲，能令眾生於三有中而生怖畏。二生怖畏已，令入二乘聖道。三入聖道已，生究竟涅槃心。為破如此增上慢心故，說大乘法花等真實法教，令諸眾生捨本所執，攝取慈悲般若方便四攝，已於無上乘中而成熟之。五成熟已，授其無上菩提道記。是名化身事。

復次依此三身，隨一一身各有一德。法身微細故，甚深是其德。應身威神具足故，廣大是其德。化身能濟度凡夫等諸眾生故，和善是其德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, as to the nirmāṇakāya: great compassion is its root, meditative concentration is what allows it to appear in transformation, and prajñā endows it with five capacities — to give rise to revulsion, to bring about entry into the noble path, to cause the abandonment of former attachments, to inspire faith and delight in the great Dharma, and to enable the reception of the prediction of unsurpassed enlightenment. These three great dharmas, at the causal stage, perfume and cultivate suchness, and establish the original vow — and it is by means of this vow that, once beyond the path, one is able, through all three bodies, to perform the work of benefiting sentient beings. Hence the nirmāṇakāya appears within this world of the five defilements, performing fourteen deeds: manifesting its original birth-story; manifesting birth in Tuṣita heaven; descending from that heaven into the intermediate state; entering the womb; leaving the womb; learning worldly skills; the youthful prince's games; going forth from home; ascetic practice; approaching the bodhi tree; defeating Māra's army; attaining buddhahood; turning the wheel of the Dharma; and entering final nirvāṇa. These fourteen deeds appear throughout this world of the five defilements, continuing until sentient beings are exhausted, and serve five purposes: first, to teach impermanence, suffering, no-self, emptiness, and the stillness of nirvāṇa, giving rise, through the sound of this correct teaching, to a sense of dread toward the three realms of existence; second, having given rise to this dread, to bring about entry into the noble path of the two vehicles; third, having entered the noble path, to give rise to the aspiration for ultimate nirvāṇa — and, in order to break through the excessive pride this aspiration can produce, to teach the true Dharma of the Lotus and similar Mahāyāna sūtras, causing sentient beings to relinquish their former attachment, and, taking up compassion, prajñā, and the four means of attraction, to bring them to maturity within the unsurpassed vehicle; fifth, once matured, to bestow upon them the prediction of unsurpassed enlightenment. These are the deeds of the nirmāṇakāya.

Next, with respect to these three bodies, each one, individually, has its own single virtue: the dharmakāya, being subtle, has profundity as its virtue; the sambhogakāya, being complete with majestic power, has vastness as its virtue; and the nirmāṇakāya, being able to rescue ordinary beings and every other kind of sentient being, has gentleness as its virtue.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Śāriputra's answer to the Buddha here is worth sitting with, since it models something the treatise never states outright: what faith actually looks like once its object is genuinely too vast to verify directly. He does not claim certainty, and he does not simply defer to authority either — he reasons from a visible sample (the virtues of discipline he can actually observe) to a warranted confidence about the whole, the way seeing every person leaving a city gate looking well cared-for lets one reasonably infer something about the city itself, without having walked its every street. This is a notably modest, almost empirical account of religious confidence for a text this metaphysically ambitious — faith here is not the suspension of reasoning, but reasoning honestly extended past the edge of what can be fully checked.

Part 20 Complete

This closes the treatise's full account of the three bodies. Part 21 turns to why these three bodies, taken together, are truly permanent — ten reasons ranging from the boundlessness of the causes that produced them to their being, by nature, never subject to arising and cessation at all — before closing the entire mark of non-change with a final debate over whether the standard Mahāyāna teaching that "all dharmas are like clouds, dreams, and illusions" contradicts the claim that every sentient being possesses buddha-nature.
