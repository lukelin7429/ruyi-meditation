Refuting the Hīnayāna Objection

The treatise's second chapter opens the "Part Refuting Attachments" (破執分) with the first of three refutations it promises — of Hīnayāna, of non-Buddhist, and of certain Mahāyāna misreadings. This page covers the first: the apparent scriptural basis, in some Hīnayāna readings, for holding that certain beings — icchantikas — are permanently excluded from nirvāṇa, and the treatise's point-by-point dismantling of that position through a rootless-beings analogy, a four-caste analogy, and a final argument that a truly fixed nature could never coherently be established at all.

Two Hīnayāna Readings in Conflict

Chinese Source

復次佛性有無，成、破立義應知。破有三種：一破小乘執、二破外道執、三破菩薩執。初破小乘執者，佛為小乘人說有眾生不住於性，永不般涅槃故。於此生疑，起不信心。

釋曰。所以生疑者，由佛說故，小乘諸部解執不同。若依分別部說，一切凡聖眾生並以空為其本，所以凡聖眾生皆從空出故。空是佛性，佛性者即大涅槃。若依毘曇薩婆多等諸部說者，則一切眾生無有性得佛性，但有修得佛性。分別眾生凡有三種：一定無佛性，永不得涅槃，是一闡提犯重禁者。二不定有無，若修時即得、不修不得，是賢善共位以上人故。三定有佛性，即三乘人：一聲聞從苦忍以上即得佛性，二獨覺從世法以上即得佛性，三者菩薩十迴向以上是不退位時得於佛性。所以然者，如經說：有眾生不住於性，永無般涅槃故。又阿鋡說：佛十力中性力所照，眾生境界有種種性，乃至麁妙等界不同，故稱性力。所以者何？一切眾生有性無性異故。有佛性者則修種種妙行，無佛性者則起種種麁惡。是故學小乘人見此二說皆有道理，未知何者為定，故起疑心。

復次生不信心者，於二說中各偏一執，故不相信。何者？若從分別部說，則不信有無性眾生；若薩婆多等部說，則不信皆有佛性故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, regarding whether buddha-nature exists or not, the establishing and refuting of positions should be understood. There are three refutations: first, refuting Hīnayāna attachments; second, refuting non-Buddhist attachments; third, refuting certain Mahāyāna attachments. As for the first: the Buddha, teaching Hīnayāna followers, said that there are sentient beings who do not abide within buddha-nature and will never attain final nirvāṇa. On hearing this, doubt arises, and a lack of faith is born.

Explanation. The reason doubt arises is that the Buddha's own statement is read differently by the various Hīnayāna schools. According to the Vibhajyavādins, all beings — ordinary and holy alike — take emptiness as their basis, since all of them emerge from emptiness; emptiness is buddha-nature, and buddha-nature is precisely mahāparinirvāṇa. According to the Abhidharma Sarvāstivādins and similar schools, however, no sentient being possesses buddha-nature inherently — it can only be obtained through cultivation. On this view, sentient beings fall into exactly three kinds: first, those who definitely lack buddha-nature and will never attain nirvāṇa — the icchantikas who have committed grave transgressions; second, those in whom it is undetermined — if they cultivate, they attain it, and if they do not, they do not, these being persons at or above the rank shared by the worthy and the good; third, those who definitely possess it, namely followers of the three vehicles: śrāvakas attain it from the level of the forbearance regarding suffering upward, pratyekabuddhas from the level of worldly dharmas upward, and bodhisattvas from the ten dedications upward, at the stage of non-retrogression. And why is this so? Because, as the sūtras say, "there are sentient beings who do not abide within nature and will never attain final nirvāṇa." And an Āgama says: "Among the Buddha's ten powers, the power that illuminates beings' natures reveals that sentient beings' conditions comprise many differing natures, down to differences of coarse and fine realm" — hence it is called the power of natures. Why? Because sentient beings differ in whether they possess this nature or not: those who possess buddha-nature cultivate all manner of excellent conduct, while those who lack it give rise to all manner of coarse evil. For this reason, students of the Hīnayāna, seeing that both readings have some rational basis, do not know which is settled, and so doubt arises.

Furthermore, as to how a lack of faith arises: each of these two readings is one-sided, holding to a single extreme, and so each fails to believe the other. How so? Followers of the Vibhajyavādin reading do not believe there are sentient beings who altogether lack buddha-nature; followers of the Sarvāstivādin and similar readings do not believe that all beings possess it.

The Rootless-Beings Analogy

Chinese Source

明有佛性者問執無性曰：汝云何有無性眾生永不般涅槃？答曰：眾生既有種種麁妙不同，故知理有有性、無性。汝若不信有無性眾生永不涅槃，而信有眾生有種種麁妙等界者，是義不然？何以故？執不平等故。

問曰：汝信有眾生種種麁妙等界，即令信有無性眾生者，亦應信有無根眾生耶？何以故？眾生由有根無根故有種種麁妙等界。汝若不信有無根眾生者，云何信有麁妙等界？若謂有麁妙等界不關有根無根者，我亦信有麁妙等界不關有性無性之義，有何過失？若汝言無有無根眾生者，我亦說無有無性眾生。

答曰：汝以有根無根例我有性無性，是義不然。何以故？汝謂無根者，為是眾生、為非眾生？若是眾生，有二過失：一者泰過過失，若無六根而是眾生者，則一切無情草木石等皆是眾生，同無根故。二者不及過失，本說六根以為眾生，既無六根，更說何物為眾生耶？而汝說無根眾生，是義不然，故知不為有根無根說麁妙等界，正為有性無性說麁妙耳。

Ru-Yi Translation

One who holds that buddha-nature is universal asks one who holds that some beings lack it: "On what grounds do you say there are sentient beings who altogether lack buddha-nature and will never attain final nirvāṇa?" He answers: "Since sentient beings plainly differ, some coarse and some refined, it stands to reason that some possess this nature and some do not." "If you do not believe that there are sentient beings who altogether lack buddha-nature and will never attain nirvāṇa, and yet you do believe that sentient beings differ in coarse and refined realms — is this not inconsistent? Because your standard is applied unequally."

Question: "You believe sentient beings exhibit differing coarse and fine realms, and on this basis you would have me believe there are natureless beings — should I then also believe there are rootless beings, beings lacking sense-faculties altogether? For it is precisely on account of having or lacking sense-faculties that beings exhibit their differing coarse and fine realms. If you do not believe there are rootless beings, then on what grounds do you believe in differing coarse and fine realms at all? If you say that differing realms have nothing to do with having or lacking sense-faculties, then I too may say that differing realms have nothing to do with having or lacking buddha-nature — what fault would there be in that? And if you say there are no rootless beings, then I too will say there are no natureless beings."

Answer: "Your comparison of having-or-lacking sense-faculties to my having-or-lacking buddha-nature does not hold. Why? As for what you call a 'rootless being' — is it a sentient being, or is it not? If it is a sentient being, two faults follow: first, the fault of excess — if something lacking the six sense-faculties could still be a sentient being, then all insentient things, grass, trees, and stones, would equally be sentient beings, since they equally lack the faculties. Second, the fault of insufficiency — sentient beings are, by definition, constituted precisely by the six sense-faculties; lacking them, what then would remain to be called a sentient being at all? So your talk of rootless beings does not hold. This shows that differences of coarse and fine are not spoken of in terms of having or lacking sense-faculties, but precisely in terms of having or lacking buddha-nature."

The Fault Turned Back

Chinese Source

難曰：若汝謂我立無根眾生有二過失者，汝立犯重。一闡提人無有佛性永不得涅槃，亦有二失：一者泰過過失，眾生本以我見無明為凡夫法。尋此無明由違人空故起，既起無明故有業報。若不違人空則無無明業報。既無無明業報等三輪，若爾應是聖人作於凡夫。若謂眾生無佛性者，但聖為凡，無凡得聖，此成泰過。二者不及過失，若汝謂有眾生無佛性者，既無空性則無無明，若無無明則無業報。既無業報，眾生豈有？故成不及。而汝謂有眾生無佛性者，是義不然。何以故？汝既不信有無根眾生，那忽信有無性眾生？以二失同故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Objection: "If you say that my positing rootless beings incurs two faults, then your own position commits an even graver version of the same. Your claim that the icchantika altogether lacks buddha-nature and will never attain nirvāṇa likewise incurs two faults. First, the fault of excess: sentient beings are, at root, constituted as ordinary beings by self-view and ignorance. This ignorance arises precisely because it violates the emptiness of person; once ignorance arises, karmic action and its retribution follow. If a being did not violate the emptiness of person, there would be no ignorance, action, or retribution. But if, as you say, such a being has no buddha-nature, there is no self-view to violate in the first place — so there would be no ignorance, action, or retribution, these three wheels, and such a being would have to be a sage merely playing the part of an ordinary being. On your claim, only a sage plays the ordinary; no ordinary being could ever become a sage — this is the fault of excess. Second, the fault of insufficiency: if you say a being lacks buddha-nature, then, having no empty nature to violate, it would have no ignorance; and having no ignorance, it would have no karmic retribution. But having no karmic retribution, how could such a 'being' exist at all? This is the fault of insufficiency. So your claim that there are sentient beings lacking buddha-nature does not hold. Why? Because you yourself do not believe there are rootless beings — how then can you suddenly believe there are natureless beings? The two faults are exactly the same."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This objection turns on a point worth making explicit: the treatise assumes ignorance itself presupposes something real to be ignorant about — the true emptiness of person that ignorance "violates" (違人空) by mistakenly positing a self where none exists. If a being genuinely had no buddha-nature — no share in that empty, luminous ground the treatise will spend the rest of the work describing — then there would be nothing there for ignorance to misconstrue in the first place, and the entire machinery of delusion, karma, and rebirth that defines an ordinary being's existence could never get started. The icchantika who supposedly lacks buddha-nature altogether would, on this reasoning, either have to already be free of the very delusion that makes beings suffer — which is absurd — or fail to qualify as a "being" undergoing saṃsāra at all. Both horns of the dilemma are fatal to the position being refuted, which is exactly the shape of argument the rootless-beings analogy modeled a moment earlier.

The Four Castes, and Whether Nature Can Be Fixed

Chinese Source

問曰：汝說有眾生無佛性者，如剎底利種，為具有四性及地獄人天等性、為不具有？若言不具有者，人應常人，永無作諸道義。若具足有者，則違經。如經中說：如來性力能了種種麁妙等界。此眾生性既其平等，經不證故。又若汝謂有眾生永不般涅槃者，義亦不然。如人先為剎底利，後作婆羅門、或人或天，無決定相故。若汝說不具足者，則與立譬相違。何以故？俱不具故。汝說無佛性眾生永不得佛，如人無天性，則應永無天報。若無天性而得天報者，亦應有無佛性眾生而得涅槃。復次若具足性與譬相似者，則無佛性眾生應具有佛性。若有有無二性，為相違不？若相違者，則應一有一無，是義不可。若無涅槃性眾生，則不應有涅槃性。汝言具二性者，義亦不然。何以故？如剎底利無婆羅門性，二性相違，決定無故。後則不得為婆羅門，乖世道故。又若俱有性義者，後時決得；若不具性義者，後決不得。若一人具此二義，定何所屬？

又問：汝立無佛性眾生，始終定無、為不定無？譬如大地初無金性，後時或有，有已更無。汝立無佛性，亦如是不？若如此者，則應得二乘性竟，後更不得；得大乘性竟，後應更失；得定性已，後更不定。雖修得通達解脫等功德，後還更失，則修道無用。決定立性，並成無用故。

又問：汝立無定性眾生如地，或時轉為金寶等物。無佛性眾生住於下性，是人性不定故能轉為涅槃者，為今生轉、為未來轉？若汝謂今生轉者，云何得轉？為值三寶得解脫三善根故轉、為不值而能得轉？若言修功德分故現在轉者，何謂無佛性眾生永住下性？是義自壞。若汝謂今世雖修善根終不得轉，未來方轉，故名住下性者，此性於未來中，為修善故轉、不修故轉？若修故轉，今修何故不轉？若言未來不修善自然轉者，現在未修，何故不轉？

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: "You say that some sentient beings lack buddha-nature. Take a person of the kṣatriya caste — does he possess, in full, the natures of all four castes, together with the natures of hell-being, human, and god, or does he not? If you say he does not possess them in full, then a human would always remain a human, and there could be no sense in which one being takes on different destinies. If you say he possesses them in full, this contradicts the sūtras, which say that the Tathāgata's power of natures illuminates beings' many differing coarse and fine realms — which makes no sense if these natures were, from the start, all equally present in full; so the sūtra offers no support either way. Moreover, if you hold that some beings will never attain final nirvāṇa, this too does not hold — just as a person who was first a kṣatriya may later become a brāhmaṇa, or be reborn as a human or a god, there being no fixed, permanent mark that binds a being to one caste or destiny forever. If you say the natures are not possessed in full, this contradicts your own analogy, since on that reading neither presence nor absence would be full, and your parallel would fail on its own terms. You say a being lacking buddha-nature will never attain buddhahood — as if a being without a god's nature must forever lack a god's reward. But if a being without a god's nature can still attain a god's reward, then a being lacking buddha-nature should likewise be able to attain nirvāṇa. Furthermore, if natures are, as your analogy requires, possessed in full, then even a being said to lack buddha-nature should, in full, possess it. If presence and absence are two mutually exclusive natures, must a being have strictly one or the other? That cannot be maintained, given the caste analogy above. If a being lacks the nature for nirvāṇa, it ought never to come to possess it. Your claim that one being possesses both natures at once likewise fails — just as a kṣatriya lacks the nature of a brāhmaṇa, these two being mutually exclusive and definitely absent one from the other, so that he could never subsequently become a brāhmaṇa, which would contradict how caste actually works in the world. And if a being possesses both natures, it will in time definitely attain nirvāṇa; if it does not, it will in time definitely not attain it. If a single person possesses both aspects at once, to which does he finally belong?"

Question, again: "When you posit sentient beings lacking buddha-nature, do you mean fixed absence from beginning to end, or unfixed absence? Consider how the great earth, at first without any nature of gold, may later come to have it, and having had it, may lose it again — is your 'lacking buddha-nature' like this as well? If so, then a being who has already attained the nature of the two lesser vehicles could later fail to attain it after all; one who has attained the nature of the Mahāyāna could later lose it again; one who has attained a fixed nature could later become unfixed once more. Though one may cultivate and attain the merits of penetration and liberation, one could afterward lose them again — in which case cultivating the path would be pointless. To posit a fixed nature in this manner renders it, in every case, useless."

Question, again: "If instead you posit that beings of undetermined nature are like the earth, sometimes turning into gold or jewels — that is, if a being lacking buddha-nature abides in a lower nature but, being undetermined, can be transformed and attain nirvāṇa — does this transformation occur in this very life, or only in some future life? If in this very life, how does it come about — through encountering the Three Jewels and attaining the three wholesome roots that lead to liberation, or without such an encounter? If you say it transforms in the present life through cultivating a portion of merit, then what do you mean by saying such beings abide forever in a lower nature? Your own position collapses by its own terms. If instead you say that although such a being cultivates wholesome roots in this life it will still not transform, and the transformation occurs only in some future life — hence 'abiding in a lower nature' now — then in that future life, will the nature transform because it cultivates goodness, or because it does not? If because it cultivates goodness, why does it not transform when it cultivates goodness now? And if it transforms of itself without cultivating goodness, why does it not transform now, when it likewise has not cultivated goodness?"

Why a Fixed Absence Cannot Be Maintained

Chinese Source

又若汝謂無佛性是定無者，如火定熱性，不可轉為水冷性。佛性亦爾，有無應定，皆不可轉。若不可轉者，汝立此定，為由因故定、不由因故定？若由因故定，此定不成定。何以故？本時未是定，由因方定故。若說不由因而定者，則無窮過失。是故我說此性亦復不定，不由因故，是義應成。如汝說定等共無因，若爾，非理之事並應得成。二者不平等過失，如人謂石女生兩兒，一白一黑。亦如兔有兩角，一利一鈍。若人不由因說，此不平等義亦應得成。如汝所說，此若不成，汝亦不立。三者失同外道，有本定有、無本定無，有不可滅、無不可生。此等過失，由汝邪執無性義生故。

問曰：若爾，云何佛說眾生不住於性、永無般涅槃耶？答曰：若憎背大乘者。此法是一闡提因，為令眾生捨此法故。若隨一闡提因，於長時中輪轉不滅。以是義故，經作是說。若依道理，一切眾生皆悉本有清淨佛性，若永不得般涅槃者，無有是處。是故佛性決定本有，離有離無故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And again, if you hold that lacking buddha-nature is a fixed and permanent absence — as fire is fixed in its nature of heat and cannot become water's nature of coldness — buddha-nature would likewise have to be fixed in its presence or absence, in every case unable to change. But if it truly cannot change, then in positing this fixity, do you mean it is fixed because of a cause, or fixed without any cause at all? If fixed because of a cause, then this "fixity" is not truly fixed — for at the outset it was not yet fixed, and became fixed only through a cause. But if you say it is fixed without any cause whatsoever, this leads to endless faults. This is why I say this nature is likewise undetermined, and is established precisely by not depending on any single external cause — this is how the matter must stand. For if, as you claim, fixity can arise together with no cause at all, then anything, however irrational, could equally come to be. Second, there is the fault of inconsistency: it would be like saying a barren woman bears two children, one white and one black, or that a rabbit has two horns, one sharp and one dull — if things could be asserted without any cause, such inconsistent claims too would have to be granted. If, on your own account, these cannot be granted, then your position cannot stand either. Third, this falls into the very same fault as the non-Buddhist schools, who hold that what fundamentally exists is fixed in existing and what fundamentally does not exist is fixed in not existing, that what exists cannot be destroyed and what does not exist cannot come to be. All these faults arise precisely from your mistaken attachment to the notion that some beings altogether lack this nature.

Question: "If that is so, then why did the Buddha say that there are sentient beings who do not abide within nature and will never attain final nirvāṇa?" Answer: "This refers to one who hates and turns his back on the Mahāyāna. Such an attitude is itself the cause of becoming an icchantika, and the Buddha spoke this way in order to make sentient beings abandon that very attitude. Should one follow this cause of icchantika-hood, one will revolve in saṃsāra for a long time without cessation — it is for this reason the sūtra speaks as it does. But according to the true principle, all sentient beings, without exception, inherently possess pure buddha-nature from the start; there is no such thing as a being who could never attain final nirvāṇa. Therefore buddha-nature is decisively, inherently existent — apart from both the extreme of fixed existence and the extreme of fixed non-existence."

Part 2 Complete

This closes the treatise's refutation of the Hīnayāna objection: the icchantika who "never attains nirvāṇa" is not a being permanently without buddha-nature, but one who, for now, turns away from the Mahāyāna — a condition the treatise insists is reversible, since a truly fixed absence could never be coherently established by any cause. Part 3 turns to the second of the three refutations, taking on non-Buddhist objections — beginning with the Vaiśeṣika claim that all dharmas possess a fixed, unchangeable self-nature, which the treatise answers with an extended argument, drawn in part from Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, that nothing arises from itself, from another, from both, or from neither.
