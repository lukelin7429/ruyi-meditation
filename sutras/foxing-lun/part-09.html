The Self-Essence Mark

The treatise now opens its longest division, the "Part Distinguishing the Marks" (辯相分), which will occupy the rest of Fascicle Two, all of Fascicle Three, and most of Fascicle Four — ten marks in all, describing buddha-nature from every angle. This page covers the first: the self-essence mark, unfolded through three similes — the wish-fulfilling jewel and the five senses of tathāgatagarbha named in the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra, the shared emptiness inside vessels of clay, silver, and gold, and the smoothness of great compassion itself.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 9 of 22 complete Source: Taishō T1610, 佛性論 (attributed to Vasubandhu, tr. Paramārtha)

Ten Marks, and the First Mark's Structure

Chinese Source

復次佛性一切種相有十義應知。言十相者，一自體相、二因相、三果相、四事能相、五總攝相、六分別相、七階位相、八遍滿相、九無變異相、十無差別相。一自體相者，有二種：一者別相、二者通相。別相有三種。何者為三？一者如意功德性、二者無異性、三者潤滑性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, buddha-nature, in every respect, has ten marks that should be understood. What are the ten? First, the self-essence mark; second, the cause mark; third, the fruit mark; fourth, the function mark; fifth, the mark of total inclusion; sixth, the mark of differentiation; seventh, the mark of stages; eighth, the mark of pervasion; ninth, the mark of non-change; tenth, the mark of non-difference. As to the first, the self-essence mark: it is of two kinds — the particular mark and the general mark. The particular mark is of three kinds. What are the three? First, the nature of wish-fulfilling virtue; second, the nature of non-difference; third, the nature of smoothness.

The Wish-Fulfilling Jewel: Five Senses of Tathāgatagarbha

Chinese Source

所言如意功德相者，謂如來藏有五種。何等為五？一如來藏自性是其藏義。一切諸法不出如來自性，無我為相故，故說一切諸法為如來藏。二者正法藏，因是其藏義。以一切聖人四念處等正法皆取此性作境，未生得生、已生得滿，是故說名為正法藏。三者法身藏，至得是其藏義。此一切聖人信樂正性、信樂願聞，由此信樂心故，令諸聖人得於四德，及過恒沙數等一切如來功德，故說此性名法身藏。四者出世藏，真實是其藏義。世有三失：一者對治可滅盡故名為世，此法則無對治故名出世。二不靜住故名為世，由虛妄心果報念念滅不住故，此法不爾故名出世。三由有倒見故，心在世間則恒倒見，如人在三界，心中決不得見苦法忍等，以其虛妄故名為世。此法能出世間故名真實，為出世藏。五者自性清淨藏，以祕密是其藏義。若一切法隨順此性則名為內，是正非邪則為清淨。若諸法違逆此理則名為外，是邪非正名為染濁。故言自性清淨藏。故《勝鬘經》言：世尊！佛性者，是如來藏、是正法藏、是法身藏、是出世藏、是自性清淨藏。由說此五藏義故，如意功德而得顯現。佛為顯此義故說如意寶。譬如人以宿業故感得如意寶珠，得此珠已，隨其意所樂事自然得成。佛性亦爾，由伏事善知識、修諸福慧感得此性，便隨修行者意，各各自得三乘之果故。如意功德是其別相。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for what is meant by the mark of wish-fulfilling virtue: this refers to the fivefold sense of tathāgatagarbha. What are the five? First, the tathāgatagarbha itself, the meaning of garbha here being self-nature: no dharma whatsoever falls outside the Tathāgata's own self-nature, whose mark is selflessness — hence all dharmas are said to be the tathāgatagarbha. Second, the storehouse of the true Dharma, the meaning of garbha here being cause: it is because every sage's true practice, such as the four foundations of mindfulness, takes this nature as its object — what has not yet arisen thereby arises, and what has already arisen thereby comes to fulfillment — that this is called the storehouse of the true Dharma. Third, the storehouse of the dharmakāya, the meaning of garbha here being full attainment: it is by faith and delight in this true nature, and faith and delight in hearing of it, that every sage attains the four virtues and every one of the Tathāgata's virtues beyond the sands of the Ganges — hence this nature is called the storehouse of the dharmakāya. Fourth, the storehouse of what transcends the world, the meaning of garbha here being reality: the mundane has three faults. First, it is called mundane because it can be eliminated by an antidote; this dharma has no antidote, and so is called that which transcends the world. Second, it is called mundane because it does not abide in stillness, the fruit of a deluded mind perishing and never abiding moment by moment; this dharma is not so, and so is called that which transcends the world. Third, it is called mundane because of inverted views — while the mind remains within the world, it invariably holds inverted views, as when a person within the three realms can never, in their mind, attain even the forbearance regarding the truth of suffering, on account of their falseness. This dharma is able to transcend the world, and so is called real — the storehouse of what transcends the world. Fifth, the storehouse of inherent purity, the meaning of garbha here being secrecy: when all dharmas accord with this nature, they are called internal, correct rather than deviant, and hence pure; when dharmas run counter to this principle, they are called external, deviant rather than correct, and hence defiled — hence it is called the storehouse of inherent purity. Hence the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra says: "World-Honored One! Buddha-nature is the tathāgatagarbha, the storehouse of the true Dharma, the storehouse of the dharmakāya, the storehouse of what transcends the world, and the storehouse of inherent purity." It is by explaining these five senses of garbha that the wish-fulfilling virtue of buddha-nature becomes manifest. The Buddha, in order to reveal this meaning, spoke of the wish-fulfilling jewel. It is like a person who, through past karma, comes into possession of a wish-fulfilling jewel — having obtained this jewel, whatever the mind delights in is spontaneously accomplished. Buddha-nature is exactly the same: by attending upon good spiritual friends and cultivating merit and wisdom, one comes into possession of this nature, and then, in accordance with the practitioner's own aspiration, each attains the fruit of one of the three vehicles. This wish-fulfilling virtue constitutes the first of the particular marks.

The Nature of Non-Difference: Clay, Silver, and Gold

Chinese Source

二無別異性者，凡夫聖人及諸佛，無分別心性過失、功德究竟清淨處，平等遍滿譬如虛空。又如土、銀、金器，此三雖異，而其性等皆是空，空處不別故，名無別異性。

釋曰：言過失者，謂凡夫。功德者，即有學聖人。究竟清淨者，即諸佛。此三處雖殊，而其性不異。此即以土喻凡夫，銀喻學者，金喻諸佛。雖復三器有異，而其空性一種故。又是有、清淨、遍滿等三義。有者顯無為義，清淨顯無染義，遍滿顯無礙義故。佛告舍利弗：眾生界不異法身、法身不異眾生界。由此義故，無二無別，唯有名字。如是佛性於三位中平等遍滿，由淨不淨品無變異故，故說如虛空性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the nature of non-difference: without discriminating between the ordinary person's flaw, the sage's virtue, and the buddha's ultimate purity, this nature is equally and universally pervasive, like empty space. It is like vessels of clay, silver, and gold — although these three differ, the space within each is equally empty, and the space itself makes no distinction between them — hence it is called the nature of non-difference.

Explanation: "flaw" refers to the ordinary person; "virtue" refers to the sage still in training; "ultimate purity" refers to the buddhas. Though these three stages differ, their nature is not different. Here, clay is the analogy for the ordinary person, silver for the one still in training, and gold for the buddhas — though the three vessels differ, the nature of the space within them is one and the same. This also carries three senses: existence, purity, and pervasion. "Existence" manifests the sense of the unconditioned; "purity" manifests the sense of being unstained; "pervasion" manifests the sense of being unobstructed. The Buddha told Śāriputra: "The realm of sentient beings is not different from the dharmakāya; the dharmakāya is not different from the realm of sentient beings." On account of this meaning, they are neither two nor separate — there is only a difference of name. Buddha-nature, in this same way, is equally and universally pervasive across the three stages; because the pure and impure aspects bring no change to it at all, it is said to have the nature of empty space.

The Nature of Smoothness: Great Compassion

Chinese Source

三潤滑性者，辯如來性。於眾生中現因果義，由大悲於眾生軟滑為相故。大悲者，有三義：一體、二大、三別異。一體義者，以般若為體。般若有二：一無分別真智、二有分別俗智。今取有分別智為大悲體，以大悲緣眾生起故。

二大義者，有五：一為資糧、二為相、三為行處、四為平等、五為最極。一資糧者，能作大福德、智慧二行資糧故。二為相者，能觀三苦眾生悉濟拔故。三為行處者，通三界眾生為境界故。四為平等者，為於一切眾生處起平等心故。五最極者，過此修外無更勝行故。

三別異義者，有八種：一為自性差別，悲無量者以無瞋為性，大悲者以無癡為性。二為相差別，悲者以苦苦為相，大悲者以三苦為相。三為行處差別，悲者以欲界為境界，大悲者通三界為境界。四為地差別，悲者以第四禪為其地，大悲者以無流如來果為其地。五境界差別，悲者以凡夫及二乘為境界，大悲者唯菩薩與佛為境界。六為德差別，悲者以離欲欲界德，大悲者離欲三界德。七為救濟有差別，悲者但有拔苦之心、無拔苦事，大悲者有心有事。八為究竟不究竟差別，悲者能小暫救濟、不能真實救，大悲者能永救濟，恒不捨離故。

潤滑者，潤以顯其能攝義，滑者顯其背失向德義。譬如水界亦有二能：一則能攝散物，唯滑不澁故。由潤故能攝，由滑故不澁，故以潤者為因，以滑者為果，故曰現因果義。復次自性清淨是其通相義者，如前實空水界等譬，並自性清淨是其通相故。如來性在煩惱中無所染污故、此四相為四惑障故、為非四人所得故、為四德作本故、為離四倒故、為滅生死對治故，故說四相通一別三。一通相者，唯有自性清淨相。三別相者，一不可思惟、二應得、三無量功德。是名自體相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, the nature of smoothness: this concerns how the Tathāgata's nature manifests, among sentient beings, the sense of cause and fruit — by virtue of great compassion, whose mark toward sentient beings is soft and smooth. Great compassion has three aspects: essence, greatness, and distinctiveness. As to its essence: it takes prajñā as its essence. Prajñā is of two kinds — non-discriminating true wisdom, and discriminating conventional wisdom. Here it is the discriminating wisdom that is taken as the essence of great compassion, since great compassion arises by taking sentient beings as its condition.

As to its greatness, there are five aspects: as provision, as characteristic, as domain of activity, as impartiality, and as the utmost. First, as provision: it serves as the provision for the two practices of great merit and great wisdom. Second, as characteristic: it is able to observe and rescue sentient beings from all three kinds of suffering. Third, as domain of activity: it takes sentient beings throughout all three realms as its field. Fourth, as impartiality: it gives rise to an impartial mind toward every sentient being without exception. Fifth, as the utmost: apart from this cultivation there is no further, superior practice beyond it.

As to its distinctiveness from ordinary compassion, there are eight aspects: first, a difference of self-nature — immeasurable, ordinary compassion has non-hatred as its nature, while great compassion has non-delusion as its nature. Second, a difference of characteristic — ordinary compassion has the suffering of pain as its characteristic, while great compassion has all three kinds of suffering as its characteristic. Third, a difference of domain — ordinary compassion takes the desire realm as its field, while great compassion takes all three realms as its field. Fourth, a difference of ground — ordinary compassion has the fourth dhyāna as its ground, while great compassion has the outflow-free fruit of buddhahood as its ground. Fifth, a difference of object — ordinary compassion takes ordinary beings and the two vehicles as its object, while great compassion takes only bodhisattvas and buddhas as its object. Sixth, a difference of virtue — ordinary compassion is free from desire for the desire realm, while great compassion is free from desire for all three realms. Seventh, a difference in rescuing — ordinary compassion has only the intention to relieve suffering, without the actual deed, while great compassion has both the intention and the deed. Eighth, a difference of finality — ordinary compassion can offer only slight, temporary relief and cannot truly save, while great compassion is able to save permanently, never once abandoning those it saves.

"Moist" reveals its sense of being able to draw together; "smooth" reveals the sense of turning away from what is lost and toward what is virtuous. It is like the water-element, which likewise has two capacities: it is able to draw scattered things together, being smooth rather than rough. Because it is moist, it can draw together; because it is smooth, it is not rough — hence "moist" is spoken of as the cause, and "smooth" as the effect, and this is what is meant by manifesting the sense of cause and fruit. Next, as to inherent purity being the general mark: as in the earlier analogies of real space and the water-element, inherent purity is, in every case, the general mark, since the Tathāgata's nature remains unstained even amid affliction. These marks correspond to the four obstructing afflictions, to what the four kinds of persons fail to attain, to what serves as the basis of the four virtues, to freedom from the four inverted views, and to the antidote that extinguishes saṃsāra — hence it is said that of these marks, one is general and three are particular. The one general mark is simply the mark of inherent purity. The three particular marks are: first, the inconceivable; second, what is to be attained; third, immeasurable virtue. This is called the self-essence mark.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The three similes in this chapter build on one another in a way worth tracing. The wish-fulfilling jewel establishes that buddha-nature is genuinely productive — not an inert possession but something that, once uncovered, spontaneously yields whatever a practitioner's aspiration calls for, the way the jewel does. The clay, silver, and gold vessels establish that this productive nature is exactly the same across every stage of the path — the ordinary person's vessel is coarser than the buddha's, but what fills each vessel is identical, unmeasured by the vessel's own quality. And great compassion's smoothness supplies the missing piece: why the nature moves toward beings at all, rather than simply sitting, uniform and available, waiting to be discovered. The chapter's own etymology is doing real work here — "moist" as cause, drawing scattered things together, and "smooth" as effect, the resulting absence of friction — because it turns great compassion from a virtue buddha-nature happens to have into the very mechanism by which an unchanging, universally shared nature nonetheless reaches out, unevenly, to wherever a particular being's suffering actually is.

Part 9 Complete

This closes the first of the treatise's ten marks. Part 10 turns to the second mark, the cause mark: the four causes — faith and delight in the Mahāyāna, non-discriminating prajñā, the samādhi that breaks through attachment to emptiness, and bodhisattva great compassion — that overcome the four obstructions blocking the icchantika, the non-Buddhist, the śrāvaka, and the pratyekabuddha from seeing their own pure dharmakāya.
