Six Middle Ways

This page maps the nine similes onto buddha-nature's two aspects — abiding in itself, and drawn forth — and, through them, onto the three bodies of a buddha: dharmakāya as true gold, sambhogakāya as a wheel-turning king still in a poor woman's womb, nirmāṇakāya as an image still within its casting mold. It then turns to the dharmakāya's own five marks, unfolding the third and richest of them — freedom from six pairs of extremes — through six of the Buddha's own parables: fearing empty space, a painter frightened by his own monster, an illusionist devoured by his conjured tiger, fire born from two rubbing sticks, and a lamp lit in a darkness centuries old.

Two Natures, Three Bodies

Chinese Source

後五譬佛性者，佛性有二種：一者住自性性、二者引出性，諸佛三身因此二性故得成就。為顯住自性故，說地中寶藏譬。此住自性佛性者，有六種德故如寶藏：一者最難得。佛性亦爾，於無數時節起正勤心，因福德智慧滿足莊嚴方始顯現故。譬如意寶藏，由勝因乃感。二者清淨無垢。由佛性與煩惱不相染故，是故譬如意寶不為不淨所污。三者威神無窮，明六神通等功德圓滿故。如意寶亦爾，隨意能辦故說寶藏譬。四者能莊嚴一切世間功德善根，於一切處相稱可故。如意寶亦爾，能為世間種種莊嚴具。五者最勝，於一切法中無與等故。亦如如意寶，物中最勝故，說寶藏為譬。六者八種世法中無有變異，為十種常住因故。真寶亦爾，雖燒打磨不能改其自性，故取寶藏以譬住自性佛性。

二者引出佛性。從初發意至金剛心，此中佛性名為引出。言引出者，凡有五位：一能出闡提位、二能出外道位、三出聲聞位、四出獨覺位、五出菩薩無明住地位。此法身能破煩惱㲉，其體顯現故。第六說菴羅樹芽為譬，如彼樹芽能破皮肉，得出生為大樹王故。說引出佛性，如菴羅樹芽能生大樹王故。

為約此兩因故，佛說三身果。一者因住自性佛性故說法身。法身有四種功德，是故第七說敗帛裹真金譬。四功德者：一自性有，如金本有，非所造作。二清淨，如金本淨，塵垢不能染污。三為一切功德所依處，如金能感種種貴物故。四平等所得，謂一切眾生並同應得，如金無的主，眾人共有，隨其功力修者即得，故說法身猶如真金。二者因引出佛性故說應身。應身有四種功德，是故第八說如貧賤女人有轉輪王胎。四功德者：一依止，依止者，三十七道品是所依止。二者正生，謂欲得應得，即是未知欲知根。三者正住，謂正得，即是知根。四正受用，即知已根。合此四義，名為應身。如胎中轉輪王子，亦有四義：一以宿業為依止；二未得王位欲得，如初生；三正得王位，如住；四得已不失，如受用，是故應身以胎中轉輪王為譬。三者因引出佛性復出化身。化身者，有三事：一有相，如水中月，以影相為體故；二由功力，以宿願所作故；三有始有終，故第九立摸中佛像為譬。

釋曰：三義者，一有相貌，譬如月影，但似而不真實。化身亦爾，非實體故。二由人工造作者，譬化身宿願所起。三明有始有終者，譬化身隨緣變化，故能有始有終。復次以三義故顯此三身，初甚深義，顯於法身。甚深者，體微細故。故此法身具足五相、五種功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

As to the latter five similes illustrating buddha-nature: buddha-nature is of two kinds — the nature abiding in itself, and the nature drawn forth — and it is on account of these two natures that the three bodies of every buddha come to be accomplished. To reveal the nature abiding in itself, the simile of the buried treasure is given. This buddha-nature resembles a treasure in six respects: it is exceedingly hard to obtain, manifesting only after countless ages of diligent effort; it is pure and stainless, never sullied by impurity; its majestic power is inexhaustible, manifesting the six supernatural powers and every other virtue in full; it is able to adorn every worldly virtue, being fitting in every place; it is supreme, having no equal among all dharmas; and it undergoes no change amid the eight worldly dharmas, being the basis of the ten causes of permanence, just as true treasure's own nature is unaltered even by burning, striking, or polishing.

Second, buddha-nature drawn forth: from the first arising of bodhicitta all the way to the vajra-like mind, buddha-nature throughout this span is called "drawn forth." There are, in all, five stages of being drawn forth: out of the icchantika's position, out of the non-Buddhist's position, out of the śrāvaka's position, out of the pratyekabuddha's position, and out of the bodhisattva's own ground of ignorance — because the dharmakāya, at each stage, is able to break through the shell of affliction, and its own essence thereby becomes manifest. The sixth simile, of the mango sprout, illustrates this: just as that sprout is able to break through the seed's skin and flesh, and emerge to become a great king of trees, so too buddha-nature drawn forth is able to give rise to the great king of trees.

It is with reference to these two causes that the Buddha speaks of the fruit as three bodies. First, on account of the buddha-nature abiding in itself, the dharmakāya is spoken of. The dharmakāya has four virtues, and hence the seventh simile, of true gold wrapped in worn-out rags, is given: it exists inherently, as gold exists inherently, not something manufactured; it is pure, as gold is inherently pure, unable to be stained; it serves as the basis for every virtue, as gold is able to attract every kind of precious thing; and it is equally attained by all sentient beings alike, as gold has no fixed owner, attained by whoever cultivates it with sufficient effort. Second, on account of the buddha-nature drawn forth, the sambhogakāya is spoken of. The sambhogakāya has four virtues, and hence the eighth simile, of the poor and lowly woman carrying a wheel-turning king in her womb, is given: support, the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment being what is relied upon; proper arising, the wish to attain what is to be attained; proper abiding, proper attainment itself; and proper enjoyment, the state of having already attained it. Just as the wheel-turning king's son within the womb likewise has four aspects — prior karma as his support, not yet having the throne but wishing for it, properly attaining the throne, and, having attained it, never losing it — so the sambhogakāya is illustrated by this simile. Third, on account of the buddha-nature drawn forth, the nirmāṇakāya further emerges. The nirmāṇakāya has three aspects: it possesses form, like the moon reflected in water, its essence being a mere reflection; it arises through effort, being made by a prior vow; and it has both a beginning and an end — hence the ninth simile, of the buddha-image still within its mold, is given.

Explanation: as to these three senses — possessing form, like the moon's reflection, resembling the moon yet not being truly real, the nirmāṇakāya is exactly the same, having no real substance. Arising through skilled fashioning illustrates how the nirmāṇakāya arises from a prior vow. Having both a beginning and an end illustrates how the nirmāṇakāya transforms in accordance with conditions, and is thus able to have both. Next, it is by way of three further meanings that these three bodies are revealed: the first, the profound meaning, reveals the dharmakāya. "Profound" means that its essence is subtle. Hence this dharmakāya is complete with five marks and five virtues.

The Dharmakāya's Five Marks: Unconditioned, and Neither One Nor Different

Chinese Source

五相者，一者無為相，離生老等四相過失故。二者無一異相，真與俗諦不一不異。復有二種：一約法辯、二就人論。約法辯不一不異者，為真通故不可言異，以俗別故不得言一。如螺白色，螺之與色不一不二。若言異者，見取螺白色時，不應得於螺解。若言一者，不應有香味觸異，但應是色。二約人論真俗不一不異者，若真與俗一，凡人見俗則應通真；若通真者，應是聖人，以不見真故，故知不一。若言異者，聖人見俗不應通真，若不通真即是凡夫；以聖人見故，不得為異，是故不一不異。

Ru-Yi Translation

The five marks are: first, the mark of the unconditioned, being free from the four faults of arising, aging, and the rest. Second, the mark of neither one nor different, the ultimate and the conventional truths being neither one nor different from one another. This has two aspects: with respect to dharmas, and with respect to persons. With respect to dharmas: because the ultimate pervades universally, it cannot be called "different"; because the conventional is particular, it cannot be called "one." It is like the whiteness of a conch shell — the shell and its color are neither one nor two. If they were different, then, upon perceiving the shell's white color, one ought not thereby gain any understanding of the shell itself. If they were one, there ought to be no difference in scent, taste, or touch — it would have to be simply color alone. With respect to persons: if the ultimate and the conventional were one, then an ordinary person, seeing the conventional, ought thereby to penetrate the ultimate as well, and would then have to be a sage — yet since he does not see the ultimate, we know they are not one. If they were different, then a sage, seeing the conventional, ought not thereby penetrate the ultimate either, and would then have to be an ordinary person — yet, since the sage does see it, they cannot be different. Hence they are neither one nor different.

Six Pairs of Extremes, Six Middle Ways

Chinese Source

三離二邊相者，有六種中道故，能出離六種二邊。何者為六？一者執可滅滅、二者執可畏畏、三者執可執執、四者執正與邪、五者執有作無作、六者執不生同生一。

執可滅與滅者，有人謂言一切諸法畢竟可滅，是名一邊。畢竟滅盡是名為空，復是一邊。因此二邊偏執而生怖畏。為離此二邊偏執故，佛說諸法不有故非可滅，不無故非不滅。非滅非不滅，是名中道，故佛立虛空譬。所以《寶頂經》云：迦葉！譬如有人怖畏虛空，椎胸叫呼，作如是言：「善友！汝等為我除此虛空，除此虛空。」迦葉！於汝意云何？此空為可除不？迦葉言：不可。世尊！佛言：迦葉！若有如是沙門婆羅門怖畏性空，我說是人失心狂亂。所以者何？迦葉！一切諸法並是說空方便。若畏此空，云何不畏一切諸法？若惜諸法，云何不惜此空？問曰：此經為顯何義？答曰：為示一切諸法本性非有故說法空，非關法滅然後得空，故於空性不應生怖。是名離可滅滅二邊顯非能滅所滅中道。

二執可畏畏。二邊者，以分別性所起色等六塵執為實苦是為一邊，生怖畏心復為一邊，此是因依他性執分別性，於中計有實苦而生怖畏。為離此二邊偏執、欲顯中道故，佛以畫師為譬：迦葉！譬如畫師作羅剎像，像甚可畏。畫師見像，自生怖畏，覆面不敢看，失心顛狂。迦葉！如是凡夫由自所作色等諸塵流轉生死，於如是法不能通達如實道理。此譬為顯何義？為明色等諸塵非是實有，但以妄想分別所作。如彼畫師自分別作羅剎惡像，見還生怖。是人亦爾，自於空中而生怖畏。

釋曰：初一譬約聲聞小乘說，此第二譬約大乘說。云何如此？以小乘人於真如中不數習故，故生怖畏。大乘人能數數習空，故不生畏。如師子子，聞師子吼不生怖畏。若不了分別、依他二性，執為實有，故被染污。

三可執執二邊者，分別可執與能執以為實有。為離此二邊故，經中佛以幻師為譬：迦葉！譬如幻師作諸幻像，所作虎等還食幻師。迦葉！如是觀行比丘隨觀一境，顯現唯空故，實無所有、虛無真實。云何能得離此二邊？由依意識生唯識智。唯識智者，即無塵體智。是唯識智若成，則能還滅自本意識。何以故？以塵無體故意識不生，意識不生故唯識自滅故。意識如幻師，唯識智如幻虎。以意識能生唯識故，唯識觀成還能滅於意識。何以故？由塵等無故，意識不生。譬如幻虎還食幻師。如提婆法師說偈言：「意識三有本，諸塵是其因；若見塵無體，有種自然滅。」

四邪正二邊者，正者通達位中真實觀行分別為正，未通達前分別為邪。為離此二邊故，以兩木生火為譬。如經中說：迦葉！譬如二木相揩即能生火，火生之時還自燒木。如是正相真實觀行，與邪相治生聖智根。智根若成，還除邪正二分別故。譬如火生還燒兩木，兩木若盡，火亦無依。邪正不二，故言中道。

五有作無作二邊者，有作者，有人執言，欲修智慧必先作意，然後事成。無作者，有人執言，智慧無事無能。何以故？由解惑相對，由解生故惑自然滅，非解能除，故說智慧無事無能。為離此二，是故立於油燈為譬。如經：迦葉！譬如燃燈，燈光既起，黑暗即滅。而彼燈光雖不作意言能滅暗、暗由我滅。而必因於光起，暗方得滅。是故燈光雖不作意，不無事能。智慧亦爾，不作是意我能滅惑，而亦非不由智慧生惑便除滅，故知智慧不無事能。若說作意我能滅惑，是名增益，即有作邊。若說智慧起時，無明自滅不由智慧，是名損減，即無作邊。為離此二邊故，說智慧生不作意、非不作意；非作故不增，非不作故不減，是名中道。

六不生同生二邊者，一不生執者，譬如凡夫相續中，煩惱恒起，未曾生道，由惑礙故。未來亦爾，故知永不解脫，即是一邊。二同生者，明諸惑於無始長時本有，若對治道與惑同時起者，可能滅惑。若道始生，此道力弱，不能滅惑，故知永不解脫。為離此二邊，是故佛說第二燈譬：迦葉！如嶮暗山巖及廟堂房舍，無數千年暗在其中，未曾有人燃燈照了。設有人能於中燃燈，得成以不？答言：得成。迦葉！此中諸暗得作念言：我住此已久，我今不去。是暗能作此意以不？不可。世尊！何以故？燈光既成，不得不去。迦葉！如是煩惱及業，從無數劫來在眾生相續中，若能生一念正思惟者，則久劫煩惱悉皆自滅。迦葉！是燈光者，即譬聖無分別智。黑暗者，即譬眾生煩惱業。由此燈譬，破道不生執。何以故？以道依因緣生故。若因緣未合，道不得生；因緣具者，道即得生。由如燃燈後方暗滅。暗滅譬者，破同生執，以暗分羸弱故可滅，是無顛倒境界故。以白淨最強有真實境，無顛倒故。是名離六種二邊，顯六種中道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, the mark of freedom from the two extremes: there are six middle ways, by which one is able to escape six kinds of paired extremes. What are the six? The extremes of attachment to what can be destroyed and its destruction; the extremes of attachment to what is fearsome and the fear itself; the extremes of the graspable and the grasping; the extremes of correct and mistaken; the extremes of effort and effortlessness; and the extremes of non-arising and co-arising as one.

As to the first pair: some hold that all dharmas can, in the end, be utterly destroyed — this is one extreme. That their utter destruction is what is called emptiness is likewise an extreme. It is from this one-sided attachment that fear is born. In order to be free of it, the Buddha teaches: dharmas, not being existent, are not what can be destroyed; and, not being non-existent, are not what fails to be destroyed either. Neither destroyed nor not destroyed — this is the middle way, and it is for this reason that the Buddha established the simile of empty space. Hence the Ratnakūṭa Sūtra says: "Kāśyapa! It is like a person who, in fear of empty space, beats his chest and cries aloud, saying: 'Good friends! Remove this empty space for me!' Kāśyapa, can this empty space be removed?" Kāśyapa said: "It cannot, World-Honored One." The Buddha said: "Kāśyapa! If there are śramaṇas or brāhmaṇas who fear the emptiness of their own nature in just this way, I say that such persons have lost their minds and gone mad. Why? Kāśyapa, every dharma is, in every case, taught as a skillful means for expressing emptiness. If one fears this emptiness, why does one not equally fear every dharma? And if one cherishes every dharma, why does one not equally cherish this emptiness?" This shows that all dharmas, by their basic nature, are not existent, and hence emptiness is spoken of — it has nothing to do with a dharma first being destroyed and only then attaining emptiness. Hence one ought not to be afraid of the nature of emptiness. This is the middle way that is neither destroyer nor destroyed.

As to the second pair, the fearsome and fear: attaching to the six sense-objects, arisen from the imagined nature, as truly suffering is one extreme; giving rise to a fearful mind on this account is the other. In order to reveal the middle way, the Buddha gives the simile of the painter: "Kāśyapa! It is like a painter who paints an image of a rākṣasa, an image utterly terrifying. Seeing his own painted image, the painter himself grows afraid, covers his face, dares not look, and loses his mind in terror. Kāśyapa, ordinary beings are exactly the same: revolving in saṃsāra through the sense-objects they themselves have fashioned, they are unable to penetrate the true principle of such things." This shows that form and the other sense-objects are not truly, really existent, but are made entirely by delusive imagination — just as that painter, having himself painted a fearsome image, is then, on seeing it, struck with fear all over again.

Explanation: the first simile is spoken with reference to Hīnayāna practitioners, this second with reference to the Mahāyāna. Why? Because Hīnayāna practitioners have not repeatedly cultivated familiarity with suchness, and so give rise to fear; Mahāyāna practitioners, able to cultivate repeated familiarity with emptiness, do not — like a lion cub, who, hearing a lion's roar, does not become afraid.

As to the third pair, the graspable and the grasping: taking both the imagined object of grasping and the subject that grasps as truly existent. The Buddha gives the simile of the illusionist: "Kāśyapa! It is like an illusionist who conjures illusory images — the conjured tiger then turns and devours the illusionist himself. Kāśyapa, a monk practicing contemplation, observing whatever object he takes up, finds it revealed as sheer emptiness — truly without any existence at all." How does one become free of these two extremes? By depending on mental consciousness to give rise to the wisdom of consciousness-only, the wisdom that knows sense-objects have no independent substance. Once this wisdom is accomplished, it extinguishes, in turn, the very mental consciousness from which it arose — since sense-objects have no substance, the consciousness that grasps them does not arise, and this consciousness's non-arising extinguishes the wisdom of consciousness-only in its turn as well. Mental consciousness is like the illusionist; the wisdom of consciousness-only is like the illusory tiger, turning to devour the very illusionist who conjured it. As the master Āryadeva says in verse: "Mental consciousness is the root of the three realms of existence; sense-objects are its cause. Once one sees that sense-objects have no substance, the seed of consciousness naturally is extinguished."

As to the fourth pair, correct and mistaken: "correct" refers to discrimination within true contemplative practice at the stage of full penetration, "mistaken" to discrimination prior to it. The simile of two sticks producing fire is given: "Kāśyapa! It is like two sticks rubbed together, able to produce fire — and once the fire arises, it turns and burns the very sticks that produced it." In just this way, the mark of correct, true contemplative practice, opposing the mark of the mistaken, gives rise to the root of sagely wisdom — and once this root is accomplished, it removes both discriminations, mistaken and correct, in turn, just as, once fire arises, it burns both sticks, and once they are consumed, the fire, too, has nothing left to depend upon. Mistaken and correct are thus not two separate things — hence the middle way.

As to the fifth pair, effort and effortlessness: some hold that, to cultivate wisdom, one must first form the intention, and only then can the deed be accomplished; others hold that wisdom has no work of its own, since, understanding and delusion being set against one another, once understanding arises, delusion is extinguished of itself, not through any active removal. The simile of the oil lamp is given: "Kāśyapa! It is like lighting a lamp — once the light arises, darkness is dispelled. Yet that lamplight does not form the intention 'I am able to dispel the darkness,' and yet it is necessarily by means of the light's arising that the darkness comes to be dispelled at all." Wisdom is exactly the same: it does not form the intention "I am able to extinguish delusion," and yet delusion is not extinguished without depending on wisdom's arising either. If one says it is by forming the intention that delusion is extinguished, this is superimposition, the extreme of effort. If one says that ignorance is extinguished of itself, not through wisdom at all, this is denial, the extreme of effortlessness. In order to be free of both, it is said that wisdom's arising is neither an act of intention nor without intention — not being an act, it adds nothing; not being without intention, it subtracts nothing. This is the middle way.

As to the sixth pair, non-arising and co-arising as one: the attachment to non-arising holds that, since afflictions constantly arise within an ordinary being's continuum while the path has never once arisen, and the future will be exactly the same, one concludes there can never be any liberation at all. The attachment to co-arising holds that, since afflictions have existed since beginningless ages, only if the antidotal path arose simultaneously with the affliction could it extinguish it — yet the path, when it first arises, is weak, unable to extinguish affliction, so again one concludes there can never be liberation. To be free of both, the Buddha gives a second lamp simile: "Kāśyapa! Consider a perilous, dark mountain cave, in which darkness has remained for countless thousands of years, no one ever having lit a lamp within it — supposing someone lit a lamp there, could this be accomplished?" Answer: "It could." "Kāśyapa! Could the darkness think to itself, 'I have dwelt here so long; I will not leave now'?" "It could not, World-Honored One. Because, once the lamplight is produced, the darkness cannot fail to depart." "Kāśyapa, affliction and karma are exactly the same: though present in sentient beings' continuum from countless eons past, should one give rise, even for a single moment, to correct thought, the afflictions of these countless ages will all, of themselves, be extinguished. This lamplight is the simile for the sages' own non-discriminating wisdom; the darkness is the simile for sentient beings' affliction and karma." By means of this simile, the attachment to "the path does not arise" is broken, since the path arises dependent on causes and conditions — once they are complete, the path is able to arise, just as the darkness is dispelled only after the lamp is lit. And the darkness's dispelling breaks the attachment to co-arising, since darkness, being weak and inferior, a non-veridical state, can be dispelled, while the pure white light, the strongest, constitutes a truly real state free from inversion. This is what is meant by leaving behind these six paired extremes, and revealing the six corresponding middle ways.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

These six parables, all drawn from the Ratnakūṭa Sūtra's Kāśyapa Chapter (迦葉品), share a structure worth naming: each one takes an intuitive fear or a plausible half-truth and shows it collapsing from a direction the listener did not expect. The person afraid of empty space is not told space is dangerous and must be endured — he is told his fear is simply incoherent, since nothing is there to remove. The painter is not told his monster is secretly benign — he is told the fear is entirely self-authored, present nowhere but in his own prior brushstrokes. And the sixth simile, the lamp lit after a thousand years of darkness, answers a worry the treatise has clearly anticipated from its own readers: if affliction is truly beginningless, doesn't that make it effectively permanent, no single moment of practice ever enough to counter so vast an accumulation? The parable's answer is that duration and power don't trade off the way that worry assumes — a single lamp erases centuries of dark in an instant, because darkness was never a substance accumulating strength, only ever the absence of light.
