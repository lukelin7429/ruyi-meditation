The Fruit Mark

This chapter takes up the third mark: the fruit that the four causes of the previous chapter produce — the dharmakāya's four pāramitās of permanence, bliss, self, and purity, quoting directly from the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra's famous reversal of the four ordinary marks of impermanence, suffering, no-self, and impurity. The chapter closes by showing that even bodhisattvas at the ten grounds are still subject to four subtle obstructions, and that the four virtues in their fullest sense belong to the Tathāgata's dharmakāya alone.

Four Inverted Views, and Their Correction

Chinese Source

復次果相義應知。果相者，有二處：一者地前凡聖二位，不得四德。二者十地諸位，地前有如是信樂等四德為清淨佛性因，為對治四倒。如來法身四相功德波羅蜜是其果應知。四倒者，於色等五陰實是無常起於常見、實苦起樂見、實無我起我見、實不淨起淨見，是名四倒。倒者有三義：一見所滅、二修所滅、三非二所滅。見真諦時能除見倒，定破思惑能除想倒，非二所滅能除心倒。

為對治此四，說四無倒。何者為四？於色等五陰未有有、已有應滅，故實無常，如實起無常解。苦時苦故、樂滅時苦故、捨三時苦故，故實是苦，於中生苦解。無常為因、無常為果，由因果得成，以依他執故果不自在。因亦如是，未有有、已有還無。既由前因，是故依他亦不自在。離因果外無別餘法為我，是故無我為實，生無我解。不淨有二種：一色、二非色。色不淨有三，謂初、中、後。初者，始入胎和合，種子不淨。中者，出胎已後，飲食資養多諸不淨。後者，捨身已後，身體壞時，種種不淨故。非色者，或喜、或憂、或惡、或無記、或不離欲諸繫縛等故非色，由此等法故不淨。是以聖人通觀三界皆是不淨。如是五陰，如實不淨，生不淨解。此四皆實，是故非倒。

若約佛性常等四德，此四無倒還成顛倒；為對此倒，是故安立如來法身四德。四德者，一常波羅蜜、二樂波羅蜜、三我波羅蜜、四淨波羅蜜。如《勝鬘經》說：世尊！是諸眾生生顛倒心，於內五取陰，無常見常、苦中見樂、無我見我、不淨見淨。世尊！一切聲聞獨覺由空解未曾見一切智智境，如來法身應修不修故。若大乘人由信世尊故，於如來法身便作常樂我淨等解，是人則不名倒，名得正見。云何如此？世尊！如來法身是常樂我淨諸波羅蜜。若人作是見者名為正見，是如來胸子。胸子者，恒在佛心胸故。復次如來四德波羅蜜，由因次第漸深，應知逆說翻後為前，謂淨我樂常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the fruit mark should be understood. The fruit mark concerns two settings: first, the two stages of the ordinary person and the sage prior to the bodhisattva grounds, who have not yet attained the four virtues; second, the stages of the ten grounds themselves. Prior to the grounds, the four causes — faith and delight and the rest — serve as the pure cause of buddha-nature, functioning as the antidote to the four inverted views. What should be understood as their fruit is the dharmakāya's four marks, virtues, and pāramitās. The four inverted views are: with respect to the five aggregates, which are in truth impermanent, giving rise to the view of permanence; which are in truth suffering, giving rise to the view of bliss; which are in truth without self, giving rise to the view of self; and which are in truth impure, giving rise to the view of purity. Inversion has three aspects: what is eliminated by insight, what is eliminated by cultivation, and what is eliminated by neither. At the moment of seeing the truth, the inversion of view is removed; through settled concentration breaking the afflictions of thought, the inversion of conception is removed; and through what is eliminated by neither, the inversion of mind is removed.

As their antidotes, the four non-inverted views are taught. What are the four? With respect to the five aggregates: what did not previously exist now comes to exist, and what has come to exist must cease — hence they are, in truth, impermanent. Suffering when in pain, suffering when pleasure ceases, and suffering across all three feelings — hence they are, in truth, suffering. Impermanence serves as cause, impermanence as effect, and because they arise through dependence on other conditions, the effect is not self-determining; the cause is likewise so, coming to exist and then returning to non-existence, itself depending on a prior cause. Apart from this chain of cause and effect, there is no further, separate dharma that could serve as a self — hence "without self" is what is real. Impurity is of two kinds, physical and non-physical. Physical impurity is of three kinds: at the beginning, the impurity of the seed at the moment of entering the womb; in the middle, the great impurity of being sustained by food and drink after birth; at the end, every kind of impurity once the body is cast off and decomposes. Non-physical impurity refers to states such as elation, sorrow, unwholesome or indeterminate states, or fetters not yet freed from desire. It is for this reason that the sages, surveying the three realms, see them all as impure. All four of these — impermanence, suffering, no-self, impurity — are real, and are therefore not inversions.

Yet when measured against buddha-nature's own four virtues of permanence and the rest, these four "non-inverted" views themselves become, in turn, inversions — and it is precisely as their antidote that the four virtues of the Tathāgata's dharmakāya are established: the pāramitā of permanence, the pāramitā of bliss, the pāramitā of self, and the pāramitā of purity. As the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra says: "World-Honored One! These sentient beings give rise to an inverted mind, and, with respect to the five aggregates of clinging within, see permanence where there is impermanence, see bliss where there is suffering, see self where there is no self, and see purity where there is impurity. World-Honored One! All śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, through their understanding of emptiness, have never once seen the domain of the wisdom of all wisdoms — the Tathāgata's dharmakāya, which ought to be cultivated, they leave uncultivated. But if a follower of the Mahāyāna, through faith in the World-Honored One, forms, with respect to the Tathāgata's dharmakāya, the understanding of permanence, bliss, self, and purity, this person is not to be called inverted, but is said to have attained right view. How is this so? World-Honored One! The Tathāgata's dharmakāya is precisely the pāramitās of permanence, bliss, self, and purity. One who holds this view holds right view, and is a true child at the Tathāgata's own breast" — meaning one who remains always close to the Buddha's own heart. Furthermore, as to the Tathāgata's four pāramitās, since they deepen in sequence by way of their causes, they should be understood as stated here in reverse order, naming the later first: purity, self, bliss, permanence.

Four Causes, Four Fruits

Chinese Source

由一闡提憎背大乘，為翻彼樂住生死不淨故。修習菩薩信樂大乘法，得淨波羅蜜是其果應知。

由一切外道色等五陰無我性類計執為我，而是色等法與汝執我相相違故恒常無我。諸佛菩薩由真如智至得一切法無我波羅蜜，是無我波羅蜜。與汝所見無我相不相違故，如來說是相恒常無我，是一切法真體性故，故說無我波羅蜜是我。如經偈說：「二空已清淨，得無我勝我，佛得淨性故，無我轉成我。」諸外道等於五取陰中執見有我。為翻其我執虛妄，故修習般若波羅蜜至得最勝無我，即我波羅蜜是其果應知。

由諸聲聞人怖畏生死苦樂，住生死苦滅靜中。為翻此樂意，故修習破虛空三昧，一切相世出世法樂波羅蜜是其果應知。

由獨覺聖人者不觀眾生利益等事，但樂獨處靜住。為翻此意，故修習菩薩大悲為利益眾生事，乃至窮於生死常所持護，常波羅蜜是其果應知。

如是信樂大乘般若波羅蜜、破虛空三昧、菩薩大悲等四因，能成就如來法身四功德波羅蜜，是故佛說由此四德一切如來唯法界為勝。由如虛空，取虛空為邊際極後際之後。如是四句現何等義？由修習信樂大乘法故，諸佛至得最極清淨波羅蜜故，佛說唯法界為勝為上。由修習般若波羅蜜故，至得眾生世界器世界極無我波羅蜜。五陰名眾生世間即人空，國土四大名器世界即是法空，是二空所顯故，故說由如虛空。為修習破空三昧等故，一切處諸法自在如意應得，故取虛空為邊際。由修習菩薩大悲故，於諸眾生常起悲心，護持無有邊際，故說極後際之後。後際之後者，假令後際有後，菩薩大悲亦能過之，是故通辯地前聖凡二位不得四德。

Ru-Yi Translation

On account of the icchantika's hatred and turning away from the Mahāyāna — in order to reverse his delight in dwelling within the impurity of saṃsāra — cultivating the bodhisattva's faith and delight in the Mahāyāna teaching yields, as its fruit, the pāramitā of purity.

On account of all non-Buddhists mistakenly attaching to a self within the five aggregates, which are by nature without self — yet these very dharmas stand in contradiction to the mark of self you attach to, and are therefore permanently without self — the buddhas and bodhisattvas, by means of the wisdom of suchness, attain the pāramitā of no-self with respect to all dharmas. This pāramitā of no-self does not contradict the very mark of "no-self" you yourselves perceive; it is on this basis that the Tathāgata declares this mark to be permanently without self, since it is the true essential nature of all dharmas — and hence the pāramitā of no-self is itself called "the self." As the sūtra says in verse: "The twofold emptiness already purified, one attains a supreme self that is no-self; the Buddha, having attained this pure nature, sees no-self transformed into the self." All the non-Buddhists attach to the view that a self exists within the five aggregates of clinging. In order to reverse this false attachment, cultivating the perfection of prajñā and attaining the most excellent no-self yields, as its fruit, precisely the pāramitā of self.

On account of śrāvakas dreading the suffering of saṃsāra's pleasure and pain, and abiding instead in the stillness of the cessation of saṃsāric suffering — in order to reverse this delight, cultivating the samādhi that breaks through emptiness yields, as its fruit, the pāramitā of bliss with respect to every mark, mundane and supramundane alike.

On account of the pratyekabuddha sage's failure to attend to the benefit of sentient beings, taking delight instead only in dwelling alone in stillness — in order to reverse this attitude, cultivating bodhisattva great compassion for the benefit of sentient beings, sustaining and protecting them without limit throughout the whole of saṃsāra, yields, as its fruit, the pāramitā of permanence.

In this way, these four causes — faith and delight in the Mahāyāna, the perfection of prajñā, the samādhi that breaks through emptiness, and bodhisattva great compassion — are able to accomplish the Tathāgata's dharmakāya's four virtuous pāramitās. It is for this reason that the Buddha says that, by means of these four virtues, all tathāgatas take the dharma-realm alone as supreme — like empty space, taking empty space itself as their outermost limit, beyond even the very last limit. What meaning do these four phrases reveal? Because of cultivating faith and delight in the Mahāyāna teaching, the buddhas attain the most utterly pure pāramitā — hence the dharma-realm alone is said to be supreme and highest. Because of cultivating the perfection of prajñā, they attain the pāramitā of utter no-self with respect to both the world of sentient beings and the world of physical containers — the five aggregates, the world of sentient beings, being the emptiness of person, and the land's four great elements, the world of physical containers, being the emptiness of dharma; it is because these are revealed by this twofold emptiness that it is said to be "like empty space." Because of cultivating the samādhi that breaks through emptiness and the rest, they attain freedom and spontaneity with respect to every dharma in every place — hence empty space itself is taken as their outermost limit. Because of cultivating bodhisattva great compassion, they perpetually give rise to a compassionate mind toward all sentient beings, protecting them without any limit at all — hence, "beyond even the very last limit." Even supposing there were something still beyond the very last limit, bodhisattva great compassion would still be able to surpass it. This concludes the general account of why the two stages prior to the grounds, the ordinary person and the sage, have not yet attained the four virtues.

Four Subtle Obstructions Even at the Ten Grounds

Chinese Source

復次十地由四障故，未得極果四德，金剛後心方乃得之應知。何以故？以出三界外有三種聖人，謂聲聞、獨覺、大力菩薩。住無流界有四種怨障，由此四怨障故不得如來法身四種功德波羅蜜。四怨障者，一方便生死、二因緣生死、三有有生死、四無有生死。

一方便生死者，是無明住地，能生新無漏業，譬如無明生行。或因煩惱方便生同類果，名為因緣，如無明生不善行。若生不同類果，但名方便，如無明生善行、不動行故。今無明住地生新無漏業亦爾，或生同類、或不同類，生福行名為同類，以同緣俗故；生智慧行名不同類，以智是真慧故。是名方便生死。二因緣生死者，是無明住地所生無漏業，是業名為因緣生死。譬如無明所生行是業，但感同類、不生不同類果，善行但生樂果、不善但招苦報，故名因緣生死。方便生死，譬凡夫位；因緣生死，譬須陀洹以上但用故業不生新業。三有有生死者，是無明住地為方便、無漏業為因，三種聖人是意所生身。譬如四取為緣，有漏業為因，三界內生身。有有者，未來生有。更有一生，名為有有。如上流阿那含人於第二生中般涅槃者，餘有一生故，故名有有。四無有生死者，是三聖意生最後身為緣，是不可思惟退墮。譬如生為緣，老死等為過失。是故無明住地為一切煩惱所依止處，而一切煩惱通名無明者，以無明為眾惑根本，根本既未滅盡，由為一切煩惱垢臭穢熏習故，阿羅漢、辟支佛及自在菩薩不能至得無所染污大淨波羅蜜。

復次依此緣，此無明住地微細妄想相遊行未息，故極不能至得無行無想大我波羅蜜。因此無明住地為緣，及微細妄想所起無漏業為因，得起三種意生身故，不能至得極離因果苦大樂波羅蜜。若未證得業難生難滅盡無餘如來甘露界，及未證得不可思惟退墮界，未滅謝故，不能至得極無別異老死等大常波羅蜜。復次應知，無明住地如煩惱難，無漏業如業難，三種意生身如果報難，不可思惟退墮如過失難。若在三種意生身中，則無常樂我淨波羅蜜。故如來法身是常等四波羅蜜，以如來法身一切煩惱習氣皆滅盡故，是名極淨；一切我無我虛妄執滅息故，故名大我；意所生身因果究竟盡故，故名大樂；生死涅槃平等通達故，故名大常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, it should be understood that, at the ten grounds themselves, on account of four obstructions, the four virtues of the ultimate fruit have not yet been attained — they are attained only at the final moment of the vajra-like mind. Why? Because, beyond the three realms, there are three kinds of sages — śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas of great power — who, dwelling within the outflow-free realm, are subject to four enemy-obstructions, on account of which they do not yet attain the Tathāgata's dharmakāya's four virtuous pāramitās. The four are: birth-and-death through skillful means, through causes and conditions, of further existence, and of no further existence.

As to the first, birth-and-death through skillful means: this refers to the ground of ignorance, which is able to give rise to new outflow-free karma, just as ignorance gives rise to karmic formations. When an affliction, by way of skillful means, gives rise to an effect of the same kind, this is "causal and conditional," as when ignorance gives rise to unwholesome action; when it gives rise to an effect of a different kind, this is merely "skillful means," as when ignorance gives rise to wholesome action. The ground of ignorance's giving rise to new outflow-free karma is likewise so: giving rise to meritorious action is of the same kind, since it equally takes the conventional as its condition; giving rise to wise action is of a different kind, since wisdom is true prajñā. As to the second, birth-and-death through causes and conditions: this refers to the outflow-free karma produced by the ground of ignorance, bringing about only an effect of the same kind, as wholesome action produces only a pleasant fruit and unwholesome action only a painful retribution. Birth-and-death through skillful means is comparable to the stage of the ordinary person; birth-and-death through causes and conditions is comparable to the stage from stream-entry onward, where only prior karma is exercised and no new karma is produced. As to the third, birth-and-death of further existence: here the ground of ignorance serves as skillful means, and outflow-free karma serves as cause, giving rise to the mind-made bodies of the three kinds of sages — comparable to how the four kinds of clinging and karma-with-outflow give rise to a body born within the three realms. "Further existence" means a future rebirth still to come, as with an "upstream" non-returner who attains parinirvāṇa in a second, subsequent birth, one further birth still remaining. As to the fourth, birth-and-death of no further existence: here the final mind-made body of the three kinds of sages serves as the condition for what is called an "inconceivable falling back," comparable to how birth itself serves as the condition for old age and death. It is for this reason that the ground of ignorance is the basis on which every affliction depends, and every affliction is, in general, called "ignorance," since ignorance is the very root of all delusion — and since this root has not yet been utterly extinguished, arhats, pratyekabuddhas, and even bodhisattvas of self-mastery, still perfumed by the foul residue of every affliction, are unable to attain the great, unstained pāramitā of purity.

Furthermore, since the subtle mark of delusive thought stirred by this ground of ignorance has not yet come to rest, they are utterly unable to attain the great pāramitā of self, free of activity and free of conception. Because the outflow-free karma arising from subtle delusive thought gives rise to the three kinds of mind-made bodies, they are unable to attain the great pāramitā of bliss, utterly free from the suffering of cause and effect. And so long as one has not yet realized the Tathāgata's ambrosial realm, where the difficulty of karma's arising and of its utter extinction are both overcome, and has not yet realized the realm beyond inconceivable falling back, one is unable to attain the great pāramitā of permanence, utterly free from any distinction such as old age and death. Furthermore, it should be understood that the ground of ignorance corresponds to the difficulty of affliction; outflow-free karma, to the difficulty of karma; the three kinds of mind-made bodies, to the difficulty of karmic retribution; and inconceivable falling back, to the difficulty of fault. So long as one remains within the three kinds of mind-made bodies, one is without the pāramitās of permanence, bliss, self, and purity. It is the Tathāgata's dharmakāya alone that possesses these: because it has utterly extinguished every affliction and its habitual residue, it is called utterly pure; because every false attachment, to self or to no-self, has been stilled, it is called the great self; because the cause and effect of the mind-made body have been utterly exhausted, it is called great bliss; and because saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are equally and fully penetrated, it is called great permanence.

Two Causes for Each of the Four Virtues

Chinese Source

復次四德各有二緣義應知。初有二因緣故，說如來法身有大淨波羅蜜：一者本性清淨名為通相、二者無垢清淨故名別相。本性清淨通聖凡有，故名為通。無垢清淨但佛果有，所以名別。復有二種因緣，說如來法身有大我波羅蜜：一由遠離外道邊見執故無有我執、二由遠離二乘所執無我邊故則無無我妄執，兩執滅息，故說大我波羅蜜。復有二種因緣，說如來法身有大樂波羅蜜：一由一切苦集相滅盡無餘故、拔除習氣相續盡故；二由一切苦滅相證得故，三種意生身滅不更生故，苦滅無餘，是名大樂波羅蜜。復有二種因緣，說如來法身有大常波羅蜜：一無常生死不損減者遠離斷邊、二常住涅槃無增益者遠離常邊，由離此斷常二執故，名大常波羅蜜。故《勝鬘經》說：若見諸行無常，是名斷見，不名正見。若見涅槃常住，是名常見，非是正見。是故如來法身離於二見，名為大常波羅蜜。由此如實法界道理門故，即是涅槃、即是生死，不可分別。即是得入不二法門，亦不一不二，住無住處故。由滅諸惑不住生死，由本願故不住涅槃。由般若故諸惑得滅，由大悲故本願得成。故《不可思量經》偈中說：「諸惑成覺分，生死成涅槃，修習大方便，諸佛叵思議。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, it should be understood that each of the four virtues has two grounds. First, there are two reasons why the Tathāgata's dharmakāya has the great pāramitā of purity: its inherent purity, called the general aspect, shared by both sages and ordinary beings alike; and its purity free of defilement, called the particular aspect, belonging only to the fruit of buddhahood. There are, further, two grounds for the great pāramitā of self: because it is free from the non-Buddhists' extreme attachment to a self, there is no attachment to self; and because it is free from the two vehicles' extreme attachment to no-self, there is no false attachment to no-self either — both these attachments having been stilled, it is called the great pāramitā of self. There are, further, two grounds for the great pāramitā of bliss: because the mark of the arising of all suffering has been utterly extinguished without remainder, its habitual residue entirely uprooted; and because the mark of the cessation of all suffering has been realized, the three kinds of mind-made body, once extinguished, not arising again — this is called the great pāramitā of bliss. There are, further, two grounds for the great pāramitā of permanence: that impermanent saṃsāra suffers no further diminishment, free from the extreme of annihilation; and that abiding, permanent nirvāṇa admits of no further increase, free from the extreme of eternalism. It is by being free from both these extremes that it is called the great pāramitā of permanence. Hence the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra says: "To see all conditioned formations as impermanent is called the view of annihilation, not right view. To see nirvāṇa as permanently abiding is called the view of eternalism, not right view." It is for this reason that the Tathāgata's dharmakāya, free from both these views, is called the great pāramitā of permanence. By way of this gate of the true dharma-realm's own principle, nirvāṇa is precisely saṃsāra, and cannot be separated from it. This is precisely entering the gate of non-duality — being neither one nor two, abiding in the place of non-abiding. By extinguishing all afflictions, one does not abide in saṃsāra; by one's original vow, one does not abide in nirvāṇa either. Through prajñā, afflictions are extinguished; through great compassion, the original vow is fulfilled. Hence the Acintyabuddhaviṣaya Sūtra says, in verse: "The afflictions themselves become factors of awakening; saṃsāra itself becomes nirvāṇa. Cultivating this great skillful means — the buddhas are truly inconceivable."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The closing verse — "the afflictions themselves become factors of awakening; saṃsāra itself becomes nirvāṇa" — can sound, out of context, like a license for spiritual bypassing: if affliction just becomes awakening on its own, why bother with the whole apparatus of four causes and four obstructions this chapter has just spent so much effort laying out? The treatise's own argument rules that reading out. The verse names a result, not a shortcut — it is reached only by the pairing the chapter keeps returning to at every level: prajñā to extinguish the afflictions, great compassion to keep the original vow alive so that extinguishing them does not become mere withdrawal into private peace. Saṃsāra "becomes" nirvāṇa only for someone who has done the very specific work of neither abandoning beings nor being enslaved by delusion — which is exactly why the chapter insists, twice over, that even bodhisattvas at the ten grounds are still working through subtle obstructions no less real for being harder to see than the icchantika's outright hatred of the Mahāyāna.

This closes the treatise's third mark, the fruit mark.
