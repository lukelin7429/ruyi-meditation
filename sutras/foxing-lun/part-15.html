Does the Dharmakāya Exist?

This page describes the dharmakāya's mark, taste, and function, catalogues nine kinds of mental proliferation still built around attachment to a self, and then opens one of the treatise's sharpest debates: an outside interlocutor's argument that, since the dharmakāya is never apprehended by any of the six consciousnesses, it must be decisively non-existent — no different from a rabbit's horns. The treatise's answer turns on an analogy to the meditative power of reading another person's mind, and on what would follow if correct spiritual practice bore no fruit at all.

The Dharmakāya's Mark, Taste, and Function

Chinese Source

復次說法身相者，諸苦靜息是法身相，為靜苦緣故。復次說法身味。味者有二：一不退墮故名為味、二安樂故名為味。眾生在生死中乃至夢中並未曾見；若修正行人求見此法，得見之時即得不退安樂，故以安樂為味。復次說法身事。事者，以無相為事，五陰相於中盡無餘故。又以無戲論為事。戲論有三：一貪愛、二我慢、三諸見，是三戲論如來滅之已盡，故以無戲論為事。戲論者有三義：一能違礙實理、二名虛誑世間、三障隔解脫，初違正境，次違正行，後違正得。合此三義，名為戲論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the mark of the dharmakāya is spoken of: the stilling of every suffering is the mark of the dharmakāya, for it is the condition on which suffering is stilled. Next, the taste of the dharmakāya is spoken of. "Taste" carries two senses: it is called taste because it is non-retrogressing, and it is called taste because it is blissful. Sentient beings, throughout saṃsāra, have never once seen it, even in their dreams; but if a practitioner of correct conduct seeks to see this dharma, then, at the moment of seeing it, he attains bliss that never retrogresses — hence bliss is taken as its taste. Next, the function of the dharmakāya is spoken of. "Function" here means having signlessness as its function, since the marks of the five aggregates are, within it, utterly extinguished without remainder. It also means having freedom from mental proliferation as its function. Mental proliferation is of three kinds: craving, conceit, and views — these three, the Tathāgata has already utterly extinguished, and hence freedom from mental proliferation is its function. Mental proliferation carries three senses: it is able to obstruct true principle; it is called false and deceptive with respect to the world; and it obstructs and blocks liberation — the first violating the correct object, the second violating correct practice, the third violating correct attainment. Combining these three senses is what is called mental proliferation.

Nine Kinds of Mental Proliferation About the Self

Chinese Source

又戲論有九種：一通計我、二的計是我、三計我應生、四計我不更生、五計我有色應生、六計我無色應生、七計我有想應生、八計我無想應生、九計我非想非非想應生。一通計我者，於五陰中通執有我，而不能分別即離，但漫執故。二是我者，於現世五陰中隨取一陰為我。而言是者，是的別義，定是二處：一定在現世有、二定在一陰上執，故名為是。

三計我應生者，一切諸見不出有無二種，由有見故所以執常。於無見中復有二種：一邪見者，謂一切無因無果，並撥三世故；二斷見者，謂唯有現在，不信未來故。四計我不更生者，此計因斷見起。

五計我有色應生者，於欲色二界中以色為我，此執則因常見故起。六計我無色應生者，於無色界中計受心法為我。觀色壞滅，此三法不滅，因常見故起。七計我有想應生者，於三界中除無想及非想天，所餘諸處並計有想為我，因常見起。八計我無想應生者，計無想天及草木等為我，以同無想心故，由因常見起。九計我非想非非想應生者，此計有頂處為我，以觀想為繫縛，計涅槃為坑塹，若不除想無由解脫，有繫縛故。若併除想，復恐失我，墮涅槃塹。何以故？想與於我不得相離故。不得棄及與不棄，為繫縛故欲除於想，故名非想；恐失我故不敢併除，故名非非想。由此散亂心，不得涅槃，故說此等名為戲論。若能觀證法身，一切戲論並不復生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, mental proliferation is of nine kinds: general attachment to a self, specific attachment that this is the self, the view that the self will be reborn, the view that the self will not be reborn again, the view that the self will be reborn with form, the view that the self will be reborn without form, the view that the self will be reborn with perception, the view that the self will be reborn without perception, and the view that the self will be reborn into neither-perception-nor-non-perception. As to the first, general attachment: this attaches, generally, to the existence of a self somewhere among the five aggregates, without being able to distinguish whether the self is identical with or separate from them — a diffuse attachment only. As to the second, this-is-the-self: this takes some one of the five aggregates in the present life and attaches to it, specifically, as the self.

As to the third, the view that the self will be reborn: every view falls into one of two kinds, existence or non-existence — holding the view of existence gives rise to attachment to eternalism. Within the view of non-existence, there are, further, two kinds: wrong view, which holds that there is no cause and no effect at all, and denies the three times entirely; and the view of annihilation, which holds that only the present exists, and does not believe in any future. As to the fourth, the view that the self will not be reborn again: this view arises on the basis of the view of annihilation.

As to the fifth, the view that the self will be reborn with form: within the desire and form realms, one takes form as the self — this attachment arises on the basis of the view of eternalism. As to the sixth, the view that the self will be reborn without form: within the formless realm, one takes feeling and the mental dharmas as the self, observing that form has perished while these dharmas have not — this too arises on the basis of eternalism. As to the seventh, the view that the self will be reborn with perception: throughout the three realms, apart from the heaven of no-perception and the peak of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, in every other place one takes perception to be the self. As to the eighth, the view that the self will be reborn without perception: one takes the heaven of no-perception, or even plants and trees, to be the self, on account of their sharing a mind free of perception. As to the ninth, the view that the self will be reborn into neither-perception-nor-non-perception: this takes the peak of existence as the self, regarding perception itself as a fetter and nirvāṇa as a pit — without eliminating perception there is no liberation, since it is a fetter, yet if one eliminates it entirely, one fears losing the self and falling into the pit of nirvāṇa, since perception and self, on this view, cannot be separated. Unable either to discard it or not to discard it, wishing to eliminate it as a fetter, this is called "no perception"; yet fearing the loss of self, one dares not eliminate it entirely, and so it is called "not no-perception" either. It is because of this very confused mind that nirvāṇa cannot be attained — hence all these are called mental proliferation. If one is able to contemplate and realize the dharmakāya, no mental proliferation whatsoever arises again.

An Objection: The Dharmakāya Is Like a Rabbit's Horns

Chinese Source

外曰：於法身中何用立此相等諸義？如汝所立，法身應決定是無，不可執故。若物非六識所得，決定是無，如兔角。兔角者，非六識所得，定是無故。法身亦爾，是故法身決定是無，何用諸義？

答曰：汝言非六識所見故法身無者，是義不然。何以故？以由方便能證涅槃故。想稱正行是名方便，由此方便是故法身可知可見，譬如由他心通故則能得見出世聖心。

釋曰：他心通者，有三種因緣所得，兩是方便，一是正道方便，二者一因天耳、二由天眼。因天耳故聞覺觀聲，由此聲故得知他心。依天眼能見他肉心孔中有水水相，若黑則知癡、生黃則知貪、赤則知瞋、青白則知善、見縹色時知是無記。因於耳目方便故，比知他心。次正道者，若欲得他心通，須緣自心。先修觀行不用現在心觀現世，心自體不得一時見故，以現在心能觀過去心。何以故？可追緣故，從遠至近，次第向後。初則觀無量念，如是漸漸至一剎那。乃至滅一剎那，於自心觀中而得自在，然後取前人心作自境界以修觀行。初入觀時須作願心，起要期意。先須假想觀前人身身相具足，如是遣析除皮肉骨三相都盡，唯餘心在，細細修習。緣前人心，隨其利鈍、遠近、奢促，自能徹見。如彼所緣，我皆能見種種諸心故。如他聖心，雖過六根境，亦能得見。如來法身亦復如是，雖非六識所見，由方便正行所以能見，故知是有、不得同無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: "What use is there in establishing all these marks and meanings with respect to the dharmakāya? On your own account, the dharmakāya ought to be decisively non-existent, since it cannot be grasped at all. If a thing is not apprehended by any of the six consciousnesses, it is decisively non-existent — as with a rabbit's horns. A rabbit's horns, not being apprehended by any of the six consciousnesses, are decisively non-existent. The dharmakāya is exactly the same — hence the dharmakāya is decisively non-existent. What use, then, is there in all these meanings?"

Answer: "Your claim that the dharmakāya does not exist because it is not seen by the six consciousnesses does not hold. Why? Because nirvāṇa can be realized by means of skillful means. Thought that accords with correct practice is what is called skillful means, and it is by means of this skillful means that the dharmakāya can be known and seen — just as, by means of the meditative power of knowing another's mind, one is able to see the transcendent mind of a sage."

Explanation: the power of knowing another's mind is attained through three causes and conditions — two of them being external means, one being the proper means of the path itself. The two are: by way of the divine ear, and by way of the divine eye. By way of the divine ear, one hears the sounds of another's reflective thought, and thereby comes to know their mind. By way of the divine eye, one is able to see, within the aperture of another's fleshly heart, a watery reflection — black indicating delusion, yellow indicating craving, red indicating anger, blue-white indicating wholesomeness, and pale blue-grey indicating an indeterminate state. It is by means of these two, ear and eye, that one comes to infer another's mind. As to the proper means of the path itself: if one wishes to attain the power of knowing another's mind, one must first take one's own mind as the object. First, one cultivates contemplation without using the present mind to observe the present world, since the mind's own substance cannot be seen at one and the same moment that it is doing the seeing — instead, one uses the present mind to observe a past mind, since a past mind can still be traced and taken as an object, moving from what is more distant to what is nearer, in due sequence, backward through time. At first, one observes an immeasurable number of moments of thought; then, gradually, one narrows this down to a single instant, until, even within that single instant, one attains mastery over one's own mind in contemplation. Only then does one take another person's mind as one's own object, and cultivate contemplation upon it. On first entering this contemplation, one must form a resolved intention, giving rise to a firm resolve. First, one must imaginatively visualize the other person's body, complete with all its physical marks, and then progressively strip away the marks of skin, flesh, and bone, until nothing remains but the mind — cultivating this, ever more finely. Taking hold of that other person's mind, whether sharp or dull, near or far, hurried or slow, one becomes able to see through to it entirely of oneself. Just as one is thus able to see whatever mind is taken as an object, in all its variety, so too, though a sage's own mind lies beyond the reach of the six sense-faculties and their objects, it too can still be seen. The Tathāgata's dharmakāya is exactly the same: though it is not seen by the six consciousnesses, it can nonetheless be known and seen by means of skillful means and correct practice — hence we know it exists, and cannot be equated with non-existence.

If the Dharmakāya Did Not Exist, Practice Would Be Fruitless

Chinese Source

復次更有別義知法身不無。何以故？若法身無者，則諸正行皆應空失。以正見為先行，攝戒定慧等善法故，所修正行不空無果，由此正行能得果故，故知法身非無。若汝說法身定無，而正行能令至五陰入等滅盡故，當知正行不無果者。是義不然。何以故？涅槃不有故。若五陰等無是涅槃者，則去來二世陰等並無應是涅槃，而此二世陰等無處既非涅槃，故知不取陰無之處名為涅槃。又若汝取此為涅槃者，狂醉等人應有涅槃。而其無者，故知陰等無處非涅槃也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, there is another sense in which one may know the dharmakāya is not non-existent. Why? Because if the dharmakāya did not exist, then every instance of correct practice would have to come to nothing. Since correct practice takes right view as its guide, and gathers in wholesome dharmas such as discipline, concentration, and wisdom, the correct practice one cultivates is not empty of fruit — and it is because this correct practice is able to attain its fruit that we know the dharmakāya is not non-existent. If you say that the dharmakāya is decisively non-existent, yet correct practice is able to bring about the utter extinction of the five aggregates and sense-fields, and that this shows correct practice is not without fruit — this does not hold. Why? Because, on this reading, nirvāṇa would not truly exist. If the mere absence of the five aggregates were nirvāṇa, then the absence of aggregates in the past and future would equally have to be nirvāṇa — yet this is not, in fact, held to be nirvāṇa. Hence we know that a mere place of the aggregates' absence is not what is called nirvāṇa. And further, if you take this as nirvāṇa, then a person who is insane or drunk ought equally to possess nirvāṇa — yet they do not. Hence we know that a mere place where the aggregates are absent is not nirvāṇa.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The mind-reading analogy is a carefully chosen answer to the rabbit's-horns objection, because it grants the objector's premise rather than disputing it: the dharmakāya genuinely is not an object of the six ordinary consciousnesses, exactly as claimed. What the analogy disputes is the inference from "not an object of ordinary perception" to "non-existent." A meditator's own mind, and a fortiori another person's mind, is likewise never an object of the six consciousnesses in the way a color or a sound is — visual consciousness sees form, not mind — yet the treatise takes the traditional attainment of reading another's mind as uncontroversial proof that something real can be known by a method other than the six senses. The drunk-and-insane counterexample in the next section is doing complementary work: it blocks the cheaper alternative reading, where "nirvāṇa" just means the five aggregates' cessation with no further positive reality behind it, by pointing out that on that reading, unconsciousness itself would count as liberation — which nobody, including the objector, actually believes.

Part 15 Complete

The debate over whether the dharmakāya exists continues to build. Part 16 follows it through its most intricate turn: the outside interlocutor's retreat to defining nirvāṇa as simply "the present-tense absence of the five aggregates," and the treatise's extended dismantling of that position — arguing that on such a reading, the very practice of the eightfold path could never coherently produce nirvāṇa at all — before the chapter closes with the treatise's own positive account of nirvāṇa as neither form nor non-form, and the conclusion of the entire mark of total inclusion.
