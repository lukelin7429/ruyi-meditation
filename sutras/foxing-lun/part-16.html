What Nirvāṇa Is Not

This page completes the treatise's longest chapter, the mark of total inclusion, by working through every plausible way an interlocutor might try to reduce nirvāṇa to something simpler than what the treatise claims it is — the present-tense absence of the five aggregates, mere cessation, mere non-arising of affliction — and showing that each reading collapses on its own terms. The chapter closes with the treatise's own account of why nirvāṇa alone, among all dharmas, is genuinely permanent, unlike even the non-Buddhists' supposedly eternal atoms.

Why "Present-Tense Absence" Cannot Be Nirvāṇa

Chinese Source

又若汝言現世五陰無為涅槃者，是義不然。何以故？無是無故。若法現在則非是無，更互相違故。若法已滅則非現在，若現在則不滅，以有無不得並故；復次依現在陰修聖道時，不應得涅槃故。八聖道者依現在五陰得成，是時現世五陰實有未無，此中永無得涅槃義。云何如此？汝立計言現世五陰無處名為涅槃。依現五陰修八聖道得於涅槃，是故汝執現世五陰無名涅槃者，是義不然。若汝言是時煩惱非現在故，無過失者。是亦不然，若汝言修八聖道時五陰現在者，則諸煩惱非是現世，是時煩惱不得生故。由八聖道得證得見，是故現世得涅槃者，是義不然。何以故？煩惱先非現世故，修道則無用，修聖道時是諸煩惱未除滅故。汝見非相應，便言非現世，若隨逆流。初修行時煩惱已不相應，此不相應則非現世，此即是汝所計涅槃。若汝執此是涅槃者，聖道未起時已應滅惑，已得涅槃故，後修聖道則無復用。是八聖道能滅諸惑及得涅槃者是義不可，故知煩惱滅處不名涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, if you say that nirvāṇa is simply the present-tense absence of the five aggregates — this does not hold. Why? Because non-existence is non-existence. If a dharma is present, it is not non-existent, since the two directly contradict one another; if a dharma has already ceased, it is not present — for if it were present, it could not yet have ceased, since existence and non-existence cannot coincide. Furthermore, at the very moment one cultivates the noble path by relying on the present aggregates, one ought not to attain nirvāṇa at all. The eightfold noble path is accomplished by depending on the present five aggregates — and at that very moment, the present five aggregates truly exist and are not yet absent, so there could, in this case, never be any sense of attaining nirvāṇa. How is this so? You have posited that nirvāṇa means the absence of the present five aggregates. Yet it is precisely by relying on the present five aggregates that one cultivates the eightfold noble path and thereby attains nirvāṇa — so your position does not hold. If you say that, at that moment, the afflictions are simply not present, and so no fault follows, this too does not hold. If you say that, at the time one cultivates the eightfold noble path, the five aggregates are present, then the afflictions would have to be not present at that very time, since afflictions cannot arise then. But to say that nirvāṇa is attained in the present, by means of realizing and seeing through the eightfold noble path, does not hold either. Why? Because if the afflictions were, from the start, simply not present, cultivating the path would serve no purpose — yet, at the time one cultivates the noble path, these very afflictions have not yet been removed. You see that they are "not corresponding" to the mind at that moment, and on this basis call them "not present" — but this runs contrary to the actual order of events. At the very start of practice, the afflictions have already ceased to correspond to the mind — and this "non-correspondence," on your reading, would already be "not present," which is exactly what you take to be nirvāṇa. If you insist that this is nirvāṇa, then, before the noble path had even arisen, the affliction would already have to be extinguished, and nirvāṇa already attained — in which case the subsequent cultivation of the noble path would serve no further purpose at all. So the claim that the eightfold noble path is what extinguishes the afflictions and attains nirvāṇa cannot be sustained on this reading — hence we know that a mere place where afflictions have ceased is not what is called nirvāṇa.

Why Mere Cessation Cannot Be the Dharmakāya

Chinese Source

若汝說由聖言故，欲等滅盡名為涅槃，如《吉祥經》偈說：「滅盡及離欲，無死墮微妙，若人見此經，成佛得寂滅。無法與此等，若證則無憂，是真妙法寶，由是義吉祥。」若汝言欲等諸惑非現世非有是盡，由此三義名滅諦者。是義不然。何以故？第四果同此義故。佛經中說：何者名阿羅漢果？以三界惑盡名羅漢果。但以欲等滅為果，此果實不如此，正以出世果報四陰戒等五身為羅漢果，但由此惑滅，羅漢果得成故，於果中說此因名。涅槃亦爾，由涅槃故諸惑得滅，是故因中說於果名。是故汝說由八聖道能得涅槃道不空者，是義不然。

復次法身，住時短促、有為法相、非功用得，是三過失，於汝義則壞。若汝所說欲等惑滅說名法身，汝所立義中則有三失：一是住時短促。短促者，諸法念念不住是名滅盡，以滅盡故不得暫住。汝云何立此滅盡義為法身耶？以短促時故，失法身常義。二有為法相過失者，若此滅盡是法身，法身則是有為法相。法身若是有為相者，無有是處。三有非功用過失者，此一切諸法依因緣滅則是自性。何以故？滅為本故。若滅非功用得，法身亦不因功用而得。既無功而得者，睡眠狂醉等亦應皆得涅槃。以此三失故，汝義不成。復次若滅盡為涅槃者，則與有為諸法相應，以滅有同動苦二法故。夫有為法皆為欲等惑火所燃，故恒散動不住，則為生老死等所壞，是故恒苦。如佛說言：比丘！生老死等是有為法。故一切有為恒燃恒苦。是死者即是滅盡，此滅盡即有為。若是有為，故知汝立法身不得是常。既非常者，汝義寧立？何以故？則汝義計最靜為動、最樂為苦，是故不然。

若汝說從此一滅永無復生為涅槃者。是義不然。何以故？不離前諸過失故。汝言永滅者，生與死滅不異故，是義不可。若汝說永滅為涅槃者，道亦應成涅槃。何以故？無有一滅永不復生。無有此法，我今隨汝意說我願有此滅。若有此滅，終不能離前諸失故。又道滅後煩惱亦復不生。若謂永滅是涅槃者，是道用滅應是涅槃。又若汝說能滅為涅槃者，無別涅槃以為能滅，唯以道用正為能滅，故知道體應能為滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

If you say that, on the authority of the sages' own words, the utter extinction of desire and the rest is what is called nirvāṇa — as the Maṅgala Sūtra says in verse: "Utter extinction and freedom from desire, the deathless, subtle and sublime — whoever sees this teaching attains buddhahood and quiescent extinction. No dharma equals this; whoever realizes it has no more sorrow; this is the true and wondrous jewel of the Dharma — for this reason it is called auspicious" — and if you say that desire and the like being not present, not existent, and utterly extinguished together constitute what is named the truth of cessation, this does not hold. Why? Because this would be the same error as wrongly identifying the fourth fruit of arhatship with mere cessation. The Buddhist scriptures ask: what is called the fruit of arhatship? It is called the fruit of arhatship because the afflictions of the three realms are extinguished. But to take the mere extinction of desire as the fruit itself is, in truth, not correct — properly speaking, it is the supramundane resultant body of the mental aggregates together with morality and the rest that constitutes the fruit of arhatship; it is only because affliction has been extinguished that the fruit of arhatship comes to be accomplished, and so, within the fruit, this causal name is spoken of. Nirvāṇa is exactly the same: it is because of nirvāṇa that the afflictions come to be extinguished — hence, within the cause, the name of the fruit is spoken of. For this reason, your claim does not hold as you have construed it.

Furthermore, if the dharmakāya were merely this cessation, it would incur three faults — brief duration, being a conditioned mark, and not being attained through effort — and your position would collapse under them. First, brief duration: dharmas perish moment by moment, and this is called "utter extinction" — being utter extinction, it cannot abide even briefly. How, then, can you posit this extinction as the dharmakāya? Because of its brief duration, it loses the sense of the dharmakāya's permanence. Second, the fault of being a conditioned mark: if this extinction were the dharmakāya, the dharmakāya would then be a conditioned mark — and there is no basis for the dharmakāya to be a conditioned mark. Third, the fault of not requiring effort: every dharma, when it ceases dependent on causes and conditions, does so by its own nature, since cessation is simply the basic tendency of things. If cessation is attained without any effort, then the dharmakāya, too, would be attained without effort — but if it could be attained effortlessly, then even sleep or drunken stupor ought equally to attain nirvāṇa. On account of these three faults, your position cannot stand. Furthermore, if utter extinction were nirvāṇa, it would then correspond to every conditioned dharma, since this "extinction" would share both the marks of instability and suffering that belong to conditioned things. Every conditioned dharma is, in every case, burned by the fire of desire and its afflictions, and hence is constantly restless and unstable, and is destroyed by aging and death — hence it is constantly suffering. As the Buddha said: "Monks! Birth, aging, and death are conditioned dharmas." Hence every conditioned thing constantly burns and constantly suffers. This "death" is precisely utter extinction, and this utter extinction is precisely a conditioned thing. If it is conditioned, then we know that your proposed dharmakāya cannot be permanent. And if it is not permanent, how can your position possibly stand? Why? Because your position would then be reckoning the very stillest as motion, and the very blissful as suffering — and this cannot be right.

If you say that nirvāṇa is this single cessation after which there is never any further arising — this does not hold either. Why? Because it does not escape the very faults just described. Your claim of "permanent cessation" would make no distinction between birth-and-death's own cessation and any other cessation — this cannot be right. If you say that permanent cessation is nirvāṇa, then even the ordinary functioning of the path itself would have to become nirvāṇa. Why? Because there is, in fact, no such thing as a cessation that never again gives rise to anything — no such dharma exists; but suppose, for the sake of argument, I grant your position — even so, it could never escape the faults already described. Moreover, once the path has extinguished affliction, the afflictions do not arise again either — so if you hold that this permanent cessation is nirvāṇa, then the mere cessation of the path's own function would have to be nirvāṇa. And further, if you say that what does the extinguishing is itself nirvāṇa, then there would be no separate nirvāṇa apart from this extinguishing function — it would be solely the function of the path that truly does the extinguishing, and so we would have to conclude that the very substance of the path itself must be capable of extinguishing itself.

Why "Non-Arising of Affliction" Fails Too

Chinese Source

若汝說煩惱不生為涅槃者。是義不然。何以故？不免二種失故。若煩惱不生，即與滅盡同體，如前死滅等五難過失故。是不生者，為在道前、為在道後？若在道前，未修道時已應得之，修道無用。若在道後，則屬未來，過三世義則不成立。又汝立無生，其義云何？為是有惑故無生、為是無惑故無生？若煩惱有，則無無生。何以故？有無二法不得一時同處故。譬如一人行時不住、住時不行，以二法相違，行住不得俱故。故知煩惱在時則不得立，以無生故。若無煩惱立無生者，是時煩惱既無，約誰而辯無生耶？譬如人說石女之女不能生兒。

復次汝立煩惱無生者，為是物有、為非物有？是物有者，如四大等有實法有，假名此等是有物。非物有者，如空花兔角等，了自無故，故言非物有。若言是有物者，則由四緣所成。四緣所成故，無生義則不立。以是義故，有為無為則無差別。若汝說是物有，不為四緣成者，即同邪見外道所執自性、隣虛、我、時、方等故。若非有物是涅槃者，但有名字，便如人說兔角尖利。若爾，即同前破，則修道無用。

Ru-Yi Translation

If you say that the non-arising of affliction is nirvāṇa — this does not hold either. Why? Because it cannot escape two faults. If the affliction simply does not arise, this is the same in substance as utter extinction, and incurs the same faults already described concerning death and cessation. As for this "non-arising": does it occur before the path, or after it? If before, then, even prior to cultivating the path, one ought already to have attained it, and cultivating the path would serve no purpose. If after, then it belongs to the future — and this fails to establish any coherent relation to the three times at all. And further, what exactly do you mean by positing "non-arising"? Is it non-arising because affliction exists, or non-arising because affliction does not exist? If affliction exists, then there is no non-arising at all. Why? Because existence and non-existence cannot occupy the same place at the same time — just as a person cannot be both moving and standing still at once. Hence we know that, so long as affliction is present, "non-arising" cannot be established. And if you posit "non-arising" on the basis that affliction is absent, then, affliction already being absent at that point, with respect to what would "non-arising" even be asserted? It would be like saying that the daughter of a barren woman cannot bear a child.

Furthermore, when you posit that the non-arising of affliction is your nirvāṇa, is this non-arising itself something that exists as a thing, or something that does not exist as a thing? If it exists as a thing, then, like the four great elements and other really existing dharmas, it would be conventionally designated as an existing thing. If it does not exist as a thing, then, like sky-flowers or a rabbit's horns, understood to be simply non-existent, it would be said not to exist as a thing at all. If you say it exists as a thing, then it must be accomplished by the four kinds of conditions — and if so, the sense of "non-arising" cannot be established, for there would then be no difference at all between the conditioned and the unconditioned. If you say it exists as a thing yet is not accomplished by the four conditions, this is exactly the same as the wrong view held by non-Buddhists who attach to self-nature, atoms, self, time, and space as uncaused. And if this "non-existent thing" is what nirvāṇa is, then it is nothing more than a name — like someone speaking of a rabbit's horn as "sharp." If this is so, it falls under exactly the same refutation as before, and cultivating the path would serve no purpose at all.

A Coarse Name for a Subtle Reality, and Nirvāṇa's True Permanence

Chinese Source

外曰：若不取無生為涅槃者，云何佛說無生滅盡為涅槃耶？答曰：道依涅槃能使煩惱未來不生、現在者滅，因中說果，故名涅槃為無生滅盡。外曰：何故果說因名而不直取果體自名？答曰：極微細故。一切法中無有能細涅槃之者，是故若得此法，一切欲等諸惑滅不更生，故假此麁名顯以細理，理相可知，故此經中作此說也。

問曰：云何知此涅槃極微細耶？答曰：大仙不樂說法故，無分別聖智所知故。大師觀涅槃極微細故，觀眾生根性不相稱故，是故佛心迴向寂靜入於涅槃，不欲說法。故經言：我寧不說法，疾入於涅槃故。無分別聖智所知者，如《摩健持經》說：世尊！若涅槃是有，我今自有聰明利智，云何不見？佛言：涅槃實有，汝今未得無分別聖智故不得見。以微細故，非聞慧所得；以真實故，非思慧所得。思慧雙能通達真俗諦故，不得周於妙理無思之界。以甚深故，非世間修慧所得。修慧但得淺理，未能通達甚深之理，以此微細非言語能說故，借麁名顯於妙理。由無分別聖智所見故，故言微細得成。

外曰：若涅槃是修道所得者，與未能修道之人不得相應，既不相應是故不共，若不共則是無常。答曰：若從此向前是無、向後是有者，可說無常。無此義故，前際無有故說無生。若汝說未修道時不能得故無無生者，是義不然。何以故？非道所生故。此法必須因道得至，非道能生，是故未修時不得言無，是故無生義立。無後際故，是故無滅。中際無業煩惱等，故無變異。以無生異滅等三失，故說常住，不同外道以無因故一名常住也。

正義者不取因為常住法，如涅槃不由因故有。汝謂隣虛等亦不由因故有，無如是義。何以故？涅槃離有離無、四謗不及，以不由因，故得是常。汝計隣虛既有，有故不得無因，是有因即無常故。若汝說涅槃無三失故常住，隣虛等亦無三失，故常住者。是義不然。何以故？汝證義相不成就故。涅槃常住不與隣虛同相異相，隣虛等是有分別故，無有證義得成；涅槃是無分別故，涅槃常住，隣虛等常住則不得立。復次隣虛等亦不成就。何以故？以獨聚不成故。獨者義不成，以四大不相離故。若聚者亦不成就，以相麁故，並非隣虛塵故。若離涅槃，無有一法是常住故。以涅槃實有常住，依方便得解脫故、修道不空過故，故有涅槃。前際等無故，故知常住。過色等相故，故說非色。不離清涼等色相故，故說非非色。大功用無分別智所得故，故說真有。因出世大精進所成就道佛所得故，故知實有。如經中說：比丘！是法實有，不生不起、不作無為。故知涅槃實常住。此法是如來轉依，是故名總攝竟，亦云相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

Outsider: "If you do not take non-arising to be nirvāṇa, then why does the Buddha say that non-arising and utter extinction are nirvāṇa?" Answer: "The path, by depending on nirvāṇa, is able to make future afflictions not arise and present ones cease — the cause is here spoken of in terms of the fruit, and hence nirvāṇa is called 'non-arising and utter extinction.'" Outsider: "Why speak of the fruit using the name of its cause, rather than taking the fruit's own essence and naming it directly?" Answer: "Because it is utterly subtle. Among all dharmas, there is nothing more subtle than nirvāṇa — hence, when this dharma is attained, all afflictions such as desire are extinguished and never arise again, and so this coarser name is provisionally borrowed to point toward the subtle principle."

Question: "How do we know that this nirvāṇa is utterly subtle?" Answer: "Because the Great Sage was reluctant to teach the Dharma at all, and because it is known only by the sages' own non-discriminating wisdom. The Teacher, observing that nirvāṇa is utterly subtle, and observing that sentient beings' own faculties did not match it, therefore inclined his mind toward stillness and entering nirvāṇa, and had no wish to teach the Dharma — hence the sūtra says: 'I would rather not teach the Dharma, and swiftly enter nirvāṇa.' As for its being known only by non-discriminating sagely wisdom: as the sūtra says, 'World-Honored One! If nirvāṇa truly exists, then, having sharp and clear intelligence of my own, why do I not see it?' The Buddha replied: 'Nirvāṇa truly exists — it is only because you have not yet attained non-discriminating sagely wisdom that you cannot see it.' Because it is subtle, it cannot be attained through the wisdom of hearing; because it is real, it cannot be attained through the wisdom of reflection, which, though able to penetrate both the ultimate and conventional truths, cannot encompass the wondrous principle's realm beyond all thought. Because it is utterly profound, it cannot be attained through the wisdom of mundane cultivation, which attains only shallow principles. It is because this subtlety cannot be expressed in words at all that a coarser name is borrowed to point toward the wondrous principle."

Outsider: "If nirvāṇa is something attained through cultivating the path, then it is not shared in common with a person who has not yet cultivated the path — and, not being shared in common, it must be impermanent." Answer: "Impermanence could only be said of something that is absent up to a certain point and then present afterward. Since this is not the case, we say there is no arising even at the earlier point. If you say that, because it cannot be attained before cultivating the path, there is therefore no such thing as 'non-arising' — this does not hold. Why? Because nirvāṇa is not, in fact, produced by the path at all. This dharma must be reached by means of the path, but the path does not generate it — hence, even before it is cultivated, one cannot say nirvāṇa is absent, and so the sense of 'non-arising' is established. Having no later boundary, it likewise has no cessation. And having, in the interim, no karma or affliction, it has no change either. Because it is free of these three faults — arising, alteration, and cessation — it is said to be permanently abiding, and this is not at all the same as the non-Buddhists' calling something 'permanent' merely because it is uncaused."

The correct position does not take "having no cause" as itself what makes a dharma permanent — nirvāṇa merely happens, additionally, not to arise from a cause. You suppose that atoms, too, are permanent because they are equally uncaused — but this is not so. Why? Because nirvāṇa is apart from both existence and non-existence, beyond the reach of the four kinds of denial, and it is precisely because it does not arise from a cause that it is permanent. Your atoms, however, you hold to actually exist — and, existing, they cannot be without a cause; and having a cause, they must be impermanent. If you say that nirvāṇa is permanent because it is free of the three faults, and atoms too are free of the three faults, and are therefore equally permanent — this does not hold. Why? Because the mark by which you would prove this cannot itself be established. Nirvāṇa's permanence does not share the same mark as, nor differ in mark from, atoms — atoms involve discrimination, and so no proof of their permanence can be established, while nirvāṇa is free of discrimination, and so nirvāṇa's permanence, unlike that of atoms, can indeed be established. Furthermore, atoms themselves cannot even be established, since they cannot be established either singly or in aggregate: taken singly, the sense fails, since the four great elements cannot be separated from one another; taken in aggregate, this too fails, since an aggregate is coarse, and is therefore no longer, properly speaking, an atom at all. Apart from nirvāṇa, there is no other dharma at all that is permanently abiding. Nirvāṇa truly exists as permanent, since liberation is attained by means of skillful means, and cultivating the path does not pass by in vain. Having no beginning point, we know it is permanent. Being beyond the mark of form, it is said to be formless; yet, not being apart from qualities such as coolness, it is said to be not non-form either. Being attained only by the great functioning of non-discriminating wisdom, it is said to be truly existent. Being attained by the buddhas through the path accomplished by supreme, supramundane diligence, we know it is truly real. As the sūtra says: "Monks! This dharma truly exists — unborn, unarisen, unmade, unconditioned." Hence we know that nirvāṇa is truly permanent. This dharma is the Tathāgata's transformed basis — and it is for this reason that the mark of total inclusion is concluded, also called correspondence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The treatise's own justification for calling nirvāṇa "non-arising and cessation," despite having just spent this entire chapter proving nirvāṇa is neither of those things, is worth sitting with: the cause is named for the fruit, a coarse label borrowed for something too subtle to name directly. This is the same interpretive move the treatise has used before — provisional teachings pointing at a definitive truth they cannot state outright — but applied here to its own scriptural vocabulary rather than to a rival school's. It is a quiet admission that even the treatise's own key terms, "non-arising," "cessation," "extinction," are makeshift, chosen because sentient beings' own faculties cannot yet match what they gesture toward. The closing atom comparison then does something sharper: it does not simply assert nirvāṇa's permanence against the non-Buddhists' claim for atoms, but shows that "permanent because uncaused" was never, on inspection, a coherent description of an atom in the first place — while the argument for nirvāṇa's permanence never rested on that reasoning at all.

Fascicle Three's Longest Chapter Complete

This closes the mark of total inclusion — the treatise's longest chapter, run across four pages, from the ocean and sun similes through the seven names of the dharmakāya to this final, exhaustive defense of nirvāṇa's permanence against every attempt to reduce it to mere absence. Part 17 moves through the three short chapters that close Fascicle Three: the mark of differentiation, sorting sentient beings by their relation to buddha-nature; the mark of stages, locating buddha-nature across the impure, pure, and utterly pure positions; and the mark of pervasion, on how buddha-nature fills all three stages as evenly as space fills a clay, silver, or gold vessel.
