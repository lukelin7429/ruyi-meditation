Refuting the Vaiśeṣika

Having answered the Hīnayāna objection, the treatise turns to the second of its three refutations: non-Buddhist claims that all dharmas possess a fixed, unchangeable self-nature (svabhāva). This page follows the argument from the opening claim — that fire, water, saṃsāra, and nirvāṇa each have their own immutable nature, since none can transform into the other — through the pot-and-quality problem, a citation of Nāgārjuna's fourfold negation of arising, and a long analysis of sound that closes, at the end of this page, with the treatise's own note that it has thereby "refuted the Vaiśeṣika."

The Claim: Fixed Self-Nature Everywhere

Chinese Source

復次為外道不識佛性故，彼立義應知。有外道說：一切諸法皆有自性，等有不空，性各異故。若諸法悉空無自性者，則水火色心、生死涅槃並無自性。自性既無，應可轉火為水、轉於涅槃更作生死。何以故？等無自性故。現見火性定熱，不可為水；水性定濕，不可為火。涅槃生死亦復如是，不可互相轉作，如此二法並有自性故。若互可轉，則修道無用。故知諸法各有自性，是故不空。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, since non-Buddhists fail to recognize buddha-nature, their position should be understood. Certain non-Buddhists hold: all dharmas possess a self-nature; they equally exist and are not empty, because each nature is distinct. If all dharmas were entirely empty and without self-nature, then water, fire, form, mind, saṃsāra, and nirvāṇa would all be without self-nature. And if self-nature were altogether absent, it should then be possible to turn fire into water, or to turn nirvāṇa back into saṃsāra. Why? Because, on the emptiness view, they would equally lack self-nature. But we plainly observe that the nature of fire is fixed as heat and cannot become water, and the nature of water is fixed as wetness and cannot become fire. Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are likewise so — they cannot be transformed into one another, precisely because these two also possess fixed self-nature. If they could be mutually transformed, then cultivating the path would be pointless. Hence, they conclude, each dharma possesses its own self-nature, and is therefore not empty.

Nothing Can Be Found Under Analysis

Chinese Source

復次為破外道自性義應知。難曰：汝說諸法各有自性不空，性定異者，是義不然。何以故？自性決定不可得故。決定者，離此泰近泰遠八種不可見因外，若物定有則應可見，若物定無則不可見，譬如兔角及蛇耳等，以決定智、依道理覓決不可得，定永無故。諸法自性亦復如是，故知諸法無自性故空。

若汝說瓶等諸物更互各異，如瓶異衣等者，是義不然。何以故？瓶與色等，為即自性、為離自性？若定即離者，義皆不可。若是一者，則不應有八，瓶與數相違故，一義不立。若定異者，緣色則不應得瓶，如人緣牛曾不見馬。故瓶等即離自性皆不可得。若汝說有性有故諸法有，自性不空者，是義不然。何以故？有性無自性故。有性若是自性有者，則不離空有二處。若有中有者，則二有相並無能所用。法既以有，何勞復須自性有耶？若無中有者，那不能令兔角龜毛等有？故知二處不立。

復次問曰：汝說自性與瓶等，為一、為異？若一者，則不應有八性；若有八者，一數即乖。若言異者，則不通有便無言智。何以故？汝言由自性有，故有言說及生智慧。今既是異故，知無言說。無言說故，智慧不生。有無即離，皆不可得，故自性定無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the refutation of this notion of self-nature should be understood. Objection: "Your claim that each dharma possesses its own self-nature and is not empty, each nature being fixedly distinct, does not hold. Why? Because a fixed self-nature can nowhere be found. As to 'fixed': apart from the eight causes of non-perception — too near, too far, and the rest — if a thing definitely exists it ought to be perceivable, and if a thing definitely does not exist it will not be perceived. As with a rabbit's horns or a snake's ears, sought by decisive cognition and by reasoning and never found, such things are decisively, permanently non-existent. The self-nature of dharmas is exactly the same: sought and never found. Hence we know dharmas are without self-nature, and are therefore empty."

"If you say that things such as pots differ from one another — a pot differing from a robe, for instance — this too does not hold. Why? Is a pot identical with its own self-nature, or separate from it? Neither identity nor separateness can be maintained. If they are identical, then there should not be eight categories of quality, for a pot, being one, would conflict with being counted as many, and so the claim of identity cannot stand. If they are definitely separate, then apprehending its color should not yield the cognition 'pot' — as one who apprehends a cow never thereby sees a horse. Hence a pot, whether identical with or separate from its self-nature, can in neither case be established. If you say dharmas exist because 'existence-ness' itself exists, and so self-nature is not empty — this too does not hold. Why? Because 'existence-ness' itself has no further self-nature. If existence-ness possessed a self-nature by which it exists, it could not escape being situated in one of two cases: within existence, or within non-existence. If it exists within something that already exists, then the two 'existences' stand together with no distinct function of agent and object — since the thing already exists, what need is there for a further, self-natured existence? If it exists within non-existence, why could this not equally make rabbit-horns and tortoise-hair exist? So neither case can be established."

Again, question: "Is self-nature one with a pot, or different from it? If one, then there should not be eight categories of nature; and if there are eight, then oneness is contradicted. If different, then there is no direct connection between them, and so no speech or cognition of the pot could arise — for you hold that it is precisely because self-nature exists that speech and cognition become possible, yet since they are now, on your own account, different, no such connection is possible, and without it, cognition cannot arise. Whether identical or separate, existent or non-existent, self-nature can in no case be established — therefore self-nature is decisively non-existent."

Words Are Empty Too, and the Fourfold Negation

Chinese Source

又若汝說，汝言亦空。是故一切諸法不空者，此義不然。何以故？如是語言入諸法攝故。語言亦空，故知諸法皆空。若汝說語言可聞故不空者，是義不然。何以故？語言自性不可得故，語言因緣種種異故。異相者，有八事：一覺；二觀；三功用；四風氣；五八處，八處者，臍、胸、喉、舌根、項、齒、鼻、脣；六音聲；七名字；八開閉。具此八義故，言聲得生。分別語言並入一切諸法攝，故知同皆是空。

又汝言，若汝說空平等者，云何於八種因緣但生語言、不生餘法？是義不然。何以故？汝不識他義本故。若有人立不從因緣能出語言，汝對此人可施此難。我今說因果決定、不無因緣。因果定者，如從因生果。若果不從因生，則應本來有果。若因不生果，果何緣有？若因果俱無性者，則自他同無。云何自生、不生於他？為自果生，他果不生故，不得無性。由因生故，不可說有；從他生故，不可說無。以是義故，我說因果決定，汝難不成。如《中論》偈言：「一切處諸法，從自不得生，從他二亦爾，從無因亦然。」

初言一切處諸法者，明處有三：一約四生處，謂三界生處及無流界生處。此四攝一切內外處盡，四中所有一切諸法攝法亦盡。二約內道外道攝一切所有法，處通世出世皆盡。三約有情無情攝一切法皆盡，處通三世攝有皆盡，故言一切處諸法。次三句以四種因緣覓諸法實生皆不可得：一從自、二從他、三俱從自他、四不從自他。尋此四句皆無生義，故知諸法悉非性有。

一不從自生者，若從自生，生則無用。自既以有，何勞復生？故文言從自不得生。二若從他生，何不生於異果？同皆是無故，故言從他不得生。三俱從自他生者，亦復不然。前約異體相續立自他義，如兩物相望故互為自他。以張望王，張即為自、王即為他；以王望張，王自張他，義亦如是。此二他性為一、為異？若兩他性是一者，則無自他義，非兩相望故。若彼他義異此他義者，彼即不成他，以異他性故。彼他既非他，此他亦復失本。由他望我故，我有他義；他既非他，我他亦失，本由他故有。自他義既空，自性理失，竟何俱從自他生耶？故言二亦然。次約同類因果相望論自他者，本由種子為因，能生芽果。芽必由因，故名為果；種必生果，故得名因。因之與果為一、為異？若定一者，則無生義，本已是有，復何用生？若定異者，則應生異果。既俱是異因，何故但生自果、不生餘果？既自他一異俱不可得，故知不俱從自他生，所以文言二亦爾故。四不從自他生者，是無因義。若汝謂諸法不從因緣而自有者，則一切諸法互能相生，火應生水、水能生火，等無因緣故。若不爾者，無因生義即不得成，故文言從無因亦然。於四句中求覓生相並不可得，是故當知決定無生。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, if you say that your own words are also empty, and that all dharmas are therefore not empty — this does not hold either. Why? Because such words themselves are included among all dharmas. Words, too, are empty; hence we know all dharmas are empty. If you say words are not empty because they can be heard, this does not hold either. Why? Because the self-nature of words cannot be found; the causes and conditions of words are themselves many and various. These factors number eight: first, awareness; second, attention; third, functional effort; fourth, the movement of breath; fifth, the eight places of articulation — the navel, chest, throat, root of the tongue, nape, teeth, nose, and lips; sixth, sound itself; seventh, names; and eighth, the opening and closing of the mouth. It is by the completion of these eight factors that spoken sound arises. Words, thus analyzed, are themselves included among all dharmas, and so we know they too, alike, are empty.

And you say further: "If you claim emptiness is universal, why is it that these eight causes and conditions give rise only to words, and not to other dharmas?" This objection does not hold. Why? Because you have not understood the true basis of my position. If someone claimed that words arise without any causes or conditions at all, you might rightly press this objection against him. But what I say is that cause and effect are determinate — that they are not without causes and conditions. That cause and effect are determinate means the effect arises from its cause: if the effect did not arise from its cause, it would have to already exist from the outset; and if the cause did not produce the effect, from what would the effect ever come? If you say cause and effect are both entirely without any nature at all, then self-production and production-by-another would be equally absent — but how, then, could this effect arise from its own cause, and not arise from something else? Because a given effect arises from its own proper cause and does not arise from another, causation cannot be said to be utterly without nature. Yet because it arises from its cause, it cannot be said to truly exist with fixed self-nature; and because it arises dependent on other conditions, it cannot be said to be non-existent either. For this reason cause and effect are determinate, and the objection does not succeed. As the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā says in verse: "In every place, no dharma is ever born from itself, nor from another; not from both, nor without a cause."

As to the opening phrase, "in every place, no dharma" — "place" here has three senses. First, with respect to the four places of birth: the places of birth within the three realms, together with the place of birth in the unconditioned realm. These four comprehend the whole of the internal and external sense-fields, and every dharma comprehended by them is likewise included without remainder. Second, with respect to the teachings held within the Buddhist path and outside it, comprehending every dharma, mundane and supramundane, without remainder. Third, with respect to sentient and insentient things, comprehending every dharma without remainder, spanning the three times and including everything that exists without remainder. Hence it says, "in every place, no dharma." The next three lines seek, through four possible causes and conditions, the real arising of dharmas, and in every case find it cannot be established: first, arising from self; second, arising from another; third, arising from both self and other together; fourth, arising from neither. Examining all four positions, none yields any coherent sense of arising — hence we know that no dharma truly exists by way of inherent self-nature.

First, as to not arising from self: if a thing arose from itself, its arising would serve no purpose — since it already exists as itself, what need would there be for it to arise again? Hence the text says, "not born from itself." Second, as to arising from another: if it arose from another, why would it not equally give rise to any and every different effect, since prior to arising both the actual effect and any other possible effect are equally non-existent in the cause? Hence the text says, "not born from another." Third, as to arising from both self and other together: this too does not hold. The notions of "self" and "other" were established above with respect to distinct, continuous substances, as two things regarded relative to one another become, mutually, "self" and "other." Regarding Zhang from Wang's perspective, Zhang is "self" and Wang is "other"; regarding Wang from Zhang's perspective, Wang is "self" and Zhang is "other" — the sense is the same either way. Now, are these two "other-natures" one, or different? If they are one, then there is no real sense of self and other at all, since they are not truly regarded relative to one another as distinct. If the "other" in one case differs from the "other" in the other case, then each fails to qualify as a genuine "other," since genuine otherness would require a shared, determinate other-nature. Since neither qualifies as a genuine other, each correspondingly loses its own basis as a "self" as well — for it is only by being regarded relative to an other that one comes to have the sense of being a "self" at all, but since the other is not truly other, the sense of self is lost along with it, having depended, from the start, on the other. Once the notions of self and other are thus empty, the very rationale of self-nature is lost — so how, in the end, could anything arise jointly from self and other? Hence the text says, "from both, it is likewise so."

Next, regarding self and other considered with respect to cause and effect within the same class: take the seed as cause, able to produce the sprout as its effect. The sprout necessarily depends on its cause, and is thus called "effect"; the seed necessarily gives rise to its effect, and is thus called "cause." Is the cause one with its effect, or different from it? If they are definitely one, then there is no sense of arising at all, since the effect already exists from the start as identical with the cause — what need would there be for it to arise? If they are definitely different, then the cause should be able to give rise to any different effect equally — yet since every other thing would then equally count as a "different cause" with no special connection to this effect, why would the seed give rise only to its own proper effect, and not to any other? Since self and other, identity and difference, can in no case be established between cause and effect, arising jointly from self and other cannot be maintained either — which is why the text says, "from both, it is likewise so." Fourth, as to not arising without cause: this is the position of causeless arising. If you hold that dharmas exist of themselves, without depending on causes and conditions, then all dharmas ought to be able to give rise to one another indiscriminately — fire should give rise to water, and water should be able to give rise to fire, since on this view all alike lack causes and conditions. If this is not accepted, then the notion of causeless arising cannot be established either — hence the text says, "without a cause, it is likewise so." Having sought the mark of arising through all four positions and found it nowhere, we should therefore know that arising is decisively non-existent.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This citation is worth pausing on: it is a nearly verbatim quotation of the opening verse of Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (中論, chapter 1, verse 3), the foundational text of the Madhyamaka school, embedded within a treatise that the rest of this collection classifies under the Tathāgatagarbha and Yogācāra corpus. It is a useful reminder that these later doctrinal categories — Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, tathāgatagarbha thought — were not walled off from one another in the way a modern syllabus might suggest. A sixth-century treatise arguing for universal buddha-nature reaches, without any sense of crossing a boundary, for the sharpest tool Nāgārjuna's school had built for dismantling the appearance of fixed, independent essences — because the very point of both projects, from this treatise's perspective, is the same: to clear away every candidate for a truly existent, unchangeable "self-nature," Vaiśeṣika's included, so that what is left standing is precisely the empty, luminous ground the treatise calls buddha-nature.

Sound Cannot Be Perceived as Real Either

Chinese Source

復次若汝難，即與證量相違。若諸法無實性者，則能所皆不可得。聲不至耳，耳不得聲，我現見聲耳相對所以得聞，故知不空者。是義不然。何以故？是能所及證量自性皆不可得故。汝言由自性得成故不空者，是義不然。何以故？此自性於根塵證量中，一異有無等皆不可得故，自性不成。

若汝說云何不可得？由多因成故。若法有自性，即不由因得成，已成物者更生無用故。若汝言多因各生聲自性，譬如鼓聲必由手桴等因緣，隨此手等各自分有得聲義者。是義不然。何以故？前自他等四句中覓生不可得，由性空以顯故。若一性不成者，多性云何成？若汝立自性者，是因不勞立。何以故？自性自是有，何復用因為？

若汝說一果由一因得成，果以因為體故。若爾，但應一人得聞。何以故？由一人擊鼓，但一人應聞，餘人那並得聞？若多人共聞，則知因果不得一體。若汝說有多果，即從多因生，隨至而取。如人散種田中，人田是一，而種子眾多，所生芽等亦復不少。亦如一人打鼓，鼓聲眾多，故人聞亦多者。是義不然。何以故？本不可取故。若本已有自性，何得稱言現見因打鼓已後方聞聲？若汝說聲自性本有，由八種不了故未得者。是義不然。何以故？雖近遠等亦不得聞，故知本無自性。

若汝說一聲轉作多聲者。是義不然。何以故？一多數相違故，倒義應成，汝義本壞。一多數相違者，汝所立義聲有三種：一但與果相違、二雙與因果相違、三但與因相違。初剎那聲但與第二剎那聲相違，最後剎那聲但與因相違，無更有別果。中間無數聲前後相望，有無量因果，自俱相違。以是義故，一聲生無量聲者，是義不可。何以故？一時俱聞故。若前後生多聲者，則應前後而聞。不前後聞，一時俱聞者，故知非一聲生無量聲。復次倒義得成故者，若汝謂一能生多，我亦言多能生一。汝若不信多能生一，我亦不信一能生多。復次汝義本壞故者，汝義云有物德事等三種，唯於物中可說有德有事，不於德中更復立德。而汝今於聲德中分別有數量德，寧不自乖本執耶？

若汝說是數量但依名句味、不依於聲，譬如劫來燒屋實是火燒，以火與劫相應故，非是劫燒；由火依劫故，故云劫燒。數量亦爾，實是名句味家德，此名句味與聲相應故。數量德依聲而說故，我義本不失者。是義不然。何以故？名句味耳根所得故。此名句味為是聲、非聲耶？若是聲者，不應有德，諸德無體故。若非聲者，聞聲之時則不應得。於名句味若一時得，云何復言名句味等有於數量而聲無耶？故知汝義自乖於本。此前破鞞世師。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next: "If you press this objection, it contradicts direct perception itself. If dharmas have no real nature, then neither the perceiving subject nor the perceived object could be established at all — yet in fact, sound does not travel all the way to the ear, nor does the ear itself possess sound, and we directly observe that sound and ear, meeting one another, is precisely how hearing occurs. Hence we know dharmas are not empty." This objection does not hold. Why? Because the perceiving subject, the perceived object, and direct perception itself can none of them be established as possessing self-nature. Your claim that hearing is accomplished through self-nature, and is therefore not empty, does not hold — because, examined within the sense-faculty, the sense-object, and the act of perception, this self-nature can in no case be established, whether as identical, as different, as existent, or as non-existent; hence self-nature is not established.

"You ask why it cannot be established? Because it is accomplished through many causes together." If a dharma possessed self-nature, it would not need to be accomplished through causes at all — for a thing already accomplished by its own nature to arise again through causes would serve no purpose. If you say the many causes each contribute their own share to producing sound's self-nature — as, for instance, the sound of a drum necessarily arises from the hand, the drumstick, and other causes and conditions, each contributing its own portion toward the arising of sound — this does not hold either. Why? Because, as shown above, arising cannot be found through any of the four positions of self, other, both, or neither, precisely because all such nature is empty. If a single nature cannot be established, how could many natures together be established? And if you posit self-nature at all, then causes need not be invoked in the first place — for if self-nature already exists of itself, what further use would there be for a cause?

If you say one effect is accomplished by one cause, the effect taking the cause as its substance, then only one person ought to be able to hear it. Why? Because if one person strikes the drum, only that one person should hear it — how could other people hear it as well? But since many people do hear it together, we know that cause and effect cannot be a single substance. If you say there are many effects because they arise from many causes, each person taking hold of the sound as it reaches them — as when a person scatters seed across a field, the person and the field each being one, yet the seeds many, so that the sprouts that arise are likewise not few; or as when one person strikes a drum, the drum-sounds are many, so that the people who hear it are likewise many — this does not hold either. Why? Because it could never be freshly apprehended at all. If it already possessed self-nature from the start, how could it be said that we now observe hearing the sound only after the drum has been struck? If you say sound's self-nature exists inherently from the start, but is not apprehended beforehand only because of the eight factors of non-perception, this does not hold either. Why? Because even accounting for nearness, distance, and the rest, it still cannot be heard beforehand; hence we know it has no self-nature from the start at all.

If you say one sound transforms into many sounds, this does not hold either. Why? Because "one" and "many" are mutually contradictory in number; the inverted conclusion would then follow, and your own position would collapse by its own terms. As to "one" and "many" being mutually contradictory: on your own account, sound would have to be of three kinds — one that stands in contradiction only to its effect, one that stands in contradiction to both cause and effect together, and one that stands in contradiction only to its cause. The first-instant sound would stand in contradiction only to the second-instant sound; the very last-instant sound would stand in contradiction only to its cause, having no further separate effect; and the innumerable intermediate sounds, regarded before and after one another, would involve limitless mutually contradicting causes and effects. For this reason, the claim that one sound gives rise to countless sounds cannot be maintained. Why? Because they are all heard at once. If the many sounds arose one after another, they should likewise be heard one after another — but since they are not heard successively, and are instead heard all at once, we know that one sound does not give rise to countless sounds. Furthermore, as to how the inverted conclusion follows: if you hold that one can give rise to many, then I may equally say that many can give rise to one; if you do not believe that many can give rise to one, then I likewise do not believe that one can give rise to many. Furthermore, as to how your own position collapses by its own terms: your system holds that there are three categories — substance, quality, and action — and that only within substance can one speak of it possessing qualities and actions; one does not, in turn, posit a further quality within a quality. Yet you now distinguish, within the quality of sound itself, a further quality of numerical quantity — does this not directly contradict your own foundational position?

"If you say that this numerical quantity belongs, rather, to the phonemes, words, and phrasal sense that convey meaning, and not to sound itself — just as when a house burns down over an eon, it is in truth fire that burns it, and because fire occurs together with the eon it is loosely called 'the eon's burning,' the fire being merely conjoined with the eon; so too the numerical quantity truly belongs to the quality of phonemes, words, and phrasal sense, and it is only because these are conjoined with sound that the quality of quantity is spoken of in connection with sound — and since this quantity is thus attributed to sound only derivatively, my position is not, after all, undermined." This does not hold either. Why? Because phonemes, words, and phrasal sense are themselves apprehended by the faculty of hearing. Are these phonemes, words, and phrasal sense themselves sound, or not sound? If they are sound, then, by your own principle, they should not possess a further quality, since qualities have no substance of their own apart from a substance to bear them. If they are not sound, then they should not be apprehended at the moment sound is heard. But since phonemes, words, and phrasal sense are apprehended at one and the same time as sound is heard, how can you still maintain that phonemes, words, and the rest possess numerical quantity while sound itself does not? Hence we know your position contradicts its own foundation. This concludes the refutation of the Vaiśeṣika.

Part 3 Complete

This closes the treatise's refutation of the Vaiśeṣika claim that dharmas possess a fixed, findable self-nature — pursued through the pot-and-quality problem, Nāgārjuna's fourfold negation of arising, and a sustained analysis of sound that leaves no room for a self-nature to hide, not even behind the seeming solidity of direct perception.
