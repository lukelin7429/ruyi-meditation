The Effect Already in the Cause

This page closes the treatise's refutation of non-Buddhist positions, working through the Sāṃkhya doctrine of satkāryavāda — that the effect already, in some sense, pre-exists within its cause — and asking a question the doctrine cannot answer: before anything has manifested, where, exactly, is this "self-nature" supposed to be located? The chapter then turns to a final, intricate argument about memory itself, testing whether an unchanging self or an unchanging primal cognition could ever actually explain how remembering works — and closes with the treatise's own summary verdict on the entire non-Buddhist refutation.

Where Would Self-Nature Even Be?

Chinese Source

復次為破僧佉執應知。若汝說因中有果故，諸量不成，如諸法有自性者。是義不然。何以故？因果一體故。汝謂自性因中即有果，諸法中即有自性，故得比知。若爾，因果為一，為異？若一者，一體無有異故，不可相比。若汝說因果不一者，則自違汝義。若因中定有於果，即成二失：一者以因即果則失於因，自性一故，只得是果，云何有因？二者若已有果，因則何用？本由因生，果既已有，何復用因？若汝說是因有故自性不失者。是義不然。何以故？自性處所無故。若汝說由密空處故，云何無處所者。是義不然。何以故？密空處並未有故。密者，謂四大、四塵八物。空者，謂空大、聲塵二物。如此五大五塵二處並未有者，自性云何得立？自性未變異時，五唯五大，並未有故。空大是空處，四大是密處，二處昔未有時，汝立自性為在何處？

若汝說先自性未有空密者。是義不然。何以故？生因違本故。若變異前未有空密，則無因用。當變異時始有空密方有因用者，是則變異，應能生自性。若汝說是時此二未有者。是義不然。何以故？因不成就故，何物為先耶？若汝謂無物先自性者。是義不然，自性無住處故，若無處所則違汝聖言故。迦毘羅仙為阿修利婆羅門說：昔初唯有一暗冥，此中但有智。由處所既無法引聖言，即自相違。自性及我並不成就，復以何譬得立自性？

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the refutation of the Sāṃkhya position should be understood. If you say that because the effect already exists within the cause, our claim that the means of valid cognition cannot be established, and that instead all dharmas possess self-nature — this does not hold. Why? Because cause and effect would then be a single substance. You hold that the effect already exists within self-nature as cause, and that self-nature already exists within all dharmas as their effect, and it is on this basis that self-nature can be inferred. If so, are cause and effect one, or different? If one, then, being a single substance with no distinction, they could not serve to infer one another at all. If you say cause and effect are not one, then you contradict your own position. If the effect definitely already exists within the cause, two faults follow: first, since the cause would then simply be the effect, the cause is lost — self-nature being one and the same, what remains is only the effect; how, then, could there be a cause at all? Second, if the effect already exists, what use is the cause? The effect is supposed to arise from the cause in the first place — but if the effect already exists, what further use would there be for the cause? If you say that, because the cause exists, self-nature is not thereby lost — this does not hold either. Why? Because self-nature would then have no place to be located at all. If you say self-nature is located in the "solid" and "space" regions of the unmanifest, why do you then say there is no place for it? This does not hold. Why? Because the regions of the "solid" and of "space" do not yet exist prior to manifestation. The "solid" refers to the four great elements together with the four subtle elements, eight substances in all; "space" refers to the space-element and the sound-quality, two substances. Since these five great elements and five subtle elements, in their two regions, do not yet exist before manifestation, where could self-nature be established at all? Before self-nature undergoes its transformation into manifest form, the five subtle elements and five great elements have not yet come to exist. The space-element constitutes the "space" region, the four great elements the "solid" region — but before either of these two regions yet existed, where, on your account, was self-nature situated?

If you say that, prior to manifestation, self-nature simply does not yet have the space and solid regions — this does not hold either. Why? Because this contradicts the very basis of causal production. If, before transformation, the space and solid regions did not yet exist, then self-nature would have no locus for its causal function. If it is only at the moment of transformation that the space and solid regions first come to exist, and only then does the causal function occur, then it would be the transformation itself that gives rise to self-nature, rather than the reverse. If you say that, at that time, these two regions did not yet exist either, this does not hold either. Why? Because causation itself is not established — what, then, would even come first? If you say that nothing at all precedes self-nature, this does not hold, because self-nature would then have no locus whatsoever, and to have no locus contradicts your own scriptural authority. The sage Kapila taught the brāhmaṇa Āsuri: "In the beginning there was only a single darkness, within which there was only intellect." Since, on your own analysis, there is, in fact, no place for self-nature at all, this directly contradicts your own scripture. Neither self-nature nor the self can be established — by what analogy, then, could self-nature ever be established?

A Challenge Turned Back on Itself

Chinese Source

若汝說第三是其譬喻者。亦復不然。何以故？有二過失故，一者自失本義、二者因性不成。若汝說無異即是譬者。是義不然，第三不成故。若汝說由無異故第三得成者。是亦不然，但有數故。若汝說數有，即乖義本。若汝所說義本若有數、數即義者。是亦不然。何以故？數滅時義亦壞故，變異與自性則為無異，如不如並皆不立。自性及變異無差別故，則因與果為一並亦不成。

若汝說若因果一者，則無言語故，汝難亦無。若因果異者，一義不然。何以故？有三過失故，一自違汝義、二成自顛倒、三言語無因。一自違義本者云何？如此今我取汝義還難汝，汝為信為不信？汝若信我難，汝義即壞。若不信我難者，汝難亦非難。何以知然？由我難故。汝語義得成者，我義亦立。汝所立義，是何時中？為在我難前、為後、為俱時？若在前者，我未有難，汝對破誰？若在我難後，我義已成，汝義那立？若俱時者，則無能所，如江海水一時和合，云何辯異？汝我二義便無差別，即成我義。二自顛倒者，汝取生義難我顯義，此非難處。若我立生義，汝可以三時為難，難我生者不離三時故。若三時不立，是生不成可爾。顯義者，由道理故立。若有道理，則三時無異；若無道理，則不能顯義。是故不成，不由三時故。是故難生，須依三時。今所難者須依道理，汝將時節難道理義故，自成顛倒。三言語無因者，言語由義得成，若無義者語言不立，因果一體異義不成，故無語言。

若汝說是因是果者。是義不然。何以故？有無常過故。果既無常，因亦應爾。若因無常，則違汝義本。汝因是常、果是無常，果色等變異不可得故。若汝說由色所比故非不可得者。義亦不然，所比無有故，並如前破。證量不成故，比量亦不成，未有空密處故，因無住所故不得成。若汝說若無可比，比量亦得成，如比有四句：一有比無、二無比有、三有比有、四無比無者。是義不然。石女兒兔角等並應可比，色等可證非可證此義何用？是因無道理故，不可得成。若汝說如隣虛空塵以果比因，自性亦爾者。是義不然。以隣虛不成故、因果異故、離色等塵不可得故，故譬不得成立。若汝說取非證比非所證，如地下水不可知見，比自性我亦如是者。是義不然。何以故？若汝能因石女兒比龜毛者，我則信汝因隣虛塵得比自性。若汝說如燈自成，比量亦爾者。是義不然。何以故？離色等八物，燈不可見故。如隣虛塵離色等亦不可得，所比自性亦不可得，故知無自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

If you say that the third category, the unmanifest state itself, serves as the analogy that establishes self-nature — this too does not hold. Why? Because it carries two faults: first, it loses its own foundational meaning; second, causal nature is not thereby established. If you say that "non-difference" between transformation and self-nature is itself the analogy — this does not hold, because the third category is not established in the first place. If you say that it is precisely because of non-difference that the third category is established — this is not so either, for this involves nothing but an appeal to mere numbering without real distinction. If you say instead that number itself truly exists as a real category — this directly contradicts your own foundational position. And if your own foundational teaching held that number itself constitutes meaning — this too does not hold. Why? Because when number ceases, meaning would collapse along with it; transformation and self-nature would then be non-different, so that neither "as manifest" nor "as not-manifest" could be established at all. Since self-nature and its transformations would have no distinguishing difference, then cause and effect being one could not be established either.

If you say that, if cause and effect were truly one, there would be no meaningful speech at all, and so my objection itself would likewise have no force — note that if cause and effect are different, the "oneness" thesis does not hold either. Why? Because three faults follow: first, self-contradiction of your own position; second, self-inversion; third, speech without cause. As to the first: here I now take your own principle and turn it back against you — do you believe my objection, or not? If you believe it, your own position collapses. If you do not believe it, then your objection against me is likewise no objection at all. How do we know this? Because it is by means of my objection that your statement is able to have any force in the first place — and if your position is thereby established, my own position is equally established. As for your own thesis, at what time does it hold — before my objection, after it, or simultaneously with it? If before, I had not yet raised my objection — against whom, then, were you arguing? If after my objection, my own position is already established — how, then, could your position still stand? If simultaneous, there is no subject and object at all, as when river-water and sea-water merge together all at once — how could any distinction between them be drawn? In every case, your position and mine turn out to be indistinguishable, which is to say, my own position stands. As to the second, self-inversion: you take up the notion of "arising" to challenge my notion of "revealing" — but this is not the proper point to press. If I had put forward a thesis of arising, you could rightly challenge me using the three times, before, after, and simultaneous, since anything called "arising" cannot escape falling into one of the three. But "revealing" is established on the basis of reasoning, not of arising: if there is sound reasoning, the three times make no difference to it; if there is no sound reasoning, nothing can be revealed at all — in which case the failure lies in the reasoning, not in the three times. You have used the framework of time to challenge a matter of reasoning, and this is exactly what turns your own argument inside out. As to the third, speech without cause: speech is established by depending on meaning; without meaning, speech cannot be established. If the thesis that cause and effect are a single substance cannot be established, there is no meaning to your speech at all.

If you say instead that they exist as cause and as effect — this does not hold either. Why? Because this incurs the fault of impermanence: since the effect is impermanent, the cause would have to be so as well; and if the cause is impermanent, this contradicts your own foundational position, which holds that the cause is permanent while the effect alone is impermanent — yet the effect's transformations of color and the rest can never actually be found apart from the cause changing too. If you say that, because it can be inferred by way of color, it is therefore not unfindable — this too does not hold, because what is to be inferred does not actually exist, as refuted above. Since direct perception cannot be established, inference built upon it cannot be established either; and since the space and solid regions do not yet exist, the cause has no locus, and so cannot be established. If you say that inference can be established even without anything to be inferred, as inference admits four possibilities — inferring the absent from the present, the present from the absent, the present from the present, and the absent from the absent — this does not hold. On such a view, even a barren woman's son or a rabbit's horn ought to be inferable — of what use, then, is the distinction between what can and cannot be established by direct perception? This inference has no rational basis, and so cannot be established. If you say that, just as one infers the atomic cause from its aggregate effect, self-nature can likewise be inferred, this does not hold either, because the atom itself cannot be established, because cause and effect are different, and because self-nature, apart from color and the rest, cannot be found. If you say self-nature is grasped neither by direct perception nor by inference, as underground water cannot itself be seen yet its presence is known, and that self-nature and the self are known in just this way — this does not hold either. Why? If you could infer tortoise-hair from the son of a barren woman, then I would believe that you could infer self-nature from the atom. If you say that, just as a lamp establishes itself, so too does this inference establish itself — this does not hold either. Why? Because, apart from its eight constituent substances, a lamp cannot even be perceived at all. Just as the atom, apart from color and the rest, cannot be found, so too the self-nature to be inferred cannot be found — hence we know there is no self-nature.

What Makes Memory Possible

Chinese Source

若汝說憶知有故，故證量比量並成，若無證比復何所憶者。是義不然。若離三種知外方有憶知，證比不成故。三種知者，一異境知、二能別知、三一知。異境知者，如人見瓶，後時憶衣，此非謂憶。汝義亦爾，色即異物，已見色時便憶於瓶，故義不可。二能別知者，譬如張即見瓶，王即後憶者，無有是處。汝義亦爾，覺能取境，我能憶知，此兩別類不同。是覺前見，我後憶知，無是義故。三一知者，如人正看瓶時，不得生憶，若有憶知則二過失：一者兩知一時不得俱起，以心是一故，心取色時不得取聲。二者根正對境未捨，那復得憶餘境？汝覺亦爾，唯是一知，無念念滅，從變異初至解脫前際恒是一故，是憶知中無有憶知。若翻此三知，憶知得成。謂境界同類、能知同類、知念念滅不得一時，備此三義憶知得成。汝憶知不成，故證比等量亦不得成，以是緣故自性義壞。

若汝說憶知由自性，所以得成一異有無等造。初念取境則備三義：一境、二人、三智，並一時俱有。至第二念時，前三已滅、後三續起，前後同類是為一義，前後各一亦是一義，前無後有是為異義，前能生後是其有義，前滅後生是其無義。備此諸義，故有憶知。由自性故，憶知得成者。內曰：若爾，我大歡喜，翻成我義，有何損失。我今由此義故說無自性，譬如前種能生後穀，此前後穀不一不二、不有不無。若一者則無前後，若異者則前穀後豆，故無一異。自性不有不無者，因滅果生故，因滅故不有、果生故不無。因時未有果故不可說有，果生時必由因故不可說無。以是義故，因果憶知並得成就，故說無自性。如穀前後不一不異，能作種種諸事。憶知亦爾，前後不一不異，故能憶知種種諸境。

若汝聞憶知若是無常，云何修習者。答曰：已無常故則有修習，譬如劫波婆種子種植於地，以佉陀羅汁而溉灌之，後時出生芽莖枝葉是其自相無有異色，唯果熟時即成佉陀羅色，為相紅赤，異劫波婆自相本色。如是心者作法非法，是法次第前心熏習後心，如是方便熏習不失，故不一異。至果熟時，苦樂等果即各顯現，是故從無自性心諸業得生，如劫波種子後同佉陀羅色。若作此執，有自性心，是名憶知。或有人說，壽者亦是憶知。若作此執，即無憶知心及壽者，有自性故則無忘失。若汝說有忘失者，有二過失：一者憶知若有自性則無忘失，若有忘失則無憶知。如此忘失，為有自性、為無自性？若有自性，與憶知性為相違不？若不相違，於一物中便應一時俱有憶忘兩心並起。若相違者，但有自性忘失則無憶知，憶知既無，何所忘失？若汝說是知先生後滅故說忘失，若爾者自性則不成。若有憶知，自性、作具無用，憶知恒有故。若汝說由作具故得成者，則無自性，有過失故。若作者與具一體，則無憶知，知恒有故。若知無者，心或時無，則無自性。心或時有，則無憶知。若無憶知，心則應滅。

若汝說有別物故心得是常者。證知之時應不憶知。即以證知當憶知處，是故不失憶知體者。是義不然。何以故？若物常住則無數習，同類相似乃可數習。若無自性，似義可成。若有自性，則應恒守一物一事，故無相似。無相似故則無數習，無數習故則無憶知。若汝立自性憶知者，比量等並不成就，以一體故。一切疑心應是決智、欲即離欲、偷即是施、即婦是母。是覺若與自性不異，為當先有、為當後有？若先有者，所覺既無，覺何所覺？若後有者，則因中果義即自壞。若汝說本有者，所覺何物？若言我是所覺者。是義不然，以證比二量我不可得故。物若是有，不離二量所得。譬如五塵，五根五塵者，智不重生故，是名證量。比量者，智重生故。先見識生，後比知有根，以識不離根故。如先見烟，後知有火。我者則不爾，不如色等可證，過根境故，不如諸根可比。無有相應事故，故我非所覺。是故覺若在前，則不成覺。故知一切法如實無自性，唯真實空是其體性。

Ru-Yi Translation

If you say that, because memory exists, direct perception and inference are both thereby established — for without direct perception and inference, what would there be to remember? — this does not hold. If memory could exist apart from three kinds of cognition, direct perception and inference could not be established through it. The three kinds are: cognition of a different object, cognition by a different subject, and cognition as a single, undivided act. As to cognition of a different object: if a person sees a pot and afterward remembers a robe, this is not what we mean by "remembering." Your position amounts to the same thing: color is one thing, and the self, the alleged rememberer, is a different thing — having once seen a form, the self would then simply remember "a pot," with no real connecting link between them, and so this cannot be right. As to cognition by a different subject: it would be as if Zhang were the one who saw the pot, and Wang were the one who later remembered it — this makes no sense at all. Your position amounts to the same thing: cognition takes hold of the object, while the self does the remembering, these being two entirely distinct kinds of thing; the cognition sees it first, and the self remembers it afterward, and there is no coherent sense in which this could work. As to cognition as a single, undivided act: when a person is directly looking at a pot, no memory can arise at that very moment — and if memory could occur then, two faults would follow: first, two cognitions cannot arise simultaneously within a single mind, since the mind is one — when the mind is taking hold of a visible form, it cannot, at that same moment, be taking hold of a sound. Second, while the sense-faculty is still engaged with its object and has not yet released it, how could one, at the same time, remember some other object? Your notion of cognition is likewise held to be a single, undivided cognition, without arising and ceasing moment by moment, remaining one and the same from the very beginning of manifestation until the very threshold of liberation — and within such an unchanging cognition, there could be no memory at all. Only by reversing all three of these points can memory be properly established: the object remembered and the object originally cognized must belong to the same class, the cognizing subject must be of the same class across the two moments, and cognition must perish moment by moment rather than remaining a single undivided act. Only when these three conditions are met can memory be established. Since memory cannot be established on your system, direct perception and inference and the other means of valid cognition built upon it cannot be established either — and for this reason, the thesis of self-nature collapses.

If you say that memory is grounded in self-nature, and that on this basis identity, difference, existence, and non-existence can all be established: in the first moment of cognition, three factors are complete at once — the object, the person, and the cognition — all present together simultaneously. By the second moment, the first three have already ceased, and a second set of three continues to arise; since the earlier and later sets belong to the same class, this constitutes their "identity"; since each, considered separately, is itself a single unit, this too constitutes a kind of "identity"; since the earlier set is absent while the later set is present, this constitutes their "difference"; since the earlier set is able to give rise to the later, this constitutes "existence"; and since the earlier set has ceased when the later arises, this constitutes "non-existence." It is because all these senses are thus complete that memory exists — and it is by means of self-nature that memory is thereby established, so runs the objection. Insider: If that is so, I am delighted — for this simply turns into my own position, and there is nothing lost by it at all. This is precisely the reasoning by which I say there is no self-nature: just as an earlier grain-seed gives rise to a later grain, this earlier and later grain are neither one nor two, neither existent nor non-existent. If they were one, there would be no "earlier" and "later" at all; if they were entirely different, then the earlier could be grain and the later could turn out to be beans — hence they are neither simply one nor simply different. As for self-nature being neither existent nor non-existent: it is because the cause ceases as the effect arises — since the cause has ceased, it does not truly persist as existent, and since the effect has newly arisen, it is not simply non-existent either. At the time of the cause, the effect does not yet exist, so it cannot be said to already exist; yet when the effect arises, it necessarily depends on the cause, so it cannot be said to be non-existent either. For this very reason, cause, effect, and memory can all be properly established — which is exactly why I say there is no fixed self-nature. Just as the grain, earlier and later, is neither one nor different, and is thereby able to perform all its various functions, so too memory: earlier and later moments of it are neither one nor different, and it is for this very reason that it is able to remember all its various objects.

If you ask: if memory is impermanent, how could it ever be cultivated through practice? Answer: it is precisely because it is impermanent that cultivation is possible at all. It is like planting the seed of the kapittha tree in the earth and watering it with the juice of the khadira tree: afterward, when the sprout, stem, branches, and leaves emerge, they show, in their own characteristic appearance, no different color at all — it is only when the fruit ripens that it takes on the khadira's own color, a deep red, quite different from the kapittha's own original coloring. Mind is exactly the same: wholesome and unwholesome deeds are performed, and each earlier moment of mind, in due sequence, perfumes the moment of mind that follows it — and it is precisely through this skillful means of perfuming, without any loss, that mind is neither simply one nor simply different across time. When the fruit finally ripens, its results of pleasure and pain, each in turn, become manifest — and it is thus that karmic actions arise from a mind that has no fixed self-nature, just as the kapittha seed eventually comes to share the khadira's own color. If, on the other hand, one insists that mind must possess a fixed self-nature, this is precisely what is meant by treating "memory" as requiring an unchanging substrate. Some further hold that the self, as an enduring experiencer, is likewise required by memory. But if one insists on this position, there could be no memory or self at all — for if mind possesses fixed self-nature, there could be no forgetting. If you say that forgetting does occur even so, two faults follow. First: if memory possesses fixed self-nature, there could be no forgetting; and if forgetting occurs, there could be no fixed-nature memory in the first place. As for this "forgetting" itself — does it possess fixed self-nature, or not? If it possesses self-nature, is it in contradiction with the nature of memory, or not? If not in contradiction, then within a single thing, remembering and forgetting, these two states of mind, would have to arise together at one and the same time. If they are in contradiction, then wherever the self-nature of forgetting is present, memory cannot be — but if memory is thereby altogether absent, what, then, is there left to be forgotten at all? If you say that this cognition simply arises and then later ceases, and it is this that is called "forgetting," then, on this account, your posited self-nature of mind cannot be established after all. If memory truly existed with a fixed self-nature, then the instrument that produces it would serve no purpose, since memory would already exist permanently on its own. If you say it is by means of this instrument that memory is thereby produced, then mind has no fixed self-nature after all, since this incurs exactly the fault we are pointing to. If the agent and its instrument were a single substance, there could be no memory at all, since cognition would then be permanently present with nothing left for the instrument to newly produce. If cognition simply did not exist at all at certain times, the mind would, at times, not exist, and so would have no fixed self-nature; and if the mind exists only at certain times, there could be no fixed-nature memory. And if there is no memory, then the mind would have to be understood as ceasing altogether at those other times.

If you say that mind can remain permanent precisely because it is a distinct substance apart from its momentary cognitions, then at the moment of direct perception itself, there could be no memory occurring; yet you would then have to say that direct perception itself simply stands in for the place of memory, so that the substance of memory is never lost — but this does not hold. Why? If a thing remains permanently unchanging, there can be no repeated practice at all — repeated practice is possible only where successive instances resemble one another as belonging to the same kind. Without fixed self-nature, this notion of "resemblance" can be sustained; but with fixed self-nature, each moment would have to permanently retain its own single, unchanging identity, and so there could be no resemblance between successive instances at all. Without resemblance, there can be no repeated practice; and without repeated practice, there can be no memory. If you insist on positing memory as fixed by self-nature, then inference and the other means of valid cognition built on it cannot be established either, since everything would collapse into a single undifferentiated substance — every doubting mind would have to already be a decided cognition, desire would have to be identical with freedom from desire, theft would have to be identical with giving, and one's own wife would have to be identical with one's own mother. If this primal cognition is not different from self-nature itself, does it exist before its object, or after it? If before, then, since the object cognized does not yet exist, what could this cognition possibly be cognizing? If after, then your own thesis that the effect already exists within the cause is undermined by its own logic. If you say it exists together with its object from the very beginning, what, then, is the object being cognized? If you say the object is the self itself — this does not hold, because the self cannot be established by either direct perception or inference. If a thing truly exists, it must be apprehended by one of these two means. Take, for instance, the five sense-objects apprehended by the five sense-faculties: since cognition of them does not need to be produced a second time, being already directly given, this is called direct perception. Inference, by contrast, is precisely where cognition must be produced a second time: one first sees smoke arise, and only afterward infers the presence of fire, because the inferred cognition is never separate from what actually grounds it — as one first sees smoke and only then knows there is fire. The self is not like this at all: it cannot be directly perceived, as color and the rest can, being entirely beyond the reach of the sense-faculties and their objects; nor can it be inferred, as the sense-faculties themselves can be, since there is no observable connected phenomenon corresponding to it at all. Therefore the self is not something that can be cognized in the first place. And so, if this primal cognition were said to precede its object, it could not properly be called a cognition at all. Hence we know that, in truth, no dharma whatsoever possesses self-nature — true emptiness alone is their real nature.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

It is worth stepping back, at the close of this long chapter, to notice what the treatise has actually been doing since Part 3. Three separate non-Buddhist systems — Vaiśeṣika's fixed substances and qualities, the Sāṃkhya's eternal sound and its unmanifest self-nature, and the mechanics of memory each system tried to explain — have all been shown to fail for structurally the same reason: each requires something to hold still, unchangingly, as the fixed ground beneath a world that plainly does change. And each time, the treatise's answer is not that nothing exists, but that identity across time is never the simple, single-substance sameness these systems demand — as with the grain-seed becoming grain, "neither one nor different." This is exactly the conceptual space the treatise needs cleared before it can say, starting in Fascicle Two, what buddha-nature actually is: not a fixed, findable thing hiding somewhere underneath appearances, in the way a Vaiśeṣika substance or a Sāṃkhya self-nature was supposed to be, but something that can be truly "always there" for every sentient being precisely without being that kind of thing at all.

Part 5 Complete

This closes the "Part Refuting Attachments," and with it the treatise's entire opening polemical arc — first against a Hīnayāna misreading, then against three non-Buddhist systems in turn. Part 6 completes Fascicle One with the third and final refutation promised at the start: certain Mahāyāna practitioners' own confused grasp of the two truths, holding too rigidly to either the conventional or the ultimate. It is a short chapter, and closes the fascicle before the treatise turns, in Fascicle Two, to its positive account of what buddha-nature actually is.
