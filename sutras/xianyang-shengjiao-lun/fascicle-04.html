The Qualities, and the Nine Topics Drawn Together

The ninth and last topic is the qualities, and it climbs: the four measureless minds and the eight liberations that any practitioner may cultivate; then the masteries and totalities; then qualities that belong to a buddha alone — the thirty-two marks, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the eighteen things held in common with no one, and the knowledge of all aspects. Then the chapter turns back on itself. A closing verse explains why there are exactly nine topics and why they come in this order; another shows how to index the entire body of the Buddha's word by matter and by concept; and the chapter ends by naming the six approaches through which all of it is to be studied.

Where This Fascicle Picks Up

Fascicle 3 carried the eighth topic, the fruits, through to the arhat's six abidings. This fascicle opens the ninth and last topic of the chapter on Gathering the Topics, and then closes the chapter itself — which has run across four fascicles and accounts for roughly a fifth of the whole treatise.

Classical text is set in the left-hand column below, our English in the right. See also this site's note on translation method, and the note on this treatise's method in fascicle 1.

1. Topic Nine: The Four Measureless Minds

The canonical formula for loving-kindness is taken apart word by word, and then the same treatment is given in compressed form to compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — each of which turns out to negate a slightly different form of ill will.

Chinese Source

如是已說果，諸功德今當說。頌曰：

無量諸解脫、　勝處與遍處、

無諍、妙願智、　無礙解、神通。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fruits having been expounded, the qualities shall now be expounded.

The measureless minds, the liberations,
the bases of mastery and the totalities,
non-contention, the knowledge of vows,
the analytic knowledges, and the supernormal powers.

Chinese Source

論曰：無量者，謂四無量，廣說如經。一、慈無量，謂慈心俱，無怨無憎無有損害，廣大無量極善修習，於一方面如是次第乃至十方一切無邊世界，意解遍滿具足住。

慈心俱者，於無苦無樂眾生欲施樂，具阿世耶心相應故。無怨者，即彼對治，欲加苦具瞋故。無憎者，即彼對治，障礙樂具瞋故。無損害者，即彼對治，欲與不宜，瞋故。廣者，於見所行作意故。大者，於聞所行作意故。無量者，於覺知所行作意故。極善修習者，由串習相應，離諸蓋故。於一方面如是次第乃至十方一切無邊世界者，遍緣器世間及有情世間故。意解者，緣意解思惟境界故。遍滿者，緣無間有情境界故。具足住者，如前靜慮中說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The exposition: the measureless minds are the four measureless minds, expounded at length in the scriptures. First, measureless loving-kindness: accompanied by a mind of loving-kindness, without enmity, without hatred, without any wish to injure, broad, great, measureless, most excellently cultivated, one dwells having fully attained a resolve that pervades — toward one quarter, and so in sequence, out to all the boundless worlds of the ten directions.

Accompanied by a mind of loving-kindness: toward beings who are neither suffering nor at ease, one wishes to bestow ease, being associated with a mind possessed of that disposition. Without enmity: the counteragent to the hatred that wishes to inflict the equipment of suffering. Without hatred: the counteragent to the hatred that obstructs the equipment of ease. Without any wish to injure: the counteragent to the hatred that wishes to give what is unsuitable. Broad: attention with respect to what is encountered by seeing. Great: attention with respect to what is encountered by hearing. Measureless: attention with respect to what is encountered by awareness. Most excellently cultivated: by being associated with repeated familiarity, one is free of the hindrances. Toward one quarter, and so in sequence out to all the boundless worlds of the ten directions: because one takes as object, pervasively, both the receptacle world and the world of beings. Resolve: because one takes as object the domain contemplated by resolve. Pervading: because one takes as object the domain of beings without a gap. Dwells having fully attained: as stated above in the section on the absorptions.

二、悲無量，謂悲心俱，乃至廣說。悲心俱者，於有苦眾生欲拔苦，具阿世耶心相應故。無怨者，即彼對治，與苦害故。無憎者，即彼對治，障礙拔苦害故。無損害者，即彼對治，欲與不宜不喜樂故。餘如前說。

三、喜無量，謂喜心俱，乃至廣說。喜心俱者，於有樂眾生隨喜彼樂，阿世耶心相應故。無怨者，即彼對治，欲與苦具不喜樂故。無憎者，即彼對治，障礙樂具不喜樂故。無損害者，即彼對治，欲與不宜不喜樂故。餘如前說。

四、捨無量，謂捨心俱，乃至廣說。捨心俱者，欲令不染，阿世耶心相應故。無怨者，即彼對治，令染貪瞋故。無憎者，即彼對治，障礙除染貪瞋故。無損害者，即彼對治，顛倒不染貪及瞋故。餘如前說。

Second, measureless compassion: accompanied by a mind of compassion, and so on at length. Accompanied by a mind of compassion: toward beings who suffer, one wishes to draw out their suffering, being associated with a mind possessed of that disposition. Without enmity: the counteragent to the injury of inflicting suffering. Without hatred: the counteragent to the injury of obstructing the drawing out of suffering. Without any wish to injure: the counteragent to taking no delight in wishing to give what is unsuitable. The rest as above.

Third, measureless sympathetic joy: accompanied by a mind of joy, and so on at length. Accompanied by a mind of joy: toward beings who are at ease, one rejoices in their ease, being associated with a mind of that disposition. Without enmity: the counteragent to taking no delight, wishing instead to give the equipment of suffering. Without hatred: the counteragent to taking no delight, obstructing instead the equipment of ease. Without any wish to injure: the counteragent to taking no delight, wishing instead to give what is unsuitable. The rest as above.

Fourth, measureless equanimity: accompanied by a mind of equanimity, and so on at length. Accompanied by a mind of equanimity: one wishes that beings be undefiled, being associated with a mind of that disposition. Without enmity: the counteragent to what defiles with craving and hatred. Without hatred: the counteragent to what obstructs the removal of defiling craving and hatred. Without any wish to injure: the counteragent to the distortion of not being undefiled with respect to craving and hatred. The rest as above.

Reading note

Each of the four is aimed at a different kind of being, and that is what distinguishes them: loving-kindness at those who are neither suffering nor at ease, compassion at those who suffer, sympathetic joy at those who are at ease, and equanimity at all of them with respect to defilement. Between them the first three exhaust the possible conditions a being can be in. There is no one to whom none of them applies.

The gloss on broad, great, and measureless is unexpected and quite precise: the three words are not intensifiers but three ranges of acquaintance. Broad covers beings one has seen; great, beings one has only heard of; measureless, beings one merely knows must exist. The formula therefore describes an expansion outward from the people actually in front of one, through those reported, to those never encountered at all. This is a far more workable account of how a measureless mind is built than any exhortation to feel more.

2. What the Four Measureless Minds Are Made Of

Chinese Source

此四無量體性云何？謂慈以無瞋善根為體，悲以不害善根為體，喜以不嫉善根為體，捨以無貪無瞋善根為體，皆是憐愍眾生法故。於此四中慈唯無瞋，次二無量無瞋一分，捨是無貪無瞋一分。又復與彼相應等持諸心心法并彼眷屬，皆是四無量體當知。先由增上法行善修治心，復依清淨靜慮，方得清淨無量應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the essential nature of these four measureless minds? Loving-kindness has the wholesome root of non-hatred as its essence; compassion has the wholesome root of non-harming; sympathetic joy has the wholesome root of non-envy; equanimity has the wholesome roots of non-greed and non-hatred. All of them are states of pity toward beings. Among these four, loving-kindness is non-hatred alone; the next two measureless minds are each a portion of non-hatred; and equanimity is a portion of non-greed and non-hatred. Moreover, the concentration associated with them, together with all the mind and mental factors and their retinue, should be understood to be the essence of the four measureless minds. It should be understood that only after the mind has first been well cultivated by the practices that give it dominance, and then in dependence on a purified absorption, are the measureless minds obtained in purity.

Reading note

This short passage does real analytic work: it locates all four measureless minds within the eleven wholesome mental factors catalogued in fascicle 1, so that nothing new has to be added to the inventory of mind. Loving-kindness simply is non-hatred; compassion is non-harming, which fascicle 1 defined as compassion-rooted; equanimity is a portion of non-greed and non-hatred. The one apparent exception is sympathetic joy, assigned to non-envy — a factor not in the wholesome eleven but the negation of one of the twenty secondary afflictions. The measureless minds are not extraordinary states bolted onto an ordinary mind. They are ordinary wholesome factors extended without limit.

The last sentence sets a real precondition, and it is easy to skip. The measureless minds are obtained in purity only after the mind has been prepared by the Dharma practices and rests on a purified absorption. Warm feeling toward beings is available to anyone; the measureless mind of this description is a meditative attainment.

3. The Eight Liberations

Eight, and the last five are the formless attainments and the cessation of perception and feeling put to a particular use. What is striking is the reason given for each: the bodhisattva enters them in order to gain a specific mastery, and to be freed of what obstructs it.

Chinese Source

諸解脫者，謂八解脫，廣說如經。一、有色諸色觀解脫。有色者，依有色定意解思惟故。諸色者，若色如勝處中廣自分別。觀者，於諸色中為變化自在故、意解思惟顯示彼相故。二、內無色想外諸色觀解脫。內無色想者，依無色定意解思惟故。外者，除眼等根意解思惟餘色故。諸色觀者，如前說。三淨解脫身作證具足住解脫。淨者，一向意解思惟淨妙色，為得增上安樂住故。解脫者，解脫淨不淨色功用，障礙心故。身者，意身故。作證者，由智斷得作證故。具足住者，如前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The liberations are the eight liberations, expounded at length in the scriptures.

First, the liberation of one possessing form who contemplates forms. Possessing form: contemplating by resolve in dependence on a concentration that has form. Forms: forms as distinguished at length in the section on the bases of mastery below. Contemplates: in order to have mastery in transforming those forms, and because by resolve one contemplates and displays their marks.

Second, the liberation of one without inner perception of form who contemplates outer forms. Without inner perception of form: contemplating by resolve in dependence on a formless concentration. Outer: setting aside the eye and the other faculties, one contemplates by resolve the remaining forms. Contemplates forms: as above.

Third, the liberation of dwelling having fully attained the pure liberation, witnessed with the body. Pure: single-mindedly contemplating by resolve pure and excellent form, in order to obtain a surpassing abiding in ease. Liberation: liberation from the exertion over pure and impure form, which obstructs the mind. Body: the mental body. Witnessed: witnessed through knowledge and abandonment. Dwells having fully attained: as above.

無色諸解脫，如前分別。此中差別者，為欲證得一切種身業自在故，及為解脫彼障故，復除光色，作無邊虛空意解思惟故，名第四無邊虛空處解脫。為欲發起聖神通、無諍、願、智、無礙辯等諸功德故，又為證得能助發起彼諸功德心自在故，又為解脫彼障故，復作無邊識意解思惟故，名第五無邊識處解脫。行者作如是發起功德方便已，令第四靜慮起現在前發諸功德，為欲證得最勝無漏住自在故，又為解脫彼障故，復作無所有意解思惟故，名第六無所有處解脫。為欲證得最第一有住自在故，又為解脫彼障故，復作非想非非想意解思惟故，名第七非想非非想處解脫。為欲證得最勝寂靜住自在故，又為解脫彼障故，復從非想非非想處心進止出入息滅攀緣故，名第八想受滅解脫。

The formless liberations are distinguished as above. The differences here are these. In order to realize mastery over bodily action in every aspect, and in order to be freed of what obstructs that, one further removes light and color and contemplates boundless space by resolve; this is called the fourth liberation, of the sphere of the infinity of space.

In order to arouse the qualities of the noble supernormal powers, non-contention, the knowledge of vows, and unobstructed eloquence, and in order to realize the mastery of mind that assists in arousing them, and in order to be freed of what obstructs that, one further contemplates boundless consciousness by resolve; this is called the fifth liberation, of the sphere of the infinity of consciousness.

When the practitioner has thus prepared the means of arousing those qualities, he causes the fourth absorption to become manifest and brings the qualities forth. Then, in order to realize mastery in the supreme uncontaminated abiding, and in order to be freed of what obstructs that, he further contemplates nothingness by resolve; this is called the sixth liberation, of the sphere of nothingness. In order to realize mastery in the abiding that is the foremost of all existence, and to be freed of what obstructs it, he further contemplates neither-perception-nor-non-perception by resolve; this is called the seventh liberation, of the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. In order to realize mastery in the supreme abiding of stillness, and to be freed of what obstructs it, he then, from the mind of that sphere, advances and halts, the in-and-out breath ceases, and he lets go of what the mind reaches for; this is called the eighth liberation, the cessation of perception and feeling.

Reading note

Read against fascicle 2, this passage changes what the formless attainments are for. There the four spheres were described as a ladder of increasing subtlety, ending in a state too faint to liberate. Here the same four states are entered deliberately, each as the means to a particular mastery — infinite space for mastery over bodily action, infinite consciousness for arousing the supernormal powers and eloquence, nothingness for the uncontaminated abiding, neither-perception-nor-non-perception for the highest existence. The formless attainments are not being climbed. They are being used.

The detail about the fourth absorption is worth noting. Having prepared the means through the fifth liberation, the practitioner comes back down to the fourth absorption to actually produce the qualities. The supernormal powers are not exercised from the formless spheres; they are exercised from the fourth absorption, which the formless work has made possible. And the eighth liberation is described physiologically as well as mentally — 出入息滅, the in-and-out breath ceases — which is the standard mark of the attainment of cessation.

4. The Eight Bases of Mastery

Mastery over the objects of meditation, worked out in pairs — few and many, then the four colors, each given two similes.

Chinese Source

勝處者，謂八勝處，廣說如經。一、內有色想外諸色觀少，若好若惡若劣若勝，於彼諸色勝知勝見，得如是想名初勝處。內有色想者，如解脫中說。外者，謂除眼等根，顯餘色故。諸色觀者，如前說。少者，謂資具攝色，意解思惟故。若好若惡者，謂淨不淨色之所攝色，意解思惟故。若劣若勝者，謂淨不淨聲香味觸之所攝色，意解思惟故。於彼諸色勝者，謂能治所治作意思惟，障礙功用所不惱故。知者，用奢摩他道。見者，用毘鉢舍那道。得如是想者，謂於實勝中得實勝，無慢想故；於不勝中得實不勝，無慢想故。二、內有色想外諸色觀多，乃至名第二勝處。內無色想中觀少觀多二種亦爾。內有色想外諸色觀者，如前說。多者，顯示有情世間、器世間色遍思惟故。餘如前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The bases of mastery are the eight bases of mastery, expounded at length in the scriptures.

First: one who has inner perception of form contemplates outer forms as few — whether fine or foul, inferior or superior — and with respect to those forms knows and sees with mastery, obtaining such a perception. This is the first base of mastery. Inner perception of form: as stated among the liberations. Outer: setting aside the eye and the other faculties, it displays the remaining forms. Contemplates forms: as above. Few: the form comprised in one's requisites, contemplated by resolve. Fine or foul: the form comprised in pure and impure visible form, contemplated by resolve. Inferior or superior: the form comprised in pure and impure sound, smell, taste, and touch, contemplated by resolve. Mastery with respect to those forms: because in attending to and contemplating the counteragent and what it counteracts, one is not troubled by the exertion that obstructs. Knows: by the way of calm abiding. Sees: by the way of insight. Obtaining such a perception: because with respect to what is genuinely superior one gets it as genuinely superior, without a conceited perception; and with respect to what is not superior one gets it as genuinely not superior, without a conceited perception.

Second: one who has inner perception of form contemplates outer forms as many, and so on — this is the second base of mastery. And the two, contemplating few and contemplating many, are likewise found for one without inner perception of form. Many: because it displays the pervasive contemplation of the form of the world of beings and of the receptacle world. The rest as above.

五、內無色想外諸色觀青。青顯、青可見、青光，猶如烏莫迦花，或如婆羅痆斯染青衣色。如是黃、赤、白色皆應廣說。此中差別者，黃色如羯尼迦羅花，或如婆羅痆斯染黃衣色。赤色如槃豆時縛迦花，或如婆羅痆斯染赤衣色。白色如烏奢那星，或如婆羅痆斯鮮白衣色。青者，謂總句。青顯者，謂俱生青。青可見者，謂和合成青。青光者，謂彼二所出鮮淨光青。如青色，黃、赤、白色亦復如是廣說應知。餘如前說。於一處說二譬喻者，此顯俱生、和合二種色故。

此八勝處與修三種緣色解脫作所依止，後四勝處意解思惟欲界天色及色界色。又復應知、是諸勝處為治下地種子隨逐作意思惟、非為對治自地所治作意思惟。

Fifth: one without inner perception of form contemplates outer forms as blue — blue in color, blue to see, blue in radiance, like the umaka flower, or like the color of a blue-dyed garment of Vārāṇasī. So too should yellow, red, and white be expounded at length. The differences are these: yellow like the karṇikāra flower, or like a yellow-dyed garment of Vārāṇasī; red like the bandhujīvaka flower, or like a red-dyed garment of Vārāṇasī; white like the star Uśanas, or like the fresh white cloth of Vārāṇasī.

Blue: the general term. Blue in color: blue arisen together with the thing. Blue to see: blue produced by combination. Blue in radiance: the fresh, pure blue light issuing from those two. As with blue, so should yellow, red, and white be expounded at length. The rest as above. That two similes are given in each case displays the two kinds of form — that which arises together with the thing, and that which is produced by combination.

These eight bases of mastery serve as the support for cultivating the three liberations that take form as their object; and the last four bases of mastery contemplate by resolve the form of the gods of the realm of desire and the form of the realm of form. It should further be understood that these bases of mastery are for counteracting the attention that pursues the seeds of the lower levels, and not for counteracting the attention that is to be counteracted on one's own level.

Reading note

The gloss on "obtaining such a perception" is the most useful line in the section, and it names the exact failure the bases of mastery are guarding against. Mastery here does not mean seeing everything as superior, nor as inferior. It means that with respect to what is genuinely superior one perceives it as genuinely superior, and with respect to what is not, likewise — 無慢想, without a conceited perception in either case. The meditative achievement is accuracy without self-reference, which is harder than either flattery or dismissal.

The pairing of "knows" with calm abiding and "sees" with insight is a small piece of exegesis worth carrying: wherever the canonical phrase 勝知勝見 appears, the two verbs are not synonyms but the two halves of meditative practice. And the similes are worth noticing for what they are — a flower and a dyed cloth for each color. The text explains the doubling: one simile for form that arises with the thing, one for form produced by combination. Even a passage on the colors of meditation objects is being made to carry an Abhidharma distinction.

5. The Ten Totalities

Chinese Source

遍處者、謂十遍處，廣說如經。謂地遍處，一能解了上下及傍無二無量。如是水、火、風遍。青、黃、赤、白、虛空識遍上下及傍無二無量。地遍處者，由色所依遍滿故，彼能依色亦遍滿，由彼增長故。一能解了者，謂能證此觀補特伽羅。上下及傍者，謂遍滿諸方及四維故。無二者，離餘諸界，及不雜顯色，遍滿故。無量者，無有分齊，相遍滿故。如地遍處，餘水、火、風、青、黃、赤、白亦復如是，如其所應。虛空遍處者，謂對治一切色相作意思惟遍滿故，餘如前說。識遍處者，謂緣無量識作意思惟遍滿故，餘如前說。

此中由三解脫故得勝色自在，由得彼已方可說言勝色自在，極成就故。識處已上無有遍滿，所緣無量形段依止分別遠離故。應知勝處及與遍處，是諸解脫能清淨道，由諸勝處勝所緣故、由諸遍處所緣遍故，能令解脫清淨應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The totalities are the ten totalities, expounded at length in the scriptures — namely the earth totality, which one resolves upon as above, below, and across, non-dual and measureless; and likewise the water, fire, and wind totalities; and the blue, yellow, red, white, space, and consciousness totalities, above, below, and across, non-dual and measureless.

The earth totality: because that on which form depends is pervasive, the form that depends on it is likewise pervasive, since it grows from that. One resolves upon: the person able to realize this contemplation. Above, below, and across: because it pervades all the quarters and the four intermediate directions. Non-dual: because it is apart from the other elements and unmixed with visible color, and so pervades. Measureless: because there is no demarcation, and the mark pervades. As with the earth totality, so with water, fire, wind, blue, yellow, red, and white, each as is fitting. The space totality: because the attention that counteracts all marks of form is contemplated as pervading; the rest as above. The consciousness totality: because attention taking measureless consciousness as its object is contemplated as pervading; the rest as above.

Here it is through the three liberations that mastery over form is obtained; and only after obtaining them can one speak of mastery over form, since it is then fully accomplished. Above the sphere of consciousness there is no pervading, because the object there is measureless and is far removed from any discrimination resting on shape and extent. It should be understood that the bases of mastery and the totalities are the paths that purify the liberations: because the bases of mastery have superior objects, and because the totalities have pervasive objects, they can make the liberations pure.

Reading note

The closing sentence gives the architecture of these three lists, which are otherwise easy to receive as three unrelated inventories. The eight liberations are the attainments; the eight bases of mastery and the ten totalities are the paths that purify them — the first by giving the object superiority, the second by giving it pervasiveness. Mastery and totality are therefore not further attainments beyond liberation but the training that makes liberation clean.

The remark that there is no pervading above the sphere of consciousness explains why the list stops at ten. Nothingness and neither-perception-nor-non-perception cannot be totalities, because a totality is by definition something spread through all directions, and those two spheres have removed exactly the kind of object that could be spread. The ten totalities end where shape and extent end.

6. Non-contention, the Knowledge of Vows, and the Four Analytic Knowledges

Chinese Source

Reading note

Non-contention is often taken to mean an inner freedom from quarrelsomeness. The definition here is outward-facing and rather more interesting: it is the knowledge that can 守護他煩惱行, guard against others' afflicted conduct — that is, the capacity to conduct oneself so that one does not become the occasion of another person's affliction. Non-contention is a skill in not provoking, and it is classed as a form of knowledge rather than of restraint.

The four analytic knowledges divide the work of exposition into names, meanings, etymologies, and eloquence — and the fourth is what makes the other three usable. Note that all six qualities in this section share a single formula: 無癡智見性, the nature of undeluded knowledge and vision, with concentration and its mental factors. These are not separate faculties but one clarity described by what it is applied to.

Third, the power realized through the knowledge and vision of the differences of minds: in order to enter into the conduct of others' minds in every aspect; the rest as above. Fourth, the power realized through the knowledge and vision that recollects former abodes: in order to enter into the courses of conduct of the prior limit in every aspect; the rest as above. Reading note

Each of the six is defined by a purpose clause — 為, in order to — rather than by a capacity possessed. This keeps them continuous with what fascicle 3 said of the practice of supernormal powers: that they serve to draw in beings not yet within the teaching. The first is glossed simply as displaying mastery over bodily action, which is a notably deflationary account of what the tradition otherwise describes as walking through walls and multiplying bodies.

The sixth is the odd one out and always has been. Five of these powers are shared with non-Buddhist adepts; the knowledge of the exhaustion of the contaminants is not, and it is the only one whose purpose is deliverance rather than display or perception. Its inclusion in the list is what makes the six a Buddhist set rather than a catalogue of yogic attainments.

The single mark that is expounded is expounded on a strict four-part pattern — the cause that brought it about, the effect it has on a beholder, and then three things to be said of it: its mark, its emblem, its shape. Asaṅga gives one worked example and then tells the reader to run the same pattern over the other thirty-one. This is characteristic of the treatise's whole method, announced in its opening verse: 文約義周, the words brief, the meaning complete.

Note the causal claim in the example. The mark of well-set feet is brought about (感得) by having fully maintained 平等行, conduct that was even — an ethical cause producing a physical mark. The thirty-two are not divine endowments but karmic results, and the passage says plainly what they are for: a person who sees the Buddha even briefly is convinced. They are a teaching aid, working on those who have not yet heard a word.

Chinese Source

Reading note

The repeated closing formula — 能如實問記, able to answer truly — reframes what a "power" is here. These ten are not capacities to act but capacities to answer: whatever question is put about causation, karma, meditative states, faculties, dispositions, tendencies, destinations, past lives, future lives, or the means of liberation, the Tathāgata can respond truthfully and without obstruction. The powers are the epistemic ground of the fearlessnesses that follow immediately after, which are precisely about being unafraid of challenge.

Powers four, five, and six form a diagnostic trio that matches the fourth measureless attention in fascicle 3 — faculties keen or dull, dispositions, and the depth of latent tendencies. What a bodhisattva contemplates in order to know how to teach, a buddha simply knows without obstruction. The first purity is the strangest and most striking item in the section: mastery in 取住捨, taking up, maintaining, and laying down his own support — that is, over the possession of a body itself.

10. The Four Fearlessnesses

Each fearlessness is staged as a challenge that could be brought and cannot land. Two concern what the Buddha has attained; two concern what he teaches.

Chinese Source

無畏者，謂四無畏，廣說如經。一、佛作誠言：我是正等覺者。若有難言：於是法中不正等覺。我於此難正見無緣，是故無畏。謂如來證得妙善清淨一切種智故。二、佛作誠言：我諸漏已盡。若有難言：如是如是諸漏未盡。我於此難正見無緣，是故無畏。謂如來證得妙善清淨一切種斷故。此二無畏依自利德。

三、佛作誠言：我為弟子說障礙法，染必為障。若有難言：染習此法不能為障。我於此難正見無緣，是故無畏。謂依如來為所化有情說一切種所對治法。四、佛作誠言：我為弟子說出離道，修定出離。若有難言：雖修此道不能出離，不正盡苦及證苦邊。我於此難正見無緣，是故無畏。謂依如來為所化有情說一切種能對治法。此二無畏依利他德。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fearlessnesses are the four fearlessnesses, expounded at length in the scriptures.

First, the Buddha declares truly: I am perfectly enlightened. Should anyone object, "You are not perfectly enlightened with respect to this teaching," then — I see rightly that there is no ground for such an objection, and therefore I am without fear. This is because the Tathāgata has realized the excellent, wholesome, and pure knowledge of all aspects.

Second, the Buddha declares truly: my contaminants are exhausted. Should anyone object, "Such and such contaminants are not yet exhausted," then — I see rightly that there is no ground for such an objection, and therefore I am without fear. This is because the Tathāgata has realized the excellent, wholesome, and pure abandonment of all aspects. These two fearlessnesses rest on the qualities of benefit to himself.

Third, the Buddha declares truly: I have taught my disciples the obstructing states, and defilement is certainly an obstruction. Should anyone object, "Practicing these defilements cannot obstruct," then — I see rightly that there is no ground for such an objection, and therefore I am without fear. This rests on the Tathāgata's teaching to beings of all the aspects of what is to be counteracted.

Fourth, the Buddha declares truly: I have taught my disciples the path of deliverance, and by cultivating concentration one is delivered. Should anyone object, "Though one cultivates this path one is not delivered, and does not rightly exhaust suffering or realize its end," then — I see rightly that there is no ground for such an objection, and therefore I am without fear. This rests on the Tathāgata's teaching to beings of all the aspects of what can counteract. These two fearlessnesses rest on the qualities of benefit to others.

Reading note

The formula 正見無緣 is worth translating carefully, because the whole structure turns on it: I see rightly that there is no ground. Fearlessness is not composure in the face of criticism, nor confidence in one's own rightness. It is the state of having actually looked for what the objection could rest on and having seen that there is nothing there. The Buddha's fearlessness is described as an act of seeing, not of feeling — which is consistent with the ten powers just given, all of them defined as the capacity to answer truly.

The two-and-two division is stated explicitly and is worth keeping: the first pair concerns what the Buddha has attained, the second what he has taught. A teacher can be beyond reproach in attainment and still be challenged on the adequacy of the teaching — that the things he calls obstructions are not really obstructions, or that the path he prescribes does not actually deliver. Asaṅga gives those two objections equal weight with the first two, and answers them the same way.

11. What Needs No Guarding, the Three Establishments of Mindfulness, and the End of Habitual Traces

Chinese Source

不護者，謂三不護，廣說如經。一、如來現行身業妙善清淨，無不清淨現行身業可須覆藏，是故不護。謂如來一切種一切時身業妙善清淨故，為所化有情正說法時，能以勝力折伏攝受一切徒眾。如身業不護，如是第二語業不護、第三意業不護應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

What needs no guarding: the three things needing no guarding, expounded at length in the scriptures. First, the Tathāgata's bodily action as it operates is excellent, wholesome, and pure; there is no impure bodily action of his that would need to be hidden away, and therefore it needs no guarding. That is, because the Tathāgata's bodily action is excellent, wholesome, and pure in every aspect and at every time, when he rightly teaches the Dharma to beings he can, by that surpassing power, both subdue and gather in every assembly. The establishments of mindfulness: the three establishments of mindfulness, expounded at length in the scriptures. That is, when gathering in an assembly to be trained, he abides in supreme equanimity with respect to the differences of conduct among the three kinds of assembly — a mind that neither loves, nor resents, nor is defiled.

The permanent ending of the habitual traces: the Tathāgatas have gone out from the births and deaths of beginningless, measureless, incalculable great aeons; and because they have realized a nature that does not follow along after them, they realize the Tathāgata's excellent and pure knowledge and abandonment.

Reading note

The three things needing no guarding are stated in terms of concealment, and the reasoning is worth following. Every teacher has conduct that must be managed in front of an audience — kept back, presented carefully. The Tathāgata has nothing of the kind in body, speech, or mind, and the consequence drawn is not moral but rhetorical: because there is nothing to hide, he can teach with a force that both subdues and attracts an assembly. Authority here is a function of having no concealment to maintain.

The three establishments of mindfulness in this Mahāyāna sense are quite different from the four of the thirty-seven: they concern the teacher's mind toward three kinds of audience — traditionally those who listen well, those who do not, and those who are mixed. The mind neither loves the first, resents the second, nor is defiled by the third. And the ending of habitual traces names what distinguishes a buddha from an arhat in this system: an arhat has cut the afflictions, but the residual behavioral habits they left behind — the traces — go only with buddhahood.

Thirteenth, unattached and unobstructed knowledge with respect to the past: because in the instant of turning his attention to it he knows it in every aspect. As with the past, so should the fourteenth, with respect to the future, and the fifteenth, with respect to the present, be understood. Sixteenth, all the Tathāgata's bodily action has knowledge as its guide and proceeds in accordance with knowledge: because it is aroused and taken up by knowledge, and is therefore skillful in means at all times. As with bodily action, so should the seventeenth, verbal action, and the eighteenth, mental action, be understood.

Reading note

The fourth reason the great compassion is called great deserves attention, because it is the one that concerns the object rather than the subject. The other three are about the compassion itself — its basis, its long cultivation, the knowledge that draws it out. The fourth is about what it looks at: suffering that is 極深固, utterly deep-set, and 種種堅牢, stubborn in every variety. Great compassion is great in part because of how bad the thing it faces actually is. It is not enlarged by being diffused.

The sixth exclusive quality is the moral center of the eighteen. 無不擇已捨 — no relinquishing without deliberation — is glossed as never finally abandoning the affairs of beings. A buddha may set something down, but never inadvertently, and never for good. Read alongside the fifth and fourth measureless attentions of fascicle 3, where leaving someone alone was listed as a means of training, this is the guarantee that such abandonment is always tactical.

Note the recurring qualifier 不染污 in the first three. The claim is not that a buddha never laughs or never misspeaks in any sense, but that there is nothing defiled of that kind. This is the standard way the tradition preserves the accounts of the Buddha's ordinary behavior in the scriptures while making these exclusive claims.

13. The Knowledge of All Aspects

Chinese Source

That is, the nature of unobstructed knowledge with respect to the differences of number and mark, in every aspect, among the two kinds of things, defiled and pure — together with the concentration associated with it and all the mind and mental factors.

Reading note

The second paragraph is doing something the first does not prepare one for. Having defined omniscience as unobstructed knowledge of defiled and pure things, Asaṅga adds that the whole visible career of a buddha — being born, having a body, speaking, awakening under the tree, turning the wheel, entering final quiescence — is also called the knowledge of all aspects. The events of the Buddha's life are here classified not as biography but as an operation of omniscient knowledge, displayed because there are beings for whom something must be done. The Tathāgata "abides in the uncontaminated realm" throughout; what appears in the world is display.

The closing sentence is the treatise being honest about its own scale. Everything in this long section on the qualities has been an abbreviation, given 為釋經義, in order to explain the meaning of the scriptures, with the reader referred to the Bodhisattva Grounds of the Treatise on the Stages of Yogic Practice for the full treatment. Asaṅga is condensing his own source and says so.

14. Why There Are Exactly Nine Topics, in This Order

With all nine topics expounded, the chapter turns back on itself. A long verse gives the rationale for the number and the sequence — and reveals that the nine were structured all along as one basis, two defilements, and six purifications.

Ru-Yi Translation

The exposition: among the nine topics above, the first — everything — is the support of two things: the support of defilement, and the support of purity. By the next two topics defilement is subsumed: by the topic of the realms, and by the topic of defilement. By the last six topics purity is subsumed: by the topic of the truths, of the basis, of the factors of enlightenment, of beings, of the fruits, and of the qualities.

By the two topics, defilement is subsumed in respect of the support of defilement and the differences of defilement. By the six topics, purity is subsumed in respect of: the objective domain of purity; the mind's not scattering within that domain; the right means, undistracted, with respect to that object; the differences of stage in one who has that right means; the causes of speech and the rest; the fruit of the right means; and the qualities of that fruit. Among these, the differences of stage and the causes of speech — these two establish the topic of beings, as should be understood. The causes of speech and the rest means: for ease of speech; for according with the world; for avoiding what is fearful; and for causing people to know with confidence the accomplishment of the qualities and the faults of themselves and others. For these reasons the number of these nine topics is fixed, and their sequence is fixed, as should be understood.

Reading note

This is the chapter's own architecture, and it is worth setting out plainly. Topic one, "everything," is neutral ground — the inventory of what exists, which serves equally as the support of defilement and of purity. Topics two and three are the defiled world: where beings are, and what is wrong with them. Topics four through nine are purification, and they run in a strict functional order: the truths give the object, the basis keeps the mind from scattering on it, the factors of enlightenment are the right means, persons are the stages of those means, the fruits are the result, and the qualities are what the result possesses. Nothing in the chapter was arranged by convention.

The most curious item is the justification for the topic of persons. Two reasons are given: persons mark the differences of stage, and persons are 言說等因, the causes of speech. That second reason is then unpacked in a way that is unusually candid about the pragmatics of doctrine — one speaks of persons for ease of speech, to accord with how the world talks, to avoid what is fearful, and so that people may confidently recognize qualities and faults in themselves and others. In a system that denies the self, the category "person" is retained because one cannot teach without it. That is a real answer, given in one line, to a question the tradition has been asked for two thousand years.

Having fixed the number and the order, the chapter provides an index: the Buddha's entire word can be surveyed under two headings, matter and concept, and the concept side is then given as four sets of summary verses.

Reading note

An 嗢拕南 (uddāna) is a mnemonic verse — a string of catchwords, each standing for a body of material, strung together so the whole can be held in memory and unpacked in order. What follows in the next sections is the unpacking: each catchword in these four verses is given its content in turn. The four verses together are meant to be an exhaustive index of Buddhist doctrine under which any question and answer can be filed.

The distinction between matter (事) and concept (想) is the organizing move. Under "matter" the Buddha's words are indexed by the form of the scripture — collections arranged by ascending number, expositions of a broad meaning, collections of differing topics, which correspond to recognizable genres of the canon. Under "concept" they are indexed by topic, and that is what these forty-odd catchwords provide. Between the two indexes, one can find one's way to anything the Buddha said.

Reading note

The seam in this list is easy to spot and is placed deliberately. Everything down to the six supernormal powers is shared vocabulary — realms, feelings, truths, absorptions, the thirty-seven, the four fruits. Then comes 復依廣乘, "further, in dependence on the Great Vehicle," and the register changes completely: the five items, the two emptinesses, the three natures, the three naturelessnesses, the four investigations. Asaṅga's closing sentence names the division outright. This is a doxographic map disguised as a word list, and it says that the two vehicles do not disagree about the shared vocabulary; the Great Vehicle simply keeps going.

Several of the Mahāyāna entries are the seeds of whole treatises elsewhere on this site. The four investigations and their four resulting full knowledges are the method of the Mahāyānasaṃgraha; the three naturelessnesses are the subject of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra's central chapter; the two extremes of superimposition and denial are what the Madhyāntavibhāga is named for. Here each gets a single line, because here they are index entries.

Reading note

The entry on nominal designation is the shortest complete statement of Yogācāra's two-tier account of the conventional that this treatise gives. A being is designated nominally upon nothing but things — that is, "person" is a label placed on dharmas. But dharmas in turn are designated nominally upon nothing but marks. The move that Abhidharma made once, dissolving the person into dharmas, is here made twice, and the second application is what distinguishes the Mahāyāna's selflessness of phenomena from the śrāvaka's selflessness of persons.

The forward reference at the end is worth flagging for readers following this project: 如成現觀品當說, "as will be stated in the chapter on Establishing Direct Realization," points ahead to the eighth chapter of this treatise, which falls in fascicles 16 and 17. Asaṅga is cross-referencing his own work in progress.

Thirteen catchwords, and the first five are the five aggregates given under new names — a compact demonstration of how the same material can be re-indexed.

Chinese Source

Reading note

The five aggregates renamed are the most interesting entries in this verse. Form is called place, because form is what occupies extension. Feeling is called position, because a feeling is where one stands with respect to what is met. Perception is discrimination; formations are action; and consciousness is appropriation, 執持, the same word used in fascicle 1 for what the ālayavijñāna does to the sense faculties. Each alias names the aggregate by its function rather than its content.

The last entry, hoarding, is the sharpest thing in the verse. 藏護 — to store up and guard — is glossed as yearning after the past, hoping for the future, and clinging to the present. It names as a single act of hoarding what one might otherwise take for three separate temporal failings. The mind's relation to all three times is one behavior: keeping.

Reading note

The five conditions of association are the technical heart of this verse and repay slow reading, because they are stated as five successive filters. A factor is associated with what is other than itself, never with itself; among those others, only with the compatible; among the compatible, only with those of matching grade; among those, only with the simultaneous; and among the simultaneous, only with those on the same level of the realms. Each clause removes a candidate class. This is what the word "associated" has been doing throughout the treatise's definitions of the mental factors, unpacked at last.

The long paired list under "discernment" is not meant to be read as prose. It is a checklist of the axes along which any dharma can be located — a set of questions to put to whatever one is examining. Notice that it is exactly this kind of exhaustive cross-classification that the Abhidharmasamuccaya performs at length, item by item, over hundreds of pages. Here the same apparatus is compressed into an index entry.

Reading note

The seven attentions are the standard Yogācāra sequence for working through any level of practice, and they map onto how meditation on a given stage actually proceeds: first one understands what one is dealing with; then one resolves upon it; then one becomes secluded from what is being left behind; then one gathers up the delight of what has been gained; then one examines whether the work is really done; then one brings the means to their culmination; and last one abides in the fruit of that culmination. The fifth is the safeguard against premature satisfaction, placed exactly where a practitioner would be most tempted to stop.

The entry on the sacred teaching is a small history of how the Buddha actually taught, and it is given in the order of a gradual training: refuges, precepts, provision for hearers, the teacher-disciple relation, and then the classical graduated discourse — giving, moral discipline, birth in the heavens, the faults of desire — before defilement and purity are set out and deliverance is taught. The famous sequence is here treated as one entry in an index, but the ordering is preserved intact.

The exposition: if practitioners would rightly practice with respect to the nine topics above — fully knowing, permanently abandoning, realizing, and cultivating and gathering the qualities and the rest — they should train by the ten Dharma practices and the six approaches. The ten Dharma practices have been stated above. The six approaches are: the approach of the truly real; the approach of realization; the approach of guidance; the approach that departs from the two extremes; the approach of the inconceivable; and the approach of intent.

Reading note

The four verbs applied to the nine topics are the four tasks of the four noble truths, redeployed: fully knowing, permanently abandoning, realizing, and cultivating. What was said of suffering, origin, cessation, and path is here said of the whole nine-topic inventory. This is the chapter's closing claim about its own status — it is not a reference work but a set of things to be done.

The six approaches are named and not explained, and the omission is itself informative: the last of them, 意樂理趣, the approach of intent, is the hermeneutical principle that a teaching must be read for what the teacher intended by it rather than for what it literally says. Having just spent an entire chapter building an exhaustive index of doctrine, Asaṅga closes by naming intent as one of the six ways in which all of it must be approached. The ten Dharma practices — from copying a text out to taking it as the object of calm abiding and insight — supply the other half of the answer, and readers will find them in fascicle 2.

This completes the chapter on Gathering the Topics, which has run across four fascicles: everything, the realms, defilement, the truths, the basis, the factors of enlightenment, persons, the fruits, and the qualities — nine topics, shown at the end to be one neutral support, two defilements, and six purifications, in a sequence Asaṅga argues could not be otherwise.
