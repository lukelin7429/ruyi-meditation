Mahāsthāmaprāpta's Chapter on Recollecting the Buddha

from the Śūraṅgama Sūtra《大勢至菩薩念佛圓通章》

A 244-character testimony, spoken by the bodhisattva Mahāsthāmaprāpta before the Buddha — the shortest of the "five Pure Land sūtras," and the one that gives the tradition its central image: a mother and child who are never far apart, so long as both remember each other.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T945, Śūraṅgama Sūtra, Fascicle 5 Reading notes after Yuanying's 楞嚴經講義 Status: complete
Chinese title
大勢至菩薩念佛圓通章 — "Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva's Chapter on Perfect Penetration Through Recollecting the Buddha"
Source
Not an independent sūtra in the Taishō canon — an excerpt from Fascicle 5 of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra (楞嚴經, T945), traditionally attributed to the early-8th-century translator Paramiti; the twenty-fourth of twenty-five bodhisattvas' testimonies of how each first entered samādhi
Tradition
Mahāyāna — added to the Pure Land canon in the early 20th century by the Republican-era master Yinguang (印光), as the fourth of the "five Pure Land sūtras" (淨土五經), immediately before the Vows of Samantabhadra
Speaker
Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva (大勢至菩薩), together with fifty-two companion bodhisattvas, answering the Buddha's own question about how each of the twenty-five assembled sages first attained samādhi

244 Chinese characters — the shortest text on this site's Pure Land shelf by a wide margin, short enough to memorize in an afternoon
Difficulty
★☆☆☆☆ to read — plain, image-driven prose built around two analogies (mutual remembrance between friends; a mother and her child). The difficulty, such as it is, lies entirely in the four-character phrase the whole chapter is famous for: 都攝六根，淨念相繼, "gathering in all six sense-faculties, this pure recollection continuing without a break."

Why a Chapter, Not a Sūtra

Unlike the other four texts on this site's Pure Land shelf, this one has no independent existence in the Taishō canon at all. It is 244 characters lifted from the middle of Fascicle 5 of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra — one testimony among twenty-five, offered by twenty-five different bodhisattvas and arhats, each describing to the Buddha how they personally first broke through to samādhi. Twenty-three of the twenty-four testimonies that precede it work through a sense-faculty or an element — sight, sound, taste, earth, water, fire, the mind itself. Mahāsthāmaprāpta's is the twenty-fourth, and its method is neither a sense-faculty nor an element in the usual sense: it is the recollection of a buddha's name.

It was Yinguang (印光, 1861–1940), the thirteenth patriarch of the Pure Land school as later tradition counts him, who lifted this passage out of its original setting and placed it alongside the three classical Pure Land sūtras — the Amitābha Sūtra, the Immeasurable Life Sūtra, and the Contemplation Sūtra — reasoning that a canon built entirely around Amitābha's own words was missing the direct, personal testimony of one of his own two chief attendant bodhisattvas. He later added a fifth text, the closing chapter of the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, completing what practitioners now call the "five Pure Land sūtras" (淨土五經). Read on its own terms, within the Śūraṅgama Sūtra itself, this passage is one contemplative method among twenty-five, offered without particular privilege. Read as Yinguang re-framed it, lifted free of that context and placed at the head of its own tradition, it becomes something closer to a manifesto: the single clearest statement, anywhere in the canon, of what it actually means to hold a buddha in mind.

The Chapter, in Full

This site's full translation of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, together with Yuanying's line-by-line commentary, already includes this passage in its original setting — see Fascicle 14 of that commentary translation for the complete text with every gloss reproduced in full. What follows here is the same translation, read as its own short, self-contained chapter.

Chinese Source

大勢至法王子，與其同倫，五十二菩薩，即從座起，頂禮佛足，而白佛言：

Ru-Yi Translation

Mahāsthāmaprāpta, the Dharma-Prince, together with his fellow companions, fifty-two bodhisattvas in all, rose at once from their seats, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha:

Chinese Source

我憶往昔，恆河沙劫，有佛出世，名無量光，十二如來，相繼一劫，其最後佛，名超日月光，彼佛教我，念佛三昧。

Ru-Yi Translation

"I recall how, in a kalpa as distant as the sands of the Ganges, there appeared a buddha named Boundless Light — twelve Tathāgatas appearing one after another within this same kalpa — and the very last of these, named Light Surpassing Sun and Moon, taught me the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha."

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying traces Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own name to three sources at once: the Contemplation Sūtra's "with the light of his own wisdom, illuminating everything universally, causing beings to depart from the three lower destinies and attain unsurpassed power — hence he is named Mahāsthāmaprāpta"; the Sūtra of Questions of Viśeṣacintā-brahma's more startling gloss, "wherever I set my own foot down, the whole great-thousand-world-system trembles, together with every demon palace within it"; and the Sūtra of Compassionate Flowers, which recounts that in a past age, when Amitābha served as a wheel-turning king, Avalokiteśvara was his eldest son and Mahāsthāmaprāpta his second — both now dwelling at Amitābha's own left and right hand in the Land of Bliss, each awaiting, in turn, his own eventual succession to buddhahood. On the "fifty-two bodhisattvas" who rise alongside him, Yuanying is explicit that this is not a literal headcount: it names the fifty-two classical stages of the bodhisattva path (the ten faiths, ten abidings, ten practices, ten transferences, ten grounds, and equal enlightenment), meaning that everyone who has ever taken up this same practice of recollecting the Buddha, at whatever stage of the path, rises together with him in this verse.

On why this testimony is placed twenty-fourth, immediately after Maitreya's account of the consciousness-element and immediately before Avalokiteśvara's ear-faculty account closes the assembly, Yuanying draws a direct parallel between the two bodhisattvas placed at the very end: Avalokiteśvara is placed last because the ear-faculty is what best suits beings of this particular world, and Mahāsthāmaprāpta is placed second-to-last for the same reason in reverse — his method of recollecting the Buddha may not be the single sharpest tool for this world's own ear-trained beings, but weighed against the needs of beings across all ten directions at once, "simplest, easiest, most sudden and complete of all," it is foremost by a different, wider measure.

Chinese Source

譬如有人：一專為憶，一人專忘，如是二人，若逢不逢，或見非見。二人相憶，二憶念深，如是乃至，從生至生，同於形影，不相乖異。

Ru-Yi Translation

"It is like two people, one wholly given to remembering the other, while the other constantly forgets him — such two people, whether they happen to meet or not, whether they see each other or not, remain, in effect, as good as strangers. But should both, instead, remember each other, this shared remembrance growing ever deeper between them, then, life after life, they remain together like a form and its own shadow, never parting from one another."

Chinese Source

十方如來，憐念眾生，如母憶子。若子逃逝，雖憶何為？子若憶母，如母憶時，母子歷生，不相違遠。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The Tathāgatas of the ten directions, in their compassion, hold sentient beings in mind exactly as a mother holds her own child in mind. But should the child run away and disappear, what use would the mother's own remembrance be? Yet should the child hold his own mother in mind exactly as the mother holds him in mind, then mother and child, through every life they pass through, are never far apart from one another."

Reading note — after Yuanying

Yuanying reads these two analogies as one argument told twice, each time with the stakes raised. The first pair — two friends, one remembering, one forgetting — shows only that memory held on a single side accomplishes nothing: "should they happen to meet, or see each other, it amounts, in effect, to not meeting, to not seeing, at all." He illustrates this with the story of Chan Master Fenggan, said in local tradition to be Amitābha's own manifestation, who lived unrecognized at Guoqing Monastery on Mount Tiantai for years, milling rice and riding a tiger in and out of the grounds, until an official named Lü Qiuyin, cured of a headache by his touch, finally sought him out — only for Fenggan to have already vanished, along with the monks Hanshan and Shide, once revealed as manifestations of Mañjuśrī and Samantabhadra. The Buddha's own compassion, Yuanying says, is present in the world in exactly this way at every moment; what is missing, on the human side, is the remembering.

The second analogy tightens the relation from friendship to blood: "among all worldly forms of loving affection, none is more urgent and intense than a mother's own love for her child." Yuanying draws out the asymmetry the passage insists on — a mother's love for her child, however constant, still weakens if the child grows openly rebellious, "her own heart filling with regret and resentment instead," while a buddha's regard for beings does not: "the more severe a being's own rebellion and wrongdoing, the deeper still the Buddha's own thought of him grows." Where an ordinary mother's love is bounded by a single lifetime, this compassion follows a being "life after life, never once regressing." The child who runs away and disappears entirely — not merely forgetful now, but actively fleeing — is Yuanying's gloss for outright unbelief; he cites the old story of a woman east of the city whom the Buddha wished to liberate but who fled into her own room and covered her eyes rather than look at him, even after he made the very wall before her turn transparent as glass. No amount of being remembered can reach a mind that has turned entirely away.

Chinese Source

若眾生心，憶佛念佛，現前當來，必定見佛，去佛不遠，不假方便，自得心開。如染香人，身有香氣，此則名曰：香光莊嚴。

Ru-Yi Translation

"If a sentient being's own mind holds the Buddha in mind and recollects him, then, now or in ages to come, that being is certain to behold the Buddha, drawing near to the Buddha before very long — requiring no further skillful means at all, this mind opens of itself. It is like a person steeped in fragrance, whose own body naturally carries this same fragrance with it — this is called being adorned by fragrance and light."

Chinese Source

我本因地，以念佛心，入無生忍。今於此界，攝念佛人，歸於淨土。

Ru-Yi Translation

"At my own causal stage, I took up this very mind of recollecting the Buddha, and thereby entered the patient acceptance of the unborn. Now, in this present world, I gather in those who recollect the Buddha, and lead them home to the Pure Land."

Reading note — after Yuanying

On "gathering in the mind" (憶佛念佛), Yuanying insists on a distinction the chapter's brevity leaves easy to miss: "some in the world hold that recollecting the Buddha is merely a matter of reciting with the mouth — this is mistaken; and even to say it is a matter of recollecting with the discriminating mind of consciousness is likewise mistaken." He distinguishes recollection through actual practice (事持) — a mind fixed single-mindedly on the Buddha's name, moment following moment without a break — from recollection through principle (理持), in which the recollecting mind and the Buddha recollected are seen to be one and the same thing, "subject and object not two different things at all." He treats these not as rival methods but as one practice deepening into the other: the beginner practices the first; the fruit of long practice is the second.

On "the fragrance of one steeped in incense," Yuanying maps the image directly onto three levels of capacity: the sharpest practitioner is steeped in the fragrance of the Buddha's own mind (recollection through principle), the middling practitioner in the fragrance of his body, and the beginner in the fragrance of his name alone (recollection through practice) — three depths of the same single method, not three different methods. And on Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own attainment — "I took up this very mind of recollecting the Buddha, and thereby entered the patient acceptance of the unborn" — Yuanying notes that this bodhisattva's own rank is equal enlightenment, one stage short of buddhahood itself, and reaches for the classical image of an elephant, a horse, and a rabbit crossing the same river at three different depths: "the rabbit crosses along the surface, its feet barely touching the water; the horse stretches its head above the water; the elephant submerges its entire body, walking along the very riverbed below." Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own forbearance, on this reading, was realized at the elephant's depth — proof, Yuanying says, that this single practice carries a beginner and a bodhisattva of equal enlightenment alike, each according to how far into the water they have gone.

Chinese Source

佛問圓通，我無選擇，都攝六根，淨念相繼，得三摩地，斯為第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

"As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: without any need for further selection at all, simply gathering in all six sense-faculties together at once, this pure recollection continuing without a break, and thereby attaining samādhi, is foremost."

Reading note — after Yuanying

The chapter's most quoted phrase gets Yuanying's most careful gloss. "Gathering in all six sense-faculties" (都攝六根) does not mean choosing one sense-faculty over the other five, the way the twenty-three testimonies before this one each worked through a single faculty or element; it means gathering the underlying, undivided brightness common to all six at once, "no longer letting it rely on any single faculty to condition any single object — and once this single, essential brightness is thus gathered in, the function of all six faculties ceases to operate separately at all." This is why Yuanying calls this chapter's own method "the great element of the faculties" (根大), distinct from every faculty-specific method that came before it in the assembly. "Pure recollection continuing without a break" (淨念相繼) he glosses just as precisely: freedom from every stray thought is the "pure," a mind fixed single-mindedly on the Buddha is the "recollection," and unbroken continuity — "moment after moment, without a single interruption" — is what turns an ordinary recitation into this samādhi's own cause.

He closes by taking up the question the chapter's own placement invites: if Mañjuśrī, judging among all twenty-five testimonies at the end of this same fascicle, selects Avalokiteśvara's ear-faculty method as best suited to this world, how can Mahāsthāmaprāpta's method also be called "foremost"? His answer keeps both claims standing at once, at two different scales: "matched to the particular capacity of this world — the sentient beings of this Sahā world having the sharpest ear-faculty of all — Avalokiteśvara's own method is rightly selected. But considered generally, applicable throughout the ten directions at once... there is no other gate whatsoever that can compare to it, and so it, too, is rightly called foremost." One method is the sharpest tool for beings born here; the other is the widest gate for beings anywhere at all — and Yinguang's decision, nine centuries after Yuanying wrote this, to lift precisely this second claim out of its original setting and place it at the head of an entire tradition, is itself already an answer to which scale mattered more to him.
