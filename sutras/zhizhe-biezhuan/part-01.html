Separate Biography of Great Master Zhizhe of Tiantai

This is the earliest full life of Zhiyi (538–597), written by his disciple Guanding (561–632) soon after the master's death; Guanding says at the end that he began collecting the facts in 601. It follows Zhiyi from his family background and childhood vows, through his ordination, his training under Huisi at Mount Dasu, where he awakened while reciting the Lotus, and his eight years of teaching at Waguan Monastery in the Chen capital, Jinling. It then describes his withdrawal to Mount Tiantai in 575, his night on Huading peak, the founding of a pond for releasing life, his summons back to the Chen court, and, after the Sui conquest, his relationship with Yang Guang, the Prince of Jin and later Emperor Yang, who received the bodhisattva precepts from him and gave him the title Zhizhe. The final sections record his last instructions, his death at Shicheng in 597, ten signs reported after his death, a list of his writings, notes on several disciples, and Guanding's own account of how he compiled the work. Much of the text quotes letters exchanged with princes and rulers, which are translated in full.

Family, Birth, and Childhood

Chinese Source

大師諱智顗。字德安。俗姓陳氏。頴川人也。高宗茂績盛傳於譜史矣。暨晉世遷都。家隨南出寓居江漢。因止荊州之華容縣。父起祖學通經傳談吐絕倫。而武策運籌偏多勇決。梁湘東王蕭繹之荊州。列為賓客。奉教入朝領軍。朱异見而歎曰。若非經國之才。孰為英王之所重乎。孝元即位拜使持節散騎常侍益陽縣開國侯。母徐氏溫良恭儉。偏勤齋戒夢香煙五彩輕浮。若霧縈迴在懷欲拂去之。聞人語曰。宿世因緣寄託王道。福德自至何以去之。又夢吞白鼠因覺體重。至於載誕夜現神光揀宇。煥然兼輝隣室。隣里憶先靈瑞呼為王道。兼用後相復名光道。故小立二字。眼有重瞳父母藏護不欲人知。而人自知之矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Great Master's personal name was Zhiyi and his courtesy name De'an. His secular surname was Chen, and he was a man of Yingchuan. The distinguished achievements of his ancestors are amply recorded in the genealogies and histories. When the Jin moved its capital, the family followed it south and took up residence in the region of the Yangzi and Han, settling in Huarong county of Jingzhou. His father, Qizu, was learned in the classics and their commentaries and unequaled in conversation, and in military strategy and planning he was especially bold and decisive. When Xiao Yi, Prince of Xiangdong of the Liang, went to Jingzhou, he was enrolled among the prince's guests; at the prince's command he entered the court and commanded troops. Zhu Yi saw him and remarked with admiration, "If he were not a man with the talent to govern a state, how would he be valued by so heroic a prince?" When [Emperor] Xiaoyuan ascended the throne, he was appointed Commissioner Bearing the Credentials, Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary, and Founding Marquis of Yiyang county. His mother, of the Xu family, was gentle, good, respectful, and frugal, and especially diligent in keeping the fasts and precepts. She dreamed that incense smoke of five colors drifted lightly like mist and swirled into her bosom. She wished to brush it away, but heard someone say, "This is a karmic connection from former lives, entrusted to the royal path. Blessings and virtue come of themselves; why brush them away?" She also dreamed that she swallowed a white mouse, and on waking felt her body heavy. On the night of his birth a divine light appeared, lighting up the house and shining brightly into the neighboring rooms as well. The neighbors, recalling the earlier auspicious signs, called him Wangdao ("Royal Path"); adding the later sign, they also named him Guangdao ("Path of Light"). Hence as a child he bore both names. He had double pupils in his eyes; his parents hid this and did not want people to know, but people came to know it anyway.

Ru-Yi Translation

At the age of seven he loved to go to the monasteries. The monks taught him the "Universal Gate" chapter by word of mouth, and after a single recitation he had it. But his parents forbade him and would not let him go often. He kept going over what he had recited, regretting what he had not yet heard, and suddenly, of himself, he knew the rest of the text; later, when checked against the sūtra, nothing was missing. The villagers marveled: "Reviewing the old and knowing the new — is it like this?" At fifteen he met the fall of [Emperor] Xiaoyuan; his family and state were destroyed, and his relatives scattered and were displaced. He lamented how hard it is for glory and gatherings to last, and grieved at how easily ruin and separation come. Before the image at Changsha he made a great and broad vow: he vowed to become a śramaṇa and take upon himself the bearing of the true Dharma as his heavy charge. His sincerity having been answered, he dreamed that the auspicious image flew to the courtyard of his house, put a golden hand in through a crack in the window, and stroked his head three times. From then on he deeply loathed the prison of household life and thought of ending the root of suffering. But his parents, out of love, would not yet give permission. Though he tried to comply with them, he could not sleep or eat in peace. So he carved sandalwood and drew images, opened the canon and searched the sūtras, and day and night bowed and recited, thought following thought without a break.

Ru-Yi Translation

Once, while bowing to the Buddha with his whole body cast on the ground, he suddenly, as in a dream, saw an extremely high mountain overlooking a great sea, clear and still, lush and luxuriant, each setting off the other. At the summit was a monk who beckoned him to come up. In a moment the monk stretched out his arm down to the foot of the mountain, took hold of him and helped him climb, and led him into a monastery. There he saw the image he had made, inside the hall. In the dream he wept and stated his vow: to learn the Buddhadharma of the three times, to answer a thousand masters of treatises and expound without obstruction, and not to let the kindness of the four requisites given by the world be in vain. The monk who had stretched out his arm raised his hand, pointed to the image, and said further, "You will dwell here. You will end your life here." When he awoke, he found himself prostrate before the Buddha, and the tears of his dream had fallen to the ground in a stream. With sorrow and joy mingled in his heart, he was more diligent than ever.

Leaving Home

Chinese Source

後遭二親殄喪丁艱荼毒。逮于服訖從兄求去。兄曰。天已喪我親汝重割我心。既孤更離安可忍乎。跪而對曰。昔梁荊百萬一朝僕妾。于時久役江。湖之心不能復處。碨磊之內欲報恩酧德。當謀道為先唐聚何益。銘肌刻骨意不可移。時王琳據湘。從琳求去。琳以陳侯故舊。又嘉此志節資給法具。深助隨喜年十有八。投湘州果願寺沙門法緒而出家焉。緒授以十戒導以律儀。仍攝以北度詣慧曠律師。兼通方等故北面事焉。後詣大賢山。誦法華經無量義經普賢觀經。歷涉二旬三部究竟。進修方等懺心淨行勤勝相現前。見道場廣博妙飾莊嚴。而諸經像縱橫紛雜。身在高座足躡繩床。口誦法華手正經像。是後心神融淨爽利。常日逮受具足律藏。精通先世萠動而常樂禪悅怏怏。江東無足可問。

Ru-Yi Translation

Later he suffered the death of both parents and the bitter grief of mourning. When the mourning was over, he asked his elder brother for leave to go. His brother said, "Heaven has already taken our parents from me, and now you cut my heart again. Orphaned, and then to be parted — how could I bear it?" He knelt and replied, "Once the million [people] of Liang and Jing became servants and concubines in a single morning. At that time I was long in service; a heart [set on] rivers and lakes can no longer stay within this rugged [world]. I wish to repay kindness and requite virtue, and should make seeking the path my first concern; what use is gathering together in vain? It is engraved in my flesh and carved in my bones; my intent cannot be moved." At that time Wang Lin held Xiang [province], and he asked Lin for leave to go. Lin, an old acquaintance of the Marquis Chen [his father], and admiring his resolve, provided him with the requisites for the Dharma and deeply supported and rejoiced in it. At the age of eighteen he went to the śramaṇa Faxu of Guoyuan Monastery in Xiangzhou and left the household life. Faxu conferred the ten precepts on him and guided him in the rules of the Vinaya. He then took him north across [the river] to the Vinaya master Huikuang, who was also versed in the Vaipulya [sūtras], and so he served him as a disciple. Later he went to Mount Daxian and recited the Lotus Sūtra, the Sūtra of Immeasurable Meanings, and the Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra; within twenty days he had completed all three. He went on to cultivate the Vaipulya repentance. His mind was pure and his practice diligent, and superior signs appeared before him: he saw the place of practice broad and vast, wondrously adorned, yet the sūtras and images were scattered in disorder; he himself was on a high seat, his feet resting on a rope chair, reciting the Lotus with his mouth and setting the sūtras and images in order with his hands. After this his mind and spirit were fused, pure, clear, and keen. In the course of time he received full ordination and mastered the Vinaya-piṭaka. The [seeds of] former lives stirred, and he constantly delighted in the joy of meditation, but he was dissatisfied that in Jiangdong there was no one worth asking.

With Huisi at Mount Dasu

Chinese Source

時有慧思禪師。武津人也。名高嵩嶺行深伊洛。十年常誦七載方等九旬常坐一時圓證。希有能有事彰別傳。昔在周室預知佛法。當禍故背北游南。意期衡嶽以希棲遁權止光州大蘇山。先師遙飡風德如飢渴矣。其地乃是陳齊邊境兵刃所衝。而能輕於生重於法。忽夕死貴朝聞涉險而去。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time there was the meditation master Huisi, a man of Wujin. His fame was high above the Song peaks and his practice deep as the Yi and Luo rivers. For ten years he constantly recited [the Lotus], for seven years [practiced] the Vaipulya [repentance], and for ninety days sat constantly, and then realized it perfectly all at once — a rare and capable man, whose deeds are set out in a separate biography. Formerly, in the realm of the Zhou, he had foreseen [what would befall] the Buddhadharma; facing calamity, he turned his back on the north and traveled south, intending to dwell in retreat on Mount Heng, and stopped for a time at Mount Dasu in Guangzhou. Our late master, from afar, hungered and thirsted for his teaching and virtue. That place lay on the border between Chen and Qi, where weapons clashed; yet he could make light of life and weigh the Dharma heavily. Disregarding "dying in the evening" and valuing "hearing in the morning," he braved the dangers and went.

Ru-Yi Translation

When he first bowed his head at Huisi's feet, Huisi said, "Long ago on Vulture Peak we listened to the Lotus together. Drawn by those past conditions, you have come again now." He then showed him the Samantabhadra place of practice and explained to him the four peaceful practices. Thereupon, from dusk to dawn, with painstaking effort, he trained his mind as he had been taught. At that time he was bold in seeking the Dharma but poor in provisions: he cut cypress for incense, and when the cypress ran out he went on with chestnut; he rolled up the blind to let in the moonlight, and when the moon set he burned pine. He took no idle breath and spoke no idle word. After fourteen days, reciting as far as the "Medicine King" chapter — "The buddhas together praised him: This is true diligence; this is called a true offering of the Dharma" — on reaching this one line, his body and mind opened out, and in stillness he entered concentration. His dhāraṇī arose from stillness, and he illuminated the Lotus clearly, like a high radiance looking down into a dark valley; he penetrated the marks of all dharmas, like a long wind roaming through great space. When he reported his realization to his teacher, the teacher expounded further, spreading wide the net of the teaching with every mesh of the Dharma complete. From sunset he asked in detail, question linked to question, until dawn. What his own mind had awakened to and what he received from his teacher — four nights of progress were worth more than a hundred years. "Asking one and knowing ten" is no adequate comparison. His contemplative wisdom was unobstructed and the gates of meditation unblocked; his past habituations opened out, brilliant as flowers in bloom. Master Huisi said with admiration, "Had it not been you, none could have realized it; had it not been I, none could have recognized it. The concentration you entered is the preliminary expedient of the Lotus samādhi; the dhāraṇī you brought forth is the first, the revolving dhāraṇī. Even if masters of letters came in thousands of crowds and myriads of throngs, they could never exhaust your eloquence. Among those who teach the Dharma, you are the very first."

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time there was the meditation master Huimiao, whose conduct affected to be above the ordinary and whose eloquence confused the listeners of the day. He said of himself to his disciples, "What I expound and spread is the true lion's roar; what others say is the howl of a jackal. Who, with the mind's eye not yet open, would not be deceived?" Our late master properly cited the sūtra texts, with the cardinal doctrine alongside, struck at the key points, and examined and questioned him closely, and Miao lost his evidence. When the wind of wisdom winnows, the chaff can be recognized; when the water of concentration washes, sand and gravel are easily seen. Thereupon his deluded followers knew to turn back; asking for the ford, they recognized the crossing. That very night [the master] dreamed of a three-storied pavilion: Miao stood below, and he himself sat above. Another person, baring his arms and glaring, said, "Why do you slight Miao? Why do you doubt the Dharma? You should question me!" Our late master raised difficulties and set several barriers; host and guest went back and forth, until the angry man's words ran out and his reasoning failed, and, tongue-tied, he fell silent. [The master] then admonished him: "Apart from the true mark of all dharmas, everything else is the work of Māra." Having admonished him, he no longer saw Miao or the angry man. Someone who heard him in the night thought he was talking in his sleep. In the morning he went to Huisi and related these signs in full. The master said, "Look at the chapter on non-retrogression in the Prajñā: how many kinds of conduct, types, and appearances of the ninety-six paths [it lists]! The sūtra says that when a person teaches the Dharma, spirits help to frighten [opponents]. Since by day you broke the banner of pride and by night drove off the evil band, it is fitting that the deviant does not trouble the true Dharma."

Ru-Yi Translation

When Master Huisi had finished making the gold-lettered Larger [Prajñāpāramitā] Sūtra, he himself opened its profound meaning and then ordered [Zhiyi] to lecture in his place. So his wisdom was comparable to the sun and moon and his eloquence like a river hanging down; rolling up and unrolling, everything fit and had its reason. Only on the three samādhis and the wisdom of the three contemplations did he consult [Huisi] for examination; all the rest he decided himself. Master Huisi, holding a ruyi scepter, came to the seat and praised him: "This may be called the Dharma entrusted to a minister of the Dharma, so that the Dharma-king has nothing to do." The Vinaya master Huikuang also came and sat in the assembly. Huisi said to him, "This old monk has listened to the Dharma of your worthy son." He answered, "He was born of the meditation master; he is not Kuang's son." [Huisi] said further, "Huisi too has no merit in it; it is the power of the Lotus." When the lecturing in his place was finished, Master Huisi admonished him: "For a long time I have longed for Mount Heng in the south, regretting that I had no one to whom to entrust the Dharma. That you have roughly found its gate greatly suits my wish. My understanding does not yield to yours, but our conditions must part. Now I entrust it to you. You should hold the Dharma, suit it to conditions, transmit the lamp, and transform beings. Do not be the last one who cuts off the seed."

Teaching in Jinling

Chinese Source

既奉嚴訓不得扈從衡嶽。素聞金陵仁義淵藪試往觀之。若法弘其地則不孤付囑。仍共法喜等二十七人同至陳都。然上德不德又知音者。寡有一老僧。厥名法濟。即何凱之從叔也。自矜禪學倚臥。問言。有人入定聞攝山地動。知僧詮練無常此何禪也。答曰。邊定不深邪乘闇入。若取若說定壞無疑。濟驚起謝曰。老僧身嘗得此定。向靈耀則公說之則所不解說已永失。今聞所未聞。非直善知法相。亦乃懸見他心。濟以告凱。凱告朝野由是聲馳道俗請益成蹊。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having received this strict instruction, he could not follow [Huisi] to Mount Heng. He had long heard that Jinling was a deep pool of humanity and righteousness, and thought to go and see it: if the Dharma could be spread there, he would not fail the entrusting. So, together with Faxi and others, twenty-seven in all, he went to the Chen capital. But high virtue does not display itself as virtue, and those who truly understood him were few. There was an old monk named Faji, the paternal uncle of He Kai, who, priding himself on his learning in meditation, asked while leaning back on his couch, "Someone entered concentration, heard the earth shake at Mount She, and knew that the monk Quan was practicing [the contemplation of] impermanence. What kind of dhyāna is this?" He answered, "A concentration on the border, not deep; something deviant entering in the dark. If one grasps it or speaks of it, the concentration will surely be ruined." Faji rose in alarm and apologized: "This old monk himself once attained this concentration. I told Master Ze of Lingyao about it, but he did not understand it, and once I had spoken of it, it was lost forever. Today I hear what I had never heard. You not only know the features of dharmas well, but also see into others' minds from afar." Reading Notes for Part 1

Translator's notes

Names and titles: personal, place, and temple names are in pinyin without tones. "His present Majesty" (至尊, 皇上) and "the Great Prince" (大王) are Yang Guang, Prince of Jin, later Emperor Yang of Sui; the crown prince of the Chen whose letter is quoted signs himself Yuan (淵). Guangzhou in the Huisi passage is 光州 (in present Henan), not 廣州. Jianye and Jinling both mean the Chen capital (present Nanjing).

"This is true diligence; this is called a true offering of the Dharma": Lotus, "Medicine King" chapter; wording follows the site's Lotus Sūtra page.

"Apart from the true mark of all dharmas, everything else is the work of Māra": a saying traced to the Great Treatise; the same words appear in the Fozu tongji (T2035). Exact source not located.

Huisi's praise "the Dharma entrusted to a minister of the Dharma" and the exchange with Huikuang are also given in Zhanran's Zhiguan fuxing (T1912), with slightly different wording.

Text problems: CBETA's punctuation is often off; the translation follows the sense where it breaks a phrase, e.g. 「于時久役江。湖之心」 (read 「久役，江湖之心」), 「國十餘。齋身當四講」 (read 「國齋十餘，身當四講」), 「無貫者。萬人朝議」 (read 「無貫者萬人。朝議」). Probable copying errors: 草知我誰 for 莫知我誰; 何曰 for 何日; 臆覩 for 憶覩; 子時計詡 for 于時計詡; 不建 probably for 不逮. "More than fifty" listeners at Jiangling (五十餘人) may be an error for a larger number; translated as written.

"Futou and Xuangao" (浮頭玄高): Xuangao is the fifth-century meditation master; "Futou" is uncertain (possibly a corrupt form of another master's name).

"Guang and You" (光猷) in the Prince of Yongyang's vow-text: probably two earlier monks paired with Dao'an and Huiyuan (perhaps Faguang and Zhu Daoyou / Bai Daoyou); not identified with certainty.

"As Bhaiṣajyarāja attended on Thunder-Voice": in the Lotus, "King Wondrous Adornment" chapter, the two sons who visit the buddha Cloud-Thunder-Voice (雲雷音宿王華智) are said to have become the bodhisattvas Bhaiṣajyarāja and Bhaiṣajyasamudgata. Jñānākara is the son of Mahābhijñājñānābhibhū in the "Parable of the Phantom City" chapter.

"Shan meng . late, after" (善萠／晚後): a fanqie-style pun on the characters of a name, read as an omen of the Chen's fall; the exact name and reading are not explained in the text.

"A man lives by three, and serves them as one": Guoyu (Jin yu) — father, teacher, and ruler.

"The merchant departs and leaves gold; the physician goes and leaves medicine . the mad children": echoes the physician parable of the Lotus, "Lifespan" chapter (site: "to cure his mad children").

"Stepping by the shadow" (步影): measuring the sun's shadow to make sure the meal is taken before noon.

"The twenty-five people to whom a hundred [pieces of] gold may be entrusted": allusion not located.

Ten signs: in the fourth sign the dates run "eighteenth day of the tenth month" and then "twelfth day of that month"; translated as written.

Book titles: the Samādhi of Awakening Attention (覺意三昧), Six Wondrous Gates, Sequential Gates of the Dharma-Realm, and Gradual Gates of Dhyāna (次第禪門 = the Shi chan boluomi) follow earlier drafts of this round; Smaller Calming and Contemplation (小止觀) and Procedure for the Lotus Samādhi (法華三昧行法) are new. The Profound Meaning and Calming and Contemplation are named here in their early titles (法華玄義, 圓頓止觀).
