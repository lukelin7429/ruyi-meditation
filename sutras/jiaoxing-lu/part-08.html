Poems from the Capital, Records of Disciples, and the Stele, Stūpa Inscription, and True Record of Fazhi

The eighth part closes fascicle six and opens fascicle seven. Fascicle six ends with documents on Zhili's reputation and school: Chao Yuezhi's 1111 preface to the twenty-three poems sent to Zhili by monastic officials of the capital around 1020, when he had vowed to burn his body and Yang Yi was urging him to remain; the poems themselves, each with its author's titles; Shenzhao's address on the conferred robe; a lineage placing Zhili as the twenty-ninth patriarch; the story of the lost Humane Kings commentary from Japan; short accounts of Shenzhao's awakening, of Renyue's break with his teacher over the measure of the Buddha's body, of Miaowu's letter answering Renyue, of Zhili's lecturing, and of the categorized collections compiled from his lectures. Fascicle seven opens with its table of contents and the three basic biographical sources: Zhao Bian's stele of 1080, Hu Fang's stūpa inscription, and Zequan's True Record of 1033, which lists Zhili's lectures, writings, and repentance retreats. Part 1 quoted all three, and the English here follows Part 1 wherever the wording coincides. The part ends with Zunshi's preface to Pointing to the Essentials, printed with a line-by-line commentary of unknown authorship that explains its allusions.

Ru-Yi Translation

Edict Attendant Chao's Preface Recording the Poems Presented to Great Master Fazhi

Chinese Source

今天台教觀之徒，稱四明尊者，追配古人。其言為國中之法也，可謂盛矣。（說之）自北方來，聞而異之。究其名氏，是謂法智禮公。於是乎矍然加歎曰：此我高祖文元公與楊文公之所稱者歟。我雖昧夫天台之學，未讀法智所著之書，而固已得其人矣。既而法智三世孫明智立公，視以高僧（簡長）等紀，贈詩二十三首曰：吾祖法智，得名凜然至今者，蓋有所自矣。當是時，翰林主人晁公、楊公所貽之文既已流傳矣。其輩行中聲名人、篇章之美，又如何哉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now the followers of the Tiantai teaching and contemplation call [him] the Venerable of Siming and rank him with the ancients. Their word is the standard within the country; it may be called flourishing indeed. I (Yuezhi), coming from the north, heard this and found it remarkable. When I inquired into his name, he was called Lord [Zhi]li, Fazhi. Thereupon I started and sighed, saying: "Is this the one whom my great-great-grandfather Lord Wenyuan and Lord Wen Yang praised? Though I am ignorant of the learning of Tiantai and have not read the books Fazhi wrote, I have certainly already come to know the man." Afterward Fazhi's third-generation descendant, Lord [Zhong]li, Mingzhi, showed me the twenty-three poems presented [to Fazhi] recorded by the eminent monk (Jianchang) and others, saying: "That our patriarch Fazhi has a name still awe-inspiring today surely has its source. At that time the writings left by the masters of the Hanlin, Lord Chao and Lord Yang, had already circulated. And what of the renowned men among their contemporaries and the beauty of their compositions!

Chinese Source

然是詩，吾祖法智初不自有之，乃落於杭州式公。不知其歷幾歲，凡傳幾何人，而復歸於法智之舊室，亦可歎也。已將移于石，願得以序之。（說之）竊以謂法智遠處身於東海之陂，而名聲振燿京師，既久而彌隆。其頌德辭翰，雖散而復合，亦可以為修德之勸也。後有攬者，其所感可勝言哉。烏虖！景德祥符之風，此亦非其躅歟。政和元年十一月庚申十三日，朝請郎監明州船場飛騎尉賜緋魚袋晁（說之）序。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Yet these poems our patriarch Fazhi did not at first keep himself; they fell to Lord [Zun]shi of Hangzhou. No one knows how many years they passed through or through how many people they were handed down, and then they returned to Fazhi's old chamber; this too is worth sighing over. They are about to be transferred to stone, and I wish to obtain [a piece] to preface them." I (Yuezhi) humbly consider that Fazhi placed himself far off on the shore of the Eastern Sea, yet his fame shook and shone in the capital, and the longer [it lasted] the greater it grew. The writings praising his virtue, though scattered, have come together again; this too may serve as an encouragement to cultivate virtue. As for those who later take them up, can what they feel be put into words? Alas! The manner of the Jingde and Xiangfu eras — is this not also one of its traces? Prefaced by Chao (Yuezhi), Gentleman for Court Audiences, Supervisor of the Mingzhou Shipyard, Flying Cavalry Commandant, granted the scarlet [robe] and fish pouch, on the thirteenth day, gengshen, of the eleventh month of the first year of Zhenghe [1111].

Xu means to exhort and encourage, as in the Documents: "Exert yourselves, my officers!" "Going out or staying, speaking or silent" and so on are taken entirely from the Zhouyi, "Appended Statements," which explains that the way of the noble person is never forgotten in the four postures. Teaching and contemplation, the provisional and the real, are the pivot of entering the path in our school. Clothing covers the body; food fills hunger; a staff is leaned on by the hand; an armrest is leaned on by the body: these are metaphors for teaching and contemplation and the other dharmas being used morning and evening and being indispensable.

Ru-Yi Translation

Great indeed! As for one who wears the robe of tranquil forbearance and occupies the room of great compassion, leading people on step by step skillfully, people cannot find words to praise him.

This uses the three dharmas of the Lotus to praise his practice of benefiting others. The sūtra says: "enter the Tathāgata's room, put on the Tathāgata's robe, and sit in the Tathāgata's seat." Covering all is called the room; screening evil is called the robe; non-attachment is called the seat. Being complete in these three and spreading the holy transformation, he is a support for the world. The Analects says: "The Master skillfully leads people on step by step." The note says: "Xunxun is the appearance of order; you means to advance."

《釋籤》十不二門者，今昔講流以為一難文也。或多注釋，各陳異端，孰不自謂握靈蛇之珠、揮彌天之筆。豈思夫一家教觀，殊不知其啟發之所。公覽之再嘆，豈但釋文未允，柰何委亂大綱，山隤角崩，良用悲痛。

The ten gates of nonduality in the Annotations have been regarded by lecturers of past and present as one difficult text. Many have annotated and explained it, each setting out divergent views; who does not think himself holding the pearl of the numinous snake and wielding the heaven-filling brush? How could they consider that, as to the teaching and contemplation of our school, they quite fail to know where it opens and starts? The Lord read them and sighed again and again: not only were their explanations of the text inaccurate, but how [could they] throw the great framework into confusion, [so that] the mountain collapses and the horns break? This is truly grievous.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Many have annotated and explained it" refers to those after Jingxi, such as Fengxian's Pointing to the Pearl and Gushan's Proving the Meaning. Two stories are cited to compare the various masters each priding themselves on their excellence. Formerly the Marquis of Sui went on a journey and saw a herd boy hit and injure a snake's head. The marquis pitied it, applied medicine, treated it, and left. One night he happened to see a light shining in his courtyard; it was a snake holding a pearl in its mouth on the ground. It said: "I am originally a dragon's son, who changed form to play, was injured by the herd boy, and was saved by you; so now I bring a pearl in thanks." The marquis obtained the pearl and presented it to the King of Chu. The king placed it in his hall, and it gave off light like day. According to the Biographies of Monks of the Liang, Dharma Master Dao'an penetrated the scriptures in letter and principle, and his virtue and repute were exalted. At that time Xi Zaochi, whose sharp eloquence was renowned under heaven, went especially to visit him. When they were seated, he announced: "Xi Zaochi of the four seas." [Dao]an said: "Shi Dao'an of the filling heaven." People of the time praised his reply. The Record of Rites, "Tangong," says: "Is Mount Tai collapsing? Is the roof beam breaking?" The Documents, "Great Declaration," says: "The hundred clans are fearful, as if their horns were breaking." These two texts lament the rise and fall of persons; here they are applied to the decline of the Dharma.

Chinese Source

將欲正舉，捨我而誰？遂而正析斯文、旁援顯據，綽有餘刃兼整大途，教門權實、今時同昧者，於茲判矣，別理隨緣其類也。觀道所託，連代共迷者，於茲見矣，《指要》所以其立也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wishing to put it right, who but I? So he correctly analyzed this text and cited clear evidence alongside, with ample room for his blade, and also set the great road in order. The provisional and the real of the teaching gate, about which people of the present time are equally in the dark, are here judged — the distinct principle following conditions is of this kind. That on which the path of contemplation relies, about which successive generations have been confused, is here seen — this is why Pointing to the Essentials was established.

Chinese Source

《孟子》紀孔子曰：當今之世，舍我其誰。正析之言，如《楞嚴》曰：披剝萬象，析出精明。援，引也。綽，猶寬緩之貌。軻書曰：豈不綽綽然有餘裕哉。又《莊子》明。庖丁為文惠君解牛數千，而刀若新發硎，恢恢乎其於游刃必有餘地。別理隨緣者，藏通則教理俱權，圓則教理俱實，此三易曉；唯別教權理實蘊乎隨緣之義，意稍難明。四明能顯述之，故天竺稱可觀道。連代共迷者，以山外諸師建言玄句不通修，復云觀真不觀妄故。《指要》斥之曰：有人解今一念云是真性，恐未稱文旨等是也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Mencius records Confucius as saying: "In the present age, if not I, who?" As for "correctly analyzed," it is like the Śūraṅgama's "peeling away the myriad images and analyzing out the essential brightness." Yuan means to cite. Chuo is like the appearance of ample ease. [Meng] Ke's book says: "Is it not ample, with room to spare?" Also Zhuangzi explains: Cook Ding cut up several thousand oxen for Lord Wenhui, yet his knife was as if fresh from the whetstone; "spacious, there is surely room to spare for the play of the blade." As for "the distinct principle following conditions": in the Tripiṭaka and shared [teachings], teaching and principle are both provisional; in the perfect [teaching], teaching and principle are both real; these three are easy to understand. Only in the distinct teaching, where the teaching is provisional and the principle real, is the meaning of following conditions contained, and its sense is somewhat hard to understand. Siming was able to bring it out and expound it, so Tianzhu approved it. "The path of contemplation, about which successive generations have been confused": because the Off-Mountain masters asserted that the Profound [Meaning] and Textual Commentary do not include cultivation, and further said that one contemplates the true and not the deluded. Pointing to the Essentials rejects this, saying: "Someone explains the present single thought as the true nature; I fear this does not match the purport of the text," and so on.

Chinese Source

至若《法華》、《止觀》綱格之文，隱栝錯綜，略無不在。後之學者足以視近見遠，染指知味。《易》不云乎：通天下之志，定天下之業，斷天下之疑。實此一二萬言得矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the texts of the framework of the Lotus and Calming and Contemplation, straightened and interwoven, there is hardly anything not present. Later students can, by it, see the near and see the far, dip a finger and know the flavor. Does the Changes not say: "It penetrates the aims of all under heaven, settles the undertakings of all under heaven, and resolves the doubts of all under heaven"? Truly this is obtained in these ten or twenty thousand words.

Chinese Source

綱格者，《輔行》曰：綱謂綱紀，如網之外圍。格謂格正，如物之大體。彼以八教判釋如綱格，教隨機異如網目。此則不然，隨文用與也。隱栝，《荀子》二出，一曰：府然若渠堰隱栝之於已也。注曰：渠堰所以制水，隱栝所以制木。一曰：故枸木必將待隱栝，鈍金必將待礱厲。注曰：枸讀為鈎，曲也。隱栝，正曲木之具。《廣雅》錯，廁也。綜，總也。《說文》謂機縷持絲交者，即錯要其文，綜理其義也。視近見遠，《中庸》言：君子之道，知遠知近。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Framework" (gangge): the Fuxing says: "Gang means the guiding rope, like the outer edge of a net; ge means straightening, like the main body of a thing." There it takes the eight teachings in judgment and explanation as the framework, and the teachings varying with capacities as the meshes of the net. Here it is not so; the words are used according to the text. "Straightened" (yinkuo) appears twice in Xunzi. Once it says: "Restrained as if by dikes and straightening frames upon oneself." The note says: "Dikes are for controlling water; straightening frames are for controlling wood." Once it says: "Thus crooked wood must wait for the straightening frame, and blunt metal must wait for the whetstone." The note says: "枸 is read 鈎, crooked; the straightening frame is a tool for straightening crooked wood." The Guangya: "Cuo means to place among; zong means to gather." The Shuowen calls [zong] the heddle of the loom that holds the crossing threads: that is, interweaving its text and gathering and ordering its meanings. "See the near and see the far": the Mean says: "The way of the noble person knows the far and knows the near."

Chinese Source

染指者，《左傳》說：楚人献[元/?]於鄭靈公。子家將見子公之食指動，以示子家曰：他日我如此，必甞異味矣。及入宰夫將解[元/?]，相視而笑。公問之，子家以告。及食，召子公，弗與也。子公怒，染指於鼎，甞之而出。今以《指要》為鼎，若人研覈則為染指，由此得諳教觀等味也。易不下雖用彼言，不用彼意。彼意□天下之志未通，易能通之；天下之業未定，易能定之；天下之疑未斷，易能斷之。迴此三義歸乎《指要》，誠謂窮理盡性之書矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Dip a finger": the Zuozhuan relates: A man of Chu presented a turtle (written with an unencoded character) to Duke Ling of Zheng. Zijia was about to see [the duke]; Zigong's index finger moved, and he showed it to Zijia, saying: "Whenever mine does this, I am sure to taste something rare." When they went in, the cook was about to cut up the turtle (written with an unencoded character), and they looked at each other and laughed. The duke asked why, and Zijia told him. When the meal came, the duke summoned Zigong but did not give him any. Zigong was angry, dipped his finger in the cauldron, tasted it, and left. Here Pointing to the Essentials is taken as the cauldron; if one studies it thoroughly, that is dipping one's finger, and by this one comes to know the flavors of teaching and contemplation and so on. "The Changes" and what follows uses its words but not its meaning. Its meaning is: [where] the aims of all under heaven [a missing character] are not penetrated, the Changes can penetrate them; [where] the undertakings of all under heaven are not settled, the Changes can settle them; [where] the doubts of all under heaven are not resolved, the Changes can resolve them. Turning these three meanings to Pointing to the Essentials, it is truly to be called a book that exhausts principle and fully [realizes] the nature.

I, Shi, respectfully [note that I] was his fellow student. Readers, do not say that I too am partisan. Take what is excellent in its principle; do not take what is excellent in feeling. Where the text and principle are clear, who can conceal it? So I say.

懺主單著名諱，古今皆然。如僧祐《弘明．序》云：祐以末學。《涅槃疏》緣起，章安自敘云：頂滯於豫章。亦有獨書上字者，如君山《籤．序》云：普早歲在塵是也。二師同學，同氣連枝，真難弟難兄也。故《石塔記》云：通居寶雲，日敷教觀。逾二記（知禮、遵式）子衿之高者。黨，類也。《釋名》曰：五百家為黨。《論語》孔子曰：吾聞君子不黨，君子亦有黨乎？疏曰：相助匿非曰黨。孔子既答陳司敗而退，司敗復楫弟子巫馬期進而問曰：我聞君子不阿黨。今孔子言：昭公知禮。即君子亦有黨乎？取長其理者，《孝經．序》曰：在理或當，何必求人。大哉《指要》之作，文理既正，是誠不可匿者也。

That the Repentance Master writes only his personal name is the same in past and present. For example, Sengyou's preface to the Hongming [ji] says: "[Seng]you, as a latecomer to learning…"; and in the account of the origin of the Nirvāṇa Commentary, Zhang'an narrates of himself: "[Guan]ding was detained in Yuzhang." There are also those who write only the first character, as when Junshan's preface to the Annotations says: "Pu in my early years was in the dust." The two masters were fellow students, of the same breath and connected branches; truly it is hard to say which is the elder brother and which the younger. Thus the Stone Stūpa Record says: "[Yi]tong lived at Baoyun and daily spread the teaching and contemplation for more than two decades; (Zhili and Zunshi) were the highest of his students." Dang means a kind. The Shiming says: "Five hundred households make a dang." In the Analects, [said of] Confucius: "I have heard that the noble person is not partisan; is the noble person also partisan?" The commentary says: "Helping one another to conceal wrongs is called partisanship." After Confucius had answered Chen Sibai and withdrawn, Sibai bowed to the disciple Wuma Qi, [had him] come forward, and asked: "I have heard that the noble person is not partisan. Now Confucius says that Duke Zhao knew ritual. Is the noble person also partisan?" "Take what is excellent in its principle": the preface to the Classic of Filial Piety says: "If in principle it may be right, why seek [it from] others?" Great is the making of Pointing to the Essentials! Its text and principle being correct, it truly cannot be concealed.
