Five Kinds of Skill, and Abiding in the Dharma

The treatise now names five kinds of skill that together make a teaching quickly received, fully retained, and correctly understood, and closes this stretch of 法品 by asking what it actually means to abide in the Dharma — not hearing and reflection pursued alone, nor cultivation pursued alone, but the two held together without either one ever being set aside.

1. The Five Kinds of Skill

Chinese Source

於諸法中云何法善巧？謂多聞故。云何義善巧？謂於阿毘達磨、毘奈耶中善知其相故。云何文善巧？謂善知訓釋文詞故。云何詞善巧？謂能善知我我所等世俗言詞，不深執著隨順說故。云何前際後際密意善巧？謂能善知於前際領受、於後際出離故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With respect to the Dharma, what is skill in the teaching itself? It is having extensive learning. What is skill in meaning? It is thoroughly knowing the characteristics of things as set forth within the higher teaching and the discipline. What is skill in wording? It is thoroughly knowing how to explain and interpret words and phrases. What is skill in terms? It is thoroughly knowing conventional terms such as "I" and "mine," speaking in accordance with them without becoming deeply attached to them. What is skill in the hidden intention regarding past and future? It is thoroughly knowing how to receive the teaching in light of the past and how to find deliverance in light of the future.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 frames these five kinds of skill as answering a single practical question already posed elsewhere in the scriptures: what allows a monk to receive a teaching quickly, retain a great deal of it, retain it correctly, and never subsequently lose what has been retained? Skill in the teaching itself — simply having heard a great deal — is what allows quick reception, since one already broadly familiar with the wording picks up new passages with ease. Skill in meaning, thoroughly knowing how the higher teaching and the discipline actually characterize things, is what allows a great deal to be retained, since properly understanding the underlying categories lets many further passages be gathered in under them. Skill in wording and skill in terms together allow correct reception: knowing precisely how to explain difficult phrasing, and knowing how to use everyday terms like "I" and "mine" appropriately without being taken in by them, together ensure that what is heard is understood exactly as intended, free of distortion. And skill in the hidden intention regarding past and future is what prevents what has been retained from ever being lost, since a teaching properly received in light of what has come before naturally leads on to the deliverance still to come, one stage supporting the next without any of it becoming irrelevant along the way. Recognizing the Tathāgata's own hidden intention in this way, T1606 adds, is what allows a practitioner to grasp the teaching's genuine substance rather than its surface alone.

2. What It Means to Abide in the Dharma

Chinese Source

於諸法中云何住法？若不得修慧，唯勤方便修習聞思，不名住法。若不得聞思，唯勤方便修習修慧，亦不名住法。若俱得二種方便安住，乃名住法。若唯於法受持讀誦、為他演說、思惟其義，是名聞思。若修三摩地方便不知足，是名修慧。三摩地方便者，謂無間殷重方便及無倒方便。不知足者，謂不生味著修上奢摩他方便。

Ru-Yi Translation

With respect to the Dharma, what does it mean to abide in the Dharma? If one has not attained the wisdom born of cultivation, and diligently applies oneself only to cultivating the wisdom born of hearing and reflection, this is not called abiding in the Dharma. If one has not attained the wisdom born of hearing and reflection, and diligently applies oneself only to cultivating the wisdom born of cultivation, this too is not called abiding in the Dharma. Only when one has attained and abides securely in both approaches together is this called abiding in the Dharma.

Simply retaining, reciting, expounding to others, and reflecting on the meaning of the Dharma — this is called hearing and reflection. Cultivating the means of concentration without ever becoming satisfied — this is called the wisdom born of cultivation. The means of concentration are uninterrupted, earnest effort and effort free of distortion. Never becoming satisfied means never growing complacent or self-indulgent, and continuing to cultivate ever more advanced means of calm-abiding.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 grounds this definition in a sūtra where the World-Honored One himself specifically addresses "a monk who abides in the Dharma," and the point of citing it is precisely to rule out a common half-measure: retaining, reciting, and expounding the teaching — genuine and necessary as these are — constitute only hearing and reflection, and a practitioner who does this alone, without ever taking up sustained meditative cultivation, has not yet attained what abiding in the Dharma actually requires. Equally, a practitioner who cultivates concentration alone, however diligently, without ever having first properly heard and reflected on the teaching being cultivated, likewise falls short. It is specifically the combination — genuine learning feeding into genuine practice, and genuine practice never treated as separable from what has been learned — that the sūtra calls abiding in the Dharma, and it is this same combination, T1606 notes, that the Buddha elsewhere summarizes simply as "not abandoning yoga," never letting go of either the learning or the disciplined practice that learning is meant to support.
