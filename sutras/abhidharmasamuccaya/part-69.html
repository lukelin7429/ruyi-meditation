Exhaustion-Knowledge, and the Truth of the Path Complete

The truth of the path reaches its close: exhaustion-knowledge and non-arising-knowledge, the ten factors of one beyond training sorted into the five aggregates of discipline, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation, and the treatise's own four-aspect summary — completing not only the truth of the path but the entire treatise's account of all four noble truths.

1. Exhaustion-Knowledge and Non-Arising-Knowledge

Chinese Source

云何盡智？謂由因盡所得智，或緣盡為境。云何無生智？謂由果斷所得智，或緣果不生為境。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is exhaustion-knowledge? It is the knowledge obtained through the exhaustion of the cause, or knowledge that takes exhaustion itself as its object. What is non-arising-knowledge? It is the knowledge obtained through the cutting off of the fruit, or knowledge that takes the fruit's own non-arising as its object.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 distinguishes these two knowledges by which half of the causal chain each one looks toward. Exhaustion-knowledge looks backward, toward the cause: it is the knowledge that arises specifically because the origination of suffering — craving itself — has now been permanently exhausted, leaving nothing further behind; equally, it can simply take that very exhaustion as its own object. Non-arising-knowledge looks forward, toward the fruit: it is the knowledge that arises specifically because it is now known, with certainty, that every future fruit of suffering that cause would otherwise have produced will simply never arise at all; equally, it can take that guaranteed future non-arising as its own object. The two together complete a single realization from both ends at once — the cause is exhausted, and therefore the fruit that cause would have produced can never come to be.

2. The Ten Factors of One Beyond Training

Chinese Source

十無學法，當知依止無學戒蘊、定蘊、慧蘊、解脫蘊、解脫知見蘊說。何以故？無學正語、正業、正命，是無學戒蘊。無學正念、正定，是無學定蘊。無學正見、正思惟、正精進，是無學慧蘊。無學正解脫，是無學解脫蘊。無學正智，是無學解脫知見蘊。

Ru-Yi Translation

The ten factors of one beyond training should be understood as stated in dependence on the five aggregates of one beyond training: the aggregate of discipline, the aggregate of concentration, the aggregate of wisdom, the aggregate of liberation, and the aggregate of the knowledge and vision of liberation. Why is this so? Correct speech, correct action, and correct livelihood of one beyond training constitute the aggregate of discipline of one beyond training. Correct recollection and correct concentration of one beyond training constitute the aggregate of concentration of one beyond training. Correct view, correct thought, and correct effort of one beyond training constitute the aggregate of wisdom of one beyond training. Correct liberation of one beyond training constitutes the aggregate of liberation of one beyond training. Correct knowledge of one beyond training constitutes the aggregate of the knowledge and vision of liberation of one beyond training.

Reading note — after T1606

This passage sorts the ten factors first introduced back in Part 63's account of the eightfold path — now each one specifically qualified as belonging to one beyond training — into five distinct aggregates that map, unmistakably, onto the treatise's own threefold training in discipline, concentration, and wisdom, extended by two further aggregates unique to the arhat's completed state. The eightfold path's own speech, action, and livelihood become the aggregate of discipline; recollection and concentration become the aggregate of concentration; view, thought, and effort become the aggregate of wisdom. Beyond the eight, two further factors complete the ten: correct liberation, forming its own aggregate of liberation — the very fact of being freed — and correct knowledge, forming the aggregate of the knowledge and vision of liberation — the further, reflexive knowing that one has in fact been freed. It is specifically this last pairing, T1606 implies, that distinguishes liberation as achieved from liberation as merely present but unrecognized: the arhat possesses not only freedom itself but the settled certainty of having attained it.

3. The Four Aspects of the Truth of the Path

Chinese Source

如是道諦總有四種行相差別，謂道相、如相、行相、出相。云何道相？因此尋求真實義故。云何如相？以能對治諸煩惱故。云何行相？善能成辦心令不顛倒故。云何出相？趣真常迹故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The truth of the path, taken as a whole, has four distinct aspects: the aspect of path, the aspect of rightness, the aspect of accomplishment, and the aspect of deliverance.

Why is it called the aspect of path? Because it is by means of this that the true meaning is genuinely sought out. Why is it called the aspect of rightness? Because it is able to counteract every affliction. Why is it called the aspect of accomplishment? Because it is able to skillfully accomplish a mind free of distortion. Why is it called the aspect of deliverance? Because it leads to the true and constant track that is deliverance itself.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 glosses each term with a single supporting reason, and the pattern of the previous three truths' closing summaries — suffering, origination, and cessation, each already given four analogous aspects of its own — repeats here for the fourth and final time. It is called the aspect of path because every noble one relies on this very path as their own road to genuinely realizing the true meaning of things. It is called the aspect of rightness because every affliction is, by its very nature, contrary to reason, and it is this path alone that is able to remove it. It is called the aspect of accomplishment because a mind that has not yet awakened to true principle falls into distorted views — taking the impermanent as permanent, and so forth — and it is this path that skillfully corrects that very distortion, allowing the mind to know the true meaning free of any such reversal. And it is called the aspect of deliverance because this noble path alone is capable of leading all the way to that final, true, and constant track which is deliverance's own destination.

4. Mundane and Supramundane Aspects Compared

Chinese Source

於諸諦中十六行相皆通世間及出世間。世間出世間有何差別？於所知境不善悟入善悟入性差別故、有障無障性差別故、有分別無分別性差別故。所以者何？於諸諦中無常苦等十六世間行相，不善通達真如性故、煩惱所隨眠故、依名言門起戲論故。出世行相與此相違。出世行相現在前時，雖復現證見無常義，然不依名言戲論門見此是無常義。如無常行相於無常義，餘行相於餘義隨其所應當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Across the four truths, all sixteen aspects apply equally to both the mundane and the supramundane. What, then, is the difference between the mundane and the supramundane? It lies in the difference between not skillfully penetrating the object known and skillfully penetrating it; in the difference between being obstructed and being unobstructed; and in the difference between operating with conceptual discrimination and operating without it.

Why is this so? Among the four truths, the sixteen mundane aspects — impermanence, suffering, and the rest — fail to thoroughly penetrate the true nature of things, remain accompanied by the underlying tendencies of affliction, and give rise to conceptual proliferation by way of names and language. The supramundane aspects are the very opposite of this. When a supramundane aspect is actually present, although it does indeed directly realize and see the meaning of impermanence, it does not see this meaning of impermanence by way of names and conceptual proliferation. Just as it is with the aspect of impermanence toward the meaning of impermanence, so too, correspondingly, should it be understood with every other aspect toward its own corresponding meaning.

Reading note — after T1606

This closing passage answers a question that has quietly followed the treatise through its entire account of the four truths: the very same sixteen aspects — four for each truth — have been used throughout to describe both an ordinary mundane practitioner's understanding and a noble one's supramundane direct realization, so what actually distinguishes them, if the list of aspects itself never changes? T1606's answer is that the difference is never in which aspect is contemplated, but in how it is known. The mundane version of "impermanence," for instance, is still bound up with concepts and language — one thinks "this is impermanent," mediated by names, and that very mediation leaves the underlying afflictive tendencies undisturbed and understanding still imperfectly penetrating. The supramundane version sees the very same impermanence directly, with nothing standing between the mind and the fact itself — no naming, no conceptual proliferation, and consequently no obstruction left in its way. With this distinction drawn, the treatise's entire fourfold examination of the noble truths — suffering, origination, cessation, and now the path, each given its own detailed analysis and its own four-aspect summary — comes to its complete conclusion.
