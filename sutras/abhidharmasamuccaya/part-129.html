Withdrawing from Debate, and the Twelve Rare Conditions

The rules of debate close with two brief remaining parts — knowing when to withdraw rather than engage, and the three qualities debate most fundamentally requires — before the treatise turns, in its own voice, to a sobering exhortation: quoting the Buddha's own warning, in the Mahāyāna Abhidharma Sūtra, that bodhisattvas seeking swift awakening should generally avoid disputation altogether, given twelve conditions genuinely difficult to obtain even when debate seems warranted.

1. Withdrawing from Debate, and What Debate Requires

Chinese Source

第六論出離，謂觀察德失令論出離或復不作。若知敵論非正法器、時眾無德，自無善巧不應興論。若知敵論是正法器、時眾有德，自有善巧方可興論。第七論多所作法，略有三種：一善達自他宗，由此堪能遍興談論；二無畏，由此堪能處一切眾而興論端；三辯才，由此堪能於諸問難皆善辯答。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth, withdrawal from debate: examining the actual merits and defects involved, one either withdraws from a debate or declines to enter it at all. If it is known that the opponent is not a genuine vessel for the Dharma, that the assembled audience lacks good qualities, and that one's own skill is insufficient, debate should not be undertaken. If it is known that the opponent is a genuine vessel for the Dharma, that the assembled audience possesses good qualities, and that one's own skill is sufficient, debate may then be undertaken.

The seventh, what debate most fundamentally requires, is, in brief, of three kinds: thorough mastery of both one's own and the opposing tradition, by which one is capable of engaging in discourse on any matter whatsoever; fearlessness, by which one is capable of initiating discourse in the midst of any assembly; and eloquence, by which one is capable of skillfully answering every challenge raised.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 clarifies what makes an opponent "not a genuine vessel" for debate: one who cannot actually be led out of an unwholesome position and into a wholesome one. An audience "lacking good qualities" is one that is not honest and fair-minded, given instead to entrenched bias and favoritism. And being without sufficient skill means not yet having genuinely mastered the six parts of debate already examined — its essential nature, venue, basis, adornment, the ways it can be lost, and now this very assessment of whether to engage at all. Where any of these three conditions is absent, withdrawal is the wiser course; where all three are genuinely present, engaging becomes appropriate.

2. Why a Bodhisattva Should Generally Avoid Disputation

Chinese Source

復次若欲自求利益安樂，於諸論軌應善通達，不應與他而興諍論。如薄伽梵於《大乘阿毘達磨經》中說如是言：若諸菩薩欲勤精進修諸善品、欲行真實法隨法行、欲善攝益一切有情、欲得速證阿耨多羅三藐三菩提者，當正觀察十二處法，不應與他共興諍論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further: one who genuinely seeks their own benefit and happiness should thoroughly master these very rules of debate, and yet should not, on that account, actually engage others in disputation. As the World-Honored One states in the Mahāyāna Abhidharma Sūtra: bodhisattvas who wish to diligently cultivate every wholesome quality, to practice genuinely in accordance with the Dharma, to skillfully benefit every sentient being, and to swiftly realize unsurpassed and perfect awakening should correctly observe twelve conditions, and should not engage others in disputation.

Reading note — after T1606

The twelve conditions the sūtra names, each described as genuinely rare and difficult to obtain, are these: that a listener capable of genuine faith and understanding is rare, when the profound meaning of unsurpassed truth is actually being taught; that a questioner who approaches with a genuinely receptive, teachable mind is rare; that an audience wise enough to fairly weigh merit and fault is rare; that discourse free of the six specific faults — clinging to a wrong position, evasive confusion, mistimed speech, retreating under pressure, coarse language, and an angry mind — is rare; that engaging in debate without any trace of hostility is rare; that safeguarding another's own state of mind while debating is rare; that safeguarding one's own settled concentration while debating is rare; that debating without the wish to see oneself win and the other lose is rare; that finding oneself outmatched without becoming afflicted by it is rare; that finding one's own mind already afflicted and still managing to settle it again is rare; that, once unsettled, continuing to cultivate wholesome qualities as before is rare; and that, amid all this instability, still being able to swiftly attain concentration if not yet attained, and swiftly attain liberation if concentration is already attained, is rarest of all.

T1606 adds that this same passage explains why "genuine practice in accordance with the Dharma" is named separately from mere diligence: to make clear that such practice must spring from a genuinely pure motivation, not from any desire for gain or recognition, and that skillfully benefiting sentient beings is what distinguishes the bodhisattva's own path from the śrāvaka's, since benefiting others is held as the more excellent conduct. T1606 further explains the underlying reason disputation proves so hazardous: the Mahāyāna's own nature is unsurpassed and profound, difficult enough to genuinely understand even with a mind free of contention — and engaging in dispute typically means raising questions not out of a genuine wish to understand, but simply to find fault, while an opponent participating in bad faith is rarely honest or fair-minded to begin with, given instead to entrenched bias. Given how easily debate compounds all six faults just named, and how rarely the calm, undisturbed mind genuine progress actually requires can be sustained in the midst of it, a bodhisattva genuinely seeking swift awakening is, on this sober accounting, generally better served avoiding disputation altogether — mastering these rules thoroughly enough to use them only when genuinely necessary, rather than as an invitation to use them often.
