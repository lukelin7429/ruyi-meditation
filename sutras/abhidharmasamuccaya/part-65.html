Calm-Abiding and Insight, and the Four Types of Path

The treatise now defines its two foundational meditative capacities in full: calm-abiding, unfolding through nine progressive stages of settling the mind, and insight, unfolding through four progressive modes of discernment — before sorting every practitioner into one of four types of path, according to whichever of the two capacities has, or has not yet, been attained.

1. Calm-Abiding: The Nine Stages of Settling the Mind

Chinese Source

奢摩他者，謂於內攝心令住、等住、安住、近住，調順寂靜、最極寂靜，專注一趣平等攝持。

Ru-Yi Translation

Calm-abiding is: gathering the mind inward so that it is made to abide, to abide evenly, to abide securely, to abide close by, to be tamed, to be stilled, to be most utterly stilled, to be focused single-pointedly, and to be evenly sustained.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 walks through all nine stages named in this single compressed sentence, and together they trace a single continuous arc from a mind still scattered outward to one that no longer even needs deliberate effort to stay gathered. To make the mind abide is simply the very first act of drawing attention in from external objects and fixing it, however roughly, on one. To make it abide evenly is to keep that initial, still-coarse fixation going continuously, in progressively finer and briefer intervals, rather than losing it after a single moment. To make it abide securely is to notice whenever attention has drifted outward and promptly draw it back in. To make it abide close by is to stay near the object through sustained recollection from the very outset, so that outward drift becomes rarer to begin with. To be tamed is to recognize the specific dangers of distraction — attractive forms and the like — clearly enough that the mind is actively subdued and no longer drawn to stray toward them. To be stilled is to see just as clearly the dangers of the agitated, distracted, unwholesome thoughts and secondary afflictions that arise from within, and to master the mind against these as well. To be most utterly stilled is to catch even the sudden, momentary flickers of distraction or unwholesome thought that still occasionally arise despite all this, and suppress them the instant they appear. To be focused single-pointedly is to sustain diligent, uninterrupted effort until the mind rests continuously in an excellent concentration without any further gap. And to be evenly sustained is the final stage, where — through this thorough prior cultivation — the concentrated mind simply continues on its own, no longer needing any deliberate effort at all to keep it free of distraction.

2. Insight: The Four Modes of Discernment

Chinese Source

毘鉢舍那者，謂簡擇諸法、最極簡擇、普遍尋思、周審觀察，為欲對治麁重相結故、為欲制伏諸顛倒故、令無倒心善安住故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Insight is: discerning the dharmas, discerning them most utterly, investigating them comprehensively, and examining them thoroughly and carefully — undertaken in order to counteract the coarse aspect of afflictive bondage, in order to master every distorted view, and so that an undistorted mind may become well established.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 pairs each of these four terms with a specific scope of knowing. Discerning the dharmas means comprehending the full extent of what there is to know — surveying phenomena exhaustively rather than partially. Discerning them most utterly means comprehending them exactly as they actually are, in their genuine nature, not merely in their surface extent. Investigating them comprehensively means bringing a discriminating, actively reasoning wisdom to bear in actually establishing the specific characteristics of each dharma under examination. And examining them thoroughly and carefully means pursuing that same investigation in full, exhaustive detail, leaving nothing about the object still unexamined. Taken together, these four modes of discernment are what actually counteracts the coarse, still-unrefined residue of affliction, masters every form of distorted view, and finally allows an undistorted mind to settle securely — insight's own distinctive contribution, working always in partnership with the settled stability that calm-abiding alone supplies.

3. The Four Types of Path

Chinese Source

又依奢摩他、毘鉢舍那立四種道。或有一類已得奢摩他非毘鉢舍那，此類依奢摩他進修毘鉢舍那。或有一類已得毘鉢舍那非奢摩他，此類依毘鉢舍那進修奢摩他。或有一類不得奢摩他亦非毘鉢舍那，此類專心制伏惛沈掉舉雙修二道。或有一類已得奢摩他及毘鉢舍那，此類奢摩他毘鉢舍那二道和合平等雙轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, on the basis of calm-abiding and insight, four types of path are established. There is a type who has already attained calm-abiding but not insight; this type relies on calm-abiding to progress in cultivating insight. There is a type who has already attained insight but not calm-abiding; this type relies on insight to progress in cultivating calm-abiding. There is a type who has attained neither calm-abiding nor insight; this type concentrates the mind on mastering torpor and restlessness, cultivating both paths together at once. And there is a type who has already attained both calm-abiding and insight; this type has the two paths of calm-abiding and insight operate jointly, evenly, and in unison.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 spells out what practice actually looks like under each of these four conditions. The first type, having already attained calm-abiding, continues sitting in stillness through the nine stages already established, but — not yet having insight — must sit again specifically to discern the dharmas through the four modes just described, building insight on top of an already-settled mind. The second type does the reverse: having already attained insight, this practitioner sits to discern the dharmas first, and takes that very activity of discernment as the basis from which calm-abiding is then cultivated. The third type has neither capacity yet, and so advances both together at once: listening to and retaining the teaching first, then cultivating correct insight on that basis, and taking that insight itself as the lead-in to cultivating calm-abiding as well — the two developing side by side rather than one first. And the fourth type, having already attained both, simply lets calm-abiding and insight operate together, mutually reinforcing, from the very outset. What unifies all four routes, T1606 notes, is that none bypasses either capacity — every practitioner must eventually possess both calm-abiding and insight in full; the four types differ only in which one comes first and how the second is then built upon it.
