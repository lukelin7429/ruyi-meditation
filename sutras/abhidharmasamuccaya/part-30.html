Even Form Is Momentary

Momentariness is easy enough to accept for mind, which visibly flickers from thought to thought — but the treatise now takes on a harder case: proving that physical form, seemingly so solid and continuous, is equally momentary, perishing and being replaced anew in every single instant. The same close analysis then dissolves the atom itself into a purely conceptual limit, with no genuine substance of its own to be found anywhere.

1. Six Proofs That Form Perishes Moment by Moment

Chinese Source

前說無常皆剎那相，此云何知？如心心所是剎那相，當知色等亦剎那相，由心執受故、等心安危故、隨心轉變故、是心所依故、心增上生故、心自在轉故。又於最後位變壞可得故、生已不待緣自然滅壞故，當觀色等亦念念滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Impermanence, as already stated, is entirely a matter of momentariness — how is this known? Just as mind and its mental factors are momentary, form too should be understood as momentary, for six reasons: because it is appropriated by mind; because it shares mind's own safety and danger; because it alters in step with mind; because it serves as mind's own support; because it is produced through mind's own dominant power; and because it is freely transformed by an accomplished mind's own mastery. Further, since alteration and decay are found even in form's very last stage, and since, once arisen, form perishes on its own without waiting for any further destroying condition to arrive, form too should be observed to perish moment by moment.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 unpacks each of the six proofs in turn. Being "appropriated by mind" means the body is held together, moment by moment, by a momentary mind's own continuous act of appropriation — since that appropriating mind is itself momentary, what it appropriates must perish and be renewed at the same rate. "Sharing mind's own safety and danger" points to the plain fact that a body decays the instant consciousness withdraws from it, showing body and mind rise and fall together, moment for moment. "Altering in step with mind" is simply the visible fact that the body changes as the mind passes through pain, pleasure, craving, and hatred. "Serving as mind's own support" reasons that whatever a momentary thing depends on must itself be equally momentary, the way fire depends on momentarily-consumed fuel. "Being produced through mind's own dominant power" reasons from cause to effect: since the very causes and conditions that produce form are themselves momentary, form itself, as their product, cannot be anything but momentary too. And "freely transformed by an accomplished mind's mastery" points to an advanced meditator's demonstrated capacity to reshape form at will, moment by moment, through the power of resolve alone — a capacity that would make no sense if form were not already, at every moment, freshly arising and freshly ceasing, available to be reshaped.

T1606 also flags the final, more general argument as decisive on its own: whatever a thing's ultimate fate at its very last moment turns out to be — dissolution, decay — that same fate, on close examination, is already present at every earlier moment too, simply too subtle to notice; and since nothing that has genuinely arisen needs to wait for some further destructive cause to eventually make it perish (its own arising already guarantees its eventual ceasing), the only consistent conclusion is that form, like mind, is dissolving and being renewed continuously, not merely at the single dramatic moment anyone happens to notice its final breakdown.

2. The Four Great Elements, and What "Constructed" Actually Means

Chinese Source

如世尊說：諸所有色，彼一切若四大種、若四大種所造。此依何意說？依容有意說同在一處，依此而有，是造義。若於此聚此大種可得，當知此聚唯有此大種非餘。或有聚唯一大種、或有二大種、或有乃至一切大種，所造色亦爾。若於此聚此所造色可得，當知此聚唯此非餘。或有聚唯一所造色、或二所造色、或有乃至多所造色，隨其所應。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the World-Honored One said: all form whatsoever is either the four great elements themselves, or what the four great elements construct. What is this statement's own intent? It speaks with reference to what may possibly co-occur in a single location — being present on this basis is what "constructed" means. If, within a given cluster, only a specific great element is found, that cluster is to be understood as containing only that element and no other. Some clusters contain only one great element; some contain two; some contain up to all four — and the same holds for constructed form. If, within a given cluster, only a specific constructed form is found, that cluster contains only that form and no other. Some clusters contain only one constructed form; some contain two; some contain up to several, as is fitting in each case.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 illustrates this with concrete examples: a dried lump of clay contains only the earth element; that same clay, once wetted, contains earth and water together; heated, it contains earth, water, and fire together — the specific mixture of elements actually present in a given cluster of matter varies case by case, not fixed by any rule that every physical object must always contain all four elements equally. The same applies to constructed form: light alone is a case of a single constructed form; sound together with fragrant, wind-borne smoke is a case of two; and so on, following the same fitting logic in every case.

3. The Atom: A Conceptual Limit, Not a Genuine Substance

Chinese Source

又說麁聚色極微集所成者，當知此中極微無體，但由覺慧漸漸分析細分損減，乃至可析邊際，即約此際建立極微。為遣一合想故，又為悟入諸所有色非真實故。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is further said that coarse, massed form is made up of a gathering of atoms — but it should be understood that the atom itself has no genuine substance at all. Rather, through discerning wisdom progressively analyzing form into ever-finer divisions, reducing it further and further, an eventual analytical limit is reached, and it is precisely with reference to this limit that "the atom" is established. This is done for two reasons: to dispel the notion of things as solid, unified wholes, and to bring one to realize that form of every kind is not genuinely, substantially real.

Reading note — after T1606

The treatise's own answer to "why posit atoms at all, if they have no genuine substance?" identifies two distinct pedagogical purposes at once. First, ordinary perception habitually experiences a table, a body, or a mountain as a single, solid, unified thing — "one combined whole" — and progressively analyzing that whole down toward its ever-finer, ultimately merely conceptual limit is a direct method for dismantling that habitual sense of solidity, which in turn helps loosen the deeper habit of imagining a unified "self" taking up rebirth from moment to moment. Second, and more radically, once analysis has been carried all the way down to a limit that turns out to have no genuine substance of its own at all, the practitioner is brought to realize that form in general — not merely this or that particular composite object, but physical form as such — was never genuinely, substantially real to begin with, a realization the treatise explicitly identifies as the entry point into the doctrine of consciousness-only. The atom, on this account, is less a claim about physics than a meditative instrument: a rung on a ladder that is itself dissolved once the climb is complete.

Part 30 Complete

The treatise has now shown that even physical form, not only mind, is momentary through and through, and that the atom itself — the seemingly solid endpoint of physical analysis — turns out to be a purely conceptual limit rather than a genuine substance.
