The Four Principles of Reasoning

Having catalogued the objects the Dharma may be contemplated through, the treatise turns to the question of method: by what principles is diligent, careful investigation of the Dharma actually to proceed? Four principles of reasoning are given — dependence, function, proof, and inherent nature — together forming the treatise's own compact theory of correct reasoning.

The Four Principles

Chinese Source

若欲於法勤審觀察，由幾道理能正觀察？由四道理，謂觀待道理、作用道理、證成道理、法爾道理。云何觀待道理？謂諸行生時要待眾緣。云何作用道理？謂異相諸法各別作用。云何證成道理？謂為證成所應成義，宣說諸量不相違語。云何法爾道理？謂無始時來於自相共相所住法中，所有成就法性法爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one wishes to diligently and carefully investigate the Dharma, by how many principles of reasoning is correct investigation actually carried out? By four principles of reasoning: the principle of dependence, the principle of function, the principle of proof, and the principle of inherent nature.

What is the principle of dependence? It is this: when conditioned phenomena arise, they necessarily depend on a multitude of conditions. What is the principle of function? It is this: dharmas of differing character each perform their own distinct function. What is the principle of proof? It is this: in order to establish whatever meaning is to be proven, one sets forth statements, drawn from the valid means of knowledge, that are free of contradiction. What is the principle of inherent nature? It is this: since beginningless time, within the dharmas that abide in their own specific and shared characteristics, there is a nature that is simply established as it is, of its own accord.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 grounds each of these four principles in a concrete example, and together they cover every basic form correct reasoning about the Dharma can take. The principle of dependence is illustrated by ordinary causal necessity itself: a sprout arises only in dependence on a seed together with season, water, and soil; a moment of consciousness arises only in dependence on a sense faculty, an object, and mental attention — nothing conditioned simply appears from nowhere. The principle of function is illustrated by the specific, non-interchangeable roles distinct things actually play: the eye faculty serves specifically as the basis for eye-consciousness, form serves specifically as its object, and eye-consciousness itself serves specifically to discern form — just as a goldsmith and a silversmith each skillfully work their own distinct material into finished ornaments, each dharma performs a function proper to itself alone, not to any other. The principle of proof is illustrated by the actual practice of establishing a claim through the valid means of knowledge — direct perception, inference, and reliable testimony — proposing a thesis and defending it only by statements that these do not contradict. And the principle of inherent nature is illustrated by facts so basic they require no further proof at all: fire simply burns, water simply flows and moistens — as a sūtra says, the eye, though perfectly formed and pure, is empty, is not permanent, and so on up through being without self, simply because that is its nature, as it has always already been. Together these four principles distinguish what actually needs an argument (dependence, function, proof) from what is instead the very bedrock on which every such argument ultimately rests (inherent nature).
