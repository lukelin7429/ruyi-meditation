Mutual Inclusion, and What the Three Terms Mean

Three categories have now each been built in full — but they are, in the end, three ways of cutting up the very same reality. This part shows exactly how much overlap that implies (only three items, it turns out, subsume everything), tests the boundary with a set of tetralemma questions on eye versus the eye element, explains why the aggregates and elements come in their given order, and closes with the treatise's own account of what "aggregate," "element," and "sense field" actually mean — ending in the five classical similes for the five aggregates.

1. Three Items That Subsume Everything

Chinese Source

由此道理，諸蘊界處三法所攝，謂色蘊、法界、意處。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this reasoning, the aggregates, elements, and sense fields are, together, subsumed under just three items: the aggregate of form, the dharma element, and the mental-faculty sense field.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 states the logic plainly: the aggregate of form already subsumes all ten form-elements; the dharma element already subsumes itself (feeling, formations, and the eight unconditioned dharmas); and the mental-faculty sense field already subsumes all seven consciousness-elements. Nothing in the entire eighty-item spread of aggregates, elements, and sense fields falls outside these three — which is exactly why "three categories" (三法) is the name this treatise's very first chapter gives itself.

2. Eye Versus the Eye Element: A Tetralemma

Having shown how much the three categories overlap, the treatise now tests the boundary between two terms that might seem interchangeable — "eye" (a sense field) and "the eye element" — through the classical four-cornered analysis: is there anything that is one but not the other, both, or neither?

Chinese Source

如說眼及眼界，若有眼亦眼界耶？設有眼界亦眼耶？或有眼非眼界，謂阿羅漢最後眼。或有眼界非眼，謂處卵㲉、羯邏藍時、頞部曇時、閉尸時，在母腹中若不得眼，設得已失；若生無色異生所有眼因。或有眼亦眼界，謂所餘位。或有無眼無眼界，謂已入無餘依涅槃界，及諸聖者生無色界。如眼與眼界，如是耳鼻舌身與耳等界，隨其所應盡當知。

Ru-Yi Translation

As is said of eye and the eye element: whatever has eye, does it also have the eye element? And whatever has the eye element, does it also have eye? There is a case of eye without the eye element: an arhat's final eye, present in the very last moment before entering final nirvāṇa without remainder. There is a case of the eye element without eye: one still within an egg, or in the kalala, arbuda, or peśī stage within the mother's womb, not yet having attained an eye, or having attained one and then lost it; or an ordinary being born into the formless realm, who retains only the seed-cause for an eye, never the organ itself. There is a case of both eye and the eye element: every other occasion. And there is a case of neither: one who has already entered the realm of nirvāṇa without remainder, and every noble one born into the formless realm. As with eye and the eye element, so too, applied as is fitting, for ear, nose, tongue, and body with their corresponding elements.

若有意亦意界耶？設有意界亦意耶？或有意非意界，謂阿羅漢最後意。或有意界非意，謂處滅定者所有意因。或有意亦意界，謂所餘位。或有無意無意界，謂已入無餘依涅槃界。

Whatever has manas, does it also have the mental-faculty element? And whatever has the mental-faculty element, does it also have manas? There is a case of manas without the mental-faculty element: an arhat's final manas. There is a case of the mental-faculty element without manas: one abiding in the attainment of cessation, who retains only the seed-cause for manas. There is a case of both: every other occasion. And there is a case of neither: one who has already entered the realm of nirvāṇa without remainder.

Reading note — after T1606

The four embryonic stages named here — kalala (a translucent, curdled droplet), arbuda (a soft, swelling mass), and peśī (a firm, oblong mass, the "closed" stage the treatise here calls 閉尸) — are the same first three of the classical five embryonic stages used elsewhere on this site's Abhidharmakośa pages, kept as untranslated Sanskrit terms throughout this treatise as well. The tetralemma's point is a precise one: "eye" (as a sense field) and "the eye element" almost always coincide, but they are conceptually distinct enough that four genuinely different cases can be constructed — an arhat's very last moment of physical eye-function, present as an ordinary sense field but no longer generating any further seed for a future eye (hence no longer counted as "element" in the sense of an ongoing causal capacity); an unborn being who has the underlying seed-capacity (element) without yet having, or having already lost, the functioning organ itself; the ordinary case of both together; and the case of neither, once physical existence itself has ended for good, or one is born beyond form entirely. The manas/mental-faculty-element pair mirrors this exactly, with the attainment of cessation replacing the embryonic stages as manas's own temporary absence.

3. Why the Aggregates and Elements Come in Their Given Order

Chinese Source

何故諸蘊如是次第？由識住故，謂四識住及識。又前為後依故，如其色相而領受故，如所領受而了知故，如所了知而思作故，如所思作隨彼彼處而了別故。又由染污清淨故，謂若於是處而起染淨，若由領受取相造作故染污清淨，若所染污及所清淨，由此理故說蘊次第。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why do the aggregates come in this particular order? Because of the four grounds where consciousness abides, plus consciousness itself. Further, because each earlier aggregate serves as the support for the one after it: one registers a feeling in accordance with a form's own character; one construes an image in accordance with what has been registered; one is driven into action in accordance with what has been construed; and one discriminates, in whatever place is relevant, in accordance with what has been driven into action. Further, because of defilement and purity: wherever defilement or purity actually arises, whether through registering, construing an image, or acting, and whatever it is that becomes defiled or purified — it is for this reason that the aggregates are spoken of in this sequence.

何故諸界如是次第？由隨世事差別轉故。云何世事差別而轉？謂諸世間最初相見，既相見已更相問訊，既問訊已即受沐浴塗香花鬘，次受種種上妙飲食，次受種種臥具侍女，然後意界處處分別，以內界次第故建立外界，隨此次第建立識界。如界次第，處亦如是。

Why do the elements come in this particular order? Because they follow the sequence in which worldly affairs actually unfold. What is this sequence? When people in the world first meet one another, having met, they exchange greetings; having exchanged greetings, they receive bathing, scented ointments, and garlands; next they receive various fine foods; next they receive various bedding and attendants; and only afterward does the mental faculty turn to discriminate each of these in turn. Because the internal faculties follow this sequence, the external objects are established in the same order, and, following that same order again, the consciousnesses are established in turn. As with the order of the elements, so too for the sense fields.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 unpacks the four grounds where consciousness abides (identified elsewhere in the broader Yogācāra corpus as form, feeling, perception, and formations — the four non-consciousness aggregates, each a "ground" that consciousness settles into and takes root in) as the deeper structural reason the aggregates are counted in exactly this order, before restating the same point through ordinary causal sequence: seeing form causes a feeling, a felt experience gets construed into a recognizable image, a construed image drives one into some action, and action results in further discriminating consciousness of the outcome — each aggregate genuinely depending on the one before it, not merely listed in arbitrary sequence. The elements' order, by contrast, is explained through nothing more exotic than ordinary social custom: a guest is welcomed with sight, then speech, then touch (bathing, ointment), then smell (garlands), then taste (food), then further touch (bedding) — with the mental faculty's own reflective sorting-through of the whole visit coming last, only once every physical sense has already had its turn.

4. What "Aggregate," "Element," and "Sense Field" Mean

Chinese Source

蘊義云何？諸所有色，若過去若未來若現在、若內若外、若麁若細、若劣若勝、若遠若近，彼一切略說一色蘊，積聚義故，如財貨蘊。如是乃至識蘊。又苦相廣大故名為蘊，如大材蘊。如契經言：如是純大苦蘊集故。又荷雜染擔故名為蘊，如肩荷擔。

Ru-Yi Translation

What does "aggregate" mean? Whatever form there is — past, future, or present; internal or external; coarse or fine; inferior or superior; distant or near — all of it, taken together, is called, in brief, a single aggregate of form, in the sense of a heap gathered together, just as a heap of wealth is called an aggregate. So too for the remaining aggregates, up through the aggregate of consciousness. It is further called an aggregate because its own mark of suffering is so vast, like a great heap of timber — as the sūtra says: thus a purely great heap of suffering is amassed. And it is further called an aggregate because it bears the burden of defilement, the way a shoulder bears a burden.

界義云何？一切法種子義；又能持自相義；又能持因果性義；又攝持一切法差別義。處義云何？識生長門義是處義。

What does "element" mean? The sense of being the seed for every dharma; the sense of holding its own particular characteristic; the sense of holding the nature of cause and effect; and the sense of gathering and holding every dharma's distinguishing features.

What does "sense field" mean? The sense of being a gateway through which consciousness is generated and grows — this is what "sense field" means.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 draws out one further layer behind the "heap" etymology: the sūtra's own list of eleven kinds of craving (concern for one's own body, hope, attachment, love of an inner self, love of an object, desire, love of concentration, painful craving for what is bad, joyful craving for what is good, distant craving, and near craving) is what actually grounds the treatise's finer distinctions — past, future, present, internal, external, and the rest — since it is precisely with reference to what each of these eleven kinds of craving can seize on that form (and each further aggregate in turn) gets sorted into a "past," "internal," "coarse," or "distant" instance to begin with. "Element," T1606 notes, unpacks its four senses onto the eighteen elements quite literally: the six faculty-elements and six object-elements hold the causal nature of what will produce a consciousness; the six consciousness-elements hold the resultant nature of what that causal pairing actually produces — cause and effect, quite exactly, distributed across the eighteen.

5. Five Similes for the Five Aggregates

三法品 — the treatise's opening chapter on the three categories — closes with the single most quoted passage in the whole Abhidharma tradition on the aggregates' own nature: the Buddha's own five similes, one for each aggregate.

Chinese Source

如佛所說：色如聚沫，受如浮泡，想如陽焰，行如芭蕉，識如幻化。以何義故色如聚沫乃至識如幻化？以無我故、離淨故、少味故、不堅故、不實故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the Buddha said: form is like a mass of foam, feeling is like a bubble, perception is like a mirage, formations are like a banana tree, consciousness is like an illusion. For what reason is form like a mass of foam, and so on down to consciousness being like an illusion? Because of the absence of self, because of the absence of purity, because of the scarcity of savor, because of the lack of solidity, and because of the lack of genuine substance.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 makes clear that the five similes and the five reasons are meant to be read together, matched one to one — each aggregate's simile chosen precisely because it best displays one of the five failures of a genuine self: foam looks solid from a distance but collapses the instant it is grasped, mirroring form's utter lack of any real self within it; a bubble is inherently unstable and short-lived, mirroring feeling's lack of any lasting purity; a mirage promises water that was never actually there, mirroring perception's thin, insubstantial "savor" — the sense that what it construes is genuinely nourishing or reliable; a banana tree, once peeled layer by layer, reveals no solid core or heartwood at its center at all, mirroring formations' lack of any solid, driving agent behind them; and an illusion appears vivid and convincing while having no genuine substance whatever behind its appearance, mirroring consciousness's own lack of any real, substantial knower standing behind the act of knowing. Taken together, the five similes are this chapter's own closing argument in miniature: everything painstakingly built across the last ten parts — every aggregate, element, and sense field, catalogued down to the last mental factor — amounts, in the end, to exactly this: form gathered like foam, feeling as brief as a bubble, perception as thin as a mirage, formations as hollow as a banana stalk, and consciousness as insubstantial as a conjured illusion.

Part 10 Complete

The three categories' basic construction is now complete: their count, their marks, their full internal detail, how they mutually include one another, why each comes in its given order, what the three terms themselves mean, and the five closing similes. This is not yet the end of 三法品, though — the chapter's second half now opens a much longer inquiry the treatise calls its "hundred-gate analysis": the very same aggregates, elements, and sense fields examined again from dozens of further angles — real versus nominal existence, worldly versus ultimate truth, contaminated versus uncontaminated, and on through the twelve links of dependent origination that eventually close the chapter.
