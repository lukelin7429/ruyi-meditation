Why the Vast Teachings Are Called the Bodhisattva's Basket

The treatise turns to the vast teachings' own special status: why, among all twelve genres, this one alone is called the bodhisattva's basket of transcendent practice; why it is called vast and profound; and why some beings meet it with fear while others, though drawn to it, grasp its words too literally to ever find actual deliverance through it.

1. Why the Vast Teachings Alone Are the Bodhisattva's Basket of Transcendent Practice

Chinese Source

何因緣故唯方廣一分名為菩薩波羅蜜多藏？由此分中廣說一切波羅蜜多，數故、相故、次第故、釋詞故、修故、差別故、攝故、所治故、功德故、更互決擇故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it that only the vast teachings, among the twelve genres, are called the bodhisattva's basket of transcendent practice? Because it is specifically within this genre that every transcendent practice is set forth at length, through its number, its characteristic, its sequence, its etymology, its cultivation, its distinctions, what it comprehends, what it counteracts, its qualities, and its mutual determination against the others.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 devotes an extended discussion to unpacking each of these ten headings in turn for all six transcendent practices — generosity, discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom — worked out in genuinely exhaustive detail. Only the first of the ten is worth carrying forward briefly here, since it settles a question this treatise's own compact language leaves open: why exactly six, no more and no fewer? T1606's answer is that the six sort cleanly into two groups matching the two dimensions of the bodhisattva path. Generosity, discipline, and patience are the means to an excellent rebirth — generosity yielding great wealth, discipline yielding a body born into honor, and patience yielding a wide circle of devoted followers. Diligence, concentration, and wisdom are the means to decisive spiritual progress itself — diligence sustaining the effort to cultivate wholesome qualities, concentration actually subduing the afflictions, and wisdom permanently uprooting their underlying tendencies. Together, the six are exactly what is needed for a bodhisattva to remain in saṃsāra without being bound by it and to approach nirvāṇa without settling into it — the very definition, elsewhere in this treatise's Yogācāra corpus, of the non-abiding nirvāṇa unique to the Mahāyāna path.

2. Vast and Profound

Chinese Source

何緣方廣分名廣大甚深？由一切種智性廣大甚深故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is the genre of the vast teachings called vast and profound? Because the very nature of omniscience itself is vast and profound.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 explains this brief line as a case of the fruit lending its own name to the cause: the vast teachings are what actually leads to omniscience, and omniscience — the most vast and profound fruit there is — passes its own quality of vastness and profundity back onto the very teaching that produces it. It is precisely because what these teachings ultimately yield is this most far-reaching of all attainments that the teachings themselves are singled out, among the twelve genres, as vast and profound in their own right.

3. Fear, and Literal Clinging Without Deliverance

Chinese Source

何因緣故一分眾生於方廣分廣大甚深不生勝解反懷怖畏？由遠離法性故、未種善根故、惡友所攝故。何因緣故一分眾生於方廣分廣大甚深雖生勝解而不出離？由深安住自見取故、常堅執著如言義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is it that some beings, faced with the vastness and profundity of these teachings, fail to arrive at genuine conviction and instead grow fearful? It is because they are removed from the very nature the bodhisattva lineage requires, because they have not yet planted the wholesome roots this path calls for, and because they have fallen in with harmful companions.

Why is it that some beings, though they do arrive at genuine conviction concerning the vastness and profundity of these teachings, nevertheless fail to find actual deliverance through them? It is because they remain deeply settled in grasping to their own views, and because they persistently and rigidly cling to the words themselves as though the words alone were the entire meaning.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 unpacks each cause of fear in turn: lacking the bodhisattva lineage's own underlying nature, one's mind simply remains too limited to hold genuine conviction in the face of teachings this vast, and fear is the natural result; even a being who does possess that lineage may not yet have planted the specific wholesome roots — a genuine vow toward supreme awakening among them — needed to actually appreciate what is being taught; and even one who has planted such roots may still fall in with companions who disparage the Mahāyāna, and fear grows from that harmful influence in turn. As for those who believe but still cling literally: T1606 offers the treatise's own example — hearing that all dharmas are said to be utterly without own-nature, such a person concludes outright that the nature and characteristics of every dharma simply do not exist at all, mistaking a scripture whose meaning is not fully explicit for one whose meaning is. It is precisely this literal misreading, T1606 notes, that the Buddha addresses in the Great Dharma Mirror Sūtra, where he warns that bodhisattvas who grasp at words rather than reasoning correctly about the Dharma give rise to twenty-eight distinct wrong views.
