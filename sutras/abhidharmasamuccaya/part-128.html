The Adornment of Debate, and Defeat in Debate

Two further parts of the rules of debate: the six qualities that adorn genuinely excellent debate — mastery of both one's own and the opposing position, flawless delivery, fearlessness, eloquence, composure, and graciousness — and the three specific ways a debate is actually lost, including nine distinct verbal faults every debater should learn to recognize and avoid.

1. The Adornment of Debate: Six Qualities

Chinese Source

第四論莊嚴，謂依論正理而發論端，深為善美，名論莊嚴。此復六種：一善自他宗、二言音圓滿、三無畏、四辯才、五敦肅、六應供。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fourth, the adornment of debate: initiating discourse in accordance with correct reasoning, so that it becomes deeply excellent and admirable — this is called the adornment of debate. It is, further, of six kinds: mastery of one's own and the opposing tradition, flawless delivery, fearlessness, eloquence, composure, and graciousness.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 explains each in turn. Mastery of one's own and the opposing tradition means being thoroughly and fluently versed, without any gap, in both the wording and the meaning of one's own position and the position one is debating against. Flawless delivery means correctly understanding the principles of language before ever initiating discourse, so that one's speech is free of error and never becomes muddled. Fearlessness means that even when surrounded, in the midst of a great assembly, by countless skilled opponents holding entrenched, mistaken views, one's own speech remains calm and undaunted. Eloquence means that one's words flow without hesitation or obstruction. Composure means never speaking hastily or rashly, but waiting until an opponent has genuinely finished speaking before responding. And graciousness means possessing a naturally virtuous and harmonious disposition, speaking with warmth and skillfully accommodating the opponent's own state of mind.

2. Defeat in Debate: Three Ways, and Nine Verbal Faults

Chinese Source

第五論負，謂捨言、言屈、言過。捨言者，謂自發言稱己論失、稱他論德。言屈者，謂假託餘事方便而退，或說外事而捨本宗、或現忿怒憍慢覆藏等，如經廣說。言過者，略有九種：一雜亂、二麁獷、三不辯了、四無限量、五非義相應、六不應時、七不決定、八不顯了、九不相續。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fifth, defeat in debate: conceding defeat outright, being forced into retreat, and verbal faults. Conceding defeat outright means openly declaring one's own argument mistaken and praising the opponent's argument as correct. Being forced into retreat means withdrawing under the pretext of some unrelated matter, abandoning one's own original position by shifting to some tangential subject, or displaying anger, arrogance, or evasive concealment, exactly as set forth at length in scripture.

Verbal faults are, in brief, of nine kinds: incoherence, harshness, unintelligibility, excess, irrelevance, mistiming, inconsistency, obscurity, and discontinuity.

Reading note — after T1606

T1606 explains each of the nine verbal faults in turn. Incoherence means abandoning the actual matter under discussion and rambling at length about something else entirely. Harshness means speaking with sudden, agitated, hostile urgency. Unintelligibility means that the assembly and one's opponent alike cannot actually follow the meaning being expressed. Excess means piling up repetitive phrasing until the intended meaning becomes either inflated or diminished beyond recognition. Irrelevance covers five further sub-faults — speech with no real content, speech that contradicts the very point it claims to support, speech that undermines one's own reasoning, speech that merely restates what was already meant to be proven rather than proving it, and speech that invites further objections rather than settling them. Mistiming means presenting one's points out of their proper order. Inconsistency means asserting a position and then abandoning it, then reasserting it again, shifting back and forth so rapidly that no stable position can actually be discerned. Obscurity means departing from the accepted terms of the discussion and answering without genuinely comprehending it, mixing formal and colloquial language together in a confused muddle. And discontinuity means simply breaking off mid-speech, leaving the argument incomplete.

Together, these three categories of defeat and their nine underlying faults give a debater a precise vocabulary for recognizing exactly how an argument goes wrong — whether through open concession, evasive retreat, or one of nine specific failures of the speech itself.
