Suffering, Emptiness, and Selflessness

Three more of the truth of suffering's four shared marks: why the scripture's own claim "whatever is impermanent is suffering" holds — and exactly where it does not — followed by emptiness, examined through the treatise's own threefold scheme matched to the three natures, and selflessness, precisely bounded against a rival philosophy's own account of what a self would have to be.

1. Why "Impermanent" Entails "Suffering" — And Where It Does Not

Chinese Source

何等苦相？謂或三苦、或八苦、或六苦，廣說如前，是名苦相。何故經說若無常者即是苦耶？由三分無常為緣，苦相可了知故。謂生分無常為緣，苦苦性可了知故；滅分無常為緣，壞苦性可了知故；俱分無常為緣，行苦性可了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the mark of suffering? The three, eight, or six kinds of suffering already given at length above. Why does the sūtra say that whatever is impermanent is suffering? Because the mark of suffering is made known through three divisions of impermanence in turn: through the impermanence of arising, the suffering of suffering is made known; through the impermanence of ceasing, the suffering of change is made known; and through the impermanence common to both, the suffering of conditioned existence itself is made known.

即依此義，薄伽梵說：諸行無常、諸行變壞。又依此義言，諸所有受我說皆苦。又於生滅二法所隨諸行中，有生等八苦，性可了知故，佛說言：若無常者即是苦。又於無常諸行中有生等苦可了知者，如來依此密意說言：由無常故苦，非一切行。

It is on this basis that the Bhagavat said: all formations are impermanent, all formations pass away. And it is on this same basis that it is said: whatever feeling there is, I declare it all to be suffering. And it is because the eight kinds of suffering — birth and the rest — can be made known specifically within formations subject to arising and ceasing that the Buddha said: whatever is impermanent is suffering. And it is specifically with reference to formations in which suffering like birth can actually be discerned that the Tathāgata, with this hidden intent, said: suffering exists because of impermanence — not that every single impermanent thing is thereby suffering.

Reading note — after T1606

The closing qualification is a careful piece of textual interpretation worth sitting with, since the scriptural formula "whatever is impermanent is suffering" seems, on its face, universal and unqualified. T1606 explains why the treatise resists reading it that literally: were the formula taken as an absolute universal, then the noble path itself — which is impermanent, since it too arises and ceases — would have to count as suffering, an absurd result no one actually holds. The Tathāgata's real, hidden intent, on this reading, is narrower and more precise: the formula applies specifically to formations in which the eight recognizable kinds of suffering can actually be discerned, not to every impermanent thing without exception. This is a useful general lesson in how this tradition reads apparently sweeping scriptural claims — checking a formula against what it would absurdly entail if taken with maximal literalness, and recovering the narrower, coherent claim the Buddha actually intended.

2. Emptiness, and Its Three Kinds

Chinese Source

何等空相？謂若於是處此非有，由此理正觀為空。若於是處餘是有，由此理如實知有，是名善入空性。如實知者，不顛倒義。於何處誰非有？於蘊界處，常恒凝住不變壞法我我所等非有。由此理，彼皆是空。於何處誰餘有？即此處無我性。此我無性無我有性，是謂空性。故薄伽梵密意說言：有如實知有，無如實知無。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the mark of emptiness? Wherever something is absent, correctly seeing it as absent, by this same principle, is what emptiness means. Wherever something else remains present, genuinely knowing that presence, by this same principle, is called skillfully entering emptiness. "Genuinely knowing" means being free of distortion. Where, and what, is absent? Within the aggregates, elements, and sense fields, a permanent, fixed, unchanging self and what-belongs-to-a-self are absent. By this principle, they are empty. Where, and what, remains present? Precisely the selfless nature present in that same place. This absence of self, and this presence of a selfless nature, together are what emptiness means. It is for this reason that the Bhagavat, with hidden intent, said: what is present, one genuinely knows as present; what is absent, one genuinely knows as absent.

復有三種空性，謂自性空性、如性空性、真性空性。初依遍計所執自性觀，第二依依他起自性觀，第三依圓成實自性觀。

There are, further, three kinds of emptiness: emptiness of intrinsic nature, emptiness as suchness, and emptiness as true reality. The first is contemplated with reference to the discriminated, attached-to nature; the second with reference to the dependent nature; and the third with reference to the perfectly accomplished nature.

Reading note — after T1606

Emptiness's own definition here is worth reading slowly, since it deliberately refuses the crude reading of emptiness as bare, total non-existence: something is genuinely absent (a fixed self), but something else genuinely remains present (a selfless nature) — and it is this precise, balanced formula, holding both halves together, that the Bhagavat is said to intend by the paired scriptural phrase "what is present, know as present; what is absent, know as absent." T1606's three-kind scheme then maps this same emptiness directly onto the three-natures framework already used across this site's Cheng Weishi Lun and Mahāyānasaṃgraha pages: emptiness of intrinsic nature is seen with respect to the discriminated-and-attached-to nature, since that nature's own claimed self-existence is simply never there at all; emptiness as suchness is seen with respect to the dependent nature, since dependently arisen things, though genuinely occurring, are empty of any fixed, independent essence of their own; and emptiness as true reality is seen with respect to the perfectly accomplished nature — suchness itself, which is not merely an absence but the genuinely, ultimately real nature that emptiness, rightly understood, actually is.

3. Selflessness, Precisely Bounded

Chinese Source

何等無我相？謂如我論者所立我相，蘊界處非此相，由蘊界處我相無故，名無我相。故薄伽梵密意說言：一切法皆無我。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the mark of selflessness? Whatever mark of self a rival philosophy of self posits, the aggregates, elements, and sense fields simply do not bear that mark — since the aggregates, elements, and sense fields lack the very mark of self such philosophies posit, this absence is called the mark of selflessness. It is for this reason that the Bhagavat, with hidden intent, said: all dharmas are without self.

如世尊說：此一切非我所，此非我處此非我我。於如是義，應以正慧如實觀察。此言何義？謂於外事密意說此一切非我所，於內事密意說此非我處此非我我。所以者何？以於外事唯計我所相，是故但遣我所；於內事通計我我所相，是故雙遣我我所。

As the World-Honored One said: this is not what belongs to me, this is not where I am, this is not my self. This meaning should be genuinely contemplated with correct wisdom. What does this saying mean? With hidden intent, it says of external things that they are simply not what belongs to me; and with hidden intent, it says of internal things both that this is not where I am and this is not my self. Why the difference? Because, with respect to external things, one only ever superimposes the mark of "what belongs to me" — hence only that is negated. But with respect to internal things, one superimposes both the mark of "self" and of "what belongs to self" together — hence both are negated at once.

Reading note — after T1606

The treatise's own definition of selflessness is deliberately relational rather than free-standing: it does not attempt to prove, from first principles, that no self of any conceivable kind could ever exist; instead, it takes whatever mark of self a rival philosophical school actually posits — permanence, unchanging unity, independent agency, and the like — and simply points out that the aggregates, elements, and sense fields, examined honestly, never actually bear that specific mark anywhere within them. This is a notably modest and precise argumentative strategy: rather than a sweeping metaphysical proof, it is closer to a standing challenge, answerable to whatever specific account of selfhood is actually on the table.

The closing distinction between external and internal things resolves a puzzle in the scriptural formula's own asymmetric wording: why does the Buddha negate only "what belongs to me" for external objects, but negate both "where I am" and "my self" for internal ones? T1606's answer is that ordinary self-grasping itself is asymmetric — no one seriously imagines that an external tree or field simply is their own self, only that it belongs to them in some sense; but with respect to one's own body and mind, both claims are superimposed at once — that these things constitute the self, and that the self, in turn, resides within and possesses them. The scriptural formula's own precise wording, read this way, is not stylistic variation but exact diagnostic precision, negating exactly the specific error actually being made in each case.
