Rejoicing, the Dharma Teacher's Six Faculties, and Never Disparaging

This part is the middle section of Zhanran's tenth fascicle. It comments on four chapters of the Lotus: Merits of Rejoicing, Merits of the Dharma Teacher, Never Disparaging, and the opening of the Tathāgata's Spiritual Power. On the site these are all in fascicle-010 of the Textual Commentary. For the Merits of Rejoicing chapter, Zhanran reads the title through the four siddhāntas, with "following" and "joy" taken in terms of both the trace and the origin. He then defends the Commentary's count of fifty persons who pass the teaching on and rejects the older readings in terms of superior, ordinary, and inferior capacities. He also matches the fifty merits of the one who urges another to listen with the sūtra's own list. For the Dharma teacher's six faculties he sets aside Guangzhai's arithmetic and other masters' theories of eight hundred and twelve hundred merits, and reads the faculties as semblance forms of the five eyes and of their mutual use. The longest section is on Never Disparaging. Zhanran answers Jiaxiang's seven contrasts with six of his own, sets the bodhisattva's practice against the peaceful practices point by point, and asks why a monk may bow to laypeople. He also uses Vasubandhu's Treatise against the Yogācāra teaching that some beings lack buddha-nature. The part ends with the ten spiritual powers as signs of the four onenesses now and in the future.

Ru-Yi Translation

In explaining the chapter title, the fourfold conclusion of the name is also the intent of the four siddhāntas, though not in order; in principle one must digest them so that the meanings correspond. The chapters below, however, are all the dissemination of the two gates of origin and trace. Therefore here, although it digests both intents within the therapeutic, the meaning also extends to the other three siddhāntas. Since the text of the Merits of Rejoicing chapter measures the first of the five grades after the extinction, and its meaning corresponds to the head of the four faiths in the present, and both raise their rank through hearing of the long lifespan, it must set forth both gates, the present and the former. The Merits of the Dharma Teacher chapter below properly corresponds to the stage of the four faiths in the present and to the stages after rejoicing; therefore it does not again keep both explanations, since they have been fully explained in the Distinguishing [Merits] and the [Merits of] Rejoicing. Again, it praises the function of the five kinds of Dharma teacher, who can enter the [purity of the] six faculties, the same as the stage of the four faiths in the present. From Never Disparaging on, since it speaks of the Lotus in general, how could it be only in the trace?

Ru-Yi Translation

In the first, the mundane, the text first briefly explains the name, next explains it broadly, and third concludes generally. Because the first runs through the later, it first explains the name. In the first text, first "following" (隨), next "joy" (喜). In the first, the explanation of following, fact and principle are simply different names for the provisional and the real; penetrating this provisional and real, it is neither provisional nor real, hence "without any duality or distinction." That is, it is the fact and principle of following and according with the opening of the provisional to reveal the real. Next, in the broad explanation of joy, it says "for oneself and for others" (己人): this is still the power of fact and principle within the trace. Because principle has fact, one can rejoice for others; because fact has principle, one can rejoice for oneself. Again, being nondual yet dual, one rejoices for self and other; being dual yet nondual, one penetrates that there is neither self nor other. Next, from "Hearing this profound and abstruse Dharma" on, in the second explanation, it too first explains following as containing the merit of the provisional and the real. Next, from "rejoicing for oneself, there is wisdom" on, it explains joy again as containing the merit of compassion and wisdom. Though it says rejoicing for oneself, it is properly for the benefit of others; though it says rejoicing for others, it is properly to reveal oneself; hence it says there is wisdom and there is compassion. Because one hears the sūtra oneself and can also teach others, only when compassion and wisdom are complete is it called joy. Moreover, at the beginning of hearing the sūtra, practice and vow are simultaneous; hence a single phrase or a single verse benefits both self and others. Here this beginning mind sets up only one's own practice, and it rejoices for others by the power of vows. From "the provisional and the real" on, it concludes the two preceding intents and together establishes the chapter name. The provisional and the real conclude following; wisdom and severance conclude joy. Compassion is converting others; converting others belongs to liberation, and liberation belongs to severance. And because it rejoices in the fact and principle of self and other, it belongs to the mundane.

Ru-Yi Translation

From "Furthermore, 'according with principle'" on, the therapeutic, in terms of the origin gate: it too first explains and next concludes the chapter [name]. In the first, the explanation, first following, next joy. Within following, first the explanation proper; next, from "that is, through the breadth of phenomena" on, it concludes by forming the aspect of contemplation, interfusing fact and principle; third, it concludes. The first text is first principle, next fact. First, principle: if one believes in the long duration, belief necessarily relies on principle. The principle is no different from the wondrous principle within the trace, but it points to the long-ago origin, attributing the merit to real realization. The principle is deep and the time distant; hence it says "profound and far-reaching." As for saying "believing and according with it": hearing of the long duration in principle, how would one dare harbor secret doubts? Hence there is not a hair of doubt about the long-ago principle. Next, "according with phenomena" is simply the aspect of the Tathāgata's benefiting beings since his original attainment. Within the trace there is only a crosswise discussion of the forms of conversion; within the origin one must add the lengthwise aspect of the long-distant. Hence the long duration of conversion is lengthwise and the breadth of conversion is crosswise, because in between, stage after stage, it pervades the ten directions. "Spreading" (該亘) simply means pervading crosswise and lengthwise. The aspect of contemplation: without principle there is no means to convert; without conversion there is no means to reveal principle. That is, setting out the near fact of the trace one sees the distant principle of the origin. This too means that origin and trace, though different, are inconceivably one, and though one, origin and trace remain distinct; hence it says "not two, and yet two."

Ru-Yi Translation

As for saying "superior," "inferior," and "ordinary": the intent is that each later one gradually surpassing [the earlier] is "superior," each later one similar is "ordinary," each later one gradually weaker is "inferior." Thus by the gradually inferior it shows by comparison the ordinary and the superior: within the inferior, the merit of the very last, the fiftieth person, is still great; how much more the ordinary and the superior down to the fiftieth? Though the text does not refute this, in principle it is not wholly so. One need only rely on the gradually inferior to show the first by comparison with the later; what use are the ordinary and the superior? The ordinary means that first and later are similar; the superior means that the later surpasses the earlier; neither is within the scope of measurement. As for the present correct explanation: because the ancients' explanation, which does not take them as fifty stages, harms the text and loses the principle, I now assist it, temporarily relying on the gates of the teachings to establish the number of persons. It counts only six assemblies and does not list the śikṣamāṇā; this too matches the number on a first approximation and excludes them for the moment. How could there be śikṣamāṇās who do not hear the sūtra? Nor does the intent need to follow the gate of existence, taking great bhikṣus as the persons of the first assembly. Here, although it accumulates persons, gates, and practices to reach forty-eight, the intent makes clear that in each teaching every person, following one gate and one practice, can hear from the persons of the Dharma assembly. The reason for the two explanations: the first, in terms of the three teachings, corresponds in meaning to the former teachings having fifty persons who now hear the perfect; the second is that now again they form fifty, meaning that those hearing the sūtra are all opened and revealed and wholly become the four persons. Hence in the perfect there is nothing by which to distinguish them in number; relying for the moment on the method of counting, it stops at one seven [times seven], as in "seven generations" and the like, taking the great among the sevens; hence forty-nine. Reading Notes for Part 29

Translator's notes

Fascicle and site pages. All the lemmas in this part are from the site's fascicle-010 of the Textual Commentary: the Merits of Rejoicing, Merits of a Teacher of the Dharma, Never Disparaging, and the first part of the Tathāgata's Spiritual Power. The site's chapter titles there are "The Merit of Rejoicing," "The Merits of a Teacher of the Dharma," "Never Disparaging," and "The Tathāgata's Spiritual Power." In Zhanran's own sentences the chapter names follow the Lotus list used elsewhere in this translation.

Following and joy. Zhanran explains 隨喜 as two words, 隨 "following" and 喜 "joy," and maps the Commentary's four explanations of the title onto the four siddhāntas (units 3–15). The good and sturdy tree that grows a hundred spans underground, and the kalaviṅka that sings in the egg, are images for the beginning mind; he refers to his notes on Calming and Contemplation for both.

Fifty persons. In unit 20 Zhanran rejects the southern reading of the fifty persons as superior, ordinary, and inferior, and supports the Commentary's two counts: six kinds of disciple in four gates and two practices (forty-eight, plus first and last), or the "great seven" (seven times seven, plus one). The four extra kinds of birth (by touch, smell, sand, and sound) are from Paramārtha's Treatise on Manifesting Consciousness (T1618). The ranking of the Brahmā blessing follows the Great Treatise (T1509).

The fifty merits. Units 21–33 pair the merits listed in the Lotus for one who urges another to go and hear the sūtra (on the site's Lotus page: "born in the very same place as bodhisattvas who have attained dhāraṇīs, sharp in faculties and wisdom .") into lines of two items each; the English follows the site's Lotus wording where it can.

Rejoicing in the Prajñā sūtras. The line in unit 20 is close to the Large Prajñāpāramitā (T223), where the Buddha says that if such a person arouses the mind for awakening, he too rejoices.

Six faculties. Units 47–52 argue that the six faculties of the Dharma teacher are equally pure and that the numbers 800 and 1,200 only show that adornment can "expand" or "contract." The "three sūtras and one treatise" are the Large Prajñāpāramitā, the Zhengfahua (Dharmarakṣa's translation of the Lotus), the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, and Vasubandhu's Treatise on the Lotus. In unit 47, 根耳鼻三 is read as 眼耳鼻三 "eye, ear, and nose."

The six and four wheels. The Necklace Sūtra (T1485) lists the six wheels used to name the stages; the Commentary uses four, with the iron wheel as the ten faiths.

Jiaxiang. Jiaxiang is Jizang (549–623). Zhanran answers each of his seven contrasts between the Never Disparaging chapter and the preceding chapters (unit 54) and offers six of his own.

Buddha-nature and Vasubandhu. In units 54–55 Zhanran sets the Treatise on the Lotus, which says Never Disparaging "knows that beings possess buddha-nature," against the Yogācāra teaching of the Vijñaptimātratā that some beings lack the seed. He argues that each treatise of Vasubandhu sets forth its own sūtra. Kāśyapa's lament that the śrāvakas are like "scorched seed" is in the Vimalakīrti (on the site's Vimalakīrti page: "become, with respect to the Great Vehicle, no better than scorched seed").

Lotus quotations. The lines on Never Disparaging's death and later lives follow the site's Lotus page ("When this monk was about to die ."; "After his death, he encountered two thousand koṭis of buddhas, every one named Candrasūryapradīpa"; "all named Meghasvararāja"). Never Disparaging's message of twenty-four characters is the formula "I deeply revere you; I would never dare disparage or treat you with contempt . you shall attain buddhahood." The site's Lotus has "great arrogance" for 增上慢; here "overweening pride" is used.

Peaceful practices and Never Disparaging. Unit 55 lists ten points on which the beginner of the Peaceful Practices chapter differs from Never Disparaging; the image of the jewel in the topknot and the dream of the stages are from the Peaceful Practices chapter.

Bowing to laypeople. In unit 58 Zhanran answers the objection that monks should not bow to laypeople. Superior Intent (勝意) is the monk of the Sūtra on the Non-Activity of All Dharmas (T650) who reviled another monk, fell into hell, and later benefited. The Nirvāṇa and Vimalakīrti cases cited by others are, he says, not parallels.

Ten spiritual powers. Units 66–67 read the ten powers as signs of the four onenesses, present (powers two to five) and future (powers seven to ten). "表將" is glossed by Zhanran as "signify what is to be."

Doubtful readings. In unit 34, 第五十八 is read as 第五十人 "the fiftieth person." In unit 65, 此字 is probably 此中, matching the Commentary's "Here." In unit 20, 答不從所兼不共不別 is translated literally.
