The Six Signs of This World and the Questions of Maitreya

This part is the upper third of the third fascicle of Zhanran's Notes on the Textual Commentary on the Lotus. It covers the last pages of the site's Textual Commentary fascicle two and the opening of fascicle three, all still within the Introduction chapter of the Lotus. Zhanran goes through Zhiyi's treatment of the six signs of this world in order: the Sūtra of Immeasurable Meanings as an alternate name of the Lotus, the Buddha's entering concentration after preaching, the four flowers, the six kinds of quaking, the assembly's joy, and the light from the white tuft. He then turns to the six signs seen in other worlds, Maitreya's doubt, and the verses in which Maitreya poses his question. Throughout he defends Zhiyi against earlier readers (Jizang, Daosheng, Master She, and others who are not named) and explains why the four wheels of the Necklace can be "borrowed" to describe stages of the perfect teaching. He also argues that the other worlds seen in the light display the Five Periods in order, from the Avataṃsaka to the stūpas raised after a buddha's nirvāṇa. The part ends with the verses on the bodhisattvas' practices and the Sūtra of the Five Kings. Phrases quoted from the Textual Commentary use the site's published English.

The Teaching for Bodhisattvas and the Buddha's Protection

Chinese Source

「教菩薩法」明因人所依，此去仍帶異名以釋，故加之以處。處為能生之一法，一法秖是究竟相，故云諦理。諦理乃與法華不殊，故燈明佛歎《法華經》，亦云「教菩薩法佛所護念」，故得引下普令等文以之為證，證義處也。所以經名在序，但云「無量義」耳。以兼正故，教菩薩法加於處也。取下三昧來通釋之，使兩處義齊俱序並正。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The Dharma that teaches the bodhisattvas" makes clear what persons at the causal stage rely upon. From here on [the commentary] still explains it by bringing in the alternate names, and so it adds "locus" (處) to it. The locus is the single dharma that gives rise [to the immeasurable]; the single dharma is simply the ultimate mark, and so it says "the principle of the truths." The principle of the truths is no different from the Lotus; therefore, when the Buddha Candrasūryapradīpa praises the Lotus Sūtra, he too says "a teaching for bodhisattvas, guarded and held in mind by the Buddha himself," and so [the commentary] can cite the passage below, "universally causing all the assembly" and so on, as proof — proof of the locus of meaning. That is why the sūtra named in the prologue is called only "Immeasurable Meanings." Because it also includes the main section, "the Dharma that teaches the bodhisattvas" adds "locus." It takes the samādhi below to explain them together, so that the meaning of both places is level, both being prologue and both being main section.

嘉祥云：「此有二義，一者實相名為無量，二者實相所生名為無量。」今謂無量之名可名所生，實相之稱應申能生，雖立能所俱名無量則未可也；若對異名能生之法名為實相，斯則可矣。直爾釋序意都不然。經自釋云：「無量義者從一法生」，生即所生，一法能生即實相也。古來匠者，如何得以能生釋耶？故《論》云：「此是如來欲說法時至成就」，既云「欲說」，非即全同，若其全同即是已說，故《論》存序，乃云欲說，意兼於正則為異名，論其二途今釋準彼，亦順下文三昧為歎。若所入三昧唯依所生列名，秖應但云「無量」，故知經名文在所生，意兼能出，所入三昧義必雙含。所以前消論云「欲說此經先入此定」，今從經所表邊，復以義處歎之，及引文證全在此經。

Ru-Yi Translation

Jiaxiang [Jizang] says: "This has two meanings: first, the true mark is called immeasurable; second, what the true mark gives rise to is called immeasurable." I say: the name "immeasurable" may name what is given rise to, and the term "true mark" should express what gives rise; to set up both what gives rise and what is given rise to and call both "immeasurable" is not acceptable. If, with reference to the alternate name, the dharma that gives rise is called the true mark, that is acceptable. But to explain the prologue simply in this way is not its intent at all. The sūtra explains itself: "Immeasurable meanings arise from a single dharma." "Arise" is what is given rise to; the single dharma that gives rise is the true mark. How could the masters of old explain [immeasurable] as what gives rise? Therefore the Treatise speaks of "the accomplishment of the arrival of the time when the Tathāgata is about to preach the Dharma." Since it says "about to preach," [that sūtra] is not entirely the same [as the Lotus]; if it were entirely the same, it would already have been preached. Hence the Treatise keeps it as prologue and says "about to preach"; its intent includes the main section, and so it is an alternate name. As for these two lines, the present explanation follows that [treatise], and also accords with the samādhi in the text below, taken as praise. If the samādhi entered were named only according to what is given rise to, it should simply be called "immeasurable." Hence we know that the sūtra's name, in its wording, lies in what is given rise to, while in its intent it includes what gives forth, and the samādhi entered must in meaning contain both. That is why, in working through the Treatise earlier, it said, "Wishing to preach this sūtra, he first enters this concentration." Now, from the side of what the sūtra signifies, it again praises it as the locus of meaning, and the passages cited as proof are all in this sūtra.

「佛護念」者，果人所護既是能生無量義處，復是佛所證得，豈佛所證而非實相？故引自住而以為證。昔未說故名之為護，約法約機皆護念故。從「雖欲」下明護念意，佛意本欲唯說能生，故說《無量義》時機仍未發，隱而不說，故云「護念」。「故無量義」下一護念言亦成兩向，但彼經文雖云從一出多，未云從多歸一，猶是覆相名為護念。若不爾者，則已說《法華》，何名為序？以未說故，故云「雖欲開示」等也。以未說故「護」，未暢故「念」。言「久默」者，自昔至今。「斯要」等意思之可知。若唯從所生非專佛護，在昔通說無時不然，故《法華論》云：「蓮華二義：一者出水，二者開敷。」彼如出水、此若開敷，所以仍名為蓮故華，但有未開當開之別。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "protected and kept in mind by the Buddha": what the person at the stage of fruition protects is the locus that gives rise to immeasurable meanings, and is moreover what the Buddha has realized; how could what the Buddha realized not be the true mark? Hence it cites "the Buddha himself abides" as proof. Because it was not preached before, it is called "protected"; both in terms of the Dharma and in terms of capacities it is protected and kept in mind. From "though wishing" on, it makes clear the meaning of protecting and keeping in mind: the Buddha's intent originally wished to preach only what gives rise; but when he preached the Immeasurable Meanings, the capacities had not yet been aroused, so he concealed it and did not preach it; hence it says "protected and kept in mind." The single expression "protected and kept in mind" after "for this reason . immeasurable meanings" (故無量義) likewise points in two directions: although the text of that sūtra says that the many issue from the one, it does not say that the many return to the one; this is still a concealed aspect, which is called protecting and keeping in mind. Were it not so, the Lotus would already have been preached; how could [that sūtra] be called a prologue? Because it has not yet been preached, it says "though wishing to open and reveal it" and so on. Because it is not yet preached, "protected"; because it is not yet fully expressed, "kept in mind." "Long kept silent" means from of old until now. "This essential truth" and the rest: reflect on the meaning and it can be understood. If it were only in terms of what is given rise to, it would not be something the Buddha specially protected; in the past it was preached generally, at all times. Therefore the Lotus Treatise says: "The lotus flower has two meanings: first, emerging from the water; second, opening and spreading." That [sūtra] is like emerging from the water, this one like opening; that is why it is still named "lotus," and so "flower," with only the difference between not yet opened and about to open.

Entering Concentration after the Preaching

Chinese Source

釋入定者，先舉所入之定，次明能入身心。初文中三：先結入意，次「非禪」下釋結意，三「疑者」下釋疑。初結意者，且約彼經彼定而相成者理則可見。次釋結意者又二：初明定慧之用，互有相資各有力用；次明相即，即定慧體。初相成者，先說後定且從序說，先定後說如下釋疑，佛居果位必無先後，為順化儀現有先後，究而論之其體相即。次立疑者，且依序問，凡諸化儀，皆先定後說，此中何以先說後定？答中先順問答佛之常儀，次申定意，欲明一定義分兩途，次「說此」下述為序意。今時何故先說後定？常儀說已即應眾散，更入此定肅其現眾，眾既不散得為今序。次「何者」下述為正意，以一定中義兼二意，意雖復二，時眾但見無量義後即便入定，不知所入為是何定？不知定後為說何法？故結集者復符佛旨，述所說經但云無量，述所入定即加其處。若從義處以出無量，顯成序意；若收無量以入義處，密成正宗。雖加義處眾亦莫知。言「若作次第」等者，亦順化儀辨定先後，即以不次第而論次第，於佛內照豈可分張。「若明文」等者，謂如來當時不先示定體，故使彌勒勤勤置問，乃是經家於別序中且覆別以從通。

Ru-Yi Translation

In explaining the entering of concentration, [the commentary] first names the concentration entered, and next makes clear the body and mind that enter. The first passage has three parts: first, it concludes the intent of entering; next, from "without meditative concentration" on, it explains the conclusion; third, from "One might doubt this" on, it resolves the doubt. As for the first, concluding the intent: taking that sūtra and that concentration as completing each other, the principle can be seen. The next, explaining the conclusion, again has two parts: first it makes clear the function of concentration and wisdom, that they assist each other and each has its own power and function; next it makes clear that they are mutually identical, which is the essence of concentration and wisdom. As for the first, completing each other: preaching first and concentration after is spoken for the time being in terms of the prologue; concentration first and preaching after is as in the resolving of the doubt below. The Buddha, dwelling at the stage of fruition, certainly has no before and after; to accord with the methods of conversion he shows a before and after; examined to the end, their essence is mutually identical. Next, setting up the doubt: it asks, for the time being, according to the prologue: in all the methods of conversion, concentration comes first and preaching after; why here is there preaching first and concentration after? In the answer, first, following the question, it answers with the Buddha's ordinary practice; next it sets out the intent of the concentration, wishing to make clear that one concentration divides in meaning into two lines; next, from "having finished preaching this sūtra" on, it states the intent as prologue. Why now is there preaching first and concentration after? By ordinary practice, once the preaching is over the assembly should disperse; entering this concentration again holds the present assembly in solemn attention, and since the assembly does not disperse, it can serve as the present prologue. Next, from "Why so?" on, it states the intent as main section, for within one concentration the meaning includes two intents. Although the intents are two, the assembly at the time saw only that after the Immeasurable Meanings he entered concentration; they did not know what concentration he entered, nor what Dharma he would preach after the concentration. Hence the compilers in turn matched the Buddha's intent: describing the sūtra preached, they said only "immeasurable"; describing the concentration entered, they added "locus." If the immeasurable issue from the locus of meaning, this openly forms the intent of prologue; if the immeasurable are gathered to enter the locus of meaning, this secretly forms the main section. Even though "locus of meaning" is added, the assembly still did not know. As for "If we speak in terms of sequence" and so on: this too distinguishes the before and after of the concentrations in accord with the methods of conversion, discussing sequence on the basis of non-sequence; in the Buddha's inner illumination, how could they be divided out? As for "were this made plainly evident in the text" and so on: it means that the Tathāgata at that time did not first show the essence of the concentration, and so caused Maitreya to pose his question earnestly; this is the sūtra's compilers, within the specific prologue, for the time being covering the specific so as to follow the general.

Body and Mind Unmoving

Chinese Source

「彌勒」等者，「慇懃」指四伏疑，「靳固」指四伏難，皆累至四故曰「慇懃」等。「靳」字（居覲切），牢也。二定並得為序，故云「其義轉明」。「身心」下明所依身心，先明不動所以，以得所緣實相故令身心不動。次云「身之」等者，釋上身心所依處也。故知身心不動亦由義處，故本源理性俱名為處，對彼身心假施二稱，如來實證色心體一，即此色心是三德故，欲說本有理妙常經，先以色心不動表之。又身之與心俱表示迹，今以迹表本故云「虛空常寂」。次引大通入法華定證身心也。故此定體名異理同，若分所入相同時別，上二句證身，下二句證心。「身若」下重譬，身心稱理故也。非常住法身，不可以金剛喻，非本有理定，不可以虛空比。「無量義」下結此身心功歸義處。「稱為」下釋疑。疑云：定若依處應唯稱處，何得復存無量名耶？「此定」下釋也。言「無量」者，所照得名。所照者何？即所生是。亦非異時故云「而照」，無量即處存亦何妨。「若作」等者，向且存異名，定體身心不動，若將此相以表序者，以此不動等正表序後當說一實，今指不動不分別時，如義處也。上句釋身、下句釋心，是則却對定前身心動運分別，如無量也。先開後合序義灼然，次更問答釋疑。先問可見。次答意者，若準常儀說已便散，何足為奇？今說已入定知後不徒然，後若不徒然，前定體應別，故眾集說定皆表當聞，故令時眾肅有所待。「肅」字息六切，《爾雅》云：「肅肅翼翼，恭也。」翼翼恭恭，心有所得。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "Maitreya's earnest inquiry" and so on: "earnest" (慇懃) refers to the four concealed doubts, and "firm reticence" (靳固) refers to the four concealed objections; both accumulate to four, hence "earnest" and so on. 靳 (pronunciation note) means "firm." Both concentrations can serve as prologue, hence it says "its meaning becomes all the clearer." From "body and mind" on, it makes clear the body and mind that rely [on the locus]. First it makes clear why they are unmoving: because he has attained the true mark that is the object, body and mind are made unmoving. Next, "The fundamental source of the body" and so on explains the locus on which the body and mind above rely. Hence we know that the unmoving of body and mind is also due to the locus of meaning; therefore the fundamental source and the principle-nature are both called the locus, two names provisionally assigned in relation to body and mind. The Tathāgata truly realizes that form and mind are one in essence, because this very form and mind are the three virtues; wishing to preach the sūtra of the originally present, wondrous, permanent principle, he first signifies it by the unmoving of form and mind. Further, body and mind both signify the manifested trace; now the trace signifies the origin, hence "empty space" and "ever tranquil." Next it cites Mahābhijñājñānābhibhū's entering the Lotus concentration as proof of body and mind. Hence the essence of this concentration differs in name but is the same in principle; if one distinguishes what is entered, the features are the same but the times differ. The first two phrases prove the body, the last two prove the mind. From "the body is like vajra" on, the similes are repeated, because body and mind accord with principle. Without the permanently abiding dharma-body it could not be likened to vajra; without the originally present concentration of principle it could not be compared to empty space. From "the samādhi of the locus of immeasurable meanings" on, it concludes that the merit of this body and mind returns to the locus of meaning. From "As for why it is called" on, it resolves a doubt. The doubt says: if the concentration relies on the locus, it should be called only "locus"; how can it still keep the name "immeasurable"? From "this samādhi" on is the resolution. "Immeasurable" takes its name from what is illuminated. What is illuminated? It is what is given rise to. Nor is it at a different time, hence "illuminating"; since the immeasurable is itself the locus, what harm is there in keeping [the name]? As for "If we take up its meaning as prologue" and so on: just now it kept the alternate name for the time being, the essence of the concentration being the body and mind unmoving; if this feature is used to signify the prologue, this unmoving and the rest precisely signify that after the prologue the one real will be preached. Here it points to the time of not moving and not discriminating, which is like the locus of meaning. The first phrase explains the body and the second the mind; this in turn contrasts with the body and mind moving, acting, and discriminating before the concentration, which is like the immeasurable. First opening, afterward merging: the meaning of prologue is plain. Next, a further question and answer resolves a doubt. First the question, which can be seen. Next, the intent of the answer: if by ordinary practice the assembly dispersed once the preaching was over, what would be extraordinary in that? Now, having preached, he enters concentration; we know that what follows is not in vain. If what follows is not in vain, the essence of the earlier concentration must be different. Hence the gathering of the assembly, the preaching, and the concentration all signify what is about to be heard, and so cause the assembly at the time to wait solemnly. 肅: pronunciation note. The Erya says: "Su su and yi yi mean reverent." Yi yi, reverent and respectful: the mind has something it has obtained.

「雖入開定」等者，開定之言仍前序意，未盡其旨故立雖言，意既在合定體豈違？常定尚未曾云從一出多，況誰曾云從多歸一？即開表合故與常殊。文殊引古既云「皆有此事」，故知一定二義不疑，故云何以證今？「豈可以」等者，古人不立說定為瑞，故通斥之，乃集經者大權所置，故非凡下之所測量。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "Although the Buddha enters the 'opening' samādhi" and so on: the words "opening samādhi" continue the earlier intent of prologue; because they have not yet exhausted its point, it uses "although." Since the intent lies in merging, how could the essence of the concentration differ? Of ordinary concentrations it has never even been said that the many issue from the one, let alone has anyone said that the many return to the one. The opening itself signifies the merging, hence it differs from the ordinary. Since Mañjuśrī, citing the ancient [buddha], says "this very circumstance is included among them," we know that one concentration has two meanings, without doubt; hence it says "how could it serve as evidence for the present one?" As for "how could one possibly" and so on: the ancients did not set up the preaching and the concentration as signs, so it rejects them in general. [These signs] were arranged by the compilers of the sūtra with great provisional power, and so are not what ordinary, lowly people can fathom.

Ru-Yi Translation

In explaining the four flowers, [the commentary] first gives the old masters and the sūtras and treatises, making clear that the names of the flowers are not fixed. Further, the Great Prajñā [Sūtra] also says "agreeable, greatly agreeable, soft, greatly soft." Though the teachings differ, their texts mostly list four. As for what Master [Fa]yun experienced, said to be like mica, this was merely an omen on a single occasion. Within what is signified, in rejecting the old [view] as "too narrow and does not fit the case," it first rejects its narrowness and next its not fitting. As for first rejecting the narrowness: now, with sixteen in each teaching, how could the old view compare? Hence the censure "does not even exhaust the sixteen assemblies within the Tripiṭaka teaching," because there are only four. Moreover, it simply says "sixteen": sixteen of which teaching? So one should go through the teachings to sort out the sixteen. One might also censure: is it the sixteen of the initiating assembly, or . the sixteen of those forming connections? Hence it says "let alone the forty-eight assemblies." Hence we know that speaking of the four, bhikṣus and the rest, although it includes the initiating assembly and the rest, has no principle by which to reveal them. If the four, the initiating assembly and the rest, are set out, they include the four, bhikṣus and the rest, let alone śrāvakas, bodhisattvas, and the mixed classes; within each class, every one is endowed with initiating assembly and the rest. Because the ancients describe four, it must be stated in terms of bhikṣus and the rest. From "Moreover, the mark of the flowers" on, it explains "does not fit": these flowers secretly recompense [the fact] that they now obtain the wondrous cause and will proceed to the wondrous fruit. The ancients simply said the four signify bhikṣus and the rest, and so invited the present criticism. What is rained down is flowers; flowers should signify cause; the fourfold assembly already has [its cause], so why need it be signified further? This censures the ancients for not knowing that the raining of flowers signifies the present wondrous cause, which differs from the cause of the past. If it signified the fourfold assembly hoping only for a new fruit, why rain flowers? This censures the ancients for not knowing that the scattering on the Buddha signifies that the wondrous fruit is to come, which differs from the fruit of the past. Present and past causes and fruits, coarse and wondrous, are forever apart; to lump them under one name leaves present and past without basis. Further, Master [Dao]sheng also says, "It signifies that the four fruits are not real." This uses the text in which the bodhisattva of the Tripiṭaka rejects the Lesser; knowing that the four fruits are not real does not yet reach an understanding of the shared teaching, so how could it reveal the Lotus? Next, from "Now I say" on, is the explanation proper. First it raises the partial causes of the past against the present perfect cause; the perfect cause of the past is not distinct [from the present], so it rejects only three teachings (and so on). In the Tripiṭaka it speaks only of the two vehicles, because they cannot be joined [to a higher teaching]. It should be as the Profound Meaning says: "The three causes of the past differ greatly," and so on. "The buddha-cause" is simply the perfect cause. Translator's notes

Quoted lemmas: Zhanran's comments run from "the Dharma that teaches the bodhisattvas" near the end of the site's Textual Commentary fascicle two to the verses on giving in fascicle three. Words in quotation marks from that text use the site's English; where the site's phrasing does not show the word Zhanran is glossing, the Chinese is given in parentheses.

The notes on Calming and Contemplation: references such as "the notes on fascicle one of Calming and Contemplation" point to Zhanran's Fuxing (T1912). The Four Foundations of Mindfulness (unit 19) is Zhiyi's Sinianchu (T1918).

The Lotus Treatise (unit 2): "the accomplishment of the arrival of the time when the Tathāgata is about to preach the Dharma" is the third of the seven accomplishments in Vasubandhu's Upadeśa (T1519). The two meanings of the lotus, "emerging from the water" and "opening," are also from that treatise. "Immeasurable meanings arise from a single dharma" is in the Sūtra of Immeasurable Meanings (T276).

"For this reason . immeasurable meanings" (unit 2): the phrase 故無量義 is not in the site's Chinese of the Textual Commentary, which reads 是故如來之所護念. It is translated as a reference to the sentence ending "hence it is called 'protected and kept in mind'"; the exact lemma is uncertain.

Rāhula and general conversion (unit 11): the wording 今佛但羅云通化 is obscure. It is read as contrasting the sixteen princes of Mahābhijñājñānābhibhū, who repeated the Lotus to form connections, with the present Buddha, whose only son Rāhula is converted together with the whole assembly.

Master Yun and the mica flowers (unit 18): Zhanran alludes to an omen of flowers "like mica" said to have fallen while Fayun of Guangzhai lectured; the source of the story was not located.

Borrowing the distinct (unit 19): Zhanran insists that the four wheels of the Necklace (copper, silver, gold, crystal) are distinct-teaching stages "borrowed" to grade the perfect stages; "sometimes borrowing the high to complete the low" is in Calming and Contemplation fascicle six (T1911). "The three causes of the past differ greatly" is in the Profound Meaning (T1716).

Dharma Master She (涉法師, units 20, 47): an earlier commentator on the Lotus, cited again in Part 8; he could not be identified.

Causes of earthquakes (unit 20): the Dīrgha Āgama (T1, Youxing jing) gives eight causes; Zhanran quotes the six that coincide with events of a buddha's life. The Pure Conduct Sūtra is the site's Upāsaka-śīla Sūtra.

The mouth (unit 29): Zhanran glosses 面門 in the Mahāparinirvāṇa passage as "the mouth."

Zhang'an's own view (unit 29): the remark on light drawn in through the ribs, which the site translates "in my own view," is attributed by Zhanran to Zhang'an (Guanding), the recorder of the Textual Commentary.

The Sūtra of the Twelve Links (unit 29): the passage on eight kinds of stūpa is cited by later authors (e.g., Fazang, T1733) but was not located in a sūtra of that name.

The two matters (unit 47): Zhanran takes "two matters" as merit and the dharma-body, from the Golden Light line "the gathering together of merit adorns the Buddha's body," not as the wind and the ground.

Orchids and chrysanthemums (unit 48): the old saying that spring orchids and autumn chrysanthemums each excel in their season; both readings of the line are allowed.

The Sūtra of the Five Kings (unit 55): T523; Zhanran's quotation abridges the sūtra. The four wheel-turning kings who hired a barber are cited from the Dīrgha Āgama; the passage was not checked line by line.
