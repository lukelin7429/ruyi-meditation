The Bases of the Parable and the Joy of Śāriputra

This part comments on the close of the Textual Commentary's fourth fascicle and the opening of its fifth: the verses of the Skillful Means chapter that "form the basis" for the parable of the burning house, and the beginning of the Parable chapter. Zhanran first finishes the discussion of the correct cause and the six dharmas, then defends Zhang'an's decision to set out the parable-bases in advance. He reviews the older schemes of five, six, and ten parables (the first master, Master Yao, Masters Chang and Long, and Guangzhai), explains the "four faults" found in Guangzhai, and shows how the present scheme of a general parable of six elements and particular parables of four can be opened or merged to fit all three rounds of teaching. He then glosses the five turbidities, the six destinies, the Buddha's three weeks under the tree, the first turning of the wheel, and the three kinds of "seeking" by which the hearers' capacity is stirred. In the Parable chapter he explains the title, the parable as it appears in each of the four teachings, the relation of the Lotus to the Avataṃsaka, and Śāriputra's threefold joy of body, speech, and mind, read through the four siddhāntas.

The Correct Cause and the Six Dharmas

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, from "Now, this correct cause" on, it matches the correct [cause] and the aggregates and sense-fields against the cultivated conditioning and revealing [causes]: the three causes and the six dharmas are neither identical nor apart. As for "this correct cause is not identical with the six ordinary mundane dharmas" and so on: each raises one side. It should say that the aggregates and sense-fields are neither identical with nor apart from the inherent correct cause, and again neither identical with nor apart from the cultivated conditioning and revealing [causes]. The correct cause is the principle of the six dharmas, and therefore it is not apart from them; but because it is principle, it is not identical with them. The conditioning and revealing [causes] stand to the six in just the same way.

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it is easy to show. The correct [cause] is originally "not apart," and here "not identical" is added; the conditioning and revealing [causes] are originally "not identical," and here "not apart" is added. Hence each must contain both before it can be said to exhaust the principle. This uses the Great Sūtra's simile of [the blind men] feeling the elephant; the text of the simile is quoted in full in my notes on the third [fascicle] of Calming and Contemplation. As for "the six dharmas": the five aggregates and the spiritual self. Therefore these six are also called the correct cause, since they all belong to suffering; and they are also called the conditioning and revealing [causes], since they include delusion and karma. Here there must be two pairings, general and specific. Specifically: among the six, formations and the spiritual self are the seed of the conditioning cause; the remaining [mental] factors and form are the seed of the revealing cause; consciousness is the seed of the correct cause. In the general pairing, it is fully as in the present text: the inherent-virtue correct cause of the six dharmas and the cultivated conditioning and revealing [causes] of the six dharmas are neither identical nor apart; therefore the cultivated six and the inherent six are one essence without difference. One should know that the aggregates and sense-fields are simply the ever abiding. If this text is set beside the general and specific pairings in Calming and Contemplation, that passage pairs them with the three virtues and the four virtues of the Great Sūtra; each has its own intent.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "at the site of enlightenment" and so on: the principle of the fruit was accomplished first, hence it says "long dwelling within it, unfolded outward" (久暢); for now, measured from the site of quiescence, the forty-some years count as "long unfolded." As for "the sentiment of beings was heavily obstructed": apart from the perfect [teaching] within the three flavors, all the rest are heavily obstructed. As for "and so on": it means distinguishing this finely by going through the collections and in terms of the flavors. As for the passage above having four [points]: judged by the above, there are only three: first, the intent of appearing in the world; second, opening the provisional; third, revealing the real. Since it now says four, one should know that the passage above can also be made four: one should say that the fourth concludes the above three ages as a whole by the oneness of persons and the oneness of principle. It may not be like the above: the principle agrees and the text differs. Hence it now says four; and there are two further differences: first, above it first opens the provisional and then reveals the real, while here it first reveals the real and then opens the provisional; second, within the real the passage above has only teaching and practice, while here it has principle and teaching, or principle and practice. The differences do no harm, and so it does not speak of them separately.

Five, Six, and Ten Parables: The Old Schemes

Chinese Source

「指上本下」者，下譬品文用此為本，譬必有法故云「本下」。「文義交加」者，此中指下、至下指此，故云「尋疏」等也。章安預點使無眩亂，眩亂在鈍，利者何憂？然諸師分譬與法參互，宿世舛隔，欲為譬本取定莫從，故先敘非、後方正釋。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "cite what stands above while glossing what lies below" (指上本下): the text of the Parable chapter below uses this as its basis; a parable must have a dharma, hence "the basis for what is below" (本下). As for "text and meaning become tangled together": here it points to what is below, and when it reaches below it points back here; hence it says "consulting the commentary" and so on. Zhang'an marked it in advance so that there would be no dazzlement and confusion. Dazzlement and confusion belong to the dull; what worry is there for the sharp? Yet the masters divided parable and dharma in cross-mixtures, and set the past lives at odds; if one wished to fix a basis for the parable, there was nothing to follow. Hence it first sets out what is wrong, and only afterward gives the explanation proper.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the five parables of the first master: first, the parable of the elder, which is the text from "in a kingdom or city" on; its dharma-teaching is the text from "I too am now like this" on. Second, the parable of pondering how to save [the children], which is the text from "The elder had this thought" on; its dharma-teaching is the text from "I, with the buddha-eye, observe" on. Third, the parable of luring with the provisional, which is the text from "At that time the elder then had this thought" on; its dharma-teaching is the text from "when I first sat at the site of enlightenment" on. Fourth, the parable of equality, which is the text from "At that time the elder bestowed on each" on; its dharma-teaching is the text from "I saw the buddha's disciples . whose aspiration seeks" on. Fifth, the parable of the absence of falsehood, which is the text from "Śāriputra, what do you think" on; its dharma-teaching is the text from "you should have no doubt" on. In our school the first is the general parable, and the general parable has six [elements]; how could it be the elder alone? Hence it is not adopted. The second, "pondering how to save," is in our school the [parable of] being unable to save the children; it is merely the parable, within the particular parables, of using the Great [Vehicle] to seek fitness. Hence one knows that merely saying "pondering how to save" does not exhaust the principle; for within using the Great to save the children there are three [points]: seeking fitness, the absence of capacity, and setting the transformation to rest. One should not speak only of "pondering how to save." From the third parable on, our school, from "I" on, divides it within the particular parables into six meanings, while they have only three; the basis for the parable is insufficient, and so this too is not adopted.

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Yao takes all of the sections on the five buddhas as revealing the real. As for "four sections": he merges the first section into the section on Śākyamuni, and combines the provisional and the real into one section; he takes the section on the five turbidities as the inability to teach the one; and he further takes the passage of pressing [faith] home as "those who cannot attain it." None of this fits the text, and so it is not adopted. As for his saying that within the parable there are only three, so that the third, "those who cannot attain it," is missing: since the parable has no such passage, the [scheme] is inconsistent throughout. In the list given in the text, opening the provisional is put first; but opening the provisional is revealing the real, and should be matched with the second, the passage on equal bestowal. Thus his basis for the parable, and his parable, are far too sparse. Moreover, among the section-gates on the five buddhas in the prose, [the section on] the buddhas [in general] is still general and further lacks the five turbidities, and the three buddhas, by analogy, are briefer still in their text; Śākyamuni, the lord of the teaching, is somewhat fuller in the features of his text. Hence he takes only Śākyamuni and divides it into four sections. Master Yao is loose and diffuse, and so he is not adopted.

Ru-Yi Translation

From "There are those who evaluate this scheme" on: this is someone else who took our school's meaning and still mixed it with Guangzhai, and so used this idea to fault Guangzhai: since both are fine divisions, why not make nineteen phrases? Setting Guangzhai's ten parables against the present general six and particular four, the general and the particular each opened and combined give eighteen phrases; adding Guangzhai's one phrase makes nineteen phrases. How so? Guangzhai's first is our school's general parable, which should be divided into six; Guangzhai merges it into one. From his second, seeing the fire, on, our school divides the particular into four parables, while Guangzhai makes nine. First, seeing the fire: we now divide it into three, Guangzhai has only one. His third, "when I first" and so on, is the second of the present text's particular four, setting aside the Great and bestowing the lesser, which Guangzhai divides into two passages.

Thus the particular is divided into twelve. Guangzhai further opens the first of the four passages of the third of the present text's four particular parables into two passages, escaping the danger and seeking the cart. Ours is already extremely fine, yet it makes only eighteen; if the sixth of the particular twelve were further opened into two, there would be the four faults: it is precisely for this reason. Once it is opened into two it becomes thirteen, and together with the six of the general it makes nineteen; why not call it nineteen phrases? As for why, though it is now eighteen, it is not called eighteen phrases: it is simply because the dharma-teaching, the parable, the past lives, and belief and understanding do not have this meaning. Wishing to make it fit throughout, we again merge it into five, or into the two of general and particular; with few chapters and sections, the upper and lower easily agree. Hence the present text has three ways of dividing the text. Guangzhai fixes ten and has none of this expansion and contraction: going forward it does not make nineteen, going back it does not make five, and his general and particular, broad and brief, again do not correspond. Moreover, he opens the great capacity into two sections, which is a hindrance within the dharma-teaching, and so it should not be opened. Yet compared with the other masters, Guangzhai is the best.

The Four Faults and the Six Comparisons

Chinese Source

「若以」至「六義之內」者，若立此六則攝十九入此六中，何以不但云為六義耶？何者？依前總六却合為一，於別四中但離第二以為二段，則為五章，對前總一即成六義。若全依今文但成五義，他且依彼而為問辭。「又十譬」至「參差」等者，開為十譬，法譬二處尚自參差，況與信解等文合耶？故「法說」下出參差相。又光宅句數與今細開似同，而章目未穩，故亦不用。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "if judged by phrase" up to "within these six meanings": if these six are set up, then the nineteen are gathered into these six; why not simply call them the six meanings? How so? Following the earlier general six, merged back into one, and in the particular four dividing only the second into two sections, there are five sections; with the earlier general one, these make six meanings. If one follows the present text wholly, there are only five meanings; he follows that [scheme] for the moment and makes it the wording of his question. As for "Moreover, with these ten parables" up to "fall out of step with one another" and so on: opened into ten parables, the dharma and the parable in their two places are still out of step; how much more when combined with the texts of belief and understanding and the rest! Hence from "in the direct teaching" on, it sets out the features of being out of step. Moreover, Guangzhai's number of phrases seems the same as our fine division, but his chapter headings are not sound, and so he too is not adopted.

Ru-Yi Translation

Below, in concluding, it speaks of "these four faults": first, before and after; second, great and small; third, presence and absence; fourth, citing the text. Now the first, the fault of before and after not agreeing: if, in the present particular fine division, the fifth is divided into fifth and sixth, together with the seventh passage as the father's joy, then set against the dharma-teaching, the seeking of the cart and the father's joy turn round before and after. As for saying that in the dharma-teaching the seeking of the cart comes first: first it says "Śāriputra, you should know, I saw the buddha's disciples" up to "those whose aspiration seeks the buddha-path"; these two lines are the seeking of the cart coming first, which is Guangzhai's sixth passage. Next, in the two lines and one phrase, only the last phrase says "now I rejoice without fear," that is, the father's joy comes after. As for saying that in the parable-teaching the father's joy comes first: first it says "At that time the elder, seeing his children come out safely," and so on; as for the seeking of the cart coming after: only the following passage says "Then the children each said to their father," and so on.
