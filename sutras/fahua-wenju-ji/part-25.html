The Peaceful Practices: Practice-Ground, Approach-Ground, and the Four Practices

This part is the first section of Zhanran's ninth fascicle. It comments on the Peaceful Practices chapter of the Lotus, whose explanation begins at the end of the site's fascicle-008 of the Textual Commentary and continues at the start of fascicle-009. Zhanran opens by showing how the room, robe, and seat of the Teacher of the Dharma chapter run through the Jeweled Stūpa, Devadatta, and Upholding chapters as the norm for spreading the sūtra, and why a chapter for beginning practitioners still takes the three virtues as its title. He then follows the Commentary's five steps: the three explanations of the title, why the chapter comes, the order of the four practices, their essence, and the detailed explanation. The longest stretches concern the practice-ground and the approach-ground. Zhanran reads the practice-ground through one, two, and three dharmas, opening the two forbearances into four, five, six, or forty-two. For the approach-ground he takes up the ten things to be kept far, the settled mind, and the eighteen emptinesses, which he maps onto the ten modes of contemplation of Calming and Contemplation. The rest covers the faults to avoid in speech, the four evils and four contemplations of mind, the vow, the jewel in the king's topknot, and the dreams that mark the stages of the path.

Ru-Yi Translation

Explanations of chapters, past and present, all give [accounts of] their arising, with ten conditions, five conditions, and so on, and make clear the reason for their coming with three intents, five intents, and so on. Here it is not so: [the Commentary] only follows the momentum of each chapter's text and explains it meaning by meaning, not necessarily in a uniform way. Hence, on reaching the explanation of this chapter, one should restate in detail the intent of the preceding four chapters. Namely, the three from the Teacher of the Dharma on all take the three of the Teacher of the Dharma, room, robe, and seat, as the norm of dissemination. Hence in explaining the titles of the preceding three chapters and in digesting their text, all rely on these three to make clear the chapters' original intent; where, following the textual features, separate accounts of their arising differ, they differ only in the beginnings and ends of the text. Because the Teacher of the Dharma chapter is the beginning of the dissemination, it lists the three dharmas fully as the norm; moreover, the disseminator spreads the main teaching. Hence it has the teacher rely on the three to establish his resolve; only then can he make illustrious the scripture he propagates, causing beings to admire it so that the Dharma can be propagated; aiding [them] in hidden ways and benefiting them openly, he is the envoy of the Tathāgata. If not so, why does the World-Honored One, in commanding people to propagate the sūtra, weigh their capability [to find] those fit to bear the charge, so that those of this land and of other lands, from above and from below, differ in being advanced or not? If one's own practice does not increase beings' faith, it is like one sick with fever selling cooling medicine; hence one cannot transmit the sūtra rashly.

The Three Meanings of the Title

Ru-Yi Translation

This Peaceful [Practices] chapter, though it is for beginning practice, also uses the three virtues to title the chapter, because within the whole chapter there is nothing but the three virtues and the practice of the three virtues. Within [the Commentary's treatment] there are five: first, a general explanation by three meanings; next, it makes clear the reason for the coming of the four practices; third, the order of the four practices; fourth, the difference in essence of the four practices; fifth, the explanation proper. The first passage has two: first the listing, next the explanation. In the explanation, first brief, then broad. In the brief, first, the explanation "according to the literal matter": the two kinds of action [of body and mind], at peace and at ease, advance the practice of verbal action in propagating the sūtra. In "according to the text of the preceding chapter" (附文) there are two: first, following the Teacher of the Dharma; next, following the present chapter. The first passage is wholly in terms of the three virtues and the three tracks. If the dharma body has the practice of the three virtues, it makes the adorned dharma body at peace. Hence the Profound Meaning says: "the Dharma-body's own substance remains plain and unadorned, disregarded and belittled even by the devas and the nāgas"; hence it is complete with the three dharmas, which together guide the practice of propagating the sūtra.

Why the Chapter Comes, and the Order and Essence of the Four Practices

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, from "This chapter is the fourth sense" on, it explains the reason for its coming, again in two: first it makes clear that deep practitioners do not need it; next, from "But if one of initial resolve" on, it makes clear that it comes because beginning practitioners need it. The first passage makes clear the people who do not need it. As for "If the twenty thousand and the eighty koṭis of nayutas" and so on: at the beginning of the Upholding chapter, twenty thousand bodhisattvas together with their retinues all vowed before the Buddha to propagate the sūtra. Some copies of the text say "eight thousand koṭis"; it should say eighty koṭis. That is, in the Upholding chapter, after the nuns asked for predictions, the Buddha had given the predictions, and the nuns had spoken verses praising the Buddha, at that time the World-Honored One looked at the eighty koṭis of bodhisattvas and others. After the Buddha praised them, these bodhisattvas, mindful of the Buddha's charge, [said] they would act as the Buddha taught, and so on. From "deeply understand" on, it directly makes clear the practices not needed, matching all four practices to discuss what is not needed. First, deeply understanding the provisional and the real, they do not need the first practice, because the first practice has one not dwell together with the two vehicles and so on, for fear of indiscriminately receiving provisional dharmas. "Broadly know" makes clear that they do not need the second practice, because the second practice has one not speak of the faults of gradual dharmas and not rely on the perfect to despise the partial; the partial is just the gradual. From "and, further, have mastered" on, it makes clear that they do not need the third practice, because the third practice has one take care of the two vehicles and not rebuke the distinct with the perfect. From "the sovereign freedom of spiritual power" on, it makes clear that they do not need the fourth practice, because the fourth practice has one lead [beings] into the real only after obtaining spiritual powers.

Room, robe, and seat. Unit 3 shows Zhanran reading the Jeweled Stūpa, Devadatta, and Upholding chapters through the room, robe, and seat of the Teacher of the Dharma chapter, which he identifies with the three virtues and the "secret treasury" of buddha-nature in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra.

The five receivings. In unit 9 the 五受 are the five modes of "receiving" negated in the Prajñā sūtras' samādhi of not receiving (受亦不受, 不受亦不受, and so on), not the five feelings; the site's "five kinds of feeling" is kept inside the quotation.

The four, five, and six forbearances. Unit 34 shows how the Commentary derives the forbearances from the sūtra's phrase 柔和善順: 柔和 for subduing and 善順 for according, with 善 split off for the forbearance of faith and 和 for the forbearance of harmonious accord.

The eighteen emptinesses and the ten modes. In unit 59 Zhanran counts seventeen emptinesses from seven of the Commentary's nine compound phrases (2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 3 + 4) and adds "the true mark" to make eighteen. He then matches the chapter's words to the ten modes of contemplation of Calming and Contemplation: the true mark as the wondrous object, compassion as arousing the mind, calming and contemplation as settling the mind, "all" as breaking pervasively, the eighteen as the factors of the path, non-discrimination as passage and blockage, the ten disturbances as assisting, and the dreams as the last three modes.

Sources found. Bakkula's eighty years without a thought of desire (unit 62) is the Bakkula Sūtra, no. 34 of the Madhyama Āgama (T26). The verse "The aggregates of the Dharma taught by the Sage number eighty thousand" (unit 84) is in the Abhidharmakośa (T1558). The ground of the only child (unit 12) is in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T374). The Great Collection story of the rākṣasa king (unit 77) and the Great Treatise passage on the four kinds of self-praise and blame were not located in this wording.

Lotus quotations. "Should they come to him at times," "as is fitting," "Nor, further, does he dwell in things high, middling," "those who study the Buddha's path, seeking out their weaknesses," "neither hear it, nor know it," "by the power of spiritual penetration," "Seeing his worthy and sagely army," and the phrases of the topknot parable (unit 85) follow the site's Lotus page. In unit 34, "never rash" and "his mind never startled" also follow the Lotus page, since Zhanran quotes the sūtra there.

Praise to the face. Zhanran reads 面譽對毀 (unit 72) as "praising [one] to his face amounts to disparaging [the other] to his face"; the site renders it "to praise a person to his face while disparaging him behind his back." His example of the Zhang and Zhao households (unit 77) explains 約張說趙長.

Uncertain readings. "Yata (伽耶陀) is also called Veda" (unit 50) is translated literally. In unit 71, 始未 is read as 始末, "from beginning to end." In unit 76, 制面防喻 is read with 喻 as 諛, "flattery." The "four categories after faith" (unit 90) is translated literally; it may mean the faculties, powers, path, and fruits named in the Commentary.
