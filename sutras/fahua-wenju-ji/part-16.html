Śāriputra's Prediction, the Elder, and the Burning Gate

This part comments on the rest of the Textual Commentary's fifth fascicle, from Śāriputra's verses of receiving and understanding to the opening of the parable of the burning house. Zhanran first sections the verses by the three kinds of action and explains why the prose joins receiving and understanding while the verses set them out each in turn. He then treats the Buddha's confirmation of Śāriputra, the seven kinds of "unsurpassed" from the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā, and the four reasons for granting prediction, which he sets against Jiaxiang's ten. The prediction of the Buddha Flower Light, the long eons before it, and the assembly's offerings and dedication follow. With Śāriputra's request on behalf of the middling faculties the parable begins: the elder's name and conduct are matched to the three lands, his ten virtues are read as worldly, world-transcending, and contemplative, and the ten virtues of contemplation are aligned with the ten modes of contemplation. Zhanran refutes the older views of the single door as principle alone or teaching alone, explains the house, the fire, and the four sides as the four inversions, the eight sufferings, and the foundations of mindfulness, and closes on the question of whether the gate of the teaching is itself burned.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the verses. Above it says that "within the verses, the receiving and the understanding are set forth each in turn." In the prose before, the body is taken as receiving and the mind as understanding, which looks entirely like setting them forth each in turn. Yet it says "stated together" because within receiving there is understanding joined, and within understanding there is receiving joined; hence it says "together." Now it says "each in turn" because each of the three kinds of action has receiving and understanding; though this looks like making them clear together, within each single kind of action receiving and understanding are divided of themselves; hence it is called "each." It must first be briefly sectioned, and afterward one can follow the text. Since it is divided into three, they are the statement, the explanation, and the conclusion. As for the first, the general statement: "I have heard" states speech, and "joy" states the mind. As for "having also attained the Buddha" (兼佛): it also includes "from the Buddha"; that is, it also states the body. Since it is a general statement, for the moment it uses "I have heard." From "this voice of the Dharma" on is receiving; "my heart is filled with great joy" is understanding. Next, the explanation of the three kinds of action. In the first one and a half lines, versifying the bodily receiving and understanding, the first line is bodily receiving and the next half line is bodily understanding. Next, from "I dwelt" on, eleven lines make clear the verbal receiving and understanding. Within this again two: first, nine lines make clear the body being far away; next, two lines make clear entering the dharma-nature. The first again has two: first, eight lines make clear verbal receiving; next, one line makes clear verbal understanding, since he receives and understands the fault of speech in the past. Within entering the dharma-nature there are two: the first one and a half lines make clear verbal receiving, and the next half line makes clear verbal understanding, since he receives and understands what speech attained in the past. Next, from "but now" on, nine and a half lines of mental receiving and understanding: the first eight lines are mental receiving, the next one and a half lines mental understanding. In the last two and a half lines, the general conclusion, the first one and a half lines conclude receiving and the next one line concludes understanding. Receiving means outwardly receiving what the Buddha teaches; understanding is inwardly accepting the Buddha's intent. The reason the prose joins them while the verses separate them is that together they make one meaning. Moreover, taken as a whole, this text of nine and a half lines of mental receiving and understanding versifies "the earlier joy of the mind's attainment of wondrous understanding"; for the moment, following the earlier text, it is likewise named by understanding. Yet within it one must divide more minutely, and it can also be made eight: first, two lines receive that the past was not real; next, one line directly versifies the wondrous understanding from hearing in the first round, that is, it versifies the revealing of the real within the Skillful Means [chapter]; third, one line goes back to versify the time of the brief teaching at the beginning of the round of the dharma-teaching above, when doubt and regret arose; fourth, one line versifies the section on the buddhas [in general] above: "every kind" versifies the opening of the provisional above, and "parable" versifies the udumbara flower above; fifth, from "the Buddha spoke" on, one line versifies the section on the buddhas of the past; sixth, from "of the present" on, one line versifies the section on the buddhas of the present and future; seventh, from "As the World-Honored One of the present" (如今) on, one line versifies the section on Śākyamuni above; eighth, from "The World-Honored One" on, one and a half lines repent the transgression and reproach himself, verifying that the fault of the first doubt lay in himself.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "I would constantly, day and night" and so on: if the sages, too, still took dusk and dawn as dusk and night, and reflected on this [at those times], what would be worth relating? Therefore here one should follow what is signified. The explanation has two layers, both in terms of the past teaching. Within the capacity this doubt was spontaneously and obscurely present but not yet made plain; [hence] there is this account. If [nirvāṇa] is within birth-and-death, that is the Mahāyāna intent; if it is not within birth-and-death, that is the Lesser Vehicle intent. Having already attained the lesser, he did not know where this realization lay. "Constantly" (嘗) means "formerly" (曾). In the past this doubt had been present in the capacity; this sets the Tripiṭaka against the Mahāyāna as inside and outside. If he now attains awakening, this doubt is surely dispelled. Further, from "Or again, birth-and-death" on, next, birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are both called night, and getting to hear the principle of the middle is called day. This sets the distinct and perfect against the Tripiṭaka and shared, so that doubt arises obscurely. Only after hearing the opening and revealing is this doubt removed; this is because in the Vaipulya and the Prajñā both were heard. Yet the words of the commentary are few. It should further say that the real principle of the middle way is day. In the past, in the Great, he heard this principle of the middle; though, set against what he had already realized, hesitation arose obscurely; not yet having realized this middle, it was as if the sun were still hidden; and on just hearing the brief opening there was still this doubt. How much more so, having dwelt in the lesser in the past: only now, hearing the five buddhas broadly reveal the real and the provisional, are both doubts dispelled, as when the sun rises.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "Furthermore, there are two kinds of people in the world" and so on: the intent is a parable. The śrāvakas passed through all the [teaching] seats, now rebuked, now entrusted, now glad, now grieved, and came to the Lotus having fully undergone hardship. Of their own practice and of benefiting beings there is nothing they have not heard and known; they only thought it was not their own charge, and so between self and others there was a slight difference. Hence they differ from those who make a fresh start in the various flavors. Hence one knows [it is like one who] starts from a humble station and passes through both civil and military [offices], passing from shallow ranks to deep ones and knowing what is and is not possible for people; as the bodhisattvas of [the Tathāgata] Fragrance-Accumulated further learned the twofold flow. Hence those who make a fresh start are little versed in the many practices. Yet the two ways are each superior and inferior in turn; one cannot hold to a single direction. As for "winnowed and refined through the five flavors": it refers generally to all five flavors as the winnowing, not to the Prajñā alone. As for the sūtra's "undefiled and inconceivable" and so on: formerly he thought the undefiled reached only [nirvāṇa] without remainder; now at last he knows it reaches the real site of awakening. As for "understanding what is Māra and what is not Māra" and so on: formerly, hearing what differed from the original, he took the Buddha to be Māra; now at last he knows that what his former foolish perception mistook was the Buddha; hence it says so. As for the sūtra's "The Buddha, through every kind of cause" and so on: before, Śākyamuni's opening of the provisional was used to explain the buddhas' revealing of the real. It says, "I expound all things through countless skillful means, through every kind of cause and condition, through parables and words," and so on, up to the sections on the buddhas of the three times, each of which says "likewise, through ." up to "all of this Dharma is for the sake of the one buddha vehicle."

The Buddha's Confirmation and the Seven Kinds of Unsurpassed

Ru-Yi Translation

In the conclusion, as for "concluding summation" (結成): it can again be made two: the first one and a half lines versify concluding, and the next one line versifies completing. Within the first, concluding, there are three: the first three phrases conclude the Buddha's voice, that is, the verbal joy. The next three phrases conclude the mental joy, again using hearing the Buddha to include the body. Hence there are joy and the removal of doubt; [then] what is realized and what is completed: the reward elicited and its functioning. As for "confirming the three points of meaning related above": seeing the Buddha confirms the bodily joy, hearing the Dharma confirms the verbal joy, and awakened understanding confirms the mental [joy]. In the past he had already taught the Great; why is it only today that he attains bodily nearness? Though he was deluded in between, having once heard was not in error; this shows the condition for hearing again today. Teaching the lesser midway was still not for the sake of the lesser; that he now again gets to hear and believe is due to the past teaching. This verifies that the present mental understanding, measured against the past, is not false. Yet the three, seeing the Buddha and the rest, each generally contain the three kinds of receiving. As for "the circumstance of his seeing the Buddha": the great [teaching] seen in the past becomes the condition for attaining the real today, for only the real is called "seeing." As for "the circumstance of his grief, regret, and hearing of the Dharma": since forgetting in between, it is due to taking up the lesser. Moreover, from hearing [the teaching of] the two butters up to the brief teaching, all grief and regret are due to the lesser in the past. As for "not false": in the past he originally heard the Great, so his faith now is not false.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, it cites the Daśabhūmika[-vibhāṣā] to explain "unsurpassed," and next cites the Necklace to explain "path": for the moment it borrows the names of the distinct [teaching] to reveal the meaning of the perfect. The meaning perfect and the name distinct: it is so in every case. Others lack this intent, and it cannot be used loosely. This text of the seven kinds of unsurpassed lists two layers. In the first layer, the first one is the fruit and the latter six lie in the cause. Among the six causes, the first two are the six harmonies, the next two merit and wisdom, the next two realization and practice. Though divided into three pairs, they lend each other strength; therefore the six causes are all called unsurpassed. The cause is practiced looking toward the fruit; hence the fruit comes first and the cause after. As for the six harmonies: the first, "unsurpassed in reception and upholding," is body, speech, and mind; because there are these three harmonies, one benefits oneself and benefits others. The six harmonies of the Mahāyāna take in all dharmas without remainder. The two hindrances are simply the afflictive and the cognitive. As for these two hindrances, if discussed in the distinct [teaching], before the grounds, non-knowing is only the dust-and-sand outside the realms; if it extends above and below, non-knowing takes in the non-knowing inside and outside [the realms] and also ignorance. Hence one knows it is only a difference of opening and merging. Within "unsurpassed in practice," it speaks only of the holy and the pure [practices] and omits the other three, because the heavenly [practice] is what is realized, and the sick and infant [practices] are the functioning of the fruit. The two of cultivation realize the one; these two have that capacity. Therefore only two are listed and not the other three. The second layer is established again from the fruit: the six causes are named from the fruit, and it pursues the names and explains them in detail to bring out the intent of the sūtra. Since below it concludes, "passages such as these variously make clear the unsurpassed path," the meaning of "path" is immeasurable and is completed according to whichever teaching; hence it says that the present sūtra takes the perfect as the unsurpassed path. That is, the path belonging to the unsurpassed is called the unsurpassed path, and each single unsurpassed contains the unsurpassedness of all paths.

The Four Meanings of Prediction

Chinese Source

These four [points of] prediction are all present in the three rounds. In the middle round, of the four, only the word "middling" in the second point is removed; hence at the lower round there are only three, not four. Moreover, of these four, the first point contains the three below. Moreover, the first and fourth are relative to the past, and the second and third only to the present. Moreover, the first and fourth concern only oneself, and the second and third concern others. Moreover, the third extends to the future and the present; the first is only the present, the second only the future, and the fourth only the past.

Ru-Yi Translation

Jiaxiang's ten points are profuse and do not fit. Now I roughly point them out so that their profusion and brevity may be known. First, attesting that the understanding is not false; second, causing him to have no doubt or regret. These two belong to confirming; why need they be called prediction again? Third, leading beings to give rise to faith: this belongs to our third. Fourth, causing Śāriputra to aspire to the fruit: this belongs to the praise of the two wisdoms of the five buddhas at the beginning of the chapter, which was meant to open the provisional and also to make him aspire to the ultimate. Here it is only the granting of a prediction of the eight aspects; at the first abiding he attains it himself, and there is no need to speak of aspiring. Fifth, for drawing in the eight groups: it directly draws in those of the same kind, and the eight groups only incidentally. This is the same as the incidental sense in the second [point] of our text: formerly the two vehicles were not predicted, so those of the two-vehicle faculties and nature were forever cut off from this hope; now they are predicted, and incidentally the eight groups are drawn in; hence it says prediction is needed. Sixth, for leading beings to rebirth [in the land]: this is the same as our intent. Translator's notes

Quoted phrases. All of this part comments on the site's fascicle-005 of the Textual Commentary, from Śāriputra's verses to the burning gate. Quoted phrases follow the site's English. Where the site has "kalpas," "eons" is written; the first abiding is called an abiding (unit 7, 16), where the site has "abode." In units 28–33 the site renders 門 as "door," in units 38–39 as "gate"; Zhanran's own sentences follow whichever word the site uses in the passage being explained.

The Lotus. Lines quoted directly from the sūtra follow the site's Lotus ("The golden-colored thirty-two marks," "undefiled and inconceivable," "each of them took off the upper robe," "turning of themselves," "hearing what they have never heard," "This Dharma abides in its own Dharma-position"). The Lotus reads 常 ("constantly") where the Textual Commentary has 甞, which Zhanran glosses as "formerly" (unit 3).

The Teacher of the Dharma. The passage cited in unit 19 is the opening of that chapter of the Lotus; the site's wording ("even a single verse or a single phrase") is adjusted to Zhanran's order (一句一偈).

Jiaxiang. Jiaxiang is Jizang (549–623). His ten reasons for prediction (unit 7) were not located in this form; his Commentary on the Lotus (T1721) gives six reasons, several of which overlap with the ten listed here.

The Sūtra of the Great Compassion Space-Treasury. Source not located. The story of Śāriputra practicing for sixty eons and retreating when a brahmin begged for his eye is in the Great Treatise (T1509), fascicle twelve, which Jizang's Commentary also cites.

The three hundred monks. The Great Treatise, fascicle forty, comments on the three hundred monks who offered their robes on hearing the prajñā; among the explanations it gives is that they could obtain robes again the same day (unit 17).

The seven kinds of unsurpassed. The list from the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā and the ten lines from the Necklace are quoted in the site's fascicle-005; Zhanran's "heavenly, sick, and infant practices" (unit 5) are the other three of the five practices of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra.

The four pillars of the Nirvāṇa. The verse "What was not is now; what was is now not" follows the site's Nirvāṇa Sūtra (where the two halves stand in the opposite order). In Zhanran's own sentences 本無今有 is "originally nonexistent and now existent." The site's Textual Commentary reads 炊然 ("blazing") where Zhanran and the Lotus have 歘然 ("suddenly").

The Great Sūtra, fascicle twelve. "Suffering has the meaning of oppression" (in the form "suffering is the mark of oppression") and "within lesser suffering they perversely conceive of pleasure" are both in fascicle twelve of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T374), the Holy Practice chapter, as is Kāśyapa's objection about seeking form.

The Abhidharmakośa. The verses on the two gates of cultivation and the chain of bones (unit 36) and on the eyelash (unit 36) are from the Abhidharmakośa (T1558/T1560); the verse on the four births (unit 37) is from its chapter on the world.

The ninety-six paths. The Sūtra of the Ninety-Six Paths is cited the same way in Zhanran's notes on Calming and Contemplation (T1912); the sūtra itself was not located. The Sarvāstivāda Treatise is the Sarvāstivāda-vinaya-vibhāṣā (T1440).

The ten virtues and the ten modes. In unit 31 Zhanran lines up the ten virtues of the contemplative elder with the ten modes of contemplation: the correct object, arousing vows, settling the mind, breaking everywhere, penetrating obstruction, the factors of the path, auxiliary practice, knowing the stages, patient endurance, and freedom from attachment to dharmas.

Historical allusions. Deng Tong (unit 30) was the favorite of Emperor Wen of the Han; the histories say he died of hunger under Emperor Jing, not Emperor Ai. Shi Chong's Golden Valley estate was near Luoyang. "Seasoning the broth" alludes to Gaozong of the Shang (Yin) and his minister Fu Yue.

Textual difficulties. Several readings are uncertain: "mental [joy]" for 意意 (unit 5); "perfect purity" (圓淨) probably for "the purity of the land" (unit 8); "acknowledged [prediction]" for 得認 (unit 7); "On first seeing" for 生見 (unit 38); "the low bench" for 凡案, read as 几案 (unit 39). Alternatively in unit 39: "if the lesser had no impermanence, how could the great be impermanent?"
