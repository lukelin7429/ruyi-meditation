Opening, Showing, Awakening, Entering, and the Five Turbidities

This part continues Zhanran's notes on the Textual Commentary's reading of the Skillful Means chapter, from its use of the Upadeśa on the Lotus to the questions about the five turbidities. Zhanran first follows the Upadeśa's three rounds of explanation, for the two vehicles, for bodhisattvas, and for ordinary beings, to argue that the Buddha's knowledge and vision is the same for all three kinds of persons and that bodhisattvas of the other teachings must also be merged. He then explains why the commentary reads the passage by the four ones rather than the ten wonders of the Profound Meaning, and works through the oneness of principle in terms of the four stages, the four wisdoms, the four gates, and contemplating the mind, harmonizing the borrowed terms of the distinct teaching with the perfect. He defends the commentary's reading of "no remaining vehicle, whether two or three" against Guangzhai and two other masters, cites the Necklace on the three kinds of three vehicles and on the nine periods, and explains the sections on the buddhas of the three periods. The last part comments on the five turbidities: their essence and characteristics, the measures of time in the Abhidharmakośa, the sixty-two views, and the fourfold distinctions showing when the turbidities obstruct the great and the lesser vehicles.

Ru-Yi Translation

From "The Fahua Lun says" on: it cites the Treatise to explain. The Treatise has three passages. The first is in terms of the three vehicles, meaning that the two vehicles, together with the bodhisattva vehicle, equally attain and equally possess [it]. Within this it first cites the Treatise, and next, from "As for the sequence the Lun speaks of" on, explains the Treatise's meaning. As for "sequence": it does not conflict with the sequence of the present explanation. According to the meaning of the Treatise's text, the first takes the unsurpassed as opening. The Treatise's text also says, "The Buddha's knowledge and vision: what the Tathāgata is able to realize, because he knows that meaning as it truly is." Since the sūtra's text says "to cause beings," if it were realized by the Buddha alone, why would it say "to cause beings"? Hence without the present explanation the meaning cannot be made to work.

Ru-Yi Translation

The second phrase shows that [all] equally possess the unsurpassed. The Treatise's text at first only says, "the two vehicles are equal with the Buddha in the dharma-body"; so where this explanation speaks of the three vehicles, it is not the bodhisattva alone; hence it combines the bodhisattva with the two vehicles in explaining, which means that the dharma-body of the three vehicles is the same as the Buddha's. The third phrase causes them to know the unsurpassed; this is awakening. The fourth phrase: because they realize the unsurpassed, it is called non-retrogression. Realizing is simply attaining, that is, entering the first abiding. The Treatise's intent is that the first three phrases together cause them to enter the stage of the first abiding. First, opening points to what is known and seen as the unsurpassed; next, [showing is that] this object is equally possessed; next, [awakening] causes them to awaken to what they equally possess; next, [entering] causes them to enter this object, hence it says non-retrogression. Since it first speaks of the two vehicles realizing together with the bodhisattvas, we know that the sūtra's intent lies first with the two vehicles; therefore the Treatise's text does not exclude those of fixed nature, all the more since the Treatise says, "those not yet ripe will then ripen." Since "will" is not "not," one cannot say [they] lack [it].

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, as for "in terms of the bodhisattva": this verifies that bodhisattvas and the two [vehicles] must [both] be merged; if they did not need merging, why establish a separate explanation, the same as for the two vehicles? In explaining the first phrase, the Treatise has no separate explanation. Now [the commentary] says "as before," [meaning] the same attainment as persons of the two vehicles. That the dull are the same as the two vehicles has good reason; hence we know that before [this sūtra] it was not the unsurpassed. In explaining showing it says, "for bodhisattvas who have doubts, it causes them to know . in accordance with reality"; hence we know that before this they harbored doubts and were not real. [To say that] doubt belongs to the shared [teaching] and merging to the distinct is, in principle, not right: if they were not merged, how would they know reality as it is? Moreover, knowing reality as it is, they must still practice. In explaining awakening it says, "for those who have not yet given rise to the resolve for bodhi, it causes them to give rise to the resolve." Had they not given rise to the resolve, they would not be called bodhisattvas; hence we know that before this, though they had the name bodhisattva, they had not given rise to the perfect resolve. In explaining entering it says, "for those who have already given rise to the resolve for bodhi, it causes them to enter the Dharma." Hence "those who have not yet" may point to the Tripiṭaka and shared [teachings], and the words "already given rise" mostly point to the distinct teaching: though the distinct teaching, knowing the middle, seems to have already given rise [to it], it does not enter the path of realization, so it must be caused to enter. The words "the Buddha's knowledge and vision" properly exclude the provisional. One should know that the bodhisattvas of the three teachings have doubts, have not given rise [to the resolve], and have not entered the true and permanent; that is why the Treatise's text plainly has bodhisattvas caused to open, show, awaken, and enter. Yet [some] stubbornly refuse to allow this, openly contradicting the Treatise's text.

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, in the explanation in terms of ordinary beings, the Treatise again does not explain the phrase on opening, and now [the commentary] again refers to the first passage as the same; hence we know that ordinary beings are the same as the three vehicles. The second phrase, though explained, is worded wholly the same as the second phrase in the first [explanation], that of the two vehicles. Only at the third phrase does it differ from the two previous explanations; hence we know that ordinary beings are the same as the persons of the three vehicles in having the dharma-body and buddha-nature. Hence in the last two phrases ordinary beings differ from the three vehicles, because non-Buddhists reckon permanence, and because all beings do not yet know bodhi. Since a separate round of explanation is made for ordinary beings, we know one may not explain it in terms of deep stages; hence in explaining the phrase on awakening it says "causes . to awaken," and in the phrase on entering, "causes them to enter . bodhi." One should know that ordinary beings neither know nor are aware that they have the unsurpassed Dharma, nor have they ever given rise to the resolve for bodhi, and yet all are said to be the same as the three vehicles. Hence we know that in the three rounds the persons differ but the meaning is the same; therefore in the first phrase all may be the same in meaning. Next, in explaining the bodhisattvas' second phrase it says, "causes them to know . in accordance with reality"; "in accordance with reality" is simply another name for the dharma-body, so this too differs in wording but is the same in meaning. The third phrase: set against the two vehicles' not knowing, it says "so that they may know it"; set against bodhisattvas who have already given rise to the resolve, it causes them to give rise to it again; set against ordinary beings, it causes them to differ from non-Buddhists, and also causes the non-Buddhists to awaken. Here the meaning differs but the intent is the same. In the fourth phrase: set against the two vehicles' retreating from the great, it says "non-retrogression"; set against bodhisattvas who have given rise [to the resolve] but not yet entered, it says "enter the Dharma"; set against ordinary beings, who have never given rise [to it], it says directly "the great path." One should know that it is only the one knowledge and vision of the Buddha, which causes these persons, different as they are, to open the same [thing]. Hence we know that by naming the three vehicles and ordinary beings it gathers the capacities exhaustively, and all are called "beings." Gathering the six realms [of ordinary beings] and the three vehicles, set against the real, is the explanation by the ten dharma-realms. If in terms of what they head for, which is only the one buddha-realm, it is the explanation by the buddha-realm. The three rounds, pervading in meaning, are the explanation in terms of stages. The buddha-realm set against the nine again forms separating and combining; moreover, the Treatise's explanation of the four phrases is the same in meaning as the present four explanations, by stages and the rest.

As for "The Lun explicates it phrase by phrase": in the Treatise's three explanations each phrase is explained separately; now in the first explanation [the commentary] separates [each] into four, so that it becomes three rounds each with four explanations. But it raises only the two vehicles as the example for the rest, which is also raising the difficult to imply the easy, since the two vehicles are the difficult case. In the present first explanation it first matches the Treatise's text. First, taking the realization of non-retrogression as the explanation by the four stages: this is the Treatise's fourth phrase; since it says non-retrogression, opening, showing, awakening, and entering are all non-retrogression in thought. Next, making clear what the Buddha realizes and attains as the explanation by the four wisdoms: this is the first phrase. The Treatise says, "apart from the wisdom of all things there is no further matter." Next, taking the meaning of sameness: this is the second phrase. The Treatise says, "the two vehicles are equal in the dharma-body, in no respect different." Without contemplating the mind, how would one know the sameness? Next, taking the place of not knowing the ultimate: this is the third phrase. The place is what is penetrated; the two vehicles do not know it, and now it is to cause them to know it. Knowing is the gate, and the gate is what penetrates; hence it makes the explanation by the four gates. Since it is so for the two vehicles, bodhisattvas and ordinary beings are all likewise; thus all three kinds require the perfect teaching.

Ru-Yi Translation

The gate of the teaching serves as what penetrates; only by entering contemplation and cultivating wisdom can one reach the stage and know and see the Buddha's sphere. If another explanation were made, the words "to cause" would be set out in vain, and where would the Buddha's knowledge and vision be? Hence our school's explanation of meaning relies on the sūtra and accords with the Treatise, fits practice and attains principle. Is not the intent of the Buddha's appearance in the world just so? In explaining the Buddha's intent now, how could one go against it? If one stretches the stages deep, ordinary beings have no hope. If opening is made clear only after the two obstructions are removed, then showing and the other two would all be on the buddha-ground; this teaching would not even be the share of those before the grounds, so what benefit would it be to ordinary beings and the lesser? As for "and so on" (云云): one should explain in detail the meaning of the horizontal and vertical explanations, in order to set out the horizontal and vertical use of the Treatise's text. For in the Treatise's three explanations each is explained vertically, while now within each explanation each phrase is horizontal and vertical, so that the Treatise's four phrases also become horizontal and vertical. Though there are horizontal and vertical, the intent lies in nonduality.

Now these four ones, according to the digesting of the text below, all face two ways; each follows one side, and left and right include each other, in order to distinguish the four ones. Hence, set against each other, the four each still contain four, let alone the two ways. Why? If what is known is principle, then what knows belongs to wisdom, those who receive are persons, and what expresses is teaching. The present text contains both, so for the time being it follows principle and calls it the oneness of principle. Now, in revealing this principle, principle is not revealed alone: through the gate of wisdom it contemplates the three, and only by passing together through the stages traversed is principle revealed. This is in turn taking the three, persons, teaching, and practice, through the stages to take principle, the persons who contemplate the mind. The four gates are teaching; the four wisdoms are practice. The three depend on one another to break delusion, enter the stages, and climb to the ultimate principle. If so, the latter three, completing one another, should also be so. Therefore, here first, it raises the truth that is relied on, which cannot reveal itself; wisdom is what uses, and through it principle is seen, so it says "it is only in terms of the eye of wisdom that it can be known and seen." Ru-Yi Translation

The meaning in the answer fully uses the intent of the fourth phrase above. First, heavy obstruction. Next, from "on hearing" on, dull faculties. The Buddha had not yet preached; the old physicians spoke of the names permanence, self, and so on; according with their reckoning, the names impermanence and so on can treat the turbidities; hence [the turbidities] obstruct only the great and not the lesser. Like one kind of capacity: because the five turbidities are not yet removed, they cannot bear to hear the great; on reaching the Deer Park the lesser treats the turbidities, and afterward they hear the great. That is why, if views are not removed, they cannot bear to hear of permanence. Reckoning the aggregates as virtues is what the four foundations of mindfulness treat. 騃 also means delusion. Hence it censures the old physicians for using only false permanence as not knowing milk, that is, not recognizing the medicine. "Does not know the root cause from which the illness arose" and so on is not recognizing the illness. "Does not know when a remedy should be given or withheld" and so on is not knowing the method of giving medicine. Not understanding these three, they lack the visiting physician's art. The two kinds of permanence differ like good and bad milk; the heaviness and lightness of the delusions of views is called the root source; first cutting off and afterward using, hence it is called giving and withholding. 餌 means food. Because the times of eating differ, they reckon only the names of permanence and bliss, hence it says "does not know good milk from bad." Preaching self increases false habits, hence it says "does not know the root cause." Not understanding that the times of preaching permanence differ, hence it says "does not know when a remedy should be given or withheld." Hence the sūtra says, "If the Buddha, the World-Honored One, had first preached permanence, those receiving the teaching would say that this Dharma is the same as that of those non-Buddhists"; hence it says "if I were to praise" and so on. The two physicians are fully [explained] in my notes on fascicle three of Calming and Contemplation.

Translator's notes

Quoted phrases. This part comments on the site's fascicle-004 of the Textual Commentary, from its citation of the Upadeśa ("The Fahua Lun says") to the question why the five turbidities obstruct the Great Vehicle but not the lesser. The site's wording is kept inside quotations, including "one-in-principle" and "reality-mark"; 住 is "abiding" throughout ("the first abiding," "the ten abidings"), and the four ones are "the oneness of principle, of the teaching, of practice, and of persons."

The Upadeśa. The sentence "The Buddha's knowledge and vision: what the Tathāgata is able to realize, because he knows that meaning as it truly is" (unit 2) is in the Upadeśa on the Lotus (T1520). "Those not yet ripe will then ripen" was not found in this wording; T1520 has a related phrase, "to cause the unripe to ripen." In unit 2, 與令解釋次第不違 is read with 今 ("the present explanation") for 令.

The ten wonders. Units 5–11 compare the four ones with the ten "ones" of the Profound Meaning (principle, wisdom, practice, stage, the three dharmas, appeal and response, retinue, spiritual powers, preaching, and benefit). Zhanran defends the sūtra's order of the last four.

Obscure wording. Unit 13 (念體即是，是體非理，是事非是) is rendered literally; the sense seems to be that the two delusions share one essence with the principle they obstruct. In unit 18, 四引證除位 is read as "citing proof for the stage [where obstruction is] removed." In unit 24, 以歸理二 is read as "return them to the oneness of principle" (理一). In unit 27, 「欲以已」下 is taken to mean the sūtra's "they wish to show beings the buddha's knowledge and vision."

Insight and wisdom. In unit 24 Zhanran distinguishes 慧 ("insight," the cause) from 智 ("wisdom," the fruit) in the names of the four wisdoms; the site renders both "wisdom."

The Necklace. The passages on the three kinds of three vehicles and on the Inexhaustible Gate (unit 26) are in the Pusa Yingluo Jing (T656), fascicle ten in the Taishō text, in the chapter on the three paths and three vehicles, where the question is put by Śāriputra; Zhanran cites them as fascicles nine and eight. The god of the Pure Abodes and the nine periods (unit 27) are in fascicle thirteen (chapter on the Pure Abode god), so the Textual Commentary's "thirteen" agrees with the Taishō text, while Zhanran's "eleven" may reflect an older division.

Guangzhai, Jiaxiang. Guangzhai is Fayun; Jiaxiang is Jizang. The Great Sūtra's "distinct five flavors" refers to the five flavors as the stages of the bodhisattva in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra.

The Abhidharmakośa. The verses on the minimum of form and of time (unit 34) are in the Abhidharmakośa (T1558); the passage on the minimum of name is a paraphrase. "Window-dust" is the mote seen in a sunbeam.

The Karuṇāpuṇḍarīka. The Karuṇāpuṇḍarīka (T157) speaks of the age when "the human lifespan is twenty thousand years" as the start of the evil world of the five turbidities (unit 35).

Characters. In unit 36 Zhanran corrects the characters of the Textual Commentary's text (虺蚖, 撓, 囂/嚚); the site's Chinese already reads 嚚.

The ten powers. The passage on the fourth power (unit 54) is in the Great Treatise (T1509), fascicle twenty-four; the quotation is a paraphrase.

The old physician. Units 55–56 refer to the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's parable of the old physician who gives milk as a cure for everything and the visiting physician who knows when to give and withhold it. The sentence "If the Buddha, the World-Honored One, had first preached permanence, those receiving the teaching would say that this Dharma is the same as that of those non-Buddhists" is in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T374), fascicle four.

"Considering this closely." In unit 54 Zhanran suspects that something has dropped out of the Textual Commentary's text at "considering this closely, it may be understood."

The moon-love samādhi. The "simile of the moon-love [samādhi]" (unit 22) refers to the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's account of the samādhi the Buddha entered for King Ajātaśatru.

The closing title. The Taishō text closes this fascicle with 法華文句記卷第五（上）.
