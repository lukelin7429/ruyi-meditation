The Burning House, the Ten Objections, and the Essence of the Cart

This part follows the Textual Commentary through the prose of the parable of the burning house in the Parable chapter, from the children who "neither perceive nor know" the fire to the elder's equal bestowal of the great cart. Zhanran first reads the four phrases on not perceiving and not knowing in terms of the four truths, by ordinary beings, by stages, and by the three times, and explains why suffering felt in the five sense-consciousnesses breeds no weariness. He then works through the gates of persuasion and admonition, the flower-basket, bench, and table, and the "one gate, narrow and small." The longest section takes up an older claim that only the two lesser vehicles ask for the carts while bodhisattvas do not. Zhanran sets out its ten objections, refutes each, and replaces them with four phrases on cutting off affliction and asking, opened into sixteen and traced through the five flavors, arguing that every vehicle must ask and that capacity truly moves only in the perfect teaching. The part closes with the number and essence of the carts, the faults of Guangzhai's and Zhuangyan's views, the ten wisdoms of the lesser cart, and a new account of "asking in sentiment."

Not Perceiving and Not Knowing: The Four Truths

Chinese Source

「不覺不知」下約於四諦釋此四句，但初舉文應具列四句，但是文略，於中為三：初約凡釋，次約位釋，三約三世。初文又三：謂標、釋、結。初文者，「都不言」下標苦也。「火」者，三界五濁八苦也。「不解火」下標集也，火本能燒如集招苦。「既不知」下標失道也，既不畏傷故云不驚，傷即失也。「不慮斷」下標失滅也，無道諦身慧命亦斷。「眾生」下釋前四句，陰必是苦釋前有苦，既全不覺即不識苦。「不知」去釋前集也，「四倒三毒」釋前有集，既全不知故不識集。三毒修惑，四倒見惑。「既不」下釋失道滅，由不知苦集，故道滅俱失。「不識惑」者，不識集也，既不識集不憂慮苦。「惑侵法身」失道諦也，必損慧命失滅諦也。「如是」下結。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "neither perceiving nor knowing" on, it explains these four phrases in terms of the four truths. In first citing the text it ought to have listed all four phrases; the text is simply abbreviated. Within it there are three [parts]: first, the explanation in terms of ordinary beings; next, the explanation in terms of stages; third, [the explanation] in terms of the three times. The first text again has three: the statement, the explanation, and the conclusion. As for the first text: from "not so much as speaking of there being a fire" on, it states suffering. "Fire" is the eight sufferings of the five turbidities in the three realms. From "not understanding that fire" on, it states origination: fire by its nature burns, as origination brings on suffering. From "Since they do not know" on, it states the loss of the path: since they do not fear harm, it says "not startled"; harm is loss. From "give no thought to their lives being cut short" on, it states the loss of cessation: without the body of the truth of the path, the life of wisdom too is cut off. From "Sentient beings" on, it explains the preceding four phrases. The aggregates are necessarily suffering: this explains the preceding "suffering"; since they entirely fail to perceive, they do not recognize suffering. From "do not know" on, it explains the preceding origination: "the four inversions and the three poisons" explains the preceding "origination"; since they entirely fail to know, they do not recognize origination. The three poisons are afflictions of cultivation; the four inversions are afflictions of view. From "not even recognizing" on, it explains the loss of the path and of cessation: because they do not know suffering and origination, path and cessation are both lost. As for "not even recognizing delusion itself": this is not recognizing origination; since they do not recognize origination, they are not troubled about suffering. "Delusion invades the dharma-body": this is the loss of the truth of the path; it necessarily injures the life of wisdom: this is the loss of the truth of cessation. From "in just this way" on is the conclusion.

Ru-Yi Translation

From "Because they have not heard" on is next the explanation by way of stages. Within it, first the first two phrases are explained in direct order, twice over; next the last two phrases are explained in reverse. In the first text, first the two phrases are matched to hearing and reflection: it ought to say that not perceiving [concerns] hearing and reflection and not knowing [concerns] the wisdom of cultivation; the text is simply abbreviated. Next, the two phrases are carried forward to the stages of the sages. Next, the reverse explanation: it ought to say, "because there is no seeing of the truths, they are not startled; because there is no contemplation, they are not afraid"; the text explains in reverse, hence it says, "Seeing the truths brings startled awakening," and so on. This makes clear that the contemplation of the four truths is accomplished and the two afflictions of view and thought are ultimately exhausted. Because the afflictions are exhausted, the four truths are fully manifest. On first attaining the seeing of the truths, delusion and awakening are first divided, as one long lost who first finds the right [way]; hence it says "startled awakening." Then one further wearies of and fears the road of delusion until it is exhausted. Next, in terms of the three times: as for its not mentioning the past or origination: in the present one contemplates future suffering as due to present origination, and again contemplates present suffering as due to past origination, as in [the twelve] causes and conditions, which is called the cycle of rebirth. Though it discusses the three times, it properly contemplates present suffering in order to cut off present origination, so that the suffering to come will cease. Hence we know that not perceiving present suffering is to be deluded about both suffering and origination; by this one can bring on the suffering of the future world; and since one is deluded about suffering and origination, in principle there is no path or cessation. It cites the suffering of two times to include the three times, fully as in the quoted text; and it includes origination simply by means of suffering. Yet although the three preceding explanations are three, in terms of ordinary beings, of stages, and so on, taken all together they are all delusion about the truths; because of delusion about the truths, the eight sufferings press upon the body.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the five sense-consciousnesses" and so on: "pressing" (逼) means "near." The turbidity lying in the five consciousnesses is called "near"; because it touches the self closely (切己), it is called "pressing upon the body." The simultaneous mental consciousness receives the painful object together [with them], not at the first instant before there is discrimination; moreover, these five consciousnesses are in their essence the ripened [retribution], the eight sufferings. What could be nearer than this? Hence how could this simultaneous [consciousness] weary of it? Hence this sovereign mind and its mental associates cannot use this mental consciousness to complete contemplation; they can only discriminate so as to form the three sensations. The meaning of the three sensations being formed, it says "touching the self closely"; the pressing being severe, it is "close." Hence each suffering arises from the five consciousnesses facing their objects; then it reaches the sixth and is discriminated again, further setting up the cause of suffering. How could weariness arise?

Ru-Yi Translation

From "It is also said" on, it brings out another explanation: formerly having formed [a bond with] the Mahāyāna is called the seed; the merit is the wisdom belonging to the dharma-body, and this wisdom is taken as the essence. Pressed by suffering, the great principle, wisdom, and vehicle all meet with suffering and origination; not perceiving and not knowing is called being pressed by the fire. From "Here, then," on, it speaks only in terms of the names of the turbidities, with no need to match them to the great: since, once they have flowed on [in birth and death], they do not know even the lesser, it gathers all the meanings together to form the five turbidities. As for the four, "neither perceiving nor knowing, neither startled nor afraid," being the turbidity of sentient beings: the four phrases are simply delusion about the four truths; because of delusion about the truths, sentient beings are themselves turbid. Next, "the fire coming to press upon the body" and so on as the turbidity of lifespan: suffering flourishing and life shortened make life turbid. "The mind's feeling no weariness or distress" and so on as the turbidity of the eon: because they do not weary of suffering, they are always in the three realms and are bound to meet the three lesser [calamities]; this is called the turbidity of the eon. As for "and so on" (云云): the intent is to reject the old master; there is no need to match this to the great. Because of the five turbidities, this properly amplifies the fire seen above; hence above it says only "arising on every side."

Ru-Yi Translation

From "the elder had this thought" on, it first explains the body and hands that bestow, and next the flower-basket [and so on] that are bestowed; since both the bestower and the bestowed are set aside, it says "not attained." The first text is in ten: first, the statement; second, citing the matching correlation; third, from "by the virtue of severance through samādhi" on, making clear what the two meanings rely on; fourth, from "the power of wisdom and severance" on, making clear the function of the two meanings; fifth, from "This wisdom and severance" on, making clear the gates of the two meanings; sixth, from "persuasion is" on, explaining by the four siddhāntas; seventh, from "When the Tathāgata" on, making clear the intent of using the two meanings; eighth, from "hence it is to be understood" on, the concluding return; ninth, from "the earlier text" on, citing proof; tenth, from "The earlier praise of the elder" on, pointing again to the earlier proof. The first four can be understood. Fifth, as for "This wisdom and severance" and so on: cause and fruit are paired: persuasion is the gate belonging to wisdom, and admonition is the gate belonging to severance; hence it says they enter by the two gates. That within the gate of persuasion it again speaks of admonition is because the essence of the gates in principle contains each other; since each is convenient for one meaning, they are for now spoken of separately. Sixth, in the four siddhāntas it says, "these two siddhāntas serve as the skillful means for the siddhānta of ultimate meaning": the two siddhāntas are the cultivated wisdom and severance; the ultimate meaning is the dharma-body of inherent virtue. At the beginning of the Skillful Means chapter, taking hold of beings by the dharma-body, wisdom, and severance did not succeed; therefore he ceased transforming. Question: why does it not speak of the mundane siddhānta? [Answer:] The two gates each differ, differing according to what [beings] delight in; also, hearing the two and rejoicing are both the mundane [siddhānta]. Hence this buddha-vehicle produces good and destroys evil, producing the eight gates everywhere: the four gates of the perfect and the distinct, each with two, [the two] each with four. Hence these two gates gather all dharmas. The rest is as in the text.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, in the explanation proper, from "Taking [also]" (亦取) on, the matching correlation: the Buddha himself explains the meaning; how could it be the same as worldly sentiment? Within it there are five: first, citing the text below; next, from "spiritual power" on, the explanation proper, the body and hands pointing to the earlier [explanation], and explaining the flower-basket and so on; third, from "The Tathāgata" on, making clear the intent of using the three; fourth, from "The flower-basket" on, making clear the intent of establishing the names. The first three can be understood. As for "its meaning is compressed and profound" (名略義玄): knowledge and vision being two, the name is brief; gathering all dharmas, the meaning is profound; as the flower-basket having one foot is brief, and holding much is profound. "The four fearlessnesses" are briefly as in the Sequential Gates of the Dharma-Realm and my notes on the seventh fascicle of Calming and Contemplation; the Great Treatise explains them broadly. Speaking generally of fearlessness: the ten powers being full within, the outer function has no timidity; this is called fearlessness. The Buddha himself says truly: "I am one who knows all and sees all. There is no śramaṇa or brāhmaṇa, no god, Māra, or Brahmā, nor any other assembly, [that can rightly] say the Tathāgata does not know, down to there being not even the slightest mark of fear." As for "matching the four noble truths": [the fearlessness of teaching] the path that exhausts suffering is the truth of suffering; teaching the obstructions to the path is the truth of origination; omniscience is the truth of the path; the exhaustion of outflows is the truth of cessation. The four kinds of four truths are the four sets of four fearlessnesses. Here it must be in the perfect: pervading all, the four apart from the two make four; the two both rely on the truths, the truths being what is relied on; hence it is called "settled" (安). Next, among the ten powers, speaking generally of power: the inner real wisdom and function attained by the buddhas is fully clear, unsurpassable and indestructible; hence it is called power. Fearlessness and the powers, distinguished as inner and outer, each pervasively gathering [all], are called "crosswise"; the ten powers, relying on principle, are called "lengthwise." Then, the ten bases making the truths clear, how is it not greatly settled? Though fearlessness and the powers are inner and outer, here both are in terms of function, hence inner and outer are complete. Fifth, from "the three sets of seven days" on, it concludes the intent of bestowing transformation. As for "and yet sentient beings were unable to bear it": this properly bestows the great; the words "unable to bear" are said by taking the intent.

The One Gate, Narrow and Small

Chinese Source

「唯有一門」至「狹小」者，於中二，先指上類同，次分門解釋。初言「義如上說」者，如上車門，此中亦是宣一佛乘之車門也。若已出宅門未入車門者，取難當故名為狹小。初言「別者」，別約三義分字釋也。於中先正釋，「教理」下釋所以。初正釋者，一既是理、門又屬教，小即無機，以無行故不入理教，故名狹小。言「不容斷常七方便」者，應云斷常及七方便，於實不入，義言不容。言「眾生不能以此理教自通」者，但守方便行也。言通釋者，理教行三，一一通明一門狹小，以不入故不得至果。初約理者，唯一法界故云「無雜」，由一理故遍通一切，令至此理故云「能通」，由子不入故云「難知」。約教者，唯約圓理對教，以明出入秖是理教故耳。云「凡夫不知出」者，則不及二乘已從宅門小教而出。「不知入」者，不知車門，兩教二乘但從門出，故云「少知」。昔教未詮，「永不知入」。「菩薩雖自知出」等者，通菩薩也。三藏菩薩雖有出教，三祇百劫並未出故。又通教菩薩約鈍根者，未知入處與二乘同，故云「亦」也。「奪七方便」者，前與言之猶云知出，猶未入實名不知入，若奪說者出亦不知。何者？而不自知上中下性故不知出。與而言之，通別衍門猶可知入，然帶教道故奪說者，別教地前皆不知故，尚不自知上中下性，況一地雨？從不知權邊云皆不知，故別教地前雖知中道，教道仍權，據教道論乃至入地，入地必證故廢不論。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for from "there is only the one gate" to "narrow and small": within it there are two: first, pointing to the above as the same in kind; next, explaining in divisions. First, as for "the sense of 'gate' has been explained above": as with the cart-gate above, here too it is the cart-gate that proclaims the one buddha-vehicle. Those who have already come out by the gate of the house but have not entered the cart-gate find it hard to take on, hence it is called narrow and small. First, as for "Taken specifically": specifically it explains the characters separately by three meanings. Within it, first the explanation proper; from "The teaching and its principle" on, explaining the reason. First, the explanation proper: since "one" is principle and "gate" belongs to teaching, "small" means lacking capacity; having no practice, they do not enter principle and teaching, hence it is called narrow and small. As for "it cannot accommodate the seven expedient stages that hold to eternalism or annihilationism": it ought to say "annihilationism and eternalism, and the seven expedient stages"; they do not enter the real, and the meaning is expressed as "cannot accommodate." As for "sentient beings are unable to make their own way through by means of this principle and this teaching": they only keep to the practice of expedients. As for "Taken generally": principle, teaching, and practice each generally make clear that the one gate is narrow and small; because they do not enter, they cannot reach the fruit. First, in terms of principle: there being only the one dharma-realm, it says "without admixture"; because of the one principle it conveys through to all, causing them to reach this principle, hence it says "able to convey through"; because the children do not enter, it says "hard to know." In terms of teaching: only the perfect principle is matched with the teaching, to make clear that going out and entering are only principle and teaching. As for "ordinary people do not know its point of departure": they do not come up to the two vehicles, who have already gone out by the house-gate, the lesser teaching. "Nor do they know its point of entry": they do not know the cart-gate; the two vehicles of the two teachings only go out by the gate, hence it says "but a little" (少知). The former teaching had not yet expressed it, [hence] "never know the point of entry." As for "bodhisattvas, though they know for themselves the point of departure," and so on: these are the bodhisattvas of the shared [teaching]. For the bodhisattvas of the Tripiṭaka [teaching], though they have the teaching of going out, through three asaṃkhyeyas and a hundred eons they have not yet gone out. Again, the dull-faculty bodhisattvas of the shared teaching do not yet know the point of entry, the same as the two vehicles; hence it says "likewise." Notes on the Textual Commentary on the Lotus, Fascicle Six (Middle)

Reading Notes for Part 17

Translator's notes

Quoted phrases. This part comments on the prose of the burning-house parable in the site's fascicle-005 of the Textual Commentary, from "neither perceiving nor knowing" to the "two chapter-headings, two extensive expositions, and two explanations" of the equal bestowal. The site's wording is kept inside quotations; where Zhanran reads a phrase differently, the character is given and the difference is noted here.

"Pressing close." In units 2 and 13 Zhanran reads 切己, "touching the self closely"; the site's text has 切已, "already pressing close." The quotation keeps the site's words.

Name brief, meaning profound. Zhanran takes 名略義玄 as two clauses: the name ("knowledge and vision") is brief, the meaning profound, and he takes the flower-basket's 一足 as "one foot." The site reads "called brief because its meaning is compressed and profound" and "a single vessel."

The four fearlessnesses. The Buddha's declaration in unit 6 follows the formula found in the Great Treatise (T1509, fascicle twenty-five) and the Madhyamāgama. The clause 遍一切四離二為四 is rendered literally; its exact sense is unclear.

Annihilation, eternalism, and the seven expedients. Zhanran says 斷常七方便 should be read as "annihilationism and eternalism, and the seven expedient stages"; the site merges them into "the seven expedient stages that hold to eternalism or annihilationism." He also reads 奪七方便 as "speaking by stripping, [none of] the seven expedients," against the site's "stripped of the seven expedient stages."

Śāriputra's retreat. The site renders 六心中退 as "Śāriputra's six mental states of retrogression"; Zhanran, following the Necklace (T1485), reads it as a retreat at the sixth mind of the ten abidings. "At the resting place he taught two" alludes to the Phantom City chapter, where two nirvāṇas are taught for the sake of rest.

Worthies and sages. The six phrases follow the Chapter on the Worthies and Sages of the Abhidharmakośa (T1558). The thirty-two aspects are the sixteen aspects of the four truths in the desire realm and in the higher realms.

The ten objections. The objections and refutations are quoted from the Textual Commentary; Zhanran's names for the masters are kept. 詭累 is glossed by Zhanran as "deceptive" and "piled up," where the site's text, reading 觀其詭累, has "entangles and confines itself."

The initial resolve. "The two, the initial resolve [and the ultimate], are not separate" (unit 25) alludes to the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's verse 發心畢竟二不別 (T374, fascicle thirty-eight).

The Zheng Fahua. Dharmarakṣa's translation (T263) speaks of elephant-, horse-, and goat-carts and finally of a great white-elephant cart.

The ten wisdoms. Unit 50 follows the Abhidharmakośa's scheme of dharma-knowledge, subsequent knowledge, conventional knowledge, and knowledge of others' minds; "the world has six arhats" refers to the Buddha being counted with the five monks after the first sermon.

The Mahāyānasaṃgraha. The site's lemma calls it the She Dacheng; that the heavenly eye of a lower stage does not see a higher one is general Abhidharma doctrine.

Unit 51. 此依未次 is rendered "this does not follow the sequence"; 未 may be a copying error for 文 ("this follows the order of the text").

The closing title. The Taishō text closes this fascicle with 法華文句記卷第六（中）.
