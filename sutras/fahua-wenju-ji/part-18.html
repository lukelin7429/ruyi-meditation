The Great Cart, the Burning House in Verse, and the Slanderers of the Sūtra

This part finishes Zhanran's notes on the prose of the burning-house parable and follows the Textual Commentary through most of its verses. He begins with the equal bestowal of the great cart: the children are equal because the buddha-nature is equal, and the cart is equal because every practice, opened, is the wondrous vehicle. He explains each adornment of the cart (the fourfold eloquence, the four immeasurables, the cushions, the crimson pillow, the white ox) and defends the long list against the idea that one phrase would suffice, since the list is the method of contemplation itself. He then treats the treasuries of practice and principle, the equality of the children argued even from those "who are not his children," freedom from falsehood, and the three lands as "every world." In the verses he glosses the birds, beasts, and spirits of the house as the dull and sharp hindrances, compares the eight kinds of arrogance with the seven prides of the Abhidharmakośa, and traces the fire through the desire, form, and formless realms. The part ends with the four truths taught to those of lesser wisdom and with the slanderer who cuts off the seed of buddhahood.

Ru-Yi Translation

From "first, the equality of the children" on, it states the two chapter-headings. As for "Because the children are equal, the mind toward them is equal": first it makes clear the equality of the children. Because none are not children, the mind is necessarily equal; if the mind is equal, the children are necessarily equal. The mind is the mind-nature, hence the buddha-nature is equal; because all are children, the mind has no partiality; moreover wealth and Dharma are abundant; therefore the mind is equal. As for "the equality of the cart": first it makes clear the intent of equality: what is given is not two, therefore it says equal. It only points out that what they practiced is none other than the wondrous vehicle; simply because the nature is the same, the meaning of giving is equal. From "And as for the phrase" on, it uses the equality of the children to resolve a doubt about the equality of the cart: since it says the carts are equal, why does it give "to each"? Former practices differ and the stages are not one; arriving here, what is taught is none other than the one vehicle. Why? If a single person is not covered, it is not called the equality of the children. Moreover it says that the original practice should cover all dharmas; if a single thing is not given, it is not called equal giving: namely form and mind, adverse and favorable, dependent and principal, practice and principle, cause and fruit, self and other, understanding and delusion, lesser and great, wisdom and merit. Hence we know the equal giving is only opening the three vehicles and the six destinies, none of which is not the one suchness; hence each suchness pervasively gathers, pervasively contains, and pervasively enters all. Which sentient being lacks a share of the site of awakening in the four directions? Who does not in principle have the great cart's accompanying particulars? It waits for the time and waits for conditions; that is why. Hence only today does it say "gave to each." As for "spanning crosswise" and so on: the dharma-realm and the three truths are neither crosswise nor lengthwise. Though without crosswise and lengthwise, the dharma-realm, from its pervasiveness, is conveniently called crosswise; the three truths, differing in name, are conveniently called lengthwise; being nondual, they reveal each other; reflect on it and it can be seen.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the fourfold eloquence": namely of dharma, meaning, phrasing, and teaching; it is much the same as the seven eloquences. "Dharma" means discriminating the names of all dharmas without obstruction, discriminating the three vehicles without breaking the dharma-nature. "Meaning" means clearly penetrating the meaning of all dharmas, knowing that all meanings enter the true mark. "Phrasing" is speech and names adorned, able to make [beings] understand according to what is fitting: all sentient beings of different regions and kinds, many or few, broad or narrow, of the various destinies, men and women, the three ages and nine ages, the various teachings and gates: those who hear all understand. "Delight in teaching" means being able in one letter to teach all letters, in one word to teach all words, in one dharma to teach all dharmas; whatever is taught is none other than true, and the twelve [divisions] and the eighty-four thousand are taught according to what the faculties delight in. As for "the four immeasurable states of mind": the four truths are the object, because two are suffering and two are bliss. Originally it is the inconceivable pure practice; hence the Great Sūtra says, "Loving-kindness that is existent, nonexistent, neither existent nor nonexistent: such loving-kindness is called the Tathāgata's loving-kindness." Hence the loving-kindness of the three truths gathers the four pervasively. The four vows and the four means of attraction are both judged by their object; only so do they differ from other sūtras. "The four great vows" are fully as in Calming and Contemplation, fascicles one and five. These four vows are the four immeasurables above. "The four means of attraction" are briefly as in my notes on the seventh fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. The spiritual powers and the seven factors of awakening both take what is without object (無緣) as what they rely on, as made clear in fascicle seven of Calming and Contemplation, in the truth of the path without fabrication.

Ru-Yi Translation

"綩" is embroidered cloth. "綖" is the cloth covering the emperor's crown, the part hanging in front and behind. That is not the intent here; here it should be written "筵," that is, a spread mat. "Cultivation, refinement, and permeation": as in the Sequential Gates of the Dharma-Realm, which sets out the names and marks fully. "The objects of calming-and-contemplation dhyāna" (止觀禪境): in the Profound Meaning, in [the section on] the noble practice of concentration, all are cultivated for the sake of the true mark; reaching the fruit, in dhyāna the superior function is inexhaustible. As for the crimson pillow's "support to steady it" (支昂): this is the pillow outside the cart; when the cart stops it must be propped, lest it tilt up, hence "prop against tilting." "支" means to support; "昂" means to rise. As for "corresponds to being in motion and yet still, being still and yet in motion" (譬動靜相即): when the cart moves the pillow is idle, and when the cart rests the pillow is in use; when in use it is always still, when idle it is always moving. The real essence and its function are likewise. From one's own cause to the fruit, the dharma-nature does not move; hence, like the wind, it does not shift and yet arrives in stillness; the myriad practices are without fabrication, and the various wisdoms cannot observe it; thus the three virtues are all nondual. That is because the three are identical with the one. As for "the pillow within the cart": wisdom as the head, practice as the body, the three pearls (三珠) as the pillow; what is rested on attains principle, the dharma-principle being so. As for "a red radiance" and so on: no other dharma intervening is called "beyond all discrimination"; light is a metaphor for wisdom, hence it is called the light of wisdom. Vermilion is a pure color and purple a mixed one; hence red represents light without admixture. Nanshan's annotated sūtra sound-glosses say: "In the West there are no wooden pillows; all are of red leather with cotton and wool inside, used for leaning and lying." It is red and also bright.

Ru-Yi Translation

The white ox is in three: first making clear its function; next, from "White is the source of every color" on, discussing its essential virtue; third, from "Again, the four foundations of mindfulness" on, discussing the aspects of practice. In the first text: the cultivated prajñā can guide the provisional and the middle; the truths and conditions of the three teachings all reach the ultimate, hence it says "all the way to." This name is general and must be distinguished by essence. Next, in the essential virtue it says, "White is the source of every color": the original essence is without stain, hence it says "fundamentally pure"; cultivation matches nature, hence it says "corresponds." As for "that its substance is furnished" and so on: it reveals the perfect wisdom. The essence of delusion is originally pure; discussing cultivation in terms of nature, it is called "not stained." This is fullness within and cleanness without. From "Again, the four foundations of mindfulness" on, it explains the aspects of practice in terms of the object: mindfulness is wisdom and foundation is object; the four contemplations contemplate the foundations, foundations and contemplation joined into one, as the whole white [ox] is the body. This contemplation of the object, good full and evil exhausted, can be likened to the right efforts. Because this is accomplished, it further makes desire, mindfulness, reflection, and one-mindedness form the indeterminate supernatural powers, transformations upon transformations unceasing, spontaneously always so, free in going and staying; hence it says "match the practitioner's own mind" (稱意). The remaining dharmas are explained by analogy, as with the factors of the path.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "Servants for its escort": matched by analogy with the three virtues, within the essence of the cart there should be a division into the correct and the conditioning [causes], with the ox matched to the revealing [cause]; then in meaning it corresponds to two of cultivation and one of nature, and the one also contains the three, high and broad, nondual. The "servants" here and the "escort" (儐從) of the verse differ in words and agree in meaning. "儐" means to advance, to guide. "Attendants" (侍衛) serve on the left and right. "僕" is the lowest rank. Let those who now practice the Mahāyāna measure their own minds: are they the same as or different from what this sūtra text lists? If a single phrase were enough, which phrase would it be? If a single phrase sufficed, why need all the phrases? Would the Buddha speak wrongly, forcibly indulging in elaborate words, tediously listing the appointments of a cart to dazzle and mislead Kāśyapa? This great arhat had long been the head of the saṅgha, and for more than forty years had not received the true transformation. Barely hearing the expedients, the general praise of the two wisdoms, and the brief opening and revealing, his clinging was stirred and doubt arose; his sentiment hesitated, and [Śāriputra] earnestly requested three times; he heard broadly the five buddhas' tenfold opening of the provisional; he also saw Śāriputra's comprehension with the three kinds of action, the eight classes [of beings] following the example, the fourfold assembly repaying the kindness, and the Tathāgata's clear confirmation and prediction. Through all these layers his past seed was still not opened; barely hearing of the great cart, he was then fit for the prediction. Hence we know that the method of contemplation of this vehicle is complete, though it is kept trimmed and brief, roughly pointing out the ten contemplations; if the aspects of practice were set out broadly, how could they be exhausted? Without separate explanation he awakened to non-arising: compared with those above he counts as middling, compared with those below he is still sharp. If a single phrase of this cart-metaphor were set up in vain, it would show the Buddha guilty of frivolous speech, or impute to the compilers the fault of adding and mixing, and blame the translators for the error of confusion. If the matching has its reason, then the manner of practice can be taken as a model. How could one who studies the great contemplation suddenly discard it all? Having lost the great rule, there is no small path to follow; with great and lesser both lost, one may fear falling to a false crossing. Riding a broken donkey-vehicle, where would one be heading? As for "corresponds to the perfect fullness of merit and wisdom at the stage of the fruit": practice and principle are exhausted; merit completes practice; wisdom heads for principle. The virtue of practicing for oneself being complete, it is full within; the virtue of transforming others being complete, it overflows without; pervading all dharmas, the wealth is immeasurable; all possessing the two virtues, it says "every kind."

The Treasuries of Practice and Principle; The Equality of the Children

Ru-Yi Translation

As for listing the two treasuries: because practice matches principle and principle relies on practice, gathering each other mutually, they can be given equally. From "every dharma" on, it cites proof of the two treasuries. If one does not understand the treasury of principle of the aggregates and sense-fields, one cultivates the treasury of practice of the perfections in vain devotion. As for "every dharma tends toward": the text is in the Chapter on Setting Out of the Great [Perfection of Wisdom]. "Tends" means to tend toward and enter: all dharmas enter into one dharma; as one dharma is so, all dharmas are likewise; practice and principle each gather all dharmas. The opening and combining of the text on all dharmas tending toward [one] are as cited in the second fascicle of Calming and Contemplation. From "Practicing this practice and this principle for oneself" on, it next explains "filled" and "overflowing": first directly in terms of practice and principle; next, from "real wisdom" on, what guides and what illuminates, which is the meaning of the cultivated three virtues. The fruit at its extreme benefits beings, hence it is called "filled to overflowing." Though there are two explanations, the Buddha's wisdom does not differ. In the extensive statement of the equality of the mind there are also two; it is only because it explains the children by the mind that it says "mind." The text has three meanings that together are called the equality of the mind: first, wealth; second, all are children; third, no partiality. Therefore the mind is equal. It should also further say that the reason the mind is equal is that all are children. Immeasurable wealth is the fulfillment of one's own practice; all being children without partiality is the ripening of the conditions for transformation. From "if he were wealthy" on, it explains equality conversely by inequality: because wealth or children is lacking, the mind is unequal. One should know that "not children" and "wealth unequal" are lodged in the former teachings. Hence in the former partial teachings the lord of the teaching and the capacities to be transformed lacked both meanings: as the lords of the two teachings were poor in the wealth of Dharma, they cannot be called rich; not knowing the eternally abiding, they cannot be "all children"; even if within their own teaching they are called children, their gathering of capacities is not pervasive, so they too are not children and there is partiality. Lacking one or the other is not equality; lacking both can be understood. In the distinct teaching, the path of teaching, though it says all are children, still lacks the two meanings; the path of realization is of itself the same, equal in its share without lack. The explanation of the equality of the children below can be understood by analogy. Therefore in the present sūtra, outside the one real there is no other dharma, and "all sentient beings are my children." The conditioning cause still gathers scattered goods; the revealing seed gathers even a single phrase; the correct [cause] does not abandon even the uninterrupted [hell]; only so does it match the word "all." Therefore the Profound Meaning, in making clear benefit, [covers] the prologue, the main section, and dissemination, and the future forever; King Wondrous Adornment, Devadatta, Never Disparaging, and the nāga girl can be understood by analogy of meaning. How much more, the words "those who should" do not exclude the four [evil] destinies, and the teaching according to what is fitting does not choose between the favorable and the adverse! This is the general sense of equal giving. Hence we know the words "the children are equal, the mind is equal": how could they concern only the four great śrāvakas?
