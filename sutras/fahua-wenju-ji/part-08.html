Mañjuśrī's Answer: Conjecture, Former Seeing, and Clear Judgment

This part is the middle third of the third fascicle of Zhanran's Notes on the Textual Commentary on the Lotus. It follows the site's Textual Commentary fascicle three to the end of the Introduction chapter. Zhanran first finishes the verses of Maitreya's question: the six perfections as seen in other worlds, which he sorts into the four teachings with long quotations from the Viśeṣacintā, the Necklace, and the Āgamas; the stūpas over the relics; and the four concealed objections that Maitreya overcomes. The larger portion explains Mañjuśrī's answer in its four steps: conjecture, the brief and the extended answers from things formerly seen, and the answer of clear judgment. Here Zhanran compares the five phrases "about to preach the great Dharma" and the rest with the eight phrases of the Lotus Treatise. He sets out the three samenesses of the past buddha Candrasūryapradīpa with the present Buddha, as already-past, present, and yet-to-come, and he rejects Master Yao's view that the "person serving as occasion" is the one entrusted with propagating the sūtra. Shorter notes explain the clan Bhāradvāja, the eight princes, the difference between opening the trace and revealing the origin, and the four closing verses. Phrases quoted from the Textual Commentary use the site's published English.

◎《經》云「被法服」者，如《瓔珞經》云：「若天龍八部鬪爭，念此袈裟生慈悲心」，意令比丘安可不忍，亦令俗眾生慕樂故，龍得一縷牛角一觸等（云云）。彼王所慕與此大同，此中祇合明所見意以序表正，諸度行相功德及袈裟等，但是寄此汎明之耳。然必須辨行體顯教以分味殊。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sūtra says "putting on the Dharma robes": as the Necklace Sūtra says, "If the eight classes of gods and dragons are fighting, by thinking of this kāṣāya they give rise to kindness and compassion." The intent is: how could a bhikṣu not be patient? It also causes lay beings to give rise to longing and delight; hence the dragon who obtained a single thread, the ox that touched it once with its horn, and so on (and so on). What those kings longed for is largely the same as this. Here it is only fitting to make clear the intent of what was seen, using the prologue to signify the main section; the features of the practice of the perfections, their merits, the kāṣāya, and so on are merely expounded broadly here in passing. Yet one must distinguish the essence of the practice and reveal the teaching, so as to separate the different flavors.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the patience of endurance with sentient beings" and so on: the text has two explanations: first, it uses three phrases generally to explain the three patiences; next, it matches each phrase separately to one of the three patiences. One should attach [them] to the text and explain the reason. If the three patiences are matched to the four teachings: patience toward beings and the patience of ascetic practice lie specifically in the first teaching; generally they are objects for all four — who does not need both? Specifically, they lie with those before the grounds who seek the buddha-path; though this is not here, it is spoken of by analogy. The next passage takes reciting the sūtra as the ultimate meaning: if one did not seek buddhahood, patience would have nothing to do with reciting; hence reciting the sūtra is the same as seeking the buddha-path. The patience of ultimate meaning generally lies also in the three; specifically, only in the perfect and the distinct; hence the three phrases differ among the teachings. As in the Separately Translated Āgama: the Buddha was at Śrāvastī, and a brahmin came to the Buddha and in all kinds of ways reviled the Buddha and vexed the Buddha. The Buddha said to the brahmin: "Suppose you offer all kinds of food and drink to the king and send them to your relatives; if they do not accept them, to whom do they belong?" The brahmin said: "They belong to me." The Buddha said: "This is likewise. Since I do not accept it, it returns to you." Hence this non-acceptance is also patience toward beings; and so this patience toward beings belongs specifically to the Tripiṭaka, because it is in the Āgama; it extends to the shared teaching, because in principle there is no accepting — let alone that it is used generally and shared by all the teachings. Since its name is general, its features must be explained. Next, within vigor, the true mark may also be general to four and specific to two. As for 窳 ["the cowardly and timid"]: like a melon in a hole, that is, sick.

Ru-Yi Translation

In dhyāna, "in the general course, both may include a cultivation grounded in the root dhyānas": because all the teachings cultivate them. If one penetrates the root, it becomes the world-transcending and the supreme world-transcending, fully as made clear in [the section on] the object of dhyāna in Calming and Contemplation. The present text is brief in wording and says only "the supreme, world-transcending" and "root": root is the Tripiṭaka, world-transcending is the shared teaching, supreme is the distinct and the perfect. Further, all four teachings take the root as their object; hence the explanation of the first line extends generally to the teachings. In the first line, "freedom from desire" is the shared teaching. Further, from "the root dhyānas" on, it is the Tripiṭaka and shared teachings, because both cultivate the root dhyānas, the liberations, and so on. Yet one should know that the contemplative practice still differs, so as to distinguish the two teachings. "Freedom from the desires of the two vehicles" in the distinct is spoken for the time being from the difficult side; "freedom from desire" in the middle way extends in meaning to the perfect and the distinct, and receives its name from the perfect. It explains "deeply cultivating" again because, owing to deep cultivation, freedom from desire differs; and within the root too it should deepen progressively. Here, from the root up to "the two vehicles possess all six," it also contains the intent of both the Tripiṭaka and shared teachings. The five powers of the distinct teaching are as the text [says]; [the sixth] may also be deferred to the first ground and above. "In the perfect teaching all six powers are possessed from beginning to end": only because, in terms of principle, it is perfect, undefiled, and without loss, are six powers set up in meaning; speaking in fact, at the first ground or the first abiding the undefiled power is partially attained. "Settled in dhyāna" and so on: because the earlier general explanation covered deep and shallow, now the supreme dhyāna lies specifically in the distinct and the perfect.

Ru-Yi Translation

In explaining prajñā for converting others, as for "complete in concentration and wisdom": a person of the distinct, benefiting beings, horizontally contains all the teachings up to the perfect teaching; here it speaks from the ultimate, comparing in turn, so that only at the grounds and abidings is it complete. Therefore in the text, beginning from the concentrations of form, it is also called "equal"; being equal, it is complete; if even the tainted are equal, how much more the rest? The liberations and the rest being named equal or unequal is judged in terms of the untainted phenomenal dhyānas: since they speak of disgust and turning away, they mostly belong to wisdom; the nine concentrations are called concentrations, judged from the name, because they are entered without interval. Of the ten totalities, the first eight belong to wisdom and the last two to concentration; further, the first eight lie within form and the form realm. They also attain equality of themselves, as said before; the earlier is causes and conditions. From "and again, the two vehicles" on is the classification by the teachings: the two vehicles are the first two teachings; with bodhisattvas and buddhas the meaning of the four teachings is complete. From "the contemplation of emptiness" on is the classification by contemplation. "Defeating the armies of Māra" and so on: the four buddhas each have features of subduing the four Māras, fully as in fascicle eight of Calming and Contemplation and its notes. If matched to the teachings, the empty also [covers] the two, the provisional the distinct, and the middle the perfect, for it contains both meanings, teaching and contemplation. Up to the grounds and abidings, each breaks the eight Māras and the ten Māras; the eight and ten Māras are fully as in fascicle eight of Calming and Contemplation and its notes. Breaking them completely in the ultimate, hence "all." "Beating the Dharma-drum of the wheel of Brahmā" and so on: it is what is turned by the true and wondrous Brahmā voice. Having subdued Māra, he should turn the Dharma-wheel; the text, speaking from the real, says only the first abiding; speaking generally, each of the four buddhas can turn it, and even "true and wondrous" extends to the four buddhas. Next, in terms of the non-sequential, as for "following whatever is seen": Question: what was made clear just now was what was seen; could it not be "following whatever is seen"? Answer: all are "following what is seen," but the two lines differ; from the side of the non-sequential it is most fully "following what is seen." Since one sight is admitted to pass through all five periods, what harm is there in seeing practices sequential and non-sequential?

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the Tripiṭaka teaching and its subsequent preaching" and so on: this is entirely what that buddha preached; just as one sees persons and sees practices, it does not prevent seeing the preaching. "The dhyāna of equanimity" is the fourth dhyāna. It may also be the equanimity of forgetting concern in the distinct and perfect; forgetting that dhyāna, it is called equanimity. "The dhyāna of compassion": the Vibhāṣā says: "In the first dhyāna compassion is easy to cultivate, in the second dhyāna joy is easy to cultivate, in the third dhyāna kindness is easy to cultivate, in the fourth dhyāna equanimity is easy to cultivate." Since the dhyāna of compassion here is said to be for converting others, how could it be only the first dhyāna? Even in the Vibhāṣā there are general and specific, let alone in the great teaching; hence each dhyāna should be said to include kindness up to joy and equanimity.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the Avataṃsaka and the Viśeṣacintā Sūtra" and so on: the Avataṃsaka contains two, the Viśeṣacintā contains four. Hence at the beginning of the second fascicle of the Viśeṣacintā, the bodhisattva Jālinīprabha emits light that illuminates everywhere asaṃkhyeyas of lands in the ten directions, and all afflictions and all illnesses, meeting the light, become peaceful and happy. Afflictions and the sufferings of illness are both said to be "all." Up to the Buddha himself emitting the light of the six perfections, and those touched by it receive benefit; hence all four are contained. Taking the first ground as analogous to the Buddha should be no problem, so it can be cited. The first fascicle says: "The Buddha said to the Brahmā Viśeṣacintā: To teach beings the mind of all-knowledge is called giving; not abandoning the bodhi-mind is called keeping the precepts; not seeing the arising and ceasing of the mind's features is called patience; seeking the mind and not finding it is called vigor; removing the coarseness of body and mind is called dhyāna; leaving all conceptual proliferation is called wisdom." Is this not the six perfections of the Tripiṭaka? The second fascicle says: "I say that giving is called nirvāṇa — the foolish call it great wealth — because it enters the true mark of all dharmas; keeping the precepts is nirvāṇa, because it neither makes nor arises; patience is nirvāṇa, because it ceases from moment to moment; vigor is nirvāṇa, because it grasps nothing; dhyāna is nirvāṇa, because it neither craves nor clings; wisdom is nirvāṇa, because it does not grasp features." It also says: "The equality of giving is sarvajña, . up to prajñā is sarvajña." It also says: "Giving neither gives nor is stingy, . up to prajñā, neither wise nor foolish." Are these not all features of the six perfections of the shared teaching? The fourth fascicle says: "Penetrating that all dharmas have nothing to be abandoned is called dāna; penetrating that all dharmas have nothing leaked or lost is called śīla; penetrating that all dharmas have nothing harmed is called patience; penetrating that all dharmas are equal is called dhyāna; penetrating that all dharmas have no feature of arising is called wisdom." Are these not features of the six perfections of the distinct and the perfect? The Avataṃsaka is fully as cited in the notes on fascicle seven of Calming and Contemplation. Further, as in the Bodhisattvabhūmi, each of the six perfections has nine [kinds]. These are all features of receiving benefit from the light. Because the name "buddha-path" is general and the dhyāna of compassion is not restricted, the first ground, the first abiding, and the seventh ground of the shared [teaching] can all enter it; hence a general explanation is made. Since the four features are distinguished, the five periods can be told apart; therefore other lengthy discussions are not needed.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Who have never slept" is fully as in fascicle four of Calming and Contemplation. But that is an expedient [preparation], while here it is the main cultivation. It also extends to the four teachings; in the Lesser Vehicle it is as with Aniruddha, fully as in the notes on fascicle four of Calming and Contemplation. Here it is within the Great, to seek the buddha-path, so it cites the Pratyutpanna as the method of practice. Pratyutpanna (般舟) is translated "the buddhas standing [before one]." This is cited as the foremost among [practices] for removing sleep, because of the ninety days of constant walking. According to the collection it is also general, because it extends to the teachings. "Lacking nothing" up to "ultimate completion": the names of these ten precepts come from the Great Treatise and also extend to the teachings, fully as in Calming and Contemplation and the Profound Meaning. Here the ten, from beginning to end, are all bodhisattvas, so all seek the buddha-path. "The two patiences with sentient beings and with the nature of dharmas": it combines the earlier two patiences as patience toward beings, and the ultimate meaning as patience toward dharmas. The first half [line] is the person who is patient, the next half the object endured, and the last half the intent of using patience; the intent includes both beings and dharmas. Hence we know that the name "patience toward beings" is general in name and specific in meaning: in the Tripiṭaka, the phenomenal patience of arising and ceasing is patience toward beings; in the gates of the Mahāyāna, patience toward beings and patience toward dharmas are forever different from the Tripiṭaka. "Power": the Āgama says: "There are six kinds of power: a small child's crying is its power, a woman's anger is her power, a king's arrogance is his power, an arhat's vigor is his power, the buddhas' compassion is their power, a bhikṣu's patience is his power." As for "free of frivolous play" and so on: the five hindrances are fully as in fascicle four of Calming and Contemplation (and so on). The first line and a half make clear what is left behind; the next half line makes clear the intent of leaving: since the dhyāna of all the teachings leaves the five hindrances, the intent lies in the buddha-path. As in the Ratnakūṭa Sūtra: "Kāśyapa said: There are four things to run from in haste and leave beyond a hundred yojanas: first, gain and support; second, bad friends; third, bad assemblies; fourth, living together with much frivolous laughter, or anger and quarreling, and so on." It also says: "If someone beats and maims the beings of a great trichiliocosm, and someone with an evil mind vexes a person who has aroused the bodhi-mind, the fault of the latter exceeds the former." The four verses from "I saw" on differ from the old explanation that takes them as a conclusion. They must versify the intent of the answer of judgment above: if they were made a concluding statement, they would be superfluous, and the text of the answer of judgment would be lacking; how could one not take them as versifying the answer of judgment? "Versifies the earlier 'about to preach the great-vehicle sūtra'": it should also say "named the Lotus of the Wondrous Dharma."
