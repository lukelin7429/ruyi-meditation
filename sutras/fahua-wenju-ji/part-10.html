The Ten Pairs of the Provisional and the Real and the Praise of the Two Wisdoms

This part continues the Textual Commentary's broad explanation of the Skillful Means chapter and then begins its reading of the sūtra text. Zhanran comments on the ten pairs of the provisional and the real (fact and principle, principle and teaching, teaching and practice, bondage and liberation, cause and effect, essence and function, gradual and sudden, opening and uniting, shared and distinct, and the siddhāntas), insisting that even the contaminated mind and evil conduct fall within what the Lotus calls skillful means. He compares Zhiyi's proof texts with the Upadeśa on the Lotus, explains how the ten are bound into the provisional and real of self, other, and self-and-other, and shows how the three explanations of the title run through the five flavors, the various sūtras, and origin and trace. Turning to the text, he explains the Buddha's rising from samādhi by the four siddhāntas, cites the Vibhāṣā on sitting cross-legged and the Abhidharmakośa on the faculties of the face, and follows the twofold praise, explanation, and conclusion of the two wisdoms, arguing against Guangzhai and the "old masters" on how the passage is divided. He warns that reading the Lotus by the Mahāyānasaṃgraha's four intentions would turn it into a teaching of meaning not fully drawn out. The part ends at the praise that cuts off speech.

The Ten Pairs Explained: Fact and Principle, Principle and Teaching

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the explanation proper. First, in explaining fact and principle, it first explains and next gives the reason. In the explanation it first explains principle, saying, from "principle is suchness (tathatā)" to "principle is named the real." Where is the real of principle? It is in mind, intellect, and consciousness; hence principle has no place where it is kept. Being present everywhere in fact, fact is named the provisional. Hence the Abhidharmakośa says, "What accumulates and gives rise is called mind; what deliberates is called intellect; what discerns is called consciousness." There it is entirely without identity with principle. In the Mahāyāna, the eighth consciousness is called mind, the seventh intellect, and the sixth consciousness; that teaching takes them as delusion and also lacks identity with principle; hence these are partial and lesser teachings. The contaminated dharmas are entirely without purity of the nature, and few know that they are the abiding principle. Hence one knows that the contaminated, though it takes purity and the like as its object, all belongs to fact, fully as made clear in the gate of the nonduality of fact and principle. Hence in [giving] the reason it says, "Without principle there would be no basis on which to establish fact," and "fact has the merit of manifesting principle," and so [the sūtra] praises skillful means. Who would be willing to take the contaminated mind and the rest of the three realms as what the Tathāgata praises in the Skillful Means chapter? If it were not so, where would the meaning of "to cause beings [to open the Buddha's knowledge and vision]" lie? How would one digest the words "the marks of the world [are constantly abiding]"?

Ru-Yi Translation

In explaining principle and teaching, it first explains properly, and next, from "Without teaching" on, makes clear the reason. In the first, the explanation of principle, it first explains briefly and next cites an example. First it says "taking the preceding principle and fact together": this joins the preceding principle and delusion. Hence one knows that ignorance and the dharma-nature, up to all dharmas outside the [three] realms, are all what is expressed, because the name and substance of this mind, intellect, and consciousness fully contain all dharmas outside the realms. Who knows that the teaching of the Lotus takes such dharmas as what it expresses? If it were not so, from where would King [Wondrous] Adornment of wrong views and Devadatta of evil rebellion have obtained [their predictions]? Next, citing an example: this cites understanding as an example for delusion. When principle is understood, the real and the conventional are both truths, and the understanding is gained from delusion; hence within delusion they are called for the time being fact and principle. By expressing this principle the teaching is formed; viewing the teaching from principle, the teaching is named the provisional; principle lies in delusion, and delusion too is named the real. Hence the names "provisional" and "real" are not obtained in only one place. The teaching of the fruit speaks of this: what expresses is also provisional. Hence one knows that the teaching only expresses its principle, and therefore the Tathāgata praises this teaching. If not the present sūtra, who would be willing to praise this teaching that expresses delusion as the Skillful Means chapter? If it were not so, what is praised upon rising from samādhi?

Bondage and Liberation, Cause and Effect, and the Remaining Pairs

Next, bondage and liberation: the name "practice" is still general and still includes going against and according; hence bondage and liberation are used to distinguish the provisional and the real, and it is named bondage and liberation. Again, with regard to the past in general: in the various sūtras, [stages] before the grounds still go against principle, because the provisional is not yet opened; in this sūtra, [even] a snap of the fingers is nothing but a cause of buddhahood, because the real is revealed. Who knows that in this sūtra the Buddha's evil conduct too may be called adept skillful means? The simile of the corpse runs universally through everything, fully as made clear in the gate of the nonduality of cultivation and nature.

Ru-Yi Translation

Cause and effect is in three: first, the explanation proper; next, from "Without effect" on, the reason; third, from "the two contemplations" on, the explanation that completes it. In the explanation that completes it, it says "the two contemplations serve as the expedient path": this shows the mark in terms of dharma for the time being and reveals it by borrowing the provisional as an example; in each layer it includes all the teachings in general; how could it be confined to the two contemplations of the distinct [teaching]? So too in essence and function, gradual and sudden, opening and uniting, and shared and distinct, there are the dharmas of essence and function and so on of all the teachings: giving rise to function from essence, opening the gradual from the sudden, uniting the sudden from the gradual, and so on. Next, essence and function: this again points to the initial abiding as the partial fruit, and this fruit has the function of the hundred realms. It says "the establishing of all dharmas": in the preceding fact and principle, defiled conditions were taken as "all dharmas"; here it is the dharmas of pure conditions, fully as made clear in the gate of the nonduality of defilement and purity. Next, in gradual and sudden: first, self and other both have gradual and sudden; second, transforming others and giving rise to function, the meaning combines provisional and real, and both are the provisional internal to the essence. For the sake of these dharmas of provisional and real that benefit others, opening and uniting must be made clear. In opening and uniting, "the gradual, of itself, neither unites with itself," because the two teachings of the Tripiṭaka and the shared do not set aside the lesser; "nor unites with the sudden," because the bodhisattvas of the three teachings do not enter the real. Next, the shared and the distinct: in the preceding gate of the gradual and the sudden, though the gradual contained the half teaching, the half lay at the start of the gradual, whereas the present half runs through the later [stages]. The siddhāntas judge the preceding essence and function up to shared and distinct; explain them on the model of the preceding. Next, from "should now be explained by means of the fourfold thesis" on, the conclusion showing the method. It says "the fourfold thesis": mutual breaking and the rest, the four, fully as explained before.

Next, the citation proper. First, in fact and principle it says "not . as . the threefold world": this means "not the same as." Beginning with the two vehicles, all differ from the three realms, which is not enough to distinguish the present [teaching]. Now, this text is formed from the preceding phrase, "neither suchness nor difference," and so it is not the same as the fact and principle of the expedient teaching. In principle and teaching, principle is called "still and extinguished," because the two principles, real and conventional, cannot be spoken. Next, in the teaching it raises "the five bhikṣus": this speaks from the start of the gradual. It should also say, "for those without the nature, it preaches humans and gods; up to the asuras, it is the lower grade of good; up to preaching the four truths without fabrication." Hence one knows that from principle neither can be spoken, and from fact both great and lesser can be spoken. Proving teaching and practice: "hears" proves teaching, and "good practice" proves practice. The two texts from "what you now practice" on both prove practice; one should broadly use this sūtra to make clear the marks of good practice becoming buddhahood. The word "various" in meaning contains teaching and practice, because there are causes and conditions of the present and the past. Next, from "only the departure from falsehood" on, proving bondage and liberation: "only the departure" and so on: within the lesser, leaving falsehood is called liberation, and falsehood is called bondage. Though the lesser is liberation, it is not complete liberation; the liberation of the lesser, with regard to the great, is still called bondage; hence it says "not yet having attained," and this is the proof. Great and lesser both have bondage and liberation; only the present is named liberation. From "they practice in full" on, proving cause and effect: "practice in full" is the cause; "at the site of enlightenment" is the effect. Here one must make clear the difference between the fruits of origin and trace, and so it notes "(and so on)." "Buddha eye" is essence; "behold the sentient beings of the six destinies" is function. "First see" is sudden; "studied the Lesser Vehicle" is gradual. The note "(and so on)": the five periods differ, but merge and lead into the sudden. "The poor son" is opening; "entrustment with the family wealth" is uniting. "The phantom city" is shared; "the treasure-place" is distinct. "The various" is the four siddhāntas.

Each must be explained so that the meaning fits the ten pairs and accords with the intent of this sūtra (and so on). First it cites the two phrases "the buddhas'" and so on. "Every object and every cognition" and so on: fact and principle are both objects, and the object is principle. Wisdom is what knows; viewed against what expresses, both are called principle, because both are what is expressed.

Next, from "accomplished" on, proving essence and function: "accomplished what is profound" is the essence complete; "according with what is fitting" is function. One should know that the text abbreviates the words "according with what is fitting." Next, from "since I" on, proving cause and effect: it takes properly the word "attained" to prove the cause of attaining the effect, which is also an effect that has a cause. From "various" on, proving gradual and sudden: "various" proves the gradual, "to bring about the departure" proves the sudden. If one takes "ultimate" and so on, the empty, the middle, and so on are principle, and the provisional and so on are fact. If this text is set against the provisional and real below and so on, all are discussed in terms of [what is] internal to the essence, because within this collection they have already been opened. It says "every buddha alike is thus": in revealing the real, all the buddhas raise the five buddhas as being the same in fact.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, in citing the treatise, [the commentary] differs slightly from the Upadeśa: it has only eight pairs, lacking opening and uniting and bondage and liberation. Benefit is shared and distinct, because opening the distinct to bring out the shared is for benefiting beings. Bondage and liberation differ slightly from cause and effect, and so they are not matched separately. The Upadeśa explains "the buddhas' wisdom is profound" as proof of profundity. "Is fivefold": here, in terms of what is realized, it is judged to belong to principle; though divided into five, it does not go beyond realization. "Meaning" means the flavor of meaning, because what the Buddha's wisdom realizes has real meaning. "The real substance" means the principle realized; "inner realization" means one's own practice according with the object; "what is relied upon" means making clear properly the principle relied upon; "unsurpassed" praises the principle realized. On reaching the fruit, it surpasses the three, five, seven, and nine vehicles and so on; the Upadeśa itself further explains the unsurpassed profundity. It says "great awakening": the Upadeśa uses the word "great" to explain "unsurpassed"; it is not citing awakening to prove "unsurpassed." Because the third has its own proof of profundity, the reading above in terms of this chapter also takes the first phrase as proving principle and the next phrase as proving teaching. It says "āgama": this means "the incomparable dharma," that is, the verbal teaching. The preceding five prove principle and are able to give rise to teaching, and so it is called "principle and teaching." Reading Notes for Part 10

Translator's notes

Quoted phrases. Zhanran continues through the site's third fascicle of the Textual Commentary, from the explanation of the ten pairs to the praise that cuts off speech. His fascicle four (upper) therefore still comments on fascicle-003 of the site. Where he paraphrases or uses a variant ("deep and lofty, horizontal and vast" for 深高橫度, where the Textual Commentary has 深高橫廣), the site's wording is kept and the character given.

The gates of nonduality. Units 2 and 7 refer to the gates of "the nonduality of fact and principle," "the nonduality of cultivation and nature," and "the nonduality of defilement and purity" in Zhanran's Ten Gates of Nonduality. The last two match gates in that work; there is no gate named "fact and principle," and the reference may be to the gate of form and mind or to the treatment of fact and principle in general.

The Abhidharmakośa. "What accumulates and gives rise is called mind; what deliberates is called intellect; what discerns is called consciousness" (unit 2) and "Where there is the body faculty, nine things; ten things where there are other faculties" (unit 33) are from the Abhidharmakośa (T1558).

The Upadeśa. Units 9 and 25 compare the Textual Commentary's matching of the ten pairs with the Upadeśa on the Lotus (T1519/T1520), whose version of the sūtra adds "hard to see, hard to awaken to, hard to know." The five reasons the Buddha does not address the bodhisattvas (unit 25) are quoted from it with small differences.

"Not as the threefold world." In unit 8 Zhanran glosses 不如三界見於三界 as "not the same as [how] the three realms [see]." The site's English of the Textual Commentary ("not seeing the threefold world as it sees the threefold world") reads the phrase differently.

The ten modes of the Profound Meaning. The ten that Zhanran lists in unit 25 are from the Profound Meaning, fascicle nine. He gives "abiding in the one while making use of the two" where the Profound Meaning has "the three"; on the site the list reads "abolishing . gathering," here "setting aside . merging."

The Vibhāṣā. The reasons for sitting cross-legged in unit 25 are from the Vibhāṣā (T1546), in fascicle twenty-one of the Taishō text (Zhanran says twenty-two), in the miscellaneous section, under the sūtra phrase "sitting upright and cross-legged, fixing the mind in front."

Four intentions. In unit 40 Zhanran uses the four intentions of the Mahāyānasaṃgraha (the intention of equality, of another time, of another meaning, and of beings' inclinations) to argue that the Lotus is of fully drawn-out meaning and must be read by its text.

"Several treatises." In unit 41 Zhanran suspects that the Textual Commentary's "according to the various treatises" should read "according to the Upadeśa," and that "the very limit of fact and principle" has dropped a word.

The Book of Han. The saying on Heaven's earnestness (鄭重) in unit 50 is quoted from the Book of Han.

Masters Yin and Guan. Master Yin and Master Guan, quoted by the Textual Commentary on why the Buddha "stops," are earlier Lotus commentators who have not been identified further here. In unit 55 Zhanran's "Hence it differs from Yin" is his own heading, not a phrase of the Textual Commentary.

"Gradation." 若干 ("a number," "several") is rendered "gradation," as on the site, because Zhanran contrasts the countable expedient wisdom with real wisdom, which admits no such division.

Abidings. The site's "ten abidings" is used throughout; "the initial abiding" (初住) is the first of them.

The closing title. The Taishō text closes this fascicle with 法華文句記卷第四（上）.
