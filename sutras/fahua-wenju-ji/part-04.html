The Great Disciples from Kāśyapa to Rāhula

This part is the upper third of the second fascicle of Zhanran's Notes on the Textual Commentary on the Lotus. It continues his comments on Zhiyi's list of the great disciples, from Mahākāśyapa through Rāhula, covering the end of the site's Textual Commentary fascicle one and the beginning of fascicle two. For each disciple Zhanran does three things. He sorts Zhiyi's account of causes and conditions into the four siddhāntas (the mundane, for the individual, the therapeutic, the ultimate meaning). He supplies the source stories that Zhiyi only alludes to, often quoting at length from the Āgamas, the Great Treatise, the Jātaka Sūtra, and the Vinaya: Kāśyapa and the twelve dhūtas, the Buddha's ten miracles for the three Kāśyapa brothers, the thief who was Śāriputra in a past life, the contest between Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana at Lake Anavatapta, Maudgalyāyana and the nāga kings, Kātyāyana's debates. And he explains the technical terms in the readings by the teachings, by origin and trace, and by contemplation, correcting copying errors and older glosses along the way. A short digression in the Kātyāyana section criticizes practitioners of his own day who make a show of never lying down. Phrases quoted from the Textual Commentary use the site's published English.

Mahākāśyapa

Chinese Source

迦葉緣起，《傳》中最廣，豈可具書？迦葉是姓故云氏也。負圖者，如此方河圖。《十二遊經》云：「佛成道第三年始度五人，第四年化大迦葉及三兄弟，第五年化目連、身子。」「聚落」，多人所居，但勝人當名。「畏勝王」者，民物不合勝王耳。「三捨」者，捨此已下是也。「後時佛語」等者，《增一》云：「佛在迦蘭陀與五百比丘俱，時迦葉乞食前至佛所，却坐一面。佛言：汝年老長大志衰根弊，可捨乞食及十二頭陀，亦可受請并受長衣。迦葉曰：我不從佛教，若如來不成佛，我作辟支佛，辟支佛法盡壽行蘭若行。佛言：善哉！善哉！多所饒益。若迦葉行頭行在世者，我法久住增益人天，三惡道滅成三乘道。」《十住婆沙》十二頭陀一一各具十種功德，廣在第十四卷〈十二頭陀品〉中。《增一》四十六在增十一文中，樹下、露坐合之為一，故知法相不可一準。《大論》四十九，說無生忍為十二頭陀，屬通非藏，故今約教極在涅槃。有無同異具如《止觀》第四記。

Ru-Yi Translation

The account of Kāśyapa's causes and conditions is most extensive in the Transmission [of the Dharma Treasury]; how could it be written out in full? "Kāśyapa" is a clan name, hence it says "Clan." "Bearing a chart": like the River Chart of this land. The Sūtra of the Twelve Roamings says: "In the third year after the Buddha attained the path he first saved the five men; in the fourth year he converted Mahākāśyapa and the three brothers; in the fifth year he converted Maudgalyāyana and Śāriputra." "Village": where many people live; it is simply that an eminent person bears its name. "Fearing to offend the reigning king by outdoing him": a commoner's possessions ought not to outdo the king's. "To renounce in this threefold way": what follows "He gave up" is meant. "Later the Buddha said to him" and so on: the Ekottarika says: "The Buddha was at Kalandaka with five hundred bhikṣus. Then Kāśyapa, having begged his food, came first to the Buddha and sat back to one side. The Buddha said: You are old and aged, your will is weak and your faculties worn; you may give up begging and the twelve dhūtas, and you may also accept invitations and long robes. Kāśyapa said: I do not follow the Buddha's instruction. If the Tathāgata had not become a buddha, I would have become a pratyekabuddha, and a pratyekabuddha's practice is to practice the forest-dwelling practice to the end of life. The Buddha said: Excellent! Excellent! It brings much benefit. If Kāśyapa's dhūta practice remains in the world, my Dharma will long abide, humans and gods will increase, the three evil paths will be extinguished, and the paths of the three vehicles will be accomplished." In the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā, each of the twelve dhūtas has ten kinds of merit; this is extensive in fascicle fourteen, the "Chapter on the Twelve Dhūtas." In the Ekottarika, fascicle forty-six, in the section of elevens, "under a tree" and "in the open" are combined into one; hence we know that the features of the dharmas cannot be held to a single standard. The Great Treatise, fascicle forty-nine, speaks of the acceptance of non-arising as the twelve dhūtas; this belongs to the shared [teaching], not the Tripiṭaka, and so here, in terms of the teachings, it culminates in the Nirvāṇa [Sūtra]. Having or lacking, sameness and difference, are fully as in the notes on fascicle four of Calming and Contemplation.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The four divine samādhis" are the concentrations of the four bases of supernatural power; by the four bases there are these four functions. "Formlessness": because he is able to vanish from sight. "Boundless intent": because he knows the minds of others. "Pure accumulation": because he is able to transform the defiled. "Non-regression": because he is able to enter the evil [realms]. As for "the four concentrations": these mean that he will certainly attain the dhyānas and the untainted, is able to discern well, and is complete in the precepts; by these things he attains non-regression. "Mandhātṛ" is the King Born from the Crown. "All the gods and men alike called him the Buddha's teacher": the Ekottarika says: "Kāśyapa, hearing gods and humans call him the Buddha's teacher, rose, kissed the Buddha's feet, and said: The Buddha is my teacher; I am his disciple."

From "On another occasion, Kāśyapa" on: as the Separately Translated Āgama, fascicle twenty, says: "The Buddha was at Kalandaka. Kāśyapa and Ānanda entered the city to beg. Further, in the Separately Translated Āgama, the rule for begging is: go from house to house, and if it is not enough, stop. Some say: go to seven houses, and if it is not enough, stop.

「四大弟子」者，迦葉、賓頭盧、羅云、軍屠鉢漢。文列七大，若并本族大及諸天戀慕為慈悲大，合為九大。若以此為四悉者，姓及捨受即世界；三行大即為人，聞名覩行皆生善；故第四印可即是對治，以佛印可息疑怪故；位與慈悲即第一義，見理入位故。集法持法並入位之功。集法中引肇公者，明集法功多。又文中「位大」者，不獨論無學位也，以德高望重所掌職大。「云云」者，因緣縱多並為四悉攝盡。

Ru-Yi Translation

"The four great disciples": Kāśyapa, Piṇḍola, Rāhula, and Kuṇḍopadhānīya. The text lists seven greatnesses; if one adds the greatness of his original clan, and the greatness of compassion seen in the gods' longing for him, together there are nine. If these are made into the four siddhāntas: the clan, the renouncing, and the receiving are the mundane; the third, the greatness of practice, is for the individual, because hearing his name and seeing his practice all generate good; hence the fourth, being affirmed and sealed, is the therapeutic, because the Buddha's affirmation stills doubt and wonder; rank and compassion are the ultimate meaning, because, seeing principle, one enters the stages. Compiling the Dharma and upholding the Dharma are both the merit of entering the stages. In compiling the Dharma, citing Sengzhao makes clear that the merit of compiling the Dharma is great. Further, "the greatness of rank" in the text does not discuss only the stage beyond training: because his virtue was high and his reputation weighty, the office he held was great. As for "(and so on)": however many the causes and conditions, they are all wholly embraced by the four siddhāntas.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, in [the reading] by the teachings, it first speaks in terms of phenomenal objects; next, from "Let us take, for instance," on, it distinguishes the teachings in terms of the contemplation of the truths. Among the phenomenal objects, "free of the five fears": the king, thieves, water, fire, and wicked sons. "Two concern the rule of robes" and so on: from the beginning, pair them in order by number; but the text lacks "begging in sequence." The three teachings below all take the first matters as their object; the expounder must give them in detail. Understanding the twelve dhūtas, each is made into the eighty-eight proclivities, the thirty-seven factors, and so on; if this is so for the dhūtas, how much more for the other, lesser practices! We who unworthily share in the stream of the path — hearing this superior Dharma, shall we not examine our own mind and conduct? "What merely seems continuous and unbroken": arising and ceasing from moment to moment, one in delusion calls it continuous; ordinary beings, not understanding, falsely call it permanent. All three feelings are suffering; "what is merely agreeable," the agreeable feeling, is in fact suffering. Movement is not mastery; because it moves, there is no self. In the shared teaching the phenomenal object points to the above; it distinguishes the contemplation of the truths only in terms of the features of practice — this too can be seen. In the distinct teaching, one "relies on the dharma-body," because, aiming at the dharma-body, one cultivates the two virtues. As for the "(and so on)" after the text: it does not clarify in detail the shaking off at each stage, horizontally and vertically, because the horizontal and vertical, self and other, gates of the stages of the distinct [teaching] cannot all be given at once. Since in the perfect teaching it says that the dwelling is itself the [other] two, we can verify that these are the originally present three virtues; what is attained through cultivation is likewise. "To shake off one thing is to shake off all things" and so on does not go beyond the three virtues: one being all practices is the robe; all being one wisdom is the food; neither one nor all is the body, the dwelling. As for "(and so on)": the features of the practice are not yet clarified; for now they are paired with the dhūtas.

Ru-Yi Translation

Cha (剎) should be chamo [kṣetra], here "field": the royal land of one buddha. Here it is called a "commemorative pillar" because it marks a field-domain; hence many sūtras say "marker-pillar." If one wishes to clarify the four siddhāntas: the first is the mundane, because clan and dwelling place differ; from "at the time of the Buddha Vipaśyin" on is for the individual — jointly erecting the pillar as a good cause; from "The Buddha performed" on is the therapeutic, because seeing the Buddha can remove evil; from "The Buddha then said to him" on is the ultimate meaning, because one is able to see the true principle within the Lesser Vehicle.

Ru-Yi Translation

"The Buddha performed ten kinds of miraculous transformation": the text lists ten matters for now, but the texts of the Vinaya and the Treatise are not limited to these ten. Further, although the text lists them, the matters are not given in detail; the Ekottarika and the Ruiying clarify them extensively, and I now briefly bring them out so that the text can be seen. The Ekottarika says: "The Buddha entered Kāśyapa's cave; the poisonous dragon emitted fire and so on; the Buddha gathered up the poisonous dragon and kept it in his bowl, and came to Kāśyapa. Kāśyapa invited him to stay, making offerings for three months. When the time came, he invited [the Buddha] to the meal; the Buddha said: Go ahead. Then he went to the jambu tree and took a jambu fruit. And so on up to: [Kāśyapa said:] Though the śramaṇa has spiritual power, it is not equal to the truth of my path. Next he went to Pūrvavideha and took a bibhītaka fruit; next he went to Godānīya and took a harītakī fruit; next he went to Uttarakuru and took naturally growing rice (each time, as before, he said, 'The śramaṇa .' and so on). Again, in Jambudvīpa, he took an āmalaka fruit. When [Kāśyapa] wished to hold a great sacrifice, the five hundred disciples wished to split firewood, but the axes, once raised, would not come down. Kāśyapa asked the Buddha; the Buddha said: Do you wish them to come down? The axes then came down. Once down, they again would not rise (as before). They wished to light the fire, and the fire would not light; they wished to put out the fire, and the fire would not go out (as before).

Ru-Yi Translation

"Kāśyapa thought: At the great sacrifice I intend, kings and nobles will surely come; Gautama is handsome, and if people see him he will make me lose my gain; if he does not come tomorrow, I will be very fortunate. The Buddha, knowing this, went for the time being to the north to take rice, to Godānīya to take milk, and went to Lake Anavatapta to eat. When he returned to the stone cave in the evening, Kāśyapa asked: Why did you not come yesterday? The Buddha said: I knew your mind, so I did not come — and told him everything above in full. Again, because the Four Heavenly Kings came to hear the Dharma, there was light in the night; the next day he asked the Buddha, and the Buddha told him all. Next Śakra and Brahmā came, and it was likewise. Kāśyapa asked: Can you make my grandfather come to hear the Dharma? The Buddha then made him come (and so on). The Ganges suddenly rose; Kāśyapa, fearing that the Buddha would be drowned by the water, sent disciples to look: the water did not cover his feet, and he was walking on the water (as before). The Buddha said: You are a blind man; your eyes see nothing of such miracles, and still you insist that your path is true. The Buddha said: Can you walk on water? Only now shall we walk on the water together (and so on). If you do not abandon your wrong views, I will make you suffer for long eons. Hearing this, he bowed his head at the Buddha's feet and asked to repent, and then told his disciples: Each of you should do as he sees fit; my teacher is the World-Honored One. He said to his disciples: When I saw him subdue the dragon, I already had a mind to take refuge in the Buddha. And so on up to: the five hundred disciples all heard 'Welcome' and attained the fruit of the śramaṇa, and they threw the implements of their art into the water, which carried them down the current. The two younger brothers, who also had five hundred disciples, saw the fire-implements coming down (and so on). They too all [heard] 'Welcome' and became śramaṇas. The Buddha said: All buddhas alike have five tasks: first, to turn the Dharma wheel; second, to teach the Dharma for their father; third, to teach the Dharma for their mother; fourth, to guide ordinary beings to establish the bodhisattva practice; fifth, to give predictions to bodhisattvas. Therefore I go there to teach the Dharma for my father. His father the king then had a thousand Śākyas go forth to surround him."

Ru-Yi Translation

In [the reading] by the teachings, it ought also to list the four teachings fully in explaining the practice and virtue of the three persons; it lists the five flavors instead because the flavors embrace the teachings. In origin and trace, the three persons are paired with the three virtues: in the perfect virtues each person is necessarily endowed with all three, but each is named from the one virtue he particularly represents; since they represent them jointly, their essence is the same and their nature one. In the contemplation it is likewise: first one attains the middle way, next one negates the inverted [views] of the extremes, finally one illuminates the truths of the extremes. Quoted lemmas: Zhanran's comments follow the site's Textual Commentary from "Mahākāśyapa" (end of fascicle one) to "Rāhula" (beginning of fascicle two). The notes on Calming and Contemplation: references such as "the notes on fascicle four of Calming and Contemplation" point to Zhanran's Fuxing (T1912); "the Explanatory Notes" is his commentary on the Profound Meaning.

"(as in the commentary)": in the long quotations Zhanran writes 如疏 where Zhiyi's text already gives the passage; he is supplying only what Zhiyi left out.

Kāśyapa's lion's roar (unit 2): the episode with Sthūlanandā is in the Separately Translated Saṃyukta Āgama (T100). The dhūta dialogue is in the Ekottarika Āgama (T125).

The five fears (unit 3): Zhanran lists king, thieves, water, fire, and wicked sons; the site's Textual Commentary lists water, fire, theft, robbery, and royal exaction. The lemma keeps the site's wording.

Maudgalyāyana's names (unit 10): Zhanran reads 吉占 as the father's personal name, and explains the five characters 目伽略兮度 as a miscopy of 目伽路子度, the vocative with which a buddha of another world addresses Maudgalyāyana in the Great Treatise, fascicle ten. Gaṇaka Maudgalyāyana (Madhyama Āgama no. 144, fascicle 35) and Gopaka Maudgalyāyana are other bearers of the name.

The ten universals (unit 13): the site's Chinese includes 十一切 ("the ten universals") among the phenomenal meditations, though the site's English does not show it. The verse on the eighteen transformations is Zhanran's own mnemonic.

The Pāyāsi debate (unit 15): Zhanran notes that the ten rounds of question and answer come from the Dīrgha Āgama's account of Kumāra-Kāśyapa refuting Pāyāsi, which the Textual Commentary borrows as an example for Kātyāyana.

Exclusive sitting (unit 15): the criticism of those who make a show of never lying down is Zhanran's own comment on the practice of his day.

"Sālala cave" (unit 18): Zhanran treats 俱薩羅 (site "Kosala") as another transliteration of the cave's name 娑羅邏, not the kingdom.

The wisdom-eye (unit 18): Zhanran says the contemplation-of-mind passage for Aniruddha lacks the wisdom-eye and supplies the missing words himself; the site's text matches the Chinese as received.

Fault of speech (unit 21): 有口失緣 is read as the story behind Revata's name "Ever Speaking." The Golden Treasury Sūtra (金藏經) was not located.

Piṇḍola-Bharadvāja (unit 22): the site renders 畢陵伽婆蹉 as Piṇḍola-Bharadvāja. Zhanran's list of the four great disciples (unit 2) names 賓頭盧, Piṇḍola, as a different person; 畢陵伽婆蹉 is usually identified as Pilindavatsa.
