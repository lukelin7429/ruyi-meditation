The General Prologue: From "Thus" to "Amid Vulture Peak"

This part continues Zhanran's comments on the first fascicle of the Textual Commentary. It finishes the demonstration of origin and trace — why only Śākyamuni's first attainment counts as the origin, and why an endless regress of earlier buddhas does not matter for practice — and of contemplating the mind, where precepts, concentration, and wisdom each form three sections. It then turns to the general prologue of the Lotus and follows Zhiyi's fourfold explanation of its opening words: "thus," "I heard," "at one time," "Buddha," "abiding," "Rājagṛha," "Vulture Peak," and "amid." Under "thus" Zhanran argues that the eight teachings are indispensable for reading the Lotus and rebukes those who say Tiantai has only four teachings; under "I heard" he discusses the compilations of the canon, the kinds of "self," and the four Ānandas; under "Buddha" he sets out the four buddhas of the four teachings; under "abiding" he defends Zhiyi's use of the Great Treatise; and under Rājagṛha and Vulture Peak he adds sources for the legend of King Spotted-Foot and for the mountain's names. Several brief gaps in the Textual Commentary are noted and supplied.

Demonstrating Origin and Trace: The One Origin

Chinese Source

「次示本迹者」，為二：先法，次譬。初法為五：先、指本因所稟；次、「但佛」下，指本果所說；三、中間行化；四、今日所說；五、未來所說。自從本因所稟莫非真實三段，雖俱真實本不可多，故下三文咸同一本因果真實三段教相。此中正簡久本真實，是故云也。然以本因所稟亦是彼佛迹說恐無窮，故但在今佛因果為本，據理非不稟餘佛化，因緣約教既指今佛，故明本迹。且廢於他，故指今經壽量為釋迦本，不得更指前佛所說。前佛復有前佛，故云無窮，唯指一佛則無斯過，降茲一本餘皆是迹。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "Next, to demonstrate the 'trace and origin' reading": in two, first the Dharma, next the simile. The Dharma first, in five: first, pointing to what was received in the original cause; next, from "But since buddha looks back to buddha" on, pointing to what was taught in the original fruit; third, the conversion in between; fourth, what is taught today; fifth, what will be taught in the future. From what was received in the original cause on, all are none other than the true and real three sections; though all are true and real, the origin cannot be many; hence the three passages below all share the one teaching aspect of the true and real three sections of the original cause and fruit. Here it properly distinguishes the long-ago origin as true and real, hence it speaks so. Yet since what was received in the original cause is also that [earlier] buddha's trace teaching, it might be without end; hence it takes only the present Buddha's cause and fruit as the origin. In principle it is not that he received no other buddha's conversion; but since causes and conditions and [the explanation] in terms of the teachings already point to the present Buddha, it makes clear origin and trace [in him]. For now it sets the others aside, and so points to the Lifespan [chapter] of the present sūtra as Śākyamuni's origin, and may not point further to what earlier buddhas taught. Earlier buddhas had earlier buddhas again, hence it says "without end"; pointing only to one buddha avoids this fault; below this one origin, all the rest are traces.

Ru-Yi Translation

At a time when there is no teaching, one is inwardly perfumed and awakens by oneself; on the day when there is a teaching, how can one cling to delusion? It is like a hundred deluded blind men who all do not know the road: one of the deluded reaches [the goal] first and teaches the others; if the others cling to their folly and do not accept the teaching of the first, whose fault is it? Moreover, consider: the benefit gained in Śākyamuni's one course of conversion is hard to conceive, and the benefit before that is beyond reckoning — how could one not receive the teaching? Yet in the end it is a useless argument, and it cannot be taken as the end, because it is of no benefit to those who receive the teaching. Again, in the worlds of the ten directions there are also those who awaken by smelling fragrance, feeling touch, or gazing; how could one seek its beginning in the teaching through sound?

Demonstrating Contemplating the Mind and the Use of the Four Explanations

Chinese Source

「次示觀心相者」，先總、次別。總中有二重：一、約修行；二、約法門。行謂所行法即所用，行必先戒次定後慧，用必先以慧擇後方定戒，定為戒本故戒復居後。又觀心者，如《玄》文中，或約行相、或約法門、或從觀境，故銷文例之亦對三分，義當觀心。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "Next, to demonstrate the 'contemplation of mind' reading": first general, then specific. In the general there are two layers: first, in terms of practice; second, in terms of the Dharma-gate. "Practice" means the dharma practiced, and the Dharma-gate is what is used. Practice must have precepts first, concentration next, and wisdom last; use must first sift with wisdom and only afterward [apply] concentration and precepts — concentration being the root of precepts, precepts again come last. Again, contemplating the mind, as in the Profound Meaning, is sometimes in terms of the marks of practice, sometimes in terms of the Dharma-gate, sometimes from the object of contemplation; hence in explaining the text it follows these patterns and also matches the three sections, which in meaning is contemplating the mind.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the specific intent: each of the three sections has three. First, the three of precepts: the expedients are like the prologue, the fourfold announcement like the main [section], and the conclusion like the dissemination. As for "the preliminary expedients": everything before the single announcement is expedient. The three sections of concentration: the twenty-five expedients are the prologue, entering contemplation in the sitting posture is the main [section], and applying it to conditions while walking and standing is the dissemination; or study may be the prologue, one's own practice the main, and teaching others the dissemination. The other two are all likewise. Next, further, in terms of skill in entering, emerging, and abiding and so on: this lists them according to the sūtra; if one follows the three sections, then entering is the prologue, abiding the main, and emerging the dissemination. The three of wisdom are likewise: following the text and following the meaning, the order differs; the three sections should say emptiness is the prologue, the middle the main, and the provisional the dissemination.

Taking contemplating the mind as a general name: among those who contemplate, the three in between are [the assembly that] forms bonds; [the assembly] matching the occasion uses contemplation in general; the shadow-and-echo [assembly] occasions and brings out the work of conversion. If it is achieved, it becomes the contemplation of partial realization. Therefore people say that the one passage of contemplating the mind is the broadest, yet in principle it is most essential. Moreover, the Great Master does this now for the sake of beginners in study: from "thus" to "departed," seeing differing texts, they recognize the one contemplation; they also recognize that in the past sūtras the text is the same and the contemplation differs, and that both text and contemplation differ; and they also recognize that in the present teaching text and contemplation are both the same, while the combined and the rest, the opened and the rest, can be recognized by analogy. Therefore it is necessary to explain by contemplating the mind. Again, the explanation by causes and conditions follows the momentum of the meaning and must distinguish the present from the past.

The General and Specific Prologues

Chinese Source

先釋序中，先對辨通別，次正釋通序。初文又二：先分二文，次正辨異。中云「通序通諸教」者，亦可云通序通諸部，別序別一部；亦可云通序通諸經，別序別一教。今經部教唯在一教，故且以教對經言之。又通序名通而體別，別序事別而義通，義通故通有別序，體別故別在今經，故知今經通別俱別別在佛乘，以如是等不關諸經，方可得名正家之序。正名序家之正，故一家相承三段可識。故釋如是竟一部炳然，不然豈有送客之序而敘遊山，禪祖碑文而譚律頌，故須摩頂至足皆是一身，豈以通途之言能消通別二序？若不異者，發起徒施。於中先標離合。云「或五六七」者，五者如文，合佛及處；六則離佛及處；七則離我與聞。

Ru-Yi Translation

In first explaining the prologue, it first distinguishes general and specific in contrast, and next explains the general prologue properly. The first passage is again in two: first it divides the two texts, and next it properly distinguishes their difference. As for "The general prologue is common to all the Buddha's teachings": one could also say the general prologue is common to all collections and the specific prologue particular to one collection; one could also say the general prologue is common to all sūtras and the specific prologue particular to one teaching. The collection and teaching of the present sūtra lie in only one teaching, so for now it speaks by setting teaching against sūtra. Again, the general prologue is general in name but specific in substance; the specific prologue is specific in phenomena but general in meaning. Because its meaning is general, the general [prologue] has a specific prologue; because its substance is specific, the specific lies in the present sūtra. Hence we know that in the present sūtra both general and specific are specific, specifically in the buddha vehicle; since "thus" and the rest have nothing to do with the other sūtras, only then can it be called the prologue of the main [section]. "Main" is named as the main of this prologue; hence the three sections as transmitted in our school can be recognized. Hence once "thus" has been explained, the whole sūtra is illuminated; otherwise, how could there be a preface for seeing off a guest that narrates a mountain excursion, or a stele inscription for a Chan patriarch that discusses vinaya verses? Hence from crown to foot it must all be one body; how could the general words of the common way explain both the general and the specific prologues? If they were not different, the occasioning would be in vain. Within this, it first states separating and combining. As for "five, or six, or seven": five is as in the text, combining the Buddha and the place; six separates the Buddha and the place; seven separates "I" and "heard."

Even if there is incidental explanation in the extended explanation below, it is only a side reference. First, as for "points to the substance of the Dharma that was heard": though the four explanations below refer generally to the whole sūtra, specifically it lies in the main section and the dissemination; it may also include the specific prologue, for harboring doubt, answering the question, and the Immeasurable Meanings are all called hearing. The rain of flowers and the trembling of the earth, spoken of in terms of what they signify, all signify hearing; hence the sūtra from beginning to end is the substance of what is heard. As for "the hearing and the retaining occurred together in harmony" and so on: it is the meeting of causes and conditions. Speaking generally, the five items are none other than causes and conditions, as said earlier in the general discussion of the four intents. "Arising one after another in sequence" is for now a first approximation; if hearing "thus" and the lord of conversion stand first, it can also be used generally.

"Thus" Explained by the Four Siddhāntas

Chinese Source

次「又如是」下，廣釋。先釋「如是」。初因緣中世界即是歡喜，故云不諍，諸佛皆然未足別顯；若約今經，先施次開方名不諍，事在約教故今未論。今引諸佛以異顯同，亦世界義。「舉時方」者，如是可信良有時方，即說如是得益時處，方則崛山猶通於昔，時顯別味令處非通，即生實信，方是今經為人悉也。「阿漚」者，阿無、漚有，一切外經以二字為首，以其所計此二為本，部內所明不出所計，故立如是對破外人不如不是。故準下約教外典全無，故云破惡。《百論》云：「外曰：汝指何為善法耶？內曰：惡止善作。外曰：汝經有過初不吉故；我經不爾，初後皆吉。內曰：凡一切法有於三種，謂自、他、共。以汝吉法無自等故，故我先破有無自生及他、共等。」故計有無為自他等，名之為惡，故我經先止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, from "Furthermore, as for 'thus'" on, the extended explanation. First explaining "thus." In the first, causes and conditions, the mundane is joy, hence it says "does not contend"; all buddhas are so, which is not enough to reveal [this sūtra] specifically. If in terms of the present sūtra, first setting out and then opening is what is called not contending; this matter lies in [the explanation] in terms of the teachings, so it is not discussed now. Here it cites the buddhas to show sameness through difference, which is also the meaning of the mundane. As for "citing the time and place": "thus" is credible precisely because there is a time and place, namely the time and place at which benefit was gained when "thus" was taught. As for the place, Vulture Peak still covers the past; the time shows a specific flavor and makes the place not general; then real faith arises — only this is the individual [siddhānta] of the present sūtra. As for "a" and "ou": "a" is non-being, "ou" is being. All outside scriptures take these two syllables as their head, because what they hold takes these two as the root, and what is shown in their collections does not go beyond what they hold. Hence "thus" is set up to refute the outsiders as "not as, not is." Hence, as the [explanation] in terms of the teachings below shows, the outside scriptures wholly lack it; hence it says "refuting evil." The Hundred Treatise says: "The outsider said: What do you point to as good dharmas? The insider said: Stopping evil and doing good. The outsider said: Your scripture has a fault, because its beginning is not auspicious; my scripture is not so, auspicious at beginning and end. The insider said: All dharmas are of three kinds, namely self, other, and both. Because your auspicious dharma has no self and the rest, I first refute arising from self, from other, from both, from being and non-being, and so on." Hence holding being and non-being as self, other, and so on is called evil, and so our scripture first stops it.

Reading Notes for Part 2

Translator's notes

Quoted phrases. As in Part 1, phrases Zhanran quotes from the Textual Commentary follow the site's English of fascicle 1, and his own readings are explained where they differ.

A probable copying error. In unit 11, 拂遊 ("sweeping away the wandering") is probably an error for 拂迹 ("sweeping away the trace"); the translation reads "sweeping away [the trace]."

An obscure passage. The sentence in unit 12 on the assemblies (those who "form bonds," those "matching the occasion," the "shadow-and-echo" assembly) refers to the traditional four assemblies of a sūtra; the syntax is compressed and the translation is tentative.

"Salt" and "all." In unit 12 Zhanran suggests that 鹹 ("salt") in the Textual Commentary's "ten thousand streams . salt" is an error for 咸 ("all alike"). The site's English, "alike returning to the salt sea," keeps both senses.

Hundred Treatise and Middle Treatise. The exchange in unit 16 condenses the opening of the Hundred Treatise (T1569), which has "your scripture has a fault, because its beginning is not auspicious" and "stopping evil." The line "even arising from causes and conditions is not possible, let alone without causes and conditions" (unit 19) gives the sense of the Middle Treatise but was not found in T1564 in that wording.

The Vimalakīrti works. Unit 21 records how Zhiyi's earlier ten-fascicle Profound Meaning of the Vimalakīrti was divided into the Meaning of the Four Teachings (six fascicles), the Four Siddhāntas (two), and the Three Contemplations (two).

The four arrows. The simile in unit 25 is from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T374), close to verbatim. The Avataṃsaka line on the net of the teaching was not found in that wording (source not located).

Longtuo. Zhanran (unit 27) reads 龍陀 in 龍陀之本 as the name of a buddha: Paramārtha identifies Subhūti as "the Blue Longtuo Buddha of the east," and a Ratnakūṭa passage (which Zhanran says he did not check) names Śāriputra's future buddhahood "Golden Longtuo." The name is kept in transliteration.

The arhats' verses. The first verse in unit 31 is in the Great Treatise (T1509), with 券 for Zhanran's 家; the verse beginning "Alas, all existence is suffering" is found in the Aśoka Legend (T2042) and the Transmission of the Dharma-Treasury (T2058). These are verses of grief, not of joy.

The four propositions on self. The verse in unit 31 is in the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā (T1521), ending "all these are wrong theories."

Nirvāṇa Sūtra passages. Ānanda "the one of much learning" (unit 35), the eight inconceivable qualities of Ānanda (unit 42), and the Licchavis and the youth Invincible (unit 46) are in T374, with small differences of wording. Zhanran's note identifies Invincible (無勝) with Ajita.

Other sources located. The three minor calamities (unit 46) are from the Abhidharmakośa (T1558); the name Sahā as "endurance" (unit 55) is from the Karuṇāpuṇḍarīka (T157), fascicle five; kāla and samaya (unit 43) are from the Great Treatise; the drunken elephant and Śāriputra's meeting with Aśvajit (unit 61) are from the Records of the Western Regions (T2087); the Laṅkāvatāra (T671) mentions Spotted-Foot. The Ekottarika passage on Vulture Peak's other names (unit 65) corresponds to a passage in T125 naming Burden-Bearing Mountain and Seers' Cave Mountain, with different wording; the "Broad and Universal Mountain" name was not found there.

The seven hundred. In unit 42, 舍那迦那 is taken as Śāṇakavāsin, who in some accounts brought the Vṛjis' ten practices before the second council; the identification is tentative.

The tone of 應. In unit 60 Zhanran specifies that 應 in "the Tripiṭaka buddha 應涅槃" is read in the level tone, "ought to [enter] nirvāṇa," not "responds to."

Odes. The osprey poem quoted in unit 65 is the first poem of the Book of Odes, "Guan ju"; Zhanran says the character in Emperor Wu's remark should be 睢.
