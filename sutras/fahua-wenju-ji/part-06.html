The Miscellaneous Assembly and the Specific Prologue

This part finishes Zhanran's comments on the assemblies listed at the start of the Lotus and begins his reading of the specific prologue. For the "miscellaneous assembly" of gods, nāgas, kiṃnaras, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, and humans, he supplies sources the Textual Commentary only names: the Abhidharmakośa on Mount Sumeru and on the body sizes and lifespans of the gods, the Dīrgha Āgama on the garuḍas, and the Great Treatise on Rāhu and the moon. He also adds explanations in terms of the teachings where Zhiyi left them out, and argues that eclipses can be calculated in advance while calamities still depend on the karma of beings. With the specific prologue he argues that "at that time" points to the moment before the six signs, and he defends the fourfold division of every assembly into initiating assembly, those matching the occasion, echoing assembly, and those forming bonds against a critic who said it had "meaning but no text." He then explains how the six signs signify the four ones and the ten wonders, how the Immeasurable Meanings serves as prologue to the Lotus against the views of earlier masters, and why both prologue and main section teach permanence.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, "As for the third listing, that of 'the miscellaneous assembly.'" As for "the old commentators called it 'the assembly of ordinary beings'" and so on: for example, the eight nāgas are mostly bodhisattvas of the eighth ground, and in the Avataṃsaka they all attained inconceivable liberation. As for "among them are those of the path": the followers led by King Ajātaśatru — how could they be a lay assembly alone? "The five destinies": hell is absent, that is, both vehicle and precepts are slack. "The two realms": the formless is absent, that is, precepts urgent and vehicle slack. "The eight groupings": desire-realm gods, form-realm gods, nāgas, gandharvas, kiṃnaras, asuras, garuḍas, and humans, fully as listed below. As for "The Vaipulya sūtras likewise list the hell-realm": fully as in the fourth [fascicle] of my Explanatory Notes: precepts slack and vehicle urgent. As for "the Sūtra of the Intermediate State likewise tells of converting beings of the formless realm": in the first fascicle of the Intermediate State [Sūtra], the "Dividing the Body" chapter, the bodhisattva Concentration-Transformation King said to the Buddha: "Delightful is the teaching of this meaning, making it clear to beings, so that on hearing the Dharma they awaken easily. There are also those hard to awaken: some are in what has resistance, some in what lacks resistance, some are visible, some invisible." Since under "having resistance" there are the visible and the invisible: the visible with resistance is as before; the invisible with resistance is like the stinking-hair ghosts, whose presence is known by their smell wherever they go; the invisible without resistance are the other ghosts and spirits. "Some are in [what] has form, some in the formless, some in neither perception nor non-perception": having form is the two realms of desire and form; the formless is the lower three spheres of space. Though they are hard to convert, the Buddha either goes to them or gathers them here. That this sūtra lacks them is perhaps an abbreviation and the like; hence it says "cannot be forced into a single uniform pattern." Again, a detailed discussion by vehicle and precepts is fully as in the Commentary on the Vimalakīrti.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "The old commentators said that human beings, being the lords of this land": the intent refers to King Ajātaśatru's assembly among the eight groupings. Here too it is not used, so it cites the Immeasurable Meanings to refute it. How could ghosts, spirits, and so on have to be guests from outside? As for "remains unclear": though in view of the Immeasurable Meanings the humans need not come last, there may perhaps be some intent. As for "with the spirits and demons appearing more than once": that is, the kiṃnaras, gandharvas, and asuras. As for "intermingled with the capacities of the many beings they teach": one should further say that the various capacities differ: the analytic intermingled with the entity, the partial intermingled with the perfect (and so on). As for "This is the interpretation in terms of the teachings": the two vehicles are the two vehicles of two teachings; the bodhisattvas are the bodhisattvas of three teachings, since all three teachings have the wisdom of emerging into the provisional. Since the buddha-path is said to be "one including all," it can only be the perfect. The two vehicles and the bodhisattvas are both [counted] from the beginning; the buddha-path means those who cultivated the perfect from the very start, not coming by the gradual [route].

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the Thirty-Three Heavens, the three luminaries, and the Four Heavenly Kings: the Abhidharmakośa says: "Sumeru has four terraces, each ten thousand apart, / projecting sixteen thousand, eight, four, and two thousand; / the Karoṭapāṇis, the Mālādharas, / the Sadāmattas, and the Great Kings' host / dwell in order on the four levels, / and also on the other seven mountains. / The summit of Sumeru is eighty thousand; / there the Thirty-Three dwell; / at its four corners are four peaks, / where the Vajrapāṇis dwell; / the central palace is named Sudarśana, / ten thousand yojanas around; / outside, four gardens adorn it: / the Chariot, the Rough, the Mixed, the Joyful." If forming the four siddhāntas: obtaining his name from his dwelling place is the mundane; being able to give is the individual; reflecting on meanings is the therapeutic; lord of the gods is the ultimate meaning. As for [the explanation] in terms of the teachings: the Āgama proves the Tripiṭaka, the Prajñā the shared. As for the distinct and perfect: in the Avataṃsaka Sūtra Śakra is in the second ground; hence it is clear that in both teachings he attains the Śūraṅgama concentration. As for "after two thousand and twenty-four eons beyond the Bhadrakalpa" and so on: fully as in the Prajñā. As for "(and so on)": the meaning of receiving a prediction corresponds to the bodhisattvas of three teachings. In origin and trace it says "partaking together of the ambrosia of true reality": the sixth [fascicle] of the Great Sūtra, the "Four Reliances" chapter, says, "as in the Thirty-Three Heavens there is the wondrous ambrosia, the medicine of immortality," a simile for the principle being the same from the first abiding upward. "Bright Moon" and so on: origin and trace both come from the Correct Lotus; the origin must have a teaching. As for the intent of "the interpretation through contemplation": the three truths are the heaven of the ultimate meaning; the truths are the object, the object gives birth to wisdom, and wisdom is like the son. As for "(and so on)": as the objects differ and the wisdoms differ, the births of sons are not the same. As for "dwell upon four mountains of precious substance": this is simply Sumeru; the Sūtra of the True Dharma's Mindfulness says, "The four levels each have ten dwelling places," fully as in the Profound Meaning. As for "two hundred and forty thousand li in breadth": I do not know its source. According to the Abhidharmakośa, [the kings] dwell on half the girth of Sumeru, on four levels; the verse says "projecting sixteen thousand, eight, four, and two thousand," because each level going upward is reduced by half.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "In the east, Dhṛtarāṣṭra" and so on: the names are in the second [fascicle] of the Golden Light, the "Four Heavenly Kings" chapter: "At that time the heavenly king Dhṛtarāṣṭra, the heavenly king Virūḍhaka, the heavenly king Virūpākṣa, the heavenly king Vaiśravaṇa," beginning from the east and ending in the north. The third [fascicle], the "Ghosts and Spirits" chapter, lists twenty-eight classes. As for "not permitting outsiders" and so on: as trace they guard the four trees, and since the four trees signify the four virtues, the trace signifies the origin: in origin they protect the twin trees of the four virtues. Though there are four withered and four flourishing, the principle of neither withered nor flourishing also does not go beyond permanence, bliss, self, and purity; hence protecting the eight trees is protecting the four virtues. Protecting the four virtues has two meanings: first, not permitting partial grasping, since partial grasping becomes wrong; second, not permitting total grasping, since total grasping becomes clinging. One must know its principle, which is called neither withered nor flourishing. As for "branches and leaves" here: since each virtue contains all four virtues, if one grasps one name partially without knowing that it contains the [other] virtues, that is called grasping the branches and leaves and so on; hence the outside teachings all have these names. Therefore the text says "The branches and trunk are likened to permanence" and so on, properly revealing that each virtue contains four virtues.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the protecting of the eight forms of craving and views": under each truth there is one [form of] craving and one of views; the four kinds of four truths each, according to their teaching, remove their craving and views. Hence the four kings each govern [their spirits] so that they do not trouble people, just as each protects against views and craving so that they do not grasp objects. If one follows the later explanation, "king" is the king samādhi. The teaching-lords of each teaching may also all be kings, each breaking craving and views. As for the "(and so on)" after "others transform the five desires for one": it should fully make clear the body size and lifespan of the desire-realm gods. The Abhidharmakośa says: "The humans of Jambudvīpa measure / three and a half or four cubits; / the humans of the eastern, western, and northern continents / increase by doubling in order. / The desire-realm gods [measure] a krośa in quarters, / increasing one by one; / the form-realm gods [measure in] yojanas, / the first four increasing by halves; / above this they increase by doubling, / only the Cloudless reduced by three. / Fifty years among humans / are one day and night of the lowest heaven; / by this its lifespan is five hundred; / the five above increase by doubling. / In form there is no day and night; / eons equal the body's size. / In the formless, the first is twenty thousand; / each after increases by two [myriads]. / From Lesser Light up, and in the heavens below, / the great [eon], whole or half, is the eon." If one discusses height for each heaven: as is the distance below, so is the distance above.

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth [fascicle] of the Separately Translated Āgama makes clear at length the Brahmās coming down; the eighth and eleventh make clear at length the gods praising the Buddha. When the gods come, they all take human form. The fourth says: "The Brahmā king came to pay homage to the Buddha; the Buddha entered the fire-light samādhi, and he could not come forward; so he went to Kokālika's door and called him. Kokālika asked: 'Who is calling?' He answered: 'The Brahmā king.' He asked again: 'Did the Buddha predict that you had attained non-returning?' He answered: 'So it is.' He asked again: 'A non-returner is called "one who does not come"; why have you come?' The Brahmā king said: 'Such a person should not be spoken to.'" Hence we know they wholly follow human ways, and still there is going against [ordinary] feelings in order to admonish Kokālika. As for "There would in each case be interpretations in terms of the teachings" and so on: since [Brahmā] requests the lord of the Dharma-wheel for a whole lifetime, if he requests the great it is great, and if he requests the small it is small. As for origin and trace: in origin he abides in the pure, one, real, wondrous object; contemplation removes delusion and defilement, and both may be called "pure." Again, in the text, from the explanation of the gods on, only Śakra has all four explanations; the three luminaries, the Four Kings, the Sovereign and the others, and the form-realm gods, though they have causes and conditions, all lack [the explanation] in terms of the teachings. Yet the gods always follow the World-Honored One and hear the Dharma everywhere; they too should be judged shallow or deep according to the flavors of the teachings, and in terms of opening and revealing and so on, like the cited cases of Śāriputra and others receiving predictions; but since in spreading the teaching this matter is slight, it only gives examples. Hence from the eight nāgas on it is mostly so; only the kiṃnaras have [the explanation in terms of] the teachings, because the meaning is convenient. As for the "(and so on)" after "distinguishing countless dharma-gates": because of "many heads," it takes the provisional contemplation as the head for now; that is, one provisional is all provisional. If one speaks generally, one empty is all empty and one middle is all middle, which are also "many heads."

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the Dīrgha Āgama" and so on: that sūtra fully makes clear the lake issuing four great rivers, as in the commentary. "A" means "without"; "nouda" (耨達) means "affliction," because it lacks those three afflictions. As for "abides in the Lotus Samādhi": all that is realized by the real path is called the Lotus Samādhi; here it simply explains the flower by the flower. As for "the four kiṃnaras": the Saṃyuktābhidharmahṛdaya says: "They are included in the animal destiny; the kiṃnara has one horn and the gandharva no horn; their bodily marks are roughly alike." As for "they play the dharma-gates of the four teachings" and so on here: since through all five flavors there are kiṃnaras and the like, one should know that what they play is also partial, perfect, and distinct; coming to the present sūtra, they reveal only the real truth. As in the Sūtra of the Kiṃnara King Druma: the kiṃnara king Druma, with immeasurable kiṃnaras, immeasurable gandharvas, and immeasurable gods, played eighty-four thousand pure and wondrous musical sounds and came to where the Buddha was; as soon as the strings and songs stirred, the sound shook the trichiliocosm; Sumeru, king of mountains, rose and sank, dipped and lifted; and all the śrāvakas rose from their seats as if dancing in play. The bodhisattva Heavenly Crown asked Kāśyapa: "You are foremost in few desires, contentment, and dhūta practice, yet today you are like a little child." Kāśyapa answered: "It is not my original mind." Hence we know that sūtra belongs to the Vaipulya collection, using the great to rebuke the small; the kiṃnaras played the music of the distinct and the perfect, so that the śrāvakas could not keep still; hence on reaching the Lotus, in principle it should be purely wondrous.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the Ten Jeweled Mountains": the Avataṃsaka lists them fully, as already in my Notes to the fifth [fascicle] of Calming and Contemplation. Ru-Yi Translation

Next, explaining the specific prologue (this enters the third fascicle of the Textual Commentary). "The specific prologue" gets its name in contrast with the general; for now it speaks in contrast within its own teaching. The Buddha and the disciples, and the two passages before and after the sūtra, can be understood. Again, if following the Tathāgata's final instructions, the general prologue is only general and the specific prologue only specific. If one sets [the time from] the Tathāgata's appearance in the world up to the present sūtra against the past and distinguishes them, this specific prologue is specific in intent but also general: being general in narrating the past, it lies only in the present sūtra; the general prologue is general in text but specific in intent: being specific in the present, all sūtras generally have it. If one discusses general and specific directly in terms of the past: the general prologue is general in text and general in meaning, because the collections contain various teachings; there is also general in meaning but specific in intent, being specific in the various flavors. The specific prologue is specific in text and specific in meaning, not concerning other collections, as the matter of holding the bowl and joining the lids lies in its own sūtra; there is also specific in meaning but general in intent, being general to the various teachings. Again, one may also say the general prologue is general in text but specific in meaning, being specific in the number of teachings it matches according to the collection; the specific prologue is specific in text but general in meaning, narrating generally the number of teachings within the collection; this is in order to cover general and specific comprehensively. When the two prologues are set against each other, their names divide of themselves. In the present sūtra, as for saying that it narrates the past generally: in teaching the Dharma it narrates again the emergence and arising; in emitting light, gradual and sudden are both illumined; only the flowers and the earth specifically signify this scripture, the intent secret and not yet proclaimed; entering concentration, the meaning combines opening and merging; the assembly's joy, its delight and stirring differing from the past, is in terms of the past different from the present; harboring doubt concerns both past and present and hesitates about the future; answering the question narrates broadly the three samenesses and gathers in the whole lifetime. Since the sūtra's intent is far-reaching, its prologue too is unusual; that is why other sūtras simply occasion [their teaching] directly.

As for "At that time": the time when the six auspicious signs were about to be displayed. The ancients said it is the time when the assembly gathered; they did not grasp the purport of the words. Whenever it says "at that time," it always points to the end of the preceding event and the beginning of the following one; here it points to before the display of the six signs, when, as he was about to teach the Immeasurable Meanings Sūtra, the assembly first gathered around him.
