The Three Bodies, the Ten Physicians, and the Four Faiths

This part is the upper section of Zhanran's tenth fascicle. It finishes his notes on the Lifespan chapter, which the Textual Commentary treats at the end of the site's fascicle-009, and then takes up the Distinguishing Merits chapter at the start of fascicle-010. The first half is on the claim that the Buddha announces his extinction but is never extinguished. Zhanran applies this to each of the three bodies in turn, follows the Commentary's use of the four siddhāntas to show that the announcement removes evil and produces good, and then works through the ten kinds of physician in the parable of the good physician, comparing them with the old and visiting physicians and the six flavors of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra. A long answer on the three causes in the Buddha's "sons" leads him to call the Lotus the teaching that sets the great headrope. In the Distinguishing Merits chapter he weighs Guangzhai and Vasubandhu's Treatise on increasing the path and diminishing birth and rejects both, and he argues that the Lotus reaps the harvest that the Nirvāṇa only gleans. The part closes with the four faiths and the five grades, where a remark on upholding the sūtra in place of stūpa and saṅgha becomes a firm warning that beginners must still keep the vinaya.

Again in two: first, the general statement; [next,] from "As the Vimalakīrti Sūtra says" on, the explanation. Ru-Yi Translation

That Kātyāyana section has five phrases in all. The first four phrases are Tripiṭaka in name and shared in meaning; the last phrase is common to the four teachings in name, while its meaning is confined to the gate of the Mahāyāna. Since the concluding text [of that section] extends to the perfect and the distinct, the first four phrases are here concluded as the shared, without involving the perfect and the distinct; that one [last] phrase, though concluded as the shared, still extends to the perfect and the distinct. Here, following the meaning of "extending," it can become the perfect. If one grasps this intent, the various phrases can be understood; hence [the Commentary] cites it briefly.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "dharmas are originally unarisen, so there is nothing to extinguish" (法本不生故無可滅): this is the meaning of quiescent extinction. As for saying "proclaiming extinction": this proclaims quiescent extinction. It is the extinction that extinguishes birth, not the extinction that is identical with birth; the extinction identical with birth is non-extinction, so one should know that this extinction is called non-extinction. The dharma-body constantly abides, without extinction or non-extinction; here "quiescent extinction" corresponds in meaning to proclaiming extinction. From "Why is this?" on, it explains the intent of proclaiming extinction: it speaks of quiescent extinction for those who have not penetrated; if one penetrates quiescent extinction, it points back to birth. Yet if it pointed wholly to birth, there would be no benefit for the indolent; hence extinction must be proclaimed.

Fifth, from "The extinguishing of delusion" on, it next distinguishes the perfect and the distinct. The distinct teaching still belongs to the extinction of impermanence, because in the ten abidings it is the same as the Lesser Vehicle: only after extinguishing the delusions of the three realms does the understanding of conventional wisdom that emerges into the provisional arise, and entering the middle is likewise. Here, in terms of the perfect teaching, it proclaims quiescent extinction. As for "this birth and extinction" and so on: though the distinct and the perfect differ, both are called birth and extinction; though both are birth and extinction, all are [discerned] in terms of the principle-nature. Sixth, "Where the two conditions of delusion and understanding are altogether absent" and so on makes clear the conditions for proclaiming extinction. The intent is: [birth and extinction come] from delusion and from understanding, hence it says "delusion and understanding." Strictly speaking, only understanding is the condition for proclaiming, because the distinct and the perfect both give rise to understanding from delusion.

In the first, the non-extinction of the dharma-body, it says "with regard to its own essence": it does not look to the other bodies, because the other two bodies must look to the dharma[-body]. If essence were set against function, the function would turn out to be extinction.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, non-extinction in terms of the reward-body, in two. First, it restates what was said before: non-extinction is the reward's matching the dharma[-body]; since the dharma[-body] is not extinguished, the reward too is not extinguished. Next, from "Argued strictly" (以理) on, it explains in terms of fact and principle set against each other: first, in terms of principle there is no extinction; next, from "Yet, from the standpoint that where wisdom is present" on, in terms of fact there is extinction. From the side of principle it is non-extinction. In the first, in terms of principle, it says "because the two have met" and so on: it examines delusion and wisdom by four alternatives, again examining in terms of essence: delusion is not even extinguished, and the essence of wisdom destroys nothing. This uses Siṃhanāda's challenge in the Great Sūtra. The challenge says: "If vipaśyanā destroys afflictions, why further cultivate śamatha?" The Buddha questioned him in return: "If you say that wisdom can destroy afflictions, does it destroy them by reaching them, or does it destroy them without reaching them? If it destroys them by reaching them, ordinary beings could destroy them. If it destroys them without reaching them, the first thought-moment should destroy them; if the first thought-moment does not destroy them, the later ones do not destroy them either. If it destroys them both by reaching and by not reaching them, this is not so." Investigating in this way, who has wisdom able to destroy afflictions? As for "jointly, or independently": the Buddha said, "Just as one blind man cannot see forms, so even accompanied by many blind men he cannot see." Insight and concentration, joint and separate, can be understood by the same account. This is the reward-wisdom's inability to extinguish delusion. Next, as for "Yet, from the standpoint that where wisdom is present" and so on: it further settles the matter in terms of fact: wisdom can extinguish delusion, but wisdom is not called extinguished.

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, making clear non-extinction in terms of the response-body: it too looks to the dharma[-body] and the reward[-body]. The reward is in terms of the dharma[-body]; hence for the dharma-body above it said only "with regard to its own essence." Here, the response-body's unceasing benefit of beings is also non-extinction. Hence it says, "being constant and unchanging, respond and function without ever ceasing," because the three bodies match one another. Since the dharma[-body] and the reward[-body] are pervasive, the essence of the response is also pervasive. Capacities are as they are of themselves; even where there are none, the dharma of response is always thus. If it were not so, then even though it explained the perfect and constant, it would be the same as birth and extinction. As for saying "beings themselves being inexhaustible, there is accordingly no extinction": at the time of extinction, beings are in fact not yet exhausted; what does this mean? One should question in return: verify that beings are not yet exhausted, and then there is no extinction. Hence the proclaiming of extinction is for the sake of those who do not conceive the thought that he is hard to encounter, not because beings are exhausted. Hence one knows that the response-body is always present and not extinguished; why only the dharma[-body]? If one does not penetrate, the dharma[-body] and the reward[-body] are also extinguished; why only the response[-body]?

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, making clear that the three bodies are neither born nor extinguished. The dharma-body, as before, does not fall under birth and extinction. The reward-body: wisdom of itself penetrates wisdom, and there is nothing that can extinguish it; wisdom belongs to the active side, and since it is neither born nor extinguished, how could it cause delusion either to be born or to be extinguished? The response-body: because of continuity it is not born; because of continuity it is not extinguished. For the three bodies it says only "neither birth nor extinction" (不生滅): the character "not" runs on to the character "extinction," meaning neither born nor extinguished; it says only 不生滅 for convenience of phrasing. "And so on" (云云): this brings out the intent of the three bodies' being neither born nor extinguished, because they interfuse. Here, on account of a single phrase of text, the response-body's being not extinguished yet proclaiming extinction, it broadly opens the extinction, the non-extinction, and the neither-birth-nor-extinction of the three bodies. If it were not so, birth and extinction would be fixed; how then could it be called the three bodies being neither identical nor separate? If one grasps this intent, then throughout the teachings of a whole lifetime, hearing just a single word proclaiming extinction, one recognizes all the meanings of extinction and non-extinction, and how extinction and non-extinction differ according to how many bodies are included. Only then does one reach the original ground, originally neither born nor extinguished; only then does one reach the extinction and non-extinction in the path of conversion in between and today; because all the trace teachings of in between and today, converted from the original ground, do not go beyond the four alternatives of the three bodies.

The Four Siddhāntas and the Harm of Non-Extinction

Chinese Source

釋不滅有損中為二：初釋唱由，次以四悉帖釋損益，此中且寄應佛以釋。「由此眾生」至「二善損而不生」者，由不唱滅，懈怠之徒真中二善俱不生長，見思已生尚自不斷，別惑未生安能令斷？惑不斷故，唯能損於真中二善。已生未生等具如《止觀》第七記中料簡。

Ru-Yi Translation

In explaining the harm of non-extinction there are two: first, explaining the reason for proclaiming; next, using the four siddhāntas to fit an explanation of harm and benefit. Here, for the moment, it relies on the response-buddha to explain. From "It is on account of this very heaviness of beings' defilement" to "the two kinds of good are impaired and never arise": if extinction is not proclaimed, neither of the two kinds of good, of the real and of the middle, arises and grows in the indolent; views and thought already arisen are not even cut off, so how could the distinct delusions not yet arisen be cut off? Because delusion is not cut off, it only impairs the two kinds of good, of the real and of the middle. "Already arisen," "not yet arisen," and so on are fully as sorted out in the notes on fascicle seven of Calming and Contemplation.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, from "But if extinction is manifested in accordance with the four siddhāntas" to "the ultimate-meaning siddhānta": it uses the four siddhāntas to fit the explanation of proclaiming extinction; therefore proclaiming extinction has the benefit of the four siddhāntas. Because extinction is proclaimed, good arises and evil is extinguished; hence it divides the four siddhāntas to match good and evil. As for "the ultimate-meaning siddhānta extinguishes evil not yet arisen" and so on: the ultimate-meaning siddhānta can see the middle way and can destroy ignorance; therefore ignorance is called evil not yet arisen. As for "the therapeutic siddhānta extinguishes evil already arisen": whenever "therapeutic" is said, it always treats present evil. As for "the mundane siddhānta gives rise to good not yet arisen": desire comes at the beginning and the mind's aim is distant; for example, hearing the Dharma and giving rise to joy, subtle good will arise. Again, the mundane is the aggregates and sense-fields; if the aggregates and sense-fields are transformed, the dharma-body is revealed; therefore the dharma-body is called good not yet arisen. Again, at the time of desire good roots have not yet arisen, and they also arise from this; it is called giving rise to what has not yet arisen. As for "the individual siddhānta gives rise to good already arisen": the real truth, seen from the middle, is called good already arisen; even if it has not yet arisen, it is far from the middle, and following what is near it is called "already." As for "And again: the mundane siddhānta may also extinguish evil already arisen": it explains again from what is near, only removing present reckonings; this can be understood. As for "the therapeutic siddhānta may also extinguish evil not yet arisen": because the path of treatment is long.

Ru-Yi Translation

From "as when the five aggregates of the meditative absorptions" on, it explains the mundane just mentioned. As when one cultivates dhyāna in order to extinguish the evil of desire, the form aggregate arises and can extinguish the aggregates of desire; setting realm against realm is called the mundane (世界, "world-realm"), hence the aggregates of the desire realm are called evil already arisen. By this standard, the formless removes form, emptiness removes the three realms, and the middle extinguishes transformational [birth and death]; with respect to the aggregates of one's present stage, all are called already arisen; cultivating the higher to extinguish the lower is called extinguishing what has already arisen. The therapeutic treats what has not yet [arisen]; the text does not explain it again, but although the name "treatment" is the same, it extends up to equal awakening. In [the account of] the assembly of upāsakas in the Great Sūtra it says: "permanent and impermanent, bliss and non-bliss," and so on, "always delighting in contemplating such gates of treatment." Since these are what King Pure and Stainless Renown uses to treat, they are not near. In the other teachings, cultivating contemplation, the contemplation goes against principle; even if there is some flavor of it, it does not accord with the middle principle; even when expedient contemplation is completed it is still called visualization, let alone when it is not yet completed? Again, seeing these signs, though not yet truly realized, one temporarily sees the two lands through the power of contemplation; only when the three delusions are partially extinguished does one correspond to them permanently, and then it is no longer called visualization. Judging by the earlier explanation of the four siddhāntas, even the ultimate meaning at equal awakening is generally called visualization. The land with residue is shared by great and lesser: those of the two vehicles of the Tripiṭaka and shared [teachings] who have cut off the shared delusions are still named by the original [land] and see it by that standard. As for "'and further seeing the Sahā world filled entirely with bodhisattvas' is the appearance of the land of true reward": this too is other-enjoyment. Relying on this visualization, gradually deepening and gradually completed, entering the stage of the first abiding one sees pervasively and spontaneously, responding and functioning without limit.

Translator's notes

Fascicle and site pages. Units 3–25 comment on the end of the Lifespan chapter, which closes the site's fascicle-009 of the Textual Commentary; from unit 26 on Zhanran follows the Distinguishing Merits chapter at the start of fascicle-010. Lemmas use the site's English. Where the site has "abode" for 住, "abiding" is written in Zhanran's sentences, and where it has "forbearance of non-arising," Zhanran's own sentences use "acceptance of non-arising."

The Kātyāyana passage. The Commentary cites the Vimalakīrti's Kātyāyana section, where Vimalakīrti reinterprets impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self, and cessation (T475; on the site's Vimalakīrti page: "That the Dharma was never, in the first place, kindled, and so now has nothing that needs to be extinguished — this is the meaning of cessation"). Zhanran's lemma in unit 8 reads 法本不生故無可滅, close to but not the same as the site's Chinese 法本不生今則無滅.

Quiescence and illumination. The Necklace Sūtra (T1485) lists 照寂慧 and 寂照慧 among its six kinds of insight and assigns them to equal and wondrous awakening. Zhanran (unit 8) says the Commentary borrows these distinct-teaching names for the perfect teaching.

Siṃhanāda's challenge. The argument in unit 12 about whether wisdom destroys afflictions by reaching them or not reaching them is from the Siṃhanāda chapter of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T374, fascicle 31), including the simile of the blind men.

The upāsakas' gates of treatment. The quotation in unit 15 is from the list of upāsakas at the opening of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T374, fascicle 1), where the leader is named 威德無垢稱王; Zhanran writes 淨無垢稱王.

The ten physicians and the six flavors. "The king's territory is pure, peaceful, and quiet" is from the Siṃhanāda chapter of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T374, fascicle 30). The six flavors in unit 17 (suffering as sour, impermanence as salty, and so on) are from its Tathāgata-Nature chapter (T374, fascicle 4). The four marks of a good physician attributed to the Āgama: source not located in this form.

The three causes. Unit 21 explains why the Commentary bundles all three inherent causes into the "correct cause" for the hundred sons. "The gate of the nonduality of cultivation and nature" is one of the ten nondual gates of Zhanran's Explanatory Notes. Zhanran's image of the Lotus as the "great headrope" (大綱) contrasts it with the detailed meshes (綱目) of other scriptures.

Guangzhai and the Treatise. Units 29–31 weigh Guangzhai's and Vasubandhu's readings of the eight worlds and of "eight births to one birth." Zhanran suspects the translator of the Treatise (T1519/T1520) rather than Vasubandhu, comparing it with the eight or nine consciousnesses of the Mahāyānasaṃgraha. "Wanders and drifts" (遊瀁) quotes the Commentary's verdict on Guangzhai.

Gleaning. The list in unit 31 follows the end of the Kauṇḍinya chapter of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T374, fascicle 40) closely, with abridgment; the sūtra has "fifteen thousand" where Zhanran has "fifty thousand" for the second item. "In the country of Magadha immeasurable people aroused the bodhi mind" is at the end of the Pure Practice chapter (T374, fascicle 20).

Lotus quotations. The sūtra's lines are taken from the site's Lotus page: "I shall now devise a skillful means," "In other lands," "these see my very body," "At times, for the sake of this multitude," "Such is the power of my wisdom," "May I, too, in the age to come," "in exactly this way," "deep heart," and "atimuktaka."

Precepts for practitioners of the perfect teaching. In unit 53 Zhanran answers the objection that if the sūtra replaces stūpa and saṅgha, the precepts too might be dropped. He allows beginners deeply engaged in the four kinds of samādhi a little latitude in minor rules, but otherwise insists on the full vinaya and on the Brahmā Net bodhisattva precepts. A "bird-rat person" (鳥鼠人) is one who belongs to neither side, like a bat.

Doubtful readings. In unit 35, 實位彌丁 is translated as "the more modest the real stage," taking 丁 as an error for 下 "lower," since the Commentary's second reading places the person lower. The phrases 答意 (unit 35) and 問中 (unit 36) may have been transposed in transmission; they are translated literally. In unit 47, 仍本為名準彼而見 is translated literally.

Vairocana. 遮那 (unit 51) is short for 毘盧遮那, Vairocana.
