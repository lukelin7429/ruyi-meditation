The Profound State of the Ten Suchnesses and the Ten Points on the Three Rounds

This part continues Zhanran's notes on the Skillful Means chapter. It opens with his claim that "the true mark of the dharmas" is the root principle of the Lotus and of all the teachings, and then follows the Textual Commentary as it rejects the older readings of the ten suchnesses by Guangzhai, the Northern masters, Master Yao, and Master Chang, and sets out its own four explanations: by the ten dharma-realms, by the buddha-realm, by opening and uniting, and by rank. Zhanran argues that the five questions in the Upadeśa on the Lotus can be read as eighteen phrases that match these four explanations, and he supplies the Āgama and treatise sources behind the four inconceivables. After notes on the verses praising the two wisdoms and on the raising of doubt and the three requests, he turns to the Textual Commentary's ten points of analysis for the extended opening of the three to reveal the one. Here he defends Zhiyi against Guangzhai and Kaishan on whether real śrāvakas exist, against Master Yao on the "thickness" of delusion, and against the older view of how faculties are transformed, and discusses whether bodhisattvas and pratyekabuddhas awaken in the three rounds. The part ends at the sixth point, on comprehension.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, from "the true mark of the dharmas" (諸法) on, explaining the profound state: this is the root principle of the Lotus, the starting point of all the teachings, the key to explaining its meaning, the refuge of beings, the support for arousing the resolve, the functioning essence of provisional devices, the source of delusion and awakening, the root principle of the virtues of the fruit, the full compass of the whole teaching career, and the final end of the Five Periods. Whoever obtains these ten meanings and with them digests the various divergent views will be at ease and without error. This is why it first praises the wisdom that depends [on it], namely the provisional and real of the five buddhas. On what do the provisional and real depend? On what is called the wondrous object. If the object does not match the wisdom, it is not even the Buddha's wisdom; how much less can those who have no object to discuss and simply speak of setting [things] against each other understand this inconceivable wondrous wisdom? This is why it refutes the various masters at length. Next, it establishes [its own view] at length. The Profound Meaning and Calming and Contemplation take this as their master theme; of all the meanings our school uses that embrace much and contain richly, there is none that does not come from this. Fearing that later generations would still lose what they return to, I am earnest and repetitive, because I have personally seen that those who study this teaching are still in the dark about it. So one must reflect on it! So one must reflect on it! If one is deluded about this intent, the paths of the various teachings will of themselves lose their direction, and the streams of the whole teaching career will dry up here.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for Guangzhai's "threefold-times-three" (三三): it means person, teaching, and cause, because in the past there was no meaning of the fruit; the three vehicles each have three, and "since it is a single principle, not empty." As for "the mark of reality": one should say, not empty, therefore real; the non-mark taken as the mark; hence it is called the true mark. As for "among the four ones, it singles out the one principle" (四一之中偏舉理): according to Guangzhai's intent, since he agrees with the older masters, he does not set up the oneness of principle, but only says that principle is the root of the four ones, so that all four are called "one"; this falls short of the present text's saying that it is one of the four. From here up to "the Northern masters" is all Guangzhai's explanation. As for "the bodhisattva teaching taking the six perfections as its substance": Guangzhai too sets up the bodhisattva of the three incalculable eons and takes him as the bodhisattva among the three vehicles, that is, the Tripiṭaka bodhisattva of the present text. In recent times this meaning has been entirely abandoned. What the five hundred compiled must go back to the five parts of India. If [the Lotus] merely merged those who retreated, and the great of themselves returned to the Buddha, so that the path had nothing to do with them, then not only would those of fixed nature be extinguished forever, but the bodhisattva too would be empty talk. As for "and so on" (云云): it should make clear that pratyekabuddhas and śrāvakas take the [twelve] links and the [four] truths as their substance. As for "The object that is the real has four "suchness" terms, broadening this single principle into four": the four, cause, condition, and the rest, are classed as the real object, so with these it discusses the oneness of principle at length.

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Chang explains only in terms of what pertains to the Buddha and sets up only the one real, without the three provisional. Matching [the suchnesses] to the ten powers is not without this principle, but measured against the present four explanations of the buddha vehicle, it does not cover even one explanation completely, because it lacks the ultimate empty, provisional, middle, and so on. Within it, "characteristics" cites in general the marks of the ten powers; next, it matches nature and the rest specifically, with substance as the root. As for the final "taken together" (總): it explains [the power of knowing] what is possible and what is not in terms of the preceding eight powers; that is, with root and branch it gathers together all the preceding nine. As for "The various explanations set forth above are none of them without their own approach": the various explanations can be seen. Measured against the explanation of our school, each obtains a small part of the ten realms and ten suchnesses; hence it says "their own approach" (一途). Though Guangzhai seems to obtain the four noble [realms] himself, he sets up only nine, and moreover splits the nine into five provisional and four real. The Northern [masters] and Master Yao, though they set up all ten, do not divide and classify them, and keep them only within the three vehicles, also without the one real. Yet, gathering the various explanations together: since they allow the three vehicles and the one vehicle, and the three and the one all have the ten, nature, characteristics, and the rest, why do they not speak of the ten of the six destinies? The four noble [realms] are phenomena from cause to fruit; the six destinies are also phenomena and should be likewise. Since cause and fruit are the same, how could the ten meanings be lacking? The four noble [realms] are what illuminates and the six destinies what is illuminated; the ten realms are what is illuminated and the buddha vehicle what illuminates. Hence the Buddha's wisdom illuminates the ten realms and ten suchnesses, fully endowed with the three truths. The words "ultimately equal" and so on make the principle plain; why then [do they make] the buddhas each rely on one approach, so that the Buddha's object and wisdom are incomplete? Our master did not refute them in detail, but refuted Guangzhai in general, saying "they do not accord with principle, nor do they fit the text" and so on. "Principle" means reasoning; "text" means the present text. The first, the text, can be seen. Next, in refuting by principle, it first poses the difficulty with the four and the five among the ten. As for "cause, effect" (因果): cause and condition are the "cause"; fruit and recompense are the "effect." From "If the real belongs" on, it refutes in terms of persons, still following the four and the five; if it followed the present text, it should discuss presence and absence in terms of the ten realms. Next, from "If the meaning does not extend" on, it concludes the refutation. The text says "the dharmas": these must gather in the ten realms, each realm with ten suchnesses, and the true mark is the essence of the realms and suchnesses. If there were only one [realm] and only four [suchnesses], then outside the essence nine [realms] and five [suchnesses] would still be left over; how much less would all ten of the ten be suchness?

Since the Treatise says that each and every dharma has nine aspects, one knows that Guangzhai should not classify them separately by four and five, and the masters should not each rely on three or one. Hence one knows that if one relies simply on the ten realms and ten dharmas, all the schools collapse, let alone the four explanations that surpass past and present. Only by digesting the text in this way does one match the sūtra's purport; where is the point in the masters' splitting it up? This Dharmottara is the author of the Saṃyuktābhidharmahṛdaya. In the Vibhāṣā there is the treatise master Dharmatrāta (法救), from whom the author of the Saṃyuktābhidharmahṛdaya inherited, and he is named after his master. Since it relies on the Great Treatise without dividing [the suchnesses] separately, and in each phrase says "corresponds to the Lotus Sūtra's "suchness of characteristics,"" and so on, even if not detailed, it escapes the faults above.

Third, the explanation proper. First, the ten realms, in ten [steps]: first, stating and listing; next, from "Though dharmas" on, the realms and suchnesses gather the dharmas; third, from "Take, for instance, the hell-realm" on, showing the marks; fourth, from "hence the Abhidharma" on, using the lesser as an example for the great; fifth, from "One should understand" on, judging by principle and analogy that the realms and suchnesses are present; sixth, from "when these are illumined" (若照) on, the judgment. As for "their own respective positions" (自位): although it makes clear the ten realms, each realm with ten, for now, illuminating the realm itself, the nine are provisional and the one is real. Hence it must conclude "within the one is contained the immeasurable": one realm contains ten, each with ten suchnesses, whether self or other, cause or fruit, because they lie in a single mind. Hence it says, "within the immeasurable is contained the one," and "within the one is contained the immeasurable." It cannot be spoken as one, nor as many, nor as provisional, nor as real. Cause and fruit, good and evil, empty and existent, small and great, ordinary and sage, gradual and sudden, opening and uniting, mind and dharmas, the dependent and the principal, one and many freely; all dharmas are thus. Hence one knows that those who discuss the dharma-realm have not exhausted this wonder, and so are confused about the phenomena of the fruit and deluded about the principle of the cause. Seventh, from "If illumined from the standpoint of the six destinies" on, it also refutes Guangzhai: since Guangzhai has no ten realms, it refutes him here on his own terms. Even if one followed the ten realms, each with ten suchnesses, taken individually and distinctly they would be only the conceivable object; how much more Guangzhai, who has only the four noble [realms], with the four and the five differing. Eighth, from "The reason is this" (所以) on, concluding the ranks: first it re-establishes the object, saying "the immeasurable within the one." As for "they are entirely cut off from this principle" and so on: disdaining themselves as having no share, hence "cut off from this principle"; following their thoughts into divergent views, hence "confused by sentiment." Being both cut off and confused, what use is it merely to possess it? As for "the two vehicles" and so on: these are the two vehicles of the two teachings. The three paths are just what they renounce and do not contemplate; avoiding emptiness to seek emptiness, they instead aid a lesser liberation. As for "bodhisattvas" and so on: the Tripiṭaka and shared [teachings] illuminate six [realms], and the distinct illuminates in sequence; hence "does not extend everywhere." All are deluded about their own realm and do not reach the buddha-realm. The words "not being fully clear" still cover the ten grounds; hence here it should point to the grounds of the distinct [teaching] and to [those in] the cause. As for "complete in both the horizontal and the vertical": the immeasurable within the one is horizontal; the immeasurable being the one is vertical; since many and one are mutually identical, it says "complete." Ninth, from "alone" (唯獨) on, citing proof; the concluding intent is as in the text. Tenth, from "above in the Fahua Xuanyi" on, pointing to the fuller treatment, as fully in the text of the Profound Meaning at the end of the wondrous object: "Unfolded, it fills the dharma-realm, and one does not know whence it comes; gathered in, no one knows where it is, and one does not know whither it goes," and in the explanation of dharmas it explains the ten realms and ten suchnesses at length.

This, in the text of the present chapter, is the true mark of the dharmas belonging to the buddha-fruit; in the parable there, it is the adorned great carriage that reaches the site of awakening; in the past lives there, it is the islet where the buddha of the ultimate fruit opens the provisional [to reveal] the real; in the origin gate there, it is the wondrous Dharma to which the buddha who attained long ago accords. If the main section can be recognized, how could one be confused about dissemination? The words "a single phrase, a single verse" are all the more to be trusted, and the teaching of the three virtues and three tracks is as clear as if before one's eyes. If one obtains this intent, expounding broadly for eight years does not go beyond a single thought, and passing fifty minor eons does not move an instant. By analogy one knows that the responses to capacities over a whole lifetime rest in the mind-nature, the buddha-activities of the ten directions are plainly before one's eyes, the faculties and natures of the dharma-realm are surveyed and easily penetrated, and all that is taught according to what is fitting points to the treasury of principle. In the concluding rebuke, the eye is used as a metaphor: taking for the moment the Lesser Vehicle's wisdom-eye that sees emptiness and speaking generously, it says they obtain one eye. Bodhisattvas from the initial abiding up to equal awakening still have ignorance; here it rebukes only the bodhisattvas of the expedient teachings who have not seen the middle; all are like "peering through the dimness of night."

Translator's notes

Quoted phrases. This part comments first on the end of the site's fascicle-003 of the Textual Commentary (the profound state, the four explanations of the ten suchnesses, the verses on the two wisdoms, the raising of doubt) and then, from unit 24, on the opening of fascicle-004 (the ten points of analysis, up to the sixth). Quoted phrases follow the site's English. Three of Zhanran's lemmas, "this fully" (此具), "opening" (離開), and "first opening" (初開, Part 12), do not appear in the site's Chinese; his copy evidently differed, and they are translated literally. In unit 10 his copy read "these ten realms" (十界) where the site's text has "these three virtues" (三德).

Abidings. 住 is rendered "abiding" ("the first abiding," "the initial abiding") throughout.

The four ones. In unit 2 Zhanran explains Guangzhai's 四一 as the four ones (teaching, practice, person, and principle), of which principle is one. His copy has 偏 ("singles out") where some texts read 遍 ("universally").

Dharmottara. Unit 2 identifies the Dharmottara (達磨欝多羅) who matched the Great Treatise's nine aspects to the ten suchnesses as the author of the Saṃyuktābhidharmahṛdaya, named after the earlier master Dharmatrāta (法救). The Taishō text of that treatise (T1552) is attributed to Dharmatrāta. The Profound Meaning (T1716) also cites Dharmottara.

The Upadeśa. The five questions ("What dharmas? How dharmas? Resembling what dharmas? Of what mark? Of what essence?") and their four rounds of answers in unit 10 are from the Upadeśa on the Lotus (T1519). Zhanran counts 5 + 5 + 3 + 5 phrases, the "eighteen phrases" he matches to the four explanations.

The Profound Meaning. "Unfolded, it fills the dharma-realm ." (unit 3) is from the end of the section on the wondrous object in the Profound Meaning (T1716). In unit 18, the Profound Meaning's report of Guangzhai reads "because it is various, one knows it is real"; the context suggests "provisional" was meant, and the second "real" may be a copying error.

The Āgamas. The four inconceivables (unit 11) are in the Ekottarika Āgama (T125), fascicle eighteen, where the Buddha lists the world, beings, dragons, and buddha-lands. The man who sees armies enter a lotus fiber is Saṃyukta Āgama (T99), sūtra 407 in fascicle sixteen, where the pond is named 拘絺羅 (Juchiluo).

The Great Treatise and the Vibhāṣā. The sentence about leaving the ground by four fingers' breadth (unit 16) and "the five destinies each have their own power of what is so of itself" (unit 11) were not located verbatim in the Great Treatise (T1509). The question whether offerings reach ghosts "because this destiny is superior" is discussed in the Vibhāṣā (T1546).

Emperor Wu of the Liang. Unit 15 refers to the rites of offering to the beings of the six destinies associated with Emperor Wu.

The Larger Prajñā. The passage on the full and brief and on bodhisattvas of sharp faculties (unit 24) is from the Skillful Means chapter (no. 69) in fascicle twenty-one of the Larger Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra (T223).

"Four" or "ten." In unit 24, "distinguishing the meanings in four" (義四分別) stands where one would expect "by ten meanings" (十義); the text may be corrupt.

Kaishan's "outer ordinary." Kaishan (Zhizang of Kaishan Temple) held that the śrāvakas, already at the stage of the four abodes of mindfulness, knew the one vehicle; Zhanran answers that knowing at the initial undertaking does not mean there are no real śrāvakas now.

The Four Mindfulnesses. The work cited at the end of unit 33 is Zhiyi's Four Mindfulnesses (T1918).

Wordplay on "world." In unit 17 Zhanran reads the 世 of 世雄 ("Hero of the World") also as "age," so that the line can refer to the buddhas of the three ages.
