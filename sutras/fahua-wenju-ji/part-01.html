The Title, the Divisions of the Text, and the Four Modes of Explanation

This part opens Zhanran's Notes on the Textual Commentary on the Lotus (法華文句記), his phrase-by-phrase subcommentary on Zhiyi's Textual Commentary on the Lotus as recorded by Guanding (Zhang'an). It covers the opening pages of the Textual Commentary's first fascicle: the title "text and phrases," the six difficulties in Guanding's note under the chapter title, the meanings of "prologue" and "chapter," and the history of dividing sūtras into sections — Dao'an's three sections, the schemes of Long of Mount Lu, Xuanchang, and Guangzhai Fayun, and Zhiyi's twofold division of the Lotus into a trace gate and an origin gate, each with its own prologue, main section, and dissemination. Zhanran then explains the four modes of explanation used throughout the commentary: causes and conditions, the teachings, origin and trace, and contemplating the mind. He argues that four is the "middle course," reads causes and conditions as appeal and response, and shows how planting, ripening, and harvesting run through the three sections of the sūtra. Throughout he insists that the Textual Commentary can be read only together with the Profound Meaning, and that every mode of explanation must be tied to the Lotus's own teaching of opening the provisional and revealing the distant origin.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "text and phrases" (文句): "text" (文) means written characters, which run through the whole sūtra from beginning to end; hence it is said that "text is characters," and these are what the [other] two rely on. "Phrase" (句) means the phrase and its pause in reading, and in meaning it covers both long and short; hence it is said, "Names express self-nature; phrases express distinctions." Here we also do not discuss their substance, whether it is form, formations, or the like; now we only divide the text by phrases, and so it is called "text and phrases." Among the chapter-commentaries of old, some were titled only "Commentary" (疏), and some only "Chapters" (章). "Chapter" means a composition of fine wording; the Odes says, "Those gentlemen of the capital, when they speak, their words form a composition (章)." It also means a chapter-section: dividing into sections and explaining them makes so many chapters. "Commentary" (疏) is a word for conveying the intent; it also means a record. Further, read as 疎, it means opening up (疏通), setting out in order (疏條), and carving through (疎鏤). That neither term is used now is for the reason just given.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the meaning of the six difficulties in the note under the title: this uses [the verse] in the round of teaching by Dharma below, where the Dharma is praised as rare: "That buddhas arise and appear in the world is a thing remote and hard to encounter; and even when one does appear in the world, that he should teach this Dharma is harder still. Through immeasurable, countless eons, even to hear this Dharma is hard; and one able to listen to this Dharma — such a person, too, is hard to find." Within the sūtra's two difficulties of hearing the Dharma and being able to listen, Zhang'an opened the meaning into four — transmitting and translating and the rest — making six difficulties in all. Now, hearing the Dharma lay properly in the assembly of that time; and since Ānanda['s transmission] in meaning reaches the semblance and final ages, it is by relying on transmission and translation that one can hear it. A buddha's appearing is already difficult; even when he appears, one may not meet him; hence the sūtra says that through many eons even hearing is difficult — how much more cultivating it, opening into awakening, expounding, recording, and circulating it? Among the six, the first two concern the lord [of the teaching] and the flavor; the third and fourth praise the teaching and praise the practice; the fifth and sixth rejoice in one's own good fortune and examine oneself. Hence the first two lie with the Buddha, the third and fourth belong to the teachers, and the fifth and sixth censure oneself. So in each of the three pairs, the first is general and the second specific. In the first two, appearing in the world is still general, because it covers the whole lifetime of teaching; the next, teaching, is specific, being specific to the present sūtra. In the third and fourth, the first, the teachers who transmitted and translated, covers all the teachings; the next, the teacher who instructed, is specific to the one from whom we received. In the fifth and sixth, hearing the lectures is still general, because it is shared with others; recording after a single hearing is specific, because it belongs to oneself alone. So among the three pairs the scope goes from broad to narrow, and therefore each later one is narrower than the one before. Hence, from the Buddha's appearing down to hearing the one who awakened by himself expound it once and recording it, recording the buddha vehicle is the most difficult of all.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the lord and the flavor": the lord's arising is not easy, and moreover only after passing through four flavors did he expound this sūtra; hence we know that the first two are truly not easy. As for "praising the teaching": transmitting and translating are not easy, crossing mountains and seas; and even after reaching this land, it can be transmitted only when people and ruler are united in the Way and the country is free of calamities. As for "praising the practice": [Zhiyi] received [the teaching] from Nanyue, yet his realization did not come from another; what he had planted in former lives supported him, and his wondrous awakening burst forth. As for "examining oneself": [Zhang'an] rejoices in his own good fortune at having heard, and humbly says he merely recorded it. Hence the preface to the Profound Meaning says: "In Jiangling I was privileged to receive the Profound Meaning; in Jianye I first heard him lecture on the sūtra's text; piecing it together, I managed to hear it through only once." How much more — heard at twenty-seven, put in order at sixty-nine, more than forty years from beginning to end — does this make a seventh difficulty, that of revising and completing it! Adding the difficulty of the compilation before transmission and translation makes eight difficulties, since at the time of the compilation too it relied on the harmonious union of kings, ministers, and the great assembly, so that neither Māra nor outsiders could find an opening. "Revised" (添, adding) means adding refutations of the ancient masters and citations of sūtras and treatises, that is, rejecting the wrong to reveal the right; "polished" (削, pruning) means pruning away the prolix and giving the text its literary form, so that the wording is concise and the meaning rich. Refuting the ancients is not wholly one's own, since it brings in their differing views, so it is called only "adding"; pruning lies solely with oneself, since the first record was overgrown, so it had to be called "pruning." Hence the preface to the Profound Meaning says: "In places I have matched it against the sincere words of sūtra and treatise, to confirm its own depth and subtlety; in places I have set out the differing interpretations of various masters, to show where they fall short of the perfect teaching."

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "that together we may aspire to the Buddha's wisdom": had he not received it in person on the numinous peak and attained accord in awakening at the place of practice, searched out the purport of the teachings of a whole lifetime and revealed the ultimate tenet of the five flavors, what could have served as the foundation for the buddha-wisdom of later worthies? Hence the words "buddha-wisdom" require opening the provisional and the real at the stage of fruition of the three teachings, and bringing out the great and the small that are combined and accompanied in the four flavors; then person, principle, teaching, and practice have somewhere to return, and opening, showing, awakening, and entering are without difference — only this is the buddha-wisdom of the present sūtra.

Why the Title Is Explained at Length and the Text Only in Outline

Chinese Source

「委釋」等者，別解題名方七八卷，以初名中總三法故，三法始末亘一部故。何者？一部之中莫過本迹，本地總別超過諸說，迹中三一功高一期，故一中之三永殊前教，即三之一不與他同，即迹而本壽量方譚，即本之迹具在今說。且如迹中體非因果，依之以辨因果，因果取體方有勝用，如是三法並由開顯。若不先了能開之妙，將何以明所開之麁？故對迹辨本理須分判，所以釋題不可率爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "A detailed exegesis" and so on: the separate explanation of the title takes fully seven or eight fascicles, because the first [of the five], the name, comprehends the three dharmas, and the three dharmas run through the whole sūtra from beginning to end. Why? Within the whole sūtra nothing surpasses origin and trace. In the original ground, the general and the specific surpass all [other] teachings; in the trace, [the opening of] the three and the one is supreme in merit within one lifetime of teaching. Hence the three within the one are forever different from the earlier teachings, and the one identical with the three is not the same as the others. The origin identical with the trace is spoken of only in the Lifespan [chapter]; the trace identical with the origin is fully present in the present teaching. For example, in the trace, the essence is neither cause nor effect, yet on the basis of it cause and effect are distinguished; cause and effect take the essence, and only then is there the superior function. These three dharmas all come from opening and revealing. If one does not first understand the wondrous that opens, how will one make clear the coarse that is opened? Hence in setting the trace against [the origin] to distinguish the origin, one must in principle make divisions; this is why explaining the title cannot be done casually.

Ru-Yi Translation

The separate explanation under the title is in principle not easy; and because the great meaning has been set out in detail in explaining the title, on reaching the sūtra text [Zhiyi] only roughly divides chapters and sections. The title "Textual Commentary" (文句, text and phrases) is truly due to this. Hence if one only divides text and phrases, the great principle is not made manifest; if one speaks only of the profound purport, one is confused about where [passages] begin and end; if the two are mixed together in exposition, neither has a place to return. Therefore, whenever the explanation reaches a great meaning, it always points to the Profound Meaning's name, essence, cardinal doctrine, and function; the three and the one, the general and the specific; [and the explanations] by way of practice and in terms of the teachings. Hence we know that those who are wholly confused about the great purport of the Profound Meaning yet wish to explain the sūtra by text and phrases — firmly blind to the depth and subtlety of the contemplation of principle and explaining the meaning only through phenomenal marks — when they speak of propagating this scripture, they are far off indeed!

Ru-Yi Translation

Now I ask those who propagate the sūtra: is it for fame and profit, which block oneself, or out of great compassion, to benefit others? If one's own practice is dark about the wondrous tenet, how is that different from leading others without eyes? Both are confused; self and others alike drown. Moreover, the proclamation of the wondrous Dharma is not only in the main section, for all twenty-eight chapters are called wondrous; hence within every chapter essence and the rest are all present, and under every phrase the name "wondrous" is generally concluded. Teaching, practice, person, and principle include one another, so that the wondrous purport is not lost and it somewhat resembles propagation. The admonition of the robe, the seat, and the room — reflect on it and restrain yourself. Yet to set out every phenomenal difficulty everywhere cannot always be done; now, following what suits the meaning, [the explanation] is broad or brief as the occasion requires. Hence "Skillful Means," "Peaceful Practices," "Lifespan," and "Universal Gate" are all the source of origin and trace and the pivot of this sūtra; they must be examined in detail, and the rest as is suitable.

The Four Points at the Head of Every Chapter

Chinese Source

「序者」下，一部大名已指前釋，一品別號此下略申。叡公亦有二十八品生起次第，今家隨釋準意可知。然品品初通有四意，謂釋名、來意、釋妨、釋文。釋名必須因緣等四；來意、釋妨或有或無。或釋名中即帶來意，有妨須釋何例然耶？又釋名與義更互有無，故釋名一種，或於義前別釋，或帶名以釋義，縱於義前別釋，還將別以貫義。名義若顯則一品可從，故使品品全同部旨，如是釋者，方鏡於迷途；徒自云云，此彼淆混。若得此意，至下易知。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "As for 'prologue'" on: the great name of the whole sūtra has already been referred to the earlier explanation; the separate title of one chapter is briefly set out from here on. Master [Seng]rui also had [an account of] the order in which the twenty-eight chapters arise; our school explains them as they come, and the intent can be understood by analogy. Now at the beginning of every chapter there are in general four points: explaining the name, the reason for its coming, resolving objections, and explaining the text. Explaining the name must use the four, causes and conditions and the rest; the reason for its coming and resolving objections are sometimes present and sometimes not. Sometimes the reason for its coming is carried within the explanation of the name; when there is an objection it must be resolved — what fixed rule is there? Further, explaining the name and [explaining] the meaning are each present or absent in turn; so, as to the one matter of explaining the name, sometimes it is explained separately before the meaning, sometimes the name is carried along to explain the meaning; and even when it is explained separately before the meaning, the separate [explanation] is still used to thread through the meaning. If name and meaning are clear, the whole chapter can follow; this makes every chapter wholly the same as the purport of the whole sūtra. Only explanation like this is a mirror on the road of confusion; mere talk muddles this and that. If one grasps this intent, what comes below is easy to understand.

Explaining the Name "Prologue"

Chinese Source

今初釋名。云「訓庠序」等者，先釋字訓，次釋字義。然釋訓中復似從義。庠謂安庠，學舍養宮並非今意。《爾雅》云：「東西牆謂之序，別內外也。」此可借用以釋別序。如由別序方異諸經，通序異外亦可兼用，所以初用字訓正從別序，故云階位等也；兼用安庠，即非忽卒越次意也。亦二序共有。若對辨者，必先證信後發起故，必先發起後證信故，必先二序後方正故，二序各有，前後亦爾。故光宅生起，非全失理，但闕表報，是故斥之。故階位之言義兼通別，問答之語不關於通，先瑞後問次第故爾。彌勒、文殊即賓主也，若問若答皆庠序故，伏疑伏難賓主存焉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now first, explaining the name. As for "it is glossed as 'orderly sequence'" and so on: first it explains the gloss of the characters, next the meaning of the characters. Yet the explanation by gloss also seems to follow the meaning. 庠 means "calm and orderly"; [its senses of] a school or a hall for nurturing [the aged] are not the present meaning. The Erya says: "The east and west walls are called 序; they separate inner from outer." This can be borrowed to explain the specific prologue. Since it is by the specific prologue that [this sūtra] differs from all other sūtras, while the general prologue differs from outsiders, it can also be used for both. Hence the first use, the gloss of the characters, properly follows the specific prologue, and so it says "ranks and positions" and so on; using "calm and orderly" as well means not abruptly skipping the order. It is also shared by both prologues. If one distinguishes them in contrast: there must first be the attestation of faith and afterward the occasioning; there must first be the occasioning and afterward the attestation of faith; there must first be the two prologues and only afterward the main [section]; and each of the two prologues likewise has a before and after. Hence Guangzhai's order of arising is not wholly without reason; it merely lacks [the account of] the signs and their answering, and so it is rejected. The words "ranks and positions" in meaning cover both the general and the specific; "question and answer" have nothing to do with the general, because the signs come first and the questions after, in that order. Maitreya and Mañjuśrī are guest and host; since both questioning and answering are orderly, guest and host are present in the subdued doubts and subdued objections.

Of the two prologues the general still crowns the specific: of the two, the general comes first and best earns the name, crowning what follows; to crown is to head, hence it says "placed at the head." Thus only the twenty-seven chapters are properly called the sūtra; [this chapter] is called sūtra because the prologue follows the main [section] in sharing its name, hence it says "Introduction Chapter of the Sūtra of the Lotus Blossom of the Wondrous Dharma."

As for "occasion" and "narration": the auspicious signs and the doubts are the occasion; the questions and answers are the narration. Assembling the audience can also be regarded as a distant occasion, but since it is remote it is set aside for now.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "the introductory passages that lead into the main teaching" (哢引): this makes it clear by a simile. It is the prelude (引) of those who sing (哢), and so it is called the "song-prelude" (哢引). Again, the song proper can also be called 引, just as the meanings of "song-prelude" and "dance-prelude" can be understood. Translator's notes

Quoted phrases. Zhanran comments on the Textual Commentary phrase by phrase. Every phrase he quotes is given in the wording of this site's English translation of Textual Commentary fascicle 1, even where that wording is looser than his reading; where he reads a phrase differently, his reading is explained in the translation or below. The site's Textual Commentary renders 感應 as "stimulus and response"; in Zhanran's own sentences it is translated "appeal and response," and 感應道交 "the mutual resonance between response and appeal."

Lotus quotations. The verse on the six difficulties (unit 4) and the Bhaiṣajyarāja, Gadgadasvara, and Universal Gate chapter endings (unit 20) follow the site's Lotus English (with "eon" for "kalpa"). The site's Lotus names 宿王華 "Bhadravyūhamati," and that name is kept here. The line from the Lifespan chapter in unit 30 ("speaking of himself, or speaking of another") is translated here, as its site wording could not be matched.

Guanding's preface. The lines cited in units 5–6 come from Guanding's preface to the Profound Meaning and follow the site's English of that preface. Zhanran's first citation in unit 5 is a paraphrase that reverses the order of Jiangling and Jianye.

The six (and eight) difficulties. Zhanran counts the "seventh" difficulty as Guanding's own revision of the record over some forty years, and an eighth as the original compilation of the canon.

The Ekottarika preface. The verse dividing the canon (unit 23) and the verse on the six perfections (unit 25) are from the opening verses of the Ekottarika Āgama (T125); the wording in the vault copy is close but not identical.

The Six-Foot treatises. Zhanran's list in unit 25 gives Kātyāyanīputra as author of the Prajñapti-pāda; later tradition usually names Maudgalyāyana. The Great Treatise passage on the eight skandhakas corresponds to T1509, which asks where "the Eight Skandhaka Abhidharma and the six-part abhidharma" came from; Zhanran abbreviates.

The Treatise on the Lotus. The seven accomplishments and five parts (unit 26) follow Bodhiruci's version (T1520). The short glosses inside the quotation (on the Immeasurable Meanings, entering concentration, the emitted light, and asking) are not in T1520 and appear to be Zhanran's own.

Dong'an. Unit 28 cites a Commentary on the Lotus by Dharma Master Kuang of Yong'an Temple, Dongyang (d. 639); the work is lost and the quotation could not be checked. "Folding back three fingers" at death was read as a sign of the third fruit.

"Nine tracks." Sengzhao's saying in unit 46 is traced by Zhanran to Daorong's scheme of nine tracks (九轍) for the Lotus; source not located.

蹄 and 罤. In unit 49 Zhanran argues that the snare in "fish-trap and snare" should be written 罤, not 蹄 ("hoof" or "track"). The CBETA text writes 免 for 兔 ("rabbit") in his quotations of the Cangjie Chapters and Zhuangzi; the translation reads "rabbit."

The five hundred years. The Vinaya-mātṛkā (T1463) does list five centuries in which liberation, concentration, precepts, learning, and giving are "firm." The fivefold five hundred years ending in "quarrels are firm," attributed to the Great Collection, was not found in that wording in T397 (source not located).

The firefly simile. The Larger Prajñā line in unit 70 is not located verbatim; the image of a bodhisattva's prajñā surpassing fireflies is common in prajñā literature.

Hexi. In unit 70 "Hexi" is Daolang of Hexi, an early commentator on the Nirvāṇa Sūtra; the quotation on the Vyākaraṇa is from his lost commentary (source not located). In unit 27 "Hexi" is Feng of Hexi, a different person.

Variant characters. Zhanran's lemma in unit 17 reads 哢引 where the site's Chinese has 弄引; he discusses misreadings of both characters in the cursive script.

Lotus chapter names. Chapter titles in Zhanran's own sentences follow the short forms Introduction, Skillful Means, Parable, Teacher of the Dharma, Jeweled Stūpa, Devadatta, Upholding, Peaceful Practices, Lifespan, Spiritual Power, Bhaiṣajyarāja, Wondrous Sound, Universal Gate, Dhāraṇī, King [Wondrous] Adornment, and Encouragement; the quoted Textual Commentary wording keeps the site's own titles ("Prologue," "Wonderful Sound," "Guanyin," and so on).
