The Translation

15. King Sunetra, Who Ended Theft by Ending Poverty〔和默王本生〕

Chinese Source

聞如是：一時佛在舍衛國祇樹給孤獨園，佛告諸比丘：「昔者有國王號和默，王行仁平，愛民若子，正法治國，民無怨心。其國廣大郡縣甚多，境界熾盛，五穀豐熟，國無災毒，壽八萬歲。和默聖王明令宮中，皇后貴人百官侍者，執綱維臣，教以正法，各理所部。王常慈心愍念眾生，悲其愚惑狂悖自墜，尋存道原喜無不加，哀護眾生如天帝釋，殺、盜、淫泆、兩舌、惡口、妄言、綺語、嫉妒、恚、癡，如此之凶無餘在心。孝順父母敬愛九親，尋追賢者尊戴聖人，信佛、信法、信沙門言，信善有福為惡有殃，以斯忠政十善明法自身執行。重勅后妃下逮賤妾，皆令尊奉相率為善，布告四鎮臣民巨細，皆令帶誦心執修行。「國有貧者，不任窮困，失計行盜，財主得之，將以啟聞。王曰：『爾盜乎？』盜者曰：『實盜。』王曰：『爾何緣盜乎？』盜者曰：『實貧困無以自活，違聖明法蹈火行盜。』王悵愍之，嘉其至誠，恧然內愧，長歎而云：『民之飢者即吾餓之，民之寒者即吾裸之。』重曰：『吾勢能令國無貧者，民之苦樂在我而已。』即大赦其國，出藏珍寶布施困乏，飢渴之人即飲食之，寒者衣之，病者給藥，田園舍宅、金銀珠璣、車馬牛錢恣意所索，飛鳥走獸都及眾蟲，五穀蒭草亦從所好。「自王布施之後，國豐民富相率以道，民無殺者，盜人財物、婬人婦女、兩舌、惡口、妄言、綺語、嫉妬、恚、癡，兇愚之心，寂而消滅，皆信佛、信法、信沙門，信為善有福、作惡有殃。舉國和樂，鞭杖不行，仇敵稱臣，戰器朽于藏，牢獄無繫囚，人民稱善，我生遇哉。天龍鬼神無不助喜，祐護其國，毒害消竭，五穀豐熟，家有餘財，王內獨喜，即得五福：一者長壽，二者顏華日更好色，三者德勳八方上下，四者無病氣力日增，五者四境安隱心常歡喜。「王後壽終，如強健人，飽食快臥，忽然上生忉利天上。其國人民奉王十戒，無入地獄、餓鬼、畜生道中者，壽終魂靈皆得上天。」佛告諸沙門：「時和默王者，吾身是也。」諸沙門聞經皆大歡喜，為佛作禮而去。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard: at one time the Buddha was at Jetavana, in Anāthapiṇḍada's Park in the country of Śrāvastī. The Buddha said to the monks: "Long ago there was a king named Sunetra, who governed with benevolence and fairness, loving his people as his own children, ruling the country by the true Dharma, so that the people held no resentment. His country was vast, its districts many, its borders thriving, its five grains ripening in abundance, free of calamity and harm, and its people lived for eighty thousand years. King Sunetra issued clear instructions within his palace, to the queen, the noble consorts, the hundred officials and attendants, and the ministers who upheld the law, teaching them all the true Dharma, each governing their own charge. The king always held a compassionate heart, grieving for living beings, sorrowful over their foolish delusion and reckless self-destruction, and, seeking always the root of the Way, delighted in adding to it without end, protecting living beings with pity as Śakra himself does. Killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, divisive speech, harsh speech, false speech, and idle chatter, envy, anger, and delusion — none of these evils remained in his heart. He was filial and reverent to his parents, honored and loved his nine classes of relatives, sought out and honored the worthy, revered the sages, believed in the Buddha, believed in the Dharma, believed the words of renunciants, believed that good deeds bring blessing and evil deeds bring disaster — and with this loyal governance and the ten virtues as his clear law, he practiced it in his own person. He commanded further that from his queens and consorts down to the lowliest servant girls, all should honor and follow this teaching and strive together toward goodness, and he proclaimed it to the people of all four garrisons, high and low, that all should recite it in their hearts and carry it out in their conduct. "There was a poor man in the country who, unable to bear his poverty and hardship, resorted in desperation to theft; the owner of the property caught him and reported it to the king. The king said: 'Did you steal?' The thief said: 'I truly stole.' The king said: 'Why did you steal?' The thief said: 'I am truly too poor to support myself, and so, against the clear law of the sage, I walked through fire and committed theft.' The king, moved with sorrow, honored his utter sincerity, and, ashamed within himself, sighed at length and said: 'When the people go hungry, it is I who starve them; when the people go cold, it is I who leave them unclothed.' He said further: 'I have the power to ensure this country has no poor at all — the suffering and joy of my people rest with me alone.' He then declared a great amnesty throughout the country, opened his treasury and gave its jewels to relieve the poor and needy, giving food and drink to the hungry and thirsty, clothing to the cold, medicine to the sick, land, houses, gold, silver, pearls, carriages, horses, oxen, and money to whoever asked for them, and even birds, beasts, and every crawling creature, grain and fodder, were given according to what each desired. "From the time the king began this giving, the country prospered and its people grew wealthy, and together they followed the Way. No one among the people killed, stole another's property, violated another's wife, spoke divisively, harshly, falsely, or idly, or harbored envy, anger, or delusion; such wicked and foolish hearts quietly dissolved away, and all came to believe in the Buddha, believe in the Dharma, believe in renunciants, believe that goodness brings blessing and evil brings disaster. The whole country lived in harmony and joy; the rod and the lash went unused; enemies declared themselves vassals; weapons of war rotted away in storage; the prisons held no prisoners; the people praised him, saying, 'How fortunate we are to have been born in this age!' The gods, dragons, and spirits all rejoiced and helped him, protecting his country; every poison and harm was exhausted, the five grains ripened in abundance, every household had surplus wealth, and the king himself, filled with quiet joy, attained five blessings: first, long life; second, a countenance that grew more radiant day by day; third, virtue and merit renowned in all eight directions, above and below; fourth, freedom from illness, his strength increasing daily; and fifth, peace within all four borders, his heart always joyful. "When the king's life later came to its end, he was, like a strong and healthy man who has eaten his fill and sleeps soundly, suddenly reborn above in the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven. The people of his country, upholding the king's ten precepts, none of them entered the paths of hell, hungry ghosts, or animals; when their lives ended, their spirits all rose to heaven." The Buddha said to the monks: "The King Sunetra of that time was I myself." The monks, hearing this teaching, all rejoiced greatly, bowed to the Buddha, and departed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A rare story in this fascicle where the central act of giving is triggered not by a divine test or a dramatic sacrifice, but by the king's own plain self-examination after sentencing a single thief — his conclusion that poverty itself, not the poor man's character, is the true cause of the crime, is treated as sufficient grounds to empty the entire treasury.

16. The Sūtra of the Four Brahmin Householders四姓經

Chinese Source

佛說四姓經聞如是：一時佛在舍衛國祇樹給孤獨園。是時四姓家遭宿命殃，貧窶尤困，草衣草席，菜糜自供；雖為極困，足不蹈無道之宅，手不執無道之惠，志行清淨，眾邪不能染其心。朝稟暮講，經戒不釋於口，世尊所歎，眾智所敬，雖衣食不供於身口，奉養聖眾，隨家所有菜糜草席，不忽一日。諸沙門曰：「四姓貧困，常有飢色，吾等不可受彼常食。經說沙門一心守真，戒具行高，志如天金，不珍財色唯經是寶，絕滅六飢故誓除饉，何耻分衛而不行乎？」共詣佛所，本末陳之。世尊默然。後日四姓身詣精舍，稽首畢一面坐。佛念諸沙門前所啟事，問四姓曰：「寧日慈施供養比丘不？」對曰：「唯然。舉門日供，但恨居貧，菜糜草席，枉屈聖賢以為默默。」眾祐曰：「布施之行，惟在四意，慈心向彼，悲心追愍，喜彼成度，護濟眾生。雖施微薄，其後所生天上人中二道為常，所願自然，眼色、耳聽、鼻香、口味，身服上衣，心皆欣懌，不懼乏無也。若施葌薄，心又不悅，後得其福，福中之薄。官位七寶，得不足榮；處在薄中，心又慳儉不敢衣食，惴惴恰恰未嘗歡喜，腹飢身寒有似乞人，徒生徒死，無善以自祐也。若施以好，心不懇誠，憍慠自恃，身不供恪，綺求華名，欲遠揚己。後有少財，世人空稱，以為巨億；內懼劫奪，衣常葌薄，食未甞甘，亦為空生空死。比丘未甞履其門，遠離三尊，恒近惡道。惠以好物，四等敬奉，手自斟酌，存意三尊，誓令眾生逢佛昇天，苦毒消滅，後世所生願無不得，值佛生天必如志願也。」此章，別本在《薩和檀王經》後

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the Buddha spoke the Sūtra of the Four Brahmin Householders. Thus have I heard: at one time the Buddha was at Jetavana, in Anāthapiṇḍada's Park in the country of Śrāvastī. At that time there were four brahmin families who had met with misfortune from past lives and were extremely poor and destitute, wearing clothes of straw and sitting on mats of straw, sustaining themselves on plain vegetable gruel; yet, however extreme their poverty, their feet never trod the halls of the unrighteous, their hands never took the gifts of the unrighteous, their will and conduct remained pure, and no wickedness could stain their hearts. Morning and evening they recited and discussed the teaching, the scriptures and precepts never leaving their lips, praised by the World-Honored One and respected by every person of wisdom; though their own clothing and food were insufficient, they still served and supported the community of monks, never once neglecting, in a single day, to offer whatever vegetable gruel and straw mats their household possessed. The monks said among themselves: "These four families are so poor, always with a hungry look about them — we cannot go on accepting their ordinary meals. The scriptures teach that a renunciant should hold single-mindedly to the truth, that the precepts should be complete and one's conduct lofty, one's will like heavenly gold, treasuring nothing of wealth or beauty but the Dharma alone, cutting off entirely the six hungers, vowing to be free of want — how then could we feel any shame in refusing their alms?" Together they went before the Buddha and told him this from beginning to end. The World-Honored One remained silent. Some days later, the four families came in person to the monastery, bowed their heads, and sat to one side. The Buddha, recalling what the monks had said to him earlier, asked the four families: "Have you been giving each day, out of kindness, to support the monks?" They answered: "Yes indeed. Our whole household offers something every day — we only regret that, being poor, our vegetable gruel and straw mats have wrongly troubled the worthy and the sagely, and that we have offered them in such silence." The Blessed One said: "The practice of giving rests on four intentions: a heart of loving-kindness toward the recipient, a heart of compassion following after them in sympathy, a heart of joy at their attainment, and a heart of protection for the relief of living beings. Even if what is given is meager, one who gives with these four intentions will, in lives to come, always be born in heaven or among humans, and whatever they wish for will come naturally — sight, hearing, smell, taste, fine clothing for the body, and a heart always joyful, never fearing want. "But if one gives out of meagerness of heart, and the heart itself is not glad in the giving, the blessing received afterward will be the most meager kind of blessing. Even office and rank, even the seven treasures, will bring no true glory to one in this meager state; the heart, still stingy and grasping, will not dare to enjoy food or clothing, always anxious and uneasy, never once truly joyful, hungry and cold as though begging, living for nothing and dying for nothing, with no true good to rely on. And if one gives something fine but without a sincere heart, proud and self-satisfied, the body not truly reverent in the act, seeking only an outward show and a far-reaching name for oneself — though one may later have a little wealth, and the world may falsely call it a fortune, still one lives in constant fear of being robbed of it, one's clothing always meager, one's food never truly savored — this too is living for nothing and dying for nothing. Monks will never once set foot at such a person's gate; they remain far from the Three Jewels and forever close to the paths of evil. But to give what is fine, with the four immeasurable virtues held in reverence, measuring the gift out with one's own hand, mindful always of the Three Jewels, vowing that living beings may meet the Buddha and rise to heaven, that bitter suffering may be dissolved, that whatever is wished for in future lives may all be attained — meeting the Buddha, being born in heaven — this will certainly be granted, exactly as one has vowed." [This chapter, in a separate edition, appears after the Sūtra of King Sarvadatta.]

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The Buddha's own four-part analysis of giving here — the quality of intention behind a gift mattering as much as, or more than, its material value — reads almost as a corrective gloss on several of the fascicle's other stories, which tend to measure generosity mainly by its scale. Worth noting the source text's own honesty about this chapter's uncertain placement: a marginal note preserved in the Chinese original states that another edition places this chapter after the story of King Sarvadatta (Story 13) rather than here.

17. The Brahmin Vairāmbha, Whose Giving Was Outweighed by a Single Meal〔維藍梵志本生〕

Chinese Source

昔有梵志，名曰維藍，榮尊位高，為飛行皇帝，財難籌算，體好布施：名女上色，服飾光世，以施與人；金鉢盛銀粟，銀鉢盛金粟，澡甕盥槃四寶交錯；金銀食鼎，中有百味；秦水名牛皆以黃金韜衣其角，一牛者日出四升湩；皆從犢子，織成寶服，明珠綻綴；床榻幃帳，寶絡光目；名象良馬，金銀鞍勒，絡以眾寶；諸車華蓋，虎皮為座，彫文刻鏤無好不有。自名女以下至于寶車，事事各有千八十四枚，以施與人。維藍慈惠，八方上下天龍善神無不助喜。「如維藍惠，以濟凡庶，畢其壽命無日疲懈，不如一日飯一清信具戒之女，其福倍彼不可籌算。又為前施并清信女百，不如清信具戒男一飯。具戒男百，不如具戒女除饉一飯。女除饉百，不如高行沙彌一人飯。沙彌百，不如沙門一人，具戒行者，心無穢濁內外清潔。凡人猶瓦石，具戒高行者，若明月珠也；瓦石滿四天下，猶不如真珠一矣。「又如維藍布施之多，逮于具戒眾多之施，不如飯溝港一。溝港百，不如頻來一。頻來百，不如不還一。不還百，不如飯應真一人。「又如維藍前施及飯諸賢聖，不如孝事其親。孝者盡其心無外私。百世孝親，不如飯一辟支佛。辟支佛百。不如飯一佛。佛百，不如立一剎、守三自歸，歸佛歸法歸比丘僧。盡仁不殺，守清不盜，執貞不犯他妻，奉信不欺，孝順不醉，持五戒，月六齋，其福巍巍，勝維藍布施萬種名物，及飯賢聖，甚為難算矣。「持戒不如等心慈育眾生，其福無盡也。雖為菜糜草席，執三自歸，懷四等心，具持五戒，山海可秤量，斯福難籌算也。」佛告四姓：「欲知維藍者，我身是。」四姓聞經，心大歡喜，作禮而去。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a brahmin named Vairāmbha, of the highest and most honored rank, a wheel-turning emperor of the skies, his wealth beyond reckoning, whose nature delighted in giving: famous women of the finest beauty, adorned with garments that outshone the world, he gave away to others; golden bowls filled with silver grain, silver bowls filled with golden grain, washing-vessels and basins of the four treasures interlaid; golden and silver cooking-vessels holding a hundred flavors; fine cattle from Qin, each with its horns sheathed in gold, each giving four pints of milk a day, together with their calves, woven into treasured garments studded with bright pearls; couches, curtains, and canopies gleaming with jeweled strands; famous elephants and fine horses saddled and bridled in gold and silver, adorned with every treasure; carriages with flowered canopies, seats of tiger skin, carved and engraved with every fine ornament — from the famous women down to the jeweled carriages, each kind of gift numbered one thousand and eighty-four, and all of it he gave away to others. Vairāmbha's kindness and giving moved gods, dragons, and good spirits in all eight directions, above and below, to rejoice and help him. "Yet as great as Vairāmbha's giving was, relieving the common multitude for his entire lifetime without a single day's weariness, it does not compare to feeding, for even one day, a single laywoman who keeps the precepts fully — the blessing of that single meal outweighs his giving beyond all reckoning. And feeding a hundred such laywomen does not compare to feeding, for one meal, a single layman who keeps the precepts fully. Feeding a hundred such laymen does not compare to feeding, for one meal, a single nun free of faults. Feeding a hundred such nuns does not compare to feeding one novice monk of lofty conduct. Feeding a hundred such novices does not compare to feeding a single monk whose precepts are complete and whose conduct is pure inside and out, free of all defilement. Ordinary people are like tile and stone; one of complete precepts and lofty conduct is like a moon-bright pearl — tile and stone might fill the whole world, and still not equal a single true pearl. "And great as Vairāmbha's giving was, even reaching the level of feeding the many who keep the precepts fully, it does not compare to feeding a single stream-enterer. A hundred stream-enterers do not compare to a single once-returner. A hundred once-returners do not compare to a single non-returner. A hundred non-returners do not compare to feeding a single arhat. "And even Vairāmbha's earlier giving, and feeding all these sages and worthies, does not compare to serving one's own parents with filial devotion. True filial devotion holds nothing back and harbors no private selfishness. A hundred lifetimes of filial devotion to one's parents does not compare to feeding a single pratyekabuddha. A hundred pratyekabuddhas do not compare to feeding a single buddha. A hundred buddhas do not compare to establishing a single stūpa, or to holding to the three refuges — taking refuge in the Buddha, in the Dharma, and in the community of monks. To hold fully to kindness and not kill; to hold to purity and not steal; to hold to chastity and not violate another's spouse; to hold to trustworthiness and not deceive; to be filial and reverent and not become intoxicated; to keep the five precepts and observe the six fast days each month — this blessing towers higher, surpassing even Vairāmbha's ten thousand kinds of famous gifts and his feeding of every worthy and sage, and is exceedingly difficult to reckon. "And keeping the precepts does not compare to nurturing all living beings with an equal, compassionate heart — that blessing is inexhaustible. Even if one's food is only vegetable gruel and one's seat only a straw mat, if one holds to the three refuges, embraces the four immeasurable minds, and keeps the five precepts fully, that blessing can outweigh the mountains and seas — it is beyond all reckoning." The Buddha said to the four families: "If you wish to know who Vairāmbha was — I myself am he." The four families, hearing this teaching, rejoiced greatly in their hearts, bowed, and departed.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A story built as an extended ranking, moving in careful stages from the most extravagant material giving imaginable down to something much smaller and, by the story's own logic, more valuable: a single sincere meal offered to a fully precepted renunciant. The ranking culminates not in some further act of giving at all, but in filial devotion to one's own parents and the keeping of the five precepts — a deliberate reversal of the story's own opening emphasis on material abundance.

18. The Deer King Who Offered Himself to the Royal Kitchen〔鹿王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩身為鹿王，厥體高大，身毛五色，蹄角奇雅，眾鹿伏從數千為群。國王出獵，群鹿分散，投巖墮坑，盪樹貫棘，摧破死傷所殺不少。鹿王覩之，哽噎曰：『吾為眾長，宜當明慮擇地而遊，苟為美草而翔於斯，凋殘群小，罪在我也。』徑自入國，國人覩之，僉曰：『吾王有至仁之德，神鹿來翔。』以為國瑞，莫敢干之。乃到殿前，跪而云曰：『小畜貪生，寄命國界。卒逢獵者，蟲類奔迸，或生相失，或死狼籍。天仁愛物，實為可哀。願自相選，日供太官，乞知其數，不敢欺王。』王甚奇曰：『太官所用日不過一，不知汝等傷死甚多。若實如云，吾誓不獵。』「鹿王退還，悉命群鹿，具以斯意示其禍福。群鹿伏聽，自相差次。應先行者每當就死，過辭其王，王為泣涕，誨喻之曰：『覩世皆死，孰有免之？尋路念佛，仁教慈心，向彼人王慎無怨矣！』日日若茲。中有應行者而身重胎，曰：『死不敢避，乞須㝃娠。』更取其次，欲以代之。其次頓首泣涕而曰：『必當就死。尚有一日一夜之生、斯須之命，時至不恨。』「鹿王不忍枉其生命，明日遁眾，身詣太官。廚人識之，即以上聞。王問其故，辭答如上。王愴然為之流淚曰：『豈有畜獸懷天地之仁殺身濟眾，履古人弘慈之行哉！吾為人君，日殺眾生之命，肥澤己體。吾好兇虐，尚犲狼之行乎？獸為斯仁，有奉天之德矣。』王遣鹿去還其本居，勅一國界：『若有犯鹿者與人同罰。』「自斯之後，王及群寮率化，黎民遵仁不殺，潤逮草木，國遂太平。菩薩世世危命濟物，功成德隆，遂為尊雄。」佛告諸比丘：「時鹿王者，是吾身也。國王者，舍利弗是。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was born as the king of the deer, his body tall and grand, his fur of five colors, his hooves and antlers of rare and elegant form; thousands of deer submitted to him and followed as his herd. When the king went out hunting, the herd scattered in every direction, plunging over cliffs, falling into pits, crashing through trees, and being pierced by thorns, so that many were injured or killed. Seeing this, the deer king choked back tears and said: "I am the leader of this herd, and I ought to have chosen our grazing ground with clear judgment; instead, for the sake of sweet grass, I let us wander into this place, and now the young and weak among the herd are broken and destroyed — the fault is mine." He went straight into the city, and the people, seeing him, said together: "Our king's virtue has reached the highest benevolence, and a divine deer has come to us" — and they took it as an omen of good fortune for the kingdom, and no one dared harm him. He came before the throne and, kneeling, said: "This lowly beast clings to life like any other, entrusting its fate to the bounds of your kingdom. We suddenly met your hunters, and our kind scattered in flight — some lost their families, some died in disorder. Heaven's own kindness extends to all creatures — this is truly a pitiable thing. I beg to select from among ourselves, and offer one deer daily to your royal kitchen, so that you may know our exact number, and we will not dare deceive the king." The king, greatly astonished, said: "The royal kitchen uses no more than one deer a day — I did not know that so many of your kind were dying. If it is truly as you say, I vow never to hunt again." "The deer king returned and summoned the whole herd, explaining fully this plan and its consequences, both fortune and misfortune. The herd listened obediently, and arranged among themselves an order of succession. Whoever's turn it was to go would, before facing death, come and take leave of the king; the king wept for each of them, and taught them, saying: 'Every being in this world must die — who has ever escaped it? As you go, hold the Buddha in mind, and toward that human king, be careful never to harbor resentment!' Day after day it went on like this. Among them there was one whose turn had come but who was heavy with young, and she said: 'I do not dare avoid death, but I beg to wait until after I have given birth.' Another deer was chosen to go in her place; but that next deer, bowing his head and weeping, said: 'I too must go to my death without complaint. But I still have one day and one night of life left, however brief — when the time truly comes, I will hold no regret.' "The deer king, unable to bear seeing this deer's life taken unjustly out of turn, went the next day of his own accord, quietly leaving the herd, and presented himself at the royal kitchen. The kitchen staff recognized him and reported this at once to the king. The king asked the reason, and the deer king answered exactly as before. The king, deeply moved, wept and said: 'How could it be that a mere beast should hold within it the benevolence of heaven and earth, risking its own life to save the multitude, walking the very path of ancient sages' great compassion! I, who am a ruler of men, kill living beings' lives every day to fatten my own body. I delight in cruelty and violence — am I not conducting myself like a jackal or a wolf? This beast holds such benevolence, honoring the very virtue of heaven itself.' The king sent the deer king back to his herd, and commanded throughout the kingdom: 'Whoever harms one of these deer shall be punished as though he had harmed a human being.' "From that time on, the king and all his officials led the people to follow this transformation, and the people upheld benevolence and refrained from killing, its grace extending even to the grass and trees, and the kingdom at last knew great peace. The bodhisattva, life after life, risked his own life to save other beings, and through this his merit was accomplished and his virtue grew ever greater, until at last he became the most honored and heroic of all." The Buddha said to the monks: "The deer king of that time was I myself. The king was Śāriputra." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

One of the best-known images in the wider Jātaka tradition across Buddhist cultures, appearing in variant forms well beyond this specific telling: a deer king who negotiates a sustainable arrangement with a hunting king, then breaks that arrangement's own logic by offering himself in place of a pregnant doe whose turn has come. The king's transformation — from a hunter who did not know his own hunting killed far more than he realized, to a ruler who forbids harming deer entirely — happens in a single conversation, with no gradual arc.

19. The Swan Who Fed Her Chicks with Her Own Flesh〔鵠鳥本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩身為鵠鳥，生子有三。時，國大旱，無以食之，裂腋下肉以濟其命。三子疑曰：『斯肉氣味與母身氣相似無異，得無吾母以身肉飡吾等乎？』三子愴然有悲傷之情。又曰：『寧殞吾命，不損母體也。』於是閉口不食。母覩不食而更索焉。天神歎曰：『母慈惠難喻，子孝希有也。』諸天祐之，願即從心。」佛告諸比丘：「鵠母者，吾身是也。三子者，舍利弗、目連、阿難是也。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」此章，別本在〈維藍章〉後

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was born as a swan, and had three chicks. At that time there was a great drought in the country, and she had nothing with which to feed them, so she tore open the flesh under her own wing to sustain their lives. The three chicks, growing suspicious, said to one another: "The taste and smell of this flesh is no different from our mother's own body — could it be that our mother is feeding us with the flesh of her own body?" The three chicks were struck with grief and sorrow, and said: "We would rather lose our own lives than harm our mother's body," and so they closed their beaks and would not eat. Their mother, seeing them refuse to eat, offered it to them again. A heavenly spirit, moved, said: "A mother's kindness and giving is beyond description, and her children's filial devotion is a rare thing indeed." The gods gave their blessing, and every wish was granted exactly as hoped." The Buddha said to the monks: "The mother swan was I myself. The three chicks were Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, and Ānanda." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave. [This chapter, in a separate edition, appears after the chapter on Vairāmbha.]

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The shortest story in this fascicle, and one of only two stories so far framed entirely around filial devotion moving in the child-to-parent direction rather than a bodhisattva's own giving to strangers. The chicks' refusal to eat, once they suspect the source of their food, gives this story a different emotional register than the fascicle's other self-sacrifice stories: here the sacrifice is nearly refused by its own intended beneficiaries. A marginal note in the source records this chapter's placement as disputed between editions, appearing elsewhere directly after the Vairāmbha story (Story 17).

20. The Peacock King Who Healed a Whole Kingdom〔孔雀王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為孔雀王，從妻五百，委其舊匹，欲青雀妻。青雀唯食甘露好菓，孔雀為妻日行取之。其國王夫人有疾，夢覩孔雀，云其肉可為藥。寤以啟聞，王命獵士疾行索之。夫人曰：『誰能得之，娉以季女，賜金百斤。』「國之獵士分布行索，覩孔雀王從一青雀在常食處，即以蜜󰉓每處塗樹，孔雀輙取以供其妻。射師以󰉓塗身尸踞，孔雀取󰉓，人應獲焉。孔雀曰：『子之勤身，必為利也。吾示子金山，可為無盡之寶，子原吾命矣。』人曰：『大王賜吾金百斤，妻以季女；豈信汝言乎？』即以獻王。「孔雀曰：『大王懷仁，潤無不周，願納微言，乞得少水，吾以慈呪，服之疾即愈矣；若其無效，受罪不晚。』王順其意，夫人服之，眾疾皆愈，華色煒曄，宮人皆然。舉國歎王弘慈，全孔雀之命，獲延一國之壽。雀曰：『願得投身于彼大湖，并呪其水，率土黎民，眾疾可愈。若有疑望，願以杖捶吾足。』王曰：『可。』雀即呪之。國人飲水，聾聽盲視，瘖語𤹪申，眾疾皆然。「夫人疾除，國人竝得無病，無有害孔雀之心。雀具知之，向王陳曰：『受王生潤之恩，吾報濟一國之命。』報畢乞退，王曰：『可。』雀即翔飛昇樹重曰：『天下有三癡。』王曰：『何謂三？』『一者吾癡，二者獵士癡，三者大王癡。』王曰：『願釋之。』雀曰：『諸佛重戒以色為火，燒身危命之由也。吾捨五百供養之妻，而貪青雀，索食供之有如僕使，為獵網所得，殆危身命。斯吾癡也。獵士癡者，吾至誠之言，捨一山之金，棄無窮之寶，信夫人邪偽之欺，望季女之妻，覩世狂愚皆斯類矣。捐佛至誠之戒，信鬼魅之欺，酒樂婬亂，或致破門之禍，或死入太山其苦無數，思還為人，猶無羽之鳥欲飛昇天，豈不難哉？婬婦之妖喻彼䰡𩲐，亡國危身靡不由之，而愚夫尊之；萬言無一誠也，而射師信之。斯謂獵者愚矣。王得天醫除一國疾，諸毒都滅，顏如盛華，巨細欣賴而王放之。斯謂王愚矣。』」佛告舍利弗：「孔雀王自是之後，周旋八方輙以神藥，慈心布施，愈眾生病。孔雀王者，吾身是。國王者。舍利弗是。獵士者，調達是。夫人者。調達妻是也。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was the king of the peacocks, with five hundred peacock wives, but he set aside his own former mate and desired instead a blue sparrow-hen as his wife. The blue sparrow ate only sweet dew and fine fruit, and the peacock king went out each day to gather it for her. The queen of that country's king fell ill, and dreamed she saw a peacock, and was told in the dream that its flesh could serve as medicine. Waking, she reported this, and the king ordered his hunters to go at once and seek it out. The queen said: "Whoever can capture it, I will give him my youngest daughter in marriage, and reward him with a hundred catties of gold." "The kingdom's hunters spread out to search, and found the peacock king together with a single blue sparrow at their usual feeding place; they smeared honey on the trees at that spot, and the peacock, as always, gathered it for his wife. The hunter smeared honey on his own body and lay hidden, still as a corpse; when the peacock came to take the honey, the man seized him. The peacock said: 'Your labor and effort must surely be for some gain. Let me show you a mountain of gold, an inexhaustible treasure — spare my life in return.' The man said: 'The great king has promised me a hundred catties of gold and his youngest daughter in marriage — why should I believe your words?' and he presented the peacock to the king. "The peacock said: 'The great king holds benevolence in his heart, and his grace reaches everywhere without exception — I beg you to accept my humble words. Let me have a little water, and I will bless it with a compassionate incantation; drink it, and the illness will be cured at once. If it has no effect, it will not be too late to punish me then.' The king followed his suggestion; the queen drank it, and every illness was cured, her complexion glowing radiant, and the palace women likewise. The whole kingdom praised the king's vast compassion in sparing the peacock's life, through which the lives of the entire kingdom were extended. "The peacock then said: 'I wish to be allowed to go to that great lake, and bless its waters with my incantation as well, so that the common people of the realm may have their many illnesses cured. If you have any doubt of this, strike my foot with a rod as a guarantee.' The king said, 'Very well.' The peacock then blessed the lake. The people of the kingdom who drank of it — the deaf could hear, the blind could see, the mute could speak, the crippled could stand upright — every affliction was healed in this way. "With the queen's illness cured and the people of the kingdom all free of sickness, no one bore any thought of harming the peacock. The peacock, knowing all of this, said to the king: 'I have received the king's own life-giving grace, and I have repaid it by relieving the lives of this whole kingdom.' Having repaid his debt, he asked leave to depart, and the king said, 'You may go.' The peacock then took flight and rose into a tree, and said again: 'There are three fools in this world.' The king asked, 'What are the three?' 'The first is my own folly; the second is the hunter's folly; the third is the great king's folly.' The king said, 'Please explain.' The peacock said: 'The buddhas' weighty precepts teach that lust for beauty is fire itself, the very cause of a body burned and a life endangered. I abandoned my own five hundred devoted wives out of greed for a blue sparrow, gathering food for her as though I were her servant, and so was caught in the hunter's net, nearly losing my life. This is my own folly. As for the hunter's folly: my words to him were utterly sincere, and yet he gave up an entire mountain of gold, cast aside inexhaustible treasure, and instead believed the queen's false and deceitful promise, hoping only for her youngest daughter as his wife — the world's foolish and reckless people are all of this same kind. To abandon the Buddha's own sincere precepts and believe instead the deceptions of ghosts and demons, indulging in wine, music, and lechery, sometimes bringing ruin upon one's whole household, sometimes dying and falling into the hell of Mount Tai with immeasurable suffering, and then wishing to be reborn human again — this is like a wingless bird wishing to fly up to heaven. Is this not difficult indeed? The seductive charm of a wicked woman is like a hidden trap — the ruin of kingdoms and the endangering of lives so often come from just this, and yet foolish men still honor and desire it; ten thousand of her words hold not a single truth, and yet the hunter believed them. This is why I call the hunter a fool. As for the king: having obtained a heavenly physician who cured the sickness of an entire kingdom, dispelling every poison, restoring faces to full bloom, bringing joy to young and old alike — and yet the king lets him go free. This is why I call the king a fool.'" The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "From that time on, the peacock king traveled throughout the eight directions, always using his divine medicine and compassionate heart to give freely and heal the sicknesses of living beings. The peacock king was I myself. The king was Śāriputra. The hunter was Devadatta. The queen was Devadatta's wife." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This story's peacock king closes with an unusual move for this fascicle: rather than the Buddha simply identifying the story's characters at the end, the peacock itself delivers a three-part moral lecture on folly — his own, the hunter's, and the king's — naming a specific vice for each (lust, credulity, and ingratitude) before the Buddha's own brief identification of the characters. This makes it one of the more explicitly didactic stories translated so far, closer to a fable with its moral stated outright than to a story left to speak for itself.

21. The Rabbit Who Threw Himself on the Fire〔兔王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者梵志，年百二十，執貞不娶婬泆窈盡，靖處山澤不樂世榮，以茅草為廬、蓬蒿為席，泉水山果趣以支命，志弘行高，天下歎德。王娉為相，志道不仕，處于山澤數十餘載，仁逮眾生禽獸附恃。時，有四獸，狐、獺、猴、兔，斯四獸曰：『供養道士，靖心聽經。』積年之久，山菓都盡，道士欲徙尋果所盛。四獸憂曰：『雖有一國榮華之士，猶濁水滿海，不如甘露之斗升也。道士去者，不聞聖典，吾為衰乎！各隨所宜求索飲食，以供道士，請留此山，庶聞大法。』僉然曰：『可。』獼猴索果，狐化為人，得一囊󰉓，獺得大魚，各曰：『可供一月之粮。』兔深自惟：『吾當以何供道士乎？』曰：『夫生有死，身為朽器，猶當棄捐；食凡夫萬，不如道士一。』即行取樵然之為炭，向道士曰：『吾身雖小，可供一日之粮。』言畢即自投火，火為不然。道士覩之，感其若斯，諸佛歎德，天神慈育。道士遂留，日說妙經，四獸稟誨。」佛告諸沙門：「梵志者，錠光佛是也。兔者，吾身是也。獼猴者，秋鷺子是也。狐者，阿難是也。獺者，目連是也。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a brahmin, a hundred and twenty years old, who held to chastity and never married, cutting off entirely all lustful desire, dwelling quietly among the mountains and marshes, taking no delight in worldly glory, building his hut of thatch and his mat of coarse weeds, sustaining his life on spring water and mountain fruit, his resolve vast and his conduct lofty, praised throughout the world for his virtue. A king wished to make him his minister, but, devoted to the Way, he would not take office, and had dwelt in the mountains and marshes for several decades, his benevolence reaching even to the birds and beasts, who came to rely on him. At that time there were four animals — a fox, an otter, a monkey, and a rabbit — and these four animals said to one another: "Let us support this ascetic, and quietly listen to his teaching." For years this continued, until at last the mountain's fruit was entirely exhausted, and the ascetic wished to move on in search of a place where fruit was plentiful. The four animals, distressed, said: "Though there be a man of worldly glory and honor in some kingdom, he is like muddy water filling the sea — not worth even a single measure of sweet dew. If the ascetic leaves, we will no longer hear the sacred teachings — how impoverished we will be! Let each of us seek out food according to our own ability, to support the ascetic, and beg him to remain on this mountain, that we may go on hearing the great Dharma." They all agreed together, "Yes, let us do this." "The monkey gathered fruit; the fox transformed himself into a man and obtained a bag of dried meat; the otter caught a great fish; each said, 'This can supply a month's provisions.' The rabbit thought deeply to himself: 'What can I offer to support the ascetic?' and said: 'All that lives must die; this body is but a fragile vessel, destined to be cast aside in the end. To feed ten thousand ordinary men is not worth feeding a single ascetic.' He went at once and gathered firewood and made it into charcoal, and said to the ascetic: 'Though my body is small, it can supply one day's provisions.' Having said this, he threw himself into the fire at once — but the fire would not burn him. The ascetic, witnessing this, was deeply moved by such devotion; the buddhas praised his virtue, and the heavenly spirits nurtured him with compassion. The ascetic then remained, and each day expounded the wondrous teaching, and the four animals received his instruction." The Buddha said to the monks: "The ascetic was the Buddha Dīpaṃkara. The rabbit was I myself. The monkey was Śāriputra. The fox was Ānanda. The otter was Maudgalyāyana." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This is the fascicle's version of a story sometimes called "the rabbit in the moon" in other Buddhist retellings across Asia, where the rabbit's self-sacrifice becomes an origin story for a mark visible on the moon's surface — a detail this particular telling does not include, ending instead simply with the fire refusing to burn him. The ascetic's identity as the future Buddha Dīpaṃkara connects this story directly to Story 24 later in this same fascicle, where Dīpaṃkara appears again in a different configuration of characters.

22. The Householder Who Turned a Dead Rat into a Fortune〔理家本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為大理家，積寶齊國，常好濟貧惠逮眾生，受一切歸猶海含流。時，有友子，以泆蕩之行，家賄消盡。理家愍焉，教之曰：『治生以道，福利無盡。』以金千兩給子為本。對曰：『敬諾。不敢違明誨。』即以行賈。性邪行嬖，好事鬼妖，婬蕩酒樂，財盡復窮。如斯五行𣩠盡其財，窮還守之。時，理家門外糞上有死鼠，理家示之曰：『夫聰明之善士者，可以彼死鼠治生成居也。有金千兩而窮困乎？今復以金千兩給汝。』「時有乞兒，遙聞斯誨愴然而感，進猶乞食，還取鼠去，循彼妙教，具乞諸味，調和炙之，賣得兩錢；轉以販菜，致有百餘。以微致著，遂成富姓。閑居憶曰：『吾本乞兒，緣致斯賄乎！』寤曰：『由賢理家訓彼兒頑，吾致斯寶。受恩不報，謂之背明。』作一銀案，又為金鼠，以眾名珍滿其腹內，羅著案上。又以眾寶瓔珞其邊，具以眾甘，禮彼理家，陳其所以：『今答天潤。』理家曰：『賢哉，丈夫！可為教訓矣。』即以女妻之，居處眾諸都以付焉，曰：『汝為吾後，當奉佛三寶，以四等心救濟眾生。』對曰：『必修佛教矣。』後為理家之嗣，一國稱孝。」佛告諸沙門：「理家者，吾身是也。彼蕩子者，調達是。以鼠致富者，槃特比丘是。調達懷吾六億品經，言順行逆，死入太山地獄。槃特比丘，懷吾一句，乃致度世。夫有言無行，猶膏以明自賊，斯小人之智也。言行相扶，明猶日月，含懷眾生成濟萬物，斯大人之明也。行者是地，萬物所由生矣。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was a great householder, whose accumulated wealth rivaled that of the whole country, and who always delighted in relieving the poor, his kindness reaching every living being, receiving all who came to him just as the sea receives every river that flows into it. At that time, a friend's son, through dissolute and reckless conduct, had exhausted his entire family fortune. The householder, taking pity on him, taught him, saying: "Make your living through honest means, and its benefit will be inexhaustible," and gave him a thousand taels of gold as capital. The young man answered, "I will honor this respectfully, and dare not disobey your clear instruction," and set out at once to trade. But by nature he was corrupt and self-indulgent, fond of ghosts and demons and their strange rites, given over to lechery, wine, and pleasure, and once again his wealth was entirely exhausted, and he returned to poverty just as before. In this way he squandered his entire fortune five times over, and each time he returned to the householder in his poverty. One day, outside the householder's gate, there lay a dead rat upon a dungheap; the householder pointed it out to him and said: "A truly clever and capable man could make his fortune even from that dead rat there. And yet you, given a thousand taels of gold, remain in poverty? Here — I will give you another thousand taels." "At that moment, there was a beggar child nearby who overheard this teaching and was deeply moved; he came forward as if to beg for food, but instead picked up the dead rat and took it away. Following the wisdom of what he had overheard, he begged for various seasonings, roasted the rat and prepared it, and sold it for two coins; with this he began selling vegetables, and in time accumulated over a hundred coins. From this small beginning he grew steadily, until at last he became a wealthy man. Living at ease one day, he reflected: 'I was once a beggar child — how did I come to this wealth?' And he realized: 'It was through the wise householder's teaching of that foolish, careless young man that I gained this fortune. To receive such a kindness and not repay it would be to turn my back on this insight.' He had a silver tray made, and upon it a golden rat, its belly filled with every kind of famous treasure, set out upon the tray, and further adorned all around with strings of precious gems, and, together with every kind of delicacy, went to pay his respects to the householder, explaining the whole reason: 'I have come today to repay the grace of heaven's own blessing upon me.' The householder said: 'How admirable you are, a true man among men! You may well receive further instruction.' He gave him his own daughter in marriage at once, and entrusted all his household affairs and dwellings to him, saying: 'As my heir, you must honor the Buddha and the Three Jewels, and use the four immeasurable minds to relieve and save living beings.' He answered, 'I will certainly practice the Buddha's teaching.' He later became the householder's heir, and the whole country praised his filial devotion." The Buddha said to the monks: "The householder was I myself. The dissolute young man was Devadatta. The one who grew wealthy through the rat was the monk Panthaka. Devadatta, though he holds within him six hundred million verses of my teaching, speaks in accordance with it while acting against it, and at death will fall into the hell of Mount Tai. The monk Panthaka, though he holds only a single line of my teaching, has thereby crossed beyond this world entirely. To have words without corresponding action is like using oil to light one's own destruction — this is the wisdom of the petty man. But when word and deed support one another, bright as the sun and moon, embracing living beings and relieving all things — this is the wisdom of the great man. Right conduct is the very ground from which the ten thousand things are born." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A story notably free of any divine tester, tragic sacrifice, or mortal danger — its entire arc is an economic parable about capital, judgment, and honest effort, closer in spirit to a folktale than to the fascicle's more typical pattern of extreme self-giving. The closing identification is unusually pointed: Devadatta, despite reciting an enormous quantity of the Buddha's own teaching, is condemned for the gap between his words and his conduct, while the historical monk Panthaka — traditionally remembered as slow-witted and able to retain only a single verse — is praised for the opposite reason.

23. The Lone Mother Reborn as a Hundred Sons〔國王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔有獨母為理家賃，守視田園。主人有徨，餉過食時。時至欲食，沙門從乞。心存斯人，絕欲棄邪厥行清真。『濟四海餓人不如少惠淨戒真賢者。』以所食分盡著鉢中，蓮華一枚著上貢焉！道人現神足放光明，母喜歎曰：『真所謂神聖者乎！願我後生百子若茲。』母終神遷，應為梵志嗣矣。其靈集梵志小便之處，鹿𦧧小便即感之生。時滿生女，梵志育焉。年有十餘，光儀庠步，守居護火。女與鹿戲，不覺火滅。父還恚之，令行索火。女至人聚，一躇步處一蓮華生。火主曰：『爾遶吾居三匝，以火與爾。』女即順命，華生陸地圍屋三重；行者住足，靡不雅奇。「斯須宣聲聞其國王，王命工相相其貴賤。師曰：『必有聖嗣，傳祚無窮。』王命賢臣娉迎禮備，容華奕奕，宮人莫如。懷妊時滿，生卵百枚。后妃逮妾靡不嫉焉，豫刻芭蕉為鬼形像，臨產以髮被覆其面，惡露塗芭蕉，以之示王。眾妖弊明，王惑信矣。群邪以壺盛卵，密覆其口，投江流矣。「天帝釋下，以印封口，諸天翼衛，順流停止，猶柱植地。下流之國，其王於臺遙覩水中有壺流下，韑輝光耀似有乾靈，取之觀焉。覩帝印文，發得百卵，令百婦人懷育溫煖。時滿體成，產為百男。生有上聖之智，不啟而自明，顏景跨世，相好希有，力幹勢援，兼人百倍，言音之響有若師子之吼。王即具白象百頭，七寶鞍勒，以供聖嗣，令征隣國；四鄰降伏，咸稱臣妾。「又伐所生之國，國人巨細靡不悚慄。王曰：『孰有能却斯敵者乎？』夫人曰：『大王無懼，視敵所由，攻城何方？臨之興觀，為王降之。』王即視敵所由而立觀矣。母登觀，揚聲曰：『夫逆之大，其有三矣。不遠群邪招二世咎，斯一也。生不識親而逆孝行，斯二也。恃勢殺親毒向三尊，斯三也。懷斯三逆，其惡無蓋。爾等張口，信現于今。』母捉其乳，天令湩射遍百子口。精誠之感，飲乳情哀，僉然俱曰：『斯則吾親。』泣涕交頸，叉手步進，叩頭悔過。親嗣始會，靡不哀慟。二國和睦，情過伯叔。異方欣然靡不稱善。「諸子覩世無常如幻，辭親學道，遠世穢垢。九十九子皆得緣一覺。一子理國，父王崩，為王。大赦眾罪，壞牢獄、裂池塞，免奴使，慰孝悌、養孤獨，開帑藏大布施，隨民之願給。以十善為國法，人人帶誦，家有孝子。興立塔寺，供養沙門，誦經論道口無四惡，諸毒歇盡，壽命益長。天帝養護，猶親育子。」佛告諸沙門：「留為王者，吾身是也。父王者，今白淨王是。母者，舍妙是。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a solitary mother who worked as a hired hand for a householder, watching over his fields and gardens. Her master, distracted by some matter, brought her meal to her late, past the proper hour. Just as she was about to eat, a renunciant came to beg. She thought of him with her whole heart, her desires cut off, her wickedness set aside, her conduct pure and true. She thought: 'To relieve every hungry person within the four seas is not equal to a small gift given to a truly worthy and pure-precepted one.' She placed her entire portion of food into his bowl, and set a single lotus flower atop it as an offering. The renunciant then showed his spiritual powers, radiating light, and the woman, delighted, exclaimed: 'This is truly what is meant by a sage and a divine being! I vow that in a future life I may have a hundred sons just like him.' When the mother's life ended and her spirit passed on, she was to become the heir of a certain brahmin. Her spirit gathered at the place where the brahmin relieved himself, and a doe, drinking of it, was thereby made pregnant. When her time was full, she gave birth to a girl, and the brahmin raised her as his own. By the age of ten and more, she had a radiant bearing and graceful step, and she watched over the household's fire. One day, playing with a deer, she let the fire go out without noticing. Her father, returning, grew angry with her, and sent her out to fetch fire. Wherever the girl walked among the crowds, a lotus flower sprang up at each step she took. The keeper of the fire she sought said: 'Circle my dwelling three times, and I will give you fire.' The girl obeyed, and lotus flowers sprang up on the bare ground, encircling the house three times over; those who saw this, walking or standing still, could not help marveling at it. "Word of this soon reached the ears of the country's king, who commanded a physiognomist to examine her and determine her fortune. The master said: 'She will surely bear a sacred heir, whose line will continue without end.' The king sent a worthy minister to formally receive her as his bride, with full ceremony; her beauty and radiance shone brightly, unmatched by any of the palace women. When her time came to give birth, she bore a hundred eggs instead. The other queens and consorts, every one of them, grew jealous of her. They secretly carved a demon-like figure out of a banana-plant trunk, and at the time of her delivery, covered its face with hair and smeared it with the fluids of childbirth, and showed it to the king. The many wicked women thus obscured the truth, and the king, deceived, believed them. The wicked conspirators sealed the hundred eggs inside a jar, covered its mouth in secret, and cast it into the river to drift away. "Śakra, lord of the gods, came down and sealed the mouth of the jar with his own seal; the gods of heaven escorted and guarded it, and, following the current, it came to rest as though rooted to the spot, like a pillar planted in the earth. In the kingdom downstream, that country's own king, from his tower, saw from a distance a jar floating in the water, its brilliant radiance as though it held some divine spirit within, and had it retrieved and examined. Seeing Śakra's own seal upon it, he opened it and found a hundred eggs within, and had a hundred women each nurture and warm one. When their time was full, their bodies were formed, and a hundred boys were born. From birth they possessed the wisdom of the highest sages, understanding without being taught, their bearing surpassing that of the age, their marks of excellence rare in the world, their strength and capability a hundred times that of ordinary men, their voices, when they spoke, like the roar of a lion. The king at once provided a hundred white elephants, saddled and bridled with the seven treasures, to equip these sacred heirs, and sent them to conquer the neighboring states; the four neighboring kingdoms submitted, all proclaiming themselves vassals. "They then attacked the very kingdom of their birth, and the people of that country, high and low, all trembled in fear. The king said: 'Who can withstand this enemy?' His queen said: 'Great King, have no fear. Watch from where the enemy approaches, and see by what means they attack the city; go up to observe them, and I will subdue them for you.' The king then went to watch from where the enemy would come, and set up an observation platform. The mother climbed the tower and called out in a loud voice: 'There are three great forms of rebellion in this world. To follow a crowd of the wicked without hesitation and thereby bring calamity upon two generations — this is the first. To be born and not recognize one's own parent, and thereby commit an act against filial devotion — this is the second. To rely on one's own power to kill one's own parent, and turn poison against the Three Jewels — this is the third. To hold all three of these forms of rebellion at once is a wickedness beyond measure. Open your mouths now, and you will see the proof of what I say.' Their mother then grasped her own breasts, and heaven caused her milk to spray out and reach the mouths of all hundred sons at once. Moved by this display of utter sincerity, and by the deep feeling stirred in them by drinking her milk, they all said together: 'This is truly our own mother!' Weeping, they embraced one another's necks, and, hands joined, came forward step by step, bowing their heads and confessing their fault. Now that mother and children were reunited for the first time, there was no one who did not weep for joy. The two kingdoms became reconciled, their affection surpassing even that between brothers; the people of both lands rejoiced, and there was no one, near or far, who did not praise this as good. "The sons, seeing the impermanence of the world, like an illusion, took leave of their parents to study the Way, distancing themselves from the world's defilements. Ninety-nine of the sons all attained the awakening of pratyekabuddhas. The one remaining son governed the kingdom; when his father the king passed away, he became king in his place. He granted a great amnesty for every crime, tore down the prisons, filled in the moats, freed all slaves and servants, comforted the filial and the fraternal, cared for orphans and widowers, opened the treasury for great acts of giving, granting the people's every wish. He made the ten virtues the law of the land, and every person recited them, and every household had its filial children. He built stūpas and temples, made offerings to the monks, recited the sūtras and discussed the Way, his mouth free of the four evils of speech; every poison was exhausted, and lifespans grew ever longer. Śakra, lord of the gods, protected and nurtured this kingdom as though it were his own child." The Buddha said to the monks: "The King Liu who ruled that kingdom was I myself. The father-king is now King Śuddhodana. The mother is Māyā." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

One of this fascicle's most elaborate stories, folding together several distinct motifs: a single act of pure giving that seeds a vow for future rebirth, a virgin-birth-like conception from a deer's contact with a holy site, palace intrigue and a false accusation of monstrous birth, and a climactic reunion staged through the literal, physical proof of a mother's milk reaching a hundred children at once. The reconciliation scene, where verbal accusation fails but the bodily fact of nursing succeeds, is one of the more visually striking single images in the collection so far.

24. The Ascetic Who Vowed to Become the Buddha Dīpaṃkara's First Disciple〔梵志本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，時為梵志，經學明達，國人師焉。弟子五百，皆有儒德，體好布施，猶自護身。時世有佛，號啑如來、無所著、正真尊、最正覺，將導三界還神本無。菩薩覩佛，欣然自歸，請佛及僧七日留家，以禮供養。梵志弟子各諍所主。一人年稚師使之行，還請事作。師曰：『有事無作者，爾攝之焉。』童子對曰：『唯燈無主者也。』師曰：『善哉。』弟子以𤬪盛麻油膏，淨自洗浴，白㲲纏頭，自手然之。「天人龍鬼覩其猛力，靡不拊手驚愕，而歎：『世未曾有，斯必為佛矣。』佛嘉之焉，令明徹夜而頭不損，心定在經霍然無想，七日若茲都無懈惓念矣。佛則授決：『却無數劫汝當為佛，號曰錠光。項中肩上各有光明，教授拯濟，眾生獲度其為無量。』天人鬼龍，聞當為佛，靡不嘉豫稽首拜賀。「梵志念曰：『彼其得佛，吾必得也，須當受決。』而佛去焉。前稽首曰：『今設微供誠吾盡心，願授吾決。』佛告梵志：『童子作佛之時，當授爾決。』梵志聞當得佛，喜忘有身，自斯之後，遂大布施，飢食寒衣，病給醫藥，蜎飛蚑行蠕動之類，隨其所食以時濟之。八方諸國，稱為仁父也。」佛告舍利弗：「童子者，錠光佛是。梵志者，吾身是也。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was, at that time, an ascetic, learned and accomplished in the scriptures, and the people of the country made him their teacher. He had five hundred disciples, all of them men of Confucian virtue, whose nature delighted in giving, though they still guarded their own persons carefully. At that time there was a buddha in the world, called the Tathāgata Dīpaṃkara, the one attached to nothing, the truly and rightly honored, the most perfectly enlightened, who guided the three realms back to their original, formless nature. The bodhisattva, seeing this buddha, took refuge in him with great joy, and invited the Buddha and his community of monks to remain in his household for seven days, that he might honor and provide for them. Among the ascetic's disciples, each vied for a particular task to attend to. One disciple, still young, whom the teacher had sent on an errand, returned and asked to be given some task to perform. The teacher said: 'There is one task with no one yet assigned to it — you may take charge of it.' The young disciple answered: 'The only such task is that of the lamp, which has no one tending it.' The teacher said, 'Very good.' The disciple filled a vessel with sesame oil, bathed himself thoroughly, wrapped his head in white cloth, and lit it with his own hand, his own head becoming the lamp's wick. "Gods, humans, dragons, and spirits, witnessing his fierce resolve, could not help clapping their hands in astonishment, exclaiming: 'Never has the world seen such a thing — this one is surely destined to become a buddha!' The Buddha praised him for this, and caused the light to burn through the whole night without his head being harmed at all, his mind settled entirely in the teaching, free of any distracting thought, and for all seven days he continued in this way, showing not the slightest weariness. The Buddha then bestowed on him this prediction: 'After countless kalpas you shall become a buddha, named Dīpaṃkara, "the Lamp-Bearer." Light will shine from your neck and shoulders, and through your teaching you will lift up and deliver living beings beyond all measure.' Gods, humans, spirits, and dragons, hearing that he would become a buddha, all rejoiced and bowed their heads in congratulation. "The ascetic teacher thought to himself: 'If he can attain buddhahood, then surely I too can attain it — I ought to also receive such a prediction.' But the Buddha had already departed. He came before him, bowed his head, and said: 'I have made this humble offering with my whole sincere heart — I beg that you grant me too a prediction.' The Buddha said to the ascetic: 'When this young disciple becomes a buddha, he shall grant you your prediction.' Hearing that he too would one day attain buddhahood, the ascetic, in his joy, forgot even that he had a body of his own, and from that time on, gave ever more greatly — feeding the hungry, clothing the cold, providing medicine for the sick, and, according to what each required, relieving even the smallest flying, crawling, and wriggling creatures with food at the proper time. The countries of all eight directions praised him as a benevolent father." The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "The young disciple was the Buddha Dīpaṃkara. The ascetic teacher was I myself." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This story and Story 21 both feature the Buddha Dīpaṃkara, though in reversed roles: here Dīpaṃkara is the star pupil whose extreme devotion (lighting his own head as a lamp) earns him the first prediction of buddhahood, while the bodhisattva figure is his teacher, who receives his own prediction only secondhand, through Dīpaṃkara's future authority rather than the Buddha's own. This indirect structure — one prediction of buddhahood enabling a second, later one — is a device this fascicle does not use elsewhere.

25. The Householder, the Grateful Turtle, and the Ungrateful Man〔理家本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，為大理家，積財巨億，常奉三尊，慈向眾生。觀市覩鼈，心悼之焉，問價貴賤。鼈主知菩薩有普慈之德、尚濟眾生，財富難數，貴賤無違，答曰：『百萬，能取者善，不者吾當烹之。』菩薩答曰：『大善！』即雇如直，持鼈歸家，澡護其傷，臨水放之。覩其遊去，悲喜誓曰：『太山餓鬼眾生之類，世主牢獄早獲免難，身安命全如爾今也。』稽首十方，叉手願曰：『眾生擾擾，其苦無量，吾當為天為地，為旱作潤，為漂作筏，飢食渴漿，寒衣熱涼，為病作醫，為冥作光；若有濁世顛倒之時，吾當於中作佛度彼眾生矣。』十方諸佛皆善其誓，讚曰：『善哉！必獲爾志。』「鼈後夜來齕其門，怪門有聲，使出覩鼈，還如事云。菩薩視之，鼈人語曰：『吾受重潤，身體獲全，無以答潤。蟲水居物知水盈虛，洪水將至必為巨害矣。願速嚴舟，臨時相迎。』答曰：『大善！』明晨詣門，如事啟王。王以菩薩宿有善名，信用其言：『遷下處高。』時至鼈來曰：『洪水至，可速下載，尋吾所之，可獲無患。』船尋其後，有蛇趣船，菩薩曰：『取之。』鼈云：『大善！』又覩漂狐，曰：『取之。』鼈亦云：『善。』又覩漂人搏頰呼天，哀濟吾命，曰：『取之。』鼈曰：『慎無取也，凡人心偽，尠有終信，背恩追勢，好為兇逆。』菩薩曰：『蟲類爾濟，人類吾賤，豈是仁哉？吾不忍也。』於是取之。鼈曰：『悔哉！』遂之豐土。鼈辭曰：『恩畢請退。』答曰：『吾獲如來、無所著、至真、正覺者，必當相度。』鼈曰：『大善！』鼈退，蛇狐各去。「狐以穴為居，獲古人伏藏紫磨名金百斤，喜曰：『當以報彼恩矣。』馳還曰：『小蟲受潤，獲濟微命。蟲穴居之物，求穴以自安，獲金百斤，斯穴非塚非家非劫非盜。吾精誠之所致，願以貢賢。』菩薩深惟：『不取徒捐，無益於貧民；取以布施，眾生獲濟，不亦善乎！』尋而取之。漂人覩焉，曰：『分吾半矣。』菩薩即以十斤惠之。漂人曰：『爾掘塚劫金，罪福應柰何？不半分之，吾必告有司。』答曰：『貧民困乏，吾欲等施；爾欲專之，不亦偏乎？』漂人遂告有司，菩薩見拘，無所告訴，唯歸命三尊，悔過自責，慈願：『眾生早離八難，莫有怨結如吾今也。』「蛇狐會曰：『奈斯事何？』蛇曰：『吾將濟之。』遂銜良藥開關入獄。見菩薩狀，顏色有損，愴而心悲，謂菩薩言：『以藥自隨，吾將齚太子，其毒尤甚，莫能濟者。賢者以藥自聞，傅則愈矣。』菩薩默然。蛇如所云，太子命將殞，王令曰：『有能濟茲，封之相國，吾與參治。』菩薩上聞，傅之即愈。王喜問所由，囚人本末自陳。王悵然自咎曰：『吾闇甚哉！』即誅漂人，大赦其國，封為國相，執手入宮，並坐而曰：『賢者說何書？懷何道？而為二儀之仁，惠逮眾生乎？』對曰：『說佛經，懷佛道也。』王曰：『佛有要決？』曰：『有之。佛說四非常，在之者，眾禍殄，景祐昌。』王曰：『善哉！願獲其實。』曰：『乾坤終訖之時，七日竝列巨海都索，天地烔然，須彌崩壞，天人鬼龍、眾生身命，霍然燋盡。前盛今衰，所謂非常矣。明士守無常之念，曰天地尚然，官爵國土，焉得久存？得斯念者，乃有普慈之志矣。』王曰：『天地尚然，豈況國土？佛說非常，我心信哉。』「理家又曰：『苦之尤苦者，王宜知之。』王曰：『願聞明誡。』曰：『眾生識靈微妙難知，視之無形，聽之無聲，弘也天下，高也無蓋，汪洋無表，輪轉無際。然飢渴于六欲，猶海不足于眾流，以斯數更太山燒煮諸毒眾苦；或為餓鬼，洋銅沃口役作太山；或為畜生，屠割剝裂，死輙更刃，苦痛無量。若獲為人，處胎十月，臨生急笮，猶索絞身，墮地之痛猶高隕下，為風所吹若火燒己，溫湯洗之甚沸銅自沃，手葌摩身猶刃自剝，如斯諸痛甚苦難陳。年長之後，諸根竝熟，首白齒隕，內外虛耗，存之心悲，轉成重病，四大欲離，節節皆痛，坐臥須人，醫來加惱。命將欲終，諸風竝興，截筋碎骨，孔竅都塞。息絕神逝，尋行所之。若其昇天，天亦有貧富貴賤，延算之壽，福盡罪來，下入太山、餓鬼、畜生，斯謂之苦。』王曰：『善哉！佛說苦要，我心信哉！』「理家又曰：『夫有必空，猶若兩木相鑽生火，火還燒木，火木俱盡，二事皆空。往古先王宮殿臣民，今者磨滅不覩所之，斯亦空也。』王曰：『善哉！佛說空要，我心信哉！』「理家又曰：『夫身地水火風矣，強為地，軟為水，熱為火，息為風。命盡神去，四大各離，無能保全，故云非身矣。』王曰：『善哉！佛說非身，吾心信哉！身且不保，豈況國土乎？痛夫我先王，不聞無上正真、最正覺非常苦空非身之教矣。』「理家曰：『天地無常，誰能保國者乎？胡不空藏，布施貧飢之人乎？』王曰：『善哉！明師之教快哉！』即空諸藏而布施貧乏，鰥寡孤兒令之為親為子，民服炫煌，貧富齊同，舉國欣欣，含笑且行，仰天歎曰：『菩薩神化乃至於茲乎？』四方歎德，遂致太平。」佛告諸沙門：「理家者，是吾身也。國王者，彌勒是。鼈者，阿難是。狐者，鶖鷺子是。蛇者，目連是。漂人者，調達是。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was a great householder, whose accumulated wealth rivaled that of the whole country, who always honored the Three Jewels and held kindness toward all living beings. Passing through the market one day, he saw a turtle for sale, and his heart was moved with sorrow for it; he asked its price. The turtle's owner, knowing the bodhisattva's reputation for universal compassion, delighting in relieving the multitude, and his wealth beyond counting, would not haggle over the price, and answered: 'A million coins — if you can pay that, very well; if not, I will boil it.' The bodhisattva answered: 'Very good!' and paid the exact price at once, took the turtle home, tended its wounds, and released it by the water. Watching it swim away, moved by both sorrow and joy, he made this vow: 'May the hungry ghosts of Mount Tai, and every living being bound in the world's own prisons, soon be freed from their hardship, and find their bodies at peace and their lives whole, just as this turtle now does.' He bowed his head to the ten directions, joined his hands, and vowed: 'Living beings are in such turmoil, their suffering beyond measure; I will be as heaven for them, as earth for them; in drought I will be moisture for them, in flood I will be a raft for them; food for the hungry, drink for the thirsty, clothing for the cold, coolness for the heated, a physician for the sick, a light in the darkness; and if ever there comes an age of chaos and confusion in this defiled world, I will become a buddha within it and deliver these living beings.' The buddhas of the ten directions all approved this vow, and praised him, saying: 'Excellent! You shall surely attain your resolve.' "Later, in the night, the turtle came and gnawed at his gate; startled by the sound at his door, he sent someone out to look, and it was the turtle, come to repeat exactly what had happened before. The bodhisattva looked at it, and the turtle spoke to him in human speech: 'I have received your profound grace, and my body has been made whole — I have nothing to offer in return. As a creature that dwells in the water, I know when the waters will rise or fall, and a great flood is coming that will surely cause immense harm. I beg you to prepare a boat swiftly, and when the time comes, I will come to guide you.' He answered, 'Very good.' The next morning he went to the palace gate and reported this to the king exactly as it had happened. The king, because the bodhisattva had long held a reputation for goodness, trusted his words, and had the people move from low ground to higher ground. When the time came, the turtle arrived and said: 'The flood has come — you must board your boat at once and follow me, and you will surely be spared any harm.' The boat followed after the turtle. A snake, caught in the flood, made for the boat, and the bodhisattva said, 'Take it aboard.' The turtle said, 'Very good.' Next he saw a fox being swept along, and said, 'Take it aboard,' and the turtle again said, 'Good.' Then he saw a drowning man, beating his own cheeks and crying out to heaven, begging to have his life saved, and said, 'Take him aboard.' The turtle said: 'Take care not to take him — men's hearts are deceitful by nature, and few of them remain faithful to the end; they betray kindness in pursuit of their own advantage, and are inclined toward wicked, rebellious deeds.' The bodhisattva said: 'A mere creature you were willing to save, and yet you would despise a fellow human being — how could that be right? I cannot bear to leave him.' And so he took the man aboard as well. The turtle said, 'You will regret this,' and they went on to a place of high, fertile ground. The turtle then took his leave, saying: 'My debt is repaid — I ask now to depart.' He answered: 'When I attain the state of Tathāgata, the one attached to nothing, the truly and rightly awakened, I will surely deliver you as well.' The turtle said, 'Very good,' and departed; the snake and the fox likewise went their own ways. "The fox, making its den, found an ancient hoard of pure refined gold, a hundred catties of it, and said with delight: 'I must use this to repay that kindness.' It hurried back and said: 'This lowly creature received your grace, and its small life was saved. As a creature that dwells in a den, I sought out a den to make my home in, and there I found a hundred catties of gold — this den is no ancient grave, no household treasury, no stolen hoard, no plunder from theft. It is the result of my own sincere devotion, and I wish to offer it to you, worthy one.' The bodhisattva reflected deeply: 'To refuse it would be to waste it uselessly, of no benefit to the poor; to accept it and use it for giving would relieve living beings — would this not be good?' And so he accepted it at once. The man who had nearly drowned, seeing this, said: 'Give me half of it.' The bodhisattva gave him ten catties of it. The drowning man said: 'You dug up a grave and stole this gold — what crime and punishment do you think this deserves? If you do not give me half, I will surely report you to the authorities.' He answered: 'The poor and needy people are in hardship, and I wish to give to them equally — would it not be unfair for you alone to claim this for yourself?' The drowning man then reported him to the authorities, and the bodhisattva was seized, with no one to appeal to; he could only take refuge in the Three Jewels, repent and reproach himself, and make this compassionate vow: 'May all living beings soon be free of the eight difficulties, and may none of them ever be bound by such a grievance as mine is now.' "The snake and the fox, meeting together, said: 'What can be done about this?' The snake said, 'I will go and save him.' It took a fine medicine in its mouth, and, opening the prison gate, entered the cell. Seeing the bodhisattva's condition, his complexion diminished by suffering, it was moved with sorrow, and said to the bodhisattva: 'Keep this medicine with you. I am going now to bite the crown prince — his poison will be especially severe, and no one will be able to cure him. Worthy one, when you hear of this, apply the medicine, and he will be cured at once.' The bodhisattva said nothing. The snake did exactly as it had said, and the crown prince's life hung by a thread; the king proclaimed: 'Whoever can save him will be made chief minister, and will share in the governance of this kingdom.' The bodhisattva, hearing of this, came forward and applied the medicine, and the prince was healed at once. The king, delighted, asked how this had come about, and the prisoner told his whole story from beginning to end. The king, grieved, reproached himself: 'How very blind I have been!' He at once had the drowning man executed, granted a great amnesty throughout the kingdom, made the bodhisattva chief minister, took him by the hand into the palace, and, sitting together with him, said: 'Worthy one, what teachings do you expound, what path do you hold to, that you embody such benevolence toward heaven and earth, your grace reaching every living being?' He answered: 'I expound the Buddha's scriptures, and hold to the Buddha's path.' The king said: 'Does the Buddha have some essential teaching?' He said: 'He does. The Buddha teaches the four truths of impermanence — one who holds to them sees every calamity vanish, and bright blessing flourish.' The king said: 'Excellent! I wish to hear its substance.' He said: 'When heaven and earth themselves come to their final end, seven suns will appear together and dry up the whole ocean; heaven and earth will blaze with fire, Mount Sumeru itself will collapse, and gods, humans, dragons, and every living being's body and life will be consumed in an instant. What flourished before withers now — this is what is meant by impermanence. The wise hold fast to the awareness of impermanence, saying: if heaven and earth themselves are like this, how could office, rank, or kingdom ever last long? Whoever grasps this awareness thereby holds the resolve of universal compassion.' The king said: 'If even heaven and earth are like this, how much more so a mere kingdom? What the Buddha teaches of impermanence — my heart truly believes it.' "The householder said further: 'There is a suffering more bitter than any other, and the king should know of it.' The king said, 'I wish to hear this clear teaching.' He said: 'The consciousness of living beings is subtle and mysterious, difficult to know; looked at, it has no form; listened for, it makes no sound; vast as it is across all the world, high as it is beyond any covering, boundless as an ocean with no visible shore, turning in a cycle with no end. And yet it hungers and thirsts after the six desires, as the ocean is never satisfied by all the rivers that flow into it, and so, again and again, it passes through the burning and boiling torments of Mount Tai and every manner of suffering; sometimes it becomes a hungry ghost, molten copper poured down its throat, forced to labor in Mount Tai; sometimes it becomes an animal, butchered and flayed, dying again and again beneath the blade, its pain beyond measure. If it manages to become human, it dwells ten months in the womb, and at the moment of birth is crushed and squeezed as though strangled by a cord; the pain of falling to the ground is like falling from a great height, buffeted by wind as though burned by fire, bathed in water so hot it is like being doused in molten copper, touched by hands and cloth as though flayed by a blade — such pains as these are bitter beyond telling. When one has grown older, the faculties all ripen and decline, the hair turns white and the teeth fall out, the body wastes away within and without, and to dwell in this state is a sorrow in itself, turning gradually into grave illness; as the four elements of the body seek to separate, every joint aches, and one can neither sit nor lie down without help, and even the physician's visit only adds to one's distress. As life is about to end, every wind-element in the body rises up at once, severing the sinews and shattering the bones, and every opening of the body becomes blocked; the breath ceases and the spirit departs, following the path determined by one's own past deeds. Even if one rises to heaven, heaven too has its poor and its rich, its noble and its base, and a lifespan that, however long, will eventually be used up; when one's blessings are exhausted and one's transgressions come due, one falls again into Mount Tai, into the realm of hungry ghosts, or into the animal realm — this is what is called suffering.' The king said: 'Excellent! What the Buddha teaches of suffering — my heart truly believes it.' "The householder said further: 'All that exists must, in the end, be empty — like two pieces of wood rubbed together to make fire, the fire then burning up the very wood that made it, until fire and wood are both entirely gone; both are, in the end, empty. The palaces, ministers, and people of kings long past are now all worn away, and no one knows where they have gone — this too is emptiness.' The king said: 'Excellent! What the Buddha teaches of emptiness — my heart truly believes it.' "The householder said further: 'The body is composed of earth, water, fire, and wind. What is solid is earth; what is soft is water; what is warm is fire; what breathes is wind. When life ends and the spirit departs, these four elements each go their separate ways, and nothing can keep them whole together — this is why it is said there is no fixed self.' The king said: 'Excellent! What the Buddha teaches of no-self — my heart truly believes it. If even this body cannot be kept forever, how much less a kingdom? How painful it is that my late royal father never heard this teaching of the unsurpassed, true and right, most perfect awakening — of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self!' "The householder said: 'Heaven and earth themselves are impermanent — who, then, could ever truly keep a kingdom? Why not empty your treasuries, and give to the poor and hungry?' The king said: 'Excellent! How fine is the wise teacher's instruction!' He at once emptied every treasury and gave freely to the poor and needy, treating widowers, widows, and orphans as his own family and children; the people, moved by his radiant example, poor and rich made equal, the whole country rejoicing, walked about smiling, and, looking up to heaven, exclaimed: 'Has the bodhisattva's divine transformation truly reached even this far?' The four directions praised his virtue, and at last great peace was achieved." The Buddha said to the monks: "The householder was I myself. The king was Maitreya. The turtle was Ānanda. The fox was Śāriputra. The snake was Maudgalyāyana. The drowning man was Devadatta." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The longest and most structurally ambitious story in this fascicle, nesting a rescued turtle, snake, and fox alongside a rescued but ultimately treacherous human being, then resolving the human's betrayal only through the very snake the bodhisattva chose to save over the turtle's explicit warning. The story's second half turns into an extended doctrinal dialogue — the four noble truths of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, walked through one at a time with the king's own repeated affirmation — giving this story a didactic density closer to a sūtra than a folktale.

26. The Monk Who Fed a Louse, and the Debt That Could Not Be Repaid〔沙門本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為沙門行，恒處山林，慈心悲愍，眾生長苦輪轉三界，何以濟之？靖心思惟，索道弘原，當以拯眾。而衣有虱，身痒心擾，道志不立，手探尋之即獲虱矣。中心愴然，求以安之。正有獸骨，徐以置中矣。虱得七日之食，盡乃捨邁。展轉生死，菩薩得佛，經緯教化。」時，天大雪絕行路人，國有理家請佛并數千比丘，供養七日。厥心肅穆，宗室僉然，而雪未晞。佛告阿難：「勅諸沙門皆還精舍。」阿難言：「主人恭肅，厥心未墮，雪盛未息，分衛無處。」世尊曰：「主人意訖，不復供惠也。」佛即引邁，沙門翼從還于精舍。明日世尊告阿難：「汝從主人分衛。」阿難奉教而行，造主人門。門人覩之，無問其所以也。有頃迴還，稽首長跪，如事啟焉。又質其原，「彼意無恒，何其疾乎？」佛即為具說如上。又曰：「阿難！吾以慈心濟虱微命，惠之朽骨七日之食，今獲供養盡世上獻。宿命施恩，恩齊七日，故其意止，不復如前也，豈況慈心向佛逮沙門眾？持戒清淨無欲高行，內端己心，表以慈化，恭惠高行比丘一人，踰施凡庶累劫盡情也。所以然者？比丘擁懷佛經，有戒、有定、有慧、解脫、度知見種，以斯五德慈導眾生，令遠三界萬苦之禍矣。」阿難曰：「遇哉斯理家！面獲慈養如來、無所著、正真道、最正覺、道法御、天人師，并諸沙門，或有溝港、頻來、不還、應真，或有開士，建大弘慈將導眾生者乎！斯福難量，其若海矣；難稱，其猶地也。」佛言：「善哉！阿難！真如所云。佛時難遇，經法難聞，比丘僧難得供養，如漚曇華時一有耳。」佛說如是，比丘歡喜，稽首承行。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva practiced as a monk, always dwelling in mountain forests, his heart full of compassion and sorrow, grieving that living beings suffer so long, turning endlessly through the three realms — how might he relieve them? Stilling his mind, he reflected deeply, seeking the vast source of the Way, resolved to save the multitude. But there was a louse in his robe, and his body itched and his mind was disturbed, and his resolve for the Way could not settle; reaching in with his hand, he found the louse. His heart was moved with sorrow, and he sought a way to give it comfort. Nearby there happened to be an animal's bone, and he gently placed the louse inside it. The louse fed on it for seven days, and when it was gone, the louse departed. Turning through birth and death again and again, the bodhisattva at last attained buddhahood, and taught and transformed beings across the land." At that time, heavy snow blocked all the roads, and a householder of the country invited the Buddha and several thousand monks to receive offerings for seven days. His heart was solemn and reverent, and his whole household joined him in this devotion, but the snow had not yet melted. The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Instruct the monks to return to the monastery." Ānanda said: "The host is reverent and solemn, his heart has not wavered, and though the snow is heavy and has not yet stopped, there is nowhere else for us to go begging." The World-Honored One said: "The host's intention has already run its course — he will not go on providing for us." The Buddha then set out, and the monks followed him back to the monastery. The next day, the World-Honored One said to Ānanda: "Go and beg alms from that same host." Ānanda, following this instruction, went and arrived at the host's gate. The gatekeeper, seeing him, asked him nothing of his purpose. After a while, Ānanda returned, bowed his head, knelt, and reported exactly what had happened. He then questioned the Buddha about this: "How could that man's intention change so quickly?" The Buddha then explained the whole matter as told above, and said further: "Ānanda, I once, out of a compassionate heart, saved the small life of a louse, giving it an old bone to feed on for seven days; and now, having received offerings for the rest of my life in this world, this is because that former gift of kindness lasted exactly seven days — and so this man's own intention, too, comes to its natural end after this same span, and does not continue as it did before. How much more true this is of a compassionate heart directed toward the Buddha and the community of monks. To honor with reverence a single monk of pure precepts, free of desire and lofty in conduct, sincere within and expressing that sincerity outwardly through compassionate transformation, surpasses giving to ordinary people over countless kalpas, however heartfelt. And why is this so? Because a monk holds within him the Buddha's own teaching, possessing precepts, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the wisdom and vision of liberation — with these five virtues he guides living beings with compassion, leading them far from the ten thousand calamities of the three realms." Ānanda said: "How fortunate that householder was, to receive in person the compassionate nurture of the Tathāgata, the one attached to nothing, the true and right Way, the most perfectly awakened, the guide of the Dharma, the teacher of gods and men, together with the monks — among whom are those who have attained the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and arhatship, and bodhisattvas who establish great and vast compassion to guide living beings! Such blessing is beyond measure, vast as the ocean; beyond description, boundless as the earth itself." The Buddha said: "Well said, Ānanda! It is exactly as you say. A buddha is difficult to encounter, the Dharma is difficult to hear, and to make offerings to the community of monks is difficult to attain — like the udumbara flower, which blooms only once in a great while." Having spoken this teaching, the monks rejoiced, bowed their heads, and carried it out. This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This fascicle's final story closes the entire giving chapter on an unusually self-referential note: the Buddha explains his own present circumstances (the sudden withdrawal of a host's hospitality) by reaching back into his own past life as the source of the pattern, making the story less about a stranger's virtue than about the Buddha directly narrating the karmic mechanics of a debt repaid exactly to its original term. The text embedded here also carries the closing lines of the entire giving chapter and the opening lines of the chapter that follows, on the perfection of moral precepts — a chapter-boundary this translation observes by ending here and picking up the precepts chapter in Fascicle 4.
