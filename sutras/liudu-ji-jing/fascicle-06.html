The Translation

55. The Man Who Let Himself Be Pierced With a Thousand Needles for a Single Verse〔凡人本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，時為凡人，聞佛名號、相好、道力、功德巍巍，諸天共宗，則高行者眾苦都滅矣。菩薩存想，吟泣無寧，曰：『吾從得天師經典翫誦，執行以致為佛，愈眾生病令還本淨乎？』時佛去世，無除饉眾，莫由受聞。隣有凡夫其性貪殘，覩菩薩精進志銳，曰：『吾知佛三戒一章，爾欲稟乎？』菩薩聞之，其喜無量，稽首足下，伏地請戒。知偈者曰：『斯為無上正真、最正覺、道法御、天人師之要教也，子欲徒聞之，豈其然乎？』答曰：『請問法儀，厥義何之？』曰：『爾審懇誠者，身毛一孔一針刺之，血流身痛心不悔者，尊教可聞矣。』答曰：『聞佛則殞，吾欣為之，豈況刺身而生存者乎？』即布針以刺身，血若流泉，菩薩喜於聞法，得無痛之定。「天帝釋覩菩薩志銳，為其愴然，化令舉身一毛孔者有一針矣。其人覩之照厥志高，即授之曰：『守口攝意，身無犯惡，除是三行，得賢徑度。是諸如來、無所著、正真尊、最正覺戒真說也。』菩薩聞戒歡喜稽首，顧視身針霍然不現，顏景弈弈，氣力踰前，天人鬼龍靡不歎懿。志進行高，踵指相尋，遂致得佛，拯濟眾生。」佛告諸比丘：「授菩薩偈者，今調達是。調達雖先知佛偈，猶盲執燭炤，彼不自明，何益於己？菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was, at that time, an ordinary man, who heard of the Buddha's name, his fine marks, the power of his attainment, and his towering merit and virtue, honored together by all the gods, and that through his lofty conduct every suffering of living beings could be entirely dissolved. The bodhisattva, holding this in his mind, wept without rest, saying: "If I could receive the teachings of this heavenly teacher and recite and practice them, and thereby attain buddhahood, could I not heal the sicknesses of living beings and restore them to their own original purity?" But the Buddha had already left the world at that time, and there was no community of monks through whom he could receive this teaching. Nearby there lived an ordinary man of greedy and cruel nature, who, seeing the bodhisattva's sharp and eager resolve, said: "I know a single chapter of the Buddha's own three precepts — do you wish to receive it?" The bodhisattva, hearing this, was filled with immeasurable joy, bowed his head at the man's feet, and, prostrate on the ground, begged for the teaching. The one who knew the verse said: "This is the essential teaching of the unsurpassed, true and right, most perfectly awakened one, the guide of the Dharma, the teacher of gods and men — do you truly think you may simply hear it for nothing?" He answered: "I beg to ask what the proper offering should be — what would that consist of?" He said: "If your sincerity is truly earnest, then let a needle be driven into every single pore of your body, and if, though your blood flows and your body aches, your heart does not regret it, then you may hear this honored teaching." He answered: "To hear the Buddha's teaching even at the cost of my own life is something I would gladly do — how much more so merely to be pierced and yet live?" He then had needles set out and driven into his own body, until his blood flowed like a spring, and the bodhisattva, rejoicing at the prospect of hearing the Dharma, entered a state of concentration free of pain. "Śakra, lord of the gods, witnessing the bodhisattva's sharp resolve, was moved with compassion for him, and by his transformative power made it appear that every single pore of his body bore a needle. The man, seeing this, recognized the loftiness of his resolve, and bestowed the teaching upon him: 'Guard your mouth, restrain your intentions, let your body commit no evil — cultivate these three practices, and you will attain a swift crossing to virtue. This is the true teaching of the precepts of the Tathāgata, the one attached to nothing, the true and honored one, the most perfectly awakened.' The bodhisattva, hearing this precept, rejoiced and bowed his head, and, looking back at his own body, found the needles had vanished entirely; his countenance shone radiant, his strength surpassing what it had been before, and there was no god, human, spirit, or dragon who did not sigh in admiration. His resolve advancing, his conduct growing ever loftier, step following step, he at last attained buddhahood, and relieved living beings." The Buddha said to the monks: "The one who bestowed the verse upon the bodhisattva is now Devadatta. Though Devadatta knew the Buddha's own verse before anyone else, he was like a blind man holding a torch to light the way for others — unable to see by its light himself, what benefit was it to him?" This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This opening story of the diligence chapter makes the chapter's own theme unusually explicit: diligence here is measured not by outward achievement but by willingness to endure real physical cost for a fragment of teaching, and the story's sharpest irony — that the one who transmits the precept, Devadatta, gains nothing from knowing it himself — is stated outright by the Buddha rather than left for the reader to infer.

56. The Macaque King Who Bridged a River With His Own Body〔獼猴王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，為獼猴王，常從五百獼猴遊戲。時世枯旱，眾果不豐，其國王城去山不遠，隔以小水，猴王將其眾入苑食果。苑司以聞。王曰：『密守，無令得去。』猴王知之，愴然而曰：『吾為眾長，禍福所由，貪果濟命而更誤眾。』勅其眾曰：『布行求藤。』眾還藤至，競各連續，以其一端縛大樹枝。猴王自繫腰登樹投身，攀彼樹枝，藤短身垂，勅其眾曰：『疾緣藤度。』眾以過畢，兩掖俱絕，墮水邊岸，絕而復蘇。國王晨往案行，獲大獼猴，能為人語，叩頭自陳云：『野獸貪生恃澤附國，時旱果乏，干犯天苑，咎過在我。原赦其餘，蟲身朽肉，可供太官一朝之餚也。』王仰歎曰：『蟲獸之長，殺身濟眾，有古賢之弘仁。吾為人君，豈能如乎？』為之揮涕，命解其縛，扶著安土，勅一國中恣猴所食，有犯之者罪與賊同。還向皇后陳其仁澤：『古賢之行未等於茲，吾仁糸髮，彼踰崑崙矣。』后曰：『善哉！奇矣斯蟲也。王當恣其所食無令眾害。』王曰：『吾已命矣。』」佛告諸比丘：「獼猴王者，吾身是也，國王者，阿難是也，五百獼猴者，今五百比丘是。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was the king of the macaques, always wandering and playing together with five hundred macaques. At that time the world suffered a great drought, and fruit grew scarce; the country's royal city was not far from the mountains, separated only by a small stream, and the macaque king led his troop into the royal garden to eat its fruit. The garden's overseer reported this. The king said: "Guard it secretly, and do not let them escape." The macaque king, learning of this, said with sorrow: "As leader of this troop, whatever fortune or misfortune befalls it is my own responsibility; out of greed for fruit to sustain our lives, I have led my troop into danger." He commanded his troop: "Spread out and gather vines." The troop returned with vines, which they joined end to end, tying one end to the branch of a great tree. The macaque king tied the other end around his own waist, climbed the tree, and threw himself across, gripping the branch on the far side — but the vine was too short, and his body hung suspended in the gap. He commanded his troop: "Cross quickly along the vine." Once they had all crossed, the strain tore through both his arms, and he fell to the water's edge below, his breath failing before reviving again. The king of that country, making his morning rounds, came upon the great macaque, who was able to speak in human language, and, bowing his head, explained of his own accord: 'This wild creature, clinging to life, relied on your own royal favor; when the drought left our fruit scarce, we trespassed against your heavenly garden, and the fault is mine alone. I beg you to forgive the rest of my troop — as for this decaying flesh of a mere insect-like creature, it may serve as a single morning's dish for your royal kitchen.' The king, looking up, sighed: 'The leader of mere insects and beasts, giving his own life to save the multitude, holds within him the vast benevolence of the ancient sages. I, who rule as a lord of men, could I ever match this?' He wept for him, ordered his bonds released, had him carried gently to level ground, and commanded throughout the kingdom that the macaques be allowed to eat freely, with any who harmed them to be punished as a common thief. Returning, he told the queen of this display of benevolent grace: 'Even the conduct of the ancient sages did not equal this — my own benevolence is but a single hair, while his surpasses even Mount Kunlun.' The queen said: 'Excellent! What a marvel this creature is. The king should let it eat as it wishes, and allow no harm to come to the rest.' The king said, 'I have already commanded it so.'" The Buddha said to the monks: "The macaque king was I myself. The king was Ānanda. The five hundred macaques are now these five hundred monks." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A close cousin of Story 45's bridge-of-monkeys device in the giving chapter (itself well known elsewhere in Buddhist literature), but reclassified here under diligence: the macaque king's sustained physical strain, holding the gap open long enough for his entire troop to cross, is credited to his tireless effort rather than to the act of self-sacrifice alone.

57. The Deer King Who Offered His Own Body as a Bridge〔鹿王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，身為鹿王，力勢踰眾，仁愛普覆，群鹿慕從，所遊近苑。牧人以聞，王率士眾合圍逼之。鹿王乃知，垂泣而曰：『爾等斯厄，厥尤由我也。吾將沒命，濟爾群小。』鹿王就索，下前兩足曰：『登吾踊出，爾等可全矣。』群鹿如之，咸獲免矣。身肉決裂，血若流泉，躃地纔息，其痛難言。群鹿啼呼，徘徊不去。人王覩其體殘，血流丹地，不見鹿眾，曰：『斯者何以？』鹿王對曰：『執操不淑，稟命為獸，尋求美草以全微命，干犯國境罪應尤重。身肉雖盡，兩脾五藏完具尚存，惟願太官給一朝膳。』王曰：『爾何緣若茲乎？』鹿王本末陳其所以。其王惻然為之流淚曰：『爾為畜生，含乾坤之弘仁，毀命以濟眾；吾為人君，苟貪好殺殘天所生？』即布重命，勅國黎庶，自今絕獵無貪鹿肉，裂索舉鹿，安厝平地。群鹿覩其王，仰天悲號，各前舐瘡，分布採藥，咀咋傅之。人王覩焉，重為抆淚曰：『君以子愛育其眾，眾以親恩慕其君，為君之道可不仁乎？』自斯絕殺尚仁，天即祐之，國豐民熙，遐邇稱仁，民歸若流。」佛告鶖鷺子：「鹿王者，吾身是也。五百鹿者，今五百比丘是也。人王者，阿難是。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was born as the king of the deer, his strength surpassing the herd, his benevolent love covering all, and the herd of deer, drawn by his example, followed him to graze near a royal garden. The gardener reported this, and the king led his soldiers to encircle and close in upon them. The deer king, learning of this, wept and said: "This danger you face is entirely my own fault. I will give up my own life to save you, my humble followers." The deer king came forward and offered himself, lowering his two front legs, and said: "Climb up and leap over me, and you may all be saved." The herd did as he said, and every one of them escaped unharmed. His own body's flesh was torn open, his blood flowing like a spring, and, collapsing to the ground, he could barely draw breath, his pain beyond words. The herd cried out in mourning, circling him and unwilling to leave. The human king, seeing his broken body, the blood reddening the ground, and no longer seeing the rest of the herd, asked: "What has happened here?" The deer king answered: "My own conduct was unworthy, and, having received the fate of a beast, I sought fine grass to sustain my meager life, trespassing against the kingdom's own borders — a fault deserving of the gravest punishment. Though my flesh is now torn apart, my two haunches and five organs remain whole and intact — I beg only that they may provide a single morning's meal for your royal kitchen." The king asked, "By what cause have you come to this?" The deer king told him the whole story from beginning to end. The king, moved to sorrow, wept and said: "You, though a mere beast, hold within you the vast benevolence of heaven and earth, destroying your own life to save the multitude; and I, who rule as a lord of men, indulge instead in wanton killing, destroying what heaven itself has given life to?" He at once issued a solemn decree, commanding the people of his kingdom: "From this day forward, all hunting is forbidden, and no one shall covet the flesh of deer." He had the ropes cut and the deer lifted up, and settled gently upon level ground. The herd of deer, seeing their king, raised their voices to heaven in mournful cries, and came forward one by one to lick his wounds, and scattered to gather medicinal herbs, chewing them and applying them to his wounds. The human king, witnessing this, again wiped away his tears and said: "A ruler who nurtures his people as his own beloved children, and a people who cherish their ruler out of the same familial love in return — is this not what the way of a true ruler should be?" From that time on, he gave up killing and honored benevolence, and heaven itself blessed him for it; the kingdom prospered and the people flourished, and near and far alike praised his benevolence, and the people returned to him like a flowing river." The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "The deer king was I myself. The five hundred deer are now these five hundred monks. The human king was Ānanda." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A direct companion to Story 18 in the giving chapter, sharing the same basic image of a deer king offering his own body as a bridge for his herd, but classified here under diligence rather than giving — worth noting as one of several instances in this collection where a very similar story appears more than once under different chapter headings, without the text drawing attention to the repetition.

58. Sugandha the Deer, Betrayed by the Man He Saved From Drowning〔修凡鹿王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，身為鹿王，名曰修凡，體毛九色覩世希有，江邊遊戲。覩有溺人，呼天求哀，鹿愍之曰：『人命難得而當殞乎？吾寧投危以濟彼矣。』即泅趣之曰：『爾勿恐也，援吾角騎吾背，今自相濟。』人即如之。鹿出人畢，息微殆絕。人活甚喜，遶鹿三匝，叩頭陳曰：『人道難遇，厥命惟重，大夫投危濟吾重命，恩踰二儀，終始弗忘，願為奴使供給所乏。』鹿曰：『爾去，以吾軀命累汝終身。夫有索我，無云覩之。』溺人敬諾：『沒命不違。』「時，國王名摩因光，稟操淳和慈育黎庶。王之元后厥名和致，夢見鹿王身毛九色，其角踰犀。寐寤以聞：『欲以鹿之皮角為衣為珥，若不獲之妾必死矣。』王重曰：『可。』晨向群臣說鹿體狀，布命募求，獲者封之一縣，金鉢滿之銀粟，銀鉢滿之金粟。募之若斯，溺人悅焉，曰：『吾獲一縣，金銀滿鉢，終身之樂；鹿自殞命，余何豫哉？』即馳詣宮，如事陳聞啟之。斯須面即生癩，口為朽臭，重曰：『斯鹿有靈，王當率眾乃獲之耳。』王即興兵渡江尋之。「鹿時與烏素結厚友。然其臥睡不知王來，烏曰：『友乎！王來捕子。』鹿疲不聞，啄耳重云：『王來殺爾。』鹿驚覩王彎弓向己，疾馳造前跪膝叩頭曰：『天王假吾漏刻之命，欲陳愚情。』王覩鹿然，即命息矢。鹿曰：『王重元后勞躬副之，吾終不免矣。天王處深宮之內，焉知微蟲之處斯乎？』王手指云：『癩人啟之。』鹿曰：『吾尋美草食之，遙覩溺人呼天求哀，吾愍于窮，投危濟之。其人上岸喜叩頭曰：「吾命且喪而君濟之，願給水草為終身奴。」吾答之曰：「爾去，自在所之，慎無向人云吾在斯。」』鹿王又曰：『寧出水中浮草木上著陸地，不出無反復人也。劫財殺主，其惡可原；受恩圖逆，斯酷難陳。』王驚曰：『斯何畜生而懷弘慈，沒命濟物不以為艱，斯必天也！』王善鹿之言，喜而進德，命國內曰：『自今日後恣鹿所食，敢有犯者罪皆直死。』王還，元后聞王放之，恚盛心碎，死入太山。天帝釋聞王建志崇仁，嘉其若茲，化為鹿類盈國食穀，諸穀苗稼掃土皆盡，以觀其志。黎庶訟之。王曰：『凶訛保國，不若守信之喪矣。』釋曰：『王真信矣。』遣鹿各去，穀豐十倍，毒害消歇，諸患自滅。」佛告諸比丘：「時鹿王者，吾身是也。烏者，阿難是也。王者，鶖鷺子是也。溺人者，調達是也。王妻者，今調達妻是。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was born as the king of the deer, named Sugandha, his fur of nine colors, a rarity such as the world had never seen, wandering along a riverbank. He saw a man drowning, crying out to heaven and begging for help; the deer, moved with pity, said: "Human life is hard to attain — should it be lost so easily? I would rather risk my own danger to save him." He swam to the man and said: "Do not be afraid — take hold of my antlers and climb upon my back, and I will save you now." The man did as he was told. Once the deer had brought him safely to shore, the man's breath had grown faint, nearly failing entirely. Once revived, the man, overjoyed, circled the deer three times, bowed his head, and said: "The way of humanity is hard to attain, and life itself is a most weighty thing — you, great one, risked your own danger to save this weighty life of mine; your grace surpasses heaven and earth themselves, and I shall never forget it to the end of my days. I beg to serve you as your own servant, to provide for whatever you may lack." The deer said: "Go your own way. To have your entire life depend on my own existence would be too great a burden for you. If anyone should ever ask after me, say that you have not seen me." The drowning man respectfully agreed: "Even at the cost of my own life, I will not betray this." "At that time, the king of that country was named Mo-yin-guang, of a pure and gentle disposition, nurturing his people with compassion. The king's principal queen, named He-zhi, dreamed she saw a deer king with nine-colored fur, his antlers surpassing even a rhinoceros's horn. Waking, she reported this: 'I wish to have this deer's hide and antlers made into a garment and ornaments — if I cannot obtain them, I shall surely die.' The king said, 'It shall be so.' The next morning he described the deer's appearance to his assembled ministers, and issued a proclamation offering a reward: whoever found it would be granted an entire county, and bowls filled to overflowing with gold and silver grain. Hearing of this offer, the rescued man was delighted, and thought: 'If I gain an entire county and bowls filled with gold and silver, that would bring me a lifetime of pleasure — what is the deer's own death to me?' He hurried to the palace and reported exactly what he had witnessed. In that very instant, his face broke out in leprous sores, his mouth filled with a rotten stench, and he said again: 'This deer possesses spiritual power — the king himself must lead his own troops to capture it.' The king then raised an army, crossed the river, and set out in search of it. "The deer had, at that time, formed a close friendship with a crow, and, while he lay sleeping, unaware that the king had come, the crow said: 'Friend! The king has come to capture you!' The deer, exhausted, did not hear, and the crow pecked at his ear, calling again: 'The king has come to kill you!' The deer, startled awake, saw the king with his bow drawn and aimed at him, and rushed forward at once, kneeling and bowing his head, saying: 'Heavenly King, grant this creature a moment's respite, that I may speak my humble thoughts.' The king, seeing the deer behave this way, ordered his arrow lowered. The deer said: 'The king has troubled himself, weighed down by his queen's own wish, to come in person — I cannot escape this now. But the Heavenly King dwells deep within his own palace — how could he have known where this humble creature dwelt?' The king pointed and said: 'The leprous man revealed it.' The deer said: 'I was searching for fine grass to eat when, from a distance, I saw a drowning man crying out to heaven and begging for help. Moved with pity for his desperate plight, I risked my own danger to save him. Once ashore, the man, overjoyed, bowed his head and said: "My life was about to be lost, and you, sir, have saved it — I beg to provide you with water and grass and serve as your servant for the rest of my days." I answered him: "Go your own way, wherever you please — take care never to tell anyone that you have seen me here."' The deer king said further: "I would rather rescue driftwood floating on the water and carry it to dry land than rescue a man who cannot be trusted to return a kindness. To plunder wealth and kill one's own lord — such wickedness might still be forgiven; but to receive a kindness and plot betrayal in return — such cruelty is beyond description.' The king, astonished, said: 'What manner of beast is this, holding within it such vast compassion, risking its own life to save another without counting the difficulty — this must surely be a heavenly being!' The king, moved by the deer's words, rejoiced and advanced in virtue, and commanded throughout his kingdom: 'From this day forward, let the deer eat freely as it wishes; whoever dares to harm it shall be punished with death.' The king returned, and when the queen heard that the king had released the deer, her fury shattered her own heart, and she died and fell into the hell of Mount Tai. Śakra, lord of the gods, hearing of the king's resolve to honor benevolence, praised him for it, and, to test his resolve further, transformed a multitude of deer-like creatures to fill the kingdom and devour its grain, so that every young shoot and crop was swept clean from the land. The common people brought complaints before the king. The king said: 'To preserve the kingdom through wickedness and deceit is not as good as losing it while keeping one's word.' Śakra said: 'The king's sincerity is now proven true.' He then sent the deer away, and the harvest grew tenfold, every poison and harm dissolved, and every affliction vanished of itself." The Buddha said to the monks: "The deer king of that time was I myself. The crow was Ānanda. The king was Śāriputra. The drowning man was Devadatta. The king's wife is now Devadatta's own wife." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The most elaborately plotted animal story in this chapter, combining a rescued man's betrayal, a queen's fatal jealousy, a crow's timely warning, and a divinely staged test of the king's own resolve (Śakra sending phantom deer to devastate the harvest) into a single sustained narrative — the drowned man's betrayal and the crow's loyalty are set as deliberate mirror images of each other.

59. The Horse King Who Carried Shipwrecked Merchants Past a Land of Demons〔驅耶馬王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，身為馬王，名曰駈耶，常處海邊渡漂流人。時，海彼岸有婬女鬼，其數甚多，若覩商人，即化為城郭居處，田園伎樂飲食，變為美人，顏華暐曄，要請商人，酒樂娛之。鬼魅惑人，皆留匹偶，一年之間，婬鬼厭故，以鐵錞刺其咽，飲其血、食其肉、吮其髓。馬王遙覩婬鬼噉人，為之流淚。因飛渡海，之海彼岸，獲成擣粳米，馬王食飲畢，登山呼曰：『誰欲度者？』如此三矣。商人聞之喜曰：『常聞神馬哀度危難，今其臻乎！』喜而趣之，曰：『哀度吾等。』馬曰：『爾等去者，婬鬼必當提子示爾，號呼而追。有顧戀之心者，吾去後，鬼必復以鐵錞錞爾咽，飲爾血、吞爾肉。正心存善，可得全命矣。夫欲歸者，騎吾背援吾鬣尾，捉頭頸自由所執，更相攀援，必活覩親也。』商人信用其言者，皆獲全命歸覩六親，婬惑之徒信鬼妖蠱靡不見噉。夫信正去邪，現世永康矣。」佛告諸比丘：「時馬王者，吾身是也。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was born as the king of the horses, named Qūyē, who always dwelt by the sea, ferrying across those cast adrift upon its waters. On the far shore of that sea dwelt lustful demon-women, very numerous, who, upon seeing merchants, would transform their surroundings into cities and dwellings, with gardens, music, food, and drink, and transform themselves into beautiful women of radiant loveliness, inviting the merchants in, entertaining them with wine and music. Bewitched by these demons, the merchants would all stay on as their consorts, and after a year had passed, the lustful demons, growing tired of them, would pierce their throats with an iron spike, drink their blood, eat their flesh, and suck out their marrow. The horse king, seeing from a distance how these demons devoured the merchants, wept for them. Flying across the sea to the far shore, he found some pounded, husked rice; once he had eaten and drunk his fill, he climbed a mountain and called out: 'Who wishes to be carried across?' He called this out three times. The merchants, hearing this, said with delight: 'We have long heard that the divine horse compassionately carries people across danger — now he has arrived!' They rushed to him joyfully, saying: 'Take pity on us and carry us across.' The horse said: 'If you leave now, the lustful demons will surely bring your own children and display them before you, wailing and pursuing after you. This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A companion piece to Story 37 in the precepts chapter, sharing its basic premise of demon-women luring merchants to their deaths, but here the rescuer is an animal (a horse) rather than a human witness, and the story is reclassified from precepts to diligence, crediting the horse's tireless, repeated ferrying rather than any single act of resistance.

60. The Fish King Who Beached Himself to Free His Netted Kin〔魚王本生〕

I will save you all." The fish king then thrust his own head down into the mud and, holding his tail up, lifted the net, and the whole school rushed out to freedom; every fish, having survived, drew close to him in devotion." One of the shortest stories in the collection, its entire dramatic weight compressed into a single image: the fish king thrusting his own head into the mud to hold a net open with his tail, so his entire school can escape through the gap his own body creates.

61. The Turtle King Who Foresaw Danger, and the One Who Would Not Listen〔龜王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，身為龜王，晝夜精進思善方便，令眾生神得還本無。又有龜王，共處深山，俱覩蝘蜒登樹自投，如斯無寧。菩薩占曰：『斯危身之象矣，吾等宜早避之為善。』其一龜王，專愚自由，不從真言。菩薩盡心，濟其從者令得免難。十日之後，象王徒眾就樹燕息，蝘蜒自投墮象耳中，則驚啼呼！羣象犇赴，其來縱橫踐殺諸龜。龜王恚曰：『知事若茲而不指云，吾死爾生，於心善乎？累劫尋爾，逢必殘戮。』」佛告諸比丘：「善占龜者，吾身是也。自專不去者，調達是也。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was born as the king of the turtles, day and night diligently seeking skillful means to bring living beings' own spirits back to their original, formless nature. There was another turtle king dwelling together with him deep in the mountains, and together they observed lizards climbing a tree and throwing themselves down from it, again and again without rest. The bodhisattva divined from this: "This is an omen of danger to come — we should withdraw from this place at once, for our own good." But the other turtle king, obstinate and self-assured, would not heed this true word. The bodhisattva, with all his heart, worked to save those who followed him from this coming disaster. Ten days later, a herd of elephants came to rest beneath that same tree, and a lizard threw itself down and fell into an elephant's ear; the elephant, startled, cried out and bolted, and the whole herd stampeded wildly in every direction, trampling and killing the turtles there. The obstinate turtle king, in his fury, said: "You knew this would happen and did not tell me plainly — that I should die while you live — is this truly the conduct of a good heart? Through kalpa after kalpa I will pursue you, and wherever we meet, I will surely do you harm." The Buddha said to the monks: "The turtle who foresaw the danger and divined it well was I myself. The one who was obstinate and would not leave was Devadatta." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A rare story in this collection where the bodhisattva's foresight goes unheeded and the consequence falls not on himself but on those who ignored his warning — the obstinate turtle king's dying curse, vowing pursuit across future lives, is one of the few instances where a character's resentment toward the bodhisattva is allowed to stand unresolved at the story's own close.

62. The Parrot King Who Feigned Death to Escape a Noisy Court〔鸚鵡王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，為鸚鵡王，徒眾三千。有兩鸚鵡，力幹踰眾，口銜竹莖以為車乘，王乘其上飛止遊戲。常乘莖車，上下前後左右，鸚鵡各五百眾，六面輔翼合有三千，貢獻所珍，娛樂隨時。王深自惟：『眾讙亂德無由獲定，吾將權焉。』託病不食，佯死棄眾。其諸眾者以簟覆之，各捐而去；王興求食。諸鸚鵡眾詣他山鸚鵡王所曰：『吾王喪矣，願為臣僕。』曰：『爾王死者以屍相示，若其真喪，吾將納爾眾。』還取屍霍然不見，四布行索獲其王矣，僉然為禮，復故供養。王曰：『吾尚未喪，爾等委捐。諸佛明訓，覩世無親唯道可宗，沙門以鬚髮為亂志之穢，故捐棄之崇無欲行。爾等讙閙，邪聲亂志，獨而無偶，上聖齊德。』言畢飜飛，閑處窈寂，棄欲無為，思惟定行，諸穢都滅，心如天金。」佛告諸比丘：「時鸚鵡王者，吾身是。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was the king of the parrots, with a following of three thousand. There were two parrots among them, whose strength surpassed the rest, who held bamboo stalks in their beaks to form a kind of carriage, upon which the king rode as they flew about in play. He always rode this bamboo-stalk carriage, surrounded above, below, before, behind, and on either side by five hundred parrots each, six groups in all totaling three thousand, offering him their finest treasures and entertaining him according to the season. The king reflected deeply to himself: "This constant noisy commotion allows no way to attain a settled and virtuous mind — I must resort to some skillful expedient." He feigned illness, refused to eat, and pretended to die, abandoning the crowd. His followers covered him with a mat and departed, each going their own way; the king then rose and sought food for himself. The parrots went to the parrot king of another mountain and said: "Our own king has died — we wish to become your subjects." That king said: "If your king has truly died, show me his body; if he is truly dead, I will accept you into my following." They returned to fetch the body, but it had vanished entirely; searching in every direction, they found their own king again, and all bowed before him in reverence, and resumed their former service and offerings. The king said: "I was not yet dead, and yet you abandoned me so readily. The buddhas' own clear teaching holds that in this world there is no one truly close to another — only the Way itself deserves to be honored. Renunciants shave their heads and beards as a mark of casting off the defilement of a disturbed will, and so give up such attachments to honor a conduct free of desire. Your constant clamor and noise, your wicked sounds disturbing my resolve — being alone, without companions, is what makes the equal virtue of the highest sages." Having said this, he flew off, and settled in a quiet and secluded place, giving up desire and inaction, contemplating the practice of concentration, until every defilement was entirely dissolved, and his heart became like heavenly gold." The Buddha said to the monks: "The parrot king of that time was I myself." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A story built on an unusual form of skillful deception: the parrot king fakes his own death not to escape danger but to escape the mere noise and distraction of his own devoted followers, reasoning that constant company itself has become an obstacle to settled concentration — diligence here means protecting solitude rather than enduring hardship.

63. The Dove King Who Taught His Flock to Fast Their Way to Freedom〔鴿王本生〕

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，身為鴿王，徒眾五百，於國王苑翱翔索食。國王覩之，勅令牧夫率網張捕，其眾巨細無有子遺，籠而閉之，食以粳米肥肉，太官以供肴膳。鴿王見拘，一心念佛，悔過興慈，願：『令眾生拘者得解，疾離八難無如我也。』謂諸鴿曰：『佛經眾戒，貪為元首，貪以致榮者，猶餓夫獲毒飲矣。得志之樂其久若電，眾苦困己其有億載。爾等捐食，身命可全矣。』眾對之曰：『見拘處籠，將欲何冀乎？』王曰：『違替佛教縱情貪欲，靡不喪身者也。』己自捐食，肥體日耗，間關得出，顧謂餘曰：『除貪捐食可如我也。』言畢飛去。」佛告諸比丘：「鴿王者，吾身是也。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was born as the king of the doves, with a following of five hundred, soaring and seeking food within a royal garden. The king of that country, seeing them, ordered his herdsmen to spread nets and capture them; the whole flock, great and small, was left with none escaping, caged and confined, fed on fine rice and fattened meat, to be presented to the royal kitchen as delicacies. The dove king, seeing himself captured, held the Buddha in mind with a single-pointed heart, repenting his faults and giving rise to compassion, vowing: "May all captured beings be released, and may they swiftly escape the eight difficulties, as I now cannot." He said to the other doves: "Among the many precepts of the Buddha's scriptures, greed is named foremost; to pursue glory through greed is like a starving man drinking poison. The pleasure of getting one's own way lasts only as long as a flash of lightning, while the multitude of sufferings that follow endure for a hundred million ages. If you give up eating, your lives may yet be preserved whole." The others answered him: "Caught and confined within this cage, what hope is there left for us?" The king said: "To violate the Buddha's teaching and indulge one's own greedy desire — there is no one who does not lose their life through this." He himself gave up eating, and his body grew leaner day by day, until at last, through the bars of the cage, he was able to slip free; looking back at the others, he said: "Give up greed and abandon eating, and you may become as I am now." Having said this, he flew away." The Buddha said to the monks: "The dove king was I myself." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A close companion to the giving chapter's own dove and parrot stories, but classified under diligence specifically for its emphasis on sustained self-discipline: the dove king's own body visibly wasting away, day by day, until he becomes thin enough to slip through the cage bars, is presented as a direct fruit of tireless self-restraint rather than a single decisive act.

64. The Monk Who Became a Bee to Wake His Sleepy Companion蜜蜂王經〔精進辨比丘本生〕

Chinese Source

佛說蜜蜂王經聞如是：一時佛在舍衛國祇樹給孤獨園。佛告諸弟子：「當勤精進聽聞諷誦，莫得懈怠陰蓋所覆。吾念過去無數劫時，有佛名一切度王如來、無所著、最正覺，時為一切諸天人民不可計數而說經法。是時眾中有兩比丘，其一比丘名精進辯，一比丘名德樂正，共聽經法。精進辯者聞經歡喜，應時即得阿惟越致，神通具足；德樂正者，睡眠不覺，獨無所得。時，精進辯謂德樂正言：『佛者難值，億百千世時乃一出耳，當曼精進為眾作本，如何睡眠？夫睡眠者陰蓋之罪，當自勗勉有覺寤心。』時德樂正，聞其教詔便即經行，於祇樹間甫始經行復住睡眠，如是煩亂不能自定，詣泉水側坐欲思惟，復坐睡眠，時，精進辯便以善權往而度之，化作蜜蜂王，飛趣其眼如欲螫之。時，德樂正驚覺而坐，畏此蜂王，須臾復睡，時，蜜蜂王飛入腋下螫其胸腹，德樂正驚，心中懅悸不敢復睡。時，泉水中有雜色花，憂曇、拘文，種種鮮潔。時，蜜蜂王飛住華上食甘露味，時德樂正端坐視之，畏復飛來不敢復睡。思惟蜂王觀其根本，蜂王食味不出華中，須臾之頃，蜂王睡眠，墮污泥中；身體沐浴已，復還飛住其華上。時，德樂正向蜜蜂王說此偈言：「『是食甘露者，其身得安隱，不當復持歸，遍及其妻子。如何墮泥中，自污其身體，如是為無黠，敗其甘露味。又如此華者，不宜久住中，日沒華還合，求出則不能。當須日光明，爾乃復得出，長夜之疲冥，如是甚勤苦。』「時蜜蜂王，向德樂正，說偈報言：「『佛者譬甘露，聽聞無厭足，不當有懈怠，無益於一切。五道生死海，譬如墮污泥，愛欲所纏裹，無智為甚迷。日出眾華開，譬佛之色身，日沒華還合，世尊般泥曰。值見如來世，當曼精進受，除去睡陰蓋，莫呼佛常在。深法之要慧，不以色因緣，其現有智者，當知為善權。善權之所度，有益不唐舉，而現此變化，亦以一切故。』「時德樂正聽聞其說，即得不起法忍，解諸法本，逮陀隣尼，乃知精進辯善權方便。常獨經行不復懈怠，應時亦得不退轉地。」佛告阿難：「爾時精進辯者，今我身是也。德樂正者，彌勒是也。」佛語阿難：「我爾時俱與彌勒共聽經法，彌勒時睡眠獨無所得。設我爾時不行善權而救度者，彌勒于今在生死中未得度脫。聞是法者常當精進，廣勸一切皆令除去睡眠之蓋，當造光明智慧之本。」說是事時，無央數人皆發無上平等度意。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the Buddha spoke the Sūtra of the Bee King. Thus have I heard: at one time the Buddha was at Jetavana, in Anāthapiṇḍada's Park in the country of Śrāvastī. The Buddha said to his disciples: "You should diligently apply yourselves to hearing, reciting, and studying the teaching, and never let yourselves be covered by the hindrance of sloth. I recall that, countless kalpas ago, there was a buddha named Sarvābhibhū, the Tathāgata, the one attached to nothing, the most perfectly awakened, who at that time was expounding the Dharma to countless gods and human beings. Among the assembly were two monks, one named Vīryamati and the other named Guṇanandin, who listened together to the teaching. Vīryamati, hearing the teaching, rejoiced, and at that very moment attained the stage of irreversibility, his spiritual powers complete; Guṇanandin, however, fell asleep without realizing it, and gained nothing at all. Vīryamati said to Guṇanandin: 'The Buddha is difficult to encounter, appearing only once in a hundred thousand million ages — one should diligently apply oneself as a foundation for benefiting others; how can you fall asleep? Sleep is the fault of the hindrance of torpor — you must exert yourself and keep a wakeful mind.' Guṇanandin, hearing this instruction, then began to walk in meditation, but, having only just begun walking among the Jeta trees, again fell into sleep while standing still; troubled and unable to steady himself in this way, he went to sit by a spring, meaning to meditate, but again fell asleep while seated. At that time Vīryamati, employing a skillful expedient, went to deliver him, transforming himself into a bee-king, flying at his eyes as though to sting him. Guṇanandin, startled, woke and sat up, fearful of this bee-king, but soon fell asleep again; the bee-king then flew beneath his arm and stung his chest and belly, and Guṇanandin, startled and his heart pounding with fright, no longer dared to sleep. "In the spring there grew flowers of every color, udumbara and pundarīka blossoms, each fresh and pure. The bee-king flew and settled upon a flower, feeding on its sweet nectar; Guṇanandin, sitting upright, watched it, fearing it might fly at him again, and so dared not sleep. Reflecting on the bee-king, he observed its very nature: the bee-king, feeding on the nectar, never left the flower itself; but after a while, the bee-king, growing drowsy, fell into the muddy water below; having bathed its own body there, it flew back and settled once more upon the flower. Guṇanandin then spoke this verse to the bee-king: 'You who feed on this sweet nectar, your body finds ease and peace — you should not then carry it home to share with your own wife and children. Why, then, do you fall into the mud, defiling your own body there? To act in such a way is without wisdom, ruining the very taste of the nectar. And this flower, too, is not a fitting place to dwell in for very long — when the sun sets, the flower closes again, and whoever seeks to leave then cannot. Only when the sun's light returns can one find one's way out again — the long night's weary darkness is a very great hardship indeed.' "The bee-king then answered Guṇanandin with this verse in return: 'The Buddha may be likened to sweet nectar — one should listen to him without ever growing weary or satisfied, and never fall into laziness, which benefits no one at all. The sea of birth and death across the five paths is like falling into the mud, bound up in craving and desire, with no wisdom, utterly confused. When the sun rises, the many flowers open — this is like the Buddha's own physical form; when the sun sets, the flower closes again — this is the World-Honored One's own final nirvāṇa. To encounter the age of the Tathāgata, one should exert oneself diligently, casting off the hindrance of sleepy torpor, and never assume the Buddha remains forever. The essential wisdom of this profound teaching is not a matter of outward appearance — the wise, seeing this, will recognize it as a skillful expedient. What a skillful expedient delivers is never a wasted effort, and this transformation I now show you is likewise for the sake of all beings.' As for the sacrifice of an ox to seek healing for a sick father: to seek life for one by taking the life of another is a very grave inauspiciousness, like drinking poison in hopes of curing an illness. That father is himself soon to die, and, once dead, will become an ox, and, through life after life of being led to slaughter, will suffer disaster without end. Now, this very ox being offered in sacrifice, when its own life ends, its spirit will return and take on a human body, escaping this cycle of grief and suffering — this too is why I smiled. As for the child who scratched his mother's face — this child was, in a past life, the man's lesser wife, and the mother was, in that life, his principal wife; the daughter's own nature was deeply lustful, and she harbored jealousy in her heart, always treating the principal wife with cruelty. That lesser wife, holding her resentment and hatred, at the end of her life was reborn as the principal wife's own son, and has now come to take his revenge, clawing at her face and wounding her body — and so she dares not resent it, and this too is why I smiled. The hearts of living beings hold no constancy at all — what was once hated is now loved; what constancy, then, could there ever be? All of this is seen but not understood within a single lifetime — how much less understood across many kalpas? The scripture says: "Whoever is blocked by attachment to beauty is blind to the great Way; whoever listens exclusively to wicked sounds will never hear the echo of the Buddha's own voice." This is why I smiled. Worldly glory is like a flash of lightning, vanishing in an instant — you should awaken to impermanence, and not keep company with the foolish, but instead cultivate virtuous conduct and the wondrous practice of the six perfections. I will now return to my former dwelling, but I shall surely come again to your door another day.' Having said this, he vanished at once, and the woman returned home in a state of troubled unease. Observing fasts with solemn devotion, longing and reverent, word of this spread throughout the entire kingdom, and there was no one, from the king down to his officials, who did not honor and respect her for it. "The merchant later did indeed appear at her gate, his appearance ugly and his clothing worn, and said: 'My friend is within — call her out to me.' The gatekeeper went in to report this, describing his appearance exactly. The woman came out and said: 'You are not my friend.' Śakra laughed and said: 'You do not recognize me even when I merely change my form and clothing — how much less, then, would you recognize me across different lifetimes, casting off one form to take on another?' He said further: 'Be diligent in honoring the Buddha — the Buddha is difficult to encounter in this world, and a monk of lofty conduct is difficult to find and serve; life hangs by the span of a single breath — do not let yourself be led astray by worldly delusion.' Having said this, he vanished. The whole kingdom rejoiced and marveled at this, and each took up the lofty and wondrous practice of the six perfections." The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "The woman of that time is now Maitreya. Śakra, lord of the gods, was I myself." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A structural twin to Story 65's three-part smile, but expanded here to five separate riddles, each requiring Śakra's own explanation before the pattern of the story becomes clear — the device of interlocking micro-narratives (a father reborn as a son, an ox reborn as a child, a spurned lesser wife reborn as an avenging son) recurs across several of this fascicle's stories as a favored structure for teaching karmic causation through multiple simultaneous examples rather than a single chain.

72. The Woman Who Asked the Buddha for a Husband Without Jealousy女人求願經〔婦人本生〕

Chinese Source

女人求願經「昔者菩薩，身為女人，厥婿稟氣兇愚妬忌，每出商行，以妻囑隣獨母，母奉佛戒為清信行。時佛入國，王逮臣民靡不受戒，獨母聞經，還為婦說之。婦喜歎曰：『斯即無上正真道、最正覺者也。』從母聞佛，即遙稽首。齋日母曰：『可往聽化乎？』婦喜曰：『可。』尋之城外，忽存婿妬，悵然不悅，旋居自鄙：『吾殃重乎？』母還為陳：『天龍鬼神、帝王臣民聽經，或得沙門四道者，或受菩薩決者。佛時難值，經法難聞，爾還為乎？』婦聞佛德，流淚具陳婿妬之意。母曰：『可試一行。』婦曰：『敬諾。』明日即隨母行覩佛，五體投地却立靜心，視佛相好念佛清淨真是天尊。佛問女：『爾來何願？』即稽首而對：『我聞佛為無上正真道、最正覺、道法御、天人師，德如恒沙，智若虛空，六通四達得一切智，勢來請尊，願佛哀我。』世尊告曰：『佛為一切護，恣汝所願。』女人稽首曰：『夫人處世未獲本無者，皆以欲故為匹偶居，令我世世與至德偶居，同志無嫉妬行。二曰，身口意行端正絕世。三曰，世世虔奉三尊，心垢日消，進道無倦，諸佛祐助，眾邪不能遏，必獲一切智，濟眾生難。』眾祐歎曰：『善哉，善哉！令汝得之。』婦大歡喜稽首，退歸本居。厥婿賈還，乘舟水行，當以斯日至。天帝覩婦高行，發願無雙，助喜歎善，為興風雨，住其舟行明日乃臻。「婦後壽終，神生有道之家，容華光世，年長出適為國儒士之妻，國稱高賢。時，婿入海採寶欲濟窮民，婦居家以禮自衛，猶城衛寇，國王后妃大臣妻妾靡不仰則，詣門雲集稟婦德儀。婦夜寐覺，憶世無常：『榮富猶幻孰獲長存，躬為坏舟我神載之，猶獲月影望天寶者也。勞心苦身何益於己？夢幻皆空，天神世榮其歸若茲矣。明晨當索無上正真、天中之天為吾師焉。』晨興即覩石塔在庭，佛像金耀，琢壁書經，歎佛為眾聖之師三界獨步。婦喜歎曰：『是則如來、應儀、正真道、最正覺者乎！』即五體投地遶廟三匝，散華燒香，然燈懸繒，晨夜肅虔，稽首恭禮。王后國婦請承清風退邪崇真。隣有兇夫賈逢婦婿，曰：『子妻造妖，虛立鬼廟，朝暮香熏呪咀妖蠱，願令爾喪，不祥之甚。』婿歸，婦啟曰：『妾前一夜覺世無常，晨覩宗靈，無上正真絕妙之像來在中庭，妾今供事，燒香然燈，懸繒奉華，朝夕禮拜，稽首自歸，子當事之，必合聖則。』婿大歡喜，一心肅虔，國人巨細僉然承風，如是八萬四千餘歲。」佛告鶖鷺子：「爾時婦人者，吾身是也。時婿者，彌勒是也，獨母者，鶖鷺子是也。隣兇夫者，調達是也。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sūtra of the Woman's Petition. "Long ago the bodhisattva was born as a woman, whose husband, by nature, was wicked, foolish, and consumed by jealousy; whenever he traveled on business, he entrusted his wife to the care of a neighboring widow, who herself honored the Buddha's precepts and practiced as a devout laywoman. At that time the Buddha entered the country, and the king, together with his ministers and people, all received the precepts; the widowed mother, hearing the scripture, returned and told the wife of it. The wife, delighted, exclaimed: 'This is truly the unsurpassed, true and right Way, the most perfectly awakened one!' Hearing of the Buddha from the widow, she bowed her head from afar in reverence. On a fast day, the widow said: 'Shall we go to hear his teaching?' The wife said with delight, 'Yes.' But as they set out toward the edge of the city, she suddenly remembered her husband's jealous nature, and, troubled, lost her earlier joy, and, returning home, reproached herself: 'Is my own misfortune too heavy to overcome?' The widow returned and told her: 'Gods, dragons, spirits, kings, ministers, and people all came to hear the teaching — some attained one of the four fruits of a monk's path, and some received the prediction of bodhisattvahood. The Buddha is difficult to encounter, and his teaching is difficult to hear — will you truly turn back now?' The wife, hearing of the Buddha's virtue, wept, and told her fully of her husband's jealous nature. The widow said: 'Try going, just this once.' The wife said, 'I will do so respectfully.' The next day she followed the widow and saw the Buddha, prostrating herself fully on the ground, and, withdrawing to stand, stilled her mind, gazing upon the Buddha's fine marks, and, contemplating the Buddha's pure nature, recognized him truly as the honored one of heaven. The Buddha asked the woman: 'What is your wish in coming here?' She bowed her head and answered: 'I have heard that the Buddha is the unsurpassed, true and right Way, the most perfectly awakened, the guide of the Dharma, the teacher of gods and men, his virtue as vast as the sands of the Ganges, his wisdom as boundless as empty space, possessing the six spiritual powers and the four means of understanding and attaining complete wisdom — moved by your power, I have come to petition the honored one; I beg the Buddha to take pity on me.' The World-Honored One told her: 'The Buddha protects all beings equally — ask freely for whatever you wish.' The woman bowed her head and said: 'People in this world who have not yet attained their own original nature all form pairs out of desire; I wish that in every life to come, I may be paired with a spouse of the utmost virtue, sharing the same resolve, free of any jealous conduct between us. Second, I wish that my own bodily, verbal, and mental conduct may be upright, surpassing that of the world. Third, I wish that in every life to come, I may reverently honor the Three Jewels, my mind's defilements dissolving day by day, advancing on the Way without ever growing weary, protected always by the buddhas, so that no wicked force can obstruct me, until at last I attain complete wisdom and relieve living beings from their hardships.' The Blessed One sighed and said: 'Excellent, excellent! May you attain exactly this.' The wife, overjoyed, bowed her head, and returned home to her former dwelling. Her husband, returning from his trading journey, traveled by boat upon the water, and was due to arrive on that very day. Śakra, lord of the gods, witnessing the wife's lofty conduct, her vow unmatched by any other, helped her rejoice and praised her as good, raising wind and rain to delay her husband's boat, so that he did not arrive until the following day. "When the wife's life later came to its end, her spirit was reborn into a household that held the Way, her beauty radiant before the world; when she grew to maturity, she was married to a Confucian scholar of the kingdom, and the kingdom praised her as a woman of the highest worth. At that time her husband went out to sea to gather treasures, wishing to relieve the kingdom's poor, and the wife, remaining at home, guarded herself with proper ritual, as a city guards against invaders, and the kingdom's queen, consorts, ministers, and their wives and concubines all looked up to her as a model, coming to her gate in crowds to receive instruction in her own virtuous conduct. One night, waking from sleep, the wife reflected on the impermanence of the world: 'Glory and wealth are like an illusion — who could ever hold onto them forever? This body is like a leaking boat carrying my own spirit, like reaching for the moon's reflection while gazing at a heavenly treasure. What benefit is there to myself in wearing out my mind and exhausting my body over such things? Dreams and illusions are both empty, and the glory of gods and of this world return in just the same way. Tomorrow morning I shall seek out the unsurpassed, true and right one, the heaven of heavens, to be my own teacher.' Rising the next morning, she saw a stone stūpa standing in her own courtyard, the Buddha's image gleaming gold, scriptures carved and inscribed upon its walls, praising the Buddha as the teacher of every sage, walking alone through the three realms. The wife, delighted, exclaimed: 'Is this not the Tathāgata, the worthy one, the true and right Way, the most perfectly awakened one?' She prostrated herself fully on the ground, circled the shrine three times, scattered flowers, burned incense, lit lamps, and hung silk banners, reverent morning and night, bowing her head in respectful worship. The queen and the women of the kingdom sought to receive her pure influence, casting aside wickedness to honor the truth. A wicked man in the neighborhood, a merchant, met her husband upon his return, and said: 'Your wife has built some strange shrine, an empty temple to some ghost, burning incense morning and night, chanting curses and practicing sorcery, wishing for your death — this is most inauspicious.' Her husband returned home, and the wife told him: 'One night I awoke and reflected on the impermanence of the world, and the next morning I saw a sacred spirit, an unsurpassed and truly wondrous image, standing in the courtyard; I now honor and serve it, burning incense, lighting lamps, hanging banners, and offering flowers, bowing in reverence morning and night, and have taken refuge in it of my own accord. You too should honor it, and it will surely accord with the sacred principle.' Her husband, overjoyed, honored it with a single, reverent heart, and the people of the kingdom, high and low, all received this influence in the same way, and it continued thus for eighty-four thousand years and more." The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "The woman of that time was I myself. The husband of that time is now Maitreya. The widowed mother was Śāriputra. The wicked neighbor was Devadatta." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A story explicitly framed around a woman's own petition and the specific terms of her request — freedom from jealousy in her future marriages, integrity of conduct, and steady spiritual progress — rather than around any act of self-sacrifice, making it one of the few stories in this collection where the central virtuous act is a spoken vow rather than a physical deed.

73. The Widow Who Vowed to Become a Man in Order to Attain Buddhahood然燈授決經〔獨母本生〕

Chinese Source

然燈授決經「昔者菩薩，身為女人，少寡守節，歸命三尊，處貧樂道，精進不倦，蠲除兇利，賣膏為業。時有沙門，年在西夕，志存高行，不遑文學，內否之類謂之無明矣。禮敬有偏，終始無就，分衛麻油以供佛前，獨母照然，貢不缺日。有一除饉，稽首佛足，叉手質曰：『斯老除饉，其雖尠明，戒具行高，然燈供養，後獲何福？』世尊歎曰：『善哉問也！是老除饉，却無數劫，當為如來、無所著、正真道、最正覺，項有重光，將導三界，眾生得度，其為無數。』獨母聞之，馳詣佛所，稽首陳曰：『除饉然燈，膏即吾所貢云，其當獲為無上正真道，將導眾生還神本無，天人鬼龍靡不逸豫。唯願加哀，復授吾決。』佛告女人：『女身不得為佛、緣一覺道、梵、釋、魔天、飛行皇帝，斯尊巍巍非女人身所得作也。夫欲獲彼，當捐穢體受清淨身。』女稽首曰：『今當捐之。』還居淨浴，遙拜而曰：『夫身者四大之有，非吾長保也。』登樓願曰：『以今穢身惠眾生之飢渴者，乞獲男躬，受決為佛。若有濁世眾生盲冥背正向邪無知佛者，吾當於彼世拯濟之也。』自高投下，觀者寒慄。佛知至意，化令地軟猶天綩綖，覩身無害，即化為男，厥喜無量。馳詣佛所踊躍而云：『受世尊恩，已獲淨身。唯願加哀，授吾尊決。』佛歎之曰：『爾之勇猛世所希有，必得為佛無懷疑望，然燈除饉，其得佛時當授汝號。』天人鬼龍聞當為佛，皆向拜賀，還居咨歎，各加精進。爾時，勸發群生不可計數。」佛告鶖鷺子：「時老比丘者，錠光佛是也。獨母者，吾身是。菩薩銳志度無極精進如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sūtra of Dīpaṃkara's Prediction. "Long ago the bodhisattva was born as a woman, widowed young and holding to her chastity, taking refuge in the Three Jewels, dwelling in poverty and yet delighting in the Way, diligent and untiring, casting off wicked profit, and making her living by selling oil. There was, at that time, a monk advanced in years, his resolve set on lofty conduct, with no time for scholarly learning, and those inwardly wanting in understanding called him ignorant. Though his reverence and worship were uneven, and his practice never quite reached completion, he offered sesame oil, obtained through begging, before the Buddha's image without fail; the widow, seeing this, contributed her own offering day after day without ever missing one. A certain fasting monk, bowing his head at the Buddha's feet and joining his hands, questioned him: 'This old, fasting monk, though his understanding is somewhat lacking, holds fully to the precepts and his conduct is lofty, and offers lamps in devotion — what blessing will he obtain in the future?' The World-Honored One sighed and said: 'A fine question indeed! This old, fasting monk will, after countless kalpas, become the Tathāgata, the one attached to nothing, the true and right Way, the most perfectly awakened, with a radiant light at his neck, and will guide the three realms, delivering living beings beyond all number.' The widow, hearing this, hurried to the Buddha, bowed her head, and said: 'The oil offered by that fasting monk for his lamp was in fact contributed by myself — will I too attain the unsurpassed, true and right Way, and guide living beings back to their own original nature, so that gods, humans, spirits, and dragons may all find peace and ease? I beg only that you take pity on me, and grant me a prediction as well.' The Buddha said to the woman: 'A woman's body cannot become a buddha, a pratyekabuddha, Brahmā, Śakra, a demon-king of the heavens, or a flying, wheel-turning emperor — these towering and exalted states cannot be attained in a woman's body. If you wish to attain them, you must cast off this defiled form and receive instead a pure body.' The woman bowed her head and said: 'I will cast it off now.' Returning home, she bathed herself in purity, and, bowing from afar, said: 'This body is but a composite of the four elements — it is not something I can keep forever.' Climbing to a high tower, she made this vow: 'I now give this defiled body to relieve the hunger and thirst of living beings; I beg to be granted a man's own body, and to receive the prediction of buddhahood. If there are living beings in some future age of turbidity, blind and confused, turning their backs on the truth to follow what is false, unaware of the Buddha, I will deliver them then in that very world.' She then threw herself down from the height, and all who witnessed it trembled with dread. The Buddha, knowing her utmost sincerity, caused the ground to soften like a heavenly cushion, so that her body came to no harm, and transformed her at once into a man; his joy was beyond measure. He hurried to the Buddha, and, leaping with joy, said: 'Having received the World-Honored One's own grace, I have now obtained a pure body. I beg only that you take pity on me, and grant me the honored prediction.' The Buddha said in praise: 'Your courage is a rarity such as the world has seldom seen — you shall surely attain buddhahood, and need have no further doubt. Because you offered a lamp to this fasting monk, when he attains buddhahood, he shall bestow upon you your own title.' Gods, humans, spirits, and dragons, hearing that he would become a buddha, all came to bow and offer their congratulations, and, returning to their own dwellings, sighed in praise and each redoubled their own diligence. At that time, the number of living beings thereby encouraged and inspired was beyond all counting." The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "The elderly monk of that time was the Buddha Dīpaṃkara. The widow was I myself." This is how the bodhisattva, through his sharp and eager resolve, practiced the boundless perfection of diligence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This is this collection's own version of a widely told story about a poor widow's lamp-offering, here given an unusual final turn: rather than ending with the widow's offering alone being praised, the story pivots to her own request for a prediction of buddhahood, which the Buddha initially refuses on the grounds that a woman's body cannot attain it, and resolves only through her own literal, physical transformation into a man after a leap from a high tower — a passage worth reading alongside this collection's other treatments of gender, which this translation presents without softening, exactly as the source text states it.
