The Translation

11. King Poye Who Gave His Own Head波耶王經〔波耶王本生〕

Chinese Source

波耶王經「昔者波羅㮈國王名波耶，治國以仁，干戈廢，杖楚滅，囹圄毀，路無呼嗟，群生得所，國豐民熾，諸天歎仁。王城廣長四百里，圍千六百里，王日飯此中人，皆從其願。鄰國聞其國豐熟，災害消滅，與臣謀曰：『彼國豐熟兆民富樂，吾欲得之，往必剋矣。』臣妾僉曰：『喜從王願。』即興師之仁國。「仁國群臣以聞，欲距之矣。仁王慘然而曰：『以吾一人之身，戮兆民身，愛吾一人命，杌兆民之命，一口再食、一身數衣，與時何諍？而去春天之德，取豺狼之殘乎？吾寧去一世之命，不去大志，恕己安群生，蓋天之仁也。』權謂臣曰：『各退，明日更議。』夜則踰城，遁邁入山坐一樹下。「有梵志來，其年六十，問王曰：『彼仁國王萬福無恙乎？』答曰：『彼王已喪命矣。』梵志聞之頓地哀慟。王問之曰：『汝哀何甚重乎？』答曰：『吾聞彼王，仁逮群生潤如帝釋，故馳歸命；而彼凋喪，吾老窮矣。』王曰：『彼仁王者，我則是也。隣國王聞吾國豐熟，民熾寶多，命其武士曰：「得吾首者，賞男女之使各千人，馬千疋、牛千頭，金銀各千斤。」今子取吾首，金冠及劒為明證，之彼王所，賞重多，可為傳世之資。吾心欣然也。』答曰：『不仁逆道，寧死不為也。』王曰：『斯翁恃吾以活，而令窮哉？吾今以首惠汝，令汝無罪。』起稽首十方，流涕誓曰：『群生危者，吾當安之；背真向邪者，吾當令歸命三尊。今以首拔子之窮，令子無罪矣。』引劒自毀，以濟彼難。「梵志以首冠劒詣彼王所，王問舊臣：『仁王力當千人，而為此子所獲乎？』舊臣頓首躃地，哀慟痛莫能對。更問梵志，梵志本末陳之。兆民路踊巷哭，或吐血者，或息絕而尸視者。彼王逮臣武士，巨細靡不噢咿。王仰天長嘆曰：『吾無道哉！殘天仁子矣。』取仁王尸及首，連之以金薄，其身坐著殿上，三十二年為天子，後乃立其子為王。隣國靡不子愛之也。仁王壽終即生天上。」佛告諸比丘：「仁王者，我身是。隣國王者，目連是。其國群臣者，今諸比丘是。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a king of Vārāṇasī named Poye, who governed his country with benevolence — weapons of war set aside, the rod and lash abolished, prisons torn down, no cries of complaint heard on any road. All his living subjects had what they needed; the country prospered and its people flourished, and the gods themselves praised his benevolence. His royal city stretched four hundred leagues across and sixteen hundred leagues around, and the king daily fed all who dwelt within it, granting every request. A neighboring king, hearing how rich and prosperous this country was, its calamities all dispelled, plotted with his ministers: "That country is rich and its countless people are wealthy and content — I wish to take it, and if I go, I am sure to conquer it." His ministers all said, "We are glad to follow the king's wish," and he raised an army against the benevolent country. The ministers of the benevolent country heard of this and wished to resist. The benevolent king, grieved, said: "To save the bodies of countless people by means of my own single body, to love my own one life while destroying the lives of countless others — one mouth eating twice, one body wearing several sets of clothes — what quarrel is there worth this? Why should I abandon the virtue of springtime's heaven to seize the cruelty of a wolf? I would rather give up a single lifetime's span than give up my great resolve — to forgive myself and give peace to the multitude, this is the benevolence of heaven itself." He said to his ministers as a stratagem: "Withdraw for now — we will deliberate further tomorrow." That night he climbed over the city wall and fled into the mountains, where he sat beneath a tree. A brahmin came by, sixty years old, and asked the king: "Is the benevolent king of that country in good health, free of trouble?" He answered: "That king has already lost his life." The brahmin, hearing this, threw himself to the ground in grief. The king asked him: "Why do you grieve so deeply?" He answered: "I heard that king's kindness reached every living being, his grace like Śakra's own, and so I hurried here to entrust my life to him — and now he has perished, and I am old and destitute." The king said: "That benevolent king — I am he. The neighboring king, hearing how rich my country was, its people flourishing and its treasures many, commanded his warriors: 'Whoever brings me his head will be rewarded with a thousand male and a thousand female servants, a thousand horses, a thousand oxen, and a thousand catties each of gold and silver.' Now, if you take my head, with my golden crown and sword as proof, and bring them to that king, the reward will be very great, enough to provide for you for the rest of your life. My heart rejoices at this." He answered: "This is not benevolence but a betrayal of the Way — I would rather die than do such a thing." The king said: "This old man relies on me to live, and shall I let him remain destitute instead? I now give you my head as a gift, so that you bear no crime in taking it." He rose, bowed his head to the ten directions, and, weeping, made this vow: "Whatever living being is in danger, I will bring them peace; whoever turns their back on truth to follow what is false, I will bring them to take refuge in the Three Jewels. Now, with my head, I lift you out of your poverty, and you bear no crime in this." He drew his sword and took his own life, to relieve the old man's hardship. The brahmin, crowning his own head with the king's, carried the sword to that other king. The king asked his old ministers: "The benevolent king's strength was a match for a thousand men — how could this man have captured him?" The old ministers bowed their heads to the ground, so grieved they could not answer. He asked the brahmin again, and the brahmin told him the whole story from beginning to end. The countless people wept in the streets and alleys; some vomited blood, some breathed their last and became corpses where they stood. From the king down to his warriors, high and low alike, there was no one who did not wail in sorrow. The king looked up to heaven and sighed at length: "I have acted without the Way! I have destroyed heaven's own benevolent son." He took the benevolent king's body and head and joined them together with gold leaf, and seated the body upon the throne, ruling as the Son of Heaven for thirty-two years, after which he set up the benevolent king's own son as ruler. The neighboring kingdoms all loved him as their own child. When the benevolent king's life came to its end, he was reborn in the heavens. The Buddha said to the monks: "The benevolent king was I myself. The king of the neighboring country was Maudgalyāyana. That country's ministers are now these monks before me." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This story compresses the self-sacrifice pattern of earlier stories into an even starker form: rather than a staged test by Śakra, an old man's simple grief becomes the occasion for the king's decision, and the exchange happens without any divine intervention or reversal. Note the story's own frank acknowledgment of its logic — a king willing to give his own head to spare a poor man's shame at killing him, which the rival king who receives it recognizes immediately as a wrong he has committed rather than a victory he has won.

12. Prince Kalyāṇa and His Faithless Wife波羅奈國王經〔迦蘭王本生〕

Chinese Source

波羅㮈國王經「昔者波羅㮈國王太子名迦蘭，兄弟二人，父王喪身，以國相讓，無適立者。兄將妻遁邁入山學道，止臨江水。時，他國有犯罪者，國政杌其手足、截其鼻耳，敗舡流之。罪人呼天相屬。道士聞之愴然，悲楚曰：『彼何人哉？厥困尤甚。夫弘慈恕己危命，濟群生之厄者，斯大士之業矣。』投身于水盪波截流，引舟著岸，負之還居，勤心養護，瘡愈命全。積年有四，慈育無倦。「妻淫無避與罪人通，謀殺其婿，曰：『子殺之，吾與子居。』罪人曰：『彼賢者矣，奈何殺之？』妻辭如前，罪人曰：『吾無手足，不能殺也。』妻曰：『子坐，吾自有計矣。』詐為首疾，告其婿曰：『斯必山神所為也，吾欲解之。明日從君以求祈福。』婿曰：『大善！』明日遂行山岸，高四十里，三面壁立，覩者皆懼。妻曰：『術法，子向日立，吾自祭之。』婿即向日，妻佯遶之數周，推落山下。山半有樹，樹葉緻厚而柔軟也，道士攀枝得立，樹菓甘美食之自全。樹側有龜，亦日食菓，覩樹有人，懼不敢往。其飢五日冒昧趣菓，兩俱無害，遂相摩近。道士超踊騎龜，龜驚跳下地；天神祐之，兩俱無損。「因還故國，弟以國讓兄。兄以恕己弘慈拯濟群生。王治其國，日出布施，四百里內，人車馬眾寶飯食自由，東西南北惠育如之。王功名周著，十方歎德。「妻以婿為死，國人無識己者，負杌婿入國，自陳：『結髮室家遭世衰亂，身更凋殘，服天王慈惠，故來乞匃。』國人嘉其如斯，教之曰：『天王普慈，育逮群生，明日當出東門布施。汝其逆之，貴汝善行，賜汝必多。』「明日從王乞匃，王默識之，具為群臣說妻本末。一臣曰：『當燒之。』一臣曰：『斬之。』執法大臣曰：『夫罪莫大于去正入邪，為悖逆之行者矣。當釘兇人著蠱女之背使長負焉。』群臣僉曰：『善哉。』『從其所好，執持之明矣。』王以十善化，民靡不欣戴。王逮臣民，終生天上；罪人夫妻，死入地獄。」佛告諸比丘：「時王者，我身是。罪人者，調達是，妻者，懷杅女子是。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the crown prince of the king of Vārāṇasī was named Kalyāṇa; he had one brother, and when their father the king died, he yielded the throne to his brother rather than take it for himself, since no successor had been formally named. The elder brother took his wife and fled into the mountains to study the Way, settling by a riverbank. At that time, in another country, there was a condemned criminal whom the government had mutilated, cutting off his hands and feet, his nose and ears, and set him adrift on a broken raft. The condemned man cried out to heaven again and again. The ascetic, hearing him, was moved to grief and said: "What man is this, in such extreme suffering? To show great compassion, to risk one's own life for another's sake, to relieve the multitude of their hardship — this is the work of a great being." He threw himself into the water, breasting the waves and cutting across the current, drew the raft to shore, carried the man home on his back, and tended him with devoted care until his wounds healed and his life was whole. For four years he nursed him with tireless compassion. But the ascetic's wife was unfaithful, and secretly took up with the condemned man, and plotted to kill her husband, saying: "If you kill him, I will live with you." The condemned man said: "He is a worthy man — how could I kill him?" The wife repeated her words as before, and the condemned man said: "I have no hands or feet — I cannot kill him." The wife said: "Sit still — I have a plan of my own." She feigned a headache, and told her husband: "This must be the doing of a mountain spirit — I wish to appease it. Tomorrow, come with me to pray for good fortune." The husband said, "Very well." The next day they went to a cliff, forty leagues high, sheer on three sides, so fearsome that anyone who saw it trembled. The wife said: "For this rite, you must face the sun, and I will perform the offering." The husband faced the sun as told, and the wife pretended to circle him several times before shoving him off the cliff. Halfway down the mountainside there was a tree with dense, soft leaves; the ascetic, catching hold of its branches, was able to stand there, and its fruit was sweet and sustained him. Beside the tree lived a tortoise, who also fed on its fruit daily; seeing a man in the tree, it was afraid and would not approach. After five days of hunger, the ascetic, driven by need, reached carelessly for the fruit; neither harmed the other, and they gradually drew near one another. The ascetic leapt and mounted the tortoise's back; the tortoise, startled, leapt down to the ground below — and by the protection of a heavenly spirit, neither of them was harmed. He then made his way back to his former country, and his younger brother yielded the throne to him. He governed the country with the same self-forgiving, far-reaching compassion, relieving the multitude, giving alms daily, and within four hundred leagues, people, carts, horses, every treasure and provision of food were freely available to all, his kindness reaching out equally in every direction. His achievements and fame spread everywhere, and the ten directions praised his virtue. His wife, believing her husband dead, and not recognized by anyone in that country, carried the crippled condemned man into the kingdom and declared: "We are a married couple caught in the world's misfortunes, our bodies broken and withered; hearing of the Heavenly King's kindness and giving, we have come to beg." The people of the country, praising her for this, instructed her: "The Heavenly King's compassion is universal, reaching every living being — tomorrow he will go out the eastern gate to give alms. Go and meet him there; your virtuous conduct will be honored, and your reward will surely be great." The next day she came to beg from the king. The king recognized her silently, and told his ministers the whole story of his wife's past. One minister said, "She should be burned." Another said, "She should be beheaded." The minister in charge of law said: "No crime is greater than abandoning the true path to follow what is false, committing an act of such rebellion against nature. The wicked man should be nailed to the back of this poisonous woman, that he may bear the weight of her for the rest of his life." All the ministers said together, "Excellent" — "let her have exactly what she desired, and hold her to it plainly." The king governed by the ten virtues, and there was no one among his people who did not rejoice and honor him. The king, together with his ministers and people, at the end of their lives were all reborn in the heavens; the condemned man and his wife, at their deaths, fell into hell. The Buddha said to the monks: "The king of that time was I myself. The condemned man was Devadatta. The wife was the woman who bore the poisoned vessel." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A story built in two distinct halves that only connect at the very end: an act of raw compassion (rescuing a drowning, mutilated criminal) followed by that same rescued man's betrayal, engineered by the ascetic's own wife. The bodhisattva's second miraculous survival — falling from a cliff onto a tree, then escaping on a tortoise's back — receives no explanation beyond the bare fact of it, in keeping with this collection's general reluctance to dwell on the mechanics of its own miracles.

13. King Sarvadatta, Who Gave Himself and His Wife薩和檀王經〔薩和檀王本生〕

Chinese Source

薩和檀王經「昔者國王號薩和檀，解曰一切施也，有所求索不逆人意。布施如是，其王名字流聞八方，莫不聞知。時，文殊師利欲往試之，化作年少婆羅門，從異國來詣王宮門，語守門者：『我從遠來，欲見大王。』時守門者即白如此。王甚歡喜，即出奉迎，如子見父，前為作禮，便請令坐。問訊：『道人所從來耶？冒涉塗路得無疲倦？』逝心言：『我在他國聞王功德，故來相見，今欲乞匃。』王言：『大善！所欲得者，莫自疑難。今我名為一切之施，欲求何等？』婆羅門言：『我不用餘，欲得王身與我作奴，及王夫人為我作婢。若能爾者，便隨我去。』王甚歡悅，報言：『大善！今我身者，定自可得，願屬道人供給使令。其夫人者，大國王女，當往問之。』「時，王即入語夫人言：『今有道人，年少端正，從遠方來，欲乞我身持用作奴，今復并欲索卿作婢，當如之何？』其夫人言：『王報云何？』王言：『我已許之作奴，未許卿耳。』時夫人言：『王為相棄，獨自得便，不念度我。』是時夫人即隨王出，白道人言：『願得以身供道人使。』「時婆羅門復語王言：『審實爾不？吾今欲去。』王白道人：『我生布施，未曾有悔，從道人耳。』逝心曰：『汝當隨我，皆悉徒跣，不得著履，當如奴法，莫得不掩。』王與夫人，皆言：『唯諾。從大家教，不敢違命。』時，婆羅門便將奴婢涉道而去。文殊師利即以化人，代其王處及夫人身，領理國事令其如故。「王夫人者，本大國王女，端正無雙，手足柔軟，生長深宮不更寒苦。又復重身懷妊數月，步隨大家舉身皆痛，足底破傷不能復前，疲極在後。時，婆羅門還顧罵言：『汝今作婢，當如婢法，不可以汝本時之態。』夫人長跪白言：『不敢！但小疲極住止息耳。』㖑言：『疾來，促隨我後。』前到國市別賣奴婢，各與一主相去數里。「時，有長者買得此奴，使守斯舍，諸有埋者令收其稅不得妄動。是時婢者，所屬大家夫人甚妒，晨夜令作初不懈息。其後數日時婢㝃娠所生男兒，夫人恚言：『汝為婢使，那得此兒？』促取殺之。隨大家教，即殺其兒，持行埋之。往到奴所，得共相見，言：『生一男兒，今日已死。不持錢來，今寧可得唐埋之不？』其奴報曰：『大家甚急！備聞此者，罪我不小。卿促持去，更索餘處，不須住此。』王與夫人雖得相見，不說勤苦，各無怨心。「如是言語須臾之頃，恍惚如夢，王及夫人自然還在本國中宮正殿上坐，如前不異，及諸群臣後宮婇女，皆悉如故，所生太子亦自然活。王及夫人心內自疑，何緣致此？文殊師利在虛空中，坐七寶蓮華上，現身色相，讚言：『善哉！今汝布施，至誠如是。』王與夫人踊躍歡喜，即前作禮。文殊師利為說經法，三千剎土為大震動，覆一國人，皆發無上正真道意，王與夫人應時即得不起法忍。」佛告阿難：「是時王者，即我身是。時夫人者，今俱夷是。時太子者今羅云是。」佛言：「阿難！我宿命時，布施如是，用一切人故不惜身命，至無數劫無有恨悔，無所榮冀，自致正覺。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there was a king named Sarvadatta, which means "the one who gives all" — whatever was asked of him, he never refused. His giving was such that his name and fame spread to the eight directions, and there was no one who had not heard of him. At that time, Mañjuśrī, wishing to test him, transformed himself into a young brahmin and came from a foreign country to the gate of the king's palace, saying to the gatekeeper: "I have come from far away and wish to see the great king." The gatekeeper reported this at once. The king was overjoyed, and went out to receive him himself, as a son greeting a father, bowing before him and inviting him to be seated. He asked: "Renunciant, where have you traveled from? Was your journey not exhausting?" The visitor said: "In another country I heard of the king's virtue, and so I have come to see you — and now I wish to beg something of you." The king said: "Very good! Whatever you wish to have, do not hesitate to ask. I am called 'the one who gives all' — what is it you seek?" The brahmin said: "I want nothing else — I wish to have the king himself as my slave, and the king's wife as my servant. If you can do this, then come with me at once." The king was overjoyed, and answered: "Very good! As for myself, this can certainly be granted — I am willing to belong to you and serve at your command. But as for my wife, she is the daughter of a great king — I must go and ask her." The king went in and said to his wife: "There is now a renunciant, young and dignified, come from a distant land, who wishes to beg for my own person to use as a slave — and now he wishes as well to ask for you to be his servant. What should be done?" His wife said: "What did the king answer him?" The king said: "I have already granted myself as his slave, but I have not yet granted him you." His wife then said: "Would the king abandon me, gaining his own release while giving no thought to freeing me as well?" She then went out with the king at once, and said to the visitor: "I am willing to give my own person to serve the renunciant." The brahmin then said again to the king: "Is this truly so? I wish to leave now." The king said to the visitor: "I have practiced giving since birth, and have never once regretted it — I will follow you, renunciant." The visitor said: "You must follow me entirely barefoot, without shoes, exactly as a slave should, and you must not conceal anything." The king and his wife both said, "As you command. We follow our master's instruction, and dare not disobey." The brahmin then led the two servants along the road. Mañjuśrī, by his transformative power, placed a substitute in the king's place and his wife's place, to govern the country's affairs as before, exactly as they had been. The king's wife was originally the daughter of a great king, unrivaled in beauty, her hands and feet soft and delicate, raised deep within the palace, never having known cold or hardship. She was also several months pregnant. Walking behind her master, her whole body ached, the soles of her feet blistered and torn, until she could go no further, and fell far behind, utterly exhausted. The brahmin turned and scolded her: "Now that you are a servant, you must behave as a servant should — you cannot keep the manner you once had." The wife knelt and said: "I would not dare! I am only resting a moment from exhaustion." He shouted: "Hurry up, follow close behind me!" They came to the market of that country, where he sold the two slaves separately, to two different masters, each several leagues apart. A wealthy elder bought the man as a slave, and set him to guard a burial ground, collecting the fees owed by those who came to bury their dead there, without allowing any disturbance. As for the woman, the mistress of the household she was sold to was extremely jealous, and made her work morning and night without rest. Some days later, the servant-woman became pregnant and gave birth to a son; the mistress, furious, said: "You are a servant here — how dare you have this child?" and ordered him killed at once. Following her mistress's order, she killed her own child and went to bury him. Coming to where her husband was, they were able to see each other, and she said: "I gave birth to a son, and today he has already died. I have brought no money — might I still be allowed to bury him here without payment?" Her husband answered: "My master is very strict — if he learns of this, my punishment would not be small. Take him away quickly and find another place — you cannot stay here." Though the king and his wife were able to see each other in this way, they spoke nothing of their suffering, and neither harbored any resentment. And in the space of just this moment of speaking, as if in a dream, the king and his wife suddenly found themselves back in their own country, seated in the main hall of their own palace exactly as before, and all their ministers and palace women were there exactly as they had been, and the son who had been born to them was alive once more. The king and his wife, astonished in their hearts, wondered how this had come to be. Then Mañjuśrī appeared in the sky, seated upon a lotus of the seven treasures, showing his true radiant form, and praised them: "Excellent! Your giving has been as sincere and complete as this." The king and his wife leapt up in joy and came forward to bow before him. Mañjuśrī then taught them the Dharma; the three-thousandfold world shook greatly, and every person throughout the entire country gave rise to the unsurpassed, true and right resolve, and the king and his wife themselves attained at once the forbearance of the unarising Dharma. The Buddha said to Ānanda: "The king of that time was I myself. The wife of that time is now Yaśodharā. The son of that time is now Rāhula." The Buddha said: "Ānanda, in that past life I gave in this way, for the sake of all people, never begrudging my own body or life, and through countless kalpas I never once felt regret, seeking no glory or reward for myself, until I arrived at true and complete awakening on my own." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Unlike most stories in this fascicle, here the tester is not Śakra but Mañjuśrī, the bodhisattva of wisdom — worth noting as one of the few named alternatives to Śakra's usual testing role across this collection. The story's most striking structural choice is how casually its own central cruelty (a pregnant queen sold into forced labor, her newborn son killed at her mistress's order) is narrated without any editorializing, before being revealed as an illusion undone in an instant — a device this fascicle uses more than once to test the reader's own reaction to suffering that turns out, retroactively, not to have been real at all.

14. Prince Sudāna, Who Gave Away Everything須大拏經〔須大拏太子本生〕

Chinese Source

須大拏經「昔者葉波國王號曰濕隨，其名薩闍，治國以正，黎庶無怨。王有太子，名須大拏，容儀光世，慈孝難齊，四等普護，言不傷人。王有一子寶之無量，太子事親同之於天。有知之來，『常願布施拯濟群生，令吾後世受福無窮。愚者不覩非常之變，謂之可保。有智之士照有五家，乃尚布施之士。十方諸佛、緣一覺、無所著尊，靡不歎施為世上寶。』太子遂隆普施，惠逮眾生。欲得衣食者應聲惠之，金銀眾珍、車馬田宅，無求不與。光馨遠被，四海咨嗟。父王有一白象，威猛武勢躃六十象，怨國來戰，象輙得勝。「諸王議曰：『太子賢聖無求不惠。遣梵志八人之太子所，令乞白象。若能得之，吾重謝子。』受命即行，著鹿皮衣履屣執瓶，𨱜杖遠涉，歷諸郡縣千有餘里，到葉波國，俱柱杖翹一脚向宮門立，謂衛士曰：『吾聞太子布施貧乏潤逮群生，故自遠涉乞吾所乏。』衛士即入，如事表聞。太子聞之欣然馳迎，猶子覩親，稽首接足慰勞之曰：『所由來乎苦體如何？欲所求索以一脚住乎？』對曰：『太子德光周聞八方，上達蒼天下至黃泉，巍巍如太山，靡不歎仰。卿為天人之子，吐言必信。審尚布施不違眾願者，今欲乞匃行蓮華上白象，象名羅闍惒大檀。』太子曰：『大善！唯上諸君金銀雜寶恣心所求，無以自難。』即勅侍者，疾被白象金銀鞍勒牽之來矣。左持象勒，右持金甕，澡梵志手，慈歡授象。梵志大喜，即呪願竟，俱升騎象含笑而去。「相國百揆靡不悵然，僉曰：『斯象猛力之雄，國恃以寧，敵仇交戰，輙為震奔。而今惠讎國，將何恃？』俱現陳曰：『夫白象者，勢力能躃六十象，斯國却敵之寶。而太子以惠重怨，中藏日虛，太子自恣布施不休，數年之間，臣等懼舉國妻子必為施惠之物矣。』王聞其言，慘然久而曰：『太子好喜佛道，以賙窮濟乏慈育群生，為行之元首。縱得禁止假使拘罰，斯為無道矣。』百揆僉曰：『切磋之教儀無失矣，拘罰為虐臣敢聞之？逐令出國置于田野，十年之間令慙自悔，臣等之願也。』王即遣使者就誥之曰：『象是國寶，惠怨胡為？不忍加罰，疾出國去。』使者奉命誥之如斯。太子對曰：『不敢違天命。願乞布施濟乏七日，出國無恨。』使者以聞，王曰：『疾去，不聽汝也。』使者反曰：『王命不從。』太子重曰：『不敢違天命。吾有私財，不敢侵國。』使者又聞，王即聽之。「太子欣然勅侍者：『國中黎庶有窮乏者，勸之疾來，從其所欲恣之無違。國土官爵、田宅財寶，幻夢之類，靡不磨滅。』兆民巨細奔詣宮門，太子以飲食衣被、七寶諸珍，恣民所欲布施訖竟，貧者皆富。妻名曼坻，諸王之女，顏華韑耀，一國無雙，自首至足皆以七寶瓔珞。謂其妻曰：『起聽吾言，大王徙吾著檀特山，十年為限。汝知之乎？』妻驚而起，視太子淚出，且云：『將有何罪乃見迸逐，捐國尊榮處深山乎？』答其妻曰：『以吾布施虛耗國內，名象戰寶以施怨家。王逮群臣恚逐我耳。』妻即稱願：『使國豐熟，王臣兆民富壽無極，惟當建志於彼山澤成道弘誓矣。』「太子曰：『惟彼山澤恐怖之處，虎狼害獸難為止矣。又有毒蟲魍魎斃鬼、雷電霹靂風雨雲霧，其甚可畏。寒暑過度，樹木難依，蒺䔧礫石非卿所堪。爾王者之子，生於榮樂、長於中宮，衣則細軟，飲食甘美，臥則帷帳，眾樂聒耳，願則恣心。今處山澤，臥則草蓐、食則果蓏，非人所忍，何以堪之乎？』妻曰：『細靡眾寶帷帳甘美，何益於己？而與太子生離居乎？大王出時以幡為幟，火以煙為幟，婦人以夫為幟，吾恃太子猶孩恃親，太子在國布施四遠，吾輙同願。今當歷嶮而猶留守榮，豈仁道哉？儻有來乞不覩所天，心之感絕，必死無疑。』太子曰：『遠國之人來乞妻子，吾無逆心。爾為情戀，儻違惠道都絕洪潤，壞吾重任也。』妻曰：『太子布施覩世希有，當卒弘誓慎無倦矣。百千萬世，無人如卿逮佛重任，吾不敢違也。』太子曰：『善！』即將妻子詣母辭別，稽首于地愍然辭曰：『願捐重思，保寧玉體，國事鞅掌願數慈諫，無以自由枉彼天民。當忍不可忍，含忍為寶。』母聞訣辭顧謂侍曰：『吾身如石、心猶剛鐵，今有一子而見迸逐，吾何心哉？未有子時結願求嗣，懷妊之日如樹含華，日須其成，天不奪願令吾有子，今育成就而當生離乎？』夫人嬪妾，嫉者快喜不復相敬。「大子妻兒稽首拜退，宮內巨細靡不哽噎，出與百揆吏民哀訣，俱出城去，靡不竊云：『大子國之聖靈、眾寶之尊，二親何心而逐之乎？』大子坐城外謝諸送者，遣之還居。兆民拜伏，僉然舉哀，或有躃踊呼天，音響振國。與妻進道，自知去本國遠，坐一樹下。有梵志自遠來乞，解身寶服、妻子珠璣，盡以惠之，令妻子昇車執轡而去。始欲就道，又逢梵志來從乞馬，以馬惠之；自於轅中挽車進道。又逢梵志來匃其車，即下妻子，以車惠之。太子車馬衣裘身寶雜物，都盡無餘，令妻嬰女，己自抱男。處國之時，施彼名象眾寶車馬，至見毀逐，未曾恚悔，和心相隨，歡喜入山。三七二十一日乃到檀特山中。「太子覩山樹木茂盛，流泉美水甘果備焉，鳧鴈鴛鴦遊戲其間，百鳥嚶嚶相和悲鳴。太子覩之謂其妻曰：『爾觀斯山，樹木參天尠有折傷，群鳥悲鳴，每處有泉，眾果甚多以為飲食；唯道是務，無以違誓。』山中道士皆守節好學，有一道士名阿周陀，久處山間有玄妙之德。即與妻子詣之稽首，却叉手立，向道士曰：『吾將妻子來斯學道，願垂洪慈，誨成吾志也。』道士誨之，太子則焉，柴草為屋，結髮葌服，食果飲泉。男名耶利，衣小草服，從父出入；女名罽拏延，著鹿皮衣，從母出入。處山一宿，天為增泉其味重甘，生藥樹木名果茂盛。「後有鳩留縣老貧梵志，其妻年豐，顏華端正，提瓶行汲。道逢年少遮要，調曰：『爾居貧乎無以自全，貪彼老財庶以歸居。彼翁學道內否不通，教化之紀，希成一人。專愚𢤱悷，爾將所貪乎？顏狀醜黑，鼻正匾𠥶，身體繚戾，面皺脣䫂丁可反，言語蹇吃，兩目又青，狀類若鬼，舉身無好，孰不僫憎？爾為室家，將無愧厭乎？』婦聞調婿，流淚而云：『吾覩彼翁鬢鬚正白，猶霜著樹。朝夕希心，欲其早喪。未即從願，無如之何？』歸向其婿，如事具云，曰：『子有奴使，妾不行汲。若其如今，吾去子矣。』婿曰：『吾貧，緣獲給使乎？』妻曰：『吾聞布施上士名須大拏，洪慈濟眾虛耗其國。王逮群臣，徙著山中。其有兩兒，乞則惠卿。』「妻數有言，愛婦難違，即用其言，到葉波國，詣宮門曰：『太子安之乎？』衛士上聞。王聞斯言，心結內塞，涕泣交流，有頃而曰：『太子見逐，惟為斯輩；而今復來乎？』請現勞倈，問其所以。對曰：『太子潤馨，遐邇詠歌，故遠歸命，庶自穌息。』王曰：『太子眾寶布施都盡，今處深山，衣食不充，何以惠子？』對曰：『德徽巍巍，遠自竭慕，貴覩光顏，沒齒無恨也。』王使人示其徑路。「道逢獵士，曰：『子經歷諸山，寧覩太子不？』獵士素知太子迸逐所由，勃然罵曰：『吾斬爾首，問太子為乎？』梵志恧然而懼曰：『吾必為子所殺矣！當權而詭之耳。』曰：『王逮群臣令呼太子還國為王。』答曰：『大善！』喜示其處。「遙見小屋，太子亦覩其來。兩兒覩之中心怛懼，兄弟俱曰：『吾父尚施，而斯子來，財盡無副，必以吾兄弟惠與之。』擕手俱逃。母故掘蔭其埳容人，二兒入中以柴覆上，自相誡曰：『父呼無應也。』「太子仰問，請其前坐，果漿置前。食果飲畢，慰勞之曰：『歷遠疲倦矣！』對曰：『吾自彼來，舉身惱痛，又大飢渴。太子光馨，八方歎懿，巍巍遠照。有如太山，天神地祇，孰不甚善，今故遠歸窮，庶延微命。』太子惻然曰：『財盡無惜矣。』梵志曰：『可以二兒給養吾老矣。』答曰：『子遠來求兒，吾無違心。』太子呼焉，兄弟懼矣。又相謂曰：『吾父呼求，必以惠鬼也。』違命無應。太子隱其在埳，發柴覩之，兒出抱父戰慄涕泣，呼號且言：『彼是鬼也！非梵志矣！吾數覩梵志，顏類未有若茲，無以吾等為鬼作食。吾母採果來歸何遲？今日定死，為鬼所噉。母歸索吾，當如牛母索其犢子，狂走哀慟。父必悔矣。』太子曰：『自生布施未甞微悔，吾以許焉，爾無違矣。』梵志曰：『子以普慈相惠，兒母歸者即敗子洪潤違吾本願，不如早去。』太子曰：『卿願求兒，故自遠來，終不敢違，便可速邁。』「太子右手沃澡，左手持兒，授彼梵志。梵志曰：『吾老氣微，兒捨遁邁之其母所，吾緣獲之乎？太子弘惠，縛以相付。』太子持兒令梵志縛，自手執繩端，兩兒躃身宛轉父前，哀號呼母曰：『天神地祇山樹諸神一哀，告吾母意云：「兩兒以惠人，宜急捨彼菓，可一相見。」』哀感二儀，山神愴然，為作大響有若雷震。母時採果，心為忪忪，仰看蒼天不覩雲雨，右目瞤左腋痒，兩乳湩流出相屬，母惟之曰：『斯怪甚大！吾用菓為？急歸視兒，將有他乎？』委菓旋歸，惶惶如狂。「帝釋念曰：『菩薩志隆，欲成其弘誓之重任，妻到壞其高志也。』化為師子，當道而蹲。婦曰：『卿是獸中之王，吾亦人中王子，俱止斯山，吾有兩兒皆尚微細，朝來未食須望我耳。』師子避之，婦得進路。迴復於前化作白狼，婦辭如前，狼又避焉。又化為虎，適梵志遠，乃遂退矣。「婦還，覩太子獨坐，慘然怖曰：『吾兒如之而今獨坐，兒常望覩吾以菓歸，奔走趣吾，躃地復起，跳踉喜笑，曰：「母歸矣，飢兒飽矣。」今不覩之，將以惠人乎？吾坐兒立各在左右，覩身有塵，競共拂拭。今兒不來，又不覩處，卿以惠誰？可早相語。禱祀乾坤，情實難云，乃致良嗣。今兒戲具，泥象泥牛、泥馬泥猪，雜巧諸物縱橫于地，覩之心感，吾且發狂，將為虎狼鬼𩲐盜賊吞乎？疾釋斯結，吾必死矣！』太子久而乃言：『有一梵志來索兩兒云：「年盡命微欲以自濟。」吾以惠之。』婦聞斯言，感踊躃地，宛轉哀慟流淚且云：『審如所夢，一夜之中夢覩老𥥧貧窶梵志，割吾兩乳執之疾馳；正為今也。』哀慟呼天，動一山間。云：『吾子如之，當如行求乎？』「太子覩妻哀慟尤甚，而謂之曰：『吾本盟爾隆孝奉遵，吾志大道，尚濟眾生，無求不惠，言誓甚明。而今哀慟，以亂我心？』妻曰：『太子求道，厥勞何甚？夫士家尊在于妻子之間，靡不自由，豈況人尊乎？願曰：「所索必獲，如一切智。」』「帝釋諸天僉然議曰：『太子弘道普施無蓋，試之以妻觀心如何。』釋化為梵志來之其前，曰：『吾聞子懷以乾坤之仁，普濟群生布施無逆，故來歸情，子妻賢貞德馨遠聞，故來乞匃，儻肯相惠乎？』答曰：『大善！』以右手持水澡梵志手，左手提妻適欲授之。諸天稱壽莫不歎善，天地卒然大動，人鬼靡不驚焉。梵志曰：『止！吾不取也。』答曰：『斯婦豈有惡耶？婦人之惡斯都無有，婦人之禮斯為備首矣。然其父王唯有斯女，盡禮事婿不避塗炭，衣食趣可不求細甘，勤力精健顏華踰輩。卿取吾喜，除患最善。』梵志曰：『婦之賢快誠如子言，敬諾受之。吾以寄子，無以惠人。』又曰：『吾是天帝釋，非世庸人也，故來試子。子尚佛慧影，範難雙矣，今欲何願，恣求必從。』太子曰：『願獲大富，常好布施無貪踰今；令吾父王及國臣民思得相見。』天帝釋曰：『善！』應時不現。「梵志喜獲其志，行不覺疲，連牽兩兒欲得望使。兒王者之孫，榮樂自由，去其二親為繩所縛，結處皆傷，哀號呼母，鞭而走之。梵志晝寢，二兒迸逃，自沈池中，荷蒻覆上，水蟲編身。寤行尋求，又得兒矣；捶杖縱橫，血流丹地。天神愍念，解縛愈傷，為生甘果，令地柔軟。兄弟摘果，更相授噉，曰：『斯果之甘猶苑中果，斯地柔軟如王邊縕綖矣。』兄弟相扶仰天呼母，涕泣流身。梵志所行，其地岑巖，礫石刺棘，身及足蹠，其瘡毒痛，若覩樹果，或苦且辛，梵志皮骨相連，兩兒肌膚光澤，顏色復故。「歸到其家，喜笑且云：『吾為爾得奴婢二人，自從所使。』妻覩兒曰：『奴婢不爾，斯兒端正，手足悅澤不任作勞，孚行衒賣，更買所使。』又為妻使，欲之異國。天惑其路，乃之本土。兆民識焉，僉曰：『斯太子兒也！大王孫矣！』哽噎詣門上聞。王呼梵志將兒入宮，宮人巨細靡不噓唏！王呼欲抱，兩兒不就。王曰：『何以？』兒曰：『昔為王孫，今為奴婢。奴婢之賤，緣坐王膝乎？』問梵志曰：『緣得斯兒？』對之如事。曰：『賣兒幾錢？』梵志未答，男孫勦曰：『男直銀錢一千，特牛百頭；女直金錢二千，牸牛二百頭。』王曰：『男長而賤，女幼而貴。其有緣乎？』對曰：『太子既聖且仁，潤齊二儀天下喜附，猶孩依親，斯獲天下之明圖，而見遠逐捐處山澤，虎狼毒蟲與之為隣，食菓衣草，雷雨震人，夫財幣草芥之類耳，坐見迸棄，故知男賤也。黎庶之女，苟以華色處在深宮，臥即縕綖，蓋以寶帳，衣天下之名服，食天下之貢獻，故女貴也。』王曰：『年八孩童，有高士之論，豈況其父乎？』宮人巨細聞其諷諫莫不舉哀。梵志曰：『直銀錢一千，特牛、牸牛各百頭。惠爾者善，不者自已。』王曰：『諾。』即雇如數，梵志退矣。「王抱兩孫坐之于膝，王曰：『屬不就抱，今來何疾乎？』對曰：『屬是奴婢，今為王孫。』曰：『汝父處山，何食自供？』兩兒俱曰：『薇菜樹果以自給耳。日與禽獸百鳥相娛，亦無愁心。』王遣使者迎焉。使者就道，山中樹木俯仰屈伸，似有跪起之禮，百鳥悲鳴哀音感情。太子曰：『斯者何瑞？』妻臥地曰：『父意解釋，使者來迎，神祇助喜，故興此瑞。』妻自亡兒臥地，使者到乃起拜王命矣。使者曰：『王逮皇后捐食銜泣，身命日衰，思覩太子。』太子左右顧望，戀慕山中樹木流泉，收淚昇車。「自使者發，舉國歡喜，治道掃除豫施帳幔，燒香散華伎樂幢蓋，舉國趍蹌，稱壽無量。大子入城頓首謝過，退勞起居。王復以國藏珍寶都付太子，勸令布施。隣國困民歸化首尾，猶眾川之歸海。宿怨都然，拜表稱臣，貢獻相銜。賊寇尚仁，偷賊競施，干戈戢藏，囹圄毀矣。群生永康，十方稱善。積德不休，遂獲如來、無所著、正真道、最正覺、道法御、天人師，獨步三界為眾聖王矣。」佛告諸比丘：「吾受諸佛重任誓濟群生，雖嬰極苦，今為無蓋尊矣。太子後終，生兜術天；自天來下，由白淨王生，今吾身是也。父王者，阿難是。妻者，俱夷是。子男羅云是，女者羅漢朱遲母是。天帝釋者，彌勒是。射獵者，優陀耶是。阿周陀者，大迦葉是。賣兒梵志者，調達是。妻者，今調達妻旃遮是。吾宿命來勤苦無數，終不恐懼而違弘誓矣。以布施法為弟子說之，菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」六度集經卷第二六度集經卷第三吳康居國沙門康僧會譯布施度無極經此有十一章

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the king of the country of Yeba was named Śibi, called Sarvadatta; he governed his country with rectitude, and his people held no grievance against him. The king had a crown prince named Sudāna, whose bearing shone before the world, whose kindness and filial devotion could not be matched, who protected all beings equally through the four immeasurable virtues, and whose words never wounded anyone. The king treasured this one son beyond measure, and the prince served his parents as though they were heaven itself. From those with understanding he learned: "Always vow to give and relieve the multitude, that you may receive boundless blessing in ages to come. The foolish do not see the truth of impermanence, and think what they hold can be kept forever. The wise see that wealth belongs, in the end, to five different owners, and so they honor the practice of giving. The buddhas of the ten directions, the pratyekabuddhas, and the honored ones attached to nothing — none of them fail to praise giving as the treasure of this world." The prince then made his giving ever more abundant, his kindness reaching every living being. Whoever wished for clothing or food, he gave it at once upon hearing the request; gold, silver, every kind of treasure, carriages, horses, land and houses — nothing was ever refused to those who asked. His radiant fame spread far and wide, and all within the four seas sighed in wonder at it. The king had a white elephant, mighty and fierce, able to overcome sixty ordinary elephants in battle; whenever enemy states came to attack, this elephant always secured the victory. The kings of rival states deliberated together: "The prince is wise and sage-like, and refuses nothing anyone asks of him. Let us send eight brahmins to the prince, to beg for the white elephant. If they can obtain it, we will reward them greatly." Having received this charge, the brahmins set out at once, wearing deerskin robes and sandals, carrying water-vessels and staffs, traveling far, passing through district after district, over a thousand leagues, until they reached the country of Yeba. There, leaning on their staffs, standing on one foot before the palace gate, they said to the guards: "We have heard that the prince gives to the poor and needy, his grace reaching every living being, and so we have come from far away to beg for what we lack." The guards went in and reported this exactly as they had heard it. The prince, hearing this, rushed out joyfully to welcome them, as a son seeing his own parents, bowing his head and touching their feet, and comforted them, saying: "From where have you come, and how have your bodies endured such hardship? Why do you stand on only one foot to make your request?" They answered: "The prince's virtue and radiance are known throughout the eight directions, reaching up to the blue heavens and down to the yellow springs below, towering like Mount Tai itself — there is no one who does not sigh in admiration. As the son of gods and men, whatever you say is certain to be true. Since you truly honor the practice of giving and never disappoint anyone's hopes, we now wish to beg for the lotus-treading white elephant, named Rājavarddhana." The prince said: "Very good! Take also whatever gold, silver, and varied treasures you wish — do not hesitate on my account." He commanded his attendants at once to swiftly saddle the white elephant in gold and silver harness and bring it forth. With his left hand he held the elephant's harness, and with his right hand a golden vessel, and he washed the brahmins' hands himself, and with kindness and joy presented them the elephant. The brahmins, overjoyed, spoke their blessing, and, laughing together, mounted the elephant and departed. The chief ministers and all the officials of the state were dismayed, and said together: "This elephant's fierce strength was equal to sixty others — the country relied on it for its safety; whenever we clashed with an enemy, it always put them to flight. And now it has been given to a rival country — what shall we rely on now?" They came before the king together and said: "The white elephant, whose power could overcome sixty elephants, was this country's treasure against our enemies. And yet the prince gives it to our sworn enemies, our treasury growing emptier by the day, the prince giving without restraint and without rest — within a matter of years, we, your servants, fear that the whole country's wives and children will become objects given away." The king, hearing this, was grieved, and after a long silence said: "The prince delights in the Buddha's way, relieving the poor and destitute and nurturing all beings with compassion — this is the very foundation of proper conduct. To forcibly restrain him, to punish and imprison him, would itself be a betrayal of the Way." The officials all said together: "To instruct and correct him through proper discipline would not be wrong, but to imprison and punish him would be cruelty — how could we, your servants, propose such a thing? Let him be banished from the country and sent to live in the wilderness, that in ten years' time he may come to regret this himself and reform — this is our wish, we who serve you." The king then sent a messenger to proclaim this to the prince: "The elephant is a treasure of the state — why give it to our enemy? Though I cannot bear to punish you, you must leave the country at once." The messenger delivered this proclamation exactly as commanded. The prince answered: "I dare not disobey the command of heaven. I beg only for seven days, to complete my giving to the poor and needy, and I will leave the country without regret." The messenger reported this, and the king said: "He must leave at once — this is not permitted." The messenger returned and said, "The king's command will not be altered." The prince said again: "I dare not disobey the command of heaven. But I have my own private wealth, which I have not touched from the state's own treasury." The messenger reported this as well, and the king permitted it. The prince, overjoyed, commanded his attendants: "Whoever among the people of this country is poor and in need, urge them to come quickly, and grant them whatever they desire, without refusal. The land, office, houses, fields, and treasures of this world are all like illusions in a dream — none of them lasts forever." The people, great and small, hurried to the palace gate, and the prince gave freely of food, clothing, and the seven treasures of every kind, granting the people's every wish, until the poor had all become wealthy. His wife, named Madrī, was the daughter of a king, her beauty radiant and unmatched in the whole country, adorned from head to foot with jewels of the seven treasures. He said to his wife: "Rise and hear what I must say. The great king has banished me to Mount Daṇḍaka, for a term of ten years. Do you understand this?" His wife, startled, rose and looked at the prince, tears falling, and said: "What crime have you committed, that you should be driven out, giving up your kingdom's honor and glory to dwell deep in the mountains?" He answered his wife: "Because my giving has depleted the country's wealth, and I gave away the famous war-elephant, our greatest treasure, to our enemy — the king and his ministers, in their anger, have driven me out." His wife at once made her own vow: "May the country prosper, may the king, his ministers, and all the countless people find wealth and long life without limit — and let me set my own resolve on that mountain wilderness, to help fulfill your great vow there." The prince said: "That mountain wilderness is a place of terror — tigers and wolves and harmful beasts are hard to escape there. There are also poisonous insects, goblins, and vengeful spirits, thunder and lightning, wind and rain and fog, all fearsome beyond measure. The cold and heat pass to extremes, the trees offer little shelter, and the thorny brambles and stones are more than you could bear. You are the daughter of a king, born to glory and raised deep within the palace — your clothes have always been soft and fine, your food sweet and delicious, your bed curtained and canopied, surrounded always by pleasant sounds, every wish granted freely. Now, dwelling in the mountain wilds, sleeping on straw mats, eating wild fruit — this is not something a person could bear. How could you endure it?" His wife said: "What good are fine silks, curtained beds, and sweet delicacies to me, if I must live apart from the prince? When the great king travels, a banner marks his way; fire is marked by its smoke; a wife is marked by her husband — I rely on the prince as a child relies on its parent. When the prince gave to the four directions within the kingdom, I shared equally in that same vow. Now, when you must cross such dangers, should I remain behind alone, clinging to glory? How could that be the way of a loving wife? And if someone should come to beg while you have no wife present to help you give, your heart would be utterly bereft — I would surely die of it, without a doubt." The prince said: "People from distant lands come to beg for wives and children, and I have no heart to refuse them. If, out of attachment and longing for me, you should betray the way of giving and cut off entirely this vast flow of generosity, you would only ruin the great task I have taken on." His wife said: "The prince's giving is a rare thing in this world — you must complete this vast vow, and never grow weary of it. Through a hundred thousand myriad ages, no one has taken on so great a task as you, one that leads even to buddhahood — I would not dare stand in your way." The prince said, "Very well," and took his wife and children to bid farewell to his mother, bowing his head to the ground and speaking to her with deep sorrow: "I beg you to set aside your heavy grief and preserve your own precious health. The affairs of the kingdom are many and pressing — please offer your wise counsel often, and do not let your own freedom cause wrong to the heaven-given people of this land. One must endure what cannot be endured — patience itself is the true treasure." His mother, hearing this farewell, turned to her attendants and said: "My body feels like stone, my heart like hardened iron — I have only this one son, and now he is being driven away. What is left in my heart? Before I had a son, I prayed and vowed for an heir; through the months I carried him, I was like a tree holding its blossom, waiting day by day for it to open. Heaven did not deny my prayer and gave me this son — and now that he has grown to maturity, must I be parted from him forever?" Among the king's wives and concubines, those who were jealous of the prince's mother rejoiced at this, showing her no more respect than before. The prince, his wife, and children bowed and took their leave; within the palace, high and low, there was no one who did not choke back tears. Going out, he bid farewell to the officials and the common people in grief, and together they left the city. There was no one who did not whisper: "This prince is the country's own sacred treasure, the most honored of all its riches — what were his parents thinking, to drive him away?" The prince sat outside the city and thanked those who had come to see him off, sending them back to their homes. The countless people bowed down and wept together in mourning; some threw themselves to the ground and cried out to heaven, their voices shaking the whole country. Traveling on with his wife, knowing they had gone far from their own country, he sat beneath a tree. A brahmin came from far away to beg, and the prince took off his own jeweled robes and his wife's and children's pearls and gave them all away, and had his wife and children mount the carriage while he himself took up the reins. As they set out again, they met another brahmin who came asking for the horse, and he gave the horse away as well, and drew the carriage himself between the shafts. They met yet another brahmin who came asking for the carriage, and he at once brought down his wife and children and gave the carriage away too. The prince's carriage, horse, robes, and every treasure and possession were now entirely gone, and he had his wife carry their daughter while he himself carried their son. While he had still been in the kingdom, he had given away the famous elephant and every treasure and carriage, and even now, being driven out and stripped of everything, he never once felt anger or regret, and went into the mountains with a peaceful heart and gladness. After three weeks, twenty-one days, they at last reached Mount Daṇḍaka. The prince saw that the mountain's trees grew thick and lush, its flowing springs sweet and pure, its fruit abundant, with ducks, geese, and mandarin ducks playing among them, and a hundred kinds of birds calling mournfully to one another. Seeing this, the prince said to his wife: "Look at this mountain — its trees reach to the sky, hardly a branch broken; the birds cry out in sorrow, and springs are found everywhere, with abundant fruit to sustain us. Let us devote ourselves only to the Way, and never betray our vow." Among the mountain's ascetics, all held fast to their discipline and loved learning; there was one ascetic named Acyuta, who had long dwelt in the mountains and possessed a profound and subtle virtue. The prince, with his wife and children, went to him, bowed his head, and, standing with hands joined, said: "I have brought my wife and children here to study the Way — I beg you to extend your vast compassion, and teach and complete my resolve." The ascetic instructed him, and the prince followed his teaching, building a hut of brush and grass, binding up his hair, wearing garments of woven grass, eating fruit and drinking from the springs. His son was named Jāli, and, wearing small clothes of woven grass, followed his father in and out; his daughter was named Kṛṣṇājinā, and, wearing a robe of deerskin, followed her mother in and out. After they had dwelt in the mountains for a single night, heaven itself, in response, made the springs increase and grow sweeter still, and medicinal plants and trees bearing famous fruits grew lush around them. Later, there was an old and poor brahmin of Kuru county, whose wife was young, her face fair and lovely, who went daily to draw water carrying her jar. On the road she met some young men who blocked her way and mocked her, saying: "You live in poverty with nothing to sustain you — you're only greedy for that old man's wealth, hoping to make your home secure. But that old fellow, for all his learning of the Way, has achieved nothing at all — hardly one in a thousand ever does. He is foolish, stubborn, and unyielding — what is it you're actually after? His face is ugly and dark, his nose flat and crooked, his body twisted and bent, his face wrinkled, his lips drooping, his speech stuttering, his eyes a sickly color — he looks like some kind of ghost, nothing at all pleasing about his whole appearance — who could fail to despise him? As his wife, do you feel no shame or disgust?" Hearing her husband mocked in this way, the wife wept and said: "I see that old man's beard and hair are entirely white now, like frost settled on a tree. Morning and night I hope for his death to come soon — but my wish has not yet been granted, and there's nothing I can do about it." She went home to her husband and told him exactly what had happened, saying: "If you had a servant, I would not have to fetch water myself. If things go on as they are, I will leave you." The husband said: "I am poor — how could I ever obtain a servant?" His wife said: "I have heard that the greatest giver in the world is the prince named Sudāna, whose vast compassion in relieving the multitude has emptied his own country's treasury, so that the king and his ministers banished him to the mountains. He has two children — ask, and he will give them to you." His wife spoke of this again and again, and, unable to refuse his beloved wife, he followed her wish and went to the country of Yeba, and stood at the palace gate, asking: "Is the prince at peace?" The guard reported this within. The king, hearing this news, felt his heart clench, tears flowing freely, and after a moment said: "The prince was driven out because of people just like this — and now this fellow comes again?" He had the man brought in, treated him kindly, and asked his purpose. He answered: "The prince's fragrant virtue is sung of near and far, and so I have come from afar to entrust myself to him, hoping to find some relief." The king said: "The prince has already given away every treasure he had, and now dwells deep in the mountains, without even enough clothing and food for himself — what could he possibly give you?" He answered: "His virtue rises higher and higher — I have come from far away simply out of longing for it; even to see his face once more would leave me without regret, even at the moment of death." The king had someone show him the way. On the road he met a hunter, who asked him: "You have passed through these mountains — have you seen the prince, by any chance?" The hunter had long known the reason for the prince's banishment, and, flaring with anger, cursed him: "I should cut off your head — asking about the prince, indeed!" The brahmin, terrified, thought to himself: "I am certainly going to be killed by this man!" and, as a stratagem, lied: "The king and his ministers have sent for the prince to return to the country and become king." The hunter said, "Very good!" and happily showed him the way. From a distance he saw a small hut, and the prince also saw him coming. His two children, seeing him, were struck with dread, and the brother and sister said to each other: "Our father still loves to give, and now this man comes — our wealth is entirely gone, with nothing left to replace it, so he is sure to give us away to him." Hand in hand, they fled together. Their mother had long ago dug a hiding place that could hold a person; the two children went in and covered themselves with brushwood, warning each other: "If father calls, do not answer." The prince looked up and asked the visitor to come sit before him, and set out fruit and drink. After he had eaten and drunk, the prince comforted him, saying: "You must be exhausted from such a long journey!" He answered: "I have come from far away, and my whole body aches, and I am terribly hungry and thirsty besides. The prince's fragrant fame is praised in all eight directions, its glory reaching far and wide, towering like Mount Tai — among gods and spirits of heaven and earth, who does not admire it? And so I have come from afar in my poverty, hoping to extend what little life remains to me." The prince, moved with compassion, said: "My wealth is gone, but I begrudge nothing." The brahmin said: "Then let your two children serve and support this old man." He answered: "You have come from far away to ask for my children — I have no heart to refuse you." The prince called out to them, and the brother and sister, frightened, said to each other again: "Father is calling for us — he is surely going to give us to that demon," and, disobeying, did not answer. The prince, discovering where they hid, uncovered the brushwood, and the children came out and clung to their father, trembling and weeping, crying out: "That is a demon! Not a brahmin at all! We have seen many brahmins, and none of them ever looked like that — do not let us become food for a demon! Why is mother taking so long to bring back fruit? Today we will surely die, eaten by a demon. When mother comes back looking for us, she will be like a mother cow searching for her calf, running wild in grief. Father will surely regret this." The prince said: "Since I was born I have practiced giving, and never once felt the slightest regret — I have already promised him, and you must not disobey." The brahmin said: "If you give them to me out of universal compassion, and their mother returns before I leave, she will surely destroy this act of grace and go against my own true wish — it would be better for me to leave quickly." The prince said: "You have come from so far away seeking my children — I will never disobey you. Let us proceed at once." The prince washed his right hand, and, holding the children with his left, handed them to the brahmin. The brahmin said: "I am old and my strength is failing — if the children run off to find their mother, how could I ever catch them? In your great generosity, please bind them and give them to me that way." The prince held the children while the brahmin bound them, and with his own hand took hold of the end of the rope. The two children threw themselves down and writhed before their father, wailing and crying out for their mother: "Gods and spirits of heaven and earth, spirits of the mountain trees, take pity on us — tell our mother's heart: 'Your two children have been given away — put down that fruit and hurry, that you may see them once more.'" Their grief moved heaven and earth, and the mountain spirits themselves, moved to sorrow, made a great sound like a peal of thunder. Their mother, gathering fruit at that moment, felt her heart suddenly uneasy; she looked up at the blue sky and saw no sign of rain or cloud, yet her right eye twitched and her left armpit itched, and both her breasts began flowing with milk one after another. She thought to herself: "This is a very strange omen! What use is this fruit to me now?" She abandoned the fruit and turned back at once, frantic as though gone mad. Śakra thought to himself: "The bodhisattva's resolve rises ever higher, and he means to complete the weighty burden of his great vow — but his wife, returning now, might ruin his lofty resolve." He transformed himself into a lion and crouched in the road. The wife said: "You are the king of beasts, and I too am the wife of a prince among men — we both dwell on this same mountain. I have two children, both still very young, who have had nothing to eat since morning and are waiting for me now." The lion moved aside, and the wife was able to pass. Turning back onto the path ahead, Śakra transformed himself into a white wolf, and the wife spoke to it just as before, and the wolf too moved aside. He then transformed into a tiger, and, since the brahmin was now far ahead, at last withdrew. The wife returned and saw the prince sitting alone, and, filled with dread, said: "Where are my children? They have not come now, when they always wait for me and rush to meet me the moment I return, falling and rising again in their eagerness, leaping and laughing with joy, saying, 'Mother is back! Your hungry children can eat now!' Now I do not see them — have you given them away? I would sit and the children would stand at my sides, and if I had dust on me, they would rush to brush it off together. Now the children do not come, and I do not see where they have gone — to whom have you given them? Tell me at once. I prayed to heaven and earth, longing with all my heart, and at last was granted such fine children. Now their playthings — a clay elephant, a clay ox, a clay horse, a clay pig, all sorts of little toys — lie scattered on the ground, and seeing them my heart is overwhelmed, and I feel I am going mad. Have they been devoured by tigers, wolves, demons, or thieves?" She said: "Release me from this anguish at once, or I will surely die!" The prince, after a long silence, said: "A brahmin came, asking for the two children, saying: 'My years are nearly spent, my life is fragile, and I wish to sustain myself.' I gave them to him." Hearing this, his wife, overcome, threw herself to the ground, writhing in grief, tears flowing, and said: "This is exactly as in my dream! Last night I dreamed I saw an old, destitute, poverty-stricken brahmin cut off both my breasts and carry them away, running swiftly — this was surely a sign of what has now happened." She wept and cried out to heaven, and her grief shook the whole mountain. She said: "My children are gone — should I go now and search for them?" Seeing his wife's grief growing ever more extreme, the prince said to her: "I originally vowed with you to uphold and follow devoted conduct together — my resolve is set on the great Way, to relieve all living beings, refusing no request made of me; this vow has been utterly clear from the start. Why now, in your grief, do you seek to disturb my resolve?" His wife said: "The prince seeks the Way — how could such hardship not exist along that path? A man's household depends on his wife and children being at his own command in all things — how much more so for one honored above all men? My prayer is only this: 'Whatever is sought, may it be granted, as though by the wisdom that knows all things.'" Śakra and the gods of heaven deliberated together: "The prince has expanded the Way through universal giving, holding nothing back — let us test him through his wife, and see what is truly in his heart." Śakra transformed himself into a brahmin and came before him, saying: "I have heard that you hold the benevolence of heaven and earth within you, relieving all living beings universally, giving without ever refusing — and so I have come to entrust my own feelings to you. Your wife is virtuous and chaste, and her fragrant fame is heard far and wide — and so I have come to beg for her. Might you be willing to give her to me?" He answered: "Very good!" With his right hand he poured water to wash the brahmin's hands, and with his left hand took hold of his wife, about to hand her over. The gods of heaven praised this with wishes for his long life, and there was no one who did not sigh in admiration; heaven and earth shook violently all at once, and there was no person or spirit who was not astonished. The brahmin said: "Stop! I will not take her." He answered: "Does this woman have some fault? A woman's faults, she has none at all — every rule of a wife's proper conduct, she fulfills to the utmost. Her father the king had only this one daughter, and she has served her husband with complete propriety, never shrinking from the hardest circumstances, content with the barest clothing and food, never seeking fine delicacies, diligent and strong, her beauty surpassing all others. If you take her, it would be my own joy — nothing could better remove my burden." The brahmin said: "Her virtue and swiftness of mind are truly as you say — with respect, I accept her. But I entrust her back to you now — I do not wish to give her to anyone else." He then said: "I am Śakra, lord of the gods, not an ordinary man of this world — I came to test you. Your reflection of the Buddha's wisdom is truly beyond compare — what is it you wish for now? Ask freely, and it shall be granted." The prince said: "I wish to obtain great wealth, and to continue delighting in giving, without greed, more than ever before; and I wish that my father the king and the people of my country might long to see me again." Śakra said, "Very good!" and vanished at once. The brahmin, overjoyed at having gotten what he wished, walked on without feeling his own fatigue, leading the two children along, hoping to make use of them as servants. The children, grandchildren of a king, accustomed to freedom and comfort, now separated from their parents and bound with rope, their skin chafed and wounded wherever the ropes touched, wept and cried out for their mother as he whipped them onward. When the brahmin lay down to sleep, the two children slipped away and hid themselves in a pond, covering themselves with reeds, until aquatic insects covered their bodies. Waking, he searched and found them again, and beat them with his staff without restraint, until blood flowed and reddened the ground. The heavenly spirits, moved to pity, loosened their bonds and healed their wounds, made sweet fruit grow for them, and softened the ground beneath them. The brother and sister picked the fruit and fed each other, saying: "This fruit is as sweet as the fruit in our own garden; this ground is as soft as the silk cushions beside our father's throne." Leaning on each other, they looked up to heaven and cried out for their mother, tears covering their bodies. Wherever the brahmin walked, the ground was rocky and jagged, thorns and brambles cutting his body and the soles of his feet, the wounds burning with pain; whenever he saw fruit, some of it was bitter and harsh — the brahmin's skin hung loose on his bones, while the children's skin remained smooth and glossy, their complexions restored as before. Arriving home, he said with delight: "I have obtained two servants for you, to use as you wish." His wife, seeing the children, said: "These are no servants — these children are refined and handsome, their hands and feet delicate and fine, unfit for labor. Take them and sell them, and buy others to serve us instead." So, at his wife's bidding, he set out for another country, but the gods confused his path, and he found himself back in his original homeland instead. The people recognized the children at once, saying together: "These are the prince's own children! The great king's own grandchildren!" and, choking with emotion, hurried to the palace gate to report this. The king had the brahmin bring the children into the palace; within the palace, high and low alike, there was no one who did not weep. The king reached out to embrace them, but the two children would not come to him. The king asked, "Why not?" The children said: "We were once the king's own grandchildren — now we are servants. How could servants, so lowly, dare sit upon the king's own lap?" He asked the brahmin: "How did you come to have these children?" He told him exactly what had happened. The king asked: "For how much did you buy the children?" Before the brahmin could answer, the grandson spoke up quickly: "My price is a thousand silver coins and a hundred bulls; my sister's price is two thousand gold coins and two hundred cows." The king said: "The boy, though older, is priced lower, and the girl, though younger, is priced higher — is there a reason for this?" He answered: "The prince is both a sage and a man of complete benevolence, his grace equal to that of heaven and earth, beloved by all the world, as a child relies on its own parent — he attained the world's most brilliant renown, and yet was driven out and abandoned in the mountain wilderness, dwelling among tigers, wolves, and poisonous creatures, eating fruit and wearing grass, exposed to thunder and storm — wealth and possessions are things of no more worth than straw to him, and he cast them aside without a thought, and this is why we know the boy is of lesser price. As for the ordinary daughters of common people, valued only for their beauty and kept deep within palace walls, sleeping on silk cushions beneath jeweled canopies, wearing the finest garments of the realm and eating the tribute of the whole world — this is why the girl is of greater worth." The king said: "An eight-year-old child speaks with such lofty understanding — how much more so his father!" Within the palace, high and low alike, hearing this indirect reproach, all began to mourn together. The brahmin said: "A thousand silver coins, and a hundred bulls and a hundred cows — if you wish to give me this, very well; if not, that is fine too." The king said, "Agreed," and paid him the exact sum, and the brahmin departed. The king held his two grandchildren on his lap and said: "A moment ago you would not come to me — why do you come so readily now?" They answered: "A moment ago we were servants; now we are the king's own grandchildren." He asked: "While your father dwelt in the mountains, what did he live on?" The two children answered together: "Only wild greens and fruit from the trees sustained us. Day after day we took our joy among the birds and beasts, and our hearts held no sorrow." The king sent a messenger to welcome the prince home. As the messenger traveled, the trees of the mountain bent and swayed as though bowing in greeting, and a hundred birds cried out in mournful, moving song. The prince said, "What omen is this?" His wife, lying on the ground, said: "Father's heart has softened, and a messenger comes to welcome us home — the spirits themselves rejoice, and this is why these signs appear." Ever since the loss of her children, the wife had lain upon the ground; when the messenger arrived, she rose at last and bowed to receive the king's command. The messenger said: "The king and queen have given up eating, weeping constantly, their bodies growing weaker day by day, longing to see the prince again." The prince looked about him, filled with longing for the trees and flowing springs of the mountain, then wiped away his tears and mounted the carriage. From the moment the messenger had set out, the whole country rejoiced; the roads were cleared and swept, curtains and canopies set up in advance, incense burned and flowers scattered, music played, banners and parasols raised, the whole country hurrying joyfully, wishing him boundless long life. The prince entered the city, bowed his head in apology for his past fault, and withdrew to attend to his parents. The king then entrusted the entire treasury of the kingdom to the prince, and encouraged him to give as he wished. The poor and troubled people of neighboring countries came to submit to him one after another, like countless rivers flowing into the sea. Old grudges were entirely dissolved; rival states bowed and declared themselves his vassals, offering tribute one after another. Even bandits and thieves came to honor his benevolence, vying with each other to give; weapons of war were put away and stored, and prisons were torn down. All living beings found lasting peace, and the ten directions praised him as good. His accumulated virtue never ceasing, he at last attained the state of Tathāgata, the one attached to nothing, of true and right awakening, the most perfectly enlightened, the guide of the Dharma and teacher of gods and men, walking alone through the three realms as the king of all sages. The Buddha said to the monks: "I took upon myself the weighty task entrusted by all the buddhas, vowing to relieve every living being, and though I endured the most extreme suffering, I have now become the one honored above all, without equal. The prince, at the end of that life, was reborn in Tuṣita Heaven; and from that heaven he came down and was born to King Śuddhodana — that is myself, here before you now. The father-king was Ānanda. The wife was Yaśodharā. The son was Rāhula; the daughter is now the arhat Vajrā's mother. Śakra, lord of the gods, was Maitreya. The hunter was Udāyin. The ascetic Acyuta was Mahākāśyapa. The brahmin who bought the children was Devadatta. His wife is now Devadatta's own wife, Cañcā. Through countless past lives I have endured such hardship, and never once, out of fear, did I go back on my great vow." He taught this teaching of giving for the sake of his disciples. This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

By far the longest and most famous story in this fascicle — a close cousin of the story known elsewhere in the Buddhist world as Prince Vessantara's, and likely drawing on the same underlying narrative tradition, though this Chinese telling diverges from the Pāli version in many particulars of detail and sequence. Its structure escalates in careful stages: first wealth, then the elephant, then the family's own possessions, and only at the very end the children and the wife herself — each gift harder to give than the last, and each met by the prince with the same unwavering resolve. The brahmin who buys the children is revealed at the story's close to be Devadatta, which recasts his cruelty along the way not as incidental hardship but as part of a longer, recurring antagonism the fascicle keeps returning to. Worth noting as a genuine feature of this telling rather than an oversight: the children's suffering under their new owner is described in more direct physical detail than almost anything else in this fascicle so far, and the text does not soften it before restoring them unharmed.
