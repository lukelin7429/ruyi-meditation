Fascicle 1

Ten stories on the perfection of giving《六度集經》卷第一

The opening ten past-life stories of the Liudu Ji Jing's first chapter, the Perfection of Giving (布施度無極章) — a bodhisattva who cuts his own flesh to save a dove, another who gives his head to a starving tigress, and a merchant prince who begins bailing out the ocean by hand rather than surrender what he has earned.

Chinese
《六度集經》, "Sūtra of the Collected Six Perfections" — translated by Kang Senghui (康僧會), a monk of Sogdian descent, at Jianye (modern Nanjing) sometime between 251 and 280 CE, during the Kingdom of Wu.
Structure
Eight fascicles, ninety-one past-life stories in all, organized under the six perfections (六度): giving, precepts, patience, diligence, meditation, and wisdom. This fascicle carries the first ten stories of the giving chapter, which continues across Fascicles 2 and 3.
Genre
Jātaka — stories of the Buddha-to-be's countless past lives as a bodhisattva, here transmitted as a distinct Chinese collection rather than a translation of the Pāli Jātakatthavaṇṇanā, sharing some episodes with the Pāli tradition and diverging in many others.
Closing formula
Nearly every story ends with the Buddha identifying who each character in the story now is — a convention this translation preserves in full each time it appears.
Difficulty
★★☆☆☆ to read — these are narrative stories, not doctrinal argument. The difficulty, where it exists, is tonal: some stories give without any expectation of reward, and some involve suffering described plainly, without softening.

About This Collection

The Liudu Ji Jing collects ninety-one stories of the Buddha's past lives as a bodhisattva, organized under the six perfections (六度, pāramitā) that structure the entire Mahāyāna path: giving, moral discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom. It survives as one of the earliest and most substantial bodies of narrative Buddhist literature translated into Chinese, the work of Kang Senghui, a monk of Sogdian ancestry born and raised in Jiaozhi (in what is now northern Vietnam), who traveled north to the Kingdom of Wu and became one of the first Buddhist teachers active south of the Yangtze.

This collection is a genuine sibling to the Pāli Jātaka tradition rather than a translation of it — a separate Chinese-language stream of past-life narrative that shares its basic premise and a handful of specific episodes with the better-known Pāli collection (already partially translated elsewhere on this site, in the 82 stories for which a public-domain verse translation currently exists), while diverging from it in most of its actual content, structure, and telling. Where the Pāli Jātaka collection is organized by length and preserved today mostly in verse, with the connecting prose narrative surviving only in a later, separately copyrighted commentarial layer, the Liudu Ji Jing survives complete, as continuous prose narrative, organized instead by which perfection each story illustrates.

How to read this fascicle

Each of the ten stories below is self-contained — read them in any order, or straight through as they appear here. Nearly every story follows the same closing convention: after the tale is told, the Buddha identifies who each character has become in the present — himself, often a disciple such as Ānanda or Śāriputra, or, notably, his cousin and rival Devadatta, who recurs throughout this fascicle as the villain of story after story. This convention is not unique to this collection — it appears throughout Jātaka literature generally — but it is worth watching for as a recurring structural device rather than treating each instance as a surprise.

The Translation

1. The Bodhisattva Who Would Not Be Deceived菩薩本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，其心通真，覩世無常，榮命難保，盡財布施。天帝釋覩菩薩慈育群生布施濟眾，功勳巍巍，德重十方，懼奪己位，因化為地獄，現于其前曰：『布施濟眾，命終魂靈入于太山地獄，燒煮萬毒。為施受害也，爾惠為乎？』菩薩報曰：『豈有施德而入太山地獄者乎？』釋曰：『爾其不信，可問辜者。』菩薩問曰：『爾以何緣處地獄乎？』罪人曰：『吾昔處世，空家濟窮，拯拔眾厄，今受重辜處太山獄。』菩薩問曰：『仁惠獲殃，受施者如之乎？』釋曰：『受惠者，命終昇天。』菩薩報曰：『吾之拯濟，唯為眾生；假如子云，誠吾願矣。慈惠受罪，吾必為之。危己濟眾，菩薩上志也。』釋曰：『爾何志願，尚斯高行？』答曰：『吾欲求佛，擢濟眾生，令得泥洹不復生死。』釋聞聖趣，因却叩頭曰：『實無布施慈濟眾生，遠福受禍入太山獄者也。子德動乾坤，懼奪吾位，故示地獄以惑子志耳。愚欺聖人，原其重尤。』既悔過畢，稽首而退。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva's mind was open to what was true. Seeing that the world is impermanent and that a glorious life is hard to keep, he gave away everything he had. Śakra, lord of the gods, watching the bodhisattva nurture all beings with compassion and relieve the multitude through giving, saw his merit rising higher and higher, his virtue growing weighty in all ten directions — and Śakra, afraid the bodhisattva would take his own throne, conjured up a vision of hell and made it appear before him, saying: "You give and relieve the multitude — but when your life ends, your spirit will enter the great hell of Mount Tai and be boiled in ten thousand torments. Is this the reward your generosity earns you? Will you still give?" The bodhisattva answered: "How could there be one who gives with virtue and yet falls into the great hell of Mount Tai?" Śakra said: "If you don't believe me, ask the guilty man there." The bodhisattva asked the man: "What brought you to this hell?" The sinner said: "In my former life I emptied my house to relieve the poor, and pulled the multitude out of danger — and now I bear this heavy guilt in the hell of Mount Tai." The bodhisattva asked: "Does kindness truly bring disaster, then? And what of the one who receives the gift?" Śakra said: "The one who receives the gift, when his life ends, rises to heaven." The bodhisattva replied: "What I have relieved, I have relieved only for the sake of living beings. If it is truly as you say, then this is exactly what I wish for. To receive suffering for the sake of kindness and giving — I will do it gladly. To endanger oneself in order to relieve the multitude is the highest aspiration of a bodhisattva." Śakra said: "What is it, then, that you aspire to, that you hold to such a lofty resolve?" He answered: "I seek to become a Buddha, to lift up and relieve living beings, so that they may reach nirvāṇa and never again be born and die." Hearing this noble aim, Śakra bowed his head and said: "There truly is no one who gives with compassion and relieves living beings who is repaid with disaster and falls into the hell of Mount Tai. Your virtue has moved heaven and earth, and fearing you would take my throne, I showed you this hell to confuse your resolve. I deceived a sage out of foolishness — please forgive my grave fault." Having confessed and repented, he bowed his head and withdrew. This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This first story establishes the collection's basic pattern in miniature: a temptation staged by Śakra, lord of the gods, who repeatedly appears throughout this fascicle as an anxious rival rather than a settled authority, testing whether the bodhisattva's giving is genuine before withdrawing in shame once it proves so. The logical trap Śakra sets — kindness rewarded with hell — is answered not by argument but by the bodhisattva's willingness to accept even that outcome, which is itself the proof the story is built to deliver.

2. King Sarvada and the Dove薩波達王本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為大國王，號薩波達，布施眾生恣其所索，愍濟厄難常有悲愴。天帝釋覩王慈惠德被十方，天神鬼龍僉然而曰：『天帝尊位初無常人，戒具行高慈惠福隆，命盡神遷則為天帝。』懼奪己位，欲往試之，以照真偽。帝命邊王曰：『今彼人王，慈潤霶霈福德巍巍，恐于志求奪吾帝位。爾化為鴿，疾之王所，佯恐怖，求哀彼王。彼王仁惠，必受爾歸；吾當尋後從王索爾。王終不還，必當市肉，以當其處。吾詭不止，王意清真，許終不違，會自割身肉以當其重也。若其秤肉隨而自重，肉盡身痛其必悔矣。意有悔者，所志不成。』「釋即化為鷹，邊王化為鴿。鴿疾飛趣于王足下，恐怖而云：『大王哀我！吾命窮矣！』王曰：『莫恐莫恐！吾今活汝。』鷹尋後至，向王說曰：『吾鴿爾來，鴿是吾食，願王相還。』王曰：『鴿來以命相歸，已受其歸，吾言守信，終始無違。爾苟得肉，吾自足爾，令重百倍。』鷹曰：『吾唯欲鴿，不用餘肉，希王當相惠。而奪吾食乎？』王曰：『已受彼歸，信重天地，何心違之乎？當以何物令汝置鴿歡喜去矣？』鷹曰：『若王慈惠必濟眾生者，割王肌肉令與鴿等，吾欣而受之。』王曰：『大善！』即自割髀肉秤之令與鴿重等；鴿踰自重，自割如斯，身肉都盡未與重等，身瘡之痛其為無量。王以慈忍心願鴿活，又命近臣曰：『爾疾殺我，秤髓令與鴿重等。吾奉諸佛，受正真之重戒，濟眾生之危厄，雖有眾邪之惱，猶若微風，焉能動太山乎？』「鷹照王懷守道不移，慈惠難齊，各復本身。帝釋、邊王稽首于地曰：『大王！欲何志尚，惱苦若茲？』人王曰：『吾不志天帝釋及飛行皇帝之位，吾覩眾生沒于盲冥，不覩三尊、不聞佛教，恣心于凶禍之行，投身于無擇之獄。覩斯愚惑，為之惻愴。誓願求佛，拔濟眾生之困厄，令得泥洹。』天帝驚曰：『愚謂大王欲奪吾位，故相擾耳。將何勅誨？』王曰：『使吾身瘡愈復如舊，令吾志尚布施濟眾行高踰今。』天帝即使天醫神藥，傳身瘡愈、色力踰前，身瘡斯須豁然都愈。釋却稽首，遶王三匝歡喜而去。自是之後，布施踰前。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was a great king named Sarvada. He gave to all beings whatever they asked for, and was always moved to sorrowful compassion for those in danger and distress. Śakra, seeing the king's kindness and giving cover the ten directions, and hearing the gods, spirits, and dragons all say together, "The throne of Śakra was never held by one person forever — whoever's precepts are complete and whose conduct is lofty, whose kindness and giving are abundant, when his life ends he will become the next Śakra" — grew afraid the king would take his own place, and wished to go and test him, to see whether his virtue was true or false. Śakra ordered a border-god: "That human king's kindness pours down like rain, his virtue rises higher and higher — I fear he seeks to take my throne. Change yourself into a dove, fly swiftly to the king, feign terror, and beg him for mercy. The king is compassionate, and will surely take you in. Then I will come after you and ask the king to give you back. If the king will not give you back, he will surely have to buy meat to replace you. I will not stop deceiving him until his resolve, however pure and true, however unwavering his promise, finally breaks — until he cuts his own flesh to replace you. If the flesh keeps growing heavier the more you weigh it, and his flesh runs out while he is still in pain, he is sure to regret it. And if regret arises in him, then what he has resolved to be will not be accomplished." So Śakra changed into a hawk, and the border-god changed into a dove. The dove flew swiftly to the king's feet and cried out in terror: "Great King, have pity on me! My life is at its end!" The king said: "Do not fear, do not fear — I will save you now." The hawk arrived close behind, and said to the king: "I have come for my dove — the dove is my food. I beg the king to return it to me." The king said: "The dove came to me and entrusted its life to me. I have already taken it in, and my word must be kept, without wavering from beginning to end. If you truly want meat, I will give you enough to satisfy you a hundred times over." The hawk said: "I want only the dove, no other meat. I beg the king to grant my wish — will you snatch away my food?" The king said: "I have already taken it in. My trust is as weighty as heaven and earth — how could I go against it in my heart? Tell me, what must I give you so that you will let the dove go and leave content?" The hawk said: "If the king's kindness and giving truly means to relieve all beings, then cut flesh from the king's own body, equal in weight to the dove, and I will gladly accept it." The king said: "Very well." He at once cut flesh from his own thigh and weighed it against the dove — but the dove kept outweighing it. He cut and cut like this, and though all the flesh of his body was gone, it still had not equaled the dove's weight, and the pain of his wounds was beyond measure. Still, with a mind of compassion and patience, wishing only that the dove live, the king ordered a minister close by: "Kill me quickly, and weigh my very marrow against the dove. I have taken the vow before all buddhas, the weighty vow of true and right conduct, to relieve living beings from their danger and distress. Though the troubles of the wicked may arise, they are like a faint breeze — how could they ever move Mount Tai?" Seeing that the king's heart held to the Way without wavering, that his kindness could not be matched, the hawk and the dove both returned to their true forms. Śakra and the border-god bowed their heads to the ground and said: "Great King, what is it you aspire to, that you would suffer so for this?" The human king said: "I do not aspire to the throne of Śakra or to the rank of any flying emperor. I see that living beings are sunk in blindness and darkness, that they do not see the Three Jewels or hear the Buddha's teaching, that they give their hearts freely to disaster and calamity and throw themselves into the hell with no reprieve. Seeing their folly and delusion, I am moved to sorrow for them. I vow to seek buddhahood, to lift living beings out of their danger and distress, and bring them to nirvāṇa." Śakra, astonished, said: "I foolishly thought the Great King wished to take my throne, and so I troubled you. What instruction do you have for me now?" The king said: "Let my body's wounds be healed and made whole as before, and let my resolve toward giving and relieving the multitude rise higher than it does even now." Śakra then sent heavenly physicians with divine medicine, and the wounds of the king's body were healed, his complexion and strength surpassing what they had been before — in an instant his wounds were entirely healed. Śakra bowed his head again, circled the king three times in joy, and departed. From that time on, the king's giving surpassed what it had been before. This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The story of the king who cuts his own flesh to ransom a dove from a hawk is one of the most widely retold images in Buddhist narrative literature, appearing in numerous versions across the wider Jātaka tradition. Worth noting as a genuine textual variant rather than an inconsistency in this translation: the dove's weight never equals the flesh offered, even after the king's entire body is spent — a detail some later retellings smooth into a cleaner equivalence, but which this version keeps as its own sharper point about a gift that cannot be measured out in exact exchange.

3. The Poor Man Who Became a Fish貧人本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩貧窶尤困，與諸商人俱之他國，其眾皆有信佛之志，布施窮乏濟度眾生。等人僉曰：『眾皆慈惠，爾將何施？』答曰：『夫身假借之類靡不棄捐，吾覩海魚，巨細相吞，心為愴愴。吾當以身代其小者，令得須臾之命也。』即自投海。海大魚飽，小者得活。魂靈化為鱣魚之王，身有里數。「海邊有國，其國枯旱，黎庶飢饉更相吞噉。魚為流淚曰：『眾生擾擾，其苦痛哉？吾身有里數之肉，可供黎民旬月之乏。』即自盪身上于國渚，舉國噉之以存生命，輦肉數月而魚猶生。天神下曰：『爾為忍苦，其可堪哉？何不放壽，可離斯痛也？』魚曰：『吾自絕命神逝身腐，民後飢饉將復相噉，吾不忍覩。』心為其感矣，天曰：『菩薩懷慈難齊。』天為傷心曰：『爾必得佛，度吾眾生矣。』「有人以斧斫取其首，魚時死矣。魂靈即感為王太子。生有上聖之明，四恩弘慈，潤齊二儀。愍民困窮，言之哽咽。然國尚旱，靖心齊肅，退食絕獻，頓首悔過曰：『民之不善，咎在我身，願喪吾命惠民雨澤。』日日哀慟，猶至孝之子遭聖父之喪矣。精誠達遠，即有各佛五百人來之其國界。「王聞心喜，悅若無身，奉迎稽首，請歸正殿。皇后、太子靡不肅虔，最味法服供足所乏，五體投地稽首叩頭，涕泣而曰：『吾心穢行濁，不合三尊四恩之教，苦酷人民，罪當伐己。流被下劣，枯旱累載，黎庶飢饉怨痛傷情。願除民灾，以禍罪我。』諸各佛曰：『爾為仁君，慈惻仁惠，德齊帝釋，諸佛普知。今授汝福，慎無慼也。便疾勅民，皆令種穀。』王即如命，男女就業，家無不修，稻化為蓏，農臣以聞。王曰：『須熟。』蓏實覆國，皆含稻穬，中容數斛，其味苾芬，香聞一國；舉國欣懌，歎詠王德。四境讎國皆稱臣妾，黎民雲集，國界日長。率土持戒，歸命三尊，王及臣民壽終之後，皆生天上。」佛言：「時貧人者，吾身是也。累劫仁惠拯濟眾生，功不徒朽，今果得佛，號天中天，為三界雄。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was extremely poor and destitute, and traveled with a group of merchants to another country. All of them held faith in the Buddha, and gave to the poor and destitute to relieve living beings. The others all said to him: "Everyone here is giving with compassion — what will you give?" He answered: "There is nothing borrowed in this body that is not, in the end, cast aside. I have seen the fish of the sea, the large swallowing the small, and my heart aches for them. I will offer my own body in place of the smaller ones, so that they may have even a brief moment of life longer." He at once threw himself into the sea. The great sea-fish were satisfied, and the smaller ones lived. His spirit was reborn as the king of the sturgeon-fish, his body many leagues long. By the sea there was a country suffering drought, its people starving and reduced to devouring one another. The fish wept and said: "Living beings are in such turmoil — how bitter their suffering is! My body has leagues of flesh; it can supply the people for ten days, a month." He at once beached himself upon the country's shore, and the whole country ate of him to preserve their lives, carrying away his flesh for months while the fish still lived. A heavenly spirit came down and said: "You endure such suffering — can you truly bear it? Why not let go of your life, and be free of this pain?" The fish said: "If I end my own life now, my spirit will depart and my body will rot, and the people will starve again later and once more devour one another — I cannot bear to see that." The spirit was moved by this, and said: "The bodhisattva's compassion cannot be matched." Grieved at heart, the spirit said: "You will surely become a buddha, and deliver my people." Then a man came with an axe and cut off the fish's head, and the fish died. His spirit was then conceived as the crown prince of that kingdom's king. From birth he had the wisdom of a sage, and his compassion, born of the Four Infinite Virtues, spread broadly like heaven and earth. Grieving for the people's poverty and hardship, he could hardly speak for choking on his own tears. And still the country suffered drought. He stilled his mind and disciplined himself, withdrew from his meals and gave up his offerings, and bowed his head in repentance, saying: "The wrongdoing of the people is my own fault. I would give up my own life in exchange for the blessing of rain for my people." Day after day he mourned in this way, like a most filial son mourning the death of a sage father. His sincerity reached far, and five hundred pratyekabuddhas came at once to the borders of his country. Hearing of this, the king's heart rejoiced as though he had forgotten his own body; he went out to welcome them, bowing his head, and invited them to the main hall. The queen and the crown prince, none of them failing in reverence, offered the finest robes of the Dharma and all that was needed, prostrating themselves fully on the ground, bowing their heads, weeping and saying: "My mind is impure and my conduct is defiled; I have not lived up to the teaching of the Three Jewels and the Four Infinite Virtues, and have brought suffering upon my people through harshness — the blame should fall on me and me alone. I have brought this on the lowly and the humble; the drought has lasted for years, and the starving people's resentment and suffering wound my heart. I beg you to remove this calamity from my people, and let the blame and disaster fall on me instead." The pratyekabuddhas said: "You are a benevolent ruler, compassionate and sorrowful, kind and giving, your virtue equal to Śakra's own — all buddhas already know this. Now we grant you good fortune; be at ease and grieve no more. Quickly command your people, and have them all sow their grain." The king did as he was told, and men and women alike took up their work; every household tended its fields, and the rice-shoots transformed and bore fruit in extraordinary abundance, and the farming officials reported it to him. The king said, "Wait until it is ripe." The fruit-pods covered the whole country, each containing several bushels of grain, their fragrance filling the entire land; the whole country rejoiced, praising the king's virtue. The four bordering rival states all proclaimed themselves vassals, and the people gathered like clouds, and the borders of the kingdom grew wider day by day. Throughout the land, all upheld the precepts and took refuge in the Three Jewels, and when the king, his ministers, and his people came to the end of their lives, they were all reborn in the heavens. The Buddha said: "The poor man of that time was I myself. Through many kalpas of kindness and giving, relieving living beings, my merit was never wasted, and now I have attained buddhahood, called Heaven-Among-Heavens, hero of the three realms." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This story chains three lives into one arc without pause: the poor man's self-sacrifice becomes a great fish who feeds a starving country, and the fish's sacrificed body is reborn again as a prince whose own grief over famine draws five hundred pratyekabuddhas to end it. The chain is the point — each act of giving becomes the seed of the next, across bodies and lifetimes, rather than a single isolated event with a single reward.

4. The One Who Gave His Head to Feed a Starving Tigress菩薩本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩，時為逝心，恒處山澤，專精念道不犯諸惡。食果飲水不畜微餘，慈念眾生愚癡自衰，每覩危厄沒命濟之。行索果蓏，道逢乳虎。虎乳之後，疲困乏食，飢饉心荒，欲還食子。菩薩覩之愴然心悲，哀念眾生處世憂苦其為無量，母子相吞其痛難言，哽咽流淚。迴身四顧，索可以食虎，以濟子命。都無所見，內自惟曰：『夫虎肉食之類也。』深重思惟：『吾建志學道，但為眾生沒在重苦欲以濟之，令得去禍身命永安耳。吾後老死，身會棄捐，不如慈惠濟眾成德。』即自以首投虎口中。以頭與者，欲令疾死不覺其痛耳。虎母子俱全。諸佛歎德，上聖齊功，天龍善神有道志者，靡不愴然。進行或得溝港、頻來、不還、應真、緣一覺、有發無上正真道意者。以斯猛志，跨諸菩薩九劫之前，誓於五濁為天人師，度諸逆惡令偽順道。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva, at that time a wandering ascetic, dwelt always among the mountains and marshes, single-mindedly devoted to the Way and committing no wrong. He ate only fruit and drank only water, keeping nothing in store, and out of compassion for living beings, foolish and self-destroying as they are, whenever he saw danger and calamity he risked his life to save them. Once, going out to gather fruit, he came upon a nursing tigress. Having just given birth, she was exhausted and starving, and in her hunger and desperation her mind grew wild, and she meant to turn and devour her own cubs. Seeing this, the bodhisattva was struck with grief and sorrow, mourning the immeasurable suffering of living beings caught in this world, that mother and child should devour one another — a pain beyond words — and he choked back tears. He looked all around for something to feed the tiger, to save the cubs' lives, but found nothing at all. He thought to himself: "Tigers are meat-eating creatures by nature." And he reflected still more deeply: "I have set my resolve to study the Way only for the sake of living beings sunk in such heavy suffering, that I might relieve them and let them find lasting peace of body and life. Later, when I grow old and die, this body will be cast aside all the same — better to give it now in an act of kindness that relieves the multitude and perfects virtue." He at once threw his own head into the tiger's mouth, offering his head so that death would come quickly and he would not feel the pain. Both the mother tiger and her cubs were saved whole. All the buddhas praised his virtue, and the sages above shared in his merit; among the gods, dragons, and good spirits, none who held to the Way could keep from being moved. Through this, some among them attained the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, arhatship, the awakening of a pratyekabuddha, or gave rise to the unsurpassed and truly awakened mind. Through this fierce resolve he surpassed the other bodhisattvas by nine kalpas, vowing to become a teacher of gods and humans in this world of the five corruptions, delivering the most rebellious and evil, and turning falsehood into accord with the Way. This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Known across the wider Buddhist world as the story of the starving tigress, this is one of the most famous single images in all of Jātaka-adjacent literature, later carved and painted across Central and East Asia. This telling is notably spare compared to some later elaborations — there is no drawn-out internal debate, only a brief moment of reasoning before the act. The closing lines, crediting this single act with vaulting the bodhisattva nine kalpas ahead of his peers, mark it as one of this fascicle's most consequential stories.

5. King Kanti and the Gift of His Own Head乾夷王本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為大國王，國名乾夷，王號偏悅，內明外仁，顏和正平，民從其化，獄無繫囚，黎民貧乏恣所求索，慈惠和潤，恩如帝釋。「他國逝心服王仁施從眾所欲，群邪妬嫉以偽毀真，詣宮門曰：『吾聞明王濟黎民之困乏，猶天潤之普覆。』告衛士曰：『爾可聞乎？』近臣以聞，王即現矣。「逝心現曰：『明王仁澤被于四國，有識之類靡不咨嗟，敢執所願欲以上聞。』王曰：『大善！』逝心曰：『天王尚施，求則無違。時宜應用人首為事，願乞王首以副望矣。』王曰：『吾首何好而欲得之乎？吾有眾寶益以惠子。』逝心不受，又使工匠作七寶首，各數百枚，以與逝心。逝心曰：『唯欲王首耳。』「王未甞逆人，即自下殿以髮纏樹曰：『吾以首惠子。』逝心拔刀疾步而進，樹神覩之忿其無道，以手搏其頰，身即繚戾，面為反向，手垂刀隕。王得平康，臣民稱壽，悲喜交集，諸天歎德，可謂內施乎！四王擁護，眾毒消歇，境界無病，五穀豐熟，牢獄裂毀，君民欣欣。」佛告諸沙門：「時乾夷國王者，即吾身也。逝心者，調達是。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was a great king of a country called Kanti, and the king's name was Pien-yueh — inwardly wise and outwardly kind, his countenance gentle and upright. His people followed his transforming influence; his prisons held no captives; the poor and destitute could ask for whatever they wished; his kindness and giving flowed like water, his grace like Śakra's own. An ascetic from another country, envious of the king's benevolence and giving and of how the people followed his every wish, and full of wicked jealousy, wanted to use falsehood to destroy what was true. He came to the palace gate and said: "I have heard that the enlightened king relieves the people's poverty and hardship, as heaven's rain covers all things equally." He said to the guard: "Will you hear me?" The attendant reported this, and the king appeared at once. The ascetic, once before him, said: "Great King, your benevolent grace covers the four directions; there is no one with any understanding who does not sigh in wonder at it. I dare to state my wish, and beg to have it heard." The king said: "Very good!" The ascetic said: "Heavenly kings still give; when something is sought of them, they do not refuse. Now the occasion calls for a human head — I beg to ask for the king's own head, to fulfill my hope." The king said: "What good is my head, that you should want it? I have many treasures — let me add to them and give them to you." The ascetic would not accept them. The king then had craftsmen fashion heads of the seven treasures, several hundred of them, and gave them to the ascetic. The ascetic said: "I want only the king's own head." The king had never turned anyone away. He came down from the hall at once, tied his hair to a tree, and said: "I give my head to you." The ascetic drew his sword and stepped quickly forward. The spirit of the tree, seeing this, was outraged at such an outrage against the Way, and struck the ascetic's cheek with its hand; his body twisted and writhed, his face turned backward, his hand dropped, and the sword fell. The king recovered his health, and his ministers and people all wished him long life, joy and grief mingled together; the gods above praised his virtue — this may truly be called giving from within! The Four Heavenly Kings gave him their protection, every poison and pestilence was dispelled, the land was free of sickness, the five grains ripened in abundance, prisons broke open, and ruler and people alike rejoiced together. The Buddha said to the monks: "The King of Kanti at that time was I myself. The ascetic was Devadatta." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A shorter, starker variant of the same self-giving pattern as Story 2, without a merciful reversal engineered by the recipient — here it is the tree-spirit, not the king himself, who intervenes to prevent the ascetic from completing the killing blow. The story closes with the collection's first explicit identification of a story's villain with Devadatta, the Buddha's own cousin and antagonist within his lifetime — a naming convention that recurs throughout this fascicle and ties every past-life story back to people in the audience's own present.

6. The King Who Gave Away His Kingdom國王本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為大國王，理民以慈，恕己度彼，月月巡行貧乏，拔濟鰥寡、疾藥糜粥。每出巡狩，則命後車具載眾寶衣被醫藥，死者葬之。每覩貧民輙自咎責：『君貧德，民窮矣；君富德，民家足。今民貧，則吾貧矣。』王慈若斯，名被十方。「第二帝釋坐為其熱，釋心即懼曰：『彼德巍巍，必奪吾位。吾壞其志，行即畢乎！』便自變化為老梵志，從王乞銀錢一千，王即惠之。曰：『吾西𥥧，恐人盜之。願以寄王。』王曰：『吾國無盜。』重曰：『寄王。』王即受之。「天又化為梵志詣宮門，近臣以聞，王即現之。梵志歎曰：『大王功名流布八極，德行希有。今故遠來，欲有所乞。』王曰：『甚善！』曰：『吾宿薄祐生在凡庶，欣慕尊榮，欲乞斯國。』王曰：『大善！』即與妻子，輙輕乘而去。「天帝復化為梵志，從王乞車；以車馬惠之。與妻子進路，依山止宿。有五通道士與王為友，侻憶王德仰視其宿，覩之失國，靖心禪息，覩天帝釋，貪嫉奪國委頓疲疵。道士以神足忽然之王所，曰：『將欲何求，勞志若茲？』曰：『吾志所存，子具知之。』道士即化為一轅之車，以送王還，晨各離矣。「天化為梵志復乞其車，即復惠之。轉進未至彼國數十里，天復化為前梵志來索銀錢。王曰：『吾以國惠人，侻忘子錢。』梵志曰：『三日必還吾錢。』王即以妻子各質一家，得銀錢一千以還梵志。妻侍質家女，女浴脫身珠璣眾寶以懸著架，天化為鷹撮衣寶去。女云：『婢盜！』錄之繫獄。其兒與質家兒俱臥，天夜往殺質家兒矣。死家取兒付獄，母子俱繫，飢饉毀形，呼嗟無救，吟泣終日，罪成棄市。「王賃得銀錢一千，行贖妻、子，歷市覩之，即存念十方諸佛，自悔過曰：『吾宿命惡乃致茲乎！』靖心入禪，神通之明覩天所為。空中有聲曰：『何不急殺之乎？』王曰：『吾聞，帝釋普濟眾生，赤心惻愴，育過慈母，含血之類莫不蒙祐，爾為無惡緣獲帝位乎？』釋懷重毒惡熟罪成，生入太山；天人龍鬼莫不稱善。「地主之王，即釋妻、子之罪。二王相見，尋問其原，具陳所由，國無巨細靡不墮淚。地主之王分國而治；故國臣民尋王所在，率土奉迎。二國君民，一哀一喜。「時王者，吾身是。妻者，俱夷是。子者，羅云是。天帝者，調達是。山中梵志，舍利弗是。彼國王者，彌勒是。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was a great king who governed his people with compassion, measuring himself by what he would wish for another. Month after month he made his rounds among the poor and destitute, rescuing widowers and widows, providing medicine for the sick and gruel for the hungry. Whenever he went out on his rounds, he ordered the carriages behind him laden with treasures, clothing, and medicine, and buried those who had died. Whenever he saw a poor person, he would reproach himself, saying: "If the ruler is poor in virtue, the people are impoverished; if the ruler is rich in virtue, the people's households are full. Now that the people are poor, it is I who am poor." Such was the king's kindness, and his name spread to the ten directions. The second Śakra, disturbed by the heat of this, grew afraid in his heart, saying: "His virtue rises higher and higher — he will surely take my throne. I must break his resolve before it is too late!" So he transformed himself into an old brahmin, and came to the king to beg for a thousand pieces of silver. The king gave them to him at once. The brahmin said: "I am going west, and fear thieves may steal them — I wish to leave them in the king's keeping." The king said: "There are no thieves in my country." He insisted again: "Leave them with the king." The king accepted them. Heaven then transformed itself into another brahmin who came to the palace gate; the attendant reported this, and the king appeared. The brahmin said in praise: "Great King, your fame and achievement spread to the eight directions, your virtuous conduct is a rare thing in this world. I have come from far away because I have something to ask." The king said: "Very good." The brahmin said: "My past fortune was thin, and I was born a common man, but I long for honor and glory — I wish to ask for this kingdom." The king said: "Very well." He at once gave it to him, together with his wife and child, and departed in a light carriage. Heaven again transformed into a brahmin and asked the king for his carriage; he gave it to him as well, and continued on his way with his wife and child, stopping to rest by a mountain. There was an ascetic of the five supernatural powers who was a friend of the king; recalling the king's virtue, he looked up and observed the stars, and saw that the king had lost his kingdom. He stilled his mind in meditation and beheld Śakra, greedy and jealous, seizing the kingdom, leaving the king exhausted and worn out. By his spiritual powers the ascetic came at once to where the king was, and said: "What is it you seek, that you toil so?" The king said: "What is in my heart, you already know it all." The ascetic then transformed himself into a one-shaft cart and carried the king back partway; at dawn they parted. Heaven, transformed as a brahmin, again asked for the cart, and he gave it to him as well. Continuing on, before they had gone another few dozen leagues to reach that other country, Heaven again transformed into the earlier brahmin and came to ask for the silver coins. The king said: "I gave away my kingdom to another and had forgotten your coins." The brahmin said: "You must return my coins within three days." So the king pawned his wife and son, each to a separate household, and obtained a thousand silver coins to repay the brahmin. His wife, pawned to one household, bathed and took off her pearls and jewels and hung them on a rack; Heaven transformed into a hawk and snatched away her clothes and jewels. The woman was accused: "The servant has stolen them!" and she was seized and imprisoned. Their son, sleeping together with the household's own son, was visited in the night by Heaven, who killed the household's son. The dead child's family took the body to the authorities, and mother and son were both imprisoned, starved and disfigured, crying out with no one to help them, weeping and lamenting all day, and their crime was made complete and they were sentenced to execution in the marketplace. The king, having earned the thousand silver coins as a laborer, went to redeem his wife and son, and passing through the marketplace he saw them there. He at once called to mind the buddhas of the ten directions, and repented, saying to himself: "What evil in my past life has brought this upon me!" He stilled his mind and entered meditation, and with the clarity of spiritual insight he saw what Heaven had done. A voice came from the sky: "Why not kill them at once?" The king said: "I have heard that Śakra relieves all living beings broadly, his sincere heart moved to sorrow for them, nurturing them beyond even a mother's love, so that no creature with blood in its veins fails to receive his blessing — how is it that you, having gained your throne through no evil cause, now act like this?" Śakra, his heart heavy with poison and evil, his sin ripening to fullness, then fell alive into the hell of Mount Tai; among gods, humans, dragons, and spirits, none failed to call this good. The king of that land then released the king's wife and son from their crime. The two kings met, and asked each other the whole story from beginning to end, and stated the full circumstances; throughout the kingdom, high and low alike, none failed to weep. The king of that land divided his kingdom and shared its rule with him; and the ministers and people of his former kingdom, seeking out where their king now was, came from across the land to welcome him back. Between the two kingdoms, grief and joy mingled together. "The king of that time was I myself. The wife was Yaśodharā. The son was Rāhula. Śakra was Devadatta. The ascetic in the mountains was Śāriputra. The king of that other country was Maitreya." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The longest and most elaborate story in this fascicle so far, and the first to identify nearly every character by name at its close — the king's wife as Yaśodharā, his son as Rāhula, Devadatta as the jealous Śakra, Śāriputra as the mountain ascetic, and Maitreya, the future buddha, as the foreign king who ultimately restores what was lost. The story's real center of gravity is not the giving itself but the king's refusal to accept Devadatta's escalating series of deceptions with anger — his patience under repeated, engineered loss is treated as inseparable from his generosity.

7. The King Who Learned What "Private Wealth" Truly Means國王本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為大國王，理民以正，心無偏頗，然不遊觀。國相啟曰：『願一出遊。』王曰：『大善！』明日即出。人民悅豫，普得其所，覩國富姓居舍妙雅，瓦以金銀，服飾光道，曰：『吾國豐哉！』心甚欣豫。還宮憶之，曰：『斯諸理家，何益於國乎？』勅錄其財為軍儲矣。「有一理家，其私財有三千萬，以疏現王。王怒曰：『何敢面欺乎？』對曰：『少來治生，凡有私財宅中之寶，五家之分，非吾有也。』曰：『何謂私財？』對曰：『心念佛業，口宣佛教，身行佛事，捐五家分興佛宗廟，敬事賢眾供其衣食，慈養蜎飛蠕動蚑行之類；心所不安不以加之，斯之福德隨我所之，猶影隨形，所謂私財也。五家分者：一水，二火，三賊，四官，五為命盡。身逮家寶捐之於世，已當獨逝，殃福之門未知所之，覩世如幻，故不敢有之也。計五家分可有十億，斯為禍之窠藪，常恐危己，豈敢有之也。願士眾輦之，以除吾憂。』「王曰：『誠哉斯言也。』即遣之去。退入齋房，靖心精思，即醒寤曰：『身尚不保，豈況國土妻子眾諸，可得久長乎？』即撰錄佛經誦文釋義，心垢照除，進貞臣納忠諫大赦其國，還民寶，序群僚，議寬正。謂群臣曰：『夫不覩佛經妙義重戒者，其為聾盲矣！彼理家富，唯我貧矣！』即勅國界散出財寶，賑給貧困恣民所欲，立佛廟寺懸繒燒香，飯諸沙門，身自六齋。如斯三年，四境寧靖，盜賊都息，五穀熟成，民無飢寒。王後壽終，即上生第二天。」佛告諸沙門：「時王者，吾身是。理家者，秋鷺子是。勸王觀國者，阿難是。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was a great king who governed his people with rectitude, his heart free of bias, though he never went out to see the sights of his own land. His chief minister proposed to him: "I wish that you would go out on a single excursion." The king said: "Very good!" and the next day he went out. The people were joyful, everyone content in their place; he saw the country's wealthy households, their dwellings elegant, their roof tiles of gold and silver, their clothing and ornaments shining on the road, and said, "How rich my country is!" and his heart was greatly pleased. Returning to the palace, he thought it over and said: "What good do these wealthy households do for the country?" and ordered their wealth confiscated as military provisions. There was one wealthy household whose private fortune amounted to thirty million; he had it reported to the king. The king grew angry and said: "How dare you deceive me to my face?" The man answered: "From my youth I have made my living, and whatever private wealth, whatever treasures lie within my house, are divided five ways — they are not truly mine." The king asked: "What do you mean by 'private wealth'?" He answered: "Wealth I hold in my mind for the Buddha's work, proclaim with my mouth for the Buddha's teaching, and carry out with my body for the Buddha's deeds; wealth I give up from the 'five shares' to build temples for the Buddha's lineage, to honor and serve the community of the worthy, providing their clothing and food, and to nurture with compassion even the smallest flying, crawling, wriggling creatures — wealth that, wherever my heart finds no unease in giving it, I give freely — this good fortune follows me wherever I go, as a shadow follows a form. This is what I call my true private wealth. As for the 'five shares': the first is water, the second is fire, the third is thieves, the fourth is the government, and the fifth is the ending of one's own life. When this body reaches its end, the treasures of the household are cast aside in this world, and one must go on alone; whether misfortune or fortune awaits, one does not know — I see the world as an illusion, and so I dare not claim any of it as my own. As for the fortune reckoned at thirty million, this could well amount to a hundred million, and it is nothing but a den of disaster, and I always fear it will endanger me — how could I dare claim it as mine? I beg that your officers carry it all away, and relieve me of this worry." The king said: "Truly, these are wise words," and sent the man away. He withdrew to his private chambers, stilled his mind, and reflected deeply, and suddenly awoke to the truth, saying: "This very body cannot even be kept safe forever — how much less the kingdom, the land, my wife, my son, and all my possessions — how could any of it last long?" He at once began collecting the Buddha's scriptures, reciting the texts and expounding their meaning, and the defilements of his mind were cleared away. He advanced upright ministers and accepted their loyal counsel, granted a general amnesty throughout his kingdom, returned the people's wealth, put his officials in order, and deliberated on lenient and just governance. He said to his ministers: "Whoever has not seen the wondrous meaning and weighty precepts of the Buddha's scriptures is truly deaf and blind! That wealthy household is rich — only I am poor!" He at once ordered his kingdom's treasures distributed, relieving the poor and hardship-stricken according to their needs, establishing Buddhist temples hung with silk banners and burning incense, feeding the community of monks, and observing the six fast days himself. In this way, for three years, all four borders of his land were at peace, banditry entirely ceased, the five grains ripened, and the people knew neither hunger nor cold. When the king later came to the end of his life, he was reborn in the second heaven." The Buddha said to the monks: "The king of that time was I myself. The wealthy man was Śāriputra. The one who urged the king to go out and see his country was Ānanda." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This story turns on a deliberately provocative redefinition of "private wealth" — the wealthy householder's claim that only what is given away, not what is hoarded, is truly his own, since hoarded wealth is always already divided five ways among water, fire, thieves, government, and death. The king's own conversion, prompted entirely by this one encounter, models the kind of turn from acquisitive rule to open-handed governance that recurs as a pattern across several of this fascicle's stories about kings.

8. Xiantan the Householder, Thrown Down a Well仙歎理家本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為大理家，名曰仙歎，財富無數。覩佛明典，覺世無常，榮命難保，財非己有，唯有布施功德不朽。令告黎民：『若有貧乏，恣願取之。』如斯數月。時，政寬民富無財乏者。仙歎念曰：『惟當市藥，供護眾疾耳。』即市良藥濟眾生命，慈育普至，恩無不周。累年之惠，德香遠熏，四方病者馳來，首尾歎其弘潤，以德配天。「財賄都盡身行採寶，去家百餘里，於一水上逢數乘車載重病者，曰：『爾所之乎？』答曰：『之仙歎所，庶全餘命。』仙歎即還，從王貸金五百兩，市藥以療，病者悉瘳。自與商人入海採寶，所獲弘多。還國置舟步行，道乏無水，仙歎得一井水，呼等人汲之，却自取飲。商人覩其所得白珠，光耀絕眾，貪為尤惡，毀聖殘仁，共排仙歎投之于井。菩薩仁德感神動祇，天神接承令不毀傷。「商人還國，王曰：『仙歎何之？』對曰：『去國即別，不知所之。』曰：『爾乃殺之乎！』曰：『不也。』「仙歎於井覩空傍穴，尋之而進出彼家井，准七日行得其本國。王曰：『何緣空還乎？』對曰：『不遇。』王靖思曰：『其必有以乎！』召商人問：『爾誠首之即活，欺者死矣。』即皆首之，付獄定罪。仙歎涕泣，馳詣宮門叩頭請罪。王曰：『違政也。』又重請曰：『愚者倒見，未足明責。原其無知也。』王嘉仙歎之仁覆，原商人之凶罪，勅令還物。商人僉曰：『仙歎不奉佛者，豈有斯仁乎？』各擇名寶以還之矣。仙歎各受其半，商人叩頭曰：『蒙祐命全，願盡納焉。』於斯受之，以還王金，又大布施。王逮臣民，相率受戒，子孝臣忠，天神榮衛，國豐民康，四境服德，靡不稱善。」佛言：「時仙歎者，是我身也。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was a great householder named Xiantan, whose wealth was beyond counting. Seeing the Buddha's clear teaching, he awoke to the truth that the world is impermanent, that a glorious life is hard to keep, and that wealth is never truly one's own — only the merit of giving does not decay. He proclaimed to the people: "Whoever is poor and in need, take freely as you wish." For several months it went on like this. In time, the government grew lenient and the people prospered, and there was no one left in want. Xiantan then thought: "I should now buy medicine, to care for and protect those who are sick." So he bought fine medicine to save the lives of the multitude, and his kindness and nurturing reached everyone, his grace leaving no one untouched. Year after year his kindness continued, and his virtue's fragrance spread far and wide; the sick came hurrying from all four directions, and from first to last they praised his boundless bounty, his virtue matching that of heaven itself. When his wealth was entirely exhausted, he set out himself to gather treasures. A hundred leagues or more from home, upon a river he came upon several carts laden with the gravely ill. He asked: "Where are you going?" They answered: "To Xiantan's place, hoping to preserve what life remains to us." Xiantan turned back at once, borrowed five hundred taels of gold from the king, bought medicine and treated them, and all the sick recovered. He then went out to sea with merchants to gather treasures, and what they obtained was very great. Returning to the country, he left the ship and traveled on foot; on the road, lacking water, Xiantan found a well and called the others to draw from it, then went to drink himself. The merchants, seeing the brilliant white pearls he had obtained, their light outshining everyone else's, grew wickedly greedy, and betraying his sagely kindness, together they pushed Xiantan and threw him into the well. But the bodhisattva's compassionate virtue moved the spirits and gods, and a heavenly spirit caught him so that he was not harmed. The merchants returned to the country, and the king asked: "Where has Xiantan gone?" They answered: "We parted ways as soon as we left the country — we do not know where he went." The king said: "Did you kill him, then?" They said: "No." Xiantan, down in the well, saw an empty passage to one side, and followed it until he emerged at the bottom of that same household's well, and within seven days' journey he reached his own country. The king asked: "Why have you returned empty-handed?" He answered: "I found nothing." The king thought quietly to himself: "There must be more to this." He summoned the merchants and said: "If you confess the truth now, you will live; if you lie, you will die." They all confessed at once, and were handed over to the authorities and sentenced. Xiantan wept, and hurried to the palace gate, bowing his head and begging for their pardon. The king said: "This is against the law." Xiantan pleaded again: "Their view was simply mistaken and foolish — it does not deserve so severe a punishment. Please forgive their ignorance." The king, honoring Xiantan's boundless kindness, pardoned the merchants' grave crime, and ordered their property returned to them. The merchants all said together: "If Xiantan did not honor the Buddha, how could he have such compassion?" and each of them chose fine treasures to give back to him. Xiantan accepted only half of what each offered, and the merchants bowed their heads and said: "By your grace our lives were spared whole — we beg you to accept it all." So he accepted it, and with it repaid the king's gold, and made a great gift besides. The king, together with his ministers and people, all took the precepts together; sons were filial, ministers were loyal, the gods gave their glorious protection, the country prospered and the people were at peace, and all within its borders submitted to this virtue, none failing to praise it. The Buddha said: "Xiantan at that time was I myself." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Where earlier stories in this fascicle place their central act of giving at the story's climax, this one places it in the ordinary, sustained background — years of unremarkable medical charity — before the plot's actual crisis, a betrayal by the very merchants Xiantan traveled with, arrives almost as an afterthought. His plea for their pardon after they have tried to kill him for his pearls is treated by the story as continuous with, not separate from, the charitable habit that opens it.

9. Pushi, the Merchant Prince Who Drained the Sea普施商主本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩從四姓生，墮地即曰：『眾生萬禍，吾當濟焉。不覩佛儀，不聞明法，吾當開其耳目，除其盲聾，令之覩聞無上正真，眾聖之王、明範之原也。布施誘進靡不服從矣。』九親驚曰：『古世之來未聞幼孩而為斯云，將是天龍鬼神之靈乎！當卜之焉。』即答親曰：『吾為上聖之所化，懷普明之自然，非彼眾妖，慎無疑矣。』言畢即默。親曰：『兒有乾坤弘潤之志，將非凡夫乎！』名兒曰普施。「年有十歲，佛諸典籍、流俗眾術靡不貫綜，辭親濟眾布施貧乏。親曰：『吾有最福之上名也，爾可恣意布施眾貧矣。』對曰：『不足。』乞作沙門：『賜吾法服、應器、策杖，以斯濟眾，即吾生願也。』親憶兒始生之誓，無辭禦焉，即從其願聽為沙門。「周旋教化經一大國，國有豪姓，亦明眾書，覩普施儀容堂堂光華韑曄，厥性惔怕淨若天金，有上聖之表，將為世雄也。謂普施曰：『有欲相告，願足聖人，吾有陋女願給箕󰆠之使。』答曰：『大善！須吾還也。』即進路之海邊，附載度海，上岸入山，到無人處，遙覩銀城宮殿明好。時，有毒蛇遶城七匝體大百圍，見普施來仰然舉首。普施念曰：『斯含毒類必有害心，吾當興無蓋之慈以消彼毒也。夫兇即火也，慈即水矣，以水滅火，何嘗不滅！』即坐興慈定，『願令眾生早離八難，心去惡念逢佛見法，與沙門會，得聞無上正真明道，心開垢滅，如吾所見也。』興斯慈定，蛇毒即滅垂首而眠。「普施登其首入城，城中有天神，覩普施來，欣豫而曰：『久服聖德，今來翔茲，成吾本心也。願留一時九十日。』普施然許。天王即以正事委付近臣，身自供饌，朝夕肅懷，稟受諸佛非常、苦、空、非身之高行，濟眾之明法。時，日食畢，普施進路，天王以明月真珠一枚送之曰：『以珠自隨，明四十里。志願發云，眾寶滿足。若後得佛，願為弟子親侍聖側。』普施曰可。「即復前行，覩黃金城，嚴飾踰銀，又有毒蛇圍城十四匝，巨軀倍前舉首數丈。普施復思弘慈之定，蛇毒即消垂首而眠。登之入城中，有天人，覩普施歡喜曰：『久服靈耀，翔茲甚善，願留二時百八十日，吾願盡養，惟留威神。』即然許之，留為說法無上明行，訖即辭退。天人復以神珠一枚送之，明耀八十里，志之所願，眾寶滿其里數。『若子得道，願為弟子，神足無上。』「受其神珠，即復進路，覩琉璃城，光耀踰前，又有毒蛇巨軀甚大，遶城二十一匝，仰首瞋目當彼城門，復坐深思普慈之定，誓濟眾生，毒歇垂首。登之而入城中。有天人喜辭猶前，『請留三時，願供所志。』期竟辭退，又送神珠一枚，明耀百六十里，珠之所在，眾寶尋從滿其明內，在志所欲無求不獲。『子若得無上正真覺道者，吾願為弟子，有最明之智。』曰：『必獲爾願。』普施得珠曰：『斯足以濟眾生之困乏。』返其舊居。「海諸龍神僉會議曰：『吾等巨海，唯斯三珠為吾榮華。道士悉得，吾等何榮？寧都亡諸寶，不失斯珠。』海神化為凡人，當普施前立曰：『吾聞仁者獲世上寶，可得觀乎？』即以示之。神搏其首即取其珠。普施惟曰：『吾歷險阻經跨巨海乃獲斯寶，欲以拯濟眾生困乏，反為斯神所見奪乎？』曰：『爾還吾珠，不者吾竭爾海。』海神答曰：『爾言何虛？斯之巨海，深廣難測，孰能盡之？天日可殞，巨風可却，海之難竭猶空難毀也。』「普施曰：『昔吾錠光佛前願得道力，反覆眾海指擢須彌，震動天地又移諸剎。佛從吾志與吾願，吾今得之。今爾鬼𩲐糸髮之邪力，焉能遏吾正真之勢乎？』即說：經曰：『吾自無數劫來，飲母乳湩、啼哭之淚、身死血流，海所不受。恩愛難絕、生死難止，吾尚欲絕恩愛之本、止生死之神。今世抒之不盡，世世抒之。』即住併兩足，瓢抒海水投鐵圍外。「有天名遍淨，遙聞之，深自惟曰：『昔吾於錠光佛前，聞斯人獲其志願，必為世尊度吾眾生。』天即下，助其抒水，十分去八。海神悔怖曰：『斯何人哉？而有無極之靈乎！斯水盡矣，吾居壞也。』即出眾寶空其諸藏以與普施。普施不受，曰：『唯欲得吾珠耳。』諸神還其珠，普施返其水。「旋其本土，尋路布施，所過之國，國無貧民，處處諸國無不改操五戒十善以為國政，開獄大赦，潤逮眾生，遂至得佛。」佛告諸沙門：「普施者，我身是。父者，白淨王是。母者，即吾母舍妙是。道士女者，今俱夷是。時，銀城中天者，今現阿難是。金城中天者，目連是。琉璃城中天者，舍利弗是。菩薩累劫勤行四恩，誓願求佛，拯濟眾生。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was born into a family of the four castes, and the moment he touched the ground he spoke, saying: "Living beings suffer ten thousand calamities; I will relieve them. They do not see the Buddha's presence, do not hear his clear teaching — I will open their ears and eyes, remove their blindness and deafness, and lead them to see and hear the unsurpassed truth, the king of all sages, the source of all clear models. Through giving I will draw them forward, and none will fail to submit." His nine classes of relatives, astonished, said: "Never since ancient times has an infant spoken such words — could this be the spirit of some heavenly dragon or god! We must divine this." The child answered his relatives at once: "I am transformed by the highest sages, and I hold within me the natural clarity of universal brightness — I am none of those strange spirits; have no doubt of this." Having said this, he fell silent. His relatives said: "This child holds the vast, generous resolve of heaven and earth — surely he is no ordinary man!" and they named him Pushi, "Universal Giving." By the age of ten he had mastered all the Buddhist scriptures and every worldly art and craft, and he took leave of his family to relieve the multitude through giving to the poor and needy. His family said: "We hold the finest and most fortunate of names — you may give as freely as you wish to relieve the poor and needy." He answered: "It is not enough," and asked to become a monk: "Grant me the robes of the Dharma, the alms bowl, and the walking staff, that I may use them to relieve the multitude — this has been my life's wish since birth." His family, recalling the vow he had made at his very birth, could find no words to refuse him, and so, following his wish, allowed him to become a monk. Traveling about and teaching, he passed through a great country, where there was a wealthy and powerful household, likewise learned in many texts, who, seeing Pushi's dignified and radiant bearing, his nature calm and pure as heavenly gold, bearing the marks of a supreme sage, one destined to become a hero of the world, said to Pushi: "I have something to ask of you, and hope the sage will grant it. I have an unworthy daughter, and wish to give her to you as a servant to attend your needs." He answered: "Very well — but you must wait until I return." He then set out toward the sea, took passage across it, came ashore, and entered the mountains, arriving at a place with no one else present, where he saw from a distance a silver city, its palace halls bright and beautiful. At that time a poisonous serpent coiled seven times around the city, its body vast, over a hundred spans around; seeing Pushi approach, it raised its head. Pushi thought: "Such a poisonous creature surely means harm — I will raise a boundless compassion to dissolve its poison. Malice is like fire, and compassion is like water; when has water ever failed to quench fire?" He sat at once and entered a meditation of compassion, vowing: "May all living beings soon be free of the eight difficulties, may their hearts abandon evil thoughts, may they meet the Buddha, see the Dharma, join the assembly of monks, and hear the unsurpassed, true and clear path, their hearts opening and their defilements dissolving, just as I myself have seen." Having entered this meditation of compassion, the serpent's poison dissolved at once, and it lowered its head and slept. Pushi climbed over its head and entered the city. Within the city was a heavenly spirit, who, seeing Pushi come, said with delight: "I have long revered your sagely virtue — that you have come flying here now fulfills what has long been in my heart. I beg you to stay a single season, ninety days." Pushi consented. The heavenly king then entrusted his official duties to a close attendant, and personally prepared meals for him, attending him morning and night with the utmost reverence, receiving instruction in the buddhas' teaching of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, and the clear teaching for relieving the multitude. When the season's provisions were exhausted, Pushi set out again on his way, and the heavenly king gave him a bright-moon pearl as a parting gift, saying: "Keep this pearl with you, and it will light forty leagues around you; whatever you wish for and speak aloud, every treasure will be provided in full. And if you attain buddhahood one day, I wish to be your disciple, attending closely at your holy side." Pushi said this would be so. He continued on his way, and saw a city of gold, its splendor surpassing the silver city, and again a poisonous serpent coiled fourteen times around it, its enormous body twice the size of the last, raising its head several spans high. Pushi again entered his meditation of vast compassion, and the serpent's poison dissolved at once, and it lowered its head and slept. He climbed over it and entered the city. There was a heavenly being within, who, seeing Pushi, said with joy: "I have long revered your spiritual brilliance — that you have come flying here is truly wonderful. I beg you to stay two seasons, a hundred and eighty days, and I wish to provide for your every need, only asking that you leave me your spiritual power." Pushi consented, and stayed, teaching him the unsurpassed clear conduct, and when the time was complete he took his leave. The heavenly being again gave him a spirit pearl as a parting gift, its light reaching eighty leagues, providing whatever treasures were wished for within that range: "If you attain the Way, I wish to be your disciple, foremost in spiritual powers." Receiving this spirit pearl, he continued on his way again, and saw a city of lapis lazuli, its brilliance surpassing even the last, and again a poisonous serpent, its enormous body larger still, coiled twenty-one times around the city, raising its head and glaring with furious eyes at the city gate. Again he sat and entered deeply into his meditation of universal compassion, vowing to relieve all living beings, and the poison was dissolved and the serpent lowered its head. He climbed over it and entered the city. The heavenly being there rejoiced and spoke as the others had: "I beg you to stay three seasons, and wish to provide for whatever you desire." When the time was complete he took his leave, and was given yet another spirit pearl, its light reaching a hundred and sixty leagues, and wherever the pearl was, every treasure would follow and fill that radius, whatever the heart desired, nothing withheld. "If you attain the unsurpassed, true and complete awakening, I wish to be your disciple, possessing the most brilliant wisdom." He said: "You shall surely have your wish." Pushi, having obtained the pearl, said: "This is enough to relieve the poverty and hardship of all living beings," and turned back toward his former home. The dragon spirits of the sea, all of them, held council together, saying: "In all our vast ocean, these three pearls alone are our glory. Now this ascetic has obtained them all — what glory is left to us? We would rather lose every other treasure than lose these pearls." A sea-spirit transformed himself into an ordinary man and stood before Pushi, saying: "I have heard that you, a man of virtue, have obtained the treasures of this world — might I see them?" Pushi showed them to him at once, and the spirit struck at his head and seized the pearls. Pushi thought: "I crossed every danger and traversed the vast sea to obtain these treasures, meaning to use them to relieve the poverty and hardship of living beings — and now they are seized from me by this spirit?" The spirit said: "Give me back my pearls, or I will drain your sea dry." The sea-spirit answered: "What empty words are these? This vast ocean is so deep and wide that none could ever exhaust it — the sun and moon might fall from the sky, the mightiest wind might be turned back, sooner than this sea could ever run dry, just as emptiness itself can never be destroyed." Pushi said: "Long ago, before the Buddha Dīpaṃkara, I vowed to gain the power of the Way, to overturn every ocean and pluck up Mount Sumeru itself, to shake heaven and earth and move every buddha-land. The Buddha granted my resolve and gave me my wish, and now I have attained it. How could the petty, hair-thin power of a ghost or spirit like you ever obstruct the momentum of my true and right course?" And he spoke these words: "Since numberless kalpas past, I have drunk my mother's milk, wept tears in my crying, and shed the blood of my own death — and even the sea could not hold it all. The bonds of love are hard to sever, birth and death are hard to end, and still I wish to sever the very root of love's bonds, to end the very cycle of birth and death. If this present age cannot exhaust the sea, then age after age I will go on bailing it out." He stood at once, planted both feet together, and began scooping the seawater out with a dipper and casting it beyond the Iron Ring Mountains. There was a heaven-being named Universal Purity, who, hearing this from afar, thought deeply to himself: "Long ago, before the Buddha Dīpaṃkara, I heard that this man would achieve his vow, and would surely, as the World-Honored One, deliver my own people." The heaven-being came down at once and helped him bail out the water, until eight-tenths of it was gone. The sea-spirit, terrified and repentant, said: "What manner of being is this, to have such boundless spiritual power! The water will soon be gone, and my dwelling destroyed with it." He brought out every treasure at once, emptying every one of his storehouses, and gave them all to Pushi. Pushi refused them, saying: "I want only my pearls back." The spirits returned his pearls to him, and Pushi returned their water to the sea. Returning to his own land, he gave freely along the way in every country he passed, and in every country he passed through, no one remained poor; everywhere, the countries reformed their governance according to the five precepts and the ten good deeds, opened their prisons and granted amnesty, and his grace reached every living being, until at last he attained buddhahood. The Buddha said to the monks: "Pushi was I myself. My father was King Śuddhodana. My mother was my mother, Māyā. The ascetic's daughter is now Yaśodharā. The heavenly being in the silver city is now Ānanda. The one in the golden city is Maudgalyāyana. The one in the lapis-lazuli city is Śāriputra. Through many kalpas the bodhisattva diligently practiced the Four Infinite Virtues, vowing to seek buddhahood and to relieve living beings." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

By far the longest and most elaborate story in this fascicle, structured as a journey through three cities of ascending value — silver, gold, and lapis lazuli — each guarded by a serpent whose poison is dissolved not by force but by a formal meditation on compassion, and each yielding a wish-fulfilling pearl from a grateful spirit who asks, in return, only to become the bodhisattva's future disciple. The closing confrontation with the sea-spirits, in which Pushi begins bailing out the entire ocean by hand rather than surrender what he has earned, is the fascicle's most vivid single image of resolve — and the story's final identifications tie an unusually large cast of the Buddha's own historical circle (his parents, his wife, and three of his most senior disciples) to figures met along this one journey.

10. King Dīrghāyu, Who Gave Up His Kingdom to Spare His People長壽王本生

Chinese Source

昔者菩薩為大國王，名曰長壽，太子名長生。其王仁惻，恒懷悲心，愍傷眾生，誓願濟度，精進不惓。刀杖不行，臣民無怨，風雨時節，寶穀豐沃。隣國小王執操暴虐，貪殘為法，國荒民貧，謂群臣曰：『吾聞長壽，其國豐富，去斯不遠，懷仁不殺，無兵革之備。吾欲奪之，其可獲乎？』群臣曰：『可。』則興戰士到大國界。「蕃屏之臣，馳表其狀惟願備豫。長壽則會群臣議曰：『彼王來者，惟貪吾國民眾寶多。若與之戰，必傷民命，利己殘民貪而不仁，吾不為也。』群臣僉曰：『臣等舊習軍謀兵法，請自滅之，無勞聖思。』王曰：『勝則彼死，弱則吾喪，彼兵、吾民皆天生育，重身惜命，誰不然哉？全己害民，賢者不為也。』群臣出曰：『斯天仁之君不可失也。』自相撿率以兵拒賊。「長壽覺之，謂太子曰：『彼貪吾國，懷毒而來。群臣以吾一人之身，欲殘民命。今吾委國，庶全天民。其義可乎？』太子曰：『諾。』父子踰城，即改名族隱於山草。「於是貪王遂入其國，群臣黎庶失其舊君，猶孝子喪其親，哀慟躃踊無門不然。貪王募之黃金千斤、錢千萬。「長壽出，於道邊樹下坐精思，悲愍眾生生死勤苦，不覩非常、苦、空、非身，為欲所惑，其苦無數。遠國梵志，聞王好施，濟眾生之命，遠來歸窮於樹下息。俱相問訊，各陳本末。梵志驚曰：『天王何緣若茲乎？』流淚自陳：『吾餘年無幾，故來乞匃，庶存餘命。大王亡國，吾命窮矣。』即為哀慟。王曰：『子來歸窮，而正值吾失國，無以濟子，不亦痛乎！』抆淚而曰：『吾聞新王募吾甚重，子取吾首，可獲重賞。』答曰：『不然。遙服天王仁濟眾生，潤等天地，故委本土庶蒙自濟。今勅斬首，不敢承命矣。』王曰：『身為朽器，豈敢保哉？夫生有死，孰有常存？若子不取，會為灰土矣。』梵志曰：『天王布天仁之惠，必欲殞命以濟下劣者，惟願散手相尋去耳。』王即尋從，之故城門，令縛以聞。「國人覩王，哀號動國。梵志獲賞。貪王命於四衢生燒殺之。群臣啟曰：『臣等舊君當就終沒，乞為微饌以贈死靈。』貪王曰：『可。』百官黎民哀慟塞路，躃踊宛轉靡不呼天。太子長生亦佯賣樵，當父前立。父覩之，仰天曰：『違父遺誨，含兇懷毒，蘊於重怨，連禍萬載非孝子矣。諸佛四等弘慈之潤，德韜天地，吾尋斯道殺身濟眾，猶懼不獲孝道微行，而況為虐報讎者乎？不替吾言，可謂孝矣。』子不忍視父死，還入深山。「王命終矣，太子哀呼，血流于口，曰：『吾君雖有臨終盡仁之誡，吾必違之，當誅毒鴆。』遂出傭賃，為臣種菜。臣偶行園，覩菜甚好，問其意狀。園監對曰：『市賃一人，妙于園種。』臣現問曰：『悉所能乎？』曰：『百工之巧，吾為其首。』臣請其王令為上饌，有踰太官。王曰：『斯食誰為之乎？』臣以狀對。王即取之，令為厨監。每事可焉，擢為近臣，告之曰：『長壽王子，吾之重讎，今以汝為蕃屏。』即曰：『唯然。』王曰：『好獵乎？』對曰：『臣好之。』王即出獵，馳馬逐獸與眾相失，唯與長生俱處山三日，遂至飢困，解劒授長生，枕其膝眠。長生曰：『今得汝不乎？』拔劒欲斬之，忽憶父命，曰：『違父之教，為不孝矣。』復劒而止。「王寤曰：『屬夢長生欲斬吾首，將何以也？』對曰：『山有強鬼喜為灼熱，臣自侍衛，將何懼矣？』王復還臥，如斯三者也，遂投劒曰：『吾為仁父，原赦爾命。』王寤曰：『夢見長生原吾命矣。』太子曰：『長生者，吾身是也。念父追讎之于今矣。吾父臨沒口遺仁誡，令吾遵諸佛忍辱、惡來善往之道。而吾含極愚之性，欲以兩毒相注。三思父誡，三釋劒矣。願大王疾相誅除重患也，身死神遷，惡意不生。』王悔過曰：『吾為暴虐，不別臧否。子之先君，高行純備，亡國不亡行，可謂上聖乎！子存親全行，可謂孝乎！吾為豺狼殘生苟飽，今命在子赦而不戮，後豈違之乎！今欲返國，由何道也？』對曰：『斯惑路者，吾之為也。』將王出林與群寮會。王曰：『諸君識長生不乎？』僉曰：『不識。』王曰：『斯即長生矣！今還其國，吾返本居。自今為伯仲，禍福同之。』立太子之日，率土悲喜交并莫不稱壽。貪王還其國，更相貢獻，遂致隆平。」佛告諸沙門：「時長壽王者，吾身是也。太子者，阿難是。貪王者，調達是。調達世世毒意向我，我輙濟之。阿難與調達本自無怨，故不相害也。吾世世忍不可忍者，制意立行，故今得佛為三界尊。菩薩慈惠度無極行布施如是。」六度集經卷第一六度集經卷第二吳康居國沙門康僧會譯布施度無極章此有四章

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago the bodhisattva was a great king named Dīrghāyu, "Long Life," whose crown prince was named Dīrghāyus, "Long-Living." The king was benevolent and compassionate, always holding a heart of sorrow for others, grieving for living beings and vowing to relieve and deliver them, tireless in his diligence. He never resorted to sword or staff, and his ministers and people held no resentment; the rains came in their season, and the grain was rich and abundant. The king of a small neighboring country, harsh and violent in his rule, greedy and cruel in his governance, whose own country was wasted and whose people were poor, said to his ministers: "I have heard that Dīrghāyu's country is rich and abundant, and it is not far from here; he holds to kindness and will not kill, and keeps no defenses of arms. If I wish to seize it, could it be done?" His ministers said, "It could." So he raised soldiers and brought them to the borders of the great country. The border officials sent word in haste, hoping the king would prepare his defenses. Dīrghāyu gathered his ministers and deliberated, saying: "That king comes only out of greed for my people and my abundant treasures. If we fight him, we will surely harm many lives — to profit oneself while destroying one's own people is greed without benevolence, and I will not do this." His ministers all said together: "We, your servants, have long studied the arts of war and military strategy — allow us to destroy him ourselves, and spare Your Majesty this trouble." The king said: "If we win, his people die; if we lose, my own people are lost. His soldiers and my own people are both the offspring of heaven — who does not treasure his own body and cherish his own life? To preserve oneself at the cost of harming the people is something a worthy man will not do." His ministers went out, saying: "Such a heaven-blessed and benevolent lord we cannot afford to lose," and gathered themselves together to resist the enemy with arms regardless. Dīrghāyu, aware of this, said to the crown prince: "That king covets my country and comes with poison in his heart. My ministers, for the sake of my single life, wish to destroy the lives of my people. If I now give up the kingdom, perhaps the lives of my people can all be spared. Would this not be right?" The crown prince said, "Yes." Father and son together climbed over the city wall, and, changing their names and lineage, hid themselves among the mountains and grasses. Then the greedy king entered the country. His ministers and people, having lost their former lord, mourned as a filial son mourns the loss of a parent, wailing and beating their breasts without restraint. The greedy king offered a reward of a thousand catties of gold and ten million coins for Dīrghāyu's capture. Dīrghāyu, having left, sat beneath a tree by the roadside, deep in sorrowful thought, grieving for the painful toil of living beings caught in birth and death, who do not see impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, and are deceived by their desires into countless sufferings. A brahmin from a distant country, having heard of the king's love of giving and his relief of the lives of the multitude, had come from far away, seeking refuge in his poverty, and rested beneath this same tree. They greeted each other, and each told the other his circumstances. The brahmin, astonished, said: "Heavenly King, how have you come to such a state?" and wept, telling his own story: "My remaining years are few, and so I have come to beg, hoping to preserve what life I have left. Now that the Great King has lost his kingdom, my own life too is at its end." And he wept for him. The king said: "You have come to me in your poverty and need, and it happens to be just the moment I have lost my kingdom, and I have nothing with which to help you — is this not a painful thing!" Wiping away his tears, he said: "I have heard that the new king offers a very great reward for me — if you take my head, you may claim this rich reward." The brahmin answered: "It is not so. From afar I have long revered the Heavenly King's benevolence in relieving living beings, his grace equal to heaven and earth, and this is why I came here from my own country, hoping to find relief for myself. Now, though you command me to take your head, I dare not accept such an order." The king said: "This body is but a fragile vessel — how could I hope to keep it forever? All that is born must die; what has ever lasted always? If you do not take it, it will in the end become dust and ash all the same." The brahmin said: "Heavenly King, if you truly wish, out of your heaven-wide benevolence, to give up your life to relieve the lowly and humble, then I beg only that you release your hands and let me go my own way." The king then followed him at once to the gate of his former city, and had himself bound and reported. The people of the country, seeing their king, wailed and their grief shook the whole land. The brahmin received his reward. The greedy king ordered him burned alive at the crossroads of the city. The ministers pleaded: "We, your servants, wish only, since our former lord must now come to his end, to be permitted a small offering to send off his departing spirit." The greedy king said, "Very well." Officials and common people alike, grieving in the streets, threw themselves down and writhed, all crying out to heaven. The crown prince Dīrghāyus, disguised as a firewood seller, stood before his father as he passed. The father, seeing him, looked up to heaven and said: "To go against a father's final teaching, to harbor malice and nurse a grudge, binding oneself to disaster for ten thousand generations — this is not the conduct of a filial son. The buddhas' boundless compassion, born of the Four Infinite Virtues, its virtue enfolding heaven and earth — I follow this path even to the point of giving my own life to relieve the multitude, and still I fear I may fail to achieve even the smallest measure of filial conduct — how much more so one who returns violence with vengeance? If you do not defy my words, this may be called filial piety." The son could not bear to watch his father die, and returned deep into the mountains. The king's life then came to its end. The crown prince cried out in grief, blood flowing from his mouth, and said: "Though my lord, at the very end, gave me his final teaching of perfect benevolence, I must go against it — I will surely put this poisonous tyrant to death." He then went out and hired himself as a laborer, working as a gardener for the minister of that king. The minister, passing through his garden one day, saw how fine the vegetables were, and asked about it. The garden overseer answered: "We hired a man in the market, most skilled in gardening." The minister asked him directly: "Can you do everything?" He answered: "Among all the hundred crafts, I am foremost." The minister had him prepare a feast for the king, surpassing even the royal kitchens. The king asked: "Who prepared this food?" and the minister told him the truth. The king summoned him at once, and made him steward of the kitchen. Everything he did pleased the king, and he was raised to the rank of a close attendant. The king said to him: "The son of King Dīrghāyu is my greatest enemy — I now make you my border guard." He said, "As you command." The king asked: "Do you enjoy hunting?" He answered, "I do." The king went out hunting, and, chasing after game on horseback, became separated from his companions, and was left alone with Dīrghāyus in the mountains for three days, until they grew hungry and exhausted. The king loosened his sword and gave it to Dīrghāyus, and lay down to sleep with his head on Dīrghāyus's knee. Dīrghāyus thought: "Now I have you." He drew the sword to strike, but suddenly remembered his father's command, and said: "To go against my father's teaching would be unfilial," and sheathed the sword and stopped. The king woke and said: "I dreamed just now that Dīrghāyus meant to cut off my head — what could this mean?" He answered: "The mountains have fierce spirits who delight in causing burning fevers — I myself am your guard, what is there to fear?" The king lay back down again, and this happened three times in all, until at last Dīrghāyus threw down the sword and said: "I will be a benevolent father to him, and forgive and spare his life." The king woke and said: "I dreamed that Dīrghāyus forgave my life." The crown prince said: "Dīrghāyus is myself. I have carried the thought of avenging my father down to this very moment. My father, at the point of death, left me his final teaching of benevolence, commanding me to follow the buddhas' way of patience, of repaying evil with good. And yet I, harboring the most extreme foolishness in my nature, thought to let two poisons destroy each other. Three times I remembered my father's teaching, and three times I sheathed my sword. I beg that the Great King put me to death at once and be rid of this grave danger — when my body dies and my spirit departs, no evil intention will arise again." The king, repenting, said: "I have been cruel and violent, unable to tell good from evil. Your late father, of the highest and most complete conduct — his kingdom was lost, but his conduct was not lost — he may truly be called a supreme sage! And you, preserving your father's memory and keeping his conduct whole — you may truly be called filial! I have been a wolf, devouring life to fill my own belly, and now that my life lies in your hands, you spare me instead of killing me — how could I ever go against this? Now I wish to return to my own kingdom — by which road should I go?" He answered: "It was I who confused the way — that was my own doing." He led the king out of the forest to meet with his assembled officials. The king said: "Does anyone here recognize Dīrghāyus?" They all said, "We do not know him." The king said: "This is Dīrghāyus himself! I now return his kingdom to him, and I will return to my own land. From this day we shall be as brothers, sharing fortune and misfortune alike." On the day the crown prince was established as king, grief and joy mingled throughout the land, and there was no one who did not wish him long life. The greedy king returned to his own country, and from then on the two exchanged tribute with one another, and lasting peace was achieved. The Buddha said to the monks: "King Dīrghāyu at that time was I myself. The crown prince was Ānanda. The greedy king was Devadatta. Devadatta has borne poisonous intent toward me life after life, and life after life I have relieved him all the same. Ānanda and Devadatta originally held no grudge against one another, and so did not harm each other. Life after life I have endured what is hard to endure, mastering my will and establishing my conduct, and so I have now attained buddhahood, honored as the World-Honored One of the three realms." This is how the bodhisattva, practicing the boundless perfection of compassionate giving, gave.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The longest story in this fascicle, and the only one built around a full political drama rather than a single act of giving: a king surrenders his own kingdom specifically to spare his people the cost of defending it, is betrayed into execution by a beggar he had every reason to trust, and is avenged — almost, three times over — by a son who each time remembers his father's dying instruction to return violence with patience rather than vengeance. The final reconciliation, in which the usurping king restores the kingdom entirely on the strength of the prince's forgiveness, closes the fascicle's opening chapter on giving with a story that is, in the end, as much about patience as about generosity — a foreshadowing of the chapter on patience yet to come.
