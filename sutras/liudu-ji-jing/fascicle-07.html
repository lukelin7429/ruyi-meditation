The Translation

74. What the Perfection of Meditation Means〔得禪法〕

Chinese Source

禪度無極者云何？端其心，壹其意，合會眾善，內著心中，意諸穢惡，以善消之。凡有四禪：一禪之行，去所貪愛五妖邪事，眼覩華色心為淫狂，去耳聲、鼻香、口味、身好，道行之志必當遠彼。又有五蓋：貪財蓋、恚怒蓋、睡眠蓋、淫樂蓋、悔疑蓋。有道無道，有佛無佛，有經無經，心意識念，清淨無垢，心明覩真得無不知，天龍鬼妖所不能惑。猶人有十怨脫身離之，獨處山間眾所不知、無所復畏，人遠情慾，內淨心寂，斯謂一禪。心獲一禪進向二禪，第二之禪，如人避怨，雖處深山懼怨尋之，逾自深藏，行家雖遠十情慾怨，猶恐慾賊來壞道志，得第二禪，情慾稍遠不能污己。第一之禪，善惡諍已，以善消惡，惡退善進。第二之禪，喜心寂止，不復以善住消彼惡也。喜善二意，悉自消滅，十惡煙絕，外無因緣來入心者。譬如高山其頂有泉，無流入者，亦非龍雨水自內出，水淨泉滿，善內心出，惡不復由耳目鼻口入，御心如是，便向三禪。第三之禪，守意牢固，善惡不入，心安如須彌，諸善不出。外事善惡寂滅不入，心猶蓮華根莖在水，華合未發為水所覆。三禪之行，其淨猶華，去離眾惡身意俱安。御心如是，便向四禪。善惡皆棄，心不念善亦不存惡，心中明淨猶琉璃珠，又如士女淨自沐浴，名香塗身，內外衣新，鮮明上服，表裏香淨，菩薩心端獲彼四禪。群邪眾垢無能蔽其心，猶若淨繒在作何色。又如陶家埏埴為器，泥無沙礫任作何器。又猶鍛師熟煉名金，百奇千巧從心所欲。菩薩心淨得彼四禪，在意所由，輕舉騰飛，履水而行，分身散體，變化萬端，出入無間存亡自由。摸日月，動天地，洞視徹聽靡不聞見，心淨觀明得一切智，未有天地眾生所更，十方現在眾心所念，未萠之事，眾生魂靈為天為人，入太山、餓鬼、畜生道中，福盡受罪，殃訖受福，無遠不知。夫得四禪，欲得溝港、頻來、不還、應儀，各佛如來、至真平等正覺、無上之明，求之即得。猶若萬物皆因地生，自五通智至于世尊，皆四禪成，猶眾生所作非地不立。眾祐又曰：『群生處世，正使天帝仙聖巧黠之智，不覩斯經，不獲四棄之定者，猶為愚矇也。』既有智慧，而復一心即近度世。此為菩薩禪度無極一心如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the essential nature of the perfection of meditative concentration? To make the mind upright, to unify one's intention, to gather every good quality within, holding it fast in the mind, while dissolving every defilement and evil through goodness. There are, in general, four levels of meditative absorption. In the first absorption's practice, one gives up the five wicked and deceptive things bound up with craving: the eye seeing beautiful form and the mind growing wild with lust for it; one must likewise give up sound heard by the ear, fragrance smelled by the nose, taste savored by the mouth, and pleasing sensation felt by the body — the resolve of one who practices the Way must hold itself far from these. There are also five hindrances: the hindrance of craving for wealth, the hindrance of anger and rage, the hindrance of drowsiness, the hindrance of lustful pleasure, and the hindrance of regret and doubt. Whether the Way exists or not, whether a buddha exists or not, whether scriptures exist or not — the mind's own awareness and thought, pure and free of defilement, the mind bright and beholding the truth, attains a state where nothing remains unknown, and gods, dragons, and wicked spirits can no longer deceive it. It is like a person who has ten enemies and manages to free himself from all of them, dwelling alone in the mountains where the crowd knows nothing of him and he need fear nothing further; far from the passions of ordinary people, inwardly pure and the mind stilled — this is called the first absorption. Once the mind has attained the first absorption, it advances toward the second. In the second absorption, it is like a person avoiding an enemy: though he dwells deep in the mountains, still fearing the enemy might seek him out, he conceals himself yet more deeply. Though the household passions of the ten cravings are now far away, one still fears that the thief of desire might come to destroy one's resolve for the Way; upon attaining the second absorption, the passions grow gradually more distant and can no longer defile oneself. In the first absorption, good and evil still contend with one another, with good dissolving evil, evil retreating as good advances. In the second absorption, the mind of joy comes to rest, and one no longer relies on goodness to actively dissolve evil. Both joy and goodness themselves entirely dissolve away, and the smoke of the ten evils is cut off; from outside, no further cause or condition can enter the mind. It is like a spring at the summit of a high mountain, into which nothing flows from outside, and which is not filled by rain from a dragon, but wells up purely from within itself, its water pure and the spring full; goodness arises from within the mind itself, and evil no longer enters through ear, eye, nose, or mouth. Governing the mind in this way, one then advances toward the third absorption. In the third absorption, one's guard over the mind grows firm and solid, and neither good nor evil can enter; the mind is as settled as Mount Sumeru, with no goodness escaping outward and no external matter of good or evil, entirely stilled, entering in. The mind is like a lotus, its root and stem within the water, its blossom closed and not yet opened, still covered by the water. The practice of the third absorption is pure as that blossom, departed from every evil, the body and mind both at peace. Governing the mind in this way, one then advances toward the fourth absorption. Both good and evil are entirely cast aside; the mind neither dwells on goodness nor retains any evil, and is bright and pure within, like a crystal jewel — or like a man or woman freshly bathed, anointed with fragrant perfume, dressed within and without in fresh new robes, radiant and fine, fragrant and pure both inside and out. The bodhisattva's mind, made upright in this way, attains these four absorptions. No wicked host or defilement can any longer obscure his mind, just as a piece of pure silk can be dyed whatever color one wishes, or as a potter shapes clay free of sand and grit into whatever vessel he desires, or as a skilled smith, having thoroughly refined fine gold, can shape it into a hundred marvels and a thousand cunning forms exactly as his heart wills. The bodhisattva, his mind purified, attaining these four absorptions, may do as his intention leads him: rising lightly and flying through the air, walking upon water, dividing and scattering his own body, transforming himself in countless ways, appearing and vanishing without any gap, coming and going as freely as he wishes. He may reach out to the sun and moon, move heaven and earth, seeing and hearing without obstruction, missing nothing; his mind pure and his vision clear, he attains complete wisdom, knowing what has passed between heaven, earth, and living beings even before it occurred, knowing the thoughts held in every mind of the ten directions in the present moment, and even matters not yet sprouted into being; he knows where the spirits of living beings, whether reborn as gods or as humans, or entering Mount Tai, the realm of hungry ghosts, or the animal realm, will receive punishment when their blessing is exhausted or receive blessing when their punishment is complete — nothing, however distant, remains unknown to him. Whoever attains these four absorptions and wishes to attain the fruit of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, or arhatship, or the state of any buddha, the Tathāgata, the truly and equally awakened, the unsurpassed brightness — need only seek it, and it is attained. Just as the ten thousand things all arise from the earth, so too, from the wisdom of the five spiritual powers up to that of the World-Honored One himself, all is accomplished through these four absorptions, just as nothing living beings create can stand without the earth beneath it. The Blessed One said further: "Living beings dwelling in this world — even should they possess the cleverness of Śakra, lord of the gods, or of an immortal sage, if they have not encountered this scripture and have not attained the concentration that abandons the four hindrances, they remain, in truth, no different from the ignorant." Once wisdom is present, and the mind further becomes single-pointed, one draws near to liberation from the world. This is how the bodhisattva, with a single-pointed mind, practiced the boundless perfection of meditative concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This opening story departs from the collection's usual narrative pattern entirely, reading instead as a direct doctrinal exposition of the four meditative absorptions (dhyāna) — no protagonist, no plot, no closing identification of characters. It functions as the chapter's own preamble in miniature, laying out the technical framework (the five hindrances, the four absorptions, their respective marks) that the following eight stories will illustrate through narrative.

75. A Monk's Progress Through the Four Absorptions〔比丘得禪〕

Chinese Source

昔者比丘，飯畢澡漱，入深山丘墓間樹下坐，叉手低頭，一心滅念。內意心中消去五蓋，五蓋滅後，其心煚然，冥退明存。顧愍天人蜎飛蚑行蠕動之類，傷其愚惑懷斯五蓋，遏絕明善之心。消去五蓋諸善即強，猶若貧人舉債治生，獲利還彼餘財修居，日有利入，其人心喜。又如奴使免為良民，困病獲瘳，九族日興，牢獄重罪逢赦得出。又如重寶渡海歷險，還家見親其喜無量。心懷五蓋猶斯五苦，比丘見諦去離五蓋，猶彼凡人免上五患，蓋退明進，眾惡悉滅，道志強盛即獲一禪。自一禪之二禪，凡有三行：一曰勤仂，二曰數念，三曰思惟。自斯三事得成四禪，以一禪至二禪，以二禪之三禪，以三禪之四禪。四禪勝三禪，三禪勝二禪，二勝一。第一之禪，十惡退，五善進。何謂十惡？眼樂色、耳音、鼻香、口味、身好，并上五蓋，謂之十惡。何謂五善？一計、二念、三愛、四樂、五曰一心。斯五善處內。第二之禪不計、不念，制心內觀，善行在內，不復由耳目鼻口出入，善惡二行不復相干，心處在內唯有歡喜也。三禪之行除去歡喜，心尚清淨，怕然寂寞，眾祐各佛應儀曰：『諸能滅欲淨其心者，身終始安。』第四之禪喜心去，得寂定。一禪耳為聲亂，二禪心為念亂，三禪心歡喜亂，四禪心為喘息亂。一禪耳聲止，進至二禪，二禪念滅，進至三禪，三禪歡喜滅，進至四禪，四禪喘息滅得空定。菩薩禪度無極一心如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago a monk, having finished his meal and rinsed his mouth, entered deep into the mountains, and, beneath a tree among the hills and graves, sat with hands joined and head lowered, his mind single-pointed and every thought extinguished. Within his mind, he dissolved away the five hindrances, and, once these five hindrances were dissolved, his mind became bright and clear, darkness withdrawing and brightness remaining. Looking with compassion upon the gods, humans, and every flying, crawling, and wriggling creature, he grieved for their foolish confusion, bound as they were by these five hindrances, which cut off the bright and virtuous mind within them. Once the five hindrances are dissolved, every virtue grows strong of itself — it is like a poor man who borrows money to establish his livelihood, and, having gained profit from it, repays the debt and still has wealth remaining to sustain his household, with daily profit coming in besides, so that his heart rejoices. Or it is like a servant freed to become a commoner, or a man gravely ill who recovers, his extended family flourishing day by day, or a prisoner condemned to a grave crime who is granted amnesty and released. Or it is like one who, carrying precious treasure across the sea through great danger, returns home to see his own family again, his joy beyond measure. A mind burdened by the five hindrances suffers exactly these five kinds of hardship; a monk who sees the truth and departs from the five hindrances is like that ordinary man freed from these five afflictions — as the hindrances retreat, brightness advances, and every evil is entirely dissolved, and, the resolve for the Way growing strong and full, the first absorption is attained. "From the first absorption to the second, there are, in general, three practices: first, diligent effort; second, sustained mindfulness; third, deep reflection. Through these three, the four absorptions are accomplished — from the first absorption to the second, from the second to the third, and from the third to the fourth. The fourth absorption surpasses the third, the third surpasses the second, and the second surpasses the first. In the first absorption, the ten evils retreat and the five virtues advance. What are the ten evils? The eye's delight in form, the ear's in sound, the nose's in fragrance, the mouth's in taste, the body's in pleasing sensation, together with the five hindrances mentioned above — these together are called the ten evils. What are the five virtues? First, calculation; second, sustained thought; third, love; fourth, joy; fifth, single-pointedness of mind. These five virtues dwell within. In the second absorption, calculation and sustained thought are set aside, and the mind, restrained, observes only within; virtuous conduct dwells entirely within, no longer passing in and out through ear, eye, nose, and mouth, and good and evil conduct no longer interfere with one another; the mind, dwelling within, holds only joy. The practice of the third absorption removes even this joy, the mind still pure, calm and tranquil; the Blessed One, addressing each buddha of arhat-attainment, said: 'Whoever can extinguish desire and purify the mind will find the body at peace from beginning to end.' In the fourth absorption, even the mind of joy departs, and one attains still, settled concentration. In the first absorption, the ear is disturbed by sound; in the second, the mind is disturbed by thought; in the third, the mind is disturbed by joy; in the fourth, the mind is disturbed by the very breath itself. In the first absorption, the disturbance of sound through the ear is stilled, and one advances to the second; in the second, thought is dissolved, and one advances to the third; in the third, joy is dissolved, and one advances to the fourth; in the fourth, even the breath itself is dissolved, and one attains the concentration of emptiness." This is how the bodhisattva, with a single-pointed mind, practiced the boundless perfection of meditative concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A second consecutive doctrinal exposition rather than a story, this one tracing a monk's own progress through the four absorptions in closer technical detail, including a fivefold breakdown of what dissolves at each stage (sound, thought, joy, and finally the breath itself). Read alongside Story 74, these two texts function as a linked pair establishing the chapter's vocabulary before the narrative material that follows.

76. Sixteen Contemplations That Lead to a Single-Pointed Mind〔菩薩得禪〕

Chinese Source

菩薩志道，凡以幾事，能令內淨心一得禪？或見老者，頭白齒落形體變異，覩之意悟曰：『吾後必然。』一心得禪。或覩病者身心困痛，猶被杖楚。悵然悟曰：『吾後必然。』一心得禪。或覩眾生壽命終訖，息絕熅逝，神遷身冷，九族捐之，遠著外野，旬日之間胮脹爛臭，或為狐犬眾鳥所噉，肌肉生蟲，蟲還食身，膿血惡露滂沱流地，骸骨解散，節節異處，足趺脛髀，尻脊脇臂，頭齒髑髏，各自分離。道人念曰：『夫生有死，人物猶幻，會即有離，神逝體散。吾豈得止，獨不如彼乎？』覩之愴然，一心得禪。或見久死體骨消滅，泥土同塵。深自惟曰：『吾體方爾。』一心得禪。或以聞太山湯火之毒、酷裂之痛，餓鬼飢饉積年之勞，畜生屠剝割截之苦，存之愕然，一心得禪，或見窮凍餓死，或見履非之人為王法所戮。道人念曰：『斯人遭患由無道志，吾不精進必復如彼也。』一其心得禪。深惟內觀，下即為𡱁尿所迫，上即為寒熱所愶，覺身可惡，一心得禪。或見惡歲五穀不豐，民窮為亂更相挌戰，死屍縱橫，覩之愴然：『吾不為道必復如之。』一其心得禪。覩盛有衰榮財難保，少壯有老病壽猶電光，憶之愕然，一其心得禪。念佛巍巍相難雙矣，皆由清淨致為眾祐，存之欣然，一其心得禪。念經深義，沙門高行，一其心得禪。惟身行善，前後積德，一其心得禪。惟愚所求違佛明法，勞而益罪，諸天處世守戒奉齋，自致升天榮壽無量，一其心得禪。受佛深經反覆思之，為眾訓導，中心歡喜，一其心得禪。存憶眾生有成輙壞，壞皆苦痛，惟之愴然，一其心得禪。眾生之性莫能自保，來始之變，道人自懼，命盡卒至，或墮惡道，視世榮樂真偽如夢，志重醒悟，一其心得禪。諸食入口與涕唾澆灒，外好內臭化成屎尿，憶之可惡，一其心得禪。兒在母腹，初如凝粥，以漸長大，三十八七日，身體皆成，臨生之難，多危少安。既生之後，諸病並進，或一或十、或五十至百年，皆當老死無免斯患，惟己亦然，一其心得禪。有存即滅，尋之無處，三界皆空，志無貪慕，悲念眾生不覩佛經，邪欲所蔽無知非常，誓願拯濟，一其心得禪。「志成行高，懷四等心愍育眾生，猶若慈母哀護幼兒，兒隨輩熙戲，母以慈心行索，覩兒為泥塵所污，飢渴啼呼。覩兒若茲，悲淚抱歸，洗浴衣食，身康心悅，慈母歡喜，愛攝徘徊不捨如前。道人慈悲愛護眾生，踰彼慈母，教天下人蜎飛蚑行蠕動之類，奉佛覩經，親沙門眾，採執佛戒懷而行之，遠離三惡，心念善、口言善、身行善，抑上三惡，永興三善，長不令更太山、地獄、餓鬼、畜生窮苦險處。安以無極之福堂，尋復追誨，懼其處福為之憍蕩，恣縱惡心還處三塗，亦榮祿之禍，非常苦空之變以誡之也。勸取無為，如彼慈母攝護之意也。「思十六事，一其心得禪。何謂十六？喘息長短即自知，喘息動身即自知，喘息微著即自知，喘息快不快即自知，喘息止走即自知，喘息歡慼即自知，自惟萬物無常喘息自知，萬物過去不可追得喘息自知，內無所思棄捐所惟喘息自知，放棄軀命不棄軀命喘息自知。道人深思，有是即得是，無是不得是，夫生必有老死之患，魂靈不滅即更受身。不生即無老，不老即無死，念是一其心得禪。道人以眼觀世生死，但以十二因緣，念此一其心得禪。「道人以五事自觀形體：一曰自觀面類數變，二曰苦樂數移，三曰志意數轉，四曰形體數異，五曰善惡數改。是謂五事。數有變異猶如流水前後相及，念此一其心得禪。道人念禪當云何？目見死人自頭至足，諦思熟視存想著心，行坐臥起飯飲萬役，常念著心以固其志，得禪自在所念。譬如人炊數斛米飯欲知熟未，直取一米捻𭶥視之，一米熟者明餘者皆熟。道志若茲，心之迴走，猶水之流，道人直念一事，心停意淨，應儀真道，滅度可得。「第一之禪欲得應儀，可得不？曰「中有得者有不得者。何行能得？何行不得？於一禪中，有念有愛，道則不成。天地無常，虛空難保，盡內穢垢，無貪愛念，志淨如斯，應真可得。二三至四，執心當如一禪。志存一禪未得應儀，命終可趣，即上七天受壽一劫；在二禪終，上十一天受壽二劫；處三禪終，上十五天受壽八劫；處四禪終，上十九天壽十六劫。「道人自觀內體惡露都為不淨，髮膚髑髏皮肌，眼瞬涕唾，筋脉肉髓，肝肺腸胃，心膽脾腎，屎尿膿血，眾穢共合乃成為人。猶若以囊盛五穀也，有目瀉囊，分別視之，種種各異。明人如此內觀其身，四大種數各自有名都為無人，以無欲觀乃覩本空，一其心得禪。「道人深觀別身四大，地水火風。髮毛骨齒，皮肉五藏，斯即地也。目淚涕唾，膿血汗肪，髓腦小便，斯即水也。內身溫熱主消食者，斯即火也。喘息呼吸，斯即風也。譬如屠兒殺畜刳解，別作四分具知委曲。道人內觀分別四大，此地彼水，火風俱然，都為無人，念之志寂，一其心得禪。道人自覺喘息長短，遲疾巨細皆別知之，猶人削物自知深淺，念息如此，一其心得禪。菩薩禪度無極一心如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

By how many means does the bodhisattva's resolve for the Way come to purify the mind within and attain a single-pointed absorption? Sometimes, seeing an old person, his hair white and teeth fallen out, his body and form transformed, one is struck with sudden understanding: "I too must surely become like this." With this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Sometimes, seeing a sick person, body and mind in pain and distress as though beaten with a rod, one is struck with sorrowful understanding: "I too must surely become like this." With this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Sometimes, seeing a living being whose life has come to its end, breath extinguished and vapor departed, the spirit gone and the body grown cold, cast aside by the extended family and left out in the distant wilderness — within ten days, swollen and rotting and foul, devoured perhaps by foxes, dogs, or birds, the flesh crawling with worms, the worms in turn feeding on the flesh, pus and blood and foul fluid flowing over the ground, the bones falling apart, joint separated from joint, the feet, ankles, shins, thighs, hips, spine, ribs, arms, head, teeth, and skull each scattered on their own — the practitioner of the Way reflects: "All that is born must die; people and things alike are mere illusions, and what comes together must eventually part; the spirit departs and the body scatters. How could I alone remain unaffected, different from this?" Seeing this, struck with sorrow, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Sometimes, seeing bones long dead, their form dissolved into dust indistinguishable from mud, one reflects deeply: "My own body will soon be just the same." With this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Sometimes, hearing of the poison and burning fire of Mount Tai, its cruel and searing pain, of the hungry ghosts' years of starving toil, of the animal realm's slaughter, flaying, and cutting, one is struck with dread; with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Sometimes, seeing one who has frozen or starved to death, or one condemned and executed by the king's own law, the practitioner of the Way reflects: "This person met such misfortune because he lacked the resolve for the Way — if I am not diligent, I too will surely become like this." With this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Reflecting deeply and observing within, one recognizes that below, one is pressed by the urgency of one's own bodily wastes, and above, threatened by cold and heat, and, feeling the body itself as something detestable, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Sometimes, seeing a year of bad harvest, the five grains failing to ripen, the people, in their desperation, turning to chaos and fighting one another, corpses strewn everywhere, one is struck with dread: "If I do not follow the Way, I too will surely become like this." With this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Seeing abundance turn to decline, glory and wealth hard to keep, youth turn to old age and sickness, a lifespan as brief as a flash of lightning, recalling this with a shock of realization, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Reflecting on the Buddha's towering greatness, beyond compare, all achieved through purity, becoming thereby the Blessed One, one holds this thought with delight, and with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Reflecting on the profound meaning of the scriptures, and the lofty conduct of monks, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Reflecting on one's own bodily conduct, the good, and the virtue one has accumulated over time, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Reflecting on how the foolish seek what runs contrary to the Buddha's own clear teaching, laboring in vain and only increasing their own guilt, while the gods, dwelling in this world, keep the precepts and observe the fasts, thereby ascending to the heavens with boundless glory and lifespan, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Receiving the Buddha's own profound scriptures and reflecting on them again and again, teaching and guiding the multitude with them, one's heart filled with joy, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. Recalling that living beings, once they have achieved something, inevitably see it collapse, and that this collapse always brings suffering, reflecting on this with sorrow, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. The nature of living beings can never be kept secure; from the moment of their very arising, they are already changing — the practitioner of the Way, fearing this for himself, knows that when his own life comes to its end, it may arrive suddenly, and he may fall into an evil path; seeing the glory and pleasure of this world as true or false, just as one sees a dream, his resolve turns all the more toward awakening, and with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. All food, once it enters the mouth, mixes with saliva and spit; fair without and foul within, it transforms into excrement and urine — recalling this as something detestable, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. A child in its mother's womb is at first like congealed gruel, and gradually grows larger; after thirty-eight sevens of days, the body is entirely formed, and the danger of birth itself holds much peril and little safety. Once born, sicknesses of every kind arise in succession, whether one lives one year, ten, fifty, or a hundred — all must grow old and die, with no one escaping this affliction; reflecting that this too awaits oneself, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. What exists is bound to be extinguished, and, seeking it afterward, no trace can be found; the three realms are all empty, and one's resolve holds no craving or attachment; grieving for living beings who do not see the Buddha's scriptures, obscured by wicked desire and ignorant of impermanence, one vows to relieve them, and with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. "Once this resolve is accomplished and conduct grows lofty, holding the four immeasurable minds and caring for living beings with compassion, it is like a loving mother caring for her infant child: as the child plays with its companions, the mother, with a compassionate heart, goes to seek him out, and, seeing the child soiled with mud and dust, hungry and thirsty and crying out, is moved with grief and tears, and, embracing him, carries him home, bathes him, clothes and feeds him, until his body is well and his heart at ease; the loving mother, rejoicing, holds and cherishes him, tending him without ceasing, exactly as before. The practitioner of the Way, in compassion, cares for living beings even beyond how that loving mother cares for her child, teaching every being of heaven, whether flying, crawling, or wriggling, to honor the Buddha, see the scriptures, draw close to the community of monks, take up the Buddha's precepts, and hold and practice them, departing far from the three evils; the mind reflecting goodness, the mouth speaking goodness, the body practicing goodness — suppressing the three evils above, and forever establishing the three virtues, so that one need never again pass through the extreme suffering of Mount Tai, hell, the hungry-ghost realm, or the animal realm. He brings them to rest in the boundless hall of blessing, and, following after, continues to instruct them, fearing that, dwelling in blessing, they might grow proud and reckless, and, indulging a wicked heart, fall back again into the three lower paths; the calamity that even glory and rank can bring, and the changing nature of impermanence, suffering, and emptiness, are held up as warnings against this. He urges them instead to take up what is unconditioned, just as that loving mother's own mind of care and protection would have them do. "Contemplating sixteen matters, one attains a single-pointed mind and absorption. What are these sixteen? Knowing for oneself whether the breath is long or short; knowing for oneself when the breath causes the body to move; knowing for oneself when the breath is subtle or pronounced; knowing for oneself when the breath is comfortable or uncomfortable; knowing for oneself when the breath stops or races; knowing for oneself when the breath is joyful or sorrowful; reflecting that all things are impermanent, and knowing this through the breath itself; knowing that what has passed cannot be pursued or regained, and knowing this through the breath; holding no thought within, casting aside all reflection, and knowing this through the breath; releasing attachment to one's own body and life, or not yet releasing it, and knowing this through the breath. The practitioner of the Way, reflecting deeply, understands: where this is present, that follows; where this is absent, that does not follow. All that is born must inevitably meet the affliction of old age and death; if the spirit is not extinguished, it simply receives another body. Without birth, there is no old age; without old age, there is no death. Reflecting on this, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. The practitioner of the Way, observing the world's birth and death with his own eyes, does so only through the twelve links of dependent origination; reflecting on this, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. "The practitioner of the Way observes his own bodily form through five matters: first, that the countenance changes repeatedly; second, that pain and pleasure shift repeatedly; third, that one's own resolve and intention turn repeatedly; fourth, that the bodily form itself changes repeatedly; fifth, that good and evil alternate repeatedly. These are the five matters. Their repeated change is like flowing water, one moment following upon the last; reflecting on this, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. How should the practitioner of the Way contemplate meditation itself? Seeing a dead person from head to foot, examining and reflecting on this closely, holding it fixed within the mind — whether walking, sitting, lying down, rising, eating, or engaged in any of the ten thousand tasks — one should hold this constantly in mind, so as to make one's resolve firm, and attain absorption freely according to what one contemplates. It is like a person cooking several bushels of rice who wishes to know whether it is fully cooked: he need only take a single grain, press it between his fingers, and examine it — if that one grain is cooked, it is clear that the rest are cooked as well. The resolve for the Way is just the same: the mind's wandering is like flowing water, and the practitioner of the Way, holding to a single object of reflection, brings the mind to rest and the intention to purity, and the true Way of the arhat, of liberation, may thereby be attained. "Can the first absorption alone allow one to attain the state of an arhat? Some do attain it, and some do not. What conduct attains it, and what conduct fails? Within the first absorption itself, if any trace of thought or craving remains, the Way cannot be accomplished. Heaven and earth are impermanent, and emptiness itself is hard to hold onto secure; exhausting every inner defilement, free of any thought of craving or attachment, with one's resolve pure in just this way, the state of a true arhat may be attained. From the second through to the fourth, one should hold the mind exactly as in the first absorption. If one's resolve rests only in the first absorption without yet attaining arhatship, then, when life ends, one may still advance: rising to the seven heavens to receive a lifespan of one kalpa; dying while in the second absorption, one rises to the eleven heavens to receive a lifespan of two kalpas; dying while in the third, one rises to the fifteen heavens to receive a lifespan of eight kalpas; dying while in the fourth, one rises to the nineteen heavens to receive a lifespan of sixteen kalpas. "The practitioner of the Way, observing his own body's inward impurity, sees that it is entirely unclean: hair, skin, skull, flesh, blinking eyes, tears and spittle, sinews and vessels, flesh and marrow, liver and lungs, intestines and stomach, heart and gallbladder, spleen and kidneys, feces, urine, pus, and blood — the coming together of these many impurities is what makes up a human being. It is like a sack filled with the five grains: whoever has eyes may empty the sack and examine each grain separately, each one distinct from the rest. The wise, observing their own body within in this way, recognize that the various elements each have their own name, and that there is, in truth, no fixed 'person' at all; through this observation free of desire, one comes to see the fundamental emptiness, and, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. "The practitioner of the Way, deeply observing the body's separate four elements — earth, water, fire, and wind — recognizes: hair, bones, teeth, skin, flesh, and the five internal organs are the earth element. Tears, spittle, pus, blood, sweat, fat, marrow, brain, and urine are the water element. The body's own inner warmth, which governs the digestion of food, is the fire element. The breath's own inhalation and exhalation is the wind element. It is like a butcher slaughtering an animal, cutting it apart into its four separate portions, knowing each in full detail. The practitioner of the Way, observing within and distinguishing the four elements in this way — this is earth, that is water, and fire and wind likewise — recognizes that there is, in truth, no fixed 'person' at all; reflecting on this, his resolve stilled, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained. The practitioner of the Way, aware for himself of the breath's length, speed, and subtlety, distinguishing each of these clearly, is like a person carving something who knows for himself how deep his cut goes; reflecting on the breath in this way, with this single-pointed thought, absorption is attained." This is how the bodhisattva, with a single-pointed mind, practiced the boundless perfection of meditative concentration.

By far the longest single story in the entire collection, essentially a manual of contemplative technique rather than a narrative — cataloguing more than a dozen distinct objects of meditation (the elderly, the sick, the decomposing corpse, the impermanence of harvests, the sixteen stages of mindfulness of breathing, the anatomical contemplation of the body's parts, the four elements) each explicitly said to lead to single-pointed absorption on its own. Its scope and technical density set it apart from every other story in this collection, closer to a meditation manual embedded within the narrative frame than to a jātaka tale proper.

77. The Prince Who Saw an Old Man, a Sick Man, and a Corpse〔太子得禪〕

Chinese Source

太子出遊，王勅國內無令眾穢當彼王道。太子出城，第二天帝化為老人，當其車前，頭白背僂，倚杖羸步。太子曰：「斯人何乎？」御使對曰：「老人矣！」「何謂為老？」曰：「四大根熟，餘命無幾。」太子曰：「吾後亦當老乎？」對曰：「自古有老，無聖免茲。」太子曰：「吾謂尊榮與凡有異，而俱不免，榮何益己？」還宮存之，一心得禪。王問僕曰：「太子出遊觀國喜乎？」對曰：「道觀老叜，存世非常，心不為欣。」王懼去國，重益樂人，惑之以榮華，亂之以眾音，欲壞其道意令守尊位也。後復出遊，王重勅曰：「無令羸老在道側也。」前釋復化為病人，體疲氣微，肉盡骨立，惡露塗身，倚在門側。曰：「斯復何人？」對曰：「病人也。」曰：「何謂為病？」「飲食不節，臥起無常，故獲斯病，或愈或死。」曰：「吾亦飲食不節，臥起無常，當更病乎？」對曰：「有身即病，無免斯患。」太子曰：「吾不免患，後必如之。」還宮存之，一心入禪。後出，帝釋復化為死人，舁擔建旐，哀慟塞路。曰：「斯復何人？」對曰：「死人。」「何謂為死？」「命終神遷，形骸分散，長與親離，痛夫難處。」太子曰：「吾亦然乎？」對曰：「上聖之純德，無免斯患。」迴車還宮，一心入禪。後復出遊，之王田廬，坐樹下。覩耕犁者，反土蟲出，或傷或死，鳥追食之。心中愴然，長歎曰：「咄眾生擾擾，痛焉難處。」念之悵如，一心入禪。時，日盛出照太子身，樹為低枝不令日炙。王尋所之，遙覩無上聖德之靈，悲喜交集，不識投身，稽首為禮，太子亦俱稽首于地。父子辭畢，王還于宮，太子一心入禪。菩薩禪度無極一心如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

The crown prince went out to travel, and the king commanded that nothing unclean within the kingdom should appear along the prince's own road. As the prince left the city, the second heavenly Śakra transformed himself into an old man, standing before his carriage, his hair white, his back bent, leaning on a staff and walking feebly. The prince asked, "What manner of person is this?" His charioteer answered: "This is an old man." "What is meant by old age?" He answered: "The four elements of the body have grown fully ripe, and little life remains to him." The prince said, "Shall I too become old one day?" He answered: "Since ancient times all have grown old — no sage has ever escaped this." The prince said: "I had thought that those of exalted rank were different from ordinary people, and yet none can escape this — what benefit, then, is such glory to oneself?" Returning to the palace, he reflected on this, and with this single-pointed thought, absorption was attained. The king asked his attendant: "Did the prince enjoy his journey through the kingdom?" He answered: "He observed an old man along the way, and, reflecting on the impermanence of the world, his heart found no joy in it." The king, fearing the prince might leave the kingdom, increased still further the number of musicians and performers, hoping to bewitch him with glory and confuse him with music, wishing to break his resolve for the Way and keep him bound to his exalted rank instead. Later, the prince went out once more, and the king again commanded: "Let no feeble old person appear along the road." The same Śakra as before then transformed himself into a sick man, his body exhausted and his breath weak, his flesh wasted to the bone, foul discharge smeared upon his body, leaning against a gate. The prince asked, "What manner of person is this?" He answered, "A sick man." He asked, "What is meant by sickness?" "Through immoderate eating and drinking, and irregular rest and rising, one contracts such illness as this, whether one then recovers or dies." The prince said: "I too eat and drink without moderation, and rest and rise without regularity — must I too fall ill in the same way?" He answered: "Whoever has a body is subject to illness — no one escapes this affliction." The prince said: "If I cannot escape this affliction, I too must one day become like this." Returning to the palace, he reflected on this, and entered absorption with a single-pointed mind. Later, going out again, Śakra transformed himself once more, this time into a dead man, borne on a bier with mourning banners raised, the road filled with the sound of grief. The prince asked, "What manner of person is this?" He answered, "A dead man." "What is meant by death?" "When life ends, the spirit departs, the body and its members scatter apart, and one is parted forever from one's own loved ones — a pain most difficult to bear." The prince asked, "Shall I too become like this?" He answered: "Even the purest virtue of the highest sages cannot escape this affliction." Turning his carriage back to the palace, he entered absorption with a single-pointed mind. Later, going out once more, he came to the king's own fields and farmhouses, and sat beneath a tree. Seeing a plowman turning the soil, insects were exposed, some wounded, some killed, and birds came to devour them. His heart was struck with sorrow, and he sighed at length: "Alas, living beings are caught in such turmoil — how painful and difficult their circumstance is!" Reflecting on this with a heavy heart, he entered absorption with a single-pointed mind. At that time the sun blazed down upon the prince's own body, and the tree lowered its branches to shield him from its heat. The king, seeking him out, saw from a distance this sign of unsurpassed sacred virtue, and, overcome with mingled sorrow and joy, threw himself down and bowed his head in reverence, unable to contain himself; the prince, too, bowed his head to the ground in return. 79. The Night of Enlightenment Beneath the Bodhi Tree〔太子得禪〕

Chinese Source

太子未得道時，取地槀草，於樹下叉手正坐，棄眾垢念，清其心，一其志。自念曰：「今日為始，肌筋枯腐，於此不得佛者，吾終不起。」菩薩即得一禪，二三至四禪，即於一夜得一術闍，知無數劫父母兄弟妻子九族。二夜之中得二術闍，自知無數劫貧富貴賤長短白黑，眾生心中有念無念，得無不知。三夜之中得三術闍，三毒都滅。夜向明時，佛道成矣。深自思曰：「吾今得佛，甚深甚深難知難了，微中之微、妙中之妙也。今佛道成得無不知。」起至龍水所，龍名文隣，文隣所處，水邊有樹，佛坐樹下曰：「昔者錠光佛授吾尊決，當為釋迦文佛，真如所聞，吾今得佛矣。自無數劫來，布施、持戒、忍辱、精進、禪定、明度，積功之願，始今得極尊，作善福歸，不亡我功。」佛適念之，便入禪度無極。佛在水邊，光明徹照龍所居處。龍覩光影，鱗甲皆起。龍甞見三佛：拘婁秦佛、拘那鋡牟尼佛、迦葉佛，三佛得道，皆在此坐。明悉照龍所居，龍覩光明念曰：「斯光與前三佛光影齊同，世間得無復有佛乎？」龍大歡喜，出水左右顧視，覩佛坐樹下，身有三十二相，紫磨金色，光明奕奕過月踰日，相好端正如樹有華。龍前趣佛，頭面著地，遶佛七匝，身去佛四十里，以七頭覆佛上。龍喜作風雨七日七夕，佛端坐不動不搖不喘不息，七日不食得佛，心喜都無有想。龍大歡喜，亦七日不食無飢渴念，七日畢風雨止，佛禪覺悟。龍化為梵志，年少鮮服，長跪叉手，稽首問曰：「得無寒無熱無飢無渴，功福會聚，眾毒不加，處世為佛，三界特尊，豈不快哉？」佛告龍曰：「過去諸佛經說，眾生離三惡道得為人快，處世閑居守道志快，昔者所聞今皆獲快，處世懷慈不害眾生快，天魔重毒皆歇快，惔怕無欲不慕榮快，於世得道為天人師，志空、不願、無相之定。眾欲之有身，還神於本無，長存之寂，永與苦絕，斯無上之快矣。」龍稽首言：「自今以後，自歸佛歸法。」佛告龍：「方有眾聖，其誓應儀欲除饉苦，亦當豫自歸之。」龍曰：「諾。」自歸除饉眾。畜生之中歸佛先化，斯龍為首。菩薩禪度無極一心如是。

Before the prince had yet attained the Way, he gathered dried grass from the ground and, beneath a tree, sat upright with hands joined, casting aside every defiled thought, purifying his mind and unifying his resolve. He made this vow to himself: "From this day forward, though my very sinews and flesh should wither and decay, if I do not attain buddhahood here, I will never rise from this seat." The bodhisattva then attained the first absorption, and progressed through the second and third to the fourth, and in that single night attained the first watch of insight, knowing the countless kalpas of his own past parents, brothers, wife, children, and nine classes of relatives. In the second watch of that night, he attained the second insight, knowing for himself, across countless kalpas, poverty and wealth, honor and lowliness, long and short life, fair and dark complexion, and every thought, present or absent, in the minds of living beings, missing nothing. In the third watch of that night, he attained the third insight, and the three poisons were entirely extinguished. As the night drew toward dawn, the Way of buddhahood was accomplished. Reflecting deeply on this, he thought: "I have now attained buddhahood — how profound, how truly profound this is, how hard to know and hard to fully grasp, the subtlest of the subtle, the most wondrous of the wondrous. He rose and went to the dwelling place of a dragon named Mucilinda; where Mucilinda dwelt, beside the water, there stood a tree, and the Buddha sat beneath it, saying: "Long ago the Buddha Dīpaṃkara bestowed upon me his own honored prediction, that I would become the Buddha Śākyamuni — exactly as I once heard it then, so I have now attained buddhahood. Just as the Buddha reflected on this, he entered the boundless perfection of meditative concentration. The dragon, filled with joy, emerged from the water and looked about, and saw the Buddha seated beneath the tree, his body bearing the thirty-two marks, the color of refined purple gold, his radiance shining brighter than the moon and outshining even the sun, his fine features perfectly formed like a tree in full blossom. The dragon came before the Buddha, touched his forehead to the ground, and circled the Buddha seven times; then, though its own body extended forty leagues from the Buddha, it spread its seven heads to cover him. The dragon, in its joy, raised wind and rain for seven days and seven nights, while the Buddha sat perfectly still, unmoving, unshaking, without even the sound of breath, and, having gone seven days without food, attained buddhahood, his heart joyful and entirely free of thought. The dragon, likewise overjoyed, went seven days without food and felt no hunger or thirst. When the seven days were complete, the wind and rain ceased, and the Buddha awoke from his meditation. The dragon then transformed itself into a young brahmin, dressed in fine, fresh robes, and, kneeling with hands joined, bowed his head and asked: "Are you now free of cold, free of heat, free of hunger, free of thirst? Your merit and blessing have come together, and no poison can now touch you; dwelling in this world as a buddha, honored uniquely above the three realms — is this not truly a joy?" The Buddha said to the dragon: "The scriptures of the buddhas of the past teach that living beings, once free of the three lower paths and able to become human, find this joyful; dwelling in the world in quiet retreat and holding to the resolve for the Way is joyful; what I once only heard of, I have now attained in full, and this is joyful; dwelling in the world holding compassion and harming no living being is joyful; every heavy poison of the heavenly demons has now been dispelled, and this is joyful; calm, tranquil, and free of desire, no longer longing for worldly glory, is joyful; attaining the Way in this world to become the teacher of gods and men, resolved in emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness, is joyful. The very possession of a body bound up in craving and desire is returned instead to the spirit's own original, formless nature, abiding forever in stillness, forever cut off from suffering — this is the unsurpassed joy of all." The dragon bowed his head and said: "From this day forward, I take refuge in the Buddha and in the Dharma." The Buddha said to the dragon: "There will yet be many sages who, vowing to become arhats, wish to remove the suffering of hunger, and you too should take refuge in them in advance." The dragon said, "I will." He took refuge, of his own accord, in the community of monks. Among all the animal realm, this dragon was the very first to take refuge in the Buddha's teaching." 80. Pukkusa and the Storm the Buddha Never Heard〔佛得禪〕

Chinese Source

The World-Honored One said: "Long ago, when I dwelt beneath a simple thatched roof in the county of Ātumā, sitting in reflection on the root of birth and death, a violent storm arose, with hail, thunder, and lightning, which killed four fine oxen and two brothers who had been plowing. Very many people of that county gathered to see this. I, at that time, came out to walk in meditation, and a man approached me; I asked him: 'What is the crowd gathered to see?' He told me exactly what had happened. He then asked: 'Where was the Buddha at the time?' I answered, 'Alone beneath my own roof.' He asked, 'Were you asleep at the time?' I said, 'No.' He said: 'How could you be awake and yet not hear it? Your resolve for the Way is profound indeed. From this day forward, I wish to serve the World-Honored One as my teacher, honor the five pure precepts, and become a devout lay follower, holding to the truth for the rest of my life.'" Pukkusa, hearing this, felt his heart open and his doubts dissolve, his joy beyond measure, and, turning to his attendants, instructed them: "Within my storehouse there are a thousand robes woven of gold thread — select the finest of them and bring them to me, for I wish to offer them to the Buddha." His attendants, following this command, returned home to fetch them, and Pukkusa, with his own hands, draped the robe over the Buddha's body, then withdrew and bowed his head, saying: "I beg only that the World-Honored One might, from this day forward, deign to grace with your sacred presence the devout lay followers of my own region, and also honor my own household with a visit, that my entire clan, high and low, may each personally attend to your needs and make offerings to the Buddha. Though one might spend an entire lifetime, with the utmost reverent heart, honoring gods, dragons, spirits, and every flying, crawling, and wriggling creature, this would still not equal feeding a single monk for even one day — how much more so the unsurpassed, true and right Buddha himself? I beg you to extend your vast compassion and grant me this boundless blessing." The World-Honored One said, "Very good." This is how the bodhisattva, with a single-pointed resolve for the Way, practiced the boundless perfection of meditative concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

A story built entirely around demonstrating the depth of the Buddha's own meditative absorption through a specific, verifiable external test — five hundred carts passing unnoticed — rather than through any of this collection's more common narrative devices of trial, sacrifice, or transformation; the nested anecdote, in which the Buddha caps Pukkusa's own story about his teacher with an even more extreme example from his own past, is a rare instance of the Buddha directly outdoing a disciple's praise rather than simply receiving it.

81. The Bodhisattva Ever-Weeping, Who Wandered in Search of a Teacher〔常悲菩薩本生〕

Chinese Source

眾祐自說，為菩薩時，名曰常悲。常悲菩薩，常流淚且行。時世無佛，經典悉盡，不覩沙門賢聖之眾，常思覩佛聞經妙旨。時世穢濁，背正向邪，華偽趣利猶蛾之樂火；四等六度永康之宅，而世廢佛斯法，就彼危禍，以自破碎也，故為愁荒哀慟且行。往昔有佛名影法無穢如來王，滅度來久，經法都盡。常悲菩薩，夢見其佛為其說法云：「慎無貢高，學士之行，去心恩愛之垢，無著六情之塵勞，無遺眾愛毛髮之大。藏爾心內，諸念寂滅是為無為。」菩薩從佛聞斯法，猶餓夫得甘食，其喜無量，心垢除，入淨定。即棄家捐妻子，入深山處閑寂，以山水果蓏自供，處山舉手椎心哀號而云：「吾生怨乎！不值佛世，不聞佛經，十方現在至真世尊，洞視徹聽，皆一切知，恍惚髣髴，暉靡不之，願現尊靈，令吾覩佛，得聞弘摸大道極趣。」哀聲適訖，天神下曰：「明士乃爾，莫復哀號，佛有大法，名明度無極之明。過去諸佛，今現在、甫當來，皆由斯成，爾必索之誦習其文，懷識其義奉而行之，爾必得四無所畏、十種力、十八不共。身色紫金，項光無際。十方經道，爾為明主，眾聖之尊、天人之師，應儀各佛所無有也。」常悲菩薩仰視報曰：「當由誰聞斯尊法乎？以何方便之何國土？厥師族名？」天人報曰：「爾自斯正東行，無念色痛想行識，無念苦樂善惡、耳目鼻口身心吾我，及人往世所更、來世之事，無念地水火風空，青黃白黑都及眾色，貪淫瞋恚，愚癡嫉妬，男女九族，左右前後高下遲疾。無念有佛無佛、有經道無經道、有賢聖無賢聖，空爾意、絕眾願。爾之執心無違吾教，今覩明度無極聖典。」常悲菩薩仰曰：「敬諾，終始戢之。」天人重曰：「精進存之。」言竟忽然不現。菩薩受教，端心內淨，東行索之。數日即止，深自思曰：「吾宿薄祐，生不值佛，世無沙門，君臣憒憒，無知佛者。明度無極、除冥尊師，去斯幾里，未覩之頃。」心中悲猛，舉哀而行，精誠之至感於諸佛。上方佛來，飛在其前，身色紫金，相好絕聖，面若滿月，項有日光，諸天翼從，寶帳華蓋，作樂散華，叉手垂首。佛歎菩薩曰：「善哉善哉！爾之快健，覩世希有。」菩薩見佛，且喜且悲，稽首而曰：「願佛哀我，斷我繫、解吾結、開吾盲、愈吾病，為吾說經。」佛告之曰：「三界皆空，夫有悉無，萬物若幻，一生一滅，猶若水泡，覩世皆然。爾其思之，吾為爾說經，端心諦聽，慎無忘也。自是東行二萬里，有國名揵陀越，諸菩薩城也，一國之內皆是上士無凡庸人，欲為說諸菩薩之德，劫數已盡，其德有餘。至尊上德菩薩名法來，於彼諸聖猶星有月，懷諸經典，其明無限，敷演明度無極之經，反覆教人，諸菩薩有受經者、誦者、書者、定經原者，爾往見焉，必為爾師。勸爾索佛，疾馳就之，自當為爾說內外明度無極景德。」常悲菩薩聞佛歎彼菩薩名德，心入法喜，得現在定，眾想都寂，悉覩諸佛為己說明度無極之德，歎己精進索佛之勳，僉曰：「善哉！求佛之志爾為得之。吾於往昔始發意時亦皆然也。已逝、甫來、現在諸佛，皆如爾索矣。爾必得佛，濟一切生也。」常悲菩薩從定寤，左右顧視不復覩諸佛，即復心悲流淚且云：「諸佛靈耀自何所來？今逝焉如？」菩薩禪度無極一心如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Blessed One himself related that, when he was a bodhisattva, he was named Sadāprarudita, "Ever-Weeping." The bodhisattva Ever-Weeping wept constantly as he traveled. At that time, the world was without a buddha, its scriptures entirely exhausted, and no community of monks or worthy sages could be found anywhere; he constantly longed to behold a buddha and hear the profound meaning of the scriptures. The age was corrupt and defiled, turning its back on truth to follow wickedness, chasing false glamour and profit as moths are drawn to flame; though the dwelling place of lasting peace lay in the four immeasurable virtues and the six perfections, the age had abandoned the Buddha and this very teaching, rushing instead toward danger and disaster, breaking itself apart in the process — and so he traveled on, mournful and grieving. Long ago there had been a buddha named the Tathāgata King of Stainless Reflected Dharma, whose extinction had occurred long ago, and whose scriptures and teachings were now entirely exhausted. The bodhisattva Ever-Weeping dreamed he saw this very buddha teaching him the Dharma, saying: "Take care never to be arrogant. The conduct of a true student is to remove the defilement of attachment and affection from the mind, to hold no attachment to the dust and toil of the six senses, and to leave behind not even the smallest hair's breadth of craving. Store this within your own mind, and let every thought within you come to rest — this is the unconditioned." The bodhisattva, hearing this teaching from the buddha in his dream, was like a starving man given sweet food, his joy beyond measure; the defilements of his mind were removed, and he entered a state of pure concentration. He at once left his household, gave up his wife and children, and entered deep into the mountains to dwell in quiet solitude, sustaining himself on the mountain's water and fruit. Dwelling there, he raised his hands, struck his own chest, and cried out in mournful lament: "How resentful is my own life! Not to have encountered the age of a buddha, not to have heard the Buddha's own scriptures — the World-Honored Ones of true reality, present now in the ten directions, who see and hear without obstruction, knowing all things, however faint or distant, their radiance reaching everywhere — I beg you to manifest your sacred presence, and let me behold a buddha, that I may hear the vast model of the great Way in its fullest sense." As soon as his mournful cry ended, a heavenly spirit descended and said: "A man of such understanding as you need not cry out in grief any longer. The Buddha holds a great teaching, called the boundless perfection of wisdom. The buddhas of the past, those present now, and those yet to come, all attain their accomplishment through this very teaching. You must seek it out, recite and study its text, hold its meaning in mind, and practice it — and you will surely attain the four kinds of fearlessness, the ten powers, and the eighteen unique qualities of a buddha. Your body will become the color of purple gold, and a boundless light will shine from your neck. Throughout the scriptures and teachings of the ten directions, you will become their bright master, the most honored of all sages, the teacher of gods and men — a distinction no arhat or any other buddha possesses." The bodhisattva Ever-Weeping, looking up, answered: "From whom shall I hear this honored teaching? By what skillful means, and in what land? What is the name and lineage of this teacher?" The heavenly being answered: "Travel due east from here, without any thought of form, sensation, perception, mental formation, or consciousness; without any thought of suffering or pleasure, good or evil, ear, eye, nose, mouth, body, mind, or self; without any thought of what a person has experienced in past lives or what is yet to come in future ones; without any thought of earth, water, fire, wind, or space, of blue, yellow, white, black, or any color at all; without craving, lust, anger, delusion, envy; without any thought of male or female, of the nine classes of kin, of left or right, before or behind, high or low, slow or swift. Without any thought of whether a buddha exists or not, whether scriptures and the Way exist or not, whether the worthy and the sagely exist or not; empty your own intention, and cut off every desire and wish. If your resolve holds fast without departing from this teaching, you will now behold the sacred canon of the boundless perfection of wisdom." The bodhisattva Ever-Weeping, looking up, said: "I receive this respectfully, and will hold to it from beginning to end." The heavenly being said further: "Hold to this with diligence." Having said this, he vanished at once. The bodhisattva, receiving this instruction, made his mind upright and pure within, and traveled eastward in search of this teaching. "After several days he stopped, and reflected deeply to himself: "My past fortune was thin, and I was born without ever encountering a buddha; the world holds no community of monks, and ruler and ministers alike are confused and know nothing of the Buddha. The teacher of the boundless perfection of wisdom, who dispels darkness — how many more leagues until I reach him? I have not yet caught even a glimpse of him." His heart filled with fierce sorrow, he raised his voice in mourning as he traveled on, and the depth of his utmost sincerity moved the buddhas themselves. A buddha from the upper realms came, flying before him, his body the color of purple gold, his fine features surpassing every sage, his face like the full moon, a solar radiance at his neck, escorted by gods, beneath jeweled canopies and flowered parasols, music playing and flowers scattering, hands joined and heads bowed low. The buddha praised the bodhisattva, saying: "Excellent, excellent! Your vigor and vitality are a rarity such as the world has seldom seen." The bodhisattva, seeing the buddha, was filled with mingled joy and sorrow, and, bowing his head, said: "I beg the Buddha to take pity on me, to sever my bonds, to loosen my knots, to open my blindness, to heal my sickness, and to expound the scripture for my sake." The Buddha told him: "The three realms are all empty; what exists is, in truth, nothing at all; the ten thousand things are like illusions, one arising and one perishing, like a bubble on water — this is how it is throughout the world, without exception. Reflect on this well, and I will expound the scripture for you; make your mind upright, listen carefully, and take care never to forget it. From here, travel east twenty thousand leagues, and you will find a country called Gandhavatī, the city of the bodhisattvas, where every person within that entire land is of the highest order, with no ordinary or common people at all. I wish to speak to you of the virtue of these many bodhisattvas: though the number of kalpas allotted to them has already passed, their virtue still remains in abundance. The most honored and virtuous bodhisattva among them, named Dharmodgata, is like the moon among the stars compared to these many sages, holding within him every scripture, his brightness without limit, expounding the sūtra of the boundless perfection of wisdom, teaching people again and again. Among the bodhisattvas there, some receive the scripture, some recite it, some copy it out, and some determine its fundamental principles — go to him, and you will surely find him to be your own teacher. He will urge you to seek the Buddha, and you should hurry swiftly to him, for he alone can expound for you the full inner and outer glory and virtue of the boundless perfection of wisdom." The bodhisattva Ever-Weeping, hearing the Buddha's own praise of this bodhisattva's name and virtue, entered into the joy of the Dharma, attained a concentration fixed in the present moment, and every thought entirely stilled; he beheld all the buddhas expounding for him the virtue of the boundless perfection of wisdom, praising his own diligence in seeking the Buddha, and they all said together: "Excellent! Your resolve to seek the Buddha, you have now attained it. In ages past, when we ourselves first gave rise to this very resolve, we were all exactly as you are now. The buddhas of the past, those yet to come, and those present now — all sought in just the way you now seek. You shall surely attain buddhahood, and deliver every living being." The bodhisattva Ever-Weeping, waking from his concentration, looked all about him but could no longer see any of the buddhas, and, his heart again filled with sorrow, wept and said: "From where did the buddhas' own sacred radiance come, and where has it now gone?" This is how the bodhisattva, with a single-pointed mind, practiced the boundless perfection of meditative concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This is this collection's own version of the story of Sadāprarudita, "Ever-Weeping," found in the Perfection of Wisdom literature more broadly — a bodhisattva whose entire practice consists of grief-driven searching for a teaching that has vanished from the world, resolved only through an extended, almost liturgical list of things not to think about, delivered by a heavenly being as the price of admission to the teaching itself. The story's own ending, in which the vision dissolves and Ever-Weeping is left weeping once more, refuses the kind of clean resolution most of this collection's stories provide.

82. The Two Ascetics Who Held the Sun Hostage Over a Grudge〔那賴梵志本生〕

Chinese Source

昔有兩菩薩，志清行淨，內寂無欲，表如天金，去穢濁之群，處山澤，鑿石為室。閑居靖志，菅衣草席，食果飲泉，清淨無為，志若虛空，四禪備悉，得五通智：一能徹視無遐不覩，二能洞聽無微不聞，三能騰飛出入無間，四能通知十方眾生心中所念，五能自知無數劫來宿命所更。梵釋仙聖，諸天龍鬼，靡不稽首。處山澤六十餘年，悲念眾生展轉愚冥，不覩為惡後有重殃，約情棄欲敬奉三尊，福至響應必獲其榮。二梵志者：一名題耆羅，二名那賴。題耆夜興誦經，疲極臥出，那賴時亦誦經，誤蹈題耆羅首，題耆即興而曰：『誰蹈吾首者，明旦日出一竿，破爾之首為七分善乎！』那賴曰：『誤蹈爾耳，祝誓何重？瓦器不行之類，尚有相觸，豈況於人共處，終年而不誤失乎？爾言常誠，明旦日出，吾首必為七分矣。吾當制日不令其出。』遂爾不出。五日之間，舉國幽冥，炬燭相尋，眾官不修，君民惶惑，會群寮、請道士。王曰：『日之不出，其咎安在？』道士之中有五通者曰：『山中道士，兩有微諍，故制日令不出耳。』王曰：『其諍有緣乎？』道士具以本未為王說之。王曰：『奈之何？』答曰：『王率群寮民無巨細，馳詣于彼稽首和解，彼必慈和。』王即有詔如道士令，詣于山澤叩頭曰：『國豐民寧，二尊之潤，而今不和，率土失所。其咎在我，黎民無過，願赦之。』那賴曰：『王勤曉彼意，彼意解者、吾放日矣。』王之題耆羅所，宣那賴旨。王即曰：『令彼以泥塗其首放日。』泥首即破為七分，那賴無為。王臣黎民靡不欣懌，兩道士為王廣陳治國，當以四等無蓋之慈，勸奉五戒載十善而行。王及臣民，僉然受戒。王還國有詔曰：『人無尊卑，帶五戒十善經，以為國政。』自斯之後，王潤逮草木，忠臣誠且清讓，父法母儀室家各尚，守道貞信，家有孝子。眾祐曰：『兩菩薩覩其國主不知三尊，臣民憒憒邪見自蔽，猶冥中閉目行。愍其徒死不覩佛經，故為斯變，欲其覩明也。』」佛告諸比丘：「那賴者，吾身是。題耆羅者，彌勒是。菩薩禪度無極一心如是。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Long ago there were two bodhisattvas, of pure resolve and clean conduct, inwardly still and free of desire, their outward bearing like heavenly gold, departed from the wicked crowd, dwelling among the mountains and marshes, where they had carved a dwelling out of stone. Living there in quiet retreat, their resolve stilled, wearing garments of grass and mats of straw, sustaining themselves on fruit and spring water, pure and unconditioned, their resolve as vast as empty space, they had perfected all four absorptions, and attained the wisdom of the five spiritual powers: first, the ability to see clearly without limit, missing nothing however distant; second, the ability to hear clearly, missing nothing however faint; third, the ability to fly and pass through solid matter without obstruction; fourth, the ability to know directly the thoughts held in the minds of living beings throughout the ten directions; fifth, the ability to know for themselves their own past lives across countless kalpas. Brahmā, Śakra, immortal sages, and every heavenly dragon and spirit bowed their heads before them without exception. Dwelling among these mountains and marshes for more than sixty years, they grieved for living beings turning endlessly in their own foolish confusion, unaware that committing evil brings grave calamity afterward; restraining their own passions and giving up desire, honoring the Three Jewels, they knew that blessing arrives as swiftly as an echo answers a sound, and that this would surely bring its own glory in time. Of these two ascetics, one was named Tiqiluo, and the other was named Nalai. Tiqiluo, reciting scriptures late into the night, grew exhausted and lay down to rest; Nalai, also reciting scriptures at that time, by accident trod upon Tiqiluo's own head. Tiqiluo rose at once and said: "Who has trodden upon my head? Tomorrow morning, as soon as the sun's first pole-length of light appears, may your head be split into seven pieces — would that not be fitting!" Nalai said: "It was only an accident that I trod on you — why make such a grave curse of it? Even lifeless vessels of clay may accidentally strike one another — how much more so when people live together, over the course of an entire year, without a single mishap? If your word is truly to be trusted, then tomorrow, as soon as the sun rises, my own head must surely split into seven pieces. I shall simply prevent the sun from rising at all." And so he did, and the sun did not rise. "Within five days, the entire kingdom was plunged into darkness, torches and lamps sought out on every side, government offices left unattended, and ruler and people alike thrown into confusion and dread; the assembled officials gathered, and ascetics were summoned for counsel. The king asked: "What is the cause of the sun's failure to rise?" Among the ascetics summoned, one who possessed the five spiritual powers said: "Two ascetics in the mountains hold a slight grievance against one another, and this is why the sun has been prevented from rising." The king asked, "Is there some particular cause for this dispute?" The ascetic told the king the whole matter from beginning to end. The king asked, "What is to be done?" He answered: "Let the king lead his officials and people, high and low, to go there together, bow their heads, and seek reconciliation — they will surely be moved to kindness and harmony." The king then issued a decree exactly as the ascetic advised, and, going to the mountains and marshes, bowed his head and said: "The kingdom's prosperity and the people's peace are due to the grace of you two honored ones, and now that you are no longer in harmony, the whole land has lost its proper order. The fault is mine alone; the people are without blame — I beg you to forgive them." Nalai said: "If the king can persuade him and resolve his grievance, I will release the sun at once." The king went to Tiqiluo and conveyed Nalai's own message. The king then said: "Let him smear mud upon his own head before releasing the sun." As the mud-covered head was struck by the first light, it split at once into seven pieces, while Nalai himself remained unharmed. The king, his ministers, and the people all rejoiced without exception, and the two ascetics gave the king extensive counsel on governing his kingdom, urging him to practice the boundless compassion of the four immeasurable virtues, and to honor the five precepts and carry out the ten virtues. The king, together with his ministers and people, all received the precepts together. Returning to his kingdom, the king issued a decree: "Let no person, of whatever rank, fail to hold the scripture of the five precepts and the ten virtues as the very governance of this land." From that time on, the king's own grace reached even to the grass and trees; loyal ministers were sincere and yielding, fathers followed the proper way and mothers their own proper conduct, each household honoring its own duties, holding fast to the Way with chastity and trust, and every family had its own filial children. The Blessed One said: "The two bodhisattvas, seeing that the ruler of that kingdom did not know the Three Jewels, and that his ministers and people, confused and beset by wrong views, walked as though with closed eyes in darkness, grieved that this multitude, doomed to die without ever seeing the Buddha's own scriptures, brought about this very transformation, that they might come to see the light." The Buddha said to the monks: "Nalai was I myself. Tiqiluo was Maitreya." This is how the bodhisattva, with a single-pointed mind, practiced the boundless perfection of meditative concentration.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

This chapter's final story turns on an unusually petty originating grievance — one ascetic accidentally treading on another's head during nighttime recitation — escalated, through a literalized curse and its literal-minded countermeasure, into a kingdom-wide crisis requiring royal intervention. The story's own closing explanation, that the two ascetics staged this entire crisis deliberately to shock a spiritually blind kingdom into awareness, reframes what reads at first as an ordinary personal conflict as an intentional, orchestrated teaching device.
