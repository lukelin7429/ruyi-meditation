Kāśyapaparivarta.

One of the oldest surviving Mahāyāna sūtras — quoted in Nāgārjuna's own circle, preserved in Sanskrit, Tibetan, and four separate Chinese translations, teaching a "middle way of true observation" that treats emptiness itself as something never to be seized as one more fixed view. The Buddha teaches it directly to his disciple Mahākāśyapa, moving from catalogues of bodhisattva conduct through a great chain of similes to the sūtra's philosophical core, then closes with a parable of five hundred confused monks awakened by a monk the Buddha conjures for the purpose. This collection translates the whole text across nine pages.

How to read this collection

This is a mid-length sūtra by the standards of this site — roughly 11,090 characters, close in scale to the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra — but it has no chapter divisions of its own the way that sūtra does. Instead it moves through long, unbroken runs of parallel "four dharmas" lists, an extended chain of similes, a dense philosophical core on the middle way, and a closing narrative parable, so this collection splits it at natural topic boundaries rather than at even intervals, grouping the long catalogues together and giving the philosophically dense middle section and the closing parable more room of their own.

No commentary on this sūtra by any premodern figure survives in any tradition. The modern scholar-monk Yin Shun (印順, 1906–2005) lectured on this very text, but as a living author's work it remains under Taiwan copyright until 2055 per this site's commentator-vetting rule, and is not used here. This collection's reading notes are Ru-Yi Meditation Center's own, offered honestly as such.

The Sūtra's Own Structure

The Buddha's teaching to Mahākāśyapa moves through five distinct registers in turn: catalogues of bodhisattva conduct in matched "four dharmas" lists (Parts 1–3), a great chain of similes for bodhisattva virtue (Part 4), the sūtra's philosophical core on the middle way of true observation (Parts 5–6), a warning against monastic hypocrisy built around the true nature of the mind and the fourfold typology of śramaṇa (Parts 7–8), and a closing narrative parable of confusion resolved through paradox (Part 9).

Parts 1–3
Catalogues of the Bodhisattva Path
A long run of matched "four dharmas" lists — what loses and what gains prajñāpāramitā, what a true bodhisattva does and does not do — culminating in the Thirty-Two Dharmas that earn the name "bodhisattva" outright.
Parts 1–3 · complete
Part 4
The Elements, the Moon, the Lion, and the Lotus
A great chain of similes for bodhisattva virtue drawn from earth, water, fire, wind, the moon, the sun, the lion, the elephant, the lotus, and the rivers running to the sea.
Part 4 · complete
Parts 5–6
The Middle Way of True Observation
The sūtra's philosophical core: avoiding the extremes of existence and non-existence, self and non-self; dependent origination forward and reverse; and why emptiness itself must never become one more fixed view to cling to.
Parts 5–6 · complete
Parts 7–8
The True Nature of Mind, and Four Kinds of Śramaṇa
A physician treating according to the actual illness; an extended investigation into what the mind actually is; and a warning against monastic hypocrisy built around four kinds of śramaṇa, only the last of which is genuine.
Parts 7–8 · complete
Part 9
Five Hundred Monks, and a Monk the Buddha Conjures
Verses on what it truly means to keep the precepts, the parable of five hundred confused monks brought to awakening by paradox, and the sūtra's closing praise of itself.
