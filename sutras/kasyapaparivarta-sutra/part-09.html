Five Hundred Monks, and a Monk the Buddha Conjures

The sūtra closes with a narrative rather than another catalogue: verses on true precept-keeping bring eight hundred monks to liberation, but five hundred more flee in confusion — and the Buddha resolves it not by correcting them directly, but by conjuring a monk who meets them as a fellow traveler, and asking one disorienting question that undoes their whole assumption about what nirvāṇa actually is.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 9 of 9 complete Source: Taishō T0351 (tr. unknown, Jin dynasty) Reading notes: Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Verses on What It Truly Means to Keep the Precepts

Chinese Source

於是世尊，說此頌曰：

「持戒不有亦無垢，　持戒無憍而不倚；
持戒不闇無所縛，　持戒無塵無污穢；
究竟止息無上寂，　無想不想亦無穢；
諸慟眾倚一切斷，　是為迦葉持佛戒。

不著身口不倚命，　不貪一切受生死，
以正去來住正道，　是為迦葉持佛戒。

不著世間不倚世，　得明無闇無所有；
無有己想無他想，　斷一切想得清淨；
無此彼岸無中間，　於此彼岸亦不著；
無縛無詐無諸漏，　是為迦葉持佛戒。

謂名及色意不著，　禪定正念調御心；
無有吾我無我所，　是為迦葉稱住戒。

不倚禁戒得解脫，　不叩持戒為歡喜；
於此上求八正道，　是謂持戒清淨相。

不期持戒不依定，　謂修習此得智慧；
無有無得是聖性，　清淨聖戒佛所稱。

謂己身見心解脫，　我是我所終不起；
心能解空佛境界，　如是持戒莫能勝。

善住淨戒得禪定，　已獲禪定修智慧；
已修智慧便得脫，　已逮解脫平等戒。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Then the World-Honored One spoke this verse:

"Precept-keeping neither exists nor is stained;
precept-keeping has no pride, and leans on nothing;
precept-keeping is not obscured, bound by nothing;
precept-keeping is free of dust, free of every defilement;
ultimate stillness, unsurpassed quiescence;
free of thought, free of no-thought, and free of stain as well;
every sorrow and every reliance entirely cut off —
this, Kāśyapa, is keeping the Buddha's precept.

Not clinging to body or speech, not leaning on life itself,
not craving any rebirth one might receive,
going and coming rightly, abiding on the right path —
this, Kāśyapa, is keeping the Buddha's precept.

Not clinging to the world, not leaning on the world,
attaining light with no darkness, and nothing possessed at all;
no notion of self, no notion of other,
every notion cut off, purity attained;
no this shore, no far shore, no space between them,
and no clinging even to this shore or that;
no bondage, no falseness, no contamination at all —
this, Kāśyapa, is keeping the Buddha's precept.

Unattached in mind to name or to form,
meditative absorption, right mindfulness, a mind well-trained;
no "I" at all, and nothing that is "mine" —
this, Kāśyapa, is what deserves to be called abiding in the precept.

Not leaning on the precept itself to attain liberation,
not congratulating oneself for keeping the precept with joy,
and, above even this, seeking the eightfold noble path —
this is called the pure mark of precept-keeping.

Not hoping for anything from precept-keeping, not relying on meditative concentration,
cultivating this and thereby attaining wisdom;
having nothing, attaining nothing — this is the very nature of a noble sage;
this pure, noble precept is what the Buddha himself praises.

The view of a self in one's own body, the mind liberated from it,
"I" and "mine" never arising at all;
a mind able to understand emptiness — the Buddha's own domain —
precept-keeping such as this, nothing can ever surpass.

Well-abiding in pure precepts, one attains meditative absorption;
having attained meditative absorption, one cultivates wisdom;
having cultivated wisdom, one thereby attains liberation;
having reached liberation, one has attained the precept of perfect equality."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

These eight verses set Part 8's prose definition of true precept-keeping — neither the position nor its negation, applied item by item — to a form meant to be memorized and recited rather than reasoned through. The image of "no this shore, no far shore, no space between them, and no clinging even to this shore or that" reaches for the same crossing-metaphor this site's Diamond Sūtra uses in its own raft simile, but pushes further: the Diamond Sūtra's raft is set down once the far shore is reached, while this verse denies that "this shore" and "far shore" are even the right categories to begin with. The closing verse's own five-step sequence — precepts, then meditative absorption, then wisdom, then liberation, then "the precept of perfect equality" — names, without yet using its technical term, exactly the fivefold dharma-body the sūtra's own narrative will name explicitly a few lines later.

Five Hundred Confused Monks, and a Monk the Buddha Conjures

Chinese Source

說此偈已，八百比丘逮得漏盡，三萬二千人遠塵離垢諸法眼生。五百比丘昔已得定，聞佛說此甚深之法不能解了，從座起去。於是，大迦葉白世尊曰：「此五百比丘昔已得定，聞是深法不能解了，即便起去。」

世尊告大迦葉曰：「此五百比丘貢高慢故，不能解此無漏淨戒。是所說法甚深微妙，諸佛之道極甚深妙，非是未種善根與惡知識共相隨者所能解了。此五百比丘，昔迦葉如來興出世時，悉為異學弟子。聞迦葉如來說法時，計著有故，一聞說法心得歡喜。以是因緣，身壞命終，生忉利天。從彼命終還生人間，於我法中出家學道。此諸比丘為見所壞，聞是深法不能解了，今始造緣不復生於惡趣之中，此身終已當得滅度。」

於是，世尊告尊者須菩提曰：「汝去化彼五百比丘。」須菩提白佛言：「唯世尊！此五百比丘不受佛教，何況我耶？」

於是，世尊化作比丘在彼道中。五百比丘見已，往詣化比丘所，問化比丘曰：「諸賢！欲何所至？」化比丘曰：「欲詣山澤遊住安樂。所以者何？向聞世尊所可說法，我不能解了故。」即言：「諸賢！我等亦聞世尊說法，不能解了，而有恐怖，欲詣山澤遊住安樂。」

化比丘曰：「諸賢！且來當共誼計，莫得有諍，非沙門法。諸賢！稱說般泥洹者，為何等法般泥洹耶？是身中何者眾生？何者我人壽命？謂般泥洹，何所法盡便得般泥洹？」

五百比丘曰：「婬怒癡盡便得般泥洹。」化比丘問曰：「諸賢！有婬怒癡盡耶？而言此盡便得般泥洹。」

五百比丘答曰：「諸賢！婬怒癡者，不在於內而不在外，亦不在兩中間，亦非無思想而有也。」

化比丘曰：「是故諸賢！不當思想，亦莫反想。若不思想、不反想者，則無染不染。若無染不染者，是說息寂。諸賢當知，所有戒身，亦不生亦不般泥洹，定、慧、解脫、度知見身，亦不生亦不般泥洹。諸賢！因此五分法身說泥洹者，是法遠離、空無所有，無取、無斷。如是諸賢！云何可想般泥洹耶？是故諸賢！莫想於想，莫想於無想，亦莫斷想及與無想，若斷想無想者是為大緣。諸賢！若入想知滅定者，於是似有所作。」

說此法時，五百比丘諸漏永盡心得解脫。即詣佛所，稽首佛足，却坐一面。

Ru-Yi Translation

When he had spoken this verse, eight hundred monks attained the exhaustion of every contamination, and thirty-two thousand people, far removed from dust and defilement, gave rise to the pure Dharma-eye. Five hundred monks who had already attained meditative concentration in the past, hearing the Buddha speak this exceedingly profound teaching, were unable to understand it, and rose from their seats and departed. Then the Elder Mahākāśyapa said to the World-Honored One: "These five hundred monks, who had already attained meditative concentration in the past, on hearing this profound teaching, were unable to understand it, and at once rose and departed."

The World-Honored One said to Mahākāśyapa: "These five hundred monks, because of their pride, are unable to understand this uncontaminated, pure precept. This teaching that has been spoken is exceedingly profound and subtle; the path of every buddha is exceedingly profound and subtle indeed, and cannot be understood by those who have not yet planted roots of goodness and who keep company with bad companions. These five hundred monks, in ages past, when the Tathāgata Kāśyapa arose in the world, were all disciples of other, non-Buddhist schools. On hearing the Tathāgata Kāśyapa teach the Dharma, because they still clung to existence, they nonetheless, upon hearing that one teaching, felt joy in their hearts. By this very cause and condition, when their bodies perished and their lives ended, they were reborn among the gods of Trāyastriṃśa. When their lives there came to an end, they were reborn once more among humans, and in my own teaching they went forth to study the path. These monks, ruined by their own fixed views, are unable to understand this profound teaching — but from this occasion forward, they will no longer be reborn among the evil destinies, and once this present life ends, they will attain complete cessation."

Then the World-Honored One said to the Elder Subhūti: "Go and instruct those five hundred monks." Subhūti said to the Buddha: "But World-Honored One! These five hundred monks would not even accept the Buddha's own teaching — how much less would they accept mine?"

Then the World-Honored One conjured a monk, who appeared on the road ahead of them. The five hundred monks, seeing him, went up to this conjured monk and asked him: "Venerable ones — where are you headed?" The conjured monk said: "I wish to go dwell peacefully, roaming the mountain forests. Why is this? Because, having just heard the teaching the World-Honored One spoke, I was unable to understand it." They said at once: "Venerable ones! We too heard the World-Honored One's teaching and could not understand it, and, filled with fear, we likewise wish to go dwell peacefully, roaming the mountain forests."

The conjured monk said: "Venerable ones! Come, then, let us reason this through together — let there be no quarreling, for that is not the way of a śramaṇa. Venerable ones! When one speaks of attaining complete nirvāṇa, exactly which dharma is it that attains complete nirvāṇa? Within this body, which part is the being? Which part is the self, the person, the lifespan? As for attaining complete nirvāṇa — which dharma, once exhausted, actually yields complete nirvāṇa?"

The five hundred monks said: "Once lust, anger, and delusion are exhausted, one attains complete nirvāṇa." The conjured monk asked: "Venerable ones! Is there any real 'lust, anger, and delusion' to be exhausted at all, that you should say their exhaustion yields complete nirvāṇa?"

The five hundred monks answered: "Venerable ones! Lust, anger, and delusion are not within, and not without, and not anywhere between the two — nor, without any thought at all, do they exist."

The conjured monk said: "Therefore, venerable ones, one should neither hold to thought nor turn against thought. If one neither holds to thought nor turns against it, then there is neither defilement nor non-defilement. If there is neither defilement nor non-defilement, this is what is called true stillness and quiescence. Venerable ones, you should know: the precept-body itself neither arises nor attains complete nirvāṇa; the bodies of concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge-and-vision of liberation likewise neither arise nor attain complete nirvāṇa. Venerable ones! When 'nirvāṇa' is spoken of in terms of this fivefold dharma-body, this dharma is itself remote, empty, without anything at all — nothing to grasp, and nothing to cut off. So then, venerable ones, how could one form any notion at all of attaining complete nirvāṇa? Therefore, venerable ones, do not fix your thought upon thought, and do not fix your thought upon no-thought either; and do not cut off thought, or cut off no-thought, either — for to cut off thought and no-thought is itself a great conditioning cause. Venerable ones! If one enters the concentration of the cessation of thought and perception, this too only resembles having accomplished something."

When this teaching was spoken, the five hundred monks' every contamination was forever exhausted, and their minds attained liberation. They went at once to where the Buddha was, bowed their heads to the Buddha's feet, and withdrew to sit to one side.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Subhūti's own answer to being asked to help — "these five hundred monks would not even accept the Buddha's own teaching, how much less mine" — is itself worth sitting with as a piece of teaching in its own right, a senior disciple declining to overestimate his own authority relative to a peer's evident failure. The Buddha's actual solution is more striking still: rather than sending anyone with greater authority, he conjures a monk who presents himself as sharing the five hundred monks' own confusion and fear exactly, meeting them as a fellow traveler rather than a corrective voice from above. Only once they have recognized him as one of their own does he ask the single question that dismantles their entire premise: not "how do you attain nirvāṇa," which would have let them recite an answer already memorized, but "which dharma, exactly, is it that attains it" — a question that exposes, before any doctrine is offered, that they had never actually identified what they thought they were trying to extinguish.

The five hundred monks' own answer — that lust, anger, and delusion are "not within, not without, not in between, nor without thought" — already shows real sophistication; they are not naive, and their confusion is not ignorance of the words. What resolves it is the conjured monk's explicit refusal to let their own correct analysis calcify into one more fixed position: "do not fix your thought upon thought, and do not fix your thought upon no-thought either... do not cut off thought and no-thought, either." This is the identical structure Parts 5, 7, and 8 have each used before — neither the extreme nor its negation — but delivered here as a direct liberating instruction inside a story, rather than as doctrine stated for its own sake. The "fivefold dharma-body" named explicitly in this passage — precept, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge-and-vision of liberation — is the same fivefold structure the closing verse of the previous section had already traced without naming it.

Subhūti's Catechism With the Now-Awakened Monks

Chinese Source

於是，尊者須菩提問諸比丘曰：「諸賢，向去何所？今從何來？」諸比丘曰：「尊者須菩提！佛所說法無去無來。」

須菩提復問諸賢：「師為是誰？」諸比丘曰：「謂不生、不滅是。」須菩提復問：「云何知法？」答曰：「無縛、無解。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！云何解脫？」答曰：「無明滅而明生也。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！誰弟子耶？」答曰：「謂如是得如是正智。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！何時當滅度耶？」答曰：「如來所化般泥洹。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！所作已辦耶？」答曰：「吾我所作悉皆已斷。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！誰同梵行？」答曰：「不行三界。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！結已盡耶？」答曰：「諸法至竟滅。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！降伏魔耶？」答曰：「諸陰不可得。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！順尊教耶？」答曰：「無身、口、意。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！清淨福田耶？」答曰：「無受亦無所受。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！度生死耶？」答曰：「無常無斷。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！向福田地耶？」答曰：「一切諸著悉已解脫。」須菩提復問：「諸賢！趣何所耶？」答曰：「隨如來之所化也。」

如是，尊者須菩提問，五百比丘答。時彼大眾聞已，八百比丘諸漏永盡心得解脫，三萬二千人遠塵離垢諸法眼生。於是，尊者須菩提白世尊曰：「甚奇、甚特！此寶嚴經，饒益發起趣摩訶衍諸族姓子、族姓女。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Then the Elder Subhūti asked the monks: "Venerable ones, where were you going just now? And where have you come from?" The monks said: "Elder Subhūti! In the Dharma the Buddha teaches, there is no going and no coming."

Subhūti asked the venerable ones further: "Then who is your teacher?" They answered: "That which is unborn and unceasing — that is our teacher." Subhūti asked further: "How do you know the Dharma?" They answered: "By neither bondage nor liberation." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! What is liberation?" They answered: "Ignorance ceasing, and light arising." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Whose disciples are you?" They answered: "Whoever attains exactly this attains exactly this right wisdom — that is whose disciples we are." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! When will you attain complete cessation?" They answered: "Whenever the Tathāgata's own conjured form attains complete nirvāṇa." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Is what had to be done, done?" They answered: "Whatever 'I' and 'mine' might do has already been entirely cut off." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Who shares your holy conduct?" They answered: "Whoever does not act within the three realms." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Are your fetters exhausted?" They answered: "All dharmas are, in the end, ceased." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Have you subdued Māra?" They answered: "The aggregates cannot be found at all." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Do you comply with the honored teaching?" They answered: "There is no body, no speech, no mind." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Are you a pure field of merit?" They answered: "There is no receiving, and nothing received." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Have you crossed beyond birth-and-death?" They answered: "Neither permanent, nor cut off." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Are you headed toward the ground of merit?" They answered: "Every attachment there is has already been liberated." Subhūti asked further: "Venerable ones! Where are you headed?" They answered: "Wherever the Tathāgata's own transformative power leads."

Thus did the Elder Subhūti ask, and the five hundred monks answer. When the whole assembly heard this exchange, eight hundred monks' every contamination was forever exhausted, their minds attaining liberation, and thirty-two thousand people, far removed from dust and defilement, gave rise to the pure Dharma-eye. Then the Elder Subhūti said to the World-Honored One: "How extraordinary, how rare! This Ratnakūṭa sūtra brings such great benefit, arousing every son and daughter of good family who sets out toward the Mahāyāna."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Fourteen ordinary questions about a monk's status and progress — where are you going, who is your teacher, are your fetters exhausted, have you subdued Māra — each receive an answer that declines to confirm or deny the question's own premise. This is the same technique this site's Vimalakīrti Sūtra is famous for pushing to its furthest extreme in its own "thunderous silence" on non-duality, and the same move this site's Sumatidārikā-paripṛcchā uses when Sumati answers Mañjuśrī's question about what she "abides in" by denying there is anything to abide in at all. Here the device is run fourteen times in a row as a formal catechism rather than a single dramatic exchange, and the cumulative effect is different from either: no single answer carries the weight of the whole teaching, and the repetition itself demonstrates that the same non-committal structure applies uniformly, whatever ordinary question is put to it.

One answer stands out for its dry, exact humor: asked when they will attain complete cessation, the monks reply, "whenever the Tathāgata's own conjured form attains complete nirvāṇa" — pointing back at the very monk who taught them, a being with no more independent existence to extinguish than the illusion-similes of Part 5 already established for the mind itself. The monks' own realization, staged through a teacher who was never a real person to begin with, undercuts in advance any reading of their attainment as the acquisition of some new, real possession.

The Sūtra's Own Praise, and the Merit of Upholding It

Chinese Source

須菩提復問世尊：「諸族姓子、族姓女，說此寶嚴經者，得幾所福？」

世尊答曰：「若族姓子、族姓女，說此《寶嚴經》教授他人，書寫經卷在所著處，是為天上天下最妙塔寺。若從法師聞受持讀誦，書寫經卷者，當敬法師為如如來。若敬法師供養奉持者，我記彼人必得無上正真道，命終之時要見如來，是人當得十種身清淨。云何為十？一者，死時歡喜無厭；二者，眼目不亂；三者，手不擾亂；四者，心不擾亂；五者，身不煩擾；六者，不失大小不淨；七者，心不污穢；八者，心不錯亂；九者，手不摸空；十者，隨其坐命終。是謂十種身清淨也！

「復次迦葉！當得十種口清淨。云何為十？一者，善音；二者，軟音；三者，樂音；四者，愛音；五者，柔和音；六者，無礙音；七者，敬音；八者，受音；九者，天所受音；十者，佛所受音。是謂十種口清淨也！

「復次迦葉！當得十種意清淨。云何為十？一者，無恚不怒他人；二者，無恨不語；三者，不求短；四者，無結縛；五者，無顛倒想；六者，心無懈怠；七者，戒不放逸；八者，意樂布施歡喜受；九者，離貢高慢；十者，得三昧定，獲一切佛法。是為十種意清淨也！

「復次迦葉！若有恒沙國土滿中七寶，供養如恒沙等諸佛、如來、等正覺及弟子眾，如恒沙劫一切施安，至般泥洹後起七寶塔；不如是族姓子、族姓女聞此寶嚴經，受持諷誦為他人說，不誹謗也。若有女人說此經者，是女人終不墮惡趣，亦不復受女人身也。

「復次迦葉！若有族姓，欲以一切珍妙供養此經典者，當受持讀誦書寫經卷為他人說，是為供養此經典已。若有受持諷誦書寫為人說者，則為供養諸佛如來。」

佛說此經時，尊者大迦葉，一切天、龍、鬼神、世間人民，聞佛說已，歡喜奉行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Subhūti asked the World-Honored One further: "What merit does a son or daughter of good family gain by teaching this Ratnakūṭa sūtra?"

The World-Honored One answered: "If a son or daughter of good family teaches this Ratnakūṭa sūtra to others, and writes out its scroll wherever they may be, that place becomes the very finest stūpa and temple in all the heavens and on earth. If one hears it from a Dharma-teacher and upholds, recites, and copies out its scroll, one should revere that teacher exactly as one would the Tathāgata himself. If one who reveres, makes offerings to, and upholds such a teacher does so, I predict of that person that they will certainly attain the unsurpassed, true path, and, at the moment of death, will surely behold the Tathāgata — and this person will attain ten kinds of bodily purity. What are the ten? First, joy without any weariness at the moment of death; second, eyes that remain undisturbed; third, hands that remain undisturbed; fourth, a mind that remains undisturbed; fifth, a body that remains untroubled; sixth, no loss of control over the body's own impurities; seventh, a mind that remains unstained; eighth, a mind that remains undisordered; ninth, hands that do not grope at empty air; tenth, dying seated exactly as one wishes. These are the ten kinds of bodily purity.

"Furthermore, Kāśyapa! One will attain ten kinds of purity of speech. What are the ten? First, a wholesome voice; second, a gentle voice; third, a pleasing voice; fourth, an endearing voice; fifth, a soft and harmonious voice; sixth, an unobstructed voice; seventh, a respectful voice; eighth, a voice that is well received; ninth, a voice the gods themselves receive; tenth, a voice the Buddha himself receives. These are the ten kinds of purity of speech.

"Furthermore, Kāśyapa! One will attain ten kinds of purity of mind. What are the ten? First, no anger, never becoming enraged at another; second, no resentment, and no speaking out of it; third, never seeking another's faults; fourth, no bondage; fifth, no inverted thinking; sixth, a mind free of laziness; seventh, precepts kept without negligence; eighth, delighting in giving and joyfully receiving it; ninth, freedom from pride; tenth, attaining meditative absorption, and gaining every buddha-dharma. These are the ten kinds of purity of mind.

"Furthermore, Kāśyapa! Suppose there were lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, every one filled with the seven treasures, and these were offered to buddhas, tathāgatas, fully and rightly awakened ones, and their disciples as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, giving them peace through every kind of offering for kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, and, after their complete nirvāṇa, raising a stūpa of the seven treasures for each — even this could not equal a son or daughter of good family who hears this Ratnakūṭa sūtra, upholds it, recites it, and explains it to others without ever slandering it. If a woman is the one who teaches this sūtra, that woman will never again fall into the evil destinies, nor will she ever again receive a woman's body.

"Furthermore, Kāśyapa! If any son or daughter of good family wishes to make offerings of every rare and wondrous thing to this scripture, they should uphold it, recite it, copy out its scroll, and explain it to others — this alone already constitutes making offerings to this very scripture. Whoever upholds, recites, copies, and explains it to others thereby makes offerings to every buddha, every tathāgata."

When the Buddha had finished speaking this sūtra, the Elder Mahākāśyapa, and every god, nāga, spirit, and person of the world, hearing what the Buddha had taught, rejoiced and carried it out with reverence.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

The sūtra names itself directly here — "this Ratnakūṭa sūtra" (此寶嚴經) — the same self-naming already met once in Part 4, now repeated at the close as the closing merit-of-upholding-this-text passage nearly every Mahāyāna sūtra ends with in some form. The specific promise that a woman who teaches this sūtra "will never again receive a woman's body" sits alongside two very different strategies this same site's collection has already translated for the identical underlying concern: the Śrīmālādevī Sūtra's queen receives her prediction of buddhahood explicitly as a woman, with no transformation of any kind, while the Sumatidārikā-paripṛcchā's heroine transforms her own body from woman to man as a deliberate demonstration that no fixed mark of gender was ever real to begin with. This sūtra's own promise reads as a third, more straightforward variant — rebirth in a body understood to carry fewer social obstacles, offered as an incentive rather than staged as a philosophical proof — and this site does not treat any of the three approaches as correcting or superseding the others; each belongs to the specific rhetorical work its own sūtra is doing at that moment.

The Kāśyapaparivarta Is Complete

This closes the ninth and final part, and with it the whole translation of the Kāśyapaparivarta — one of the oldest surviving Mahāyāna sūtras, moving from catalogues of bodhisattva conduct through a chain of similes into its own philosophical core on the middle way of true observation, an account of how a bodhisattva's own dharma arises from within affliction rather than apart from it, a warning against monastic hypocrisy built around four kinds of śramaṇa, and a closing narrative parable in which confusion is resolved not by authority but by a question that dissolves its own premise.
