# Kāśyapaparivarta — Translation Notes

These notes record recurring translation decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of the Kāśyapaparivarta ("The Chapter of Kāśyapa"), translated into Chinese under the title *Fo Shuo Mohe Yan Baoyan Jing* (佛說摩訶衍寶嚴經, "The Sūtra of the Mahāyāna Jewel-Heap, Spoken by the Buddha"), also called *Da Jiaye Pin* (大迦葉品, "The Great Chapter of Kāśyapa"), Taishō T0351, an anonymous translation dated to the Jin dynasty. They exist so that vocabulary stays consistent across all nine parts, and so that terminology without precedent anywhere else on this site is recorded for any later text to inherit rather than re-coin.

## The text, and why it matters

The Kāśyapaparivarta is one of the oldest surviving Mahāyāna sūtras, quoted or alluded to by Nāgārjuna's own circle and considered by modern scholarship among the earliest textual witnesses to a fully developed Mahāyāna teaching on the middle way between existence and non-existence — a teaching that predates, and likely shaped, the more systematic treatment Nāgārjuna gives the same subject in the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, already translated on this site. A Sanskrit manuscript survives and has been published in modern critical editions; the text also survives in Tibetan and in no fewer than four separate Chinese translations, a strong sign of how early and how widely it circulated. Those four Chinese versions are: T350 (遺日摩尼寶經, tr. Lokakṣema, 2nd century, the earliest and most archaic); T351 (this translation, tr. unknown, Jin dynasty); the version embedded as the forty-third assembly of the compiled *Ratnakūṭa Sūtra* (大寶積經, T310); and T352 (大迦葉問大寶積正法經, tr. Dānapāla, Song dynasty, the latest). This collection translates T351, chosen because it survives as its own complete, independent text rather than requiring extraction from the much larger compiled *Ratnakūṭa*, and because its Jin-dynasty Chinese is considerably more approachable than Lokakṣema's very early, highly compressed rendering.

The sūtra takes the form of the Buddha's direct teaching to his disciple Mahākāśyapa — hence its name — and moves through several distinct registers: long catalogues of "four dharmas" describing wholesome and unwholesome bodhisattva conduct; an extended chain of similes for bodhisattva virtue drawn from the four elements, the heavenly bodies, and ordinary life; the sūtra's philosophical core, a teaching on "the middle way of true observation" that treats emptiness itself as something never to be clung to as one more fixed position; a warning against monastic hypocrisy built around four kinds of śramaṇa, only the last of which is called genuine; and a closing narrative parable in which five hundure confused monks are brought to awakening by a monk the Buddha conjures for the purpose, followed by a dialogue-catechism between Subhuti and those same monks, now awakened, that plays with paradox in a way this site's readers of the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* will recognize immediately.

The base text runs to roughly 11,090 Chinese characters, translated across nine parts.

## Reading-note sourcing

No commentary on this sūtra by any premodern figure survives in any tradition this site has been able to locate. The modern scholar-monk Yin Shun (印順, 1906–2005) devoted one of his own lecture-records, *Baoji Jing Jiangji* (寶積經講記), specifically to this text — his own preliminary remarks there distinguish "the ancient *Ratnakūṭa*" (古寶積經, this text and its sibling translations) from the later compiled 120-fascicle *Ratnakūṭa* collection, confirming this text's identity as the object of his lecture. As a work by a scholar who died in 2005, however, it remains under Taiwan copyright until 2055 per this site's commentator-vetting rule, and is neither used nor cited here. This collection's reading notes are Ru-Yi Meditation Center's own, offered honestly as such.

## Structure

| Part | Base text covers | Chinese length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The nidāna; the four dharmas that lose and that gain prajñāpāramitā; forgetting and never forgetting the bodhisattva mind | 818 characters |
| 2 | Eight further sets of "four dharmas": crookednesses to remove, compliant marks, the four evils, the four wisdoms, deviations, the bodhisattva's path, bad friends, good friends | 759 characters |
| 3 | False bodhisattvas; true virtues; the four great treasuries; transcending every Māra; embracing all good; immeasurable merit — culminating in the Thirty-Two Dharmas of a true bodhisattva | 873 characters |
| 4 | The great chain of similes — earth, water, fire, wind, moon, sun, lion, elephant, lotus, tree, rivers — closing with the charge to study this sūtra and observe things truly | 801 characters |
| 5 | The middle way of true observation: avoiding the extremes of existence and non-existence, self and non-self; dependent origination forward and reverse; why emptiness itself must never become one more thing to cling to | 1,312 characters |
| 6 | How a bodhisattva arises out of affliction, not apart from it: the lotus in mud, the tree regrowing from its root, the prince born of a servant woman — every bodhisattva a true child of the Buddha | 876 characters |
| 7 | The physician who treats according to the actual illness; the classical factors of awakening as medicine; the extended investigation into the true nature of mind | 1,370 characters |
| 8 | Monks who flee like dogs instead of looking inward; the bonds and afflictions of a renunciant's life; the four kinds of śramaṇa — appearance, false demeanor, reputation, and the real thing | 1,903 characters |
| 9 | Verses on what it truly means to keep the precepts; the parable of the five hundred monks and Subhuti's catechism with the Buddha's conjured monk; the sūtra's closing praise and the merit of upholding it | 2,378 characters |

Total: roughly 11,090 characters, split at natural topic boundaries rather than at even intervals — several of the sūtra's own long runs of parallel "four dharmas" lists are grouped together, while the philosophically dense middle section and the closing narrative parable are each given more room.

## Core terminology

Renderings are matched first to this site's existing Madhyamaka and Yogācāra pages wherever the same term already appears there; where this sūtra's own vocabulary has no site precedent, a rendering is coined fresh and recorded here.

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 般若波羅蜜 | prajñāpāramitā | Kept as a loanword, matching this site's *Middle Treatise* and *Perfection of Wisdom* pages. |
| 泥洹 / 般泥洹 | nirvāṇa / parinirvāṇa | 泥洹 is an archaic transliteration of nirvāṇa common in early Chinese translations (predating the later standard 涅槃); rendered simply as "nirvāṇa" throughout rather than preserving the archaism, since no site precedent distinguishes the two transliterations as different concepts. |
| 中道真實觀 | the middle way of true observation | Coined fresh for this collection; this sūtra's own name for its central teaching, distinct from — though a clear ancestor of — the "middle way" (中道) of this site's Madhyamaka texts. |
| 薩芸若 | sarvajñā, all-knowing wisdom | An archaic transliteration of sarvajña ("all-knowing"); rendered by sense rather than preserved as a transliteration, since the sūtra's own Chinese already glosses it as a quality of wisdom (智) in context. |
| 三十七品道寶 | the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment | Matched to this site's *Madhyāntavibhāga* pages, which already use this term for the same standard list (the four foundations of mindfulness, four right exertions, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of awakening, and the noble eightfold path). |
| 五分法身 | the fivefold dharma-body | Coined fresh for this collection: precept, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge-and-vision of liberation — the classical five components of an arhat's or buddha's "body" understood as accomplishment rather than physical form. |
| 化生 | rebirth by transformation | Matched to this site's existing usage (e.g. the *Śrīmālādevī Sūtra*'s discussion of the four modes of birth), for apparitional rebirth without gestation. |
| 摩尼珠 / 摩尼寶 | the wish-fulfilling jewel / a maṇi jewel | Matched to this site's *Treatise on Buddha Nature*, which already uses this rendering at length. |
| 沙門 | śramaṇa | Kept as a loanword; this sūtra's own fourfold typology of śramaṇa (appearance, false demeanor, reputation, and the real thing) is one of its most quoted passages. |
| 化比丘 | the conjured monk | Coined fresh for this collection: a monk manifested by the Buddha's own power for the sole purpose of teaching the five hundred confused monks in Part 9, paralleling this sūtra's own earlier account of a mind-made body's unobstructed movement. |

## What this text contributes to the site's Madhyamaka corpus

This sūtra's central teaching — that true observation avoids the paired extremes of existence and non-existence, permanence and annihilation, self and non-self, and above all treats emptiness itself as something never to be seized as one further fixed view — anticipates, in narrative and homiletic form, the systematic argument this site's *Middle Treatise*, *Twelve Gate Treatise*, and *Hundred Treatise* make in verse and commentary. Where this sūtra teaches through simile, direct address to Mahākāśyapa, and a closing narrative parable, those later Madhyamaka treatises make the identical refusal — of both being and non-being — into a formal method. Readers who know this site's Madhyamaka texts will recognize the same underlying commitment appearing here in its earliest attested Mahāyāna form.
