Fascicle One: The Introduction and the Narration of the Sūtra

The Textual Commentary on the Golden Light Sūtra (T1785) records Zhiyi's line-by-line lectures on Dharmakṣema's four-fascicle Golden Light Sūtra, written down by his disciple Guanding; it is the companion of the Profound Meaning of the Golden Light Sūtra, which treats the title. This first fascicle opens by dividing the sūtra into introduction, main teaching, and propagation, rejecting the divisions of earlier masters and of Paramārtha, and arguing that the three sections cannot be sealed off from one another. It then comments on the opening words — "Thus," "I heard," "At one time," "the Buddha," "dwelling," Rājagṛha, Gṛdhrakūṭa — usually first reporting older explanations, then Zhiyi's own reading by the four teachings, and finally a reading by "contemplating the mind." Most of the fascicle treats the sūtra's opening verses as a "narrating introduction" that previews the later chapters and the five profound meanings (name, essence, cardinal doctrine, function, classification of the teaching). The line listing the misfortunes of those who should hear the sūtra becomes a long excursus: each misfortune is traced to breaking one of the five precepts, and each precept is then read at the level of each teaching, ending with the "Dharma-gates" in which the perfect teaching's person kills, steals, loves, speaks falsely, and drinks only to free beings.

How the Sūtra Is Divided

Chinese Source

The Tripiṭaka master Paramārtha divides the new text into twenty-two chapters: the first chapter is the introduction, the nineteen chapters from "Lifespan" to "Giving Up the Body" are the main [teaching], and the last two chapters are the propagation. Paramārtha explains: "'Lifespan' clarifies the teacher's fruit; the two chapters 'Repentance' and 'Praise' clarify the disciples' cause; 'Predictions' is the disciples' fruit; 'Curing Illness' is the teacher's cause; from 'The Four Heavenly Kings' on [the text] properly discusses power and function. Since the earlier teacher's fruit and disciples' cause are the main [teaching], how can the later cause and fruit of teacher and disciples be the propagation?"

Ru-Yi Translation

Our master says: dividing the text is at root a matter of human sentiment. Human sentiment all holds that the introduction does not yet discuss the path, and that the propagation, petering out at the end, may not discuss the cause and fruit of teacher and disciples. This is not so. The three sections cannot be blocked off and cut apart. The introduction at root introduces the main [teaching] and the propagation, so the introduction has three senses; the main at root is the main for the introduction and the propagation, so the main too has three senses; the propagation at root propagates the main and the introduction, so the propagation too has three senses — because "its words are good at the beginning, in the middle, and at the end." Moreover, the faculties and natures by which beings attain the path are not fixed; how could the introduction be allowed to have no flavor and the propagation to peter out at the end? Since it does not peter out at the end, what harm would there be in its teaching the cause and fruit of teacher and disciples? Just as there is no harm in the main [teaching] being taught within the introduction. That the propagation contains the intent of the main [teaching] all the more urges on proclamation and practice, and does not go against the sūtra's intent. Further, the Lotus makes clear that the seer Asita is the teacher's cause, and the "Upholding" chapter gives the disciples predictions of their fruit, yet the masters all classify these as propagation. Here there is a precedent, and the meaning is not harmed. Granting or withholding depends on the person; there is no need to cling to it hard.

Now, from "Thus have I heard" into the "Lifespan" [chapter], down to where the gods and dragons gather in Faith-Mark Bodhisattva's chamber, is the introductory section; from "At that time the four buddhas" on, through the "Emptiness" chapter, is the section of the main teaching; from the "Four Heavenly Kings" chapter to the end of the sūtra is the propagation section. "Introduction" introduces the benefit about to come. "Main" means discussing the path precisely in accord with the capacities. "Propagation" (流通): 流 is named for pouring downward; 通 is named for not being blocked — wishing to make the water of the true Dharma pour from now into what is to come, so that the fish-trap and rabbit-snare of the sage's teaching are not blocked in future ages. The sūtra says "its words are good at the beginning, in the middle, and at the end"; this is just that meaning.

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone in doubt says: How can the introductory part enter into a main chapter? All sūtras are like this. For example, the Vimalakīrti has no introductory chapter, and its introduction lies within the main teaching; in the Larger [Prajñāpāramitā] the main teaching lies within the introductory chapter; in the Nirvāṇa the introductory part enters into the main chapters. All sūtras are like this and no one doubts them; why are you puzzled only by this? This was the intent of whoever produced the sūtra: because the four buddhas' resolving of the doubt arises abruptly and its text stands steeply apart, the introductory part was drawn into the "Lifespan" [chapter]. Now we follow what suits the meaning and may not divide evenly by chapters.

The Three Senses of "Introduction"

Chinese Source

序有三義：一次緒、二敘述、三發起。次緒者，居一部之初冠、眾說之首，故言次緒。敘述者，敘於方將、述於當益，故言敘述。發起者，發其信心、起於教也。一段經文含此三義，故題為序品。品者，梵語稱為跋渠，此為品也，品是類義。此中文句氣類相從，節之為跋渠。例如律中有篇聚、毘曇有犍度爾。從「如是」下，是次緒；從「是時如來」下，是敘述；從「其室自然廣博嚴事」下，是發起。

Ru-Yi Translation

The introduction has three senses: first, ordering; second, narrating; third, initiating. "Ordering": it stands at the head of the whole text, the cap and first of all that is taught; hence "ordering." "Narrating": it narrates what is about to come and recounts the benefit to come; hence "narrating." "Initiating": it brings forth their faith and initiates the teaching. One section of the sūtra's text contains these three senses, so it is titled the introductory chapter. "Chapter" (品): in Sanskrit it is called varga; here it is 品. 品 means "class": within it, phrases and sentences of like kind follow one another and are marked off as a varga, just as the Vinaya has its sections and groups and the Abhidharma its skandhakas. From "Thus" on is the ordering; from "At this time the Tathāgata" on is the narrating; from "his chamber spontaneously became broad and vast, adorned" on is the initiating.

The Ordering Introduction

次緒之序，舊云五事；地人開佛，是為六事。此經天龍集信相室，不聞前序、不聞後夢，亦得是同聞，亦得是非也。同聞眾少不次第（云云）。此之五六，亦名印定序，三世諸佛經，初皆安「如是」故。亦名通序，與諸經同。亦為通名，作本故。亦名經後序，結集者所置故。亦名經前序，遺囑令安故。亦名破邪序，對破外道阿漚故。亦名證信序，令聞者不疑故。天台師云：總此六說，都是四悉檀意也。諸佛諸經同，是世界悉檀也；經前經後為利來世，是為人悉檀也；對破外道，是對治悉檀也；信順無疑，是第一義悉檀也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The ordering introduction is said by the old [masters] to have five items; the Dilun masters split off "the Buddha," making six items. In this sūtra the gods and dragons gather in Faith-Mark's chamber; they did not hear the earlier introduction nor the later dream; so they may count as those who heard together, and also may not. The assembly that heard together is lacking and not given in order (and so on). These five or six are also called the introduction that seals and certifies, because the sūtras of all buddhas of the three times place "Thus" at the beginning. It is also called the general introduction, being the same as other sūtras. It is also [the basis of] the general name, because it serves as its basis. It is also called the introduction after the sūtra, because it was placed by the compilers. It is also called the introduction before the sūtra, because [the Buddha's] final instructions ordered it to be placed [there]. It is also called the introduction that refutes the false, because it counters and refutes the non-Buddhists' "a-u." It is also called the introduction that certifies faith, because it makes hearers free of doubt. The Master of Tiantai says: taken together, these six explanations are all the intent of the four siddhāntas. That all buddhas and all sūtras are the same is the mundane siddhānta; [placing it] before and after the sūtra to benefit future ages is the individual siddhānta; countering and refuting the non-Buddhists is the therapeutic siddhānta; believing, according, and having no doubt is the siddhānta of ultimate meaning.

"Thus"

Chinese Source

舊解如名不異，是曰無非。阿難所傳文句似瀉水分瓶，與佛一種，故不異稱如；文下之理允當無謬，故無非曰是。略而言之，文如理是。肇師云：「如是者，信順之辭也。信則所聞之理會，順則師資之道成。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The old explanation: not different is called "thus" (如); nothing wrong is called "so" (是). The phrases and sentences transmitted by Ānanda are like water poured from one vessel into another, the same as the Buddha's; hence not differing is called "thus." The principle beneath the words is fitting and without error; hence nothing wrong is called "so." Put briefly, the words are "thus" and the principle is "so." Master [Seng]zhao says: "'Thus' is a word of faith and accord. With faith, the principle heard is met; with accord, the way of teacher and disciple is accomplished."

真諦三藏云：「如是者，決定也。數決定、理決定。佛說此經有若干文句，若多成增謗、若少成減謗，阿難傳之如瀉水，不多不少，故數決定。佛說無相之理不有不無，若有墮增、若無墮減，阿難傳之無增無減，故理決定。」龍樹解如是者，「信順之辭也。信者言是事如是，不信者言是事不如是。」此之四解，各據一悉檀意。舊解語總不顯文詮何等理為如？何等理為文所詮為是？既不顯了，只是世界悉檀意；肇師據信順是為人；三藏文理決定為對治；龍樹信順如是為第一義（云云）。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tripiṭaka master Paramārtha says: "'Thus' means definiteness: definiteness of number and definiteness of principle. The Buddha taught this sūtra in a certain number of phrases and sentences; if more, it would become slander by addition; if fewer, slander by subtraction. Ānanda transmitted it like pouring water, neither more nor less; hence definiteness of number. The principle of no-mark that the Buddha taught is neither existent nor nonexistent; if [held as] existent one falls into addition, if nonexistent into subtraction. Ānanda transmitted it without addition or subtraction; hence definiteness of principle." Nāgārjuna explains "thus" as "a word of faith and accord. One who has faith says, 'This matter is thus'; one without faith says, 'This matter is not thus.'" These four explanations each rest on the intent of one siddhānta. The words of the old explanation are general and do not make clear what principle the text expresses that is "thus," or what principle expressed by the text is "so." Since it is not made clear, it is only the intent of the mundane siddhānta. Master Zhao, resting on faith and accord, is the individual [siddhānta]; the Tripiṭaka master's definiteness of text and principle is the therapeutic; Nāgārjuna's faith and accord, "thus," is the ultimate meaning (and so on).

Ru-Yi Translation

Now we give two explanations, general and particular. The Buddha taught in accord with the marks of dharmas, and Ānanda transmitted in accord with the marks of what he heard; hence "thus." The Buddha understood in accord with the marks of dharmas, and Ānanda received with an ocean-like capacity; hence "so" (and so on). The particular explanation: the non-Buddhists say "a-u" signifies auspiciousness, but their words go against their principle, so it is not "thus," and their principle differs from their words, so it is not "so"; one cannot see that, because "a-u" is at the beginning, the middle and end are all auspicious. [Here] the words are like their principle, hence "thus"; the principle is like the words, hence "so." Now we say: the Tripiṭaka sūtras begin with "thus," but the two truths are each different, so it is not "thus," and the principle is shallow, so it is not "so." In the Mahāyāna the two truths are identical to each other, hence "thus"; the principle is deep, hence "so." Now we say: the three [kinds of] people hear together yet each understands differently, so it is not "thus"; their realization and entry are superior and inferior, so it is not "so." Only what the bodhisattva hears is "thus"; only what the bodhisattva reaches is "so." Now we say: words that clarify the middle apart from the extremes are not "thus"; a middle way outside the two truths is not "so." The nature of written words is apart [from marks] — this is "thus," hence "thus"; all dharmas are the Buddha-dharma — this is called "so."

初破邪明正，即三藏經如是。次破異明同，即通教經如是。次破淺明深，即別教經如是。次破離明中，即圓教經如是。此經既是方等，通被根性不同，作種種說無咎。觀心解者，觀與境冥故為如，境即正觀故為是。經言「如此觀者即是正觀，若他觀者名為邪觀。」即其義也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first refutes the false to clarify the correct: this is the "thus" of the Tripiṭaka sūtras. The next refutes difference to clarify sameness: the "thus" of the shared-teaching sūtras. The next refutes the shallow to clarify the deep: the "thus" of the distinct-teaching sūtras. The next refutes separation to clarify the middle: the "thus" of the perfect-teaching sūtras. Since this sūtra is a vaipulya [sūtra] that reaches in common people of differing faculties and natures, there is no fault in giving all kinds of explanations. The explanation by contemplation of mind: contemplation merges with its object, hence "thus"; the object is itself correct contemplation, hence "so." A sūtra says, "Contemplating thus is correct contemplation; contemplating otherwise is called wrong contemplation." This is just its meaning.

"I Heard," "At One Time," "The Buddha"

Chinese Source

「我聞」者，舊云：阿難不師心，親承佛旨，故曰我聞。真諦曰：「我是器義。一散心名覆器，無聞慧故。二忘心名漏器，雖得而失，無思慧故。三倒心名穢器，非而謂是，無修慧故。阿難無三過，唯是善好器，親承有在，故言我聞。」《釋論》云「耳根不壞，聲在可聞處，作心諦聽，因緣和合故稱聞。阿難與聽眾述佛遺旨，親承不謬，故言我聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"I heard": the old [explanation] says that Ānanda did not take his own mind as teacher but personally received the Buddha's meaning; hence "I heard." Paramārtha says: "'I' means vessel. First, a scattered mind is called an overturned vessel, because it lacks the wisdom of hearing. Second, a forgetful mind is called a leaking vessel: though it obtains, it loses, because it lacks the wisdom of reflection. Third, an inverted mind is called a soiled vessel: it calls wrong right, because it lacks the wisdom of cultivation. Ānanda had none of these three faults; he was simply a good vessel, and his personal receiving had its basis; hence 'I heard.'" The Great Treatise says: "The ear faculty is unimpaired, the sound is within range of hearing, and one attends with the mind and listens carefully; because causes and conditions come together, it is called 'hearing.' Ānanda related to the listeners the meaning the Buddha bequeathed, personally received without error; hence 'I heard.'"

Ru-Yi Translation

Our master explains "I" as having four senses: I as I, I as no-I, no-I as I, and the true I in which I and no-I are not two; these are matched to people of four kinds of faculty (and so on). "Heard" also has four senses: hearing hearing, hearing not-hearing, not-hearing hearing, and not-hearing not-hearing, matched to the people of the dharmas of the four teachings (and so on). There are four kinds of Ānanda: Ānanda the Joyful, Ānanda the Worthy, Ānanda the Keeper of the Treasury, and Ānanda the Ocean — four names established for four kinds of conditions. Ānanda the Joyful is I as I, using hearing hearing: he personally received the buddha of the sixteen-foot body and upheld the Tripiṭaka dharma; hence "I as I, hearing hearing." Ānanda the Worthy is I as no-I, using hearing not-hearing: he personally received the buddha of the combined sixteen-foot and specially honored body and upheld the shared dharma; hence "I as no-I, hearing not-hearing." Ānanda the Keeper of the Treasury is no-I as I, using not-hearing hearing: he personally received the buddha of the specially honored body and upheld the distinct dharma; hence "no-I as I, not-hearing hearing." Ānanda the Ocean is I and no-I as not two, using not-hearing not-hearing: he personally received the Dharma-body buddha and upheld the perfect dharma; hence "I and no-I as not two, using not-hearing not-hearing." This sūtra is taught in common to the three vehicles; hearing the one voice, each understands differently; so it is necessary to distinguish (and so on). Explanation by contemplation: if one practices the contemplation that grasps the higher and loathes the lower, this is I as I, hearing hearing. If one practices either of the two kinds, analysis or embodying, of the contemplation of entering emptiness from the provisional, both are I as no-I, hearing not-hearing. If one practices the contemplation of emerging from emptiness into the provisional, it is no-I as I, not-hearing hearing. If one practices the contemplation of the middle way, it is the true I, not-hearing not-hearing.

問：釋大乘經，何得以五戒對義？答：開合五戒，大有所關。《提謂經》云「五戒者天地之大忌，上對五星、下配五岳、中成五藏。犯之者違天觸地，自伐其身也。」又對五常，不殺對仁、不盜對義、不淫對禮、不飲酒對智、不妄語對信。又對五經，不殺對《尚書》、不盜對《春秋》、不淫對《禮》、不妄語對《詩》、不飲酒對《易》。又對十善，殺盜淫是身三、妄語攝口四、飲酒攝意三。俗不能護口，略制一不妄語。《釋論》云「說重者是妄語」，則已攝三。飲酒是邪命自活、增益恚癡。出世以智慧為首，生死以三毒為根，若能禁酒，是防止意地三毒，長養出世智慧也。是為開五戒出十善。十善是舊法輪王所用，亦名性罪性善，都是一切罪之根本。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: In explaining a Mahāyāna sūtra, how can the five precepts be matched to the meaning? Answer: Opening up and combining the five precepts has great bearing. The Tiwei Sūtra says, "The five precepts are the great taboos of heaven and earth: above they correspond to the five planets, below they match the five sacred peaks, and in between they form the five organs. One who violates them goes against heaven and offends earth, attacking his own body." Again, they correspond to the five constants: not killing to humaneness, not stealing to righteousness, no lust to propriety, not drinking wine to wisdom, no false speech to trustworthiness. Again, they correspond to the Five Classics: not killing to the Book of Documents, not stealing to the Spring and Autumn Annals, no lust to the Rites, no false speech to the Odes, not drinking wine to the Changes. Again, they correspond to the ten wholesome [deeds]: killing, stealing, and lust are the three of body; false speech includes the four of speech; drinking wine includes the three of mind. Lay people cannot guard their speech [entirely], so in brief a single [precept of] no false speech is set. The Great Treatise says, "What is grave is called false speech"; so it already includes the [other] three. Drinking wine is living by wrong livelihood and increases anger and delusion. The world-transcending takes wisdom as its head; birth-and-death takes the three poisons as its root. If one can abstain from wine, this guards against the three poisons in the ground of mind and nourishes world-transcending wisdom. This is opening the five precepts to bring out the ten wholesome [deeds]. The ten wholesome [deeds] are what the wheel-turning kings of old used; they are also called offense by nature and good by nature, and they are the root of all offenses.

又五戒對五陰，不殺即色陰、不盜即受陰、不淫即想陰、不妄語即行陰、不飲酒即識陰。五陰開四念處，念處開三十七品，三十七品開三脫門，三脫門開涅槃，故云色能發戒、受受禪定、想慧悟虛通、行發解脫、識即知見。當知五戒能成五分法身，辦二乘之法也。又五戒亦是大乘法門，《提謂經》云「五戒是佩長生之符、不死之印」，即常德也。「出入無亂、往還無間」，即淨德也。「統御一身」，即我德也。「以立道根」，即樂德也。此是五戒對四德。束五戒為三業，即對三無失、三不護、三輪不思議化、三密、三軌、三身、三佛性、三般若、三涅槃、三智、三德等無量三法門，橫竪無邊際，與虛空法界等，亦是無盡藏法門，亦是無量義三昧。舉要言之，即是一切佛法也。◎

Ru-Yi Translation

Again, the five precepts correspond to the five aggregates: not killing is the aggregate of form; not stealing, the aggregate of feeling; no lust, the aggregate of perception; no false speech, the aggregate of formations; not drinking wine, the aggregate of consciousness. The five aggregates open into the four foundations of mindfulness; the foundations of mindfulness open into the thirty-seven factors; the thirty-seven factors open into the three gates of liberation; the three gates of liberation open into nirvāṇa. Hence it is said: form can give rise to precepts; feeling receives meditative concentration; perception is wisdom awakening to the empty and penetrating; formations give rise to liberation; consciousness is knowledge and vision. Know that the five precepts can accomplish the fivefold Dharma-body and achieve the dharmas of the two vehicles. Further, the five precepts are also a Mahāyāna Dharma-gate. The Tiwei Sūtra says, "The five precepts are wearing the talisman of long life and the seal of deathlessness": this is the virtue of permanence. "Going out and coming in without disorder, going and returning without interruption": this is the virtue of purity. "Governing the whole body": this is the virtue of self. "Thereby establishing the root of the path": this is the virtue of bliss. This is the five precepts corresponding to the four virtues. Gathering the five precepts into the three kinds of action, they correspond to the three without fault, the three needing no guarding, the inconceivable transformation of the three wheels, the three secrets, the three tracks, the three bodies, the three buddha-natures, the three prajñās, the three nirvāṇas, the three wisdoms, the three virtues, and immeasurable other threefold Dharma-gates, boundless horizontally and vertically, equal to empty space and the dharma-realm. This is also the Dharma-gate of the inexhaustible treasury, also the samādhi of immeasurable meanings. To state the essentials, it is all the Buddha-dharma.

Not Killing

Chinese Source

◎復次害命名事殺，不害命名事不殺。法門解者，析法名理殺，體法名理不殺。當知不殺之戒種種不同，論其果報亦復不同。若作意防護，如馬著勒、如牧牛執杖者，報在人道百二十年，唯得肉眼無有四眼。若任運性成如河注海者，報在六天，極長者九百二十六億七千萬歲，唯得天眼未得三眼。若加修客戒、無常苦無我等慧者，報在變易，壽七百阿僧祇，唯得慧眼未得二眼。若加修常無常等慧，報在蓮華藏海受法性身，分得五眼，分得常壽。比佛猶是諸根不具，壽命損減，況前諸根諸壽邪？若圓教人，持事不殺戒，又持理不殺戒，不壞於身而隨一相，不斷癡愛起於明脫，體陰界入無所毀傷，若子若果不生不滅，成就智慧居常寂光土，常壽湛然五眼具足，得根自在耳見眼聞，得命自在脩短自任，是則名為究竟持戒，諸根具足命不損減也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, harming life is called phenomenal killing; not harming life is called phenomenal not killing. Explained as Dharma-gates: analyzing dharmas is called killing in principle; embodying dharmas is called not killing in principle. Know that the precept of not killing is not of one kind, and its retributions also differ. If one guards it with deliberate intention, like a horse wearing a bit or an oxherd holding a staff, the retribution is in the human path, a hundred and twenty years, obtaining only the physical eye and not the four [other] eyes. If it is accomplished by nature spontaneously, like a river pouring into the sea, the retribution is in the six heavens, the longest being nine hundred twenty-six koṭis and seventy million years, obtaining only the heavenly eye and not the three [other] eyes. If one further cultivates the guest precepts and the wisdom of impermanence, suffering, no-self, and so on, the retribution is in [the birth-and-death of] change, a lifespan of seven hundred asaṃkhyeyas, obtaining only the wisdom-eye and not the two [other] eyes. If one further cultivates the wisdom of permanence and impermanence and so on, the retribution is in the Lotus Treasury Ocean, receiving a body of the dharma-nature, partially obtaining the five eyes and partially obtaining permanent life. Compared with the Buddha, this is still "faculties incomplete, lifespan diminished"; how much more the former faculties and lifespans! If a person of the perfect teaching upholds the phenomenal precept of not killing and also upholds the precept of not killing in principle — not destroying the body yet following the one mark, not cutting off delusion and craving yet giving rise to clarity and liberation, embodying the aggregates, realms, and entrances without damaging anything, whether seed or fruit, neither arising nor ceasing — he accomplishes wisdom and dwells in the land of eternal tranquil light, his life permanent, still and deep, the five eyes complete; attaining mastery of the faculties, he sees with the ears and hears with the eyes; attaining mastery of life, he is long-lived or short-lived at will. This is called ultimately upholding the precepts: the faculties complete and the lifespan not diminished.

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, why would the person of the perfect teaching merely uphold it? This precept is only killing, only kindness: he also does phenomenal killing and also killing in principle. Like King Xianyu, who killed five hundred brahmins, giving them the eye to see the Buddha and a lifespan of ten eons. Further, as for killing as a Dharma-gate: analyzing and sweeping away encumbrances and attachments and purifying all afflictions, like the tree spirit breaking off its branches so as not to receive the enemy bird, like the fire at the end of the eon burning wood so that ash and charcoal both vanish. Hence the Aṅgulimāla says, "I vow to cut off the aggregates, realms, and entrances; I cannot uphold the precept of not killing." All the dusts and toils are the seed of the Tathāgata; cutting off this seed completely is called Buddha, accomplishing the vajra-like, subtle and wondrous Dharma-body, still and deep, responding to all. Only killing, only kindness, he lets his form descend into the nine paths and, as fits them, shows a long or a short life; according to what they see, he uses lacking or complete faculties to transform and save them. The earlier practices of the precepts are shallow, near, narrow, and blocked; they are not the thoroughfare. The perfect precept is vast and far-reaching; the paths differ but meet as one; hence it is called ultimately upholding the precept of not killing. The precept of not killing, from humans and gods up to the Buddha, is vast in breadth and length; its meaning is like this. How can it be called a matter of the Lesser Vehicle?

Not Stealing

Chinese Source

復次不與取名事盜，與取名事不盜。法門解者，如佛言曰：「他物莫取，名法門不盜。菩提無與者而取菩提，是名法門盜。」不盜之戒種種不同，若持戒作業求可意果，可意果者無常速朽悉是他物，臭如糞果、害如毒食，有智之人所不應求，設使得之心不甘樂，云何殷勤飲苦食毒而自毀傷？貧窮四姓即此三界洄澓困苦，豈過有流？三障障佛，第一義天之所捨離，是名為盜，非不盜也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, taking what is not given is called phenomenal stealing; taking what is given is called phenomenal not stealing. Explained as Dharma-gates, as the Buddha said: "Not taking others' things is called not stealing as a Dharma-gate; taking bodhi, though no one gives bodhi, is called stealing as a Dharma-gate." The precept of not stealing is not of one kind. Suppose one upholds precepts and does deeds seeking agreeable fruits. Agreeable fruits are impermanent and quickly decay; all are others' things, foul as dung-fruit, harmful as poisoned food, what the wise should not seek; even if obtained, the mind takes no sweet delight in them. Why so earnestly drink bitterness and eat poison, harming oneself? "Poor and in hardship," the four castes — these are the whirlpools and hardship of the three realms; how do they go beyond the stream of existence? The three obstructions obstruct buddhahood, and one is abandoned by the heaven of ultimate meaning. This is called stealing, not not-stealing.

Like the woman Vasumitrā: humans see her as a human, goddesses see her as a goddess; those who see her obtain the samādhi of seeing all buddhas; those who hold her hand obtain the samādhi of reaching the buddha-lands; those who kiss her, the samādhi of utmost love; those who embrace her, the samādhi of merging in suchness; each obtains a Dharma-gate. Also like the bodhisattva who moves in Māra's realm without defiling the precepts, transforming into immeasurable bodies and keeping company with immeasurable heavenly maidens, causing them all to give rise to the resolve for bodhi. Ordinary, foolish people, amid the lower sufferings, perversely produce the notion of happiness; they raise the banner of arrogance and beat the drum of self-importance, saying form is the self, the self is form, there is self in form, there is form in self; clinging to existence they fight with nonexistence, clinging to nonexistence they fight with existence; relying on annihilation and permanence, they give rise to the sixty-two frivolous views; they destroy the wisdom-eye and do not see the true and real; they increase views and grow all wrongs; the poison of "I and mine" is extremely strong; they possess all four faults of speech — here briefly marked as false speech. The Thirty-three Heavens take the yellow leaves of birth-and-death to be true gold; [the heaven of] neither-perception, with subtle afflictions in its own ground, mistakenly reckons [itself] nirvāṇa. Not Drinking Wine

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, whether from grain or from grass, what darkens and confuses the mind is called phenomenal wine. Explained as Dharma-gates: delusion and inverted views are called wine. Inverted views are of many kinds. Wine is the root of everything unwholesome and evil; it can produce thirty-six kinds of faults and brings on five hundred lifetimes without hands; it [makes one] slight punishment, lose propriety, and expose what was hidden, sleep in filth, and blunder into water and fire. Such are its faults, yet people still esteem it. In the Jin era, drinking to the full was hailed as an art; at the Wei court, the clear and the turbid were [called] sages and worthies; Bi Zhuo styled himself a wine-follower; Zheng Quan vowed to become a wine jar. Bamboo and silk record them, past and present sing of them — doing what should not be done, singing what should not be sung. If this is not drunkenness on wine, what is?

Ru-Yi Translation

The Great Treatise says: "A Dharma master was teaching a king the offenses and blessings of the five precepts. The king objected: 'Drinking wine brings madness, and those who drink are very many; why are the mad so few?' The Dharma master merely raised his hand and pointed at the non-Buddhists, then spoke of other matters. The non-Buddhists, deceitfully boastful, said: 'The king's objection is very deep; this bald man on the high seat cannot answer it.' The king said: 'The Dharma master has already answered. The mad are not few; he pointed at you. You are just protected and not exposed.'" This is the drunkenness of worldly people.

The Textual Commentary on the Golden Light Sūtra, Fascicle One

Reading Notes for Fascicle 1

Translator's notes

Sūtra quotations. Every lemma from the Golden Light Sūtra uses the English of this site's translation of the sūtra (Part 1, Chapter 1 "Introduction" and Chapter 2 "Lifespan"). Where Zhiyi paraphrases a line ("close friends in internal strife," "the king's law applied from outside") he is not quoting, and the paraphrase is translated as such.

"Old text" and "new version." 舊本／舊文 is Dharmakṣema's four-fascicle, eighteen-chapter translation commented on here; 新本／新文 is Paramārtha's expanded twenty-two-chapter version (now lost), which added among others a chapter on the three bodies and the brahmin's teaching that the Dharma-body has no relics.

Introduction, main teaching, propagation. 序／正／流通 follow the threefold division used on the site's Lotus commentaries. The three senses of 序 — 次緒 ordering, 敘述 narrating, 發起 initiating — and the many names for the opening formula (印定序, 通序, 證信序 …) are translated literally.

"Thus" (如是). The commentary plays on 如 "thus/like" and 是 "so/right"; the Chinese characters are given in parentheses at first mention. The four readings are matched to the four siddhāntas (mundane / individual / therapeutic / ultimate meaning, as on the site's Fahua Xuanyi), then to the four teachings.

Quotations from the Great Treatise (T1509). The definition of hearing, the explanation of 王舍, the four immeasurables ("east … broad"), and the story of the king and the drunkards are from the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-upadeśa; "Whoever wants to measure the immeasurable Dharma …" (p2) is also there. Exact wording in Zhiyi is often a paraphrase.

Other quotations. "Riding the path of things as they really are …" is cited from the Satyasiddhi; this wording was located only in a Dunhuang Vimalakīrti commentary (T2772), not in T1646. "The Tathāgata comes from nowhere …" is the Diamond Sūtra (T0235). "Skillfully entering, leaving, and dwelling …" is Lotus ch. 15; "starving and emaciated …" is Lotus ch. 4 (verse). "His mind is pure, having passed beyond all dhyānas …" is the opening verse of the Vimalakīrti (T0475); "For people of overweening pride …" is Vimalakīrti ch. 7 (the goddess); "not entering the ranks of the assembly" is ch. 3 (Subhūti); "first he draws them with the hook of desire" is ch. 8; "entering the wine shops" and "the inner palace" are ch. 2. "I am greed-Śrī …" is from the Zhufa wuxing jing (T0650), where Mañjuśrī speaks. "The Great Sūtra's river with whirlpools" (p2) and "the teacher of buddhas is the Dharma" are in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (T0374/T0375).

Sources not located. The Tiwei Sūtra (an early Chinese apocryphon on the five precepts) survives only in fragments; its sentences here could not be checked. The Aṅgulimāla lines ("I vow to cut off the aggregates …", "Holding the vase of true emptiness …"), "From the past until now I have been made drunk by sounds and forms," "He comes from the eleven emptinesses," "Only the Buddha alone has the pure precepts," and "What is grave is called false speech" were located only in this commentary or in later works quoting it; source not located in the canonical sūtras.

"Contemplating thus is correct contemplation." The formula is close to the refrain of the Contemplation Sūtra (site page: "To contemplate in this way is called right contemplation; to contemplate otherwise is called wrong contemplation"), but the wording differs; 正觀 is kept as "correct contemplation" to match its other uses in this text.

The four Ānandas and the buddha-bodies. 丈六身佛 "the buddha of the sixteen-foot body," 尊特身佛 "the buddha of the specially honored body," and 丈六尊特合身佛 "the buddha of the combined sixteen-foot and specially honored body" correspond to the Tripiṭaka, distinct, and shared teachings; the Dharma-body buddha to the perfect.

Gṛdhrakūṭa. The contemplation reading glosses 靈 "numinous," 鷲 "vulture," and 山 "mountain," i.e., the Chinese name 靈鷲山 "Numinous Vulture Mountain," not the transliteration in the lemma.

"Golden Light." In the title, 光明 is rendered once as "Light"; when Zhiyi separates 金／光／明 he is translated as gold / light / brightness.

"My own view" (私謂). This is Guanding's own interjection as recorder.

The precepts excursus. 事殺／理殺 "phenomenal killing / killing in principle" and 法門 "Dharma-gate" readings follow the glossary's 事／理 and 析法／體法 (analysis / embodying). 客戒 "guest precepts" are precepts received from outside, contrasted with good by nature. King Xianyu (仙豫) is the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's king who killed brahmins who slandered the Vaipulya sūtras. Vasumitrā is the courtesan of the Avataṃsaka "Entering the Dharma-Realm" chapter.

"Paths differ but meet as one." 徑異會同 (units 26–30) once appears as 經異會同 in unit 29 ("the scriptures differ"); it is translated as written. Alternatively it is a copying variant of 徑.
