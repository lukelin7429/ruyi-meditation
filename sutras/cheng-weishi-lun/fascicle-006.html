Fascicle 6 · 成唯識論卷第六

The Eleven Wholesome Mental Factors

Fascicle 6 turns to the third of the six mental-factor categories: the eleven factors present only in a wholesome mind. It opens with faith (defined, against two rival views, as the mind's own inner clarity), the paired self-restraint of shame and embarrassment, the three wholesome roots — non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion — and diligence.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T31, no. 1585 Covers: the opening of the eleven wholesome mental factors — faith, shame, embarrassment, and the three wholesome roots

Faith: The Mind's Own Clarity 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 信

A seventh root verse lists all eleven wholesome mental factors. The treatise takes them up one at a time, beginning with faith — defined carefully against two rival proposals, each of which turns out to describe some other factor instead.

Three Kinds of Faith, and Its True Nature 釋「信」

Chinese Source

已說遍行、別境二位。善位心所，其相云何？頌曰：善謂信、慚、愧，無貪等三根，勤、安、不放逸、行捨及不害。

Ru-Yi Translation

The universal and particular categories have now been explained. What is the nature of the wholesome category? The verse states: "The wholesome factors are: faith, sense of shame, sense of embarrassment, the three wholesome roots beginning with non-greed, diligence, pliancy, heedfulness, equanimity, and non-harming."

論曰：唯善心俱，名善心所。謂信、慚等，定有十一。云何為信？於實、德、能，深忍、樂、欲，心淨為性；對治不信，樂善為業。然信差別，略有三種：一、信實有，謂於諸法實事、理中，深信忍故；二、信有德，謂於三寶真淨德中，深信樂故；三、信有能，謂於一切世、出世善，深信有力，能得能成，起希望故。由斯對治彼不信心，愛樂證修世、出世善。忍謂勝解，此即信因；樂欲謂欲即是信果。

The treatise states: whatever accompanies only a wholesome mind is called a wholesome mental factor. Faith, shame, and the rest number, definitively, eleven.

What is faith? Its nature is deep acceptance, delight, and aspiration with respect to what is real, what possesses virtue, and what has capability, taking the mind's own clarity as its essence; its function is counteracting lack of faith and delighting in what is wholesome.

Faith, broadly speaking, is of three distinct kinds: first, faith in what is real — deeply accepting the true facts and principles of all dharmas; second, faith in what possesses virtue — deeply believing in and delighting in the genuine, pure virtues of the Three Jewels; third, faith in what has capability — deeply believing that every mundane and supramundane wholesome quality has the power to be attained and accomplished, giving rise to aspiration accordingly. It is through this that lack of faith is counteracted, and one comes to delight in realizing and cultivating both mundane and supramundane wholesome qualities. Acceptance here refers to resolve, which is faith's cause; delight and aspiration refer to aspiration itself, which is faith's result.

礭陳此信，自相是何？豈不適言，心淨為性？此猶未了，彼「心淨」言，若淨即心，應非心所；若令心淨，慚等何別？心俱淨法，為難亦然。此性澄清，能淨心等。以心勝故，立心淨名。如水清珠，能清濁水。慚等雖善，非淨為相。此淨為相，無濫彼失。又諸染法，各別有相。唯有不信，自相渾濁，復能渾濁餘心、心所。如極穢物，自穢穢他。信正翻彼，故淨為相。

But precisely what is faith's own essential characteristic? Is it not already stated to be "the mind's own clarity"? This is not yet fully clear: if this "mind's clarity" simply means the mind itself when clear, then faith should not count as a mental factor at all; but if it means something that causes the mind to become clear, how does this differ from shame and the rest, which likewise are wholesome dharmas that clarify the mind when present together with it? The same difficulty would apply to any such dharma accompanying the mind.

Rather, faith's own nature is itself limpid and clear, and it is this that clarifies the mind and the rest — since mind is the more prominent factor, this clarity is named "the mind's clarity," just as a water-purifying jewel is said to clarify murky water. Shame and the other wholesome factors, though wholesome, do not have clarity as their defining characteristic — assigning clarity specifically to faith avoids any risk of confusing it with these others.

Furthermore, every defiled dharma has its own distinct characteristic, yet it is lack of faith alone whose own characteristic is itself turbid and murky, and which is, moreover, capable of further muddying the other mental factors that accompany it — just as an especially filthy object both is itself filthy and makes other things filthy too. Faith is precisely the opposite of this, and so has clarity as its defining characteristic.

有說：信者愛樂為相。應通三性，體應即欲。又應苦、集非信所緣。有執信者隨順為相。應通三性，即勝解、欲。若印順者，即勝解故。若樂順者，即是欲故。離彼二體，無順相故。由此應知，心淨是信。

Some hold: faith's defining characteristic is delight. If so, it should extend to all three moral qualities, and its essential nature would simply be aspiration — and, further, suffering and its origin could then never be objects of faith at all.

Some hold that faith's defining characteristic is conforming assent. If so, it should likewise extend to all three moral qualities, and would simply be resolve or aspiration — for if it means confirming assent, that is simply resolve; if it means delighted assent, that is simply aspiration; apart from these two, there is no separate characteristic of "conforming" to speak of. By this, it should be understood that the mind's own clarity is what faith truly is.

Reading note: The definition of faith as "the mind's own clarity" is doing careful philosophical work, and the muddy-water analogy is worth sitting with: a water-clarifying gem doesn't add anything foreign to the water, it simply lets the water's own natural clarity settle out and show itself. Faith, on this account, works the same way on the mind — not injecting some external belief-content, but clarifying the mind's own capacity to accept and delight in what is real, virtuous, and possible. The mirror-image point about lack of faith is equally sharp: an unreliable, turbid mind doesn't just fail to see clearly, it actively muddies whatever else is present with it — which is exactly the psychological reality behind why persistent doubt and cynicism tend to corrode other wholesome qualities rather than simply coexisting alongside them.

The refutations of "faith is delight" and "faith is conforming assent" both use the same move: show that the proposed definition already has a name (aspiration, or resolve), and that reusing it for faith would collapse a needed distinction — specifically, it would make faith extend to unwholesome and neutral states too, when faith by definition is exclusively wholesome, and would leave no way to have faith in something inherently unpleasant to accept, like the first noble truth of suffering.

Shame and Embarrassment: Two Kinds of Moral Restraint 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 慚、愧

The next two wholesome factors are a paired set — one grounded in self-respect, the other in regard for the opinion of others — and the treatise works through a careful debate over how two factors that look, on the surface, almost identical can still be genuinely distinct.

Self-Respect and Worldly Opinion 釋「慚」、「愧」

Chinese Source

云何為慚？依自、法力，崇重賢、善為性；對治無慚，止息惡行為業。謂依自、法尊、貴增上，崇重賢、善，羞恥過惡，對治無慚，息諸惡行。云何為愧？依世間力，輕拒暴惡為性；對治無愧，止息惡行為業。謂依世間訶、厭增上，輕拒暴惡，羞恥過罪，對治無愧，息諸惡業。羞恥過惡是二通相；故諸聖教，假說為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a sense of shame? Its nature is honoring and esteeming what is worthy and good, relying on the power of one's own self-respect and one's regard for the dharma; its function is counteracting shamelessness and bringing wrongdoing to a halt. That is: relying on the dominant influence of self-respect and regard for the dharma, one honors and esteems what is worthy and good, feels ashamed of one's own faults and misdeeds, counteracts shamelessness, and brings all wrongdoing to a halt.

What is a sense of embarrassment? Its nature is disdaining and rejecting violence and wrongdoing, relying on the power of worldly opinion; its function is counteracting shamelessness before others and bringing wrongdoing to a halt. That is: relying on the dominant influence of worldly reproach and disapproval, one disdains and rejects violence and wrongdoing, feels embarrassed before others over one's faults and transgressions, counteracts shamelessness before others, and brings all wrongdoing to a halt.

Feeling ashamed of one's faults is a characteristic shared by both of these — hence the scriptures sometimes speak of this shared feature as though it were their defining essence.

若執羞恥，為二別相，應慚與愧，體無差別。則此二法定不相應，非受、想等有此義故。若待自、他，立二別者，應非實有，便違聖教。若許慚、愧實而別起，復違論說十遍善心。崇重、輕拒若二別相，所緣有異，應不俱生。二失既同，何乃偏責？誰言二法所緣有異？不爾，如何？善心起時，隨緣何境，皆有崇重善，及輕拒惡義。故「慚」與「愧」，俱遍善心，所緣無別。豈不我說亦有此義？汝執「慚」、「愧」自相既同，何理能遮前所設難？然聖教說：「顧自、他者」，自法名自，世間名他。或即此中，崇拒善惡，於己益損，名自、他故。

Should one instead hold that feeling ashamed of one's faults is itself the two factors' distinct defining characteristic — then shame and embarrassment would have no real difference in essence at all, and these two dharmas could then certainly never be mutually associated together, since no two factors sharing an identical essence, such as feeling and perception, could ever coexist in this way.

Should one instead establish the two as distinct with reference to self versus others — this would mean they are not genuinely, substantially real, which would contradict the scriptures' own teaching. Should one grant, instead, that shame and embarrassment are genuinely real and arise as distinct factors — this would again contradict the treatise's statement that ten factors are universal to every wholesome mind.

Should one hold that honoring-and-esteeming and disdaining-and-rejecting are themselves the two factors' distinct characteristics, with correspondingly distinct objects — then the two should never arise together at all. In reply: since this fault applies equally to your own position, why single out mine for criticism? Who said the two factors' objects must differ? Should you object: if not that, then what? In reply: whenever a wholesome mind arises, whatever object it takes, it always involves both honoring what is good and disdaining what is bad — hence shame and embarrassment are both universal to every wholesome mind, without any difference in object between them. But does your own position not equally involve this same point? Since you yourself grant that shame's and embarrassment's own characteristics are identical, by what reasoning could this possibly block the difficulty raised against you?

The scriptures, however, do speak of "regard for self, and for others": "self" refers to self-respect and regard for the dharma; "others" refers to worldly opinion. Or else, within this very framework, honoring the good and disdaining the bad, insofar as they benefit or harm oneself, are themselves what is meant by "self" and "others."

Reading note: The genuine difficulty here is that shame and embarrassment, as ordinarily described, seem to differ only in their source of authority (an internal standard versus public opinion), not in what they actually do — both restrain wrongdoing through a felt sense of shame. The treatise's own solution splits the difference cleverly: it locates the two factors' real, distinct essence not in their feeling-tone (which is genuinely shared) but in their orientation — one toward honoring what is good, the other toward disdaining what is bad — while grounding the "self versus other" language in what each one relies on for its authority, not in a difference of object. This lets both count as universal wholesome factors, always present together, while still being two genuinely distinct things rather than one thing wearing two names.

The Three Wholesome Roots 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 無貪、無瞋、無癡

Next come the three roots that directly counteract the three unwholesome roots — greed, hatred, and delusion — beginning with the two simplest, non-greed and non-hatred, before a longer debate over whether non-delusion is really just another name for wisdom.

Non-Greed and Non-Hatred 釋「無貪」、「無瞋」

Chinese Source

「無貪等」者，等無瞋、癡。此三名根，生善勝故。三不善根，近對治故。云何無貪？於有、有具，無著為性；對治貪著，作善為業。云何無瞋？於苦、苦具，無恚為性；對治瞋恚，作善為業。善心起時，隨緣何境，皆於有等，無著、無恚，觀有等立，非要緣彼。如前慚、愧，觀善、惡立，故此二種，俱遍善心。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Non-greed and the rest" — "the rest" means non-hatred and non-delusion. These three are called "roots," since they are especially capable of generating what is wholesome, and since they are the direct counteragents to the three unwholesome roots.

What is non-greed? Its nature is non-attachment with respect to existence and the means of existence; its function is bringing about wholesome action. What is non-hatred? Its nature is non-aversion with respect to suffering and the means of suffering; its function is bringing about wholesome action.

Whenever a wholesome mind arises, whatever object it takes, it always involves non-attachment and non-aversion with respect to existence and the rest — these are established with reference to existence and the like in general, not requiring that the object actually be existence itself. Just as shame and embarrassment, discussed above, are established with reference to good and bad in general, so too are these two universal to every wholesome mind.

Is Non-Delusion Just Another Name for Wisdom? 釋「無癡」

Chinese Source

云何無癡？於諸理事，明解為性；對治愚癡，作善為業。有義：無癡即慧為性。《集論》說：此報、教、證、智決擇為體，生得、聞、思、修所生慧。如次皆是決擇性故。此雖即慧，為顯善品，有勝功能，如煩惱見，故復別說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is non-delusion? Its nature is clear understanding with respect to principles and things; its function is bringing about wholesome action.

One view holds: non-delusion has wisdom as its very nature. The Abhidharmasamuccaya says: its essence consists in the discriminating capacity that comes from karmic ripening, from instruction, from realization, and from wisdom itself — that is, innate wisdom, and wisdom born of learning, reflection, and cultivation, respectively corresponding to each of these. Although this is simply wisdom, it is discussed separately here in order to highlight its especially pronounced function within the wholesome category — just as the affliction of wrong view is discussed separately even though it, too, is simply a form of wisdom.

有義：無癡非即是慧，別有自性，正對無明。如無貪、瞋，善根攝故。論說：大悲無瞋、癡攝，非根攝故。若彼無癡，以慧為性，大悲如力等，應慧等根攝。又若無癡無別自性，如不害等，應非實物。便違論說：十一善中，三世俗有，餘皆是實。然《集論》說：慧為體者，舉彼因果，顯此自性。如以忍、樂，表「信」自體，理必應爾。以貪、瞋、癡六識相應，正煩惱攝。起惡勝故，立不善根。斷彼必由通、別對治。通唯善慧，別即三根。由此無癡必應別有。

Another view holds: non-delusion is not simply identical with wisdom, but has its own distinct nature, directly opposed to ignorance — just as non-greed and non-hatred, too, are included among the wholesome roots in their own right. The treatise states that great compassion is included under non-hatred and non-delusion, not under the powers as a distinct category. If non-delusion had wisdom as its very nature, then great compassion, like the powers and the rest, would have to be included under wisdom and the roots equally. Furthermore, if non-delusion had no distinct nature of its own — like non-harming and the rest — it should then not be a genuinely real, substantial thing, which would contradict the treatise's statement that, among the eleven wholesome factors, three are conventionally designated while the rest are all genuinely real.

As for the Abhidharmasamuccaya's statement that wisdom is its essence — this simply cites its cause and effect in order to display its own nature, in just the same way that acceptance and delight were used above to display faith's own essential nature; this must be understood the same way here.

Craving, hatred, and delusion, being associated with the six consciousnesses, are properly classified as afflictions in the strict sense — and because they are especially capable of giving rise to wrongdoing, they are established as the unwholesome roots. Severing them requires both a general and a specific counteragent: the general counteragent is simply wholesome wisdom; the specific counteragents are these very three roots. By this, non-delusion, too, must certainly have its own distinct existence.

Reading note: The stakes of this debate are higher than they first appear: if non-delusion just is wisdom under another name, then the wholesome roots collapse from three into effectively two-and-a-half, and the treatise's later claim that eleven wholesome factors exist, eight of them genuinely real and distinct, runs into trouble. The winning argument leans on a useful general principle worth extracting: when a scripture defines one thing by naming its causes and effects (here, wisdom as non-delusion's cause and effect), that is a common expository shortcut, not proof of strict identity — the same move was already used to explain faith via "acceptance and delight" without implying faith simply is those things. The deeper point is that wisdom, craving, hatred, and delusion belong to a general system that needs two layers of defense: a general antidote (wisdom, which can counter any affliction) and specific, dedicated antidotes (the three roots, each opposing exactly one unwholesome root) — collapsing non-delusion into wisdom would quietly erase that second, specific layer.
