Fascicle 6 · 成唯識論卷第六

The Eleven Wholesome Mental Factors

Fascicle 6 turns to the third of the six mental-factor categories: the eleven factors present only in a wholesome mind. It opens with faith (defined, against two rival views, as the mind's own inner clarity), the paired self-restraint of shame and embarrassment, the three wholesome roots — non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion — and diligence.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T31, no. 1585 Covers: the opening of the eleven wholesome mental factors — faith, shame, embarrassment, and the three wholesome roots

Faith: The Mind's Own Clarity 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 信

A seventh root verse lists all eleven wholesome mental factors. The treatise takes them up one at a time, beginning with faith — defined carefully against two rival proposals, each of which turns out to describe some other factor instead.

Three Kinds of Faith, and Its True Nature 釋「信」

Chinese Source

已說遍行、別境二位。善位心所，其相云何？頌曰：善謂信、慚、愧，無貪等三根，勤、安、不放逸、行捨及不害。

Ru-Yi Translation

The universal and particular categories have now been explained. What is the nature of the wholesome category? The verse states: "The wholesome factors are: faith, sense of shame, sense of embarrassment, the three wholesome roots beginning with non-greed, diligence, pliancy, heedfulness, equanimity, and non-harming."

論曰：唯善心俱，名善心所。謂信、慚等，定有十一。云何為信？於實、德、能，深忍、樂、欲，心淨為性；對治不信，樂善為業。然信差別，略有三種：一、信實有，謂於諸法實事、理中，深信忍故；二、信有德，謂於三寶真淨德中，深信樂故；三、信有能，謂於一切世、出世善，深信有力，能得能成，起希望故。由斯對治彼不信心，愛樂證修世、出世善。忍謂勝解，此即信因；樂欲謂欲即是信果。

The treatise states: whatever accompanies only a wholesome mind is called a wholesome mental factor. Faith, shame, and the rest number, definitively, eleven.

What is faith? Its nature is deep acceptance, delight, and aspiration with respect to what is real, what possesses virtue, and what has capability, taking the mind's own clarity as its essence; its function is counteracting lack of faith and delighting in what is wholesome.

Faith, broadly speaking, is of three distinct kinds: first, faith in what is real — deeply accepting the true facts and principles of all dharmas; second, faith in what possesses virtue — deeply believing in and delighting in the genuine, pure virtues of the Three Jewels; third, faith in what has capability — deeply believing that every mundane and supramundane wholesome quality has the power to be attained and accomplished, giving rise to aspiration accordingly. It is through this that lack of faith is counteracted, and one comes to delight in realizing and cultivating both mundane and supramundane wholesome qualities. Acceptance here refers to resolve, which is faith's cause; delight and aspiration refer to aspiration itself, which is faith's result.

礭陳此信，自相是何？豈不適言，心淨為性？此猶未了，彼「心淨」言，若淨即心，應非心所；若令心淨，慚等何別？心俱淨法，為難亦然。此性澄清，能淨心等。以心勝故，立心淨名。如水清珠，能清濁水。慚等雖善，非淨為相。此淨為相，無濫彼失。又諸染法，各別有相。唯有不信，自相渾濁，復能渾濁餘心、心所。如極穢物，自穢穢他。信正翻彼，故淨為相。

But precisely what is faith's own essential characteristic? Is it not already stated to be "the mind's own clarity"? This is not yet fully clear: if this "mind's clarity" simply means the mind itself when clear, then faith should not count as a mental factor at all; but if it means something that causes the mind to become clear, how does this differ from shame and the rest, which likewise are wholesome dharmas that clarify the mind when present together with it? The same difficulty would apply to any such dharma accompanying the mind.

Rather, faith's own nature is itself limpid and clear, and it is this that clarifies the mind and the rest — since mind is the more prominent factor, this clarity is named "the mind's clarity," just as a water-purifying jewel is said to clarify murky water. Shame and the other wholesome factors, though wholesome, do not have clarity as their defining characteristic — assigning clarity specifically to faith avoids any risk of confusing it with these others.

Furthermore, every defiled dharma has its own distinct characteristic, yet it is lack of faith alone whose own characteristic is itself turbid and murky, and which is, moreover, capable of further muddying the other mental factors that accompany it — just as an especially filthy object both is itself filthy and makes other things filthy too. Faith is precisely the opposite of this, and so has clarity as its defining characteristic.

有說：信者愛樂為相。應通三性，體應即欲。又應苦、集非信所緣。有執信者隨順為相。應通三性，即勝解、欲。若印順者，即勝解故。若樂順者，即是欲故。離彼二體，無順相故。由此應知，心淨是信。

Some hold: faith's defining characteristic is delight. If so, it should extend to all three moral qualities, and its essential nature would simply be aspiration — and, further, suffering and its origin could then never be objects of faith at all.

Some hold that faith's defining characteristic is conforming assent. If so, it should likewise extend to all three moral qualities, and would simply be resolve or aspiration — for if it means confirming assent, that is simply resolve; if it means delighted assent, that is simply aspiration; apart from these two, there is no separate characteristic of "conforming" to speak of. By this, it should be understood that the mind's own clarity is what faith truly is.

Reading note: The definition of faith as "the mind's own clarity" is doing careful philosophical work, and the muddy-water analogy is worth sitting with: a water-clarifying gem doesn't add anything foreign to the water, it simply lets the water's own natural clarity settle out and show itself. Faith, on this account, works the same way on the mind — not injecting some external belief-content, but clarifying the mind's own capacity to accept and delight in what is real, virtuous, and possible. The mirror-image point about lack of faith is equally sharp: an unreliable, turbid mind doesn't just fail to see clearly, it actively muddies whatever else is present with it — which is exactly the psychological reality behind why persistent doubt and cynicism tend to corrode other wholesome qualities rather than simply coexisting alongside them.

The refutations of "faith is delight" and "faith is conforming assent" both use the same move: show that the proposed definition already has a name (aspiration, or resolve), and that reusing it for faith would collapse a needed distinction — specifically, it would make faith extend to unwholesome and neutral states too, when faith by definition is exclusively wholesome, and would leave no way to have faith in something inherently unpleasant to accept, like the first noble truth of suffering.

Shame and Embarrassment: Two Kinds of Moral Restraint 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 慚、愧

The next two wholesome factors are a paired set — one grounded in self-respect, the other in regard for the opinion of others — and the treatise works through a careful debate over how two factors that look, on the surface, almost identical can still be genuinely distinct.

Self-Respect and Worldly Opinion 釋「慚」、「愧」

Chinese Source

云何為慚？依自、法力，崇重賢、善為性；對治無慚，止息惡行為業。謂依自、法尊、貴增上，崇重賢、善，羞恥過惡，對治無慚，息諸惡行。云何為愧？依世間力，輕拒暴惡為性；對治無愧，止息惡行為業。謂依世間訶、厭增上，輕拒暴惡，羞恥過罪，對治無愧，息諸惡業。羞恥過惡是二通相；故諸聖教，假說為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a sense of shame? Its nature is honoring and esteeming what is worthy and good, relying on the power of one's own self-respect and one's regard for the dharma; its function is counteracting shamelessness and bringing wrongdoing to a halt. That is: relying on the dominant influence of self-respect and regard for the dharma, one honors and esteems what is worthy and good, feels ashamed of one's own faults and misdeeds, counteracts shamelessness, and brings all wrongdoing to a halt.

What is a sense of embarrassment? Its nature is disdaining and rejecting violence and wrongdoing, relying on the power of worldly opinion; its function is counteracting shamelessness before others and bringing wrongdoing to a halt. That is: relying on the dominant influence of worldly reproach and disapproval, one disdains and rejects violence and wrongdoing, feels embarrassed before others over one's faults and transgressions, counteracts shamelessness before others, and brings all wrongdoing to a halt.

Feeling ashamed of one's faults is a characteristic shared by both of these — hence the scriptures sometimes speak of this shared feature as though it were their defining essence.

若執羞恥，為二別相，應慚與愧，體無差別。則此二法定不相應，非受、想等有此義故。若待自、他，立二別者，應非實有，便違聖教。若許慚、愧實而別起，復違論說十遍善心。崇重、輕拒若二別相，所緣有異，應不俱生。二失既同，何乃偏責？誰言二法所緣有異？不爾，如何？善心起時，隨緣何境，皆有崇重善，及輕拒惡義。故「慚」與「愧」，俱遍善心，所緣無別。豈不我說亦有此義？汝執「慚」、「愧」自相既同，何理能遮前所設難？然聖教說：「顧自、他者」，自法名自，世間名他。或即此中，崇拒善惡，於己益損，名自、他故。

Should one instead hold that feeling ashamed of one's faults is itself the two factors' distinct defining characteristic — then shame and embarrassment would have no real difference in essence at all, and these two dharmas could then certainly never be mutually associated together, since no two factors sharing an identical essence, such as feeling and perception, could ever coexist in this way.

Should one instead establish the two as distinct with reference to self versus others — this would mean they are not genuinely, substantially real, which would contradict the scriptures' own teaching. Should one grant, instead, that shame and embarrassment are genuinely real and arise as distinct factors — this would again contradict the treatise's statement that ten factors are universal to every wholesome mind.

Should one hold that honoring-and-esteeming and disdaining-and-rejecting are themselves the two factors' distinct characteristics, with correspondingly distinct objects — then the two should never arise together at all. In reply: since this fault applies equally to your own position, why single out mine for criticism? Who said the two factors' objects must differ? Should you object: if not that, then what? In reply: whenever a wholesome mind arises, whatever object it takes, it always involves both honoring what is good and disdaining what is bad — hence shame and embarrassment are both universal to every wholesome mind, without any difference in object between them. But does your own position not equally involve this same point? Since you yourself grant that shame's and embarrassment's own characteristics are identical, by what reasoning could this possibly block the difficulty raised against you?

The scriptures, however, do speak of "regard for self, and for others": "self" refers to self-respect and regard for the dharma; "others" refers to worldly opinion. Or else, within this very framework, honoring the good and disdaining the bad, insofar as they benefit or harm oneself, are themselves what is meant by "self" and "others."

Reading note: The genuine difficulty here is that shame and embarrassment, as ordinarily described, seem to differ only in their source of authority (an internal standard versus public opinion), not in what they actually do — both restrain wrongdoing through a felt sense of shame. The treatise's own solution splits the difference cleverly: it locates the two factors' real, distinct essence not in their feeling-tone (which is genuinely shared) but in their orientation — one toward honoring what is good, the other toward disdaining what is bad — while grounding the "self versus other" language in what each one relies on for its authority, not in a difference of object. This lets both count as universal wholesome factors, always present together, while still being two genuinely distinct things rather than one thing wearing two names.

The Three Wholesome Roots 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 無貪、無瞋、無癡

Next come the three roots that directly counteract the three unwholesome roots — greed, hatred, and delusion — beginning with the two simplest, non-greed and non-hatred, before a longer debate over whether non-delusion is really just another name for wisdom.

Non-Greed and Non-Hatred 釋「無貪」、「無瞋」

Chinese Source

「無貪等」者，等無瞋、癡。此三名根，生善勝故。三不善根，近對治故。云何無貪？於有、有具，無著為性；對治貪著，作善為業。云何無瞋？於苦、苦具，無恚為性；對治瞋恚，作善為業。善心起時，隨緣何境，皆於有等，無著、無恚，觀有等立，非要緣彼。如前慚、愧，觀善、惡立，故此二種，俱遍善心。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Non-greed and the rest" — "the rest" means non-hatred and non-delusion. These three are called "roots," since they are especially capable of generating what is wholesome, and since they are the direct counteragents to the three unwholesome roots.

What is non-greed? Its nature is non-attachment with respect to existence and the means of existence; its function is bringing about wholesome action. What is non-hatred? Its nature is non-aversion with respect to suffering and the means of suffering; its function is bringing about wholesome action.

Whenever a wholesome mind arises, whatever object it takes, it always involves non-attachment and non-aversion with respect to existence and the rest — these are established with reference to existence and the like in general, not requiring that the object actually be existence itself. Just as shame and embarrassment, discussed above, are established with reference to good and bad in general, so too are these two universal to every wholesome mind.

Is Non-Delusion Just Another Name for Wisdom? 釋「無癡」

Chinese Source

云何無癡？於諸理事，明解為性；對治愚癡，作善為業。有義：無癡即慧為性。《集論》說：此報、教、證、智決擇為體，生得、聞、思、修所生慧。如次皆是決擇性故。此雖即慧，為顯善品，有勝功能，如煩惱見，故復別說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is non-delusion? Its nature is clear understanding with respect to principles and things; its function is bringing about wholesome action.

One view holds: non-delusion has wisdom as its very nature. The Abhidharmasamuccaya says: its essence consists in the discriminating capacity that comes from karmic ripening, from instruction, from realization, and from wisdom itself — that is, innate wisdom, and wisdom born of learning, reflection, and cultivation, respectively corresponding to each of these. Although this is simply wisdom, it is discussed separately here in order to highlight its especially pronounced function within the wholesome category — just as the affliction of wrong view is discussed separately even though it, too, is simply a form of wisdom.

有義：無癡非即是慧，別有自性，正對無明。如無貪、瞋，善根攝故。論說：大悲無瞋、癡攝，非根攝故。若彼無癡，以慧為性，大悲如力等，應慧等根攝。又若無癡無別自性，如不害等，應非實物。便違論說：十一善中，三世俗有，餘皆是實。然《集論》說：慧為體者，舉彼因果，顯此自性。如以忍、樂，表「信」自體，理必應爾。以貪、瞋、癡六識相應，正煩惱攝。起惡勝故，立不善根。斷彼必由通、別對治。通唯善慧，別即三根。由此無癡必應別有。

Another view holds: non-delusion is not simply identical with wisdom, but has its own distinct nature, directly opposed to ignorance — just as non-greed and non-hatred, too, are included among the wholesome roots in their own right. The treatise states that great compassion is included under non-hatred and non-delusion, not under the powers as a distinct category. If non-delusion had wisdom as its very nature, then great compassion, like the powers and the rest, would have to be included under wisdom and the roots equally. Furthermore, if non-delusion had no distinct nature of its own — like non-harming and the rest — it should then not be a genuinely real, substantial thing, which would contradict the treatise's statement that, among the eleven wholesome factors, three are conventionally designated while the rest are all genuinely real.

As for the Abhidharmasamuccaya's statement that wisdom is its essence — this simply cites its cause and effect in order to display its own nature, in just the same way that acceptance and delight were used above to display faith's own essential nature; this must be understood the same way here.

Craving, hatred, and delusion, being associated with the six consciousnesses, are properly classified as afflictions in the strict sense — and because they are especially capable of giving rise to wrongdoing, they are established as the unwholesome roots. Severing them requires both a general and a specific counteragent: the general counteragent is simply wholesome wisdom; the specific counteragents are these very three roots. By this, non-delusion, too, must certainly have its own distinct existence.

Reading note: The stakes of this debate are higher than they first appear: if non-delusion just is wisdom under another name, then the wholesome roots collapse from three into effectively two-and-a-half, and the treatise's later claim that eleven wholesome factors exist, eight of them genuinely real and distinct, runs into trouble. The winning argument leans on a useful general principle worth extracting: when a scripture defines one thing by naming its causes and effects (here, wisdom as non-delusion's cause and effect), that is a common expository shortcut, not proof of strict identity — the same move was already used to explain faith via "acceptance and delight" without implying faith simply is those things. The deeper point is that wisdom, craving, hatred, and delusion belong to a general system that needs two layers of defense: a general antidote (wisdom, which can counter any affliction) and specific, dedicated antidotes (the three roots, each opposing exactly one unwholesome root) — collapsing non-delusion into wisdom would quietly erase that second, specific layer.

Diligence and Pliancy 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 勤、安

The treatise continues through the eleven wholesome factors with diligence — broken into five progressive stages matched to the path — and pliancy, the tranquil ease that counteracts torpor.

Five Stages of Diligence 釋「勤」

Chinese Source

勤謂精進，於善、惡品修、斷事中，勇、悍為性；對治懈怠，滿善為業。「勇」表勝進，簡諸染法。「悍」表精純，簡淨無記。即顯精進，唯善性攝。此相差別略有五種。所謂：被甲、加行、無下、無退、無足。即經所說：有勢、有勤、有勇、堅猛、不捨善軛，如次應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Diligence means vigorous effort: its nature is courage and fortitude in cultivating the wholesome and abandoning the unwholesome; its function is counteracting laziness and bringing the wholesome to full completion. "Courage" indicates superior advancement, distinguishing it from every defiled dharma; "fortitude" indicates purity and genuineness, distinguishing it from the morally neutral. Together, these show that vigorous effort belongs exclusively to the wholesome category.

Its distinct aspects, broadly speaking, number five: donning armor, applying effort, not faltering, not retreating, and not being satisfied. This corresponds to what the sūtra calls: possessing power, possessing diligence, possessing courage, being firm and vigorous, and not abandoning the wholesome yoke — each should be understood in the same order.

此五別者，謂初發心、自分、勝進，自分行中，三品別故；或初發心、長時、無間、慇重、無餘，修差別故；或資糧等，五道別故。二乘究竟道，欣大菩提故，諸佛究竟道，樂利樂他故；或二加行、無間、解脫、勝進別故。

These five are distinguished as follows: with reference to the initial arousal of intention, one's own portion of practice, and superior advancement — within one's own portion of practice further divided into three grades; or with reference to distinct stages of cultivation — the initial arousal, the sustained long duration, uninterrupted continuity, earnest intensity, and leaving nothing incomplete; or with reference to the distinct stages of the path — the provisions and the rest, five stages in all. The two vehicles' ultimate path delights in great awakening; the buddhas' ultimate path delights in benefiting and bringing happiness to others; or else these are distinguished according to the two kinds of practice — preliminary effort, uninterrupted progress, liberation, and superior advancement.

Pliancy: Tranquil Ease 釋「安」

Chinese Source

「安」謂輕安，遠離麁重，調暢身心，堪任為性；對治惛沈，轉依為業。謂此伏除能障定法，令所依止，轉安適故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Pliancy" means tranquil ease: its nature is being free of coarseness and heaviness, harmonizing and refreshing body and mind, and being fit for use; its function is counteracting torpor and transforming one's basis. That is: it suppresses and removes the dharmas that obstruct concentration, causing what one relies on to become transformed into a state of ease.

Reading note: The five-stage breakdown of diligence maps neatly onto anyone's experience of a long undertaking: donning armor is the resolve made before starting, applying effort is the work itself, and not-faltering, not-retreating, and not-being-satisfied name the three ways a sustained effort typically fails (giving up under difficulty, backsliding after initial progress, and settling for good-enough) — diligence, properly cultivated, is precisely what resists all three failure modes at once. Pliancy's function — "transforming one's basis" — is a phrase worth pausing on: it names a real, felt shift where a body and mind that were rigid, heavy, and resistant become workable and responsive, which is exactly the physical precondition every serious meditator eventually needs before deeper concentration becomes possible at all.

Two Factors With No Separate Essence 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 不放逸、行捨

Heedfulness and equanimity are unusual among the eleven wholesome factors: the treatise argues that neither has a genuine, distinct essence of its own — each is simply a name for four other factors working together.

Heedfulness: Just Four Factors by Another Name 釋「不放逸」

Chinese Source

「不放逸」者：精進、三根，於所斷、修，防、修為性；對治放逸，成滿一切世、出世間善事為業。謂即四法，於斷修事，皆能防、修，名不放逸。非別有體，無異相故。於防惡事、修善事中，離四功能，無別用故。雖信、慚等，亦有此能，而方彼四，勢用微劣；非根、遍策，故非此依。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Heedfulness" means that diligence and the three wholesome roots, with respect to what is to be abandoned and what is to be cultivated, have guarding and cultivating as their nature; their function is counteracting heedlessness and bringing to full completion every mundane and supramundane wholesome undertaking. That is: it is precisely these four dharmas which, with respect to abandoning and cultivating, are capable of guarding against and cultivating — this capacity itself is called heedfulness. It has no separate essence of its own, since it has no characteristic distinct from theirs: apart from the functioning of these four, there is no separate function involved in guarding against wrongdoing and cultivating what is wholesome. Although faith, shame, and the rest also have some capacity in this direction, compared to these four their power and function are markedly weaker; and, not being roots, and not acting as the constant driving factor, they are not what heedfulness relies on.

豈不防、修是此相用？防、修何異精進、三根？彼要待此，方有作用。此應復待餘，便有無窮失。勤唯遍策，根但為依，如何說彼有防、修用？

But is not guarding-and-cultivating precisely this factor's own defining characteristic and function? How does guarding-and-cultivating differ from diligence and the three roots? Should one object that those depend on this factor in order to have their own function — then this factor would, in turn, have to depend on yet another factor, leading to an infinite regress. Diligence alone constantly drives the mind forward, and the roots serve merely as its support — so how could one still claim that heedfulness has some separate guarding-and-cultivating function of its own?

汝防、修用，其相云何？若普依持，即無貪等。若遍策錄，不異精進。止惡、進善，即總四法。令不散亂，應是等持。令同取境，與觸何別？令不忘失，即應是念。如是推尋不放逸用，離無貪等，竟不可得。故不放逸，定無別體。

What, then, is the actual character of your proposed guarding-and-cultivating function? If it means universally serving as a support, that is simply non-greed and the rest. If it means constantly driving and directing, that is no different from diligence. If it means restraining evil and advancing the good, that is simply the combined function of these four factors together. If it means preventing distraction, that should simply be concentration. If it means causing a shared apprehension of the object, how does that differ from contact? If it means preventing forgetting, that should simply be recollection. Investigated this way, heedfulness's supposed function, apart from non-greed and the rest, can nowhere actually be found. Hence heedfulness certainly has no separate essence of its own.

Equanimity: Stillness Once Defilement Is Cleared 釋「行捨」

Chinese Source

云何「行捨」？精進、三根，令心平等、正直、無功用住為性；對治掉舉，靜住為業。謂即四法，令心遠離掉舉等障，靜住名捨。平等、正直、無功用住，初、中、後位，辯捨差別。由不放逸先除雜染；捨復令心寂靜而住。此無別體，如不放逸，離彼四法，無相、用故。能令寂靜，即四法故；所令寂靜，即心等故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is equanimity? Diligence and the three roots, causing the mind to abide in evenness, uprightness, and effortlessness, are its nature; its function is counteracting restlessness and abiding in stillness. That is: it is precisely these same four dharmas which cause the mind to be free of restlessness and similar obstructions, and this stillness of abiding is called equanimity. "Evenness," "uprightness," and "effortless abiding" distinguish equanimity's initial, middle, and final stages, respectively. Heedfulness first removes defilement; equanimity then causes the mind to abide in this stillness.

This, too, has no separate essence — like heedfulness, apart from these four dharmas, it has no characteristic or function of its own. What actually brings about this stillness is simply these four dharmas; what is brought to stillness is simply mind and the rest.

Reading note: Heedfulness and equanimity are the treatise's clearest examples of what might be called "compound" mental factors — real, meaningful names for a genuine pattern of activity, without that pattern requiring any additional, separately-existing ingredient beyond the four factors already doing the work. The infinite-regress argument used against heedfulness is a reusable piece of philosophical hygiene worth remembering: whenever a proposed new factor is defended by saying "the others need this one to actually function," ask whether that same defense could be run again for the new factor itself — if so, nothing has actually been explained, only postponed.

The heedfulness-then-equanimity sequence also captures something true about how settling the mind actually proceeds: first the active work of guarding against wrongdoing and cultivating the good (heedfulness) clears out the coarse defilement, and only then can the mind settle into the progressively subtler stillness of evenness, uprightness, and finally effortlessness (equanimity) — cultivation before stillness, not stillness instead of cultivation.

Non-Harming, and the Wholesome Factors Left Unnamed 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 善 · 不害及餘

The eleventh and final named wholesome factor is non-harming. The treatise then turns to a broader question: the root verse's word "and" implies further wholesome factors — the simple opposites of the various secondary afflictions — and asks why some of these get their own separate name while most do not.

Non-Harming: A Portion of Non-Hatred 釋「不害」

Chinese Source

云何「不害」？於諸有情，不為損惱，無瞋為性；能對治害，悲愍為業。謂即無瞋，於有情所，不為損惱，假名不害。無瞋，翻對斷物命瞋；不害，正違損惱物害。無瞋與樂；不害拔苦。是謂此二麁相差別。理實無瞋實有自體；不害依彼一分假立。為顯慈、悲，二相別故。利樂有情，彼二勝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is non-harming? Its nature is non-hatred with respect to not causing sentient beings injury or distress; its function is counteracting harmfulness through compassion. That is: it is precisely non-hatred, applied to not causing sentient beings injury or distress, that is provisionally named "non-harming." Non-hatred is the direct opposite of the hatred that takes life; non-harming is what directly opposes the harm that injures and distresses beings. Non-hatred bestows happiness; non-harming removes suffering — this is the coarse-grained difference between the two.

In genuine reality, non-hatred alone has real, substantial existence; non-harming is provisionally established with reference to one portion of it, in order to display the distinct characteristics of loving-kindness and compassion respectively — since benefiting and bringing happiness to sentient beings is what these two excel at.

有說：不害非即無瞋，別有自體，謂賢善性。此相云何？謂不損惱。無瞋亦爾，寧別有性？謂於有情不為損惱，慈悲賢善，是無瞋故。

Some hold: non-harming is not simply identical with non-hatred, but has its own distinct essence, namely a wholesome, virtuous nature. In reply: what, then, is this characteristic? It is simply not causing injury or distress — but non-hatred is exactly the same in this respect, so how could it possibly have some separate nature of its own? Not causing sentient beings injury or distress, with loving-kindness, compassion, and virtue, is precisely what non-hatred itself is.

Delight, Aversion, and the Unnamed Wholesome Factors 釋「及」· 欣、厭等

Chinese Source

「及」顯十一，義別心所，謂欣、厭等善心所法。雖義有別，說種種名，而體無異，故不別立。欣謂欲俱，無瞋一分，於所欣境，不憎恚故。不忿、恨、惱、嫉等亦然；隨應正翻瞋一分故。厭謂慧俱無貪一分，於所厭境，不染著故。不慳、憍等，當知亦然。隨應正翻，貪一分故。不覆、誑、諂無貪、癡一分；隨應正翻，貪、癡一分故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "and" indicates that, beyond these eleven, there are further mental factors distinguished in meaning — such as delight, aversion, and other such wholesome mental dharmas. Although they differ in meaning and are given various distinct names, their essence is no different from what has already been established, and so they are not separately listed.

"Delight," accompanied by aspiration, is a portion of non-hatred, since one feels no resentment or hostility toward the object one delights in. Non-wrath, non-resentment, non-vexation, non-envy, and the like are likewise so, each correspondingly the direct opposite of some portion of hatred. "Aversion," accompanied by wisdom, is a portion of non-greed, since one is not attached to the object one is averse to. Non-stinginess, non-arrogance, and the like should be understood the same way, each correspondingly the direct opposite of some portion of greed. Non-concealment, non-deceit, and non-flattery are portions of non-greed and non-delusion, each correspondingly the direct opposite of some portion of greed and delusion.

有義：不覆唯無癡一分；無處說覆亦貪一分故。有義：不慢信一分攝，謂若信彼，不慢彼故。有義：不慢捨一分攝，心平等者，不高慢故。有義：不慢慚一分攝，若崇重彼，不慢彼故。有義：不疑即信所攝，謂若信彼，無猶豫故。有義：不疑：即正勝解，以決定者，無猶豫故。有義：不疑即正慧攝，以正見者，無猶豫故。不散亂體，即正定攝。正見、正知，俱善慧攝。不忘念者，即是正念。悔、眠、尋、伺，通染、不染，如觸、欲等，無別翻對。

Some hold: non-concealment is only a portion of non-delusion, since nowhere is concealment itself said to be a portion of greed as well. Some hold: non-conceit is included as a portion of faith, since one who has faith in another does not feel conceit toward them. Some hold: non-conceit is included as a portion of equanimity, since one whose mind abides in evenness does not become arrogant. Some hold: non-conceit is included as a portion of shame, since one who honors and esteems another does not feel conceit toward them.

Some hold: non-doubt is simply included under faith, since one who has faith in something feels no hesitation regarding it. Some hold: non-doubt is simply correct resolve, since one who has reached a settled determination feels no hesitation. Some hold: non-doubt is simply included under correct wisdom, since one who holds correct view feels no hesitation.

Non-distraction's essence is simply included under correct concentration. Correct view and correct knowledge are both included under wholesome wisdom. Not forgetting is simply correct recollection. Regret, sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought extend to both defiled and undefiled states, like contact and aspiration, and have no separate opposite counterpart established for them.

何緣諸染所翻善中，有別建立，有不爾者？相、用別者，便別立之；餘善不然，故不應責。又諸染法遍六識者，勝故翻之，別立善法。慢等、忿等，唯意識俱。害雖亦然，而數現起損惱他故；障無上乘勝因悲故；為了知彼增上過失，翻立不害。失念、散亂及不正知，翻入別境，善中不說。

Why is it that, among the wholesome dharmas opposing the various defiled ones, some are separately established as distinct factors while others are not? Wherever there is a distinct characteristic and function, a distinct factor is established; where this is not so for the remaining wholesome opposites, one should not press the objection. Furthermore, defiled dharmas that pervade all six consciousnesses, being especially significant, have their opposites separately established as distinct wholesome dharmas.

Conceit and the rest, wrath and the rest, are associated only with the mental consciousness — harmfulness, too, is like this, yet, because it repeatedly manifests to injure and distress others, and because it obstructs compassion, the supreme cause of the unsurpassed vehicle, its opposite, non-harming, is separately established, in order to make its especially serious fault clearly known. Forgetfulness, distraction, and incorrect knowledge, when opposed, fall back into the particular mental factors, and so are not separately discussed among the wholesome factors.

Reading note: This closing section makes an important methodological point explicit: the treatise's fifty-one-factor taxonomy is not meant to be exhaustive of every nameable wholesome mental state — countless further ones (delight, non-envy, non-arrogance, and so on) genuinely exist and are worth naming in ordinary speech, but each of them reduces, on analysis, to a portion of one of the eleven already established. The principle for when something does earn its own separate listing is economy of explanation: only when a state has a characteristic and function that can't be accounted for by anything already on the list does it merit its own entry.

Non-harming's special status is a genuinely important exception, and the reasoning behind it says something about how this tradition weighs different faults: harmfulness gets its own dedicated opposite, unlike most other secondary afflictions' opposites, specifically because it obstructs compassion — "the supreme cause of the unsurpassed vehicle." This is a telling admission about priorities: of all the ordinary vices this system tracks, the one whose absence gets promoted to its own named virtue is the one most directly opposed to the very capacity the entire Mahāyāna path is built around.

Closing Questions: Real or Nominal, Universal or Not 十一善之總料簡

Chinese Source

染、淨相翻，淨寧少染？淨勝染劣，少敵多故。又解理通，說多同體，迷情事局，隨相分多。故於染、淨，不應齊責。此十一法，三是假有，謂不放逸、捨及不害，義如前說。餘八實有，相、用別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since wholesome and defiled dharmas are mutual opposites, why are there fewer wholesome factors named than defiled ones? Wholesome states are superior in strength while defiled states are inferior, so a smaller number can match a greater. Furthermore, understanding is comprehensive, and so a single wholesome factor is said to share its essence across many cases, whereas deluded, afflicted states are narrow and particular, and so are divided into many distinct characteristics accordingly. Hence one should not press the same objection equally against both the wholesome and the defiled categories.

Of these eleven factors, three are merely conventionally existent — heedfulness, equanimity, and non-harming — as already explained above. The remaining eight are genuinely, substantially real, since each has its own distinct characteristic and function.

有義：十一四遍善心，精進、三根，遍善品故。餘七不定。推尋事理，未決定時，不生信故。慚、愧同類，依處各別，隨起一時，第二無故。要世間道斷煩惱時，有輕安故。不放逸、捨，無漏道時，方得起故。悲愍有情時，乃有不害故。論說：十一六位中起。謂決定位，有信相應。止息染時，有慚、愧起，顧自、他故。於善品位，有精進、三根。世間道時，有輕安起，於出世道，有捨、不放逸。攝眾生時，有不害故。

One view holds: of these eleven, four are universal to every wholesome mind — diligence and the three roots — since these pervade the wholesome category as a whole. The remaining seven are not fixed: while still investigating a matter and not yet reaching a settled conclusion, faith does not arise; shame and embarrassment, though of the same general kind, rely on distinct bases, so that whenever one arises at a given moment, the other is absent; pliancy is present only when severing afflictions by way of a mundane path; heedfulness and equanimity arise only on the uncontaminated path; and non-harming is present only when feeling compassion for sentient beings.

The treatise states: these eleven arise across six distinct occasions — at the moment of settled determination, faith is associated; when bringing defilement to a halt, shame and embarrassment arise, with regard for self and for others respectively; in a wholesome state generally, diligence and the three roots are present; on the mundane path, pliancy arises; on the supramundane path, equanimity and heedfulness are present; and when gathering sentient beings to oneself, non-harming is present.

有義：彼說未為應理。推尋事理，未決定心，信若不生，應非是善。如染心等，無淨信故。慚、愧類異，依別境同，俱遍善心，前已說故。若出世道輕安不生，應此覺支非無漏故。若世間道無捨、不放逸，應非寂靜防惡修善故；又應不伏掉、放逸故。有漏善心既具四法，如出世道應有二故。善心起時，皆不損物，違能損法，有不害故。論說：「六位起十一」者，依彼彼增，作此此說。故彼所說，定非應理。

Another view holds: that account does not yet properly accord with reason. While still investigating a matter and not yet settled, if faith did not arise at all, that state should then not be wholesome in the first place — just as a defiled state of mind, lacking pure faith, is not wholesome. Shame and embarrassment, though differing in kind, rely on the same general object, and are both universal to every wholesome mind, as already explained above.

If pliancy did not arise on the supramundane path, then this factor of awakening should then not be uncontaminated at all. If the mundane path lacked equanimity and heedfulness, it should then be incapable of stillness, of guarding against wrongdoing, and of cultivating the good; nor could it suppress restlessness and heedlessness. Since a contaminated wholesome mind already possesses these four factors, the supramundane path, by the same token, should certainly possess them as well. Whenever a wholesome mind arises, it never harms any being, being directly opposed to whatever is capable of causing harm — hence non-harming, too, is present.

The treatise's statement that "these eleven arise across six occasions" is spoken only with reference to whichever factor is especially pronounced on each occasion — hence that earlier account is certainly not correct.

應說信等十一法中，十遍善心，輕安不遍。要在定位，方有輕安，調暢身心，餘位無故。〈決擇分〉說：十善心所定、不定地皆遍善心，定地心中，增輕安故。有義：定加行，亦得定地名。彼亦微有調暢義故。由斯欲界亦有輕安。不爾，便違〈本地分〉說：信等十一通一切地。有義：輕安唯在定有。由定滋養，有調暢故。論說：欲界諸心、心所，由闕輕安，名不定地。說一切地有十一者，通有尋伺等三地皆有故。

It should be said, instead: among faith and the other eleven factors, ten are universal to every wholesome mind, while pliancy alone is not universal — pliancy, harmonizing and refreshing body and mind, is present only in a state of meditative concentration, and absent in every other state. The Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī says: these ten wholesome mental factors are universal to every wholesome mind, whether in a state of concentration or not, while, in a mind in a state of concentration specifically, pliancy is additionally present.

Another view holds: the preliminary stage of concentration, too, can be called a "stage of concentration," since it, too, has a faint measure of harmonizing and refreshing. By this, pliancy can be present even in the desire realm. Were this not so, it would contradict the Basic Section's statement that faith and the other ten factors extend to every stage.

Another view holds: pliancy exists only in actual meditative concentration, since it is precisely concentration's nourishing power that produces this harmonizing refreshment. The treatise says: the mind and mental factors of the desire realm, lacking pliancy, are for this reason called "the stage without concentration." The statement that all eleven are present in every stage refers to their being present throughout the three stages that possess coarse and subtle discursive thought and the like.

此十一種，前已具說。第七、八識，隨位有無。第六識中，定位皆具。若非定位，唯闕輕安。有義：五識唯有十種，自性散動，無輕安故。有義：五識亦有輕安。定所引善者，亦有調暢故。成所作智俱，必有輕安故。此善十一，何受相應？十五相應。一除憂、苦，有逼迫受，無調暢故。此與別境皆得相應。信等欲等，不相違故。十一唯善。輕安非欲，餘通三界。皆學等三。非見所斷。《瑜伽論》說：信等六根，唯修所斷，非見所斷。餘門分別，如理應思。

These eleven factors have already been fully explained above. For the seventh and eighth consciousnesses, whether present or absent depends on the stage. For the sixth consciousness, all eleven are present in a state of concentration; outside of concentration, only pliancy is absent.

One view holds: the five sense-consciousnesses have only ten of these, since their very nature is scattered and unstable, lacking pliancy. Another view holds: the five sense-consciousnesses, too, can have pliancy, since wholesome states drawn forth by concentration also involve this harmonizing refreshment, and the wisdom of accomplished activity, which accompanies them, certainly involves pliancy as well.

With which feelings are these eleven wholesome factors associated? Ten of them are associated with all five feelings; one — pliancy — is associated with all except sorrow and pain, since these are oppressive feelings, incompatible with harmonizing refreshment.

These are all capable of being associated with the particular mental factors as well, since faith and the rest are not incompatible with aspiration and the rest. All eleven are exclusively wholesome. Pliancy is absent in the desire realm; the rest extend to all three realms. All eleven can belong to those in training, beyond training, or neither. None is eliminated on the path of seeing. The Yogācārabhūmi says: faith and the other five roots are eliminated only on the path of cultivation, never on the path of seeing. Further distinctions should be considered according to reason, as appropriate.

Reading note: The asymmetry question — why the treatise names fewer wholesome factors than defiled ones despite treating them as mirror opposites — gets a genuinely interesting two-part answer: wholesome states are individually more potent (so fewer are needed to do the same work), and a single wholesome factor can cover ground that affliction needs several narrower, more specific factors to cover, since delusion tends to fragment into many particular distortions where clarity remains comparatively unified.

The pliancy debate is worth tracking because it turns on a real, felt distinction in meditation practice: is the preliminary calming that happens on the approach to concentration already a form of pliancy, or does genuine pliancy require having actually entered concentration proper? The treatise's own resolution favors requiring genuine concentration, which matters practically — it means the deep bodily ease practitioners describe is being treated as a marker of an actual attainment, not just a byproduct of trying.

The Six Fundamental Afflictions 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 煩惱

The eighth root verse turns from the wholesome to the afflictive: the six fundamental afflictions — craving, hatred, delusion, conceit, doubt, and wrong view — that give this entire category its name. The treatise defines the first five before turning, in the next section, to wrong view's own five distinct forms.

Craving, Hatred, Delusion, Conceit, and Doubt 釋「貪」、「瞋」、「癡」、「慢」、「疑」

Chinese Source

如是已說善位心所，煩惱心所其相云何？頌曰：煩惱謂貪、瞋、癡、慢、疑、惡見。論曰：此貪等六性是根本煩惱攝故，得煩惱名。

Ru-Yi Translation

The wholesome category has now been explained. What is the nature of the afflictive mental factors? The verse states: "The afflictions are: craving, hatred, delusion, conceit, doubt, and wrong view." The treatise states: these six, being by nature included among the fundamental afflictions, receive the name "afflictions."

云何為貪？於有、有具，染著為性；能障無貪，生苦為業。謂由愛力，取蘊生故。云何為瞋？於苦、苦具，憎恚為性；能障無瞋，不安隱性，惡行所依為業。謂瞋必令身、心熱惱，起諸惡業，不善性故。

What is craving? Its nature is attachment and clinging with respect to existence and the means of existence; its function is obstructing non-greed and giving rise to suffering. That is: it is through the power of craving that the appropriated aggregates arise.

What is hatred? Its nature is aversion and hostility with respect to suffering and the means of suffering; its function is obstructing non-hatred, being itself an unsettled, disturbed nature, and serving as the basis for wrongdoing. That is: hatred invariably causes body and mind to burn with agitation, giving rise to various wrongful acts, being itself unwholesome by nature.

云何為癡？於諸理、事，迷闇為性；能障無癡，一切雜染所依為業。謂由無明，起疑、邪見、貪等煩惱、隨煩惱業，能招後生雜染法故。

What is delusion? Its nature is being benighted and in the dark with respect to principles and things; its function is obstructing non-delusion, and serving as the basis for every kind of defilement. That is: it is through ignorance that doubt, wrong view, craving, and the other primary and secondary afflictions and karmic actions arise, and these are capable of drawing forth further defiled dharmas in future births.

云何為慢？恃己於他，高舉為性；能障不慢，生苦為業。謂若有慢，於德、有德，心不謙下，由此生死輪轉無窮，受諸苦故。此慢差別有七、九種，謂於三品、我、德處生。一切皆通見修所斷，聖位我慢既得現行，慢類由斯起亦無失。

What is conceit? Its nature is relying on oneself to hold oneself above others, swelling with pride; its function is obstructing non-conceit and giving rise to suffering. That is: wherever conceit is present, the mind refuses to humble itself before virtue or the virtuous, and it is because of this that one revolves endlessly through birth and death, undergoing every kind of suffering.

This conceit, in its distinct forms, numbers seven, or nine, arising with respect to three grades and with respect to self and virtue as objects. All are eliminated on both the path of seeing and the path of cultivation — and since even a noble one can still have self-conceit manifest, there is no contradiction in holding that the various kinds of conceit can likewise arise in such a person.

云何為疑？於諸諦理，猶豫為性；能障不疑善品為業。謂猶豫者，善不生故。

What is doubt? Its nature is hesitation with respect to the truths and principles; its function is obstructing non-doubt and wholesome qualities. That is: one who hesitates cannot give rise to what is wholesome.

Reading note: These five definitions form a recognizable psychological progression: craving and hatred are the two basic orientations of pulling-toward and pushing-away; delusion is the more fundamental confusion that makes both possible and, crucially, is described here as the generative root from which doubt, wrong view, and every other affliction ultimately spring; conceit is what happens when the sense of self that delusion sustains starts comparing itself to others; and doubt is simply the failure to settle on the truths that would otherwise dissolve all of this. The claim that even a "noble one" — someone who has made real progress on the path — can still have self-conceit manifest is a sobering, realistic touch: advanced spiritual attainment doesn't automatically eliminate this particular affliction, which is why it is eliminated on both the path of seeing (the initial breakthrough) and the path of cultivation (the long process afterward), unlike afflictions that vanish the moment insight occurs.

Is Doubt Just Another Name for Wisdom? 釋「疑」自相

Chinese Source

有義：此疑以慧為體，猶豫簡擇說為疑故。「毘」助「末底」，是疑義故，「末底」般若義無異故。有義：此疑別有自體，令慧不決，非即慧故。《瑜伽論》說：「六煩惱中，見世俗有，即慧分故。餘是實有，別有性故。」「毘」助「末底」，執慧為疑，「毘」助「若南」，智應為識。界由助力，義便轉變，是故此疑非慧為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

One view holds: doubt has wisdom as its essence, since hesitant discrimination is what is called "doubt." The prefix "vi-" attached to "mati" gives the meaning of doubt, since "mati" itself is no different in meaning from wisdom.

Another view holds: doubt has its own distinct essence, since it is what prevents wisdom from reaching a decision, and so is not simply identical with wisdom. The Yogācārabhūmi says: "Among the six afflictions, wrong view is conventionally existent, since it is simply a portion of wisdom; the rest are genuinely real, each having its own distinct nature." If the prefix "vi-" attached to "mati" were taken to mean that wisdom itself becomes doubt, then, by the same logic, "vi-" attached to "jñāna" should mean that wisdom becomes mere consciousness — but a root's meaning shifts precisely because of such prefixes; hence this doubt does not, in fact, have wisdom as its essence.

What is wrong view? Its nature is defiled wisdom, inverted speculation with respect to the truths and principles; its function is obstructing correct view and drawing forth suffering. That is: one who holds wrong views mostly undergoes suffering as a result.

This view's mode of activity, in its distinct forms, numbers five. First, the view of a substantial body: clinging to the five grasped aggregates as "self" and "what belongs to self"; its function is serving as the basis for every other view and speculative position. This view's distinct forms, comprising twenty specific cases, sixty-five variants, and the like, are all included among the discriminated, acquired kind.

二、邊執見：謂即於彼，隨執斷、常，障處中行、出離為業。此見差別，諸見趣中，有執前際四遍常論、一分常論；及計後際有想十六；無想、俱非各有八論；七斷滅論等，分別起攝。

Second, the extremist view: clinging, with reference to that same self, to either annihilationism or eternalism; its function is obstructing the middle way and liberation. This view's distinct forms, among the various speculative positions, include: with reference to the past, the four theories of total eternalism and the theory of partial eternalism; and, with reference to the future, the sixteen theories positing a percipient afterlife, the eight theories each positing a non-percipient and a neither-percipient-nor-non-percipient afterlife, and the seven theories of annihilation, and the like — all included among the discriminated, acquired kind.

三、邪見：謂謗因果、作用、實事及非四見諸餘邪執，如增上緣，名義遍故。此見差別，諸見趣中，有執前際二無因論、四有邊等、不死矯亂；及計後際五現涅槃；或計自在、世主、釋、梵及餘物類，常恒不易；或計自在等是一切物因；或有橫計諸邪解脫；或有妄執非道為道，諸如是等皆邪見攝。

Third, wrong view proper: denying cause and effect, causal function, and real entities, along with every other wrongful clinging not included among the other four views — its scope and definition are as broad as that of a dominant condition. This view's distinct forms, among the various speculative positions, include: with reference to the past, the two theories of no cause, the four theories of a finite or infinite world and the like, and eel-wriggling equivocation; and, with reference to the future, the five theories of a presently-manifest nirvāṇa; or holding that Īśvara, the lord of the world, Śakra, Brahmā, or other such beings are permanent and unchanging; or holding that Īśvara and the like are the cause of all things; or various wrongly-conceived notions of liberation; or falsely taking what is not the path to be the path — all such positions as these are included under wrong view proper.

四、見取：謂於諸見及所依蘊，執為最勝，能得清淨，一切鬪諍所依為業。五、戒禁取：謂於隨順諸見戒禁及所依蘊，執為最勝，能得清淨，無利勤苦所依為業。然有處說：執為最勝，名為見取，執能得清淨，名戒取者，是影略說，或隨轉門。不爾，如何非滅計滅、非道計道，說為邪見，非二取攝。

Fourth, clinging to views: clinging to one's own views, together with the aggregates they depend on, as supreme and capable of bringing about purity; its function is serving as the basis for every kind of dispute and contention.

Fifth, clinging to rules and rituals: clinging to rules and rituals that accord with one's views, together with the aggregates they depend on, as supreme and capable of bringing about purity; its function is serving as the basis for fruitless, exhausting hardship.

In certain passages, however, it is said that clinging to something as supreme is called "clinging to views," while clinging to something as capable of bringing purity is called "clinging to rules and rituals" — this is an abbreviated way of speaking, or else a provisional, conventional mode of exposition. Were this not so, how could mistaking what is not cessation for cessation, and what is not the path for the path, be classified under wrong view proper rather than under these two kinds of clinging?

Reading note: The five views form a logical sequence worth tracing: the view of a substantial body is the foundational error (mistaking the five aggregates for a self), the extremist view is what that mistaken self gets stretched into once posited (does it end at death, or continue forever?), wrong view proper is the catch-all for denying cause-and-effect outright, and the last two — clinging to views and clinging to rules and rituals — are what happens when any of the above gets defended as *the* path to purity, generating dispute in the first case and pointless austerity in the second. The doctrinally famous move here is treating the view of a substantial body as the root of the entire list: every other wrong view, on this analysis, is downstream of first mistaking something impermanent and composite for a genuine, unified self.

The debate over whether doubt reduces to wisdom is a nice specimen of this text's willingness to settle disputes through etymology when scripture and reasoning alone don't fully resolve things — but the deciding move is actually functional, not linguistic: wisdom discriminates and settles; doubt is defined by its failure to settle. A faculty whose whole nature is indecision cannot simply be a form of the faculty whose job is deciding.

Innate or Acquired, and Which Afflictions Coexist 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 煩惱 · 俱生分別、相應

The treatise now asks two further questions about the ten afflictions: which arise spontaneously, innate from birth, versus only through deliberate reflection or bad influence; and, in detail, which of the ten can coexist with which others in a single mind.

Innate Versus Acquired Through Reflection 釋「俱生」、「分別起」

Chinese Source

如是總別十煩惱中，六通俱生及分別起，任運、思察，俱得生故。疑後三見，唯分別起，要由惡友或邪教力，自審思察，方得生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these ten afflictions, general and specific together, six extend to both the innate and the discriminated, acquired kind, since they can arise either spontaneously or through deliberate reflection. Doubt, and the last three views, are exclusively of the discriminated, acquired kind, since they can arise only through the influence of a bad companion or a wrong teaching, requiring one's own deliberate reflection to come about.

邊執見中通俱生者，有義：唯斷。常見相麁，惡友等力方引生故。《瑜伽》等說：「何邊執見是俱生耶？謂斷見攝。」學現觀者，起如是怖：「今者我，我何所在耶？」故禽獸等若遇違緣，皆恐我斷，而起驚怖。有義：彼論依麁相說，理實俱生，亦通常見。謂禽獸等，執我常存，熾然聚集長時資具。故《顯揚》等諸論皆說：於五取蘊，執斷、計常，或是俱生或分別起。

As for the extremist view's extension to the innate kind: one view holds it applies only to the annihilationist form, since the eternalist form has a coarser character, requiring the influence of a bad companion and the like to be drawn forth. The Yogācārabhūmi and similar texts say: "What extremist view is innate? It is the annihilationist kind." Those training toward direct realization give rise to a fear like this: "Where, now, will I, myself, go?" — hence even birds and beasts, when encountering an adverse condition, all become afraid that their self will be annihilated, and give rise to terror.

Another view holds: that treatise speaks only with reference to the coarser aspect; in genuine reality, the innate kind extends to the eternalist form as well. Consider birds and beasts, who cling to their self as permanently enduring, and eagerly accumulate provisions for the long term. Hence the Prakaraṇāryavācā-śāstra and other treatises all say: clinging to the five grasped aggregates as annihilated or as permanent can be either innate or discriminated, acquired.

The Full Compatibility Matrix 釋十煩惱互相應

Chinese Source

此十煩惱，誰幾相應？貪與瞋、疑，定不俱起，愛、憎二境，必不同故；於境不決，無染著故。貪與慢、見，或得相應，所愛、所陵境非一故，說不俱起。所染、所恃境可同故，說得相應。於五見境，皆可愛故，貪與五見相應無失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these ten afflictions, which can be associated with which, and how many at once? Craving and hatred, and craving and doubt, certainly never arise together, since the objects of love and of hatred can never be the same; and with respect to an object still undecided, there is no attachment to speak of.

Craving, and conceit or wrong view, sometimes can and sometimes cannot be associated: since the object one loves and the object one looks down upon need not be the same, they are sometimes said not to arise together; but since the object one is attached to and the object one relies on for pride can be the same, they are also said to be capable of association. Since the objects of all five views can themselves be objects of craving, craving can be associated with all five views without contradiction.

瞋與慢、疑或得俱起；所瞋、所恃境非一故，說不相應。所蔑、所憎境可同故，說得俱起；初猶豫時，未憎彼故，說不俱起。久思不決，便憤發故，說得相應；疑順、違事，隨應亦爾。瞋與二取，必不相應，執為勝、道，不憎彼故。此與三見，或得相應，於有樂蘊，起身、常見，不生憎故，說不相應。於有苦蘊，起身、常見，生憎恚故，說得俱起。斷見翻此，說瞋有、無。邪見誹撥惡事、好事，如次說瞋或無或有。

Hatred, and conceit or doubt, sometimes can and sometimes cannot arise together: since the object one hates and the object one relies on for pride need not be the same, they are sometimes said not to be associated; but since the object one disdains and the object one hates can be the same, they are also said to be capable of arising together. At the very first moment of hesitation, since one has not yet come to hate the object, they are said not to arise together; but after prolonged, unresolved deliberation, irritation and anger can result, and so they are also said to be capable of association. Doubt with respect to agreeable or disagreeable matters should be understood correspondingly in the same way.

Hatred and the two kinds of clinging are certainly never associated, since one holds these as supreme and as the true path, and so does not hate them. Hatred, and the remaining three views, sometimes can and sometimes cannot be associated: giving rise to the view of a substantial body or the eternalist view with respect to a pleasant set of aggregates does not give rise to hatred, and so they are said not to be associated; but giving rise to the view of a substantial body or the eternalist view with respect to a painful set of aggregates does give rise to hatred, and so they are said to be capable of arising together. The annihilationist view is the reverse of this in whether hatred is present or absent. Wrong view, in denying either something bad or something good, is said, correspondingly, to be sometimes without hatred and sometimes with it.

慢於境定，疑則不然。故慢與疑，無相應義。慢與五見，皆容俱起，行相展轉，不相違故。然與斷見，必不俱生，執我斷時，無陵、恃故。與身、邪見一分亦爾。疑不審決，與見相違，故疑與見，定不俱起。五見展轉，必不相應，非一心中，有多慧故。癡與九種，皆定相應，諸煩惱生，必由癡故。

Conceit is fixed with respect to its object, whereas doubt is not — hence conceit and doubt can never be associated. Conceit and the five views can all arise together, since their modes of activity, considered in turn, are not mutually opposed. Yet conceit and the annihilationist view certainly never arise together, since, at the very moment one clings to the self as annihilated, there is nothing left to look down upon or to take pride in — and the same holds for a portion of the view of a substantial body and of wrong view.

Doubt, lacking settled confirmation, is directly opposed to view — hence doubt and any of the views certainly never arise together. The five views, considered pairwise, certainly never arise together, since a single mind cannot contain more than one act of discriminating wisdom at once.

Delusion, however, is certainly associated with all nine of the other afflictions, since every affliction arises only through delusion.

此十煩惱，何識相應？藏識全無；末那有四；意識具十；五識唯三：謂貪、瞋、癡。無分別故。由稱量等，起慢等故。

With which consciousnesses are these ten afflictions associated? The storehouse consciousness has none of them at all; manas has four; the mental consciousness has all ten; the five sense-consciousnesses have only three — craving, hatred, and delusion — since, lacking discrimination, they cannot give rise to conceit and the rest, which require weighing and comparing.

Reading note: The birds-and-beasts example in the innate-versus-acquired debate is a genuinely striking piece of empirical reasoning for a text this abstract: the treatise notices that animals, who have never been exposed to a "bad companion" or a wrong teaching capable of instilling a philosophical position, nevertheless plainly act out of both a terror of ceasing to exist and (on the second view) an assumption that they will go on existing and so hoard resources for later — evidence, on this reading, that some version of the self-view underlying both annihilationism and eternalism must be built into the mind prior to any explicit doctrine at all, not merely learned from misguided teachers.

The compatibility matrix rewards being read as a coherent psychological model rather than pure classification: craving and hatred can't coexist because they're opposite orientations toward the same instant, but craving and conceit can, because loving something and feeling superior about it are not in tension at all — you can love your own possessions and feel proud of them at the same moment. Delusion's blanket compatibility with every other affliction (the only one on the list) reflects its role as the foundational confusion the other nine all depend on; it is less a peer among the ten and more the shared soil they all grow from, which is exactly why none of them can arise without it.

Feelings, Particular Factors, and Moral Quality 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 煩惱 · 何受、何性所攝

The treatise continues its systematic cross-tabulation of the ten afflictions: which feelings each can be associated with, which particular mental factors, and their moral classification.

Which Feelings Accompany Each Affliction 釋「何受相應」

Chinese Source

此十煩惱，何受相應？貪、瞋、癡三，俱生分別，一切容與五受相應。貪會違緣，憂、苦俱故；瞋遇順境，喜、樂俱故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With which feelings are these ten afflictions associated? Craving, hatred, and delusion — whether innate or discriminated, acquired — can all be associated with any of the five feelings, since craving can encounter an adverse condition and thus be associated with sorrow or pain, and hatred can encounter a favorable condition and thus be associated with joy or pleasure.

有義：俱生分別起慢，容與非苦四受相應，恃苦劣蘊，憂相應故。有義：俱生亦苦俱起，意有苦受，前已說故。分別慢等，純苦趣無，彼無邪師、邪教等故。然彼不造引惡趣業，要分別起能發彼故。疑後三見，容四受俱。欲疑無苦等，亦喜受俱故；二取若緣憂俱見等，爾時得與憂相應故。

One view holds: conceit, whether innate or discriminated, acquired, can be associated with any of the four feelings other than pain, since relying with pride on an inferior, suffering aggregate is associated with sorrow. Another view holds: the innate kind can be associated with pain as well, since the mental consciousness can have the pain-faculty, as already explained above. The discriminated, acquired kind of conceit and the rest is absent in realms of unmixed suffering, since these lack the wrong teachers and wrong teachings needed to produce it — yet beings there still do not create the karma that draws them into the lower destinies, since only the discriminated, acquired kind is capable of giving rise to that.

Doubt, and the last three views, can be associated with any of four feelings: even in the desire realm, doubt and the rest can be associated with joy as well; and the two kinds of clinging, when taking as their object a view itself associated with sorrow, can at that time be associated with sorrow too.

有義：俱生身邊二見，但與喜、樂、捨、受相應，非五識俱，唯無記故。分別二見，容四受俱，執苦俱蘊為我、我所。常、斷見翻此，與憂相應故。有義：二見若俱生者，亦苦受俱，純受苦處，緣極苦蘊，苦相應故。論說：俱生一切煩惱，皆於三受現行可得，廣說如前，餘如前說。

One view holds: the innate forms of the view of a substantial body and the extremist view are associated only with joy, pleasure, and equanimous feeling, since they are never associated with the five sense-consciousnesses, being exclusively morally neutral. The discriminated, acquired forms of these two views can be associated with any of four feelings, since one can cling to an aggregate accompanied by suffering as "self" or "what belongs to self" — the eternalist and annihilationist views, conversely, are then associated with sorrow.

Another view holds: even the innate forms of these two views, when they arise, can be associated with pain as well, since, in realms of unmixed suffering, taking an extremely painful aggregate as one's object is associated with pain. The treatise states: every innate affliction can manifest in association with any of the three basic feelings, as explained at length above; the rest is as already stated.

此依實義，隨麁相者，貪、慢、四見，樂、喜、捨俱。瞋唯苦、憂、捨受俱起。癡與五受皆得相應。邪見及疑四俱，除苦。貪、癡俱樂，通下四地，餘七俱樂，除欲通三，疑、獨行癡欲唯憂、捨，餘受俱起，如理應知。

This is according to genuine reality; considered with reference to the coarser, more general pattern: craving, conceit, and four of the views are associated with pleasure, joy, and equanimity; hatred is associated only with pain, sorrow, and equanimity; delusion can be associated with all five feelings; wrong view and doubt are associated with four, excluding pain. Craving and delusion, associated with pleasure, extend through the lower four stages; the remaining seven, associated with pleasure, extend through three realms excluding the desire realm; doubt, and delusion operating independently, are associated only with sorrow and equanimity in the desire realm, and with the remaining feelings elsewhere, as should be understood according to reason.

Particular Factors and Moral Quality 釋「與別境相應」、「何性所攝」

Chinese Source

此與別境幾互相應？貪、瞋、癡、慢容五俱起，專注一境，得有定故。疑及五見，各容四俱，疑除勝解，不決定故；見非慧俱，不異慧故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With how many of the particular mental factors can these be mutually associated? Craving, hatred, delusion, and conceit can all be associated with all five, since they can focus single-pointedly on an object, and so can involve concentration. Doubt, and the five views, can each be associated with only four: doubt excludes resolve, since it lacks settled determination; the views are never associated with wisdom, since they are not distinct from wisdom to begin with.

此十煩惱何性所攝？瞋唯不善，損自、他故，餘九通二。上二界者，唯無記攝，定所伏故。若欲界繫分別起者，唯不善攝，發惡行故。若是俱生，發惡行者，亦不善攝，損自、他故。餘無記攝，細不障善，非極損惱自、他處故。當知俱生身、邊二見，唯無記攝，不發惡業，雖數現起，不障善故。

To what moral category do these ten afflictions belong? Hatred is exclusively unwholesome, since it harms both oneself and others; the remaining nine extend to both unwholesome and neutral. Those bound to the upper two realms are exclusively morally neutral, since they are held in check by meditative concentration. Discriminated, acquired afflictions bound to the desire realm are exclusively unwholesome, since they give rise to wrongdoing; innate afflictions that give rise to wrongdoing are likewise unwholesome, since they harm both oneself and others; the rest are morally neutral, being too subtle to obstruct the wholesome, and not severely harming oneself or others. It should be understood that the innate view of a substantial body and the innate extremist view are exclusively morally neutral, since they never give rise to wrongdoing — even though they manifest repeatedly, they do not obstruct what is wholesome.

此十煩惱何界繫耶？瞋唯在欲，餘通三界。

To which realm are these ten afflictions bound? Hatred is bound only to the desire realm; the remaining nine extend to all three realms.

Reading note: Hatred stands out across every one of these cross-tabulations as the odd one out — exclusively unwholesome, exclusively bound to the desire realm, exclusively associated with painful feeling — and the pattern is worth naming: hatred is the one affliction this system treats as having no "quiet," subtle, harmless-seeming form at all. Craving, conceit, and even wrong view can operate so faintly they barely register as unwholesome, held in check by meditative concentration in the upper realms; hatred cannot, because its very definition requires an active aversion that meditative stillness makes structurally impossible to sustain. This is presumably why later Buddhist ethical writing treats hatred as, in some respects, the most urgent of the three poisons to uproot — it is the one that cannot hide.

The innate view of a substantial body being classified as harmless enough to never obstruct the wholesome is a striking, almost counterintuitive claim worth sitting with: the very belief this entire treatise has been built to dismantle — the ordinary, unreflective sense of being a self — is here treated as karmically neutral in its innate form, doing no direct harm to oneself or others. Its danger isn't that it makes people cruel; it's that it's the permanent, quiet foundation everything else, including genuine cruelty when it does arise, ultimately depends on.

Crossing Realms, and Which Truth Each Affliction Misreads 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 煩惱 · 緣上下地、迷諦所斷

The treatise closes its treatment of the six fundamental afflictions with two final questions: can an affliction bound to one realm still take another realm as its object, and — the classical scheme for how afflictions are eliminated — which of the four noble truths does each affliction fail to understand.

Taking Another Realm as Object 釋「緣上」、「緣下」地

Chinese Source

生在下地，未離下染，上地煩惱，不現在前。要得彼地根本定者，彼地煩惱容現前故。謂有漏道，雖不能伏分別起惑及細俱生，而能伏除俱生麁惑，漸次證得上根本定。彼但迷事，依外門轉，散亂、麁動，正障定故。得彼定已，彼地分別、俱生諸惑，皆容現前。生在上地，下地諸惑，分別、俱生皆容現起。生第四定中有中者，由謗解脫，生地獄故；身在上地，將生下時，起下潤生俱生愛故。而言生上，不起下者，依多分說，或隨轉門。

Ru-Yi Translation

When born in a lower realm, and not yet free of that realm's own defilement, the afflictions of an upper realm do not manifest. Only once one attains that upper realm's fundamental meditative absorption can that realm's afflictions manifest. That is: although a contaminated path cannot suppress discriminated, acquired afflictions or subtle, innate ones, it can suppress and remove the coarser, innate afflictions, and thereby gradually attain the fundamental absorption of a higher realm — since it is precisely being deluded about particular objects, operating through the outward-facing sense-doors, being scattered and coarsely agitated, that directly obstructs concentration. Once that absorption is attained, both the discriminated, acquired and innate afflictions of that realm can manifest.

When born in an upper realm, the afflictions of a lower realm, both discriminated, acquired and innate, can also manifest. Consider one in the intermediate state after the fourth absorption: through slandering liberation, such a being can be reborn in hell — that is, while still embodied in the upper realm, at the very moment of being about to be reborn below, the innate craving that moistens and nourishes rebirth in the lower realm arises. As for the statement that a being born in the upper realm does not give rise to lower-realm afflictions, this is spoken with reference to what holds for the most part, or else as a merely provisional exposition.

下地煩惱，亦緣上地。《瑜伽》等說：欲界繫貪，求上地生，味上定故。既說瞋恚憎嫉滅、道，亦應憎嫉離欲地故。總緣諸行，執我、我所斷常，慢者得緣上故。餘五緣上，其理極成。而有處言：貪、瞋、慢等不緣上者，依麁相說，或依別緣，不見世間執他地法為我等故；邊見必依身見起故。上地煩惱亦緣下地，說生上者，於下有情，恃己勝德，而陵彼故。總緣諸行，執我、我所、斷、常、愛者，得緣下故。疑後三見，如理應思。而說上或不緣下者，彼依多分或別緣說。

A lower realm's afflictions can also take an upper realm as their object. The Yogācārabhūmi and similar texts say: craving bound to the desire realm can seek rebirth in an upper realm, delighting in that realm's absorption. And since it is said that hatred can resent and be jealous of cessation and the path, it should likewise be able to resent and be jealous of the realm free of desire. Taking all conditioned phenomena generally as its object, clinging to a self and to what belongs to self, whether annihilated or permanent, conceit can equally take an upper realm as its object. That the remaining five can take an upper realm as their object is thereby well established.

Yet in certain passages it is said that craving, hatred, conceit, and the rest do not take an upper realm as their object — this is spoken with reference to their coarser, more general pattern, or with reference to a more specific object, since one does not ordinarily see beings clinging to a dharma of another realm as their own self, and since the extremist view necessarily arises in dependence on the view of a substantial body.

An upper realm's afflictions can likewise take a lower realm as their object: a being born in the upper realm can rely on their own superior virtue to look down upon beings in the lower realm. Taking all conditioned phenomena generally as its object, clinging to a self and to what belongs to self, whether annihilated or permanent, or with craving, can equally take a lower realm as its object. Doubt, and the last three views, should be understood accordingly, following the same reasoning. As for the statement that an upper realm's afflictions do not take a lower realm as their object, this too is spoken with reference to what holds for the most part, or with reference to a more specific object.

Which Truth Each Affliction Fails to Understand 釋「迷諦所斷」

Chinese Source

此十煩惱，學等何攝？非學無學，彼唯善故。此十煩惱何所斷耶？非非所斷，彼非染故。分別起者，唯見所斷，麁易斷故；若俱生者，唯修所斷，細難斷故。見所斷十，實俱頓斷，以真見道總緣諦故。然迷諦相，有總、有別。總謂十種，皆迷四諦。苦、集是彼因依處故；滅、道是彼怖畏處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

To which of the three categories — trainee, beyond-training, or neither — do these ten afflictions belong? None of them belongs to trainee or beyond-training, since those categories are exclusively wholesome. On what path are these ten afflictions eliminated? None of them is beyond elimination altogether, since they are, by nature, defiled. The discriminated, acquired kind is eliminated only on the path of seeing, being coarse and easily eliminated; the innate kind is eliminated only on the path of cultivation, being subtle and difficult to eliminate.

The ten afflictions eliminated on the path of seeing are, in genuine reality, all severed together, all at once, since the true path of seeing takes the four truths together, as a whole, as its object. Yet the way each affliction is deluded with respect to the truths has both a general and a specific aspect. Generally speaking, all ten are deluded with respect to all four truths, since suffering and its origin are the basis they depend on as their cause, while cessation and the path are what they find fearsome.

別謂別迷四諦相起，二唯迷苦，八通迷四，身、邊二見，唯果處起，別空、非我，屬苦諦故。謂疑、三見，親迷苦理。二取執彼三見、戒禁及所依蘊為勝能淨，於自、他見及彼眷屬，如次隨應，起貪、恚、慢。相應無明，與九同迷；不共無明，親迷苦理。疑及邪見，親迷集等。二取、貪等，准苦應知。然瞋亦能親迷滅、道，由怖畏彼，生憎嫉故。

Specifically speaking, each is deluded with respect to a distinct aspect of the four truths: two are deluded only with respect to suffering, while eight extend to delusion regarding all four. The view of a substantial body and the extremist view arise only with reference to the domain of the result, specifically emptiness and no-self, which belong to the truth of suffering.

Doubt, and the remaining three views, are directly deluded regarding the principle of suffering. The two kinds of clinging, holding these three views, the rules and rituals, and the aggregates they depend on to be supreme and capable of purifying, give rise, correspondingly, to craving, hostility, or conceit toward one's own view and its adherents, or toward another's. Associated ignorance is deluded together with these nine in the same way; exclusive ignorance is directly deluded regarding the principle of suffering. Doubt and wrong view are directly deluded regarding the origin of suffering, and so on. The two kinds of clinging, craving, and the rest should be understood correspondingly, following the pattern already given for suffering. Hatred, too, can be directly deluded regarding cessation and the path, since fear of these gives rise to resentment and jealousy toward them.

迷諦親疎，麁相如是。委細說者，貪、瞋、慢、三見、疑俱生，隨應如彼。俱生二見及彼相應愛、慢、無明，雖迷苦諦，細難斷故，修道方斷。瞋餘愛等，迷別事生，不違諦觀，故修所斷。雖諸煩惱，皆有相分，而所仗質，或有或無，名緣有事、無事煩惱。彼親所緣，雖皆有漏，而所仗質，亦通無漏，名緣有漏、無漏煩惱。緣自地者，相分似質，名緣分別所起事境。緣滅、道諦及他地者，相分與質不相似故，名緣分別所起名境。餘門分別如理應思。

The direct and indirect delusion regarding the truths, in coarse outline, is as described above. Considered in fuller detail: the innate forms of craving, hatred, conceit, the three views, and doubt follow the corresponding pattern already given. The innate forms of the view of a substantial body and the extremist view, together with the craving, conceit, and ignorance associated with them, though deluded regarding the truth of suffering, are subtle and difficult to eliminate, and so are eliminated only on the path of cultivation. Hatred and the remaining forms of craving arise with respect to particular objects, and do not conflict with direct contemplation of the truths, and so are likewise eliminated on the path of cultivation.

Although every affliction has a perceived aspect, the underlying basis it relies on may or may not exist — hence they are called afflictions that take a real or an unreal basis as their object. What they directly take as their object is always contaminated, yet the underlying basis they rely on may itself be uncontaminated — hence they are called afflictions that take a contaminated or an uncontaminated basis as their object. Those that take their own realm as their object have a perceived aspect resembling their underlying basis, and so are called afflictions taking a genuine object as constructed by discrimination; those that take cessation, the path, or another realm as their object have a perceived aspect that does not resemble their underlying basis, and so are called afflictions taking a merely nominal object as constructed by discrimination. Further distinctions should be considered according to reason, as appropriate.

Reading note: The which-truth-each-affliction-misreads scheme is one of classical Abhidharma's most influential contributions to how Buddhist traditions diagnose spiritual obstacles, and it repays careful attention: rather than treating "ignorance of the truths" as one undifferentiated fog, it maps each affliction onto a specific cognitive failure — some (self-view, the extremist view) are narrowly about misreading one's own aggregates as suffering rather than self; others (doubt, wrong view, the clinging pair) can misfire against any of the four truths, including fearing and resenting the very cessation and path that would liberate them. This is worth pausing on: it is entirely possible, on this account, to feel active resentment toward the idea of liberation itself, not just toward ordinary suffering.

The rule that all ten discriminated, acquired afflictions are severed together, in one stroke, at the moment of genuine insight is a distinctive feature of this tradition's account of awakening's opening breakthrough: understanding is not doled out truth by truth, incrementally chipping away at each affliction in turn — the four truths are seen together as a single, unified realization, and every acquired affliction tied to any of them collapses at once. What remains after that breakthrough — the innate, subtler forms — is precisely what the long, gradual path of cultivation exists to address, since insight alone, however total, does not undo habits laid down since beginningless time.

The Twenty Secondary Afflictions Begin 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 隨煩惱

A new root verse lists all twenty secondary afflictions, sorted into three categories by how broadly each one operates. The treatise takes them up one at a time, starting with four that turn out, on close analysis, to be nothing more than hatred wearing different faces.

Twenty Factors, Three Categories 釋「隨煩惱」二十種、三類

Chinese Source

已說根本六煩惱相，諸隨煩惱其相云何？頌曰：隨煩惱謂：忿、恨、覆、惱、嫉、慳、誑、諂與害、憍、無慚與無愧、掉舉與惛沈、不信并懈怠、放逸及失念、散亂不正知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The nature of the six fundamental afflictions has now been explained. What is the nature of the secondary afflictions? The verse states: "The secondary afflictions are: wrath, resentment, concealment, vexation, envy, stinginess, deceit, flattery, harmfulness, and arrogance; lack of shame and lack of conscience; restlessness and torpor; lack of faith and laziness; heedlessness and forgetfulness; distraction and incorrect knowledge."

論曰：唯是煩惱分位差別、等流性，故名隨煩惱。此二十種，類別有三：謂忿等十，各別起故，名小隨煩惱；無慚等二，遍不善故，名中隨煩惱；掉舉等八，遍染心故，名大隨煩惱。

The treatise states: these are called "secondary afflictions" because they are simply distinct phases of the fundamental afflictions, outflows sharing their same nature. These twenty kinds, by category, are of three sorts: wrath and the other nine, each arising separately and independently, are called the minor secondary afflictions; lack of shame and lack of conscience, pervading every unwholesome state, are called the intermediate secondary afflictions; restlessness and the other seven, pervading every defiled mind, are called the major secondary afflictions.

Four Faces of Hatred: Wrath, Resentment, Concealment, Vexation 釋「忿」、「恨」、「覆」、「惱」

Chinese Source

云何為忿？依對現前不饒益境，憤發為性；能障不忿，執杖為業。謂懷忿者，多發暴惡身表業故。此即瞋恚一分為體，離瞋無別忿相用故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is wrath? Its nature is indignant outburst in response to a currently present, unfavorable object; its function is obstructing non-wrath and taking up a weapon. That is: one who harbors wrath frequently gives rise to violent, wrongful bodily expression. This has, as its essence, simply a portion of hatred, since apart from hatred there is no separate characteristic or function of wrath to be found.

云何為恨？由忿為先，懷惡不捨，結怨為性；能障不恨，熱惱為業。謂結恨者，不能含忍，恒熱惱故。此亦瞋恚一分為體，離瞋無別恨相用故。

What is resentment? Preceded by wrath, its nature is harboring ill will without letting go, binding oneself to a grudge; its function is obstructing non-resentment and causing burning distress. That is: one who binds themselves to a grudge cannot tolerate and forbear, and so is constantly burning with distress. This, too, has, as its essence, simply a portion of hatred, since apart from hatred there is no separate characteristic or function of resentment to be found.

云何為覆？於自作罪，恐失利、譽，隱藏為性；能障不覆，悔惱為業。謂覆罪者，後必悔惱，不安隱故。有義：此覆，癡一分攝。論唯說此癡一分故，不懼當苦，覆自罪故。有義：此覆，貪、癡一分攝，亦恐失利譽，覆自罪故。論據麁顯，唯說癡分，如說掉舉是貪分故，然說掉舉遍諸染心，不可執為唯是貪分。

What is concealment? Its nature is concealing one's own committed wrongdoing, out of fear of losing gain or reputation; its function is obstructing non-concealment and causing regret and distress. That is: one who conceals their wrongdoing will inevitably later feel regret and distress, being unable to find peace.

One view holds: this concealment is included as a portion of delusion, since the treatise speaks of it only as a portion of delusion — one conceals one's own wrongdoing without fearing the future suffering it will bring.

Another view holds: this concealment is included as a portion of both craving and delusion, since one also conceals one's own wrongdoing out of fear of losing gain and reputation. The treatise, speaking with reference to the more conspicuous, general pattern, mentions only the portion of delusion — just as it speaks of restlessness as a portion of craving, and yet also states that restlessness pervades every defiled mind, so restlessness cannot be held to be exclusively a portion of craving either.

云何為惱？忿、恨為先，追觸暴熱，佷戾為性；能障不惱，䖧螫為業。謂追往惡，觸現違緣，心便佷戾，多發囂暴、凶鄙麁言，䖧螫他故。此亦瞋恚一分為體，離瞋無別惱相用故。

What is vexation? Preceded by wrath and resentment, its nature is recalling and being struck by fierce heat, becoming obstinate and perverse; its function is obstructing non-vexation and stinging others like a scorpion. That is: recalling past wrongs, and being struck by present adverse conditions, the mind becomes obstinate and perverse, frequently giving rise to clamorous, violent, vicious, and coarse speech, stinging others like a scorpion. This, too, has, as its essence, simply a portion of hatred, since apart from hatred there is no separate characteristic or function of vexation to be found.

Reading note: Wrath, resentment, and vexation form a small, coherent life-cycle of anger worth noticing as a sequence: wrath is the immediate outburst at a present provocation; resentment is what happens when that outburst isn't released but instead is bound into a lasting grudge; vexation is what happens later still, when old grudges get triggered again by some new adverse event, producing the characteristic obstinate, scorpion-like cruelty of someone whose anger has had time to compound. All three are explicitly analyzed as nothing but hatred appearing at different moments and in different guises, not as independently existing forces — a genuinely useful psychological point: nursing a grudge doesn't introduce some new poison into the mind, it simply lets the original spark of hatred smolder and spread across time.

Concealment breaks this pattern, tracing instead to delusion (or, on the second view, delusion plus craving) rather than hatred — the debate over its exact composition is really a debate about what motivates hiding wrongdoing: pure unwillingness to face the truth (delusion), or fear of losing something one wants to keep (craving added in). Either way, the treatise's closing methodological note is worth remembering for the rest of this section: when the treatise assigns a secondary affliction to "a portion of" some fundamental affliction, that's often a claim about its most conspicuous cause, not an exhaustive account of everything contributing to it.

Envy, Stinginess, Deceit, Flattery, Harmfulness, Arrogance 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 隨煩惱 · 小隨（續）

The remaining six minor secondary afflictions complete the list — three more portions of hatred and craving, and two, deceit and flattery, jointly traced to both craving and delusion together.

The Rest of the Minor Secondary Afflictions 釋「嫉」、「慳」、「誑」、「諂」、「害」、「憍」

Chinese Source

云何為嫉？徇自名利，不耐他榮，妬忌為性；能障不嫉，憂慼為業。謂嫉妬者聞見他榮，深懷憂慼，不安隱故。此亦瞋恚一分為體，離瞋無別嫉相用故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is envy? Pursuing one's own fame and gain, unable to bear another's glory, its nature is jealousy and resentment; its function is obstructing non-envy and causing sorrow and distress. That is: one who is jealous, upon hearing or seeing another's glory, harbors deep sorrow and distress, unable to find peace. This, too, has, as its essence, simply a portion of hatred, since apart from hatred there is no separate characteristic or function of envy to be found.

云何為慳？耽著財、法，不能慧捨，祕悋為性；能障不慳，鄙畜為業。謂慳悋者，心多鄙澁，畜積財法，不能捨故。此即貪愛一分為體，離貪無別慳相用故。

What is stinginess? Indulging in attachment to wealth or to the dharma, unable to release it with wisdom, its nature is secretive hoarding; its function is obstructing non-stinginess and base accumulation. That is: one who is stingy has a mind that is largely base and ungenerous, hoarding wealth and the dharma, unable to let go of them. This has, as its essence, simply a portion of craving, since apart from craving there is no separate characteristic or function of stinginess to be found.

云何為誑？為獲利譽，矯現有德，詭詐為性；能障不誑，邪命為業。謂矯誑者，心懷異謀，多現不實邪命事故。此即貪、癡一分為體，離二無別誑相用故。云何為諂？為網他故，矯設異儀，險、曲為性；能障不諂教誨為業。謂諂曲者，為網帽他，曲順時宜，矯設方便，為取他意或藏己失。不任師、友正教誨故，此亦貪、癡一分為體，離二無別諂相用故。

What is deceit? In order to gain profit or reputation, falsely presenting oneself as possessing virtue, its nature is deceptive fraudulence; its function is obstructing non-deceit and wrongful livelihood. That is: one who practices deceit harbors ulterior schemes in the mind, frequently presenting false, wrongful means of livelihood. This has, as its essence, simply a portion of craving and delusion, since apart from these two there is no separate characteristic or function of deceit to be found.

What is flattery? In order to ensnare others, falsely putting on a different manner, its nature is treachery and crookedness; its function is obstructing non-flattery and honest instruction. That is: one who is crooked and flattering, in order to ensnare and deceive others, bends and accommodates to suit the occasion, falsely devising expedient means, either to win over another's intentions or to conceal one's own faults. Being thereby unable to accept the correct instruction of a teacher or friend, this, too, has, as its essence, simply a portion of craving and delusion, since apart from these two there is no separate characteristic or function of flattery to be found.

云何為害？於諸有情，心無悲愍，損惱為性；能障不害，逼惱為業。謂有害者，逼惱他故。此亦瞋恚一分為體，離瞋無別害相用故。瞋、害別相，准善應說。云何為憍？於自盛事，深生染著，醉傲為性；能障不憍，染依為業。謂憍醉者，生長一切雜染法故。此亦貪愛一分為體，離貪無別憍相用故。

What is harmfulness? Its nature is a mind without compassion toward sentient beings, injuring and distressing them; its function is obstructing non-harming and oppressing others with distress. That is: one who is harmful oppresses others with distress. This, too, has, as its essence, simply a portion of hatred, since apart from hatred there is no separate characteristic or function of harmfulness to be found. The distinct characteristics of hatred and harmfulness should be understood by analogy with non-hatred and non-harming, discussed above among the wholesome factors.

What is arrogance? Its nature is deep attachment to one's own flourishing circumstances, becoming intoxicated with pride; its function is obstructing non-arrogance and serving as the basis for defilement. That is: one intoxicated with arrogance generates and nourishes every kind of defiled dharma. This, too, has, as its essence, simply a portion of craving, since apart from craving there is no separate characteristic or function of arrogance to be found.

Lack of Shame and Lack of Conscience 釋「無慚」、「無愧」

Chinese Source

云何無慚？不顧自、法，輕拒賢善為性；能障礙慚，生長惡行為業。謂於自、法無所顧者，輕拒賢善，不恥過惡，障慚生長，諸惡行故。云何無愧？不顧世間，崇重暴惡為性；能障礙愧，生長惡行為業。謂於世間無所顧者，崇重暴惡，不恥過罪，障愧生長，諸惡行故。不恥過惡，是二通相；故諸聖教，假說為體。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is lack of shame? Its nature is disregarding self-respect and regard for the dharma, disdaining and rejecting what is worthy and good; its function is obstructing shame and nourishing wrongdoing. That is: one who disregards self-respect and regard for the dharma disdains and rejects what is worthy and good, feels no shame over faults and misdeeds, and thereby obstructs shame and nourishes all wrongdoing.

What is lack of conscience? Its nature is disregarding worldly opinion, honoring and esteeming violence and wrongdoing; its function is obstructing conscience and nourishing wrongdoing. That is: one who disregards worldly opinion honors and esteems violence and wrongdoing, feels no embarrassment over faults and transgressions, and thereby obstructs conscience and nourishes all wrongdoing.

Feeling no shame over one's faults is a characteristic shared by both of these — hence the scriptures sometimes speak of this shared feature as though it were their defining essence.

若執不恥為二別相，則應此二體無差別。由斯二法應不俱生，非受、想等有此義故；若待自、他，立二別者，應非實有，便違聖教；若許此二實而別起，復違論說俱遍惡心。不善心時，隨緣何境，皆有輕拒善及崇重惡義故，此二法俱遍惡心，所緣不異，無別起失。然諸聖教說不顧自、他者，自、法名自，世間名他。或即此中拒善、崇惡，於己益損，名自、他故。而論說為貪等分者，是彼等流，非即彼性。

Should one instead hold that feeling no shame is itself the two factors' distinct defining characteristic — then these two dharmas would have no real difference in essence at all. By this, the two dharmas should then certainly never be mutually associated together, since no two factors sharing an identical essence, such as feeling and perception, could ever coexist in this way. Should one instead establish the two as distinct with reference to self versus others — this would mean they are not genuinely, substantially real, which would contradict the scriptures' own teaching. Should one grant, instead, that these two are genuinely real and arise as distinct factors — this would again contradict the treatise's statement that both pervade every unwholesome mind equally.

Whenever an unwholesome mind arises, whatever object it takes, it always involves both disdaining the good and honoring the bad — hence these two dharmas are both universal to every unwholesome mind, without any difference in object between them, and so no fault arises from holding them to be distinct.

The scriptures, however, do speak of "disregard for self, and for others": "self" refers to self-respect and regard for the dharma; "others" refers to worldly opinion. Or else, within this very framework, rejecting the good and honoring the bad, insofar as they benefit or harm oneself, are themselves what is meant by "self" and "others." As for the treatise's classifying these as portions of craving and the like — this refers to their being mere outflows of craving and the rest, not that they are simply identical with these in essence.

Reading note: These six complete a striking pattern the treatise has been building across every minor secondary affliction: none of them turns out to be a genuinely new psychological ingredient. Each is craving, hatred, or delusion (or a combination) wearing a specific social costume — envy is hatred triggered by comparison, stinginess is craving refusing to release its grip, deceit and flattery are craving and delusion working together to manipulate others' perceptions, harmfulness is hatred with no compassion left to restrain it, arrogance is craving intoxicated with one's own good fortune. The taxonomy is, in effect, an argument that the entire visible landscape of everyday vice reduces to just three underlying roots, endlessly recombined and redirected.

Lack of shame and lack of conscience mirror shame and conscience exactly, right down to the same structural puzzle (two factors sharing an identical felt quality but needing to be genuinely distinct) and the same solution (locate the real difference in orientation — toward self-respect versus toward worldly opinion — rather than in the shared feeling itself). It's worth noticing that the treatise resolves this recurring type of puzzle the same way every time it comes up: not by inventing a new distinguishing feeling, but by pointing to what a factor is oriented toward or grounded in.

Restlessness, Torpor, and Lack of Faith 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 隨煩惱 · 大隨（一）

The eight major secondary afflictions pervade every defiled mind without exception. The treatise opens with three of the most consequential for meditation practice specifically: restlessness and torpor, which directly oppose the two pillars of calm-abiding and insight, and lack of faith, which — like its opposite, faith — is defined by the mind's own quality rather than by any specific object.

Restlessness: Does It Reduce to Craving? 釋「掉舉」

Chinese Source

云何掉舉？令心於境不寂靜為性；能障行捨、奢摩他為業。有義：掉舉貪一分攝，論唯說此是貪分故，此由憶昔樂事生故。有義：掉舉非唯貪攝，論說掉舉遍染心故，又掉舉相謂不寂靜，說是煩惱共相攝故，掉舉離此無別相故。雖依一切煩惱假立，而貪位增，說為貪分。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is restlessness? Its nature is causing the mind to be unsettled with respect to its object; its function is obstructing equanimity and calm-abiding.

One view holds: restlessness is included as a portion of craving, since the treatise speaks of it only as a portion of craving — it arises through recollecting past pleasurable matters.

Another view holds: restlessness is not exclusively included under craving, since the treatise states that restlessness pervades every defiled mind; furthermore, restlessness's own characteristic is simply being unsettled, which is said to be a shared characteristic common to every affliction — apart from this, restlessness has no separate characteristic of its own. Although it is provisionally established with reference to every affliction generally, since it intensifies especially in states of craving, it is spoken of as a portion of craving.

有義：掉舉別有自性，遍諸染心，如不信等，非說他分，體便非實，勿不信等亦假有故。而論說為世俗有者，如睡眠等，隨他相說。掉舉別相，謂即囂動，令俱生法，不寂靜故。若離煩惱無別此相，不應別說障奢摩他，故不寂靜非此別相。

Another view holds: restlessness has its own distinct nature, pervading every defiled mind, just as lack of faith and the rest do — one should not, merely because it is spoken of as a portion of something else, conclude that its essence is thereby unreal, lest lack of faith and the rest, too, would have to be considered merely conventional. As for the treatise's calling it conventionally existent, this is spoken with reference to its resembling another factor's characteristic, just as with sleep and the like. Restlessness's own distinct characteristic is precisely agitation, causing the dharmas that accompany it to be unsettled. If restlessness had no distinct characteristic of its own apart from the afflictions in general, the treatise should not have separately stated that it obstructs calm-abiding specifically — hence "unsettled" cannot be its distinct characteristic.

Torpor: Heavy and Dull, Not Simply Delusion 釋「惛沈」

Chinese Source

云何惛沈？令心於境，無堪任為性；能障輕安、毘鉢舍那為業。有義：惛沈癡一分攝，論唯說此是癡分故，惛昧沈重是癡相故。有義：惛沈非但癡攝，謂無堪任是惛沈相，一切煩惱皆無堪任，離此無別惛沈相故。雖依一切煩惱假立，而癡相增，但說癡分。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is torpor? Its nature is causing the mind to be unfit for use with respect to its object; its function is obstructing pliancy and insight.

One view holds: torpor is included as a portion of delusion, since the treatise speaks of it only as a portion of delusion — being benighted and heavy is delusion's own characteristic.

Another view holds: torpor is not exclusively included under delusion, since being unfit for use is torpor's characteristic, and every affliction is, in some sense, unfit for use — apart from this, torpor has no separate characteristic of its own. Although it is provisionally established with reference to every affliction generally, since delusion's characteristic intensifies especially within it, it is spoken of merely as a portion of delusion.

有義：惛沈別有自性，雖名癡分而是等流，如不信等，非即癡攝。隨他相說名世俗有，如睡眠等，是實有性。惛沈別相，謂即瞢重，令俱生法，無堪任故。若離煩惱無別惛沈相，不應別說障毘鉢舍那故。無堪任非此別相，此與癡相有差別者，謂癡於境，迷闇為相，正障無癡，而非瞢重，惛沈於境瞢重為相，正障輕安而非迷闇。

Another view holds: torpor has its own distinct nature — although called "a portion of delusion," it is in fact simply delusion's outflow, just as lack of faith and the rest are, not identical with delusion itself. It is said to be conventionally existent with reference to its resembling another factor's characteristic, just as with sleep — but its actual nature is genuinely real. Torpor's own distinct characteristic is precisely heaviness and dullness, causing the dharmas that accompany it to be unfit for use. If torpor had no distinct characteristic of its own apart from the afflictions in general, the treatise should not have separately stated that it obstructs insight specifically — hence "unfit for use" cannot be its distinct characteristic either.

The difference between torpor and delusion's own characteristic is this: delusion has, as its characteristic, being benighted and in the dark with respect to its object, directly obstructing non-delusion, without necessarily being heavy and dull; torpor has, as its characteristic, being heavy and dull with respect to its object, directly obstructing pliancy, without necessarily being benighted.

Lack of Faith: The Mind's Own Turbidity 釋「不信」

Chinese Source

云何不信？於實、德、能不忍樂欲，心穢為性；能障淨信，惰依為業。謂不信者多懈怠故。不信三相，翻信應知。然諸染法，各有別相，唯此不信，自相渾濁，復能渾濁餘心、心所，如極穢物，自穢、穢他，是故說此心穢為性。由不信故，於實、德、能不忍樂欲，非別有性，若於餘事，邪忍樂欲，是此因果，非此自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is lack of faith? Its nature is the mind's own turbidity, lacking acceptance, delight, and aspiration with respect to what is real, what possesses virtue, and what has capability; its function is obstructing pure faith and serving as the basis for laziness. That is: one who lacks faith is largely lazy as a result.

Lack of faith's three aspects should be understood as the reverse of faith's three. Yet whereas every other defiled dharma has its own distinct characteristic, it is lack of faith alone whose own characteristic is itself turbid and murky, and which is, moreover, capable of further muddying the other mental factors and mind that accompany it — just as an especially filthy object both is itself filthy and makes other things filthy too — and this is why it is said to have the mind's own turbidity as its defining nature.

It is because of this lack of faith that one lacks acceptance, delight, and aspiration with respect to what is real, what possesses virtue, and what has capability — this is not some further, separate characteristic; and if, with respect to other matters, one has a wrongful acceptance, delight, or aspiration, that is this factor's cause or effect, not its own essential nature.

Reading note: Restlessness and torpor form a natural pair worth holding together: they are the two ways a mind in meditation can fail, in exactly opposite directions — restlessness is excess energy the mind can't settle, torpor is a deficit of energy the mind can't lift. It's fitting that they obstruct the two respective pillars of meditative development: restlessness blocks calm-abiding (which requires settling), torpor blocks insight (which requires alertness). Both debates over their "true nature" turn on the same underlying question already seen with heedfulness and equanimity: is this a compound name for a shared feature of every affliction, or a genuinely distinct thing? Here the treatise comes down on the side of genuine distinctness for both, on the strength of a specific, targeted function (obstructing calm-abiding specifically, or insight specifically) that a merely generic "unsettled" or "unfit for use" quality shared by every affliction could not explain.

Lack of faith's definition deliberately echoes faith's own definition from earlier in this fascicle — both are defined by the mind's own quality (clarity versus turbidity) rather than by content, which is the more revealing choice: this isn't a specific false belief, it's a pervasive, self-perpetuating cloudiness that, like an actually filthy object, contaminates whatever else is nearby. The parallel to faith is exact and intentional — the treatise is showing that lack of faith isn't faith's absence so much as its active opposite, doing to the mind's clarity what faith does to purify it, but in reverse.
