Fascicle 2 · 成唯識論卷第二

Even "Arising and Ceasing" Are Only Names

Fascicle 2 opens by finishing what fascicle 1 started — a few last realist categories to clear away — before the treatise turns, for the first time, from tearing down other positions to building up its own: the case for the ālayavijñāna, the storehouse consciousness.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T31, no. 1585 Covers: the four conditioned characteristics through the refutation of unconditioned dharmas

Picking Up Where Fascicle 1 Left Off

The Taishō scroll boundary falls mid-argument: fascicle 1 finished the two mindless attainments, and fascicle 2 opens by finishing the same list of "forces disjoined from mind" with one more entry — the four characteristics of conditioned existence (生住異滅: arising, abiding, change, and ceasing). Only once every candidate for a real, mind-independent dharma has been examined and found wanting does the treatise close out "the first three lines" of the opening verse and move to "the latter three lines" — which is where the positive theory of mind actually begins.

Translation conventions carry over unchanged from fascicle 1; see the translation notes for the running glossary.

Are Arising, Abiding, Change, and Ceasing Real Things? 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十二）· 破餘乘（七）

Sarvāstivāda holds that every conditioned dharma carries four real, attached characteristics that mark its passage through time: arising (生), abiding (住), change (異), and ceasing (滅). The treatise refutes these as separately real, then offers its own account of why we still find it useful to speak this way.

Chinese Source

復如何知，諸有為相異色、心等，有實自性？契經說故。如契經說：有三有為之有為相，乃至廣說。此經不說異色、心等有實自性，為證不成。非第六聲便表異體，色、心之體即色、心故。非能相體定異所相，勿堅相等異地等故。若有為相異所相體，無為相體應異所相。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, further, is it known that the characteristics of conditioned dharmas have a real nature distinct from form, mind, and the rest? Because scripture says so — as when scripture says, "there are three characteristics of the conditioned," and so on. But scripture never says these have a real nature apart from form, mind, and the rest — cited as proof, it proves nothing.

The mere grammatical possessive — "the characteristics of the conditioned" — does not by itself indicate some separate entity; the substance of form and mind simply is form and mind. Nor is a characteristic-marker necessarily different in substance from what it marks, any more than "solidity" is different from earth itself. And if the characteristics of the conditioned really were different in substance from what they characterize, the characteristic of the unconditioned should equally be different from it — which no one holds.

又生等相，若體俱有，應一切時齊興作用；若相違故，用不頓興，體亦相違，如何俱有？又住、異、滅用不應俱。能相、所相體俱本有，用亦應然，無別性故。若謂彼用更待因緣，所待因緣應非本有。又執生等便為無用。所相恒有而生等合，應無為法亦有生等，彼此異因，不可得故。

Furthermore: if arising and the other three characteristics all exist simultaneously as substances, their functions should all operate at once. But if their functions cannot operate all at once because they contradict one another, then their substances must be equally contradictory — so how could they all exist together in the first place? By the same token, abiding, change, and ceasing should not be able to function together either.

If marker and marked both exist inherently, their functions should exist inherently too, since substance and function are not held to differ. If you say their function still depends on further causes and conditions, then those further conditions cannot themselves be inherently existent — and positing "arising" and the rest becomes pointless work, since the further conditions are doing the real causing. And if what is characterized exists eternally, with arising and the rest merely attaching to it, then unconditioned dharmas — equally eternal — should have arising and the rest too, and no reason can be found for why they alone are exempt.

又去、來世，非現、非常，應似空花，非實有性。生名為有，寧在未來？滅名為無，應非現在。滅若非無，生應非有。又滅違住，寧執同時？住不違生，何容異世。故彼所執，進退非理。

Furthermore: the past and future, being neither present nor permanent, should be like a sky-flower — not really existent. If "arising" means "to exist," how could it belong to the future, where nothing yet exists on their own account? If "ceasing" means "to not exist," it should not belong to the present. And if ceasing is not simply nonexistence, then arising, by the same logic, should not simply be existence either. If ceasing is incompatible with abiding, how can the two be held to occur in the same moment — yet abiding is not incompatible with arising, so why force the two into different times? Whichever way their position turns, it does not hold up.

What's Really Going On: A Provisional Account 四相皆是假立

Chinese Source

然有為法，因緣力故，本無今有，暫有還無，表異無為，假立四相。本無今有，有位名生；生位暫停，即說為住；住別前後，復立異名；暫有還無，無時名滅。前三有故，同在現在，後一是無，故在過去。如何無法與有為相？表此後無，為相何失？

Ru-Yi Translation

In fact, conditioned dharmas, through the power of causes and conditions, are originally nonexistent and now exist, and, having existed for a while, return to nonexistence; and, to mark their difference from the unconditioned, the four characteristics are provisionally established. "Originally nonexistent, now existing" — this moment of coming to exist is called arising. The stage where what has arisen briefly persists is called abiding. Distinguishing earlier from later within that abiding gives the further name "change." "Having existed for a while, then returning to nonexistence" — the moment of no longer existing is called ceasing.

Since the first three involve existence, they belong alike to the present; the last is a nonexistence, and so belongs to the past. But how can a nonexistence serve as a "characteristic" at all? — It marks the subsequent nonexistence of that dharma; there is no fault in that.

生表有法先非有，滅表有法後是無，異表此法非凝然，住表此法暫有用。故此四相，於有為法，雖俱名表，而表有異。此依剎那假立四相，一期分位亦得假立。初有名生；後無名滅；生已相似相續名住；即此相續轉變名異。是故四相皆是假立。

"Arising" marks that an existent dharma did not exist before; "ceasing" marks that it will not exist afterward; "change" marks that it is not static and frozen; "abiding" marks that it has, for a while, an active function. So although all four characteristics alike mark conditioned dharmas, what each one marks is different.

This account applies the four characteristics to a single instant; but they can equally be applied, provisionally, to an entire lifespan: the first moment of existing is "arising," the final nonexistence is "ceasing," the similar, continuous succession after arising is "abiding," and the gradual transformation within that very continuity is "change." The four characteristics, in every case, are nothing more than provisional constructions.

Reading note: This has real practical stakes beyond the abstract debate. If arising, abiding, and ceasing were separately real "things" attached to every dharma, impermanence would be a feature imposed on phenomena from outside. Here, instead, the four marks are just how we describe the plain fact that conditioned things don't stay the same from moment to moment — an observation, not an extra layer of reality bolted onto experience. It's worth noticing, too, that the treatise offers a two-scale account almost in passing: the same four words describe both a single instant and an entire lifespan, which is exactly how "impermanence" gets used in ordinary Buddhist teaching — sometimes about the breath-by-breath flicker of experience, sometimes about a whole life arising, lasting, changing, and ending.

Meaning Doesn't Need Its Own Special Substance 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十三）· 破餘乘（八）

Realist schools also hold that "name," "phrase," and "syllable" — the units that let sound carry meaning — are real entities distinct from sound itself. The treatise argues that sound's own physical structure already does all the expressive work required, closing the last item on its list of forces disjoined from mind.

Chinese Source

復如何知異色、心等，有實詮表名、句、文身？契經說故。如契經說，佛得希有名、句、文身。此經不說異色、心等有實名等，為證不成。若名、句、文異聲實有，應如色等，非實能詮。謂聲能生名、句、文者，此聲必有音韻屈曲，此足能詮，何用名等。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, further, is it known that there is a real, expressive "name-body," "phrase-body," and "syllable-body" distinct from form, mind, and the rest? Because scripture says so — as when scripture says the Buddha attained a rare name-body, phrase-body, and syllable-body. But scripture never says these really exist apart from form, mind, and the rest — cited as proof, it proves nothing.

If name, phrase, and syllable really exist as something different from sound, they should, like form, be incapable of actually expressing meaning. If you say sound gives rise to name, phrase, and syllable, then the sound must already carry modulation and inflection — and that alone should be enough to express meaning. What further need is there for "names" on top of it?

若謂聲上音韻屈曲即名、句、文，異聲實有，所見色上形量屈曲，應異色處別有實體。若謂聲上音韻屈曲，如絃管聲非能詮者，此應如彼聲，不別生名等。又誰說彼定不能詮？聲若能詮，風鈴聲等應有詮用，此應如彼不別生實名、句、文身。若唯語聲能生名等，如何不許唯語能詮。何理定知能詮即語？寧知異語別有能詮？語不異能詮，人天共了，執能詮異語，天愛非餘。

If you say the modulation and inflection within sound just is name, phrase, and syllable, really existing as something different from sound — then the shape seen within color should equally be a real, separate entity apart from color, which no one holds. If you say that modulation and inflection, like the sound of a stringed or wind instrument, cannot by itself express meaning — then ordinary speech-sound, by the same logic, should not separately generate names either. And who says instrumental sound definitely cannot express meaning? If any sound can express meaning, even a wind-chime should have expressive power, and speech-sound, just like it, would need no separate real "name-body" to do the expressing.

If only speech-sound can generate names, why not simply grant that speech alone expresses meaning? By what reasoning could you be certain that what expresses meaning is something other than speech itself? That speech is what expresses meaning — not something apart from it — is something ordinary people and gods alike already understand; insisting otherwise is nobody's view but the insister's own.

What Name, Phrase, and Syllable Actually Are 名句文身假立

然依語聲分位差別，而假建立名、句、文身。名詮自性；句詮差別；文即是字，為二所依。此三離聲雖無別體，而假、實異，亦不即聲。由此法、詞二無礙解，境有差別；聲與名等，蘊、處、界攝亦各有異。且依此土說名、句、文依聲假立，非謂一切；諸餘佛土亦依光明、妙香、味等假立三故。

Rather, based on distinct conditions within speech-sound, "name-body," "phrase-body," and "syllable-body" are provisionally established. A name expresses a thing's own nature; a phrase expresses relations between things; a syllable is simply the spoken or written letter, the basis for both of the others. Though these three have no substance apart from sound, they are provisional while sound is real, so they cannot simply be identified with sound either — which is why the two kinds of unobstructed understanding, of reality and of language, take different objects, and why sound and name are classified differently among the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements, even though one is built entirely on the other.

This account — that name, phrase, and syllable rest on sound — holds for our world; it is not meant universally. In other buddha-lands, the same three may just as well be provisionally built on light, or fragrance, or taste instead.

Reading note: This is, in effect, an ancient argument about philosophy of language: does meaning require its own special kind of stuff — an abstract "name" riding on top of mere sound — or can the physical structure of an utterance (its modulation, its inflection, its pattern) carry meaning all by itself? The treatise takes the second position and backs it with a clean test: if instrumental music, which nobody thinks is meaningful, has exactly the same kind of acoustic modulation as speech, then modulation alone can't be what makes speech meaningful — and if modulation isn't enough, positing a further invisible "name-entity" riding on top of it doesn't actually solve anything either. The line about other buddha-lands building meaning out of light or fragrance instead of sound is a small but telling detail: the point was never that sound specifically is metaphysically special, only that some physical medium, structured the right way, is all meaning ever needs.

Closing the List 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十四）· 破餘乘（九）

Chinese Source

有執隨眠異心、心所，是不相應行蘊所攝。彼亦非理，名貪等故，如現貪等，非不相應。執別有餘不相應行，准前理趣皆應遮止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some hold that latent tendencies (anuśaya) are different from mind and mental factors, and belong among the forces disjoined from mind. This too is unreasonable: they are called "craving" and the like — the very same names used for manifest craving — and manifest craving is certainly not disjoined from mind. Whatever other forces disjoined from mind anyone might still wish to insist on, beyond what has already been discussed, should be turned back by this same line of reasoning.

Reading note: A latent tendency is craving or aversion in its dormant form — not currently active, but still shaping the mind-stream from beneath the surface. The treatise's point is simple: calling it "latent craving" already concedes that it's a form of craving, and craving is uncontroversially mental. Positing a special disjoined-from-mind category just to house its dormant phase adds nothing. With this brief dismissal, the treatise closes its entire list of forces disjoined from mind — the last realist category standing between the reader and the treatise's central positive claim: that consciousness alone, transforming, accounts for everything examined so far.

Space, Cessation, and Suchness: One Reality, Five Names 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十五）· 破餘乘（十）

Beyond the forces disjoined from mind, realist schools posit a further category entirely: unconditioned dharmas (無為法) — space, and several kinds of cessation, held to be eternal, real, and untouched by causation. This is the last realist category in fascicle 2's refutation, and it resolves into one of the treatise's most quietly beautiful teachings.

Nothing Qualifies as "Definitely Real" 破無為定實有

Chinese Source

諸無為法離色、心等決定實有，理不可得。且定有法，略有三種：一、現所知法，如色、心等。二、現受用法，如瓶、衣等，如是二法，世共知有，不待因成。三、有作用法，如眼、耳等，由彼彼用證知是有。無為非世共知定有，又無作用，如眼、耳等。設許有用，應是無常，故不可執無為定有。然諸無為所知性故，或色、心等所顯性故，如色、心等，不應執為離色、心等實無為性。

Ru-Yi Translation

That unconditioned dharmas really exist, apart from form, mind, and the rest, cannot be established on examination. Things that are definitely existent are, broadly, of three kinds: things directly known, like form and mind; things directly experienced and used, like a pot or a piece of cloth — these two kinds are commonly known to exist by everyone, without needing proof by inference; and things that have a function, like the eye and ear faculties, whose existence is proven by that very function. The unconditioned is not commonly known by everyone to definitely exist, nor does it have any function like the eye or ear — and if it did have a function, it would have to be impermanent. The unconditioned, then, cannot be held to definitely, really exist as a separate substance. Rather, it is simply a knowable nature, or a nature revealed by form, mind, and the rest — like form and mind themselves — and should not be treated as some real, separate "unconditioned" thing.

又虛空等為一、為多？若體是一，遍一切處，虛空容受色等法故，隨能合法，體應成多，一所合處餘不合故。不爾，諸法應互相遍。若謂虛空不與法合，應非容受，如餘無為。又色等中有虛空不？有應相雜，無應不遍。一部、一品結法斷時，應得餘部、餘品擇滅；一法緣闕得不生時，應於一切得非擇滅。執彼體一，理應爾故。

Furthermore: is space one thing, or many? If its substance is a single unity pervading everywhere, then, since space is held to accommodate form and the other dharmas within it, it should become multiple in step with the many dharmas it accommodates — the place it joins with one thing is not the place it joins with another. Deny this, and every dharma should end up pervading every other dharma's place, which is absurd. And if space does not actually join with dharmas at all, then it does not really accommodate them either — no different, in that respect, from any other unconditioned dharma.

Furthermore: is there space within form itself, or not? If there is, space and form should intermingle; if not, space should not be all-pervading after all. The same problem recurs for cessation through discernment: severing the afflictions of one class should then yield the cessation belonging to every other class at once; and a single missing condition should yield universal cessation-without-discernment for everything at once. This is simply what follows from insisting that each is a single, undivided substance.

若體是多，便有品類，應如色等，非實無為。虛空又應非遍容受。餘部所執離心、心所實有無為，准前應破。又諸無為許無因果故，應如兔角，非異心等有。

If, instead, its substance is multiple, it has different kinds and classes, and so, like form, should not be a real unconditioned dharma — and space should then no longer be able to accommodate everything universally either. Whatever other unconditioned dharmas any other school posits as really existing apart from mind and mental factors should be refuted by this same reasoning. And since the unconditioned is granted to have neither cause nor effect, it should, like the horns of a rabbit, not be held to exist as anything different from mind at all.

What "Unconditioned" Actually Points To 無為假立二種

Chinese Source

然契經說，有虛空等諸無為法，略有二種：一、依識變假施設有。謂曾聞說虛空等名，隨分別有虛空等相，數習力故，心等生時，似虛空等無為相現。此所現相，前後相似，無有變易，假說為常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Scripture does speak of space and the other unconditioned dharmas as existing — but this happens in two distinct ways. First, they are provisionally posited based on what consciousness transforms: having once heard the term "space" and formed a conceptual image accordingly, one then, through the power of repeated habituation, has a mind that manifests an appearance resembling the unconditioned characteristic of "space" whenever it arises. Because this manifested appearance stays similar from one moment to the next, without any real change, it is provisionally called "permanent."

二、依法性，假施設有。謂空、無我所顯真如，有、無、俱非，心言路絕，與一切法非一、異等，是法真理，故名法性。離諸障礙，故名虛空；由簡擇力，滅諸雜染，究竟證會，故名擇滅；不由擇力，本性清淨，或緣闕所顯，故名非擇滅；苦、樂受滅，故名不動；想、受不行，名想受滅。此五皆依真如假立，真如亦是假施設名。

Second, they are provisionally posited based on the true nature of dharmas. That is: the suchness revealed by the emptiness of self and of dharmas is neither existent, nor nonexistent, nor both; it lies beyond the reach of thought and language; it is neither identical with nor different from any dharma. Being the true principle underlying all dharmas, it is called "the nature of dharmas."

This very suchness, free of every obstruction, is called "space." Realized ultimately through the power of discerning wisdom that extinguishes all defilement, it is called "cessation through discernment." Originally pure not through the power of discernment but simply revealed through the absence of a condition, it is called "cessation without discernment." Where the sensation of suffering and of pleasure has ceased, it is called "the unmoving." Where perception and sensation no longer operate, it is called "the cessation of perception and sensation." All five of these names are provisionally established based on suchness — and "suchness" itself is likewise only a provisionally established name.

遮撥為無，故說為有；遮執為有，故說為空；勿謂虛、幻，故說為實；理非妄倒，故名真如。不同餘宗離色、心等有實常法名曰真如。故諸無為非定實有。

To block the denial that it exists at all, it is called "existent." To block the attachment that it exists as some separate real thing, it is called "empty." So that it not be mistaken for something merely illusory, it is called "real." Because its principle is free of delusion and distortion, it is called "suchness." This is nothing like other schools' "suchness" — some really existent, eternal thing they hold to stand apart from form, mind, and the rest. Unconditioned dharmas, therefore, are not definitely, really existent as separate substances.

Reading note: This passage resolves a whole list of Abhidharma's "unconditioned" categories — space, two kinds of cessation, motionlessness, cessation of perception-and-feeling — into one reality named five different ways, depending on which angle is in view: called "space" when emphasizing its freedom from all obstruction, "cessation through discernment" when emphasizing wisdom's role in reaching it, "cessation without discernment" when emphasizing that it needed no such effort, and so on. None of these names points to a separate metaphysical object; all of them point back to suchness (真如, tathatā) — which the treatise immediately adds is itself just a provisional name, not a hidden eternal substance either.

The four-part gloss at the end is worth sitting with slowly: "existent" to block nihilism, "empty" to block reification, "real" to block dismissing it as illusion, "suchness" to name that this whole account is not itself a distortion. Every term is doing corrective work against a specific mistake, not describing a thing. With this, fascicle 2 has now closed every realist category examined since fascicle 1 began — self, the forces disjoined from mind, and now the unconditioned. What remains is to explain what actually accounts for everything just cleared away.
