Fascicle 2 · 成唯識論卷第二

Even "Arising and Ceasing" Are Only Names

Fascicle 2 opens by finishing what fascicle 1 started — a few last realist categories to clear away — before the treatise turns, for the first time, from tearing down other positions to building up its own: the case for the ālayavijñāna, the storehouse consciousness.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T31, no. 1585 Covers: the four-part model of cognition, and how private minds share one world

Picking Up Where Fascicle 1 Left Off

The Taishō scroll boundary falls mid-argument: fascicle 1 finished the two mindless attainments, and fascicle 2 opens by finishing the same list of "forces disjoined from mind" with one more entry — the four characteristics of conditioned existence (生住異滅: arising, abiding, change, and ceasing). Only once every candidate for a real, mind-independent dharma has been examined and found wanting does the treatise close out "the first three lines" of the opening verse and move to "the latter three lines" — which is where the positive theory of mind actually begins.

Translation conventions carry over unchanged from fascicle 1; see the translation notes for the running glossary.

Are Arising, Abiding, Change, and Ceasing Real Things? 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十二）· 破餘乘（七）

Sarvāstivāda holds that every conditioned dharma carries four real, attached characteristics that mark its passage through time: arising (生), abiding (住), change (異), and ceasing (滅). The treatise refutes these as separately real, then offers its own account of why we still find it useful to speak this way.

Chinese Source

復如何知，諸有為相異色、心等，有實自性？契經說故。如契經說：有三有為之有為相，乃至廣說。此經不說異色、心等有實自性，為證不成。非第六聲便表異體，色、心之體即色、心故。非能相體定異所相，勿堅相等異地等故。若有為相異所相體，無為相體應異所相。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, further, is it known that the characteristics of conditioned dharmas have a real nature distinct from form, mind, and the rest? Because scripture says so — as when scripture says, "there are three characteristics of the conditioned," and so on. But scripture never says these have a real nature apart from form, mind, and the rest — cited as proof, it proves nothing.

The mere grammatical possessive — "the characteristics of the conditioned" — does not by itself indicate some separate entity; the substance of form and mind simply is form and mind. Nor is a characteristic-marker necessarily different in substance from what it marks, any more than "solidity" is different from earth itself. And if the characteristics of the conditioned really were different in substance from what they characterize, the characteristic of the unconditioned should equally be different from it — which no one holds.

又生等相，若體俱有，應一切時齊興作用；若相違故，用不頓興，體亦相違，如何俱有？又住、異、滅用不應俱。能相、所相體俱本有，用亦應然，無別性故。若謂彼用更待因緣，所待因緣應非本有。又執生等便為無用。所相恒有而生等合，應無為法亦有生等，彼此異因，不可得故。

Furthermore: if arising and the other three characteristics all exist simultaneously as substances, their functions should all operate at once. But if their functions cannot operate all at once because they contradict one another, then their substances must be equally contradictory — so how could they all exist together in the first place? By the same token, abiding, change, and ceasing should not be able to function together either.

If marker and marked both exist inherently, their functions should exist inherently too, since substance and function are not held to differ. If you say their function still depends on further causes and conditions, then those further conditions cannot themselves be inherently existent — and positing "arising" and the rest becomes pointless work, since the further conditions are doing the real causing. And if what is characterized exists eternally, with arising and the rest merely attaching to it, then unconditioned dharmas — equally eternal — should have arising and the rest too, and no reason can be found for why they alone are exempt.

又去、來世，非現、非常，應似空花，非實有性。生名為有，寧在未來？滅名為無，應非現在。滅若非無，生應非有。又滅違住，寧執同時？住不違生，何容異世。故彼所執，進退非理。

Furthermore: the past and future, being neither present nor permanent, should be like a sky-flower — not really existent. If "arising" means "to exist," how could it belong to the future, where nothing yet exists on their own account? If "ceasing" means "to not exist," it should not belong to the present. And if ceasing is not simply nonexistence, then arising, by the same logic, should not simply be existence either. If ceasing is incompatible with abiding, how can the two be held to occur in the same moment — yet abiding is not incompatible with arising, so why force the two into different times? Whichever way their position turns, it does not hold up.

What's Really Going On: A Provisional Account 四相皆是假立

Chinese Source

然有為法，因緣力故，本無今有，暫有還無，表異無為，假立四相。本無今有，有位名生；生位暫停，即說為住；住別前後，復立異名；暫有還無，無時名滅。前三有故，同在現在，後一是無，故在過去。如何無法與有為相？表此後無，為相何失？

Ru-Yi Translation

In fact, conditioned dharmas, through the power of causes and conditions, are originally nonexistent and now exist, and, having existed for a while, return to nonexistence; and, to mark their difference from the unconditioned, the four characteristics are provisionally established. "Originally nonexistent, now existing" — this moment of coming to exist is called arising. The stage where what has arisen briefly persists is called abiding. Distinguishing earlier from later within that abiding gives the further name "change." "Having existed for a while, then returning to nonexistence" — the moment of no longer existing is called ceasing.

Since the first three involve existence, they belong alike to the present; the last is a nonexistence, and so belongs to the past. But how can a nonexistence serve as a "characteristic" at all? — It marks the subsequent nonexistence of that dharma; there is no fault in that.

生表有法先非有，滅表有法後是無，異表此法非凝然，住表此法暫有用。故此四相，於有為法，雖俱名表，而表有異。此依剎那假立四相，一期分位亦得假立。初有名生；後無名滅；生已相似相續名住；即此相續轉變名異。是故四相皆是假立。

"Arising" marks that an existent dharma did not exist before; "ceasing" marks that it will not exist afterward; "change" marks that it is not static and frozen; "abiding" marks that it has, for a while, an active function. So although all four characteristics alike mark conditioned dharmas, what each one marks is different.

This account applies the four characteristics to a single instant; but they can equally be applied, provisionally, to an entire lifespan: the first moment of existing is "arising," the final nonexistence is "ceasing," the similar, continuous succession after arising is "abiding," and the gradual transformation within that very continuity is "change." The four characteristics, in every case, are nothing more than provisional constructions.

Reading note: This has real practical stakes beyond the abstract debate. If arising, abiding, and ceasing were separately real "things" attached to every dharma, impermanence would be a feature imposed on phenomena from outside. Here, instead, the four marks are just how we describe the plain fact that conditioned things don't stay the same from moment to moment — an observation, not an extra layer of reality bolted onto experience. It's worth noticing, too, that the treatise offers a two-scale account almost in passing: the same four words describe both a single instant and an entire lifespan, which is exactly how "impermanence" gets used in ordinary Buddhist teaching — sometimes about the breath-by-breath flicker of experience, sometimes about a whole life arising, lasting, changing, and ending.

Meaning Doesn't Need Its Own Special Substance 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十三）· 破餘乘（八）

Realist schools also hold that "name," "phrase," and "syllable" — the units that let sound carry meaning — are real entities distinct from sound itself. The treatise argues that sound's own physical structure already does all the expressive work required, closing the last item on its list of forces disjoined from mind.

Chinese Source

復如何知異色、心等，有實詮表名、句、文身？契經說故。如契經說，佛得希有名、句、文身。此經不說異色、心等有實名等，為證不成。若名、句、文異聲實有，應如色等，非實能詮。謂聲能生名、句、文者，此聲必有音韻屈曲，此足能詮，何用名等。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, further, is it known that there is a real, expressive "name-body," "phrase-body," and "syllable-body" distinct from form, mind, and the rest? Because scripture says so — as when scripture says the Buddha attained a rare name-body, phrase-body, and syllable-body. But scripture never says these really exist apart from form, mind, and the rest — cited as proof, it proves nothing.

If name, phrase, and syllable really exist as something different from sound, they should, like form, be incapable of actually expressing meaning. If you say sound gives rise to name, phrase, and syllable, then the sound must already carry modulation and inflection — and that alone should be enough to express meaning. What further need is there for "names" on top of it?

若謂聲上音韻屈曲即名、句、文，異聲實有，所見色上形量屈曲，應異色處別有實體。若謂聲上音韻屈曲，如絃管聲非能詮者，此應如彼聲，不別生名等。又誰說彼定不能詮？聲若能詮，風鈴聲等應有詮用，此應如彼不別生實名、句、文身。若唯語聲能生名等，如何不許唯語能詮。何理定知能詮即語？寧知異語別有能詮？語不異能詮，人天共了，執能詮異語，天愛非餘。

If you say the modulation and inflection within sound just is name, phrase, and syllable, really existing as something different from sound — then the shape seen within color should equally be a real, separate entity apart from color, which no one holds. If you say that modulation and inflection, like the sound of a stringed or wind instrument, cannot by itself express meaning — then ordinary speech-sound, by the same logic, should not separately generate names either. And who says instrumental sound definitely cannot express meaning? If any sound can express meaning, even a wind-chime should have expressive power, and speech-sound, just like it, would need no separate real "name-body" to do the expressing.

If only speech-sound can generate names, why not simply grant that speech alone expresses meaning? By what reasoning could you be certain that what expresses meaning is something other than speech itself? That speech is what expresses meaning — not something apart from it — is something ordinary people and gods alike already understand; insisting otherwise is nobody's view but the insister's own.

What Name, Phrase, and Syllable Actually Are 名句文身假立

然依語聲分位差別，而假建立名、句、文身。名詮自性；句詮差別；文即是字，為二所依。此三離聲雖無別體，而假、實異，亦不即聲。由此法、詞二無礙解，境有差別；聲與名等，蘊、處、界攝亦各有異。且依此土說名、句、文依聲假立，非謂一切；諸餘佛土亦依光明、妙香、味等假立三故。

Rather, based on distinct conditions within speech-sound, "name-body," "phrase-body," and "syllable-body" are provisionally established. A name expresses a thing's own nature; a phrase expresses relations between things; a syllable is simply the spoken or written letter, the basis for both of the others. Though these three have no substance apart from sound, they are provisional while sound is real, so they cannot simply be identified with sound either — which is why the two kinds of unobstructed understanding, of reality and of language, take different objects, and why sound and name are classified differently among the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements, even though one is built entirely on the other.

This account — that name, phrase, and syllable rest on sound — holds for our world; it is not meant universally. In other buddha-lands, the same three may just as well be provisionally built on light, or fragrance, or taste instead.

Reading note: This is, in effect, an ancient argument about philosophy of language: does meaning require its own special kind of stuff — an abstract "name" riding on top of mere sound — or can the physical structure of an utterance (its modulation, its inflection, its pattern) carry meaning all by itself? The treatise takes the second position and backs it with a clean test: if instrumental music, which nobody thinks is meaningful, has exactly the same kind of acoustic modulation as speech, then modulation alone can't be what makes speech meaningful — and if modulation isn't enough, positing a further invisible "name-entity" riding on top of it doesn't actually solve anything either. The line about other buddha-lands building meaning out of light or fragrance instead of sound is a small but telling detail: the point was never that sound specifically is metaphysically special, only that some physical medium, structured the right way, is all meaning ever needs.

Closing the List 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十四）· 破餘乘（九）

Chinese Source

有執隨眠異心、心所，是不相應行蘊所攝。彼亦非理，名貪等故，如現貪等，非不相應。執別有餘不相應行，准前理趣皆應遮止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some hold that latent tendencies (anuśaya) are different from mind and mental factors, and belong among the forces disjoined from mind. This too is unreasonable: they are called "craving" and the like — the very same names used for manifest craving — and manifest craving is certainly not disjoined from mind. Whatever other forces disjoined from mind anyone might still wish to insist on, beyond what has already been discussed, should be turned back by this same line of reasoning.

Reading note: A latent tendency is craving or aversion in its dormant form — not currently active, but still shaping the mind-stream from beneath the surface. The treatise's point is simple: calling it "latent craving" already concedes that it's a form of craving, and craving is uncontroversially mental. Positing a special disjoined-from-mind category just to house its dormant phase adds nothing. With this brief dismissal, the treatise closes its entire list of forces disjoined from mind — the last realist category standing between the reader and the treatise's central positive claim: that consciousness alone, transforming, accounts for everything examined so far.

Space, Cessation, and Suchness: One Reality, Five Names 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十五）· 破餘乘（十）

Beyond the forces disjoined from mind, realist schools posit a further category entirely: unconditioned dharmas (無為法) — space, and several kinds of cessation, held to be eternal, real, and untouched by causation. This is the last realist category in fascicle 2's refutation, and it resolves into one of the treatise's most quietly beautiful teachings.

Nothing Qualifies as "Definitely Real" 破無為定實有

Chinese Source

諸無為法離色、心等決定實有，理不可得。且定有法，略有三種：一、現所知法，如色、心等。二、現受用法，如瓶、衣等，如是二法，世共知有，不待因成。三、有作用法，如眼、耳等，由彼彼用證知是有。無為非世共知定有，又無作用，如眼、耳等。設許有用，應是無常，故不可執無為定有。然諸無為所知性故，或色、心等所顯性故，如色、心等，不應執為離色、心等實無為性。

Ru-Yi Translation

That unconditioned dharmas really exist, apart from form, mind, and the rest, cannot be established on examination. Things that are definitely existent are, broadly, of three kinds: things directly known, like form and mind; things directly experienced and used, like a pot or a piece of cloth — these two kinds are commonly known to exist by everyone, without needing proof by inference; and things that have a function, like the eye and ear faculties, whose existence is proven by that very function. The unconditioned is not commonly known by everyone to definitely exist, nor does it have any function like the eye or ear — and if it did have a function, it would have to be impermanent. The unconditioned, then, cannot be held to definitely, really exist as a separate substance. Rather, it is simply a knowable nature, or a nature revealed by form, mind, and the rest — like form and mind themselves — and should not be treated as some real, separate "unconditioned" thing.

又虛空等為一、為多？若體是一，遍一切處，虛空容受色等法故，隨能合法，體應成多，一所合處餘不合故。不爾，諸法應互相遍。若謂虛空不與法合，應非容受，如餘無為。又色等中有虛空不？有應相雜，無應不遍。一部、一品結法斷時，應得餘部、餘品擇滅；一法緣闕得不生時，應於一切得非擇滅。執彼體一，理應爾故。

Furthermore: is space one thing, or many? If its substance is a single unity pervading everywhere, then, since space is held to accommodate form and the other dharmas within it, it should become multiple in step with the many dharmas it accommodates — the place it joins with one thing is not the place it joins with another. Deny this, and every dharma should end up pervading every other dharma's place, which is absurd. And if space does not actually join with dharmas at all, then it does not really accommodate them either — no different, in that respect, from any other unconditioned dharma.

Furthermore: is there space within form itself, or not? If there is, space and form should intermingle; if not, space should not be all-pervading after all. The same problem recurs for cessation through discernment: severing the afflictions of one class should then yield the cessation belonging to every other class at once; and a single missing condition should yield universal cessation-without-discernment for everything at once. This is simply what follows from insisting that each is a single, undivided substance.

若體是多，便有品類，應如色等，非實無為。虛空又應非遍容受。餘部所執離心、心所實有無為，准前應破。又諸無為許無因果故，應如兔角，非異心等有。

If, instead, its substance is multiple, it has different kinds and classes, and so, like form, should not be a real unconditioned dharma — and space should then no longer be able to accommodate everything universally either. Whatever other unconditioned dharmas any other school posits as really existing apart from mind and mental factors should be refuted by this same reasoning. And since the unconditioned is granted to have neither cause nor effect, it should, like the horns of a rabbit, not be held to exist as anything different from mind at all.

What "Unconditioned" Actually Points To 無為假立二種

Chinese Source

然契經說，有虛空等諸無為法，略有二種：一、依識變假施設有。謂曾聞說虛空等名，隨分別有虛空等相，數習力故，心等生時，似虛空等無為相現。此所現相，前後相似，無有變易，假說為常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Scripture does speak of space and the other unconditioned dharmas as existing — but this happens in two distinct ways. First, they are provisionally posited based on what consciousness transforms: having once heard the term "space" and formed a conceptual image accordingly, one then, through the power of repeated habituation, has a mind that manifests an appearance resembling the unconditioned characteristic of "space" whenever it arises. Because this manifested appearance stays similar from one moment to the next, without any real change, it is provisionally called "permanent."

二、依法性，假施設有。謂空、無我所顯真如，有、無、俱非，心言路絕，與一切法非一、異等，是法真理，故名法性。離諸障礙，故名虛空；由簡擇力，滅諸雜染，究竟證會，故名擇滅；不由擇力，本性清淨，或緣闕所顯，故名非擇滅；苦、樂受滅，故名不動；想、受不行，名想受滅。此五皆依真如假立，真如亦是假施設名。

Second, they are provisionally posited based on the true nature of dharmas. That is: the suchness revealed by the emptiness of self and of dharmas is neither existent, nor nonexistent, nor both; it lies beyond the reach of thought and language; it is neither identical with nor different from any dharma. Being the true principle underlying all dharmas, it is called "the nature of dharmas."

This very suchness, free of every obstruction, is called "space." Realized ultimately through the power of discerning wisdom that extinguishes all defilement, it is called "cessation through discernment." Originally pure not through the power of discernment but simply revealed through the absence of a condition, it is called "cessation without discernment." Where the sensation of suffering and of pleasure has ceased, it is called "the unmoving." Where perception and sensation no longer operate, it is called "the cessation of perception and sensation." All five of these names are provisionally established based on suchness — and "suchness" itself is likewise only a provisionally established name.

遮撥為無，故說為有；遮執為有，故說為空；勿謂虛、幻，故說為實；理非妄倒，故名真如。不同餘宗離色、心等有實常法名曰真如。故諸無為非定實有。

To block the denial that it exists at all, it is called "existent." To block the attachment that it exists as some separate real thing, it is called "empty." So that it not be mistaken for something merely illusory, it is called "real." Because its principle is free of delusion and distortion, it is called "suchness." This is nothing like other schools' "suchness" — some really existent, eternal thing they hold to stand apart from form, mind, and the rest. Unconditioned dharmas, therefore, are not definitely, really existent as separate substances.

Reading note: This passage resolves a whole list of Abhidharma's "unconditioned" categories — space, two kinds of cessation, motionlessness, cessation of perception-and-feeling — into one reality named five different ways, depending on which angle is in view: called "space" when emphasizing its freedom from all obstruction, "cessation through discernment" when emphasizing wisdom's role in reaching it, "cessation without discernment" when emphasizing that it needed no such effort, and so on. None of these names points to a separate metaphysical object; all of them point back to suchness (真如, tathatā) — which the treatise immediately adds is itself just a provisional name, not a hidden eternal substance either.

The four-part gloss at the end is worth sitting with slowly: "existent" to block nihilism, "empty" to block reification, "real" to block dismissing it as illusion, "suchness" to name that this whole account is not itself a distortion. Every term is doing corrective work against a specific mistake, not describing a thing. With this, fascicle 2 has now closed every realist category examined since fascicle 1 began — self, the forces disjoined from mind, and now the unconditioned. What remains is to explain what actually accounts for everything just cleared away.

Even "Consciousness-Only" Can Become an Attachment 依教廣成分 · 破法執（十六）· 總結

Having worked through every specific school and category, the treatise now steps back for a general argument covering all of them at once — and delivers one of its most important cautions: the teaching of Consciousness-Only is a remedy for a mistaken clinging, not a new absolute to cling to in its place.

Chinese Source

外道、餘乘所執諸法異心、心所非實有性，是所取故，如心、心所。能取彼覺亦不緣彼，是能取故，如緣此覺。諸心、心所依他起故，亦如幻事，非真實有。為遣妄執心、心所外實有境故，說唯有識。若執唯識真實有者，如執外境，亦是法執。

Ru-Yi Translation

All the dharmas that non-Buddhist schools and other Buddhist vehicles hold to exist as something different from mind and mental factors are not really existent, since they are things grasped as an object — just as mind and mental factors themselves are, when reflected upon, yet no one takes them to be external substances for that reason. Nor does the very cognition that grasps such an object thereby take that object as something ultimately real, since it is itself simply what grasps — just as the cognition that in turn reflects on that grasping is simply what grasps, without requiring some further ultimate object either. Mind and mental factors, since they arise in dependence on other things, are themselves like a magical illusion — not independently, truly real either.

It is in order to dispel the mistaken clinging to some real object existing outside mind and mental factors that "Consciousness-Only" is taught. But if one then clings to Consciousness-Only itself as truly, independently real — the very same way one clung to an external object before — that too is dharma-attachment.

Reading note: This is arguably the single most important methodological line in the entire treatise, and it is easy to read past. "Consciousness-Only" is not offered as a new metaphysical bedrock to replace the external world realists believed in — it is a corrective, aimed at a specific mistake (clinging to something as existing independently, outside the mind), and if you turn around and cling to "consciousness" itself the same way, you have simply relocated the same error rather than corrected it. The teaching is a raft for crossing, not a new shore to build a house on.

Innate and Discriminated Dharma-Attachment 依教廣成分 · 法執二種

Just as fascicle 1 distinguished innate from discriminated self-attachment, the treatise now draws the identical distinction for dharma-attachment — the deeper habit of treating things, not just a self, as fixed and independently real.

Chinese Source

然諸法執略有二種：一者俱生；二者分別。俱生法執，無始時來虛妄熏習，內因力故，恒與身俱，不待邪教及邪分別，任運而轉，故名俱生。此復二種：一、常相續，在第七識，緣第八識，起自心相，執為實法；二、有間斷，在第六識，緣識所變蘊、處、界相，或總、或別，起自心相，執為實法。此二法執細故難斷，後十地中數數修習勝法空觀，方能除滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Attachment to dharmas, in brief, is of two kinds: innate, and discriminated. Innate dharma-attachment, through deluded habituation from beginningless time, arises constantly together with the body by its own inner causal power; it does not depend on mistaken teaching or mistaken discrimination, but operates spontaneously — hence it is called innate.

This too is of two kinds. One is continuous and unbroken: located in the seventh consciousness, which takes the eighth consciousness as its object, it generates its own mental image and clings to it as a real dharma. The other occurs with interruption: located in the sixth consciousness, which takes the appearance of the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements as transformed by consciousness — as a whole or individually — it generates its own mental image and clings to it as a real dharma. Because these two are subtle, they are hard to sever; only later, across the ten grounds, through repeated practice of contemplating the higher emptiness of dharmas, can they finally be eliminated.

分別法執，亦由現在外緣力故，非與身俱，要待邪教及邪分別然後方起，故名分別。唯在第六意識中有。此亦二種：一、緣邪教所說蘊、處、界相，起自心相，分別、計度，執為實法。二、緣邪教所說自性等相，起自心相，分別、計度，執為實法。此二法執麁故易斷，入初地時，觀一切法，法空真如，即能除滅。

Discriminated dharma-attachment, by contrast, is brought about by the power of present external conditions; it does not arise together with the body, but requires a mistaken teaching and mistaken discrimination before it can arise — hence it is called discriminated. It exists only in the sixth, cognizing consciousness.

This too is of two kinds: one takes the characteristics of the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements as described by a mistaken teaching, generates its own mental image, and constructs it as a real dharma; the other takes the characteristics of "own-nature" and the like as described by a mistaken teaching, generates its own mental image, and constructs it as a real dharma. Because these two are coarse, they are easy to sever; upon entering the first ground, by contemplating the suchness of the emptiness of dharmas in all things, they are eliminated at once.

如是所說一切法執，自心外法或有或無，自心內法一切皆有，是故法執皆緣自心所現似法，執為實有。然似法相從緣生故，是如幻有，所執實法妄計度故，決定非有。故世尊說：「慈氏當知，諸識所緣，唯識所現，依他起性，如幻事等。」如是外道、餘乘所執離識我、法皆非實有。

In every kind of dharma-attachment just described, an object among the dharmas outside one's own mind may or may not be present — but an object among the dharmas within one's own mind is always present. Every kind of dharma-attachment, then, takes hold of some dharma-like appearance manifested by one's own mind, and mistakenly clings to it as really existent. But this dharma-like appearance, arising as it does from conditions, exists the way an illusion exists; the real dharma mistakenly clung to, being arbitrarily constructed, definitely does not exist at all. Hence the World-Honored One says: "Maitreya, you should know: whatever the various consciousnesses take as their object is simply what consciousness itself manifests. It has the nature of dependent origination, and is like a magical illusion." In this way, the self and the dharmas that non-Buddhist schools and other Buddhist vehicles cling to, apart from consciousness, are, in every case, not really existent.

故心、心所決定不用外色等法為所緣緣，緣用必依實有體故。現在彼聚心、心所法，非此聚識親所緣緣，如非所緣，他聚攝故。同聚心所亦非親所緣，自體異故，如餘非所取。由此應知，實無外境，唯有內識，似外境生。是故契經伽他中說：「如愚所分別，外境實皆無，習氣擾濁心，故似彼而轉。」

Therefore, mind and mental factors certainly do not take external form and the other supposedly external dharmas as their object-condition, since the function of an object-condition must depend on a really existent substance. The mind and mental factors belonging to some other, separate stream of consciousness cannot serve as the direct object-condition for this stream, since, not being its own object, they belong to a different group entirely. Even a mental factor within the very same stream is not the direct object of its accompanying consciousness, since its own substance remains distinct — no different from anything else not directly grasped as an object. From this it should be understood that there really is no external object at all: there is only inner consciousness, manifesting an appearance that resembles one. Hence the scripture says, in verse: "As the foolish discriminate it, the external object is, in every case, entirely nonexistent; it is habitual tendencies that disturb and cloud the mind, causing it to turn and manifest resembling that object."

Reading note: The structure here is deliberately identical to fascicle 1's self-attachment doctrine: innate dharma-attachment runs beneath conscious thought, in the seventh and sixth consciousnesses, and is only worn down gradually across the bodhisattva path's ten grounds; discriminated dharma-attachment is built from explicit wrong teachings and philosophical constructions, confined to the sixth consciousness, and is cut through all at once upon first entering the path. The parallel is the point: attachment to a fixed self and attachment to fixed, independently real things are not two unrelated problems, but the same underlying habit of mind — reifying what is actually a dependent, ever-changing process — applied to two different targets.

With this, the treatise has now closed the entire refutation launched all the way back at the start of fascicle 1: both self and dharmas have been shown, exhaustively, to be provisional designations laid over what consciousness transforms. What remains — starting in the very next section — is to explain, positively, what that transforming consciousness actually is.

If Nothing Is Real, How Can Even "Provisional" Language Work? 依教廣成分 · 釋妨難 · 假必依真

One serious objection remains, and it strikes at the method itself: metaphor seems to need something real to anchor it. This section closes the entire argument launched at the start of fascicle 1 — after this, the treatise turns from refutation to construction.

The Objection 假必依真事

Chinese Source

有作是難：若無離識實我、法者，假亦應無，謂假必依真事、似事、共法而立，如有真火、有似火人、有猛赤法，乃可假說此人為火，假說牛等應知亦然。我、法若無，依何假說，無假說故，似亦不成，如何說心似外境轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone raises this objection: if there is no real self or dharmas apart from consciousness, then even provisional designation should be impossible. Provisional designation must rest on a real thing, a resembling thing, and a quality shared between them — as when there is a real fire, a person who resembles fire, and a fierce, ruddy quality shared by both — only then can one provisionally call this person "fire." The same should hold for provisionally calling something "a cow," and so on. If self and dharmas do not exist at all, on what basis could they be provisionally spoken of? And if provisional designation is impossible, then even saying that mind merely "resembles" an external object cannot be established either — so how can you still claim that mind turns as if it were an external object?

The Response, in Three Steps 不依類、不依實、不依真事

彼難非理，離識我、法，前已破故。依類、依實假說火等，俱不成故。依類假說理且不成，猛、赤等德非類有故。若無共德而假說彼，應亦於水等假說火等名。若謂猛等雖非類德，而不相離，故可假說。此亦不然，人類猛等現見亦有，互相離故。類既無德又互相離，然有於人假說火等，故知假說不依類成。

This objection is unreasonable, since a real self and real dharmas apart from consciousness have already been refuted above. In fact, provisional designation grounded in class-membership, and provisional designation grounded in a real shared quality, both fail.

Grounding it in class-membership fails first: qualities like "fierce" and "ruddy" do not themselves belong to any class the way substances do. And if there were no shared quality at all, one might as well call water "fire" too — nothing would make fire the apt comparison and not water. If you say fierceness and ruddiness, though not class-qualities themselves, are still inseparable from fire — that fails too: a fierce person, in plain experience, is often found nowhere near actual fire. Since the class has no defining quality, and the supposed shared quality is in any case separable from fire, and yet "fire" is still applied to such a person — provisional designation plainly does not rest on class-membership.

依實假說，理亦不成。猛、赤等德非共有故，謂猛、赤等在火、在人其體各別，所依異故，無共假說，有過同前。若謂人、火，德相似故，可假說者，理亦不然。說火在人，非在德故。由此假說不依實成。

Grounding it in a real shared quality fails too: "fierce" and "ruddy" are not, in fact, shared — the fierceness in fire and the fierceness in a person are distinct in substance, since they depend on entirely different bases, so there is no truly shared quality to ground the comparison, and the same faults just given recur. If you say it rests merely on resemblance between the two qualities — that fails too: we say the fire is "in" the person, not that one quality is "in" another quality. Provisional designation, then, does not rest on a real shared quality either.

又假必依真事立者，亦不應理。真謂自相，假智及詮俱非境故。謂假智、詮不得自相，唯於諸法共相而轉，亦非離此有別方便施設自相為假所依。然假智、詮必依聲起，聲不及處，此便不轉，能詮、所詮俱非自相，故知假說不依真事。由此但依似事而轉，似謂增益，非實有相，聲依增益，似相而轉，故不可說假必依真。

Furthermore, the claim that provisional designation must, in any case, rest on some real thing does not hold up either. "Real" means a thing's own particular character — but neither provisional concept nor provisional language, functioning as they do, ever actually takes hold of that particular character as its object. Provisional cognition and provisional language operate only on the shared, general characteristics dharmas hold in common; there is no further special mechanism by which they could instead fasten onto a thing's own unique character as their basis. And provisional cognition and language necessarily depend on words arising — wherever words do not reach, they simply do not operate at all — so neither the word doing the designating nor the concept designated is ever a thing's own particular character. From this it can be known that provisional designation does not, after all, rest on something real. It operates only on a resembling appearance — "resembling" here meaning something superimposed, not a thing's own real character. Since language works by way of exactly this kind of superimposition, turning on a merely resembling appearance, one cannot say that provisional designation must rest on something real.

Closing the Case 結頌

Chinese Source

是故彼難不應正理。然依識變，對遣妄執真實我、法，說假似言。由此契經伽他中說：「為對遣愚夫所執實我、法，故於識所變，假說我、法名。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The objection, therefore, does not accord with reason. Rather, it is based on what consciousness transforms — and precisely in order to dispel the deluded clinging to a truly real self and real dharmas — that this language of provisional resemblance is spoken. Hence the scripture says, in verse: "In order to dispel what the unenlightened cling to as a real self and real dharmas, based on what consciousness transforms, the names 'self' and 'dharmas' are provisionally spoken."

Reading note: The deepest move in this section is easy to miss: it isn't just answering the objection about "self" and "dharmas" specifically — it is making a general claim about how all concepts and words work. Ordinary language never actually reaches a thing's own bare, particular reality; it always operates on shared, general characteristics abstracted across many things (which is exactly why the same word "fire" can apply to a campfire, a stove flame, and a fierce person). If that is true of language in general, then the demand that "self" and "dharma" specifically must be anchored in something fully, independently real is really a demand no word could ever meet — not a special problem for Consciousness-Only.

With this, fascicle 2 completes the argument that has run continuously since the opening homage of fascicle 1: neither a real self, nor real dharmas, nor even the possibility of provisional language itself, require anything to exist independently of consciousness. The treatise now turns, for the first time, from refutation to construction — the next section begins unpacking "the latter three lines" of the opening verse: what the three transformations of consciousness actually are, starting with the ālayavijñāna.

Two Kinds of "Transformation" 依教廣成分 · 明唯識相 · 別解三句出能變體

This is the hinge of the entire treatise. Everything up to this point has been clearing ground; from here, the treatise builds. Before naming the three transforming consciousnesses in detail, it first defines exactly what "transformation" (能變) means — a technical distinction the rest of the work depends on.

Chinese Source

識所變相雖無量種，而能變識類別唯三：一謂異熟，即第八識，多異熟性故；二謂思量，即第七識，恒、審思量故；三謂了境，即前六識，了境相麁故。「及」言顯六合為一種。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although what consciousness transforms into takes countless forms, the kinds of consciousness capable of transforming are only three. First, the ripening consciousness — the eighth consciousness — so called because it is characterized, for the most part, by ripening. Second, the deliberating consciousness — the seventh consciousness — so called because it constantly and closely deliberates. Third, the consciousness that discerns objects — the first six consciousnesses — so called because their manner of discerning objects is comparatively coarse. The word "and," in the verse's list, shows that these six are grouped together as a single kind.

此三皆名能變識者，能變有二種：一、因能變，謂第八識中「等流」、「異熟」二因習氣。等流習氣，由七識中善、惡、無記熏令生長；異熟習氣由六識中有漏善、惡熏令生長。

All three are called "transforming consciousnesses" because transformation is itself of two kinds. The first is causal transformation: the two kinds of causal habitual tendencies within the eighth consciousness — the "homogeneous-outflow" tendency and the "ripening" tendency. The homogeneous-outflow tendency grows through the perfuming left by wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral activity in the seven active consciousnesses. The ripening tendency grows through the perfuming left by contaminated wholesome and unwholesome activity in the six sense-consciousnesses.

二、果能變，謂前二種習氣力故，有八識生現種種相。等流習氣為因緣故，八識體相差別而生，名等流果，果似因故。異熟習氣為增上緣，感第八識，酬引業力，恒相續故，立異熟名；感前六識，酬滿業者，從異熟起，名異熟生，不名異熟有間斷故。即前異熟及異熟生，名異熟果，果異因故。此中且說我愛執藏，持雜染種能變果識，名為異熟，非謂一切。

The second is resultant transformation: through the power of these two habitual tendencies, the eight consciousnesses arise and manifest their various distinct appearances. Because the homogeneous-outflow tendency serves as a direct cause, the eight consciousnesses arise with their own distinct substances and characteristics — this is called the "homogeneous-outflow result," since the result resembles its cause. Because the ripening tendency serves as a contributing condition, it draws forth the eighth consciousness, repaying the power of life-projecting karma, continuing constantly without break — hence the name "ripening." It also draws forth the first six consciousnesses, repaying completing karma; since these arise from the ripening consciousness but are themselves interrupted, they are called "ripened arisings" rather than simply "ripening." The ripening consciousness together with these ripened arisings are together called the "result of ripening," since the result differs in kind from its cause.

What is specifically meant here by "the ripening consciousness" is the consciousness that is clung to and cherished as a self, that stores seeds, and that, holding the seeds of defilement, is capable of transforming into a resultant consciousness — this is the sense of "ripening" meant in the present context, not every possible sense of the term.

Reading note: Two technical distinctions, both essential going forward. Causal transformation (因能變) happens whenever an experience leaves a trace — a habitual tendency, or "seed" — planted in the eighth consciousness. There are two kinds of these traces: "homogeneous-outflow" seeds (等流習氣), left by ordinary activity of any moral quality, which ripen into more of the same kind of experience later; and "ripening" seeds (異熟習氣) specifically left by karmically-charged wholesome and unwholesome action, which ripen into an overall life and its general circumstances. Resultant transformation (果能變) is what happens when those seeds later mature: homogeneous-outflow seeds produce a "homogeneous-outflow result" (the next moment resembling this one), while ripening seeds produce the "result of ripening" — the eighth consciousness itself, which continues unbroken across a whole life sustaining that life's basic conditions, and the first six consciousnesses' episodic ripening into specific pleasant or painful experiences within it.

This vocabulary — seed, perfuming, homogeneous-outflow, ripening — is the treatise's technical language for how karma actually works mechanically, without a soul carrying it from one life to the next: experience leaves traces in the eighth consciousness, and those traces later mature into new experience, life after life, with nothing further required to hold the process together.

The Ālayavijñāna Verse 依教廣成分 · 隨標廣釋 · 釋初能變

This is arguably the single most important verse in the whole treatise — the formal introduction of the ālayavijñāna, the storehouse consciousness. Everything about it, packed into a few dense lines, will be unpacked across most of the rest of fascicle 2 and into fascicle 3.

Chinese Source

說能變三名，而未廣辯能變三相。且初能變其相云何？頌曰：初阿賴耶識，異熟一切種，不可知執受，處了常與觸，作意、受、想、思，相應唯捨受。是無覆無記，觸等亦如是，恒轉如瀑流，阿羅漢位捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

The three names of the transforming consciousnesses have been given, but their three natures have not yet been explained. What, then, is the nature of the first transformation?

The verse says: The first is the ālayavijñāna — ripening, holding all seeds. What it grasps and sustains, and its own domain, are unknowable. It is constantly joined with contact, attention, feeling, perception, and volition, and only ever with neutral feeling. It is unobstructed and morally indeterminate; contact and the other mental factors that accompany it are likewise so. It flows on constantly, like a rushing torrent, and is relinquished at the stage of the arhat.

Reading note: Nearly every word of this verse names something the treatise will spend pages unpacking — worth a slow first pass now, since the vocabulary carries forward for the rest of the work.

異熟一切種Two of the ālaya's three defining roles, named here: "ripening" (its result — what past karma matures into) and "holding all seeds" (its cause — every latent tendency from every past experience).
不可知執受，處了What it takes as its object is unknowable to ordinary awareness: the physical body it sustains and appropriates (執受), the surrounding world (處), and its own subtle activity of cognizing (了) — all too fine-grained to be consciously noticed.
常與觸、作意、受、想、思相應The "five universal mental factors" (contact, attention, feeling, perception, volition) that accompany every act of consciousness whatsoever — present even in the ālaya's barest, most basic activity.
唯捨受Its accompanying feeling is always neutral — neither pleasant nor painful. The ālaya itself never rejoices or suffers; only the six active consciousnesses built on top of it do.
無覆無記"Unobstructed and indeterminate": not itself an obstacle to realization, and not itself karmically wholesome or unwholesome — a morally neutral basis, not a moral agent.
恒轉如瀑流The treatise's central image for this consciousness: like a river that is never the same water twice, yet is unmistakably one continuous river — constant, yet never static.
阿羅漢位捨Not that the consciousness itself ceases, but that the name "ālaya" — tied to its role in self-clinging — no longer applies once that clinging is fully uprooted at arhatship.

Three Characteristics: What Stores, What Ripens, What Holds Seeds 自相 · 果相 · 因相

Chinese Source

論曰：初能變識，大小乘教名阿賴耶，此識具有能藏、所藏、執藏義故。謂與雜染互為緣故，有情執為自內我故，此即顯示初能變識所有自相，攝持因果為自相故。此識自相，分位雖多，藏識過重，是故偏說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The treatise says: the first transforming consciousness is called, in both Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna teaching, the ālaya — the "storehouse" — because this consciousness carries a threefold meaning: that which stores, that which is stored, and that which is clung to as a store. That is: it mutually serves as condition together with defilement, and sentient beings cling to it as their inner self. This reveals the self-characteristic of the first transforming consciousness, since holding and sustaining cause and effect together is exactly what constitutes that self-characteristic. Although this consciousness's self-characteristic has many aspects, its role as "storehouse" carries the greatest weight, and so this aspect is specifically emphasized in its name.

此是能引諸界、趣、生，善、不善業異熟果故，說名異熟。離此命根、眾同分等恒時相續勝異熟果不可得故。此即顯示初能變識所有果相。此識果相，雖多位、多種，異熟寬、不共，故偏說之。

This consciousness draws forth the ripened results of wholesome and unwholesome karma across every realm, destiny, and mode of birth — hence it is called "ripening." Apart from it, the life-faculty, the sameness-of-kind, and the rest — the constant, unbroken, superior ripened result that sustains an entire life — could not be found. This reveals the result-characteristic of the first transforming consciousness. Though its result-characteristic takes many stages and many forms, "ripening" is the broadest and most distinctive, and so this aspect is specifically emphasized.

此能執持諸法種子令不失故，名一切種。離此餘法能遍執持諸法種子不可得故，此即顯示初能變識所有因相。此識因相雖有多種，持種不共，是故偏說。初能變識，體相雖多，略說唯有如是三相。一切種相應更分別。

This consciousness holds the seeds of every dharma without letting them be lost — hence it is called "holding all seeds." Apart from it, no other dharma capable of universally holding the seeds of all dharmas could be found. This reveals the cause-characteristic of the first transforming consciousness. Though its cause-characteristic has many aspects, its holding of seeds is unique and shared by nothing else, and so this aspect is specifically emphasized.

Although the substance and characteristics of the first transforming consciousness are many, in brief, they amount to just these three. The meaning of "holding all seeds" should now be examined further.

Reading note: Three characteristics, three timeframes, one consciousness. The self-characteristic (自相) is what it is right now: a storehouse, holding seeds and being clung to as self. The cause-characteristic (因相) looks forward: every seed it holds is a cause waiting to ripen into future experience. The result-characteristic (果相) looks backward: it is itself the ripened result of all past karma, the thread that makes one continuous life — sustaining the life-faculty and bodily continuity — out of countless separate past actions. The same single consciousness, described from three different temporal angles, is named three different ways: storehouse, all-seeds, ripening.

What Is a Seed? 依教廣成分 · 一切種相 · 種子義

The treatise now turns to unpack "holding all seeds" — the ālaya's cause-characteristic. Seeds are the mechanism behind everything discussed so far: how experience leaves a trace, and how that trace later matures into new experience. This section defines what a seed actually is, before the next section takes up one of Yogācāra's most famous internal debates: are seeds already there from the start, or only ever planted by experience?

Chinese Source

此中何法名為種子？謂本識中親生自果，功能差別。此與本識及所生果不一、不異，體用、因果理應爾故。雖非一、異，而是實有，假法如無，非因緣故。此與諸法既非一、異，應如瓶等是假非實。若爾，真如應是假有，許則便無真勝義諦。然諸種子唯依世俗說為實有，不同真如。

Ru-Yi Translation

What, in this context, is meant by "seed"? It is a distinct functional capacity within the root consciousness that directly gives rise to its own result. This capacity is neither identical with nor different from the root consciousness and the result it produces — this follows from the very logic of substance and function, and of cause and effect. Although neither identical nor different, it is really existent — for a merely provisional thing, having no efficacy of its own, cannot serve as a cause and condition.

One might object: since this seed is neither identical with nor different from other dharmas generally, it should, like a pot, be merely provisional, not real. But if that reasoning held, suchness too — also described as neither identical with nor different from dharmas — would have to be merely provisional, and accepting that would leave no true ultimate reality at all. Seeds, however, are said to be really existent only in the sense of conventional truth, unlike suchness, which is really existent in the sense of ultimate truth.

種子雖依第八識體，而是此識相分；非餘，見分恒取此為境故。諸有漏種與異熟識，體無別故，無記性攝；因果俱有善等性故，亦名善等。諸無漏種，非異熟識性所攝故；因果俱是善性攝故，唯名為善。

Although seeds depend on the substance of the eighth consciousness, they are that very consciousness's own perceived-aspect — not anyone else's — since its perceiving-aspect constantly takes them as its object. Contaminated seeds, since their substance is not different from the ripening consciousness itself, are classed as morally indeterminate; yet since both their cause and their effect can carry a wholesome or other moral quality, they can secondarily be called "wholesome" and so on. Uncontaminated seeds, however, are not included within the ripening consciousness's own nature, since both their cause and their effect are of a wholesome nature — they are called only "wholesome."

若爾，何故〈決擇分〉說二十二根一切皆有異熟種子，皆異熟生？雖名異熟而非無記，依異熟故名異熟種，異性相依如眼等識。或無漏種，由熏習力轉變成熟，立異熟名，非無記性所攝異熟。

If so, why does the "Chapter of Investigation" say that all twenty-two faculties have ripening seeds, and are all "ripened arisings"? — Though called "ripening," they need not be morally indeterminate; they are called "ripening seeds" simply because they depend on the ripening consciousness, even while differing from it in nature — just as the eye-consciousness and the rest depend on it while having their own distinct qualities. Or, alternatively: uncontaminated seeds, through the power of perfuming, transform and mature, and so are given the name "ripening" in this looser sense — not the "ripening" that belongs to a morally indeterminate nature.

Reading note: The opening definition is doing careful, deliberate philosophical work: a seed is neither simply identical with the ālaya (or it couldn't be a distinct "capacity" within it) nor simply separate from it (or it couldn't be part of the same causal stream) — and rather than let that "neither same nor different" description collapse seeds into being merely nominal, like a label with no real efficacy, the treatise insists seeds are genuinely causally effective, and therefore really existent — just existent at the conventional level, not the ultimate level reserved for suchness itself. This is a recurring move in Yogācāra metaphysics: reality comes in more than one register, and "not ultimately real" does not mean "does no work."

The distinction between contaminated seeds (有漏種, tied to ordinary defiled experience, sharing the ālaya's own neutral moral quality) and uncontaminated seeds (無漏種, the rare seeds of wisdom and liberation, which are never neutral but always wholesome) matters enormously for the path: uncontaminated seeds are what eventually mature into awakening itself, and the next section's debate over where they come from is really a debate about how liberation becomes possible at all.

Where Do Seeds Come From? Two Extreme Answers 依教廣成分 · 種子本有、新熏之諍（一）

Here begins one of Yogācāra's most famous internal debates: are seeds already there in every mind from the start, or are all of them planted by experience? The treatise presents two opposing views before offering its own synthesis in the next section.

View One: All Seeds Are Inherently Pre-existent 一切種子皆本性有

Chinese Source

此中有義：一切種子皆本性有，不從熏生。由熏習力但可增長。如契經說：一切有情無始時來有種種界，如惡叉聚，法爾而有。界即種子差別名故。又契經說：「無始時來界，一切法等依」，界是因義。《瑜伽》亦說：諸種子體無始時來性雖本有，而由染淨新所熏發。

Ru-Yi Translation

On this question, one view holds: all seeds inherently exist from the start; they do not arise from perfuming. Perfuming can only cause them to increase and grow. As scripture says: all sentient beings, from beginningless time, possess various elements, clustered together like akṣa seeds, existing just as they naturally are — "element" being simply another name for the distinct varieties of seeds. Scripture also says: "the beginningless element is the equal basis of all dharmas," where "element" carries the sense of "cause." The Yogācārabhūmi likewise says that although the substance of seeds has, from beginningless time, inherently existed, it is nonetheless activated anew by defiled or pure perfuming.

諸有情類無始時來，若般涅槃法者，一切種子皆悉具足，不般涅槃法者，便闕三種菩提種子。如是等文，誠證非一。又諸有情既說本有五種性別，故應定有法爾種子，不由熏生。又《瑜伽》說：地獄成就三無漏根，是種非現。又從無始展轉傳來，法爾所得本性住性。由此等證，無漏種子法爾本有，不從熏生。有漏亦應法爾有種，由熏增長，不別熏生。如是建立，因果不亂。

Among sentient beings, from beginningless time, those capable of ultimately attaining nirvāṇa possess every kind of seed in full; those incapable of it lack the seeds of the three kinds of awakening. Passages like these offer more than one proof. Furthermore, since sentient beings are said to have five inherently distinct spiritual dispositions, there must certainly be naturally-existing seeds, not produced by perfuming. The Yogācārabhūmi also says that even hell-beings possess the three uncontaminated faculties in seed form, though not manifest — and that these have been transmitted, one after another, from beginningless time, naturally obtained as an abiding nature. From such proofs, uncontaminated seeds naturally, inherently exist, and do not arise from perfuming; contaminated seeds should likewise naturally exist, merely increased through perfuming rather than separately produced by it. Established this way, cause and effect remain unconfused.

View Two: All Seeds Arise from Perfuming 種子皆熏故生

Chinese Source

有義：種子皆熏故生。所熏、能熏俱無始有，故諸種子無始成就。種子既是習氣異名，習氣必由熏習而有，如麻香氣，花熏故生。如契經說：諸有情心，染淨諸法所熏習故，無量種子之所積集。論說：內種定有熏習，外種熏習或有或無。又名言等三種熏習，總攝一切有漏法種。彼三既由熏習而有，故有漏種必藉熏生。無漏種生亦由熏習，說聞熏習、聞淨法界等流正法而熏起故，是出世心種子性故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Another view holds: all seeds arise because of perfuming. What is perfumed and what does the perfuming have both existed since beginningless time, so all seeds have likewise been established since beginningless time — through an endless process, not as an unchanging starting inventory. Since "seed" is simply another name for "habitual tendency," and a habitual tendency must come into being through perfuming — just as sesame oil takes on fragrance only because it has been perfumed by flowers.

As scripture says: the minds of sentient beings, perfumed by defiled and pure dharmas, accumulate countless seeds. Another treatise says: inner seeds certainly involve perfuming; outer seeds may or may not. The three kinds of perfuming — of verbal expression and the rest — together encompass the seeds of every contaminated dharma; since these three arise through perfuming, contaminated seeds must, without exception, be produced by relying on perfuming. Uncontaminated seeds, too, arise through perfuming — this is why "the perfuming of hearing" is spoken of: hearing the true teaching that flows from the pure dharma-realm perfumes and activates them, since they are exactly the seed-nature of the mind that transcends the world.

有情本來種姓差別，不由無漏種子有無，但依有障、無障建立。如《瑜伽》說：於真如境，若有畢竟二障種者，立為不般涅槃法性；若有畢竟所知障種非煩惱者，一分立為聲聞種性，一分立為獨覺種性；若無畢竟二障種者，即立彼為如來種性。故知本來種性差別，依障建立，非無漏種。所說：「成就無漏種」言，依當可生，非已有體。

The distinct spiritual dispositions sentient beings are said to have from the start are not established by whether uncontaminated seeds already exist, but by whether the two obstructions can ultimately be removed. As the Yogācārabhūmi says: with respect to suchness, those whose seeds of both obstructions can never be ultimately eliminated are established as never attaining nirvāṇa; those whose seeds of the cognitive obstruction alone can never be ultimately eliminated are established, in part, with the disposition of a śrāvaka, and in part with that of a pratyekabuddha; those with no seeds of either obstruction that can never be eliminated are established with the disposition of a tathāgata. From this it can be known that inherent dispositional differences are established on the basis of the obstructions, not on the basis of uncontaminated seeds. The statement that everyone "possesses uncontaminated seeds," then, refers to what can, in the future, come to arise — not to something that already substantially exists now.

Reading note: Both positions are trying to protect something important. The "all inherent" view wants to explain why spiritual disposition seems to vary so much from birth to birth, before any teaching or practice could have shaped it — surely something must already be there. The "all perfumed" view wants to keep the causal story clean and empirical — a habitual tendency, by definition, is something a habit produces, and positing seeds that were simply always there, unexplained, feels like cheating the very mechanism the theory is supposed to describe. Notice the second view's clever move against the first: differing spiritual dispositions don't require differing pre-existing seed inventories at all — they can instead be explained by whether the two obstructions (afflictive and cognitive) happen to be removable in a given person's case, which is a structural fact, not evidence of some seed already sitting there waiting.

Both views marshal real scriptural support, and neither is dismissed as simply wrong — the next section shows why Dharmapāla's school judged that the truth requires both mechanisms working together, not a choice between them.

The Synthesis: Both Kinds of Seeds Exist 依教廣成分 · 種子本有、新熏之諍（二）

Rather than choosing between the two extreme positions, the school this treatise follows argues that both mechanisms are real, and that neither extreme can survive on its own — starting with why "only inherent" fails.

Two Kinds of Seed 本性住種 · 習所成種

Chinese Source

有義：種子各有二類。一者本有，謂無始來異熟識中法爾而有，生蘊、處、界功能差別。世尊依此，說諸有情無始時來有種種界，如惡叉聚，法爾而有，餘所引證廣說如初。此即名為本性住種。二者始起，謂無始來，數數現行熏習而有。世尊依此，說有情心染淨諸法所熏習故，無量種子之所積集。諸論亦說：染淨種子由染淨法熏習故生，此即名為習所成種。

Ru-Yi Translation

A further view holds: seeds are, in every case, of two kinds. The first is the inherently pre-existent kind: the distinct functional capacities for producing the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements, existing naturally within the ripening consciousness from beginningless time. It is on this basis that the World-Honored One speaks of sentient beings possessing, from beginningless time, various elements, clustered like akṣa seeds, existing just as they naturally are — the other citations given earlier apply here too. This is called the "naturally-abiding seed."

The second is the newly-arisen kind: seeds that come into being through repeated perfuming by manifest activity, across beginningless time. It is on this basis that the World-Honored One speaks of the minds of sentient beings accumulating countless seeds because they are perfumed by defiled and pure dharmas; other treatises likewise say that defiled and pure seeds arise because of perfuming by defiled and pure dharmas. This is called the "seed formed through habituation."

Why "Only Inherent" Fails 若唯本有，理教相違

若唯本有，轉識不應與阿賴耶為因緣性。如契經說：「諸法於識藏，識於法亦爾，更互為果性，亦常為因性。」此頌意言：阿賴耶識與諸轉識，於一切時展轉相生，互為因果。《攝大乘》說：阿賴耶識與雜染法互為因緣，如炷與焰展轉生燒，又如束蘆互相依住，唯依此二建立因緣，所餘因緣不可得故。

If seeds were only inherently pre-existent, the evolving consciousnesses could not serve as a causal condition for the ālaya at all. As scripture says: "All dharmas are stored in consciousness, and consciousness likewise is stored in dharmas — each in turn serves as the other's result, and each is likewise always a cause for the other." This verse means that the ālayavijñāna and the evolving consciousnesses, at every moment, generate one another in turn, each serving as both cause and effect for the other. The Mahāyānasaṃgraha says: the ālayavijñāna and defiled dharmas serve as each other's cause and condition, just as a wick and its flame continually give rise to one another's burning, or as bundled reeds mutually lean on and sustain each other — it is only by relying on these two that a genuine cause-and-condition relationship can be established at all, since no other such relationship can be found.

若諸種子不由熏生，如何轉識與阿賴耶有因緣義？非熏令長可名因緣，勿善、惡業與異熟果為因緣故。又諸聖教說有種子由熏習生，皆違彼義；故唯本有，理、教相違。

If seeds were not produced by perfuming at all, how could the evolving consciousnesses have any genuine cause-and-condition relationship with the ālaya? Merely causing growth through perfuming, without real production, cannot be called a cause-and-condition relationship — otherwise, by that same weak standard, wholesome and unwholesome karma would have to count as a cause-and-condition for the ripened result, which no one holds. And every scripture that speaks of seeds arising through perfuming would be directly contradicted. The "only inherently pre-existent" view, then, conflicts with both reason and scripture.

Reading note: The synthesis keeps what each extreme view was protecting. "Naturally-abiding seeds" (本性住種) explain why spiritual disposition can vary before any teaching or practice has shaped a person — some capacities really were already there. "Seeds formed through habituation" (習所成種) explain how experience genuinely produces new tendencies, not just amplifies old ones — which turns out to be necessary for a different reason than either original view emphasized: without genuine production, the ālaya and the seven active consciousnesses couldn't really cause each other at all, only ever "help along" something already complete — and the wick-and-flame image, where each side truly needs the other to keep burning, only makes sense if something new is actually being generated back and forth, not merely revealed.

Why "Only Newly-Arisen" Fails 依教廣成分 · 種子本有、新熏之諍（三）

The synthesis now turns to the opposite extreme, and along the way takes on a rival school's famous claim that "the mind is originally pure" — pressing hard on what that phrase could possibly mean.

Chinese Source

若唯始起，有為無漏無因緣故，應不得生，有漏不應為無漏種，勿無漏種生有漏故，許應諸佛有漏復生，善等應為不善等種。分別論者雖作是說：心性本淨，客塵煩惱所染污，故名為雜染，離煩惱時，轉成無漏，故無漏法非無因生。而心性言，彼說何義？

Ru-Yi Translation

If seeds arose only through perfuming, conditioned uncontaminated dharmas, having no prior cause-condition, could never arise at all. Contaminated seeds cannot serve as the seed for uncontaminated results either — uncontaminated seeds are not supposed to produce contaminated ones, and if you allowed that they could, even Buddhas should be able to lapse back into contaminated states, and wholesome seeds should end up producing unwholesome results.

The Vibhajyavādins say: the nature of mind is originally pure; it is defiled only by adventitious afflictions, hence called "defiled"; freed of afflictions, it turns uncontaminated — so the uncontaminated is not produced without a cause. But what, exactly, do they mean by "the nature of mind"?

若說空理，空非心因，常法定非諸法種子，以體前後無轉變故。若即說心，應同數論，相雖轉變而體常一，惡、無記心又應是善，許則應與信等相應，不許便應非善心體，尚不名善，況是無漏？有漏善心既稱雜染，如惡心等，性非無漏，故不應與無漏為因，勿善、惡等互為因故。若有漏心性是無漏，應無漏心性是有漏，差別因緣不可得故。

If they mean the principle of emptiness — emptiness cannot be a cause of mind, and, being an eternal, unchanging principle, it certainly cannot serve as any dharma's seed, since its substance never changes. If they mean mind's own substance — this becomes just like the Sāṃkhya position already refuted: appearance changes while substance remains eternally one. Unwholesome and neutral states of mind should then be wholesome too; grant this, and they should be associated with faith and the other wholesome factors; deny it, and that substance cannot really be called wholesome at all, let alone uncontaminated.

Since even wholesome-but-contaminated mind is called "defiled," just like unwholesome mind, its nature is not uncontaminated — so it should not serve as a cause for the uncontaminated, any more than wholesome and unwholesome states cause each other. And if a contaminated mind's nature really were uncontaminated, then, by the same token, an uncontaminated mind's nature should be contaminated — no distinguishing cause could ever explain why only one direction holds.

又異生心若是無漏，則異生位無漏現行應名聖者。若異生心性雖無漏，而相有染，不名無漏，無斯過者，則心種子亦非無漏，何故汝論說有異生唯得成就無漏種子，種子、現行性、相同故。然契經說：心性淨者，說心空理所顯真如，真如是心真實性故。或說心體非煩惱故，名性本淨，非有漏心性是無漏，故名本淨。由此應信有諸有情無始時來有無漏種，不由熏習，法爾成就，後勝進位熏令增長，無漏法起，以此為因。無漏起時，復熏成種，有漏法種類此應知。

Furthermore, if an ordinary being's mind were uncontaminated in nature, then its uncontaminated manifest activity should already qualify that ordinary being as a noble one. If you say the ordinary being's mind-nature is uncontaminated but its outward characteristics remain defiled, and so it isn't actually called uncontaminated — avoiding that fault — then its seed, too, should not be called uncontaminated by the same logic. Why, then, does your own treatise say an ordinary being can already possess genuinely uncontaminated seeds, when seed and manifest activity are supposed to share the same nature?

What scripture actually means by "mind's nature is pure" is the suchness revealed by mind's emptiness, since suchness is mind's true nature — or it means mind's substance is not itself affliction, hence "originally pure" in that sense, not that a contaminated mind's nature is somehow already uncontaminated. One should therefore believe that sentient beings possess, from beginningless time, uncontaminated seeds not produced by perfuming but naturally established as they are; later, at more advanced stages, perfuming causes them to grow, and uncontaminated dharmas arise, taking these seeds as their cause. When the uncontaminated arises, it perfumes and forms further seeds in turn — contaminated seeds should be understood the same way.

Reconciling Scripture: Both Kinds of Perfuming, Both Kinds of Cause 結歸二類種子

諸聖教中，雖說內種定有熏習，而不定說一切種子皆熏故生，寧全撥無本有種子？然本有種亦由熏習，令其增盛方能得果，故說內種定有熏習。其聞熏習非唯有漏，聞正法時亦熏本有無漏種子，令漸增盛，展轉乃至生出世心，故亦說此名聞熏習。聞熏習中，有漏性者是修所斷，感勝異熟，為出世法勝增上緣。無漏性者非所斷攝，與出世法正為因緣。此正因緣微隱難了，有寄麁顯勝增上緣，方便說為出世心種。

Although the sacred teachings say inner seeds certainly involve perfuming, they never say that every seed without exception arises purely from perfuming — so how could one deny inherently pre-existent seeds altogether? Rather, inherently pre-existent seeds are also affected by perfuming, which strengthens them enough to actually yield their result — this is the sense in which inner seeds "certainly involve perfuming."

"The perfuming of hearing" is not only of a contaminated kind: hearing the true teaching also perfumes the inherently pre-existent uncontaminated seeds, gradually strengthening them until, in due course, the mind that transcends the world arises — this too falls under "the perfuming of hearing." Within it, the contaminated portion is eliminated through cultivation and brings a superior ripened result, serving as a powerful contributing condition for the supramundane teaching; the uncontaminated portion is not eliminated, and serves as its direct cause. Because this direct cause is extremely subtle and hard to discern, scripture speaks instead in terms of the more evident contributing condition, provisionally calling it "the seed of the mind that transcends the world."

依障建立種姓別者，意顯無漏種子有無，謂：若全無無漏種者，彼二障種永不可害，即立彼為非涅槃法；若唯有二乘無漏種者，彼所知障種永不可害，一分立為聲聞種姓，一分立為獨覺種姓；若亦有佛無漏種者，彼二障種俱可永害，即立彼為如來種姓。故由無漏種子有無，障有可斷、不可斷義，然無漏種微隱難知，故約彼障顯性差別。不爾，彼障有何別因而有可害、不可害者？若謂法爾有此障別，無漏法種寧不許然？若本全無無漏法種，則諸聖道永不得生，誰當能害二障種子，而說依障立種姓別？既彼聖道必無生義，說當可生，亦定非理。然諸聖教處處說有本有種子，皆違彼義，故唯始起，理、教相違。

As for establishing dispositional differences on the basis of the obstructions — this is meant precisely to indicate whether uncontaminated seeds are present. Someone with no uncontaminated seeds at all has seeds of both obstructions that can never be eliminated, and so is established as one who will never attain nirvāṇa; someone with only the uncontaminated seeds of the two lesser vehicles has cognitive-obstruction seeds that can never be eliminated, and is established with the disposition of a śrāvaka or a pratyekabuddha; someone who also has the uncontaminated seeds of buddhahood can eliminate the seeds of both obstructions entirely, and is established with the disposition of a tathāgata. The presence or absence of uncontaminated seeds, in other words, is precisely what makes the obstructions eliminable or not — but since uncontaminated seeds are too subtle to discern directly, the teaching speaks in terms of the obstructions instead.

Otherwise, what distinguishing cause would explain why the obstructions are eliminable for some and not others? If you say this difference simply exists naturally as a given — why not equally allow that uncontaminated seeds simply exist naturally too? And if there were originally no uncontaminated seeds at all, the noble path could never arise for anyone — so who would ever eliminate the two obstructions' seeds, such that dispositions could still be established on their basis? Since, on this view, the noble path genuinely could never arise, saying it merely "can arise in the future" is equally unreasonable. Yet the sacred teachings, again and again, speak of inherently pre-existent seeds — all of which the "only newly-arisen" position contradicts. It, too, conflicts with both reason and scripture.

由此應知，諸法種子各有本有、始起二類。

From this, it should be understood that the seeds of all dharmas are, in every case, of two kinds: inherently pre-existent, and newly arisen.

Reading note: The interrogation of "the mind is originally pure" is a small masterclass in philosophical pressure-testing: whatever the Vibhajyavādins mean by "mind's nature," the argument forces a dilemma — either it names an abstract principle (emptiness), which can't cause anything, or it names mind's own concrete substance, which then makes an ordinary unenlightened person's mind already secretly enlightened, an absurd result. The resolution reframes "originally pure" as pointing to suchness (真如) — mind's ultimate nature when correctly seen — not to some hidden uncontaminated core sitting inside every contaminated thought right now.

The larger payoff is a genuinely elegant reconciliation: uncontaminated seeds are inherently present from the start (explaining why liberation is possible at all, for anyone with the right disposition), but they still require perfuming — specifically, hearing the true teaching — to grow strong enough to actually mature into awakening. Practice does not create the possibility of liberation from nothing; it activates and strengthens a capacity that was already there. Both "extreme" views turn out to have been half right.

The Six Marks of a Seed 依教廣成分 · 種子六義

With the origin question settled, the treatise now gives seeds a precise technical definition — six necessary conditions, together sufficient to rule out everything that merely resembles a seed (an unchanging cosmic principle, a rival school's cross-category causation, even a literal grain of wheat) from counting as one in the strict Yogācāra sense.

Chinese Source

然種子義，略有六種。一、剎那滅。謂體纔生無間必滅，有勝功力，方成種子。此遮常法，常無轉變，不可說有能生用故。二、果俱有。謂與所生現行果法俱現和合，方成種子。此遮前後及定相離，現、種異類互不相違，一身俱時有能生用，非如種子自類相生，前後相違，必不俱有。雖因與果有俱、不俱，而現在時可有因用，未生、已滅無自體故，依生現果立種子名，不依引生自類名種，故但應說與果俱有。

Ru-Yi Translation

The meaning of "seed," in brief, involves six marks. First, momentary cessation: as soon as its substance arises, it must cease without interruption — only then, possessing a superior functional capacity, does it count as a seed. This rules out permanent things, since a changeless thing cannot be said to have any capacity to generate something new.

Second, co-existing with its result: only when it exists together, in the present moment, combined with the manifest result it produces, does it count as a seed. This rules out anything that produces its result before or after itself, or that is necessarily separate from it. A manifest activity and its seed, though different in kind, do not contradict one another, and can both have generative capacity within the same stream at the same time — unlike a seed generating a further seed of its own kind, which unfolds strictly one after another and so cannot coexist with itself. Only in the present moment can a cause have real functional force, since what has not yet arisen, or has already ceased, has no substance of its own — so "seed" is named specifically for its power to generate a manifest result, not for generating a later seed of its own kind.

三、恒隨轉。謂要長時一類相續，至究竟位，方成種子。此遮轉識，轉易間斷，與種子法不相應故。此顯種子自類相生。四、性決定。謂隨因力生善、惡等，功能決定，方成種子。此遮餘部執異性因生異性果有因緣義。

Third, constantly continuing: only when it continues, over a long span, as a single unbroken kind, all the way to its final stage, does it count as a seed. This rules out the evolving consciousnesses, which shift and are subject to interruption, and so do not fit the pattern proper to a seed-dharma — this mark describes seed generating further seed of its own kind.

Fourth, fixed nature: only when its capacity to produce a wholesome, unwholesome, or other result is fixed and determinate, in accordance with the power of its own cause, does it count as a seed. This rules out other schools' claim that a cause of one moral quality can genuinely produce a result of a different moral quality.

五、待眾緣。謂此要待自眾緣合，功能殊勝，方成種子。此遮外道執自然因不待眾緣恒頓生果；或遮餘部緣恒非無。顯所待緣非恒有性，故種於果非恒頓生。六、引自果。謂於別別色、心等果，各各引生方成種子。此遮外道執唯一因生一切果；或遮餘部執色、心等互為因緣。

Fifth, depending on the coming-together of conditions: only when it depends on its own full set of conditions coming together — at which point its capacity becomes especially potent — does it count as a seed. This rules out non-Buddhist schools' claim of a spontaneous cause that produces its result constantly and all at once, without depending on conditions, and also rules out other schools' claim that the needed conditions are simply always present. Rather, the conditions depended upon are not constantly present, so a seed does not instantly produce its result regardless of circumstances.

Sixth, drawing forth its own result: only when it draws forth, respectively, its own particular result — material or mental — does it count as a seed. This rules out non-Buddhist schools' claim that a single cause produces every result indiscriminately, and also rules out other schools' claim that material and mental dharmas can cause one another across categories.

唯本識中功能差別具斯六義，成種非餘，外穀麥等，識所變故，假立種名，非實種子。此種勢力生近、正果，名曰生因；引遠、殘果，令不頓絕，即名引因。

Only the distinct functional capacities within the root consciousness possess all six of these marks and so count as genuine seeds — nothing else does. Ordinary things like grain and wheat are themselves transformations of consciousness, and are called "seeds" only by loose, provisional analogy — they are not real seeds in this sense. A genuine seed's power to produce a near, direct result is called its "generative cause"; its power to draw forth a more distant, residual result, keeping the causal thread from breaking off entirely, is called its "drawing cause."

Reading note: Each of the six marks is doing specific defensive work against a specific rival theory, listed here for quick reference:

1. Momentary cessationRules out any eternal, unchanging entity as a seed — including a rival school's changeless cosmic principle.
2. Co-existing with its resultA seed and the activity it produces exist together, now — not a cause that acts across a temporal gap.
3. Constantly continuingRules out the six active consciousnesses (which stop, e.g. in deep sleep or absorption) from being seeds themselves — only the unbroken ālaya qualifies.
4. Fixed natureA wholesome seed cannot ripen into an unwholesome result, or vice versa — moral quality is conserved through the causal chain.
5. Depending on conditionsRules out both a "spontaneous," condition-independent cause and the claim that supporting conditions are simply always available.
6. Drawing forth its own resultA mental seed produces a mental result, a material seed a material result — rules out cross-category causation and any single cause producing everything.

The closing distinction — "generative cause" versus "drawing cause" — matters for understanding how karma unfolds over a lifetime and beyond: some seeds ripen almost immediately into the next moment of experience, while others lie in reserve, only drawn on much later, keeping a causal thread alive across what would otherwise look like a gap.

What Can Perfume, and What Can Be Perfumed 依教廣成分 · 能熏所熏四義

One term has been doing heavy lifting throughout this whole discussion — "perfuming" (熏習) — without yet being precisely defined. The treatise now gives it the same rigorous treatment just given to "seed": four necessary marks for whatever receives a perfuming, and four for whatever does the perfuming.

Four Marks of What Can Be Perfumed 所熏四義

Chinese Source

依何等義立熏習名？所熏、能熏各具四義，令種生長，故名熏習。何等名為所熏四義？一、堅住性。若法始終一類相續能持習氣，乃是所熏。此遮轉識及聲、風等，性不堅住，故非所熏。二、無記性。若法平等，無所違逆，能容習氣，乃是所熏。此遮善染勢力強盛，無所容納，故非所熏。由此如來第八淨識，唯帶舊種，非新受熏。

Ru-Yi Translation

On what basis is the term "perfuming" established? What is perfumed and what does the perfuming must each possess four marks; only then, causing seeds to grow, does the process properly count as perfuming.

The four marks of what can be perfumed are, first, stability: only something that continues, start to finish, as a single unbroken kind, capable of holding habitual tendencies, can be perfumed — ruling out the evolving consciousnesses, sound, wind, and the like, whose nature is not stable. Second, moral neutrality: only something even-tempered and unresisting, able to accommodate habitual tendencies, can be perfumed — ruling out anything with an intensely wholesome or defiled force, which has no room left to accommodate anything further. This is why the Tathāgata's pure eighth consciousness, at buddhahood, carries only its old seeds and no longer receives new perfuming.

三、可熏性。若法自在，性非堅密，能受習氣，乃是所熏。此遮心所及無為法，依他、堅密，故非所熏。四、與能熏共和合性。若與能熏同時、同處，不即、不離，乃是所熏。此遮他身、剎那前後，無和合義，故非所熏。唯異熟識具此四義，可是所熏，非心所等。

Third, receptivity: only something self-sufficient, not dense and impenetrable by nature, capable of receiving habitual tendencies, can be perfumed — ruling out the mental factors, which depend on their consciousness rather than standing on their own, and the unconditioned dharmas, which are utterly impenetrable to change. Fourth, capacity for union with what perfumes it: only something existing at the same time and place as what perfumes it, neither identical with it nor separate from it, can be perfumed — ruling out another person's mind-stream, and any moment before or after the present one, neither of which can genuinely unite with a given act of perfuming. Only the ripening consciousness possesses all four marks, and so only it can be perfumed — not the mental factors, and not anything else.

Four Marks of What Can Perfume 能熏四義

何等名為能熏四義？一、有生滅。若法非常，能有作用，生長習氣，乃是能熏。此遮無為前後不變，無生長用，故非能熏。二、有勝用。若有生滅，勢力增盛，能引習氣，乃是能熏。此遮異熟心、心所等，勢力羸劣，故非能熏。

The four marks of what can perfume are, first, having arising and ceasing: only something impermanent, capable of active function, can grow habitual tendencies, and so can perfume — ruling out the unconditioned, which never changes and has no growing function at all. Second, having superior force: only something that, besides arising and ceasing, also has a strong, vigorous force capable of drawing forth habitual tendencies can perfume — ruling out the ripening consciousness itself and its own mental factors, whose force is too weak.

三、有增減。若有勝用，可增可減，攝植習氣，乃是能熏。此遮佛果圓滿善法，無增無減，故非能熏。彼若能熏，便非圓滿，前後佛果應有勝劣。四、與所熏和合而轉。若與所熏同時、同處，不即、不離，乃是能熏。此遮他身、剎那前後，無和合義，故非能熏。唯七轉識及彼心所有勝勢用，而增減者，具此四義，可是能熏。

Third, being capable of increase and decrease: only something that, besides having superior force, can also increase or decrease, gathering and planting habitual tendencies, can perfume — ruling out the perfected wholesome dharmas of buddhahood, which neither increase nor decrease. If they could perfume, buddhahood would not truly be perfect, and one Buddha's attainment would have to outrank another's, which is absurd. Fourth, operating in union with what is perfumed: only something existing at the same time and place as what it perfumes, neither identical with it nor separate from it, can perfume — ruling out another person's mind-stream and any moment before or after the present one. Only the seven evolving consciousnesses and their associated mental factors — which have superior force and are capable of increase and decrease — possess all four marks, and so only they can perfume.

How Perfuming Actually Works 熏習義成

Chinese Source

如是能熏與所熏識，俱生俱滅，熏習義成。令所熏中種子生長，如熏苣蕂，故名熏習。能熏識等，從種生時，即能為因，復熏成種，三法展轉因果同時，如炷生焰，焰生焦炷，亦如蘆束，更互相依，因果俱時，理不傾動。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, what perfumes and what is perfumed arise and cease together, and the meaning of "perfuming" is established: it causes the seeds within what is perfumed to grow, just as sesame seeds take on fragrance from being perfumed by flowers — hence the name. When the perfuming consciousness and its factors arise from a seed, they immediately act as a cause, and perfume again to form further seeds. These three — seed, manifest activity, and new seed — thus turn upon one another as cause and effect all at the same time: just as a wick gives rise to a flame, and the flame in turn scorches the wick, or just as bundled reeds mutually lean on and depend upon one another. Cause and effect occurring together in this way is a principle nothing can shake.

Reading note: Notice how tightly the two four-mark lists mirror and complete each other: what is perfumed must be stable and neutral enough to hold an impression (only the ālaya qualifies), while what does the perfuming must be active and forceful enough to leave one (only the seven active consciousnesses qualify) — the ālaya itself is specifically excluded from perfuming anything, since a passively receptive substrate can't simultaneously be the vigorous force doing the impressing. The rule barring buddhahood's perfected qualities from perfuming anything further is a quietly important point about what awakening actually is: not an infinitely improvable state that keeps accumulating new seeds forever, but a completion.

The closing image — seed, its manifest activity, and the new seed that activity leaves behind, all turning on each other as cause and effect within the very same moment — is the treatise's complete mechanical picture of how experience and karma actually propagate, without any further soul or substance required to hold the process together. This essentially completes "holding all seeds," the ālaya's cause-characteristic. What remains is the rest of the opening verse: the ālaya's unknowable object, its constant association with the five universal mental factors, and the conditions under which it is finally relinquished.

What the Ālaya Perceives, and How It Splits into Subject and Object 依教廣成分 · 釋不可知執受、處了

The treatise now unpacks another piece of the opening verse: "grasping-and-holding, and its domain, and its discerning are unknowable." This introduces both what the ālaya takes as its object, and — for the first time in the treatise — the basic mechanism behind every act of cognition whatsoever: the split into a perceived side and a perceiving side.

Chinese Source

是謂略說一切種相。此識行相、所緣云何？謂不可知執受、處了。「了」謂了別，即是行相。識以了別為行相故。「處」謂處所，即器世間，是諸有情所依處故。「執受」有二：謂諸種子及有根身。諸種子者，謂諸相、名、分別習氣，有根身者，謂諸色根及根依處。此二皆是識所執受，攝為自體，同安危故。執受及處俱是所緣，阿賴耶識因緣力故，自體生時，內變為種及有根身，外變為器，即以所變為自所緣，行相仗之而得起故。

Ru-Yi Translation

This concludes the brief account of the characteristics of all seeds. What, then, are this consciousness's perceiving-activity and its object? The verse says its grasping-and-holding, its domain, and its discerning are unknowable. "Discerning" means cognizing and discriminating — this is its perceiving-activity, since consciousness takes discerning as its very activity. "Domain" means location, the receptacle-world, since it is what all sentient beings depend on and dwell within. "Grasping-and-holding" is of two kinds: seeds, and the body together with its faculties. "Seeds" here means the habitual tendencies of characteristics, names, and discrimination; "the body with its faculties" means the physical sense-faculties and their physical bases. Both are grasped and held by this consciousness, taken in as its very own substance, sharing the same safety and danger with it.

Both what is grasped-and-held and the receptacle-world are this consciousness's object. Through the power of its own causes and conditions, when the ālayavijñāna's own substance arises, it transforms, internally, into seeds and the body-with-faculties, and, externally, into the receptacle-world — and it is precisely this transformed appearance that becomes its own object; its perceiving-activity arises only in dependence on it.

此中「了」者，謂異熟識於自所緣有了別用，此了別用，見分所攝。然有漏識自體生時，皆似所緣、能緣相現，彼相應法應知亦爾。似所緣相說名相分，似能緣相說名見分。若心、心所無所緣相，應不能緣自所緣境，或應一一能緣一切，自境如餘，餘如自故。若心、心所無能緣相，應不能緣，如虛空等，或虛空等；亦是能緣。故心、心所必有二相。如契經說：「一切唯有覺，所覺義皆無，能覺所覺分，各自然而轉。」

Here, "discerning" means that the ripening consciousness has, with respect to its own object, a function of discriminating cognition — and this function belongs to its perceiving-aspect. Whenever a contaminated consciousness's own substance arises, it always manifests as though split into an object side and a subject side, and its associated mental factors do likewise. The side that resembles the object is called the perceived-aspect; the side that resembles the subject is called the perceiving-aspect.

If mind and mental factors had no perceived-aspect, they could not take their own respective objects as objects at all — or else each would have to be able to take absolutely anything as its object, since its own object would be no different from any other. And if mind and mental factors had no perceiving-aspect, they could not cognize anything at all, and would be no different from empty space — or else empty space itself would have to count as something that cognizes. Mind and mental factors, therefore, must necessarily have these two aspects. As scripture says, in verse: "Everything is only cognition; what is cognized has no independent reality at all — the divisions of cognizer and cognized each simply arise, each according to its own nature."

Reading note: This is the treatise's first full statement of a mechanism that applies to every single act of cognition, not just the ālaya's: consciousness never simply "meets" a pre-existing object. Instead, each moment of awareness splits itself into two correlative aspects — a perceived-aspect (相分, the "object" side) and a perceiving-aspect (見分, the "subject" side) — both manifestations of the very same act of consciousness, not an independent knower confronting an independent thing. For the ālaya specifically, this split produces its three kinds of object at once: it transforms internally into seeds and the physical body (which it grasps and holds as its own), and externally into the surrounding world.

The Four Parts of Every Act of Cognition 依教廣成分 · 三分、四分之說

This is one of the most influential passages in the entire treatise — Dharmapāla's four-part model of cognition, still discussed in Buddhist philosophy departments today. It answers a simple but deep question: when a moment of awareness happens, and you later remember having been aware, what made that memory possible?

Two Earlier Positions 執有離識境者 · 達無離識境者

Chinese Source

執有離識所緣境者，彼說外境是所緣，相分名行相，見分名事，是心、心所自體相故。心與心所同所依、緣，行相相似，事雖數等，而相各異，識、受、想等相各別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who hold that an object exists apart from consciousness say: the external object is what is grasped; the perceived-aspect is merely called the "perceiving-activity"; and the perceiving-aspect is called the "substance," since, on their view, it simply is mind and mental factors' own essential character, with nothing further needed. On this view, mind and its mental factors share the same basis and object, and resemble each other in perceiving-activity, though their number of "substances" differs in character — since consciousness, feeling, perception, and the rest each have their own distinct nature.

達無離識所緣境者，則說相分是所緣，見分名行相，相、見所依自體名事，即自證分。此若無者，應不自憶心、心所法，如不曾更境必不能憶故，心與心所同所依根，所緣相似，行相各別，了別、領納等作用各異故，事雖數等，而相各異，識、受等體有差別故。

Those who have realized that no object exists apart from consciousness say instead: the perceived-aspect is what is grasped; the perceiving-aspect is the perceiving-activity; and the underlying substance both aspects depend on is called the "self-witnessing aspect." If this did not exist, one could never remember one's own past mental states — just as one can never remember an object one has never actually experienced. Mind and its mental factors share the same underlying faculty and resemble each other in object, but differ in perceiving-activity, since their functions — discerning, receiving, and so on — are each distinct.

Three Parts, Then a Fourth 相分 · 見分 · 自證分 · 證自證分

然心、心所一一生時，以理推徵各有三分，所量、能量、量果別故，相、見必有所依體故。如《集量論》伽他中說：「似境相所量，能取相、自證，即能量及果，此三體無別。」

In fact, whenever mind or a mental factor arises, careful examination shows it has three parts: what is measured, what does the measuring, and the result of measuring are distinct, and the perceived- and perceiving-aspects must have some substance they depend on. As the Pramāṇasamuccaya says, in verse: "The aspect resembling the object is what is measured; the aspect that grasps it, and the self-witnessing, are the measuring and its result. These three have no separate substance" — three functions of one and the same consciousness.

又心、心所若細分別應有四分，三分如前，復有第四證自證分。此若無者，誰證第三？心分既同應皆證故。又自證分應無有果，諸能量者必有果故。不應見分是第三果，見分或時非量攝故。由此見分不證第三，證自體者必現量故。

Analyzed more finely, mind and mental factors should be seen to have a fourth part as well: the three already given, plus the self-witnessing of the self-witnessing. Without this fourth part, what would verify the third part itself? Since all parts of mind belong to the same kind, the third should require verification just as the first two clearly do. Furthermore, without a fourth part, the self-witnessing aspect would have no result of its own — yet whatever does the measuring must produce a result. Nor can the perceiving-aspect serve as that result, since the perceiving-aspect is sometimes mistaken, while what verifies self-witnessing must always be accurate, direct perception.

此四分中前二是外，後二是內。初唯所緣，後三通二，謂第二分但緣第一，或量、非量，或現、或比。第三能緣第二，第四證自證分，唯緣第三，非第二者，以無用故，第三、第四皆現量攝，故心、心所四分合成，具所、能緣，無無窮過，非即、非離，唯識理成。

Of these four parts, the first two are, relatively, "outer"; the latter two are "inner." The first part is purely an object, never a subject; the latter three can each serve as either. The second part takes only the first as its object, and may be accurate or mistaken, direct or inferential. The third part takes the second as its object. The fourth takes only the third as its object, not the second, since that would serve no purpose — the third and fourth are both, without exception, accurate, direct perception. Mind and mental factors are thus composed of these four parts together, each equipped with an object-side and a subject-side relative to its neighbors, with no infinite regress — the fourth verifies the third, and needs nothing further to verify itself. These four parts are neither identical with nor separate from one another; this is exactly how the truth of Consciousness-Only is established, even within a single moment of cognition.

Scriptural Support, and How Many Parts You Really Need 或攝為三、二、一

Chinese Source

是故契經伽他中說：「眾生心二性，內外一切分，所取能取纏，見種種差別。」此頌意說：眾生心性二分合成，若內、若外皆有所取、能取纏縛，見有種種，或量、非量，或現、或比，多分差別，此中見者是見分故。如是四分或攝為三，第四攝入自證分故。或攝為二，後三俱是能緣性故，皆見分攝，此言見者是能緣義。或攝為一，體無別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hence scripture says, in verse: "The nature of sentient beings' minds is twofold — in every part, inner and outer, there is the entanglement of what is grasped and what grasps, and seeing shows many kinds of difference." This means: the nature of the mind is composed of two aspects; in every part, inner or outer, there is this entanglement of grasped and grasping, and "seeing" shows many distinctions — sometimes accurate, sometimes mistaken, sometimes direct, sometimes inferential. Here "seeing" refers to the perceiving-aspect.

These four parts can equally be grouped as three, since the fourth is simply another instance of self-witnessing; or as two, since the latter three are all, by nature, functions of the subject, and so all fall under "perceiving-aspect" in a broad sense; or even as one, since ultimately no separate substance underlies any of them.

如《入楞伽》伽他中說：「由自心執著，心似外境轉，彼所見非有，是故說唯心。」如是處處說唯一心，此一心言亦攝心所。故識行相即是了別，了別即是識之見分。

As the Laṅkāvatāra says, in verse: "Because of attachment to one's own mind, mind turns as though it were an external object; what is seen this way does not exist — hence it is said to be mind-only." Scripture speaks this way, of "one mind," again and again — and this term also includes the mental factors. Consciousness's perceiving-activity, therefore, is precisely its discerning function, and this discerning function is precisely consciousness's own perceiving-aspect.

Reading note: A concrete way to hold all four parts in mind: suppose you see a red apple. The perceived-aspect (相分) is the appearance of red-apple-ness arising within consciousness — not the apple "out there," but consciousness's own image of it. The perceiving-aspect (見分) is the seeing of it — the subject-side of that same moment. Now suppose someone asks you, a moment later, "were you aware of seeing it?" You can answer yes — which means something in that original moment already registered the seeing itself, not just the apple. That registering is the self-witnessing aspect (自證分). And you can even be confident that your answer about being aware is itself accurate, not a confused guess — which requires one further, silent check behind the scenes: the self-witnessing of the self-witnessing (證自證分), the part that guarantees the self-witnessing itself isn't the kind of thing that can be mistaken, without needing yet another part behind that (it verifies itself and the third part in a single stroke, closing off what would otherwise be an infinite regress of "but who verifies the verifier?").

The passage's closing move is important and easy to miss: none of this four-way division is meant as a final metaphysical inventory. The four parts can just as validly be counted as three, two, or even one — "four" is simply the most useful resolution for explaining memory and self-awareness, not a claim that a moment of mind literally contains four separate things. This whole analysis, remember, is itself just an application of the treatise's basic method: even the inner structure of a single thought is "neither identical with nor separate from" itself, exactly like everything else examined so far.

How Billions of Private Minds Share One World 依教廣成分 · 釋「處」

If each sentient being's ālaya only ever transforms and perceives its own private stream — never touching anyone else's — how is it that everyone seems to inhabit the very same physical world? This section works out the answer.

Chinese Source

所言「處」者，謂異熟識由共相種成熟力故，變似色等器世間相，即外大種及所造色。雖諸有情所變各別而相相似，處所無異，如眾燈明各遍似一。誰異熟識變為此相？有義：一切。所以者何？如契經說：一切有情，業增上力共所起故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "domain": the ripening consciousness, through the maturing power of its shared-characteristic seeds, transforms and manifests an appearance resembling the receptacle-world of form and the rest — the outer elements and the matter composed from them. Although what each individual sentient being transforms is, strictly speaking, separate, the appearances resemble one another and occupy no distinct locations — just as the light of many lamps, each entirely filling the room, appears to be a single, unified light.

Whose ripening consciousness transforms into this shared world? One view: everyone's, without exception — as scripture says, it arises jointly through the combined power of the karma of all sentient beings.

有義：若爾，諸佛菩薩應實變為此雜穢土，諸異生等應實變為他方、此界諸淨妙土。又諸聖者厭離有色，生無色界，必不下生，變為此土，復何所用？是故現居及當生者，彼異熟識變為此界。經依少分說「一切」言，諸業同者皆共變故。

Another view: if that were so, Buddhas and bodhisattvas would still have to transform this defiled land into existence, and ordinary beings would have to transform pure lands in other worlds they have never even visited. And noble ones who, weary of form, are reborn into the formless realm certainly do not descend again — so what use would their consciousness transforming this land be? Rather, only the ripening consciousness of those presently living here, or about to be reborn here, transforms this world. Scripture's word "all" refers only to the relevant group: everyone whose karma is of the same kind, who jointly transform it together.

有義：若爾，器將壞時，既無現居及當生者，誰異熟識變為此界？又諸異生厭離有色，生無色界，現無色身，預變為土，此復何用？設有色身，與異地器，麁細懸隔，不相依持，此變為彼，亦何所益？然所變土本為色身依持、受用，故若於身可有持用便變為彼。由是設生他方自地，彼識亦得變為此土，故器世界將壞、初成，雖無有情而亦現有。此說一切共受用者，若別受用准此應知，鬼、人、天等所見異故。

Yet another view: if that were so, then at the moment the physical universe is about to be destroyed, with no one presently living there and no one yet to be reborn there, whose consciousness transforms it? And beings reborn formless, having no physical body at all, would have no use for transforming a physical land in advance for some future rebirth — and even granting they somehow did, a body suited to one realm and a world suited to an entirely different one are worlds apart, with no way to support each other, so what benefit could there possibly be?

Rather, the land that consciousness transforms exists, from the start, to support and be used by a physical body — so wherever a body could potentially make use of it, that consciousness can transform it into being there. This is why, even if one were reborn elsewhere, one's consciousness could still transform this present land too — and why, even as the physical universe is about to be destroyed, or has only just newly formed, with no sentient beings currently present, it can nevertheless still manifest as existing. This explains what is used in common by everyone; individual, differing perceptions — as ghosts, humans, and gods each see the very same thing differently — should be understood by the same reasoning, adjusted accordingly.

Reading note: This is Yogācāra working out a genuinely difficult problem: if there is only consciousness, and every consciousness is strictly private, how does a shared, public world make sense at all? The answer turns on "shared-characteristic seeds" (共相種) — seeds that, while still each individual's own, are similar enough across many beings' karma that their private transformations align into what looks and functions like one continuous world, the way many separate lamps blend into what looks like a single light rather than many overlapping ones. Nobody's private world literally merges with anyone else's; they simply resemble each other closely enough, for beings who share enough karmic history, that "the same world" is a workable description of what's actually many private, mutually-consistent transformations happening in parallel.

The famous example elsewhere in Buddhist literature — a river seen by a human as water, by a hungry ghost as pus or fire, and by a god as jeweled nectar — is exactly the phenomenon this section is setting up to explain: not one shared object interpreted differently, but different beings' consciousnesses transforming their own version of "that place" according to their own karma, aligned closely enough with humans' version to count, for practical purposes, as the same river.
