Fascicle 7 · 成唯識論卷第七

Completing the Mind, and the Case for Consciousness-Only

Fascicle 7 opens with the fourth and final category of mental factors — regret, sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought — which completes the treatise's full taxonomy of fifty-one mental factors across six categories. It then closes the long discussion of the eight consciousnesses and their accompanying factors: asking whether a mental factor is the same as mind or different from it, mapping the five special states in which consciousness itself does not manifest, and confirming that the eight consciousnesses are neither strictly one nor strictly different. The second half of the fascicle turns to new ground, opening the formal proof of the Consciousness-Only doctrine itself — with its four wisdoms, its answers to the standing objections about external color, dreams, and other minds — and closes with the treatise's account of the four conditions that govern all causation, down to the twenty-two faculties.

The Fourth Category: What Makes a Factor "Indeterminate" 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 不定 · 悔、眠

The twenty secondary afflictions have now been fully explained. An eighth root verse introduces the sixth and final category of mental factors: the four "indeterminates" — regret, sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought — so called because none of them is fixed to any one moral quality, to any one mind, or to any one meditative stage. The treatise begins with regret and sleep.

Why "Indeterminate" 總釋「不定」

Chinese Source

已說二十隨煩惱相。不定有四，其相云何？頌曰：不定謂悔、眠，尋、伺二各二。論曰：悔、眠、尋、伺於善、染等皆不定故，非如觸等定遍心故，非如欲等定遍地故，立不定名。

Ru-Yi Translation

The characteristics of the twenty secondary afflictions have now been fully explained. The indeterminate factors number four; what are their characteristics? The verse states: "The indeterminates are regret and sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought — two, each two."

The treatise states: regret, sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought are called "indeterminate" because each is indeterminate with respect to being wholesome, defiled, or otherwise; because, unlike contact and the rest, they are not fixed to accompany every mind; and because, unlike aspiration and the rest, they are not fixed to pervade every meditative stage.

Regret and Sleep Defined 釋「悔」、「眠」

Chinese Source

悔謂惡作，惡所作業，追悔為性，障止為業。此即於果假立因名，先惡所作業，後方追悔故；悔先不作，亦惡作攝，如追悔言：我先不作如是事業，是我惡作。眠謂睡眠，令身不自在，昧、略為性，障觀為業。謂睡眠位，身不自在，心極闇劣，一門轉故。昧簡在定，略別寤時，令顯睡眠非無體用。有無心位假立此名，如餘蓋纏心相應故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regret means "ill-doing": regretting a deed already done is its nature; obstructing calm is its function. This is a case of provisionally naming an effect after its cause — one first does the ill deed, and only afterward comes to regret it. Regretting having failed to do something is likewise included under "ill-doing," as when one regrets, saying: "I did not do such-and-such before — that itself was my ill-doing."

Sleep means slumber: causing the body to lose its self-mastery, and obscurity and contraction, are its nature; obstructing insight is its function. That is: in the state of sleep, the body loses its self-mastery, the mind becomes extremely dim and weak, and consciousness operates through a single channel alone. "Obscurity" distinguishes it from meditative absorption; "contraction" distinguishes it from the waking state — together showing that sleep is not without its own real substance and activity. Even in a mindless moment of sleep, this name is still provisionally extended to it, on the analogy of how the other hindrances and entanglements, too, are said to be associated with mind.

有義：此二唯癡為體，說隨煩惱及癡分故。有義：不然，亦通善故。應說此二染癡為體；淨即無癡，論依染分說隨煩惱及癡分攝。有義：此說亦不應理，無記非癡、無癡性故。應說惡作思、慧為體，明了思擇所作業故。睡眠合用思、想為體，思、想種種夢境相故。論俱說為世俗有故。彼染污者是癡等流，如不信等，說為癡分。有義：彼說理亦不然，非思、慧、想纏彼性故。應說此二各別有體，與餘心所行相別故。隨癡相說，名世俗有。

One view holds: these two have delusion alone as their essence, since they are said to be secondary afflictions and portions of delusion.

Another view holds: this is not so, since these two also extend to wholesome states. It should instead be said that these two have defiled delusion as their essence when defiled; when pure, they are simply non-delusion — the treatise's statement that they are secondary afflictions and portions of delusion refers only to their defiled portion.

Another view holds: this account, too, does not accord with reason, since a morally neutral state is neither delusion nor non-delusion in nature. It should instead be said that ill-doing has intention and discernment as its essence, since it clearly deliberates upon a deed already done; sleep, in turn, has intention and conception jointly as its essence, since intention and conception together give rise to a dream's various images. In both cases the treatise calls them conventionally existent for this very reason. Their defiled instances are simply an outflow of delusion, spoken of as "portions of delusion" just as lack of faith and the rest are.

Another view holds: this account, too, does not accord with reason, since intention, discernment, and conception, in their nature as entanglements, are not identical with these two. It should instead be said that these two each have their own distinct essence, since their mode of activity differs from every other mental factor; they are called "conventionally existent" simply because they are described in terms that follow delusion's own pattern.

Reading note: This is the sixth and final category of mental factors — after the five universal, five particular, eleven wholesome, six root afflictions, and twenty secondary afflictions, the "indeterminates" complete the treatise's full inventory of fifty-one. Their defining trait is that none of them is fixed the way the others are: contact and feeling are fixed to arise with every mind whatsoever; aspiration and the rest are fixed to particular objects; but regret, sleep, and discursive thought can attach to a wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral mind alike, arise or not arise unpredictably, and are absent in some meditative stages while present in others.

The four-round debate over whether regret and sleep reduce to delusion, or have their own essence built from intention and discernment, or conception, is a version of an argument this treatise runs repeatedly: a scripture's shorthand description (calling something "a portion of delusion") is not automatically proof of strict identity, especially once a counterexample turns up — here, the fact that regret and sleep can occur in a wholesome mind, where delusion by definition cannot.

Coarse and Subtle Discursive Thought 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 不定 · 尋、伺

The remaining two indeterminates are a closely linked pair — one broad and coarse, one narrow and precise — both built from the same underlying materials, and the treatise turns next to a debate over what the verse's compressed phrase "two, each two" actually means.

Seeking Out and Examining Closely 釋「尋」、「伺」

Chinese Source

尋謂尋求，令心怱遽於意言境，麁轉為性；伺謂伺察，令心怱遽於意言境，細轉為性。此二俱以安、不安住身心分位所依為業。並用思、慧一分為體，於意言境不深推度及深推度，義類別故，若離思、慧，尋、伺二種體類差別不可得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Coarse discursive thought means seeking out: causing the mind to move hastily over a mentally-conceived, name-bound object in a coarse manner is its nature. Subtle discursive thought means examining closely: causing the mind to move hastily over a mentally-conceived, name-bound object in a subtle manner is its nature. Both have as their function providing the basis for the body and mind's abiding, whether at ease or ill at ease. Both take a portion of intention and discernment jointly as their essence, distinguished by whether one probes an object shallowly or deeply — for apart from intention and discernment, no distinct essential kind for coarse and subtle discursive thought can be found at all.

What "Two, Each Two" Really Means 釋「二各二」

Chinese Source

「二各二」者，有義：尋、伺各有染、淨二類差別。有義：此釋不應正理，悔、眠亦有染、淨二故。應說如前諸染心所，有是煩惱、隨煩惱性，此二各有不善、無記，或復各有纏及隨眠。有義：彼釋亦不應理，不定四後有此言故。應言「二」者顯二種二：一謂悔、眠；二謂尋、伺。此二二種，種類各別，故一「二」言，顯二二種。此各有二，謂染、不染。非如善、染各唯一故。或唯簡染，故說此言，有亦說為隨煩惱故。為顯不定義，說「二各二」言，故置此言深為有用。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "two, each two": one view holds that coarse and subtle discursive thought each have two distinct kinds, defiled and pure.

Another view holds this does not accord with correct principle, since regret and sleep, too, have both a defiled and a pure kind. It should instead be said, as with the various defiled mental factors already discussed, that some are afflictions in the strict sense and others secondary afflictions — these two indeterminates each have an unwholesome and a neutral kind, or, alternatively, each has both an active entanglement and a dormant underlying tendency.

Another view holds that this, too, does not accord with reason, since the phrase comes only after all four indeterminates have already been named. It should instead be said that "two" indicates two sets of two: one set is regret and sleep; the other is coarse and subtle discursive thought. These two sets differ in kind, and so the single word "two" indicates two twofold sets. Each of the four, in turn, has two aspects — defiled and undefiled — unlike the wholesome and defiled categories, each of which is only of a single kind. Or else the phrase serves simply to set the defiled instances apart, since some are also spoken of as secondary afflictions. The phrase "two, each two" is stated precisely to make the meaning of "indeterminate" clear — so this wording, in fact, is put to very good use.

Real or Merely Conventional? 四中假、實

Chinese Source

四中尋、伺定是假有，思、慧合成，聖所說故。悔、眠，有義：亦是假有，《瑜伽》說為世俗有故。有義：此二是實物有，唯後二種說假有故。世俗有言隨他相說，非顯前二定是假有，又如內種體雖是實，而論亦說世俗有故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these four, coarse and subtle discursive thought are definitely conventionally existent, formed jointly out of intention and discernment, as the sages themselves have stated.

As for regret and sleep — one view holds that these, too, are conventionally existent, since the Yogācārabhūmi describes them as conventionally existent. Another view holds that these two are genuinely, substantially real things, since that same passage speaks of conventional existence only with reference to the latter two. The term "conventionally existent," when applied to regret and sleep, follows the pattern of describing something in terms of what it resembles, and does not show that the first two must be conventionally existent — just as the inner seeds, though genuinely real in essence, are nevertheless also spoken of by the treatise as "conventionally existent."

Reading note: The verse's four characters, "二各二" ("two, each two"), compress an entire debate into a phrase almost too terse to parse — the treatise's own preferred reading takes it as identifying two natural pairs (regret-and-sleep, seeking-and-examining) rather than saying each of the four factors comes in two moral flavors, though it acknowledges that reading is available too. This kind of close grammatical debate over a single compressed verse-phrase recurs throughout the treatise; it is worth remembering that the root verses were composed to be memorized, and density was the point — the prose commentary exists precisely to unpack ambiguities like this one.

The Fifty-First Factor: A Milestone 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 不定 · 四之料簡

The treatise now cross-tabulates the four indeterminates against everything already established — consciousness, feeling, the other mental factors, moral quality, realm, and the path of elimination — in the same systematic way it closed out the wholesome factors and the two categories of affliction. With this cross-tabulation complete, all fifty-one mental factors, across all six categories, will have been fully accounted for.

Mutual Compatibility, and Which Consciousnesses They Accompany 四之俱起、八識相應

Chinese Source

四中尋、伺定不相應，體類是同，麁、細異故。依於尋、伺有染、離染立三地別，不依彼種現起有無，故無雜亂。俱與前二容互相應，前二亦有互相應義。四皆不與第七、八俱，義如前說；悔、眠唯與第六識俱，非五法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these four, coarse and subtle discursive thought definitely never co-occur with each other, since, though the same in kind, they differ as coarse and subtle. It is with reference to the presence or absence of coarse and subtle discursive thought that the three meditative stages are distinguished — not with reference to whether their seeds are manifestly active — so there is no confusion involved. Both can occur together with the first two, regret and sleep, and the first two, in turn, can also occur together with each other.

None of the four is ever associated with the seventh or eighth consciousness, for reasons already explained above; regret and sleep are associated only with the sixth consciousness, since they are not among the five sense-consciousnesses' own factors.

有義：尋、伺亦五識俱，論說：五識有尋、伺故。又說：尋、伺即七分別，謂有相等。《雜集》復言：任運分別謂五識故。有義：尋、伺唯意識俱。論說：尋求、伺察等法皆是意識不共法故。又說：尋、伺憂、喜相應，曾不說與苦、樂俱故。捨受遍故，可不待說。何緣不說與苦、樂俱？雖初靜慮有意地樂，而不離喜，總說喜名；雖純苦處有意地苦，而似憂故，總說為憂。又說：尋、伺以名身等義為所緣，非五識身以名身等義為境故。然說五識有尋、伺者，顯多由彼起，非說彼相應。《雜集》所言：任運分別謂五識者，彼與《瑜伽》所說分別，義各有異：彼說任運即是五識，《瑜伽》說此是五識俱分別意識相應尋、伺。故彼所引為證不成，由此五識定無尋、伺。

One view holds that coarse and subtle discursive thought, too, can be associated with the five sense-consciousnesses, since the treatise states that the five sense-consciousnesses possess coarse and subtle discursive thought, and states, further, that coarse and subtle discursive thought are precisely the "seven kinds of discrimination," including discrimination-with-respect-to-sign and the rest. The Abhidharmasamuccaya likewise says that spontaneous discrimination refers to the five sense-consciousnesses.

Another view holds that coarse and subtle discursive thought are associated only with the mental consciousness. The treatise states that seeking-out and close-examination are exclusive properties of the mental consciousness, and states, further, that they are associated with sorrow and joy but never with pain or pleasure — since they are pervasively associated with equanimous feeling, this need not be separately stated. Why is it not said that they occur with pain and pleasure? Although the first meditative absorption possesses a mental pleasure, this is never separated from joy, and so is simply called by the name "joy"; although a realm of pure suffering possesses a mental pain, this resembles sorrow, and so is simply called "sorrow." It is stated, further, that coarse and subtle discursive thought take the meaning of names and terms as their object, whereas the five sense-consciousnesses do not take the meaning of names and terms as their object at all.

As for the statement that the five sense-consciousnesses "possess" coarse and subtle discursive thought — this shows only that they largely arise on account of these, not that they are literally associated with them. As for the Abhidharmasamuccaya's statement that spontaneous discrimination refers to the five sense-consciousnesses — its meaning differs from what the Yogācārabhūmi calls "discrimination": that text speaks of the spontaneous kind as being precisely the five sense-consciousnesses themselves, while the Yogācārabhūmi speaks of this as the coarse and subtle discursive thought that co-occurs with the five sense-consciousnesses yet is associated with the mental consciousness. Hence what is cited does not succeed as proof — and for this reason, the five sense-consciousnesses definitely lack coarse and subtle discursive thought.

Feeling, the Particular Factors, and the Wholesome and Afflicted Categories 四之受、別境、善、煩惱相應

Chinese Source

有義：惡作憂、捨相應，唯慼行轉，通無記故。睡眠喜、憂、捨受俱起，行通歡、慼、中庸轉故。尋、伺憂、喜、捨、樂相應，初靜慮中意樂俱故。有義：此四亦苦受俱，純苦趣中，意苦俱故。四皆容與五別境俱，行相、所緣不相違故。悔、眠但與十善容俱，此唯在欲，無輕安故。尋、伺容與十一善俱，初靜慮中輕安俱故。悔但容與無明相應，此行相麁，貪等細故。睡眠、尋、伺十煩惱俱，此彼展轉不相違故。悔與中、大隨惑容俱，非忿等十，各為主故。睡眠、尋、伺二十容俱，眠等位中皆起彼故。

Ru-Yi Translation

One view holds: ill-doing is associated with sorrow and equanimity, since it operates only in a distressed mode, though it can also be morally neutral. Sleep arises together with joy, sorrow, and equanimous feeling, since it can operate in a joyful, distressed, or neutral mode alike. Coarse and subtle discursive thought are associated with sorrow, joy, equanimity, and pleasure, since the first meditative absorption possesses a mental pleasure occurring together with them. Another view holds: these four are also associated with painful feeling, since a realm of pure suffering possesses a mental pain occurring together with them as well.

All four can occur together with the five particular mental factors, since their modes of activity and objects are not mutually incompatible.

Regret and sleep can occur together with only ten of the eleven wholesome factors, since these two exist only in the desire realm, which lacks pliancy. Coarse and subtle discursive thought can occur together with all eleven, since the first meditative absorption possesses pliancy occurring together with them.

Regret can occur together with only one of the fundamental afflictions, ignorance, since its mode of activity is coarse, while craving and the rest are subtle. Sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought, can occur together with all ten fundamental afflictions, since these do not mutually conflict.

Regret can occur together with the middling and major secondary afflictions, but not with wrath and the other nine minor ones, since each of these minor afflictions must itself take the leading role whenever present. Sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought, can occur together with all twenty secondary afflictions, since these can all arise during sleep and states like it.

Moral Quality, Realm, Reach, and the Path of Elimination 四之三性、界地、迷諦所斷

Chinese Source

此四皆通善等三性，於無記業亦追悔故。有義：初二唯生得善，行相麁鄙及昧略故；後二亦通加行善攝，聞所成等有尋、伺故。有義：初二亦加行善，聞、思位中有悔、眠故。後三皆通染、淨無記，惡作非染，解麁猛故。四無記中，悔唯中二，行相麁猛，非定果故。眠除第四，非定引生，異熟生心亦得眠故。尋、伺除初，彼解微劣，不能尋察名等義故。惡作、睡眠唯欲界有，尋、伺在欲及初靜慮，餘界地法皆妙靜故。悔、眠生上必不現起，尋、伺上下亦起下上。下上尋、伺能緣上下。有義：悔、眠不能緣上，行相麁近，極昧略故。有義：此二亦緣上境，有邪見者悔修定故，夢能普緣所更事故。

Ru-Yi Translation

All four extend to the three moral qualities — wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral — alike, since one can even come to regret a morally neutral deed. One view holds: the first two, regret and sleep, belong only to innate wholesomeness, since their mode of activity is coarse, unrefined, obscure, and contracted; the latter two also extend to wholesomeness cultivated through effort, since wholesome states arisen from learning and the like possess coarse and subtle discursive thought. Another view holds: the first two, too, can belong to wholesomeness cultivated through effort, since regret and sleep are present during the stages of learning and reflection.

The latter three extend to defiled-neutral and undefiled-neutral states alike; ill-doing does not extend to the defiled-neutral, since its understanding is too coarse and forceful. Among the four kinds of neutral state — the karmically-resultant, the deportment-related, the craft-related, and the meditation-produced — regret belongs only to the middle two, since its mode of activity is coarse and forceful, and it is not a product of meditative concentration; sleep is absent only from the meditation-produced kind, since it is not itself brought about by concentration, though even a karmically-resultant mind can experience sleep; coarse and subtle discursive thought are absent only from the craft-related kind, since that understanding is too feeble to seek out or examine the meaning of names and the like.

Ill-doing and sleep exist only in the desire realm; coarse and subtle discursive thought exist in the desire realm and the first meditative absorption, since the dharmas of every other realm and stage are subtle and still.

Regret and sleep, once one is born into an upper realm, certainly never manifest again; coarse and subtle discursive thought bound to a lower or upper stage can each still take the other as their object. One view holds: regret and sleep cannot take an upper realm as their object, since their mode of activity is coarse, immediate, and extremely obscure and contracted. Another view holds: these two, too, can take an upper realm as their object, since one who holds a wrong view can regret having cultivated meditative concentration, and a dream can range universally over anything one has ever experienced.

悔非無學，離欲捨故；睡眠、尋、伺皆通三種。求解脫者有為善法皆名學故；學究竟者有為善法皆無學故。悔、眠唯通見、修所斷，亦邪見等勢力起故，非無漏道親所引生故，亦非如憂，深求解脫故。若已斷故名非所斷，則無學眠非所斷、攝。尋、伺雖非真無漏道，而能引彼，從彼引生，故通見、修、非所斷攝。有義：尋、伺非所斷者，於五法中唯分別攝，《瑜伽》說彼是分別故。有義：此二亦正智攝，說：正思惟是無漏故，彼能令心尋求等故。又說：彼是言說因故，未究竟位於藥、病等未能遍知，後得智中為他說法必假尋、伺，非如佛地無功用說，故此二種亦通無漏。雖說尋、伺必是分別，而不定說唯屬第三，後得正智中亦有分別故。餘門准上，如理應思。

Regret does not belong to the beyond-training category, since it is abandoned once one is free of desire; sleep, and coarse and subtle discursive thought, all extend to all three categories — trainee, beyond-training, and neither. Every conditioned wholesome dharma belonging to one still seeking liberation is called "training"; every conditioned wholesome dharma belonging to one who has completed the training is called "beyond training."

Regret and sleep are eliminated only on the path of seeing or the path of cultivation, since they, too, arise through the power of wrong view and the like, are never directly brought forth by the uncontaminated path itself, and, unlike sorrow in its deeper form, do not arise from a genuine search for liberation. Since something is called "not subject to elimination" precisely because it has already been eliminated, sleep in one beyond training should likewise be classified as not subject to elimination. Coarse and subtle discursive thought, though not themselves the genuine uncontaminated path, are nevertheless capable of leading toward it, and can also be brought forth by it in turn — hence they extend to the path of seeing, the path of cultivation, and the not-subject-to-elimination category alike.

One view holds: coarse and subtle discursive thought, in their not-subject-to-elimination aspect, are included, among the five categories of dharmas, only under "discrimination," since the Yogācārabhūmi states that they are discrimination. Another view holds: these two are also included under correct wisdom — for it is said that correct intention is itself uncontaminated, since it is what causes the mind to seek out and the like; and it is said, further, that they are the cause of verbal expression, since one who has not yet reached the ultimate stage cannot yet fully know matters such as medicine and illness, and subsequently-attained wisdom, when teaching others, must necessarily rely on coarse and subtle discursive thought, unlike the effortless teaching found at the stage of buddhahood — hence these two also extend to the uncontaminated. Although it is said that coarse and subtle discursive thought must be discrimination, this does not mean they belong exclusively to the third of the five categories, since subsequently-attained correct wisdom, too, involves discrimination.

Every remaining point of classification should be considered by analogy with what has already been explained above.

Reading note: With this cross-tabulation, all fifty-one mental factors are now fully accounted for: the five universal factors, the five particular factors, the eleven wholesome factors, the six root afflictions, the twenty secondary afflictions, and, with this section, the four indeterminates. This is a genuine milestone — the treatise's entire map of the mind's possible contents, built up piece by piece since early in this fascicle sequence, is now complete. Everything that follows in the treatise builds on this finished taxonomy rather than adding new entries to it.

Worth noticing on the way to that milestone: coarse and subtle discursive thought turn out to be the most flexible of all fifty-one factors — compatible with every one of the four painful-to-pleasant feelings, present through both mundane and uncontaminated states, and even capable of accompanying a buddha's own subsequently-attained wisdom when teaching others in ordinary language. This is a quiet but important point: even complete awakening does not eliminate discursive thought as such, only its compulsive, self-referential misuse — a buddha teaching the dharma in words still, in some sense, "thinks it through" in order to speak, just without any of the distortion an unawakened mind brings to the same activity.

Mind and Its Factors: Neither the Same, Nor Different 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 料簡六位心所 · 心所與心一異

With all fifty-one mental factors now defined, the treatise pauses to ask a foundational question about the whole taxonomy just completed: are these mental factors genuinely distinct things existing apart from mind itself, or are they simply mind's own shifting configurations, given fifty-one different names? Both answers, taken on their own, run into scriptural difficulty.

Two Horns, Two Difficulties 釋一異之難

Chinese Source

如是六位諸心所法，為離心體有別自性？為即是心分位差別？設爾何失？二俱有過。若離心體有別自性，如何聖教說唯有識？又如何說心遠、獨行，染、淨由心，士夫六界？《莊嚴論》說復云何通？如彼頌言：許心似二現，如是似貪等，或似於信等，無別染、善法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Are these mental factors, across all six categories, possessed of a distinct essence apart from mind itself? Or are they simply mind's own positional differences? Suppose either horn — what is the difficulty? Both alternatives involve faults.

If they have a distinct essence apart from mind itself, then how can the sacred teaching say that only consciousness exists? And how can it be said that mind travels far and moves alone, that defilement and purity are governed by mind, and that a person is composed of six elements? How, moreover, can the Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra's own statement be reconciled with this? As that treatise's verse says: "It is granted that mind manifests appearing as two aspects; in just the same way, it appears as craving and the rest, or as faith and the rest — there being no separate defiled or wholesome dharmas apart from this."

若即是心分位差別，如何聖教說心相應？他性相應，非自性故。又如何說心與心所俱時而起，如日與光？《瑜伽論》說復云何通？彼說心所非即心故。如彼頌言：五種性不成，分位差過失，因緣無別故，與聖教相違。

If, instead, they are simply mind's own positional differences, then how can the sacred teaching speak of mind's "association" with mental factors — since "association" by definition means association between two dharmas of differing nature, not with one's own nature? And how can it be said that mind and mental factors arise together at the same moment, like the sun and its light? How, moreover, can the Yogācārabhūmi's own statement be reconciled with this, since it states that mental factors are not simply identical with mind? As that treatise's verse says: "The five categories would not be established; this is the fault of mere positional difference; since cause and condition would then be no different between the two, this contradicts the sacred teaching."

Neither Separate Nor the Same 釋「非離非即」

Chinese Source

應說離心有別自性，以心勝故，說唯識等，心所依心勢力生故，說似彼現，非彼即心。又識心言亦攝心所，恒相應故。唯識等言及現似彼，皆無有失，此依世俗。若依勝義，心所與心非離非即，諸識相望應知亦然，是謂大乘真、俗妙理。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be said: mental factors have a distinct essence apart from mind. Because mind is the more prominent factor, it is said that "only consciousness" exists; because mental factors arise dependent upon mind's own power, it is said that they "appear resembling it" — not that they are simply identical with mind. Moreover, the terms "consciousness" and "mind" also include mental factors within their own scope, since these are constantly associated together. Hence both the statement "only consciousness" and the statement that mental factors "appear resembling" mind are equally free of fault — this, however, is spoken from the conventional standpoint.

From the ultimate standpoint, mental factors and mind are neither separate nor identical; and the same should be understood to hold for how the various consciousnesses relate to one another as well. This is the subtle principle governing the Mahāyāna's ultimate and conventional truths.

Reading note: This short section closes the entire six-category discussion of mental factors with a genuinely elegant resolution: rather than picking one horn of the dilemma, the treatise dissolves it by distinguishing two levels of truth. Conventionally, mind is spoken of as "prominent" and mental factors as merely "resembling" or "appearing like" its own activity — which is why calling the doctrine "Consciousness-Only" is not false, even though mental factors are, in fact, real. Ultimately, though, the very question "same or different?" turns out to rest on a false choice: mind and its factors are so intimately bound — arising, ceasing, and taking the same object together — that neither "one thing" nor "two separate things" fully captures the relationship.

The closing line is easy to read past but is doing real structural work for the rest of the treatise: "the same should be understood to hold for how the various consciousnesses relate to one another as well." This quietly extends the same non-identity, non-difference logic to the relationship between the eight consciousnesses themselves — the exact question the treatise takes up next, and the very question that will occupy the remainder of this fascicle before it turns to proving Consciousness-Only outright.

The Yogācārabhūmi's citation verse, though extremely compressed, most plausibly points back to the standard fivefold classification of all dharmas established early in this system — mind, mental factors, form, factors dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned. If mental factors were nothing but mind's own positional variants, that classification's second category would simply collapse into its first, which is precisely the "fault" the verse names.

When the Eight Consciousnesses Fall Silent 明唯識相 · 釋第三能變 · 廣六位相 · 現起分位

Having finished the mind's contents, the treatise turns to when the eight consciousnesses actually arise. A ninth root verse explains how the root consciousness, the five sense-consciousnesses, and the mental consciousness each follow a different rhythm of activity — and names five special states in which even the constantly-active mental consciousness falls silent.

The Verse: How the Eight Consciousnesses Arise 頌釋現起分位

Chinese Source

已說六識心所相應，云何應知現起分位？頌曰：依止根本識，五識隨緣現，或俱或不俱，如濤波依水。意識常現起，除生無想天，及無心二定，睡眠與悶絕。

Ru-Yi Translation

The six consciousnesses' association with mental factors has now been explained. How should their conditions of arising be understood? The verse states: "Resting on the root consciousness, the five sense-consciousnesses arise according to conditions — together, or not together — like waves resting upon water. The mental consciousness constantly arises, except when born in the heaven of no-conception, in the two mindless concentrations, in sleep, and in fainting."

論曰：「根本識」者，阿陀那識，染、淨諸識生根本故。「依止」者，謂前六轉識以根本識為共親依。「五識」者，謂前五轉識，種類相似，故總說之。「隨緣現」言顯非常起，「緣」謂作意、根、境等緣，謂五識身內依本識，外隨作意、五根、境等眾緣和合方得現前，由此或俱或不俱起，外緣合者有頓、漸故。如水濤波隨緣多少，此等法喻廣說如經。

The treatise states: "the root consciousness" refers to the ādāna-consciousness, since it is the root from which all defiled and pure consciousnesses arise. "Resting on" means that the first six transforming consciousnesses take the root consciousness as their shared, direct support. "The five consciousnesses" refers to the first five transforming consciousnesses, spoken of together since they are similar in kind. The phrase "arise according to conditions" shows that they do not arise constantly — "conditions" meaning attention, the sense faculties, the object, and the rest: the five sense-consciousness-bodies rely inwardly on the root consciousness, and, depending outwardly on the coming together of attention, the five sense faculties, their objects, and the rest of the many conditions, only then do they actually manifest. On this account they arise either together or not together, since the outer conditions may converge all at once or only gradually — like water's waves, rising according to however many conditions converge. This simile, and further teachings of this kind, are explained at length in the sūtras.

由五轉識行相麁動，所藉眾緣時多不具，故起時少，不起時多。第六意識雖亦麁動，而所藉緣無時不具，由違緣故，有時不起。第七、八識行相微細，所藉眾緣一切時有，故無緣礙令總不行。又五識身不能思慮，唯外門轉起，藉多緣，故斷時多，現行時少；第六意識自能思慮，內、外門轉，不藉多緣。唯除五位，常能現起，故斷時少，現起時多，由斯不說此隨緣現。

Because the mode of activity of the five transforming consciousnesses is coarse and unstable, the many conditions they depend on are often incomplete, and so the times they arise are few, and the times they do not arise are many. The sixth, the mental consciousness, though its mode of activity is likewise coarse, nevertheless never lacks the conditions it depends on — yet, owing to obstructing conditions, there are still times it does not arise. The seventh and eighth consciousnesses have a subtle mode of activity, and the many conditions they depend on are present at all times, so no obstructing condition is capable of preventing their activity altogether.

Furthermore, the five sense-consciousness-bodies are incapable of deliberation, and operate only outwardly, depending on many conditions — hence the times they are interrupted are many, and the times they are manifestly active are few. The sixth, the mental consciousness, is itself capable of deliberation, and operates both inwardly and outwardly, without depending on many conditions. Except in five particular states, it is constantly capable of manifesting — hence the times it is interrupted are few, and the times it is manifestly active are many. For this reason, it is not described as "arising according to conditions" in the same way as the five sense-consciousnesses.

The Heaven of No-Conception 釋「生無想天」

Chinese Source

五位者何？「生無想」等。無想天者，謂修彼定，厭麁想力，生彼天中，違不恒行心及心所，想滅為首，名無想天。故六轉識於彼皆斷。有義：彼天常無六識，聖教說彼無轉識故；說彼唯有有色支故；又說彼為無心地故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are these five states? "Born in the heaven of no-conception," and the rest. The heaven of no-conception is where, through cultivating that concentration and the power of aversion toward coarse conceptualization, one is born into that heaven; there, the mind and mental factors that do not operate constantly are suppressed, with the cessation of conceptualization foremost among them — hence it is called "the heaven of no-conception." The six transforming consciousnesses, accordingly, are all interrupted there.

One view holds: in that heaven, the six consciousnesses are permanently absent, since the sacred teaching states that beings there lack the transforming consciousnesses, states that they possess only the physical constituent among the five aggregates, and states, further, that this is a mindless stage.

有義：彼天將命終位，要起轉識然後命終，彼必起下潤生愛故，《瑜伽論》說：「後想生已，是諸有情從彼沒」故。然說彼無轉識等者，依長時說，非謂全無。有義：生時亦有轉識，彼中有必起潤生煩惱故，如餘本有，初必有轉識故。《瑜伽論》說：「若生於彼，唯入不起，其想若生，從彼沒」故，彼本有初，若無轉識，如何名入？先有後無，乃名入故。

Another view holds: in that heaven, at the moment approaching death, the transforming consciousnesses must arise before death actually occurs, since one must give rise to the craving that moistens rebirth into a lower state; the Yogācārabhūmi states: "once conceptualization has arisen again, these beings then pass away from that state." The statement that they lack the transforming consciousnesses refers only to the long duration of that state, not to their being utterly absent throughout.

Another view holds: at the moment of birth there, too, the transforming consciousnesses are present, since the intermediate-state being must give rise to the affliction that moistens rebirth, just as, in every other state of embodied existence, the transforming consciousnesses are necessarily present at its very outset. The Yogācārabhūmi states: "once born there, one only enters and does not arise again; once conceptualization arises, one passes away from that state" — if, at the very outset of that existence, the transforming consciousnesses were entirely absent, how could this be called "entering" at all? Only where something is present first and then absent can it properly be called "entering."

〈決擇分〉言：「所有生得心、心所滅，名無想」故，此言意顯彼本有初，有異熟生轉識暫起宿因緣力，後不復生。由斯引起異熟無記分位差別，說名無想，如善引生二定名善。不爾，轉識一切不行，如何可言唯生得滅？故彼初位轉識暫起。彼天唯在第四靜慮，下想麁動難可斷故，上無無想異熟處故。即能引發無想定思，能感彼天異熟果故。

The Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī states: "the cessation of whatever mind and mental factors are innately produced is called no-conception." This shows that, at the very outset of that existence, the karmically-resultant transforming consciousnesses briefly arise through the power of a prior causal condition, and thereafter do not arise again. It is on account of this that the karmically-resultant, morally-neutral positional difference thus brought about is called "no-conception," just as the two wholesome concentrations are called "wholesome" because they are brought forth by a wholesome cause. Were this not so — were the transforming consciousnesses never active there at all — how could it be said that only what is innately produced ceases? Hence, at that initial stage, the transforming consciousnesses do briefly arise.

That heaven exists only within the fourth meditative absorption, since, below it, conceptualization is too coarse and unstable to be readily suppressed, and, above it, there is no place bearing the karmic result of no-conception. It is precisely the volition that brings forth the concentration of no-conception that is capable of producing that heaven's karmic result.

The Concentration of No-Conception 釋「無想定」

Chinese Source

「及無心二定」者，謂無想、滅盡定，俱無六識，故名無心。無想定者，謂有異生伏遍淨貪，未伏上染，由出離想，作意為先，令不恒行心、心所滅，想滅為首，立無想名，令身安和，故亦名定。

Ru-Yi Translation

"And the two mindless concentrations" refers to the concentration of no-conception and the concentration of cessation — both entirely lack the six consciousnesses, and are therefore called "mindless."

The concentration of no-conception: an ordinary being who has suppressed craving for the third absorption's pervasive purity, but has not yet suppressed defilement toward what lies above it, through an aspiration for liberation, preceded by a specific attention, causes the mind and mental factors that do not operate constantly to cease, with the cessation of conceptualization foremost among them — hence it is named "no-conception." It causes the body to become peaceful and harmonious, and so is also called a "concentration."

修習此定，品別有三：下品修者，現法必退，不能速疾還引現前，後生彼天，不甚光淨，形色廣大，定當中夭。中品修者，現不必退，設退速疾還引現前，後生彼天，雖甚光淨，形色廣大，而不最極，雖有中夭而不決定。上品修者，現必不退，後生彼天，最極光淨，形色廣大，必無中夭，窮滿壽量後方殞沒。

Its cultivation divides into three grades. One who cultivates it at the lowest grade will certainly regress from it within this very life, and cannot quickly bring it forth again; when later born into that heaven, one will not be especially radiant and pure, one's physical form will be vast, and one will certainly die prematurely. One who cultivates it at the middle grade need not regress from it, and, should one regress, can quickly bring it forth again; when later born into that heaven, though quite radiant and pure, with a vast physical form, one will not be at the utmost extreme, and although premature death may occur, it is not certain. One who cultivates it at the highest grade will certainly not regress from it; when later born into that heaven, one will be radiant and pure to the utmost degree, with a vast physical form, will certainly not die prematurely, and will pass away only after the full span of one's lifetime is exhausted.

此定唯屬第四靜慮。又唯是善，彼所引故。下上地無，由前說故。四業通三，除順現受。有義：此定唯欲界起，由諸外道說力起故，人中慧解極猛利故。有義：欲界先修習已，後生色界能引現前，除無想天至究竟故。此由厭想、欣彼果入，故唯有漏。非聖所起。

This concentration belongs exclusively to the fourth meditative absorption. It is, moreover, exclusively wholesome, since it is brought forth by a wholesome cause. It is absent from every stage below or above, for the reasons already explained. It extends to three of the four categories of karmic ripening, excluding only that ripening experienced within the very life in which the act is performed.

One view holds: this concentration arises only in the desire realm, since it is brought forth by the persuasive power of non-Buddhist teachers, and the understanding of those in the human realm is especially sharp and forceful. Another view holds: having first cultivated it in the desire realm, one can later, born in the form realm, bring it forth again, except at the very culmination of the heaven of no-conception itself. This concentration is entered through aversion toward conceptualization and delight in that heaven's result, and so it is exclusively contaminated — it is never brought forth by a noble one.

The Concentration of Cessation 釋「滅盡定」

Chinese Source

滅盡定者，謂有無學或有學聖，已伏或離無所有貪，上貪不定，由止息想，作意為先，令不恒行、恒行染污心、心所滅，立滅盡名，令身安和，故亦名定。由偏厭受、想，亦名滅彼定。修習此定，品別有三：下品修者，現法必退，不能速疾還引現前；中品修者，現不必退，設退速疾還引現前；上品修者，畢竟不退。

Ru-Yi Translation

The concentration of cessation: a noble one, whether beyond training or still in training, who has suppressed or become free of craving for the sphere of nothingness — craving for what lies above this being not fixed either way — through an aspiration for stillness, preceded by a specific attention, causes both the mind and mental factors that do not operate constantly, and the defiled ones among those that do operate constantly, to cease; hence it is named "cessation." It causes the body to become peaceful and harmonious, and so is also called a "concentration." Because it especially involves aversion toward feeling and conceptualization, it is also called "the concentration that extinguishes these two."

Its cultivation likewise divides into three grades: one who cultivates it at the lowest grade will certainly regress from it within this very life, and cannot quickly bring it forth again; one who cultivates it at the middle grade need not regress from it, and, should one regress, can quickly bring it forth again; one who cultivates it at the highest grade will never regress from it at all.

此定初修必依有頂，遊觀無漏為加行入，次第定中最居後故，雖屬有頂而無漏攝。若修此定已得自在，餘地心後，亦得現前。雖屬道諦而是非學非無學攝，似涅槃故。此定初起唯在人中，佛及弟子說力起故，人中慧解極猛利故，後上二界亦得現前，《鄔陀夷經》是此誠證，無色亦名意成天故。於藏識教未信受者，若生無色不起此定，恐無色、心成斷滅故；已信生彼亦得現前，知有藏識不斷滅故。

This concentration, when first cultivated, must be entered by relying on the peak of existence, using uncontaminated contemplative insight as its preparatory practice, since it comes last among the concentrations arranged in sequence — though it belongs to the peak of existence, it is nevertheless classified as uncontaminated. Once one has attained mastery in cultivating it, it can also manifest following a mind of any other stage. Though it belongs to the truth of the path, it is classified as neither a trainee's nor a beyond-training's attainment, since it resembles nirvāṇa itself.

This concentration, when first arisen, occurs only among humans, since it is brought forth by the persuasive power of the Buddha and his disciples, and the understanding of those in the human realm is especially sharp and forceful; afterward, it can also manifest in the two upper realms — the Udāyī Sūtra serves as genuine proof of this, since the formless realm, too, is called "the heaven of mind alone." One who has not yet come to believe in the teaching of the storehouse consciousness, if born into the formless realm, will not bring forth this concentration, fearing that, in the absence of form, the mind itself would be utterly annihilated; one who already believes, however, can bring it forth even when born there, knowing that the storehouse consciousness exists and is never utterly annihilated.

要斷三界見所斷惑方起此定，異生不能伏滅有頂心、心所故，此定微妙，要證二空隨應後得所引發故。有義：下八地修所斷惑中，要全斷欲，餘伏或斷，然後方能初起此定，欲界惑種二性繁雜，障定強故，唯說不還、三乘無學及諸菩薩得此定故，彼隨所應生上八地皆得後起。有義：要斷下之四地修所斷惑，餘伏或斷，然後方能初起此定，變異受俱煩惱種子障定強故，彼隨所應，生上五地，皆得後起。

One must have eliminated the afflictions eliminated on the path of seeing throughout the three realms before this concentration can arise at all, since an ordinary being is incapable of suppressing and eliminating the mind and mental factors of the peak of existence; this concentration is subtle and profound, brought forth only through the subsequently-attained wisdom that arises, as appropriate, from realizing the two emptinesses.

One view holds: among the afflictions of the lower eight stages eliminated on the path of cultivation, one must have entirely eliminated those of the desire realm, while the rest need only be suppressed or eliminated, before this concentration can first arise — since the seeds of the desire realm's afflictions are of a doubly complex nature and obstruct concentration especially strongly. Hence it is said that only non-returners, the beyond-training of all three vehicles, and bodhisattvas attain this concentration; once attained, it can, as appropriate, arise again after being born into any of the eight upper stages.

Another view holds: one must have eliminated the afflictions of the lower four stages eliminated on the path of cultivation, while the rest need only be suppressed or eliminated, before this concentration can first arise — since the seeds of the afflictions associated with changeable feeling obstruct concentration especially strongly; once attained, it can, as appropriate, arise again after being born into any of the upper five stages.

若伏下惑能起此定，後不斷退生上地者，豈生上已却斷下惑？斷亦無失，如生上者，斷下末那俱生惑故。然不還者對治力強，正潤生位不起煩惱，但由惑種潤上地生，雖所伏惑有退、不退，而無伏下生上地義，故無生上却斷下失。若諸菩薩先二乘位已得滅定，後迴心者，一切位中能起此定。若不爾者，或有乃至七地滿心，方能永伏一切煩惱，雖未永斷欲界修惑，而如已斷，能起此定，論說已入遠地，菩薩方能現起滅盡定故。有從初地即能永伏一切煩惱，如阿羅漢，彼十地中皆起此定，經說菩薩前六地中亦能現起滅盡定故。

Should one object: if merely suppressing the lower afflictions is enough to bring forth this concentration, and one is later reborn in an upper stage without having eliminated them, would this not mean eliminating the lower afflictions only after already being reborn above? In reply: there is no fault in this — just as one born in an upper stage does eliminate the innate afflictions of manas bound to the lower stage. A non-returner's counteracting power, however, is especially strong: at the very moment of moistening rebirth, no affliction actually arises; rebirth into the upper stage is brought about solely through the affliction's seed. Although the suppressed affliction may or may not later regress, there is no sense in which suppressing a lower affliction is what brings about rebirth in an upper stage — hence there is no fault in eliminating the lower affliction only after being reborn above.

Should a bodhisattva have already attained the concentration of cessation while still at the level of the two vehicles, and only afterward turn their mind toward the Mahāyāna, they are capable of bringing forth this concentration at every subsequent stage. If this is not the case, then only upon completing the seventh ground is one capable of permanently suppressing every affliction — and although the afflictions of the desire realm eliminated on the path of cultivation have not yet been permanently eliminated, one can, as though they had been eliminated, bring forth this concentration; the treatise states that only a bodhisattva who has already entered the far-reaching ground is capable of manifesting the concentration of cessation. There are also those who, from the first ground onward, are already capable of permanently suppressing every affliction, just as an arhat is — such bodhisattvas bring forth this concentration throughout all ten grounds, since the sūtras state that a bodhisattva can manifest the concentration of cessation even within the first six grounds.

Fainting, Sleep, and the Six Mindless Occasions 釋「睡眠與悶絕」

Chinese Source

無心「睡眠與悶絕」者，謂有極重睡眠、悶絕，令前六識皆不現行。疲極等緣所引身位，違前六識，故名極重睡眠，此睡眠時，雖無彼體，而由彼似彼故，假說彼名。風熱等緣所引身位，亦違六識，故名極重悶絕，或此俱是觸處少分。除斯五位，意識恒起。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Mindless sleep and fainting" refers to an extremely deep sleep or fainting spell that causes all of the first six consciousnesses to cease manifesting entirely. A bodily state brought about by conditions such as extreme fatigue, which is incompatible with the first six consciousnesses, is called "extremely deep sleep" — during this sleep, although sleep's own essence as a mental factor is actually absent, it is provisionally so named because the state resembles it. A bodily state brought about by conditions such as wind or heat disorders, likewise incompatible with the six consciousnesses, is called "extreme fainting," or else both of these are simply a portion of the tangible sense-object. Apart from these five states, the mental consciousness is constantly active.

正死、生時亦無意識，何故但說五位不行？有義：死、生「及」、「與」言顯。彼說非理，所以者何？但說六時名無心故，謂前五位及無餘依。應說死、生即悶絕攝，彼是最極悶絕位故，說「及」、「與」言顯五無雜。此顯六識斷已，後時依本識中自種還起，由此不說入無餘依。此五位中異生有四，除在滅定，聖唯後三，於中如來、自在菩薩唯得有一，無睡、悶故。

At the very moments of death and rebirth, the mental consciousness is likewise absent — why, then, does the verse speak of only five states in which it does not operate? One view holds: death and rebirth are indicated by the words "and" and "with." This does not accord with reason — why so? Because only six occasions, in total, are called "mindless": the five states already named, together with the state of remainderless nirvāṇa. It should instead be said that death and rebirth are simply included within fainting, since they represent fainting's most extreme instance; the words "and" and "with" are stated to show that these five states are kept distinct from one another. This also shows that, once the six consciousnesses have ceased, they subsequently arise again from their own seeds within the root consciousness — and it is for this reason that the verse does not mention entering remainderless nirvāṇa.

Among these five states, an ordinary being can experience four, excluding the concentration of cessation; a noble one can experience only the latter three, and, among these, a tathāgata or a bodhisattva who has attained mastery experiences only one, since they are free of sleep and fainting.

Reading note: The wave-on-water simile for the five sense-consciousnesses is one of this treatise's most memorable images, and it earns its place: waves need water to exist at all, yet the water's presence never guarantees that a wave, at any given moment, will actually be there — exactly the relationship between the root consciousness and the five sense-consciousnesses it supports. The deeper point buried in the technical detail is a sliding scale of independence: the five senses need the most external conditions and so are active least often; the sixth, mental consciousness, needing fewer, is active almost constantly; and the seventh and eighth are so free of external dependency that nothing can ever fully interrupt them — which is precisely why none of the five listed exceptions (the heaven of no-conception, the two mindless concentrations, sleep, and fainting) ever touches manas or the storehouse consciousness at all.

The five states catalogued here are worth holding together as a set, since each represents a different route to the same rare event — the temporary absence of the sixth consciousness: one is a very long-term rebirth destination (the heaven of no-conception), two are meditative attainments open respectively to non-Buddhist ascetics and to advanced Buddhist practitioners (the concentration of no-conception and the concentration of cessation), and two are ordinary bodily conditions everyone already knows firsthand (sleep and fainting). The debates over exactly when the six consciousnesses stop and restart in the heaven of no-conception — whether they are ever truly absent "for the entire duration," or merely at both bookends of an otherwise mindless stretch — turn on a question with real stakes for the whole system: consciousness, on this account, is never allowed to simply switch off into nothing and switch back on from nothing, since even the most extreme suspension of the six active consciousnesses is still bookended by seeds returning to manifest activity from the root consciousness that never itself stops.

The concentration of cessation gets an unusually generous, almost devotional treatment here — three grades of stability, a whole debate over exactly which afflictions must be eliminated first, and an explicit accommodation for bodhisattvas who attain it far earlier than the ordinary path would predict. This reflects its exceptional status: it is the only one of the five states available exclusively to the spiritually advanced, and the text is visibly concerned to explain how a Mahāyāna practitioner's path through it can look different from an arhat's without undermining the general rule.
