Fascicle 3 · 成唯識論卷第三

Contact, Attention, and What the Ālaya Feels

Fascicle 3 picks up exactly where the opening verse left off: the ālayavijñāna's constant association with the five universal mental factors, its feeling (always neutral), its moral quality, and the long argument — promised at the end of fascicle 2 — that this consciousness never truly ceases, even in the deepest states of meditation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T31, no. 1585 Covers: the five universal mental factors — contact and attention

The Five Universal Mental Factors 依教廣成分 · 常與觸作意受想思相應

The opening verse promised that the ālaya is "constantly joined with contact, attention, feeling, perception, and volition" — the five mental factors present in every single act of consciousness, of any kind, without exception. Fascicle 3 works through each one in turn, starting with contact and attention.

Contact 觸

Chinese Source

此識與幾心所相應？常與觸、作意、受、想、思相應。阿賴耶識無始時來乃至未轉，於一切位恒與此五心所相應，以是遍行心所攝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With how many mental factors does this consciousness associate? It constantly associates with contact, attention, feeling, perception, and volition. From beginningless time, until it is finally transformed, the ālayavijñāna constantly associates with these five mental factors in every state, since they are the universally-operating mental factors.

「觸」謂三和分別變異，令心、心所觸境為性，受、想、思等所依為業。謂根、境、識更相隨順，故名三和。觸依彼生，令彼和合，故說為彼；三和合位皆有順生心所功能，說名變異；觸似彼起，故名分別。根變異力，引觸起時，勝彼識、境，故《集論》等但說分別根之變異。和合一切心及心所，令同觸境，是觸自性。既似順起心所功能，故以受等所依為業。

"Contact" means the distinctive change arising from the coming-together of three things — faculty, object, and consciousness — mutually according with one another; its nature is to bring mind and mental factors into contact with an object, and its function is to serve as the basis for feeling, perception, volition, and the rest. Contact arises in dependence on this coming-together, and in turn unites them — hence it is said to belong to them. At the very moment the three come together, a capacity favorable to generating further mental factors is already present — this is its "transformation." Contact arises resembling this coming-together — hence it is called "distinctive."

Since it is specifically the faculty's own transformation that draws contact into being, and this outweighs the consciousness and object in explanatory importance here, some treatises speak only of the faculty's transformation. Uniting all of mind and mental factors, bringing them together onto their object, is contact's essential nature — and since it already favors the arising of further mental factors, serving as their basis is its function.

《起盡經》說：受、想、行蘊一切皆以觸為緣故，由斯故說識、觸、受等因二、三、四和合而生。《瑜伽》但說與受、想、思為所依者，思於行蘊為主勝故，舉此攝餘。《集論》等說為受依者，以觸生受近而勝故，謂觸所取可意等相與受所取順益等相，極相隣近，引發勝故。然觸自性，是實非假，六六法中，心所性故，是食攝故，能為緣故，如受等性，非即三和。

The Sūtra on Arising and Ceasing says the aggregates of feeling, perception, and formations are all conditioned by contact — which is why consciousness, contact, feeling, and the rest are said to arise from the coming-together of two, three, or four factors, depending on the case. The Yogācārabhūmi names contact as the basis specifically for feeling, perception, and volition because volition is the foremost member of the formations-aggregate, and naming it stands in for the rest. Other texts name it specifically as the basis for feeling because contact's production of feeling is especially immediate and forceful: the pleasant-or-otherwise character contact grasps and the favorable-or-harmful character feeling grasps are extremely close to one another.

Contact's own nature, however, is real, not merely provisional: it counts as a genuine mental factor among the standard enumerations, it is included among the nutriments, and it can serve as a condition — just like feeling and the other genuine mental factors. It is not simply identical with the bare coming-together of the three that gives rise to it.

Attention 作意

Chinese Source

作意謂能警心為性，於所緣境，引心為業；謂此警覺，應起心種，引令趣境，故名作意。雖此亦能引起心所；心是主故，但說引心。有說：令心迴趣異境，或於一境，持心令住，故名作意。彼俱非理，應非遍行，不異定故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Attention" has, as its nature, the capacity to rouse the mind; its function is to draw the mind toward its object. This rousing draws out the very seed that ought to give rise to mind, directing it toward its object — hence the name. Though it also rouses the mental factors, since mind is the principal, it is simply said to "draw the mind."

Some say attention means turning the mind toward a different object, or holding the mind steady on a single object — but neither account is correct: were that its meaning, it could not operate universally across every state of mind, and it would be indistinguishable from concentration, which specifically means holding the mind steady on one object.

Reading note: "Universal" (遍行) mental factors are the five present in absolutely every moment of consciousness, of any kind, in any being — as opposed to factors like faith or anger that only arise sometimes. Contact is the most foundational: the bare coming-together of a sense faculty, an object, and consciousness, which makes any further mental activity (feeling something, perceiving it, willing something about it) possible at all — nothing can be felt or perceived or willed without contact having already occurred. Attention is what "aims" consciousness at an object in the first place, rousing it out of dormancy — carefully distinguished here from concentration, which holds attention fixed rather than merely directing it. Both factors, remember, are present even in the ālaya's most basic activity — the barest possible flicker of awareness already involves this much structure.
