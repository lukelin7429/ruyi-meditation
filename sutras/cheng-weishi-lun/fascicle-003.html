Fascicle 3 · 成唯識論卷第三

Contact, Attention, and What the Ālaya Feels

Fascicle 3 picks up exactly where the opening verse left off: the ālayavijñāna's constant association with the five universal mental factors, its feeling (always neutral), its moral quality, and the long argument — promised at the end of fascicle 2 — that this consciousness never truly ceases, even in the deepest states of meditation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T31, no. 1585 Covers: the torrent argument through the eighth consciousness's full set of names

The Five Universal Mental Factors 依教廣成分 · 常與觸作意受想思相應

The opening verse promised that the ālaya is "constantly joined with contact, attention, feeling, perception, and volition" — the five mental factors present in every single act of consciousness, of any kind, without exception. Fascicle 3 works through each one in turn, starting with contact and attention.

Contact 觸

Chinese Source

此識與幾心所相應？常與觸、作意、受、想、思相應。阿賴耶識無始時來乃至未轉，於一切位恒與此五心所相應，以是遍行心所攝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With how many mental factors does this consciousness associate? It constantly associates with contact, attention, feeling, perception, and volition. From beginningless time, until it is finally transformed, the ālayavijñāna constantly associates with these five mental factors in every state, since they are the universally-operating mental factors.

「觸」謂三和分別變異，令心、心所觸境為性，受、想、思等所依為業。謂根、境、識更相隨順，故名三和。觸依彼生，令彼和合，故說為彼；三和合位皆有順生心所功能，說名變異；觸似彼起，故名分別。根變異力，引觸起時，勝彼識、境，故《集論》等但說分別根之變異。和合一切心及心所，令同觸境，是觸自性。既似順起心所功能，故以受等所依為業。

"Contact" means the distinctive change arising from the coming-together of three things — faculty, object, and consciousness — mutually according with one another; its nature is to bring mind and mental factors into contact with an object, and its function is to serve as the basis for feeling, perception, volition, and the rest. Contact arises in dependence on this coming-together, and in turn unites them — hence it is said to belong to them. At the very moment the three come together, a capacity favorable to generating further mental factors is already present — this is its "transformation." Contact arises resembling this coming-together — hence it is called "distinctive."

Since it is specifically the faculty's own transformation that draws contact into being, and this outweighs the consciousness and object in explanatory importance here, some treatises speak only of the faculty's transformation. Uniting all of mind and mental factors, bringing them together onto their object, is contact's essential nature — and since it already favors the arising of further mental factors, serving as their basis is its function.

《起盡經》說：受、想、行蘊一切皆以觸為緣故，由斯故說識、觸、受等因二、三、四和合而生。《瑜伽》但說與受、想、思為所依者，思於行蘊為主勝故，舉此攝餘。《集論》等說為受依者，以觸生受近而勝故，謂觸所取可意等相與受所取順益等相，極相隣近，引發勝故。然觸自性，是實非假，六六法中，心所性故，是食攝故，能為緣故，如受等性，非即三和。

The Sūtra on Arising and Ceasing says the aggregates of feeling, perception, and formations are all conditioned by contact — which is why consciousness, contact, feeling, and the rest are said to arise from the coming-together of two, three, or four factors, depending on the case. The Yogācārabhūmi names contact as the basis specifically for feeling, perception, and volition because volition is the foremost member of the formations-aggregate, and naming it stands in for the rest. Other texts name it specifically as the basis for feeling because contact's production of feeling is especially immediate and forceful: the pleasant-or-otherwise character contact grasps and the favorable-or-harmful character feeling grasps are extremely close to one another.

Contact's own nature, however, is real, not merely provisional: it counts as a genuine mental factor among the standard enumerations, it is included among the nutriments, and it can serve as a condition — just like feeling and the other genuine mental factors. It is not simply identical with the bare coming-together of the three that gives rise to it.

Attention 作意

Chinese Source

作意謂能警心為性，於所緣境，引心為業；謂此警覺，應起心種，引令趣境，故名作意。雖此亦能引起心所；心是主故，但說引心。有說：令心迴趣異境，或於一境，持心令住，故名作意。彼俱非理，應非遍行，不異定故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Attention" has, as its nature, the capacity to rouse the mind; its function is to draw the mind toward its object. This rousing draws out the very seed that ought to give rise to mind, directing it toward its object — hence the name. Though it also rouses the mental factors, since mind is the principal, it is simply said to "draw the mind."

Some say attention means turning the mind toward a different object, or holding the mind steady on a single object — but neither account is correct: were that its meaning, it could not operate universally across every state of mind, and it would be indistinguishable from concentration, which specifically means holding the mind steady on one object.

Reading note: "Universal" (遍行) mental factors are the five present in absolutely every moment of consciousness, of any kind, in any being — as opposed to factors like faith or anger that only arise sometimes. Contact is the most foundational: the bare coming-together of a sense faculty, an object, and consciousness, which makes any further mental activity (feeling something, perceiving it, willing something about it) possible at all — nothing can be felt or perceived or willed without contact having already occurred. Attention is what "aims" consciousness at an object in the first place, rousing it out of dormancy — carefully distinguished here from concentration, which holds attention fixed rather than merely directing it. Both factors, remember, are present even in the ālaya's most basic activity — the barest possible flicker of awareness already involves this much structure.

Feeling, Perception, Volition — and Why the Ālaya Only Ever Feels Neutral 依教廣成分 · 受、想、思 · 唯捨受

The remaining three universal factors complete the picture, and answer why the verse specifically says the ālaya is joined "only with neutral feeling" — never pleasure, never pain.

Feeling 受

Chinese Source

「受」謂領納順、違、俱非境相為性，起愛為業，能起合、離、非二欲故。有作是說：受有二種：一、境界受，謂領所緣；二、自性受，謂領俱觸。唯自性受是受自相。以境界受共餘相故。彼說非理，受定不緣俱生觸故。若似觸生，名領觸者，似因之果，應皆受性。又既受因，應名因受，何名自性？

Ru-Yi Translation

"Feeling" has, as its nature, the receiving of an object's character as favorable, unfavorable, or neither; its function is to give rise to craving, since it can produce the desire for union, for separation, or for neither.

Some say feeling is of two kinds: "object-feeling," which receives the object itself, and "own-nature feeling," which receives the accompanying contact — and that only the latter is feeling's true character, since object-feeling shares its character with other mental factors. This is unreasonable: feeling certainly does not take the contact arising alongside it as its object. If feeling is said to "receive contact" merely because it arises resembling and following from contact, then every effect resembling its cause would have to count as feeling. And if this "own-nature feeling" is really just contact's effect, it should be called "feeling caused by contact," not "feeling's own nature" at all.

若謂如王食諸國邑，受能領觸所生受體，名自性受。理亦不然，違自所執不自證故。若不捨自性，名自性受，應一切法皆是受自性。故彼所說，但誘嬰兒。然境界受，非共餘相，領順等相，定屬己者，名境界受，不共餘故。

If one answers that feeling "receives" contact-produced feeling the way a king "consumes" the cities of his realm — meaning governs, not literally eats — this too fails, since it contradicts their own claim that feeling does not cognize itself. And if "own-nature feeling" simply means "not departing from one's own nature," then every dharma whatsoever would qualify, since everything equally doesn't depart from its own nature. What is said here is fit only to placate small children. Object-feeling, in truth, shares its character with nothing else: receiving an object's favorable-or-otherwise character, definitely as one's own, is uniquely feeling's own affair.

Perception and Volition 想 · 思

「想」謂於境取像為性，施設種種名言為業，謂要安立境分齊相，方能隨起種種名言。「思」謂令心造作為性，於善品等役心為業，謂能取境正因等相，驅役自心令造善等。此五既是遍行所攝，故與藏識決定相應，其遍行相後當廣釋。此觸等五與異熟識行相雖異，而時、依同，所緣、事等，故名相應。

"Perception" has, as its nature, the grasping of an image with respect to an object; its function is to establish various verbal designations — only once an object's boundary-marks are established can such designations follow. "Volition" has, as its nature, causing the mind to act; its function is to drive the mind toward wholesome activity and the like — grasping an object's correct causal character and driving one's own mind to act accordingly.

Since these five are among the universally-operating factors, they certainly associate with the storehouse consciousness; their universal character will be explained more fully later. Though contact and the rest differ from the ripening consciousness in specific perceiving-activity, they share its time and basis, and match it in object and in number — hence they are said to "associate" with it.

Why Only Neutral Feeling 唯捨受

Chinese Source

此識行相極不明了，不能分別違、順境相，微細、一類、相續而轉，是故唯與捨受相應。又，此相應受唯是異熟，隨先引業轉；不待現緣，住善、惡業勢力轉故。唯是捨受；苦、樂二受是異熟生，非真異熟，待現緣故，非此相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

This consciousness's perceiving-activity is extremely unclear and indistinct; it cannot discriminate an object as favorable or unfavorable. Being subtle, uniform, and continuous, it therefore associates only with neutral feeling. Furthermore, the feeling associated with it is only ever true ripening, following the power of karma that projected it long ago — it does not depend on present conditions, operating purely through the force of past wholesome or unwholesome karma, and so is only ever neutral. Painful and pleasant feelings are "ripened arisings" rather than true ripening in this strict sense, since they depend on present conditions — and so are not associated with this consciousness.

又，由此識常無轉變，有情恒執為自內我；若與苦、樂二受相應，便有轉變，寧執為我，故此但與捨受相應。若爾，如何此識亦是惡業異熟？既許善業能招捨受，此亦應然。捨受不違苦、樂品故，如無記法，善、惡俱招。

Furthermore, it is precisely because this consciousness is constant and never felt to change that sentient beings constantly cling to it as an inner self; were it associated with painful or pleasant feeling, it would then be felt to change — and how, then, could it still be clung to as self? Hence it associates only with neutral feeling.

If so, how can this consciousness also be the ripened result of unwholesome karma? — Since wholesome karma is already granted to bring about neutral feeling, the same should hold for unwholesome karma too: neutral feeling conflicts with neither the painful nor the pleasant class, just as a morally neutral dharma in general can be brought about by either wholesome or unwholesome karma.

Reading note: The argument for "only neutral feeling" is doing double duty. Descriptively, the ālaya's activity is too subtle and uniform to register anything as pleasant or painful — that discrimination only happens at the level of the six active consciousnesses built on top of it. But there's a second, sharper point: if the ālaya itself felt pleasure or pain, it would be felt to change from moment to moment, and it is precisely its felt changelessness that makes it so convincing as "the self" to a mind clinging to it — the seventh consciousness's grip on the ālaya as "I" depends on the ālaya presenting as still, constant, unmoved. A restless, fluctuating ālaya would be a much less persuasive candidate for a fixed self, which is exactly the illusion this whole treatise has been dismantling since fascicle 1.

Why the Ālaya Excludes Every Other Mental Factor 依教廣成分 · 非別境等相應 · 無覆無記

Having established what the ālaya does associate with, the treatise now explains, in detail, why it associates with nothing else — neither the five "particular" mental factors, nor any wholesome or defiled state — and gives its moral quality a precise technical name.

Why Not the Five Particular Mental Factors 非別境相應

Chinese Source

如何此識非別境等心所相應？互相違故。謂欲希望所樂事轉；此識任運，無所希望。勝解印持決定事轉；此識瞢昧，無所印持。念唯明記曾習事轉，此識昧劣，不能明記。定能令心專注一境；此識任運；剎那別緣。慧唯簡擇德等事轉；此識微昧，不能簡擇。故此不與別境相應。此識唯是異熟性故，善、染污等亦不相應。惡作等四無記性者，有間斷故，定非異熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why does this consciousness not associate with the five "particular" mental factors? Because each one contradicts its nature. Desire operates by hoping for something longed for — but this consciousness operates spontaneously, hoping for nothing. Resolve operates by firmly holding a settled matter — but this consciousness is too dim to hold anything firmly. Mindfulness operates only by clearly remembering something previously practiced — but this consciousness is too weak to remember clearly. Concentration fixes the mind on a single object — but this consciousness operates spontaneously, taking a different object moment by moment. Wisdom operates only by discerning merit from fault — but this consciousness is too faint to discern anything at all. For these reasons, it does not associate with the particular mental factors.

Since this consciousness is purely of a ripening nature, it likewise does not associate with wholesome or defiled factors. As for regret and the other three factors classed only as "indeterminate" — being subject to interruption, they certainly cannot be of a true ripening nature either.

Why It Is "Unobstructed and Indeterminate" 無覆無記

Chinese Source

法有四種：謂善、不善、有覆無記、無覆無記，阿賴耶識何法攝耶？此識唯是無覆無記，異熟性故。異熟若是善、染污者，流轉還滅應不得成，又此識是善、染依故，若善、染者，互相違故，應不與二俱作所依；又此識是所熏性故，若善、染者，如極香臭，應不受熏。無熏習故，染、淨因果俱不成立，故此唯是無覆無記。

Ru-Yi Translation

Dharmas are of four kinds: wholesome, unwholesome, obstructed-indeterminate, and unobstructed-indeterminate. Which does the ālayavijñāna belong to? This consciousness is purely unobstructed-indeterminate, since it is of a ripening nature. If the ripening consciousness were wholesome or defiled, the whole cycle of transmigration and its cessation could never be established. Moreover, this consciousness serves as the basis for both wholesome and defiled activity; were it itself one or the other, it could not — since wholesome and defiled contradict each other — serve as the basis for both. And since this consciousness has the very nature of what can be perfumed, if it were already wholesome or defiled, it would, like something intensely fragrant or foul, be incapable of taking on any further impression — and without that capacity, the causes and effects of both defilement and purification could never be established at all. Hence this consciousness is purely unobstructed-indeterminate.

「覆」謂染法障聖道故，又能蔽心，令不淨故；此識非染，故名「無覆」。「記」謂善、惡，有愛、非愛果，及殊勝自體可記別故；此非善、惡，故名「無記」。「觸等亦如是」者，謂如阿賴耶識，唯是無覆無記性攝，觸、作意、受、想、思亦爾，諸相應法，必同性故。

"Obstructed" means that a defiled dharma obstructs the noble path, and also clouds the mind, rendering it impure — since this consciousness is not defiled, it is called "unobstructed." "Determinate" means having a wholesome or unwholesome quality, producing a desirable or undesirable result, with a distinctive nature specific enough to be designated one way or the other — since this consciousness is neither wholesome nor unwholesome, it is called "indeterminate."

"Contact and the rest are likewise so" means: just as the ālayavijñāna is purely unobstructed-indeterminate, so too are contact, attention, feeling, perception, and volition — since factors that associate together must necessarily share the same moral nature.

Reading note: Every exclusion argued here follows the same pattern already familiar from earlier in the treatise: each candidate mental factor is defined by a specific, active quality (hoping, firmly holding, clearly remembering, fixating, discerning) that directly contradicts the ālaya's defining bluntness — subtle, spontaneous, uniform, indistinct. The moral-neutrality argument is the more consequential one: a self-help or self-harm-only ālaya couldn't do its actual job, which is to be a neutral, always-available medium that both defiled and pure states of mind can equally deposit their seeds into and draw from. If the ālaya itself carried a moral charge, it would resist perfuming from the opposite kind of activity — a permanently "wholesome" ālaya could never be perfumed by unwholesome deeds, which would make the entire mechanism of accumulating negative karma impossible to explain, and the reverse for liberation. Its neutrality is not a moral failing; it is precisely what makes it capable of recording anything at all.

What "Likewise So" Does — and Doesn't — Mean 依教廣成分 · 釋「觸等亦如是」

The verse's phrase "contact and the rest are likewise so" gets read two ways. One reading pushes it too far — treating the accompanying five factors as identical to the ālaya in every respect, including holding seeds. The treatise closes off that overreach.

Chinese Source

有義：觸等如阿賴耶，亦是異熟及一切種，廣說乃至無覆無記，「亦如是」言，無簡別故。彼說非理。所以者何？觸等依識，不自在故。如貪、信等不能受熏，如何同識，能持種子？又，若觸等亦能受熏，應一有情有六種體。若爾，果起從何種生？理不應言從六種起，未見多種生一芽故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A further view holds: contact and the rest, just like the ālaya, are also "ripening" and "possessing all seeds," and so on down to "unobstructed-indeterminate," since the phrase "likewise so" makes no distinction and so should apply across the board. This is unreasonable. Why? Contact and the rest depend on consciousness and are not self-sufficient, just as craving, faith, and the other mental factors cannot themselves be perfumed — so how could they, like consciousness itself, be capable of holding seeds?

Furthermore, if contact and the rest could also be perfumed, a single sentient being would end up with six separate seed-holding substances. If so, which one would any given result actually arise from? It cannot arise from all six at once, since we never observe many seeds jointly producing a single sprout.

若說果生唯從一種，則餘五種便為無用；亦不可說次第生果，熏習同時，勢力等故；又不可說六果頓生，勿一有情一剎那頃，六眼識等俱時生故。誰言觸等亦能受熏持諸種子？不爾，如何觸等如識名一切種？謂觸等五，有似種相，名一切種，觸等與識所緣等故，無色觸等有所緣故，親所緣緣定應有故，此似種相不為因緣生現識等，如觸等上似眼根等非識所依，亦如似火無能燒用，

If it arises from only one, the other five seeds would be entirely useless. Nor can they produce their results in sequence, since all six are perfumed simultaneously with equal force; nor can all six results arise at once, since that would absurdly mean six eye-consciousnesses arising together within a single instant for one being.

Who, though, ever claimed that contact and the rest can themselves be perfumed and hold seeds? If not, then how can they, like consciousness, be called "possessing all seeds"? — Because contact and the other four have an appearance resembling seeds in this looser sense, sharing the same object as consciousness; even though formless, they must still have some object. This seed-resembling appearance, however, does not actually serve as a genuine cause producing manifest consciousness, just as the eye-faculty-resembling appearance within contact is not itself a real basis for consciousness, and just as an image resembling fire has no actual power to burn.

彼救非理，觸等所緣似種等相，後執受處方應與識而相例故。由此前說一切種言，定目受熏、能持種義；不爾，本頌有重言失。又彼所說，「亦如是」言，無簡別故，咸相例者，定不成證，勿觸等五，亦能了別，觸等亦與觸等相應。由此故知「亦如是」者，隨所應說，非謂一切。

This attempted rescue does not hold up: any seed-resembling character among the objects of contact and the rest should only be compared to consciousness's own object later, in the specific context of "grasping-and-holding" — not appealed to here. The earlier phrase "possessing all seeds," then, definitely refers specifically to the capacity to be perfumed and to hold seeds — otherwise the root verse would contain a pointless repetition.

Furthermore, the claim that "likewise so" makes no distinction and so must apply across the board proves nothing: taken that far, contact and the other four would also have to be capable of discerning cognition, and would have to associate with further instances of contact and the rest — an absurd, endless regress. "Likewise so," then, means only what is appropriate in each respective case — not that literally everything applies without exception.

Reading note: This is a small but important piece of hermeneutical housekeeping: a single word in a compressed verse ("亦," "likewise") could, taken too literally, collapse an important distinction the treatise has been carefully building — between the ālaya, which genuinely holds seeds, and its five accompanying mental factors, which merely resemble it in certain limited respects (moral quality, subtlety, the kind of object they take) without sharing its unique seed-holding function. The reductio is decisive: if "likewise" meant "identical in every respect," contact would have to be able to cognize things, and would need its own accompanying contact, in an infinite regress. Verses compress; commentary exists precisely to stop compression from being mistaken for identity.

Neither Permanent Nor Interrupted: The Torrent 依教廣成分 · 恒轉如瀑流

One of the treatise's most quoted passages. If the ālaya isn't an eternal soul, how does it hold a life — and a whole chain of lives — together? If it isn't simply annihilated moment to moment, how is it not eternal after all? The answer is a single image: a torrent.

Constant, Yet Flowing 恒 · 轉

Chinese Source

阿賴耶識為斷？為常？非斷、非常，以「恒轉」故。「恒」謂此識無始時來，一類、相續，常無間斷，是界、趣、生施設本故，性堅、持種令不失故。「轉」謂此識無始時來，念念生滅前後變異，因滅果生，非常、一故，可為轉識熏成種故。「恒」言遮斷，「轉」表非常，猶如瀑流，因果法爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is the ālayavijñāna impermanent, or permanent? Neither impermanent nor permanent, since it "constantly flows." "Constant" means this consciousness, from beginningless time, continues as a single, unbroken kind, never interrupted — it is the very basis on which the realms, destinies, and modes of birth are established, and its stable nature holds seeds without letting them be lost. "Flows" means this consciousness, from beginningless time, arises and ceases moment by moment, changing from one instant to the next — its cause ceasing exactly as its result arises, never remaining a single unchanging thing — and so it can be perfumed by the evolving consciousnesses to form new seeds. "Constant" rules out interruption; "flows" shows it is not permanent — it is exactly like a rushing torrent, cause and effect simply operating according to their own nature.

如瀑流水，非斷、非常，相續長時有所漂溺，此識亦爾，從無始來生滅相續，非常、非斷，漂溺有情令不出離。又如瀑流，雖風等擊，起諸波浪而流不斷，此識亦爾，雖遇眾緣，起眼識等而恒相續。又如瀑流，漂水下上魚、草等物，隨流不捨，此識亦爾，與內習氣外觸等法恒相隨轉。

Just as the water of a rushing torrent is neither interrupted nor permanent, and yet, continuing over a long span, carries things along and can drown them — so too this consciousness, continuing in arising and ceasing from beginningless time, is neither permanent nor interrupted, and carries sentient beings along, keeping them from escaping saṃsāra. And just as a torrent, though struck by wind, raises waves on its surface without its flow ever breaking — so too this consciousness, though it encounters countless conditions and gives rise to the eye-consciousness and the rest, nevertheless continues constantly, without interruption. And just as a torrent carries fish, grass, and other things along on and beneath its surface, never releasing them from the current — so too this consciousness moves constantly together with its own inner habitual tendencies and outer factors like contact.

如是法喻，意顯此識無始因果非斷、常義。謂此識性無始時來，剎那剎那果生因滅。果生故非斷，因滅故非常，非斷、非常是緣起理，故說此識恒轉如流。

The point of this teaching and its simile is to show that this consciousness's beginningless causal process is neither interrupted nor permanent. This consciousness's nature, from beginningless time, has its result arise and its cause cease in every single instant. Because the result arises, it is not interrupted; because the cause ceases, it is not permanent. Being neither interrupted nor permanent is exactly the principle of dependent origination — hence it is said that this consciousness constantly flows, like a torrent.

Defending "Neither Interrupted Nor Permanent" 釋非斷非常之難

Chinese Source

過去、未來既非實有，非常可爾；非斷如何？斷豈得成緣起正理？過去、未來若是實有，可許非斷；如何非常？常亦不成緣起正理。豈斥他過，己義便成？若不摧邪難以顯正。前因滅位後果即生，如秤兩頭低、昂時等，如是因果相續如流，何假去、來，方成非斷？

Ru-Yi Translation

An objection: given that the past and future are not really, substantially existent, "not permanent" is understandable — but how can it also be "not interrupted"? How could mere interruption ever amount to the correct principle of dependent origination? And on the other hand: if the past and future WERE really, substantially existent, "not interrupted" would be understandable — but then how could it be "not permanent"? Permanence, too, would fail to be the correct principle of dependent origination.

Does merely pointing out a fault in another's position thereby establish one's own? — Without refuting what is mistaken, it is hard to make clear what is correct. At the very moment a prior cause ceases, the subsequent result arises at once — just as the two ends of a scale rise and fall at exactly the same time. Cause and effect continue on in just this way, like a flowing stream. What need is there to posit a substantially real past and future in order to establish "not interrupted"?

因現有位，後果未生，因是誰因？果現有時，前因已滅，果是誰果？既無因果，誰離斷、常？若有因時，已有後果，果既本有，何待前因？因義既無，果義寧有？無因無果，豈離斷、常？因果義成，依法作用，故所詰難，非預我宗。

Turning the challenge back: at the very moment a cause presently exists, its later result has not yet arisen — so of what, exactly, is it "the cause" at that point? At the moment a result presently exists, its prior cause has already ceased — so of what is it "the result"? If there is no genuine relation of cause and effect at all on your view, how can you claim to have escaped either eternalism or nihilism yourself?

And if the result already existed at the very time the cause existed, then, since the result would already inherently exist, what need would it have to wait for a prior cause at all? If the very notion of "cause" collapses this way, how could "effect" still stand? With neither genuine cause nor genuine effect, how have you escaped eternalism or nihilism either? On our own account, cause and effect are established simply in terms of a dharma's functional activity — so this whole challenge never even touches our position.

Reading note: The torrent image is doing exact philosophical work, not just poetic decoration: a river is unmistakably one continuous thing you can point to, name, and follow for miles — and yet not one drop of water in it now was there a moment ago. "Constant" (恒) captures the first fact; "flowing" (轉) captures the second; neither alone is the whole picture, and Buddhist teaching has always warned against exactly the two extremes this simile threads between — eternalism (treating the self or mind as a fixed, unchanging thing) and nihilism (treating it as so momentary that nothing genuinely carries over from one instant, or one life, to the next).

The logical defense that follows is a compact classic of Buddhist debate: rather than trying to explain how cause and effect relate as two substantially real things existing at different times (which generates paradoxes whichever way you set it up — do they coexist, or not?), the treatise simply defines causation in terms of functional activity within an ongoing process, sidestepping the whole framework that generated the paradox. The next section takes this further, showing that a rival school's own attempt to explain causal continuity — momentary "coexistence" of a single event's arising-instant and ceasing-instant — collapses under exactly the same kind of scrutiny.

Why a Rival Account of Causal Continuity Fails 依教廣成分 · 破餘部因果相續說

Other schools tried to explain causal continuity without positing an ālayavijñāna at all — by claiming that a single instant secretly contains two moments, arising and ceasing, sharing one substance. The treatise shows this doesn't actually work.

Chinese Source

有餘部說：雖無去、來而有因果恒相續義。謂現在法極迅速者，猶有初、後，生、滅二時，生時酬因，滅時引果，時雖有二，而體是一，前因正滅，後果正生，體相雖殊，而俱是有。如是因果非假施設，然離斷、常，又無前難，誰有智者捨此信餘？

Ru-Yi Translation

Some other schools say: although there is no substantially real past or future, cause and effect can still genuinely continue. Even an extremely swift present dharma still has an earlier and a later moment — arising and ceasing. At the moment of arising, it repays its cause; at the moment of ceasing, it draws forth its effect. Though there are two moments, the substance is one and the same: the prior cause is ceasing exactly as the later effect is arising — their characteristics differ, but both exist at that shared instant. Causation understood this way is not merely provisional, yet it avoids both eternalism and nihilism, and faces none of the difficulties raised earlier — what wise person would abandon this account for another?

彼有虛言，都無實義，何容一念而有二時？生、滅相違，寧同現在？滅若現在，生應未來。有故名生，既是現在，無故名滅，寧非過去？滅若非無，生應非有；生既現有，滅應現無，又二相違，如何體一？非苦、樂等見有是事，生、滅若一，時應無二；生、滅若異，寧說體同？故生、滅時俱現在有，同依一體，理必不成。經部師等因果相續，理亦不成，彼不許有阿賴耶識能持種故。由此應信大乘所說因果相續緣起正理。

This is empty talk, with no real substance behind it. How could a single instant contain two separate moments? Arising and ceasing contradict each other — how could both belong to the very same present? If ceasing belongs to the present, arising should then belong to the future. "Existing" is what defines arising — if that counts as present, then ceasing, defined by "not existing," should count as past, not present. And if ceasing is not simply nonexistence, arising should not simply be existence either; and if arising genuinely exists in the present, ceasing should then be simply absent from it — being mutually contradictory this way, how could the two share one substance?

We never observe this kind of thing happening even with pleasure and pain and the like. If arising and ceasing were truly one and the same, there should not really be two separate moments; if they are genuinely different, how can their substance be called the same? The claim that arising-moment and ceasing-moment are both simultaneously present, sharing one substance, simply cannot be sustained. The Sautrāntikas' account of causal continuity likewise cannot be sustained, since they do not accept an ālayavijñāna capable of holding seeds in the first place. From all this, one should believe in the Mahāyāna's account of dependent-origination causal continuity.

Reading note: The rival account tries to have it both ways: avoid positing a substantially real past and future (which the treatise itself also avoids), while still explaining continuity without needing anything like the ālaya — by claiming a single instant secretly has two sub-moments that share one substance. The refutation is a clean dilemma: either "arising" and "ceasing" are really the same thing (in which case there's only ever one moment, not two, and the whole "two moments" account was empty from the start), or they're really different (in which case they can't share a single substance, and the claimed continuity between them is unexplained). Positing a genuine storehouse consciousness that carries seeds forward, the treatise argues, is what actually does the explanatory work that this rival account only pretends to do.

When Is the Name "Ālaya" Finally Relinquished? 依教廣成分 · 阿羅漢位方究竟捨

The opening verse said the ālaya is "relinquished at the stage of the arhat." This section pins down exactly what that means — and, since "arhat" here turns out to cover more than the ordinary sense of the word, resolves an apparent tension in the scriptures about non-retreating bodhisattvas.

Chinese Source

此識無始恒轉如流，乃至何位當究竟捨？阿羅漢位方究竟捨。謂諸聖者斷煩惱障究竟盡時，名阿羅漢。爾時，此識煩惱麁重永遠離故，說之為「捨」。此中所說阿羅漢者，通攝三乘無學果位，皆已永害煩惱賊故；應受世間妙供養故；永不復受分段生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

This consciousness constantly flows, like a torrent, from beginningless time — so at what stage is it finally, ultimately relinquished? Only at the stage of the arhat. That is: when a noble one has completely exhausted the afflictive obstruction, they are called an arhat, and at that point, since this consciousness is forever free of coarse, heavy affliction, it is said to be "relinquished."

The term "arhat" here encompasses the fruit of no-further-learning across all three vehicles alike, since all have permanently slain the thief of affliction, are worthy of the world's finest offerings, and will never again undergo ordinary birth-and-death.

云何知然？〈決擇分〉說：諸阿羅漢、獨覺、如來皆不成就阿賴耶故；《集論》復說：若諸菩薩得菩提時，頓斷煩惱及所知障，成阿羅漢及如來故。若爾，菩薩煩惱種子未永斷盡非阿羅漢，應皆成就阿賴耶識，何故即彼〈決擇分〉說不退菩薩亦不成就阿賴耶識？彼說二乘無學果位迴心趣向大菩提者，必不退起煩惱障故，趣菩提故，即復轉名不退菩薩，彼不成就阿賴耶識。即攝在此阿羅漢中，故彼論文不違此義。

How is this known? The Yogācārabhūmi's "Chapter of Investigation" says that arhats, pratyekabuddhas, and tathāgatas alike do not possess the ālaya. The Abhidharmasamuccaya further says that when bodhisattvas attain awakening, they simultaneously sever both the afflictive and the cognitive obstructions, thereby becoming both arhats and tathāgatas at once.

If so, bodhisattvas whose afflictive seeds have not yet been permanently severed — and who are therefore not yet arhats — should still possess the ālayavijñāna. Why, then, does that same chapter say even "non-retreating" bodhisattvas do not possess it? — That passage speaks of practitioners of the two lesser vehicles who, having already reached the fruit of no-further-learning, turn their aspiration toward great awakening: since they will certainly never again lapse into the afflictive obstruction, and are now heading toward awakening, they are simply renamed "non-retreating bodhisattvas" at that point — they are already included within "arhat" as just defined, so the passage does not actually conflict with this account.

Reading note: "Arhat" is being used here in an unusually wide, technical sense — not just the śrāvaka ideal familiar from earlier Buddhism, but anyone in any of the three vehicles who has permanently finished with the afflictive obstruction, including fully awakened Buddhas. This wide usage is what lets the treatise resolve what looks, on the surface, like a contradiction: a passage saying even certain bodhisattvas "do not possess the ālaya" isn't carving out some strange exception — it's simply using "arhat" broadly enough to already include them.

Which Bodhisattvas Have Already Relinquished the Name? 依教廣成分 · 不動地與初地之諍

Bodhisattvas relinquish the "ālaya" name before full arhatship, once they become genuinely "non-retreating." But at which point on the ten-ground bodhisattva path does that actually happen? Two views compete, and the treatise sides decisively with one.

View One: the Eighth Ground 不動地已上

Chinese Source

又不動地已上菩薩，一切煩惱永不行故，法駛流中任運轉故；能諸行中起諸行故；剎那剎那轉增進故，此位方名不退菩薩。然此菩薩雖未斷盡異熟識中煩惱種子，而緣此識我見、愛等，不復執藏為自內我，由斯永捨阿賴耶名，故說不成阿賴耶識，此亦說彼名阿羅漢。

Ru-Yi Translation

A further view holds: it is only bodhisattvas at the Immovable Ground — the eighth — and above who truly count as "non-retreating," since from that point on no affliction ever manifests again; they proceed spontaneously within the swift stream of the Dharma; they can give rise to every kind of practice within a single practice; and they advance moment by moment without cease. Though such a bodhisattva has not yet completely severed the afflictive seeds within the ripening consciousness, the self-view and self-love that take this consciousness as their object no longer cling to and store it as an inner self — so the name "ālaya" is permanently relinquished here, and such a bodhisattva is also called an "arhat" in this sense.

View Two: the First Ground 初地已上

有義：初地已上菩薩，已證二空所顯理故；已得二種殊勝智故；已斷分別二重障故；能一行中起諸行故；雖為利益起諸煩惱，而彼不作煩惱過失故，此亦名不退菩薩。然此菩薩雖未斷盡俱生煩惱，而緣此識所有分別我見、愛等，不復執藏為自內我，由斯亦捨阿賴耶名，故說不成阿賴耶識，此亦說彼名阿羅漢。故《集論》中作如是說：十地菩薩雖未永斷一切煩惱，然此煩惱猶如呪、藥所伏諸毒，不起一切煩惱過失。一切地中，如阿羅漢已斷煩惱，故亦說彼名阿羅漢。

Another view holds: it is already bodhisattvas at the First Ground and above, since they have already realized the principle revealed by the two emptinesses, attained the two kinds of superior wisdom, and severed the two discriminated obstructions; they can give rise to every practice within a single practice; and though they may still give rise to afflictions for the sake of benefiting beings, those afflictions produce no real fault — these too are called "non-retreating." Though such a bodhisattva has not yet fully severed innate affliction, the discriminated self-view and self-love that take this consciousness as their object no longer cling to and store it as self — so the name "ālaya" is likewise relinquished here too, and such a bodhisattva is also called an "arhat." Hence the Abhidharmasamuccaya says: though bodhisattvas across the ten grounds have not yet permanently severed every affliction, these afflictions, like poison suppressed by a spell or medicine, produce no fault — so, throughout these grounds, such bodhisattvas are, like an arhat who has already severed affliction, likewise called "arhat."

Why the First-Ground View Fails 破初地說

Chinese Source

彼說非理。七地已前猶有俱生我見、愛等，執藏此識為自內我，如何已捨阿賴耶名？若彼分別我見、愛等，不復執藏，說名為捨，則預流等諸有學位，亦應已捨阿賴耶名，許便違害諸論所說。

Ru-Yi Translation

This position is unreasonable. Prior to the Seventh Ground, bodhisattvas still have innate self-view and self-love, which cling to and store this consciousness as an inner self — how, then, could they already have relinquished the name "ālaya"? And if merely no longer clinging to it via the discriminated forms of self-view and self-love were enough to count as "relinquishing," then stream-enterers and others still in training — who have likewise already severed the discriminated views — should equally have already relinquished the name "ālaya." But granting that directly contradicts what every treatise says.

地上菩薩所起煩惱皆由正知，不為過失，非預流等得有斯事，寧可以彼例此菩薩？彼六識中所起煩惱，雖由正知不為過失，而第七識有漏心位，任運現行執藏此識，寧不與彼預流等同？由此故知彼說非理。

One might object: the afflictions arising in bodhisattvas above the first ground all occur through correct knowledge and so produce no fault — stream-enterers have no such quality, so how can they be compared? — But although the afflictions arising within these bodhisattvas' six active consciousnesses do occur through correct knowledge and produce no fault, their seventh consciousness, in its contaminated state, still spontaneously and actively clings to and stores this eighth consciousness as a self — so how is this genuinely any different from the case of stream-enterers? From this it can be known that the first-ground view is unreasonable.

Only the Name Is Relinquished — Not the Whole Consciousness 捨名非捨體

然阿羅漢斷此識中煩惱麁重究竟盡故，不復執藏阿賴耶識為自內我。由斯永失阿賴耶名，說之為捨，非捨一切第八識體。勿阿羅漢無識持種，爾時便入無餘涅槃。

In fact, it is only the arhat — having completely, finally exhausted the coarse, heavy afflictions within this consciousness — who no longer clings to and stores the ālayavijñāna as an inner self. It is this that permanently loses the name "ālaya," and this is what "relinquished" means — not that the entire eighth consciousness itself is discarded. Otherwise, an arhat would have no consciousness left at all to hold seeds, and would, at that very moment, simply enter final nirvāṇa without remainder — which plainly does not happen.

Reading note: The decisive point in this whole debate is subtle but crucial: "ālaya" is a name tied to a specific relationship — the seventh consciousness's innate, ongoing clinging to the eighth consciousness as "me." As long as any trace of that innate (not merely intellectual) clinging remains, the name still applies, no matter how refined or fault-free the bodhisattva's conduct has otherwise become. That is why intellectually severing the "discriminated" self-view at the first ground isn't enough — it leaves the deeper, wordless, always-running sense of self-as-ālaya fully intact, unaffected until the eighth ground, when even that finally stops operating.

The closing clarification matters for anyone tracking this argument across the whole treatise: "relinquishing the ālaya" was never a claim that consciousness itself disappears at awakening — that would make an arhat's continued living body a metaphysical impossibility. What disappears is a specific function — the being-clung-to-as-self — while the same underlying consciousness continues, still holding seeds, still supporting an ongoing life, now simply free of that particular illusion. The next section turns to the many other names this consciousness carries across its different states, which is exactly what makes sense of how it can survive its own most famous name being retired.

One Consciousness, Many Names 依教廣成分 · 第八識異名

"Ālaya" turns out to be just one of several names this consciousness carries — and not even the one that lasts longest. This section lays out the full set, showing exactly which stage of the path each name belongs to.

Chinese Source

然第八識雖諸有情皆悉成就，而隨義別立種種名。謂或名心，由種種法熏習種子所積集故；或名阿陀那，執持種子及諸色根令不壞故；或名所知依，能與染、淨所知諸法為依止故；或名種子識，能遍任持世、出世間諸種子故。此等諸名通一切位。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although every sentient being, without exception, possesses this eighth consciousness, various names are given to it depending on the particular aspect being emphasized. It is sometimes called "mind" (心), since it accumulates the seeds deposited by countless dharmas' perfuming. It is sometimes called "ādāna" (阿陀那, the appropriator), since it grasps and holds the seeds and the physical sense-faculties, keeping them from decaying. It is sometimes called "the basis of the knowable" (所知依), since it supports every defiled and pure knowable dharma. It is sometimes called "the seed-consciousness" (種子識), since it universally holds the seeds of both mundane and supramundane dharmas. These four names apply across every stage, without exception, from ordinary beings up through Buddhas.

或名阿賴耶，攝藏一切雜染品法令不失故，我見、愛等執藏以為自內我故。此名唯在異生、有學，非無學位、不退菩薩，有雜染法執藏義故。或名異熟識，能引生死善、不善業異熟果故。此名唯在異生、二乘諸菩薩位，非如來地猶有異熟無記法故。或名無垢識，最極清淨諸無漏法所依止故。此名唯在如來地有，菩薩、二乘及異生位，持有漏種可受熏習，未得善淨第八識故。

It is sometimes called "ālaya" (storehouse), since it takes in and stores every defiled dharma without letting it be lost, and since self-view and self-love cling to it and store it as an inner self. This name applies only to ordinary beings and those still in training — not to those beyond training or non-retreating bodhisattvas — since it specifically carries the sense of storing defilement as a clung-to self.

It is sometimes called "the ripening consciousness," since it draws forth the ripened results of wholesome and unwholesome karma that lead to birth-and-death. This name applies only to ordinary beings, the two lesser vehicles, and bodhisattvas — not to the stage of a Tathāgata, since only up to that point does it still carry morally indeterminate ripened dharmas.

It is sometimes called "the stainless consciousness" (無垢識), since it supports the most utterly pure, uncontaminated dharmas. This name exists only at the stage of a Tathāgata: bodhisattvas, the two lesser vehicles, and ordinary beings still hold contaminated seeds capable of receiving further perfuming, and have not yet attained a wholesome, pure eighth consciousness.

如契經說：如來無垢識，是淨無漏界，解脫一切障，圓鏡智相應。阿賴耶名過失重故，最初捨故，此中偏說。異熟識體菩薩將得菩提時捨，聲聞、獨覺入無餘依涅槃時捨。無垢識體無有捨時，利樂有情無盡時故。心等通故，隨義應說。

As scripture says, in verse: "The Tathāgata's stainless consciousness is the pure, uncontaminated realm, liberated from every obstruction, associated with the wisdom of the great perfect mirror." The name "ālaya" is singled out for special mention because its associated fault is the most serious, and because it is the very first of these names to be relinquished. "The ripening consciousness" is relinquished when a bodhisattva is about to attain awakening, or when śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas enter final nirvāṇa. "The stainless consciousness," however, is never relinquished at any point, since there is no end to benefiting and bringing joy to sentient beings. "Mind" and the other universally-applying names should each simply be used as appropriate to the aspect of meaning under discussion.

Reading note: Laid out as a timeline, the names retire in a striking order — not gradually fading together, but dropping out one at a time as specific problems get solved. "Ālaya" goes first, at the eighth ground, once innate self-clinging finally stops. "Ripening consciousness" goes next, at full awakening or final nirvāṇa, once there's no more karmic ripening left to undergo. "Mind," "ādāna," "the basis of the knowable," and "the seed-consciousness" never retire at all — they describe functions (accumulating impressions, sustaining the body, supporting cognition, holding seeds) that a Buddha's consciousness still performs, just without defilement attached. And "the stainless consciousness" only ever switches on, at full awakening, and never switches off.

The upshot: this is one continuous consciousness wearing different names as its situation changes, not a series of different entities replacing one another. "Ālaya" was always a name for one phase of something much longer-lived — which is exactly why losing that name at the eighth ground is a milestone worth celebrating, not evidence that anything has been destroyed.
