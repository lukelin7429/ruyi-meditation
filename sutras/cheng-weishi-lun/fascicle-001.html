Fascicle 1 · 成唯識論卷第一

To Whom We Bow, and Why This Treatise Exists

This page begins Ru-Yi's own English translation of the Cheng Weishi Lun (成唯識論), the treatise that systematizes the Yogācāra teaching of Consciousness-Only. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T31, no. 1585 Covers: the opening homage through the first verse and its initial commentary

Project Background

The Cheng Weishi Lun is not the work of a single author in the ordinary sense. Its core is Vasubandhu's Triṃśikā — thirty verses summarizing the Consciousness-Only teaching, composed in Sanskrit around the 4th–5th century CE. Vasubandhu died before writing a prose commentary on his own verses, and in the centuries that followed, ten different Indian masters wrote independent commentaries trying to explain what he meant — among them Sthiramati, Citrabhāna, and, most influentially, Dharmapāla.

In 659 CE, the Tang dynasty monk-pilgrim Xuanzang (玄奘) — who had studied at Nālandā under Dharmapāla's own student Śīlabhadra — took all ten commentaries back to China and wove them into a single continuous Chinese text, built primarily around Dharmapāla's interpretation but incorporating the other nine where they sharpen or complete the picture. That woven text is the Cheng Weishi Lun. It is, in other words, already a translation project in its own right: Xuanzang's synthesis of a whole tradition of commentary into one coherent voice.

This treatise has no surviving Sanskrit original — only Xuanzang's Chinese compilation survives complete. It became the doctrinal foundation of the Faxiang (法相) school in China and the Hossō school in Japan, and remains one of the most rigorous philosophical treatments of mind in the Buddhist canon.

Translation Method

Two English translations of this treatise already exist: Wei Tat's Doctrine of Mere-Consciousness (1973) and Francis H. Cook's Demonstration of Consciousness Only, published in the BDK English Tripiṭaka series (1999). Both remain under copyright. We do not reproduce or paraphrase either of them here — this is Ru-Yi's own translation, made directly from the Chinese source text preserved by CBETA, which is in the public domain.

  • We keep the structure of Xuanzang's Chinese and preserve key technical terms consistently — see the translation notes for the running glossary.
  • Where CBETA preserves the traditional outline (科判) used to teach this text for over a thousand years, we follow that outline to structure the page, rather than inventing our own divisions.
  • Every completed section can be revised after review by Dharma teachers and readers familiar with the Chinese text.

Homage to the Field of Merit 歸敬福田

The treatise opens, as Indian śāstras traditionally do, with a verse of homage — not to a single figure, but to two levels of realization at once.

Chinese Source

稽首唯識性、滿、分清淨者，

Ru-Yi Translation

I bow my head to the nature of Consciousness-Only — to the one who is fully pure in it, and to those who are pure in it only in part.

Reading note: Commentators read "fully pure" (滿清淨) as the Buddha, who has completed the realization of Consciousness-Only without remainder, and "pure in part" (分清淨) as bodhisattvas, who have realized the same nature but are still walking the path toward it. One verse, two bows: to the goal, and to everyone still on the way to it.

Stating the Purpose of the Treatise 敘釋論意

Chinese Source

我今釋彼說，利樂諸有情。

Ru-Yi Translation

I now explain what they have taught, for the benefit and happiness of all sentient beings.

Vasubandhu states his motive in a single line: not scholarship for its own sake, but benefit for living beings. Everything that follows — the thirty verses that make up the rest of this treatise — is offered in that spirit.

Three Masters, One Passage 以長行明本論主造論之意

Immediately after the verse, the traditional outline attributes the next few lines — one continuous passage — to three different commentators in turn, each reading the same words as emphasizing a different reason for the treatise. This is a first, small example of exactly what the whole Cheng Weishi Lun does at every stage: hold several masters' readings side by side rather than flattening them into one.

Sthiramati (安慧): severing the obstructions, attaining the fruits

Chinese Source

今造此論，為於二空有迷謬者生正解故。生解為斷二重障故，由我、法執二障具生，若證二空，彼障隨斷。斷障為得二勝果故，由斷續生煩惱障故，證真解脫；由斷礙解所知障故，得大菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

This treatise is composed now so that those who are confused or mistaken about the two emptinesses may arrive at right understanding. Right understanding arises in order to sever the two obstructions, for the two obstructions arise together with the two attachments — attachment to self and attachment to dharmas. When the two emptinesses are realized, those obstructions are severed accordingly.

The obstructions are severed in order to attain the two supreme fruits: by severing the afflictive obstruction, which perpetuates rebirth, true liberation is realized; by severing the cognitive obstruction, which blocks understanding, great bodhi is attained.

Citrabhāna (火辨): opening the eyes of the confused

Chinese Source

又為開示謬執我、法，迷唯識者，令達二空，於唯識理如實知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And also, to open the eyes of those who wrongly grasp at self and dharmas and are confused about Consciousness-Only, so that they may penetrate the two emptinesses and know the truth of Consciousness-Only exactly as it is.

Dharmapāla (護法): refuting four specific mistaken views

Chinese Source

復有迷、謬唯識理者，或執外境如識非無；或執內識如境非有；或執諸識用別體同；或執離心無別心所。為遮此等種種異執，令於唯識深妙理中得如實解，故作斯論。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, those who are confused or mistaken about the principle of Consciousness-Only. Some cling to the view that external objects, like consciousness itself, are not unreal. Some cling to the view that inner consciousness, like its objects, does not truly exist either. Some cling to the view that the various consciousnesses differ in function but share a single essence. Some cling to the view that apart from mind there are no separate mental factors.

To turn back these various mistaken attachments, and to bring students to a true understanding of the profound and subtle principle of Consciousness-Only, this treatise is therefore composed.

Reading note: Dharmapāla's four views, read together, map the whole space of ways to get Consciousness-Only wrong: naive realism (the world outside is just as real as the mind perceiving it), an over-correction into nihilism (nothing, inside or out, truly exists), collapsing all eight consciousnesses into one undifferentiated mind, and denying that mental factors (caitasikas, like attention or feeling) have any reality distinct from mind itself. The rest of the treatise systematically works through and corrects each of these.

Three attachments were just named in passing and will matter for the rest of this fascicle: the belief in a fixed self (我), the belief in fixed, independently real dharmas (法), and the pair of obstructions — afflictive (煩惱障) and cognitive (所知障) — that these beliefs produce. See the translation notes for how we render these terms consistently across all ten fascicles.

Self and Dharmas Are Only Provisionally Spoken Of 明唯識相 · 略釋難標宗

The prefatory homage is finished. The treatise now turns to the treatise proper — Vasubandhu's thirty verses, with Xuanzang's woven commentary. It opens the only way a treatise on Consciousness-Only can open: by facing the obvious objection.

Raising the Question 將發論端寄問徵起

Chinese Source

若唯有識，云何世間及諸聖教說有我、法？

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is only consciousness, how is it that the world, and the sacred teachings themselves, speak of a self and of dharmas?

This is the question the entire treatise exists to answer. Everyone — ordinary people and scripture alike — talks constantly about "I" and about "things." If Consciousness-Only is true, what are they actually talking about?

The First Verse 舉頌依義正答

Chinese Source

頌曰：由假說我、法，有種種相轉。彼依識所變，此能變唯三：謂異熟、思量，及了別境識。

Ru-Yi Translation

The verse says: Because self and dharmas are only provisionally spoken of, all their various characteristics arise. These depend on what consciousness transforms — and this capacity for transformation is of only three kinds: the ripening consciousness, the deliberating consciousness, and the consciousnesses that discriminate objects.

Reading note: This verse names, for the first time, the treatise's whole architecture of mind: the ripening consciousness (異熟識, the ālayavijñāna, which stores the results of past action and slowly "ripens" them into experience), the deliberating consciousness (思量識, manas, the seventh consciousness, whose constant self-referential grasping is the root of the sense of "I"), and the consciousnesses that discriminate objects (了別境識, the familiar six: the five senses plus ordinary discursive thought). Self and dharmas, the verse claims, are just names laid over what these three keep transforming.

Briefly Explaining How the Verse Answers the Question 長行釋 · 略釋頌答外徵

Chinese Source

論曰：世間、聖教說有我、法，但由假立，非實有性。我謂主宰，法謂軌持。彼二俱有種種相轉：「我種種相」謂有情、命者等，預流、一來等。「法種種相」謂實、德、業等；蘊、處、界等。「轉」謂隨緣施設有異。

Ru-Yi Translation

The treatise says: When the world and the sacred teachings speak of a self and of dharmas, this is only a provisional positing — it is not that either truly, really exists. "Self" means something that rules and possesses; "dharma" means something that holds to a pattern and maintains its own character.

Both of these appear under all sorts of names. The various names for "self" are things like "sentient being," "life-possessor," or, in the language of the path, "stream-enterer," "once-returner." The various names for "dharmas" are things like the categories of substance, quality, and action, or the Buddhist categories of aggregates, sense-fields, and elements. "Arise" means that, depending on the occasion, different such names get applied.

如是諸相若由假說，依何得成？彼相皆依識所轉變而假施設。「識」謂了別，此中識言亦攝心所，定相應故。「變」謂識體轉似二分，相、見俱依自證起故，依斯二分施設我、法，彼二離此無所依故。

If all these names are only provisional, what are they actually based on? All of them are provisionally set up in dependence on what consciousness transforms. "Consciousness" here means the activity of discriminating and cognizing; the word also covers the mental factors that always accompany it, since they never occur apart from it.

"Transforms" means that the substance of consciousness turns and manifests as two divisions: a perceived aspect and a perceiving aspect, both arising from a deeper, self-witnessing aspect of consciousness. Self and dharmas are named in dependence on these two divisions — apart from them, self and dharmas would have nothing at all to stand on.

或復內識轉似外境。我、法分別熏習力故，諸識生時變似我、法，此我、法相雖在內識，而由分別似外境現，諸有情類無始時來緣此執為實我、實法，如患夢者，患夢力故，心似種種外境相現，緣此執為實有外境。

Or, put another way: inner consciousness turns and manifests as if it were an outer object. Because of habits built up by endlessly discriminating "self" and "dharmas," whenever the various consciousnesses arise, they transform and appear as self and dharmas. Although these appearances of self and dharmas exist only within consciousness, discrimination makes them show up as if they were something outside it.

Sentient beings, from beginningless time, have taken hold of these appearances and clung to them as a real self and real dharmas — exactly as someone caught in a troubling dream, through the sheer force of the dream, has a mind that produces every kind of outer scene, and, while dreaming, clings to those scenes as real.

愚夫所計實我、實法都無所有，但隨妄情而施設故，說之為假。內識所變似我、似法雖有，而非實我、法性，然似彼現，故說為假。外境隨情而施設故，非有如識；內識必依因緣生故，非無如境，由此便遮增、減二執。境依內識而假立故，唯世俗有；識是假境所依事故，亦勝義有。

The real self and real dharmas imagined by the unenlightened do not exist at all — they are posited only in step with deluded feeling, and so are called provisional. What consciousness transforms into, resembling a self and resembling dharmas, does exist, but it is not the true nature of self or dharmas; because it only resembles them, it too is called provisional.

Because outer objects are posited only in step with deluded feeling, they do not exist the way consciousness does; because inner consciousness necessarily arises from causes and conditions, it is not simply nonexistent the way an outer object is. This blocks both mistaken extremes — overstating what is there, and denying it altogether. Since objects are only provisionally set up in dependence on inner consciousness, they exist conventionally, not ultimately; since consciousness is the real ground that the provisional object depends on, consciousness exists ultimately as well.

Reading note: This paragraph is doing a great deal of work with very few technical moves. First it defines "self" and "dharmas" as functions, not things: a self is "whatever rules and possesses," a dharma is "whatever holds a fixed pattern" — definitions built to be filled by a real referent or an illusory one. Then it introduces the perceived aspect (相分) and perceiving aspect (見分) of consciousness, both grounded in a deeper self-witnessing aspect (自證分) — the beginning of the treatise's famous multi-part model of how a single act of cognition can seem to split into a knower and a known.

Notice the careful middle path at the end: this is not a claim that the outside world is simply nothing (that would be the "denying" extreme, 減執), nor that it is exactly as real as it appears (the "overstating" extreme, 增執). Objects exist only conventionally (世俗有), as dependent appearances; consciousness itself exists ultimately (勝義有), as their real basis. The dream simile is doing real philosophical work here too: a dream-image is not nothing — it has causes, and it has effects on the dreamer — but it is also not what it appears to be.
