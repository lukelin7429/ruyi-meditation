Fascicle 1 · 成唯識論卷第一

To Whom We Bow, and Why This Treatise Exists

This page begins Ru-Yi's own English translation of the Cheng Weishi Lun (成唯識論), the treatise that systematizes the Yogācāra teaching of Consciousness-Only. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T31, no. 1585 Covers: the opening homage through non-Buddhist schools, and the refutation of atoms as real

Project Background

The Cheng Weishi Lun is not the work of a single author in the ordinary sense. Its core is Vasubandhu's Triṃśikā — thirty verses summarizing the Consciousness-Only teaching, composed in Sanskrit around the 4th–5th century CE. Vasubandhu died before writing a prose commentary on his own verses, and in the centuries that followed, ten different Indian masters wrote independent commentaries trying to explain what he meant — among them Sthiramati, Citrabhāna, and, most influentially, Dharmapāla.

In 659 CE, the Tang dynasty monk-pilgrim Xuanzang (玄奘) — who had studied at Nālandā under Dharmapāla's own student Śīlabhadra — took all ten commentaries back to China and wove them into a single continuous Chinese text, built primarily around Dharmapāla's interpretation but incorporating the other nine where they sharpen or complete the picture. That woven text is the Cheng Weishi Lun. It is, in other words, already a translation project in its own right: Xuanzang's synthesis of a whole tradition of commentary into one coherent voice.

This treatise has no surviving Sanskrit original — only Xuanzang's Chinese compilation survives complete. It became the doctrinal foundation of the Faxiang (法相) school in China and the Hossō school in Japan, and remains one of the most rigorous philosophical treatments of mind in the Buddhist canon.

Translation Method

Two English translations of this treatise already exist: Wei Tat's Doctrine of Mere-Consciousness (1973) and Francis H. Cook's Demonstration of Consciousness Only, published in the BDK English Tripiṭaka series (1999). Both remain under copyright. We do not reproduce or paraphrase either of them here — this is Ru-Yi's own translation, made directly from the Chinese source text preserved by CBETA, which is in the public domain.

  • We keep the structure of Xuanzang's Chinese and preserve key technical terms consistently — see the translation notes for the running glossary.
  • Where CBETA preserves the traditional outline (科判) used to teach this text for over a thousand years, we follow that outline to structure the page, rather than inventing our own divisions.
  • Every completed section can be revised after review by Dharma teachers and readers familiar with the Chinese text.

Homage to the Field of Merit 歸敬福田

The treatise opens, as Indian śāstras traditionally do, with a verse of homage — not to a single figure, but to two levels of realization at once.

Chinese Source

稽首唯識性、滿、分清淨者，

Ru-Yi Translation

I bow my head to the nature of Consciousness-Only — to the one who is fully pure in it, and to those who are pure in it only in part.

Reading note: Commentators read "fully pure" (滿清淨) as the Buddha, who has completed the realization of Consciousness-Only without remainder, and "pure in part" (分清淨) as bodhisattvas, who have realized the same nature but are still walking the path toward it. One verse, two bows: to the goal, and to everyone still on the way to it.

Stating the Purpose of the Treatise 敘釋論意

Chinese Source

我今釋彼說，利樂諸有情。

Ru-Yi Translation

I now explain what they have taught, for the benefit and happiness of all sentient beings.

Vasubandhu states his motive in a single line: not scholarship for its own sake, but benefit for living beings. Everything that follows — the thirty verses that make up the rest of this treatise — is offered in that spirit.

Three Masters, One Passage 以長行明本論主造論之意

Immediately after the verse, the traditional outline attributes the next few lines — one continuous passage — to three different commentators in turn, each reading the same words as emphasizing a different reason for the treatise. This is a first, small example of exactly what the whole Cheng Weishi Lun does at every stage: hold several masters' readings side by side rather than flattening them into one.

Sthiramati (安慧): severing the obstructions, attaining the fruits

Chinese Source

今造此論，為於二空有迷謬者生正解故。生解為斷二重障故，由我、法執二障具生，若證二空，彼障隨斷。斷障為得二勝果故，由斷續生煩惱障故，證真解脫；由斷礙解所知障故，得大菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

This treatise is composed now so that those who are confused or mistaken about the two emptinesses may arrive at right understanding. Right understanding arises in order to sever the two obstructions, for the two obstructions arise together with the two attachments — attachment to self and attachment to dharmas. When the two emptinesses are realized, those obstructions are severed accordingly.

The obstructions are severed in order to attain the two supreme fruits: by severing the afflictive obstruction, which perpetuates rebirth, true liberation is realized; by severing the cognitive obstruction, which blocks understanding, great bodhi is attained.

Citrabhāna (火辨): opening the eyes of the confused

Chinese Source

又為開示謬執我、法，迷唯識者，令達二空，於唯識理如實知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And also, to open the eyes of those who wrongly grasp at self and dharmas and are confused about Consciousness-Only, so that they may penetrate the two emptinesses and know the truth of Consciousness-Only exactly as it is.

Dharmapāla (護法): refuting four specific mistaken views

Chinese Source

復有迷、謬唯識理者，或執外境如識非無；或執內識如境非有；或執諸識用別體同；或執離心無別心所。為遮此等種種異執，令於唯識深妙理中得如實解，故作斯論。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, those who are confused or mistaken about the principle of Consciousness-Only. Some cling to the view that external objects, like consciousness itself, are not unreal. Some cling to the view that inner consciousness, like its objects, does not truly exist either. Some cling to the view that the various consciousnesses differ in function but share a single essence. Some cling to the view that apart from mind there are no separate mental factors.

To turn back these various mistaken attachments, and to bring students to a true understanding of the profound and subtle principle of Consciousness-Only, this treatise is therefore composed.

Reading note: Dharmapāla's four views, read together, map the whole space of ways to get Consciousness-Only wrong: naive realism (the world outside is just as real as the mind perceiving it), an over-correction into nihilism (nothing, inside or out, truly exists), collapsing all eight consciousnesses into one undifferentiated mind, and denying that mental factors (caitasikas, like attention or feeling) have any reality distinct from mind itself. The rest of the treatise systematically works through and corrects each of these.

Three attachments were just named in passing and will matter for the rest of this fascicle: the belief in a fixed self (我), the belief in fixed, independently real dharmas (法), and the pair of obstructions — afflictive (煩惱障) and cognitive (所知障) — that these beliefs produce. See the translation notes for how we render these terms consistently across all ten fascicles.

Self and Dharmas Are Only Provisionally Spoken Of 明唯識相 · 略釋難標宗

The prefatory homage is finished. The treatise now turns to the treatise proper — Vasubandhu's thirty verses, with Xuanzang's woven commentary. It opens the only way a treatise on Consciousness-Only can open: by facing the obvious objection.

Raising the Question 將發論端寄問徵起

Chinese Source

若唯有識，云何世間及諸聖教說有我、法？

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is only consciousness, how is it that the world, and the sacred teachings themselves, speak of a self and of dharmas?

This is the question the entire treatise exists to answer. Everyone — ordinary people and scripture alike — talks constantly about "I" and about "things." If Consciousness-Only is true, what are they actually talking about?

The First Verse 舉頌依義正答

Chinese Source

頌曰：由假說我、法，有種種相轉。彼依識所變，此能變唯三：謂異熟、思量，及了別境識。

Ru-Yi Translation

The verse says: Because self and dharmas are only provisionally spoken of, all their various characteristics arise. These depend on what consciousness transforms — and this capacity for transformation is of only three kinds: the ripening consciousness, the deliberating consciousness, and the consciousnesses that discriminate objects.

Reading note: This verse names, for the first time, the treatise's whole architecture of mind: the ripening consciousness (異熟識, the ālayavijñāna, which stores the results of past action and slowly "ripens" them into experience), the deliberating consciousness (思量識, manas, the seventh consciousness, whose constant self-referential grasping is the root of the sense of "I"), and the consciousnesses that discriminate objects (了別境識, the familiar six: the five senses plus ordinary discursive thought). Self and dharmas, the verse claims, are just names laid over what these three keep transforming.

Briefly Explaining How the Verse Answers the Question 長行釋 · 略釋頌答外徵

Chinese Source

論曰：世間、聖教說有我、法，但由假立，非實有性。我謂主宰，法謂軌持。彼二俱有種種相轉：「我種種相」謂有情、命者等，預流、一來等。「法種種相」謂實、德、業等；蘊、處、界等。「轉」謂隨緣施設有異。

Ru-Yi Translation

The treatise says: When the world and the sacred teachings speak of a self and of dharmas, this is only a provisional positing — it is not that either truly, really exists. "Self" means something that rules and possesses; "dharma" means something that holds to a pattern and maintains its own character.

Both of these appear under all sorts of names. The various names for "self" are things like "sentient being," "life-possessor," or, in the language of the path, "stream-enterer," "once-returner." The various names for "dharmas" are things like the categories of substance, quality, and action, or the Buddhist categories of aggregates, sense-fields, and elements. "Arise" means that, depending on the occasion, different such names get applied.

如是諸相若由假說，依何得成？彼相皆依識所轉變而假施設。「識」謂了別，此中識言亦攝心所，定相應故。「變」謂識體轉似二分，相、見俱依自證起故，依斯二分施設我、法，彼二離此無所依故。

If all these names are only provisional, what are they actually based on? All of them are provisionally set up in dependence on what consciousness transforms. "Consciousness" here means the activity of discriminating and cognizing; the word also covers the mental factors that always accompany it, since they never occur apart from it.

"Transforms" means that the substance of consciousness turns and manifests as two divisions: a perceived aspect and a perceiving aspect, both arising from a deeper, self-witnessing aspect of consciousness. Self and dharmas are named in dependence on these two divisions — apart from them, self and dharmas would have nothing at all to stand on.

或復內識轉似外境。我、法分別熏習力故，諸識生時變似我、法，此我、法相雖在內識，而由分別似外境現，諸有情類無始時來緣此執為實我、實法，如患夢者，患夢力故，心似種種外境相現，緣此執為實有外境。

Or, put another way: inner consciousness turns and manifests as if it were an outer object. Because of habits built up by endlessly discriminating "self" and "dharmas," whenever the various consciousnesses arise, they transform and appear as self and dharmas. Although these appearances of self and dharmas exist only within consciousness, discrimination makes them show up as if they were something outside it.

Sentient beings, from beginningless time, have taken hold of these appearances and clung to them as a real self and real dharmas — exactly as someone caught in a troubling dream, through the sheer force of the dream, has a mind that produces every kind of outer scene, and, while dreaming, clings to those scenes as real.

愚夫所計實我、實法都無所有，但隨妄情而施設故，說之為假。內識所變似我、似法雖有，而非實我、法性，然似彼現，故說為假。外境隨情而施設故，非有如識；內識必依因緣生故，非無如境，由此便遮增、減二執。境依內識而假立故，唯世俗有；識是假境所依事故，亦勝義有。

The real self and real dharmas imagined by the unenlightened do not exist at all — they are posited only in step with deluded feeling, and so are called provisional. What consciousness transforms into, resembling a self and resembling dharmas, does exist, but it is not the true nature of self or dharmas; because it only resembles them, it too is called provisional.

Because outer objects are posited only in step with deluded feeling, they do not exist the way consciousness does; because inner consciousness necessarily arises from causes and conditions, it is not simply nonexistent the way an outer object is. This blocks both mistaken extremes — overstating what is there, and denying it altogether. Since objects are only provisionally set up in dependence on inner consciousness, they exist conventionally, not ultimately; since consciousness is the real ground that the provisional object depends on, consciousness exists ultimately as well.

Reading note: This paragraph is doing a great deal of work with very few technical moves. First it defines "self" and "dharmas" as functions, not things: a self is "whatever rules and possesses," a dharma is "whatever holds a fixed pattern" — definitions built to be filled by a real referent or an illusory one. Then it introduces the perceived aspect (相分) and perceiving aspect (見分) of consciousness, both grounded in a deeper self-witnessing aspect (自證分) — the beginning of the treatise's famous multi-part model of how a single act of cognition can seem to split into a knower and a known.

Notice the careful middle path at the end: this is not a claim that the outside world is simply nothing (that would be the "denying" extreme, 減執), nor that it is exactly as real as it appears (the "overstating" extreme, 增執). Objects exist only conventionally (世俗有), as dependent appearances; consciousness itself exists ultimately (勝義有), as their real basis. The dream simile is doing real philosophical work here too: a dream-image is not nothing — it has causes, and it has effects on the dreamer — but it is also not what it appears to be.

Refuting a Real Self, Six Ways 依教廣成分 · 廣釋頌（一）

Having shown that self and dharmas are provisionally posited on what consciousness transforms, the treatise now makes good on the claim by working through, and dismantling, every serious philosophical account of what a real, substantial self could be. This is the "extensive explanation of the verse" — a systematic tour through Indian philosophy's menu of ātman-theories.

Chinese Source

云何應知實無外境，唯有內識似外境生？實我、實法不可得故。如何實我不可得耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

How can it be known that there is really no outer object, and that it is only inner consciousness that appears as though it were an outer object? Because a real self and real dharmas cannot be found to exist. How can it be known that a real self cannot be found?

Three Views of Self by Size 執我體常、周遍 / 量不定 / 至細

諸所執我，略有三種：一者執我體常、周遍，量同虛空，隨處造業受苦、樂故；二者執我，其體雖常，而量不定，隨身大小有卷、舒故；三者執我體常、至細，如一極微，潛轉身中作事業故。

The self that various schools cling to comes, in brief, in three kinds. First, some hold that the substance of self is constant and all-pervading, equal in extent to space itself, since it acts and undergoes suffering or happiness wherever it is. Second, some hold that, although the substance of self is constant, its extent is not fixed, since it expands and contracts with the size of the body. Third, some hold that the substance of self is constant and extremely subtle, like a single atom, since it moves in secret through the body, carrying out its work.

初且非理，所以者何？執我常、遍，量同虛空，應不隨身受苦、樂等；又常、遍故，應無動轉，如何隨身能造諸業？又所執我，一切有情為同為異？若言同者，一作業時一切應作，一受果時一切應受，一得解脫時一切應解脫，便成大過；若言異者，諸有情我更相遍故，體應相雜，又一作業、一受果時，與一切我處無別故，應名一切所作、所受。若謂作、受各有所屬無斯過者，理亦不然，業、果及身，與諸我合，屬此非彼不應理故；一解脫時一切應解脫，所修、證法一切我合故。

The first view is simply unreasonable. Why? If self is constant and all-pervading, equal in extent to space, it should not undergo suffering or happiness together with any one body; and being constant and all-pervading, it should have no movement at all — so how could it act through a body?

Further: is this self the same for every sentient being, or different for each? If it is the same, then when one being acts, all should act; when one receives a result, all should receive it; when one attains liberation, all should attain it — a great absurdity. If it is different for each, then, since every self equally pervades everywhere, their substances would have to intermingle; and when one acts or receives a result, since it occupies the very place every other self also occupies, that act or result should belong to all of them alike. If one answers that action and result each belong only to their own self, that fails too — action, result, and body are joined equally to every self, so there is no ground to say they belong to this one and not that one; and when one being reaches liberation, all should reach it, since the practice and realization involved are joined to every self alike.

中亦非理，所以者何？我體常住，不應隨身而有舒、卷，既有舒、卷如槖籥風，應非常住，又我隨身應可分析，如何可執我體一耶？故彼所言如童竪戲。

The second view is likewise unreasonable. Why? If the substance of self is permanent, it should not expand and contract with the body; but if it does expand and contract, like air in a bellows, then it is not permanent after all. And if self can be divided up along with the body, how can it still be claimed to be a single unity? What is said here amounts to nothing more than children's play.

後亦非理，所以者何？我量至小如一極微，如何能令大身遍動？若謂雖小而速巡身，如旋火輪，似遍動者，則所執我非一、非常，諸有往來，非常、一故。

The third view is likewise unreasonable. Why? If self is as minute as a single atom, how could it move an entire large body throughout? If one answers that, though small, it races through the body so fast — like a whirling firebrand that seems to trace a whole ring of fire — that it appears to move everywhere at once, then the self so described is neither a single unity nor permanent, since whatever comes and goes in this way cannot be both permanent and one.

Three Views of Self by Relation to the Aggregates 即蘊 / 離蘊 / 非即非離蘊

又所執我，復有三種：一者即蘊；二者離蘊；三者與蘊非即非離。初即蘊我，理且不然，我應如蘊非常、一故；又內諸色定非實我，如外諸色有質礙故；心、心所法亦非實我，不恒相續，待眾緣故；餘行、餘色亦非實我，如虛空等，非覺性故。中離蘊我，理亦不然，應如虛空，無作、受故。後俱非我，理亦不然，許依蘊立，非即、離蘊，應如瓶等，非實我故。又既不可說有為、無為，亦應不可說是我、非我。故彼所執實我不成。

There are, further, three more kinds of self that are clung to: self as identical with the five aggregates; self as separate from the aggregates; and self as neither identical with nor separate from the aggregates.

The first is simply unreasonable: such a self would have to be, like the aggregates themselves, impermanent and plural, not one enduring unity. The physical elements within the body are certainly not the real self, since, like physical things outside the body, they have material resistance. Mind and mental factors are also not the real self, since they do not endure in unbroken continuity but depend on many conditions. The remaining formations are likewise not the real self, since, like space, they have no faculty of awareness.

The second is likewise unreasonable: such a self would have to be, like space, incapable of acting or of experiencing anything.

The third is likewise unreasonable: granted that it is established in dependence on the aggregates while being neither identical with nor separate from them, it would then be like a pot — which is also established in dependence on its parts while being neither identical with nor separate from them — and a pot is not a real self either.

And since it cannot even be said whether such a self is conditioned or unconditioned, it likewise cannot be said whether it is self or not-self. Therefore the real self clung to in all these ways is not established.

Reading note: These six views are not a random list — together they cover the live options in classical Indian philosophy for what a substantial self could be. A self "equal in extent to space" recalls the all-pervading puruṣa of Sāṃkhya thought; a self that expands and contracts with the body, or is atom-sized and races around inside it, are the kinds of positions Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika and other schools worked out to explain how an unchanging self could still act through a changing body.

The second set — identical with, separate from, or neither identical with nor separate from the aggregates — targets a different fault line. "Neither identical with nor separate from the aggregates" was, historically, the position of the Vātsīputrīya (犢子部), a school within Buddhism itself that proposed a "person" (pudgala) irreducible to the five aggregates yet not a self apart from them either. The treatise's pot analogy is deliberately deflating: a pot, too, depends on its parts without being identical to any one of them — that dependency is just ordinary conventional existence, not evidence of a hidden real self underneath it.

Innate and Discriminated Self-Attachment 依教廣成分 · 廣釋頌（二）

Two quick dilemmas close out the abstract arguments, and then the treatise turns to something more practical: not just showing that no theory of self survives scrutiny, but explaining what self-attachment actually is, psychologically, and why some of it is so much harder to remove than the rest.

Two More Dilemmas 有無思慮 / 有無作用

Chinese Source

又諸所執實有我體，為有思慮？為無思慮？若有思慮，應是無常，非一切時有思慮故；若無思慮，應如虛空，不能作業，亦不受果。故所執我，理俱不成。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: does the real self that is clung to possess thought, or not? If it possesses thought, it must be impermanent, since thought is not present at all times. If it does not possess thought, it would be like space — incapable of performing action or experiencing a result. Either way, the self that is clung to cannot be established.

又諸所執實有我體，為有作用？為無作用？若有作用，如手足等，應是無常。若無作用，如兔角等，應非實我，故所執我，二俱不成。

Furthermore: does the real self that is clung to have function, or not? If it has function, then, like the hands and feet, it must be impermanent. If it has no function, then, like the horns of a rabbit, it cannot be a real self. Either way, the self that is clung to is not established.

Is the View of Self Accurate Perception? 我見所緣境

又諸所執實有我體，為是我見所緣境不？若非我見所緣境者，汝等云何知實有我？若是我見所緣境者，應有我見非顛倒攝，如實知故。若爾，如何執有我者所信至教皆毀我見，稱讚無我，言無我見能證涅槃；執著我見，沈淪生死。豈有邪見能證涅槃，正見翻令沈淪生死？又諸我見不緣實我，有所緣故，如緣餘心；我見所緣定非實我，是所緣故，如所餘法。是故我見不緣實我，但緣內識變現諸蘊，隨自妄情，種種計度。

Furthermore: is the real self that is clung to the object taken by the view of self, or not? If it is not, then how could you claim to know a real self exists at all? If it is, then that view of self would have to count as accurate perception rather than a distorted view, since it would be knowing something exactly as it is. But if so, why do the very scriptures trusted by those who cling to a self all condemn the view of self and praise the teaching of no-self — saying that freedom from the view of self can realize nirvāṇa, while attachment to it sinks one in birth-and-death? How could a mistaken view realize nirvāṇa, while a correct view instead sinks one in birth-and-death?

Moreover, no view of self ever actually takes a real self as its object, for the simple reason that it has an object at all — like any other mental state that takes an object. And whatever the view of self does take as its object is certainly not a real self, for the simple reason that it is something taken as an object — like anything else so taken. The view of self, then, does not take a real self as its object; it only takes hold of the aggregates that inner consciousness itself has manifested, and, following its own deluded feeling, constructs them in various ways.

Two Kinds of Self-Attachment 俱生我執 · 分別我執

Chinese Source

然諸我執略有二種：一者俱生；二者分別。俱生我執無始時來虛妄熏習，內因力故，恒與身俱；不待邪教及邪分別，任運而轉，故名俱生。此復二種：一、常相續，在第七識，緣第八識，起自心相，執為實我；二、有間斷，在第六識，緣識所變五取蘊相，或總、或別，起自心相，執為實我。此二我執，細故難斷，後修道中，數數修習勝生空觀，方能除滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Attachment to self, in brief, is of two kinds: innate, and discriminated. Innate self-attachment, through deluded habituation from beginningless time, arises constantly together with the body by its own inner causal power; it does not depend on any mistaken teaching or mistaken discrimination but operates spontaneously — hence it is called innate.

This too is of two kinds. One is continuous and unbroken: located in the seventh consciousness, which takes the eighth consciousness as its object, it generates its own mental image and clings to it as a real self. The other occurs with interruption: located in the sixth consciousness, which takes the appearance of the five grasped aggregates as transformed by consciousness — as a whole or individually — it generates its own mental image and clings to it as a real self. Because these two are subtle, they are hard to sever; only later, on the path of cultivation, through repeated practice of contemplating the emptiness of a higher kind of arising, can they finally be eliminated.

分別我執亦由現在外緣力故，非與身俱，要待邪教及邪分別然後方起，故名分別，唯在第六意識中有。此亦二種：一、緣邪教所說蘊相，起自心相，分別、計度，執為實我；二、緣邪教所說我相，起自心相，分別、計度，執為實我。此二我執麁故易斷，初見道時，觀一切法生空真如，即能除滅。

Discriminated self-attachment, by contrast, is brought about by the power of present external conditions; it does not arise together with the body, but requires a mistaken teaching and mistaken discrimination before it can arise — hence it is called discriminated. It exists only in the sixth, cognizing consciousness.

This too is of two kinds: one takes the characteristics of the aggregates as described by a mistaken teaching, generates its own mental image, and constructs it as a real self; the other takes the characteristics of a self as described by a mistaken teaching, generates its own mental image, and constructs it as a real self. Because these two are coarse, they are easy to sever; at the very moment of first entering the path of seeing, by contemplating the true suchness of the emptiness of self in all dharmas, they are eliminated at once.

如是所說一切我執，自心外蘊，或有、或無；自心內蘊，一切皆有。是故我執皆緣無常五取蘊相，妄執為我。然諸蘊相，從緣生故，是如幻有；妄所執我，橫計度故，決定非有。故契經說：苾芻當知，世間沙門、婆羅門等所有我見，一切皆緣五取蘊起。

In every kind of self-attachment just described, an object among the aggregates outside one's own mind may or may not be present — but an object among the aggregates within one's own mind is always present. Every kind of self-attachment, then, takes the impermanent appearance of the five grasped aggregates as its object, and mistakenly clings to it as self. The appearance of the aggregates, since it arises from conditions, exists the way an illusion exists; the self mistakenly clung to, since it is arbitrarily constructed, definitely does not exist at all. As the scripture says: "Monks, you should know that whatever view of self exists among the ascetics and brahmins of this world, all of it takes the five grasped aggregates as its object."

Reading note: This is one of the most practically important distinctions in the whole treatise. Discriminated self-attachment (分別我執) is the kind built out of explicit wrong views and bad philosophy — it only exists in the sixth (thinking) consciousness, and, being "coarse," it is removed all at once at the moment of first genuine insight (見道, the path of seeing). Innate self-attachment (俱生我執) is something else entirely: it needs no philosophy at all, runs constantly beneath conscious thought in the seventh consciousness (manas) as it clings to the eighth (ālayavijñāna), and surfaces intermittently in the sixth. It is "subtle," and cannot be removed by a single insight — only worn down through sustained meditative cultivation over the rest of the path.

In plain terms: understanding, intellectually, that there is no fixed self is the easy part, and it can happen in a single moment of clear seeing. The felt sense of being a self — the one that flinches, that takes things personally, that quietly persists even in someone who has fully understood the doctrine — is the hard part, and no amount of correct philosophy removes it by itself.

Four Objections, Answered 依教廣成分 · 廣釋頌（三）

The refutation of self invites an obvious pushback: if there is no self, how do memory, moral responsibility, and the whole project of liberation even make sense? The treatise takes each objection in turn.

Without a Self, How Is There Memory? 憶、識、誦、習、恩、怨等事

Chinese Source

實我若無，云何得有憶、識、誦、習、恩、怨等事？所執實我既常無變，後應如前，是事非有；前應如後，是事非無。以後與前，體無別故。若謂我用，前後變易，非我體者，理亦不然。用不離體，應常有故；體不離用，應非常故。然諸有情，各有本識，一類相續，任持種子，與一切法更互為因，熏習力故，得有如是憶、識等事。故所設難，於汝有失，非於我宗。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is really no self, how could there be such things as memory, recognition, recitation, habit, gratitude, and resentment? — Since the self you cling to is held to be constant and unchanging, whatever comes later should be exactly as what came before, so these changing things should not exist at all; or whatever came before should be exactly as what comes later, so these things should never have failed to exist even earlier. For, on your view, its substance is no different between before and after.

If you answer that it is only the self's function, not its substance, that changes over time, that does not hold up either: function is never apart from substance, so the function should be constant too; and substance is never apart from function, so the substance should not be constant either.

It is, rather, that every sentient being has its own root consciousness, continuing on as a single stream, maintaining seeds, and serving as a mutual cause together with all dharmas — and it is through the power of this habituation that memory, recognition, and the rest are possible. The challenge you raise, then, is a fault in your own position, not in ours.

Without a Self, Who Acts and Who Receives? 誰能造業？誰受果耶？

若無實我，誰能造業？誰受果耶？所執實我，既無變易，猶如虛空，如何可能造業、受果？若有變易，應是無常。然諸有情心、心所法因緣力故，相續無斷，造業、受果，於理無違。

If there is no real self, who creates karma, and who receives its result? — The self you cling to, being changeless, is just like space: how could it possibly create karma or receive a result? And if it does change, then it must be impermanent. It is, rather, that the mind and mental factors of sentient beings, through the power of causes and conditions, continue on without interruption, and in this way create karma and receive its result — which is in no way inconsistent with reason.

Without a Self, Who Is Liberated? 誰於生死輪迴？誰求涅槃？

我若實無，誰於生死輪迴諸趣？誰復厭苦求趣涅槃？所執實我，既無生滅，如何可說生死輪迴？常如虛空，非苦所惱，何為厭捨求趣涅槃？故彼所言常為自害。然有情類，身、心相續，煩惱、業力輪迴諸趣，厭患苦故，求趣涅槃。

If there is really no self, who revolves through the realms of birth-and-death, and who grows weary of suffering and seeks nirvāṇa? — The self you cling to, being free of arising and ceasing, could hardly be said to revolve through birth-and-death at all; being constant, like space, and untroubled by suffering, why would it ever need to grow weary and seek nirvāṇa? What you say only ever undercuts your own position. It is, rather, that sentient beings, through the continuity of body and mind, revolve through the realms of birth-and-death by the power of afflictions and karma, and, growing weary of suffering, seek nirvāṇa.

由此故知定無實我，但有諸識無始時來前滅後生，因果相續，由妄熏習，似我相現，愚者於中妄執為我。

From all this, it can be known for certain that there is no real self. There are only the various consciousnesses which, from beginningless time, cease and arise in succession, continuing on as cause and effect; and, through deluded habituation, they give rise to an appearance resembling a self, which the unenlightened mistakenly cling to as self.

Reading note: Every one of these objections has the same shape: it assumes that only a fixed, unchanging self could explain continuity — memory linking past to present, an agent linking action to its result, a traveler linking one life to the next. The treatise's answer is always the same move: continuity does not need to be carried by something changeless. A stream of consciousness that "ceases and arises in succession" while "continuing on as cause and effect" can do everything a self was supposed to do — hold memory, bear karma, travel through rebirth, and eventually reach liberation — without there being anyone, fixed and unchanging, underneath it doing the holding.

This closes the treatise's refutation of a real self. With that finished, fascicle 1 turns next to refuting the second half of the opening verse's claim: that dharmas, too, are only provisionally spoken of — a much longer argument that works through the rival philosophical schools of the day one by one.

Against the Sāṃkhya School 依教廣成分 · 破法執（一）· 破數論

The refutation of self is finished. The treatise now turns to the second half of the opening verse's claim — that dharmas, too, are only provisionally spoken of — and works through the major philosophical schools of the day one at a time, starting with Sāṃkhya.

Chinese Source

如何識外實有諸法不可得耶？外道、餘乘所執外法，理非有故。外道所執云何非有？

Ru-Yi Translation

How can it be known that no dharmas really exist apart from consciousness? Because the external dharmas clung to by non-Buddhist schools, and by other Buddhist schools, do not, on examination, hold up. How, then, do the positions of the non-Buddhist schools fail to hold up?

且數論者執我是思，受用薩埵、剌闍、答摩所成大等二十三法，然大等法三事合成，是實非假，現量所得。

Consider first the Sāṃkhya school. They hold that the self (puruṣa) is pure consciousness, which experiences twenty-three principles — "the great" (mahat) and so on — composed out of three factors: sattva, rajas, and tamas. They hold that these principles, being formed by the combination of the three factors, are real, not merely provisional, and are directly perceived as such.

彼執非理，所以者何？大等諸法，多事成故，如軍、林等，應假非實，如何可說現量得耶？又大等法，若是實有，應如本事，非三合成。薩埵等三，即大等故，應如大等，亦三合成，轉變非常，為例亦爾。

This is unreasonable. Why? Since these principles are formed out of many factors, they should — like an army, or a forest, each made of many separate things — be merely provisional, not real. How, then, can they be said to be directly perceived as single, real things?

Further: if "the great" were truly real, it should be, like the three original factors themselves, not something composed of three parts. And if sattva and the other two just are "the great," then, like "the great," they too should be composed of three parts and subject to change rather than constant — the same problem simply recurs.

又三本事，各多功能，體亦應多，能、體一故。三體既遍，一處變時，餘亦應爾，體無別故。許此三事體、相各別，如何和合共成一相？不應合時變為一相，與未合時，體無別故。若謂三事體異相同，便違己宗體、相是一，體應如相，冥然是一，相應如體，顯然有三，故不應言三合成一。

Further: since each of the three original factors has many capacities, and capacity and substance are one and the same on your view, each should also have many substances. And since the substance of the three factors pervades everywhere, when one changes in one place, the others should change there too, since their substances are not distinct.

You grant that these three factors are distinct in both substance and characteristic — so how could combining them produce a single characteristic? It should not be that, once combined, they suddenly become a single characteristic, when their substance is no different from what it was before combining. And if you say the three are distinct in substance but identical in characteristic, that contradicts your own tenet that substance and characteristic are one and the same: substance should then be, like the characteristic, obscurely a single thing, or the characteristic should be, like the substance, evidently threefold. Either way, you cannot say the three combine into one.

又三是別，大等是總，總、別一故，應非一、三。此三變時，若不和合成一相者，應如未變，如何現見是一色等？若三和合成一相者，應失本別相，體亦應隨失。不可說三各有二相：一、總；二、別。總即別故，總亦應三，如何見一？若謂三體各有三相，和雜難知，故見一者，既有三相，寧見為一？復如何知三事有異？

Further: the three factors are particulars, and "the great" is their universal; and since universal and particular are, on your view, identical, "the great" should be neither simply one nor simply three. When the three change, if they do not combine into a single characteristic, things should remain as they were before changing — so how do we plainly observe a single, continuous form? But if they do combine into a single characteristic, they should lose their original distinct characteristics, and their very substance should be lost along with them.

Nor can you say each of the three has two aspects — a universal aspect and a particular aspect — for since universal and particular are identical on your view, the universal aspect too should be threefold, and then how could it appear as one? And if you say each of the three substances already has all three aspects, mixed together and hard to tell apart, and that this is why they appear as one — then, given that three aspects are genuinely present, how could they appear as one? And for that matter, how would you even know the three factors are distinct in the first place?

若彼一一皆具三相，應一一事能成色等，何所少，待三和合，體亦應各三，以體即相故，又大等法皆三合成，展轉相望，應無差別，是則因、果、唯量、諸大、諸根差別皆不得成。若爾，一根應得一切境，或應一境一切根所得，世間現見情與非情、淨穢等物，現、比量等，皆應無異，便為大失。故彼所執實法不成，但是妄情計度為有。

If each of the three already possesses all three aspects, then each one alone should be able to produce color and the rest — what would it still be missing, that it should need to wait for the three to combine? And each substance should likewise be threefold, since substance and aspect are identical on your view. Moreover, since every principle of "the great" and so on is alike composed of the same three factors, then compared with one another they should show no difference at all — and the distinctions between cause and effect, between sense-objects and their measures, between the elements, and between the sense-faculties could none of them be established.

If that were so, then a single sense-faculty should apprehend every object, or a single object should be apprehended by every sense-faculty — and the world's plain distinctions between the sentient and the insentient, the pure and the impure, and everything else, along with direct perception and inference themselves, should all collapse into no difference. This is a very great fault. The real dharmas the Sāṃkhya school clings to, then, are not established; they are merely constructed out of deluded feeling and supposed to exist.

Reading note: Sāṃkhya philosophy holds that the universe unfolds from a single root matter (prakṛti) made of three constituent strands (guṇas) — sattva, rajas, and tamas — which combine to produce "the great" (mahat, roughly: the faculty of discernment) and, from there, the rest of the twenty-three principles of the manifest world, all experienced by a separate, uninvolved witness-self (puruṣa). Their case rests on treating that combination as a genuinely single, directly perceived real thing, not just a convenient label for three things acting together.

The refutation is a purely internal one — it does not import Buddhist premises at all, only Sāṃkhya's own commitment that "substance and characteristic are one and the same" (體即相). Taken on its own terms, that commitment cannot explain how three distinct substances become one thing without either losing their distinctness or never really combining in the first place. The pattern — take an opponent's own axioms and show they collide with each other — is one the treatise will use again and again against the schools that follow.

Against the Vaiśeṣika School, Part One 依教廣成分 · 破法執（二）· 破勝論上

Vaiśeṣika philosophy sorts everything that exists into six fundamental categories (padārtha): substance (實), quality (德), action (業), universality (同), particularity (異), and inherence (和合). This is the longest single refutation in the fascicle, so we present it in two parts. Part one covers the opening dilemmas and the attack on treating the elements and their qualities as separate categories.

Chinese Source

勝論所執實等句義，多實有性，現量所得。彼執非理。所以者何？諸句義中且常住者，若能生果，應是無常，有作用故，如所生果；若不生果，應非離識實有自性，如兔角等。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Vaiśeṣika school holds that its categories — substance and the rest — mostly possess real existence, and are directly perceived as such. This is unreasonable. Why? Consider, among these categories, whatever is held to be permanent. If it is capable of producing an effect, it must be impermanent, since it then has a causal function — like the effect it produces. If it produces no effect, it can have no real nature apart from consciousness — like the horns of a rabbit.

諸無常者，若有質礙，便有方分，應可分析，如軍、林等，非實有性；若無質礙，如心、心所，應非離此有實自性。

Consider, among what is impermanent, whatever has material resistance. It must then have spatial parts, and so must be divisible — like an army, or a forest — and so has no real, independent existence. If it lacks material resistance, then, like mind and mental factors, it can have no real nature apart from these.

又彼所執地、水、火、風應非有礙，實句義攝，身根所觸故，如堅、濕、煖、動。即彼所執堅、濕、煖等，應非無礙，德句義攝，身根所觸故，如地、水、火、風，地、水、火三，對青色等，俱眼所見，准此應責。故知無實地、水、火、風與堅、濕等各別有性，亦非眼見實地、水、火。

Furthermore: on their own view, earth, water, fire, and wind — classified as substances — should have no material resistance, since substance and quality are held to be different categories, yet earth and the rest are perceived by the body's sense of touch, just as hardness, wetness, heat, and motion are. And by the same token, hardness, wetness, heat, and the rest — classified as qualities — should not lack material resistance either, since they too are perceived by the body's sense of touch, just as earth, water, fire, and wind are. Earth, water, and fire, moreover, are all seen by the eye together with their color — the same challenge applies here as well. From this it can be known that there is no real "earth," "water," "fire," or "wind" existing separately from "hardness," "wetness," and the rest; nor does the eye ever actually see a real earth, water, or fire apart from its qualities.

又彼所執實句義中，有礙、常者，皆有礙故，如麁地等，應是無常。諸句義中，色根所取，無質礙法，應皆有礙，許色根取故，如地、水、火、風。

Furthermore: within their category of substance, whatever has material resistance and is nonetheless held to be permanent must, simply because it has material resistance, be impermanent — like a coarse lump of earth. And among all their categories, whatever is grasped by a physical sense faculty but is said to lack material resistance ought instead to have material resistance, since it is granted to be grasped by a physical sense faculty — just as earth, water, fire, and wind are.

Reading note: The strategy here is to take a distinction the Vaiśeṣika school insists on — that "earth" as a substance and "hardness" as its quality belong to two fundamentally different kinds of category — and show that nothing in actual experience supports keeping them apart. Both are felt by touch; both, for earth, water, and fire, are seen by the eye together with color. If the only evidence for a substance and its quality is exactly the same act of perception, the argument asks, what independent reason is there to file them under two different ultimate categories rather than treating "quality" as just another name for how a single thing appears to the senses?

Against the Vaiśeṣika School, Part Two 依教廣成分 · 破法執（三）· 破勝論下

Part one attacked the substance/quality split for the elements. Part two goes after the more abstract categories that hold the whole system together: "Existence" (有性) as the highest universal, the lower universals that make a substance count as a substance, and "inherence" (和合) — the special relation the school needs to connect a thing to its own qualities.

Is "Existence" Itself a Real Thing? 破有性

Chinese Source

又彼所執非實德等，應非離識有別自性，非實攝故，如石女兒，非有實等，應非離識有別自性，非有攝故，如空花等。彼所執有，應離實等無別自性，許非無故，如實、德等。若離實等，應非有性，許異實等故，如畢竟無等。如有非無，無別有性，如何實等有別有性？若離有法，有別有性，應離無法，有別無性，彼既不然，此云何爾？故彼有性唯妄計度。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: whatever is not included among substance, quality, and the rest should have no separate real nature apart from consciousness, precisely because it is not so included — like the son of a barren woman. And whatever is not included under "the existent" should likewise have no separate real nature apart from consciousness, precisely because it is not so included — like a sky-flower.

Their category of "Existence" itself, then: if it is granted to be not nonexistent, it should have no nature separate from substance, quality, and the rest — just as substance and quality themselves need no further "existence" standing behind them. But if it is separate from substance and the rest, it should fail to be Existence at all, precisely because it is admitted to differ from them — like an absolute nonexistent, which is likewise different from them and equally fails to confer existence. Just as "what exists is not nonexistent" without needing some separate thing called "Existence" to make it so, how could substance and the rest need a separate "Existence" of their own? And if, apart from what exists, there must be a separate "Existence," then, apart from what does not exist, there ought equally to be a separate "Non-existence" — but no one holds that. So why hold this? Therefore their "Existence" is nothing but a mistaken construction.

The Same Problem, One Level Down 破同異性

又彼所執實、德、業性，異實、德、業，理定不然，勿此亦非實、德、業性，異實等故，如德、業等。又應實等非實等攝，異實等性故，如德、業、實等。地等諸性對地等體，更相徵詰，准此應知。如實性等，無別實等性，實等亦應無別實性等。若離實等有實等性，應離非實等，有非實等性。彼既不爾，此云何然？故同異性唯假施設。

The same dilemma recurs one level down. They also hold that "substance-ness," "quality-ness," and "action-ness" — the specific natures that make something count as a substance, a quality, or an action — are different from substance, quality, and action themselves. This too is unreasonable: on their own logic, being different from substance disqualifies something from being substance-ness, exactly as quality and action, being different from substance, are correctly excluded from being substance-ness — so substance-ness, being just as different from substance, should equally fail to be substance-ness. And, running the same test the other way, substance and the rest should then fail to be included under substance-ness at all, since they are equally different from it.

The identical cross-examination applies again between, say, the specific nature "earth-ness" and the substance earth itself, and so on for every category — by now the pattern should be clear. Substance-ness needs no further "substance-ness-ness" standing behind it any more than substance itself needed a separate "Existence"; and if a separate substance-ness were required for substance to be substance, then a separate "non-substance-ness" should equally be required for what is not substance — which no one holds. Therefore the categories of universality and particularity that generate claims like this are merely provisional constructions.

Reading note: Even by the standards of this fascicle, this passage compresses a great deal into very few characters — the treatise signals as much itself with phrases like "the pattern should be understood" (准此應知), trusting a trained reader to extend the argument rather than spelling out every step. The shape of it, though, is exactly the dilemma just used against "Existence," now aimed at every lower-level universal the Vaiśeṣika system needs (substance-ness, quality-ness, action-ness, and so on): either the universal is separate from the particulars it explains, in which case it cannot actually be what makes them what they are, or it isn't separate, in which case it was never a distinct real entity to begin with. Posit a separate universal to solve that problem and you only regenerate it one level up.

Inherence, and Why None of This Is Ever Directly Perceived 破和合句義

Chinese Source

又彼所執和合句義定非實有，非有、實等諸法攝故，如畢竟無。彼許實等現量所得，以理推徵尚非實有，況彼自許和合句義非現量得，而可實有？設執和合是現量境，由前理故，亦非實有。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: their category of "inherence" — the special relation that is supposed to bind a substance to its own qualities — is certainly not really existent, since it is not included among Existence, substance, and the rest, any more than an absolute nonexistent is. They themselves grant that substance and the other categories are directly perceived, and yet, examined by reason, even these turn out not to be really existent — how much less, then, could inherence, which they themselves admit is not even directly perceived? And even if one insists that inherence is directly perceived after all, it fails for exactly the same reasons already given.

然彼實等，非緣離識實有自體現量所得，許所知故，如龜毛等。又緣實智，非緣離識實句自體現量智攝，假合生故，如德智等。廣說乃至緣和合智，非緣離識和合自體現量智攝，假合生故，如實智等。故勝論者實等句義，亦是隨情妄所施設。

In fact, none of these — substance and the rest — are ever directly perceived as possessing a real essence apart from consciousness, since they are, by their own admission, merely objects known to the mind — like the proverbial hair of a tortoise. The cognition that takes substance as its object is not a direct perception of some real essence of "substance" existing apart from consciousness, because it arises only through a provisional coming-together of conditions — exactly like the cognition that takes quality as its object. And so on, up through the cognition that takes inherence as its object: it too is not a direct perception of some real essence of "inherence" apart from consciousness, since it likewise arises only through a provisional coming-together of conditions — exactly like the cognition that takes substance as its object.

Therefore the categories of the Vaiśeṣika school — substance and all the rest — are, in the end, nothing more than constructions set up in accordance with deluded feeling.

Reading note: The closing move steps back from category-by-category attacks to a single, simpler observation: pick any of the six categories, and the mental act of cognizing it is itself a conditioned, constructed event — "arising through a provisional coming-together of conditions" (假合生) — never a bare, unmediated contact with some mind-independent essence. If even the school's own best case, substance, fails this test, nothing built on top of it (quality, inherence, and the rest) can pass it either. This finishes the treatise's refutation of Vaiśeṣika.

Against a Creator God, Eternal Sound, and Eternal Atoms 依教廣成分 · 破法執（四）

Three shorter arguments follow before the treatise takes on the largest remaining target — the realist schools within Buddhism itself. Each of these three positions was live in classical Indian thought: a single all-powerful creator god, the eternity of Vedic sound, and eternal, indivisible atoms of matter.

A Single, Eternal Creator God 破大自在天等

Chinese Source

有執有一大自在天，體實、遍、常，能生諸法。彼執非理，所以者何？若法能生，必非常故；諸非常者，必不遍故；諸不遍者，非真實故。體既常、遍，具諸功能，應一切處、時，頓生一切法。待欲或緣方能生者，違一因論。或欲及緣亦應頓起，因常有故。餘執有一大梵、時、方、本際、自然、虛空、我等，常住、實有，具諸功能，生一切法，皆同此破。

Ru-Yi Translation

Some hold that there is a single great sovereign god (Maheśvara), whose substance is real, all-pervading, and eternal, and who generates all dharmas. This is unreasonable. Why? Whatever is capable of generating something cannot be eternal; whatever is not eternal cannot be all-pervading; and whatever is not all-pervading cannot be truly real in the way claimed.

If this god's substance really is eternal and all-pervading, possessing every capacity, then it should generate all dharmas everywhere and at all times, all at once. If it can generate things only when it depends on some desire or condition, this contradicts the claim that it is a single, sufficient cause. And if the desire and the conditions must themselves also arise all at once — since, on this view, the cause is eternally present — the same problem simply recurs.

Others hold that there is a single Brahmā, or time, or space, or a primordial origin, or nature, or the void, or a cosmic self, which is eternal, really existent, possesses every capacity, and generates all dharmas — every one of these is refuted in exactly the same way.

Eternal, Meaning-Bearing Sound 破聲常論

有餘偏執，明論聲常，能為定量，表詮諸法；有執一切聲皆是常，待緣顯發，方有詮表。彼俱非理，所以者何？且明論聲，許能詮故，應非常住，如所餘聲。餘聲亦應非常聲體，如瓶、衣等，待眾緣故。

Some hold, more specifically, that the sound of the Vedic scriptures is eternal, and can serve as a definitive means of valid knowledge, expressing and explaining all things; others hold that all sound whatsoever is eternal, and only becomes manifest — and so able to express meaning — when the right conditions bring it out. Both are unreasonable. Why? Consider the sound of the Vedic scriptures first: since it is granted to be able to express meaning, it cannot be eternal, any more than any other sound can. And the substance of sound in general likewise cannot be eternal, since, like a pot or a piece of cloth, it depends on many conditions to come into being.

Eternal, Partless Atoms 破極微實有論

Chinese Source

有外道執地、水、火、風、極微實、常，能生麁色，所生麁色不越因量，雖是無常，而體實有。彼亦非理，所以者何？所執極微，若有方分，如蟻行等，體應非實。若無方分，如心、心所應不共聚生麁果色，既能生果，如彼所生，如何可說極微常住？

Ru-Yi Translation

Some non-Buddhist schools hold that atoms of earth, water, fire, and wind are real and eternal, and capable of generating gross, visible matter; the gross matter so generated does not exceed the measure of its causes, and, though impermanent itself, has a substance that is really existent. This too is unreasonable. Why? If the atoms they posit have spatial parts, then, like a line of marching ants, their substance cannot be truly single and real. If they have no spatial parts, then, like mind and mental factors, they should be incapable of clustering together to produce gross matter as an effect. And since they clearly are capable of producing an effect, then — being, like what they produce, subject to a causal process — how can atoms still be called eternal?

又所生果不越因量，應如極微，不名麁色，則此果色應非眼等色根所取，便違自執。若謂果色，量德合故，非麁似麁，色根能取。所執果色，既同因量，應如極微，無麁德合；或應極微亦麁德合；如麁果色，處無別故。

Furthermore: if the effect produced does not exceed the measure of its cause, it should be, like the atom itself, not describable as "gross" at all — and then this resulting matter should not be graspable by the eye or the other physical sense faculties, which contradicts their own position. If they answer that the resulting matter, through a combination of measure and quality, is not truly gross but only appears gross, and so is graspable by the senses — then, since the resulting matter is of the same measure as its cause, it should, like the atom, lack any such combination; or else the atom itself should equally possess it, since, on their view, it occupies the very same place as the gross matter it produces.

若謂果色遍在自因，因非一故，可名麁者，則此果色體應非一，如所在因，處各別故。既爾，此果還不成麁，由此亦非色根所取。若果多分合故成麁，多因極微合應非細，足成根境，何用果為？既多分成，應非實有，則汝所執，前後相違。

If they answer that the resulting matter is spread across all its causal atoms, and, since the causes are not a single thing, it may still be called "gross" — then the substance of the resulting matter should likewise not be a single thing, since the causes it resides in occupy separate places; and in that case it still fails to be gross, and so still cannot be grasped by the senses. If they answer that it becomes gross simply by being composed of many parts — then the many atomic causes, once combined, should already be gross enough to serve as a sense-object by themselves; what further need is there for a separate "result"? And being composed of many parts, the result should not be really existent after all — which contradicts what they claimed at the start.

又果與因俱有質礙，應不同處，如二極微。若謂果、因體相受入，如沙受水，藥入鎔銅。誰許沙、銅體受水、藥？或應離、變，非一、非常，又麁色果體若是一，得一分時應得一切，彼、此一故，彼應如此，不許違理，許便違事，故彼所執進、退不成，但是隨情虛妄計度。

Furthermore: since both the resulting matter and its causal atoms have material resistance, they should not occupy the same place, any more than two atoms can. If they answer that the substance of the result and of the cause interpenetrate each other, as sand takes in water, or a drug dissolves into molten copper — but who would really grant that the very substance of sand or copper takes in water or a drug, rather than the water merely filling the gaps between grains? Either the result must be separate from its cause, or a transformation of it — and either way, it cannot be both one with the cause and eternal.

Finally: if the substance of the resulting gross matter is a single unity, then grasping one part of it should mean grasping the whole, since the part and the whole would then be one and the same. To deny this contradicts their own logic; to grant it contradicts plain fact. Whichever way they turn, then, their position cannot be established — it is nothing but a mistaken construction, following mere feeling.

Reading note: The creator-god argument turns the god's own perfections against it: an all-pervading, eternal cause with every capacity should produce everything, everywhere, always — reality plainly isn't like that, so either the god isn't really all-pervading and eternal, or it needs help from conditions after all, which defeats the point of positing a single sufficient cause in the first place. The atomism argument is longer because it is answering a genuinely reasonable-sounding picture — solid matter built up from tiny, real, indivisible building blocks — and has to show, piece by piece, that "gross matter" can never be made to add up consistently out of atoms that are supposed to have no parts, no place, and no change. Notice that this argument does not depend on anything specifically Buddhist; it is pure mereology, the logic of parts and wholes, and it will resurface again later when the treatise turns to the atomism of realist Buddhist schools.

Four Ways to Get It Wrong, and No Fifth 依教廣成分 · 破法執（五）

Before moving on from non-Buddhist schools entirely, the treatise steps back and closes the case with a single, exhaustive argument. Whatever a school believes about the relationship between any particular thing and its "Existence," there are only ever four logically possible positions — and all four fail.

Chinese Source

然諸外道品類雖多，所執有法不過四種：一、執有法與有等性其體定一，如數論等。彼執非理，所以者何？勿一切法即有性故，皆如有性，體無差別，便違三德、我等體異，亦違世間諸法差別。又若色等即色等性，色等應無青、黃等異。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although non-Buddhist schools come in many varieties, what they hold about the relation between a particular existent and its universal "Existence" reduces to only four positions.

First: some hold that a particular existent and "Existence" are definitely identical in substance — the Sāṃkhya school, among others. This is unreasonable. Why? If every dharma simply were identical with "Existence," then all dharmas, being alike identical with it, should show no difference in substance from one another — which contradicts Sāṃkhya's own claim that the three guṇas and the self differ in substance, and also contradicts what the world plainly observes: that things differ from one another. Furthermore, if form were identical with "form-ness," form itself should show no difference between, say, blue and yellow.

二、執有法與有等性其體定異，如勝論等。彼執非理，所以者何？勿一切法非有性故，如已滅無，體不可得，便違實等自體非無，亦違世間現見有物。又若色等非色等性，應如聲等非眼等境。

Second: some hold that a particular existent and "Existence" are definitely different in substance — the Vaiśeṣika school, among others. This is unreasonable. Why? If this were so, no dharma would actually possess Existence at all, so that, like something already vanished into nonexistence, its substance could never be found — which contradicts their own claim that the substance of substance and the rest is not nonexistent, and also contradicts what the world plainly observes: that things do exist. Furthermore, if form were different from "form-ness," form should be, like sound, something the eye cannot perceive.

三、執有法與有等性亦一、亦異，如無慚等，彼執非理，所以者何？一、異同前一、異過故；二相相違，體應別故，一、異體同俱不成故。勿一切法皆同一體，或應一、異是假非實，而執為實理定不成。

Third: some hold that a particular existent and "Existence" are both identical and different. This is unreasonable. Why? The "identical" half faces every fault just given against identity, and the "different" half faces every fault just given against difference. Moreover, since being identical and being different are mutually contradictory, their substances would have to be distinct — so a single thing being, in one substance, both identical and different at once cannot be established. And if they retreat to saying not every dharma need share a single substance in this way, then "identical" and "different" become merely provisional labels, not real — so treating them as real is, either way, untenable.

四、執有法與有等性非一、非異，如邪命等。彼執非理，所以者何？非一、異執同異、一故。非一、異言為遮為表？若唯是表，應不雙非；若但是遮，應無所執；亦遮、亦表應互相違；非表、非遮應成戲論。又非一、異，違世共知有一、異物，亦違自宗色等有法決定實有。是故彼言唯矯避過，諸有智者勿謬許之。

Fourth: some hold that a particular existent and "Existence" are neither identical nor different — the Ājīvika school, among others. This is unreasonable. Why? "Neither identical nor different" faces the same difficulty as "both identical and different," since it too presupposes a definite relation between identity and difference. And does the phrase "neither identical nor different" function as a denial, or as an assertion? If purely an assertion, it should not be phrased as a double negation; if purely a denial, then nothing is actually being claimed; if both denial and assertion at once, the two sides contradict each other; if neither assertion nor denial, it collapses into empty chatter. Moreover, "neither identical nor different" contradicts what the world plainly knows — that any two things are either identical or different — and it also contradicts their own school's insistence that form and the other existents are definitely, really existent. This claim, then, is nothing but an evasive dodge meant to escape every fault already raised — the wise should not be misled into accepting it.

Reading note: This is a classic four-cornered analysis (a pattern often called the tetralemma in Buddhist logic): identical, different, both, neither — exhausting every logically possible relationship between a thing and a universal supposed to explain why it exists. The first two positions are the very schools already refuted at length above (Sāṃkhya and Vaiśeṣika), now shown to be two horns of one dilemma rather than two unrelated theories. The fourth is traditionally read as targeting the Ājīvikas, a fatalist school notorious in Buddhist literature for evasive, "eel-wriggling" answers — which is exactly the treatise's complaint about "neither identical nor different": it sounds like a sophisticated middle position but, on inspection, asserts nothing at all.

This closes the refutation of non-Buddhist schools (外道) entirely. Fascicle 1 has one major target left: schools within Buddhism itself that, unlike Consciousness-Only, hold that matter and certain abstract structural dharmas are real, mind-independent things.

Against Realist Buddhist Schools: Are Atoms Real? 依教廣成分 · 破法執（六）· 破餘乘（一）

Non-Buddhist schools are finished. The treatise now turns to its last and, in a sense, closest target: schools within Buddhism itself — chiefly the Sarvāstivāda — that hold matter and certain abstract structural dharmas to be real and mind-independent, even while accepting core Buddhist teachings like no-self. The final stretch of fascicle 1 takes this apart piece by piece, beginning with atoms.

Chinese Source

餘乘所執，離識實有色等諸法，如何非有？彼所執色、不相應行及諸無為，理非有故。且所執色總有二種：一者有對，極微所成；二者無對，非極微成。彼有對色定非實有，能成極微非實有故。謂諸極微，若有質礙，應如瓶等，是假非實。若無質礙，應如非色，如何可集成瓶、衣等？

Ru-Yi Translation

How is it that the form and other dharmas which other Buddhist schools hold to really exist apart from consciousness are not, in fact, real? Because what they hold to be real — form, the forces disjoined from mind, and the unconditioned dharmas — do not hold up on examination. The form they posit is, broadly, of two kinds: resistant form, composed of atoms, and non-resistant form, not composed of atoms.

Resistant form is certainly not really existent, because the atoms supposed to compose it are not really existent either. If atoms have material resistance, they should, like a pot, be merely provisional, not real — since a pot, too, is a composite. If they lack material resistance, they should be, like non-material things, incapable of combining into a pot or a piece of cloth.

又諸極微，若有方分，必可分析，便非實有。若無方分，則如非色，云何和合承光、發影？日輪纔舉照柱等時，東、西兩邊光影各現，承光、發影處既不同，所執極微定有方分。又，若見、觸壁等物時，唯得此邊不得彼分。既和合物即諸極微，故此極微必有方分。

Furthermore: if atoms have spatial parts, they must be divisible, and so cannot be really existent. If they have no spatial parts, then, like non-material things, how could they combine to catch light and cast shadow? The moment the sun rises and shines on a pillar, light appears on the east side and shadow on the west; since the side that catches light and the side that casts shadow are not the same, the atoms they posit must have spatial parts. Furthermore: when one sees or touches a wall, one apprehends only this side, not that other part. Since a combined object just is its atoms, those atoms must have spatial parts.

又諸極微隨所住處，必有上、下、四方差別，不爾便無共和、集義。或相涉入應不成麁，由此極微定有方分。執有對色即諸極微，若無方分，應無障隔。若爾，便非障礙有對。是故汝等所執極微必有方分，有方分故，便可分析，定非實有。故有對色實有不成。

Furthermore: wherever an atom is located, it must have an upper side, a lower side, and the four directions distinguished — otherwise there would be no way for atoms to combine and cluster together at all; lacking such distinctions, they would simply merge into and interpenetrate one another, and could never form anything gross. For this reason, atoms must have spatial parts. And since resistant form is held to just be these atoms, if the atoms had no spatial parts, there would be no obstruction between them at all — but then they would not really be "resistant" in the first place.

Therefore, the atoms you posit must have spatial parts; and having spatial parts, they must be divisible, and so cannot be really existent. Resistant form, then, is not established as really existent.

Reading note: This is one of the most famous arguments in Buddhist philosophy of mind, and it is a genuine dilemma, not a rhetorical flourish: an atom is supposed to be the final, indivisible unit of matter, with no smaller parts — but anything with an "east side" and a "west side," a surface that catches light and a surface that stays in shadow, already has parts, and so is not truly indivisible. Strip away all spatial parts to get a genuinely partless unit, and you lose the very features — extension, a facing side, the capacity to block light or touch — that let atoms combine into anything solid in the first place. Later in this section, the treatise makes its own, very different use of the word "atom" as a conceptual limit rather than a real building block — worth watching for.
