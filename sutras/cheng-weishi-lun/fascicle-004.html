Fascicle 4 · 成唯識論卷第四

The Four Foods, and the Deepest Absorption

Fascicle 4 opens by finishing what fascicle 3 began: the last three of the ten purely rational arguments for the ālayavijñāna's existence — from the four kinds of food that sustain a living being, through the treatise's most sustained and technical argument (that this consciousness alone explains why "cessation-attainment" is not literal unconsciousness), to the final argument from moral cause-and-effect. The treatise then closes this entire line of proof with its own summary judgment.

The Argument From the Four Foods 依教廣成分 · 證有此識 · 正理（八）· 四食

Fascicle 3 closed with the seventh of ten purely rational arguments for the ālayavijñāna's existence. The eighth turns to a basic sūtra teaching: that every sentient being depends on food to stay alive.

Four Kinds of Food 段食 · 觸食 · 意思食 · 識食

Chinese Source

又契經說：一切有情皆依食住，若無此識，彼識食體不應有故。謂契經說食有四種：一者、段食，變壞為相。謂欲界繫香、味、觸三，於變壞時能為食事。由此色處非段食攝，以變壞時，色無用故。二者、觸食，觸境為相，謂有漏觸纔取境時，攝受喜等，能為食事。此觸雖與諸識相應，屬六識者食義偏勝，觸麁顯境，攝受喜、樂及順益捨，資養勝故。三、意思食，希望為相。謂有漏思與欲俱轉，希可愛境，能為食事。此思雖與諸識相應，屬意識者食義偏勝，意識於境，希望勝故。四者、識食，執持為相。謂有漏識由段、觸、思勢力增長，能為食事。此識雖通諸識自體，而第八識食義偏勝，一類相續執持勝故。由是《集論》說此四食，三蘊、五處、十一界攝。此四能持有情身命，令不壞斷，故名為食。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the sūtras speak of all sentient beings as sustained by food. Without this consciousness, the very substance of that "consciousness food" could not exist. The sūtras speak of four kinds of food:

First, edible food, characterized by decomposing. This refers to the desire realm's scent, taste, and touch, which serve as food precisely as they decompose; visible form is not included among edible food, since form serves no purpose in the process of decomposing.

Second, contact food, characterized by contact with an object. This refers to contaminated contact, which, the very moment it takes hold of an object, gathers in delight and the like, and so serves as food. Although this contact is associated with all the consciousnesses, its function as food is especially pronounced when it belongs to the six sense-consciousnesses, since their contact grasps its object coarsely and vividly, gathering in joy, pleasure, and beneficial equanimity, and so nourishes especially well.

Third, mental-volition food, characterized by anticipation. This refers to contaminated volition, which arises together with desire, anticipating some agreeable object, and so serves as food. Although this volition is associated with all the consciousnesses, its function as food is especially pronounced when it belongs to the mental consciousness, since the mental consciousness anticipates its object especially strongly.

Fourth, consciousness food, characterized by grasping and holding. This refers to contaminated consciousness which, through the accumulated power of edible, contact, and volitional food, serves as food. Although this consciousness is common to the essential nature of all the consciousnesses, its function as food is especially pronounced in the eighth consciousness, since it alone grasps and holds in a single, continuous kind especially well.

Hence the Abhidharmasamuccaya says these four foods are included within three aggregates, five sense-fields, and eleven elements. These four sustain a sentient being's body and life, keeping them from decaying and breaking off — hence they are called "food."

段食唯於欲界有用，觸、意思食雖遍三界而依識轉，隨識有無。眼等轉識有間、有轉，非遍、恒時能持身命，謂無心定、睡眠、悶絕、無想天中有間斷故，設有心位，隨所依、緣、性、界、地等有轉易故，於持身命，非遍、非恒。

Edible food is useful only in the desire realm. Contact food and mental-volition food, though pervading all three realms, operate in dependence on consciousness, following whatever consciousness is present or absent. The eye-consciousness and the other evolving consciousnesses are interrupted and keep changing, and so cannot sustain body and life universally and constantly: they are interrupted in mindless meditative absorption, in sleep, in unconsciousness, and in the heaven of no-thought; and even in states where mind is present, they keep changing according to their supporting basis, condition, moral nature, realm, and stage — so, in sustaining body and life, they are neither universal nor constant.

諸有執無第八識者，依何等食？《經》作是言：「一切有情皆依食住。」非無心位過去、未來識等為食；彼非現、常；如空花等，無體用故。設有體用非現在攝，如虛空等，非食性故，亦不可說入定心等與無心位有情為食，住無心時彼已滅故，過去非食已極成故。又不可說無想定等不相應行即為彼食，段等四食所不攝故，不相應法非實有故。有執滅定等猶有第六識，於彼有情能為食事。彼執非理，後當廣破。

For those who hold there is no eighth consciousness — what, then, serves as food during a mindless state? The sūtra says: "All sentient beings are sustained by food." It cannot be past or future consciousness that serves as food during a mindless state, since these are neither present nor constant, being, like a flower blooming in empty space, without real substance or function. And even granting them some substance or function, if that is not included in the present moment, they would be like empty space, not the kind of thing food can be. Nor can one say that the mind which entered absorption serves as food for the being now in a mindless state, since, once abiding in that mindless state, that prior mind has already ceased — and it is already well established that something past cannot serve as food. Nor can one say that the forces disjoined from mind, such as the attainment of no-thought, themselves serve as that food, since they are not included among the four foods already listed, and disjoined forces have no real substance to begin with. Some hold that even in cessation-attainment and the like, a sixth consciousness remains present, capable of serving as food for that being — this view is unreasonable, and will be refuted at length below.

又彼應說生上二界無漏心時，以何為食？無漏識等破壞有故，於彼身、命不可為食。亦不可執無漏識中有有漏種能為彼食，無漏識等猶如涅槃，不能執持有漏種故。復不可說上界有情身、命相持即互為食，四食不攝彼身、命故，又無色無身，命無能持故，眾同分等無實體故。由此定知異諸轉識，有異熟識一類恒、遍執持身、命，令不壞斷。世尊依此，故作是言：一切有情皆依食住。

Furthermore, they ought to explain: when a being born in the upper two realms gives rise to an uncontaminated mind, what then serves as food? An uncontaminated consciousness works to break down contaminated existence, and so cannot serve as food for that being's body and life. Nor can one hold that an uncontaminated consciousness contains contaminated seeds capable of serving as its food, since an uncontaminated consciousness, being like nirvāṇa itself, cannot hold contaminated seeds at all. Nor, again, can one say that in the upper realms body and life simply sustain each other mutually as food, since body and life are not themselves included among the four foods; and in the formless realm there is no body at all, so life would have nothing there to sustain it — and a "shared class of being," as an abstract designation, has no real substance either. By this it can be known with certainty that, distinct from the evolving consciousnesses, there is a ripening consciousness — single in kind, constant, and all-pervading — which grasps and holds body and life, keeping them from decaying and breaking off. It is on this basis that the World-Honored One made the statement: all sentient beings are sustained by food.

唯依「取蘊」建立有情，佛無有漏，非有情攝，說為有情依食住者，當知皆依示現而說。既異熟識是勝食性，彼識即是此第八識。

"Sentient being" is established only with reference to the grasped aggregates — a Buddha, having nothing contaminated, is not included among sentient beings; wherever it is said that a Buddha, too, is sustained by food, this should be understood as spoken only for the sake of a provisional appearance. Since it is the ripening consciousness, then, that has the pre-eminent nature of food, that consciousness is precisely this eighth consciousness.

Reading note: "Food" here means something much broader than what we eat — the treatise defines it as whatever actually keeps a body and a life from falling apart, moment to moment. Edible food is the obvious case; but contact, anticipation, and the grasping-and-holding of consciousness itself are all, on this analysis, equally food, because they equally nourish continued existence. The argument's real target, though, is narrower and sharper: whatever "consciousness food" is, it has to be present at literally every moment a being is alive, including the moments — dreamless sleep, deep absorption, the heaven of no-thought — where the six ordinary consciousnesses have gone dark. Something has to still be quietly "eating" in those gaps, or a being would starve out of existence every time it fell asleep.

The formless-realm case, again, is where the argument bites hardest: a being there has an uncontaminated mind and no body at all in the ordinary sense, and the treatise walks through every candidate — past consciousness, disjoined forces, mutual body-life support — showing none of them can coherently serve as food. Only a single, constant, all-pervading ripening consciousness threads every one of these needles at once.

The Argument From Cessation-Attainment 依教廣成分 · 證有此識 · 正理（九）· 滅定

The ninth argument is the treatise's longest and most technical: a sustained debate over what "cessation-attainment" (nirodha-samāpatti) — a meditative state in which even feeling and perception cease — actually eliminates, and what, if anything, must remain.

A Body Without a Departed Consciousness 滅盡定 · 識不離身

Chinese Source

又契經說：住滅定者，身、語、心行無不皆滅，而壽不滅，亦不離煖，根無變壞，識不離身。若無此識住滅定者，不離身識不應有故。謂眼等識行相麁、動，於所緣境起必勞慮，厭患彼故，暫求止息，漸次伏除至都盡位，依此位立住滅定者，故此定中彼識皆滅，若不許有微細一類恒、遍執持壽等識在，依何而說識不離身？

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the sūtras speak of one abiding in cessation-attainment: physical, verbal, and mental activity have all, without exception, ceased, yet life does not cease, warmth does not depart, the faculties do not decay, and consciousness does not leave the body. Without this consciousness, there could be no consciousness that "does not leave the body" for one abiding in cessation-attainment.

The eye-consciousness and the rest have a coarse, agitated mode of activity; in taking hold of their objects they inevitably give rise to weariness, and, growing averse to this, one seeks a temporary respite, gradually suppressing and eliminating them until they are entirely exhausted — it is with reference to this state that one is said to abide in cessation-attainment. In this absorption, then, these consciousnesses have all ceased entirely. If one does not accept that some subtle, single-kind, constant, all-pervading consciousness remains, grasping and holding life and the rest, on what basis could it possibly be said that "consciousness does not leave the body"?

若謂後時彼識還起，如隔日瘧名不離身，是則不應說心行滅，識與想等起滅同故。壽、煖諸根應亦如識，便成大過，故應許識如壽、煖等實不離身。又此位中若全無識，應如瓦礫非有情數，豈得說為住滅定者？又異熟識此位若無，誰能執持諸根、壽、煖？無執持故皆應壞滅，猶如死屍便無壽等。既爾，後識必不還生，說不離身，彼何所屬？諸異熟識捨此身已，離識餘身無重生故。

Should one claim that consciousness merely arises again later, the way a recurring fever is still called "not departed" — then it should not be said that mental activity has ceased at all, since consciousness arising and ceasing would work exactly the same way as perception and the rest, which the sūtra explicitly says has ceased. Life, warmth, and the faculties would, on this same logic, have to work just like consciousness too — which is a grave error. One should, instead, accept that consciousness genuinely does not leave the body, in just the same way that life and warmth do not.

Furthermore, if consciousness were entirely absent in this state, the being would be no different from a tile or a stone, no longer counted among sentient beings at all — how, then, could it be said to be "abiding in cessation-attainment"? And if the ripening consciousness, too, were absent in this state, what would grasp and hold the faculties, life, and warmth? Lacking anything to grasp and hold them, they would all decay and perish, just like a corpse, and life and the rest would then be gone entirely. If that were so, a later consciousness could certainly never arise again — and this "not leaving the body" that is spoken of would then belong to nothing at all, since once the ripening consciousness has abandoned this body, no other body can ever be taken up again apart from consciousness.

又若此位無持種識，後識無種，如何得生？過去、未來不相應法非實有體已極成故。諸色等法離識皆無受熏、持種亦已遮故。然滅定等無心位中，如有心位定實有識，具根、壽、煖，有情攝故。由斯理趣，住滅定者，決定有識，實不離身。

Furthermore, if there were no seed-holding consciousness in this state, a later consciousness would have no seeds to draw on — how could it possibly arise? It is already well established that past and future disjoined dharmas have no real substance; and it has already been shown that form and the other such dharmas, apart from consciousness, can neither be perfumed nor hold seeds. In cessation-attainment and the other mindless states, then, just as in states where mind is present, there must certainly be a real consciousness present, complete with faculties, life, and warmth, since the being is still counted among sentient beings. By this line of reasoning, one abiding in cessation-attainment certainly has consciousness, which genuinely does not leave the body.

Ruling Out a Sixth Consciousness 滅盡定 · 非第六識

Chinese Source

若謂此位有第六識，名不離身，亦不應理，此定亦名無心定故。若無五識名無心者，應一切定皆名無心，諸定皆無五識身故。意識攝在六轉識中，如五識身，滅定非有。或此位識行相、所緣不可知故，如壽、煖等，非第六識。若此位有行相、所緣可知識者，應如餘位，非此位攝。本為止息行相、所緣可了知識，入此定故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should one claim that a sixth consciousness is present in this state, and that this is what "does not leave the body" means — this, too, is unreasonable, since this very absorption is also called "the mindless absorption." If the mere absence of the five sense-consciousnesses were enough to call a state "mindless," then every meditative absorption would have to be called mindless, since the five sense-consciousnesses are absent in all of them. But the mental consciousness, too, is included among the six evolving consciousnesses — so, just like the five sense-consciousnesses, it too is simply absent in cessation-attainment.

Or again: the consciousness present in this state has a mode of activity and cognitive object that cannot be discerned at all, just like life and warmth — and so it cannot be the sixth consciousness. If a consciousness with a discernible mode of activity and object were present in this state, it would have to belong to some other, ordinary state, not to this one — for it is precisely in order to bring a discernible, active consciousness to rest that one enters this absorption in the first place.

又若此位有第六識，彼心所法為有？為無？若有心所，經不應言住此定者心行皆滅，又不應名滅受想定。此定加行，但厭受、想，故此定中唯受、想滅；受、想二法資助心強，諸心所中獨名心行，說心行滅，何所相違？

Furthermore, if a sixth consciousness were present in this state, would its accompanying mental factors be present, or not? If mental factors were present, the sūtra should not say that, for one abiding in this absorption, mental activity has ceased entirely — nor should this absorption be called "the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception."

Should one reply: the preliminary practice for this absorption is simply growing weary of feeling and perception specifically, so within this absorption only feeling and perception cease; and since feeling and perception are the two factors that most strongly assist the mind, they alone, among all the mental factors, are singled out as "mental activity" — so what contradiction is there in saying that mental activity has ceased, while other, unnamed mental factors remain?

無想定中應唯想滅，但厭想故，然汝不許，既唯受、想資助心強，此二滅時，心亦應滅。如身行滅而身猶在，寧要責心令同行滅？若爾，語行尋、伺滅時，語應不滅，而非所許。然行於法有遍、非遍，遍行滅時，法定隨滅；非遍行滅，法或猶在。非遍行者謂入出息，見息滅時，身猶在故；尋、伺於語是遍行攝，彼若滅時，語定無故。受、想於心亦遍行攝，許如思等大地法故。受、想滅時，心定隨滅，如何可說彼滅心在？

By that same logic, in the attainment of no-thought, only perception ought to cease, since its preliminary practice is simply growing weary of perception specifically — yet you yourselves do not accept this. And since it is granted that feeling and perception are the two factors that most strongly assist the mind, when these two cease, the mind itself should cease along with them.

One might object: just as physical activity — breathing — can cease while the body itself still remains, why must the mind be held to cease along with the very factors said to be its "activity"? But then, when verbal activity — coarse and subtle discursive thought — ceases, speech itself should likewise not cease, which you do not accept either. The truth is that "activity" stands to its associated dharma in two different ways, universal and non-universal: when a universal activity ceases, its dharma certainly ceases along with it; when a non-universal activity ceases, its dharma may still remain. In-breath and out-breath are non-universal to the body — the body plainly still remains even once the breath has visibly ceased. Coarse and subtle discursive thought, however, is universal to speech — once it ceases, speech is certainly altogether absent. Feeling and perception, likewise, are universal to mind, since they are admitted, like volition, to be among the mental factors present in every mental state whatsoever. When feeling and perception cease, then, the mind certainly ceases along with them — how, then, could one say that the mind remains once they have ceased?

又許思等是大地法，滅受、想時，彼亦應滅。既爾，信等此位亦無，非遍行滅餘可在故，如何可言有餘心所？既許思等此位非無，受、想應然，大地法故。又此定中若有思等亦應有觸，餘心所法無不皆依觸力生故。若許有觸亦應有受，觸緣受故。既許有受，想亦應生，不相離故。如受緣愛，非一切受皆能起愛，故觸緣受，非一切觸皆能生受，由斯所難，其理不成。彼救不然，有差別故。謂佛自簡唯無明觸所生諸受為緣生愛，曾無有處簡觸生受。故若有觸，必有受生，受與想俱，其理決定。或應如餘位，受、想亦不滅，執此位中有思等故，許便違害心行滅言，亦不得成滅受想定。

Furthermore, it is granted that volition and the rest are among the mental factors present in every mental state whatsoever — so when feeling and perception cease, these too should cease. And if that is so, then faith and the other wholesome factors would likewise be absent in this state, since it cannot be that a universal factor ceases while some other factor remains — so how could one say that any mental factor at all remains? Yet if it is granted that volition and the rest are not, in fact, absent in this state, then feeling and perception should be present too, being equally factors present in every mental state.

Furthermore, if volition and the rest were present in this absorption, contact, too, should be present, since there is no mental factor whatsoever that does not arise through the power of contact. And if contact is granted to be present, feeling, too, should be present, since contact conditions feeling. And once feeling is granted, perception, too, must arise, since the two are never separated from one another.

One might object: just as feeling conditions craving, yet not every feeling actually gives rise to craving, so too contact conditions feeling, yet not every contact actually gives rise to feeling — by this reasoning, the argument above does not hold. But this defense does not work, since the two cases are not parallel: the Buddha himself specifically singled out only feeling arisen from the contact of ignorance as the condition for craving; he never, anywhere, singled out only some contacts as conditions for feeling. Hence wherever contact is present, feeling must certainly arise, and feeling and perception occur together — this reasoning is settled.

Or else, one should hold that, just as in every other state, feeling and perception do not cease in this state either — but to hold that volition and the rest are present in this state is already to violate the sūtra's own statement that mental activity has ceased entirely, and this could then no longer be called "the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception" at all.

若無心所，識亦應無，不見餘心離心所故，餘遍行滅，法隨滅故，受等應非大地法故，此識應非相應法故。許則應無所依、緣等，如色等法亦非心故。又契經說：意、法為緣，生於意識，三和合觸，與觸俱起，有受、想、思。若此定中有意識者，三和合故，必應有觸，觸既定與受、想、思俱，如何有識而無心所？

If, on the other hand, no mental factors were present, then consciousness itself should be absent too, since no other mental consciousness is ever seen apart from its mental factors; since, when a universal factor ceases, its dharma certainly ceases along with it; since feeling and the rest would then not be universal factors after all; and since this consciousness would then not be a genuinely associated dharma at all. To grant this would leave it with nothing to depend on and no condition to rely on, no different from form and the other dharmas that are not mind at all.

Furthermore, the sūtras say: mind and dharma serve as conditions, giving rise to mental consciousness; the three coming together is contact, and together with contact arise feeling, perception, and volition. If a mental consciousness were present in this absorption, then, given the coming together of the three, contact must certainly be present — and once contact is certainly present together with feeling, perception, and volition, how could there be a consciousness present with no mental factors at all?

若謂餘時，三和有力，成觸、生觸，能起受等。由此定前厭患心所，故在定位三事無能，不成生觸，亦無受等。若爾，應名滅心所定，如何但說滅受、想耶？若謂厭時唯厭受、想，此二滅故，心所皆滅，依前所厭，以立定名。既爾，此中心亦應滅，所厭俱故，如餘心所。不爾，如何名無心定？

Should one claim that, at other times, the three coming together have the power to form and give rise to contact and stir up feeling and the rest, but that, since one has grown weary of the mental factors prior to entering this absorption, the three lack that power while in this absorption, so that no contact is formed and no feeling arises — then this absorption ought to be called "the attainment of the cessation of all mental factors," so why does the sūtra speak only of the cessation of feeling and perception specifically?

Should one reply that, at the moment of growing weary, one grows weary specifically of feeling and perception, so that once these two cease, every mental factor ceases along with them, and the absorption is named after that specifically-targeted weariness — then, by that same logic, the mind itself ought to cease here too, since it is equally implicated in what was grown weary of, just like every other mental factor. If not, then how could this be called "the mindless absorption" at all?

又此定位意識是何？不應是染或無記性，諸善定中無此事故；餘染、無記心必有心所故，不應厭善起染等故。非求寂靜翻起散故。若謂是善，相應善故，應無貪等善根相應。此心不應是自性善或勝義善，違自宗故，非善根等及涅槃故。若謂此心是等起善，加行善根所引發故，理亦不然，違自宗故，如餘善心非等起故。善心無間起三性心，如何善心由前等起？故心是善，由相應力。既爾，必與善根相應，寧說此心獨無心所？故無心所，心亦應無。

Furthermore, what moral nature does the mental consciousness in this state possess? It cannot be defiled or morally neutral, since no such thing occurs within any wholesome absorption; and any other defiled or neutral mind must have mental factors present, so it could not be that one grows weary of something wholesome only to give rise to something defiled instead — one who seeks quiescence certainly does not, in turn, produce agitation.

Should one instead claim it is wholesome — being wholesome, it would have to be associated with the wholesome roots such as non-greed, yet it has already been shown to have no mental factors at all. This mind, then, cannot be either intrinsically wholesome or ultimately wholesome, since that would contradict your own position, lacking as it does the wholesome roots and the rest, and lacking nirvāṇa as its object. Should one claim, instead, that this mind is wholesome by concomitant derivation, drawn forth by the power of the wholesome roots in one's preliminary practice — this, too, does not hold, since it likewise contradicts your own position: no other wholesome mind is ever called wholesome merely by concomitant derivation in this way. A wholesome mind is immediately followed by minds of all three moral qualities — how, then, could a wholesome mind be said to derive its quality merely from what preceded it? A mind is wholesome, rather, precisely through its association with the wholesome mental factors — and if that is so, it must certainly be associated with the wholesome roots, so how could you still maintain that this mind alone has no mental factors at all? Hence, having no mental factors, this consciousness, too, should not exist.

如是推徵，眼等轉識於滅定位非不離身，故契經言不離身者，彼識即是此第八識，入滅定時不為止息，此極寂靜執持識故。無想等位，類此應知。

Through this line of examination, it is shown that the eye-consciousness and the other evolving consciousnesses do not, in the state of cessation-attainment, remain "not departed from the body" at all. The consciousness the sūtra means by "not leaving the body," then, is precisely this eighth consciousness — for one does not enter cessation-attainment in order to bring this consciousness itself to rest, since it is this extremely tranquil consciousness that grasps and holds the body throughout. The state of no-thought should be understood in just the same way.

Reading note: This is the treatise's longest and most closely argued proof, because "cessation-attainment" (nirodha-samāpatti, sometimes called "the attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception") is a genuinely hard case: an advanced meditator in this state has, by every canonical account, no feeling, no perception, and no discernible mental activity at all — yet is described as still alive, warm, and with consciousness "not departed" from the body. If that consciousness is simply the ordinary sixth (mental) consciousness, the treatise needs to explain why the same state is elsewhere flatly called "the mindless absorption." Its answer runs through a chain of dominoes that is worth tracing slowly: feeling and perception are "universal" mental factors, present in absolutely every mental state without exception; if they are truly, completely absent, then volition, contact, and every other universal factor must be absent too, by the very same reasoning; and a consciousness with literally none of its universal supporting factors is not a coherent thing at all — it isn't a diminished mind, it's simply no mind, in the ordinary sixth-consciousness sense.

The moral-quality dilemma near the end closes off the last escape route: even granting, for argument's sake, that some trace of sixth consciousness survives, it can't be wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral without smuggling back in exactly the mental factors just shown to be absent — there is simply no coherent way to describe what this "surviving mind" could be. What is left standing, once every version of "it's actually the sixth consciousness" collapses, is the eighth: something that was never doing the coarse, effortful cognitive work the meditator was trying to quiet in the first place, and so was never a candidate for cessation at all. It doesn't survive cessation-attainment despite being mental activity — it was never the kind of mental activity the practice targets, which is exactly why "not leaving the body" can still be true of it when it is false of everything else.

The Final Argument, and the Case Rests 依教廣成分 · 證有此識 · 正理（十）· 染淨心

The tenth and final rational argument turns to the broadest claim of all: that a mind's defilement or purity is what makes a sentient being defiled or pure in the first place — and traces what that claim requires all the way through to liberation itself.

What Holds Karma's Books Across a Lifetime — Or Many 正理（十）· 染淨心

Chinese Source

又契經說：心雜染故，有情雜染；心清淨故，有情清淨。若無此識，彼染、淨心不應有故。謂染、淨法以心為本，因心而生，依心住故，心受彼熏，持彼種故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the sūtras say: because the mind is defiled, sentient beings are defiled; because the mind is pure, sentient beings are pure. Without this consciousness, that defiled or pure mind could not exist. Defiled and pure dharmas take mind as their root: they arise because of mind and abide in dependence on mind, since mind alone receives their perfuming and holds their seeds.

然雜染法略有三種：煩惱、業、果，種類別故。若無此識持煩惱種，界、地往還，無染心後，諸煩惱起，皆應無因，餘法不能持彼種故，過去、未來非實有故。若諸煩惱無因而生，則無三乘學、無學果，諸已斷者皆應起故。若無此識持業、果種，界、地往還，異類法後，諸業、果起，亦應無因，餘種、餘因前已遮故。若諸業、果無因而生，入無餘依涅槃界已，三界業果還復應生，煩惱亦應無因生故。又行緣識應不得成，轉識受熏，前已遮故。結生染識非行感故；應說名色，行為緣故，時分懸隔無緣義故。此不成故，後亦不成。

Defiled dharmas, broadly speaking, are of three kinds — afflictions, karma, and their results — each a distinct category. Without this consciousness holding the seeds of affliction, then, after passing between realms and stages, and after an interval with no defiled mind at all, the afflictions that later arise would have no cause whatsoever, since no other dharma can hold those seeds, and the past and future have no real substance. If afflictions arose with no cause at all, there could be no fruits of training or of having-finished-training in any of the three vehicles, since afflictions already severed would then simply arise again regardless.

Without this consciousness holding the seeds of karma and its results, then, after passing between realms and stages, and after a period of dissimilar dharmas, the karma and results that later arise would likewise have no cause, since it has already been shown that no other seed or cause can serve this role. If karma and its results arose with no cause at all, then, having entered the nirvāṇa realm without remainder, the karma and results of the three realms would have to arise all over again, since afflictions, too, would then arise with no cause.

Furthermore, the link "formations condition consciousness" could not be established at all, since it has already been shown that the evolving consciousnesses cannot be perfumed. The consciousness that binds one to a new birth in a defiled state is not, in fact, drawn forth by formations; rather, it should properly be said that name-and-form is what formations condition, since the interval of time between a past life's formations and the rebirth-linking moment would otherwise leave no coherent conditioning relationship at all. Since this first point fails, what follows from it fails as well.

諸清淨法亦有三種：世、出世道、斷果別故。若無此識持世、出世清淨道種，異類心後，起彼淨法皆應無因，所執餘因前已破故。若二淨道無因而生，入無餘依涅槃界已，彼二淨道還復應生，所依亦應無因生故。又出世道初不應生，無法持彼法爾種故；有漏類別非彼因故，無因而生非釋種故。初不生故，後亦不生，是則應無三乘道果。

Pure dharmas, likewise, are of three kinds — the mundane path, the supramundane path, and the fruit of severance — each a distinct category. Without this consciousness holding the seeds of the mundane and supramundane pure paths, then, after an interval of dissimilar states of mind, the arising of these pure dharmas would have no cause at all, since every alternative cause proposed for this has already been refuted. If these two pure paths arose with no cause, then, having entered the nirvāṇa realm without remainder, these two pure paths would have to arise all over again, since whatever they depend on would likewise arise without cause.

Furthermore, the supramundane path could never even arise for the very first time, since there would be nothing to hold its innate seed beforehand — a contaminated dharma, being of an altogether different category, cannot serve as its cause, and arising with no cause at all cannot explain a specific seed either. Since it could not arise the first time, it could never arise afterward either — and this would mean there could be no fruit of the path in any of the three vehicles at all.

若無此識持煩惱種，轉依、斷果亦不得成，謂道起時，現行煩惱及彼種子俱非有故；染、淨二心不俱起故，道相應心不持彼種，自性相違，如涅槃故；去、來得等非實有故；餘法持種理不成故。既無所斷，能斷亦無，依誰由誰而立斷果？若由道力，後惑不生，立斷果者，則初道起應成無學；後諸煩惱皆已無因，永不生故。

Without this consciousness holding the seeds of affliction, the transformation of one's basis and the fruit of severance could not be established either. Consider: when the path arises, both the manifest afflictions and their seeds are, at that very moment, absent — since a defiled mind and a pure mind cannot arise together, the mind associated with the path does not itself hold those seeds, its very nature being opposed to them, just as nirvāṇa's is; and since the past and future "possession" of a quality has no real substance, and it has already been shown that no other dharma can hold seeds. If, then, there is nothing actually severed, there can be no "severing" either — on what basis, and by what, could the fruit of severance ever be established?

Should one hold that the fruit of severance consists simply in the fact that, through the power of the path, afflictions no longer arise afterward — then the very first moment the path arises would already have to count as the attainment beyond further training, since every affliction thereafter would already have no cause at all, and so could never arise again.

許有此識，一切皆成，唯此能持染、淨種故。證此識有，理趣無邊，恐厭繁文，略述綱要。別有此識，教理顯然，諸有智人，應深信受。

Grant that this consciousness exists, however, and everything holds together perfectly, since it alone can hold the seeds of both defilement and purity.

The lines of reasoning proving this consciousness's existence are, in truth, without limit; fearing to weary the reader with excessive text, I have only sketched their outline here. That this consciousness exists as something distinct is plain from both scripture and reason alike — those of wisdom should place their deep faith and acceptance in it.

Reading note: The final argument is the broadest of the ten, because it has to hold across the single widest gap the tradition asks it to explain: not just sleep, or deep absorption, or a single lifetime, but the entire arc from an ordinary being's first affliction to a fully liberated arhat's final severance of it. Karma and affliction have to be held somewhere between the moment they're incurred and the (possibly much later, possibly next-lifetime) moment they ripen or are severed; the same is true, in mirror image, of the path's own wholesome seeds, which have to be held somewhere between cultivation and fruition. Nothing intermittent can hold either side of this ledger open across such gaps — which is exactly the same structural requirement every one of the previous nine arguments has been building toward from a different angle.

The closing lines are worth pausing on in their own right: after ten increasingly technical arguments, the author explicitly steps back and says, in effect, "there is far more that could be said; I have only given the outline." That is an unusual thing for a treatise this dense to admit, and it signals something important about how this whole section wants to be read — not as an exhaustive catalogue, but as a representative sample meant to establish a pattern of reasoning, with the invitation to apply the same pattern further oneself. This closes the entire proof of the ālayavijñāna's existence, spanning fascicles 2 through 4 of the treatise: the complete scriptural case, and all ten arguments from reason alone. Fascicle 4 continues from here into an entirely new topic — the second of the three kinds of "transforming" consciousness, manas, the seventh consciousness.

Introducing Manas, the Second Transformation 明唯識相 · 釋第二能變 · 略明能變相

With the ālayavijñāna's existence fully established, the treatise turns to the second of the three "transforming" consciousnesses named back in fascicle 2: manas, traditionally called the seventh consciousness. Three verses from Vasubandhu's root text introduce it, followed by the treatise's own line-by-line commentary.

The Root Verses 頌曰

Chinese Source

如是已說初能變相；第二能變其相云何？頌曰：

次第二能變，是識名末那，依彼轉，緣彼，思量為性相。

四煩惱常俱，謂：我癡、我見，并我慢、我愛，及餘觸等俱。

有覆無記攝，隨所生所繫。阿羅漢、滅定、出世道無有。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far the first transformation has been explained. What, then, is the nature of the second transformation? The verses state:

Next, the second transformation is the consciousness called manas. It turns in dependence on that [first consciousness], takes it as its object, and has deliberation as its nature and characteristic.

Four afflictions constantly accompany it: self-delusion and self-view, together with self-conceit and self-love, and other factors such as contact besides.

It belongs to the covered-yet-neutral moral category, and is bound according to wherever it is born. It is absent in arhats, in cessation-attainment, and on the supramundane path.

Why "Manas," Not "Consciousness" 釋「末那」名

Chinese Source

論曰：次初異熟能變識，後應辯思量能變識相。是識聖教別名末那，恒、審思量勝餘識故。此名何異第六意識？此持業釋，如藏識名，識即意故；彼依主釋，如眼識等，識異意故。然諸聖教恐此濫彼，故於第七但立意名。又摽意名，為簡心、識，積集、了別劣餘識故，或欲顯此與彼意識為近所依，故但名意。

Ru-Yi Translation

The treatise states: having discussed the ripening-transformation consciousness first, we should next explain the nature of the deliberating-transformation consciousness. This consciousness is given the distinct scriptural name manas, since it constantly and closely deliberates in a way that surpasses the other consciousnesses.

How does this name differ from the sixth, the mental consciousness (also called "意識", literally "manas-consciousness")? Here, "manas" is a compound in which the term simply describes the very activity itself — as with the name "storehouse consciousness," where "consciousness" simply is this "storehouse-ing." But "mental consciousness," as a name for the sixth, is a compound naming something in relation to what it depends on — like "eye-consciousness" and the rest, where the consciousness is distinct from the eye, its mere support. Fearing this might cause the two to be confused with one another, the scriptures give the seventh only the bare name "manas."

The name "manas" is also chosen to distinguish this consciousness from "mind" (citta) and "consciousness" (vijñāna) as general terms, since it is inferior to the other consciousnesses both in accumulating seeds and in discriminating objects; or else, the name is meant to show that this consciousness serves as the near, proximate support for that other, sixth "mental consciousness" — hence it is called simply "manas."

What Manas Depends On 釋「依彼轉」

Chinese Source

「依彼轉」者，顯此所依。「彼」謂即前初能變識，聖說此識依藏識故。有義：此意以彼識種而為所依，非彼現識，此無間斷，不假現識為俱有依方得生故。有義：此意以彼識種及彼現識俱為所依，雖無間斷，而有轉易，名轉識故，必假現識為俱有依，方得生故。「轉」謂流轉，顯示此識恒依彼識取所緣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"It turns in dependence on that" shows what this consciousness depends on. "That" refers to the very first transformation, the consciousness already discussed — the scriptures teach that this consciousness depends on the storehouse consciousness.

One view holds: manas takes only the seeds of that consciousness as its support, not its manifest, currently-active form — since manas itself is without interruption, it does not need a manifest consciousness to serve as its co-arising support in order to arise.

Another view holds: manas takes both the seeds of that consciousness and its manifest, currently-active form together as its support — for although manas, too, is without interruption, it nevertheless undergoes constant change, and is itself accordingly called an "evolving consciousness"; it must, therefore, rely on a manifest consciousness as its co-arising support in order to arise.

"Turns" means flowing on in continuity, showing that this consciousness constantly relies on that consciousness in order to take hold of its object.

Three Kinds of Support, for Every Mind 諸心、心所皆有所依 · 三種所依

Chinese Source

諸心、心所皆有所依，然彼所依，總有三種：一、因緣依，謂自種子，諸有為法皆託此依，離自因緣必不生故；二、增上緣依，謂內六處，諸心、心所皆託此依，離俱有根必不轉故；三、等無間緣依，謂前滅意，諸心、心所皆託此依，離開導根必不起故。唯心、心所具三所依，名有所依，非所餘法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every mind and mental factor has something it depends on. These supports, broadly speaking, are of three kinds. First, the seed-support: one's own seeds, on which every conditioned dharma relies, since nothing can arise apart from its own causal condition. Second, the co-arising support: the six internal sense-bases, on which every mind and mental factor relies, since none can operate apart from a co-existing sense-faculty. Third, the immediately-preceding-condition support: the mind that has just ceased, on which every mind and mental factor relies, since none can arise apart from a faculty that opens the way and guides it onward.

Only mind and mental factors possess all three of these supports, and so are said, in the fullest sense, to "have a support" — no other dharma does.

Reading note: The name "manas" is doing careful, deliberate work here, and the treatise's grammatical fussiness has a real payoff: the seventh consciousness isn't just called "manas" as a convenient label — it is manas, constant self-referential deliberation, in exactly the way the storehouse consciousness simply is storehousing. Compare this to "eye-consciousness," which is consciousness caused by, but not identical with, the eye. That distinction sets up something important for later: whatever this constant deliberation is doing, it isn't optional or occasional the way ordinary thought is — it is this consciousness's very nature, running continuously in the background of every ordinary experience.

The two scholarly opinions on "what manas depends on" (seeds only, versus seeds-and-manifest-consciousness together) look like a technical footnote, but it's setting up a recurring feature of fascicle 4: Xuanzang's compilation regularly presents rival interpretations from earlier Yogācāra masters before settling on one (usually Dharmapāla's), rather than silently picking a single answer. Watching for this "有義...有義..." pattern is a useful reading strategy for the rest of the manas discussion.

The three-part framework of "support" (所依) is worth holding onto, since the entire massive debate that follows — whether the five physical sense-faculties really are the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses — is fought entirely in these terms. A seed-support explains why a dharma can arise at all; a co-arising support explains why it needs a particular physical basis moment to moment; the immediately-preceding-condition support explains why mental events form an unbroken chain, each one opening the way for the next.

Are the Senses Just Seeds? 明唯識相 · 釋第二能變 · 俱有依

The treatise now turns to the second of the three supports named earlier — the "co-arising support" (俱有依) — and opens with one of its longest technical debates: are the five physical sense-faculties (the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body) really just the seeds of their corresponding consciousnesses, or something more?

The Opening View, and Its Scriptural Backing 有作是說 · 五根即五識種

Chinese Source

次俱有依，有作是說：眼等五識意識為依，此現起時，必有彼故。無別眼等為俱有依，眼等五根即種子故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the co-arising support. Some hold: the eye-consciousness and the other four sense-consciousnesses take the mental consciousness as their co-arising support, since whenever these arise, that mental consciousness must be present. There is no separate eye-faculty and the rest serving as a co-arising support, since the eye and the other four physical sense-faculties are simply the seeds of their respective consciousnesses.

《二十唯識》伽他中言：「識從自種生，似境相而轉，為成內、外處，佛說彼為十。」彼頌意說：世尊為成十二處故，說五識種為眼等根，五識相分為色等境，故眼等根即五識種。《觀所緣論》亦作是說：「識上色功能，名五根應理，功能與境色，無始互為因。」彼頌意言：異熟識上能生眼等色識種子名色功能，說為五根，無別眼等，種與色識常互為因，能熏與種遞為因故。第七、八識無別此依，恒相續轉，自力勝故。第六意識別有此依，要託末那而得起故。

The Viṃśatikā's verse says: "Consciousness arises from its own seed, and manifests appearing as if it had an object; in order to establish the internal and external sense-fields, the Buddha spoke of these as ten." The meaning of this verse is: in order to establish the twelve sense-fields, the World-Honored One spoke of the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses as the eye-faculty and the rest, and of the perceived aspect of the five sense-consciousnesses as form and the other objects — hence the eye-faculty and the rest are simply the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses.

The Ālambanaparīkṣā likewise says: "The form-generating function within consciousness is rightly called the five faculties; this function and the form-object have, since beginningless time, mutually served as each other's cause." The meaning of this verse is: the capacity within the ripening consciousness capable of generating the eye-consciousness and the other form-perceiving consciousnesses is called, by the name "form-function," the five faculties — there is no separate eye and the rest apart from this — since this seed and the form-consciousness are constantly each other's cause, the perfuming consciousness and the seed serving, in turn, as each other's cause.

The seventh and eighth consciousnesses have no separate such support, since they continue on in unbroken succession by their own power alone. The sixth, mental consciousness, does have a separate such support, since it must rely on manas in order to arise.

The Refutation 有義：彼說理、教相違

Chinese Source

有義：彼說理、教相違。若五色根即五識種，十八界種應成雜亂，然十八界各別有種，諸聖教中處處說故。又五識種各有能生相、見分異，為執何等名眼等根？若見分種，應識蘊攝；若相分種，應外處攝，便違聖教眼等五根，皆是色蘊內處所攝。又若五根即五識種，五根應是五識因緣，不應說為增上緣攝。又鼻、舌根即二識種，則應鼻、舌唯欲界繫；或應二識通色界繫，許便俱與聖教相違。眼、耳、身根即三識種，二界、五地為難亦然。又五識種既通善、惡，應五色根非唯無記。又五識種無執受攝，五根亦應非有執受。又五色根若五識種，應意識種即是末那，彼以五根為同法故。又《瑜伽論》說眼等識皆具三依，若五色根即五識種，依但應二。又諸聖教說眼等根皆通現種，執唯是種，便與一切聖教相違。

Ru-Yi Translation

Another view holds: that account conflicts with both reason and scripture.

If the five physical sense-faculties were simply the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses, the seeds of the eighteen elements would collapse into confusion — yet each of the eighteen elements has its own distinct seed, as the scriptures state again and again.

Furthermore, the seed of each sense-consciousness gives rise to both a perceived aspect and a perceiving aspect, each distinct — so which of these is meant to be called the "eye-faculty" and the rest? If it is the seed of the perceiving aspect, it should be included in the consciousness aggregate; if it is the seed of the perceived aspect, it should be included among the external sense-fields — either way, this contradicts the scriptures' teaching that the five physical faculties are all included within the form aggregate, as internal sense-fields.

Furthermore, if the five faculties were simply the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses, the five faculties would have to serve as the causal condition for the five consciousnesses, and could not be said to serve merely as a dominant condition.

Furthermore, if the nose- and tongue-faculties were simply the seeds of their two consciousnesses, then the nose and tongue faculties would have to be bound only to the desire realm — or else these two consciousnesses would have to extend into the form realm as well — and either alternative equally contradicts scripture. The same difficulty arises for the eye-, ear-, and body-faculties as the seeds of their three consciousnesses, spanning two realms and five stages.

Furthermore, since the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses can be wholesome or unwholesome, the five physical faculties would then have to be more than merely morally neutral. Furthermore, since the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses are not included among what is "grasped and sustained," the five faculties, too, would then have to lack this quality.

Furthermore, if the five physical faculties were simply the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses, the seed of the mental consciousness would have to simply be manas, since manas takes the five faculties as its parallel case. Furthermore, the Yogācārabhūmi states that the eye-consciousness and the rest each possess all three kinds of support — yet if the five physical faculties were simply the seeds of the five sense-consciousnesses, there would only be two. Furthermore, the scriptures everywhere teach that the eye-faculty and the rest are both manifest and seed together — to hold that they are only seeds directly contradicts every one of these scriptures.

有避如前所說過難，朋附彼執復轉救言：異熟識中能感五識增上業種，名五色根；非作因緣生五識種，妙符二頌，善順《瑜伽》。彼有虛言都無實義，應五色根非無記故；又彼應非唯有執受，唯色蘊攝，唯內處故；鼻、舌唯應欲界繫故；三根不應五地繫故；感意識業應末那故；眼等不應通現、種故；又應眼等非色根故。又若五識皆業所感，則應一向無記性攝；善等五識既非業感，應無眼等為俱有依，故彼所言非為善救。

To evade the difficulties just raised, some who side with that view offer a further, revised defense: the karmic seeds within the ripening consciousness that are capable of drawing forth the five sense-consciousnesses as a dominant condition — these, specifically, are called the five physical faculties, not the seeds that serve as the direct causal condition generating the five sense-consciousnesses; this, they claim, beautifully accords with the two verses, and agrees well with the Yogācārabhūmi.

This, too, is empty talk with no real substance. On this account, the five physical faculties would still have to be more than merely morally neutral; they would still have to be more than merely something "grasped and sustained," included only within the form aggregate, only among the internal sense-fields; the nose- and tongue-faculties would still have to be bound only to the desire realm; the three faculties would still not have to span the five stages; the karma that draws forth the mental consciousness would then have to be manas itself; the eye-faculty and the rest would still not have to be both manifest and seed together; and the eye-faculty and the rest would, on this account, not even properly be physical faculties at all.

Furthermore, if the five sense-consciousnesses were entirely the product of karma, they would have to be exclusively morally neutral throughout — yet wholesome and other varieties of the five sense-consciousnesses plainly are not the product of karma, so on this view there would be no eye-faculty and the rest to serve as their co-arising support at all. This revised defense, then, is no genuine rescue.

又諸聖教處處皆說阿賴耶識變似色根及根依處、器世間等，如何汝等撥無色根？許眼等識變似色等；不許眼等藏識所變，如斯迷謬，深違教、理。然伽他說：種子功能名五根者，為破離識實有色根，於識所變似眼根等，以有發生五識用故，假名種子及色功能。非謂色根即識業種。

Furthermore, the scriptures everywhere teach that the ālayavijñāna transforms into the semblance of the physical sense-faculties, their supporting bases, and the receptacle-world. How, then, can you deny the physical faculties altogether? You yourselves accept that the eye-consciousness and the rest transform into the semblance of form and the other objects — yet you refuse to accept that the eye-faculty and the rest are likewise transformations of the storehouse consciousness. Such confusion runs deeply contrary to both scripture and reason.

As for the verses' calling seed-function the "five faculties" — this was meant only to refute the view that the physical sense-faculties are really, substantially existent apart from consciousness. Since what consciousness transforms into, resembling the eye-faculty and the rest, has the capacity to give rise to the five sense-consciousnesses, it is provisionally named "seed" and "form-function." It does not mean that the physical faculty simply is the karmic seed of consciousness.

又緣五境明了意識，應以五識為俱有依，以彼必與五識俱故，若彼不依眼等識者，彼應不與五識為依，彼此相依勢力等故。又第七識雖無間斷，而見道等既有轉易，應如六識有俱有依，不爾，彼應非轉識攝，便違聖教轉識有七，故應許彼有俱有依，此即現行第八識攝。如《瑜伽》說有藏識故，得有末那；末那為依，意識得轉。彼論意言現行藏識為依止故，得有末那，非由彼種。不爾，應說有藏識故，意識得轉。由此彼說理、教相違。

Furthermore, the clear mental consciousness that takes hold of the five sense-objects should have the five sense-consciousnesses as its co-arising support, since it must always occur together with them — if it did not depend on the eye-consciousness and the rest, then it should not serve as a support for them either, since mutual dependence requires an equal relationship on both sides.

Furthermore, although the seventh consciousness is itself uninterrupted, it nevertheless undergoes change upon entering the path of seeing and the like — so, like the other evolving consciousnesses, it too should have a co-arising support; otherwise, it could not be included among the "evolving consciousnesses" at all, which would contradict the scriptures' teaching that there are seven evolving consciousnesses. One should, therefore, accept that it does have a co-arising support, namely the currently-manifest eighth consciousness — as the Yogācārabhūmi says: because there is a storehouse consciousness, manas can exist; with manas as its support, the mental consciousness can operate. That treatise means that it is the currently-manifest storehouse consciousness, serving as a support, that allows manas to exist — not its mere seed. Were this not so, it would instead have had to say: because there is a storehouse consciousness, the mental consciousness can operate. By this, that earlier view is shown to conflict with both reason and scripture.

A Provisional Tally, Then a Refinement 是故應言 · 有義：此說猶未盡理

Chinese Source

是故應言前五轉識，一一定有二俱有依，謂五色根、同時意識；第六轉識決定恒有一俱有依，謂第七識，若與五識俱時起者，亦以五識為俱有依；第七轉識決定唯有一俱有依，謂第八識；唯第八識恒無轉變，自能立故，無俱有依。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hence it should be said: each of the first five evolving consciousnesses certainly has two co-arising supports — the five physical faculties, and the simultaneous mental consciousness. The sixth evolving consciousness certainly, constantly has one co-arising support, the seventh consciousness — and, whenever it arises together with the five sense-consciousnesses, it also takes them as a co-arising support. The seventh evolving consciousness certainly has only one co-arising support, the eighth consciousness. Only the eighth consciousness, constantly unchanging, is established by its own power alone, and has no co-arising support at all.

有義：此說猶未盡理。第八類餘既同識性，如何不許有俱有依？第七、八識既恒俱轉，更互為依，斯有何失？許現起識以種為依，識種亦應許依現識，能熏異熟，為生、長、住依，識種離彼不生、長、住故。又異熟識有色界中能執持身，依色根轉，如契經說：阿賴耶識業風所飄，遍依諸根，恒相續轉；《瑜伽》亦說：眼等六識各別依故，不能執受有色根身。若異熟識不遍依止有色諸根，應如六識非能執受，或所立因有不定失。是故，藏識若現起者，定有一依，謂第七識，在有色界亦依色根；若識種子定有一依，謂異熟識，初熏習位亦依能熏。餘如前說。

Another view holds: this account still does not exhaust the reasoning. The eighth consciousness, being of the same essential nature as consciousness as the others, why should it not also be granted a co-arising support? Since the seventh and eighth consciousnesses constantly turn together, what fault is there in holding that they serve as each other's mutual support?

If one grants that a manifest consciousness takes a seed as its support, one should equally grant that the seed, in turn, depends on the manifest consciousness — for the consciousness that perfumes the ripening consciousness serves as the support for the seed's arising, growth, and continuance, since the seed cannot arise, grow, or continue apart from it.

Furthermore, in the realm of form, the ripening consciousness is able to grasp and hold the body precisely by operating in dependence on the physical sense-faculties, as the sūtra says: the ālayavijñāna, driven by the winds of karma, universally relies on the sense-faculties, continuing on without interruption; and the Yogācārabhūmi likewise says: because the eye-consciousness and the other five each depend separately on their own faculty, they are unable to grasp and sustain the physical body as a whole. If the ripening consciousness did not universally rely on the physical sense-faculties, it, too, should be unable to grasp and sustain the body, just like the six sense-consciousnesses — or else the reason given here would suffer the fallacy of being inconclusive.

Hence: when the storehouse consciousness is currently manifest, it certainly has one support, the seventh consciousness — and, within a realm possessing form, it also relies on the physical sense-faculties. When the consciousness-seed of the eighth is what is meant, it certainly has one support, the ripening consciousness itself — and, at the initial moment of being perfumed, it also relies on the perfuming consciousness. Everything else remains as stated before.

Support Versus Mere Dependence — The Final Word 有義：未了所依與依別 · 定說八識俱有依

Chinese Source

有義：前說皆不應理，未了所依與依別故。「依」謂一切有生滅法，仗因託緣而得生、住，諸所仗託皆說為依，如王與臣互相依等。若法決定、有境、為主，令心心所取自所緣，乃是所依，即內六處，餘非有境、定、為主故，此但如王，非如臣等。故諸聖教唯心、心所名有所依，非色等法，無所緣故。但說心所，心為所依；不說心所，為心所依，彼非主故。然有處說依為所依，或所依為依，皆隨宜假說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Another view holds: none of the accounts given above is correct, since they fail to distinguish "support" proper from mere "dependence" in general.

"Dependence" means any dharma whatsoever, subject to arising and ceasing, that relies on causes and conditions in order to arise and continue — anything at all that something else relies on in this general sense is called a "dependence," just as a king and his ministers depend on one another.

But only a dharma that is fixed and determinate, possesses a cognitive object, and serves as the governing factor, causing mind and its mental factors to take hold of their own object, counts as a genuine "support" — namely, the six internal sense-bases. Everything else lacks a cognitive object and lacks this fixed, governing quality, and is therefore merely like the minister, not like the king.

Hence the scriptures speak only of mind and mental factors as "having a support," never form and other such dharmas, since these last have no cognitive object at all. One speaks of mental factors having mind as their support, but never of mind having mental factors as its support, since the mental factors are not the governing party. In certain places, though, "dependence" is loosely spoken of as "support," or "support" as "dependence" — these are simply convenient, informal usages, adapted to context.

由此五識俱有所依定有四種：謂五色根、六、七、八識，隨闕一種必不轉故。同境、分別、染淨、根本，所依別故。聖教唯說依五根者，以不共故，又必同境，近相順故。第六意識俱有所依唯有二種：謂七、八識，隨闕一種必不轉故。雖五識俱取境明了，而不定有，故非所依。聖教唯說依第七者，染淨依故，同轉識攝，近相順故。第七意識俱有所依但有一種：謂第八識，藏識若無，定不轉故。如伽他說：「阿賴耶為依，故有末那轉，依止心及意，餘轉識得生。」

By this, then, the eye-consciousness and the other four sense-consciousnesses certainly have four kinds of co-arising support: the five physical faculties, and the sixth, seventh, and eighth consciousnesses — lacking any one of these, they simply cannot operate, since object, discrimination, moral affectedness, and root each require a distinct support. The scriptures speak only of their depending on the five faculties specifically, because this support is not shared with any other consciousness, and because it necessarily shares the very same object, being the nearest, most closely corresponding support.

The sixth, mental consciousness has only two kinds of co-arising support: the seventh and eighth consciousnesses — lacking either, it cannot operate. Although it often takes hold of its object together with, and just as clearly as, the five sense-consciousnesses, this is not always so, and so they do not count as its support. The scriptures speak only of its depending on the seventh, because that is its support for moral affectedness, because it is likewise included among the evolving consciousnesses, and because it is the nearest, most closely corresponding support.

The seventh, manas has only a single co-arising support: the eighth consciousness — without the storehouse consciousness, it certainly cannot operate. As the verse says: "Because there is the ālaya as support, manas turns; relying on this mind and on manas, the remaining evolving consciousnesses can arise."

阿賴耶識俱有所依亦但一種：謂第七識，彼識若無，定不轉故。論說：藏識恒與末那俱時轉故；又說藏識恒依染污，此即末那。而說三位無末那者，依有覆說，如言四位無阿賴耶，非無第八，此亦應爾。雖有色界亦依五根，而不定有，非所依攝；識種不能現取自境，可有依義而無所依。心所所依，隨識應說，復各加自相應之心。若作是說，妙符理、教。

The ālayavijñāna, likewise, has only a single co-arising support: the seventh consciousness — without that consciousness, it certainly cannot operate. The treatise states that the storehouse consciousness constantly turns together with manas; and it also states that the storehouse consciousness constantly depends on a defiled mind, which is precisely manas. As for the teaching that manas is absent in three states — this refers only to its defiled, covered aspect, just as it is said that the ālaya is absent in four states, without meaning the eighth consciousness itself is absent; the same should be understood here.

Although, in a realm possessing form, the eighth consciousness also relies on the five physical faculties, this is not invariably so, and so they are not counted among its supports. A consciousness-seed, for its part, cannot itself take hold of its own object in a manifest way, so it may be said to have a "dependence" in the general sense, yet has no genuine "support."

The support of the mental factors should be stated in accordance with whichever consciousness they accompany, with the further addition, in each case, of the mind associated with them.

If the matter is stated in this way, it accords beautifully with both reason and scripture.

Reading note: This debate is really about a single, sharp question hiding inside a much longer discussion: is the eye literally identical with a kind of seed inside consciousness, or does consciousness merely transform into something that resembles a physical eye, while a genuinely separate physical basis still exists? The "seeds-only" side has real scriptural ammunition — two citable verses that, read carelessly, do seem to identify the sense-faculties with seed-function. The refutation's most elegant move is showing those verses don't need to be read that way at all: they were written to deny that the sense-faculties are substantially real apart from consciousness altogether (a different, more radical claim), not to assert that faculty and seed are simply the same thing. Once that's cleared up, an avalanche of ordinary Abhidharma bookkeeping — the eighteen elements, moral quality, karmic bondage to particular realms, whether something counts as "grasped and sustained" — falls into place naturally, exactly the way it's supposed to for a real physical basis and comes apart the moment you try to force it onto a seed instead.

The final view's distinction between "support" (所依) and mere "dependence" (依) is the payoff for the whole debate, and it's a genuinely useful piece of conceptual hygiene: almost anything can be said to "depend" on almost anything else in some loose sense — a king depends on his ministers just as much as they depend on him — but only something that is fixed, has a genuine cognitive object, and actively governs what a mind can perceive deserves to be called that mind's support in the strict sense. This is what finally lets the treatise assign a clean, definite number of supports to each of the eight consciousnesses without hedging: four for the first five (their own faculty, plus the sixth, seventh, and eighth), two for the sixth (seventh and eighth), one for the seventh (the eighth alone), and one for the eighth (the seventh alone) — a tidy, mutually reinforcing structure where even the most "independent" consciousness, the ālaya, still turns out to need exactly one thing to lean on.

The Guiding Support: Can the Five Senses Sustain Themselves? 明唯識相 · 釋第二能變 · 開導依

The last of the three kinds of support is the "guiding support" (開導依) — what a moment of mind needs, immediately before it, to open the way and guide it into arising. This debate asks whether the five sense-consciousnesses can guide their own next moment, or whether they always need the mental consciousness to do it for them.

The Opening View, and a Two-Part Objection 有作是說 · 唯第六識為開導依

Chinese Source

後開導依，有義：五識自、他前後不相續故，必第六識所引生故，唯第六識為開導依。第六意識自相續故，亦由五識所引生故，以前六識為開導依。第七、八識自相續故，不假他識所引生故，但以自類為開導依。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the guiding support. Some hold: the five sense-consciousnesses, whether considered in their own class or across classes, do not continue in succession from one moment to the next, and must be drawn forth by the sixth, mental consciousness — hence only the mental consciousness serves as their guiding support. The sixth, mental consciousness, being self-continuous, and also drawn forth by the five sense-consciousnesses, takes the prior moment of any of the first six consciousnesses as its guiding support. The seventh and eighth consciousnesses, being self-continuous and never drawn forth by any other consciousness, take only their own prior moment, of their own kind, as their guiding support.

有義：前說未為究理。且前五識未自在位，遇非勝境，可如所說。若自在位，如諸佛等，於境自在，諸根互用，任運決定，不假尋求，彼五識身寧不相續？等流五識，既為決定、染淨、作意勢力引生，專注所緣，未能捨頃，如何不許多念相續？

Another view holds: the account just given does not exhaust the reasoning. It is true that, for the first five consciousnesses not yet at the stage of mastery, encountering an ordinary rather than exceptional object, the account given may hold. But at the stage of mastery — as with the buddhas, who have complete mastery over their objects, whose faculties can each perform any other's function, arising spontaneously and decisively, requiring no searching at all — how could their five sense-consciousnesses fail to continue in succession?

Even the ordinary, homogeneously-flowing five sense-consciousnesses, once drawn forth by the force of decisive cognition, of moral affectedness, and of attention, remain focused on their object without a moment's release — how, then, could one refuse to accept that they continue across many successive moments?

故《瑜伽》說：決定心後，方有染淨，此後乃有等流眼識善、不善轉，而彼不由自分別力，乃至此意不趣餘境，經爾所時，眼、意二識或善、或染，相續而轉，如眼識生，乃至身識，應知亦爾。彼意定顯經爾所時，眼、意二識俱相續轉，既眼識時非無意識，故非二識互相續生。若增盛境相續現前，逼奪身心不能暫捨，時五識身，理必相續，如熱地獄、戲忘天等。

Hence the Yogācārabhūmi says: only after a decisive cognition does moral affectedness arise; only after this does the homogeneously-flowing eye-consciousness turn wholesome or unwholesome — yet this does not occur through its own power of discrimination, but rather, for as long as the mental consciousness does not turn to some other object, the eye-consciousness and the mental consciousness, whether wholesome or defiled, continue turning together throughout that entire span — and just as with the eye-consciousness, so it should be understood with the arising of the body-consciousness and the rest.

The clear intent of this passage is to show that, throughout that entire span, the eye-consciousness and the mental consciousness both continue turning together — since, whenever the eye-consciousness is present, the mental consciousness is never absent, this is not a case of the two consciousnesses merely generating each other in succession, one after the other.

If an intensified object is continuously present, overwhelming body and mind so that they cannot let go of it even for a moment, then, at that time, the five sense-consciousnesses must, by reason, continue in succession — as in the scorching hells, or in the heaven of frivolous forgetting.

故《瑜伽》言：若此六識為彼六識等無間緣，即施設此名為意根。若五識前後定唯有意識，彼論應言：若此一識為彼六識等無間緣。或彼應言：若此六識為彼一識等無間緣。既不如是，故知五識有相續義。

Hence the Yogācārabhūmi states: whenever any of these six consciousnesses serves as the equal-immediate-antecedent condition for any of these six consciousnesses, it is on this basis that the name "mental faculty" is established. If the five sense-consciousnesses, before and after, invariably had only the mental consciousness as their antecedent, that treatise ought instead to have said: whenever this one consciousness serves as the equal-immediate-antecedent condition for these six consciousnesses. Or else it ought to have said: whenever these six consciousnesses serve as the equal-immediate-antecedent condition for this one consciousness. Since it says neither of these, it can be known that the five sense-consciousnesses do have the capacity to continue in succession.

Extending the Question to Every Consciousness 推及藏識、末那、轉依位

Chinese Source

五識起時，必有意識能引後念意識令起，何假五識為開導依？無心睡眠、悶絕等位，意識斷已，後復起時，藏識、末那既恒相續，亦應與彼為開導依。若彼用前自類開導，五識自類何不許然？此既不然，彼云何爾？

Ru-Yi Translation

Whenever the five sense-consciousnesses arise, the mental consciousness is always present too, capable of drawing forth the next moment's mental consciousness — so why should the five sense-consciousnesses need to rely on themselves as their own guiding support?

In states such as dreamless sleep or unconsciousness, the mental consciousness has ceased entirely; when it arises again later, the storehouse consciousness and manas, being themselves constantly continuous, ought equally to serve as its guiding support at that point. If the mental consciousness is instead said to use its own prior, same-kind moment as its guide even then, why should the five sense-consciousnesses not equally be allowed their own prior, same-kind moment as their guide? If this is not accepted for the five senses, then on what grounds is it accepted for the mental consciousness?

平等性智相應末那初起必由第六意識，亦應用彼為開導依。圓鏡智俱第八淨識，初必六、七方便引生。又異熟心依染污意，或依悲願相應善心。既爾，必應許第八識亦以六、七為開導依。由此彼言都未究理。

When manas, associated with the wisdom of equality, first arises, it must be drawn forth by the sixth, mental consciousness — so this, too, ought to use that mental consciousness as its guiding support. When the purified eighth consciousness, associated with the great mirror wisdom, first arises, it, too, must be drawn forth through the skillful means of the sixth and seventh consciousnesses. And the ripening mind, before this transformation, depends on a defiled manas, or else on a wholesome mind associated with compassion and vow. Given this, one must equally accept that the eighth consciousness, too, takes the sixth and seventh as its guiding support. By this, the view stated above does not exhaust the reasoning at all.

應說五識前六識內，隨用何識為開導依；第六意識用前自類或第七、八為開導依；第七末那用前自類或第六識為開導依；阿陀那識用前自類及第六、七為開導依。皆不違理，由前說故。

One should say, instead: the five sense-consciousnesses may take whichever of the prior six consciousnesses as their guiding support; the sixth, mental consciousness may take its own prior moment, or the seventh or eighth, as its guiding support; the seventh, manas, may take its own prior moment, or the sixth consciousness, as its guiding support; and the appropriating consciousness may take its own prior moment, or the sixth and seventh, as its guiding support. None of this conflicts with reason, for the reasons already given.

The Final Word: Only One's Own Kind Can Guide 有義：亦不應理 · 八識各唯自類為開導依

Chinese Source

有義：此說亦不應理。開導依者，謂有緣法、為主，能作等無間緣。此於後生心、心所法，開避引導名開導依。此但屬心，非心所等。若此與彼無俱起義，說此於彼有開導力；一身八識既容俱起，如何異類為開導依？若許為依，應不俱起，便同異部心不並生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Another view holds: this account, too, is unreasonable. The "guiding support" means a dharma possessing a cognitive object, serving as the governing factor, capable of acting as the equal-immediate-antecedent condition — it is called the "guiding support" because it opens the way and guides forward the mind and mental factors that arise after it. This applies only to mind, not to mental factors and the like.

If one thing has no capacity to arise simultaneously with another, it may be said to have a genuine guiding power with respect to that other — but since a single body's eight consciousnesses can indeed all arise together at once, how could a consciousness of one kind serve as the guiding support for a consciousness of another kind? If one grants that it can serve as such a support, one would have to hold that the two cannot arise simultaneously at all — which would be the same, mistaken position held by certain other schools, that different minds cannot occur together.

又一身中諸識俱起，多少不定，若容互作等無間緣，色等應爾，便違聖說等無間緣唯心、心所。然《攝大乘》說：色亦容有等無間緣者，是縱奪言，謂假縱小乘色、心前後有等無間緣，奪因緣故，不爾，「等」言應成無用。若謂「等」言非遮多少，但表同類，便違汝執異類識作等無間緣。

Furthermore, within a single body, the number of consciousnesses arising together is not fixed — if it were permissible for different-kind consciousnesses to serve as each other's equal-immediate-antecedent condition, then form and the like ought to be permitted the same role, which would contradict the scriptures' teaching that the equal-immediate-antecedent condition belongs only to mind and mental factors.

As for the Mahāyānasaṃgraha's statement that form, too, may have an equal-immediate-antecedent condition — this is a conditional concession, made only for the sake of argument: it provisionally grants the Hīnayāna position that form and mind, in succession, have an equal-immediate-antecedent condition, only in order to then deny that they have a causal condition. Were this not the intended sense, the word "equal" would serve no purpose at all. And if one holds that the word "equal" does not restrict the number but merely indicates a shared kind, this directly contradicts your own position that consciousnesses of different kinds can serve as this condition for one another.

是故八識各唯自類為開導依，深契教理，自類必無俱起義故。心所此依，應隨識說。雖心、心所異類並生，而互相應，和合似一，定俱生滅，事業必同，一開導時，餘亦開導，故展轉作等無間緣，諸識不然，不應為例。然諸心所非開導依，於所引生無主義故。

Hence each of the eight consciousnesses takes only its own kind as its guiding support — this accords deeply with both reason and scripture, since dharmas of the very same kind can never arise simultaneously with one another. The support for the mental factors should be stated in accordance with whichever consciousness they accompany.

Although mind and its mental factors, being of different kinds, arise together, they are nevertheless mutually associated, blend as if into a single thing, certainly arise and cease together, and necessarily share the same activity — so that when one opens the way and guides forward, the others do so as well, and thus they can, in turn, serve as each other's equal-immediate-antecedent condition. The various distinct consciousnesses are not like this, and so cannot be treated as an equivalent case. Yet none of the mental factors themselves serve as the guiding support, since they lack the governing quality needed for what they draw forth.

若心、心所等無間緣各唯自類，第七、八識初轉依時，相應信等，此緣便闕，則違聖說諸心、心所皆四緣生。無心睡眠、悶絕等位意識雖斷，而後起時彼開導依即前自類，間斷五識應知亦然，無自類心於中為隔名無間故，彼先滅時，已於今識為開導故，何煩異類為開導依？

Should one object: if the equal-immediate-antecedent condition for mind and mental factors is invariably restricted to their own kind, then, at the very first moment the seventh and eighth consciousnesses undergo their transformation of basis, the faith and other mental factors newly associated with them would then lack this condition altogether — which would contradict the scriptures' teaching that every mind and mental factor arises from all four conditions.

In states such as dreamless sleep or unconsciousness, although the mental consciousness has ceased, when it arises again later its guiding support is simply its own prior moment of the same kind — and the interrupted five sense-consciousnesses should be understood in just the same way, since no mind of a different kind intervenes in the gap to be called "immediate." Once that prior moment of its own kind has ceased, it has already served as the guiding support for the present consciousness — so why trouble to posit a different kind of consciousness as the guiding support at all?

然聖教中說前六識互相引起，或第七、八依六、七生，皆依殊勝增上緣說，非等無間，故不相違。《瑜伽論》說：若此識無間，諸識決定生，說此為彼等無間緣。又此六識為彼六識等無間緣，即施設此名意根者，言總意別，亦不相違。故自類依，深契教理。

As for the scriptures' statements that the first six consciousnesses draw forth one another, or that the seventh and eighth arise in dependence on the sixth and seventh — these are all spoken with reference to an especially strong dominant condition, not the equal-immediate-antecedent condition, and so there is no real contradiction. The Yogācārabhūmi's statement — that whenever this consciousness's immediate cessation is followed with certainty by the arising of some consciousness, it is called that consciousness's equal-immediate-antecedent condition, and that when any of these six consciousnesses is the equal-immediate-antecedent condition for any of these six, this is the basis for the designation "mental faculty" — speaks in general terms while meaning something more specific in each case, and so this, too, is not in conflict. Hence, taking only one's own kind as the guiding support accords deeply with both reason and scripture.

Reading note: The whole debate turns on a distinction easy to miss on a first pass: a consciousness can obviously be influenced, drawn out, or made more likely by another kind of consciousness (the mental consciousness clearly shapes what the eye-consciousness attends to), without that other consciousness being what technically "opens the way" for it moment to moment. The final, settled view insists on keeping these two relationships separate — call the first a "dominant condition" (增上緣), a real but looser kind of influence, and reserve "guiding support" for the second, stricter relationship, which can only hold between two things of the very same kind, precisely because two things of different kinds can perfectly well exist side by side at once (as all eight consciousnesses regularly do), while two moments of the very same kind of consciousness can never coexist — one has to end exactly where the next begins, which is the whole reason a "guide" is even needed there.

Once that distinction is in place, some genuinely striking implications fall out of it almost as an afterthought: even a buddha's five sense-consciousnesses, freed of all ordinary limitation, can sustain themselves in unbroken succession without needing the mental consciousness to relaunch them every instant — mastery, here, looks like self-sufficiency rather than dependence. And the treatise is careful to close a possible loophole at the transformation of basis (轉依) — the momentous shift into awakened wisdom — by noting that even brand-new wholesome mental factors arising for the first time in a transformed mind still get their guiding condition from the ordinary rule, not some special exception: continuity of kind, not the identity of what came before it, is what makes something a guide.

With this, the treatise closes a genuinely long digression on what supports the second transformation. As the closing lines note, the root verse's own phrase — "turns in dependence on that" — was never meant to mention all three supports explicitly; it names only the two (seed and co-arising) that matter most for identifying manas's object, leaving the guiding support, and this entire debate about it, implicit.

What Manas Takes as Its Object 明唯識相 · 釋第二能變 · 所緣

Having settled what manas depends on, the treatise turns to the verse's next phrase — "takes it as its object" — and works through four successive views on exactly what, within the ālayavijñāna, manas actually grasps.

Four Views, Narrowing to One 釋「緣彼」

Chinese Source

如是已說此識所依，所緣云何？謂即「緣彼」。「彼」謂即前此所依識，聖說此識緣藏識故。有義：此意緣彼識體，及相應法。論說：末那我、我所執恒相應故。謂緣彼體及相應法，如次執為我及我所。然諸心所不離識故，如「唯識」言，無違教失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far we have explained what this consciousness depends on. What, then, is its object? It is precisely what the verse means by "takes it as its object." "It" refers to the very consciousness just discussed, on which this one depends — the scriptures teach that this consciousness takes the storehouse consciousness as its object.

One view holds: this manas takes both that consciousness's own essence and its associated mental factors as its object. The treatise says that manas is constantly associated with the grasping of self and of what belongs to self — meaning it takes that consciousness's essence and its associated factors as its object, clinging to them, respectively, as "self" and as "what belongs to self." Since mental factors, moreover, are never separate from consciousness, as the very term "Consciousness-Only" implies, this does not conflict with scripture.

有義：彼說理不應然，曾無處言緣觸等故。應言此意但緣彼識見及相分，如次執為我及我所，相、見俱以識為體故，不違聖說。

Another view holds: that account does not hold up, since nowhere is it said that manas takes contact and the other mental factors as its object. One should say, instead, that manas takes only that consciousness's perceiving aspect and perceived aspect as its object, clinging to them, respectively, as "self" and as "what belongs to self" — since both the perceived and perceiving aspects have consciousness itself as their essence, this does not conflict with the scriptures' teaching.

有義：此說亦不應理。五色根境非識蘊故，應同五識，亦緣外故；應如意識，緣共境故；應生無色者不執我所故，厭色生彼不變色故。應說此意但緣藏識及彼種子，如次執為我及我所。以種即是彼識功能，非實有物，不違聖教。

Another view holds: this account, too, is unreasonable. If manas took the perceived aspect as its object, then, since the five physical faculties and their objects are not themselves part of the consciousness aggregate, manas should, like the five sense-consciousnesses, take external objects as well; it should, like the mental consciousness, take an object shared with others; and a being reborn in the formless realm should then fail to cling to anything as "what belongs to self" at all, since one who is reborn there, having grown weary of form, no longer has the ālaya transforming into form.

One should say, instead, that manas takes only the storehouse consciousness itself and its seeds as its object, clinging to them, respectively, as "self" and as "what belongs to self" — since a seed is simply that consciousness's own functional capacity, not a separately, substantially existing thing, this does not conflict with the scriptures' teaching.

有義：前說皆不應理。色等種子，非識蘊故，論說種子是實有故。假應如無，非因緣故。又此識俱薩迦耶見，任運一類，恒相續生，何容別執有我、我所？無一心中有斷、常等二境，別執俱轉義故，亦不應說二執前後，此無始來一味轉故。

Another view holds: none of the accounts given above is correct. Seeds such as that of form are not themselves part of the consciousness aggregate, and the treatise states that seeds are genuinely, substantially existent — were they merely conceptual constructs, they would be as good as nonexistent, and could not serve as a causal condition at all.

Furthermore, the view of a substantial self accompanying this consciousness arises spontaneously, as a single, unbroken kind, continuing on without interruption — how, then, could it possibly cling separately to two distinct things, a "self" and a "what belongs to self," as if these were two different objects? There is no single mind in which two opposed objects — such as annihilation and permanence — could be separately grasped simultaneously; nor should one say the two graspings occur one after the other, since this clinging has flowed on as a single, undifferentiated whole since beginningless time.

應知此意但緣藏識見分非餘，彼無始來一類相續似常、一故，恒與諸法為所依故。此唯執彼為自內我，乘語勢故，說我所言。或此執彼是我、之我，故於一見義說二言。若作是說，善順教理。多處唯言有我見故，我、我所執，不俱起故。

It should be understood, instead, that this manas takes only the storehouse consciousness's perceiving aspect as its object, nothing more — since that perceiving aspect, from beginningless time, has continued as a single, unbroken kind, resembling something permanent and unitary, and constantly serves as the support for all dharmas. This manas clings to it only as an inner self; the mention of "what belongs to self" is simply carried along by force of ordinary speech. Or else, this clinging takes it to be "my self," so that a single act of view-grasping is described in two verbal expressions.

If the matter is stated in this way, it accords well with both reason and scripture, since many passages speak only of a view of self, and the graspings of "self" and of "what belongs to self" never arise together at once.

未轉依位，唯緣藏識。既轉依已，亦緣真如及餘諸法。平等性智，證得十種平等性故。知諸有情勝解差別，示現種種佛影像故。此中且說未轉依時，故但說此緣彼藏識。悟迷通局，理應爾故。無我、我境，遍、不遍故。如何此識緣自所依？如有後識，即緣前意。彼既極成，此亦何咎？

Before the transformation of basis, manas takes only the storehouse consciousness as its object. Once the transformation has occurred, it takes suchness and all other dharmas as its object as well, since the wisdom of equality realizes the ten kinds of equality, and knows the differing degrees of resolve among sentient beings, manifesting various images of buddhahood accordingly. What is discussed here, though, concerns only the state before this transformation, and so only its taking the storehouse consciousness as object is mentioned. Awakening is unrestricted, delusion is restricted — this is simply how reason has it: the mind free of self and the mind bound to it are, respectively, all-pervading and not all-pervading in their object.

How could this consciousness take as its object the very consciousness it depends on? Just as a later consciousness may take an earlier moment of mind as its object — and if that is already well established, what fault could there be here?

Deliberation: The Defining Trait 釋「思量為性相」

Chinese Source

頌言：「思量為性相」者，雙顯此識自性、行相。意以思量為自性故，即復用彼為行相故。由斯兼釋所立別名，能審思量名末那故。未轉依位，恒、審思量所執我相；已轉依位，亦審思量無我相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The verse's phrase "has deliberation as its nature and characteristic" shows both this consciousness's essential nature and its mode of activity together: manas has deliberation as its very essential nature, and, precisely because of this, it also uses that same deliberation as its mode of activity. This, in turn, explains the distinct name given to it: it is called "manas" precisely because it constantly and closely deliberates. Before the transformation of basis, it constantly and closely deliberates on the mark of a clung-to self; once the transformation has occurred, it likewise constantly and closely deliberates on the mark of no-self.

Reading note: The four-view narrowing here follows a pattern the treatise has used before: start with the most inclusive candidate for what manas grasps (the ālaya's essence plus everything associated with it), and eliminate pieces one at a time until only what the evidence actually demands remains. The winning view is strikingly minimal — manas clings only to the ālaya's perceiving aspect, nothing else — and the reasoning for it is worth sitting with: self-grasping, whatever it is, has to be a single, continuous, undivided act, since a single moment of mind can't simultaneously hold two different objects the way "self" and "what belongs to self" would require if they were genuinely separate targets. What looks, in ordinary language, like grasping both "self" and "what is mine" turns out to be one single, seamless act of clinging to one thing (the ālaya's ever-continuous perceiving aspect), described with two words purely as a manner of speaking.

This has a real payoff for understanding what self-grasping actually is, on this account: not a belief you could argue someone out of with a single counter-example, but a moment-to-moment perceptual habit, running as continuously as the ālaya itself, mistaking that consciousness's own uninterrupted continuity for the mark of a permanent self. It also explains, in passing, why deliberation (思量) survives the transformation of basis rather than simply switching off: manas doesn't stop deliberating when delusion ends, it starts deliberating on no-self instead — the constant, close attention is the fixed trait; what it attends to is what changes.
