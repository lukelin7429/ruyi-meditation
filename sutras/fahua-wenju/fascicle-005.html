妙法蓮華經文句卷第五上

Ru-Yi Translation

Commentary on the Words of the Sūtra of the Lotus Blossom of the Wondrous Dharma, Fascicle Five, Upper Part

天台智者大師說

Expounded by the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai

Beneath the Tree of Enlightenment 我始坐道場：三七日思惟

Chinese Source

◎從「我始坐道場」下，第二十七行半，頌施方便化，就此為二：初有六行半，明念用大乘擬不得；次「尋念」下，第二十一行，明念同諸佛用三乘化，稱宜可得。就初念用大化，又為三：初，一行半，明用大擬宜；次「眾生」下，第二，三行，明眾生無機；次「我即」下，第三，二行，明念息大化也。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "when I first sat at the place of enlightenment" onward, twenty-seven-and-a-half lines versify the bestowing of the transforming teaching by skillful means. This falls into two: first, six-and-a-half lines make clear that he reflected on using the Great Vehicle but could not obtain success with it; next, from "reflecting" onward, twenty-one lines make clear that he reflected on transforming beings in common with the other buddhas by means of the three vehicles, and thereby found fitness and could obtain success. Within the first — reflecting on the use of the great transformation — this again falls into three: first, one-and-a-half lines make clear the fitness he sought in using the Great Vehicle; next, from "sentient beings" onward, second, three lines make clear that sentient beings lacked the capacity for it; and next, from "I then" onward, third, two lines make clear his reflection that brought the great transformation to rest.

「始坐道場」者，至理無時，假時化物，為化之初故言始也。事釋者，初在此處修治得道故言道場，坐此樹下得三菩提故名道樹，感樹恩故觀察，念地德故經行，道成賽澤之時，欲以大法擬宜眾生也。

As for "first sat at the place of enlightenment": the ultimate principle knows no time, but he borrowed time in order to transform beings, and, this being the beginning of that transformation, it is called "first." According to the explanation by events: he first cultivated and attained the path in this very place, and hence it is called "the place of enlightenment"; sitting beneath this tree he attained the three kinds of awakening, and hence it is called "the tree of enlightenment"; being moved by his debt of gratitude to the tree he contemplated it, and, mindful of the virtue of the ground, he walked back and forth upon it — and at the moment his path reached its consummation, its fruits ripe and its blessings spread abroad, he wished to bring the Great Dharma to fitness for sentient beings.

觀心釋者，樹即十二因緣之大樹也，深觀緣起自成菩提，欲以無漏法林樹蔭益眾生，故言「觀樹」。「經行」者，大乘三十七品是行道法，自以道品履一切地得成佛道，欲以此法化度眾生，是故起行。樹地無有分別，豈須報恩？

According to the explanation by the contemplation of mind: the tree is precisely the great tree of the twelve links of dependent origination; contemplating deeply the arising of conditions, he himself accomplished bodhi, and, wishing to shelter and benefit sentient beings with the forest of the undefiled dharma, this is called "contemplating the tree." As for "walking back and forth": the thirty-seven factors of the Great Vehicle are the dharmas of practicing the path — treading every ground by means of these factors of the path, he himself accomplished the buddha-path, and, wishing to transform and liberate sentient beings by means of this dharma, he therefore rose and walked. Tree and ground make no distinction between themselves — how, then, could gratitude need to be repaid?

《未曾有經》云：「秖以通化傳法，名報恩耳。」《過去因果經》云：「佛成道初一七日，思惟我法妙無能受者；二七日思眾生上、中、下根；三七日思惟誰應先聞法。即至波羅柰，為五人說四諦，陳如得法眼淨，頞髀拔提、十力迦葉、摩訶男、拘利未得道，佛重說四諦，四人得法眼淨。佛又說五陰無常、苦、空、非我，五人得阿羅漢。佛為佛寶，四諦為法寶，五人及佛是六阿羅漢，即是僧寶。」

The Sūtra of Unprecedented Wonders says: "It is only by means of universally transforming beings and transmitting the Dharma that this is called repaying gratitude." The Sūtra of Past Causes and Effects says: "In the first week after the Buddha accomplished the path, he reflected that his Dharma was subtle and profound, with none able to receive it; in the second week he reflected upon the superior, middling, and inferior faculties of sentient beings; and in the third week he reflected upon who ought to hear the Dharma first. He then went to Vārāṇasī and taught the four truths to the five men. Kauṇḍinya attained the pure dharma-eye, while Aśvajit, Bhadrika, Daśabala-Kāśyapa, and Mahānāma-Kulika had not yet attained the path; the Buddha taught the four truths once more, and the four men attained the pure dharma-eye. The Buddha then further taught that the five aggregates are impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, and the five men attained arhatship. The Buddha is the jewel of the Buddha, the four truths are the jewel of the Dharma, and the five men together with the Buddha, being six arhats, are precisely the jewel of the Saṅgha."

小雲疏云：「初三七日時已是說《法華》。下文宿王華智佛在七寶菩提樹下說《法華經》，當知今佛在菩提樹亦說《法華》，而鈍根眾生不堪，允同諸佛開三教化，後於王城說一乘耳。」若推智者意，是則先在菩提樹下說於佛慧，後在餘處說佛慧，例如今佛先說《華嚴》後說《法華》，故文云：「始見我身，聞我所說，入如來慧。」除先修習學小乘者，而今亦令入如來慧，與此義同也。

The Xiaoyun commentary says: "Already, in the course of these first three weeks, the Lotus was being taught. The text below tells how the Buddha King of the Constellation-Flower Wisdom, beneath the seven-jeweled tree of enlightenment, taught the Lotus Sūtra — one should understand from this that the present Buddha, too, beneath the tree of enlightenment, was likewise teaching the Lotus, but that beings of dull faculties could not bear it, and so, in keeping with the other buddhas, he opened the transforming teaching of the three, only afterward, in the royal city, teaching the one vehicle." If one traces out the Great Master's own intent, however, it is rather that he first, beneath the tree of enlightenment, taught the buddha's wisdom, and only afterward, elsewhere, taught the buddha's wisdom again — just as the present Buddha first taught the Avataṃsaka and only afterward taught the Lotus. Hence the text says: "From the moment they first saw my body and heard what I taught, they all entered into the wisdom of the Tathāgata" — excepting only those who had formerly cultivated and studied the Lesser Vehicle, who are now likewise caused to enter the wisdom of the Tathāgata; the meaning is the same as this.

五比丘者，諸女聽仙人說法，惡生王瞋割兩臂、耳、鼻等血變為乳。惡生王者拘鄰是，仙人者佛是，佛誓令得甘露，令初聞法音也。

As for the five bhikṣus: certain women were listening to a seer teach the Dharma, and King Evil-Born, in his fury, cut off the seer's two arms, ears, and nose, whereupon the blood was transformed into milk. King Evil-Born is none other than Kauṇḍinya in a former life, and the seer is none other than the Buddha in a former life; the Buddha vowed to cause him to attain the nectar of immortality, and this brought about his first hearing the sound of the Dharma.

Reading note: A few names in this passage resist certain identification. "King Evil-Born" translates 惡生王 by its plain sense rather than guessing at a Sanskrit original, since the story — a jealous king mutilating a teaching seer whose blood turns to milk — is the familiar Kṣāntivādin (Sage of Patience) tale usually told of King Kalirāja; here Zhiyi identifies the seer as a past life of the Buddha and the king as a past life of Kauṇḍinya. The four companions of Kauṇḍinya among the original five ascetics (頞髀拔提、十力迦葉、摩訶男、拘利) are rendered here as Aśvajit, Bhadrika, Daśabala-Kāśyapa, and Mahānāma-Kulika, following the standard identifications of the group of five, though the Chinese transliterations admit some variation across sources. "The Xiaoyun commentary" (literally "Small Cloud") is cited by Zhiyi only this once in the surviving text; its author is not otherwise identified here.

Why the Buddha First Taught the Five 何故初為五人轉法輪

Chinese Source

問：
何故初為五人轉法輪？

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Why did he first turn the wheel of the Dharma for the five men?

答：
人先見諦故、人是現見故、人為證故、佛所行事業與人同故，諸天從人中得善利故、人中有四眾故。

Answer: Because it is human beings who first behold the truths; because human beings are directly present to be seen; because human beings serve as witnesses; because the undertakings the Buddha carries out are the same as those of human beings; because the gods obtain their good benefit by way of human beings; and because it is among human beings that the fourfold assembly exists.

輪王出世聲至他化自在，憍陳如得道聲至梵天，佛得道聲至首陀會，何故爾？

When a wheel-turning king comes into the world, the sound of it reaches as far as the Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven; when Kauṇḍinya attained the path, the sound of it reached as far as the Brahmā heaven; and when the Buddha attained the path, the sound of it reached as far as the Śuddhāvāsa heavens — why is this so?

答：善業、名譽業、稱讚父母師長業，有上中下故爾也。若使有頂有耳識者，佛聲亦至彼。輪王行十善，善生欲天，欲天喜我眷屬增多故；陳如離欲故徹梵；佛最勝至尼吒(云云)。若依大乘，佛得道聲遍至百億尼吒，又遍十方無量無邊世界尼吒(云云)。

Answer: This is because wholesome karma, karma that brings renown, and karma that draws praise from parents and teachers each come in superior, middling, and inferior grades. If there were an ear-consciousness present even at the summit of existence, the sound of the Buddha would reach there too. The wheel-turning king practices the ten wholesome deeds, and wholesome deeds give rise to birth in the heavens of desire, the gods of desire rejoicing that their following has thereby increased; Kauṇḍinya, having left desire behind, reached through to the Brahmā heaven; and the Buddha, being most supreme of all, reached as far as Akaniṣṭha, and so on. According to the Great Vehicle, the sound of the Buddha's attainment of the path reaches pervasively to a hundred koṭis of Akaniṣṭha heavens, and further pervades the Akaniṣṭha heavens of the immeasurable, boundless worlds of the ten directions, and so on.

The Three Weeks of Contemplation 三七日：初七思法說，次七思譬說，後七思因緣說

Chinese Source

初轉法輪處、菩提樹處、初忉利下處、大神變處，此四處諸佛皆定，餘處不定。又除轉法輪一處，其三處決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

The place of the first turning of the Dharma wheel, the place of the tree of enlightenment, the place of the first descent to Trāyastriṃśa, and the place of the great supernatural transformation — these four places are fixed alike for every buddha, while all other places are not fixed. And, setting aside the one place of the turning of the Dharma wheel, the other three places are determined.

「三七日」者，舊云「思理教等」，又云「勸誡等」。瑤師云：「事之至深，至聖猶思而後行。一七思佛智微妙，二七思眾生根性不同，三七思法藥萬品，即舉偈證之：『我所得智慧，微妙最第一，眾生諸根鈍，云何而可度？』」

As for "the three [periods of] seven days": the old interpretation says this refers to "reflecting upon principle, upon the teaching," and so forth, or again to "urging and admonishing," and so forth. Master Yao says: "A matter of the utmost profundity is one which even the utmost sage must reflect upon before acting. In the first week he reflected upon the Buddha's wisdom, subtle and wondrous; in the second week he reflected upon the differing faculties and natures of sentient beings; and in the third week he reflected upon the ten thousand kinds of dharma-medicine" — and he cites a verse in proof of this: "The wisdom I have attained is subtle and supreme; the faculties of sentient beings are all dull — how, then, may they be brought across?"

今明佛在法身之地寂而常照，恒以佛眼洞覽無遺，豈始至道場淹留三七方思此事？言三七者，明有所表也，表佛初欲三周說法，故假言三七耳。

The present explanation makes clear that the Buddha, dwelling in the ground of the dharma-body, is ever still and yet ever illuminating, constantly surveying all things without omission by means of the buddha-eye — how, then, could it be that only upon first arriving at the place of enlightenment did he linger for three weeks before reflecting upon this matter? The mention of "three sevens" makes clear that something is being represented thereby: it represents the Buddha's original intention to teach in three rounds, and hence the expression "three sevens" is only a provisional manner of speaking.

初七思法說，次七思譬說，後七思因緣說，皆無機不得，是故息大施小也，此偏就圓教大乘為釋耳。若通途約大乘釋者，初七思惟欲說圓教大乘，次七思惟欲說別教，後七思惟欲說通教大乘，皆無機不得，是故息大說三藏三乘，為方便之化也。

In the first seven days he reflected on the direct-teaching exposition; in the next seven days he reflected on the parable teaching; and in the last seven days he reflected on the causal-condition teaching — in each case finding no capacity to receive it and being unable to teach it, and hence the Great Vehicle was set to rest and the Lesser bestowed instead; this, however, is an explanation made specifically in terms of the perfect teaching of the Great Vehicle. If one explains it in the general manner, with reference to the Great Vehicle as a whole, then in the first seven days he reflected on his wish to teach the perfect teaching of the Great Vehicle, in the next seven days he reflected on his wish to teach the distinct teaching, and in the last seven days he reflected on his wish to teach the shared teaching of the Great Vehicle — in each case finding no capacity to receive it and being unable to teach it, and hence the Great Vehicle was set to rest and the three vehicles of the Tripiṭaka teaching were taught instead, as a transformation by skillful means.

觀心釋者，初欲觀中道，中道妙難觀不得；次欲觀即假，即假觀分別智難生不得；後欲觀即空，即空巧度又不得，方觀方便析法小觀也。

According to the explanation by the contemplation of mind: at first he wished to contemplate the middle way, but the middle way is subtle and difficult to contemplate, and he could not do so; next he wished to contemplate the provisional as such, but the discerning wisdom of the contemplation of the provisional as such is difficult to bring forth, and he could not do so; and last he wished to contemplate the empty as such, but the skillful crossing-over of the contemplation of the empty as such likewise could not be attained — and only then did he contemplate the lesser contemplation that analyzes dharmas by skillful means.

Setting the Great Vehicle to Rest 眾生諸根鈍：明無機、明念息化

Chinese Source

從「眾生諸根鈍」下，第二，三行，明無機。又為三：初，半行，明障重；次「如斯」下，第二，半行，明不堪聞；「爾時梵王」者下，第三，有二行，明諸梵雖請說，大佛知無機，所以不說。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "the faculties of sentient beings are all dull" onward is, second, three lines making clear the absence of capacity. This again falls into three: first, half a line makes clear the heaviness of their obstruction; next, from "such as this" onward, second, half a line makes clear their inability to bear hearing it; and next, from "at that time the Brahmā kings" onward, third, two lines make clear that, though the various Brahmā kings requested that he teach, the great Buddha, knowing there was no capacity for it, therefore did not teach.

「我即自思惟」下，第二，有二行，明念欲息化，又二：初，一行半，明無機強說，聞則有損；後，半行，正明息化。

From "I then reflected within myself" onward is, second, two lines making clear his reflection and his wish to set the transformation to rest; this again falls into two: first, one-and-a-half lines make clear that, were he to force himself to teach in the absence of capacity, hearing it would only cause harm; and second, half a line directly makes clear the setting of the transformation to rest.

Reflecting on the Buddhas of the Past 尋念過去佛：於一佛乘方便說三

Chinese Source

從「尋念過去佛」下，第二，有十一行，頌上「於一佛乘方便說三」也。就此為二：初，十行，正明化得；後，一行，釋疑。就前十行有四：初，一行，明三乘擬宜；次「作是思」下，第二，六行半，明有機；次「思惟是」下，第三，一行半，明施化；次「是名」下，第四，一行，明受行。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "reflecting upon the past buddhas" onward is, second, eleven lines versifying the earlier statement, "within the one buddha-vehicle, by skillful means, he taught it as three." This falls into two: first, ten lines directly make clear the transformation attained; and second, one line resolves a doubt. Within the former ten lines there are four parts: first, one line makes clear the seeking of fitness by means of the three vehicles; next, from "making this reflection" onward, second, six-and-a-half lines make clear that capacity was present; next, from "reflecting on this" onward, third, one-and-a-half lines make clear the bestowal of the transformation; and next, from "this is called" onward, fourth, one line makes clear the accepting and practicing of it.

「尋念」者，念彼雖無大機，不容永捨，要以方便而誘濟之，非都不知開三，欲引同諸佛，故云尋念也。

As for "reflecting upon": though he reflected that they lacked the capacity for the Great Vehicle, he could not bear to abandon them forever, and needed to entice and deliver them by skillful means; it is not that he was wholly unaware of the opening of the three, but that, wishing to draw them into accord with the other buddhas, this is called "reflecting upon."

「作是思惟」下，第二，六行半，明有小機。此又為二：初，四行半，明諸佛歎；後，二行，明釋迦酬順。上欲大化，於彼無機，故諸佛不歎；今欲說小曲會根緣，則始終得度，所以佛歎也。

From "making this reflection" onward is, second, six-and-a-half lines making clear that a lesser capacity was present. This again falls into two: first, four-and-a-half lines make clear the praise of the other buddhas; and second, two lines make clear Śākyamuni's response in accord with them. Above, when he wished to transform beings by the Great Vehicle, they had no capacity for it, and hence the other buddhas offered no praise; but now, wishing to teach the Lesser in a way that bends to meet their roots and conditions, they will in the end and from the beginning be delivered, and this is why the buddhas praise him.

The Buddhas' Praise, and Śākyamuni's Response 諸佛歎、釋迦酬順：南無

Chinese Source

就初佛歎為五：初，三句，釋迦自敘諸佛現。「佛現」者，由念佛方便力故現，現由擬法、會機二義故佛現。「善哉」下，第二，一行一句，明諸佛正歎釋迦，能隱實設權，故云善哉。為一施三引入佛慧，即是「第一導師」。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the first, the praise of the buddhas, there are five parts: first, three phrases in which Śākyamuni himself relates the appearing of the other buddhas. As for "the buddhas appeared": they appeared by the power of his skillful reflecting upon the buddhas, and this appearing came about for two reasons — seeking fitness for the Dharma, and bringing it into accord with capacity. From "excellent" onward, second, one line and one phrase make clear the other buddhas' direct praise of Śākyamuni, who is able to conceal the real and set forth the provisional, and hence they say "excellent." Bestowing the one as three in order to draw beings into the buddha's wisdom is precisely what is meant by "the foremost guide."

「得是無上法」者，即是得實智微妙第一也。「而用方便力」者，隨諸一切佛，隱實用權也。「我等亦皆得」下，第三一行，明諸佛亦隱實用權，如文。

"Having attained this unsurpassed Dharma" means precisely having attained the real wisdom, subtle and supreme. "And yet employing the power of skillful means" means that, in accordance with each and every buddha, he conceals the real and makes use of the provisional. From "we too have all attained" onward, third, one line makes clear that the other buddhas likewise conceal the real and make use of the provisional, as the text states.

從「少智樂小法」下，第四，一行，雙釋二義，為眾生少智不堪聞大所以隱實，而復樂小所以施權。「雖復說三」下，第五，半行，雙結二義，雖復說三，終為顯實也。

From "those of little wisdom, delighting in the lesser dharmas" onward, fourth, one line explains both meanings at once: it is because sentient beings of little wisdom cannot bear to hear the Great that the real is concealed, and because they delight in the lesser that the provisional is bestowed. From "though he has also taught the three" onward, fifth, half a line concludes both meanings at once: though he taught the three, this was in the end for the sake of revealing the real.

「舍利弗當知」下，第二，有二行，明釋迦酬順。既聞諸佛歎，對曰「南無」，「南無」此云敬從，又二：初，一行，發言酬順；後，一行，念順物機。

From "Śāriputra, you should know" onward is, second, two lines making clear Śākyamuni's response in accord with them. Having heard the other buddhas' praise, he responds by saying "Namo" — "Namo" here meaning "reverently to submit"; this again falls into two: first, one line in which he utters the words of his response in accord; and second, one line in which he reflects on according with the capacity of beings.

從「思惟是事」下，第三，一行半，正明施教也。「諸法寂滅相，不可以言宣」下，即是前說中道無性佛種之理，此理非數又不可說，今以方便作三乘說；又非生非滅，而以方便作生滅說；又偏真之理亦非示說，以方便故作四門說。初為五人說無常，有門也。

From "reflecting on this matter" onward is, third, one-and-a-half lines directly making clear the bestowal of the teaching. From "the mark of the quiescent extinction of all dharmas cannot be proclaimed in words" onward is precisely the principle, spoken of earlier, of the buddha-seed of the middle way that is without fixed nature: this principle, being beyond number and beyond speech, is now, by skillful means, made into a teaching of three vehicles; being also neither arisen nor extinguished, it is by skillful means made into a teaching of arising and extinction; and the principle of the one-sided real likewise admits of no direct showing, and so, by skillful means, it is made into a teaching of the four approaches. His first teaching of impermanence, for the five men, is the approach of being.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the Parable Chapter. First, the general explanation: "parable" means comparison and analogy, and "illustration" means instruction and clarification — relying on this to compare with that, borrowing the shallow to instruct in the deep. Earlier, the prose and its verses concerning the five buddhas in general were fully set forth; those of superior faculties and sharp wisdom attained awakening on hearing it in its fullness, but those of the middling and lesser sort remained caught in confusion, not yet released from it — and so, his great compassion never resting, his skillful wisdom without bound, he moved the tree to teach of the wind and raised the fan to illustrate the moon, causing them thereby to awaken and understand, and hence it is called "parable and illustration."

As for the specific explanation: worldly dharmas are compared with world-transcending dharmas, so that, by way of what they have already experienced, they hear of what they have never before experienced, and leap up in joyful delight — as in the sūtra's comparing a worldly father and son with the world-transcending teacher and disciple. Further, worldly arising-dharmas are compared with world-transcending arising-dharmas, so that, receiving the Buddha's voice and teaching, they do not lose the Great Vehicle — as in the sūtra's telling how the father knows that each of his sons, from the first, has had his own fondness for particular playthings and treasures. Further, worldly extinction is compared with world-transcending extinction, so that, though they have already attained the undefiled, hearing it further removes their grief and vexation — as in the sūtra's telling how, "being their father, I should draw them out from their calamity of suffering and cause them to escape the burning."

Further, worldly non-arising and non-extinction are compared with world-transcending non-arising and non-extinction, so that they may abide securely within real wisdom, knowing "I shall surely become a buddha" — as in the sūtra's telling how they "ride upon this jeweled vehicle straight to the place of enlightenment." One should understand that the Buddha, with a single voice, taught by way of parable and illustration, skillfully causing those of middling and lesser capacity to obtain the benefit of the four siddhāntas — and hence it is called "the Parable Chapter."

Śāriputra's Three Joys 身喜、口喜、意喜

◎從「長者見是大火」下，是第二，別譬也。別更為四：初、長者見火譬，譬上佛見五濁，四行偈為本；二、捨机用車譬，譬上釋迦為五濁寢大施小始坐道場，十七行半偈為本；三、等賜諸子大車譬，譬上釋迦示真實相，我見佛子等志求佛道者，六行偈為本；四、長者無虛妄譬，譬上我為諸法王，二行半偈為本。就初見火，其文有四，其意但三：一、明能見；二、明所見；三、明驚怖；四、廣前所見。但成三意，「長者見」標出能見，譬上我以佛眼觀見也；「是大火從四面起」者，標出所見，譬上所見六道眾生也；「即大驚怖」，譬上為是眾生故而起大悲心也。「而諸子等於火宅內」下，廣第二所見之火也，還是釋成驚怖之義。身、受、心、法，即宅之四邊，從此四邊，起淨、樂等四倒，八苦之火眾苦皆集，若知身不淨、苦、無常，即煩惱火滅。舊有三解：一云四大為四面，六識並託其中；二即四生；三云四倒。依下文，以生、老、病、死為四邊也。「即大驚怖」者，念其退大善故驚，憂其將起重惡故怖；驚即對慈，念其無樂；怖即對悲，憂其有苦。「我雖能於此所燒之門安隱得出」者，即是釋成驚怖慈悲之義，雖是未盡之辭，明佛以智慧力能尋正教見所詮諦，不為五濁、八苦所危故名「安」，四倒暴風所不能動故名「隱」，蕭然累外故名「得出」。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "the elder sees this great fire" onward is the second of the four parts of the parable — the specific parable. This too falls into four: first, the parable of the elder seeing the fire, which parallels the Buddha seeing the five turbidities above, taking the four lines of verse as its basis; second, the parable of setting aside the bench to use the cart, which parallels Śākyamuni, on account of the five turbidities, holding back the great vehicle and bestowing the lesser upon first taking his seat at the site of enlightenment, taking the seventeen and a half lines of verse as its basis; third, the parable of equally bestowing the great cart upon every child, which parallels Śākyamuni showing the mark of true reality — "I see the buddha's children, those whose resolve seeks the buddha-path" — taking the six lines of verse as its basis; fourth, the parable of the elder's freedom from falsehood, which parallels "I am the king of all dharmas" above, taking the two and a half lines of verse as its basis. As for the first, seeing the fire: the text falls into four parts, though its sense comes to only three — first, making clear the ability to see; second, making clear what is seen; third, making clear the terror that arises; fourth, extending what was seen before. But these amount, in sense, to only three points: "the elder sees" marks out the ability to see, paralleling "I, with the buddha-eye, observe and see" above; "this great fire arising on every side" marks out what is seen, paralleling the sentient beings of the six destinies seen above; and "and so he was greatly terrified" parallels "and so he gave rise to a mind of great compassion for the sake of these sentient beings" above. From "and yet his children remained within the burning house" onward extends the second point, what is seen of the fire, and this in turn explains and completes the sense of the terror. Body, sensation, mind, and dharmas are the four sides of the house; from these four sides arise the four inversions — of purity, of pleasure, and the rest — and the fire of the eight forms of suffering, in which every kind of suffering gathers; but if one knows that the body is impure, is suffering, and is impermanent, then the fire of affliction is extinguished. There have been three older explanations: one says that the four great elements are the four sides, with the six consciousnesses lodged within them; a second says they are the four modes of birth; a third says they are the four inversions. Following the text below, birth, old age, sickness, and death are taken as the four sides. As for "and so he was greatly terrified": he is startled at the thought that they are retrogressing from the great good, and is fearful with the worry that grave evil is about to arise in them — the startlement corresponds to loving-kindness, being mindful that they have no joy, while the fear corresponds to compassion, worrying that they have suffering. "Though I am able to come safely out through this burning gate" is what explains and completes the meaning of this terror and this compassion; though the phrase does not yet exhaust the sense, it makes clear that the Buddha, by the power of wisdom, is able to seek out the correct teaching and see the truth it expresses — because he is not endangered by the five turbidities or the eight forms of suffering, he is called "safe"; because he cannot be shaken by the fierce winds of the four inversions, he is called "secure"; and because he stands serenely free of all entanglement, he is called "coming out."

而眾生不爾，為火所燒，如來慈悲猶為憂火所熾，故言「雖」也。

Sentient beings, however, are not so — they are burned by the fire, and the Tathāgata's compassion, correspondingly, is inflamed by the fire of his own concern for them; hence the text says "though."

經言所燒之門者，今問：「教為門者，此教為燒？為不燒？」救云：「教門不燒，佛教為門，能通所燒之人，所通之人被燒，名能通門名燒，如門內人死，名門為衰，門實不衰。」又問：「若爾，教是常住，非有為法；若不爾，何故不燒？」今解：「不爾。夫門有件有空，非件無以標門，非空無以通致，件可灰燼，空不可燒。教有能詮、所詮，若非詮辯無以為教，若非所詮何以得出？詮辯可是無常，所詮非復無常，得教下所詮故，名安隱得出；能詮磨滅故，言所燒之門。不從所燒之門，何由安隱得出？

As to the sūtra's speaking of "the burning gate": one may ask, "if the teaching itself is the gate, is this teaching burned, or is it not burned?" The response is: "the gate of the teaching is not burned. The Buddha's teaching serves as the gate, able to convey through it the person who is burned; the person conveyed through it is burned, and so the gate that conveys is spoken of as burned — just as, when a person inside a gate dies, the gate itself is spoken of as inauspicious, though the gate has not actually declined." One may ask further: "if that is so, then the teaching is permanent, not a conditioned dharma — but if it is not so, then why is it not burned?" The explanation here is: "it is not so. A gate has both substance and empty space; without substance there would be nothing to mark out the gate, and without the empty space there would be nothing through which to pass and arrive — the substance can be reduced to ash, but the empty space cannot be burned. The teaching, too, has both what expresses and what is expressed: without the power to express and expound, there would be nothing to serve as the teaching, and without what is expressed, how could one ever come out? The power to express and expound may indeed be impermanent, but what is expressed is not, in turn, impermanent — because one attains, beneath the teaching, what is expressed, this is called coming out safely and securely, and because the power to express is subject to dissolution, this is called the burning gate. Were it not for this burning gate, how could one ever come out safely and securely?

藉於言教契於所詮，《大經》云：『因無常故而果是常』，如此釋者，如經於所燒之門也。若小乘無常教門，此從所燒門出；若大乘常住教門，文字即解脫者，此教即理，體達燒無燒而安隱得出。若就如來權智，即是從所燒門出；若就實智，體於所燒安隱得出，故先作衣裓几案出之不得，後以無常出之，即此意也。」

It is by relying on the spoken teaching that one comes into accord with what it expresses; the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, 'because the cause is impermanent, the fruit is permanent' — an explanation of this kind is exactly what the sūtra means by 'the burning gate.' If it is the impermanent gate of the teaching of the lesser vehicle, one comes out through this burning gate; if it is the permanent, abiding gate of the teaching of the great vehicle, in which the very words are themselves liberation, then this teaching is itself identical with principle, and, embodying the truth that burning is itself no burning, one comes out safely and securely. In terms of the Tathāgata's provisional wisdom, one comes out through the burning gate; in terms of his real wisdom, one embodies the very burning itself and comes out safely and securely — and this is exactly the sense in which the father first tries to bring the children out with the flower-basket and the low bench and fails, and only afterward brings them out by means of impermanence."

「樂著嬉戲」，著見名嬉，著愛名戲；又耽湎四見名嬉，唐喪其功名戲，著愛亦爾；耽湎五塵名嬉，空無所獲名戲，空生徒死而無厭離，如彼兒戲。「不覺不知」者，都不言有火名不覺，不解火是熱法名不知，既不知火熱，不畏傷身，名不驚，不慮斷命故不怖，眾生全不覺五陰、八苦，不知四倒、三毒，既不識惑云何憂慮？惑侵法身傷於慧命，如是不覺於苦、不知於集、不驚傷道、不怖失滅，以不聞四諦教則無聞慧名不覺，不得思慧名不知；不得見解名不覺，不得思惟解名不知。見諦即驚悟，思惟即厭怖。又不覺現在苦，不知未來苦，故下文云「現受眾苦，後受地獄等苦」即此義。

"Delighting in and absorbed in play": attachment to views is called "delighting," and attachment to craving is called "play"; or again, indulgence in the four wrong views is called "delighting," while squandering one's efforts in vain is called "play," and it is the same with attachment to craving; indulgence in the five sense-objects is called "delighting," while gaining nothing at all from emptiness is called "play" — being born into emptiness and dying pointlessly, with never any weariness or wish to leave it, exactly like the play of a child. As for "neither perceiving nor knowing": not so much as speaking of there being a fire at all is called "not perceiving"; not understanding that fire is by nature something hot is called "not knowing." Since they do not know the fire is hot, they do not fear being harmed by it, and this is called "not startled"; since they give no thought to their lives being cut short, they are "not afraid." Sentient beings entirely fail to perceive the five aggregates and the eight forms of suffering, and do not know the four inversions and the three poisons — not even recognizing delusion itself, how could they be troubled by it? Delusion invades the dharma-body and injures the life of wisdom, and in just this way they neither perceive suffering nor know its origin, are neither startled at harm to the path nor afraid of the loss of cessation. Because they have not heard the teaching of the four noble truths, they lack the wisdom born of hearing, and this is called "not perceiving"; because they have not attained the wisdom born of reflection, this is called "not knowing"; because they have not attained the understanding born of insight, this is called "not perceiving"; and because they have not attained the understanding born of contemplation, this is called "not knowing." Seeing the truths brings startled awakening; contemplating them brings weariness and fear. Moreover, they neither perceive the suffering of the present nor know the suffering of the future — hence the text below says, "now undergoing every kind of suffering, and afterward undergoing the sufferings of hell and the rest" — this is exactly that meaning.

「逼身」者，五識也；心者，意識，心王也。身為八苦所逼而心不厭惱也。亦云曾種大乘功德，是法身智慧為體，體為四倒所逼，而不知不覺。「心不厭患」者，不厭無常之苦，不患煩惱之集也。

"Pressing upon the body" refers to the five sense-consciousnesses; "mind" refers to the mental consciousness, the sovereign mind. The body is pressed upon by the eight forms of suffering, and yet the mind feels no weariness or distress at this. It is also said that, having formerly planted the merit of the great vehicle, one's very essence is the wisdom of the dharma-body, and this essence, pressed upon by the four inversions, remains without knowing or perceiving. "The mind feels no weariness or distress" means that one is not wearied by the suffering of impermanence, nor distressed by the arising of affliction.

「無求出意」者，不修道求滅也。今謂火宅本譬五濁，嬉譬見濁，戲譬煩惱濁，不覺不知、不驚不怖譬眾生濁，火來逼身苦痛切已譬命濁，心不厭患無求出意譬劫濁，此與五濁相當(云云)。

"Having no intention of seeking to come out" means not cultivating the path in search of cessation. Here, then, the burning house fundamentally corresponds to the five turbidities: "delighting" corresponds to the turbidity of views; "play" corresponds to the turbidity of affliction; "neither perceiving nor knowing, neither startled nor afraid" corresponds to the turbidity of sentient beings; the fire coming to press upon the body, its pain already pressing close, corresponds to the turbidity of lifespan; and the mind's feeling no weariness or distress, and having no intention of seeking to come out, corresponds to the turbidity of the eon — and this matches the five turbidities point for point, and so on.

Setting Aside the Bench for the Cart: Persuasion and Admonition 捨机用車譬：勸門與誡門

Chinese Source

從「是長者作是思惟」下，是第二，捨几用車譬，譬上寢大施小，上六行半，明大擬不得；後十一行，用小擬得。上不得有三：一、思大擬宜；二、無機；三、息化。今譬為二：初、用勸門擬宜；二、用誡門擬宜。就勸、誡各三：一、擬宜；二、不受；三、放捨。勸門三者：一、從「長者作是思惟身手有力」下，譬上念用大化於三七日中思惟如此事；二、從「復更思惟」下，明子不受，譬上無機眾生諸根鈍云何而可度；三、從「或當墮落為火所燒」下，即是放捨善誘，譬上無機息化我寧不說法，疾入於涅槃也。「長者作是思惟」下，譬上三七日思惟也。「身手」等者，引下合譬云「但以神力及智慧力」以釋此譬，身譬神通荷負，手譬智慧提拔，依三昧斷德則有神通，依智慧智德則有說法，智斷之力能成法身，此之智斷還從勸、誡兩門入，勸即為人悉檀，誡即對治悉檀，此二悉檀，為第一義悉檀而作方便。如來初欲勸門擬宜眾生令眾善奉行成就十力無畏一切種智，而眾生不堪；次欲以誡門擬宜令諸惡莫作證大涅槃，眾生不堪無機息化，故知念用大乘，秖是勸、誡兩悉檀，神通、智斷耳，故上文云「定慧力莊嚴以此度眾生」即其義也。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "at that time the elder had this thought" onward is the second, the parable of setting aside the low bench and using the cart, which parallels the holding back of the great vehicle and the bestowal of the lesser above: the first six and a half lines concern the great vehicle, contemplated but not attained; the following eleven lines concern the lesser vehicle, contemplated and attained. The "not attained" above falls into three — first, contemplating whether the great vehicle is fitting; second, the absence of the capacity for it; third, the ceasing of the transforming activity. The parable here likewise falls into two: first, employing the gate of persuasion to gauge its fitness; second, employing the gate of admonition to gauge its fitness. Within persuasion and admonition, each in turn falls into three — contemplating its fitness, its not being accepted, and its being set aside. As for the three points of the persuasion gate: first, from "the elder had this thought: my body and hands have strength" onward parallels the earlier contemplation, over three sets of seven days, of using the great vehicle in just this way; second, from "and thought further still" onward makes clear that the children do not accept it, paralleling how sentient beings lacking the capacity, their faculties dull, could ever be brought across; third, from "or they may fall and be burned by the fire" onward is precisely the setting-aside of this well-intentioned enticement, paralleling how, the beings lacking the capacity, the transforming activity ceases, and I might as well not teach the Dharma at all, but swiftly enter nirvāṇa. "The elder had this thought" corresponds to the earlier contemplation over the three sets of seven days. As for "body and hands" and the like: citing what follows in the matching correlation, which says "by spiritual power and the power of wisdom alone," this explains the present parable — the body corresponds to spiritual powers that can bear a burden, and the hands correspond to wisdom that can lift one up; by the virtue of severance through samādhi there is spiritual power, and by the virtue of wisdom there is the teaching of the Dharma, and the power of wisdom and severance is able to accomplish the dharma-body. This wisdom and severance in turn enters by way of the two gates of persuasion and admonition: persuasion is the individual siddhānta, and admonition is the therapeutic siddhānta, and these two siddhāntas serve as the skillful means for the siddhānta of ultimate meaning. When the Tathāgata first wished, through the gate of persuasion, to gauge whether sentient beings were fit to undertake every good deed and accomplish the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, and the wisdom of all modes, sentient beings were unable to bear it; when he next wished, through the gate of admonition, to gauge whether they were fit to refrain from every evil deed and realize great nirvāṇa, sentient beings were likewise unable to bear it, lacking the capacity, and so the transforming activity ceased — hence it is to be understood that the contemplated use of the great vehicle was simply these two siddhāntas of persuasion and admonition, spiritual power and the power of wisdom and severance, and this is exactly the meaning of the earlier text, "adorned with the power of concentration and wisdom, by this he brings sentient beings across."

前歎長者，其年衰邁即譬智斷，智斷即是身手力也。「衣裓几案」者，三藏法師云，衣裓是外國盛花之器，貢上貴人用此貯之。

The earlier praise of the elder, that his years were advanced and his strength in decline, corresponds precisely to wisdom and severance, and wisdom and severance are exactly the strength of his body and hands. As for "the flower-basket, the low bench, and the table": the Master of the Tripiṭaka says that the flower-basket is a vessel used in foreign lands to hold flowers, presented as tribute to persons of rank, who use it to store things.

舊云：「衣襟譬大乘因，几案譬大乘果，初擬大乘因果，是則無機也。」舊又云：「此物譬大乘戒、定、慧，初七思惟所得法，此如用衣裓；二七思惟眾生根緣，如用几；三七思惟樹地恩，如用案。」云此義出《阿含經》。今取合譬文，若我但以神力及智慧力，讚如來知見、力、無所畏者，眾生不能以此得度。神力即是身，慧力即是手，如前說，知見譬衣裓，無畏譬几，十力譬案，如來以神通，發動此三法，以智慧宣說此三法無機息化。衣裓几案等，略、中、廣之異耳。略說名如來知見，知即一切種智，見即佛眼，名略義玄，譬如衣裓一足而多含。處中說，即名四無所畏，用對四諦如几，於法小廣，於物小安隱。或作廣說名為十力，橫豎該括，如案多足則無傾覆也，於法則廣物則大安，於三七日中思惟，欲作如此廣略佛法，而眾生不堪，故言衣裓几案也。

The older explanation says: "the garment-flap — the flower-basket — corresponds to the cause of the great vehicle, and the table corresponds to its fruit; contemplating first the cause and fruit of the great vehicle is precisely what has no capacity for it." The older explanation also says: "these objects correspond to the great vehicle's precepts, concentration, and wisdom — the dharma attained by contemplation through the first seven days is like the use of the flower-basket; contemplating the root-conditions of sentient beings through the second seven days is like the use of the bench; and contemplating the debt owed the tree and the earth through the third seven days is like the use of the table," and this sense, it is said, derives from an Āgama sūtra. Taking now the text of the matching correlation: "if I were to praise the Tathāgata's knowledge and vision, power, and fearlessness by spiritual power and the power of wisdom alone, sentient beings could not be brought across by this" — spiritual power is exactly the body, and the power of wisdom is exactly the hands, as explained before; knowledge and vision correspond to the flower-basket, fearlessness corresponds to the bench, and the ten powers correspond to the table. The Tathāgata, by his spiritual powers, sets these three dharmas in motion, and by his wisdom proclaims these three dharmas; but, there being no capacity for them, the transforming activity ceases. The flower-basket, the bench, and the table differ only as the brief, the middling, and the extensive statement of the same thing. The brief statement is called the Tathāgata's knowledge and vision — "knowledge" is the wisdom of all modes, and "vision" is the buddha-eye — called brief because its meaning is compressed and profound, like the flower-basket, a single vessel that yet contains a great deal. The middling statement is called the four fearlessnesses, matching the four noble truths as the bench does, somewhat broader in its teaching and somewhat more settled in its object. Or, made into the extensive statement, it is called the ten powers, comprehending everything both crosswise and lengthwise, like the table with its many legs, which cannot be overturned — broader still in its teaching, and greater still in the settledness of its object. Through the contemplation of the three sets of seven days, he wished to make of the Buddha-dharma something at once this extensive and this brief, and yet sentient beings could not bear it — hence the text speaks of the flower-basket, the bench, and the table.

「復更思惟」下，第二，明子不受，譬上無機。「惟有一門而復狹小」，門義如上說。今更明通別。別者，「一」謂一理，一道清淨；「門」謂正教，通於所通；「小」謂不容斷常七方便等。教理寬博則非狹小，眾生不能以此理教自通，將談無機，故言狹小耳。

From "and thought further still" onward is, second, making clear that the children do not accept it, paralleling the absence of capacity above. As for "there is only the one gate, and it, moreover, is narrow and small": the sense of "gate" has been explained above, and here it is further clarified in general and specific terms. Taken specifically: "one" means the one principle, the one path of purity; "gate" means the correct teaching, which conveys through to what it conveys; and "small" means that it cannot accommodate the seven expedient stages that hold to eternalism or annihilationism, and the like. The teaching and its principle are, in themselves, vast and expansive, and are not narrow and small — it is only that sentient beings are unable to make their own way through by means of this principle and this teaching, and, about to speak of the absence of capacity, the text therefore calls it narrow and small.

通者，理純無雜故言「一」，即理能通故言「門」，微妙難知故言「狹小」。教者，十方諦求更無餘乘，唯一佛乘故言「一」，此教能通故言「門」，此教微妙凡夫不知出處，是不知權，不知入處，是不知實；二乘因聞少知出要，永不知入；菩薩雖自知出，亦不知入，奪七方便皆不知入出，上文云：「若我讚佛乘，眾生沒在苦。」不能以教自通，將談無機故言「狹小」。行者圓因自行，行大直道無留難故，故名為「一」，善行菩薩道，直至道場故名為「門」，妙行難行方便無機，故言「狹小」耳。舊解：人天小善故云「幼稚」，無大乘善名「未有所識」。今明二萬佛所教無上道，大乘善根微弱名「幼稚」，若聞大乘能生謗毀，名「未有所識」也。

Taken generally: since principle is pure and without admixture, it is called "one"; since it is principle that is able to convey through, it is called a "gate"; and since it is subtle and wondrous, hard to know, it is called "narrow and small." As teaching: sought throughout the ten directions, there is truly no other vehicle — only the one buddha-vehicle — hence it is called "one"; because this teaching is able to convey through, it is called a "gate"; and because this teaching is subtle and wondrous, ordinary people do not know its point of departure — this is not knowing the provisional — nor do they know its point of entry — this is not knowing the real. The two vehicles, on hearing but a little of it, know only the essential point of departure, but never know the point of entry; bodhisattvas, though they know for themselves the point of departure, likewise do not know the point of entry; and stripped of the seven expedient stages, none of them knows either entry or departure — hence the text above says, "were I to praise the buddha-vehicle, sentient beings would sink into suffering." Because they cannot make their own way through by means of the teaching, and the text is about to speak of the absence of capacity, it therefore says "narrow and small." As practice: because the perfect cause is a self-cultivated practice, practicing the great, direct path without any hindrance, it is called "one"; because one skillfully practices the bodhisattva path, going straight to the site of enlightenment, it is called a "gate"; and because this wondrous practice is difficult to practice, and there is no capacity for this skillful means, it is called "narrow and small." For my part, I would refute this both generally and point by point. "Asking" is simply another word for seeking and requesting: in the mind it is called seeking; in speech it is called requesting; in the body it is called begging — like one in the dark seeking knowledge, one who is hungry requesting food, or one who is lost asking the way. Wherever one dwells in a state not yet reached, how could there fail to be this principle of asking? It is because of the asking that the granting follows, and because of the granting that the joy follows; and the present text is furnished throughout with request, granting, and joy alike. In the dharma-teaching, the twelve hundred, with Śāriputra at their head, made their earnest threefold request; among the bodhisattvas, with Maitreya at their head, the buddha's own children, numbering in the tens of thousands, pressed their palms together with a reverent mind, wishing to hear the complete path. At the outset of the parable-teaching, Śāriputra makes his request on behalf of persons of middling faculties, and also, in general, on behalf of the fourfold assembly, and, by extension, on behalf of persons of inferior faculties as well, the text saying, "excellent, World-Honored One — we beg you to explain this causal condition for the sake of the fourfold assembly." The granting in the dharma-teaching says, "since you have now three times earnestly requested it, how could I fail to speak?" The granting in the parable-teaching says, "I shall use a parable to make this meaning still clearer." The granting through causal conditions says, "the causal conditions of my own past lives and of yours — I shall now speak of them." When the dharma-teaching was finished, Śāriputra rejoiced; when the parable-teaching was finished, Kāśyapa and the others rejoiced; and when the teaching of past-life causal conditions was finished, Pūrṇa rejoiced. Moreover, the text of the matching correlation says, "causing the children, day and night, for kalpa after kalpa, to enjoy themselves constantly, riding, together with the bodhisattvas, upon this jeweled vehicle, straight to the site of enlightenment." Because of the joy, one knows there was a granting; because of the granting, one knows there was a request — the three rounds of teaching, in all three of their meanings, make this brilliantly plain in the text itself — so why, then, should it be said, one-sidedly, that two of the three ask, and one does not?

Point by point, then: as to the first, that only the Tripiṭaka teaching makes clear that bodhisattvas do not cut off affliction — according to the Lotus Sūtra there are four cases: the obstruction removed and the great capacity moving; the obstruction not yet removed and the great capacity moving — and wherever the capacity moves, one knows there is asking. As to the second, that no sūtra of the great vehicle speaks of a bodhisattva asking for the fruit of the lesser vehicle: the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says, "persons of the three vehicles alike, by the path beyond all verbal expression, cut off affliction and enter nirvāṇa" — if the cutting off of affliction and entering nirvāṇa are the same for all three, why should there be no asking? As to the third, that the thirty-third mind-moment is called bodhisattva, while at the thirty-fourth, thought having been exhausted, one becomes a buddha outright, so from whom would a buddha ask: this is still the sense of the Tripiṭaka teaching — if, when the obstruction is not yet removed, the great capacity is already moving, how much more so at the thirty-third mind-moment should it not be moving? And where it moves, there one knows there is asking. As to the fourth, that bodhisattvas, not yet having cut off residual habits and ignorance, ought not to ask, while once these are exhausted and buddhahood is attained, from whom would a buddha ask: this is the sense of the shared teaching held in common by the three vehicles — if, while the fetters remain entire and the obstruction still stands, the great capacity is already moving, how much more so where only residual habits and ignorance remain? As to the fifth, that "only this one matter is real" — the real being precisely the true — so how could there suddenly be any further asking: this mistakes the "one" of the absolute, beyond all relative comparison, outside of which there is no further dharma at all, for the "one" of former days set against a "two," outside of which there still remains a further dharma — the name is the same, but the substance differs; this is like grasping in the dark at rubble and fish-eyes and calling them the moon's own radiant pearls — how could the wise ever pity such folly, and so on. As to the sixth, that from the Prajñāpāramitā sūtras up through the Lotus Sūtra, the teaching is the same as the entrusting of the treasure, and there ought therefore to be no asking: have you not heard of the shared and the unshared Prajñāpāramitā — the unshared needs no asking, but the shared ought not to be without asking, and so on. As to the seventh, that at the opening of the "Skillful Means" chapter, the earlier teaching of the lesser is recounted as skillful means, but the earlier teaching of the greater is not recounted as skillful means, and the greater, therefore, not being skillful means, there is accordingly no asking for it —

Have you not heard, in the "Lifespan of the Tathāgata" chapter, that "I went forth from home in my youth and attained perfect awakening," and that all that comes between, whether lesser or greater, whether my own or another's, is entirely my own skillful means, and that it is likewise so for every buddha — how, then, could there fail to be asking? As to the eighth, that if bodhisattvas asked, they ought to comprehend in response, and comprehension in response being absent, one thereby knows they do not ask: have you not heard that, when the dharma-teaching was finished, the devas, nāgas, and the fourfold assembly all comprehended in response — and if these were not bodhisattvas, then what were they? And in the "Teacher of the Dharma" chapter, all three vehicles alike receive the prediction of buddhahood — if there were no comprehension in response, how could the prediction suddenly be bestowed? As to the ninth, that coming out of the suffering of the three realms and attaining the peace and happiness of nirvāṇa is precisely when the bestowal and the asking occur, while bodhisattvas have not yet come out and have not yet realized this, and therefore do not ask: this too is merely the sense of the Tripiṭaka teaching. As to the tenth, that it is only once the children are seated at ease that the cart is bestowed, the practice of the two lesser vehicles having ceased being what is meant by "seated at ease," while the practice of bodhisattvas has not ceased and is therefore not "seated at ease," so how could they suddenly ask for the cart: this too is merely the earlier sense. But there is asking where practice has ceased, and there is also asking where practice has not yet ceased; moreover, a bodhisattva's practice of practice is itself precisely his riding of the vehicle, and the vehicle is obtained precisely by asking — how, then, can it be said there is no asking? Seeing how this view entangles and confines itself to the Tripiṭaka teaching alone, these ten objections were accordingly set up — but this is a mere glimpse through a narrow tube, altogether missing the larger picture, and I shall now explain the matter, distinguishing it point by point. There is a bodhisattva who does not cut off affliction and does not ask for the cart — this is the bodhisattva of the Tripiṭaka teaching; there is one who cuts off affliction and does ask for the cart — this is the bodhisattva of the shared teaching; there is one who both cuts off affliction and does not, both asks and does not — this is the bodhisattva of the distinct teaching; and there is one who neither cuts off affliction nor fails to, neither asks nor fails to ask — this is the bodhisattva of the perfect teaching. And going further, across the five flavors: within the flavor of milk there are two senses — first, both cutting off and not cutting off, both asking and not asking; second, neither cutting off nor not cutting off, neither asking nor not asking. Within the flavor of curdled milk there is a single sense, not cutting off and not asking. Within the flavor of fresh butter all four senses are present. Within the flavor of clarified butter only three senses are present. Within the flavor of ghee there is a single sense. This is the broad outline and overall structure of the matter. And within each and every phrase, each and every sense, there are, moreover, four further cases: the obstruction removed and the capacity moving; the obstruction not yet removed and the capacity moving; the obstruction both removed and not yet removed, and the capacity moving; and the obstruction neither removed nor not removed, and the capacity moving. This school of thought, failing to see this, clings to the one and denies the three — a matter deeply to be pitied.

People commonly hold differing counts of the number of carts, and differing accounts of the cart's essence: some say that three carts are spoken of at first, and afterward the two are merged back into the one; some say that three are spoken of at first, and afterward all three are merged back into the one; and some say that four are spoken of at first, and afterward the three are merged back into the one. In bringing forth the sense of the sūtra, then, one ought not simply to trust what people say. This passage of the text cites how the buddhas of the past taught the four noble truths for śrāvakas, the twelvefold chain of causes and conditions for pratyekabuddhas, and the six perfections for bodhisattvas; and that the present Buddha likewise speaks of three, the count being the same. The Avataṃsaka Sūtra, fascicle eight, says: "for those of inferior capacity, sunk in despair, the path of the śrāvaka is shown to them; for those of dull faculties who delight in causes and conditions, the path of the pratyekabuddha is taught to them; for those of sharp faculties who possess compassion, the path of the bodhisattva is taught to them; and for those who delight in the unsurpassed great matter, the immeasurable Dharma of the buddhas is taught." Fascicle thirty-six likewise says: "the threefold liberation issues from the śrāvaka vehicle; the dharma free of contention issues from the pratyekabuddha vehicle; the six perfections and the four means of attraction issue from the great vehicle; and the knowledge of all dharmas issues from the buddha-vehicle." And its ninth ground speaks of the mark of the śrāvaka vehicle, the mark of the pratyekabuddha vehicle, the mark of the bodhisattva vehicle, and the mark of the tathāgata vehicle. The Dilun, in its explanation of the second ground, contemplates how the accumulation of the ten unwholesome deeds causes one to fall into the three lower destinies, while the accumulation of the ten wholesome deeds brings birth among the gods. These ten wholesome deeds, joined with the contemplative wisdom of the four noble truths, together form the śrāvaka; these ten wholesome deeds, joined with the contemplative wisdom that does not depend on hearing from another, together form the pratyekabuddha; these ten wholesome deeds, joined with contemplative wisdom that is complete and pure, together form the ground of the bodhisattva; and these ten wholesome deeds carried to their utmost, joined with the wisdom of all modes and the entirety of the buddha-dharma, together form the buddha. The Yingluo Sūtra, fascicle thirteen, says: "the buddhas of the ten directions teach the three vehicles; within the one vehicle, three are further opened out and combined into nine vehicles, and all nine vehicles alike converge and enter the equal, great wisdom." Though the sages have spoken in this way, if one is unable to bring these accounts into harmony, but instead sets them against one another as right and wrong, this is to malign the Dharma and to slander persons — no fault could be graver. Here, then, the matter is distinguished in terms of the teachings. If it is said that the approaches of the three vehicles differ, while the ultimate truth is the same, this is the Tripiṭaka teaching; if it is said that the approaches of the three vehicles are the same, and the ultimate truth is entirely the same, this is the shared teaching; if it is said that the three vehicles branch into three times three, nine vehicles, or that four vehicles, differing each from the other in their shallow and deep stages, alike enter the same equal, great wisdom, this is the distinct teaching; and if it is said that the three vehicles, the nine vehicles, and the four vehicles, every one of them, correspond to the equal, great wisdom without any duality or difference whatsoever, this is the perfect teaching. And, going further, distinguishing across the five flavors: the flavor of milk makes clear only the bodhisattva vehicle and the buddha-vehicle; the flavor of curdled milk makes clear only the three vehicles as distinct from one another; the flavor of fresh butter makes fully clear the three vehicles, the four vehicles, and the nine vehicles, each in its own proper measure, without confusing one with another; the flavor of clarified butter is the same as the flavor of fresh butter, except that it excludes the three vehicles as distinct from one another; and within the flavor of ghee only the buddha-vehicle is taught purely, with no further vehicle remaining at all. If one grasps this sense, these differing accounts present no difficulty; but if one does not know it, they only add to needless dispute. As for people's accounts of the differing essence of the buddha-vehicle: the master of Guangzhai [Fayun] takes the twin wisdoms of exhaustion and of non-arising, at the very culmination of the buddha-fruit, as the essence of the cart — going far beyond five hundred yojanas, this is "high" as against the former teaching, and, containing within it the ten thousand virtues in full, this is "broad" as against the former teaching. The master of Zhuangyan takes the totality of the ten thousand practices, as cause, for its essence — seeking upward is "high," and transforming beings below is "broad." The older explanation does not take merit as the essence, since merit is shared even by ordinary people, and takes wisdom alone as the essence. Another older explanation takes both merit and wisdom together as the essence. But the text says, "riding upon these three carts," and "enjoying themselves freely with undefiled faculties, powers, factors of awakening, the path, meditative concentration, liberation, and samādhis" — how could this be wisdom alone? Yet another master takes only understanding as the essence, since the understanding of emptiness is unmoving and is therefore not adopted — but the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising is itself precisely this understanding. And a certain lesser-vehicle teacher takes the wisdom of emptiness as the essence of the cart, the text saying, "through the long night we have cultivated the dharma of emptiness," and so on; while the great vehicle likewise takes real wisdom and skillful means together as the essence of the cart, since the essence of the cart corresponds to "being," and it is only what "is" that can be set in motion. For my part, I would say that these various masters, in explaining the essence of the buddha-vehicle, compete each to point to some particular measure of it — how does this differ from a crowd of blind men groping at an elephant, disputing over its tail and its tusks?

According to the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai, it is the true reality of all dharmas that is precisely the essence of the cart, while all the manifold jewels and adornments are merely its ornaments — this will be pointed out again when we reach the text on the bestowal of the cart. The older explanation says: the small cart is the small fruit. The fruit has both conditioned and unconditioned merit, and it is specifically the conditioned merit that is taken to correspond to the motion of the cart, its motion carrying one into the nirvāṇa without remainder. Within the conditioned fruit are both merit and wisdom together, but wisdom is taken as primary, while merit belongs to the accompanying particulars; of the ten kinds of wisdom, eight run through both cause and fruit, while the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising belongs only to the stage of the fruit — it is these two forms of wisdom, then, that are taken to correspond to the cart of the fruit, and it is for this reason that the cart is said to be outside the gate. Yet the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says, "this vehicle comes out from the three realms, and comes to abide within sarvajña, the all-knowing wisdom" — if, before one has yet come out, one is already riding this very vehicle in competing to come out of the burning house, why should it then also be said that the cart is outside the gate? And if it were already outside from the start, by what means would one ride out of the house at all? The truth is, however, that the vehicle runs through both cause and fruit: the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, which cut off the afflictions of view and of thought, are entirely the vehicle of the cause, while the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising is entirely called the vehicle of the fruit — it is only once the vehicle of the cause has, through its causal working, cut off and exhausted every affliction, that one attains the vehicle of the fruit, the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising, and hence it is said that the cart is outside the gate. But since the fruit is primary and the cause is secondary, it is spoken of, from the standpoint of the fruit, as the cart being outside the gate; yet if the sense of "vehicle" lies, within, in the cutting off of the fetters and being carried thereby, then how, without, could the fruit, which does not itself carry, ever be called a "vehicle"? And yet the fruit has no motion of cutting off affliction of its own — it is only by means of the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising entering the nirvāṇa without remainder that there is, at last, this good motion — but if one rides the cause all the way to the fruit, for what reason would one then still ask for the cart?

The older explanation speaks of "asking in capacity" and "asking in sentiment." "Asking in capacity" may readily be understood. As for "asking in sentiment": the Buddha taught the doctrine of exhaustion and non-arising, and an arhat, having realized this fruit, then used his spiritual powers and heavenly eye to try to observe the future, and yet still saw the birth-and-death of transformation stretching on vast and unbroken — and so he began to doubt his own attainment of the realization of exhaustion and non-arising, thinking: if it were truly non-arising, how could he still see it arising? Given this vast unbroken continuation, he suspected that what he had formerly taken as ultimate was not in fact so, and, in his own sentiment, sought from the Buddha what had been promised him before — this is what is meant by "asking in sentiment." But if one searches the text of the sūtra, there are no such words to be found there; and if one presses the sense of this "asking," the sense does not hold together either — the text offers nothing that can be made to fit it. As to pressing the sense: the text below says, "of his own accord, giving rise to the thought that he had attained extinction" — but if he had already seen, by his heavenly eye, that birth-and-death still continued, why would he then also give rise to the thought that he had attained extinction? This is self-contradictory. Moreover, an arhat after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, not encountering another buddha, would be unable to resolve his doubt — but if he had already, by his own heavenly eye, clearly seen birth-and-death continuing, what need would there be to see a buddha in order to resolve it? And moreover, the heavenly eye of the first dhyāna cannot even see into the second dhyāna — how much less could it see the birth-and-death of transformation? This too runs contrary to the She Dacheng [the Mahāyānasaṃgraha]. And further, if an arhat, having attained undefiled karma, used his heavenly eye to see the future birth-and-death and its retribution belonging to the transformation-body, then a person at this very moment who cultivates the five precepts and the ten wholesome deeds ought likewise to be able to see, of himself, his own future retribution — but one should understand that retribution beyond the three realms could never be seen by the heavenly eye at all. This account of "asking in sentiment," then, is not to be adopted. What is here meant by "asking in sentiment" is rather this: formerly, relying on the teaching, the arhat held that the exhaustion and non-arising he had attained could enter the nirvāṇa without remainder; but then, within the Vaipulya teachings, he saw the inconceivable powers of the bodhisattvas, and heard himself rebuked by Vimalakīrti — and he reasoned: if what I have attained is real, then the great being ought not to have humbled and rebuked me so; but if what I have attained is not real, then the Buddha ought not to have declared it true — and so he said he was lost, not knowing what to make of it. Then, reaching the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra, he came to comprehend the great Dharma, and, hearing it, the wish to delight in the great vehicle arose within him, and he wished then to advance and cultivate the great vehicle, and yet could not know whether he would attain it or not — all of this is precisely what is meant by having already, in sentiment, asked for the great vehicle. Hence, when Śāriputra comprehends in response, he raises this former doubting sentiment, saying: "seeing the bodhisattvas receive the prediction of buddhahood, and finding myself no part of this, I reproached myself in sorrow, and wished to ask the World-Honored One whether I had lost this, or had not lost it" — this is precisely pointing to how, already in the Vaipulya teachings, there had been this asking in sentiment. As for the addition, now, of asking in speech: it is upon hearing the brief verse at the opening of the "Skillful Means" chapter, and hearing there in outline that the Buddha's teaching is entirely skillful means, that he then holds fast to this present "skillful means" and doubts that the former teaching was not yet ultimate, and so says, "I do not now know where this meaning tends" — his former doubting sentiment stirred, he voices his threefold request, seeking and asking for the reality of what was formerly taught: his capacity lies in the great vehicle, while his sentiment seeks the reality of what came before. And this same pattern — seeking the great vehicle in sentiment, while asking in speech after the reality of what came before — holds equally for the six perfections and for the shared teaching.

From "why is this" onward matches the third point above, the confirming conclusion, freedom from falsehood — namely, that the elder, knowing of himself that his wealth was immeasurable, and wishing to benefit his children, promised three and gave one, and this was no falsehood. This explanation differs slightly from the one before: there, the sense was that the children's intended coming-out did not lie in the three carts themselves, so that, once they had come out, even had nothing further been given, this too would not have been false. Here, it is made clear instead that the Tathāgata's appearance in the world was, from the start, meant to teach the great vehicle, but, because those of lesser wisdom were attached to and delighted in the three realms, he enticed them by skillful means, and, once they had come out, gave them the great vehicle after all — this exactly matches his original intention, and hence it is said that he is able to give sentient beings the Dharma of the great vehicle, only they are not all able to receive it in full.

若《華嚴》中能受，即為與大，不俟開一為三；不能受者，以方便力於一佛乘分別說三，三由眾生，非佛本意，故用此釋成不乖本心不虛也。◎

Where beings are able to receive it, as in the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, the great vehicle is given to them directly, without waiting to open the one into the three; where they are unable to receive it, then, by the power of skillful means, the one buddha-vehicle is distinguished and spoken of as three — the three arising from sentient beings themselves, and not from the Buddha's original intention. It is by this explanation, then, that not going against the original intention, and freedom from falsehood, are both established.
