Explaining the "Medicinal Herbs" Chapter: The Fourfold Title 釋藥草喻品：因緣釋

Chinese Source

釋藥草喻品

Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the "Medicinal Herbs" Chapter

此中具山川雲雨，獨以「藥草」標名者，土地是能生，雲雨是能潤，草木是所生所潤，所生所潤通皆有用，而藥草用強，有漏諸善悉能除惡，無漏為最，無漏眾中四大弟子，以譬領佛譬深會聖心，佛讚善哉甚為希有，述其得解以喻其人，故稱「藥草喻品」。

This chapter is furnished throughout with mountains and rivers, clouds and rain, and yet it is named solely for "the medicinal herbs." Why is this? The earth is what does the producing; the cloud and the rain are what do the moistening; the grasses and trees are what is produced and what is moistened — and all of what is produced and moistened has its use, yet among these the medicinal herbs are of the strongest use. Every contaminated form of wholesome deed is able to remove some evil, but the uncontaminated is the finest of all, and among the assembly of the uncontaminated, the four great disciples, by their parable, took hold of the Buddha's own parable and deeply met the sage's mind. The Buddha praised them, saying, "Excellent, most rare indeed," and recounted their attainment of understanding so as to illustrate this very kind of person — and hence it is called the "Medicinal Herbs" chapter.

夫藥草叢育日久，一蒙雲雨扶疏暐曄，芽莖豐蔚於外，力用充潤於內，譬諸無漏住最後身有餘涅槃，更不願求無上佛道；今得聞經自乘佛乘兼以運人，文云「我等今日真是聲聞」，以佛道聲令一切聞，內外自他具勝力用，故稱「藥草喻品」。

Now, medicinal herbs, having been cultivated in their clump over many days, no sooner meet with cloud and rain than they spread and flourish, radiant and bright — their sprouts and stems grow rich and lush without, while their strength and function are filled and moistened within. This is a parable for the uncontaminated ones who dwell in their final body, in nirvāṇa-with-remainder, no longer wishing to seek the unsurpassed path of buddhahood — and yet, now hearing this sūtra, they themselves ride the buddha-vehicle and, moreover, carry others upon it as well. The text says, "we, today, are truly śrāvakas," causing the sound of the buddha-path to be heard by all; within and without, for self and for others, they now possess this excellent strength and function — and hence it is called the "Medicinal Herbs" chapter.

夫藥草者，能除四大風冷，補養五臟還年駐色，今蒙雲雨忽成藥王，餌之遍治眾病變體成仙，譬諸無漏聞經，破無明惑開佛知見，文云「我等今日真是佛子，無上寶聚不求自得」，佛子所應得者皆已得之，面於佛前得受記莂，嘉著而稱微，故言「藥草喻品」。

Now, a medicinal herb is able to remove the wind and cold of the four great elements, to nourish and restore the five viscera, and to renew one's years and preserve one's color — and here, once it meets with the cloud and the rain, it suddenly becomes the very king of medicines, which, taken, cures every illness at once and transforms the body into that of an immortal. This is a parable for the uncontaminated ones who, hearing this sūtra, break through the affliction of ignorance and open the Buddha's own knowledge and vision. The text says, "we, today, are truly children of the Buddha; the unsurpassed treasure, sought by us not at all, is nonetheless attained" — everything that a child of the Buddha ought to attain, they have now already attained, and, in the Buddha's own presence, receive the conferral of the prediction. This is fine and excellent, yet is spoken of as something subtle and modest — and hence it is said, "the 'Medicinal Herbs' chapter."

前一番是師弟領述，世界悉檀意也；次一番生善，為人意也；次一番是對治、第一義兩悉檀意也。

The first of these three explanations is the teacher and disciples taking hold of and recounting one another's meaning — the sense of the mundane siddhānta. The second gives rise to wholesome roots — the sense of the individual siddhānta. The third carries the sense of both the therapeutic siddhānta and the siddhānta of ultimate meaning together.

是名「因緣釋品」。餘約教、本迹、觀心，準例可知，不復記(云云)。

This is called "explaining the chapter title by causes and conditions." The remaining three — in terms of the teachings, trace and origin, and the contemplation of mind — may be known by analogy with the preceding cases, and are not separately recorded here (and so on, as before).

The Section of Confirming Understanding: What Exactly Is Being Praised 述成段：領權領實

Chinese Source

此品是譬說中第三述成段。舊云：述其十三偈歎佛恩深，又述其教作人譬文言曲巧。師云：不應偏爾，經稱善說如來真實功德，備述其領權領實，明文在此；十三偈止是二乘齊教荷恩，教作人譬是佛權功德耳。今言都述其周遍領解，始天性結緣、中間追誘、終於付財，自微自著無量無邊諸恩德也。其文為二：一、略述成，二、廣述成。略述又二：一、雙述善哉，二、領所不及。雙述者，一善哉述其兩處領實，一善哉述其兩處領權。「善說如來真實功德」者，「真實」是述實，「功德」是述權。又《華嚴》之擬宜領實也，三藏之誘引領權也，方等之體信，《般若》之領教，俱領權實也，《法華》之付財，專論實也。辭致曲巧故言「善說」，皆是佛法故言「真實」。

This chapter is the third section of confirmation within the parable-teaching. The ancients said that this confirms the thirteen verses giving thanks for the depth of the Buddha's kindness, and also confirms that the parable of the man taught to work is delicately and skillfully phrased. Our teacher says: this should not be read so one-sidedly. The sūtra speaks of "well expounding the Tathāgata's true and real merits," and this fully confirms their taking hold of both the provisional and the real — the plain text is right here. The thirteen verses are only the two lesser vehicles bearing the kindness common to the teaching, while the parable of the man taught to work is the Buddha's own provisional merit. What is meant here instead is that this confirms their taking hold of the whole, all-pervading understanding — beginning with the bond formed by natural affinity, continuing through the pursuit and enticement, and ending with the bestowal of the wealth — every kindness and virtue, subtle and manifest alike, without measure and without bound. The text falls into two: first, the brief confirmation; second, the extended confirmation. The brief confirmation again falls into two: first, the twofold "excellent," and second, taking hold of what has not yet been reached. As for the twofold "excellent": one "excellent" confirms their taking hold of the real in two places, and one "excellent" confirms their taking hold of the provisional in two places. As for "well expounding the Tathāgata's true and real merits": "true and real" confirms the real, and "merits" confirms the provisional. Moreover, the *Avataṃsaka*'s conjectural fitness took hold of the real; the Tripiṭaka teaching's enticement took hold of the provisional; the Vaipulya teaching's mutual trust of heart, and the *Prajñāpāramitā*'s taking hold of the teaching, both took hold of the provisional and the real together; and the *Lotus*'s bestowal of the wealth speaks solely of the real. Because its phrasing is delicate and skillful, it is called "well expounded"; because it is entirely the Buddha-dharma, it is called "true and real."

「誠如所言」者，印定之旨也。又從「如來復有」下，述其領所不及。云何不及？謂退進、橫豎、亦橫亦豎、非橫非豎皆不及也。所以者何？大雲普覆遍荷清涼，大雨俱霑無不蒙澤，咸令世間皆得知見未曾有法，那忽齊教止領二乘得益？不道人天小草，是為退所不及。菩薩名上草，亦名小樹大樹，敷榮欝茂自他饒益而復不領，是為進所不及。又十法界同成佛法界，那忽止領二乘，餘八法界都不涉言？是為橫所不及。又七方便從淺至深皆入真實，餘五方便都不在言，是為豎所不及。又三世利益未曾暫廢，是為亦橫亦豎所不及。

As for "truly, it is just as you have said": this is the sense of the Buddha's own seal of confirmation. Again, from "the Tathāgata has, further," onward, this confirms their taking hold of what they have not yet reached. In what respect have they not reached it? It is said that they have not reached it with respect to the lesser and the greater, the horizontal and the vertical, both horizontal and vertical together, and neither horizontal nor vertical. Why is this so? The great cloud covers everything universally, bearing coolness to all alike; the great rain moistens everything together, and there is nothing that does not receive its favor — causing the entire world, without exception, to attain the knowledge and vision of a dharma never before known. How, then, could the confirmation, common only to the teaching, take hold of nothing but the two lesser vehicles' own benefit? It says nothing of humans and devas, the lesser herb — this is what is not reached with respect to the lesser. The bodhisattva is named the superior herb, and is also named the small tree and the great tree, spreading forth in bloom, rich and luxuriant, benefiting self and others alike — and yet this, too, is left untaken-hold-of; this is what is not reached with respect to the greater. Again, the ten dharma-realms all alike become the buddha-dharma-realm — how, then, could the confirmation come to a stop, taking hold only of the two lesser vehicles, with the remaining eight dharma-realms never once mentioned? This is what is not reached with respect to the horizontal. Again, the seven skillful means, from the shallow to the deep, all alike enter into the true and real, and yet the remaining five skillful means go entirely unmentioned; this is what is not reached with respect to the vertical. Again, the benefit spanning the three periods of time has never once ceased; this is what is not reached with respect to both the horizontal and the vertical together.

夫山川谿谷(云云)，總言一地，一地能生未嘗揀擇攘彼受此，草木種子皆依於地更無餘依，一雲靉靆無處不密，一雨一味不隔枯榮，普潤既同普得增長，如來平等不可思議，實不先頓後漸、初三末一，如龍興慶雲普雨於一切身心不降雨除熱得清涼，是為五乘七方便十方三世平等廣大，甚深博遠不可思議無有差別，是為非橫非豎領所不及。不及之旨非都頓奪，特以初心望後心，未窮極地故云不盡耳。又初悟初阿，亦具後茶功德，但齊教之領未暇進領橫豎周遍耳。又權行大士宜應如此也。

Now, mountains, rivers, streams, and valleys (and so on, as before) — taken all together, these are called a single ground; and a single ground, in its capacity to produce, has never once made a choice, rejecting this and accepting that. The seeds of grasses and trees all rely upon the ground, with no other reliance besides; a single cloud, dense and unbroken, leaves no place unfilled; a single rain, of a single flavor, does not withhold itself from the withered or the flourishing. Since the universal moistening is one and the same, the universal growth attained is likewise one and the same — the Tathāgata's own equality is beyond conceivable thought. In truth, it is not that the sudden comes first and the gradual comes after, nor that the three come at the start and the one comes at the end — it is like the dragon raising up an auspicious cloud, its rain falling universally upon every body and mind, without there being any body or mind upon which the rain does not fall, removing the heat and bringing coolness. This is the equality, vastness, extreme profundity, breadth, and remoteness, beyond conceivable thought and without any differentiation at all, of the five vehicles, the seven skillful means, the ten directions, and the three periods of time — this is what is not reached with respect to neither the horizontal nor the vertical. The purport of "not yet reached" is not that everything is snatched away all at once — it is only that, measuring the beginner's mind against the mind of one further along, the beginner has not yet exhausted the ultimate ground, and hence it is said that the confirmation is not exhaustive. Moreover, the very first awakening, the very first letter A, already possesses the merit of the very last letter, Cha — it is only that the confirmation common to the teaching has not yet had the leisure to go further and take hold of the horizontal and vertical dimensions in their entirety. And it is fitting, too, that the great beings who practice by way of the provisional should be exactly like this.

The Extended Confirmation: Opening the Three to Reveal the One 廣述成：開三顯一、約教約智

Chinese Source

廣述成又二：長行、偈頌。長行又二：初述成開三顯一，次從「汝等迦葉」下，結歎。初有法譬合。法中又二：初先舉法王者，不虛勸信也；次「於一切法」下，正述開三顯一。夫人王外無所畏、內不二言；法王亦爾，眾惡已盡、發言誠諦。舊云：述中根不虛，獎下根信受。今言：佛法雖多不出權實，權實之外更無別法，而言「無量」者，此意難信，故舉法王勸信，又為下大雲譬作本。從「於一切法」下，約教明開權顯實。從「如來觀知」下，約智明開權顯實。由二教顯二智，由二智說二教，智教相成也。「一切法」者，謂七方便，橫也；對一實，為豎也。若言不爾，何故二萬億佛所初發大心，中間取小，又流轉五趣，又十法界一人尚具，況七方便耶？此法雖多，方便波羅蜜照之罄無不盡，以隨其類音說之無不逗會，為人天說戒善，為二乘說諦緣，為三藏說事度，為通教說無生，為別教說次第開如來藏，是名述其領開三也。

The extended confirmation again falls into two: the prose section and the verse section. The prose section again falls into two: first, confirming the opening of the three to reveal the one; and second, from "you, Kāśyapa," onward, the concluding praise. The first has the dharma-teaching, the parable, and the matching correlation. Within the dharma-teaching there are, again, two: first, raising up the Dharma-king, so as to urge faith without falsehood; and second, from "with respect to every dharma" onward, directly confirming the opening of the three to reveal the one. Now, a king among men has, outwardly, nothing to fear, and, inwardly, speaks with no two words; the Dharma-king is exactly the same — every evil already exhausted, he utters only words that are sincere and true. The ancients said that this confirms the middling capacity's freedom from falsehood, and encourages the lesser capacity to receive it with faith. What is meant here instead is that, although the Buddha-dharma is manifold, it does not go beyond the provisional and the real, and beyond the provisional and the real there is no further, separate dharma — and yet, when it is spoken of as "immeasurable," this sense is difficult to believe, and so the Dharma-king is raised up to urge faith, and this, moreover, forms the basis for the parable of the great cloud that follows below. From "with respect to every dharma" onward, this makes clear, in terms of the teachings, the opening of the provisional to reveal the real. From "the Tathāgata observes and knows" onward, this makes clear, in terms of wisdom, the opening of the provisional to reveal the real. By means of the two teachings, the two wisdoms are made manifest; by means of the two wisdoms, the two teachings are expounded — wisdom and teaching bring one another to completion. As for "every dharma": this means the seven skillful means, and is the horizontal dimension; set against the one real, it becomes the vertical dimension. Were this not so, why else would it be that, in the presence of twenty thousand koṭis of buddhas, the great mind was first brought forth, and yet, in the interval, the lesser was taken up instead, and one flowed on through the five destinies as well? Even a single person among the ten dharma-realms already possesses all of this in full — how much more so the seven skillful means! Though this dharma is manifold, the perfection of skillful means illumines it entirely, leaving nothing unexhausted, and, according to each kind's own tongue, expounds it so that nothing fails to meet its mark: for humans and devas it teaches the precepts and wholesome deeds; for the two lesser vehicles it teaches the truths and the causal conditions; for the Tripiṭaka teaching it teaches the practices and the perfections; for the shared teaching it teaches non-arising; and for the distinct teaching it teaches the gradual opening of the tathāgatagarbha. This is called confirming their taking hold of the opening of the three.

從「其所說法」下，約教述其顯實也。「地」者實相也。究竟非二故名「一」，其性廣博故名為「切」，寂而常照故名為「智」，無住之本立一切法故名為「地」，此圓教實說也。凡有所說皆令眾生到此智地，顯實之文灼然如日，云何闇寱作餘解耶？例《大品》廣歷諸法皆摩訶衍，衍即大乘，乘即實相，實相即一切智地，上文云「唯此一事實」指此地也，「餘二則非真」，指七方便也。此約漸頓二教，述其開權顯實也。從「如來觀知一切諸法」下，約智述開權顯實。

From "the dharma that this expounds" onward, this confirms, in terms of the teachings, its revealing of the real. "The ground" is true reality itself. Being, in the end, not two, it is named "one"; its nature being vast and broad, it is named "all"; being still and yet ever illuminating, it is named "wisdom"; and being the root of non-abiding upon which every dharma is established, it is named "ground" — this is the real teaching spoken of by the perfect teaching. Whatever is expounded is, in every case, so as to bring sentient beings to this very ground of wisdom; the text that reveals the real is as bright and evident as the sun — how, then, could one, as though talking in one's sleep, produce some other reading of it? Compare the *Larger Sūtra*'s broad passage through every dharma, all of it the Mahāyāna — "yāna" is precisely the Great Vehicle, "vehicle" is precisely true reality, and true reality is precisely the ground of the wisdom of all modes. The passage above, "only this one matter is real," points to this very ground; "the other two are not true" points to the seven skillful means. This, in terms of the gradual and sudden teachings, confirms the opening of the provisional to reveal the real. From "the Tathāgata observes and knows every dharma" onward, this confirms, in terms of wisdom, the opening of the provisional to reveal the real.

The Twin Wisdoms: Knowing Where Every Dharma Tends, and Knowing It to the End 觀一切歸趣、深心所行

Chinese Source

「觀一切歸趣」，是能照權也；「究竟明了」者，能照實也，二智所照偏圓兩境通達無礙，故能說權實二教，此舉智釋教也。「知所歸趣」是識藥，「深心所行」是知病，病藥俱是權法，權法各有歸趣，戒善等近趣人天，若作緣義，低頭舉手遠趣佛果；念處道品等近趣涅槃，若作福德莊嚴，汝等所行是菩薩道遠趣寶所；乃至六度通、別等法，近遠歸趣途轍不同，可解。又戒善是人天藥，諦緣度是三乘人藥，乃至通、別等亦可解。「深心所行」有二種：深心著於依正，又深心著所執之法。著依正者，起深重十惡障人天乘；著所執法，起四倒三道六蔽四住五住等，障諸聖乘。當知深心病相不同，權智照之通達無礙。「又於諸法究盡明了」者，實智所照也。

"Observing where everything tends" is the wisdom able to illumine the provisional; "knowing it to the very end" is the wisdom able to illumine the real. What the twin wisdoms illumine — the partial and the perfect, these two objects — is penetrated without any obstruction, and hence the Buddha is able to expound the two teachings, the provisional and the real; this raises up wisdom so as to explain the teaching. "Knowing where things tend" is knowing the medicine; "what the deep mind engages in" is knowing the illness. Both illness and medicine belong to the provisional dharma, and the provisional dharma each has its own tendency: the precepts and wholesome deeds and the like tend, in the near term, toward humans and devas, yet, taken in the sense of a causal condition, even a single bowed head or raised hand tends, in the distant term, toward the fruit of buddhahood; the foundations of mindfulness and the factors of the path and the like tend, in the near term, toward nirvāṇa, yet, taken as the adornment of merit, "what you yourselves practice is the bodhisattva path" tends, in the distant term, toward the place of treasure; and so on, up through the six perfections and the shared and distinct teachings and the rest — the near and distant tendencies follow different courses, and this may be understood by analogy. Again, the precepts and wholesome deeds are the medicine for humans and devas; the truths, causal conditions, and perfections are the medicine for the three vehicles of people; and so on for the shared and distinct teachings and the rest, which may likewise be understood. "What the deep mind engages in" has two kinds: the deep mind's attachment to its retributive body and its surrounding world, and the deep mind's attachment to the dharmas it grasps at. Attachment to the retributive body and its world gives rise to the ten evils in their deep and heavy form, obstructing the vehicles of humans and devas; attachment to the dharmas grasped at gives rise to the four inversions, the three paths, the six coverings, the four abodes of delusion, the five abodes of delusion, and the rest, obstructing every one of the noble vehicles. One should know that the marks of the deep mind's illness are not all the same, and that the wisdom of the provisional illumines them without any obstruction. As for "and, with respect to every dharma, knows it to the very end and understands it clearly": this is what the wisdom of the real illumines.

一切權法無不入實，故言「究竟」，實智所知故言「了」，佛眼所見故言「明」。若此智照此藥此病，不照彼藥彼病，彼智照彼不能照此，種別不同者，權智照也；一智遍照一切藥一切病，實智照也。能示眾生如此圓境智，故言「一切智慧」也。又「一切法」者，謂十法界也，十法各各相欲不同，各獲果報歸趣亦異。知諸法盡者名知病，知一切深心所著名知藥。藥有深淺，《大品》云「如實智知貪欲心瞋癡心」，以如實智知名知深心，如理通達無有障礙，若戒善、諦、緣、度等一切法藥，悉用如實智知者，名「通達無礙」。又權智文中通達無礙者，約權論實；實智文中，又於諸法諸法者，約實論權。二文亙現者，此明實是權實、權是實權，當知究竟非權非實，非差別非不差別，以智方便權有差別，悉到智地則無差別。如地無差別草木若干，若干無若干、無若干若干。又如約心論法、約法論心，心有諸數、法無諸數，心不離法、法不離心，無數而數、數而無數耳。權實亦爾(云云)。

There is no provisional dharma at all that does not enter into the real, and hence it is called "to the very end"; it is known by the wisdom of the real, and hence it is called "clearly understood"; it is seen by the buddha-eye, and hence it is called "understood." If a given wisdom illumines only this medicine and this illness, and does not illumine that medicine and that illness, and that wisdom illumines that but is unable to illumine this — such a difference of kind belongs to the illumination of the wisdom of the provisional; but a single wisdom that universally illumines every medicine and every illness belongs to the illumination of the wisdom of the real. Being able to show sentient beings this very perfect object of wisdom is why it is called "every wisdom." Again, as for "every dharma": this means the ten dharma-realms, whose ten dharmas each have their own different aspirations, and each obtain a different fruit of retribution and a different tendency. Knowing every dharma to the very end is called knowing the illness; knowing everything to which the deep mind is attached is called knowing the medicine. The medicine has both the deep and the shallow. The *Larger Sūtra* says, "by the wisdom of things as they are, one knows the mind of greed and desire, the mind of anger and delusion" — knowing by means of the wisdom of things as they are is called knowing the deep mind, penetrating in accordance with principle, without any obstruction. If, with respect to the precepts and wholesome deeds, the truths, the causal conditions, the perfections, and every other dharma-medicine, one comes to know all of them by means of the wisdom of things as they are, this is called "penetrating without obstruction." Moreover, in the passage on the wisdom of the provisional, "penetrating without obstruction" speaks of the real in terms of the provisional; and in the passage on the wisdom of the real, "and with respect to every dharma" speaks of the provisional in terms of the real. The two passages, appearing alongside one another, make clear that the real is the provisional-within-the-real, and the provisional is the real-within-the-provisional — one should know that, ultimately, it is neither the provisional nor the real, neither differentiated nor undifferentiated. By means of the skillful means of wisdom, the provisional has its differentiations, yet, once all of it reaches the ground of wisdom, there is no differentiation at all — just as the ground, without differentiation, yet gives rise to however many kinds of grass and tree, and however many kinds there are, are yet without "however many," and being without "however many," are yet however many. It is, again, as with speaking of dharmas in terms of mind and speaking of mind in terms of dharmas: mind has its many categories, while dharma is without categories; mind is not apart from dharma, and dharma is not apart from mind — being without number, and yet numbered; being numbered, and yet without number. The provisional and the real are exactly the same (and so on, as before).

From "it is like" onward, second, the parable-teaching. The text falls into two: first, the parable-teaching, and after it, the further sectarian confirmation and praise. The parable has its opening and its matching correlation. The opening falls into two: first, the differentiated parable, which parallels the provisional teaching and the provisional wisdom confirmed above; and second, the undifferentiated parable, which parallels the real teaching and the real wisdom confirmed above. The three kinds of herbs and the two kinds of trees, delicate or dense, are not equal — hence they are called "differentiated"; a single ground and a single rain support and moisten universally — hence they are "undifferentiated." Observed at the level of their branching outcomes, each seems different from every other; traced back to their root and flourishing, none is other than the ground and the rain. Matched inwardly, the wisdom of skillful means illumines the seven and the five each differently, while the wisdom of the real, going forth to illumine them, comes in the end to a single reality — the one reality is the seven and the five, and the seven and the five are the one reality; the differentiated is the undifferentiated, and the undifferentiated is the differentiated (and so on, as before). The differentiated parable has six points: first, the earth; second, the grasses and trees; third, the dense cloud; fourth, the pouring rain; fifth, the receiving of moisture; and sixth, growth. As for the first, the parable of the earth: the ancients, taken as a whole, raised up the earth of the three-thousandfold world and, particularly, brought forth the mountains, rivers, streams, and valleys as the causal factor of habituation for the five vehicles — the valley, receiving much water, is a parable for the bodhisattva; the stream is a parable for the pratyekabuddha, and the river for the śrāvaka; and the mountain, being high and receiving little moisture, is a parable for the vehicle of humans and devas. What is meant here instead is that the causal factor of habituation ought properly to be likened to the seed's receiving of moisture and its growth, and yet, while the earth and the mountains and rivers do receive moisture, they lack the two senses of the seed and of growth; moreover, the matching text below says, "throughout the world, devas, humans, and asuras," and the verse says, "among every assembly of devas and humans" — neither takes the earth and the rest as a parable for the causal factor of habituation, and hence this reading is not used here. Here, instead, "the great-thousandfold world" is taken as a parable for the world of sentient beings, and "the mountains, rivers, streams, valleys, and earth" as a parable for the world of the five aggregates. The world has no separate dharma of its own — it is formed by the mountains, rivers, streams, valleys, and earth; sentient beings have no separate dharma of their own — they are formed by the five aggregates. The earth, taken as a whole, is a parable for the aggregate of consciousness, and the mountains, rivers, streams, and valleys are a parable for the remaining four aggregates. The grasses and trees that depend upon them, though they depend upon the earth and the rest, are not themselves identical with the earth and the rest — the substantial trunk of the grass or tree is called simply "grass" or "tree," while the seed of the grass or tree has no further separate name of its own; only what has the function of producing is called "seed," and what is produced, the substantial trunk, is called "grass" or "tree" — both alike are rooted in the earth, and the earth is thus their very root. Matched inwardly with the causal factor of habituation and its habituated fruit: though these depend upon the five aggregates, the five aggregates are not themselves identical with the cause and the fruit — it is only that, relying upon the aggregates, one comes to have the causal factor of habituation, and growth brought to completion is called the habituated fruit. Since fruit and cause arise in dependence upon the aggregates, the parable of the mountains, rivers, and earth is thereby established; and the seed of the grass or tree, receiving moisture and growing, is thereby wholly established as a parable as well. To bring out the distinct parable still further: though a mountain is lofty and steep, it, too, has the five marks of the low and the raised, and even the earth, though level, likewise has the five marks of the hillock and the pool — these are parables for the five aggregates of the five vehicles: the towering mountain is a parable for the bodhisattva's five aggregates; the river is a parable for the pratyekabuddha, and the stream for the śrāvaka; the earth is a parable for the deva; and the low valley is a parable for the human. Each and every one of these five aggregates has its own reliance for the causal factor of habituation and the habituated fruit, just as the mountains, rivers, streams, valleys, and earth are all a reliance for the seed and the substantial trunk and the rest. Again, taking "the great-thousandfold world" as a parable for the principle of the correct cause, which serves universally as the reliance for everything; "the mountains, rivers, streams, valleys, and earth" as a parable for the form and mind of sentient beings' aggregates, elements, and sense-fields as fruits of retribution; and "the grasses, trees, and thickets" as a parable for sentient beings' causal factor of habituation — these three dharmas are not separate from one another: the habituated depends upon the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields, and the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields do not go beyond the dharma-nature, just as the grasses and trees depend upon the mountains and rivers, and the mountains and rivers depend upon the world (and so on, as before). The six passages stand out plainly as they are — why, then, force some other reading upon them? And, their order being just as it is, why interweave the sūtra's own text, copying pieces out of their proper sequence?

From "the grasses and trees it produces" onward, second, the parable of the grasses and trees produced. "Grasses" is the general name for every plant, and "trees" is the collective term for every tree; many grasses form a clump, and many trees form a grove. Whatever has the strongest power to cure illness is called "medicine" — just as, among wholesome dharmas generally, all are able to cure some evil, yet the uncontaminated wholesomeness has by far the finer sense of curing affliction. Within the grasses and trees below, the tree and the grove, their branches and trunks giving broad shade and being of great use as timber, are thus a metaphor for the two kinds of bodhisattva. As for "seeds of every kind": these are the seeds of cause and fruit for the five vehicles and the seven forms of wholesomeness, and hence it is said "every kind" — that is, each kind has its own designation, which is its "name," and each has its own substance and mark, which is its "form." From "dense cloud" onward, third, the parable of the dense cloud. A cloud has both form and color, and shelters and shades — the text below has "thunder resounding from afar" — the sheltering and shading is a parable for the Buddha's loving-kindness and compassion; the form and color are a parable for the Buddha's responsive manifestation in the world; and the thunder is a parable for the Buddha's word and teaching. The dense cloud is precisely the three mysteries: loving-kindness and compassion are the mystery of mind, form and color are the mystery of body, and thunder is the mystery of speech. As for "spreading everywhere": this means pervading — being both dense and pervasive, it is called "spreading everywhere." By means of the Buddha's loving-kindness and compassion suffusing all things, the response-body expounds the Dharma throughout the ten dharma-realms, and hence it is called "spreading everywhere." The *Jinglü Yixiang* [an encyclopedic compilation of Assorted Categories from the Sūtras and Vinaya] says: "Clouds have five colors — those with much wind are blue-green; those with much fire are red; those with much earth are yellow and white; and those with much water are black. There are five circumstances in which no rain falls: first, when wind rises and blows it away; second, when fire rises and scorches it; third, when the asuras take hold of it with their hands and draw it into the sea; fourth, when the rain-master is licentious and disordered; and fifth, when the king of the country fails to govern well, so that the rain-master, in his anger, withholds the rain" (and so on, as before). From "pouring down all at once, equally" onward, fourth, the parable of the pouring rain.

From "the Tathāgata expounds the Dharma of a single mark" onward, second, matching the undifferentiated parable. The opening above had three parts, and the matching here likewise has three, though not in the same order. From "a single mark, a single flavor" onward, matching the single ground and the single rain together. From "that is to say" onward, explaining the single ground and the single rain together. From "those sentient beings who" onward, matching the passage above that the various grasses and trees each have their own differentiation. "A single mark" means that the minds of sentient beings share one and the same mark of true suchness — this is the single ground. "A single flavor" means that the dharma of the one vehicle expounds, in common, a single principle — this is the single rain.

Formerly, from the one true reality, it was, by skillful means, opened out into seven marks, and, with respect to the dharma of the one vehicle, distinguished and expounded as seven teachings — yet the Buddha knows that, in the end, all alike return to a single mark, a single flavor. From "that is to say" onward, explaining the single mark and the single flavor together. The mind-nature of sentient beings is precisely the virtue of their own nature, and the three marks of liberation, of departing, and of quiescent cessation — the Tathāgata's single voice, expounding these three dharmas, is precisely the three flavors, and these three marks then become the very object and condition from which the practice of the middle way arises; in the end, this is attained as the fruit of the wisdom of all modes, and hence it is said, "to the very end, arriving at the wisdom of all modes." The matching of the grasses' and trees' differentiated parable is explained further below, and is not recorded again here. At times, this is made as a threefold matching: first, the undifferentiated sense, matching the single ground and single rain above; second, the differentiated sense, matching the grasses' and trees' differentiation above; and third, the Tathāgata's own ability to know, explaining and completing the first two senses. As for the undifferentiated: this means the single mark, the single flavor — the single mark matches the single ground above. "The mark of liberation" means the mark of birth-and-death is absent; "the mark of departing" means the mark of nirvāṇa is absent; and "the mark of cessation" means that mark itself is absent as well — there being only true reality, it is called the single mark; the single mark is precisely the root of non-abiding upon which every dharma is established, and non-abiding, being without mark, is precisely the absence of differentiation. To establish every dharma is to have differentiation. The differentiated is like the grasses and trees; the undifferentiated is like the single ground. Though the ground is without differentiation, it is nonetheless able to produce the differing kinds — peach, plum, grass, and tree; and though peach, plum, grass, and tree differ from one another, they are alike a single, solid mark.

若知地具桃李，即識實中有權，解無差別即是差別；若知桃李堅相，即識權中有實，解差別即是無差別。以是義故，以一相合上一地譬也。一味即是實教純一無雜，例一相可解。「解脫」者，無分段、變易二邊業縛，故名「解脫相」。「離相」者，得中道智慧，此慧能遠離二邊無所著，故名「離相」。「滅相」者，二邊因滅得有餘涅槃，二邊果滅得無餘涅槃，故名「滅相」。句句例作差無差別義，準一相可解。「究竟至於一切種智」者，若得二邊滅相，即是通、別二惑盡，入佛知見，以一切種智心中行般若，初發、畢竟二不別，故言「究竟」，此即佛之智慧，故言「一切種智」也。

If one knows that the ground contains the peach and the plum, one thereby recognizes that, within the real, there is the provisional — understanding the undifferentiated to be precisely the differentiated; if one knows that the peach and the plum share a single, solid mark, one thereby recognizes that, within the provisional, there is the real — understanding the differentiated to be precisely the undifferentiated. For this reason, the single mark is matched with the parable of the single ground above. "The single flavor" is precisely the real teaching, pure, single, and unmixed — this may be understood by analogy with the single mark. As for "liberation": it is free of the karmic bonds of the two extremes, of the segmented and the transformational, and hence it is named "the mark of liberation." As for "departing": it attains the wisdom of the middle way, and this wisdom is able to depart far from the two extremes, with nothing to which it clings, and hence it is named "the mark of departing." As for "cessation": the cause of the two extremes ceasing, one attains nirvāṇa-with-remainder; the fruit of the two extremes ceasing, one attains nirvāṇa-without-remainder — hence it is named "the mark of cessation." Each phrase, in turn, may be understood, by analogy with the single mark, as carrying this same sense of the differentiated and the undifferentiated. As for "to the very end, arriving at the wisdom of all modes": if one attains the mark of the two extremes' cessation, this is precisely the exhaustion of the two kinds of affliction, of the shared and the distinct teachings, and one enters the Buddha's own knowledge and vision; practicing the perfection of wisdom within a mind possessed of the wisdom of all modes, the first arousing of the mind and the final attainment are not two apart — hence it is called "to the very end"; and this is precisely the Buddha's own wisdom, hence it is called "the wisdom of all modes."

From this point on, what is made clear as differentiation is this: sentient beings are the differentiation of what is provisional and what is real among the mountains and rivers, and also the differentiation among the seeds; the Tathāgata is precisely the cloud; hearing the Dharma is precisely the rain; reading, reciting, and practicing is precisely the moistening; and merit is precisely the growth — and all such differentiation, these beings are entirely unable to know. "Those sentient beings who" raises up the people who do not know. "The Dharma" means the dharma heard as a single voice; "uphold and expound" directly makes clear their not knowing. What is upheld and expounded differs, and practice differs as well: humans and devas form an understanding of the precepts and wholesome deeds, while the three vehicles form an understanding of the truths, causal conditions, and perfections — the understanding already differing, this is precisely differentiation. As for "the merit they attain, they are themselves unaware of": this makes clear that, though the five kinds of people each receive the teaching, they do not know that the Buddha's is a single-flavored, undifferentiated teaching, nor do they know of the seven kinds of skillful means; each forms his own understanding and each holds fast to his own understanding as real — this is precisely not knowing the provisional, and also not recognizing the real; this is precisely differentiation of which one is oneself unaware. Second, the Tathāgata's being able to know. Reducing the number somewhat, ten objects are raised, gathered together into four senses: first, knowing in terms of four dharmas; second, knowing in terms of three dharmas; third, knowing in terms of two dharmas; and fourth, being able to know in terms of a single dharma. In terms of four dharmas: this means the seed, the mark, the substance, and the nature. As for the seed: the three paths are the seed of the three virtues. The *Vimalakīrti [Sūtra]* says, "the whole company of every affliction is the Tathāgata's own seed" — this makes clear that, by way of the path of affliction itself, there is already prajñā. It also says, "the five acts of unmediated retribution all give rise to the mark of liberation" — this is because, by way of what is unwholesome itself, there is already the liberation of what is wholesome.

Every sentient being is already, in mark, nirvāṇa itself, and can never again be extinguished — this is precisely birth-and-death being the dharma-body. This discusses the seed in terms of correlation; discussed in terms of category, every bowed head and every raised hand is entirely the seed of liberation; every mundane wisdom and every mind of understanding of the three vehicles is precisely the seed of prajñā; and every being that has a mind whatsoever shall, in the end, become a buddha — this is precisely the seed of the dharma-body. The three dharmas are precisely the three kinds of wisdom, and these, again, have three layers: first, the object of the three wisdoms; second, the substance of the three wisdoms; and third, the causal conditions of the three wisdoms. "What matter does one recall" makes clear the function of the three wisdoms. Recalling and taking hold of the matter recalled is precisely the object of the three wisdoms. From "how does one recall" onward: recalling is recording the dharma one has heard, and this is precisely the substance of the wisdom of recollection. From "by what dharma does one recall" onward, this is precisely the three wisdoms' taking hold of their object — hearing the Dharma is their causal condition. Again, with respect to the object of the three wisdoms, it is because the object, the wisdom, and the causal condition come together that the dharma of the three wisdoms is obtained, and this, too, is called the causal condition. The causes of the five vehicles each obtain their own fruit — this is precisely differentiation; but sentient beings' suchness and the Buddha's suchness are one and the same suchness, without a second suchness, being but a single cause and a single fruit — this is precisely the undifferentiated. The Tathāgata is able to know both this differentiation and this lack of differentiation; and that the differentiated is precisely the undifferentiated, and the undifferentiated precisely the differentiated, the Tathāgata is able to know this as well. From "sentient beings abide in every kind of ground" onward, this is, in terms of a single dharma, the Tathāgata's being able to know.

The seven skillful means abide within their seven stations, and hence it is said "every kind of ground" — this is precisely differentiation. But when the Tathāgata sees this with his own buddha-eye, which knows things as they truly are, it is like the many streams entering the sea and losing their own original flavor — this is precisely the undifferentiated. Speaking in accordance with what others wish to hear, expounding by means of wisdom's skillful means — this is the Tathāgata's being able to know the differentiated; and every dharma that is expounded arrives, in every case, at the ground of the wisdom of all modes — this is the Tathāgata's being able to know the undifferentiated (and so on, as before).

From "the Tathāgata knows this" onward, fourth, citing what came before, this concludes and summarizes his ability to know. "A single mark, a single flavor" and the rest are as explained before. That the single mark, single flavor, liberation, departing, cessation, and the rest are distinguished for the sake of the conditions being taught is precisely the immeasurable within the one; that "ultimate nirvāṇa, in the end returning to emptiness" is precisely the one within the immeasurable. This, citing what came before, once more explains the undifferentiated.

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? If the single mark, the single flavor, liberation, departing, and cessation were the very substance of the two lesser vehicles' own dharma, this would still be differentiated speech; but here it is made the Great Vehicle's own ultimate nirvāṇa, in the end returning to emptiness — and this is entirely undifferentiated. "Ultimate nirvāṇa" concludes every phrase before it as being, in no case, the two lesser vehicles' nirvāṇa-with-remainder or nirvāṇa-without-remainder, but rather this very ultimate nirvāṇa. "The mark of constant, quiescent cessation" concludes every phrase as being not the cessation of the Lesser Vehicle, but rather constant and abiding cessation. The text above says, "every dharma, from its very origin, is constantly, of itself, the mark of quiescent cessation" — this is exactly this same sense. "In the end returning to emptiness" is not the emptiness of ashen extinction, but rather the emptiness of the middle way, the ultimate meaning — spoken solemnly, striking the palm together, to distinguish the real from what merely differs as the provisional. The ancients said that "in the end returning to emptiness" means that, though one's spiritual powers extend one's lifespan immeasurably, manifesting oneself again and again, redoubling the number beyond what came before, once the lifespan is exhausted, one returns, in the end, to ashen extinction — hence it is said, "in the end returning to emptiness." But this is the suffering of a suffering buddha, the suffering of a suffering sūtra — how could this possibly be said? Guangzhai [Fayun] says: "returning, in the end, to entering [nirvāṇa]-with-remainder, relinquishing the impermanent body and its wisdom." Someone has raised a difficulty with this reading: if this were so, how would it differ at all from the two lesser vehicles? The sūtra text raises two instances of "ultimate": the first, "ultimate, arriving at the wisdom of all modes," raises up the fruit of wisdom, set against the two lesser vehicles' wisdom, which is not ultimate; the second, raising up "ultimate nirvāṇa, the mark of constant, quiescent cessation, in the end returning to emptiness," raises up severance, set against the two lesser vehicles' severance, which is not ultimate. From the very text of "ultimate," one knows that this is not the emptiness of the Lesser Vehicle. Master Longyin says: "the emptiness of great nirvāṇa is called emptiness because it is without the mark of any dharma and without any affliction, and, in the end, returns to constant, abiding, ultimate-meaning emptiness." Master Zhong says: "in the end, it returns to the wisdom of ultimate-meaning emptiness." Someone says: "because the fruit of buddhahood is without any entanglement, it is called emptiness." A certain annotator says: "that both being and emptiness are utterly swept away is what is called emptiness." Every one of the ancient masters, without exception, refrained from any lesser-vehicle reading of this — why, then, should Guangzhai alone have read it so?

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

From "you, Kāśyapa" onward, second, the further sectarian confirmation and recounting, so as to resolve a doubt. The doubt is this: hearing of the Buddha's immeasurable merit, one might suppose that the four disciples' understanding, common only to the teaching, need not necessarily be real. Hence the Buddha confirms and recounts that, although it does not yet reach the very ground of buddhahood, what is common to the teaching is nonetheless not false.

This, too, is drawing forth the lesser capacity, so as to bring them likewise to awakening. The text falls into two: first, the recounting; second, the explanation. First, praising them as "most rare": an ordinary being who reflects and turns back, upon hearing, is able to obtain benefit; the bodhisattva, this being already his own affair, understands it without much wonder; but for one in the station of the unconditioned proper to be able to relinquish his own attainment and enter the real is most rare indeed — being able to know the expounding of the Dharma as fitting recounts their taking hold of the opening of the three. Next, saying they are able to receive it with faith directly recounts their taking hold of the revealing of the one. "Why is this" explains the sense of the recounting, making clear that the Buddha, upon the single path, taught three — profound, mysterious, and difficult to understand — and yet you were able to have faith in it. In my own view, the earlier text says, "the Tathāgata has, further, immeasurable merit, which you could never finish recounting," and the later text says, "you are most rare indeed" — the Buddha's kindness spreads universally, like cloud and rain that leave nothing unmoistened; the Buddha's kindness bears universally, like the great earth that leaves nothing ungrown — how could it possibly be for a single capacity, a single quarter, and nothing more? Hence it is said, "you could never finish recounting it." Though the Buddha's kindness is universal, sentient beings, in their daily use of it, are themselves unaware of it — just as the three herbs and two trees, rooted in the earth and receiving the moisture of the rain, are unable to know it. That you are able to know these ten kindnesses, beginning to end, is most rare indeed. "Those not yet delivered, I shall cause to be delivered," and the rest, recounts their knowing the kindness of the Buddha's four great vows — most rare indeed. Sentient beings being secure and at ease in this present life, born afterward in a good place, and receiving joy by means of the path, recounts their knowing the kindness of great loving-kindness that bestows joy — most rare indeed. Having heard the Dharma and being freed of every obstruction, and, according to the strength each is able to bear, gradually entering the path, recounts their knowing the kindness of great compassion that removes suffering — most rare indeed. The wheel-turning king, Śakra, and Brahmā being the lesser medicinal herb recounts their knowing the kindness of urging what is wholesome and removing the heat of affliction — most rare indeed. Knowing the uncontaminated dharma able to attain nirvāṇa, and the pratyekabuddha's own realization, being the middling medicinal herb, recounts their knowing the kindness of removing every heat of view and craving — most rare indeed. The superior herb and the small tree — this is being ashamed of the lesser and longing for the greater — recounts their knowing the kindness of sheltering them from shame — most rare indeed. The great tree recounts their knowing the kindness of adornment — most rare indeed. The single ground and single rain, the very matter of the real itself, recounts their knowing the kindness of the bestowal of wealth, the taking of the seat, and being at ease and free in body, mind, wealth, and dharma — most rare indeed. When the Buddha recounts these as differentiated praise, the text of the ten kindnesses is thereby exhausted; but when he recounts them as undifferentiated praise, it is precisely a single, great kindness.

Answer: if one holds the view that there is a prediction and a person predicted, this view must be broken through; but the bodhisattva's own vow and its prediction — this prediction must be granted. By the worldly truth, there is a prediction; by the truth of ultimate meaning, there is none. As for the four siddhāntas, applied as fitting, this is as explained below. There is the general prediction, as at the start of the "Dharma Teacher" chapter; there is the particular bestowal of prediction, as expounded after the three cycles of teaching; there is the prediction by way of the correct cause, as with Sadāparibhūta ("Never Disparaging"); there is the prediction by way of the conditioning cause, as with the ten kinds of offering in the "Dharma Teacher" chapter; there is the prediction by way of the revealing cause, as with the conferral upon persons of the three faculties; the prediction by way of the correct cause is broad, while that by way of the conditioning and revealing causes is narrow; some predictions are slow to come, others swift; some are the Buddha's own prediction, as in this text, others a bodhisattva's own prediction, as with Sadāparibhūta — and though such a one lacks the fixed determination of kalpa and land, this, too, counts as a prediction; and there is, further, the prediction given in advance, as with the future disciples in the "Phantom City" chapter. The *Yingluo Sūtra* [the *Sūtra of the Bodhisattva's Adornments*], in its ninth fascicle, sets out eight kinds of conferring the prediction: oneself knowing it while others do not; the many all knowing it while oneself does not; oneself and the many both knowing it; oneself and the many both not knowing it; the near being aware while the distant is unaware; the distant being aware while the near is unaware; both being aware; and both being unaware. As for the many all knowing it while oneself does not: one has aroused the mind broadly and vastly, and attained fearlessness and skillful means. Originally, every buddha, for the sake of the one great cause and condition, appears in the world, causing sentient beings to open, show, awaken to, and enter the Buddha's own knowledge and vision. Now that this great cause is already made manifest, the Buddha having already finished expounding it, and sentient beings having already entered it, the Buddha's own original intent is thereby conveyed, and the wish of sentient beings fulfilled — by rights, the prediction ought to be granted, for how could a father, meeting his own son, fail to bestow the wealth upon him? Again, the practitioner's vows and practice through immeasurable ages have their aim fixed upon the present buddha — the text says, "because their original vow was thus, they thereby obtain this very prediction" — these two conditions are why the prediction is granted by the mundane siddhānta. Again, the two lesser vehicles, hearing the sūtra, change from the lesser and enter the greater, their perfect cause now being complete, and a cause must inevitably call forth its fruit — hence the Tathāgata grants the prediction. The assembly of that time, all alike aware, arouse the vow, vowing to become, in body of birth and dharma-body alike, inward and outward members of the household, or else vowing merely to be born in that land so as to benefit sentient beings — these two are why the prediction is granted by the individual siddhānta. Again, the granting of the prediction to the two lesser vehicles breaks through the bodhisattva who wishes to retreat from the great and enter the lesser. Why is this? If it were fixed and certain that the two lesser vehicles could retreat and become lesser, then, there now being no [true] two lesser vehicles, what would there be left to retreat into? Again, it breaks through the one who wishes to arouse the mind of the two lesser vehicles: since even they attain realization and yet relinquish it, why should I myself take hold of it? Again, it breaks through the one who has not yet changed from the lesser, causing him thereupon to change from the lesser, while the one about to attain the realization of the lesser does not, after all, take hold of that realization. These four are why the prediction is granted by the therapeutic siddhānta. Again, non-arising being manifest before one, this must come by way of real understanding opening, without error, the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, and, moreover, by way of clearly understanding buddha-nature — and hence the prediction is granted. The Lesser Vehicle, entering the real, is fixed and certain to become buddha; if this is so, then, since every sentient being likewise possesses buddha-nature, why is the prediction not granted to all? Yet sentient beings, though possessing the correct cause, lack the [necessary] condition; now, hearing the sūtra, faith and understanding, condition and correct cause, are together complete, opening the Buddha's own knowledge and vision — knowing buddha-nature, seeing the Buddha's own dharma, and seeing buddha-nature — these two are why the prediction is granted by the siddhānta of ultimate meaning. These four kinds of prediction take in and exhaust every kind of receiving prediction discussed above (and so on, as before). "Conferring the prediction" is also called "receiving the prediction," "receiving the decisive verdict," and "receiving the elucidation" — "conferring" carries the sense of granting; "receiving" carries the sense of obtaining; "prediction" is the recording of the matter; "decisive verdict" is its being fixed and determined; and "elucidation" is its being made fully clear. The person of middling capacity, hearing the Dharma — the parable of the two cycles opening the three to reveal the one — fully takes hold of the understanding and the Tathāgata's own confirmation, and, though he himself already knows he shall become a buddha, the particulars of time and circumstance are not yet made certain; but, once he receives the Buddha's own sincere word, conferring upon him his future fruit, the kalpa and the land fixed and determined, and the near and the distant made fully clear, he then rejoices greatly. Since this is named from the Buddha's own conferring and granting, it is called the "Prediction" chapter.

Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the chapter "The Conjured City." "Conjured" names what spiritual power brings about: because of spiritual power, something that was not comes to be all at once, and this is called "conjured." "Guarding against wrongdoing and warding off the enemy" is what is meant by "city." Correlated within, to the nirvāṇa of the two lesser vehicles, this is what provisional wisdom brings about. By the power of provisional wisdom, what is not is spoken of as though it were; the teaching is used as the "conjuring," guarding against wrong thought and warding off wrong views, and this is named "nirvāṇa"; it revives and leads beings toward the real, yet is not itself the ultimate, and so it is called "cessation and deliverance" [parinirvāṇa]. 問：此品說因緣事，下根得悟，應名宿世品。

Question: this chapter expounds a matter of causes and conditions, by which those of the lowest capacity attain awakening — should it not be named "the chapter of past lives"?

答：品初廣說因緣，末則結譬化城，若從前應稱宿世，經從末故言化城。又上根疑薄，但取道樹三七思惟，以明機緣；中根疑濃加以譬喻，探取二萬億佛所教無上道，以為機緣；下根疑復厚，則明宿世久遠機緣。若從宿世之始，明久遠因緣，語其中間言其化城，明其究竟言其寶所，經家處中標品，收得初後，從此義便，故言化城品。

Answer: the opening of the chapter expounds the causes and conditions at length, while its close draws the parable of the conjured city to a conclusion — if it were named from its opening, it ought to be called "past lives," but the sūtra names it from its close, and so it is called "the conjured city." Moreover, for those of the highest capacity, whose doubt is slight, it takes up only the three-times-seven days of contemplation beneath the awakening tree to make clear the occasion of their capacity; for those of middling capacity, whose doubt is heavier, a parable is added, drawing on the unsurpassed path taught at the assemblies of twenty thousand koṭis of buddhas, as the occasion of their capacity; and for those of the lowest capacity, whose doubt is heavier still, the occasion of their capacity is made clear through the vastly distant past lives. If one takes the very beginning of those past lives, this makes clear the causes and conditions of the distant past; speaking of the interval between, one calls it "the conjured city"; and making clear its final point, one calls it "the place of treasure." Those who compiled the sūtra, standing at the mean, named the chapter so as to gather in both the beginning and the end — and it is for the convenience of this meaning that it is called "the chapter of the conjured city."

觀解，「忽生眾生」者，心性本淨，陰入界覆之則闇，若修觀慧本性理顯。又兩山是二諦，其間是中道。日月光是二智，佛光是中道。無分別智光，照本有三諦洞明也。「爾時東方」下，第二，十方梵。文為二：先九方，後上方。九方為四：一東，二東南，三南，四總明六方。前三方梵，文各有七：一覩瑞，二驚駭，三相問決，四尋光見佛，五三業供養，六請法，七默許，皆如文。上方梵止有六，世尊即說故無默許。舊云東、東南請小，七方請大，上方請小大。若《釋論》明梵本請大，佛雖說小未遂所請，若說般若猶酬梵請耳。若依〈方便品〉文，梵王請大，然佛法道同不應偏請。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the contemplation of mind: "beings that suddenly appeared" — the nature of mind is originally pure, and it is only the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements covering it over that make it dark; if one cultivates the wisdom of contemplation, its original nature and principle become manifest. Again, the two mountains between the sun's rising and setting are the two truths, and the space between them is the middle way. The light of sun and moon is the twin wisdoms of the provisional and the real, and the buddha's own light is the middle way. It is the light of non-discriminating wisdom, illuminating the three truths that were there all along, making them thoroughly clear. From "at that time, in the east" onward is, second, the Brahmā kings of the ten directions. This text has two parts: first, the nine directions; second, the direction above. The nine directions fall into four: first, the east; second, the southeast; third, the south; fourth, a general account covering the remaining six directions together. For each of the first three directions, the Brahmā kings' text has seven parts: first, witnessing the auspicious sign; second, being startled and alarmed; third, questioning one another and coming to a decision; fourth, following the light and seeing the buddha; fifth, making offerings with body, speech, and mind; sixth, requesting the Dharma; and seventh, the buddha's silent assent — all just as the text has it. For the direction above, the Brahmā king's account has only six parts, since the World-Honored One at once began to teach, and so there is no silent assent. The old reading holds that the east and southeast request the lesser teaching, the seven directions that follow request the greater, and the direction above requests both the lesser and the greater. If one follows the Treatise's account, the Brahmā kings originally request the greater; though the buddha in fact teaches the lesser, he has not yet fulfilled what was requested — it is only when he later teaches the Perfection of Wisdom that the Brahmā kings' request is at last answered. But if one follows the text of the "Skillful Means" chapter, the Brahmā king requests the greater, and yet the buddhas' path and Dharma are everywhere the same, and ought not to answer one request while ignoring the rest.

但經論存略，諸師偏據耳。又如今佛自始至終，具轉五味法輪，一一皆酬梵請，彼亦應然。初十六子請轉滿教，如今佛說《華嚴》；東、東南二方請轉半教，如今佛說三藏；後七方請轉對半明滿，如今佛說方等；上方梵請帶半明滿，如今佛說《般若》，後十六子請廢半明滿，如今佛說《法華》醍醐教也。今古節目文義相應(云云)。「爾時大通智受十方」下，結緣近由。由佛受請說法，故後得覆講正作結緣。文為二：一、先轉半字法輪，二、諸子請轉廢半明滿字法輪。初為三：一、受請，二、正轉，三、聞法得道。此中應說三乘，如序品文，而今不說者，正為下根論結緣開顯等，略不言六度耳。三轉者，謂示、勸、證(云云)。亦將三轉對示教利喜，示即示轉，教即勸轉，利喜即證轉也。亦對見諦思惟無學也，為聲聞三轉，為緣覺再轉，為菩薩一轉。何故爾？由根利鈍。此一往說耳。通方例皆三轉。何故三轉？諸佛語法，法至於三，為眾生有三根故，《大論》及《婆沙》悉作此說。問：初為五人，云何作三根耶？復有八萬諸天，何故無三根？為生三慧、三根、三道故。「十二行」者，一約四諦教，二約十六行。教十二者，即示勸證是也；行十二者，三轉皆生眼智明覺。又教十二為能轉，行十二為所轉。

It is only that the sūtras and treatises preserve this in abbreviated form, and the various masters each rely on one part of it alone. Moreover, just as the present buddha, from beginning to end, fully turns the Dharma wheel of the five flavors, answering every single Brahmā request one by one, so too must it have been with that ancient buddha. The sixteen sons' first request, to turn the complete teaching, corresponds to the present buddha's teaching of the Flower Garland Sūtra; the east and southeast directions' request, to turn the partial teaching, corresponds to the present buddha's teaching of the Tripiṭaka; the seven directions that follow, requesting a teaching that opposes the partial and makes plain the complete, correspond to the present buddha's teaching of the Vaipulya sūtras; the Brahmā king above, requesting a teaching that carries the partial while making plain the complete, corresponds to the present buddha's teaching of the Prajñāpāramitā; and the sixteen sons' later request, to set aside the partial and make plain the complete, corresponds to the present buddha's teaching of the ghee-teaching of the Lotus Sūtra. The chapter-headings of past and present thus correspond to one another in their text and meaning (and so on). From "at that time, Mahābhijñājñānābhibhū accepted the ten directions'" onward — that is, accepted their request — is the near reason for forming the bond. Because the buddha accepted the request and taught the Dharma, only afterward could there be the relay-teaching that directly forms the bond. This text has two parts: first, first turning the partial-letter Dharma wheel; second, the sons requesting that he turn the Dharma wheel that sets aside the partial and makes plain the complete letter. The first has three parts: first, accepting the request; second, the direct turning; third, hearing the Dharma and attaining the path. Within this, the three vehicles ought to be spoken of, as in the text of the Introduction chapter, and the reason they are not spoken of here is that this passage speaks precisely of forming the bond and opening the provisional to reveal the real for the sake of those of the lowest capacity, and so it simply omits mention of the six perfections. The three turnings are called: showing, urging, and verifying (and so on). One may also correlate the three turnings with "showing, instructing, benefiting, and delighting": showing is the turning of showing; instructing is the turning of urging; and benefiting and delighting is the turning of verifying. One may also correlate them with seeing the truth, cultivating contemplation, and being beyond further training: for the śrāvaka, there are three full turnings; for the pratyekabuddha, two turnings; and for the bodhisattva, a single turning. Why is this so? Because of the sharpness or dullness of their faculties. This, however, is only one way of speaking of it — as a general rule, throughout, there are always three turnings. Why three turnings? Because, in the way the buddhas speak the Dharma, the teaching reaches completion at three, since sentient beings have three kinds of faculties — the Great Treatise and the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā both say as much. Question: at first there were only the five men — how could there be three kinds of faculties among them? And there were, besides, eighty thousand gods present — why is there no mention of three kinds of faculties for them? It is in order to give rise to the three kinds of wisdom, the three kinds of faculties, and the three paths. As for "the twelve aspects": one may take this in terms of the teaching of the four noble truths, or in terms of the sixteen minds. The twelve aspects of the teaching are precisely showing, urging, and verifying; the twelve aspects of practice are that each of the three turnings gives rise to the eye, the wisdom, the clarity, and the realization. Again, the twelve aspects of the teaching are what does the turning, while the twelve aspects of practice are what is turned.

Ru-Yi Translation

The twelve aspects of practice are the wheel; the twelve aspects of the teaching are not the wheel. If one takes this as two wheels: as for "the eye, the wisdom, the clarity, and the realization" — in terms of the forty-eight dharmas, these four minds are opened out into sixteen minds. That is: the patience regarding the dharma of suffering is the "eye"; the knowledge of the dharma of suffering is the "wisdom"; the subsequent patience is the "clarity"; and the subsequent knowledge is the "realization" — and the remaining three truths (origin, cessation, and the path) are likewise, and so sixteen minds are formed. Each of the three kinds of person attains all sixteen minds, and so forty-eight aspects are formed. The twelve aspects of the truths are the Dharma wheel of the teaching; the twelve aspects are the Dharma wheel of practice. In the wheel of the teaching, what does the turning is only the single provisional wisdom, while what is turned consists of the twelve aspects of the teaching. In the wheel of practice, the teaching that does the turning has twelve aspects, and the practice that is turned likewise has twelve aspects. This may apply generally to all three kinds of person, or specifically to one kind alone. Here, with regard to the three kinds of person on the path of seeing the truth, those of sharp faculties, upon hearing the turning of showing, at once give rise to the eye, the wisdom, the clarity, and the realization — and since all three kinds of person are raised together, this is called "the twelve aspects." As for those unable to turn it: even the śramaṇa, not having heard it, cannot yet know it — how much less turn it? The pratyekabuddha, though awakened, cannot express it in words; the brāhmaṇa, though he may have heard its name, does not understand its principle; and it is likewise so for Māra and Brahmā. "Turning," properly speaking, means conveying this Dharma so that it enters another's mind, bringing that person to awakening and breaking the sixty-two views — only this is called "turning the Dharma wheel"; since Māra, Brahmā, and the rest lack this capacity, they cannot turn it. There is a reading that holds that, within the Great Vehicle's four noble truths, one next turns the two truths, next the one truth, and next the truth beyond all truths — all of which express the sense of folding in and unfolding. The four noble truths of the Lesser Vehicle take arising-and-ceasing as their essence; those of the Great Vehicle take non-arising as their essence (and so on).

「十二因緣」者，還是別相細觀四諦耳。約苦、集即有無明老死，約道、滅即有無明滅乃至老死滅也。又三人通觀十二緣，二乘生滅十二緣，為菩薩無生十二緣。無生十二緣，本不生、今不滅，相生則相、生傳傳滅(云云)。又三乘亦通論四諦，二乘有量四諦，為菩薩無量四諦。又六度亦通三人，《大品．發趣品》云「阿羅漢、支佛，因六波羅蜜至彼岸。」《攝大乘》云「凡夫、二乘皆有六度，但不同耳。」若爾應俱名波羅蜜，然二乘行到涅槃彼岸亦稱波羅蜜，但不能到佛道彼岸，比菩薩異耳。《阿毘曇》有六足，六足即六度，《寶雲經》明三乘毘尼(云云)。第三，聞法得悟者，初少中多。「不受」者，不受四見，悟初果也。「得解脫」者，脫子、果兩縛，得無學也。「深妙定」者，即俱解脫也(云云)。

As for "the twelve links of dependent origination": this, too, is simply a detailed contemplation of the four noble truths under its particular aspects. In terms of suffering and its origin, there is ignorance and old-age-and-death arising in sequence; in terms of the path and cessation, there is the cessation of ignorance and so on up through the cessation of old-age-and-death. Again, all three kinds of person may contemplate the twelve links in common, but for the two lesser vehicles the twelve links are of arising-and-ceasing, while for the bodhisattva the twelve links are of non-arising. In the twelve links of non-arising, what was originally unborn is now unceasing; where there is mutual arising there is mutual ceasing, and what arises passes on, in turn, to what ceases (and so on). Again, the three vehicles hold the four noble truths in common as well: for the two lesser vehicles the four noble truths are finite in measure, while for the bodhisattva the four noble truths are immeasurable. Again, the six perfections, too, are shared among the three kinds of person: the Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra, in its chapter on "Setting Forth," says, "the arhat and the pratyekabuddha reach the far shore by means of the six pāramitās." The Compendium of the Great Vehicle says, "the ordinary worldling and the two lesser vehicles all possess the six perfections, only they are not the same." If this is so, then all should equally be called "pāramitā" — and indeed, the two lesser vehicles' practice, reaching the far shore of nirvāṇa, is likewise called "pāramitā," only it is unable to reach the far shore of buddhahood, and this is where it differs from the bodhisattva's. The Abhidharma [Jñānaprasthāna] speaks of "the six feet," and these six feet are precisely the six perfections; the Ratnamegha Sūtra makes clear the vinaya of the three vehicles (and so on). Third, as for those who hear the Dharma and attain awakening: at first few, and later many. As for "do not accept": this means not accepting the four mistaken views, and awakening to the first fruit of stream-entry. As for "attains liberation": this means being freed from the two fetters of rebirth and of attachment to the fruit already attained, and attaining the state beyond further training. As for "the profound and wondrous concentration": this is precisely the liberation that is both of mind and of wisdom (and so on).

The Sixteen Princes Renew Their Request 十六王子重請說法

Chinese Source

「爾時十六王子皆出家」下，第二，重請滿字法輪。文為七：一、出家，二、請法，三、所將亦出家，四、佛受請，五、時眾有解不解，六、時節，七、說已入定。「諸根」者，六根也，六根清淨故言通利。又六根互用故言「通」，入佛境界故言「利」。「智慧明了」者，開示悟入也，彼佛初說圓頓，諸子大乘功德悉皆具足，愍諸方便重請佛開權顯實也。「聲聞皆已成就」者，明其障除機動，是故為請。「我等志願如來知見」者，此《法華經》但明佛之知見，唯志於此，即正請滿字廢半之文，明顯若此也。「過二萬劫」者，上開三既久，不容中間無事，望下文意，二萬劫中必說方等《般若》，文云「說六波羅蜜及諸神通事」，般若是行、神通是事，諸方等經多明不可思議事行也。頌中又云「分別真實法」，即是《大品》明實相般若意也。十六沙彌信受及二乘，即信得解者；其餘千萬皆生疑惑，是不解眾。此不解眾即與十六子，結法華之緣者也。第七，明說法已入定，此正是結緣之近由。由佛入定不出，諸疑惑眾無所諮問，十六於後為不解者覆講說經也。文中明入定處所，即是靜室，正入定即是住於禪定，入定時節即是八萬四千劫(云云)。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "at that time the sixteen princes, all of them, left the household" onward is, second, the renewed request for the complete-letter Dharma wheel. This text has seven parts: first, leaving the household; second, requesting the Dharma; third, those they led likewise leaving the household; fourth, the buddha accepting the request; fifth, the assembly at that time, some understanding and some not; sixth, the passage of time; and seventh, having finished teaching, entering samādhi. As for "faculties": this means the six sense faculties — because the six faculties were pure, it is said they were "thoroughly sharp." Again, because the six faculties can be used interchangeably, it is called "thorough"; and because they enter the buddha's own domain, it is called "sharp." As for "wisdom, clear and understanding": this is opening, showing, awakening, and entering. That ancient buddha had at first taught the perfect and sudden teaching, and the sons were already fully endowed with every merit of the Great Vehicle; taking pity on beings needing the various skillful means, they made a renewed request that the buddha open the provisional to reveal the real. As for "the śrāvakas have all already accomplished what they sought": this makes clear that their hindrances were removed and their capacity had stirred, and this is why the request was made. As for "our resolve and wish is set on the Tathāgata's own knowledge and vision": this Lotus Sūtra makes clear only the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, and their resolve is set on this alone — this is precisely the text of the direct request for the complete letter that sets aside the partial, made plain just as it appears. As for "after twenty thousand kalpas had passed": since the opening of the three vehicles above already took so long, there could hardly have been nothing happening in the interval; looking to the sense of the text below, within these twenty thousand kalpas the Vaipulya teachings and the Prajñāpāramitā must surely have been taught — the text says, "expounding the six pāramitās and the matters of the various spiritual powers"; the Prajñāpāramitā is the teaching of practice, and the spiritual powers are the teaching of events, and the Vaipulya sūtras for the most part make clear inconceivable events and practices. The verse, further, says "distinguishing the true and real Dharma," and this is precisely the sense of the Prajñāpāramitā of true reality made clear in the Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra. The sixteen novices who received it with faith, together with those of the two lesser vehicles, are those who believed and attained understanding; the remaining thousands and myriads all gave rise to doubt and confusion, and these form the assembly that did not understand. This assembly that did not understand is precisely the one that, together with the sixteen sons, formed the bond that leads to the Lotus. Seventh, making clear that, having finished teaching the Dharma, he entered samādhi — this is precisely the near reason for the forming of the bond. Because the buddha entered samādhi and did not emerge, the assembly caught in doubt and confusion had no one to consult, and it was the sixteen who, afterward, gave the relay-teaching of the sūtra for the sake of those who did not understand. The text makes clear the place where he entered samādhi — namely, a quiet chamber; his directly entering samādhi — namely, abiding in meditative concentration; and the length of time he remained in samādhi — namely, eighty-four thousand kalpas (and so on).

The Bond Directly Formed 正結緣

Chinese Source

「是時十六菩薩知佛入室」下，第二，正結緣。就此有二：先法說結緣，次譬說結緣。就法說有三：第一明昔日共結緣，第二明中間更相值遇，第三明今還說法華。第一有四：一者知佛入定，二者王子覆講，三者眾得利益，四佛從定起稱歎菩薩。由佛入定所以得說，佛知一化將畢不復熟此段之人，故令王子共其結緣；又知此等必由王子究竟得度，所以入定久而不出也(云云)。「是十六沙彌」下，第一，知佛入定。「各升法座」下，第二，覆說《法華》。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "at that time, the sixteen bodhisattvas, knowing that the buddha had entered his chamber" onward is, second, the direct forming of the bond. Within this there are two: first, forming the bond as spoken in the dharma-teaching; second, forming the bond as spoken in the parable-teaching. Within the dharma-teaching there are three: first, making clear the common forming of the bond in past ages; second, making clear their meeting one another again in the interval; third, making clear the return, in the present, to teaching the Lotus. The first has four parts: first, knowing that the buddha had entered samādhi; second, the princes' relay-teaching; third, the assembly's gaining of benefit; and fourth, the buddha rising from samādhi to praise the bodhisattvas. It was precisely because the buddha entered samādhi that this teaching could take place — the buddha, knowing that his single transformative work was about to be complete, and that those in this group were not yet ripe, made the princes form the bond together with them; and, knowing besides that these beings must, in the end, be delivered by means of the princes, he for this reason remained long in samādhi and did not emerge (and so on). From "these sixteen novices" onward is, first, their knowing that the buddha had entered samādhi. From "each ascending the dharma-seat" onward is, second, the relay-teaching of the Lotus.

「一一皆度」下，第三，說法利益。皆發菩提心故云「度」，若初發心時誓願當作佛已過於世間，即是度七方便彼岸義也。「大通智勝過八萬」下，第四，佛從定起稱歎勸信。此中復二：一、正稱歎菩薩，二、「汝等皆當」下，勸物親近。勸物親近中又二：先勸親近，次「所以者何」下，第一、釋勸意，「佛告諸比丘是十六」下，第二、明中間常相逢值。逢值有三種，若相逢遇常受大乘，此輩中間皆已成就不至于今；若相逢遇，遇其退大仍接以小，此輩中間猶故未盡，今得還聞大乘之教；三但論遇小不論遇大，則中間未度，于今亦不盡，方始受大乃至滅後得道者是也。

From "each and every one he delivered" onward is, third, the benefit of the teaching. Because they all gave rise to the resolve for awakening, it is called "delivered" — for as soon as one first gives rise to this resolve, vowing to become a buddha, one has already passed beyond the world, and this is precisely the sense of crossing to the far shore of the seven expedient means. From "Mahābhijñājñānābhibhū, after eighty thousand kalpas" onward is, fourth, the buddha rising from samādhi to praise them and to urge faith. This, again, has two parts: first, directly praising the bodhisattvas; second, from "you should all" onward, urging beings to draw near to them. Urging beings to draw near to them, again, has two parts: first, urging them to draw near; second, from "why is this" onward, explaining the intent behind this urging. From "the Buddha told the monks: these sixteen" onward is, second, making clear that in the interval they constantly met one another again. There are three kinds of such meeting: if they met and constantly received the Great Vehicle, then these beings had already reached completion within that same interval, and had not to wait until now; if they met, and the one who had retreated from the great was still received with the lesser, then these beings had not yet exhausted that interval, and only now come to hear again the teaching of the Great Vehicle; and third, if only their meeting with the lesser is spoken of, and not their meeting with the great at all, then they were not yet delivered within that interval, nor are they exhausted even now — these are the ones who only now, for the first time, receive the great, up to and including those who attain the path only after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa.

A Question on the Long Road to Arhatship 問答：塵點劫方成羅漢

Chinese Source

問：如上塵數多許時節，今始得羅漢，當知無生法忍何易可階。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: given such an inconceivable, dust-like number of ages as described above, only now do they attain arhatship — one should then know how difficult it truly is to ascend to the patient acceptance of non-arising.

答：一云，大聖善巧，依四悉檀作如是說，或說佛道長遠，或說佛易得，對治厭道長者說短，於道生輕易想者說長，或為發生宿善，或隨世間所欲，或為聞說長短即得入第一義，當知言如許劫方今得羅漢者，此是權行四悉檀，引諸實行令入道耳。

Answer: one reading holds that the great sage, skillful in his means, speaks in this way in accordance with the four siddhāntas: sometimes he speaks of the buddha-path as long and distant, and sometimes he speaks of it as easily attained. To counter those who, wearied of the long path, regard it as too great a burden, he speaks of it as short; to those who form the thought that the path is something to be taken lightly, he speaks of it as long. Sometimes this is done to bring forth wholesome roots from past lives; sometimes it accords with what beings in the world themselves desire; and sometimes it is simply so that, upon hearing the path spoken of as long or as short, they might at once enter the siddhānta of ultimate meaning. One should understand, then, that speaking of attaining arhatship only after so many kalpas is the provisional practice of the four siddhāntas, drawing beings of genuine practice onward so that they may enter the path.

As for "the time for nirvāṇa has arrived": whenever a buddha appears in the world, at the time his teaching and guidance are about to be complete, he teaches this sūtra — as with Kāśyapa Buddha, Candrasūryapradīpa Buddha, and the rest, who taught this sūtra and then entered nirvāṇa; and Śākyamuni, having taught the Lotus, likewise proclaims that he shall soon pass away. As for "the assembly, moreover, is pure": this is precisely the virtue of severance. As for "their faith and understanding are firm": faith is the fourfold unshakable faith, and understanding is the correct, undefiled understanding. Fully penetrating the truth of ultimate reality, and complete with every meditative concentration — this is the establishing of wisdom and severance. At that time they were fit to be taught the great path, and, hearing it, would certainly receive it with faith. Again, "the assembly, moreover, is pure" may also mean that they had attained the benefit of the Tripiṭaka teaching, and were spared its dangers. "Their faith and understanding are firm" means that, with regard to the Vaipulya teaching, their hearts came to trust one another mutually. "Fully penetrating the dharma of emptiness" means that, having heard the Prajñāpāramitā teaching expounded, their minds came to full penetration within the dharma of emptiness — this is precisely the sense of the relay-teaching. From "and so he gathered together the bodhisattvas" onward is the direct teaching of the Lotus. Gathering the bodhisattvas is the gathering together of the kinsfolk and the rest, and it is at this gathering that this sūtra is taught. What was explained earlier — that the kinsfolk are the great beings of the dharma-body who appear as an answering assembly — is here confirmed, and its meaning made plain by this very text. "Gathering the bodhisattvas" is the gathering of the kinsfolk; "and the assembly of śrāvakas" is the summoning of the son. From "monks, you should know the Tathāgata's skillful means" onward explains the intent behind opening the three vehicles. If there is, in the world, no one of the two lesser vehicles who truly attains parinirvāṇa, why did the Tathāgata earlier teach the provisional teaching at all? The explanation runs: monks, you should know that the Tathāgata's skillful means come from a deep knowledge that sentient beings have a lesser nature and desire, clinging to the five sense-objects and worn down by the five turbidities — and it is for this reason that he first taught the three, so as to break through their obstructions and spare them from danger, and only afterward taught the one.

The Five Hundred Yojanas: A Survey of Readings 五百由旬異解

Chinese Source

「五百由旬」者，基師：三界結惑為三百，七地所斷習氣為四百，八地已上所斷無明為五百。今謂非正別義，又非三乘通義。又有家云，流來生死、變易生死、中間生死、分段生死，但取三種開為五百，不取流來；流來是有識之初、反源之始，故不說之。有人難此云，《勝鬘》云「因五果二」，果二者謂分段、變易；因五者謂五住。語果既別開流來與中間，語因亦應更廣五住。無據不可用。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "five hundred yojanas": Master Ji holds that the afflictions bound up with the three realms make the three hundred, the habitual tendencies severed on the seventh ground make the four hundred, and the ignorance severed from the eighth ground onward makes the five hundred. This, it seems to me, is neither the correct meaning of the distinct teaching nor the shared meaning of the three vehicles. There is also a school that speaks of birth-and-death as flowing on, birth-and-death of transformation, birth-and-death of the intervening state, and birth-and-death by allotted portion — taking only three of these to open into the five hundred, and not taking "flowing-on" birth-and-death; "flowing-on" is the very beginning of consciousness, the starting point from which one turns back to the source, and this is why it is not spoken of here. Someone objects to this, saying: the Śrīmālā Sūtra speaks of "five causes and two effects" — the two effects being birth-and-death by allotted portion and birth-and-death of transformation, and the five causes being the five abodes of affliction. Since, in speaking of the effect, it already opens "flowing-on" apart from the "intervening" state, then, in speaking of the cause, it ought likewise to further expand the five abodes of affliction. This has no basis, and cannot be used.

《大論》明肉身菩薩即分段，法身菩薩謂變易。又云，阿羅漢捨三界報身受法性身，故知生死二耳。有人云，三百喻三界，四百喻七地，二國中間難過，五百喻八地已上。難者言，四百喻七地，則應三百喻六地，六地與二乘齊功，二乘極久唯六十劫、或百劫，菩薩至六地時，二十二大僧祇，二乘於佛道紆迴不應得齊。今謂此非別義亦非通義。

The Great Treatise makes clear that the physical-bodied bodhisattva undergoes birth-and-death by allotted portion, while the dharma-bodied bodhisattva undergoes birth-and-death of transformation. It also says that the arhat relinquishes the retribution-body of the three realms and receives instead a body of the dharma-nature — from which one knows that birth-and-death is of these two kinds only. Some say: the three hundred stands for the three realms, the four hundred stands for the seventh ground, the difficulty of passing between these two realms is what lies between, and the five hundred stands for the eighth ground and beyond. Those who object to this say: if the four hundred stands for the seventh ground, then the three hundred ought to stand for the sixth ground — yet the sixth ground is equal in accomplishment to the two lesser vehicles, and the two lesser vehicles, at the very longest, take only sixty or a hundred kalpas, while the bodhisattva, upon reaching the sixth ground, has already passed through twenty-two great asaṃkhyeya kalpas — the two lesser vehicles' own circuitous path to buddhahood ought not, then, to be counted as equal to it. It seems to me that this is neither the meaning of the distinct teaching nor the shared meaning of the three vehicles.

有人言，三界為三百，七住及二乘為四百，七住已上為五百，如《大經》云「初果八萬劫至菩提心處」，如三根人至此處即領解，五種人至此處名度五百也，此取極鈍故云八萬劫到，利人不必爾，如佛世得四果者，聞《法華》皆發心，何必八萬劫？難云，經明度三百由旬立二地，豈是度三人也？若五人並發菩提心名度五百者，乃大經之一意，明五人發心離於五位，非此中意。此中明三百是權度在化城，五百至寶所名實度，廢化城進寶所，若五人皆度皆進，失化城譬意。

Some say: the three realms make the three hundred, the seventh abiding together with the two lesser vehicles make the four hundred, and everything from the seventh abiding onward makes the five hundred — as the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "the first fruit of stream-entry reaches the place of the resolve for awakening after eighty thousand kalpas." Just as persons of the three kinds of faculties, on reaching this place, at once receive and understand it, so the five kinds of person, on reaching this place, are said to have "crossed the five hundred." This is taken with regard to the very dullest of faculties, and this is why it speaks of "reaching it after eighty thousand kalpas" — for those of sharper faculties it need not be so; just as those who attained the fourth fruit of arhatship in the buddha's own lifetime, on hearing the Lotus, all at once gave rise to the resolve — how could this require eighty thousand kalpas? Those who object to this say: the sūtra makes clear that crossing the three hundred yojanas establishes the two grounds of the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha — how, then, could this mean the crossing of three kinds of person? If it is instead the five kinds of person together giving rise to the resolve for awakening that is called "crossing the five hundred," this is indeed one sense found in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, making clear that the five kinds of person give rise to the resolve and depart from the five stages, but this is not the sense intended here. What is made clear here is that the three hundred is the provisional deliverance found within the conjured city, while the five hundred, reaching the place of treasure, is called the real deliverance — the conjured city is set aside and one advances to the place of treasure. If the five kinds of person are all delivered and all advance equally, this loses the very sense of the parable of the conjured city.

有人云，三界為三百，聲聞為四百，緣覺地為五百，凡夫三界障，二乘涅槃障，亦是有空二見。《華嚴》藥樹不生深水火坑，火坑即三界，深水即二乘，三界是二乘之牢獄，二乘是菩薩之牢獄，又是福智二邊，不能自行不能化他。《大品》明四百由旬，合二乘為一百，《法華》開為五百。《大品》明菩薩度凡聖二地，未明二乘是權，闕化城之意，既未論化城，亦未明寶所。

Some say: the three realms make the three hundred, the śrāvaka stage makes the four hundred, and the pratyekabuddha stage makes the five hundred — the ordinary worldling's obstruction is the three realms, and the two lesser vehicles' obstruction is nirvāṇa itself, which is likewise the two views of existence and of emptiness. In the Flower Garland Sūtra, the medicinal tree does not grow in deep water or in a pit of fire — the pit of fire is precisely the three realms, and the deep water is precisely the two lesser vehicles; the three realms are the prison of the two lesser vehicles, and the two lesser vehicles are the prison of the bodhisattva — these are, again, the two extremes of merit and of wisdom, unable either to cultivate themselves or to transform others. The Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra makes clear the four hundred yojanas, combining the two lesser vehicles into a single hundred, while the Lotus opens this out into the five hundred. The Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra makes clear the bodhisattva's crossing of the two grounds of the ordinary worldling and of the noble one, but does not yet make clear that the two lesser vehicles are themselves the provisional, and so it lacks the sense of the conjured city — since it does not yet discuss the conjured city, neither does it make plain the place of treasure.

From "at that time, the guide, knowing this" onward is, third, dissolving the conjured city and leading them on to the place of treasure. Within this there are two: first, knowing that they have rested; second, heading toward the place of treasure. As for "having now rested and being no longer weary": this parallels the arrival of the time for nirvāṇa, above — the assembly, moreover, being pure, and spared from danger, with the great capacity now stirred. "And at once caused the conjured city to vanish," leading them toward the place of treasure — this parallels the direct teaching of the Lotus, above, showing the true and real mark of all things. The place of treasure carries two senses: taken as the ultimate, the extreme fruit of buddhahood is the place of treasure — the text above says, "only a buddha, together with a buddha, can fully fathom the true reality of all dharmas." Taken as a partial entering, the abiding of the very first arising of the resolve is the place of treasure — hence the text above says, "the unsurpassed treasure, sought by us not at all, yet attained," and again says, "attained a portion of the Buddha's Dharma" — what a child of the Buddha ought to attain, he has already attained in full. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "the stream-enterer reaches it after eighty thousand kalpas" — reaching, that is, the place of the very first arising of the resolve for awakening. This is taken with regard to the dullest of faculties, who, left to their own natural course, reach it only after eighty thousand or ten thousand kalpas and so on; but for one like those of the four fruits within the Tripiṭaka teaching, who enter the great without the passing of any great length of time at all, how could eighty thousand or ten thousand kalpas possibly be required (and so on)?

Old Debates: The Cart and the City 舊問：車城之辨

Chinese Source

舊問：車城皆譬無生智，車何故無？城何故有？而車三城一、車動城靜？

Ru-Yi Translation

An old question: the cart and the city both stand as parables for the wisdom of non-arising — why, then, does the cart not exist while the city does exist? And why are there three carts but only one city, the cart in motion while the city is still?

答：長者道門外有車，有隔故諸子不見，可得假設門外有車，車實無也；城在逈地不得假設，故城是有也。就理教者，執三教取理，則三教皆得理，此即有義如城也；將理取教，理既唯一，此教即無三家之果，即車義據無也。車三城一者，諸人同息一處，所以城一也；車就三人，三人所樂不同故三。理教者，三家盡無生不異，如城；三人正使雖同盡，而習氣有盡不盡，有傍得知見，有不得者，故用此莊嚴盡無生，此義不同如三車。三家盡無生智，明因盡果亡，此處是極，如城靜；盡無生智運入無餘，如車動也。難(云云)。今明約眾生心，車、城俱有；約佛智明，亦有亦無。權智所明為有，如城；實智所明為無，如車(云云)。化城正意為退大取小人，傍為發軫學小人。上二周正意，為發軫學小人，傍為退大人也。三車通今昔，化城正是引教意，未道是化也。

Answer: outside the elder's gate there was a cart, but a partition kept it hidden, so that the sons could not see it — one may thus provisionally posit that there is a cart outside the gate, when in reality there is no cart at all. The city, however, stands out in the open ground and cannot be provisionally posited in this way — and this is why the city does exist. Taken with regard to the teaching and the principle: if one holds fast to the three teachings in order to grasp the principle, then all three teachings alike attain the principle — this is the sense in which there is something, like the city. If one takes hold of the teaching by way of the principle, then, since the principle is but one, this teaching has no fruit belonging to the three separate houses — this is the sense in which the cart is held to be nonexistent. As for there being three carts but one city: since all these people alike come to rest in a single place, the city is one; the cart, however, is taken with respect to the three kinds of person, whose delights are not the same, and so it is three. Taken with regard to the teaching and the principle: the exhaustion of arising, which all three houses alike attain, does not differ — it is like the city. As for the three kinds of person: though they alike exhaust the direct outflows, their habitual tendencies are exhausted in some and not in others; some attain, incidentally, knowledge and vision, and some do not — and this is why this adornment of the exhaustion of arising is used, and why this sense differs, like the three carts. The wisdom of the exhaustion of arising possessed by all three houses makes clear that, the cause exhausted, the effect too is gone — this place is the ultimate, like the city being still; while the wisdom of the exhaustion of arising, conveying one into the nirvāṇa without remainder, is like the cart in motion. This objection stands (and so on). Now it should be made clear: taken with regard to sentient beings' own minds, both cart and city exist; taken with regard to the Buddha's own wisdom made manifest, both exist and do not exist. What provisional wisdom makes manifest is existence, like the city; what real wisdom makes manifest is non-existence, like the cart (and so on). The direct intent of the conjured city is for those who retreat from the great and take up the lesser; incidentally, it is for those first setting out to study the lesser. In the two periods above, the direct intent was for those first setting out to study the lesser, and incidentally for those who retreat from the great. The three carts run through both present and past alike; the conjured city is directly the sense of drawing beings onward through the teaching, and it has not yet been said that this itself is the conjuring.

問：化為三車，與化城何異？

Question: the conjuring made into the three carts — how does this differ from the conjured city?

答：三車為說法輪作譬，化城為神通輪作譬。又車約聲作譬，諸子聞而不見；城為色作譬。問：城與二使云何？使能指示，如教詮理；城為息患，教動而城靜。教即四諦、十二緣有異；城是二智入無餘不異。教通因果；城車但在果。教通有為無為；城車但在無為。權智謂車是無，名教施設故；實智謂車是有，無離文字說解脫故。權智照城為有，引眾生故；實智照城是無，偏真非實故。權智照車是三，逗三緣故；實智照車是一，俱會一乘故。權智照城為一，是偏真故；實智照城為三，如來藏故。權智照城為靜，是灰斷故；實智照城為動，滅此化故。權智照車為運，運入無餘故；實智照車為靜，不動不出故。作如此釋豈與舊同？舊秖在小乘中作義。

Answer: the three carts are a parable made for the wheel of the teaching of the Dharma; the conjured city is a parable made for the wheel of spiritual powers. Again, the cart is a parable made with respect to sound — the sons hear of it but do not see it; the city is a parable made with respect to form. Question: how do the city and the two hindrances of the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha compare? The hindrances are able to be pointed out and shown, like the teaching expounding the principle; the city is for the sake of resting from affliction — the teaching is in motion, while the city is still. The teaching, that is, the four noble truths and the twelve links, differs between vehicles; the city, that is, the twin wisdoms entering the nirvāṇa without remainder, does not differ. The teaching runs through both cause and effect; the city and the cart lie only within the effect. The teaching runs through both the conditioned and the unconditioned; the city and the cart lie only within the unconditioned. Provisional wisdom holds that the cart does not exist, since it is a device set up through the teaching's own naming; real wisdom holds that the cart does exist, since liberation is never spoken of apart from the written word. Provisional wisdom illuminates the city as existing, since it draws sentient beings onward; real wisdom illuminates the city as not existing, since it is one-sidedly true but not the real. Provisional wisdom illuminates the cart as three, meeting three different conditions; real wisdom illuminates the cart as one, since all alike are gathered into the one vehicle. Provisional wisdom illuminates the city as one, since it is one-sidedly true; real wisdom illuminates the city as three, since it is the tathāgatagarbha holding all three within it. Provisional wisdom illuminates the city as still, since it is the cessation of ashes and the extinguishing of consciousness; real wisdom illuminates the city as moving, since this very conjuring is itself dissolved. Provisional wisdom illuminates the cart as conveying, since it conveys one into the nirvāṇa without remainder; real wisdom illuminates the cart as still, since it neither moves nor departs. How could an explanation made in this way be the same as the old reading? The old reading remains, after all, confined within the meaning of the Lesser Vehicle alone.

問：凡五處開三顯一，為有何異？

Question: altogether, there are five places where the three are opened to reveal the one — what differences are there among them?

答：通論無異，別論有差。〈方便品〉約教開三顯一，文云「如來但以一佛乘故，為眾生說法，無二亦無三」也。〈火宅〉約行開三顯一，車是運義，運則譬行，文云「各乘大車遊於四方，嬉戲快樂」也。〈信解〉中約人開三顯一，結會傭作之人即是長者之兒，「我等昔來真是佛子」也。〈藥草喻〉中，約差別無差別明權實，不的去取，但明眾生不知，佛令其知。若七種差別即知權，同依一理無差別即知實。差別無差別，無差別而差別，令知此意耳，終不說言無一有一，此約自行權實二智隨自意語，故佛能知而眾生不知也。亦是通前通後，知不知明權實也。今化城正約理開三顯一，寶所化城，皆是小大兩理，破除二乘化理，顯於寶所真實一理也。下去五百領解舉珠為譬，亦是約理也。

Answer: taken generally, there is no difference; taken specifically, there are distinctions. In "Skillful Means," the three are opened to reveal the one in terms of the teaching — the text says, "the Tathāgata, only by means of the one buddha vehicle, teaches the Dharma to sentient beings; there is no second, and no third." In the "Burning House," the three are opened to reveal the one in terms of practice — the cart carries the sense of conveying, and conveying is a parable for practice; the text says, "each riding in the great cart, they roamed in every direction, at play and full of joy." In "Faith and Understanding," the three are opened to reveal the one in terms of the person — correlating the hired laborer with the elder's own child, "we, from of old, have truly been children of the Buddha." In the "Parable of the Medicinal Herbs," the provisional and the real are made clear in terms of difference and non-difference, without settling definitely on taking one and rejecting the other — it simply makes clear that sentient beings do not know, and that the Buddha causes them to know. If the seven kinds of difference among plants are known, then the provisional is known; if the single principle they share alike, without difference, is known, then the real is known. Difference within non-difference, and non-difference within difference — this is meant only to make this sense known, and it never once says there is no one vehicle and yet there is one, or the reverse — this is spoken in terms of the twin wisdoms of the provisional and the real within one's own practice, spoken according to one's own intent, and this is why the Buddha is able to know it while sentient beings do not. This, too, runs through what came before and what follows, making clear the provisional and the real through knowing and not knowing. Here, in the conjured city, the three are opened to reveal the one directly in terms of principle — the place of treasure and the conjured city are both the two principles of the lesser and the greater, breaking through the provisional principle of the two lesser vehicles so as to reveal the true, single principle of the place of treasure. Further on, in the "Five Hundred's" receiving and understanding, the parable of raising up the pearl is likewise taken in terms of principle.

Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the chapter "The Prediction for Five Hundred Disciples." This chapter, in fact, bestows the prediction on twelve hundred — why, then, does it take its title from "five hundred"? It is because the five hundred receive their prediction under a single, shared name, and it is the five hundred who voice aloud their own receiving and understanding, and this is why the chapter is titled from them. This chapter is the second section within the exposition through causes and conditions. Within the bestowal of predictions there are two parts: first, the prediction given to the twelve hundred; second, the prediction given to the two thousand. The twelve hundred, again, has two parts: first, the bestowal on Pūrṇa; second, the bestowal on the twelve hundred. The bestowal on Pūrṇa, again, has two parts: first, narrating his silent receiving and understanding; second, the Tathāgata's own account and prediction. First, it narrates his gaining understanding and his joy; next, it narrates his silently contemplating and receiving and understanding. His joy, again, has two parts: first, narrating the reason for his gaining understanding; second, narrating his joy at gaining understanding. The reason for his gaining understanding has four parts: first, hearing the Dharma parable, the two periods that open the three to reveal the one; second, the bestowal of predictions on the five great disciples such as Śāriputra; third, hearing, further, the matter of the bond formed in past lives; and fourth, hearing, further, of the buddhas', the Tathāgatas', threefold, unobstructed penetrating knowledge. Contemplating that vast distance as though it were today is precisely the power of great, self-mastering spiritual penetration, censuring the two lesser vehicles as different, confined to a mere eighty thousand kalpas. As for "having heard from the Buddha this wisdom": this receives what was revealed as the real within "Skillful Means" and the "Burning House"; "taught by skillful means according to what is fitting" receives the opening of the provisional in both those places. "The great disciples" receives the opening of the provisional; "bestowing the prediction of awakening" receives the revealing of the real; "the karmic connections from past lives" receives the revealing of the real; and "spiritual power" receives the opening of the provisional. From "gained what he had never had before" onward narrates his joy at gaining understanding — first making clear the inward joy of his understanding, and next the outward form of his reverence. Because what he had never before heard — the opening of the provisional to reveal the real — he now, for the first time, has heard, it is said, "gained what he had never had before"; because he had removed his love of nirvāṇa and severed the particular delusions, it is said, "his heart pure"; because the Buddha's own knowledge and vision was opened to him, it is for this reason "leaping with joy"; and because his understanding came by way of the Buddha, reverence arose in him. Taken in terms of the trace and the origin, this is simply rejoicing in every true and real practice.

「而作是念」下，正明默念領解。初明默念領解，次明默求發迹請記。上二周得悟，皆發言領解，此何默念？上為下根未悟，事須彰言勸動；今下根已悟，無所勸動，故默念不言。又上來但領解不求發迹言則不嫌，今則亦解亦發，亦解故念，避物譏嫌故默，默念允宜也。又默念領解是大領解，如《淨名》默然是真入不二法門也。

From "and thought to himself" onward is the direct account of his silent contemplation and receiving and understanding. First, it makes clear his silent contemplation and receiving and understanding; next, it makes clear his silently seeking to have his own trace disclosed and to request a prediction. In the two periods above, where others attained awakening, all voiced their receiving and understanding aloud — why, here, is it silent contemplation instead? Above, it was for the sake of those of the lowest capacity, not yet awakened, that the matter had, of necessity, to be voiced aloud so as to move and encourage them; here, those of the lowest capacity have already awakened, and there is nothing left to encourage, and this is why it is silent contemplation, unspoken. Moreover, in what came before, only receiving and understanding was involved, with no request to have one's trace disclosed, and so speaking it aloud gave no offense; here, both understanding and disclosure are involved together — because there is understanding, he contemplates it silently, and, to avoid the criticism of others, he keeps silent — silent contemplation is precisely what is fitting here. Moreover, silent contemplation and receiving and understanding are themselves the great receiving and understanding — just as, in the Vimalakīrti Sūtra, Vimalakīrti's silence is the true entering of the gate of non-duality.

又權實不可思議，非言非念而言而念，非言而言故上來口陳領解，亦非念而念，今則默念領解。上來何意不求發迹？為下根未悟，是故不求發迹。今下根已解，權化事足也。若下根發迹，則知中上亦權；若約上中，則於下不便故也。「世尊甚奇特所為希有」者，領實智也。「隨順世間若干種性而為說法」，即領權智也，是七種方便之根性也。此領〈方便品〉中開權顯實意也。「拔出眾生處處貪著」者，即是領〈火宅〉中開權顯實也。「我等於佛功德言不能宣」者，領上《藥草喻》中如來有無量功德汝等所不能及也。既云言不能宣，亦是念所不及也。「唯佛能知」下，即是默念求發迹請記也。「我等」者，通念請發諸人迹也。「深心」是本今，現作是迹。「本願」者，大慈誓願也，大慈下化故我為誓，上求作佛故我有願。請上求即是求記，請說下化即求發迹。又從深心故，明其三世助佛宣化；從本願故即與授記也。

Again, the provisional and the real are inconceivable — neither spoken nor thought, and yet spoken and thought. Because it is not spoken, and yet is spoken, there was, above, receiving and understanding voiced aloud; because it is not thought, and yet is thought, here there is silent contemplation, receiving and understanding. Why, above, was there no seeking to have one's trace disclosed? Because those of the lowest capacity had not yet awakened, and so no disclosure of trace was sought. Now that those of the lowest capacity have already come to understanding, the work of provisional transformation is complete. If those of the lowest capacity have their trace disclosed, then it will be known that those of the middling and highest capacities are likewise provisional; and if this is taken up with regard to the middling and highest, it would be unfitting for those of the lowest capacity — this is why it comes only now. "The World-Honored One is exceedingly extraordinary, and what he does is rare indeed" receives the real wisdom. "According to the every kind of nature found in the world, he teaches the Dharma" receives the provisional wisdom — this is the nature and capacity of the seven kinds of skillful means. This receives the sense of opening the provisional to reveal the real within the "Skillful Means" chapter. "Drawing beings out from their attachments wherever they cling" is precisely receiving the opening of the provisional to reveal the real within the "Burning House." "We could never express in words the Buddha's own merit" receives, from above, the "Parable of the Medicinal Herbs," where the Tathāgata's measureless merit is something none of you can match. Since it is said that words cannot express it, this too is something thought cannot reach. From "only the Buddha can know" onward is precisely the silent contemplation seeking to have his trace disclosed and requesting a prediction. As for "we": this refers, all together, to the thought of requesting that everyone's own trace be disclosed. "The deep wish" is the origin manifesting, now, as this trace. As for "the root vow": this is the vow of great compassion — because of this great compassion, transforming beings below, there is a vow made on my part; and because of the seeking of buddhahood above, there is a wish held by me. To request what is sought above is precisely to request the prediction; to request the speaking of what is transformed below is precisely to seek the disclosure of the trace. Again, from the deep wish, it becomes clear that, throughout the three periods of time, he has assisted the Buddha in proclaiming and transforming beings; and from the root vow, the prediction is thereupon bestowed.

The prose has two parts: first, the account of the trace and the origin; second, the bestowal of the prediction. The very establishing of it, saying "do you see" carries two senses: first, whether they see his trace as that of the lesser; second, whether they see the merit of his origin.

The assembly saw only the trace, taking him for a śrāvaka, and were unable to know that his origin was that of a bodhisattva — and this is why it says "do you see." From "I have always called him" onward sets forth his trace. As a trace, he is foremost among those who teach the Dharma — yet were it not for the wondrous root that is the dharma-body, there would be no way for so foremost a trace to be manifested. In the past, it was said only that, within his trace, he was foremost in teaching the Dharma; now it is otherwise — within the unsurpassed Dharma, he has long since attained the foremost place. This raises the trace so as to reveal the origin. From "and have likewise always praised" onward sets forth his fundamental ground. The gates of the Dharma, the myriad practices of merit and wisdom, are why it says "of every kind." Since his fundamental ground already holds gates of the Dharma of every kind, how, then, could his trace be merely that of the two lesser vehicles?

此舉本以明迹也。「精勤護持」下，別釋也。「助宣我法」者，即是迹中助宣半滿之法。迹為下根聲聞，即是護持助宣酪法；迹在方等示受彈訶，即是護持助宣生蘇法；迹領《般若》即是護持助宣熟蘇法；迹在《法華》得悟，即是護持助宣醍醐法。上總本中云「我常稱歎種種功德」者，即此意也。具足權實功德，而迹起五味，助佛調熟實行眾生，豈非精勤助宣之意也。別釋本迹功德，「能於四眾示教」者，分別半字教也；「具足解釋」者，助宣滿字般若教也；「而大饒益」者，助佛饒益半滿之眾生也；「同梵行者」是迹所化半滿弟子也。「自捨如來」下，別述本地功德也。「自捨」者，降妙覺已來也。「無能知」者，七種方便也。

This raises the origin so as to make clear the trace. From "diligently upholding and proclaiming" onward is the separate explanation. As for "proclaiming my Dharma": this is, within his trace, proclaiming both the partial and the complete Dharma. His trace, as one of the lowest capacity, a śrāvaka, is precisely his upholding and proclaiming the milk-teaching; his trace, within the Vaipulya period, manifesting as one who receives rebuke and correction, is precisely his upholding and proclaiming the fresh-butter teaching; his trace, receiving the Prajñāpāramitā, is precisely his upholding and proclaiming the refined-butter teaching; and his trace, coming to awakening within the Lotus, is precisely his upholding and proclaiming the ghee-teaching. Above, in speaking of his fundamental origin as a whole, it says, "I have always praised his every kind of merit" — this is precisely this same sense. Fully complete with the merit of both the provisional and the real, and his trace giving rise to the five flavors, assisting the Buddha in ripening beings of true and real practice — how could this not be precisely the sense of diligently upholding and proclaiming? Separately explaining the merit of his trace and origin: "able to teach, instruct" the fourfold assembly means distinguishing the partial-letter teaching; "his complete explanation" means proclaiming and upholding the complete-letter teaching of the Prajñāpāramitā; "great benefit" means assisting the Buddha in benefiting beings of both the partial and the complete; and "his fellow practitioners" are precisely his trace's own disciples, transformed by both the partial and the complete teachings. From "apart from the Tathāgata himself" onward is a separate account of the merit of his fundamental ground. As for "apart from": this means from the time he descended from the wondrous awakening onward. As for "none can match": this refers to the seven kinds of skillful means.

「汝等勿謂助宣我法」下，第二，就過去佛世顯其本行，非直止於我所助宣半滿之法，久遠佛所亦復助宣半滿之法；取今日助宣為發迹，取過去助宣為顯本。顯本有二：一、遠本，二、近本。遠本冥邈，為信良難，故略而不述，但舉九十億近本，有宿命智能知近本，故舉近以證遠。就九十億文，具明助佛宣揚五味之教調熟眾生。「護持助宣」，即擬助宣半字酪味法：「佛之正法」即擬助宣方等生蘇味法；又於「空法明了」者，即擬助宣熟蘇味法，亦如今佛轉教說於《般若》，明六波羅蜜互相收攝旋轉無礙，九十億佛所，亦助宣揚如今無異，彼佛世人咸皆謂之實是聲聞，于時既未發迹，但謂被加命轉般若，不言是大菩薩也；「化無量眾生令立三菩提」者，即是助宣醍醐味法，在文可解(云云)。「亦於七佛」下，第三，約三世佛所修因，如文。此亦例前助宣半滿五味之法，利益大小也。◎

From "you should not suppose that he only assisted in proclaiming my Dharma" onward is, second, revealing his fundamental practice with regard to the ages of past buddhas — not confined only to his assisting in proclaiming my own partial and complete Dharma, but, in ages long past as well, likewise assisting in proclaiming the partial and complete Dharma. Taking today's assisting proclamation as the disclosed trace, and taking his assisting proclamation in the past as the revealed origin. The revealed origin has two parts: the distant origin, and the near origin. The distant origin is dim and remote, truly hard to give faith to, and so it is stated only in brief and not fully narrated; only the near origin of ninety koṭis of buddhas is raised, since it is by the wisdom of past lives that the near origin can be known — and so the near is raised in order to bear witness to the distant. Within the text on the ninety koṭis, it is fully made clear how he assisted the buddhas in proclaiming the teaching of the five flavors, ripening sentient beings. "Upholding and proclaiming" corresponds to assisting in the proclamation of the partial-letter, milk-flavored dharma; "the buddha's true Dharma" corresponds to assisting in the proclamation of the Vaipulya, fresh-butter-flavored dharma; and, further, "clear and penetrating in the emptiness taught" corresponds to assisting in the proclamation of the refined-butter-flavored dharma — just as the present buddha, in his own relay-teaching, expounds the Prajñāpāramitā, making clear how the six pāramitās mutually gather one another in, turning without obstruction; in the presence of the ninety koṭis of buddhas, he likewise assisted in proclaiming it, no different from now, and the people of that buddha's world all alike took him to be truly a śrāvaka, since at that time his trace had not yet been disclosed, and it was said only that he had been charged with relaying the teaching of the Prajñāpāramitā, and it was not said that he was a great bodhisattva. "Transforming measureless sentient beings, causing them to establish the threefold awakening" corresponds to assisting in the proclamation of the ghee-flavored dharma, and can be understood from the text itself (and so on). From "and, under seven buddhas as well" onward is, third, with regard to the causal practice cultivated across the buddhas of the three periods of time, just as the text has it. This, too, follows the pattern of what came before — assisting in the proclamation of both the partial and the complete across the five flavors, benefiting both the lesser and the greater.
