Apparent Extinction, Never Extinct

This page continues Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Fahua Wenju (妙法蓮華經文句). Fascicle 9 closes 安樂行品 ("Peaceful Practices"), covers 從地踊出品 ("Bodhisattvas from Beneath the Earth"), and opens 如來壽量品 (the "Tathāgata's Lifespan" chapter) — one of the sutra's most important chapters, where the Buddha reveals that his awakening lies immeasurably far in the past, and that his apparent passing into nirvāṇa is itself a skillful means, never an actual extinction. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Neither Distance nor Nearness: Contemplating the Emptiness of All Dharmas 非遠非近：觀一切法空

Chinese Source

妙法蓮華經文句卷第九上　天台智者大師說◎非遠非近文為三：一、總標境智，二、別釋，三、結成。「觀」者，中道觀智也，「一切法」者，十法界境也。若單論智，智無所觀，故舉一切以顯皆空。「如實」去，別釋也。二邊三諦無一異名「如」，非七方便故名「實」，以實為相故言「如實相」。「不顛倒」者，無八倒也。「不動」者，不為二死所動也。「不退」者，心心寂滅入薩婆若海也。「不轉」者，不如凡夫轉生死，不如二乘轉凡聖。「如虛空」者，但有名字，字不可得，中道觀智亦但有字，求不可得。「無所有性」者，無自、他、共、無因等性也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Commentary on the Sūtra of the Lotus Blossom of the Wondrous Dharma, Fascicle Nine, Upper Part. Spoken by the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai. The text on "neither distance nor nearness" has three parts: first, setting forth the object and the wisdom that knows it together as a whole; second, explaining each in turn; third, concluding the discussion. "Contemplating" means the contemplative wisdom of the middle way; "all dharmas" means the objective realm of the ten dharma-realms. If wisdom alone were spoken of, wisdom would have nothing to contemplate — hence "all" is raised up, so as to show that everything, without exception, is empty. From "true" onward is the explanation given in turn. That the two extremes and the three truths carry no difference between them is named "true"; that this lies beyond the reach of the seven kinds of expedient practitioners is named "reality"; and it is because reality itself serves as the very mark that it is called "the true mark of reality." "Undistorted" means free of the eight distortions. "Unmoved" means not moved by the two kinds of birth-and-death. "Never turning back" means that, thought after thought, in utter quiescence, one flows into the ocean of omniscience. "Unchanging" means not subject to change as an ordinary worldling changes through birth-and-death, nor as the two lesser vehicles change from the ordinary to the sagely. "Like empty space" means that it possesses only a name, and even this name cannot be grasped — the contemplative wisdom of the middle way, too, possesses only a name, which likewise cannot be found however one searches for it. "Without any nature of its own being" means it possesses no nature of self, of other, of both together, or of no cause at all.

「一切言語道斷」者，不可思議也。「不生」者，惑智理皆不生也。「不出」者，如來所治，畢竟不復發也。「不起」者，諸方便皆寂滅也。「無名」者，名不能名也。「無相」者，相不能相也。

"Every path of language is cut off" means it is inconceivable. "Unborn" means that affliction, wisdom, and principle alike are, none of them, ever born. "Unemergent" means that what the Tathāgata has already remedied never arises again to the very end. "Unarisen" means that every expedient means has already come to quiescent rest. "Nameless" means that no name is able to name it. "Markless" means that no mark is able to mark it.

「無所有」者，無二邊之有也。「無量」者，非數法也。「無邊」者，無方所也。「無礙無障」者，遍一切處也。「但以因緣有」者，結也。上直明中道觀慧，今明雙照二邊，理性畢竟清淨如上所說，非解非惑，而從惑因緣生生死，從解因緣生涅槃。又因緣有有於涅槃，從顛倒生者生於生死，此則雙照意顯也。「常樂觀如是等法」者，即三諦等法也。又「但因緣有從顛倒生」者，結不思議三諦境也。「故說」者，不思議教也。「常樂觀」者，結不思議三觀也。又觀一切法空如實相，標觀體；不顛倒去九句，釋觀相。不為二邊八倒所動，名不倒不動；不墮二乘凡夫二地，故云不退不轉。

"Without anything at all" means without the being of either of the two extremes. "Immeasurable" means it is not a dharma subject to number. "Boundless" means it has no location, no direction. "Unhindered, unobstructed" means it pervades every place without exception. "Only through causes and conditions does it have being" concludes the passage. Above, the text made clear directly the contemplative wisdom of the middle way; here it makes clear the illumining of both extremes at once — the nature of principle is, in the end, pure exactly as described above, neither an understanding nor a delusion, and yet from the causes and conditions of delusion, birth-and-death is produced, while from the causes and conditions of understanding, nirvāṇa is produced. Again, having being through causes and conditions is having being with regard to nirvāṇa, while what is produced from distorted views is produced with regard to birth-and-death — and this is precisely where the meaning of illumining both at once is disclosed. "Constantly and joyfully contemplating dharmas such as these" refers to the dharmas of the three truths and their like. And again, "only through causes and conditions does it have being, and it is produced from distorted views" concludes the discussion of the object of the inconceivable three truths. "And so it is taught" refers to the inconceivable teaching. "Constantly and joyfully contemplating" concludes the discussion of the inconceivable threefold contemplation. Furthermore, "contemplating the emptiness of all dharmas, their true mark of reality" sets forth the essence of the contemplation, while the nine phrases from "undistorted" onward explain the mark of the contemplation. Not being moved by the two extremes or the eight distortions is named "undistorted, unmoved"; not falling into either of the two stages of the two lesser vehicles or of the ordinary worldling is why it says "never turning back, unchanging."

此二句明智用理。非未來故不生，非過去故不出，非現在故不起，《釋論》五十一云「如虛空，無入、無出、無住相」，《攝大乘》亦爾，無未來入處，無過去出處，無現在住處。第四十三云「因邊不起名不出，緣邊不起名不生。」凡有十九句，初一句總，後十八句對《大品》十八空。如實相即第一義空；不顛倒即內空，內無六入我我所；不顛倒不動即外空，外不為六塵流動也；不退者即內外空，十二入空故，故言不退；不轉即空空，空破諸法，諸法是所破，空是能破，無復諸法唯有空在，此空亦空故言空空；空既空故無復能轉，故言不轉也；如虛空即是大空；執方計破故言如虛空；無所有性即畢竟空；諸法無遺餘故，名畢竟空；以畢竟空故，無所有性也，一切言語道斷即一切空，一切空不可說故言語道斷；不生即有為空，有為是因緣和合，既不合即不生，不出即無為空；無名出離，出離法空故名不出；不起即無始空，求原初不可得故無起；無名即性空可解；無相即相空；實無所有即不可得空；無量即有法空；有法即有量，有量既空故言無量無邊即無法空；無法則是邊表，今空故則無邊無礙，即有法無法空；二不可得故言無礙，無障即散空，妨障不可得故言無障。十八空皆是中道正慧，皆名為空，隨十八種境故言十八耳。《大經》云「如來常修十八空義故」，故用十八空，用釋十八句也。

These two phrases make clear the working of wisdom with regard to principle. Not being of the future is why it is unborn; not being of the past is why it is unemergent; not being of the present is why it is unarisen. The Treatise, fascicle fifty-one, says: "Like empty space, it carries no mark of entering, of emerging, or of abiding" — and the Compendium of the Great Vehicle says the same: there is no place of entering belonging to the future, no place of emerging belonging to the past, and no place of abiding belonging to the present. Its fascicle forty-three says: "Not arising with regard to cause is named unemergent; not arising with regard to condition is named unborn." In all, this passage carries nineteen phrases: the first is a summary statement, and the remaining eighteen correspond, one by one, to the eighteen kinds of emptiness of the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra. "The true mark of reality" is precisely the emptiness of ultimate meaning. "Undistorted" is precisely internal emptiness, for within there is no six sense-gates, no self, and nothing belonging to self. "Undistorted, unmoved" is precisely external emptiness, for without, one is not swept along by the six sense-objects. "Never turning back" is precisely internal-and-external emptiness, since the twelve sense-fields are themselves empty, and hence it says "never turning back." "Unchanging" is precisely the emptiness of emptiness: emptiness itself breaks apart every dharma, every dharma being what is broken and emptiness being what does the breaking, so that no dharma remains and only emptiness itself is left — and yet this very emptiness, too, is itself empty, and this is why it is called "the emptiness of emptiness"; and since emptiness is itself empty, there remains nothing left that could still be changed, which is why it says "unchanging." "Like empty space" is precisely great emptiness, since it breaks apart even the clinging to spatial direction, and this is why it says "like empty space." "Without any nature of its own being" is precisely ultimate emptiness, since no dharma leaves anything whatsoever remaining, which is why it is named ultimate emptiness — and it is precisely because of this ultimate emptiness that it is "without any nature of its own being." "Every path of language is cut off" is precisely the emptiness of all dharmas, since the emptiness of all things cannot be spoken of, and this is why every path of language is cut off. "Unborn" is precisely the emptiness of the conditioned, since the conditioned is what arises from the coming together of causes and conditions, and, this coming-together no longer occurring, it is unborn; "unemergent" is precisely the emptiness of the unconditioned, for release is what is meant by "namelessness," and it is because this very release is itself empty of any dharma that it is named "unemergent." "Unarisen" is precisely the emptiness without beginning, since no origin can ever be found however one searches for it, and this is why there is no arising. "Nameless" is precisely the emptiness of inherent nature, which may be readily understood; "markless" is precisely the emptiness of specific marks. "In truth, without anything at all" is precisely the emptiness of the unattainable. "Immeasurable" is precisely the emptiness of what has dharmas, since to have dharmas is to have measure, and, this very measure being empty, it is called immeasurable; "boundless" is precisely the emptiness of what has no dharmas, since to have no dharmas would itself mark out a limit — yet, this too being empty, there is then no boundary and no hindrance, and this, then, is precisely the emptiness both of what has dharmas and of what has none, for neither of the two can ever be attained, which is why it says "unhindered." "Unobstructed" is precisely the emptiness of dispersal, since no obstacle or hindrance can ever be attained, which is why it says "unobstructed." All eighteen kinds of emptiness are none other than the correct wisdom of the middle way, and every one of them is named "emptiness" — it is only because they follow eighteen distinct objects that they are spoken of as eighteen in number. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "because the Tathāgata constantly cultivates the meaning of the eighteen kinds of emptiness" — and this is why the eighteen kinds of emptiness are used here, used to explain these eighteen phrases.

The Verses of Practice-Ground and Approach-Ground: Ten Things to Keep at a Distance 偈頌行近二處：十種當遠

Chinese Source

偈有二十八行三句，為三：初一行，頌標章；次二十二行，頌修行；後五行三句，明行成。長行行、近別釋，偈中合頌，正言意同開合互現，廣略之解彌復可依。上行、近二文各有三，今偈合頌不復次第。初、「應入行」下十四行，頌事遠近，上有十種遠離，頌中略不次第，在文可見。亦是頌人空行處，取意即兼頌近處三意，故偈云「是則名為行處近處」。「常離國王」者，比丘親近國王有十非法：一、陰謀王命，二、王誅大臣，三、典藏亡寶，四、宮人懷妊，五、王身中毒，六、大臣諍競，七、二國交兵，八、王悋不施民，九、歛民物，十、多疾疫，謂比丘行呪。有此十事，一切臣民謂是比丘所作，作此謗比丘，即謗法亦謗佛，故佛不令親近王也。「外道梵志」者，《摩隥伽經》云「初人名梵天造一韋陀。次名白淨，變一為四：一名讚誦韋陀，二名祭祀，三名歌詠，四名禳災。一一各三十二萬偈，合成一百二十八萬偈，有一千七百卷也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The verse section has twenty-eight lines and three further half-lines, and forms three parts: the first line versifies the setting-forth of the section; the next twenty-two lines versify the cultivation of the practice; and the final five lines and three half-lines make clear the accomplishment of the practice. Where the prose section explained the practice-ground and the approach-ground each in turn, the verse section versifies them together in combination — the exact wording differs, but the meaning is the same, opening and combining each disclosing the other in turn, and the fuller and the more concise explanations may each still be relied upon. Above, the two prose sections on the practice-ground and the approach-ground each had three parts of their own; here the verse versifies them together in combination, no longer in the same sequence. First, the fourteen lines from "one should enter into the practice" onward versify the phenomenal aspect of distance and nearness. Above there were ten things to keep at a distance; within the verse these are given briefly and out of sequence, as may be seen from the text itself. This, too, versifies the practice-ground in terms of the emptiness of person, and, taking the sense of it, versifies together as well all three senses of the approach-ground — hence the verse says, "this, then, is what is meant by the practice-ground and the approach-ground." As for "constantly keeping at a distance from kings": a monk who draws near to a king risks ten improprieties: first, plotting against the king's own life; second, the king's own execution of a minister; third, the keeper of the treasury losing its jewels; fourth, a palace woman becoming pregnant; fifth, poison being found within the king's own body; sixth, ministers contending and quarreling; seventh, two kingdoms going to war; eighth, the king being stingy, giving nothing to his people; ninth, the seizing of the people's own goods; and tenth, an abundance of plague and pestilence, said to be the monk's own doing through the working of spells. Should any of these ten things occur, every minister and commoner will say that it was the monk's own doing, and, raising this slander against the monk, will thereby slander the Dharma as well, and slander the Buddha besides — and this is why the Buddha does not allow a monk to draw near to a king. As for "the brahmin followers of non-Buddhist paths": the Mātaṅga Sūtra says: "The first man was named Brahmā, who composed a single Veda. The next was named Śuddha, 'the pure and white one,' who transformed this one Veda into four: the first named the Veda of Praise and Recitation, the second the Veda of Sacrifice, the third the Veda of Song, and the fourth the Veda of Averting Calamity — each of these, thirty-two myriad verses in length, together making a hundred and twenty-eight myriad verses in all, comprising one thousand seven hundred fascicles.

次名弗沙，有二十五弟子，各於一韋陀能廣分別，遂有二十五韋陀。次有人名鸚鵡，變一韋陀為十八。次有人名善道，有二十一弟子，變為二十一韋陀。如是展轉變為千二百六韋陀也。」毘陀論此云智論，婆耶娑造，凡四種：一信力毘陀，明事火滅罪；二、耶受毘陀，明供養婆羅門得福；三、娑摩毘陀，明和合二國；四、阿陀婆毘陀，明鬪戰。讀此四論自稱一切智人。毘伽羅此名記論，婆尼尼造，明種種經書并諸雜語。衛世師論，優留佉造，此翻最勝，出世八百年明六諦。迦毘羅此翻黃頭，亦云龜種，造論名僧佉，僧佉此云無頂，因人名論故言迦毘羅，說二十五諦。「小乘三藏學者」，佛在波羅柰，最初為五人說契經修多羅藏；佛在羅閱祇，最初為須那提說毘尼藏；佛在毘舍離獼猴池，最初為跋耆子說阿毘曇藏。五百羅漢初夜集阿毘曇藏《相續解脫經》，此為三藏學也。

Next was one named Puṣya, who had twenty-five disciples, each able to broadly distinguish a single Veda, and so there came to be twenty-five Vedas. Next there was one named Parrot, who transformed a single Veda into eighteen. Next there was one named Good Path, who had twenty-one disciples, and transformed the Veda into twenty-one. In this way, passed down and transformed generation after generation, it became one thousand two hundred and six Vedas." Vyākhyāna, here translated "treatise of wisdom," composed by Vyāsa, is of four kinds in all: first, the Veda of Faith's Power, teaching the fire-rite that extinguishes transgression; second, the Veda of Rightful Receiving, teaching that making offerings to brahmins wins merit; third, the Sāman Veda, teaching the harmonizing of two kingdoms; and fourth, the Atharva Veda, teaching warfare. One who has studied all four of these treatises calls himself a man of all-knowing wisdom. Vyākaraṇa, here named "treatise of records," composed by Pāṇini, sets forth every kind of scripture and every manner of miscellaneous discourse. The Vaiśeṣika treatise, composed by Ulūka, is here translated "the most excellent"; it arose eight hundred years after the Buddha, and expounds six categories. Kapila, here translated "Tawny-Head," also called "Tortoise-Seed," composed the treatise named Sāṃkhya — Sāṃkhya here translated "peerless" — and, the treatise being named for the man, it is spoken of as Kapila's own; it expounds twenty-five categories. As for "students of the Tripiṭaka of the lesser vehicle": while the Buddha was at Vārāṇasī, he first taught the sūtra-basket to the five ascetics; while the Buddha was at Rājagṛha, he first taught the vinaya-basket to Sudinna; while the Buddha was at the Monkey Pond of Vaiśālī, he first taught the abhidharma-basket to a son of the Vṛjis. Five hundred arhats, in the first watch of the night, compiled the abhidharma-basket together with the Sūtra of Successive Liberations — and this constitutes the study of the Tripiṭaka.

「深著五欲」，欲相者，四天下人、龍、須輪、四天王皆根相到，忉利天以風為事，炎天相近為事，兜率相牽為事，化樂天相視為事，他化自在心念為事，上天皆離欲。「寡女處女」者，阿難問佛：如來滅後見女人云何？佛言：勿與相見，設見勿共語，設共語當專心念佛。「及諸不男」，彼名般吒者，此翻黃門。黃門者，有男女形不能男女。「入里乞食」者，《雜阿含》云「有一羊往糞聚飽食，還群貢高我得好食，比丘亦如是，得四事已起染著欲想不知出要，設不得，恒生想，設得，向諸比丘貢高毀篾他人，我得彼不能得，是為羊比丘乞食。師子王遇大獸即噉，不味不著，得小獸即噉，不鄙不薄；比丘亦爾，得四事供養不起染著無有欲想自知出要，設不得利養，不起亂念無增減心，是為師子王比丘乞食。乞食行役病四事，而前後八時明八精進、八懈怠。乞食前作是念，為修道補飢瘡，乞雖未得不廢念行；乞食得已，為報恩念道不輟，前後兩時倍加精進。餘三事前後亦如是，反此名八懈怠。《寶雲經》明乞食作四分：一分奉同梵行，一分與匃人，一分施鬼神，一分自食。

"Deeply attached to the five desires": as for the mark of desire, human beings of the four continents, dragons, asuras, and the Four Heavenly Kings all consummate desire through the joining of the sense-organs; the Trāyastriṃśa heaven takes wind as its consummation; the Yāma heaven, mutual nearness; the Tuṣita heaven, mutual grasping of hands; the Nirmāṇarati heaven, mutual gazing; and the Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven, a mere thought arising in the mind — while the heavens above these are all free of desire altogether. As for "widows and unmarried women": Ānanda once asked the Buddha, "After the Tathāgata's own extinction, how should one look upon women?" The Buddha said: "Do not look upon them; and should you happen to see one, do not speak with her; and should you happen to speak with her, then fix your mind wholly on mindfulness of the Buddha." As for "and every eunuch": these are called paṇḍakas, here translated "yellow gate" — a "yellow gate" being one who, though possessed of a male or female form, is capable of neither. As for "entering the village to beg for alms": the Saṃyukta Āgama says: "There was once a sheep that went to a dung-heap and ate its fill, then returned to the flock, boasting of its own fine meal — and a monk may be exactly the same: once he has obtained the four requisites, he gives rise to defiled, craving thoughts and does not know the way of release; and if he fails to obtain them, he forever longs for them, while if he does obtain them, he boasts before the other monks and disparages everyone else, saying, 'I have obtained what others could not' — this is what is called 'the sheep-monk's way of begging.' When the lion king encounters a great beast, he devours it, feeling neither relish nor attachment; and when he obtains a small beast, he devours it as well, feeling neither disdain nor contempt — a monk should be exactly the same: having obtained the offering of the four requisites, he gives rise to no defiled attachment and no craving thought, himself knowing the way of release; and should he fail to obtain any benefit, he gives rise to no disordered thought and no mind of increase or decrease — this is what is called 'the lion-king monk's way of begging.'" Begging for alms, traveling on errands, and illness are the four matters, and, morning and evening across eight periods, this discourse makes clear eight kinds of diligence and eight kinds of laziness. Before begging for alms, one should reflect: "I do this to sustain my practice of the path, healing the wound of hunger" — and even should one's begging go unrewarded, one should not thereby abandon mindful practice; and once one has received alms, one should be mindful of the path in gratitude and never let this lapse — in both these periods, morning and evening, diligence is doubled. The remaining three matters, morning and evening, are likewise so; the reverse of this is what is called the eight kinds of laziness. The Ratnamegha Sūtra makes clear that alms received in begging should be divided into four parts: one part offered to those who share the pure conduct, one part given to beggars, one part offered to spirits, and one part kept for oneself.

Completing the Body Peaceful Practice 身安樂行明行成

Chinese Source

「又復不行」下，第二，八行，頌非遠非近理遠近處。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "and again, neither doing" onward is, second, eight lines, versifying the principled distance and nearness of "neither distance nor nearness."

The practice of calming has four aspects: first, not speaking of others' faults; second, not treating others with contempt; third, not praising or disparaging; and fourth, not harboring resentment or aversion. First, as to not delighting in speaking of the faults found in others' scriptures: a person may have faults in what he has heard, but what fault could the Dharma itself possess? The dharmas of the seven kinds of expedient means are the Buddha's own words spoken to accord with others' capacities, and are named "of provisional meaning" — and to fault their dharma would be to trouble the very people who hold them, which is not the mark of the peaceful practice at all. Second, as to "nor treating others with contempt": one should not, relying on the perfect teaching, disparage the partial teachings, nor prize the real while slighting the provisional. Third, as to not speaking of others' merits and faults: this first forbids speaking of any person whatsoever, and next singles out the śrāvakas in particular. A person hates to hear his own faults spoken aloud, and so one does not discourse on his shortcomings; and to praise a person to his face while disparaging him behind his back is why one does not proclaim his merits either; nor should one, in speaking to one person, praise a second in order to mock the first, since to praise another's merit is thereby to needle at one's own shortcoming, using the one to satirize the other; nor should one, in speaking to one person, disparage a second, since to speak ill of one behind his back is likewise to speak ill of oneself behind one's own back — and it is for this reason that both praise and blame alike are to be set aside. And again, just as the sūtras speak of the practice of emptiness: one reads and recites it oneself, and teaches others to read and recite it, without slandering others, without speaking of others' faults, and without praising one's own merits. As for the śrāvakas, their capacities, too, are not fixed: to praise the two lesser vehicles risks causing them to retreat from the great vehicle and take hold of the small; while to disparage the two lesser vehicles risks causing them to lose both great and small alike, grasping neither. Fourth, as to "not giving rise to a mind of resentment or aversion": to think that a certain person's dharma obstructs one's own path is precisely a mind of resentment, and to think him base and inferior is precisely a mind of aversion — the moment such a motive stirs in the mind, it is voiced aloud at once, and it is by blocking off the very source from which fault-finding speech arises that one gives rise to no resentment or aversion.

Third, the mind peaceful practice likewise has both a prose section and a verse section. The prose, too, has three parts: setting forth the section, explaining the practice, and concluding with its accomplishment. Within the explanation, too, calming comes first and contemplation follows. Calming has four aspects: first, not envying or flattering; second, not treating others with contempt or reviling them; third, not troubling or disturbing others; and fourth, not contending or competing. Now, the two lesser vehicles, wishing swiftly to escape birth-and-death, first eliminate craving and desire, while the bodhisattva first eliminates hatred and wrong views — envy is the defilement of hatred, and flattery is the defilement of wrong views. Envy and jealousy run contrary to the mind of loving-kindness and compassion, and are no method by which to transform others; flattery and deceit run contrary to the path of wisdom, and are no method by which to cultivate oneself. If wisdom is obstructed, by what means could one seek the path above? If loving-kindness and compassion are hindered, by what means could one transform beings below? A bodhisattva of the peaceful practice must, above all, cast these two aside. From "nor treating others with contempt or reviling them" onward: one ought not, by means of the conduct of the perfect teaching, rebuke the distinct teaching — one may reprove where the person's capacity permits reproof, but must not revile where it does not, for there is the risk of causing a retreat from good roots already planted. From "monk" onward: one ought not, by means of the perfect teaching, rebuke the shared teaching — for if the person originally has no capacity for the great vehicle, and one forcibly rebukes him by means of the perfect teaching, this runs contrary to his mind and only becomes a torment; having already been rebuked with regard to the shared teaching, and not yet having understood the perfect teaching either, he is left doubting what came before and regretting what comes after, losing both the great and the small alike. Straying circuitously from the path is named "exceedingly far," and this torments the practitioner of the distinct teaching; sinking into emptiness and taking hold of its realization is named "not attained," and this torments the practitioner of the shared teaching. Being weary of birth-and-death is named "indolence" — the Karuṇāpuṇḍarīka Sūtra explains that one of the lesser vehicle is, in this sense, indolent.

As for the object of compassion: these are not bodhisattvas at all, taking in, throughout, those who have never yet aroused a mind directed toward the expedient teachings, and are named "not bodhisattvas." Not turning even toward the expedient teachings at all, how much less toward the real teaching itself. This object of compassion embraces everyone within the three realms — these, too, must be granted bliss, but since their wandering in transmigration has no end in sight, they accord precisely with the vow of compassion, and one ought to draw out the causes and effects of their transgressions — hence it says, "giving rise to a mind of great compassion."

From "one should reflect thus" to "teaching the Dharma according to what is fitting" is precisely the reason the vow of loving-kindness arises: because those who delight in the lesser vehicle cling to the Buddha's own expedient means as though it were the real truth itself, failing to comprehend the perfect path, this is called "a great loss" — and this great loss is the reason the vow of loving-kindness arises. From "not hearing, not knowing" onward is the reason the vow of compassion arises: because they have not yet aroused a mind directed toward either the partial or the perfect path, and have not heard either of these two paths, not hearing the partial path is to be without the wisdom born of hearing; not knowing is to be without the wisdom born of reflection; and not perceiving is to be without the wisdom born of cultivation. And they are, besides, without the three wisdoms of the perfect teaching. Why is this?

不問故不聞，不信故不知，不解故不修，偏圓三慧權實皆無，甚可憐愍，起悲之由。從「其人雖不問不信此經」去，正發誓願。彼雖不問不信偏圓二道，菩薩不約偏發誓，但欲與其圓道三慧，故言「雖不問不信此經，我得三菩提引令得入」也。誓願、菩提、智慧、神通，皆約安樂行得。何者？深觀如來座，故得智慧力，四辯莊嚴，能以慧拔也；深觀如來室、如來衣，得大善寂力，不起滅定現諸威儀；神通福德莊嚴先以定動也。

Because they do not ask, they do not hear; because they do not believe, they do not know; because they do not understand, they do not cultivate — the three wisdoms of both the partial and the perfect teachings, provisional and real alike, are entirely absent in them, a state exceedingly to be pitied — and this is the reason the vow of compassion arises. From "though this person neither asks about nor believes in this sūtra" onward, the vow itself is formally set forth. ◎釋從地踊出品師嚴道尊鞠躬祇奉，如來一命四方奔踊，故言「從地踊出品」。三世化導惠利無疆，一月萬影孰能思量？召過以示現，弘經以益當，故言「從地踊出品」。虛空湛然無早無晚，或者執迹而闇其本，召昔示今破近顯遠，故言「從地踊出品」。寂場少父、寂光老兒，示其藥力咸令得知，故言「從地踊出品」。文云「是從何所來，以何因緣集」，今以諸義釋品，顯四悉檀因緣之解，故言「從地踊出品」。此下是大段，第二開師門之近迹，顯佛地之遠本。其文為三：一、從此下，至「汝等自當因是得聞」，序段也；二、從「爾時釋迦告彌勒」下，至〈分別功德品〉彌勒說十九行偈，正說段也；三、從偈後下十一品半，流通段(云云)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the chapter "Bodhisattvas from Beneath the Earth." The teacher stern, the path revered, disciples bow low in reverent service; at the Tathāgata's single summons, they come rushing up from every direction — and this is why it is called "the chapter of emerging from the earth." Across the three times, teaching and guiding, bestowing benefit without limit — a single moon casting a myriad reflections, who could ever fully fathom it? Summoning the past so as to manifest it in the present, proclaiming the sūtra so as to benefit the present age — and this is why it is called "the chapter of emerging from the earth." Empty space itself, clear and still, knows neither early nor late; yet some cling to the trace and remain in the dark as to its source. Summoning what is past to disclose it in the present, breaking open the near to reveal the distant — and this is why it is called "the chapter of emerging from the earth." At the quiescent site of awakening, a father seemingly young; in the land of quiescent light, a child seemingly old — the medicine's own power is disclosed here, causing every one to come to know it — and this is why it is called "the chapter of emerging from the earth." The text itself says, "From where have they come, and by what cause and condition have they gathered here?" — and now, by means of these several senses, the chapter's title is explained, disclosing the understanding proper to the causes and conditions of the four siddhāntas — and this is why it is called "the chapter of emerging from the earth." From this point on is the great division, second within the sūtra as a whole: opening the near trace of the teacher's own gate, so as to disclose the distant source of the buddha-ground. This text forms three parts: first, from this point on down to "you shall, by means of this, come to hear it for yourselves," is the prologue section; second, from "at that time Śākyamuni told Maitreya" onward, down to Maitreya's speaking of the nineteen-line verse in the "Distinguishing the Merits" chapter, is the section of the main teaching itself; and third, from after that verse down through the remaining eleven and a half chapters, is the section of the teaching's transmission to the future, and so on.

The Prologue: Bodhisattvas Rise from the Earth 序文：踊出

Chinese Source

序文為二：一、踊出，二、疑問。踊出為三：一、他方菩薩請弘經，二、如來不許，三、下方踊出。他方菩薩聞通經福大，咸欲發願住此弘宣，故請為之。如來止之凡有三義：汝等各各自有己任，若住此土廢彼利益，一；又他方此土結緣事淺，雖欲宣授必無巨益，二；又若許之則不得召下，下若不來迹不得破、遠不得顯，是為三義，如來止之。召下方來亦有三義：是我弟子應弘我法，以緣深廣能遍此土益，遍分身土益，遍他方土益，又得開近顯遠，是故止彼而召下也。從「佛說是時」下，是第三，下方踊出，為二：一、經家敘相，二、明問訊。兩段各五。初五者：一、踊出，二、身相，三、住處，四、聞命，五、眷屬。住處者，常寂光土也，常即常德，寂即樂德，光即淨我，是為四德祕密之藏，是其住處，以不住法住祕藏中。下方者，法性之淵底，玄宗之極地，故言下方。

Ru-Yi Translation

The prologue has two parts: first, the emerging from the earth; second, the raising of a doubt and question. The emerging from the earth has three parts: first, the bodhisattvas from other lands requesting to proclaim the sūtra; second, the Tathāgata's declining their offer; and third, the bodhisattvas beneath the earth emerging. The bodhisattvas from other lands, hearing that the merit of proclaiming this sūtra is great, all wished to make the vow to remain in this land and broadly teach it, and so they made their request to do so. The Tathāgata declined their offer for three reasons in all: each of them already has his own proper task, and to remain in this land would be to forsake the benefit owed to their own — this is the first; their karmic connection with this land, formed only shallowly, means that even were they to proclaim the teaching here, it could bring no great benefit — this is the second; and, again, were their offer accepted, there would then be no occasion to summon those beneath the earth, and, should those beneath the earth fail to come forth, the trace could not be broken open, nor the distant source ever disclosed — this is the third reason, and it is for these three reasons that the Tathāgata declined. The summoning of those from beneath the earth likewise has three reasons: these are his own disciples, and it is fitting that they proclaim his own Dharma; their karmic connection is deep and broad, able to reach the benefit of this land in full, the benefit of the lands of the emanation-buddhas, and the benefit of other lands besides; and, again, only through them can the near be opened to reveal the distant — and this is why the offer of those others was declined, and those from beneath the earth summoned instead. From "as the Buddha spoke these words" onward is, third, the bodhisattvas beneath the earth emerging, and this has two parts: first, the sūtra-compilers' own narration of the scene; second, making clear the greeting exchanged. Each of these two sections, in turn, has five parts. The first five are: the emerging itself; their bodily marks; their dwelling place; their hearing of the summons; and their retinues. As for their dwelling place: this is the land of eternal, quiescent light — "eternal" being the virtue of permanence, "quiescent" the virtue of bliss, and "light" the virtue of purity and true self — this is the secret treasury of the four virtues, and it is this that is their dwelling place, dwelling within the secret treasury by means of a dharma that dwells nowhere at all. As for "beneath": this is the very depth of the abyss of the dharma-nature, the ultimate ground of the mysterious teaching, and this is why it is called "beneath."

在下不屬此，空中不屬彼，非此非彼即中道也。出此不在上不在此下，不上不下住在空中，亦是中道也。來之由者，聞命故來，弘法故來，破執故來，顯本故來，皆如上說。所將眷屬者，若人情往望，謂領六萬五萬恒沙者為多，領三二一者為少，單己者隻獨；若依文往尋，六萬五萬者為少，單己者為多。文云單己獨處者，其數轉過上，若依法門者，一一皆是導師德，能引眾人至於寶所，當知一己非獨、六萬非多，一即一道清淨，二即定慧，三即戒定慧，四即四諦，五即五眼，六即六度，一一度具十法界，一一界各有十，十即有百，百即具千，十善即有萬，一度具萬，六度即六萬法門，多不為多、一不為少，非多非少而多而少(云云)。

Being beneath does not belong to this world above, and being in the sky does not belong to that world beneath — being neither this nor that is precisely the middle way. Emerging from beneath, they are neither above nor still beneath — being neither above nor below, dwelling in the sky, this too is precisely the middle way. As for the reason for their coming: they come because they have heard the summons; they come to proclaim the Dharma; they come to break open attachment; and they come to disclose the source — all exactly as explained above. As for the retinues they bring with them: judged by ordinary human feeling, one might say that those leading a retinue of sixty or fifty thousand Ganges-sands are many, that those leading three, two, or one are few, and that one entirely alone is solitary indeed; but, sought out according to the text itself, those with sixty or fifty thousand are, in fact, few, while the one entirely alone is, in fact, many. The text itself says that those entirely alone, dwelling in solitude, exceed in number even those counted above — and if one seeks this out through the Dharma-gate, every single one of them, without exception, possesses the virtue of a guide, able to lead the whole multitude of beings to the place of treasure, and one should know that the solitary one is not truly alone, nor the sixty thousand truly many. One is the purity of the single path; two is concentration and wisdom; three is moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom; four is the four noble truths; five is the five eyes; six is the six perfections — and each single perfection is itself furnished with all ten dharma-realms, and each single realm, in turn, has its own ten, so that ten becomes a hundred, and a hundred becomes a thousand, and the ten forms of goodness become ten thousand, so that a single perfection is furnished with ten thousand, and the six perfections together become sixty thousand Dharma-gates — many is not truly many, and one is not truly few; it is neither many nor few, and yet it is both many and few at once, and so on.

The Bodhisattvas Pay Homage: Fifty Small Kalpas as Half a Day 三業供養與五十小劫

Chinese Source

「是諸菩薩從地出」下，第二，問訊，為五：一、三業供養，二、陳問訊之辭，三、佛答安樂，四、偈頌隨喜，五、如來述歎。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "these bodhisattvas, having come forth from the earth" onward is, second, the greeting, which forms five parts: first, the offering made through the three karmic actions; second, the words of greeting spoken; third, the Buddha's answer concerning his peace and ease; fourth, the verse expressing rejoicing; and fifth, the Tathāgata's own words of praise.

就初三業供養，經五十小劫謂如半日，四眾遍見，此乃隱長而現短，借其神力令狹而見廣，俱是不可思議也。「拜遶」是身、「讚法」是口、「瞻仰」是意；「五十小劫」與「半日」者，此是時節不可思議。如來所見不以二相，下方菩薩常面稱揚，如來默然常受其讚，解者即短而長，謂五十小劫，惑者即長而短，謂如半日，斯為本迹而作弄引。如來未說，闇本而執迹；佛若開顯，悟近而達遠，亦知不思議一也。四眾遍見菩薩者，亦是不思議也。夫肉眼、天眼所見不遠，而今所覩充滿虛空，見雨猛知龍大，見花盛知池深，見應滿虛空，則知真彌法界也。初、標四導師。

As to the first, the offering made through the three karmic actions: fifty small kalpas passed, and yet it seemed no more than half a day, the fourfold assembly seeing this throughout — this is precisely concealing what is long while manifesting it as short, borrowing his own spiritual power to cause what is narrow to be seen as vast, and both alike are inconceivable. "Bowing and circling" is the karmic action of the body; "praising the Dharma" is that of speech; and "gazing up in reverence" is that of the mind. As for "fifty small kalpas" and "half a day": this is the inconceivability of time itself. What the Tathāgata himself perceives carries no duality of appearance, and the bodhisattvas beneath the earth constantly praise him face to face, while the Tathāgata sits in silence, constantly receiving their praise — one who understands sees the short as long, calling it fifty small kalpas, while one who is deluded sees the long as short, calling it no more than half a day — and this is precisely a deliberate device, luring the listener onward, in terms of the source and the trace. So long as the Tathāgata has not yet spoken, one remains in the dark as to the source and clings only to the trace; but once the Buddha opens and discloses it, one awakens from the near and reaches through to the distant — and this, too, is a way of knowing that the inconceivable is, in truth, one. That the fourfold assembly sees these bodhisattvas everywhere throughout is likewise inconceivable: the fleshly eye and the heavenly eye alike see, ordinarily, no great distance, and yet what is now beheld fills the whole of empty space — seeing rain fall in torrents, one knows the dragon to be vast; seeing flowers bloom in abundance, one knows the pool to be deep; and seeing this response fill all of space, one thereby knows that the true nature pervades the entire dharma-realm. First, the setting forth of the four leading teachers.

The Four Leaders' Greeting, and the Buddha's Answer 四導師問訊與如來具答

Chinese Source

次、陳問辭。問又二：長行、偈頌。長行有二：一、問如來安樂，二、問眾生易度(云云)。但舉四人者，欲擬開示悟入四十位耳，如《華嚴》但舉法慧、德林、金幢、金藏說四十位(云云)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the words of greeting spoken. The question, too, has two parts: a prose section and a verse section. The prose has two parts: first, asking after the Tathāgata's own peace and ease; second, asking whether sentient beings are easily brought across, and so on. That only four persons are named here is meant to correspond to the fourfold pattern of opening, showing, awakening to, and entering the Buddha's wisdom across forty stages — just as the Huayan [the Avataṃsaka Sūtra] names only Dharmamati, Guṇavana, Vajradhvaja, and Vajragarbha to expound its own forty stages, and so on.

三，如來具答。安樂、易度兩事相成，易度則安樂，安樂則易度。

Third, the Tathāgata answers in full. The two matters of peace-and-ease and being easily brought across bring each other to completion: where beings are easily brought across, there is peace and ease; and where there is peace and ease, beings are easily brought across.

易度為兩：一、根利德厚，世世已來常受大化，始見我身即稟《華嚴》入如來慧，果熟易零，是眾生易度；二、根鈍德薄，世世已來不受大化，為是人故須開鈍說漸，三藏、方等、《般若》而調伏之，亦令此人今聞《法華》入於佛慧。比前雖難，於佛甚易，佛識其宜方便得所，薄須塗熨慧悟是同。今略舉十意釋之：第一、始見今見，第二、開合不開合，第三、豎廣橫略，第四、本一迹多迹共本獨，第五、加說不加說，第六、變土不變土，第七、多處不多處，第八、斥奪不斥奪，第九、直顯實開權顯實，第十、利根初熟鈍根後熟。

Being easily brought across has two aspects: first, those of sharp faculties and abundant virtue, who, life after life until now, have constantly received the Buddha's own great teaching, and who, the very moment they first see my body, receive the Huayan teaching and enter the Tathāgata's wisdom — their fruit already ripe and easily falling, these are the sentient beings easily brought across; second, those of dull faculties and meager virtue, who, life after life until now, have not received the great teaching — and it is for the sake of such people that the dull must be opened and the gradual taught, subduing them by means of the Tripiṭaka teaching, the Vaipulya teaching, and the Perfection of Wisdom teaching, so that these people, too, upon now hearing the Lotus, may enter the Buddha's own wisdom. Compared to the first, this is more difficult — yet for the Buddha it remains altogether easy, since the Buddha, discerning what is fitting for them, applies the skillful means that suits their need, whether it be light or requiring further application, and the wisdom and awakening finally attained is the very same in both cases. Here, ten senses are briefly raised to explain this: first, the initial seeing and the present seeing; second, the opened-out and unopened, the combined and uncombined; third, the vertical breadth and the horizontal brevity; fourth, one source with many traces, the traces held in common while the source is unique to each; fifth, the added teaching and the teaching not added; sixth, the changed land and the unchanged land; seventh, many places and not many places; eighth, the rejecting-and-reclaiming and the not rejecting-and-reclaiming; ninth, directly disclosing the real, and opening the provisional to disclose the real; and tenth, the sharp faculty ripening at first, and the dull faculty ripening only later.

第一始見今見者，初成道時名始見，法華座席久後真實名今見也。日照高山即說於頓，不開不合；為不入者開頓說漸，五味調伏令漸歸頓。頓直豎入入於法界故言豎廣，不歷方便故言橫略，今歷五味即是橫廣，得入佛慧亦是豎廣。一臺故本一，千葉故迹多，迹與眾經同故言共，本與眾經異故言獨。加四菩薩說四十位，自說開示悟入不加於他。花王世界故言不變，二變土田故言變土。七處八會是為多處，耆闍崛山遠處虛空故不多處。滅化城改客作故言斥奪，無如此事故不斥奪。行大直道名直顯實，決了聲聞法，名開權顯實。根利緣熟始入佛慧，根鈍後熟今入佛慧。緣宜不同略為十異，種智法界等無差別，故文云「始見我身聞我所說，即皆信受入如來慧，除先修習學小乘者。」今於此經入於佛慧，明文在茲不須疑也。諸師見其緣異，逐緣異解迷不知反去道轉遠，若識理同千車共轍，佛慧則無殊也。舊云，《華嚴》了義滿字常住，《法華》不了義非滿非常，今以此文並之，若始入是了義，今入不了義者，始入是佛慧，今入非佛慧，若佛慧既齊了義亦等，滿字常住悉然(云云)。地人呼《華嚴》為圓宗，《法華》為不真宗，今亦用此文並之。

First, "the initial seeing and the present seeing": at the time of his first attainment of the path, this is named the initial seeing; at the seat of the Lotus, long afterward, disclosing what is truly real, this is named the present seeing. When the sun first shone on the high mountains, this was precisely the teaching of the sudden — neither opened out nor combined; but for the sake of those unable to enter it, the sudden was opened and the gradual taught, tempering beings through the five flavors so as to gradually bring them back to the sudden. The sudden enters directly and vertically into the dharma-realm, and this is why it is called "vertical breadth"; it does not pass through any expedient means, and this is why it is called "horizontal brevity"; and now, passing through the five flavors, this is precisely "horizontal breadth," while attaining entry into the Buddha's wisdom is likewise "vertical breadth." There being one dais, the source is one; there being a thousand petals, the traces are many. The traces, being shared with the many other sūtras, are called "held in common"; the source, differing from the many other sūtras, is called "unique." Adding the four bodhisattvas to expound the forty stages is speaking of opening, showing, awakening to, and entering by oneself, without adding what belongs to another. Being the World of the Flower King, it is called "unchanged"; the second, changing the land as its field, is called "changed." The seven locations and eight assemblies are "many places"; Gṛdhrakūṭa, its far reach spreading into empty space, is "not many places." Doing away with the phantom city and changing the traveler's own status is called "rejecting and reclaiming"; there being no such matter here, this is "not rejecting and reclaiming." Practicing the great, direct path is named "directly disclosing the real"; resolving and clarifying the śrāvaka's own dharma is named "opening the provisional to disclose the real." The sharp faculty, its karmic connection ripe, enters the Buddha's wisdom at the outset; the dull faculty, ripening only later, enters the Buddha's wisdom only now. Though the fittingness of their karmic connections differs, these are, in brief, ten distinctions — yet the wisdom of all modes and the dharma-realm itself carry no difference at all, which is why the text itself says, "the very moment they first see my body and hear what I teach, they at once believe and accept it, entering the Tathāgata's wisdom — excepting only those who have already trained themselves in and grown attached to the lesser vehicle." That they now enter the Buddha's wisdom by means of this very sūtra is made plain in this text itself, and there is no need for doubt about it. The various masters, seeing that their karmic connections differ, follow this difference into differing interpretations, and, lost in confusion, fail to realize that in turning away they only stray further from the path — yet, if one recognizes that the principle is the same, like a thousand carts following a single track, then the Buddha's wisdom is, in the end, no different at all. The old explanation holds that the Huayan is the teaching of complete meaning, the full character, ever abiding, while the Lotus is the teaching of incomplete meaning, neither full nor ever abiding — but, judging by this very text, if the initial entry is the teaching of complete meaning while the present entry is of incomplete meaning, then the initial entry would be the Buddha's wisdom while the present entry would not be the Buddha's wisdom at all; yet if the Buddha's wisdom is one and the same in both cases, then the teaching of complete meaning, too, is equal in both, and the full character, ever abiding, is present throughout in just the same way, and so on. The masters of the Dilun school call the Huayan the perfect teaching's own school, and the Lotus a school not yet truly real — and here, too, this same text may be used to weigh their claim against it.

Maitreya's Rejoicing, and the Buddha's Praise 領解隨喜與如來述歎

Chinese Source

第四，菩薩領解隨喜。能問者，即是《華嚴》中四大士，《法華》中身子三請，俱是能問也。所問者，即是問佛智慧也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, the bodhisattvas' own understanding and rejoicing. As for those able to ask: these are precisely the four great masters within the Huayan, and, within the Lotus, Śāriputra's threefold request — both alike are those able to ask. As for what is asked: this is precisely the asking after the Buddha's own wisdom.

第五，如來述歎者，與問碩異，問家隨喜。能問人皆是菩薩，及所化人聞已信行，我等隨喜。如來述歎能化人生隨喜者，此義云何？

Fifth, as for the Tathāgata's own words of praise: this differs markedly from the question itself — the questioner's own rejoicing. Those able to ask are all themselves bodhisattvas, and, together with those they have taught and transformed, upon hearing this, believe and practice it, saying, "we too rejoice." That the Tathāgata should praise the one who transforms others, giving rise to rejoicing in this way — what, then, is the meaning of this?

然能問者皆是古佛，汝能隨喜即是如來，菩薩隨喜其迹，如來述歎其本，此亦密表壽量(云云)。「爾時彌勒及八萬大士」下，第二，疑問序。自寂場已降，今座已往，十方大士來會不絕，雖不可限，我以補處智力悉見悉知，而於此眾不識一人，然我遊化十方覲奉諸佛，諸佛大眾快所諳知，就履歷之處亦所不識，若來若去如是推之皆所不識。又彼諸大士是前進先達，彌勒是後番末學，後不知前故所不識。又彼等大士，本實相底應現十方，別頭教化所有真應，非彌勒境界，是故不識。又佛託弘經召諸大士，大士聞師命故來，密聞壽量，非時眾所知，故言不識。此約四悉檀，釋疑問序也(云云)。

Yet those able to ask are themselves, in truth, ancient buddhas — that you should be able to rejoice is itself precisely being a Tathāgata. The bodhisattvas rejoice in his trace, while the Tathāgata praises his own source — and this, too, secretly points ahead to the teaching on his own lifespan, and so on. From "at that time Maitreya, together with eighty thousand great masters" onward is, second, the prologue raising a doubt and question. From the site of quiescent awakening down to the present seat, great masters from the ten directions have come to this assembly without cease, and though their number cannot be measured, I, by the power of the wisdom proper to one who is next in line for buddhahood, see and know every one of them — and yet, among this assembly, I do not recognize even a single one. And though I myself have traveled and taught throughout the ten directions, paying homage to every buddha, and the great assemblies of every buddha are ones I know well and thoroughly, still, as to where these particular ones have themselves traveled, I do not recognize this either — whether coming or going, reasoned out in just this way, I recognize none of them at all. Again, these great masters are ones who have gone before, already far advanced, while Maitreya belongs to a later cohort, a student still at the rear — and it is because the later cannot know the earlier that he fails to recognize them. Again, these great masters, at the very depth of the true mark of reality, manifest in response throughout the ten directions, each teaching and transforming beings through his own distinct true response, which lies beyond Maitreya's own domain altogether — and this, too, is why he fails to recognize them. Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the chapter "Lifespan." First, setting out the divergent interpretations. Master Sengrui's preface says: "That the lifespan is immeasurable kalpas is still not enough to make clear its full duration; that the emanation-bodies are without number is still not enough to distinguish that they are of a different substance. This being so, the lifespan settles the matter of its not being any fixed number, and the emanation-bodies make clear that they are not different; Samantabhadra reveals that there is no separate accomplishment apart from this, and Prabhūtaratna makes clear that there is no extinguishing of it." Daolang of Hexi says: "This makes clear that the Dharma-body's true and transformed aspects are not different, and that its abiding and its ceasing are, in principle, one; Prabhūtaratna's appearing makes clear that the Dharma-body constantly abides, and the 'Lifespan' chapter makes clear that it is of the same measure as the great void." The Daochang Contemplation says: "Bringing the three together and returning them to the one is the beginning of the vehicle; extinguishing the trace and clarifying the spirit is the end of the vehicle. 'Extinguishing the trace' means the trace comes to rest; 'clarifying the spirit' makes the origin plain — hence the trace is impermanent while the origin is permanent." A certain annotator says: "What is not subject to the count of existing or perishing is called 'lifespan'; what is beyond the bounds of cultivation, of dying young or living long, is called 'measure.' The Dharma-body is not encompassed by any span of years or forms; it causes the great being, cultivating and treading to the utmost, to be illumined by it — it does not take Gayā as the place where buddhahood was accomplished, nor a hundred years as the fixed span of a lifetime." Zhu Daosheng says: "As for this form-body buddha, he responds and manifests, yet has no real form at all — and if the form itself is not real, how could there be any 'lifespan' to speak of? This being so, the ten thousand forms all arrive at the same point, and past and present become one: the past, too, is the present, and the present, too, is the past; there is no time in which he is not present, no place in which he does not dwell. If there were a time in which he were not present, a place in which he did not dwell, that would only be so from the standpoint of sentient beings — it is not so for the Buddha himself. This is why, when the lifespan is spoken of in its fullest sense, it is said that Gayā is indeed the place — yet, rightly understood, this 'Gayā' is no longer really 'Gayā' at all, and since 'Gayā' itself is negated, how could that 'long span' alone remain fixed and 'so'? Once 'long' and 'short' are both cast away, 'long' and 'short' abide eternally." As the masters of former generations have said, in the way just described, most of them explain permanence in terms of immeasurability; more recent teachers, for the most part, say that the lifespan is a measurable quantity — that it exceeds the sands of the Ganges at first and doubles that number afterward — yet in the end it returns to some outer limit, and so explain it as impermanence. Furthermore, some who are confused hold that since this chapter clarifies "lifespan and measure," and "measure" is impermanent, how could one construe it as permanence? I now answer this: the chapter speaks directly of "lifespan and measure" — it does not say the lifespan has a measure, nor does it say the lifespan is without measure; if I construe it as impermanence and another construes it as permanence, what fault could there be in either? Like a snipe and a clam locked in mutual struggle, I simply take advantage of their exhaustion. One ought to furnish all four readings together: that it is truly finite yet spoken of as infinite, as in the Amitāyus Sūtra; that it is truly infinite yet spoken of as finite, as in this chapter and in the Suvarṇaprabhāsa Sūtra; that it is truly infinite and spoken of as infinite, as when the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "Only a buddha together with another buddha knows that his lifespan is immeasurable"; and that it is truly finite and spoken of as finite, as when the Buddha, at eighty years, proclaimed his extinction. Question: Reasoning from the doctrine, permanence may be granted — but what textual basis is there for it?

Answer: One who has understanding values the principle; one who remains in the dark clings only to the letter. To seek out the meaning conveyed and thereby meet the essential doctrine — this is the true intent of the teaching; if one instead clings stubbornly to the husk and chaff of the words alone, what use is there in still asking after the bridge once one has already crossed? Moreover, the teaching is fundamentally given for the sake of particular conditions — as the conditions differ, so does what is said; sometimes it follows what delights, sometimes what is fitting, sometimes what remedies, sometimes what awakens, and once awakening is reached, one has arrived — why linger any longer wandering the crossing paths? This is the meaning of the saying that the true Dharma-jewel of nirvāṇa is entered through beings' many different gates. There is also the matter of how much or how little text is given to a point: the Nirvāṇa Sūtra takes the constant abiding of the Buddha in the future as its main doctrine, and so its text on this point is extensive, while it does not take the long-past accomplishment of buddhahood as its main doctrine, and so its text on this point is scant — yet if one simply followed whichever gets more text and discarded whichever gets less, that would be the talk of Māra, not the Buddha's own teaching. This sūtra takes the long-past accomplishment as its main doctrine, and so its text marking out the dust-mote-numbered worlds is extensive, while its text on constant abiding in the future is scant — and if here, too, one followed whichever gets more and discarded whichever gets less, one's head would be split into seven pieces, like the branch of an āmra tree snapped in violation of a vow. It is like the emperor's own decree: whether long or short, neither may be disobeyed — to disobey it is to incur guilt. The "Skillful Means" chapter says: "The mark of the world constantly abides; having realized this at the place of awakening, the guiding teacher then speaks of it by skillful means" — this passage is precisely about constant abiding, unceasing, in the future. It also says: "I dwell always here." It also says: "I dwell always on Vulture Peak, and in every other dwelling place besides." The Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra says: "The place gathered in and accomplished by the pāramitā of permanence, the place established by the pāramitā of self" — passages such as these, teaching permanence, are likewise not few in number. Moreover, this sūtra everywhere makes the Dharma-body clear — and how could the Dharma-body possibly fail to be permanent?

The Twofold and Threefold Tathāgata 二如來三如來

Chinese Source

釋品，「如來」者，十方三世諸佛二佛三佛本佛迹佛之通號也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the chapter title. "Tathāgata" is the common title shared by the buddhas of the ten directions and the three times — by the twofold Tathāgata, the threefold Tathāgata, and the Tathāgata considered as origin or as trace alike.

「壽量」者，詮量也，詮量十方三世二佛三佛本佛迹佛之功德也。

"Lifespan" means to give full measure and account of something: it gives full measure of the merit and virtue of the buddhas of the ten directions and the three times, of the twofold Tathāgata and the threefold Tathāgata, of the Tathāgata as origin and as trace.

今正詮量本地三佛功德，故言「如來壽量品」。如來義甚多，且明二三如來，餘例可解。二如來者，《成論》云「乘如實道來成正覺故名如來」。乘是法如如智，實是法如如境，道是因、覺是果。若單論乘者，如如無所知；單明實者，如如無能知。境智和合則有因果，照境未窮名因，盡源為果，道覺義成，即是乘如實道來成正覺，此真身如來也。以如實智，乘如實道，來生三有示成正覺者，即應身如來也。三如來者，《大論》云「如法相解、如法相說，故名如來」。如者法如如境，非因非果，有佛無佛性相常然，遍一切處而無有異為如，不動而至為來，指此為法身如來也。法如如智，乘於如如真實之道來成妙覺，智稱如理，從理名如、從智名來，即報身如來。故《論》云「如法相解故名如來」也。以如如境智合故，即能處處示成正覺。水銀和真金，能塗諸色像，功德和法身，處處應現往，八相成道轉妙法輪，即應身如來。故《論》云「如法相說故名如來」也。

Here, the primary point is to give full measure of the merit and virtue of the three buddha-bodies at the original ground — and this is why it is called the chapter "The Tathāgata's Lifespan." The senses of "Tathāgata" are extremely numerous; here I shall make clear only the twofold and threefold Tathāgata, and the rest may be understood by analogy. As for the twofold Tathāgata: the Satyasiddhi Śāstra says, "Riding the path of thusness and reality, one comes to accomplish true awakening — hence one is called Tathāgata." "Riding" refers to the wisdom of the thusness of dharmas; "reality" refers to the objective realm of the thusness of dharmas; "the path" is the cause, and "awakening" is the fruit. If one speaks of "riding" alone, thusness has nothing that knows it; if one makes "reality" clear alone, thusness has nothing capable of knowing. Only when the objective realm and the wisdom that knows it are joined together is there any cause and fruit at all: illuminating the realm without yet exhausting it is called the cause, and reaching the very source of it is the fruit — and once the sense of "the path" and "awakening" is thus fulfilled, this is precisely "riding the path of thusness and reality to come and accomplish true awakening," which is the true-body Tathāgata. And by means of the wisdom of thusness and reality, riding the path of thusness and reality, to come and be born within the three realms of existence, manifesting the accomplishing of true awakening — this is the response-body Tathāgata. As for the threefold Tathāgata: the Great Treatise says, "Understanding in accordance with the mark of dharmas, and speaking in accordance with the mark of dharmas — hence one is called Tathāgata." "Thusness" here refers to the objective realm of the thusness of dharmas, which is neither cause nor fruit; whether or not a buddha appears, its nature and mark remain forever constant, pervading every place without the slightest variation — this is "thusness"; and to arrive without ever having moved is "coming" — this points to the Dharma-body Tathāgata. The wisdom of the thusness of dharmas, riding the true path of thusness to come and accomplish sublime awakening, its wisdom matching perfectly with principle — named "thusness" from the side of principle, and named "coming" from the side of wisdom — this is precisely the reward-body Tathāgata. Hence the Treatise says, "Understanding in accordance with the mark of dharmas — hence one is called Tathāgata." And because the objective realm of thusness and the wisdom of thusness are joined together, the Tathāgata is thereby able, everywhere, to manifest the accomplishing of true awakening. Just as mercury, mixed with true gold, can be applied to coat every kind of image, so merit and virtue, joined with the Dharma-body, respond and manifest everywhere it goes, accomplishing the path through the eight aspects of a buddha's life and turning the sublime Dharma-wheel — this is the response-body Tathāgata. Hence the Treatise says, "Speaking in accordance with the mark of dharmas — hence one is called Tathāgata."

法身如來名毘盧遮那，此翻遍一切處；報身如來名盧舍那，此翻淨滿；應身如來名釋迦文，此翻度沃焦。是三如來若單取者則不可也，《大經》云「法身亦非、般若亦非、解脫亦非，三法具足稱祕密藏、名大涅槃」，不可一異縱橫並別，圓覽三法稱假名如來也。《梵網經》結成華嚴教，華臺為本、華葉為末，別為一緣作如此說，而本末不得相離。《像法決疑經》結成涅槃，文云「或見釋迦為毘盧遮那，或為盧舍那」，蓋前緣異見，非佛三也。《普賢觀》結成法華，文云「釋迦牟尼名毘盧遮那」，乃是異名非別體也。總眾經之意，當知三佛非一異明矣。

The Dharma-body Tathāgata is named Vairocana, which translates as "pervading every place"; the reward-body Tathāgata is named Locana, which translates as "pure and full"; and the response-body Tathāgata is named Śākyamuni, which translates as "one who ferries across the parched and scorched." Yet to take any one of these three Tathāgatas by itself alone will not do. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "It is not the Dharma-body, nor is it prajñā, nor is it liberation — only when these three dharmas are complete together is it called the secret treasury, named the great nirvāṇa." One cannot treat them as simply one or simply different, nor arrange them vertically or horizontally, nor set them apart as separate — only when all three dharmas are viewed together, as one perfect whole, can they be given the provisional name "Tathāgata." The Brahma Net Sūtra, in bringing the Avataṃsaka teaching to its conclusion, takes the lotus dais as the origin and the lotus petals as the branches — this is a particular teaching given for a particular occasion, and yet origin and branches cannot, even so, be separated from one another. The Sūtra Resolving Doubts About the Semblance Dharma, in bringing the Nirvāṇa teaching to its conclusion, says: "Some see Śākyamuni as Vairocana, others as Locana" — this is simply a matter of different beings, with different karmic conditions, seeing him differently; it does not mean there are three separate buddhas. The Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra, in bringing the Lotus teaching to its conclusion, says: "Śākyamuni is named Vairocana" — this, too, is a different name, not a different substance. Gathering together the intent of all these sūtras, one should understand clearly that the three buddha-bodies are neither simply one nor simply different.

Question: This chapter names no three buddhas — how, then, can this reading be justified?

Answer: Though it does not set out the names, it nonetheless contains their meaning in full. The text says, "not thus, not otherwise" and "not as the three realms themselves see the three realms" — this is not the partial thusness but the perfect thusness made manifest, which is precisely the sense of the Dharma-body Tathāgata. It also says, "the Tathāgata sees and knows, exactly as it truly is, the mark of the three realms" — this is precisely the wisdom of thusness matching the objective realm of thusness; the knowledge and vision of the wisdom of all modes is exactly the buddha eye — this is precisely the sense of the reward-body Tathāgata. It also says, "sometimes revealing his own self and his own affairs, sometimes another's self and another's affairs" — this is precisely the sense of the response-body Tathāgata. Now, if the threefold Tathāgata is taken purely in terms of inherent virtue, this is a "horizontal" reading; if taken purely in terms of cultivated virtue — Dharma-body first, reward-body next, response-body last — this, too, is a "vertical" reading. What the present sūtra expounds, in its perfect teaching, is a threefold Tathāgata that is neither vertical nor horizontal — and if even distinguishing the vertical and horizontal Tathāgata already falls short of the sūtra's own present intent, how much more so the Tathāgata of the Tripiṭaka or shared teaching? Furthermore, even where the perfect Tathāgata is made clear prior to the Lotus, this, too, is spoken only from within the trace. The threefold Tathāgata disclosed by "opening the trace to reveal the origin" is permanently distinct from anything found in any other sūtra. The Treatise says: "In order to manifest the accomplishing of great, unsurpassed bodhi, three kinds of bodhi are shown: first, the bodhi of the response-and-transformation body, manifesting wherever it is fitting to manifest — as when the sūtra speaks of 'going forth from the palace of the Śākya clan'; second, the bodhi of the reward buddha, having fulfilled the ten grounds and attained constant nirvāṇa — as when the sūtra says, 'since I actually attained buddhahood, immeasurable, boundless kalpas have passed'; and third, the bodhi of the Dharma buddha, meaning the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, pure and unchanging in nirvāṇa — as when the sūtra says, 'the Tathāgata sees and knows, exactly as it truly is, the mark of the three realms.'" The sūtra contains this meaning in full, and the Treatise supplies its very names — if one did not offer the explanation given above, how could sūtra and treatise ever be brought into accord?

Next, clarifying "lifespan and measure": "lifespan" carries the sense of "receiving." Because true suchness does not stand apart from any dharma, it is called "receiving"; because the objective realm and the wisdom that knows it correspond to one another, it is called "receiving"; and because a single lifetime's karmic reward, received as a hundred years, continues without break, it too is called "receiving." As for the Dharma-body Tathāgata's life of principle-as-suchness: whether or not a buddha appears, its nature and mark remain forever constant, and one does not speak of it in terms of corresponding or not continuing — it is likewise neither finite nor infinite. The text saying "not thus, not otherwise, not unreal, not real" is precisely giving full account of the Dharma-body's life of principle-as-suchness. In giving full account of the reward-body Tathāgata: the wisdom of thusness matches the objective realm of thusness — the realm giving rise to wisdom is "reward," and wisdom merging with the realm is "receiving." Since the realm is already infinite and boundless, constantly abiding and never extinguished, its wisdom, too, is exactly the same — the capacity and what it covers are equally vast. The text saying, "Such is the power of my wisdom: its light of insight shines without limit, my lifespan lasting countless kalpas, attained through long cultivation of karma," is precisely giving full account of the reward-body Tathāgata's life of wisdom. As for giving full account of the response body: the response body shares in its karmic conditions — where the condition is long, it, too, is long; where the condition is brief, it, too, is brief (and so on) — for what could it possibly be, apart from those very conditions? The text saying, "again and again manifesting birth, again and again manifesting extinction," and also declaring, in each case, a different name, a different length of years, is precisely giving full account of the response buddha's life as shared with its conditions.

復次，法身非量非無量，報身金剛前有量、金剛後無量，應身隨緣則有量、應用不斷則無量。通途詮量三句在聖、一句屬凡，有量無常都非佛義。舊來所說乃是增減兩謗加誣於佛，非魔是何？

Furthermore: the Dharma-body is neither finite nor infinite; the reward-body is finite prior to the vajra-mind and infinite after it; and the response-body is finite insofar as it follows its conditions, yet infinite insofar as its responsive activity never ceases. In the general account, three of these four propositions belong to the sages, and one alone belongs to ordinary beings — "finite and impermanent" is simply not what the Buddha means at all. What earlier teachers have said on this point amounts to the two slanders of exaggeration and diminishment, falsely imputed to the Buddha — if this is not the work of Māra, what is it?

四句詮量其義已顯，為未解者，更常等四句料簡：先別作、次通作。別者，非常非無常，雙非理極即法身也；常者即報身也，報智境合亦非常非無常，但取正智圓滿不生不滅，過金剛心之前故，取常為報身耳；亦常亦無常應身也，應用無盡為亦常，數唱涅槃名亦無常，無常者，金剛心已前智用增進，乃至凡夫生滅出沒皆是無常。三佛各一句，凡夫共一句，此約別教別分別也。通途圓說者，一一如來悉備四句。法身四者：非常非無常，雙破凡聖八倒故；常者如虛空常故；無常者無凡夫生滅倒故；亦常亦無常者，寂而雙照故。報身四者：非常非無常者，智冥境故，常者出過二乘故：無常者無生滅倒故：亦常亦無常能雙照故。應身四者：非常非無常者，非報非生死故；常者常應同故；無常者同無常故；亦常亦無常者，兩存故。凡夫既得無常一句，通途亦作四句，但有性德之理，尚無四句名字，況行用耶？可以意得不俟說也。一身即是三身，不一不異，當知一佛身即具諸身壽命功德，隨緣感見長短不同。《大經》云「凡夫二乘見佛壽命猶如冬日，菩薩所見猶如春日，唯佛見佛壽命無量猶如夏日。所以然者，凡夫博地翳障朦朧，藏通二乘雖斷四住不見中道，若捨分段受法性身未破無明，彼土所奉猶是勝應，當知二乘秖見冬日。若諸菩薩未登地住，所見同前；若破無明乃至受分法身，與而為語得見報身壽命，奪而為語猶是勝應，未窮報身之源，未盡法性之極，所見佛壽猶是春日。唯佛與佛窮性盡源，見法身壽猶如夏日。」

The meaning of these four propositions has now been made plain; but for those who have not yet fully understood, let me sort them out once more through the standard fourfold pattern of permanent and impermanent, first taken separately, then taken together as one. Taken separately: "neither permanent nor impermanent" is the doubled negation carried to its utmost principle, which is the Dharma-body; "permanent" is the reward-body — though the reward-body's wisdom and its objective realm, joined together, are likewise neither permanent nor impermanent, here I take only the sense that its true wisdom, perfectly full, neither arises nor ceases once past the vajra-mind, and on that basis take "permanent" for the reward-body; "both permanent and impermanent" is the response-body — its responsive activity, never exhausted, is "permanent," while its repeated proclaiming of nirvāṇa is named "impermanent"; as for "impermanent," the increasing and advancing of its wisdom-activity prior to the vajra-mind, down to the arising and passing of ordinary beings, are all impermanence. Each of the three buddha-bodies thus takes one proposition, and ordinary beings share the remaining one — this is the distinction drawn according to the distinct teaching. In the perfect teaching's general account, however, each and every Tathāgata possesses all four propositions in full. The Dharma-body's four are: "neither permanent nor impermanent," because it doubly breaks through the eight inversions of both ordinary beings and sages; "permanent," because it is permanent like empty space; "impermanent," because it is free of the ordinary being's inverted view of arising and ceasing; and "both permanent and impermanent," because it is quiescent and yet illuminates both at once. The reward-body's four are: "neither permanent nor impermanent," because its wisdom merges with its objective realm; "permanent," because it surpasses the two lesser vehicles; "impermanent," because it is free of the inverted view of arising and ceasing; and "both permanent and impermanent," because it can illuminate both at once. The response-body's four are: "neither permanent nor impermanent," because it is neither the reward-body nor birth-and-death; "permanent," because its constant responding is uniform; "impermanent," because it shares in impermanence; and "both permanent and impermanent," because both hold true for it at once. Ordinary beings, meanwhile, obtain only the single proposition "impermanent" — yet in the general account, they too can be given all four propositions, though this concerns only the principle of their inherent virtue, which does not yet even have the four propositions as names, let alone their actual functioning in practice — this may be grasped in the mind, without need of further explanation. One body is precisely the three bodies, neither one nor different; one should understand that a single buddha-body already possesses the merit and virtue of every body and every lifespan at once, and that beings, according to their own conditions, perceive and encounter it as longer or shorter. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Ordinary beings and the two lesser vehicles see the Buddha's lifespan as if it were a winter's day; bodhisattvas see it as if it were a spring day; only a buddha sees a buddha's lifespan as immeasurable, as if it were a summer day. Why is this so? Ordinary beings, mired in the earthly plane, are dim and clouded by obstruction; the two lesser vehicles of the Tripiṭaka and shared teachings, though they have severed the four abodes of affliction, do not see the middle way — if they set aside their segment-bound body to receive a Dharma-nature body, not yet having broken through fundamental ignorance, what that land honors is still only the superior response-body; one should understand that the two lesser vehicles see only a winter's day. As for bodhisattvas who have not yet ascended to the grounds or the abodes, what they see is the same as before; but once fundamental ignorance is broken, down to receiving a partial Dharma-body, then, speaking in terms of what is granted, they attain sight of the reward-body's lifespan, while, speaking in terms of what is withheld, this is still only the superior response-body — not yet having exhausted the source of the reward-body, nor reached the utmost limit of the Dharma-nature, what they see of the Buddha's lifespan is still only a spring day. Only a buddha together with another buddha, having exhausted the nature and reached its very source, sees the Dharma-body's lifespan as if it were a summer day."

《大經》舉三譬譬之，於諸常中虛空第一，一切壽命如來第一，此譬法身壽命無始無終性相凝湛不同應報也；二譬如四河皆歸大海，此譬報身所修萬善皆感佛報壽命海中也；三阿耨達池出四大河，此譬應身壽命從法報出，同他長短也。此品詮量通明三身，若從別意正在報身。何以故？義便文會。義便者，報身智慧上冥下契，三身宛足，故言義便；文會者，我成佛已來甚大久遠，故能三世利益眾生，所成即法身，能成即報身，法報合故能益物，故言文會。以此推之，正意是論報身佛功德也。

The Nirvāṇa Sūtra offers three similes for this: among all permanent things, empty space is foremost; among all lifespans, the Tathāgata's is foremost — this is a simile for the Dharma-body's lifespan, without beginning and without end, its nature and mark forever still and clear, not the same as the reward- or response-body. Second, it is likened to the four rivers that all return to the great ocean — this is a simile for how every good the reward-body has cultivated all converges within the ocean of the buddha-reward's lifespan. Third, the Anavatapta Lake gives rise to four great rivers — this is a simile for how the response-body's lifespan issues from the Dharma-body and reward-body, sharing its length or brevity with others. This chapter, in giving full account, makes all three bodies clear together; yet if one asks for a single, distinguishing emphasis, its true focus lies in the reward-body. Why is this so? Because it is fitting both in doctrine and in the text itself. It is fitting in doctrine because the reward-body's wisdom merges above with principle and matches below with response, and all three bodies are thereby made fully complete — hence "fitting in doctrine." It is fitting in the text because "since I attained buddhahood, it has been exceedingly long" is precisely why the Buddha is able to benefit beings throughout the three times: what is thereby accomplished is the Dharma-body, and what does the accomplishing is the reward-body — and because Dharma-body and reward-body are joined, he is able to benefit beings; hence "fitting in the text." Reasoning in this way, the chapter's true intent lies in discussing the merit and virtue of the reward-body buddha.

Some take the conventional as the origin, and the response arising from the conventional as the trace — trace and origin are then profound and vast, and those on the lower grounds cannot reach their outer bounds by speech or thought, and so they, too, are said to be inconceivably one. Furthermore: these three are not really three, and yet not really one either — "neither three nor one" is the origin, and "both three and one" is the trace, and both alike cut off the path of language and extinguish the sphere of mental activity, being inconceivably one. It is not clear which point the various masters intend, exactly, when they speak of trace and origin as "inconceivably one." As gathered here, all four schemes just given belong to the inconceivable oneness found within the trace alone; what points, at a distance, to the original ground is the inconceivable threefold and fourfold scheme taken as the true origin. From this genuine origin the trace is manifested; and by means of this very trace the origin is revealed — though trace and origin are distinct, they are inconceivably one. Trace and origin understood in this way — how could they fail to differ from what the other sūtras teach? How could they fail to differ from what the other masters say?

「於是中間」下，拂執迹上之疑，疑因疑果，昔教所說處處行因，又處處得記即是果疑。今拂除此疑，指然燈佛者即拂因疑，又復言其入於涅槃即拂果疑，如此因果非復一條，皆我方便非實說也，故名拂疑。或有人云，方便說然燈佛是我之師，然實是釋迦現作，非生現生、非滅現滅，故言又復言其涅槃。今謂不爾，但取前釋。何者？然燈佛于時緣熟，以佛像化之；我緣未熟，但為菩薩從佛得記，得記即是果義，行行即是因，消文自足。言「其」者，即是中間施化之其耳，非謂然燈也。又中間益物，即有形聲兩益，若言值然燈佛者，此有形益，又復言其入於涅槃者，彼佛滅後助佛揚化，即有聲益，若爾形聲兩益，皆屬中間因耳。既有形聲之生，生必有死，死即入涅槃，名此為果耳。不得言中間已成佛果，何者？《法華》之前未說成佛，何得有佛果之疑？舊以然燈是我現作，此亦非解，《法華》前經未論昔已成佛，何教說然燈是我所現而拂此疑耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

From "in the interval between" onward dispels the doubt raised concerning the trace — a doubt about the cause and a doubt about the fruit. What the earlier teaching taught, of practicing the cause in place after place and receiving the prediction in place after place, is precisely this doubt about the fruit. The present passage dispels this doubt: pointing to Dīpaṃkara Buddha dispels the doubt about the cause, and further saying that he entered nirvāṇa dispels the doubt about the fruit — and since this cause and this fruit are, in truth, not one single continuous thread at all, everything is simply my own skillful means, not a statement of literal fact, and this is why it is called "dispelling the doubt." Some say that the skillful teaching that Dīpaṃkara Buddha was my own teacher is actually a case of Śākyamuni himself manifesting as that buddha — not really being born yet manifesting birth, not really ceasing yet manifesting cessation — and this is why it is said, again, that he entered nirvāṇa. I hold that this is not so, and take only the earlier explanation. Why is this? Because Dīpaṃkara Buddha's own conditions had, at that time, already matured, and so he transformed beings by means of a buddha's own image; my own conditions had not yet matured, and so, as a bodhisattva, I simply received the prediction from that buddha — receiving the prediction is precisely the sense of the fruit, and practicing the practices is precisely the cause, and the text's own meaning is thereby fully accounted for. The word "his" refers only to the "his" of the transforming activity carried out in the interval — it does not refer to Dīpaṃkara at all. Moreover, the benefiting of beings in this interval brings both a benefit through form and a benefit through sound: to say "I encountered Dīpaṃkara Buddha" is the benefit through form; and to say, further, that he entered nirvāṇa is that, after that buddha's extinction, he aided in spreading his teaching — this is the benefit through sound. If this is so, then both the benefit through form and the benefit through sound belong entirely to the cause accomplished in the interval. And since there was a birth marked by form and sound, birth necessarily entails death, and death here means entering nirvāṇa — this alone is what is meant by "fruit." One cannot say that buddhahood, as a fruit, had already been accomplished within this interval. Why not? Because prior to the Lotus, buddhahood had not yet been spoken of at all — how, then, could there be any doubt about a "fruit of buddhahood" to begin with? The old view that Dīpaṃkara was myself in manifestation is likewise mistaken: no sūtra prior to the Lotus discusses buddhahood as already accomplished in the past — by which teaching, then, could it be said that Dīpaṃkara was my own manifestation, so as to dispel this doubt?

Ru-Yi Translation

From "if there are beings who come into my presence" onward is the third part, directly making clear what is fitting in benefiting beings, which itself falls into two parts: first, stimulus and response; second, bestowing transformation. "Come into my presence" means that beings of the past, whether of gradual or of sudden capacity, silently reach and touch the Dharma-body. "Observing with the buddha eye" means that, having long ago already attained buddhahood, the Buddha uses the buddha eye to survey and illuminate without the slightest omission or error, about to rouse forth the inferior and the superior response accordingly, in order to benefit them. Wholesome capacity, in general, carries two powers: one, to bring about the flowering reward of birth among gods and humans; the other, to bring about the fruit-reward of the buddha path. If one observes with only the dharma eye, one knows every good deed and, according to whether its condition is weighty or light, sees each attain its own flowering reward, yet cannot ultimately know that it will, in the end, attain the fruit-reward of the wisdom of all modes; but with the buddha eye, one perfectly illuminates every good deed and knows both its beginning and its end. This sūtra teaches, uniformly, that the buddha eye observes and knows that every good deed of every being will, in the end, attain buddhahood — this is the true intent of the one great cause and condition for which the Buddha appears in the world.

「信等諸根」者，信等五根也，慧根即了因，餘根即緣因。此二善根各有利鈍，通攝頓漸機緣：頓機利鈍即是圓、別根機，漸機利鈍即是藏、通機緣。又小乘根名鈍，大乘根名利。又小乘根名利，人天乘名鈍。十法界眾生所有善根利鈍為機，不用惡法，惡法非緣、了二因也。如來悉照十界善機，隨所應度而現形聲饒益也。

"Faith and the other faculties" means the five faculties beginning with faith. The faculty of wisdom is precisely the revealing cause, and the remaining faculties are the conditioning cause. These two good roots each have their sharp and dull forms, embracing, together, the capacities of both sudden and gradual practitioners: the sharp and dull of the sudden capacities are precisely the faculties of the perfect and distinct teachings, and the sharp and dull of the gradual capacities are precisely the faculties of the Tripiṭaka and shared teachings. And again, the Hīnayāna faculty is called dull, and the Mahāyāna faculty is called sharp; or again, the Hīnayāna faculty is called sharp, and the vehicle of gods and humans is called dull. It is the sharpness or dullness of whatever good roots the beings of the ten dharma-realms possess that constitutes their capacity — evil dharmas are not made use of here, since evil is neither the conditioning nor the revealing cause. The Tathāgata illuminates the wholesome capacity of all ten realms without exception, and, according to what each is fit to be brought across by, manifests form and sound to enrich and benefit them.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Not truly being born yet manifesting birth is a way of bestowing both the sudden and the gradual transformations together — the seven kinds of expedient practitioner may well be counted among those who "delight in the lesser dharma," but those to whom the sudden and perfect teaching responds ought to be exactly those who delight in the great teaching. How, then, can this be uniformly judged as "delighting in the lesser dharma"?

Answer: Just now I gave only the outline of this sense; let me now explain it fully, in four distinct meanings altogether. First, with regard to former days: though such beings had roused the great resolve, they were unable to apply themselves single-mindedly, and, largely attached to their own base desires, could not go forth from the household life — hence their base desires are named "the lesser dharma." Second, with regard to the present: while the Buddha had not yet appeared in the world, the gods and humans, though they possessed the capacity for the great teaching, had hearts stained by worldly pleasures and attached to wrong views, and so are named as "delighting in the lesser dharma."

此二義與下譬「宛轉于地」意同也。三、約修行，雖不樂於三界弊欲小法，而樂三乘灰斷亦名小法；雖不樂於三乘近果，而樂歷別修於一乘，不能於一心圓頓普修，故名樂小。此三意約因門，明樂小法也。四、約果門，樂聞近成之小，出釋氏宮始得菩提，不欲樂聞長遠大久之道，故言樂小。此等小心非始今日，若先樂大，佛即不說始成，說始成者皆為樂小法者耳。

These first two senses correspond in meaning to the parable below of "writhing upon the ground." Third, with regard to practice: though such beings do not delight in the base desires and the lesser dharmas of the three realms, they delight in the ashen extinction of the three vehicles, and this, too, is named "the lesser dharma"; and though they do not delight in the near fruits of the three vehicles, they delight in cultivating the one vehicle by traversing its stages one by one, unable to cultivate it fully, all at once, with a single perfect and sudden mind — hence this, too, is named "delighting in the lesser." These three senses concern the gate of the cause, making clear what "delighting in the lesser dharma" means. Fourth, with regard to the gate of the fruit: delighting in hearing of the lesser matter of a near accomplishment — going forth from the palace of the Śākya clan and only then attaining bodhi — and having no wish to hear of the great and far-distant path, is likewise called "delighting in the lesser." Such a lesser-minded disposition did not begin only today; had beings truly delighted in the great from the very start, the Buddha would never have spoken of a near accomplishment at all — whenever he speaks of a near accomplishment, it is entirely for the sake of those who delight in the lesser dharma.

Manifesting birth itself falls into two further parts: manifesting birth, and not truly being born. "Manifesting birth" means the trace itself manifests as birth; "not truly being born" means it is not, in fact, the beginning of any birth at all. "For the sake of such people, I say that I first attained bodhi" — the passage above made clear the two capacities, sharp and dull, that come to touch the Dharma-body; here it manifests the two responses, superior and inferior, in turn — the inferior response answers the dull faculty, and the superior response answers the sharp faculty. As for the inferior response's birth of the physical body: entering the womb through correct wisdom, being born and taking seven steps, as related of Mahākātyāyana's own account, down through the six years of ascetic practice and beyond — this is called the birth of the physical body. From "yet I truly attained" onward makes clear that the origin is, in truth, unborn; it is only that gods, humans, and asuras, seeing these two kinds of physical-body and Dharma-body birth, suppose them to be an actual beginning of birth — but this is not so. It is in order to benefit those who delight in the lesser dharma, whose merit is thin and whose defilement is heavy, and to cause them to attain the buddha path — hence it says, "only by means of skillful means, teaching and transforming beings." "Speaking in this way" means not truly being born, yet manifesting birth — hence it says, "speaking in this way." Other sūtras break through only the inferior response's birth of the physical body as not truly being birth, without yet breaking through even the inferior response's birth of the Dharma-body as not truly being birth; the present sūtra breaks through, directly, the superior response's birth of the Dharma-body as not truly being birth as well. Because, since I truly attained buddhahood so long and distant a time ago, one thereby knows that today's birth of the Dharma-body, whether of the inferior or the superior response, is entirely broken through as not truly being birth at all — and this is permanently different from what any other sūtra teaches.

As for the first, "not false": both the gradual and the sudden capacities, receiving these two kinds of benefit through form and sound, are equally, truly benefited. The passage above, on the past, made clear that all rejoice — as if this were merely the benefit of the mundane siddhānta; the present passage makes clear that none of it is false, in that form and sound, superior and inferior alike, meet the two capacities and, through them, obtain all four siddhāntas, none of them false. The Great Treatise makes clear that the four siddhāntas are all equally real: the mundane is real, and the therapeutic and individual are real as well; yet, argued strictly, these three are "real" only within the world's own conventions, and this very "reality" is, from another angle, unreal, since within these same conditions there is also a worldly reality of the three, while the ultimate-meaning siddhānta alone is truly beyond even that. If one applies this same reality and unreality to the two gates of trace and origin, the benefit received by gradual and sudden practitioners is likewise both unreal and real: in the past, the provisional practice, not yet attaining the benefit of the real path, was, in its cause, unreal; and clinging to the near trace, not yet attaining the true and real benefit of the original ground, was, in its fruit, unreal as well. Now, hearing the teaching of the trace gate and equally entering the true mark of reality, one attains the real benefit within the cause; and hearing the teaching of the origin gate, one removes the very state of mind that clings to the near, and attains the real benefit of the far-distant fruit-ground. What is obtained now is thus two real benefits, set against the two unreal ones of before. As for beings of the perfect and sudden capacity: within the two gates of trace and origin, one real and one unreal, they attain the practice of the middle way — this is attaining the real benefit within the cause, while still clinging to the near fruit, which remains, in that respect, unreal; now, hearing the teaching of the cause, they gain no further separate real benefit beyond what they already had, but hearing the teaching of the distant fruit, they at once attain the real benefit of the fruit itself — where before there was one unreal element, now there is one real one attained — and this is why it is said that everything is entirely true, never false.

The arising of accumulation is called "retreating"; the manifesting of the fruit of impermanence is called "emerging." There is likewise neither an abiding in the world of birth and death nor an entering into the extinction of nirvāṇa — both of these are equally extinguished, and hence it says, "nor is there one who abides in the world, nor one who attains extinction." It is neither the reality of extinction nor the unreality of birth and death — hence it says, "not real, not unreal." It is neither the separateness of the mundane nor the true suchness of what transcends the mundane — hence it says, "not thus, not otherwise." These four together make the middle way clear. When both extremes are doubly negated, and the concluding clause then fixes on one extreme — as, for instance, "neither birth nor death," concluding with "birth"; "both birth and death," concluding with "death"; "both retreating and emerging," concluding with "retreating"; "neither retreating nor emerging," concluding with "emerging"; "neither unreal nor real," concluding with "real"; "both unreal and real," concluding with "unreal" — every formulation of this kind is now negated in turn, down to negating even the single, the compound, and the fully comprehensive formulations as well, and only then is the intent of the middle way truly revealed.

"Not as the three realms themselves see the three realms" means it is unlike what the two kinds of beings within the three realms perceive as the mark of the three realms; only the Buddha alone illuminates, exhaustively and exactly as it truly is, the reality of the three realms, possessing within himself the very function of real wisdom — and this, too, is speaking in accordance with his own intent, and this, too, is sometimes speaking of his own self's affairs; hence the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says, "within the ultimate meaning there is nothing at all to be distinguished." As for the Tathāgata's provisional wisdom, knowing and seeing the mark of the three realms exactly as they are measured, this corresponds to how beings within the three realms themselves see them: knowing and seeing, exactly as it truly is, that there is no such thing as the two kinds of death, yet, in accordance with others' own intent, manifesting a body subject to the two kinds of death and speaking of them as if they existed; that there is neither retreating nor emerging, yet, in accordance with others' own intent, speaking of retreating and emerging as if they existed; that there is neither one who abides in the world nor one who attains extinction, yet, in accordance with others' own intent, manifesting birth in the world and manifesting entry into nirvāṇa, speaking of one who abides in the world and one who attains extinction as if they existed; that there is no reality, yet speaking of the reality of nirvāṇa; that there is no unreality, yet speaking of the unreality of the three realms; that there is no distinction among the three times, yet speaking as if there were; that there is no suchness of ultimate truth, yet speaking as if there were. To be identical with the three realms in seeing the three realms is, in every case, speaking in accordance with others' own intent, and is named "sometimes speaking of another's self's affairs," or "showing another's self's affairs." Because the Tathāgata's twin wisdoms clearly discern the two truths, the twin benefits of form and word are, for this very reason, entirely true, never false.

The sūtra cites the cause in order to set the fruit in relief — and that fruit is precisely beyond all counting. The sūtra says that the lifespan attained through long ages of cultivated practice is "innumerable kalpas" — this is not a lifespan merely prolonged through supernatural power! Why is this so? When the Buddha, cultivating the perfect cause, ascended to the initial abode, he had already attained a constant lifespan — a constant lifespan beyond all reckoning, already doubling beyond any number given before it — how much more so, then, the fruit itself? How, then, can one discard the very fruit that this cause was cited to set in relief, and instead cling stubbornly only to the cause that was doing the citing? Even granting that this cause is already constant and not impermanent — it is exactly as with a crown prince: even in his princely years his allowance is already inexhaustible, so how much less could it ever be exhausted once he ascends to the supreme and honored throne? The plain text stands right here before us — why must one twist and turn it about, only to mislead later generations?

From "yet now, not in reality" onward is the second part: the proclaiming of extinction within the trace. All three bodies alike carry this meaning of apparent extinction though not actually extinct. As the Vimalakīrti Sūtra says, "dharmas are originally unarisen, and so now there is no extinction" — this is precisely the Dharma-body's not being extinguished; yet it also says, "this is the meaning of quiescent extinction" — this is precisely the proclaiming of extinction. Why is this? If one has already fully penetrated this, one does not proclaim quiescent extinction at all — it is proclaimed only for the sake of those who have not yet penetrated it. If one speaks of illumination-within-quiescence, this is precisely the proclaiming of extinction; if one speaks of quiescence-within-illumination, this is precisely the proclaiming of birth. Now the Dharma-body, though neither born nor extinguished, nevertheless has its own birth and extinction: if the mind, deluded, clings and grasps, then affliction is born and wisdom is extinguished; if the mind, awakened, is free of any defilement, then wisdom is born and affliction is extinguished. The extinguishing of delusion and the birth of understanding — this is the extinction of impermanence; but if understanding is born and delusion extinguished, this is itself quiescent extinction — and this birth and extinction, in every case, is discerned solely in terms of the dharma-nature itself. Where the two conditions of delusion and understanding are altogether absent, then there is nothing to proclaim as this birth and extinction at all.

This — that brightness and darkness do not, in fact, remove each other — is exactly the meaning of the reward-body's never being extinguished. But beings who have not yet penetrated this, hearing it, at once suppose that they themselves are already the Buddha, and grow arrogant and self-indulgent, no longer cultivating the path — and so it is proclaimed once again that the path is able to extinguish delusion: when affliction is present, wisdom is absent, and when wisdom is present, affliction is absent — is this not, then, exactly wisdom extinguishing affliction?

應身非滅唱滅者，應是法報之用，體既無滅用豈有窮？即應身不滅，但為眾生若常見佛則生憍恣故，唱我於今夜當取滅度。又法身當體明不滅，報身說不滅必約法身，以理而論，智慧能破為到故破，不到故破為共為獨。如此推理無有能破之功，即智慧不滅惑義；就有智慧則無煩惱，即是慧能滅惑。應身說不滅須約法報，法報常然應用不絕，眾生不盡即不滅度。若法身當體論不生滅，報身了達無能生滅，應身相續不生滅(云云)。

As for the response-body's apparent extinction though not actually extinct: the response-body is the very functioning of the Dharma-body and the reward-body — and if their essence itself is never extinguished, how could their function ever come to an end? Hence the response-body, too, is never truly extinguished; it is only that, were beings to see the Buddha constantly present, they would grow arrogant and self-indulgent, and so it is proclaimed, "this very night I shall pass into extinction." Furthermore, the Dharma-body, with regard to its own essence, is made clear as never extinguished; when the reward-body is spoken of as never extinguished, this must in every case be understood with reference to the Dharma-body. Argued strictly, whether wisdom's "destroying" of affliction is said to occur because the two have met, or because they have not met, or jointly, or independently — reasoned through in any of these ways, there is, in fact, no real function of "destroying" to be found at all; this is precisely the meaning of wisdom never truly extinguishing delusion. Yet, from the standpoint that where wisdom is present affliction is absent, this is indeed wisdom able to extinguish delusion. When the response-body is spoken of as never extinguished, this must be understood with reference to both the Dharma-body and the reward-body: the Dharma-body and reward-body, being constant and unchanging, respond and function without ever ceasing, and, beings themselves being inexhaustible, there is accordingly no extinction at all. In sum: with regard to its own essence, the Dharma-body has, properly speaking, neither birth nor extinction; the reward-body, fully penetrated, has nothing capable of birth or extinction; and the response-body, continuing on without interruption, likewise has neither birth nor extinction (and so on, as the text has it).

Beings who delight in and cling to the lesser dharma, whose obstruction by views and thought is heavy, would, upon hearing that the three bodies are never extinguished, cease to cultivate the path at all, and it would become very hard indeed for them ever to attain accord with it. The first three kinds of physician are precisely the "old physician using milk-medicine" spoken of in the Great Sūtra. The latter seven are all "visiting physicians": those without any real skill use only such remedies as impermanence and suffering, like using bitter and sour medicines; those with skill, having come from afar, in the end have their patients drink milk once more — and this last is the truly consummate good physician. The twelve divisions of the teachings, whose text and principle are extremely profound, correspond to "thoroughly versed in medical formulas"; treating beings' illnesses in accordance with the four siddhāntas corresponds to "skilled in treating every illness." The Sūtra of Infinite Meanings says, "the king of physicians, the great king of physicians" — it is precisely because of this greatness as a physician that he is called the good physician.

「多諸子息」，「若十」，聲聞；「二十」，支佛；「百數」，菩薩。菩薩之子，凡有三種子義：一、就一切眾生，皆有三種性德，佛性即是佛子，故云「其中眾生悉是吾子」，此文云「多諸子息」也。約十心數法即有百子，心王為正因佛性，慧是了因性，餘九相扶起屬緣因性，一數起時九數扶助，如是成百也。性德佛子非善非惡而通善惡，故此十數及與心王為通心數，是以性德三因，悉屬正因佛子。

"Having many sons": "perhaps ten" refers to the śrāvakas; "perhaps twenty," the pratyekabuddhas; "a hundred or so," the bodhisattvas. As for the bodhisattva-sons, there are, in all, three distinct senses of "son." First, with regard to all beings without exception: every being possesses the three inherent virtues, and buddha-nature itself is precisely the buddha's son — hence it is said, "all the beings among them are my own sons," and this passage's "having many sons" refers to this. In terms of the ten mental factors, there are, correspondingly, a hundred sons: the mind-king is the correct-cause buddha-nature, wisdom is the revealing-cause nature, and the remaining nine, mutually supporting its arising, belong to the conditioning-cause nature; when any one of the ten arises, the other nine support and assist it, and in this way a hundred are formed. The inherent-virtue buddha-sons are neither good nor evil, yet pervade both good and evil alike, and it is for this reason that these ten factors, together with the mind-king, are called the pervasive mental factors — and it is on this account that the three causes of inherent virtue all belong to the correct-cause buddha-sons.

二者就昔結緣為佛子，如十六王子覆講《法華》，時聞法者亦生微解，即成了因性；昔微能修行，為緣因性；正性為本，此三因並屬緣因，資發今日一實之解，故以昔日結緣，為緣因佛子，即火宅中三十子也。此約十信，一信起時即具餘九，還有百信，故得結緣為佛子也。

Second, with regard to the bonds formed in past ages, [beings] are the buddha's sons: as when the sixteen princes repeated the exposition of the Lotus Sūtra, and those who heard the Dharma at that time likewise gave rise to a slight understanding, which became the revealing-cause nature; their slight capacity, then, to cultivate practice became the conditioning-cause nature; and the correct nature, being the root of both, means that all three causes here belong properly to the conditioning cause, providing the resources that give rise to today's understanding of the one real teaching. Hence, on account of the bonds formed in past ages, [beings] are named the conditioning-cause buddha-sons — these are precisely the thirty sons within the burning house. This is spoken of in terms of the ten stages of faith: when any one stage of faith arises, the remaining nine are present within it as well, so that there are, correspondingly, a hundred stages of faith — and it is on this basis that bonds formed in the past can make [beings] the buddha's sons.

三者了因之子，即是今日聞《法華經》，安住實智中，我定當作佛，決了聲聞法，是諸經之王，從佛口生、得佛法分，故名真子。此亦有三因性，今既顯了見於佛性，並屬了因佛子；百子之義還將十數入十善法中，十信入初住中，是故正因通於本末。此文明百子，不取了因子，了因子屬下不失心服藥中明之。

Third, the sons of the revealing cause: this refers to those who, today, hear the Lotus Sūtra, abide securely within real wisdom, and know for certain, "I shall assuredly become a buddha," fully resolving the teachings of the śrāvakas — this being the king of all sūtras — and, born from the Buddha's own mouth, receiving their share of the buddha-dharma, are for this reason named true sons. These, too, possess the three causal natures, and, having now clearly manifested and seen the buddha-nature, all belong to the revealing-cause buddha-sons. As for the meaning of the "hundred sons": this brings the number ten back once more into the ten wholesome practices, just as the ten stages of faith enter into the initial abode — and it is for this reason that the correct cause runs through both the origin and the trace alike. This passage makes clear the hundred sons without taking up the sons of the revealing cause; the sons of the revealing cause are made clear below, in the passage on the sons who, not having lost their right mind, take the medicine.

The Sons Who Have Not Lost Their Right Mind 不失心者服藥病差

Chinese Source

「以有事緣遠至他國」者，譬過去應化中現滅也。「諸子於後」下，第二，還已復去，譬現世益物，又二：一、諸子於後飲毒，譬上機應相關，見諸眾生德薄垢重，眾生於佛滅後，樂著三界邪師之法，故云「飲他毒藥」，即是「遊行詣他國」，輪轉諸趣墮在三界，故云「宛轉于地」。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having urgent business, he traveled far away to another land" corresponds to the manifesting of extinction within the response and transformation of the past. From "the sons, afterward" onward is the second part: his returning and then departing again, corresponding to the benefiting of beings in the present age, which itself falls into two: first, the sons afterward drinking poison, corresponding to what was said above about the mutual bearing of capacity and response — seeing that beings' merit is thin and their defilement heavy, beings, after the Buddha's extinction, delight in and cling to the teachings of the false teachers of the three realms, and hence it is said, "they drink another's poisonous medicine" — this is precisely "wandering off to another land" — and, turning about through the various destinies, they fall into the three realms, hence it is said, "writhing upon the ground."

「是時其父還來歸家」下，譬上我少出家得三菩提第二非生現生也。上有二，形聲及利益不虛，今言諸子飲毒去譬上形益，信受邪師之法名為「飲毒」，「失心」是無大小機感生，不失心是有大小機感生。又失心者，貪著三界，失先所種三乘善根也，為是人非滅唱滅。不失心者，雖著五欲而不失三乘善根，為是人故非生現生，善強惡輕見佛即能修道斷結，如子見父求藥病愈；善輕惡重得見佛，亦求護而不修道，如子見父求救不肯服藥，父為此子唱言應死。

From "at that time, the father returned home" onward corresponds to what was said above concerning "I, while young, left the household life and attained unsurpassed bodhi" — that is, the second sense, not truly being born yet manifesting birth. What was said above fell into two: the benefit through form and sound, and the benefit's being never false; here, speaking of the sons drinking poison corresponds to the former, the benefit through form. Believing and accepting the teachings of the false teachers is named "drinking poison"; "losing one's right mind" means being born without any capacity, great or small, capable of stimulating a response, while "not losing one's right mind" means being born with such a capacity, great or small, present. Furthermore, those who have "lost their right mind" are greedily attached to the three realms and have lost the good roots of the three vehicles that they had planted before — and it is for the sake of such people that apparent extinction though not actually extinct is proclaimed. Those who have "not lost their right mind," though attached to the five desires, have not lost the good roots of the three vehicles — and it is for the sake of such people that not truly being born yet manifesting birth is spoken of: for those whose good is strong and evil slight, upon seeing the Buddha they are at once able to cultivate the path and cut off the fetters, like a son who, seeing his father, seeks the medicine and is cured of his illness; for those whose good is slight and evil heavy, upon attaining sight of the Buddha, they, too, seek his protection but do not cultivate the path, like a son who, seeing his father, seeks his rescue yet refuses to take the medicine, so that the father, for this son's sake, proclaims that he is bound to die.

「遙見」者，明佛出世時眾生亦見色身，而為見、思障隔五分，不得親奉法身，故云「遙」也。見聞佛出皆有喜敬之事，現諸經文梵王請等，例是求救之辭也。「父見子等苦」下，譬上聲益，又二：初譬佛受請轉二諦法輪也。「而作是言」下，譬誡勸。「經方」者，即十二部教也，「藥草」即教所詮八萬法門也。從佛出十二部乃至出《涅槃》，此出漸頓藥草也。直從佛出十二部，此出頓藥草也；從佛出修多羅，此出漸藥草也。「色」者譬戒，戒防身口事相彰顯也。

"Seeing from a distance" makes clear that, when the Buddha appears in the world, beings do indeed see his physical body, yet, being obstructed by the five-part obstruction of views and thought, they cannot personally attend upon the Dharma-body — hence it is said, "from a distance." Seeing and hearing of the Buddha's appearance, they all feel joy and reverence, and the various sūtra texts that show Brahmā and others making their requests are, in every case, instances of exactly this kind of plea for rescue. From "the father, seeing his sons suffering thus" onward corresponds to the benefit through sound spoken of above, which itself falls into two: first, corresponding to the Buddha accepting the request and turning the Dharma-wheel of the two truths. From "and he spoke these words" onward corresponds to the parable of admonishing and urging. "Medical formulas" refers precisely to the twelve divisions of the teachings; "medicinal herbs" refers to the eighty-four thousand dharma-gates that these teachings set forth in full. From the Buddha's expounding of the twelve divisions all the way to his expounding of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra — this brings forth medicinal herbs both gradual and sudden. Expounding the twelve divisions directly brings forth the sudden medicinal herbs; expounding the sūtras in their gradual sequence brings forth the gradual medicinal herbs. "Color" corresponds to the precepts, since the precepts guard the actions of body and speech, their outward marks made plainly manifest.

「香」者譬定，功德香熏一切也。「味」者譬慧，能得理味也。此戒定慧即八正道，修八正道能見佛性。又「色」是般若，照了法性之色分明無礙；「香」是解脫，斷德離臭也；「味」是法身，理味也。三法不縱不橫名祕密藏，依教修行得入此藏也。說三乘空三昧力如「擣」、無相如「篩」、無作如「合」，一一三昧具戒定慧也。又空觀如「擣」、假觀如「篩」、中觀如「合」，此三觀各不離戒定慧。將此法與漸頓眾生令修行，名「服」也。

"Fragrance" corresponds to concentration, the fragrance of merit and virtue perfuming all things. "Flavor" corresponds to wisdom, able to attain the flavor of principle itself. These three — precepts, concentration, and wisdom — are precisely the eightfold noble path, and cultivating the eightfold noble path is able to reveal the buddha-nature. Furthermore: "color" is prajñā, the color of dharma-nature illuminated clearly and without obstruction; "fragrance" is liberation, the virtue of severance free of any foul odor; "flavor" is the Dharma-body, the very flavor of principle. These three dharmas, neither vertically nor horizontally arranged, are named the secret treasury, and it is by practicing in accordance with the teaching that one is able to enter this very treasury. The power of the three vehicles' emptiness-samādhi is spoken of as "pounding"; signlessness, as "sifting"; wishlessness, as "combining" — and each single samādhi is equipped with the precepts, concentration, and wisdom all together. Furthermore, the contemplation of emptiness is "pounding," the contemplation of the provisional is "sifting," and the contemplation of the middle is "combining" — and these three contemplations, each in turn, never depart from the precepts, concentration, and wisdom. Giving this Dharma to gradual and sudden beings alike, causing them to cultivate it, is named "taking the medicine."

從「而作是言」乃至「可服」，即是勸門也。從「速除苦惱」乃至「無復眾患」，即是誡門也。將誡勸二教，令諸眾生服法藥也。「其諸子中不失心者」下，譬上得益不虛。上有二：一、不虛，次、釋不虛，今云「其諸子中不失心者服藥病差」，即譬上皆實不虛也。釋不虛，不作譬也。◎

From "and he spoke these words" down through "you should take this" is precisely the gate of urging; from "swiftly remove your suffering and affliction" down through "and never again have any further trouble" is precisely the gate of admonishing. By employing these two gates together, admonishing and urging, he causes all his sons to take the medicine of the Dharma. From "among these sons, those who had not lost their right mind" onward corresponds to what was said above about the benefit actually attained being never false. What was said above fell into two: first, that it is never false; second, the explanation of why it is never false. Here it is said, "among these sons, those who had not lost their right mind took the medicine and their illness was cured" — this corresponds precisely to what was said above, that everything is entirely true, never false. As for the explanation of why it is never false — no further parable is given for this. ◎
