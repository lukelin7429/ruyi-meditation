The Dragon Princess's Instant

This page continues Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Fahua Wenju (妙法蓮華經文句). Fascicle 8 is the most chapter-dense in the whole treatise: it closes the prediction chapters, gives all of 法師品 ("Teacher of the Dharma"), 見寶塔品 (the "Jeweled Stūpa" that rises from the earth), 提婆達多品 ("Devadatta," with the dragon princess's instant attainment of buddhahood), and 勸持品 ("Encouraging the Upholding of This Sūtra"), before opening 安樂行品 ("Peaceful Practices"). We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Commentary on the Words of the Sūtra of the Lotus Blossom of the Wondrous Dharma, Fascicle Eight, Upper Part

天台智者大師說

Expounded by the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai

The Land's Purity: The Sevenfold Prediction, and the Ten Virtues 授記正文：國土莊嚴與十善十德

Chinese Source

◎「漸漸具足菩薩」下，第二，授記。文為七：一、明因圓；二、「過無量」下，明果滿；三、明國土廣淨；四、明國劫名字；五、明佛壽量；六、明法住大久；七、明佛滅後供養舍利。就第三國廣淨中復五：一、明國大嚴淨。「地平如掌」者，經直言如掌不言手掌，手掌不平則非所引，海底有石名掌，此石無有一微塵許不平，當是類如海掌耳。又《賢劫經》正明如佛手掌，非引人掌也。二、明純是善道。三、明人天福慧具足，《月藏》第九法食、喜食、禪食，經文總言法喜禪悅，別分應有三種差別。四、明菩薩聲聞眾數甚多。五、總結也。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "gradually becoming complete as a bodhisattva" onward is, second, the bestowal of the prediction. The text has seven parts: first, making clear the fullness of the cause; second, from "measureless" onward, making clear the completeness of the fruit; third, making clear the vastness and purity of the land; fourth, making clear the names of the land and the kalpa; fifth, making clear the buddha's lifespan; sixth, making clear the long duration of the Dharma's abiding; and seventh, making clear the offerings made to the relics after the buddha's parinirvāṇa. Within the third, the vastness and purity of the land, there are, again, five parts: first, making clear the land's great adornment and purity. As for "the ground level as a palm": the sūtra says simply "as a palm," not "as a palm of the hand" — for the palm of a hand is not perfectly level, and so it would not be the fitting reference; there is, at the bottom of the sea, a stone called a "palm," and this stone does not have so much as a single mote of dust's worth of unevenness, and this must be the kind of "palm" meant, the sea's palm. The Bhadrakalpika Sūtra, moreover, makes clear that it means truly the palm of the buddha's own hand, not a citation of a human's palm. Second, making clear that it is purely the good destinies. Third, making clear that the merit and wisdom of humans and devas there are complete — the ninth fascicle of the Candragarbha speaks of the food of the Dharma, the food of joy, and the food of dhyāna, while the sūtra text here speaks of them together simply as "the joy of the Dharma and the delight of dhyāna," though, distinguished separately, there ought to be these three kinds of distinction. Fourth, making clear that the numbers of bodhisattvas and śrāvakas there are very great. Fifth, the general conclusion.

《月藏》第五云「不殺得十功德，一於一切眾生得無所畏，乃至第十命終生善道後作佛，國無害仗之具國人長壽；不盜十功德，一果報具足而大，為事決斷無有難礙，乃至第十死生善道，後作佛國華寶莊嚴充滿；不淫十功德，一諸根律儀為事決斷，乃至死生善道，後作佛國無女人；不妄語十功德，一眾生信其言，乃至死生善道，後作佛時國無臭穢常滿寶華；不兩舌十功德，一身不可壞，乃至死生善道，後作佛國魔不能壞眷屬；不惡口得十功德，一柔軟語乃至死生善道，作佛國法聲充滿；不綺語十功德，一天人愛敬，乃至死生善道，作佛國眾生強記不忘；不貪十功德，一身根不缺，乃至死生善道，作佛國無魔外道；不瞋十功德，一離一切瞋，乃至死生善道，作佛國人得三昧；不邪見十功德，一心性柔善，乃至死生善道作佛國人正信。」

The fifth fascicle of the Candragarbha says: "Not killing brings ten kinds of merit: first, gaining freedom from fear toward every sentient being, and so on up to the tenth, that at the end of one's life one is born into a good destiny and afterward becomes a buddha, in a land without weapons for doing harm, whose people live long lives. Not stealing brings ten kinds of merit: first, one's karmic reward is ample and great, one is decisive in one's affairs and meets with no obstruction, and so on up to the tenth, that at death one is born into a good destiny and afterward becomes a buddha whose land is filled with flowers and jeweled adornments. Not engaging in sexual misconduct brings ten kinds of merit: first, one's faculties are disciplined and one is decisive in one's affairs, and so on up to death and birth into a good destiny, afterward becoming a buddha whose land has no women. Not speaking falsely brings ten kinds of merit: first, beings trust one's word, and so on up to death and birth into a good destiny, so that when one afterward becomes a buddha the land has no foulness and is forever filled with jeweled flowers. Not speaking divisively brings ten kinds of merit: first, one's body cannot be destroyed, and so on up to death and birth into a good destiny, afterward becoming a buddha whose land no demon can destroy the retinue of. Not speaking harshly brings ten kinds of merit: first, gentle and soft speech, and so on up to death and birth into a good destiny, becoming a buddha whose land is filled with the sound of the Dharma. Not speaking idly brings ten kinds of merit: first, being loved and honored by devas and humans, and so on up to death and birth into a good destiny, becoming a buddha whose land's beings have strong memory and do not forget. Not being covetous brings ten kinds of merit: first, one's bodily faculties are never lacking, and so on up to death and birth into a good destiny, becoming a buddha whose land has no demons or non-Buddhist paths. Not harboring anger brings ten kinds of merit: first, being free of every anger, and so on up to death and birth into a good destiny, becoming a buddha whose land's people attain samādhi. And not holding false views brings ten kinds of merit: first, a nature of mind that is gentle and good, and so on up to death and birth into a good destiny, becoming a buddha whose land's people hold right faith."

The Verses Recapitulating the Disclosed Trace and the Prediction 偈頌：頌發迹授記

Chinese Source

偈有二十一行半，頌上發迹授記。初，有十四行，頌發迹；次，七行半，頌授記。初復二：前七行，總發諸聲聞迹，頌上我等之意也；後七行，頌上發滿願迹。總中有五：初一行，總標佛子為行難思，已得垂迹之法。次「知眾樂小法」下，第二，一行，明垂迹之由。次「以無數方便」下，第三，二行，明垂迹利益。次「內祕」下，第四，二行，明內懷大道外現小失。次「若我具足」下，第五，一行，指略抑廣。「小欲」者，示求小乘也；「懈怠」，示退大乘也。非但示為聲聞，亦作外道及三毒凡夫也。身子示瞋、難陀示貪、調達示癡(云云)。

Ru-Yi Translation

The verses have twenty-one-and-a-half lines, versifying, above, the disclosing of the trace and the bestowal of the prediction. First, fourteen lines versify the disclosing of the trace; next, seven-and-a-half lines versify the bestowal of the prediction. The first, again, has two parts: the first seven lines disclose, generally, the traces of the various śrāvakas, versifying the sense of "we," above; the last seven lines versify, above, the disclosing of Pūrṇa's trace. Within the general part there are five subparts: first, one line generally sets forth that the buddha's sons have practiced what is hard to conceive of, and have already attained the dharma of manifesting a trace. Next, from "knowing that the assembly delighted in the lesser Dharma" onward, second, one line makes clear the reason for manifesting a trace. Next, from "by means of measureless skillful means" onward, third, two lines make clear the benefit of manifesting a trace. Next, from "inwardly kept secret" onward, fourth, two lines make clear his inwardly holding the great path while outwardly manifesting the lesser deficiency. Next, from "were I to speak in full" onward, fifth, one line points to what is brief and holds back what is extensive. As for "of little desire": this shows him seeking the lesser vehicle; "indolent" shows him withdrawing from the Great Vehicle. It is not only that he manifested as a śrāvaka — he also acted as a follower of other paths, and as one of the three poisons, an ordinary being. Śāriputra manifested anger, Nanda manifested greed, and Devadatta manifested delusion (and so on).

「今此富」下，第二，七行，頌發滿願迹本。上文有三，今略頌二。初，五行，頌顯過去本；後「未來亦供養」下，第二，二行，頌三世中佛所行因；略不頌七佛及現佛也。

From "this Pūrṇa here" onward, second, seven lines versify the disclosing of Pūrṇa's trace and his fundamental origin. The text above had three parts, and here, in brief, only two are versified: first, five lines versify revealing the past origin; next, from "in the future he shall likewise make offerings" onward, second, two lines versify the causal practice of the buddha across the three periods of time — omitting, in brief, the versifying of the seven buddhas and the present buddha.

「供養諸如來」下，七行半，頌授記。上文有七，今頌其四：初半行，頌因圓。次半行，頌果滿。「其國名淨」下三句，超頌國劫名號。「菩薩眾甚多」下五行三句，頌國土廣淨。略不頌壽命、法住、滅後起塔也。

From "making offerings to the various tathāgatas" onward, seven-and-a-half lines versify the bestowal of the prediction. The text above had seven parts, and here four are versified: first, half a line versifies the fullness of the cause; next, half a line versifies the completeness of the fruit; from "its land, called Pure" onward, three phrases versify, out of order, the names of the land and the kalpa; and from "the bodhisattva assembly, very numerous" onward, five lines and three phrases versify the vastness and purity of the land — omitting, in brief, the versifying of the lifespan, the abiding of the Dharma, and the raising of a stūpa after the parinirvāṇa.

The Prediction for the Twelve Hundred 授千二百記

Chinese Source

第二，授千二百記：一、念請，二、與記，三、領解。請、記如文。授記文有長行、偈頌。長行有三：一、總許千二百記；二、別授陳如，陳如最初悟道，居首上座故別授記；三、別授記五百，五百名同須別與記。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the bestowal of the prediction on the twelve hundred: first, their silent request; second, the bestowal of the prediction; third, their receiving and understanding. The request and the bestowal are as the text has them. The text bestowing the prediction has both a prose section and a verse section. The prose has three parts: first, generally granting the prediction to the twelve hundred; second, separately bestowing it on Kauṇḍinya — since Kauṇḍinya was the very first to awaken to the path and holds the foremost seat, he is separately given the prediction; and third, separately bestowing it on the five hundred — since the five hundred share a single name, they must be separately given the prediction.

問：
但見五百得記，不見千二百。

Question:
Only the five hundred are seen to receive the prediction — the twelve hundred are nowhere to be seen.

答：
此五百即千二百數，頌中末後一行半總授七百，故是千二百也。

Answer:
These five hundred are themselves among the number of the twelve hundred; in the verses, the last line-and-a-half generally bestows the prediction on the remaining seven hundred, and this is why it comes to twelve hundred.

又〈持品〉云「我先總記一切聲聞皆已授記」，即指今一行半，非是止授七百聲聞也。

Moreover, the "Encouraging Devotion" chapter says, "I have already, in general, given the prediction to every śrāvaka" — this refers precisely to the present line-and-a-half, and does not mean that the prediction was bestowed only on the seven hundred śrāvakas.

The Parable of the Jewel Sewn in the Robe 五百領解：繫珠醉酒譬

Chinese Source

偈頌有十一行，為二：初九行半，頌記陳如及與五百；後一行半，總授一切聲聞記。五百領解，文有長行、偈頌。長行先經家敘其歡喜，次自陳領解。經家先慶今得解歡喜，次愧昔不解故自責。慶中先明內心慶喜，後明外形恭敬也。悔過自責者，即是明其愧昔不解也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The verses have eleven lines, in two parts: first, nine-and-a-half lines versify the prediction given to Kauṇḍinya and the five hundred; second, the last line-and-a-half generally bestows the prediction on every śrāvaka. The receiving and understanding of the five hundred has both a prose section and a verse section. The prose first has the sūtra's own narrator recount their joy, and next has them themselves declare their own receiving and understanding. The narrator first celebrates their now gaining understanding and their joy, and next their shame at their former lack of understanding, for which they reproach themselves. Within the celebration, it first makes clear their inward joy of celebration, and afterward the outward form of their reverence. Their repenting of their fault and reproaching themselves is precisely what makes clear their shame at their former lack of understanding.

「世尊我等」下，第二，自陳領解，有二：一、法說，二、譬說。法說中初悔得少為足，次責根鈍難悟。從「世尊」乃至「滅度」，是悔責昔迷得小為足不知求大；從「今乃知之」，是責根鈍始悟不早知之，今知小非究竟、大為真實也。譬中二：初、略，次、正舉譬。「如無智者」，略舉譬況。「所以者何」下，釋無智意也。

From "World-Honored One, we" onward, second, is their own declaring of their receiving and understanding, which has two parts: first, the dharma-teaching; second, the parable-teaching. Within the dharma-teaching, they first repent of having taken the little for enough, and next reproach themselves for the dullness of their faculties, so hard to awaken. From "World-Honored One" up through "parinirvāṇa" is their repenting and reproaching themselves for the past delusion of taking the lesser for enough and not knowing to seek the greater; from "only now do we know it" is their reproaching themselves for the dullness of their faculties, having only now, for the first time, awakened to it, not knowing it sooner — now knowing that the lesser is not the ultimate, and the greater is the true and real. Within the parable there are two parts: first, in brief; next, the direct raising of the parable. "Like one lacking wisdom" briefly raises the parable's likeness. From "for what reason" onward explains the sense of lacking wisdom.

譬說有二：一者醉酒譬，譬法說自悔得少為足不知求大，領前法譬宿世中施權意；二者親友覺悟譬，譬法說自責根鈍難悟，今乃知之，領前法譬宿世中顯實。就初復三意：一者繫珠譬，領上王子結緣；二者醉臥不覺譬，領上遇其退大；三者起已遊行譬，領上接之以小。

The parable-teaching has two parts: first, the parable of drunkenness, which illustrates the dharma-teaching's self-reproach for taking the little as enough and not knowing to seek the greater, receiving the earlier dharma-parable's sense of bestowing the provisional in ages past; second, the parable of the awakening friend, which illustrates the dharma-teaching's self-reproach for the dullness of faculties, so hard to awaken, only now knowing it, receiving the earlier dharma-parable's revealing of the real in ages past. Within the first, again, there are three senses: first, the parable of the sewn-in jewel, receiving, above, the bond formed with the prince; second, the parable of lying drunk and unaware, receiving, above, his encountering the retreat from the great; and third, the parable of rising and wandering off, receiving, above, his being received with the lesser.

「譬如有人」者，即二乘人也。「親友」者，昔日第十六王子也。「家」即大乘教為家也。「醉酒而臥」者，當于爾時大機暫發無明暫伏，以得聞經內心微解，以無明重故還復迷失。醉有二義：一、重醉，都不覺知；二、輕醉，微覺尋忘亦名不覺。雖有二義，終成繫珠如毒鼓耳。

As for "suppose there were a man": this is precisely a person of the two lesser vehicles. As for "a close friend": this is the sixteenth prince of former days. As for "house": this is the Great Vehicle teaching taken as a house. As for "grew drunk on wine and lay down": at that very time, the great capacity briefly stirred and ignorance was briefly subdued, so that, on hearing the sūtra taught, there was a faint understanding within the heart — yet, because the ignorance was heavy, they fell back once more into delusion. Drunkenness carries two senses: first, heavy drunkenness, in which there is no awareness at all; second, light drunkenness, in which there is a faint awareness that is soon forgotten, likewise called unawareness. Though there are these two senses, in the end it comes to the sewing-in of the jewel, just as with the drum smeared with poison.

「官事當行」者，明王子餘處機興逗緣往應，故云「當行」。弘法化他此非私務，故云「官事」。「無價寶珠」者，一乘實相真如智寶也。「繫其衣裏」者，慚愧忍辱能遮瞋恚及防外惡，即是外衣；信樂之心內裏善根，即是內衣。于時聞法微信樂欲，即了因智願種子也。

As for "having official business, he had to go": this makes clear that, elsewhere, the prince's capacity stirred with beings, and he went to respond to the connection, and this is why it says "had to go." Proclaiming the Dharma and transforming others is not a private affair, and this is why it says "official business." As for "a priceless jewel": this is the jewel of the wisdom of the one-vehicle real mark, true suchness. As for "sewed it into the lining of his robe": shame, self-reproach, and forbearance, able to block anger and guard against outward evil, are precisely the outer robe; and the mind of faith and delight, the wholesome root within the lining, is precisely the inner robe. The faint faith, delight, and aspiration that arose at that time on hearing the Dharma are precisely the seed of the revealing cause, the wisdom-vow.

第二，「醉臥不覺知」者，無明心重尋復不憶，此領中間懈退不受大法也。第三，「起已遊行他國」者，領上中間接之以小，受三乘化也，善根欲發厭苦求樂，故云「起已遊行」。無明覆解不知向本求大乘衣食，故言向他國求於小乘衣食。若魔佛相望，生死魔界為他國，佛法大小皆為本國；就小大相望，小乘未免生死，猶是他國，大乘永免生死，乃為本土。

Second, as for "lying drunk and unaware": the mind of ignorance, being heavy, soon forgets again, and this receives the retreat and unwillingness to accept the Great Dharma, in the interval, above. Third, as for "rising, he wandered off to another country": this receives, above, his being received with the lesser in the interval, undergoing the transformation of the three vehicles; as his wholesome root sought to arise, weary of suffering and seeking ease, and this is why it says "rising, he wandered off." Covered by ignorance and understanding not to seek the clothing and food of the Great Vehicle at his own root, it is therefore said that he sought, in another country, the clothing and food of the lesser vehicle. If Māra and the Buddha are set against each other, then the realm of Māra, birth-and-death, is "another country," while the Buddha's Dharma, whether lesser or great, is all "his own country"; but if the lesser and the great are set against each other, then the lesser vehicle has not yet escaped birth-and-death, and is still "another country," while the Great Vehicle forever escapes birth-and-death, and is thus "his own land."

究竟還源也，明背大乘國往小乘土，不知從珠取給，而傭作自資獲一日價，得少為足也。

This is, in the end, returning to the source — it makes clear his turning his back on the country of the Great Vehicle and going to the land of the lesser vehicle, not knowing to draw upon the jewel for his provision, but instead hiring himself out to support himself, earning a single day's wage, and taking the little as enough.

The Parable of the Friend's Awakening 親友覺悟譬

Chinese Source

「於後會遇」下，第二，親友發覺譬，領上以是本因緣今說《法華》等賜大車也。此為三：先、呵責，二、示珠，三、勸貿。呵責譬上動執生疑，示珠譬宿世因緣，勸貿譬得記作佛，三周皆有此三意。法說中我令脫苦縛，即是呵責；五佛章即是開示；身子得記即是勸貿。譬說中我先不言皆為菩提，即是呵責；三車一車即是示珠；中根得記即是勸貿。下根宿世因緣汝等善聽，即是呵責；覆講結緣還為說大，即是示珠；下根得記即是勸貿。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "later, meeting by chance" onward, second, is the parable of the friend's awakening, receiving, above, "by reason of this original bond, I now teach the Lotus" and so on, and the bestowal of the great cart. This has three parts: first, reproach; second, showing the jewel; third, urging him to trade it in. The reproach illustrates, above, the stirring of attachment and the arising of doubt; showing the jewel illustrates the bond formed in past lives; urging him to trade it in illustrates receiving the prediction of buddhahood — all three of the periods of teaching carry these same three senses. Within the dharma-teaching, "I made you free of the bonds of suffering" is precisely the reproach; the chapter of the five buddhas is precisely the disclosure; and Śāriputra's receiving the prediction is precisely the urging to trade it in. Within the parable-teaching, "I did not at first say that it was all for the sake of awakening" is precisely the reproach; the three carts becoming one cart is precisely the showing of the jewel; and the middling capacity's receiving the prediction is precisely the urging to trade it in. For the lowest capacity, "listen well to the karmic bond of past lives" is precisely the reproach; the relay-teaching that formed the bond and now again teaches the great is precisely the showing of the jewel; and the lowest capacity's receiving the prediction is precisely the urging to trade it in.

繫珠中三意望三周者，始在佛樹以大擬，即是繫珠；無機息化即是醉臥；尋施方便即是起行。譬喻中二萬億佛所，即是繫珠；遣傍人追悶絕不受，即是醉臥；三車引得即是起行。因緣中大通智勝佛所，即是繫珠；中路懈退即是醉臥；接之以小即是起行。此等皆名領權也。「某年日月」者，指大通佛所也。

The three preceding periods of teaching belong to the trace gate, where the direct teaching of receiving, understanding, and the bestowal of the prediction has now come to its end; the five chapters that follow are the trace gate's transmission and flow — not merely for the benefit of that present assembly, but wishing, further, to moisten ages yet to come, and this is why there are these five chapters of transmission and flow. The two chapters "The Teacher of the Dharma" and "The Jeweled Stūpa" make clear that the merit of proclaiming the sūtra runs deep and its blessing weighs heavy, transmitting and making flow the vast benefit not yet heard of; the single chapter "Devadatta" cites his proclaiming the sūtra in a former age, benefiting both himself and others together, in order to bear witness to how deep and heavy this merit is; in the "Encouraging Devotion" chapter, the eighty thousand great beings who accomplish the power of forbearance proclaim the sūtra in this world, while those newly given the prediction proclaim it in other worlds; and as for the single chapter "Peaceful Practices" — the old explanation says it is meant to receive and transmit those who might retreat, though this may or may not be entirely so. Those still outside the ranks of the sages, of newly initial resolve, delight in this excellent blessing, but, seeing śrāvakas fearful and hesitant, and hearing that bodhisattvas are cast out and reviled, they look back upon their own weak strength, of no benefit to themselves or others, and so give rise to retreat and sinking down; the Buddha, for the sake of such people, teaches the peaceful practices, so that, relying upon them, the Dharma may be proclaimed without fear of danger or hardship.

Pointing to particular persons and asking whether they were seen matches precisely those persons who uphold the sūtra and gain its blessing.

As for those before the Buddha whose capacity meets the moment in wondrous awakening — these are precisely the twenty-five hundred of broad learning and deep understanding, all of whom have already, in what came before, received the general and the separate predictions; those now distinguished as kinds are, whether the classes of the eightfold host or the classes of the fourfold assembly and the three vehicles, all bound to this Lotus assembly. As for "all before the Buddha": this makes clear the occasion — that they encountered the Buddha there on his seat. As for "a single phrase, a single verse": this is hearing the Dharma to the very least degree. As for "even a single thought-moment": this is the occasion of the very briefest span. As for "all receive the prediction that they shall attain awakening": this makes clear that, though what was heard was of the very least, and the occasion of the very briefest span, the merit of their rejoicing brought them, in the end, to the buddha-fruit — how much more so, then, for one who hears it in full and upholds it his whole life through, with the five kinds of transmission and flow and offerings of the three karmas! (And so on.) As for "hearing a single phrase, a single verse": those of little hearing and shallow understanding all now receive the prediction — if even the least receive it, how much more those of much and of deep understanding? Taking the least to imply the most, its reach is universal and broad, just so. Since the lower period of teaching is thus, the middling and highest are likewise so, and this may be known by inference, without need of further explanation.

The old explanation says pratyekabuddhas and bodhisattvas do not receive predictions in this way — but the present text bestows the prediction on all three vehicles alike, and there is no need for doubt on this point.

It is also called a caitya, a term for a memorial that holds no bodily relics. Since this particular stūpa holds an entire, undissolved body, it cannot properly be called a caitya. The Āgamas set out "the four relic-sites" (a term glossed in the text with a pronunciation note): the place of birth, the place of attaining the path, the place of turning the Dharma wheel, and the place of entering extinction — stūpas are raised at these four places, and the jeweled stūpa here is the relic-site of a former buddha's entry into extinction. The sūtra says that the Buddha's three bodies are born from this very sūtra: every buddha sits in the place of enlightenment by means of it, every buddha turns the Dharma wheel by means of it, and every buddha enters parinirvāṇa by means of it — so this Lotus Sūtra itself is precisely the fourfold relic-site of the buddhas of the three periods of time.

先佛已居，今佛並坐，當佛亦然。此塔出來明顯此事，四眾皆覩，故言「見寶塔品」。《瓔珞經》善吉問「生身全身碎身功德等耶？」佛言「不等。色身言教化訓，三業具足清淨，眾生得至道場；全碎舍利正可威神光明，供養得福，是故不等。」又問頂王如來「十二那術劫說法教化，舍利亦爾此應是等。」佛言「皆頂王如來神力所作耳。」彼經格全碎舍利皆由生身佛力，今經格生身全碎舍利、法身偏圓舍利皆從經出，顯此經功德弘持力大，從地涌來證明此事，四眾皆覩，故言「見寶塔品」。北地師云：「佛為身子說經時，寶塔已現為作證明，若說經竟，來證何等？經家作次第，安置三周後耳。」此乃人情則不可信。今依《薩雲分陀利經》云「佛說法華無央數偈時，有七寶塔從地涌出，中有金床，床上有佛，字袍休蘭羅，漢言大寶，歎釋尊言：『我故來供養，願坐我金床，更為我說《薩雲分陀利》。』」依此經證即是說三周，後更請壽量，明文聖說而不肯用，人之穿鑿那可承耶？此塔正為證前請後從地涌出，四眾皆覩，故言「見寶塔品」。地師說多寶是法身佛；《釋論》說多寶誓願化身來證經，此文亦爾。師言，法身無來無出，報身巍巍堂堂，應身普應一切，若即此謂是三佛者，未盡其體也，秖是表示而已，多寶表法佛，釋尊表報佛，分身表應佛，三佛雖三而不一異，應作如此說，如此信解也。此四番即四悉檀解見寶塔云云。塔出為兩：一、發音聲以證前，開塔以起後。證前者，證三周說法皆是真實，若略言真實者，皆與實相相應也；若廣言真實者，離四句絕百非也；若處中說者，八不名真實。塔從地涌示不滅，分座共坐示不生，入塔示不常，現塔示不斷，分身示不一，全身示不異，多寶讓座示不來，釋迦坐半座示不出，八不顯然，故是真實。

His buddhahood is already established, the present buddha is seated beside him, and buddhas yet to come will do likewise. The stūpa's emergence makes this fact manifest, seen by the entire fourfold assembly, and this is why it is called the chapter "The Jeweled Stūpa." The Yingluo Sūtra ("Jeweled Necklace") records Subhūti asking, "Are the merits of a living body, an entire body-relic, and a fragmented body-relic equal?" The Buddha answers, "They are not equal. The physical body speaks and teaches and instructs, its three karmic activities entirely pure, and by it sentient beings are brought to the place of enlightenment; whole or fragmented relics can only shine with awe-inspiring light, and making offerings to them earns merit — for this reason they are not equal." Subhūti then asks about the Tathāgata Crown-King: "Across twelve nayutas of kalpas he taught and transformed beings by preaching the Dharma, and his relics did the same — should not these, then, be equal?" The Buddha answers, "All of this was simply the working of the Tathāgata Crown-King's own spiritual power." That sūtra measures whole and fragmented relics entirely by the power of the living buddha; the present sūtra, by contrast, measures the living body, the whole or fragmented relics, and the partial or perfect relics of the dharma-body as all issuing from the sūtra itself, making clear that the merit and the power to uphold and transmit this sūtra are vast — and it is precisely to bear this out that the stūpa rises from the earth, seen by the entire fourfold assembly, and this is why it is called "The Jeweled Stūpa."

A Northern master says: "When the Buddha was teaching this sūtra for Śāriputra's sake, the jeweled stūpa had already appeared to bear witness — so once the teaching of the sūtra was finished, what more was there left for it to confirm? It must be that the compilers of the sūtra simply arranged the sequence of the text, and placed it after the three rounds of teaching." This, however, is merely a matter of human opinion and cannot be trusted. Here we rely instead on the Sarvajñā-puṇḍarīka Sūtra, an early alternate translation of the Lotus, which says: "While the Buddha was speaking the countless verses of the Lotus, a stūpa of the seven treasures rose up out of the earth. Within it was a golden couch, and upon the couch a buddha, whose name, rendered into Chinese, is 'Great Treasure.' He praised the World-Honored Śākyamuni, saying: 'I have come especially to make offerings — I beg you to sit upon my golden couch, and speak for me, once more, the Sarvajñā-puṇḍarīka.'" According to the testimony of this sūtra, then, this occurs precisely at the point of the three rounds of teaching, after which comes the further request concerning the Buddha's lifespan — the plain wording of the sage's own teaching is right here, and yet it is simply not made use of. How can anyone's forced, private reading of the text be trusted over this? This stūpa arises from the earth precisely to bear witness to what came before and to open the request for what is to follow, seen by the entire fourfold assembly, and this is why it is called "The Jeweled Stūpa."

A Northern master says that Prabhūtaratna is the dharma-body buddha; the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra says that Prabhūtaratna is a transformation-body who comes, by the power of his own vow, to bear witness to the sūtra — and the present text agrees with this. My own teacher says: the dharma-body neither comes nor goes; the reward-body is towering and majestic; the response-body responds universally to all — and to say simply that Prabhūtaratna is any one of these three bodies fails to exhaust what he truly is. This is, rather, only a matter of symbolic representation: Prabhūtaratna represents the dharma-buddha, Śākyamuni represents the reward-buddha, and the emanation-buddhas represent the response-buddha. Though the three buddha-bodies are three, they are neither strictly one nor strictly different — this is how the matter ought to be stated, and this is how it ought to be received in faith and understanding.

These four accounts correspond to the four siddhāntas' explanation of the jeweled stūpa's appearance, and so on. The stūpa's emergence serves two purposes: first, its emitting a great sound bears witness to what came before; second, its opening initiates what is to follow. As to bearing witness to what came before: it bears witness that the teaching given across the three rounds is, in every case, true and real. Stated briefly, "true and real" means being in accord with the true mark of reality; stated at length, it means being free of the four propositions and cut off from the hundred negations; stated in a middle way, it means the eight negations themselves constitute the true and real. The stūpa's rising from the earth shows non-extinction; the sharing of the seat shows non-arising; entering the stūpa shows non-permanence; the stūpa's own appearing shows non-severance; the emanation-buddhas show non-oneness; the whole, undissolved body shows non-difference; Prabhūtaratna's yielding of his seat shows non-coming; Śākyamuni's sitting on half the seat shows non-departing — the eight negations stand out plainly here, and this is why it is true and real.

As for the interpretation through the contemplation of mind: cultivating contemplation in accordance with the sūtra, the contemplating wisdom and its object, the dharma-body, must inevitably meet — just as the stūpa's coming bears witness to the sūtra. Because Prabhūtaratna has come forth, the three buddha-bodies are thereby made manifest; and because the sūtra is upheld, the three bodies are thereby possessed in full. Ru-Yi Translation

From "Mahāpratibhāna, by the Tathāgata's own spiritual power" onward is, second, setting forth the emanation-buddhas' gathering from afar. Within this there are seven parts: first, Mahāpratibhāna's request to see Prabhūtaratna; second, the response, gathering the emanation-buddhas; third, Mahāpratibhāna's request that they be gathered; fourth, the emission of light to summon them from afar; fifth, the buddhas' arriving together; sixth, the purifying and adorning of the buddha-lands; seventh, the granting of consent to open the stūpa. Explaining the first request: it says "by relying on the Buddha's spiritual power," because to open the stūpa the buddhas must first be gathered, and gathering the buddhas means the entrustment, and the entrustment means summoning the disciples from below — and once those from below emerge, it is fitting that the near be opened to reveal the distant. This is the very reason for so great a matter — how could it not be the Buddha's own spiritual power that prompts this question? The remaining sections may be understood from the text itself. From "at that time the Buddha emitted a ray from the tuft of white hair" onward is, fourth, the emission of light to summon them from afar. As for the threefold transformation of the land into purity: this arises precisely from samādhi, and the samādhi has three aspects — the first transformation of the Sahā world is the liberation of turning away, able to transform defilement into purity; the second transformation, of two hundred myriads of koṭis of nayutas of worlds, is the mastery, sovereign and unobstructed, of the sphere of superiority; the third transformation, of a further two hundred myriads of koṭis of nayutas, is the unobstructed reach into every sphere whatsoever. Again, the first transformation into purity signifies the purifying-away of the four fundamental afflictions; the second signifies the purifying-away of the dust-and-sand-like afflictions; the third signifies the purifying-away of ignorance itself. "At that time the buddhas, seated upon lion thrones" — seventh, the granting of consent to open the stūpa — has, again, five parts: first, the buddhas' greeting one another and stating their consent; second, Śākyamuni's opening of the stūpa; third, the fourfold assembly's seeing and hearing together; fourth, the two buddhas' dividing the seat and sitting together; fifth, the fourfold assembly's request for empowerment. That the buddhas together grant their consent to open the stūpa is like the granting of consent within a monastic assembly's formal procedure. The Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra speaks of a certain number of buddhas granting consent; the Avataṃsaka likewise speaks of a certain number of buddhas of the ten directions together teaching the Avataṃsaka; the Mahāprajñāpāramitā likewise speaks of a thousand buddhas together teaching the Prajñāpāramitā — yet none of these say that these are Śākyamuni's own emanation-buddhas. Measured against the present sūtra, however, they, too, ought to be emanation-buddhas — it is only that, being expressed there by way of expedient means, this was not made explicit at the time. The present sūtra not only gives their number, but states outright that these are emanation-buddhas, all of them coming to grant their consent.

From "at that time Śākyamuni, seeing" onward is, second — the opening of the stūpa is precisely the opening of the provisional, and the seeing of the buddha within is precisely the revealing of the real; this, too, bears witness to what came before while also being on the verge of opening what is to follow. "As one draws back a bolt and key" means removing the obstruction that blocks the capacity of beings from stirring. From "with a great sound" onward is, second, Śākyamuni's calling for volunteers to seek out those who will transmit the sūtra, which itself has three parts: first, the great call for volunteers. From "the Tathāgata will not be here much longer" onward is, second, making clear that the time for entrustment has arrived. From "the Buddha wishes to" onward is, third, making clear that the entrustment has a destination. As for "having a destination": while the Buddha remains in the world, he benefits beings according to their capacities and teaches the true Dharma himself, without needing to rely on anyone else; but now that his conditions for transforming beings are exhausted, and he wishes this Dharma to benefit beings without limit, he must therefore entrust it for its continued transmission. As for "the entrustment having a destination," this carries two senses: first, giving it a near destination — entrusting it to the eighty thousand bodhisattvas and twenty thousand more who have long dwelt in this world, to proclaim it abroad in this land; second, giving it a distant destination — entrusting it to his own original disciples, as numerous as the motes of dust in a thousand worlds below, so that it may be transmitted everywhere they reach — and this, too, sets in motion the matter of his lifespan.

Reading note: This closing verse section marks the hinge of the whole chapter: everything before it has established, through the stūpa's rising and opening, that the teaching just given is true; everything from here forward turns to who will carry that teaching onward once the Buddha himself is gone. Zhiyi's own count of lines — three and a half here, eight and a half there, thirty-six in the last movement — is not pedantry for its own sake; it is a way of showing that the verses' own architecture already anticipates the chapters still to come, in which volunteers must in fact step forward to answer this call.

Devadatta is also called Devadhūta, which here translates as "heat in the heavens." He broke apart the saṃgha and led five hundred monks away; Śāriputra grew weary of it. While the breakaway monks slept soundly, Mahāmaudgalyāyana raised them up and led them back. When Devadatta awoke, he vowed to avenge this grudge: he lifted a stone thirty cubits long and fifteen cubits wide and hurled it at the Buddha; a mountain spirit's hand deflected it, but a splinter struck and wounded the Buddha's foot, drawing blood. He induced King Ajātaśatru to loose a drunken elephant upon the Buddha; he struck the nun Utpalavarṇā, and she died; and he hid poison beneath his ten fingernails, intending, as he bowed at the Buddha's feet, to wound him — these together make the five grave transgressions. Taken as only the three most serious transgressions, they are counted as inciting the king, the poisoned nails, and the wounding of the Buddha together. Because he was, by nature, bound to carry out such rebellious acts, at his very birth the hearts of gods and humans alike grew hot — and it is from this that his name is taken, called "heat in the heavens"; this is his trace. Taken according to the origin, however: because sentient beings suffer from affliction, the bodhisattva displays this very "heat," sharing in their sickness so as to lead them out of it. The reason this chapter comes here is to draw on the ancient example of one who transmitted the sūtra and thereby brought benefit, showing this to be no falsehood, and to make clear that the evidence of teaching and transformation in the present is likewise no empty claim — raising up the past so as to exhort the present, that the sūtra's transmission may continue.

Reading note: Zhiyi's own citation of the Baochang Catalogue is a small piece of textual history worth sitting with: the "Devadatta" chapter was, for a time, missing from the Lotus Sūtra as it circulated south of the Yangzi, kept back among the palace attendants at Chang'an, and only later restored to its familiar place through the independent judgment of two masters comparing manuscripts against Dharmarakṣa's earlier translation. Zhiyi's insistence that Devadatta's "trace" of villainy conceals an "origin" of cool, untroubled bodhisattvahood is the same trace-and-origin logic this commentary has applied to every disciple since fascicle one — but nowhere else is it pressed so hard, against so unlikely a candidate.

Ru-Yi Translation

From "he told the assembled monks: this king" onward is, second, tying together past and present, which itself has two parts: first, directly tying together past and present, as the text has it; from "because of Devadatta" onward is, second, making clear that the merit and its reward are, for both teacher and disciple, alike fulfilled. This fulfillment, again, has two parts: first, setting forth the disciple's own causal merit as already fulfilled; next, from "the Buddha told the fourfold assembly" onward is, second, setting forth the teacher's own wondrous fruit as soon to be accomplished. Within the disciple's own case there are, again, three divisions: first, setting forth the fulfillment of the cause; next, from "the thirty-two marks" onward, setting forth the perfection of the fruit; and afterward, from "entirely on account of Devadatta" onward, concluding with testimony to the benefit that comes from transmitting the sūtra. First, as to "complete with the six perfections": the sense of this "crossing over" is extremely rich. As the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra explains it: relinquishing both dependent and direct retribution is called generosity; guarding against the seven wrong branches of conduct is called moral discipline; enduring blows and abuse without retaliation is called forbearance; carrying a task through from beginning to end is called diligence; the four dhyānas and the eight meditative absorptions are called meditative concentration; and discerning each ground of practice so as to bring disputes to rest is called the perfection of wisdom. Or again, if the ten good deeds are gathered into six: from not killing through not speaking falsely is generosity; not speaking divisively is moral discipline; not speaking harshly is forbearance; not speaking idly is diligence; not being greedy or hateful is meditative concentration; and not holding wrong views is the perfection of wisdom. The Bodhisattva-śīla treatise, fascicle ten, says: "The six perfections are of three kinds. The first is the countering kind, countering stinginess, malice, hatred, sloth, distraction, and delusion, respectively, and so on. The second is the kind that generates one another in sequence: relinquishing one's household leads to upholding moral discipline, which, when insulted, calls for forbearance; having forborne, one applies diligence; having applied diligence, one brings the five faculties into balance; and once the faculties are balanced, one comes to know the dharma-realm. The third is the kind that bears fruit as reward: wealth, physical beauty, strength, longevity, peace, and eloquence." And another sūtra says: giving is rewarded with wealth, moral discipline with a good rebirth, forbearance with an upright appearance, diligence with spiritual powers, meditative concentration with rebirth in the heavens, and wisdom with the breaking of affliction. Examples such as these are, in every case, the Tripiṭaka teaching's own account of the marks of the six perfections.

若施受財物三事皆空名檀，不見持犯名戒，能忍所忍不可得名忍，身心不動名精進，不亂不味名禪，非智非愚名般若，如此流例即通教中六度相。

If the giver, the receiver, and the wealth given — all three — are seen as empty, this is called generosity; not perceiving any duality of keeping or breaking precepts is called moral discipline; the one who forbears and that which is forborne both being unobtainable is called forbearance; body and mind unmoved is called diligence; neither being scattered nor being caught in meditative flavor is called meditative concentration; and being neither wise nor foolish is called the perfection of wisdom. Examples of this kind are the shared teaching's own account of the marks of the six perfections.

若言檀有十利，伏慳煩惱，捨心相續，與眾生同資產，生豪富家，生生施心現前，四眾愛樂，處眾不怯畏，勝名遍布，手足柔軟，乃至詣道場，恒值善知識。戒有十利者，滿一切智如佛所學，智者不毀，誓願不退，安住於行，棄捨生死慕樂涅槃，得無纏心，得勝三昧，不乏信財。忍有十利者，火刀毒水皆不能害，非人所護身相莊嚴，閉惡道生梵天，晝夜常安，身不離喜樂。精進有十利者，他不能折伏，佛所攝，非人所護，聞法不忘，未聞能聞，增長辯才，得三昧性，少病惱，隨食能銷，如優鉢花增長。禪有十利者，安住儀式行慈境界，無悔熱，守護諸根，得無食喜，離愛欲修禪不空，解脫魔羂安住佛境，解脫成熟。般若有十利者，不取施相，不依戒，不住忍力，不離身心精進，禪無所住魔不能擾，他言論不能動，達生死底起增上慈，不樂二乘地。

Suppose it is said that generosity brings ten benefits: it subdues the affliction of stinginess; the mind of relinquishing continues on; one shares one's own wealth with sentient beings; one is born into a wealthy and noble family; life after life, the mind of giving stands manifest before one; the fourfold assembly loves and delights in one; one dwells among the assembly without fear or timidity; a superior reputation spreads far and wide; one's hands and feet become soft and supple; and, in the end, one arrives at the place of enlightenment, forever encountering good spiritual friends. As for moral discipline bringing ten benefits: one becomes complete in all wisdom, just as the Buddha himself learned; the wise do not slander one; one's vows do not regress; one abides securely in one's own conduct; one relinquishes birth-and-death and longs for the joy of nirvāṇa; one attains a mind free of entanglement; one attains a surpassing samādhi; and one is never lacking in the wealth of faith. As for forbearance bringing ten benefits: fire, blade, poison, and water are none of them able to harm one; one is protected by non-human beings; one's bodily form is adorned; one is closed off from the evil destinies and born in the Brahmā heavens; one is at peace both day and night; and one's body never departs from joy and happiness. As for diligence bringing ten benefits: others are unable to subdue one; one is gathered in by the Buddha; one is protected by non-human beings; what one has heard of the Dharma is never forgotten; what one has not yet heard, one comes to hear; one's eloquence continually increases; one attains the nature of samādhi; one suffers little illness or affliction; whatever food one takes is easily digested; and one flourishes like the utpala flower. As for meditative concentration bringing ten benefits: one abides securely within the proper forms of conduct, practicing within the domain of loving-kindness; one is free of regret and vexation; one guards and protects the faculties; one attains the joy of needing no food; freed from craving and desire, one's cultivation of meditation is never empty; one is freed from Māra's snare and abides securely within the buddhas' own domain; and liberation itself comes to full ripeness. As for the perfection of wisdom bringing ten benefits: one does not grasp at any mark of giving; one does not rely upon moral discipline as a mark; one does not dwell in the power of forbearance as a mark; one is inseparable from bodily and mental diligence; meditative concentration abides nowhere, and Māra is unable to disturb it; the arguments of others are unable to move one; one penetrates to the very depths of birth-and-death and gives rise to an ever-increasing loving-kindness; and one no longer delights in the ground of the two lesser vehicles.

四事應修檀：一、修道者破慳貪故；二、莊嚴菩提故；三、自他利益，欲施施時施已皆歡喜名自利，飢渴者得除是名利他；四、得後世大善果，後世獲大尊貴饒財。四事應持戒：自修善法滅惡，戒莊嚴菩提攝眾生，臥覺安、不悔恨、於眾生無害心，後受人天得涅槃等樂。四事應修忍：修忍除不忍，莊嚴菩提攝眾生，彼此離怖畏、後世無瞋、眷屬不壞、不受苦惱，得人天涅槃樂。四事應須修精進：進破懈怠，莊嚴菩提攝眾生，增善法是自利、不惱他是利他，後得大力致菩提。四事應修禪定：定破亂心，莊嚴菩提攝眾生，身心寂靜是自利、不惱眾生是利他，後受清淨身、安隱得涅槃。四事應修般若：智慧破無明，莊嚴菩提攝眾生，智慧自樂是自利、能教眾生是利他，能壞煩惱及智障等是大果。如此流例，是別教明六度相也。

There are four grounds on which generosity ought to be cultivated: first, because the one who cultivates the path thereby breaks through stinginess and greed; second, because it adorns the path to awakening; third, for the benefit of both oneself and others — for in wishing to give, in the act of giving, and once the giving is done, one is filled with joy, and this is called benefiting oneself, while removing hunger and thirst from those in need is called benefiting others; and fourth, because it obtains, in future lives, a great and excellent fruit, so that in future lives one attains great honor and abundant wealth. There are four grounds on which moral discipline ought to be upheld: it cultivates good dharmas and extinguishes evil in oneself; discipline adorns the path to awakening and gathers in sentient beings; one is at peace whether lying down or waking, free of remorse, and holds no thought of harm toward any being; and afterward one receives, among gods and humans, the equal joy of nirvāṇa. There are four grounds on which forbearance ought to be cultivated: cultivating forbearance removes the failure to forbear; it adorns the path to awakening and gathers in sentient beings; both parties are freed from fear, one is free of anger in future lives, one's family and companions are not broken apart, and one does not suffer distress; and one attains, among gods and humans, the joy of nirvāṇa. There are four grounds on which diligence must be cultivated: diligence breaks through indolence; it adorns the path to awakening and gathers in sentient beings; increasing good dharmas is benefiting oneself, and not troubling others is benefiting others; and afterward one attains great strength and arrives at awakening. There are four grounds on which meditative concentration ought to be cultivated: concentration breaks through a scattered mind; it adorns the path to awakening and gathers in sentient beings; body and mind at peace and still is benefiting oneself, and not troubling sentient beings is benefiting others; and afterward one receives a pure body and attains nirvāṇa in security. There are four grounds on which the perfection of wisdom ought to be cultivated: wisdom breaks through ignorance; it adorns the path to awakening and gathers in sentient beings; wisdom's own joy is benefiting oneself, and being able to teach sentient beings is benefiting others; and being able to destroy affliction and the obstruction of the knowable is a great fruit indeed. Examples of this kind are the distinct teaching's own account of the marks of the six perfections.

《月藏》第一云「若眾生唯依讀誦求菩提，是人為著世俗，尚不調己煩惱何能調他？是人著嫉妬名利富貴，高心自是輕慢毀他，尚不得欲界善根，況色無色善根？況二乘菩提？況無上菩提？如星火不能乾海，口氣不能動山，藕絲不能稱岳。何者？世俗不能滿菩提。何者是第一義？謂造一切福事，若修身修心修慧，以第一義熏修，則速滿六波羅蜜：若行若坐捨攀緣想是檀，捨攀緣不犯是尸，於境界不生瘡疣是羼，不捨於離是精進，於事中不放逸是禪，於諸法體性無生是般若。復次於陰捨是檀，不計念陰是尸，於陰無我想是羼，於陰起怨想是進，於陰不熾然是禪，於陰畢竟棄是般若。於界捨是檀，於界不擾濁是尸，於界捨因緣是羼，於界數數捨是進，於界不起發是禪，於界如幻想是般若。如是等是名第一義諦善巧方便甚深法要，能滿六波羅蜜。」以此法自為、為他，三世菩薩悉修是法成菩提，故非世俗也。此法能息眾生煩惱道苦道，安置菩提道。華嚴七地方明念念具十波羅蜜修習一切佛法：以求佛道善根與一切眾生是檀，能滅一切煩惱熱是尸，於一切眾生無所傷是忍，求善無厭是進，修道心不散常向一切智是禪，忍諸法不生門是般若，能起無量智門是方便，求轉勝智是願，魔邪不能阻是力，於一切法相如實說是智，是十波羅蜜具故，四攝、道品、三解脫、一切助菩提法，於念念中皆具足，諸地皆念念具足，此地勝故。如此例是圓教六度相也。

The Candragarbha Sūtra, fascicle one, says: "If sentient beings rely only on reading and reciting in seeking awakening, such a person is thereby attached to the world's own conventions — unable even to bring his own afflictions into balance, how could he bring another's into balance? Such a person, attached to jealousy, fame, profit, and wealth, arrogant and self-satisfied, looking down on and slandering others, cannot attain even the roots of goodness proper to the desire realm — how much less those of the form and formless realms? How much less the awakening of the two lesser vehicles? How much less unsurpassed awakening? It is like a spark of fire unable to dry up the ocean, a breath of air unable to move a mountain, a strand of lotus fiber unable to weigh down a great peak. Why is this so? Because worldly convention is unable to bring awakening to fulfillment. What, then, is the meaning of ultimate truth? It means accomplishing every kind of blessed act — cultivating the body, cultivating the mind, cultivating wisdom — and, by being suffused with ultimate truth, one swiftly brings the six perfections to fulfillment: whether walking or sitting, relinquishing the thought that clings to conditions is generosity; relinquishing this clinging without transgression is moral discipline; not giving rise to any blemish with regard to one's circumstances is forbearance; not relinquishing this effort and not departing from it is diligence; within one's activity, not being negligent is meditative concentration; and seeing the nature of every dharma as non-arising is the perfection of wisdom. Again: relinquishing the aggregates is generosity; not fixating in thought upon the aggregates is moral discipline; the thought of no-self with regard to the aggregates is forbearance; giving rise to a sense of them as an enemy is diligence; not being inflamed by the aggregates is meditative concentration; and ultimately abandoning the aggregates is the perfection of wisdom. Relinquishing the elements is generosity; not being disturbed or clouded with regard to the elements is moral discipline; relinquishing the causes and conditions of the elements is forbearance; relinquishing them again and again is diligence; not stirring up attachment with regard to the elements is meditative concentration; and seeing the elements as illusion-like is the perfection of wisdom. Teachings such as these are called the profound essentials of skillful means according to the truth of ultimate meaning, able to bring the six perfections to fulfillment." By means of this Dharma, for one's own sake and for the sake of others, the bodhisattvas of the three periods of time all cultivate this very Dharma and accomplish awakening — and so it is not merely worldly convention. This Dharma is able to bring to rest sentient beings' own path of affliction and path of suffering, and to establish them upon the path of awakening. The Avataṃsaka, at the seventh ground, explains further how, thought after thought, the bodhisattva is complete with the ten pāramitās, cultivating and practicing the entire Dharma of the buddhas: taking the root of goodness that seeks the buddha-path and sharing it with every sentient being is generosity; being able to extinguish every heat of affliction is moral discipline; bringing no harm whatsoever to any sentient being is forbearance; seeking what is good without ever tiring is diligence; the mind of cultivating the path remaining undistracted, ever turned toward all-knowing wisdom, is meditative concentration; forbearing the gate of the non-arising of all dharmas is the perfection of wisdom; being able to give rise to the gate of measureless wisdom is skillful means; seeking ever more excellent wisdom is the vow; being unobstructed by demonic and deviant forces is power; and speaking of the mark of every dharma exactly as it truly is, is knowledge. Because the ten pāramitās are thus complete, the four means of gathering beings, the factors of the path, the three gates of liberation, and every dharma that assists awakening are, thought after thought, entirely complete — every ground possessing, thought after thought, this same completeness, since this ground surpasses the others. An example of this kind is the perfect teaching's own account of the marks of the six perfections.

Reading note: This is the fourfold-teaching method (三藏／通／別／圓) applied at unusual length: the same six perfections named identically in each of the four accounts, yet meaning something progressively less fixed each time — from literal renunciation of goods, to renunciation understood as already empty, to a calculus of virtues cultivated for four distinct reasons apiece, to a reading in which each perfection is already complete with all ten pāramitās in a single thought. Zhiyi's citations from the Candragarbha and the Avataṃsaka are not decoration; they are his evidence that this progression is not his own invention but is already latent across the wider canon.

As for how the present sūtra explains the armor: it takes mindfulness of the Buddha as the armor, which is mindfulness of the dharma-buddha, the ultimate meaning, since the Buddha himself is precisely the Dharma. The Peaceful Practices: Three Approaches to the Title 安樂行品題三釋

Chinese Source

釋安樂行品釋此品為三：依事、附文、法門。事者，身無危險故安，心無憂惱故樂，身安心樂故能進行；附文者，著如來衣則法身安，入如來室故解脫心樂，坐如來座故，般若導行進，此附上品文釋耳。住忍辱地故身安，而不卒暴故心樂，觀諸法實相故行進。又法門者，安名不動，樂名無受，行名無行。不動者六道生死，二聖涅槃所不能動，既不緣二邊則身無動搖。上文云「身體及手足，靜然安不動，其心常憺怕，未曾有散亂」，則安住不動如須彌頂，常住不動法門也。樂者不受三昧廣大之用，不受凡夫之五受，乃至圓教中五受生見亦皆不受，有受則有苦，無受則無苦，無苦無樂乃名大樂。無行者，若有所受即有所行，無受則無所行，不行凡夫行，不行賢聖行，故言無行，而行中道，是故名行，即法門也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Explaining the chapter "The Peaceful Practices." This chapter may be explained in three ways: according to the literal matter, according to the text of the preceding chapter, and according to the dharma-gate. As to the matter: the body free of danger is why it is called "peace," the mind free of anxiety is why it is called "ease," and the body at peace and the mind at ease are why one is able to advance in practice. As to the text: being clothed in the robe of the Tathāgata is the reason the dharma-body is at peace; entering the room of the Tathāgata is the reason liberation brings ease to the mind; and sitting in the seat of the Tathāgata is the reason the perfection of wisdom guides one's advance in practice — this simply follows the text of the preceding chapter in its explanation. Abiding in the ground of forbearance is why the body is at peace; not being hasty or violent is why the mind is at ease; and contemplating the true mark of all dharmas is why one advances in practice. As to the dharma-gate: "peace" is named "unmoving," "ease" is named "without receiving," and "practice" is named "without doing." "Unmoving" means that neither birth-and-death within the six destinies nor the nirvāṇa of the two lesser vehicles as sages is able to move it — since it clings to neither of these two extremes, the body has no agitation. The text above says, "the body and its very limbs, still, at peace, unmoving, the mind ever calm and settled, never once scattered" — this is abiding, unmoving, like the summit of Mount Sumeru, the dharma-gate of constant, unmoving abiding. "Ease" means not receiving the vast function even of samādhi itself, not receiving the five kinds of feeling proper to an ordinary being, and, further, not receiving even the arising of any view concerning the five kinds of feeling as found within the perfect teaching — for where there is receiving, there is suffering, and where there is no receiving, there is no suffering; and being free of both suffering and ordinary pleasure is what is called "great bliss." "Without doing" means that where there is something received, there is something done, and where there is no receiving, there is nothing done — not doing what an ordinary being does, and not doing what a sage does, is why it is called "without doing"; and yet, in walking the middle way, this is precisely why it is called "doing" — this, too, is the dharma-gate.

今更廣事解，夫安樂者即大涅槃，從果立名也；行者即涅槃道，從因得名也。諸餘因果俱苦，如常見外道行於苦行還得苦果；若因樂果苦，如斷見外道恣情取樂後得苦報；若因苦果樂，如析法二乘無常拙度加功苦至方入涅槃。今言安樂行者，因果俱樂，即是大品如實巧度。《大經》云「定苦行者謂諸凡夫，苦樂行者聲聞緣覺，定樂行者謂諸菩薩也。」絓七方便麁因麁果，皆非安樂行；獨此妙因妙果，稱安樂行也。

Now, explaining the matter still more broadly: "peace and ease" is precisely great nirvāṇa, its name given from the standpoint of the fruit; "practice" is precisely the path to nirvāṇa, its name obtained from the standpoint of the cause. In every other case, cause and fruit are both suffering — as when an eternalist outside the path practices painful austerities and, in the end, obtains only a painful fruit; or the cause is pleasure and the fruit suffering, as when an annihilationist outside the path indulges his every desire and, in the end, receives only a painful retribution; or the cause is suffering and the fruit pleasure, as when the two lesser vehicles, through the crude method of analysis and impermanence, apply painful effort until, at last, they enter nirvāṇa. Here, however, what is called the peaceful and blissful practice has cause and fruit both blissful — this is precisely the skillful, genuine method spoken of in the Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "Those fixed in painful practice are ordinary beings; those whose practice is sometimes painful, sometimes blissful, are śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas; those fixed in blissful practice are the bodhisattvas." All the crude causes and crude fruits belonging to the seven kinds of expedient practitioners are, none of them, this peaceful and blissful practice — only this wondrous cause and wondrous fruit deserve the name "the peaceful and blissful practice."

更廣依文釋者，安樂行是涅槃道，涅槃有三義，謂三德祕藏；行有三義，謂止行、觀行、慈悲行。止行者，三業柔和違從俱寂，即是體法身行，即上文如來衣也。觀行者，一實相慧無分別光，即體般若行，即上如來座也。慈悲行者，四弘誓願廣度一切，即體解脫行，即上文如來室也。總此三行為涅槃道，總於三德為行之境，境稱安樂、道稱為行。《大論》云「菩薩從初發心，常觀涅槃行道因時用此三行法，導三業為行，三業淨故即是淨於六根，六根若淨發相似解而得入真，果時名佛眼耳等。」因名止行，果名斷德；因名觀行，果名智德；因名慈悲，行果名恩德。又因名三業，果名三密；因時慈悲導三業利他，果時名三輪不思議化。

Explaining still more broadly according to the text of the sūtra: the peaceful and blissful practice is the path to nirvāṇa, and nirvāṇa carries three meanings — namely, the secret treasury of the three virtues; the practice, too, carries three meanings — namely, the practice of calming, the practice of contemplation, and the practice of compassion. As for the practice of calming: the three karmic activities made gentle and harmonious, and both what runs contrary and what accords brought equally to rest — this is precisely the embodiment of the practice of the dharma-body, precisely the "robe of the Tathāgata" spoken of above. As for the practice of contemplation: the wisdom of the one true mark of reality, its light free of discrimination — this is precisely the embodiment of the practice of the perfection of wisdom, precisely the "seat of the Tathāgata" spoken of above. As for the practice of compassion: the four great vows, broadly delivering every being — this is precisely the embodiment of the practice of liberation, precisely the "room of the Tathāgata" spoken of above. Taking these three practices together as the path to nirvāṇa, and the three virtues together as the domain of that practice — the domain is named "peace and ease," and the path is named "practice." The Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra says, "From the very first arousing of the mind, the bodhisattva constantly contemplates nirvāṇa; walking the path, in the time of cause, he makes use of these three practices, guiding his three karmic activities into practice — and because these three activities are purified, the six faculties, too, are thereby purified; and once the six faculties are purified, an understanding resembling true insight arises, and he thereby enters the truth — and in the time of the fruit, this is named the buddha-eye, the buddha-ear, and so on." In the time of cause it is named the practice of calming, and in the time of fruit it is named the virtue of severance; in the time of cause it is named the practice of contemplation, and in the time of fruit it is named the virtue of wisdom; in the time of cause it is named the practice of compassion, and in the time of fruit it is named the virtue of grace. Again, in the time of cause these are named the three karmic activities, and in the time of fruit they are named the three mysteries; in the time of cause, compassion guides the three activities for the benefit of others, and in the time of fruit this is named the inconceivable transformation of the three wheels.

如此觀時無復分別，一切諸法中悉有安樂性，一切眾生即大涅槃不可復滅，行於非道通達佛道，此即絕待明安樂行，此行與涅槃義合。

Contemplated in this way, there is no longer any discrimination at all: within every single dharma there is entirely the nature of peace and bliss, and every sentient being is itself great nirvāṇa, never again to be extinguished — walking the very path that is not the path, one penetrates through to the buddha-path itself. This is precisely the explanation of the peaceful and blissful practice through the absolute, beyond all relative comparison, and this practice accords, in its very meaning, with nirvāṇa itself.

彼云，復有一行是如來行，如來是人、安樂是法，如來是安樂人、安樂是如來法，總而言之，其義不異，別亦不異。此明寂滅忍法空座如來室，彼明金銀寶樹，寶樹即無漏寶林，無漏與空寂滅忍合，金沙大河直入西海，即一實慧與諸法空合，得道女人則無諂曲，此無緣大慈與如來室合，彼呼為無餘義，此呼為無上道。又五行義亦與衣座室意同也。

That Nirvāṇa Sūtra likewise says that there is, further, a single practice that is the Tathāgata's own practice: the Tathāgata is the person, and peace and bliss is the dharma; the Tathāgata is the person of peace and bliss, and peace and bliss is the Tathāgata's own dharma — taken altogether, their meaning is no different, and taken separately, it is likewise no different. Here, the forbearance of extinction, the dharma of emptiness, and the seat are spoken of together with the room of the Tathāgata; there, in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, trees of gold and silver are spoken of — these jeweled trees are precisely the undefiled, jeweled grove, and the undefiled corresponds to emptiness and the forbearance of extinction; the great river of golden sand flowing directly into the western sea is precisely the one true wisdom corresponding to the emptiness of all dharmas; and the woman who attains the path, free of all flattery and crookedness, corresponds to the great compassion that clings to nothing, and to the room of the Tathāgata. What that sūtra calls "the meaning of the remainderless," this sūtra calls "the unsurpassed path." The sense of the five practices, too, accords in just the same way with the robe, the seat, and the room.

Reconciling with the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's Rebuke 大經折伏義釋疑

Chinese Source

問：《大經》明親附國王持弓帶箭摧伏惡人，此經遠離豪勢謙下慈善，剛柔碩乖云何不異？

答：《大經》偏論折伏，住一子地，何曾無攝受？此經偏明攝受，頭破七分，非無折伏。各舉一端適時而已，理必具四。何者？適時稱宜即世界意？攝受即為人意，折伏即對治意，悟道即第一義意也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: The Nirvāṇa Sūtra describes drawing close to kings, bearing bow and arrow, and subduing wicked men by force; this sūtra, by contrast, speaks of keeping far from the powerful, of humility, and of loving-kindness — one is hard, the other soft, utterly at odds — how, then, can they be said to be no different?

Answer: The Nirvāṇa Sūtra emphasizes subjugation, yet, in the same passage, abides in the ground of regarding all beings as an only child — when has it ever been without a gathering, sheltering compassion? This sūtra emphasizes gathering and sheltering, yet also speaks of a head split into seven pieces — it, too, is not without subjugation. Each simply raises up one aspect suited to its own occasion, but the underlying principle must, of necessity, include all four siddhāntas. Why is this so? Being fitted and suited to the occasion is precisely the sense of the mundane siddhānta; gathering and sheltering is the sense of the individual siddhānta; subjugation is the sense of the therapeutic siddhānta; and awakening to the path is the sense of the siddhānta of ultimate meaning.

Why This Chapter Comes Here 此品來意

Chinese Source

廣法門釋者，應明不動門、不受門、不行門，略不記也。此品是迹門流通第四意，若二萬八十億那由他受命弘經，深識權實廣知漸頓，又達機緣、神力自在，濁世惱亂不障通經，不俟更示方法；若初依始心，欲修圓行入濁弘經，為濁所惱，自行不立亦無化功，為是人故須示方法明安樂行，故有此品來也。此安樂行有何次第？然法華圓行，一行無量行，不可思議，何定前後？今且一緒，〈法師品〉略示弘經，則以益他為本，先明入室，此中辨惡世弘經安諸逼惱，先著如來衣，前後互現耳。若約行次者，諸法從本已來，常自寂滅相，若乖寂起相，應先以般若蕩累，則初坐座；諸法不生而般若生，同體慈悲愍眾故行道，次入如來室；既以慈悲化世必涉違從，決須安忍，次著如來衣。雖作此次，說非行時，行時入空即具一切法，況慈忍耶。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the broader explanation through the dharma-gate: one should make clear the gate of unmoving, the gate of not-receiving, and the gate of not-doing — but this is abbreviated here and not set down in full. This chapter is the fourth sense in which the trace gate flows forth in transmission. If the twenty thousand and the eighty koṭis of nayutas of bodhisattvas, having received the command to proclaim the sūtra, deeply understand the provisional and the real, broadly know both the gradual and the sudden, and, further, have mastered the right conditions and the sovereign freedom of spiritual power, then the disturbances of a corrupt age present no obstacle to their transmitting the sūtra, and there is no need to show them any further method. But if one of initial resolve and early practice, wishing to cultivate the perfect practice and proclaim the sūtra amid corruption, is troubled by that very corruption, unable to establish his own practice and so accomplishing no work of transformation — for the sake of such a person, a method must be shown, making clear the peaceful and blissful practice, and this is why this chapter comes here. What order, then, does this peaceful and blissful practice follow? Yet the Lotus's own perfect practice is one practice that is measureless practice, inconceivable — how could any fixed order of before and after be established for it? Here, however, taking up one single thread: the "Teacher of the Dharma" chapter briefly showed the proclaiming of the sūtra, taking the benefiting of others as its root, and first made clear the entering of the room; and here, in discussing how one proclaims the sūtra amid an evil age, secure against every affliction, it first speaks of being clothed in the robe of the Tathāgata — the two orders simply appear interchangeably. If one takes up the actual order of the practice: all dharmas, from the very beginning, are, of themselves, marked by quiescent extinction — but should one depart from this stillness and give rise to any distinguishing mark, one should first use the perfection of wisdom to sweep such entanglements away, and this is why the seat is sat in first; dharmas do not arise, and yet the perfection of wisdom itself arises, and, sharing the very same body as compassion, one pities beings and so walks the path, and this is why one next enters the room of the Tathāgata; and since it is by compassion that one transforms the world, one must inevitably encounter both what accords and what runs contrary, and must therefore, of necessity, be secure in forbearance, and this is why one next puts on the robe of the Tathāgata. Though this order is given here, when one actually speaks it is not yet the time of practice — and at the time of practice, entering emptiness is already to be complete with every dharma at once, how much more compassion and forbearance?

Four Historical Schemes for the Four Practices 四安樂行諸師異解

Chinese Source

四安樂行者，舊云：一、假實二空為體，二、說法為體，三、離過為體，四、慈悲為體。基師云：一、空，二、離憍慢，三、除嫉妬，四、大慈悲。龍師云：一、身遠諸惡漸近空理，二、除口過，三、除意嫉，四、起慈悲。南岳師云：一、無著正慧，二、口不說過，三、敬上接下，四、大慈悲。天台師云：止觀慈悲，導三業及誓願，身業有止故離身麁業，有觀故不得身，不得身業不得能離，無所得故不墮凡夫；有慈悲故勤修身業廣利一切，不墮二乘地，有止行故著忍辱衣，有觀行故坐如來座；有慈悲故入如來室，止行離過即成斷德，觀行無著即成智德，慈悲利他即成恩德，恩德資成智德，智德能通達斷德，是名身業安樂行。餘口意誓願亦如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the four peaceful practices: the old masters said: first, the emptiness of both the provisional and the real as their essence; second, teaching the Dharma as their essence; third, being free of transgression as their essence; fourth, compassion as their essence. Master Ji says: first, emptiness; second, being free of arrogance; third, removing jealousy; fourth, great compassion. Elder Long of Mount Lu says: first, the body kept far from every evil, drawing gradually near to the principle of emptiness; second, removing faults of speech; third, removing the jealousy of the mind; fourth, arousing compassion. Master Nanyue says: first, unattached, true wisdom; second, the mouth speaking no fault; third, reverence for those above and consideration for those below; fourth, great compassion. The Tiantai Master says: calming, contemplation, and compassion guide the three karmic activities and the vow. Bodily action has calming, and so it is free of the coarse activity of the body; it has contemplation, and so does not grasp the body as real, and, not grasping bodily action as real, is not merely able to be free of it — and, having nothing that is grasped, it does not fall into the state of an ordinary being. Having compassion, it diligently cultivates bodily action to benefit all broadly, and does not fall into the ground of the two lesser vehicles. Having the practice of calming, one is clothed in the robe of forbearance; having the practice of contemplation, one sits in the seat of the Tathāgata; having compassion, one enters the room of the Tathāgata. Calming's freedom from fault accomplishes precisely the virtue of severance; contemplation's freedom from attachment accomplishes precisely the virtue of wisdom; compassion's benefiting of others accomplishes precisely the virtue of grace — the virtue of grace nourishes and brings to completion the virtue of wisdom, and the virtue of wisdom is able to penetrate through to the virtue of severance. This is named the peaceful and blissful practice of bodily action; the remaining practices of speech, mind, and vow are likewise so.

The Chapter's Structure: Question and Answer 品文問答

Chinese Source

品文有問、有答。問中先歎前品深行菩薩能如此弘經，後問淺行菩薩云何惡世宣說是經。「佛告」下，第二，答，中有三：一、標四行章行，二、解釋修行方法，三、總明行成之相。初標章如文。

Ru-Yi Translation

The chapter's text contains both a question and an answer. Within the question, Mañjuśrī first praises the bodhisattvas of deep practice spoken of in the preceding chapter, who are able to proclaim the sūtra in this way, and afterward asks how a bodhisattva of shallow practice ought to expound this sūtra amid an evil age. From "the Buddha told" onward is, second, the answer, which itself has three parts: first, setting forth the section on the four practices; second, explaining the method of cultivating this practice; third, generally making clear the mark of the practice's accomplishment. The first, setting forth the section, may be understood from the text itself.

The Practice-Ground and the Approach-Ground 行處與近處

Chinese Source

「一者安住」下，第二，解釋方法，即為四別。初文又二：一、釋方法，二、結行成。修行有長行、偈頌。長行又二，初「一者」下，標行近；次，「文殊」下，釋行近。初、雙標，如文。次，釋，中又二：謂行處、近處。或云內凡初行名行處，若久習純熟，漸能近理名近處，引前品近果之行為例也。或云行處約因、近處約果，行處明智、近處明境。瑤師云：「七住已上心體於理為行處，已過分段也，自此已還無生未能現前，漸近於理為近處。同是分段，此二是行始，通為一安樂行也。」私謂初家以行為淺，若《大經》云「十地菩薩以行故見不了了」，當知行則不淺。後家以近為淺，若《淨名》云「近無等等佛自在慧」，此則近更成深。若兩行俱深，則成前品菩薩弘經之行，不關初心方法；若兩行俱淺，即七方便人所行，何關圓行方法？故不可偏據深淺。然行名進趣，近名親習，親習故進趣，進趣故親習，復何淺深？又行近是上方法，行處是如來衣，近處是如來座，座衣既不淺深，行近何得優劣？又忍辱必內懷至理歷緣耐事，目之為行；空座必體達外緣棲息真境，目之為近。蓋事理互現，復何淺深？若爾，何故分行近？詣理略說名行處，附事廣說名近處，說有廣略、理無淺深。

Ru-Yi Translation

From "first, abiding securely" onward is, second, explaining the method, which is itself divided into four. The first part, again, has two divisions: first, explaining the method; second, concluding with the accomplishment of the practice. The cultivation of the practice has both a prose section and a verse section. The prose, again, has two parts: the first, from "first" onward, sets forth the practice-ground and the approach-ground together; the next, from "Mañjuśrī" onward, explains the practice-ground and the approach-ground in turn. The first, the joint setting-forth, may be understood from the text itself. Next, the explanation, again, has two parts: namely, the practice-ground and the approach-ground. Some say that the initial practice of an ordinary being still within the seven expedient stages is named the practice-ground, and that, once long habituated and matured, gradually able to draw near to the principle, this is named the approach-ground — citing, as an example, the practice that draws near to the fruit spoken of in the preceding chapter. Others say that the practice-ground concerns the cause and the approach-ground concerns the fruit, that the practice-ground makes clear wisdom and the approach-ground makes clear its object. Master Yao of Jiangdong says: "From the seventh abode upward, the substance of mind, with regard to principle, is the practice-ground, having already passed beyond the stage of segmented birth-and-death; from this point on, the truth of non-arising has not yet become fully manifest, and drawing gradually near to principle is the approach-ground. Since both remain within segmented birth-and-death, these two together are only the beginning of the practice, and, taken together, form a single peaceful and blissful practice." My own view is this: the first school takes "practice" as the shallower of the two — yet the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "Bodhisattvas of the ten grounds still see the truth, through practice, imperfectly" — from which it should be known that "practice" is, in fact, not shallow at all. The second school takes "approach" as the shallower — yet the Vimalakīrti Sūtra says, "drawing near to the peerless, unequaled wisdom of the buddhas, sovereign and free" — by which "approach," rather, becomes something deeper still. If both practices are taken as equally deep, then this becomes the practice of proclaiming the sūtra proper to the bodhisattva of the preceding chapter, and has nothing to do with the method for one of initial resolve; and if both are taken as equally shallow, then this is simply what the seven kinds of expedient practitioners already practice, and what, then, does it have to do with the method of the perfect practice? For this reason, one cannot lean one-sidedly on either "deep" or "shallow" to distinguish them. And in fact, "practice" means advancing forward, while "approach" means becoming closely habituated — becoming closely habituated is precisely what allows one to advance forward, and advancing forward is precisely what allows one to become closely habituated — so how, then, could there be any difference of depth between them? Again, "practice" and "approach" together are the method spoken of above in the sūtra; the practice-ground is the robe of the Tathāgata, and the approach-ground is the seat of the Tathāgata — and since the seat and the robe carry no difference of depth, how could "practice" and "approach" admit any difference of superiority between them? Again, forbearance necessarily means holding fast, inwardly, to the ultimate principle, enduring circumstance after circumstance and bearing every matter through — this is what is meant by "practice"; the empty seat necessarily means embodying and penetrating outward conditions, dwelling securely within the true domain — this is what is meant by "approach." Since phenomenal conduct and principle manifest each other in turn in this way, how, again, could there be a difference of depth? If this is so, then why divide "practice" from "approach" at all? It is only that, reaching directly for principle and speaking of it briefly, this is named the practice-ground, while attaching to the phenomenal matter and speaking of it at length, this is named the approach-ground — the manner of speaking of it may be brief or extended, but principle itself admits no difference of depth.

The Practice-Ground Through a Single Dharma 一法釋行處

Chinese Source

今約三法明行處。一、直緣一諦，一諦為一切所歸，為一切作本，而遍無分別，一切所歸者，即忍辱地也。地即中道，諸法歸之，故名為地。眾行休息，故名忍辱。此即行不行之行也。為一切作本者，如萬物得地而生，眾行得理而成，若得理本，在剛能柔、在逆能順，在暴能治、在驚能安，無量功德從中道地生，地無所生而生功德，即不行行之行。遍無分別者，則不分別不行與行差別之相，故云又復不行不分別等也，即是非行非不行。無三行而三行，故名為行；同一實諦，故名為處。如此行處合上經文，休息眾行合如來衣，隨生功德合如來室，遍無分別合如來座，是名一法釋行處，是弘經方軌也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, in terms of three successive approaches to a single dharma, the practice-ground may be explained. First: directly taking hold of the one truth — the one truth to which all things return, and which serves as the very root of all things, while remaining, throughout, free of discrimination. "That to which all things return" is precisely the ground of forbearance: the ground is the middle way, to which every dharma returns, and this is why it is called "the ground"; and every practice comes to rest within it, and this is why it is called "forbearance." This, then, is the practice that is the practice of not practicing. "That which serves as the root of all things" means that, just as the myriad things obtain the earth and thereby are born, so every practice obtains this principle and thereby is accomplished — and once one has obtained this root, this principle, then in what is rigid one is able to be gentle, in what is contrary one is able to accord, in what is violent one is able to bring order, and in what is alarming one is able to bring peace: measureless merit is born from the ground of the middle way, and the ground, though it gives birth to nothing at all, gives birth to this very merit — this, then, is the practice of not-practicing that is nonetheless practice. "Remaining, throughout, free of discrimination" means not discriminating between the mark of not-practicing and that of practicing — hence it says, further, "not practicing and not discriminating," and so on — this is precisely neither practice nor non-practice. Having no three practices, and yet being three practices, is why it is named "practice"; being one and the same true truth is why it is named "the ground." This practice-ground, matched to the sūtra text given above, corresponds: the resting of every practice corresponds to the robe of the Tathāgata; merit born as a consequence corresponds to the room of the Tathāgata; and being throughout free of discrimination corresponds to the seat of the Tathāgata — this is what is meant by explaining the practice-ground through a single dharma, and it is the very compass and bearing by which the sūtra is transmitted.

Through Two Dharmas: The Two Forbearances 二法釋行處

Chinese Source

二、約二法，即生、法二忍，二忍即生、法二空。二空異二乘。何者？人、法二空約真俗假實，明二空二忍悉見中道，故不同二乘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, in terms of two dharmas: namely, the forbearance concerning sentient beings and the forbearance concerning dharmas, which are, in turn, precisely the two emptinesses of self and of dharmas. These two emptinesses differ from those of the two lesser vehicles. Why is this? The two emptinesses of person and of dharma, measured against the true, the conventional, and the provisional and the real, make clear that both the two emptinesses and the two forbearances alike see the middle way — and this is why they are not the same as those of the two lesser vehicles.

若更開者即四忍，若作五忍指善字為信忍，若作六忍指和字為和從忍；若對地即開四十二忍，一地尚有四十一地功德，一忍寧無四十一忍法耶？今且約四忍消文，謂伏、順、無生、寂滅忍也。此四忍與別教異，彼前二忍是生忍，位則淺；後二忍是法忍，位則深。今圓生法悉通，四忍亦通。何者？二空理即是中道，初住修四忍入中見二空理，乃至後心亦窮二空理。《大經》云「發心畢竟二不別」。若約無淺深判四忍者，從初發心圓伏五住，至金剛頂皆名伏忍；初後悉不違實相，名順忍；初後悉不起二邊心，名無生忍；初後悉休息眾行，名寂滅忍。聞生死不怱卒畏苦，聞涅槃不怱卒證樂，聞佛常與無常、二乘作佛不作佛，聞生死涅槃異與不異，聞佛道長短難易非長非短非難非易等皆不驚怖，行此行者，從始至終以二空理忍諸法，即著如來衣；安住二空理，即坐如來座；愍諸眾生，即入如來室。二空四忍名為行，理即是處，是名約二法明行處為弘經方軌也。

Opened further still, these become the four forbearances; made into five forbearances, the character "good" is taken to name the forbearance of faith; made into six, the character "harmony" is taken to name the forbearance of harmonious accord. Matched against the bodhisattva grounds, these open out into forty-two forbearances — for if a single ground already possesses the merit of the other forty-one grounds, how could a single forbearance lack the dharma of the other forty-one forbearances? Here, however, we simply explain the text in terms of the four forbearances: namely, the forbearance of subduing, of according, of non-arising, and of extinction. These four forbearances differ from those of the distinct teaching: there, the first two forbearances are the forbearance concerning sentient beings, and their stage is shallow, while the latter two are the forbearance concerning dharmas, and their stage is deep. Here, in the perfect teaching, sentient-being and dharma are both, throughout, interpenetrating, and the four forbearances, too, are throughout interpenetrating. Why is this? The principle of the two emptinesses is itself the middle way — from the very first abode, cultivating the four forbearances, one enters the middle and sees the principle of the two emptinesses, and, all the way to the very last stage of mind, one still fully fathoms this same principle of the two emptinesses. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "Between the arousing of the mind and its ultimate completion, the two are not different." If the four forbearances are judged without regard to depth or shallowness: from the very first arousing of the mind, perfectly subduing the five fundamental afflictions all the way up to the very summit of the vajra stage — all of this is named the forbearance of subduing; from beginning to end never contrary to the true mark of reality is named the forbearance of according; from beginning to end never giving rise to a mind fixed on either of the two extremes is named the forbearance of non-arising; and from beginning to end bringing every practice to rest is named the forbearance of extinction. Hearing of birth-and-death, one is not hastily overcome with dread of suffering; hearing of nirvāṇa, one does not hastily rush to realize its bliss; hearing that the Buddha is both eternal and impermanent, that the two lesser vehicles both become buddhas and do not become buddhas, that birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are both different and not different, that the buddha-path is both long and short, both difficult and easy, and neither long nor short, neither difficult nor easy — hearing all such things, one is, in every case, undismayed. One who practices this practice, from beginning to end, forbears every dharma by means of the principle of the two emptinesses, and this is precisely being clothed in the robe of the Tathāgata; abiding securely in the principle of the two emptinesses is precisely sitting in the seat of the Tathāgata; and pitying every sentient being is precisely entering the room of the Tathāgata. The two emptinesses and the four forbearances are named "the practice," and the principle itself is precisely "the ground" — this is what is meant by explaining the practice-ground in terms of two dharmas, and it, too, is the compass and bearing by which the sūtra is transmitted.

Through Three Dharmas: The Three Truths 三法釋行處

Chinese Source

三、約三法，三法即不思議三諦也。「住忍辱地」總論三諦，如有地可據，方能忍辱也；「柔和善順」者，善順真諦，能忍虛妄見愛寒熱等，故言善順也。「而不卒暴心不驚」者，安於俗諦，忍眾根緣稱適機宜，故云不卒暴，體忍違從故心不驚也。「於法無所行」等者，即安中諦，能忍二邊，故云無所行。正住中道故云「觀實相」，亦不得中實，故云「不分別」。此則據三諦之地名處，忍五住之辱名行。行亦為三，謂止行即行不行，觀行即非行非不行，慈悲行即不行行，合上衣座舍等，是為約三法明行處，辯弘經方軌也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, in terms of three dharmas: the three dharmas are precisely the inconceivable three truths. "Abiding in the ground of forbearance" speaks of the three truths as a whole, as though there were a ground on which one may rely, and only then is one able to forbear. "Gentle, harmonious, and skillfully according" means according skillfully with the truth of the ultimate, able to forbear the false views and cravings, the cold and the heat, and the rest — and this is why it says "skillfully according." "Not being hasty or violent, the mind unafraid" means being secure within the truth of the conventional, forbearing every root and condition of sentient beings and according with what fits their capacity — and this is why it says "not hasty or violent"; embodying forbearance toward both what accords and what runs contrary is why "the mind is unafraid." "With regard to dharmas, having nothing whatsoever to do," and the rest, means being secure within the truth of the middle, able to forbear both extremes, and this is why it says "having nothing to do." Abiding rightly in the middle way is why it says "contemplating the true mark of reality"; and yet not grasping even this middle truth is why it says "without discriminating." This, then, takes the ground of the three truths as "the ground," and forbearing the disgrace that comes from the five fundamental afflictions as "the practice." The practice, too, is threefold: the practice of calming is precisely the practice that is not practicing; the practice of contemplation is precisely neither practice nor non-practice; and the practice of compassion is precisely the not-practicing that is practice — matching, above, the robe, the seat, and the room, and so on. This is what is meant by explaining the practice-ground in terms of three dharmas, discerning thereby the compass and bearing by which the sūtra is transmitted.

龍師云：「住忍辱地總舉生、法二忍，下別明二忍，『柔和善順』明身業也，『而不卒暴』是口業，『心亦不驚』是意業。此就三業明修生忍。『於諸法無所行』，不行有相也；『而觀如實相』，行空平等也；『亦不行不分別』，不行無相也，有無兩亡會於中道，此三句明修法忍。得是二忍，結為行處。」彼明二忍，未知約何？

若三教二忍，全非《法華》之義；若約圓教，不應隔別不融(云云)。

Elder Long of Mount Lu says: "Abiding in the ground of forbearance raises up, as a whole, the two forbearances concerning sentient beings and concerning dharmas; what follows explains these two forbearances separately: 'gentle and skillfully according' makes clear bodily action, 'not hasty or violent' is verbal action, and 'the mind, too, unafraid' is mental action — this, in terms of the three karmic activities, makes clear the cultivating of the forbearance concerning sentient beings. 'With regard to every dharma, having nothing to do' is not doing what has marks; 'and contemplating things as their true mark' is practicing the equality of emptiness; 'nor doing, nor discriminating' is not doing what is markless — both being and non-being alike set aside, meeting in the middle way: these three phrases make clear the cultivating of the forbearance concerning dharmas. Attaining these two forbearances is concluded as the practice-ground." It is not clear, however, in terms of what teaching he explains these two forbearances.

If taken according to the two forbearances of the three lesser teachings, this is entirely contrary to the meaning of the Lotus; and if taken according to the perfect teaching, it ought not to be set apart, unfused with the rest — and so on.

The Approach-Ground: What Must Be Kept at a Distance 近處十種應遠

Chinese Source

「云何名近」下，第二，近處。文為三：遠十惱亂，即遠故論近，亦是附戒門助觀：修攝其心，即近故論近，亦是附定門助觀也；觀一切法空，即非遠非近論近，亦是附慧門助觀。上直緣理住忍辱地，今戒門廣出眾辱之緣應修遠離，非持刀仗亦不棄捨，但以正慧而遠離之，當知遠近廣上行所不行也。上直明不暴驚，今定門廣出修定心、修定處、修定要門，以定力故，在暴而治、在驚而安，當知即近論近，廣上不行行也。上直明無所行，今廣觀一切空具歷諸境，無量無邊無礙無障，當知非遠非近，廣上非行非不行。就初有十種應遠者：一、豪勢，二、邪人法，三、兇險戲，四、旃陀羅，五、二乘眾，六、遠欲想，七、遠不男，八、遠危害，九、遠譏嫌，十、遠畜養等。「路伽耶」，此云惡論，亦云破論。「逆路」者，逆君父之論。又「路」名為善論，亦名師破弟子；「逆路」名惡論，亦名弟子破師。「那羅延」者，上伎戲，亦云綵畫，其身作變異，又云緣幢擲倒之屬也。分十種為二邊：九是生死，一是涅槃，二俱遠離，即寂滅之異名耳。觀心釋十種云云，近近處有三意(云云)。◎

Ru-Yi Translation

From "what is meant by 'approach'" onward is, second, the approach-ground. The text has three parts: keeping far from the ten disturbances is "distance" spoken of as "approach" — this, too, is a matter of assisting contemplation by way of the gate of moral discipline; cultivating and gathering in one's own mind is "nearness" spoken of as "approach" — this, too, is a matter of assisting contemplation by way of the gate of concentration; and contemplating the emptiness of every dharma is "neither distance nor nearness" spoken of as "approach" — this, too, is a matter of assisting contemplation by way of the gate of wisdom. Above, the practice-ground directly took hold of principle, abiding in the ground of forbearance; here, the gate of moral discipline broadly sets forth the conditions of every disgrace, teaching that one ought to cultivate keeping one's distance from them — not by taking up sword or staff, nor yet by abandoning them outright, but by keeping one's distance from them through right wisdom alone — and one should know that this "distance" broadly extends what the practice above did not practice. Above, the practice-ground simply made clear not being hasty or alarmed; here, the gate of concentration broadly sets forth the cultivating of a settled mind, a settled place, and the essential gateways of settled concentration — by the power of concentration, one brings order to what is violent and peace to what is alarming — and one should know that this is precisely "nearness" spoken of as "approach," broadly extending the not-doing-that-is-doing spoken of above. Above, the practice-ground simply made clear having nothing to do; here, one broadly contemplates the emptiness of all things, passing through every circumstance without measure, without bound, without hindrance, and without obstruction — and one should know that this is precisely "neither distance nor nearness," broadly extending the neither-doing-nor-not-doing spoken of above. Within the first part there are ten things one ought to keep at a distance: first, the powerful and mighty; second, persons and teachings that are deviant; third, dangerous and violent games and shows; fourth, caṇḍālas, the outcastes; fifth, the company of the two lesser vehicles; sixth, thoughts of desire, to be kept at a distance; seventh, eunuchs, to be kept at a distance; eighth, danger and harm, to be kept at a distance; ninth, occasion for mockery, to be kept at a distance; and tenth, the raising of animals and the like, to be kept at a distance. "Lokāyata" here means "evil discourse," also called "discourse that overturns." As for "contrary discourse": this means discourse contrary to one's sovereign or father. Again, "discourse" is called "good discourse," also named a teacher's refuting his own disciple; "contrary discourse" is named "evil discourse," also named a disciple's refuting his own teacher. As for "Nārāyaṇa": this refers to superior forms of entertainment, also called painting and coloring, changing and transforming one's own body, or, again, climbing poles and turning somersaults, and things of that kind. Dividing these ten into two extremes: nine belong to birth-and-death, and one to nirvāṇa — and keeping equally far from both is simply another name for quiescent extinction. The interpretation of these ten through the contemplation of mind, and so on — the approach and the approach-ground carry, in all, three senses, and so on.

Reading note: This ten-item list of what a teacher of the sūtra should keep at a distance — the powerful, deviant teachings, dangerous entertainments, and the rest — reads, on its surface, as a plain code of conduct. Zhiyi's own closing move, however, is to insist that even this literal list resolves into the same threefold structure as everything else in the chapter: distance corresponds to moral discipline, nearness to concentration, and the "neither distance nor nearness" of contemplating emptiness to wisdom — so that a list of worldly precautions turns out, on inspection, to be the same three practices of robe, room, and seat carried down into the texture of daily conduct. This is where fascicle eight closes and fascicle nine takes up the "neither distance nor nearness" gate in full.
