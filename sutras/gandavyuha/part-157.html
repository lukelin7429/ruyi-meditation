The Girl's Own Verse, First Five Stanzas

Having heard the deva's words, the girl approaches the prince herself, standing with palms joined, and answers him in verse — describing her own unequaled beauty and wisdom, a mind already free of anger, greed, and delusion, and, turning outward, praising the prince's own body and its auspicious marks.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 157 of 454 complete Source: Taishō T278, 入法界品第三十四之十三 (Buddhabhadra, Eastern Jin) Reading notes after Fazang's 華嚴經探玄記 (T1733)

Five Stanzas: Her Own Beauty, Her Own Purity

Chinese Source

「時，彼女人聞是語已，詣太子所，合掌而立，以偈白言：

「『我色世間最，　智慧無倫匹，
才妙善言論，　觀者無厭足。

太子應當知，　我心善貞潔，
志尚心端直，　清淨無所染。

遠離於瞋恚，　貪欲及愚癡，
以真淨直心，　饒益諸群生。

我見太子身，　相好自莊嚴，
見已喜無量，　諸根悉調伏。

妙體猶淨金，　髮美紺青色，
額廣目明徹，　必為自在王。』」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, that girl, having heard these words, went to where the prince was, and, standing with palms joined, spoke to him in verse, saying:

"My own form is supreme in the world, my wisdom without equal or peer; my talent is sublime, skilled in discourse, and those who behold me never grow weary of it.

The prince ought to know: my mind is well and truly chaste; my aspiration is upright and my mind straightforward, pure and untainted by anything.

Departing far from anger and hatred, greed and desire, and folly and delusion, with a true, pure, upright mind, I bring benefit to the sprouting multitudes.

Beholding the prince's own body, adorned of itself with every auspicious mark, having beheld it I rejoice without measure; every one of my own faculties is subdued and tamed.

Your sublime body is like pure gold, your hair beautiful, a deep blue-black hue; your forehead broad, your eyes bright and clear — you shall surely become a sovereign king."

Reading note — after Fazang

The girl's own opening stanzas are unusual for what they do not do: rather than repeating her refusal, or explaining the dream that has just been revealed to her, she instead names her own qualities plainly — beauty, wisdom, and a mind already free of anger, greed, and delusion — and then turns directly to praising the prince's own body. This is not capitulation; it reads, across the stanzas that follow, more as an assessment offered on equal footing, a girl who has just glimpsed a newly awakened buddha addressing a future king without deference, cataloguing his own marks the way a physiognomist might, before the account reveals what she actually intends to do with this encounter.
