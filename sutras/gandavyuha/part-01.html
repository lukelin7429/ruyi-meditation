The Assembly at Jetavana, and Why Samantabhadra and Mañjuśrī Stand First

The Buddha at Jetavana, in the Hall of the Layered Pavilion, together with five hundred great bodhisattvas — Samantabhadra and Mañjuśrī foremost among them, followed by a hundred and forty-one further companies of named bodhisattvas — five hundred great śrāvakas, and the kings of the heavens. This opening page sets the entire chapter's scene, before Sudhana has even entered the story.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang's commentary reads the setting itself as three concentric statements of perfection before a single bodhisattva is even named: the Buddha's own presence ("智正覺世間圓滿," the perfection of the world of wisdom and awakening), the physical setting's own perfection ("器世間圓滿" — city, park, and hall in turn), and, in the assembly that follows, the perfection of the world of sentient beings. On the setting itself, he glosses Śrāvastī (舍衛城) as "the City of the Hearer" — 聞者城 — tracing the name to a sage once resident there whose disciple, known simply as "the Hearer," first received the teaching from him and later built the city on that spot in his memory; Jeta's Grove and Anāthapiṇḍada's Park he reads as a joint gift, Prince Jeta ("Victory-Won," 誓多) donating the trees and the elder Anāthapiṇḍada — "one who gives to the orphaned and the solitary," so named for his charity to the parentless and childless alike — purchasing the land in gold, the two together founding the monastery and both lending it their names. The hall itself he glosses by its own three-character name: complete in substance is called "great" (大); complete in virtue is called "adorned" (嚴); and function arising from that substance is why it is called "layered" or "many-storied" (重閣) — a pavilion whose height expresses what its foundation already is.

On why Samantabhadra and Mañjuśrī alone stand at the head of five hundred bodhisattvas, Fazang gives three reasons that all point toward the same pairing. First, and most structurally: 普賢當法界門，是所入也；文殊當般若門，是能入也 — "Samantabhadra corresponds to the gate of the Dharma-realm, which is that which is entered; Mañjuśrī corresponds to the gate of prajñā, which is that which enters" — together naming, before the chapter has done anything else, exactly what its own title promises: a realm to be entered, and the wisdom that enters it. Second, by mastery: Samantabhadra has sovereign mastery in samādhi, Mañjuśrī sovereign mastery in prajñā. Third, by scope: Samantabhadra makes manifest what is vast and all-encompassing, Mañjuśrī what is deep and penetrating — breadth paired with depth. Every one of Sudhana's fifty-three teachers to come will, in one way or another, unfold some portion of this same pairing; this page states it once, at the very threshold, so that the pairing can simply be recognized rather than re-argued each time it recurs.

Reading note — after Fazang, continued

The hundred and forty-one further bodhisattvas are not, on Fazang's reading, a random inventory — 列名中有一百四十一大位，十十有十四位 — they fall into fourteen name-groups of (nominally) ten each, a handful running one or two names short or long, and each group's shared word carries its own significance: "banner" (幢) signifies conduct and virtue rising visibly above the rest; "adorned in splendor" (端嚴) signifies the twofold adornment of merit and wisdom; "treasury" (藏) signifies virtue held complete within, the way heartwood is held within a tree; "eye" (眼) signifies illumination of the Dharma-realm; "heavenly crown" (天冠) signifies pure virtue crowning the very peak of the mind; "crest" or topknot (周羅) signifies virtue weighty enough to command honor; "light" (光) carries a double sense, the light of the body and the light of wisdom, illuminating outward and inward at once; the second "banner" set shifts from standing alone to subduing what opposes it; "voice" (音) signifies a voice pleasing enough to delight whatever faculty hears it; "supreme" (上) signifies surpassing the assembled multitude; "sublime virtue" (妙德) signifies auspiciousness made visibly excellent; "king" (王) signifies sovereignty within the Dharma; the second "voice" set shifts from the voice's own beauty to its skill in expounding subtle teaching; and "awakening" (覺) signifies a clarity that illumines principle while discerning the capacities of those it teaches. Fazang is explicit that these fourteen groups are not fourteen separate topics so much as fourteen facets of the same two leading bodhisattvas' own qualities, distributed and multiplied across the assembly they head — which is also why, on his count, the full list runs to exactly a hundred and forty-one named positions before the text simply closes with "such were these five hundred bodhisattvas," leaving the remaining number implicit rather than individually named.

The Bodhisattvas' Qualities, the Śrāvakas, and the Kings of the Heavens

Chinese Source

此諸菩薩，皆悉出生普賢之行，境界無礙，充滿一切諸佛剎故；持無量身，悉能往詣一切佛故；具足無礙淨眼，見一切佛明自在故；至無量處，一切諸佛成正覺時，悉能往詣，現前見佛無休息故；無量智光，普照一切諸法海故；於無量劫，說不可盡，辯清淨故；究竟虛空界，智慧境界悉清淨故；無所依止，隨其所應，現色身故；除滅癡曀，善分別知眾生界故；虛空智慧，放大光網，普照一切諸法界故。復與五百大聲聞俱，悉覺真諦，證如實際，深入法性，離生死海；安住如來虛空境界，離結使縛；不著一切，遊行虛空，於諸佛所疑惑悉滅，深入信向諸佛大海。復與諸天王俱，悉已恭敬供養過去諸佛，長夜饒益一切眾生，心常行慈，未曾忘失；守護群生，入勝智門，不捨一切眾生；出生諸佛正法境界，守護佛法，受持佛性，生如來家，專求一切智門。

Ru-Yi Translation

All these bodhisattvas had given rise to the practice of Samantabhadra: their domain was unobstructed, filling every buddha-land; they held countless bodies, able to go before every buddha; they were endowed with unobstructed, pure eyes, seeing the radiant sovereignty of every buddha; reaching countless places, whenever any buddha attained perfect awakening they were able to go there, beholding that buddha face to face without cease; their immeasurable light of wisdom universally illumined the ocean of every dharma; through countless eons their eloquence, inexhaustible, remained pure; their wisdom, reaching to the very limit of the realm of space, was entirely pure; resting on no fixed ground, they manifested physical bodies according to what was needed; having dispelled the haze of delusion, they skillfully discerned and knew the realm of sentient beings; and their wisdom, vast as space, released a great net of light universally illumining every dharma-realm.

Together with them were five hundred great śrāvakas, all of whom had awakened to the true truth, realized reality as it is, penetrated deeply into the dharma-nature, and left behind the ocean of birth and death; abiding in the Tathāgata's own realm of space, they were free of the fetters of affliction; unattached to anything, they wandered freely through space, and every doubt regarding the buddhas had been extinguished in them, so deeply had they entered into faith toward the great ocean of the buddhas.

Together with them, too, were the kings of the heavens, all of whom had already reverently made offerings to the buddhas of the past, and through long nights had brought benefit to every sentient being, their minds constantly practicing loving-kindness without ever forgetting it; guarding all beings, they had entered the gate of supreme wisdom, never abandoning a single sentient being; giving rise to the domain of every buddha's true Dharma, they guarded the Buddha-dharma, upheld the buddha-nature, were born into the household of the Tathāgata, and single-mindedly sought the gate of omniscience.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang reads the ten clauses praising the bodhisattvas two ways at once, without treating either as exclusive of the other: as qualities every one of the five hundred equally possesses, and — more precisely — as ten phrases each keyed to one of the fifteen leading positions named just before them. The very first clause, praising a domain "unobstructed, filling every buddha-land," he reads as praising Samantabhadra and Mañjuśrī specifically, since universal practice and a domain without limit are exactly what the two leaders, between them, accomplish; the clauses that follow work outward through the fourteen name-groups in the same paired fashion — bodies reaching everywhere paired with eyes that see everywhere, wisdom-light illumining the Dharma paired with eloquence never exhausted, and so on to the final clause, wisdom vast as space releasing a net of light, which closes the list the way "awakening, light of the Dharma-realm" closed the name list itself.

On the five hundred great śrāvakas, Fazang first explains the honorific "great" (大) in the ordinary sense — these are śrāvakas of the fourth and final fruit, of the unshakeable lineage, foremost among śrāvakas as Śāriputra and his companions are elsewhere — but adds a second, sharper reading of his own: 大者是第四果故，又是不動種姓故...又釋此等並是大菩薩示現作聲聞 — "or again: these are all great bodhisattvas manifesting in the form of śrāvakas," exactly as the newly-translated Avataṃsaka's chapter on the Tathāgata's inconceivable state describes, appearing here as if deaf and blind to what is unfolding around them precisely in order to make the profundity of this teaching visible by contrast. The ten qualities praising them fall into four movements on his reading: the first three name the three trainings completed — awakening to truth is the path of seeing, realizing the ultimate is the path of cultivation, penetrating the nature is the path beyond further training; the next two describe an abiding in the emptiness of self that these śrāvakas share, in part, with the Tathāgata's own twofold emptiness, and a corresponding freedom from the nine fetters and ten proclivities; the next two describe mastery of spiritual power and non-attachment to the world; and the last two describe doubt fully extinguished and faith made unshakeable — which Fazang reads as itself a mark of these figures' true, hidden stature, since what they are shown believing without doubt is nothing less than a buddha's own most profound self-mastered fruit.

The kings of the heavens receive ten qualities in five deliberate pairs: prior cause, in offerings made to past buddhas and long benefit given to beings; present practice, compassion entering into wisdom; abiding in the impure while making the pure manifest, since they do not abandon defiled sentient beings even as they bring forth the Buddha's own pure Dharma; guarding the Dharma in two directions at once — outwardly protecting the teaching so that it continues to circulate, inwardly upholding their own true nature so that its realization is never lost; and finally the "household" pair, born into the Buddha's own family and single-mindedly pursuing the work proper to that family — omniscience itself named here as a "gate," Fazang notes, precisely because it is what leads all the way through to a buddha's own wisdom, not merely toward it.
