Eighty-Four Thousand Lights From a Single Mouth

The hymn continues: from the buddha's own mouth there pour forth wondrous lights, eighty-four thousand in number, universally illuminating every world, dispelling every affliction there. Endowed with the merit of wisdom, according with sentient beings in three ways, he transcends the world like space itself, yet constantly manifests within the world. Although he manifests, according with the world, the suffering of birth, old age, sickness, and death, or manifests instead an abiding, long-enduring lifespan, his own nature remains like space, unchanged. The tathāgata discerns and knows every class of sentient being, every one of their faculties and their nature and desires, causing them to abide in all-wisdom.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 448 complete Source: Taishō T278, 入法界品第三十四之十七 (Buddhabhadra, Eastern Jin) Reading notes after Fazang's 華嚴經探玄記 (T1733)

Manifesting Birth, Old Age, Sickness, and Death, Yet Unchanged as Space

Chinese Source

「佛口放妙光，　八萬四千數，
普照諸世界，　除滅眾煩惱。

具足智功德，　三種順眾生，
離世如虛空，　常現於世間。

雖復隨世現，　生老病死苦，
或復現住壽，　其性如虛空。

如來分別知，　一切眾生類，
諸根及性欲，　令住薩婆若。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"From the buddha's own mouth there pour forth wondrous lights, eighty-four thousand in number,
Universally illuminating every world, dispelling every affliction there.

Endowed with the merit of wisdom, according with sentient beings in three ways,
He transcends the world like space itself, yet constantly manifests within the world.

Although he manifests, according with the world, the suffering of birth, old age, sickness, and death,
Or manifests instead an abiding, long-enduring lifespan, his own nature remains like space, unchanged.

The tathāgata discerns and knows every class of sentient being,
Every one of their faculties and their nature and desires, causing them to abide in all-wisdom."

Reading note — after Fazang

The eighty-four thousand lights from the buddha's own mouth directly parallel the eighty-four thousand defilements and their eighty-four thousand antidote-teachings named several stanzas earlier — Fazang reads this precise numerical correspondence as no coincidence, but the hymn's own way of stating that each specific affliction has, quite literally, its own specific ray of light assigned to dispel it. "His own nature remains like space, unchanged," repeated here for a third time across this stretch of the hymn, functions as this whole sequence's refrain: whatever the buddha manifests outwardly — birth, sickness, death, or a lifespan of any length — never touches or alters what he actually is, exactly as clouds and weather never alter the sky they pass through.
