Fascicle 3 · 菩提品第十 · Complete

Enlightenment

One chapter fills this entire fascicle — the treatise's longest, and, in Li Baiyao's own preface, its most subtle: "it transforms the eight consciousnesses to accomplish the four wisdoms, and gathers the four wisdoms to furnish the three bodies." The chapter opens from omniscience as the fruit of every practice described so far, moves through the buddha as the unsurpassed refuge and the transformation of the basis with its ten qualities, states the dharma-realm's profundity in three dimensions — character, place, and activity — and along the way states, in a single quiet verse, that all sentient beings are called the tathāgatagarbha. It closes by working out what a buddha's transformations, maturing of beings, three bodies, and four wisdoms actually consist in, and ends on the image of separate rivers entering one sea.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Fascicle 3 complete Source: Taishō T1604, 大乘莊嚴經論卷第三, 菩提品第十 Chinese attribution: 無著菩薩造 (compiled by Asaṅga)

Where This Fascicle Picks Up

Fascicle 2 closed with the bodhisattva maturing both himself and others, the six perfections applied to beings across this life and the next. This fascicle asks what all of that practice is for: what omniscience actually is, what happens when the basis is transformed, how profound the resulting dharma-realm is in its character, its place, and its activity, and what a buddha's body, wisdom, and ceaseless activity toward beings actually consist in. The single chapter here, 菩提品, is unbroken by any internal division in the source beyond its own verse-and-prose alternation; the section headings below are this translation's own, added for navigation.

Classical text is set in the left-hand column, our English in the right. See also this site's note on translation method.

1. The Fruit of Omniscience, and the Buddha-Body Free of Duality

Chinese Source

釋曰：已說菩薩成熟眾生，次說菩薩得一切種智。

偈曰：

一切難已行，　一切善已集，

一切時已度，　一切障已斷。

釋曰：此偈顯一切種智因圓滿。一切難已行者，由具足行無量百千種難行行未曾疲倦故。一切善已集者，由具足聚集諸波羅蜜自性善根故。一切時已度者，由具足經長時大劫阿僧祇故。一切障已斷者，由具足斷一切大乘障，謂諸地所有微細障故。

偈曰：

成就一切種，　此即為佛身，

譬如大篋開，　眾寶無不現。

釋曰：此偈顯一切種智果圓滿。有三義分別：一至得、二自性、三譬喻。成就一切種者，謂至得分別，從此已後成就一切種智故。此即為佛身者，謂自性分別，即說一切種智為佛身體故。譬如大篋開眾寶無不現者，謂譬喻分別，不可思議菩提分寶皆現前故。已說一切種智為佛身，次說此身無二相。

Ru-Yi Translation

The exposition: the bodhisattva's maturing of sentient beings having been stated, next the bodhisattva's attainment of omniscience is explained.

Every difficulty has been practiced through;
every good has been gathered in;
every span of time has been traversed;
every obstruction has been cut off.

The exposition: this verse displays the fulfillment of the causes of omniscience. Every difficulty has been practiced through means that one has fully practiced measureless hundreds of thousands of kinds of difficult practices without ever tiring. Every good has been gathered in means gathering the wholesome roots that are the own-nature of the perfections. Every span of time has been traversed means passing through vast, incalculable eons. Every obstruction has been cut off means fully cutting off every obstruction of the Mahāyāna — the subtle obstructions belonging to each of the grounds.

Accomplishing every mode of knowing —
this is itself the buddha-body;
it is like a great casket opened,
where every jewel appears, none lacking.

The exposition: this verse displays the fulfillment of the fruit of omniscience, distinguished by three aspects: attainment, own-nature, and simile. Accomplishing every mode of knowing refers to attainment, in that from this point on omniscience is accomplished. This is itself the buddha-body refers to own-nature, in that omniscience itself is said to be the substance of the buddha-body. Like a great casket opened, where every jewel appears refers to simile, in that the inconceivable jewels that are the factors of enlightenment all appear before one. Omniscience having been explained as the buddha-body, next this body's mark of non-duality is explained.

Chinese Source

偈曰：

白法為佛身，　非無亦非有，

佛為法寶因，　法則善根因。

釋曰：白法為佛身者，轉六波羅蜜等一切善法為佛體故。非無亦非有者，此體非無。何以故？真如無別故。亦復非有。何以故？自性不成就故。是名無二相。佛為法寶因者，佛說一切法故，及以神通力故。法則善根因者，眾生為田，善根為穀，如是法寶於所化眾生田生長善根穀故。

偈曰：

具法亦離法，　如藏亦如雲，

生法雨法雨，　故成如是譬。

釋曰：此偈重顯前義。具法亦離法者，諸佛具足一切善法故、遠離一切不善法故。如藏亦如雲者，佛寶如藏，法寶如雲。問：此以何義？答：生法雨。法雨故，成如是譬。佛寶能出生法寶，與大藏相似。法寶能生長一切眾生善根，與大雲相似。已說佛身無二相，次說是無上歸依。

Ru-Yi Translation

White dharmas make up the buddha-body;
it is neither non-existent nor existent;
the Buddha is the cause of the Dharma-jewel,
and the Dharma is the cause of wholesome roots.

The exposition: white dharmas make up the buddha-body means that the six perfections and all wholesome dharmas are transformed into the buddha's own substance. Neither non-existent nor existent means: this substance is not non-existent, since suchness is not something separate from it; and it is not existent, since it does not have an own-nature independently accomplished. This is called the mark of non-duality. The Buddha is the cause of the Dharma-jewel means that the Buddha teaches every dharma, and acts by the power of supernormal ability. The Dharma is the cause of wholesome roots means: sentient beings are the field, and wholesome roots the grain, so that this Dharma-jewel causes the grain of wholesome roots to grow in the field of the beings it transforms.

Complete with dharmas, yet apart from dharmas;
like a treasury, and like a cloud;
giving birth to the Dharma, raining the rain of the Dharma —
for this reason these similes hold.

The exposition: this verse restates the previous meaning. Complete with dharmas, yet apart from dharmas means the buddhas are complete in every wholesome dharma, and far removed from every unwholesome one. Like a treasury, and like a cloud means the Buddha-jewel is like a treasury, and the Dharma-jewel is like a cloud. Question: what is the sense of this? Answer: giving birth to the Dharma, raining the rain of the Dharma — this is why these similes hold. The Buddha-jewel gives birth to the Dharma-jewel, resembling a great treasury; the Dharma-jewel gives rise to and increases the wholesome roots of all sentient beings, resembling a great cloud. The buddha-body's mark of non-duality having been explained, next it is explained that this is the unsurpassed refuge.

Reading note

非無亦非有 sets the pattern for how this entire chapter will handle the buddha-body, and it is worth noticing how differently it argues for "not existent" than for "not non-existent." Not non-existent rests on identity with suchness — nothing could make suchness itself absent. Not existent rests on the buddha-body's lacking any independently accomplished own-nature — even the highest attainment in this treatise's system does not get to be a self-standing substance. The same two-sided refusal that fascicle 2 used to describe ultimate reality in general is now applied to the single most exalted thing this treatise has to describe.

The treasury-and-cloud pairing works because a treasury and a cloud fail in opposite directions if pushed too far: a treasury just sits there, however full; a cloud produces without limit but has nothing of its own to give, only what it releases. Pairing the Buddha-jewel with the treasury and the Dharma-jewel with the cloud credits the Buddha with a fixed, if inexhaustible, store, and credits the Dharma he teaches with an active, self-renewing generativity — together accounting for how a teaching can be received once and yet never be exhausted by any number of hearers.

2. The Unsurpassed Refuge: What the Buddhas Save Beings From

Chinese Source

偈曰：

諸佛常救護，　眾生三染污，

諸惑諸惡行，　及以生老死。

釋曰：此偈略顯救護義。諸佛常救護者，由畢竟救護故。問：救護何法？答：眾生三染污，謂煩惱染污、業染污、生染污。諸惑者，即煩惱染污。諸惡行者，即業染污。及以生老死者，即生染污。問：云何救護？答：於此三種眾生，一切時救護不捨，即是畢竟義。

偈曰：

諸災及惡趣，　身見亦小乘，

如是諸眾生，　一切皆救護。

釋曰：此偈廣顯救護義。諸災者，謂盲聾瘖瘂狂亂形殘等眾生。由佛力故，盲者得視、聾者得聽、瘂者能言、狂者得正、亂者得定、形殘者得具足，如是救護。惡趣者，謂地獄等眾生，放光照觸，令得離苦不復更入，如是救護。身見者，謂著我眾生，令得人無我，解入二乘涅槃，如是救護。小乘者，謂二乘性不定眾生，方便引入大乘，如是救護。

Ru-Yi Translation

The buddhas constantly rescue and protect
sentient beings from three defilements:
the afflictions, unwholesome deeds,
and birth, old age, and death.

The exposition: this verse briefly displays the meaning of rescue and protection. The buddhas constantly rescue and protect means they do so to the very end. Question: from what do they rescue and protect beings? Answer: from the three defilements of sentient beings — of the afflictions, of karma, and of birth. The afflictions is the defilement of the afflictions. Unwholesome deeds is the defilement of karma. And birth, old age, and death is the defilement of birth. Question: how do they rescue and protect? Answer: with regard to these three, they rescue and protect at all times without forsaking them — this is the sense of "to the very end."

Every calamity, and the evil destinies,
the view of a substantial body, and the Lesser Vehicle —
all sentient beings such as these
are, every one, rescued and protected.

The exposition: this verse displays the meaning of rescue at length. Every calamity refers to beings who are blind, deaf, mute, deranged, disturbed, or maimed in body. By the Buddha's power, the blind gain sight, the deaf gain hearing, the mute speak, the deranged gain sanity, the disturbed gain stability, and the maimed gain wholeness — this is how they are rescued. The evil destinies refers to beings such as those in the hells; by radiating light that touches them, he frees them from suffering and they enter such destinies no more — this is how they are rescued. The view of a substantial body refers to beings attached to a self; he causes them to attain the selflessness of persons, and to enter the nirvāṇa of the two vehicles — this is how they are rescued. The Lesser Vehicle refers to beings of unfixed disposition within the two vehicles; by skillful means he draws them into the Mahāyāna — this is how they are rescued.

Chinese Source

偈曰：

佛為勝歸處，　無比故無上，

如前種種畏，　無不令脫者。

釋曰：此偈顯歸依勝。由佛無譬喻，故為無上。是故如前所說三種染污眾生，及餘災等眾生，一切皆能救護。

偈曰：

諸佛善滿身，　一切世間勝，

妙法化眾生，　以度悲海故。

釋曰：此偈顯歸依勝因。諸佛善滿身一切世間勝者，此由自利究竟，由力無畏等諸善功德自性滿故。妙法化眾生以度悲海故者，善解教化眾生方便，及度大悲海岸究竟故。

偈曰：

盡於未來際，　普及一切生，

恒時利益彼，　是說歸依大。

釋曰：此偈顯歸依大。大有三義：一者時大，窮一切眾生生死際故。二者境大，以一切眾生為境故。三者事大，恒時作利益救脫其苦令出離故。已說無上歸依，次說如來轉依相。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha is the supreme refuge,
unequaled, and therefore unsurpassed;
of the various fears described above,
there is none from which he does not bring release.

The exposition: this verse displays the supremacy of this refuge. Since the Buddha has no equal, he is unsurpassed; therefore all the beings afflicted by the three defilements above, and by calamity and the rest, he is able to rescue and protect, every one.

The buddhas' bodies are full of good,
surpassing all the world;
the wondrous Dharma transforms sentient beings,
for the sea of compassion has been crossed.

The exposition: this verse displays the cause of this refuge's supremacy. The buddhas' bodies are full of good, surpassing all the world means this comes from the culmination of self-benefit, the own-nature of their good qualities — the powers, the fearlessnesses, and the rest — being full. The wondrous Dharma transforms sentient beings, for the sea of compassion has been crossed means they have mastered the means of transforming beings, and reached the far shore of the sea of great compassion to its culmination.

To the very limit of the future,
reaching universally to every being,
constantly benefiting them —
this is called the greatness of the refuge.

The exposition: this verse displays the greatness of this refuge, of three kinds: the greatness of time, reaching through the entire span of every being's saṃsāra; the greatness of scope, taking all sentient beings as its scope; and the greatness of deed, constantly benefiting, rescuing, and freeing beings from suffering. The unsurpassed refuge having been explained, next the mark of the Tathāgata's transformation of the basis is explained.

Reading note

The three defilements — of the afflictions, of karma, and of birth — are the same threefold scheme used to classify defilement throughout this site's Yogācāra corpus, and naming them at the head of the chapter on enlightenment is a deliberate move: before describing what buddhahood is, the treatise first describes what buddhahood is for. The catalogue of specific calamities that follows — blindness, deafness, muteness, madness — reads almost like a hospital ward, and the specificity is the point. This chapter's later pages will rise to the most abstract vocabulary in the entire treatise; it opens instead on bodies that cannot see or hear or speak, made whole.

身見, the view of a substantial body, and 小乘, the Lesser Vehicle, are listed as calamities to be rescued from in exactly the same breath as blindness and hell — a placement that says something about how this treatise ranks its categories of harm. Being attached to a self, or being fixed in a vehicle aimed only at one's own liberation, are treated here not as intellectual errors to be corrected in argument (as fascicle 1's chapter on establishing the tenet handled them) but as afflictions on the same footing as physical and karmic suffering, addressed by the same compassionate rescue.

3. The Transformation of the Basis, and Its Ten Qualities

Chinese Source

偈曰：

二障種恒隨，　彼滅極廣斷，

白法圓滿故，　依轉二道成。

釋曰：此偈顯示轉依有離有得。二障種恒隨彼滅極廣斷者，此明所治遠離，謂煩惱障、智障二種種子，無始已來恒時隨逐，今得永滅。極者，一切地。廣者，一切種。此皆斷故。白法圓滿故依轉二道成者，此明能治成就。謂佛體與最上圓滿白法相應，爾時依轉得二道成就：一得極清淨出世智道、二得無邊所識境界智道，是名轉依。

偈曰：

彼處如來住，　不動如山王，

尚悲樂滅人，　況著諸有者。

釋曰：此偈顯示如來轉依諸轉中勝。何以故？如來轉依住無漏界處，如山王鎮地安住不動。如此轉已，見於聲聞緣覺樂寂滅人尚生憐愍，何況遠邊下賤著有苦惱眾生。

Ru-Yi Translation

The seeds of the two obstructions constantly follow along;
their cessation is cut off to the utmost, everywhere;
because the white dharmas are brought to fullness,
the transformed basis accomplishes two paths.

The exposition: this verse displays that the transformation of the basis involves both a departure and an attainment. The seeds of the two obstructions constantly follow along; their cessation is cut off to the utmost, everywhere clarifies the departure from what is remedied: the seeds of the afflictive and cognitive obstructions, which have followed along since beginningless time, are now forever extinguished. To the utmost means at every ground; everywhere means in every kind — all cut off. Because the white dharmas are brought to fullness, the transformed basis accomplishes two paths clarifies the accomplishment of the remedy: the buddha's substance corresponds to the supreme, fulfilled white dharmas, and by this transformation two paths are accomplished — the path of utterly pure, transcendent wisdom, and the path of the wisdom of the boundless field of what is known. This is called the transformation of the basis.

In that state the Tathāgata abides,
unmoving, like the king of mountains;
he has compassion even for those who delight in cessation —
how much more for those attached to existence.

The exposition: this verse displays that the Tathāgata's transformation of the basis is supreme among all transformations. Why? Because the Tathāgata's transformed basis abides in the uncontaminated realm, as fixed and unmoving as the king of mountains planted in the earth. Having so transformed, he has pity even for śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas who delight in quiescence — how much more for beings, distant and lowly, attached to existence and afflicted by suffering.

Chinese Source

偈曰：

他利及無上，　不轉及不生，

廣大與無二，　無住亦平等，

殊勝與遍授，　是說如來轉，

顯示十功德，　差別義應知。

釋曰：此二偈顯示如來轉依有十種功德差別。何等為十？一者他義轉，謂轉依已，為利他故。二者無上轉，謂轉依已，一切法中而得自在，過二乘轉故。三者不轉轉，謂轉依已，染污諸因不能轉此依，彼依轉故。四者不生轉，謂轉依已，一切染污法畢竟不起故。五者廣大轉，謂轉依已，示現得大菩提及般涅槃故。六者無二轉，謂轉依已，生死涅槃無有二故。七者不住轉，謂轉依已，有為無為俱不住故。八者平等轉，謂轉依已，與聲聞緣覺同解脫煩惱障故。九者殊勝轉，謂轉依已，力無畏等一切佛法無與等故。十者遍授轉，謂轉依已，恒以一切乘而教授故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Benefiting others, and the unsurpassed;
not transforming back, and not arising;
vast, and non-dual;
non-abiding, and equal;
surpassing, and universally bestowing —
these describe the Tathāgata's transformation,
displaying ten distinct qualities
that should be known.

The exposition: these two verses display that the Tathāgata's transformation of the basis has ten distinct qualities. What are the ten? First, for the benefit of others, in that, once transformed, it is for others' benefit. Second, unsurpassed, in that one obtains mastery over all things, surpassing the transformation of the two vehicles. Third, not transforming back, in that the causes of defilement can no longer transform this basis, it having itself been transformed. Fourth, not arising, in that no defiled dharma ever arises again. Fifth, vast, in that one displays the attainment of great enlightenment and final nirvāṇa. Sixth, non-dual, in that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are not two. Seventh, non-abiding, in that one abides neither in the conditioned nor the unconditioned. Eighth, equal, in that one is liberated from the afflictive obstruction equally with śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas. Ninth, surpassing, in that the powers, the fearlessnesses, and the rest have nothing equal to them. And tenth, universally bestowing, in that one constantly teaches by means of every vehicle.

Reading note

轉依有離有得— the transformation of the basis involves both a departure and an attainment — states in five characters a structural point this treatise has been building toward since fascicle 1: liberation is never described here as pure subtraction. The seeds of the two obstructions are indeed extinguished, but the verse pairs that departure with an equally real attainment, the path of transcendent wisdom and the path of knowing the boundless field of objects. A reading that treated enlightenment as simply the absence of affliction would only have half the picture this treatise is offering.

Of the ten qualities, the eighth is easy to read past and worth pausing on: 平等轉, equal transformation, is the one place in the entire list where the buddha's attainment is deliberately described as no different from the śrāvaka's — 與聲聞緣覺同解脫煩惱障故, equally liberated from the afflictive obstruction. Every other quality in the list marks a way the buddha's transformation surpasses the two vehicles; this one marks a point of genuine parity. The distinction the treatise cares about is not that buddhas are free of affliction and arhats are not, but that buddhas are additionally free of the cognitive obstruction the other nine qualities describe.

4. The Buddha Pervading All Like Space, and Why Some Do Not See It

Chinese Source

偈曰：

如空遍一切，　佛亦一切遍；

虛空遍諸色，　諸佛遍眾生。

釋曰：此偈顯示佛體一切遍，與虛空相似。初二句直說，後二句釋說。譬如虛空遍一切色聚，佛體亦爾，遍一切眾生聚。若以眾生現非佛故，言佛體不遍者，是義不然，未成就故。

偈曰：

譬如水器壞，　月像不現前；

如是眾生過，　佛像亦不現。

釋曰：此偈顯示佛體雖遍而眾生不見。譬如水器破壞不見月像，如是眾生過失不見佛像，此義得成。

偈曰：

譬如火聚性，　或然或滅盡；

如是諸佛化，　或出或涅槃。

釋曰：此偈顯示諸佛教化有出有沒。譬如火性有時熾然有時滅盡，諸佛教化亦復如是，有時示現出世、有時示現涅槃。如是已說如來轉依，次說如來事業恒無功用。

Ru-Yi Translation

As space pervades everything,
the Buddha too pervades everything;
as empty space pervades all form,
the buddhas pervade all sentient beings.

The exposition: this verse displays that the buddha-substance pervades everything, resembling empty space. The first two lines state this directly; the last two explain it. It is like empty space, which pervades every mass of form; the buddha-substance is just the same, pervading every mass of sentient beings. If one were to say, because sentient beings do not presently appear as buddhas, that the buddha-substance does not pervade them, this would not follow, since they have simply not yet accomplished it.

It is like a water-vessel that is broken —
the moon's reflection does not appear in it;
just so, owing to the fault of sentient beings,
the buddha's reflection likewise does not appear.

The exposition: this verse displays that, although the buddha-substance pervades all, sentient beings do not see it. It is like a broken water-vessel in which the moon's reflection is not seen; just so, owing to the fault of sentient beings, the buddha's reflection is not seen — this is how the point is established.

It is like the nature of a mass of fire,
now blazing, now extinguished entirely;
just so, the buddhas' transformative teaching
now appears in the world, now enters nirvāṇa.

The exposition: this verse displays that the buddhas' teaching and transformation of beings has both an appearing and a disappearing. It is like the nature of fire, at times blazing and at times extinguished; the buddhas' teaching is just the same, at times displaying an appearance in the world, and at times displaying entry into nirvāṇa. The Tathāgata's transformation of the basis having thus been explained, next it is explained that the Tathāgata's activity is constantly without exertion.

Reading note

The broken-vessel simile is doing careful philosophical work, not just decorating the point. If the buddha-substance pervades all beings equally, as the space simile just established, then the difference between a being who perceives a buddha and one who does not cannot lie in the buddha at all — it has to lie entirely on the receiving end. A cracked vessel does not diminish the moon; it only fails to hold a reflection of it. The doctrine of universal pervasion and the fact that most beings do not experience it are not in tension on this account, because the whole explanatory burden has been shifted onto 眾生過, the fault of sentient beings, before the tension can even arise.

Reading note

大我, the great self, is the most provocative term in this section, and the treatise is careful to define it in a way that keeps it from collapsing back into exactly the self it spent fascicle 1 and fascicle 2 refuting. The "self" here is not a substantial owner of experience but 清淨如, pure suchness itself — selflessness pushed to its own purity and then re-named, not a self smuggled back in through the side door. Reading 大我 as continuous with the earlier chapters' selflessness, rather than as a reversal of it, is necessary to keep the whole treatise consistent with itself.

The four marks of the pure dharma-realm — purity, the great self, the indeterminate, and liberation — each get their non-dual treatment through a different analytic strategy: purity through the never-tainted/now-cleared pair, the great self through a redefinition of the term itself, the indeterminate through an explicit citation of the classical fourfold undetermined questions about the Tathāgata after death, and liberation through the iron-and-cataract simile's careful "neither X nor not-X" applied to the ending of a state rather than to a substance. Four marks, four different tools — the chapter does not force a single argument-form to do all the work.

Reading note

非一亦非多 for the dharma-realm's place is a different non-duality from anything argued in the preceding sections, and its reasoning is worth isolating: not one, because each buddha's dharma-realm accords with the distinct path that particular buddha traversed to reach it (前身隨順); not many, because the dharma-realm is not itself a body or substance that could be multiplied like objects in space. The same move recurs at the chapter's most famous line, a few verses ahead: individuality is preserved on the side of the path each being walks, while the realm reached is not the kind of thing that comes in countable units to begin with.

The seventh of the eight similes for activity is the one that qualifies everything around it, and it would be easy to build a triumphalist reading of buddha-activity without it: light "obscured by clouds," 眾生過失為障, obstructed by the faults of sentient beings — an explicit admission that the dharma-realm's activity does not simply overpower every obstacle. Read against the broken-vessel simile earlier in this fascicle, the pattern is consistent throughout the chapter: whatever fails to reach a being is explained by a fault on that being's side, never by any limitation in what is pervading and shining on the other.

7. "All Sentient Beings Are Called the Tathāgatagarbha"

Chinese Source

偈曰：

釋曰：此偈顯示法界是如來藏。一切無別故者，一切眾生、一切諸佛等無差別，故名為如。得如清淨故者，得清淨如以為自性，故名如來。以是義故，可說一切眾生名為如來藏。已說無漏界甚深，次說諸佛變化。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because there is no difference among all beings,
and because purity, once attained, is suchness —
therefore it is said that all sentient beings
are called the tathāgatagarbha.

The exposition: this verse displays that the dharma-realm is the tathāgatagarbha. Because there is no difference among all beings means that all sentient beings and all buddhas are equally without difference, and are therefore called suchness. Because purity, once attained, is suchness means that, having attained pure suchness as one's own-nature, one is called a tathāgata. For this reason, it can be said that all sentient beings are called the tathāgatagarbha. Reading note

This is one verse and three lines of prose, and it is easy to read past at the pace this chapter otherwise moves — but it is the single most consequential sentence in the treatise. The argument is compressed to the point of terseness: all beings are without difference from all buddhas (in the sense already established at length — the dharma-realm they share is neither one nor many, and purity was never something added from outside); and a tathāgata just is one who has taken pure suchness as his own-nature. Put the two together and the conclusion follows in the space of a single verse: every being already stands in the relation to buddhahood that the word "womb" or "matrix" (garbha) names — not yet born as a buddha, but already carrying what buddhahood consists in.

It is worth being precise about what this verse does and does not claim, since the doctrine became one of the most contested in later Buddhist philosophy. It does not say all beings are already buddhas in every respect — the entire treatise up to this point has been describing a path, with grounds, obstructions, and a transformation of the basis still to be accomplished. What it grounds is narrower and, if anything, more radical: that the difference between an unenlightened being and a buddha is not a difference in what each fundamentally is, only in whether purity has yet been attained. The chapter does not pause to reconcile this with fascicle 1's separate teaching that some beings lack the lineage altogether (無性位); readers curious about how later Yogācāra and other Mahāyāna traditions handled that tension may wish to consult this site's translation-notes page for this collection.

Reading note

The four-tier hierarchy of transformation — world, śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, bodhisattva, buddha — reads as a scale of what can be overridden by what, and it is worth noticing that it is a purely relative measure: nothing here says what a buddha's transformation consists in positively, only that nothing else can surpass it. The positive content arrives only in the eight faculty-by-faculty transformations that follow, which read almost like an inventory audit of everything an unenlightened being is made of — the five senses, the defiled mental faculty, sense-objects and their consciousnesses, discrimination, the container world itself, desire, and even the notion of space — each one claimed back and re-described as a vehicle for benefiting beings rather than a source of bondage.

示現妻無染, displaying a consort without any taint, is a startling item to find on this list, and it is there for a specific doctrinal reason: it addresses the biographical fact that the historical Buddha, before his renunciation, had a wife and a child. Rather than treating this as an embarrassment to be explained away, the treatise absorbs it into the very structure of transformed desire — a buddha can display the outward form of desire's objects while the defilement that ordinarily accompanies them has already been transformed out of existence. The same logic that let the treatise call an enlightenment-lifetime's biography "skillful means" earlier in this fascicle is doing its most concrete work here.

Reading note

熟熟不無餘 — ripened again and again, never without remainder — quietly closes off a question the chapter's earlier language might otherwise invite. If maturation runs all the way from gathering through growth, ripening, and freedom, a reader could suppose the process eventually completes and stops. The verse insists otherwise: because the world itself is inexhaustible, ripening keeps recurring even after a being reaches the final stage, which is another way of saying that a buddha's work toward any one being is genuinely never finished, not merely open-ended in principle.

The four similes that close this section — a lamp lighting other lamps without dimming, a sun radiating without effort, an ocean receiving every river without filling, and the earlier casting of light without discrimination — share a single structural feature worth naming: in every case, the source is undiminished by what it gives. This is the section's real answer to a question a careful reader might ask after fascicle 2's chapter on the twofold benefit: does a buddha's activity toward measureless beings eventually run into any kind of limit or cost? Four different images, and each gives the same answer.

Reading note

食身, literally "the body that feasts," is this treatise's own preferred term for what other texts on this site, and this very chapter a few lines later, call 受用身, the enjoyment body — the text explicitly glosses the two as the same thing (受用身，謂食身). The literal image is worth keeping in view even where the translation uses the more familiar "enjoyment body": this is the body that partakes of the Dharma as food, 於大集眾中作法食, making the Dharma-food amid a great assembly. It is a body defined by an act of communal nourishment, not merely by private bliss.

The three-way division of labor among the bodies is exact and worth holding onto: the intrinsic-nature body is the support (由依), the enjoyment body is defined through mind (由心), and the transformation body through deed (由業) — support, mind, and deed being three genuinely different categories, not three synonyms for "aspect." And the causal chain running through them — the intrinsic-nature body as root, the enjoyment body arising from it and in turn causing the transformation body — means the three are not simply parallel manifestations but a single lineage, each generating the one after it, which is why the treatise can call the enjoyment body's accomplishment self-benefit and the transformation body's accomplishment the benefit of others: the more private attainment must exist before it can be extended outward.

Ru-Yi Translation

Upholding what is heard, and a mind of equality;
opening up the Dharma, and performing deeds —
in dependence on these four meanings,
the four wisdoms arise in this order.

The exposition: upholding what is heard means upholding the Dharma one has heard. A mind of equality means attaining equality between self and other with regard to all beings. Opening up the Dharma means expounding the true Dharma. Performing deeds means giving rise to every kind of transformative activity. universality, and beginninglessness;
because of these, not one —
because of a common basis, not many.

The exposition: this verse displays that the buddhas are neither one nor many. Not one: by distinctness of nature, of not being in vain, of universality, of beginninglessness, and of lacking difference. Distinctness of nature: the natures of the boundless buddhas are each distinct — were there only one buddha, those still to attain enlightenment could not; this does not follow, so the buddhas are not one. Not in vain: were the accumulation of merit and wisdom in vain, other bodhisattvas would fail to attain enlightenment; since it is not in vain, this does not follow, so the buddhas are not one. Universality: were there only one buddha, that buddha would fail to benefit all beings; since the Buddha establishes all beings as future buddhas, this does not follow, so the buddhas are not one. Beginninglessness: were there only one original buddha, that buddha would have attained buddhahood without the twofold accumulation; this does not follow, so the buddhas are not one. Lacking difference: were there a distinct buddha lacking the twofold accumulation, this does not follow, so the buddhas are not one. Not many: by reason of a common basis, the Dharma-body of every buddha resting upon the very same uncontaminated realm. The buddhas' wisdom having been explained, next the means of entering buddhahood are explained.

Reading note

The mapping from eight consciousnesses to four wisdoms is not one-to-one, and the asymmetry is worth flagging: the eighth consciousness alone becomes the mirror wisdom, the seventh alone becomes the wisdom of equality, the sixth alone becomes the wisdom of wondrous contemplation — but the wisdom of accomplishing what is to be done absorbs all five sense-consciousnesses at once. Eight consciousnesses become four wisdoms not because pairs of them merge, but because the five sense-consciousnesses, which never individuated into separate wisdoms the way the deeper three did, collapse together into a single outward-facing function once transformed. The mirror wisdom's role as 諸智因, the cause of all the others, together with this asymmetric collapse, is why this treatise's own summary can then align each wisdom with one of the three bodies — mirror and equality wisdom together as the Dharma-body, contemplative wisdom as the enjoyment body, and the wisdom of accomplishing what is to be done as the transformation body.

The five-part proof that buddhas are not one is a genuine philosophical argument, not a devotional flourish, and its structure deserves to be seen clearly: each of the five reasons is a reductio, assuming a single buddha and showing that some fact already established elsewhere in this treatise — that beings still attain enlightenment, that the two accumulations are not wasted effort, that the Buddha benefits everyone, that buddhahood requires the twofold accumulation as its cause — would then become false. The conclusion "not many," by contrast, needs only one sentence, since it rests on the single fact, already established in this chapter, that every buddha's Dharma-body rests on the same uncontaminated realm. Multiplicity is disproven at length; unity through a shared basis is disproven briefly — the treatise clearly considers the second claim the easier of the two to establish.

Reading note

分別若恒有，真實則永無 states, as a bare conditional, the same logic this treatise has used since fascicle 2's chapter on reality: discrimination and the real are not two competing items that might coexist in different proportions, but strict alternatives — wherever one is fully present, the other is fully absent, and vice versa. What is new here is the pointed application: a bodhisattva who spends long years reverently contemplating the Buddha's extraordinary qualities, hoping this devotion will speed his own attainment, is engaged in an activity that is itself still discrimination — subject and admired object, self and other still standing apart — and is therefore, on this treatise's own logic, moving no closer to what he seeks. 彼有慢故, because he still has conceit, is a startlingly blunt diagnosis to apply to what looks, on the surface, like devout practice.

The rivers-into-the-sea image closes the treatise's longest chapter on a claim about plurality answered, at last, by union rather than by argument. Every earlier section of this fascicle proved non-duality analytically — five characteristics, ten qualities, eight similes, five reductios against buddhas being one. This final image instead just shows what happens: separate rivers, each with its own bed and its own modest capacity, become, on entering the sea, one bed, one capacity, vastly larger than the sum of what any of them held alone. The treatise's closing exhortation — that the wise should seek to arouse the mind for this — asks for exactly the same thing the very first fascicle asked for at its opening similes: not agreement with an argument, but the arousing of a mind that wants to become part of what has just been described.

Fascicle 3 Complete

One chapter, the treatise's longest and, by its own preface, its most subtle. Omniscience is given as the fruit of every practice in the two fascicles before it, and the buddha-body that results is neither existent nor non-existent. The buddha is named the unsurpassed refuge from three defilements, and the transformation of the basis that produces him is given ten distinct qualities. The dharma-realm's profundity is stated in three dimensions — character, place, and activity — with four marks and eight similes, and, at the chapter's quiet center, all sentient beings are called the tathāgatagarbha. The buddhas' transformations reach into every faculty a being is made of; their maturing of beings never completes, since the world itself never ends; their three bodies stand in a single causal lineage from intrinsic nature through enjoyment to transformation; their four wisdoms arise from the transformation of eight consciousnesses; and they are shown to be neither one nor many, entered not by devotion alone but by a discrimination that finally discriminates itself away — ending, like separate rivers reaching one sea, in a single understanding vast enough for every being it was ever meant to benefit.
