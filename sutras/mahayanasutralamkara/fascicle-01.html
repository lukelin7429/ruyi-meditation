The Case for the Mahāyāna, and the Bodhisattva Lineage

The treatise opens with its own reason for existing: an "ornament" is not decoration for its own sake but a set of five similes for how a teaching moves a mind, from first faith through to realization. From there it takes up the question a skeptical contemporary would actually have asked — is the Mahāyāna really the Buddha's word, or a later invention? — and answers with eight independent arguments, several of which work by showing that the objector's own premises, followed through, prove the opposite of what he intended. Having established the Mahāyāna, the treatise asks why anyone would take refuge in it above the other two vehicles, and answers in the extravagant register of royal birth: bodhicitta as seed, wisdom as mother, compassion as wet-nurse. And it closes this fascicle by asking what makes a bodhisattva a bodhisattva in the first place — the lineage, its existence proven from four kinds of observable difference among beings, its nine topics run out to their least comfortable conclusion: some beings, on this treatise's own account, do not have it at all.

Where This Fascicle Picks Up

This is the opening fascicle of the Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra (大乘莊嚴經論), translated into Chinese by Prabhākaramitra in the early Tang and here translated into English for the first time on this site. The Taishō text opens with an official preface by Li Baiyao, the court official who wrote it up by imperial decree — a document about the translation project itself rather than the treatise's own teaching. That preface, together with the question of how to describe this treatise's authorship, is addressed on this collection's index page rather than repeated here; what follows below is the treatise proper, starting from its own first verse.

The Chinese source marks "偈曰" for verse and "釋曰" for the prose that expounds it — not "頌曰" and "論曰" as in this site's other Yogācāra treatises, a difference of the translator's own idiom rather than of content. No verse-only extract of this text survives, unlike the companion text used to verify other treatises on this site; the boundary between root verse and prose exposition here is simply given by the source's own alternation of 偈曰 and 釋曰, which is unambiguous throughout.

Classical text is set in the left-hand column, our English in the right. See also this site's note on translation method.

1. The Origin: Five Similes for the Ornament

Chinese Source

偈曰：

義智作諸義，　言句皆無垢，

救濟苦眾生，　慈悲為性故。

巧說方便法，　所謂最上乘，

為發大心者，　略以五義現。

釋曰：莊嚴大乘經論，誰能莊嚴？答：義智能莊嚴。問：義智云何莊嚴？答：開作諸義。問：以何開作？答：以言及句。問：以何等言？以何等句？答：以無垢言、以無垢句。無垢言者，謂能至涅槃城。無垢句者，謂字句相應。若離無垢言句，則於諸義不能開曉。

問：以何義故莊嚴？答：為救濟苦眾生故。問：眾生自苦，何因救濟？答：為菩薩者大悲為體，生憐愍故。問：若救他苦，莊嚴何法？答：莊嚴如來巧說方便法。問：何等方便法？答：所謂最上乘。問：為誰故莊嚴？答：為發大乘心者。問：以幾義莊嚴？答：略以五義示現。問：何者五義？

Ru-Yi Translation

Wisdom of meaning brings forth all meanings;
its words and phrases are all stainless;
it rescues suffering sentient beings,
for compassion is its very nature.

Skillfully taught, a method of skillful means —
this is called the supreme vehicle —
for those who arouse the great mind,
it is shown in brief through five meanings.

The exposition: as for the "ornament" of the Mahāyāna sūtras and treatises — who is able to ornament it? Answer: the wisdom of meaning is able to ornament it. Question: how does the wisdom of meaning ornament it? Answer: by opening up and bringing forth all its meanings. Question: by what means does it open and bring them forth? Answer: by words and by phrases. Question: with what kind of words, with what kind of phrases? Answer: with stainless words and stainless phrases. Stainless words are those able to reach the city of nirvāṇa. Stainless phrases are those in which the letters and the phrasing correspond to one another. Apart from stainless words and phrases, one is unable to open up and make clear the various meanings.

Question: for the sake of what meaning does it ornament? Answer: in order to rescue and save suffering sentient beings. Question: sentient beings suffer of their own accord — for what reason should they be rescued? Answer: because for the bodhisattva great compassion is his very substance, and pity arises in him. Question: if it rescues the suffering of others, what dharma does it ornament? Answer: it ornaments the Tathāgata's skillfully taught method of skillful means. Question: what method of skillful means is this? Answer: it is called the supreme vehicle. Question: for whose sake does it ornament? Answer: for the sake of those who arouse the mind of the Great Vehicle. Question: by how many meanings does it ornament? Answer: it is shown in brief by five meanings. Question: what are the five meanings?

it is like a flower just now in bloom,
it is like eating a delicious meal,
it is like understanding a piece of writing,

Question: what is their meaning? Answer: the simile of gold made into an ornament is so that one has faith and turns toward it, in that it transforms that person's mind. The simile of the flower in bloom is so that one receives its teaching, in that it opens and displays it to him. The simile of the delicious meal is so that one reflects upon it, in that one obtains the taste of the Dharma. The simile of understanding a piece of writing is so that one cultivates it, in that one no longer needs to deliberate over it. The simile of opening the jeweled casket is so that one realizes the true jewel that is the factor of enlightenment, in that one awakens to and realizes it oneself. By these five meanings the Great Vehicle is distinguished, and this is able to cause that person to give rise to love and delight in it. 釋曰：譬如美質加莊像現在於鏡，則生勝喜。何以故？為有悅故。菩薩亦爾，莊嚴妙法義入自心，則生勝喜。何以故？為有聞故。

問：彼法有何功德須此莊嚴，強欲令他恭敬信受耶？

譬如飲藥苦，　病差則為樂；

住文及解義，　苦樂亦如是。

譬如難事王，　因事得威力；

如是難解義，　因解得法財。

譬如見生寶，　不別則不愛；

如是聞妙法，　不覺亦不喜。

釋曰：此三偈次第顯示妙法有三功德，一顯斷障因功德、二顯自在因功德、三顯妙喜因功德。問：此義云何？答：如飲苦藥，初時則苦，以難服故；後時則樂，以病差故。此法亦爾，住文時苦，味難得故；解義時樂，障病破故。如事嚴王，初時則苦，難得意故；後時則樂，與威力故。此法亦爾，思惟時苦，深難解故；思度時樂，長聖財故。如見生寶，未別時則不愛，謂無用故；識別時則深重，知有用故。此法亦爾，修行時則不喜，謂空無用故；修度時則深悅，知有大用故。緣起品究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like an already beautiful form,
adorned, that looking in a mirror gives rise to surpassing joy —
the wondrous Dharma, once ornamented,
obtains a joy that is foremost still.

The exposition: it is like a beautiful form, adorned, whose reflection appears in a mirror — this gives rise to surpassing joy. Why is this? Because there is delight in it. It is the same for the bodhisattva: when the meaning of the ornamented wondrous Dharma enters his own mind, surpassing joy arises. Why is this? Because there is the hearing of it.

Question: what merit does that Dharma have, that it needs this ornamenting, as though one wished to force others into reverent faith and acceptance?

It is like drinking a bitter medicine —
once the illness is cured, it becomes a joy;
dwelling in the letter, and understanding the meaning,
are bitter and joyful in just this way.

It is like the difficulty of serving a stern king —
through that service one gains authority and power;
just so, the difficulty of understanding the meaning —
through that understanding one gains the wealth of the Dharma.

It is like seeing a rough, uncut gem —
without discerning it, one does not love it;
just so, hearing the wondrous Dharma —
without awareness of it, one does not delight in it.

The exposition: these three verses, in order, display three merits of the wondrous Dharma: first, the merit that is the cause of cutting off obstructions; second, the merit that is the cause of mastery; and third, the merit that is the cause of wondrous joy. Question: what is their meaning? Answer: it is like drinking a bitter medicine — at first it is bitter, since it is hard to swallow; afterward it is a joy, since the illness is cured. This Dharma is just the same: dwelling in the letter is bitter, since its flavor is hard to obtain; understanding its meaning is a joy, since the obstruction and the illness are broken. It is like serving a stern king — at first it is bitter, since it is hard to win his favor; afterward it is a joy, since he grants authority and power. This Dharma is just the same: reflecting upon it is bitter, since it is profound and hard to understand; weighing it thoroughly is a joy, since it lengthens one's noble wealth. It is like seeing a rough, uncut gem — before it is discerned one does not love it, since it seems to have no use; once it is recognized one values it deeply, knowing it has use. This Dharma is just the same: in the course of practice one does not delight in it, since it seems empty and useless; once one has weighed and measured its practice, one delights in it deeply, knowing it has great use. The chapter on the origin is complete.

Reading note

莊嚴, "ornament," is doing more work here than the English cognate suggests. An ornament in this treatise's own account is not applied to something already complete for the sake of appearance; each of the five similes names a stage of actually taking hold of the teaching — faith, reception, reflection, cultivation, realization — and the ornament is whatever brings that stage about. The mirror simile in the second set makes the same point from the opposite direction: a beautiful face doesn't need the mirror to be beautiful, but the joy of seeing it needs the mirror to arise at all. The treatise's premise is that the Dharma is like the face and the ornamenting exposition is like the mirror — necessary not for the thing to be true, but for a joy in the thing to be possible for anyone else.

The three bitter-then-sweet similes are worth reading as a warning built into the treatise's own opening pages. Anyone who takes up a text this dense should expect exactly the sequence described: hard going at the level of the letter, harder still at the level of working out what it means, and only then — not before — the payoff. The treatise is telling its reader, before a single doctrine has been argued, not to mistake early difficulty for a sign that the difficulty won't resolve.

2. Establishing the Tenet: Eight Causes That Prove the Mahāyāna Is the Buddha's Word

Chinese Source

釋曰：有人疑此大乘非佛所說，云何有此功德可得？我今決彼疑網，成立大乘真是佛說。

偈曰：

不記亦同行，　不行亦成就，

體、非體、能治、　文異八因成。

釋曰：成立大乘，略有八因；一者不記、二者同行、三者不行、四者成就、五者體、六者非體、七者能治、八者文異。第一不記者，先法已盡後佛正出，若此大乘非是正法，何故世尊初不記耶？譬如未來有異，世尊即記。此不記故，知是佛說。第二同行者，聲聞乘與大乘非先非後一時同行，汝云何知此大乘獨非佛說？第三不行者，大乘深廣，非忖度人之所能信，況復能行外道制諸論，彼種不可得，是故不行。由彼不行，故是佛說。第四成就者，若汝言餘得菩提者說有大乘，非是今佛說有大乘。若作此執，則反成我義。彼得菩提，亦即是佛，如是說故。第五體者，若汝言餘佛有大乘體，此佛無大乘體。若作此執，亦成我義。大乘無異，體是一故。第六非體者，若汝言此佛無大乘體，則聲聞乘亦無體。若汝言聲聞乘是佛說故有體，大乘非佛說故無體。若作此執，有大過失。若無佛乘而有佛出說聲聞乘者，理不應故。第七能治者，由依此法修行得無分別智，由無分別智能破諸煩惱。由此因故，不得言無大乘。第八文異者，大乘甚深非如文義，不應一向隨文取義，言非佛語。

Ru-Yi Translation

The exposition: there are those who doubt that this Mahāyāna was spoken by the Buddha, asking: how could it then possess such merit? I shall now resolve the net of their doubt, and establish that the Mahāyāna is truly the Buddha's word.

Not predicted, and practiced together;
not practiced, and yet accomplished;
substance, non-substance, the remedy,
and difference of text — eight causes establish it.

The exposition: to establish the Mahāyāna, there are in brief eight causes: first, not predicted; second, practiced together; third, not practiced; fourth, accomplished; fifth, substance; sixth, non-substance; seventh, the remedy; and eighth, difference of text. First, not predicted: when an earlier teaching has already run its course, a later Buddha properly appears. If this Mahāyāna were not the true Dharma, why did the Bhagavat not predict it at the outset, as he does predict some future difference when one is to arise? Because he did not predict it, one knows it is the Buddha's own word. Second, practiced together: the śrāvaka vehicle and the Mahāyāna have been practiced together at one and the same time, neither before nor after the other — how then would you know that this Mahāyāna alone was not spoken by the Buddha? Third, not practiced: the Mahāyāna is profound and vast, not something a person of mere speculation is able to have faith in, how much less able to practice; were it a treatise fabricated by outsiders, that seed of theirs could not be obtained, and so it is not practiced by them — and because it is not practiced by them, it must be the Buddha's word. Fourth, accomplished: if you say that it was some other being who attained enlightenment who taught that there is a Mahāyāna, and not this present Buddha — if you hold to this, it in fact accomplishes my own thesis instead, for that being who attained enlightenment is himself just a buddha, since it is said in just this way. Fifth, substance: if you say that other buddhas possess the substance of the Mahāyāna but this buddha does not — if you hold to this, it too accomplishes my own thesis, for the Mahāyāna is not different from buddha to buddha; its substance is one. Sixth, non-substance: if you say that this buddha has no substance of the Mahāyāna, then the śrāvaka vehicle likewise would have no substance; and if you say instead that the śrāvaka vehicle, being spoken by the Buddha, has substance, while the Mahāyāna, not being spoken by the Buddha, has none — if you hold to this, it is a grave error, for it does not stand to reason that there should be no buddha-vehicle at all, and yet a buddha appear and teach the śrāvaka vehicle. Seventh, the remedy: by practicing in reliance on this Dharma one obtains non-discriminating wisdom, and by non-discriminating wisdom one is able to destroy the afflictions — for this reason, one cannot say there is no Mahāyāna. Eighth, difference of text: the Mahāyāna is exceedingly profound and is not as its literal wording suggests; one should not, simply following the letter, seize upon its apparent sense and say it is not the Buddha's word.

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, if you say that as for the first, not predicted — this is because the Buddha, with a mind free of exertion, simply set it aside — if you hold to this, the meaning does not follow.

The buddhas, for three reasons —
present seeing, and guarding the Dharma,
and the Tathāgata's unobstructed wisdom —
could not simply have set it aside, as you suppose.

The exposition: if this Mahāyāna were not spoken by the Buddha, that would be a grave obstruction indeed. The buddhas have three reasons why they could not have failed to predict it: first, effortless wisdom constantly arises in them — that eye of theirs constantly sees; second, they constantly exert right diligence, guarding and protecting the true Dharma; and third, the Tathāgata's power of wisdom is without any obstruction. For these three reasons, your claim that he set it aside and did not predict it does not stand to reason. Furthermore, if you say that as for substance, it is the śrāvaka vehicle that is the substance of the Mahāyāna, since it is by this very vehicle that great enlightenment is attained — if you hold to this, the meaning does not follow.

偈曰：

釋曰：有四因緣，非即以聲聞乘為大乘體，非全故、非不違故、非行故、非教授故。非全者，聲聞乘無有利他教授，但為自厭離欲解脫而教授故。非不違者，若言聲聞乘以自方便而教授他，即是他利教授。是義不然。何以故？雖以自利安他，彼亦自求涅槃勤行方便，不可以此得大菩提故。非行者，若汝言若能久行聲聞乘行則得大菩提果。是義不然，非方便故。聲聞乘非大菩提方便，不以久行，非方便能得大乘果。譬如搆角求乳，不可得故。非教授者，如大乘教授，聲聞乘無。是故聲聞乘不得即是大乘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not the whole, not without conflict,
not the practice, not the instruction —
for these reasons the śrāvaka vehicle
is not, after all, the very substance of the Mahāyāna.

The exposition: there are four reasons why the śrāvaka vehicle is not the very substance of the Mahāyāna: it is not the whole, it is not free of conflict, it is not the practice, and it is not the instruction. Not the whole: the śrāvaka vehicle has no instruction that benefits others; it instructs only for one's own weariness, freedom from desire, and liberation. Not free of conflict: if it is said that the śrāvaka vehicle, by its own methods, instructs others, and that this is therefore an instruction that benefits others — this does not follow, because although by benefiting himself he brings ease to others, that practitioner is himself still seeking nirvāṇa and diligently applying methods for his own sake; one cannot, by this, attain great enlightenment. Not the practice: if you say that by practicing the conduct of the śrāvaka vehicle for a long time one attains the fruit of great enlightenment — this does not follow, since it is not the right method; the śrāvaka vehicle is not a method for great enlightenment, and it is not by long practice, when it is the wrong method, that one attains the fruit of the Mahāyāna, any more than milk can be obtained by milking a horn. Not the instruction: the instruction of the Mahāyāna is something the śrāvaka vehicle lacks entirely. For these reasons the śrāvaka vehicle cannot be said to be the very substance of the Mahāyāna.

Reading note

The eight causes are not a uniform set of arguments; they mix genuinely independent evidence (practiced together, not practiced by outsiders) with a sharper kind of move that this treatise reaches for repeatedly — taking an opponent's own premise and showing it proves the thesis it was meant to deny. The fourth and fifth causes both work this way: grant, for the sake of argument, that the Mahāyāna belongs to some other buddha rather than to the historical Buddha, or that it belongs to buddhas in general but not to this one — either concession already presupposes that a buddha teaches the Mahāyāna, which is the very point at issue. The opponent cannot state the objection without already conceding the conclusion. This is the treatise's characteristic apologetic style: not accumulating probability but closing off every escape route one at a time.

搆角求乳 — milking a horn in search of milk — is the sharpest image in the passage refuting the śrāvaka path as a route to buddhahood, and it is worth noticing exactly what it targets. The argument is not that long practice of the wrong thing yields too little; it is that no amount of it yields anything at all, because a horn was never the kind of thing that gives milk. Applied to the śrāvaka path, the claim is equally absolute: not that the śrāvaka vehicle is a slower road to the same destination, but that persistence along it cannot arrive there by definition, since its own aim — one's own liberation, not that of others — points somewhere else from the start.

3. Five Differences, Three Marks, and the True Method to Enlightenment

Chinese Source

復次今更示汝相違義。

偈曰：

發心與教授，　方便及住持，

時節下上乘，　五事一切異。

釋曰：聲聞乘與大乘有五種相違，一發心異、二教授異、三方便異、四住持異、五時節異。聲聞乘若發心、若教授、若勤方便，皆為自得涅槃故。住持亦少，福智聚小故。時節亦少，乃至三生得解脫故。大乘不爾，發心、教授、勤方便皆為利他故。住持亦多，福智聚大故。時節亦多，經三大阿僧祇劫故。如是一切相違，是故不應以小乘行而得大乘果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, I shall now show you further the sense in which they conflict.

The arousing of the mind, and the instruction,
the method, and the sustaining,
and the span of time — in the lesser and the higher vehicle,
in these five matters, everything differs.

The exposition: the śrāvaka vehicle and the Mahāyāna conflict in five ways: the arousing of the mind differs, the instruction differs, the method differs, the sustaining differs, and the span of time differs. In the śrāvaka vehicle, whether it is the arousing of the mind, the instruction, or the diligent application of method, all of these are for the sake of attaining nirvāṇa for oneself alone. Its sustaining, too, is slight, since its accumulation of merit and wisdom is small. Its span of time, too, is short, since liberation is attained in as few as three lifetimes. The Mahāyāna is not like this: the arousing of the mind, the instruction, and the diligent application of method are all for the sake of benefiting others. Its sustaining, too, is great, since its accumulation of merit and wisdom is vast. Its span of time, too, is long, since it passes through three great incalculable eons. Since they conflict in all these ways, one should not expect to attain the fruit of the Mahāyāna by practicing the conduct of the Lesser Vehicle.

Reading note

The three marks of Buddha-word — entering the sūtras, being displayed in the vinaya, not conflicting with the emptiness of things — are a standard of the objector's own choosing, and the treatise's reply is to accept the standard rather than dispute it. This is a different tactic from the eight causes: instead of showing the objection self-defeating, it simply meets the bar on its own terms, three for three. The Mahāyāna's own emptiness of things (法空) is offered as satisfying exactly the third mark that the objector thought would rule the Mahāyāna out.

非依語教而生，是依證智而生 — the distinction between a wisdom that has a support and one that does not runs through this whole passage and resurfaces later in the treatise's account of the lineage: 有依者智依教生，非證智故. A wisdom built on instruction can always be argued with, since it rests on what someone else said; a realizational wisdom has nowhere for the argument to get a grip. The five reasons given for why mere speculation cannot reach the Mahāyāna are, read together, a portrait of what philosophical argument alone can and cannot do — it can go as far as the conventional truth, and it runs out exactly where the ultimate begins.

"If there is a difference, then it is the perilous place" means: if you grant that there is a substance of the Mahāyāna distinct from the others, then this substance is itself the path of omniscient wisdom — the foremost of perilous places, being difficult to cross, and one should accept this on faith and look up to it; for what reason should one fear? "It has no analogy" means: there is no second Mahāyāna appearing at the same time to which it might be compared — why then fear the one, when one would not fear a second? "It is taught in manifold ways" means: this Mahāyāna does not teach emptiness alone, but also teaches the great accumulation of merit and wisdom; one should understand this intent — why fear emptiness alone? "It is taught in continuity" means: at all times it consistently and continuously teaches emptiness, and this is not something you have just now heard for the first time — why then fear it? "It is taught through many gates" means: in sūtra after sūtra it is taught differently through many gates, displaying its great essential function, breaking apart all discrimination, and yielding non-discriminating wisdom; were there no great function apart from this, the Tathāgata would only need to speak of emptiness, and would not speak of suchness, the nature of things, the true limit, and so on — since it is taught that there are many gates, why fear emptiness alone? "It is not as its literal wording suggests" means: the Mahāyāna is profound and is not as its literal wording suggests — why, then, following the letter and seizing on its apparent sense, fear its emptiness? "The buddhas' body is exceedingly profound" means: buddha-nature is profound and difficult to awaken to and recognize all at once; one should seek to understand it clearly — why fear it? Reading note

The four causes of fear anticipate the whole fourth chapter of this fascicle before it has been announced: 非性 — lacking the lineage — is named first among them, and the treatise will spend the rest of this fascicle arguing that the lineage is a real, observable difference among beings rather than a polite fiction. Fear of the teaching is being diagnosed here as a symptom with a cause, not a reasonable response to something genuinely dangerous — which sets up the sharper claim later that some beings, described at length in the section on the lineage below, do not merely fear the Mahāyāna but structurally cannot yet take it up.

The seven-part refutation of fear (無異即互無 through 諸佛甚深體) is built as a dilemma with no comfortable branch: either the śrāvaka vehicle just is the Mahāyāna, in which case there is nothing distinct to fear, or it is genuinely different, in which case what one fears is the very path of omniscience, which is a reason for reverence rather than dread. Every subsequent point in the list closes off some other way of reading the fear as sensible — "you've heard this before," "it isn't just emptiness," "the wording isn't literal" — until nothing is left standing except, in the treatise's own diagnosis, the lack of the lineage itself.

Reading note

由汝不解故，成我無上乘 is the boldest turn in the whole chapter: the opponent's own confession of incomprehension is offered back as proof of the very thesis he was disputing. It only works because the treatise has spent the entire chapter establishing, independently, that the Mahāyāna is meant to exceed ordinary speculative reach — 有依、不定、緣俗、不普、退屈 from the earlier section. Given that setup, failing to understand is not neutral evidence; on the treatise's own terms it is close to what one would expect to find in something genuinely unsurpassed. Note that this move only has force against an opponent who has already been walked through the argument that speculative wisdom is bounded — used on its own it would just be an unfalsifiable dodge, and the chapter has been careful not to rely on it until everything else is in place.

The closing sequence — warding off slander, wrong reflection, and finally ill will — narrows in scope at each step and ends on the smallest object: 惡意自性惡, ill will is evil by its own nature, full stop, before it has done anything to anyone. The chapter that spent its opening half proving a metaphysical thesis through eight independent arguments ends by simply asking the reader not to hate what he does not yet understand. That is a modest place to land after such an ambitious beginning, and the modesty looks deliberate: having shown that disbelief is forgivable and even useful, the treatise draws the line only at the malice that sometimes accompanies it.

Reading note

The four-part image of good birth — bodhicitta as seed, the perfection of wisdom as mother, the twin accumulations as womb, great compassion as wet-nurse — assigns the generative role to wisdom and the nurturing role to compassion, which is worth noticing rather than passing over as decorative. In this treatise's own vocabulary the mother is not a passive vessel; 般若波羅蜜為生母 makes wisdom the active parent, the thing that actually gives rise to the bodhisattva, while compassion's role is to sustain what wisdom has already produced. The pairing recurs constantly in this Yogācāra corpus in less figurative language — wisdom cuts through, compassion carries forward — but rarely as vividly as here.

The similes shift from family (seed, mother, womb, wet-nurse) to court (prince, then great minister) across these three verses, and the shift is not accidental: each stage widens the bodhisattva's field of action outward, from what he is made of, to what he will inherit, to what he is entrusted to do on behalf of others while he waits to inherit it. The minister's four merits are all about access and discretion — entering the forbidden palace, keeping the king's secret words — which fits a bodhisattva's actual situation better than the prince's four, which are about succession. One is a description of eventual status; the other, of daily conduct in the meantime.

Reading note

The eight fruits are staged along the whole of the path rather than clustered at its end — merit at the very first moment of faith, reverence at the arousing of bodhicitta, joy at each intentional rebirth, and only much later the two culminating fruits at buddhahood itself. Reading them in order is a corrective to any picture of the bodhisattva path as pure deferral, all cost now for a payoff later; the treatise's own accounting has something being gained at nearly every stage, long before enlightenment is reached.

有捨、滅捨 — the abandoning that is not abiding in saṃsāra, and the abandoning that is not abiding in nirvāṇa — names, in eight characters, what this site's other Yogācāra pages call non-abiding nirvāṇa (無住涅槃), discussed at length in the Mahāyānasaṃgraha's treatment of the fruit of relinquishment. It is worth noting that this treatise reaches the same doctrine by a different route — not as the resolution of a technical problem about how a buddha's mind operates, but as the eighth and final fruit of an act as simple as taking refuge. The technical doctrine and the devotional starting point turn out to be describing the same destination.

The marks of the lineage having been stated, next the classes of the lineage are explained.

The fixed, and the unfixed;
the non-retrogressing, or the falling away —
according, in due order, to the conditions one meets,
the classes are of four kinds.

The exposition: in brief, the classes of the bodhisattva lineage are of four kinds, in order: first, the fixed; second, the unfixed; third, the non-retrogressing; and fourth, the falling away. The fixed do not retrogress whatever conditions they meet. The unfixed fall away depending on the conditions they meet.

Reading note

The four-part proof of the lineage's existence is an argument from observed difference rather than from scripture alone: sentient beings visibly differ in what they have faith in, in whether their practice advances, and in the level of enlightenment they reach, and the treatise's claim is simply that these differences need a cause, and that a difference in the underlying lineage is the only cause that fits all four patterns at once. 若無性差別，則亦無信差別 is repeated with only its noun changed across all four sub-arguments — this is a single argument form applied four times, not four separate proofs, and its force depends entirely on there being no better explanation available for why practitioners of apparently equal effort and instruction still diverge.

性種 and 習種 — the innate lineage and the habituated lineage — set up a distinction this treatise will keep returning to but never fully resolve in this fascicle: is the potential for buddhahood something a being already has, prior to any practice, or something built up through habituation over time? The treatise's own answer refuses to choose only one: 彼有者，因體有故；非有者，果體非有故 — the lineage as cause already exists, but the lineage as fruit does not yet exist. Read this way, "nature" here names neither a fixed essence nor a pure construction, but a real capacity for a merit not yet brought forth — 功德度義, the sense of bringing forth merit, which is closer to a verb than a noun.

9. Faults, Merits, the Two Similes, and Those Without the Lineage

Chinese Source

習惑與惡友，　貧窮屬他故。

偈曰：

功德亦四種，　雖墮於惡道，

釋曰：菩薩種性雖有如前過失，若墮惡道，應知於中復有四種功德：一者遲入，不數墮故。二者速出，不久住故。三者苦薄，逼惱輕故。四者悲深，哀愍眾生亦成就故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The classes of the lineage having been stated, next the faults of the lineage are explained.

It should be known that the bodhisattva lineage
has, in brief, four faults:
habituation to affliction, and evil companions,
poverty, and being subject to another.

The exposition: in brief, the faults of the bodhisattva lineage are of four kinds: first, habituation to affliction, in that one fails to practice the qualities and instead practices the afflictions to excess; second, evil companions, in that one is apart from good spiritual friends and keeps company with corrupt people; third, poverty, in that all the necessary provisions are wanting and scarce; and fourth, being subject to another, in that one is bound and attached to another person, without mastery of oneself.

The faults of the lineage having been stated, next the merits of the lineage are explained.

Its merits are likewise fourfold:
though one may fall into an evil destiny,
one enters it late and quickly emerges,
one's suffering is slight, and one's compassion runs deep.

The exposition: although the bodhisattva lineage has the faults just described, should it fall into an evil destiny, one should know that even there it possesses four further merits: first, entering late, in that one does not fall there often; second, emerging quickly, in that one does not remain there long; third, slight suffering, in that the affliction and torment there are light; and fourth, deep compassion, in that even there one's pity for sentient beings remains accomplished.

The merits of the lineage having been stated, next the simile of gold for the lineage is explained.

It is like the nature of superior gold,
whose yield is of four kinds —
all manner of good, and all manner of wisdom,
all manner of purity, and all manner of supernormal power.

The exposition: what superior gold yields has four aspects — it is exceedingly abundant, it is radiant, it is free of impurity, and it is malleable. It is the same for the bodhisattva lineage: first, it is the support for measureless wholesome roots; second, it is the support for measureless wisdom; third, it is the support for the purification of all afflictive and cognitive obstructions; and fourth, it is the support for every kind of supernormal power and transformation.

The simile of gold for the lineage having been stated, next the simile of the jewel for the lineage is explained.

It is like the nature of a wondrous jewel,
which is the cause of four accomplishments —
great fruit, and great wisdom,
great concentration, and great significance.

The exposition: the nature of a wondrous jewel is the support for four accomplishments — the accomplishment of its genuineness, of its color, of its shape, and of its size. It is the same for the bodhisattva lineage: first, it is the cause of great enlightenment; second, it is the cause of great wisdom; third, it is the cause of great concentration, concentration being what makes the mind abide steadily; and fourth, it is the cause of great significance, in that it brings boundless sentient beings to maturity.

釋曰：無般涅槃法者，是無性位。此略有二種：一者時邊般涅槃法、二者畢竟無涅槃法。時邊般涅槃法者，有四種人：一者一向行惡行、二者普斷諸善法、三者無解脫分善根、四者善根不具足。畢竟無涅槃法者，無因故，彼無般涅槃性，此謂但求生死、不樂涅槃人。

偈曰：

廣演深大法，　令信令極忍，

釋曰：廣演深大法者，為利他故。謂無智者，令得大信。已大信者，令成就極忍，能行不退。已極忍者，令究竟成就無上菩提。二知者，謂諸凡夫及諸聲聞。若得如是，彼諸二人則知自性性德圓滿性最為殊勝。

偈曰：

增長菩提樹，　生樂及滅苦，

自他利為果，　此勝如吉根。

釋曰。如是種性能增長極廣功德大菩提樹、能得大樂、能滅大苦、能得自他利樂以為大果，是故此性最為第一，譬如吉祥樹根。菩薩種性亦爾。種性品究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

The stage of possessing the lineage having now been distinguished at length, next the stage of lacking the lineage is distinguished.

Practicing only evil conduct,
universally cutting off all wholesome dharmas,
having no portion of liberation,
and having little good and no cause for it.

The exposition: those without the dharma of final nirvāṇa are those in the stage of lacking the lineage. This is, in brief, of two kinds: first, those whose final nirvāṇa is temporarily deferred, and second, those whose dharma of nirvāṇa is ultimately absent altogether. Those whose final nirvāṇa is temporarily deferred are of four kinds of persons: first, those who practice only evil conduct; second, those who have universally cut off all wholesome dharmas; third, those who have no wholesome root that is a portion of liberation; and fourth, those whose wholesome roots are incomplete. Those whose nirvāṇa is ultimately absent altogether are without the cause for it, and so are without the nature of final nirvāṇa at all — this refers to persons who seek only saṃsāra and take no delight in nirvāṇa.

The absence of the lineage having been stated, next the leading of beings into it is explained.

Widely proclaiming the profound and great Dharma,
bringing beings to faith, and to utmost patience,
and finally to great enlightenment —
by this, the two who come to know recognize the supremacy of the two natures.

The exposition: widely proclaiming the profound and great Dharma is done for the sake of benefiting others. That is, one who lacks wisdom is brought to attain great faith; one who already has great faith is brought to accomplish utmost patience, able to practice without retrogression; and one who already has utmost patience is brought to the final accomplishment of unsurpassed enlightenment. The two who come to know refers to ordinary beings and to śrāvakas. Once they attain this, these two kinds of persons come to know that the own-nature and the perfected quality of this nature's own merit are of the most surpassing kind.

Question: in what way is it supreme?

It makes the tree of enlightenment grow tall,
it brings forth joy and extinguishes suffering,
and the benefit of both self and other is its fruit —
this supremacy is like an auspicious root.

The exposition: this lineage is able to make the great tree of enlightenment, vast with merit, grow tall; it is able to bring forth great joy; it is able to extinguish great suffering; and it is able to obtain the benefit and joy of both self and other as its great fruit. For this reason this nature is foremost of all, like the root of an auspicious tree. The bodhisattva lineage is just the same. The chapter on the lineage is complete.

Reading note

The stage of lacking the lineage (無性位) is stated here with unusual bluntness for a treatise that has spent two chapters arguing that fear of the Mahāyāna is unwarranted and correctable. The treatise itself distinguishes two very different claims and is careful not to blur them: 時邊般涅槃法, those whose final nirvāṇa is merely deferred by a run of bad conduct, negligible wholesome roots, or incomplete practice — all conditions that could in principle change — and 畢竟無涅槃法, those who are without the cause for nirvāṇa altogether, described not as incapable but as simply not wanting it, 但求生死、不樂涅槃. This is one version of the doctrine later Yogācāra literature systematizes as the icchantika, and it remained genuinely contested within the tradition for centuries after this treatise; the text here states it as a bare classification within the nine topics rather than arguing for it at length, and does not reconcile it explicitly with the universal buddha-nature taught in other Mahāyāna sources. Readers encountering the doctrine for the first time here should know that its scope and even its permanence were themselves live questions among later commentators, not a settled matter this treatise alone determines.

The gold and jewel similes, read side by side, divide the lineage's contribution in two: gold names what the lineage produces in abundance (善、智、淨、通— every wholesome thing, without limit), while the jewel names what the lineage makes possible in kind rather than quantity (大果、大智、大定、大義 — specific, singular accomplishments of the highest grade). Gold is a metaphor for output; the jewel is a metaphor for capacity. Together they cover the two things a cause needs to explain about its effects — how much, and how good — and the chapter's closing image, the auspicious tree, folds both back into a single organic figure: a root that determines both how large the tree grows and what kind of fruit it bears.

Fascicle 1 Complete

Four chapters, and the treatise's whole opening case. The origin chapter explains what "ornament" means and why a true teaching still benefits from skillful exposition. The chapter establishing the tenet answers the era's live objection — is this really the Buddha's word? — with eight independent causes, several of which trap the objection in its own premises, and closes by naming fear, slander, wrong reflection, and ill will as the real obstacles once the doubt itself has been addressed. The chapter on refuge explains why the Mahāyāna refuge exceeds the others in four ways, cast partly in the language of family (seed, mother, womb, wet-nurse) and partly in the language of court (prince, minister), and ending in eight fruits that begin at the very first moment of faith. And the chapter on the lineage lays out all nine of its topics — existence, supremacy, own-nature, marks, classes, faults, merits, and two similes — before closing on the treatise's least comfortable claim: that some beings, on its own account, do not yet have the cause for liberation at all. Fascicle 2 opens the chapter on arousing the mind of enlightenment (發心品), the practice by which the lineage described here first becomes active.
