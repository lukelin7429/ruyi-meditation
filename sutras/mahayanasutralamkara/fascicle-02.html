Arousing the Mind, the Twofold Benefit, Reality, Supernormal Power, and Maturing Beings

Five chapters carry the bodhisattva from the lineage described in fascicle 1 into active practice. The chapter on arousing the mind gives its four greatnesses, its four grounds of difference, an eleven-part anatomy running from root to culmination, and twenty-two similes for what the aroused mind is like — earth, gold, fire, ocean, a wish-fulfilling gem. The chapter on the twofold benefit argues that self and other, once seen truly, are without difference, and then argues in the next breath that benefiting others is still supreme. The chapter on reality states the fivefold character of the ultimate — neither existent nor non-existent, neither identical nor different — and maps the five stages by which a practitioner comes to see it directly. The chapter on supernormal power catalogues six kinds of knowledge and what equips them. And the chapter on maturing beings gives nine factors of self-maturation, each analyzed into cause, substance, and function, before turning outward to how the six perfections mature others across this life and the next.

Where This Fascicle Picks Up

Fascicle 1 established that the Mahāyāna is the Buddha's word, that its refuge is foremost, and that the bodhisattva lineage is a real and observable distinction among beings — closing on the uncomfortable admission that some beings do not yet have it. This fascicle assumes the lineage is present and asks what it does: how the mind of enlightenment is aroused from it, how that arousing plays out as conduct that benefits self and other without distinction between them, what reality actually is once it is seen without distortion, what supernormal power a bodhisattva develops along the way, and how a bodhisattva matures both himself and the beings around him.

1. Arousing the Mind: Its Marks, and Its Differences by Ground

Chinese Source

釋曰：如是已分別菩薩種性，次分別菩薩發菩提心相。

偈曰：

勇猛及方便，　利益及出離，

四大三功德，　二義故心起。

釋曰：菩薩發心，有四種大：一勇猛大，謂弘誓精進甚深難作，長時隨順故。二方便大，謂被弘誓鉀已，恒時方便勤精進故。三利益大，謂一切時作自他利故。四出離大，謂為求無上菩提故。復次此四種大，顯示三種功德。第一第二大顯示作丈夫所作功德，第三大顯示作大義功德，第四大顯示受果功德。此三功德以二義為緣，所謂無上菩提及一切眾生，由此思故發菩提心。已說發心相，次說發心差別。

偈曰：

信行與淨依，　報得及無障，

發心依諸地，　差別有四種。

釋曰：菩薩發心，依諸地有四種差別：一信行發心，謂信行地。二淨依發心，謂前七地。三報得發心，謂後三地。四無障發心，謂如來地。已說差別，次當廣釋。問：如此發心，以何為根？何所依止？何所信？何所緣？何所乘？何所住？何等障難？何等功德？何等自性？何所出離？何處究竟？

Ru-Yi Translation

The exposition: the bodhisattva lineage having thus been distinguished, next the mark of the bodhisattva's arousing the mind of enlightenment is distinguished.

Valor, and skillful means,
benefit, and renunciation —
four greatnesses, three merits,
and two objects: by these the mind arises.

The exposition: the bodhisattva's arousing of the mind has four greatnesses: first, the greatness of valor, in that his vast vow and diligence are exceedingly profound and difficult to accomplish, and he follows through with them over a long time. Second, the greatness of skillful means, in that having donned the armor of his vast vow, he constantly and diligently applies skillful means. Third, the greatness of benefit, in that at all times he acts for the benefit of both himself and others. Fourth, the greatness of renunciation, in that he seeks unsurpassed enlightenment. Furthermore, these four greatnesses display three merits: the first and second display the merit of doing what a great man does; the third displays the merit of accomplishing great significance; and the fourth displays the merit of receiving the fruit. These three merits take two objects as their condition — unsurpassed enlightenment, and all sentient beings — and it is by reflecting on these that the mind of enlightenment is aroused. The mark of arousing the mind having been stated, next the distinctions within it are explained.

By faith and practice, and by the pure support,
by the ripened attainment, and by the unobstructed —
arousing the mind, in dependence on the grounds,
has four kinds of distinction.

The exposition: the bodhisattva's arousing of the mind, in dependence on the grounds, has four distinctions: first, arousing the mind by faith and practice, which is the ground of faith and practice; second, by the pure support, which is the first seven grounds; third, by the ripened attainment, which is the last three grounds; and fourth, without obstruction, which is the ground of the Tathāgata. The distinctions having been stated, they shall now be explained at length. Question: as for this arousing of the mind — what is its root? What is its support? What does it have faith in? What does it take as its object? What vehicle does it ride? Where does it abide? What is its obstacle and difficulty? What is its merit? What is its own-nature? What is its renunciation? Where does it culminate?

偈曰：

大悲與利物，　大法將種智，

勝欲亦大護，　受障及增善，

福智與修度，　及以地地滿，

初根至後竟，　隨次解應知。

釋曰：菩薩發心以大悲為根。以利物為依止。以大乘法為所信。以種智為所緣，為求彼故。以勝欲為所乘，欲無上乘故。以大護為所住，住菩薩戒故。以受障為難，起異乘心故。以增善為功德。以福智為自性。以習諸度為出離。以地滿為究竟，由地地勤方便與彼彼相應故。如此已廣分別，次說受世俗發心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Great compassion, and benefiting beings;
the great Dharma, and omniscience;
supreme aspiration, and great protection;
receiving obstruction, and increasing good;
merit and wisdom, and cultivating the perfections;
and the fulfillment ground by ground —
from the first, the root, to the last, the culmination,
in this order they should be understood.

The exposition: the bodhisattva's arousing of the mind has great compassion as its root; benefiting beings as its support; the Mahāyāna Dharma as what it has faith in; omniscience as its object, since it is sought for its sake; supreme aspiration as its vehicle, since it aspires to the unsurpassed vehicle; great protection as its abode, in that it abides in the bodhisattva precepts; receiving obstruction as its difficulty, in that a mind for a different vehicle may arise; increasing good as its merit; merit and wisdom as its own-nature; the cultivation of the perfections as its renunciation; and the fulfillment ground by ground as its culmination, since it diligently applies skillful means and corresponds to each ground in turn. This having now been distinguished at length, next receiving the conventional arousing of the mind is explained.

Reading note

大悲為根 — great compassion as the root of arousing the mind — is worth pausing on, since much of this treatise's Yogācāra apparatus elsewhere gives wisdom pride of place. Here the eleven-fold anatomy runs from compassion at the root through omniscience as the object sought and finally to the ground-by-ground fulfillment as culmination — wisdom appears in the sequence, but as what is aimed at and cultivated, not as what starts the process. The treatise's own ordering suggests that for a Mahāyāna text organized around the bodhisattva rather than the arhat, the motivating cause has to be something that already includes other beings within it, and only compassion does that from the very first moment.

The four grounds of difference (信行, 淨依, 報得, 無障) quietly compress the entire bodhisattva path into four stages: the ground of faith and practice before the path of seeing, the first seven grounds where the support is "pure" but not yet effortless, the last three grounds where enlightenment's fruits begin to ripen of themselves, and the stage of a buddha where nothing obstructs it further. The treatise will spend the rest of this fascicle, and much of the fascicles that follow, filling in what happens at each of these stages — this verse is the outline the later material is hung on.

2. Conventional and Ultimate Arousing, and Twenty-Two Similes

Chinese Source

偈曰：

友力及因力，　根力亦聞力，

四力總二發，　不堅及以堅。

釋曰：若從他說得覺而發心，是名受世俗發心。此發心由四力：一者友力發心，或得善知識隨順故。二者因力發心，或過去曾發心為性故。三者根力發心，或過去曾行諸善根所圓滿故。四者聞力發心，或處處說法時無量眾生發菩提心故。又習善根者，或現在如法常聞受持等故。復次彼四力發心，總為二種：一者不堅發，謂友力發心故。二者堅發，謂因等三力發心故。已說世俗發心，次說第一義發心。

偈曰：

親近正遍知，　善集福智聚，

於法無分別，　最上真智生。

釋曰：第一義發心，顯有三種勝：一教授勝，親近正遍知故。二隨順勝，善集福智聚故。三得果勝，生無分別智故。此發心名歡喜地，由歡喜勝故。問：此勝以何為因？

偈曰：

諸法及眾生，　所作及佛體，

於此四平等，　故得歡喜勝。

釋曰：四平等者，一法平等，由通達法無我故。二眾生平等，由至得自他平等故。三所作平等，由令他盡苦如自盡苦故。四佛體平等，由法界與我無別決定能通達故。已說勝因，次說勝差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The power of a friend, and the power of cause,
the power of roots, and the power of hearing —
these four powers, in total, are two kinds of arousing:
the unsteady, and the steady.

The exposition: if one arouses the mind upon gaining understanding through another's teaching, this is called receiving the conventional arousing of the mind. This arousing comes from four powers: first, by the power of a friend, in that one has gained the support of a good spiritual friend; second, by the power of cause, in that one has, in a former life, already made this one's nature by arousing the mind; third, by the power of roots, in that one has, in a former life, already brought various wholesome roots to fulfillment; and fourth, by the power of hearing, in that, wherever the Dharma is taught, measureless sentient beings arouse the mind of enlightenment — and also in that one who has cultivated wholesome roots presently hears, upholds, and practices in accordance with the Dharma continually. These four powers of arousing the mind fall, in total, into two kinds: the unsteady arousing, by the power of a friend; and the steady arousing, by the other three powers of cause, of roots, and of hearing. The conventional arousing of the mind having been stated, next the ultimate arousing of the mind is explained.

Drawing near to the perfectly awakened one,
well gathering the accumulations of merit and wisdom,
and being without discrimination toward things —
the supreme, true wisdom is born.

The exposition: the ultimate arousing of the mind displays three excellences: first, the excellence of instruction, in drawing near to the perfectly awakened one; second, the excellence of accordance, in having well gathered the accumulations of merit and wisdom; and third, the excellence of the fruit attained, in that non-discriminating wisdom is born. This arousing of the mind is called the ground of joy, by reason of the excellence of joy. Question: what is the cause of this excellence?

Things, and sentient beings,
what is to be done, and the substance of buddhahood —
by equality with respect to these four,
the excellence of joy is attained.

The exposition: the four equalities are: first, the equality of things, in penetrating the selflessness of things; second, the equality of sentient beings, in attaining the equality of self and other; third, the equality of what is to be done, in bringing about the extinguishing of others' suffering just as one's own; and fourth, the equality of the substance of buddhahood, in penetrating, with certainty, that the dharma-realm and oneself are not different. The cause of the excellence having been stated, next the distinctions within it are explained.

Chinese Source

偈曰：

生位及願位，　亦猛亦淨依，

餘巧及餘出，　六勝復如是。

釋曰：第一義發心，復有六勝：一生位勝、二願位勝、三勇猛勝、四淨依勝、五餘巧勝、六餘出勝。問：此六云何勝？

偈曰：

生勝由四義，　願大有十種，

勇猛恒不退，　淨依二利生，

巧便進餘地，　出離善思惟，

如此六道理，　次第成六勝。

釋曰：生勝由四義者，一種子勝，信大乘法為種子故。二生母勝，般若波羅蜜為生母故。三胎藏勝，大禪定樂為胎藏故。四乳母勝，大悲長養為乳母故。願大有十種者，十大願如《十地經》說，發此願勝故。勇猛恒不退者，能行難行永不退故。淨依二利生者，一知自近菩提、二知利他方便故。巧便進餘地者，得趣上地方便故。出離善思惟者，思惟住諸地中所建立法故。問：云何思惟？答：如所建立分齊分別知故，以是分別亦知無分別故。已說發心，次說譬喻顯此發心。

Ru-Yi Translation

The stage of birth, and the stage of the vow,
and valor, and the pure support,
further skill, and further renunciation —
the six excellences are just these.

The exposition: the ultimate arousing of the mind further has six excellences: the excellence of the stage of birth, of the stage of the vow, of valor, of the pure support, of further skill, and of further renunciation. Question: in what way are these six excellent?

Birth is excellent through four meanings;
the vow is great, of ten kinds;
valor never turns back;
the pure support gives rise to the twofold benefit;
skill advances to the further grounds;
renunciation is well reflected upon —
these six principles, in this order,
accomplish the six excellences.

The exposition: birth is excellent through four meanings — the excellence of the seed, in that faith in the Mahāyāna Dharma is its seed; of the birth-mother, in that the perfection of wisdom is its birth-mother; of the womb, in that the bliss of great meditative concentration is its womb; and of the wet-nurse, in that the nourishing power of great compassion is its wet-nurse. The vow is great, of ten kinds means: the ten great vows, as taught in the Sūtra of the Ten Grounds, are excellent because this vow is aroused. Valor never turns back means one is able to practice the difficult practices and never turn back. The pure support gives rise to the twofold benefit means: knowing that one is oneself near to enlightenment, and knowing the skillful means for benefiting others. Skill advances to the further grounds means attaining the skillful means for proceeding to the higher grounds. Renunciation is well reflected upon means reflecting on the things established within the stage of each ground. Question: how does one reflect? Answer: since one discriminates and knows the boundaries as they have been established, and by this very discrimination one also knows the absence of discrimination. Arousing the mind having been stated, next similes are given to display this arousing of the mind.

Reading note

Of the four powers behind the conventional arousing of the mind, only 友力, the power of a friend, is called unsteady; the other three — cause, roots, and hearing — are all steady, and all three, on inspection, are really the same power under different names: something planted in a previous life ripening now. The asymmetry is a quiet piece of realism about spiritual friendship. A good teacher can trigger the arousing of the mind, but the treatise does not credit the trigger with the same durability as what was already latent — a friend can occasion the moment, but cannot supply what was not already there to be occasioned.

The four equalities that explain why the ultimate arousing of the mind is called "the ground of joy" are built as a chain, each depending on the one before: equality of things (there is no self anywhere), which grounds equality of self and other (if neither has a self, there is nothing left to unequally favor), which grounds equality of what is to be done (another's suffering is exactly as real, and as worth ending, as one's own), which finally grounds equality with the substance of buddhahood itself. Joy, on this reading, is not a separate reward tacked onto the realization — it is what naturally follows once the last of the four barriers between self and everything else has genuinely fallen.

3. Twenty-Two Similes for the Aroused Mind

Chinese Source

偈曰：

如地如淨金，　如月如增火，

如藏如寶篋，　如海如金剛，

如山如藥王，　如友如如意，

如日如美樂，　如王如庫倉，

如道如車乘，　如泉如喜聲，

如流亦如雲，　發心譬如是。

釋曰：如此發心，與諸譬喻何義相似？答：譬如大地，最初發心亦如是，一切佛法能生持故。譬如淨金，依相應發心亦如是，利益安樂不退壞故。譬如新月，勤相應發心亦如是，一切善法漸漸增故。譬如增火，極依相應發心亦如是，益薪火熾積行依極故。譬如大藏，檀波羅蜜相應發心亦如是，以財周給亦無盡故。譬如寶篋，尸波羅蜜相應發心亦如是，功德法寶從彼生故。譬如大海，羼提波羅蜜相應發心亦如是，諸來違逆心不動故。譬如金剛，毘梨耶波羅蜜相應發心亦如是，勇猛堅牢不可壞故。譬如山王，禪波羅蜜相應發心亦如是，物無能動以不亂故。譬如藥王，般若波羅蜜相應發心亦如是，惑智二病此能破故。譬如善友，無量相應發心亦如是，一切時中不捨眾生故。譬如如意珠，神通相應發心亦如是，隨所欲現能成就故。譬如盛日，攝相應發心亦如是，如日熟穀成熟眾生故。譬如美樂，辯相應發心亦如是，說法教化攝眾生故。譬如國王，量相應發心亦如是，能為正道不壞因故。譬如倉庫，聚相應發心亦如是，福智法財之所聚故。譬如王路，覺分相應發心亦如是，大聖先行，餘隨行故。譬如車乘，止觀相應發心亦如是，二輪具足安樂去故。譬如涌泉，總持相應發心亦如是，聞者雖多，法無盡故。譬如喜聲，法印相應發心亦如是，求解脫者所樂聞故。譬如河流，自性相應發心亦如是，無生忍道自然而流不作意故。譬如大雲能成世界，方便相應發心亦如是，示現八相成道化眾生故。如此等及二十二譬譬彼發心，如《聖者無盡慧經》廣說應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Like the earth, like pure gold,
like the new moon, like a growing fire,
like a great treasury, like a jeweled casket,
like the ocean, like a diamond,
like the king of mountains, like the king of medicines,
like a good friend, like a wish-fulfilling gem,
like the sun, like beautiful music,
like a king, like a storehouse,
like a road, like a chariot,
like a spring, like a joyful sound,
like a river, and like a cloud —
arousing the mind is like all of these.

The exposition: in what sense does this arousing of the mind resemble these similes? Answer: it is like the great earth — the very first arousing of the mind is like this, since it is able to give rise to and sustain all buddha-dharmas. It is like pure gold — arousing the mind joined with resolve is like this, since its benefit and ease do not turn back or decay. It is like the new moon — arousing the mind joined with diligence is like this, since all wholesome things gradually increase. It is like a growing fire — arousing the mind joined with utmost reliance is like this, since, as fuel is added, its blaze grows and its accumulated practice relies on this utmost degree. It is like a great treasury — arousing the mind joined with the perfection of generosity is like this, since it supplies wealth all around without exhaustion. It is like a jeweled casket — joined with the perfection of morality, since the jeweled Dharma of merit is born from it. It is like the great ocean — joined with the perfection of patience, since it is unmoved by whatever comes in opposition. It is like a diamond — joined with the perfection of diligence, since it is valiant, firm, and cannot be destroyed. It is like the king of mountains — joined with the perfection of meditative concentration, since nothing can move it, being undisturbed. It is like the king of medicines — joined with the perfection of wisdom, since it is able to cure the two illnesses of affliction and of obstruction. It is like a good friend — joined with the immeasurables, since it never abandons sentient beings at any time. It is like a wish-fulfilling gem — joined with the supernormal powers, since it is able to accomplish whatever is wished for as it appears. It is like the blazing sun — joined with gathering beings, since, as the sun ripens the grain, it matures sentient beings. It is like beautiful music — joined with eloquence, since by teaching the Dharma it gathers in beings through instruction. It is like a king — joined with due measure, since it is the indestructible cause of the right path. It is like a storehouse — joined with accumulation, since it is where the wealth of the Dharma, merit and wisdom, is gathered. It is like a royal road — joined with the factors of enlightenment, since the great sage goes first and the rest follow after. It is like a chariot — joined with calm abiding and insight, since, its two wheels complete, it travels at ease. It is like a gushing spring — joined with the mnemonic formulas, since, however many hear it, the Dharma is never exhausted. It is like a joyful sound — joined with the seal of the Dharma, since those who seek liberation delight to hear it. It is like a flowing river — joined with its own-nature, since the path of the forbearance of non-arising flows on of itself, without deliberate effort. It is like a great cloud able to bring a world-system into being — joined with skillful means, since it displays the eight aspects of accomplishing the path in order to transform sentient beings. These, along with the rest, are the twenty-two similes for this arousing of the mind, as is taught at length in the Sūtra of the Noble One's Inexhaustible Wisdom, and should be so understood.

Reading note

The treatise is explicit that this list is a citation, not its own invention — 如《聖者無盡慧經》廣說應知 — and the twenty-two similes read differently once that is kept in mind. They are not a poetic flourish appended to the philosophical content; they are themselves the content, a mnemonic system for holding the entire bodhisattva path — the six perfections, the immeasurables, the supernormal powers, calm abiding and insight, the mnemonic formulas, the eight aspects of a buddha's life — in twenty-two concrete images. A practitioner who has memorized "diamond is diligence, ocean is patience, king of mountains is meditative concentration" has, in effect, memorized the path's structure without needing the abstract vocabulary at all.

Several similes reward a second look at exactly what they are similes for. The wish-fulfilling gem is not generosity, as intuition might suggest, but the supernormal powers — the point being not that it gives things away, but that it produces whatever is wished for on demand. And the great cloud, last of the twenty-two, is matched to skillful means specifically because of the eight aspects of a buddha's life (descent, conception, birth, renunciation, awakening, teaching, and so on) that it enables — a cloud that "brings a world-system into being" is doing something structural, not merely beneficial, which is exactly what skillful means is asked to do among the perfections.

4. Why One Should Not Fear Suffering, and Should Not Retrogress

Chinese Source

已說發心譬喻，次說不發心過失。

偈曰：

思利及得方，　解義亦證實，

如是四時樂，　趣寂則便捨。

釋曰：菩薩有四種樂。一思利樂，謂思惟利益他時。二得方樂，謂至得巧方便時。三解義樂，謂解了大乘意時。四證實樂，謂證人法無我時。若人棄捨眾生趣向寂滅，應知是人不得菩薩如是四樂。已呵不發心，發心者應讚歎。

偈曰：

最初發大心，　善護無邊惡，

善增悲增故，　樂喜苦亦喜。

Reading note

無間如樂處 — the Avīci hell, to the bodhisattva, is like a place of pleasure — is the most extreme claim in this section, and it is worth reading precisely rather than loosely. The claim is not that suffering has stopped hurting; it is that the bodhisattva's compassion is strong enough that entering the worst possible place to help beings there produces no hesitation, no dread that would slow him down — the psychological register of "going there" collapses toward the register of "going somewhere pleasant" because the will to go is undivided. It is a claim about the absence of internal resistance, not a claim that suffering has become painless.

The passage builds three successive lines of defense against retrogression — the four unique joys unavailable to the lesser vehicles, illusion-like non-attachment to outcomes, and finally the sheer momentum of compassion that treats hell as no obstacle — and each is strictly stronger than the last. It reads as though the treatise anticipated that a reader might grant the first argument and still feel afraid, grant the second and still hesitate, and is not willing to stop arguing until fear itself has been addressed directly and by name, in 遮畏苦心, warding off the mind that fears suffering.

Reading note

The verse asks a real question and the treatise does not pretend it has an easy answer: if self and other are equal, 二利何差別 — what difference is there between the two benefits? The resolution turns on a distinction between two kinds of equality that could easily be run together. The bodhisattva's mind toward self and other is equal in the sense that matters for ethics — neither is discriminated against in what is owed to it — and yet 愛則於彼勝, in the specific matter of love, the other still prevails. Equality of standing does not require equality of preference; a parent can hold this exact asymmetry toward a child without contradiction, loving the child more while owing the child nothing less than is owed to anyone. The treatise's own answer to why this still counts as "no difference" is that the asymmetry runs in a direction that dissolves the self-interest the distinction was meant to track in the first place.

The thirteen accordances and the eight conditions that establish them map, respectively, onto a process (what happens to the being being benefited, stage by stage, ending at buddhahood) and onto a discipline (what the bodhisattva himself must avoid doing while carrying it out — not erring, not exalting himself, not clinging). Read side by side, the eight read as a check on the thirteen: it would be entirely possible to walk someone through all thirteen stages while quietly congratulating oneself at each one, and the eight conditions exist specifically to rule that out.

Reading note

The four grades of beings — bound to desire, bound to subtler existence, seeking only their own cessation, and moved by great compassion — form a single ascending scale rather than four unrelated types, and it is worth noticing that the third grade, 樂滅斷煩惱, is described without hostility even though it names the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha paths this treatise elsewhere argues are inferior to the Mahāyāna. The fault named here is narrower than wrongness: such practitioners have correctly diagnosed that affliction causes suffering and correctly acted to end it in themselves; what distinguishes the fourth grade is only that its diagnosis and its remedy both extend to everyone else as well.

The citation from the Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra is one of the few places in this fascicle where the treatise quotes an outside scripture by name rather than simply stating its own doctrine, and the logic of the quotation is worth following closely: it argues from the Mahāyāna's capacity to surpass the whole of saṃsāra back to a claim about the ultimate status of form — if form were truly, substantially existent, nothing could surpass the domain it structures. The argument runs from soteriological fact (this teaching does surpass everything) to metaphysical conclusion (therefore nothing in that domain is truly, substantially existent), rather than the more familiar direction of starting from emptiness and deriving liberation from it.

Reading note

The fivefold "neither X nor Y" of this chapter is easy to mistake for the Madhyamaka tetralemma, but its logic is different, and the difference matters. Nāgārjuna's fourfold negation clears away every possible predicate to leave nothing standing. Here, each "neither" is explained in terms of the three natures rather than as a blanket refusal of predication: neither existent nor non-existent because the imagined and dependent natures are absent while the true mark is present; neither identical nor different because of how the perfectly accomplished nature relates to the other two. This is Yogācāra's own route to non-duality, arrived at through the architecture already built in fascicle 1's chapter on the lineage, not an import from a rival school's method.

迷苦及迷苦者 draws a distinction easy to collapse: not understanding suffering's own nature, and not understanding that there is no sufferer, are named as two separate delusions rather than one. A meditator could plausibly grasp the first — that all conditioned experience is inherently unsatisfactory — while still quietly assuming there is someone to whom this unsatisfactoriness happens. The treatise's own gloss on 苦者, "the sufferer," is unusually precise: it is not a self bound up with suffering, but simply suffering's own failure to cease. Naming both delusions separately blocks a reader from thinking the first insight already implies the second.

Reading note

The five stages named here — gathering the great accumulation, penetration, the path of seeing, the path of cultivation, and culmination — are this treatise's own statement of the classical five-path scheme (資糧, 加行, 見道, 修道, 究竟) that structures the entire Yogācāra account of the path elsewhere on this site, most systematically in the Cheng Weishi Lun. What is worth noticing here is how compressed the middle transition is: the shift from the second stage's 善住唯識, dwelling well in the recognition that everything is mind, to the third stage's actual path of seeing, is stated as though it followed automatically — 從彼後現見法界, "from that point on, he directly sees the dharma-realm" — with no further practice named in between. Other treatises in this corpus spend far more space on exactly what closes that gap; this one states the destination and leaves the mechanics for elsewhere.

心外無有物，物無心亦無 is the sharpest formula in the whole passage, and it resists a common misreading. It is not simply "nothing exists outside the mind, therefore only mind exists" — that would leave the mind standing as the one remaining substantial thing. The verse's second half withdraws that too: once there is no object, there is equally no grasping mind, since a mind without anything to grasp is not the kind of thing this treatise is willing to call existent either. Both terms of the subject-object pair are dissolved together, which is exactly what 解脫於二相, liberation from the two marks, requires — a philosophy that stopped at "everything is mind" would still be trapped inside one of the two marks it claims to be free of.

The exposition: the bodhisattva's supernormal power has three kinds of greatness: the greatness of mastery, in that, whereas sentient beings, owing to the afflictions, lack mastery of themselves, the bodhisattva's power of wisdom is able to settle them in mastery; the greatness of joy, in that he is constantly diligent in benefiting sentient beings and is single-mindedly joyful in it; and the greatness of fearlessness, in that, moving through the three realms of existence, he attains the utmost valor, like a lion. Reading note

The six functions of supernormal power (自, 他, 光, 戲, 化, 淨) are organized around a single distinction that recurs across every treatise in this site's Yogācāra corpus: what a bodhisattva does that touches only his own experience, and what he does that acts on others. The function of self is the seeing of worlds as illusion — an internal cognitive achievement with no external effect at all — while every one of the other five functions is defined entirely by its effect on someone else: light that terrifies māras, play that models absorption for the assembly, transformation that meets three different registers of need, and purification that operates on lands and on the beings within them. The taxonomy makes visible something the earlier chapters argued for directly: even the treatise's account of what looks like private meditative accomplishment is only one item on a much longer list, the rest of which is entirely outward-facing.

The equipment for the powers — six knowledges, three insights, eight liberations, eight masteries, ten totalities, and the various absorptions — is this treatise's own compressed citation of the classical meditative curriculum found at length in the Treatise on the Stages of Yogic Practice, most fully at fascicle 12, where the liberations, masteries, and totalities are explained one by one as a graded sequence: the liberations first loosen a practitioner's grip on the object, the masteries then let him dominate it at will, and the totalities extend that mastery until the chosen object — earth, water, the color blue — fills the entire field of experience. This treatise does not repeat that explanation; it assumes the reader already has it, and simply names the set as furniture a bodhisattva needs before the supernormal powers proper can function.

Reading note

The cause-substance-function template (因/體/業) applied nine times running is a formal device worth naming, since the treatise otherwise varies its analytical structures from chapter to chapter. Running the same three questions against nine different factors makes each entry directly comparable to the others — one can ask, of any of the nine, what produces it, what it consists in once produced, and what it does once formed — and the repetition itself becomes a kind of argument: maturation, whatever its content, always has this same three-part anatomy, cause preceding substance preceding function.

Two of the nine reward comparison. Patience's cause is described as 數習成性, repeated practice becoming nature — an acquired trait built by habituation, the same vocabulary this treatise's fascicle 1 used for how the seeds of the ālayavijñāna are formed. Steadfastness, by contrast, is defined almost entirely negatively, by what fails to move it: 惡魔波旬不能障礙, the évil māra cannot obstruct it. One maturation is described from the inside, as something built; the other is described from the outside, as something that survives attack. Read together they suggest the nine are not all the same kind of achievement — some are cultivated, and some are simply what remains once cultivation has held under pressure.

The exposition: this verse displays that the mark of great maturation is of three kinds: the greatness of stage, reaching through the four stages, establishing beings in a good path and in the three vehicles; the greatness of grade, compassion carried to its utmost through three grades — the lowest being the ground of faith and practice, the middle being the first ground through the seventh, and the highest being the eighth, ninth, and tenth grounds; and the greatness of duration, its span boundless, extending to the very limit of the future. The bodhisattva benefits sentient beings in this way — this is called the mark of great maturation. The chapter on maturing beings is complete.

Reading note

世間不自愛，何況能愛他 is a genuinely striking piece of moral psychology to find stated this bluntly: ordinary people, on the treatise's own account, do not really succeed even at loving themselves — they cannot settle themselves in what actually benefits them, 尚不能自安利處 — so their capacity to love others is built on a foundation that was never solid to begin with. The bodhisattva's relinquishing of self-love is not offered as a further sacrifice added on top of an already-secure self-regard; it is offered as the only way to build a love of others that is not compromised from the start by the same confusion that made ordinary self-love unreliable.

Each of the six perfections, applied to maturing others, is analyzed by the same 現在/未來 structure — a benefit realized now, and a benefit planted for later — which quietly answers a question a skeptical reader might have carried since the opening chapters of fascicle 1: does benefiting others actually help them in any way that isn't purely long-term and abstract? The pattern insists otherwise every single time. Generosity fills present need before it plants future roots; patience earns present goodwill before it plants a future seed; even diligence, applied to a being who manages only a single moment of a wholesome mind, credits that moment as a real, present gain, not merely as a down payment on something later. The treatise closes its account of maturing others, and this fascicle, on the claim that the two timeframes are never separated — nothing here is only for later.

Fascicle 2 Complete

Five chapters, carrying the lineage of fascicle 1 into motion. Arousing the mind is given its four greatnesses, its four grounds, an eleven-part anatomy, and twenty-two similes, before the chapter turns to why fear of suffering and retrogression are both unwarranted for a mind this constituted. The twofold benefit resolves the tension between self and other being equal yet other-benefit being called supreme, then works out thirteen ways that benefit reaches beings at every stage up to buddhahood. Reality is given its fivefold non-dual character and its five stages of realization, from the first gathering of merit and wisdom to the ocean of a buddha's own qualities. Supernormal power is anatomized into six knowledges, three fruits, six functions, three correspondences, and the classical meditative equipment that provisions it all. And maturing beings closes the fascicle in two directions at once — nine factors that mature the bodhisattva himself, each with its own cause, substance, and function, and six perfections that mature everyone else, each yielding a benefit now and a seed for later. Fascicle 3 takes up the chapter on enlightenment itself (菩提品), the treatise's longest single chapter and, by its own preface, its most subtle.
