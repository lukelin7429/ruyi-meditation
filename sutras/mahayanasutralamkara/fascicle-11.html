Fascicle 11 · 覺分品第二十一之二 · Complete

The Factors of Enlightenment, Part Two

Fascicle 10 closed with the eight path-factors. This fascicle finishes 覺分品第二十一 with a sequence of practices — calm-abiding and insight, five skillful means, three kinds of dhāraṇī, the bodhisattva's vows, the three concentrations, and the four Dharma seals — before the chapter turns, in its second half, into the treatise's most sustained philosophical argument so far. Impermanence is traced down to momentary destruction and defended through fifteen, then nine, then fourteen distinct lines of reasoning covering mind, body, and the physical elements alike, answered at every turn by an imagined objector. The same rigor is then trained on the self: neither one with nor different from the aggregates it is falsely identified with, neither the condition nor the master behind any sense faculty's own function, and finally accounted for as a mere convention the Buddha had good reason to keep using even after teaching there is nothing real behind it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Fascicle 11 complete Source: Taishō T1604, 大乘莊嚴經論卷第十一 Chinese attribution: 無著菩薩造 (compiled by Asaṅga)

Where This Fascicle Picks Up

Fascicle 10 closed with the eight path-factors, completing the classical thirty-seven items usually meant by "the factors of enlightenment." This fascicle shows that the chapter, in this treatise, was never only about that list. Its second half turns almost entirely into philosophical argument, in a register closer to Abhidharma debate than to the devotional or practical material surrounding it — an extended demonstration, structured as a running dialogue with an unnamed objector, that everything conditioned is destroyed moment by moment, and that no real, substantial self can be found anywhere behind the person who is nonetheless spoken of constantly, including by the Buddha himself.

1. Calm-Abiding and Insight

Chinese Source

釋曰：已說菩薩修習道分，次說菩薩修習止觀。偈曰：

安心於正定，　此即名為止；

正住法分別，　是名為觀相。

釋曰：安心於正定此即名為止者，謂心依正定而不見心，非無正定而立止故，是名止相。正住法分別是名為觀相者，謂依正住分別法體，是名觀相。問：此二行云何修？偈曰：

普欲諸功德，　是二悉應修，

一分非一分，　修有單雙故。

釋曰：普欲諸功德是二悉應修者，若人遍欲求諸功德，是人於止觀二行悉應修習。如經中說，佛告諸比丘：若有所求，云何令得？諸比丘！離欲離惡不善法，乃至廣說。諸比丘！有二法應須修習，所謂止觀。一分非一分者，一分謂或止或觀，非一分謂止觀合。問：何故？答：修有單雙故。單修者一分，或止修或觀修。雙修者非一分，謂止觀合修。問：此二行云何種差別？復云何業？偈曰：

能通及能出，　無相亦無為，

淨土及淨果，　是二即為業。

釋曰：此偈上半明種差別，下半明業。此二法在信行地名依止修。若入大地，復有四種差別：一能通修，謂入初地。二能出修，謂入乃至六地，於彼六地出有相方便故。三無相修，謂入第七地。四無為修，謂入後三地。作功用修名有為，後三地不作功用故名無為。此五是種差別。淨土者，依後三地修淨土行；淨果者，作轉依行。此二淨即是彼業。已說菩薩止觀，次說菩薩修習五種巧方便。

Ru-Yi Translation

The exposition: the bodhisattva's cultivation of the path-factors having been explained, the bodhisattva's cultivation of calm-abiding and insight is next explained.

Settling the mind in correct concentration —
this itself is called calm-abiding;
correctly abiding in the discernment of phenomena —
this is called the mark of insight.

The exposition: settling the mind in correct concentration is itself called calm-abiding — the mind resting on correct concentration without perceiving a separate mind, calm-abiding not being established apart from correct concentration; this is the mark of calm-abiding. Correctly abiding in the discernment of phenomena is called the mark of insight — resting on correct abiding, discerning the substance of phenomena; this is the mark of insight. Question: how are these two practiced?

One who universally seeks every merit
should cultivate both of these entirely;
as a single part, or not a single part —
since cultivation is both single and paired.

The exposition: one who universally seeks every merit should cultivate both of these entirely — if a person seeks every kind of merit without exception, that person should cultivate both calm-abiding and insight without exception. As a sūtra teaches, the Buddha told the monks: "If there is something sought, how is it obtained? Monks — being free of desire, free of unwholesome and evil dharmas," and so on, at length. "Monks, there are two dharmas that must be cultivated: namely, calm-abiding and insight." As a single part, or not a single part: a single part means either calm-abiding alone or insight alone; not a single part means calm-abiding and insight combined. Question: why? Answer: since cultivation is both single and paired. Cultivating singly is a single part — either cultivating calm-abiding, or cultivating insight. Cultivating in pairs is not a single part — calm-abiding and insight cultivated together. Question: what are the distinctions of kind between these two practices? And what is their function?

Able to penetrate, and able to emerge;
signless, and unconditioned;
the pure land, and the pure fruit —
these are the function of the two.

The exposition: the first half of this verse clarifies the distinctions of kind; the second half displays their function. On the ground of conviction and practice, these two dharmas are called cultivation resting on support. Once one enters the great grounds, there are four further distinctions: first, penetrating cultivation, entering the first ground; second, emerging cultivation, entering through the sixth ground, since across these six grounds one emerges through skillful means still bearing signs; third, signless cultivation, entering the seventh ground; fourth, unconditioned cultivation, entering the last three grounds — cultivation that still exerts effort is called conditioned, and since the last three grounds exert no effort, it is called unconditioned. These five are the distinctions of kind. The pure land is cultivating the practice of purifying a buddha-land, resting on the last three grounds; the pure fruit is performing the practice of the transformation of the basis. These two purities are their very function. The bodhisattva's calm-abiding and insight having been explained, the bodhisattva's cultivation of the five kinds of skillful means is next explained.

Reading note

The five distinctions of calm-abiding and insight quietly retrace the same ground-by-ground progression this treatise has now used for several other pairs across these two fascicles — the twin accumulations' five causes, the four correct exertions' ten obstructions. Each time, a practice that could in principle be described as a single, uniform discipline is instead broken across the bodhisattva path's specific joints: penetrating at the first ground, emerging through the sixth, turning signless at the seventh, and effortless only from the eighth onward. The recurrence of this move suggests the treatise's real interest lies less in any one practice's internal content than in insisting that no bodhisattva practice, however basic, is exempt from being reshaped by where on the path it is performed.

2. The Five Skillful Means, and the Three Kinds of Dhāraṇī

Chinese Source

偈曰：

自熟與成生，　速果并作業，

生死道不絕，　說此為五巧。

釋曰：五種巧方便者，一自熟佛法，以無分別智為巧方便；二成熟眾生，以四攝法為巧方便；三速得菩提，以懺悔隨喜請轉法輪生起勝願為巧方便；四作業成就，以二門為巧方便，二門者謂陀羅尼門及三昧門，以此二門能成就利益眾生業故；五生死道不絕，以無住處涅槃為巧方便。問：云何巧差別？云何巧業？偈曰：

菩薩巧無等，　差別依諸地，

能成自他利，　說是名為業。

釋曰：此偈上半明巧差別，下半明巧業。差別者，此五方便於諸菩薩最上無等。何以故？於諸地中不與二乘共故，是故差別。業者，能成就自身他身一切利益，是名為業。已說菩薩巧方便，次說菩薩陀羅尼。偈曰：

業報及聞習，　亦以定為因，

依止此三行，　持類有三種。

釋曰：陀羅尼品類有三種，一報得，由先世業力得故；二習得，由現在聞持力而得故；三修得，由依定力得故。問：云何種差別？偈曰：

二小一為大，　一大復三種，

地前與地上，　不淨及淨故。

釋曰：二小一為大者，於彼三種品類中，報得及習得應知此二為小，修得者應知此一為大。一大復三種者，於彼大種類中，應知復有三種，謂軟、中、上。未入地菩薩所有為軟；已入不淨地菩薩所有為中，謂初七地；入清淨地菩薩所有為上，謂後三地。問：云何業？偈曰：

應知諸菩薩，　恒依陀羅尼，

開法及持法，　作業皆如是。

釋曰：此中應知諸菩薩依止陀羅尼恒開示妙法及常受持，以此為業。已說菩薩陀羅尼，次說菩薩起諸願。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ripening oneself, and maturing beings;
swiftly attaining the fruit, and accomplishing one's function;
the path of birth-and-death unbroken —
these are taught as the five skillful means.

The exposition: the five kinds of skillful means: first, ripening the buddha-dharma in oneself, taking non-discriminating wisdom as the skillful means; second, maturing beings, taking the four means of gathering as the skillful means; third, swiftly attaining enlightenment, taking repentance, rejoicing, requesting the turning of the wheel of the Dharma, and generating supreme vows as the skillful means; fourth, accomplishing one's function, taking the two gateways as the skillful means — the two gateways being the gateway of dhāraṇī and the gateway of absorption, since through these two gateways one is able to accomplish the work of benefiting beings; fifth, the path of birth-and-death unbroken, taking nirvāṇa without fixed abode as the skillful means. Question: what are the distinctions among these skillful means? What is their function?

The bodhisattva's skillful means are unequaled;
their distinctions rest on the various grounds;
being able to accomplish benefit for self and other —
this is said to be their function.

The exposition: the first half of this verse clarifies the distinctions among the skillful means; the second half displays their function. As to distinctions: these five means are, among all bodhisattvas, foremost and unequaled — why? Because, throughout the various grounds, they are not shared with the two vehicles; hence their distinction. As to function: being able to accomplish every benefit for both oneself and others — this is their function. The bodhisattva's skillful means having been explained, the bodhisattva's dhāraṇī is next explained.

Karmic ripening, and hearing and habituation;
and concentration, too, as cause —
resting on these three practices,
retention is of three kinds.

The exposition: dhāraṇī has three kinds: first, attained through ripening — attained through the power of past-life karma; second, attained through habituation — attained through the power of hearing and retaining in this present life; third, attained through cultivation — attained through resting on the power of concentration. Question: what are the distinctions of kind?

Two are lesser, and one is greater;
the one that is greater is again threefold —
before the grounds, and on the grounds,
in the impure and the pure stages.

The exposition: two are lesser, and one is greater — among these three kinds, that attained through ripening and that attained through habituation should be understood as the two lesser kinds, while that attained through cultivation should be understood as the one greater kind. The one that is greater is again threefold — within this greater kind, there are three further grades: mild, middling, and superior. That possessed by a bodhisattva not yet entered onto a ground is mild; that possessed by a bodhisattva already entered onto the impure grounds is middling — namely, the first seven grounds; and that possessed by a bodhisattva entered onto the pure grounds is superior — namely, the last three grounds. Question: what is its function?

Know that the bodhisattva,
constantly resting on dhāraṇī,
opens up the Dharma and retains the Dharma —
such, entirely, is its function.

The exposition: here, it should be known that the bodhisattva, resting on dhāraṇī, constantly opens up and reveals the sublime Dharma, and constantly receives and retains it — this is its function. The bodhisattva's dhāraṇī having been explained, the bodhisattva's arising of vows is next explained.

Reading note

The three kinds of dhāraṇī are graded by origin rather than by present strength — karmic ripening from a past life, habituation through hearing in this one, and cultivation through concentration — yet only the third counts as "great," and the other two are demoted to "lesser" regardless of how vivid or reliable they might feel to the person who has them. The implicit standard is not how retention presents itself introspectively but how it was produced: a capacity one merely inherited or absorbed, however impressive, ranks below the same capacity deliberately built through one's own cultivation.

3. The Bodhisattva's Vows

Chinese Source

偈曰：

思欲共為體，　智獨是彼因，

諸地即為地，　二果亦為果。

應知差別三，　種種大清淨，

此業有二種，　自利與利他。

釋曰：此二偈以六義分別諸願，一自性、二因、三地、四果、五差別、六業。彼思欲相應共為自性。以智為因。諸地為地。二果為果，謂即果及未來果。以諸願為因，心得遂故。心遂者，如心所欲皆成就故。又以願力遊諸願果，所謂身放光明、口發音響乃至廣說。差別有三種，一種種，謂信行地願如是如是欲得故。二廣大，謂入地菩薩十大願故。三清淨，謂後後諸地轉轉清淨，乃至佛地極清淨故。是名差別。彼業二種，一自利成就、二利他成就，是名為業。已說菩薩諸願，次說菩薩修習三三昧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Intention and aspiration together are their substance;
wisdom alone is their cause;
the grounds themselves are their ground;
and twofold fruits are also their fruit.

Know that their distinctions are three:
manifold, vast, and pure;
this function is of two kinds:
accomplishing oneself, and benefiting others.

The exposition: these two verses distinguish vows through six meanings: own-nature, cause, ground, fruit, distinctions, and function. Intention joined with aspiration together is their own-nature. Wisdom is their cause. The grounds are their ground. Twofold fruits are their fruit — namely, the immediate fruit and the future fruit. Vows are their cause, since through them the mind attains what it seeks — attaining what it seeks meaning that whatever the mind wishes is accomplished. Further, through the power of vows one roams through the fruits of one's vows — namely, the body emitting light, the voice uttering sounds, and so on, at length. Their distinctions are of three kinds: first, manifold — the ground of conviction and practice's vows wishing to attain this and that, variously; second, vast — the bodhisattva's ten great vows upon entering the grounds; third, pure — becoming ever purer, ground after ground, until utterly pure at the buddha ground. These are the distinctions. Their function is of two kinds: accomplishing oneself, and accomplishing the benefit of others — this is their function. The bodhisattva's vows having been explained, the bodhisattva's cultivation of the three concentrations is next explained.

Reading note

Wisdom as the sole cause of a vow is a narrower claim than it first appears, and worth setting against ordinary intuitions about what motivates a vow — longing, compassion, devotion. The treatise does not deny that intention and aspiration are present; it names them the vow's own-nature, its very substance. But it reserves the causal role for wisdom alone, which suggests a vow is not, on this account, wisdom's opposite or supplement but one of its direct products: a vow arises because one has correctly seen what needs to be done, and the wishing is simply what that correct seeing feels like from the inside.

4. The Three Concentrations, and the Four Dharma Seals

Chinese Source

偈曰：

應知二無我，　及以二我依，

二依常寂滅，　三定所行境。

釋曰：三三昧有三種所行，一人法二無我，是空三昧所行；二彼二執所依五取陰，是無願三昧所行；三彼依畢竟寂滅，是無相三昧所行。彼三種所取體為三種境界，彼三種能取體為三種三昧，是名三三昧。問：三三昧名義云何？偈曰：

空定無分別，　無願厭背生，

無相恒樂得，　彼依常寂滅。

釋曰：空定無分別者，無分別義是空三昧義，由人法二我不分別故。無願厭背生者，厭背義是無願三昧義，由厭背我執所依故。無相恒樂得彼依常寂滅者，樂得義是無相三昧義，由樂得彼所依畢竟寂滅故。問：三三昧云何起？偈曰：

應知及應斷，　及以應作證，

次第空等定，　修習有三種。

釋曰：應知及應斷及以應作證者，應知謂人法二無我，應斷謂二我執所依，應證謂彼依畢竟寂滅。次第空等定修習有三種者，此中為知人法二無我故修空三昧，為斷彼二執所依故修無願三昧，為證彼依畢竟寂滅故修無相三昧。已說菩薩修習三三昧，次說菩薩四法憂陀那。偈曰：

如前三三昧，　四印為依止，

菩薩如是說，　為利群生故。

釋曰：四法印者，一者一切行無常印、二者一切行苦印、三者一切法無我印、四者涅槃寂滅印。此中應知，無常印及苦印為成無願三昧依止，無我印為成空三昧依止，寂滅印為成無相三昧依止。菩薩說此四印為三三昧依止，皆為利益諸眾生故。問：何等是無常義乃至何等是寂滅義？偈曰：

無義分別義，　不真分別義，

息諸分別義，　是名四印義。

釋曰：此中諸菩薩以無義是無常義，由分別相畢竟常無故。以分別義是無我義，由分別相唯有分別，此二是分別相，由無體故。不真分別義是苦義，由三界心心法為苦體故，此是依他相。息諸分別義是寂滅義，此是真實相。復次應知依他相，復以剎那剎那壞為無常義。問：云何成立剎那壞義？

Ru-Yi Translation

Know that the twofold selflessness,
and the support of the twofold notion of self,
and that support's constant stillness —
are the objects ranged over by the three concentrations.

The exposition: the three concentrations have three objects: first, the twofold selflessness of person and phenomena, the object of the concentration of emptiness; second, the five aggregates of appropriation that support the twofold grasping of self, the object of the concentration of wishlessness; third, that support's utter stillness, the object of the concentration of signlessness. These three objects grasped are the three domains; the three subjects that grasp them are the three concentrations — hence, the three concentrations. Question: what is the meaning of the names of the three concentrations?

The concentration of emptiness is without discrimination;
wishlessness is weariness turning away from birth;
signlessness is constantly delighting in attainment —
of that support's constant stillness.

The exposition: the concentration of emptiness is without discrimination — the meaning of non-discrimination is the meaning of the concentration of emptiness, since it does not discriminate the twofold self of person and phenomena. Wishlessness is weariness turning away from birth — the meaning of weariness and turning away is the meaning of the concentration of wishlessness, since it is weary of and turns away from what supports the grasping of self. Signlessness is constantly delighting in attainment, of that support's constant stillness — the meaning of delighting in attainment is the meaning of the concentration of signlessness, since it delights in attaining that support's utter stillness. Question: how do the three concentrations arise?

What is to be known, and what is to be cut off,
and what is to be personally realized —
in this order, emptiness and the rest —
cultivation is of three kinds.

The exposition: what is to be known, what is to be cut off, and what is to be personally realized: what is to be known is the twofold selflessness of person and phenomena; what is to be cut off is what supports the twofold grasping of self; what is to be personally realized is that support's utter stillness. In this order, emptiness and the rest — cultivation is of three kinds: here, in order to know the twofold selflessness of person and phenomena, the concentration of emptiness is cultivated; in order to cut off what supports that twofold grasping, the concentration of wishlessness is cultivated; and in order to personally realize that support's utter stillness, the concentration of signlessness is cultivated. The bodhisattva's cultivation of the three concentrations having been explained, the bodhisattva's four Dharma seals are next explained.

Just as the three concentrations above,
the four seals are their support;
the bodhisattva teaches them thus,
for the benefit of the multitude of beings.

The exposition: the four Dharma seals are: first, the seal that all conditioned things are impermanent; second, the seal that all conditioned things are suffering; third, the seal that all dharmas are without self; fourth, the seal that nirvāṇa is still. Here, it should be known that the seals of impermanence and suffering serve as the support for accomplishing the concentration of wishlessness; the seal of no-self serves as the support for accomplishing the concentration of emptiness; and the seal of stillness serves as the support for accomplishing the concentration of signlessness. The bodhisattva teaches these four seals as the support for the three concentrations, entirely for the benefit of sentient beings. Question: what is the meaning of impermanence, and so on through the meaning of stillness?

The meaning of what is without meaning, the meaning of discrimination;
the meaning of unreal discrimination;
the meaning of the stilling of every discrimination —
these are the meanings of the four seals.

The exposition: here, the bodhisattva takes what is without meaning as the meaning of impermanence, since the discriminated mark is utterly, constantly without existence. He takes the meaning of discrimination as the meaning of no-self, since the discriminated mark exists only as discrimination — both what is discriminated and its self are the discriminated mark, being without any substance. He takes the meaning of unreal discrimination as the meaning of suffering, since the mind and mental factors of the three realms are the very substance of suffering — this is the dependent mark. And he takes the meaning of the stilling of every discrimination as the meaning of stillness — this is the true, real mark. Furthermore, it should be known that the dependent mark, additionally, is taken as the meaning of impermanence through its being destroyed moment by moment. Question: how is momentary destruction established?

Reading note

The four Dharma seals, in their classical form, function as an inventory of four separate marks of existence — impermanent, suffering, selfless, still — but this treatise instead assigns each seal a specific job supporting one of only three concentrations, with impermanence and suffering doubled up as joint support for the single concentration of wishlessness. The effect is to fold what looks like four independent doctrines into three functional roles: two seals that make one weary of grasping, one seal that empties what is grasped of any self, and one seal that lets stillness itself become an object of realization rather than a merely negative absence.

5. Establishing Momentary Destruction — Fifteen Reasons

Chinese Source

偈曰：

由起及從因，　相違亦不住，

無體與相定，　隨轉并滅盡，

變異因亦果，　執持與增上，

隨淨及隨生，　成義有十五。

釋曰：此二偈以十五義成立剎那剎那滅義，一由起、二從因、三相違、四不住、五無體、六相定、七隨轉、八滅盡、九變異、十因、十一果、十二執持、十三增上、十四隨淨、十五隨生。由此十五義，剎那壞義可得成立。

第一由起者，諸行相續流名起，若無剎那剎那滅義而有諸行相續流名起者不然。若汝言物有暫時住，後時先者滅、後者起名相續者，則無相續，由暫住時後起無故。

第二從因者，凡物前滅後起必籍因緣，若離因緣則無體故。若汝言彼物初因能生後時多果者，不然，初因作業即便滅盡，豈得與後諸果作因？若汝言初因起已更不起者，建立此因復何所用？若汝言起已未滅後時方滅者，彼至後時誰為滅因？

第三相違者，若汝復執是能起因復為滅因者，不然，起滅相違，同共一因無此理故，譬如光暗不並冷熱不俱，此亦如是，是故起因非即滅因。若如汝執諸行起已非即滅者，則違阿含及道理。違阿含者，佛語諸比丘：諸行如幻是壞滅法，是暫時法剎那不住。違道理者，諸修行人於諸行生滅中思惟剎那剎那滅，若不如是，於臨終時見彼滅相，則無厭惡離欲解脫，是則同餘凡夫。

第四不住者，若汝言諸行起已得有住者，為行自住？為因他住？若行自住，何故不能恒住？若因他住，彼住無體，何所可因？二俱不爾，是故剎那剎那滅義得成。

第五無體者，若汝執住因雖無壞因未至是故得住，壞因若至後時即滅，非如火變黑鐵者，不然，壞因畢竟無有體故。火變鐵譬，我無此理，鐵與火合黑相似滅赤相似起，能牽赤相似起是火功能，實非以火變於黑鐵。又如煎水至極少位，後水不生，亦非火合水方無體。

第六相定者，佛說有為法有為相一向決定，所謂無常。汝執諸行起已非即滅者，是有為法則有少時而非無常，便墮非一向相。

第七隨轉者，若汝言若物剎那剎那新生者，云何於中作舊物解？應說由相似隨轉得作是知，譬如燈焰相似起故起舊燈知，而實差別，前體無故。

第八滅盡者，若汝言云何得知後物非前？應說由滅盡故。若住不滅，則後剎那與初剎那住無差別；由有差別，故知後物而非前物。

第九變異者，若汝言物之初起非即變異者，不然，內外法體後邊不可得故。由初起即變，漸至明了，譬如乳至酪位酪相方現，而變體微細難可了知，由相似隨轉謂是前物，以是故剎那剎那滅義得成。

第十因者，若汝許心是剎那滅，彼心起因謂眼色等諸行，彼果剎那滅故因亦剎那，由不可以常因起無常果故。

第十一果者，彼眼等諸行亦是心果，是故剎那滅義得成，由不可以無常因起常果故。

第十二執持者，若汝言云何得知眼等諸行亦是心果？應說由心執持得增長故。

第十三增上者，又如佛說：心將世間去，心牽世間來，由心自在世間隨轉。識緣名色，此說亦爾，故知諸行是心果。

第十四隨淨者，淨是禪定人心，彼人諸行隨淨心轉。如經中說：修禪比丘具足神通心得自在，若欲令木為金則得隨意。故知諸行皆是心果。

第十五隨生者，如作罪眾生所得外物一切下劣，作福眾生所得外物一切妙好，故知諸行皆是心果。因是剎那、果非剎那，無此道理，由因自在故。如是總成立一切內外諸行是剎那已，次別成立內法是剎那。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through arising, and through following from a cause;
through contradiction, and through not abiding;
through the absence of any substance, and the fixedness of the mark;
through resembling continuation, and through utter cessation;

through transformation, cause, and fruit;
through retention, and through dominance;
through following purity, and through following karmic arising —
these fifteen meanings establish it.

The exposition: these two verses establish the meaning of momentary destruction through fifteen meanings: arising, following from a cause, contradiction, not abiding, absence of substance, the fixedness of the mark, resembling continuation, utter cessation, transformation, cause, fruit, retention, dominance, following purity, and following karmic arising. Through these fifteen meanings, the meaning of momentary destruction can be established.

First, through arising: conditioned things flowing on in continuity is called arising. Were there no meaning of momentary destruction, yet conditioned things still flowed on in continuity called arising, this would not hold. If you say a thing abides for a time, and afterward the earlier ceases and the later arises, and this is called continuity — then there would be no continuity at all, since during the time of temporary abiding, no later arising exists.

Second, through following from a cause: whenever a thing, having previously ceased, later arises, it must rely on causes and conditions; apart from causes and conditions, it would have no substance. If you say that a thing's initial cause is able to generate many later fruits, this does not hold — the initial cause, once it has performed its function, ceases entirely; how, then, could it act as cause for later fruits? If you say the initial cause, once arisen, arises no further, then what further use is there in establishing this cause? If you say that, having arisen, it does not yet cease, and only ceases at a later time — then, when that later time comes, what is the cause of its cessation?

Third, through contradiction: if you further hold that the very cause that gives rise to a thing is also the cause of its cessation, this does not hold — arising and cessation are contradictory, and there is no principle by which a single cause could serve both, just as light and darkness do not coexist, and cold and heat do not coincide; it is the same here — hence the cause of arising is not the very cause of cessation. If, as you hold, conditioned things, once arisen, do not immediately cease, this contradicts both the scriptures and reason. It contradicts the scriptures because the Buddha told the monks: conditioned things are like an illusion, a phenomenon of destruction, a temporary phenomenon, not abiding for even a moment. It contradicts reason because practitioners, contemplating the arising and cessation of conditioned things, reflect on their momentary destruction; were this not so, then, facing death and seeing the mark of cessation, there would be no weariness, no freedom from desire, no liberation — and one would be no different from any other ordinary being.

Fourth, through not abiding: if you say that conditioned things, once arisen, come to abide, does the conditioned thing abide by itself, or does it abide through some other cause? If it abides by itself, why can it not abide forever? If it abides through some other cause, that abiding has no substance of its own — what, then, could serve as its cause? Neither alternative holds; hence the meaning of momentary destruction is established.

Fifth, through absence of substance: if you hold that the cause of abiding is present while the cause of destruction has not yet arrived, and so the thing abides, and only ceases once the cause of destruction later arrives — unlike fire transforming black iron to red — this does not hold, since a cause of destruction is, in the end, without any substance at all. As for the simile of fire transforming iron, I hold no such view: when iron meets fire, its blackness resembles ceasing and its redness resembles arising — it is fire's function that draws forth the resemblance of redness arising; fire does not, in reality, transform black iron into something new. Likewise, when water is boiled down to almost nothing, no further water then arises — nor does this mean that water, once joined with fire, becomes without substance only then.

Sixth, through the fixedness of the mark: the Buddha taught that the conditioned mark of conditioned dharmas is fixed without exception — namely, impermanence. If you hold that conditioned things, once arisen, do not immediately cease, then a conditioned dharma would, for however brief a time, not be impermanent — and this would fall into a mark that is not fixed without exception.

Seventh, through resembling continuation: if you say that, were a thing newly arising moment by moment, how could one form the notion of it as the same, old thing — it should be answered that this knowledge arises through a resembling continuation: just as a lamp's flame, arising in resemblance moment by moment, gives rise to the notion of "the same old lamp," when in fact each moment differs, the prior substance having ceased to exist.

Eighth, through utter cessation: if you say, how is it known that a later thing is not the former one — it should be answered that this is known through utter cessation: were it to abide without ceasing, then the later moment's abiding would be no different from the first moment's abiding; since there is a difference, one knows the later thing is not the former thing.

Ninth, through transformation: if you say that a thing, at its very first arising, does not yet transform, this does not hold, since neither internal nor external dharmas can be found to have a fixed final state. Through transforming from its very first arising, it gradually becomes manifest — just as milk, arriving at the stage of curd, only then displays the mark of curd — yet the substance of this transformation is so subtle it is difficult to discern, and through its resembling continuation one takes it to be the former thing. For this reason, the meaning of momentary destruction is established.

Tenth, through cause: if you grant that mind is momentarily destroyed, then the causes that give rise to mind — namely, the conditioned things of eye, form, and the rest — since their fruit is momentarily destroyed, their cause must likewise be momentary, since a permanent cause cannot give rise to an impermanent fruit.

Eleventh, through fruit: those conditioned things — eye and the rest — are also mind's fruit; hence the meaning of momentary destruction is established, since an impermanent cause cannot give rise to a permanent fruit.

Twelfth, through retention: if you say, how is it known that eye and the other conditioned things are also mind's fruit — it should be answered that this is known because they are retained by mind and thereby grow.

Thirteenth, through dominance: further, as the Buddha taught: mind leads the world away, mind draws the world in — the world turns following mind's mastery. That consciousness conditions name-and-form is spoken in just this sense; hence one knows that conditioned things are mind's fruit.

Fourteenth, through following purity: purity is the mind of one in meditative concentration; that person's conditioned things turn following the pure mind. As a sūtra teaches: a monk who cultivates meditation, complete with supernormal powers, whose mind has attained mastery — should he wish wood to become gold, he attains it at will. Hence one knows that conditioned things are entirely mind's fruit.

Fifteenth, through following karmic arising: just as a being who commits wrongdoing obtains external things that are entirely inferior, while a being who does good obtains external things that are entirely excellent — hence one knows that conditioned things are entirely mind's fruit. There is no principle by which the cause is momentary yet the fruit is not, since the cause has mastery over it. Having thus established, in general, that all conditioned things — internal and external — are momentary, the momentariness of internal dharmas in particular is next established.

Reading note

These fifteen arguments are not fifteen independent proofs stacked for redundancy but a chain that keeps returning to a single move: whatever the objector proposes as a stable interval of "abiding" between arising and cessation, the treatise asks what causes the abiding to end, and shows that no answer is available that does not either smuggle in a second, unexplained cause or collapse the interval back to zero. The fire-and-iron simile in the fifth argument is worth pausing on for its own sake, since it is doing real philosophical work rather than merely illustrating: the treatise does not deny that iron visibly glows red under fire, but insists this is fire continuously replacing black moments with red-resembling ones, not fire acting on a single enduring piece of iron that "becomes" red — the same distinction the entire fifteen-part argument is built to defend at the level of every conditioned thing whatsoever.

Reason six is the sparest of the fifteen and perhaps the most purely logical: it does not appeal to scripture, simile, or an imagined counter-scenario, but simply notes that impermanence was already agreed to be an exceptionless mark of every conditioned dharma, and that any interval of static abiding, however brief, would be a counterexample to that very universality the objector has already granted. It functions less as a proof in its own right than as a lever the treatise uses to force the objector back onto positions the other fourteen arguments still need to close off.

6. Momentariness of Internal Dharmas — Fourteen Kinds of Arising, Nine Causes

Chinese Source

偈曰：

初起及續起，　長起及依起，

變起與熟起，　劣起亦勝起，

明起無明起，　及以異處起，

種起無種起，　像起十四起。

釋曰：此二偈以十四種起成立內法諸行是剎那義。一者初起，謂最初自體生。二者續起，謂除初剎那，餘剎那生。三者長起，謂眠食梵行正受長養故生。四者依起，謂眼等諸識依止眼等根生。五者變起，謂貪等染污令色等變生。六者熟起，謂成胎、嬰兒、童子、少壯、中年、老位等生。七者劣起，謂諸惡道生。八者勝起，謂諸善道生。九者明起，謂欲界後二天及色界無色界一切天生。十者無明起，謂除前明處所餘諸處生。十一者異處起，謂此處死彼處生。十二者種起，謂除阿羅漢最後五陰生。十三者無種起，謂前所除最後五陰生，由後生種子無故。十四者像起，謂入解脫禪者定自在力故諸行像生。問：復以何因成立此十四種起？偈曰：

續異及斷異，　隨長亦隨依，

住過及去過，　無住無無死，

亦有隨心相，　行者應當知，

如此九種因，　成前十四起。

釋曰：此二偈以九種因成立前十四起。九種因者，一續異、二斷異、三隨長、四隨依、五住過、六去過、七無住、八有死、九隨心。

第一續異者，此因成立第一初起。若最初起時因體無差別者，則後時諸行相續而起亦無差別，因體無差別故。由因有差別故，後餘諸行剎那得成。

第二斷異者，此因成立第二續起。若一一剎那無差別因者，則後時斷差別亦不可得。由斷有差別故，諸行剎那此義得成。

第三隨長者，此因成立第三長起。能令諸行圓滿故名為長，若無剎那而有諸行長養者，不然，由彼住故。若諸行得住，則不得漸大圓滿，非謂長養。

第四隨依者，此因成立第四依起。若執能依不住、所依得住者，不然，如人乘馬，人去馬不去，無有此理。如是識依於根，識有剎那，依無剎那，不然亦爾。

第五住過者，此因成立六起，謂變起、熟起、劣起、勝起、明起、無明起。成立變起、熟起者，若執諸行初起即住不滅者，不然，無變起故。謂貪等變色永不可得，由初無變後亦爾故。若初無變，後諸熟位亦不可得，由先有變後方熟故。成立劣起、勝起剎那亦爾。若執諸行得住，而有善惡熏習次第與果者，不然，諸行不住，次第相續各得與果，此義可爾。成立明起、無明起剎那亦爾。若諸行得住則明起亦無，不住則有，由心轉故。無無明起亦爾，後時無變異故。

第六去過者，此因成立第十一異處起。若執諸行往餘處名去者，不然，我今問汝：諸行去作為起已將諸行往餘處？為不起將諸行往餘處？若起已將往者，此處起已餘處不起，此即是住，而言去者是義相違。若不起將往者，不起則本來無去，而言去者，此語無義。又復若諸行去，作住此處，即作所作令諸行去，是亦不然，住則不得到餘處故。若諸行到餘處方作所作，是亦不然，無有離去而有諸行到餘處故。若此處住、若餘處住，離諸行外畢竟求作不可得，是故不異諸行相續而有去。作去既無體，則剎那義成。若汝言若實無去，云何世人見去？應說由無間相續假說名去，實無去體。若汝言復有何因諸行得相續去？應說因緣無量，有心力自在，如威儀等去；有宿業自在，如中陰中去？有手力自在，如放箭擲石去；有依止自在，如乘車乘船去；有使力自在，如風吹物去；有自體自在，如風性傍去、火性上去、水性下去、有術力自，在如依呪依藥在空而去；有磁石自在，能令鐵去；有通力自在；如乘通去。如是等有無量因緣，能令諸行相續，假說名去。是義應知。

第七無住者，此因成立第十二種起。若諸行得住，餘時更有種子起者，不然，剎那剎那無餘因故。若諸行不住，後種子起，是義可然。

第八有死者，此因成立第十三無種起。若無剎那，而有死時無種起者，不然。先有種起，後命終時，方無種起，是亦不然。由一一剎那因無體故，是故死心剎那不可得成。

第九隨心者，此因成立第十四像起。由心自在剎那剎那彼像得起，若無剎那而像得起，無此理故。問：如是別成立內有為法剎那已，復有何因能成立外法四大及六種造色是剎那耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Initial arising, and continued arising;
growing arising, and dependent arising;
transforming arising, and ripened arising;
inferior arising, and superior arising;

luminous arising, and non-luminous arising;
and arising in another place;
arising as a seed, and arising without a seed;
image arising — fourteen arisings.

The exposition: these two verses establish the momentariness of internal dharmas' conditioned things through fourteen kinds of arising. First, initial arising: the very first generation of one's own being. Second, continued arising: generation in every moment apart from the first. Third, growing arising: generation nourished and grown through sleep, food, the holy life, and correct absorption. Fourth, dependent arising: the eye consciousness and the other consciousnesses generated resting on the eye and the other faculties. Fifth, transforming arising: form and the rest generated as transformed by craving and the other defilements. Sixth, ripened arising: generation as the completed embryo, infant, child, youth, middle age, old age, and so on. Seventh, inferior arising: generation in the evil destinies. Eighth, superior arising: generation in the good destinies. Ninth, luminous arising: generation in the last two heavens of the desire realm and in every heaven of the form and formless realms. Tenth, non-luminous arising: generation in every place besides the luminous places just named. Eleventh, arising in another place: dying in this place, being generated in that place. Twelfth, arising as a seed: generation of the five aggregates apart from an arhat's very last. Thirteenth, arising without a seed: generation of that very last set of five aggregates just excepted, since no seed for a further generation exists. Fourteenth, image arising: one who has entered the liberative meditations, through the power of mastery in concentration, generating conditioned things as mere images. Question: by what further causes are these fourteen kinds of arising established?

Difference in continuation, and difference in interruption;
following growth, and following dependence;
the fault of abiding, and the fault of going;
not abiding, and the fault of no death;

and, further, following the mark of mind —
the practitioner should know that
these nine causes
establish the fourteen arisings above.

The exposition: these two verses establish the fourteen arisings above through nine causes: difference in continuation, difference in interruption, following growth, following dependence, the fault of abiding, the fault of going, not abiding, the fault of no death, and following mind.

First, difference in continuation: this cause establishes the first, initial arising. Were there no difference in the substance of the cause at the very first arising, then the later conditioned things arising in continuation would likewise show no difference, since the cause's substance would show no difference. Because there is, in fact, a difference in cause, the momentariness of the subsequent conditioned things is established.

Second, difference in interruption: this cause establishes the second, continued arising. Were there no difference of cause in each and every moment, then a later difference of interruption could not be found either. Because there is, in fact, a difference of interruption, the momentariness of conditioned things is established by this meaning.

Third, following growth: this cause establishes the third, growing arising. Being able to bring conditioned things to fullness is called growth; were there no momentariness, yet conditioned things still grew and were nourished, this would not hold, since they would then be abiding. Were conditioned things to abide, they could not gradually increase to fullness — this would not be growth at all.

Fourth, following dependence: this cause establishes the fourth, dependent arising. Were it held that the dependent does not abide while its support abides, this does not hold — it is like a person riding a horse: there is no principle by which the person goes while the horse does not go. It is the same here: consciousness depends on the faculty; for consciousness to be momentary while its support is not momentary likewise does not hold.

Fifth, the fault of abiding: this cause establishes six arisings — transforming, ripened, inferior, superior, luminous, and non-luminous arising. As to establishing transforming and ripened arising: were it held that conditioned things, at their very first arising, already abide without ceasing, this does not hold, since there would then be no transforming arising at all — craving and the other defilements' transformation of form could never be found, since if there were no transformation at first, there could be none later either. And if there were no transformation at first, the later stages of ripening could not be found either, since ripening only comes about because transformation occurred first. Establishing inferior and superior arising as momentary follows the same reasoning: were it held that conditioned things abide, yet wholesome and unwholesome habituation still gave their fruits in sequence, this does not hold — only if conditioned things do not abide, but continue in succession, can each in turn properly give its fruit. Establishing luminous and non-luminous arising as momentary follows the same reasoning: were conditioned things to abide, luminous arising, too, would not exist; only because they do not abide does it exist, since mind turns on. The absence of non-luminous arising follows likewise, since there would then be no transformation at any later time.

Sixth, the fault of going: this cause establishes the eleventh, arising in another place. Were it held that conditioned things going to another place is called "going," this does not hold — I now ask you: does "going" mean the conditioned things, having arisen, then carry themselves to another place? Or does it mean, without having arisen, they carry themselves to another place? If, having arisen, they carry themselves there — then, having arisen in this place, they do not arise in the other place; this is simply abiding, and to call it "going" contradicts this very meaning. If, without having arisen, they carry themselves there — then, not having arisen, there was, to begin with, no going at all, and to call it "going" is a meaningless statement. Furthermore, if conditioned things go, whether the action is performed while abiding in this place — this too does not hold, since, abiding, they could not reach another place; or whether the action is performed only once they reach another place — this too does not hold, since there is no reaching another place apart from the going of conditioned things themselves. Whether abiding in this place or abiding in the other place, apart from conditioned things themselves, no "action" whatsoever can ultimately be found — hence "going" is nothing other than the very continuity of conditioned things. Since the action of going itself has no substance of its own, the meaning of momentariness is thereby established. If you say, if there is truly no going, how do ordinary people perceive going — it should be answered that this is conventionally called "going" owing to uninterrupted continuity, there being, in reality, no substance of going at all. If you ask, then by what cause do conditioned things come to continue as "going" — it should be answered that the causes and conditions are innumerable: there is mastery through the force of mind, as in going through bodily deportment; mastery through past karma, as in going through the intermediate state; mastery through the force of the hand, as in an arrow shot or a stone thrown; mastery through a support, as in going by carriage or by boat; mastery through the force of an agent, as in an object blown by the wind; mastery through one's own nature, as wind's nature is to go sideways, fire's nature to go upward, water's nature to go downward; mastery through the power of technique, as going through the air by reliance on a spell or a medicine; mastery through a lodestone, able to make iron go; and mastery through supernormal power, as going by riding the superknowledges. There are innumerable such causes and conditions of this kind, able to make conditioned things continue, conventionally called "going." This meaning should be understood.

Seventh, not abiding: this cause establishes the twelfth, arising as a seed. Were conditioned things to abide, and at some further time a seed still arose, this would not hold, since, moment by moment, there would be no further cause. Only if conditioned things do not abide can a later seed's arising rightly occur.

Eighth, the fault of no death: this cause establishes the thirteenth, arising without a seed. Were there no momentariness, yet at the time of death there were still an arising without a seed, this would not hold. Nor would it hold that there is a seed's arising first, and only later, at the ending of the lifespan, comes an arising without a seed — since, in each and every moment, the cause has no substance of its own; hence, without momentariness, the mind at death could never be established.

Ninth, following mind: this cause establishes the fourteenth, image arising. Through mind's mastery, that image arises moment by moment; were there no momentariness, yet the image still arose, there would be no principle by which this could occur. Question: internal conditioned dharmas having thus been separately established as momentary, by what further causes can the four great elements and the six kinds of derived form belonging to external dharmas be established as momentary?

Reading note

The sixth cause's refutation of "going" is the most purely philosophical passage in this entire argument, since it does not merely defend momentariness against a rival account but dissolves an ordinary concept — motion — into nothing but a convenient way of talking about continuity. Neither horn of the dilemma the treatise poses (does the arisen thing carry itself elsewhere, or does the not-yet-arisen thing carry itself elsewhere) survives scrutiny, and what remains once both are refuted is not silence but a long list of the many specific causes — bodily deportment, karmic momentum, a thrown stone, a lodestone's pull — that make the useful fiction of "going" applicable in each different case, even though no single thing named "going" is ever actually found to exist behind any of them.

7. Momentariness of External Dharmas, and a Final Challenge

Chinese Source

偈曰：

由滋及由涸，　性動增亦減，

二起與四變，　薪力及漸微，

亦說隨心起，　及以難問成，

一切諸外法，　無非剎那體。

釋曰：此二偈以十四因成立外法是剎那。水有二因，一滋、二涸。若無剎那，水或時滋長或時乾涸不可顯現。若人作如是問：既無剎那水，有何因而滋？復有何因而涸？彼則不能答。今見水有滋涸，故知剎那是水滋涸因。

風有三因，一性動、二增盛、三減息。若風性住則無動時，行無體故。亦無增盛、亦無減息，由彼住故。

地有六因，謂二起四變。二起者，由水由風地起可得，謂劫生時彼地是水風果，故知地亦是剎那。四變者，由四所作，地變可得。一業力所作，由眾生業力有差別故。二人功所作，由掘鑿等故。三諸大所作，由火水風故。四時節所作，由時改轉異相現故。若無剎那，四變不可得，因無體故。如地有六因知是剎那，色香味觸六因亦爾，是故亦是剎那。

火有一因，所謂薪力。薪力火增故火得起，已共火起薪即不得住，火燒薪已火亦不住。若火不由薪，後時無薪，火應久住，由隨同義故。

火聲在後，說聲有一因，所謂漸微。譬如鍾聲，後時漸微可得。若無剎那，後時小聲無可得理。

法入色有一因，謂隨心起。如受戒時隨心下中上起，心因剎那故，彼果亦剎那。是故外法剎那亦成。

復次總由難問故。我今問汝：何故欲得諸行無常，不欲得諸行剎那滅？若汝言一一剎那滅不可知者，不然，譬如燈焰，於不動位彼剎那亦不可知，汝何故不欲令彼體無剎那？若汝言燈焰體有剎那細故不可覺者，諸行亦爾，何故不欲令有剎那？若汝言燈焰與諸行不相似者，不然，不相似有二種：一自性不相似、二時分不相似。若此自性不相似者，此譬得成，非自體為譬故。非如以燈喻燈、以牛喻牛，譬則不成。若取時分不相似者，此譬亦成，由燈焰及諸行皆剎那相似故，若非剎那譬則不成。今更問汝：如人乘乘，其乘住時其人去不？答：不。若爾者，所依根住，能依識去，亦無道理。若汝言：何故？現見燈焰念念滅，燈炷如是住。應說汝見非見，由炷相續剎那剎那有壞有起，汝不如實知故。

若汝言：諸行剎那如燈焰者，世人何故不知？應說由諸行是顛倒物故，相續剎那隨轉此不可知，而實別別起，世人謂是前物，生顛倒知。若不爾，則無無常常倒。倒體若無，染污亦無，復從何處而有解脫？由是難問，則諸行剎那成。成立無常義已，次成立無我義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through moistening, and through drying;
its nature's motion, increase, and decrease;
twofold arising, and fourfold transformation;
the force of fuel, and gradual attenuation;

and, further, arising following mind,
and establishment through challenge —
every external dharma, without exception,
has momentariness as its very substance.

The exposition: these two verses establish external dharmas as momentary through fourteen causes. Water has two causes: moistening, and drying. Were there no momentariness, water's sometimes moistening and increasing, and sometimes drying up, could not be manifest. Were someone to ask: given no momentariness in water, by what cause does it moisten, and by what further cause does it dry — this could not be answered. Since water is now seen to moisten and to dry, one knows that momentariness is the cause of water's moistening and drying.

Wind has three causes: its nature is motion, its increase is intensifying, and its decrease is subsiding. Were wind's nature to abide, there would be no time of motion, since the conditioned thing would have no substance of its own; nor would there be intensifying or subsiding, since it would then be abiding.

Earth has six causes: twofold arising, and fourfold transformation. As to twofold arising: earth is found to arise from water and from wind — at the time of the eon's genesis, that earth was the fruit of water and wind — hence one knows earth, too, is momentary. As to fourfold transformation: earth's transformation is found through four kinds of action: first, action by the force of karma, since sentient beings' karmic force differs; second, action by human labor, through digging and excavating and the rest; third, action by the great elements, through fire, water, and wind; fourth, action by the seasons, through the changing appearance that time brings about. Were there no momentariness, these four transformations could not be found, since the cause would have no substance. Just as earth's momentariness is known through these six causes, so too are the six causes of form, smell, taste, and the tangible known — hence these, too, are momentary.

Fire has one cause: namely, the force of fuel. Through the force of fuel, fire increases and is thereby able to arise; once fuel has arisen together with fire, the fuel can no longer abide, and once fire has burned the fuel, the fire, too, does not abide. Were fire not dependent on fuel, then, once fuel later ran out, fire should still long abide — by the same principle of parallel dependence that it does not.

Following the discussion of fire, sound is next taught to have one cause: namely, gradual attenuation. Just as with the sound of a bell, its gradual attenuation over time can be observed; were there no momentariness, there would be no way for its sound to become fainter over time.

Form included within the mental sphere has one cause: namely, arising following mind. Just as, at the time of receiving the precepts, it arises following the mind's inferior, middling, or superior grade — since mind, the cause, is momentary, its fruit, too, is momentary. Hence external dharmas' momentariness is likewise established.

Further, this is established, in sum, through challenge. I now ask you: why do you wish to accept that conditioned things are impermanent, yet do not wish to accept that conditioned things are momentarily destroyed? If you say that momentary destruction, moment by moment, cannot be known — this does not hold: consider a lamp's flame — even in what appears to be a motionless state, its momentariness likewise cannot be directly known; why, then, do you refuse to accept that its own substance, too, is momentary? If you say that the flame's substance has a momentariness too subtle to perceive — conditioned things are just the same; why, then, do you refuse to accept that they too have momentariness? If you say the flame and conditioned things are not comparable — this does not hold: non-comparability is of two kinds — non-comparability in own-nature, and non-comparability in temporal duration. If it is non-comparability in own-nature that you intend, then this simile still holds, since a simile need not share the very same substance as what it illustrates — otherwise, only a lamp could illustrate a lamp, only an ox could illustrate an ox, and no simile could ever hold at all. If it is non-comparability in temporal duration that you intend, this simile still holds, since both the lamp's flame and conditioned things resemble one another precisely in being momentary — were they not momentary, the simile could not hold. Let me ask you further: when a person rides in a vehicle, does the person go while the vehicle stays put? Answer: no. If so, then for the supporting faculty to stay while the supported consciousness goes is likewise without principle. If you say: why, then, is it that the lamp's flame is directly seen to be extinguished moment by moment, while the lamp's wick is seen to abide just as it is — it should be answered that what you see is not what you think you see: the wick, too, continues in destruction and arising moment by moment, and you simply do not know this as it really is.

If you say: if conditioned things are momentary like a lamp's flame, why do ordinary people not know this — it should be answered that, since conditioned things are the very object of inverted perception, their momentary continuation cannot be directly known; though they in fact arise as separate, distinct moments, ordinary people take them to be the same, prior thing, giving rise to inverted knowledge. Were this not so, there would be no inverted perception of the impermanent as permanent. And if this inversion had no substance to speak of, then there would be no defilement either — and from where, then, would liberation come? Through this line of challenge, the momentariness of conditioned things is thereby established. Momentariness having been established, the meaning of no-self is next established.

Reading note

The fourteen causes across water, wind, earth, fire, sound, and mental form are not applying one uniform argument fourteen times but selecting, in each case, whichever specific phenomenon about that element could not be explained without momentariness — moistening and drying for water, the three states of motion for wind, geological transformation for earth, fuel-dependence for fire, fading volume for sound. The variety is itself an argument: momentariness is not being imposed on the physical world from an a priori commitment but recovered, element by element, from the ordinary behavior each already visibly displays.

The closing challenge's vehicle simile lands with unusual force because it inverts the reader's own analogy against them: anyone who accepts that a rider moving while seated in a stationary-seeming vehicle is absurd has already granted the very principle — that the supported cannot outlast or outrun its support — the treatise needs to argue that a supposedly momentary consciousness cannot rest on a supposedly enduring faculty. The final answer to why ordinary people fail to notice momentariness is worth sitting with in full: not that momentariness is a subtle technical fact easily missed, but that failing to see it is definitionally what an inverted, defiled mind does — which means, the treatise implies, that the entire prospect of liberation depends on this fact being real and simply overlooked, not on it being uncertain.

Question: is the person to be said to exist, or to be said not to exist?

The person is conventional, not really existent;
to speak of it as real is to find nothing real there —
since it is established, through inversion and defilement,
as the cause of defilement.

The exposition: the person is conventional, not really existent — the person may be said to exist as a mere convention, not as a real substance. On this view, one falls into neither one-sided extreme, being free of both the extreme of existence and non-existence. Question: is the person really existent — how, then, is it known to be non-existent? Answer: because nothing real can be found. That person cannot be found to be real in the way form and the rest can, since it is not something realized by direct cognition and wisdom. Question: granting that the person is not realized by direct cognition and wisdom — the Buddha nevertheless said that a self is presently to be found; your claim that it cannot be found does not hold. Answer: this "can be found" refers to something found through inversion, not something really found — since the Buddha's teaching that one conceives a self where there is no self is precisely what is called inversion. Question: how is this known to be an inversion? Answer: because of defilement. The view of a substantial self is a defilement — namely, the grasping of self and what belongs to self; were it not an inversion, it would not be a defilement. Question: how is it known that the grasping of self is a defilement? Answer: because it is the cause of defilement — since craving and the other defilements arise with the grasping of self as their cause, one knows it to be a defilement.

Question: as you grant, the person is said to conventionally exist with respect to the five aggregates of form and the rest — is this person one with the aggregates, or different from them?

The conventional person and the real aggregates
cannot be said to be either one or different;
were one to say they are one, or different,
two faults would arise.

The exposition: the conventional person and the aggregates cannot be said to be either one or different; were one and different to be asserted, two faults would arise. The two faults: were one to say the person and the aggregates are one, then the aggregates would simply be the person, and the person would be real. Were one to say the person and the aggregates are different, then, although the aggregates would not be the person, the person would still be real. For this reason, the person is a mere designation; whether one or different cannot be said — this is why the Tathāgata's setting this question aside without answering it is thereby established as correct.

If one holds the person to be real,
then whether one or different should be answerable;
that whether one or different cannot be said —
this claim, then, has no principle behind it.

The exposition: if a person, going against the Teacher's instruction, holds that a real person exists, then whether this real person is one with or different from the aggregates should be answerable; yet to hold that whether one or different with the aggregates cannot be said, while also holding it is real, is a claim with no principle behind it. If you say the person cannot be said to be one or different from the aggregates, just as fire is neither different from nor not different from fuel — this does not hold.

Through the distinctness of their marks, and through worldly perception;
the noble ones' own teaching, too, does not support this;
fire and fuel are not beyond speaking of,
since both can be found.

The exposition: through the distinctness of their marks: fire is the fire element, fuel is made of the other elements, each having its own distinct mark; hence fire and fuel are different. Through worldly perception: ordinary people see fuel apart from fire — such as wood yet to be burned — and also see fire apart from fuel — as when wind blows a flame away from its fuel; hence fire and fuel are different. The noble ones' own teaching, too, does not support this: the World-Honored Buddha nowhere taught that whether fire and fuel are one or different cannot be said; hence your holding that whether fire and fuel are one or different cannot be said has no principle behind it. If you say that fire is never seen apart from fuel, and that the wind aspect simply is the fuel — this does not hold, since both can be found separately, fire and wind having two distinct marks.

Reading note

The "two faults" argument is a small, self-contained dilemma worth isolating from its surrounding context: saying the person is identical to the aggregates makes the aggregates themselves real in an objectionable sense, while saying it is different from them makes the person a separate real entity — either answer smuggles in exactly the substantial self the whole argument is meant to rule out. What makes this more than a debating trick is that the treatise then explains why the Buddha's own refusal to answer whether self and the aggregates are one or different was not evasion but the only coherent response available once one has correctly seen that "person" names a convention rather than a referent that could stand in either relation to anything.

The fire-and-fuel rebuttal is aimed at a real rival position — a Pudgalavādin-style claim that the person is neither identical to nor different from the aggregates, comparable to how fire is neither identical to nor separate from what it burns — and the treatise's counter is that this comparison actually undercuts its own conclusion: fire and fuel are observably distinguishable both by their differing marks and by ordinary perception, which is precisely why no scripture ever needed to declare their relationship indescribable. If fire and fuel are describable after all, the analogy meant to shelter an indescribable person collapses along with it.

9. Refuting the Person as Perceiver and as Agent

二有故識起，　人緣則非義，

Furthermore, verse:

Since it is through the presence of two conditions that consciousness arises,
taking the person as a condition is without meaning;
the desired ceasing and the undesired arising —
to speak of the person as generating these is likewise without principle.

The exposition: if one holds that a person is really existent, calling it the seer, the hearer, the perceiver, the cognizer, the eater, the knower, the speaker — then, as to the arising of the eye consciousness and the rest, is the person said to be their agent by being their condition, or is the person said to be their agent by being their master? If the person is their condition — since it is through the presence of two conditions that consciousness arises, taking the person as a condition is without meaning, since no power belonging to the person whatsoever can be seen in the arising of consciousness. Furthermore, verse:

Within your holding the person to be real,
what function can be established for it?
Forcing what is unreal to be real
contradicts the Buddha's threefold enlightenment.

The exposition: if the person were really existent, by what function could you establish it? Whatever is really existent must necessarily have some function — as the pure form of the eye and the rest is established through the function of seeing and the rest. The person has no such function by which it could be established; hence the person is not really existent. Furthermore, your forcing what is unreal — the person — to be treated as real contradicts the Tathāgata's three kinds of enlightenment: first, profound enlightenment; second, unshared enlightenment; third, transcendent enlightenment. Were a real person to be seen, this would not be profound enlightenment, would not be enlightenment unshared with outsiders, and would not be enlightenment unfamiliar to the ordinary world. Hence this holding is what the ordinary world grasps at, what outsiders cling to, and what is constantly practiced in birth-and-death.

Furthermore, if the person is the seer, and so on through the cognizer — do the eye and the other faculties have their own function, or no function of their own? If they have their own function, does it arise spontaneously, or does it arise through the person? Question: what doubt does this raise?

If the function arises spontaneously,
then three faults arise;
if the person serves as its condition,
then the eye and the rest would have no function of their own.

The exposition: if it is said that the function of the eye and the rest arises spontaneously, then the person performs no deed at all with respect to the eye and the rest, and three faults arise. If it is said that the function arises through the person as its condition, then the eye and the other faculties would have no function of their own whatsoever. The person would not be the agent;
the function would not always be arising;
arising would not occur all at once —

The exposition: were the function of the eye and the rest to arise spontaneously, not depending on the person's action, then the person would not be an agent — how, then, could it be called the seer, and so on through the cognizer? This is the first fault. Were the function of the eye and the rest to arise spontaneously, it should then always be arising, and should not sometimes arise and sometimes not; this is the second fault. Were the function of the eye and the rest always arising, then its arising should occur all at once — why, then, could all the senses not function simultaneously? This is the third fault. For this reason, to say that it arises spontaneously does not hold. Question: what further fault attaches to taking the person as the condition?

If the person abides, the function was previously absent;
if the person perishes, then the person is cut off;
and were there some further, third substance,
there is no such thing to serve as the condition.

The exposition: if it is said that the person's abiding serves as the condition for its function — since the person would then be constantly existent, why would the function be absent at first and only present later? This does not hold. If it is said that the person's perishing serves as the condition — then the person would fall into impermanence; this too does not hold. If it is said that there is some further, third person, neither abiding nor perishing, that serves as the condition — no such thing exists. Reasoned in this way, a real person cannot be found.

Reading note

The dilemma between person-as-condition and person-as-master is worth reading as two different pictures of what agency would even mean if a self were real. As a mere condition, the person turns out to contribute no describable causal power the two ordinary conditions of faculty and object don't already supply — it becomes an idle wheel. As a master exercising actual control, it runs immediately into an empirical fact anyone can verify by introspection: unwanted mental states keep arising and wanted ones keep vanishing, exactly contrary to what a genuine controller should permit. The argument's force comes from neither horn requiring anything exotic — both are refuted by observations available to anyone paying ordinary attention to their own experience.

10. Scriptural Refutations, and Why the Buddha Still Speaks of "a Person"

Chinese Source

復次偈曰：

諸法無我印，　及說真實空，

有我有五過，　是故知無我。

釋曰：《法印經》中佛說：一切法無我。《真實空經》中佛說：有業有報，作者不可得。捨前陰起後陰，起滅唯法。《增五經》中說：若執有我，有五過失：一者墮於見處起我見命者見、二者同於外道、三者僻行邪行、四者於空不欲不信不住、五者聖法不得清淨。如是依阿含說，有實人亦不可得。

問：若無實人，云何世尊處處經中而說有人，謂知者、負擔者及建立隨信行等人耶？偈曰：

由依染淨法，　位斷說有異，

行異相續異，　無實假說人。

釋曰：由依染污法及清淨法有位差別及斷差別，故建立假人有差別。若無假人差別，則不可說有行差別及相續差別。如《知經》中說：何等諸法謂染污法？何等為知謂清淨法？如《負擔經》中說：何者負擔？謂染污法。何者棄擔？謂清淨法。若無行差別及相續差別，則不可說此二法為知者、負擔者。菩提分法多位差別，謂方便道、見道、修道、究竟道。若無行及相續差別，則不可說彼菩提分法有隨信行等人差別。由無實人，約法差別可得假說。以此道理，故知所說但是假人。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, verse:

The seal that all dharmas are without self,
and the teaching of true emptiness;
having a self carries five faults —
hence one knows there is no self.

The exposition: in the Dharma Seal Sūtra, the Buddha taught: all dharmas are without self. In the True Emptiness Sūtra, the Buddha taught: there is karma and there is its ripening, yet no agent can be found; the former aggregates are relinquished, and later aggregates arise — arising and ceasing belong only to dharmas. In the Increase-by-Five Sūtra, it is taught: holding that there is a self carries five faults: first, falling into a view, giving rise to the view of a self and the view of a life-principle; second, being the same as outsiders; third, deviant conduct and wrong practice; fourth, being unwilling toward, without faith in, and unable to abide in emptiness; fifth, being unable to attain the purity of the noble Dharma. Reasoned in this way, according to the scriptures, a real person cannot be found either.

Question: if there is no real person, why does the World-Honored One, in sūtra after sūtra, speak of a person — calling it the knower, the bearer of the burden, and establishing such persons as the one who follows through conviction, and the rest?

Resting on defiled and pure dharmas,
their stages and their severance are said to differ;
conduct differs, and continuity differs —
this is the unreal, conventionally spoken person.

The exposition: resting on defiled dharmas and pure dharmas, there are differences of stage and differences of severance; hence differences among conventional persons are established. Were there no differences among conventional persons, one could not speak of differences in conduct or differences in continuity. As the Knower Sūtra teaches: what dharmas are called defiled? What is called knowing is the pure dharmas. As the Burden Sūtra teaches: what is the burden? The defiled dharmas. What is relinquishing the burden? The pure dharmas. Were there no difference in conduct or in continuity, these two dharmas could not be spoken of as "the knower" and "the one who bears the burden." The dharmas belonging to the factors of enlightenment have many differences of stage — the path of skillful means, the path of seeing, the path of cultivation, and the ultimate path. Were there no difference in conduct or in continuity, one could not speak of these dharmas belonging to the factors of enlightenment as having distinctions of person such as one who follows through conviction. Since there is no real person, conventional speech is possible only in terms of differences among dharmas. By this reasoning, one knows that what is spoken of is merely a conventional person.

Reading note

The answer to why the Buddha still speaks of "the knower" or "the bearer of the burden" is not that these are convenient fictions floating free of anything real, but that they track genuine differences — of stage, of severance, of what has and has not yet been abandoned — among dharmas themselves. "Person" language, on this account, is not arbitrary shorthand; it is the only available way to speak about differences in conduct and continuity that are real, once one has correctly identified what is doing the differing. The convention is licensed by real distinctions in the dharmas, even though nothing real corresponds to the word "person" taken as a single referent.

11. Not for Arousing Self-View; the Chapter's Close

Chinese Source

若佛意不說是假人，說實人則無用，由起眾生我見故。偈曰：

不為起我見，　由見已起故；

無始已習故，　無用應解脫。

釋曰：佛不應為起眾生我見說有實人，由眾生我見先已起故。亦不為令眾生數習我見說有實人，由眾生我見先已習故。亦不為令我見眾生得解脫故說有實人，一切無功用者皆應自然得解脫故。以是故一切未見諦者有我見而無解脫，非如苦體先時不見後時方見。人不如是，非先不見後時方見。又如苦體先時不見後亦不見，即無解脫；人體亦爾，先時亦見後時亦見，則無解脫。若實有我則決定有我所，從此二執即起我愛及餘煩惱，如是則無解脫。以是故不應欲得有實人，以我見等過皆悉起故。如是別說菩提分已，次總結前義。偈曰：

慚羞等功德，　菩薩常具足，

自利既不捨，　亦令他利成。

釋曰：此義如前所顯略說。覺分品究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Were it not the Buddha's intention to speak of a merely conventional person, then speaking of a real person would serve no purpose, since it would only give rise to sentient beings' view of a self.

Not for the sake of giving rise to the view of a self —
since this view has already arisen;
since it has already been habituated from beginningless time —
it would serve no purpose; and liberation should follow of itself.

The exposition: the Buddha would not have spoken of a real person for the sake of giving rise to sentient beings' view of a self, since sentient beings' view of a self had already arisen beforehand. Nor would he have spoken of a real person in order to make sentient beings habituate the view of a self further, since sentient beings' view of a self had already been habituated beforehand. Nor would he have spoken of a real person in order to liberate beings from the view of a self, since anyone requiring no effort at all should then attain liberation spontaneously. For this reason, every being who has not yet seen the truths has the view of a self yet has no liberation — unlike the substance of suffering, which is not seen at first and only seen later. The person is not like this — it is not that it goes unseen at first and is only seen later. Moreover: just as, were the substance of suffering not seen at first nor seen later either, there would be no liberation — so too with the substance of a person: whether seen at first or seen later, there would, in either case, be no liberation. Were there really a self, there would necessarily also be something belonging to that self, and from these two graspings, self-love and the other afflictions would arise — and thus there would be no liberation at all. For this reason, one should not wish there to be a real person, since all the faults of self-view and the rest would thereby entirely arise. The factors of enlightenment having thus been separately explained, the foregoing meaning is now summarized as a whole.

Shame and embarrassment and the other merits —
the bodhisattva is forever complete with them;
neither forsaking benefit for himself,
he also brings about benefit for others.

The exposition: this meaning is as already displayed above, stated here in brief. The chapter on the factors of enlightenment is complete.

Reading note

The argument that a real self cannot even be defended as a useful pedagogical fiction is easy to skim past, but it closes off an escape route a modern reader might reach for instinctively — "perhaps the Buddha taught self language because it helps beings eventually let go of self-view, the way a ladder is climbed and then discarded." The treatise blocks this directly: self-view is not something the Buddha's teaching could be introducing, since it is already present in every unawakened being from beginningless habituation, and it is not something a real self could help dissolve either, since a real self's presence is exactly what generates self-love and the rest of the afflictions that block liberation in the first place. There is no version of the ladder metaphor available once self-view is understood to be the disease rather than a stage on the way to the cure.

The chapter's final verse closes 覺分品第二十一 by looping back to its very first topic across two fascicles — the sense of shame examined at the start of fascicle 10 — rather than to the philosophically weightier material on momentariness and no-self that has just filled most of this fascicle. That the treatise chooses shame as the chapter's summarizing image, over the classical thirty-seven factors or the metaphysics defending them, is itself a small argument: everything this chapter has established, however abstract, is offered in the end as something inseparable from an ordinary, recognizably ethical disposition — never forsaking one's own benefit while still bringing about another's.

Fascicle 11 Complete

覺分品第二十一 closes here, and its second half turns out to be the treatise's most demanding sustained argument so far. After a run of practices — calm-abiding and insight distinguished across five stages of the bodhisattva path, five skillful means unshared with the two vehicles, three kinds of dhāraṇī graded by how they were acquired rather than how they feel, vows caused by wisdom alone, three concentrations keyed to three distinct supports, and four Dharma seals reassigned to back only three of them — the fascicle pivots into philosophy proper. Momentary destruction is established first through fifteen general reasons, each closing off one way an opponent might posit a stable interval of "abiding" between arising and cessation; then through fourteen kinds of arising peculiar to internal, mental dharmas, traced to nine underlying causes, including a sustained dissolution of ordinary "going" into nothing but causally continuous appearance; then through fourteen further causes covering water, wind, earth, fire, sound, and mental form; and finally through a direct challenge built on the humble image of a lamp's flame, concluding that failing to see momentariness is not an innocent oversight but definitionally what a still-deluded mind does.

The same rigor is then turned on the self: neither identical to nor different from the aggregates it is confused with, refuted through the fire-and-fuel analogy a rival school had used to shelter it, incapable of serving as either the condition or the master behind any sense faculty's function, unsupportable by scripture, and finally accounted for as a conventional way of tracking real differences among dharmas — a convention the Buddha had every reason to keep using and no reason to abandon, since self-view was never something his teaching introduced and a real self could never have been the cure for it. The chapter's very last verse closes not on this metaphysics but on the sense of shame examined at the start of fascicle 10, folding two fascicles of argument back into a single, recognizably ethical disposition.
