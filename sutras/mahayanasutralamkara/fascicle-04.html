Fascicle 4 · 明信品第十一、述求品第十二之一 · Complete

Faith and Understanding, and Seeking

After a fascicle on enlightenment itself, this fascicle turns to the ordinary practitioner's own instruments: faith, and how it is sought and put to work. The chapter on faith gives it thirteen marks and thirteen seeds, catalogues what obstructs each one, and praises its merits with four similes — a hungry dog, a blind tortoise, a fearful servant, a sovereign king. The chapter on seeking that follows is the treatise's most technical stretch so far: the three baskets of scripture and the nine causes that establish them, the objects of cognition and the three wisdoms born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, eighteen kinds of attention that absorb the entire classical list of thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, and the opening move into what reality actually is — argued, at the fascicle's close, through the oldest and most exact of Buddhist images: the magician, and what is and is not real about what he conjures.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Fascicle 4 complete Source: Taishō T1604, 大乘莊嚴經論卷第四, 明信品第十一 · 述求品第十二之一 Chinese attribution: 無著菩薩造 (compiled by Asaṅga)

Where This Fascicle Picks Up

Fascicle 3 was the treatise's single longest chapter, working out what enlightenment itself consists in — the buddha-body, the transformation of the basis, the tathāgatagarbha, the three bodies and four wisdoms. This fascicle steps back down to the practitioner's own footing: what faith is, in exhaustive classificatory detail, and then what it means to actively seek — the Dharma in its three traditional divisions, the objects of meditation, the wisdom born of hearing and reflecting and cultivating, the eighteen modes of attention that structure practice, and finally reality itself, approached through the classical simile of the magician's illusion. The source marks this second chapter as 述求品第十二之一, "part one" of the chapter on seeking; part two continues in fascicle 5.

1. Thirteen Marks and Thirteen Seeds of Faith

Chinese Source

釋曰：已說無上菩提隨順。菩提者，所謂信，此信相今當說。

偈曰：

已生及未生，　正受及似受，

他力亦自力，　有迷亦不迷，

現前不現前，　聽法及求義，

觀察等十三，　分別於信相。

釋曰：信相差別有十三種。一者已生信，謂過去現在信。二者未生信，謂未來信。三者正受信，謂內信。四者似受信，謂外信。五者他力信，謂麁信，由善友力生故。六者自力信，謂細信，由自力生故。七者有迷信，謂惡信，由顛倒故。八者不迷信，謂好信，由無倒故。九者現前信，謂近信，由無障故。十者不現前信，謂遠信，由有障故。十一者聽法信，謂聞信，由聞生故。十二者求義信，謂思信，由思生故。十三者觀察信，謂修信，由修生故。已說信相差別，次說信種差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The exposition: what accords with unsurpassed enlightenment has been explained. As for enlightenment, there is what is called faith; the marks of this faith shall now be explained.

Already arisen, and not yet arisen;
genuinely received, and only apparently received;
by another's power, and by one's own power;
deluded, and undeluded;
present, and not present;
from hearing the Dharma, and from seeking its meaning;
and from contemplation — these thirteen
distinguish the marks of faith.

The exposition: the marks of faith are distinguished as thirteen kinds. First, faith already arisen — faith belonging to the past and present. Second, faith not yet arisen — belonging to the future. Third, genuinely received faith — inward faith. Fourth, only apparently received faith — outward faith. Fifth, faith by another's power — coarse faith, arising through the power of a good spiritual friend. Sixth, faith by one's own power — subtle faith, arising through one's own power. Seventh, deluded faith — faulty faith, arising through distortion. Eighth, undeluded faith — good faith, arising without distortion. Ninth, present faith — near faith, being unobstructed. Tenth, not present faith — distant faith, being obstructed. Eleventh, faith from hearing the Dharma — faith born of hearing. Twelfth, faith from seeking its meaning — faith born of reflection. Thirteenth, faith from contemplation — faith born of cultivation. The marks of faith having been distinguished, next the seeds of faith are distinguished.

偈曰：

可奪間無間，　有多亦有少，

有覆及無覆，　相應不相應，

有聚亦無聚，　極入亦遠入，

復此十三義，　分別於信種。

釋曰：信種差別亦有十三。一者可奪信，謂下品信。二者有間信，謂中品信。三者無間信，謂上品信。四者多信，謂大乘信。五者少信，謂小乘信。六者有覆信，謂有障信，由不能勝進故。七者無覆信，謂無障信，由能勝進故。八者相應信，謂熟修信，由恒行及恭敬行故。九者不相應信，謂不熟修信，由離前二行故。十者有聚信，謂有果信，由能得大菩提故。十一者無聚信，謂無果信，由不能得大菩提故。十二者極入信，謂功用信，從初地至七地故。十三者遠入信，謂極淨信，從八地至佛地故。已說信種，次說信障難。

Ru-Yi Translation

Assailable, intermittent, and unbroken;
abundant, and slight;
obscured, and unobscured;
joined, and not joined;
bearing fruit, and not bearing fruit;
thoroughly entered, and distantly entered —
these further thirteen meanings
distinguish the seeds of faith.

The exposition: the seeds of faith are likewise distinguished as thirteen kinds. First, assailable faith — faith of the inferior grade. Second, intermittent faith — of the middling grade. Third, unbroken faith — of the superior grade. Fourth, abundant faith — Mahāyāna faith. Fifth, slight faith — Lesser Vehicle faith. Sixth, obscured faith — obstructed, unable to advance further. Seventh, unobscured faith — unobstructed, able to advance. Eighth, joined faith — fully cultivated, through constant and reverent practice. Ninth, unjoined faith — not fully cultivated, lacking these two. Tenth, faith bearing fruit — able to attain great enlightenment. Eleventh, faith not bearing fruit — unable to attain it. Twelfth, thoroughly entered faith — the faith of applied effort, from the first ground to the seventh. And thirteenth, distantly entered faith — the faith of utmost purity, from the eighth ground to the ground of buddhahood. The seeds of faith having been explained, next the obstacles and difficulties of faith are explained.

Reading note

Two exhaustive lists of thirteen, one for the marks of faith and one for its seeds, sound at first like the same catalogue twice — but the distinction the treatise is drawing is worth making explicit, since it never states it directly. The marks describe faith as it presently shows itself: whether it has arisen yet, whether it came from within or from a teacher, whether it is deluded or clear. The seeds describe faith's trajectory and durability: whether it can be shaken, whether it belongs to the lesser or greater vehicle, whether it will actually bear the fruit of enlightenment. A faith could score well on several marks — genuinely received, undeluded, born of contemplation — and still be, by seed, only intermittent or fruitless. The two lists are measuring different things about the same phenomenon: its present character, and its future.

他力信 and 自力信, faith by another's power and faith by one's own power, quietly recur throughout both lists in a way that rewards tracking. Faith sparked by a good friend is called 麁信, coarse faith, and among the seeds it corresponds to what is called unsteady — this is the same distinction fascicle 2's chapter on arousing the mind drew among the four powers behind the conventional arousing of bodhicitta, where the power of a friend alone, among four causes, was singled out as unsteady. The pattern is consistent across the treatise: a teacher can occasion faith, but cannot supply the durability that only self-generated conviction, ripened from one's own prior habituation, provides.

2. What Obstructs Faith, Its Merits, and Four Similes

Chinese Source

偈曰：

多忘亦懈怠，　行迷并惡友，

善羸及邪憶，　放逸復少聞，

聞喜及思喜，　因定增上慢，

應知此等過，　障礙於信相。

釋曰：障者，相違義。多忘者，於已生信為障。懈怠者，於未生信為障。行迷者，於正受、似受信為障，如先所受能受執著故。惡友者，於他力信為障，以倒法令受故。善羸者，於自力信為障。邪憶者，於不迷信為障。放逸者，於現前信為障。少聞者，於聽法信為障，不聽了義故。聞喜者，於求義信為障，少思惟故。思喜及定慢者，於觀察信為障，少修及不細觀察故。問：何障障信種？

偈曰：

不厭及不習，　有厭亦有覆，

無應及無聚，　應知障信種。

釋曰：不習者，於可奪信、有聞信為障。不厭者，於小信為障，不厭生死故。有厭者，於大信為障，厭生死故。有覆者，於無覆信為障。無應者，於相應信為障。無聚者，於有聚信為障。已顯示信障難，次讚歎信功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Excessive forgetting, and laziness;
delusion in conduct, and evil companions;
weakness in the good, and wrong recollection;
heedlessness, and little learning;
delight in what is heard, and delight in what is reflected upon;
and, through concentration, an overweening conceit —
know that these faults
obstruct the marks of faith.

The exposition: obstruction has the sense of conflicting with. Excessive forgetting obstructs faith already arisen. Laziness obstructs faith not yet arisen. Delusion in conduct obstructs genuinely and apparently received faith, in that one clings to what was grasped before. Evil companions obstruct faith by another's power, causing one to receive a distorted teaching. Weakness in the good obstructs faith by one's own power. Wrong recollection obstructs undeluded faith. Heedlessness obstructs present faith. Little learning obstructs faith from hearing, since one does not hear the definitive meaning. Delight in what is heard obstructs faith from seeking the meaning, since one reflects too little. Delight in what is reflected upon, and the conceit that comes from concentration, obstruct faith from contemplation, since one cultivates and contemplates too little. Question: what obstructs the seeds of faith?

Not being weary of it, and not practicing it;
being weary of it, and being obscured;
not corresponding, and not bearing fruit —
know these as obstructing the seeds of faith.

The exposition: not practicing obstructs assailable and intermittent faith. Not being weary obstructs slight faith, one not being weary of saṃsāra. Being weary obstructs abundant faith, one being weary of saṃsāra. Being obscured obstructs unobscured faith. Not corresponding obstructs joined faith. Not bearing fruit obstructs fruit-bearing faith. The obstacles to faith having been displayed, next the merits of faith are praised.

Chinese Source

偈曰：

信有大福德，　不悔及大喜，

不壞將堅固，　進位并得法，

自利與他利，　亦復速諸通，

以此諸功德，　讚歎信利益。

釋曰：大福德者，讚現在信。不悔者，讚過去信，不追變故。大喜者，讚正受信及似受信，定相應故。不壞者，讚友力信，正道不壞故。堅固者，讚自力信，不退捨故。進位者，讚不迷信、現前信、聽法信、求義信、觀察信、有間信。得法者，讚無間信。自利者，讚少信。他利者，讚多信。速通者，讚諸自分信，謂無覆信、相應信、有聚信、極入信、遠入信。

偈曰：

狗龜奴王譬，　次第譬四信，

習欲習諸定，　自利利他人。

釋曰：譬如餓狗求食無厭，諸習欲人信亦復如是，於一切時種種信故。譬如盲龜水中藏六，諸習外定人信亦復如是，唯知修習世間定故。譬如賊奴畏主勤作，諸自利人信亦復如是，為怖生死勤方便故。譬如大王自在詔勅，諸利他人信亦復如是，增上教化無休息故。菩薩自解諸信，復廣分別令他得解，如是勸諸眾生生大乘信。已讚信功德，次遮下劣心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Faith has great merit,
freedom from regret, and great joy,
indestructibility and firmness,
advancement in stage, and attainment of the Dharma,
benefit for self and for other,
and swiftness in the powers —
by these several merits
the benefit of faith is praised.

The exposition: great merit praises present faith. Freedom from regret praises past faith, which does not later change. Great joy praises genuinely and apparently received faith, joined with concentration. Indestructibility praises faith by another's power, the right path not being destroyed. Firmness praises faith by one's own power, not turning back. Advancement in stage praises undeluded, present, hearing, seeking-the-meaning, contemplative, and intermittent faith. Attainment of the Dharma praises unbroken faith. Benefit for self praises slight faith. Benefit for others praises abundant faith. Swiftness in the powers praises the faiths of one's own portion — unobscured, joined, fruit-bearing, thoroughly entered, and distantly entered faith.

The dog, the tortoise, the servant, and the king —
these similes, in order, are for four kinds of faith:
one habituated to desire, one habituated to absorption,
one who benefits himself, and one who benefits others.

The exposition: it is like a hungry dog seeking food without satiety — the faith of one habituated to desire is just the same, seeking various things at all times. It is like a blind tortoise withdrawing its six limbs in water — the faith of one habituated to outsiders' absorptions is just the same, knowing only how to cultivate worldly concentration. It is like a thieving servant, fearing his master, diligent in his work — the faith of one who benefits himself is just the same, diligently applying methods for fear of saṃsāra. It is like a great king, issuing decrees with mastery — the faith of one who benefits others is just the same, teaching with authority, without rest. The bodhisattva, having understood these kinds of faith himself, further distinguishes them at length to bring understanding to others, and in this way encourages sentient beings to give rise to Mahāyāna faith. Faith's merits having thus been praised, next an inferior state of mind is warded off.

Reading note

The blind tortoise withdrawing into its shell is a stock Buddhist image for the rarity of a human birth suited to hearing the Dharma, and it is worth noticing how differently this treatise redeploys it. Here it names not a rare opportunity but a limitation: faith habituated to outsiders' meditative absorptions, competent at withdrawing from the world but knowing only 世間定, worldly concentration, and nothing beyond it. The tortoise is skilled at exactly one thing, and that one thing is not enough — a sharper use of a familiar image than the one most readers will already have in mind.

The four similes run on a spectrum from smallest to largest scope — a dog wants food, a tortoise wants shelter, a servant wants safety from punishment, a king wants to govern well — and matching them to their referents makes the ranking explicit: desire, then withdrawal, then self-benefit, then benefiting others, in ascending order of how much of the world the faith in question actually takes into account. The king is not praised for wanting more, in the way the dog wants more food; he is praised because what he wants already includes everyone he governs.

3. The Three Baskets: Nine Causes and Fourfold Meanings

Chinese Source

偈曰：

人身及方處，　時節皆無限，

三因菩提得，　勿起下劣心。

釋曰：人身及方處時節皆無限者，得無上菩提有三因無限：一者人身無限，由人道眾生得無限故。二者方處無限，由十方世界得無限故。三者時節無限，由盡未來際剎那剎那得無限故。三因菩提得勿起下劣心者，由此三因無限，是故諸菩薩於無上菩提不應退屈起下劣心。已遮下劣心，次顯福德勝。

偈曰：

得福由施彼，　非由自受用，

依他說大乘，　不依自義法。

釋曰：得福由施彼非由自受用者，譬如以食施彼則得大福，由利他故。非自受用能得大福，由自利故。問：若爾，菩薩云何得福？答：依他說大乘不依自義法。諸菩薩如是依他說大乘經得大福德，不依自利說小乘經得大福德。已說勝福，次說得果。

偈曰：

大法起大信，　大信果有三，

信增及福增，　得佛功德體。

釋曰：大法起大信大信果有三者，謂有智人於大乘聖法而生大信，由此大信得三種果。問：得何等果？答：信增及福增得佛功德體。此明：一得大信果，信增長故。二得大福果，福增長故。三得大菩提果，功德無等及佛體大故。明信品究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Human birth, and place,
and time — all are without limit;
by these three causes, enlightenment is attained —
do not give rise to an inferior state of mind.

The exposition: unsurpassed enlightenment is attained through three causes without limit: human birth without limit, the beings of the human realm who attain it being without limit; place without limit, the worlds of the ten directions being without limit; and time without limit, its attainment, moment by moment to the very limit of the future, being without limit. By these three causes, enlightenment is attained — do not give rise to an inferior mind means: because these three causes are without limit, bodhisattvas should not shrink back or give rise to an inferior state of mind. This having been warded off, next the supremacy of merit is displayed.

Merit is obtained by giving to another,
not by enjoying it oneself;
by expounding the Mahāyāna for the sake of others,
not a teaching relying on one's own benefit.

The exposition: merit is obtained by giving to another, not by enjoying it oneself means: it is like giving food to another, which obtains great merit, since it benefits another — not consuming it oneself, which does not, since it only benefits oneself. Question: if this is so, how does the bodhisattva obtain merit? Answer: by expounding the Mahāyāna for the sake of others, not a teaching relying on one's own benefit. Bodhisattvas obtain great merit by expounding the Mahāyāna sūtras for others' sake, not by expounding the Lesser Vehicle sūtras for their own benefit. This supreme merit having been explained, next its fruit is explained.

From the great Dharma, great faith arises;
great faith has three fruits:
the increase of faith, and the increase of merit,
and the attainment of the substance of the Buddha's own merit.

The exposition: a person of wisdom gives rise to great faith in the noble Dharma of the Mahāyāna, and through this great faith obtains three fruits: the fruit of great faith, faith itself increasing; the fruit of great merit, merit increasing; and the fruit of great enlightenment, merit becoming unequaled and the substance of buddhahood becoming vast. The chapter on illuminating faith is complete.

Chinese Source

釋曰：如是已說種種信，次說以信求諸法。

偈曰：

三藏或二攝，　成三有九因，

熏覺寂通故，　解脫生死事。

釋曰：三藏或二攝者，三藏謂修多羅藏、毘尼藏、阿毘曇藏。或二謂此三由下上乘差別故。復次為聲聞藏及菩薩藏。問：彼三及二，云何名藏？答：由攝故。謂攝一切所應知義。問：云何成三？答：成三有九因。立修多羅者，為對治疑惑，若人於義處處起疑，為令彼人得決定故。立毘尼者，為對治受用二邊，為離樂行邊遮有過受用，為離苦行邊聽無過受用。立阿毘曇者，為對治自心見取，不倒法相此能示故。復次立修多羅者，為說三學。立毘尼者，為成戒學、心學，由持戒故不悔，由不悔故隨次得定。立阿毘曇者，為成慧學，不顛倒義此能擇故。復次立修多羅者，為正說法及義。立毘尼者，謂成就法及義，由勤方便煩惱滅故。立阿毘曇者，為通達法及義，由種種簡擇此為方便故。由此九因，故立三藏。問：別用如此，通用云何？答：熏覺寂通故解脫生死事。此明解脫生死是其通用。由聞故熏，由思故覺，由止故寂，由觀故通。由此四義，生死諸事永得解脫。

偈曰：

經律阿毘曇，　是各有四義，

具解成種智，　一偈得漏盡。

釋曰：若略說三藏，一一各有四義。若菩薩能了此義，則成就一切種智。若聲聞能了一偈，則得諸漏永盡。問：云何一一四種義？

Ru-Yi Translation

The exposition: the various kinds of faith having thus been explained, next seeking the various dharmas by means of faith is explained.

The three baskets, or gathered as two,
are established as three through nine causes;
through habituation, awakening, quiescence, and penetration,
one is liberated from the affairs of saṃsāra.

The exposition: the three baskets, or gathered as two: the three baskets are the sūtra basket, the vinaya basket, and the abhidharma basket. Gathered as two, these three divide by the lower and higher vehicle — or, alternatively, into the śrāvaka basket and the bodhisattva basket. Question: why are these called "baskets"? Answer: because of their gathering — they gather in every meaning that is to be known. Question: how are they established as three? Answer: through nine causes. The sūtras are established as the remedy for doubt: when a person doubts the meaning here and there, they bring him to certainty. The vinaya is established as the remedy for the two extremes of enjoyment: departing from the extreme of pleasure-seeking, it restrains faulty enjoyment; departing from the extreme of painful austerity, it permits faultless enjoyment. The abhidharma is established as the remedy for grasping at one's own views, displaying the marks of things without distortion. Furthermore, the sūtras are established to teach the three trainings. The vinaya is established to accomplish the training in morality and in mind, since upholding morality brings freedom from regret, and freedom from regret brings concentration in turn. The abhidharma is established to accomplish the training in wisdom, discerning meaning without distortion. Furthermore, the sūtras correctly teach the Dharma and its meaning; the vinaya accomplishes the Dharma and its meaning, extinguishing the afflictions through diligent method; and the abhidharma penetrates the Dharma and its meaning, through manifold discernment as its method. By these nine causes, the three baskets are established. Question: their individual functions being thus, what is their shared function? Answer: through habituation, awakening, quiescence, and penetration, one is liberated from the affairs of saṃsāra. Through hearing, one is habituated; through reflection, one awakens; through calm abiding, one attains quiescence; and through insight, one penetrates. By these four, the affairs of saṃsāra are forever left behind.

The sūtras, the vinaya, and the abhidharma
each have their own four meanings;
one who fully understands them accomplishes omniscience;
one who grasps even a single verse attains the exhaustion of contaminants.

The exposition: in brief, the three baskets each have four meanings. A bodhisattva who understands these meanings accomplishes omniscience; a śrāvaka who understands even a single verse attains the forever exhaustion of contaminants. Question: what are these four meanings, one by one?

偈曰：依故及相故，法故及義故，如是四種義，是說多羅義。

釋曰：修多羅有此四義，一依、二相、三法、四義。依者，是處、是人、是用，謂隨是何國土、隨是何諸佛、隨是何眾生，如來依此三種說修多羅。相者，謂世諦相及第一義諦相。法者，謂陰、界、入、緣生、諦、食等法。義者，謂釋所以。

釋曰：阿毘曇有此四義，一對、二數、三伏、四解。對者，是向涅槃法，諦、菩提分、解脫門等說故。數者，是相續法，於一一法色非色、可見不可見等差別無量說故。伏者，是勝上法，於諍論眾中決判法義退彼說故。解者，是釋義法，由阿毘曇，修多羅義易可解故。

Reading note

The nine causes for the three baskets are given in three passes, each organized around a different concern — remedy, training, and correct teaching — and it is worth noticing that the same three baskets get re-derived three separate times rather than once. This is not redundancy; it is the treatise showing that the threefold division is over-determined, arrived at independently from three different starting questions (what problem does each basket solve? what training does it accomplish? what does it correctly convey?), which is a stronger claim than deriving the number three from any single consideration alone.

The seven kinds of release from an offense, buried within the vinaya's fourfold meaning, include one item easy to read past: 性得, natural attainment, whereby at the moment of seeing the truths directly, subtle offenses simply cease to have substance, "naturally attained upon realizing the emptiness of things." This quietly folds the entire monastic disciplinary apparatus — repentance, formal procedures, probation — into a hierarchy that culminates in something no procedure can produce: a realization that dissolves the very category of subtle transgression from the inside, rather than absolving it from the outside.

4. Seeking the Object, and the Three Wisdoms

Chinese Source

偈曰：

佛說所緣法，　應知內外俱，

得二無二義，　二亦不可得。

釋曰：佛說所緣法應知內外俱者，佛說一切所緣法有三種，一內、二外、三俱，彼能取自性身等為內，所取自性身等為外，合二自性為俱。得二無二義二亦不可得者，於此內外二緣，如其次第得無二義。問：云何得？答：若所取義與能取義無別觀，若能取義與所取義無別觀。復次合二為一，由於內外二緣得如如故。如是彼二無有二義，則此二緣亦不可得。問：已說得緣，云何得智？

偈曰：

三緣得三智，　淨持意言境，

了別義光已，　安心唯有名。

釋曰：三緣，謂如前說內外俱三境。三智，謂聞思修三慧。由依三緣，能得三慧。問：云何得？答：若於三緣淨持意言境，即得聞慧。意言者，分別也。淨者，信決定。持者，擇彼種。由此得聞慧。若於三緣了別義光已，即得思慧。謂知義及光不異意言，由此得思慧。若於三緣安心唯有名，即得修慧。謂知義及光但唯是名，由此得修慧。如先所說二緣不可得，是故應知彼三緣者是聞思修三慧依止。已說求緣，次說求作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha teaches that the object taken as condition
should be known as inward, outward, or both;
attaining the sense that these two are non-dual,
even these two cannot ultimately be found.

The exposition: the Buddha teaches that every object taken as condition is of three kinds — inward, outward, or both. The own-nature of the body and the rest that grasps is inward; the own-nature that is grasped is outward; and the two joined together is both. Attaining the sense that these two are non-dual, even these two cannot ultimately be found means: with regard to these two conditions, inward and outward, one attains, in this order, the sense of their non-duality. Question: how? Answer: by observing that what is grasped is not different from what grasps, and what grasps is not different from what is grasped. Joining the two as one, one attains suchness with regard to both. Since these two thus have no dual sense, these two conditions themselves cannot ultimately be found either. Question: the attaining of the object having been explained, how is wisdom attained?

From the three conditions, three kinds of wisdom are attained:
purely holding the domain of mental discourse;
having discerned the light of its meaning;
and settling the mind on there being only name.

The exposition: the three conditions are, as stated before, inward, outward, and both. The three wisdoms are those born of hearing, of reflection, and of cultivation. In dependence on the three conditions, the three wisdoms are attained. Question: how? Answer: purely holding, with regard to the three conditions, the domain of mental discourse attains the wisdom of hearing — mental discourse being discrimination, pure meaning faith that is settled, and holding meaning selecting out its kind. Having discerned, with regard to the three conditions, the light of its meaning attains the wisdom of reflection — that is, knowing that the meaning and its light are not different from mental discourse. Settling the mind, with regard to the three conditions, on there being only name attains the wisdom of cultivation — that is, knowing that the meaning and its light are only name. As stated before, the two conditions cannot ultimately be found; therefore these three conditions are the support for the three wisdoms of hearing, reflection, and cultivation. Seeking the object having been explained, next seeking attention is explained.

Reading note

意言, mental discourse, is the hinge term this whole passage turns on, and its placement is deliberate: the wisdom of hearing is defined as purely holding this domain, not the words heard themselves. The claim quietly embedded here is that what one actually works with, even at the most basic stage of learning through hearing, was never the raw external teaching but already one's own internal discursive representation of it. The three wisdoms then trace a single object — mental discourse and its light — through three depths of understanding: first holding it purely, then seeing that its light is not different from mental discourse itself, and finally settling on the recognition that both are only name. Nothing changes about what is being examined across the three stages; only how thoroughly its status as mental discourse is understood.

Reading note

The eighteenth item in this list is really thirty-seven items compressed into one, and the compression is worth pausing on: 修種作意, the attention of cultivating the seed, absorbs the entire classical inventory of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment — the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of supernormal power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of enlightenment, and the eightfold path — treating them not as a separate curriculum but as one mode of attention among eighteen. Read this way, the mainstream Abhidharma path-scheme that other treatises on this site present as the whole of practice becomes, in this treatise's architecture, a single ingredient nested inside a larger classification the śrāvaka teachings never named.

方便作意's fifth method, understanding penetration, ends its eleven sub-kinds on a pair worth reading together: 通達義不可得, penetrating that meaning cannot be found, followed immediately by 通達不可得不可得, penetrating that this very unfindability cannot be found either. The list does not stop at the negation; it negates the negation as well, refusing to let "meaning cannot be found" harden into a new positive fact that could itself be grasped. This is the same move fascicle 3 made with the buddha-substance — neither existent nor non-existent — applied here to the epistemic step of the practitioner's own search, rather than to the object ultimately sought.

6. Seeking Reality: The Three Natures and Their Two Purities

Chinese Source

偈曰：

離二及迷依，　無說無戲論，

三應及二淨，　二淨三譬顯。

釋曰：離二及迷依無說無戲論者，此中應知，三性俱是真實。離二者，謂分別性真實，由能取所取畢竟無故。迷依者，謂依他性真實，由此起諸分別故。無說無戲論者，謂真實性真實，由自性無戲論不可說故。三應及二淨二淨三譬顯者，三應謂初真實應知，第二真實應斷，第三真實應淨。二淨，謂一者自性清淨，由本來清淨故；二者無垢清淨，由離客塵故。此二清淨，由三種譬喻可得顯現，謂空、金、水。如此三譬，一則俱譬自性清淨，由空等非不自性清淨故。二則俱譬無垢清淨，由空等非不離客塵清淨故。

偈曰：

法界與世間，　未曾有少異，

眾生癡盛故，　著無而棄有。

釋曰：法界與世間未曾有少異者，非法界與世間而有少異。何以故？法性與諸法無差別故。眾生癡盛故著無而棄有者，由眾生愚癡熾盛，於世間無法不應著而起著，於如如有法不應捨而棄捨。已說求真實，次說求真實譬喻。

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of the two, and the basis of delusion;
inexpressible and free of discursive elaboration;
three that should be known, and two purities;
these two purities are displayed by three similes.

The exposition: here it should be understood that all three natures are reality. Free of the two is the reality of the discriminated nature, since what is grasped and what grasps are, in the end, both absent. The basis of delusion is the reality of the dependent nature, since it is what gives rise to every discrimination. Inexpressible and free of discursive elaboration is the reality of the true nature, since its own-nature is free of discursive elaboration and cannot be expressed. The three that should be — the first reality should be known, the second should be abandoned, and the third should be purified. The two purities are: purity of own-nature, since it was pure from the very beginning; and purity from taint, since adventitious dust has since departed. These two purities can be displayed by three similes — space, gold, and water. Each of these three is a simile for both purities: for purity of own-nature, since space and the rest are never anything but pure in their own-nature to begin with; and for purity from taint, since space and the rest are never anything but pure once free of adventitious dust.

The dharma-realm and the world
have never differed in the least;
but sentient beings, so thick with delusion,
cling to what is absent, and forsake what is present.

The exposition: the dharma-realm is not different from the world in the least, since the nature of things and the various dharmas are without difference. Because sentient beings are so thickly deluded, they cling to what, in the world, is absent and should not be clung to, and forsake what, as suchness, is present and should not be forsaken. Seeking reality having been explained, next the similes for seeking reality are explained.

Reading note

Assigning each of the three natures its own task — the discriminated to be known, the dependent to be abandoned, the true to be purified — gives this treatise's three-natures scheme something the bare ontological distinction between them does not supply on its own: a program of action, not just a classification. Knowing, abandoning, and purifying are three different verbs because they name three different relationships a practitioner has to hold toward the three, and collapsing them into one undifferentiated task — treating them all as simply "to be understood," say — would blur exactly the distinction the chapter on reality worked so hard to establish two fascicles earlier.

The space-gold-water triad for the two purities is chosen with some care: each image already carries an intact and a compromised state in ordinary experience — space obscured by cloud rather than altered, gold buried in ore rather than transformed, water muddied rather than changed in kind — which lets a single simile carry both purities at once, since nothing about the obscuring agent (cloud, ore, mud) was ever part of what it obscured. The same figure that argues purity of own-nature also argues purity from taint, because in each case the taint was always adventitious to begin with.

Reading note

The magician simile does exactly the double duty this chapter needs, and it is worth being precise about which half maps to which claim. The magician's spell has no real substance — nothing genuinely turns into anything — and this is the simile for how the dependent and discriminated natures are ultimately empty. But the illusory image is nonetheless findable, conventionally, as an image; a spectator really does see something. This second half is the simile for how false discrimination is nonetheless real at the level of the conventional truth. A simile that only carried the first half would collapse into a denial that anything happens at all; carrying both halves at once is what lets this treatise avoid nihilism while still denying substantial existence to the two lower natures.

The illusory-king-versus-illusory-king simile answers a question that a careful reader would have to ask sooner or later: if the pure dharmas that remedy defilement are, on this treatise's own account, equally illusion-like, in what sense is one an improvement on the other? The answer given is entirely functional rather than ontological — one illusion can still prevail over another within the shared domain both operate in, the way one conjured army can rout a second conjured army without either soldier being any more real than the other. This is also the treatise's own explanation for why the bodhisattva who understands this "is without conceit," 是故無慢: knowing that his remedy is itself illusion-like removes any basis for pride in wielding it.

Reading note

The eight similes divide by a logic the treatise states outright but is easy to miss on a first pass: they are not eight interchangeable illustrations of "things are unreal" but a precisely mapped anatomy, each simile assigned to a specific component of experience — the inward sense-fields get two similes (illusion and reflected image), the outward sense-fields get two (dream and shadow), mind and its factors get one (mirage), and the Dharma taught, meditative absorption, and intentional rebirth each get their own single, non-repeated simile (echo, moon, magical creation). The doubling for inward and outward sense-fields versus the singleton status of the last three suggests these final three are being treated as achievements rather than as bare components of an unenlightened being's makeup — teaching, absorption, and a bodhisattva's chosen rebirth are each singular enough to need only one image apiece.

化, magical creation, closing the list as the simile for a bodhisattva's intentional taking of birth, quietly answers a question this fascicle's earlier illusion-similes section raised without naming it: given that ordinary rebirth, driven by karma and affliction, is already illusion-like on this treatise's account, what distinguishes a bodhisattva's deliberate return to saṃsāra from just more of the same delusion? The answer is 不染一切所作事故, that he is untainted by everything he thereby does — the same illusory-appearing act, but performed without the grasping that makes an ordinary rebirth a compulsion rather than a choice.
