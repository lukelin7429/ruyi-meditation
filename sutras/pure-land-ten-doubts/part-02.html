Is Seeking Birth a Betrayal of Emptiness?

The second doubt presses harder than the first: if all dharmas are, at root, unborn and empty, isn't seeking rebirth anywhere — even in a pure land — already a contradiction? The answer turns on a distinction the whole treatise depends on: that "unborn" was never a claim that nothing happens, but a claim about how what happens actually happens.

Doubt 2: Does Seeking Rebirth Contradict the Teaching of No-Birth?

Chinese Source

第二疑
問：「諸法體空，本來無生，平等寂滅。今乃捨此求彼，生西方彌陀淨土，豈不乖理哉？又經云：『若求淨土，先淨其心，心淨故即佛土淨。』此云何通？」

答：「釋有二義：一者、總答；二者、別答。總答者，汝若言：『求生西方彌陀淨土，則是捨此求彼，不中理。』者，汝執住此，不求西方，則是捨彼著此，此還成病，不中理也。又轉計云：『我亦不求生彼，亦不求生此。』者，則斷滅見。故《金剛般若經》云：『須菩提！汝若作是念，發阿耨菩提者，說諸法斷滅相。莫作是念！何以故？發菩提心者，於法不說斷滅相。』

「二、別答者，夫不生、不滅者，於生緣中，諸法和合，不守自性，求於生體，亦不可得，此生生時無所從來，故名不生。不滅者，諸法散時，不守自性，言我散滅，此散滅時去無所至，故言不滅。非謂因緣生外，別有不生、不滅。亦非不求生淨土，喚作無生。為此，《中論》偈云：『因緣所生法，我說即是空，亦名為假名，亦名中道義。』又云：『諸法不自生，亦不從他生，不共不無因，是故知無生。』又《維摩經》云：『雖知諸佛國，及與眾生空，而常修淨土，教化諸群生。』又云：『譬如有人，造立宮室，若依空地，隨意無礙；若依虛空，終不能成。』諸佛說法，常依二諦，不壞假名而說諸法實相。智者熾然求生淨土，達生體不可得，即是真無生，此謂心淨故即佛土淨。愚者為生所縛，聞生即作生解，聞無生即作無生解，不知生者即是無生，無生即是生。不達此理，橫相是非，嗔他求生淨土，幾許誤哉！此則是謗法罪人，邪見外道也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Doubt 2

Question: "The essence of all dharmas is empty; they are, at root, unborn, equally in complete cessation. Yet now you would have us abandon this and seek that — to be born in Amitābha's Pure Land in the west. Is this not a betrayal of the principle itself? And a sūtra says: 'If you seek the pure land, first purify your mind — for when the mind is pure, the buddha-land is pure.' How can these be reconciled?"

Answer: "There are two ways to resolve this: a general answer, and a specific one. As for the general answer — if you say that seeking rebirth in Amitābha's Pure Land in the west is a matter of abandoning this and seeking that, and so does not accord with principle, then your own attachment to staying here, refusing to seek the west, is itself a matter of abandoning that and clinging to this — and this too fails to accord with principle. And if you shift your position again and say, 'I seek neither to be born there, nor to be born here,' this is the view of annihilation. This is why the Diamond Sūtra says: 'Subhūti! If you should think that one who gives rise to the mind of unsurpassed enlightenment thereby teaches the mark of the annihilation of all dharmas — do not think this way. Why is this so? One who gives rise to the mind of enlightenment does not, in regard to any dharma, teach the mark of annihilation.'

"As for the specific answer: what is meant by 'unborn' and 'unceasing' is this — within the conditions that bring about birth, all dharmas come together in combination, holding to no nature of their own; and if one seeks the very substance that is born, it cannot be found either. Because this birth, at the very moment it is born, comes from nowhere, it is called unborn. As for unceasing: when all dharmas disperse, they hold to no nature of their own that could be said to disperse and cease; and because this dispersal and cessation, at the very moment it occurs, goes nowhere, it is called unceasing. This does not mean that, apart from what arises through causes and conditions, there is some separate 'unborn, unceasing' to be found; nor does it mean that simply not seeking rebirth in the pure land is what should be called 'unborn.' This is why a verse of the Middle Treatise says: 'What arises through causes and conditions, I say, is itself emptiness; it is also called a provisional name, and this too is the meaning of the middle way.' And it also says: 'No dharma is born of itself, nor is it born from another; it is not born from both together, nor is it born without cause — and so we know it to be unborn.' And the Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: 'Though one knows that all buddha-lands, and sentient beings too, are empty, one still constantly cultivates the pure land, and teaches and transforms the multitude of beings.' And it also says: 'It is like a person building a palace or a hall: resting it on solid ground, one may build as one wishes, without obstruction; but resting it on empty space, one could never complete it.' The buddhas, in expounding the Dharma, always rely on the two truths — never destroying the provisional name, and yet expounding the true reality of all dharmas. The wise one, ardently seeking rebirth in the pure land, penetrates to the fact that the substance of birth cannot be found — and this is itself the true seeing of no-birth. This is what is meant by: because the mind is pure, the buddha-land is pure. The foolish one is bound by the very word 'birth': hearing 'birth,' he at once forms the understanding of birth; hearing 'no-birth,' he at once forms the understanding of no-birth — not knowing that birth is itself no-birth, and no-birth is itself birth. Failing to penetrate this principle, he recklessly sets up right against wrong, and grows angry at those who seek rebirth in the pure land. How very mistaken this is! Such a one is a person guilty of slandering the Dharma, one of wrong view, an outsider to the path."

Reading note — after Chengyu

Chengyu opens by conceding that the questioner's own premise is correct as far as it goes — "dharmas are of two kinds, mind-dharmas and phenomena-dharmas, and both are indeed empty" — but insists the objection "gets the principle right while missing the actual practice," a gap the rest of the answer is built to close. He reads the Middle Treatise verse Zhiyi quotes as a compressed statement of Tiantai's own core apparatus: the first line names the object being contemplated, the remaining three name the contemplating mind itself in its three aspects — emptiness, the provisional, and the middle — what Tiantai calls the single mind's threefold contemplation, one truth seen three ways at once. It is a small moment in the commentary, but it is Chengyu's clearest signal that whoever composed or transmitted this treatise, its argument leans on categories unmistakably Zhiyi's own.

On the Vimalakīrti citation, Chengyu splits it along its own grammar: the first half states the ultimate truth, in which nothing has any fixed mark; the second half states the conventional truth, in which everything remains exactly as different as it appears — "the true is the true of the conventional truth, in which the ten thousand distinctions dissolve of themselves; the conventional is the conventional of the true truth, in which a single life remains constantly other than the rest." And on the line the whole doubt turns on — "because the mind is pure, the buddha-land is pure" — he offers two readings rather than choosing between them. Read by way of inherent nature, every thought is already the body of the Dharma, every speck of dust already the land of unmoving light, and no practice or stage need be named at all. Read by way of cultivated virtue removing defilement, the three pure practices and the sixteen contemplations, borrowing Amitābha's vow-power while arousing one's own faith, are exactly what is meant by leaving this defiled land for that pure one — cause and effect laid out in full, nothing skipped. "Though two gates are named," Chengyu concludes, "they return, in the end, to one and the same reality."

This closes Part 2. The pattern the answer sets here — that a teaching about emptiness was never a license to stop distinguishing anything, and that "no-birth" and "birth" turn out, rightly understood, to be the same insight seen from two directions — recurs throughout the rest of this treatise.
