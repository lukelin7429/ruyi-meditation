Can Ten Final Thoughts Undo a Lifetime of Evil?

The eighth doubt is the treatise's most demanding piece of moral arithmetic: how could ten thoughts of buddha-recitation at the moment of death possibly outweigh karma accumulated since beginningless time? The answer weighs karma not by quantity or duration but by three qualities of the mind itself — what it rests on, what it depends on, and whether it is decisive.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 8 of 10 complete Source: Taishō T1961, 淨土十疑論 (attrib. Zhiyi) Reading notes after Chengyu's 注十疑論 (X1149)

Doubt 8: How Can Ten Deathbed Thoughts Outweigh a Lifetime of Evil?

Chinese Source

第八疑
問：「眾生無始已來，造無量業，今生一形，不逢善知識，又復作一切罪業，無惡不造。云何臨終，十念成就，即得往生，出過三界？結業之事，云何可通？」

釋曰：「眾生無始已來，善惡業種多少強弱，並不得知。但能臨終遇善知識，十念成就者，皆是宿善業強，始得遇善知識，十念成就。若惡業多者，善知識尚不可逢，何可論十念成就？又汝以無始已來，惡業為重，臨終十念為輕者。今以道理，三種校量，輕、重、不定，不在時節久近、多少。云何為三？一者、在心；二者、在緣；三者、在決定。在心者，造罪之時，從自虛妄、顛倒生；念佛者，從善知識，聞說阿彌陀佛，真實功德名號生，一虛、一實豈得相比？譬如萬年闇室，日光暫至而闇頓滅，豈以久來之闇，不肯滅耶？在緣者，造罪之時，從虛妄、癡闇心，緣虛妄境界，顛倒生；念佛之心，從聞佛清淨真實功德名號，緣無上菩提心生，一真、一偽豈得相比？譬如有人被毒箭中，箭深毒磣，傷肌破骨，一聞滅除藥鼓，即箭出毒除。豈以箭深毒磣，而不肯出也？在決定者，造罪之時以有間心、有後心也；念佛之時，以無間心、無後心，遂即捨命，善心猛利，是以即生。譬如十圍之索，千夫不制；童子揮劍，須臾兩分。又如千年積柴，以一豆火焚，少時即盡。又如有人，一生已來，修十善業，應得生天，臨終之時，起一念決定邪見，即墮阿鼻地獄。惡業虛妄，以猛利故，尚能排一生之善業，令墮惡道。豈況臨終猛心念佛，真實無間善業，不能排無始惡業，得生淨土？無有是處。又云：『一念念佛，滅八十億劫生死之罪。』為念佛時，心猛利故，伏滅惡業，決定得生，不須疑也。上古相傳，判十念成就，作別時意者，此定不可。何以得知？《攝論》云：『由唯發願故。』全無有行。《雜集論》云：『若願生安樂國土，即得往生；若聞無垢佛名，即得阿耨菩提者，並是別時之因。』全無有行。若將臨終，無間十念猛利善行，是別時意者，幾許誤哉！願諸行者，深思此理，自牢其心，莫信異見，自墜陷也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Doubt 8

Question: "Sentient beings, since beginningless time, have created measureless karma; in this present life alone, having failed to meet a good teacher, they have committed every kind of offense, with no evil left undone. How, then, could ten thoughts at the moment of death be enough to attain rebirth, surpassing the three realms altogether? How can the whole matter of accumulated karma be reconciled with this?"

Explanation: "How much wholesome and evil karma a sentient being has sown, since beginningless time, and how strong or weak each of these seeds may be, is simply not something we can know. But whenever a being, at the moment of death, manages to meet a good teacher and accomplish the ten thoughts, this is invariably because that being's wholesome karma from past lives was already strong, and it is precisely this that allows him to meet a good teacher at all and accomplish the ten thoughts. If a being's evil karma predominates, he will not even be able to meet a good teacher — how, then, could the ten thoughts even come up for discussion? You take it that the evil karma of beginningless time is heavy, and the ten thoughts at the moment of death are light. Let us now weigh this by reason, in three ways, to show that lightness and heaviness are not fixed, and do not depend on how long a span of time is involved, or how great a quantity. What are the three? First, what the mind rests on; second, what condition it depends on; third, whether it is decisive. As for what the mind rests on: when one commits an offense, it arises from one's own falsehood and inverted thinking; but the mind that is mindful of the Buddha arises from hearing, from a good teacher, of the true and real merit-name of Amitābha Buddha. How could the one, being false, be compared to the other, being real? It is like a room dark for ten thousand years: let sunlight reach it for even a moment, and the darkness is instantly dispelled — how could the darkness, however long it has lasted, refuse to disperse? As for what condition it depends on: when one commits an offense, it arises from a mind of falsehood and benighted delusion, taking a false and unreal object as its condition, and is thereby inverted; but the mind that is mindful of the Buddha arises from hearing the Buddha's pure and true merit-name, taking the unsurpassed aspiration for enlightenment as its condition. How could the one, being false, be compared to the other, being true? It is like a person struck by a poisoned arrow, the wound deep and the poison fierce, piercing flesh and breaking bone — yet the moment he hears the sound of the drum that dispels poison, the arrow comes out and the poison is gone. How could the arrow, however deep, or the poison, however fierce, refuse to be drawn out? As for whether it is decisive: when one commits an offense, there remains, in the mind, a gap, and something further still to come; but at the moment of buddha-recitation, the mind has no gap and nothing further to come — one lets go of life at once, the wholesome mind fierce and sharp, and it is for this reason that rebirth is attained immediately. It is like a rope wound ten times thick, which a thousand strong men cannot break — yet a mere child, with a single sweep of a sword, cuts it in two in an instant. Or it is like firewood piled up over a thousand years, which a single bean's worth of fire, once kindled, consumes in but a short while. Or again, it is like a person who, over an entire lifetime, has cultivated the ten wholesome deeds and should by rights be reborn in a heaven — yet who, at the very moment of death, gives rise to a single decisive thought of wrong view, and thereby falls at once into the Avīci hell. Evil karma, though false and unreal, is able, through sheer fierce sharpness, to overturn even an entire lifetime of wholesome karma and send a being falling into an evil destiny — how much more, then, should the fierce and unbroken true wholesome karma of buddha-recitation at the moment of death be unable to overturn evil karma accumulated since beginningless time, and bring one to rebirth in the pure land? There is no such possibility that it could fail. It is also said: 'A single thought of buddha-recitation extinguishes the karmic offense of eighty koṭis of kalpas of birth and death.' This is because, at the moment of buddha-recitation, the mind is fierce and sharp, and thereby subdues and extinguishes evil karma, so that rebirth is certain — there is no need to doubt this.

"An old tradition, handed down from ancient times, holds that the accomplishment of the ten thoughts should be read as 'teaching of a deferred benefit' — this is certainly wrong. How do we know this? The Compendium of the Great Vehicle says: 'Because it is a matter of nothing but making a vow' — with no actual practice at all involved. The Compendium of Abhidharma says: 'If one vows to be reborn in the land of peace and bliss, and thereby attains rebirth; or if one hears the name of a stainless buddha, and thereby attains unsurpassed enlightenment' — these are both instances of a cause whose benefit is deferred, with no actual practice involved. But if one were to take the fierce and unbroken wholesome practice of the ten thoughts at the very moment of death, and call this too a matter of deferred benefit, how very mistaken this would be! I beg every practitioner to reflect deeply on this principle, to fortify their own mind, and not to trust in divergent views, lest they bring about their own downfall."

Reading note — after Chengyu

Before the answer even begins, Chengyu sharpens the question's own stakes, quoting the Weiwu Sanmei Sūtra's account of the Buddha telling Ānanda why so few in this world attain the path at all: the mind is so thick with defiled thought that in a single day and night some eighty-four hundred million thoughts arise and pass — each wholesome one ripening into a wholesome result, each evil one into an evil result, as surely as an echo answers a sound or a shadow follows a shape. "Who could hear this," Chengyu asks, "and not be alarmed? Even ordinary wholesome karma is barely enough to secure rebirth by itself — how much more should evil thought be feared?" It is a small addition, but it makes the questioner's objection land harder before Zhiyi's three-part answer is allowed to soften it.

On the third of those three grounds — decisiveness — Chengyu notes that the same logic cuts both ways: a "mind with a gap, and something further to come" describes an offense committed before the moment of death itself, but an offense committed at the very instant of death, with no gap and nothing further to follow, carries an equally heavy, equally immediate weight — which is exactly why the treatise can say, a few lines later, that a single decisive thought of wrong view at the moment of death sends a lifetime of wholesome karma straight into the Avīci hell. The mechanism that lets ten fierce, unbroken thoughts of buddha-recitation overturn a lifetime of evil is the same mechanism, run in reverse, that lets one fierce thought of wrong view overturn a lifetime of good. He glosses the parable images plainly: the ten-times-wound rope is beginningless evil karma, too great in quantity for a thousand strong men — ordinary beings — to break by struggle; the child with a single sword-stroke is a beginning practitioner accomplishing the ten thoughts, the two severed halves nothing more dramatic than the bending of an arm, one world let go of and the other entered in the same motion.

On the "deferred-benefit" reading Zhiyi rejects outright, Chengyu supplies the doctrinal source being disputed: the Mahāyānasaṃgraha's own fourfold typology of the Buddha's "hidden intent," of which the second — "deferred-time intent" — covers cases like vowing toward the land of peace and bliss with vow alone and no accompanying practice, illustrated there by the analogy of a single coin that eventually, over time, yields a thousand — not because one coin becomes a thousand overnight, but because it is the seed from which a later, larger sum eventually grows. Zhiyi's objection, as Chengyu reads it, is not with that category itself but with the old masters' mistake in applying it here: the ten thoughts at the deathbed are not vow without practice, in the way the Mahāyānasaṃgraha's example is — they are themselves fierce, unbroken, actual practice, and mislabeling them "deferred" empties out the very urgency the treatise has spent this whole doubt establishing. Chengyu closes with a line of his own that reads as much like advice as commentary: "One who wishes to be reborn in the pure land must have both practice and vow in full. Practice without vow has nowhere to go; vow without practice accomplishes nothing. When practice and vow support each other, there is nothing that cannot be brought about."

Part 8 Complete

This closes Part 8. Having settled the question of timing — that a lifetime of evil karma is no match for a truly decisive final thought — the treatise turns in Part 9 to a question of space: the pure land lies ten trillion buddha-lands away, and its own source texts seem to exclude women and those with incomplete faculties.
