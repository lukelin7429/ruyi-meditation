The Tripiṭaka Teaching: The Śrāvaka's Stages and the Pratyekabuddha

This part covers fascicle six and fascicle seven up to the fifth of the twelve links. Mengrun first finishes the five methods for stilling the mind (the seven rounds of loving-kindness, counting the breath and the six wondrous gates, the three kinds of dependent origination, and buddha-recollection against three obstructions), then the specific and general foundations of mindfulness. For the inner ordinary he explains the four wholesome roots from the Kośa: the sixteen aspects of the truths, how middle forbearance "reduces objects and aspects" over thirty-one rounds, and what each root guarantees. The sagely stages take up most of the part. He explains the sixteen minds of the path of vision, why seven births remain for the stream-enterer, and the different kinds of severing (natural, sequential, leaping), with a long set of questions and answers on the "family-to-family" and "one-interval" sages. Under the non-returner he lists the seven kinds of parinirvāṇa; under the arhat, the kinds liberated by wisdom, liberated both ways, and "without doubt," the six arhats of the Kośa, and whether arhats can regress. The part closes with the pratyekabuddha, how he differs from the śrāvaka in ten ways, and the first five of the twelve links.

Ru-Yi Translation

"Kin first and enemies later": going from the easy to the difficult, one forms the contemplation in accord with the mind. As for Analyzing the Profound giving the highest kin the lowest bliss only at the sixth contemplation, and at the seventh giving the highest kin middle bliss and the middle kin the lowest bliss: first, because in cultivating the contemplation in order there is not yet leisure to give; second, following the order of the seven rounds, in each round of applying the contemplation one first gives the highest bliss, and the middle and lowest are not essential, so they are given at a later time. Though the objects come earlier and later, at each object there are always the three [kinds of bliss], making enemy and kin equal so as to break the obstruction of anger. As for the Fuxing opening it into nine rounds: first, following the order from kin to enemy, one gives bliss in sequence, without waiting for the sixth and seventh to turn back and take the earlier kin as object. Further, as to whether the earlier objects obtain [the bliss] or not: it is for breaking the obstruction that one for now directs the mind thus; in fact the person before one has not actually obtained bliss. Hence Analyzing the Profound, upper [fascicle], says: "Question: If one has bliss oneself, it can be bestowed on others; but if one has no bliss oneself, what will one bestow? Answer: If one has no bliss of one's own to bestow, one directs the mind to take the bliss of the gods, bodhisattvas, and others and bestow it, wishing that those beings, enemy and kin equally, may receive such bliss." Again, the Commentary on the Nirvāṇa says: "Though one wishes to remove suffering, one does not actually remove suffering; all these are empty words. Though one wishes to give bliss, [others] do not actually obtain bliss; this is a provisional way of speaking." Further, in applying the contemplation the practitioner should regard enemies and foes as having been, for example, his parents in the past; only then can he give bliss to enemy and kin equally. This is set out at length in Analyzing the Profound, upper [fascicle] (and so on).

Ru-Yi Translation

Clinging to conditions and pondering run counter to concentration; hence it is called "much distraction." The breath has four marks. Calming and Contemplation 8 (17) says: "What has sound is called wind; guarding it scatters [the mind]. What is knotted and stagnant is called vapor; guarding it knots [the mind]. What does not go fully out and in is called panting; guarding it tires [the mind]. What is soundless and unstagnant and goes fully out and in is called breath; guarding it concentrates [the mind]" (end of quotation). "Counting" means from one to ten, neither more nor less, keeping the mind from scattering. The fifth [fascicle] of the Gates of Dhyāna (2) has four masters. The first master counts the out-breath: [the body is] neither tense nor bloated, the body is then light and agile, and it is easy to enter samādhi. The second master counts the in-breath, following the breath to gather inward. The third master does not fix on out or in, but simply counts whichever is convenient. The fourth master counts according to the four seasons. The sūtra masters rely properly on the third master, yet they do not allow counting both out and in, for fear of causing illness. The Sanskrit ānāpāna is rendered here "bringing in" (the in-breath) and "sending away" (the out-breath). It is the first gate by which the buddhas of the three times enter the path, shared by the three vehicles and the four teachings. Further, the breath is used to make clear the six wondrous gates, namely counting, following, calming, contemplating, returning, and purity. Gathering the mind on the breath, from one to ten, is called counting. With a subtle mind relying on the breath, knowing it going in and knowing it going out, is called following. Breath and mind quiet and thinking stilled is called calming. Discriminating and analyzing is called contemplating. Turning the mind to shine back is called returning. The mind relying on nothing and the waves of falsehood not arising is called purity (as in the Sequential Gates of the Dharma-Realm, upper [fascicle]). Here it is an auxiliary path of the Lesser Vehicle, and is called only counting the breath.

迷倒不了撥無因果故曰愚癡須知着我及計斷常并執性實三皆迷倒因緣者法界次第中（十七）云展轉感果為因互相由藉為緣（文）如無明為因能與行支為緣乃至生支為因能與老死為緣四教義二（十八）云十二因緣有三種不同一者三世十二因緣（過去二支因現在五支果現在三支因未來二支果）二者二世十二因緣（現在有十未來有二）三者一念十二因緣（此約現在隨一念心起即具十二因緣）三世破斷常二世破着我一念破性實也（文）

Ru-Yi Translation

Deluded and inverted, not understanding, denying cause and effect: hence it says "deluded." You should know that attachment to self, reckoning [things] annihilated or eternal, and holding to a real nature are all three deluded and inverted. "Dependent origination" (因緣, causes and conditions): Sequential Gates of the Dharma-Realm, middle [fascicle] (17), says: "Bringing about effects in turn is the cause; depending on one another is the condition" (end of quotation). For example, ignorance as cause can serve as condition for the link of formations, and so on up to the link of birth as cause serving as condition for aging-and-death. Meaning of the Four Teachings 2 (18) says: "The twelve links of dependent origination are of three different kinds. First, the twelve links of the three times (two links that are causes in the past, five links that are effects in the present, three links that are causes in the present, two links that are effects in the future). Second, the twelve links of the two times (ten in the present, two in the future). Third, the twelve links in a single thought (this is in terms of the present: as soon as a single thought of mind arises, it contains the twelve links). The three times break annihilation and eternity; the two times break attachment to self; the single thought breaks [belief in] a real nature" (end of quotation).

Ru-Yi Translation

Fuxing 7A (end) says: "'The three times break annihilation and eternity': because the three times continue one another, [things are] not annihilated; because the three times pass away in turn, [they are] not eternal. Further, the past breaks eternity, the future breaks annihilation, and the present breaks both annihilation and eternity. 'The two times break the self': the two times, present and future, contain the twelve links. Giving rise to love toward the father and anger toward the mother is called ignorance; when the father emits the seed, taking it as one's own is called formations; from the link of consciousness on to the link of aging-and-death it is the same as in the two times" (end of quotation). Fuxing 8A (11) says: "'A single thought' does not mean the very briefest instant; it means that when wholesome or unwholesome karma is completed, this is called a single thought, different from the linked and bound marks of the three times and the two times; hence it is called a single thought. All are impermanent, so there is no real nature" (end of quotation). See also Wondrous Profound [Meaning] 2 (16) and Gates of Dhyāna 3 (9). Yet that these three kinds of dependent origination break delusion (attachment to self being included) is according to what the Great Collection and the Dhyāna Sūtras say; the Abhidharma and the Great Sūtra instead use the expedient of the elements to break attachment to self. All this follows what suits and pleases the capacities.

若束十二為三道輪轉相生者俱舍云三煩惱二業七事亦名果（雖有十二而二三為性三謂或業事二謂因果）畧果及畧因由中可比二（後際畧果前際畧因中間廣說可比知也比凖俱舍缺畧義若凖婆沙前際畧果後際畧因乃全畧義補注十二引論具釋）又云從惑生惑業從業生於事從事事惑生有支理惟此（十二因緣通名有支道理惟若此也此名束十二輪為三道以能通義與輪轉義同廣如輔行三下十四釋）。

Ru-Yi Translation

If the twelve are bundled into the three paths that revolve and give rise to one another, the Kośa says: "Three are afflictions, two are karma, seven are things, also called effects" (though there are twelve, two and three are their natures: the three are delusion, karma, and things; the two are cause and effect). "The effects are abbreviated and the causes abbreviated; from the middle the two can be inferred" (the later limit abbreviates the effects and the former limit abbreviates the causes; the middle is explained at length, so they can be inferred and known. According to the Kośa, this is the meaning of partial abbreviation; according to the Vibhāṣā, the former limit abbreviates the effects and the later limit abbreviates the causes, which is the meaning of total abbreviation; Supplementary Notes 12 cites the treatise and explains it in full). It further says: "From delusion arise delusion and karma; from karma arise things; from things, things and delusion arise; the principle of the links of existence is only this" (the twelve links are commonly called the links of existence, and their principle is only like this. This is called bundling the twelve wheels into the three paths, because the meaning of "leading through" [in the word "path"] is the same as the meaning of "revolving," as explained at length in Fuxing 3B (14)).

Ru-Yi Translation

The Fuxing cites the Kośa: "The efficient, the coexistent, the homogeneous, the associated, the pervasive, and maturation: the causes are held to be only six kinds." "For now, relying on the Great Treatise, I briefly set out the marks of the six causes, because the Great Treatise is what our school takes up and uses and its names are somewhat the same; hence I rely on it for now," and so on up to: "Further, mind and mental factors arise from four conditions; the [state] without perception and the concentration of cessation arise from three conditions, excluding the object-condition; the other mental factors, the formations not associated [with mind], and form arise from two conditions, excluding the sequential condition and the object-condition. Other conditioned dharmas are inferior, so none of them arises from a single condition. Mind and mental factors born of retribution arise from five causes, excluding the pervasive cause; uncontaminated mind and mental factors arise from three causes, namely the associated, the shared, and the unobstructing." The Notes on the Vimalakīrti says: "The twelve are only the four and the six"; hence one knows it is only a matter of separating and combining in the explanation. For example, ignorance is simply what leads through to formations: this is the homogeneous cause. Formations necessarily have the four marks: this is the coexistent [cause]. The five divisions within formations are also the homogeneous [cause] (views pass through the four truths; habituated thought passes through one truth). The mind and mental factors within ignorance and formations act together and bring about [effects] together, and what they do is necessarily the same [: this is the associated cause]. Once formations exist, they necessarily bring on consciousness and the rest, [the cause of] maturation. These formations (necessarily have) the pervasive delusions of the five divisions [: this is the pervasive cause]. As for the four conditions, the Treatise says that the dominant [condition] is the efficient cause, and that the causal condition is in nature the five [other] causes; this can be known by comparison with the six causes.

The Gates of Dhyāna only says: "Being intelligent and of sharp faculties, discriminating and calculating, yet not obtaining right wisdom, and grasping principle with a wrong mind: this is called delusion" (end of quotation). Ru-Yi Translation

Calming and Contemplation says "the obstruction of drowsiness"; the Four Foundations of Mindfulness says "the obstruction of oppression." The fourth [fascicle] of the Gates of Dhyāna makes clear three kinds of obstruction, cured by recollecting the three bodies (that extends to the Mahāyāna; here, for now, the auxiliary path of the Lesser Vehicle is made clear). The obstructions are evil karma. The three kinds are: first, the obstruction of dullness, darkness, and blockage (drowsiness and the neutral), cured by recollecting the thirty-two marks of the response-body; second, the obstruction of evil thoughts (wishing to commit the five heinous deeds, the ten evils, and the like), cured by recollecting the powers, fearlessnesses, and so on of the reward-body; third, the obstruction of oppression by objects (the body suddenly in pain, or seeing oneself without hands and feet, burned by fire, thirsting for water, and so on), cured by recollecting the empty, tranquil, unconditioned Dharma-body (end of quotation). If the bodies are matched to the teachings, as Fuxing 1B (7) says: "Each of the first three teachings recollects one body, namely the birth-body, the response-body, and the reward-body; the perfect [teaching] recollects the Dharma-body, in which all bodies are complete" (end of quotation).

The Specific and General Foundations of Mindfulness

Chinese Source

二別相念處（如前四念處是）。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root text: Second, the specific-mark mindfulness (the four foundations of mindfulness above).

Chinese Source

妙玄四（二十三）云五障既除觀慧諦當能觀四諦而正以苦諦為初門作四念處觀破四顛倒（文）㭊玄上（二十）云別謂各別身受心法不同故相謂行相觀此四法作不淨等行相故言念觀者然觀體非念觀是其慧推求觀察知不淨等故（乃至）處謂處所謂身受心法是念所緣住止之處故（文）於五陰境脩四念處為破四倒合五為四受則六根對六塵義兼內外故獨為一想行一向居內故合為一又此念處別名屬慧通亦有定輔行三下（十一）云四境上心故名為定（文）。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wondrous Profound [Meaning] 4 (23) says: "Once the five obstructions are removed, the wisdom of contemplation is accurate and one can contemplate the four truths. Taking precisely the truth of suffering as the first gate, one makes the contemplation of the four foundations of mindfulness to break the four inversions" (end of quotation). Analyzing the Profound, upper [fascicle] (20), says: "'Specific' (別) means separately: body, feeling, mind, and dharmas are not the same. 'Mark' (相) means the aspect of practice: contemplating these four dharmas one makes aspects of practice such as impurity; hence it speaks of 'mindfulness.' Contemplation in itself is not mindfulness; contemplation is wisdom, investigating and observing and knowing impurity and so on" (and so on up to) "'Foundation' (處) means place: body, feeling, mind, and dharmas are the places where mindfulness rests upon its objects" (end of quotation). One cultivates the four foundations of mindfulness on the object of the five aggregates in order to break the four inversions. The five are combined into four: feeling is the six faculties facing the six objects, its meaning embracing inner and outer, so it alone makes one; perception and formations lie entirely within, so they are combined into one. Further, these foundations of mindfulness are specifically wisdom, and in common also involve concentration. Fuxing 3B (11) says: "Because the mind is on the four objects, it is called concentration" (end of quotation).

Ru-Yi Translation

Root text: Third, the general-mark mindfulness: one contemplates the body as impure, and feeling, mind, and dharmas as all impure, and so on up to contemplating dharmas as without self, and body, feeling, and mind as also without self; the cases in between are known by analogy. (The above three groups are called the outer ordinary, also called the stage of accumulating provisions.)

此有四句四念處一云一境別觀別正是別相念處二境別觀總三境總觀別此二是總相之方便四境觀俱總是總相念處（文）初則一藥對一倒中間二句觀心漸熟或別於一境總用四觀或別用一觀總觀四境第四境觀純熟舉一俱得也若㭊玄凖俱舍䟽前三皆別相攝第四句方是總相位今依妙玄四念處初句是別後三皆總今此正當境總觀別謂別用一觀總觀四境又上停心破障四念處惟觀苦諦至內凡位方觀四諦妙玄四（三十三）云七賢位人明識四諦此約觧說心行理外名外凡資粮者從喻也欲越三有此為資粮。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are four alternatives here. Four Foundations of Mindfulness 1 says: "First, object specific and contemplation specific: this is precisely the specific-mark mindfulness. Second, object specific and contemplation general; third, object general and contemplation specific: these two are the expedients for the general mark. Fourth, object and contemplation both general: this is the general-mark mindfulness" (end of quotation). The first is one medicine against one inversion. In the middle two alternatives the contemplating mind gradually matures: either specifically on one object one uses all four contemplations generally, or one uses one contemplation specifically to contemplate all four objects generally. At the fourth, object and contemplation are fully mature, and raising one, all are obtained. As for Analyzing the Profound, following the Commentary on the Kośa, the first three are all included in the specific mark, and only the fourth alternative is the stage of the general mark. Here, according to the Wondrous Profound [Meaning] and the Four Foundations of Mindfulness, the first alternative is specific and the last three are all general. What is given here corresponds exactly to "object general and contemplation specific," that is, using one contemplation specifically to contemplate all four objects generally.
