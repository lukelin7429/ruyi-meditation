The Five Gates of Mindfulness

Having spoken his verse, Vasubandhu now turns to prose to explain it — opening by naming, one by one, the five practices the verse has already performed without naming them: worship, praise, aspiration, contemplation, and dedication. The fifth of these, dedication, turns out to carry a distinction — between a "going" aspect and a "returning" aspect — that would become one of the most consequential single ideas in the entire later history of Pure Land Buddhism.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 2 of 7 complete Source: Taishō T1524, tr. Bodhiruci, Northern Wei Reading notes after Tanluan's 往生論註 (T1819)

Naming the Five Gates

Chinese Source

論曰：此願偈明何義？觀安樂世界，見阿彌陀佛，願生彼國土故。

云何觀？云何生信心？若善男子善女人，修五念門成就者，畢竟得生安樂國土，見彼阿彌陀佛。何等五念門？一者禮拜門；二者讚歎門；三者作願門；四者觀察門；五者迴向門。

云何禮拜？身業禮拜阿彌陀如來、應、正遍知，為生彼國意故。

云何讚歎？口業讚歎，稱彼如來名，如彼如來光明智相，如彼名義，欲如實修行相應故。

云何作願？心常作願，一心專念畢竟往生安樂國土，欲如實修行奢摩他故。

云何觀察？智慧觀察，正念觀彼，欲如實修行毘婆舍那故。彼觀察有三種。何等三種？一者觀察彼佛國土功德莊嚴；二者觀察阿彌陀佛功德莊嚴；三者觀察彼諸菩薩功德莊嚴。

云何迴向？不捨一切苦惱眾生，心常作願迴向為首，成就大悲心故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The treatise says: What does this verse of aspiration make clear? It makes clear the contemplation of the world of Peace and Bliss, the beholding of Amitābha Buddha, and the vow to be born in that land.

How does one contemplate? How does one give rise to a mind of faith? If a good son or good daughter cultivates the five gates of mindfulness to completion, he or she will, in the end, attain birth in the land of Peace and Bliss, and behold Amitābha Buddha there. What are the five gates of mindfulness? First, the gate of worship; second, the gate of praise; third, the gate of aspiration; fourth, the gate of contemplation; and fifth, the gate of dedication.

What is worship? It is worshiping, with the karma of the body, the Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly and Fully Awakened One, Amitābha — with the intention of being born in that land.

What is praise? It is praising, with the karma of speech, calling upon that Tathāgata's own name — according with that Tathāgata's own mark of luminous wisdom, and with the very meaning of that name — wishing to accord with true and real practice.

What is aspiration? It is the mind constantly giving rise to this vow, single-mindedly and exclusively holding in thought one's ultimate rebirth in the land of Peace and Bliss — wishing to accord with the true and real practice of śamatha.

What is contemplation? It is contemplating with wisdom, rightly and mindfully observing that land — wishing to accord with the true and real practice of vipaśyanā. This contemplation is of three kinds. What are the three? First, contemplating the merit and adornment of that buddha's land; second, contemplating the merit and adornment of Amitābha Buddha himself; and third, contemplating the merit and adornment of the bodhisattvas of that land.

What is dedication? It is never abandoning any suffering, afflicted sentient being — the mind constantly making dedication itself its foremost vow, so as to bring to completion a mind of great compassion.

Reading note — after Tanluan

Before working through the five gates individually, Tanluan sets out his own ten-part map of the entire prose commentary to come: the verse's general intent, the arising of contemplation and faith, the substance of contemplative practice, the purifying entry into the aspiring mind, skillful transformative dedication, the obstructions to bodhi, what accords with bodhi, the correspondence of name and meaning, the accomplishment of the vow, and the completion of beneficial practice. He then glosses "gate" (門) itself as simply meaning entry and exit — "as when a person, having found a door, passes in and out through it without obstruction." On this reading, the first four gates — worship, praise, aspiration, and contemplation — are gates for entering the Pure Land, while the fifth, dedication, is the gate for going back out again into the work of teaching. The structure of the whole practice, in other words, is not a straight line toward an exit from this world, but a door that swings both ways.

On the specific formula "Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened One," Tanluan notes this names only three of the traditional ten epithets of a buddha — "since a full account would exceed what pen and paper could hold, the sūtras always select according to their own governing purpose rather than exhausting the list." He glosses each of these three in turn: Tathāgata, because "he comes as all buddhas come, by the peaceful path of reality itself, and does not go on to any further becoming" — the same term this site's other translations render "thus come"; Worthy One, because he has exhausted every affliction and is thereby worthy to receive the offerings of every being in heaven and on earth; and Perfectly and Fully Awakened One, because he knows the true, unbroken mark of every dharma, "where the working of the mind ceases and the path of language is cut off" — a knowing so total it can equally be called a kind of unknowing.

Tanluan devotes an unusually long digression to the gate of praise — specifically, to the question of whether merely holding a name in mind can actually accomplish anything. He distinguishes, with deliberately homespun examples, between cases where "the name is itself identical with the dharma" and cases where "the name differs from the dharma, the way a finger pointing at the moon is not the moon itself." Ordinary words are of the second kind — but he insists some names are of the first: an old charm against swelling, recited without any direct contact with the affliction, that nonetheless cures it; a nine-character phrase soldiers of his own day recited silently in battle, believed to turn aside weapons; a cramping muscle relieved simply by calling out the name of a quince, with no quince anywhere present — "this is something I have felt the effect of in my own body." If names this ordinary can carry this kind of power, Tanluan argues, how much less should Amitābha's own name, freely and specifically vowed by that buddha himself to save whoever calls upon it, be dismissed as a mere finger pointing at something else.

On śamatha and vipaśyanā, rendered in earlier translations simply as "stopping" and "observing," Tanluan objects that these single-character glosses are too broad to capture what this treatise actually means — "as though calling every kind of tree simply 'wood' were enough to distinguish a pine from a willow." He gives three specific senses in which this practice "stops": first, that single-minded recollection of Amitābha's own name and land already stops every evil, in the same way a fire is stopped before it starts; second, that the Pure Land itself, lying beyond the three realms, naturally stops any evil of body, speech, or mind in whoever is born there; and third, that Amitābha's own sustaining power as a fully awakened buddha naturally stops any lingering wish to settle for the lesser attainment of a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. Vipaśyanā, likewise, carries two specific senses beyond generic "observation": contemplating the land's three kinds of adornment so as to actually acquire their corresponding true merit, not merely picture them; and, having been reborn there and beheld Amitābha directly, coming to share, even before full attainment, in the very peace that fully realized bodhisattvas already possess.

The gate of dedication receives the note this page is built around. Tanluan divides dedication into two aspects: "the going aspect" (往相), in which one dedicates one's own accumulated merit to all sentient beings, vowing that all might go together to be reborn in Amitābha's Pure Land; and "the returning aspect" (還相), in which, having been reborn there and having accomplished the power of śamatha and vipaśyanā, one turns back and re-enters the dense thicket of birth and death, to teach and guide every sentient being toward the buddha-path together. "Whether going or returning," he concludes, "both are for the sake of drawing sentient beings across the sea of birth and death" — a single motion, not two separate acts, of leaving this world and then choosing, freely, to come back to it. Centuries later, this exact pairing became one of the load-bearing pillars of Shinran's own systematic theology in Japan, where he would go on to argue that even the "going" aspect of this dedication is itself already a gift of Amitābha's vow-power, rather than something the practitioner accomplishes unaided — a doctrinal move that starts, unmistakably, from this single sentence of Tanluan's.
