Chapter 10 · 證果第十 · Complete — Final Chapter

The Fruit of Realization

The closing chapter, and the most doctrinally dense: three contemplations — moving from the conventional into emptiness, from emptiness back out into the conventional, and finally the middle way that holds both at once — traced all the way through to what is actually realized. Baojing closes the entire commentary, and the whole work, by naming the same single practice he has pointed to throughout: reciting Amitābha's name as this threefold contemplation already complete.

I. Moving From the Conventional Into Emptiness 從假入空觀

Chinese Source

〖文〗若行者如是修止觀時，能了知一切諸法皆由心生，因緣虛假不實故空，以知空故，即不得一切諸法名字相，則體真止也。爾時上不見佛果可求，下不見眾生可度，是名從假入空觀，亦名二諦觀，亦名慧眼，亦名一切智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: If a practitioner, cultivating calming and insight in this way, is able to fully understand that every dharma arises from mind alone, and, being merely a causally-conditioned, illusory appearance, is unreal and therefore empty — and, knowing this emptiness, no longer grasps at the mark or name of any dharma at all — this is calming through embodying true reality. At this point, one no longer sees, above, any fruit of buddhahood to be sought, nor, below, any sentient being to be brought across. This is called the contemplation moving from the conventional into emptiness, also called the contemplation of the two truths, also called the wisdom-eye, also called the wisdom that knows all things generally.

〖講〗前九章已竟，前五章為方便，乃是助行；第六七八九四章，為正修；既以正助二行為真因，必有所證之果位，故第十章明證果。妄心默契為之證。果即果覺，以其有因必有果，即證自心中本具之果也。謂吾等眾生，自迷昧以來，忽而天堂，忽而地獄，而此心未減一絲毫；十方諸佛，成等正覺，而此心未增一絲毫。所謂在凡不減，在聖不增……其實因不離自心，果亦不離自心，因果悉在一心之中……華嚴經云：「諸法所生，唯心所現，十法因果，世界微塵，因心成體。」……然既知諸法因心而有，而心又因何而生耶？當知前明，境本無生因心有，不知心本無生因境有，可見一切境界，即為生心之因緣。心又為一切境界之因緣，彼此互相為因，互相為緣，以內六根為因，外六塵為緣，塵不自塵，因根而有；根本無根，因境而有。故境生，故種種心生；境滅，故種種心滅。既知境本無生因心有，可見境本無生，心亦既知心本無生因境有，可見心亦無生，塵亦空，根亦空，故文云：「因緣虛假不實故空。」既當體本空，何故而有一切諸法耶？當知一切諸法，無不是因緣和合，虛妄假生；若因緣別離，虛妄名滅，生唯緣生，滅唯緣滅，幻緣生滅，其諸法本體，本無生，本不可得……茲就三自性而言，我人最初一念不覺為因，輾轉變現為緣，故幻出根身器界，本屬非有而有，而眾生妄執為實有，即遍計執性也；然一切諸法既隨緣起，皆屬虛相非實，故有即非有，幻妄稱相，此即依他起性也；本無自性，諸有如幻，當體即空，惟是一心，心外無法，本無分別，當體即是圓成實性也。若作如是觀，則不起分別，妄念不流，名為體真止。

Commentary: The nine chapters above are now complete: the first five, skillful means, being supporting practice; the sixth through ninth, actual practice. Having established both the primary and supporting practices as the genuine cause, there must, correspondingly, be a fruit-stage realization to which they lead — hence this tenth chapter explains the fruit realized. "Realization" here means the false mind coming into silent accord with reality itself. "Fruit" means the fruit of awakening — for wherever there is cause there must be fruit; this is realizing the fruit already inherent within one's own mind.

We sentient beings, ever since falling into delusion, pass now through heaven, now through hell — yet this mind of ours has never diminished by the slightest degree; every buddha of the ten directions has accomplished perfect, complete awakening — yet this same mind has never increased by the slightest degree either. This is what is meant by "unchanged in the ordinary being, unchanged in the sage"... In truth, the cause is never separate from one's own mind, and the fruit, too, is never separate from one's own mind — cause and fruit are entirely contained within this one mind... The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "Whatever dharmas arise are manifested by mind alone; every cause and effect, every world, down to the smallest mote of dust, is given substance by mind."

But if every dharma exists because of mind, from what, then, does mind itself arise? One should know, as already explained, that an object has, from the start, no arising of its own, existing only because of mind — and, likewise, mind, too, has, from the start, no arising of its own, existing only because of its object. From this it can be seen that every circumstance is itself the very cause and condition giving rise to mind, and mind is, in turn, the very cause and condition of every circumstance — each mutually serving as cause and condition for the other. The inward six faculties serve as cause; the outward six sense-objects serve as condition. A sense-object is not, in itself, a sense-object — it exists only because of a faculty; and a faculty, at root, has no faculty of its own — it exists only because of its object. Hence, because of an object, various minds arise; and because an object ceases, various minds cease.

Since an object has, from the start, no arising of its own, existing only because of mind, one sees that the object itself has no arising; and since mind, too, has, from the start, no arising of its own, existing only because of its object, one sees that mind itself has no arising either — the sense-object is empty, and the faculty is empty as well. Hence the root text says: "Being merely a causally-conditioned, illusory appearance, unreal, and therefore empty." But if its very substance is, from the start, empty, why then does every dharma appear to exist at all? One should know that every dharma, without exception, arises through the coming-together of causes and conditions, an illusory, provisional arising; when causes and conditions separate, this illusory appearance is called ceasing — arising is merely conditioned arising, ceasing merely conditioned ceasing, an illusory arising-and-ceasing — while the very substance of every dharma is, at root, unborn, at root unfindable...

Stated in terms of the three natures: our very first moment of non-awareness serves as cause, transforming and manifesting further through condition, and so the illusory faculties, body, and outward world are conjured forth — inherently not existing, and yet seemingly existing — and sentient beings falsely cling to this as truly, substantially existing: this is the imagined nature. Yet every dharma, arising as it does through conditions, belongs entirely to illusory appearance, not to what is truly real — existing, and yet not truly existing, an illusory appearance given a name: this is the dependent nature. Having, at root, no fixed nature of its own, every existing thing being illusory, its very substance being empty, being mind alone, with nothing outside of mind, inherently free of discrimination: this, in its very substance, is the perfected, complete, real nature. Contemplating in this way, no discrimination arises, and idle thought no longer flows — this is called calming through embodying true reality.

〖講〗大概修奢摩他空觀，宜依寂體而行。寂體者，自性不動也，於三如來藏中，即屬空如來藏，所謂靈光獨耀，迥脫根塵，了了見，無一物，於中了無人我是非彼此之別，了達吾人現前之一念，本來無相，念即無念，假名為念，於此為所觀之境。又觀此無念之一念，為能觀之智，即此前念已滅，後念未生，中間孤楞楞一念，能觀之智，觀此本性無念之境，此時境智一如，有何妄念可起，亦不見佛念可生……是故爾時上不見有佛道可成，下不見有眾生可度，佛界無所取，魔界無所捨，了知真如界內，絕生佛之假名，平等會中，不見自他之形相……當知末世眾生，所知障重，欲修止觀，成就定慧，殊為不易；若老老實實，執持一句彌陀，即是從假入空，將此名號，持至一心不亂，則從有念而至無念，從有生而入無生，念至能所雙忘時，所謂念空真念，即是修從假入空觀，亦名二諦觀。觀真諦，泯一切法，觀俗諦，則建立一切法。行者觀一切諸法，因緣虛假不實故空，即是真空觀；雖然因緣不實，空無所有，而不妨萬象差別，即是俗諦觀……亦名慧眼，亦名一切智。凡夫肉眼，為見思幻翳所障，觀一切法，皆是實有，若得空理，則觀一切法皆悉無相，以其慧眼了知空故。一切智，能了知一切內法內名，及一切外法外名，故名一切智。知一切智，二諦觀，慧眼等，悉是從假入空觀之異名辭，以名異而體同故。

Commentary: In general, cultivating the śamatha of emptiness-contemplation should proceed by relying on the stillness of one's own substance — "the stillness of one's own substance" meaning the inherent nature's own unmovingness. Among the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha, this belongs to the "empty" tathāgatagarbha — a solitary, spiritual light shining forth alone, entirely free of sense-faculty and sense-object, seeing with utter clarity that there is not a single thing there at all, with no distinction whatsoever, within it, between self and other, right and wrong, this and that. Fully understanding that this very thought presently before us, from the start, has no mark of its own — a thought that is itself no-thought, merely given the provisional name "thought" — this becomes the object of contemplation. And contemplating this single, thoughtless thought is itself the contemplating wisdom — precisely the moment when the preceding thought has already ceased and the following thought has not yet arisen, a single, solitary, vivid thought standing in between — this contemplating wisdom observing this same objectless, inherently thoughtless state: at this point, object and wisdom become one and the same — what idle thought could possibly arise, and what thought of even the Buddha could possibly be conceived?

...Hence, at this point, one no longer sees, above, any buddha-path to be accomplished, nor, below, any sentient being to be brought across; there is nothing in the buddha-realm to grasp, and nothing in the demon-realm to reject; fully understanding that, within the realm of suchness, even the provisional names of "sentient being" and "buddha" are entirely gone, and that, within this assembly of equality, no form of self or other is to be seen at all...

One should know that sentient beings of this final age carry a heavy obstruction of what-is-already-known, and cultivating calming and insight to accomplish concentration and wisdom is genuinely difficult indeed — but if one, honestly and wholeheartedly, holds fast to a single recitation of Amitābha's name, this is itself moving from the conventional into emptiness: holding this name until the mind becomes single-pointed and unconfused, one moves from having thought into no-thought, from having birth into the unborn — and when this recitation reaches the point where subject and object are both forgotten, this is what is called "reciting emptiness, the true recitation" — itself cultivating the contemplation moving from the conventional into emptiness, also called the contemplation of the two truths. Contemplating the ultimate truth dissolves every dharma; contemplating the conventional truth establishes every dharma. A practitioner contemplating that every dharma, being merely causally-conditioned and illusory, is unreal and therefore empty, is precisely the contemplation of true emptiness; yet, though causes and conditions are unreal, empty, with nothing there at all, this does not prevent the whole array of differentiated phenomena from still appearing — this is precisely the contemplation of the conventional truth...

This is also called the wisdom-eye, also called the wisdom that knows all things generally. An ordinary being's own fleshly eye, obstructed by the illusory film of views and thought, sees every dharma as truly, substantially existing; but once one attains the principle of emptiness, one sees every dharma as, in every case, markless, since the wisdom-eye fully understands this emptiness. "The wisdom that knows all things generally" is able to understand every inward dharma and inward name, and every outward dharma and outward name — hence it is called this. Knowing that "the wisdom that knows all things generally," "the contemplation of the two truths," "the wisdom-eye," and the rest, are all merely different names for the very same contemplation moving from the conventional into emptiness — different in name, one and the same in substance.

The Warning Against Stopping Here 住此觀之失

Chinese Source

〖文〗若住此觀，即墮聲聞辟支佛地，故經云：諸聲聞眾等自歎言，我等若聞淨佛國土，教化眾生，心不喜樂。所以者何？一切諸法，皆悉空寂，無生無滅，無大無小，無漏無為，如是思惟，不生喜樂。當知若見無為入正位者，其人終不能發三菩提心，此即定力多故，不見佛性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Should one remain fixed within this contemplation alone, one falls into the stage of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. Hence a sūtra says: the assembly of śrāvakas themselves lamented, saying, "Were we to hear of purifying a buddha-land or teaching and transforming sentient beings, our minds would take no joy in it at all. Why is this? Every dharma is, in every respect, empty and still, unborn and unceasing, neither great nor small, outflow-free and unconditioned — reflecting in this way, no joy arises in us." One should know that anyone who sees the unconditioned and takes this to be entering the correct stage will, in the end, never be able to give rise to the mind of complete awakening — this is precisely because concentration predominates, and the buddha-nature is not seen.

〖講〗此下乃引證明執著之失。當知如來設教，無非是逗機施化，而機有千差，故所施之教，亦有萬別。然雖有差異，究其所歸，理旨一也。所謂方便有多門，歸元無二路……足見如來所說一切觀門，無非是為成佛之方便，所謂先以欲鉤牽，後令入佛智，非以方便而為究竟也，故云住此觀，即墮聲聞辟支佛地。聞四諦聲而悟道者，謂之聲聞……梵語辟支迦羅，孤山云此翻緣覺，亦翻獨覺……當知無論緣覺獨覺，悉是佛教中小乘初步入門功夫，若住此而不肯前進，則失佛法大利……以此種人，斷盡三界見思，逃出生死樊籠……以是深厭生死。所謂三界如牢獄，視生死等冤家，遂保守真空，不肯出假化導，專抱消極厭世主義，飲三昧酒，墮無為坑，如來呵之為焦芽敗種者，即是此輩……然須知此空無漏境界，甚為至要，切不可視之為小乘，所謂十方同聚會，各各學無為，此是選佛場，心空及第歸。學佛人最難行者，即是初步空無漏功夫……若能達到此空無漏理，則假中亦易矣！但偏執空寂，定執無生無滅，無大無小即落於偏空，故復須從空入有，空而不空，乃至中道覆已。本社以念佛為歸實為至當，謂從有念而無念，由有生而證無生，表面觀之，斷煩惱，了生死，捨娑婆，而趣極樂，似屬消極，然一生極樂，即證妙果，乘願再來，普度眾生，故消極處便是積極也。

Commentary: What follows below cites proof of the fault in clinging to this state. One should know that the Tathāgata's own teaching is always matched to the particular occasion and capacity, and since capacities differ by the thousands, the teachings given likewise differ by the ten thousands — yet however different, traced to their final destination, the underlying principle is one and the same. As it is said: "There are many gates of skillful means, but no second road back to the source."

This is enough to show that every contemplative gate the Tathāgata taught is nothing but a skillful means toward buddhahood — first drawing beings in with the hook of desire, and only afterward leading them into the Buddha's own wisdom; skillful means was never meant as the final destination itself. Hence it is said: remaining fixed within this contemplation alone, one falls into the stage of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha. One who awakens to the path by hearing the sound of the Four Noble Truths is called a śrāvaka... "Pratyekabuddha," in Sanskrit — Mount Gu explains this as "one awakened through conditions," also "one awakened alone"...

One should know that whether pratyekabuddha or solitary awakener, both are merely the very first, entry-level effort within the Hīnayāna of the Buddha's own teaching — remaining fixed here, unwilling to press further forward, one loses the great benefit of the Buddhadharma entirely... A person of this kind, having entirely severed the views and thought of the three realms, escapes the very cage of birth-and-death... and so grows deeply weary of it — regarding the three realms as a prison, and birth-and-death as a hated enemy, guarding this true emptiness alone, unwilling to emerge and take up conventional existence to guide others, embracing an entirely passive, world-renouncing stance instead, drinking the wine of this samādhi and falling into the pit of the unconditioned — this is precisely the kind of person the Tathāgata rebukes as "a scorched sprout, a rotten seed."

Yet one must also understand that this state of empty, outflow-free realization is itself of the very greatest importance, and must never be dismissed as merely Hīnayāna — as it is said, "gathered together from the ten directions, each one studying the unconditioned — this is the very field where buddhas are chosen, and one returns having passed with an empty mind." The hardest step for anyone studying the Buddhadharma to actually take is precisely this first step of empty, outflow-free effort... Once one is able to reach this principle of emptiness and outflow-freedom, the conventional and the middle become far easier to reach as well! But clinging one-sidedly to this empty stillness, fixed on the notion of the unborn and unceasing, neither great nor small, one falls into a one-sided emptiness instead — hence one must, further, move from emptiness into existence, empty and yet not empty, all the way to the middle way itself.

Our own society holds that taking reciting the Buddha's name as one's true refuge is entirely fitting here — moving from having thought into no-thought, from having birth into realizing the unborn. On the surface, this looks passive: severing affliction, resolving birth-and-death, leaving this world of endurance behind to head toward the Pure Land. Yet once born in the Pure Land, one immediately realizes this wondrous fruit, and, riding one's own vow, returns again to broadly bring sentient beings across — hence what appears passive is, in truth, active.

II. Moving From Emptiness Into the Conventional 從空入假觀

Chinese Source

〖文〗若菩薩為一切眾生，成就一切佛法，不應取著無為而自寂滅。爾時應修從空入假觀，則當諦觀心性雖空，緣對之時，亦能出生一切諸法，猶如幻化，雖無定實，亦有見聞覺知等相差別不同。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: If a bodhisattva, for the sake of every sentient being, wishes to accomplish every buddha-dharma, one should not cling to the unconditioned and rest content in one's own stillness alone. At this point one should cultivate the contemplation moving from emptiness into the conventional, clearly observing that, although the nature of mind is itself empty, when it meets its various conditions it is nonetheless able to give rise to every dharma, exactly like an illusory transformation — though nothing fixed or real about it, it nonetheless carries every kind of differentiated mark of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing.

〖講〗二乘之人，證偏空以為究竟，不欲從空出假，教化眾生；菩薩則不然，有上求下化之功，具自利利他之德。最初發心，於四諦境，發四弘誓願，自利利他，了知諸法如幻化，不妨以幻作幻，所以為一切眾生，成就一切佛法……如來之法，即十力四畏十八不共等法。當知菩薩，亦令成就如是等法，了知如幻，不妨作如幻佛事，度如幻眾生，故於中不應取著無為，而自寂滅。爾時應修空出假觀，了知無為如幻，故無為無所不為，了達寂滅如幻，寂滅即非寂滅，菩薩了達如是，故發廣大心，度諸眾生……了達如幻，故名從空出假觀。諦即審實不虛之義，觀即是觀察，謂諦審觀察自心本性，雖無方隅，亦無分齊，了不可得。而歷緣對境之時，亦能出生一切諸法，上至四聖，下至六凡，世出世間，一切諸法，皆因此心性而有，雖有而空，有即非有，故云猶如幻化；雖無定實，而非有而有，而亦有見聞覺知等相差別不同……楞嚴經云：「虛空本非群相，而不妨諸相發揮。」虛空即喻自心，言自心有隨緣之用，故現一切法，但世人將幻化，作為實有，如幻師以巾，幻作兔馬，誤以為真。大乘止觀云：「所言觀者，雖知本不生今不滅，而以心性緣起，不無虛妄世用，猶如作夢，非有而有，故名為觀。」當知一切諸法，無非是幻，有是幻，空是幻，中道亦是幻，十方諸佛亦是幻，我輩今日建道場，作佛事亦是幻，永明大師曰：「降伏鏡裡魔軍，大作夢中佛事，廣度如幻含識，證得如幻菩提。」……吾人能學如幻觀，受用無盡，了達蓮社是幻，不妨認真辦幻社，以度幻生，了知念佛如幻，不妨一心念如幻佛，乃至作如幻之事，講如幻之經。維摩證得如幻，故丈室能容八萬四千燈王寶座；摩耶習此如幻，故一腹能納三千大千世界。於此濁世，處身甚難，能學從空出假，習如幻觀，為自利利他之最要緊法也。

Commentary: A follower of the two lesser vehicles, realizing one-sided emptiness, takes this to be the final destination, unwilling to emerge from emptiness into conventional existence to teach and transform sentient beings. A bodhisattva is not like this — seeking the path above and transforming beings below, possessing the virtue of benefiting both self and other. From the very first arising of the resolve, giving rise to the four universal vows toward the Four Noble Truths, benefiting both self and other, understanding every dharma to be illusory, one does not hesitate to use illusion to work with illusion — hence, for the sake of every sentient being, accomplishing every buddha-dharma...

The Tathāgata's own dharma is precisely the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the eighteen distinctive qualities, and the rest. One should know that a bodhisattva likewise brings these same qualities to accomplishment, understanding them as illusory, yet not hesitating to carry out illusory buddha-work and bring illusory sentient beings across — hence one should never cling to the unconditioned and rest content in one's own stillness alone. At this point one should cultivate the contemplation moving from emptiness into the conventional, understanding the unconditioned itself to be illusory — and so the unconditioned becomes capable of everything; understanding stillness itself to be illusory — stillness is then no longer merely stillness. A bodhisattva who fully understands this gives rise to a vast, expansive mind, bringing every sentient being across...

Fully understanding this illusory nature is what is called the contemplation moving from emptiness into the conventional. "Clearly" carries the sense of examining thoroughly, without falsehood; "contemplation" means observation — that is, clearly examining and observing one's own mind's inherent nature: though it has no direction and no fixed measure, ultimately unfindable, yet, meeting whatever circumstance and object arise, it is nonetheless able to give rise to every dharma — reaching, above, the four sagely realms, and, below, the six ordinary destinies, every mundane and transmundane dharma alike arising because of this very nature of mind. Though existing, it is empty; existing, it is not truly existing — hence it is said, "exactly like an illusory transformation." Though nothing fixed or real about it, it is not existing, and yet exists; and it carries, too, every kind of differentiated mark of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing...

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra says: "Empty space is, in itself, no particular appearance at all, and yet does not refuse to let every appearance play itself out within it." Empty space here is a metaphor for one's own mind — meaning that one's own mind has the capacity to follow conditions, and so every dharma manifests through it; but people of the world take this illusory transformation to be truly, substantially real, exactly as a magician's silk scarf, transformed into the illusion of a rabbit or a horse, is mistaken by onlookers for the real thing. The Mahāyāna Calming and Insight Treatise says: "What is called 'contemplation' is this: though one knows that what was originally unborn is, now, likewise unceasing, mind's own nature, arising through conditions, cannot help but carry a certain illusory, worldly function — exactly like the experience of a dream, not truly existing, and yet existing — hence it is called contemplation."

One should know that every dharma, without exception, is illusory — existence is illusory, emptiness is illusory, the middle way is illusory, the buddhas of the ten directions are illusory, and even our own building of this place of practice today, carrying out this very Buddhist work, is illusory as well. Great Master Yongming said: "Subduing the demonic armies within the mirror, carrying out great buddha-work within a dream, broadly bringing across illusory sentient beings, realizing illusory bodhi itself."

...We, learning this illusory contemplation, gain benefit without end: understanding that our own lotus society is itself illusory, we do not hesitate to run this illusory society in earnest, in order to bring illusory beings across; understanding that reciting the Buddha's name is itself illusory, we do not hesitate to recite this illusory buddha's name single-mindedly, and even to carry out illusory activities, expound illusory sūtras. Vimalakīrti, having realized this illusory nature, was able to fit eighty-four thousand jeweled lion-thrones within his own small room; Māyā, having practiced this same illusory contemplation, was able to hold the entire trichiliocosm within her own womb. In this turbid age, it is extremely difficult simply to exist at all — being able to study this movement from emptiness into the conventional, practicing this illusory contemplation, is the single most essential method for benefiting both self and other.

Commentary: A practitioner who has genuinely accomplished the contemplation moving from emptiness into the conventional, though fully understanding that everything, inward body and mind and outward world alike, sentient and non-sentient alike, is, in the end, empty and still, is nonetheless able, within this very emptiness and stillness, to cultivate every kind of illusory practice — building a place of practice, carrying out buddha-work — exactly like planting trees in empty space, fully understanding it to be illusory and unreal; like the path a fish leaves in water or a bird's track through the sky, gone the instant it passes, leaving no trace behind; treating illusory illness with illusory medicine, expounding illusory sūtras, teaching illusory dharmas — and though teaching measureless dharmas to bring across measureless sentient beings, the one who brings across and those brought across alike are, without exception, illusory...

Once a bodhisattva's own wisdom of the paths of practice has fully manifested, one is thereby able to accomplish unhindered eloquence. This unhindered eloquence is of four kinds, also called the four kinds of unhindered wisdom: unhindered eloquence in meaning, unhindered eloquence in the Dharma itself, unhindered eloquence in expression, and unhindered eloquence in joyful teaching. Possessing these four forms of eloquence, one is fully able to benefit every sentient being of the six destinies, fully understanding this illusory, unhindered, condition-according skillful means — this is called the expedient calming that accords with conditions...

Just as Chan Master Yongming Yanshou carried out one hundred and eight separate tasks every single day — for another person this would be an exhausting burden, yet the master himself considered it no burden at all, since, within his own mind, not a single thought arose, with nothing there at all; though busy, he was never truly busy; though laborious, he never truly labored — since none of it, without exception, was anything but illusory. This is precisely the achievement of the expedient calming that accords with conditions. One should know that this expedient, condition-according approach suits us best of all — as when eating, turning to observe the very one who eats; as when drinking tea, turning to observe the very one who drinks...

An ancient worthy said: "Following conditions, one recognizes one's own nature — with neither the illusory nor the real remaining." And again: "Walking is itself dhyāna, sitting is itself dhyāna." And again: "Walking is itself Amitābha, sitting is itself Amitābha." All of this points to precisely this same function...

This is the contemplation moving from emptiness into the conventional, also called the contemplation of equality — moving from the emptiness of nirvāṇa into the conventional existence of birth-and-death, understanding that this emptiness was never bare emptiness alone, but empty and yet not empty, emptiness and the conventional practiced together — hence it is called the contemplation of equality... Also called the Dharma-eye, also called the wisdom of the paths of practice. The Dharma-eye observes the conventional alone. "The wisdom of the paths of practice" means a bodhisattva, transforming others, using every path to bring forth every wholesome seed within sentient beings...

A bodhisattva bringing beings across must, without fail, possess this wisdom of the paths of practice before the Dharma-eye can shine perfectly complete — otherwise bringing beings across becomes extremely difficult. Śāriputra, though possessing very great wisdom, once had two disciples — one a goldsmith, the other a grave-keeper. Śāriputra instructed the grave-keeper to cultivate the contemplation of counting the breath, and the goldsmith to cultivate the contemplation of impurity; both applied themselves repeatedly, entirely in vain, with no benefit whatsoever, and both grew discouraged. The Buddha's own wisdom, observing this, recognized that the two had been assigned the wrong practice for their own natures, and instead instructed the goldsmith to cultivate counting the breath, and the grave-keeper to cultivate the contemplation of impurity — within a few days, both attained the fruit of the path. Hence it is said: "Impurity wrongly applied to the furnace-worker; breath-counting never to leave the grave-keeper alone." To bring beings across and truly benefit them, this wisdom of the paths of practice is indispensable...

Yet this wisdom is not easily realized. Now that we have reached this final age, if one wishes to spread the Dharma and benefit beings, there is nothing for it but honest recitation of the Buddha's name — taking up a single recitation and holding to it straight through, right then and there free of any notion of self, other, sentient being, or lifespan, forgetting body and mind inwardly, leaving the whole world behind outwardly — this is precisely calming; and each and every recitation of the Buddha's name remaining utterly clear and distinct — this is precisely insight. Once this recitation reaches the point where subject and object are both forgotten, self and other no longer two, one turns back and, through this very illusory gate of buddha-recollection, teaches and transforms illusory sentient beings, regardless of whatever attachment or affliction they carry, treating it entirely with Amitābha's own universal medicine, causing every sentient being to dissolve their own illusory reflected image and, at the end of life, be born in the illusory Pure Land itself. Were this truly accomplished, its wondrous benefit could hardly be expressed in words at all!

One should know that cultivating both the emptiness-contemplation and the conventional-contemplation are both, alike, skillful means toward buddhahood — one must never cling to either. Should one remain fixed within the contemplation moving from emptiness into the conventional alone, wisdom predominates while concentration remains slight, and though one sees the buddha-nature, it is not seen with full clarity, since one remains caught between the two extremes, unable to see the buddha-nature with perfect clarity. Though a bodhisattva accomplishes both of these two contemplations, they remain, even so, expedient contemplative gates, not correct contemplation itself.

III. The Middle Way 中道正觀

Chinese Source

〖文〗故經云：前二種為方便道。因是二空觀，得入中道第一義觀，雙照二諦，心心寂滅，自然流入薩婆若海。若菩薩欲於一念中具足一切佛法，應修息二邊分別止，行於中道正觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Hence a sūtra says: the first two contemplations are both skillful means. Through these two contemplations of emptiness, one enters the contemplation of the middle way, the supreme truth — illuminating both truths at once, thought after thought reaching still cessation, naturally flowing into the ocean of all-knowing wisdom. If a bodhisattva wishes to hold every buddha-dharma complete within a single thought, one should cultivate the calming that stills discrimination between the two extremes, and practice the correct contemplation of the middle way.

〖講〗上文言但修空觀，偏於定，不能證於中道；但修假觀，偏於慧，亦不能證於中道，必須止觀雙修，定慧均齊，方能證於中道……修空觀，則證人空；修假觀，則證法空。若修中道第一義諦觀，謂我法二執俱遣，能空之空亦除，空執兩忘，即證空空。修中道觀者，雙照二諦，非離開空有，為中道觀。若離開空有，別有中道，此即是但中。如雲外見月，不得稱為圓中道，必須雙照二諦，以不離二邊，而不即二邊故。夫吾人介爾一念心中，具足三千性相，百界千如，一一無非空假中。若說是有，則當體了不可得；倘說是空，則三千歷歷明明，當一念起時，直照起處，了不可得即空義也。然起處，歷歷明明，即假義也。歷歷明明而了不可得，非空非假，雙離二邊，即空即假，雙照二邊，即中道義。故云因是二空觀，得入中道第一義諦觀，心心寂滅，念念常住，空有不二，遮照同時，自然而念念流入薩婆若海。薩婆若，此云智慧，即念念流入諸佛大智慧海也！若菩薩欲於一念中，具足一切佛法者，當知吾人一念介爾心中，無法不備，所謂百千三昧，同在心源；河沙功德，同歸方寸，那一法離於自心，法法無非是自心所現故。但眾生在迷，將自心之如來妙能，蔭而不顯。若菩薩欲於一念心中，具足成就一切佛法，應須修於息二邊分別止，行於中道正觀，方能成就。言息二邊分別者，二邊，即指一切對待法言，如空有、我無我、常無常、大小、高下、長短、親疏等，凡對待者皆是。當知二邊對待法，其體本無定實，以其皆由比較而生，如大小對待，大非真大，因小見大；小非真小，因大見小是也。今對於一切之境，不起分別，故謂之息不著空有，不偏一法，不厭生死，不欣涅槃，無邊可止即邊即中。道無二邊，有何可止，蓋不期止而自止，故又謂之不止止，此為最高功夫，即是息二邊分別止。正即不偏不邪也，謂不著空有，而不離空有，雙遮雙照，遮照同時，如是而修，故謂之行於中道正觀。

Commentary: The passage above shows that cultivating only the emptiness-contemplation leans toward concentration and cannot realize the middle way; cultivating only the conventional-contemplation leans toward wisdom and likewise cannot realize the middle way. One must cultivate calming and insight together, concentration and wisdom evenly balanced, before the middle way can actually be realized... Cultivating the emptiness-contemplation realizes the emptiness of self; cultivating the conventional-contemplation realizes the emptiness of dharmas. Cultivating the contemplation of the middle way, the supreme truth, means that clinging to both self and dharmas is dispelled together, and even the very emptiness that dispels them is removed as well — both this emptiness and this clinging forgotten together — this realizes the emptiness of emptiness itself.

Cultivating the contemplation of the middle way illuminates both truths at once — it is not a third, separate thing apart from emptiness and existence. Were there some separate middle way apart from emptiness and existence, this would be a "mere middle" alone — like seeing the moon through clouds, which cannot be called the perfect middle way. One must, rather, illuminate both truths at once, neither separate from the two extremes nor identical with either extreme.

Within a single, momentary thought of our own mind, the three thousand realms of nature and characteristic, the hundred realms and thousand suchnesses, are already complete — every one of them, without exception, at once empty, at once conventional, at once the middle. Were one to say it exists, its very substance is ultimately unfindable; were one to say it is empty, the three thousand realms remain utterly clear and distinct. The very moment a single thought arises, illuminating directly the place from which it arises, finding it ultimately unfindable — this is the meaning of emptiness. Yet this place of arising remains utterly clear and distinct — this is the meaning of the conventional. Being utterly clear and distinct, and yet ultimately unfindable — neither empty nor conventional, doubly free of both extremes; at once empty and at once conventional, doubly illuminating both extremes — this is the meaning of the middle way.

Hence it is said: through these two contemplations of emptiness, one enters the contemplation of the middle way, the supreme truth, thought after thought reaching still cessation, moment after moment ever-abiding, emptiness and existence not two, negation and illumination occurring together, naturally flowing, thought after thought, into the ocean of all-knowing wisdom. "Sarvajña" means wisdom — flowing, thought after thought, into the great ocean of every buddha's own wisdom!

If a bodhisattva wishes to hold every buddha-dharma complete within a single thought, one should know that within this single, momentary thought of our own mind, there is no dharma not already present — the hundreds of thousands of samādhis all share this same source in the mind; merit as numerous as grains of sand along the Ganges all returns to this same single inch of the heart — what single dharma is ever separate from one's own mind, since every dharma, without exception, is manifested by one's own mind alone? It is only that sentient beings, lost in delusion, leave their own mind's Tathāgata-like wondrous capacity shrouded and unrevealed. If a bodhisattva wishes to hold every buddha-dharma complete and accomplished within a single thought, one must cultivate the calming that stills discrimination between the two extremes, and practice the correct contemplation of the middle way, before this can actually be accomplished.

As for "stilling discrimination between the two extremes": "the two extremes" refers to every pair of opposed terms — emptiness and existence, self and no-self, permanence and impermanence, great and small, high and low, long and short, close and distant, and every such relative pairing. One should know that a relative, opposed pair of this kind has, at root, no fixed, real substance of its own, since each arises entirely through comparison with the other — as in the pairing of great and small: "great" is not truly great in itself, but appears great only relative to something small; "small" is not truly small in itself, but appears small only relative to something great. Now, giving rise to no discrimination toward any object at all, this is called "stilling" — clinging to neither emptiness nor existence, favoring no single dharma, neither averse to birth-and-death nor delighting in nirvāṇa, with no extreme left to still at all, since the extreme is itself already the middle. The path has no two extremes to begin with — what, then, is there left to still? It is simply stilled without ever expecting to be stilled at all — hence it is also called "the stilling that does not still." This is the very highest effort of all, and is precisely the calming that stills discrimination between the two extremes. "Correct" means neither one-sided nor deviant — that is, clinging to neither emptiness nor existence, yet never separate from emptiness or existence either, doubly negating and doubly illuminating both at once — cultivating in this way is what is called practicing the correct contemplation of the middle way.

Root Text: How does one cultivate correct contemplation? Fully embodying the knowledge that mind's own nature is neither ultimately real nor merely conventional, one stills the mind that fixes on either the ultimate or the conventional — this is called correctness. Clearly contemplating that mind's own nature is neither empty nor conventional, and yet without destroying the dharma of emptiness or the conventional either. If one is able to illuminate this fully, one thereby penetrates the middle way within mind's own nature, perfectly illuminating both truths at once. If one is able to see the middle way, the two truths, within one's own mind, one thereby sees the middle way, the two truths, within every dharma — and yet does not grasp even at the middle way and the two truths themselves, since no fixed, determinate nature can ultimately be found there either. Commentary: "How does one" opens with the question itself. "Cultivating correct contemplation" means turning to illuminate and fully embody the understanding that this very thought presently before us, mind's own nature, is neither ultimately real nor merely conventional — "not ultimately real" meaning not truly empty, "not merely conventional" meaning not merely wondrous existence — stilling the mind that fixes on either the ultimate or the conventional. This is precisely the wondrous śamatha, the great gate of ultimate truth: one thing empty, everything empty, none of the ten dharma-realms not empty — this is precisely the ultimate emptiness of great prajñā itself, called, within the Mahāyāna Calming and Insight Treatise, the gate of great, still calming.

Yet clearly contemplating mind's own nature, though neither empty nor conventional, without destroying the dharma of emptiness or the conventional either — every appearance remaining vivid and distinct, empty and yet not empty, the three thousand realms remaining exactly as they are — this is precisely the wondrous samāpatti, the great gate of conventional truth...

If one is able to illuminate this fully, one thereby penetrates the middle way within mind's own nature, able to perfectly illuminate both the ultimate and conventional truths at once — within one's own mind, discrimination still blazes forth, and yet the very substance remains ever still; the very substance remains ever still, and yet discrimination still arises through conditions — this is called perfectly cultivating the calming and insight of the middle way. One should know that this is, in its entirety, a sudden-teaching dharma-gate — wondrous in the extreme, yet far from easy to cultivate, and not easily grasped except by a person of superior capacity...

If one is able to see the middle way, the two truths, within one's own mind, one thereby sees the middle way, the two truths, within every dharma. Why is this? Because every dharma is mind alone — and since every dharma is mind alone, once one's own mind sees the middle way and the two truths, every dharma, without exception, is likewise the middle way and the two truths, manifesting within one's own mind as well, since every circumstance is itself transformed by mind. Yet though one sees the middle way, the two truths, within every dharma, one does not grasp even at the middle way and the two truths themselves, since no fixed, determinate nature can ultimately be found there either...

Within every dharma, existence is never fixed existence, and emptiness is never fixed emptiness; doubly negating both, this negation of both is itself never fixed either — the middle way is not itself fixed as the middle way. Neither extreme is established, and the middle way itself rests on nothing at all. Yet this state of the correct contemplation of the middle way cannot be captured in words at all — free of the mark of verbal expression, free of the mark of mental grasping, known only through direct realization. That it is spoken of here in writing at all is only because those still lost in confusion cannot otherwise understand it, and so words are borrowed to point toward it — the path, at root, has no words of its own, and words are merely borrowed to reveal it. Though words are borrowed here, the state actually realized through genuine effort is not, in itself, words at all — it must be genuinely practiced and put into actual effect before it can be known.

因緣所生法　我說即是空　亦名為假名　亦名中道義
深尋此偈意，非惟具足分別中觀之相，亦是兼明前二種方便觀門旨趣。當知中道正觀，則是佛眼一切種智，若住此觀，則定慧力等，了了見佛性。安住大乘，行步平正，其疾如風，自然流入薩婆若海。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: As a verse in the Middle Treatise says:
"Whatever dharma arises through causes and conditions, I say is precisely empty; it is also called a provisional name, and also called the meaning of the middle way."
Examining this verse's own meaning deeply, it not only fully distinguishes the mark of the middle-way contemplation itself, but also explains the underlying intent of the two preceding, expedient contemplative gates as well. One should know that the correct contemplation of the middle way is precisely the buddha-eye, the wisdom that knows all things in every one of their modes. Remaining within this contemplation, concentration and wisdom become equal in strength, and one sees the buddha-nature with perfect clarity. Abiding in the Great Vehicle, one walks with a steady, even step, swift as the wind, naturally flowing into the ocean of all-knowing wisdom.

〖講〗天台二祖慧文大師讀中觀論。至四諦品，因緣所生法，乃至亦名中道義之四句偈時，恍然大悟，頓了諸法無非因緣所生，無不即空、即假、即中，圓三觀理，後將一心三觀，傳之於南嶽，南嶽傳之於智者，智者發揚而光大之，遂說三大部五小部，創立法華一宗，判別一代五時，抗折諸家，超乎群說，足徵此四句偈，為台宗建立三觀，根本發源之處，其義理深妙可知……佛眼者，非凡夫之肉眼，亦非諸天之天眼，不同二乘之慧眼，菩薩之法眼，唯佛眼能具足前四眼之用，五眼一眼中見，無事不知，無事不明，無事不見……偈曰：「天眼通非礙，肉眼礙非通，法眼唯觀俗，慧眼了知空，佛眼如千日，照異體還同。」雖遍照十界差別之異相，而差即無差，其體還同，無差而差，照異即假，差而無差，體同即空，空假不二，即中道觀，是知三觀一觀，名曰妙觀，五眼一眼，名為佛眼；三智一智，名為一切種智。若住此觀，則定慧力等，了了見於佛性，空即定，假即慧，空假不二，即是定慧力等。二乘偏於空，菩薩偏於假，至佛住方得空假不二，中道妙觀現前，了了見於佛性，安住大乘等。引法華譬喻品中，等賜大車譬，正喻行人，修圓頓止觀……大乘即指大白牛車乘，譬三諦實相妙理，豎無初後，橫絕邊涯，即空、即假、即中，具足百界千如，故名大乘。白牛，即喻稱性妙觀……安住大乘者，無住而住，謂之安住，即任運流入之謂。行步平正者，圓觀即止即觀，即觀即止，定慧不二，七覺調平。其疾如風者，譬圓觀稱性而作，任運入於無功用道，又喻八正道中行，速疾到薩婆若海也。當知此證果章中，所修之止觀，一一與前正修章所明不同，以其前來所明，乃是分證以前之緣修。今所明者，乃是分證見道後之稱性真修，故以安住大乘，自然流入等語言之，閱者不可忽略。

Commentary: The Tiantai school's second patriarch, Great Master Huiwen, reading the Middle Treatise, upon reaching the chapter on the Four Noble Truths and this same four-line verse — "whatever dharma arises through causes and conditions... also called the meaning of the middle way" — suddenly attained great awakening, immediately understanding that every dharma, without exception, arises through causes and conditions, and is, without exception, at once empty, at once conventional, at once the middle: the complete principle of the three contemplations. He later transmitted this single mind's threefold contemplation to Nanyue, who transmitted it in turn to Zhiyi — and Zhiyi expounded and expanded it further still, setting forth the three major and five minor works, founding the Tiantai school based on the Lotus Sūtra, distinguishing the five periods of the Buddha's teaching career, surpassing every other school and rising above every other account. This is enough to show that this four-line verse is the very root and source from which the Tiantai school established its threefold contemplation — its principle can be seen to run very deep and subtle indeed.

The "buddha-eye" is neither an ordinary being's fleshly eye, nor a heavenly being's heavenly eye, nor the same as the two lesser vehicles' wisdom-eye or a bodhisattva's Dharma-eye — only the buddha-eye is able to encompass the function of all four of these eyes together; seeing through all five eyes as one single eye, there is nothing it does not know, nothing it does not understand, nothing it does not see... A verse says: "The heavenly eye penetrates but does not obstruct; the fleshly eye obstructs but does not penetrate; the Dharma-eye observes the conventional alone; the wisdom-eye fully knows emptiness; the buddha-eye is like a thousand suns, illuminating every difference while its own substance remains one and the same." Though illuminating every differentiated appearance across the ten realms, difference is itself no difference at all, its very substance remaining one and the same; no difference and yet differentiated — illuminating difference is itself the conventional; differentiated and yet no difference — the same substance is itself emptiness. Emptiness and the conventional not two — this is precisely the contemplation of the middle way.

From this we know: the three contemplations are one contemplation, called the wondrous contemplation; the five eyes are one eye, called the buddha-eye; the three wisdoms are one wisdom, called the wisdom that knows all things in every one of their modes. Remaining within this contemplation, concentration and wisdom become equal in strength, and one sees the buddha-nature with perfect clarity — emptiness is precisely concentration, the conventional is precisely wisdom, and emptiness and the conventional not being two is precisely concentration and wisdom equal in strength. The two lesser vehicles lean toward emptiness; bodhisattvas lean toward the conventional; only upon reaching buddhahood itself does one attain emptiness and the conventional as not two, the wondrous contemplation of the middle way manifesting fully, seeing the buddha-nature with perfect clarity, abiding in the Great Vehicle, and so on.

This cites the parable of the equally-bestowed great cart from the Lotus Sūtra's own Parable Chapter, precisely illustrating a practitioner cultivating perfect and sudden calming and insight... "The Great Vehicle" refers to the great white ox-cart, a metaphor for the three truths' own wondrous principle of true reality — vertically without beginning or end, horizontally beyond every boundary, at once empty, at once conventional, at once the middle, complete with the hundred realms and thousand suchnesses — hence it is called the Great Vehicle. The white ox is a metaphor for the wondrous contemplation according with one's own true nature...

"Abiding in the Great Vehicle" means abiding without abiding — this is called "abiding," that is, flowing in naturally, without effort. "Walking with a steady, even step" means the perfect contemplation is at once calming and insight, at once insight and calming, concentration and wisdom not two, the seven factors of awakening kept in perfect balance. "Swift as the wind" is a metaphor for the perfect contemplation acting spontaneously in accord with one's own true nature, flowing naturally into the path of effortless action — also a metaphor for walking the eightfold noble path, swiftly reaching the ocean of all-knowing wisdom.

One should know that the calming and insight cultivated in this chapter on the fruit realized differs, in every respect, from what was explained in the earlier chapter on actual practice — for what was explained there was the conditioned cultivation prior to any partial realization, while what is explained here is the true cultivation according with one's own nature after partial realization of the path has already occurred. Hence it is described in terms such as "abiding in the Great Vehicle" and "naturally flowing" — the reader should not pass over this distinction lightly.

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Walking the Tathāgata's own walk, entering the Tathāgata's own room, wearing the Tathāgata's own robe, sitting in the Tathāgata's own seat — one adorns oneself with the very adornment of the Tathāgata himself. One attains purity of the six faculties, enters the Buddha's own realm, clings to nothing within any dharma at all, and every buddha-dharma manifests before one, accomplishing the samādhi of buddha-recollection. Abiding in the Śūraṅgama samādhi, this is precisely the samādhi of universally manifesting physical form — entering universally into the buddha-lands of the ten directions, teaching and transforming sentient beings, adorning and purifying every buddha-land, making offerings to every buddha of the ten directions, upholding the entire treasury of every buddha's own Dharma, complete in every pāramitā of practice, realizing entry into the stage of a great bodhisattva — and so becomes a companion equal to Samantabhadra and Mañjuśrī themselves.

〖講〗上既修圓頓止觀，安住大乘，則念念流入薩婆若海，即是行如來行，入如來室，著如來衣，坐如來座。如來行者，謂無一行不是如來行，一行一切行，一切行一行，行者至此，能行同如來，故名行如來行；如來衣者，忍辱柔和是；如來座者，諸法空是，如來室者，大慈悲是……今行者，著如來衣，入如來室，坐如來座者，正顯念念入於聖流，非前凡小可比也，是則以如來莊嚴而自莊嚴。如來莊嚴，不外乎福慧二德，六度為能莊嚴，法身為所莊嚴，能所不二，即妙莊嚴……末世眾生，根機淺薄，欲修一心三觀，行如來行，以如來莊嚴而自莊嚴，是為甚難，若修念佛，較易成就。正念佛時，即一心三觀，能念之心空寂，即空觀；所念之佛歷明，即假觀；非空非假，即中道觀；念至一心不亂，則一心三觀自然現前……獲得六根清淨者：我等眾生，眼為色所染，耳為聲所染，乃至意為法塵所染，見不超色，耳不越聲，六根悉不清淨；若行人修觀至相似分證位，初信即斷見惑顯真理，二信至七信，斷思惑盡，不為三界六塵所染，此時六根互用，眼不特見色，而能聽聲，舌不特別味，而亦能知法，故獲六根清淨入佛境界……是則於一切法無所染著，一切好境，不生貪愛，一切惡境，不生憎厭，了達好惡平等，憎愛一如，所謂：「百花叢裡過，一葉不沾身。」此時一切佛法，皆現在前，成就念佛三昧，即是一心三觀，念念與佛相應，得大自在也。

Commentary: Having, above, cultivated perfect and sudden calming and insight, abiding in the Great Vehicle, thought after thought flowing into the ocean of all-knowing wisdom, this is precisely walking the Tathāgata's own walk, entering the Tathāgata's own room, wearing the Tathāgata's own robe, sitting in the Tathāgata's own seat. "The Tathāgata's own walk" means that no single act is anything other than the Tathāgata's own act — one act is every act, every act is one act; a practitioner who reaches this point acts exactly as the Tathāgata acts, hence "walking the Tathāgata's own walk." "The Tathāgata's own robe" is patience and gentleness; "the Tathāgata's own seat" is the emptiness of every dharma; "the Tathāgata's own room" is great compassion...

A practitioner who now wears the Tathāgata's own robe, enters the Tathāgata's own room, and sits in the Tathāgata's own seat shows precisely that, thought after thought, one has entered the sagely stream — no longer comparable to the ordinary being or the lesser vehicles of before — and adorns oneself with the very adornment of the Tathāgata himself. The Tathāgata's own adornment is nothing other than the twin virtues of merit and wisdom: the six pāramitās as the adorning agent, the Dharma-body as what is adorned, agent and object not two — this is precisely the wondrous adornment itself...

Sentient beings of this final age, their own faculties shallow and weak, find it extremely difficult to cultivate the single mind's threefold contemplation, walking the Tathāgata's own walk, adorning oneself with the Tathāgata's own adornment — but cultivating buddha-recollection is comparatively easy to accomplish. Precisely while reciting the Buddha's name, this is itself the single mind's threefold contemplation: the reciting mind, empty and still, is the emptiness-contemplation; the recited Buddha, vivid and clear, is the conventional-contemplation; neither empty nor conventional is the contemplation of the middle way. Reciting until the mind becomes single-pointed and unconfused, the single mind's threefold contemplation manifests naturally of itself...

As for "attaining purity of the six faculties": we sentient beings have the eye defiled by form, the ear defiled by sound, and, further, the mind defiled by mental objects — seeing never surpasses form, hearing never surpasses sound, and none of the six faculties are pure at all. If a practitioner cultivating contemplation reaches the stage of approximate, partial realization — at the first degree of faith already severing the affliction of views and revealing true principle, from the second through the seventh degree of faith entirely severing the affliction of thought, no longer defiled by the six sense-objects of the three realms — at this point the six faculties become interchangeable: the eye is not only able to see form but also to hear sound; the tongue is not only able to distinguish taste but also to know mental objects — hence one attains purity of the six faculties and enters the Buddha's own realm...

This means clinging to nothing within any dharma at all — giving rise to no craving toward any good circumstance, no aversion toward any bad circumstance, fully understanding good and bad as equal, aversion and craving as one and the same — as it is said: "passing straight through a thicket of a hundred flowers, without a single petal clinging to the robe." At this point every buddha-dharma manifests before one, accomplishing the samādhi of buddha-recollection — this is precisely the single mind's threefold contemplation, thought after thought according with the Buddha himself, attaining great freedom and ease.

The Fruit at the Beginning, and at the End 初心後心證果

Chinese Source

〖文〗華嚴經中，初發心時便成正覺，了達諸法真實之性，所有慧身不由他悟。亦云：初發心菩薩，得如來一身作無量身。亦云：初發心菩薩即是佛。涅槃經云：發心畢竟二不別，如是二心前心難。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "At the very moment of first giving rise to the resolve, one already accomplishes perfect awakening, fully understanding the true nature of every dharma — this wisdom-body is realized entirely of itself, not through another." It further says: "A bodhisattva who has just given rise to the resolve attains the Tathāgata's own single body, manifesting it as countless bodies." It further says: "A bodhisattva who has just given rise to the resolve is already a buddha." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "The mind at the very first resolve, and the mind at the very end, are ultimately not two different things — yet, between these two, the first is the harder."

〖講〗此引經證釋。初發心住菩薩，即能現身百界，八相作佛之義……初心即圓十住之第一發心住，即別教初地位也。住前已將見思塵沙二惑斷盡，至此而能初破一品無明，證一分中道，則與法性真理相應，故能作分證佛相。此之作佛，乃是八相成道之佛，非究竟離垢妙極法身佛，故云初發心時，便成正覺，且能了達諸法真實之性。真實之性，乃是無性，無性之性，即是如來藏圓覺妙性……亦云：「初發心菩薩，得如來一身作無量身。」一身即法身，無量即應身，蓋圓初住時，則能三因圓發，三德圓顯，正因佛性發，成法身德，緣因善性成解脫德，了因慧性發，成般若德。亦云初發心菩薩，即是佛，此亦從圓初住而言，因初住能現身百界，八相作佛，二住千界，三住萬界，如是乃至無量世界，乃一一悉能八相作佛，度諸有情，當知凡夫初發心時，亦可稱佛，不過是理即佛，非分證究竟之佛也……如是二心前心難者，因為因地發心，最為不易，縱使發心，求其圓常正信，尤為不易。古人云：「千里程途，不離最初一步。」又云：「萬丈高樓從地起。」若地基穩固，則千層萬層，亦隨之而建，菩薩發心亦如是。若最初發得圓常真心，則五十五位真菩提路，亦隨之而起，故云如是二心前心難。

Commentary: This cites scripture to prove the point. A bodhisattva who has just entered the abode of first arousing the resolve is thereby already able to manifest a body throughout the hundred realms, accomplishing buddhahood through the eight aspects... "The initial mind" here is precisely the very first of the ten abodes in the perfect teaching, corresponding to the initial ground in the separate teaching. Prior to this abode, one has already entirely severed the afflictions of views, thought, and dust-and-sand; reaching this point, one is able, for the very first time, to break through a single portion of ignorance and realize a single portion of the middle way, thereby according with the true principle of the Dharma-nature — hence one is able to manifest a partial realization of buddhahood. This "becoming a buddha" is buddhahood as manifested through the eight aspects, not the ultimate, fully purified, wondrous, ultimate Dharma-body buddha — hence it is said, "at the very moment of first giving rise to the resolve, one already accomplishes perfect awakening," fully understanding the true nature of every dharma. This "true nature" is precisely no-nature; this natureless nature is precisely the tathāgatagarbha's own perfect, wondrous nature of awakening...

It further says: "A bodhisattva who has just given rise to the resolve attains the Tathāgata's own single body, manifesting it as countless bodies." "Single body" means the Dharma-body; "countless" means the response-body — for at the very first of the perfect teaching's ten abodes, the three causes fully manifest and the three virtues fully appear together: the correct-cause buddha-nature manifests, accomplishing the virtue of the Dharma-body; the condition-cause wholesome nature accomplishes the virtue of liberation; the complete-cause wisdom-nature manifests, accomplishing the virtue of prajñā. It further says that a bodhisattva who has just given rise to the resolve is already a buddha — this too speaks from the perspective of the perfect teaching's first abode, since at this first abode one is already able to manifest a body throughout a hundred realms, accomplishing buddhahood through the eight aspects; at the second abode, a thousand realms; at the third, ten thousand realms; and so on, up through measureless worlds, each and every one manifesting buddhahood through the eight aspects, bringing sentient beings across. One should know that an ordinary being, at the very moment of first giving rise to the resolve, may likewise be called a buddha — but only "buddha in principle," not a buddha of partial or ultimate realization.

As for why "between these two minds, the first is the harder": because giving rise to the resolve at the causal stage is, in itself, extremely difficult — and even having given rise to it, seeking a perfect, constant, correct faith is harder still. The ancients said: "A journey of a thousand miles never departs from its very first step." And again: "A building ten thousand feet tall rises from the ground up." If the foundation is solid, a thousand, ten thousand stories can be built upon it in turn — a bodhisattva's own arising resolve is exactly the same. If, at the very first, one gives rise to a perfect, constant, true mind, the fifty-five stages of the true path to bodhi will, in turn, rise up from it as well — hence it is said, "between these two minds, the first is the harder."

The dragon-girl then took the jeweled necklace from around her own neck and offered it to the World-Honored One, saying to Śāriputra: "You have seen me offer this jeweled necklace — was this offering to the Buddha quick or not?" He answered: "Very quick indeed." The dragon-girl thereupon immediately went to the stainless world in the south and became a buddha there, bringing sentient beings across.

This attainment of buddhahood, swift as it was, was still buddhahood manifested through the eight aspects, the buddhahood of one who has just entered the abode of first arousing the resolve. The scriptures cited above are all cited to show that the initial mind is already complete with every buddha-dharma — this is precisely the initial "a" gate spoken of in the Larger Sūtra, its final gate being the "dha" gate, together illustrating the forty-two stages; it is precisely what the Lotus Sūtra means by opening sentient beings' own knowledge and vision of the Buddha — for though it speaks of "opening," the further steps of showing, awakening, and entering are already implied within it; and it is precisely what the Nirvāṇa Sūtra means by seeing the buddha-nature and thereby abiding in great nirvāṇa — for though it speaks of the initial mind realizing the path, the final mind is already implied within it as well. This has briefly explained the mark of the fruit of partial realization attained by an initial-stage bodhisattva through cultivating calming and insight.

〖文〗次明後心證果之相，後心所證境界則不可知，今推教所明，終不離止觀二法。所以者何？如法華經云：殷勤稱歎，諸佛智慧則觀義，此即約觀以明果也。涅槃經廣辯百句解脫以釋大涅槃者，涅槃則止義，是約止以明果也。故云：大般涅槃名常寂定，定者即是止義。法華經中雖約觀明果，則攝於止，故云：乃至究竟涅槃常寂滅相，終歸於空。涅槃中雖約止明果，則攝於觀，故以三德為大涅槃。此二大經雖復文言出沒不同，莫不皆約止觀二門辨其究竟，並據定慧兩法以明極果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Next, explaining the mark of the fruit realized at the final mind: the state realized at the final mind cannot itself be directly known, but, reasoning from what the teachings themselves make clear, it never departs from these same two methods of calming and insight. Why is this? As the Lotus Sūtra earnestly praises the buddhas' own wisdom, this is the meaning of insight — explaining the fruit in terms of insight. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own extended discussion of the hundred phrases of liberation, explaining great nirvāṇa, takes nirvāṇa itself in the sense of calming — explaining the fruit in terms of calming. Hence it is said: great nirvāṇa is called the ever-still concentration, and concentration here carries precisely the meaning of calming. Though the Lotus Sūtra explains the fruit in terms of insight, this is itself encompassed within calming — hence it says, "reaching, in the end, the ever-still, extinguished mark of nirvāṇa, ultimately returning to emptiness." Though the Nirvāṇa Sūtra explains the fruit in terms of calming, this is itself encompassed within insight — hence it takes the three virtues as constituting great nirvāṇa. Though these two great sūtras differ in how their own words appear and recede, both, without exception, distinguish the ultimate fruit in terms of the two gates of calming and insight, and both explain the ultimate fruit based on these same two dharmas of concentration and wisdom.

〖講〗前明初心證分證果之相，次明後心證究竟果之相。後對前言，即是最後之一心也，然亦可互相展轉論後心，如二住對初住為後心，三住對二住為後心，所謂初地不知二地事，等覺不知妙覺事。智者大師，不過位居五品，云何能知後心所證境界？既不可知，如何能明？大師以己所證，與聖教量之推究所明，不但能證之行，不離止觀，所證之果，亦不離止觀。所謂大乘因者止觀是，大乘果者亦止觀是，如是推教所明，則無錯謬矣！……涅槃經中，如來性品第四十二迦葉菩薩，殷勤啟請，求世尊重為廣說大涅槃之義，世尊為之廣辯，共有百句，各各不同，悉皆顯大涅槃，解脫真義……涅槃所以約止明果者，因其涅名不生，槃名不滅，不生不滅，即是止，止即定義，故涅槃以止而明極果也……涅槃二字略稱，具足云般涅槃那，即是不生不滅。大者，摩訶也……所以欲名常寂定者，因為小乘教中亦有不生不滅，但彼不能稱為常寂定，因其了生死，只能了一半；斷煩惱，亦只能斷一半。唯獨大乘度二種生死，滅五住煩惱，當知念一句彌陀，亦即是常寂定，以其有事理二一心不亂故……涅槃有三種：所謂圓淨涅槃，即般若德；方便淨涅槃，即解脫德；性淨涅槃，即法身德。法華與涅槃，二種大經，雖復文言，有出有沒不同，出則明顯，沒即隱密。而二經之中，觀攝止，止攝觀，莫不皆約止觀二門，辨其究竟，並據定慧二法，以明極果，以其在因為止觀，在果為定慧故也。

Commentary: What came before explained the mark of the fruit of partial realization at the initial mind; this now explains the mark of the fruit of ultimate realization at the final mind. "Final" is spoken relative to "initial" — that is, the very last of all minds — though this too can be spoken of in relative, shifting terms: the second abode, relative to the first, is itself a "final mind"; the third abode, relative to the second, is likewise a "final mind" — as it is said, the first ground does not know the affairs of the second ground, and even equal enlightenment does not know the affairs of wondrous enlightenment. Great Master Zhiyi himself occupied no higher than the fifth of the six stages of purity — how, then, could he possibly know the state realized at this final mind? And if it truly cannot be known, how can it be explained at all? The master, reasoning from his own realization together with the measure of the sacred teachings, made clear that not only the practice that leads to realization, but the very fruit realized as well, never departs from calming and insight. As it is said: the Great Vehicle's cause is precisely calming and insight, and the Great Vehicle's fruit is likewise precisely calming and insight — reasoning from the teachings in this way, there can be no error in it!

...In the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own chapter on the Tathāgata's own nature, the bodhisattva Kāśyapa earnestly requests the World-Honored One to further explain, at length, the meaning of great nirvāṇa, and the World-Honored One answers at length, in a hundred distinct phrases altogether, each different from the last, all of them revealing the true meaning of great nirvāṇa and liberation... The reason nirvāṇa explains the fruit in terms of calming is this: "nir-" means unborn, "-vāṇa" means unceasing — being unborn and unceasing is itself calming, and calming carries precisely the meaning of concentration; hence nirvāṇa explains the ultimate fruit in terms of calming...

"Nirvāṇa" is an abbreviated form, in full "parinirvāṇa" — precisely the unborn and unceasing. "Great" means "mahā"... The reason it is called "the ever-still concentration" is this: the Hīnayāna teaching, too, speaks of the unborn and unceasing, but this cannot properly be called "the ever-still concentration," since it resolves birth-and-death only halfway, and severs affliction only halfway as well. Only the Mahāyāna crosses over both kinds of birth-and-death entirely, extinguishing all five categories of dwelling-affliction. One should know that reciting a single recitation of Amitābha's name is likewise itself this very ever-still concentration, since it carries both the phenomenal and the noumenal forms of the single, unconfused mind together...

Nirvāṇa is of three kinds: the perfect, pure nirvāṇa, which is the virtue of prajñā; the expedient, pure nirvāṇa, which is the virtue of liberation; and the inherently pure nirvāṇa, which is the virtue of the Dharma-body. The Lotus and Nirvāṇa sūtras, though their own words appear and recede differently — appearing where explicit, receding where implicit — both, within themselves, have insight encompassing calming and calming encompassing insight; both, without exception, distinguish the ultimate fruit in terms of the two gates of calming and insight, and both base the ultimate fruit on these same two dharmas of concentration and wisdom — since, at the causal stage, these are called calming and insight, and, at the fruit stage, they are called concentration and wisdom.

〖文〗行者當知初中後果皆不可思議。故新譯金光明經云：前際如來不可思議，中際如來種種莊嚴，後際如來常無破壞。皆約修止觀二心以辨其果故。般舟三昧經中偈云：
諸佛從心得解脫　心者清淨名無垢
五道鮮潔不受色　有學此者成大道

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: A practitioner should know that the initial, middle, and final fruits are all inconceivable. Hence the newer translation of the Golden Light Sūtra says: "The former-period Tathāgata is inconceivable; the middle-period Tathāgata carries every kind of adornment; the latter-period Tathāgata is ever free of ruin" — all of this distinguished in terms of the two minds cultivated through calming and insight. A verse in the Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra says:
"Every buddha attains liberation through mind itself; this mind, being pure, is called stainless. The five destinies, clean and pure, take on no defiling color — whoever studies this accomplishes the great path."

〖講〗行者當知初中後果，不可思議。初果即初發心住，破一品無明，能分身百界，八相作佛，故不可思議。中果，從二住至十住，乃至經歷行向地等覺四十位，於百千萬億世界，分身作佛，故不可思議，以其能破四十一品無明。後果即妙覺位，證到究竟極果，至此無明破盡，垢染全除，究竟登於涅槃山頂，永別無明父母，所謂三覺圓，萬德備，究竟圓滿阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，成等正覺，故不可思議……般舟三昧，即是常行三昧，九十日為期，始終不坐不臥，亦名佛立三昧。若三昧功夫修成時，佛則立現在前。此經偈中云：諸佛從心得解脫，諸佛即過去現在未來三世十方一切諸佛，悉皆從此一念心性中，而得解脫。若離此心之外，另求有所得有所證者，無有是處。古語云：「諸佛妙道，皆於眾生心行中求。」心行者，眾生妄心流動不息，故稱心行，心者清淨名無垢者，眾生之心，本自清淨，本自光明，雖有煩惱，而此心不被煩惱所染，其體原是清淨潔白，故名無垢。五道鮮潔不受色，五道，即天人地獄畜生餓鬼，修羅即在五道中，包括在內，眾生雖流落於五道，而自心仍然清淨鮮潔，不受污色所染，故云五道鮮潔不受色。所以有志之士，能於此心而學習止觀者，則能成就無上大乘之佛道矣！所謂是心是佛，是心作佛，是心是萬法，是心作萬法。觀經云：「諸佛正遍知海，皆從心而生。」故於此心而習止觀，無不圓成，故云有學此者成大道。

Commentary: A practitioner should know that the initial, middle, and final fruits are all inconceivable. The initial fruit is precisely the abode of first arousing the resolve, breaking through one portion of ignorance, able to manifest a body throughout a hundred realms, accomplishing buddhahood through the eight aspects — hence it is inconceivable. The middle fruit runs from the second abode through the tenth, and further, through the forty stages of practices, transferences, grounds, and equal enlightenment, manifesting a body throughout hundreds of thousands of myriads of koṭis of worlds, accomplishing buddhahood — hence it is inconceivable, since it is able to break through forty-one portions of ignorance. The final fruit is precisely the stage of wondrous enlightenment, realizing the ultimate fruit itself — at this point ignorance is entirely broken through, every defilement entirely removed, ascending at last to the very summit of nirvāṇa's own mountain, permanently parting from ignorance, one's own former parent — the three forms of awakening perfectly complete, every virtue fully present, ultimately and perfectly accomplishing unsurpassed, complete awakening — hence it is inconceivable.

The Pratyutpanna Samādhi is the constant-walking samādhi, practiced over a ninety-day period, never sitting or lying down from beginning to end, also called the samādhi of the standing Buddha — once this samādhi's own effort is accomplished, a buddha stands manifest before the practitioner. This sūtra's own verse says: "Every buddha attains liberation through mind itself" — every buddha of the three time periods and ten directions, past, present, and future alike, attains liberation entirely from within this very single thought, this nature of mind. Seeking attainment or realization somewhere apart from this mind is simply impossible. An old saying goes: "Every buddha's own wondrous path is to be sought entirely within sentient beings' own activity of mind." "Activity of mind" means the constant, unceasing flow of sentient beings' own idle thought — hence it is called "activity of mind."

"This mind, being pure, is called stainless": sentient beings' own mind is, from the start, inherently pure, inherently luminous — though affliction is present, this mind is never truly defiled by that affliction, its own substance remaining, from the start, pure and unblemished — hence it is called "stainless." "The five destinies, clean and pure, take on no defiling color": the five destinies are the heavens, humans, hell, animals, and hungry ghosts — with the asuras included among these five as well. Though sentient beings fall and wander among these five destinies, their own mind remains, even so, clean and pure, taking on no defiling color at all — hence it is said, "the five destinies, clean and pure, take on no defiling color." This is why a person of true aspiration, able to study calming and insight through this very mind, is able to accomplish the unsurpassed buddha-path of the Great Vehicle! This is what is meant by: this mind is buddha, this mind creates buddhahood; this mind is every dharma, this mind creates every dharma. The Contemplation Sūtra says: "Every buddha's own ocean of perfect, universal knowledge arises entirely from mind." Hence, studying calming and insight through this very mind, nothing fails to reach perfect accomplishment — hence it is said, "whoever studies this accomplishes the great path."

Closing Exhortation 結歸誓願

Chinese Source

〖文〗誓願所行者，須除三障五蓋；如或不除，雖勤用功，終無所益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: As for what a vow calls one to actually carry out: one must remove the three obstructions and the five hindrances. Should they not be removed, however diligently one applies effort, in the end no benefit follows at all.

〖講〗誓願所行者一段，乃智者大師最後至囑。誓願二字，足徵大師悲心，懇切已極。誓以要其心；願以成其志。唯願修學行者，除三障、去五蓋；三障，一煩惱障，二業障，三報障，有種種煩惱致造種種業，有此業，故感生死之果報。欲除三障者，因其障卻三德故也；煩惱障障般若德，業障障解脫德，報障障法身德。所謂三障障乎三德，若破除之，即三障而顯三德。五蓋者，即上所說貪欲、嗔恚、乃至疑蓋，有此五蓋，致被蓋覆吾人之清淨心，如或不除，雖勤用功，則終無所益，譬如紅日，被煙影雲霧阿修羅手所遮，不得出現，必須摒棄五種之障，方克有效。全部止觀，共有十章，章章莫不以止觀念佛，顯其究竟，此一行文，雖大師最後之至囑，而亦可作為流通分，願諸學佛同仁，研閱此者，請勤修止觀。此觀者何？老實念佛也！

Commentary: This passage, "as for what a vow calls one to actually carry out," is Great Master Zhiyi's own final exhortation. The very words "vow" themselves are enough to show how deeply earnest the master's own compassionate intent truly was. A vow binds the mind to its purpose; an aspiration brings one's own resolve to completion. It is his sole wish that practitioners taking up this study remove the three obstructions and cast off the five hindrances. The three obstructions are: the obstruction of affliction, the obstruction of karma, and the obstruction of retribution — various afflictions give rise to various karma, and this karma, in turn, brings about the fruit of birth-and-death as retribution. These three obstructions must be removed precisely because they obstruct the three virtues in turn: the obstruction of affliction obstructs the virtue of prajñā; the obstruction of karma obstructs the virtue of liberation; the obstruction of retribution obstructs the virtue of the Dharma-body. That is, the three obstructions obstruct the three virtues — and breaking through the obstructions is itself what reveals the virtues.

The five hindrances are precisely covetous desire, anger, and the rest, through doubt, already explained above — carrying these five hindrances leaves our own pure mind covered over; should they not be removed, however diligently one applies effort, in the end no benefit follows at all, exactly like the red sun obscured by smoke, shadow, cloud, mist, and the hand of an asura, unable to shine forth at all. One must, without fail, cast aside these five kinds of obstruction before any real effect can follow.

This entire text of calming and insight comprises ten chapters in all, and there is not a single chapter that does not, in the end, reveal calming-and-insight-through-reciting-the-Buddha's-name as its own ultimate point. Though this one closing passage is the master's own final exhortation, it may equally well serve as this work's own dissemination section. It is my wish that every fellow student of the Buddhadharma who studies and reads this text will diligently cultivate calming and insight. And what, in the end, is this very contemplation? Simply this: honest, wholehearted recitation of the Buddha's name!

Master Baojing's own commentary closes exactly where Fazi's preface opened it: with the claim that Tiantai's calming and insight and Pure Land recitation of the Buddha's name are not two different paths, but one and the same practice seen from two sides.
