Discarding the Hindrances

Chapter 2 rebuked desire arriving from outside, through the senses. Chapter 3 turns inward: five hindrances that rise up in the mind itself once a practitioner has actually sat down to meditate, each covering over the originally pure mind the way smoke, dust, cloud, mist, or an eclipsing hand can each, in their own way, blot out the sun or moon.

Status: complete Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: book.bfnn.org Covers: the five hindrances — covetous desire, anger, drowsiness, restlessness and regret, doubt

The General Principle 總明棄蓋之要

Chinese Source

〖文〗所言棄蓋者，謂五蓋也。一、棄貪欲蓋。前說外五塵中生欲，今約內意根中生欲，謂行者端坐修禪，心生欲覺，念念相續，覆蓋善心，令不生長，覺已應棄。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: What is meant by "discarding the hindrances" is discarding the five hindrances. The first is discarding the hindrance of covetous desire. What was explained earlier concerned desire arising in the outward five sense-objects; what is meant here is desire arising within the inward mind-faculty itself — that is, while a practitioner sits upright cultivating dhyāna, a thought of desire arises in the mind, one thought following another without a break, covering over the wholesome mind and keeping it from growing. Once this is recognized, it should be discarded.

〖講〗前來具五緣、訶五欲二章已竟，今第三章明棄五蓋。所言五蓋者，即貪欲、瞋恚、睡眠、掉悔、疑是也。因此五法，能蓋覆我人自性清淨心。前訶五欲，則外塵不入，今棄五蓋，則內心不起，如是則意地清淨，清淨則堪佇道器。一棄貪欲蓋：前者所說，乃於外五塵中生欲，謂五根對五塵而起貪著也，今者約內意根中生欲，即前塵落謝之影子，轉入意地之中，追緣過去，逆慮未來，雖不同五根之攀攬五塵，而內心中妄念叢生，因追緣前塵，則別生一種貪欲，故云內心中起欲。謂修行者，於正身端坐，修禪之時，所謂身體及手足，宛然安不動，宛若一道者，殊不知虛有其表，雖外具威儀，而內心中胡思亂想，東忖西度，或由可愛之聲而欲想，或追憶前人美貌端嚴而生愛染，如是思量分別，念念相續不斷，如瀑流水相似，前念後念，無有休息，幾不知自身端坐為何物，遂將本具之善心，永遠沈沒，不能生長，此實為修行者一大障礙，若覺悟之，即能滅除。古人云：不怕念起，只怕覺遲，須早覺悟，宜急棄之。

Commentary: The two chapters above, being provided with the five conditions and rebuking the five desires, are now complete; this third chapter explains discarding the five hindrances. What is meant by "the five hindrances" is precisely: covetous desire, anger, drowsiness, restlessness and regret, and doubt. These five things are able to cover over our own mind's inherently pure nature. Chapter 2's rebuking of the five desires kept the outward dust of the senses from entering; this chapter's discarding of the five hindrances keeps the inward mind itself from arising in this way — so that the ground of the mind becomes pure, and, once pure, is fit to be a vessel for the path.

First, discarding the hindrance of covetous desire: what was explained before concerned desire arising amid the outward five sense-objects, that is, the five faculties giving rise to craving attachment when they meet the five sense-objects. What is meant here is desire arising within the inward mind-faculty itself — that is, the mere afterimage of a sense-object already passed, now carried over into the ground of the mind, chasing after the past and anticipating the future. Though this differs from the five faculties directly grasping and clinging to the five sense-objects, idle thoughts still crowd up within the mind — and because one chases after an image of a sense-object already gone, a further, separate kind of covetous desire arises from this; hence it is said that desire arises within the mind itself.

This means: while a practitioner sits with body upright, cultivating dhyāna — body, hands, and feet all seemingly settled and unmoving, seeming every bit a genuine practitioner of the path — little do they know that this is merely an outward show; though outwardly composed and dignified, within the mind wild, disordered thoughts run this way and that — perhaps drawn into a fantasy by some pleasing sound once heard, or recalling someone's fine, dignified appearance from before and giving rise to attached longing. Thoughts of this kind, discriminating one thing from another, follow one after the next without a break, exactly like a cascading torrent, one thought after another, never resting, until one hardly even knows what this body sitting upright actually is anymore — and in this way, the wholesome mind one already possesses is permanently submerged, unable to grow. This is, in truth, a great obstruction for a practitioner; but once it is recognized, it can be dissolved. As the ancients said: "Do not fear a thought arising — fear only that recognizing it comes too late." One must recognize it early, and discard it at once.

Chinese Source

〖文〗所以者何？如術婆伽欲心內發，尚能燒身，況復心生欲火，而不燒諸善法。貪欲之人，去道甚遠。所以者何？欲為種種惱亂住處，若心著欲，無由近道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Why is this so? Just as, with Śūrpaka, when desire arose from within, it was still able to burn the body itself — how much more so when a fire of desire arises in the mind, is it not able to burn away every wholesome quality? A person given to covetous desire is very far indeed from the path. Why is this so? Because desire is the very dwelling-place of every kind of vexing disturbance; if the mind clings to desire, there is no way to draw near the path at all.

〖講〗貪欲妄想，其害至大。如術婆伽，乃印度一淫女子，彼欲心從內而發，尚且身被燒壞，況復心中自生欲火，而不能燒諸善法。當知貪欲之人，去道甚遠，蓋欲為種種煩惱擾亂所住之處，菩提道乃是種種清淨安穩所住之處，一穢一淨，一生死，一涅槃，彼此天淵迥殊，凡聖遼絕，故名去道甚遠，若心愛著貪欲，則無由進道。故偈有云：「貪欲甚似水，能沒般若津，欲行菩提道，蓋貪護真心。」足見欲蓋之有妨礙於道，不可不慎歟。

Commentary: Covetous desire and idle thought — the harm they do is very great indeed. Śūrpaka was a courtesan of India; when desire arose in her from within, her very body was burned and ruined by it — how much more, then, is a fire of desire arising within the mind able to burn away every wholesome quality? One should know that a person given to covetous desire is very far indeed from the path, for desire is the very dwelling-place of every kind of afflicted disturbance, while the bodhi-path is the dwelling-place of every kind of pure, settled peace. One is defiled, the other pure; one is birth-and-death, the other nirvāṇa — the two as far apart as heaven from earth, ordinary being from sage utterly remote — hence it is said that one is very far indeed from the path. If the mind clings in fondness to covetous desire, there is no way to make progress on the path at all. Hence a verse says: "Covetous desire is exactly like water, able to drown the very ford of prajñā; if you wish to walk the bodhi-path, guard your true mind against the cover of desire." This is enough to show how greatly the hindrance of desire obstructs the path — one cannot be too careful of it.

Chinese Source

〖文〗如除蓋偈說：
入道慚愧人　持缽福眾生　云何縱塵欲　沈沒於五情
已捨五欲樂　棄之而不顧　如何還欲得　如愚自食吐
諸欲求時苦　得時多怖畏　失時懷熱惱　一切無樂處
諸欲患如是　以何能捨之　得深禪定樂　即不為所欺

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: As a verse on discarding the hindrances says:
"One who enters the path with a sense of shame carries the alms-bowl to bring blessing to all beings — why, then, indulge the desire for the senses' dust, sinking down into the five feelings?
Having already renounced the pleasure of the five desires, casting it off without a backward glance — how could one wish to have it back again? This is like a fool eating their own vomit.
Every desire brings suffering while it is sought, brings much dread once it is obtained, and brings burning distress when it is lost — nowhere in it is there any true pleasure at all.
Such is the trouble every desire brings — by what means, then, can one cast it off? Attain the deep pleasure of dhyāna-concentration, and one will no longer be deceived by it."

〖講〗入道慚愧人句，正言其修行入道之者，應當欲生大慚愧，生怖畏，悲眾生之在迷，嘆生死之未了，務祈道業早就，應當去欲持缽，福利一切眾生，為眾生廣種福田，作三世諸佛標指。云何縱塵欲沈沒於五情：云何二字乃責備之辭，為何恣縱放情於五欲六塵。為修行者，於此五欲當棄捨之，全體放下絲毫不顧，如何還欲求之不得，當知此為迷中倍人，正如愚癡之人自食己吐唾，豈不可悲而可笑歟。諸欲求時苦，得時多怖畏，失時懷熱惱。視諸眾生，誰不為求五欲，而梯山航海，戴月披星，奔走往返，縱使求得，亦是多怖畏，多畏懼，待遺失之時，而復心懷憂惱。由此觀之，三界之內無一寸土為快樂之地，是故當知，諸欲患如是。然欲以何法而能捨之？無他，若能深得禪定之樂，即不為之所欺耳。

Commentary: The line "one who enters the path with a sense of shame" speaks precisely of one who takes up practice and enters the path: they ought to feel great shame over desire, feel dread of it, grieve for sentient beings still lost in delusion, lament that birth-and-death remains unresolved, and earnestly seek to bring their own practice to completion soon — casting desire aside and carrying the alms-bowl instead, bringing blessing to every sentient being, planting broad fields of merit on their behalf, becoming a landmark pointing the way for the buddhas of past, present, and future.

"Why, then, indulge the desire for the senses' dust, sinking down into the five feelings" — "why" here is a word of reproach: why indulge and give free rein to feeling amid the five desires and six sense-objects? For a practitioner, these five desires ought to be cast off entirely, set down completely without the slightest backward glance — how, then, could one wish to seek them back again? Know that this is a person doubly lost in delusion, exactly like a foolish person eating their own vomit — is this not both sorrowful and absurd at once?

"Every desire brings suffering while it is sought, brings much dread once it is obtained, and brings burning distress when it is lost" — look at sentient beings everywhere: who does not, in seeking the five desires, climb mountains and cross seas, travel by starlight and moonlight, rushing back and forth? And even once it is obtained, it brings much dread, much fear — and once it is lost, the mind is left holding distress and anxiety all over again. Seen in this light, there is not a single inch of ground within the three realms that is a place of true happiness — hence one should know that this is exactly the trouble every desire brings. By what method, then, can one cast it off? There is no other way: if one can attain the deep pleasure of dhyāna-concentration, one will no longer be deceived by it at all.

2. The Hindrance of Anger 瞋恚蓋

Chinese Source

〖文〗二棄瞋恚蓋。瞋是失佛法之根本，墜惡道之因緣，法樂之冤家，善心之大賊，種種惡口之府藏。是故行者於坐禪時，思惟此人現在惱我，及惱我親，讚歎我冤，思惟過去未來亦如是，是為九惱，故生瞋恨，瞋恨故生怨，以怨心生故，便起心惱彼。如是瞋恚覆心，故名為蓋，當急棄之，無令增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Second, discarding the hindrance of anger. Anger is the very root of losing the Buddhadharma, the cause and condition for falling into the evil destinies, the sworn enemy of the joy of the Dharma, the great thief of the wholesome mind, the storehouse of every kind of harsh, abusive speech. This is why, when a practitioner sits in meditation and reflects that this person now troubles me, or troubles those dear to me, or praises someone I hold as an enemy — and reflects that this has likewise held true in the past and will hold true in the future — this makes nine grounds for vexation, and so anger and resentment arise; from anger and resentment, enmity arises; and once the mind of enmity has arisen, one then gives rise to the thought of troubling that other person in turn. Anger of this kind covers over the mind — hence it is called a hindrance, and must be discarded at once, never allowed to grow.

〖講〗此是第二棄瞋恚蓋：此瞋火燒人，最為厲害。古人云：「一念瞋心起，百萬障門開。」又云：「瞋火焰焰燒盡功德之林。」又云：「起一念之瞋，能燒盡菩提之種。」足見瞋是失佛法之根本，墜惡道之因緣，是法樂之冤家，為善心之大賊，種種惡口罵詈，皆從此起，故云府藏。當知惡口罵詈，其過非常重大。古云：「一言以興邦，一言亦能喪邦。」口業之造罪，不可不慎。若行者，於坐禪時，當正念未彰之先，即自思維；或緣於過去違情之境上，而起瞋；或復緣於此人現在惱我，及惱我親戚，或讚歎己之冤家，此是現在三種瞋。過去亦有惱我，惱我親，及讚我冤家之三種。未來亦然，三世類推，即有九種，是為九惱。因緣九惱生瞋恨，由瞋恨而生怨，由怨而生煩惱，由是瞋恨繁起，冤怒叢生，遂將本有之真心蓋覆殆盡，故名為蓋。故行者當急棄之，無使令其增長。

Commentary: This is the second, discarding the hindrance of anger. This fire of anger burns a person — nothing is more severe. The ancients said: "Let a single thought of anger arise, and a hundred thousand gates of obstruction open at once." And again: "The blazing flame of anger burns away the whole forest of merit." And again: "Give rise to a single thought of anger, and the very seed of bodhi can be burned away entirely." This is enough to show that anger is the very root of losing the Buddhadharma, the cause and condition for falling into the evil destinies, the sworn enemy of the joy of the Dharma, the great thief of the wholesome mind — every kind of harsh, abusive speech arises from it, hence it is called its "storehouse." One should know that harsh, abusive speech carries an extremely grave fault. The ancients said: "A single word can raise up a kingdom; a single word can likewise bring one down." The offense created by verbal karma is not something to be careless about.

If, while a practitioner sits in meditation, before right mindfulness has yet become clear, they find themselves reflecting — perhaps recalling some past circumstance where their own feelings were crossed, and anger arises from it; or reflecting that this person now troubles me, or troubles my own relatives, or praises someone I hold as an enemy — these are the three present forms of anger. There are likewise three past forms — troubling me, troubling my relatives, and praising my enemy — and three future forms as well; extending across all three time periods gives nine in total, and these are the nine grounds for vexation. Through the condition of these nine grounds, anger and resentment arise; from anger and resentment, enmity arises; from enmity, further affliction arises — and so anger and resentment multiply, enmity and rage crowd up thick, until the true mind one already possesses is covered over almost entirely. Hence it is called a hindrance. A practitioner must discard it at once, and never let it grow.

Chinese Source

〖文〗如釋提婆那以偈問佛：
何物殺安樂　何物殺無憂　何物毒之根　吞滅一切善
佛以偈答言：
殺瞋則安樂　殺瞋則無憂　瞋為毒之根　瞋滅一切善
如是知已，當修慈忍以滅除之，令心清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: As Śakra, lord of the gods, once asked the Buddha in verse:
"What thing, killed, brings peace? What thing, killed, brings freedom from sorrow? What thing is the very root of poison, swallowing up and destroying every wholesome quality?"
And the Buddha answered in verse:
"Kill anger, and peace follows. Kill anger, and freedom from sorrow follows. Anger is the very root of poison; anger destroys every wholesome quality."
Knowing this, one should cultivate loving-kindness and patience to dissolve it away, so that the mind becomes pure.

〖講〗初問謂何物能殺安樂？何物能可殺無憂？安樂者，即涅槃，無憂者，即是菩提。所謂無上涅槃寂靜樂，無上菩提覺法樂。若能證到清淨涅槃，即能安隱快樂於常寂光中，遠離見思塵沙無明之憂惱。若殺去安樂，則獲不安樂，戕去無憂，即復有憂。此文乃反顯，當知不安樂，即是生死，有憂即是煩惱。何物為三毒之根？何物能吞滅一切諸善之法？佛即以偈答言，當知如來大慈，有求必應，正如洪鐘待扣，有扣則鳴，故即答曰：「殺瞋則安樂，殺瞋則無憂。」瞋即是煩惱，若能將瞋心除滅，即獲清淨涅槃，亦能遠離無憂，是故當知，瞋為毒之根，瞋能伏滅一切善。故古偈云：「瞋似心中火，能燒功德林，欲行菩提道，戒瞋護真心。」修行人，如是知已，當修慈忍以滅除之，令心清淨。前所言瞋恨雖多，不出三種：一非理瞋，二順理瞋，三諍論瞋。今言慈忍，亦有三種：若修生緣慈，即對治非理瞋；修法緣慈，對治順理瞋；若修無緣慈，則能對治諍論瞋，由修三慈之方便，則能遠離瞋蓋；瞋心既盡，何愁心不清淨，止觀之不就也。

Commentary: The opening question asks: what thing, killed, is able to bring peace? What thing, killed, is able to bring freedom from sorrow? "Peace" here is nirvāṇa; "freedom from sorrow" is bodhi — that is, the unsurpassed, still peace of nirvāṇa, and the unsurpassed joy of the awakened Dharma that is bodhi. If one can realize pure nirvāṇa, one thereby settles into peaceful happiness within the ever-still light, leaving far behind the distress of views, thought, dust-and-sand afflictions, and ignorance. Put the other way around: kill off peace, and one is left with an absence of peace; destroy freedom from sorrow, and sorrow returns once more — this passage speaks by negation, so that one knows an absence of peace is precisely birth-and-death, and the presence of sorrow is precisely affliction. What thing is the root of the three poisons? What thing is able to swallow up and destroy every wholesome dharma?

The Buddha then answers in verse — for the Tathāgata's own great compassion answers whatever is asked of it without fail, exactly like a great bell that rings the moment it is struck — and so answers: "Kill anger, and peace follows; kill anger, and freedom from sorrow follows." Anger is itself affliction; if one can dissolve away the mind of anger, one thereby attains pure nirvāṇa, and is likewise able to leave sorrow behind entirely. Hence one should know: anger is the very root of poison, and anger is able to subdue and destroy every wholesome quality. Hence an old verse says: "Anger is like a fire within the mind, able to burn away the whole forest of merit; if you wish to walk the bodhi-path, guard your true mind by abstaining from anger." A practitioner, knowing this, should cultivate loving-kindness and patience to dissolve it away, so that the mind becomes pure.

What was said above about anger and resentment, though it takes many forms, does not go beyond three: anger without any reasonable cause, anger with some reasonable cause, and anger arising from dispute and argument. What is now called "loving-kindness and patience" likewise has three forms: cultivating loving-kindness toward sentient beings directly counters anger without cause; cultivating loving-kindness grounded in the Dharma counters anger with cause; and cultivating loving-kindness with no object at all counters anger arising from dispute. Through cultivating these three skillful forms of loving-kindness, one is able to leave the hindrance of anger far behind — and once the mind of anger is exhausted, why should one worry that the mind will fail to become pure, or that calming and insight will fail to be accomplished?

3. The Hindrance of Drowsiness 睡眠蓋

Chinese Source

〖文〗三棄睡眠蓋。內心昏闇名為睡；五情闇蔽，放恣支節，委臥睡熟為眠。以是因緣，名為睡眠蓋，能破今世後世實樂法心，及後世生天及涅槃樂。如是惡法，最為不善。何以故？諸餘蓋情，覺故可除；睡眠如死，無所覺識，以不覺故，難可除滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Third, discarding the hindrance of drowsiness. Dullness within the mind is called "drowsiness"; the five senses grown dark and obscured, the limbs left slack, lying prone in deep sleep, is called "sleep." For this reason it is called the hindrance of drowsiness-and-sleep, able to destroy the mind's own true joy in the Dharma both in this life and in lives to come, as well as the joy of being born in a heaven or attaining nirvāṇa in a life to come. An unwholesome state of this kind is the most unwholesome of all. Why is this? Every other hindrance can be removed once it is recognized — but drowsiness is like death itself, with no awareness at all; and precisely because there is no awareness of it, it is very hard to remove.

〖講〗此第三棄睡眠蓋：如人端坐，自心不生貪欲，亦不瞋恚，但終日六識昏昏貪著睡眠，所謂終日於黑暗鬼窟中作活計。云何睡眠？謂內心昏闇，名為睡；五情闇蔽，將手足四肢，恣意放縱，所謂放浪形骸，恣意睡臥，名之為眠。以是因緣名為睡眠蓋。當知此睡眠蓋，不特昏昧，空過光陰而已！且能破今世後世真實法樂之心，亦能破後世生天，及涅槃等樂。當知睡眠惡法，最為不善，此蓋較諸餘蓋尤為惡劣。如貪等蓋，起覺即可以滅除，睡眠則昏暗無知，人事不省，猶如死人一般，絲毫無所覺醒。古人云：「熟眠如小死，切莫於睡眠因緣，空過一生。」故世尊訶阿那律陀尊者曰：「咄咄何為睡，螺螄蚌蛤類，一睡一千年，永不聞佛名。」

Commentary: This third is discarding the hindrance of drowsiness. This is the person who, sitting upright, gives rise to no covetous desire and no anger in their own mind, yet whose six consciousnesses spend the whole day dull and craving sleep — going about their business, so to speak, in a dark demon's cave the entire day.

What is "drowsiness-and-sleep"? Dullness within the mind is called "drowsiness"; the five senses grown dark and obscured, letting the hands, feet, and four limbs go entirely slack — one's whole bodily frame left to sprawl, sunk in unrestrained sleep — is called "sleep." For this reason it is called the hindrance of drowsiness-and-sleep. One should know that this hindrance does not merely leave one dull and pass the time emptily — it is also able to destroy the mind's own true joy in the Dharma both in this life and in lives to come, and destroy, too, the joy of a future birth in the heavens or of nirvāṇa itself. One should know that this unwholesome state is the most unwholesome of all, worse even than the other hindrances. A hindrance such as covetous desire can be dissolved the moment it is recognized — but drowsiness leaves one dark and unaware, oblivious to everything around one, exactly like a dead person, with not the slightest awakening at all. As the ancients said: "Deep sleep is like a small death — never let the mere circumstance of sleep pass an entire lifetime by in emptiness." It is also like standing between two blades in the midst of battle, in a state of utter terror — at such a time, how could one possibly lie down at ease and sleep?

This is why a practitioner should know that sleep is a great darkness in which nothing at all is seen, deceiving a person day after day, robbing them of their own clarity. Before a person falls asleep, every scene of the world stands present before them, utterly clear and distinct — but the moment sleep sets in, the whole earth goes dark, since sleep covers over one's own mind, so that nothing at all, neither subject nor object, is seen. With a loss as great as this, how can one sleep again?

Restlessness, though it takes many forms, does not go beyond three. First, bodily restlessness: the body given to wandering aimlessly east and west, roaming about with no purpose, and every kind of idle horseplay, or being unable to sit or stand settled — this is bodily restlessness. Second, verbal restlessness: as in singing loudly or humming tunes, arguing over rights and wrongs, delighting in useless chatter and laughter, worldly talk, and the like — these are the marks of verbal restlessness. Third, mental restlessness: the mind following its own feelings into laxity, indulging the mind's own whims, whether dwelling on some mental image of a dharma-object, or grasping at some scene from the past, and every kind of unwholesome discursive thought — all of this is called mental restlessness.

One should know that a person of restless mind is exactly like an intoxicated elephant with no hook to guide it — even without drink, an elephant is difficult enough to restrain, how much more so once intoxicated and without a hook at all: wild disorder and calamity need hardly be said. Because doubt covers over the mind, one is unable to attain a mind of faith with regard to the various dharmas — and lacking a mind of faith, one gains nothing at all within the Buddhadharma, exactly like a person who enters a mountain of treasure but, having no hands, is unable to take any of it. Now, the faults of doubt are many, and not every one of them obstructs concentration — but the doubt spoken of here is precisely the kind that obstructs concentration.

〖講〗上來四蓋已竟，此第五棄疑蓋。疑者疑疑惑惑，猶豫不決之謂。有此疑心，則於佛法不能生於正信，遂失如來無上知見，故此疑心，實為眾惡之本。當知此疑，非特為眾惡之本，且為萬善之先，如禪宗之看話頭，先欲人起疑情，則以此疑為寶，所謂大疑則大悟，小疑則小悟，不疑則不悟，是知此疑又為功之首。然今所言疑者，非禪宗之疑，乃猶豫不決之疑，故能蓋覆真心，退失信願，於諸佛之無上妙法中，不得生於信心，當知佛法大海，信為能入。華嚴經云：「信為道元功德母，長養一切諸善根。」若無信心，則於佛法之中，空無所獲。欲研究佛學深邃佛理，非從信入手不可，故云：「天雨雖多，豈能潤無根之樹，佛門廣大，亦難度不信之人。」無信之人，猶如無根之樹，佛海廣大，如天雨之過多，以廣大法雨，難潤無信根之人，縱經塵劫，亦無利益。譬如有人，入於寶山，往取珠寶，若無其手，終無能取。何以故？以其無有手故。當知無信心者，亦復如是。手即喻信心，寶山即喻佛法寶藏，寶藏者何？即所謂三明六通、四諦六度、三十七道品、十力四無所畏、十八不共等法，悉皆如來寶藏。若無信心，則於佛海中，難得實益。然則二字，乃轉語之辭，謂疑道繁多，未必悉障於禪定，此簡非今所講之疑蓋，乃障定之疑蓋也。

Commentary: The four hindrances above are now complete; this fifth is discarding the hindrance of doubt. "Doubt" means a state of doubtful confusion, unable to decide either way. With this doubting mind present, one is unable to give rise to correct faith with regard to the Buddhadharma, and so loses the Tathāgata's own unsurpassed knowledge and vision — hence this doubting mind is, in truth, the root of every evil.

One should know, however, that this doubt is not only the root of every evil, but is also, in another sense, the very forerunner of every good — as in the Chan tradition's own practice of watching the meditation topic, where one is first made to give rise to a state of doubt, and this very doubt is prized as a treasure: as it is said, "great doubt brings great awakening, small doubt brings small awakening, no doubt brings no awakening at all" — so that this doubt is, in that context, the very head of the whole undertaking. But the doubt spoken of here is not the Chan tradition's own kind of doubt; it is, rather, the doubt of being unable to decide either way, which is able to cover over the true mind, and cause one to retreat from faith and aspiration, unable to give rise to a mind of faith within the buddhas' own unsurpassed, wondrous Dharma.

One should know that faith is what enables one to enter the great ocean of the Buddhadharma at all. The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "Faith is the very source of the path, the mother of merit, nourishing and raising up every wholesome root." Without a mind of faith, one gains nothing at all within the Buddhadharma. To study the profound principles of Buddhist teaching, one has no choice but to begin from faith — hence it is said: "However abundant the rain from heaven, how can it moisten a rootless tree? However vast the gate of the Buddhadharma, it is hard indeed to bring across a person without faith." A person without faith is exactly like a rootless tree; the ocean of the Buddhadharma is vast, like an abundance of rain from heaven — but this vast rain of the Dharma cannot moisten a person with no root of faith, even should measureless kalpas pass, to no benefit at all.

It is like a person who enters a mountain of treasure to gather jewels, but, having no hands, is in the end unable to take any of it. Why is this? Because they have no hands. One should know that a person without a mind of faith is exactly the same. The hand here stands for faith; the mountain of treasure stands for the treasury of the Buddhadharma. And what is this treasury? It is the three insights and six supernormal powers, the Four Noble Truths and six pāramitās, the thirty-seven factors of the path, the ten powers and four fearlessnesses, the eighteen distinctive qualities, and every such dharma — all of it the Tathāgata's own treasury. Without a mind of faith, one finds it hard to gain any real benefit at all within this ocean of the Buddhadharma.

"Now" signals a shift: doubt, as a path, takes many forms, and not every one of them obstructs dhyāna-concentration — this qualifies the discussion, distinguishing the doubt now being discussed, the hindrance of doubt that specifically obstructs concentration.

Three Kinds of Doubt 三種疑

Chinese Source

〖文〗疑者有三種：一者疑自，而作是念，我諸根闇鈍，罪垢深重，非其人乎？自作此疑，定法終不得發。若欲修定，勿當自輕，以宿世善根難測故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Doubt is of three kinds. The first is doubting oneself, thinking to oneself: "My own faculties are dull, my offenses and defilements grave — surely I am not the right kind of person for this?" Giving rise to a doubt of this kind, the dharma of concentration will, in the end, never open up. If one wishes to cultivate concentration, one must not belittle oneself in this way, since the depth of one's own wholesome roots from past lives is impossible to fathom.

〖講〗疑有三種不同：一者疑自，謂行人端坐修觀時，心中便作如是念，我今善根微薄，罪障深重，必非道器，何敢仰攀上乘，修斯道法耶？自心若作如是疑想，則禪定之法，終不得開發。若修定時，切不可自暴自棄，須知吾人從無量劫來，何曾不發過大心，不修過大行，決已早種善根，況且若無善根，今何獲此人身。當知一切聖賢，皆由凡夫做，十方諸佛，亦從眾生成，決無天然彌勒，自然釋迦，自當信己之宿世善根深厚，勿可自輕，皆是長者之真子，正好從此發大勇猛，進修勝法，何愁佛果之不證，涅槃之不得也。

Commentary: Doubt differs into three kinds. The first is doubting oneself: while a practitioner sits upright cultivating contemplation, a thought like this arises in the mind: "My own wholesome roots are so slight, my karmic obstructions so grave — surely I am not fit to be a vessel for the path at all — how dare I aspire to the Great Vehicle and cultivate a teaching such as this?" If a thought of doubt like this arises in one's own mind, the dharma of dhyāna-concentration will, in the end, never open up. When cultivating concentration, one must, above all, never give way to self-abandonment — one should know that across measureless kalpas, when have we ourselves never once given rise to a great resolve, never once cultivated a great practice? Wholesome roots have certainly already been planted long ago — and besides, without any wholesome roots at all, how could one have obtained this human body now?

One should know that every sage and worthy became so from an ordinary being; every buddha of the ten directions likewise became a buddha from being a sentient being — there is no such thing as a Maitreya who was simply born a buddha, or a Śākyamuni who became so of himself, without cause. One ought, then, to trust that one's own wholesome roots from past lives already run deep, and never belittle oneself — every one of us is a true child of the Elder, and this is exactly the moment to give rise to great courage from here, and press forward in cultivating this excellent teaching — why, then, worry that the fruit of buddhahood will not be realized, or that nirvāṇa will not be attained?

〖文〗二者疑師，彼人威儀相貌如是，自尚無道，何能教我？作是疑慢，即為障定。欲除之法，如摩訶衍論中說。如臭皮囊中金。以貪金故。不可棄其臭囊。行者亦爾。師雖不清淨。亦應生佛想。

Root Text: The second is doubting the teacher: "That person's own bearing and appearance are just so-so — they themselves have no genuine attainment of the path, so how could they possibly teach me?" Giving rise to a doubt and disdain of this kind is itself an obstruction to concentration. As for the method of removing it, the Mahāyāna treatise explains it thus: it is like gold within a foul-smelling sack — out of desire for the gold, one does not discard the foul sack because of it. A practitioner should be exactly the same: even if the teacher is not himself pure, one should still give rise to the thought of a buddha toward him.

〖講〗此第二疑師，即是疑他。謂疑我平素間，所親近之大德尊宿、善知識，觀其威儀，動靜相貌，與平常人無異，無有一絲毫過人之處，由此觀之，自尚無道，何能教我，須己欲立而立人，既無過人之處，而欲教化於人，恐怕以盲引眾盲，相牽落火坑，我若事奉之，終必有害無益。若作如是疑慢在心，即為障於禪定，禪定不開，般若之門為之深閉矣！若欲卻去疑師之法，如摩訶衍大論中說：「譬如臭皮囊中金，不可因厭臭皮囊，而棄於真金；若愛於金，則不可棄其皮囊。」行者亦復如是，師則例之臭皮囊，凡所說之法，即喻於金，切不可因人而棄其法，謂依法不依人也，否則妄生批評，橫論長短，是則名大愚且狂之輩。汝豈不聞，古聖賢之求法於人耶？如雪山大士，從鬼以請法，帝釋天主，拜畜而為師，此實依法不依人之一證也！須知師雖不清淨，亦應當生一種諸佛之想，因彼雖凡身，而口所言者，乃諸佛之法，諸佛之行，當以觀佛之心，而視師家，須翹勤懇切，聞法如對佛面。故云：「師雖不清淨，亦應生佛想。」

Commentary: This second, doubting the teacher, is doubting another person. It means doubting a great virtuous elder or good spiritual friend one has ordinarily kept close to, observing their bearing, their movements, and their appearance, and finding them no different from an ordinary person, with not the slightest quality that surpasses others — and thinking, from this, "they themselves have no genuine attainment of the path, so how could they possibly teach me? One must first establish oneself before one can establish another — since they show nothing that surpasses others, and yet wish to teach and transform others, I fear this is a case of one blind person leading a crowd of blind people, pulling each other down into a pit of fire together; if I serve this person, in the end it can only bring harm and no benefit." If a doubt and disdain of this kind takes hold in the mind, it becomes an obstruction to dhyāna-concentration; and once concentration fails to open up, the gate of prajñā is thereby shut fast.

As for the method of driving away this doubt of the teacher, the great Mahāyāna treatise explains it thus: "It is like gold within a foul-smelling sack — one does not, out of loathing for the foul sack, discard the genuine gold along with it; and if one truly desires the gold, one does not discard the sack either." A practitioner should be exactly the same: the teacher corresponds to the foul-smelling sack, and whatever teaching he expounds corresponds to the gold — one must never, because of the person, discard the teaching itself; that is, one relies on the Dharma, not on the person. Otherwise, giving rise to idle criticism, arguing over this fault and that at random, is precisely what is called the conduct of a greatly foolish and reckless person.

Have you not heard how the sages and worthies of old sought the Dharma from others? Just as the Great Snow-Mountain Ascetic sought the teaching from a demon, and Śakra, lord of the gods, bowed to an animal and took it as his own teacher — this is, in truth, proof of relying on the Dharma and not on the person! One must understand that even if the teacher is not himself pure, one should still give rise to a certain thought of the buddhas toward him — for although he himself is an ordinary being, what his mouth speaks is precisely the buddhas' own Dharma, the buddhas' own conduct. One should regard the teacher with the very same mind with which one contemplates a buddha, listening to the teaching with earnest, respectful sincerity, as though facing the Buddha himself. Hence it is said: "Even if the teacher is not himself pure, one should still give rise to the thought of a buddha toward him."

Root Text: The third is doubting the teaching. People of the world mostly cling to their own original assumptions, and are unable to place immediate faith in a teaching once received, receiving and practicing it with a reverent mind. If hesitant doubt arises in the mind instead, the teaching cannot color the mind at all. Why is this? The meaning of this obstruction of doubt is as a verse says:
"Like a person standing at a fork in the road, doubtful and confused, with nowhere to go — so too is doubt amid the true mark of every dharma.
Because of doubt, one fails to diligently seek out the true mark of every dharma; the view of doubt is born from delusion, the very worst among evils.
Amid what is wholesome and what is not, amid birth-and-death and nirvāṇa, there is, with certainty, a real dharma present — give rise to no doubt in the midst of it.
If you go on harboring doubt and confusion, you will be bound by the king of death and his gaoler-officers, like a deer seized by a lion — with no way to escape.
While still in the world, though doubt may arise, one should rejoice in wholesome dharmas all the same — just as, surveying a fork in the road, one should follow whichever path is truly favorable."

〖講〗所言疑法者，即疑己所稟受修持之法。世間之人，大多數執著先心為是，於後所受之法，而不能生信。如修止觀，則疑此止觀法門，是真耶？是偽耶？修之是否能令離苦耶？不能離苦耶？又如念佛之人，則疑念佛法門，是否能生西方？見阿彌陀佛？或疑此法，是魔羅所說？抑如來親口所宣？若於法生疑，則時時改途易轍，朝三暮四，疑惑癡闇，覆被真心。所謂坐佛海邊而渴死，豈非庸人之輩乎？如是即法不染心，永失如來無上涅槃。譬如有人在於歧路；歧路即三叉之路也。路多則不知何所往，疑惑必定無所趣向。諸法實相中，疑亦復如是。諸法即指一切大小凡聖偏圓頓漸權實等諸法；言實相者，即無相也，亦即所謂真如實相，當知實相無相，即空義；實相無不相，即假義也；實相無相不相，不相無相，即中義也。須知一切法，不出空假中，空假中不離一切法，謂一切法真如，一切法實相，故云：諸法如實相。若於諸法實相生疑，則措手無從，然而如來所說，八萬四千法門，一一皆入於實相，無非真如圓通法門，以其佛法如大海，無量無邊。倘生疑惑，即不得入，故云疑亦復如是。又云：疑故不勤求諸法之實相，當知一切見愛疑惑，悉從愚癡顛倒而生，謂於一切惡中又生諸惡，於是從迷至迷，惡上起惡，故云：惡中之惡者，善不善法中，生死及涅槃：善法即涅槃法，不善法，即生死法，生死乃是迷法，涅槃乃是悟法，然一切諸法之中，必定實實在在有真正之法在，所謂生死中有涅槃，煩惱中有菩提，生滅中有不生滅，波水中有溼性，且不可於中兩生疑心。汝若常懷疑惑，蓋覆真性，諸如斯輩，則為閻羅死王之所指揮，受獄卒差吏所繫縛！又如猛獅子之搏麋鹿，欲求其解脫，無有是處。吾人自無始至於今日，疑惑之根深厚，欲求勿疑，實為甚難。但在世雖有疑，常當隨喜善法，切不可以因疑惑之因緣，而喪失般若之妙法，譬如於歧道，十字街前，岔路甚多，究竟以誰為是，以誰為非，務須自具擇法之力，惟以利好者行之，謂自要於己有好處者，則無庸疑惑，即勇猛精進前行可也。因佛教法門雖多，窮其究竟之處，無非歸於一實，所謂方便有多門，歸元無二路，貴在惟專惟勤，一門深入，入一無妄不起思量分別，驀直行去，自有到家之一日。故佛法之中，信為能入，智為能度，倘若無有信心，雖在佛法之中，而與佛法不相應，不能得佛法之真實利益，故云空無所獲。既有如是等種種因緣，須覺知疑悔之過患，急當去之。

Commentary: What is meant by "doubting the teaching" is doubting the very teaching one has received and taken up in practice. Most people of the world cling to their own first assumptions as correct, and cannot give rise to faith in a teaching received afterward. As with cultivating calming and insight — one doubts whether this dharma-gate of calming and insight is genuine or false, whether cultivating it can actually free one from suffering or not. Or, as with one who recites the Buddha's name — one doubts whether this dharma-gate of reciting the Buddha's name can actually bring about rebirth in the Western Land, or a vision of Amitābha Buddha, or doubts whether this teaching was spoken by Māra himself, or genuinely proclaimed by the Tathāgata's own mouth. If doubt arises with regard to the teaching itself, one constantly changes course, fickle from morning to evening, doubt and delusion covering over the true mind. This is what is meant by "sitting at the very edge of the Buddha's own ocean, and dying of thirst" — is this not the conduct of a truly common, unremarkable person? In this way the teaching fails to color the mind at all, and one permanently loses the Tathāgata's own unsurpassed nirvāṇa.

"Like a person standing at a fork in the road" — a fork is a three-way junction; with many roads before one, one does not know which way to go, and doubtful confusion inevitably leaves one with nowhere to head toward. Doubt amid the true mark of every dharma is exactly the same. "Every dharma" refers to every teaching, great and small, ordinary and sagely, partial and perfect, gradual and sudden, provisional and real. "True mark" means the markless — that is, suchness, true reality itself. One should know: true reality having no mark is the meaning of emptiness; true reality having no mark that is not also a mark is the meaning of the conventional; true reality being neither marked nor unmarked, neither unmarked nor marked, is the meaning of the middle. One must understand that every dharma does not go beyond emptiness, the conventional, and the middle, and that emptiness, the conventional, and the middle are themselves never separate from any dharma — that is, every dharma is suchness, every dharma is true reality; hence it is said, every dharma is exactly as true reality itself. If doubt arises with regard to the true mark of every dharma, one has, quite literally, nowhere to put one's hand. Yet the eighty-four thousand dharma-gates the Tathāgata taught each, without exception, enter into this same true reality — nothing but gates of true suchness and perfect penetration, since the Buddhadharma is like a great ocean, measureless and boundless. Should doubtful confusion arise, one simply cannot enter it — hence it is said, "so too is doubt."

Again it says: "Because of doubt, one fails to diligently seek the true mark of every dharma." One should know that every view, craving, and doubtful confusion arises entirely from foolish delusion — that is, further evil arises out of evil already present, so that one passes from delusion into further delusion, evil arising on top of evil; hence it is said, "the very worst among evils." "Amid what is wholesome and what is not, amid birth-and-death and nirvāṇa" — the wholesome dharma is the dharma of nirvāṇa, the unwholesome dharma is the dharma of birth-and-death; birth-and-death is the dharma of delusion, nirvāṇa is the dharma of awakening — yet within every dharma without exception, there is, with utter certainty, a real, genuine dharma actually present: that is, within birth-and-death there is nirvāṇa, within affliction there is bodhi, within arising-and-ceasing there is the unarisen and unceasing, within the wave there is the water's own wetness — and one must not, in the midst of this, give rise to doubt on either side.

If you constantly harbor doubtful confusion, covering over your own true nature, then a person of this kind will be commanded by Yama, the king of death, and bound by his gaoler-officers — and, like a deer seized in the jaws of a fierce lion, seeking to be set free will be entirely futile. From beginningless time until this very day, the root of our own doubtful confusion runs deep, and to ask that no doubt arise at all is, in truth, extremely difficult. But even while doubt may still arise in the midst of worldly life, one should constantly rejoice in wholesome dharmas all the same, and never, because of the mere circumstance of doubtful confusion, let go of the wondrous Dharma of prajñā.

It is like standing before a crossroads with many forking paths — which, in the end, is the right one, and which the wrong one? One must, of necessity, possess the power to discern the Dharma for oneself, and simply follow whichever path is truly favorable — that is, whichever genuinely benefits oneself — with no need for doubtful hesitation at all, but advancing with courage and diligence instead. For although the Buddha's own teaching offers many gates, traced to their ultimate point every one of them returns to the one single reality — as it is said, "there are many gates of skillful means, but no second road back to the source" — what matters is single-minded diligence, entering deeply through one gate alone, entering the one without giving rise to any false discrimination, and pressing straight ahead; the day one arrives home will come of itself.

This is why, within the Buddhadharma, faith is what enables entry, and wisdom is what enables one to cross over — and if one lacks a mind of faith, then even while remaining within the Buddhadharma, one fails to accord with it at all, and is unable to gain any true benefit from it; hence it is said, "one gains nothing at all." Given causes and conditions of this kind, one must recognize the fault of doubt and regret, and cast it away at once.

Chinese Source

〖文〗問曰：不善法廣。塵數無量，何故但棄五法？答曰：此五蓋中，即具有三毒等分，四法為根本，亦得攝八萬四千諸塵勞門。（一）貪欲蓋，即貪毒。（二）瞋恚蓋，即瞋毒。（三）睡眠及疑，此二法是癡毒。（四）掉悔，即是等分攝。合為四分煩惱。一中有二萬一千，四中合為八萬四千，是故除此五蓋，即是除一切不善之法。行者如是等種種因緣，棄於五蓋。

One division holds twenty-one thousand, and the four divisions together hold eighty-four thousand — this is why removing these five hindrances is precisely removing every unwholesome dharma there is. A practitioner, for causes and conditions such as these, discards the five hindrances.

〖講〗此乃假問答釋疑。或有問曰：不善之法很廣，其數無量無邊，何故惟獨但棄貪瞋癡等之五蓋法耶？答曰：此五蓋中即具有貪瞋癡之三毒及等分，以此四法而為根本故。貪即貪欲，瞋謂瞋恚，愚即愚癡，此三各有所屬，惟等分一種，則遍收前貪瞋癡三種煩惱，一時一念之中，即具有貪瞋癡，或貪而復瞋，或瞋而復癡。或三毒對峙齊起，故名為等分煩惱。惡法雖多，不出八萬四千。八萬四千，亦以貪瞋癡等分為根本，故云：亦得收攝八萬四千，諸塵勞門。所言八萬四千諸塵勞門者，塵謂染污為義，以其種種邪見煩惱，悉能染污自性清淨心，故名為塵。勞即勞役也，謂凡夫眾生，常被邪見煩惱之所勞役，曠劫輪迴，無有了期。然論其根本，不出乎貪瞋癡慢疑身邊戒見邪之十使，隨以一使為首，則餘九使為助，謂每一使中各具十使，遂成一百。約三世各有一百，則成為三百煩惱。而現在之一百，時促不論相助，仍是一百，於過去未來二世二百之中，又各以一使為首，九使而為其助，共成二千，合現在之一百，共成二千一百。又約貪瞋癡等分，四種眾生，各有二千一百，共成八千四百。又約四大六衰，地水火風即四大，色聲香味觸法即六衰，以其能衰損善法故，此十種各有八千四百，總成八萬四千塵勞煩惱也。然煩惱雖有八萬四千，而以五蓋收攝殆盡。何以言之，八萬四千煩惱，悉以貪瞋癡三毒而為根本。然三毒不出五蓋，五蓋即攝三毒，謂（一）貪欲蓋，即攝貪毒。（二）瞋恚蓋，即攝瞋毒。（三）睡眠蓋及疑蓋，此二法即為痴毒所收攝。所言三種皆名為毒者，毒謂鴆毒，飲之即能喪身失命。當知貪瞋癡之三種煩惱，能毒我人法身父母，唐喪其功德，故為三毒。第四掉悔，即是等分所收攝，因掉悔有內，有外，內中即心掉，外則身口掉，此掉遍於身口意七支，故為等分所攝，開之則五蓋，合之則為四分煩惱。每一分中具有二萬一千諸塵勞門。四分合共則有八萬四千，是故應知。若除棄此五蓋，即是除棄一切不善之法。行者因有如是等種種因緣，須急棄於五蓋不可忽略也。

Commentary: This is a constructed question and answer, resolving a point of doubt. Someone might ask: unwholesome dharmas are very vast, numberless and boundless — why, then, discard only these five hindrances of greed, anger, and delusion? The answer: these five hindrances already contain within them the three poisons of greed, anger, and delusion, together with the "equal-portion" affliction — these four things serving as the root of everything else. Greed is covetous desire; anger is ill will; delusion is foolishness — these three each have their own distinct sphere. The single "equal-portion" affliction, however, gathers up all three of these — greed, anger, and delusion together — so that within a single moment, a single thought, greed, anger, and delusion may all be present at once: greed followed by anger, anger followed by delusion, or all three poisons rising together in mutual confrontation — hence it is called the "equal-portion" affliction.

Unwholesome dharmas, however many, do not exceed eighty-four thousand in number, and these eighty-four thousand likewise take greed, anger, delusion, and the equal-portion affliction as their root — hence it is said that the five hindrances are also able to encompass all eighty-four thousand gates of dusty toil. As for "the eighty-four thousand gates of dusty toil": "dust" here carries the sense of defilement, since every kind of wrong view and affliction is able to defile one's own mind's inherently pure nature, hence it is called "dust." "Toil" means being pressed into labor — meaning that ordinary sentient beings are constantly pressed into labor by wrong views and afflictions, revolving through measureless kalpas with no end in sight.

Yet traced to their root, these do not exceed the ten proclivities of greed, anger, delusion, pride, doubt, the view of a fixed self, extreme views, clinging to precepts and rituals, [wrong] views, and deviant views. Taking any one of these ten as the leading factor, the remaining nine serve as its support, so that each single proclivity already contains all ten within it, making one hundred in total. Reckoned across the three time periods, each with its own hundred, this makes three hundred afflictions — though the present period's own hundred, being so brief, is not reckoned together with the others, and remains simply a hundred; while the past and future periods, two hundred between them, each likewise take one proclivity as leading factor with the other nine as support, making two thousand together, which, combined with the present period's hundred, comes to two thousand one hundred. Reckoned again across the four kinds of being distinguished by greed, anger, delusion, and the equal portion, each with its own two thousand one hundred, this comes to eight thousand four hundred. Reckoned once more across the four great elements and six forms of decay — earth, water, fire, and wind being the four great elements; form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and mental objects being the six forms of decay, since they are able to diminish wholesome dharmas — each of these ten categories carries its own eight thousand four hundred, coming altogether to eighty-four thousand forms of dusty, toiling affliction in all.

Yet although afflictions number eighty-four thousand, the five hindrances are able to encompass almost the whole of them. Why is this said? Because all eighty-four thousand afflictions take the three poisons of greed, anger, and delusion as their root, and the three poisons themselves do not exceed the five hindrances — the five hindrances already encompass the three poisons: (1) the hindrance of covetous desire encompasses the poison of greed; (2) the hindrance of anger encompasses the poison of anger; (3) the hindrances of drowsiness and doubt, these two, are encompassed within the poison of delusion. As for why all three are called "poisons": poison here means a lethal venom — drinking it is able to destroy the very body and take one's life. One should know that the three afflictions of greed, anger, and delusion are able to poison the very father and mother of our own Dharma-body, destroying its merit for nothing — hence they are called the three poisons. The fourth, restlessness-and-regret, is encompassed by the equal-portion affliction, since restlessness has both an inward and an outward form — inwardly, mental restlessness; outwardly, bodily and verbal restlessness — this restlessness pervading all seven aspects of body, speech, and mind together, hence it is encompassed by the equal-portion affliction. Opened out, these become the five hindrances; gathered together, they become four divisions of affliction, each division containing twenty-one thousand gates of dusty toil, the four divisions together making eighty-four thousand — this, then, is what should be understood. If these five hindrances are removed and discarded, this is precisely removing and discarding every unwholesome dharma there is. A practitioner, for causes and conditions such as these, must discard the five hindrances at once, and never treat the matter lightly.

Closing Similes 結歸喻

Chinese Source

〖文〗譬如負債得脫，重病得差，如饑餓之人得至豐國，如於惡賊中得自免濟，安隱無患。行者亦如是，除此五蓋，其心安隱，清涼快樂。如日月以五事覆翳，煙、塵、雲、霧、羅侯阿修羅手障，則不能明照。人心五蓋亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: It is like a debtor freed from debt, or a person gravely ill who has recovered; like a starving person who has reached a land of plenty; like one who has managed to free themselves from the midst of fierce bandits, settled and at peace, with nothing left to fear. A practitioner is exactly the same: once these five hindrances are removed, the mind becomes settled, cool, and joyful. It is like the sun and moon obscured by five things — smoke, dust, cloud, mist, and the eclipsing hand of the asura-king Rāhu — unable, in any of these five cases, to shine forth clearly. The five hindrances of the human mind are exactly the same.

〖講〗此借譬喻以明除五蓋，得益之相。行者，具有五蓋，即如負債之人，不得解脫，五蓋棄之，即得解脫自在。有五蓋者，如有重病相似，五蓋若棄，則重病得差。除五蓋者，又如飢餓之人，得至豐國，如於惡賊之中得自免濟，如是則其身其心，自在快樂，安隱無患。行者除卻五蓋，其心安隱，清涼快樂。若五蓋不除，安有清涼快樂之可談耶！須知五蓋之覆被自心，猶如五事之障蔽日月。言五事者，謂煙塵雲霧羅侯阿修羅手之五事。羅侯阿修羅王之手最大，能遮日月之光，餘如煙塵雲霧，亦能遮日月之光，不能明照大地。人心之五蓋，亦能障被自性清淨心不得顯現，此以喻顯也。

Commentary: This borrows a series of similes to make clear what is actually gained by removing the five hindrances. A practitioner who still carries the five hindrances is like a debtor unable to free themselves — and once the five hindrances are discarded, one attains freedom and ease, exactly as a debt is cleared. To carry the five hindrances is like carrying a grave illness — and once the five hindrances are discarded, the grave illness is cured. Removing the five hindrances is also like a starving person reaching a land of plenty, or like one who has managed to free themselves from the midst of fierce bandits — in either case, body and mind alike become free, joyful, settled, and at peace.

Once a practitioner has removed the five hindrances, the mind becomes settled, cool, and joyful. If the five hindrances are not removed, how could there be any coolness or joy to speak of at all! One must understand that the five hindrances cover over one's own mind exactly as five things obscure the sun and moon. These five things are smoke, dust, cloud, mist, and the hand of the asura-king Rāhu. The hand of the asura-king Rāhu is the greatest of the five, able to block the very light of the sun and moon; the rest — smoke, dust, cloud, and mist — are likewise each able to block the light of the sun and moon, so that it cannot shine clearly upon the earth below. The five hindrances of the human mind are able, in exactly the same way, to obstruct one's own mind's inherently pure nature from manifesting at all — this is what this simile makes clear.

Reading note: Baojing's closing move — mapping all eighty-four thousand traditionally enumerated afflictions onto just five hindrances via the three poisons plus one "equal-portion" catch-all — is worth reading alongside his identical move in chapter 2's turtle-and-jackal parable and chapter 1's ten methods of repentance: across this whole commentary, an apparently unwieldy, open-ended list turns out, on closer inspection, to have a small, memorizable structure underneath it. The "doubt" section is also worth noting for how carefully Baojing distinguishes the doubt this chapter warns against (paralyzing indecision) from the doubt Chan practice deliberately cultivates (the "great doubt" of working a meditation topic) — the same word covering two, in his telling, nearly opposite spiritual postures.
