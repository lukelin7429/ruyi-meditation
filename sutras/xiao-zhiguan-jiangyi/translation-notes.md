# Xiuxi Zhiguan Zuochan Fayao Jiangshu (Master Baojing's Commentary) Translation Notes

These notes record recurring translation decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of Master Baojing's (寶靜法師) *Xiūxí Zhǐguān Zuòchán Fǎyào Jiǎngshù* (修習止觀坐禪法要講述, "A Lecture Commentary on the Essentials of Practicing Calming and Insight for Sitting Meditation"). This is a vernacular commentary on Zhiyi's own short meditation manual, the *Tóngméng Zhǐguān* (童蒙止觀, "Calming and Insight for Beginners"), also known as the *Xiǎo Zhǐguān* (小止觀, "Lesser Calming and Insight"). See the companion notes at [`sutras/shi-chan-boluomi/translation-notes.md`](../shi-chan-boluomi/translation-notes.md) for the site's general approach to Zhiyi's meditation writings, and [`sutras/surangama-sutra-jiangyi/translation-notes.md`](../surangama-sutra-jiangyi/translation-notes.md) for the closest structural model — a modern master's clause-by-clause vernacular commentary on an earlier text, with no prior English translation of the commentary itself.

## Who Baojing was, and why this text

Master Baojing (寶靜, 1899–1940) was the forty-fourth-generation patriarch of the Tiantai lineage and the principal dharma-heir of Master Dixian (諦閑法師), the leading reviver of Tiantai teaching and monastic education in early-twentieth-century China. Baojing succeeded Dixian as abbot of Guanzong Monastery (觀宗寺) in Ningbo — the seat of Tiantai studies Dixian had rebuilt — and carried that work forward through his own founding of the Hongfa Research Society (弘法研究社) for training students in Tiantai doctrine and practice.

This commentary began as a series of oral lectures. In the autumn of 1934 (甲戌年秋), lay disciples Zeng Bishan (曾璧山) and Lü Biecheng (呂碧城) of the Xianghai Lotus Society (香海蓮社) invited Baojing to lecture on Zhiyi's *Tóngméng Zhǐguān*. Two of his students, Fazi (法慈) and Minzhi (敏智), took down the lectures; Fazi's own preface (dated to this same lecture series) records that the two recorders' draft was afterward submitted back to Baojing for correction before being edited into its final form and printed. Fazi's preface is explicit about the pairing this commentary keeps drawing on throughout: that Tiantai's teaching on calming and insight (止觀) and Pure Land recitation of the Buddha's name are "mutually entailing, not mutually separate" (相即而不相離) — a theme that recurs constantly in Baojing's own gloss of Zhiyi's text.

## Relation to Zhiyi's root text and to Dharmamitra's published translation

Zhiyi's own root text, the *Tóngméng Zhǐguān* (童蒙止觀, also titled *Xiūxí Zhǐguān Zuòchán Fǎyào* — the same eight-character title Baojing's commentary borrows and expounds), already has a complete, published English translation: Bhikshu Dharmamitra's *The Essentials of Buddhist Meditation* (Kalavinka Press, 2009). That translation covers only Zhiyi's own short root text. It does not include Baojing's commentary — a separate, much longer twentieth-century work that quotes Zhiyi's text passage by passage and then explains it clause by clause for a modern lay audience, weaving in his own Tiantai-and-Pure-Land synthesis, historical anecdotes, and pastoral asides that have no counterpart in Zhiyi's own spare original. As far as we are aware, no English translation of Baojing's commentary specifically exists; this is the unique contribution of this translation project.

Because Dharmamitra's translation of the root text is already published and well known, we take the same care here that the *Shì Chán Bōluómì Cìdì Fǎmén* project already takes with Zhiyi's other meditation writings: **every quoted passage of Zhiyi's root text is translated independently from the Chinese**, without consulting, echoing, or drifting toward Dharmamitra's existing wording, sentence structure, or section framing. Baojing's own commentary prose is, in any case, an entirely separate composition with no existing English rendering to avoid.

## The root text embedded in the commentary

Baojing's method, like Yuanying's in the *Léngyán Jīng Jiǎngyì*, quotes a passage of Zhiyi's root text and then explains it clause by clause. We follow the same inline convention already established for that project (itself following the convention used for the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra*):

- Chinese source: bold `〖文〗` for a quoted passage of Zhiyi's root text, bold `〖講〗` for Baojing's own commentary.
- English translation: **Root Text:** for the quoted passage, **Commentary:** for Baojing's explanation.

We use `〖文〗` / **Root Text:** rather than `〖經〗` / **Sūtra:** because Zhiyi's *Tóngméng Zhǐguān* is not itself a sūtra (a discourse attributed to the Buddha) but a treatise (論) — the same distinction already kept for the *Shì Chán Bōluómì* project. Where Baojing quotes a passage that overlaps with material already translated in the *Shì Chán Bōluómì* project (both being Zhiyi's own writing on dhyāna), we do not automatically reuse that project's wording — the *Xiǎo Zhǐguān* and the *Shì Chán Bōluómì Cìdì Fǎmén* are distinct compositions in Zhiyi's own hand, addressed to different audiences (the *Xiǎo Zhǐguān* explicitly for beginners, per its own subtitle 童蒙), and we translate each passage fresh in its own context unless the underlying Chinese is truly identical.

## Structure

Zhiyi's root text opens with a short prologue (quoting "commit no evil, do every good, purify one's own mind" as the whole of the Buddha's teaching, then explaining why calming and insight are the most direct gate into it) and a listing of its own ten-topic outline, before the ten chapters proper begin. We treat this prologue material as front matter — summarized in English on this project's index page and in Baojing's biography above, rather than translated in full on a chapter page — and begin each chapter page's translation at the chapter's own opening line, matching how a reader of the Chinese text would actually navigate to that chapter.

The ten chapters (十意/十章), in Zhiyi's own order:

1. 具緣第一 — Being Provided with the Conditions: the five prerequisite conditions for practice (pure precept-observance, sufficient clothing and food, a quiet dwelling place, setting worldly affairs aside, staying close to good spiritual friends).
2. 訶欲第二 — Rebuking the Five Desires: turning away from the five sense-desires (form, sound, smell, taste, touch).
3. 棄蓋第三 — Discarding the Hindrances: setting aside the five hindrances (desire, ill will, drowsiness, restlessness and regret, doubt).
4. 調和第四 — Regulation: harmonizing food, sleep, body, breath, and mind.
5. 方便行第五 — Skillful Means: the five conditions proper to the method itself (aspiration, mindfulness, diligence, discernment, singleness of mind).
6. 正修行第六 — Actual Practice: practice while seated, and practice carried into every activity and circumstance.
7. 善根發第七 — The Arising of Wholesome Roots: recognizing genuine signs of progress as they appear.
8. 覺知魔事第八 — Awareness of Demonic Affairs: recognizing and not being deceived by obstructive states.
9. 治病第九 — Curing Illness: diagnosing and treating illness that arises from or obstructs practice.
10. 證果第十 — The Fruit of Realization: what is actually attained.

We keep this ten-chapter structure as the site's own page structure — one page per chapter — rather than subdividing further, since each chapter (unlike the multi-fascicle *Léngyán Jīng Jiǎngyì*) is short enough to read in one sitting even at full commentary length.

## Core terminology

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 止觀 | calming and insight (śamatha-vipaśyanā) | The treatise's central pair of terms. 止 ("calming," śamatha) and 觀 ("insight," vipaśyanā) are kept as a fixed pair throughout, spelled out with the Sanskrit on first use per chapter, then in plain English afterward — matching this site's general preference for accessible English over dense transliteration where a clear rendering exists. |
| 童蒙止觀 / 小止觀 | the *Tóngméng Zhǐguān* / the *Xiǎo Zhǐguān* | Zhiyi's own root text under commentary here — left in transliteration as a title, per this site's convention for named works (e.g. the *Shì Chán Bōluómì Cìdì Fǎmén*), rather than translated inline as "the Lesser Calming and Insight" each time it is named. |
| 具緣 | the [five] conditions | Chapter 1's own term for its five prerequisites; see Structure above for the five. |
| 善知識 | a good spiritual friend | kalyāṇamitra. Baojing distinguishes three kinds in chapter 1: the protecting friend (外護, who provides material support), the fellow-practitioner (同行, who practices alongside one and keeps one accountable), and the teaching friend (教授, who actually instructs). Keep this threefold distinction fixed when it recurs. |
| 頭陀 | dhutaṅga, ascetic practice | Transliterated per common English Buddhist usage; Baojing's chapter 1 discussion covers the traditional twelve dhutaṅga practices grouped under food, dwelling, and robes. |
| 三品持戒 | the three grades of precept-observance | Chapter 1's threefold grading of practitioners by how they hold precepts: upper grade (never broken, from before ordination onward), middle grade (unbroken major precepts, minor lapses sincerely repented), lower grade (even major precepts broken, requiring Mahāyāna-style repentance since Hīnayāna teaching holds the four root offenses unrepentable). Keep this threefold grading fixed if it recurs in later chapters. |
| 波羅夷 | pārājika | The four root monastic offenses (killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false claims of spiritual attainment) — transliterated per standard English Buddhist-studies usage. |
| 事懺 / 理懺 | repentance through ritual form / repentance through principle | A recurring pair in chapter 1's discussion of confession: 事懺 (also called 作法懺, "repentance according to a set method") is repentance carried out through actual ritual observance — bathing, incense, prostration, a fixed period of practice; 理懺 (also called 無相懺, "signless repentance" or 實相懺, "repentance through the true mark of reality") is repentance through directly contemplating that the offense, like the mind that gave rise to it, has no fixed, findable nature. Baojing is explicit that the two are not really separable — genuine ritual repentance already implicitly contains the contemplative kind, and vice versa — matching the 事/理 pairing already logged in the *Shì Chán Bōluómì* notes. |
| 尸羅 | śīla | Transliterated; glossed in-line as "precept-observance" where it appears as its own term (Baojing glosses the transliteration itself, e.g. "尸羅, meaning precept-observance," so both the transliteration and its plain-English sense appear together on first use). |
| 阿蘭若 | araṇya | A wilderness dwelling-place suited to practice — transliterated, matching the term's standing as a fixed technical term throughout Buddhist meditation literature, rather than translated case-by-case as "forest," "hermitage," or "quiet place." |
| 檀越 | a donor | dāna-pati — a lay patron who supports a practitioner materially, discussed in chapter 1's account of the lower grade of alms-food. |
| 邪命 | wrong livelihood | The four kinds of improper means of subsistence for a mendicant (named in chapter 1 by the direction implied in each: "mouth facing down," "mouth facing up," "mouth facing the four directions," "mouth facing everywhere at once" — farming/manual trades, astrology/weather-prediction, networking/travel for gain, and fortune-telling/medicine/physiognomy, respectively). Keep all four named individually when this term recurs. |
| 五欲 | the five desires | Chapter 2's own subject: desire for sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and touch (色/聲/香/味/觸) — the five sense-objects considered specifically as objects of craving. Kept as "desire for [sense]" per item (e.g. "desire for sights") rather than translating 色 as "form" in this context, since chapter 2 is concerned with craving, not the doctrinal category of rūpa. |
| 結使 | fetters | The afflictions that bind a being to saṃsāra, imaged in chapter 2 as a knotted rope; a dormant fetter can "rise up again" (起) when re-triggered by craving, as opposed to remaining subdued or "lying dormant" (臥). Keep "fetters" fixed rather than the more general "afflictions" wherever this specific term (結使, not simply 煩惱) is used. |
| 三途 | the three [evil] paths | Chapter 2's own etymological gloss for the three evil destinies (hell, animals, hungry ghosts), each named for its characteristic torment: the "fire path" (火途, hell, burned continuously), the "blood path" (血途, animals, devouring one another), and the "knife path" (刀途, hungry ghosts, beaten with blades and staves by ghost-kings). This is Baojing's own folk etymology for 三途, distinct from the more common doctrinal list (hell, hungry ghosts, animals) — keep the fire/blood/knife imagery attached to this specific gloss if it recurs. |
| 阿那波那 / 數息 | ānāpāna / counting the breath | Chapter 2's closing practice instruction: counting each breath from one to ten and back, gathering the six faculties inward. Transliterated (ānāpāna) on first use with the plain-English gloss "counting the breath" alongside it, then referred to in plain English afterward. |
| 頻婆娑羅王 / 優填王 | King Bimbisāra / King Udayana | Two kings cited in chapter 2's account of desire for form, both drawn from well-known canonical anecdotes: Bimbisāra's entering an enemy kingdom for the courtesan Āmrapālī, and Udayana's cutting off the hands and feet of five hundred ascetics out of jealousy. Keep these two accepted Sanskrit forms of their names rather than a bare pinyin transliteration. |
| 一角仙 | the One-Horned Sage | Ṛṣyaśṛṅga, cited in chapter 2's account of desire for touch: an ascetic who lost his spiritual powers after being seduced, and was made to be ridden on the neck by the courtesan responsible, cited as a warning about the severity of desire for touch specifically. |
| 五蓋 | the five hindrances | Chapter 3's own subject: covetous desire (貪欲蓋), anger (瞋恚蓋), drowsiness (睡眠蓋), restlessness and regret (掉悔蓋), and doubt (疑蓋) — inward mental states, as distinct from chapter 2's five desires arriving through the outward senses. Standard rendering of the five nīvaraṇa; keep "hindrances," not "obstructions" or "coverings," fixed throughout. |
| 九惱 | the nine grounds for vexation | Chapter 3's structure for how anger arises: three time periods (past, present, future) each crossed with three provocations (this person troubles me, troubles those dear to me, or praises my enemy) = nine. Distinct from, but structurally similar to, the twelve categories of birth and other fixed enumerations elsewhere on this site — keep the 3×3 structure explicit if cited again. |
| 掉 / 悔 | restlessness / regret | Chapter 3's paired hindrance (auddhatya-kaukṛtya). 掉 ("restlessness") is graded into bodily, verbal, and mental forms; 悔 ("regret") is, unlike the other four hindrances, not inherently a fault — Baojing is explicit that regret only becomes a hindrance when it is not resolved but instead lodges in the mind as ongoing anxiety. Keep the two terms distinguished even though they are treated as one compound hindrance. |
| 三種疑 | the three kinds of doubt | Chapter 3's own threefold breakdown of the hindrance of doubt: doubting oneself (疑自, that one's own capacity is too poor for practice), doubting the teacher (疑師, that an unimpressive-seeming teacher cannot have anything to teach), and doubting the teaching (疑法, that the teaching itself may be false or ineffective). Baojing is careful to distinguish this paralyzing doubt from the Chan tradition's own cultivated "great doubt" used in working a meditation topic — the same Chinese word (疑) names two different things. |
| 三毒 / 等分 | the three poisons / the equal-portion affliction | Chapter 3's closing framework mapping the five hindrances onto the standard three poisons (greed, anger, delusion) plus a fourth category, the "equal-portion" affliction, which covers restlessness-and-regret precisely because it does not belong to any single poison alone but partakes of all three at once. Used to explain how the five hindrances are said to encompass all eighty-four thousand afflictions. |
| 調和 | regulation | Chapter 4's own subject and title: bringing five things (food, sleep, body, breath, mind) into proper balance, illustrated by a potter working clay and a lutenist tuning strings — neither too slack nor too tense in every case. Kept as "regulation" / "regulating," not "harmonizing," as the primary rendering, though "bringing into balance" is used freely in surrounding prose for readability. |
| 風 / 喘 / 氣 / 息 | wind / panting / coarse breath / true respiration | Chapter 4's own fourfold classification of the breath during seated practice — the first three unregulated (audible, catching, or merely unobstructed-but-not-fine), the fourth the actual goal state (soundless, uncatching, fine, continuous, "as though barely there and yet never quite gone"). Keep all four terms distinct; do not collapse to a generic "breath" or "breathing." |
| 沈 / 浮 | sinking / floating | Chapter 4's pair for an unregulated mind during seated practice: sinking is dullness and drowsiness (remedied by fixing attention on the tip of the nose), floating is agitation and distraction (remedied by settling attention at the navel). Distinct from, but structurally parallel to, chapter 3's 掉 (restlessness) and the drowsiness hindrance — chapter 4 treats these as ordinary technical adjustments during a sitting, not moral hindrances to be "discarded." |
| 寬 / 急 | slack / tense | Chapter 4's second pair, applied to both body and mind: a body sat too loosely (寬) versus held too rigidly (急); a mind gathered too laxly versus gripped too tightly (producing, per Baojing, an actual physical ache in the chest). Kept as "slack" and "tense" consistently for both the bodily and mental application of this pair. |
| 入 / 住 / 出 | entering / dwelling / leaving | Chapter 4's own threefold structure for a single sitting, applied in turn to body, breath, and mind: the beginning of a sitting (moving from coarse to fine), the sustained middle of a sitting (correcting whichever of the three drifts out of balance), and the end of a sitting (moving back from fine to coarse, with its own physical winding-down procedure to avoid the "wind-affliction" of leaving too abruptly). This same 入/住/出 structure recurs in chapter 4's closing Lotus Sūtra citation and should be expected again in later chapters' own accounts of practice. |
| 五法 | the five skillful means | Chapter 5's own subject: aspiration (欲), diligence (精進), mindfulness (念), discerning wisdom (巧慧), and singleness of mind (一心) — the last of the five preparatory chapters, closing the text's first half before chapter 6 turns to actual practice. Distinct from chapter 1's five conditions (具緣) and chapter 4's five things to regulate (調和); keep all three fives distinguished by name when cross-referencing. |
| 非智不禪，非禪不智 | no dhyāna without wisdom, no wisdom without dhyāna | Chapter 5's closing formula for the non-duality of calming (止) and insight (觀) themselves, glossed by Baojing as concentration (定) and wisdom (慧) requiring each other reciprocally. Watch for this same formula, or a close paraphrase of it, recurring at other structurally significant closing points in the text — it echoes the title's own five-fold exegesis in the front matter. |
| 坐中修 / 歷緣對境修 | practice while seated / practice carried into every activity and circumstance | Chapter 6's own two-part structure, its central division: five approaches usable only while actually sitting, versus twelve more (six activities × six sense-faculties) for a practitioner whose "encumbered body must inevitably engage in worldly affairs." Keep this pairing distinguished — later chapters may refer back to either half specifically. |
| 三止 | the three kinds of calming | Chapter 6's own threefold breakdown of 止 (calming), used within the first of the five seated approaches: calming through fixing the mind and guarding an object (繫緣守境止, e.g. on the tip of the nose), calming through restraining the mind (制心止, catching and setting down each thought as it arises), and calming through embodying true reality (體真止, seeing that every dharma arises from causes and conditions and so has no fixed nature). The first two are called "matters of outward technique" (事相); the third is the deeper, principle-based form. Distinct from the chapter 3 sense of "discarding" — these are active meditative techniques, not moral renunciations. |
| 對治觀 / 正觀 | the counteracting contemplation / correct contemplation | Chapter 6's own twofold breakdown of 觀 (insight): the counteracting contemplation (also called the supporting contemplation, 助觀) matches a specific antidote to a specific predominant affliction — the contemplation of impurity (不淨觀, esp. the ninefold contemplation of a corpse's decay) for covetous desire, the contemplation of loving-kindness (慈心觀) for anger, the contemplation of distinguishing the elements (界分別觀) for attachment to a self, and the contemplation of counting the breath (數息觀, ānāpāna) for discursive scattered thought; correct contemplation (正觀) is the direct contemplation of every dharma as empty, conventional, and the middle at once — the three truths (三諦) doctrine central to Tiantai teaching. |
| 六緣 | the six activities | Chapter 6's own list for practice carried into daily life: walking (行), standing (住), sitting (坐), lying down (臥), working (作), and speaking (語) — each treated with the same paired structure (a check on motive before the activity, then a calming and an insight practice within it). Distinct from, but paired with, 六根 (the six faculties) below — together the "twelve matters" (十二事) of this chapter's second half. |
| 六根門中修止觀 | cultivating calming and insight through the gate of the six faculties | Chapter 6's parallel list to the six activities: the eye seeing form, the ear hearing sound, the nose smelling scent, the tongue tasting flavor, the body feeling touch, and the mind knowing mental objects — each with its own worked story (Aniruddha regaining sight as the heavenly eye, Pilindavatsa and the thorn, Bhadrapāla and the bathhouse water) illustrating calming and insight applied to that specific sense. Closes with the doctrine of the six faculties' interchangeable use (六根互用) — the same six names (Aniruddha, Upananda, Gaṅgā, Gavāṃpati, Śūnya, Mahākāśyapa) already logged in the companion Śūraṅgama Sūtra Jiǎngyì project's glossary for this same list; keep the same six names and pairings when this recurs. |
| 善根 | wholesome root | Chapter 7's own subject: latent karmic potential from past-life cultivation, stirred back into activity by present practice, not created fresh by it. Chapter 7 names five kinds specifically (息道 the breath-method, 不淨觀 the contemplation of impurity, 慈心 loving-kindness, 因緣觀 the contemplation of dependent origination, 念佛 buddha-recollection) — distinct from the "outward" wholesome roots of chapter 7's own opening (generosity, precept-observance, filial devotion, and the like), which are ordinary virtuous conduct rather than meditative attainment. |
| 八觸 | the eight sensations | Chapter 7's fixed list of bodily sensations marking the transition from access concentration into the first dhyāna: pain, itching, cold, warmth, lightness, heaviness, roughness, and smoothness — arising one or two at a time, never all at once, and not to be mistaken for a demonic disturbance. |
| 九想 / 八背捨 / 八勝處 / 大不淨觀 | the nine contemplations / the eight liberations / the eight stations of mastery / the great contemplation of impurity | Chapter 7's graded sequence within the contemplation of impurity, matched to three grades of craving: the nine contemplations (already detailed in chapter 6) counter craving for another's physical form; the eight liberations (also called the eightfold emancipation) counter craving for one's own or another's body specifically; the eight stations of mastery build on the liberations once the contemplating mind has matured, able to break through pure or impure appearances at will; the great contemplation of impurity counters craving pervading every object at once. Keep this fourfold progression distinguished — each is a distinct named technique, not a synonym for the others. |
| 九十五種 | the ninety-five kinds [of deviant, non-Buddhist practice] | Chapter 7's fixed number (traditional in Chinese Buddhist polemics) for the total of India's non-Buddhist schools and demonic spirit-cults a practitioner risks according with if they cling to a false meditative state. Cited as a warning, not itemized individually. |
| 邪偽禪 / 真正禪 | false, demonic dhyāna / genuine, correct dhyāna | Chapter 7's central distinction, extending chapter 6's fourth approach (countering subtle attachment within concentration): both states can feel equally vivid and blissful from within, and the test is never the quality of the experience itself but whether the practitioner clings to it. Watch for this same warning recurring in chapter 8's treatment of demonic affairs. |
| 四魔 | the four demons | Chapter 8's opening framework: the demon of affliction (煩惱魔), the demon of the aggregates/sense-fields/elements (陰入界魔), the demon of death (死魔), and the demon of ghosts and spirits (鬼神魔, also called 天子魔, "the demon of the god-son"). Baojing treats the first three as ordinary matters resolved through one's own correct mindfulness, and devotes the chapter almost entirely to the fourth. |
| 精魅 / 堆剔鬼 / 魔惱 | nature-spirits / the harassing spirit / Māra's own disturbance | Chapter 8's own threefold grading of the demon of ghosts and spirits, from mildest to most dangerous: nature-spirits (old wood or stone that has absorbed the essence of sun and moon, tied to the twelve double-hours and thirty-six beasts, dispelled by naming them), the harassing spirit (a dreaded night-yakṣa type, dispelled by invoking the precepts and its own founding ancestor's story), and Māra's own disturbance (the heavenly demon-king himself, hardest to subdue, manifesting agreeable, disagreeable, or neutral sense-states tied to the three poisons). Keep this threefold grading distinct — later chapters may refer back to any one specifically. |
| 卻魔二法 | the two methods for driving off a demon | Chapter 8's own structure, mirroring the calming/insight pairing used for every other obstacle in this commentary: first cultivating calming (recognizing the demonic state as illusory, neither anxious nor attached, letting it fade of itself); if that fails, cultivating insight (turning to observe the very mind that perceives the demon, finding neither subject nor object); and, failing even that, holding to the non-duality of the demon-realm and the buddha-realm as one and the same suchness. |
| 權病 / 實病 | expedient illness / actual illness | Chapter 9's opening distinction: "expedient illness" is a fruit-stage teaching device (Vimalakīrti's own famous illness, used as an occasion to teach) that this chapter does not address; "actual illness" is the ordinary, causal-stage illness arising from one's own karmically produced body, which is this chapter's real subject throughout. |
| 四大增損 / 五藏生患 | imbalance in the four great elements / illness arising from the five organs | Chapter 9's own twofold diagnostic framework: illness tied to earth, water, fire, or wind falling out of balance (each with its own fixed hundred-and-one-illness cluster), versus illness tied to a specific one of the five organs (heart, lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys), each correlated with its own governing sense-faculty and a fixed five-phase (wood/fire/metal/water/earth) correspondence. Kept as two distinct diagnostic lenses, not collapsed into one. |
| 三種得病因緣 | the three causes of illness | Chapter 9's own threefold classification, cutting across the diagnostic categories above: illness from imbalance in the four elements or five organs (ordinary, physical), illness caused by ghosts and spirits (illustrated with Śāriputra struck by the spirit Kāla, from the Ekottara Āgama), and illness from karmic retribution (each of the five precepts, if broken, correlated with a specific organ-and-faculty pair it afflicts). |
| 六氣 / 十二息 | the six kinds of breath / the twelve kinds of breath | Chapter 9's two breath-based methods for treating illness through insight, kept carefully distinguished: the six (chuī, hū, xī, hē, xū, sì, each an audible exhalation matched to a specific organ) versus the twelve (rising, sinking, filling, scorching, growing, dissolving, warming, cooling, piercing, sustaining, harmonizing, replenishing, each paired with its own visualization) — Baojing is explicit that the two use unrelated methods despite the superficial resemblance, and warns against non-Buddhist practitioners treating either as a secret esoteric art in its own right. |
| 十法 | the ten [supporting habits for treating illness] | Chapter 9's closing checklist for making any treatment method actually work: faith, application, diligence, dwelling within the object, distinguishing the illness's cause, skillful means, sustained practice, knowing what to take up and set aside, maintaining protection, and recognizing what should be kept private. Structurally similar to — but a distinct list from — the five skillful means of chapter 5; do not conflate the two fives/tens when cross-referencing. |
| 從假入空觀 / 從空入假觀 / 中道正觀 | the contemplation moving from the conventional into emptiness / the contemplation moving from emptiness into the conventional / the correct contemplation of the middle way | Chapter 10's own threefold structure — Tiantai's "threefold contemplation in a single mind" (一心三觀), the doctrinal core the entire commentary builds toward. Also called, respectively: the contemplation of the two truths / the wisdom-eye / the wisdom that knows all things generally (第一); the contemplation of equality / the Dharma-eye / the wisdom of the paths of practice (第二); and the buddha-eye / the wisdom that knows all things in every one of their modes (第三). Baojing is explicit that the first two, alone, remain "expedient contemplative gates, not correct contemplation itself" — only the third is the goal. Keep all three names for each contemplation distinguished when cross-referencing, since Baojing uses whichever name fits the immediate context. |
| 三諦 | the three truths | Chapter 10's own repeated formula: every dharma is, at once, empty (空), conventional (假), and the middle (中) — not three different claims about a dharma but three simultaneous aspects of the same single reality, "raising one already implies all three." The doctrinal payload behind the Middle Treatise's own four-line verse Baojing cites as the historical origin of Tiantai's threefold contemplation (via Huiwen's own awakening on reading it). |
| 三因 / 三德 | the three causes / the three virtues | Chapter 10's pairing for what is realized at the very first of the ten abodes: the correct cause (正因, buddha-nature itself) manifesting as the virtue of the Dharma-body; the condition cause (緣因, wholesome nature) manifesting as the virtue of liberation; the complete cause (了因, wisdom nature) manifesting as the virtue of prajñā. Also mapped, elsewhere in this same chapter, onto the three obstructions (三障: affliction, karma, retribution) that block each virtue in turn — the whole work's closing exhortation is to remove these three obstructions specifically. |
| 初心 / 後心 | the initial mind / the final mind | Chapter 10's own relative pairing for the very first stage of realization (the abode of first arousing the resolve, already able to manifest buddhahood through the eight aspects, illustrated with the Lotus Sūtra's dragon-girl) against the very last (wondrous enlightenment itself, "inconceivable," known only by inference from scripture, since even Zhiyi's own attainment did not reach it). Baojing notes the pairing is relative and iterates at every stage ("the second abode is a 'final mind' relative to the first") — do not read "initial" and "final" as fixed absolute terms outside their immediate comparison. |

Keep this table growing as new terms recur in later chapters — add a row rather than improvising a fresh translation of a term that already has an entry.

## Register

- Baojing lectures in clear, accessible vernacular Chinese (白話), addressed explicitly to lay beginners — noticeably plainer and more discursive than Yuanying's *Léngyán Jīng Jiǎngyì*, and further still from Zhiyi's own terse classical root text. The English should read as clear, warm expository prose, not artificially elevated or archaic — matching the register of an oral lecture transcribed for print.
- Baojing draws constantly on Pure Land practice as his worked example whenever he illustrates an abstract point about calming and insight (e.g. reciting the Buddha's name single-mindedly as an instance of 止, hearing one's own recitation clearly as an instance of 觀). Preserve these asides rather than trimming them as digressions — they are the commentary's own consistent teaching method, not incidental color.
- Zhiyi's own root-text passages, by contrast, are terse and enumerative (e.g. the ten methods of aiding repentance, the four kinds of wrong livelihood) — keep the enumerated structure explicit in translation (numbered or clearly parallel clauses) even where Baojing's surrounding commentary expands into looser prose.
- Mark every chapter's status honestly. A chapter page should say plainly whether it covers the whole chapter or only part of it, matching the practice already established for the *Shì Chán Bōluómì* and *Léngyán Jīng Jiǎngyì* projects.

As of chapter 10, this translation is complete: all ten chapters of Baojing's commentary are translated and live on the site (具緣第一, 訶欲第二, 棄蓋第三, 調和第四, 方便行第五, 正修行第六, 善根發第七, 覺知魔事第八, 治病第九, 證果第十). Every quotation of Zhiyi's own root text was translated independently from the Chinese throughout, without reference to Bhikshu Dharmamitra's existing published translation of that root text alone — see "Relation to Zhiyi's root text and to Dharmamitra's published translation" above. Any future work on this project is refinement (fixing errors, tightening prose, expanding the glossary above) rather than translating further chapters — there are none left.
