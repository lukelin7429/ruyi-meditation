Chapter 6 · 正修行第六 · Complete

Actual Practice

The longest chapter in the whole text, and the hinge the other nine turn on: five ways to work with a seated mind that won't settle, then twelve more — six ordinary activities and six sense-faculties — for carrying the same calming and insight into a life that cannot simply stay seated forever. Baojing closes by naming what he has been building toward all along: a single recitation of Amitābha's name as calming and insight already complete.

Status: complete Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: book.bfnn.org Covers: practice while seated (five approaches) and practice carried into every activity and sense-faculty

Two Ways to Practice 修止觀二種

Chinese Source

〖文〗修止觀者有二種：一者於坐中修，二者歷緣對境修。一於坐中修止觀者，於四威儀中亦乃皆得，然學道者坐為勝，故先約坐以明止觀。略出五意不同：一對治初心麤亂修止觀。所謂行者初坐禪時，心麤亂故，應當修止以除破之。止若不破，即應修觀。故云對破初心麤亂修止觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Those who cultivate calming and insight do so in two ways: first, practicing while seated; second, practicing carried into every activity and circumstance. As for practicing calming and insight while seated — this can, in fact, be accomplished in any of the four bodily postures, but for one who studies the path, sitting is the most excellent, and so calming and insight are explained first in terms of sitting. Briefly, there are five distinct approaches. The first is countering the coarse agitation of the beginner's mind: when a practitioner first sits in meditation, the mind is coarse and agitated, and one should cultivate calming to break this apart. If calming alone cannot break it, one should then cultivate insight. Hence this is called countering the beginner's coarse agitation through calming and insight.

〖講〗上來所講五章，備足二十五法，乃為正修之方便。此第六正修，即正式修行做功夫也。若眾緣雖具，而不如理真實用功，則道業仍難剋就……是故行者，若眾緣具備，身心內外清淨，一切煩惱障不起，正可從此一心修止觀。然修習止觀，有二種不同，一者坐中修習止觀，二者歷緣對境修止觀。先明第一於坐中修習止觀，本於四威儀中，皆得修習止觀，於行住坐臥中，皆有威可畏，有儀可則……學道者，即指初心修習止觀之人。上來所言四威儀中，均能做功夫，乃約久已棲心道業，功用純熟者而言，若初心行人，則於坐中修習，最為殊勝，以其心念易息，境界易空故，所以首先約坐中，以明止觀也。然修止觀行門繁多，其法非一，今但明五意，故云略出，蓋此五意，即能攝一切觀門矣。所言五種修止觀者，一對治初心麤亂修止觀，二對治心沈浮病修止觀，三隨便宜修止觀，四對治定中細心修止觀，五為均齊定慧修止觀，第一言對治初心麤亂修止觀者，謂修行辦道之初心人，最初坐禪修觀之時，倘若心念麤獷，散亂不休，爾時應當修止以除破之，止乃止其散動；倘若以止不能除卻麤心散亂之病，即應修觀以破除之，觀即觀其昏暗。當知病患雖多，不出昏散二種。凡夫眾生，不是昏沈，即是散亂，若一修止觀，即能破除昏散二病，以其止能伏息麤心妄想，觀能照破微細煩惱，故云對破初心麤亂修止觀也。

Commentary: The five chapters explained above, together making up twenty-five preparatory factors, are all skillful means leading up to actual practice. This sixth chapter, actual practice, is where genuine practice — putting in real effort — actually begins. Even with every condition in place, if one does not truly apply effort in accordance with principle, one's own practice along the path still remains hard to accomplish... This is why a practitioner, once every condition is in place, body and mind pure both inwardly and outwardly, with no affliction or obstruction arising, is well placed to cultivate calming and insight with a single, undivided mind from this point on.

Cultivating calming and insight takes two distinct forms: first, cultivating it while seated; second, cultivating it carried into every activity and circumstance. First explaining the practice while seated: calming and insight can, in principle, be cultivated in any of the four bodily postures — walking, standing, sitting, or lying down — each carrying its own dignity to be respected and its own proper form to be followed... "One who studies the path" refers here specifically to a beginner in cultivating calming and insight. What was said above, that practice can be carried out in all four postures, applies to one who has long settled the mind on the path and whose skill has become fully mature; for a beginning practitioner, practicing while seated is by far the most excellent, since the mind's own thoughts settle more easily and its objects empty out more easily this way — hence calming and insight are explained first in terms of sitting.

Yet the avenues for cultivating calming and insight are numerous, and its methods are not just one; here only five approaches are explained — hence it is said "briefly" — for these five approaches are already able to encompass every gate of contemplation. The five kinds of cultivating calming and insight are: first, countering the beginner's coarse agitation; second, countering the affliction of a sinking or floating mind; third, following whatever is fitting; fourth, countering subtle attachment within concentration; fifth, balancing concentration and wisdom evenly.

As for the first, countering the beginner's coarse agitation: this means that when a beginning practitioner, taking up practice on the path, first sits in meditation to cultivate contemplation, should the mind's own thoughts be coarse and rough, scattered without rest, one should at that time cultivate calming to break this apart — calming being precisely the calming of this scattered agitation. Should calming alone fail to remove this affliction of a coarse, scattered mind, one should then cultivate insight to break it apart instead — insight being precisely the illuminating of this very dullness. One should know that however many afflictions there may be, they do not exceed two kinds: dullness and scatteredness. An ordinary sentient being is, at any given moment, either sunk in dullness or else scattered in agitation; and once calming and insight are cultivated together, both of these afflictions can be broken — for calming is able to subdue and still the coarse mind's idle thought, while insight is able to illuminate and break through subtle affliction. Hence this is called countering the beginner's coarse agitation through calming and insight.

I. Practice While Seated 坐中修

1. Countering the Beginner's Coarse Agitation 對治初心麤亂修止觀

Zhiyi treats this first approach at far greater length than the other four, since it is where a beginner actually starts: three ways of cultivating calming, followed by two ways of cultivating insight.

Three Ways of Cultivating Calming 修止三種

Chinese Source

〖文〗今明修止觀有二意：一者修止，自有三種：一者繫緣守境止。所謂繫心鼻端、臍間等處，令心不散。故經云：繫心不放逸，亦如猿著鎖。二者制心止。所謂隨心所起，即便制之，不令馳散。故經云：此五根者，心為其主，是故汝等當好止心。此二種皆是事相，不須分別。三者體真止。所謂隨心所念一切諸法，悉知從因緣生，無有自性，則心不取。若心不取，則妄念心息，故名為止。如經中說云：
一切諸法中　因緣空無主　息心達本源　故號為沙門

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Cultivating calming and insight now has two aspects. The first is cultivating calming, which is itself of three kinds. The first is calming through fixing the mind and guarding an object: fixing the mind on the tip of the nose, the navel, or some similar place, so that the mind does not scatter. Hence a sūtra says: "Fix the mind and do not let it run loose — exactly like a monkey kept on a leash." The second is calming through restraining the mind: whatever thought arises in the mind, restraining it at once, not letting it run off scattered. Hence a sūtra says: "Of these five faculties, the mind is their master — you should therefore skillfully still the mind." These first two are both matters of outward technique, and need no further distinction. The third is calming through embodying true reality: whatever dharma the mind happens to think of, knowing entirely that it arises from causes and conditions and has no fixed nature of its own, the mind then takes hold of nothing — and once the mind takes hold of nothing, idle thought comes to rest of itself, hence it is called calming. As a sūtra says:
"Within every dharma, causes and conditions are empty, with no master over them — stilling the mind, one reaches the very source; hence one is called a śramaṇa."

〖講〗修止觀有二意，一者修止，二者修觀。於修止中自有三種之別，一者繫緣守境止，此乃繫念法門，如行人念麤心浮，即將心繫緣一處，安守於一境，或繫心於自身之鼻端上，或繫緣腹臍丹田間，或緣想諸佛相好亦可，總之，令心不馳散也……故經云：繫心不放逸，亦如猿著鎖。二者制心止，即以放下制止之功夫，而伏滅妄念，不同上來之繫緣諸境，即將自心之念頭微微觀照，觀其隨心所起之念頭，看他起處即便制之，若貪欲念起，即須放下，瞋心念起亦放下，愚癡念起亦復放下，無論何種念起，即以放下二字而制伏之，不令馳散……此二種止，皆是事相，最易明見，不須詳為分別。

Commentary: Cultivating calming and insight has two aspects: cultivating calming, and cultivating insight. Within cultivating calming there are three distinct kinds. The first is calming through fixing the mind and guarding an object — this is a method of fixed mindfulness. If a practitioner's own thoughts are coarse and the mind is prone to drift, one fixes the mind on a single place, keeping guard over a single object — whether fixing the mind on the tip of one's own nose, or on the elixir field at the navel, or even taking the buddhas' own excellent marks as the object of contemplation — in every case, simply keeping the mind from running off scattered... Hence a sūtra says: "Fix the mind and do not let it run loose — exactly like a monkey kept on a leash."

The second is calming through restraining the mind — that is, the effort of setting down and restraining, subduing and dissolving idle thought. Unlike the method above, of fixing the mind on some object, this instead observes one's own mind's thoughts very closely, watching whatever thought arises in the mind and restraining it the moment it appears: if a thought of greed arises, one sets it down at once; if a thought of anger arises, one likewise sets it down; if a thought of delusion arises, one likewise sets it down — whatever kind of thought arises, one subdues it with these same two words, "set down," never letting it run off scattered... These first two forms of calming are both matters of outward technique, very easily understood at a glance, and need no further, detailed distinction.

〖講〗此從性理而言，義稍深微，不於事相而著覺，乃於理體而觀察，了知隨心所念一切諸法，悉從因緣而生。一切，盡際包括也……然諸法雖多，不出依正報……無論依報之法，正報之法，悉知從因緣和合而生。既由因緣而生，悉從因緣而滅，緣生緣滅，幻妄稱相，其實緣生無性，求其生滅去來，了不可得。何以故？自心本無生滅故，但於心性緣起，不無虛妄取用，所以有生有滅，有來有去，當知生乃虛妄因緣和合而有生，虛妄因緣別離而名滅。依緣而生生即非生，依緣而滅滅即非滅，當體無有自性。了達諸法無生名真空。凡所有相，皆是虛妄。既知其虛妄，則心不生取著。若心不生取著，則妄念之心不息而息矣，是名體真止。所謂體達諸法，無非真空，則妄念自止息，起信論云：「一切境界，皆以妄念而有差別，若無妄念，即離一切境界之相，即是如來平等法身。」依此法身，說名本覺，是知息念，最為要緊，若能如是止息妄念，故名為體真止。經云：「一切諸法中，因緣空無主。」一切即賅羅世出世法，謂一切諸法之中，求其實體，了不可得，以其皆從因緣而生，當體無自性，無他性，無共性，無無因性。以真空而為其體，無有主宰故也。沙門二字，為釋子之通稱……又云：息心達本源，故號為沙門。若能體真息心，即達本源之心地，因此心為一切諸法之本，心若息，則本源達矣！

Commentary: This third kind speaks in terms of the nature of reality itself, and its meaning is somewhat deeper and more subtle: it does not fasten its attention on outward technique, but observes instead the very substance of principle, coming to understand that every dharma the mind happens to think of arises entirely from causes and conditions. "Every" here means comprehensively, without exception... Yet however many dharmas there are, they do not exceed the dependent and direct fruits of karma... whether a dependent or a direct dharma, one understands it entirely to arise from the coming-together of causes and conditions. Having arisen through causes and conditions, it must likewise cease through causes and conditions — arising and ceasing through conditions, called into existence as an illusory appearance — yet, in truth, what arises through conditions has no fixed nature at all, and seeking its arising, ceasing, coming, or going, none of it can ultimately be found. Why is this? Because one's own mind has, from the start, no arising or ceasing at all — it is only through the conditioned arising of mind's own nature that there is, unavoidably, some illusory taking-hold, and so there appears to be arising and ceasing, coming and going. One should know that "arising" is merely the coming-together of illusory causes and conditions called "arising," and "ceasing" is merely the separating-apart of illusory causes and conditions called "ceasing." What arises through conditions, in its very arising, is not truly arising at all; what ceases through conditions, in its very ceasing, is not truly ceasing at all — its very substance has no fixed nature of its own. Fully understanding that no dharma is truly born is called true emptiness. Whatever has a mark at all is entirely illusory — and once one knows it to be illusory, the mind gives rise to no clinging attachment toward it. And once the mind gives rise to no clinging attachment, the mind of idle thought comes to rest without needing to be forcibly rested at all — this is called calming through embodying true reality.

This is what is meant by fully embodying that every dharma is nothing but true emptiness, so that idle thought comes to rest of itself. The Awakening of Faith says: "Every object of experience is differentiated only through idle thought; without idle thought, one is free of every mark of every object, and this is precisely the Tathāgata's own equal Dharma-body." Relying on this Dharma-body, this is called original awakening — from this we know that resting idle thought matters more than almost anything else. Being able to bring idle thought to rest in just this way is called calming through embodying true reality.

The sūtra says: "Within every dharma, causes and conditions are empty, with no master over them." "Every" here comprehends both mundane and transmundane dharmas alike — meaning that, seeking the true substance within any dharma at all, none can ultimately be found, since every one of them arises entirely from causes and conditions, its very substance having no nature of its own, no nature from elsewhere, no nature shared in common, and no nature arising without cause — its substance being true emptiness itself, with nothing at all presiding over it as master.

"Śramaṇa" is the general term for a follower of the Buddha... and, as it is further said, "stilling the mind, one reaches the very source — hence one is called a śramaṇa." If one is able to embody true reality and still the mind in this way, one thereby reaches the very ground of the mind at its own source — for this mind is the very root of every dharma, and once the mind is stilled, its source is reached!

Chinese Source

〖文〗行者於初坐禪時，隨心所念一切諸法，念念不住，雖用如上體真止，而妄念不息，當反觀所起之心，過去已滅，現在不住，未來未至，三際窮之，了不可得。不可得法，則無有心；若無有心，則一切法皆無……又觀此心念，以內有六根，外有六塵。根塵相對，故有識生；根塵未對，識本無生。觀生如是，觀滅亦然，生滅名字，但是假立。生滅心滅，寂滅現前，了無所得，是所謂涅槃空寂之理，其心自止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: When a practitioner first sits in meditation, every dharma the mind thinks of, thought following thought, never stays put — and if, even using the calming through embodying true reality explained above, idle thought still does not come to rest, one should turn to observe the very mind from which it arises: the past already gone, the present not staying, the future not yet arrived — investigated across all three time periods, it ultimately cannot be found. If a dharma cannot be found, then there is no mind there at all; and if there is no mind, then no dharma exists at all either... One further observes this thought of the mind: inwardly there are the six faculties, outwardly the six sense-objects. Faculty and object meeting each other, consciousness arises; faculty and object not yet meeting, consciousness has, from the start, no arising at all. Observing its arising is like this; observing its ceasing is just the same — the very names "arising" and "ceasing" are merely provisional labels. Once the mind of arising-and-ceasing itself ceases, still cessation manifests before one, with nothing at all left to be found — this is precisely the principle of nirvāṇa's own empty stillness, and the mind is calmed of itself.

〖講〗行者倘於上三種止，不得利益，於隨心所起之念，仍然念念遷謝，流住不停，前念後念，相續不斷，如大海之波浪相似，妄想雜念，不能止息，則須返觀所起之心，此即修觀。因一切法皆從心起，唯心所現，若無有心，則一切法何由而起？境本無生，因心而有，心若無時，則境亦無。了知一切法既由心所造，即回光返照我此一念能造之心，究竟有何形相？此心圓耶、方耶、是青耶、黃耶？或是過去、現在、未來，或在內外中間？若言過去，過去已滅，現在現在不住，未來未來未至。三際推窮，此一念能觀之心，了不可得，以其此心豎無初後，橫絕邊涯，不落分劑方隅，故非內外中間，非長短方圓，非青黃赤白，非過去現在未來，一切處，一切時，求心當體，了不可得……然雖了不可得，而非同頑空木石之無知，故云非無剎那之念，胡來見胡，漢來見漢。現在一念有情無情之境界，無不覺知明了清楚，故云：任運覺知念起。

Commentary: Should a practitioner, applying the three kinds of calming explained above, still gain no benefit — if the thoughts arising from the mind still, one after another, shift and pass away, flowing without ever settling, one thought following the next in unbroken succession, exactly like waves on the great ocean, idle and disordered thought unable to come to rest — one must then turn to observe the very mind from which these thoughts arise. This is itself the cultivation of insight. Because every dharma arises entirely from mind, manifested by mind alone — if there were no mind, by what means could any dharma arise at all? An object has, from the start, no arising of its own; it exists only because of mind. If mind were absent, the object too would be absent.

Understanding that every dharma is thus produced by mind, one turns the light around and looks back at this very thought, this mind capable of producing it: what shape does it actually have, in the end? Is this mind round, or square? Is it blue, or yellow? Does it belong to the past, the present, or the future? Is it located inside, outside, or somewhere in between? If one says it belongs to the past — the past is already gone. The present does not stay put. The future has not yet arrived. Investigated across all three time periods, this very thought, this mind capable of observation, ultimately cannot be found at all — for this mind has, vertically, no beginning and no end, and, horizontally, no boundary or edge whatsoever, falling into no fixed measure or location; hence it is neither inside, outside, nor in between, neither long nor short, neither round nor square, neither blue, yellow, red, nor white, belonging to neither past, present, nor future — in every place, at every time, seeking the mind's own substance, it ultimately cannot be found at all.

Yet although it ultimately cannot be found, this is not the same as the blank unknowing of an inert void, wood, or stone — hence it is said that this is not the absence of instant-by-instant awareness: when a Persian comes, one sees a Persian; when a Chinese person comes, one sees a Chinese person. Whatever sentient or non-sentient circumstance presents itself in the present moment is, without exception, known and understood with perfect clarity — hence it is said, "awareness arises spontaneously the moment a thought does."

〖講〗上來返觀此一念能起之心，竟在何處？三際窮之，無有處所。而於不可得中，非無剎那之念，而任運覺知心念所起，行者又復返觀此心念，因何而起？吾人內心，有眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、意之六根，根有勝義浮塵之不同。浮塵根，即父母所生之身是，虛假不實，故名浮；無見聞覺知之用，故名塵。二勝義根，此根凡夫肉眼有礙，不能見，天眼能見之……如眼能見色，耳能聞聲，乃至意能知法，此為內六根區別之相。外則有色、聲、香、味、觸、法之六塵境界，因六根對六塵，互相交偶，於中故有生六識。若根塵不相黏，識則無由而起，因其單根不生，獨塵難成也。是知根塵為能生，識心為所生，六根為能對，六塵為所對，彼此互相依靠，互相建立，當知能對所對，能生所生，悉皆幻化無常，畢竟空無實體……如是緣生無性，當體既空，返觀能生之心，既如是，而返觀所滅之境，亦復如是。謂觀心不生，觀法亦不生，觀心無常，觀法亦復無常。故云觀生如是，觀滅亦然，是知生而滅，滅而生，生生滅滅，滅滅生生，生滅名字，但是假立，以其緣生緣滅，虛假不實，但幻妄稱相而已！行者善能返觀，不特將所生之法不可得，即將能觀之心亦復不可得，能所雙亡，心境俱寂，是則真空寂滅之理，朗然顯現在前，謂生滅既滅，寂滅現前，此時大地平沈，虛空粉碎，此處無人無我無眾生。永嘉大師云：「了了見，無一物，亦無人亦無佛，大千沙界水中漚，一切凡聖如電拂。」不但六凡無所得，四聖亦無所得，不特妄是空，真亦是空。至此地步，唯是平等一心而已，尚何有其他哉！

Commentary: Having, above, turned to observe this very thought, this mind from which it arises — where, in the end, is it actually located? Investigated across the three time periods, it has no location at all. Yet within this very unfindability, awareness is never absent even for a single instant, and spontaneously knows whatever thought arises in the mind. A practitioner, turning to observe this thought once more, asks: from what does it actually arise?

Our own inward mind has six faculties — eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind — and each faculty has both a coarse, material form and a subtle, essential form. The coarse, material faculty is precisely the body born of one's own parents, illusory and unreal, hence called "coarse"; it has no function of seeing, hearing, sensing, or knowing on its own, hence it too is called mere "matter." The subtle, essential faculty, by contrast, is obstructed from an ordinary being's own fleshly eye and cannot be seen at all, though the heavenly eye is able to see it... just as the eye is able to see form, the ear is able to hear sound, and the mind, in turn, is able to know mental objects — these are the distinguishing marks of the six inward faculties. Outwardly there are the six sense-objects of form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and mental objects — and because the six faculties meet the six sense-objects, pairing with one another, the six consciousnesses are thereby produced. If faculty and object do not actually connect, consciousness has no way to arise at all, since a faculty alone cannot produce it, nor can an object alone bring it about. From this we know that faculty and object together are the producing cause, and the mind of consciousness is what is produced; the six faculties are what meets, and the six sense-objects are what is met — each depending on the other, each established through the other. One should know that meeter and met, producer and produced, are all illusory and impermanent, ultimately empty, with no real substance at all.

Arising through conditions in this way, having no fixed nature, its very substance already empty — turning to observe the very mind that produces it is exactly the same, and turning to observe the very object that ceases is exactly the same as well. That is, observing that the mind does not truly arise, one observes that the dharma too does not truly arise; observing that the mind is impermanent, one observes that the dharma too is impermanent. Hence it is said, "observing its arising is like this; observing its ceasing is just the same" — from which we know that arising is itself ceasing, and ceasing is itself arising, arising upon arising, ceasing upon ceasing, ceasing upon ceasing, arising upon arising — the very names "arising" and "ceasing" are merely provisional labels, since what arises and ceases through conditions is illusory and unreal, merely an illusory appearance given a name.

A practitioner skillfully able to turn and observe in this way finds not only that the dharma produced cannot be found, but that the very mind capable of observing it cannot be found either — subject and object both gone, mind and its object both stilled — and this is precisely when the principle of true emptiness and still cessation manifests brilliantly clear before one: arising-and-ceasing having already ceased, still cessation manifests. At this moment, the whole earth sinks level and empty space itself shatters — here there is no person, no self, and no sentient being at all. Great Master Yongjia said: "Seeing with utter clarity, there is not a single thing — no person, and no buddha either; the great chiliocosm's own sand-grain worlds are but bubbles on water, and every ordinary being and sage alike is like a flash of lightning." Not only do the six ordinary destinies attain nothing at all — the four sagely realms attain nothing either; not only is the false itself empty — the true, too, is empty. Having reached this point, there remains only the single, equal mind itself — what else could there possibly be beyond this?

〖文〗二者修觀有二種：一者對治觀。如不淨觀，對治貪欲；慈心觀，對治瞋恚；界分別觀，對治著我；數息觀，對治多尋思等，此不分別也。

Root Text: Second, cultivating insight is itself of two kinds. Commentary: This second part explains cultivating insight. The three ways of cultivating calming explained above — fixing the mind and guarding an object, restraining the mind, and embodying true reality — are now fully explained. Cultivating insight likewise has two kinds: the counteracting contemplation, and correct contemplation. The counteracting contemplation, also called the supporting contemplation, is precisely what counters the specific affliction present within one's own mind... While sitting in meditation, a practitioner must observe which idle thought predominates within their own mind.

(1) If thoughts of covetous desire predominate, one should cultivate the contemplation of impurity to counter it... Among these, best suited to a beginning, ordinary practitioner, easiest to benefit from, and most essential of all, is none other than the ninefold contemplation... One should know that these nine impure marks, though cultivated as an imagined contemplation, are nonetheless able to accomplish a great task — like a corpse floating in the great ocean, which a drowning person may cling to and thereby be saved. This is the contemplation of impurity, countering covetous desire.

(2) If thoughts of anger predominate, one should counter it with the contemplation of loving-kindness... this is precisely cultivating the contemplation of loving-kindness to counter the fire of anger. Contemplation of loving-kindness likewise has, roughly, three distinct kinds: for anger without any reasonable cause arising on its own, one cultivates loving-kindness toward sentient beings directly to counter it; for anger with some reasonable cause, arising when another person troubles or provokes one, one cultivates loving-kindness grounded in the Dharma to counter it; and for anger arising from insisting oneself is right and another is wrong, born of dispute, one cultivates loving-kindness with no object at all to counter it. A practitioner, using these three skillful forms of loving-kindness, extinguishes the fire of anger, and the mind attains a cool clarity, naturally according with the path.

(3) If thoughts of attachment to a self predominate, one counters it with the contemplation of distinguishing the elements. What is meant by "distinguishing" is this: "element" means a bounded category, since our own body is entirely a temporary aggregation of the seven great elements — earth, water, fire, wind, space, the faculties, and consciousness... Observed in this way, searching among the faculties, sense-objects, consciousnesses, and elements for a self, none can ultimately be found — and where none can be found, there is, in fact, no self there at all. Yet sentient beings fail to understand this, and falsely cling to a self where there is no self — falsely taking the four great elements to be their own bodily form, falsely taking the shadow-traces of the six sense-objects to be their own mind. In truth the five aggregates are, at root, empty, and the four great elements are not a self. If one is genuinely able to investigate and distinguish in this way, the thought clinging to a self is dissolved.

(4) If thoughts of discursive searching and scattered distraction predominate, one counters it with the contemplation of counting the breath. "Counting the breath" means counting one's own breath through the nose — setting aside the windy, panting, or coarse states, and counting only the fine, continuous breath, as though barely there and yet never quite gone, passing in and out through the nose, from one up to ten, and then from ten back to one — counting the inhalation but not the exhalation, or the exhalation but not the inhalation — continuous and fine throughout, the mind resting on the breath and the breath resting on the mind, with no other object of attention at all, following the breath alone, the count kept perfectly clear. In this way, every searching, discursive thought within the mind is entirely dissolved without remainder — hence it is said, "these need no further distinction."

"Already understood" means having already understood that every dharma is empty, with nothing there at all — and once this point is reached, no idle or disordered thought remains. Once idle thought is gone, the mind is pure of itself. If one can investigate these four lines deeply, more than half of what follows in this text will already be understood! This concludes the first approach, countering the beginner's coarse agitation through calming and insight.

Root Text: Second, countering the affliction of a sinking or floating mind through calming and insight. If, while sitting in meditation, a practitioner's mind grows dark and blocked, blankly neutral or dazed, or prone to excessive sleepiness, one should at that time cultivate insight to illuminate it clearly. If, while seated, the mind is prone to float, agitated and restless, ill at ease, one should at that time cultivate calming to still it. 〖講〗此第二對治心沈浮病修止觀。行者於靜坐禪觀之時，其心或昏沈闇塞，或浮動輕躁，或無記，或瞪瞢。無記者，即無所記別也，亦有蓋覆明了二種不同……行者若於靜坐中，有此等蓋覆無記發現之時，即應當修觀照了，念茲在茲，既不昏沈，亦無令其境界不分明之闇覆無記現前。若於坐中無如上所說之無記昏沈病，而其心浮動輕躁不安亦不好；心浮動，即心掉舉；輕躁不安，即是身掉舉；如是身心掉舉，內外不安，心散念浮，亦足以損法身，失功德，當此浮動病發時，亦急須棄之，應當修止止之，不令浮動。此是略說修止觀對治浮沈病之相，然須善識藥病相對而治之，即是以止治散，以觀治昏。所謂宜止則止，宜觀則觀，謂知病識藥，應病與藥，切不可亂投藥石，反增病患，故云一一不得有乖僻之失。乖者亂也，僻者偏也，即不得錯亂修習之謂。

While a practitioner sits still cultivating dhyāna-contemplation, the mind may grow dull and dark, blocked and closed off; or it may float, agitated and restless; or it may fall into a blank, neutral state, or become dazed and vacant. This "blank, neutral state" means having nothing recorded or distinguished within it — this too has two kinds, a covering form and a clear, aware form...

If, while sitting still, a practitioner finds this kind of covering, blank-neutral state arising, one should cultivate insight to illuminate it clearly, holding this awareness continuously, so that one falls neither into dullness nor into the dark, covering blankness in which objects fail to appear distinctly. A floating mind is mental restlessness; agitated unease is bodily restlessness — and restlessness of body and mind together, unsettled both inwardly and outwardly, the mind scattered and thought adrift, is likewise enough to damage the Dharma-body and destroy one's own merit. When this affliction of floating restlessness arises, one must likewise cast it off at once, cultivating calming to still it, not letting it float.

This is a brief account of the mark of countering the affliction of sinking or floating through calming and insight — but one must skillfully recognize which remedy matches which affliction and treat it accordingly: using calming to treat scatteredness, and insight to treat dullness. That is, calming where calming is called for, insight where insight is called for — knowing the illness, recognizing the remedy, prescribing according to the illness — one must never apply the wrong remedy carelessly, only worsening the affliction instead; hence it is said that one must never allow any confusion or error in this. "Confusion" means disorder; "error" means one-sidedness — that is, one must never practice this in a disordered, mismatched way.

If, upon cultivating insight, one immediately feels the mind and spirit grow clear, still and at ease, one should know that insight is fitting here, and should use insight to settle the mind. This is a brief account of following whatever is fitting in cultivating calming and insight — but one must skillfully practice according to whatever proves fitting, so that mind and spirit find peace, the affliction of the hindrances comes to rest, and every dharma-gate is realized.

〖講〗正修章中有五義修止觀不同，前二已竟，今第三明隨便宜修止觀。此觀由前對治浮沈病修止觀而來，言隨便宜者，即是隨自己之便，不拘修止，亦不局修觀，隨自意便而修，惟求其適當，獲益為要。前雖為對治心沈之病，修於觀照而對治之，而心仍不得明淨，亦不能獲佛法之利益，爾時當試修止以止之。試修，即試驗後方修，若試驗後與心相應，即不妨依而修之。行者於坐禪時，睡魔俱多，本應修觀對之，但習久不得益，則不妨修止以止之，久之睡病亦能消滅也。所謂以毒攻毒，身心清淨，即是以止安心之功夫。若於坐禪時，雖為對治心浮動故修止，而心不住，亦無法利，當試修觀。若於觀中，即覺心神明，寂然安隱，當知宜觀，即當用觀安心。前來明隨便宜修止，今明隨便宜修觀。若行者，於坐禪中，雖修止對治浮沈病魔，而心仍復輕躁，不能安住，爾時當試修觀。若修觀時，即自覺知，心神明淨，湛寂安隱，即與觀相應，則以修觀，而安其心，此為隨便宜修止觀之相。是則修習止觀，須善巧約便利合宜者修之，則心安神怡，煩惱之患息滅，波羅密門，由此證入矣。

Commentary: Among the five distinct approaches within the actual-practice chapter, the first two are now complete; this third explains following whatever is fitting in cultivating calming and insight. This approach follows on from the previous one, countering the affliction of sinking or floating. "Following whatever is fitting" means following whatever proves convenient for oneself — neither restricted to cultivating calming alone, nor confined to cultivating insight alone, but practicing according to whatever suits oneself, seeking only what is truly fitting, with actual benefit as the goal.

Having, before, cultivated insight to counter the affliction of a sinking mind, if the mind still fails to become clear and pure, and gains no benefit from the Dharma, one should at that time try cultivating calming instead to still it. "Try cultivating" means practicing only after testing it — if, after testing, it accords well with the mind, one need not hesitate to rely on it and practice it further. If a practitioner, while sitting in meditation, is troubled by heavy drowsiness, one would ordinarily cultivate insight to counter it — but if long practice brings no benefit, one need not hesitate to cultivate calming instead to still it, and in time the affliction of drowsiness, too, can be dissolved. This is what is meant by "using poison to counter poison" — body and mind becoming pure through this very effort of using calming to settle the mind.

If, while sitting in meditation, one cultivates calming to counter a floating mind, and yet the mind still fails to settle, gaining no benefit from the Dharma, one should try cultivating insight instead. If, upon cultivating insight, one immediately feels the mind and spirit grow clear, still and at ease, one should know that insight is fitting here, and should use insight to settle the mind. What came before explained following whatever is fitting in cultivating calming; this now explains following whatever is fitting in cultivating insight.

If a practitioner, while sitting in meditation, cultivates calming to counter the demon of a sinking or floating mind, and yet the mind remains agitated and restless, unable to settle, one should at that time try cultivating insight instead. If, upon cultivating insight, one becomes aware of oneself that the mind and spirit have grown clear and pure, deep and still, at ease — this accords with insight, and so one should cultivate insight to settle the mind. This is the mark of following whatever is fitting in cultivating calming and insight. Cultivating calming and insight, then, requires skillfully practicing whatever genuinely proves fitting and convenient, so that the mind is at peace and the spirit content, the affliction of the hindrances comes to rest, and the gate of pāramitā is thereby realized.

4. Countering Subtle Attachment Within Concentration 對治定中細心修止觀

Chinese Source

〖文〗四對治定中細心修止觀。所謂行者先用止觀對破麤亂，亂心既息，即得入定，定心細故，覺身空寂，受於快樂；或利便心發，能以細心取於偏邪之理。若不知定心止息虛誑，必生貪著。若生貪著，執以為實；若知虛誑不實，如愛見二煩惱不起，是為修止。雖復修止，若心猶著愛見，結業不息，爾時應當修觀，觀於定中細心。若不見定中細心，即不執著定見。若不執著定見，則愛見煩惱業悉皆摧滅，是名修觀。此則略說對治定中細心修止觀相。分別止觀方法，並同於前，但以破定見微細之失為異也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Fourth, countering subtle attachment within concentration through calming and insight. When a practitioner first uses calming and insight to break through coarse agitation, and this agitated mind has come to rest, one attains concentration — and because the settled mind is now fine and subtle, one feels the body itself as empty and still, and experiences a certain pleasure from it; at times a certain keen, ready mind may arise, able, with this subtle mind, to seize upon some skewed, deviant understanding. If one does not recognize that this settled mind's own state is itself illusory and unreal, one is certain to give rise to craving attachment toward it — and having given rise to craving attachment, one clings to it as real. If one instead recognizes it as illusory and unreal, then neither the affliction of craving nor the affliction of [wrong] views arises — this is cultivating calming. Yet even having cultivated calming this way, should the mind still cling to craving or views, and the resulting karmic entanglement still not come to rest, one should then cultivate insight, observing this very subtle mind within concentration. If one no longer sees any subtle mind within concentration to speak of, one no longer clings to any view about concentration; and once one no longer clings to any view about concentration, the karmic affliction of craving and views is entirely broken apart — this is cultivating insight. This is a brief account of countering subtle attachment within concentration through calming and insight; the method of distinguishing calming from insight here is the same as before, differing only in that it breaks through this subtle failing of clinging to a view about concentration itself.

〖講〗此第四是為久修者所對治，初修行者，則無此病，此種境界，最為微細，麤心之人，不易得故。行者對破麤心亂想，即將自心之浮動妄念消滅，爾時即得安心入禪定之中，於其定中心念微細，故便覺自身空空寂寂，受於快樂。有時或利便之心間發，遂於定中，橫生計度，知見叢生。因心細與定境相應故，微念思山則見山，微念思水則現水，以是行者，便為殊勝境界，取於偏邪之理，執之為實，不肯去捨，殊不知此全是定心止息之一點虛誑之境，法塵之影子，若固執之，終不免落邪見之禍。楞嚴經云：「知見立知，即無明本。」又云：「止作聖心，名善境界，若作聖解，即受群邪。」此等悉是訓誨坐禪人之誠語，祈留意焉。若了知此境虛誑不實，則不生貪著，不起分別，則見煩惱不起；不生貪染，則愛煩惱無由生；不起見愛，心地安靜，是為修止。然雖復修止止之，假若自心幻境，不能棄除，猶念念貪著，愛見結業煩惱仍然不息，行者則不修止，應當修觀而觀照之。返觀定中細心，一心澄神寂照，如貓捕鼠然，以是微微照了，定語細心，了不可得，是名細心中修觀。每見世之外道，於禪定中，稍嘗一點禪味，便以為己之功夫究竟，從此起邪見，撥因果，既不識修止觀對治，復執之為實，此迷中倍人也，悲夫！

Commentary: This fourth approach counters what applies specifically to one who has already practiced for a long time — a beginning practitioner has no such affliction to speak of, since this particular state is extremely subtle, not easily encountered by a mind still coarse. When a practitioner breaks through coarse mental agitation, dissolving the mind's own floating, idle thoughts, the mind is thereby able to settle into dhyāna-concentration itself. Within this concentration, one's thoughts grow fine and subtle, and one feels one's own body as utterly empty and still, experiencing a certain pleasure from it. At times a certain keen, ready mind may arise between thoughts, giving rise, within concentration, to unwarranted speculation, various views crowding in — since the mind, now fine and subtle, accords with the state of concentration itself, a faint thought of a mountain brings a mountain into view, a faint thought of water brings water into view — and so a practitioner takes this to be some excellent, superior state, seizing upon a skewed, deviant understanding, clinging to it as real, unwilling to let it go — little knowing that this is, in its entirety, nothing but a single illusory scene arising from the settled mind's own stillness, a mere shadow-trace of a mental object; clinging stubbornly to it can hardly avoid the calamity of falling into wrong views.

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra says: "Setting up further knowledge on top of what is already known and seen is itself the very root of ignorance." And again: "When a state is merely recognized without claiming sagehood, it is a wholesome state; but the moment it is taken as a sign of sagely attainment, one falls prey to every kind of deviant influence." Words of this kind are, in every case, sincere instruction for one sitting in meditation — I beg the reader to take heed of them.

If one fully understands that this state is illusory and unreal, one gives rise to no craving attachment toward it, and no discriminating view arises; giving rise to no craving attachment, no affliction of craving has any way to arise; giving rise to no view, the ground of the mind remains settled — this is cultivating calming. Yet even having cultivated calming to still it this way, should one's own illusory state prove impossible to cast aside, still craved and clung to from thought to thought, the karmic entanglement of craving and views still failing to come to rest — a practitioner should then set calming aside and cultivate insight instead, illuminating this state directly. Turning to observe this very subtle mind within concentration, with a single, focused mind, settling the spirit and observing in stillness — exactly like a cat stalking a mouse — through this faint, careful illumination, this subtle mind spoken of within concentration ultimately cannot be found at all. This is called cultivating insight amid the subtle mind.

One often sees non-Buddhist practitioners in the world who, having tasted just the slightest flavor of dhyāna within concentration, immediately take their own effort to be already complete, and from this give rise to wrong views, denying cause and effect entirely — neither recognizing the need to counter this through cultivating calming and insight, nor able to see the state for what it is, clinging to it as real instead. This is a person doubly lost in delusion — how sorrowful!

5. Balancing Concentration and Wisdom 均齊定慧修止觀

Chinese Source

〖文〗五為均齊定慧修止觀。行者於坐禪中因修止故，或因修觀，而入禪定。雖得入定，而無觀慧，是為癡定，不能斷結。或觀慧微少，即不能發起真慧，斷諸結使，發諸法門。爾時應當修觀破析，則定慧均等，能斷結使，證諸法門。行者於坐禪時因修觀故，而心豁然開悟，智慧分明，而定心微少，心則動散，如風中燈，照物不了，不能出離生死。爾時應當復修於止，以修止故，則得定心，如密室中燈，即能破暗，照物分明，是則略說均齊定慧二法修止觀也。行者若能如是於端身正坐之中，善用此五番修止觀意，取捨不失其宜，當知是人善修佛法，能善修故，必於一生不空過也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Fifth, balancing concentration and wisdom in cultivating calming and insight. If a practitioner, through cultivating calming or through cultivating insight, enters dhyāna-concentration while sitting in meditation, but, having entered concentration, has no contemplative wisdom present at all, this is a deluded concentration, unable to sever the fetters. Or, if contemplative wisdom is present only in a slight degree, one is thereby unable to bring forth true wisdom, sever the fetters, or open up any dharma-gate. At such a time one should cultivate insight to analyze and break through, so that concentration and wisdom become evenly balanced, able to sever the fetters and realize every dharma-gate.

If a practitioner, through cultivating insight while sitting in meditation, has the mind suddenly open into awakening, wisdom shining clear, but has only a slight degree of concentration present, the mind becomes agitated and scattered, like a lamp in the wind, unable to illuminate anything clearly, unable to escape birth-and-death. At such a time one should cultivate calming once more — and through cultivating calming, one attains a settled mind, like a lamp within a sealed room, able to dispel darkness and illuminate everything with perfect clarity. This is a brief account of balancing concentration and wisdom in cultivating calming and insight. If a practitioner, sitting upright with body composed, is able to skillfully apply the intent of these five approaches to cultivating calming and insight, taking up or setting aside each as fitting without error, one should know that such a person is skillfully cultivating the Buddhadharma — and, cultivating it skillfully, this very life will certainly not pass by in emptiness.

〖講〗此第五修止觀。不斷結使者，不能斷除煩惱之結使也。證諸法門者，即證圓頓諸波羅密法門也。觀慧微少者，即有止無觀，定多於慧也；定心微少者，即有觀無止，慧多於定也。若有定無慧，此定是枯定；若有慧無定，此慧亦是狂慧。若欲斷除結使，證諸法門，非止觀齊修，定慧均等不可。在因名止觀，在果名定慧，止觀二法，如鳥之兩翼，車之二輪，離則兩傷，合則雙美，故以密室中燈為喻，正顯止觀決要均等，方能脫離苦海。當知止觀齊修，定慧均等者何，無他，即一句彌陀是。梵語阿彌陀，此云無量光壽，光即是慧，壽即是定，光壽不二，即是定慧均等之妙止觀。如吾人念一句佛號，能念之心，所念之佛，了不可得，即是止。能念所念，歷歷明明，即是觀。當知正歷歷明明時，當體了不可得，正了不可得時，而原來歷歷明明，當下即止即觀，即觀即止，止觀均定，定慧不二之無量光壽也。行者若能善以念佛法門而修止觀，一生不致空過，現前當來決定成佛。

Commentary: This fifth approach cultivates calming and insight together. "Unable to sever the fetters" means unable to sever the binding fetters of affliction. "Realizing every dharma-gate" means realizing the perfect and sudden dharma-gates of every pāramitā. "Contemplative wisdom present only in a slight degree" means having calming without insight, concentration exceeding wisdom; "concentration present only in a slight degree" means having insight without calming, wisdom exceeding concentration. Concentration without wisdom is a barren concentration; wisdom without concentration is likewise a reckless wisdom. If one wishes to sever the fetters and realize every dharma-gate, there is no way but cultivating calming and insight together, balancing concentration and wisdom evenly.

At the causal stage these are called calming and insight; at the fruit stage they are called concentration and wisdom. Calming and insight together are like a bird's two wings, or a cart's two wheels — separated, both are harmed; joined, both flourish together. Hence the simile of a lamp within a sealed room is used, precisely to show that calming and insight must, without fail, be kept in balance before one can escape the sea of suffering at all.

One should know that cultivating calming and insight together, balancing concentration and wisdom evenly — what is this, in the end, but a single recitation of Amitābha's name? In Sanskrit, Amitābha; in Chinese, "measureless light and measureless life." Light is precisely wisdom; life is precisely concentration — light and life being not two different things is precisely the wondrous calming and insight of concentration and wisdom evenly balanced. When we recite a single recitation of the Buddha's name, the mind that recites and the Buddha recited cannot ultimately be found — this is calming. Yet the reciting mind and the recited Buddha remain utterly clear and distinct — this is insight. One should know: precisely when they are utterly clear and distinct, their very substance ultimately cannot be found; and precisely when it ultimately cannot be found, they remain, as ever, utterly clear and distinct — at once calming and insight, at once insight and calming, calming and insight evenly settled, concentration and wisdom not two — this is precisely measureless light and measureless life itself. If a practitioner is able to cultivate calming and insight skillfully through this very gate of reciting the Buddha's name, this life will not pass by in emptiness, and buddhahood is certain to be accomplished, whether in this present life or a life to come.

II. Practice Amid Every Activity and Circumstance 歷緣對境修

Chinese Source

〖文〗復次，第二明歷緣對境修止觀者：端身常坐，乃為入道之勝要，而有累之身，必涉事緣，若隨緣對境而不修習止觀，是則修心有間絕，結業觸處而起，豈得疾與佛法相應。若於一切時中常修定慧方便，當知是人必能通達一切佛法。云何名歷緣修止觀？所言緣者，謂六種緣：一行、二住、三坐、四臥、五作作、六言語。云何名對境修止觀？所言境者，謂六塵境、一眼對色、二耳對聲、三鼻對香、四舌對味、五身對觸、六意對法。行者約此十二事中修止觀故，名為歷緣對境修止觀也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Further, second, explaining practice carried into every activity and circumstance: sitting upright at length is the most excellent essential for entering the path — yet this encumbered body must inevitably engage in worldly affairs, and if one fails to cultivate calming and insight while following along with whatever circumstance and object one meets, the cultivation of the mind is left broken with gaps, and karmic entanglement arises wherever one turns — how, then, could one hope to accord swiftly with the Buddhadharma? But if one constantly cultivates this same skillful means of concentration and wisdom at every single moment, one should know that such a person will certainly come to penetrate the whole of the Buddhadharma. What is meant by practice carried into every activity? "Activity" here means six kinds: walking, standing, sitting, lying down, working, and speaking. What is meant by practice carried into every circumstance? "Circumstance" here means the six sense-objects: the eye meeting form, the ear meeting sound, the nose meeting smell, the tongue meeting taste, the body meeting touch, and the mind meeting mental objects. A practitioner who cultivates calming and insight across these twelve matters is said to practice calming and insight carried into every activity and circumstance.

〖講〗上來正修章中共分二大科，一坐中修，二歷緣對境修。初坐中修分五種不同……今明第二歷緣對境修止觀也，但於未明之先，先須識其大意，然後一一所修，處處與佛海相應矣！當知現前所有種種境界，悉是自心中之見相二分，心為能見，境為所見，以能見之見分，見於所見之相分。當知見相二分，本無其體，悉由自證分證自證分而為其體，當體即空假中，不思議圓三諦理。由此觀之，則物物頭頭悉是妙諦，所以云山河及大地，全露法王身，羽毛並麟甲，普現諸三昧。又云舉一莖草，即是丈六金身，翠竹黃花，無非般若……然理雖如是，事必漸顯，故歷緣對境，一一須如實而修，方免入海算沙，望洋興嘆……而有累之身，必涉事緣，二句，顯其不能端身常坐之義。因吾人生於世間，有此幻軀，處於生活場上，終日為衣、食、住、等所逼迫，必涉世事塵緣，欲常坐修道，誠為難事，故必須隨緣對境而修止觀，則不致空過……若於一切時中，歷一切緣，對一切境之中，時時修於止觀，常常安心定慧，以如是方便，無論歷何緣，對何境，即須返觀自心，推究此境緣，從何處來，由何而去，時時觀察，推其究竟，原無實體，當知是人在塵不染塵，雖和光混俗，不為世事所羈累，即世諦是佛法，即塵勞為佛事，是則真俗融通三昧印，至此所謂終日吃飯，未曾咬著一粒米，終日穿衣，未曾掛著一線絲，惟道是求，惟止觀是修。然雖終日一心求道，而世事仍然不妨做，無礙自在，所謂山仍是山，水還是水，萬境歷歷，雖萬象羅列於前，而心中了無所得，生心無住，即是隨緣對境而修止觀也。能如此，欲期開佛知見，證諸法門，豈難事哉！

Commentary: The actual-practice chapter above divides into two major sections: practice while seated, and practice carried into every activity and circumstance. Practice while seated divided further into five distinct approaches... Now the second, practice carried into every activity and circumstance, is explained — but before explaining it in detail, one must first grasp its general meaning, so that whatever one then practices, everywhere, will accord with the ocean of the Buddhadharma.

One should know that every circumstance presently before us is entirely the seeing-portion and appearing-portion within one's own mind: mind is the seer, the object is what is seen, the seeing-portion capable of seeing meeting the appearing-portion that is seen. One should further know that these two portions, seeing and appearing, have, at root, no substance of their own — both are given substance entirely by the self-witnessing portion and the witness-to-the-self-witnessing portion, their very substance being at once empty, at once conventional, at once the middle: the inconceivable, perfect principle of the three truths. Seen in this light, every single thing, without exception, is itself a wondrous truth — hence it is said that mountains, rivers, and the whole earth entirely reveal the Dharma-king's own body, and every feather and every scale manifests every samādhi everywhere; and again, that lifting up a single blade of grass is itself the sixteen-foot golden body, and green bamboo and yellow chrysanthemum are nothing but prajñā itself...

Yet although the principle is exactly this, the actual matter must still be revealed gradually — hence, amid every activity and circumstance, one must genuinely practice in accordance with this principle at every single point, or else fail to avoid the futility of counting grains of sand in the ocean, sighing helplessly at its vastness.

"Yet this encumbered body must inevitably engage in worldly affairs" — these two lines reveal precisely why one cannot simply sit upright at length forever. Because we are born into this world with this illusory body, situated within the arena of ordinary life, pressed the whole day through by clothing, food, shelter, and the rest, we must inevitably engage in worldly affairs and dusty entanglements — wishing to sit constantly and cultivate the path alone is, in truth, an impossible task. Hence one must cultivate calming and insight while following along with whatever circumstance and object one meets, so that this time does not pass by in emptiness...

If, at every moment, amid every activity, meeting every circumstance, one constantly cultivates calming and insight, constantly settling the mind in concentration and wisdom — through a skillful means of this kind, whatever activity one is engaged in, whatever circumstance one meets, one need only turn to observe one's own mind, investigating where this circumstance actually comes from and where it actually goes, observing this at every moment, tracing it to its ultimate source, where, in fact, it has no real substance at all. One should know that a person who does this remains amid the dust of the world without being stained by that dust — blending their own light with the ordinary world, yet never bound or encumbered by worldly affairs. Conventional truth is itself the Buddhadharma; the very toil of the dusty world is itself the Buddha's own work — this is the seal of samādhi in which the ultimate and the conventional are fully interfused. At this point, one is, as it is said, eating the whole day through without ever once truly biting into a single grain of rice, wearing clothes the whole day through without ever once truly touching a single thread — seeking nothing but the path, cultivating nothing but calming and insight.

Yet even seeking the path with a single mind the whole day through, worldly affairs may still be carried on without hindrance, freely and without obstruction — mountains remain mountains, and rivers remain rivers just as before, every circumstance vivid and distinct — and though the whole teeming array of phenomena is arrayed before one, the mind holds nothing at all within it, giving rise to thought that clings to nothing. This is precisely cultivating calming and insight while following along with whatever circumstance and object one meets. Able to do this, how could opening the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, and realizing every dharma-gate, possibly remain a difficult task?

A. The Six Activities 六緣

1. Walking 行

Chinese Source

〖文〗一行者。若於行時，應作是念：我今為何等事欲行？為煩惱所使，及不善無記事行，即不應行。若非煩惱所使；為善利益如法事，即應行。云何行中修止？若於行時，即知因於行故，則有一切煩惱善惡等法，了知行心，及行中一切法，皆不可得，則妄念心息，是名修止。云何行中修觀？應作是念：由心動身，故有進趣，名之為行。因此行故，則有一切煩惱善惡等法。即當反觀行心，不見相貌，當知行者及行中一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: First, walking. When about to walk, one should think: "For what purpose am I now about to walk?" If it is driven by affliction, or an unwholesome or morally neutral matter, one should not walk. If it is not driven by affliction, but is instead a wholesome, beneficial matter in accordance with the Dharma, one should walk. What is cultivating calming while walking? While walking, knowing that because of this walking, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises — fully understanding that neither the mind that walks nor any dharma within this walking can ultimately be found — idle thought comes to rest: this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight while walking? One should think: it is because the mind moves the body that there is advancing forward, called "walking"; because of this walking, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises. Turning at once to observe this very mind that walks, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the walker nor any dharma within this walking has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗此下正當於行、住、坐、臥、等十二事中，一一解釋修止觀之相。一行者，即於行路時，修習止觀。倘若於行路時，但於未開步之先，即應作觀，中起如是之念頭，我今為何等事而欲行路耶？若此行，是為煩惱所驅使，及作殺、盜、淫、妄之不善事，或無記事而行者，即不應行。若此行非煩惱所使，乃是為善利益，如法事而行，或為修戒定慧，燒香禮拜，散花供佛聽經聞法等事而行者，即應行，總之諸惡莫作，眾善奉行者，即應行，否則不應行。言行中修止者，所謂於行路之時，即能了知因於行路之故，則現有一切善惡等法，若無此行，即無一切行中事，即所謂萬法唯行。又復應知，行與不行，唯心所現，因心而有，若不起此一念行走之心，即不起所行之足，及有所行之路，可見此心，以為能行。足所行，路之引機，心若不行，則足亦不能行，又安有所行之路者哉！若知境本無生因心有，則萬法唯心，一切法趣此心，是趣不過，若知心本無生因境有，則萬法唯行，一切法趣此行，是趣不過。推其究竟，能無一定之能相，所無一定之所相，能所本寂，是名修止。止既如是，觀亦復然，故古來祖師，專行常行三昧。

Commentary: What follows now explains the mark of cultivating calming and insight, one by one, through each of the twelve matters — walking, standing, sitting, lying down, and the rest. First, walking: cultivating calming and insight while walking along a road. Before even taking the first step, one should already contemplate, giving rise to a thought like this: "For what purpose am I now about to walk this road?" If this walking is driven by affliction, or by an unwholesome act such as killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, or false speech, or by some morally neutral matter, one should not walk. If this walking is not driven by affliction, but is instead for the sake of some wholesome, beneficial matter in accordance with the Dharma — walking to cultivate precepts, concentration, and wisdom, to burn incense, to bow, to scatter flowers in offering to the Buddha, to listen to a sūtra or hear the Dharma taught — one should walk. In sum: committing no evil, doing every good — this is when one should walk; otherwise, one should not.

As for cultivating calming while walking: this means that, while walking along a road, one is able to understand that, because of this very walking, every kind of wholesome or unwholesome dharma now appears — without this walking, none of these matters within walking would exist at all, which is to say, every dharma is walking alone. One should further know that walking and not walking are both manifested by mind alone, existing only because of mind — if this single thought of walking did not arise, the foot that walks would not move, nor would there be any road to be walked upon at all. From this it can be seen that this mind is what makes walking possible; the foot is what walks, and the road is merely the occasion drawing it forward. If the mind does not walk, the foot cannot walk either — how, then, could there be any road walked upon at all? If one knows that an object has, from the start, no arising of its own, existing only because of mind, then every dharma is mind alone, and every dharma converges on this mind, going no further. If one knows that mind, too, has, from the start, no arising of its own, existing only because of its object, then every dharma is walking alone, and every dharma converges on this walking, going no further. Traced to its ultimate source, the subject has no fixed mark of its own as subject, and the object has no fixed mark of its own as object — subject and object being, at root, both still — this is cultivating calming. As calming is like this, so too is insight — hence the patriarchs of old devoted themselves to the constant-walking samādhi.

2. Standing 住

Chinese Source

〖文〗二住者。若於住時，應作是念：我今為何等事欲住？若為諸煩惱及不善無記事住，即不應住；若為善利益事，即應住。云何住中修止？若於住時，即知因於住故，則有一切煩惱善惡等法，了知住心、及住中一切法皆不可得，則妄念心息，是名修止。云何住中修觀？應作是念，由心駐身，故名為住，因此住故，則有一切煩惱善惡等法，則當反觀住心，不見相貌，當知住者及住中一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Second, standing. When about to stand still, one should think: "For what purpose am I now about to stand?" If it is for the sake of some affliction, or an unwholesome or morally neutral matter, one should not stand. If it is for the sake of some wholesome, beneficial matter, one should stand. What is cultivating calming while standing? While standing, knowing that because of this standing, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises — fully understanding that neither the mind that stands nor any dharma within this standing can ultimately be found — idle thought comes to rest: this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight while standing? One should think: it is because the mind settles the body in place that there is what is called "standing"; because of this standing, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises. Turning to observe this very mind that stands, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the one who stands nor any dharma within this standing has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗此住中修止觀。住即止住，即安住而非行之謂。由心駐身者，以其身由心所使，心住身則住，若心不住，則身亦不住矣。畢竟空寂者，謂能住之心，及所住之法，一一悉皆離四句，絕百非，當體本寂，與真空相應，故云畢竟空寂。餘如文。

Commentary: This explains cultivating calming and insight while standing. "Standing" here means resting in place — that is, settling still, as distinct from walking. "It is because the mind settles the body in place" means that the body is governed by the mind: when the mind settles, the body settles; if the mind does not settle, the body cannot settle either. "Ultimately empty and still" means that both the mind that stands and the dharma within which it stands are, in every respect, free of the four extremes and beyond every hundred forms of negation — their very substance being, at root, still, according perfectly with true emptiness — hence it is called "ultimately empty and still." The rest is as explained in the text.

3. Sitting 坐

Chinese Source

〖文〗三坐者。若於坐時，應作是念：我今為何等事欲坐？若為諸煩惱，及不善無記事等，即不應坐；為善利益事，則應坐。云何坐中修止？若於坐時，則當了知因於坐故，則有一切煩惱善惡等法，而無一法可得，則妄念不生，是名修止。云何坐中修觀？應作是念：由心所念壘腳安身，因此則有一切善惡等法，故名為坐，反觀坐心，不見相貌，當知坐者及坐中一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Third, sitting. When about to sit, one should think: "For what purpose am I now about to sit?" If it is for the sake of some affliction, or an unwholesome or morally neutral matter, one should not sit. If it is for the sake of some wholesome, beneficial matter, one should sit. What is cultivating calming while sitting? While sitting, understanding that because of this sitting, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises, and yet not a single one of them can ultimately be found — idle thought fails to arise: this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight while sitting? One should think: it is because the mind intends to fold the legs and settle the body that there is what is called "sitting"; because of this, every kind of wholesome or unwholesome dharma arises. Turning to observe this very mind that sits, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the one who sits nor any dharma within this sitting has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗由心所念，壘腳安身者，謂心生則種種法生，心起念故，方使雙腳壘疊安置於身，收足不行也。所謂結跏趺坐，若能於坐中，念一句阿彌陀佛名號，能所雙泯而雙歷，即是修止觀。因止觀二法，不離自心，念佛即是念自心，自心即是止觀。此心從無始以來，不昏不散，不沈不浮，不散故空空寂寂，即止，不昏故歷歷明明，即觀。若能老老實實執持一句彌陀名號，則自心之止觀，自然顯現矣！然此坐之一字，務須簡別，不可一概而論，若因遊戲快樂，於歌舞場中而坐，則不應坐。若為了生死，求佛道，於說法處，修道場中，聽經辦道而坐，是則應坐。惡者不應坐即止，善者當應坐即觀，故須簡別之也。

Commentary: "It is because the mind intends to fold the legs and settle the body" means that once mind arises, every dharma follows from it — the mind giving rise to this intention is precisely what causes both legs to be folded and the body settled in place, the feet drawn in and no longer walking. This is what is called sitting in the lotus posture. If one is able, while sitting, to recite a single recitation of Amitābha Buddha's name, so that subject and object are both effaced and yet both remain vividly present at once, this is itself cultivating calming and insight — for calming and insight are never apart from one's own mind: reciting the Buddha's name is itself being mindful of one's own mind, and one's own mind is itself calming and insight. This mind, from beginningless time, has been neither dull nor scattered, neither sinking nor floating — not scattered, hence utterly empty and still, which is calming; not dull, hence utterly clear and distinct, which is insight. If one can, honestly and wholeheartedly, hold fast to a single recitation of Amitābha's name, one's own mind's calming and insight will manifest of itself!

Yet this word "sitting" must, above all, be carefully distinguished, and not spoken of as though it were all one thing. If one sits for the sake of idle amusement, in some hall of song and dance, one should not sit there. If one sits in order to resolve birth-and-death and seek the buddha-path, in a place where the Dharma is taught, a place of practice, listening to sūtras and taking up the path — this is when one should sit. Where sitting would be evil, one should not sit — this is calming; where sitting would be wholesome, one should sit — this is insight; hence this distinction must be carefully made.

4. Lying Down 臥

Chinese Source

〖文〗四臥者。於臥時應作是念：我今為何等事欲臥？若為不善放逸等事，則不應臥，若為調和四大故臥，則應如師子王臥。云何臥中修止？若於寢息，則當了知因於臥故，則有一切善惡等法，而無一法可得，則妄念不起，是名修止。云何臥中修觀？應作是念：由於勞乏，即便昏闇，放縱六情，因此則有一切煩惱善惡等法。即當反觀臥心，不見相貌，當知臥者及臥中一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Fourth, lying down. When about to lie down, one should think: "For what purpose am I now about to lie down?" If it is for some unwholesome, indulgent matter, one should not lie down; if it is to regulate the four great elements, one should lie down as the lion-king lies down. What is cultivating calming while lying down? While resting, understanding that because of this lying down, every kind of wholesome or unwholesome dharma arises, and yet not a single one of them can ultimately be found — idle thought fails to arise: this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight while lying down? One should think: because of fatigue and weariness, the mind grows dim and dark, and the six senses are let loose unrestrained — because of this, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises. Turning at once to observe this very mind that lies down, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the one who lies down nor any dharma within this lying down has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗行、住、坐、臥、四威儀中，咸能修習止觀，故一切時，一切處，無不成為止觀之功用矣。然臥之為法，本為不善，何以能修止觀耶？因臥能調和色身，補養精神之不足……若因為調和自身之四大而臥者，即應如獅子王臥。言四大者，謂吾人之身，攬外界地、水、火、風，而成內身四大……一地大，地以堅礙為性……二水大。水以潤濕為性……三火大，火以燥熱為性……四風大，風以動為性……當知四大如毒蛇，若一大不調，則有一百零一病，故須調和適宜之。若行者，欲調和此四大故，臥則應如師子王而臥，師王之臥非實昏也，以其惺惺寂寂，寂寂惺惺，非同常人之恣縱睡眠，熟寢而臥也。云何臥中而修止觀耶？若於寢息時，須了達因臥故，而有戒、定、慧之善法，殺、盜、淫等之惡法，若微細推究觀察之，畢竟空無實體，是知臥中求於生滅、迷悟、去來，了不可得，如是則妄念不起，故名修止。於臥中修觀者，謂當正臥之時，心中應作如是念，由於勞乏，即便昏闇，放縱六情。六情者，即眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、意之六根也。因此則有一切善不善法，爾時即當返觀此一念能臥之心，不見相貌，當知能臥及所臥中一切法，其體空寂，畢竟了不可得，如是行者，是名修觀。

Commentary: Calming and insight can be cultivated in all four bodily postures — walking, standing, sitting, and lying down — so that at every moment, in every place, without exception, all become the very working of calming and insight. Yet lying down is, in itself, generally something unwholesome — how, then, can calming and insight be cultivated through it? Because lying down is able to regulate the physical body, replenishing what the spirit lacks...

If one lies down in order to regulate one's own four great elements, one should lie down as the lion-king lies down. As for the "four great elements": our own body draws together the outward elements of earth, water, fire, and wind to form the four great elements of the inward body... The great element of earth has solidity and obstruction as its nature... the great element of water has moistness as its nature... the great element of fire has dryness and heat as its nature... the great element of wind has movement as its nature... One should know that the four great elements are like venomous snakes: should even one of them fall out of balance, a hundred and one illnesses can follow, hence they must be carefully regulated into harmony.

If a practitioner wishes to regulate these four great elements, one should lie down as the lion-king lies down — the lion-king's own repose is not truly dull sleep at all, but alert yet still, still yet alert, entirely unlike an ordinary person's unrestrained, deep slumber. How, then, can calming and insight be cultivated while lying down? While resting, one must fully understand that, because of this lying down, wholesome dharmas such as precepts, concentration, and wisdom, and unwholesome dharmas such as killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct, all arise — and investigating and observing this minutely, none of it, in the end, has any real substance at all. From this one knows that seeking arising and ceasing, delusion and awakening, coming and going, within this act of lying down, none of it can ultimately be found — and in this way idle thought fails to arise, hence it is called cultivating calming.

As for cultivating insight while lying down: while actually lying down, a thought like this should arise in the mind: because of fatigue and weariness, the mind grows dim and dark, and the six senses are let loose unrestrained. "The six senses" are precisely the six faculties — eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. Because of this, every kind of wholesome or unwholesome dharma arises — and at that moment one should turn to observe this very thought, this mind capable of lying down, finding it to have no appearance at all; knowing that both the one who lies down and every dharma within this lying down are, in their very substance, empty and still, ultimately unfindable at all — a practitioner who does this is said to be cultivating insight.

5. Working 作

Chinese Source

〖文〗五作者。若作時應作是念：我今為何等事欲如此作？若為不善無記等事，即不應作；若為善利益事，即應作。云何名作中修止？若於作時，即當了知因於作故，則有一切善惡等法，而無一法可得，則妄念不起，是名修止。云何名作時修觀？應作是念：由心運於身手造作諸事，因此則有一切善惡等法，故名為作。反觀作心，不見相貌，當知作者及作中一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Fifth, working. When about to do some work, one should think: "For what purpose am I now about to do this?" If it is for some unwholesome or morally neutral matter, one should not do it; if it is for some wholesome, beneficial matter, one should do it. What is cultivating calming while working? While working, understanding that because of this work, every kind of wholesome or unwholesome dharma arises, and yet not a single one of them can ultimately be found — idle thought fails to arise: this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight while working? One should think: it is because the mind moves the body and hands to carry out various tasks that there is what is called "working"; because of this, every kind of wholesome or unwholesome dharma arises. Turning to observe this very mind that works, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the one who works nor any dharma within this work has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗此明第五種於工作之時，亦可修習止觀。所謂運水搬柴，迎賓送客，穿衣吃飯，咳吐掉臂，皆不可棄修止觀。作謂造作，當知作之一字，實為眾善之首，萬惡之魁，故行者，於未作之先，務須審察籌量，微細推究，所作之事，是損耶？益耶？若此作於人有益，當應勇猛精進而作，若於人有損，即須止之，不可應作。反觀作心者，即反觀能作之心，不在內外中間，不見相貌，能作所作，畢竟空寂，故名為觀。又復應知作之一字，能力甚大，不特人天因果，因造作而成，抑且上至四聖因果，亦因造作而有，若不造作，則了無一物。謂是心是地獄，是心作地獄，是心是佛，是心作佛，足見流轉生死，安樂妙常，唯此作字，更非他物。是知善惡以心為本，心為造作之本，以其心動故身動，身動故境動，若心不動，則境亦不動矣！古時有鐵匠者，終歲打鐵，製造兵器為生活，道人憐而憫之，化其念佛，每一擊念一句佛，彼遵命而行，久而久之，功夫成熟，所謂水到渠成也，至臨命終時，遂說偈曰：「釘釘鐺鐺，久煉成鋼，江上太平，我往西方。」以此觀之，念佛打鐵即是修止觀，尚希諸仁者不可輕而忽之。

Commentary: This explains the fifth: calming and insight can also be cultivated while working. Whether carrying water and fetching firewood, greeting guests and seeing them off, dressing and eating, coughing or swinging the arms — none of it need be set apart from cultivating calming and insight. "Work" means undertaking some task; one should know that this single word "work" is, in truth, the very head of every good and the very chief of every evil, and so, before undertaking any task, a practitioner must carefully examine and weigh it, investigating minutely whether the task in question does harm or brings benefit. If this work benefits others, one should carry it out with courage and diligence; if it harms others, one must stop and never undertake it.

"Turning to observe this very mind that works" means turning to observe this very mind capable of work, finding it neither inside, outside, nor in between, with no appearance at all — subject and object, worker and work, ultimately empty and still — hence it is called insight. One should further know that this single word "work" carries very great power indeed — not only are the causes and effects of the human and heavenly realms brought about through undertaking action, but even the causes and effects of the four sagely realms above them come about through undertaking action as well; without undertaking action, nothing at all would exist. It is said: this mind is hell, this mind creates hell; this mind is buddha, this mind creates buddhahood — enough to show that both the flow of birth-and-death and the wondrous, ever-abiding peace of liberation come from nothing other than this single word, "work." From this we know that good and evil are rooted in mind, and mind is the very root of all undertaking — for when the mind moves, the body moves, and when the body moves, the surrounding world moves; if the mind does not move, the surrounding world, too, does not move!

Long ago there was a blacksmith who spent his whole life forging iron, making weapons for his living. A wandering teacher, taking pity on him, converted him to reciting the Buddha's name — with every strike of the hammer, reciting a single recitation of the Buddha's name. The blacksmith followed this instruction, and in time his practice matured — as it is said, "when the water arrives, the channel forms of itself" — and when his life was about to end, he uttered this verse: "Clang, clang, clang, clang — long forged, it has become fine steel; all is at peace upon the river, and I now go to the Western Land." Seen in this light, reciting the Buddha's name while forging iron is itself cultivating calming and insight — may every reader of good will take care never to regard this lightly.

6. Speaking 語

Chinese Source

〖文〗六語者。若於語時，應作是念：我今為何等事欲語？若隨諸煩惱，為論說不善無記等事而語，即不應語，若為善利益事，即應語。云何名語中修止？若於語時，即知因此語故，則有一切煩惱善惡等法，了知語心及語中一切煩惱善不善法，皆不可得，則妄念心息，是名修止。云何語中修觀？應作是念：由心覺觀鼓動氣息，衝於咽喉唇舌齒齶。故出音聲語言，因此語故，則有一切善惡等法，故名為語。反觀語心，不見相貌，當知語者及語中一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。如上六義修習止觀，隨時相應用之，一一皆有前五番修止觀意，如上所說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Sixth, speaking. When about to speak, one should think: "For what purpose am I now about to speak?" If it is driven by affliction, speaking of some unwholesome or morally neutral matter, one should not speak; if it is for some wholesome, beneficial matter, one should speak. What is cultivating calming while speaking? While speaking, knowing that because of this speech, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises — fully understanding that neither the mind that speaks nor any afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma within this speech can ultimately be found — idle thought comes to rest: this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight while speaking? One should think: it is because the mind's own discursive activity stirs the breath, striking against throat, lips, tongue, teeth, and palate, that sound and speech are produced; because of this speech, every kind of wholesome or unwholesome dharma arises. Turning to observe this very mind that speaks, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the one who speaks nor any dharma within this speech has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight. Practicing calming and insight through these six activities above, applying them as fitting to each moment, every one of them already carries within it the intent of the five approaches explained earlier, exactly as described above.

〖講〗第六口語言時，亦能修止觀。所謂麤言及細語，皆歸第一義，世間一切治世語言及諸喜、怒、哀、樂，悉與實相不相違背……云何名語中修止耶？所言語中修止者，謂若於語時，即應推究。了知因此語故，則有一切煩惱善惡等法……煩惱雖多，不出三種，一見思，迷理起分別為之見，對境起貪愛為之思。二塵沙，謂眾生心中之妄念習氣，若空中塵，河中沙之多也。三無明，即無所明了，昏闇晦昧之謂……以三止止伏惑，以三觀智，照破三惑，發本三智，顯自性三諦，則將本地風光，徹底顯露矣。然何能於語言修止耶？即於語時，不觀於所，返觀於能，了知能語之心，了不可得，則語中所言一切善惡等法，皆不可得。以其離能無所故，如是能所，悉皆了不可得，則虛妄之心念息滅，是名修止。云何語中修觀？應作是念：死人亦具一口，何以不能語言，則我之講話，皆由覺觀鼓動氣息等，故有言語音聲。覺觀者，麤心在緣為覺，細心分別為觀。是知語言，由內鼓動氣息，衝於咽喉唇舌齶，故有音聲，否則欲言，豈可得乎？當知口不能言，因心而有言。若口能言者，則世之啞子，亦應能言，彼何而不言耶？足見言之非口也明矣！以其動之於心，方能言之於口，若返觀能言之心，則非內外中間，亦非青、黃、赤、白，求其心體，不見相貌，當體即空即假即中，不特能語之心，即空假中，抑所語中之一切善惡等言，亦即空即假即中，畢竟空寂，如是則名語中修觀。昔慧可二祖，至達摩初祖處求開示，以刀斷臂，痛不可忍，乃祈初祖言：「請師與吾安心。」祖曰：「將心來與汝安。」大師答言：「覓心了不可得。」初祖曰：「吾與汝安心竟。」此即與止觀畢竟空寂之義相符合，修止觀之門徑甚繁，若教以念一句彌陀，六字洪名，至簡且易，若能不起思量分別，驀直念去，即與止觀相應，所以真修行者，切勿好高騖遠，一門深入，如鼠之嚙棺，今日明日，必能透出，否則東嚙西嚙，終不能出矣！

Commentary: This sixth explains that calming and insight can be cultivated even while speaking with the mouth. Coarse words and refined speech alike return to the same first principle; every kind of ordinary, worldly speech, and every joy, anger, sorrow, and delight it expresses, is never truly at odds with true reality itself...

What is cultivating calming while speaking? This means that, while speaking, one should investigate at once, understanding that because of this speech, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises... Affliction, however many forms it takes, does not exceed three: first, views and thought — confusion about principle giving rise to discriminating views, craving arising toward an object giving rise to attached thought; second, dust-and-sand afflictions — the sentient mind's own idle thoughts and habitual tendencies, as numerous as dust motes in the air or grains of sand in a river; third, ignorance — that is, a state of having nothing clearly understood at all, dark and obscured.

...Through the three kinds of calming, one subdues these afflictions; through the wisdom of the three contemplations, one illuminates and breaks through these three kinds of affliction, bringing forth the three kinds of wisdom inherent from the start, revealing the three truths of one's own nature — and in this way, the original scenery of one's own ground is fully and thoroughly revealed. But how, then, can calming actually be cultivated through speech? While speaking, rather than observing the object spoken of, one turns instead to observe the very subject that speaks — fully understanding that this mind capable of speech ultimately cannot be found, and so whatever wholesome or unwholesome matter is spoken of within this speech likewise cannot ultimately be found, since apart from a subject there is no object at all. In this way, both subject and object are, without exception, ultimately unfindable, and the illusory mind of idle thought comes to rest — this is called cultivating calming.

What is cultivating insight while speaking? One should think: a dead person, too, still has a mouth — why, then, can a dead person not speak? My own speaking must, then, come entirely from discursive activity stirring the breath and the rest, and so sound and speech arise. "Discursive activity" means: a coarse mind engaging its object is called awareness; a fine mind discriminating within it is called reflection. From this we know that speech comes from an inward stirring of the breath, striking against throat, lips, tongue, and palate, and so sound is produced — otherwise, how could one possibly wish to speak at all? One should know that the mouth, by itself, cannot speak; speech exists only because of mind. If the mouth alone could speak, then the mute of the world would also be able to speak — why, then, can they not? This is enough to make clear that speech does not belong to the mouth at all. It is only because the mind is stirred that the mouth is then able to speak.

Turning to observe this very mind capable of speech, one finds it neither inside, outside, nor in between, neither blue, yellow, red, nor white — seeking its own substance, it has no appearance at all, its very substance being at once empty, at once conventional, at once the middle. Not only is the mind capable of speech itself empty, conventional, and the middle — every wholesome or unwholesome word spoken within that speech is likewise empty, conventional, and the middle, ultimately empty and still. This is what is called cultivating insight while speaking.

Long ago, the Second Patriarch Huike went before the First Patriarch Bodhidharma seeking instruction, and, the pain unbearable, cut off his own arm with a knife, and begged the First Patriarch: "Master, please settle my mind for me." The Patriarch said: "Bring me this mind, and I will settle it for you." The great master answered: "Searching for the mind, it cannot ultimately be found." The First Patriarch said: "Then I have already settled your mind for you." This accords precisely with the meaning of calming and insight as ultimately empty and still. The gateways for cultivating calming and insight are exceedingly numerous — but were one taught simply to recite a single recitation of Amitābha, these six great characters, this is simplest and easiest of all: if one can, without giving rise to any discriminating thought, press straight ahead reciting it, this already accords with calming and insight. This is why a genuine practitioner should never chase after lofty, distant goals, but should enter deeply through a single gate instead — like a rat gnawing through a coffin: gnawing at the very same spot, today and tomorrow, it will certainly break through in the end; but gnawing here and there at random, it will never break through at all!

B. The Six Sense-Faculties 六根門中修止觀

1. Seeing 眼見色

Chinese Source

〖文〗次六根門中修止觀者：一眼見色時修止者。隨見色時，如水中月，無有定實，若見順情之色，不起貪愛；若見違情之色，不起瞋惱；若見非違非順之色，不起無明及諸亂想，是名修止。云何名眼見色時修觀？應作是念：隨有所見，即相空寂。所以者何？於彼根塵空明之中，各無所見，亦無分別，和合因緣，出生眼識，次生意識，即能分別種種諸色，因此則有一切煩惱善惡等法。即當反觀念色之心，不見相貌，當知見者及一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Next, cultivating calming and insight through the gate of the six faculties. First, cultivating calming when the eye sees form. Whatever form is seen, know it to be like the moon reflected in water, with nothing fixed or real about it: if one sees a form agreeable to one's own feelings, no craving attachment arises; if one sees a form disagreeable to one's own feelings, no anger or vexation arises; if one sees a form neither agreeable nor disagreeable, no ignorance or disordered thought arises — this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight when the eye sees form? One should think: whatever is seen, its very mark is empty and still. Why is this? Within that meeting of faculty, object, space, and light, there is, in itself, nothing seen and no discrimination at all — it is only through the coming-together of causes and conditions that visual consciousness is produced, and, following it, mental consciousness, which is then able to discriminate among the various forms; because of this, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises. Turning at once to observe this very mind that thinks of form, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the seer nor any dharma within this seeing has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗上明六緣修止觀已竟，此第二明六根對六塵修止觀也。初心之人，修習止觀，欲與止觀相應，得其利益，頗不易易，以其止觀不與煩惱相應故。務須死心蹋地放下一切，能所不二，不起分別，忘身忘體，而後可當知止觀二法，是破執著之利斧，亡情見之鋼刀，趣菩提之階梯，除煩惱之妙藥也……一眼見色時修止觀者，隨眼所見種種色相雖多，要不出三種：一顯色……二形色……三表色……又阿毘曇論復有三種不同：一可見可對色……二不可見可對色……三不可見無對色……行者修止，若隨眼所見，如上所述之種種色，了知悉是空中華，水中月，有即非有，非有而有，無有定實。若見順情可悅之色，當作如幻如化，如泡如影，不起貪愛。若見違情可憎之色，不起瞋惱，若見非違非順中庸之色，即不憎不愛，不起無明，及諸亂想，是名修止。當知前來所明之顯色形色表色等種種色中，一一皆具違順非違非順之不同，行者自詳之可也。

Commentary: The six activities above are now complete; this second part explains cultivating calming and insight through the six faculties meeting the six sense-objects. For a beginning practitioner, cultivating calming and insight so that it truly accords with practice and actually yields benefit is far from easy, since calming and insight do not, by nature, accord with affliction — one must resolve utterly, letting everything go, subject and object no longer two, giving rise to no discrimination, forgetting the very body itself, before any real benefit follows. One should know that calming and insight are the sharp axe that breaks attachment, the steel blade that destroys emotional views, the very stairway to bodhi, the wondrous medicine that removes affliction...

As for cultivating calming and insight when the eye sees form: whatever the eye sees, however many forms there may be, they do not exceed three kinds — first, manifest color... second, shape... third, expressive form... The Abhidharma treatises further distinguish three other kinds: visible and resistant form... invisible yet resistant form... and invisible, non-resistant form...

A practitioner cultivating calming, whatever form the eye happens to see among those described above, understands it entirely to be a flower conjured in the sky, the moon reflected in water — seemingly existing yet not truly existing, not truly existing yet seemingly there, with nothing fixed or real about it at all. If one sees a form agreeable and pleasing to one's own feelings, one should regard it as illusory, like a bubble or a shadow, giving rise to no craving attachment. If one sees a form disagreeable and repugnant to one's own feelings, one gives rise to no anger or vexation. If one sees a form neither agreeable nor disagreeable, a neutral form, one is neither repelled nor drawn, giving rise to no ignorance and no disordered thought at all — this is cultivating calming. One should know that within every kind of manifest color, shape, and expressive form explained above, each one carries its own distinction of agreeable, disagreeable, and neutral — the reader may work this out in detail for themselves.

〖講〗上來明眼見色修止已竟，次明見色修觀。行者應作是念，隨有所見，違順中庸之色，悉如空華水月，夢幻泡影，其相本自空寂，了無纖塵之法可得也……當知能見所見，其性空寂，所謂離能所、絕對待，畢竟空寂，故名修觀……如阿那律陀因發憤而失其雙目，世尊憐而憫之，命修樂見照明金剛三昧，遂得天眼，不因肉眼而能觀見三千大千世界，如觀掌中果，此即從眼見色，修止觀而得益。可見一切處，皆可修止觀，所謂根根塵塵，悉是真實圓通；物物頭頭，盡是菩提之道。

Commentary: Cultivating calming when the eye sees form is now explained; next, cultivating insight when the eye sees form. A practitioner should think: whatever is seen, agreeable, disagreeable, or neutral, is entirely like a flower conjured in the sky or the moon reflected in water, like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, or a shadow — its very mark being, from the start, empty and still, with not the least trace of any dharma there to be found at all... One should know that both the seer and the seen are, in their very nature, empty and still — free of both subject and object, beyond every kind of relative opposition, ultimately empty and still. This is called cultivating insight.

Just as Aniruddha, having lost both his eyes through excessive exertion, was, out of the World-Honored One's own compassion, instructed to cultivate the vajra samādhi of joyfully seeing and illuminating clarity, and thereby attained the heavenly eye — able, without relying on the fleshly eye at all, to observe the whole trichiliocosm as clearly as an object held in the palm of the hand. This is precisely gaining benefit from cultivating calming and insight through the eye's own seeing of form. From this it can be seen that calming and insight can be cultivated everywhere at all — every faculty, every dust-object, is, without exception, true perfect penetration; every single thing, without exception, is itself the very path of bodhi.

2. Hearing 耳聞聲

Chinese Source

〖文〗二耳聞聲時修止者。隨所聞聲，即知聲如響相，若聞順情之聲，不起愛心；違情之聲，不起瞋心；非違非順之聲，不起分別心，是名修止。云何聞聲中修觀？應作是念：隨所聞聲，空無所有，但從根塵和合，生於耳識，次意識生，強起分別，因此即有一切煩惱善惡等法，故名聞聲。反觀聞聲之心，不見相貌，當知聞者及一切法，畢竟空寂，是名為觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Second, cultivating calming when the ear hears sound. Whatever sound is heard, know it to be like an echo: if one hears a sound agreeable to one's own feelings, no attached fondness arises; a sound disagreeable to one's own feelings, no anger arises; a sound neither agreeable nor disagreeable, no discriminating thought arises — this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight while hearing sound? One should think: whatever sound is heard, it is empty, with nothing there at all — it arises only through the meeting of faculty and object, producing auditory consciousness, followed by mental consciousness, which then forcibly gives rise to discrimination; because of this, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises, and this is called "hearing sound." Turning to observe this very mind that hears sound, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the hearer nor any dharma has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗此第二明耳對聲塵修止觀。即知聲如響相，一切聲皆如山谷之答響，非是真實，不過幻妄稱相而已……當知一切眾生，為煩惱所使，終日循聲流轉，不能反聞聞自性，若能反觀聞之心，不見相貌，聞者及所聞一切法，畢竟空寂，所謂反聞聞自性，性成無上道者此也。楞嚴耳根圓通云：「初於聞中，入流亡所。所入既寂，動靜二相，了然不生。如是漸增，聞所聞盡，盡聞不住。覺所覺空，空覺極圓。空所空滅，生滅既滅，寂滅現前，忽然超越，世出世間，十方圓明。」此正與此觀中，能聞所聞，畢竟空寂之理相符，故名修觀。

Commentary: This second part explains cultivating calming and insight through the ear meeting the dust of sound. Knowing sound to be like an echo means that every sound is exactly like an echo answering back from a mountain valley — not truly real at all, merely an illusory appearance given a name...

One should know that every sentient being, driven by affliction, spends the whole day following sound outward, unable to turn hearing back to hear their own true nature. If one is able to turn and observe this very mind that hears, finding it to have no appearance at all — both the hearer and everything heard being, in the end, empty and still — this is precisely what is meant by "turning hearing back to hear one's own nature, and this nature becoming the unsurpassed path." The Śūraṅgama Sūtra's own account of the ear-faculty's perfect penetration says: "At first, within hearing itself, one enters the stream and forgets the object heard. Once what is entered into is itself stilled, the two marks of motion and stillness no longer arise at all, utterly clear. Increasing gradually in this way, both the hearing and what is heard are exhausted; and even this exhaustion of hearing does not remain fixed. Awareness and what is aware of become empty; this very emptiness of awareness becomes utterly perfect. Emptiness and what is emptied are themselves dissolved; arising-and-ceasing having thus ceased, still cessation manifests before one — and suddenly one transcends both the mundane and the transmundane, perfectly bright throughout the ten directions." This accords precisely with this contemplation's own principle — that the hearer and the heard are, in the end, empty and still — hence it is called cultivating insight.

3. Smelling 鼻嗅香

Chinese Source

〖文〗三鼻嗅香時修止者。隨所聞香，即知如燄不實，若聞順情之香，不起著心，違情之臭，不起瞋心；非違非順之香，不生亂念，是名修止。云何名聞香中修觀？應作是念：我今聞香，虛誑無實。所以者何？根塵合故，而生鼻識，次生意識，強取香相，因此則有一切煩惱善惡等法，故名聞香。反觀聞香之心，不見相貌，當知聞香及一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Third, cultivating calming when the nose smells a scent. Whatever scent is smelled, know it to be like a mirage, unreal: if one smells a fragrance agreeable to one's own feelings, no clinging arises; an odor disagreeable to one's own feelings, no anger arises; a scent neither agreeable nor disagreeable, no disordered thought arises — this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight when smelling a scent? One should think: this scent I now smell is illusory, with nothing real about it. Why is this? It is only through the meeting of faculty and object that olfactory consciousness arises, followed by mental consciousness, which forcibly seizes upon the mark of the scent; because of this, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises, and this is called "smelling a scent." Turning to observe this very mind that smells the scent, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the smelling nor any dharma has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗第三明鼻嗅香時修止觀。謂行者隨所聞一切香氣……然此香塵，有隨緣之能，用之於善則善，用之於惡則惡，可見此香之一字，實昇沈之因緣，善惡之根本也……知因緣和合，虛妄有生，因緣別離，虛妄名滅，生滅名妄，滅妄名真，如是反觀，此能聞之心，竟在何處？微細推究，非內非外非中間，無有方隅，亦無形相。當知能聞所聞，其性空寂，所謂離能所、絕對待，畢竟空寂，故名修觀。

Commentary: This third part explains cultivating calming and insight when the nose smells a scent. Whatever scent a practitioner happens to smell... yet this dust of scent has the capacity to follow conditions either way: used for good, it becomes good; used for evil, it becomes evil — from which it can be seen that this single word "scent" is, in truth, the very cause and condition of both rising and falling, the very root of both good and evil...

Knowing that the coming-together of causes and conditions gives rise to an illusory appearance of arising, and their separating-apart gives an illusory name of ceasing — arising and ceasing being merely illusory names, and the extinguishing of this illusion being called true — turning to observe in this way, where, in the end, is this very mind capable of smelling actually located? Investigated minutely, it is neither inside, outside, nor in between, with no direction and no shape of its own at all. One should know that both the smelling and what is smelled are, in their very nature, empty and still — free of both subject and object, beyond every kind of relative opposition, ultimately empty and still. This is called cultivating insight.

4. Tasting 舌受味

Chinese Source

〖文〗四舌受味時修止者。隨所受味，即知如於夢幻中得味，若得順情美味，不起貪著，違情惡味，不起瞋心，非違非順之味，不起分別意想，是名修止。云何名舌受味時修觀？應作是念：今所受味，實不可得。所以者何？內外六味，性無分別，因內舌根和合，則舌識生，次生意識，強取味相，因此則有一切煩惱善惡等法。反觀緣味之識，不見相貌，當知受味者及一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Fourth, cultivating calming when the tongue receives a taste. Whatever taste is received, know it to be like a taste obtained within a dream, illusory: if one receives a delicious taste agreeable to one's own feelings, no craving attachment arises; a foul taste disagreeable to one's own feelings, no anger arises; a taste neither agreeable nor disagreeable, no discriminating thought arises — this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight when the tongue receives a taste? One should think: this taste now received cannot, in truth, ultimately be found at all. Why is this? Inward and outward, the six flavors have, by nature, no distinguishing feature of their own; it is only through the coming-together of the inward tongue-faculty that gustatory consciousness arises, followed by mental consciousness, which forcibly seizes upon the mark of the taste; because of this, every kind of afflicted, wholesome, or unwholesome dharma arises. Turning to observe this very consciousness that attends to taste, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the one who receives the taste nor any dharma has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗凡食物時，受酸甜苦辣鹹淡等味，皆可修止觀。古人云：終日食飯，未嘗食著一顆米，以其心無分別故。舌以味為緣，而生舌識，舌識依根而生，舌根因識能嘗，是能嘗者，即名舌識。次生同時意識，而能分別其好惡，以舌根為能分別，味塵為所分別，能所和合，故云識生。

Commentary: Whenever eating, receiving flavors such as sour, sweet, bitter, spicy, salty, or bland, calming and insight can equally be cultivated. As the ancients said: "Eating rice the whole day through, one has never once truly tasted a single grain of it" — since the mind gives rise to no discrimination at all. The tongue, taking taste as its condition, gives rise to gustatory consciousness; gustatory consciousness arises depending on the faculty, and the tongue-faculty is able to taste through consciousness — this capacity to taste is precisely called gustatory consciousness. Following it, mental consciousness arises at the same moment, able to discriminate what is pleasant or unpleasant — the tongue-faculty being the discriminating subject, the dust of taste being the discriminated object, subject and object meeting together, hence it is said that consciousness arises.

5. Touching 身受觸

Chinese Source

〖文〗五身受觸時修止者。隨所覺觸，即知如影，幻化不實，若受順情樂觸，不起貪著；若受違情苦觸，不起瞋惱；受非違非順之觸，不起憶想分別，是名修止。云何身受觸時修觀？應作是念：輕重冷煖澀滑等法，名之為觸；頭等六分，名之為身。觸性虛假，身亦不實。和合因緣，即生身識。次生意識，憶想分別苦樂等相，故名受觸。反觀緣觸之心，不見相貌，當知受觸者及一切法，畢竟空寂，是名修觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Fifth, cultivating calming when the body feels a touch. Whatever touch is felt, know it to be like a shadow, illusory and unreal: if one feels a pleasant touch agreeable to one's own feelings, no craving attachment arises; a painful touch disagreeable to one's own feelings, no anger or vexation arises; a touch neither agreeable nor disagreeable, no recollecting or discriminating thought arises — this is cultivating calming. What is cultivating insight when the body feels a touch? One should think: light or heavy, cold or warm, rough or smooth, and the like, are called "touch"; the head and the other five parts together are called "the body." The nature of touch is illusory, and the body too is unreal — it is through the coming-together of causes and conditions that bodily consciousness arises, followed by mental consciousness, which recollects and discriminates marks such as pain and pleasure; hence this is called "feeling a touch." Turning to observe this very mind that attends to touch, finding it to have no appearance at all — knowing that neither the one who feels the touch nor any dharma has any ultimate substance at all — this is cultivating insight.

〖講〗觸者，即著也。凡身上或冷煖風吹，及男女身分柔軟細滑，妙衣上服等，皆謂之觸塵……如佛弟子，畢陵伽婆蹉尊者，初發心出家，從佛入道，數聞如來，說諸世間，不可樂事，日間乞食城中，心思沙門，不覺路中毒刺傷足，舉身疼痛，即自思維，我念有知，知此得痛，雖覺覺痛，覺清淨心，無痛痛覺；又復思維，如是一身，甯有雙覺，若有雙覺，即應有雙痛，何故今只一足痛耶？如是反觀，攝念未久，身心忽空，不覺有能痛，亦不覺有所痛，當下能所雙忘，心地清淨。違情之觸塵尚且如是，則順情非違非順之觸塵境界，思之可知，是名修止……如跋陀婆羅尊者，先於威音王佛所，聞法出家，於浴僧時，隨例入室，忽悟水因，既不洗塵，亦不洗體，中間安然得無所有，妙觸清淨，本來無染，其清淨無染處，即與畢竟空寂，無二無別，故名為修觀也。

Commentary: "Touch" means contact. Whatever cold or warm wind blows against the body, or the soft, fine smoothness of a man or woman's body, or fine garments worn upon it — all of this is called the dust of touch...

The Buddha's own disciple, the Venerable Pilindavatsa, upon first resolving to leave the household life and take up the path under the Buddha, heard the Tathāgata repeatedly teach that nothing in the world is truly a source of joy. One day, begging for alms in the city, his mind fixed on the śramaṇa's own path, he failed to notice a poisonous thorn on the road and injured his foot — pain shooting through his whole body. He reflected at once: "This awareness I have, aware that pain has occurred — though it is aware of the pain, this very awareness, in its own pure nature, has no pain within the awareness itself." He reflected further: "If this single body possessed two separate awarenesses, there ought to be two separate pains — why, then, is only this one foot in pain?" Turning to observe in this way, before long, gathering his attention, body and mind suddenly became empty — he no longer felt any subject that felt the pain, nor any object of pain felt at all; subject and object were, in that instant, both forgotten, and the ground of his mind became pure. If even a disagreeable touch can be handled this way, then an agreeable or neutral touch, one may readily infer, can be handled the very same way — this is called cultivating calming.

The Venerable Bhadrapāla, formerly having heard the Dharma and left the household life under the Buddha Bhīṣmagarjitasvararāja, once entered the bathhouse along with the other monks at bathing time, and suddenly awakened to the true nature of water: it neither washes away dust, nor truly washes the body — right in between the two, at ease, he attained a state in which nothing at all remained, a wondrous touch, pure, originally undefiled. This place of purity, free of defilement, is not two, not different, from ultimate emptiness and stillness itself — hence this is called cultivating insight.

6. Mind-Cognition 意知法

Chinese Source

〖文〗六意知法中修止觀相，如初坐中已明訖。自上依六根修止觀相，隨所意用而用之，一一具上五番之意，是中已廣分別，今不重辨。行者若能於行住坐臥見聞覺知等一切處中修止觀者，當知是人真修摩訶衍道。如大品經云：佛告須菩提，若菩薩行時知行，坐時知坐，乃至服僧伽梨，視眴一心，出入禪定，當知是人名菩薩摩訶薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Sixth, the mark of cultivating calming and insight through the mind's own knowing of mental objects is as already explained at the very start of the section on practice while seated. From the account above of cultivating calming and insight through the six faculties, applying it as fitting to whatever occasion, each already carries within it the intent of the five approaches explained earlier, already distinguished at length there, and not repeated again here. A practitioner able to cultivate calming and insight in this way through walking, standing, sitting, lying down, seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing — throughout every occasion at all — should know that this is a person genuinely practicing the Mahāyāna path. As the Larger [Prajñāpāramitā] Sūtra says: the Buddha told Subhūti, "If a bodhisattva, walking, knows that he walks; sitting, knows that he sits; even down to putting on the saṃghāṭī robe, gazing and blinking, single-minded throughout, entering and leaving dhyāna-concentration alike — know that this is what is called a bodhisattva-mahāsattva."

〖講〗第六明意根修習止觀。即意識緣前五塵落謝之影子也，然意識緣法塵之相，如前初坐中修止觀，已廣明竟……當知此意識力用最大，以其能遍緣一切，通徹三境……三境者：（1）性境……（2）獨影境……（3）帶質境……三量者：（1）現量……（2）比量……（3）聖教量……自上依眼等六根修止觀相，隨自意用而用之，一一悉皆具上番修止觀義……據上六根中，一一各對六塵境而修止觀，言之則有六，其實性體之中，本無六與不六，以其性中相知故。因吾人本有之妙明真心，常於六根門頭，放光動地，本無善惡能所之相可得，但眾生迷倒，為煩惱所礙，遂將本有之真心，不隔而隔，於自心之菩提涅槃非背而背，以致見不超色，耳不越聲，若能反見見自性，反聞聞自性，謂一根既反源，六根咸解脫……如涅槃經云：「如來一根，則能見色、聞聲、嗅香、別味、覺觸、知法。」一根既爾，餘根亦然，此即六根互用之義。他如阿那律陀無目能見；跋難陀龍，無耳而聽；殑伽神女，非鼻聞香；憍梵缽提，異舌知味；舜若多神，無身覺觸；摩訶迦葉，不因心念，而圓明了知……行者若能於行住坐臥等六緣，以及見聞覺知之六根，對六塵境上，於二六時中，能修習止觀者，當知是人，真修摩訶衍道。梵語摩訶，此含三義：謂大多勝，衍即樂義，略而言之，即是大乘，謂行人於一切時中，念茲在茲，修習止觀，是人即是真修摩訶衍大乘之道。當知大乘之道，貴在自利利他，福慧二莊嚴。若修止，即福德莊嚴，若修觀，即智德莊嚴。又六度中，前五度修福德，第六度智慧，如是福慧，皆為能莊嚴。正因法身為所莊嚴，能所不二，即是妙莊嚴。行者修習止觀，即是以此莊嚴而自莊嚴，故曰是真修摩訶衍道。

Commentary: This sixth explains cultivating calming and insight through the mind-faculty itself — that is, mental consciousness attending to the leftover shadow-traces of the previous five sense-objects. Mental consciousness attending to the mark of a mental object was already explained at length at the very start of the section on practice while seated...

One should know that this mental consciousness carries the very greatest power of all, able to attend comprehensively to everything, penetrating all three kinds of cognitive object... The three kinds of object are: (1) the object of direct nature... (2) the object of isolated shadow-image... (3) the object of borrowed substance... The three kinds of valid cognition are: (1) direct perception... (2) inference... (3) reliance on the sacred teaching...

From the account above of cultivating calming and insight through the eye and the other five faculties, applying it as fitting to whatever occasion, each already carries within it the same meaning as the five approaches explained earlier... Based on the above, cultivating calming and insight is spoken of as sixfold, one for each of the six faculties meeting the six sense-objects in turn — but in truth, within the very substance of their nature, there is, from the start, no "six" or "not six" at all, since this nature is itself mutually known throughout. Because our own inherent, wondrously bright true mind constantly emits its own light and shakes the earth at every one of the six sense-gates, there is, from the start, no mark of good or evil, subject or object, to be found there at all — it is only because sentient beings, upside-down in delusion, are obstructed by affliction, that this inherent true mind, though never truly separated, comes to seem separated, and one's own mind's bodhi and nirvāṇa, though never truly turned away from, comes to seem turned away from — so that seeing never goes beyond form, and hearing never goes beyond sound. But if one is able to turn seeing back to see one's own nature, and turn hearing back to hear one's own nature — one faculty, once turned back to its own source, and all six faculties are liberated together...

As the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "The Tathāgata's single faculty is able to see form, hear sound, smell scent, distinguish taste, feel touch, and know mental objects, all at once." If this holds for one faculty, it holds for every other faculty as well — this is precisely the meaning of the six faculties' interchangeable use. So too: Aniruddha, without eyes, is able to see; the nāga Upananda, without ears, is able to hear; the river-goddess Gaṅgā, without a nose, smells fragrance; Gavāṃpati, with an unusual tongue, knows taste; the space-god Śūnya, without a body, feels touch; and Mahākāśyapa, without relying on conscious thought at all, knows all things with perfect, complete clarity...

A practitioner able to cultivate calming and insight in this way — through the six activities of walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, and through the six faculties of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing meeting the six sense-objects, at every hour of the day and night alike — should know that this is a person genuinely practicing the path of Mahāyāna. In Sanskrit, "mahā" carries three senses: great, many, and supreme; "yāna" means a vehicle — stated briefly, this is precisely the Great Vehicle. A practitioner who, at every moment, holds this constantly in mind, cultivating calming and insight, is a person genuinely practicing this very path of the Mahāyāna, the Great Vehicle.

One should know that the path of the Great Vehicle values, above all, benefiting both self and other, adorned with both merit and wisdom. Cultivating calming is the adornment of merit; cultivating insight is the adornment of wisdom. Among the six pāramitās, the first five cultivate merit, and the sixth cultivates wisdom — merit and wisdom together being what adorns; the true-cause Dharma-body being what is adorned; subject and object no longer two, this is precisely the wondrous adornment itself. A practitioner cultivating calming and insight is, through this very adornment, adorning themselves — hence it is said, this is truly practicing the path of the Mahāyāna.

Closing Citations 結歸

Chinese Source

〖文〗復次，若人能如是一切處中修行大乘，是人則於世間最勝最上無與等者。釋論偈中說：
閑坐林樹間　寂然滅諸惡　澹泊得一心　斯樂非天樂
人求世間利　名衣好床褥　斯樂非安隱　求利無厭足
衲衣在空閑　動止心常一　自以智慧明　觀諸法實相
種種諸法中　皆以等觀入　解慧心寂然　三界無倫匹

Ru-Yi Translation

Root Text: Further, if a person is able to practice the Great Vehicle in this way, everywhere at all, such a person stands supreme and unsurpassed in the world, with no equal at all. A verse in the Treatise says:
"Sitting quietly among trees in the forest, one extinguishes every evil in stillness — attaining a single, undivided mind in tranquil detachment, this joy is not the joy of the heavens.
People seek worldly gain — fine robes, a fine bed and bedding — but this joy brings no true peace, and the seeking of gain is never satisfied.
Wearing the patched robe amid quiet solitude, moving or still, the mind ever one — illuminated by one's own wisdom, one contemplates the true reality of every dharma.
Amid every kind of dharma, one enters through the very same contemplation of equality — the mind of understanding wisdom utterly still, with nothing in the three realms its equal."

〖講〗若人以下，至最上無與等者二句，乃是讚美持法之人，功德不可思議，因其能荷擔如來家業，以悲智二法，攝受眾生，故於世間之中，最尊貴，最高尚，世人無有一個能與相等。釋論即大智度論……澹泊得一心，即是一心湛然；當知我等眾生，心猿意馬，東奔西馳，竟日與煩惱相應，何能澹泊一心？務須端坐念實相，方能剋此……斯樂非天樂，言其世間快樂，與出世快樂比較，有凡聖之差，天淵之別，世間之樂，是有漏有為，即是生滅，終有敗壞，出世間之樂，是無漏無為，無為即是不生滅，終無有壞，故云斯樂非天樂。即了知諸行無常，是生滅法，生滅滅已，寂滅為樂，寂滅之樂，迥非人世天上之生滅樂可比，明從假入空觀者，即此義也。人求世間利下四句，正顯凡夫愚癡，迷真逐妄，認賊為子，將虛作實……經云：「三界無安，猶如火宅，眾苦充滿，甚可怖畏。」眾生無知，於三界火宅之中，不覺不知，不驚不怖，反於其中，互相鬥爭，以強陵弱，終日惟名聞是求，惟金錢是貪……悲夫！眾生顛倒，可稱甚矣！衲衣在空閑以下，言其出生有路，登涅槃有門，不致為火所燒，永受痛苦……動止心常一，此即是止；自以智慧明，觀諸法實相，此即是觀。諸法即約依正而言，以習般若之智，觀一切依正諸法，法法皆是實相。實相者，無相也……種種諸法之中，皆以等觀入，如修空觀，則一空一切空，假中俱空；修假，則一假一切假，空中俱假；修中，一中一切中，空假俱中，中外無空假，空假外無中。舉一即具三，言三即是一，三一不二，故謂之平等觀，亦名圓觀。解慧心寂然，三界無倫匹二句，正明用功修觀之人，不可與世人同日而語……上來正修大章至此已竟，然於諸修行中，此念佛止觀為最勝，以其此法，乃世尊無問自說，至簡易，至圓頓，至善，至美，但能老實念佛，即是止觀不二，若人但念阿彌陀，是名無上妙止觀也。

Commentary: "If a person," down through "with no equal at all," praises one who upholds this Dharma as possessing inconceivable merit — for such a person is able to shoulder the Tathāgata's own household work, gathering in sentient beings through the twin dharmas of compassion and wisdom, and so stands, within the world, most honored and most exalted, with no one in the world able to equal them. "The Treatise" is the Mahāyāna treatise, the Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise itself...

"Attaining a single, undivided mind in tranquil detachment" is precisely the mind being utterly still and clear. One should know that we sentient beings, minds like monkeys, thoughts like galloping horses, rushing east and west, in accord with affliction the whole day through — how could such a mind possibly attain tranquil, detached oneness? One must sit upright and hold true reality in mind before this can actually be accomplished...

"This joy is not the joy of the heavens" speaks of worldly happiness compared against transmundane happiness — a difference between the ordinary and the sagely, as far apart as heaven from the depths. Worldly happiness is conditioned and outflow-bound, that is, subject to arising and ceasing, and comes, in the end, to ruin; transmundane happiness is outflow-free and unconditioned — unconditioned meaning unborn and unceasing, never coming to ruin at all — hence it is said, "this joy is not the joy of the heavens." That is, understanding that every conditioned activity is impermanent, a dharma of arising and ceasing, and that once arising-and-ceasing itself has ceased, still cessation itself becomes the true joy — this joy of still cessation is utterly incomparable to any joy of arising and ceasing found among humans or even in the heavens. This is precisely the meaning of the contemplation moving from the conventional into emptiness.

"People seek worldly gain," the four lines that follow, reveal precisely how an ordinary, foolish being, lost in delusion, chases after falseness, mistaking a thief for one's own child, taking the illusory for the real... A sūtra says: "The three realms find no peace, exactly like a house on fire, filled with every kind of suffering, truly a thing to be feared." Sentient beings, in their ignorance, remain within this burning house of the three realms neither aware nor alarmed, neither startled nor afraid — fighting among themselves instead, the strong preying on the weak, seeking nothing all day long but fame, craving nothing but money... How sorrowful — the depth of sentient beings' own upside-down delusion can truly be called extreme!

"Wearing the patched robe amid quiet solitude," what follows, shows that there is indeed a road out of this, a gate ascending to nirvāṇa, so as never to be burned by this fire, never to suffer this pain forever... "Moving or still, the mind ever one" is precisely calming; "illuminated by one's own wisdom, one contemplates the true reality of every dharma" is precisely insight. "Every dharma" here refers to both the dependent and direct fruits of karma together — cultivating the wisdom of prajñā, one contemplates every dependent and direct dharma alike, and every single dharma is true reality itself. "True reality" means the markless...

"Amid every kind of dharma, one enters through the very same contemplation of equality": cultivating the contemplation of emptiness, once one thing is empty, everything is empty — the conventional and the middle both empty as well; cultivating the conventional, once one thing is conventional, everything is conventional — emptiness and the middle both conventional as well; cultivating the middle, once one thing is the middle, everything is the middle — emptiness and the conventional both the middle as well, with nothing outside the middle apart from emptiness and the conventional, and nothing outside emptiness and the conventional apart from the middle. Raising one already implies all three; speaking of three is itself speaking of one — the three and the one not two different things — hence this is called the contemplation of equality, also called the perfect contemplation.

"The mind of understanding wisdom utterly still, with nothing in the three realms its equal" — these two lines show precisely that a person who genuinely applies effort in cultivating this contemplation cannot be spoken of in the same breath as an ordinary worldly person at all...

This concludes the great chapter on actual practice. Yet among every form of practice, this calming and insight of reciting the Buddha's name is supreme of all — for this method is one the World-Honored One taught entirely of his own accord, without even being asked, the very simplest, the very most perfect and sudden, the very best, the very finest of all. If one can simply, honestly recite the Buddha's name, this is already calming and insight, not two different things. If a person recites nothing but "Amitābha," this alone is called the unsurpassed, wondrous calming and insight.

Reading note: This is by far the longest chapter in the commentary, and its own structure enacts the very point it is making: five increasingly subtle approaches for a practitioner who can still simply sit down and meditate, followed by twelve more for the practitioner who cannot — because a body "encumbered," in Baojing's own word, must inevitably work, eat, talk, and move through the world. The stories Baojing reaches for throughout the second half (the blacksmith reciting the Buddha's name with every hammer-strike, Huike cutting off his own arm to ask Bodhidharma to settle his mind, Pilindavatsa reasoning his way past a thorn wound) are worth noticing as a set: each takes an ordinary, often painful circumstance and treats it as no different in kind from sitting on a cushion — the same calming and insight, applied to whatever is actually happening. The chapter's own final line, naming nembutsu recitation as calming and insight already complete, is the clearest statement anywhere in this commentary of the Tiantai-and-Pure-Land synthesis Fazi's preface named as its whole governing theme.
