Letters of the Chen Ministers and the Prince of Jin

This part covers items 19 to 58, the second half of fascicle two and the first half of fascicle three. It opens with the last Chen documents: letters from the officials Xu Ling and Mao Xi, Xu Ling's five vows, and Xu Xiaoke's stele inscription on the release of life at Tiantai, the longest literary piece in the collection. After the Sui conquest of the Chen (589) come an edict of Emperor Wen to Zhiyi and two letters from Yang Jun, Prince Xiao of Qin. Most of the part is correspondence between Zhiyi and Yang Guang, Prince of Jin and later Emperor Yang. It includes the prince's invitation, the formal statement on receiving the bodhisattva precepts in 591, when he took the name Zongchi, and his list of gifts. Then come the letters about Zhiyi's journeys to Mount Lu, Mount Heng, and Jingzhou, and the founding of Yuquan Monastery. Other items concern the destruction of monasteries at Jiangzhou, the gift of a bodhisattva crown, and the prince's request for teaching. That request led to the Vimalakīrti commentary. The part ends with Zhiyi's replies on ordinations, an amnesty after a Golden Light rite, and gifts. Several letters are also quoted in Guanding's Separate Biography.

Ru-Yi Translation

Ling respectfully salutes [you]. Yesterday I took part in the Dharma assembly of [Shen], Equal in Ceremony, and partook of the sweet dew and the fearless roar; the assembly all submitted. Truly [you are] a torch of the true Dharma, illuminating all who have not yet awakened; I congratulate myself that in my remaining years I have been able to meet this wondrous teaching. I will soon come to consult you further; here I do not express my heart [fully]. Respectfully, with salutation.

Ru-Yi Translation

Ling respectfully salutes [you]. My heart's turning toward you is hard to set out in full. When Master Ba arrived, I received your letter of the twentieth day of the third month, which comforted the longing of many years. In the chill of the wheat season, how is your health? I hope you grow stronger day by day, and that in the mountains in spring and summer there are no further obstacles or troubles. I await your further word. For these two or three years old age has suddenly come upon me: my eyes and ears are dim and deaf, my mind and breath dull and blocked, so that I am no longer [fit] to be among people. Moreover, last year my sixth son died young; the pain and suffering became an illness that is still not cured, and just this month there was another bereavement. Year after year it has been so, and my anxious thoughts grow ever deeper. I reflect that the rest of my life cannot be much longer, and I have no way to bow before you and be received; what words can express my attachment and longing? I respectfully commend Master Zao, who is now returning [with this] plain letter, not in proper order. Your disciple Xu Ling respectfully salutes [you].

Ru-Yi Translation

The Five Vows of Xu Ling of the Chen, Presented to Meditation Master Zhizhe. Ling respectfully salutes [you]. Your disciple longs to leave the cage but has no means of being transformed into a winged being. Since my good roots are weak, I hope to adorn [myself] with the power of vows. First, I vow at the end of my life to accomplish right mindfulness. Second, I vow not to pass again through the hells and the three [evil] destinies. Third, I vow to return at once among humans and take birth in a place neither high nor low. Fourth, I vow to leave home as a youth and keep the precepts according to the Dharma. Fifth, I vow not to fall into being a monk who follows the vulgar current. Relying on this vowing mind to spur [myself] on in my western twilight, I now write out my sincere wishes in red and look up to beg you to witness them. Ling respectfully salutes [you].

Ru-Yi Translation

For years I have looked up to you with attachment, which is not easy to put into words. I hear that this summer you are circumambulating at the stone image, and I admire it without limit. I also hear that you wish to establish a place of practice on Tiantai, [to be begun] when the summer is over. Students from near and far who come to take refuge should by rights become ever more numerous; settling your mind in forests and fields, you delight yourself with the joy of the Dharma, and your meditation and lecturing do not cease. The forty-two syllable-gates that you had sent along — though I kept them a long time and read them through, I do not understand them; having no way to consult you, my regret keeps accumulating. From Nanyue too there is news from time to time: Meditation Master Zhao is on the ridges of the mountain, and the community is no different from when the great master was alive. Master Shan is lecturing on the Great Treatise on the mountain; he too is downcast, waiting and hoping [for you] to return and hold the reins of the great Dharma. If not, return to Zhongling or Mount She; these too are places to rest the mind. Why must you go to a distant region to found a place of practice? I hope you will not forget the people of the capital and its borderlands. Who would know that my dedication and inclining heart accumulate at every moment? Having no occasion to see your face, I gaze east and sigh in grief. Take care of your virtue. The trusted messenger now returns [with this] plain letter, not complete. Your disciple Mao Xi respectfully salutes [you].

Ru-Yi Translation

Xi's next letter. The autumn colors are still hot; how is your health in the path? Do meditation and bowing not wear on your virtue? I, your disciple, am old and ill one thing after another; decoctions and medicines have no effect, and moreover I cannot be at leisure, so I am more and more worn out. I hear that you have moved to Folong; I fear I will never again see your face, and my grief and sighing are both deep. I consider that [your] fundamental work is broad salvation; to guard an empty cliff alone, I fear, goes against the bodhisattva's purpose of covering all. Recently, with Xu of Danyang and other good friends, I have deliberated in detail: since it is equally a mountain, what difference is there between Zhongling and Tiantai? I earnestly hope you will think it over three times, and not be stuck on that and this. As for the capital being all the more [the place], Master Ba will set out by mouth one or two of the reasons. I hope you will take care of your virtue in the path. Your disciple Mao Xi respectfully salutes [you].

Xi's next letter. I have just received a letter from Nanyue; the mountain community is well. I now send you two pieces of jian incense, two jin of frankincense, and three hundred areca nuts. I could not get more; it is a token — please do not blame me. Your disciple Mao Xi respectfully salutes [you].

Ru-Yi Translation

Composed by Xu Xiaoke of Donghai, Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary in Regular Service and Libationer of the National University of the Chen. The Great Change has no substance, and so all kinds [of beings] flourish together; the Great One has no name, and so the perfected man sets up teaching. Looking up he observed, looking down he took as model; he drew from afar and sought from the side, combining the three powers, [so that] the ten thousand things are blown [into being]. Establishing offices [like] the Three Terraces and the Ding-handle [stars], the Five Peaks serve as guardians; distinguishing the regions with earls and pastors, the Four Rivers flow apart. [Yu] opened the Yi Gate and surveyed the eight bounds; he cut through the Dragon Gate and embanked the nine marshes. He spread [the lesson of] "repeated danger" and through it benefited the people; [the Duke of Zhou] examined the site and divined at Luo, and set up lords and leaders. Great indeed is the timely use of [the hexagram] Kan! Our Emperor, made a sage and concentrating his spirit, rides the charts and holds the registers; without action and without desire, his way accords with [the sages of] Fenyang; with folded hands and hanging robes, his virtue rises to perfect order. The celestial signs are correct and bright, and the ashes in the pitch-pipes accord with the seasons; beyond the borders there is no anxiety, and within the seas there is order. He spreads the [songs of] Feng and Ya among Chinese and barbarians, and his harmony and splendor surpass former ages — so lofty that it is hard to find words to praise it.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for brilliantly opening the rule of the Dharma and gloriously spreading the tree of the path, so that transformation is manifest in the ten good [deeds] and broadly saves the four kinds of birth: Meditation Master Zhiyi of Xiuchan Monastery on Tiantai lives on vegetables and coarse silk, practicing austere restraint, reverently spreading the imperial influence and upholding [the Dharma] in the semblance and final [ages]. The Meditation Master's secular surname is Chen; he is a man of Yingchuan, a descendant of [the house of] Gui. [Like] the four friends who startled the company, he is much admired by those who hear of him from afar; [like] the six marvels ranked in nobility, the worthies of his line have been recorded age after age. His grandfather Quan died early; his father, Qizu, under the Liang was Commissioner Bearing the Credentials, Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary, and Founding Marquis of Yiyang county. The Meditation Master left home as a youth; intelligent and quick, he awoke easily. He lodged in the gorges of Jing, and traveled teaching in You and Bing. Going from north to south, he practiced dhyāna and wisdom together. Exploring the wonders of Yu's Cave, he chose Mount Tai[tai] by divination. The road of the white cock came out; the cliff of green marrow opened. Climbing among the cassia he built a hut, and rested there in quiet. Where the forest interweaves with five willows, the scent of sandalwood is already fragrant; a stūpa appears in three stories, in the end hanging [in the air] the color of crystal. On the cloudy cliffs heavenly music sounds without being played; in the stone chamber the golden countenance leaves a shadow without form. The beautiful ridges tower steeply and the waves are vast; the great billows and sea-mirages cover the distance and envelop the void; tall pines in the huge gorges touch the clouds and screen the sun; flying and running creatures gather and breed, rare and strange. The jade hidden in the earth is easily distinguished by observing the bent staff; the pearl sunk in the abyss can be discerned by seeing the round current. Bodhisattvas with supernormal powers and śramaṇas [free] as they wish shake their staffs and charm springs, rise into the air and make their bowls dance; immortals who received the cinnabar float on rushing waters and come from time to time, and perfected men avoiding office ride the whirlwind and arrive circling. The land is grand and beautiful; the numinous prophecy lies here.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Meditation Master's blessings and wisdom are a firm foundation; his fame and radiance bring benefit. Ji Shang'er, General of Proclaimed Ferocity and Administrator of Linhai, a descendant of Zixun — his family famed for its arts of prescription, selling medicine and ascending as an immortal, renowned in the Han of old — holding the tally as governor in this bright age, requested him to turn the Dharma-wheel and lecture on the Golden Light Sūtra, one [complete] sūtra. The former General of Cloud Cavalry and Administrator of Linhai, Chen Sizhan, and his nephew Chen Yaoqing and others, men of this locality, in military insignia and embroidered robes, of high rank in their home district, respectfully invited the Meditation Master next to lecture on the scripture of the Lotus. With a white-tusk round fan he first opened the gate of the Vinaya-piṭaka; with a jade-handled whisk he expounded alongside [it] the verses of the king of sūtras. [Like the pearl] tied [in the robe], he first instructed the intimate friend, and his drunkenness was removed; [like] the drum in the dream about to sound, the doubts of Brahmā and Māra were dispelled. So he made both admonition and encouragement clear and explained widely misfortune and blessing. Shang'er further encouraged and instructed the weir-owners Yan Xuzu, Yang Gonghe, and the other worthies; all the hundred gentlemen established their sincere vows, earnest in their red-hearted sincerity, their white [good] deeds harmonizing in discussion. They lamented their past entanglement, like [the duke who went to] Tang [to watch fishing], and sighed over the future conditions of fishing on the Pu. Each gave up his weir-business and fish-dams, sixty-three places in all.

Reading Notes for Part 2

Translator's notes

Shared passages with the Separate Biography — The Prince of Jin's precept statement (unit 34), his request for teaching (unit 94), Zhiyi's refusal (unit 96), and the second request (unit 98) are quoted, shortened, in `zhizhe-biezhuan/p1_body.md` ("Under the Sui: The Prince of Jin"). The biography's English is reused where the Chinese is the same ("I am ashamed of the rugged small path…", "Sadāprarudita exhausted his marrow for Dharmodgata…", "Master Rong with his strong mouth was first to have his horns broken…"). Where this text is fuller or punctuated differently, it is translated as written: unit 34 has a long opening invocation, "his years approach sixty" (年將耳順; Zhiyi was in fact 54 in 591), and "Rāhula… the eight princes… the sixteen śrāmaṇeras", none of which the biography quotes. In unit 94, 色心無作觸仰勝緣 and 保解毘尼昔年虔受 are two clauses here; the biography joins them ("Form and mind without fabrication I reverently received in former years").

Differences from the biography — Unit 12 names the governor who invited the Golden Light lectures as Ji Shang'er (計尚兒), General of Proclaimed Ferocity and Administrator of Linhai; the biography has Ji Xu (計詡). Both agree on the sixty-three weirs. Unit 36 gives the precept name as "Bodhisattva Zongchi"; the biography gives Zongchi ("Total Retention") and has the prince name Zhiyi "Zhizhe" in return. Unit 4 already calls Zhiyi "Zhizhe" in a Chen letter, before the name was given in 591; this must be a later heading by the compiler.

Courtesy formulas — The renderings fixed in Part 1 are kept: 和南 "respectfully salutes [you]", 謹和南 "Respectfully, with salutation", 稽首和南 "bow(s) his head and respectfully salute(s)", 道體何如 "how is your health in the path?". New fixed forms: 敬白 "respectfully addresses [you]" (Zhiyi signs 沙門某敬白 "The śramaṇa So-and-so respectfully addresses [you]"); 白 "addresses [you]"; 珍德／願珍德／敬德 "take care of your virtue"; 不具／不宣具 "not written in full"; 右牒 "Notice".

The stele inscription (units 12–21) — Xu Xiaoke's text is full of classical allusions. The opening paraphrases the Book of Changes (太易, 坎之時用大矣哉, the hexagrams Bi 比 and Meng 蒙 in unit 17) and the legends of Yu the Great and the Duke of Zhou. "Lamented their past entanglement, like [the duke who went to] Tang" refers to Duke Yin of Lu, who was rebuked for going to Tang to watch fishing (Zuozhuan, Duke Yin 5). "Fishing on the Pu" is Zhuangzi declining office. In unit 20, Kuai Shen (噲參, written 噌參) healed a crane that brought him pearls; Jingkang is Kong Yu, who freed a tortoise; the pearl at Sui is the snake's gift to the Marquis of Sui; "the carriage and canopy at Yuqi" (于期軒蓋) was not identified. 二緣樹下 and 五旬座上 in unit 12 are rendered literally; their reference was not found. The character [竺-二+(一/(尸@邑))] (unit 12) is taken as 簄, a bamboo fish-weir, so [竺…]主 = "weir-owners".

Quotations in unit 115 — "If some violate the king's law… I will free them all" condenses the vows of the repentance chapter of the Golden Light Sūtra (T663, 懺悔品; checked in the vault). The wording follows this round's `jinguangming-jing/p1_body.md` ("If some violate the king's law / … bonds, cangues, and chains"). The Consecration Sūtra passage is from T1331 (佛說灌頂經, fascicle 12, the bodhisattva Jiutuo's words), checked in the vault; there it is the king who should release prisoners when he or his family is ill.

Other quotations — "Without dhyāna there is no wisdom; without wisdom there is no dhyāna" (unit 94) is from the Chinese Dharmapada (法句經 T210: 無禪不智、無智不禪). "Faith is what can enter" (unit 96) echoes the Great Treatise (T1509, fascicle 1: 佛法大海，信為能入，智為能度). "A single phrase that dyes the spirit does not decay through eons" (unit 96): source not located (it is quoted in later works such as the Zongjing lu). "A man lives by three, and serves them as one" (unit 94) is from the Guoyu (Jinyu). "Those who penetrate do not go against [one another]" (unit 98) is from Guṇavarman's dying verse in the Biographies of Eminent Monks (T2059: 偏執有是非，達者無違諍); 求那 is taken as Guṇavarman. "Whom shall we now ask?" (unit 98) echoes the opening chapter of the Lotus (今當問誰), where Maitreya and the assembly wonder about the Buddha's light. Unit 79: "dyed to spoil the color" is the Brahmā's Net Sūtra (T1484: 皆使壞色).

Unit 35, the list of gifts — 欝泥 (yuni) qualifying many robes is unexplained; it may be a color or a kind of cloth. 舍勒 is taken as śāṭaka, an under-robe. 聖種納袈裟 is rendered "patched kāṣāya of the noble lineage" (聖種 = the four noble lineages, ariyavaṃsa). Measure words 屯, 禪 (布三十禪) are kept in italics or literally. The ten four-character phrases are written in ten decorative scripts (claw, dragon, hanging-needle, hanging-dew, flying-white, inverted-shallot, fish, tadpole, seal, great seal), noted in the interlinear comments.

Names and places — 蔣州 (the former Chen capital region) is "Jiangzhou"; 江州 (Xunyang, at Mount Lu) is "Jiang Prefecture" to keep the two apart. 匡山／廬山 Mount Kuang / Mount Lu (both names appear). 建初烏瓊 (unit 79) is read as Dharma Master Wuqiong of Jianchu Monastery, perhaps one of "the two Qiongs" of unit 98. 傳中詵 (unit 65) is taken as "Zhongshen, the messenger"; it may be a name, Chuan Zhongshen. 王灌指 (unit 65) is read as Wang Guan, with 指 "specially", because unit 67 names him Wang Guan. 李元操 (unit 23) appears as 李元慘 in unit 84; translated as written (Li Yuancao / Li Yuancan).

Doubtful readings — Unit 45 逮旨須取明，二日垂別 is read "[you] must set out, taking dawn of the second day for parting." Unit 53 猥延滿軋 is unclear; rendered "[This letter] drags on unworthily." Unit 69 而發此至江州遂下請僧料 is rendered loosely; alternatively, "when [the envoy] set out from here to Jiang Prefecture, the provisions for inviting monks were sent down." Unit 75 庶應旦夕將聖德果，亦復差機 is uncertain. Unit 77 名符天冠 "the name matching the heavenly crown" may anticipate the crown of unit 94. Unit 92 轉輪斯奉實惟日暮 and 第率從便蕃 are uncertain. Unit 107 is loosely punctuated in CBETA; the reading "[It is] not so: my long-held wish [is not for] Qixia" is one possibility.

Dates — Unit 23 is dated the tenth year of Kaihuang (590) and addressed to Guangzhai Monastery, though the biography has Zhiyi on Mount Lu after the fall of the Chen. Unit 84 (593) is addressed to Xiuchan Monastery although Zhiyi was then in Jingzhou.

Cross-references — Unit 98's account of the Waguan debates and Nanyue's prediction matches the biography. Huiri and Fayun Places of Practice (unit 69) recur in Part 3 (unit 48).
