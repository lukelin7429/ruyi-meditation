The Disciples Decline, Part I

弟子品第三（上）

The Buddha sends five of his most senior disciples, one after another, to inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness — and each recalls a past encounter in which the layman's teaching left him speechless, and asks to be excused.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
Five of the Buddha's ten great disciples decline, in turn, to visit the ill Vimalakīrti: Śāriputra (foremost in wisdom), Mahāmaudgalyāyana (foremost in spiritual powers), Mahākāśyapa (foremost in ascetic practice), Subhūti (foremost in understanding emptiness), and Pūrṇa Maitrāyaṇīputra (foremost in eloquence in teaching).
Structure
All ten disciples' declines share one pattern: the Buddha names a disciple, the disciple protests unfitness, and recalls a specific past encounter with Vimalakīrti in which his own specialty was turned back on him. The second five disciples continue in Part 4.

1. Śāriputra and the True Meaning of Sitting in Meditation

Chinese Source

爾時長者維摩詰，自念：「寢疾于床，世尊大慈，寧不垂愍？」佛知其意，即告舍利弗：「汝行詣維摩詰問疾。」舍利弗白佛言：「世尊！我不堪任詣彼問疾。所以者何？憶念我昔，曾於林中宴坐樹下，時維摩詰來謂我言：『唯，舍利弗！不必是坐，為宴坐也。夫宴坐者，不於三界現身、意，是為宴坐；不起滅定而現諸威儀，是為宴坐；不捨道法而現凡夫事，是為宴坐；心不住內亦不在外，是為宴坐；於諸見不動，而修行三十七品，是為宴坐；不斷煩惱而入涅槃，是為宴坐。若能如是坐者，佛所印可。』時我——世尊！——聞說是語，默然而止，不能加報，故我不任詣彼問疾。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the elder Vimalakīrti thought to himself: "I lie here ill upon my bed — will the World-Honored One, in his great compassion, not show me some pity?" The Buddha, knowing his thought, said to Śāriputra: "Go and inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness." Śāriputra said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am not able to go and inquire after his illness. And why? I recall that once, when I was sitting in meditation beneath a tree in the forest, Vimalakīrti came and said to me: 'Ah, Śāriputra — sitting like this is not necessarily what is meant by sitting in meditation. As for sitting in meditation: not manifesting body or mind anywhere within the three realms — this is sitting in meditation. Not rising up out of cessation, and yet manifesting every dignified deportment — this is sitting in meditation. Not abandoning the way of the path, and yet manifesting the affairs of an ordinary person — this is sitting in meditation. A mind that abides neither within nor without — this is sitting in meditation. Unmoved with respect to every view, and yet practicing the thirty-seven factors of the path — this is sitting in meditation. Not cutting off affliction, and yet entering nirvāṇa — this is sitting in meditation. If you are able to sit like this, it is the sitting the Buddha himself sanctions.' At that time, World-Honored One, when I heard him say this, I fell silent and could not reply — this is why I am not able to go and inquire after his illness."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva's note on why the Buddha begins with Śāriputra, when he already knows perfectly well that none of the ten disciples will accept the assignment, is worth having before the pattern repeats nine more times across this chapter and the next: Śāriputra is foremost in wisdom among the five hundred disciples, so sending him first — and having him decline first — is what will make Vimalakīrti's stature register most vividly with the rest of the assembly still watching. Nothing about the choice is arbitrary or cruel; it is staging.

The six redefinitions of "sitting in meditation" (宴坐) are not simply saying the same thing six times. Sengzhao reads the first as the most fundamental: the Dharma-body's true meditative sitting has no body or mind appearing anywhere within the three realms at all — form and spirit both extinguished, beyond anything seeing or hearing could register — whereas Śāriputra, still carrying a body and mind produced by past karmic recompense, treats the forest as a refuge from disturbance, which is itself still a subtle way of "appearing within the three realms," just a quieter corner of it. Zhu Daosheng's gloss on the final couplet — not severing affliction, yet entering nirvāṇa — makes the same move at a different level: once someone genuinely realizes the nature of things, if they go on prizing nirvāṇa as a separately superior state to be attained, they become bound by their own attainment of it; seeing that not-cutting-off-affliction already is entering nirvāṇa removes even that last, subtle duality between the practice and its goal. Śāriputra's silence at the end of the passage is not embarrassment for its own sake — it is the correct response to a teaching that has run past every category his own training gave him to answer with.

2. Maudgalyāyana and How the Dharma Cannot Be Taught

Chinese Source

佛告大目犍連：「汝行詣維摩詰問疾。」目連白佛言：「世尊！我不堪任詣彼問疾。所以者何？憶念我昔入毘耶離大城，於里巷中為諸居士說法。時維摩詰來謂我言：『唯，大目連！為白衣居士說法，不當如仁者所說。夫說法者，當如法說。法無眾生，離眾生垢故；法無有我，離我垢故；法無壽命，離生死故；法無有人，前後際斷故；法常寂然，滅諸相故；法離於相，無所緣故；法無名字，言語斷故；法無有說，離覺觀故；法無形相，如虛空故；法無戲論，畢竟空故；法無我所，離我所故；法無分別，離諸識故；法無有比，無相待故；法不屬因，不在緣故；法同法性，入諸法故；法隨於如，無所隨故；法住實際，諸邊不動故；法無動搖，不依六塵故；法無去來，常不住故；法順空，隨無相，應無作；法離好醜，法無增損，法無生滅，法無所歸；法過眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、心；法無高下，法常住不動，法離一切觀行。唯，大目連！法相如是，豈可說乎？夫說法者，無說無示；其聽法者，無聞無得，譬如幻士，為幻人說法，當建是意，而為說法。當了眾生根有利鈍，善於知見無所罣礙，以大悲心讚于大乘，念報佛恩不斷三寶，然後說法。』維摩詰說是法時，八百居士發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。我無此辯，是故不任詣彼問疾。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Mahāmaudgalyāyana: "Go and inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness." Maudgalyāyana said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am not able to go and inquire after his illness. And why? I recall that once, when I had entered the great city of Vaiśālī and was teaching the Dharma to a group of householders in the streets, Vimalakīrti came and said to me: 'Ah, Mahāmaudgalyāyana — teaching the Dharma to lay householders in white robes should not be done the way you, sir, have just done it. As for teaching the Dharma, one should teach it exactly as it is. The Dharma has no living beings in it, since it is free of the defilement of a living being. The Dharma has no self in it, since it is free of the defilement of a self. The Dharma has no lifespan in it, since it is free of birth and death. The Dharma has no person in it, since past and future are both cut off in it. The Dharma is always still, since it has extinguished every mark. The Dharma is apart from any mark, since it has nothing it takes as an object. The Dharma has no name or word, since language is cut off in it. The Dharma admits of no statement, since it is free of thought and reflection. The Dharma has no shape or form, since it is like empty space. The Dharma is free of idle speculation, since it is ultimately empty. The Dharma has nothing that belongs to a self, since it is free of what belongs to a self. The Dharma makes no distinctions, since it is free of every kind of discriminating consciousness. The Dharma has nothing to compare it to, since it depends on nothing set against it. The Dharma belongs to no primary cause, since it rests on no secondary condition. The Dharma is identical with the nature of all things, since it enters into every phenomenon. The Dharma follows suchness, since it follows nothing at all. The Dharma abides at the ultimate limit of reality, since it is unmoved by any extreme. The Dharma has no motion, since it does not depend on the six sense objects. The Dharma has no coming or going, since it never abides anywhere. The Dharma accords with emptiness, follows signlessness, and responds with no action; the Dharma is apart from beauty and ugliness, the Dharma neither increases nor diminishes, the Dharma neither arises nor ceases, the Dharma has nowhere it returns to; the Dharma goes beyond eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind; the Dharma has no high or low, the Dharma abides forever unmoved, the Dharma is apart from every kind of contemplative practice. Ah, Mahāmaudgalyāyana, the mark of the Dharma is like this — how could it be spoken? Whoever teaches the Dharma neither states it nor demonstrates it; whoever listens to the Dharma neither hears it nor obtains anything from it. It is like a conjurer teaching the Dharma to a conjured person — one should establish this understanding, and only then teach the Dharma. One should understand whether the faculties of one's listeners are sharp or dull, be skilled in a knowledge and vision that is unobstructed, praise the Great Vehicle with a mind of great compassion, and remember to repay the Buddha's kindness by never letting the Three Jewels lapse — only then should one teach the Dharma.' When Vimalakīrti taught this Dharma, eight hundred householders gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. I do not have such eloquence — this is why I am not able to go and inquire after his illness."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva

Kumārajīva's note on the setup is a small but telling piece of context: these particular householders' faculties were sharp enough to hear a teaching on emptiness directly, but Maudgalyāyana, not yet able to discern this, defaulted to the standard beginner's sequence — giving, precepts, the reward of heavenly rebirth — the seven-step method meant for audiences not yet ready for anything harder. The rebuke that follows is not a claim that the beginner's method is wrong in general; it is that Maudgalyāyana misjudged who was actually in front of him.

The list itself is worth reading as one claim said roughly twenty different ways rather than twenty separate assertions — each clause targets a different habitual way a listener might quietly re-solidify "the Dharma" into a fixed thing (a self, a name, a comparison, a cause, a direction of travel) the moment it is spoken about, and each "because" clause names exactly which reification that particular phrasing would otherwise smuggle back in. The passage's practical payoff comes in its closing image: teaching the Dharma "like a conjurer teaching the Dharma to a conjured person" — a teacher who does not treat their own teacher-self, or their listener's listener-self, as anything more solid than the Dharma itself is being described as. Maudgalyāyana's own admission at the end — "I do not have such eloquence" — is not really about eloquence; it is that his teaching still assumed exactly the kind of fixed listener Vimalakīrti's speech has just spent twenty clauses dissolving.

3. Mahākāśyapa and Begging Without Favoring the Poor

Chinese Source

佛告大迦葉：「汝行詣維摩詰問疾。」迦葉白佛言：「世尊！我不堪任詣彼問疾。所以者何？憶念我昔於貧里而行乞，時維摩詰來謂我言：『唯，大迦葉！有慈悲心而不能普，捨豪富從貧乞。迦葉！住平等法，應次行乞食。為不食故，應行乞食，為壞和合相故，應取揣食，為不受故，應受彼食；以空聚想，入於聚落，所見色與盲等，所聞聲與響等，所嗅香與風等，所食味不分別，受諸觸如智證，知諸法如幻相，無自性，無他性，本自不然，今則無滅。迦葉！若能不捨八邪，入八解脫，以邪相入正法，以一食施一切，供養諸佛及眾賢聖，然後可食；如是食者，非有煩惱，非離煩惱，非入定意，非起定意，非住世間，非住涅槃；其有施者，無大福，無小福，不為益，不為損，是為正入佛道，不依聲聞：迦葉！若如是食，為不空食人之施也。』時我——世尊！——聞說是語，得未曾有，即於一切菩薩深起敬心，復作是念：『斯有家名，辯才智慧乃能如是！其誰聞此不發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心？』我從是來，不復勸人以聲聞、辟支佛行。是故不任詣彼問疾。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Mahākāśyapa: "Go and inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness." Kāśyapa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am not able to go and inquire after his illness. And why? I recall that once, when I was begging for alms in a poor quarter of the city, Vimalakīrti came and said to me: 'Ah, Mahākāśyapa — you have a compassionate mind, but it does not extend everywhere; you avoid the wealthy and beg only among the poor. Kāśyapa, one who abides in the Dharma of equality should beg for alms house by house, in order. One should beg for the sake of not-eating; one should accept a lump of food for the sake of breaking apart the mark of what is only assembled together; one should receive that food for the sake of not-receiving. Enter the village with the perception of an empty village; regard what you see as no different from what the blind see; regard what you hear as no different from an echo; regard what you smell as no different from the wind; do not discriminate among the tastes of what you eat; receive every sensation the way a sage receives direct realization of the truth; know that every phenomenon is like an illusion, without a nature of its own and without a nature borrowed from anything else — never, in the first place, kindled, and so, now, with nothing that needs to be extinguished. Kāśyapa, if you are able, without abandoning the eight wrong ways, to enter the eight liberations; to enter true Dharma by way of what is wrong; to give a single meal to all beings and make offering with it to every buddha and every sage — only then may you eat; and eating in this way, you are neither someone with affliction nor someone free of affliction, neither someone entering meditative absorption nor someone rising from it, neither someone dwelling in the world nor someone dwelling in nirvāṇa. As for whoever gives you alms, there is for them no great merit and no small merit, no benefit and no harm by it — this is the true entry into the buddha-path, one that does not rely on the path of the śrāvaka. Kāśyapa, if you eat in this way, you do not eat what people give you in vain.' At that time, World-Honored One, when I heard him say this, I gained something I had never had before, and gave rise, at once, to a deep reverence toward every bodhisattva, and thought to myself: 'How is it that someone with only the name of a householder has eloquence and wisdom such as this? Who, hearing this, would not give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening?' From that time on, I have never again urged anyone toward the practice of the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha. This is why I am not able to go and inquire after his illness."

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Mahākāśyapa's habit of begging only in poor neighborhoods was, by Sengzhao's account, itself a deliberate compassionate practice, not carelessness: the poor, having planted little merit in past lives, need the chance to give alms now more urgently than the wealthy do, since neglecting them risks compounding their poverty in lives to come. Vimalakīrti's rebuke does not dispute the compassion behind this — it names, precisely, what "not universal" (不能普) means: even a genuinely compassionate discrimination between who deserves the opportunity to give is still a discrimination, still an uneven regard for beings that the Dharma of equality does not itself make. This lands with particular weight on Kāśyapa specifically, since he was, by tradition, the Buddha's own foremost ascetic, famous precisely for choosing the poor over the wealthy as an act of compassion — the rebuke targets not a lapse in his practice but the very thing he was most known for.

The four technical terms folded into "one should accept a lump of food for the sake of breaking apart the mark of what is assembled together" (為壞和合相故，應取揣食) refer to a fourfold classification of nourishment used elsewhere in the canon — physical, "wish-sustained" food (as when a sight of nearby provisions alone can keep someone alive), karmic food (as when hell-beings, eating nothing, are sustained by the karma still requiring them to suffer), and consciousness-food (how formless beings persist by unbroken continuity of awareness alone). Vimalakīrti's point in invoking it is not the classification itself but what it implies: an ordinary lump of alms food (揣食) is, like the body that eats it, just another instance of the five aggregates provisionally assembled together — so eating it correctly means eating with the same understanding of "no fixed self here to be fed" that governs everything else Vimalakīrti tells Kāśyapa to observe on his alms round.

4. Subhūti and the Bowl He Almost Set Down

Chinese Source

佛告須菩提：「汝行詣維摩詰問疾。」須菩提白佛言：「世尊！我不堪任詣彼問疾。所以者何？憶念我昔，入其舍從乞食，時維摩詰取我鉢盛滿飯，謂我言：『唯，須菩提！若能於食等者，諸法亦等，諸法等者，於食亦等；如是行乞，乃可取食。若須菩提不斷婬、怒、癡，亦不與俱；不壞於身，而隨一相；不滅癡愛，起於明脫；以五逆相而得解脫，亦不解不縛；不見四諦，非不見諦；非得果，非不得果；非凡夫，非離凡夫法；非聖人，非不聖人；雖成就一切法，而離諸法相，乃可取食。若須菩提不見佛，不聞法，彼外道六師——富蘭那迦葉、末伽梨拘賒梨子、刪闍夜毘羅胝子、阿耆多翅舍欽婆羅、迦羅鳩馱迦旃延、尼犍陀若提子等——是汝之師，因其出家，彼師所墮，汝亦隨墮，乃可取食。若須菩提入諸邪見，不到彼岸；住於八難，不得無難；同於煩惱，離清淨法；汝得無諍三昧，一切眾生亦得是定——其施汝者，不名福田；供養汝者，墮三惡道——為與眾魔共一手作諸勞侶——汝與眾魔及諸塵勞，等無有異——於一切眾生而有怨心，謗諸佛，毀於法，不入眾數，終不得滅度。汝若如是，乃可取食。』時我——世尊！——聞此語茫然，不識是何言？不知以何答？便置鉢欲出其舍。維摩詰言：『唯，須菩提！取鉢勿懼。於意云何？如來所作化人，若以是事詰，寧有懼不？』我言：『不也！』維摩詰言：『一切諸法如幻化相，汝今不應有所懼也。所以者何？一切言說不離是相，至於智者，不著文字故無所懼。何以故？文字性離，無有文字，是則解脫；解脫相者，則諸法也。』維摩詰說是法時，二百天子得法眼淨，故我不任詣彼問疾。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Subhūti: "Go and inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness." Subhūti said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am not able to go and inquire after his illness. And why? I recall that once, when I entered his house to beg for food, Vimalakīrti took my bowl, filled it with rice, and said to me: 'Ah, Subhūti — if you can be equal-minded about food, then you are equal-minded about every phenomenon; and if you are equal-minded about every phenomenon, then you are equal-minded about food as well. Begging in this way, only then may you take this food. Subhūti, if you can, without cutting off desire, anger, and delusion, also not be entangled with them; without destroying the body, still follow the single mark; without extinguishing delusion and craving, still give rise to clarity and liberation; through the very mark of the five heinous transgressions, still attain liberation — neither bound nor unbound by them; if you neither see the four noble truths nor fail to see them; if you neither attain a fruit of the path nor fail to attain one; if you are neither an ordinary person nor apart from the ways of an ordinary person; if you are neither a sage nor not a sage; if, though accomplishing every phenomenon, you remain apart from the mark of every phenomenon — only then may you take this food. Subhūti, if you neither see the Buddha nor hear the Dharma; if those six heterodox teachers — Pūraṇa Kāśyapa, Maskarin Gośālīputra, Sañjayin Vairaṭīputra, Ajita Keśakambala, Kakuda Kātyāyana, and Nirgrantha Jñātiputra — are your own teachers, and, having gone forth under them, you fall where they fall, and you too fall along with them — only then may you take this food. Subhūti, if you enter into every wrong view and do not reach the other shore; if you dwell among the eight difficulties and do not attain freedom from difficulty; if you are one with affliction and apart from the pure Dharma; you have attained the samādhi of non-contention, and every being has attained that same samādhi as well — whoever gives to you gains no field of merit by it; whoever makes offering to you falls, by it, into the three evil destinies; you are joined hand in hand with every demon as their fellow in defilement; you are no different at all from every demon and every defilement; you hold a mind of enmity toward every being, you slander every buddha, you disparage the Dharma, you are not counted among the fourfold assembly, and you will never in the end attain extinction — if you are like this, only then may you take this food.' At that time, World-Honored One, hearing this, I was utterly at a loss — I did not know what kind of talk this was, and did not know how to answer. I set down the bowl and made to leave his house. Vimalakīrti said: 'Ah, Subhūti, take the bowl, and do not be afraid. What do you think — if a person conjured up by the Tathāgata were challenged with words like these, would that conjured person feel afraid?' I said: 'No.' Vimalakīrti said: 'Every phenomenon is just like a conjured, illusory mark — you should not be afraid now. And why? Because every statement whatsoever does not depart from this very mark; as for the wise, it is precisely because they are not attached to words that they have nothing to fear. And why? Because the nature of words is itself apart from anything fixed — where there is no fixed word, that itself is liberation; and the mark of liberation is nothing other than every phenomenon itself.' When Vimalakīrti taught this Dharma, two hundred devas attained the pure Dharma-eye. This is why I am not able to go and inquire after his illness."

Reading note — after Sengzhao and Kumārajīva

This is the most startling passage in the chapter, and this site's convention is to face difficult passages honestly rather than soften them, so it is worth saying plainly: read on its surface, Vimalakīrti appears to tell Subhūti that only a being sunk in every possible corruption — bound to heretical teachers, indistinguishable from demons, guilty of the five heinous transgressions, slandering the Buddha and the Dharma — is fit to eat this food. Sengzhao names exactly what is happening and gives it a technical label: from the six heretical teachers through "never attain extinction," the whole passage is a deliberate case of "speaking in reverse in order to arrive at great accord" (逆談以成大順). It is not a literal endorsement of any of these conditions. Each clause is constructed so that its surface meaning is the opposite of what it is actually pointing at — Subhūti's own gloss on the pattern, offered elsewhere in this commentary, is that "if you don't see the Buddha" secretly means "if you understand there is no fixed, graspable Buddha to be seen" — and understood that way, you are, in fact, seeing correctly. The passage's method is to make grasping at any fixed spiritual attainment — including "I have correctly seen the Buddha," including "I am not like the six heretical teachers" — visible as still a form of attachment, by phrasing the alternative in the most alarming terms available.

Subhūti's fear at being unable to parse this, and his attempt to simply leave with the bowl still empty, is treated in the text itself as the natural response of someone who has taken the words at face value rather than as a device — and Vimalakīrti's answer does not walk the content back. Instead he reframes the entire exchange: if a being conjured up by the Buddha's own power were challenged this way, it would feel no fear, because it would understand there is no fixed self there to be accused of anything. "Every phenomenon is just like a conjured, illusory mark" — including Subhūti, including the accusations just leveled at him, including the words used to level them. Two hundred devas attain the pure Dharma-eye at the end of this exchange, which is worth noting as this chapter's own verdict on the passage: within the sūtra's world, the shock of the method is treated as having worked, not as having gone too far.

5. Pūrṇa and the Monks Who Forgot Their Own Vow

Chinese Source

佛告富樓那彌多羅尼子：「汝行詣維摩詰問疾。」富樓那白佛言：「世尊！我不堪任詣彼問疾。所以者何？憶念我昔於大林中，在一樹下為諸新學比丘說法。時維摩詰來謂我言：『唯，富樓那！先當入定，觀此人心然後說法。無以穢食置於寶器，當知是比丘心之所念，無以琉璃同彼水精。汝不能知眾生根源，無得發起以小乘法。彼自無瘡，勿傷之也；欲行大道，莫示小徑；無以大海，內於牛跡；無以日光，等彼螢火。富樓那！此比丘久發大乘心，中忘此意，如何以小乘法而教導之？我觀小乘智慧微淺，猶如盲人，不能分別一切眾生根之利鈍。』時維摩詰即入三昧，令此比丘自識宿命：曾於五百佛所植眾德本，迴向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。即時豁然，還得本心，於是諸比丘稽首禮維摩詰足。時維摩詰因為說法，於阿耨多羅三藐三菩提不復退轉。我念聲聞不觀人根，不應說法，是故不任詣彼問疾。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Pūrṇa Maitrāyaṇīputra: "Go and inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness." Pūrṇa said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am not able to go and inquire after his illness. And why? I recall that once, in a great forest, I was teaching the Dharma beneath a tree to a group of newly ordained monks. Vimalakīrti came and said to me: 'Ah, Pūrṇa — you should first enter meditative absorption and observe these people's minds, and only then teach the Dharma. Do not place foul food in a jeweled vessel. You should know what these monks are actually thinking in their minds — do not mistake crystal for lapis lazuli. You are not able to know the root of these beings' faculties — do not rouse them with the teaching of the Lesser Vehicle. They themselves have no wound — do not injure them. If you wish to travel the great road, do not point the way down a narrow path; do not pour the great ocean into a cow's hoofprint; do not set the light of the sun beside the glow of a firefly. Pūrṇa, these monks gave rise long ago to the mind of the Great Vehicle, and have only forgotten this intention in the meantime — how could you teach and guide them with the Lesser Vehicle? I observe that the wisdom of the Lesser Vehicle is slight and shallow, like a blind man unable to distinguish the sharpness or dullness of any being's faculties.' At that time Vimalakīrti entered meditative absorption, and caused these monks to recall their own past lives on their own: that they had, long ago, planted every root of virtue in the presence of five hundred buddhas, and had dedicated that merit toward unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. At once it became clear to them, and they recovered their original resolve; thereupon the monks bowed their heads to Vimalakīrti's feet. Vimalakīrti then taught them the Dharma, so that they would never again turn back from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. I reflected that a śrāvaka who does not first observe a person's faculties should not teach the Dharma — this is why I am not able to go and inquire after his illness."

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Sengzhao's note on the difference in perceptual range between an arhat and a bodhisattva gives this vignette its real stakes: even the śrāvaka disciples with the deepest meditative concentration can, at best, see back some eighty thousand aeons into a being's past — and only while actually absorbed in that concentration, not as a standing capacity — while those with shallower concentration see barely more than a present head-count. A bodhisattva's vision, by contrast, is described as reaching without limit. Pūrṇa's fault was not laziness but exactly this gap: judging the monks' readiness from what his own considerable but bounded perception showed him, when what mattered — that they had already vowed themselves to the Great Vehicle lifetimes ago and simply forgotten it under present conditions — was invisible from where he stood.

This is also the one encounter among these first five where Vimalakīrti's method is not verbal at all. Rather than argue Pūrṇa out of his approach, he simply enters meditative absorption and lets the monks recover their own memory of the vow they had already made — the same vow Pūrṇa had just judged them incapable of, based only on what they showed him in the present moment. It is worth holding onto this variation in method as this chapter's larger pattern continues: not every exchange in this sūtra is won with words, and this one makes the case for Vimalakīrti's teaching entirely through what the monks themselves are shown to already contain.
