Seeing Akṣobhya Buddha

見阿閦佛品第十二

Asked how he actually sees the Tathāgata, Vimalakīrti answers with a long meditation built entirely of negations — neither this nor that, neither coming nor going, neither pure nor defiled. Śāriputra, startled by the sheer scale of what he has witnessed, asks where such a being could possibly have come from, and the answer is a shock in its own right: Vimalakīrti died out of a pure land called Wonderful Joy, whose buddha is Akṣobhya, "the Unmoved," to be born in this far harder world instead. The chapter closes with the sūtra's single most physical miracle — an entire buddha-land, severed free with one hand like clay from a potter's wheel, set down inside this one without disturbing a single grain of it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
Vimalakīrti's long negation-meditation on how to truly see the Tathāgata; Śāriputra's question about where Vimalakīrti died from, and the teaching that dying and being born are themselves illusions; the revelation that Vimalakīrti came from the pure land Wonderful Joy, home of the Buddha Akṣobhya, and the sun-and-darkness analogy for why a bodhisattva chooses a harder world instead; Vimalakīrti severing that entire land free with one hand and setting it down inside this world, unchanged, so the assembly can see it directly; and the sūtra's own closing account of the merit in hearing, holding, and passing on this teaching.

49. How to See the Tathāgata

Chinese Source

爾時世尊問維摩詰：「汝欲見如來，為以何等觀如來乎？」維摩詰言：「如自觀身實相，觀佛亦然。我觀如來前際不來，後際不去，今則不住；不觀色，不觀色如，不觀色性，不觀受、想、行、識，不觀識如，不觀識性，非四大起，同於虛空；六入無積，眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、心已過；不在三界，三垢已離，順三脫門，具足三明，與無明等；不一相、不異相，不自相、不他相，非無相、非取相；不此岸，不彼岸，不中流而化眾生；觀於寂滅，亦不永滅；不此不彼，不以此，不以彼；不可以智知，不可以識識；無晦無明，無名無相，無強無弱，非淨非穢；不在方，不離方，非有為，非無為，無示無說；不施不慳，不戒不犯，不忍不恚，不進不怠，不定不亂，不智不愚，不誠不欺，不來不去，不出不入，一切言語道斷；非福田，非不福田，非應供養，非不應供養；非取非捨，非有相，非無相；同真際，等法性，不可稱，不可量，過諸稱量；非大非小，非見非聞，非覺非知，離眾結縛；等諸智，同眾生，於諸法無分別；一切無失，無濁無惱，無作無起，無生無滅；無畏無憂，無喜無厭無著；無已有，無當有，無今有；不可以一切言說分別顯示：世尊！如來身為若此，作如是觀，以斯觀者，名為正觀；若他觀者，名為邪觀。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the World-Honored One asked Vimalakīrti: "When you wish to see the Tathāgata, by what manner of contemplation do you actually see him?" Vimalakīrti said: "Just as I contemplate the true reality of my own body, so too do I contemplate the Buddha. I contemplate that the Tathāgata does not come from any prior moment, does not go toward any future moment, and does not abide in the present moment either. I do not contemplate his form, nor the suchness of his form, nor the nature of his form; I do not contemplate his feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness, nor the suchness of his consciousness, nor the nature of his consciousness. He does not arise from the four great elements, but is the same as empty space. The six sense-bases hold nothing accumulated within him; his eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind have already passed beyond all of that. He does not abide within the three realms; he has already left behind the three defilements, and accords with the three gates of liberation; he is complete in the three insights, yet these are equal to ignorance itself. He is neither a single mark nor a different mark, neither his own mark nor another's mark; he is neither without any mark at all, nor a mark to be grasped at. He is neither this shore, nor the far shore, nor the current between them, and yet he converts beings all the same; he contemplates utter stillness, yet is not permanently extinguished. He is neither this nor that, neither by means of this nor by means of that; he cannot be known by ordinary knowledge, nor cognized by ordinary consciousness. He is without darkness and without brightness, without name and without mark, without strength and without weakness; he is neither pure nor defiled. He is not located in any direction, yet is not apart from any direction either; he is neither conditioned nor unconditioned; he has nothing to display and nothing to explain. He is neither giving nor withholding, neither disciplined nor transgressing, neither patient nor angered, neither diligent nor idle, neither settled nor scattered, neither wise nor foolish, neither sincere nor deceitful, neither coming nor going, neither emerging nor entering — every path of language is entirely cut off before him. He is neither a field of merit, nor not a field of merit; neither worthy of offerings, nor unworthy of them. He is neither to be grasped, nor to be relinquished; neither possessed of a mark, nor without one. He is the same as the true limit of reality, equal to the nature of all phenomena; he cannot be named, cannot be measured, surpassing every possible measure. He is neither great nor small, neither seen nor heard, neither sensed nor known, free of every bond and fetter. He is equal to every wisdom, the same as every being, making no distinction whatsoever among any phenomenon. In him there is nothing lost and nothing gained, no turbidity and no distress, no action and no arising, no birth and no cessation. He is without fear and without sorrow, without joy, without aversion, and without attachment. He has no existence already past, no existence yet to come, and no existence in the present moment either — and he cannot be shown or distinguished by any manner of speech whatsoever. World-Honored One, the Tathāgata's own body is exactly such as this, and this is how I contemplate it. To contemplate in this way is what is called correct contemplation; to contemplate in any other way is what is called mistaken contemplation."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva, Sengzhao, and Daosheng

Kumārajīva frames the whole passage as using the same method of negating every fixed characteristic found in the Mādhyamika and Śata treatises, applied here to make clear the Buddha's own emptiness of any fixed nature — which is why he glosses the chapter's governing method once at the start rather than annotating each individual negation on its own. Sengzhao's opening description of what "buddha" even names is worth having whole: "the utmost designation for one who has exhausted principle and fully realized his own nature — a great awakening. This way is empty and mysterious, entirely cut off from any ordinary bound: the mind cannot know it by intellection, form cannot gauge it by image. It acts the same as every other thing while dwelling in a realm of non-action; it exists within the domain of number and speech while resting in a homeland beyond all speech. It is not existent, yet cannot be called nonexistent; not nonexistent, yet cannot be called existent — silent and vast, nothing can fathom it, and not knowing what else to call it, one is forced to call it 'awakening.'" His governing principle for the entire chain of negations that follows is equally worth keeping: "whoever is one with what is attained, attains along with it; whoever is one with what is lost, loses along with it — so one who is true becomes one with the true, one who is false becomes one with the false." The Tathāgata's own numinous awareness is silently attuned to the one true reality, and the mark of that true reality is itself the mark of the Tathāgata — which is why, Sengzhao notes, this very sūtra elsewhere says that to see the mark of true reality is to see the Buddha. Vimalakīrti's claim that contemplating his own body's true reality and contemplating the Buddha are one and the same act rests entirely on this identity.

Daosheng's reading of the opening clause — "he does not come from any prior moment, does not go toward any future moment, does not abide in the present moment" — is worth having for its plain logical force: if seeing the Buddha meant literally seeing this particular figure as buddha, that premise is already mistaken; not seeing any buddha there at all is the true seeing of the Buddha. Were there a buddha in the past, he should still be coming toward the present now — but he does not come, which shows he was never truly in the past. Were there a buddha in the future, he should be receding away from the present — but he does not go, which shows he will never truly be in the future. Were there a buddha in the present, he should be abiding somewhere right now — but he does not abide, which shows he is not truly in the present either. It is worth reading this whole meditation alongside the other long negation-chains this sūtra has already built — the goddess's list of what beings resemble, the thirty-one bodhisattvas' answers on nonduality — as the same underlying method turned, for the first time in this text, directly onto the figure of the Buddha himself rather than onto beings or phenomena in general.

50. Neither Dying Nor Being Born

Chinese Source

爾時舍利弗問維摩詰：「汝於何沒而來生此？」維摩詰言：「汝所得法有沒生乎？」舍利弗言：「無沒生也。」「若諸法無沒生相，云何問言：『汝於何沒而來生此？』於意云何？譬如幻師，幻作男女，寧沒生耶？」舍利弗言：「無沒生也。」「汝豈不聞佛說諸法如幻相乎？」答曰：「如是！」「若一切法如幻相者，云何問言：『汝於何沒而來生此？』舍利弗！沒者為虛誑法，敗壞之相；生者為虛誑法，相續之相。菩薩雖沒，不盡善本；雖生，不長諸惡。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Śāriputra asked Vimalakīrti: "Where did you die from, to come and be born here?" Vimalakīrti said: "Does the Dharma you yourself have attained involve any dying and being born?" Śāriputra said: "It does not." "If no phenomenon has any mark of dying and being born, then why do you ask, 'Where did you die from, to come and be born here?' What do you think — is it like a conjurer who conjures up an illusory man or woman: does that illusion die and get born?" Śāriputra said: "It does not." "Have you never heard the Buddha teach that every phenomenon is just like an illusion?" He answered: "I have — it is so." "If every phenomenon is just like an illusion, then why do you ask, 'Where did you die from, to come and be born here?' Śāriputra, dying is itself a deceptive phenomenon, the mark of something falling into ruin; being born is itself a deceptive phenomenon, the mark of something continuing on. Though a bodhisattva dies, he does not exhaust his roots of goodness; though he is born, he does not increase any evil."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva reads Śāriputra's question as arising naturally from what he has just witnessed: such extraordinary spiritual virtue must surely have come from an exalted place, so he asks where. Vimalakīrti, worried that the question itself smuggles in a belief in real dying and being born, turns it back on Śāriputra directly rather than simply answering it — and Kumārajīva notes a second possible motive as well: some might suspect Vimalakīrti's own allotted lifespan is not yet exhausted, hence the pointed question about his death, and the answer — that a bodhisattva "does not exhaust his roots of goodness, does not increase evil" — is itself the real answer to that suspicion, showing his time in this life to be complete entirely on his own terms rather than cut short. Sengzhao's gloss on the illusionist analogy is characteristically compact: birth is like an illusion appearing, death is like an illusion departing, and nothing whatsoever is exempt from this — so what, exactly, was the question even asking about in the first place?

The distinction that closes the exchange is the section's real point, and Kumārajīva's explanation of it is worth having in full: an ordinary being's roots of goodness are already exhausted by the time death comes, so their mind at that final moment is inevitably distorted, and it is from that very distorted mind that their next birth proceeds — which is why, for an ordinary being, dying extinguishes what good remained and being born increases what evil follows. A bodhisattva is nothing like this: his own transformation from one life to the next is entirely pure, going wherever he wills by his own free choice, so he is simply free of the one affliction that governs an ordinary being's cycle of death and rebirth. Read this way, the chapter's real answer to "where did you die and come from" turns out not to be a place at all, but a description of what kind of mind is doing the dying and the being born — which is exactly what makes the specific place named in the very next passage worth taking seriously rather than as mere scenery.

51. The Land of Wonderful Joy

Chinese Source

是時佛告舍利弗：「有國名妙喜，佛號無動。是維摩詰於彼國沒，而來生此。」舍利弗言：「未曾有也！世尊！是人乃能捨清淨土，而來樂此多怒害處。」維摩詰語舍利弗：「於意云何？日光出時與冥合乎？」答曰：「不也！日光出時，即無眾冥。」維摩詰言：「夫日何故行閻浮提？」答曰：「欲以明照，為之除冥。」維摩詰言：「菩薩如是！雖生不淨佛土，為化眾生故，不與愚闇而共合也，但滅眾生煩惱闇耳！」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the Buddha told Śāriputra: "There is a land called Wonderful Joy, whose buddha is named Akṣobhya, 'the Unmoved.' It is from that land that Vimalakīrti died, and came to be born here." Śāriputra said: "This is truly unprecedented, World-Honored One! That such a man should be able to give up a pure land, and come instead to take pleasure in this place of so much anger and harm!" Vimalakīrti said to Śāriputra: "What do you think — when the sun's light appears, does it ever mingle together with darkness?" He answered: "No — when the sun's light appears, there is no darkness left at all." Vimalakīrti said: "Then why does the sun travel through Jambudvīpa at all?" He answered: "It wishes to illuminate it with its brightness, and to remove its darkness by doing so." Vimalakīrti said: "A bodhisattva is exactly the same. Though he is born into an impure buddha-land, it is in order to convert its beings — he never mingles together with their ignorance and darkness; he only extinguishes the darkness of their affliction."

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Sengzhao's own gloss on Śāriputra's astonishment is short but telling in what it concedes: "this world, compared to every other land, has the most anger and harm in it" — a small, matter-of-fact admission from within the tradition's own commentary about how this particular world is regarded relative to others, echoing the same "hard world" framing this sūtra has used since the fragrant-rice chapter's contrast between teaching by fragrance and teaching by harsh words.

The sun-and-darkness analogy is the section's real teaching, and it earns a slow reading, since it resolves what could otherwise look like a straightforward contradiction — how can a being from a pure land live in an impure one without becoming impure himself? The sun does not travel through Jambudvīpa in order to become dim by association with the dark it passes through; it travels there precisely because darkness is the one condition its own light is suited to remove, and the two never actually mix even while occupying the same place at the same time. Vimalakīrti's closing line makes the parallel explicit and complete: a bodhisattva enters an impure land purely for the sake of converting its beings, never joining with their ignorance, only extinguishing it — the same non-mingling this sūtra's very first chapters described in Vimalakīrti's own household, now generalized into a principle for why a bodhisattva chooses a difficult world at all.

52. Lifting a Buddha-Land in One Hand

Chinese Source

是時大眾渴仰，欲見妙喜世界無動如來，及其菩薩、聲聞之眾。佛知一切眾會所念，告維摩詰言：「善男子！為此眾會，現妙喜國無動如來，及諸菩薩、聲聞之眾，眾皆欲見。」於是維摩詰心念：「吾當不起于座，接妙喜國鐵圍山川、溪谷江河、大海泉源、須彌諸山及日月星宿、天龍鬼神、梵天等宮，并諸菩薩、聲聞之眾，城邑聚落、男女大小乃至無動如來，及菩提樹、諸妙蓮華，能於十方作佛事者——三道寶階從閻浮提至忉利天，以此寶階諸天來下，悉為禮敬無動如來，聽受經法；閻浮提人亦登其階，上昇忉利，見彼諸天：妙喜世界成就如是無量功德——上至阿迦膩吒天，下至水際，以右手斷取，如陶家輪，入此世界，猶持華鬘，示一切眾。」作是念已，入於三昧現神通力，以其右手斷取妙喜世界，置於此土。彼得神通菩薩及聲聞眾，并餘天、人俱發聲言：「唯然，世尊！誰取我去，願見救護。」無動佛言：「非我所為，是維摩詰神力所作。」其餘未得神通者，不覺不知己之所往。妙喜世界，雖入此土而不增減，於是世界亦不迫隘，如本無異。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that moment the whole assembly, filled with longing, wished to see the land of Wonderful Joy, the Tathāgata Akṣobhya, and his assembly of bodhisattvas and śrāvakas. The Buddha, knowing what the whole assembly was thinking, said to Vimalakīrti: "Good man, for the sake of this assembly, display the land of Wonderful Joy, the Tathāgata Akṣobhya, and his assembly of bodhisattvas and śrāvakas — everyone here wishes to see it." Vimalakīrti then thought to himself: "Without rising from my seat, I will take up the land of Wonderful Joy — its ring of iron mountains, its rivers and valleys, its streams and great oceans and springs, Mount Sumeru and its surrounding peaks, its sun and moon and constellations, the palaces of its devas, dragons, spirits, and Brahmā-heaven gods, together with its assembly of bodhisattvas and śrāvakas, its cities and towns, its men and women, young and old, and even the Tathāgata Akṣobhya himself, along with his bodhi tree and its wondrous lotus flowers, everything there capable of performing the buddha's work throughout the ten directions — including the three jeweled stairways leading from Jambudvīpa up to the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, by which the devas come down to pay reverence to the Tathāgata Akṣobhya and hear the Dharma taught, and by which the people of Jambudvīpa likewise climb up to Trāyastriṃśa, to see those very devas — the land of Wonderful Joy, complete with every one of these immeasurable merits, from the Akaniṣṭha heaven above down to the very edge of the water below — I will sever it away with my right hand, as a potter cuts clay from his wheel, and bring it into this world, holding it just as one might hold a garland of flowers, to show to this whole assembly." Having thought this, he entered into meditative absorption, displayed his spiritual power, and with his right hand severed away the land of Wonderful Joy, and set it down within this world. The bodhisattvas and śrāvakas of that land who had already attained spiritual powers, together with its remaining devas and humans, all cried out together: "Oh, World-Honored One! Who has taken us away like this? We beg to be rescued and protected!" The Buddha Akṣobhya said: "This is not my own doing — it is the spiritual power of Vimalakīrti that has done this." As for the rest, who had not yet attained any spiritual powers, they did not even notice or realize that they had been moved anywhere at all. And though the land of Wonderful Joy had now entered this world, it was neither increased nor diminished in size, nor was this world itself made any more crowded by it — everything remained exactly as it had been before.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva explains why Vimalakīrti brings the entire land here in person rather than simply conjuring a distant vision of it: doing so displays his own spiritual power directly, letting those who witness the event feel genuine joy and come to a deeper realization through the concrete fact of it, rather than through a mere description. He also flags a small but telling translation choice in the "potter's wheel" image: the Sanskrit source text reads only "as one cuts clay," while the Chinese here specifies cutting exactly from the center, the way a potter's wheel is cut, so that nothing remains attached at the base and nothing is severed unevenly at the edges — a nice example of a translator sharpening a comparison rather than merely carrying it over. Sengzhao's gloss on the two different reactions among those abducted along with their land is worth pausing on for its own quiet observation: those who already possess spiritual powers perceive the change at once and cry out in alarm, while those without such powers notice nothing whatsoever — a genuinely enormous event, in other words, is only as large as the perceiver's own capacity to register it.

The section's real payoff comes in its closing line: that Wonderful Joy was "neither increased nor diminished," and that this world was not made "any more crowded" by receiving an entire buddha-land inside it. This is the same logic that ran through two of this sūtra's earlier set pieces — the thirty-two thousand borrowed lion-thrones that fit inside Vimalakīrti's one small room, and Mount Sumeru set inside a single mustard seed without either growing smaller or the seed growing larger — now performed at the scale of an entire world rather than a single room or seed. Readers who have followed this sūtra from its earlier chapters will recognize the pattern immediately: what looks like an impossible feat of container-and-contained is, each time, the same demonstration that "large" and "small" were never fixed properties of anything to begin with.

53. Fourteen Nayutas Resolve to Be Reborn There

Chinese Source

爾時釋迦牟尼佛告諸大眾：「汝等且觀妙喜世界無動如來，其國嚴飾，菩薩行淨，弟子清白。」皆曰：「唯然已見。」佛言：「若菩薩欲得如是清淨佛土，當學無動如來所行之道。」現此妙喜國時，娑婆世界十四那由他人，發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，皆願生於妙喜佛土。釋迦牟尼佛即記之曰：「當生彼國。」時妙喜世界於此國土所應饒益其事訖已，還復本處，舉眾皆見。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Śākyamuni Buddha said to the whole assembly: "Now look upon the land of Wonderful Joy, and the Tathāgata Akṣobhya — how adorned that land is, how pure the bodhisattvas' conduct there, and how spotless its disciples are." They all said: "Indeed, we have seen it." The Buddha said: "If a bodhisattva wishes to attain a pure buddha-land exactly like this one, he should study the path that the Tathāgata Akṣobhya himself has practiced." At the moment this land of Wonderful Joy was displayed, fourteen nayutas of people in the Sahā world gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening, and all vowed to be reborn in the buddha-land of Wonderful Joy. Śākyamuni Buddha immediately gave them this prophecy: "You shall be reborn in that land." Once the land of Wonderful Joy had finished accomplishing whatever benefit it was meant to bring to this world, it returned to its original place, and the whole assembly watched it go.

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Sengzhao's gloss on why the Buddha directs the assembly to study Akṣobhya's own path, rather than simply admiring the finished result they have just seen, is a single memorable line: "climbing high always proceeds from a foundation; seeking a fruit always requires tracing it back to its cause." A pure land this complete is not a prize to be wished for on its own; it is the visible outcome of a specific practice, and the display exists to point beings back toward that practice rather than to end in admiration for the display itself. Sengzhao also glosses the number given for the crowd's response — a nayuta being, in his accounting, one hundred thousand — which puts the number of beings who resolve toward awakening on the spot at something on the order of a million and a half, a concrete measure of how immediately the teaching lands, rather than remaining a purely abstract lesson about pure lands in general.

54. The Merit of This Sūtra

Chinese Source

佛告舍利弗：「汝見此妙喜世界及無動佛不？」「唯然已見，世尊！願使一切眾生得清淨土，如無動佛；獲神通力，如維摩詰。世尊！我等快得善利，得見是人，親近供養。其諸眾生，若今現在，若佛滅後，聞此經者，亦得善利；況復聞已信解、受持、讀誦、解說，如法修行？若有手得是經典者，便為已得法寶之藏；若有讀誦解釋其義，如說修行，即為諸佛之所護念；其有供養如是人者，當知即為供養於佛；其有書持此經卷者，當知其室即有如來；若聞是經能隨喜者，斯人即為取一切智；若能信解此經，乃至一四句偈，為他說者，當知此人即是受阿耨多羅三藐三菩提記。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "Have you seen this land of Wonderful Joy, and the Buddha Akṣobhya?" "Indeed, I have seen it, World-Honored One. I wish that every being might attain a pure land such as the Buddha Akṣobhya's, and might gain spiritual power such as Vimalakīrti's. World-Honored One, we ourselves have gained a swift and excellent benefit, in being able to see this man, and to draw near and make offerings to him. And every being — whether living now, or in the age after the Buddha's own passing — who hears this sūtra will likewise gain an excellent benefit; how much more so one who, having heard it, believes and understands it, upholds it, recites it, explains it, and practices in accordance with it? Whoever simply takes this sūtra into their own hands has already obtained a treasury of the Dharma's own jewels; whoever reads, recites, and explains its meaning, and practices as it teaches, is thereby held in mind and protected by every buddha; whoever makes offerings to such a person should know that this is itself an offering made to the Buddha; whoever copies out and keeps this sūtra should know that the Tathāgata himself dwells within that very room; whoever hears this sūtra and rejoices in it has thereby already taken hold of omniscience itself; and whoever is able to believe in and understand this sūtra, and explains even a single four-line verse of it to another person, should know that this person has already received the prophecy of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening."

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Sengzhao reads the Buddha's question to Śāriputra — asked right after the land of Wonderful Joy has already returned to its own place — as deliberately underlining, through Śāriputra in particular, just how rare it is for such a gathering to assemble at all, and how rare it is to hear a teaching such as this sūtra even once. The graduated ladder of merit that closes the chapter repays walking through slowly with Sengzhao's own distinctions in hand. Simply holding the physical scroll, he says, is like a person who has come into possession of a treasury of jewels but has not yet learned how to make use of them; actually reading, reciting, and explaining the text, and practicing in accordance with it, is what draws the protection of every buddha; copying and keeping the text is weightier still than merely holding it, since it makes the very room where it is kept a place where the Tathāgata himself dwells; and hearing the sūtra with genuine rejoicing already counts, in his account, as having taken hold of omniscience in outline — though it stops one rung short of the final one. That last rung belongs specifically to explaining even a single four-line verse of the sūtra to another person, which alone earns an outright prophecy of future buddhahood, since it requires not merely receiving the teaching but successfully carrying it forward to someone else.
