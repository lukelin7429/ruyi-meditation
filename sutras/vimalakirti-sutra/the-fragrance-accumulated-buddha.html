The Fragrance-Accumulated Buddha

香積佛品第十

Śāriputra quietly worries about where the assembly will eat, and Vimalakīrti answers by showing everyone a buddha-land built entirely of fragrance, forty-two Ganges' worth of buddha-lands above this one. A conjured bodhisattva is sent to fetch a single bowl of its rice, which turns out to feed the entire gathering without ever running low. And when the rice's own land asks how their buddha teaches, the answer — fragrance alone, no words at all — sets up the sharpest possible contrast with how Śākyamuni has to teach a harder world.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
Śāriputra's silent worry about the midday meal and Vimalakīrti's vision of the Land of Many Fragrances; the conjured bodhisattva sent to fetch a bowl of that land's rice, and the nine million bodhisattvas who volunteer to come see Vimalakīrti for themselves; the single bowl that feeds the entire assembly at Vaiśālī without ever being exhausted; the contrast Vimalakīrti draws between a buddha who teaches through fragrance alone and one who must teach this harder world with harsh words; and the ten wholesome practices found only in a difficult world, plus the eight qualities that let a bodhisattva move through it toward a pure land unblemished.

38. Śāriputra's Hunger, and the Land of Many Fragrances

Chinese Source

於是舍利弗心念：「日時欲至，此諸菩薩當於何食？」時維摩詰知其意而語言：「佛說八解脫，仁者受行，豈雜欲食而聞法乎？若欲食者，且待須臾，當令汝得未曾有食。」時維摩詰即入三昧，以神通力示諸大眾上方界分，過四十二恒河沙佛土，有國名眾香，佛號香積，今現在。其國香氣，比於十方諸佛世界人、天之香，最為第一。彼土無有聲聞、辟支佛名，唯有清淨大菩薩眾，佛為說法。其界一切皆以香作樓閣，經行香地，苑園皆香。其食香氣，周流十方無量世界。時彼佛與諸菩薩方共坐食，有諸天子皆號香嚴，悉發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，供養彼佛及諸菩薩，此諸大眾莫不目見。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Śāriputra thought to himself: "The hour for the midday meal is nearly here — where will all these bodhisattvas eat?" Vimalakīrti, knowing his thought, said to him: "The Buddha has taught the eight liberations, and you, sir, have received and practiced them — how could you mix a craving for food together with listening to the Dharma? If you wish to eat, wait just a little while, and I will see that you obtain a meal such as you have never had before." Vimalakīrti then entered into meditative absorption, and by his spiritual power showed the whole assembly a realm in the direction above, past as many buddha-lands as there are grains of sand in forty-two Ganges rivers, where there is a land called Many Fragrances, whose buddha, named Fragrance-Accumulated, dwells there even now. The fragrance of that land surpasses the fragrance of the devas and humans of every buddha-world in the ten directions — it is foremost above them all. In that land there is not even the name of a śrāvaka or a pratyekabuddha; there is only a pure assembly of great bodhisattvas, to whom the buddha expounds the Dharma. Throughout that realm, every single thing is made of fragrance — its towers and pavilions, the fragrant ground one walks upon, its gardens and groves, all of it fragrant — and the fragrance of its food flows out through the immeasurable worlds of the ten directions. At that very moment, this buddha was sitting down to eat together with his bodhisattvas, and there were devaputras there, every one of them named Fragrance-Adorned, who had all given rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening, making offerings to that buddha and his bodhisattvas — and there was not a single person in this whole assembly who did not see it all with their own eyes.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva gives three reasons why it is Śāriputra, and no one else, who quietly worries about lunch: his own karmic bondage is not yet so exhausted that he can simply do without food; his aspiration has not gone deep enough into the Great Vehicle for his joy in the Dharma alone to override hunger; and, being foremost among the assembled disciples, he naturally assumes the rest of the assembly must feel exactly as he does, and takes it as his own responsibility to see that everyone is fed. Sengzhao reads the moment as ordinary narrative staging put to real use — seating guests and arranging a meal is simply how any gathering works, and the sūtra uses that everyday expectation, with Śāriputra as the assembly's senior figure voicing it, to open a real doctrinal exchange. Vimalakīrti's rebuke draws its force from exactly what Śāriputra has already personally received: the Buddha's own teaching of the eight liberations is, in Sengzhao's words, "the fine fare of desirelessness, the finest food that nourishes the Dharma-body" — and Kumārajīva sharpens the point further by noting that the very first of the eight liberations begins with contemplating the body's own impurity, so a mind that genuinely held all eight in view could hardly still be craving food, itself among the unclean things that contemplation is meant to see through.

Kumārajīva also explains why Vimalakīrti does not simply conjure up an ordinary meal on the spot, which would have been the simpler magic trick: doing so would risk the appearance of illusion or outright deception, and ordinary food, however conjured, would not be "pure and wondrous" enough to actually benefit anyone. Sending instead to the Land of Many Fragrances lets the entire assembly see a genuinely pure buddha-land with their own eyes, and lets the very food that results plant the aspiration to the great way in everyone who eats it — the meal is the occasion, but the pure land it is fetched from is the actual point.

39. Sending for the Rice

Chinese Source

時維摩詰問眾菩薩言：「諸仁者！誰能致彼佛飯？」以文殊師利威神力故，咸皆默然。維摩詰言：「仁此大眾，無乃可恥？」文殊師利曰：「如佛所言，勿輕未學。」於是維摩詰不起于座，居眾會前化作菩薩——相好光明，威德殊勝，蔽於眾會——而告之曰：「汝往上方界分，度如四十二恒河沙佛土，有國名眾香，佛號香積，與諸菩薩方共坐食。汝往到彼，如我辭曰：『維摩詰稽首世尊足下！致敬無量，問訊起居，少病少惱，氣力安不？願得世尊所食之餘，當於娑婆世界施作佛事，令此樂小法者得弘大道，亦使如來名聲普聞。』」時化菩薩即於會前，昇于上方。舉眾皆見其去，到眾香界，禮彼佛足，又聞其言：「維摩詰稽首世尊足下！致敬無量，問訊起居，少病少惱，氣力安不？願得世尊所食之餘，欲於娑婆世界施作佛事，使此樂小法者得弘大道，亦使如來名聲普聞。」彼諸大士見化菩薩，歎未曾有：「今此上人從何所來？娑婆世界為在何許？云何名為樂小法者？」即以問佛。佛告之曰：「下方度如四十二恒河沙佛土，有世界名娑婆，佛號釋迦牟尼，今現在。於五濁惡世，為樂小法眾生敷演道教。彼有菩薩名維摩詰，住不可思議解脫，為諸菩薩說法，故遣化來，稱揚我名，并讚此土，令彼菩薩增益功德。」彼菩薩言：「其人何如，乃作是化？德力無畏，神足若斯！」佛言：「甚大！一切十方皆遣化往，施作佛事饒益眾生。」於是香積如來以眾香鉢盛滿香飯，與化菩薩。時彼九百萬菩薩俱發聲言：「我欲詣娑婆世界供養釋迦牟尼佛，并欲見維摩詰等諸菩薩眾。」佛言：「可往。攝汝身香，無令彼諸眾生起惑著心；又當捨汝本形，勿使彼國求菩薩者，而自鄙恥；又汝於彼莫懷輕賤，而作礙想。所以者何？十方國土，皆如虛空，又諸佛為欲化諸樂小法者，不盡現其清淨土耳！」時化菩薩既受鉢飯，與彼九百萬菩薩俱，承佛威神及維摩詰力，於彼世界忽然不現，須臾之間至維摩詰舍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Vimalakīrti then asked the assembled bodhisattvas: "Noble sirs, who among you can go and bring back that buddha's rice?" But because of Mañjuśrī's own majestic spiritual power, every one of them fell silent. Vimalakīrti said: "Are you not ashamed, before an assembly as great as this?" Mañjuśrī said: "As the Buddha himself has taught, one should not scorn those who are still beginners." Then, without rising from his seat, Vimalakīrti, right there before the whole assembly, conjured up a bodhisattva — his excellent marks and radiance, his majestic virtue, so surpassing that he outshone the entire gathering — and instructed him: "Go to the realm above, past as many buddha-lands as there are grains of sand in forty-two Ganges rivers, to the land called Many Fragrances, whose buddha, Fragrance-Accumulated, is even now sitting down to eat together with his bodhisattvas. When you arrive there, speak these words on my behalf: 'Vimalakīrti bows his head at the World-Honored One's feet, and offers his boundless reverence. He asks after your health and daily life — are you free of illness, free of trouble, and are your strength and vigor at ease? He wishes to receive what remains of the World-Honored One's own meal, so that he may use it to perform a buddha's work in the Sahā world, that those there who delight in lesser teachings may come to embrace the great way, and that the Tathāgata's own renown may be heard everywhere.'" The conjured bodhisattva rose up into the realm above right there before the assembly, and the whole gathering watched him go. He arrived in the realm of Many Fragrances, bowed at that buddha's feet, and they heard him say: "Vimalakīrti bows his head at the World-Honored One's feet, and offers his boundless reverence. He asks after your health and daily life — are you free of illness, free of trouble, and are your strength and vigor at ease? He wishes to receive what remains of the World-Honored One's own meal, wishing to use it to perform a buddha's work in the Sahā world, that those there who delight in lesser teachings may come to embrace the great way, and that the Tathāgata's own renown may be heard everywhere." The great beings of that land, seeing the conjured bodhisattva, exclaimed in wonder at this unprecedented thing: "Where has this superior being come from? Where is this Sahā world? And what does it mean, to 'delight in lesser teachings'?" And they asked their buddha about it. The buddha told them: "Below, past as many buddha-lands as there are grains of sand in forty-two Ganges rivers, there is a world called Sahā, whose buddha, Śākyamuni, dwells there even now, expounding the teaching of the way, amid the world's five corruptions, for beings who delight in lesser teachings. There is a bodhisattva there named Vimalakīrti, who abides in the inconceivable liberation and teaches the Dharma to the bodhisattvas of that world; it is he who has sent this conjured being here, to praise my name, and to praise this land as well, so that the bodhisattvas here may increase their own merit." The bodhisattvas there said: "What sort of person is he, to be able to work such a transformation? Such virtue, such fearless power, such spiritual mastery!" The buddha said: "Very great indeed — he sends such conjured beings to every one of the ten directions, to perform a buddha's work and benefit beings there." Then the Tathāgata Fragrance-Accumulated filled a bowl of many fragrances to the brim with fragrant rice, and gave it to the conjured bodhisattva. At that, nine million bodhisattvas of that land all spoke together with one voice: "We wish to go to the Sahā world, to make offerings to Śākyamuni Buddha, and also to see Vimalakīrti and the assembly of bodhisattvas there." The buddha said: "You may go. But gather in your own bodily fragrance, so that you do not stir up confused attachment in the beings there; and set aside your own true forms as well, so that those in that world who seek the bodhisattva path will not feel ashamed before you; and do not, while there, hold them in contempt, or form any thought that obstructs them. And why is this? Because every land in the ten directions is, in truth, just like empty space — and because the buddhas, wishing to convert those who delight in lesser teachings, do not always display the whole of their own pure lands." The conjured bodhisattva, having received the bowl of rice, then vanished from that world all at once, together with the nine million bodhisattvas, by the majestic power of the Buddha and the power of Vimalakīrti alike, and in a mere instant arrived at Vimalakīrti's house.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Sengzhao is direct about why Mañjuśrī silences the whole assembly at exactly this moment: it is "in order to bring out Vimalakīrti's own virtue" — the staged silence exists so that Vimalakīrti's rebuke, and the solo feat of conjuring an emanation-bodhisattva that follows it, can happen at all. The brief exchange that follows — "are you not ashamed?" against "don't scorn beginners" — Sengzhao reads as two halves of one lesson: one line spurs on those who have not yet accomplished anything, the other guards those same beginners from being shamed even while they are being spurred. Kumārajīva also catches a small but deliberate piece of phrasing in the message itself: "free of illness, free of trouble" says "few," not "none," and this is no accident — even a buddha's own displayed body remains, in its very nature as a body, never wholly free of any susceptibility to trouble at all, so the humbler phrasing is the accurate one. He also widens the sense of "those who delight in lesser teachings" beyond what it might first suggest: this names anyone whose aspiration simply does not reach very far, not narrowly practitioners of the lesser vehicle alone.

The Buddha's instructions to the nine million volunteering bodhisattvas repay a close look, since each clause is solving a distinct problem. Kumārajīva explains the logic plainly: excessive shame can genuinely destroy a person, and even a milder embarrassment can trouble them, so the warning addresses both dangers at once — concealing their fragrance so the beings of Sahā are not thrown into confused longing by it, and setting aside their own surpassing forms so that Sahā's own bodhisattva-seekers are not humiliated by the comparison. And Sengzhao's gloss on the reason given — "every land in the ten directions is, in truth, just like empty space" — generalizes the whole scene past this one buddha-land: choosing not to display the whole of one's own pure land at once is not evidence that the pure land is somehow less real or less present; it is itself an act of compassion, calibrated to exactly what beings who delight in lesser teachings are actually able to receive without being overwhelmed by it.

40. The Rice That Cannot Be Exhausted

Chinese Source

時維摩詰即化作九百萬師子之座，嚴好如前，諸菩薩皆坐其上。是化菩薩以滿鉢香飯與維摩詰，飯香普熏毘耶離城及三千大千世界。時毘耶離婆羅門、居士等，聞是香氣，身意快然，歎未曾有！於是長者主月蓋從八萬四千人，來入維摩詰舍。見其室中菩薩甚多，諸師子座高廣嚴好，皆大歡喜，禮眾菩薩及大弟子，却住一面。諸地神、虛空神及欲、色界諸天，聞此香氣，亦皆來入維摩詰舍。時維摩詰語舍利弗等諸大聲聞：「仁者可食。如來甘露味飯，大悲所熏，無以限意食之，使不消也。」有異聲聞念：「是飯少，而此大眾人人當食。」化菩薩曰：「勿以聲聞小德小智稱量如來無量福慧！四海有竭，此飯無盡！使一切人食，揣若須彌，乃至一劫，猶不能盡。所以者何？無盡戒、定、智慧、解脫、解脫知見功德具足者所食之餘，終不可盡。」於是鉢飯悉飽眾會，猶故不[歹*斯]。其諸菩薩、聲聞、天、人，食此飯者，身安快樂，譬如一切樂莊嚴國諸菩薩也；又諸毛孔皆出妙香，亦如眾香國土諸樹之香。

Ru-Yi Translation

Vimalakīrti then conjured up nine million lion-thrones, splendid and excellent just as before, and every one of the bodhisattvas took a seat upon them. The conjured bodhisattva presented the bowl, filled with fragrant rice, to Vimalakīrti, and its fragrance spread out through the whole city of Vaiśālī and the entire billion-world universe. The brahmins and householders of Vaiśālī, catching this fragrance, felt at once at ease in body and mind, and exclaimed at this unprecedented thing. The elder Moon-Canopy — foremost among the city's householders — together with eighty-four thousand others, came at once to Vimalakīrti's house; seeing the great number of bodhisattvas within it, and the many lion-thrones, so tall, so vast, and so splendid, they were filled with great joy, bowed to the assembled bodhisattvas and the great disciples, and withdrew to stand to one side. The spirits of the earth, the spirits of empty space, and the devas of the realms of desire and of form, catching this same fragrance, likewise all came to Vimalakīrti's house. Vimalakīrti then said to Śāriputra and the other great śrāvakas: "You may eat, sirs. This is the Tathāgata's rice, with the flavor of ambrosia, permeated through and through by great compassion — do not eat it with a limited mind, or it will not be digested." One of the śrāvakas present, thinking otherwise, wondered: "This rice is so little, and yet this whole great assembly must eat from it." The conjured bodhisattva said: "Do not measure the Tathāgata's immeasurable merit and wisdom by a śrāvaka's own small virtue and small wisdom! The four great oceans themselves might run dry, but this rice will never be exhausted. Though every being were to eat from it, each taking a portion the size of Mount Sumeru, and though this continued for a full eon, it still could not be used up. And why is this? Because what remains of a meal eaten by one whose merit — inexhaustible discipline, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation — is itself already complete, can itself never be exhausted." And so this single bowl of rice satisfied the entire assembly to the full, and still it was not depleted. And all the bodhisattvas, śrāvakas, devas, and humans who ate of this rice found their bodies at ease and full of joy, exactly like the bodhisattvas of every world adorned entirely with joy; and from every pore of their bodies there came forth a wondrous fragrance, just like the fragrance of the trees of the land of Many Fragrances.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva, Sengzhao, and Daosheng

Kumārajīva's small aside on why Moon-Canopy is called the city's "chief" is worth having, since it says something about how this whole sūtra imagines Vaiśālī itself: the city had no king at all, but was instead governed jointly by five hundred elders, and Moon-Canopy is named "chief" simply because the others deferred to and esteemed him above the rest — a detail that quietly matches Vimalakīrti's own household, run by no fixed hierarchy either. Daosheng draws out why the rice is said to carry "the flavor of ambrosia" and not merely to smell wonderful: nirvāṇa itself is what the tradition calls the Dharma of ambrosia, and one who eats this particular food is, by that very act, bound eventually to attain it — so the ambrosia-flavor is not a poetic flourish but a literal claim about what the food actually transmits. Kumārajīva gives three senses of "permeated by great compassion" in the same line: the rice is the ripened fruit of great compassion's own karmic action, it is what great compassion's intent has specifically willed into being, and it is what has been looked upon with a compassionate eye — and Sengzhao draws out the practical instruction folded into Vimalakīrti's warning about eating "with a limited mind": eating this food without also giving rise to the vast intention that produced it leaves the debt of gratitude it represents unpaid, while eating it precisely while giving rise to the great aspiration to the path is what "digests" it — discharging that debt without any limit at all.

The doubting śrāvaka's quiet worry, and the conjured bodhisattva's sharp reply, turn on a point of plain logic that Sengzhao states directly: an inconceivable food is simply not something the two lesser vehicles' minds are built to estimate correctly, which is exactly why the doubt arises unbidden in the first place — and an immeasurable cause, the Tathāgata's own immeasurable merit and wisdom, must produce an immeasurable result; were the cause measurable, the result would be too, but no śrāvaka's small wisdom is equipped to measure a Tathāgata's cause, so it can hardly be expected to measure the result that cause produces. The rice, in other words, isn't being praised as unusually abundant so much as it is being used to demonstrate, in front of the very disciples who might doubt it, that scale itself is the wrong category to apply to it at all.

41. Two Ways of Teaching

Chinese Source

爾時維摩詰問眾香菩薩：「香積如來以何說法？」彼菩薩曰：「我土如來無文字說，但以眾香令諸天、人得入律行。菩薩各各坐香樹下，聞斯妙香，即獲一切德藏三昧。得是三昧者，菩薩所有功德皆悉具足。」彼諸菩薩問維摩詰：「今世尊釋迦牟尼以何說法？」維摩詰言：「此土眾生剛強難化，故佛為說剛強之語以調伏之。言是地獄、是畜生、是餓鬼，是諸難處，是愚人生處；是身邪行，是身邪行報；是口邪行，是口邪行報；是意邪行，是意邪行報；是殺生，是殺生報；是不與取，是不與取報；是邪婬，是邪婬報；是妄語，是妄語報；是兩舌，是兩舌報；是惡口，是惡口報；是無義語，是無義語報；是貪嫉，是貪嫉報；是瞋惱，是瞋惱報；是邪見，是邪見報；是慳悋，是慳悋報；是毀戒，是毀戒報；是瞋恚，是瞋恚報；是懈怠，是懈怠報；是亂意，是亂意報；是愚癡，是愚癡報；是結戒，是持戒，是犯戒；是應作，是不應作；是障礙，是不障礙；是得罪，是離罪；是淨，是垢；是有漏，是無漏；是邪道，是正道；是有為，是無為；是世間，是涅槃。以難化之人心如猨猴，故以若干種法制御其心，乃可調伏，譬如象馬，[怡-台+龍]悷不調，加諸楚毒乃至徹骨，然後調伏。如是剛強難化眾生，故以一切苦切之言，乃可入律。」彼諸菩薩聞說是已，皆曰：「未曾有也！如世尊釋迦牟尼佛，隱其無量自在之力，乃以貧所樂法度脫眾生；斯諸菩薩亦能勞謙，以無量大悲生是佛土。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Vimalakīrti asked the bodhisattvas of Many Fragrances: "By what means does the Tathāgata Fragrance-Accumulated teach the Dharma?" Those bodhisattvas answered: "In our land, the Tathāgata teaches with no words or writing at all — he simply causes the devas and humans to enter the conduct of discipline by means of fragrance alone. Each bodhisattva sits beneath a fragrant tree, and upon smelling this wondrous fragrance, at once attains the concentration called the treasury of every virtue; and one who attains this concentration finds every merit a bodhisattva could have already fully complete within them." Those bodhisattvas then asked Vimalakīrti in turn: "And by what means does the World-Honored One Śākyamuni teach the Dharma here?" Vimalakīrti said: "The beings of this land are obstinate and hard to convert, and so the Buddha teaches them with harsh and forceful words, in order to tame them. He says: this is hell, this is the animal realm, this is the realm of hungry ghosts — these are the difficult places; this is the place where fools are born. This is wrongdoing of the body, and this is its retribution; this is wrongdoing of speech, and this is its retribution; this is wrongdoing of the mind, and this is its retribution. This is killing, and this is its retribution; this is taking what is not given, and this is its retribution; this is sexual misconduct, and this is its retribution; this is false speech, and this is its retribution; this is divisive speech, and this is its retribution; this is harsh speech, and this is its retribution; this is idle chatter, and this is its retribution. This is covetousness, and this is its retribution; this is ill will, and this is its retribution; this is wrong view, and this is its retribution; this is stinginess, and this is its retribution; this is breaking the precepts, and this is its retribution; this is anger, and this is its retribution; this is laziness, and this is its retribution; this is a scattered mind, and this is its retribution; this is stupidity, and this is its retribution. This is the setting of a precept, this is keeping it, and this is transgressing it; this is what should be done, and this is what should not be done; this is an obstruction, and this is what does not obstruct; this is incurring an offense, and this is being free of one; this is purity, and this is defilement; this is what is contaminated, and this is what is uncontaminated; this is the wrong path, and this is the right path; this is the conditioned, and this is the unconditioned; this is the mundane, and this is nirvāṇa. Because the minds of beings so hard to convert are like monkeys, one must rein in their minds with any number of different methods before they can be tamed — just as an elephant or a horse, wild and unbroken, must be driven with goads until the pain reaches to the very bone before it is finally tamed. Just so, with beings this obstinate and hard to convert, only the harshest and most cutting words can bring them into discipline." When those bodhisattvas heard this explained, they all said: "This is truly unprecedented! That the World-Honored One Śākyamuni should conceal his own immeasurable, unhindered power, and instead deliver beings by means of the very teachings that the poor and lowly find easy to embrace — and that these bodhisattvas here should likewise be capable of such toil and humility, sustaining themselves in this buddha-land by nothing but their own immeasurable great compassion."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva frames the contrast within a threefold taxonomy of how a buddha teaches at all: gentle, kind words for those whose good conduct leads toward pleasant results; harsh, forceful words for those whose bad conduct leads toward suffering; and a mixed mode that praises the good and condemns the bad in turn. Sengzhao states the underlying principle simply: a sage's teaching has no fixed form of its own at all — it merely responds to whatever the beings in front of it actually are, and since the beings of this land happen to be obstinate, the teaching that can actually reach them is necessarily an obstinate one in turn. Kumārajīva's own image for why is worth having in full: he describes five grades of horse, ranked by how much force is needed to break them — the first is tamed merely by seeing the shadow of the whip, the second only once actually struck, the third only once pierced by a sharp goad through the skin, the fourth only once it reaches the flesh, and the fifth only once it reaches all the way to the bone — and maps this directly onto five grades of beings, ranked by how much it takes before their minds finally wake up: the sharpest wakes merely from observing impermanence in a stranger; the next only on seeing it in a friend; the next only in a sibling or relative; the next only in a parent; and the least sharp only after undergoing extreme suffering in their own body, and even then, only once harsh words are added on top of that suffering. Read this way, the sūtra's long litany of hells, retributions, and offenses is not gratuitous grimness — it is a description, graded by exactly how much force different minds actually require, of what it takes for the least responsive among them to hear anything at all.

The visiting bodhisattvas' closing exclamation is where the chapter's real argument lands. Sengzhao reads it this way: every buddha is equal in realization, yet their traces differ — not because their attainment differs, but because the beings each one responds to differ in how narrow or low their own aspiration happens to be. Concealing one's own unhindered power, and instead matching oneself exactly to what the poor and lowly can actually receive without turning away from it, is not a lesser display than fragrance alone teaching a land that never needed harsh words in the first place — on Sengzhao's reading, it is the more unprecedented feat of the two, precisely because it costs the teacher more to meet beings exactly where they already are than to shine from a height they were never going to reach.

42. Ten Things This Hard World Has, and Eight Qualities of an Unblemished Bodhisattva

Chinese Source

維摩詰言：「此土菩薩於諸眾生大悲堅固，誠如所言。然其一世饒益眾生，多於彼國百千劫行。所以者何？此娑婆世界有十事善法，諸餘淨土之所無有。何等為十？以布施攝貧窮，以淨戒攝毀禁，以忍辱攝瞋恚，以精進攝懈怠，以禪定攝亂意，以智慧攝愚癡，說除難法度八難者，以大乘法度樂小乘者，以諸善根濟無德者，常以四攝成就眾生，是為十。」彼菩薩曰：「菩薩成就幾法？於此世界行無瘡疣，生于淨土。」維摩詰言：「菩薩成就八法，於此世界行無瘡疣，生于淨土。何等為八？饒益眾生，而不望報；代一切眾生受諸苦惱，所作功德盡以施之；等心眾生，謙下無礙；於諸菩薩視之如佛；所未聞經，聞之不疑；不與聲聞而相違背；不嫉彼供，不高己利，而於其中調伏其心；常省己過，不訟彼短，恒以一心求諸功德，是為八法。」維摩詰、文殊師利於大眾中說是法時，百千天、人皆發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，十千菩薩得無生法忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Vimalakīrti said: "It is truly as you say — the bodhisattvas of this land are firm and unwavering in their great compassion for every being. And yet a single lifetime spent benefiting beings here accomplishes more than a hundred thousand eons of practice in that land of yours. And why is this? Because this Sahā world has ten wholesome practices that no other pure land possesses. What are these ten? To gather in the poor and destitute through generosity; to gather in those who break the precepts through pure discipline; to gather in the angry through patience; to gather in the lazy through diligence; to gather in the scattered-minded through meditative concentration; to gather in the foolish through wisdom; to teach the Dharma that removes the eight difficult conditions, to deliver those caught within them; to use the teaching of the Great Vehicle to deliver those who delight in the lesser vehicle; to use every root of goodness to rescue those without virtue; and to constantly bring beings to accomplishment through the four means of attraction. These are the ten." Those bodhisattvas then asked: "How many qualities must a bodhisattva accomplish, to move through this world without blemish, and be born into a pure land?" Vimalakīrti said: "A bodhisattva who accomplishes eight qualities moves through this world without blemish, and is born into a pure land. What are these eight? To benefit beings, expecting nothing in return; to undergo every kind of suffering and distress on behalf of every being, and to give away entirely whatever merit this produces; to regard every being with an equal mind, humble and unobstructed; to look upon every bodhisattva exactly as one would a buddha; to hear a sūtra one has never heard before, and feel no doubt about it; to never set oneself in opposition to the śrāvakas; to feel no envy at another's offerings, nor pride in one's own gain, and to tame one's own mind in the midst of both; and to constantly examine one's own faults, rather than dwell on the faults of others, seeking every merit always with a single, undivided mind. These are the eight." When Vimalakīrti and Mañjuśrī had finished teaching this Dharma before the great assembly, a hundred thousand devas and humans all gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening, and ten thousand bodhisattvas attained the acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva's own image for why a single lifetime here outweighs a hundred thousand eons in a pure land is worth keeping whole: a skilled physician who happens to live through an age of plague, with disease running unchecked everywhere around him, ends up treating vastly more patients, and earning vastly more merit through that very abundance of surrounding suffering, than an equally skilled physician living out an entire healthy age ever could. A bodhisattva working in an impure land is no different — the sheer abundance of affliction around him is exactly what makes his own remedy so much more extensive, and so much more consequential, than the same remedy offered where it is barely needed. Sengzhao makes the same point more compactly: good arises precisely out of evil, and purity increases precisely by way of impurity; this world has all ten unwholesome things in full measure, which is exactly why its ten corresponding virtues can grow here to the extent they do, while the other, purely good land offers no "ground" at all on which such giving could even take place.

Sengzhao's own gloss on the eight qualities is concise enough to use directly. Undergoing suffering in place of others without a thought of reward is, he says, "the utmost of benefiting beings" — the first quality; regarding friend and foe with the same mind, lowering oneself while elevating others, is impartiality with no distinguishing feeling left in it at all — the second; a bodhisattva is "the bridge beings cross by, and what the Three Jewels themselves depend on," so to regard one as a buddha is itself what increases one's own merit — the third; receiving an unfamiliar teaching with trust rather than doubt is the fourth; and not turning against the śrāvaka path, even though the three vehicles differ, since their final aim is not different, is the fifth. The remaining three — not envying another's gain, not exalting one's own, taming the mind in the midst of both; and examining one's own faults rather than dwelling on another's — Sengzhao treats as one continuous discipline of self-correction, closing with a line worth keeping intact: "examine one's own faults, and the fault dissolves on its own; dwell on another's faults, and the fault becomes one's own." For that last quality specifically, Kumārajīva offers a small, vivid story: a monk meditating in a forest, going out at mealtime and struck by an arrow from a bandit along the road, forgives the man on the spot and blames only himself — pointing to his own belly and telling the bandit that this, and not his heart, was the true target, since it was his own body's need for food that had brought him out of the forest and into the bandit's path to begin with.
