Vimalakīrti's Skillful Means

方便品第二

The sūtra's central figure, introduced in full before he ever speaks: a layman who moves through every level of Vaiśālī society without ever being confined by it, who manifests illness as a teaching device, and whose first sermon — on the body's impermanence, and on the Dharma-body that lies beyond it — draws thousands to the mind of awakening.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
Vimalakīrti's qualities and his skillful means among kings, ministers, elders, householders, and every other class of Vaiśālī society; his decision to manifest bodily illness in order to teach; his sermon on the body's impermanence, unreality, and impurity; and his teaching that the Buddha's true body is the Dharma-body, born from immeasurable merit and wisdom.
Who Vimalakīrti is
A layman of Vaiśālī (毘耶離), already accomplished in the bodhisattva path, who has not yet spoken a single word in the sūtra that bears his name — this entire chapter is the compilers' own introduction of him, not yet his own speech

1. Vimalakīrti, Introduced

Chinese Source

爾時毘耶離大城中有長者，名維摩詰，已曾供養無量諸佛，深植善本，得無生忍；辯才無礙，遊戲神通，逮諸總持；獲無所畏，降魔勞怨；入深法門，善於智度，通達方便，大願成就；明了眾生心之所趣，又能分別諸根利鈍，久於佛道，心已純淑，決定大乘；諸有所作，能善思量；住佛威儀，心大如海，諸佛咨嗟，弟子、釋、梵、世主所敬。欲度人故，以善方便居毘耶離；資財無量，攝諸貧民；奉戒清淨，攝諸毀禁；以忍調行，攝諸恚怒；以大精進，攝諸懈怠；一心禪寂，攝諸亂意；以決定慧，攝諸無智；雖為白衣，奉持沙門清淨律行；雖處居家，不著三界；示有妻子，常修梵行；現有眷屬，常樂遠離；雖服寶飾，而以相好嚴身；雖復飲食，而以禪悅為味；若至博弈戲處，輒以度人；受諸異道，不毀正信；雖明世典，常樂佛法；一切見敬，為供養中最；執持正法，攝諸長幼；一切治生諧偶，雖獲俗利，不以喜悅；遊諸四衢，饒益眾生；入治政法，救護一切；入講論處，導以大乘；入諸學堂，誘開童蒙；入諸婬舍，示欲之過；入諸酒肆，能立其志；若在長者，長者中尊，為說勝法；若在居士，居士中尊，斷其貪著；若在剎利，剎利中尊，教以忍辱；若在婆羅門，婆羅門中尊，除其我慢；若在大臣，大臣中尊，教以正法；若在王子，王子中尊，示以忠孝；若在內官，內官中尊，化政宮女；若在庶民，庶民中尊，令興福力；若在梵天，梵天中尊，誨以勝慧；若在帝釋，帝釋中尊，示現無常；若在護世，護世中尊，護諸眾生：長者維摩詰，以如是等無量方便饒益眾生。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time there was an elder in the great city of Vaiśālī named Vimalakīrti, who had already made offerings to immeasurable buddhas, planted good roots deeply, and attained the acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena; his eloquence was unobstructed, he sported freely with spiritual powers, and had attained every dhāraṇī; he had gained fearlessness, and subdued the weariness and enmity of Māra; he had entered the profound gates of the Dharma, was skilled in the perfection of wisdom, had mastered skillful means, and had brought his great vows to fulfillment; he understood clearly where the minds of beings tend, and was able to discern the sharpness or dullness of their faculties; long on the buddha-path, his mind was already pure and mature, settled firmly in the Great Vehicle; whatever he did, he considered it well; he abided in the Buddha's own dignified bearing, his mind was as vast as the ocean, the buddhas praised him, and he was honored by disciples, by Indra, by Brahmā, and by the guardians of the world. Wishing to bring beings across, he dwelt in Vaiśālī by skillful means: with wealth beyond measure he gathered in the poor; with pure discipline he gathered in those who broke the precepts; with patience he tamed conduct and gathered in the angry and resentful; with great diligence he gathered in the indolent; with one-pointed meditative stillness he gathered in the distracted; with resolute wisdom he gathered in the unwise. Though he was a layman in white robes, he upheld the pure disciplinary conduct of a renunciant; though he dwelt in a household, he was unattached to the three realms; though he showed that he had a wife and children, he constantly practiced pure conduct; though he displayed a household, he constantly delighted in withdrawal from it; though he wore jeweled ornaments, he was adorned instead by the marks and signs; though he ate and drank, he savored the taste of meditative joy; if he went to gambling halls or places of entertainment, it was always to bring people across; he took on every heterodox teaching without ever damaging true faith; though he was learned in worldly texts, he always delighted in the Buddha's Dharma; seen and honored by all, he was foremost among those receiving homage; he upheld the true Dharma while gathering in old and young alike; in every business dealing he was agreeable, and though he gained worldly profit, he took no delight in it; he wandered the city's crossroads to benefit beings; he entered the halls of government to protect and save everyone; he entered the debating halls to guide people toward the Great Vehicle; he entered the schools to open and guide the young; he entered the brothels to show the fault of desire; he entered the taverns to steady people's resolve. If he was among elders, he was foremost among elders, and taught them the supreme Dharma; if among householders, he was foremost among householders, and cut off their attachment and greed; if among kṣatriyas, he was foremost among kṣatriyas, and taught them patience; if among brahmins, he was foremost among brahmins, and removed their pride; if among ministers, he was foremost among ministers, and taught them the true Dharma; if among princes, he was foremost among princes, and showed them loyalty and filial devotion; if among the palace officials, he was foremost among them, and rightly guided the palace women; if among the common people, he was foremost among them, and inspired them to build up merit; if among the Brahmā gods, he was foremost among them, and instructed them in the supreme wisdom; if among the followers of Indra, he was foremost among them, and revealed impermanence to them; if among the guardians of the world, he was foremost among them, and protected every being. The elder Vimalakīrti benefited beings through immeasurable skillful means such as these.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva opens his commentary on this chapter with a small but useful piece of framing: this entire chapter's praise of Vimalakīrti's virtues is the compilers' own doing, not something Vimalakīrti says of himself — "the compilers of the sūtra, carrying forward what they had heard [the Buddha say of him], set out his virtues in advance." Everything in this section happens before Vimalakīrti has spoken a single word in the text that bears his name; the sūtra is making sure its audience knows exactly who they are about to watch outmaneuver the Buddha's most senior disciples, one after another, in the chapters ahead.

"His mind was as vast as the ocean" draws one of Sengzhao's fuller similes: the ocean has five virtues worth naming one at a time — it is so clear it will not hold a corpse, it produces wondrous treasures in abundance, it can absorb a great dragon's rain poured out like cart-axles without ever overflowing, no wind or sun can ever exhaust it, and its depths cannot be fathomed. Vimalakīrti's mind, Sengzhao says, matches each of these in turn: pure enough to refuse the corpse of broken precepts, productive of the treasure of clear wisdom, able to receive the Buddha's great Dharma-rain without overflowing, unharmed by any wind or sun of demonic affliction, and, in its wisdom, simply too deep to be measured.

One line in the list of his skillful means — "he entered the brothels to show the fault of desire" — carries a specific story behind it in Kumārajīva's telling, worth having in mind for what "skillful means" actually meant in practice to this text's earliest readers. A woman of golden-hued beauty is courted by a rich young man with a thousand pieces of gold; on the way, Mañjuśrī appears to her in disguise, dressed even more finely, and when desire stirs in her at the sight of him, he tells her that to have such adornment she must first give rise to the mind of awakening — which, he says, is nothing other than her own body, empty exactly as awakening itself is empty. Having in a past life already planted the roots for this teaching to land, she attains the acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena on the spot; then, to make the lesson complete, she goes on with the young man as arranged, and once inside shows her body suddenly bloated, rotting, and foul — at which the terrified young man goes to the Buddha and is taught, and likewise attains realization. This is what "showing the fault of desire" meant in practice: not a warning delivered from outside desire, but a demonstration staged from directly within the scene desire is imagining.

2. Vimalakīrti Manifests Illness

Chinese Source

其以方便，現身有疾。以其疾故，國王、大臣、長者、居士、婆羅門等，及諸王子并餘官屬，無數千人，皆往問疾。其往者，維摩詰因以身疾廣為說法：「諸仁者！是身無常、無強、無力、無堅、速朽之法，不可信也！為苦、為惱，眾病所集。

Ru-Yi Translation

By skillful means, he manifested his own body as ill. Because of his illness, kings, ministers, elders, householders, brahmins, princes, and every other official — thousands upon thousands of people — all went to inquire after his illness. To those who came, Vimalakīrti, taking his bodily illness as the occasion, taught the Dharma at length: "Venerable sirs! This body is impermanent, without strength, without power, without firmness, a thing that swiftly decays — it cannot be trusted! It is suffering, it is affliction, the gathering-place of every sickness."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Sengzhao notes something structurally important about this single sentence: however many and varied Vimalakīrti's skillful means are shown to be in the previous section, "this sūtra's own origin is rooted in his manifesting illness — that is why the compilers set this event apart with its own account." Everything that follows for the next twelve chapters — Mañjuśrī's visit, the disciples' declines, the goddess, the entry into non-duality — happens because of the illness introduced in this single line. Kumārajīva adds the practical logic behind its placement here, right after the long catalogue of Vimalakīrti's generosity across every class of society: people who have already felt the benefit of his giving and teaching are the ones who will come in large numbers to ask after his health, and it is precisely that large, receptive crowd that lets him "broadly teach the Dharma" rather than address only a few.

3. The Body, Examined

Chinese Source

「諸仁者！如此身，明智者所不怙；是身如聚沫，不可撮摩；是身如泡，不得久立；是身如炎，從渴愛生；是身如芭蕉，中無有堅；是身如幻，從顛倒起；是身如夢，為虛妄見；是身如影，從業緣現；是身如響，屬諸因緣；是身如浮雲，須臾變滅；是身如電，念念不住；是身無主，為如地；是身無我，為如火；是身無壽，為如風；是身無人，為如水；是身不實，四大為家；是身為空，離我、我所；是身無知，如草木瓦礫；是身無作，風力所轉；是身不淨，穢惡充滿；是身為虛偽，雖假以澡浴衣食，必歸磨滅；是身為災，百一病惱；是身如丘井，為老所逼；是身無定，為要當死；是身如毒蛇、如怨賊、如空聚，陰、界、諸入所共合成。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Venerable sirs, a body such as this is not something the wise rely upon. This body is like a mass of foam — it cannot be grasped or held. This body is like a bubble — it cannot stand for long. This body is like a mirage — it arises from thirst and craving. This body is like a plantain tree — inside it there is nothing firm. This body is like an illusion — it arises from inverted views. This body is like a dream — it is a false and empty seeing. This body is like a shadow — it appears from the conditions of karma. This body is like an echo — it belongs to causes and conditions. This body is like a floating cloud — in an instant it changes and vanishes. This body is like lightning — moment to moment it does not stay. This body has no master — it is like the earth. This body has no self — it is like fire. This body has no lifespan — it is like the wind. This body has no person in it — it is like water. This body is not real — the four elements are its only home. This body is empty — free of self and of what belongs to a self. This body has no awareness — it is like grass, trees, tile, or stone. This body has no agency of its own — it is turned about by the force of the wind. This body is impure — filled entirely with foulness. This body is false and deceptive — though it is bathed, clothed, and fed, it must in the end come to ruin. This body is a calamity — beset by a hundred and one afflictions and illnesses. This body is like an old, dried-up well — pressed upon by old age. This body has no fixed term — it is bound, in the end, to die. This body is like a poisonous snake, like a bandit, like an empty village — put together only from the aggregates, the elements, and the sense-fields."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Sengzhao reads the first block of similes — foam, bubble, mirage, plantain, illusion, dream, shadow, echo, cloud, lightning — as ten variations on a single point about emptiness, each one chosen because it names something that looks substantial but has no core: a plantain trunk is "all leaf, no heartwood." The next block turns to a different argument. The body compared to earth, fire, wind, and water is not about impermanence but about selfhood specifically: "to act with total, unconstrained autonomy is what we call a self — but fire outside us rises and dies according to its fuel; fire has no autonomy of its own. Since outer fire has no self, the fire within the body, being of the same kind, has none either." The same logic runs through earth (masterless — whoever is strongest simply occupies it), wind (no fixed lifespan of its own, just gathered breath moving), and water (no fixed person in it — its shape bends to whatever contains it). Kumārajīva's summary of the whole sequence is direct: from "like foam" through "like lightning," the point is impermanence and emptiness; from earth through water, the point is that there is no fixed self anywhere to be found in what looks, from inside, so obviously like "me."

The image of the old well deserves its full telling, since the sūtra compresses in one clause a parable Kumārajīva's commentary unpacks in full, and the parable is one of the most famous in the whole tradition. A man has offended a king; fleeing the king's drunken war-elephant sent after him, he throws himself down an old, dry well. Halfway down he catches a rotten vine with one hand. Below him a venomous dragon breathes poison up at him; beside him five poisonous snakes threaten to strike; two mice, one black and one white, gnaw steadily at the very vine he is holding, which is already about to break; and above him the drunken elephant still waits at the mouth of the well. In the middle of this — genuinely mortal danger on every side — a drop of honey falls from a tree above into his mouth, and, tasting its sweetness, he forgets his terror entirely. The decoding follows in the same passage: the well is birth-and-death itself; the drunken elephant is impermanence; the poisonous dragon is the lower destinies; the five poisonous snakes are the five aggregates; the rotten vine is the life-root; the black and white mice are the waxing and waning of day and night eating away at that life-root without pause; and the drop of honey is the pleasure of the five desires — sweet enough, tasted once, to make a person forget every one of the dangers surrounding it. "This body is like an old well, pressed upon by old age" is this entire parable, compressed to five characters.

4. The Buddha's True Body Is the Dharma-Body

Chinese Source

「諸仁者！此可患厭，當樂佛身。所以者何？佛身者即法身也！從無量功德智慧生，從戒、定、慧、解脫、解脫知見生，從慈、悲、喜、捨生，從布施、持戒、忍辱、柔和、勤行精進、禪定、解脫、三昧、多聞、智慧諸波羅蜜生，從方便生，從六通生，從三明生，從三十七道品生，從止觀生，從十力、四無所畏、十八不共法生，從斷一切不善法、集一切善法生，從真實生，從不放逸生：從如是無量清淨法生如來身。諸仁者！欲得佛身、斷一切眾生病者，當發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。」 如是，長者維摩詰為諸問疾者，如應說法，令無數千人皆發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Venerable sirs, such a thing should be wearied of and renounced — you should delight instead in the Buddha's body. And why? Because the Buddha's body is the Dharma-body itself! It is born from immeasurable merit and wisdom; it is born from discipline, meditative concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation; it is born from loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity; it is born from the perfections of giving, discipline, patience, gentleness, diligent effort, meditative concentration, liberation, samādhi, learning, and wisdom; it is born from skillful means; it is born from the six spiritual powers; it is born from the three knowledges; it is born from the thirty-seven factors of the path; it is born from calm-abiding and insight; it is born from the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, and the eighteen exclusive qualities; it is born from cutting off every unwholesome thing and gathering every wholesome thing; it is born from truthfulness; it is born from vigilance: it is from immeasurable pure qualities such as these that the Tathāgata's body is born. Venerable sirs, if you wish to attain the Buddha's body, and to cut off the sickness of every being, you should give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening." In this way, the elder Vimalakīrti taught the Dharma, according to what was fitting, to all who came to ask after his illness, so that thousands upon thousands of people all gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening.

Reading note — after Zhu Daosheng and Sengzhao

Zhu Daosheng's note on this closing turn addresses a question a careful reader will already be forming: if the Dharma-body is formless, without birth and without location, what is the relationship between it and the ordinary sixteen-foot human body the assembly can actually see standing — or, in this chapter, lying ill — in front of them? His answer reaches for an image this site's other sūtra translations do not otherwise use: "it is like the sun in the sky, reflected in every vessel that holds water — the ten thousand reflections in ten thousand shapes are each what that vessel itself captures; is that the sun's own doing? If a vessel holds no water, no reflection appears in it — not because the sun refuses to shine there, but because the vessel itself fails to receive it." The sixteen-foot response-body, on this account, is neither identical to the Dharma-body nor separate from it in the way two different objects are separate — it is what the Dharma-body looks like from within a particular vessel of receptivity, exactly as reflected sunlight is neither the sun itself nor something other than sunlight.

Kumārajīva's structural note explains why this teaching comes only after the body has been thoroughly examined and found wanting, rather than being stated first: the sūtra's usual method with newer listeners is to establish revulsion before offering the alternative, since "only once someone is no longer attached to the body can they go on to study the Dharma-body — hence the teaching of weariness comes first, and the teaching of the Dharma-body after." The chapter's closing line, that Vimalakīrti taught "so that thousands upon thousands of people all gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening," is the same outcome Mañjuśrī's own teaching produces three chapters ahead, and the same outcome nearly every one of Vimalakīrti's exchanges in this sūtra will produce by its end — a pattern worth watching for as the disciples' chapter, next, shows the same layman doing this to individuals rather than to a crowd.
