Part 12 of 14 · 菩薩行品第十一

Bodhisattva Conduct

菩薩行品第十一

Vimalakīrti carries the whole assembly, thrones and all, on his open palm to go see the Buddha — and what follows traces the fragrant rice's effects through seven days and every stage of the path, then widens into the Buddha's own catalogue of every conceivable way a buddha performs the buddha's work, down to silence itself. The visiting bodhisattvas from the Land of Many Fragrances ask for a teaching to carry home, and receive the sūtra's own account of what a bodhisattva actually does: neither exhausting the conditioned, nor settling into the unconditioned.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
The assembly's journey to the Āmra Grove on Vimalakīrti's palm, and the golden light that announces it; Ānanda's question about the fragrant rice, and how long its effects last for beings at every stage of the path; the Buddha's catalogue of every way a buddha performs the buddha's work, from light and bodhi trees to silence itself, and even the eighty-four thousand doors of affliction; the equality of every buddha beneath their differing lands and bodies, and Ānanda's own humbling before the scale of a bodhisattva's qualities; and the Dharma-door of neither exhausting the conditioned nor dwelling in the unconditioned, taught to the visiting bodhisattvas of Many Fragrances before they return home.

43. The Assembly Arrives at the Āmra Grove

Chinese Source

是時佛說法於菴羅樹園，其地忽然廣博嚴事，一切眾會皆作金色。阿難白佛言：「世尊！以何因緣，有此瑞應？是處忽然廣博嚴事，一切眾會皆作金色。」佛告阿難：「是維摩詰、文殊師利，與諸大眾恭敬圍繞，發意欲來，故先為此瑞應。」於是維摩詰語文殊師利：「可共見佛，與諸菩薩禮事供養。」文殊師利言：「善哉！行矣！今正是時。」維摩詰即以神力持諸大眾并師子座，置於右掌，往詣佛所。到已著地，稽首佛足，右遶七匝，一心合掌，在一面立；其諸菩薩即皆避座，稽首佛足，亦繞七匝，於一面立；諸大弟子、釋、梵、四天王等，亦皆避座，稽首佛足，在一面立。於是世尊如法慰問諸菩薩已，各令復坐，即皆受教。眾坐已定，佛語舍利弗：「汝見菩薩大士自在神力之所為乎？」「唯然，已見！」「於汝意云何？」「世尊！我覩其為不可思議，非意所圖，非度所測。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the Buddha was teaching the Dharma in the Āmra Grove, when the ground there suddenly broadened and became magnificently adorned, and the entire assembly turned the color of gold. Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, by what cause and condition has this auspicious sign appeared — the ground here suddenly broadening and becoming magnificently adorned, and the whole assembly turning to gold?" The Buddha told Ānanda: "It is Vimalakīrti and Mañjuśrī, reverently surrounded by the great assembly, who have resolved to come here — this sign has appeared in advance of their arrival." Vimalakīrti then said to Mañjuśrī: "Let us go together to see the Buddha, and pay reverence and offerings to him with these bodhisattvas." Mañjuśrī said: "Excellent — let us go. Now is exactly the time." Vimalakīrti then, by his spiritual power, took up the whole assembly together with the lion-thrones, and placed them upon his right palm, and went to where the Buddha was. Arriving there, he set them down upon the ground, bowed his head at the Buddha's feet, circled him reverently seven times to the right, and stood to one side with palms joined in single-minded reverence. All the bodhisattvas likewise rose from their seats, bowed their heads at the Buddha's feet, circled him seven times as well, and stood to one side; and the great disciples, and Indra, Brahmā, and the Four Heavenly Kings, all likewise rose from their seats, bowed their heads at the Buddha's feet, and stood to one side. The World-Honored One then greeted each of the bodhisattvas in the proper manner, and bid them all be seated again, and they all accepted his instruction. Once the assembly had settled, the Buddha said to Śāriputra: "Have you seen what these great bodhisattvas have accomplished by their own unhindered spiritual power?" "Indeed, World-Honored One, I have seen it." "And what do you make of it?" "World-Honored One, I find it entirely inconceivable — beyond anything my own intention could have devised, and beyond anything my own measure could have gauged."

Reading note — after Sengzhao and Kumārajīva

Sengzhao opens this chapter with a line worth having in full: "the perfected one has no fixed dwelling place; wherever principle meets him, he draws near." Though the Tathāgata and Vimalakīrti dress and dwell differently, their wondrous mode of existence is one and the same wherever it is found, which is why they come and go, work transformation, and jointly proclaim the inconceivable way together. Everything the sūtra has explained so far grew out of the original errand to visit a sick man; now, on the verge of bringing the whole teaching before the Tathāgata to be sealed and confirmed, this golden sign appears first, to open the hearts of the assembly before a word is spoken. Kumārajīva gives three reasons for why Vimalakīrti proposes the visit at all: to show his own sincere intention and fulfill what he had meant to do from the start; to let the Buddha himself confirm the fragrant rice, so that its explanation might awaken many who hear it; and because, having traveled so far and so grandly already, it would not do to simply turn back without paying his respects — so together they go to consult and receive the Buddha's own bequeathed teaching. Kumārajīva's small aside on how a great being sends off and welcomes guests is worth keeping as an image for the whole scene: "ordinary people, in welcoming or seeing off a guest, must assemble chariots and light cavalry; a great being, in welcoming or seeing off, moves everyone instead by wondrous spiritual penetration" — hence an entire assembly, thrones and all, riding on one open palm.

Śāriputra's closing answer — that what he has just seen is inconceivable, beyond what his own intention could have devised or his own measure gauged — is not naivety, on Sengzhao's reading, but the considered judgment of someone who has already tried and failed to measure this power once before: he had earlier entered Vimalakīrti's own inconceivable room together with Mañjuśrī, sat on one of the very same borrowed thrones, eaten the fragrant rice, and ridden home on this same palm — and finds himself no more able to fathom it now than he was then. This sūtra's whole governing point is the inconceivable way itself, Sengzhao notes, which is exactly why it keeps putting its own inconceivable traces on display like this rather than simply asserting the point once and moving on.

44. The Fragrance That Marks the Path

Chinese Source

爾時阿難白佛言：「世尊！今所聞香，自昔未有，是為何香？」佛告阿難：「是彼菩薩毛孔之香。」於是舍利弗語阿難言：「我等毛孔亦出是香。」阿難言：「此所從來？」曰：「是長者維摩詰從眾香國取佛餘飯，於舍食者，一切毛孔皆香若此。」阿難問維摩詰：「是香氣住當久如？」維摩詰言：「至此飯消。」曰：「此飯久如當消？」曰：「此飯勢力至于七日，然後乃消。又，阿難！若聲聞人未入正位，食此飯者，得入正位，然後乃消；已入正位，食此飯者，得心解脫，然後乃消；若未發大乘意，食此飯者，至發意乃消；已發意，食此飯者，得無生忍，然後乃消；已得無生忍，食此飯者，至一生補處，然後乃消。譬如有藥，名曰上味，其有服者，身諸毒滅，然後乃消；此飯如是，滅除一切諸煩惱毒，然後乃消。」阿難白佛言：「未曾有也！世尊！如此香飯能作佛事。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, this fragrance we are smelling now is unlike anything there has ever been before — what fragrance is this?" The Buddha told Ānanda: "It is the fragrance from the pores of those bodhisattvas." Śāriputra then said to Ānanda: "Our own pores are giving off this same fragrance." Ānanda asked: "Where has it come from?" He answered: "It is because the elder Vimalakīrti brought back the Buddha's leftover rice from the Land of Many Fragrances, and everyone who ate of it in his house now has this same fragrance from every pore." Ānanda asked Vimalakīrti: "How long will this fragrance last?" Vimalakīrti said: "Until this rice is fully digested." He asked: "And how long will it take for this rice to be digested?" He answered: "This rice's own power lasts for seven days before it is finally digested. And furthermore, Ānanda: if a śrāvaka who has not yet entered the correct stage eats this rice, it will not be digested until he has entered the correct stage; if one who has already entered the correct stage eats this rice, it will not be digested until he attains liberation of mind; if one who has not yet given rise to the Great Vehicle's intention eats this rice, it will not be digested until he gives rise to that intention; if one who has already given rise to that intention eats this rice, it will not be digested until he attains the acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena; and if one who has already attained that acquiescence eats this rice, it will not be digested until he reaches the stage of being bound for buddhahood in a single further lifetime. It is like a certain medicine called the finest of flavors — whoever takes it will not have it digested until every poison in their body has been destroyed; this rice is exactly the same, and will not be digested until every poison of affliction has been destroyed." Ānanda said to the Buddha: "This is truly unprecedented, World-Honored One! That a fragrant rice such as this should be able to perform a buddha's work!"

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Daosheng

Kumārajīva explains why Ānanda only notices the fragrance now, though the previous chapter already said it had spread through the entire billion-world universe: it was not that the fragrance had never reached him, but that he was simply not yet in a position to perceive it — only now does he actually come to smell it. He also explains why the Buddha credits "that bodhisattva's" pores specifically rather than the whole assembly at once: this is exactly what lets Śāriputra point out that his own pores carry the same fragrance too, which is what actually brings out how far the rice's benefit extends. On why the fragrance's effects last precisely seven days, Kumārajīva gives two reasons: first, if someone ate this rice and it lingered too long undigested, they might grow weary of it, so it is not allowed to overstay its welcome; and second, more pointedly, for anyone actually due to attain the path through this food, its fragrance working on them is never supposed to take more than seven days to bring them all the way to sagehood — matching, he notes, other traditional "no more than seven" thresholds elsewhere in the teaching, such as a stream-enterer's at most seven further lifetimes. In every such case the point is the same: whatever needs to happen has already fully happened well within that span.

Daosheng adds two smaller but pointed observations about the graduated list of attainments tied to the rice's digestion. First, within any single seven-day span, only one stage of advancement can occur — there is no possibility of a single meal producing two leaps at once. Second, and worth noticing on its own, the list stops one rung short of buddhahood itself, ending instead at "bound for buddhahood in one more lifetime" — because buddhahood is precisely the one attainment that cannot be produced by any external cause at all. The list stopping exactly there, on Daosheng's reading, is the sūtra's own way of showing that this rice is as complete an expression of the teaching as any single external cause could possibly be, carried right up to the very edge of what an external cause can do. Kumārajīva reads Ānanda's closing exclamation as assuming the wonder of "buddha-work" has now been exhausted by this one striking example — a single bowl of rice bringing beings all the way to awakening — which is exactly why the Buddha responds, in the next passage, by widening Ānanda's sense of scale considerably.

45. Every Way a Buddha Performs the Buddha's Work

Chinese Source

佛言：「如是，如是！阿難！或有佛土以佛光明而作佛事，有以諸菩薩而作佛事，有以佛所化人而作佛事，有以菩提樹而作佛事，有以佛衣服、臥具而作佛事，有以飯食而作佛事，有以園林臺觀而作佛事，有以三十二相、八十隨形好而作佛事，有以佛身而作佛事，有以虛空而作佛事；眾生應以此緣得入律行。有以夢、幻、影、響、鏡中像、水中月、熱時炎如是等喻，而作佛事；有以音聲、語言、文字而作佛事；或有清淨佛土，寂寞無言、無說、無示、無識、無作、無為，而作佛事。如是，阿難！諸佛威儀進止，諸所施為，無非佛事。「阿難！有此四魔、八萬四千諸煩惱門，而諸眾生為之疲勞，諸佛即以此法而作佛事，是名入一切諸佛法門。菩薩入此門者，若見一切淨好佛土，不以為喜，不貪不高；若見一切不淨佛土，不以為憂，不礙不沒；但於諸佛生清淨心，歡喜恭敬，未曾有也！諸佛如來功德平等，為化眾生故，而現佛土不同。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: "Just so, Ānanda, just so! In some buddha-lands, the buddha's own light performs the buddha's work; in others, the bodhisattvas themselves perform it; in others, beings conjured by the buddha perform it; in others, the bodhi tree performs it; in others, the buddha's own robes and bedding perform it; in others, food and drink perform it; in others, gardens, groves, and terraced pavilions perform it; in others, the thirty-two marks and eighty excellent characteristics perform it; in others, the buddha's own body performs it; and in others, empty space itself performs it — beings, through these various causes, come to enter the conduct of discipline. In some lands, dreams, illusions, shadows, echoes, reflections in a mirror, the moon reflected in water, mirages in the summer heat, and other such comparisons, perform the buddha's work; in others, sound, spoken words, and written language perform it; and in certain pure buddha-lands, utterly still and silent, with no word, no explanation, no display, no cognition, no action, and no conditioning at all, this very stillness performs the buddha's work. Thus, Ānanda, there is nothing whatsoever in the bearing, the movement, or any of the doings of the buddhas that is not itself the buddha's work. Ānanda, there are these four māras, and the eighty-four thousand doors of affliction, by which every being is wearied and worn down — and the buddhas make use of this very affliction to perform the buddha's work as well; this is what is called entering the Dharma-door of every buddha. A bodhisattva who enters this door, upon seeing every kind of pure and excellent buddha-land, does not take joy in it, is neither covetous of it nor made proud by it; and upon seeing every kind of impure buddha-land, does not take sorrow in it, is neither obstructed by it nor sunk down by it. He simply gives rise to a pure mind toward every buddha, filled with joy and reverence at what has never before been seen. The merit of every tathāgata is entirely equal; it is only for the sake of converting beings that they display their buddha-lands differently.

Reading note — after Sengzhao, Kumārajīva, and Daosheng

A few entries in this catalogue are worth having in the fuller detail the commentators give them. On the bodhi tree, Sengzhao writes: "this tree's light reaches everywhere without exception, its fragrance touches everyone, its form is subtle beyond description, appearing according to what each viewer finds beautiful; and its sound utters the Dharma itself, heard according to what each listener wants to hear — this is the Tathāgata's own tree of response, and any being who simply encounters it awakens by it naturally." On the Buddha's robe, Kumārajīva records that a king of Jambudvīpa once came to possess the Buddha's own great robe during an age of plague, and by raising it on a pole for the whole city to see, everyone who looked upon it with trust and reverence recovered from their illness, their faith deepening through the very act of looking. And the catalogue's final entry — certain pure lands taught entirely by stillness, "no word, no explanation, no display, no cognition" — gets Sengzhao's clearest single verdict in the whole list: a land of purely Dharma-body bodhisattvas, outwardly wordless and inwardly free of ordinary cognition, silently transcending awakening beyond anything speech or feeling could describe — "this is the very highest grade of the buddha's work." It is worth pausing on this line specifically, since it reaches back and explains something the previous chapter left implicit: Vimalakīrti's own silence before Mañjuśrī was not an absence of teaching at all, but this same recognized category of buddha-work performed by stillness itself — simply its highest grade.

The second half of this passage makes an even bolder claim: that the eighty-four thousand doors of affliction themselves become a buddha's own instrument. Kumārajīva sorts buddha-work into three kinds — through what is wholesome, such as light or spoken teaching; through what is neutral, such as empty space; and through what is itself unwholesome, the afflictions — and compares a buddha here to a physician who sometimes cures with a gentle medicine and sometimes, more surprisingly, with what is itself poison, citing the Buddha's own use of desire to bring Nanda to the path, and anger to convert a venomous dragon. Sengzhao's own version of the same point is worth quoting whole: "a woman given to lust is troubled by desire, yet only by carrying that very desire to its utmost does she come to awakening; a venomous dragon troubled by anger is converted only by having that very anger further provoked — this is using desire to remove desire, using anger to remove anger, exactly as a skilled physician uses poison against poison. This is the boundlessness of a buddha's own work." Daosheng adds the necessary caution: "if the medicine is misapplied, the medicine itself becomes the poison; only if it actually heals does the poison become medicine" — this is a description of an exceptionally skilled physician's judgment, not a general permission. The section's closing point then follows naturally from all of this: a bodhisattva who has truly entered this gate feels neither pride at a pure land nor despair at an impure one, because every buddha's own merit is entirely equal — what differs is never the buddha, only what particular beings in front of him actually need.

46. The Same Sky Behind Every Land

Chinese Source

阿難！汝見諸佛國土，地有若干，而虛空無若干也；如是見諸佛色身有若干耳，其無礙慧無若干也。「阿難！諸佛色身、威相、種性，戒、定、智慧、解脫、解脫知見，力、無所畏、不共之法，大慈、大悲，威儀所行及其壽命，說法教化，成就眾生，淨佛國土，具諸佛法，悉皆同等，是故名為三藐三佛陀，名為多陀阿伽度，名為佛陀。阿難！若我廣說此三句義，汝以劫壽不能盡受；正使三千大千世界滿中眾生，皆如阿難多聞第一，得念總持，此諸人等以劫之壽亦不能受。如是，阿難！諸佛阿耨多羅三藐三菩提無有限量，智慧辯才不可思議。」阿難白佛言：「我從今已往，不敢自謂以為多聞。」佛告阿難：「勿起退意！所以者何？我說汝於聲聞中為最多聞，非謂菩薩。且止，阿難！其有智者不應限度諸菩薩也；一切海淵尚可測量，菩薩禪定、智慧、總持、辯才一切功德不可量也。阿難！汝等捨置菩薩所行，是維摩詰一時所現神通之力，一切聲聞、辟支佛於百千劫，盡力變化所不能作。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda, you see the many different lands of every buddha, differing one from another; but empty space itself has no such difference at all. In just the same way, the buddhas' own bodily forms appear to differ from one another, but their unhindered wisdom has no such difference at all. Ānanda, the buddhas are entirely equal to one another in their bodily form, their majestic marks, their lineage, their discipline, their concentration, their wisdom, their liberation, their knowledge and vision of liberation, their powers, their fearlessnesses, their qualities shared by no other, their great loving-kindness and great compassion, their conduct and bearing, their lifespan, their teaching and converting of beings, their bringing beings to accomplishment, their purifying of buddha-lands, and their possession of every buddha-quality in full — and it is for this reason that they are called samyaksaṃbuddha, that they are called tathāgata, and that they are called buddha. Ānanda, were I to explain the full meaning of these three titles at length, you could not receive it all even in a lifetime as long as an eon; and even if every being filling this entire billion-world universe were, like you, Ānanda, foremost in vast learning, having attained mindfulness and total retention, not even all of them together could receive it fully in a lifetime as long as an eon. Just so, Ānanda — the unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening of every buddha is without any limit or measure, and their wisdom and eloquence are inconceivable." Ānanda said to the Buddha: "From this day forward, I will no longer dare to consider myself learned at all." The Buddha told Ānanda: "Do not let this thought discourage you. And why? Because when I say you are foremost in vast learning, I mean among the śrāvakas — not in comparison to the bodhisattvas. Enough of this, Ānanda: no one with real wisdom should ever try to measure a bodhisattva by such a limit. Every ocean's depths can still be sounded and measured, but a bodhisattva's meditative concentration, wisdom, total retention, eloquence, and every one of his merits cannot be measured at all. Ānanda, set aside entirely the conduct of the bodhisattvas in general — even this single feat of spiritual power that Vimalakīrti has just now displayed before us is something that no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, striving with the whole of their power for a hundred thousand eons, could ever accomplish."

Reading note — after Daosheng, Kumārajīva, and Sengzhao

Daosheng's gloss on the opening image is worth having exactly: there is no ground apart from space — the ground itself simply arises out of space to begin with. The many differing lands are like the ground; a buddha's own unhindered wisdom is like the sky that holds them all — the sky does not vary from place to place because the ground beneath it happens to vary. Kumārajīva then unpacks the three titles that follow with some precision: seeing every phenomenon without any discrepancy is called "correct" (正); wisdom reaching everywhere without exception is called "universal" (遍); and coming as reality itself comes, going as reality itself goes, unchanged across every age and walking the same track as every sage before, is what "tathāgata" — "thus-come," equally "thus-gone" — actually names; waking from the long sleep of birth-and-death, and waking others along with oneself, is what only a buddha does. He adds a small, pointed aside here: elsewhere in this very sūtra, the Buddha's own three unanswered questions to Śāriputra, and the goddess's silent question to him in an earlier chapter, show that even Ānanda — "foremost in learning" among the śrāvakas — has not fully woken from what Kumārajīva calls this same sleep. It is a quiet callback tying this chapter directly to the goddess-and-flowers episode translated earlier on this site.

The Buddha's rebuke to Ānanda turns on a principle Sengzhao states plainly: "some things are sufficient at a lower register yet insufficient at a higher one; one cannot use what is merely sufficient at the lower register to measure what belongs to the higher." An ocean, vast as it is, can still be sounded to its very bottom — but a bodhisattva's qualities cannot be measured at all, which is precisely why Ānanda's own vast learning, real as it is among the śrāvakas, was never the right instrument to gauge Vimalakīrti's spiritual power in the first place. The section's closing line then supplies the plainest possible proof of the point: even this single feat just displayed — an entire assembly and its thrones carried on one open palm — already exceeds anything a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha could produce with the whole of their effort across a hundred thousand eons. Sengzhao's own gloss makes the scale of the claim explicit: "'a single feat' names only what has just now been shown before us — if even this single feat already lies beyond them, how much more so everything else a bodhisattva actually does."

47. Not Exhausting the Conditioned

Chinese Source

爾時眾香世界菩薩來者，合掌白佛言：「世尊！我等初見此土生下劣想，今自悔責，捨離是心。所以者何？諸佛方便不可思議，為度眾生故，隨其所應，現佛國異。唯然世尊！願賜少法，還於彼土當念如來。」佛告諸菩薩：「有盡無盡解脫法門，汝等當學。何謂為盡？謂有為法。何謂無盡？謂無為法。如菩薩者，不盡有為，不住無為。」

「何謂不盡有為？謂不離大慈，不捨大悲；深發一切智心，而不忽忘；教化眾生，終不厭倦，於四攝法，常念順行；護持正法，不惜軀命，種諸善根，無有疲厭；志常安住，方便迴向，求法不懈，說法無悋；勤供諸佛；故入生死而無所畏，於諸榮辱，心無憂喜；不輕未學，敬學如佛，墮煩惱者，令發正念，於遠離樂，不以為貴，不著己樂，慶於彼樂；在諸禪定，如地獄想，於生死中，如園觀想；見來求者，為善師想，捨諸所有，具一切智想；見毀戒人，起救護想；諸波羅蜜，為父母想，道品之法，為眷屬想；發行善根，無有齊限，以諸淨國嚴飾之事，成己佛土；行無限施，具足相好，除一切惡，淨身、口、意；生死無數劫，意而有勇，聞佛無量德，志而不倦；以智慧劍，破煩惱賊，出陰、界、入，荷負眾生，永使解脫；以大精進，摧伏魔軍，常求無念實相智慧行；於世間法少欲知足，於出世間求之無厭，而不捨世間法，不壞威儀法而能隨俗；起神通慧，引導眾生，得念總持，所聞不忘；善別諸根，斷眾生疑，以樂說辯，演法無礙；淨十善道，受天、人福；修四無量，開梵天道；勸請說法，隨喜讚善，得佛音聲；身、口、意善，得佛威儀；深修善法，所行轉勝，以大乘教，成菩薩僧；心無放逸，不失眾善：行如此法，是名菩薩不盡有為。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the bodhisattvas who had come from the land of Many Fragrances joined their palms and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, when we first saw this world, we conceived a low opinion of it, but we now regret and reproach ourselves, and set that thought aside entirely. And why? Because the skillful means of the buddhas are truly inconceivable, and it is only for the sake of delivering beings that they display their buddha-lands differently, each suited to what those beings actually need. World-Honored One, please grant us a small portion of the Dharma to take back, so that when we return to our own land we may still hold the Tathāgata in mind." The Buddha told the bodhisattvas: "There is a Dharma-door called the liberation of the exhaustible and the inexhaustible, which you should all learn. What is meant by 'the exhaustible'? It refers to conditioned phenomena. What is meant by 'the inexhaustible'? It refers to the unconditioned. A true bodhisattva neither exhausts the conditioned, nor dwells in the unconditioned."

"What is meant by not exhausting the conditioned? It means never straying from great loving-kindness, never abandoning great compassion; giving rise, deeply and without ever letting it slip from mind, to the aspiration for omniscience; teaching and converting beings without ever growing weary; constantly keeping the four means of attraction in mind and acting in accordance with them; upholding the true Dharma without begrudging even one's own body and life; planting every root of goodness without any fatigue or weariness; abiding always with a settled resolve, dedicating one's merit through skillful means; seeking the Dharma without slackening, and teaching it without any reluctance; diligently making offerings to every buddha; and so entering birth-and-death without any fear, with a mind free of either sorrow or joy amid every honor or disgrace. It means never scorning a beginner, and honoring one who is still learning as though honoring a buddha; leading those who have fallen into affliction to give rise to right mindfulness; not prizing the pleasure of withdrawal from the world as something especially valuable; not clinging to one's own happiness, but rejoicing in the happiness of others. It means regarding every meditative absorption as though it were a hell to avoid lingering in, and regarding birth-and-death itself as though it were a garden to wander through freely; regarding whoever comes seeking something from you as a good teacher, and regarding the giving away of everything one has as the very substance of omniscience; regarding one who has broken the precepts with a thought of rescue and protection; regarding the perfections as one's own father and mother, and the qualities of the path as one's own family. It means cultivating roots of goodness without any limit at all, and using the adornments of every pure land to perfect one's own buddha-land; practicing giving without limit, so as to perfect the excellent marks; removing every evil, and purifying body, speech, and mind. It means facing countless eons of birth-and-death with a courageous mind, and hearing of a buddha's immeasurable virtues without ever growing weary of the aspiration toward them; using the sword of wisdom to destroy the bandit of affliction, going beyond the aggregates, the elements, and the sense-fields, and carrying the burden of every being until they are permanently liberated; using great diligence to subdue the armies of Māra, and constantly seeking the wisdom of true reality free of any conceptual thought; being content with little in worldly matters, yet never satisfied in seeking what is beyond the world, all without ever abandoning worldly things, and following worldly custom without ever breaking the dignity of one's own conduct. It means giving rise to the wisdom of spiritual powers to guide beings, attaining mindfulness and total retention so that nothing once heard is ever forgotten; skillfully discerning the faculties of every being, resolving their doubts, and expounding the Dharma without any obstruction through an eloquence that delights in teaching. It means purifying the ten wholesome courses of action, so as to receive the blessings of devas and humans; cultivating the four immeasurable minds, so as to open the way to the Brahmā heavens; exhorting others to request the Dharma be taught, and rejoicing in and praising whatever good is done, thereby attaining a buddha's own voice. It means having a body, speech, and mind that are wholly good, thereby attaining a buddha's own bearing; cultivating wholesome practices ever more deeply, so that one's conduct grows ever more excellent; using the teaching of the Great Vehicle to form the assembly of bodhisattvas. It means a mind free of any negligence, never losing hold of any root of goodness already gathered. To practice a Dharma such as this is what is called a bodhisattva's not exhausting the conditioned."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva gives several reasons for why the visiting bodhisattvas ask for an actual teaching rather than simply departing content: they do not want to treat fragrance as the only conceivable mode of buddha-work; they intend, eventually, to travel to other, less pure worlds themselves, and want a teaching flexible enough for that; and having come so far, it would be a poor use of the journey to return with nothing further. But the reason he lingers on longest is more pointed: he wants these bodhisattvas, who live entirely at ease on nothing but fragrant food in their own pure land, to actually come to feel their debt of gratitude to their own buddha — because, in his words, "constant ease effaces the very joy of ease, and effacing that joy effaces the sense of what the joy depended on in the first place." Their own happiness has never once been interrupted, which is exactly why they have stopped noticing where it comes from; only a teaching built around difficulty and its overcoming can restore that sense of cost and source. Kumārajīva's memorable image for why a bodhisattva cultivates the conditioned at all, despite it technically being a burden, is worth having whole: it is like an enormous army of bandits besieging a great city, when a man from among the bandits' own ranks comes over and surrenders — because it is precisely through this man that the whole horde can finally be broken, he is treated with honor and provision even though he himself is, by origin, one of the bandits. Every conditioned, wholesome quality works the same way: technically still a burden, yet exactly the material through which every other burden gets overcome.

Both commentators stress why "never straying from great loving-kindness, never abandoning great compassion" opens this entire catalogue rather than appearing anywhere in the middle of it: this is the root the whole tree grows from. Kumārajīva calls loving-kindness and compassion "the very root of the buddha-way itself" — a śrāvaka, lacking them, simply exhausts the conditioned and settles into the unconditioned instead, which is exactly the outcome this whole teaching is written to prevent — and Sengzhao calls compassion "the foundation on which one enters existence at all, and the very root from which merit is planted." Everything that follows in this long list is, in effect, one root's branches. The story Kumārajīva tells to illustrate "planting every root of goodness without fatigue" deserves to be kept whole: a man comes to Śāriputra seeking ordination, and Śāriputra, examining the man's past lives with his own insight, finds no root of goodness planted in eighty thousand great eons, and turns him away — as do five hundred other disciples in turn — until the man, weeping at the monastery gate, is found by the Buddha himself, who receives him, ordains him on the spot, and reveals that this man's one true root of goodness was planted incalculable eons earlier, in a single moment of calling out to a passing buddha while fleeing a tiger up a tree. The Buddha's rebuke to Śāriputra afterward — comparing him to someone who finds a stone with gold inside it and discards it, not recognizing the gold within — gives this catalogue's confidence in "never fatigued, never limited" cultivation its most concrete grounding: even a single moment's aspiration, buried under any span of time, is never actually lost.

48. Not Dwelling in the Unconditioned

Chinese Source

「何謂菩薩不住無為？謂修學空，不以空為證；修學無相、無作，不以無相、無作為證；修學無起，不以無起為證；觀於無常，而不厭善本；觀世間苦，而不惡生死；觀於無我，而誨人不倦；觀於寂滅，而不永滅；觀於遠離，而身心修善；觀無所歸，而歸趣善法；觀於無生，而以生法荷負一切；觀於無漏，而不斷諸漏；觀無所行，而以行法教化眾生；觀於空無，而不捨大悲；觀正法位，而不隨小乘；觀諸法虛妄，無牢無人、無主無相，本願未滿，而不虛福德、禪定、智慧：修如此法，是名菩薩不住無為。

「又具福德故，不住無為；具智慧故，不盡有為。大慈悲故，不住無為；滿本願故，不盡有為。集法藥故，不住無為；隨授藥故，不盡有為。知眾生病故，不住無為；滅眾生病故，不盡有為。諸正士菩薩以修此法，不盡有為、不住無為，是名盡無盡解脫法門，汝等當學。」爾時彼諸菩薩聞說是法，皆大歡喜，以眾妙華、若干種色、若干種香，散遍三千大千世界，供養於佛及此經法并諸菩薩已，稽首佛足，歎未曾有，言：「釋迦牟尼佛乃能於此善行方便。」言已，忽然不現，還到彼國。

Ru-Yi Translation

"What is meant by a bodhisattva's not dwelling in the unconditioned? It means cultivating and studying emptiness, without taking emptiness itself as something to be personally realized and confirmed; cultivating and studying signlessness and wishlessness, without taking signlessness and wishlessness themselves as something to be personally realized and confirmed; cultivating and studying non-arising, without taking non-arising itself as something to be personally realized and confirmed. It means contemplating impermanence, without growing weary of one's own roots of goodness; contemplating the suffering of the world, without coming to detest birth-and-death; contemplating no-self, without ever growing weary of teaching others; contemplating cessation, without permanently entering into cessation; contemplating detachment, while still cultivating goodness in body and mind; contemplating that there is nowhere to which anything ultimately returns, while still turning toward and taking refuge in wholesome things; contemplating non-arising, while still using the very phenomenon of birth to carry the burden of every being; contemplating the uncontaminated, without cutting oneself off from those still contaminated; contemplating that there is nothing whatsoever to be practiced, while still teaching and converting beings by means of practice; contemplating emptiness and nothingness, without ever abandoning great compassion; contemplating the very stage of the true Dharma, without following after the lesser vehicle's own use of it. It means contemplating that every phenomenon is illusory and unreal, without substance, without a person, without a master, and without any fixed mark — yet, because one's original vow is not yet fulfilled, never treating one's own merit, meditative concentration, and wisdom as though they, too, were merely illusory and thus not worth cultivating. To practice a Dharma such as this is what is called a bodhisattva's not dwelling in the unconditioned.

"And furthermore: because he is complete in merit, he does not dwell in the unconditioned; because he is complete in wisdom, he does not exhaust the conditioned. Because of his great compassion, he does not dwell in the unconditioned; because his original vow is not yet fulfilled, he does not exhaust the conditioned. Because he gathers the medicine of the Dharma, he does not dwell in the unconditioned; because he must dispense that medicine according to need, he does not exhaust the conditioned. Because he knows the illness of every being, he does not dwell in the unconditioned; because he must cure the illness of every being, he does not exhaust the conditioned. It is by cultivating this Dharma that every true and upright bodhisattva neither exhausts the conditioned nor dwells in the unconditioned — this is what is called the Dharma-door of the liberation of the exhaustible and the inexhaustible, which you should all learn." When those bodhisattvas heard this Dharma explained, they were all filled with great joy, and scattered wondrous flowers of every color and every fragrance across the entire billion-world universe, as an offering to the Buddha, to this Dharma-teaching, and to every bodhisattva present; and having bowed their heads at the Buddha's feet, they exclaimed at this unprecedented thing, saying: "How well Śākyamuni Buddha is able to practice such skillful means, even here!" And having said this, they vanished from sight all at once, and returned to their own land.

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Sengzhao frames the shift at this point in the teaching plainly: everything from here on is contemplative practice aimed at the unconditioned itself, and the core move is one shared, in outline, by all three vehicles' three gates of liberation — emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness — except that the Great Vehicle's version of each never stops where the lesser vehicles' does. A śrāvaka's emptiness-contemplation applies only to the absence of a fixed self; a bodhisattva's applies to the absence of a fixed nature in every phenomenon whatsoever — and because there is no phenomenon it fails to reach, the contemplation of emptiness, turned on itself, likewise turns out to be empty, leaving nothing left over to personally "confirm" as a private attainment worth resting in. The long chain of paired opposites that follows all resolves the same way, and Sengzhao's own compact summary of the mechanism is worth quoting whole: "the two lesser vehicles treat impermanence as impermanence, and so tire of what is wholesome and conditioned; treat suffering as suffering, and so come to hate the suffering of birth-and-death; treat no-self as no-self, and so grow slack in teaching others; treat stillness as stillness, and so wish to remain permanently still. A bodhisattva does not treat impermanence as impermanence, and so does not tire of his own good roots; does not treat suffering as suffering, and so does not hate birth-and-death; does not treat no-self as no-self, and so never tires of teaching others; does not treat stillness as stillness, and so does not remain permanently still." The same insight, applied consistently, is what keeps every item in this list from collapsing into either extreme.

The four paired reasons that close the teaching proper supply something the long list itself left unstated: Sengzhao reads the whole list as having shown that a bodhisattva does both — neither exhausts the conditioned nor dwells in the unconditioned — without yet fully explaining why both are necessary at once. These four pairs supply the why, two by two: merit requires engagement with the conditioned to be built up at all, so it cannot rest content in the unconditioned; wisdom requires broad exposure to be sharpened at all, so it cannot exhaust its engagement with the conditioned; great compassion cannot settle into the unconditioned, and an unfulfilled vow cannot abandon the conditioned; gathering the Dharma's medicine requires stepping back from fixed views, and dispensing that medicine requires staying engaged with those who need it; and, in the most concrete pairing of all, a bodhisattva who has diagnosed a being's illness cannot simply rest satisfied at having understood it, without also supplying the cure. The chapter's brief closing scene — the visiting bodhisattvas, having received exactly the teaching they asked for, scatter flowers across the whole universe and vanish home in an instant — reads, after everything just taught, almost as a small demonstration of the lesson itself: joy expressed through an offering, immediately followed by departure without lingering anywhere at all — the very two halves of the teaching they came to receive, performed rather than merely stated, in the space of one closing line.
