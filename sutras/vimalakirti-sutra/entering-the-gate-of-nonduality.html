Entering the Gate of Nonduality

入不二法門品第九

Vimalakīrti puts one question to his assembled bodhisattvas: how does one enter the Dharma-door of nonduality? Thirty-one of them answer in turn, each naming a pair of opposites — birth and cessation, self and what belongs to it, purity and defilement, saṃsāra and nirvāṇa — and showing how it dissolves. Mañjuśrī then offers what sounds like the final word: true nonduality is beyond all speech. Asked to answer in turn, Vimalakīrti says nothing at all — and that silence is the single most famous moment in this entire sūtra.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
Thirty-one bodhisattvas, each asked to say in their own words how a bodhisattva enters the Dharma-door of nonduality, and each dissolving a different pair of opposites — birth and cessation, self and what belongs to it, purity and defilement, the conditioned and the unconditioned, saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, and many more; Mañjuśrī's own answer, that true nonduality has no place even for words about it; and Vimalakīrti's wordless response to the same question, which Mañjuśrī immediately recognizes as going one step further than his own. Five thousand bodhisattvas in the assembly attain the acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena on the spot.

35. Sixteen Doors

Chinese Source

爾時維摩詰謂眾菩薩言：「諸仁者！云何菩薩入不二法門？各隨所樂說之。」

會中有菩薩名法自在，說言：「諸仁者！生滅為二。法本不生，今則無滅，得此無生法忍，是為入不二法門。」 德守菩薩曰：「我、我所為二。因有我故，便有我所；若無有我，則無我所，是為入不二法門。」 不眴菩薩曰：「受、不受為二。若法不受，則不可得；以不可得故，無取無捨、無作無行，是為入不二法門。」 德頂菩薩曰：「垢、淨為二。見垢實性，則無淨相，順於滅相，是為入不二法門。」 善宿菩薩曰：「是動、是念為二。不動則無念，無念則無分別，通達此者，是為入不二法門。」 善眼菩薩曰：「一相、無相為二。若知一相即是無相，亦不取無相，入於平等，是為入不二法門。」 妙臂菩薩曰：「菩薩心、聲聞心為二。觀心相空如幻化者，無菩薩心、無聲聞心，是為入不二法門。」 弗沙菩薩曰：「善、不善為二。若不起善、不善，入無相際而通達者，是為入不二法門。」 師子菩薩曰：「罪、福為二。若達罪性，則與福無異，以金剛慧決了此相，無縛無解者，是為入不二法門。」 師子意菩薩曰：「有漏、無漏為二。若得諸法等，則不起漏、不漏想，不著於相，亦不住無相，是為入不二法門。」 淨解菩薩曰：「有為、無為為二。若離一切數，則心如虛空，以清淨慧無所礙者，是為入不二法門。」 那羅延菩薩曰：「世間、出世間為二。世間性空，即是出世間，於其中不入、不出、不溢、不散，是為入不二法門。」 善意菩薩曰：「生死、涅槃為二。若見生死性，則無生死，無縛無解，不然不滅，如是解者，是為入不二法門。」 現見菩薩曰：「盡、不盡為二。法若究竟盡、若不盡，皆是無盡相；無盡相即是空，空則無有盡、不盡相。如是入者，是為入不二法門。」 普守菩薩曰：「我、無我為二。我尚不可得，非我何可得？見我實性者，不復起二，是為入不二法門。」 電天菩薩曰：「明、無明為二。無明實性即是明，明亦不可取，離一切數，於其中平等無二者，是為入不二法門。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Vimalakīrti said to the assembled bodhisattvas: "Noble sirs, how does a bodhisattva enter the Dharma-door of nonduality? Let each of you speak of this exactly as you find it in yourselves."

Dharma-Mastery Bodhisattva, who was in the assembly, spoke first: "Noble sirs, birth and cessation are two. But phenomena are originally unborn, and so now there is nothing to cease; attaining this acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Guardian of Virtue Bodhisattva said: "The self and what belongs to the self are two. Because there is a self, there comes to be what belongs to the self; if there were no self, there would be nothing belonging to it at all — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Unblinking Bodhisattva said: "Grasping and not grasping are two. But if a phenomenon is not grasped, it cannot be apprehended at all; and because it cannot be apprehended, there is no taking up and no putting down, no action and no conduct — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Crown of Virtue Bodhisattva said: "Defilement and purity are two. But seeing the true nature of defilement, there is no mark of purity either, and one accords instead with the mark of cessation — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Good Constellation Bodhisattva said: "Movement and thought are two. Without movement there is no thought, and without thought there is no discrimination; one who penetrates this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Good Eye Bodhisattva said: "A single mark and no mark are two. But if one knows that a single mark is itself no mark, and does not grasp even at 'no mark,' entering into equality — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Wondrous Arm Bodhisattva said: "The bodhisattva-mind and the śrāvaka-mind are two. But one who observes the mark of mind to be empty, like an illusion or a transformation, has neither a bodhisattva-mind nor a śrāvaka-mind — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Puṣya Bodhisattva said: "Wholesome and unwholesome are two. But one who gives rise to neither the wholesome nor the unwholesome, entering into and penetrating the boundary of no mark — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Lion Bodhisattva said: "Offense and blessing are two. But if one penetrates the nature of offense, it is no different from blessing; resolving this mark with a vajra-like wisdom that decisively cuts through, being neither bound nor released — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Lion's Intent Bodhisattva said: "The contaminated and the uncontaminated are two. But if one attains the equality of every phenomenon, one gives rise to neither the notion of contamination nor of its absence, not attaching to any mark, yet not abiding in no mark either — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Pure Understanding Bodhisattva said: "The conditioned and the unconditioned are two. But if one is free of every enumeration, then the mind is like empty space; with a wisdom that is pure and unobstructed — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Nārāyaṇa Bodhisattva said: "The mundane and the supramundane are two. But the nature of the mundane is itself empty, and this emptiness is itself the supramundane; within it there is no entering, no going out, no overflowing, and no dispersing — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Good Intent Bodhisattva said: "Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are two. But if one sees the nature of saṃsāra, there is no saṃsāra at all — neither bound nor released, neither ablaze nor extinguished; one who understands in this way is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Manifest Vision Bodhisattva said: "Exhausted and unexhausted are two. But whether a phenomenon is ultimately exhausted or unexhausted, both are marks of the inexhaustible; the mark of the inexhaustible is itself emptiness, and in emptiness there is no mark of the exhausted or the unexhausted at all. One who enters in this way is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Universal Guardian Bodhisattva said: "The self and no-self are two. But if the self itself cannot be apprehended, how could its absence be apprehended? One who sees the true nature of self no longer gives rise to this duality — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Lightning-Deva Bodhisattva said: "Brightness and ignorance are two. But the true nature of ignorance is itself brightness, and brightness, too, cannot be grasped; being free of every enumeration, and equal and without duality within it — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva frames the timing of this chapter first: existence and emptiness taking the lead by turns is the constant pattern of the Buddhadharma, and since the previous chapter taught existence and skillful means at length, this one turns to teach emptiness. He also notes that since the assembly began, only two people at a time have really been speaking — everyone else has listened in silence — and Vimalakīrti now wants each bodhisattva's own understanding on display before the gathering disperses, hence the question put to all thirty-one at once. He raises, and answers, an obvious objection: why single out "nonduality," when there are countless other Dharma-gates one could name? His answer is that the affliction bound up in mere "twoness" is comparatively slight, while other gates involve broader entanglements and deeper affliction — if even "two" must be seen through, everything built on top of it follows automatically. He adds a more technical point: every phenomenon arises from conditions, and conditioned arising, at its barest minimum, requires two factors — nothing has ever been observed to arise from one condition alone — so the arising of existence bottoms out exactly at "two." Once these two are dissolved, one enters what he calls the mysterious realm; and as for why "one" itself isn't likewise refuted, his answer is characteristically sharp: the moment you name or count "one," it is already no longer purely one, since naming introduces the very plurality it claims to escape — the "nameless one" is a name provisionally set up, without any real, fixed thing standing behind it, so dissolving "two" already dissolves "one" along with it.

One gloss is worth having as a key to the whole list, since it applies to nearly every entry that follows: on Good Eye Bodhisattva's answer, Sengzhao writes that speaking of "one" is meant only to dispel "two," not to assert "one" as a real thing, and speaking of "nothing" is meant only to dispel "something," not to assert "nothing" as a real thing — but the confused hear "one" and immediately grasp at a mark of oneness, hear "nothing" and immediately grasp at a mark of nothingness, and so the very duality the answer was meant to dissolve reappears in a new form. A few individual images are worth having in full: on the first answer, Sengzhao notes that "cessation" only ever means the cessation of something born — awaken to the fact that nothing was ever truly born, and there is nothing left for cessation to be the cessation of. On Lion Bodhisattva's vajra-wisdom, Kumārajīva explains that a vajra placed on the ground does not stop until it reaches the very limit of the earth, just as the wisdom of true reality does not stop until it has exhausted the nature of every phenomenon. And on Lightning-Deva's closing answer that ignorance's true nature is itself brightness, Kumārajīva reaches for a lotus: its color is stately and pure, yet what it grows out of is not — trace the flower back to its cause, and the mind should still not become attached to either the mud or the flower as something separate from the other.

36. Fifteen More

Chinese Source

喜見菩薩曰：「色、色空為二。色即是空，非色滅空，色性自空；如是，受、想、行、識、識空為二，識即是空，非識滅空，識性自空，於其中而通達者，是為入不二法門。」 明相菩薩曰：「四種異、空種異為二。四種性即是空種性，如前際、後際空故，中際亦空。若能如是知諸種性者，是為入不二法門。」 妙意菩薩曰：「眼、色為二。若知眼性，於色不貪、不恚、不癡，是名寂滅；如是，耳、聲，鼻、香，舌、味，身、觸，意、法為二，若知意性，於法不貪、不恚、不癡，是名寂滅，安住其中，是為入不二法門。」 無盡意菩薩曰：「布施、迴向一切智為二。布施性即是迴向一切智性，如是，持戒、忍辱、精進、禪定、智慧、迴向一切智為二，智慧性即是迴向一切智性，於其中入一相者，是為入不二法門。」 深慧菩薩曰：「是空、是無相、是無作為二。空即無相，無相即無作；若空、無相、無作，則無心、意、識，於一解脫門即是三解脫門者，是為入不二法門。」 寂根菩薩曰：「佛、法、眾為二。佛即是法，法即是眾，是三寶皆無為相，與虛空等，一切法亦爾。能隨此行者，是為入不二法門。」 心無礙菩薩曰：「身、身滅為二。身即是身滅。所以者何？見身實相者，不起見身及見滅身，身與滅身無二無分別，於其中不驚、不懼者，是為入不二法門。」 上善菩薩曰：「身、口、意業為二。是三業皆無作相，身無作相，即口無作相；口無作相，即意無作相；是三業無作相，即一切法無作相。能如是隨無作慧者，是為入不二法門。」 福田菩薩曰：「福行、罪行、不動行為二。三行實性即是空，空則無福行、無罪行、無不動行。於此三行而不起者，是為入不二法門。」 華嚴菩薩曰：「從我起二為二。見我實相者，不起二法；若不住二法，則無有識；無所識者，是為入不二法門。」 德藏菩薩曰：「有所得相為二。若無所得，則無取捨；無取捨者，是為入不二法門。」 月上菩薩曰：「闇與明為二。無闇、無明，則無有二。所以者何？如入滅受想定，無闇、無明，一切法相亦復如是，於其中平等入者，是為入不二法門。」 寶印手菩薩曰：「樂涅槃、不樂世間為二。若不樂涅槃、不厭世間，則無有二。所以者何？若有縛，則有解；若本無縛，其誰求解？無縛無解，則無樂、厭，是為入不二法門。」 珠頂王菩薩曰：「正道、邪道為二。住正道者，則不分別是邪是正，離此二者，是為入不二法門。」 樂實菩薩曰：「實、不實為二。實見者尚不見實，何況非實？所以者何？非肉眼所見，慧眼乃能見，而此慧眼，無見無不見，是為入不二法門。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Joyful to Behold Bodhisattva said: "Form and the emptiness of form are two. But form is itself emptiness — it is not that form ceases and only then becomes empty; the very nature of form is itself empty. In just the same way, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, and the emptiness of consciousness, are two — but consciousness is itself emptiness; it is not that consciousness ceases and only then becomes empty, the very nature of consciousness is itself empty. One who penetrates this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Bright Appearance Bodhisattva said: "The differences among the four elements, and the difference of the element of emptiness, are two. But the nature of the four elements is itself the nature of the element of emptiness — just as the prior and posterior limits are empty, the middle limit is empty as well. One who can know the nature of every element in this way is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Wondrous Mind Bodhisattva said: "The eye and form are two. But if one knows the nature of the eye, one is neither greedy for form, nor angered by it, nor deluded about it — this is called stillness. In just the same way, the ear and sound, the nose and smell, the tongue and taste, the body and touch, the mind and mental phenomena are two — but if one knows the nature of the mind, one is neither greedy for mental phenomena, nor angered by them, nor deluded about them; this too is called stillness. Abiding within this — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Inexhaustible Intent Bodhisattva said: "Giving, and dedicating it toward omniscience, are two. But the nature of giving is itself the nature of dedication toward omniscience. In just the same way, discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom, and dedicating them toward omniscience, are two — but the nature of wisdom is itself the nature of dedication toward omniscience. One who enters the single mark within all of this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Profound Wisdom Bodhisattva said: "Emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness are two. But emptiness is itself signlessness, and signlessness is itself wishlessness; where there is emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, there is no mind, no mentation, and no consciousness — one for whom a single gate of liberation is itself all three gates of liberation is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Tranquil Faculties Bodhisattva said: "The Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha are two. But the Buddha is itself the Dharma, and the Dharma is itself the Saṅgha — these Three Jewels all have the mark of the unconditioned, equal to empty space, and every phenomenon is exactly the same. One who is able to follow this practice is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Unobstructed Mind Bodhisattva said: "The body, and the cessation of the body, are two. But the body is itself the cessation of the body. And why is this? One who sees the true mark of the body gives rise to no view either of the body or of the cessation of the body; the body and the cessation of the body are without duality and without distinction — one who neither startles nor fears in the midst of this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Supreme Goodness Bodhisattva said: "The karma of body, speech, and mind are two. But all three of these karmas have the mark of non-action — the body's mark of non-action is itself the mark of speech's non-action, and speech's mark of non-action is itself the mark of mind's non-action; and the mark of non-action in these three karmas is itself the mark of non-action in every phenomenon whatsoever. One who is able to follow the wisdom of non-action in this way is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Field of Merit Bodhisattva said: "Meritorious conduct, offensive conduct, and unmoving conduct are two. But the true nature of these three kinds of conduct is itself emptiness — in emptiness there is no meritorious conduct, no offensive conduct, and no unmoving conduct. One who gives rise to none of these three kinds of conduct is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Flower Adornment Bodhisattva said: "Duality that arises from the self is two. But one who sees the true mark of the self gives rise to no dual phenomena; and if one does not abide in dual phenomena, there is no consciousness of anything at all — one who has nothing to be conscious of is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Treasury of Virtue Bodhisattva said: "The mark of having attained something is two. But if there is nothing attained, there is nothing to take up or put down; one for whom there is nothing to take up or put down is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Moon-Ascendant Bodhisattva said: "Darkness and brightness are two. But where there is neither darkness nor brightness, there is no duality at all. And why is this? It is like entering the concentration of the cessation of feeling and perception, in which there is neither darkness nor brightness; the mark of every phenomenon is exactly the same — one who enters equally within this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Jeweled Seal in Hand Bodhisattva said: "Delighting in nirvāṇa and not delighting in the world are two. But if one neither delights in nirvāṇa nor is weary of the world, there is no duality at all. And why is this? If there were bondage, there would be release; but if there were never any bondage to begin with, who would even seek release? Where there is neither bondage nor release, there is neither delight nor weariness — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Pearl-Crowned King Bodhisattva said: "The right path and the wrong path are two. But one who abides in the right path makes no distinction between what is wrong and what is right; being free of this duality is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Delighting in the Real Bodhisattva said: "The real and the unreal are two. But even one who truly sees does not see 'the real' as a thing to be seen — how much less the unreal? And why is this? Because this is not something seen by the fleshly eye at all; only the eye of wisdom can see it, and this eye of wisdom neither sees nor fails to see anything — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality."

Reading note — after Sengzhao and Daosheng

Sengzhao's gloss on Joyful to Behold's opening answer is worth setting beside a text this site has translated elsewhere: "form is itself emptiness — it does not wait for form to cease and only then become empty. To see form as different from emptiness is exactly what makes it two in the first place." That is close to the same claim the Heart Sūtra makes more famously — form is emptiness, emptiness is form — translated on this site in its own right, and worth noticing how directly this sūtra, a century or more earlier in its Chinese translation, is already making the identical move. Later in the list, Daosheng's aside on Tranquil Faculties Bodhisattva's answer about the Three Jewels sharpens the same point one level further: "wherever there is a mark, there is a counterpart; wherever there is a counterpart, there is duality — this has nothing to do with whether one is literally counting to one or counting to three." The problem was never the specific number two; it is having any fixed mark at all for something else to stand opposite to, which is exactly why "one," as Kumārajīva noted in the previous section, never actually escapes the trap on its own.

The list's final few entries begin to lean toward the chapter's close. On Moon-Ascendant's answer about darkness and brightness, Sengzhao reaches for a concrete image from meditation practice: practitioners of the two lesser vehicles who enter the concentration of the cessation of feeling and perception pass entire days and nights, by report, without registering any difference between light and dark at all — offered here as an analogy for how a bodhisattva holds no fixed relationship to either. And the list's last entry, Delighting in the Real Bodhisattva's answer, reads as a direct bridge into what follows: "the domain of true reality's wisdom-eye is not something the fleshly eye can see," Sengzhao writes; "the wisdom-eye itself does not even see 'the real' as a graspable thing, much less the unreal. Though it is called seeing nothing, there is nothing it fails to see — this is the true substance of the wisdom-eye." The very next voice to speak is Mañjuśrī's, and it is asking, in effect, exactly what this wisdom-eye that neither sees nor fails to see would actually say if it had to answer in words.

37. Mañjuśrī's Answer, and Vimalakīrti's Silence

Chinese Source

如是諸菩薩各各說已，問文殊師利：「何等是菩薩入不二法門？」文殊師利曰：「如我意者，於一切法無言無說，無示無識，離諸問答，是為入不二法門。」於是文殊師利問維摩詰：「我等各自說已，仁者當說何等是菩薩入不二法門？」時維摩詰默然無言。文殊師利歎曰：「善哉，善哉！乃至無有文字、語言，是真入不二法門。」說是入不二法門品時，於此眾中五千菩薩，皆入不二法門，得無生法忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

When each of these bodhisattvas had spoken in turn, they asked Mañjuśrī: "What, then, is a bodhisattva's entry into the Dharma-door of nonduality?" Mañjuśrī said: "As I see it, to have no word and no speech, no display and no cognition, with respect to every phenomenon whatsoever, and to be free of every question and every answer — this is entering the Dharma-door of nonduality." Mañjuśrī then asked Vimalakīrti: "Each of us has now spoken in turn. Sir, you should tell us now — what is a bodhisattva's entry into the Dharma-door of nonduality?" At that, Vimalakīrti remained silent and said not a single word. Mañjuśrī exclaimed in praise: "Excellent, excellent! Not to have even a single word or letter — this is the true entry into the Dharma-door of nonduality." When this chapter on entering the Dharma-door of nonduality was spoken, five thousand bodhisattvas in the assembly all entered the Dharma-door of nonduality, and attained the acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva, Sengzhao, and Daosheng

Sengzhao lays out the chapter's structure as three ascending steps: every bodhisattva above, however precisely each dissolved a duality, was still "measuring out speech about the marks of dharma" in doing so; Mañjuśrī has "speech about no-speech" — he uses words to say that words don't apply; and Vimalakīrti has "no-speech about no-speech" — he simply doesn't speak, full stop. All three, Sengzhao says, are pointing at the same central truth, but their traces differ in refinement, and speech about no-speech is not the equal of no-speech about no-speech itself — which is why Vimalakīrti's silence is, in his words, "the wonder of discourse itself." Kumārajīva illustrates the same point with a story that has nothing to do with Vimalakīrti directly, but that he clearly thought explained it better than any abstraction could. Some six hundred years after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, he says, a man became a monk at sixty, mastered the entire Buddhist canon within a short time, and then made a vow: he would not sleep, and his ribs would never again touch the ground, until he had attained the Way and every quality of meditative accomplishment — which is how he came to be called the Rib Elder, and how he swiftly became an arhat of great eloquence. A brilliant non-Buddhist debater named Aśvaghoṣa — a man of sharp intellect who had mastered every scripture and could refute any argument put to him — heard of the Rib Elder's reputation and came with his disciples to challenge him, declaring publicly that he could refute any position whatsoever, and that if he failed, he would cut off his own head in shame. The Rib Elder heard the challenge and said nothing at all. Aśvaghoṣa, taking the silence for an empty reputation with nothing behind it, turned to leave in triumph — but partway down the road, he stopped and thought it through again: "I said every argument can be refuted — but that very claim refutes itself the instant it is spoken. He said nothing at all, and so there was nothing in what he said for me to refute." Realizing he had lost the debate to a man who had not uttered a single word, he turned back, told the Rib Elder he had fallen into folly and that his head was at his disposal — though, since cutting off one's topknot carried nearly the same weight as death for a person of his standing at the time, he offered that instead — and became the Rib Elder's disciple on the spot, going on to become one of the tradition's greatest philosophers, known to his contemporaries as "the second Buddha." Kumārajīva's point in telling it here is exact: to speak and be refuted is worse than to say nothing and leave nothing behind to refute — silence, deployed at exactly the right moment, is itself the stronger argument.

Daosheng adds a sharper, more technical distinction that is easy to miss on a first reading: Mañjuśrī has made clear that nonduality "cannot be spoken of," but he has not yet gone the one further step of making clear that even saying "it cannot be spoken of" is itself still a form of speaking. This, on Daosheng's reading, is precisely the gap Vimalakīrti's silence closes — not a different content from what Mañjuśrī already said, but a performance of that same content without the residual self-contradiction of asserting, in words, that no words apply. "If speech really were adequate to it," Daosheng writes, "how could one simply fall silent?" It is also worth noticing who is left to pronounce the verdict: Vimalakīrti, having said nothing, cannot very well praise his own silence, so it is Mañjuśrī — the one who has just been shown to have gone one step less far than his friend — who receives the silence, understands it without a word passing between them, and speaks on Vimalakīrti's behalf to call it excellent.
