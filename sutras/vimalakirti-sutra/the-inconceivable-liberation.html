The Inconceivable Liberation

不思議品第六

Śāriputra's unspoken worry about where everyone will sit becomes a teaching on what it actually means to seek the Dharma. Then Vimalakīrti borrows thirty-two thousand towering thrones from a distant buddha-land, and names the liberation behind it all — the one this whole sūtra is secretly named after.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
Śāriputra's silent worry about seating, and Vimalakīrti's teaching that true seeking of the Dharma seeks nothing at all; thirty-two thousand towering lion-thrones borrowed from a distant buddha-land and fit into one small room without crowding it; Mount Sumeru placed inside a mustard seed and a litany of comparable feats; and Mahākāśyapa's realization that many demon-kings across the universe are secretly bodhisattvas in disguise.
A name behind the sūtra's own name
In its final chapter, this sūtra calls itself "the Dharma-gate of the inconceivable liberation" — a title drawn directly from this part.

21. Śāriputra Looks for a Seat, and What Seeking the Dharma Means

Chinese Source

爾時舍利弗見此室中無有床座，作是念：「斯諸菩薩、大弟子眾，當於何坐？」長者維摩詰知其意，語舍利弗言：「云何仁者？為法來耶？求床座耶？」舍利弗言：「我為法來，非為床座。」維摩詰言：「唯，舍利弗！夫求法者，不貪軀命，何況床座？夫求法者，非有色、受、想、行、識之求，非有界、入之求，非有欲、色、無色之求。唯，舍利弗！夫求法者，不著佛求，不著法求，不著眾求；夫求法者，無見苦求，無斷集求，無造盡證、修道之求。所以者何？法無戲論，若言我當見苦、斷集、證滅、修道，是則戲論，非求法也。唯，舍利弗！法名寂滅，若行生滅，是求生滅，非求法也；法名無染，若染於法乃至涅槃，是則染著，非求法也；法無行處，若行於法，是則行處，非求法也；法無取捨，若取捨法，是則取捨，非求法也；法無處所，若著處所，是則著處，非求法也；法名無相，若隨相識，是則求相，非求法也；法不可住，若住於法，是則住法，非求法也；法不可見、聞、覺、知，若行見、聞、覺、知，是則見、聞、覺、知，非求法也；法名無為，若行有為，是求有為，非求法也：是故，舍利弗！若求法者，於一切法，應無所求。」說是語時，五百天子於諸法中得法眼淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Śāriputra, seeing that this room had no seats or couches, thought to himself: "Where are all these bodhisattvas and great disciples to sit?" The elder Vimalakīrti, knowing his thought, said to Śāriputra: "Well then, sir — have you come for the sake of the Dharma, or have you come seeking a seat?" Śāriputra said: "I have come for the sake of the Dharma, not for a seat." Vimalakīrti said: "Ah, Śāriputra — one who truly seeks the Dharma does not covet even his own body or life, how much less a seat to sit on! One who truly seeks the Dharma does not seek form, feeling, perception, mental formations, or consciousness; does not seek the sense-elements or the sense-fields; does not seek the realm of desire, of form, or of formlessness. Ah, Śāriputra — one who truly seeks the Dharma is not attached in his seeking to the Buddha, is not attached in his seeking to the Dharma, is not attached in his seeking to the Saṃgha; one who truly seeks the Dharma does not seek to see suffering, does not seek to cut off its origin, does not seek to realize its cessation, and does not seek to cultivate the path leading to it. And why? Because the Dharma admits of no idle speculation — if one says, 'I shall see suffering, cut off its origin, realize its cessation, and cultivate the path,' this is itself idle speculation, not the true seeking of the Dharma. Ah, Śāriputra — the Dharma is called stillness itself; if one practices toward arising and ceasing, one is seeking arising and ceasing, not the Dharma. The Dharma is called undefiled; if one becomes attached even to the Dharma, or to nirvāṇa itself, this is itself defiled attachment, not the seeking of the Dharma. The Dharma has no place to practice in; if one practices toward the Dharma as though it were somewhere to arrive at, this is itself a place practiced toward, not the seeking of the Dharma. The Dharma admits of no taking up or setting down; if one takes up or sets down the Dharma, this is itself a taking up and setting down, not the seeking of the Dharma. The Dharma has no fixed location; if one becomes attached to any location, this is itself an attachment to location, not the seeking of the Dharma. The Dharma is called signless; if one follows after marks and forms recognition based on them, this is itself the seeking of a mark, not the seeking of the Dharma. The Dharma cannot be abided in; if one abides in the Dharma, this is itself an abiding in the Dharma, not the seeking of the Dharma. The Dharma cannot be seen, heard, sensed, or known; if one practices seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing, this is itself seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing, not the seeking of the Dharma. The Dharma is called unconditioned; if one practices toward the conditioned, this is itself the seeking of the conditioned, not the seeking of the Dharma. Therefore, Śāriputra, if one truly seeks the Dharma, one should seek nothing at all, with respect to any phenomenon whatsoever." When he spoke these words, five hundred young gods attained the pure Dharma-eye with regard to every phenomenon.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva

Kumārajīva's note on why this small, unspoken worry belongs to Śāriputra specifically is worth having, since it is easy to miss the human detail underneath the doctrine: among the disciples, Śāriputra was the eldest and physically frailest, and the thought is his alone precisely because ordinary bodily fatigue, not any failure of understanding, prompts it — he does not even voice the concern aloud, since he assumes Vimalakīrti will already know his mind. Vimalakīrti's response does not solve the practical problem at all (no chairs actually appear in this passage — the thrones arrive only in the following section); instead he redirects the question itself, from "where will we sit" to what it even means to have "come" anywhere seeking anything.

The extended list that follows works by systematically denying that seeking the Dharma could be a search for any object at all — not the five aggregates, not the sense-fields, not even the four noble truths taken as things to be respectively seen, cut off, realized, and cultivated. Kumārajīva's structural point is that any of these, treated as a goal to be attained, becomes what the text calls "idle speculation" (戲論) rather than the Dharma itself, since genuine understanding does not relate to reality as an object standing apart from the one who understands it. The list closes on the same paradox this sūtra returns to more than once: seeking, done correctly, seeks nothing — not because the Dharma is unimportant, but because treating it as something separate to be acquired is itself the obstacle.

22. Thirty-Two Thousand Borrowed Thrones

Chinese Source

爾時，長者維摩詰問文殊師利：「仁者遊於無量千萬億阿僧祇國，何等佛土有好上妙功德成就師子之座？」文殊師利言：「居士！東方度三十六恒河沙國，有世界名須彌相，其佛號須彌燈王，今現在。彼佛身長八萬四千由旬，其師子座高八萬四千由旬，嚴飾第一。」於是長者維摩詰現神通力，即時彼佛遣三萬二千師子座，高廣嚴淨，來入維摩詰室，諸菩薩、大弟子、釋、梵、四天王等，昔所未見。其室廣博，悉皆包容三萬二千師子座，無所妨礙。於毘耶離城及閻浮提、四天下，亦不迫迮，悉見如故。爾時維摩詰語文殊師利：「就師子座！與諸菩薩上人俱坐，當自立身如彼座像。」其得神通菩薩，即自變形為四萬二千由旬，坐師子座，諸新發意菩薩及大弟子皆不能昇。爾時維摩詰語舍利弗：「就師子座！」舍利弗言：「居士！此座高廣，吾不能昇。」維摩詰言：「唯，舍利弗！為須彌燈王如來作禮，乃可得坐。」於是新發意菩薩及大弟子即為須彌燈王如來作禮，便得坐師子座。舍利弗言：「居士！未曾有也！如是小室，乃容受此高廣之座，於毘耶離城無所妨礙，又於閻浮提聚落、城邑，及四天下諸天、龍王、鬼神宮殿，亦不迫迮。」維摩詰言：「唯，舍利弗！諸佛菩薩，有解脫名不可思議。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time the elder Vimalakīrti asked Mañjuśrī: "Sir, you have traveled through immeasurable thousands of millions of koṭis of world-systems — in which buddha-land have you seen the finest, most excellent lion-thrones, perfected in merit?" Mañjuśrī said: "Householder, in the eastern direction, past as many world-systems as thirty-six times the sands of the Ganges, there is a world called Sumeru-Radiance, whose buddha is named Sumeru-Lamp-King, and who dwells there even now. That buddha's body is eighty-four thousand yojanas tall, and his lion-throne is eighty-four thousand yojanas high, adorned more finely than any other." At this the elder Vimalakīrti displayed his spiritual power, and at once that buddha sent thirty-two thousand lion-thrones — lofty, vast, and adorned in purity — into Vimalakīrti's very room, thrones such as none of the bodhisattvas, great disciples, Indra, Brahmā, or the Four Heavenly Kings had ever seen before. And yet the room, spacious and vast, held all thirty-two thousand lion-thrones without the least obstruction, and neither the city of Vaiśālī, nor the continent of Jambudvīpa, nor the four continents were crowded by them at all — everything appeared exactly as it had before. Vimalakīrti then said to Mañjuśrī: "Take your seat upon a lion-throne, and sit together with the other superior bodhisattvas — you should make your own body match the size of that throne." Those bodhisattvas who had attained spiritual powers transformed their own bodies to a height of forty-two thousand yojanas, and took their seats on the lion-thrones; but the bodhisattvas newly resolved on the path, and the great disciples, were unable to climb up onto them at all. Vimalakīrti then said to Śāriputra: "Take your seat upon a lion-throne." Śāriputra said: "Householder, this throne is too high and too vast — I am unable to climb onto it." Vimalakīrti said: "Ah, Śāriputra — only by paying homage to the Tathāgata Sumeru-Lamp-King will you be able to take this seat." At this the newly resolved bodhisattvas and the great disciples all paid homage to the Tathāgata Sumeru-Lamp-King, and were then able to take their seats upon the lion-thrones. Śāriputra said: "Householder, this is something never seen before! That a room this small could hold thrones this vast and lofty, without in the least crowding the city of Vaiśālī, or the villages and towns of Jambudvīpa, or the palaces of every deva, dragon-king, and spirit throughout the four continents!" Vimalakīrti said: "Ah, Śāriputra — the buddhas and bodhisattvas have a liberation called the Inconceivable."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva

Kumārajīva gives two reasons Vimalakīrti reaches all the way to a distant buddha-land for these thrones rather than simply conjuring them himself: first, to let beginning bodhisattvas in the assembly witness directly the scale of merit a fully accomplished buddha-land actually contains, deepening their own aspiration by comparison; second, to establish a living connection between the two lands through the exchange itself, so that the teaching's benefit is not confined to Vaiśālī alone. The detail of bodhisattvas literally enlarging their own bodies to match the throne's scale, while the newly resolved bodhisattvas and the great disciples simply cannot manage it, quietly restates in physical terms the gap this whole sūtra has been drawing out verbally since Part 3.

Śāriputra's small, almost comic humiliation — having to formally bow to a buddha he has never met, in a land he has never visited, simply in order to sit down — belongs to the same pattern as his earlier encounters in this sūtra: not a cruelty, but one more instance of a senior disciple's ordinary competence proving inadequate to a scale of practice he has not yet trained for. His closing exclamation — genuine astonishment that a small room could hold vast thrones without either the room or the thrones changing size — is the passage's real setup line, and Vimalakīrti's answer to it, "the buddhas and bodhisattvas have a liberation called the Inconceivable," names the concept the rest of this chapter exists to demonstrate at length.

23. Sumeru in a Mustard Seed

Chinese Source

「若菩薩住是解脫者，以須彌之高廣內芥子中無所增減，須彌山王本相如故，而四天王、忉利諸天不覺不知己之所入，唯應度者乃見須彌入芥子中，是名住不思議解脫法門。又以四大海水入一毛孔，不嬈魚、鼈、黿、鼉水性之屬，而彼大海本相如故，諸龍、鬼神、阿修羅等不覺不知己之所入，於此眾生亦無所嬈。又，舍利弗！住不可思議解脫菩薩，斷取三千大千世界，如陶家輪，著右掌中，擲過恒河沙世界之外，其中眾生不覺不知己之所往；又復還置本處，都不使人有往來想，而此世界本相如故。又，舍利弗！或有眾生樂久住世而可度者，菩薩即延七日以為一劫，令彼眾生謂之一劫；或有眾生不樂久住而可度者，菩薩即促一劫以為七日，令彼眾生謂之七日。又，舍利弗！住不可思議解脫菩薩，以一切佛土嚴飾之事集在一國，示於眾生。又菩薩以一佛土眾生置之右掌，飛到十方遍示一切，而不動本處。又，舍利弗！十方眾生供養諸佛之具，菩薩於一毛孔皆令得見；又十方國土所有日、月、星宿，於一毛孔普使見之。又，舍利弗！十方世界所有諸風，菩薩悉能吸著口中，而身無損，外諸樹木亦不摧折；又十方世界劫盡燒時，以一切火內於腹中，火事如故而不為害。又於下方過恒河沙等諸佛世界，取一佛土，舉著上方，過恒河沙無數世界，如持鍼鋒舉一棗葉，而無所嬈。「又，舍利弗！住不可思議解脫菩薩，能以神通現作佛身，或現辟支佛身，或現聲聞身，或現帝釋身，或現梵王身，或現世主身，或現轉輪王身。又十方世界所有眾聲上、中、下音，皆能變之令作佛聲，演出無常、苦、空、無我之音，及十方諸佛所說種種之法，皆於其中普令得聞。舍利弗！我今略說菩薩不可思議解脫之力，若廣說者，窮劫不盡。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"If a bodhisattva abides in this liberation, he can place the whole vast height of Mount Sumeru inside a single mustard seed, with nothing added and nothing taken away, while Sumeru, sovereign of mountains, remains exactly as it was — and the Four Heavenly Kings and the gods of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven do not even notice or realize that they themselves have been placed within it; only those beings meant to be brought across by this teaching actually see Sumeru placed inside the mustard seed. This is what is called abiding in the Dharma-gate of the inconceivable liberation. He can likewise pour the water of all four great oceans into a single pore of his skin, without disturbing the fish, turtles, crocodiles, or any other creature that lives in the water, while the ocean itself remains exactly as it was, and the dragons, spirits, and asuras living in it do not notice or realize that they have been placed within it, and these beings, too, are undisturbed by it in any way. And further, Śāriputra: a bodhisattva who abides in the inconceivable liberation can lift up this entire great trichiliocosm, just as a potter lifts his wheel, set it upon the palm of his right hand, and hurl it past world-systems as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, and the beings within it do not notice or realize where they have gone — and then he can set it back exactly in its original place, without anyone ever forming even the thought that they had traveled anywhere at all, while the world itself remains exactly as it was. And further, Śāriputra: if there are beings who wish to remain long in the world and can be brought across by this means, the bodhisattva can stretch out seven days to the length of an entire aeon, so that those beings take it to be a full aeon; and if there are beings who do not wish to remain long and can be brought across by this means, he can compress an entire aeon down to seven days, so that those beings take it to be only seven days. And further, Śāriputra: a bodhisattva who abides in the inconceivable liberation can gather the adornments of every buddha-land in the ten directions into a single land, and display them to beings; he can place the beings of a single buddha-land upon the palm of his hand and fly with them through the ten directions, showing them everything, all without ever leaving his original place. And further, Śāriputra: whatever offerings the beings of the ten directions make to the buddhas, the bodhisattva can cause them all to be seen within a single pore of his skin; and whatever suns, moons, and stars exist in the lands of the ten directions, he can cause them all to be seen within a single pore of his skin as well. And further, Śāriputra: whatever winds exist throughout the worlds of the ten directions, the bodhisattva can draw them all into his own mouth without the least harm to his body, and without breaking a single tree outside; and when the fires that consume a world at the end of an aeon are burning throughout the ten directions, he can take every one of those fires into his own belly, and the fire continues to burn exactly as before, without causing him any harm at all. And from a world below, past as many buddha-lands as the sands of the Ganges, he can take up a single buddha-land and set it above, past as many innumerable world-systems as the sands of the Ganges again, as easily and as harmlessly as lifting a single jujube leaf on the point of a needle. And further, Śāriputra: a bodhisattva who abides in the inconceivable liberation can, through spiritual power, manifest the body of a buddha, or manifest the body of a pratyekabuddha, or the body of a śrāvaka, or the body of Indra, or the body of a Brahmā king, or the body of a world-ruler, or the body of a wheel-turning king. And whatever sounds exist throughout the worlds of the ten directions — high, middle, or low in pitch — he can transform every one of them into the voice of a buddha, sounding out the teaching of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, and every teaching spoken by every buddha of the ten directions, so that it may be heard everywhere within that single sound. Śāriputra, I have now given only a brief account of the power of a bodhisattva's inconceivable liberation — were I to describe it in full, I could not exhaust it in the course of an entire aeon."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva reads the mustard-seed feat, and the ones immediately around it, as targeting a specific misconception rather than being sheer spectacle for its own sake: some in his audience apparently held that the four great elements were themselves divine, permanent, and necessarily maximal in scale — that something as vast as Sumeru simply could not, by its own fixed nature, be otherwise. Each feat in this list dismantles one part of that assumption in turn: Sumeru inside a mustard seed shows it is not fixed in size; the oceans inside a single pore show the same; the compressing and stretching of seven days into an aeon shows that even time is not a fixed container. None of these is presented as magic overriding reality — each is presented as revealing that the "fixed nature" a naive observer assumed reality to have was never really there to begin with.

Sengzhao's own framing of the passage reaches beyond this single chapter to describe what he takes to be the whole sūtra's subject: "wisdom illuminates nothing it does not penetrate; skillful means cultivates no virtue it does not perfect... this dark, still, non-doing which is nothing but doing — this is the utmost limit of the inconceivable." He goes on to note explicitly that this text, from its opening teaching on the pure land through its closing chapter on the offering of the Dharma, is the working-out of exactly this one quality throughout — which is why, in the sūtra's own final chapter, Ānanda asks what this whole teaching should be called, and the Buddha answers: "the Dharma-gate of the inconceivable liberation." This chapter earns that title in its most literal, image-driven form; the rest of the sūtra earns it in subtler ways.

24. Mahākāśyapa's Lament, and the Demon-Kings Who Are Secretly Bodhisattvas

Chinese Source

是時大迦葉聞說菩薩不可思議解脫法門，歎未曾有，謂舍利弗：「譬如有人，於盲者前現眾色像，非彼所見；一切聲聞聞是不可思議解脫法門，不能解了，為若此也！智者聞是，其誰不發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心？我等何為永絕其根，於此大乘已如敗種！一切聲聞聞是不可思議解脫法門，皆應號泣，聲震三千大千世界；一切菩薩應大欣慶，頂受此法。若有菩薩信解不可思議解脫法門者，一切魔眾無如之何。」大迦葉說是語時，三萬二千天子皆發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。爾時維摩詰語大迦葉：「仁者！十方無量阿僧祇世界中作魔王者，多是住不可思議解脫菩薩。以方便力教化眾生，現作魔王。又，迦葉！十方無量菩薩，或有人從乞手足耳鼻、頭目髓腦、血肉皮骨、聚落城邑、妻子奴婢、象馬車乘、金銀琉璃、車磲馬碯、珊瑚琥珀、真珠珂貝、衣服飲食，如此乞者，多是住不可思議解脫菩薩，以方便力而往試之，令其堅固。所以者何？住不可思議解脫菩薩，有威德力，故現行逼迫，示諸眾生如是難事；凡夫下劣，無有力勢，不能如是逼迫菩薩。譬如龍象蹴踏，非驢所堪。是名住不可思議解脫菩薩智慧方便之門。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At this Mahākāśyapa, hearing this teaching on the bodhisattva's inconceivable liberation, praised it as something never known before, and said to Śāriputra: "It is like displaying a whole array of colors and forms before someone blind — that person simply cannot see them. Just so, when every śrāvaka hears this teaching on the inconceivable liberation, none of them can truly comprehend it. What wise person, hearing this, would not give rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening? Why is it that we ourselves have had this very root permanently severed, become, with respect to the Great Vehicle, no better than scorched seed? Every śrāvaka, on hearing this teaching of the inconceivable liberation, ought to weep aloud, with a cry that shakes the whole trichiliocosm; every bodhisattva, by contrast, ought to rejoice greatly, and receive this teaching upon the crown of the head. If there is any bodhisattva who has faith in and understands this teaching of the inconceivable liberation, then the whole host of demons can do nothing at all against him." When Mahākāśyapa had spoken these words, thirty-two thousand young gods all gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. At that time Vimalakīrti said to Mahākāśyapa: "Sir — among the immeasurable, countless world-systems of the ten directions, those who act as demon-kings are, for the most part, bodhisattvas who abide in this inconceivable liberation, teaching and converting beings by the power of skillful means, manifesting themselves as demon-kings. And further, Kāśyapa: among the immeasurable bodhisattvas of the ten directions, when someone comes begging for a bodhisattva's hands, feet, ears, nose, head, eyes, marrow, brain, blood, flesh, skin, bones, villages, cities, wife and children, male and female servants, elephants, horses, and chariots, gold, silver, and lapis lazuli, giant clam-shell and agate, coral and amber, pearls and shells, clothing and food — those who beg in this way are, for the most part, bodhisattvas abiding in this inconceivable liberation, who go to test such a bodhisattva by the power of skillful means, in order to make his resolve firm. And why? Because a bodhisattva who abides in the inconceivable liberation has the majestic power to press and press beings this way, showing them how difficult such demands truly are; an ordinary, inferior being, lacking that power, could never press a bodhisattva in such a manner. It is like the trampling stride of a great elephant or dragon — no donkey could ever bear its weight. This is called the gate of wisdom and skillful means of a bodhisattva who abides in the inconceivable liberation."

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Sengzhao is careful to flag that Mahākāśyapa's call for every śrāvaka to "weep aloud, with a cry that shakes the whole trichiliocosm" is rhetorical rather than a literal prediction: an arhat's grief has, by definition, already been permanently extinguished, so the image functions to mark the sheer scale of the gap Kāśyapa is describing between what the śrāvaka path can grasp and what this teaching contains, not to suggest enlightened arhats are secretly about to burst into tears. The image works precisely because it describes something that, strictly speaking, cannot happen — which is itself the point.

Vimalakīrti's response reframes the entire chapter's demonstration of inconceivable power in ethical terms, and does so by destabilizing a distinction the assembly has likely been taking for granted throughout this sūtra: many of the demon-kings scattered across the universe, feared as adversaries of the Dharma, are themselves bodhisattvas abiding in this same inconceivable liberation, wearing that role deliberately as a teaching device. The same is true, he says, of beings who make agonizing demands on other bodhisattvas — asking for their limbs, their families, everything they own — these too are often bodhisattvas testing another bodhisattva's resolve, not malicious actors, and only a being of genuine spiritual power could press another this hard without simply causing harm. This complicates, deliberately, the neat categories of villain and hero the chapter's earlier miracles might have suggested were fixed: the same inconceivable liberation that lets a bodhisattva fold Sumeru into a mustard seed also lets him, when it serves beings better, wear the face of the very thing everyone else assumes he opposes.
