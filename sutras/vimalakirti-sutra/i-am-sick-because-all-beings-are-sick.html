Mañjuśrī Visits the Sick

文殊師利問疾品第五

Every disciple and bodhisattva has declined; only Mañjuśrī agrees to go. He finds Vimalakīrti's room emptied of everything but a single bed, and receives, across one long conversation, this sūtra's central teaching on compassion, emptiness, sickness, and what a bodhisattva's conduct actually is.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
Mañjuśrī's acceptance of the visit and the great assembly that follows him; Vimalakīrti's empty room and the "not-coming that is coming"; the sūtra's most quoted line, "because all beings are sick, I am sick"; a dialectic on emptiness built entirely from questions; how to comfort a sick bodhisattva and how a sick bodhisattva should subdue his own mind; and a long, paradox-structured catalogue of what a bodhisattva's conduct actually is.
Why this exchange matters
Every disciple and bodhisattva in the two preceding chapters declined this same errand. Mañjuśrī is the one figure in the assembly whose own wisdom is close enough to Vimalakīrti's that the conversation between them becomes, in effect, the sūtra's doctrinal center.

15. Mañjuśrī Agrees, and the Room Is Emptied

Chinese Source

爾時佛告文殊師利：「汝行詣維摩詰問疾。」文殊師利白佛言：「世尊！彼上人者，難為詶對。深達實相，善說法要，辯才無滯，智慧無礙；一切菩薩法式悉知，諸佛祕藏無不得入；降伏眾魔，遊戲神通，其慧方便，皆已得度。雖然，當承佛聖旨，詣彼問疾。」於是眾中諸菩薩、大弟子、釋、梵、四天王等，咸作是念：「今二大士文殊師利、維摩詰共談，必說妙法！」即時八千菩薩、五百聲聞、百千天人皆欲隨從。於是文殊師利與諸菩薩、大弟子眾及諸天人，恭敬圍繞，入毘耶離大城。爾時長者維摩詰心念：「今文殊師利與大眾俱來！」即以神力空其室內，除去所有及諸侍者；唯置一床，以疾而臥。文殊師利既入其舍，見其室空，無諸所有，獨寢一床。時維摩詰言：「善來文殊師利！不來相而來，不見相而見。」文殊師利言：「如是！居士！若來已，更不來；若去已，更不去。所以者何？來者無所從來，去者無所至，所可見者，更不可見。且置是事，居士！是疾寧可忍不？療治有損，不至增乎？世尊慇懃致問無量。居士！是疾何所因起？其生久如？當云何滅？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Then the Buddha said to Mañjuśrī: "Go and inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness." Mañjuśrī said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, that superior being is difficult to engage in reply. He has penetrated deeply into true reality, and is skilled in stating the essentials of the Dharma; his eloquence never falters, and his wisdom is never obstructed; he knows every rule and form of bodhisattva conduct, and there is no secret treasury of any buddha he has not entered; he has subdued every demon, and sports freely with spiritual powers; his wisdom and skillful means have both already reached the far shore. And yet, I will go, carrying out the Buddha's own sacred command, to inquire after his illness." At this, all the bodhisattvas, great disciples, and the retinues of Indra, Brahmā, and the Four Heavenly Kings in the assembly thought to themselves: "Now that these two great beings, Mañjuśrī and Vimalakīrti, are to speak together, they are sure to teach a wondrous Dharma." At once eight thousand bodhisattvas, five hundred śrāvakas, and hundreds of thousands of devas and humans all wished to accompany them. Mañjuśrī, together with the assembled bodhisattvas, great disciples, and devas and humans, entered the great city of Vaiśālī, reverently surrounded on every side. At that time the elder Vimalakīrti thought to himself: "Mañjuśrī is now coming, together with this whole great assembly." By his spiritual power he at once emptied his room of everything within it, and dismissed all his attendants, leaving only a single bed, upon which he lay as one who is ill. When Mañjuśrī entered his house, he saw the room empty, with nothing at all in it, and Vimalakīrti lying alone on a single bed. Vimalakīrti said: "Welcome, Mañjuśrī! You have come without the mark of coming, and you see without the mark of seeing." Mañjuśrī said: "Just so, householder! If one has truly come, one does not come again; if one has truly gone, one does not go again. And why? Because one who comes comes from nowhere, and one who goes goes nowhere, and what can be seen cannot, in that sense, be seen again. But let us set this matter aside for now. Householder, is your illness bearable? Is the treatment for it doing any good, and not making it worse? The World-Honored One asked after you most earnestly, with immeasurable concern. Householder, what is the cause of this illness? How long has it been arising? And how is it to be brought to an end?"

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Sengzhao raises, and deliberately declines to settle, a question a careful reader will already be asking: given that the whole assembly of thirty-two thousand bodhisattvas and five hundred disciples has now, one after another, declined this errand, why should the reader assume Mañjuśrī succeeds where all of them failed? His answer is that the text itself never actually states this — "the perfected one's transformations follow no fixed pattern, appearing and withdrawing by turns; who would dare to fix a ranking of superior and inferior among them?" Mañjuśrī agrees to go for the same reason every disciple gave a reason not to: each response fits the particular teaching role that figure needs to play in the text, not a scoreboard of who is spiritually greater than whom.

The room's sudden emptiness, staged in advance of Mañjuśrī's arrival, is itself the chapter's first teaching, delivered before a word is spoken: Vimalakīrti clears away every object and every attendant because, as Sengzhao notes, this matches Mañjuśrī's own known temperament for stillness — the empty room "receives him as he is." When Vimalakīrti then greets him with "you have come without the mark of coming, and you see without the mark of seeing," and Mañjuśrī affirms it in kind, both men are performing, as courtesy, the very teaching on non-arising that will occupy much of this sūtra elsewhere — a nice touch is that Mañjuśrī, uniquely among everyone Vimalakīrti has encountered so far, answers in exactly the same register rather than being caught short by it. This is, after all, a conversation the sūtra sets up as between equals.

16. "Because All Beings Are Sick, I Am Sick"

Chinese Source

維摩詰言：「從癡、有愛，則我病生。以一切眾生病，是故我病；若一切眾生病滅，則我病滅。所以者何？菩薩為眾生故入生死，有生死則有病；若眾生得離病者，則菩薩無復病。譬如長者唯有一子。其子得病，父母亦病；若子病愈，父母亦愈。菩薩如是，於諸眾生愛之若子。眾生病，則菩薩病；眾生病愈，菩薩亦愈。又言『是疾何所因起？』菩薩病者，以大悲起。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Vimalakīrti said: "My illness arises from ignorance and the existence of craving — this is how my illness arises. Because every being is sick, therefore I am sick; if the sickness of every being were to cease, then my own sickness would cease as well. And why is this? A bodhisattva, for the sake of beings, enters into birth-and-death; and wherever there is birth-and-death, there is sickness. If beings could be freed of sickness, then the bodhisattva would no longer be sick either. It is like an elder who has only a single son: when that son falls ill, the parents also fall ill; and when the son's illness is cured, the parents, too, are cured. It is just the same for a bodhisattva — he loves every being as his own child. When beings are sick, the bodhisattva is sick; when beings are cured, the bodhisattva, too, is cured. You also asked what is the cause of this illness's arising — a bodhisattva's illness arises from great compassion."

Reading note — after Daorong (道融) and Sengzhao

Daorong's commentary — a rare voice among the four preserved in this compilation, and worth naming here precisely because he adds something the more famous Sengzhao does not — reads the opening line as making a claim about scale, not just cause: an ordinary being's illness has ignorance and craving as its root, but a bodhisattva's illness is generated by the whole of that same root running through every being without exception, which is why the passage takes such pains, twice over, to describe how long the illness has lasted (not recently, but "for as long as ignorance and craving themselves have had no beginning") before ever answering what actually causes it. On this reading, the sūtra deliberately orders its answers — first duration, then cause — precisely because the duration itself is the more important disclosure: a newly practicing bodhisattva who has not yet lived long in birth-and-death might otherwise assume this illness is a temporary condition to be cured and set aside, rather than a stance toward every being's suffering that a bodhisattva takes up permanently and without limit.

Sengzhao's gloss on the parent-and-child simile is worth sitting with, since it makes explicit something easy to read past: "the bodhisattva's illness arises only because of beings — once their illness ceases, what further affliction could remain for him?" This is not metaphorical solidarity. The text's own logic is that a bodhisattva's compassion is not a feeling added on top of an otherwise separate existence; his condition is genuinely, causally tied to beings' condition, the way a parent's grief is not a separate event from a child's suffering but the very same event felt from where the parent stands. The closing line — that the illness arises "from great compassion" — answers Mañjuśrī's original question about cause only after this entire structure has been laid out, which is itself the chapter's point: compassion, for a bodhisattva, is not a virtue exercised from a position of health looking down at sickness, but the mechanism by which another being's sickness becomes, without any gap, one's own.

17. What the Room's Emptiness Actually Means

Chinese Source

文殊師利言：「居士！此室何以空無侍者？」維摩詰言：「諸佛國土亦復皆空。」又問：「以何為空？」答曰：「以空空。」又問：「空何用空？」答曰：「以無分別空故空。」又問：「空可分別耶？」答曰：「分別亦空。」又問：「空當於何求？」答曰：「當於六十二見中求。」又問：「六十二見當於何求？」答曰：「當於諸佛解脫中求。」又問：「諸佛解脫當於何求？」答曰：「當於一切眾生心行中求。又仁所問：『何無侍者？』一切眾魔及諸外道，皆吾侍也。所以者何？眾魔者樂生死，菩薩於生死而不捨；外道者樂諸見，菩薩於諸見而不動。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Mañjuśrī said: "Householder, why is this room empty, with no attendants in it?" Vimalakīrti said: "Every buddha-land, too, is entirely empty." He asked further: "By what is it made empty?" He answered: "It is empty by means of emptiness itself." He asked further: "What use is there in emptying emptiness?" He answered: "It is empty because there is no discrimination in that emptiness." He asked further: "Can emptiness itself be discriminated?" He answered: "Discrimination, too, is empty." He asked further: "Where is emptiness to be sought?" He answered: "It should be sought among the sixty-two views." He asked further: "Where are the sixty-two views to be sought?" He answered: "They should be sought in the liberation of the buddhas." He asked further: "Where is the liberation of the buddhas to be sought?" He answered: "It should be sought in the mental conduct of every being. And as for what you asked — why there are no attendants here — every demon and every follower of a heterodox path is my attendant. And why? Because demons delight in birth-and-death, and a bodhisattva does not abandon birth-and-death; because those of heterodox paths delight in their views, and a bodhisattva is unmoved with respect to every view."

Reading note — after Sengzhao and Zhu Daosheng

This chain of six questions builds a genuine argument rather than repeating one point, and it is worth walking through Sengzhao's stage-by-stage reading rather than treating it as pure wordplay. "Empty by means of emptiness itself" (以空空) answers with what Zhu Daosheng calls the emptiness produced by wisdom actively observing a thing's lack of fixed nature — a cognitive act, not a bare fact. Mañjuśrī's next question — what use is there in emptying an emptiness that is already empty — presses on whether this wisdom-emptiness is itself just another thing standing over against ordinary phenomena; Vimalakīrti's answer, "because there is no discrimination in it," collapses that gap: genuine wisdom does not discriminate a special "emptiness" apart from the phenomena it observes, so there is no second act of emptying required. When Mañjuśrī then asks whether emptiness itself "can be discriminated" — testing whether Vimalakīrti's own explanation just now was itself a subtle act of discrimination — the answer "discrimination, too, is empty" closes the loop rather than opening a regress: even the analytical activity used to explain emptiness has no fixed nature of its own to fall back on.

The final three questions turn from analysis to something closer to genealogy — where would one actually go looking for emptiness in practice? — and the answers are deliberately provocative: in the sixty-two wrong views, in the buddhas' own liberation, and finally in the ordinary mental conduct of every being. Sengzhao reads this chain as asserting that wrong view and liberation share a single underlying nature, so that "seeking correct wisdom's emptiness" properly starts by examining wrong view directly rather than avoiding it, and traces back, in the end, to nothing more exotic than how minds actually work. This directly sets up Vimalakīrti's closing remark, answering Mañjuśrī's original question about attendants: demons and heretics are his household precisely because a bodhisattva neither flees birth-and-death (which demons love) nor is destabilized by wrong views (which heretics hold) — he can keep them as attendants because he has nothing in him for their usual leverage to catch hold of.

18. How to Comfort a Sick Bodhisattva

Chinese Source

文殊師利言：「居士所疾為何等相？」維摩詰言：「我病無形不可見。」又問：「此病身合耶？心合耶？」答曰：「非身合，身相離故；亦非心合，心如幻故。」又問：「地大、水大、火大、風大，於此四大，何大之病？」答曰：「是病非地大，亦不離地大；水、火、風大，亦復如是。而眾生病從四大起，以其有病，是故我病。」爾時文殊師利問維摩詰言：「菩薩應云何慰喻有疾菩薩？」維摩詰言：「說身無常，不說厭離於身；說身有苦，不說樂於涅槃；說身無我，而說教導眾生；說身空寂，不說畢竟寂滅；說悔先罪，而不說入於過去；以己之疾，愍於彼疾；當識宿世無數劫苦，當念饒益一切眾生；憶所修福，念於淨命，勿生憂惱，常起精進；當作醫王，療治眾病：菩薩應如是慰喻有疾菩薩，令其歡喜。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Mañjuśrī said: "Householder, what is the mark of this illness of yours?" Vimalakīrti said: "My illness has no form, and cannot be seen." He asked further: "Does this illness unite with the body, or with the mind?" He answered: "It does not unite with the body, since the body's own mark is itself apart from it; nor does it unite with the mind, since the mind is like an illusion." He asked further: "Among the four elements — earth, water, fire, and wind — which element's illness is this?" He answered: "This illness is not the illness of the earth element, nor is it apart from the earth element; it is the same with water, fire, and wind. And yet a being's illness arises from the four elements — it is because they are ill that I, too, am ill." At that time Mañjuśrī asked Vimalakīrti: "How should a bodhisattva comfort another bodhisattva who is ill?" Vimalakīrti said: "He should speak of the body's impermanence, but not urge revulsion toward the body; he should speak of the body's suffering, but not urge delight in nirvāṇa; he should speak of the body's lack of self, but urge the guiding and teaching of beings; he should speak of the body's emptiness and stillness, but not urge ultimate extinction; he should urge repentance for past transgressions, but not urge entering into the past itself; he should use his own illness to feel compassion for the illness of others; he should recall the suffering endured across countless past aeons, and keep in mind the benefiting of every being; he should remember the merit he has cultivated, reflect on a pure livelihood, and not give rise to worry or distress, but constantly arouse diligence; he should resolve to become a king of physicians, healing every kind of sickness. A bodhisattva should comfort a sick bodhisattva in this way, so as to bring him joy."

Reading note — after Sengzhao

The four-elements question deliberately echoes Aniruddha's dilemma over the divine eye in Part 4 — is this a constructed thing or not — and gets the same kind of answer: neither identical to any single element nor separable from all of them, since, as Sengzhao puts it, "the four elements have, by their own nature, no affliction in them at all; it is only when conditions gather and their proportions throw each other off balance that affliction arises from that." The illness is real in its effects without being locatable as a fixed property of any one thing, which is the same structure the whole conversation has been building since the room's emptiness.

The list of how to comfort a sick bodhisattva is built entirely on a single recurring shape — teach the hard truth, but not the conclusion a lesser vehicle would draw from it — and Sengzhao's summary names exactly what that shape accomplishes: speak of impermanence without pushing toward revulsion, speak of suffering without pushing toward craving for nirvāṇa, speak of no-self without abandoning the work of teaching others, speak of emptiness without pushing toward final extinction. Each pairing heads off a different way a partial truth, taken as the whole truth, would tip a struggling bodhisattva either back toward ordinary clinging or sideways into a śrāvaka's premature exit from the world. The list's final image — becoming "a king of physicians, healing every kind of sickness" — closes the loop begun by Vimalakīrti's own manifest illness at the start of this sūtra: comforting a sick bodhisattva properly means redirecting his attention from his own cure toward everyone else's, which is exactly the redirection Vimalakīrti has just performed on himself in the previous section.

19. How a Sick Bodhisattva Subdues His Own Mind

Chinese Source

文殊師利言：「居士！有疾菩薩云何調伏其心？」維摩詰言：「有疾菩薩應作是念：『今我此病，皆從前世妄想顛倒、諸煩惱生，無有實法，誰受病者？所以者何？四大合故，假名為身；四大無主，身亦無我。又此病起，皆由著我，是故於我不應生著。』既知病本，即除我想及眾生想。當起法想，應作是念：『但以眾法合成此身；起唯法起，滅唯法滅；又此法者各不相知，起時不言我起，滅時不言我滅。』彼有疾菩薩為滅法想，當作是念：『此法想者，亦是顛倒，顛倒者是即大患，我應離之。』云何為離？離我、我所。云何離我、我所？謂離二法。云何離二法？謂不念內外，諸法行於平等。云何平等？謂我等、涅槃等。所以者何？我及涅槃，此二皆空。以何為空？但以名字故空。如此二法，無決定性。得是平等，無有餘病，唯有空病；空病亦空。是有疾菩薩以無所受而受諸受，未具佛法，亦不滅受而取證也。「設身有苦，念惡趣眾生，起大悲心。我既調伏，亦當調伏一切眾生；但除其病，而不除法，為斷病本而教導之。何謂病本？謂有攀緣，從有攀緣，則為病本。何所攀緣？謂之三界。云何斷攀緣？以無所得，若無所得，則無攀緣。何謂無所得？謂離二見——何謂二見？謂內見、外見——是無所得。文殊師利！是為有疾菩薩調伏其心，為斷老病死苦，是菩薩菩提。若不如是，己所修治，為無慧利。譬如勝怨，乃可為勇。如是兼除老病死者，菩薩之謂也。「彼有疾菩薩應復作是念：『如我此病，非真非有，眾生病亦非真非有。』作是觀時，於諸眾生若起愛見大悲，即應捨離。所以者何？菩薩斷除客塵煩惱而起大悲，愛見悲者，則於生死有疲厭心；若能離此，無有疲厭，在在所生，不為愛見之所覆也。所生無縛，能為眾生說法解縛，如佛所說：『若自有縛能解彼縛，無有是處；若自無縛能解彼縛，斯有是處。』是故菩薩不應起縛。何謂縛？何謂解？貪著禪味，是菩薩縛；以方便生，是菩薩解。又無方便慧縛，有方便慧解；無慧方便縛，有慧方便解。何謂無方便慧縛？謂菩薩以愛見心莊嚴佛土、成就眾生；於空、無相、無作法中，而自調伏，是名無方便慧縛。何謂有方便慧解？謂不以愛見心莊嚴佛土、成就眾生，於空、無相、無作法中，以自調伏而不疲厭，是名有方便慧解。何謂無慧方便縛？謂菩薩住貪欲、瞋恚、邪見等諸煩惱，而植眾德本，是名無慧方便縛。何謂有慧方便解？謂離諸貪欲、瞋恚、邪見等諸煩惱，而植眾德本，迴向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是名有慧方便解。文殊師利！彼有疾菩薩，應如是觀諸法。又復觀身無常、苦、空、非我，是名為慧，雖身有疾，常在生死，饒益一切，而不厭倦，是名方便；又復觀身，身不離病，病不離身，是病是身，非新非故，是名為慧，設身有疾，而不永滅，是名方便。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Mañjuśrī said: "Householder, how should a sick bodhisattva subdue his own mind?" Vimalakīrti said: "A sick bodhisattva should think to himself: 'This illness of mine now arises entirely from the false thoughts, inverted views, and afflictions of former lives — there is no real thing in it, so who is it that suffers this illness? And why? Because the four elements come together and are provisionally called a body; the four elements have no master, and so the body, too, has no self. And this illness arises entirely from attachment to a self — therefore I should give rise to no such attachment to a self.' Once he knows the root of his illness, he should at once cast off the notion of a self and the notion of other beings, and give rise instead to the notion of dharmas alone. He should think: 'This body is nothing but various dharmas come together; whatever arises is only dharmas arising, and whatever ceases is only dharmas ceasing; and moreover these dharmas do not know one another at all — when they arise, they do not say, "I am arising"; when they cease, they do not say, "I am ceasing."' That sick bodhisattva, wishing to extinguish even this notion of dharmas, should then think: 'This very notion of dharmas is itself, too, an inverted view, and an inverted view is itself a great affliction — I should depart from it as well.' How is this departure accomplished? By departing from self and from what belongs to a self. How does one depart from self and from what belongs to a self? By departing from duality. How does one depart from duality? By giving no thought to what is internal or external, and practicing equanimity toward all things. What is equanimity? It means that self and nirvāṇa are equal. And why? Because both self and nirvāṇa are empty. Empty by virtue of what? They are empty simply because they are names. Neither of these two things has any fixed, determinate nature. Once this equanimity is attained, there is no illness left at all — except the illness of emptiness itself; and even the illness of emptiness is itself empty. This is how a sick bodhisattva receives every feeling through having nothing that he receives; and, not yet having fully perfected the Buddha's own qualities, he does not extinguish feeling altogether merely to attain some private realization of his own. If his body suffers, he should think of beings in the lower destinies, and give rise to a mind of great compassion. Having tamed himself in this way, he should also work to tame every other being; he should remove only the illness, and not the dharmas themselves, teaching and guiding others by cutting off the root of the illness. What is this root of the illness? It is having something to cling to — and it is from having something to cling to that the root of illness comes. And what is it that is clung to? The three realms. How is this clinging cut off? By having nothing to attain; and if there is nothing to attain, there is no more clinging. What is meant by having nothing to attain? It means departing from the two views — and what are these two views? The view of what is internal, and the view of what is external — this is what having nothing to attain means. Mañjuśrī, this is how a sick bodhisattva subdues his own mind. To cut off the suffering of old age, sickness, and death in this way is the bodhisattva's awakening. If he does not do so, then everything he has cultivated is without real benefit or wisdom. It is like conquering an enemy — only then can one be called brave; just so, one who overcomes old age, sickness, and death together in this way is what is truly meant by a bodhisattva. That sick bodhisattva should further think: 'Just as this illness of mine is neither real nor truly existent, the illness of every being, too, is neither real nor truly existent.' Yet when he makes this observation, if he finds that a compassion tainted by love and by fixed views has arisen in him toward beings, he should let it go at once. And why? Because a bodhisattva who has cut off the guest-dust of affliction is the one who gives rise to true great compassion; a compassion tainted by love and by fixed views still carries, toward birth-and-death, a mind of weariness and aversion. If he can let this go, he will have no such weariness, and wherever he is reborn, he will not be covered over by love or by fixed views. Being unbound wherever he is born, he is able to teach the Dharma to beings and free them from their own bondage — as the Buddha himself has said: 'It is not possible for one who is himself bound to free another from their bondage; it is possible only for one who is himself unbound to free another from theirs.' Therefore a bodhisattva should not give rise to bondage in the first place. What is bondage, and what is liberation? Attachment to the flavor of meditative absorption is a bodhisattva's bondage; being reborn through skillful means is a bodhisattva's liberation. Further: to lack skillful means and have only wisdom is bondage; to have skillful means together with wisdom is liberation. To lack wisdom and have only skillful means is bondage; to have wisdom together with skillful means is liberation. What is meant by bondage through lacking skillful means and having only wisdom? It means that a bodhisattva adorns a buddha-land and brings beings to accomplishment with a mind still tainted by love and by fixed views, taming himself only within the teachings of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness — this is called bondage through lacking skillful means though having wisdom. What is meant by liberation through having skillful means together with wisdom? It means that a bodhisattva adorns a buddha-land and brings beings to accomplishment without a mind tainted by love or by fixed views, taming himself within the teachings of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness without ever growing weary of it — this is called liberation through having skillful means together with wisdom. What is meant by bondage through lacking wisdom though having skillful means? It means that a bodhisattva, while still dwelling in greed, anger, wrong views, and every other affliction, plants roots of every virtue — this is called bondage through lacking wisdom though having skillful means. What is meant by liberation through having wisdom together with skillful means? It means that a bodhisattva, having departed from greed, anger, wrong views, and every other affliction, plants roots of every virtue, and dedicates them toward unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening — this is called liberation through having wisdom together with skillful means. Mañjuśrī, a sick bodhisattva should observe every phenomenon in this way. He should also observe that the body is impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self — this is called wisdom; yet though his body is ill, he remains always within birth-and-death, benefiting everyone without ever growing weary of it — this is called skillful means. He should also observe that the body cannot be separated from illness, nor illness from the body, and that this illness and this body are neither new nor old — this is called wisdom; yet though his body is ill, he does not seek final extinction — this is called skillful means."

Reading note — after Kumārajīva, Sengzhao, and Zhu Daosheng

This teaching moves in a deliberate sequence Sengzhao and Daosheng both trace step by step: first the notion of a self receiving illness is dismantled (there is no fixed "I" for the sickness to belong to), then the substitute notion of impersonal "dharmas" arising and ceasing is offered as a corrective — only for that corrective itself to be named, one stage later, as still an inverted view in need of its own release. Kumārajīva reads this staged self-undermining as intentional pedagogy: "one falsehood is used to displace another, the way a person nurses one anger to extinguish a different one" — the teaching does not claim its intermediate steps are final truths, only tools scaled to where the listener currently stands, discarded once they have done their work. This is worth having explicitly in mind, since it is easy to mistake the "notion of dharmas" stage for the sūtra's actual conclusion when it is, by its own account, only a stepping-stone.

The bondage-and-liberation section that follows makes a claim worth stating plainly, since it cuts against a common oversimplification of this sūtra's teaching: wisdom alone, without skillful means, is explicitly named as a form of bondage, not liberation — a bodhisattva who withdraws into emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness while still privately clinging to the beings and lands he means to help remains bound by that very clinging, however correct his metaphysics. Skillful means alone, without wisdom, is equally named as bondage — planting roots of virtue while still caught in greed, anger, and wrong view. Liberation requires both together, and the passage's own definition of "liberation through skillful means" is unusually concrete: adorning a buddha-land and helping beings "without a mind tainted by love or by fixed views... without ever growing weary of it" — endurance without either grasping or burnout. The chapter's closing pair of observations — body and illness as inseparable, "neither new nor old" — brings the entire long argument back down to something almost plain: wisdom sees clearly that suffering cannot be located and removed like a foreign object, and skillful means is simply staying present in that suffering anyway, without either denying it or being destroyed by it.

20. What a Bodhisattva's Conduct Actually Is

Chinese Source

「文殊師利！有疾菩薩應如是調伏其心，不住其中，亦復不住不調伏心。所以者何？若住不調伏心，是愚人法；若住調伏心，是聲聞法。是故菩薩不當住於調伏、不調伏心，離此二法，是菩薩行。在於生死，不為污行；住於涅槃，不永滅度，是菩薩行；非凡夫行，非賢聖行，是菩薩行；非垢行，非淨行，是菩薩行；雖過魔行，而現降眾魔，是菩薩行；求一切智，無非時求，是菩薩行；雖觀諸法不生，而不入正位，是菩薩行；雖觀十二緣起，而入諸邪見，是菩薩行；雖攝一切眾生，而不愛著，是菩薩行；雖樂遠離，而不依身心盡，是菩薩行；雖行三界，而不壞法性，是菩薩行；雖行於空，而植眾德本，是菩薩行；雖行無相，而度眾生，是菩薩行；雖行無作，而現受身，是菩薩行；雖行無起，而起一切善行，是菩薩行；雖行六波羅蜜，而遍知眾生心、心數法，是菩薩行；雖行六通，而不盡漏，是菩薩行；雖行四無量心，而不貪著生於梵世，是菩薩行；雖行禪定解脫三昧，而不隨禪生，是菩薩行；雖行四念處，而不永離身、受、心、法，是菩薩行；雖行四正勤，而不捨身心精進，是菩薩行；雖行四如意足，而得自在神通，是菩薩行；雖行五根，而分別眾生諸根利鈍，是菩薩行；雖行五力，而樂求佛十力，是菩薩行；雖行七覺分，而分別佛之智慧，是菩薩行；雖行八聖道，而樂行無量佛道，是菩薩行；雖行止觀助道之法，而不畢竟墮於寂滅，是菩薩行；雖行諸法不生不滅，而以相好莊嚴其身，是菩薩行；雖現聲聞、辟支佛威儀，而不捨佛法，是菩薩行；雖隨諸法究竟淨相，而隨所應為現其身，是菩薩行；雖觀諸佛國土永寂如空，而現種種清淨佛土，是菩薩行；雖得佛道轉于法輪、入於涅槃，而不捨於菩薩之道，是菩薩行。」說是語時，文殊師利所將大眾其中八千天子，皆發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Mañjuśrī, a sick bodhisattva should subdue his own mind in this way — not abiding within it, and yet also not abiding in a mind that is unsubdued. And why? Because to abide in an unsubdued mind is the way of a fool, while to abide in a subdued mind is the way of a śrāvaka. For this reason a bodhisattva should not abide in either a subdued or an unsubdued mind — departing from both of these is a bodhisattva's conduct. To remain within birth-and-death without engaging in defiled action, and to abide in nirvāṇa without seeking final extinction, is a bodhisattva's conduct; it is neither the conduct of an ordinary person nor the conduct of a noble sage — this is a bodhisattva's conduct; it is neither defiled conduct nor purified conduct — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he has gone beyond the conduct of Māra, he manifests the subduing of every demon — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. He seeks all-knowing wisdom, yet never seeks it before its proper time — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he observes that no phenomenon truly arises, he does not settle into the correct station of that realization — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he observes the twelve links of dependent origination, he enters into every kind of wrong view — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he gathers in every being, he is not attached to any of them — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he delights in withdrawal from the world, he does not rely on the body and mind being utterly extinguished — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he moves through the three realms, he does not damage the nature of phenomena — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices emptiness, he plants every root of virtue — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices signlessness, he still delivers beings — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices wishlessness, he still manifests the taking of a body — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices non-arising, he still gives rise to every wholesome action — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the six perfections, he fully knows the minds and mental states of every being — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the six spiritual powers, he does not exhaust his own defilements — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the four immeasurable minds, he is not attached to being reborn in the Brahmā heavens — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices meditative concentration, liberation, and samādhi, he is not reborn as a consequence of that meditation — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the four foundations of mindfulness, he does not permanently withdraw from body, feeling, mind, and phenomena — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the four right exertions, he does not abandon diligence of body and mind — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the four bases of spiritual power, he attains spiritual powers with complete freedom — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the five faculties, he discerns the sharpness and dullness of every being's faculties — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the five powers, he delights in seeking the ten powers of a buddha — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the seven factors of awakening, he discerns the wisdom of a buddha — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices the eightfold noble path, he delights in practicing the immeasurable path of the buddhas — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he practices calm-abiding and insight, the practices that assist the path, he does not fall ultimately into mere stillness — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he observes that every phenomenon neither arises nor ceases, he still adorns his own body with the major and minor marks — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he manifests the deportment of a śrāvaka or a pratyekabuddha, he does not abandon the Buddha's own Dharma — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he follows the ultimately pure mark of every phenomenon, he still manifests his own body according to what is needed — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he observes that every buddha-land is eternally still, like empty space, he still manifests every kind of pure buddha-land — this is a bodhisattva's conduct. Though he attains the buddha-path, turns the wheel of the Dharma, and enters nirvāṇa, he never abandons the bodhisattva path — this is a bodhisattva's conduct." When these words were spoken, eight thousand of the young gods in Mañjuśrī's own retinue gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening.

Reading note — after Sengzhao

This closing catalogue — some thirty clauses, each shaped "though he practices X, he still does Y" — gives the whole chapter's teaching its final, most concrete form. Sengzhao's framing sentence for it is direct: to settle into an unsubdued mind is a fool's path, but to settle into a subdued mind is only a śrāvaka's path — genuine bodhisattva conduct is defined by refusing to rest in either. Read this way, the list is not thirty separate virtues but one structural pattern applied thirty times: take a legitimate, standard Buddhist practice — the four foundations of mindfulness, the eightfold path, the six perfections, meditative absorption itself — and pair it with the one thing that practice, taken to its natural conclusion, would normally lead a practitioner to give up (withdrawal from the world, cessation of engagement, permanent detachment), and then simply refuse that trade. A bodhisattva practices emptiness without abandoning the beings that emptiness might otherwise seem to render pointless to help; practices meditative absorption without letting it determine where he is reborn; observes that every buddha-land is ultimately as still as empty space, and manifests pure buddha-lands anyway.

It is worth reading this list as this chapter's answer to a question that has been implicit since Vimalakīrti first said "because all beings are sick, I am sick": if a bodhisattva has genuinely realized emptiness, non-arising, and the equality of self and nirvāṇa, what could possibly motivate him to keep acting in the world at all? The list's answer is that every one of the ordinary spiritual accomplishments a practitioner might use to justify quietly stepping back — deep meditation, correct metaphysical insight, mastery of the path's technical stages — is, for a bodhisattva, deliberately not permitted to serve that function. Eight thousand of Mañjuśrī's own retinue give rise to the awakening-mind at the close of this teaching, which this chapter offers as its own answer to whether the argument actually lands.
