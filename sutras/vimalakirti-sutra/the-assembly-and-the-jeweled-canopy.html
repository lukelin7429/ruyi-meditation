The Assembly and the Jeweled Canopy

佛國品第一

An assembly of eight thousand monks and thirty-two thousand bodhisattvas at Āmrapālī's Grove; a young man named Ratnākara whose offering of seven-jeweled parasols becomes a single canopy revealing the entire universe; and the Buddha's teaching that a bodhisattva's pure land is nothing other than a pure mind.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T475 · Kumārajīva's translation, early 5th century CE Commentary guide: Sengzhao's 注維摩詰經 (early 5th c.)
This part covers
The opening assembly at Āmrapālī's Grove; the thirty-two virtues shared by the bodhisattvas present, and the roll of their names; Ratnākara's seven-jeweled parasols merging into a single canopy that reveals the whole universe; his verse of praise; the Buddha's teaching that a bodhisattva's pure land arises from a pure mind; and Śāriputra's doubt, resolved when the Buddha presses his toe to the earth.
Setting
Āmrapālī's Grove (菴羅樹園) outside Vaiśālī — the same city where the layman Vimalakīrti, not yet introduced, lies ill

1. The Assembly, and the Bodhisattvas' Virtues

Chinese Source

如是我聞：一時，佛在毘耶離菴羅樹園，與大比丘眾八千人俱，菩薩三萬二千——眾所知識，大智本行，皆悉成就；諸佛威神之所建立，為護法城，受持正法；能師子吼，名聞十方；眾人不請，友而安之；紹隆三寶，能使不絕；降伏魔怨，制諸外道，悉已清淨，永離蓋纏；心常安住，無礙解脫；念、定、總持、辯才不斷；布施、持戒、忍辱、精進、禪定、智慧及方便力，無不具足；逮無所得不起法忍；已能隨順，轉不退輪；善解法相，知眾生根；蓋諸大眾得無所畏、功德智慧，以修其心；相好嚴身，色像第一，捨諸世間所有飾好；名稱高遠，踰於須彌；深信堅固，猶若金剛；法寶普照，而雨甘露；於眾言音，微妙第一；深入緣起，斷諸邪見，有無二邊，無復餘習；演法無畏，猶師子吼，其所講說，乃如雷震，無有量，已過量；集眾法寶，如海導師，了達諸法深妙之義；善知眾生往來所趣及心所行；近無等等佛自在慧、十力、無畏、十八不共；關閉一切諸惡趣門，而生五道以現其身；為大醫王，善療眾病，應病與藥，令得服行；無量功德皆成就，無量佛土皆嚴淨；其見聞者，無不蒙益，諸有所作，亦不唐捐：如是一切功德，皆悉具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard. At one time the Buddha was staying at Āmrapālī's Grove in Vaiśālī, together with a great assembly of eight thousand monks and thirty-two thousand bodhisattvas — all of them known and esteemed by everyone, their great wisdom and its foundational practice fully accomplished; established by the majestic power of the buddhas, they guarded the city of the Dharma and upheld the true teaching; able to roar the lion's roar, their renown reached the ten directions; unasked, they befriended and set people at ease; they continued and made the Three Jewels flourish, never letting them lapse; they subdued Māra's enmity and mastered every non-Buddhist path, already purified and forever free of every hindrance and entanglement; their minds abided constantly, unobstructed and liberated; their mindfulness, concentration, retention, and eloquence never broke off; giving, discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, wisdom, and skillful means — in all of these they lacked nothing; they had attained the acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena, in which nothing is grasped and nothing arises; already able to follow along, they turned the wheel that never turns back; skilled in understanding the marks of every phenomenon, they knew the faculties of every being; surpassing every assembly, they had gained fearlessness, and cultivated their minds with merit and wisdom; adorned in body with the marks and signs, foremost in form, they had cast off every worldly ornament; their renown, lofty and far-reaching, rose higher than Sumeru; their deep faith, firm and unshakable, was like vajra itself; the treasure of the Dharma shone from them everywhere, and rained down sweet dew; among every kind of speech, theirs was subtle and supreme; deeply entering dependent origination, they had cut off every wrong view, and of the two extremes of being and non-being not a trace remained; they expounded the Dharma fearlessly, like the lion's roar, and what they taught resounded like thunder, beyond all measure, and already past all measure; they gathered the treasures of the Dharma like a great captain guiding a ship at sea, and had penetrated the profound and subtle meaning of every phenomenon; they knew well where every being comes from and goes to, and what moves in their minds; they had drawn near to the buddhas' own unequaled, equal wisdom, the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, and the eighteen exclusive qualities; they had shut every gate to the wretched destinies, while manifesting their own bodies within the five paths; as great kings of physicians, skilled at healing every illness, they gave the medicine that fit the disease, and brought beings to take it; their immeasurable merits were every one accomplished, their immeasurable buddha-lands every one adorned and pure; whoever saw or heard them was, without exception, benefited, and nothing they did was ever done in vain — every one of these merits was fully complete in them.

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Eight thousand monks are named before thirty-two thousand bodhisattvas, and Sengzhao pauses on why: ordinary intuition might expect the reverse, since bodhisattvas outrank arhats in the Mahāyāna's own account of the path. His answer is about audience expectation, not actual rank — people generally suppose an arhat's defilements are not yet fully exhausted and a bodhisattva's wisdom is already vast, so the sūtra lists in the order its listeners would find natural, deferring to "what people's sentiment inclines them to place first," rather than asserting a doctrinal hierarchy. It is a small housekeeping note, but worth having before the sūtra's real business starts, since this text will spend its next several chapters making exactly the opposite point at length — a series of the Buddha's most senior arhat disciples, one after another, unable to hold their own against a single laymen's questioning.

2. The Names of the Bodhisattvas, and the Rest of the Assembly

Chinese Source

其名曰：等觀菩薩、不等觀菩薩、等不等觀菩薩、定自在王菩薩、法自在王菩薩、法相菩薩、光相菩薩、光嚴菩薩、大嚴菩薩、寶積菩薩、辯積菩薩、寶手菩薩、寶印手菩薩、常舉手菩薩、常下手菩薩、常慘菩薩、喜根菩薩、喜王菩薩、辯音菩薩、虛空藏菩薩、執寶炬菩薩、寶勇菩薩、寶見菩薩、帝網菩薩、明網菩薩、無緣觀菩薩、慧積菩薩、寶勝菩薩、天王菩薩、壞魔菩薩、電德菩薩、自在王菩薩、功德相嚴菩薩、師子吼菩薩、雷音菩薩、山相擊音菩薩、香象菩薩、白香象菩薩、常精進菩薩、不休息菩薩、妙生菩薩、華嚴菩薩、觀世音菩薩、得大勢菩薩、梵網菩薩、寶杖菩薩、無勝菩薩、嚴土菩薩、金髻菩薩、珠髻菩薩、彌勒菩薩、文殊師利法王子菩薩，如是等三萬二千人。復有萬梵天王尸棄等，從餘四天下來詣佛所而聽法；復有萬二千天帝，亦從餘四天下來在會坐；并餘大威力諸天、龍神、夜叉、乾闥婆、阿脩羅、迦樓羅、緊那羅、摩睺羅伽等，悉來會坐；諸比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷，俱來會坐。彼時佛與無量百千之眾，恭敬圍繞而為說法，譬如須彌山王顯于大海，安處眾寶師子之座，蔽於一切諸來大眾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Their names were: Equal Contemplation, Unequal Contemplation, Equal-and-Unequal Contemplation, Sovereign King of Meditative Mastery, Sovereign King of the Dharma, Dharma-Marks, Light-Marks, Light-Adorned, Greatly Adorned, Ratnākara ("Jewel-Store"), Eloquence-Store, Jewel-Hand, Jewel-Seal Hand, Ever-Raised Hand, Ever-Lowered Hand, Ever Sorrowing, Root of Joy, King of Joy, Eloquent Voice, Ākāśagarbha ("Space-Treasury"), Holder of the Jeweled Torch, Jewel Valor, Jewel Vision, Indra's Net, Net of Light, Contemplation Without Object, Wisdom-Store, Jewel Supreme, Heavenly King, Māra-Destroyer, Lightning Virtue, Sovereign King, Merit-Marked Adornment, Lion's Roar, Thunder Voice, Mountain-Clash Sound, Fragrant Elephant, White Fragrant Elephant, Ever Diligent, Never Resting, Wondrous Birth, Flower Adornment, Avalokiteśvara, Mahāsthāmaprāpta, Brahmā's Net, Jeweled Staff, Unsurpassed, Land-Adorning, Golden Crest, Pearl Crest, Maitreya, and Mañjuśrī, Prince of the Dharma — thirty-two thousand such beings in all. There were also ten thousand Brahmā kings, Śikhin foremost among them, who came from other four-continent worlds to the Buddha's place to hear the Dharma; and twelve thousand celestial rulers, who likewise came from other four-continent worlds to sit in the assembly; together with every other deva of great majestic power, dragon spirits, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kiṃnaras, and mahoragas, all of whom came to sit in the assembly; and monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen, who all came to sit as well. At that time the Buddha, surrounded reverently by an assembly of immeasurable hundreds of thousands, taught the Dharma for them — like Sumeru, sovereign of mountains, rising above the great ocean, he sat at ease upon a lion-seat adorned with every treasure, outshining the whole gathered assembly.

Reading note — after Sengzhao and Kumārajīva

Following the same convention as this site's other sūtra translations, these thirty-two names are rendered by their plain meaning rather than by reconstructed Sanskrit the tradition itself has never settled on — Kumārajīva's own commentary glosses most of them one at a time, name by name (寶手, "Jewel-Hand," because his hand can produce treasure without limit; 常下手, "Ever-Lowered Hand," because his hand stays lowered as a sign of compassion that does not tower over anyone). A handful are kept in Sanskrit because they are figures independently known elsewhere in the canon: Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta, familiar from the Amitābha and Immeasurable Life sūtras on this site; Ākāśagarbha, "Space-Treasury"; and, closing the list, Maitreya and Mañjuśrī — the two bodhisattvas who will shortly be asked, in turn, to go and inquire after Vimalakīrti's illness, and who both, in their own ways, decline.

Sengzhao's note on the closing simile is brief but exact: Sumeru is the mountain of vajra where Indra dwells, rising three hundred and thirty-six thousand li above the ocean's surface — and the Buddha, seated among this fourfold assembly, "outshines the whole gathered assembly the way a golden mountain shows itself above the dark sea." The image recurs elsewhere in this site's sūtra translations for the same reason it recurs here: it is the tradition's standard figure for a presence that towers not by force but simply by being what it is.

3. Ratnākara's Jeweled Canopy

Chinese Source

爾時毘耶離城有長者子，名曰寶積，與五百長者子，俱持七寶蓋來詣佛所，頭面禮足，各以其蓋共供養佛。佛之威神，令諸寶蓋合成一蓋，遍覆三千大千世界，而此世界廣長之相悉於中現；又此三千大千世界諸須彌山、雪山、目真隣陀山、摩訶目真隣陀山、香山、寶山、金山、黑山、鐵圍山、大鐵圍山，大海江河，川流泉源及日月星辰、天宮、龍宮、諸尊神宮，悉現於寶蓋中；又十方諸佛、諸佛說法，亦現於寶蓋中。爾時一切大眾，覩佛神力，歎未曾有！合掌禮佛，瞻仰尊顏，目不暫捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time there was an elder's son in the city of Vaiśālī named Ratnākara, who together with five hundred other elders' sons carried jeweled parasols of the seven treasures to where the Buddha was, bowed their heads to his feet, and each offered his parasol in homage to the Buddha. By the Buddha's majestic power, all these jeweled parasols merged into a single canopy, covering the great trichiliocosm entirely, and the whole broad expanse of this world appeared within it — moreover, every Mount Sumeru of this great trichiliocosm, every Himalaya, every Mucilinda and Mahāmucilinda mountain, every Fragrant Mountain, Jeweled Mountain, Golden Mountain, Black Mountain, Cakravāḍa and Mahācakravāḍa mountain, the great oceans, rivers, streams and springs, the sun, moon, and stars, the palaces of the devas, the palaces of the dragon-kings, and the palaces of every honored spirit — all of these appeared within the jeweled canopy; and moreover the buddhas of the ten directions, and the buddhas' teaching of the Dharma, likewise appeared within the jeweled canopy. At that time the whole assembly, beholding the Buddha's spiritual power, praised it as something never before seen! They joined their palms in homage to the Buddha, gazing up at his honored countenance without turning their eyes away for a moment.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva and Sengzhao

Kumārajīva reads this miracle as doing two things at once: displaying an immeasurable spiritual transformation to make plain how deep the Buddha's wisdom runs, and rewarding Ratnākara's small offering — a handful of parasols — with a fruit vastly out of proportion to it, "a small cause and a great result," so that what is shown here also stands as a promise about what these five hundred young men's own future buddha-lands might become. Sengzhao adds the narrower, practical reason the ten directions are shown at all: these are young men who have already given rise to the aspiration for awakening but have not yet begun cultivating a land of their own, and the vision is meant to plant that seed — the whole rest of this chapter, the Buddha's teaching on what makes a buddha-field pure, exists because of what Ratnākara is about to see and ask about here.

4. Ratnākara's Verse of Praise

Chinese Source

目淨脩廣如青蓮，　心淨已度諸禪定， 久積淨業稱無量，　導眾以寂故稽首。 既見大聖以神變，　普現十方無量土， 其中諸佛演說法，　於是一切悉見聞。 法王法力超群生，　常以法財施一切， 能善分別諸法相，　於第一義而不動， 已於諸法得自在，　是故稽首此法王。 說法不有亦不無，　以因緣故諸法生， 無我無造無受者，　善惡之業亦不亡。 始在佛樹力降魔，　得甘露滅覺道成， 已無心意無受行，　而悉摧伏諸外道。 三轉法輪於大千，　其輪本來常清淨， 天人得道此為證，　三寶於是現世間。 以斯妙法濟群生，　一受不退常寂然， 度老病死大醫王，　當禮法海德無邊。 毀譽不動如須彌，　於善不善等以慈， 心行平等如虛空，　孰聞人寶不敬承？ 今奉世尊此微蓋，　於中現我三千界， 諸天龍神所居宮，　乾闥婆等及夜叉。 悉見世間諸所有，　十力哀現是化變， 眾覩希有皆歎佛，　今我稽首三界尊。 大聖法王眾所歸，　淨心觀佛靡不欣， 各見世尊在其前，　斯則神力不共法； 佛以一音演說法，　眾生隨類各得解， 皆謂世尊同其語，　斯則神力不共法； 佛以一音演說法，　眾生各各隨所解， 普得受行獲其利，　斯則神力不共法； 佛以一音演說法，　或有恐畏或歡喜， 或生厭離或斷疑，　斯則神力不共法。 稽首十力大精進，　稽首已得無所畏， 稽首住於不共法，　稽首一切大導師， 稽首能斷眾結縛，　稽首已到於彼岸， 稽首能度諸世間，　稽首永離生死道。 悉知眾生來去相，　善於諸法得解脫， 不著世間如蓮華，　常善入於空寂行， 達諸法相無罣礙，　稽首如空無所依。

Ru-Yi Translation

Your eyes, pure, long and wide, like the blue lotus, your mind, pure, has gone beyond every meditative absorption; long accumulating pure karma, called immeasurable — you guide the multitude with stillness, and so I bow my head. Having seen the great sage's spiritual transformation, displaying fully the immeasurable lands of the ten directions, with the buddhas in them expounding the Dharma — all of this, every one of us has seen and heard. The Dharma-king's power in the Dharma surpasses every being; he ever gives the wealth of the Dharma to all; skilled in discerning the marks of every phenomenon, he is unmoved in the ultimate truth; already sovereign and free in every phenomenon — and so I bow my head to this Dharma-king. He teaches the Dharma as neither existent nor non-existent; because of causes and conditions, every phenomenon arises; there is no self, no maker, no one who receives — yet the karma of good and evil is never lost. At first, beneath the bodhi tree, by his power he subdued Māra, attained the sweet dew of cessation, and the way of awakening was accomplished; already without mind, without mental activity, without grasping conduct, he utterly overcame every non-Buddhist path. Three times he turned the wheel of the Dharma through the great thousand worlds; that wheel, from the very beginning, is forever pure; devas and humans attaining the path — this is the proof of it; the Three Jewels thereupon appeared in the world. By this wondrous Dharma he ferries every being across; once received, it never turns back, forever still; he delivers us from old age, sickness, and death, this great king of physicians — I should bow to this ocean of the Dharma, whose virtue has no bound. Unmoved by praise or blame, like Sumeru, he meets the good and the not-good alike with loving-kindness; his mind's conduct is equal, like empty space — who, hearing of this jewel among people, would not revere and receive him? Now I offer the World-Honored One this small canopy; within it my own three-thousand-fold world appears, the palaces where every deva, dragon, and spirit dwells, the gandharvas, and the yakṣas too. Everything that exists in this world is seen entirely; you of the ten powers, out of compassion, show this transformation; the assembly, beholding this rare thing, all praise the Buddha — now I bow my head to the honored one of the three realms. The great sage, the Dharma-king, to whom the multitude returns — with pure minds beholding the Buddha, none fail to rejoice; each one sees the World-Honored One right before them — this is a spiritual power shared with no other. The Buddha, with a single voice, expounds the Dharma, and beings, each according to their kind, each gain understanding, each one saying the World-Honored One speaks their own language — this is a spiritual power shared with no other. The Buddha, with a single voice, expounds the Dharma, and beings, each and every one, according to their own understanding, all receive it, practice it, and gain its benefit — this is a spiritual power shared with no other. The Buddha, with a single voice, expounds the Dharma, and some feel fear, and some feel joy, some give rise to weariness and renunciation, some have their doubts cut off — this is a spiritual power shared with no other. I bow my head to the one of the ten powers, of great diligence; I bow my head to the one who has attained fearlessness; I bow my head to the one who abides in the exclusive qualities; I bow my head to the great guide of all; I bow my head to the one able to sever every bond and fetter; I bow my head to the one who has already reached the other shore; I bow my head to the one able to ferry across every world; I bow my head to the one forever free of the path of birth and death. Knowing fully the marks of every being's coming and going, skilled in every phenomenon, he has attained liberation; unattached to the world, like the lotus flower, he ever skillfully enters the practice of emptiness and stillness; penetrating the marks of every phenomenon without obstruction — I bow my head to him, who like space rests on nothing at all.

Reading note — after Sengzhao

Two lines mid-verse carry more doctrinal weight than their brevity suggests. "Skilled in discerning the marks of every phenomenon, / he is unmoved in the ultimate truth" — Sengzhao reads this as the verse's real center of gravity: an ordinary teacher who distinguishes each thing from every other risks losing sight of their shared empty nature, while one who insists on their shared empty nature risks flattening real distinctions into a blur. The Dharma-king does both without contradiction, "discerning difference for those who love difference without betraying their sameness, and discerning sameness for those who love sameness without betraying their difference" — a description, in miniature, of the kind of teaching this entire sūtra is about to demonstrate through Vimalakīrti's own method rather than only assert here.

"Once received, it never turns back, forever still" is a quieter line worth noticing on a second pass: Sengzhao contrasts it with the ninety-six non-Buddhist paths, whose most advanced practitioners can, at best, sever enough affliction to be reborn into the formless heavens — and still eventually fall back into the three lower realms. What the Buddha's four noble truths deliver, by contrast, is a state from which no one who has genuinely attained it regresses. As with the verse of aspiration in this site's Immeasurable Life Sūtra translation, this is devotional poetry rendered here for sense rather than for the strict four-character-line meter of the Chinese; something of the original's chant-like cadence is inevitably lost in that move, and this translation claims to have carried over the content, not the music.

5. What Makes a Buddha-Field Pure

Chinese Source

爾時長者子寶積說此偈已，白佛言：「世尊！是五百長者子，皆已發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心，願聞得佛國土清淨，唯願世尊說諸菩薩淨土之行！」佛言：「善哉！寶積！乃能為諸菩薩，問於如來淨土之行。諦聽，諦聽！善思念之，當為汝說！」於是寶積及五百長者子受教而聽。佛言：「寶積！眾生之類是菩薩佛土。所以者何？菩薩隨所化眾生而取佛土，隨所調伏眾生而取佛土，隨諸眾生應以何國入佛智慧而取佛土，隨諸眾生應以何國起菩薩根而取佛土。所以者何？菩薩取於淨國，皆為饒益諸眾生故。譬如有人，欲於空地造立宮室，隨意無礙；若於虛空，終不能成！菩薩如是，為成就眾生故，願取佛國，願取佛國者，非於空也。

Ru-Yi Translation

When the elder's son Ratnākara had spoken this verse, he said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, these five hundred elders' sons have all given rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening, and wish to hear about the purity of a buddha's land. I ask only that the World-Honored One teach the practice by which bodhisattvas purify their lands." The Buddha said: "Excellent, Ratnākara! You are able to ask the Tathāgata, on behalf of the bodhisattvas, about the practice of purifying a land. Listen carefully, listen carefully, and consider it well — I shall teach it for you." Thereupon Ratnākara and the five hundred elders' sons received this teaching and listened. The Buddha said: "Ratnākara, the very multitude of living beings is a bodhisattva's buddha-field. And why is this? A bodhisattva takes up a buddha-field according to the beings he converts; he takes up a buddha-field according to the beings he tames and subdues; he takes up a buddha-field according to whatever land will lead beings into the Buddha's wisdom; he takes up a buddha-field according to whatever land will give rise to the bodhisattva root in beings. And why is this? Because a bodhisattva takes up a pure land solely to benefit living beings. It is like a person who wishes to build a palace on an empty plot of ground — he may do so freely, without hindrance; but if he tries to build it in empty space, he will never succeed. It is just the same for a bodhisattva: he vows to take up a buddha-field for the sake of bringing beings to accomplishment. To vow to take up a buddha-field is not to build it in emptiness."

"Just so, Ratnākara: as a bodhisattva follows his straightforward mind, he is able to give rise to conduct; as he follows his conduct, he attains a deep mind; as he follows his deep mind, his intention becomes tamed and subdued; as his intention is tamed and subdued, he practices as he teaches; as he practices as he teaches, he is able to dedicate his merit; as he dedicates his merit, he gains skillful means; as he gains skillful means, he brings beings to accomplishment; as he brings beings to accomplishment, his buddha-field becomes pure; as his buddha-field becomes pure, his teaching of the Dharma becomes pure; as his teaching becomes pure, his wisdom becomes pure; as his wisdom becomes pure, his mind becomes pure; as his mind becomes pure, every one of his merits becomes pure. Therefore, Ratnākara, if a bodhisattva wishes to attain a pure land, he should purify his mind; as his mind becomes pure, his buddha-field becomes pure."

Reading note — after Sengzhao

This is the line the whole sūtra is arguably named after in advance — "as the mind becomes pure, the buddha-field becomes pure" — and Sengzhao takes care to head off a reading of it that would flatten the teaching into simple subjectivism. His fullest gloss is worth having close to his own words: "the perfected one is empty and without fixed image; it takes on form only in response to beings. If form has no constant substance, how much less could a land have a fixed nature? Since the deeds and karma of the myriad kinds of beings differ, the response and recompense they bring about are not uniform — thus what is pure responds with jewels, what is defiled responds with sand and gravel. Beauty and ugliness come from the beholder, not from any fixed nature in the land itself; a land without fixed nature is the true land." Note the direction of the claim: it is not that the land is imaginary and the mind is real, but that neither has any fixed, independent nature apart from the causal relationship between them — which is precisely why a change in the cause (a purified mind) can show up as a change in what is seen.

Sengzhao goes further and heads off a specific misreading directly: some might object that if a land's purity depends on the beings perceiving it, then what results is merely each being's own karmic-appearance-land, not the Tathāgata's actual land. His response is that this objection has not yet understood how strangely the relationship between cause and response actually works here — the Buddha's true land takes formlessness itself as its substance (以無方為體), which is exactly why beings of mixed capacities can look at the same thing and see differently; grasping at its purity or impurity is the beings' own karmic response, but the land's being without any fixed, bounded nature is what makes it real. There is no second, hidden "actual" land standing behind the one different viewers see differently — the very fact that it can be seen so differently by different beings is what the text means by the land's own true nature. This passage becomes the doctrinal keystone for what happens next: an argument Śāriputra is about to have with the Buddha without yet grasping this distinction himself.

6. Śāriputra's Doubt, and the Land Made Visible

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Śāriputra, moved by the Buddha's majestic power, thought to himself: "If it is true that when a bodhisattva's mind is pure, his buddha-field is pure, then when our World-Honored One was formerly a bodhisattva, was his mind not pure? Why then is this buddha-field so impure?" The Buddha, knowing his thought, said to him: "What do you think — are the sun and moon not pure? And yet the blind do not see them." He replied: "No, World-Honored One — this is the fault of the blind, not any flaw of the sun and moon." "Śāriputra, it is because of the sins of living beings that they do not see the adorned purity of the Tathāgata's land — it is no flaw of the Tathāgata's. Śāriputra, this land of mine is pure, but you do not see it." At that time the Brahmā king Śikhin said to Śāriputra: "Do not think this way, that this buddha's land is impure. And why? I see the land of Śākyamuni Buddha as pure, like the palace of the Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven." Śāriputra said: "What I see of this land is hills and hollows, thorns and gravel, dirt, rock, and mountains, filled entirely with foulness." The Brahmā king Śikhin said: "It is because your mind, sir, has high and low places in it, and does not rely on the Buddha's wisdom, that you see this land as impure. Śāriputra, a bodhisattva regards all beings entirely with equality; his deep mind is pure, and relying on the Buddha's wisdom, he is able to see this buddha's land as pure." Thereupon the Buddha pressed his toe upon the ground, and at once the whole trichiliocosm appeared adorned with hundreds and thousands of every kind of treasure, like the buddha-land of Ratnavyūha, adorned entirely with immeasurable merit and treasure. The whole assembly praised it as something never seen before, and every one of them saw themselves seated upon a jeweled lotus. The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "Now do you see this buddha-land, adorned and pure?" Śāriputra said: "Yes indeed, World-Honored One! What I had never before seen, never before heard — now this buddha-land, adorned and pure, appears before me entirely." The Buddha said to Śāriputra: "This buddha-land of mine is always pure like this — it is only to bring across those of inferior capacity that I show it as a defiled and impure land. It is like the devas, who all eat from the same jeweled vessel, yet the color of the food differs according to each one's merit. Just so, Śāriputra: if a person's mind is pure, he sees this land's merit and adornment." When the Buddha manifested this land's adornment and purity, the five hundred elders' sons whom Ratnākara had brought all attained the acquiescence to the birthlessness of phenomena, and eighty-four thousand people gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. The Buddha then withdrew his spiritual power, and the world returned to its former state; the thirty-two thousand devas and humans seeking the vehicle of the śrāvakas understood that every conditioned thing is impermanent, became free of dust and stain, and attained the pure Dharma-eye; and eight thousand monks, no longer grasping at any phenomenon, exhausted their defilements and their minds were liberated.

Reading note — after Kumārajīva, Sengzhao, and Daosheng

Śāriputra's question is not staged foolishness — the previous section's teaching genuinely licenses it, and the Buddha's answer does not correct the logic so much as supply the missing premise: if the land's purity or impurity is a function of the beholder rather than a fixed property of the land, then a currently-impure-looking world is compatible with a fully purified mind having caused it, since what a lesser-capacity audience sees is not the whole truth of what is there. Kumārajīva makes the deliberateness of this explicit: "if the Buddha could only ever show a pure land and never conceal it, that would not actually serve beings" — the ordinary, sand-and-gravel appearance of this world is itself a considered act of skillful means, not evidence against the teaching Ratnākara just received.

Zhu Daosheng's note on the demonstration itself is the sharpest point in this section, and easy to read past too quickly: when the Buddha presses his toe to the ground, he is not manufacturing a purity that was previously absent. "This 'observing' is only a matter of borrowing the adornment to make plain that there was never any impurity — in reality, it was already manifest; there is no separate act of purifying the land involved." The gesture does not change the land; it changes what the assembly is capable of seeing of a purity that was, by the previous section's own logic, already fully there. This is also the passage's answer to a modern reader's likely first objection to the whole chapter — that "pure land is pure mind" sounds like it excuses any actual suffering or defilement as someone's failure of perception. The text's own position is narrower and stranger than that: the land was never, in fact, impure; what needed to change was not the world but the eye looking at it, and Śikhin's rebuke to Śāriputra — "your mind has high and low places in it" — locates the defect precisely there, in an uneven, ungenerous regard for other beings, not in some deficiency of the physical world itself.
