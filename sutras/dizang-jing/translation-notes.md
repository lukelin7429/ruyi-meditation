# Sūtra of Kṣitigarbha's Fundamental Vows — Translation Notes

These notes record recurring translation decisions for the Ru-Yi English translation of the *Sūtra of Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva's Fundamental Vows* (地藏菩薩本願經, *Dizang Jing*), Taishō T0412, translated into Chinese by Śikṣānanda of Khotan in the early 8th century. They exist so that vocabulary stays consistent across all six parts, and so that terminology without precedent anywhere else on this site is recorded for any later text to inherit rather than re-coin.

## The text, and its uncertain origins

No Sanskrit or Tibetan version of this sūtra has ever surfaced. Unlike the clearly Indic *Daśacakra Sūtra* (大乘大集地藏十輪經) or the *Sūtra on the Divination of the Effect of Good and Evil Actions* (占察善惡業報經), both also central to Kṣitigarbha devotion, some modern scholars have suspected Chinese authorship for this particular text; the question remains debated rather than settled, closer to the *Awakening of Faith*'s status than to a text with an undisputed Indic pedigree. What is not in doubt is its influence: within a few centuries of its appearance it had become the primary scriptural source for Kṣitigarbha's identity as bodhisattva of the hell-realms, filial devotion, and the vow to empty every hell before accepting buddhahood himself.

The base text runs to roughly 17,000 Chinese characters across thirteen chapters, translated across six parts split at the traditional six-fascicle division of Lingcheng's commentary (see Structure below) rather than at even intervals — chapter length in this sūtra varies by more than fourfold, from the 556-character eleventh chapter to the 2,319-character twelfth.

## Reading-note sourcing

Per this site's commentator-vetting convention, reading notes are grounded in a named commentator rather than presented as unattributed analysis. Lingcheng (靈椉, courtesy name 青蓮苾芻, "the Blue-Lotus monk") was a Kangxi-era monk who held himself to be the fifth-generation heir of the Tiantai teaching-and-contemplation lineage (天台教觀正宗). His commentary, the *Dizang Pusa Benyuan Jing Kezhu* (地藏菩薩本願經科注, X0384), runs to six fascicles and was, by the account given in its prefaces, the first sustained commentary on this sūtra in the roughly one thousand years since its translation — composed, Lingcheng's own colophon states, while he was ill. The prefaces attached to it were written in Japan: a monk named Zhenchang carried the manuscript across the sea to Nagasaki for publication, where it received forewords from the Ōbaku Zen monks Gaoquan Xingdun and Yuefeng Daozhang in 1688. This collection also draws, for background on Lingcheng's overall approach, on his prefatory outline of the sūtra's "five profound meanings" (五重玄義— name, essence, core teaching, function, and doctrinal classification) and on the separate doctrinal-outline text (地藏經科, X0382) that maps his six fascicles onto the sūtra's thirteen chapters; both accompany the commentary proper in the same CBETA source group.

|---|---|---|
| 1 | 忉利天宮神通品第一 (the assembly at Trāyastriṃśa); 分身集會品第二 (one body, countless emanations) | 2,874 characters |
| 2 | 觀眾生業緣品第三 (Māyā's question); 閻浮眾生業感品第四 (two kings; Bright Eyes) | 2,988 characters |
| 3 | 地獄名號品第五 (a roll call of hell); 如來讚歎品第六 (the Buddha's praise) | 2,757 characters |
| 4 | 利益存亡品第七 (what helps the dead); 閻羅王眾讚歎品第八 (King Yama's court); 稱佛名號品第九 (ten buddha names) | 3,442 characters |
| 5 | 校量布施功德緣品第十 (what generosity is worth); 地神護法品第十一 (Dṛḍha) | 1,559 characters |
| 6 | 見聞利益品第十二 (what seeing and hearing are worth); 囑累人天品第十三 (the final entrustment) | 3,344 characters |

Total: roughly 17,000 characters. This six-part split follows Lingcheng's own six-fascicle division of his commentary exactly (卷一 through 卷六, recorded at the head of each fascicle in X0384), which in turn follows the sūtra's own two-chapter and three-chapter groupings rather than an even word count — Part 5, at 1,559 characters, is barely half the length of Part 4 or Part 6, and the sūtra's own text was not reapportioned to smooth this out.

## Core terminology

| Chinese | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 地藏 | Kṣitigarbha | Kept as the standard scholarly Sanskritization (literally "earth-store" or "earth-womb") rather than translated, matching this site's convention of leaving a bodhisattva's proper name untranslated once a settled Sanskrit form exists (cf. Avalokiteśvara, Mañjuśrī) — even though, per the note above, no Sanskrit original of this sūtra itself survives. |
| 本願 | fundamental vows | The sūtra's own chosen title-word among the three names the Buddha offers in Part 3 (本願 / 本行 / 本誓力) — see that part's reading note for Lingcheng's gloss distinguishing all three. Kept singular in running prose ("Kṣitigarbha's fundamental vow") when referring to one specific vow-act, plural in the title itself where the sūtra narrates four distinct vow-tellings. |
| 無間地獄 | the hell without interval | A literal rendering of Avīci's Chinese gloss (無間, "without gap" or "without interval"), used throughout in preference to transliterating "Avīci," since the sūtra itself glosses the name at length in Part 2 via its five defining features — translating the gloss rather than the loanword keeps that etymological argument legible in English. |
| 阿鼵地獄 / 阿鼵 | Avīci | Transliterated (rather than translated as "the hell without interval," reserved for 無間) in the small number of passages, mostly in Part 3's hell roll call, where the sūtra uses this Sanskrit-derived name as a proper noun distinct from, though closely related to, 無間 — see Part 3's reading note on Lingcheng's discussion of whether the two ever named one hell or two. |
| 業道 | the path of karma / karmic path | Kept literal throughout; not softened to "karma" alone, since the sūtra consistently frames karma as a route being walked (業道眾生, "beings on the path of karma") rather than a static possession. |
| 閻浮提 | Jambudvīpa | Kept as the standard Sanskrit loanword for the southern continent of traditional Buddhist cosmology, matching this site's other sūtra pages, rather than translated as "the human world" — the sūtra's own cosmology in Part 2 distinguishes Jambudvīpa from other continents and world-systems explicitly. |
| 婆羅門 | Brahmin | Transliterated as the varṇa/caste term rather than translated, matching standard practice; kept lowercase when used generically, capitalized when referring to the specific unnamed woman of Part 1. |
| 聖女 | the holy daughter | Used for both the Brahmin woman (Part 1) and, by implication, Bright Eyes (Part 2) in the source's own honorific address to them mid-narrative; rendered consistently rather than varied for elegance, since the term itself signals the narrator's own reverence partway through each story, before either woman is identified with Kṣitigarbha. |
| 天龍八部 | the eightfold division of gods and nāgas | Standard rendering for this recurring formula (devas, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kiṃnaras, mahoragas), kept short in running prose per this site's general convention rather than spelled out in full on each occurrence. |
| 三塗 / 三惡道 | the three evil destinies | Used interchangeably per the source's own interchangeable usage — hell, the hungry-ghost realm, and the animal realm — rather than distinguished by two different English phrases that would suggest a distinction the Chinese does not make. |
| 五辛 | the five pungent roots | Garlic, two kinds of onion or scallion, leek, and asafoetida, per Lingcheng's own gloss of the Brahmajāla Sūtra's list, cited in Part 6's reading note on the water-drinking rite; kept as a fixed set-phrase rather than enumerated in running prose except where the source itself does so. |

## What Lingcheng's commentary structure contributes

Lingcheng reads this sūtra as built around four parallel retellings of Kṣitigarbha's founding vow — the elder's son moved by a buddha's marks, the Brahmin woman moved by her mother's suffering (both Part 1), the two kings who divided one vow between them, and Bright Eyes' rescue of her own mother (both Part 2) — and explicitly tracks this count across his commentary; Part 2's reading notes reproduce that count directly. He also maintains a running thread, introduced in Part 1's reading of the Brahmin woman's vision of hell and returned to at nearly every subsequent hell-description, that karma's instruments and wardens are mind-manifestation rather than externally furnished apparatus, grounded in citations from the *Vijñaptimātratā* and the Śūraṅgama's account of "retributive contact" generating its own tormentor — a position this collection's reading notes flag each time it recurs rather than treating each hell-scene as a standalone description. Where Lingcheng's own five-fold doctrinal classification (判教) places this sūtra within Tiantai's five periods — between the *Lotus Sūtra* and the *Nirvāṇa Sūtra*, in the "ghee" tier reserved for Tiantai's own most complete teachings — is introduced in Part 1's Translation Method section and not repeated at length elsewhere, since it is a claim about the whole text rather than any one passage.

## A note on this sūtra's treatment of gender

Twice in this sūtra — Bright Eyes' vow for her mother in Part 2, and the general promise to women who venerate Kṣitigarbha in Part 3 — freedom from ever again taking a woman's body is offered as a benefit devout women are presumed to want, treating female embodiment itself as a hardship comparable to poverty or illness rather than a neutral fact of rebirth. This collection's reading notes flag the assumption at both points of occurrence rather than passing over it in silence or editorializing at length; a reader coming to this sūtra after the site's *Lotus Sūtra* or *Vimalakīrti* pages, both of which stage direct challenges to exactly this assumption, may find the contrast instructive.

## A note for future Kṣitigarbha-related and Tiantai-line texts

Several renderings coined here — "the hell without interval" for 無間地獄 kept distinct from transliterated "Avīci," "the path of karma" for 業道, and "the holy daughter" for 聖女 — have no prior site precedent and were decided with an eye toward reuse should this site later take up the *Daśacakra Sūtra* or the *Sūtra on Divination*, the two other canonical pillars of Kṣitigarbha devotion Lingcheng cites repeatedly throughout his commentary, or further material from Tiantai's own commentarial tradition beyond the *Mohe Zhiguan* and *Xiao Zhiguan* already translated here. Future translators working on such texts should check this glossary before coining fresh renderings for the same terms.
