Chapter 5 · 優婆塞戒經 · 三種菩提品第五 · Why Only a Buddha Is Called Buddha

Three Roads to Awakening, and the Name Reserved for Only One

Shansheng asks the obvious, hard question: if śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas also attain bodhi, also awaken to the nature of things, also know the Four Noble Truths that comprise "everything" — why is the word "Buddha" withheld from them? The Buddha's answer works through three routes to awakening, a river crossed by three different animals, eight further marks that set him apart, and a roll call of titles that only he earns — before closing with a pointed reminder to the householder bodhisattva listening: this very discernment is hardest of all for someone still entangled in a lay life.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: Shansheng's Question — Why Isn't Every Awakened Person a Buddha?

The chapter opens, as most chapters of this sutra do, with Shansheng pressing the Buddha on a genuine difficulty rather than simply receiving a teaching. Bodhisattvas come in two kinds, householder and renunciant; bodhi itself comes in three kinds. If the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha genuinely attain bodhi, genuinely awaken to the nature of things, and genuinely know the Four Noble Truths — which between them cover absolutely everything — then on what basis is "Buddha" reserved for a third category alone?

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！如佛所說菩薩二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。菩提三種：一者、聲聞菩提，二者、緣覺菩提，三者、諸佛菩提。若得菩提名為佛者，何以故聲聞、辟支佛人不名為佛？若覺法性名為佛者，聲聞、緣覺亦覺法性，以何緣故不名為佛？若一切智名為佛者，聲聞、緣覺亦一切智，復以何故不名為佛？言一切者，即是四諦。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One! As the Buddha has taught, there are two kinds of bodhisattva: the householder and the renunciant. And there are three kinds of bodhi: the bodhi of the śrāvaka, the bodhi of the pratyekabuddha, and the bodhi of all Buddhas. If attaining bodhi alone is what makes one a Buddha, then why are śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas not called Buddhas? If awakening to the nature of phenomena is what makes one a Buddha, then śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas also awaken to the nature of phenomena — why, then, are they not called Buddhas? And if possessing the wisdom that knows all things is what makes one a Buddha, then śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas also possess the wisdom that knows all things — why, then, are they not called Buddhas? By 'all things,' I mean the Four Noble Truths."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu points out that this is exactly the same literary device the sutra uses throughout — Shansheng questions, the Buddha answers — the same shape found in the Diamond Sutra's exchange with Subhūti. The force of the question lies in how reasonable it sounds: the śrāvaka awakens upon hearing the Buddha's own voice teach the Four Truths and thereby attains the fruit of arhatship; the pratyekabuddha awakens by contemplating conditioned arising on his own. Both reach a real bodhi. If the Four Truths already cover suffering and its cause on the worldly side, cessation and the path on the transcendent side, then in what sense have the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha not already grasped "everything"? Taixu reads this as a deliberately loaded question, meant to draw out precisely the distinctions the Buddha is about to give — three questions in one, setting up three answers in turn.

2. Milestone: Three Roads to Bodhi — Hearing, Reflection, and Cultivation

The Buddha's first answer sorts the three kinds of bodhi by the very different roads that lead to each of them. A śrāvaka reaches bodhi only after hearing the Buddha's own voice teach it; a pratyekabuddha reaches a partial awakening through his own solitary reflection; only the Tathāgata, without any teacher at all, arrives at complete awakening to all things through cultivation itself.

Chinese Source

佛言：「善男子！菩提有三種：一者、從聞而得，二者、從思惟得，三者、從修而得。聲聞之人從聞得故，不名為佛；辟支佛人從思惟已少分覺故，名辟支佛；如來無師，不依聞、思，從修而得覺悟一切，是故名佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said: "Good son! Bodhi is attained in three ways: one, by hearing; two, by reflection; three, by cultivation. Because the śrāvaka attains it by hearing, he is not called a Buddha. Because the pratyekabuddha awakens to only a small part of it through reflection, he is called a pratyekabuddha. The Tathāgata, without a teacher, relying on neither hearing nor reflection, awakens to all things through cultivation — and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu notes that the śrāvaka must hear the Dharma spoken from the Buddha's own mouth before bodhi can arise at all; the pratyekabuddha needs no such spoken teaching, arriving instead at an awakening to the emptiness of self purely through his own contemplation of conditions — but only a fragment of the whole, not yet broad or deep. A bodhisattva's own path, Taixu adds, still makes use of the Buddha's teaching as a skillful means at the outset, yet ultimately awakens through a wordless, subtle realization rather than the śrāvaka's tendency to cling to the literal marks of the teaching — genuinely realizing the emptiness of the nature of all dharmas through pervasive, cultivated awakening, which is exactly what the pratyekabuddha's merely partial reflection has not yet reached. True all-encompassing wisdom, Taixu stresses, can only be won by a wisdom that needs no teacher at all, cultivated free of dependence on any word or concept whatsoever.

3. Milestone: General and Particular Characteristics — A Wisdom With Nothing Left Outside It

The Buddha's second answer turns from how bodhi is reached to what, exactly, is understood once it is reached. The nature of phenomena itself splits into general characteristics, shared across all things, and particular characteristics, unique to each — and only the Tathāgata's wisdom, together with his fully realized "conditioned wisdom," takes in both without remainder.

Chinese Source

「善男子！了知法性，故名為佛。法性二種：一者、總相，二者、別相。聲聞之人，總相知故不名為佛；辟支佛人同知總相，不從聞故，名辟支佛，不名為佛；如來世尊，總相、別相一切覺了，不依聞、思，無師獨悟，從修而得，故名為佛。善男子！如來世尊緣智具足，聲聞、緣覺雖知四諦，緣智不具，以是義故，不得名佛；如來世尊緣智具足，故得名佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! It is because he fully understands the nature of phenomena that he is called a Buddha. The nature of phenomena is of two kinds: general characteristics and particular characteristics. Because the śrāvaka knows only the general characteristics, he is not called a Buddha. The pratyekabuddha likewise knows only the general characteristics, and because he does not attain this through hearing, he is called a pratyekabuddha rather than a Buddha. The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, awakens fully to both the general and the particular characteristics — relying on neither hearing nor reflection, awakening alone without a teacher, attaining this through cultivation — and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha. Good son! The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, possesses complete conditioned wisdom. Though śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas know the Four Noble Truths, their conditioned wisdom is incomplete, and for this reason they cannot be called Buddhas; the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, possesses complete conditioned wisdom, and for this reason he attains the name Buddha.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu explains the general characteristic as the wisdom that sees the five aggregates as an assembled whole, empty of any fixed self — a level both the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha reach. The particular characteristic goes further: each individual dharma making up those five aggregates is, one by one, seen to be empty and without any fixed nature of its own — a far more exacting realization, reached only through cultivation, where all words and concepts finally become useless and a genuinely non-discriminating wisdom takes over. He reads "conditioned wisdom" as the Buddha's wisdom taking every single phenomenon whatsoever as its object, without exception, and comprehending the whole of that object-realm perfectly — an object-realm the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha, confined to the general characteristic alone, simply cannot match, precisely because they have not yet reached this pervasive, fully proper enlightenment.

4. Milestone: The Ganges Crossing — Rabbit, Horse, and Fragrant Elephant

The Buddha turns from abstract analysis to a single vivid image: three animals fording the same river of birth-and-death, only one of them ever touching the riverbed. The simile does double duty — it distinguishes depth of realization, and it distinguishes what is actually uprooted along the way.

Chinese Source

「善男子！如恒河水，三獸俱渡，兔、馬、香象。兔不至底，浮水而過；馬或至底，或不至底；象則盡底。恒河水者，即是十二因緣河也。聲聞渡時，猶如彼兔；緣覺渡時，猶如彼馬；如來渡時，猶如香象，是故如來得名為佛。聲聞、緣覺雖斷煩惱，不斷習氣，如來能拔一切煩惱、習氣根原，故名為佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! It is like the water of the Ganges River, which three animals cross together: the rabbit, the horse, and the fragrant elephant. The rabbit does not reach the riverbed, but floats across the surface of the water. The horse sometimes reaches the riverbed and sometimes does not. The elephant crosses all the way to the bottom. The water of the Ganges is the river of the twelve links of dependent origination. When the śrāvaka crosses it, he is like that rabbit; when the pratyekabuddha crosses it, he is like that horse; when the Tathāgata crosses it, he is like the fragrant elephant — and it is for this reason that the Tathāgata attains the name Buddha. Though śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas cut off the afflictions, they do not cut off the residual habits that remain from them. The Tathāgata is able to uproot the very source of both the afflictions and their residual habits, and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu notes the deliberate choice of images: the Ganges as the greatest river known in India, the fragrant elephant as the largest of elephants, standing in for the vast river of birth-and-death driven by the twelve links of dependent origination. All three animals genuinely cross; only the elephant reaches the very bottom. In the same way, the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha manage only to cut off the afflictions themselves — crossing, but not touching bottom, like the struggling rabbit and horse — while their residual habitual tendencies remain untouched beneath the surface. Only the Tathāgata reaches the riverbed itself, severing the root of both the afflictions and the habitual tendencies they leave behind, which is precisely why the crossing looks identical from a distance yet is, at the deepest level, not the same crossing at all.

5. Milestone: Eight Further Marks That Set the Buddha Apart

Having established the difference in depth, the Buddha adds eight further, more specific distinctions — pairs of qualities where the two vehicles reach only halfway and the Tathāgata reaches the whole way: two kinds of doubt, weariness in hearing and in reflection, purity of the vessel as well as the substance it holds, purity of conduct as well as of wisdom, the boundlessness of his practice, and the two obstructions overcome in a single thought-moment.

Chinese Source — two kinds of doubt

「善男子！疑有二種：一、煩惱疑，二、無記疑。二乘之人斷煩惱疑，不斷無記；如來悉斷如是二疑，是故名佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! Doubt is of two kinds: the doubt born of affliction, and indeterminate doubt. Those of the two vehicles cut off the doubt born of affliction, but do not cut off indeterminate doubt. The Tathāgata cuts off both of these kinds of doubt entirely, and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha.

Chinese Source — weariness

「善男子！聲聞之人厭於多聞，緣覺之人厭於思惟，佛於是二心無疲厭，故名為佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! The śrāvaka grows weary of extensive hearing, and the pratyekabuddha grows weary of extended reflection. The Buddha's mind knows no weariness in either of these, and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha.

Chinese Source — purity of vessel and conduct

「善男子！譬如淨物，置之淨器，表裏俱淨；聲聞、緣覺智雖清淨而器不淨，如來不爾，智器俱淨，是故名佛。善男子！淨有二種：一者、智淨，二者、行淨。聲聞、緣覺雖有淨智，行不清淨；如來世尊智、行俱淨，是故名佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! It is like a pure substance placed in a pure vessel, so that both the inside and the outside are pure. Though the wisdom of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha is itself pure, the vessel that holds it is not pure. The Tathāgata is not like this: both his wisdom and the vessel that holds it are pure, and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha. Good son! Purity is of two kinds: purity of wisdom, and purity of conduct. Though śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas possess pure wisdom, their conduct is not entirely pure. The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, possesses purity of both wisdom and conduct, and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha.

Chinese Source — boundless practice, two obstructions

「善男子！聲聞、緣覺其行有邊，如來世尊其行無邊，是故名佛。「善男子！如來世尊能於一念破壞二障——一者、智障，二者、解脫障——是故名佛。如來具足智因、智果，是故名佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! The conduct of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha has its boundaries; the conduct of the Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is without boundary — and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha. "Good son! The Tathāgata, the World-Honored One, is able in a single thought-moment to break through both obstructions — the obstruction of the knowable and the obstruction that hinders liberation — and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha. The Tathāgata possesses in full both the cause and the fruit of wisdom, and it is for this reason that he is called a Buddha.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu walks through all eight in sequence. The doubt born of affliction blocks wholesome states from arising and must be cut by anyone on the path; indeterminate doubt covers matters like exactly how many motes of dust compose the earth, or how much it weighs — questions the two vehicles have no need to resolve, but which the Buddha knows completely, down to counting every raindrop that falls across worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges. The śrāvaka awakens to the emptiness of self by hearing the Buddha's voice and then has no further wish for extensive hearing; the pratyekabuddha awakens through reflection and then has no further wish for extended reflection — only the Buddha remains tireless in both, knowing every mountain, river, mote of dust, blade of grass, and tree without exception.

On purity, Taixu reads "vessel" as the practitioner's own body and mind: the body-mind of the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha, itself the fruit of past karma, must ultimately be extinguished — ashes of the body, wisdom dissolved — to reach nirvāṇa without residue, since a body-mind still remaining is not yet counted as purified through and through. Their residual habitual tendencies likewise leave their conduct imperfect at times, where the Buddha's is pure without exception. Their self-benefiting and other-benefiting practice both carry a real limit and measure; the Buddha's carries none. And in his very last thought-moment, the Buddha severs both obstructions at once — the obstruction of the knowable, which blocks bodhi, and the obstruction of affliction, which blocks nirvāṇa — with wisdom-as-cause referring to the bodhi-mind and its cultivation, and wisdom-as-fruit referring to buddhahood itself, fully realized.

6. Milestone: The Buddha's Complete Virtue — Ten Powers, Four Fearlessnesses, Great Compassion

Having distinguished the Buddha from the two vehicles point by point, the sutra now describes his qualities directly and without comparison — speech that is never mistaken, wisdom with nothing hidden and nothing to guard, and the specific inventories of power, fearlessness, and compassion that Buddhist tradition uses to describe complete awakening.

Chinese Source

「善男子！如來出言無二無謬，亦無虛妄，智慧無礙，樂說亦爾，具足因智、時智、相智，無有覆藏，不須守護，無能說過，悉知一切眾生煩惱起結因緣、滅結因緣，世間八法所不能污，有大憐愍，救拔苦惱，具足十力、四無所畏、大悲、三念，身心二力悉皆滿足。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! Whatever the Tathāgata utters is never twofold, never mistaken, and never false; his wisdom is unobstructed, and so too is his gift for expounding the Dharma. He possesses in full the wisdom of causes, the wisdom of timing, and the wisdom of characteristics. He has nothing to conceal and nothing that needs guarding; no one can point out any fault in what he says. He fully knows, for every being, the causes and conditions that give rise to the knots of affliction and the causes and conditions that dissolve them. He cannot be stained by the eight worldly winds. He holds vast compassion, rescuing and lifting beings out of suffering. He possesses in full the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, great compassion, and the three foundations of mindfulness; the power of both his body and his mind are entirely complete.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads "unobstructed in his gift for expounding the Dharma" as speaking as freely as he wishes, with nothing holding him back. He glosses the three wisdoms concretely: knowing every being's mind and conduct is the wisdom of causes; knowing every moment in time is the wisdom of timing; knowing every causal characteristic, resultant characteristic, and distinguishing mark is the wisdom of characteristics. Because his three karmic activities of body, speech, and mind are entirely without fault, there is simply nothing left to conceal or protect. "Knots" names the afflictions themselves, so knowing their arising and their cessation means knowing exactly how affliction begins and ends in any given being; the "eight worldly things" are gain and loss, defamation and praise, acclaim and ridicule, suffering and pleasure — the eight winds no ordinary mind fully resists, yet none of which can move the Buddha at all.

He briefly unpacks the traditional lists invoked here: the ten powers include knowing right from wrong, knowing the karmic retribution of past, present, and future, knowing the various states of meditative absorption and liberation, knowing the character of each being's mind, and knowing the destination of every possible path, among others; the four fearlessnesses are the confidence to declare his own complete wisdom, to declare that he has exhausted every outflow, to name what obstructs the path, and to teach the path to the ending of suffering — a lion's roar delivered before any assembly without a trace of hesitation. Great compassion, Taixu adds, does not wait on favorable conditions to arise: the Buddha's compassion is unmoved by whether beings believe in him or not. He closes with a caution to the reader: at the very first arising of the bodhi-mind, a bodhisattva already carries this same aspiration toward great compassion, but not yet the power to act on it fully — which is exactly why sustained, diligent cultivation of both body and mind remains necessary, all the way through to the end.

7. Milestone: The Power of the Buddha's Body — Śakra's Seven-Headed Elephant

Asked to make "the power of his body" concrete rather than abstract, the sutra reaches for the most extravagant image available in its own cosmology: the elephant that carries the entire population of a heavenly city, and a comparison that dwarfs even that elephant beside a single joint of the Buddha's own body.

Chinese Source

「云何身力具足？善男子！三十三天有一大城，名曰善見，其城縱廣滿十萬里，宮室百萬，諸天一千六十六萬六千六百六十有六。夏三月時，釋提桓因欲往波利質多林中歡娛受樂，由乾陀山有一香象名伊羅鉢那，具足七頭，帝釋發念，象知即來，善見城中所有諸天處其頭上，旋行而往其林，去城五十由延。是象身力出勝一切香象身力，正使和合如是香象一萬八千，其力唯敵佛一節力，是故身力出勝一切眾生之力。世界無邊，眾生亦爾；如來心力，亦復無邊。是故如來獨得名佛，非二乘人名為佛也。

Ru-Yi Translation

"How is the power of his body complete? Good son! Among the Thirty-Three Heavens there is a great city called Sudarśana. This city measures a full hundred thousand li across, and holds a million palace halls, home to eleven million, six hundred sixty-six thousand, six hundred sixty-six devas. During the three months of summer, when Śakra, Lord of the Devas, wishes to go to the Pārijāta grove to take his ease and pleasure, the fragrant elephant Airāvaṇa, who dwells on Mount Gandhamādana and bears seven heads, knows the moment Śakra forms the wish, and comes at once. All the devas of the city of Sudarśana take their places upon its heads, and it circles about and carries them to the grove, fifty yojanas distant from the city. This elephant's bodily strength surpasses the bodily strength of every other fragrant elephant; and even if eighteen thousand such fragrant elephants were to combine their strength together, it would match no more than the strength in a single joint of the Buddha's body. So it is that the strength of his body surpasses the strength of every other being. Worlds are without limit, and beings are likewise without limit; the strength of the Tathāgata's mind is, in just the same way, without limit. It is for this reason that the Tathāgata alone attains the name Buddha, and those of the two vehicles do not attain the name Buddha.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu supplies the background a modern reader would otherwise miss: the Thirty-Three Heavens are the second heaven of the desire realm, and Sudarśana is the city crowning its summit, ruled by Śakra, lord of that heaven. Pārijāta, meaning something like "universally fragrant tree," names the king of trees; Gandhamādana, "fragrant tree mountain," is the elephant's home; Airāvaṇa itself means something like "vast and pungent," describing the elephant's own scale. The countless devas of the city, Taixu notes, can shrink or expand themselves at will, which is how so vast a population fits atop a single elephant's seven heads; a yojana, he adds, is an ancient Indian measure equivalent to roughly thirty Chinese li.

He reads the arithmetic itself as the entire point: eighteen thousand of the mightiest elephants in this cosmology, combined, equal no more than a single joint of the Buddha's body — a deliberately extravagant image chosen precisely because "worlds without limit, and the Tathāgata's mind-power likewise without limit" is not the kind of claim that argument alone can make vivid. The image does the work that abstraction cannot: it is meant to be felt, not merely accepted.

8. Milestone: Many Names for One Reality — the Buddha's Epithets

The sutra closes its portrait of the Buddha with a string of titles, each one a compressed summary of a quality already established over the course of the chapter — before stating plainly, one final time, that none of it belongs to the two vehicles, however much bodhi they may genuinely possess.

Chinese Source

以是義故，名無上師，名大丈夫，人中香象、師子、龍王、調御示導，名大船師，名大醫師、大牛之王、人中牛王，名淨蓮花、無師獨覺，為諸眾生之眼目也！是大施主，是大沙門、大婆羅門，寂靜持戒，勤行精進，到於彼岸，獲得解脫。善男子！聲聞、緣覺雖有菩提，都無是事，是故名佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"For this reason he is called the Unsurpassed Teacher, the Great Hero, the Fragrant Elephant Among Men, the Lion, the Dragon King, the Tamer and Guide, the Great Ship's Captain, the Great Physician, the King of Great Bulls, the Bull King Among Men, the Pure Lotus Flower, the One Who Awakens Alone Without a Teacher, and the Eye of all beings. He is the Great Almsgiver, the Great Śramaṇa, the Great Brāhmaṇa — tranquil, holding the precepts, diligent in effort, arrived at the far shore, having attained liberation. Good son! Though śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas possess bodhi, none of this is true of them, and it is for this reason that he alone is called a Buddha.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu glosses the list title by title: Unsurpassed Teacher, because among all teachers of gods and humans none surpasses him; Great Hero, because he alone can tame and guide beings toward the path; Great Ship's Captain, because he ferries all beings across to the far shore; Great Physician, because he heals the sicknesses of every being's mind. Where an ordinary human might be compared to an ordinary bull, the Buddha is likened to the King of Great Bulls; he is the Pure Lotus Flower because he is entirely undefiled; the One Who Awakens Alone Without a Teacher because he reached awakening beneath the bodhi tree with no instruction from anyone; and the Eye of all beings because it is he who points out the path of awakening for them to follow. He is the Great Almsgiver because he holds nothing back from anyone; śramaṇa is simply the general term for a renunciant, while brāhmaṇa denotes a lineage understood as ritually pure — and because the Buddha is the most utterly pure of all renunciants, both titles attach to him as "great." Body and mind entirely pure is what "tranquil" means here; able to end evil and cultivate good, holding the precepts without ever regressing, diligent and unflagging in effort — this is what "arrived at the far shore, having attained liberation" describes. None of this, Taixu underlines one final time, belongs to the śrāvaka or the pratyekabuddha, whatever bodhi they may genuinely hold — which is exactly why "Buddha" belongs to the Tathāgata, and to him alone.

9. Milestone: A Closing Warning — Why the Householder's Task Is the Harder One

The chapter's final lines pivot without warning from cosmic description back to the sutra's actual audience: an upāsaka precepts text addressed above all to lay practitioners. Having just spent an entire chapter distinguishing three kinds of bodhi, the Buddha closes by admitting plainly that this very distinction is far harder to make from inside a householder's life than from inside a monastery.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩有二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。出家菩薩分別如是三種菩提，是不為難；在家分別，是乃為難。何以故？在家之人多惡因緣所纏遶故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! There are two kinds of bodhisattva: the householder and the renunciant. It is no great difficulty for the renunciant bodhisattva to distinguish these three kinds of bodhi in this way; it is the householder who finds this difficult to distinguish. Why is this so? Because the householder is bound about by many evil causes and conditions."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this closing line as a deliberate note of caution aimed squarely at the householder bodhisattva listening to this teaching — a reminder that the surroundings of a lay life are genuinely harsh, and that clearly discerning these three kinds of bodhi from within them is a real difficulty, not a false modesty. He does not read this as discouragement; the sutra as a whole is addressed to precisely this audience, and its entire structure assumes lay practitioners can and do make this kind of progress. But he takes the Buddha's own honesty here seriously: the household path to recognizing the difference between the bodhi of hearing, the bodhi of reflection, and the bodhi that has no teacher at all is not offered as an easier road than the monastic one — only as a road that remains genuinely open, however much more it is entangled by the ordinary evil causes and conditions of household life.
