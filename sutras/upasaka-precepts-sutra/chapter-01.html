Chapter 1 · 優婆塞戒經 · 集會品第一 · The Assembly Convenes, and What Actually Makes a Bodhisattva

Not Born a Bodhisattva, But Becoming One

The sutra opens not with a doctrine but with an argument: the layman Shansheng presses the Buddha on whether the Buddha Dharma, too, has its own six directions to bow to each morning, and the exchange that follows forces open this scripture's real subject — whether "bodhisattva" names something a being simply is, or something a being does. By the chapter's end, the debate has produced this sutra's most startling claim: that an ordinary householder's first thought of enlightenment can outrank the attainment of a pratyekabuddha.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: The Assembly Convenes

Every sutra opens by establishing exactly who spoke, who heard, and where and when it happened — a habit meant to guarantee, from the very first line, that what follows was actually witnessed and not simply invented. This chapter's opening scene sets the whole sutra at Śrāvastī, in the well-known Jetavana Anāthapiṇḍada's Park, before an assembly notable for exactly who it does and does not include.

Chinese Source

如是我聞：一時，佛在舍衛國祇樹林中阿那邠坻精舍，與大比丘僧千二百五十人，五百比丘尼，千優婆塞，五百乞兒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus have I heard: At one time the Buddha was dwelling at Anāthapiṇḍada's Park in the Jeta Grove, in the country of Śrāvastī, together with a great assembly of twelve hundred and fifty bhikṣus, five hundred bhikṣuṇīs, a thousand upāsakas, and five hundred beggars.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads the sutra's opening formula through the traditional "five certainties" used to authenticate a scripture: certainty of the hearer (the word "I," meaning Ānanda, who compiled this text at the First Council after the Buddha's death, and who was famed among the Buddha's disciples for never forgetting a single word he heard), certainty of the time, certainty of the speaker, certainty of the place, and certainty of the assembly — each one a small guarantee against a text having simply been invented from nowhere.

He also lingers over the assembly itself, and calls out something distinctive about it: bhikṣus, bhikṣuṇīs, laymen, even five hundred beggars are named — but no gods, no asuras, no beings from any of the other five destinies at all. Every listener belongs to the human realm. Taixu takes this as the first quiet signal of the whole sutra's real orientation: whatever the following twenty-seven chapters go on to say, this is, from its very first line, a scripture addressed to human beings living human lives — which is exactly why he calls it, informally, "the sutra for householders."

He also retells, briefly, the story behind the park's own name: Prince Jeta initially demanded, half in jest, that the elder Anāthapiṇḍada cover the ground in gold before he would part with his land; the elder, determined to secure a dwelling place for the Buddha, actually began doing so, and the prince, moved by such sincerity, donated the trees himself rather than accept full payment. The park's name, Taixu notes, still literally commemorates both donors at once — Jeta's grove, and "Anāthapiṇḍada," literally "one who gives to the destitute and solitary," who paid for the rest.

2. Milestone: Shansheng's Question — Bowing to the Six Directions

Into this assembly steps an elder's son named Shansheng, already drawn toward the Buddha's teaching but still carrying a question left over from the non-Buddhist schools he once studied under: is there, within the Buddha Dharma itself, some equivalent of the popular practice of bowing each morning to six directions for long life and wealth?

Chinese Source

爾時，會中有長者子名曰善生，白佛言：「世尊！外道六師常演說法，教眾生言：『若能晨朝敬禮六方，則得增長命之與財。何以故？東方之土屬于帝釋，有供養者，釋提桓因則為護助。南方之土屬閻羅王，有供養者，彼閻羅王則為護助。西方之土屬婆樓那天，有供養者，彼婆樓那則為護助。北方之土屬拘毘羅天，有供養者，彼拘毘羅則為護助。下方之土屬于火天，有供養者，火天則為護助。上方之土屬于風天，有供養者，彼亦為護。』世尊！佛法之中，頗有如是六方不耶？」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, in the assembly there was an elder's son named Shansheng, who said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the teachers of the six non-Buddhist schools constantly proclaim their teaching, instructing beings as follows: 'If one reverently bows to the six directions every morning, one's lifespan and wealth will both increase. Why is this so? The eastern quarter belongs to Śakra; whoever makes offerings there is protected and helped by Śakra Devānām-indra. The southern quarter belongs to King Yama; whoever makes offerings there is protected and helped by King Yama. The western quarter belongs to the god Varuṇa; whoever makes offerings there is protected and helped by Varuṇa. The northern quarter belongs to the god Kubera; whoever makes offerings there is protected and helped by Kubera. The lower quarter belongs to the fire-god; whoever makes offerings there is protected by the fire-god. The upper quarter belongs to the wind-god; whoever makes offerings there is likewise protected.' World-Honored One, does the Buddha Dharma likewise have such six directions, or not?"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu identifies Shansheng's question as reflecting an entire class of teaching current in the Buddha's own day: "non-Buddhist," he specifies, does not simply mean "other religion" but any teaching whose content fails to correspond to how things actually are. Ninety-six such schools were current at the time; the "six teachers" were simply their most prominent representatives, ranging from outright denial of moral cause and effect to fatalism to extreme asceticism.

He reads Shansheng's own name — "well-born" — as itself pointing to the chapter's underlying theme: a being who has already accumulated enough good root to be born human, and who moreover manages not to waste that rare opportunity, deserves the name. Shansheng has by this point already taken some form of refuge in the Buddha, Taixu notes, yet still carries over an old habit of ritual observance from his previous teachers — which is precisely why his question, rather than being dismissed, becomes the occasion for the Buddha to redirect an old practice toward a genuinely new meaning.

3. Milestone: The Buddha's Answer — Six Directions as the Six Perfections

Rather than rejecting the six-directions framework outright, the Buddha answers on its own terms — and then quietly empties it of the external deities Shansheng was taught to fear and honor, refilling each direction with one of the six pāramitās instead.

Chinese Source

「善男子！我佛法中亦有六方，所謂六波羅蜜。東方則是檀波羅蜜。何以故？始初出者，為出智慧光因緣故。彼東方者屬眾生心，若有眾生能供養彼檀波羅蜜，則為增長壽命與財。南方即是尸波羅蜜。何以故？尸波羅蜜名之為右，若人供養，亦得增長壽命與財。西方即是羼提波羅蜜。何以故？彼西方者名之為後，一切惡法棄於後故，若有供養，則得增長壽命與財。北方即是毘離耶波羅蜜。何以故？北方名號勝諸惡法，若人供養，則得增長壽命與財。下方則是禪波羅蜜。何以故？能正觀察三惡道故，若人供養，亦得增長命之與財。上方即是般若波羅蜜。何以故？上方者即是無上無上故，若有供養，則得增長命之與財。善男子！是六方者，屬眾生心，非如外道六師所說。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, in my Buddha Dharma there are likewise six directions — namely, the six pāramitās. The east is the pāramitā of giving. Why is this? Because it is the first to rise, like the sun, on account of giving forth the light of wisdom. That east belongs to the mind of sentient beings; if a being is able to make offering to that pāramitā of giving, his lifespan and wealth will both increase. The south is the pāramitā of moral discipline. Why is this? The pāramitā of discipline is named 'the right hand,' and whoever makes offering to it likewise gains increase of lifespan and wealth. The west is the pāramitā of patience. Why is this? That west is named 'the rear,' because all evil dharmas are cast behind one; whoever makes offering to it gains increase of lifespan and wealth. The north is the pāramitā of vigor. Why is this? The north is named for overcoming every evil dharma; whoever makes offering to it gains increase of lifespan and wealth. The lower direction is the pāramitā of meditative concentration. Why is this? Because it enables correct contemplation of the three evil destinies; whoever makes offering to it likewise gains increase of lifespan and wealth. The upper direction is the pāramitā of wisdom. Why is this? Because 'upper' means 'utterly unsurpassed'; whoever makes offering to it gains increase of lifespan and wealth. Good man, these six directions belong to the mind of sentient beings — they are not as the six non-Buddhist teachers describe."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu glosses "pāramitā" plainly as "reaching the far shore" — any undertaking carried through to its complete and perfect fulfillment. He walks through why each direction is paired with the perfection it is: giving is placed in the east because, like the rising sun, it is the very first practice from which the light of wisdom begins to dawn; discipline is called "the right hand" because it is honored as superior; patience is placed in the west, "the rear," because it is precisely the practice of casting every provocation behind oneself; vigor overcomes evil, as its northern name suggests; meditative concentration correctly surveys the three evil destinies, which is why it belongs below; and wisdom, called "utterly unsurpassed," rightly stands above everything else.

He sums up the whole maneuver with evident satisfaction: a correct person can speak even a heretical teaching and make it correct. The Buddha does not reject the six-directions framework Shansheng arrived with — he empties it of its external gods and refills it with the six perfections that were, all along, latent within the practitioner's own mind.

4. Milestone: Only a Bodhisattva Can Make This Offering

Having relocated the six directions inside the practitioner's own mind, the Buddha is immediately asked the obvious next question — who, exactly, is actually capable of making offering to them? The answer is brief, but it sets up the entire debate that follows.

Chinese Source

「如是六方，誰能供養？」「善男子！唯有菩薩乃能供養。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"These six directions — who is able to make offering to them?" "Good man, only a bodhisattva is able to make such offering."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu uses this brief exchange to correct a confusion he says he still hears constantly in his own day: the popular habit of calling any carved or molded devotional image — wood, clay, or bronze alike — a "bodhisattva," regardless of what being it actually depicts. Properly speaking, he insists, "bodhisattva" is short for bodhi-sattva, "a sentient being committed to awakening," and names specifically someone who has generated the aspiration for supreme enlightenment — not any deity or spirit still bound within the six realms of rebirth, however revered.

A being who has genuinely given rise to this aspiration has, by that very fact, already stepped outside the ordinary cycle of the three realms, which is exactly why, he notes, only such a being — and no unawakened spirit, however powerful — is actually capable of making offering to the six perfections at all.

5. Milestone: What Does "Bodhisattva" Mean? — A Debate Begins

Shansheng does not let the Buddha's first answer pass unchallenged. The Buddha offers two definitions of "bodhisattva" at once — attainment of bodhi, and possession of a bodhi-nature — and Shansheng immediately spots a tension between them that becomes the chapter's central puzzle.

Chinese Source

「世尊！以何義故名為菩薩？」佛言：「得菩提故名為菩薩；菩提性故名為菩薩。」

「世尊！若言得菩提已名為菩薩者，若未供養彼六方時，云何得名為菩薩耶？若以性故名菩薩者，誰有此性？有此性者則能供養，若無性者，則不能供養！是故如來不應說言：彼六方者，屬眾生心。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One, for what reason is one called a bodhisattva?" The Buddha said: "One is called a bodhisattva because one has attained bodhi; one is called a bodhisattva because of the nature of bodhi."

"World-Honored One, if one is called a bodhisattva only after having already attained bodhi, then before one has yet made offering to those six directions, how could one already be called a bodhisattva? And if one is called a bodhisattva by reason of a bodhi-nature, then who actually possesses this nature? Whoever possesses this nature would then be able to make the offering, and whoever lacks it would be unable to make the offering! On this reasoning, the Tathāgata should not have said that those six directions belong to the mind of sentient beings."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu flags Shansheng's follow-up as the real turning point of the entire chapter — arguably of the entire sutra. The Buddha's first answer offers two definitions at once: "bodhisattva" by virtue of having attained bodhi, a claim about a result already reached, and "bodhisattva" by virtue of possessing a bodhi-nature, a claim about something already, permanently present as a cause.

Shansheng immediately spots the tension between them: if the title depends on having already attained bodhi, no one could be called a bodhisattva before making any offering at all, since attainment lies at the very end of the path, not its beginning; and if it depends on a fixed inner nature, then either everyone already has it, in which case no cultivation is actually needed, or only some beings have it, in which case the Buddha's own claim that these six directions belong to the mind of sentient beings — presumably every sentient being — would already be false. Taixu treats this objection with genuine respect, calling it exactly the kind of sharp, structurally serious question a scripture needs in order to earn the answer that follows.

6. Milestone: Neither Attainment Alone, Nor a Fixed Nature

The Buddha's reply rejects both horns of Shansheng's dilemma at once, and in doing so states what the commentary tradition treats as this sutra's real thesis: no being possesses a fixed, ready-made "bodhisattva-nature" any more than a being possesses a fixed nature as human, lion, or dog. What is called bodhisattva-nature is simply the bodhi-mind's own arising, brought about by the coming-together of good causes and conditions — never a pre-existing possession waiting to be found.

Chinese Source

「善男子！非得菩提故名菩薩。何以故？得菩提者，名之為佛，未得菩提乃名菩薩；亦非性故名菩薩也。

「善男子！一切眾生無菩提性，如諸眾生無人、天性，師子、虎、狼、狗犬等性；現在世中和合眾善業因緣故，得人、天身，和合不善業因緣故，得師子等畜生之身。菩薩亦爾，和合眾善業因緣故，發菩提心，故名菩薩。若有說言一切眾生有菩薩性者，是義不然。何以故？若有性者，則不應修善業因緣供養六方。善男子！若有性者，則無初心及退轉心；以無量善業因緣故，發菩提心名菩薩性。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, it is not because one has attained bodhi that one is called a bodhisattva. Why is this? One who has attained bodhi is called a Buddha; it is one who has not yet attained bodhi who is called a bodhisattva. Nor is one called a bodhisattva by reason of possessing a fixed nature.

"Good man, no sentient being possesses a fixed 'bodhi-nature,' just as no sentient being possesses a fixed 'human nature' or 'heavenly nature,' or a fixed 'lion nature,' 'tiger nature,' 'wolf nature,' or 'dog nature.' It is only because various good karmic causes and conditions come together in the present life that one obtains a human or heavenly body, and only because unwholesome karmic causes and conditions come together that one obtains the body of a lion or some other animal. It is the very same with a bodhisattva: it is because manifold good karmic causes and conditions come together that the bodhi-mind is generated, and it is for this reason that one is called a bodhisattva. If someone claims that all sentient beings possess a fixed 'bodhisattva-nature,' this is not correct. Why is this? Because if such a fixed nature existed, there would be no need to cultivate the causes and conditions of good karma in order to make offering to the six directions. Good man, if a fixed nature existed, there could be neither an initial arising of the mind nor any falling back from it; it is precisely because of immeasurable good karmic causes and conditions that the bodhi-mind is generated — and this generating of the bodhi-mind is itself what is called 'bodhisattva-nature.'"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu is careful to reconcile this passage with other, seemingly contradictory scriptures rather than simply picking a side. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra's teaching that "all beings have Buddha-nature" speaks from the standpoint of ultimate principle, where all things are equally empty and undifferentiated; the Lotus Sūtra's teaching that "the Buddha-seed arises from conditions" speaks from the standpoint of the eventual fruit, emphasizing how a teaching Buddha's compassionate guidance is needed to draw that potential out.

This sutra, he argues, speaks from a third and distinct standpoint — actual practice — and it is exactly the standpoint most useful for a being still deciding what to do next: one who acts like a demon has, in that moment, a demon's nature; one who practices generosity and discipline has a human or heavenly nature; and one who actually generates the bodhi-mind and takes up the bodhisattva path has, by that act alone and no earlier, a bodhisattva's nature — available to anyone, of any background, the instant they actually take it up.

7. Milestone: Ten Causes That Give Rise to the Bodhi-Mind

Having established that the bodhi-mind arises rather than being inherited, the Buddha turns to how it actually arises in practice — surveying ten distinct doorways through which different beings, starting from very different places, each come to generate it.

Chinese Source

「善男子！有諸眾生受行外道，不樂外典顛倒說故，發菩提心；或有眾生住寂靜處，內善因緣，發菩提心；或有眾生觀生死過，發菩提心；或有眾生見惡、聞惡，發菩提心；或有眾生深知自身貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡、慳嫉，為呵責故，發菩提心；或有眾生見諸外道五通神仙，發菩提心；或有眾生欲知世間有邊無邊故，發菩提心；或有眾生見聞如來不思議故，發菩提心；或有眾生生憐愍故，發菩提心；或有眾生愛眾生故，發菩提心。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, there are beings who, having practiced under non-Buddhist teachers and grown dissatisfied with the inverted teachings of those outer scriptures, generate the bodhi-mind. There are beings who, dwelling in quiet solitude, generate the bodhi-mind through an inner wholesome cause. There are beings who, contemplating the faults of birth-and-death, generate the bodhi-mind. There are beings who, having seen evil or heard of evil, generate the bodhi-mind. There are beings who, deeply recognizing their own greed, anger, delusion, stinginess, and envy, generate the bodhi-mind in order to reproach themselves for these. There are beings who, having seen non-Buddhist sages possessed of the five supernormal powers, generate the bodhi-mind. There are beings who, wishing to know whether the world has a limit or is without limit, generate the bodhi-mind. There are beings who, having seen or heard of the Tathāgata's inconceivable qualities, generate the bodhi-mind. There are beings who generate the bodhi-mind out of compassion. And there are beings who generate the bodhi-mind out of love for other beings."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats the list of ten conditions as strikingly concrete rather than abstract, and illustrates the very first one — disillusionment with an outer teaching — with an example drawn straight from his own contemporary China: practitioners of popular spirit-writing cults and planchette societies who, on actually examining their doctrines and finding them incoherent or contradicted by plain fact, abandon them for a teaching grounded in reasoned cause and effect instead.

He reads several of the ten as variations on a single insight turning inward rather than outward: recognizing one's own greed, hatred, delusion, stinginess, and envy for what they are is itself already the seed of wanting to be free of them. Two of the ten he reads as intellectual rather than emotional in character — wondering whether the universe has any actual boundary is, he notes, precisely the kind of question that drives both ancient philosophy and modern science alike, and it belongs on this list because the wish to know the truth of things, followed all the way through, itself becomes indistinguishable from the wish for enlightenment.

8. Milestone: Three Grades of Bodhicitta, None of Them Fixed

The bodhi-mind, once generated, is not a single uniform thing — the sutra distinguishes lower, middle, and upper grades, and insists that a being can move between all three in either direction, which is itself further proof that no fixed nature can be assigned to any of them.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩提之心，凡有三種，謂下、中、上。若言眾生定有性者，云何說言有三種耶？眾生下心能作中心，中心作上，上心作中，中心作下。眾生勤修無量善法，故能增上；不勤修故，便退為下。若善修進則名不退，若不修進名之為退。一切時中常為一切無邊眾生修集善故，名不退轉；若不如是，是名退轉，如是菩薩則有退心及怖畏心。若一切時中為一切眾生修集善法，得不退轉，是故我記是大喜地，不久當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。」

「善男子！三種菩提無有定性；若有定性，已發聲聞、緣覺心者，則不能發菩提之心。善男子！譬如眾僧無有定性，是三種性亦復如是。若有說言定有性者，是名外道。何以故？諸外道等無因果故，如自在天非因非果。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, the bodhi-mind is of three grades altogether: lower, middle, and upper. If one were to say that sentient beings possess a fixed nature, how could there be said to be three grades at all? A being's lower mind can become a middle mind, a middle mind an upper mind, an upper mind can fall back to a middle mind, and a middle mind can fall back to a lower mind. Because beings diligently cultivate immeasurable good dharmas, they are able to advance upward; because they fail to cultivate diligently, they fall back to the lower grade. If one cultivates and advances well, this is called 'non-regressing'; if one fails to cultivate and advance, this is called 'regressing.' Only one who, at all times, continually cultivates and accumulates good for the sake of all beings without limit is called non-regressing; whoever does not do so is called regressing — such a bodhisattva still has a mind capable of regressing and a mind of fear. But if, at all times, one cultivates and accumulates good dharmas for the sake of all beings and thereby attains non-regression, then I prophesy that this person has reached the Ground of Great Joy, and before long will attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete enlightenment."

"Good man, the three kinds of bodhi have no fixed nature; if they had a fixed nature, then one who had already given rise to the mind of a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha could never afterward give rise to the bodhi-mind. Good man, it is just as with the monastic saṅgha, which has no fixed membership — these three natures are exactly the same. Whoever claims they are fixed and determinate is speaking as a non-Buddhist. Why is this? Because the non-Buddhist teachers deny cause and effect altogether — as with their god Īśvara, who they say is neither a cause nor an effect of anything."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu maps the three grades directly onto three levels of aspiration: the "lower" mind aims at the śrāvaka's path, the "middle" mind at the pratyekabuddha's, and the "upper" mind at the bodhisattva's own supreme goal — and movement between all three runs in both directions at once, which is exactly why no fixed nature can be assigned to any of them. His illustration is the Buddhist monastic community itself: a saṅgha has no fixed membership, since people join and depart it continuously, yet it remains recognizably "the saṅgha" throughout — the three grades of bodhi-mind are exactly this fluid, not some permanently sorted set of three fixed types of person.

He is equally precise about what "non-regression" actually requires: not a single admirable effort, but ceaseless, unbroken accumulation of good on behalf of every being, at all times, without exception — even one passing thought of retreat, one flicker of fear, is already enough to count as regression. The "Ground of Great Joy" that the Buddha promises to the genuinely non-regressing practitioner is the technical name for the very first of the bodhisattva's ten grounds. He closes this section by contrasting true dependent-origination with the non-Buddhist notion of an uncaused, unconditioned creator-deity such as Īśvara — a being said to be neither cause nor effect of anything at all, and therefore, on the sutra's own logic, simply outside the entire framework of causes and conditions the rest of the argument depends on.

9. Milestone: Refuting the Brahmin's Gold-in-Stone Analogy

Anticipating an obvious counter-argument — that a bodhisattva-nature might sit dormant within a being the way gold sits within unrefined ore, needing only the right conditions to be drawn out — the Buddha names and dismantles this exact analogy as a brahmanical doctrine, on grounds both logical and strikingly physical.

Chinese Source

「善男子！或有人說菩薩之性，譬如石中定有金性，以巧方便因緣發故，得為金用；菩薩之性亦如是者，是梵志說。何以故？梵志等常言尼拘陀子有尼拘陀樹，眼有火、石，是故梵志無因無果，因即是果，果即是因。尼拘陀子具足而有尼拘陀樹，當知即是梵志因果。是義不然。何以故？因細果麁故。若言眼中定有火者，眼則被燒；眼若被燒，云何能見？眼中有石，石則遮眼；眼若有遮，復云何見？善男子！如梵志說：有即是有，無即是無；無則不生，有不應滅。若言石中有金性者，金不說性，性不說金。善男子！因緣故則有和合，緣和合故本無後有，如梵志言無即永無。是義云何？金合水銀，金則滅壞，若言有，不應滅。是義云何？若說眾生有菩薩性，是名外道，不名佛道。善男子！譬如和合石因緣故而有金用，菩薩之性，亦復如是。眾生有思，名為欲心，以如是欲，善業因緣發菩提心，是則名為菩薩性也。善男子！譬如眾生先無菩提後乃方有，性亦如是先無後有，是故不可說言定有。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, there are those who say that the bodhisattva-nature is like the nature of gold fixedly present within ore, which, through skillful means and the right conditions, is drawn out and made usable as gold; and that the bodhisattva-nature is just the same. This is the teaching of the brahmin ascetics. Why is this? The brahmins constantly say that the seed of the nyagrodha tree already contains the nyagrodha tree, and that fire and stone are already present within the eye — and on this basis they hold that there is no real cause and no real effect, since cause simply is effect and effect simply is cause. Because the nyagrodha seed already fully contains the nyagrodha tree, they take this as proving their doctrine of cause and effect. But this reasoning does not hold. Why is this? Because the cause is subtle while the effect is coarse. If fire were truly, fixedly present in the eye, the eye would be burned by it; and if the eye were burned, how could it see? If stone were present in the eye, the stone would obstruct it; and if the eye were obstructed, how, again, could it see? Good man, as the brahmins would have it: what exists simply exists, and what does not exist simply and permanently does not exist; what does not exist cannot arise, and what exists ought never to perish. But if gold's nature were truly, fixedly present within the ore, then one should not speak of it as a mere 'nature' at all — one would simply call it gold outright, and what is called gold could not, in that case, also be called a mere nature. Good man, it is only because causes and conditions come together in combination that gold appears at all — and because it arises through the coming-together of conditions, it did not exist beforehand and only exists afterward; this directly contradicts the brahmins' claim that what does not exist can never come to exist. Consider: gold combined with mercury will dissolve and be destroyed — yet if gold's existence were truly fixed and unconditioned, as they claim, it should never be capable of perishing at all! How, then, can their reasoning stand? So if it is said that sentient beings possess a fixed bodhisattva-nature, this is a non-Buddhist teaching, not the Buddha's own teaching. Good man, it is rather like this: gold becomes usable as gold only through the coming-together of causes and conditions within the ore — and the bodhisattva-nature is exactly the same. A sentient being's own intention is called the mind of aspiration; and it is by means of just such an aspiration, together with the causes and conditions of good karma, that the bodhi-mind is generated — and this alone is what is properly called 'bodhisattva-nature.' Good man, just as a sentient being at first has no bodhi and only afterward comes to possess it, the nature too is first absent and only afterward present; for this reason it can never be said that it is fixedly, permanently existent."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu unpacks the brahmanical argument being refuted here in some technical detail, since its logic is easy to misread as merely poetic. The brahmins held that cause and effect are ultimately identical — that a nyagrodha seed, being tiny, already fully contains within it the immense nyagrodha tree it will become, and, in the same way, that fire and stone are already secretly present within a healthy eye, which is why a diseased eye can feel like burning, or grow clouded. From this they concluded that what exists has always existed and what does not exist can never come to exist at all.

The Buddha's rebuttal, Taixu notes, is direct and almost experimental: a cause is properly subtle and its effect coarse, so the two cannot simply be declared identical; and if fire and stone were genuinely, literally present in a functioning eye, that eye would already be burned or blocked and could not see at all — which plainly contradicts ordinary experience. He also points to gold dissolving when combined with mercury as a second, independent counterexample: if gold's existence were truly fixed and unconditioned, as the brahmins' own logic requires, it could never be destroyed under any circumstance — yet it plainly can be.

Taixu reads the whole passage as establishing, by direct argument rather than mere assertion, that the bodhisattva-nature discussed throughout this chapter belongs to the same category as gold actually being drawn out of ore: absent beforehand, present only afterward, and arising strictly through the coming-together of one's own sincere aspiration together with the causes and conditions of good karma — never a fixed possession waiting, unconditioned, to be found.

10. Milestone: Five Marks of a Bodhisattva, and Its Two Pairs of Kinds

Having settled what bodhisattva-nature is not, the sutra turns to what actually defines a bodhisattva in practice — five motivating qualities, followed by two working distinctions, regressing against non-regressing and monastic against householder, each measured concretely by whether the relevant precepts are actually kept.

Chinese Source

「善男子！求大智慧，故名菩薩；欲知一切法真實故、大莊嚴故、心堅固故、多度眾生故、不惜身命故，是名菩薩修行大乘。」

「善男子！菩薩有二種：一者、退轉，二者、不退。已修三十二相業者，名不退轉；若未能修，是名為退。復有二種：一者、出家，二者、在家。出家菩薩奉持八重，具足清淨，是名不退；在家菩薩奉持六重，具足清淨，亦名不退。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, it is because one seeks great wisdom that one is called a bodhisattva: because one wishes to know the true reality of all dharmas, because of great adornment, because the mind is firm and unshakeable, because one wishes to save many beings, and because one does not begrudge body or life — for these reasons is it said that a bodhisattva practices the Great Vehicle."

"Good man, bodhisattvas are of two kinds: those who regress and those who do not regress. One who has already cultivated the karma productive of the thirty-two marks is called non-regressing; one who has not yet been able to cultivate it is called regressing. There is a further division into two kinds: the monastic and the householder. A monastic bodhisattva who upholds the eight grave precepts in their fullness and purity is called non-regressing; a householder bodhisattva who upholds the six grave precepts in their fullness and purity is likewise called non-regressing."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats the five defining marks named here as nothing less than a table of contents for the entire twenty-eight-chapter sutra still to come: the wish to know the true reality of all dharmas anticipates the sutra's later chapters on wisdom itself; "great adornment" anticipates the chapters explicitly devoted to the bodhisattva's two adornments, merit and wisdom; a firm, unshakeable mind anticipates the chapter on formally receiving the precepts, since without real firmness no vow actually holds; the wish to save many beings anticipates the chapters on the six perfections and on the karma that produces a Buddha's thirty-two marks; and not begrudging body or life anticipates the sutra's later teaching that a bodhisattva who has genuinely realized the emptiness of self can give away flesh and bone exactly as freely as ordinary wealth.

On the two pairs of bodhisattva that follow, Taixu is careful to note that the sutra measures "non-regression" concretely, by precept-keeping, rather than abstractly: the eight grave precepts asked of a monastic bodhisattva combine the four root prohibitions shared with the ordinary monastic code — killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying — with four further ones distinctive to the bodhisattva path itself: praising oneself while disparaging others, withholding the Dharma out of stinginess, meeting another's repentance with unforgiving anger, and teaching a counterfeit dharma dressed up as the real one. The six grave precepts asked of a householder bodhisattva, treated in full later in the sutra's own chapter on receiving the precepts, serve exactly the same function for lay practice. Taixu reads the deliberate emphasis on precept-keeping, this early and this concretely, as the clearest sign of where this sutra's true center of gravity actually lies — not in abstract metaphysics for its own sake, but in a livable, checkable ethical practice.

11. Milestone: The Householder Bodhisattva's Astonishing Merit

The chapter closes with a ranked comparison running from non-Buddhist virtue up through the four fruits of the śrāvaka path and the pratyekabuddha's attainment — only to overturn the entire ranking with a single claim: an ordinary householder's bare generation of the bodhi-mind outranks them all, precisely because it happens in the midst of everything that makes household life difficult.

Chinese Source

「善男子！外道斷欲所得福德，勝於欲界一切眾生所有福德；須陀洹人勝於一切外道異見；斯陀含人勝於一切須陀洹果；阿那含人勝於一切斯陀含果；阿羅漢人勝於一切阿那含果；辟支佛人勝於一切阿羅漢果；在家之人發菩提心，勝於一切辟支佛果。出家之人發菩提心，此不為難，在家之人發菩提心，是乃名為不可思議。何以故？在家之人多惡因緣所纏遶故。在家之人發菩提心時，從四天王乃至阿迦膩吒諸天，皆大驚喜，作如是言：『我今已得人天之師。』」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, the merit gained by non-Buddhist ascetics who cut off desire surpasses all the merit possessed by every being in the desire realm. A stream-enterer surpasses every mistaken view held by the non-Buddhist teachers. A once-returner surpasses the fruit of every stream-enterer. A non-returner surpasses the fruit of every once-returner. An arhat surpasses the fruit of every non-returner. A pratyekabuddha surpasses the fruit of every arhat. And a householder who generates the bodhi-mind surpasses the fruit of every pratyekabuddha. For a monastic to generate the bodhi-mind is not difficult; but for a householder to generate the bodhi-mind — this truly deserves to be called inconceivable. Why is this? Because a householder is entangled by so many unwholesome conditions. When a householder generates the bodhi-mind, all the gods, from the Four Heavenly Kings up to Akaniṣṭha, are struck with great astonishment and joy, and say to one another: 'We now have obtained a teacher of gods and humans!'"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu illustrates this ranked comparison with a story he clearly enjoys telling: an arhat possessed of clairvoyant sight was traveling with a young novice monk carrying his pack behind him, when the novice, walking along, suddenly and spontaneously gave rise to the bodhi-mind. The arhat, seeing at once that his young attendant had, in that single thought, already surpassed him, took the pack from the novice's own shoulders and carried it himself, insisting the boy now walk ahead. But the novice's resolve soon wavered — the bodhisattva path, he began to realize, is genuinely difficult — and the moment a thought of retreat crossed his mind, the arhat silently handed the pack straight back and had him walk behind once more. When the bewildered novice finally asked why, the arhat explained plainly: a single thought of the bodhi-mind already outranks an arhat; a single thought of regression already falls back below one.

Taixu reads the chapter's closing point — that householder bodhicitta counts as "inconceivable" precisely because household life is so thickly entangled with worldly temptation — as the sutra's real reason for existing at all, and the reason he is comfortable calling it, informally, the sutra written for householders: a lotus is genuinely remarkable not despite growing out of mud, but precisely because it does.
