Chapter 8 · 優婆塞戒經 · 名義菩薩品第八 · Bodhisattvas in Name and Bodhisattvas in Substance

Bodhisattva in Name, or Bodhisattva in Fact?

Shansheng asks the Buddha a question every practitioner eventually asks of themselves: having taken the bodhisattva vow, how do I know whether I actually am one? The Buddha's answer refuses any comforting shortcut. It lays out, in exacting and cumulative detail, exactly what a vow held in name only looks like — borrowed doctrine, hedged faith, self-pity, a taste for others' bad opinions — set directly against what a vow held in substance looks like: unhesitating generosity, patience that never curdles into resentment, and a mind that has simply stopped keeping score of its own advantage. It closes with a single blunt reminder that this whole sutra is addressed to a layperson, not a monk.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: Shansheng's Question — Two Kinds of Bodhisattva

Shansheng opens the chapter by naming a distinction the Buddha has apparently already introduced elsewhere: there are bodhisattvas in name only, and there are bodhisattvas of true substance. He asks specifically about the first kind, but as the chapter unfolds, the Buddha answers with both — as though the honest answer to "what is a nominal bodhisattva" always has to be given alongside "what is a real one," or it risks being misunderstood.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！如佛所說菩薩二種：一者、假名菩薩，二者、實義菩薩。云何名為假名菩薩？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One! As the Buddha has taught, there are two kinds of bodhisattva: first, the bodhisattva in name only, and second, the bodhisattva of true substance. What is meant by 'the bodhisattva in name only'?"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu places this chapter inside a larger movement of the sutra he calls "accumulating merit and wisdom," and reads its title quite literally: the nominal bodhisattva and the true-substance bodhisattva are distinguished here entirely by their observable characteristics — what they actually do — which is exactly why the chapter is named for the two of them together rather than for any abstract principle.

He also flags something easy to miss in the question itself: Shansheng explicitly asks only about the nominal bodhisattva, yet the Buddha's reply, taken as a whole, plainly covers both kinds. Taixu takes this as evidence that both were already implied in the question — a single distinction only really makes sense once both of its terms are on the table.

2. Milestone: The Nominal Bodhisattva — Borrowed Doctrine, No Real Compassion

The Buddha's first portrait of the nominal bodhisattva is a person who has genuinely taken the vow — and then quietly outsourced their actual practice to something else entirely: outside teachings, self-interest, resentment, and a habit of taking on other people's poison as their own opinions.

Chinese Source

「善男子！眾生若發菩提心已，樂受外術及其典籍，持諷誦讀，即以此法教化眾生，為自身命殺害他命；不樂修悲，樂於生死，常造諸業受生死樂；無有信心，於三寶所生疑網心；護惜身命，不能忍辱，語言麁獷，悔恨放逸；於己身所生自輕想——我不能得無上菩提——於煩惱中生恐怖想，亦不勤修壞結方便；常生慳貪、嫉妬、瞋心，親近惡友，懈怠、亂心，樂處無明；不信六度，不樂修福，不觀生死，常樂受持他人惡語，是名假名菩薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! If a being, having already given rise to the mind of bodhi, delights instead in accepting outside teachings and their texts — upholding, chanting, and reading them — and then uses these very teachings to instruct others; if he kills other living beings for the sake of his own life; takes no joy in cultivating compassion, but delights in birth-and-death, constantly creating karma in pursuit of its pleasures; is without genuine faith, and gives rise to a net of doubt regarding the Three Jewels; guards and cherishes his own body and life and cannot bear to practice patience; whose speech is coarse and wild, and who is given to regret and to laxity; who thinks of himself with contempt — 'I cannot attain unsurpassed bodhi' — and regards his own afflictions with dread, never diligently cultivating the skillful means to break their hold; who constantly gives rise to stinginess, jealousy, and anger; draws close to bad companions; is indolent and scattered in mind, content to dwell in ignorance; who does not believe in the six perfections, takes no joy in cultivating merit, does not truly contemplate birth-and-death, and constantly delights in taking up others' harmful words as his own — this is called the bodhisattva in name only.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu's gloss on this portrait is unsentimental: this is someone who took the bodhisattva vow and then never actually learned or taught within the Buddhadharma itself, relying on outside doctrine instead — going so far, at the extreme, as to happily adopt other people's harmful opinions as though they were his own. That drift away from the actual teaching, more than any single bad act on the list, is what earns the label "nominal."

He glosses several phrases directly: coarse, wild speech is exactly what it sounds like — crude and unruly; "regret" here means the specific regret of having taken the vow and then wishing one hadn't; "self-contempt" means simply not believing oneself capable of ever reaching unsurpassed bodhi. "Not diligently cultivating the skillful means to break the fetters" he reads very concretely — it means not practicing giving as the antidote to stinginess, and not practicing patience as the antidote to jealousy and anger. This, he notes, is one whole type of nominal bodhisattva: someone who has genuinely given rise to bodhicitta but simply never takes up the bodhisattva's actual practice.

3. Milestone: The Nominal Bodhisattva — Faith That Flinches

The Buddha's second portrait describes a different failure mode: not someone who never meant it, but someone whose vow buckles the moment its real cost becomes clear — and who keeps practicing anyway, for reasons that have quietly stopped being about awakening at all.

Chinese Source

「善男子！復有眾生發菩提心，欲得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，聞無量劫苦行修道然後乃得，聞已生悔；雖修行道，心不真實，無有慚愧；不生憐愍。樂奉外道，殺羊祀天；雖有微信，心不堅固，為五欲樂造種種惡，猗色、命、財生大憍慢，所作顛倒，不能利益；為生死樂而行布施，為生天樂受持禁戒，雖修禪定為命增長，是名假名菩薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! There is also the being who gives rise to the mind of bodhi wishing to attain unsurpassed, perfect, complete enlightenment — but on hearing that it is reached only by cultivating the path through austerities across measureless kalpas, gives rise to regret on the spot. Though he goes on practicing the path, his mind is not genuine in it, he is without conscience or shame, and no real compassion arises in him. He delights instead in serving outside paths, killing sheep to sacrifice to heaven. Though he retains a trace of faith, his mind is not firm in it; for the pleasures of the five desires he commits every manner of evil, and relying on his good looks, his longevity, or his wealth, gives rise to great arrogance, so that everything he does is upside-down and brings no real benefit. He practices giving only for the pleasures of birth-and-death, upholds the precepts only to be reborn in heaven, and even his cultivation of meditative concentration serves only to prolong his own life — this too is called the bodhisattva in name only.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this as a second, distinct type: someone whose original vow was never fully sincere to begin with, so that the moment the sheer scale of the path — measureless kalpas of real effort — actually registers, fear and regret follow naturally. He singles out conscience and shame (慚愧) as the single most important quality in practice, and defines the pair precisely: conscience is reverence for good, looking up to the conduct of buddhas and bodhisattvas and measuring oneself honestly against it; shame is refusal of evil, seeing unwholesome conduct — in others or in oneself — and firmly declining it. Together, he notes, this is close to what is simply called having "a sense of shame."

On "killing sheep to sacrifice to heaven," he draws a pointed contemporary parallel: this is no different from Buddhists in his own day who take refuge in the Three Jewels and receive the precepts, yet continue performing sacrificial rites to local gods and spirits on the side — practice and superstition run in parallel rather than the latter yielding to the former. And on giving, precepts, and meditation practiced only for heavenly rebirth or long life, his point is that the actions themselves are not the problem; it is the motive underneath them that quietly converts a real practice into a merely nominal one.

4. Milestone: The Bodhisattva of True Substance — Inner Qualities

Against both portraits of the nominal bodhisattva, the Buddha now sets a single sustained description of the real one — beginning with how such a person actually relates to the teaching itself, to their own resources, and to their own mind.

Chinese Source

「實義菩薩者，能聽深義，樂近善友，樂供養師、父母、善友，樂聽如來十二部經，受持、讀誦、書寫、思義；為法因緣不惜身命、妻、子、財物，其心堅固；憐愍一切，口言柔濡，先語實語，無有惡語及兩舌語；於自身所不生輕想；舒手惠施，無有禁固；常樂修磨利智慧刀，雖習外典，為破邪見、出勝邪見，善知方便調伏眾生；於大眾所不生恐怖，常教眾生菩提易得，能令聞者不生怖心；勤修精進，輕賤煩惱，令彼煩惱不得自在；心不放逸，常修忍辱，為涅槃果持戒、精進；

Ru-Yi Translation

"As for the bodhisattva of true substance: he is able to take in profound meaning, delights in drawing close to good companions, and delights in making offerings to his teachers, his parents, and his good companions. He delights in listening to the Tathāgata's twelve divisions of scripture, upholding, reciting, copying out, and reflecting on their meaning. For the sake of the Dharma he does not begrudge his own body and life, his wife and children, or his wealth — his mind is firm in this. He has compassion for all beings; his speech is gentle and soft, he speaks first and speaks truthfully, and is free of harsh speech and of speech that sets people against one another. Regarding himself he gives rise to no thought of contempt. He opens his hand in giving, without restraint or reservation. He constantly delights in sharpening the sword of wisdom, and though he studies outside texts, he does so only to refute wrong views and rise above them, skillfully knowing the means to tame beings. Amid a great assembly he gives rise to no fear; he constantly teaches beings that bodhi is easy to attain, and is able to keep those who hear him from feeling afraid. He diligently cultivates energetic effort, holds affliction in contempt, and does not let it gain the upper hand over him. His mind is never lax; he constantly cultivates patience, and upholds the precepts and exerts himself for the sake of the fruit of nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this whole passage, start to finish, as an exhortation rather than a static description: progress and regress in practice are never fixed. A nominal bodhisattva who genuinely takes up bodhisattva conduct can become a true-substance one; sudden advance followed by sudden retreat, or the reverse, is simply the ordinary condition of unenlightened beings. His practical advice follows directly from this: if honest self-examination shows you are still living inside the nominal portrait, the remedy is not despair but a single act of conscience and shame, turning the practice forward again — which is enough, on its own, to make one a true-substance bodhisattva.

He glosses several terms concretely. "Profound meaning" is simply the deep meaning of the Dharma itself. "Upholding, reciting, copying, explaining, reflecting on meaning" corresponds to the five practices of a Dharma teacher named in the Lotus Sutra, spelled out more fully elsewhere as ten practices toward a scripture: copying it, making offerings to it, giving it to others, listening to it attentively, reading it, upholding it, expounding it, reciting it, contemplating it, and actually practicing it. Not begrudging wife, children, or wealth for the Dharma's sake, he notes, is exactly the disposition that can carry a practitioner toward the home-leaving, monastic path. The "sword of wisdom" he identifies with the three classical forms of wisdom — from hearing the teaching, from reflecting on it, and from actually cultivating it — each capable of cutting through the conditions that feed affliction. And on "not letting affliction gain the upper hand": he defines mastery here as simply the capacity of one thing to move or control another — if affliction can move a wholesome state of mind, affliction has gained mastery over it; holding affliction in deliberate contempt is exactly how a practitioner denies it that mastery in the first place.

5. Milestone: The Bodhisattva of True Substance — Acting for Others

The description continues outward, from inner disposition to how this bodhisattva actually behaves toward other beings — in generosity, in service, and in a kind of evenness of mind that treats friend and enemy exactly alike.

Chinese Source

「願為眾生趨走給使，令彼安隱、歡娛受樂；為他受苦，心不生悔，見退菩提，心生憐愍；能救一切種種苦惱，能觀生死所有過罪，能具無上六波羅蜜；所作世事，勝諸眾生；信心堅固，修集慈悲，亦不悕求慈悲果報；於怨親中其心無二，施時平等，捨身亦爾，知無常相，不惜身命；以四攝法攝取眾生，知世諦故，隨眾生語；為諸眾生受苦之時，其心不動如須彌山，雖見眾生多作諸惡、有少善者，心終不忘；於三寶所不生疑心，樂為供養，若少財時，先給貧窮，後施福田，先為貧苦，後為富者；樂讚人善，為開涅槃；所有伎藝，欲令人學，見學勝己，生歡喜心；不念自利，常念利他，身、口、意業所作諸善，終不自為，恒為他人，是名實義菩薩。」

Ru-Yi Translation

He is willing to run and serve beings like their own attendant, so that they may be at ease and know real happiness. He bears suffering on others' behalf without any regret, and on seeing someone else fall back from bodhi, feels compassion for them rather than contempt. He is able to rescue beings from every kind of suffering and distress, able to see clearly all the faults inherent in birth-and-death, and able to fully possess the unsurpassed six perfections. In whatever worldly matters he undertakes, he surpasses other beings at them. His faith is firm; he cultivates and gathers loving-kindness and compassion, yet does not hope for any reward in return for it. Toward enemy and kin alike his mind draws no distinction; when he gives, he gives equally, and gives up his own body in the very same spirit, knowing the mark of impermanence and so not clinging to body or life. He draws beings to himself through the four means of gathering, and, understanding conventional truth, speaks to beings in terms they themselves recognize. When he bears suffering for the sake of others, his mind is unmoved, like Mount Sumeru; even seeing beings who do much evil and only the barest trace of good, he never forgets that trace of good in them. Toward the Three Jewels he gives rise to no doubt at all, and delights in making offerings to them; when his own means are scarce, he gives first to the poor and only afterward to fields of merit, attending first to the poor and suffering and only afterward to the wealthy. He delights in praising what is good in others, so as to open the way toward nirvāṇa for them. Whatever skills he himself possesses, he wishes others to learn them, and on seeing a student surpass him, feels nothing but joy. He gives no thought to his own benefit, thinking constantly instead of benefiting others; in every good deed of body, speech, and mind that he performs, he never acts for his own sake, but always for the sake of others — this is called the bodhisattva of true substance."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats "faith" here as specifically faith in the Three Jewels, and "not clinging to body or life" as simply the direct consequence of genuinely knowing that body and life are impermanent — not a special feat of asceticism. He notes that the four means of gathering beings were already explained earlier in the sutra, and glosses "conventional truth" precisely: it is the willingness to treat worldly affairs as real and meaningful on their own terms, which is exactly why this bodhisattva speaks to people in the language they themselves actually use rather than insisting on his own.

On proportionate giving — equal giving when means are ample, the poor prioritized when means are scarce — he adds a note on "skills that a bodhisattva wishes others to learn": this points to the classical five sciences a bodhisattva is meant to pursue — inner wisdom (the Dharma itself), linguistics, logic, medicine, and the practical crafts — with ordinary "skills and arts" corresponding to the latter four. He reads the entire passage, taken as a whole, as a direct exhortation: it is written specifically to encourage the reader to move from the nominal condition toward the real practice it describes.

6. Milestone: Why the Householder's Path Is the Harder One

The chapter ends the way several chapters in this section of the sutra end: with a short, direct word of encouragement, aimed squarely at the audience this entire scripture was actually taught for.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩有二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。出家菩薩為實義菩薩，是不為難；在家菩薩，是乃為難。何以故？在家之人多惡因緣所纏繞故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! There are two kinds of bodhisattva: first, the householder, and second, the one who has left the home. For a home-leaving bodhisattva to become a bodhisattva of true substance is not difficult; for a householder bodhisattva, it is difficult indeed. And why is this? Because a person living at home is bound about, on every side, by so many adverse conditions."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu notes, almost in passing, that a closing line of exactly this kind — a short word of encouragement — appears at the end of every chapter in this part of the sutra, and reads that recurring pattern itself as a sign of just how deep and earnest the Buddha's compassion actually is: never content to end a hard teaching without also leaving behind a reason to keep going.

Read against the sutra's own audience, the line carries real weight. This entire scripture was taught for Shansheng, a layman, not for a monastic assembly — so this closing acknowledgment that the householder's path to true-substance practice is the harder one is not a throwaway remark. It is the Buddha naming, directly and without minimizing it, the actual difficulty of the very audience in front of him.
