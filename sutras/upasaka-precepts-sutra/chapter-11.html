Chapter 11 · 優婆塞戒經 · 自他莊嚴品第十一 · The Chapter on Adorning Self and Other

Eight Rewards, and Who They're Actually For

Shansheng asks the Buddha a deceptively simple question: what does it actually take for a bodhisattva to benefit both himself and others at once? The answer is a list of eight things that sound like ordinary worldly rewards — long life, beauty, strength, high birth, wealth, a male body, eloquence, fearlessness before a crowd — each traced back to its own specific karmic cause and to three underlying purities of giving. But the chapter's real argument only emerges once every one of these eight is reassigned a purpose that has nothing to do with the person who possesses it: each exists solely to be spent on someone else. What follows is one of the sutra's most granular portraits of that spending in practice — patience under slander, reconciling broken friendships, treating every being as a parent — and a closing admission that living this way as a householder is far harder than living it as a monk.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: The Eight Qualities That Adorn Self and Other

The chapter opens with Shansheng asking, plainly, how many qualities a bodhisattva-mahāsattva actually needs in order to benefit both himself and others. The Buddha's answer is a concrete list of eight — six of them the ordinary stuff of worldly good fortune, the last two closer to wisdom itself.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！菩薩摩訶薩具足幾法能自他利？」「善男子！具足八法，能自他利。何等為八？一者、壽命長遠，二者、具上妙色，三者、身具大力，四者、具好種姓，五者、多饒財寶，六者、具男子身，七者、言語辯了，八者、無大眾畏。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One, with how many qualities complete must a bodhisattva-mahāsattva be endowed, to benefit both self and others?" "Good son, being endowed with eight qualities, one can benefit both self and others. What are the eight? First, a long lifespan; second, possessing a supremely excellent form; third, having great bodily strength; fourth, being born into a good lineage; fifth, possessing abundant wealth; sixth, having a male body; seventh, being eloquent in speech; eighth, having no fear before an assembly."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads "how many qualities" quite literally — as a question about how much merit is actually required before someone is genuinely equipped to help anyone besides himself. He splits the eight into two unequal groups: the first six are straightforward merit and blessing (福德), while the last two — eloquence and fearlessness — are closer to wisdom in kind. He is careful that "long life" doesn't mean some superhuman span; he points to the Buddha's own eighty years and Confucius's seventy-odd as the actual measure — long enough that a person's experience, character, and learning have had time to earn the world's respect, which is precisely what then makes helping others possible at all. Bodily strength he glosses bluntly: even buddhas and bodhisattvas need real physical vigor, because a willing heart paired with an exhausted body accomplishes nothing. He compares good lineage to the reverence shown in India toward the Brahmin and Kshatriya castes, and offers a small, sobering story about why fearlessness before a crowd earns its own place on the list: a practitioner he once knew, thoroughly versed in the scriptures and able to teach small gatherings with ease, was invited to lecture from a formal high seat before a large assembly — and froze in sudden terror, unable to speak a word. He was so undone by the humiliation that he sickened afterward and died of it.

2. Milestone: The Karmic Seed Behind Each Quality

Shansheng immediately presses further: by what causes does a bodhisattva actually come to possess these eight things? The Buddha answers item by item, pairing each quality with the specific practice, sustained across countless lifetimes, that produces it.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！何因緣故菩薩得壽命長，乃至大眾不生怖畏？」佛言：「善男子！菩薩摩訶薩無量世中慈心不殺，以是因緣，獲得長壽。無量世中常施衣、燈，以是因緣，獲得上色。無量世中常壞憍慢，以是因緣，生上種姓。無量世中常施飲食，以是因緣，身力具足。無量世中常樂說法，以是因緣，多饒財寶。無量世中呵責女身，以是因緣，得男子身。無量世中至心持戒，以是因緣，言語辯了。無量世中供養三寶，以是因緣，無大眾畏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One, for what causes and conditions does a bodhisattva obtain long life, and so on up to having no fear before an assembly?" The Buddha said: "Good son, because throughout innumerable lifetimes the bodhisattva-mahāsattva has kept a mind of loving-kindness and refrained from killing, by this cause he obtains long life. Because throughout innumerable lifetimes he has constantly given robes and lamps, by this cause he obtains a superior form. Because throughout innumerable lifetimes he has constantly broken down arrogance, by this cause he is born into a superior lineage. Because throughout innumerable lifetimes he has constantly given food and drink, by this cause his bodily strength is complete. Because throughout innumerable lifetimes he has constantly delighted in expounding the Dharma, by this cause he obtains abundant wealth. Because throughout innumerable lifetimes he has reproached the female body, by this cause he obtains a male body. Because throughout innumerable lifetimes he has upheld the precepts with a sincere mind, by this cause his speech is eloquent. Because throughout innumerable lifetimes he has made offerings to the Three Jewels, by this cause he has no fear before an assembly.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu adds a practical caveat here: these causes are not confined to some distant past life — cultivating them now can ripen within this very life as well. He grounds bodily strength in something almost literal: strength is nourished by food, so feeding the hungry earns strength as its direct reward, exactly as teaching the Dharma to others — itself a form of giving — earns wealth. He reads precept-keeping, concentration, and wisdom as one continuous chain, the same sequence the tradition uses elsewhere: genuine precepts settle the mind into concentration, and a settled mind is what allows wisdom, and therefore eloquence, to actually arise.

On the reproach of the female body, Taixu reaches for a Chinese idiom — the "hero among women" (女中丈夫), a woman of unusually forceful, unyielding character — as the type of person this passage has in mind, someone whose vigor itself moves her toward a male body in a future life. He is careful to widen the frame immediately afterward: beyond the desire realm there is no male-or-female distinction at all, the shared dharmakāya of every buddha carries no mark of sex whatsoever, and gender itself belongs only to the desire realm, arising out of craving and attachment — so that, on his own reading, both women and men alike can cultivate toward this particular result. It is worth naming plainly that this passage reflects assumptions about sex and rebirth from a much older world, offered here exactly as Taixu framed it rather than smoothed over. He closes the section with an image for the Three Jewels: unlike the various refuges of the heavens, which are always somehow incomplete, the Buddha's virtue and wisdom, the Dharma's total perfection, and the Sangha's genuine purity together form a rescue vessel amid a stormy sea — the one refuge that is actually seaworthy.

3. Milestone: Three Purities That Perfect Any Act of Giving

Before moving on, the Buddha pauses to ground all eight qualities in something more fundamental: giving itself only produces this kind of result when three things are simultaneously pure — the object given, the mind of the giver, and the one who receives it.

Chinese Source

「如是八事，有三因緣：一者、物淨，二者、心淨，三者、福田淨。云何物淨？非偷盜物，非聖遮物，非眾共物，非三寶物，非施一人迴與多人，非施多人迴與一人，不惱他得，不誑他得，不欺人得，是名物淨。云何心淨？施時不為生死善果、名稱勝他，得色、力、財，不斷家法、眷屬多饒；唯為莊嚴菩提故施，為欲調伏眾生故施，是名心淨。云何福田淨？受施之人遠離八邪，名福田淨。善男子！以如是等三因緣故，八法具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

"These eight matters rest upon three causes and conditions: first, purity of the object given; second, purity of mind; third, purity of the field of merit. What is purity of the object? It is not an object obtained by theft; not an object forbidden by the noble ones; not an object held in common by many; not an object belonging to the Three Jewels; not something given to one person and then redirected to many, nor given to many and then redirected to one; not obtained by troubling others, not obtained by deceiving others, not obtained by cheating others — this is called purity of the object. What is purity of mind? When giving, one does not give for the sake of a good result in the round of birth and death, nor for a reputation surpassing others, nor to obtain fine form, strength, or wealth, nor to keep the family lineage unbroken and one's household numerous — one gives solely to adorn bodhi, and solely in order to tame and guide sentient beings; this is called purity of mind. What is purity of the field of merit? When the one who receives the gift is far removed from the eight wrong ways, this is called purity of the field of merit. Good son, it is by means of these three causes and conditions that the eight qualities become complete.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu unpacks the objects that fail the first test: things "forbidden by the noble ones" include, for instance, the five pungent foods such as onion and garlic, traditionally treated as unclean; "held in common" rules out communal property that was never truly one's own to give; and the redirect clause closes a subtle loophole — regifting something that was itself only just given to you doesn't count as genuine giving at all. On purity of mind, he reads the disqualified motives as a checklist of ordinary self-interest: wanting a better rebirth, wanting to outshine someone else's reputation, wanting personal beauty or strength or wealth, wanting one's own family line to flourish. Set against all of that, he insists the bodhisattva's giving really only has two legitimate motives — adorning the fruit of buddhahood, and taming sentient beings — and nothing beyond these two.

He names the three roles plainly: the object given, the mind of the giver, and the recipient as the actual "field" in which merit grows. The "eight wrong ways" he glosses as the mirror image of the eightfold noble path — wrong view, wrong intention, wrong speech, wrong action, wrong livelihood, wrong effort, wrong mindfulness, wrong concentration — and notes that whoever genuinely walks the right eightfold path belongs to the noble assembly of the three vehicles, making them a field of merit in the fullest sense. He adds that these three purities aren't unique to giving at all — they quietly underlie every wholesome act a bodhisattva undertakes.

4. Milestone: Why the Bodhisattva Seeks Each Quality — Never for Himself

Having explained how the eight qualities are earned, the Buddha now turns to why a bodhisattva wants them at all — and reassigns the purpose of every single one away from the person who possesses it.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩所以求於長命，欲為眾生讚不殺故。菩薩所以求上色者，為令眾生見歡喜故。菩薩所以求上種姓，為令眾生生恭敬故。菩薩所以求具足力，為欲持戒、誦經、坐禪故。菩薩所以求多財寶，為欲調伏諸眾生故。菩薩所以求男子身，為欲成器成善法故。菩薩所以求語辯了，為諸眾生受法語故。菩薩所以求不畏大眾，為欲分別真實法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, the reason the bodhisattva seeks long life is that he wishes to praise the virtue of not killing, for the sake of sentient beings. The reason he seeks a superior form is so that sentient beings may rejoice at the sight of him. The reason he seeks a superior lineage is so that sentient beings may feel reverence toward him. The reason he seeks complete bodily strength is so that he may uphold the precepts, recite the sutras, and sit in meditation. The reason he seeks abundant wealth is so that he may tame and guide sentient beings. The reason he seeks a male body is so that he may become a fit vessel and accomplish wholesome dharmas. The reason he seeks eloquent speech is so that sentient beings may accept his teaching of the Dharma. The reason he seeks fearlessness before an assembly is so that he may distinguish and expound the true Dharma.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu draws special attention to the last item, fearlessness before a crowd, glossing it as a mind "like a great ocean" — calm and unmoved no matter the size or temper of the assembly in front of it — because its entire purpose is to deliver the true Dharma and awaken everyone present, not to protect the speaker's own composure or reputation. Read across the whole list, he treats this section as the chapter's real hinge: every one of the eight qualities named back in the opening milestone — beauty, strength, lineage, wealth, even a male body — has now been stripped of any value it might hold for its own possessor and reassigned entirely to the beings around him. Nothing on the list, on this reading, was ever meant to be kept.

5. Milestone: Virtue That Never Turns to Pride

The Buddha now describes what a bodhisattva who actually holds these eight qualities looks like day to day — beginning with the discipline of never once trading any of them for arrogance.

Chinese Source

「善男子！是故菩薩具足八法能自他利，能如是行，是名實行。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩有八法者，具足受持十善之法，樂以化人；具足受持優婆塞戒，樂以化人。雖得妙色，終不以是而生憍慢；雖持淨戒、多聞、精進、大力、好姓、多饒財寶，終不以此而生憍慢。不以幻惑欺誑眾生，不生放逸，修六和敬。菩薩具足如是等法，雖復在家，不異出家。如是菩薩終不為他作惡因緣，何以故？慚愧堅故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, this is why a bodhisattva who is endowed with these eight qualities is able to benefit both self and others; being able to practice in this way is called true practice. Good son, the bodhisattva-mahāsattva who possesses these eight qualities fully upholds the ten wholesome ways of action, and delights in using them to transform others; he fully upholds the upāsaka precepts, and delights in using them to transform others. Although he attains a fine form, he never grows arrogant on that account; although he upholds pure precepts, has heard much, is diligent, has great strength, a good lineage, and abundant wealth, he never grows arrogant on any of these accounts. He does not deceive or delude sentient beings through illusion and trickery; he does not fall into heedlessness; he cultivates the six points of reverent harmony. A bodhisattva endowed with qualities such as these, though still a householder, is no different from one who has left the household life. Such a bodhisattva never becomes, for another, a cause or condition for evil — why is this? Because his sense of shame and conscience is firm.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu widens "does not grow arrogant" well beyond the classical Confucian ideal of being "wealthy without arrogance" (富而不驕): wealth, high birth, learning, fame, virtue, even wisdom itself — none of it, he insists, licenses looking down on anyone. He draws a sharp distinction between the ten wholesome ways of action, which he treats as ordinary human-and-heavenly virtue, and the upāsaka precepts specifically, which he reads as flowing directly out of the ocean of buddha-nature and therefore capable of serving as an actual stairway out of saṃsāra — which is why the text singles precept-keeping out for special mention here. He glosses "illusion and trickery" as using one's own cleverness or skill to run beguiling manipulations on people, a warning aimed less at outright lying than at more sophisticated forms of deceit.

On the six points of reverent harmony, he notes that monastics keep a formal six-fold harmony precept toward their community, while householders owe the same respect — in body, speech, and mind alike — toward their own circle of family and friends; a householder who genuinely practices this harmonious respect is, in this one specific sense, no different from someone who has left home. He closes on the final line almost as a definition: it is precisely a firm, unwavering sense of shame and conscience that keeps a person from ever becoming, even accidentally, someone else's occasion for wrongdoing.

6. Milestone: The Five Causes of an Unshakeable Bodhisattva

The Buddha now considers a harder case: a bodhisattva reborn into circumstances where the Three Jewels are entirely absent. Even there, the text insists, such a person will not fall — and explains exactly why not.

Chinese Source

「善男子！在家之人，設於一世受持如是優婆塞戒，雖復後生無三寶處，終不造作諸惡因緣。所以者何？二因緣故：一者、智慧，二、不放逸。善男子！於後惡處不作惡事，有四因緣：一者、了知煩惱過故，二者、不隨諸煩惱故，三者、能忍諸惡苦故，四者、不生恐怖心故。菩薩具足如是四法，不為諸苦、一切煩惱之所傾動。善男子！不動菩薩有五因緣：一者、樂修善法，二者、分別善惡，三者、親近正法，四者、憐愍眾生，五者、常識宿命。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, if a person in the household life upholds these upāsaka precepts even for a single lifetime, then even if in a later birth he is born in a place where the Three Jewels do not exist, he will still never create causes and conditions for any evil. Why is this? Because of two causes and conditions: first, wisdom; second, non-heedlessness. Good son, there are four causes and conditions for not committing evil deeds even when later born into an evil place: first, clearly understanding the faults of the afflictions; second, not following after the afflictions; third, being able to bear all manner of evil and suffering; fourth, not giving rise to a mind of terror. A bodhisattva endowed with these four qualities is not shaken by any suffering or by any affliction whatsoever. Good son, the unshakeable bodhisattva has five causes and conditions: first, delighting in cultivating wholesome dharmas; second, discerning good from evil; third, drawing near to the true Dharma; fourth, having compassion for sentient beings; fifth, constantly retaining knowledge of past lives.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats "not doing evil even without the Three Jewels present" as itself the ripened fruit of prior wisdom and non-heedlessness — a kind of moral momentum that outlasts its original conditions. For bearing hardship, he reaches for two classical touchstones: the person "without fixed property yet with an unshakeable resolve" (恆產恆心), and Confucius's noble person who "remains steadfast even in poverty" (君子固窮). For being unshakeable itself, he reaches for two more — Confucius at forty, past all doubt, and Mencius at forty, his mind no longer moved — and notes that within the Buddhist path, bodhisattvas at the three-worthy stages (the ten abodes, ten practices, and ten transferences) can likewise reach this same settled immovability.

He clarifies that "the true Dharma" spans both mundane and world-transcending forms, so drawing near to it is not a narrowly monastic affair. The fifth cause draws his closest attention: constant knowledge of one's own past lives, he stresses, is not something even the wisest human sages achieve, nor even bodhisattvas at the three-worthy stages — it only becomes reliably available from the first ground onward. Naming it here, so early in the list, signals just how high a bar this final, unshakeable stability actually represents.

7. Milestone: A Portrait of the Bodhisattva at Large in the World

The Buddha now widens into one continuous, granular portrait of daily conduct — how a bodhisattva who holds these eight qualities actually behaves under slander, praise, provocation, poverty, and enmity.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩具足如是八法，若聞譏毀，心能堪忍；若聞讚歎，反生慚愧。修行道時，歡喜自慶，不生憍慢。能調惡人，見離壞眾，能令和合。揚人善事，隱他過咎，人所慚恥處，終不宣說，聞他祕事，不向餘說。不為世事而作呪誓。少恩加己，思欲大報；於己怨者，恒生善心，怨親等苦，先救怨者。見有罵者，反生憐愍；見他偷時，默然不動；見來打者，生於悲心。視諸眾生，猶如父母，寧喪身命，終不虛言。何以故？知果報故。於諸煩惱應生怨想，於善法中生親舊想。若於外法生於貪心，尋能觀察貪之過咎；一切煩惱，亦復如是。雖復久與惡人同處，終不於中生親善想；雖與善人不同居止，終不於彼而生遠想。雖復供養父母、師長，終不為是而作惡事。乏財之時，見有求者，不生惡想。雖不親近凶惡之人，而其內心常生憐愍，惡來加已，以善報之。自受樂時，不輕他人；見他受苦，不生歡喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, a bodhisattva endowed with these eight qualities, upon hearing himself slandered, is able to bear it patiently in his heart; upon hearing himself praised, he instead feels shame. When practicing the path, he rejoices and congratulates himself, without growing arrogant. He is able to tame evil people; wherever he sees a group breaking apart, he is able to bring about reconciliation. He proclaims the good deeds of others and conceals their faults; matters that would shame another person he never discloses, and secrets he has heard from others he never repeats to anyone else. He does not curse or swear oaths over worldly affairs. When a small kindness is done to him, he thinks of repaying it greatly; toward those who hold grudges against him, he continually gives rise to a good heart. When an enemy and a friend suffer equally, he rescues the enemy first. When he meets someone who reviles him, he instead feels compassion; when he sees someone stealing, he remains silent and unmoved; when someone comes to strike him, compassion arises in him. He regards all sentient beings as his own parents; he would sooner lose his own life than ever speak a falsehood. Why is this? Because he understands the workings of karmic retribution. Toward the afflictions he gives rise to the thought of an enemy; toward wholesome dharmas he gives rise to the thought of an old friend. If greed for some external thing arises in him, he immediately examines the faults of that greed; and it is the same with every other affliction. Though he may long dwell together with evil people, he never once feels close and friendly toward them on that account; though he may not live in the same place as good people, he never once feels distant from them on that account. Though he makes offerings to his parents and teachers, he never commits an evil act for their sake. In times when he lacks wealth, if someone comes asking of him, he does not give rise to an ill thought. Though he does not draw close to violent and evil people, in his inner heart he continually feels compassion for them; when evil is done to him, he repays it with good. When he himself experiences happiness, he does not look down on others; when he sees another suffering, he does not feel joy.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu is precise about why slander deserves patience rather than defense: since it has no real bearing on genuinely benefiting oneself or others, there is simply nothing at stake worth protecting — and praise, by the same logic, deserves shame instead of pleasure because it almost always outruns the truth. On reconciling broken groups, he widens the lens dramatically: breaking the harmony of a monastic community is discord in miniature; the identical dynamic, scaled up, is what starts civil conflict within a single country, and scaled up again, is what starts war between whole peoples and nations. Bringing about reconciliation, on his reading, is the same skill operating at every scale — which is exactly why it counts as benefiting both self and other. He also draws a line worldly cursing does not cross: oaths over ordinary affairs are forbidden, but transcendent vows — the Four Great Vows, for instance — are exactly what should be made.

On watching theft in silence, Taixu reads the stillness as patience rather than passivity — the correction is simply reserved for later, delivered through genuine teaching rather than confrontation in the moment. And on regarding every being as a parent, he grounds the claim quite literally in the doctrine of beginningless rebirth: across countless past lives, nearly every being now alive has, at some point, actually given us life — so "parent" here is not sentiment but a literal reckoning of a shared, immeasurably long history. The move he keeps circling back to as the chapter's real ethical center is the redirection running through this entire passage: resentment is never aimed outward at another person, only inward at one's own afflictions.

8. Milestone: Purifying Body, Speech, and Mind — The Bodhisattva as Teacher

The portrait now turns from restraint to instruction: how the bodhisattva's own purified conduct, speech, and inner life become, of themselves, a way of teaching everyone around him.

Chinese Source

「身業清淨，持四威儀，即以是法用化眾生；口業清淨，誦讀如來十二部經，即以是法用化眾生；意業清淨，修四無量，亦以是法開化眾生。假身受苦，令他受樂，甘樂為之。世間之事，雖無利益，為眾生故而亦學之；所學之事，世中最勝，雖得通達，心無憍慢，以己所知勤用化人，欲令此事逕世不絕。於親友中不令作惡，樂以上八法教化眾生。說因說果，無有錯謬。愛別離時，心不生惱，觀無常故。受樂受時，心不耽荒，觀苦、無常。善男子！菩薩具足如上八法，則能施作如是等事。

Ru-Yi Translation

"His bodily karma being pure, he upholds the four dignified forms of conduct, and by this very means he transforms sentient beings; his verbal karma being pure, he recites and reads the Thus-Come One's twelve divisions of scripture, and by this very means he transforms sentient beings; his mental karma being pure, he cultivates the four immeasurable states of mind, and by this very means as well he opens and transforms sentient beings. He would gladly take suffering upon his own body so that others might have happiness, and does so willingly. Though worldly skills bring him no benefit of their own, he studies them for the sake of sentient beings; among the things he studies, he masters what is most excellent in the world, and though he attains real mastery, his heart holds no arrogance; with what he himself knows, he diligently transforms others, wishing this benefit to continue unbroken across the generations. Among his own relatives and friends, he does not let them commit evil, and delights in teaching and transforming sentient beings by means of the eight qualities above. He explains causes and explains their fruits without any error. When separated from those he loves, his heart gives rise to no distress, because he contemplates impermanence. When experiencing pleasurable feeling, his heart does not become immersed and lost in it, because he contemplates suffering and impermanence. Good son, a bodhisattva endowed with the above eight qualities is thereby able to carry out deeds such as these."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats the purification of the three karmas here as itself the teaching method, not merely preparation for one: body through the four dignified forms of conduct — walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, each held mindfully — speech through recitation of the Thus-Come One's twelve-fold scriptural canon, and mind through the four immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity. The bodhisattva, on this reading, does not wait until he opens his mouth to begin instructing anyone; his bearing, his recitation, and his inner state are already the lesson. On studying worldly, non-Buddhist skills despite no direct personal benefit, Taixu points to the historical practice of Buddhist teachers deliberately studying non-Buddhist ascetic disciplines, purely so they could meet practitioners of other paths on their own ground and eventually convert them there.

He notes, finally, that the passage closes exactly where the whole portrait began: impermanence and suffering, kept steadily in view, are what let the bodhisattva part from those he loves without anguish and enjoy pleasure without being swept away by it. The entire apparatus built across this chapter — eight qualities, three purities, five causes, this long catalogue of conduct — cashes out, in the end, not in status or reward but in exactly this kind of unshakeable presence inside ordinary human relationships.

9. Milestone: Two Bodhisattvas, One Harder Path

The chapter closes with a single, understated distinction — one that quietly reframes everything just described as being, for most of its readers, considerably more difficult than it might have sounded.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。出家菩薩修是八法，是不為難；在家修集，是乃為難。何以故？在家菩薩多惡因緣所纏繞故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, there are two kinds of bodhisattva: the householder and the renunciant. For a renunciant bodhisattva to cultivate these eight qualities is not difficult; for a householder to gather and cultivate them is difficult indeed. Why is this? Because a householder bodhisattva is bound about by many causes and conditions for evil."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats this closing line as far more than a passing aside — he notes that some version of this same distinction recurs across nearly every chapter of the sutra, making it close to the text's central thesis rather than an incidental remark. Read against the chapter's own title, "Adorning Self and Other," the point lands with some force: everything catalogued here — the eight qualities, the three purities behind them, the five causes of an unshakeable mind, the long portrait of conduct under provocation and loss — was never calibrated for someone insulated from ordinary entanglements. It was calibrated for someone practicing it precisely amid the "many causes and conditions for evil" that a household inevitably supplies, which is exactly what the chapter, in its final line, insists makes succeeding at it so difficult, and so worth taking seriously in its own right.
