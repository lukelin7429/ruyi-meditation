Chapter 28 · 優婆塞戒經 · 般若波羅蜜品第二十八 · The final chapter — the perfection of wisdom, and the sutra's own closing scene

The Perfection That Was There All Along

Shansheng asks one last question: how does a bodhisattva cultivate pure wisdom — prajñā, the sixth and final perfection? The Buddha's answer catalogs, in careful detail, what a wisdom-bearing bodhisattva actually looks like: virtue joined to learning, the three wisdoms born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, and a reach across every classical branch of learning, from scripture to medicine to the practical arts. It then reveals something the whole sutra has been quietly building toward — that wisdom was never one perfection among six, but what actually completes the other five. And with that, the chapter closes the entire text itself: Shansheng and a thousand fellow upāsakas rise from their seats, bow to the Buddha, and take their leave, having genuinely aroused the bodhi mind at last.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: Shansheng's Question — How Does a Bodhisattva Purify Wisdom?

The chapter opens, as so many before it have, with Shansheng putting a direct question to the Buddha — this time about the sixth and last of the six perfections. Taixu notes in passing that "prajñā" is simply the Sanskrit word for wisdom, carried into Chinese by sound rather than translated by meaning, and that this chapter — the perfection-of-wisdom chapter — is deliberately placed as the very last of the entire sutra.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！菩薩云何修淨般若波羅蜜？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One, how does a bodhisattva cultivate the pure perfection of wisdom (prajñā-pāramitā)?"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu observes that some commentators distinguish "wisdom as cause" (慧) from "wisdom as fruit" (智) — the same underlying capacity named differently depending on whether it is still being cultivated or has already ripened into realization. He flags this chapter's placement as no accident: after twenty-seven chapters that have worked through giving, precepts, patience, diligence, and meditative concentration in turn, the sutra closes with wisdom precisely because, as the chapter itself will go on to show, wisdom is what was quietly at work inside all the others from the beginning.

2. Milestone: The Marks of Wisdom — Virtue, Learning, and the Three Wisdoms

The Buddha's answer opens with a long, cumulative portrait of what a wisdom-bearing bodhisattva actually looks like in practice — not a definition so much as a description built clause by clause — before turning to name three classical sources from which wisdom itself arises, and what it means to actually study a scripture rather than merely revere it.

Chinese Source

「善男子！若有菩薩持戒精進，多聞正命，修於忍辱，憐愍眾生，心多慚愧，遠離嫉妬，真實了知諸善方便；為眾受苦，不生悔退，樂行惠施，能調眾生，善知所犯輕重之相，勤勸眾生施作福業；知字知義，心無憍慢，親近善友，能自利益及利益他；恭敬三寶、諸師、和上、長老有德，於身菩提不生輕想，能觀菩提深妙功德；知善惡相，知世、出世一切聲論，知因知果，知初方便及以根本，當知是人能得智慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! Suppose there is a bodhisattva who upholds the precepts and practices with diligence; who is learned and lives by right livelihood; who cultivates patience; who feels compassion for sentient beings, whose mind is rich in a sense of shame, and who stays far from jealousy; who genuinely understands every skillful means for doing good; who undergoes suffering on behalf of others without ever regretting it or turning back; who delights in practicing generosity, who is able to guide and tame sentient beings, who skillfully discerns the marks of light and heavy transgression, and who diligently exhorts sentient beings to perform meritorious deeds; who knows both the letter and the meaning, whose mind is free of arrogance, who draws near to good friends, and who is able to benefit both himself and others; who reveres the Three Jewels, his various teachers, preceptors, and elders of virtue, who does not regard his own path to bodhi lightly, and who is able to contemplate the profound and wondrous merit of bodhi; who knows the marks of good and evil, who knows every treatise on language both mundane and transcendent, who knows cause and knows effect, and who knows the initial skillful means as well as the very root itself — of such a person it should be known that he is able to attain wisdom.

Chinese Source

如是智慧有三種：一、從聞生，二、從思生，三、從修生。從字得義，名從聞生；思惟得義，名從思生；從修得義，名從修生。能讀如來十二部經，能除疑網，能讀一切世論世事，能善分別邪正之道，是名智慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wisdom of this kind is of three types: first, that born from hearing; second, that born from reflection; third, that born from cultivation. Grasping the meaning through the letter is called that born from hearing; grasping the meaning through contemplation is called that born from reflection; grasping the meaning through actual cultivation is called that born from cultivation. Being able to read the Tathāgata's twelvefold canon of scripture, and thereby clear away the net of doubt; being able to read every worldly treatise and worldly affair, and being able to skillfully distinguish the deviant path from the correct one — this is called wisdom.

Chinese Source

能善分別十二部經，陰、界、入等因果字義，毘婆舍那、舍摩他相，上、中、下相，善、惡、無記及四顛倒，見道、修道：能善分別如是等事，是名智慧。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Being able to skillfully distinguish the twelve divisions of scripture; the causal and resultant meaning behind the terms for the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields; the marks of insight (vipaśyanā) and calm (śamatha); the marks of superior, middling, and inferior; wholesome, unwholesome, and indeterminate karma together with the four inverted views; and the path of seeing and the path of cultivation — being able to skillfully distinguish matters such as these is called wisdom."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu maps the three wisdoms directly onto the classical scheme familiar across the tradition: wisdom born from hearing (śruta-mayī), wisdom born from reflection (cintā-mayī), and wisdom born from actual cultivation (bhāvanā-mayī) — three stages, not three unrelated categories, each building on the one before it. He then reads "reading scripture, clearing away doubt" as pointing to what the tradition calls inner learning — study of the Buddhadharma itself — while "reading worldly treatises and worldly affairs" opens out onto the remaining four of the five classical sciences a bodhisattva is expected to master: linguistics, logic, medicine, and the practical arts. Wisdom, on this reading, is never confined to scripture alone; it is sought everywhere a bodhisattva can genuinely learn something true.

3. Milestone: The Merits That Complete Wisdom — Powers, Superknowledges, and the Five Sciences

Having described what wisdom looks like, the Buddha turns to what completes it — an unusually wide-ranging list that reaches from the Buddha's own unique qualities down to incantation-formulas, medicine, and the plain skill of earning and safeguarding money without ever losing sight of the Dharma.

Chinese Source

「善男子！有智之人，求於十力、四無所畏、大悲、三念處，常親近佛及佛弟子。世無佛法，樂在外道出家修學，雖處邪道，樂求正要，常修慈、悲、喜、捨之心及五通道。得五通已，觀不淨想及無常想，能說有為多諸過罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! A person of wisdom seeks the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, great compassion, and the three foundations of mindfulness, and constantly draws near to the Buddha and the Buddha's disciples. In an age when there is no Buddhadharma in the world, such a person is willing to go forth and study even among non-Buddhist paths — for even while dwelling amid a deviant path, he still seeks after what is truly correct and essential. He constantly cultivates the mind of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, together with the path of the five superknowledges. Having attained the five superknowledges, he contemplates the notion of impurity and the notion of impermanence, and is able to explain that all conditioned things are riddled with fault.

Chinese Source

為正語故，教諸眾生令學聲論，能令眾生離身心病，樂以世事教於他人，所作事業無能勝者，所謂呪方、種種醫藥。能善求財，得已能護，用以道理，如法惠施。雖知一切，不生憍慢，得大功德，不生知足，能教眾生信、施、持戒、多聞、智慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

For the sake of correct speech, he teaches sentient beings to study treatises on language, and is able to free sentient beings from ailments of body and mind; he delights in teaching others by means of worldly affairs, and in whatever undertaking he engages, none can surpass him — this refers to incantation-formulas and the various arts of medicine. He is skilled at seeking wealth, and having obtained it, is able to safeguard it, using it in accordance with what is right, and giving it in charity as the Dharma requires. Though he knows all things, he does not give rise to arrogance; having attained great merit, he does not become complacent or feel satisfied, and he is able to teach sentient beings faith, giving, upholding the precepts, learning, and wisdom.

Chinese Source

知善、不善、無記方便，善知學行因緣次第，知菩提道及道莊嚴，知諸眾生上、中、下根，知外聲論心不存著，知眾生時隨宜調伏，知眾生世及國土世，知從具足六波羅蜜。」

Ru-Yi Translation

He knows the skillful means of the wholesome, the unwholesome, and the indeterminate; he skillfully knows the sequence of causes and conditions in study and practice; he knows the path to bodhi and the path's own adornments; he knows the superior, middling, and inferior faculties of every sentient being; he knows treatises on language outside the Dharma without his mind clinging to them; he knows how to tame sentient beings, each according to what is fitting for the time; he knows the world of sentient beings and the world of the land they inhabit; and he knows how, from all of this together, the six perfections come to be fulfilled."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu again traces several items here back to the five classical sciences: treatises on language are linguistics; freeing beings from bodily and mental ailments is medicine; skillful, principled earning of wealth is the science of the practical arts. None of these, he stresses, sit outside the pursuit of wisdom — they are its actual working method, the concrete form a bodhisattva's wisdom takes when it meets the ordinary needs of ordinary beings. He also glosses the pairing near the end with some care: "the world of sentient beings" names the world of living beings themselves, while "the world of the land" names the surrounding physical environment they inhabit — together standing for the whole of space and the entirety of the realm of beings without remainder. A bodhisattva's wisdom, on this reading, is meant to reach every corner of both.

4. Milestone: Wisdom and Perfection — Not Always the Same Thing

The chapter now sharpens a distinction that has been implicit all along: wisdom and "perfection" (pāramitā) are not simply synonyms. A short, exhaustive four-part logical grid separates what is merely wisdom, what is a perfection, what is genuinely both, and — tellingly — what is neither.

Chinese Source

「善男子！有是智慧非波羅蜜，有波羅蜜非是智慧，有是智慧是波羅蜜，有非智慧非波羅蜜。是智慧非波羅蜜者，所謂一切世間智慧，聲聞、緣覺所行智慧。是波羅蜜非智慧者，無有是義。是智慧是波羅蜜者，所謂一切六波羅蜜。非智慧非波羅蜜者，所謂一切聲聞、緣覺施、戒、精進。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! There is wisdom that is not a perfection; there is a perfection that is not wisdom; there is that which is both wisdom and a perfection; and there is that which is neither wisdom nor a perfection. That which is wisdom but not a perfection refers to every kind of worldly wisdom, and the wisdom practiced by śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas. That which is a perfection but not wisdom — there is no such thing. That which is both wisdom and a perfection refers to all six perfections together. That which is neither wisdom nor a perfection refers to the giving, precept-keeping, and diligence practiced by śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu draws out the asymmetry buried in this grid: of the four positions, one is flatly declared empty — there is no such thing as a genuine perfection that is not wisdom. Whatever actually rises to the level of "perfection" is, by that very fact, already wisdom; nothing else in the list gets this same guarantee. This, he says, is exactly what sets prajñā apart from the five perfections treated in the chapters before it. Giving, precepts, patience, diligence, and meditative concentration can each be practiced by a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha and still fall short of being a true pāramitā — but once wisdom is genuinely present, that same giving or precept-keeping is lifted into perfection. From this, Taixu concludes, it becomes clear that the accomplishment of all five earlier perfections was never actually separate from prajñā to begin with; wisdom was the thing quietly completing each of them from within, chapter after chapter, all the way back to the first.

5. Milestone: Cultivating All Six Together — the True Veneration of the Six Directions

The chapter draws its teaching on wisdom to a close with a single, compact statement — one that reaches back explicitly to a promise made at the very start of the sutra, in the chapter on arousing the bodhi mind, about what actually increases a person's wealth and lifespan.

Chinese Source

「善男子！若人有能勤修如是六波羅蜜，是人名為供養六方，能增財、命。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! If a person is able to diligently cultivate these six perfections, this person is said to venerate the six directions, and is truly able to increase both wealth and lifespan."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this as a deliberate echo of the sutra's own opening promise: what is actually increased here is not merely worldly wealth and worldly lifespan, but the deeper wealth of accumulated merit and the deeper life-force of wisdom itself — and carried to its ultimate conclusion, this same increase culminates in Buddhahood, "the one honored and complete in both blessedness and wisdom" (福慧兩足尊). Twenty-six chapters after Chapter 2 first raised the ordinary wish for a longer life and more wealth as one entry point into arousing the bodhi mind, the sutra closes the loop: the six perfections, practiced together and completed by wisdom, are what that wish was pointing toward all along.

6. Milestone: Householder and Renunciant — Why the Lay Path Is Harder

Before the sutra ends, the Buddha adds one plain, unsentimental observation about who is actually undertaking this practice — an observation that speaks directly to Shansheng and to every lay disciple this entire sutra has been addressed to from its first chapter onward.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。出家菩薩修淨智慧，是不為難；在家修淨，是乃為難。何以故？在家之人多惡因緣所纏遶故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! Bodhisattvas are of two kinds: first, the householder; second, the one who has gone forth from home. For a bodhisattva who has gone forth, cultivating pure wisdom is not difficult; for one who remains a householder, cultivating this same purity is truly difficult. Why is this so? Because a person living at home is bound about by many evil causes and conditions."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu is brief here, treating the line as needing little elaboration: it simply demonstrates, in the Buddha's own words, how difficult it genuinely is for a householder bodhisattva to cultivate the perfection of wisdom. Read in the context of the whole sutra, though, the observation carries real weight — this entire twenty-eight-chapter teaching has been addressed to Shansheng precisely as a householder, not as someone who has renounced the world, and this closing acknowledgment of the lay path's particular difficulty reads less as a warning and more as an honest recognition of exactly whom the sutra was written for all along.

7. Milestone: The Sutra's Ending — A Thousand Upasakas Arouse the Bodhi Mind

The sutra closes with its own narrative frame: not a doctrinal summary, but a scene. Shansheng and a thousand fellow lay disciples, having heard this whole teaching through to its end, actually arouse the bodhi mind on the spot — and the text simply lets them rise, bow, and walk home.

Chinese Source

說是法時，善生長者子等千優婆塞發阿耨多羅三藐三菩提心。既發心已，即從坐起，禮佛而退，辭還所止。

Ru-Yi Translation

When this teaching was spoken, Shansheng, son of the elder, together with a thousand upāsakas, aroused the mind set upon unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening (anuttarā-samyak-saṃbodhi). Having aroused this mind, they at once rose from their seats, bowed to the Buddha, took their leave, and returned to their dwellings.

Chinese Source — closing colophon

優婆塞戒經卷第七

Ru-Yi Translation

End of Fascicle Seven of the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats this final scene as the demonstrated proof of the entire sutra's effectiveness — its actual result, made visible in Shansheng and the thousand upāsakas who were present to hear it. He draws a careful distinction that reframes everything the sutra has done up to this point: the bodhi mind aroused here, at the very end, is the true bodhi mind, marking real entry into the first of the ten stages of abiding as an actual bodhisattva — while the eighteen chapters that came before it, including the "arousing the bodhi mind" chapter near the sutra's opening, were only ever cultivating the capacity that made this final, genuine arousal possible. Citing the Awakening of Faith, he notes that ten thousand great kalpas of prior cultivation are traditionally said to precede true bodhicitta; only a bodhisattva who has actually entered the first abiding can begin the bodhisattva's correct practice of the six perfections at all. Everything this sutra has taught, in other words, was preparation for the single moment the closing lines now record.

He reads the assembly's final gesture symbolically, and in two directions at once: rising and bowing to the Buddha is the movement of seeking great awakening above; taking leave and returning home is the movement of going down to liberate sentient beings below — and this double movement, he insists, does not stop once Buddhahood is reached. It simply is what being a bodhisattva has always meant, before and after enlightenment alike. His closing observation reads as fitting words for the sutra's own final line: because space itself is inexhaustible, the worlds within it are inexhaustible; because worlds are inexhaustible, sentient beings are inexhaustible; and because sentient beings are inexhaustible, a bodhisattva's work of saving them is, in exactly the same way, inexhaustible. The text ends here. The practice it describes does not.
