Chapter 22 · 優婆塞戒經 · 五戒品第二十二 · The five precepts — from taking refuge to why the householder's path is the harder one

What Actually Makes a Precept Yours

Shansheng opens with a deceptively simple question: what actually separates someone who has genuinely attained the three refuges from someone who merely went through the motions? The Buddha's answer becomes the frame for the whole chapter — precepts of two kinds, worldly and of ultimate truth, the first as fragile as paint applied without any glue to hold it; the specific reasoning behind singling out wine among the five precepts; a long, plainspoken checklist of what does and does not count as actually "dwelling in accord with the Dharma"; and a closing meditation on trading a fragile body, fragile wealth, and a fragile life for their enduring counterparts. It ends on a note far from triumphant: keeping any of this, the text insists bluntly, is genuinely harder for someone still living at home than for someone who has already left it behind.

1. Milestone: Shansheng's Question — Who Actually Attains the Three Refuges?

The chapter opens with a question that sets up everything to follow: since the five precepts can only be received on the foundation of refuge already taken, Shansheng first asks what actually separates a person who has genuinely attained the three refuges from one who has not.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！何等之人得三歸依？何等之人不得三歸？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One, what kind of person attains the three refuges, and what kind of person does not attain the three refuges?"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu points out why the chapter has to begin exactly here: the five precepts require the three refuges as their prior foundation, so before anything else can be said about the precepts themselves, it must first be settled what genuinely counts as having taken refuge at all. The question is not idle formality — it is the doorway the whole chapter has to pass through before it can say anything else.

2. Milestone: How the Three Refuges Are Actually Attained

The Buddha's answer locates refuge not in a ceremony performed but in a conviction actually held — belief in cause and effect, sincere trust in the Three Jewels, and a felt sense of what unwholesome conduct actually costs.

Chinese Source

「善男子！若人信因、信果、信諦、信有得道，如是之人，則得三歸；若人至心信不可敗，親近三寶，受善友教，如是之人，則得三歸；優婆塞戒，亦復如是。若能觀是優婆塞戒多有無量功德果報，能壞無量弊惡之法。眾生無邊，受苦亦爾，難得人身；雖得人身，難具諸根；雖具諸根，難得信心；雖得信心，難遇善友；雖遇善友，難得自在；雖得自在，諸法無常。我今若造惡業，因是惡業獲得二世身心惡報，以是因緣，身、口、意惡即是我怨。設三業惡不得惡報，現在之惡亦不應作。是三惡業，現在能生弊惡色等，死時生悔，以是因緣，我受三歸及八齋法，遠離一切惡不善業。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! If a person believes in cause, believes in effect, believes in the truths, and believes that the path can genuinely be attained — such a person attains the three refuges. If a person, with utmost sincerity, believes that the Three Jewels can never be destroyed, draws near to them, and accepts the instruction of good friends — such a person likewise attains the three refuges; and it is exactly the same with the upāsaka precepts. Such a person is able to contemplate that these upāsaka precepts carry immeasurable merit and reward, and are able to destroy countless unwholesome dharmas. Sentient beings are without limit, and the suffering they undergo is, in just the same way, without limit; a human body is hard to obtain, and even once obtained, it is hard to have every faculty intact; even with every faculty intact, it is hard to gain a mind of faith; even having gained faith, it is hard to meet good friends; even having met good friends, it is hard to gain the freedom to practice; and even having gained that freedom, all dharmas remain impermanent. If I now create unwholesome karma, then because of this unwholesome karma I will reap unwholesome retribution in body and mind across two further lifetimes — and for this reason, unwholesome deeds of body, speech, and mind are truly my own enemy. Even supposing these three kinds of unwholesome karma brought no evil retribution at all, evil ought still not to be done here and now, for these three unwholesome karmas are already, in the present itself, capable of producing a wretched and unwholesome countenance, and at the moment of death they give rise to regret. For this reason I take the three refuges and the eight-precept fast, and keep far away from every unwholesome, unskillful deed."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this whole answer as locating refuge on the level of the mind rather than in any outward act: it is attained through genuine understanding of cause and effect and their fruits, not through a ritual correctly performed. He stresses what that understanding actually produces in someone who has it — a person who truly sees how karma works, the moment the slightest unwholesome impulse stirs, immediately turns on themselves with self-reproach, feels real dread at what they were about to do, and lets shame and regret dissolve the impulse before it can become an act. That immediate, felt reaction, he suggests, is the real test of whether refuge has actually taken hold, far more than any words recited at a ceremony.

3. Milestone: Two Kinds of Precept — Worldly, and of Ultimate Truth

Having settled what refuge actually requires, the Buddha turns to a distinction that runs through the rest of the chapter: precepts taken without first taking refuge in the Three Jewels are merely "worldly," and — however identical they may look on the surface to the precepts of ultimate truth — they lack the power to do what a real precept is supposed to do.

Chinese Source

「智者當觀戒有二種：一者、世戒，二、第一義戒。若不依於三寶受戒，是名世戒，是戒不堅，如彩色無膠；是故我先歸依三寶，然後受戒。若終身受，若一日一夜，所謂優婆塞戒、八戒齋法。夫世戒者，不能破壞先諸惡業；受三歸戒，則能壞之，雖作大罪，亦不失戒。何以故？戒力勢故。俱有二人同共作罪，一者受戒，二不受戒，已受戒者犯則罪重，不受戒者犯則罪輕。何以故？毀佛語故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"A wise person should observe that precepts are of two kinds: first, worldly precepts; second, precepts of ultimate truth. Precepts received without relying on the Three Jewels are called worldly precepts — such precepts are not firm, like paint applied without any glue to bind it. This is why I first take refuge in the Three Jewels, and only afterward receive the precepts, whether received for the whole of one's life or for a single day and night, as with the upāsaka precepts and the eight-precept fast. Worldly precepts, as such, are unable to break apart previously accumulated unwholesome karma; but taking the three refuges together with the precepts is able to break it apart — even if a great transgression has been committed, the precept itself is not thereby lost. Why is this? Because of the sheer power inherent in the precept. Suppose two people together commit an identical transgression, one having received the precepts and the other not: for the one who has received the precepts, the offense is heavier; for the one who has not, the offense is lighter. Why is this? Because he has violated the Buddha's own word.

Chinese Source

「罪有二種：一者、性重，二者、遮重。是二種罪，復有輕重。或有人能重罪作輕，輕罪作重，如鴦掘魔受於世戒，伊羅鉢龍受於義戒；鴦掘魔羅破於性重不得重罪，伊羅鉢龍壞於遮制而得重罪。是故有人重罪作輕，輕罪作重，是故不應以戒同故得果亦同。世戒亦有不殺、不盜，義戒亦有不殺、不盜；至不飲酒，亦復如是。如是世戒根本不淨，受已不淨，莊嚴不淨，覺觀不淨，念心不淨，果報不淨，故不得名第一義戒，唯名世戒；是故我當受於義戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Transgressions, too, are of two kinds: first, transgressions grave by their own nature; second, transgressions grave only by prohibition. Between these two kinds of transgression there is, again, a further distinction of heavier and lighter. Indeed, there are cases in which a naturally grave transgression turns out light, and a merely prohibited transgression turns out grave — as with Aṅgulimāla, who had received only the worldly precepts, and the nāga Elāpattra, who had received the precepts of ultimate truth: Aṅgulimāla broke a naturally grave precept, yet did not incur the graver offense, while Elāpattra violated a merely prohibitory rule and yet incurred the graver offense. This shows that a grave transgression can turn out light, and a light transgression can turn out grave — one should therefore not assume that because a precept is nominally the same, the fruit it produces must be the same as well. Worldly precepts, too, include not killing and not stealing; the precepts of ultimate truth likewise include not killing and not stealing, and this holds all the way to not drinking wine as well. Yet worldly precepts remain impure at their very root — impure in the receiving, impure in their outward adornment, impure in the reflection that accompanies them, impure in the mindfulness behind them, and impure in their eventual fruit. For this reason they cannot be called precepts of ultimate truth, but only worldly precepts — and this is why I take up the precepts of ultimate truth.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats this whole passage as distinguishing precepts as ordinary people keep them from precepts as the Buddha teaches them — and he draws the practical moral out plainly: because the merit produced by receiving genuine precepts is so much greater, the transgression of breaking them is correspondingly heavier, so a precept-holder has all the more reason for caution, not less. He identifies the two figures by name: Aṅgulimāla — literally "Finger Garland" — was a notorious killer before his conversion, and the nāga Elāpattra was a dragon-king whose story turns on how a single act of destruction, trivial-seeming on its surface, still carried the weight of a broken vow. "Precepts of ultimate truth," he adds, is simply another name for the Buddha's own genuine precepts, as opposed to the worldly kind that merely resemble them.

4. Milestone: In Praise of the Precepts' Virtue

Having distinguished the two kinds of precept, the Buddha turns to extolling what the genuine kind actually accomplishes — reward equal across every age, a fivefold structure that maps directly onto the five precepts themselves, and a story about a rich man's son that proves the precepts still deliver even when taken for the shallowest of reasons.

Chinese Source

「善男子！後世眾生身長八丈，壽命滿足八萬四千歲，是時受戒，復有於今惡世受戒，是二所得果報正等。何以故？三善根平等故。或有說言『可斷命處乃得戒』者，是義不然！何以故？夫禁戒者，悉於一切可殺、不可殺中得，一切可殺、不可殺者無量無邊，戒之果報亦復如是無量無邊。善男子！一切施中，施無怖畏最為第一，是故我說五大施者，即是五戒。如是五戒，能令眾生離五怖畏，是五種施，易可修行，自在無礙，不失財物，然得無量無邊福德。離是五施，不能獲得須陀洹果乃至得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。善男子！若受戒已，當知是人為諸天人恭敬守護，得大名稱，雖遭惡對，心無愁惱，眾生親附，樂來依止。阿那邠坻長者之子，雖為八千金錢受戒，亦得無量功德果報。善男子！為財受戒，尚得利益，況有至心為於解脫而當不得？

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! In a future age, when beings will stand eighty spans tall and live out a full lifespan of eighty-four thousand years, precepts received at that time and precepts received now, in this evil age, yield exactly equal reward. Why is this? Because the three wholesome roots involved are equal in either case. Some say that a precept is only truly attained where there exists something one is actually capable of killing — this is not so! Why is this? Because moral restraint of any kind is attained across the whole range of what can and cannot be killed, and what can and cannot be killed is itself immeasurable and boundless, so the reward of the precept is, in just the same way, immeasurable and boundless. Good son! Among every kind of giving, the giving of freedom from fear is foremost of all — this is why I say that the five great gifts are none other than the five precepts. These five precepts are able to free sentient beings from five kinds of fear; and these five kinds of giving are easy to practice, unobstructed and free, cost no loss of wealth, and yet yield immeasurable, boundless merit. Apart from these five gifts, no one can attain the fruit of stream-entry, still less unsurpassed, complete awakening. Good son! Know that once a person has received the precepts, they are honored and protected by gods and humans alike, gain great renown, and even when meeting with hostility, their mind knows no grief or vexation; sentient beings draw near to them and are glad to take refuge in them. The son of the elder Anāthapiṇḍika received the precepts for a mere eight thousand gold coins, and even so obtained immeasurable merit and reward. Good son! If receiving the precepts for the sake of wealth already brings such benefit, how much more so for one who, with utmost sincerity, receives them for the sake of liberation?

Chinese Source

「善男子！有五善法圍遶是戒，常得增長，如恒河水。何等為五？一者、慈，二者、悲，三者、喜，四者、忍，五者、信。若人能破慇重邪見，心無疑網，則具正念，莊嚴清淨，根本清淨，離惡覺觀。善男子！若人能遠五惡事者，是名受戒，遠離一切身、口、意惡。若有說言『離五戒已度生死』者，無有是處！善男子！若人欲度生死大海，應當至心受持五戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Good son! There are five wholesome dharmas that surround this precept and cause it to keep growing, the way the water of the Ganges keeps flowing. What are the five? First, loving-kindness; second, compassion; third, sympathetic joy; fourth, patience; fifth, faith. If a person can break through deep-seated wrong views, so that the mind is free of the net of doubt, then right mindfulness is complete, its outward adornment is pure, its very root is pure, and it is free of unwholesome reflection. Good son! A person able to keep far from these five kinds of unwholesome deed is said to have truly received the precepts, keeping far from every unwholesome deed of body, speech, and mind. If someone says, "One can cross beyond birth and death without the five precepts" — there is no such possibility! Good son! If a person wishes to cross the great ocean of birth and death, they should receive and uphold the five precepts with utmost sincerity.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this section simply as praising the actual virtue of holding the precepts — but two details are worth drawing out. First, the claim that reward stays equal across radically different ages rests entirely on the three wholesome roots — freedom from greed, from hatred, and from delusion — being what the reward actually tracks, not the external difficulty or ease of the circumstances a precept-holder happens to live in. Second, the story of Anāthapiṇḍika's son is deliberately provocative: even a payment of eight thousand gold coins, an obviously mixed and self-interested motive for receiving the precepts, still produced real merit — which the passage uses to argue, from the weaker case to the stronger one, that a person receiving the identical precepts out of genuine longing for liberation cannot possibly receive less.

5. Milestone: The Wine Precept, and the Three Fast-Days

The Buddha now addresses two objections directly: why should wine, of all things, be singled out among the five precepts, and why does the sutra retain the old custom of three fast-days a month, a practice originally borrowed from non-Buddhist observance?

Chinese Source

是五戒中，四於後世成無作戒，唯愛難斷故不得成；以是因緣，婬欲纏綿，應當至心慎無放逸！若有說言：更有無量極重之法，過去諸佛何緣不制，而制於酒？善男子！因於飲酒，慚愧心壞，於三惡道不生怖畏，以是因緣，則不能受其餘四戒，是故過去諸佛如來制不聽飲。若有說言『如來已說酒多過失，何故不在五戒初說？』是義不然！何以故？如是酒戒，名為遮重，不為性重；如來先制性重之戒，後制遮重。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these five precepts, four become, in future lives, a "non-active" precept that continues to work of its own accord — only the precept against sexual misconduct cannot fully become so, because craving is so hard to cut off. For this reason, sexual desire remains entangling, and one should be utterly sincere and heedful, never indulgent. Someone may say: there exist countless transgressions far graver than this — why did the Buddhas of the past not prohibit those instead, and prohibit wine? Good son! Because of drinking wine, the sense of conscience and shame is destroyed, and one no longer feels any dread of the three evil destinies; for this reason, one becomes unable to keep the other four precepts as well — this is why the Buddhas Tathāgata of the past prohibited its consumption. Someone may also say: "The Tathāgata has already explained wine's many faults — why, then, is it not listed first among the five precepts?" This, too, is not so. Why is this? Because the wine precept is what is called grave by prohibition, not grave by its own nature; and the Tathāgata always sets forth the precepts that are grave by nature first, and only afterward sets forth those that are grave by prohibition.

Chinese Source

「善男子！如來先說白、黑月中各有三齋，隨外道故；諸外道輩常以此日供養諸天，是故如來說有三齋。善男子！如因帳窓，帳勒故不墮，三齋之法，亦復如是，眾生若有發心受持，終不墮於三惡道中。善男子！有人若欲施時、供養三寶時、若坐禪時、若修善時、若讀經時、供養父母時，當先立制，我若不作，要自剋罰；是人福德日夜增長，如恒河流。如是五戒有五種果：一者、無作果，二者、報果，三者、餘果，四者、作果，五者、解脫果。若有具足受持五戒，當知是人得是五果。若優婆塞常能出至寺廟僧坊，到已，親近諸比丘等：既親近已，諮問法味；既問法已，當至心聽；聽已受持，憶念不忘，能分別義；分別義已，轉化眾生，是名優婆塞自利利他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Good son! The reason the Tathāgata earlier spoke of three fast-days within each bright half-month and each dark half-month of the lunar cycle was in accommodation to non-Buddhist paths — for the followers of those paths regularly make offerings to the gods on these very days, and so the Tathāgata, too, spoke of three fast-days. Good son! Just as a curtain stays up because of the cord that holds it taut, so it is with the practice of the three fast-days: if sentient beings resolve to undertake it, they will never, in the end, fall into the three evil destinies. Good son! Suppose a person, whenever about to give a gift, make offerings to the Three Jewels, sit in meditation, cultivate a wholesome deed, read a sutra, or make offerings to their parents, first sets themselves a rule — "If I fail to do this, I must punish myself for it" — such a person's merit grows day and night, like the flow of the Ganges. In this way the five precepts yield five kinds of fruit: first, the "non-active" fruit; second, the fruit of retribution; third, an incidental fruit; fourth, the fruit of the deed itself; fifth, the fruit of liberation. Anyone who receives and upholds the five precepts in full should know that they attain all five of these fruits. If an upāsaka is regularly able to go out to temples and monastic dwellings, and once there draw near to the monks; and having drawn near to them, ask about the flavor of the Dharma; and having asked, listen with the utmost sincerity; and having listened, retain what was heard and never forget it, able to discern its meaning; and having discerned its meaning, go on to transform other sentient beings with it — such a person is called an upāsaka who benefits both himself and others.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu explains the "non-active" precept technically: the moment a precept is properly received, its power is stored in the depths of consciousness as a kind of latent force, and from then on it quietly governs conduct even when a person is not deliberately thinking about it at all — much as someone who has vowed to give up meat eventually finds the smell itself unappealing, without any fresh act of will. He grounds why the precept against sexual misconduct alone resists becoming fully "non-active" in this life in the teaching that beings of the desire realm sustain their very existence through sexual desire itself — so the craving behind it runs deeper than an act of will can fully uproot, and only the fruit of non-returning finally exhausts it.

On wine, he underscores the structural point: it is classed as grave only by prohibition rather than grave by its own nature, yet the Buddha singles it out because it disables the very faculty — conscience and shame — that keeps every other precept standing, so it functions as a kind of master switch even though it is not, in itself, the worst offense on any absolute scale. He also glosses the fast-day dates precisely — the eighth, fourteenth, and fifteenth of the bright half-month, and the twenty-third, twenty-ninth, and thirtieth of the dark half-month — and reads "self-imposed penalty" as the single most practical instruction in the whole passage: setting one's own binding rule before an act of virtue is, in his view, the real engine that keeps ordinary resolve from quietly dissolving over time.

6. Milestone: What Actually Counts as Dwelling in Accord with the Dharma

The chapter now turns unusually concrete, running through one plainspoken case after another of ordinary conduct that either does or does not count as truly "dwelling in accord with the Dharma" — a term that will recur through the rest of the chapter.

Chinese Source

「若優婆塞不能習學如是所說，輕慢比丘，為求過失而往聽法，無信敬心，奉事外道，見其功德，深信日、月、五星、諸宿，是優婆塞不名堅固如法住也。

若優婆塞雖不自作五惡之業，教人作者，是優婆塞非如法住也。

若優婆塞先取他物，許為了事，是優婆塞非如法住。

若優婆塞典知官津，稅賣估物，是優婆塞非如法住。

若優婆塞計價治病，治已賣物，是優婆塞非如法住。

若優婆塞違官私制，非如法住。

若優婆塞自不作惡，不教他作，心不念惡，名如法住。

若優婆塞因客煩惱所起之罪，作已不生慚愧悔心，非如法住。

若優婆塞為身命故，作諸惡事，非如法住。

若優婆塞雖得人身，行於非法，不名為人。

Ru-Yi Translation

"If an upāsaka is unable to study and practice what has just been taught, treats monks with contempt, attends a Dharma talk only looking for fault, has no mind of faith and reverence, serves non-Buddhist teachers, regards their supposed virtues as genuine, and places deep faith in the sun, the moon, the five planets, and the lunar mansions — such an upāsaka cannot be called one who dwells firmly in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka, though not personally committing any of the five unwholesome deeds, teaches others to commit them — such an upāsaka does not dwell in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka first takes another's property, promising to settle the matter later — such an upāsaka does not dwell in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka takes charge of a government checkpoint or ferry crossing and taxes goods being bought and sold there — such an upāsaka does not dwell in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka sets a price before treating a patient's illness, and having treated them, sells off their belongings to collect it — such an upāsaka does not dwell in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka violates the regulations of the government or of a private agreement — this is not dwelling in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka neither commits evil himself, nor teaches others to commit it, nor even lets the mind dwell on evil — this is called dwelling in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka, having committed a transgression brought on by some incidental affliction, feels afterward no shame, remorse, or regret — this is not dwelling in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka commits any evil deed for the sake of his own body or life — this is not dwelling in accord with the Dharma.

If an upāsaka, though having obtained a human body, conducts himself contrary to the Dharma — he cannot truly be called a human being.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu defines the governing term simply: whoever practices in accordance with the Buddha's teaching is said to "dwell in accord with the Dharma," and whoever does not is said to fail to. He pays particular attention to the phrase "transgression brought on by incidental affliction," reading "incidental" precisely: an affliction that arises from outside circumstance, or as the residue of habits carried over from a former life, rather than something the present, conscious mind actually wanted or chose. Such an affliction, he stresses, can still produce real wrongdoing — but the marker of genuine practice is what happens immediately afterward: shame and regret that show the deed was never truly endorsed by the person committing it, as opposed to the case where no such regret ever arises at all.

7. Milestone: The Rarity of a Human Life

From the specific checklist of right and wrong conduct, the chapter widens into a sustained reflection on how genuinely rare it is to hold a human life capable of hearing and practicing the Dharma at all — a chain of "even so, still hard" clauses meant to be felt, not merely read.

Chinese Source

「若得信心，能作福德，善修正念，觀一切法皆是無常、無我、我所，於一切法心無取著，見一切法不得自在，生滅、苦、空、無有寂靜；人身難得，雖得人身，難具諸根；雖具諸根，難得正見；雖具正見，難得信心；雖得信心，難遇善友；雖遇善友，難聞正法；雖聞正法，難得受持：能如是觀，是名人身。

Ru-Yi Translation

"If a person has gained faith, is able to perform meritorious deeds, and cultivates right mindfulness well, contemplating that all dharmas are impermanent, without self, and without anything belonging to a self; whose mind clings to and grasps at no dharma whatsoever; who sees that no dharma has any true freedom of its own, but is arising and ceasing, suffering, empty, and utterly without stillness; who knows that a human body is hard to obtain, and that even once obtained, it is hard to have every faculty intact; that even with every faculty intact, it is hard to gain right view; that even with right view, it is hard to gain faith; that even having gained faith, it is hard to meet good friends; that even having met good friends, it is hard to hear the true Dharma; and that even having heard the true Dharma, it is hard to receive and uphold it — a person able to contemplate in just this way is the one who is truly said to have obtained a human body.

Reading note

The passage's logic is cumulative rather than merely emphatic: each clause does not simply repeat "this is hard" but adds a further condition on top of the one before it, so that the improbability compounds at every step — a human body, then intact faculties within that body, then right view arising within those faculties, then faith taking hold of that view, then a good friend actually met, then the true Dharma actually heard from that friend, and only then the far harder task of genuinely receiving and holding onto what was heard. Read this way, "obtaining a human body" in the sutra's own sense is not the biological fact of having been born human at all, but the entire unlikely chain arriving all the way to its final link — which is exactly why the text insists that contemplating the chain in full, not merely being alive, is what actually earns the name.

8. Milestone: Trading the Fragile for the Enduring

Having established how rare and precious this condition is, the sutra turns practical: three things a person actually has — body, wealth, and life — are each, in their ordinary form, fragile and passing, and the chapter describes exactly how each can be exchanged for an enduring counterpart of the same kind.

Chinese Source

「若人能觀欲界無常，乃至非想非非想處皆悉無常，以是因緣，不求三惡乃至非想非非想處。如是觀已，見三不堅，以不堅身易於堅身：禮拜供養，來迎去送，自手施與，親執福事，是名以不堅身易於堅身。以不堅財易於堅財：能自食用亦以布施，供給病瘦、行路之人，供養沙門、婆羅門等貧窮下賤，是名以不堅財易於堅財。以不堅命易於堅命：修於六念、慈悲喜捨，證四真諦，善能觀察生老病死，明信善惡業之果報，定知恩愛當有別離，一切眾生不得自在，未得聖道，生死力大，一切世樂常與苦俱，雖復受之，心不染著，猶如寒月求火自煖，雖復為之，終不作惡；修忍二施以潤眾生，深觀苦樂其性平等；凡所發言，言則柔軟，善化眾生，令如法住，遠離惡友，心無放逸，飲酒、博弈、射獵之事悉不為之，是名以不堅之命易於堅命。

Ru-Yi Translation

"If a person is able to contemplate that the desire realm is impermanent, and that all the way up through the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, everything without exception is impermanent, then for this very reason they will seek neither the three evil destinies nor even the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. Having contemplated in this way, they come to see that three things are fragile, and exchange each fragile thing for its enduring counterpart. They exchange the fragile body for the enduring body: by paying reverence and making offerings, by welcoming arriving guests and seeing off departing ones, by giving with their own hand, and by personally taking up works of merit — this is called exchanging the fragile body for the enduring body. They exchange fragile wealth for enduring wealth: by using it for their own sustenance yet also giving it away, supplying the sick, the frail, and travelers on the road, and making offerings to renunciants, brahmins, and those who are poor and lowly — this is called exchanging fragile wealth for enduring wealth. They exchange a fragile life for an enduring life: by cultivating the six recollections, and loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity; by realizing the four noble truths; by skillfully contemplating birth, old age, sickness, and death; by clearly believing in the fruit of wholesome and unwholesome karma; by knowing for certain that love and affection must always end in separation; by knowing that no sentient being has any true freedom so long as the noble path is not yet attained, and that the force of birth-and-death is overwhelming; by knowing that every worldly pleasure comes bound up together with suffering — and though such pleasure is still experienced, the mind is not stained or attached to it, just as one seeks a fire to warm oneself in the cold season, and though one makes use of it, one still, in the end, does no evil because of it; by cultivating patience and the two kinds of giving to nourish sentient beings, deeply seeing that suffering and pleasure share, in their essential nature, one and the same equality; by speaking only gentle words in whatever one says, skillfully transforming sentient beings and causing them to dwell in accord with the Dharma; by keeping far from evil companions, with a mind free of heedlessness; and by having nothing whatsoever to do with drinking wine, gambling, or hunting — this is called exchanging a fragile life for an enduring life.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu glosses the "enduring" side of each exchange precisely: an enduring body is the vajra-like, undefiled body finally realized on the path; enduring wealth is the wealth of merit and Dharma rather than any worldly holding; and an enduring life is the life of wisdom and compassion, as opposed to a life still bound up in afflicted karmic retribution, which remains fragile no matter how long it happens to last. He glosses the "four truths" named in the passage as the four noble truths — suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path — and reads the "two kinds of giving" as reaching both material need and spiritual instruction at once, so that neither body nor mind is left unfed by the exchange.

What he underscores most is the everyday, almost unglamorous character of the practices listed: welcoming and seeing off guests, gentle speech, simply not gambling — none of it requires renunciation of ordinary life, and that is exactly the point. Each fragile thing is not discarded but actively converted, put to use in a way that leaves behind something that does not decay, which is precisely what distinguishes this chapter's teaching from a demand to abandon body, wealth, or life altogether.

9. Milestone: Giving Rightly — and the Parable of Deer Mother

The chapter now turns to how a person of means should actually direct their giving — starting with parents and teachers, extending impartially across the whole monastic community — and drives the point home with a story the Buddha says he once told to the laywoman known as Deer Mother.

Chinese Source

「善男子！若得人身，多饒財物，兼得自在，先應供養父母、師長、和上、耆舊、持法之人，供給遠至、初行之人，疾病所須。言則柔軟，多有慚愧，不偏信敬有德一人，見有賢聖持戒、多聞，能以舍宅、飲食、臥具、衣服、病藥而供養之。深信僧中多有功德，修集向道得須陀洹果，乃至能修向阿羅漢，得阿羅漢果，修金剛三昧、電光三昧。觀如是已，平等奉施；如是施已，得無量福。是故我於《鹿子經》中告鹿子母曰：『雖復請佛及五百阿羅漢，猶故不得名請僧福。若能僧中施一似像極惡比丘，猶得無量福德果報。何以故？如是比丘雖是惡人，無戒、多聞，不修善法，亦能演說三種菩提，有因有果，亦不誹謗佛、法、僧寶，執持如來無上勝幡，正見無謬。』若供養僧，即是供養佛、僧二寶；若觀佛法功德微妙，即是具足供養三寶。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! If a person has obtained a human body, has abundant wealth, and also possesses the freedom to act, they should first make offerings to their parents, teachers, preceptors, elders, and those who uphold the Dharma; and provide for travelers who have come from afar, for those just setting out on a journey, and for whatever the sick may need. Their speech should be gentle, they should possess abundant conscience and shame, and they should not place biased faith and reverence in a single virtuous person alone; wherever they find a worthy or sage who upholds the precepts and has learned widely, they should make offerings of dwelling, food and drink, bedding, clothing, and medicine for illness. They should place deep faith in the fact that great merit exists throughout the saṅgha as a whole — among those cultivating and advancing toward the path who attain the fruit of stream-entry, all the way up to those advancing toward, and attaining, the fruit of an arhat, who cultivate the vajra-like concentration and the lightning-flash concentration. Having contemplated in this way, one should make offerings equally to the whole community; and having given in this way, one obtains immeasurable merit. This is why, in the sutra I once spoke to Deer Mother, I said to her: 'Even inviting the Buddha together with five hundred arhats does not yet earn the name of "the merit of inviting the saṅgha." If one is able to give, within the saṅgha, to a single monk who merely resembles one that is thoroughly wicked, one still obtains immeasurable merit and reward. Why is this? Because even such a monk, though an evil person — without precepts, and only widely learned, not cultivating any wholesome dharma — is nevertheless still able to expound the three kinds of bodhi, complete with their causes and their fruits; he does not slander the Buddha, the Dharma, or the Saṅgha, the Three Jewels; he holds aloft the Tathāgata's own unsurpassed and supreme banner; and his view remains correct and free of error.' If one makes offerings to the saṅgha, this is already an offering to two of the Three Jewels, the Buddha and the Saṅgha; and if one further contemplates the subtle and wondrous merit of the Buddha's own Dharma, this is offering made in full to all Three Jewels together.

Reading note

The order of giving named at the start is deliberate rather than incidental — parents and teachers first, because they are the most immediate debts a person actually owes, before the passage widens outward to strangers on the road and finally to the monastic community as a whole. The instruction against "biased faith and reverence in a single virtuous person alone" then sets up the parable's real target: a donor who only ever gives to the monk they personally admire has, in effect, turned generosity into a form of preference, which is precisely the habit the Deer Mother story is designed to break.

The parable's logic is genuinely startling on first reading — an offering to one flawed-looking monk within the saṅgha outweighing a feast for the Buddha and five hundred arhats — but its point is not that virtue does not matter. It is that giving to the saṅgha as an institution, understood as the living vessel of the Dharma across every level of attainment within it, honors something no single individual gift, however illustrious the recipient, can fully capture on its own. What still qualifies even the flawed monk in the story is exactly stated: he still holds right view, still refuses to slander the Three Jewels, and still upholds the Buddha's banner — the floor beneath which the parable does not go.

10. Milestone: Giving Without Seeking Reward, and Three Kinds of Precept

The chapter now names the single quality that turns ordinary generosity into the perfection of giving — the complete absence of any wish for return — and closes this long central teaching by naming three distinct kinds of precept a practitioner can come to hold.

Chinese Source

「若人施時不求果報，即是供養無上菩提，具足成就檀波羅蜜，修菩提道，能得未來無量功德，亦能自利及利益他。能修慈悲，為破他苦，自捨己樂。未得菩提，心無憂悔，雖聞菩提久遠難得，而其內心初無退轉，為諸眾生無量世中受大苦惱，亦不疲厭。樂如法行，不求世樂，樂處寂靜出家修道，未得出家，雖在家居，如解脫人不作眾惡，得三種戒——戒戒、定戒、無漏戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

"If, when giving, a person seeks no reward in return, this is itself an offering made to unsurpassed bodhi, and fully accomplishes the perfection of giving; cultivating the path to bodhi in this way, one is able to obtain immeasurable merit in the future, and is able, too, to benefit both oneself and others at once. Such a person is able to cultivate loving-kindness and compassion, giving up their own happiness in order to break apart the suffering of others. Though bodhi is not yet attained, their mind knows no grief or regret; though they hear that bodhi is distant and hard to attain, their inner resolve never once retreats; and though they undergo great suffering and affliction on behalf of all sentient beings across countless lifetimes, they never grow weary of it. They delight in acting in accord with the Dharma and do not seek worldly pleasure; they delight in dwelling in stillness, going forth from home to cultivate the path — and even if they have not yet gone forth, though still dwelling as a householder, they are like one already liberated, committing none of the many kinds of evil, and thereby attain the three kinds of precept: the precept of individual liberation, the precept bound up with concentration, and the precept free of all outflow.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu unpacks the three named precepts in strict order of depth. The "precept of individual liberation" is the vow taken directly, whether as a lay precept or a monastic one — the precept as an act of will, undertaken and held by choice. The "precept bound up with concentration" arises automatically once genuine meditative concentration is reached: within that concentration, the coarse afflictions native to the desire realm, such as lust and anger, simply do not arise — but only because concentration is holding them down, not because their underlying seed has actually been destroyed. The "precept free of all outflow" belongs only to a mind that has reached the path itself, and there the difference is total: it does not merely suppress unwholesome tendencies but severs their seed outright, so that they cannot arise again even without concentration actively restraining them.

Read this way, the passage traces a single continuous arc — a vow first taken by deliberate resolve, then stabilized by meditative power, and finally made permanent by wisdom that uproots the cause itself — and closes the point begun earlier in the chapter: even a lay person still living at home, so long as they give without seeking reward and hold true to this arc, is, in every way that matters, already living as one liberated.

11. Milestone: Why the Householder's Path Is the Harder One

The chapter closes with a plain, almost blunt admission: everything just described — refuge genuinely held, precepts of ultimate truth, right and wrong conduct, the rarity of a human life, giving without any thought of reward — is measurably harder to sustain for someone still living inside an ordinary household than for someone who has already left it behind.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。出家菩薩如法修行，是不為難；在家菩薩如法修行，是乃為難。何以故？在家之人多惡因緣所纏繞故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! Bodhisattvas are of two kinds: first, householder bodhisattvas; second, renunciant bodhisattvas. For a renunciant bodhisattva to practice in accord with the Dharma is not difficult; for a householder bodhisattva to practice in accord with the Dharma is truly difficult. Why is this? Because a person living at home is entangled by so many unwholesome conditions."

Reading note

The chapter offers this closing line without softening it, and it is worth reading against everything that came before it in this same chapter: the long checklist of what does and does not count as dwelling in accord with the Dharma, the warnings against profiting from another's illness or misfortune, the discipline of giving without partiality — every one of these tests is, by the sutra's own account, harder to pass while still embedded in family obligation, livelihood, and ordinary social pressure than while living a monastic life already organized around the precepts themselves. The chapter does not conclude that the householder's path is therefore lesser, only that it is harder — which, set against everything the chapter has just spent itself describing, reads less like a discouragement than like an honest measure of what it actually costs to keep any of it.
