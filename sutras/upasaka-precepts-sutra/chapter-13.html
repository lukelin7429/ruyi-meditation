Chapter 13 · 優婆塞戒經 · 攝取品第十三 · On Gathering In Disciples

How a Bodhisattva Earns the Right to Teach

Shansheng has just heard, across two chapters, what it takes to perfect the "two adornments" of merit and wisdom. Now he asks the practical question every teacher eventually faces: once a bodhisattva is actually qualified, how should he go about keeping disciples at all? The Buddha's answer moves from the four means of gathering beings in, through the difference between a corrupt teacher and a true one, into concrete instructions for four distinct teaching relationships — monastic to monastic, monastic to lay, lay to lay, and finally a king to an entire nation — before closing with a blunt admission that the layperson's version of this task is, if anything, the harder one.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: Shansheng's Question and the Four Means of Gathering

Having just heard what the two adornments of merit and wisdom require, Shansheng asks the obvious next question: once a bodhisattva actually has them, on what basis should he take on disciples at all? The Buddha's opening answer is not a technique for winning followers but a description of the motive that must precede any technique — gathering beings in through genuine care, and refusing the ordinary teacher's rewards of gratitude, reputation, income, or personal comfort.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！菩薩具足二莊嚴已，云何得畜徒眾弟子？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One, once a bodhisattva has fully perfected the two adornments, how then should he go about keeping a following of disciples?"

Chinese Source

「善男子！應以四攝而攝取之，令離諸惡，增諸善法，至心教詔，猶如一子；不求恩報，不為名稱，不為利養，不求自樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, he should gather them in by means of the four means of gathering, causing them to leave every evil behind and increase every wholesome quality, teaching and admonishing them wholeheartedly as though each were his only child — seeking no repayment of kindness, no reputation, no material gain, and no comfort for himself."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this opening as a direct continuation of the previous chapter's logic: a bodhisattva only truly counts as one who benefits others once he actually has disciples to benefit, and disciples only arrive, in the ordinary course of things, once a teacher has something genuine to offer. The "four means of gathering" he identifies as the classical set — giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and shared purpose — and he is careful to distinguish this giving from the giving practiced as one of the six perfections: the six perfections' giving is aimed inward, at wearing down the giver's own stinginess, while the four means' giving is aimed outward, at actually drawing a stranger close enough to be taught. Kind speech, in his reading, is not mere flattery but the same graduated method the Buddha himself uses in teaching — meeting a listener first on ground they already share, then working toward what they still need to hear.

He adds a caution worth sitting with: this fourfold approach belongs to the difficult, expansive style of bodhisattva practice, and plenty of teachers instead simply pass on whatever they themselves learned and enjoyed, which is far easier but tends, sooner or later, to lose a student's trust — precisely because it skips the patient work of actually meeting the student where they are before asking them to move.

2. Milestone: The Counterfeit Teacher

Before describing what a genuine teacher does, the text names, in unusually harsh terms, what happens without those four means: a teacher who keeps disciples for his own sake rather than theirs is not merely inadequate — he is actively harmful, and the text reaches for the strongest word of contempt available in its own cultural vocabulary to say so.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩若無如是等事畜弟子者，名弊惡人，假名菩薩，非義菩薩；名旃陀羅，臭穢不淨，破壞佛法，是人不為十方諸佛之所憐念。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, if a bodhisattva keeps disciples without these things, he is called a vile and wicked person — a bodhisattva in name only, not a bodhisattva in substance. He is called a caṇḍāla, foul and impure, one who destroys the Buddha-dharma; such a person is not held in compassionate regard by the buddhas of the ten directions."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu is careful to explain what "caṇḍāla" is actually doing in this sentence, since the word names the lowest, untouchable rank of the Indian caste system — someone whose very touch was traditionally considered a source of pollution. He points out that Buddhism itself rejects that hierarchy outright: the Buddha accepted caṇḍālas as ordained disciples without hesitation, so the word cannot be doing caste work here. Instead, he reads it as the sutra deliberately borrowing the era's most visceral image of contamination and redirecting it at moral corruption instead of birth — a teacher who exploits students for gratitude, status, income, or ease is, in the text's own terms, more genuinely unclean than any accident of birth could make a person.

He also notes the severity is proportional rather than arbitrary: this is not a minor lapse in teaching style being condemned, but a wholesale reversal of what a bodhisattva is for. A bodhisattva exists to benefit others; a teacher who instead extracts benefit from disciples has inverted the entire relationship, which is exactly why the text says such a person forfeits the compassionate regard even of the buddhas.

3. Milestone: The True Teacher's Method

Against that counterfeit, the text sets out, in considerable practical detail, what a genuine teacher actually does — reading a disciple's present affliction correctly, building up the disciple's learning and meditation, tolerating real hardship on the disciple's behalf, and calibrating instruction to whichever of three levels of capacity the disciple actually has.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩若能隨時教戒——所言時者，貪、恚、癡時——起貪結時，當為種種說對治法，令得除貪，餘二亦爾；次當教學十二部經、禪定三昧、分別深義；調其身心，令修六念、不放逸法；瞻養病苦不生厭心，能忍惡口誹謗罵辱，苦加身心亦當堪忍；設其有苦能為救解，除其弊惡疑網之心；善知利根、中根、鈍根，教鈍根人令生信心，中根之人能令純淑，利根之人令得解脫——若能如是勤教詔者，名義菩薩，是名善人、分陀利花、人中香象、調御丈夫，名大船師。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, if a bodhisattva is able to teach and admonish according to the moment — where 'the moment' means the moment greed, hatred, or delusion actually arises — then when the knot of greed arises he should teach a variety of antidotes so it can be removed, and likewise for the other two. Next he should teach the twelvefold canon, dhyāna, concentration, and samādhi, and the discernment of profound meaning; he should regulate the disciple's body and mind and lead him to cultivate the six recollections and the practice of non-negligence; he should nurse the sick without ever growing weary, bear harsh words, slander, and abuse with patience, and endure suffering that reaches body and mind alike; when the disciple is in distress he should be able to rescue him from it, and dissolve the net of vile doubt in his mind; he should skillfully recognize sharp, middling, and dull faculties — teaching the dull so that faith arises in them, the middling so that they grow pure and mature, and the sharp so that they attain liberation. A bodhisattva who can diligently teach and admonish in this way is called a bodhisattva of true meaning; he is called a good person, a puṇḍarīka flower, a fragrant elephant among men, a tamer of the untamed, and a great captain of the ship."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu glosses "antidote" concretely: a disciple heavy with greed is set to contemplating the body's impurity, one heavy with hatred is set to cultivating loving-kindness, and so on for each affliction in turn — the antidote is chosen to match the actual poison, not applied generically. He also draws a line most readers would miss between the twelvefold canon and the meditative attainments named alongside it: study of the canon builds the wisdom that comes from hearing and from reflection, while dhyāna and samādhi build the wisdom that comes from actual cultivation — a teacher, in his reading, is responsible for both halves, not just the bookish one.

On the closing string of honorifics, he reads "puṇḍarīka" — the white lotus — as the finest of all lotuses, offered here as the highest compliment the text has available, alongside "fragrant elephant" and "great captain," each pointing at the same idea from a different angle: a teacher who does all of this well is not merely competent but genuinely rare, someone capable of actually getting a whole boatload of disciples across to the far shore rather than losing them along the way.

4. Milestone: Worse Than an Evil Vow

The text now makes its point about a corrupt teacher's harm as sharply as it possibly can, reaching for the most extreme comparison available — a vow to commit mass killing — only to say that neglecting a disciple is, in its own reckoning, worse still.

Chinese Source

「善男子！寧受惡戒，一日中斷無量命根，終不養畜弊惡弟子，不能調伏。何以故？善男子！是惡律儀殃齊自身，畜惡弟子不能教誨，乃令無量眾生作惡，能謗無量善妙之法，破和合僧，令多眾生作五無間，是故劇於惡律儀罪。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, he would rather undertake an evil vow and cut off countless lives in a single day than keep and raise a vile, wicked disciple whom he cannot tame. Why is this? Good son, the calamity of that evil vow falls on the one who holds it alone; but keeping a wicked disciple without being able to instruct him causes countless beings to do evil, to slander countless wondrous and excellent teachings, to break the harmony of the saṃgha, and to drive many beings into the five acts of unmediated retribution — and so its offense is graver even than that of the evil vow."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu points to a real historical referent behind "an evil vow" — accounts, found elsewhere in the canon, of a false teacher who instructed a follower that killing a thousand people in a single day would itself produce enlightenment. Even measured against that extreme, he reads the sutra as saying something more extreme still: the killer's own vow poisons only the killer, but a corrupt teacher's failure poisons everyone downstream of him — the mistaught disciple, everyone that disciple in turn misleads, and the standing of the Dharma itself in the eyes of onlookers who now have reason to doubt it.

He identifies the "five acts of unmediated retribution" as the traditional list — killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, wounding a buddha, and causing a schism in the saṃgha — each carrying consequence severe enough that no intervening moment of ordinary rebirth is thought to soften it. His reading of the whole passage is that the text is not actually recommending violence as the lesser evil; it is using the shock of that comparison to make an otherwise easy-to-overlook harm — simple negligence toward a student — impossible to shrug off.

5. Milestone: Two Bodhisattvas, Two Kinds of Disciples

Having established the stakes, the text turns practical, laying out a simple grid that will organize everything still to come: bodhisattvas are either lay or monastic, and the disciples each is permitted to keep differ accordingly.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。出家菩薩有二弟子：一者、出家，二者、在家。在家菩薩有一弟子，所謂在家。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, there are two kinds of bodhisattva: lay and monastic. A monastic bodhisattva has two kinds of disciples: monastic and lay. A lay bodhisattva has only one kind of disciple, namely lay."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu keeps this short passage from reading as a mere technicality: a lay bodhisattva who took on a monastic disciple would, in his reading, actually violate the Buddha's own institutional ordering of the saṃgha — the teaching authority proper to a renunciant cannot simply be exercised by someone still living the householder's life, regardless of that person's spiritual attainment. The asymmetry is not a judgment about who is holier; it tracks a real structural difference in role. This four-part grid — monastic teaching monastic, monastic teaching lay, lay teaching lay, and, as the chapter goes on to add, lay teaching an entire kingdom — is what the rest of the chapter will now work through in order.

6. Milestone: A Monastic Teacher and a Monastic Disciple — the Eight Wisdoms

The first and, on the text's own account, most straightforward relationship is a monastic teacher training a monastic disciple: formal study of the canon, a discipline of repentance for actual offenses, and a named set of eight kinds of wisdom the disciple is meant to acquire.

Chinese Source

「出家菩薩教出家者十二部經，隨所犯罪，諭令懺悔。教習八智，何等為八？一者、法智，二者、義智，三者、時智，四者、知足智，五者、自智，六者、眾智，七者、根智，八者、分別智。善男子！菩薩摩訶薩若能如是教詔調伏出家弟子，是師弟子二人俱得無量利益，如是師徒能增三寶。何以故？如是弟子知八智已，能勤供養師長、和上、耆舊有德，能受善語，能勤讀誦，兼為法施，心不放逸，調伏眾生，能瞻病苦，給施貧乏。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"A monastic bodhisattva teaches his monastic disciple the twelvefold canon, and whenever the disciple commits an offense, instructs him to repent it. He trains him in the eight kinds of wisdom. What are the eight? First, wisdom of the Dharma; second, wisdom of meaning; third, wisdom of timing; fourth, wisdom of knowing sufficiency; fifth, wisdom of oneself; sixth, wisdom of others; seventh, wisdom of faculties; eighth, wisdom of discernment. Good son, if a bodhisattva-mahāsattva can in this way teach, admonish, and tame his monastic disciple, both teacher and disciple gain immeasurable benefit, and such a teacher and disciple together increase the Three Jewels. Why is this? Because once a disciple knows these eight kinds of wisdom, he is able to diligently make offerings to his teachers, preceptors, and elders of virtue, to receive good words, to diligently recite the scriptures, and also to give the gift of the Dharma; his mind does not grow negligent; he tames other beings; he is able to nurse the sick; and he gives to the poor and needy."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats the eight wisdoms as building directly on ground the sutra has already laid earlier, glossing the two less self-explanatory entries specifically: "wisdom of others" as the capacity to actually read another person's state of mind, and "wisdom of faculties" as the capacity to tell a sharp, a middling, and a dull disciple apart at a glance, so that teaching can be pitched correctly from the start rather than adjusted after failing. He also distinguishes "preceptor" from "teacher" in the passage's own vocabulary: the preceptor (和上, from upādhyāya) is specifically the person under whom one personally took ordination, while "teacher" more broadly covers anyone else who instructs.

He reads the closing list of what a well-trained disciple then does — supporting elders, reciting scripture, teaching others, nursing the sick, giving to the poor — as the whole point of training laid bare: the eight wisdoms are not collected for their own sake but so that this ordinary, visible pattern of a functioning monastic life can actually follow from them.

7. Milestone: A Monastic Teacher and a Lay Disciple — Non-Negligence and Shared Hardship

The relationship shifts when a monastic bodhisattva takes on a lay disciple. The core instruction becomes a long, concrete description of "non-negligence" covering a lay follower's whole life, followed by an unusually specific set of obligations the teacher himself takes on — sharing hardship, and even borrowing against the property of the Three Jewels on the disciple's behalf.

Chinese Source

「善男子！出家菩薩若有在家弟子，亦當先教不放逸法。不放逸者，即是法行；供養父母、諸師、和上、耆舊有德，施於安樂；至心受戒，不妄毀犯；受寄不抵；見恚能忍；惡口、惡語及無義語，終不為之；憐愍眾生，於諸國王、長者、大臣，恒生恭敬怖畏之心；能自調伏妻子眷屬，分別怨親，不輕眾生；除去憍慢，不親惡友；節食、除貪、少欲、知足；鬪諍之處，身不往中，乃至戲笑不說惡語，是則名為不放逸法。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, if a monastic bodhisattva has a lay disciple, he too should first teach the practice of non-negligence. Non-negligence means precisely this: practicing the Dharma — making offerings to one's parents, teachers, preceptors, and elders of virtue, and giving them ease and comfort; wholeheartedly receiving the precepts and never carelessly violating them; never repudiating what has been entrusted to one's keeping; enduring anger when one meets it; never engaging in harsh speech, abusive speech, or idle speech; feeling compassion for all beings; maintaining constant respect and reverent caution toward kings, elders, and ministers; governing one's own wife, children, and household well; distinguishing enemy from kin without holding any being in contempt; casting off arrogance and not keeping company with evil friends; moderating one's food, removing greed, having few desires and knowing contentment; never going in person to a place of quarrel or strife; and never speaking a harsh word even in joking and laughter. This is called the practice of non-negligence."

Chinese Source

「出家菩薩若畜在家弟子，先當教造不放逸法。受苦樂時，常當共俱。設在窮乏有所須者，六物之外有不應惜。病時當為求覓所須，瞻病之時不應生厭。若自無物，應四出求；求不能得，貸三寶物，差已依俗十倍償之，如波斯匿王國之正法。若不能償，復當教言：『汝今多負三寶之物，不能得償，應當勤修得須陀洹果至阿羅漢果。若能至心發菩提心，若教千人於佛法中生清淨信，若壞一人慇重邪見。』出家菩薩能教在家如是等事，是師弟子二人俱得無量利益。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"If a monastic bodhisattva keeps a lay disciple, he should first teach him this practice of non-negligence. In hardship and in ease alike, he should always share them together with his disciple. Should the disciple fall into poverty and lack some necessity, then apart from his six permitted possessions, nothing should be begrudged him. When the disciple is ill, the teacher should seek out whatever he needs, and should never grow weary of nursing him. If the teacher himself has nothing to give, he should go out in all four directions to seek it; and if he still cannot obtain it, he may borrow from the property of the Three Jewels, and once his circumstances improve, repay it tenfold according to worldly custom — as is the righteous law of King Prasenajit's own realm. If he is unable to repay it, he should then further instruct the disciple, saying: 'You now owe a great debt to the property of the Three Jewels which you cannot repay. You should instead diligently cultivate until you attain the fruit from stream-entry up to arhatship; or wholeheartedly bring forth the mind of awakening; or teach a thousand people to bring forth pure faith in the Buddha-dharma; or overturn even a single person's deeply held wrong view.' If a monastic bodhisattva can teach his lay disciple in these ways, both teacher and disciple gain immeasurable benefit."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads "non-negligence" here as a single word doing the work of an entire ethical curriculum for lay life — family duty, honest speech, sexual and financial restraint, right choice of friends, and simple avoidance of conflict, all gathered under one heading because, in his reading, negligence is the common root beneath every one of these individual failures. Two details he singles out for gloss: "not repudiating what has been entrusted" refers to property someone else has placed in one's safekeeping, which must never be quietly claimed as repayment for an unrelated debt; and the "six possessions" are the traditional monastic minimum — three robes, a bowl, bedding, and a water-strainer — invoked here as the standard the teacher measures his own generosity against, since a lay disciple, unlike a monastic, has no similar restriction and so should never be left wanting for less than that baseline.

The borrowing arrangement he treats as genuinely striking rather than a mere procedural aside: a monastic teacher is permitted to draw on Triple Gem property to rescue a struggling disciple, provided he treats it as a real debt, repayable tenfold once circumstances allow, under the model of King Prasenajit's own legal code. And where repayment by ordinary means proves impossible, he reads the alternative — cultivating specific attainments, or converting others, or overturning one entrenched wrong view — not as a loophole but as the sutra insisting that a debt to the Three Jewels can only ever be discharged in the same currency it was borrowed in: genuine spiritual benefit, not silver.

8. Milestone: A Lay Teacher and a Lay Disciple — Household as Practice Ground

When both teacher and disciple are laypeople, the same word "non-negligence" reappears, but its content shifts entirely toward the household: feeding one's dependents before oneself, guiding a good marriage, teaching the scriptures, mending broken relationships, and — notably — giving freely even to renunciants of other paths as a deliberate first step toward eventually guiding them.

Chinese Source

「善男子！在家菩薩若畜在家弟子，亦當先教不放逸法。不放逸者，供養父母、師長、和上、耆舊有德；復當供給兄弟、妻子、親友、眷屬、欲行之人及遠至者，所有僮僕作使之人，先給飲食，然後自用；又復教令信向三寶，苦樂共俱，終不偏獨，隨時賞賜，不令飢寒，終不打罵、鞭撻苦楚，應當軟言敦諭教詔；設有病者，應當瞻療，隨所乏少，當為求索。世間之事，悉以教之，婚姻求對無求卑下；教以如來五部經典，見離壞者，能為和合，既和合已，令增善心；一切出家內外諸道，隨意供養，終不選擇——何以故？先以施攝，後當調故，以六和敬而教詔之——若求財物商賈、農作、奉事王者，常當至心如法而作，既得財已，如法守護；樂為福德，見他作時心生歡喜，是則名為不放逸法。在家菩薩若能教誨在家菩薩如是事者，是師弟子二人俱得無量利益。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, if a lay bodhisattva keeps a lay disciple, he too should first teach him the practice of non-negligence. Non-negligence means: making offerings to one's parents, teachers, preceptors, and elders of virtue; further, providing for one's brothers, wife and children, relatives and dependents, for those about to travel and those arriving from afar, and for all one's servants and employees — giving them food and drink first, and only then taking one's own; further, teaching them to turn their faith toward the Three Jewels, sharing hardship and ease together and never favoring oneself alone, giving in due season so that none go hungry or cold, never beating or scolding them in cruelty, but admonishing and teaching them always with gentle words; when any of them fall ill, tending and treating them and seeking out whatever they lack; teaching them in all worldly matters, and in seeking a marriage match, never seeking one beneath their station; teaching them the Tathāgata's five collections of scripture; where there is estrangement, reconciling it, and once reconciled, causing wholesome intentions to grow further; giving freely to all renunciants, whether within the Dharma or of outside paths, without ever picking and choosing — why is this? Because one first gathers others in through giving, and only afterward tames them, teaching them by way of the six points of reverent harmony; if seeking wealth through trade, farming, or service to the government, always acting wholeheartedly and in accordance with the Dharma, and once wealth is gained, guarding it in accordance with the Dharma; delighting in meritorious deeds, and rejoicing on seeing another perform them. This is called the practice of non-negligence. If a lay bodhisattva can teach his lay disciple in these matters, both teacher and disciple gain immeasurable benefit."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu glosses "the Tathāgata's five collections of scripture" as the Dharma organized into its five traditional Āgama categories — the ordinary curriculum of a lay follower's scriptural education, distinct from the twelvefold canon assigned to monastics earlier in the chapter. He draws particular attention to the instruction to give even to practitioners of other paths: a lay teacher may do this freely as an act of skillful means, since giving first and correcting later is the accepted order of the four means of gathering, whereas he notes that a monastic bodhisattva could not do the same without breaching the boundary already set out earlier in the chapter — the permission is specifically tied to lay status, not a general rule.

On "service to the government," he reads the sutra as deliberately closing off a common excuse: a lay Buddhist is not merely permitted ordinary civic duty but is expected to perform it more conscientiously than someone without any such training, precisely because the same non-negligence that governs the household is meant to govern public office as well — there is no separate, lesser standard reserved for worldly work.

9. Milestone: The King as Teacher — a Nation as a Single Household

The chapter's final and largest teaching relationship scales the same logic up to its furthest extent: a lay bodhisattva who becomes a sovereign ruler is asked to treat an entire population the way the previous section asked an ordinary householder to treat his own family — punishing without killing, taxing lightly, avoiding seven specific vices, and using the same four means of gathering that opened the chapter, now applied to a whole kingdom.

Chinese Source

「善男子！在家菩薩若得自在為大國主，擁護民庶猶如一子。教離諸惡，修行善法。見作惡者，撾打罵辱，終不斷命。財物六分，稅取其一。見瞋惡者，教修忍辱及不放逸。所言柔軟，又能分別善惡之人，見有罪者，忍而不問。隨有財物，常行慧施。任力讀誦五部經典，善能守護身、命、財物，能化眾生不令作惡。見貧窮者，生大憐愍，自於國土常修知足，惡人讒謗終不信受，不以非法求覓財物。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, if a lay bodhisattva attains sovereignty and becomes a great ruler of a country, he protects and cares for his people as though each were his only child. He teaches them to leave every evil behind and practice wholesome ways. When he sees one who has done wrong, he may strike, beat, or rebuke him, but never takes his life. Of the people's wealth he takes only one part in six as tax. When he sees one given to anger, he teaches him to cultivate patience and non-negligence. His own speech is gentle, and he is able to tell the good person from the bad; when he sees one who has offended, he is patient and does not press the matter. Whatever wealth he has, he constantly gives with wisdom. As his strength allows, he recites the five collections of scripture; he is well able to guard his own body, life, and possessions, and is able to transform his people so that they do not do evil. When he sees the poor and destitute, he feels great compassion for them; within his own realm he constantly cultivates contentment, never believes the slanders of wicked men, and never seeks wealth by unlawful means."

Chinese Source

「如法護國，遠七種惡：一者、不樂樗蒲、圍碁、六博，二者、不樂射獵，三者、不樂飲酒，四者、不樂欲心，五者、不樂惡口，六者、不樂兩舌，七者、不樂非法取財。常樂供養出家之人，能令國人常於王所生父母想，信因信果，見有勝己不生嫉妬，見己勝他不生憍慢，知恩報恩，小恩大報。能伏諸根，淨於三業。讚歎善人，呵責惡人，先意發言，言則柔軟。自無力勢，如法屬他，取他國時，不舉四兵。眾生恐怖，能為救解，常以四攝而攝取之。善能分別種種法相，不受法者，軟言調之。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Protecting his country in accordance with the Dharma, he keeps far from seven kinds of evil: first, no delight in dice, board games, or gambling; second, no delight in hunting; third, no delight in drinking wine; fourth, no delight in lustful desire; fifth, no delight in harsh speech; sixth, no delight in divisive speech; seventh, no delight in acquiring wealth unlawfully. He constantly delights in making offerings to renunciants, and causes his people to regard him always as a father and mother; he has faith in cause and effect; seeing one superior to himself, he feels no envy, and being superior to another, he grows no arrogance; he knows kindness and repays it, repaying even a small kindness many times over. He masters his senses and purifies his three modes of action — body, speech, and mind. He praises the good and reproaches the wicked; he is first to speak, and his words are always gentle. When he himself lacks the strength to prevail, he submits rightfully to another in accordance with the Dharma; and when he takes another's kingdom, he does not raise the four armies to do it. When beings are struck with fear, he is able to rescue them from it, and constantly gathers them in through the four means of gathering. He is skillfully able to discern every kind of phenomenon, and when someone will not accept the Dharma, he tames him with gentle words."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads a righteous king of this description as, more often than not, the visible activity of a bodhisattva already well advanced on the path — traditionally associated with the first or second of the ten grounds — using worldly sovereignty itself as a vehicle for the same work a monastic teacher does with a smaller circle of disciples. "Cultivating contentment within his own realm," in his reading, is a pointed way of saying the king does not invade his neighbors: a ruler who is actually satisfied with what he already governs has no motive left for conquest.

On the seven vices, he notes the gambling game named first has a long folk association with Laozi in Chinese legend, which he takes as evidence the list was phrased to land naturally on a Chinese audience rather than translated woodenly; and on the closing instruction not to "raise the four armies" even in taking another kingdom, he reads this as ruling out aggressive war outright — the four armies being the traditional infantry, cavalry, chariotry, and war-elephants of ancient warfare — so that even a legitimate transfer of territory must happen by the same non-violent, gentle-speech methods the chapter has been describing all along, never by force. The king section, in other words, is not a separate topic from everything before it; it is the four means of gathering, and non-negligence, scaled up to the size of a country.

10. Milestone: Why the Layperson's Task Is the Harder One

The chapter closes by circling back to its own opening division of lay and monastic, only now to reverse the reader's likely assumption: it is the monastic teacher's task, not the layperson's, that the text calls easy — precisely because household life entangles a lay teacher in far more competing obligations than a monastic ever has to navigate.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩有二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。出家菩薩畜二弟子，是不為難；在家菩薩畜一弟子，是乃為難。何以故？在家之人多惡因緣所纏繞故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, there are two kinds of bodhisattva: lay and monastic. For a monastic bodhisattva to keep two kinds of disciples is not difficult; for a lay bodhisattva to keep even one kind of disciple is truly difficult. Why is this? Because a person living at home is entangled by so many adverse conditions."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats this closing line as the chapter's real punchline rather than a throwaway summary. A monastic teacher's life is already structured around teaching — the household distractions that pull at attention, money, family duty, marriage, employment, civic obligation, factional quarrels, are largely removed from the outset, which is exactly why the sutra can say two disciples pose no real difficulty for him. A lay bodhisattva, by contrast, is asked to do everything this chapter has just described — teach a disciple, run a household, guard a marriage, hold a job, and possibly govern a country — inside a life still full of every ordinary entanglement a monastic has already set down.

He reads this less as discouragement than as an honest cost accounting: any king who actually manages to govern by the standard just laid out earns, in his own words, the title of a true sage-king, and the difficulty named here is precisely what makes that achievement worth naming. The chapter's real message, in his summary, is not that lay practice is lesser, but that it is quietly more demanding — and that a lay bodhisattva who keeps even a single disciple well has, in its own way, accomplished something rarer than a monastic keeping two.
