Chapter 2 · 優婆塞戒經 · 發菩提心品第二 · A graded answer to one question — why, and how, a being actually arouses the mind set on awakening

Every Reason to Wake Up

Shansheng, the lay disciple to whom this whole sutra is addressed, asks the Buddha a single plain question: by what means does a being actually arouse the bodhi mind? The answer that follows refuses to give a single formula. Instead it lays out motive after motive in ascending order — beginning with the frankly ordinary wish for a longer life and more wealth, moving through compassion for suffering beings and the discipline of guarding one's own practice from arrogance and jealousy, and arriving only at the very end at what the chapter itself calls "true" bodhi mind: a resolve born of wisdom, strong enough to break apart even karma piled up like Mount Sumeru.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: Shansheng's Question

The chapter opens with the question that everything else in it exists to answer, addressed directly to the Buddha by the lay disciple Shansheng (善生, "Well-Born") — the same householder to whom the entire Sutra on Upasaka Precepts is spoken.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！眾生云何發菩提心？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One, by what means do sentient beings arouse the bodhi mind?"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu pauses on the single word "how" (云何) before moving on, reading it as already bundling together several distinct questions at once — by what cause, by what means, and by what actual method does this arousal happen? He treats this as the deliberate hinge that opens the Buddha's long answer: not a request for a slogan, but an invitation to a layered explanation that will move from the crudest, most self-interested motives all the way up to genuine wisdom, without skipping any of the steps in between.

2. Milestone: The First Six Reasons — An Ordinary Person's Motives

The Buddha's answer begins where an ordinary, still-unawakened person actually stands, not where a bodhisattva already stands. Taixu identifies this entire opening tier — twelve pairs of reasons in all — as "arousing the bodhi mind through faith and understanding" (信解發菩提心): the reasoning available to what the tradition calls an "outer ordinary being," someone who has not yet even reached the ten stages of faith, let alone the bodhisattva path proper. The first six pairs are given here.

Chinese Source

善男子！為二事故發菩提心：一者、增長壽命，二者、增長財物。

Ru-Yi Translation

Good son! There are two things for the sake of which one arouses the bodhi mind: first, to increase one's own lifespan; second, to increase one's own wealth.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、為不斷絕菩薩種姓，二者、為斷眾生罪苦煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

And there are two more: first, so that the bodhisattva lineage of conduct is not cut off; second, so as to cut off the sinful suffering and affliction of sentient beings.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、自觀無量世中受大苦惱，不得利益；二者、雖有無量恒沙諸佛，悉皆不能度脫我身，我當自度。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, observing for oneself that through countless ages one has undergone great suffering and affliction without ever gaining real benefit from it; second, recognizing that although there are Buddhas as numerous as the sands of countless Ganges rivers, not one of them can liberate this body of mine — I myself must be the one to bring about my own liberation.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、作諸善業，二者、作已不失。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, performing every kind of wholesome deed; second, so that having performed it, the deed is not lost.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、為勝一切人天果報，二者、為勝一切二乘果報。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, to surpass every reward-fruit attained by humans and gods; second, to surpass every fruit attained by the two lesser vehicles.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、為求菩提之道受大苦惱，二者、為得無量大利益事。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, being willing to undergo great suffering and hardship in seeking the path to bodhi; second, seeking to obtain a benefit that is truly immeasurable and vast.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu takes the opening pair seriously rather than dismissing it as crude self-interest: longevity and wealth are indeed what an ordinary person wants, he notes, yet it is only a Buddha who is genuinely and fully endowed with both blessing and lifespan without limit — so wanting them, even at this unrefined level, is already a first small gesture in the right direction. He reads the third pair against the background of the three-times law of cause and effect: once it is truly seen that endlessly creating karma and undergoing suffering brings no real benefit, and that even countless Buddhas cannot liberate a being who will not act on their own behalf, arousing the mind stops being optional.

On the fourth pair he adds an almost rueful observation: most people, by the time they have gathered enough knowledge and experience to actually be good at doing good, have simply grown old and are near death — so a wholesome deed is constantly being "lost" to time in this way. Arousing the bodhi mind, he says, is precisely what keeps a good deed from being lost like this. The sixth pair he reads as two sides of one investment: without real hardship, resolve never becomes firm; and only a benefit gained after arousing the mind escapes being casually lost again, the way every merely worldly gain eventually is.

3. Milestone: The Next Six Reasons — Deepening the Resolve

The same tier of "faith and understanding" continues with six further pairs, moving from private self-interest toward vows that reach outward — toward other beings, toward the higher stages of the path, and toward the specific obstructions that a bodhisattva's resolve is meant to sever.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、過去、未來恒沙諸佛皆如我身，二者、深觀菩提是可得法，是故發心。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, recognizing that the Buddhas of the past and future, numerous as the sands of the Ganges, were originally no different from this body of mine; second, deeply contemplating that bodhi is a dharma that can genuinely be attained — and it is for this reason that one arouses the mind.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、觀六住人雖有轉心，猶勝一切聲聞、緣覺；二者、勤心求索無上果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, observing that a person at the sixth abiding, even though their resolve may still waver, nevertheless already surpasses every śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha; second, diligently and wholeheartedly seeking the unsurpassed fruit.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、欲令一切眾生悉得解脫，二者、欲令眾生解脫、勝外道等所得果報。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, wishing that all sentient beings, every one of them, attain liberation; second, wishing that the liberation sentient beings attain surpass whatever fruit is attained by followers of non-Buddhist paths.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、不捨一切眾生，二者、捨離一切煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, not abandoning any sentient being; second, abandoning and departing from every affliction.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、為斷眾生現在苦惱，二者、為遮眾生未來苦惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, to cut off the present suffering and affliction of sentient beings; second, to forestall their future suffering and affliction.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、為斷智慧障礙，二者、為斷眾生身障。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more: first, to cut off the obstruction to wisdom; second, to cut off the bodily obstruction of sentient beings.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads the seventh pair as a kind of proof by precedent: the Buddhas of past and future, before they themselves first aroused the mind and practiced, were no different from anyone reading this text right now — and since bodhi is demonstrably attainable rather than empty fantasy, resolving to pursue it is simply reasonable. The eighth pair he glosses technically: a bodhisattva at the sixth of the ten abidings can still, in principle, backslide — true non-retrogression only arrives at the seventh — yet even at this unstable sixth stage such a person already surpasses any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, which is reason enough to press on diligently toward the higher fruit rather than settle for what has already been gained.

The tenth pair he reads as strict logical order rather than two independent wishes: a bodhisattva's great compassion must extend to inexhaustible beings, so the vow never to abandon any of them only becomes workable once the second vow — cutting off one's own afflictions first — has actually been kept, since only someone who has done that can go on to be of real use to anyone else. On the final pair he distinguishes the two obstructions precisely: the "obstruction to wisdom" is what specifically blocks a Buddha's own cognition — bare ignorance and not-knowing, technically the cognitive obstruction — while the "bodily obstruction" carried by ordinary beings is retribution generated by afflictive karma, the afflictive obstruction proper. The bodhi vow, he stresses, is aimed at severing both together, not one at the expense of the other.

4. Milestone: Cultivating the Bodhi Mind — Ten Further Practices

Taixu marks a clear shift here: this next tier is "arousing the bodhi mind through cultivation" (修習發菩提心), the practice suited to a bodhisattva already within the ten stages of faith — one step further along the path than everything in the previous two milestones, which he characterizes as belonging only to an "outer ordinary being" not yet inside the ten faiths at all.

Chinese Source

善男子！發菩提心有五事：一者、親近善友，二者、斷瞋恚心，三者、隨師教誨，四者、生憐愍心，五者、勤修精進。

Ru-Yi Translation

Good son! There are five things involved in arousing the bodhi mind: first, drawing near to good friends; second, cutting off the mind of anger and hatred; third, following the instruction of one's teacher; fourth, giving rise to a mind of compassion; fifth, diligently cultivating vigorous effort.

Chinese Source

復有五事：一者、不見他過，二者、雖見他過而心不悔，三者、得善法已不生憍慢，四者、見他善業不生妬心，五者、觀諸眾生如一子想。

Ru-Yi Translation

And there are five more things: first, not dwelling on the faults of others; second, even when another's faults are plainly seen, the mind does not turn away in regret; third, having attained wholesome dharmas, not giving rise to arrogance; fourth, seeing the wholesome deeds of others, not giving rise to a jealous mind; fifth, regarding every sentient being with the same thought one would hold for an only child.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

On the first five, Taixu notes that "good friends" is meant broadly enough to include Buddhas and bodhisattvas themselves, not only human companions — and that the sequence, from approaching such friends through to sustained vigorous effort, is exactly what is needed to actually grow a bodhisattva's mind rather than merely wish for one. The second five, he points out, turn inward and guard against the specific ways practice curdles on itself: a bodhisattva's compassionate skillful means often cannot make another person let go of a fault quickly, so the discipline is not to become disgusted or give up on them even once the fault is plainly seen.

He is especially vivid on the third and fourth items: arrogance mixed into a genuinely wholesome attainment — a good meditative state, a favorable reputation — is, in his own image, like poison stirred into good food, turning what should be a pure deed into a defiled, "outflow"-tainted one; jealousy at another's success spoils a deed the same way. The fifth item he explains through ordinary family logic: favoritism only ever arises once there is more than one child to compare — hold every being as though it were one's only child, and there is nothing left to prefer. He closes by underscoring that everything covered so far, across both this milestone and the previous two, still falls short of true arousal of the bodhi mind; it is arousal by way of faith, understanding, and cultivation — real, but not yet the genuine article.

5. Milestone: True Bodhi Mind — Wisdom, Compassion, and Three Reasons

Only now does the chapter reach what it calls genuine arousal of the bodhi mind — the resolve of someone Taixu describes as possessing real, penetrating understanding, powerful enough to break apart the fruit of unwholesome karma even when that karma is piled as high as Mount Sumeru.

Chinese Source

善男子！有智之人發菩提心已，即能破壞惡業等果如須彌山。

Ru-Yi Translation

Good son! Once a person of wisdom has aroused the bodhi mind, they are immediately able to break apart the fruit of unwholesome karma, even karma equal in mass to Mount Sumeru.

Chinese Source

有智之人，為三事故發菩提心：一者、見惡世中五濁眾生，二者、見於如來有不可思議神通道力，三者、聞佛如來八種妙聲。

Ru-Yi Translation

A person of wisdom arouses the bodhi mind for three reasons: first, seeing the beings of the five turbidities in this evil age; second, seeing that the Tathāgata possesses inconceivable spiritual power and mastery of the path; third, hearing the Buddha Tathāgata's eight kinds of wondrous voice.

Chinese Source

復有二事：一者、了了自知己身有苦，二者、知眾生苦如己受苦，為斷彼苦如己無異。

Ru-Yi Translation

And two more reasons: first, clearly knowing for oneself that one's own body is subject to suffering; second, knowing the suffering of sentient beings exactly as intimately as one's own suffering, and resolving to cut off their suffering as though it were, without any difference at all, one's own.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu glosses Mount Sumeru simply as the tallest mountain in this world-system, standing here for karma at its most immovable — the point being that genuine bodhi mind is strong enough to break even that. He reads the three reasons as splitting into a downward-facing half and an upward-facing half: seeing beings caught in the five turbidities — of the kalpa, of view, of affliction, of being, and of lifespan — moves a great-compassion mind to want to save them, yet nothing short of unsurpassed bodhi actually makes that rescue possible, so this first reason works "downward," toward liberating others; seeing the Buddha's inconceivable spiritual power, and separately hearing the Buddha's voice — traditionally catalogued as eight distinct qualities: excellent, gentle, harmonious, dignified and wise, never false, far-reaching, unerring, and inexhaustible — both work "upward," drawing the practitioner toward Buddhahood itself. Taken together, the three combine an inward foundation of compassion and wisdom with an outward, superior condition: an actual encounter with the Buddha's power and voice.

On the closing pair, he draws a sharp line between what is shared and what is distinctly Mahāyāna: clearly knowing one's own suffering, he notes, is simply the truth of suffering common to all three vehicles — the three sufferings and eight sufferings familiar from śrāvaka teaching. But knowing the suffering of others exactly as intimately as one's own, and resolving to end it as though it were literally one's own, rests on the specifically Mahāyāna conviction that self and other share a single substance. It is this second reason alone, he stresses, that actually amounts to the Mahāyāna bodhi mind; the first is common ground shared with the lesser vehicles, not yet the distinguishing mark of a bodhisattva.

6. Milestone: The True Reward — Real Wealth, Real Long Life

The chapter closes by circling back to where it began. Its opening pair of reasons named longevity and wealth as motives for arousing the bodhi mind; its final line explains what actually delivers them — and points, in passing, at an old folk practice of ritually venerating the six directions for protection and prosperity, the very practice the Buddha elsewhere reinterprets rather than simply endorses.

Chinese Source

善男子！若有人能發菩提心，當知是人能禮六方增長命、財，不如外道之所宣說。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Good son! If there is a person able to arouse the bodhi mind, know that this person's veneration of the six directions truly increases lifespan and wealth — unlike what is proclaimed by the followers of non-Buddhist paths.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this closing line as the chapter's actual point of arrival, not a throwaway blessing tacked on at the end: the moment the bodhi mind is genuinely aroused, that very act already makes a person a bodhisattva — and a bodhisattva, he says, does truly increase lifespan and wealth, but now understood as the deeper wealth of merit and dharma, not merely the ordinary version named back at the chapter's opening. Because such a person is now actually capable of liberating beings and accomplishing the path to Buddhahood, this final statement functions as what the commentarial tradition calls "revealing the true, proper teaching" — drawing a hard line between the Buddha's own six-directions veneration and the version taught by non-Buddhist paths, which promises the identical increase in life and wealth but, lacking the bodhi mind behind it, has nothing real to actually deliver on that promise.
