Chapter 3 · 優婆塞戒經 · 悲品第三 · Why Great Compassion Is the Seed From Which the Awakening-Mind Grows

Compassion, the Root of the Awakening-Mind

Shansheng presses a technical question left over from the previous chapter: when the Buddha spoke of "giving rise to the bodhi-mind," did he mean something that actually produces it, or merely something that reveals what was already there? The Buddha's answer opens into the heart of this chapter — that great compassion is the one true seed from which the awakening-mind grows — and unfolds into thirty-six distinct ways of looking at the suffering of living beings, each one a doorway into that same compassion, before turning to explain why a lay bodhisattva, precisely because home life is so entangling, needs compassion even more urgently than a monastic does.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: Shansheng's Question — A Cause That Produces, or a Cause That Merely Reveals?

The chapter opens mid-conversation, with Shansheng picking up a distinction the Buddha has just introduced: the difference between a "generative cause," which actually brings something into being, and a "revealing cause," which only makes visible what was already present. He wants to know which kind of cause is at work when a being "gives rise to" the awakening-mind in the first place.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！彼六師等不說因果，如來今說因有二種：一者、生因，二者、了因。如佛初說發菩提心，為是生因，是了因耶？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said, "World-Honored One, those six [rival, non-Buddhist] teachers do not teach cause and effect at all. But the Tathāgata now teaches that there are two kinds of cause: the generative cause and the revealing cause. When the Buddha earlier spoke of giving rise to the bodhi-mind, was that a generative cause, or a revealing cause?"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu glosses "悲" (compassion) here at the outset as more than ordinary pity for one's own family and friends — it must be extended to all sentient beings equally, and grounded in a real understanding that beings are empty of any fixed, independent self, or it never rises to the level the sutra actually means by the word. He also draws out a wordplay buried in the Chinese verb "to give rise to" (發): the same character covers both "to generate" and "to discover," much as scientific discovery is called 發見 rather than 發生. His illustration is a dark room and a lamp — when the light is switched on, every object in the room becomes visible at once, yet none of those objects was actually produced by the lamp; the light only revealed what was already there. Shansheng's question, on this reading, is really asking whether the bodhi-mind is more like the lamp's light itself, or more like the furniture the light merely uncovers.

2. Milestone: One Cause, or Twelve — The Buddha's Answer Ranges Across the Full Teaching

Rather than answering directly, the Buddha first shows Shansheng the sheer range of ways he has taught causation elsewhere — from a single cause up through twelve — before narrowing back down to the one answer this chapter actually cares about.

Chinese Source

「善男子！我為眾生或說一因，或說二因，或說三因，或說四因，或說五因，或說六、七至十二因。言一因者，即生因也。言二因者，生因、了因。言三因者，煩惱、業、器。言四因者，所謂四大。言五因者，未來五支。言六因者，如契經中所說六因。言七因者，如《法華》說。言八因者，現在八支。言九因者，如《大城經》說。言十因者，如為摩男優婆塞說。十一因者，如《智印》說。十二因者，如十二因緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, for the sake of sentient beings I sometimes teach one cause, sometimes two, sometimes three, four, five, or six, seven, and on up to twelve causes. When I speak of one cause, I mean the generative cause alone. When I speak of two causes, I mean the generative cause and the revealing cause. When I speak of three causes, I mean affliction, karma, and the receptacle-world. When I speak of four causes, I mean the four great elements. When I speak of five causes, I mean the five future limbs [of dependent origination]. When I speak of six causes, I mean the six causes taught in the sūtras. When I speak of seven causes, I mean what is taught in the Lotus Sūtra. When I speak of eight causes, I mean the eight present limbs [of dependent origination]. When I speak of nine causes, I mean what is taught in the Sūtra of the Great City. When I speak of ten causes, I mean what I taught for the sake of the upāsaka Mahānāma. When I speak of eleven causes, I mean what is taught in the Wisdom Seal [Sūtra]. When I speak of twelve causes, I mean the twelve links of dependent origination.

Chinese Source

「善男子！一切有漏法無量無邊因，一切無漏法無量無邊因，有智之人欲盡知故，發菩提心；是故如來名一切智。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, the outflow-tainted dharmas have causes without measure and without bound, and the outflow-free dharmas likewise have causes without measure and without bound. It is because the wise wish to know all of these completely that they give rise to the bodhi-mind — and this is precisely why the Tathāgata is called the One of All-Wisdom.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu walks through the list without pretending every reference is equally clear to him: the "three causes" split into affliction and karma as internal causes and the receptacle-world as an external one; the "four causes" are the earth, water, fire, and wind that make up both a body and its surrounding world alike; the "five" and "eight" causes are simply two different slices of the twelve links of dependent origination — five future limbs (craving, grasping, becoming, birth, old-age-and-death) and eight present ones (consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense fields, contact, feeling, craving, grasping, becoming). He is candid that the Sūtra of the Great City and the sūtra taught for Mahānāma are texts he could not fully verify in his own day, and flags them honestly as still awaiting confirmation rather than papering over the gap. The number that matters most for the rest of the chapter, though, is the twelfth: the familiar twelve links of dependent origination, taught in full. He reads the closing line as the real point of this whole survey — a being is called "possessed of all wisdom" not for knowing one clever cause, but for wishing to know every cause, without limit, that could ever be taught.

3. Milestone: The Answer at Last — Great Compassion Is the Seed of the Awakening-Mind

Having surveyed the full range of causes he teaches elsewhere, the Buddha finally answers Shansheng's original question directly: the bodhi-mind does have a genuine generative cause, and that cause is nothing other than great compassion.

Chinese Source

「善男子！一切眾生發菩提心，或有生因，或有了因，或有生因、了因。汝今當知：夫生因者，即是大悲；因是悲故，便能發心，是故悲心為生因也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, when sentient beings give rise to the bodhi-mind, some have only a generative cause, some have only a revealing cause, and some have both. Know this now: the generative cause is none other than great compassion. It is precisely because of compassion that one is able to give rise to the [awakening-]mind at all — this is why the compassionate mind is called the generative cause."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats this as the pivot the entire preceding survey was building toward: the unsurpassed bodhi-mind simply cannot arise apart from great compassion. He is careful about a common misreading — that merely wanting to escape one's own suffering in birth-and-death already counts as bodhicitta. Even genuine Hīnayāna practitioners who sincerely wish to be free of saṃsāra, he points out, cannot be credited with the unsurpassed awakening-mind if compassion for others is absent from their aspiration. His preferred image is agricultural rather than abstract: giving rise to the bodhi-mind without great compassion behind it is like scattering grain onto a field with no actual seed in it — nothing will ever sprout, however long one waits or however fertile the ground.

4. Milestone: Shansheng Asks How — and the Buddha Turns to Thirty-Six Ways of Seeing

Once compassion has been named as the actual seed of the awakening-mind, the practical question follows at once: how does a person actually go about cultivating it? The Buddha's answer, unfolded across the following sections, is a sustained exercise in looking — thirty-six distinct ways of observing the condition of sentient beings, each one, on its own, sufficient to give rise to compassion.

Chinese Source

「世尊！云何而得修於悲心？」

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One, how then does one go about cultivating a compassionate mind?"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu treats what follows as a single sustained contemplative exercise rather than thirty-six unrelated remarks — each clause built on the same pattern of "seeing thus, compassion arises," each one a distinct angle from which an ordinary being's condition can be genuinely seen, rather than merely known about in the abstract. He reads the list as deliberately exhaustive in scope: it moves from the most general sight of beings drowning in saṃsāra, through the particular entanglements of desire, delusion, and moral failure, out to war, famine, and social conditions, and finally up to the subtlest possible object of compassion — beings absorbed in the most refined meditative bliss the formless realms have to offer. Nothing, on this reading, ultimately falls outside the reach of a fully cultivated compassion.

5. Milestone: Thirty-Six Ways of Seeing, Part One — Drowning, Bound, and Still Unwilling to Let Go

The first twelve observations move from the most general image of beings adrift in saṃsāra to the specific ways ordinary craving, arrogance, and bad company keep a person bound to what is actually harming them.

Chinese Source

「善男子！智者深見一切眾生沈沒生死苦惱大海，為欲拔濟，是故生悲。又見眾生未有十力、四無所畏、大悲、三念，我當云何令彼具足？是故生悲。又見眾生雖多怨毒，亦作親想，是故生悲。又見眾生迷於正路，無有示導，是故生悲。又見眾生臥五欲泥而不能出，猶故放逸，是故生悲。又見眾生常為財物、妻子纏縛，不能捨離，是故生悲。又見眾生以色命故而生憍慢，是故生悲。又見眾生為惡知識之所誑惑故生親想，如六師等，是故生悲。又見眾生墮生有界，受諸苦惱，猶故樂著，是故生悲。又見眾生造身、口、意不善惡業，多受苦果，猶故樂著，是故生悲。又見眾生渴求五欲，如渴飲醎水，是故生悲。又見眾生雖欲求樂，不造樂因；雖不樂苦，喜造苦因；欲受天樂，不具足戒，是故生悲。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, the wise clearly see all sentient beings sunk in the great ocean of birth-and-death and suffering, and, wishing to pull them out and rescue them — compassion arises. Seeing further that beings do not yet possess the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, great compassion, and the three mindfulnesses [of a Buddha], and thinking, 'How shall I bring them to completeness in these?' — compassion arises. Seeing that even toward those who bear them much hatred and poison, one still forms the thought of kinship — compassion arises. Seeing beings lost on the true path, with no one to point the way — compassion arises. Seeing beings lying in the mire of the five desires, unable to climb out, and yet still indulging in heedlessness — compassion arises. Seeing beings forever bound by wealth, by wife and children, unable to let go and leave them behind — compassion arises. Seeing beings grow arrogant on account of their looks or their lifespan — compassion arises. Seeing beings deceived and led astray by evil companions, so that they mistake such companions for kin — as with the followers of the six [rival] teachers — compassion arises. Seeing beings fallen into the conditioned realms of existence, undergoing every kind of suffering and affliction, and yet still delighting in and clinging to it — compassion arises. Seeing beings creating unwholesome, evil karma of body, speech, and mind, reaping abundant suffering as its fruit, and yet still delighting in and clinging to it — compassion arises. Seeing beings thirsting after the five desires the way a thirsty man drinks salt water — compassion arises. Seeing beings who wish for happiness yet do not create the causes of happiness; who do not wish for suffering yet delight in creating the causes of suffering; who wish to enjoy heavenly bliss yet will not keep the precepts in full — compassion arises.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu glosses the recurring phrase "five desires" (五欲) plainly, as wealth, sex, fame, food, and sleep — the five things an ordinary person chases hardest — rather than the alternative technical sense of the five sense-objects, and reads the whole opening run against that backdrop. The second observation, wishing beings could possess a Buddha's unshared qualities, he reads as admiration turned outward rather than envy: seeing something genuinely admirable in another and wanting them to have it, in the same spirit as the old teaching to "see the worthy and think of becoming their equal." He also notes the third observation — regarding even an enemy with the same warmth as a friend — as already, quietly, describing the impartiality that later becomes full "great compassion," planted here in miniature at the very start of the list.

6. Milestone: Thirty-Six Ways of Seeing, Part Two — Grasping at a Self That Was Never There

The next twelve observations turn inward, toward the subtler suffering of clinging to a fixed self, fearing what cannot be avoided, and creating fresh karma even while already suffering its results.

Chinese Source

「又見眾生於無我、我所生我、我所想，是故生悲。又見眾生無定有性，流轉五有，是故生悲。又見眾生畏生、老死，而更造作生、老死業，是故生悲。又見眾生受身心苦而更造業，是故生悲。又見眾生愛別離苦而不斷愛，是故生悲。又見眾生處無明闇，不知熾然智慧燈明，是故生悲。又見眾生為煩惱火之所燒然，而不能求三昧定水，是故生悲。又見眾生為五欲樂造無量惡，是故生悲。又見眾生知五欲苦，求之不息，譬如飢者食於毒飯，是故生悲。又見眾生處在惡世，遭值虐王，多受苦惱，猶故放逸，是故生悲。又見眾生流轉八苦，不知斷除如是苦因，是故生悲。又見眾生飢渴、寒熱，不得自在，是故生悲。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Seeing beings, in what has no self and nothing belonging to a self, give rise to the notion of 'self' and 'what is mine' — compassion arises. Seeing beings, having no fixed, permanent nature of their own, revolve on through the five realms of existence — compassion arises. Seeing beings who dread birth, old age, and death, and yet go on creating the very karma that produces birth, old age, and death — compassion arises. Seeing beings undergoing suffering of body and mind, and yet still creating further karma — compassion arises. Seeing beings suffer the pain of separation from what they love, and yet not cut off their craving — compassion arises. Seeing beings dwelling in the darkness of ignorance, not knowing how to kindle the bright lamp of wisdom — compassion arises. Seeing beings burning in the fire of the afflictions, yet unable to seek out the cooling water of samādhi — compassion arises. Seeing beings commit boundless evil for the sake of the pleasures of the five desires — compassion arises. Seeing beings who know the five desires bring suffering, and yet pursue them without rest, like a starving man eating poisoned rice — compassion arises. Seeing beings living in an evil age, encountering cruel rulers, undergoing much suffering and affliction, and yet still living in heedlessness — compassion arises. Seeing beings revolving through the eight kinds of suffering, not knowing how to cut off the causes of such suffering — compassion arises. Seeing beings tormented by hunger, thirst, cold, and heat, unable to gain any mastery over their own condition — compassion arises.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu pushes the first observation in this group further than it might first appear: it is not only the self that is empty, he stresses, but everything a self might claim as "mine" — since the four great elements that compose a body and the five aggregates that compose a person are themselves without any fixed, independent essence, there is, quite literally, nowhere left to locate a self in the first place. On the fire-and-water image a few lines later, he glosses samādhi with a plain, almost proverbial line: when the mind grows still, it naturally turns cool — the antidote to the afflictions' fire is never external, but simply a settled mind. He also spells out the "eight kinds of suffering" the passage refers to only by number: birth, old age, sickness, death, not getting what one wants, being made to associate with what one hates, being separated from what one loves, and the raw fact of having five clinging aggregates at all.

7. Milestone: Thirty-Six Ways of Seeing, Part Three — War, Famine, and Even the Bliss of the Highest Heaven

The final twelve observations widen the lens outward to social and historical suffering — broken precepts, war, famine, ingratitude toward the Dharma — before the list closes on its most demanding object: the refined meditative bliss of the very summit of conditioned existence.

Chinese Source

「又見眾生毀犯禁戒，當受地獄、餓鬼、畜生，是故生悲。又見眾生色、力、壽命、安隱、辯才不得自在，是故生悲。又見眾生諸根不具，是故生悲。又見眾生生於邊地，不修善法，是故生悲。又見眾生處飢饉世，身體羸瘦，互相劫奪，是故生悲。又見眾生處刀兵劫，更相殘害，惡心增盛，當受無量苦報之果，是故生悲。又見眾生值佛出世，聞說甘露淨法不能受持，是故生悲。又見眾生信邪惡友，終不追從善知識教，是故生悲。又見眾生多有財寶，不能捨施，是故生悲。又見眾生耕田種作，商賈販賣，一切皆苦，是故生悲。又見眾生父母、兄弟、妻子、奴婢、眷屬、宗室不相愛念，是故生悲。善男子！有智之人，應觀非想非非想處所有定樂如地獄苦，一切眾生等共有之，是故生悲。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Seeing beings breaking and violating the precepts, and so bound to undergo [rebirth in] hell, as a hungry ghost, or as an animal — compassion arises. Seeing beings unable to gain mastery over their own looks, strength, lifespan, well-being, or eloquence — compassion arises. Seeing beings whose faculties are incomplete — compassion arises. Seeing beings born in outlying lands, where wholesome practice is never cultivated — compassion arises. Seeing beings living through an age of famine, their bodies wasted and thin, plundering one another — compassion arises. Seeing beings living through an age of the sword, slaughtering one another, their malice growing ever stronger, bound to reap boundless suffering as its fruit — compassion arises. Seeing beings who, meeting a Buddha appearing in the world and hearing the pure Dharma of ambrosia taught, are still unable to receive and uphold it — compassion arises. Seeing beings who trust wicked, evil companions and will never follow the teaching of a good spiritual friend — compassion arises. Seeing beings who, though possessing much wealth and treasure, cannot bring themselves to give it away — compassion arises. Seeing beings plowing the fields and planting crops, buying and selling as merchants — all of it nothing but suffering — compassion arises. Seeing beings — parents and children, brothers, husbands and wives, servants, relatives, and clansmen — failing to love and care for one another — compassion arises. Good man, the wise should regard even the meditative bliss of the Peak of Neither-Perception-Nor-Non-Perception as no different from the suffering of hell, for all sentient beings share equally in it — and so, even here, compassion arises."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu draws out a pointed, historically grounded reading of the observation about "an age of the sword" — mutual slaughter and swelling malice among nations — noting that if the peoples of the world could genuinely arrive at this same compassion, the wish for peace it produces could actually be realized; writing as he did through years of war in his own lifetime, he clearly did not intend the line as an abstraction. He treats the closing observation as the true capstone of the entire list of thirty-six: rebirth at the Peak of Neither-Perception-Nor-Non-Perception is the most refined state conditioned existence has to offer, its inhabitants absorbed in meditative bliss for a lifespan of eighty thousand great eons — and yet, since this bliss is still bound within saṃsāra, its beings are certain to fall again, potentially even into hell, once that immense span finally runs out. If compassion is warranted even for beings enjoying the very summit of worldly bliss, he argues, then plainly nothing within saṃsāra stands outside its reach.

8. Milestone: Compassion Versus Great Compassion — Four Marks of the Difference

Having laid out thirty-six ways compassion can arise, the Buddha now distinguishes ordinary compassion from the "great compassion" of one who has actually attained the Way — not as two different feelings, but as the same compassion transformed along four specific axes.

Chinese Source

「善男子！未得道時，作如是觀，是名為悲；若得道已，即名大悲。何以故？未得道時，雖作是觀，觀皆有邊，眾生亦爾；既得道已，觀及眾生皆悉無邊，是故得名為大悲也。未得道時，悲心動轉，是故名悲；既得道已，無有動轉，故名大悲。未得道時，未能救濟諸眾生故，故名為悲；既得道已，能大救濟，故名大悲。未得道時，不共慧行，是故名悲；既得道已，與慧共行，故名大悲。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, before attaining the Way, contemplating in this manner is called 'compassion'; once the Way has been attained, it is called 'great compassion.' Why is this? Before attaining the Way, although one contemplates in this way, both the contemplation itself and the beings [it embraces] remain bounded; once the Way has been attained, both the contemplation and the beings it embraces become boundless — this is why it is then called great compassion. Before attaining the Way, the compassionate mind still wavers and shifts, and so it is called compassion; once the Way has been attained, it no longer wavers or shifts, and so it is called great compassion. Before attaining the Way, one is not yet able to rescue all beings, and so it is called compassion; once the Way has been attained, one is able to rescue them on a great scale, and so it is called great compassion. Before attaining the Way, [compassion] does not yet operate together with wisdom, and so it is called compassion; once the Way has been attained, it operates together with wisdom, and so it is called great compassion."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu ties "attaining the Way" here to the realization of the emptiness of self — or, more fully, the emptiness of both self and phenomena — the level reached from stream-entry onward, or by a pratyekabuddha, or by a bodhisattva of the first ground; taken more loosely, he allows that even the resolute conduct of one who has newly given rise to the awakening-mind at the first stage of the ten abidings could be included. He is careful that the four marks are not four different compassions but one compassion viewed from four angles: how wide its scope reaches, how stable it remains under provocation, how much it can actually accomplish, and whether it moves in tandem with wisdom or on its own. The last of the four he treats as decisive — compassion without wisdom, in his reading, easily slides into sentimental attachment; it is only once compassion and wisdom operate as a single motion that it deserves the name "great."

9. Milestone: The Benefit of Cultivating Compassion — Root of All Six Perfections

Before turning to the practical question of who finds compassion hardest to cultivate, the Buddha states its benefit plainly: even short of actually ending anyone's suffering, cultivating compassion is itself the generative cause behind every one of the six pāramitās.

Chinese Source

「善男子！智者修悲，雖未能斷眾生苦惱，已有無量大利益事。善男子！六波羅蜜皆以悲心而作生因。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, when the wise cultivate compassion, even though they are not yet able to cut off the suffering and affliction of sentient beings, this already brings immeasurable, great benefit. Good man, all six pāramitās take the compassionate mind as their generative cause."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu keeps this passage brief and practical: simply holding sentient beings in mutual regard, free of the hostility and resentment that otherwise color most relationships, already constitutes an immeasurable benefit in its own right, entirely apart from whether any being's actual suffering has yet been relieved. Read together with the chapter's opening claim that compassion is the seed of the bodhi-mind, this line extends the same logic one step further — every perfection a bodhisattva later cultivates, from giving through wisdom, is itself downstream of this same compassionate root, not a separate achievement layered on top of it.

10. Milestone: Monastic and Lay Bodhisattvas — Why Compassion Is Harder, and More Necessary, at Home

The chapter turns to a distinction central to this entire sutra's concerns: between the monastic bodhisattva, for whom cultivating compassion comes relatively easily, and the lay bodhisattva, for whom it is genuinely difficult — and for whom, precisely because of that difficulty, cultivating it is what actually confers the upāsaka precepts in the first place.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩有二種：一者、出家，二者、在家。出家修悲，是不為難；在家修悲，是乃為難。何以故？在家之人多有惡因緣故。善男子！在家之人若不修悲，則不能得優婆塞戒，若修悲已，即便獲得。善男子！出家之人，唯能具足五波羅蜜，不能具足檀波羅蜜，在家之人則能具足。何以故？一切時中一切施故。是故在家應先修悲，若修悲已，當知是人能具戒、忍、進、定、智慧。若修悲心，難施能施、難忍能忍、難作能作，以是義故，一切善法悲為根本。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, there are two kinds of bodhisattva: the home-leaving [monastic] and the home-dwelling [lay]. For the home-leaving bodhisattva, cultivating compassion is not difficult; for the home-dwelling bodhisattva, cultivating compassion is truly difficult. Why is this? Because the home-dweller is surrounded by many evil conditions. Good man, if a home-dwelling person does not cultivate compassion, he cannot obtain the upāsaka precepts; once he has cultivated compassion, he obtains them at once. Good man, the home-leaving person is able to fulfill only five of the pāramitās, and cannot fulfill the pāramitā of giving in full; the home-dwelling person, however, is able to fulfill it completely. Why is this? Because he can give at every time and in every way. For this reason, the home-dweller should cultivate compassion first — and once compassion has been cultivated, know that this person thereby becomes complete in discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom as well. When the compassionate mind is cultivated, one becomes able to give what is hard to give, to bear what is hard to bear, and to do what is hard to do. For this reason, compassion is the root of all wholesome dharmas."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads "evil conditions" plainly as the ordinary entanglements of household life — providing for oneself and one's family pulls attention constantly toward self-interest, making genuine compassion for others harder to sustain than it is for someone who has already left all of that behind. But he treats the sutra's central claim here as remarkably direct: whether a lay person actually receives the upāsaka precepts turns on this cultivation of compassion specifically — compassion functions as the very substance of the precept, not merely a virtue that accompanies it. He also draws out the giving comparison carefully: monastics, he notes, mostly practice the giving of Dharma, since they rarely hold material wealth to give away, while lay bodhisattvas can practice both material and Dharma giving at every opportunity — but only once compassion comes first, in keeping with the maxim that compassion is the basis and skillful means the gate through which it acts. Without that basis, he warns, greed and anger arise all the more easily in lay life, and even acts that look like generosity curdle into something else; wisdom itself, cut off from compassion, remains mere words on a page rather than anything actually lived.

11. Milestone: The Closing Simile — Compassion That Levels Mount Sumeru

The chapter closes with a single striking image of scale: a compassion genuinely cultivated is powerful enough to dissolve evil karma as vast as Mount Sumeru itself, and to turn even the smallest wholesome act into a fruit of matching immensity.

Chinese Source

「善男子！若人能修如是悲心，當知是人能壞惡業如須彌山，不久當得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提，是人所作少許善業，所獲果報如須彌山。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good man, if a person is able to cultivate a compassionate mind such as this, know that this person is able to destroy evil karma as vast as Mount Sumeru, and will before long attain anuttarā samyaksaṃbodhi [unsurpassed, perfect awakening]. Whatever small measure of wholesome karma such a person creates, the fruit and reward it reaps will be as vast as Mount Sumeru."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu closes this chapter with a compact image of his own: compassion is like fire, and evil karma is like firewood — wherever fire actually burns, the wood stacked upon it cannot remain unconsumed for long. In exactly the same way, he says, wherever genuine compassion is actually present, accumulated evil karma cannot simply persist unaffected — which is why the sutra can promise swift progress toward unsurpassed awakening for anyone who cultivates it in earnest. Read back against the chapter's opening question, the arc is now complete: compassion was named as the seed of the bodhi-mind at the very start, and here, at the close, that same seed is shown bearing fruit disproportionate to its size — a small store of goodness returning a reward as vast as the very karma it was strong enough to dissolve.
