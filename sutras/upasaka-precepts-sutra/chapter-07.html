Chapter 7 · 優婆塞戒經 · 發願品第七 · The one continuous vow that a bodhisattva actually makes

The Vow That Makes the Bodhisattva

Having spent the previous two chapters urging the bodhisattva to arouse great compassion and set out on the path, the sutra now turns from exhortation to content: what, exactly, does a bodhisattva vow? Shansheng asks the Buddha who is actually capable of creating the karma behind the thirty-two auspicious marks just discussed in the chapter before this one, and the answer — "the wise, who make the supreme vow" — opens onto one continuous vow, recited almost without pause, that runs from the wish never to retreat from awakening all the way to the wish to feed the starving with one's own body. The chapter closes by naming what such a vow actually earns: not yet the rank of a sovereign "Dharma-king," but the standing of an elder already rich in the wealth of the Dharma — and by noting, pointedly, that the householder who manages to keep this vow amid ordinary entanglements has done something harder than the renunciant who keeps it in seclusion.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 28 chapters translated Source: Taishō T24, no. 1488 Commentary guide: Grand Master Taixu's Lecture Notes on the Sutra on Upasaka Precepts (優婆塞戒經講錄)

1. Milestone: Who Is Wise? The Question That Opens the Chapter

The chapter opens with Shansheng picking up directly where the previous chapter on the thirty-two auspicious marks left off, asking who is actually capable of creating the karma behind them. The Buddha's answer redefines "wisdom" itself, in this context, as nothing other than the capacity to make a certain kind of vow.

Chinese Source

善生言：「世尊！是三十二相業誰能作耶？」佛言：「善男子！智者能作。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Shansheng said: "World-Honored One, who is capable of creating the karma of the thirty-two marks?" The Buddha said: "Good son, the wise are capable of it."

Chinese Source

「世尊！云何名智者？」「善男子！若能善發無上大願，是名智者。

Ru-Yi Translation

"World-Honored One, what is meant by 'the wise'?" "Good son, one who is able to skillfully make the supreme, unsurpassed vow — this is called 'the wise.'"

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu pauses on the word "vow" itself before the chapter's own vow ever begins. He treats vow (願) as a particular kind of desire — desire itself being a morally neutral capacity that can turn wholesome, unwholesome, or neither, so that a genuine vow is precisely desire that has turned wholly toward the wholesome. He also insists on a distinction easy to blur: a vow is not the same as dedicating merit. Dedication redirects merit a practitioner has already earned toward some further purpose; a vow comes first, before any merit exists at all — it is what a person forms in order to begin cultivating anything in the first place, close to what ordinary Chinese usage simply calls "setting one's resolve." He compares the forming of a vow to a seed breaking open into a sprout: nothing has grown yet, but the entire direction of the growth to come has already been set.

Read against that background, "the wise" in this exchange is not a generic compliment. Taixu reads "unsurpassed" (無上) as a precise claim about scope — the broadest possible mind and the most far-reaching aspiration a being can hold — so that wisdom, in this chapter's own definition, is measured by the size of what one is willing to vow, not by any private insight kept to oneself.

2. Milestone: The Ground of the Vow — Bodhicitta Turned Toward Every Being

Before giving the vow's actual words, the sutra first describes the disposition a bodhisattva-mahāsattva must already hold: every wholesome deed already dedicated outward, and a habit of drawing near to teachers to receive the deep Dharma and hold onto it without loss.

Chinese Source

「菩薩摩訶薩發菩提心已，身、口、意等所作善業，願為眾生；將來得果，一切共之。菩薩摩訶薩常親近佛、聲聞、緣覺、善知識等，供養恭敬，諮問深法，受持不失，作是願言：

Ru-Yi Translation

"Once a bodhisattva-mahāsattva has aroused the mind of awakening, whatever wholesome karma is created through body, speech, and mind, he dedicates to all beings; and when the fruit of it ripens in the future, he shares it with all of them in common. A bodhisattva-mahāsattva constantly draws near to buddhas, śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and good spiritual friends, making offerings to them with reverence, inquiring into the profound Dharma, and upholding what he receives without ever letting it slip away. He then makes this vow:

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads "mahāsattva" as marking a genuinely advanced bodhisattva — one who has aroused bodhicitta in the full sense, which he places at the first abiding stage or the first ground and beyond, not a beginner making a first resolution. He also situates this vow within a family of vows Buddhist readers would already recognize: Samantabhadra's ten great vows, Bhaiṣajyaguru's twelve vows, Amitābha's forty-eight vows — each shaped differently around a particular bodhisattva's own path, while the four universal vows remain the one vow every bodhisattva shares in common. What all of them hold in common, he notes, is the simple wish that every being come to share fully in whatever merit is produced — exactly the shape the sutra's own opening line takes here. "Drawing near" to buddhas and teachers, itself one of Samantabhadra's own ten vows, is accordingly the vow's very first move.

3. Milestone: The Vow, Part One — Never Retreating, Meeting Cruelty With More Compassion

The vow proper now begins, opening with the resolve never to retreat from awakening no matter what suffering or abuse is encountered, followed by a series of wishes concerning the bodhisattva's own body, standing, and autonomy in future lives — the raw capacity needed to actually carry the rest of the vow out.

Chinese Source

『我今親近諸佛、聲聞、緣覺、善友，寧無量世受大苦惱，不於菩提生退轉心！眾生若以惡心打罵、毀辱我身，願我因是更增慈心，不生惡念！願我後生，在在處處不受女身、無根、二根、奴婢之身！復願令我身有自在力為他給使，不令他人有自在力而驅使我！願令我身諸根具足，遠離惡友，不生惡國邊裔之處，常生豪姓，色力殊特，財寶自在！得好念心、自在之心，心得勇健！凡有所說，聞者樂受，離諸障礙！

Ru-Yi Translation

'Now that I draw near to the buddhas, the śrāvakas, the pratyekabuddhas, and good friends — even should I undergo great suffering and affliction for countless ages, may I never once let a thought of retreat from bodhi arise! Should beings strike, revile, or insult this body of mine out of ill will, may this only increase my loving-kindness all the more, and may no evil thought ever arise in me because of it! May I, in every life to come, in every place, never be born in a female body, a body lacking male faculties, a body of indeterminate sex, or the body of a slave! May this body of mine always have the mastery to be of service to others, and never let another have the mastery to command me against my will! May this body of mine have all its faculties whole and complete; may I stay far from evil companions and never be born into an evil land or an uncivilized borderland; may I always be born into an eminent family, with an exceptional bearing and strength, and with mastery over wealth! May I attain a mind of wholesome recollection, a mind of true mastery, and a mind made bold and vigorous! Whatever I speak, may those who hear it receive it gladly, free of every obstruction!

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu is precise about what "non-retrogression" actually requires here: not merely surviving abuse, insult, and blows without breaking, but responding to them by letting compassion increase rather than letting resentment take root — he treats this reaction itself, not mere endurance, as the real content of great compassion. His reading of the vow against female, sexless, intersex, and enslaved rebirth is functional rather than doctrinal: a bodhisattva who means to labor at length on behalf of beings needs a body equal to that labor, and he glosses each of these specifically as a lack of the bodily strength, standing, or autonomy the work requires — "slave" (奴婢), in particular, he reads simply as lacking the self-mastery to act rather than be acted upon. That same concern for autonomy carries through the wish to command one's own service rather than be commanded: a bodhisattva must retain real power to teach and transform beings, never becoming a tool wielded by someone else's will.

His gloss on "evil land" points not to any particular nation but to wherever unjust rulers or unjust law prevail, while "borderland" he reads as no fixed geography at all, but any place lacking culture, learning, or a climate fit for sustained practice. "Born into an eminent family" he reads straightforwardly as a matter of practical reach: such standing draws the esteem needed to be heard and heeded, not a mark of privilege for its own sake. "Exceptional bearing and strength" is, similarly, raw physical capacity for the work ahead; and "wholesome recollection" (好念) is nothing more than a mind that keeps constantly generating wholesome thought.

4. Milestone: The Vow, Part Two — Vigilance, Selfless Benefit, and Teaching Onward

The vow now turns outward, from safeguarding the bodhisattva's own capacity to practice toward the active work of benefiting beings without any thought of personal gain or gratitude, and passing on whatever is learned to others in turn.

Chinese Source

無有放逸，離身、口、意一切惡業，常為眾生作大利益！為利眾生，不貪身命，不為身命而造惡業！利眾生時，莫求恩報！常樂受持十二部經，既受持已，轉教他人！能壞眾生惡見、惡業，一切世事所不能勝；既得勝已，復以轉教！

Ru-Yi Translation

May I be free of every laxity, free of all evil karma of body, speech, and mind, and forever bring about great benefit for beings! In benefiting beings, may I never covet my own body or life, nor commit evil karma for the sake of preserving them! And when I benefit beings, may I never seek gratitude or reward in return! May I always delight in receiving and upholding the twelve divisions of the scriptures, and having received and upheld them, pass them on in turn to teach others! May I be able to break down beings' wrong views and evil karma, and be unsurpassed in every worldly affair; and having gained mastery over these, may I likewise pass that mastery on in teaching!

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this middle stretch of the vow as a turn from personal safeguarding toward active service: having first vowed to protect his own capacity to practice, the bodhisattva now vows never to fall into laxity, and to direct whatever benefit he creates entirely outward, never treating his own body or life as something to be protected for its own sake. The insistence on "never seeking gratitude or reward" he treats as the test that separates genuine giving from a disguised transaction — a gift made in expectation of thanks has not actually been given away. And the pairing of "receiving and upholding" the twelve divisions of scripture with "teaching them onward to others" he reads as a single continuous motion rather than two separate acts: for a bodhisattva, learning the Dharma and transmitting it are not sequential stages but one and the same undertaking.

5. Milestone: The Vow, Part Three — Healing, Feeding, and a Wish for All Who Meet Me

The vow reaches its most extravagant wishes here — giving up one's own body to feed the starving, reconciling the estranged, rescuing the terrified — before closing on a single wish that anyone who so much as sees, hears of, or touches this body be freed from affliction. A final line explains what this entire vow is actually for.

Chinese Source

善治眾生身、心重病，見離壞者能令和合，見怖畏者為作救護，護已為說種種之法，令彼聞已心得調伏！見飢施身，令得飽滿，願彼不生貪惡之心；當噉我時，如食草木！常樂供養師長、父母、善友、宿德，於怨親中其心等一！常修六念及無我想、十二因緣！無三寶處，樂在寂靜，修集慈悲！一切眾生若見我身，聞、觸之者，遠離煩惱！』

Ru-Yi Translation

May I be skilled at healing beings' grave sicknesses of body and mind; wherever I see those who are estranged and broken apart, may I be able to reconcile them; wherever I see those in terror, may I become their protector and refuge, and having protected them, teach them the various teachings, so that upon hearing them their minds become tamed! Wherever I see the starving, may I give this very body of mine, that they may be filled and satisfied, wishing that they give rise to no thought of greed or ill will in doing so — may it be, when they eat of me, just as though they were eating grass or wood! May I always delight in making offerings to teachers, parents, good friends, and elders of long-standing virtue, holding my mind equally toward friend and foe alike! May I constantly cultivate the six recollections, the contemplation of no-self, and the twelve links of dependent origination! Wherever the Three Jewels are absent, may I delight in dwelling in stillness there, cultivating loving-kindness and compassion! And may all beings who see this body of mine, or who hear of it or touch it, be freed from their afflictions!'

Chinese Source

菩薩雖知除菩提已不求餘果，為眾生故，求以弘利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although the bodhisattva knows that, apart from bodhi itself, there is no other fruit worth seeking, it is for the sake of beings that he seeks this vast, far-reaching benefit.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu identifies the "grave sicknesses of body and mind" named here specifically with affliction (煩惱) — the bodhisattva's medicine is aimed less at physical illness than at greed, hatred, and delusion themselves. The startling image of the starving eating this very body "as though eating grass or wood" he reads consistently with the vow's earlier concern for autonomy: even in total self-giving, the bodhisattva still directs the encounter, deliberately shaping it so the recipient's mind takes on no burden of guilt or craving in return — the gift is engineered to leave no debt behind it. The six recollections and twelve links of dependent origination he treats as standard practice already familiar from earlier in the sutra, invoked here not to introduce new content but to root even this most extravagant vow in ordinary, disciplined contemplation; and "the Three Jewels" he glosses simply as Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha together — so that seeking out places where all three are absent, in order to practice loving-kindness precisely there, becomes the vow's own quiet argument that compassion belongs most where it is least supported.

6. Milestone: The Fruit of the Vow — An Elder Rich in Dharma, Not Yet a Dharma-King

Having given the vow's actual words, the sutra now names what establishing it actually earns: not the sovereign rank of "Dharma-king" itself, but the standing of an elder already rich in the wealth of the Dharma. It then defines that standing precisely, through fourteen sets of three qualities each.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩若能如是立願，當知是人即是無上法財長者，是求法王未得法王。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, if a bodhisattva is able to establish a vow such as this, know that this person is already an elder unsurpassed in the wealth of the Dharma — one who seeks the rank of Dharma-king, though he has not yet attained it.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩摩訶薩具足三事，則得名為法財長者：一者、心不甘樂外道典籍，二者、心不貪著生死之樂，三者、常樂供養佛、法、僧寶。「復有三事：一者、為人受苦心不生悔，二者、具足微妙無上智慧，三者、具善法時不生憍慢。「復有三事：一者、為諸眾生受地獄苦如三禪樂，二者、見他得利不生妬心，三者、所作善業不為生死。「復有三事：一者、見他受苦如己無異，二者、所修善事悉為眾生，三者、善作方便令彼離苦。「復有三事：一者、觀生死樂如大毒蛇，二者、樂處生死為利眾生，三者、觀無生法忍多諸功德。「復有三事：一者、捨身、二者、捨命，三者、捨財；捨是三事悉為眾生。「復有三事：一者、多聞無厭，二者、能忍諸惡，三者、教他修忍。「復有三事：一者、自省己過，二者、善覆他罪，三者、樂修慈心。「復有三事：一者、至心奉持禁戒，二者、四攝攝取眾生，三者、口言柔濡不麁。「復有三事：一者、能大法施，二者、能大財施，三者、以此二施勸眾生行。「復有三事：一者、常以大乘教化眾生，二者、常修轉進增上之行，三者、於諸眾生不生輕想。「復有三事：一者、雖具煩惱而能堪忍，二者、知煩惱過樂而不厭，三者、自具煩惱能壞他結。「復有三事：一者、見他得利歡喜如己，二者、自得安樂不樂獨受，三者、於下乘中不生足想。「復有三事：一者、聞諸菩薩苦行不怖，二者、見有求者終不言無，三者、終不生念我勝一切。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, when a bodhisattva-mahāsattva is complete with three things, he may be called an elder in the wealth of the Dharma: first, his mind takes no pleasure in the scriptures of other paths; second, his mind is not attached to the pleasures of birth-and-death; third, he constantly delights in making offerings to the Three Jewels — Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha. There are, again, three things: first, in suffering on others' behalf, his mind never gives rise to regret; second, he is complete with subtle and unsurpassed wisdom; third, when he possesses wholesome qualities, he gives rise to no arrogance. There are, again, three things: first, in undergoing the suffering of hell for the sake of beings, he experiences it as though it were the bliss of the third dhyāna; second, seeing another gain some benefit, he gives rise to no envy; third, whatever wholesome karma he creates is never done for the sake of birth-and-death. There are, again, three things: first, seeing another's suffering, he feels it exactly as though it were his own; second, whatever wholesome deeds he cultivates are entirely for the sake of beings; third, he skillfully devises means to free them from suffering. There are, again, three things: first, he views the pleasures of birth-and-death as though they were a great venomous serpent; second, he delights in dwelling within birth-and-death for the sake of benefiting beings; third, he contemplates the many virtues contained within the forbearance of the unborn nature of things. There are, again, three things: first, giving up his body; second, giving up his life; third, giving up his wealth — and all three of these are given up entirely for the sake of beings. There are, again, three things: first, he learns widely without ever growing weary of it; second, he is able to endure every form of ill-treatment; third, he teaches others to cultivate patience. There are, again, three things: first, he reflects constantly on his own faults; second, he skillfully covers over the faults of others; third, he delights in cultivating a mind of loving-kindness. There are, again, three things: first, he upholds the precepts with a wholehearted mind; second, he gathers beings to him through the four means of embrace; third, his speech is gentle and soft, never coarse. There are, again, three things: first, he is able to give the great gift of the Dharma; second, he is able to give the great gift of wealth; third, he encourages beings to practice both of these forms of giving in turn. There are, again, three things: first, he constantly teaches and transforms beings by means of the Great Vehicle; second, he constantly cultivates practice that advances ever further and higher; third, toward all beings he gives rise to no thought of contempt. There are, again, three things: first, although he still possesses afflictions, he is able to bear with them; second, though he knows the faults of affliction, he still delights in the world and is never disheartened by it; third, even while possessing afflictions of his own, he is able to break the fetters of others. There are, again, three things: first, seeing another gain some benefit, he rejoices as though it were his own; second, when he himself attains ease and happiness, he takes no pleasure in enjoying it alone; third, toward the lesser vehicles he gives rise to no thought of contentment. There are, again, three things: first, hearing of the austerities practiced by other bodhisattvas, he feels no fear; second, seeing one who comes to ask something of him, he never once says he has none to give; third, he never once gives rise to the thought that he surpasses all others.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu offers little further gloss on the fourteen triads themselves, remarking simply that they can be understood directly from the text — a rare moment where the commentary steps back and lets the sutra's own list do the work unaided. It is still worth noticing the deliberate borrowing in the title itself: "elder" (長者) ordinarily names someone of worldly wealth and standing, and the sutra repurposes the term precisely to claim that a bodhisattva who has established this vow already possesses a wealth measured in Dharma rather than property — real standing, just not the kind ordinarily recognized as such.

Read as a sequence, the fourteen triads move from foundational orientation (indifference to rival teachings, delight in the Three Jewels) through active service to beings (bearing suffering for others, breaking their afflictions, feeding them the Dharma and material aid alike) toward, by the final triad, an almost complete disappearance of self-regard — fearlessness before austerity, never refusing a request, never once thinking oneself superior to anyone else.

7. Milestone: The Sovereign Dharma-King Who Masters Cause and Effect

The sutra closes its discussion of the vow's fruit by explaining what the higher rank of "Dharma-king," still unattained, would actually require: not merely tracing effect from cause, but the reverse direction too, and the power both to cut off and to attain cause-and-effect at will.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩若能觀因觀果，能觀因果，能觀果因，如是菩薩能斷因果，能得因果。菩薩若能斷、得因果，是名法果，諸法之王，法之自在。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, if a bodhisattva is able to contemplate cause and to contemplate effect — able to observe effect arising from cause, and able to observe cause revealed through effect — then such a bodhisattva is able to cut off cause-and-effect, and is able to attain cause-and-effect. If a bodhisattva is able both to cut off and to attain cause-and-effect in this way, he is called one who has realized the fruit of the Dharma, a king among all dharmas, sovereign and self-mastered within the Dharma.

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu draws a careful distinction between the two directions of contemplation named here: observing effect arising from cause is the ordinary direction, while observing cause revealed through effect runs backward — his example is contemplating the Four Noble Truths starting from suffering and its origin, then working back to see how they were produced, rather than starting from a cause and predicting its result. He maps the sutra's paired "cutting off" and "attaining" cause-and-effect onto two classical virtues at once: cutting off names the virtue of elimination, ridding oneself of afflicted causes and their results, while attaining names the combined virtue of wisdom and beneficent action, the positive causes and results a bodhisattva builds up in their place.

"King," in his reading, means precisely self-mastery — commanding all things while being commanded by none — and he reaches for the Lotus Sūtra's own claim, "I am the Dharma King, sovereign within the Dharma," as the standard this term invokes. He adds a pointed qualification: an ordinary, unawakened mind is also capable of generating every kind of mental phenomenon, and is sometimes called a "mind-king" for exactly this reason — but because it has not yet achieved actual mastery over what it generates, it does not yet earn the title "Dharma-king."

8. Milestone: Harder at Home — Why the Householder's Vow Weighs More

The chapter closes with a single, pointed comparison: the same vow is far harder for a householder to sustain than for a renunciant, precisely because the householder remains bound by the ordinary entanglements of family and worldly life that a renunciant has already set aside.

Chinese Source

「善男子！菩薩有二種：一者、在家，二者、出家。出家菩薩立如是願，是不為難；在家菩薩立如是願，是乃為難。何以故？在家菩薩多惡因緣所纏繞故。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, there are two kinds of bodhisattva: the first is the householder, the second is the renunciant. For a renunciant bodhisattva to establish a vow such as this is not difficult; for a householder bodhisattva to establish a vow such as this is truly difficult. Why is this so? Because the householder bodhisattva is bound about by so many adverse conditions and entanglements."

Reading note — after Grand Master Taixu

Taixu reads this closing comparison less as praise of the layperson than as a standing warning against complacency: the point of naming the householder's path as the harder one is to keep urging constant diligence and vigilance against laxity, prodding the resolve along with repeated self-admonition rather than letting it settle into ease. The household bodhisattva, bound about by family, livelihood, and all the ordinary entanglements the world imposes, cannot rely on a secluded environment to make the vow easier to keep — which is precisely why keeping it there carries the greater weight.
