The Tripiṭaka Teaching, from Raw Milk to the Phantom City

Part 2 opens the Four Teachings of Content with a single-line answer to why a Dharma without any real thing to it should still divide into four: illness differs, so medicine differs. It then works through the first and most elementary of the four, the Tripiṭaka teaching, in complete doctrinal detail — its own version of the Four Noble Truths, the twelve links of dependent origination, the six pāramitās, and the two truths, its governing contemplation of analytical emptiness, its own six identities from an utterly ordinary person through the stream-enterer and on to arhatship or Hīnayāna buddhahood, and the ten modes of contemplation that form its vehicle.

Why Four Medicines for a Dharma with Nothing in It

Chinese Source

化法四教說 法尚無一。云何有四。乃如來利他妙智。因眾生病而設藥也。見思病重。為說三藏教。見思病輕。為說通教。無明病重。為說別教。無明病輕。為說圓教。

三藏教。四阿含為經藏。毘尼為律藏。阿毘曇為論藏。此教詮生滅四諦(苦則生異滅三相遷移。集則貪瞋癡等分四心流動。道則對治易奪。滅則滅有還無)

Ru-Yi Translation

On the Four Teachings of Content. Even a single dharma does not exist — how, then, could there be four? It is simply that the Tathāgata, in his wondrous wisdom for benefiting others, prescribes medicine according to the sentient being's own illness. Where the illness of views and habituated thought is severe, he teaches the Tripiṭaka teaching; where that illness is light, he teaches the shared teaching. Where the illness of ignorance is severe, he teaches the distinct teaching; where that illness is light, he teaches the perfect teaching.

The Tripiṭaka teaching: the four Āgamas form the sūtra-piṭaka, the Vinaya forms the discipline-piṭaka, and the Abhidharma forms the treatise-piṭaka. This teaching expounds the Four Noble Truths of generation-and-extinction (suffering is the shifting of the three marks of arising, changing, and ceasing; accumulation is the flowing agitation of the four minds — greed, anger, delusion, and the equal-portion affliction; the path is treatment that overturns and dislodges affliction; cessation is the extinguishing of existence, returning to nothingness).

Reading note — after Ouyi Zhixu

On "even a single dharma does not exist, how could there be four," Zhixu reads this opening line as the treatise announcing its own ultimate self-cancellation before it has said anything else: "all dharmas are marked by stillness and extinction, beyond what words can convey"; from the very beginning, all dharmas are, of themselves, forever marked by stillness and extinction — where "the four" is concerned, what "one" could there be either? Setting up medicine according to illness gives, in outline, only four; opening the provisional to reveal the real gives, in the end, only one. "One" names the real, "three" name the provisional, and provisional and real are only ever spoken of relative to each other — both are simply what cannot be helped. If one presses further, the real cannot even be called "one," any more than the provisional can be called "three" — hence it is said to be neither provisional nor real. Yet this "neither provisional nor real" does not mean some separate third thing exists outside provisional and real. The provisional is simply the real teaching's own provisional, and so is itself not-provisional; the real is simply the provisional teaching's own real, and so is itself not-real — exactly as a wave is simply water's own wave, and so is itself not-a-wave, and water is simply a wave's own water, and so is itself not-water. In the end both are one and the same wetness — and where would that wetness itself stand, apart from the wave and the water?

On the piṭaka names: "Āgama" (also transliterated Āgapta or Āgama) translates as "teaching," or "the tradition to which the Dharma returns," or "what was handed down as spoken," or "the unparalleled dharma." In its general sense both Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna are called Āgama; in its specific sense, the Ekottara Āgama clarifies the Dharma by way of numbered lists, the Long Āgama clarifies the arising of the world and similar matters, the Middle Āgama clarifies profound meanings, and the Miscellaneous Āgama clarifies the various dhyāna methods — and it was Mahākāśyapa who asked Ānanda to recite all four from memory at the First Council, hence "the four Āgamas." "Vinaya" (also transliterated Vinaya or Bhikṣu-vinaya) translates as "skillful taming," or "subduing," or "extinguishing," or simply "discipline." In its general sense whatever the Buddha taught is called the true Dharma's discipline; in its specific sense it names the fivefold sets of precept-categories laid down case by case as occasions arose. Mahākāśyapa asked Upāli to compile the Vinaya-piṭaka; at first it formed a single division, but later, owing to disputes, it split into the Mahāsāṃghika and Sthavira branches, and eventually into eighteen schools, of which five discipline-lineages have survived down through long transmission. "Abhidharma" (also transliterated Abhidharma) translates as "the unparalleled dharma," or "matching dharma." In its general sense whatever the Buddha taught is also called Abhidharma; in its specific sense it names Mahākāśyapa's own compilation of the Buddha's teaching on doctrine, together with the treatises composed by arhats — this alone is properly called the Abhidharma. Question: both the half-word and full-word teachings each have their own three piṭakas — why is the name "Tripiṭaka teaching" reserved for the Hīnayāna alone? Answer: for two reasons. First, in the half-word teaching the sūtra-piṭaka and discipline-piṭaka remain organizationally distinct bundles, while in the full-word teaching sūtra and discipline blend together as one. Second, the Lotus Sūtra speaks of "those attached in their studies to the small vehicle's Tripiṭaka," and the Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra everywhere uses the Mahāyāna to refute Tripiṭaka teachings — hence the half-word teaching is called "the Tripiṭaka teaching." Question: why not simply call it "the Hīnayāna teaching"? Answer: this teaching already contains the provisional dharma of all three vehicles within it, and so cannot be one-sidedly named "small."

The Twelve Links, the Six Pāramitās, and Two Truths of What Is Really There

Chinese Source

亦詮思議生滅十二因緣(無明緣行行緣識。識緣名色。名色緣六入。六入緣觸。觸緣受。受緣愛。愛緣取。取緣有。有緣生。生緣老死憂悲苦惱。無明滅則行滅。行滅則識滅。識滅則名色滅。名色滅則六入滅。六入滅則觸滅。觸滅則受滅。受滅則愛滅。愛滅則取滅。取滅則有滅。有滅則生滅。生滅則老死憂悲苦惱滅)亦詮事六度行(布施持戒忍辱精進禪定智慧)亦詮實有二諦(陰入界等實法為俗。實有滅乃為真)開示界內鈍根眾生。令修析空觀(觀於地水火風空識六界。無我我所)出分段生死。證偏真涅槃。正化二乘。傍化菩薩。亦得約當教自論六即。

Ru-Yi Translation

It also expounds the conceivable twelve links of dependent origination, generation-and-extinction (ignorance conditions volitional formations, volitional formations condition consciousness, consciousness conditions name-and-form, name-and-form conditions the six sense-bases, the six sense-bases condition contact, contact conditions feeling, feeling conditions craving, craving conditions grasping, grasping conditions becoming, becoming conditions birth, birth conditions aging-and-death, sorrow, grief, suffering, and distress. When ignorance ceases, volitional formations cease; when volitional formations cease, consciousness ceases; when consciousness ceases, name-and-form ceases; when name-and-form ceases, the six sense-bases cease; when the six sense-bases cease, contact ceases; when contact ceases, feeling ceases; when feeling ceases, craving ceases; when craving ceases, grasping ceases; when grasping ceases, becoming ceases; when becoming ceases, birth ceases; when birth ceases, aging-and-death, sorrow, grief, suffering, and distress cease). It also expounds the six pāramitās as actual practice (giving, upholding precepts, patience, diligence, dhyāna, and wisdom), and expounds the two truths of real existence (the real dharmas of the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements are the conventional; the real cessation of existence is the truth of the real). It is revealed for sentient beings of dull faculties within the three realms, having them cultivate the contemplation of analytical emptiness (contemplating the six elements — earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness — as without self or what belongs to self), so as to escape the birth-and-death of limited span and realize the one-sided nirvāṇa of the real. It directly converts the two vehicles, and incidentally converts bodhisattvas. One may also, within this teaching's own terms, discuss the six identities.

Reading note — after Ouyi Zhixu

On the twelve links, Zhixu supplies the fuller mechanics behind the base text's own bare chain, drawn — as he says explicitly — from the Yogācāra treatises rather than any Hīnayāna Abhidharma reading. Ignorance that initiates karma is of two kinds: the delusion of not knowing the ripening of karmic fruit precisely, which gives rise to evil karma in the three lower destinies; and the delusion of not knowing the three realms as impermanent and without self, which gives rise to attachment to human, heavenly, form-realm, and formless-realm rebirth and to the contaminated wholesome karma of dhyāna. From these two kinds of ignorance, once contaminated wholesome, unwholesome, and unmoving karma has been generated, the seed of future rebirth in the three realms is drawn into a single thought in the present, already complete with the seeds of name-and-form, the six sense-bases, contact, and feeling — this is "ignorance conditions volitional formations," down to "contact conditions feeling." Then, through the further ignorance of not understanding present circumstances as the fruit of past causes, craving arises toward them, and from craving, grasping — this is "feeling conditions craving," down to "craving conditions grasping." Through this watering by craving and grasping, the seeds of the five conditioned links gradually mature into the sprout of becoming, which at the body's death moves spontaneously toward rebirth, and having been reborn, spontaneously ages into death, giving rise to every sorrow and grief — this is "grasping conditions becoming," down to "birth conditions aging-and-death." Ignorance and volitional formations are thus the two links that draw the process forward; consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-bases, contact, and feeling are the five links drawn along by it; craving, grasping, and becoming are the three links that generate the next life; birth and aging-and-death are the two links generated by them. The ten links belong to cause, all within the present life; the two belong to fruit, specifically in the future — and from the fruit of birth-and-death, ignorance arises once more, and from ignorance, volitional formations arise once more, so that cause and effect throughout the three realms never break off, making plain both the wheel of rebirth and its freedom from the extremes of permanence and annihilation, without ever falling into infinite regress. Zhixu adds that although all ten fetters are, strictly speaking, each capable both of initiating karma and of nourishing rebirth, the initiating role is attributed to ignorance alone because its force there is strongest — the strong subsumes the weak — while the nourishing role is attributed to craving and grasping alone because their function there predominates — the superior subsumes the inferior.

On the "two truths of real existence," Zhixu is precise about what this teaching does and does not see through: it clarifies only the emptiness of the person, not the emptiness of dharmas — hence the five aggregates, twelve sense-bases, and eighteen elements are all held to be real dharmas. It is only in dependence on these real dharmas, gathered together, that a "person" is provisionally named; the person, though certainly without ultimate existence, still rests on dharmas that are themselves real — this is the conventional truth. Only once one cultivates the contemplation of no-self and severs views and habituated thought completely, extinguishing these real aggregates, sense-bases, and elements and reverting once more to real emptiness, does one arrive at the truth of the real.

The Six Identities, I: Ordinary Person to Outer Disciple

Chinese Source

理即者。偏真也。諸行無常。是生滅法。生滅滅已。寂滅為樂。因滅會真。滅非真諦。滅尚非真。況苦集道。真諦在因果事相之外。故依衍教判曰偏真。

名字即者。學名字也。知一切法從因緣生。不從時方梵天極微四大等生。亦非無因緣自然而生。知因緣所生法。皆悉無常無我。

觀行即者。一五停心。二別相念。三總相念。外凡資糧位也。五停心者。一多貪眾生不淨觀。二多瞋眾生慈悲觀。三多散眾生數息觀。四愚癡眾生因緣觀。五多障眾生念佛觀。以此五法為方便。調停其心。令堪修念處。故名停心也。別相念者。一觀身不淨。二觀受是苦。三觀心無常。四觀法無我。對治依於五蘊所起四倒也。總相念者。觀身不淨。受心法亦皆不淨。觀受是苦。心法身亦皆苦。觀心無常。法身受亦皆無常。觀法無我。身受心亦皆無我也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Identity in principle: this is the one-sided real. "All conditioned things are impermanent; this is the dharma of generation and extinction. When generation and extinction themselves are extinguished, this stillness-and-extinction is bliss." It is through extinction that one meets the real, yet extinction itself is not the truth of the real — indeed, extinction itself is not yet the real, how much less suffering, accumulation, and the path. The truth of the real lies entirely outside the phenomenal marks of cause and effect; hence, judged from the standpoint of the Mahāyāna teachings, it is called "one-sided."

Identity in name: this is learning the name. One knows that all dharmas arise from causes and conditions, not from time, place, Brahmā, subtle atoms, the four great elements, or the like, nor yet spontaneously, without cause or condition. One knows that whatever is produced through causes and conditions is, without exception, impermanent and without self.

Identity in contemplative practice: first, the five methods for stilling the mind; second, the specific-mark mindfulness; third, the general-mark mindfulness — these are the outer-ordinary stage of accumulating provisions. The five methods for stilling the mind are: first, for beings of much craving, the contemplation of impurity; second, for beings of much anger, the contemplation of loving-kindness; third, for beings of much distraction, the contemplation of counting the breath; fourth, for beings of much delusion, the contemplation of dependent origination; fifth, for beings of much hindrance, the contemplation of buddha-recollection. These five methods serve as skillful means, regulating and stilling the mind so that it becomes fit to cultivate the foundations of mindfulness — hence they are named the "stilling of the mind." The specific-mark mindfulness is: first, contemplating the body as impure; second, contemplating feeling as suffering; third, contemplating mind as impermanent; fourth, contemplating dharmas as without self — this counters the four inversions that arise in dependence on the five aggregates. The general-mark mindfulness is: contemplating the body as impure, one sees that feeling, mind, and dharmas are all likewise impure; contemplating feeling as suffering, one sees that mind, dharmas, and the body are all likewise suffering; contemplating mind as impermanent, one sees that dharmas, the body, and feeling are all likewise impermanent; contemplating dharmas as without self, one sees that the body, feeling, and mind are all likewise without self.

Reading note — after Ouyi Zhixu

Zhixu treats the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's four-line verse quoted for identity in principle as bearing two entirely independent readings, one "horizontal" and one "vertical," and works through both in detail here even though the base text's own six identities apply only the first, one-sided reading. Read horizontally, across all Four Teachings at once: for the Tripiṭaka teaching, the three realms' own dependent-and-direct retribution of form and mind are "all conditioned things," each and every one impermanent and marked by generation-and-extinction, and one must extinguish this very cause-and-effect to realize the true bliss of quiescent extinction. The shared teaching likewise takes the three realms' cause-and-effect as "all conditioned things," impermanent and generation-and-extinction, but embodies that generation-and-extinction as itself already extinguished, thereby realizing the same true bliss of quiescent extinction — yet in both these first two teachings, Zhixu stresses, there is in truth no one left over to receive that bliss at all, since no body or wisdom remains once extinction is complete. The distinct teaching names both segmented and transformational birth-and-death together as "all conditioned things," neither of them truly constant, both belonging to the gate of generation-and-extinction; extinguishing these two extremes reveals the principle of quiescent extinction at the middle way, and here, because the four wisdoms of bodhi and the mirror-like-wisdom-corresponding mind's own uncontaminated body and land of subtle form actually appear, this bliss truly is received. The perfect teaching names the cause-and-effect of all ten dharma-realms together as "all conditioned things," universally impermanent, universally marked by generation-and-extinction — yet it penetrates cause-and-effect as itself already the true mark of reality, impermanence as itself permanence, generation-and-extinction as itself no generation-and-extinction; hence "when generation and extinction themselves are extinguished, this stillness-and-extinction is bliss" — without severing delusion and craving, illumination and liberation nonetheless arise, as when melting ice becomes water, so that the three thousand realms, brought to fruition just as they are, are all alike called constant bliss. Read vertically instead, each of the four lines names one whole dharma-realm in turn: "all conditioned things are impermanent" gathers in the six ordinary dharma-realms, since whatever is conditioned and contaminated belongs here; "this is the dharma of generation and extinction" gathers in the Tripiṭaka and shared teachings' two-vehicle dharma-realm, since only a world-transcending noble one can actually know it as generation-and-extinction; "when generation and extinction themselves are extinguished" gathers in the distinct teaching's bodhisattva dharma-realm, since it extinguishes the two extremes to return to the middle way; "this stillness-and-extinction is bliss" gathers in the perfect teaching's buddha dharma-realm, since all dharmas, from the very outset, are constantly, of themselves, marked by stillness and extinction. Used here, within the one-sided real alone, to prove this teaching's own truth of the real: "all conditioned things are impermanent" and "this is the dharma of generation and extinction" together are the truths of suffering and accumulation; "when generation and extinction themselves are extinguished" is, taken as the three trainings that do the extinguishing, the truth of the path, and, taken as the cause-and-effect thereby extinguished, the truth of cessation — all four of these truths concern the marks of cause-and-effect as actual phenomena, hence "established truths"; "this stillness-and-extinction is bliss" concerns the principle thereby revealed as the truth of the real — a "truth not established" — hence it is said that "principle lies outside phenomena," and is called one-sided real.

The Six Identities, II: Warmth Before the Fire, and the Three Fruits of the Path

Chinese Source

相似即者。內凡加行位也。一煖二頂三忍四世第一。得色界有漏善根。能入見道。

分證即者。前三果有學位也。初須陀洹果。此云預流。用八忍八智。頓斷三界見惑。初預聖流。名見道位。二斯陀含果。此云一來。斷欲界六品思惑。餘三品在猶潤一生。三阿那含果。此云不還。斷欲界思惑盡。進斷上八地思。不復還來欲界。此二名修道位。

Ru-Yi Translation

Identity in resemblance: this is the inner-ordinary stage of intensified effort. First, heat; second, summit; third, patience; fourth, the supreme worldly dharma. One attains the contaminated wholesome roots of the form realm, and becomes able to enter the path of vision.

Identity in a portion of the truth: this is the stage of the first three fruits, still in training. First, the fruit of stream-entry, meaning "entering the stream." Using the eight patiences and eight kinds of knowledge, one instantly severs the afflictions of view throughout the three realms, first entering the stream of the noble ones — this is named the stage of the path of vision. Second, the fruit of once-returning, meaning "returning once more." One severs six of the nine grades of the desire realm's afflictions of habituated thought, the remaining three grades still nourishing one more rebirth. Third, the fruit of non-returning, meaning "not returning." One severs the desire realm's afflictions of habituated thought completely, and goes on to sever the habituated thought of the eight higher grounds, never again returning to the desire realm. These latter two are named the stage of the path of cultivation.

Reading note — after Ouyi Zhixu

On the four stages of resemblance, Zhixu keeps the classical similes attached to each name. Through the four foundations of mindfulness giving rise to the four right efforts, cutting off two evils and cultivating two goods, one diligently contemplates the Four Noble Truths and a resembling understanding is able to arise — like striking flint and first obtaining warmth before the fire itself appears — hence "heat." Through the four bases of supernormal power giving rise to concentration, the contemplation of the truths grows still clearer, like climbing to a mountain summit and seeing all four directions at once — hence "summit." Through concentration and wisdom becoming evenly balanced, wholesome dharmas advance, and the five faculties — faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — become able to stand firm and unmoving — hence "patience." Through the five faculties growing into five powers, able to break through the five obstructions and step onto the path of vision, this is the finest state attainable within any worldly, contaminated stage — hence "the supreme worldly dharma." All this contaminated wisdom gained through hearing, reflection, and cultivation serves only as a supporting condition, assisting one's own inherently uncontaminated seeds finally to manifest and enter the path of vision.

On the eight patiences and eight kinds of knowledge, Zhixu specifies the full structure: beneath each of the Four Noble Truths in the desire realm there is one patience and one knowledge — suffering-dharma patience, suffering-dharma knowledge, and so on for accumulation, cessation, and the path; beneath each of the Four Noble Truths in the form-and-formless realms together there is likewise one patience and one knowledge — suffering-analogy patience, suffering-analogy knowledge, and so on — combining to eight patiences and eight kinds of knowledge in all. Patience is uncontaminated concentration: on the uninterrupted path, concentration cuts off affliction, and this cutting-off is itself called patience — it is concentration inseparable from wisdom. Knowledge is uncontaminated wisdom: on the path of liberation, wisdom directly realizes the truth, and this realizing is itself called knowledge — it is wisdom inseparable from concentration.

The Six Identities, III: Thirty-Four Minds in a Single Instant

Chinese Source

究竟即者。三乘無學位也。一小乘第四阿羅漢果。此含三義。一殺賊二應供三無生。斷三界見思俱盡。子縛已斷果縛尚存。名有餘涅槃。若灰身泯智。名無餘涅槃。二中乘辟支佛果。此人根性稍利。逆順觀察十二因緣。斷見思惑。與羅漢同。更侵習氣。故居聲聞上。三大乘佛果。此人根性大利。從初發心。緣四諦境。發四弘誓。即名菩薩修行六度。初阿僧祇劫。事行雖強。理觀尚弱。準望聲聞。在外凡位。第二阿僧祇劫。諦解漸明。在煖位。第三阿僧祇劫。諦解轉明。在頂位。六度既滿。更住百劫。修相好因。在下忍位。次入補處生兜率天。乃至入胎出胎出家。降魔安坐不動時。是中忍位。次一剎那入上忍。次一剎那入世第一。發真無漏三十四心。頓斷見思。正習無餘。坐木菩提樹下。以生草為座。成劣應身(如釋迦丈六彌勒十六丈等)受梵王請。三轉法輪。度三根性。緣盡入滅。與阿羅漢辟支佛。究竟同證偏真法性。無復身智依正可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Identity in the ultimate: this is the stage beyond training, for the three vehicles. First, the Hīnayāna's fourth fruit, arhatship. This contains three meanings: killer of the thieves of affliction, worthy of offerings, and no further rebirth. One severs the afflictions of view and habituated thought throughout the three realms completely; the bondage of the arising cause is already severed, though the bondage of the resulting body still remains — this is named nirvāṇa with residue. If one further reduces the body to ash and extinguishes knowing altogether, this is named nirvāṇa without residue. Second, the middling vehicle's fruit of the pratyekabuddha. This person's capacity is somewhat sharper: contemplating the twelve links of dependent origination both forward and backward, he severs the afflictions of view and habituated thought the same as the arhat, but further encroaches upon their residual habituation — hence he ranks above the śrāvaka. Third, the Mahāyāna's fruit of buddhahood. This person's capacity is greatly sharp. From the first arousing of the resolve, taking the object of the Four Noble Truths, he brings forth the four universal vows — this is called the bodhisattva's cultivation of the six pāramitās. Through the first incalculable eon, though his practice in actual conduct is strong, his contemplation of principle is still weak; measured against the śrāvaka, he stands at the outer-ordinary stage. Through the second incalculable eon, his understanding of the truths gradually brightens; he stands at the stage of heat. Through the third incalculable eon, his understanding of the truths grows brighter still; he stands at the stage of the summit. Once the six pāramitās are fulfilled, he dwells a further hundred eons cultivating the causes for the marks of physical excellence; he stands at the stage of lower patience. He then enters his final rebirth before buddhahood, born in Tuṣita Heaven, and on through entering the womb, leaving the womb, going forth from home, and subduing Māra while seated unmoving — this is the stage of middle patience. In the next instant he enters upper patience; in the following instant he enters the supreme worldly dharma, and brings forth the true, uncontaminated thirty-four minds, instantly severing views and habituated thought, their habituation entirely without remainder. Seated beneath the wooden bodhi tree, on a seat of fresh-cut grass, he becomes an inferior response-body buddha (such as Śākyamuni's sixteen-foot body, or Maitreya's sixteen-mile body). Receiving Brahmā's request, he turns the Dharma-wheel three times, saving beings of three capacities; when his causal connections are exhausted he enters extinction. Together with the arhat and the pratyekabuddha, he realizes, in the end, the very same one-sided real dharma-nature — with no further body, wisdom, or dependent and direct retribution to be found.

Reading note — after Ouyi Zhixu

On the true, uncontaminated thirty-four minds, Zhixu shows how the count is reached: the path of vision's own eight patiences and eight kinds of knowledge together make sixteen minds; the path of cultivation, taken across the three realms' nine grounds, has for each ground one uninterrupted path and one path of liberation, making eighteen minds; vision and cultivation combined together make thirty-four minds in all. This concentration and wisdom both arise from beginninglessly, inherently possessed uncontaminated seeds; the merit and wisdom cultivated across three great incalculable eons serve only as the supporting condition, while the beginningless, dharma-nature-endowed uncontaminated seed is the direct, intimate cause — hence severance, realization, and manifestation all happen at once, instantly. Yet reckoned by the categories of affliction actually severed, this is spoken of as "thirty-four minds"; reckoned by what actually severs and realizes, it is simply uncontaminated concentration-and-wisdom, nothing more, and what is thereby realized is only the suchness of the emptiness of self — also called the uncompounded reality attained through discerning extinction. This uncompounded suchness does not, in itself, fall under any numerical count at all; it is only because the uncompounded dharma is spoken of in terms of the differences it makes that it comes to be named "thirty-four minds" — a count that itself further divides, in various further discussions, into gradual and sudden approaches.

Three Hundred Yojanas to the Phantom City

Chinese Source

此教具三乘法。聲聞觀四諦。以苦諦為初門。最利者三生。最鈍者六十劫。得證四果。辟支觀十二因緣。以集諦為初門。最利者四生。最鈍者百劫。不立分果。出有佛世名緣覺。出無佛世名獨覺。菩薩弘誓六度。以道諦為初門。伏惑利生。必經三大阿僧祇劫。頓悟成佛。然此三人。修行證果雖則不同。而同斷見思。同出三界。同證偏真。只行三百由旬。入化城耳。十法成乘者。一觀正因緣境。破邪因緣無因緣二種顛倒。二真正發心。不要名利。惟求涅槃(二乘志出苦輪。菩薩兼憫一切)三遵修止觀。謂五停名止。四念名觀。四遍破見愛煩惱。五識道滅還滅六度是通。苦集流轉六蔽是塞。六調適三十七品。入三脫門。七若根鈍不入。應修對治事禪等。八正助合行。或有薄益。須識次位凡聖不濫。九安忍內外諸障。十不於似道而生法愛。是為要意。利人節節得入。鈍者具十法方悟。

Ru-Yi Translation

This teaching contains the dharma of all three vehicles complete. The śrāvaka contemplates the Four Noble Truths, taking the truth of suffering as the initial gate — the sharpest attains it in three lifetimes, the dullest in sixty eons, thereby realizing the four fruits. The pratyekabuddha contemplates the twelve links of dependent origination, taking the truth of accumulation as the initial gate — the sharpest attains it in four lifetimes, the dullest in a hundred eons, without establishing intermediate fruits; arising in an age with a buddha, he is called one awakened through conditions; arising in an age without a buddha, he is called a solitary awakened one. The bodhisattva takes the universal vows and the six pāramitās, taking the truth of the path as the initial gate, subduing affliction and benefiting beings, necessarily passing through three great incalculable eons before suddenly awakening to buddhahood. Yet although these three kinds of persons differ in their cultivation and the fruit they realize, they equally sever views and habituated thought, equally escape the three realms, and equally realize the one-sided real — traveling only three hundred yojanas, entering merely the phantom city.

As for the ten modes of contemplation that form the vehicle: first, contemplating the object of correct causes and conditions, breaking the two inverted views of wrong causation and no causation. Second, truly and correctly arousing the resolve, seeking neither fame nor gain, but only nirvāṇa (the two vehicles aspire to escape the wheel of suffering; the bodhisattva, in addition, feels compassion for all). Third, honoring and cultivating calming and contemplation — the five methods for stilling the mind are called calming, and the four foundations of mindfulness are called contemplation. Fourth, comprehensively breaking through the afflictions of view and craving. Fifth, distinguishing that the path and cessation, and returning-to-cessation, and the six pāramitās, are passage, while suffering and accumulation, and the flow of transmigration, and the six coverings, are blockage. Sixth, harmonizing the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, entering the three gates of liberation. Seventh, if one's faculties are too dull to enter thereby, one should cultivate antidote practices such as the meditations that treat particular ailments. Eighth, correct practice and auxiliary practice conducted together — even where the benefit gained is slight, one must know the sequence of stages, so as not to confuse the ordinary and the noble. Ninth, bearing patiently with inward and outward obstructions of every kind. Tenth, not giving rise to attachment to the Dharma over what merely resembles the path. This is the essential meaning: a sharp person enters at each successive step; a dull one awakens only once all ten modes are complete.
