Suchness, Buddha-Nature, and the Rise and Reversal of Delusion

Part 2 continues Dixian's commentary on the first round of the Dharma Gate, the support of calming-and-contemplation. It finishes the "mind pure in its own nature," then explains "suchness" as "not false" and "not different," and turns to "buddha-nature," where the root text distinguishes the Buddha of thusness (the mind's substance, neither awakening nor nonawakening) from the Buddha of wisdom (its function as the three wisdoms). Dixian reads the root text's account of how the pure mind, perfumed by ignorance, becomes the ālaya consciousness through the scheme of the Awakening of Faith (three subtle and six coarse marks) and through the eight consciousnesses of Yogācāra, identifying "ignorance in the seed phase" with fundamental ignorance and "ignorance in the fruit phase" with the delusions of views and habituated thought. He then follows the return path through the Tiantai stages: awakening in name, in contemplative practice, and in resemblance, where the coarse delusions within the three realms cease and mind-consciousness becomes "the wisdom of no sense-objects," while subtler delusions remain. Practical advice recurs: contemplating no-thought as a method, and reciting Amitābha's name as a way of meeting both buddhas in one's own mind. The part breaks off at the reason why the subtle delusions are removed gradually.

The Mind Pure in Its Own Nature (Commentary)

Chinese Source

問者，自問也。亦名為徵。答者，自答也。亦名為釋。此心本來清淨。即亙古至今，常為染濁之法所覆，其清淨之自性，依然不改。何以故？隨緣不變故。無始者，不知其所自始。無始以來，即從古至今之意。無明者，迷闇之義。謂不明萬法唯心，心外無法也。《楞嚴經》云：此身外洎山河大地，咸是妙淨明心中所現物。蓋世出世間一切諸法，皆是唯心所起。既皆心起，見法便當見心。不可心外取法。所以禪宗祖師直指本心，而曰：盡大地是老僧一隻眼。即是此意。不明此理，便是無明。無明何以謂之染法？無明者，不覺也。不覺者，昏昧之義。乃染濁相，非清淨相也。《起信論》云：當知無明能生一切染法。以一切染法，皆是不覺相故。覆者，障也。何故此清淨心，無始以來為無明染法所覆？從來未悟故。不變隨緣故。言體雖不變，而隨染緣也。所謂不覺念起，而有無明。念起便成動相，違於本寂。無明便成昧相，違於本照。遂舉心性之全體，而為阿黎耶識。名為業相。由是而全真成妄，妄分能所。能名轉相。亦名見分。所名現相。亦名相分。三細既呈，六粗隨具。而惑業苦三。連環不息矣。蓋因其不變隨緣，從來弗悟。所以無始以來，常為無明染法所覆，而淨性不顯。然因其隨緣不變，萬劫常新。所以無始以來，雖為無明染法所覆，而性淨不改。必不改者，乃謂之性。改變則非性矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Question" (問) is asking oneself; it is also called "posing." "Answer" (答) is answering oneself; it is also called "explaining." This mind is originally pure. That is, though from ancient times to the present it has always been covered by defiled and turbid dharmas, its pure self-nature remains unchanged. Why? Because it follows conditions yet is unchanging. "Beginningless" means that one does not know from where it began; "from beginningless time" means from ancient times to the present. "Ignorance" means darkness and delusion: not understanding that the myriad dharmas are only mind and that there are no dharmas outside the mind. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra says: "Outside this body, all the way to the mountains, rivers, and the great earth, all are things manifested within the wondrous, pure, bright mind." For all dharmas, worldly and transcendent, arise from mind only. Since they all arise from the mind, seeing dharmas one should see the mind; one must not grasp dharmas outside the mind. Therefore the patriarchs of the Chan school point directly to the original mind and say, "The whole great earth is one eye of this old monk." That is the meaning here. Not understanding this principle is ignorance. Why is ignorance called a defiled dharma? Ignorance is nonawakening, and nonawakening means darkness and dimness. It is a mark of defilement and turbidity, not a mark of purity. The Awakening of Faith says: "You should know that ignorance can give birth to all defiled dharmas, because all defiled dharmas are marks of nonawakening." "Covered" means obstructed. Why has this pure mind been covered by the defiled dharmas of ignorance from beginningless time? Because it has never awakened, and because, unchanging, it follows conditions. That is, though its substance does not change, it follows defiled conditions — what is called "unawakened, a thought arises, and there is ignorance." When a thought arises, it becomes the mark of movement, contrary to original tranquility; ignorance becomes the mark of dimness, contrary to original illumination. Thereupon the whole substance of the mind-nature is taken up and becomes the ālaya consciousness, which is called the mark of karma. From this, the whole of the true becomes the false, and the false divides into subject and object. The subject is called the evolving mark, also called the seeing portion; the object is called the manifesting mark, also called the image portion. Once the three subtle marks appear, the six coarse marks follow in full, and delusion, karma, and suffering, these three, are linked without cease. For because, unchanging, it follows conditions and has never awakened, from beginningless time it has always been covered by the defiled dharmas of ignorance, and its pure nature is not revealed. Yet because, following conditions, it is unchanging and ever new through ten thousand eons, from beginningless time, though covered by the defiled dharmas of ignorance, its purity of nature does not change. What necessarily does not change is what is called nature; if it changed, it would not be nature.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: The principle that what does not change is called nature I understand. May I ask why it is able not to change? Answer: Because the defiled dharmas of ignorance are originally separate from the mind pure in its own nature, it does not change. This is like pressing the eye and producing a cataract: one sees flowers arise and flowers cease in the empty sky, yet the empty sky has no arising or ceasing at all. Still not understanding why they are separate, he asks again: How are they separate? Alas! Sentient beings have long turned their backs on awakening and united with sense-objects, taking the other as self. Here the two characters "own nature" in "the mind pure in its own nature" most deserve to be taken in. The pure mind-nature is one's own original face, one's originally possessed substance; therefore it is called "own nature." How could one be deluded about that ignorance, that dim and moving defiled dharma, and take it as one's own substance? It is like the eye seeing blue, yellow, and other colors: those blue and yellow colors are other things; what in the eye is able to see is its own nature. We can know that the nature of the eye's seeing and those blues and yellows are one self and one other, originally separate. Therefore although those blues and yellows screen my eye, my pure nature of seeing remains unchanged. How much more so, since ignorance is like flowers in the sky, originally without substance, while this mind-nature is the root of the myriad dharmas and possesses a wondrous substance. One has substance and one has none: they cannot be associated. One is defiled and one pure: they cannot be associated. "Associated" (應) means united; since they do not unite, how are they not separate?

Ru-Yi Translation

"Its substance is a nonexistent dharma" is like saying a dharma without substance: originally nonexistent and illusorily existing; therefore, existing, it is not existent. For ignorance relies entirely on the pure mind as its substance; it is an illusory mark transformed and manifested by mind and consciousness, with no substance of its own — as when one presses the eye and sees wild flowers, how could the wild flowers have a substance of their own? Therefore, because ignorance is without substance — existing, it is not existent — there is nothing that can be associated with the mind. Because it is not associated, it is said to be separate from the mind; because they are separate, it is said to be pure in its own nature. "Being at its core original awakening, it is called mind": within this purity of its own nature it really possesses spiritual, bright original awakening; it has substance and function and is at once tranquil and illuminating; therefore it is called mind. That is, it is not comparable to inert emptiness. That its nature is neither existence nor nonexistence is called "core" (中, the middle); it must not be mistaken for a relative middle. That its principle is not false or mistaken is called "real" (實); it must not be mistaken for something real with physical substance. The last phrase sums up the meaning above, to make clear why it is named the mind pure in its own nature. Students hearing this name should at once consider the meaning from the name and take it up upon their own mind-nature. Has the assembly understood? The mind-nature has substance; ignorance has no substance; they cannot be associated; originally they are separate. If you can get through to this news here, you are a hero who knows the cardinal doctrine and reaches the essence, and you have found the great road of returning to the root and going back to the source. Look closely, look closely.

Ru-Yi Translation

(K) Second, explaining suchness.

Chinese Source

問曰：云何名為真如？答曰：一切諸法，依此心有。以心為體。望於諸法，法悉虛妄。有即非有。對此虛偽法故，目之為真。又復諸法雖實非有，但以虛妄因緣，而有生滅之相。然彼虛法生時，此心不生。諸法滅時，此心不滅。不生故不增。不滅故不減。以不生不滅不增不減故，名之為真。三世諸佛，及以眾生，同以此一淨心為體。凡聖諸法，自有差別異相。而此真心，無異無相。故名之為如。又真如者。以一切法，真實如是，唯是一心。故名此一心以為真如。若心外有法者，即非真實。亦不如是。即為偽異相也。是故《起信論》言：一切諸法，從本已來離言說相，離名字相，離心緣相，畢竟平等。無有變異。不可破壞。唯是一心。故名真如。以此義故，自性清淨心復名真如也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root text: Question: Why is it called suchness? Answer: All dharmas exist in dependence on this mind. Taking the mind as substance and looking toward all dharmas, the dharmas are all false: existing, they are not existent. In contrast with these false and spurious dharmas, it is designated "true." Further, although all dharmas are really nonexistent, through false causes and conditions they have the marks of arising and ceasing. Yet when those illusory dharmas arise, this mind does not arise; when dharmas cease, this mind does not cease. Not arising, it does not increase; not ceasing, it does not decrease. Because it neither arises nor ceases, neither increases nor decreases, it is called "true." The buddhas of the three times and sentient beings alike take this one pure mind as substance. Ordinary and holy dharmas have their own distinctions and differing marks, but this true mind is without difference and without marks; therefore it is called "such." Further, "suchness" means that all dharmas are truly and really thus, being only the one mind; therefore this one mind is named suchness. If there were dharmas outside the mind, they would not be true and real, nor would they be thus; they would be spurious, differing marks. Therefore the Awakening of Faith says: "All dharmas from the very beginning are apart from the mark of speech, apart from the mark of names, apart from the mark of mental objects; they are ultimately equal, without change, indestructible; they are only the one mind; therefore it is called suchness." For this reason the mind pure in its own nature is also called suchness.

Ru-Yi Translation

"True" means not false; "such" means not different. There is a separate explanation and a joint explanation. In the separate explanation, "true" is explained first and then "such." The explanation of "true" is again in two layers. First it is explained as "not false," in two lines of text; this uses the false to reveal the true. Next it is explained as "not arising and ceasing," in three lines of text; this reveals the nature by way of the marks. Here there is direct revealing and there is reverse revealing. Direct revealing directly reveals the mind's substance of one's own nature. But the true and real mind's substance is originally apart from the marks of names and cannot be spoken of; directly revealing it is very difficult. For the sake of opening and showing it to students, nothing is better than expressing it from the reverse side; this is reverse revealing. Whenever deep and subtle principles are explained, using the reverse method makes the principle easy to understand. This is the skill of teaching the Dharma and lecturing on the sūtras. For if one can understand that all dharmas arising from the mind are without exception false and subject to arising and ceasing, then one can know that the mind's substance that gives rise to them is neither false nor subject to arising and ceasing. This is Great Master Nanyue's painstaking care, and why later people named this book Detailed Presentation of the Essentials of Mind.

Ru-Yi Translation

○ The five lines of text beginning "All dharmas" explain "true." False dharmas have differences that are not one; therefore it says "dharmas." "All" embraces every kind of dharma, worldly and transcendent. All dharmas, worldly and transcendent, exist through the combination of causes and conditions. For example, worldly ordinary beings take the deluded mind as cause and defiled objects as conditions; because deluded and defiled causes and conditions combine, there are the dharmas of the six ordinary [realms]. Transcendent sages take the awakened mind as cause and pure dharmas as conditions; because awakened and pure causes and conditions combine, there are the dharmas of the four holy [realms]. Since these conditionally arisen dharmas of ordinary beings and sages all take the mind's delusion or awakening as their cause, it says, "All dharmas exist in dependence on this mind," [and] "taking the mind as substance." This is what the Śūraṅgama says: "What all dharmas arise from is only what the mind manifests; all causes and fruits, worlds, and dust motes take on substance because of the mind." Looking from this mind of one's own, which has substance, toward dharmas without substance, one knows that all dharmas are illusory existence; therefore it says "the dharmas are all false." Since they are false and illusory existence, it says "existing, they are not existent." "Existing" refers to their illusory existence; "not existent" means they do not truly exist. And this mind-nature's own substance really has no marks of speech or names; it is only to break the false that it is revealed as true. Therefore it says, "In contrast with these false and spurious dharmas, it is designated 'true.'" This completes revealing the true as "not false."

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, although all these dharmas, existing, are not existent, through the combination of causes and conditions their existing though nonexistent is not absent. Yet when conditions combine they arise, and when conditions disperse they cease; now there, now gone, they are all marks of arising and ceasing. Because they arise and cease, they are called false. Therefore it says, "through false causes and conditions they have the marks of arising and ceasing." This is the meaning of calling the illusory and false "marks." But since dharmas have no substance and take the mind as substance, this mind does not arise and cease along with dharmas — just as an eye with cataracts sees flowers in the sky: the flowers arise and the flowers cease, but what has that to do with the sky? Where there is arising there is increase; where there is ceasing there is decrease. Since the true mind does not arise or cease, it does not increase or decrease. Thus dharmas, arising and ceasing, are called false; since this mind neither arises nor ceases, neither increases nor decreases, what is it if not true? Therefore it says "it is called 'true.'" This completes revealing the true as not arising and ceasing.

Ru-Yi Translation

Hearing these two meanings, we should at once recognize clearly that the myriad dharmas are all false and only the mind is true. But we must not mistake the mind that gives rise to thoughts and stirs ideas for the true. All dharmas arise precisely from thought; this is the root of birth and death, what the Śūraṅgama calls the false mind. The Awakening of Faith says: "Because all dharmas are originally only mind, there really is no thought; yet there is the false mind, which, unawakened, gives rise to thought and sees objects; therefore it is called ignorance." How could we take this as the true mind? Why? Because the true mind is without thought. A sūtra says: "If there are sentient beings who can contemplate no-thought, they are heading toward the Buddha's wisdom." Yet all sentient beings, from the beginning, have continued thought after thought and have never been apart from thought; now, wishing to realize and enter no-thought — how could that be easy? Yet there is an expedient dharma-gate. What is it? When a thought arises, at once contemplate and illuminate it. Right at the moment of contemplating and illuminating, the previous thought has ceased and the following thought has not yet arisen; in the present there is no thought. Then body, mind, world, and all dharmas are entirely nonexistent — bright like the clear moon, pure like a polished mirror. But the moon and the mirror are still material dharmas, forced into service as similes; the mind's substance of no-thought, its spiritual light shining alone and utterly free of faculties and objects, simply cannot be described in words. This itself is the true mind. A person just beginning to apply effort has no power of concentration yet; in an instant a second thought arises again. Then contemplate and illuminate it again in the same way. If one keeps at this long without slackening, the power of concentration becomes complete, and one enters ever better states step by step. This method is contemplating no-thought. When one's effort becomes deep and pure, one contemplates without contemplating; contemplating, there is no contemplating. There is no mark of what is contemplated and no thought that contemplates; at once tranquil and illuminating, at once illuminating and tranquil, profoundly tranquil and permanent — this is called the Buddha's wisdom. Ru-Yi Translation

Because, though the main agents have ceased, the habit-energies still remain, the words "Yet although" are used to continue. "Yet although" indicates something not yet complete. This text can be explained in three sections. The first section says that although one knows objects are illusory and without reality, and the fruit ignorance is said to cease, one still sees that there is a mark of objects being illusory. An "illusory mark" is a mark of emptiness. One does not know that this mark, existing, is not existent; that as to its original nature it originally neither arises nor ceases, being only what the one mind manifests. Not knowing this principle is fundamental nonawakening (knowing is awakening; not knowing is nonawakening), what is called ignorance in the seed phase and ignorance deluded about principle (also called ignorance deluded about the mind). The second section says that although one does not grasp illusory forms as real, and deluded thinking is said to cease, one still sees that there are illusory marks and takes them to be different from the pure mind. This too is deluded grasping, this too is deluded thinking, and it is also called illusory marks.

The third section says that since there is the deluded thinking of subtle ignorance that grasps (subtle ignorance is ignorance in the seed phase; it means that it is subtler than ignorance in the fruit phase), taking them to be different from the mind, there are also the illusory objects that are grasped, which seem different from the mind, mark after mark not one; these are deluded objects. Here "subtle ignorance" also refers to the mark of karma among the three subtle marks of the Awakening of Faith; the deluded thinking of subtle ignorance and the illusory objects it grasps are the evolving mark and the manifesting mark among the three subtle marks. At the end of each of these three sections it concludes, "Because that coarse one has ceased, ignorance in the fruit phase is said to cease," and so on. Translator's notes

Root text. Root-text units (2, 7, 10, 13, 19, 22, 28, 32, 35, 38, 42, 46, 49) reuse `./dacheng-zhiguan/p1_body.md` word for word. Where the root English covers several of Dixian's lemmas in one sentence group, it is cut at sentence boundaries (19/22, 28/32, 35/38/42/46/49); unit 38 ends mid-sentence ("Having heard this Dharma,") and unit 42 continues it in lower case.

Punctuation of the root. In unit 2 the CBETA punctuation of the Notes is 依此心有。以心為體。望於諸法, while the root English reads 以心為體望於諸法 as one clause; Dixian's own comment (unit 4) attaches 以心為體 to the preceding sentence. The root English is kept, and the commentary quotes the two phrases separately.

虛妄無明 (unit 32/33). The root English has "false ignorance"; Dixian takes 虛妄 ("false," the seeds of illusory forms) and 無明 (fruit and seed ignorance) as two items. The commentary is translated according to his reading.

Awakening of Faith (T1666). Quotations in units 0, 4, 11 were checked in T1666: "ignorance can give birth to all defiled dharmas," "Because all dharmas are originally only mind, there really is no thought…," "If there are sentient beings who can contemplate no-thought…," and the passage on "the mind-nature not arising" (Dixian abridges it with 乃至, kept as "and so on up to"). The three subtle and six coarse marks are given as the mark of karma / the mark of the perceiver (evolving mark) / the mark of objects (manifesting mark); knowledge, continuity, grasping, attaching to names, producing karma, and bondage to suffering through karma.

Śūraṅgama (T945). "What all dharmas arise from is only what the mind manifests" is in T945; "Outside this body, all the way to the mountains, rivers, and the great earth…" (unit 0) is Dixian's paraphrase of a T945 line on the "wondrous, bright, true mind," not an exact quotation.

Laṅkāvatāra. The verse in unit 13 ("There is no nirvāṇa for anything…") is in Guṇabhadra's Laṅkāvatāra (T670); the root English is reused in Dixian's comments.

Site English reused. The Amitābha Sūtra lines (unit 16) follow the site, with "eons" for "eons" (BRIEF) and the site's added glosses "Infinite Light / Infinite Life" omitted because the Chinese has none. "The quiescent, ceased mark of all phenomena cannot be conveyed in words" (unit 5) and "uttering even once, 'Homage to the Buddha'" (unit 36) follow the site's Lotus Sūtra; the Contemplation Sūtra line "this very mind is itself the thirty-two marks and the eighty subsidiary excellent features" (unit 5) follows the site. Detailed Presentation of the Essentials of Mind (unit 3) follows Zunshi's preface in `dacheng-zhiguan`.

"The Buddha teaches that higher and lower dharmas are made only by the one mind" (unit 40), attributed to the Avataṃsaka: source not located in this wording.
