The Closing Verses, Calming-and-Contemplation in Bowing, Eating, and at the Latrine, and Dixian's Dedication

Part 13 is the last part of Dixian's lecture notes and covers the end of the root text. It opens by finishing the comment on how a beginning bodhisattva, a bodhisattva of long practice, and a buddha act: the beginner must first make a vow, then enter calming, then give rise to contemplation, and Dixian ties this to the visualization that opens the Lotus Repentance Rite. He then reads the root text's eleven and a half verses line by line, matching them to the three natures and the simile of the gold snake that can be recast as a buddha image. The third main section of the treatise, "pointing things out by way of particular matters," applies calming-and-contemplation to bowing to the Buddha, eating, and using the latrine. The longest stretch defends imagining a buddha as no less real than seeing one: seeing is always made by the mind, and a buddha is seen through the consciousness of shared marks when one's appeal accords with the Buddha's response. Dixian keeps pointing these arguments toward reciting Amitābha's name. He closes by giving his three reasons for lecturing on this work and adapting the dedication verse with which Ouyi ended his Essentials Explaining.

The Activity of Beginners, Long Practitioners, and Buddhas (continued)

Chinese Source

此約因人果人，略分三等。以明功行有淺深純熟之異，故作用有難易大小之殊也。初行菩薩，謂圓五品觀行位以上。何謂欲有所作？所謂欲見釋迦牟尼佛，及十方佛者。欲得六根清淨，入佛境界。諸佛所說，悉能受持，通達演說，無障礙者。欲得與諸大士，共為等侶。一念之中，徧至十方，供養諸佛者。欲得一念之中，徧到十方，現種種身，說法度生者。欲得破四魔，淨一切煩惱，滅一切障道罪，現身入菩薩正位，具諸佛自在功德者。皆不外下說之三法。即時凡夫，欲作一切功德。不論自修利人，皆當依法行之。諦聽諦聽。其法云何？略有三種。先發願。次入止。三從止起觀。發願不外四宏誓。須知發願即須運想。蓋必觀想諸佛菩薩聖眾，慈悲威德，大放光明，徧照行者，及以眾生。如是緣念於心，誠敬請求，方為發願。不然。所發之願，便無力也。何故如是？行者自無道力，必須指定所求，禮請三寶加被。方得增勝之力，剋能成辦故。

Ru-Yi Translation

This roughly divides [practitioners] into three grades, in terms of persons at the stage of the cause and persons at the stage of the fruit, to make clear that because merit and practice differ in shallowness and depth, purity and ripeness, the activity differs in difficulty and ease, greatness and smallness. "A bodhisattva beginning practice" means [one at] the stage of contemplative practice of the five grades of the perfect teaching and above. What is meant by "wishes to do something"? It means wishing to see Śākyamuni Buddha and the buddhas of the ten directions; wishing to attain the purity of the six faculties and enter the sphere of the buddhas, able to receive and hold all that the buddhas teach, and to penetrate and expound it without obstruction; wishing to become a companion of the great bodhisattvas, in a single thought reaching everywhere in the ten directions to make offerings to the buddhas; wishing, in a single thought, to reach everywhere in the ten directions, manifest all kinds of bodies, teach the Dharma, and deliver beings; wishing to destroy the four māras, purify all afflictions, extinguish all offenses that obstruct the path, enter the correct position of a bodhisattva in this very body, and possess the sovereign merits of the buddhas. None of these lies outside the three methods spoken of below. When an ordinary being at this very time wishes to do any meritorious work, whether cultivating for himself or benefiting others, he should practice it according to this method. Listen attentively, listen attentively. What is the method? In brief there are three kinds: first, making a vow; next, entering calming; third, giving rise to contemplation from calming. Making a vow does not go beyond the four universal vows. You should know that making a vow requires exercising imagination: one must contemplate and imagine the buddhas, bodhisattvas, and holy assembly, with their kindness, compassion, and majestic virtue, greatly emitting light that shines everywhere upon the practitioner and upon sentient beings. Only when one holds them in mind in this way and sincerely and reverently makes requests is it making a vow. Otherwise the vow one makes has no power. Why is this so? The practitioner himself has no power of the path; he must specify what he seeks and bow and request the Three Jewels to bestow their aid; only then does he obtain increased and surpassing power and become able to accomplish it.

發願已。即當入止。何故如是？因尚在觀行位中。若不入止，則性不空寂。自成障礙。何能於一切有礙之法，隨念即通。故須入止，則知諸法有即非有。更無所礙。故入止已。即復起觀。何故如是？因入止時，但有捨除，而無建立。何能感應道交？故更須從止起觀，則知一切法，皆從心作。隨念即成故。此中起觀，隨念即成者。因經入止一重行法，則心性空寂。以空寂故，一切清淨。以清淨故，便得勝力加被，與所發之願相應。初行菩薩欲有所作，必須如是三法兼到。然後方能隨其心願，所作即成也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having made the vow, one should then enter calming. Why is this so? Because one is still at the stage of contemplative practice. If one does not enter calming, the nature is not empty and tranquil and becomes an obstruction of itself; how then could one, with regard to all obstructive dharmas, penetrate them as one thinks? Therefore one must enter calming; then one knows that dharmas, existing, are not existent, and there is nothing further to obstruct. Therefore, having entered calming, one then gives rise to contemplation. Why is this so? Because when one enters calming there is only relinquishing and removing, and no establishing; how could there be the mutual resonance between response and appeal? Therefore one must further give rise to contemplation from calming; then one knows that all dharmas are made from the mind and are accomplished as one thinks. Here, that one gives rise to contemplation and it is accomplished as one thinks is because, having passed through the method of practice of entering calming, the mind-nature is empty and tranquil; because it is empty and tranquil, all is pure; because it is pure, one obtains the bestowal of surpassing power, which accords with the vow one has made. When a bodhisattva beginning practice wishes to do something, he must have all three methods complete in this way; only then can he, according to his mind's vow, accomplish whatever he does.

Ru-Yi Translation

Look at the Lotus Repentance Rite and the Rite of Practice for the Great Compassion Mind Dhāraṇī: before one enters the place of practice, one must already exercise imagination, and in addition a separate gate, the contemplation of principle, is especially set out. Great Master Zhizhe admonished practitioners: "When carrying out each method, cultivate this contemplation in all of them. In each period there must be no lack in either phenomena or principle." Yet people today merely recite and bow according to the text, and that is all. This is having the ritual of phenomena without the contemplation of principle; this is why their signs of response do not reach those of the ancients. If one can apply phenomena and principle together, how could the resonance and response be conceived? This must be known.

Ru-Yi Translation

"A bodhisattva of long practice" means the stage of resemblance and above. "Is not like this" means he does not need to proceed in sequence as above. Resolving is itself making a vow. That simply resolving accomplishes it is because his merit and practice of calming-and-contemplation are already deep; everywhere and at all times he is within calming-and-contemplation and does not need to make it deliberately; therefore simply making the vow suffices. "The buddha-tathāgatas": speaking narrowly, this is the fruit of ultimate awakening. Discussed broadly, the great bodhisattva of the first abiding of the perfect teaching is also called a buddha within the cause. From the second abiding up to equal awakening, they are called causes in relation to the fruit and fruits in relation to the cause; their activity increases and surpasses stage by stage, and only on reaching the fruit of awakening is it ultimate and complete. "Again are not like this": the bodhisattva of long practice still needs to hold [objects] in mind; now they illuminate of themselves without taking objects and know of themselves without deliberating. The bodhisattva of long practice still needs to resolve; now, according to what is seen and heard, wherever there is appeal there is response; without needing to resolve, the matter accomplishes itself. "It is like" and what follows raises a simile; "The Tathāgata" and what follows matches the dharma. The maṇi jewel is a simile for the buddha-tathāgatas. Having no mind wishing to benefit, yet raining treasures according to what calls upon it, is a simile for "in whatever he does he makes no mental intention, yet he accords with what is to be benefited." "This is because" and what follows concludes it.

梵語阿僧祇劫，此言無數時。經由不可算計之長時者三，故曰三大阿僧祇劫。無明永盡曰純。大覺現前曰熟。謂諸佛如來無功用道，由於久久熏習純熟之故。非於止觀之外，更有異法也。蕅益大師曰：非因初行，安有久行。非有久行，安得成佛。故知欲成佛者，須學初行方便。其警策行人也至矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sanskrit asaṃkhyeya eon means here "countless time." Passing three times through incalculably long periods is called "three great asaṃkhyeya eons." Ignorance being permanently exhausted is called "pure"; great awakening being present is called "ripe." That is, the effortless path of the buddha-tathāgatas comes from long, long perfuming until it is pure and ripe; it is not that there is some other dharma outside calming-and-contemplation. Great Master Ouyi said: "Were it not for beginning practice, how could there be long practice? Were there no long practice, how could one become a buddha? Hence one knows that whoever wishes to become a buddha must learn the expedients of beginning practice." How thoroughly he spurs practitioners on!

佛祖說法，有散花、有貫花。散花即是長行，貫花即是偈頌。（或單曰偈，或單言頌。其義一也。）貫者，聯貫之義，喻偈頌限字成句，必加編組。不似長行，長短隨心。故長行名散花，偈頌名貫花也。云何說散貫二種，為四悉檀故。隨眾生欲樂故。偈頌以四言，或五六七言，為句。四句為一頌。頌體，略分二類。一祇夜。此云重頌。依長行所說，重復頌之也。二伽陀。此云應頌。孤起特立。或略與長行相應，而不一一頌之。此二復有廣略之分。詳於長行曰廣頌。略於長行曰略頌，今此止觀法要，長行已詳。復說伽陀者，便人總持也。共十一頌，又一句。

Ru-Yi Translation

When the buddhas and patriarchs teach the Dharma, there are scattered flowers and strung flowers. Scattered flowers are prose; strung flowers are verses. (Sometimes they are called simply ji [verse], sometimes simply song [ode]; the meaning is the same.) "Strung" means linked together, a figure for verses being formed into lines of limited length, which must be arranged and composed, unlike prose, whose length follows the mind. Therefore prose is called scattered flowers and verses are called strung flowers. Why are the two kinds, scattered and strung, spoken? For the sake of the four siddhāntas, and in accordance with the desires and delights of sentient beings. Verses take four, or five, six, or seven characters as a line, and four lines as a verse. The forms of verse are broadly divided into two classes. First, geya, rendered here as "repeated verse," which repeats in verse what was spoken in the prose. Second, gāthā, rendered here as "corresponding verse," which arises alone and stands independently, or corresponds roughly with the prose without versifying it point by point. These two are further divided into extended and abridged: those more detailed than the prose are called extended verses; those briefer than the prose are called abridged verses. Now, in these essentials of calming-and-contemplation the prose is already detailed; that he speaks gāthās again is to make it convenient for people to hold it all. There are eleven verses in all, plus one line.

Ru-Yi Translation

This section briefly versifies the support and the spheres in the prose. The first line versifies in general the substance of the nature. "Mind" is the sixth, mind-consciousness: what is called the present single thought, unchanging yet following conditions. "Nature" is the tathāgatagarbha, empty and nonempty: what is called the bright substance pure in nature, following conditions yet unchanging. "Of itself" means originally. Although this mind follows conditions all day long, the substance pure in nature does not change; therefore it says it is originally pure. The line on "all dharmas" versifies the whole of phenomena being identical to principle. "All dharmas" means the causes and fruits of the ten dharma-realms: these are phenomena. Yet all dharmas are not apart from the one mind; outside the mind there are no dharmas: this is principle. As it is said, "The perfectly fused substance of the dharma-realm makes up my single thought of mind": this is three thousand inherently possessed in principle. The four lines from "this mind is sentient beings" versify the whole of principle forming phenomena. Being deluded about this mind and forming the defiled and turbid three natures is the sentient being who flows and turns in birth-and-death; awakening to this mind and forming the pure three natures is the buddhas and bodhisattvas who realize great nirvāṇa. Birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are made only by the mind. As it is said, "Therefore my single thought of mind is, in its whole substance, the dharma-realm": this is three thousand brought about in actual phenomena. The two lines from "the one mind makes two" conclude the text above. "Makes two" means it makes birth-and-death and makes nirvāṇa. "The two have no mark of two": birth-and-death and nirvāṇa both cannot be found; when they cannot be found, there is only the one pure mind. The former line is the meaning of "always the same and always distinct"; the latter line is the meaning of "always distinct and always the same." The last four lines raise a simile. The great ocean is a simile for the one mind; the nature of the ocean being only of one salty flavor is a simile for the mind-nature being of one flavor and equal; the great ocean possessing inexhaustible treasuries is a simile for the mind-nature possessing the boundless meanings of vast and deep dharmas. This is what is called the two layers of three thousand, three thousand inherently possessed in principle and three thousand brought about in actual phenomena, dwelling together in a single thought.

Ru-Yi Translation

These thirteen lines of verse versify the whole nature giving rise to cultivation and the whole of cultivation lying in the nature; that is, they briefly versify the three sections in the prose: substance and form, cutting off and attaining, and activity. The first line warns the practitioner to listen attentively to what follows. The two words "therefore" carry on from the text above. "All practitioners" means all people who cultivate practice. Listening to sūtras and reading the teachings, and understanding the truth of principle, are practitioners of wisdom; bowing, rejoicing, and doing all kinds of meritorious deeds are practitioners of merit. The various dharma-gates do not go beyond the six perfections, and the six perfections do not go beyond merit and wisdom. Merit and wisdom are like the two wheels of a cart: they must be cultivated together. The line "should at all times" runs through all of the following text. "Should" is a word of exhortation. "At all times" means the six periods of day and night; that is, it encompasses the times of walking, standing, sitting, and lying down. An inch of time is an inch of life's light. "This day has already passed; life too diminishes accordingly." Therefore he calls to the practitioner and warns him: "Impermanence is swift; I only fear you will not be ready in time. You should, as I say, observe, know and understand, hold it in mind, and perfume and cultivate."

Ru-Yi Translation

The line on "observe" versifies the cultivation of calming-and-contemplation in dependence on the defiled discriminated nature. "Observe" means examining and investigating. "One's own body" is the aggregate of form; "one's own mind" is the four aggregates of feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness. The five aggregates are the principal reward; the six sense-objects are the dependent reward. That the six sense-objects are not mentioned here is because once one understands the principal reward, the dependent reward can be known. As for this body's birth, aging, sickness, and death, contemplate where it comes from and where it goes. This mind, thought after thought without stopping, arises, abides, changes, and ceases: when [a thought] arises, contemplate where it arises; when it falls away, contemplate where it falls away. You should know that this body's birth, aging, sickness, and death are coarse birth-and-death, and the arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing of the mind's thoughts are subtle birth-and-death. (D) Second, the gate of calming.

Ru-Yi Translation

Bowing to the Buddha by the gate of contemplation is the substance giving rise to function: what is called "the mutual resonance between response and appeal is inconceivable." Bowing to the Buddha by the gate of calming is gathering function back into substance: what is called "the one who bows and the one bowed to are empty and tranquil in nature." Therefore, knowing that all buddhas, the body, and the offering articles are all made from the mind, existing yet not existent, is entering the gate of calming of the discriminated nature; knowing that they are only the one mind is entering the gate of calming of the dependent nature; further knowing that one may not grasp the mark of the one mind is entering the gate of calming of the real nature. "Why?" and what follows explains why one may not [grasp]. For outside the mind there is no dharma that grasps the mark of this mind; one can know that what grasps and what is grasped are only one's own mind deludedly making discriminations, and this is not the pure mind; therefore one may not grasp. "Not existent in its own substance": that is, the substance of the pure mind is always without discrimination. Now, the Buddha's mind and my mind are equal, a single suchness; if there were the active and the acted-upon, it would not be empty and tranquil; how could it be called suchness? Bowing to the Buddha by the gate of calming is just so, just so.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

(D) Second, dispelling doubts, again in two: (E) first, directly resolving what is doubted; second, extending it by analogy to all dharmas. Now the first.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The meaning of the question has two parts. First: since the pure and impure dharmas above are already made by the mind, how can they be turned again? Second: moreover, since they all arise from the perfuming of the power of past karma, how can deliberate imagining in the present life transform them? The meaning of the answer is also twofold. "The mind's substance" through "cease accordingly" first explains the power of perfuming by conditions. "All dharmas" are the causes and fruits of defilement and purity. That is, although the dharmas of defilement and purity are inherently possessed by the mind-nature, without the perfuming of conditions they cannot manifest and arise. "Past karma" is the karma of the past; "past conditions" are the conditions of former times. "Apply the mind" is like saying "apply thought": that is, to those very foul marks one applies the thought of pure imagining. This refers to the gate of contemplation. "Revulsion" and "the contemplation of emptiness" refer to the gate of calming. Above it said that impure marks appear "only because past evil karma has perfumed the mind": this is the mind of revulsion. "Yet these marks of the mind, existing, are not existent," up to "equal and without thought": this is the mind of the contemplation of emptiness. Since one takes the pure imagining of the gate of contemplation as a condition to perfume one's true mind, and further uses the gate of calming to empty the mind's thoughts and remove the conditions of impurity, in this way pure marks can arise and foul marks cease accordingly. This explains the principle by which they can be turned. In sum, the mind's making and the mind's turning both rely on the power of perfuming by conditions. Then, since the foul, like a privy, and the pure, like ghee and honey, could be made by the conditions of the power of past karma perfuming the mind, why now could they not be turned by the conditions of calming-and-contemplation perfuming the mind?

The Diamond Sūtra says: "The past mind cannot be obtained, the present mind cannot be obtained, the future mind cannot be obtained." The three periods are originally provisional names; how could the mind-nature have marks of three periods? Therefore the ten times, ancient and present, are not apart from the present thought. What can certainly make according to the perfuming of conditions can certainly turn according to the perfuming of conditions; the past never obstructs the present.

That is, the merit of perfuming the mind through contemplative practice is inconceivable; since it can turn impurity into pure marks, all other pure and foul spheres can have their old marks changed by the power of contemplative imagining. For contemplating the pure as foul can remove attachment; contemplating the foul as pure can remove aversion. The five spiritual powers are all attained through the power of concentration, and the power of concentration is accomplished through contemplative practice; therefore it says it can serve as an expedient. That it speaks only of the five spiritual powers and does not reach the power of the exhaustion of outflows is because the exhaustion of outflows is not a separate power; it is only that, because outflows are exhausted, the spiritual force of the five powers becomes greater. When aversion and attachment are both removed, that is the exhaustion of outflows. Further, non-Buddhist immortals can also attain the five spiritual powers through the power of concentration; but the exhaustion of outflows requires cultivating the three trainings of precepts, concentration, and wisdom together in order to cut off the afflictions, fetters, and agents completely; it is the culmination of the sages of the three vehicles. Since this passage also mentions the immortals with the five spiritual powers, it keeps [the exhaustion of outflows] hidden and does not display it.

"Yet beginners" through "change according to thought" third explains that it is accomplished only after long effort. "Therefore" and what follows fourth concludes by revealing its efficacy. "The lesser sages of the two vehicles" means those who have not yet realized the fourth fruit. "The immortals with the five spiritual powers" refers to non-Buddhists who have attained the five spiritual powers. From the great bodhisattvas who have ascended the grounds, down through the three worthy [stages] before the grounds, the pratyekabuddhas, and the arhats, none lacks this efficacy; therefore it says "and even … and so on." "Transform phenomena directly" is changing what already exists; "making what does not exist appear" is manifesting what originally did not exist. The transformations and manifestations of spiritual powers are not like conjuring tricks that merely deceive people's eyes; they make all dharmas, when changed, truly changed, and when manifested, truly manifested, not like a turtle's hair or a rabbit's horn. You should know that becoming a buddha and becoming a bodhisattva all come from contemplative practice; how could it be only a matter of attaining spiritual powers!

Ru-Yi Translation

Because, when the bodhisattvas and the holy assembly manifest various transformations of spiritual power, sentient beings mostly cannot see them, this question comes. You should know that seeing is not difficult either. One need only perfume the mind with pure karma so that it accords with the mind of compassion and wisdom of the sages; through the mutual resonance between response and appeal the consciousness of shared marks is formed, and one can see. If one is deluded by the five aggregates and six sense-objects, grasps the illusory as real, and gives rise to greed, anger, and delusion, then one is covered and obstructed by ignorance and forms the consciousness of unshared marks; though one wishes to see, one cannot.

(F) Third, explaining the reason for the difference in spiritual powers.

Chinese Source

問曰：菩薩神通與二乘神通，有何差別。答曰：二乘神通，但由假想而成。以心外見法，故有限有量。菩薩神通，由知諸法悉是心作，唯有心相。心外無法，故無限無量也。又菩薩初學通時，亦從假想而修。但即知諸法皆一心作。二乘唯由假想習通，但言定力。不言心作，道理論之，一等心作。但彼二乘不知，故有差別也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Root text: Question: What is the difference between the spiritual powers of bodhisattvas and the spiritual powers of the two vehicles? Answer: The spiritual powers of the two vehicles are accomplished only through deliberate imagining; because they see dharmas outside the mind, they are limited and measured. The spiritual powers of bodhisattvas, because they know that dharmas are all made by the mind, only marks of the mind, with no dharmas outside the mind, are unlimited and measureless. Further, when bodhisattvas first learn the powers, they too cultivate them through deliberate imagining, but they know that dharmas are all made by the one mind. The two vehicles practice the powers only through deliberate imagining; they speak of the power of concentration and do not speak of being made by the mind. Discussed in principle, both alike are made by the mind; only the two vehicles do not know it; therefore there is a difference.

Chinese Source

二乘雖三學齊修，然定力偏多。望於菩薩，慧則為劣。何以故？不了唯心故。因此便與菩薩成其差別。蓋二乘定力，實由心作，與菩薩正等。但以不知心作故，心外見法，遂成有限量之神通。菩薩習通，亦由觀想，與二乘相同。但以悟知本心故，心外無法，故成無限量之神通。須知心量豎窮橫徧。若不了唯心，便不能照性成修，何能全其性用乎？

Ru-Yi Translation

Although the two vehicles cultivate the three trainings equally, their power of concentration predominates; compared with the bodhisattvas, their wisdom is inferior. Why? Because they do not understand mind-only. Because of this they come to differ from the bodhisattvas. For the two vehicles' power of concentration is really made by the mind, exactly equal to the bodhisattvas'; but because they do not know it is made by the mind, they see dharmas outside the mind, and so it becomes limited and measured spiritual power. The bodhisattvas' practice of the powers also comes from contemplative imagining, the same as the two vehicles'; but because they awaken to and know the original mind, there are no dharmas outside the mind, and so it becomes unlimited and measureless spiritual power. You should know that the capacity of the mind exhausts the vertical and pervades the horizontal. If one does not understand mind-only, one cannot illuminate the nature to accomplish cultivation; how could one complete its function of the nature?

This is why Mahāyāna Calming-and-Contemplation first explains the one mind as the support. In sum, the mind is the root of all dharmas, and calming-and-contemplation is the comprehensive hold of all gates of practice. That this old monk now does not lecture on other sūtras but lectures only on this work is truly because I wish the Dharma assembly to understand the root and attain the comprehensive hold. There are also those who read the teachings and visit teachers, laboring diligently for many years; if they do not understand the essential purport, they cannot avoid misdirecting their effort of cultivation. This work's grand text encompasses in meaning the three canons; it makes clear cultivation and makes clear the nature, in detail and to the point, precise and deep. If one understands this one dharma-gate, what dharma would remain unclear? This is the first.

Reading Notes for Part 13

Translator's notes

Root text. Root-text units (2–4, 7–9, 12–13, 16, 19–20, 23, 26, 29, 32, 35, 38, 41, 44, 48, 51, 54, 57, 60, 63, 66, 69, 72, 75, 78, 81, 84, 87, 90, 93, 96) reuse `./dacheng-zhiguan/p3_body.md` word for word, from "The mind's nature is pure of itself" to the end of Fascicle Four. A script confirmed that, taken together, they reproduce that English exactly. The verse pairs of the root English (four Chinese lines each) are divided line by line to match Dixian's verse units; unit 7 has five lines.

Variants in Dixian's root text. Unit 32 has 即是現前供養, and Dixian says in unit 33 that 即時 (the T1924 reading) is an error. The root English ("an offering made in their presence at this very time") follows 即時 and is unchanged. Unit 75 reads 即都見不淨 (T1924 即都不見淨). Unit 60 reads 知身及供具 (T1924 知身又供具), and unit 72 reads 盛滿 (T1924 成滿). The root English is unchanged in all of these. In unit 78 Dixian's interlinear note says that 所 in 從心所作 is superfluous; the root English already follows 從心作.

Dixian's readings against the root English. In unit 76 Dixian reads 本時 as "at present," while the root English has "the food that was pure before." In unit 79 he takes 行食之人 as the person who eats, while the root English has "the person who serves the food." In unit 61 he says 及施他己者 should read 及他施己者, "what others have given me," which matches the root English. The commentary is translated as Dixian reads; the root English is unchanged.

Ouyi's Essentials Explaining. The two sayings of Great Master Ouyi (unit 0: "Were it not for beginning practice…"; unit 17: "Just when it is a snake…") are in his Essentials Explaining, printed in the vault copy `雜藏/天台宗/X0905 大乘止觀序.md`. Dixian's closing verse (unit 98) is Ouyi's closing verse there, with 釋少分 changed to 明少分, as Dixian says.

Lotus Repentance Rite (unit 0). The list of what the beginner "wishes" (to see Śākyamuni, to purify the six faculties, to destroy the four māras…) follows the exhortation at the head of Zhiyi's Lotus Samādhi Repentance Rite (T1941). Zhizhe's admonition "When carrying out each method, cultivate this contemplation…" is T1941; its wording follows our draft of that rite (`./fahua-sanmei-chanyi/p2_body.md`). The Rite of Practice for the Great Compassion Mind Dhāraṇī is Zhili's 千手眼大悲心咒行法 (T1950), the source of the site's Great Compassion Repentance.

Contemplation Sūtra (units 27, 36, 52, 58, 97). "This mind is the buddha / makes the buddha," "When your own mind contemplates the Buddha…," "The ocean of perfect and universal knowing… arises from the mind's own contemplation," and "To contemplate Amitāyus Buddha, enter through a single mark…" follow the site's Contemplation Sūtra. "Sixteen or eight feet tall" also follows the site's thirteenth contemplation. In unit 97, "understand its meaning and purport well… not be shaken by the first meaning" echoes the upper-middle birth of the same sūtra, where the site has "the ultimate truth." Here 第一義 is kept as "the first meaning," as elsewhere in this text.

Avataṃsaka (units 39, 58). "The mind makes the tathāgatas" (root text) and "Mind, buddha, and sentient beings: these three are without difference" are in the sixty-fascicle version (T278). "One should contemplate the nature of the dharma-realm: all is made only by the mind" is in the eighty-fascicle version (T279). The ten great kings of vows and "When this person is about to die…" (unit 27) are from the Chapter on the Practices and Vows of Samantabhadra (T293). Dixian's quotation skips some clauses, and the English follows his shorter wording.

Other sūtras. "To give a single meal to all beings…" (root text, unit 69) is the site's Vimalakīrti. "This person walks a deviant path…" and "The past mind cannot be obtained…" (unit 88) are the Diamond Sūtra (T235). The root text's "He makes offerings to the immeasurable koṭis of tathāgatas…" (unit 66) was not found as such; the closest wording is in T478. "The crooked seeing of the śrāvakas" (root, unit 41) is discussed in Guanding's Nirvāṇa commentary (T1767); the sūtra passage itself was not checked.

Other sayings. "The perfectly fused substance of the dharma-realm makes up my single thought of mind" (unit 5) is the heading of the fifth gate of Chuandeng's Treatise on the Birthless Birth into the Pure Land (T1975). Dixian's second line ("my single thought of mind is, in its whole substance, the dharma-realm") adapts the treatise's 故我念佛心，全體是法界. "Alas, alas, my father and mother…" (unit 10) is from the Book of Odes ("Lu e"). "This day has already passed; life too diminishes accordingly" is the verse commonly chanted in the evening service. "The one who bows and the one bowed to are empty and tranquil in nature; the mutual resonance… is inconceivable" (unit 64) is the standard verse for bowing to the Buddha. "If you acknowledge another as the Buddha, you yourself become a māra" (unit 58): source not located.

"Rode the path of thusness" (unit 42). 乘如實道來成正覺 is the familiar gloss of tathāgata (e.g., Zhiyi's Textual Commentary, T1718). Dixian attributes it here to the two vehicles.

Outline. Unit 59 prints （巳）二供具觀; the parallel heading in unit 34 shows it should be （己）. It is rendered "(F)." 合結 (unit 6) follows the shared rendering "concluding together." 實事觀／假想觀 are "the contemplation of actual things / the contemplation by deliberate imagining" (假想 "deliberate imagining," as in the root English).

Terms. 散花／貫花 scattered flowers / strung flowers; 祇夜 geya "repeated verse"; 伽陀 gāthā "corresponding verse" (Dixian's 應頌; he also calls it 孤起); 隨自意三昧 the samādhi of following one's own intent (site Mohe Zhiguan); 十大願王 the ten great kings of vows; 迴因向果／迴事向理／迴自向他 dedicating the cause toward the fruit / phenomena toward principle / oneself toward others; 四重恩 the four heavy kindnesses; 總持 "the comprehensive hold" (unit 97); 安養 the Land of Peace and Nourishment (kept distinct from 極樂 Sukhāvatī); 盧舍那 Rocana, glossed by Dixian as "pure and full"; 老實念佛 reciting the Buddha's name honestly.

Persons and places. Haichao Monastery (海潮寺) in Hangzhou, mentioned in Dixian's own aside on the Brahmā image (unit 61).
