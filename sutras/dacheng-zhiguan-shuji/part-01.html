Preface, the Five Layers of Profound Meaning, and the Opening Dialogue

Dixian (1858–1932), abbot of Guanzong Monastery in Ningbo, lectured on the Dharma Gate of Mahāyāna Calming-and-Contemplation (attributed to Huisi). His lay disciple Jiang Miaoxu (Shengguan), with Xu Deming and Jiang's two sons, wrote the lectures down; the draft was made between the winter of 1922 and the summer of 1923 and revised over eight years into these Notes Expounding, in twenty fascicles. Part 1 opens with Jiang's preface, which presents calming and contemplation as the cultivated form of the mind's inherent tranquility and illumination. Dixian then treats the title by the five layers of profound meaning used in the Tiantai school: Ouyi Zhixu's eight pairs of agent and object for "Mahāyāna calming-and-contemplation dharma-gate," the mind pure in its own nature as the essence, calming-and-contemplation as the cardinal doctrine, removing obstructions and attaining benefits as the function, and the classification of the work as perfect in content and sudden in method. A life of Huisi follows. The commentary on the opening dialogue matches each phrase of the root text with the three calmings and three contemplations, and the part ends with the five rounds of the extensive distinctions and the first of the seven names of the one mind. Root-text sentences are marked Root text and reuse the English of the Dharma Gate already drafted in this round.

Preface to the Notes Expounding Mahāyāna Calming-and-Contemplation

Chinese Source

止觀者，定慧之程也；定慧者，寂照之徵也。寂照為本具之性德，而顯之以定慧；定慧為積進之修功，而達之以止觀也。蓋必依本寂以修止，而後得定；依本照以修觀，而後發慧。即復依止行入定，而契乎本寂；依觀行成慧，而合乎本照。是謂全性以起修，全修而在性焉。此性修不二之要門，而體用齊彰之軌道也，故分而明之。曰寂曰照，曰定曰慧，曰止曰觀，其實一也。故曰寂照同時，定慧平等，止觀雙行。同時者，寂而常照，照而常寂，不前不後也。平等者，慧由定出，定因慧廣，無偏無倚也。雙行者，即止之觀，即觀之止，非同非別也。蓋以性本同時，故必平等以雙行；以修能雙行，故乃同時而平等也。此佛菩薩所以不住生死，不住涅槃，徧界分身而未嘗來往，不作心意而妙應無方，無相而無不相，無不為而無為，甚深微妙，蔑以加矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Calming-and-contemplation is the course of concentration and wisdom; concentration and wisdom are the evidence of tranquility and illumination. Tranquility and illumination are the inherent virtue originally possessed, and they are revealed through concentration and wisdom; concentration and wisdom are the merit of cultivation accumulated by advancing, and they are reached through calming-and-contemplation. For one must rely on original tranquility to cultivate calming, and only then attain concentration; one must rely on original illumination to cultivate contemplation, and only then bring forth wisdom. Then, relying further on the practice of calming, one enters concentration and accords with original tranquility; relying on the practice of contemplation, one accomplishes wisdom and unites with original illumination. This is called giving rise to cultivation with the whole nature, the whole of cultivation lying in the nature. This is the essential gate of the nonduality of nature and cultivation, and the track on which substance and function are revealed together; therefore they are distinguished and explained. Whether we say tranquility or illumination, concentration or wisdom, calming or contemplation, in reality they are one. Hence it is said: tranquility and illumination are simultaneous, concentration and wisdom are equal, calming and contemplation are practiced together. "Simultaneous" means tranquil yet always illuminating, illuminating yet always tranquil, neither before nor after. "Equal" means that wisdom comes forth from concentration and concentration is broadened by wisdom, without leaning to either side. "Practiced together" means the contemplation that is calming and the calming that is contemplation, neither the same nor separate. For since in the nature they are originally simultaneous, they must be equal and practiced together; since in cultivation they can be practiced together, they are therefore simultaneous and equal. This is why buddhas and bodhisattvas do not abide in birth and death and do not abide in nirvāṇa; they divide their bodies throughout the realms yet have never come or gone; they do not exercise mind and intention, yet their wondrous responses have no fixed place; without marks, yet there is nothing they do not mark; there is nothing they do not do, yet they do nothing. Extremely deep, subtle, and wondrous — nothing can be added to it.

Ru-Yi Translation

Now this extremely deep, subtle, and wondrous substance and function is possessed in the mind of the present single thought of every person: in sages it certainly does not increase, and in ordinary beings it does not decrease either. Yet the original nature has long been deluded; who knows of cultivation and realization? Over the surging sea of suffering, the Great Awakened One aroused compassion. Therefore he set up [teachings] according to capacities and turned the perfect voice everywhere; like the ocean, like clouds, all of it makes this matter clear. But when the teaching was transmitted to Zhendan, the time was far from the Sage; with obstructions deep, people could not discern its fords and shores; with wisdom shallow, few could glimpse its purport. Some wandered lost in the vast wilderness, others stumbled on forked roads. Thereupon Great Master Nanyue, moved with feeling, composed the Mahāyāna Calming-and-Contemplation. As for this book, it holds the depths of provisional existence and true emptiness as a net is held by its main cord, and its method of clarifying the substance and reaching the function threads diverse strands into one. Its meaning embraces beginning and end without being prolix; its principle is analyzed to the finest hair without being petty. Nothing like it had been composed in the eastern land.

Now a physician must see through the condition of the organs, know where the pores and tissues meet, grasp the signs of a disease's changes, and examine the subtleties of its waxing and waning. Only then does he apply needles and moxa, or prepare decoctions; the techniques differ, but the measure of their effect is one. Why? Their principle lies in nothing else. This book is the same: it lays out the source and the stream, cuts open the joints, points out the steps, and returns to the root. Further, it sifts the false from the right and sweeps away doubts and attachments; its veins and channels are bright and clear, as if one were looking at one's own palm. Thus although Chan, the teachings, Pure Land, and the esoteric each have different gates and paths, if one gains its meaning and grasps how it penetrates them, one will find the joy of meeting the source on the left and on the right, and the result of doubling the effect with half the work. Why? Principle has no two destinations.

Ru-Yi Translation

Later Great Master Zhizhe followed and broadened it, further expounding the Mohe Zhiguan; its meaning was richer still and its text more abundant. Beautiful and vast, it is on everyone's lips. Yet if students do not first master Nanyue's meaning, then hearing suddenly a hundred notes joined in music, how could they distinguish its measures? And Nanyue's Dharma of mind long lay buried overseas. Though it was transmitted again in the Song, few paid it attention, and so its essentials were forgotten; even where there were commentaries and explanations, they seldom probed it. When this meaning is not clear, how can the benighted know how to cultivate, how can those who cultivate know how they fall ill, and how can those who are ill know how to be cured? Moved by the deep kindness of the sages and the wise, and cherishing this treasured book of the dharma-gate, I look up and down through past and present and sigh long.

Ru-Yi Translation

What has long been obscured must come to light; causes and conditions came together. The layman Nanyuan expressed the sincerity of his request, and Master Di of Guanzong took up the vow to reveal and spread it. Having completed the four siddhāntas and further merged the three wondrous truths, he made the Dharma torch shine again and the road of cultivation bright: the golden probe is in hand, and the eye of wisdom can open. I, [Miao]xu, though not clever, day by day took up the pen with Mr. Xu Deming and recorded, and had my two sons, Shengxing and Shengxin, follow along and write as well. Then we compiled and arranged, expounded and threaded together; what was deep and hidden we made clear by turning it over, what was subtle and wondrous we gave shape through similes. We brought it to Master Di for correction, going back and forth again and again. We began in the winter of the renxu year and finished the draft in the summer of the guihai year. It is named "notes expounding" (述記) because Master Di humbly said that he was relating what he had heard of old, and I in turn related it again. Yet fearing that technical terms and classical phrases might be hard to understand, I further explained them, revising many times, seeking only plain and accessible words; the principles were perhaps not drilled into painfully. Eight years slipped by, and the book was completed in twenty fascicles. May each person know the essentials and every dharma return to the mind; may all follow the road of awakening together, and each obtain the jewel in the robe. Now the essential of calming-and-contemplation lies in spurring cultivation; if it merely supplies talk, what has it to do with the path? Concentration and wisdom are born from the Vinaya; if the foundation is not laid, how can their effects be seen? Nanyue's book was written for those who keep the precepts and cultivate in quiet. What I wish, together with those resolved on the path, is to be diligent in this.

Ru-Yi Translation

All good and wise friends: a human body is hard to obtain, and the Buddha-dharma is hard to hear. Having obtained a human body and heard the Buddha-dharma, you must first hold the one mind upright and be utterly sincere and reverent. Why? Because this is the wondrous Dharma that transcends the world, not worldly writing. Moreover, this text differs from the other works: it is the essential art by which the buddhas of the ten directions turn back from the false and return to the true, the secret classic by which the patriarchs of every generation have clarified the mind and seen the nature. It is neither sūtra, nor vinaya, nor treatise. Yet speaking in terms of meaning, it embraces the three baskets. For this whole work from beginning to end speaks of calming-and-contemplation. Calming-and-contemplation is the training in concentration, and so it is included in the sūtra basket. Within the work, explanations and distinctions follow one upon another until the net of doubt is entirely removed; this is the training in wisdom, and so it is included in the treatise basket. If we speak of the two words "calming" and "contemplation" separately, calming is the training in concentration and contemplation is the training in wisdom. And to cultivate this calming-and-contemplation one must rely upon the Mahāyāna mind pure in its own nature. Purity in its own nature is the substance of the precepts. Phrases in the text such as "stopping delusion and returning to the true" and "clinging to conditions is forever stilled" are the training in precepts, and so it is included in the vinaya basket. You should know that what buddha after buddha has transmitted and patriarch after patriarch has handed on is this very dharma-gate. As is said, "To leave birth and death there is no other road; to enter nirvāṇa there is only this gate." Once you understand this one dharma-gate, whatever practice you use, every step is the path. If not, it will be hard to avoid going astray onto forked roads. So the importance of this Dharma assembly can be known. Listen attentively, listen attentively, and reflect on it well.

Ru-Yi Translation

The names established for all scriptures do not go beyond seven kinds: first, dharma alone; second, person alone; third, simile alone; fourth, person and dharma; fifth, person and simile; sixth, dharma and simile; seventh, person, dharma, and simile. This work is named by dharma alone, with no simile and no person. Our Great Master Lingfeng, explaining this Dharma-title, set up eight layers of agent and object — the so-called meaning of eight pairs and sixteen singles, which is of the utmost importance. The eight pairs are: what gives rise and what is given rise to; what relies and what is relied upon; what reaches and what is reached; what passes through and what is passed through; what sifts and what is sifted; what accomplishes and what is accomplished; what expresses and what is expressed; what understands and what is understood. Now I will explain them in order. On the first layer of agent and object I will speak in particular detail; for the other seven layers I will give only the gist of each. Students may consult the Essentials Explaining Calming-and-Contemplation and study them closely.

Ru-Yi Translation

○ The first layer. The two words "Mahāyāna" are what gives rise; "the dharma-gate of calming-and-contemplation" is what is given rise to. "Give rise" means to produce. Who can give rise to the dharma-gate of calming-and-contemplation? Only the Mahāyāna can give rise to it. The Mahāyāna is the single thought of mind that we originally possess; in this book it is called the mind pure in its own nature. Its meaning is very broad, and it must be explained in two sections: first separately, then together. Explained separately: first "great" (大), then "vehicle" (乘). What is "great"? It means absolute, with nothing outside it. Our present single thought of mind, however slight, cuts off all relativity; it has no inside and no outside — what is called being without before or after, without direction or corner, vertically exhausting the three times and horizontally pervading the ten directions. It fully possesses the three greatnesses of substance, mark, and function. The three greatnesses: following conditions yet unchanging, its substance is suchness — this is the greatness of substance. The mind's substance, which is the false wholly identical to the true, fully possesses merits in accord with the nature more numerous than the sands of the Ganges; in ordinary beings they do not decrease, in sages they do not increase — this is the greatness of mark. The substance and mark of this single thought's mind-nature, unchanging yet following conditions, can give birth to the causes and fruits of the ten dharma-realms; when one penetrates that the causes and fruits of these ten realms arise from conditions and are without nature, one can turn defilement into purity — this is the greatness of function. Because the one mind possesses these three greatnesses, it is called "great." It never had before and after; it is the originally great, not the spurious great that is first small and later great. Only when eyes and feet assist each other does one reach the place of treasure and arrive at the cool pond. Therefore the Great Master consents, saying, "I will make extensive openings and showings for you." The meaning is that one who did not awaken on hearing the brief explanation should be able to awaken on hearing the extensive discussion. And not only for you, but also so that others who have not heard may seek out this extensive discussion, grasp the essentials of the Dharma, and open the ground of the mind into awakening. This is precisely why the patriarch, with vast and deep compassion and vows, showed the essentials of mind in detail.

For this mind-nature is what all dharmas rely upon; if one does not first awaken to it, there is no sphere to be contemplated, and how could one distinguish the substance and form of what contemplates? And cutting off and attaining, and the activity, would have no way to arise. Therefore among the five rounds this is set first. Next, explaining the spheres: in contrast to delusion the true nature is shown, and so there is the real sphere; deluded about the mind, deluded thinking arises, and so there are the discriminated and dependent spheres.
