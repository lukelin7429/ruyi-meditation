Gathering Skillful Means and the Acquiescence to Non-Arising

The treatise turns to Acalā, the Immovable Ground — the Eighth Ground — opening with the first of its seven structural divisions. Vajragarbha names the gathering of skillful means by which a bodhisattva, having thoroughly purified every practice across the first seven grounds, arouses the power of great vows and receives the empowering strength of the Tathāgatas together with his own roots of goodness, and the commentary traces a shared characteristic common to all seven prior grounds, together with a distinct characteristic matching each one in turn. The bodhisattva then enters acquiescence to the fact that all dharmas are, from the very beginning, without arising, without coming-into-being, without characteristic, without emergence, unlost, and inexhaustible — analyzed by the commentary into four kinds of non-arising, refuting seven mistaken "realities" in turn — before attaining, through this very acquiescence, the Eighth Ground itself, the Ground Immovable, his "deep practice" unfolded through seven further named aspects and illustrated by the similes of a monk entering the absorption of cessation, a dreamer waking from a great river, and one born within the Brahmā heaven.

Gathering Skillful Means: The Shared and Distinct Characteristics of the First Seven Grounds

Chinese Source

論曰：第八地中有七種相差別：一、總明方便作集地分；二、得淨忍分；三、得勝行分；四、淨佛國土分；五、得自在分；六、大勝分；七、釋名分。云何總明方便作集地分？

經曰：爾時金剛藏菩薩言：諸佛子！若菩薩於七地中善集慧方便、善清淨諸行、善集助道法、善起大願力、善加如來力加，自善根力得力故、常念隨順如來力無畏不共佛法故、善淨深心覺故、成就福德智力故、大慈悲等不捨一切眾生行故、通達無量智道故。

論曰：總明方便作集地分者，七地總故，同相及別相。云何同相？

同相有三種：一者二種無我上上證故；二者不住道清淨故；三者彼方便智行所攝滿足助菩提分法故。如經「諸佛子！若菩薩於七地中善集慧方便故、善清淨諸行故、善集助道法」故。云何別相？善起大願力等。

初地等諸地，如經「善起大願力」故。第二地中，是故我應等行十善業道，修行一切種，令清淨具足彼處如來力故，如經「善加如來力加」故。第三地中，厭得不退禪定等，自善根力得通達故，如經「自善根力得力」故。第四地中，所說法分別智教化智障淨勝念通達佛法，如經「常念隨順如來力無畏不共佛法」故。第五地中，深淨心平等善淨深心等，如經「善淨深心覺」故。第六地中，大悲自在為首，增上觀因緣集成就福德智力，如經「成就福德智力」故。第七地中，方便智慧發起殊勝道，不捨一切眾生行，如經「大慈悲等不捨一切眾生行故」。以無量眾生界故入無量智道，如經「通達無量智道故」。如是第八地總明方便作集地分已說。云何得淨忍分？得無生法忍故。彼清淨自然無功用行應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Commentary: Within the Eighth Ground there are seven distinct aspects: first, the division generally showing the gathering of skillful means for this ground; second, the division of attaining pure acquiescence; third, the division of attaining surpassing practice; fourth, the division of purifying buddha-lands; fifth, the division of attaining mastery; sixth, the division of great excellence; and seventh, the division explaining the name. What, then, is the division generally showing the gathering of skillful means for this ground?

Sūtra: At that time the bodhisattva Vajragarbha said: Sons of the Buddha! If a bodhisattva, within the seven grounds [already traversed], has well gathered the skillful means of wisdom, well purified every practice, well gathered the dharmas that assist the path, and well given rise to the power of great vows, and well received the empowering strength of the Tathāgatas — through the strength of his own roots of goodness having attained strength; constantly mindful, according with the Tathāgatas' own strength, fearlessness, and unshared buddha-dharmas; his deep mind well purified and awakened; having accomplished the power of merit and wisdom; his great loving-kindness and compassion and the rest never abandoning the practice for the sake of all sentient beings; and thoroughly penetrating the boundless path of wisdom —

Commentary: The division generally showing the gathering of skillful means for this ground gathers together the seven grounds, having both a shared characteristic and a distinct characteristic. What, then, is the shared characteristic?

The shared characteristic has three kinds: first, because he has, in the most excellent way, realized the twofold selflessness; second, because the path of non-abiding is purified; and third, because the dharmas that assist enlightenment, gathered in by that skillful, expedient wisdom and practice, are fulfilled. As the sūtra says, "Sons of the Buddha! If a bodhisattva, within the seven grounds, has well gathered the skillful means of wisdom, well purified every practice, well gathered the dharmas that assist the path." What, then, is the distinct characteristic? It is shown through such phrases as "well giving rise to the power of great vows" and the rest.

[Matching] the First Ground and each of the other grounds in turn — as the sūtra says, "well giving rise to the power of great vows." Within the Second Ground: it is on this account that he should, cultivating in every mode the path of the ten wholesome actions, cause the strength of the Tathāgatas belonging to that stage to become pure and complete — as the sūtra says, "well received the empowering strength of the Tathāgatas." Within the Third Ground: having attained aversion to worldly existence and non-retrogression in meditative concentration and the rest, the strength of his own roots of goodness attains thorough penetration — as the sūtra says, "through the strength of his own roots of goodness having attained strength." Within the Fourth Ground: the wisdom that discriminates the Dharma taught, and the wisdom that teaches and transforms others, the purification surpassing the hindrance to wisdom, and mindfulness thoroughly penetrating the buddha-dharmas — as the sūtra says, "constantly mindful, according with the Tathāgatas' own strength, fearlessness, and unshared buddha-dharmas." Within the Fifth Ground: the equality of the pure, deep mind, and the well purified deep mind and the rest — as the sūtra says, "his deep mind well purified and awakened." Within the Sixth Ground: great compassion, self-mastery foremost, the surpassing contemplation of the gathering of dependent origination, accomplishing the power of merit and wisdom — as the sūtra says, "having accomplished the power of merit and wisdom." Within the Seventh Ground: skillful, expedient wisdom arousing the surpassing path, not abandoning the practice for the sake of all sentient beings — as the sūtra says, "his great loving-kindness and compassion and the rest never abandoning the practice for the sake of all sentient beings." Through the boundless realm of sentient beings, he enters the boundless path of wisdom — as the sūtra says, "thoroughly penetrating the boundless path of wisdom." In this way, the division generally showing the gathering of skillful means for the Eighth Ground has now been explained. What, then, is the division of attaining pure acquiescence? It is because he attains acquiescence to the non-arising of dharmas (anutpattika-dharma-kṣānti). This should be understood as a practice, pure, natural, and without effortful application.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang opens his treatment of the Eighth Ground — whose own name the treatise will not itself explain until its closing "division explaining the name," many parts from now — by stacking up multiple authorities on what "Immovable" actually means. Under "one sense," he cites the Mahāyānasaṃgraha: 由一切相有功用行不能動故，名不動地 — "because no characteristic, together with effortful application, is any longer able to move it, it is named the Immovable Ground" — noting that both Vasubandhu's and Asvabhāva's own commentaries agree, with Asvabhāva adding a precise contrast to the ground just completed: 第七地中行動、相不動，此中行相俱不動 — "within the Seventh Ground, conduct still moves while its own mark does not move; here, both conduct and mark alike do not move." Under "two senses," he cites the Suvarṇaprabhāsa-sūtra on signless right reflection reaching self-mastery so that no affliction's own activity can move it; under "three senses," the Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi, on non-discriminating wisdom continuing spontaneously, unmoved by the function of any characteristic or affliction. And from the *Daśabhūmi-vibhāṣā: 若天、魔、梵、沙門、婆羅門，無能動其願故，名不動地 — "were even the gods, māra, Brahmā, ascetics, or brahmins to try, none is able to move this bodhisattva's own vow — this is why it is named the Immovable Ground." The Benevolent Kings Sūtra, he notes, calls this same ground instead the Ground of Even Contemplation.

On the treatise's own sevenfold division, Fazang explains the logic behind grouping its first two parts together as "the skillful means for approaching this ground": 一總明方便作集地分者，是遠方便，總前七地作起此地集成方便 — "the first, the division generally showing the gathering of skillful means for this ground, is the distant skillful means, gathering together the seven prior grounds to arouse the accomplished gathering that leads into this ground"; 二得淨忍分者，是近方便，謂前地所得無生忍光，至此修熟令深清淨 — "the second, the division of attaining pure acquiescence, is the near skillful means, meaning that the light of the acquiescence to non-arising already attained at the previous ground, arriving here, is cultivated to ripeness and made deeply pure." The distance is temporal as much as logical: the opening division reaches all the way back across seven grounds' worth of practice, while the second works directly on what the bodhisattva already holds in hand.

Entry into Acquiescence to the Non-Arising of All Dharmas

Chinese Source

經曰：入一切法本來無生、無成、無相、無出、不失、無盡、不行，非有有性，初中後平等，真如無分別入一切智智。是菩薩遠離一切心意識憶想分別，無所貪著，如虛空平等。入一切法如虛空性，是名得無生法忍。

論曰：復次彼忍於四種無生中應知。四種無生者：一、事無生；二、自性無生；三、數差別無生；四、作業差別無生。是中事無生者，實有七種事：一、淨分法中本有實，此對治，如經「入一切法本來無生」故。二、新新生實，此對治，如經「無成」故。三者相實，此對治，如經「無相」故。四、後際實，此對治，如經「無出」故。五、先際實，染分中煩惱障故，此對治，如經「不失」故。六、盡實，諸眾生此對治，如經「無盡」故。七、雜染實，淨分中此對治，如經「不行」故。自性無生者，是法無我，彼法無我，自體無性故，如經「非有有性」故。彼觀事故是此忍，不得言無所有觀，法無我無二相故。數差別無生者，於三時中染淨法不增減故，如經「初中後平等」故。作業差別無生者，於真如中淨無分別佛智故，如經「真如無分別入一切智智」故，如是無生法忍觀示現。次示現行遠離報分別境界想，攝受分別性想故，如經「是菩薩遠離一切心意識憶想分別」故。想者，遠離障法想，非無治法想。

彼治想於下地中有三種勝事：一、無功用自然行，如經「無所貪著」故。二、遍一切法相，如經「如虛空平等」故。三、入真如不動自然行故，如經「入一切法如虛空性，是名得無生法忍」故。如是八地得淨忍分已說。次說得勝行分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: He enters into the fact that all dharmas are, from the very beginning, without arising, without coming-into-being, without characteristic, without emergence, unlost, inexhaustible, and without activity; not having any substantial, existent nature; equal in their beginning, middle, and end; and, through suchness free of discrimination, enters the wisdom of omniscience. This bodhisattva, far removed from every discrimination of mind, mentation, and consciousness, and from every act of recollection and conceptualization, is without any object of craving, equal like empty space. Entering into the fact that all dharmas are, in their own nature, like empty space — this is what is called attaining acquiescence to the non-arising of dharmas.

Commentary: Furthermore, this acquiescence should be understood within four kinds of non-arising. The four kinds of non-arising are: first, non-arising with respect to phenomena; second, non-arising with respect to own-nature; third, non-arising with respect to the distinction of number; and fourth, non-arising with respect to the distinction of karmic function. Here, "non-arising with respect to phenomena": there are, in reality, seven kinds of phenomena being refuted. First, the mistaken reality that, within the pure portion of dharmas, something originally exists — the antidote to this is, as the sūtra says, "enters into the fact that all dharmas are, from the very beginning, without arising." Second, the mistaken reality of ever-new arising — the antidote to this is, as the sūtra says, "without coming-into-being." Third, the mistaken reality of characteristic — the antidote to this is, as the sūtra says, "without characteristic." Fourth, the mistaken reality of a final limit — the antidote to this is, as the sūtra says, "without emergence." Fifth, the mistaken reality of an initial limit, on account of the hindrance of affliction within the defiled portion — the antidote to this is, as the sūtra says, "unlost." Sixth, the mistaken reality of exhaustion, with respect to every sentient being — the antidote to this is, as the sūtra says, "inexhaustible." Seventh, the mistaken reality of admixed defilement, within the pure portion — the antidote to this is, as the sūtra says, "without activity." "Non-arising with respect to own-nature" means the selflessness of dharmas: that very selflessness of dharmas is because their own substance is without any fixed nature — as the sūtra says, "not having any substantial, existent nature." Because he contemplates this very phenomenon, this is what constitutes this acquiescence — one cannot say that this is a contemplation of utter non-existence, since the selflessness of dharmas is a mark without duality. "Non-arising with respect to the distinction of number" means that, across the three times, defiled and pure dharmas neither increase nor decrease — as the sūtra says, "equal in their beginning, middle, and end." "Non-arising with respect to the distinction of karmic function" means that, within suchness, the buddhas' own wisdom is pure and free of discrimination — as the sūtra says, "through suchness free of discrimination, enters the wisdom of omniscience." In this way, the contemplation of acquiescence to the non-arising of dharmas has been made manifest. Next it is made manifest that this practice is far removed from the discriminating conceptualization of the object-realm belonging to karmic retribution, and takes up the conceptualization of true discriminated nature — as the sūtra says, "this bodhisattva, far removed from every discrimination of mind, mentation, and consciousness, and from every act of recollection and conceptualization." "Conceptualization" here means conceptualization far removed from the dharma of hindrance — it does not mean the absence of any conceptualization of the antidote-dharma.

This conceptualization of the antidote, in comparison with the lower grounds, has three excellent matters: first, practice that is natural and without effortful application — as the sūtra says, "without any object of craving." Second, pervading the characteristic of all dharmas — as the sūtra says, "equal like empty space." Third, entering suchness through natural, unmoving practice — as the sūtra says, "entering into the fact that all dharmas are, in their own nature, like empty space — this is what is called attaining acquiescence to the non-arising of dharmas." In this way, the division of attaining pure acquiescence within the Eighth Ground has now been explained. Next, the division of attaining surpassing practice will be explained.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang maps the sūtra passage's own structure before turning to the treatise's fourfold analysis: 文有三：初十句正明無生忍、次四句明無生忍淨、後一句結忍入位 — "the text has three parts: first, ten clauses directly setting out the acquiescence to non-arising; next, four clauses clarifying the purification of this acquiescence; and last, one clause concluding that this acquiescence brings entry into the Eighth stage." He also maps the treatise's fourfold non-arising onto three approaches to realization in turn: 又約寄位，初一在加行、次一在正體、後一在後得 — "and, provisionally assigned by stage, the first non-arising belongs to the stage of preparatory application, the next to the fundamental realization itself, and the last to subsequently attained wisdom."

On the sevenfold list of mistaken "realities" that the treatise's sevenfold refutation targets, Fazang notes a hidden symmetry running through what looks at first like an unstructured list: 論攝為七：初四明不增、後三明不減 — "the treatise gathers these into seven; the first four make manifest non-increase, and the last three make manifest non-decrease." Read this way, the entire sevenfold refutation is really a single claim about the dharma-realm's own freedom from increase or decrease, unfolded in two directions rather than seven unrelated ones.

Attaining the Eighth Ground, the Ground Immovable, and Its Sevenfold Deep Practice

Chinese Source

經曰：又佛子！如是成就法忍，菩薩即時得是第八菩薩不動地，得為深行菩薩，難可得知、無能分別、離一切相、離一切想一切貪著，無量無邊一切聲聞辟支佛所不能壞寂靜，一切寂靜而現在前。

佛子！譬如比丘得具足神通心得自在，次第入滅盡定，一切動心憶想分別皆悉盡滅。佛子！菩薩亦如是，住是第八菩薩不動地，即離一切有功用行及諸憶念，得無功用法，離身口意務住報行成。佛子！譬如有人夢中見身墮在大河，是人爾時發大勇猛、施大方便欲出此河。發勇猛時忽然便寤，寤已即離一切勇猛方便懅事。佛子！

菩薩亦如是，從初已來見諸眾生墮四大河，發大精進力廣修行道至不動地，即離一切想有功用行。是菩薩一切不行二心，諸所憶想不復現前。佛子！譬如生在梵天，欲界煩惱一切不行。如是佛子！菩薩住此菩薩不動地，一切心意識等不行，一切佛心、菩提心、菩薩心、涅槃心不行，何況當行世間心。

論曰：是中得勝行者，得深行故。深行有七種：一、難入深，如經「又佛子！如是成就法忍菩薩，即時得是第八菩薩不動地，得為深行菩薩，難可得知」故。二、同行深，諸淨地菩薩同故，如經「無能分別」故。三、境界深，能取可取不現前故，如經「離一切相、離一切想一切貪著」故。護一切障想故，言離一切貪著。四、修行深，自利利他行故，如經「無量無邊」故。五、不退深，如經「一切聲聞辟支佛所不能壞」故。六、離障深，如經「寂靜」故。七、對治現前深，如經「一切寂靜而現在前」故，真如一切寂靜故。滅盡定喻者，示彼行寂滅故，如經「佛子！譬如比丘乃至離身口意務住報行成」故。一切動心憶想分別皆悉盡滅者，無彼依止故。即離一切有功用行者，過功用行地故。得無功用法者，得彼對治法故，以得無功用法自然行故。住報行成，示現得有功用行相違法故。復住報行成者，善住阿梨耶識真如法中故。夢寤喻者，示此行中護彼過想、有正智想，此行寂滅故。如經「佛子！譬如有人夢中見身乃至諸所憶想不復現前」故。是中依清淨世間涅槃二心不行故、依境界受用念想不行故。生梵天喻者，於下地心一向不行，得報地故，此說遠離勝，如經「佛子！譬如生在梵天乃至何況當行世間心」故。是中順行不順行二分心等，佛等不行故、大乘小乘差別故。大乘小乘中眾生法差別故，無學學差別。佛等涅槃差別說應知。是中順行者，順行分中心等不行故，如經「一切心意識等不行」故。是中不順行者，不順行分中佛等不行故，如經「佛心乃至涅槃心不行」故。大乘小乘差別，大乘中差別者，佛菩薩涅槃差別故；小乘中差別者，聲聞涅槃阿羅漢等差別故。大小乘中眾生法差別者，佛菩薩差別故。法差別者，菩提涅槃差別故，小乘中無學學眾生差別故。是中法差別者，涅槃差別故。無學差別者，阿羅漢差別故；有學差別者，阿那含等差別故。如是等行皆悉不行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Further, sons of the Buddha! Having accomplished acquiescence to the Dharma in this way, the bodhisattva at that very moment attains this Eighth Ground of the bodhisattva, the Ground Immovable, becoming a bodhisattva of deep practice — difficult to fathom, impossible to discriminate, free of every characteristic, free of every conceptualization and every object of craving, boundless and limitless, a quiescence that no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha is able to destroy, a state in which every quiescence stands directly present.

Sons of the Buddha! It is like a monk who, having attained spiritual powers complete and his mind self-mastering, enters, in due sequence, the absorption of cessation, in which every agitated mind, recollection, and conceptualization is entirely and utterly extinguished. Sons of the Buddha! A bodhisattva, too, is just so: dwelling in this Eighth Ground of the bodhisattva, the Ground Immovable, he is at once far removed from every practice of effortful application and every act of recollection, attains the dharma of being without effortful application, is free from the exertions of body, speech, and mind, and abides, accomplished, within the practice belonging to his own karmic recompense. Sons of the Buddha! It is like a person who, within a dream, sees his own body fallen into a great river; at that moment this person arouses great courage and applies great skillful means, wishing to escape this river. Just as he arouses this courage, he suddenly awakens; having awakened, he is at once free from every urgent matter of courage and skillful means. Sons of the Buddha!

A bodhisattva, too, is just so: from the very beginning he has seen every sentient being fallen into the four great rivers, and has aroused the power of great diligence, broadly cultivating the practice of the path, arriving at the Ground Immovable, where he is at once free from every conceptualized practice of effortful application. This bodhisattva no longer activates either of these two minds at all, and all his acts of recollection no longer stand present before him. Sons of the Buddha! It is like one born within the Brahmā heaven, in whom every affliction of the desire realm is entirely inactive. Just so, sons of the Buddha! A bodhisattva, dwelling in this Ground Immovable of the bodhisattva, finds that mind, mentation, consciousness, and the rest are all entirely inactive; the mind of the buddhas, the mind of enlightenment, the mind of the bodhisattva, and the mind of nirvāṇa are all inactive — how much more so any worldly mind!

Commentary: Here, "attaining surpassing practice" means attaining deep practice. Deep practice is of seven kinds: first, the depth of being difficult to enter — as the sūtra says, "further, sons of the Buddha! Having accomplished acquiescence to the Dharma in this way, the bodhisattva at that very moment attains this Eighth Ground of the bodhisattva, the Ground Immovable, becoming a bodhisattva of deep practice — difficult to fathom." Second, the depth of shared practice, since it is shared alike by the bodhisattvas of every pure ground — as the sūtra says, "impossible to discriminate." Third, the depth of the object-realm, since neither the apprehending subject nor the apprehended object stands present before him — as the sūtra says, "free of every characteristic, free of every conceptualization and every object of craving." Because he guards against every conceptualization that hinders, it is said, "free of every craving." Fourth, the depth of cultivated practice, on account of the practice that benefits both himself and others — as the sūtra says, "boundless and limitless." Fifth, the depth of non-retrogression — as the sūtra says, "no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha is able to destroy." Sixth, the depth of being free from hindrance — as the sūtra says, "quiescence." Seventh, the depth of the antidote standing directly present — as the sūtra says, "every quiescence stands directly present" — since suchness itself is every quiescence. As for the simile of the absorption of cessation: it shows that this practice is quiescent and extinguished — as the sūtra says, from "sons of the Buddha! It is like a monk," all the way to "is free from the exertions of body, speech, and mind, and abides, accomplished, within the practice belonging to his own karmic recompense." "Every agitated mind, recollection, and conceptualization is entirely and utterly extinguished" means that it is without any basis to rely upon. "At once far removed from every practice of effortful application" means that he has passed beyond the ground of effortful application. "Attains the dharma of being without effortful application" means that he attains that very antidote-dharma, through attaining the natural practice of the dharma of being without effortful application. "Abides, accomplished, within the practice belonging to his own karmic recompense" makes manifest that he has attained a dharma directly contrary to the practice of effortful application. Furthermore, "abides, accomplished, within the practice belonging to his own karmic recompense" means that he well abides within the dharma of suchness belonging to the ālaya-consciousness. As for the simile of the dream and awakening: it shows that, within this practice, he guards against that mistaken conceptualization, while possessing the conceptualization of correct wisdom, since this practice is quiescent and extinguished — as the sūtra says, from "sons of the Buddha! It is like a person who, within a dream, sees his own body," all the way to "all his acts of recollection no longer stand present before him." Here, it is because, in dependence on purity, these two minds — the worldly mind and the mind of nirvāṇa — are inactive; and it is because, in dependence on the object-realm, the conceptualization of receiving and using it is inactive. As for the simile of birth in the Brahmā heaven: the mind of the lower ground is entirely inactive, since he attains the ground of this new karmic recompense — this speaks of the excellence of remoteness — as the sūtra says, from "sons of the Buddha! It is like one born within the Brahmā heaven," all the way to "how much more so any worldly mind!" Here, there are two portions, the conforming mind and the non-conforming mind, and the rest: because the buddhas and the rest are inactive, and because of the distinction between the Great Vehicle and the lesser vehicle. Within the Great Vehicle and the lesser vehicle, there is a distinction of sentient beings and of dharmas, and a distinction between the one beyond training and the one still in training. The distinct statements of "the buddhas and the rest" and "nirvāṇa" should be understood accordingly. Here, "the conforming mind" means that, within the portion of the conforming mind, mind and the rest are inactive — as the sūtra says, "mind, mentation, consciousness, and the rest are all entirely inactive." Here, "the non-conforming mind" means that, within the portion of the non-conforming mind, the buddhas and the rest are inactive — as the sūtra says, from "the mind of the buddhas" all the way to "the mind of nirvāṇa... are inactive." As for the distinction between the Great Vehicle and the lesser vehicle: the distinction within the Great Vehicle is the distinction between the buddhas, the bodhisattvas, and nirvāṇa; the distinction within the lesser vehicle is the distinction between the śrāvaka's own nirvāṇa, the arhat, and the rest. As for the distinction of sentient beings and of dharmas within the Great Vehicle and the lesser vehicle: the distinction of sentient beings is the distinction between the buddhas and the bodhisattvas; the distinction of dharmas is the distinction between enlightenment and nirvāṇa; and within the lesser vehicle, it is the distinction of sentient beings beyond training and still in training. Here, the distinction of dharmas is the distinction of nirvāṇa; the distinction of one beyond training is the distinction of the arhat; and the distinction of one still in training is the distinction of the non-returner and the rest. In this way, every such practice is, in every case, entirely inactive.

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang groups the treatise's own sevenfold "deep practice" into three clusters, each illustrated by one of the sūtra's three similes taken in reverse order: 別有七，相從為三：初三一分明此地中境分殊絕、次二一分明此地中正行廣大、後二一分明此地中離障寂滅 — "distinctly there are seven, grouped by affinity into three: the first three, as one group, make manifest that this ground's own object-realm is utterly cut off from ordinary access; the next two, as one group, make manifest that this ground's own correct practice is vast and expansive; and the last two, as one group, make manifest that this ground is free from hindrance and quiescent." The three similes — the monk entering cessation, the dream of the great river, and birth in the Brahmā heaven — then illustrate these three clusters working from the last cluster back toward the first.

On the treatise's own puzzling phrase "abides, accomplished, within the practice belonging to karmic recompense," Fazang offers two readings: 一約教道，故云得彼相違法...二約證道，故云善住阿梨耶識真如法中者 — "the first, taken from the standpoint of the teaching approach, explains it as attaining a dharma directly contrary to effortful application; the second, taken from the standpoint of the realization approach, explains it as well abiding within the dharma of suchness belonging to the ālaya-consciousness." He adds a fine point about that consciousness itself: 本識有二分：凡時唯住妄染分，今此住真淨分 — "the fundamental consciousness has two portions: in the state of an ordinary being, one abides only in its false, defiled portion; here, the bodhisattva abides in its true, pure portion" — noting that only at buddhahood does one abide in suchness alone, with no further need to speak of the ālaya-consciousness at all.

Part 36 Complete

This closes Part 36, opening the Eighth Ground, Acalā, the Ground Immovable. The treatise's own seven-part outline of this ground has now been set out, and its first two divisions translated in full: Vajragarbha's gathering of skillful means, tracing a shared characteristic common to all seven prior grounds together with a distinct characteristic matching each one in turn, from the great vows of the First Ground to the boundless wisdom of the Seventh; the bodhisattva's entry into acquiescence to the fact that all dharmas are, from the very beginning, without arising, without coming-into-being, without characteristic, without emergence, unlost, and inexhaustible, analyzed by the commentary into four kinds of non-arising that refute seven mistaken "realities" in turn; and, opening the third division, the bodhisattva's attainment, through this very acquiescence, of the Eighth Ground itself — becoming, in the treatise's own words, a "bodhisattva of deep practice," his profound attainment unfolded through seven named depths and illustrated by the similes of a monk entering the absorption of cessation, a dreamer waking free of the great river the moment he stirs, and one born within the Brahmā heaven in whom the afflictions of the desire realm no longer stir at all. Part 37 turns to one of the treatise's most celebrated passages: the bodhisattva, having settled into this profound, quiescent liberation, is roused and exhorted by all the buddhas themselves not to abandon sentient beings for the sake of final cessation — together with Vajragarbha's own explanation to Vimuktacandra of why the buddhas must intervene in this way.
