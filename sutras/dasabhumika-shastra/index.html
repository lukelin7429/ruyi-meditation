Treatise on the Sūtra of the Ten Grounds.

Vajragarbha Bodhisattva, empowered by buddhas as numerous as motes of dust, teaches the ten grounds a bodhisattva ascends between the first arising of the thought of enlightenment and buddhahood itself — Pramuditā, the Ground of Joy, through Dharmameghā, the Ground of the Dharma-Cloud, each entered through its own named set of ten minds, each closed by its own account of a threefold fruit and its own etymology. Vasubandhu's commentary, translated into Chinese by Bodhiruci and collaborators under the Northern Wei, unfolds this sūtra clause by clause across twelve fascicles — the Daśabhūmika chapter of the Avataṃsaka Sūtra given its fullest classical exegesis. This collection translates the whole of it across fifty-two parts, with reading notes drawn first from Jingying Huiyuan's commentary and, from partway through the Third Ground onward — where Huiyuan's own surviving manuscript ends — from Fazang's.

How to read this collection

The Daśabhūmikasūtra-śāstra is Vasubandhu's own commentary on the "Ten Grounds" chapter of the Avataṃsaka Sūtra — the passage that gave the later Huayan school its systematic account of the bodhisattva path. Ten grounds, twelve fascicles: each ground opens with a named set of ten minds or dharmas that arouse entry into it, unfolds through its own distinctive practice, and closes with a threefold fruit — of gentle taming, of gathered retribution, and of vow and wisdom — together with the treatise's own etymology for that ground's name. Two Chinese commentaries carry this collection's reading notes: Jingying Huiyuan's 十地經論義記 (X0753) through the Second Ground and partway into the Third, where his own manuscript physically ends; and, for every ground thereafter, Fazang's 華嚴經探玄記 (T1733), his independent exegesis of the full Avataṃsaka Sūtra.

Each page carries a continuous run of sūtra and commentary together — exactly as Vasubandhu's own text interleaves them — matched against Ru-Yi's English translation, with reading notes quoting the governing commentator's Chinese in full and translating it directly, never paraphrasing without the source in view. Terminology is matched to this site's existing Yogācāra pages wherever an established rendering already exists, and recorded in the translation notes below wherever this treatise required a fresh one.

What each page includes

  • The Chinese source text — sūtra and commentary together — beside our English translation, verified character-for-character against the base text for full coverage.
  • Reading notes — "after Jingying Huiyuan" through Part 23, "after Fazang" from Part 24 onward.
  • The treatise's own recurring structure made visible: each ground's entry-minds, its practice, and its threefold closing fruit.
  • Terminology matched to this site's existing Yogācāra pages, most closely the Awakening of Faith in the Mahāyāna.

The Ten Grounds

Each ground is named by the treatise's own closing etymology, most often through a simile — refined gold, moonlight surpassed by sunlight, a wish-fulfilling jewel's own light — mapped against what that ground alone accomplishes.

Ground 1 · Fascicles 1–3
Pramuditā, the Ground of Joy
Entered by arousing the thought of enlightenment; named for thirty clauses of joy. The ten great vows, the ten inexhaustible clauses, and the Great Renunciation, closing with a bodhisattva who often becomes a king of Jambudvīpa.
Parts 1–14 · complete
Ground 2 · Fascicle 4
Vimalā, the Ground Free of Defilement
Entered through ten straightforward minds. The ten wholesome courses of action perfected as pure moral discipline, closing with a bodhisattva who often becomes a Wheel-Turning King.
Parts 15–19 · complete
Ground 3 · Fascicle 5
Prabhākarī, the Luminous Ground
Entered through ten kinds of profound mindfulness and world-weariness. Mastery of the four dhyānas and the formless absorptions, closing with the ten qualities of a purified patience. Huiyuan's own surviving commentary ends partway through this ground.
Parts 20–23 · complete
Ground 4 · Fascicle 6
Arciṣmatī, the Blazing Ground
Entered through the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, enumerated in full. Named through a wish-fulfilling jewel's own light, said to outshine the light of the previous ground.
Parts 24–25 · complete
Ground 5 · Fascicle 7
Sudurjayā, the Ground Difficult to Conquer
Entered through ten equal, pure minds. Deep contemplation of the Four Noble Truths in both worldly and supramundane registers, closing with mastery, for others' benefit alone, of every worldly treatise and craft.
Parts 26–28 · complete
Ground 6 · Fascicle 8
Abhimukhī, the Ground of Direct Presence
Entered through ten equal dharmas. Site of the treatise's declaration that the three realms are illusory, being nothing but the making of one mind — later cited by every school of Consciousness-Only thought — and its full exposition of the twelve links of dependent origination.
Parts 29–32 · complete
Ground 7 · Fascicle 9
Dūraṅgamā, the Far-Reaching Ground
Entered through ten surpassing practices. The last ground where effortful application is still needed — its path traversed continuously since the very First Ground, enabling every ground still to come.
Parts 33–35 · complete
Ground 8 · Fascicle 10
Acalā, the Immovable Ground
Entered through acquiescence to the non-arising of all dharmas. A bodhisattva so settled in quiescent liberation that the buddhas of the ten directions must rouse him with seven successive exhortations.
Parts 36–40 · complete
Ground 9 · Fascicle 11
Sādhumatī, the Ground of Excellent Wisdom
Named for the four unhindered wisdoms of dharmas, meaning, language, and eloquence. The systematic "thicket" analysis of sentient beings' own mind, afflictions, and karma that makes a bodhisattva a great Dharma teacher.
Parts 41–45 · complete
Ground 10 · Fascicle 12
Dharmameghā, the Ground of the Dharma-Cloud
Entered upon a great jewel lotus-throne and invested by light released from between the brows of the buddhas of the ten directions — the treatise's own "coronation" scene. Closes the entire path with the great ocean's ten indestructible marks and the wish-fulfilling mani-jewel's ten surpassing qualities, each mapped across the whole of the ten grounds at once.
Parts 46–52 · complete

Translation Notes

Recurring terms, kept consistent across all fifty-two parts. Where a term already has an established rendering elsewhere on this site's Yogācāra pages, we reuse it; where this treatise's own vocabulary needed a fresh rendering, we coined one here. See the full translation notes for the complete glossary and the per-part structure table, and also this site's note on translation method.

金剛藏 — VajragarbhaThe bodhisattva empowered by the buddhas of the ten directions to teach this Dharma-gate — the treatise's principal speaker throughout.
調柔果 / 攝報果 / 願智果 — the fruits of gentle taming, gathered retribution, and vow and wisdomThe recurring threefold structure closing most grounds — illustrated by gold refined again and again in fire.
無生法忍 — acquiescence to the non-arising of dharmasThe realization by which a bodhisattva enters the Eighth Ground, Acalā; analyzed into four kinds of non-arising.
四無礙智 — the four unhindered wisdomsOf dharmas, meaning, language, and eloquence — the wisdoms that give the Ninth Ground, Sādhumatī, its very name.
陀羅尼 — dhāraṇīLeft untransliterated throughout; ten distinct named dhāraṇīs are enumerated at the Ninth Ground's close.
稠林 — thicketA dense, tangled proliferation of mental or karmic activity a bodhisattva must thoroughly know in order to teach — eleven named thickets analyzed across the Ninth Ground.
十種平等深淨心 — the ten kinds of equal, pure mindThe recurring structure by which a bodhisattva enters each ground from the Second onward, particular to each ground.
三界虛妄，但是一心作 — "the three realms are illusory, being nothing but the making of one mind"The treatise's single most historically consequential line — cited by every later treatise establishing the doctrine of Consciousness-Only.
十二因緣 — the twelve links of dependent originationTraced from ignorance to aging-and-death, expounded at length within the Sixth Ground, Abhimukhī.
三十七道品 — the thirty-seven factors of enlightenmentEnumerated in full at the opening of the Fourth Ground, Arciṣmatī.
四無量心 — the four immeasurablesLoving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, cultivated "vast, immeasurable, and free of enmity."
波羅蜜 — pāramitāLeft untransliterated throughout; the treatise pairs one pāramitā with special emphasis to each of the ten grounds.
