The Ten Equal Dharmas and the Mind-Only Declaration

The treatise turns to Abhimukhī, the Ground of Direct Presence — the Sixth Ground, escalating the Fifth Ground's own three divisions one degree further: the antidote to the pride of excellence, now specifically the pride that discriminates defiled dharmas from pure dharmas; the excellence of the practice of the non-abiding path; and the excellence of its fruit. Vajragarbha names the ten kinds of equal dharma that bring the bodhisattva into this ground, who then contemplates, with a great compassion foremost, the world's own differences in birth and rebirth destination across the five paths, and reflects that failing to truly know the Four Truths in their ultimate meaning is itself called ignorance, tracing the karmic fruit-body of name-and-form that this ignorance produces. The treatise closes this part with one of its most consequential lines: the bodhisattva's own declaration that the three realms are illusory, being nothing but the making of one mind — and the citation that the Tathāgata's own teaching of the twelve links of dependent origination is, in its entirety, grounded in this one mind.

The Three Divisions of the Sixth Ground, and the Ten Kinds of Equal Dharma

Chinese Source

論曰：如五地中三分差別，勝慢對治、不住道行勝及彼果勝。第六地亦如是應知，轉勝故。云何勝？第四地中已說眾生我慢解法慢對治，第五地中已說身淨分別慢對治，今第六地中說取染淨法分別慢對治，以十平等法故。

經曰：爾時金剛藏菩薩言：諸佛子！若菩薩已善具足第五地道，欲入第六菩薩地，當以十平等法得入第六地。何等為十？一者一切法無相平等故；二者一切法無想平等故；三者一切法無生平等故；四、一切法無成平等故；五、一切法寂靜平等故；六、一切法本淨平等故；七、一切法無戲論平等故；八、一切法無取捨平等故；九、一切法如幻、夢、影、響、水中月、鏡中像、焰化平等故；十、一切法有無不二平等故。是菩薩如是觀一切法相，除垢故、隨順故、無分別故，得入第六菩薩現前地，得明利順忍，未得無生法忍。

論曰：取染淨法分別慢對治者，謂十平等法。是中一切法無相乃至一切有無不二平等者，是十二入一切法自性無相平等故。復次相分別對治有九種：一、十二入自相想，如經「一切法無想平等故」。二、念展轉行相，如經「一切法無生平等故」。三、生展轉行相，如經「一切法無成平等故」。四、染相，如經「一切法寂靜平等故」。五、淨相，如經「一切法本淨平等故」。六、分別相，如經「一切法無戲論平等故」。七、出沒相，如經「一切法無取捨平等故」。八、我非有相，如經「一切法如幻、夢、影、響、水中月、鏡中像、焰化平等故」。九、成壞相，如經「一切法有無不二平等故」。除垢者，遠離障垢故。隨順者，隨順平等真如法故。無分別者，不生分別想故。明利者，微細慢對治故，前二地中麁中慢對治故。得軟中忍順者，隨順無生法忍故。未得無生法忍者，此忍順無生法忍，非即無生忍故。是名取染淨法分別慢對治。云何不住道行勝？

Ru-Yi Translation

Commentary: Just as, within the Fifth Ground, the distinctions were three — the antidote to the pride of excellence, the excellence of the practice of the non-abiding path, and the excellence of its fruit — the Sixth Ground should likewise be understood, since it turns ever more excellent still. In what way more excellent? Within the Fourth Ground, the antidote to the pride of self-conceit in sentient beings and the pride of understanding the Dharma was already explained; within the Fifth Ground, the antidote to the pride that discriminates the purity of one's own person was already explained; now, within this Sixth Ground, the antidote to the pride that discriminates defiled dharmas from pure dharmas is explained, through the ten kinds of equal dharma.

Sūtra: At that time the bodhisattva Vajragarbha said: Sons of the Buddha! When a bodhisattva has already well brought to completion the path of the Fifth Ground and wishes to enter the Sixth Ground, the bodhisattva ground, he should, through ten kinds of equal dharma, enter into the Sixth Ground. What are the ten? First, the equality of all dharmas being without characteristic; second, the equality of all dharmas being without conception; third, the equality of all dharmas being without arising; fourth, the equality of all dharmas being without formation; fifth, the equality of all dharmas being tranquil and still; sixth, the equality of all dharmas being originally pure; seventh, the equality of all dharmas being without idle discourse; eighth, the equality of all dharmas being without grasping or rejecting; ninth, the equality of all dharmas being like an illusion, a dream, a shadow, an echo, the moon reflected in water, an image in a mirror, and a conjured apparition; and tenth, the equality of all dharmas in which existence and non-existence are not two. This bodhisattva, contemplating the characteristics of all dharmas in this way — because he removes defilement, because he accords with them, and because he is without discrimination — attains entry into the Sixth Ground, the bodhisattva's Abhimukhī, the Ground of Direct Presence, attaining a keen and sharp receptivity that accords with reality, though not yet attaining the receptivity of the unborn dharma.

Commentary: "The antidote to the pride that discriminates defiled dharmas from pure dharmas" refers to these ten kinds of equal dharma. Here, from "all dharmas being without characteristic" all the way to "all dharmas in which existence and non-existence are not two" — these mean that the twelve sense-fields, the own-nature of all dharmas, are equally without characteristic. Furthermore, the antidote to discriminating characteristics is of nine kinds: first, the notion of the self-characteristic of the twelve sense-fields — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas being without conception." Second, the aspect of mindfulness turning on and on — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas being without arising." Third, the aspect of arising turning on and on — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas being without formation." Fourth, the characteristic of defilement — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas being tranquil and still." Fifth, the characteristic of purity — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas being originally pure." Sixth, the characteristic of discrimination — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas being without idle discourse." Seventh, the characteristic of emerging and vanishing — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas being without grasping or rejecting." Eighth, the characteristic of self being without real existence — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas being like an illusion, a dream, a shadow, an echo, the moon reflected in water, an image in a mirror, and a conjured apparition." Ninth, the characteristic of formation and dissolution — as the sūtra says, "the equality of all dharmas in which existence and non-existence are not two." "Removing defilement" means departing far from the defilement of hindrance. "According with them" means according with the dharma of equal suchness. "Without discrimination" means not giving rise to any discriminating notion. "Keen and sharp" means the antidote to a subtle, minute pride, since the two grounds before this one countered only a coarse and middling pride. "Attaining a soft-and-middling receptivity that accords" means according with the receptivity of the unborn dharma. "Not yet attaining the receptivity of the unborn dharma" means that this receptivity accords with the receptivity of the unborn dharma, without yet being the receptivity of the unborn itself. This is called the antidote to the pride that discriminates defiled dharmas from pure dharmas. What, then, is the excellence of the practice of the non-abiding path?

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang's name-etymology section stacks three sources on why this ground is called 現前, "Direct Presence" or "Manifestation." The Mahāyānasaṃgraha grounds it in the wisdom of dependent origination itself: 由緣起智，能令般若波羅蜜多現在前故，名現前地 — "through the wisdom of dependent origination, the perfection of wisdom [prajñā-pāramitā] itself is caused to become directly present — hence it is named the Ground of Direct Presence." Vasubandhu's own commentary sharpens this into a claim about non-discriminating wisdom specifically: 謂此地中住緣起智，由此智力令無分別智而得現前，悟一切法無染無淨 — "in this ground, one abides in the wisdom of dependent origination; through the power of this wisdom, non-discriminating wisdom itself is caused to become directly present, awakening to the fact that all dharmas are neither defiled nor pure." The Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra names a third route to the same presence, through repeated attention to signlessness: 現前觀察諸行流轉，又於無相多修作意方現前，名現前地 — "directly beholding the flowing-on of all formations, and, through much cultivated attention to signlessness, presence is thereby attained — hence it is named the Ground of Direct Presence."

On the ten kinds of equal dharma themselves, Fazang gives a clean structural key before working through them one by one: 此十平等分以為三：初八以無破有、次一以有破無、後一有無雙破。又初一是總、餘九為別 — "these ten equal dharmas divide into three: the first eight break existence through non-existence; the next one breaks non-existence through existence; and the last one breaks both existence and non-existence together. And of these, the first is the general statement, while the remaining nine are its particulars." The list's apparent uniformity — ten clauses, each ending "…平等故" — thus conceals a deliberate dialectical architecture rather than a simple enumeration.

Great Compassion Foremost: Birth and Rebirth Across the Five Paths, and Ignorance as Failing to Know the Truths

Chinese Source

經曰：是菩薩如是觀一切法相隨順得至，復以勝大悲為首故、大悲增上故、令大悲滿足故、觀世間生滅故。

論曰：是菩薩如是觀一切法相隨順得至者，得至不住道行勝故。不住道行勝者，不捨眾生過去現在未來大悲攝勝故、一切所知法中智淨故、一切種微細因緣集觀故、不住世間涅槃故。如經「復以大悲為首乃至觀世間生滅故」。

經曰：是菩薩觀世間生滅已，作是念：世間所有受身生處差別，皆以貪著我故，若離著我則無世間生處。菩薩復作是念：此諸凡夫愚癡所盲貪著於我，無智闇障常求有無，恒隨邪念妄行邪道，集起妄行，罪行、福行、不動行，以是行故起心種子，有漏有取想故。起未來生老死身，復生後有，業為地、無明覆蔽、愛水為潤、我心溉灌、種種見網令得增長，生名色牙。生已增長，名色增長已成諸根，諸根成已迭互相對生觸，觸相對生受，受後所悕生愛，以有愛故生取，取增長已生有，有成已生五陰身。五陰身增長已，於五道中漸漸衰變名為老，衰老變滅名為死。死後生諸熱惱，因熱惱故生一切憂悲苦惱聚。是因緣集，無有集者自然而集，無有滅者自然而滅。是菩薩如是隨順觀因緣集。

論曰：是中世間所有受身生處差別者，五道中所有生死差別，是名世間所有差別。此因緣集有三種觀門應知：一、成答相差別；二、第一義諦差別；三、世諦差別。云何成差別？初明唯因緣集，釋無我義，成一切世間所有受身生處皆以貪著我故，若離著我則無世間生處，即無我義成。若第一義中實有我相者，著我之心即是第一義智，不應在世間受身生處生。又復若第一義中實有我相者，若離著我則應常生世間。顯示此義，如經「世間所有受身生處差別，皆以貪著我故，若離著我則無世間生處」故。云何答差別？若實無我，云何著我？此中應有是難。即自答言，愚癡所盲貪著於我。此示現，如經「菩薩復作是念：此諸凡夫愚癡所盲貪著於我」故。如是實無有我，有何次第貪著於我，得有世間受身生處？成此示現，如經「無智闇障常求有無」故，如是答難差別。是中無智有無者悕求常斷，此示無明、有愛是二有支根本故，恒隨邪念妄行邪道，集起妄行罪行福行不動行。恒隨邪念者，示無明因故。妄行邪道者，示於解脫處不正行故。集起妄行者，示菩薩勝義故，菩薩雖行於有不名妄行。以是行故起心種子，有漏有取想乃至隨順觀因緣集。是中起心種子者，示生老死體性。復生後有者，隨順攝取成就罪福等行，業為地故、前說無智闇障無明覆蔽故、常求有無愛水為潤故，如是住、如是生心，我是我所、我我想是慢、我生不生，如是等種種見網。自然而滅者，性自滅故，非智緣滅。如是答難因緣集，釋無我義成已。云何相差別？若因緣無，我以何相住因緣集行？

經曰：是菩薩復作是念：不如實知諸諦第一義故名為無明，無明所作業果是名為行，依行有初心識，與識共生有四受陰名為名色，名色增長有六入，根塵識三事和合生有漏觸，觸共生故有受，受染著故名愛，愛增長故名取，從取起有漏業名有，有業有果報名生，生陰增長衰變名為老，老已陰壞名為死，死別離時愚人貪著心熱名為憂發聲啼哭名為悲，五根相對名為苦，意根相對名為憂，憂苦轉多名為惱。如是但有苦樹增長無有作者。菩薩作是念：若有作者則有作事，若無作者則無作事，第一義中無有作者、無有作事。

論曰：是中無明所作業果者，所謂名色。於中識者，彼依止故、名色與識共生故、識名色迭互相依故。若無作者於中分別作事亦無，此說因緣集有分。自體無作事故，是名有分次第因緣集觀應知。云何第一義諦差別？如是證第一義諦則得解脫彼觀故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: This bodhisattva, having in this way contemplated the characteristics of all dharmas and accorded with them to their very attainment, moreover — because a supreme great compassion is foremost, because this great compassion grows and increases, because this great compassion is brought to its fulfillment, and because he contemplates the arising and ceasing of the world —

Commentary: "This bodhisattva, having in this way contemplated the characteristics of all dharmas, accords with them to their very attainment" means that he attains the very excellence of the practice of the non-abiding path. "The excellence of the practice of the non-abiding path" means that a great compassion, gathering in sentient beings of the past, present, and future without ever abandoning them, excels; that the wisdom regarding every dharma that is known is pure; that he contemplates, in every mode, the subtle and minute arising of causes and conditions; and that he abides neither in the world nor in nirvāṇa — as the sūtra says, "moreover, because a great compassion is foremost... all the way to because he contemplates the arising and ceasing of the world."

Sūtra: This bodhisattva, having contemplated the arising and ceasing of the world, gives rise to this thought: every distinction in the world among the places where beings receive a body and are born is entirely on account of clinging in craving to a self; were one to depart from this clinging to self, there would be no place in the world where birth occurs. The bodhisattva further gives rise to this thought: these ordinary beings, blinded by ignorance and clinging in craving to a self, in the darkness and obstruction of their lack of wisdom, constantly seek after existence and non-existence, and, forever following false thoughts, wrongly practice a false path, gathering and giving rise to false conduct — conduct that is sinful, conduct that is meritorious, and conduct that is unmoving. Through this conduct, the seed of mind is aroused, since it is defiled and bound up with the notion of grasping. This arouses a body of future birth, aging, and death, giving rise once more to future existence: karma serves as the ground; ignorance serves as the covering; the moisture of craving serves as the wetting; the watering of the mind of self, and the net of manifold views, cause it to grow — and thus the sprout of name-and-form is born. Once born, it grows; once name-and-form has grown, it forms the sense-faculties; once the sense-faculties are formed, they encounter one another in turn and give rise to contact; contact, encountering its object, gives rise to feeling; after feeling comes the craving for what is longed for; because there is craving, grasping arises; once grasping has grown, existence arises; once existence is accomplished, the body of the five aggregates arises. Once the body of the five aggregates has grown, within the five paths it gradually declines and changes — this is called aging; the decline and change of aging, once it ceases, is called death. After death, every feverish affliction arises, and, on account of this feverish affliction, the entire mass of grief, sorrow, suffering, and affliction arises. This gathering through causes and conditions has no gatherer — it gathers of itself, spontaneously; and it has no one who brings it to cessation — it ceases of itself, spontaneously. This bodhisattva, according with this, contemplates in this way the gathering of causes and conditions.

Commentary: Here, "every distinction in the world among the places where beings receive a body and are born" means every distinction in birth and death across the five paths — this is called the distinction among all that exists in the world. This gathering through causes and conditions should be understood through three gates of contemplation: first, the distinction of accomplishing an answer to a difficulty; second, the distinction belonging to the truth of ultimate meaning; and third, the distinction belonging to worldly truth. What, then, is the distinction of accomplishing? First, this makes manifest that there is only the gathering of causes and conditions, explaining the meaning of no-self: it accomplishes the point that every distinction in the world among the places where beings receive a body and are born is entirely on account of clinging in craving to a self, and that, were one to depart from this clinging to self, there would be no place in the world where birth occurs — thus the meaning of no-self is accomplished. Were there, in ultimate meaning, truly a characteristic of self, then the mind that clings to self would itself be the wisdom of ultimate meaning, and it ought not to be born into any place in the world where a body is received. And further, were there, in ultimate meaning, truly a characteristic of self, then, were one to depart from clinging to self, one ought still to be constantly born into the world. This meaning is made manifest, as the sūtra says, "every distinction in the world among the places where beings receive a body and are born is entirely on account of clinging in craving to a self; were one to depart from this clinging to self, there would be no place in the world where birth occurs." What, then, is the distinction of answering? If there is truly no self, how can one cling to a self? Here there ought to be just this difficulty raised — and the sūtra itself answers it, saying that beings, blinded by ignorance, cling in craving to a self. This is made manifest, as the sūtra says, "the bodhisattva further gives rise to this thought: these ordinary beings, blinded by ignorance, cling in craving to a self." Given that there is truly no self, by what sequence does one come to cling in craving to a self, and thereby come to be born into a place in the world where a body is received? This too is made manifest, as the sūtra says, "in the darkness and obstruction of their lack of wisdom, they constantly seek after existence and non-existence" — and thus the distinction that answers the difficulty is made. Here, "lacking wisdom" and "existence and non-existence" mean seeking after permanence and seeking after annihilation; this makes manifest that ignorance and craving-for-existence are the very root of these two portions of continued existence — and thus, "forever following false thoughts, they wrongly practice a false path, gathering and giving rise to false conduct — conduct that is sinful, conduct that is meritorious, and conduct that is unmoving." "Forever following false thoughts" makes manifest the cause that is ignorance. "Wrongly practicing a false path" makes manifest an incorrect practice with regard to the place of liberation. "Gathering and giving rise to false conduct" makes manifest the bodhisattva's own supreme meaning, since, though a bodhisattva likewise engages in existence, this is not called false conduct. "Through this conduct, the seed of mind is aroused, since it is defiled and bound up with the notion of grasping" all the way to "according with this, he contemplates the gathering of causes and conditions" — here, "the seed of mind is aroused" makes manifest the very substance of birth, aging, and death. "Giving rise once more to future existence" means that, according with this, one takes hold of and accomplishes conduct that is sinful, meritorious, and the rest: "karma serves as the ground" — since, as already said, the darkness and obstruction of ignorance serve as the covering; "the constant seeking after existence and non-existence" — since the moisture of craving serves as the wetting; and thus one abides, and thus the mind is born: "I" and "what is mine," the notion of "I" and of "mine" being pride itself, "I am born" and "I am not born" — all such things are the net of manifold views. "It ceases of itself, spontaneously" means that it ceases through its own very nature, not through cessation brought about by wisdom's own causal power. In this way, the difficulty is answered and the gathering of causes and conditions is explained, and the meaning of no-self is thereby accomplished. What, then, is the distinction of characteristics? If there are no causes and conditions, then by what characteristic does the practice of the gathering of causes and conditions abide?

Sūtra: This bodhisattva further gives rise to this thought: failing to truly and really know the ultimate meaning of these truths is called ignorance; the karmic fruit produced by ignorance is called formations; in dependence on formations there is the initial mind of consciousness; arising together with consciousness there are the four aggregates received, which are called name-and-form; once name-and-form has grown, there are the six sense-bases; when the sense-faculty, its object, and consciousness — these three things — come together, contaminated contact is born; because contact arises together with it, there is feeling; because feeling becomes tainted through attachment, it is called craving; because craving grows, it is called grasping; from grasping there arises contaminated karma, which is called existence; because there is karma, there is a karmic fruit, which is called birth; when the aggregates of birth grow and then decline and change, this is called aging; once aging has occurred, the aggregates decay, and this is called death; at the moment of death's parting, when a foolish person, clinging in craving, feels a burning in the mind, this is called grief; when this breaks into a cry of weeping, this is called sorrow; when the five sense-faculties encounter their objects, this is called suffering; when the mind-faculty encounters its object, this is called distress; and when sorrow and suffering turn ever more numerous, this is called affliction. In this way there is nothing but a tree of suffering that grows, with no maker of it at all. The bodhisattva gives rise to this thought: were there a maker, there would be a deed done by that maker; and were there no maker, there would be no deed done — and, in ultimate meaning, there is neither any maker nor any deed done.

Commentary: Here, "the karmic fruit produced by ignorance" refers to name-and-form. Within this, consciousness is what name-and-form depends upon, since name-and-form arises together with consciousness, and consciousness and name-and-form depend upon one another in turn. If there is no maker, then within this the discrimination of any deed done is likewise absent — this explains the portion of existence within the gathering of causes and conditions. Since its own substance has no deed done within it, this is what should be understood as the sequential contemplation of the gathering of causes and conditions belonging to the portion of existence. What, then, is the distinction belonging to the truth of ultimate meaning? It is that, once this truth of ultimate meaning is thus realized, one thereby attains liberation from that very contemplation.

Reading note — after Fazang

On the dialectic behind "clinging to self is why beings are born, and departing from it would leave no place of birth," Fazang lays out both directions of the argument in parallel: 若我理有，著我順理，應得出世；既生世間，明我非理 — "were self real in principle, then clinging to self would accord with principle, and one ought thereby to escape the world; yet since one is born into the world, this makes manifest that self is not in accord with principle." And, reversed: 若理有我，離我違理，應生世間；既得出世，明理無我 — "were principle to contain a self, then departing from self would go against principle, and one ought thereby still to be born into the world; yet since escape from the world is attained, this makes manifest that principle itself is without self." Neither the fact of continued birth nor the fact of escape from it, on this reading, is compatible with a real self — each direction of the argument independently disproves it.

On why 無明 is named "ignorance" in the first place, Fazang traces the term to its most literal sense: 先釋癡相，謂著我故生、離我不生……無明闇者，以無慧明體是闇，故名為愚癡 — "first, the characteristic of delusion is explained: it is because one clings to self that one is born, and because one departs from self that one is not born... 'the darkness of ignorance' means that, being without the light of wisdom, its very substance is darkness — hence it is named ignorance." The name is not a moral judgment so much as an optical one: ignorance simply is the absence of the light by which the four truths could otherwise be truly known.

The Three Realms Are Mind-Only, and the Twelve Links Grounded in One Mind

Chinese Source

經曰：是菩薩作是念：三界虛妄，但是一心作。

論曰：但是一心作者，一切三界唯心轉故。云何世諦差別？隨順觀世諦即入第一義諦。此觀有六種：一、何者是染依止觀；二、因觀；三、攝過觀；四、護過觀；五、不厭厭觀；六、深觀。是中染依止觀者，因緣有分依止一心故。

經曰：如來所說十二因緣分皆依一心。所以者何？隨事貪欲共心生，即是識事即是行，行誑心故名無明，無明共心生名名色，名色增長名六入，六入分名觸，觸共生名受，受已無厭足名愛，愛攝不捨名取，此有分和合生有，有所起名生，生變熟名老，老壞名死。

論曰：此是二諦差別一心雜染和合因緣集觀。因觀者有二種：一、他因觀；二、自因觀。云何他因觀？

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: This bodhisattva gives rise to this thought: the three realms are deceptive and illusory — they are nothing but the making of one mind alone.

Commentary: "Being nothing but the making of one mind" means that the entire triple world turns on mind alone. What, then, is the distinction belonging to worldly truth? According with the contemplation of worldly truth, one thereby enters the truth of ultimate meaning. This contemplation is of six kinds: first, the contemplation of what defilement depends upon; second, the contemplation of causes; third, the contemplation that gathers in faults; fourth, the contemplation that guards against faults; fifth, the contemplation neither of weariness nor of its absence; and sixth, the deep contemplation. Here, "the contemplation of what defilement depends upon" means that the portion of existence within causes and conditions depends upon one mind.

Sūtra: The twelve links of dependent origination taught by the Tathāgata himself are all grounded in this one mind. Why is this so? In accordance with the matter at hand, craving and desire arise together with mind — this very matter is itself consciousness, and this very matter is itself formations; because formations deceive the mind, this is called ignorance; because ignorance arises together with mind, this is called name-and-form; once name-and-form has grown, this is called the six sense-bases; the portion belonging to the six sense-bases is called contact; because contact arises together with it, this is called feeling; once feeling is without any satisfaction, this is called craving; because craving grasps and does not let go, this is called grasping; this portion of existence, coming together, gives rise to existence itself; what existence gives rise to is called birth; when birth changes and ripens, this is called aging; and when aging decays, this is called death.

Commentary: This is the contemplation of the gathering of causes and conditions through the union of defilement with the one mind, distinguished according to the two truths. "The contemplation of causes" is of two kinds: first, the contemplation of causes belonging to another; and second, the contemplation of causes belonging to oneself. What, then, is the contemplation of causes belonging to another?

Reading note — after Fazang

Fazang pauses on this single line — "the three realms are illusory, being nothing but the making of one mind" — to flag its historical weight before commenting on its content at all: 此之一文諸論同引證成唯識 — "this one passage is what every treatise alike cites in common to establish the doctrine of Consciousness-Only." He then asks the question the line itself invites — 今此所說是何等心？云何名作？ — "just what kind of mind is being spoken of here? and what does it mean to say it 'creates'?" — before surveying, at length, how later Yogācāra treatises gloss the answer.

That survey runs to seven distinct positions, from the eight consciousnesses together with their mental factors and both seen and seeing aspects of experience, through to the full identification of the eighth, storehouse consciousness with the tathāgatagarbha itself. Fazang closes the fourth position, which reduces the seven active consciousnesses to functions of a single underlying mind, with a citation from the Laṅkāvatāra that this collection's own translation of that sūtra has already rendered: the ālaya, it says, is like an ocean whose waves are the many kinds of consciousness — 譬如巨海浪，無有若干相，諸識心如是，異亦不可得 — "just as the great ocean's waves have no separate character of their own apart from the ocean, so too are the mind and the various consciousnesses: no real difference between them can be found." One brief line in the treatise proper, on Fazang's reading, opens directly onto centuries of later argument over exactly what "mind" means when a sūtra says mind alone makes the three realms.

Part 29 Complete

This closes Part 29: the escalation of the Fifth Ground's three divisions into the Sixth Ground's own — the antidote to the pride that discriminates defiled dharmas from pure dharmas, the excellence of the practice of the non-abiding path, and the excellence of its fruit; Vajragarbha's ten kinds of equal dharma, structured by Fazang into eight that break existence through non-existence, one that breaks non-existence through existence, and one that breaks both together; the bodhisattva's contemplation, with great compassion foremost, of the world's differences in birth and rebirth destination across the five paths, traced back to clinging in craving to a self; his reflection that failing to truly know the Four Truths in their ultimate meaning is itself called ignorance, and the karmic fruit-body of name-and-form that this ignorance, in dependence on formations and consciousness, produces; and, closing this part, the declaration that the three realms are illusory, being nothing but the making of one mind — a line later treatises would cite in common to establish the doctrine of Consciousness-Only — together with the citation that the Tathāgata's own teaching of the twelve links of dependent origination is, in its entirety, grounded in this one mind. Part 30 continues within this same ground of worldly truth, turning to ignorance's own twofold activity and to each of the remaining eleven links traced through the same twofold form in turn, to the treatise's division of the twelve links into three unbroken portions — affliction, karma, and suffering — to the contemplation that guards against error, and to a three-time analysis of the links across the past, the present, and the future.
