The Lotus Sūtra

Why This Sūtra, and How This Guide Is Built

Every other Mahāyāna sūtra on this site describes a specific path — Sukhāvatī's forty-eight vows, Vimalakīrti's inconceivable liberation, the Diamond Sūtra's perfection of wisdom. This sūtra makes a claim about all of them at once: that the śrāvaka's path, the pratyekabuddha's path, and the bodhisattva's path were never three different destinations, only three provisional descriptions of the same one, offered because most listeners were not yet ready to hear the single truth stated directly. Everything from the burning house to the phantom city to the destitute son is this same claim, told again through a different image, for a different audience.

This translation is now complete. Kumārajīva's Lotus Sūtra runs to roughly 87,600 characters across twenty-eight chapters in seven fascicles — more than three times the length of the Immeasurable Life Sūtra, and the longest sūtra project on this site. The twenty-eight parts below cover all seven fascicles in full, following the tradition's own division of the text into two halves: the "trace gate" (迹門), fascicles one through most of five, chapters one through fourteen, arguing that the three vehicles were always skillful means for one; and the "origin gate" (本門), the remainder of fascicle five through fascicle seven, chapters fifteen through twenty-eight, disclosing who the Buddha actually is and how long he has actually been that — his true, inconceivably long lifespan, the bodhisattvas he has secretly taught since before recorded history, and the guardians, in the sūtra's own closing chapters, who vow to protect anyone who carries this teaching forward.

The teaching in one sentence

Every buddha appears in the world for one great cause alone — to open, show, help beings awaken to, and lead them into the buddha's own knowledge and vision — and the three vehicles taught for so long, to audiences not yet ready to hear this stated directly, were always skillful means pointing toward this one single truth, never a lesser or different one.

Zhiyi's "three rounds," and the trace gate's own argument

Zhiyi's commentary reads the sūtra's opening chapters as a single teaching delivered three separate times, calibrated to three levels of readiness: a direct doctrinal statement to the sharpest listeners (Śāriputra, in Parts 2–3), the same claim retold as the parable of a burning house to those who need an image before they can follow it (Parts 4–5), and, several chapters later, an ancient story reaching back over countless lifetimes for those who need a narrative before they can accept it at all (Parts 8–9). Reading these three tellings against one another — not just each in isolation — is, on Zhiyi's own account, the point of how the trace gate is built.

The origin gate: from a provoked doubt to a promise

The second half of the sūtra has its own shape, and it is worth naming before reading it. Part 16 ends on a deliberately unresolved doubt — how could the Buddha have personally trained the vast assembly rising from beneath the earth, in only the forty-odd years since his awakening at Gayā? Part 17 answers it, disclosing that his birth, awakening, and eventual parinirvāṇa were all skillful means, and that his true lifespan is one this sūtra itself declines to number. Everything after that — the purified six senses of Parts 20–21, Never-Disparaging's bow in Part 22, Bhaiṣajyarāja's self-immolation in Part 24, Avalokiteśvara's universal gate in Part 26 — works out what it means to carry a teaching this large forward, closing with Samantabhadra's own vow, in the sūtra's final chapter, to personally appear before any future reader who takes it seriously enough to hold onto it.

Two notes on contested passages

Not every chapter in the received Lotus Sūtra came from Kumārajīva's own hand at the same time. Part 13, on Devadatta and the dragon king's daughter, was not part of Kumārajīva's original twenty-seven-chapter translation — Jizang, writing in the early seventh century, records that it was translated separately from a manuscript found in Khotan and only inserted into the Lotus Sūtra decades after Kumārajīva's death. That same chapter contains this sūtra's most direct passage on gender and buddhahood; the reading note there presents the classical commentarial position and the tension a reader today will likely feel against it side by side, without resolving the question in either direction. Part 24 raises a comparable tension of its own: its account of a bodhisattva's self-immolation has a documented history of being read, at times, as literal precedent by later practitioners, and that page presents Zhiyi's emptiness-contemplation reading alongside that later reception without erasing either.

How to actually use this text

  1. Read Parts 2 through 5 as one unit. The direct statement and the burning-house parable are two tellings of a single argument; reading them back to back, rather than treating the parable as a separate story, is closer to how the sūtra itself presents them.
  2. Don't skip the ancient story in Parts 8–9 for its length. It is the longest sustained narrative in the trace gate, and it exists to answer a specific question: not just why the three vehicles were taught, but how far back this present assembly's own connection to the teaching actually reaches.
  3. Read Parts 16 and 17 together, in one sitting if possible. Part 16 ends deliberately unresolved — a doubt raised, not answered — and Part 17 is that doubt's own answer; splitting them across separate reading sessions works against how the sūtra itself paces its central disclosure.
  4. Read Parts 13 and 24 with their reading notes, not around them. These are the two chapters on this site where the reading notes are doing as much work as the translation — the textual-history and gender notes on Part 13, and the self-immolation note on Part 24, are load-bearing, not optional context.

Key Terms

一佛乘 — the one buddha vehicle: the sūtra's central claim, that the śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and bodhisattva vehicles were always this one vehicle, taught as three only as skillful means.
方便 — skillful means: read by Zhiyi, in its deepest sense for this sūtra's title, not as a lesser truth simplified for weak minds, but as the truth itself, worn in a form not yet recognized as such — the parable of the pearl sewn into a poor man's robe, in Part 10, states this claim in the sūtra's own words.
十如是 — the ten suchnesses: the sūtra's own list of how all phenomena are known, in Part 2 — and the site of some of the densest exegesis in the entire Tiantai tradition, including the "three transpositions" reading laid over the same nine characters from outside the text.
三周說法 — the three rounds of teaching: Zhiyi's scheme for how this sūtra delivers its one claim three times, to three levels of readiness — see "Zhiyi's three rounds" above.
開三顯一 — opening the three to reveal the one: the technical shorthand, used throughout the commentarial tradition, for this sūtra's central doctrinal move.
授記 — prediction: the Buddha's formal confirmation, to a specific disciple by name, of a future buddhahood complete with a name, a land, a kalpa, and a lifespan — first given to Śāriputra in Part 4, and repeated, with variations, through most of the chapters that follow it.
化城 — the phantom city: a guide's provisional rest-stop, conjured and later dissolved, standing for the two lesser nirvāṇas taught as a resting place along a road whose real destination lies further on — Part 9.
多寶佛塔 — the stūpa of Prabhūtaratna: an entire long-extinct buddha's stūpa, rising bodily from the earth to confirm this sūtra's truth wherever it is taught — Part 12.
如來室・如來衣・如來座 — the Tathāgata's room, robe, and seat: great compassion, gentleness and patience, and the emptiness of all things — the sūtra's own three-phrase discipline for anyone who teaches it after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, first named in Part 11, and recurring, in slightly different form, in Parts 15, 22, 23, 25, and 28.
迹門・本門 — the trace gate and the origin gate: Zhiyi's division of the sūtra's twenty-eight chapters into two halves — Parts 1–14 opening the three vehicles to reveal the one, and Parts 15–28 opening the Buddha's own historical "trace" to disclose the far older "origin" beneath it.
久遠實成 — awakened since a time immeasurably long past: the origin gate's central claim, disclosed in Part 17, that the Buddha's birth and awakening at Gayā were themselves skillful means concealing a lifespan this sūtra declines to number.

All Twenty-Eight Parts

All seven fascicles, complete — chapters 序品第一 through 普賢菩薩勸發品第二十八. Each part has the Chinese–English text side by side and reading notes drawn from Zhiyi's commentary. Read in order; the argument builds cumulatively, and later parts assume the earlier ones.

Part 1 · 序品第一

The Assembly and the Six Signs

Vulture Peak, the Buddha's wordless samādhi, six auspicious signs, and Mañjuśrī's account of an ancient buddha who once taught this very sūtra under circumstances identical to this one.

卷一 · T262 行 83–424 Part 2 · 方便品第二（上）

Skillful Means: The Buddha's Wisdom

A wisdom praised as "hard to understand and hard to enter," the ten suchnesses, five thousand who cannot accept it and walk out, and the declaration of the one great cause and the one buddha vehicle.

卷一 · T262 行 427–621 Part 3 · 方便品第二（下）

The Buddha's Hesitation, and the Turning of the Wheel

The verse restatement of skillful means, the merit of a child's game of sand, the Buddha's own account of nearly declining to teach at all, and the first turning of the wheel at Vārāṇasī.

卷一 · T262 行 622–962 Part 4 · 譬喻品第三（上）

The Parable of the Burning House

Śāriputra's confession of years of self-doubt, his prediction of future buddhahood, and a father who lures his children from a burning house with three carts, then gives them all one greater cart instead.

卷二 · T262 行 970–1249 Part 5 · 譬喻品第三（下）

The Burning House, Retold in Verse

The parable retold with vivid new detail, the warning that arhatship was not yet true extinction, and a stark passage on exactly who this sūtra should — and should not — be taught to.

卷二 · T262 行 1250–1516 Part 6 · 信解品第四

The Parable of the Destitute Son

Four elder disciples answer the burning house with a parable of their own: a son who flees his wealthy father, labors two decades unrecognized in his own father's house, and is gradually led to his inheritance.

卷二 · T262 行 1517–1754 Part 7 · 藥草喻品第五・授記品第六

The Medicinal Herbs, and Four Predictions

A single rain nourishing plants of every size according to their own nature, and predictions of future buddhahood for Mahākāśyapa, Subhūti, Mahākātyāyana, and Mahāmaudgalyāyana.

卷三 · T262 行 1761–2035 Part 8 · 化城喻品第七（上）

Sixteen Buddhas from One Teacher

An inconceivably ancient buddha, his sixteen sons who become novices and teach this same sūtra themselves, and their revelation as sixteen present buddhas — among them Amitābha, and Śākyamuni himself.

卷三 · T262 行 2036–2359 Part 9 · 化城喻品第七（下）

The Parable of the Phantom City

A guide conjures a walled city for exhausted travelers to rest in, then lets it vanish — and Śākyamuni's disclosure that he himself once taught this very assembly, in a lifetime beyond any of their memories.

卷三 · T262 行 2360–2516 Part 10 · 五百弟子受記品第八・授學無學人記品第九

The Jeweled Robe, and the Priceless Pearl

Pūrṇa revealed as a bodhisattva who secretly played a śrāvaka's role, a priceless pearl sewn unknowingly into a poor man's robe, and predictions for Ānanda, Rāhula, and two thousand more.

卷四 · T262 行 2523–2818 Part 11 · 法師品第十

The Teacher of the Dharma

The merit extended to anyone who hears even a single verse of this sūtra, the stūpa marking wherever it is kept, and the room, robe, and seat that anyone teaching it after the Buddha must enter, wear, and sit in.

卷四 · T262 行 2819–3002 Part 12 · 見寶塔品第十一

The Jeweled Stūpa

An entire buddha's stūpa erupts from the earth to bear witness to this sūtra, and a verse insisting that every conceivable feat of cosmic strength is easier than simply teaching it in a difficult age.

卷四 · T262 行 3003–3179 Part 13 · 提婆達多品第十二

Devadatta

A chapter with its own contested textual history, in which the Buddha's own rival is revealed as a former teacher and future buddha, and an eight-year-old dragon princess attains buddhahood in an instant.

卷四 · T262 行 3180–3293 Part 14 · 勸持品第十三

Those Who Uphold This Sūtra

Waves of vows to teach this sūtra in a hostile future age, predictions for the Buddha's foster mother and the mother of his son, and eighty myriad koṭis of bodhisattvas volunteering, unasked, to endure persecution.

卷四 · T262 行 3294–3389 Part 15 · 安樂行品第十四

The Four Peaceful Practices

Mañjuśrī asks how an ordinary bodhisattva can safely teach this sūtra in a hostile age, and the Buddha answers with four disciplines, closing with a king who rewards every soldier richly but keeps one jewel hidden until the end.

卷五 · T262 行 3396–3642 Part 16 · 從地踊出品第十五

Bodhisattvas from Beneath the Earth

The Buddha declines other-land bodhisattvas' offer to protect this sūtra, and the earth splits open as innumerable bodhisattvas he has secretly trained rise up, provoking a doubt this chapter deliberately leaves unresolved.

卷五 · T262 行 3643–3872 Part 17 · 如來壽量品第十六

The Tathāgata's Lifespan

The doctrinal center of the entire sūtra: the Buddha discloses that his birth, awakening, and eventual parinirvāṇa are skillful means, and that he has been awakened for a span of time this sūtra declines to number.

卷五 · T262 行 3873–4027 Part 18 · 分別功德品第十七

The Merit of Believing

The assembly advances through the bodhisattva path simply from hearing the Buddha's true lifespan, and the Buddha declares that one thought of faith outweighs eighty myriad koṭis of kalpas of near-perfect practice.

卷五 · T262 行 4028–4268 Part 19 · 隨喜功德品第十八

The Merit of Rejoicing

A single moment of gladness, fiftieth in a chain of secondhand retelling, is set against a donor who satisfies an entire universe's beings and leads them all to arhatship — and still exceeds it.

卷六 · T262 行 4275–4378 Part 20 · 法師功德品第十九（上）

The Purified Six Senses (I)

Anyone who upholds this sūtra gains a purified eye, ear, and nose — the same ordinary body a person was born with, functioning at a scope this chapter describes faculty by faculty, in exacting detail.

卷六 · T262 行 4379–4567 Part 21 · 法師功德品第十九（下）

The Purified Six Senses (II)

The catalog concludes with the tongue, body, and mind — every taste turned to ambrosia, the whole universe reflected silently in the body, and a mind that penetrates a single verse's meaning without error.

卷六 · T262 行 4568–4676 Part 22 · 常不輕菩薩品第二十

Never Disparaging

A bodhisattva monk who reads no scripture at all, but bows to everyone he meets and tells them they will become buddhas, is mocked and beaten for it for years — and is revealed to have been Śākyamuni himself.

卷六 · T262 行 4677–4775 Part 23 · 如來神力品第二十一・囑累品第二十二

The Tathāgata's Power, and the Final Charge

Ten great miraculous signs entrust this sūtra to the bodhisattvas risen from the earth, and the Buddha places his own hand on the head of every bodhisattva present before the visiting buddhas return home.

卷六 · T262 行 4776–4884 Part 24 · 藥王菩薩本事品第二十三

Bhaiṣajyarāja's Former Life

A bodhisattva offers his own body in flames as homage to a buddha's teaching — one of this sūtra's most extreme and most historically contested chapters, presented with its classical reading and later reception held openly together.

卷六 · T262 行 4885–5029 Part 25 · 妙音菩薩品第二十四

Gadgadasvara, the Bodhisattva of Wonderful Voice

A bodhisattva from a distant, unblemished world travels here to hear this sūtra, warned not to look down on what he finds, and is revealed to take on thirty-four forms wherever beings need him.

卷七 · T262 行 5036–5137 Part 26 · 觀世音菩薩普門品第二十五

Avalokiteśvara's Universal Gate

The sūtra's most widely known chapter on its own: release from fire, flood, and every peril for anyone who calls this bodhisattva's name, and thirty-three forms taken on to teach whoever needs reaching.

卷七 · T262 行 5161–5315 Part 27 · 陀羅尼品第二十六・妙莊嚴王本事品第二十七

Guardian Spells, and a King Won Back

Five guardians offer protective spells for teachers of this sūtra, and a king devoted to a rival teaching is won back by his own wife and two sons — three figures revealed as bodhisattvas already met earlier in the sūtra.

卷七 · T262 行 5316–5480 Part 28 · 普賢菩薩勸發品第二十八

Samantabhadra's Encouragement

The sūtra's final chapter: Samantabhadra vows to personally appear before anyone who upholds this sūtra in ages to come, and the entire assembly — present since Part 1 — rejoices, bows, and departs.

卷七 · T262 行 5481–5566

About the Source

The Chinese is Kumārajīva's translation (鳩摩羅什, Taishō T262, 406 CE), the version that became standard throughout East Asian Buddhism and the founding text of the Tiantai school. The English translation was made for Ru-Yi Meditation Center directly from this Chinese, without reference to any existing English translation, following the same conventions as this site's other sūtra pages.

Two notes on textual history and content

Part 13, on Devadatta, was not part of Kumārajīva's original translation. Jizang's early-7th-century commentary records that Kumārajīva's Lotus Sūtra had only twenty-seven chapters; the Devadatta material was separately translated from a Khotanese manuscript decades later — first as a standalone text in 490 CE, then folded into the Lotus Sūtra itself only near the end of the Liang dynasty. This site's translation includes the chapter as it has stood in the received text for many centuries, with this history noted plainly at the point where the chapter begins rather than left unstated.

Two short passages in the received text, both later additions rather than part of Kumārajīva's own translation, are omitted from this guide: an imperial preface prefacing the Universal Gate chapter (between Parts 25 and 26 in T262's line numbering), and Sengrui's translator's postface closing the received text after the sūtra's own final chapter. Neither is sūtra content in the first place, and both are addressed at the point in this guide where they are skipped rather than left unmentioned.

Reference, not a source translated

The reading notes draw primarily on Zhiyi's (智顗, 538–597) 妙法蓮華經文句, the foundational Tiantai commentary on this sūtra, along with his 妙法蓮華經玄義 for several passages this site's notes attribute to Zhanran's transmission of Zhiyi's teaching. Where their readings genuinely diverge from Zhiyi's, this guide also draws on Jizang's (吉藏, 549–623) 法華義疏 — the same commentator consulted throughout this site's Amitābha and Immeasurable Life Sūtra pages — and on Kuiji's (窺基, 632–682) 妙法蓮華經玄贊, particularly for the doctrinal position underlying Part 13's most contested passage. All three commentators' lifetimes predate the seventh century by a comfortable margin, placing them well within the public domain.

The commentaries are listed here as references consulted, not reproduced. Each "Reading note — after Zhiyi" is Ru-Yi's own English synthesis of the commentarial tradition's key points, images, and distinctions for that passage — not a translation of any commentary in full.
