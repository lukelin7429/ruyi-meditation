Gathering In All Six at Once

Thirteen more disciples testify how they each reached perfect penetration: six through the very consciousnesses that discriminate sense-objects, and seven through the great elements themselves — Ucchuṣma turning the fire of his own lust into the fire of wisdom, Dharaṇiṃdhara learning that a sword passing through his own body is no different from passing through empty space, Candraprabha dissolving his own body into a room of water, Maitreya realizing that every buddha-land, pure or defiled, is nothing but his own mind's transformation. The fascicle closes with Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own teaching: needing no special method at all beyond simply gathering in all six sense-faculties together, through the single, unbroken continuity of recollecting the Buddha's own name.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 13 carried the first eleven of twenty-five testimonies to perfect penetration — six through the sense-objects, five through the sense-faculties. This fascicle carries the next thirteen: six disciples testifying through the very consciousness that arises between each faculty and its object (Śāriputra through the eye, Samantabhadra through the ear, Sundarananda through the nose, Pūrṇa through the tongue, Upāli through the body, and Mahāmaudgalyāyana through the mind), and then seven bodhisattvas testifying through the seven great elements themselves — the classical four elements of fire, earth, water, and wind, expanded to include space, consciousness, and, last, the sense-faculties taken together as a single "great element" of their own. Ucchuṣma turns the very fire of his own past lust into the fire of wisdom; Dharaṇiṃdhara, who spent lifetimes leveling roads and carrying earth for travelers, learns from a buddha to level the very ground of his own mind instead, until a blade passing through his body meets no more resistance than a blade passing through empty space; Candraprabha dissolves his own body so completely into water that a student, peering into his meditation room, sees nothing but a room full of water and tosses in a stone out of curiosity; Ratnaprabha watches the whole world, and his own body, turned by nothing but a single, deluded wind; Ākāśagarbha's own body becomes identical with space itself; Maitreya comes to see that every buddha-land, pure or defiled, is nothing but a transformation manifested by his own mind. The fascicle closes with the longest and most detailed testimony of the whole set: Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own teaching on the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha, illustrated by the image of a mother and child who miss each other despite living side by side unless both, together, hold each other in mind — and Yuanying's own extended exposition of what it actually means to recite the Buddha's name with body, speech, and mind gathered into one.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. The companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 5 carries a condensed, English-only survey of all twenty-five testimonies, without the underlying Chinese alongside it; this jiangyi fascicle's own quotations of the full canonical text for each of these thirteen testimonies, considerably fuller than that survey, are translated fresh here, aligned to that project's own established English names for each disciple. All of Yuanying's own commentary is new.

Six Testimonies Through the Consciousnesses 寅三 六識圓通

Śāriputra: Eye-Consciousness 卯初 鶖子眼識

Chinese Source

〖經〗舍利弗，即從座起，頂禮佛足，而白佛言：我曠劫來，心見清淨，如是受生，如恆河沙，世出世間，種種變化，一見則通，獲無障礙。我於中路，逢迦葉波，兄弟相逐，宣說因緣，悟心無際。從佛出家，見覺明圓，得大無畏，成阿羅漢，為佛長子，從佛口生，從法化生。佛問圓通，如我所證，心見發光，光極知見，斯為第一。

〖講〗舍利弗，此云鶖子，起座頂禮白佛；我曠劫來，心見清淨：心見即眼識，不染色塵曰清淨。如是指眼識清淨。受生如恆河沙劫之久，世出世間，凡聖染淨諸法，六凡法界，善惡攸分，苦樂不等，四聖法界，大小差殊，權實有異，故曰種種變化。一見則通者：祗要眼識一見，隨念分別，則能通達，瞭然明白，獲無障礙，不必意識，計度分別，此顯多劫，眼識明利也。

舍利弗，初事沙然梵志。沙然歿後，無師可事，一日於中路，逢三迦葉波，兄弟相逐，宣說因緣深義，偈云：「因緣所生法，我說即是空；亦名為假名，亦名中道義。」此四句，即大乘因緣，天台宗，藏、通、別、圓四教，即依此偈而立，四句依次立四教。鶖子一聞，便悟圓理，圓理即是藏心，故曰悟心；周遍法界，故曰無際。

有云舍利弗，路逢馬勝比丘，威儀庠序，即趨問汝師是誰？答曰：我師釋迦牟尼。又問：汝師授汝何法，可得聞乎？答曰：我初入道，祗記得因緣之法：諸法從緣生，諸法從緣滅，我佛大沙門，常作如是說。舍利聞已，即得初果。歸告目連亦悟，二人率領徒眾，投佛出家，今云逢迦葉波者，或同時所遇，非獨一人，彼此互出耳。

既聞勝法，乃知師亦非常。遂從佛出家，見覺亦眼識也。嚮者眼識，雖得無礙，尚未明圓，今則更承佛誨，識精明圓，得大自在，具四無畏，為接樂小之儔，權取四果，成阿羅漢；欲伏邪外之眾，示居智魁，為佛長子，乃以智德居長也。

從佛口生，從法化生者：非說色身，乃指法身，從佛聞法，而得悟證法身，如從佛口生也。在教法中，常承法乳，長養之力，是從法化生也。

此結答圓通。如我所修所證，心見發光，即由眼識，而成無障礙智，光即智也。智光極處，徹佛知見，斯眼識最為第一。他經云：「鶖子過去，已成金龍如來，乃不捨大悲，倒駕慈航。」則知七日通達佛法，半月得證聖果，皆示現耳。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Śāriputra rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "Through boundless kalpas, my own mind-seeing has been pure. Receiving birth again and again, as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, I came to see every kind of transformation, mundane or transcendent, at a single glance, penetrating it completely, free of every obstruction. Along the road, I encountered the Kāśyapa brothers, following one after another, and, hearing them proclaim the deep meaning of causation, I awakened to a mind without any boundary at all. I then left the household life to follow the Buddha, this same seeing now made bright and perfectly complete, attaining great fearlessness, and became an arhat, the Buddha's own eldest son, born from the Buddha's own mouth, born through the Dharma's own transformation. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have realized it, mind-seeing giving off its own light, this light reaching all the way to knowledge and vision itself, is foremost."

Commentary: Śāriputra's name means "son of Śārī," his mother's own name. Rising from his seat, he bows and addresses the Buddha: through boundless kalpas, my own mind-seeing has been pure — "mind-seeing" here is exactly the eye-consciousness itself; unstained by form as a sense-object is called "pure." "In this way" points back to this same purity of the eye-consciousness. Receiving birth across kalpas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, mundane and transcendent dharmas alike — the six ordinary dharma-realms, where good and evil divide and suffering and happiness differ; the four sagely dharma-realms, where great and small differ and the provisional and the real diverge — hence, "every kind of transformation." "At a single glance, penetrating it completely": simply through the eye-consciousness's own single glance, discriminating instantly, moment by moment, he was able to penetrate through completely, everything plainly understood, free of every obstruction, with no need at all for the calculating, discriminating work of mental consciousness — this shows how sharp and keen his own eye-consciousness had grown across so many kalpas.

Śāriputra had, at first, served the wandering ascetic Sañjaya. After Sañjaya's death, with no teacher left to serve, he encountered, one day along the road, the three Kāśyapa brothers, following one after another, proclaiming the deep meaning of causation, in this verse: "whatever dharma is born of causes and conditions, this I declare to be empty; it is also called a provisional name; it is also called the meaning of the middle way." These four lines are exactly the greater vehicle's own teaching of causation — the Tiantai school's fourfold division into the tripiṭaka, shared, distinct, and perfect teachings is established directly upon this same verse, each of its four lines establishing one of these four teachings in turn. The moment Śāriputra heard this, he awakened at once to the perfect principle itself — this perfect principle being exactly the mind of the tathāgatagarbha, hence "awakened to a mind"; and, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, hence "without any boundary."

Some accounts say that Śāriputra instead encountered the bhikṣu Aśvajit along the road, his own bearing so composed and dignified that Śāriputra hurried over to ask him: "who is your teacher?" He answered: "my teacher is Śākyamuni." Śāriputra asked further: "what teaching has your teacher given you — might I hear it?" He answered: "I am new to the path myself, and remember only this teaching on causation: every dharma arises from conditions, and every dharma ceases from conditions; this is what our own great teacher, the Buddha, constantly teaches." Upon hearing this, Śāriputra attained the first fruit at once, and, returning to tell Maudgalyāyana, brought about his awakening as well — and the two of them, leading their own disciples together, took refuge in the Buddha and left the household life. The present account, speaking instead of encountering the Kāśyapa brothers, may simply describe an encounter that happened around this same time, not necessarily a single person alone — the two accounts filling each other out.

Having heard this superior teaching, he realized that this teacher, too, must be extraordinary, and so left the household life to follow the Buddha — "seeing" here, once again, being exactly the eye-consciousness itself. His own eye-consciousness, though already free of obstruction before, had not yet become bright and perfectly complete; now, receiving the Buddha's own further instruction, this consciousness's own essence grew bright and perfectly complete, attaining great freedom, complete with the four kinds of fearlessness — and, in order to receive those who delight in the lesser teaching, he provisionally took up the fourth fruit and became an arhat; and, wishing to subdue the multitudes given to wrong views and outside teachings, he showed himself foremost in wisdom, the Buddha's own eldest son — that is, foremost in the very virtue of wisdom itself.

"Born from the Buddha's own mouth, born through the Dharma's own transformation": this does not speak of the physical body, but rather of the dharma-body — hearing the Dharma from the Buddha and thereby awakening to and realizing the dharma-body is exactly like being born from the Buddha's own mouth; and, within this teaching, constantly nourished by the milk of the Dharma, growing and maturing through its own power, is exactly being born through the Dharma's own transformation.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: what he himself cultivated and realized was exactly this — mind-seeing giving off its own light, that is, the eye-consciousness becoming an unobstructed wisdom — "light" here being exactly wisdom itself. At the very furthest reach of this light of wisdom, penetrating all the way through to the Buddha's own knowledge and vision — it is exactly this eye-consciousness, then, that he holds as foremost. As another sūtra puts it: "Śāriputra, in a past age, had already become the Tathāgata Golden Dragon — yet, unwilling to set aside his own great compassion, turned his own compassionate ship back around once more." One thus knows that awakening to the whole of the Buddha-dharma within seven days, and realizing the fruit of sagehood within half a month, were both, alike, nothing but a manifestation freely chosen.

Samantabhadra: Ear-Consciousness 卯二 普賢耳識

Chinese Source

〖經〗普賢菩薩，即從座起。頂禮佛足，而白佛言：我已曾與恆沙如來，為法王子，十方如來，教其弟子菩薩根者，修普賢行，從我立名。世尊！我用心聞，分別眾生，所有知見，若於他方恆沙界外，有一眾生，心中發明普賢行者，我於爾時，乘六牙象，分身百千，皆至其處，縱彼障深，未得見我，我與其人，暗中摩頂，擁護安慰，令其成就。佛問圓通，我說本因，心聞發明，分別自在，斯為第一。

〖講〗此自述遠因。普賢菩薩：行彌法界曰普，位鄰極聖曰賢。起座頂禮白佛言：我已曾與恆沙如來，為法王子。此顯事佛之多，恆沙如來，悉皆承事，無空過者，故得證位之深，以為法王真子，位當等覺，可以承紹法王家業也。《行願品》云：「一切如來有長子，彼名號曰普賢尊，」

十方諸佛如來，教其弟子，有大乘菩薩根器者，即圓頓一類之機，修普賢行，即大乘法界行，十大願王是也。此行從我立名者有二意：一、令行人有所傚法；二、令普賢有所加被也。

世尊我用心聞：心聞即耳識。惟用耳識，隨念分別，不假五俱意，及獨頭意識，計度分別，便能分別眾生，所有知見。然眾生知見不一，有邪、正、大、小、權、實，種種差別，但用心聞，即能了知。若於他方，恆河沙界之外，極言其處之遠。有一眾生，心中發明，普賢行者：先悟毘盧性海，次入普賢行門，雖然在遠不遺，如十方世界，有無量眾生，發明普賢行者，我於爾時，乘六牙象，分身百千，皆至其處；此菩薩利生念切，及時而應。準《法華》象是白象，表清白梵行之相，六牙表六度，乘此為因，而到如來果地也。

分身百千：極言其多，非定數也。有無量眾生，發明普賢行，菩薩即現無量身，而遍應之，此不思議之力用也。一身不分而普現，群機咸應以無違，如一月當空，影臨眾水，縱彼惑重障深，能障遇聖善根，我與其人，暗中摩頂，消其惑業，除其障難，冥中擁護，令不退轉。如無障者，現身安慰，令其增進。成就、雙指有障無障，皆得成就，普賢廣大行願心也。

此結答圓通。我上來所說，最初本因，但用耳識，隨念分別。能發智慧光明，普照群機，得大自在，現前諸行，莫不資始乎此，故曰斯為第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Samantabhadra Bodhisattva rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "I have already served as a true son of the Dharma-King to Tathāgatas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges; and the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, teaching those disciples of theirs who possess the capacity for the bodhisattva vehicle, have them cultivate the practice of Samantabhadra, taking this very name of mine for this practice. World-Honored One, I make use of the mind's own hearing to discern whatever knowledge and views sentient beings may hold. If, beyond world-systems as distant as the sands of the Ganges, a single sentient being gives rise, within their own mind, to the practice of Samantabhadra, I, at that very moment, riding my six-tusked elephant, divide my body a hundred thousandfold, and go at once to that very place — and even should that being's own obstruction run too deep for them to actually see me, I nonetheless touch their crown in hidden fashion, protecting and comforting them, so that they may bring this practice to completion. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have described my own original cause, the mind's own hearing giving rise to understanding, discerning freely and without hindrance, is foremost."

Commentary: This recounts his own distant cause. Samantabhadra Bodhisattva: practice pervading the whole Dharma-realm is called "universal"; a rank bordering on the ultimate sagehood is called "worthy." Rising from his seat, he bows and addresses the Buddha: I have already served as a true son of the Dharma-King to Tathāgatas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges. This shows how many buddhas he has served, Tathāgatas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, every one of them served without a single one ever passed over — and it is exactly this that has brought him to so deep a stage of realization, a true son of the Dharma-King, his own rank that of equal enlightenment, fit to inherit the Dharma-King's own household estate. As the Chapter on Vows and Conduct puts it: "every Tathāgata has an eldest son, and his name is the Venerable Samantabhadra."

The Buddhas of the ten directions, teaching those of their own disciples who possess the capacity for the bodhisattva vehicle of the greater teaching — that is, this same class of sudden and perfect capacity — have them cultivate the practice of Samantabhadra, that is, the practice of the greater vehicle's own Dharma-realm, the ten great kingly vows themselves. That this practice takes its name from me carries two distinct senses: first, so that practitioners might have a model to follow; and second, so that Samantabhadra himself might have occasion to bestow his own blessing upon them.

"World-Honored One, I make use of the mind's own hearing": "the mind's own hearing" is exactly the ear-consciousness itself. Relying solely on the ear-consciousness, discriminating instantly, moment by moment, without recourse to the five consciousnesses' accompanying mental consciousness, or to independently operating mental consciousness, calculating and discriminating — he is thereby able to discern whatever knowledge and views sentient beings hold. Yet sentient beings' own knowledge and views are by no means uniform — deviant or correct, great or small, provisional or real, every kind of difference — and yet, simply through the mind's own hearing, he is able to know them all completely. "Beyond world-systems as distant as the sands of the Ganges" speaks, in the most emphatic terms, of how far away such a place might be. "A single sentient being... gives rise, within their own mind, to the practice of Samantabhadra": first awakening to Vairocana's own ocean of nature, and then entering the very gate of Samantabhadra's own practice — even at so great a distance, nothing is ever overlooked; and so, throughout the worlds of the ten directions, wherever boundless sentient beings give rise to this same practice of Samantabhadra, I, at that very moment, riding my six-tusked elephant, divide my body a hundred thousandfold, and go at once to every such place — this bodhisattva's own urgent intention to benefit beings answering them at the very instant needed. According to the Lotus Sūtra, this elephant is white, standing for the mark of pure, brahma-like conduct; its six tusks stand for the six perfections, riding upon which, as cause, one arrives at the Tathāgata's own fruit-stage.

"Divide my body a hundred thousandfold" speaks, in the most emphatic terms, of how many — not a fixed number at all. Wherever there are boundless sentient beings giving rise to this practice of Samantabhadra, the bodhisattva manifests boundless bodies at once, answering every one of them everywhere — this being an inconceivable power and function indeed. A single body, undivided, and yet manifesting universally; every capacity, alike, answered without exception — exactly like a single moon in the sky, its reflection falling upon every body of water at once. Even should that being's own delusion run heavy and their own obstruction deep, obstructing their own wholesome root of encountering a sage, I nonetheless touch their crown in hidden fashion, dissolving their own delusion and karma, removing their own obstruction and difficulty, protecting them invisibly so that they never regress; and for those without such obstruction, I manifest my own body directly, comforting them, so that they may advance further still. "Bring this practice to completion" points to both cases at once, obstructed or not — both alike brought, in the end, to completion in this same vast mind of Samantabhadra's own vows and practice.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: everything I have described above, this very original cause of mine, relies on nothing but the ear-consciousness, discriminating instantly, moment by moment. Capable of giving rise to the bright light of wisdom, illuminating every capacity everywhere, attaining great freedom — every practice now before us draws its own beginning from exactly this — hence, "is foremost."

Sundarananda: Nose-Consciousness 卯三 難陀鼻識

Chinese Source

〖經〗孫陀羅難陀，即從座起，頂禮佛足，而白佛言：我初出家，從佛入道，雖具戒律，於三摩地，心常散動，未獲無漏。世尊教我，及拘絺羅，觀鼻端白。我初諦觀，經三七日，見鼻中氣，出入如煙，身心內明，圓洞世界，遍成虛淨，猶如琉璃，煙相漸銷，鼻息成白。心開漏盡，諸出入息，化為光明，照十方界，得阿羅漢，世尊記我，當得菩提。佛問圓通，我以銷息，息久發明，明圓滅漏，斯為第一。

〖講〗孫陀羅，此云豔，是妻名，難陀是本名，此云喜，連妻為名曰豔喜。因佛會下，難陀多人，加豔以別之。是佛胞弟，具三十相，短佛四指，身亦金色，若入眾中，有不識者，謂是佛來。起座頂禮白佛言：我初出世俗家，從佛而入出世道。雖具戒律：言於戒雖然無缺，於定尚未時入，故曰於三摩地，心常散動。散者心攝不起，動者心定不下，因無定不能發慧，無慧不能斷惑，故未獲無漏。

世尊教我：乃因機施教。及拘絺羅，此云大膝，是舍利弗母舅，同為散心之機。故佛教觀鼻端白，兩目注視，鼻尖微有白相，令其注心一處，收攝散動也。

我初秉教諦觀，經三七日，見鼻中氣，出入如煙：當知此氣，由風火而起，鼓煩惱濁，故其狀如煙，昧者不覺。惟諦觀能見，視微若著。煙相漸銷，鼻息成白兩句：應接如煙下，觀之既久，煩惱垂盡，煙相漸銷，出入鼻息，竟成白相。身心內明者：身心即鼻識，以肉鼻同身，內明即定成之相，定功既成，便能發慧，明即智慧光明，煩惱既銷，慧光迭發。圓滿洞徹，依報世界，遍成虛淨。虛則無礙，淨則無染，皆慧光遍鎔所致，故得內明外虛，猶如琉璃，內外明徹。

首句藏心開顯，諸漏俱盡，諸出入氣息，皆化為智慧光明，普照十方世界，得成大阿羅漢，此轉煩惱成菩提之先聲。故世尊記我，當得菩提，即當來成佛也。

此結答圓通，不取嗅、聞、鼻識，而取觀息之功。我以銷息者；始而觀鼻攝心，見氣如煙；繼而煙相漸銷，鼻息成白，故曰銷息。銷息乃銷如煙之氣，而成白淨之息也。息久發明者：銷息既久，定力愈深，並息亦銷，諸出入息，稱性發化，成為智慧光明。明圓滅漏者：光明圓照十方，銷滅諸漏，不僅見思，並滅塵沙無明，惟斯鼻觀，最為第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Sundarananda rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "When I first left the household life, entering the path under the Buddha, though I kept the precepts fully, my mind, with regard to samādhi, remained constantly scattered and restless, and I had not yet attained freedom from outflows. The World-Honored One taught me, together with Kauṣṭhila, to contemplate the very tip of the nose, white and still. I contemplated this closely at first, and, after three full weeks, saw the breath within my nose, moving out and in like smoke; body and mind grew bright within, this brightness perfectly penetrating the whole world, entirely empty and pure, exactly like lapis lazuli. This smoke-like appearance gradually dissolved away, and the breath through my nose turned entirely white. My mind then opened, my outflows exhausted, and every breath, moving out and in, transformed into light itself, illuminating the ten directions — and I attained arhatship, the World-Honored One confirming that I would, in time, attain bodhi. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have realized it, dissolving the breath, until, over time, understanding dawns, this understanding perfectly complete and every outflow extinguished, is foremost."

Commentary: Sundarananda's name means "beautiful joy" — "beautiful" being the name of his own wife, and "Nanda," his own original name, meaning "joy," the two joined together into this single name. Because there were several disciples named Nanda in the Buddha's own assembly, "beautiful" is added here to distinguish him from the others. He was the Buddha's own younger half-brother, complete with all thirty of the major marks, only four finger-widths shorter than the Buddha himself, his own body likewise the color of gold — so much so that, entering the assembly, those who did not know him would sometimes mistake him for the Buddha himself. Rising from his seat, he bows and addresses the Buddha: when I first left my own worldly household, I entered, under the Buddha, this path that leads beyond the world. "Though I kept the precepts fully": this means that, with regard to the precepts, nothing was lacking — yet, with regard to samādhi, he had not yet properly entered it at all, hence, "my mind, with regard to samādhi, remained constantly scattered and restless." "Scattered" means the mind, ungathered, would not settle; "restless" means the mind, lacking samādhi, could not come to rest — and, without samādhi, wisdom cannot arise, and, without wisdom, delusion cannot be severed — hence, "had not yet attained freedom from outflows."

"The World-Honored One taught me": this is teaching matched to his own particular capacity. "Together with Kauṣṭhila" — meaning "great knee" — Śāriputra's own maternal uncle, who shared this same scattered capacity of mind. And so the Buddha taught them to contemplate the very tip of the nose, white and still — both eyes fixed steadily, a faint whiteness visible at the very tip of the nose — having them focus the mind on this single point, so as to gather in this scattered restlessness.

"I contemplated this closely at first, and, after three full weeks, saw the breath within my nose, moving out and in like smoke": one should understand that this breath arises through wind and fire together, stirring up the very turbidity of affliction — hence its appearance like smoke, something the confused fail to notice at all, visible only to one contemplating closely enough to see even the most subtle thing as though it were plainly present. "This smoke-like appearance gradually dissolved away, and the breath through my nose turned entirely white": following on from "like smoke" above, after contemplating for a long while, affliction nearing its own exhaustion, this smoke-like appearance gradually dissolved away, and the breath moving out and in through the nose came, at last, to take on this entirely white appearance. "Body and mind grew bright within": "body and mind" here is exactly the nose-consciousness itself — the physical nose being one with the body. "Bright within" is exactly the appearance samādhi itself brings about; once the power of samādhi is accomplished, wisdom can then arise — "bright" here being exactly the bright light of wisdom itself; affliction now dissolved, this light of wisdom flares forth again and again. Perfectly complete and utterly penetrating, the circumstantial world of his own retribution became, throughout, empty and pure — "empty" meaning unobstructed, "pure" meaning undefiled, both brought about entirely through this same light of wisdom melting everything within its reach — and so he attained this brightness within and emptiness without, exactly like lapis lazuli, bright and penetrating both within and without at once.

The first line here is the tathāgatagarbha's own mind opening and becoming manifest, every outflow entirely exhausted; every breath, moving out and in, transformed into the very light of wisdom, illuminating the worlds of the ten directions everywhere at once, and he thereby attained the great fruit of arhatship — this being the very first sign of affliction itself turning into bodhi. Hence the World-Honored One confirmed that I would, in time, attain bodhi — that is, attain buddhahood itself, in an age yet to come.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: he does not take up smelling, or the sense of smell, or the nose-consciousness in general, but rather the specific achievement of contemplating the breath. "Dissolving the breath": at first, contemplating the nose to gather in the mind, he saw this breath appear like smoke; and then, this smoke-like appearance gradually dissolving away, the breath through his nose turned entirely white — hence, "dissolving the breath," that is, dissolving this smoke-like breath until it became a pure, white breath instead. "Until, over time, understanding dawns": as this dissolving of the breath continued on, the power of his own samādhi growing ever deeper, even the breath itself dissolved away entirely, and every breath, moving out and in, gave rise, according to its own true nature, to this transformation, becoming the very light of wisdom. "This understanding perfectly complete and every outflow extinguished": this light, perfectly illuminating the ten directions, extinguished every outflow — not merely the afflictions of views and thought, but ignorance as numerous as motes of dust and sand as well — and so it is exactly this contemplation through the nose that he holds as foremost.

Eloquence itself divides into four kinds: first, unobstructed eloquence regarding the teaching itself, capable of expounding every mundane and transcendent dharma, every single name and mark, with nothing left unknown; second, unobstructed eloquence regarding meaning, capable of expounding the particular meaning of every dharma, penetrating through without hindrance; third, unobstructed eloquence regarding expression, capable of holding boundless, subtle meaning within a single phrase, gathering the broadest meaning into the space of only a few words; and fourth, unobstructed eloquence in teaching joyfully, according with whatever sentient beings themselves delight in, skillfully and expediently, in teaching the Dharma to them.

Understanding that true emptiness poses no obstruction to wondrous existence, and wondrous existence poses no obstruction to true emptiness either, he is able to teach every dharma, provisional or real, exactly as each occasion requires.

Our own founding teacher, the World-Honored One, knowing that I possessed this great eloquence — truly, no one knows a disciple better than his own teacher — taught and cultivated me according to this very talent. "Through the very wheel of sound itself": that is, the wheel of the mouth, teaching the Dharma — likewise exactly the wheel of the Dharma itself. "Taught me to develop it further still": taught me to observe each capacity and develop my teaching accordingly, taking the greatest care never to misapply an impure method where a furnace and bellows were needed, nor to teach breath-counting to one who belonged, instead, among the charnel-ground contemplations. "Before the Buddha, I helped him turn this wheel": helping to spread the Buddha's own transforming influence, turning the wheel of the Dharma in his stead. "Through this lion's roar of mine": that is, teaching the Dharma fearlessly. Once a lion roars, every other beast submits at once; once this wondrous Dharma is proclaimed, the deviant and those outside the teaching alike return and are transformed. Through this very tongue-consciousness, aiding the Buddha's own transforming influence above and liberating sentient beings below, he attained arhatship — the World-Honored One confirming that, among those who teach the Dharma, none surpasses him.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: through the tongue-consciousness, teaching the Dharma fearlessly, that is, through the very sound of the Dharma, subduing every demon throughout the three realms, the thieving enemies of the five aggregates, extinguishing every fetter of affliction, together with every outflow — it is exactly this tongue-consciousness, then, that he holds as foremost.

Sūtra: Upāli rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "I personally followed the Buddha over the palace wall when he left the household life; personally witnessed the Tathāgata's own six years of arduous practice; personally saw the Tathāgata subdue every demon and bring every outside teaching under control, freeing himself entirely from every worldly outflow of craving and desire. I am the very backbone and discipline of the Tathāgata's own assembly. Confirmed directly by the Buddha as pure in mind, upholding the precepts and cultivating the body, I am held up by the whole assembly as foremost. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have realized it, disciplining the body until the body itself gains freedom, and then, in due sequence, disciplining the mind until the mind, too, gains understanding, so that body and mind together become entirely unhindered — this is foremost."

Commentary: Upāli's name means "foremost" — for his own strict observance of the pure precepts made him the very backbone and discipline of the whole assembly. Rising from his seat, he bows and addresses the Buddha: "I personally followed the Buddha over the palace wall when he left the household life": according to tradition, when the Buddha was still a prince, Upāli served as an attendant official within the eastern palace; and when the Buddha turned his back on his own father and left the household life, Upāli, too, found himself derelict in his own duty, unable to remain comfortably in his own position, with no further office left to fill — and so he followed the Buddha and left the household life as well. He was not, however, dispatched by the king himself, and so does not number among the original five bhikṣus.

"Personally witnessed the Tathāgata's own six years of arduous practice": after the prince shaved his own head and beard, he had Chandaka carry his own jeweled crown and robes back to the palace, to report to his royal father and mother that they need not worry over him. King Śuddhodana then dispatched five officials, together with Chandaka, to beseech the prince to return to his own kingdom — but the prince declared: "until I accomplish the buddha-path, I will not return to my own homeland" — and so these five accompanied the prince instead. As the Sūtra of the Extensive Play recounts, they journeyed to Gayāśīrṣa Mountain, into a forest of ascetic practice, where the gods offered him hemp and rice, a single grain eaten each day. These five officials, together with Upāli, practiced alongside the prince — yet, one after another, the five eventually left, while Upāli alone never once departed, hence "personally witnessed the Tathāgata's own six years of arduous practice."

"Personally saw the Tathāgata subdue every demon and bring every outside teaching under control": the Buddha, practicing austerities in the snowy mountains, could, in principle, have attained the path there — yet, fearing this might mislead sentient beings into taking mere suffering itself for the path, he chose not to attain the path there instead. He then went to sit beneath the bodhi tree, vowing: "until I accomplish the buddha-path, I will not rise from this seat." After forty-eight days, Māra, the king of demons, dreamed of thirty-two ill omens, and, waking in terror, observed that a bodhisattva sitting beneath a tree in Jambudvīpa was about to attain complete awakening. He commanded his four demon-daughters to disrupt this meditative concentration, manifesting thirty-two seductive forms — yet the bodhisattva's own body and mind remained utterly still and unmoved, causing these daughters to see, in themselves, their own nine orifices, their own two internal organs, their own eighty-four thousand infested pores, filthy and impure, and they fled, retching, in disgust. Māra, enraged, personally led his own demon army, wishing to do him harm, yet could accomplish nothing at all — this, too, Upāli witnessed with his own eyes, hence "personally saw."

"Brought every outside teaching under control": this refers to the period after the Buddha's own attainment of the path. Before the Buddha appeared in the world, India knew only the outside teaching of Brahmanism; once the Buddha attained the path, many came to place their faith in him instead, and the outside teachers, consumed with jealousy, repeatedly sought to harm him — as when Sundarī tried to poison him with drugged rice, yet the Buddha, reflecting that his own mind held no poison within it, was unaffected by the poison and ate the food in perfect calm; or when a drunken elephant was set loose to trample him, and the Buddha, extending his own five fingers, manifested five lions, causing the drunken elephant to collapse to the ground, unmoving. He also liberated Uruvilvā-Kāśyapa and others — all of this Upāli personally witnessed as well, this bringing of every outside teaching under control.

"Freeing myself entirely from every worldly outflow of craving and desire": here Upāli recounts, in his own words, the very basis for his own awakening and realization — through personally witnessing this arduous practice, and personally seeing every demon subdued and every outside teaching brought under control, he came to believe in the benefit of transcending the world, and to grow weary of the world's own impermanence, understanding deeply that craving and desire are the very root of every outflow — and, understanding this so deeply, he naturally ceased to cling to them at all, hence "freeing myself."

This recounts his own receiving the teaching and attaining its fruit. "Receiving the Buddha's own precepts": that is, being given the two hundred and fifty precepts of a bhikṣu, the lesser vehicle's own precepts. "I advanced through them" means progressing by degrees, up to the greater vehicle's own bodhisattva precepts. "The three thousand dignified deportments": with the four postures of walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, each carrying two hundred and fifty precepts, this makes a thousand; matched against the three collections of pure precepts, each of these thousand becomes three thousand in turn. "The eighty-four thousand subtle practices": matching these three thousand deportments against the seven aspects of bodily and verbal conduct produces twenty-one thousand, and matching this further against greed, anger, delusion, and their combination produces, altogether, eighty-four thousand subtle practices.

"Both the precepts against what is inherently evil and those against what merely leads toward evil": a precept whose own substance is, in itself, evil, requiring no further prohibition to make it so — violating it directly constitutes karma, as with killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech — is called a precept against what is inherently evil. A precept whose own substance is not, in itself, evil, yet which, if permitted, might lead one toward evil, and which the Buddha therefore prohibits as a precaution — as with drinking alcohol, eating pungent foods, or breaking ground — is called a precept against what merely leads toward evil. Through this, he was able to uphold the precepts of both the greater and lesser vehicles alike, entirely pure, without a single violation.

"Body and mind alike reached utter stillness, and I attained arhatship": the lesser vehicle disciplines the body, hence the body-consciousness reaching utter stillness; the greater vehicle disciplines the mind as well, hence mental consciousness dissolving away in turn. This is exactly samādhi arising from precepts, and, once the power of samādhi is accomplished, the light of wisdom then arises, attaining the wisdom of the emptiness of self, and thereby the path of arhatship.

"I am the very backbone and discipline of the Tathāgata's own assembly": I am the very head of the Vinaya within the Tathāgata's own community, capable, before the whole assembly, of setting its own discipline and order through the precepts themselves. Governing is called "backbone"; maintaining is called "discipline" — the Buddha having me investigate every observance and violation, judging their relative severity, governing the whole sangha and maintaining the Vinaya school itself — hence, "I am the very backbone and discipline of the assembly."

"Confirmed directly by the Buddha as pure in mind": this speaks of the greater vehicle's own precept disciplining the mind — this being my own hidden practice, a total, thoughtless holding of every precept at once, graced by the Buddha's own direct confirmation that my own mental precepts are pure. The lesser vehicle's own study of disciplining the body, upholding the precepts and cultivating the body, is my own outwardly manifested trace, graced by the Buddha holding me up, before the whole assembly, as unsurpassed. 我有神力三句：上負物屬利人，此拔苦兼利物，由積劫苦行，福報所感，故得神力。車牛或指牛車，或車與牛，被污泥之所陷溺，人牛俱受苦惱；我有神力，為其推輪，拔除苦惱。

交光法師云：夫平地之行，志在普利，效力之行，不檢親疏，其心亦久平矣，何至此而方蒙平心之教乎？蓋前屬事相平心，未能悟理，今令悟知，內心外地，本惟一體，故惟平心，不分情、器，則境隨心轉，安有不平，較前豈不天淵乎？時、即國王請佛設齋之時；延、即請也。我在爾時，知佛必由此路，而至王宮，王既為國修福，屈九五之尊，而迓萬德之聖，我則平地待佛，以表敬佛之意，此待佛，即所以待教也。

毘舍如來摩頂者，愍其事行久勞，慈悲攝受也。謂我當平心地，則世界地，一切皆平：此佛欲令以理融事，故謂之曰，當平心地，以心為諸法之本，地由心造，內心既平，外相斯夷，滄桑變易，常自如如，故曰則世界地，一切皆平。淨名經云：「欲得淨土，當淨其心，隨其心淨，則佛土淨。」換言之，欲平大地，當平心地，隨其心平，則世界地平。毘舍、金粟二佛之言，若合符節。

前脩二行，屬權漸，此則心開，入圓頓。心開者：即藏心開發顯現，始知藏心隨緣成事，心歧則千差競起；此心返妄歸真，心平則法界坦然，了達內色外色，地大平等。到此則超過聲聞之智，非但不執內四大為我，亦復洞明外四大唯心矣！見自身中，地大微塵，與能造世界，所有微塵，唯是藏心平等，無有差別。

微塵自性，不相觸摩者：微塵，指內外地大色法，自性，即如來藏性心法，色、心不二，性色真空，內身外界，所有微塵，悉皆自性，不相抵觸，不相擦摩，如空合空，似水投水，不相妨礙。乃至外地大之刀兵，與內地大之身根，以刀觸身，亦無所觸傷，如空合空，似水投水也。持地所脩，具四法界：初脩平地效力，事相之行，屬事法界；蒙平心教，心平地平，以理奪事，屬理法界；心開見塵，色心不礙，屬理事無礙法界；刀兵觸身，亦無所傷，屬事事無礙法界。肇法師曰：「將頭臨白刃，猶若斬春風。」彼但證性無傷，如六祖延頸，剌客三揮利刃，俱如斬影，此則到事事無礙矣！

《指掌》云：身界二塵，該一切法，彼既同一自性，則一切法，皆同一自性，誰為能生？誰為所生？由此於一切法，悟無生理，忍信不疑。按悟處巳齊八地，但以伏斷分齊，分盡見思，故云成阿羅漢。悟大證小其故何也？以所知障輕，故得悟大，煩惱障重，故但證小。由其悟處既深，不住淺位，故迴小乘心，今入大乘菩薩位中。

聞諸如來：當指賢劫中四佛，非獨指釋迦。以前毘舍浮佛時，悟明圓理，迴小入大，故聞諸如來，宣說妙蓮華，佛知見地，先為證明。妙蓮華，即指本經，是名妙蓮華，金剛王寶覺；佛知見：即根中不生滅性，一乘寂滅場地，為諸佛之因地心，依此進修，可獲果地覺，我先證明，率眾依修，而為上首。

此結答圓通。我因諦觀，身界二地大，內塵外塵，平等無差。此由蒙平心教，藏心開顯，以無分別智，諦審觀察，相妄性真，故曰本如來藏。所有地大，無非藏性隨緣，循業發現而已；既屬循業，虛妄顯現，故曰虛妄發塵。今既悟全相即性，萬法唯心，則塵相銷除，智光圓滿，能成無上覺道，以斯地大進修，最為第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Dharaṇiṃdhara Bodhisattva rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "I recall how, in a past age, when the Tathāgata Universal Light appeared in the world, I was a bhikṣu, constantly leveling and filling every dangerous, narrow stretch along every major road, every river crossing, every field where travel was difficult, wherever the way was unfit for travel and might harm a cart or injure a horse — sometimes building a bridge, sometimes carrying sand and earth myself. I continued this same arduous work through the appearance of countless further buddhas in the world. Whenever I saw someone in the marketplace needing help carrying a load, I would carry it for them first, all the way to wherever they were headed, setting it down and departing at once, without ever accepting any payment. In the age of the buddha Viśvabhū, the world suffered from widespread famine, and I served as a porter for hire, near or far without distinction, accepting only a single coin — and whenever I came upon an ox-cart stuck fast in the mud, I used my own spiritual power to help push the wheel free, delivering them from their own suffering. At that time, the great king of that land invited the Buddha to a feast, and I, knowing his path, went ahead to level the ground for him — and the Tathāgata Viśvabhū, touching my own crown, told me: 'you should level the very ground of your own mind — for once this is done, the ground of the whole world will, of itself, become level as well.' My own mind then opened at once, and I saw that the fine dust composing my own body and the fine dust that composes the whole created world are, without any difference at all, exactly the same — this fine dust's own nature never touching or rubbing against itself, even a blade passing through it meeting no resistance at all. I then awakened, with regard to the nature of dharmas themselves, to the patient acceptance of the unborn, and attained arhatship; turning my mind toward the greater vehicle, I now stand among the ranks of the bodhisattvas, and, hearing the Tathāgatas proclaim this wondrous lotus flower, this very ground of the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, I was the very first to confirm and bear witness to it, and so stand foremost among them. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have realized it, contemplating closely how the fine dust of my own body and the fine dust of the whole world are, without any difference at all, originally nothing but the tathāgatagarbha itself, falsely giving rise to the appearance of dust — and, this dust dissolved away, wisdom now perfectly complete, accomplishing the unsurpassed path — is foremost."

Commentary: Because of his own practice of leveling roads, and, later, receiving the teaching of leveling the mind, cultivating and realizing according to this same teaching, he is named Dharaṇiṃdhara, "holder of the earth." Rising from his seat, he bows and addresses the Buddha: this recounts his own distant cause. "I recall how, in a past age, the Tathāgata Universal Light appeared in the world": this buddha, named "Universal Light," his own bodily light and light of wisdom illuminating the whole Dharma-realm everywhere at once, is foremost among the fifty-three buddhas named in this sūtra. Under this very buddha, I resolved to leave the household life and become a bhikṣu, diligently cultivating ascetic practice, constantly working along every major road necessarily traveled, every river crossing where water and land transportation met, every field where the terrain grew dangerous and narrow — "dangerous" meaning uneven, rising and falling steeply; "narrow" meaning cramped, without room to spare. Wherever the way was unfit for travel, inconvenient to pass along, certain to harm a cart or injure a horse — a cart at risk of a broken axle, a horse at risk of losing its footing — I would always level and fill it in myself, flattening what rose too high, filling what sank too low, sometimes building a bridge to ease a river crossing, sometimes carrying sand and earth myself to repair a major road.

The first line here points back to leveling and filling the road, building bridges, and carrying sand and earth, all forms of diligent, arduous labor. "Through the appearance of countless further buddhas in the world": across so long a span of time, constantly practicing without ever regressing — this closes his account of leveling roads; what follows now recounts his own labor offered in service of others. "Whenever I saw someone" refers to the elderly or the weak, lacking their own strength — a marketplace enclosure is called huán, its gate is called huì — in the midst of commerce and trade, whenever someone needed help carrying a load, I would carry it for them first, all the way to wherever they were headed, setting it down and departing at once, making no distinction between kin or stranger, noble or humble, and never once accepting any payment at all — "payment" meaning a laborer's own fee — not only never demanding it, but even refusing it when the owner of the goods offered it himself. This offering of his own labor as a form of practice, giving of his own bodily strength, fully embodies all three kinds of giving within the perfection of generosity: first, the giving of wealth — the body itself being an inner form of wealth, given here through bodily labor; second, the giving of the Dharma — widely forming wholesome connections, thereby moving and transforming others; and third, the giving of fearlessness — others, recognizing his own goodness, giving rise to no fear at all in his presence. And in never accepting payment, he gives of outer wealth as well.

The Sanskrit Viśvabhū means "pervading, self-mastering everywhere." This buddha, the very last of the adorned aeon, appeared at a time when, through the shared karma of sentient beings, the world suffered from widespread famine — that is, years of scarcity and want, the five grains failing to ripen, food scarce, and begging for alms difficult. I served as a porter for hire, carrying loads for others, near or far without distinction, treating every face, familiar or unfamiliar, with the very same regard, accepting only a single coin's worth of payment, taking just enough to sustain my own life, never craving more than this.

"Whenever I came upon an ox-cart stuck fast in the mud": the famine mentioned earlier could take either of two forms — drought, or flood. Here, "stuck fast in the mud" would correspond instead to a flood following prolonged rain. And yet, if the earlier famine was in fact a drought, the ground would necessarily have been parched — how, then, could there be mud at all? As the common saying goes: after a long dry spell, a long rain inevitably follows — the ground, baked dry during the drought, turns to mud once the rains finally come; and so this ox-cart stuck fast in the mud must belong to the period following the drought's own end.

These three lines, "I used my own spiritual power": carrying loads for others above was a benefit given to people directly; this, delivering them from suffering, benefits both people and animals together — and it was through kalpas of accumulated ascetic practice, the blessed reward this brought about, that he attained this very spiritual power. "Ox-cart" may refer either to a cart drawn by an ox, or to the cart and the ox together, both stuck fast in the muddy mire, person and ox alike suffering together — and I, with my own spiritual power, helped push the wheel free for them, delivering them from their own suffering.

Master Jiaoguang observes: surely this practice of leveling roads was already undertaken with the intention of benefiting everyone equally, and this labor offered in service of others already made no distinction between kin and stranger — his own mind, then, must already have been level and even for a very long time already — why, then, did he need to wait until this moment to receive the very teaching of leveling the mind at all? The answer is this: what came before belonged to leveling the mind only with regard to outward phenomena, without yet awakening to the underlying principle itself; now, he is made to awaken to the fact that the inner mind and the outer ground are, in truth, one and the same substance — and so, leveling the mind alone, without dividing sentient being from material world at all, causes every circumstance to turn according to the mind itself — how, then, could it fail to become level? Is this not worlds apart from what came before? "At that time" refers to the very moment the king invited the Buddha to a feast; "invited" means exactly this. I, at that time, knowing that the Buddha would necessarily travel this same road to reach the royal palace, and the king, cultivating blessings for his own kingdom, humbling his own supreme, royal dignity to welcome this sage of ten thousand virtues — I, for my part, went ahead to level the ground and await the Buddha there, so as to express my own reverence for the Buddha in this way — and this awaiting of the Buddha was, in fact, exactly an awaiting of his own teaching as well.

The Tathāgata Viśvabhū touching my own crown was an act of compassionate acceptance, taking pity on my own long labor at this very practice. "Telling me I should level the very ground of my own mind — for once this is done, the ground of the whole world will, of itself, become level as well": this buddha wished to have him interfuse principle with phenomena, and so told him: you should level the very ground of your own mind — since mind is the very root of every dharma, and the ground itself is created by the mind; once the inner mind is level, the outer appearance becomes level as well, and though the world may shift and change like the sea turning into mulberry fields, this same mind remains, throughout, exactly as it is — hence, "the ground of the whole world will, of itself, become level as well." As the Vimalakīrti Sūtra puts it: "if you wish to attain a pure land, you must purify your own mind — for as the mind becomes pure, so too does the buddha-land become pure." In other words: if you wish to level the great earth itself, you must level the very ground of your own mind — for as the mind becomes level, so too does the ground of the world become level. The words of the buddha Viśvabhū here, and of the buddha Suvarṇapuṣpa elsewhere, agree with one another as precisely as two halves of the same tally.

The two practices cultivated before belonged to the provisional, gradual teaching; this present opening of the mind now enters the sudden and perfect teaching instead. "My own mind then opened" is exactly the tathāgatagarbha's own mind opening and becoming manifest — first coming to know that this same mind of the tathāgatagarbha, following conditions, brings phenomena into being, so that, wherever the mind diverges, a thousand differences compete to arise at once; now, this mind turning falseness back into truth, once the mind itself is level, the whole Dharma-realm becomes calm and even, fully penetrating both the form within and the form without, the great element of earth equal throughout. Reaching this point, he has already surpassed the śrāvaka's own wisdom — not only no longer clinging to the four elements within as a self, but fully penetrating, as well, to the fact that the four elements without are likewise nothing but mind itself! He saw that the fine dust of the great element of earth within his own body, and the fine dust composing the whole created world, are, being nothing but this same tathāgatagarbha's own mind, equal throughout, with no difference between them at all.

"This fine dust's own nature never touching or rubbing against itself": "fine dust" here refers to the material dharma of the great element of earth, both within and without; "own nature" is exactly the mental dharma of the tathāgatagarbha's own nature — form and mind not two different things at all. The nature of form is true emptiness itself, and so every particle of fine dust, whether within the body or throughout the outer world, belongs, in every case, to this very nature, never touching or rubbing against one another at all — exactly like space combining with space, or water poured into water, posing no obstruction to one another whatsoever. Even the great element of earth without, in the form of a blade, meeting the great element of earth within, in the form of the body-faculty — a blade touching the body — causes no injury at all, exactly like space combining with space, or water poured into water. Dharaṇiṃdhara's own cultivation encompasses all four dharma-realms at once: his first practice of leveling roads and offering labor in service of others, a practice of outward phenomena, belongs to the dharma-realm of phenomena alone; receiving the teaching of leveling the mind, mind and ground alike made level, principle now overriding phenomena, belongs to the dharma-realm of principle; his mind opening and seeing this fine dust, form and mind no longer obstructing one another, belongs to the dharma-realm of the unobstructed interpenetration of principle and phenomena; and a blade touching the body causing no injury at all belongs to the dharma-realm of the unobstructed interpenetration of phenomena with one another. As Master Sengzhao put it: "bringing one's own head before the naked blade is no different from cutting through the spring breeze itself" — though he speaks only of realizing that the nature itself takes no injury, exactly as when the Sixth Patriarch stretched out his own neck, and an assassin's blade, swung three times over, fell each time as though cutting through a mere shadow — this reaches, indeed, all the way to the unobstructed interpenetration of phenomena with one another!

The Zhǐzhǎng commentary observes: the two kinds of fine dust, of the body and of the world, encompass every dharma there is — and since these, too, share this very same single nature, then every dharma alike shares this same single nature as well — who, then, could be said to give rise to anything, and what, in turn, could be said to be given rise to at all? And it is from this that he awakened, with regard to every dharma whatsoever, to this principle of the unborn, accepting and believing it without the slightest doubt. According to this account, his own point of awakening already equals the eighth ground — yet, measured only by the degree to which delusion has actually been subdued and severed, having severed only the afflictions of views and thought, he is accordingly said to have "attained arhatship." Why should his own awakening reach so deep a stage, while his actual realization remains so much shallower? Because his own obstruction to what may be known runs light, he is able to awaken so deeply; yet because his own obstruction of affliction still runs heavy, his actual realization remains only this much. And because his own point of awakening runs so deep, he does not remain fixed at this shallower stage, but instead turns his own lesser-vehicle mind toward the greater, now standing among the ranks of the bodhisattvas.

"Hearing the Tathāgatas": this should refer to the four buddhas of the present fortunate aeon already appeared, not to Śākyamuni alone. Since, already in the age of the buddha Viśvabhū, he had awakened to this same perfect principle and turned his lesser-vehicle mind toward the greater, he thus heard the Tathāgatas proclaim this wondrous lotus flower, this very ground of the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, and was the very first to confirm and bear witness to it. "This wondrous lotus flower" refers to this very sūtra itself — "this is called the wondrous lotus flower, the vajra king's precious awareness," as the verse puts it; "the Buddha's own knowledge and vision" is exactly this unborn, unceasing nature within the sense-faculty itself, the very site of this one vehicle's own utter stillness, the causal-stage mind of every buddha alike — relying on this to advance in cultivation, one may attain the very awakening of the fruit-stage itself. He was the very first to confirm and bear witness to this, leading the assembly in cultivating according to it, and so stands foremost among them.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: through contemplating closely how the two kinds of fine dust, of the great element of earth within the body and throughout the world, are equal throughout, without any difference at all — this coming about through receiving the teaching of leveling the mind, the tathāgatagarbha's own mind opening and becoming manifest, examining and observing closely with this very wisdom free of discrimination, that every appearance is false while every nature is true — hence, "originally nothing but the tathāgatagarbha itself." Every instance of the great element of earth is, in every case, nothing but this same nature of the tathāgatagarbha manifesting according to conditions, appearing in accordance with karma alone — and, belonging to this very appearing in accordance with karma, it manifests falsely — hence, "falsely giving rise to the appearance of dust." Now, having awakened to the fact that every appearance is, in its entirety, identical with the nature itself, every dharma nothing but mind alone, the appearance of dust dissolves away entirely, the light of wisdom now perfectly complete, capable of accomplishing the unsurpassed path of awakening itself — advancing in cultivation through exactly this great element of earth, then, he holds it as foremost.

Candraprabha: A Room Full of Water 卯三 月光水大

Chinese Source

〖經〗月光童子，即從座起，頂禮佛足，而白佛言：我憶往昔，恆河沙劫，有佛出世，名為水天。教諸菩薩，脩習水觀，入三摩地。觀於身中，水性無奪：初從涕唾，如是窮盡，津液精血，大小便利，身中旋復，水性一同。見水身中，與世界外，浮幢王剎，諸香水海，等無差別。我於是時，初成此觀，但見其水，未得無身。當為比丘，室中安禪。我有弟子，窺窗觀室，惟見清水，遍在室中，了無所見。童稚無知，取一瓦礫，投於水內，激水作聲，顧盼而去。我出定後，頓覺心痛，如舍利弗，遭違害鬼。我自思惟：今我已得阿羅漢道，久離病緣，云何今日，忽生心痛，將無退失？爾時童子，捷來我前，說如上事。我即告言：汝更見水，可即開門，入此水中，除去瓦礫。童子奉教，後入定時，還復見水，瓦礫宛然，開門除出我後出定，身質如初。逢無量佛。如是至於山海自在通王如來，方得亡身，與十方界，諸香水海，性合真空，無二無別。今於如來，得童真名，預菩薩會。佛問圓通，我以水性，一味流通，得無生忍，圓滿菩提，斯為第一。

〖講〗童子乃童真入道，月光具童真德，故得是名。起座頂禮白佛：我回憶往昔，恆河沙劫，有佛出世，名為水天：此佛因脩水觀，得悟性天，乃三十五佛之一。教諸菩薩者：乃為一類之機，非專為月光一人也。脩習水觀：以水為觀行，觀成得入三摩地。

此依觀久修。上八句觀內水，下五句觀外水，先觀自身中水性，無相傾奪，此為總標；下釋無奪之義。初從涕唾：在鼻曰涕，在口曰唾，此水在外易見者；在嚥下嚥曰津，在喉外溢曰液，此水近內可覺者；在骨髓曰精，在筋肉曰血，此水幽隱難知者。飲食茹退，曰大小便利。其中津液，水之清相，餘皆水之濁相，在一身之中，旋環往復，清濁雖異，水性一同，性既相同，故無傾奪。

見水身中者：見水在一身之中，旋環往復，內觀既熟，引令擴充，觀世界水。《乳峰摘脈》云：按華藏莊嚴世界海，名普光摩尼香水海，海中有一大蓮華，名種種光明芷香幢，華中有十佛剎，微塵數香水海。一一海中，各有一剎種；每一剎種，皆有二十重佛剎；累高如幢，為佛剎之王，故曰浮幢王剎。諸香水海，即指總別諸香水海。觀身內水，與世界外香水海水，雖有遠近大小，水性亦復一同，平等無有差別。又同者通也，內水外水互通，不相留礙也。

此則依教脩習。是時即脩習時，能觀之智，與所觀之境，初得相應，是為初成。此觀即水觀，觀行成就，入觀之時，不見其人，但見其水，即以水為身，尚未得無相，不能無身。

此舉事以證，尚為身累。當時我為比丘，在靜室之中，安心禪觀，即脩水觀。我有弟子，乃巾瓶執侍之人，因見久在室中，遂乃窺其窗縫，觀其室中，以探情形，唯見滿室清水，了無他物所見，此即心境相應，觀水現水，是謂定果色，定境現前也。但定果色，尋常入觀之時，唯自見之，此月光水觀，與烏芻火觀，皆能令他人見者，實觀力殊勝也。

後梁漢州綿竹縣，水觀和尚，時寇作亂，欲入綿竹。水觀和尚，即作水觀觀綿竹縣，全縣皆水，寇至惟見汪洋澤國，不見縣境，全縣人民，皆免於難，可證此義。

童子幼稚，無有知識，心懷疑慮：一、疑此室何故有水，遍滿其中？二、疑師在室中安禪，何以不見其人？不知此水是水非水，乃取一瓦礫，投於水中，激水作聲。激是激動，水既作聲，信知是水，乃顧視盼望而去。我出定後，忽然覺著心痛，就如舍利弗，遭違害鬼之事。舍利弗於耆闍崛山，入定之時，有二鬼從虛空過，一名違害，一名復害。復害謂違害言：我欲以拳打此沙門。違害勸云：勿興此意。復害不聽，違害即去。復害以拳，便打舍利弗之頭，出定後頓覺頭痛。白世尊言：體素無患，今何頭痛？佛告之曰：有伽羅鬼，手打汝頭，彼鬼大力，打須彌山，便成二分，汝若無定，身應粉碎，此鬼受報，身已陷入阿鼻地獄矣！今云違害者，乃同時所遇，錯舉其名耳。

我自心思量惟忖，疑慮莫釋：今我已得阿羅漢道，久離病緣，應當無有現業，云何今日，忽生心痛之病？將無退失句，且作二解：一、心痛之病，出定將無退失？二、阿羅漢，久離病緣，今得心痛之病，將無退失果位耶？《正脈》云：羅漢有病無病，當明子、果二縛。夫宿種今種，應召來果，而尚未受身者，謂之子縛；今已受身，即身應受，謂之果縛；若實行新證四果，已將子縛見思二惑斷盡，不受後有，不要再來受生，然現身尚未灰滅，則果縛猶存，所有病苦，即身應受，故舍利頭痛，畢陵足痛，皆斯類也。

捷來者：月光出定，童子速來我前，說如上事。我則料知，心痛必是瓦礫投水之故。遂告童子言：汝更見水，可即開門，入此水中，除去瓦礫。童子奉教，後月光復至室中入定，童子還復窺見，水中瓦礫宛然，開門除出。我後出定，身質體也如初無恙。自覺心痛者，未得亡身之故也。

逢佛無量，經劫必久，至於山海自在通王如來，此佛亦從水大圓通，成無上覺，若山若海，水性自在流通。山屬地大，俗云山高水更高，水大地大，自在無礙。王即自在之義。先修內觀，後修外觀，由淺及深，至此佛時，逢佛既多，觀力愈深、方得亡身合界、與十方世界，諸香水海之水，一味流通，性合真空者：即悟明藏性，性水真空，性空真水，無二無別，全相全性，即體即用。今於如來座下，得童真名，預入大乘菩薩之會。

此結答圓通。我因修水觀，觀內水外水，同一氣分，一味流通，等無差別，了悟水性空寂，本自無生，得無生忍，證入圓通。如欲圓滿無上菩提，惟斯水大，最為第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Candraprabha, the Moonlight Youth, rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "I recall how, in a kalpa as distant as the sands of the Ganges, there appeared a buddha named Water-Deva, who taught the bodhisattvas to cultivate the contemplation of water, thereby entering samādhi. Contemplating within my own body, I found the nature of water never displacing itself: beginning with mucus and saliva, and continuing through every bodily fluid, essence, and blood, down to what is excreted — circulating throughout the body, the nature of water remains, throughout, exactly the same. I saw that the water within my own body, and the water of the great, banner-crowned ocean-realms and every fragrant sea beyond this world, are, without any difference at all, exactly identical. At that time, having just first accomplished this contemplation, I could see only water itself, not yet having attained freedom from any sense of a body at all. I was, at that time, a bhikṣu, sitting in quiet meditation within my own room. A disciple of mine, peering in through a gap in the window to check on me, saw nothing but clear water filling the entire room, with nothing else to be seen at all. Being only a child, with no understanding of what this meant, he took up a piece of broken tile and threw it into the water, causing a splash, and then, glancing back, went on his way. When I emerged from samādhi, I suddenly felt a sharp pain in my own chest, exactly as when Śāriputra was once struck by the demon Vidhūra. I reflected to myself: I have already attained the path of arhatship, long free from any cause of illness — why, then, should I suddenly feel this pain today? Could I possibly be regressing? At that very moment, my young disciple came running to me and told me what had happened. I told him at once: should you see the water again, open the door and go into it, and remove that piece of tile. The disciple did as instructed, and, the next time I entered samādhi, he again saw the water, this same tile still plainly there within it, and, opening the door, removed it. When I emerged from samādhi afterward, my own body was exactly as it had been before. I continued, in this way, encountering countless further buddhas, until, at last, in the age of the Tathāgata King of Sovereign Penetration Over Mountains and Seas, I finally attained freedom from any sense of a body at all, this very nature of mine now one with the fragrant seas of the ten directions, true emptiness itself, with no difference between them whatsoever. And now, under this very Tathāgata, I have received the name 'Youth of Pure Chastity,' and stand among the ranks of the bodhisattvas. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have realized it, the single, unbroken flow of the very nature of water, attaining the patient acceptance of the unborn and perfecting bodhi itself, is foremost."

Commentary: "Youth" here means one who entered the path in pure chastity; Candraprabha possessed exactly this virtue of pure chastity, hence this name. Rising from his seat, he bows and addresses the Buddha: I recall how, in a kalpa as distant as the sands of the Ganges, there appeared a buddha named Water-Deva — this buddha, having cultivated the contemplation of water himself, thereby awakened to the very heaven of his own nature, one of the thirty-five buddhas named in this sūtra. "Taught the bodhisattvas" refers to this one particular class of capacity generally, not to Candraprabha alone. "Cultivate the contemplation of water": taking water itself as the object of contemplative practice, this contemplation, once accomplished, leading to the entry into samādhi.

This follows from his own long cultivation of this contemplation. The first eight lines contemplate water within; the last five contemplate water without. First contemplating his own body's own nature of water as never displacing itself is the general statement; what follows explains exactly what "never displacing" means. "Beginning with mucus and saliva": what is found in the nose is called mucus, what is found in the mouth is called saliva — this water, being on the surface, is easily seen; what descends the throat in swallowing is called "moisture," what overflows outside the throat is called "fluid" — this water, being closer to the interior, can still be felt; what is found in the bone marrow is called "essence," what is found in the sinews and flesh is called "blood" — this water, being hidden deep within, is difficult to know directly. What is expelled after eating and drinking is called urine and excrement. Among these, moisture and fluid represent water's own clear aspect, while the rest represent its own turbid aspect — circulating throughout the body, back and forth without end — and though clear and turbid differ, the nature of water itself remains exactly the same throughout; and since this nature is the same throughout, it never displaces itself at all.

"Saw that the water within my own body": having seen this water circulating throughout his own body, back and forth without end, this inner contemplation now well practiced, he extended and expanded it, to contemplate the water of the whole world as well. As the Rǔfēng Zhāimài commentary explains: according to the Flower-Treasury Adorned World-Ocean, called the Universal-Light Maṇi-Jewel Fragrant-Water Ocean, within this ocean there stands one great lotus flower, called the Banner of Every Kind of Radiant, Fragrant Light; within this flower are found buddha-lands as numerous as motes of dust, ten in number, and within each, fragrant-water oceans as numerous as motes of dust as well. Within each of these oceans, in turn, stands a single seed of a world-system; and each such seed of a world-system contains, within it, twenty further layers of buddha-lands, stacked one upon another like a great banner, the very king among buddha-lands — hence, "the great, banner-crowned ocean-realms." "Every fragrant sea" refers to these same fragrant-water oceans, taken both together and individually. Contemplating the water within his own body, and the water of these fragrant-water oceans beyond this world, though differing in their distance and their size, he found the nature of water to be exactly the same throughout both, equal, without any difference at all. And "identical" here also carries the sense of "interpenetrating" — the water within and the water without freely interpenetrating one another, posing no obstruction to each other at all.

This follows from cultivating this practice according to the teaching received. "At that time" refers to the very time of this cultivation — the wisdom capable of contemplation, and the very object being contemplated, first coming into correspondence with one another — this is what is meant by "first accomplished." This contemplation is exactly the contemplation of water; his own contemplative practice accomplished, at the very moment of entering this contemplation, one seeing him would see no person there at all, but only water itself — that is, taking water itself as his own body — yet he had not yet attained freedom from every mark whatsoever, and so could not yet attain complete freedom from any sense of a body at all.

This cites an actual event as proof that a sense of the body still remained, even so. At that time, I was a bhikṣu, settling my own mind in meditative contemplation within a quiet room — that is, cultivating this very contemplation of water. I had a disciple, one who attended to my own washbasin and water-jug — who, having noticed that I remained within this room for so long, peered in through a gap in the window to see what was happening within, and saw nothing but clear water filling the entire room, with nothing else to be seen at all. This is exactly mind and object corresponding to one another perfectly — contemplating water and manifesting water in turn — what is called "form born of samādhi," the very state of this samādhi manifesting directly before him. But ordinarily, this "form born of samādhi," at the moment of entering such a contemplation, is visible only to the one experiencing it directly; that Candraprabha's own contemplation of water, and Ucchuṣma's own contemplation of fire, could each be seen by others as well, shows truly how exceptional the power of their own contemplation had become.

Later, in the Later Liang dynasty, in Mianzhu County of Hanzhou, there lived a monk known as the Water-Contemplation Monk. When bandits once rose up in rebellion and sought to enter Mianzhu, this Water-Contemplation Monk entered his own contemplation of water, contemplating the whole county as water — and the entire county appeared, throughout, as water itself; the bandits, arriving, saw nothing but a vast, boundless lake, unable to find the county's own borders at all, and the entire population of the county was thereby spared from this calamity — a fitting confirmation of exactly this same principle.

The child, being young and without any real understanding, held two doubts in his own mind: first, wondering why this room should be full of water at all; and second, wondering why, if his teacher were sitting in meditation within this very room, he could not see him there at all. Not knowing whether this was truly water or not, he took up a piece of broken tile and threw it into the water, causing a splash — "causing" here meaning to stir up — and, the water now making a sound, he became convinced it truly was water, and, glancing back, went on his way. When I emerged from samādhi, I suddenly felt this pain, exactly as when Śāriputra was once struck by the demon Vidhūra. Śāriputra, while in samādhi on Vulture Peak, was passed over by two demons moving through the sky, one named Vidhūra, the other Punarvidhūra. Punarvidhūra said to Vidhūra: "I wish to strike this monk with my own fist." Vidhūra warned him: "do not give rise to such a thought." Punarvidhūra did not listen, and Vidhūra departed at once; Punarvidhūra then struck Śāriputra's own head with his fist, and, emerging from samādhi, Śāriputra at once felt a sharp pain in his head. He said to the World-Honored One: "my body has never once been afflicted before — why, then, this pain in my head now?" The Buddha told him: "a rākṣasa demon struck your own head with his hand; this same demon, with his own great strength, once struck Mount Sumeru itself and split it clean in two — had you not been in samādhi at the time, your own body would surely have been shattered to pieces. This demon, for his own deed, has already fallen into the Avīci hell as its own retribution." The present account, naming this demon "Vidhūra," likely encountered him at the very same occasion, and simply cites the wrong one of the two names by mistake.

I pondered this within my own mind, my own doubt unresolved: I have already attained the path of arhatship, long free from any cause of illness, and ought, therefore, to have no karma left capable of manifesting in the present at all — why, then, should this pain suddenly arise today? The line "could I possibly be regressing" admits of two readings: first, whether this very pain, now that I have emerged from samādhi, might itself be regressing; or second, whether an arhat, long free from any cause of illness, now suddenly afflicted with this pain, might be at risk of regressing from his own attained fruit altogether. The Zhèngmài commentary observes: whether an arhat may still experience illness or not depends on distinguishing two kinds of bondage, that of the "seed" and that of the "fruit": karma sown in the past or the present, destined to summon a future fruit not yet actually received in body, is called bondage of the seed; karma whose fruit the body must now actually receive, having already been received, is called bondage of the fruit. One who has genuinely, newly realized the fourth fruit has already entirely severed the seed-bondage of the afflictions of views and thought, and will never again receive rebirth — yet, the present body not yet having been extinguished, this bondage of the fruit still remains, and whatever bodily suffering must still be received by this present body will still be received — hence Śāriputra's own headache, and, later, Pilindavatsa's own foot pain, both belong to exactly this same kind.

"Came running" means: Candraprabha having emerged from samādhi, the child came running to him at once, and told him what had happened. I then reasoned that this pain must have come from the piece of tile thrown into the water, and so told the child: should you see the water again, open the door and go into it, and remove that piece of tile. The child did as instructed, and, when Candraprabha next entered his own room and entered samādhi again, the child once more peered in and saw the tile still plainly there within the water, and, opening the door, removed it. When I emerged from samādhi afterward, my own body was exactly as it had been before, entirely unharmed. That he had felt this pain at all shows only that he had not yet attained freedom from any sense of a body whatsoever.

Encountering countless buddhas, across a span of time necessarily very long, he came, at last, to the age of the Tathāgata King of Sovereign Penetration Over Mountains and Seas — this buddha, too, having accomplished perfect penetration through the great element of water, attaining unsurpassed awakening; whether mountain or sea, the nature of water flows freely through both without any obstruction at all. Mountains belong to the element of earth — as the common saying goes, "however high the mountain, the water rises higher still" — the great elements of water and earth alike free and unobstructed. "Sovereign" here means exactly this same freedom. First cultivating this contemplation inwardly, and then cultivating it outwardly as well, proceeding from the shallow to the deep — by the age of this particular buddha, having already encountered so many buddhas, the power of his own contemplation had grown deeper still, and he thereby attained freedom from any sense of a body at all, uniting with the whole world beyond, this water of every fragrant sea throughout the ten directions flowing as one single, unbroken current. "This very nature of mine now one with true emptiness itself" means exactly this: awakening to the nature of the tathāgatagarbha, that the nature of water is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true water, with no difference between the two at all — every appearance, in its entirety, identical with the nature itself; substance and function, one and the same. And now, under this very Tathāgata, he has received the name "Youth of Pure Chastity," and entered the ranks of the greater vehicle's own bodhisattvas.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: through cultivating this contemplation of water, contemplating the water within and the water without as sharing one and the same essence, a single, unbroken flow, equal without any difference at all, he awakened to the fact that the nature of water is empty and still, originally unborn of itself, and thereby attained the patient acceptance of the unborn, realizing perfect penetration. To perfect unsurpassed bodhi itself, then, it is exactly this great element of water that he holds as foremost.

〖經〗琉璃光法王子，即從座起，頂禮佛足，而白佛言：我憶往昔，經恆沙劫，有佛出世，名無量聲。開示菩薩，本覺妙明，觀此世界，及眾生身，皆是妄緣，風力所轉。我於爾時，觀界安立，觀世動時，觀身動止，觀心動念，諸動無二，等無差別。我時覺了，此群動性，來無所從，去無所至，十方微塵，顛倒眾生，同一虛妄。如是乃至，三千大千，一世界內，所有眾生；如一器中，貯百蚊蚋，啾啾亂鳴，於分寸中，鼓發狂鬧，逢佛未幾，得無生忍。爾時心開，乃見東方，不動佛國為法王子。事十方佛，身心發光，洞徹無礙。佛問圓通，我以觀察，風力無依，悟菩提心，入三摩地，合十方佛，傳一妙心，斯為第一。

〖講〗琉璃光依觀得名，取後身心發光，洞徹無礙，故得是號。梵語具云吠琉璃，此云青色寶。此法王子，常放青色光明，同彼琉璃，即《涅槃經》，琉璃光菩薩，放青色光，至釋迦座前者。今在楞嚴會上，自述圓通，起座頂禮白佛：我回憶往昔，經恆河沙劫之前，有佛出世，名無量聲，此佛亦由觀風大所成之佛。由觀一切音聲，因風而生，聲既無量，風亦無量，即聲觀風，在在可成觀境，故知此佛，亦由觀風大所成也。

開示菩薩，本覺妙明二句，有機教體用兩對：開示是教，菩薩是機，對大機說大教，故說本覺妙明，最上一乘之理。本覺即天然本具，靈覺之性，寂然不動，有感則通，在眾生分上，為本來佛性。妙是體，明是用，稱體起用，全用即體，體用不相捨離，即妙而明，即明而妙；又妙是不變義，明是隨緣義，不變常隨緣，隨緣常不變，而風性不變，常隨眾緣，而成聲也。

觀此世界，及眾生身下，示脩觀法，觀即能觀之智，世界指無情依報之世界，眾生身指有情正報之眾生，為所觀境。此依正之境，皆是無明妄緣，風力所轉變而有也。本覺即真如，真如界內，本來不立一塵，豈有世界眾生？皆由最初一念無明妄動，動則有風，風大即一念，心中動相耳，情器世間，一切諸風，莫不資始乎此，故曰皆是妄緣，風力指無明所轉變也。欲細釋此二語，可將第四卷首，佛答三種忽生之文，從性覺必明起，至彼無同異，真有為法止，可作此註腳。

我於爾時：即彼佛授觀時，指示風大發源時，風大由於本覺妙明，隨緣起念，一念妄動，自蔽妙明，妄循無明風力所轉，故有世界眾生。但能觀得風性本空，動相非有，便可旋妄復真，還歸本覺妙明之真心矣！故我依教脩觀，觀察界之安立，十方界相，皆由風力執持；世之流動，三世推遷，皆由風力密移；身之動止靜也，行、住、坐、臥，莫非風力所使；心之動念，生、住、異、滅，莫非風力所推。如此觀察，外而世界，內而身心，諸動雖多，其體無二，唯一風性，故曰等無差別。

先觀世界身心，諸動雖多，唯一風性；我此時覺了，此群動之風性，無所從來，亦無所去，當體全空，無有實體，可跟究故。十方微塵世界：此指無情之器界；以及顛倒眾生：此指有情之根身，同一虛妄，皆妄緣風力所轉耳。

如是乃至：是從廣至狹，從微塵數世界，乃至三千大千，一世界內，所有一切眾生：上是法，下舉喻。喻如一器皿之中，貯百蚊蚋，啾啾小鳴之聲亂鳴，在分寸小器之中，鼓發狂鬧：鼓動也，狂亂也。一器喻一界，蚊蚋喻眾生；一界眾生，如小器中，貯百蚊蚋，於分寸中，亂鳴鼓動狂鬧，喻各為妄緣風力所轉，於世界中，爭人競我，稱王圖霸，求名謀利，此皆狂鬧。由觀力故，觀大同小，觀妄非真，即妄緣風力，亦皆虛妄無體。逢佛未幾者：逢遇無量聲佛授觀，經時未久，速得悟證；先悟風大，來無所從，去無所至，當體性空。風大如是，諸大皆然，本無生滅之相可得，即得無生法忍，獲證圓通。

心開：乃本覺真心開顯。東方屬震，為群動之首，乃見動中有不動佛，即阿佛。梵語阿，此云不動。此乃於風性妄緣動中，親見本覺不動之真體；即於不動佛會下，為法王子，乃能遍事十方諸佛，即如觀世音，在彌陀會下，為法王子，而能遊化諸國，遍事諸佛。身心發光者：既了妄身妄心，皆屬妄緣，風力所轉，親見法身真心，乃是當人自性本具，內外洞徹，如淨琉璃，映現諸法，此即得名所以。

此結答圓通。我以因也觀察風力無依，即是無體，此了妄也；悟明本覺菩提真心，此悟真也。又觀風大，來無所從，去無所至，則風大無依，當體即空；則由妄緣風力所轉，身心世界，豈不亦空？此句即前所云，諸妄銷亡。悟菩提心，妄窮真顯，即前所云，不真何待？從此得入三摩地，合十方諸佛，所傳微妙心印：如第二卷，顯見不分科中，文殊代問，佛告文殊，十方如來，及大菩薩，於其自住三摩地中，見與見緣，並所想相，如虛空華，本無所有；此見及緣，元是菩提，妙淨明體，即諸佛所傳妙心。琉璃光，因觀風大證此，故曰斯為第一。

Sūtra: Ratnaprabha, the Vaiḍūrya-Light Dharma-Prince, rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "I recall how, in a kalpa as distant as the sands of the Ganges, there appeared a buddha named Boundless Sound, who revealed to the bodhisattvas the wondrous brightness of original enlightenment, and had them observe how this world, and the very bodies of sentient beings, are nothing but a false, conditioned appearance turned by the sheer force of wind. I, at that time, observed the world's own establishment; observed the world's own motion through time; observed the body's own movement and stillness; and observed the mind's own arising of thoughts — and found every one of these motions, without exception, to be, in the end, exactly the same, without any difference at all. I then came to realize that this very nature underlying every one of these motions comes from nowhere and goes nowhere at all, and that the world's countless motes of dust, and its inverted, deluded sentient beings, share, alike, this same falseness entirely. And so it continues, on through the whole of a single great-thousand-world-system, every sentient being within it exactly like a hundred mosquitoes shut inside a single vessel, buzzing and shrieking in mad confusion within so cramped a space. Encountering this buddha, it was not long before I attained the patient acceptance of the unborn. My own mind then opened, and I beheld, in the east, the buddha-land of the unshaken buddha, and became a Dharma-prince there — serving the buddhas of the ten directions ever since, my own body and mind giving off light, penetrating everywhere without any obstruction at all. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have realized it, observing closely that the very force of wind has nothing whatsoever to depend upon, and thereby awakening to the mind of bodhi, entering samādhi, and thus becoming one with the wondrous mind transmitted by all the buddhas of the ten directions alike, is foremost."

Commentary: Ratnaprabha, "Vaiḍūrya-Light," takes his name from this very contemplation, since his own body and mind later gave off this same light, penetrating everywhere without obstruction — hence this title. The Sanskrit term in full is vaiḍūrya, meaning "the blue-colored jewel." This Dharma-prince constantly emits this same blue-colored light, exactly like vaiḍūrya itself — as in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, where the bodhisattva Vaiḍūryaprabha emits this same blue light, arriving before Śākyamuni's own seat. Now, at this Śūraṅgama assembly, recounting his own perfect penetration, he rises from his seat and bows to the Buddha: I recall how, in a kalpa as distant as the sands of the Ganges before this one, there appeared a buddha named Boundless Sound — this buddha, too, having become a buddha through contemplating the great element of wind. Since every sound whatsoever arises through wind, and sound itself is boundless, wind itself must be boundless as well — and so, contemplating wind by way of sound, wind may be taken as the very object of contemplation anywhere at all — one thus knows that this buddha, too, became a buddha through contemplating the great element of wind in exactly this way.

"Revealed to the bodhisattvas the wondrous brightness of original enlightenment": these two lines involve two paired relationships, of capacity and teaching, and of substance and function. "Revealed" is the teaching; "the bodhisattvas" is the capacity — teaching the greater teaching to those of the greater capacity, and so speaking of the wondrous brightness of original enlightenment, the very principle of the highest, single vehicle itself. "Original enlightenment" is exactly this naturally, inherently possessed, spiritually aware nature — calm and unmoving, yet responding the instant it is touched — which, on the level of sentient beings, is called their own original buddha-nature. "Wondrous" is the substance; "bright" is the function — the function arising precisely from this same substance, and this function, in its entirety, being nothing but this substance itself, substance and function never separate from one another — wondrous precisely in being bright, bright precisely in being wondrous. And, further, "wondrous" carries the sense of never changing, while "bright" carries the sense of following every condition — never changing, yet ever following conditions; following conditions, yet never changing — and the nature of wind itself, likewise never changing, constantly follows every condition it encounters, and thereby becomes sound.

From "observe how this world, and the very bodies of sentient beings" on, the passage shows the very method of this contemplation to be cultivated: "observe" is exactly the wisdom capable of observation; "this world" refers to the insentient world as the circumstantial realm of retribution; "the very bodies of sentient beings" refers to sentient beings themselves, as the direct realm of retribution — both together serving as the very object of this contemplation. This realm, direct and circumstantial alike, is nothing but ignorance's own false, conditioned appearance, transformed and brought into being by the sheer force of wind alone. Original enlightenment is exactly suchness itself — and within the realm of suchness, not a single speck of dust is ever established at all — how, then, could there be any world or any sentient being there to begin with? All of this comes about only because that very first single thought of ignorance stirred falsely — and, once it stirred, wind resulted; this great element of wind is exactly this single thought, this very mark of stirring within the mind itself — every wind whatsoever, throughout the realm of sentient beings and the material world alike, draws its own beginning from exactly this — hence, "nothing but a false, conditioned appearance," "the force of wind" pointing to exactly this same transformation wrought by ignorance itself. To explain these two lines in full detail, one may turn to the opening of fascicle 4, where the Buddha answers the question of the three sudden arisings, from "the nature of awareness is necessarily bright" all the way to "it has neither sameness nor difference — this is truly a conditioned dharma" — this passage may serve as the fuller gloss on exactly this same point.

"I, at that time": that is, at the very moment this buddha bestowed this contemplation upon him, pointing out exactly where the great element of wind itself originates. This great element of wind arises from original enlightenment's own wondrous brightness, giving rise to thought according to conditions — a single thought stirring falsely, obscuring this wondrous brightness of its own accord, falsely following this very force of wind wrought by ignorance, and so a world and its sentient beings come to be. Yet, simply by being able to observe that the nature of wind is, at root, empty, and that the mark of its own motion is not truly there at all, one can turn falseness back into truth, and return to this same wondrous, bright, true mind of original enlightenment once more! And so, cultivating this very contemplation according to the teaching received, I observed how the world itself comes to be established — the very appearance of the ten directions being, in every case, held together by the force of wind alone; how the world flows onward through time — the three periods shifting and changing, in every case, secretly moved along by the force of wind alone; how the body moves and rests — walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, none of it other than what this force of wind brings about; and how the mind gives rise to thought — arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing, none of it other than what this same force of wind drives forward. Observing in this way, the world without and body and mind within, however many these motions may be, share, in their own substance, no difference at all — a single nature of wind alone — hence, "without any difference at all."

Having first observed that the world, the body, and the mind, however many their motions, share this single nature of wind alone, I then came to realize that this very nature underlying all these countless motions comes from nowhere and goes nowhere at all, its own substance, examined directly, entirely empty, with no real substance to it that could ever be traced or investigated at all. "The world's countless motes of dust" refers to the insentient material world; "and its inverted, deluded sentient beings" refers to the sentient beings possessing their own bodily faculties — both sharing, alike, this same falseness entirely, both alike turned by nothing but this false, conditioned force of wind.

"And so it continues" moves from the vast to the narrow — from world-systems as numerous as motes of dust, down to the whole of a single great-thousand-world-system, and every sentient being within it: the first half states the doctrine, the second offers an analogy. It is like a hundred mosquitoes shut inside a single vessel, buzzing and shrieking — "buzzing" being the sound of a faint, small cry — in mad confusion, within so cramped a space, stirred up into wild disorder — "stirred" meaning agitated, "wild" meaning frenzied. A single vessel stands for a single world; the mosquitoes stand for sentient beings — the beings of a single world, like a hundred mosquitoes shut inside so small a vessel, crying out in wild confusion within so cramped a space, standing for how each, alike, is turned by this same false, conditioned force of wind, contending for advantage within the world, each claiming supremacy for themselves, seeking fame, scheming for gain — all of it nothing but this same wild confusion. Through the power of this contemplation, seeing the great as no different from the small, seeing this very falseness as anything but true, he saw that this false, conditioned force of wind, too, is entirely without any real substance at all. "It was not long before" means: encountering the buddha Boundless Sound and receiving this contemplation from him, before very long at all, he swiftly attained this very awakening and realization — first awakening to the fact that the great element of wind comes from nowhere and goes nowhere, its own substance, examined directly, empty by nature. And if this holds true for the great element of wind, so too does it hold for every other great element as well — none of them possessing any mark of arising or ceasing that could ever actually be found — and so he attained the forbearance of the unborn nature of all things, realizing perfect penetration.

"My own mind then opened": this is exactly original enlightenment's own true mind opening and becoming manifest. The east belongs to the trigram Zhèn, foremost among all things that move — and so, within this very motion, he beheld an unmoving buddha, that is, Akṣobhya — the Sanskrit akṣobhya meaning "unmoved." This is exactly personally beholding, within the false, conditioned motion of the very nature of wind, the true, unmoving substance of original enlightenment itself — and so, under this very unmoving buddha, he became a Dharma-prince, and was thereby able to serve the buddhas of the ten directions everywhere — exactly as Avalokiteśvara, a Dharma-prince under Amitābha, is able to roam and transform every land, serving every buddha everywhere alike. "My own body and mind giving off light": having fully understood that this false body and false mind alike belong to nothing but this same false, conditioned force of wind, and having personally beheld the dharma-body's own true mind — this being something every single person already, inherently possesses in full — he became penetrating within and without alike, exactly like pure vaiḍūrya, reflecting every dharma within it — and this is exactly why he received this very name.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: through observing closely that the very force of wind has nothing whatsoever to depend upon — that is, has no substance of its own at all — this is fully understanding falseness; and awakening to this true mind of bodhi, belonging to original enlightenment itself — this is awakening to truth. And, further, observing that the great element of wind comes from nowhere and goes nowhere at all, this great element of wind has nothing to depend upon, its own substance, examined directly, empty — then how could body, mind, and world, all alike turned by this same false, conditioned force of wind, fail to be empty as well? This line is exactly the earlier "every falseness dissolves away entirely." Awakening to the mind of bodhi, falseness exhausted and truth revealed, is exactly the earlier "what, then, could possibly remain, if not truth itself?" From this, he entered samādhi, becoming one with the subtle mind-seal transmitted by the buddhas of the ten directions alike — exactly as, in fascicle 2, in the section on how seeing cannot be divided, Mañjuśrī, asking on the assembly's behalf, was told by the Buddha: the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, and the great bodhisattvas as well, abiding in their own samādhi, see that seeing itself, together with its own conditions, and every conceived appearance whatsoever, is like flowers conjured in empty space, originally nothing there at all — this very seeing, together with its conditions, being, at root, nothing but bodhi, this wondrous, pure, bright substance itself, exactly this same wondrous mind transmitted by every buddha alike. Ratnaprabha, through contemplating the great element of wind, realized exactly this — hence, "is foremost."

Ākāśagarbha: A Body the Same as Empty Space 卯五 空藏空大

Chinese Source

〖經〗虛空藏菩薩，即從座起，頂禮佛足，而白佛言：我與如來，定光佛所，得無邊身。爾時手執，四大寶珠，照明十方，微塵佛剎，化成虛空。又於自心，現大圓鏡，內放十種微妙寶光，流灌十方，盡虛空際。諸幢王剎，來入鏡內；涉入我身，身同虛空，不相妨礙。身能善入微塵國土，廣作佛事，得大隨順。此大神力，由我諦觀：四大無依妄想生滅，虛空無二，佛國本同，於同發明，得無生忍。佛問圓通，我以觀察，虛空無邊，入三摩地，妙力圓明，斯為第一。

〖講〗清涼云：混虛空為體性，故名虛空藏，以虛空為身，又名無邊身，因虛空無邊故。藏字之義，如《宗鏡》云：大集會中，虛空藏來時，純現虛空相，謂阿難言：我以自身證知，是故如所證知，能如是說，何以故？我身即是虛空，以虛空證知一切法，為虛空印所印。爾時五百聲聞，各以所著，鬱多羅僧，奉虛空藏，一時同聲說如是言。智法藏中，不墮其外。所奉之衣，即便不現，諸聲聞問：衣何至耶？虛空藏言：入我藏中。又此菩薩，以虛空為庫藏，雨十方無量阿僧祗世界，所有寶物，衣服飲食故。偈云：虛空藏菩薩，得虛空庫藏，充足諸有情，此藏無窮盡，因是名為虛空藏；即從座起，頂禮白佛。

我與如來：指釋迦如來，定光佛即然燈佛。《大論》云：太子生時，身光如燈，後發心出家，直至成佛，故名然燈。是釋迦如來，第二僧祗，授記本師。虛空藏與釋迦，但同事然燈，得無邊身，乃菩薩自述所證，非與釋迦同得也。

菩薩既以空性為身，應以空慧為手；四大寶珠：即照空四大之智珠，菩薩已得虛空身，則內四大之我已空，若外四大之法不空，則身相不純，大用不遍；故復以空智，照明十方微塵數佛剎，所有外四大，一一化成虛空；此空非斷滅空，乃空諸妄相，妄盡真純，以真空妙智，智光所融，一一化成真空妙理；為此得體，證法身德。

自心：即自己本覺真心。現出大圓鏡智：此智是佛轉第八識所成。因虛空藏，分證如來三德秘藏，故能現此智，且能於此總智，更現十種別智；十種微妙寶光，即十智，如《華嚴》三世智，乃至無邊諸佛智。智光流灌十方，盡虛空邊際：則空中所有世界，無一不在智光所照之中，色空無礙。或以一智，而現十智，照徹十法界，窮盡真空妙有，此屬現相，全事即理，而成理事無礙法界，證般若德。

此攝剎入身，及下分身入剎，依正互相攝入，圓融自在，乃事事無礙法界，不思議業用。諸幢王剎：即無量香水海中，諸浮幢王剎，乃依報廣大之境，攝入我正報身中，身同虛空，彼此不相妨礙，此廣陝自在無礙門。

此以正報入依報。身能善入者：以一身而入一國，或多身而入多國，皆不得稱為善入；今能善入者，以一身而分無量身，同時遍入微塵國，廣作無邊佛事。得大隨順：即大自在。此乃起用，證解脫德。

此大神力：即指色空無礙，依正攝入。由我下：出神力之來由，由我觀空所致，我則諦觀四大，本非心外實有，唯心所現，無體可得；無依、即無體也。乃隨妄想以生滅，念起則非有似有，念息則當體即空，故曰虛空無二。四大既同虛空，無有差別，佛國亦以四大為能成？故佛國亦自本空；同、即空也，以虛空為同故。

於同發明，得無生忍者：即於空性，發明藏性，悟明性覺真空，性空真覺，清淨本然，周遍法界。空性生即無生，諸大亦然，自不見有少法生滅之相，故曰得無生忍。前所發大自在用，皆此忍之力也。

此結答圓通。我因修空觀，觀察虛空無邊，觀成得定，入三摩地。妙力圓明者：即前色空無礙，依正互融，十方圓明，得大自在，以斯空觀，最為第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Ākāśagarbha Bodhisattva rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "In the presence of the buddha Dīpaṃkara, together with the Tathāgata here, I attained a boundless body. At that time, holding four great, precious jewels in my own hand, I illuminated the buddha-lands of the ten directions, as numerous as motes of dust, transforming them all into empty space. And, further, within my own mind, a great, round mirror appeared, giving off ten kinds of subtle, precious light from within it, flowing throughout the ten directions, reaching all the way to the very edge of empty space itself. Every banner-crowned, kingly land came flowing into this very mirror, entering into my own body — my own body now the same as empty space itself, neither ever obstructing the other. My own body is able to enter skillfully into lands as numerous as motes of dust, carrying out the Buddha's own work everywhere, attaining great, spontaneous accord with all of it. This great spiritual power comes from my own close contemplation: that the four elements have nothing to depend upon, arising and ceasing only through false thought, that empty space itself admits of no duality at all, and that every buddha-land is, at root, exactly the same — and it was from this very sameness that I attained the patient acceptance of the unborn. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have realized it, observing closely the boundlessness of empty space, entering samādhi through it, this wondrous power now perfectly bright and complete, is foremost."

Commentary: Master Qingliang observes: taking empty space itself as his own substance and nature, he is thus named Ākāśagarbha, "Treasury of Space" — and, taking empty space as his own body, he is also called "Boundless Body," since space itself is boundless. As for the meaning of "treasury" here, the Zōngjìng Lù explains: at the great assembly, when Ākāśagarbha arrived, he manifested purely the appearance of empty space itself, and said to Ānanda: "I know this through my own body directly, and so, knowing it exactly as I have realized it, I am able to speak of it in just this way. Why is this so? Because my own body is exactly empty space itself, and it is through this very empty space that I come to know every dharma there is, sealed by the very seal of empty space itself." At that time, five hundred śrāvakas each offered up their own outer robes to Ākāśagarbha, and, with a single voice, spoke these same words together: "within the treasury of wisdom's own dharma, nothing at all falls outside it." The very robes they had offered then vanished from sight at once, and these śrāvakas asked: "where have our robes gone?" Ākāśagarbha answered: "they have entered my own treasury." This bodhisattva, further, takes empty space itself as his own storehouse and treasury, raining down, throughout the boundless world-systems of the ten directions, every kind of treasure, clothing, and food there is — as the verse puts it: "the bodhisattva Ākāśagarbha has attained the treasury of empty space itself, providing fully for every sentient being, this treasury never once exhausted" — and it is for this reason that he is named Ākāśagarbha. He rises from his seat and bows to the Buddha.

"Together with the Tathāgata here": referring to the Tathāgata Śākyamuni; the buddha Dīpaṃkara here is exactly the buddha "Burning Lamp." As the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra explains: at the moment of this prince's own birth, his body's own light shone like a lamp — and, later giving rise to the resolve to leave the household life, all the way through to his own attainment of buddhahood, he was thus named "Burning Lamp." This is the very buddha who, at the second incalculable aeon, bestowed the prediction of buddhahood upon our own founding teacher. Ākāśagarbha and Śākyamuni merely happened to serve this same buddha Dīpaṃkara together — this attainment of a boundless body is Ākāśagarbha's own account of what he himself realized, not something realized together with Śākyamuni at the very same time.

Since this bodhisattva already takes the nature of emptiness as his own body, he ought, accordingly, to take the wisdom of emptiness as his own hand as well. "Four great, precious jewels" are exactly the wisdom-jewels that illuminate the four elements as empty. This bodhisattva, having already attained a body of empty space itself, has thereby already emptied the four elements composing his own inner self — yet were the outer four elements of every other dharma not likewise emptied, his own bodily appearance would remain impure, and this great function of his would fail to pervade everywhere. And so, with this same wisdom of emptiness, he illuminates the buddha-lands of the ten directions, as numerous as motes of dust, and every one of these outer four elements is, one by one, transformed into empty space itself — this emptiness not the mere emptiness of annihilation, but rather emptying every false appearance, until falseness is exhausted and truth alone remains pure, this true, empty, wondrous wisdom, its own light melting everything within its reach, transforming each into this same true, empty, wondrous principle, one by one — and it is through this that he attains this very substance, realizing the virtue of the dharma-body.

"My own mind" is exactly his own true mind of original enlightenment. "A great, round mirror appeared" — this wisdom results from the Buddha's own transformation of the eighth consciousness. Because Ākāśagarbha has partially realized the Tathāgata's own threefold, secret treasury of virtue, he is thereby able to manifest this same wisdom, and, further, to manifest, within this single, comprehensive wisdom, ten further, particular kinds of wisdom as well; these "ten kinds of subtle, precious light" are exactly these ten kinds of wisdom — as in the Avataṃsaka Sūtra's own wisdom of the three times, extending all the way to the boundless wisdom of every buddha. This light of wisdom, flowing throughout the ten directions, reaching all the way to the very edge of empty space itself, means that every world-system within this space, without exception, lies within the very reach of this light of wisdom, form and emptiness alike unobstructed. Or, alternatively: through this single wisdom, manifesting these same ten kinds of wisdom, illuminating every one of the ten dharma-realms completely, exhausting both true emptiness and wondrous existence together — this belongs to the manifestation of appearance, phenomena, in their entirety, identical with principle itself, forming the dharma-realm of the unobstructed interpenetration of principle and phenomena, and thereby realizing the virtue of prajñā.

This describes gathering every land into his own body, and, what follows, dividing his own body to enter every land in turn — direct and circumstantial retribution alike, mutually gathering into one another, perfectly interfused and free, this being exactly the dharma-realm of the unobstructed interpenetration of phenomena with one another, an inconceivable function indeed. "Every banner-crowned, kingly land" refers to the banner-crowned, kingly lands found among the boundless fragrant-water oceans — that is, the vast realm of his own circumstantial retribution — gathered into his own body, the realm of his own direct retribution, his own body now the same as empty space itself, neither ever obstructing the other — this being exactly the gate of the vast and the narrow, each at perfect ease with the other, without any obstruction at all.

This describes his own direct retribution entering into his own circumstantial retribution in turn. "My own body is able to enter skillfully": a single body entering a single land, or many bodies entering many lands, could neither properly be called "entering skillfully" — what is truly meant by "entering skillfully" here is a single body dividing itself into boundless bodies, entering, at the very same moment, lands as numerous as motes of dust everywhere at once, carrying out boundless buddha-work throughout. "Attaining great, spontaneous accord" is exactly great freedom itself — this being the very function that arises from this same attainment, realizing the virtue of liberation.

"This great spiritual power" refers to exactly this unobstructed interpenetration of form and emptiness, this mutual gathering of direct and circumstantial retribution into one another. From "comes from my own" on, the passage states the very source of this spiritual power — arising from his own contemplation of emptiness. I contemplated closely how the four elements are, at root, never truly existing outside the mind at all, nothing but what the mind itself manifests, with no substance of their own to be found anywhere — "nothing to depend upon" being exactly "no substance of their own." They arise and cease only in dependence on false thought — a thought arising, they seem, though not truly existent, to exist; a thought subsiding, their own substance, examined directly, is empty — hence, "empty space itself admits of no duality at all." Since the four elements are, in this way, the same as empty space, with no difference between them at all, and since a buddha-land, too, is composed of nothing but these same four elements, a buddha-land, too, must be, at root, empty of itself — "the same" here meaning exactly this same emptiness, taking empty space itself as what makes them all the same.

"It was from this very sameness that I attained the patient acceptance of the unborn": that is, within this very nature of emptiness, he brought to light the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha, awakening to the fact that the nature of awareness is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true awareness, originally pure, pervading the whole Dharma-realm everywhere at once. The very arising of this nature of emptiness is itself the unborn — and the same holds for every other great element as well — he no longer sees even the slightest mark of any dharma arising or ceasing at all — hence, "attained the patient acceptance of the unborn." Every great, spontaneous function described above arises entirely from the power of exactly this same forbearance.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: through cultivating this contemplation of emptiness, observing the boundlessness of empty space itself, this contemplation accomplished, samādhi attained, he entered this very samādhi. "This wondrous power now perfectly bright and complete" is exactly the earlier unobstructed interpenetration of form and emptiness, direct and circumstantial retribution interfused perfectly with one another, the ten directions perfectly bright, attaining great freedom — it is exactly this contemplation of emptiness, then, that he holds as foremost.

Maitreya: Every Land Is Mind Alone 卯六 彌勒識大

Chinese Source

〖經〗彌勒菩薩，即從座起，頂禮佛足，而白佛言：我憶往昔，經微塵劫，有佛出世，名日月燈明。我從彼佛，而得出家，心重世名，好遊族姓。爾時世尊，教我修習，唯心識定，入三摩地。歷劫以來，以此三昧，事恆沙佛，求世名心，歇滅無有。至然燈佛，出現於世。我乃得成，無上妙圓，識心三昧。乃至盡空如來國土，淨、穢有無，皆是我心，變化所現。世尊，我瞭如是，唯心識故。識性流出，無量如來，今得授記，次補佛處。佛問圓通，我以諦觀，十方唯識，識心圓明，入圓成實，遠離依他，及遍計執，得無生忍，斯為第一。

〖講〗彌勒，梵語具云梅怛利曳，此云慈氏，姓也，名阿逸多，此云無能勝，今稱慈氏，由得慈心三昧故，慈無能勝。起座頂禮白佛：我憶往昔，經微塵劫，有佛出世，名日月燈明，此佛以三智立名。我從彼佛，而得出家；此但出世俗之家，心重世間名聞利養，名與利相因，則身為利役，好奔走於貴族，交遊乎大姓，有名利可圖，故曰好遊族姓。法華經云：「貪著於名利，求名利無厭，好遊族姓家，棄捨所習誦」是也。

爾時世尊，觀機授教，因我重世名，心馳散故，教我脩唯心識定，觀察三界唯心，萬法唯識。通達一切外境，無非心識之所變現，如夢如幻，無一真實。因脩此定，心不馳散，而不外求，得入三摩地。此唯心識定成，自日月燈明佛起，歷劫以來，以此三昧，奉事恆沙諸佛。唯識定深，觀「人間富貴花間露，世上功名水上漚」。但脩內觀，無復外求，故求世名心，歇滅無有。

然燈佛乃釋迦如來，第二僧祗授記本師。出現世時，我乃得成無上妙圓，識心三昧。前雖得三昧，未臻妙圓，不能稱為無上，然積行雖經多劫，入理在一剎那，如瓜熟蒂落，一剎那間，頓悟性識明知，覺明真識，全識全性，全性全識，本如來藏。妙真如性曰妙，圓滿周遍曰圓，此即由相宗之權，而入圓教之實。

上科成定得體，此科稱體起用。此現國土，為所依處，後現如來為教化主；所現如來國土有三：淨穢有無，即對三土。或法性土，法身所依，無淨無穢，即常寂光土；次現受用土，報身所依，有淨無穢，即實報莊嚴土；三現變化土，應身所依，有淨有穢，即凡聖同居土。一一皆我識心，變化所現。既云所現，體本自空，既曰變化，相非實有，真空不礙妙有，故現國土；妙有不礙真空，故唯變現。

我既了達如是，淨穢國土，唯是心識所現之故，識性又復流出，無量如來，我雖未成佛，已能現土現佛，成辦諸佛所應作事，廣度無量眾生。今佛知我已證無上妙圓識心三昧。故得蒙授記，候補作佛，以為賢劫第五尊佛，於當來下生，龍華三會，說法度生。

此結答圓通。我以識大，諦觀十方，一切依、正，染、淨，唯是識心之所變現，了知三界唯心，萬法唯識，識心無二，一體圓明。又一解：識心具足一切法，普照一切法，法法無非識心，故曰圓明。是以能入，圓滿成就，真實之性；既入圓成實性，自可遠離，依他起性，遍計執性。

圓成實性是真體，能為諸法所依，喻如麻；依他起性，依圓成實性所起，虛妄之相，相有性無，此約依真，喻如繩；繩依麻所成故。遍計執性，於依他起性，周遍計度，分別妄執，情有理無，喻如蛇如夜間見繩妄認為蛇。今既遠離二種情執，如佛所言，若離妄想，則無師智，自然智，一切顯現，便得無生法忍，即入圓成實性，我即以斯諦觀唯識，最為第一。

《三性唯識》云：一、遍計執性，頌曰：「由彼彼遍計，遍計種種物，此遍計所執，自性無所有。」論曰：周遍計度，故名遍計；品類眾多，說為彼彼。以六、七二識，遍於一切染、淨法上，計有實我實法，名遍計所執。遍計本空，如繩上蛇。二、依他起性，頌曰：「依他起自性，分別緣所生。」論曰：眾緣所生，心心所體，及相、見分，皆依眾緣，而得起故。以依他眾緣，和合互起，猶如幻事，如麻上繩。三、圓成實性，頌曰：「圓成實於彼，常遠離前性。」論曰：於依他起上，常遠離前遍計所執，內不執實我，外不執實法，我、法二空，所顯真如為性，故云：圓成實性，此則如麻。故攝論云：「分別性如蛇，依他性如繩，圓成性如麻。」麻上生繩猶是妄，何況繩上更生蛇。

Sūtra: Maitreya Bodhisattva rose at once from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "I recall how, in a kalpa as numerous as motes of dust ago, there appeared a buddha named Candrasūryapradīpa, under whom I left the household life — though my own heart still valued worldly reputation, and I loved to move among noble families. The World-Honored One then taught me to cultivate the samādhi of mind-only, consciousness-only, thereby entering samādhi. Across kalpas ever since, through this very samādhi, I have served buddhas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, and this craving for worldly reputation has, at last, entirely come to rest. By the age of the buddha Dīpaṃkara's own appearance in the world, I had, at last, accomplished this unsurpassed, wondrous, perfectly complete samādhi of consciousness itself — extending, from there, all the way to every one of the Tathāgata's own lands throughout the whole of empty space, pure or defiled, existent or not, all of them nothing but a transformation manifested by my own mind. World-Honored One, having fully understood that it is exactly this — that everything is mind, is consciousness alone — this very nature of consciousness itself has flowed forth, manifesting countless Tathāgatas in turn; and now I have received the prediction of buddhahood, to succeed in time to the very rank of a buddha myself. As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: as I have realized it, contemplating closely how the ten directions are nothing but consciousness alone, this mind of consciousness perfectly bright and complete, entering the truly, perfectly accomplished nature itself, departing far from both the dependent nature and the imagined, universally clung-to nature, and thereby attaining the patient acceptance of the unborn, is foremost."

Commentary: Maitreya's name, in Sanskrit, is Maitreya in full, meaning "of loving-kindness," his own family name — his given name being Ajita, meaning "unsurpassed," yet he is called here "of loving-kindness" instead, since he attained the samādhi of loving-kindness, a loving-kindness that nothing can surpass. Rising from his seat, he bows and addresses the Buddha: I recall how, in a kalpa as numerous as motes of dust ago, there appeared a buddha named Candrasūryapradīpa — this buddha's own name, as explained earlier, reflecting three kinds of wisdom together. "Under whom I left the household life": this refers only to leaving his own worldly household — his own heart still valued worldly reputation and material gain — reputation and gain being causally linked, so that the body becomes enslaved to the pursuit of gain — and he loved to hurry among the noble families, mingling with the great clans, wherever reputation and gain might be found — hence, "loved to move among noble families." As the Lotus Sūtra puts it: "clinging to reputation and gain, seeking them without ever being satisfied, loving to move among the noble families, abandoning what he had studied and recited" — exactly this.

The World-Honored One, observing his own capacity and teaching accordingly, seeing that I valued worldly reputation so heavily, my own mind scattered and restless, taught me to cultivate the samādhi of mind-only, consciousness-only, observing that the three realms are nothing but mind, and every dharma nothing but consciousness alone — penetrating through to the fact that every outer object whatsoever is nothing but a transformation manifested by mind and consciousness themselves, like a dream, like an illusion, with nothing genuinely real in any of it at all. Through cultivating this samādhi, my own mind no longer scattered and restless, no longer seeking outwardly at all, I thereby entered samādhi. This samādhi of mind-only, consciousness-only, once accomplished, continued on, from the buddha Candrasūryapradīpa onward, across kalpas ever since, through this very samādhi, serving buddhas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges. As this consciousness-only samādhi deepened, he contemplated how "wealth and rank among people are like dew upon a flower; fame and achievement in this world are like foam upon the water." Cultivating only this inward contemplation, no longer seeking anything outwardly at all, this craving for worldly reputation thereby entirely came to rest.

The buddha Dīpaṃkara is the very buddha who, at the second incalculable aeon, bestowed the prediction of buddhahood upon our own founding teacher Śākyamuni. By the time of this buddha's own appearance in the world, I had, at last, accomplished this unsurpassed, wondrous, perfectly complete samādhi of consciousness itself. Though he had already attained this samādhi before, it had not yet reached this wondrous completeness, and so could not yet be called "unsurpassed" — yet though his accumulated practice spanned many kalpas, entering into this principle occurred within a single instant, exactly like a ripened melon falling from its own stem: within a single instant, he suddenly awakened to the nature of consciousness itself, luminously knowing, this awareness's own bright, true consciousness — consciousness, in its entirety, nature itself; nature, in its entirety, consciousness itself — originally nothing but the tathāgatagarbha. "Wondrous" names the nature of suchness itself; "perfectly complete" names its own perfect, all-pervading completeness — and this is exactly proceeding from the Yogâcāra school's own provisional teaching into the perfect teaching's own true reality.

The section above accomplished samādhi and attained this very substance; this section now, matching this substance, gives rise to its own function. This manifestation of buddha-lands is the very place depended upon; what follows, manifesting a Tathāgata, is the very lord who teaches and transforms beings there. The buddha-lands manifested here are of three kinds: "pure or defiled, existent or not" corresponds to three kinds of land — first, the land of dharma-nature itself, depended upon by the dharma-body, neither pure nor defiled, exactly the land of eternal, still light; second, the land of enjoyment, depended upon by the reward-body, pure without any defilement at all, exactly the land of complete reward and adornment; and third, the land of transformation, depended upon by the manifestation-body, both pure and defiled together, exactly the land shared in common by ordinary beings and sages alike. Every one of these, without exception, is nothing but a transformation manifested by my own mind of consciousness. Since it is called "manifested," its own substance is, at root, empty of itself; and since it is called "a transformation," its own appearance is not genuinely real either — true emptiness posing no obstruction to wondrous existence, hence these lands are manifested at all; wondrous existence posing no obstruction to true emptiness, hence they are nothing but this same manifestation, and nothing more.

Having now fully penetrated the fact that these lands, pure or defiled, are nothing but what mind and consciousness themselves manifest, this very nature of consciousness, further, flows forth, manifesting countless Tathāgatas in turn. Though I myself have not yet become a buddha, I am already able to manifest lands and manifest buddhas alike, accomplishing every task any buddha must undertake, liberating boundless sentient beings far and wide. The Buddha now knows that I have already realized this unsurpassed, wondrous, perfectly complete samādhi of the mind of consciousness itself, and so I have received this very prediction of buddhahood, to succeed in time to buddhahood myself, as the fifth buddha of the present fortunate aeon, descending, in an age yet to come, to teach beneath the nāga-flower tree, through three great assemblies, liberating beings there.

This closes his answer on perfect penetration: through the great element of consciousness, contemplating closely how every direct and circumstantial realm throughout the ten directions, defiled or pure, is nothing but a transformation manifested by this very mind of consciousness, he understood that the three realms are nothing but mind, and every dharma nothing but consciousness alone, this mind of consciousness without any duality at all, a single substance, perfectly bright and complete. There is a further reading as well: this mind of consciousness is itself fully complete with every dharma there is, illuminating every dharma universally, with no dharma whatsoever that is not, in itself, this same mind of consciousness — hence, "perfectly bright and complete." And so he was thereby able to enter the truly, perfectly accomplished nature itself; and, having entered this truly, perfectly accomplished nature, he could naturally depart far from both the dependent nature and the imagined, universally clung-to nature as well.

The truly, perfectly accomplished nature is the true substance itself, capable of serving as what every dharma depends upon — comparable to hemp fiber itself. The dependent nature is the false appearance that arises in dependence on this truly, perfectly accomplished nature — an appearance that exists, yet with no nature of its own behind it — this, considered in relation to what is genuinely true, is comparable to a rope, since a rope is made by depending on hemp fiber. The universally clung-to, imagined nature is what, upon this same dependent nature, one calculates and discriminates about universally, clinging falsely to it as real — existing only as a feeling, with no principle behind it at all — comparable to a snake, as when, mistaking a rope glimpsed in the dark of night, one falsely takes it for a snake. Now, having departed far from both of these two kinds of clinging based on mere feeling, exactly as the Buddha himself has said — once false thought is set aside, the wisdom that needs no teacher, wisdom spontaneous and of itself, manifests in every direction — he thereby attained the forbearance of the unborn nature of all things, that is, entered this truly, perfectly accomplished nature itself. It is exactly this close contemplation of consciousness-only, then, that he holds as foremost.

The teaching of the three natures within Consciousness-Only thought explains this as follows. First, the universally clung-to, imagined nature: as the verse puts it, "through this and that universal calculation, one universally calculates every kind of thing; what this universal calculation clings to has, in its own nature, nothing there at all." The treatise explains: calculating and discriminating universally is why it is called "universal calculation"; and since there are so many different kinds of things calculated in this way, it is called "this and that." Because the sixth and seventh consciousnesses, spreading universally across every defiled and pure dharma alike, calculate that there exists a real self and real dharmas there, this is called what universal calculation clings to. This universal calculation is, at root, empty — exactly like the snake seen upon a rope. Second, the dependent nature: as the verse puts it, "the nature that arises in dependence on other things is what discrimination, dependent on its own conditions, gives rise to." The treatise explains: what is produced by a multitude of conditions — the very substance of mind and mental factors, together with their appearing-portion and seeing-portion alike — arises, in every case, dependent on this same multitude of conditions. Because it depends on this multitude of conditions, combining and arising together with one another, it is exactly like an illusory event — comparable to a rope formed upon hemp fiber. Third, the truly, perfectly accomplished nature: as the verse puts it, "the truly, perfectly accomplished nature, with regard to this same dependent nature, is what forever departs from the former, universally clung-to nature." The treatise explains: upon this same dependent nature, forever departing from that former universally clung-to attachment — no longer clinging within to a real self, nor clinging without to real dharmas — the twin emptiness of self and of dharmas is what suchness itself, as the nature thereby revealed, actually is — hence, "the truly, perfectly accomplished nature" — this being comparable to the hemp fiber itself. Hence the Mahāyānasaṃgraha says: "the imagined nature is like the snake; the dependent nature is like the rope; the truly accomplished nature is like the hemp fiber." For a rope to arise upon hemp fiber is already a kind of falseness — how much more so, then, for a snake to arise, in turn, upon this very rope!

Amitābha's own name means "boundless life" — yet this is a boundlessness that is, in fact, itself bounded, and he too will, in time, enter nirvāṇa. After Amitābha's own nirvāṇa, the true Dharma will continue to abide in the world for a further boundless span of kalpas — the true Dharma coming to its own end in the very first half of that final night, and, in the second half of that same night, Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva attaining buddhahood, named the Tathāgata King of the Mountain of Universal Light and Merit. This buddha's own lifespan, and the duration of his own true Dharma, will likewise both be boundless; and while this true Dharma still abides in the world, Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva will teach and transform sentient beings, himself awaiting his own succession to buddhahood — and when this true Dharma comes to its own end, in the first half of that same future night, Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva will attain buddhahood in the second half of that night, named the Tathāgata King of the Well-Abiding Treasury of Merit.

"Dharma-Prince" is another title for a bodhisattva — "Dharma-King" refers to the Buddha, the Buddha being the very king of the Dharma, free and sovereign with regard to it. A Dharma-prince is capable of giving rise to four kinds of mind: first, giving rise to the vast mind — vowing to liberate boundless sentient beings, gathering in, throughout the worlds of the ten directions, those who recollect the Buddha, and leading them to the Pure Land; second, giving rise to the foremost mind — vowing to accomplish unsurpassed buddhahood, and, though already partially realizing the three virtues, still seeking the ultimate, twofold adornment of merit and wisdom; third, giving rise to the constant mind — assisting Amitābha and Avalokiteśvara across kalpas without end, never once growing weary; and fourth, giving rise to the mind free of inversion — liberating beings across kalpas without ever clinging to any mark of "liberating" at all, liberating without liberating, this very liberating being itself no liberating at all, capable of shouldering the Tathāgata's own heavy burden, capable of inheriting the Dharma-King's own household estate — a true son of the Dharma-King indeed, hence the title "Dharma-Prince."

"Together with his fellow companions, fifty-two bodhisattvas": "together" means "along with"; "fellow" means "of the same kind." This must be divided into two categories, of his own practice and of transforming others: first, in his own practice, all alike take up this same mind of recollecting the Buddha, attaining thereby the patient acceptance of the unborn — yet though they cultivate this same teaching of recollecting the Buddha together, the depth of their own accomplishment differs, some abiding at the stage of dry wisdom, others at the ten faiths, the ten abidings, the ten practices, the ten transferences, the ten grounds, or equal enlightenment — fifty-two stages in all, hence "fifty-two bodhisattvas," though this number is not meant as a strict, fixed count. Second, in transforming others, they all alike use this same teaching of recollecting the Buddha to teach and transform sentient beings, now, in this present world, gathering in those who recollect the Buddha and leading them to the Pure Land — and, according to the depth of their own accomplishment, those they transform likewise fall into this same fifty-two-fold distinction.

"Bodhisattva," in full, is bodhi-sattva — the Sanskrit "bodhi" translates as "awakening"; "sattva" translates as "sentient being," a general term covering beings of all nine realms alike. The six ordinary realms of beings have not yet severed craving and attachment; the three vehicles' own beings have not yet exhausted discriminating feeling — hence both alike are called "sentient beings." "An awakened sentient being" refers here specifically to Mahāsthāmaprāpta, and admits of three distinct senses: first, benefiting himself — being a sentient being already awakened, knowing that he himself is, at root, already a buddha, and that, should he only recollect the Buddha, buddhahood must surely follow; second, benefiting others — being capable of using this same path of his own awakening to awaken every other sentient being as well, teaching and transforming beings, so that they, with one mind, recollect the Buddha together and accomplish buddhahood alike; and third, carrying forward both wisdom and compassion at once — seeking the Buddha's own awakening above, for his own benefit, and carrying compassion below, to liberate sentient beings, for the benefit of others — and it is because of these three senses together that he is called "an awakened sentient being."

"Rose at once from their seats" and bowed at the Buddha's own two feet — offering the very crown of their own head, the most exalted part of the body, in homage to our own Buddha's own feet, the very humblest part of his body, so as to express their own utmost reverence — and said to the Buddha: this is the very ceremony of requesting the teaching, recounted here by the sūtra's own compiler.

〖經〗我憶往昔，恆河沙劫，有佛出世，名無量光，十二如來，相繼一劫，其最後佛，名超日月光，彼佛教我，念佛三昧。

〖講〗菩薩自稱曰我，乃是菩薩假我，及法身真我，不妨隨順世人，同稱為我，非同凡夫妄執之我。凡夫妄執四大妄身，以為實我，不了諸法本無我，我執既起，貪、瞋、癡，無不從此而生。或貪財為我受用，或貪色為我娛樂，或貪名為我榮耀，或貪食為我滋養，或貪睡為我安息；是我所貪之財、色、名、食、睡，設若為他人之所妨礙，以及剝奪，則瞋怒之心，勃然而起，此因貪起瞋，即愚癡。是知我執，為諸惡根本。身心因此不得安樂，世界由此不得和平。

又非同外道妄計之我。外道有二十五諦，最初為冥諦，冥諦生覺大，覺大生我心，我心生五微，五微生五大，五大生十一根，最後曰神我，計有神我為萬能，此屬邪知邪見。菩薩已得我空，故非妄執與邪計，凡、外二種我也。我憶往昔，恆河沙劫：我字已如上釋，憶者憶念，明記不忘也。過去稱為往昔。恆河亦云殑伽河，此云天堂來，狀其來處之高。此河在印度雪山之頂，無熱惱池流出，無熱惱是龍王名，此池以龍為名，池有四口，流出四河：東銀牛口，流出殑伽河，此云天堂來，即恆河，闊四十里，河底銀沙，沙細如麵。南金象口，流出信度河，此云驗河，河底金沙。西琉璃馬口，流出縛芻河，此云青河，河底琉璃沙。北玻璃師子口，流出徙多河，此云冷河，河底玻璃沙，中國黃河源。佛所住祗園，相近恆河，凡言數目之多，常取河沙為喻。此言恆河沙劫，以顯經時之久也。劫具云劫波，此云長時分。

有佛出世，名無量光者：於時有佛，出現於世間，佛梵語具云佛陀，此言覺者，乃大覺悟之人，具本覺之妙理，發始覺之妙智，證究竟覺之妙果，方名為佛。佛在因地之中，亦是人道一眾生，修成為佛。本覺者，即眾生本有之佛性，人人本具，個個不無，此性即眾生六根之中，不生不滅之真性，亦即真如妙理，隨眾生之染緣，其體不變。前云：「縱汝形銷，命光遷謝，此性云何，為汝銷滅？」隨緣不變，故謂之妙。本覺妙理，雖人人本具，多皆迷而不覺，故為眾生，或遇知識開導，或閱經教開悟，了知眾生，具有佛性，本來是佛，是為始覺，由不覺而方始覺悟也。此始覺即屬妙智，依此妙智，返照妙理，照澈心源，而成究竟覺之佛果，是謂三覺圓，萬德具，為大覺悟之人。超九界以獨尊，為三界之導師，作四生之慈父，故出現於世間。

名無量光者：此佛以光明而立號，因光明勝故，名無量光。然光有身光、智光之別，智光，諸佛同得一切種智，智光相同；身光，有照一由旬，十由旬，百千由旬，或照一世界，十世界，百千世界者。今稱無量者，則普照十方國土，無所障礙，乃至鐵圍山間，日月神光照不到，亦得大明，此無量光佛名，與彌陀名同，因在恆河沙劫之前，當非彌陀，乃同名佛也。同名諸佛甚多，如釋迦古釋迦之類。

十二如來，相繼一劫者：據大彌陀經云：無量光佛、無邊光佛、無礙光佛、無對光佛、炎王光佛、清淨光佛、歡喜光佛、智慧光佛、不斷光佛、難思光佛、無稱光佛、超日月光佛。一、無量光佛，實智照理，無限量故。二、無邊光佛，權智照事，無邊際故。三、無礙光佛，慈光與樂，無障礙故。四、無對光佛，悲光拔苦，無對敵故。五、炎王光佛，光音應化，得自在故。六、清淨光佛，惑垢既離，發淨光故。七、歡喜光佛，令他受用，生大喜故。八、智慧光佛，以大智慧，破諸惑故。九、不斷光佛，常放身光，不斷絕故。十、難思光佛，妙用無盡，難思議故。十一、無稱光佛，具足眾德，不可稱故。十二、超日月光佛，窺天鑑地，超一切故。此十二如來，相繼一劫，出現世間。

其最後佛，名超日月光：日月雖光，不照覆盆，此佛光明，耀古騰今常自若，逾於千日放光明。彼佛教我，念佛三昧：彼佛就本經文意看來，當指最後一佛，若據大本之意，十二佛名，乃無量壽佛之別號，唯一佛身，此言十二佛相繼出世，則非一體明矣。彼佛教我：言語指示謂之教，教即佛度眾生，方便之法，教以修因剋果，教以離苦得樂。

念佛三昧：即修因剋果，離苦得樂之勝方便。念佛是修行清淨三業之行，三昧是得定事理一心不亂，果能念佛，必得三昧，欲得三昧，祗要念佛。念佛有四：一、持名念佛，聞說佛名，一心稱念。二、觀像念佛，設立佛像，注目觀瞻，三、觀想念佛。以我心眼，觀彼如來。四、實相念佛，即念自性，法身真佛。此四種念佛，名字有差別，義理有淺深。

今本章念佛，乃是持名念佛，有事念理念之分：事念者，有能念之心，所念佛號，一心繫念於佛，心佛不相捨離。余常示人：事念之法，心中惟有佛，佛外更無心，口念心念，心唸口念，字字從心起，字字從口出，字字從耳入。如是念法，不至昏散，念念相繼，無有間斷。設若念久口燥，心唸口不念則可，口念心不念則不可。更有二喻，以喻念佛之法：一、當如貓捕鼠，提起全副精神，身毛皆豎。又當如雞抱卵，放下一切思想，飲啄渾忘。果能如是念佛，雖為事念，不但往生可必，而悟理亦自可期。如空谷禪師云：「不必參念佛是誰，直爾純一念去，亦自有悟日」是也。理念者不必別舉話頭，只須把一句阿彌陀佛，即念反觀，能念心外，無有佛為我所念心即是佛，所念佛外，無有心能念於佛佛即是心，能所雙亡，心佛一致，此即中道，理性念佛。終日念佛，終日無佛可念，終日無念，終日念念念佛；若言其有，則能念之心，了不可得，所念之佛，離相絕名；若言其空，則能念之心，靈靈不昧，所念之佛，歷歷分明。如是念佛，空、有不立，心、佛一如，則持名念佛，通乎實相。雖然四種念佛，後後深於前前，而理念功成，亦前前徹於後後也。

三昧：是梵語，此云正定，即一心不亂，念佛功成也。亦有事一心念佛三昧，理一心念佛三昧。何謂事一心念佛三昧？聞說念佛法門，可以橫超三界，疾出生死，即深信不疑，願生淨土，專心繫念，句句分明，念念相續，行、住、坐、臥，惟此一念，更無二念，即是以一念，而除眾念，不為內惑外境，之所雜亂。如《成具光明定意經》云：「空閒寂寞，而一其心；在眾煩惱，而一其心；乃至訕謗利失，善惡等處，而一其心」者是也。此於事上即得，理上未徹，惟得信力成就，未見道故；但屬定門，無有慧故；祗能伏妄，不能破妄；往生淨土，九品蓮華之中，則在中三品。以功力之淺深，而分上、中、下；而下品三品，乃未得事理一心，念佛三昧者之所生也。

何謂理一心念佛三昧？聞說念佛法門，即是無上深妙禪，即於念佛之時，諦實觀察，念佛即所以念心，心佛一如，能所不二，寂而常照，是無念而念，照而常寂，是念即無念，了知佛即是心，心即是佛，心佛見泯，能所情亡，寂然不動，湛然常住。此不專事相，純修理觀，而得觀力成就，能見諦故，屬慧門攝；兼得定故，安住唯心淨土，親見自性彌陀。往生淨土，當在上三品。生則決定生，去則實不去。以十萬億程外之極樂，亦不出一心之外故也。

三昧是禪觀通名，如《智論》云：「一切禪定攝心，皆名三昧」是也。此曰念佛三昧，亦名一行三昧。文殊般若經，佛告文殊：欲入一行三昧者，應處空閒，捨諸亂意，不取相貌，繫心一佛，專稱名字，隨彼方所，端身正向，能於一佛，念念相續。即是念中，能見過去、未來、現在諸佛，念一佛功德，與念無量佛，功德無二。若得一行三昧者，諸經法門，皆悉了知。

問：「此教念佛，為念十方佛耶？為念阿彌陀佛耶？」答：「為念阿彌陀佛。」普廣菩薩問佛：十方俱有佛土，何以獨讚西方？佛言：閻浮提人，心多雜亂，令其專心一境，乃得往生，若念十方諸佛，境繁意散，不成三昧，況諸佛同一法身，念一佛，即念一切佛故。

又稱念彌陀名號，隨佛本願，願云：十方眾生，聞我名號，乃至十念，若不生者，不取正覺。十念尚得往生，況一心憶念耶？念佛法門，古稱徑中徑，但能淨念相繼，便得往生，如驥驟雖超群馬，未及龍飛；鶴沖已過凡禽，爭如鵬舉。驥驟鶴沖，譬餘門念佛，群馬凡禽，譬其他法門，龍飛鵬舉，譬持名念佛，惟此持名念佛法門，但持六字洪名，便得往生淨土，圓證三不退，其功簡、其效速。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "I recall how, in a kalpa as distant as the sands of the Ganges, there appeared a buddha named Boundless Light — twelve Tathāgatas appearing one after another within this same kalpa — and the very last of these, named Light Surpassing Sun and Moon, taught me the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha."

Commentary: A bodhisattva refers to himself as "I" as a matter of provisional self, and of the true self of the dharma-body alike — there being no harm in following worldly convention and speaking of himself this way, quite unlike an ordinary being's own false clinging to a self. An ordinary being falsely clings to this false body of the four elements as a genuinely real self, failing to understand that no dharma whatsoever truly has any self at all — and once this attachment to self arises, craving, aversion, and delusion, every one of them, arise from exactly this. Craving wealth as something for this self to enjoy, craving beauty as something for this self to delight in, craving fame as something for this self to be glorified by, craving food as something to nourish this self, craving sleep as something to give this self its rest — and should this very wealth, beauty, fame, food, or sleep, so craved by this self, ever be obstructed or taken away by another, then a mind of anger and rage rises up at once — this anger arising from craving is itself delusion. One thus knows that this attachment to self is the very root of every evil — body and mind, because of it, can never find peace, and the world, because of it, can never find harmony.

Nor is this the same as the self falsely calculated by those outside the teaching. Those outside the teaching hold to twenty-five categories, the very first being the category of primordial obscurity, from which arises the great awareness, from which arises the mind of self, from which arise the five subtle elements, from which arise the five gross elements, from which arise the eleven faculties, and, last, what is called the spiritual self — calculating that this spiritual self possesses power over all things — this belonging entirely to deviant knowledge and deviant views. This bodhisattva has already attained the emptiness of self, and so speaks of himself according to neither the ordinary being's own false clinging, nor the outside teaching's own deviant calculation, but according to neither of these two kinds of "self" at all. "I recall how, in a kalpa as distant as the sands of the Ganges" — the word "I" has already been explained above; "recall" means to call to mind, remembering clearly without forgetting. What is past is called "how... ago." The Ganges is also called the Gaṅgā River, meaning "come down from the heavenly hall," describing how lofty its own source must be. This river issues from the summit of the snowy mountains of India, flowing out from Lake Anavatapta — "Anavatapta" being the name of the nāga-king for whom this lake itself is named. This lake has four outlets, from which four rivers flow: from the silver ox's mouth in the east flows the Gaṅgā River, "come down from the heavenly hall," that is, the Ganges itself, forty li wide, its riverbed of silver sand, this sand as fine as flour; from the golden elephant's mouth in the south flows the Sindhu River, meaning "the tested river," its riverbed of golden sand; from the vaiḍūrya horse's mouth in the west flows the Vakṣu River, "the blue river," its riverbed of vaiḍūrya sand; and from the crystal lion's mouth in the north flows the Sītā River, "the cold river," its riverbed of crystal sand — the very source of China's own Yellow River. The Jetavana Grove, where the Buddha himself dwelt, lies close to the Ganges, and so, whenever speaking of some vast number, this same river's sand is constantly taken as the standard image. Speaking here of "kalpas as distant as the sands of the Ganges" shows, in this same way, just how long a span of time has passed. "Kalpa," in full, is kalpa in Sanskrit, meaning "a very long division of time."

"There appeared a buddha named Boundless Light": at that time, a buddha appeared in the world — the Sanskrit "buddha," in full, is buddha, meaning "the awakened one," a person of great awakening, complete with the wondrous principle of original enlightenment, giving rise to the wondrous wisdom of initial enlightenment, and realizing the wondrous fruit of final, complete enlightenment — only one who has accomplished all of this may properly be called a buddha. Even a buddha, at the causal stage, was once a single sentient being on the human path, cultivating his way to buddhahood. "Original enlightenment" is exactly the buddha-nature every sentient being already possesses — every single one, without exception — this nature being exactly this same unborn, unceasing, true nature found within every sentient being's own six sense-faculties, exactly this same wondrous principle of suchness, its own substance never changing, however defiled the conditions a sentient being may encounter. As was said earlier: "even should your own body one day dissolve entirely, your very life's light departing and passing on — how, then, could this same nature of hearing ever be dissolved along with you?" Following conditions, yet never changing — hence it is called "wondrous." Though every single sentient being already, inherently possesses this same wondrous principle of original enlightenment, most remain deluded and unaware of it, and so remain ordinary beings — until, whether encountering a wise teacher's own guidance, or reading and awakening through the sūtras' own teaching, they come to understand that sentient beings already possess the buddha-nature, originally already buddha — this is called initial enlightenment, awakening, at last, out of what had been unenlightened before. This initial enlightenment belongs to this same wondrous wisdom, and, relying on this wondrous wisdom to illuminate this wondrous principle in turn, illuminating all the way to the very source of the mind itself, one thereby accomplishes the wondrous fruit of final, complete enlightenment — called the perfect completion of the three kinds of enlightenment, every virtue fully complete, a person of great awakening indeed. Rising above all nine realms as uniquely honored, becoming the very guide of the three realms, the compassionate father of every kind of birth — hence he appears in the world.

"Named Boundless Light": this buddha takes his own name from light itself, this light being so supremely excellent that he is named Boundless Light. Yet light itself divides into the light of the body and the light of wisdom: the light of wisdom, every buddha alike attaining this same wisdom knowing every mode, is the same light in every case; the light of the body, however, may illuminate a single yojana, ten yojanas, or a hundred thousand yojanas, or a single world, ten worlds, or a hundred thousand worlds. To be called "boundless" here means illuminating the lands of the ten directions universally, with no obstruction anywhere at all — even reaching into the very depths between the iron encircling mountains, where the divine light of neither sun nor moon can ever reach, and yet still bringing great brightness there as well. This buddha named Boundless Light shares the very same name as Amitābha himself — yet, appearing kalpas as distant as the sands of the Ganges before this present age, he cannot be Amitābha himself, but rather a different buddha bearing this same name. Many buddhas share the very same name in this way — just as there was an ancient Śākyamuni before our own.

"Twelve Tathāgatas appearing one after another within this same kalpa": according to the Larger Sūtra of Amitāyus, these are: the buddha Boundless Light, Boundless-Edge Light, Unobstructed Light, Unrivaled Light, Flame-King Light, Pure Light, Joyful Light, Wisdom Light, Unceasing Light, Inconceivable Light, Indescribable Light, and Light Surpassing Sun and Moon. First, Boundless Light: true wisdom illuminating principle itself, without any limit or measure at all. Second, Boundless-Edge Light: provisional wisdom illuminating phenomena, without any boundary or edge at all. Third, Unobstructed Light: this light of loving-kindness granting joy, without any obstruction at all. Fourth, Unrivaled Light: this light of compassion relieving suffering, with nothing capable of opposing it at all. Fifth, Flame-King Light: light and sound responding and transforming, attaining freedom thereby. Sixth, Pure Light: delusion and defilement both left behind, giving rise to this pure light. Seventh, Joyful Light: causing others to receive great benefit, giving rise to great joy thereby. Eighth, Wisdom Light: through great wisdom, breaking through every delusion. Ninth, Unceasing Light: constantly emitting this light from his own body, never once interrupted. Tenth, Inconceivable Light: its own wondrous function inexhaustible, beyond all conception. Eleventh, Indescribable Light: complete with every virtue, beyond all description. And twelfth, Light Surpassing Sun and Moon: peering into the heavens and surveying the earth, surpassing everything there is. These twelve Tathāgatas appeared in the world one after another, within this same single kalpa.

"The very last of these, named Light Surpassing Sun and Moon": though the sun and moon give off their own light, they cannot illuminate beneath an overturned basin — yet this buddha's own light, shining forth across every age, past and present alike, entirely undiminished, surpasses even a thousand suns in its own brightness. "Taught me the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha": read according to this present sūtra's own text, this refers specifically to this last of the twelve buddhas; yet according to the larger sūtra's own account, these twelve names are, in fact, alternate titles for the single buddha Amitāyus, one single buddha-body throughout — so that this present account, describing twelve buddhas appearing one after another instead, would not, strictly speaking, describe one and the same being. "Taught me": instructing him directly in words is called "teaching" — this teaching being exactly the Buddha's own skillful method for liberating sentient beings, teaching them to cultivate a cause that will bring about its corresponding fruit, teaching them to leave suffering behind and attain happiness instead.

"The samādhi of recollecting the Buddha" is exactly this same supreme, skillful means — cultivating a cause to bring about its fruit, leaving suffering behind to attain happiness. Recollecting the Buddha is the very practice of purifying one's own three karmic activities; samādhi is the very attainment of this same purity, whether through actual practice or through principle, the mind undivided and undisturbed. If one can genuinely recollect the Buddha, one will surely attain this samādhi; and if one wishes to attain this samādhi, one need only recollect the Buddha. Recollecting the Buddha takes four forms: first, recollecting the Buddha by holding his name, hearing the Buddha's own name spoken and reciting it single-mindedly; second, recollecting the Buddha by contemplating his image, setting up an image of the Buddha and fixing one's gaze upon it; third, recollecting the Buddha by contemplative visualization, using one's own mind's eye to behold the Tathāgata directly; and fourth, recollecting the Buddha through true reality itself, that is, recollecting one's own nature, the true buddha of the dharma-body itself. These four forms of recollecting the Buddha differ in name, and differ, too, in the depth of the principle each involves.

The recollection of the Buddha spoken of in this present chapter is exactly recollection by holding his name — and this, in turn, divides into recollection through actual practice and recollection through principle. Recollection through actual practice: there is a mind capable of recollecting, and a name of the Buddha that is recollected, the mind single-mindedly fixed upon the Buddha, mind and Buddha never departing from one another. I have often told others: the method of recollection through actual practice is this — within the mind, there is nothing but the Buddha, and, apart from the Buddha, no mind exists at all; reciting with the mouth, recollecting with the mind; recollecting with the mind, reciting with the mouth; every single word arising from the mind, every single word issuing from the mouth, every single word entering back in through the ear. Reciting in this way, one never falls into dullness or distraction, each recitation continuing on from the last, without a single break. Should one's mouth grow dry from reciting so long, it is permissible to recollect with the mind alone, without reciting aloud — but it is never permissible to recite aloud without the mind's own recollection accompanying it. There are, further, two images for this method of recollecting the Buddha: first, one should be like a cat catching a mouse, one's entire attention gathered up at once, every hair on the body standing on end; and second, one should be like a hen brooding over her eggs, every other thought set entirely aside, food and drink alike forgotten. If one is truly able to recollect the Buddha in this way, then, though this remains recollection through actual practice alone, not only is rebirth in the Pure Land assured, but awakening to the underlying principle, too, may be hoped for in time. As Chan Master Konggu put it: "there is no need to inquire into who it is that recollects the Buddha — simply continue this single-minded recollection directly, and a day of awakening will come of itself" — exactly this.

Recollection through principle requires no separate point of inquiry at all — one need only take up this single phrase, "Amitābha Buddha," and, even as one recollects it, turn this very recollection back upon itself: apart from this very recollecting mind, there is no Buddha there for me to recollect at all — this mind is itself the Buddha; and apart from this very Buddha recollected, there is no mind there capable of recollecting the Buddha either — this Buddha is itself the mind. Subject and object both, together, vanish; mind and Buddha become one and the same — this is exactly the middle way, recollecting the Buddha according to principle itself. Reciting the Buddha's name the whole day through, and yet, the whole day through, there is no Buddha there to be recollected at all; free of recollection the whole day through, and yet, the whole day through, recollecting, moment after moment, the Buddha himself. Were one to say this recollection genuinely exists, then this very recollecting mind can, in the end, never be found at all, and this very Buddha recollected is free of every mark, beyond every name; yet were one to say it is empty, then this very recollecting mind remains, throughout, vividly aware, and this very Buddha recollected remains, throughout, plainly, distinctly present. Recollecting the Buddha in this way, neither emptiness nor existence can be established at all, mind and Buddha become one and the same — and so recollection by holding the name comes to penetrate through to true reality itself. Though these four forms of recollecting the Buddha grow deeper, each one further than the last, once recollection through principle has borne its own fruit, this fruit, too, penetrates all the way back through each of the earlier three in turn.

"Samādhi" is a Sanskrit term, meaning "correct concentration" — that is, the mind undivided and undisturbed, this very fruit of recollecting the Buddha borne to completion. There is, further, both the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha through actual practice with a single, undivided mind, and the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha through principle with a single, undivided mind. What is meant by the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha through actual practice with a single, undivided mind? Hearing that this teaching of recollecting the Buddha can carry one directly across the three realms, swiftly escaping birth and death altogether, one believes this deeply, without the slightest doubt, vows to be reborn in the Pure Land, and fixes one's mind single-mindedly upon it, every single recitation plainly distinct, each moment's recollection continuing on from the last, whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, holding to this one thought alone, with no second thought at all — that is, using this single thought to displace every other thought entirely, no longer thrown into confusion by inner delusion or outer circumstance. As the Sūtra of the Establishment of Luminous Concentration puts it: "whether in empty solitude, or amid the crowd's own turmoil, unifying the mind throughout; even amid slander, loss, praise, or blame, unifying the mind throughout" — exactly this. This much can be attained through actual practice alone, though it has not yet penetrated through to the underlying principle — attaining only the power of faith accomplished, not yet having seen the path itself; belonging only to the gate of samādhi, without wisdom present within it at all — capable only of subduing falseness, not yet capable of breaking through it entirely. Rebirth in the Pure Land, among the nine grades of lotus blossom, would fall, at this level, among the middle three grades — the depth of one's own effort determining whether one falls among the upper, middle, or lower three grades in turn; and the lower three grades belong to those who have not yet attained this same samādhi of recollecting the Buddha with a single, undivided mind, whether through actual practice or through principle, at all.

And what is meant by the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha through principle with a single, undivided mind? Hearing that this teaching of recollecting the Buddha is itself the unsurpassed, profound, and wondrous form of meditation, one observes closely, at the very moment of recollecting the Buddha, that recollecting the Buddha is exactly recollecting one's own mind — mind and Buddha one and the same, subject and object not two different things at all — utterly still, and yet constantly illuminating: this is recollection without recollection; illuminating, and yet constantly still: this is recollection that is itself no recollection at all. Fully understanding that the Buddha is exactly the mind, and the mind is exactly the Buddha, mind and Buddha's own distinction dissolved away, subject and object's own feeling of separation vanished entirely, utterly still and unmoving, calm and ever-abiding. This does not focus exclusively on outward phenomena, but purely cultivates contemplation of principle itself, and, this power of contemplation accomplished, is capable of seeing the truth directly — hence belonging to the very gate of wisdom; while also attaining samādhi at the same time, abiding securely within this mind-only Pure Land, personally beholding Amitābha as one's own nature itself. Rebirth in the Pure Land, at this level, falls among the upper three grades — birth here being birth decisively assured, and yet, in truth, no actual "going" involved at all, since even the Land of Bliss, said to lie a hundred thousand million lands away, never actually departs from this single mind to begin with.

"Samādhi" is a general term for meditative contemplation, as the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra puts it: "every form of meditative concentration that gathers in the mind is called samādhi." This is here called "the samādhi of recollecting the Buddha," also named "the samādhi of a single practice." The Sūtra of Mañjuśrī's Perfection of Wisdom has the Buddha tell Mañjuśrī: "one who wishes to enter the samādhi of a single practice should dwell in a quiet, solitary place, setting aside every scattered thought, taking hold of no outward appearance at all, fixing the mind upon a single buddha, reciting his name exclusively, facing in whatever direction that buddha may be found, the body held upright and directly facing him, and continuing this recollection of this single buddha, moment after moment without a break. Within this very recollection, one is thereby able to see the buddhas of the past, future, and present alike — for the merit of recollecting a single buddha is no different from the merit of recollecting boundless buddhas at once. One who attains this samādhi of a single practice comes to know, thereby, every teaching found in every sūtra completely."

One might ask: "does this teaching mean recollecting the buddhas of the ten directions, or recollecting Amitābha Buddha specifically?" The answer: it means recollecting Amitābha Buddha specifically. The bodhisattva Samantavaipulya once asked the Buddha: since every one of the ten directions has its own buddha-land, why does the Buddha praise the western land alone? The Buddha answered: the people of Jambudvīpa hold minds prone to confusion and disorder — having them fix their minds upon a single object alone is what actually brings about rebirth there; were they instead to recollect the buddhas of the ten directions all at once, their own objects of focus would multiply and their minds would scatter, and no samādhi could ever be accomplished at all. And, further, since every buddha shares this same, single dharma-body, to recollect a single buddha is already to recollect every buddha at once.

And, further, reciting Amitābha's own name accords directly with this buddha's own original vow, which states: "should sentient beings of the ten directions, hearing my own name, recollect it even as few as ten times, and yet fail to be reborn there, may I myself not attain complete awakening." If even ten recollections suffice for rebirth there, how much more so a single, undivided mind's own constant recollection! This teaching of recollecting the Buddha has, since ancient times, been called "the shortest of all short paths" — for simply continuing this pure recollection without a break is enough, of itself, to bring about rebirth there. It is like a swift steed, outpacing every other horse and yet still falling short of a dragon's own flight; or a soaring crane, already surpassing every ordinary bird and yet unable to rival the great roc's own ascent. The swift steed and the soaring crane stand for recollecting the Buddha through other gates of practice; the ordinary horses and birds stand for every other teaching besides; the dragon's flight and the roc's ascent stand for recollection by holding the Buddha's own name directly — for it is this teaching of recollecting the Buddha's name alone that, simply by holding fast to these six resounding syllables, brings about rebirth in the Pure Land, perfectly realizing the three kinds of non-regression at once, its own effort the simplest of all, its own result the swiftest of all.

〖經〗譬如有人：一專為憶，一人專忘，如是二人，若逢不逢，或見非見，二人相憶，二憶念深。如是乃至，從生至生，同於形影，不相乖異。

〖講〗此明單憶無益。譬如現在世間，有二人，指親友之屬，一人專心為憶，憶念此親友，一人專憶他務，忘其親友。如是指上一憶一忘，由此二人，若逢不逢，或見非見：若逢或見，對專憶者言；不逢非見，對專忘者言。二人，譬喻佛與眾生，憶即憶念，記持不忘曰憶，繫緣不捨曰念。一專為憶，喻佛念眾生也；一人專忘，喻眾生不念佛也。佛專憶眾生，有二種意：一、佛具大悲願故，觀見一切眾生，與我本來同體，我今已成正覺，已得涅槃，眾生尚在輪迴，不了生死，故運大悲願，專憶眾生。二、佛具平等心故，佛在因地，為菩薩時，尚念念不捨眾生，況今成佛，視大地眾生，皆如一子，故以平等心，專憶眾生。

眾生不念佛，亦有二種意：一、眾生障重故，為惑、業、苦三，纏縛不脫，於人天因果，尚不肯修，何況念佛法門，故不念佛。二、眾生智暗故，於此念佛，殊勝妙法，可以斷煩惱，了生死，成佛道，現生念佛，能斷一切煩惱妄念，臨終往生，橫超三界輪迴生死，既生彼國，圓證不退，疾成佛道，不生信心，不願往生，故不肯念佛。

是如二人，若逢不逢，或見非見：如是二人，即喻上佛念眾生，眾生不念佛也。佛以大悲願力，常念眾生，遊化娑婆，乃令眾生，若逢或見，如彌陀化身為豐干禪師，在浙江台州，天台山為比丘，人皆不識，此即若逢等於不逢，或見成為非見，都由無念佛之力，及求見彌陀之願故也。

豐干禪師是彌陀化身，出自寒山之口。豐干，在天台山國清寺，住在碾米房，常騎虎出入，眾不知其為何許人。一日，豐干邀寒山、拾得，同朝五臺。曰：與我同行，是我同流，不與我同行，不是我同流。寒山問曰：汝朝五臺作甚麼？干曰：朝禮文殊。山曰：汝不是我同流，我不同去。於是干獨行，至杭州，適有閭邱胤，候補多年，家貧破產，是時省府，派任台州府知府，忽患頭痛之病，調治罔效。豐干特為造訪，闇人拒絕不見。干曰：我特來救他命，為何不見？閽人入報，閭即延見。干曰：汝何病？閭曰：頭痛欲裂。干曰：取水一杯，吾為汝治。乃為持咒訖，以水置手心，向其頭三撲，即時痛止。遂即感謝，叩詢大師法號？住何寺廟？答曰：名豐干，住台州天台山國清寺。閭曰：寺中如大師道行有幾人？干曰：我無道行。寺中高僧，如文殊化身之寒山子、普賢化身之拾得，皆在國清寺內，遊化我國。閭即備禮酬謝，干不受而去。閭到任三天，即到國清寺進香。問知客僧，寒山、拾得二大士何在？煩陪往拜見。知客曰：此二人是瘋僧，大人有何吩咐？閭曰：有欲禮拜。知客曰：可喚之前來。閭曰：不可！自當往拜。遂陪至廚房，寒山、拾得，正在灶門烘火，二人且語且笑，人皆不識，所語何義。知客呼寒山、拾得起來，大人與汝說話。閭一見即就地頂禮，二人即奔走。閭追之，至寒山岩二人入，寒山回首曰：「賊！賊！賊！豐干饒舌多話也，彌陀不事，禮我何為？」閭趨視岩中不見。寒山、拾得，自此遂不復出焉。豐干亦從此不回國清，聖人應化人間，既經洩露，不能再留。閭在山中，檢查事跡，乃於山上石岩竹木，及鄉間牆壁上，抄錄二大士詩多首，皆是佛法，諷世之意，現刊行於世。錄此因緣，以證佛念眾生，眾生不念佛，若逢不逢，或見非見之事實耳。

此明雙憶不離。二人相憶，二憶念深：自可相見相親，不相捨離，乃至生生同於形影；喻生佛念同，眾生念佛，如佛念眾生相同，久憶不忘，一切時，一切處，佛不離心，乃至盡形壽，亦所不忘也。如是乃至，從今生以至他生，同如形影，不相乖違離異。乃至，超略詞，不但今生常得見佛，乃至往生之後，常隨佛學，同於形之與影，不相違，不相離也。此形影不離之喻，有二意存焉：一、喻眾生念佛，必得見佛，生佛不相捨離，合下憶佛念佛，現前當來，必定見佛也。二、喻眾生念佛，必得成佛。因果不相捨離，念佛是因，成佛是果，如大勢至菩薩，以念佛心，入無生忍，在極樂國中，輔助彌陀弘化，及輔助普光功德山王佛，次補佛處，即念佛必得成佛，因果不相捨離也。

〖講〗上科二人之喻，親友猶疏；此科母子之喻，骨肉更親。上五句同前，喻單憶無益，下四句同前，喻雙憶不離。又首三句，合前一專為憶，次二句合前一人專忘。如來母也，眾生子也；世間慈愛最切者，莫過於母親，子不聽教，猶復念念不捨。子若悖逆，忘恩負德，種種不孝，母念或衰，心生悔恨，佛念眾生，更過於母，逆惡重者，佛念更深。又母念子，慈止一世，佛念眾生，慈心無盡，世世相隨，無有退轉。

此云十方如來，憐念眾生：憐者哀憐，念者護念，眾生久在輪迴，備受諸苦，故為佛之所哀憐；雖在生死，佛性不失，又為佛之所護念。《三昧經》云：諸佛心者，大慈悲是。慈悲所緣，緣苦眾生，若見眾生受苦惱時，如箭入心，欲拔其苦。如母親見子受苦，憶念之心更無有異也。

問：前二人一專為憶，喻彌陀念眾生；一人專忘，喻眾生不念佛；云何此云十方如來耶？答：此有二意：一、即指彌陀一佛。以三世十方，有無量彌陀故。《觀經》云：從下方金光佛剎，乃至上方，光明王佛剎，於其中間，無量塵數，分身無量壽佛，故曰十方如來。二、通指十方諸佛。謂不唯彌陀一佛，悲願如是，即十方如來，憐念亦然，正顯佛佛道同故。

若子逃逝：喻眾生不念佛，墮惡趣，受極苦。雖憶何為：喻佛念眾生，單憶無用，不能成益。問：「逃逝與上專忘，同耶？異耶？」答：不念佛不能見佛，雖遇佛若逢不逢，或見非見，不蒙法益則同；而異者，此人專忘，但是不肯念佛，佛若憶念，或可發心，而能成益。此逃逝，非唯專忘，或且謗佛，以不信招愆，墮落三途，佛雖憶念，亦復何為，即言無益也。如城東老母，佛有意往度，彼不信佛，不願見佛，逃入房中。佛以手指壁，壁如玻璃，復以二手遮其眼，還不願見佛。此足證單憶無益也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "It is like two people, one wholly given to remembering the other, while the other constantly forgets him — such two people, whether they happen to meet or not, whether they see each other or not, remain, in effect, as good as strangers. But should both, instead, remember each other, this shared remembrance growing ever deeper between them, then, life after life, they remain together like a form and its own shadow, never parting from one another."

Commentary: This shows that remembering alone, on one side only, accomplishes nothing. It is like two people in this present world — referring here to close friends or family — one wholly given to remembering, holding this friend or relative constantly in mind, while the other, his own mind given wholly to other concerns, forgets this same friend or relative entirely. "Such two people" refers back to this same pair, one remembering, one forgetting; and so, whether these two happen to meet or not, whether they see each other or not — "happen to meet" and "see each other" speak of the one who remembers; "or not" and "or not" speak of the one who forgets. These two people stand for the Buddha and sentient beings: "remember" here means to hold in mind, recalling without ever forgetting; "recollect" means fixing one's attention upon something, never letting it go. The one wholly given to remembering stands for the Buddha holding sentient beings in mind; the other, who constantly forgets, stands for sentient beings failing to recollect the Buddha. The Buddha's own constant remembrance of sentient beings carries two distinct senses: first, because the Buddha holds this same great vow of compassion, seeing every sentient being as originally one and the same body with himself — he himself having already attained complete awakening and nirvāṇa, while sentient beings remain, even now, caught in the wheel of birth and death, never understanding it — and so he carries forward this great vow of compassion, constantly holding sentient beings in mind; and second, because the Buddha holds this same mind of perfect equality — even at the causal stage, as a bodhisattva, he never once, in a single thought, set sentient beings aside, how much less, then, now that he has become a buddha, viewing every being upon this great earth as though each were his own only child, and so, with this same mind of perfect equality, constantly holds them in mind.

Sentient beings' own failure to recollect the Buddha likewise carries two distinct senses: first, because sentient beings' own obstruction runs so heavy, bound fast by delusion, karma, and suffering, unable to free themselves — unwilling even to cultivate the causes and effects leading to rebirth among gods and humans, how much less, then, this teaching of recollecting the Buddha — and so they fail to recollect him at all; and second, because sentient beings' own wisdom remains so darkened, failing to recognize that this supreme, wondrous Dharma of recollecting the Buddha is capable of severing affliction, understanding birth and death, and accomplishing the buddha-path itself — that recollecting the Buddha in this very life can sever every afflicted, false thought, and, at the moment of death, bring about rebirth there, transcending the wheel of birth and death across the three realms directly, and that, once reborn in that land, one perfectly realizes non-regression and swiftly accomplishes the buddha-path — failing to give rise to this faith, unwilling to vow rebirth there, and so unwilling to recollect the Buddha at all.

"Whether they happen to meet or not, whether they see each other or not": these same two people stand for exactly this — the Buddha holding sentient beings in mind, while sentient beings fail to recollect the Buddha in turn. The Buddha, through the power of this great vow of compassion, constantly holds sentient beings in mind, roaming and transforming this Sahā world, so that sentient beings might happen to meet him, or see him directly — as when Amitābha manifested as Chan Master Fenggan, in Tiantai, within Taizhou, Zhejiang, appearing as an ordinary bhikṣu whom no one recognized at all — this being exactly "happening to meet" amounting to not meeting at all, "seeing" amounting to not seeing at all, entirely because of this lack of any power of recollecting the Buddha, and this lack of any vow to actually seek out Amitābha directly.

That Chan Master Fenggan was Amitābha's own manifestation comes from Hanshan's own words. Fenggan dwelt at Guoqing Monastery on Mount Tiantai, living in the rice-milling room, often riding a tiger in and out of the monastery grounds, and no one there knew who he truly was. One day, Fenggan invited Hanshan and Shide to journey together to Mount Wutai, saying: "come with me, and you are of my own kind; do not come with me, and you are not." Hanshan asked him: "what would we do at Wutai?" Fenggan answered: "pay homage to Mañjuśrī there." Hanshan said: "then you are not of my own kind — I will not go with you." And so Fenggan set out alone, traveling as far as Hangzhou, where he happened to meet a man named Lü Qiuyin, who had waited many years for an official appointment, his own family fallen into poverty — and who had just then been appointed prefect of Taizhou by the provincial government, but had suddenly fallen ill with a headache that no treatment could relieve. Fenggan came specially to visit him, but the gatekeeper turned him away. Fenggan said: "I have come specially to save his very life — why, then, will you not let me see him?" The gatekeeper reported this, and Lü at once agreed to receive him. Fenggan asked: "what ails you?" Lü said: "my head aches as though it would split apart." Fenggan said: "fetch a cup of water, and I will treat you." He then recited a mantra over it, placed the water in the palm of his own hand, and struck Lü's head with it three times — and the pain ceased at once. Lü, deeply grateful, asked after the master's own Dharma name, and which monastery he dwelt in. He answered: "my name is Fenggan; I dwell at Guoqing Monastery, on Mount Tiantai, in Taizhou." Lü asked: "are there others at this monastery whose own attainment of the path equals yours, Master?" Fenggan answered: "I myself have no attainment of the path at all. The eminent monks dwelling there — Hanshan, a manifestation of Mañjuśrī, and Shide, a manifestation of Samantabhadra — both dwell within Guoqing Monastery, roaming and transforming beings in our own land." Lü prepared gifts to thank him, but Fenggan refused them and departed. Three days after taking up his own post, Lü went to Guoqing Monastery to offer incense, and asked the guest-prefect monk there where he might find these two great beings, Hanshan and Shide, so that he might be shown to them and pay his respects. The guest-prefect said: "these two are simply mad monks — what business could Your Excellency have with them?" Lü said: "I wish only to pay them my respects." The guest-prefect said: "then have them summoned here." Lü said: "no — I will go to them myself." And so he was led to the kitchen, where Hanshan and Shide were warming themselves by the stove, talking and laughing together, though no one else could make out what they were saying. The guest-prefect called out to them: "Hanshan! Shide! Come here — His Excellency wishes to speak with you." The moment Lü saw them, he prostrated himself on the ground at once — and the two of them fled. Lü pursued them, all the way to a rocky cliff at Hanshan, where the two vanished inside; Hanshan turned back and called out: "thief! thief! thief! Fenggan has talked too much — we have no business with Amitābha here — why bow to us at all?" Lü hurried to look within the cliff, but they were nowhere to be found. Hanshan and Shide never again appeared after this, and Fenggan, too, never returned to Guoqing Monastery from that time on — for once a sage's own manifestation among humankind has been revealed, he can no longer remain there. Lü, searching the mountain afterward for whatever traces remained, transcribed many poems the two great beings had left behind, upon the rocky cliffs, the bamboo and trees of the mountain, and the walls of nearby villages — every one of them expounding the Buddha-dharma, admonishing the world — and these are published and circulated even now. This account is recorded here to confirm this very same fact: that the Buddha holds sentient beings in mind, while sentient beings fail to recollect the Buddha, and so, whether they happen to meet or not, whether they see each other or not, nothing comes of it at all.

This shows that mutual remembrance, held by both together, is never parted. "Should both, instead, remember each other, this shared remembrance growing ever deeper between them": they can then naturally see and grow close to one another, never departing from each other at all, even, life after life, remaining together like a form and its own shadow — standing for how sentient beings' own recollection of the Buddha matches the Buddha's own remembrance of sentient beings exactly: held in mind for so long, never forgotten, at every time, in every place, the Buddha never departing from the mind, all the way to the very end of one's own life, never once forgotten even then. "Life after life" — that is, from this present life onward, into every future life — remaining just like a form and its own shadow, never diverging, never departing from one another. "All the way... onward" is an abbreviated expression here — not only does one constantly behold the Buddha in this present life, but even after rebirth there, one constantly follows the Buddha in further study, exactly like a form and its own shadow, never departing, never separated. This image of a form and its shadow, never parted, carries two distinct senses: first, it stands for how sentient beings who recollect the Buddha are certain to behold the Buddha in turn, sentient being and Buddha never departing from one another — corresponding to what follows: "recollecting the Buddha and holding him in mind, now or in ages to come, one is certain to behold the Buddha"; and second, it stands for how sentient beings who recollect the Buddha are certain to attain buddhahood in turn — cause and fruit never departing from one another, recollecting the Buddha being the cause, attaining buddhahood being the fruit — exactly as Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva, through this very mind of recollecting the Buddha, attained the patient acceptance of the unborn, and now, in the Land of Bliss, assists Amitābha in spreading his own teaching, awaiting his own succession, in turn, to the buddha King of the Mountain of Universal Light and Merit — that is, recollecting the Buddha is certain to result in attaining buddhahood, cause and fruit never departing from one another at all.

Sūtra: "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions, in their compassion, hold sentient beings in mind exactly as a mother holds her own child in mind. But should the child run away and disappear, what use would the mother's own remembrance be? Yet should the child hold his own mother in mind exactly as the mother holds him in mind, then mother and child, through every life they pass through, are never far apart from one another."

Commentary: The analogy above, of two people, spoke only of friends — a somewhat distant relation still; this present analogy, of mother and child, speaks instead of one's own flesh and blood — a bond closer still. The first five lines here correspond to what came before, showing that remembrance on one side alone accomplishes nothing; the last four lines, likewise corresponding to what came before, show that mutual remembrance, held by both, is never parted. And, further, the first three lines here correspond to the earlier "one wholly given to remembering," while the next two correspond to "the other constantly forgets." The Tathāgata is the mother here; sentient beings are the child. Among all worldly forms of loving affection, none is more urgent and intense than a mother's own love for her child — even when the child will not heed her own teaching, she still holds him in mind, moment after moment, never once letting him go. Should the child grow rebellious, forgetting her own kindness and betraying her own virtue, committing every kind of unfilial act, a mother's own thought of him might, in time, grow faint, her own heart filling with regret and resentment instead — yet the Buddha's own thought of sentient beings surpasses even this: the more severe a being's own rebellion and wrongdoing, the deeper still the Buddha's own thought of him grows. And, further, a mother's own love for her child lasts only for the span of a single lifetime, while the Buddha's own thought of sentient beings is a compassion without end, following them through life after life, never once regressing.

Here it is said that the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, in their compassion, hold sentient beings in mind: "compassion" means pity and sorrow; "hold in mind" means guarding and protecting. Sentient beings, caught so long in the wheel of birth and death, enduring every kind of suffering, are thus held in pity and sorrow by the Buddha; and though caught in birth and death, their own buddha-nature is never lost, and so they are, further, guarded and protected by the Buddha as well. As the Sūtra of Samādhi puts it: "what fills every buddha's own mind is nothing but this same great compassion." This compassion takes as its own object precisely suffering sentient beings — whenever a buddha sees a sentient being caught in suffering and affliction, it is exactly like an arrow piercing his own heart, and he wishes only to draw out this suffering at once — no different at all from a mother's own heart, seeing her own child in pain, holding him in mind in exactly the same way.

One might ask: the analogy above spoke of one person wholly given to remembering, standing for Amitābha holding sentient beings in mind, and one person who constantly forgets, standing for sentient beings failing to recollect the Buddha — why, then, does this present passage speak instead of "the Tathāgatas of the ten directions"? The answer: this carries two distinct senses. First, it may refer to Amitābha alone — since there exist countless Amitābhas throughout the three times and the ten directions alike. As the Contemplation Sūtra puts it: "from the buddha-land of Golden Light below, all the way up to the buddha-land of the King of Light above, and everything in between, there are boundless, countless divided bodies of the buddha Amitāyus" — hence, "the Tathāgatas of the ten directions." Second, it may refer generally to the buddhas of the ten directions as a whole — meaning that it is not Amitābha alone whose own compassionate vow is like this, but that the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, too, hold this very same compassion in mind — showing precisely that every buddha, alike, walks this same path.

"Should the child run away and disappear": this stands for sentient beings failing to recollect the Buddha, falling instead into the lower destinies, enduring the most extreme suffering there. "What use would the mother's own remembrance be": this stands for the Buddha's own remembrance of sentient beings, on his side alone, accomplishing nothing, unable to bring about any benefit at all. One might ask: "is this 'running away and disappearing' the same as 'constantly forgetting' above, or different?" The answer: not recollecting the Buddha, one cannot behold the Buddha — even when actually encountering him, it amounts to not meeting him at all, to not seeing him at all, failing, either way, to receive the Dharma's own benefit — in this, the two are the same. But they differ in this respect: the one who "constantly forgets" simply fails, for now, to recollect the Buddha — and should the Buddha continue to hold him in mind, he may yet, in time, give rise to this very resolve, and benefit may still result. But this one who "runs away and disappears" does not merely forget — he may, further, actively revile the Buddha as well, bringing this very calamity upon himself through his own lack of faith, falling into the three lower destinies — and, however much the Buddha may continue to hold him in mind even then, what use could this possibly be? This is exactly what is meant by "accomplishing nothing at all." As with the old woman east of the city, whom the Buddha wished to liberate, yet who, refusing to believe in the Buddha, unwilling even to see him, fled into her own room — the Buddha pointed his own hand at the wall, and the wall turned transparent like glass; she then covered her own eyes with both hands, still unwilling to see the Buddha even so. This alone is proof enough that remembrance on one side alone accomplishes nothing at all.

"Yet should the child hold his own mother in mind exactly as the mother holds him in mind, then mother and child, through every life they pass through, are never far apart from one another": the first two lines here correspond to the earlier "should both, instead, remember each other, this shared remembrance growing ever deeper between them"; the last two correspond to the earlier "life after life... never parting from one another" — this being exactly mutual remembrance, held by both together, bearing its own fruit. Should the child hold his own mother in mind, exactly as the mother holds her own child in mind, thought matching thought perfectly between them, the mother becoming a truly loving mother, the child becoming a truly filial son — not only will mother and child never part from one another in this present life, but, passing through life after life to come, this same bond between mother and child will never be exhausted, never departing, never growing distant from one another at all. The child here is the sentient being; the mother is the Buddha. Should a sentient being be able to hold the Tathāgata in mind exactly as the Tathāgata holds sentient beings in mind, then sentient being and Buddha resonate together perfectly, and, quite naturally, life after life, one constantly beholds the Buddha, constantly follows him in further study, never growing distant from him at all. Having taught this samādhi of recollecting the Buddha, that buddha then offered these two further images, of distant friends and of mother and child alike, precisely so that sentient beings who recollect the Buddha might see how the very merit of this recollection advances by degrees, growing closer, day by day — not only is beholding the Buddha thereby assured, but the hope of attaining buddhahood itself lies within reach as well!

〖經〗若眾生心，憶佛念佛，現前當來，必定見佛，去佛不遠，不假方便，自得心開。如染香人，身有香氣，此則名曰：香光莊嚴。

〖講〗此下法合。但合雙憶不離成益，不合單憶無益。上二句重在心字，必要心憶心念，以揀口念心不念也。憶則記持不忘，有時間斷，憶即暫念；念則繫緣不散，念念相續，念即常憶。若眾生心常憶佛，心常念佛，將佛印在心中，念茲在茲，時刻不忘。慈雲懺主云：凡涉歷緣務，而內心不忘於佛，謂之憶念。譬如世人，切事繫心，雖經歷語言，去來坐臥，種種作務，而不妨密憶，前事宛然。念佛之心，亦應如是。若或失念，速速攝還，久久成性，任運憶念，不勞作意。又復心起妄念，即便念佛，以真念而敵妄念，妄念自消。若見他人受苦，以念佛心，憐愍於他，願其離苦，如是相續，念佛繫心，能辦一切淨土功德。

此憶佛念佛，有事有理，前雖已明，此更略說：若事憶念，則專心注意，毫無難緣，能念所念，心佛分明，唯此一念，更無餘念，念念相續，成就定力，《起信》所謂：以專意念佛因緣是也。若理憶念，則以妙明心光，圓照自性，能所一如，心佛不二，唯此一緣，更無他緣，湛寂靈明，成就慧力。《起信》所謂：雖念亦無能念可念是也。如上所釋，事理二種憶念，普被三根：若是上智，則專修理憶念；或有鈍根，則專修事憶念；或有中人，則先修事，然後入理；皆隨機宜，未可一概而論。

現前當來，必見定佛：現前見佛者，於現在生中，念佛功純，或於夢中見佛。《法華經》云：若於夢中，見諸如來，坐師子座，圍繞說法等。或於定中見佛，《大集經》，剋期取證，定四十九日，文云：若人專念一方佛，或行或坐，至七七日，現身見佛，即得往生。又《般舟三昧經》定九十日，文云：若人自誓九十日，常行常立，一心繫念，於三昧中，得見阿彌陀佛。《觀經》云：「無量壽佛，相好光明，遍十方界，念佛眾生，攝取不捨，故禪觀中，皆得見也。廬山初祖，遠公大師，一生三睹聖相，至第三次，見佛身遍滿虛空，自知往生時至，遂集眾告知，前後見佛之事，乃謝絕諸緣，精進念佛。果至其時，告別諸弟子，跏趺念佛往生。

亦有現前念佛聲中見佛者，如《往生集》云：昔葛濟之夫人，一心念佛，家貧終日織布，機梭一擲，一聲佛號，頻年行之不倦，其夫信道教，勸修金丹之術；他勸夫學佛，其夫不從，遂各脩其道。一日正在織布，念佛聲中，見彌陀現全身於空中，遂即禮拜，乃喚濟之來看，濟之只見佛上半身。莊嚴光耀，亦即禮拜，由是信從，夫妻同脩淨業，同生淨土。

當來見佛者，或報盡命終，見佛來迎。《佛說阿彌陀經》云：「執持名號，若一日，乃至若七日，一心不亂。其人臨命終時，阿彌陀佛，與諸聖眾，現在其前，是人終時，心不顛倒，即得往生阿彌陀佛極樂國土。」或託質蓮胞，華開見佛，如一生念佛為因，必得往生之果，第八識從頂門而出，化佛來迎，託生九品蓮華，以蓮華為父母。品位高下，乃由一生念佛，勤惰之分，寶池蓮華，並非彌陀變化所作，乃是念佛眾生，自己心力願力所種。十方世界眾生，今天聞說念佛法門，發心念佛，求生淨土，七寶池中，即結一蓮芷，標名其上。念佛精進，華大甚速﹒光色亦好；念佛懈怠，華大亦緩；退心不念，華即枯矣。勤惰纔分，榮枯立見，是為感應冥符妙。命終往生，即生自己之華；往生者眾，不相錯謬，是為勝劣分明妙。此華是為脫凡殼之靈宮，安慧命之神宅。九品華開，隨品位為遲速；蓮華一開，即得見佛聞法，圓證三不退。所受之身，純黃金色，具三十二相，壽命同佛無量。

必定者有三意：一、因果相應：以念佛因，得見佛果，如若往生，早遲必得成佛。二、感應道交：以念力為能感，現身為能應，感應之道，必定不差。三、始本契合：專以理念，念本性佛，始覺功深，本覺顯現，於自心中，見法身佛。此皆一定不易之理，故曰必定見佛。

上科事念，夢中空中，乃至臨終，皆見他佛；理念本覺顯現，見法身佛，乃見自佛。去佛不遠有二釋：一、既得見佛或夢中空中日中，則此去往生彼國，華開見佛，事在不遠，前見佛是化身，此見佛是真身。二、既得理念，見本性佛，從此進修，往生上上品蓮華，經宿即開，面禮彌陀，親聞妙法，頓證無生法忍，則去究竟佛地不遠矣！

不假方便，自得心開者：約事念，則念佛法門，即勝異方便，不假諸餘方便門，以助顯我本性也。尚不假觀像，觀想、參究，何況其他法門。約理念，則念佛即是念心，心佛一如，自他不二；豈離惟心自佛，而假心外他佛作方便耶？自得心開：即理念功成。古德所云。「一念相應一念佛，念念相應念念佛。」自得心佛開發顯現，得成自佛，以念佛心，始本合一，成究竟佛也。

此喻憶佛念佛，必定成佛。如染香之人，身上即有香氣；法中，念佛之人，即得佛之氣分：念佛名即染佛名香，近佛身即染佛身香，開佛心即染佛心香。念佛之人，身心皆染佛香，喻上、中、下三根：上根染佛心香，中根染佛身香，下根染佛名香。下二句出三昧名，亦即法門名。以佛法身香，智慧光，莊嚴自己本覺佛。念佛之心，無相無形，不生不滅，即是法身。此心具足靈覺之性，即是智慧。今則念佛，以佛法身香，智慧光，莊嚴自己本覺心佛，故曰香光莊嚴。《起信論》云：「如世間衣服，實無於香，若人以香，而熏習故，則有香氣；無明染法，實無淨業，但以真如而熏習故，則有淨用。」無明染法者，本覺心佛，藏在無明殼也。彼論明在纏如來藏心，今經喻出纏如來藏心，故云：「心開」，即本覺心佛開顯也。

〖經〗我本因地，以念佛心，入無生忍。今於此界，攝念佛人，歸於淨土。

〖講〗上三句初句因也，二句該因徹果，三句果也，均屬自利，下三句屬利他。我本因地：即自述因地脩行；以念佛心，為本脩因地心。佛是所念，心為能念，此心非第六意識心。世人有謂念佛是口念，非也；即說是意識心念，亦非也。能念之心，是不生滅，圓湛根性真心。以大勢至菩薩，明言都攝六根，淨念相繼，不但說識心念者，非也；即單說意根念者，亦非也。此章是根大法門，若單說意根，則與須菩提，意根法門相濫。都攝六根解在下，此念佛即第一決定義所云：「得元明覺，無生滅性，為因地心，然後圓成，果地脩證」是也。入無生忍者：即依因所感之果也。入、是證入，無生、是所入之理，忍、以慧心安住此理，亦即慧之定也。念佛為能入，此忍為所入。無生之理，始終不異，即不生不滅；迷悟無差，即不垢不淨；生佛平等，即不增不減；證入法忍，地位有殊。如本經第三漸次文云：「一切如來，密圓淨妙，皆現其中。」是人即得無生法忍，謂圓教初住，即得無生法忍，破一品無明，證一分三德，分身百界，八相成道。《仁王經》云：無生忍菩薩，所謂遠不動觀慧。遠、即第七遠地行，不動、即第八不動地，觀慧、即第九善慧地。以此而觀，前後所證之理是一，能證之功行，不無淺深。喻如象、馬、兔，三獸渡河，所渡之河不異，而入水淺深，非無差別。兔子則水面渡過，腳入水中；馬則頭伸水上，身入水中渡過；象則全身入水，由河底行過。後後勝於前前，實教大菩薩，徹法流之源底，大勢至位居等覺，所證法忍，當非淺淺，上述自利，即以自利者，轉以利他。

今於此界，攝念佛人，歸於淨土者：此界、指本土娑婆五濁惡世；淨土、指西方極樂五清淨土。攝者攝受，通於能所，能攝是大勢至，所攝是念佛人；生前則以慈力攝受，令行人念佛之心，堅固不退；臨終則以願力攝受，令行人正念昭彰，接引往生。

此界如旅舍，彼土是家鄉。猶如有人，捨父逃逝，馳走他鄉，飄零孤露疇：菩薩如親友，勸令念佛，即指示歸家道路，併贈以資糧，方能得歸家鄉，親見本生父母。念佛即具信、願、行三資糧，信則念，不信則不念，念具信資；以念佛為因，願求往生之果，念具願資；念念念佛，力行不倦，念具行資。以此念佛，所具信、願、行，即贈以歸家三資糧也。能具信、願、行三資糧者，始名念佛人，始得歸於淨土也。

又念佛，即具三資糧，聞說念佛法門，心不疑貳，謂之信；信已而解，心起樂欲，謂之願；願已而念，心勤精進，謂之行。《彌陀經》云：「若有信者，應當發願」，「執持名號」是也。

又念佛，即具聞、思、脩三慧，聞說佛名，諦信不疑，為聞慧；記憶在懷，恆不忘失，為思慧；持念不輟，無有間斷，為脩慧。《佛地論》云：菩薩履三妙慧，淨土往還，是念佛人，必具三慧，方歸淨土。

〖經〗佛問圓通，我無選擇，都攝六根，淨念相繼，得三摩地，斯為第一。

〖講〗首句牒所問，第五卷前，佛問言：「我今問汝，最初發心，悟十八界，誰為圓通？從何方便，入三摩地？」故此牒所問。次三句敘本因，後二句明所證。我無選擇者：佛前備顯六根功德，囑令詳擇其可入者，吾當發明，令汝增進。我於本因，亦是從根修證，但無選擇。都攝六根：外不擇眼耳等六根之相，內不擇見聞等六根之用；都攝者：唯攝一精明，不令託根緣塵，則一精既攝，六用不行，而六根都攝矣！淨念相繼者：眾念不生曰淨，一心繫佛曰念，念念相繼續也，無有間斷；一念相應，一念佛，念念相應，念念佛；相應、乃心佛一如，即心是佛，即佛是心，念而無念，無念而念，不落空有二邊，全歸中道，即是理一心也。

得三摩地：乃依因感果，由本脩因，證圓通果。梵語三摩地，此云等持，又云等至，等持即定慧均等任持，雙離昏沉掉舉也；至即是到，由定慧平等，能到勝定故。斯為第一者：斯指都攝六根，念佛法門，最為第一。問：「文殊揀選圓通，乃選觀音耳根，偈曰：成就涅槃心，觀世音為最。今念佛法門，何得稱為第一？」答：「若對此方之機，娑婆眾生，耳根利故，所以觀音當選。若對十方通論，念佛法門，都攝六根，橫超三界，直截生死，速證菩提，無有何門可及，故稱第一。卯七勢至根大竟，併前二十三位，丑初諸聖略說竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "If a sentient being's own mind holds the Buddha in mind and recollects him, then, now or in ages to come, that being is certain to behold the Buddha, drawing near to the Buddha before very long — requiring no further skillful means at all, this mind opens of itself. It is like a person steeped in fragrance, whose own body naturally carries this same fragrance with it — this is called being adorned by fragrance and light."

Commentary: What follows now applies the analogy directly to the doctrine — matching only the benefit that comes from mutual remembrance held by both together, not the fruitlessness of remembrance on one side alone. The first two lines here emphasize the word "mind" — one must genuinely hold the Buddha in mind, genuinely recollect him with the mind itself, ruling out mere recitation with the mouth while the mind itself takes no part. "Holding in mind" means recalling without forgetting, yet admitting of occasional interruption — a more occasional recollection; "recollecting" means fixing one's attention without ever letting it scatter, continuing moment after moment without a break — a constant holding in mind. Should a sentient being's own mind constantly hold the Buddha in mind, constantly recollect him, stamping the Buddha directly upon the mind itself, kept there at every single moment, never forgotten for an instant — as Repentance-Master Ciyun put it: "wherever one's own affairs and duties may take him, so long as the inner mind never forgets the Buddha, this is called holding him in mind." It is like a person in this world whose mind remains fixed upon some urgent matter — even while going about speaking, coming and going, sitting or lying down, engaged in every kind of task, this does not prevent this same matter from remaining held secretly in mind, as vividly present as ever. The mind that recollects the Buddha should be exactly the same. Should this recollection ever lapse, one should gather it back at once — and, over time, this becomes second nature, this recollection continuing spontaneously, without the slightest further effort of will required at all. And, further, whenever a false thought arises in the mind, one should recollect the Buddha at once, using this true recollection to counter this false thought directly, and the false thought will dissolve of itself. Should one see another person suffering, one should, with this very mind that recollects the Buddha, extend compassion to them as well, wishing that they, too, might be freed from suffering — continuing on in this way, recollecting the Buddha with the mind held fast upon him, one becomes capable of accomplishing every merit belonging to the Pure Land itself.

This holding the Buddha in mind and recollecting him admits of both actual practice and principle — already explained above, though a further brief account may be given here. Recollection through actual practice means fixing the mind single-mindedly, with not the slightest difficulty in doing so, the mind that recollects and the Buddha recollected both plainly distinct, holding to this single recollection alone, with no other thought at all, each recollection continuing on from the last, thereby accomplishing the power of samādhi itself — exactly what the Awakening of Faith calls "recollecting the Buddha through this single-minded cause and condition." Recollection through principle means using this wondrous, bright light of the mind to illuminate one's own nature perfectly, subject and object one and the same, mind and Buddha not two different things at all, holding to this single object alone, with no other object at all, calm, still, and spiritually bright, thereby accomplishing the power of wisdom itself — exactly what the Awakening of Faith calls "though recollecting, there is, in truth, no one who recollects, and nothing recollected either." As explained above, these two forms of holding the Buddha in mind, through actual practice and through principle, benefit all three roots of capacity equally: one of superior wisdom cultivates recollection through principle exclusively; one of duller capacity cultivates recollection through actual practice exclusively; and one of middling capacity first cultivates through actual practice, and only later enters into principle — each matched to their own particular capacity, not to be generalized as though only one approach applied to everyone alike.

"Now or in ages to come, that being is certain to behold the Buddha": beholding the Buddha in this present life may occur, for one whose own effort in recollecting the Buddha has grown pure, within a dream — as the Lotus Sūtra puts it: "should one, within a dream, see the Tathāgatas seated upon their lion-thrones, surrounded by their own assemblies, teaching the Dharma," and so on; or it may occur within samādhi itself — as the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra describes, setting a fixed period of forty-nine days to seek this very confirmation directly, its own text saying: "should a person recollect a single buddha of a single direction single-mindedly, whether walking or sitting, by the end of these forty-nine days, that buddha will manifest his own body before him directly, and he will thereby attain rebirth there"; or, according to the Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra, setting a period of ninety days instead, its own text saying: "should a person vow to spend ninety days constantly walking or constantly standing, the mind fixed single-mindedly throughout, he will, within this very samādhi, come to behold Amitābha Buddha directly." As the Contemplation Sūtra puts it: "Amitāyus Buddha's own excellent marks and radiant light pervade the worlds of the ten directions, gathering in every sentient being who recollects him, never once letting them go" — hence, within meditative contemplation, one is able to behold him directly in every case. Master Huiyuan, the first patriarch of Mount Lu, beheld this sacred vision three times across his own life — and, the third time, seeing the Buddha's own body filling the whole of empty space, he knew, of himself, that the time of his own rebirth there had come, and so gathered his own assembly to tell them of these visions of the Buddha, before and now, took his leave of every worldly connection, and devoted himself diligently to recollecting the Buddha alone. And, indeed, when the appointed time finally arrived, he bid farewell to his own disciples and, seated in full lotus posture, recollecting the Buddha, passed away and was reborn there.

There are also cases of beholding the Buddha directly, in the very midst of reciting his name in this present life — as the Wǎngshēng Jí recounts: once, the wife of Ge Jizhi recollected the Buddha single-mindedly; though her own family was poor, she wove cloth all day long, and, with every single throw of her own shuttle, recited the Buddha's name once — continuing this practice, year after year, without ever growing weary of it. Her own husband, a follower of Daoist teaching, urged her instead to cultivate the alchemical art of the golden elixir; she, in turn, urged him to study the Buddha-dharma — yet he would not follow her, and so each continued to cultivate their own separate path. One day, while she was weaving, in the very midst of reciting the Buddha's name, she beheld Amitābha's own entire body appear in the sky before her, and at once bowed down in reverence, calling out to her husband, Jizhi, to come and see — yet Jizhi could see only the Buddha's own upper body, adorned and radiant, and likewise bowed down in reverence at once. From this time on, he too came to believe, and husband and wife together cultivated this same pure karma, and together attained rebirth in the Pure Land.

Beholding the Buddha in ages to come may occur when one's own karmic retribution is exhausted and life comes to its end, the Buddha appearing to welcome one directly. As the Buddha Speaks of Amitābha Sūtra puts it: "holding fast to this name, whether for a single day, or continuing up to seven days, the mind undivided and undisturbed throughout — when this person's own life comes to its end, Amitābha Buddha, together with his own assembly of sages, will appear directly before him, and this person, his own mind free of any confusion at the very moment of death, will attain rebirth at once in Amitābha Buddha's own Land of Bliss." Or it may occur through being reborn within a lotus womb, this lotus opening to reveal the Buddha directly — for one who has taken a whole lifetime of recollecting the Buddha as his own cause is certain to attain this same rebirth as his own fruit: the eighth consciousness departing through the very crown of the head, a transformation-body of the Buddha coming to welcome him, and his own rebirth entrusted to a lotus blossom, among the nine grades, this lotus itself serving as his own father and mother. The relative height of one's own grade depends entirely on how diligently or how idly one recollected the Buddha across this same lifetime — the lotus blossoms within this treasure-pool are, in no sense, something Amitābha himself fabricates or transforms into being, but are, rather, planted entirely by the very power of mind and vow belonging to the sentient being who recollects the Buddha. The very moment a sentient being, anywhere throughout the worlds of the ten directions, hears this teaching of recollecting the Buddha and resolves to recollect him, seeking rebirth in the Pure Land, a single lotus bud at once forms within this seven-jeweled pool, that being's own name marked upon it. Where recollection of the Buddha is diligent, this blossom grows large very quickly, its own color bright and radiant; where recollection is lax, this blossom grows large only slowly; and should one's resolve regress and one cease to recollect the Buddha at all, this blossom withers away entirely. The very moment diligence and idleness diverge, flourishing and withering follow at once — this is the wondrous, hidden correspondence between cause and effect. And when this life comes to its end and rebirth follows, one is reborn directly within one's own blossom, already formed — and, however many are reborn there at once, never once confused or mismatched with one another — this is the wondrous, plain distinction between higher and lower grades alike. This lotus blossom is exactly the very palace where one sheds one's own ordinary shell, the very spiritual dwelling where one's own life of wisdom finds its rest. As these nine grades of blossom open, some sooner, some later, according to one's own grade — yet the very moment any such blossom opens, one beholds the Buddha and hears the Dharma directly, perfectly realizing the three kinds of non-regression at once. The body one receives there is the color of pure gold, complete with all thirty-two major marks, one's own lifespan there as boundless as the Buddha's own.

"Certain" here carries three distinct senses: first, cause and fruit corresponding to one another exactly — taking the cause of recollecting the Buddha, one attains the fruit of beholding him in turn, and, should one attain rebirth there, whether sooner or later, buddhahood itself is certain to follow. Second, the mutual resonance of cause and response — this power of recollection serving as what draws forth the response, this manifested body serving as the response itself, and this path of mutual resonance between them is certain, without the slightest deviation. And third, the accord of original and initial enlightenment — devoting oneself exclusively to recollection through principle, recollecting the buddha of one's own original nature, this initial enlightenment's own effort growing deep, until original enlightenment itself stands revealed, and one beholds, within one's own mind, the very buddha of the dharma-body directly. All of this is a fixed, unchanging principle — hence, "certain to behold the Buddha."

The section above, on recollection through actual practice, describes beholding another buddha — whether in a dream, in the sky, or at the very moment of death; recollection through principle, by contrast, describes original enlightenment itself standing revealed, beholding the buddha of the dharma-body directly, that is, beholding one's own buddha. "Drawing near to the Buddha before very long" admits of two readings: first, having already beheld the Buddha — whether in a dream, in the sky, or in broad daylight — one's own eventual rebirth there, this lotus opening to reveal the Buddha directly, now lies close at hand; the earlier beholding of the Buddha was of his manifestation-body, while this present beholding is of his true body itself. Second, having already attained recollection through principle, beholding the buddha of one's own nature directly, advancing in cultivation from this point onward, one is reborn within the very highest grade of lotus, which opens the very same night, beholding Amitābha face to face, personally hearing this wondrous Dharma directly, suddenly realizing the forbearance of the unborn nature of all things — and so one draws near, before very long, to the very ground of ultimate buddhahood itself!

"Requiring no further skillful means at all, this mind opens of itself": in terms of recollection through actual practice, this teaching of recollecting the Buddha is itself the most excellent, extraordinary skillful means of all, requiring no other skillful gate whatsoever to help reveal one's own nature — not even needing to contemplate an image, or visualize the Buddha, or investigate a point of inquiry directly, how much less any other method besides! In terms of recollection through principle, recollecting the Buddha is exactly recollecting the mind itself, mind and Buddha one and the same, self and other not two different things at all — how, then, could one ever depart from this mind-only buddha of one's own, borrowing some other buddha outside the mind as a mere skillful means instead? "This mind opens of itself" is exactly recollection through principle bearing its own fruit. As the ancients put it: "a single moment's recollection in accord with the Buddha is itself a single moment's buddhahood; recollection after recollection in accord with the Buddha is itself buddhahood after buddhahood." This mind, this Buddha, opening and becoming manifest of itself, one thereby accomplishes one's own buddhahood — through this very mind that recollects the Buddha, original and initial enlightenment become one, and ultimate buddhahood itself is accomplished.

This offers an analogy for how holding the Buddha in mind and recollecting him is certain to result in buddhahood itself. Just as a person steeped in fragrance naturally carries this same fragrance upon his own body, so too, within this teaching, does a person who recollects the Buddha naturally come to share in the Buddha's own qualities: reciting the Buddha's own name is steeping oneself in the fragrance of the Buddha's own name; drawing near the Buddha's own body is steeping oneself in the fragrance of the Buddha's own body; opening the Buddha's own mind is steeping oneself in the fragrance of the Buddha's own mind. One who recollects the Buddha thus comes to have both body and mind steeped in this same fragrance of the Buddha — standing for all three roots of capacity, superior, middling, and lesser: the superior capacity steeped in the fragrance of the Buddha's own mind, the middling capacity in the fragrance of the Buddha's own body, and the lesser capacity in the fragrance of the Buddha's own name. The last two lines here give the very name of this samādhi, likewise the name of this Dharma-gate itself: adorning one's own original enlightenment, buddha as one already is, with the very fragrance of the Buddha's own dharma-body and the very light of his own wisdom. The mind that recollects the Buddha, formless, without any mark at all, unborn and unceasing, is exactly the dharma-body itself; and this same mind, complete with the nature of spiritual awareness, is exactly wisdom itself. Now, recollecting the Buddha in this way, adorning one's own original, enlightened mind and buddha with the Buddha's own dharma-body's fragrance and his own wisdom's light — hence, "adorned by fragrance and light." As the Awakening of Faith puts it: "just as ordinary worldly clothing carries, in truth, no fragrance of its own at all, and yet, should someone perfume it with incense, it comes to carry this fragrance — so too does the defiled dharma of ignorance carry, in truth, no pure activity of its own at all, and yet, perfumed by suchness itself, it comes to carry this same pure function." This "defiled dharma of ignorance" is exactly this same buddha of original enlightenment, one's own very mind, hidden away within the shell of ignorance itself. That treatise makes plain the tathāgatagarbha's own mind, still entangled within; this present sūtra illustrates the tathāgatagarbha's own mind, freed from this same entanglement — hence it speaks of "this mind opens of itself," exactly this same buddha of original enlightenment, one's own very mind, opening and becoming manifest at last.

Sūtra: "At my own causal stage, I took up this very mind of recollecting the Buddha, and thereby entered the patient acceptance of the unborn. Now, in this present world, I gather in those who recollect the Buddha, and lead them home to the Pure Land."

Commentary: Of these first three lines, the first states his cause; the second encompasses both cause and fruit together; the third states his fruit — all three, alike, belonging to the benefit of himself; the last three lines belong to the benefit of others. "At my own causal stage" recounts his own cultivation at the very causal stage itself — taking up this mind of recollecting the Buddha as his own root, causal-stage mind. The Buddha is what is recollected; the mind is what does the recollecting — and this mind is not the sixth, discriminating consciousness at all. Some in the world hold that recollecting the Buddha is merely a matter of reciting with the mouth — this is mistaken; and even to say it is a matter of recollecting with the discriminating mind of consciousness is likewise mistaken. The very mind capable of this recollection is the unborn, unceasing, perfectly calm, true mind of the sense-faculty's own nature itself. Since Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva speaks explicitly of gathering in all six sense-faculties together, this pure recollection continuing without a break, it would be mistaken to say this refers only to recollection through consciousness alone — and equally mistaken to say it refers only to recollection through the mind-faculty in isolation. This present chapter belongs to the Dharma-gate of "the great element of the faculties" — were it to speak only of the mind-faculty alone, it would be indistinguishable from Subhūti's own Dharma-gate of the mind-faculty discussed earlier. The meaning of "gathering in all six sense-faculties together" is explained further below; this recollecting of the Buddha is exactly what the first decisive meaning described: "attaining this original, bright awakening's own unborn, unceasing nature, take this as the causal-stage mind — only then can cultivation and realization at the fruit-stage be perfectly accomplished." "Entered the patient acceptance of the unborn": this is exactly the fruit brought about by this same cause. "Entered" means realized directly; "the unborn" is the very principle entered into; "acceptance" means the mind of wisdom abiding securely within this same principle — that is, exactly the very samādhi of wisdom itself. Recollecting the Buddha is what enters; this very forbearance is what is entered into. The principle of the unborn remains, from beginning to end, never different from itself — that is, unborn and unceasing; delusion and awakening make no difference to it at all — that is, neither defiled nor pure; sentient being and Buddha are equal within it — that is, neither increasing nor decreasing; yet the very stage at which one realizes and enters this forbearance still differs from one practitioner to the next. As this very sūtra's own passage on the three gradual stages puts it: "every Tathāgata's own secret, perfect, pure, and wondrous reality manifests within this very stage" — one who reaches this point attains, at once, the forbearance of the unborn nature of all things — that is, the first abiding of the perfect teaching, where this same forbearance is attained directly, one portion of ignorance broken through, one portion of the three virtues realized, dividing one's own body across a hundred realms, manifesting the eight aspects of a buddha's own life. As the Sūtra of Humane Kings puts it: "the bodhisattva of the patient acceptance of the unborn" refers to what is called "the distant, unmoving wisdom of contemplation" — "distant" being exactly the seventh ground, "far-traveling"; "unmoving" being exactly the eighth ground, "immovability"; and "wisdom of contemplation" being exactly the ninth ground, "skillful wisdom." Considered in this light, the underlying principle realized, earlier or later, remains one and the same — yet the actual depth of practice by which it is realized certainly does differ. It is like an elephant, a horse, and a rabbit, three animals crossing the very same river: the river they cross is the same river throughout, and yet how deeply each enters the water differs considerably — the rabbit crosses along the surface, its feet barely touching the water; the horse stretches its head above the water, its body passing through the water itself; and the elephant submerges its entire body, walking along the very riverbed below. Each surpasses the one before it in turn — and a great bodhisattva of the real teaching, penetrating all the way to the very source and depths of the Dharma's own current, reaches the very bottom of this same river. Mahāsthāmaprāpta, whose own rank is that of equal enlightenment, must have realized this same forbearance at a stage far from shallow indeed. Having recounted his own benefit above, he now turns this same benefit to himself into a benefit for others as well.

"Now, in this present world, I gather in those who recollect the Buddha, and lead them home to the Pure Land": "this present world" refers to our own land, this Sahā world of the five turbidities; "the Pure Land" refers to the western Land of Bliss, pure and undefiled in all five respects. "Gather in" means to receive and hold — applying to both the one who gathers and those gathered in alike: the one who gathers in is Mahāsthāmaprāpta himself; those gathered in are those who recollect the Buddha. During their own lifetime, he gathers them in through the power of loving-kindness, so that the practitioner's own mind of recollecting the Buddha remains firm and never regresses; and at the moment of death, he gathers them in through the power of his own vow, so that the practitioner's own right mindfulness shines forth clearly, and he is thereby welcomed and led to rebirth there.

This present world is like a traveler's inn; that other land is one's own true home. It is like a person who has abandoned his own father and fled, wandering off to some other land, drifting and exposed with nowhere to turn — and this bodhisattva is like a close friend, urging him to recollect the Buddha, that is, pointing out to him the very road home, and, further, giving him provisions for the journey, so that he might finally return to his own homeland and behold his own birth parents once more. Recollecting the Buddha itself already carries within it the three provisions of faith, vow, and practice: faith gives rise to recollection, and without faith there is no recollection at all — recollection thus already carries the provision of faith; taking recollection of the Buddha as the cause, one vows to seek the fruit of rebirth there — recollection thus already carries the provision of vow; and reciting the Buddha's name moment after moment, practicing diligently without ever growing weary — recollection thus already carries the provision of practice as well. This recollection of the Buddha, complete with faith, vow, and practice together, is exactly this same gift of three provisions for the journey home. Only one complete with these three provisions of faith, vow, and practice together may properly be called one who recollects the Buddha, and only such a one truly attains rebirth in the Pure Land at all.

And, further, recollecting the Buddha already carries within it these same three provisions: hearing this teaching of recollecting the Buddha taught, and holding no doubt about it in one's own mind at all, is called faith; having believed, and thereby coming to understand it, giving rise to a mind that delights in and aspires toward it, is called vow; and, having vowed, then actually recollecting the Buddha, the mind diligent and vigorous throughout, is called practice. As the Amitābha Sūtra puts it: "if there is one who has faith, he should give rise to this vow" and "hold fast to this name" — exactly this.

And, further, recollecting the Buddha already carries within it these same three kinds of wisdom, of hearing, reflection, and cultivation: hearing the Buddha's own name and believing it thoroughly, without the slightest doubt, is the wisdom of hearing; holding it in mind, constantly, never once forgetting it, is the wisdom of reflection; and continuing this recollection without ever letting it lapse, without a single interruption, is the wisdom of cultivation. As the Buddha-Bhūmi-śāstra puts it: "a bodhisattva walks these same three wondrous wisdoms in journeying to and from the Pure Land" — one who recollects the Buddha must, in exactly the same way, be complete with these same three kinds of wisdom before he can attain rebirth there at all.

Sūtra: "As the Buddha now asks after perfect penetration: without any need for further selection at all, simply gathering in all six sense-faculties together at once, this pure recollection continuing without a break, and thereby attaining samādhi, is foremost."

Commentary: The first line here restates the question originally posed — earlier in fascicle 5, the Buddha asked: "let me now ask you: at your very first resolve, awakening to the eighteen elements, which one brought you to perfect penetration? And by what skillful means did you enter samādhi?" — hence this restatement of the original question. The next three lines recount his own original cause; the last two make plain what he himself has realized. "Without any need for further selection at all": the Buddha had earlier displayed the merit of each of the six sense-faculties in full detail, instructing everyone to choose carefully whichever one they might enter through, promising to make this plain for them, so that they might advance — yet I, too, at my own causal stage, though likewise cultivating and realizing through the sense-faculty, required no such selection at all. "Gathering in all six sense-faculties together": outwardly, making no selection at all among the appearance of the six sense-faculties — the eye, the ear, and the rest; inwardly, making no selection at all among the function of these six faculties — seeing, hearing, and the rest. "Gathering in together" means gathering in only this single, essential brightness alone, no longer letting it rely on any single faculty to condition any single object — and once this single, essential brightness is thus gathered in, the function of all six faculties ceases to operate separately at all, and all six faculties are, in this very way, gathered in together at once! "This pure recollection continuing without a break": freedom from every stray thought is called "pure"; the mind fixed single-mindedly upon the Buddha is called "recollection"; "continuing" means this recollection continuing on, moment after moment, without a single interruption. A single moment's recollection in accord with the Buddha is itself a single moment's buddhahood; recollection after recollection in accord with the Buddha is itself buddhahood after buddhahood — "in accord" here meaning exactly mind and Buddha becoming one and the same, this very mind being itself the Buddha, this very Buddha being itself the mind, recollection that is itself no recollection at all, no recollection that is nonetheless itself recollection — falling neither into the extreme of emptiness nor of existence, returning entirely to the middle way itself — this is exactly this single, undivided mind realized through principle itself.

"Attaining samādhi": this is exactly the fruit brought about by this very cause — from this root cause of cultivation, realizing the fruit of perfect penetration itself. The Sanskrit samādhi means "holding both equally," also rendered "reaching both equally" — "holding equally" meaning holding calm and wisdom in perfect, sustained balance together, doubly free of both torpor and agitation at once; "reaching" meaning arriving there directly — through this same equal balance of calm and wisdom, one is thereby able to arrive at this most excellent samādhi itself. "Is foremost": this points to gathering in all six sense-faculties together, this very teaching of recollecting the Buddha, as foremost of all. One might ask: "when Mañjuśrī selects among all twenty-five approaches to perfect penetration, he chooses Avalokiteśvara's own ear-faculty, his verse declaring: 'for accomplishing the mind of nirvāṇa, Avalokiteśvara's own method is best' — how, then, can this teaching of recollecting the Buddha also be called foremost?" The answer: matched to the particular capacity of this world — the sentient beings of this Sahā world having the sharpest ear-faculty of all — Avalokiteśvara's own method is rightly selected. But considered generally, applicable throughout the ten directions at once, this teaching of recollecting the Buddha, gathering in all six sense-faculties together, transcending the three realms directly, cutting straight through birth and death itself, swiftly realizing bodhi — there is no other gate whatsoever that can compare to it, and so it, too, is rightly called foremost." This closes the seventh testimony, Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own perfect penetration through the great element of the faculties, and, together with it, closes the first section as a whole — the brief testimonies of all twenty-four sages.
