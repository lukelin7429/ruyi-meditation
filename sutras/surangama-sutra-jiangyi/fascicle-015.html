The One Who Observes the Observer

Fascicle 14 closed the brief testimonies of the first twenty-four sages. Now the twenty-fifth and last rises: Avalokiteśvara, testifying at far greater length than anyone before. This fascicle carries the ancient Buddha's own teaching of hearing, reflecting, and cultivating; the six-stage unraveling of the knot, from the sound first entered all the way to still cessation appearing of itself — including Yuanying's own remarkable account of his years spent working a huatō through three Chan retreats; the two roots from which every wondrous function then arises; the thirty-two bodies Avalokiteśvara manifests to reach whatever being calls out; and the fourteen kinds of fearlessness this same samādhi bestows.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 14 closed with the brief testimonies of the first twenty-four sages — six through the sense-objects, five through the sense-faculties, six through the six consciousnesses, seven through the great elements. Now the twenty-fifth and, by the sūtra's own later verdict, best-suited of all rises to speak: Avalokiteśvara. Where every sage before this one gave a relatively brief account, Yuanying notes three reasons Avalokiteśvara's own testimony unfolds at such length — the beings of this particular world have the sharpest ear-faculty of all; the ear-faculty had already been quietly marked out, several fascicles earlier, as the very root of perfect penetration; and Ānanda himself, prone to broad learning without steady cultivation, needs the sequential mechanics of the practice set out in careful, unhurried detail. Yuanying divides Avalokiteśvara's full testimony into four parts: declaring the ancient Buddha's own teaching, the sequential untying of the knot in cultivation and realization, elaborating in detail how wondrous function arises from this same essence (itself split into the two roots underlying that function, and three further sections — the thirty-two manifested bodies, the fourteen kinds of fearlessness, and the four inconceivable virtues), and finally the close of the testimony proper. This fascicle carries the first two parts in full, and, within the third, the two roots and the first two of its three sections — the thirty-two bodies and the fourteen kinds of fearlessness. The four inconceivable virtues, the close of the testimony, and Mañjuśrī's own verse selecting this method as best suited to this world will follow in fascicle 16.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. The companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 6 already carries a full English translation of Avalokiteśvara's opening declaration and the six-stage sequential untying passage — word for word the same text Yuanying quotes here — and that existing translation is reused verbatim throughout this fascicle's sūtra quotations for those two passages. The thirty-two manifested bodies and fourteen kinds of fearlessness, by contrast, appear there only as a representative excerpt and a compact list; here, quoted in full for the first time, they are translated fresh, aligned to that project's own established terminology. All of Yuanying's own commentary — including his lengthy, personal account of his own years of Chan practice, inserted directly into the commentary on "entering the stream and letting go of what was heard" — is new.

Declaring the Ancient Buddha's Own Teaching 寅初 陳白古佛授法

Yuanying's outline: "At that time" is exactly the moment right after Mahāsthāmaprāpta finished setting forth this own testimony on perfect penetration through the great element of the faculties, by way of recollecting the Buddha. Every sage before this one gave only a brief account; Avalokiteśvara alone, coming last, sets it forth at such length — for three reasons. First, the beings of this particular world have the sharpest ear-faculty of all, exactly as Mañjuśrī's own verse selecting among the sense-faculties puts it: "the true substance of teaching in this land lies in purity found through sound and hearing; to take hold of samādhi, one truly enters through hearing itself." Second, because of hearing the Buddha's own teaching — in the chapter on awakening and entering perfect penetration, several fascicles earlier — the ear-faculty had already been quietly selected as the very root of perfect penetration; and so this present passage cites the ancient Buddha named Avalokiteśvara, teaching this very method of hearing, reflecting, and cultivating, which is exactly to take up the ear-faculty as one's own starting point. Third, because Ānanda leans toward broad learning and has not diligently cultivated the power of samādhi, this testimony sets forth, in careful detail, the sequential method of cultivation and realization, of untying the knot stage by stage, so that Ānanda might, by this very road, find his own way home — as the verse below puts it, "why not turn that hearing which retains the Buddha's teaching back upon your own hearing?" It is for this reason that the account unfolds so unhurriedly, at such length.

On the name itself: "Observing" (read in the falling tone) the world's sounds means exactly this: the wisdom that observes taking the object observed as its own object. The name carries two distinct grounds. First, read in terms of self-benefiting cultivation at the causal stage: relying on the ear-faculty's own original awakening, the very principle-substance of the nature of hearing, one gives rise to the functioning wisdom of initial awakening's own contemplation — not flowing outward to condition sense-objects, but entering the stream to illuminate this nature directly, contemplating who, exactly, is the one hearing the world's sounds. Here, the very nature of hearing that hears is itself taken as the object observed — as the closing lines answering the question on perfect penetration, further on, put it: "through the ear-faculty, by the perfectly illuminating samādhi ... through the mark of entering the stream, I attained samādhi." Hence that ancient Buddha, the Tathāgata, praised me for so excellently attaining this very gateway of perfect penetration, and, in the midst of the great assembly, conferred on me the prophecy of my own name, Avalokiteśvara.

Second, read in terms of responding to capacities at the fruit stage, for the benefit of others: as the Universal Gate chapter of the Lotus Sūtra has it, the Buddha, answering a question posed by the Bodhisattva Akṣayamati — by what causes and conditions is Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva so named? — replies: should the boundless sentient beings of the ten directions, suffering every affliction, single-mindedly recite Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva's own name, the bodhisattva observes their very voices at that very moment, and all of them attain liberation. Here, what is observed is exactly the voice of worldly sentient beings reciting the bodhisattva's own name, and the bodhisattva, in turn, seeks out that voice to rescue them from suffering — hence the name Avalokiteśvara, "the one who observes the world's sounds." The very wisdom that observes is one and the same throughout; only the object observed differs. And so, continuing directly on from Mahāsthāmaprāpta, Avalokiteśvara rose at once from their own seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and, looking up, said to the Buddha:

Chinese Source

〖經〗世尊！憶念我昔，無數恆河沙劫，於時有佛，出現於世，名觀世音。我於彼佛，發菩提心，彼佛教我，從聞、思、修入三摩地。

〖講〗憶即記憶，念即思念，乃迴憶追念，過去無數劫前之事。此是菩薩所得，三明中宿命智明，劫以恆河沙稱，極言過去時之久遠也。於、即在義，在彼之時，有一佛出現於世間。佛為一大事因緣故出現於世；為欲開示眾生，本具佛之知見故，出現於世；為欲令眾生，悟入佛之知見故，出現於世。彼佛亦名觀世音，或因中亦由耳根修證故，或鑑機宜，當從耳根得入故，以是立名，將自行之法，輾轉以化他也。

佛教從聞、思、修，入三摩地，即開示悟入佛之知見，開示眾生，耳根聞性，即是不生不滅之佛性，此佛性即本具佛之知見。令眾生從聞、思、修；聞即聞佛開示、悟明本有佛性，為因地心，依悟而起思修，而得證入。本有佛性是正因，悟明為了因，思修屬緣因。緣了有功，正因方顯，得入佛之知見，此為彼佛出世之大因緣也。

我於彼佛，發菩提心：此菩薩自述，最初發心。彼佛即觀世音佛，梵語菩提，此翻為道。發菩提心：即發大道心，不求人天福報，聲聞緣覺，乃至權乘諸位菩薩，惟依最上乘，發菩提心，即上求無上菩提道之心也。

梵語菩提，又翻為覺；覺有三義：本覺、始覺、究竟覺。本覺即眾生本有之佛性，一切眾生，本來是佛，迷而不覺，將本覺佛性，埋沒於五陰煩惱之中。今始覺悟，雖迷不失，依此始覺智，發心勤求究竟覺之佛道，是謂發無上菩提心。菩提心、為心中之王，菩薩修行，此心為先，若不發菩提心，一切萬行，無從建立，《華嚴經》云：忘失菩提心，修諸善法，是名魔業。昔有菩薩，往昔遇佛，己發菩提心，後在世間修行，將前所發菩提心忘失，並不記憶，如是所脩世出世善，皆名魔業。問：出世善法，何以亦為魔業？答：本經五十種陰魔，聲聞、緣覺，亦列其中，即此可知，皆由不發菩提心故；忘失尚爾，況不發乎？

菩提心、亦即《起信論》三心：一者直心，正念真如法故。此念之所以為正者，不著二邊，起智觀照真如正理，即契菩提心體。二者深心，樂修一切諸善行故，好樂修習世出世間自利利他諸善行。三者大悲心，欲拔一切眾生苦故，以平等大悲心，拔除一切眾生分段變易二生死苦，此二皆發菩提心用，今發此心，為求無上菩提也。菩提心最為貴重，初發即如王子處胎，貴壓群臣，諸佛護念，萬聖加被。《華嚴百喻》，未足以盡其盛德，故我於彼佛，先發此心，以為因地心也。

彼佛教我，從聞、思、修者；此明秉受法門，既發大心，須求佛示，彼佛即指觀世音佛，教我從聞、思、修三慧下手；此之三慧，不同常途，常途則以聞經解義為聞慧，其體即耳識，與耳家同時意識，所發之勝解；思修亦即獨頭意識，將所聞之聲教，思惟修習；此皆不離生滅識心，識心為圓通之障礙。本經以捨識用根為要旨，故三慧不同常途。

今此聞慧，即從耳根聞性妙理，所起始覺妙智，不聞所聞之聲塵，但聞能聞之聞性；思慧、即正智觀察，能聞者是誰，不著空、有二邊，一味反聞聞自性；修慧、即如幻聞熏聞修，念念旋元自歸，伏歸元真，發本明耀，解六結越三空，破五陰超五濁，全憑無分別智，反聞之功。

入三摩地：此即即慧之定，由聞教信解，而起修證。有修中三摩地，即從根解結工夫；有證中三摩地，即寂滅現前境界。此有入字，乃六結盡解，證入圓通之三摩地；亦即阿難所請三名中之妙三摩，經題中了義修證也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "World-Honored One, I recall that in ages past, numberless as the sands of the Ganges, a buddha appeared in the world named Avalokiteśvara. Under that buddha I resolved on bodhi, and that buddha taught me to enter samādhi through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating."

Commentary: "Recall" is exactly remembrance, and "think of" is exactly reflection — together, calling to mind and pursuing in memory events from countless kalpas in the past. This is precisely the bodhisattva's own attainment of the knowledge of former lives, one of the three insights; measuring these kalpas by the sands of the Ganges speaks, in the most emphatic terms, of just how distant this past truly is. "In" that time carries exactly the sense of "at" — at that very time, a single buddha appeared in the world. A buddha appears in the world for the sake of one great cause and condition alone: to open and reveal to sentient beings the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, which they already possess in full; and to bring sentient beings to awaken to and enter this same knowledge and vision. That buddha, too, bore the name Avalokiteśvara — whether because he, too, at his own causal stage, cultivated and realized through the ear-faculty, or because, discerning the capacities of his own age, he judged that entry through the ear-faculty was best suited to it, and so took this very name for himself, handing down his own method of practice, in turn, to transform others.

That buddha taught entry into samādhi through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating — this is exactly opening, revealing, awakening to, and entering the Buddha's own knowledge and vision: opening and revealing to sentient beings that the ear-faculty's own nature of hearing is exactly the unborn, unceasing buddha-nature itself, and that this buddha-nature is exactly the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, already possessed in full. Having sentient beings proceed through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating: "hearing" means hearing the Buddha's own instruction and awakening clearly to this buddha-nature already possessed, taking this as the causal-stage mind; and, relying on this very awakening, giving rise to reflection and cultivation, one thereby attains realization and entry. This buddha-nature already possessed is the direct cause; awakening clearly to it is the perfecting cause; reflection and cultivation belong to the conditioning cause. Once the conditioning and perfecting causes have done their work, the direct cause stands revealed of itself, and one attains entry into the Buddha's own knowledge and vision — this is exactly the great cause and condition behind that buddha's own appearance in the world.

"Under that buddha I resolved on bodhi" is the bodhisattva's own account of their very first resolve. "That buddha" is exactly the Buddha Avalokiteśvara. The Sanskrit bodhi translates as "the path." To resolve on bodhi is exactly to resolve on this great path — seeking neither the blessings of humans or gods, nor the fruits of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, nor even the ranks of a bodhisattva of the provisional vehicles, but relying solely on the very highest vehicle, resolving on bodhi — that is, the mind that seeks, above, the unsurpassed path of bodhi itself.

The Sanskrit bodhi is also translated "awakening," and awakening carries three senses: original awakening, initial awakening, and ultimate awakening. Original awakening is exactly the buddha-nature every sentient being already possesses — every sentient being is, originally, already a buddha, only deluded and unawakened, this original, awakened buddha-nature buried beneath the five aggregates and their afflictions. Now, beginning to awaken, though deluded, nothing is actually lost — and, relying on this very wisdom of initial awakening, one resolves and diligently seeks the buddha-path of ultimate awakening: this is exactly what is meant by resolving on unsurpassed bodhi. The bodhi-mind is the very king among minds; in a bodhisattva's own practice, this mind comes first of all — without resolving on bodhi, no practice whatsoever can ever be established. As the Avataṃsaka Sūtra puts it: "to forget and lose the bodhi-mind, and yet cultivate every other wholesome dharma, is called the work of Māra." There was once a bodhisattva who, encountering a buddha in ages past, had already resolved on bodhi — yet, practicing afterward in the world, forgot this earlier resolve entirely, retaining no memory of it at all, and so every mundane or transcendent wholesome deed cultivated from then on was called, alike, the work of Māra. One might ask: how could even a transcendent, world-surpassing wholesome dharma be called the work of Māra? The answer: this very sūtra's own fifty states of demonic interference lists the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha paths among them as well — and from this alone one may know that this is entirely because the bodhi-mind was never resolved on. If even forgetting it brings this result, how much more so never resolving on it at all!

The bodhi-mind is also exactly the three minds of the Awakening of Faith: first, the straightforward mind, because it holds true, correct mindfulness of suchness itself — and what makes this mindfulness correct is precisely that it clings to neither of the two extremes, giving rise to the wisdom that contemplates the true principle of suchness directly, exactly according with the very substance of the bodhi-mind. Second, the profound mind, because it delights in cultivating every wholesome practice — taking joy in cultivating every mundane and transcendent practice that benefits both oneself and others. Third, the mind of great compassion, because it wishes to draw out every sentient being's suffering — with this same equal, great compassionate mind, drawing out every sentient being's suffering, both the suffering of birth-and-death by fixed portions and the suffering of birth-and-death by inconceivable transformation. These latter two are both the functioning of the resolve on bodhi; resolving on this mind now is exactly seeking unsurpassed bodhi itself. The bodhi-mind is the most precious thing of all — the moment it is first resolved on is exactly like a prince still in the womb, already outranking every minister in the court, watched over by every buddha, blessed by every sage. Even a hundred analogies drawn from the Avataṃsaka Sūtra could not exhaust its full magnificence — and so, under that buddha, I first resolved on this very mind, taking it as my own causal-stage mind.

"That buddha taught me hearing, reflecting, and cultivating" makes plain how this Dharma-gate was received: having already resolved on this great mind, one must still seek the Buddha's own instruction — "that buddha" pointing exactly to the Buddha Avalokiteśvara, who taught me to take up these three kinds of wisdom, of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, as my own starting point. These three kinds of wisdom are not what is ordinarily meant by the same terms. In ordinary usage, the wisdom of hearing means hearing a sūtra recited and understanding its meaning — its very substance being exactly the ear-consciousness together with the mental consciousness accompanying it at the same moment, and the superior understanding this gives rise to; reflection and cultivation, likewise, are exactly the independently operating mental consciousness, taking the sound-teaching once heard and thinking it over, practicing it further. All of this, without exception, never departs from the arising-and-ceasing discriminating mind of consciousness — and this very discriminating mind is itself the obstruction to perfect penetration. This sūtra takes discarding consciousness and relying on the sense-faculty as its own central purpose, and so these three kinds of wisdom are not what is ordinarily meant by the same terms.

Here, this wisdom of hearing is exactly the wondrous wisdom of initial awakening arising from the ear-faculty's own wondrous principle, the nature of hearing — not hearing the sound that is the object heard, but hearing only the very nature of hearing that hears. The wisdom of reflection is exactly correct wisdom's own contemplation, asking who, precisely, is the one who hears — clinging to neither the extreme of emptiness nor of existence, single-mindedly turning hearing back to hear one's own nature. The wisdom of cultivation is exactly this illusion-like habituation of hearing, and the cultivation of hearing turned back — moment after moment turning back to its own origin and returning of itself, subsiding back into its own true origin, giving rise to its own original, bright radiance, untying the six knots, passing beyond the three emptinesses, breaking through the five aggregates, surpassing the five turbidities — relying entirely on non-discriminating wisdom, and on the sheer effort of turning hearing back upon itself.

"Entered samādhi" is exactly the very samādhi inherent within this wisdom itself — from hearing this teaching, believing and understanding it, one gives rise to cultivation and realization in turn. There is a samādhi within cultivation, which is exactly the effort of untying the knot at the sense-faculty's own root; and there is a samādhi within realization, which is exactly the state where still cessation appears of itself. The word "entered" here marks exactly this: once all six knots are fully untied, one realizes and enters the very samādhi of perfect penetration itself — exactly the wondrous samādhi among the three names Ānanda originally requested, and exactly the cultivation and realization of complete meaning named in the sūtra's own title.

The Sequential Untying of the Knot 寅二 次第解結修證

Yuanying's outline: This is exactly the wondrous samādhi's own true practice — untying the knot at the sense-faculty's own root, the sequence of a single gateway entered deeply. Earlier, when the Buddha had everyone select the perfect faculty, the ear-gate had already been quietly indicated; and, within this very sequence of untying the knot, only the three emptinesses are explicitly listed, though the six knots are implicitly contained within them. And so Avalokiteśvara's own account of perfect penetration sets out, in full, the progression from shallow to deep, each stage distinctly marked — untying the six knots and breaking through the five aggregates, so as to make plain the complete meaning of cultivation and realization. The resolve on bodhi, above, was the vow; this is exactly the practice that arises from that same vow. Receiving the Dharma-gate, above, was the teaching; this is exactly the cultivation that arises from that same teaching.

Chinese Source

〖經〗初於聞中，入流亡所。

〖講〗初於聞中者：即最初乃於耳根聞性之中，下手起修；以耳根為所入之妙門，以聞性為所照之理境。從根中本覺妙理，起如幻始覺妙智；以智照理，聞熏聞修也。

此聞中二字，首宜揀別分明，不可錯用因心。一非肉耳之中，以肉耳浮塵色法，不合決定義門。二非耳識之中，以耳識隨念分別，固非菩提正因。三非意識之中，以意識生死根本，正是圓通障礙。故阿難請求佛定，佛即三番破識，欲令捨而去之；十番顯見，欲令取而用之。眼耳雖別，其性則同。今此聞中，即佛所顯之見中也；又即如來，廣會四科，遍融七大，所顯三如來藏性之中也；又即如來所顯，圓湛不生滅性，朗照萬法，不偏空有，中道之中也。若能於此體察分明，依之為本修因，自可圓成果地修證。

入流亡所者：古觀世音佛，教示從聞、思、脩，入三摩地，觀世音菩薩，依教起修，初從聞中下手，即聞慧。此句至生滅既滅，即思、修二慧；寂滅現前，即入三摩地。入流是對出流為言，耳根順聞出流奔聲，即結縛之元，反聞入流照性，即解脫之本。故諸佛異口同音，告阿難言：使汝輪轉，生死結根，唯汝六根，更非他物，令汝速證，安樂解脫，寂靜妙常，亦汝六根，更非他物。觀世音秉教所修之法門，正合本師釋迦，十方諸佛之意旨。

入流：以觀智為能入，耳門為所入，入即旋反聞機，不出流緣聲，而入流照性也。又即逆彼無始織妄業流，隨順耳根聞性真流，入流即是思慧，更兼修慧。用觀智思惟修非識心分別思惟，能聞世間音聲者是誰？亦即參究工夫，同前不隨分別，世間、業果、眾生三種相續，而斷三緣。但提起一段疑情，驀直參去，參聞者是誰，綿綿密密，無有間斷。聲動時，參聞聲者是誰？聲靜時，參聞靜者是誰？即同宗門下，參看話頭，一切時，一切處，不離一句話頭。但彼多用意根，此專用耳根，為稍異耳。同是智光內照，如佛所言，汝但不循動靜等塵，脫黏內伏，伏歸元真，則智光不外洩，所有聲塵，不期亡而自亡耳，故曰：入流亡所。入流、即是合覺，亡所、即是背塵，背塵合覺，為本經妙脩行路，至簡要、至巧妙之脩法也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "At first, within hearing itself, I entered the stream and let go of what was heard."

Commentary: "At first, within hearing itself" means exactly this: at the very outset, within the ear-faculty's own nature of hearing, one takes up the practice from there — taking the ear-faculty as the wondrous gate entered, and the nature of hearing as the principle-realm illuminated. From the sense-faculty's own original awakening, this wondrous principle, one gives rise to the illusion-like wondrous wisdom of initial awakening; using this wisdom to illuminate this principle is exactly the habituation of hearing, and the cultivation of hearing turned back upon itself.

These two words, "within hearing," must first be distinguished clearly of all else, lest one mistakenly take up the wrong cause of mind. First, this is not within the fleshly ear — the fleshly ear being a coarse, physical dharma of floating dust, and so it does not fit the gate of the second decisive meaning. Second, this is not within the ear-consciousness — the ear-consciousness discriminating instantly, moment by moment, and so it is certainly not the true, direct cause of bodhi. Third, this is not within mental consciousness — mental consciousness being exactly the very root of birth and death, and so it is exactly the obstruction to perfect penetration. This is exactly why, when Ānanda sought the Buddha's own certainty, the Buddha refuted consciousness three separate times, wishing to have it set aside and discarded, and displayed seeing ten separate times, wishing to have it taken up and put to use. Though the eye and the ear differ, their own nature is one and the same. This "within hearing," here, is exactly the same "within seeing" the Buddha displayed earlier; and it is exactly the very center of the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, displayed when the Tathāgata broadly harmonized the four categories and fully interfused the seven great elements; and it is exactly the very center of the middle way itself, this perfectly calm, unborn, unceasing nature the Tathāgata displayed, brightly illuminating the ten thousand dharmas, favoring neither emptiness nor existence. If one is able to discern this clearly here, and take it as one's own root cause of cultivation, one can naturally accomplish, perfectly and completely, the cultivation and realization of the fruit-stage.

"Entered the stream and let go of what was heard": the ancient Buddha Avalokiteśvara taught entry into samādhi through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating, and Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, relying on this teaching, took up the practice, starting, at first, within hearing itself — this being exactly the wisdom of hearing. Everything from this line up through "arising and ceasing were themselves extinguished" is exactly the two further kinds of wisdom, of reflection and cultivation; and "still cessation appeared of itself" is exactly entering samādhi. "Entering the stream" is spoken in contrast to "flowing outward": the ear-faculty following its own hearing, flowing outward, rushing after sound, is exactly the very origin of bondage; turning hearing back, entering the stream to illuminate this nature directly, is exactly the very root of liberation. And so every buddha, speaking with one and the same voice, tells Ānanda: what makes you turn in the wheel, the very root of the knot of birth and death, is nothing but your own six sense-faculties, nothing else at all; and what brings you to swift realization, peaceful, blissful liberation, the still, wondrous, ever-abiding — this too is nothing but your own six sense-faculties, nothing else at all. The Dharma-gate Avalokiteśvara cultivated, receiving this same teaching, exactly matches the very intention of our own founding teacher Śākyamuni, and of every buddha of the ten directions.

"Entering the stream": the wisdom of contemplation is what enters, and the ear-gate is what is entered — "entering" meaning exactly turning the mechanism of hearing back upon itself, no longer flowing outward to condition sound, but entering the stream to illuminate this nature directly. It is also exactly reversing that beginningless stream of interwoven, false karmic activity, and instead according with the ear-faculty's own true stream, the nature of hearing. Entering the stream is exactly the wisdom of reflection, together with the wisdom of cultivation as well — using the wisdom of contemplation to reflect and cultivate, not the discriminating thought of the mind of consciousness, asking: who, precisely, is the one who hears the world's sounds? This is exactly the same effort as an investigative inquiry — the same, as before, as not following the three continuities of the world, of karmic fruit, and of sentient beings in their discriminating, and thereby severing the three conditions. One simply raises up a single thread of doubt and pushes straight through with it, inquiring into who, exactly, is hearing — continuously, closely, without a single break. When sound stirs, inquire: who is it that hears this sound? When sound falls still, inquire: who is it that hears this stillness? This is exactly the same as, in the Chan lineage, taking up and investigating a huatō — at every moment, in every place, never departing from this single phrase. Only, that tradition mostly makes use of the mind-faculty, while this practice makes exclusive use of the ear-faculty — this being the one, slight difference between them. Both alike are the light of wisdom illuminating inward, exactly as the Buddha has said: if you simply do not follow after motion, stillness, and the other sense-objects, but slip loose from what you cling to and turn inward to subside, subsiding back into your own true origin, then the light of wisdom no longer leaks outward, and every sound-object, without even needing to be deliberately let go of, lets go of itself. Hence: "entered the stream and let go of what was heard." Entering the stream is exactly according with awareness; letting go of what was heard is exactly turning one's back on the sense-object — turning one's back on the sense-object while according with awareness is this sūtra's own wondrous path of practice, the most simple, essential, and skillful method of cultivation there is.

Yuanying's own account of "letting go of what was heard": This is simply the first step of untying, among the six knots — the knot of motion within the sound-object (sound present is called motion) — the first stage of effort, at which one first attains a genuine correspondence. This letting go of what was heard is by no means the sound-object itself being annihilated; it is only that the power of samādhi grows strong enough to bring about the effort of leaving the sense-object behind, and the sound-object, without needing to be deliberately let go of, lets go of itself. Entering the stream is the overall key to cultivating and realizing perfect penetration; letting go of what was heard is merely the first stage's own initial evidence of effect — exactly as Chan Master Yongjia put it: "the stream is never entered without letting go of what was heard, and what was heard is never let go of without entering the stream; letting go of what was heard is entering the stream in the act of letting go, and entering the stream is letting go of what was heard in the act of entering." For anyone cultivating the effort of Chan, what matters most is exactly this entering of the stream.

At twenty-one, I sailed by sea from Fujian to Suzhou, and studied under Chan Master Tianning Kai of Changzhou, taking up the single huatō "what, exactly, is my own original face?" Setting down every thought, I raised up a single thread of doubt, and investigated it continuously for three years, vowing to see my own original face and settle, once and for all, the great matter of birth and death. By the winter of my twenty-fourth year, in the midst of a Chan retreat, investigating single-mindedly and without a break, to the point that I no longer noticed the taste of food or drink, the light of my own mind illuminating this single huatō at every moment, in every place — on the afternoon of the tenth day, after the second incense-stick's period of stillness had ended, my investigation grew suddenly effective: body and mind abruptly emptied out, inner and outer dissolving into one another, the joy of this state of samādhi beyond anything words could describe. The instant a thought of delight stirred, this state of samādhi was lost at once. Afterward, through every further incense-stick, I sought to have this state of samādhi appear again, and could never once bring it back. When the retreat's own accounting of effort came, I recounted this matter in full to Master Yegong. He asked me: "from that time on, have you sought to have this state of samādhi appear again?" I answered: "yes." He then warned me at once: "you must absolutely not seek it — if you seek it, Māra will seize the opportunity, and you will become one of Māra's own kin!" I asked further: "was this state a good one?" The master said: "a mind that does not take itself for having attained sagehood is called a wholesome state; take it as an attainment of sagehood, and you fall prey to every kind of deviant influence at once. This was nothing more than your own effort of mind growing effective, bringing about a momentary lightness and ease — proceed in your cultivation from here without clinging to it and without seeking it, and there is real hope of awakening and realization yet to come." Reading this very sūtra afterward, on the fifty states of demonic interference, I came to know that one truly cannot do without drawing close to a good and wise teacher.

By the time I was twenty-eight, I studied under Chan Master Jichan of Tiantong Monastery in Ningbo, Zhejiang — and again, in the midst of a winter Chan retreat, cultivating with bold vigor, my own mind bent single-mindedly on the great matter of birth and death, on the evening of the eighth day, this same state of samādhi appeared again, deeper than before, its own joy deeper still. From this time on, I came to believe deeply that the Chan lineage does indeed hold something truly extraordinary within it. Reading the Śūraṅgama Sūtra afterward, everything I had failed to penetrate before now became entirely clear to me — and I came to believe, further, that this sūtra is exactly the very key to the gate of Chan itself, and devoted myself, heart and soul, to studying it further still, since samādhi is indeed able to give rise to wisdom, exactly as this sūtra's own words bear witness. I regret only that, afterward, taking up positions within the monastic community, reviving places of practice, administering charitable works, and overseeing the affairs of the Buddhist association, I came thereby to neglect my own Chan practice, never fully clarifying this matter belonging to my own original portion — and though widely regarded, by monastics and laypeople alike, as unrivaled in the Śūraṅgama, how is this any different from speaking of food without ever being fed by it, counting another's treasure while remaining forever poor oneself?

Further, "entering the stream and letting go of what was heard" is by no means the nature of hearing itself being cut off and annihilated — it is only that, through single-minded, focused turning of hearing back upon itself, the light of the mind turned back to illuminate its own source, the light of the mind poured inward, every moving sense-object, one after another, is let go of, while the nature of hearing itself is never extinguished. As the Buddha said earlier: sound, within hearing, has its own arising and ceasing — it is not the case that, because sound arises and sound ceases, your own nature of hearing is thereby made to exist or not exist. Yet once the moving sense-object has already been let go of, the sense-object of stillness at once appears in its place, and one hears, all day long, nothing but this state of stillness as a sense-object — one should know that this stillness, too, is itself a knot, and must likewise be untied. One should still not set aside the two further kinds of wisdom, of reflection and cultivation, and, rather than conditioning this stillness heard as an object, inquire directly: who, exactly, is the one hearing this stillness? Stillness as a sense-object is the objective realm; the nature of hearing is the mind itself — should one merely hear stillness as an object, this is still flowing outward; only by turning hearing back to ask who, exactly, is hearing does one truly turn hearing back and become free of sound.

The Marks of Motion and Stillness 動靜二相不生

Chinese Source

〖經〗所入既寂，動靜二相，瞭然不生。

〖講〗上亡所，是解「動結」，此三句乃解「靜結」，動相不過併言而已。首句所字，即牒上亡所；入字，即仍舊入流。謂所有動塵雖亡，仍是反聞入流，不捨本脩，不住靜境；此靜境即是色陰區宇，如明目人，處大暗室。既寂之寂，非是境靜之寂，乃是動靜二塵，到此俱寂之境。然所入既寂，則動結與靜結俱解，聲塵全泯，故曰：動、靜二相，瞭然不生。此瞭然不生，即動、靜二種塵相，了不可得也；此二句亦即既寂之註腳也。而工夫到此，聲塵動靜二結俱解，則色陰破矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Once what was entered into grew still, the two marks of motion and stillness became utterly unarisen."

Commentary: "Letting go of what was heard," above, untied the "knot of motion"; these three further lines untie the "knot of stillness" — the mark of motion is simply mentioned again alongside it. In the first phrase, the word "entered into" picks up exactly this same "letting go of what was heard" from before; the word "grew still" still refers to this same entering of the stream. This means: though every moving sense-object has already been let go of, one still turns hearing back, entering the stream, never setting aside this same root practice, never resting in this state of stillness either — for this state of stillness is itself exactly the domain of the aggregate of form, like a person with clear sight standing within a vast, darkened room. This "grew still" is by no means the stillness of a merely quiet objective state — it is exactly the state in which both the sense-objects of motion and of stillness alike have, at this point, both grown still together. And once what was entered into has thus grown still, both the knot of motion and the knot of stillness are untied together, the sound-object entirely dissolved away — hence, "the two marks of motion and stillness became utterly unarisen." This "utterly unarisen" means exactly that both sense-object marks, of motion and of stillness, can no longer be found at all — these two lines are themselves the very gloss on "grew still." And once effort has reached this point, both knots, of motion and stillness in the sound-object, being untied together, the aggregate of form is thereby broken through!

The Knot of the Sense-Faculty 聞所聞盡

Chinese Source

〖經〗如是漸增，聞所聞盡。

〖講〗此解「根結」。如是、指法之詞，指上反聞離塵，思修二慧，塵中二結已解，根結斯現，此根乃聚聞於耳，結滯為根之根，亦復是結，亦當解除。仍照如是本修之法，漸次增進，加功用行，定力轉深，所聞動靜二塵，既已瞭然不生，能聞之根，亦隨所聞以俱盡。塵既不緣，根無所偶，到此則根結亦解，無有能受所受，則受陰破矣！根塵既銷，識無從生，則想陰亦於此破矣！即佛前云：此根初解，先得人空，正齊於此；以塵亡根盡識泯，人無所依故。此中三結，亦即佛示，六結生起次第中所云，勞見發塵，今麤三結已解，則塵不復發，見不復勞矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Increasing this gradually, both the hearing and what is heard were exhausted."

Commentary: This unties the "knot of the sense-faculty." "This same" is a word pointing back to the method already described — pointing back to turning hearing back and leaving the sense-object behind, this same twofold wisdom of reflection and cultivation. With both knots within the sense-object now untied, the knot of the sense-faculty itself now comes into view — this faculty being exactly hearing gathered together into the ear, congealed and obstructed into this very faculty's own root — and this, too, is itself a knot, and must likewise be untied. Continuing to follow this same original method of cultivation, advancing gradually, applying still further effort, the power of samādhi growing ever deeper, the two sense-objects heard, of motion and stillness, having already become utterly unarisen, the very sense-faculty that hears is thereby exhausted together with what it hears. The sense-object no longer being conditioned at all, the sense-faculty has nothing left to pair with — and, at this point, the knot of the sense-faculty, too, is untied: there is no longer anything that receives, nor anything received, and so the aggregate of sensation is thereby broken through! Sense-faculty and sense-object both dissolved away, consciousness has nothing left from which to arise, and so the aggregate of perception, too, is broken through at this very point. This is exactly what the Buddha said earlier: once this sense-faculty is first untied, one first attains the emptiness of self — matching precisely this very point, since, the sense-object gone, the sense-faculty exhausted, and consciousness dissolved away, the self has nothing left to depend upon. These three knots here are also exactly what the Buddha displayed, in the sequence in which the six knots first arise, as "strained seeing giving rise to sense-objects" — now that these three coarser knots are already untied, sense-objects no longer arise, and seeing is no longer strained!

The Knot of Awareness 覺所覺空

Chinese Source

〖經〗盡聞不住，覺所覺空。

〖講〗此解「覺結」。盡聞二字，是牒上能聞與所聞俱盡，根塵雙泯之境，六用不行，惟餘一覺。若住此境，但得我空，未得法空，則永墮無為深坑。不住者，仍復加功用行，進觀聞性。下句為新證之境，《正脈》云：盡聞之後，根塵迥脫，湛一無邊之境現前；故今言覺者，即照此境之智也；所覺者，即此湛一之境也。盡聞若住入則境智恆對，能所仍存，終為勝進之障，即溈山所謂：具足心境也。今言覺所覺空者，謂能覺之智，與所覺之境，二俱空寂，泯然無復對待也。覺是智分，乃屬般若，智能契理如何亦空？當知此破法執，若吝惜此智，不肯放捨，即是一種愛智之法愛，亦復是結，亦當解除。《圓覺經》云：「幻塵滅故，幻心亦滅，幻心滅故，幻智亦滅，幻智滅故，幻滅亦滅；幻滅滅故，非幻不滅。」彼文全同此之解結工夫。今此覺結，即彼幻智，亦即佛示，六結生起次第，智見妄發，發妄不息。今覺結已解，則知見不發，妄不相續矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Even this exhaustion of hearing did not become a resting place, and the awareness and what it was aware of became empty as well."

Commentary: This unties the "knot of awareness." The words "exhaustion of hearing" pick up exactly this same state, described above, in which both the hearing that hears and what is heard are exhausted together, sense-faculty and sense-object both dissolved away, all six functions no longer operating, only a single awareness remaining. Should one rest within this state, one attains only the emptiness of self, not yet the emptiness of dharmas, and falls forever into the deep pit of the unconditioned. "Did not become a resting place" means one still applies further effort, advancing to contemplate the nature of hearing further still. The line that follows names this newly realized state — as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: after this exhaustion of hearing, sense-faculty and sense-object both slip entirely loose, and a single, calm, boundless state appears before one; and so, here, "awareness" refers exactly to the wisdom that illuminates this state, while "what it was aware of" refers exactly to this same calm, single state itself. Should this exhaustion of hearing become a resting place, then this state and this wisdom stand permanently opposed to one another, subject and object still remaining — and this becomes, in the end, an obstruction to further advance, exactly what Master Guishan called "still possessing mind and objective state both." Here, "the awareness and what it was aware of became empty as well" means exactly this: the very wisdom that is aware, and the very state that is its object, both alike become empty and still, dissolving away with nothing left standing over against anything else. Awareness belongs to the province of wisdom, to prajñā itself — how, then, could even this wisdom, capable of according with true principle, likewise become empty? One should know: this is exactly breaking through attachment to dharmas. Should one begrudge this very wisdom, unwilling to let it go, this is itself a kind of dharma-attachment, a fondness for wisdom itself — and this, too, is itself a knot, which must likewise be untied. As the Perfect Enlightenment Sūtra puts it: "because the illusory sense-object ceases, the illusory mind ceases as well; because the illusory mind ceases, the illusory wisdom ceases as well; because the illusory wisdom ceases, this very cessation of illusion ceases as well; and because this cessation of illusion itself ceases, what is not illusory does not cease." That passage describes exactly this same effort of untying the knot. This present knot of awareness is exactly that same illusory wisdom — and it is exactly what the Buddha displayed, in the sequence in which the six knots first arise, as "knowledge and vision falsely arising, this false arising never resting." Now that the knot of awareness is already untied, knowledge and vision no longer arise falsely, and this falseness no longer continues on!

The Knots of Emptiness and Extinction 空所空滅 · 生滅既滅寂滅現前

Chinese Source

〖經〗空覺極圓，空所空滅；生滅既滅，寂滅現前。

〖講〗此解「空結」。空即覺所覺空之空，覺即能覺之與所覺，由有此空，空彼能所二覺，則覺結雖解，空亦是結，亦當解除；以能空所空，二俱宛在，空性未圓，若恪惜空理，不肯放捨，即是愛理之法愛，還要入流照性，加功用行，參究空何所依？究而極之，以求圓滿空性。

空所空滅者：非惟所空之智境滅，即能空之空亦滅。如以木鑽木，火出木燒，二俱滅矣。今空結已解，則行陰破矣！亦即佛言，空性圓明，成法解脫，正齊於此，已得俱空之境。

生滅既滅，寂滅現前者：此解「滅結」。生滅二字，總指諸結而言。動滅靜生，靜滅根生，根滅覺生，覺滅空生，空滅滅生，六結皆生滅法，故滅結亦當解除。此結不解，恆住俱空之境，猶為圓通細障，即同「百尺竿頭坐的人，雖然得法未為真，百尺竿頭重進步，十方剎土現全身。」惟是此結，最難解除，禪門謂之末後牢關，到此境界，不肯進步，又謂之貼肉布衫難脫，此結一解，則可親見本來面目矣。

同《圓覺經》，迷智四相之壽者相。一我相，心所證者，以所證涅槃，認為我體。二人相，心所悟者，悟知所證之非。三眾生相，心所了者，了前悟證俱非，四壽者相，心所覺者，覺前前非，認己為是，即住此相中，深生法愛。譬如有人，不肯斷命，祖師門下，謂之命根不斷也。

故仍須入流照性，返窮流根，滅相迥脫，至不生滅，方是到家時節。既滅者，即觀智還元，一切生滅，悉皆滅已，此去更無可滅。此滅結，即佛前示，六結次第生起之第一結，由汝無始，心性狂亂；今六結盡解，五陰全破，狂心已歇，歇即菩提。亦即佛云：解脫法已，俱空不生，妄窮真露，寂滅真理現前。所謂寂滅者，此寂非對動之寂，從無始來，本自不動之寂也；此滅非對生之滅，從無始來，本自無生之滅也。虛心絕待，妙體孤圓，即如來藏，妙真如性，亦即一乘，寂滅場地，為真心之全體。前佛云：是名菩薩，從三摩地，得無生忍。上解六結，是從聞、思、修，此寂滅現前，是入三摩地，得證圓通。古觀世音佛，所授之法，與釋迦如來，解結修證，無二無別。既得寂滅現前，親證藏性，而入首楞嚴三昧，當登圓教初住之位。下忽然超越之下，皆稱全體所起之大用也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Once this emptiness and awareness reached their utmost fullness, even emptiness and what was emptied were extinguished. Once arising and ceasing were themselves extinguished, still cessation appeared of itself."

Commentary: This unties the "knot of emptiness." "Emptiness" here is exactly the very emptiness in "the awareness and what it was aware of became empty"; "awareness" is exactly both the awareness that is aware and what it is aware of together. Because there is this emptiness that empties out these two, the aware and the object of awareness — though the knot of awareness is thereby untied, this very emptiness is itself a knot as well, and must likewise be untied; for the emptying subject and the emptied object both still plainly remain, and the nature of emptiness itself has not yet reached its own fullness. Should one cherish this very principle of emptiness, unwilling to let it go, this is itself a fondness for principle, a dharma-attachment — one must still enter the stream to illuminate this nature directly, applying still further effort, inquiring into what, exactly, this emptiness itself depends upon, pursuing this inquiry to its very limit, so as to seek the full, perfect completion of the nature of emptiness itself.

"Even emptiness and what was emptied were extinguished": not only is the wisdom-realm that was emptied extinguished, but the very emptiness that did the emptying is extinguished as well — exactly like using one piece of wood to drill into another, until fire emerges and the wood itself burns away, both alike extinguished together. Now that the knot of emptiness is untied, the aggregate of formations is thereby broken through! This is exactly what the Buddha meant, saying that the nature of emptiness, perfectly bright and complete, accomplishes liberation from every dharma — matching precisely this very point, at which the state where both are already empty has been attained.

"Once arising and ceasing were themselves extinguished, still cessation appeared of itself": this unties the "knot of extinction." The words "arising and ceasing" refer, in general, to every one of these knots together: motion is extinguished and stillness arises; stillness is extinguished and the sense-faculty arises; the sense-faculty is extinguished and awareness arises; awareness is extinguished and emptiness arises; emptiness is extinguished and this very extinction arises — every one of the six knots is a dharma of arising and ceasing, and so this knot of extinction, too, must likewise be untied. Should this knot remain untied, and one rest forever within this state where both subject and object are already empty, this still remains a subtle obstruction to perfect penetration — exactly like the saying: "the one seated at the very top of a hundred-foot pole has attained the Dharma, yet not the true Dharma; take one further step from the very top of the hundred-foot pole, and one's whole body appears throughout the lands of the ten directions." It is exactly this knot that proves hardest of all to untie — the Chan lineage calls it the very last, most stubborn barrier; reaching this state, one is unwilling to take a single further step, calling it, too, a garment stuck fast to one's own flesh, impossible to shed. Only once this final knot is untied can one truly behold one's own original face directly.

This matches the Perfect Enlightenment Sūtra's own four marks of deluded wisdom, specifically the mark of a life-span. First, the mark of a self: what the mind takes as realized — taking the very nirvāṇa realized as one's own self-substance. Second, the mark of a person: what the mind takes as awakened to — awakening to the fact that what was realized is itself mistaken. Third, the mark of a sentient being: what the mind takes as understood — understanding that both the earlier awakening and the earlier realization were themselves mistaken. Fourth, the mark of a life-span: what the mind takes as aware of — aware that each of these earlier states was itself mistaken, yet still taking one's own present state as correct, and so remaining within this very mark, giving rise to a deep attachment to this dharma. It is exactly like a person unwilling to let their own life come to its end — the masters of the ancestral lineage call this "the life-root not yet severed."

And so one must still enter the stream to illuminate this nature directly, tracing this very stream back to its own root, the mark of extinction itself slipping entirely loose, all the way to the truly unborn and unceasing — only then does one arrive, at last, at one's own true home. "Were themselves extinguished" means exactly this: the wisdom of contemplation returns to its own origin, every arising and ceasing, without exception, already extinguished, with nothing whatsoever left to extinguish from this point on. This knot of extinction is exactly the very first knot the Buddha displayed, in the sequence in which the six knots first arise — arising, as he said, from your own mind-nature, beginninglessly deranged. Now that all six knots are fully untied and all five aggregates entirely broken through, this deranged mind has finally come to rest — and this very rest is itself bodhi. This is also exactly what the Buddha meant: once liberated from every dharma, both subject and object no longer arise, falseness exhausted and truth laid bare, the true principle of still cessation appearing before one. This so-called "still cessation": this "still" is not the stillness set against motion, but a stillness that has, from the beginningless past, never once moved at all; and this "cessation" is not the cessation set against arising, but a cessation that has, from the beginningless past, never once arisen at all. Mind emptied out, every counterpart severed, this wondrous substance stands alone, perfectly complete — this is exactly the tathāgatagarbha, this wondrous, true suchness itself; and it is exactly the one vehicle's own ground of still cessation, the true mind's own complete substance. As the Buddha said earlier: this is called a bodhisattva attaining, through samādhi, the forbearance of the unborn nature of all things. Untying the six knots, above, belongs to hearing, reflecting, and cultivating; this present "still cessation appeared of itself" is exactly entering samādhi, realizing and attaining perfect penetration. The method taught by the ancient Buddha Avalokiteśvara, and the untying of the knot in cultivation and realization taught by the Tathāgata Śākyamuni, are one and the same, without the slightest difference. Having attained this state where still cessation appears of itself, personally realizing the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha, and thereby entering the Śūraṅgama samādhi itself, one comes, at this point, to ascend to the very first of the ten abidings of the perfect teaching. Everything from "suddenly transcended," below, describes exactly the great function that arises from this same complete substance.

Cross-reference: As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: taken as a whole, this entire section on the sequential untying of the knot corresponds directly to the closing passage of fascicle four: entering the stream is exactly holding fast to the true and constant; letting go of what was heard is exactly abandoning every arising and ceasing; the exhaustion of hearing is exactly the sense-faculty, sense-object, and the discriminating mind of consciousness, all dissolving away the moment they are attended to; the awareness and what it was aware of becoming empty, together with emptiness and what was emptied being extinguished, are exactly the marks of perception as sense-object and the sentiments of consciousness as defilement, both left far behind together; and still cessation appearing of itself is exactly the Dharma-eye growing clear and bright, without the slightest discrepancy at all!

Reading this alongside Chan Master Yongjia's own text on śamatha — where entering the stream is exactly stilling thought, and letting go of what was heard is exactly letting go of the sense-object — one might paraphrase his own words accordingly: "the stream is never entered without letting go of what was heard, and what was heard is never let go of without entering the stream; letting go of what was heard is entering the stream in the very act of letting go, and entering the stream is letting go of what was heard in the very act of entering." These four lines correspond to "the two marks of motion and stillness became utterly unarisen." He continues: "once letting go of what was heard enters, this entering has no one left who enters; once entering the stream lets go, this letting go has nothing left that is let go of." These four lines, with both sense-faculty and sense-object dissolved away, correspond to "both the hearing and what is heard were exhausted." He continues further: "once letting go has nothing left that is let go of, the sense-object is left behind with nothing left standing over against it; once entering has no one left who enters, thought itself ceases, beyond even knowing." These four lines, with neither a counterpart nor any knowing left, correspond to "the awareness and what it was aware of became empty as well." He continues: "knowing extinguished, every counterpart left behind, one turns wholly toward hidden stillness." These two lines correspond to "even emptiness and what was emptied were extinguished." And he continues: "utterly silent, with nothing left to rely upon, this wondrous nature entirely of itself." These two lines correspond to "once arising and ceasing were themselves extinguished, still cessation appeared of itself" — the correspondence seeming, indeed, to fit together seamlessly, without a single point of divergence!

Only, Yongjia seems to gather in all six sense-faculties together, or perhaps he gathers in the mind-faculty alone, whereas this sūtra makes exclusive use of the ear-faculty — this being the one point of difference between them. And Yongjia, moreover, speaks only of the very first dissolving and display, with further cultivation and refinement still to follow afterward, whereas this sūtra has already spoken of a deep realization at a lofty stage, with only the manifestation of function still to be made plain afterward. Taken together and considered as a whole, the two texts' own sections correspond remarkably closely — letting one know that the perfect and sudden teaching holds no different mind between its own beginning and its own end, and that a practitioner must not dismiss this as some lofty stage set apart, having nothing to do with themselves.

"Should bodhisattvas": "should" is simply a grammatical particle here, though it may also be read as "however many" — "bodhisattvas" covers every rank, whether of the provisional or the real teaching; provisional and real, though differing, share one and the same hope of becoming a buddha. "Samādhi" is exactly the correct absorption belonging to whatever Dharma-gate is being cultivated, differing according to the stage of resembling-realization or of partial-realization; "undefiled" and "superior understanding" likewise differ by stage. "This superior understanding about to appear in its full completion": according to whichever Dharma-gate is being cultivated, the unbroken path's own causal practice already fulfilled, about to enter the path of liberation, the superior understanding thereby arising is on the verge of appearing in its full completion, though not yet fully complete. In every rank that follows, though the same word "superior understanding" recurs, its meaning differs — and even within this present category, it must be understood in two distinct senses: should a bodhisattva of the resembling-realization stage enter a resembling-realization samādhi, advancing in cultivation of the undefiled middle way, then this is a partially realized superior understanding about to appear in full completion; should a bodhisattva of the partial-realization stage enter a partial-realization samādhi, advancing in cultivation of vajra-like undefilement, then this is the ultimate superior understanding about to appear in full completion. The great being then manifests a Buddha's body, expounding the teaching of entering the Buddha-vehicle directly and at once, bringing about either a partial or an ultimate liberation.

One might ask: "the great being, realizing and entering perfect penetration, ascends only to the very first of the ten abidings of the perfect teaching — how, then, could they manifest a Buddha's body and expound the Dharma even to bodhisattvas of equal enlightenment?" The answer: "even at the first abiding, one is already able to divide one's own body across a hundred realms, manifesting the eight aspects of a buddha's own life — how, then, could this not suffice to expound the Dharma even to those of equal enlightenment? Moreover, though the great being's own recent trace stands at the first abiding, considered in terms of their own distant origin, they had already, long before, accomplished buddhahood as the Tathāgata Right Dharma Brightness — and so their expounding the Dharma even to those of equal enlightenment raises no doubt in principle at all."

Sūtra: "Should those still in training attain a stillness of wondrous brightness, this superior wonder about to appear in its full completion, I manifest, before them, the body of an independently-awakened being, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their liberation."

Commentary: This manifests the body of an independently-awakened being. All three of these categories speak of "those still in training," since none of them have yet realized the rank beyond further training. An independently-awakened being is one who, appearing in an age without a buddha, observes the transformation of things and awakens, of themselves, to the unborn — hence called "independently awakened." "Those still in training" who aspire to this independent awakening: "a stillness of wondrous brightness" means exactly this: within the path of cultivation, delighting in solitude and stillness is called "stillness"; seeking a wisdom that arises spontaneously, without a teacher, is called "wondrous brightness." "This superior wonder about to appear in its full completion" means exactly this: this superior, wondrous wisdom (that is, this spontaneous wisdom) is about to appear in its full completion, like drilling wood to make fire — the warmth already present, the fire itself about to emerge, though not yet emerged. I then manifest, before this one still in training, the body of an independently-awakened being, answering what they themselves seek, making it easy for them to believe and follow, expounding the teaching of the unborn, bringing about their liberation from the afflictions of views and thought, and their realization of the rank beyond further training. This is unlike the naturalist non-Buddhist teachers, since this being has, across many lifetimes, been habituated by the lesser teaching, and now, appearing in an age without a buddha, observes the flourishing and withering of things and, encountering these circumstances directly, awakens to the path.

Sūtra: "Should those still in training sever the twelve links of conditioned arising, this superior nature — the nature realized once these conditions are severed — this superior wonder about to appear in its full completion, I manifest, before them, the body of one awakened through causes and conditions, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their liberation."

Commentary: This manifests the body of one awakened through causes and conditions. Taking up the Buddha's own teaching of the twelve links of conditioned arising, and awakening to the principle of the unborn, such a being is called "awakened through causes and conditions." The twelve links admit of two gates: the gate of flowing on, meaning ceaseless transformation, turning endlessly through birth and death; and the gate of returning and ceasing, meaning returning to one's own original nature, extinguishing birth and death entirely. Observed forward, through the gate of flowing on, one comes to know the sequence in which these arise: twelve links in all, also called the twelve members of existence, coming down to nothing but delusion, karma, and suffering, forming the causes and effects of three lifetimes together. Ignorance conditions volitional formations (volitional formations being exactly karmic activity, arising from the delusion of ignorance — these two links being the cause, belonging to delusion and karma, in a past life); formations condition consciousness (exactly the eighth consciousness at the moment of entering the womb); consciousness conditions name-and-form (mind and form united within the womb — "name" being exactly the mind, since mind has no shape or form of its own and so is spoken of by name alone, this mind being exactly the craving and attachment that enters the womb, and "form" being exactly the father's semen and mother's blood entered within the womb); name-and-form conditions the six entrances (exactly the six sense-faculties possessed by a single body, able to take in sense-objects); the six entrances condition contact (in childhood, the six sense-faculties making contact with the six sense-objects); contact conditions sensation (a little older, sense-faculty and sense-object meeting, one comes to know reception — these five links being the effect, in the present life); sensation conditions craving (as an adult, giving rise to craving toward favorable circumstances and aversion toward adverse ones — "craving" here already includes aversion within it, this being the delusion of the present life); craving conditions grasping; grasping conditions existence (these two links being the karma of the present life — grasping is the beginning of this karma, and existence is its completion); existence conditions birth (exactly receiving birth in a future life); birth conditions old age and death (exactly the future life's own progression, from youth to old age, from old age to death — these two links being the suffering-effect of the future life). These twelve links, linked together like an unbroken chain, continue on without a break: from cause, one incurs an effect; upon this effect, one again creates a cause; from this cause, one again incurs an effect — delusion, karma, and suffering circling on endlessly, birth and death never ceasing. Hence, the gate of flowing on.

Observed in reverse, through the gate of returning and ceasing, one awakens to the principle of the unborn: ignorance ceasing, formations cease as well, and so on, all the way to birth ceasing, old age and death cease as well. What is here called "severing the twelve links" is spoken exactly in terms of this gate of returning and ceasing. "This superior nature — the nature realized once these conditions are severed": this superior nature is exactly the principle-nature of the unborn — called "superior" because it transcends every worldly dharma — and this nature is necessarily revealed only once these conditions are severed, hence, "the nature realized once these conditions are severed." "Superior wonder": because one awakens to the very emptiness of the nature of causes and conditions, it is called "superior wonder." Precisely at the moment when this superior wonder is about to appear in its full completion, though not yet fully complete, I manifest, before this one still in training, the body of one awakened through causes and conditions, comforting them in what they themselves seek, expounding the teaching that what arises through conditions has no fixed nature of its own, bringing about their liberation from birth and death by fixed portions, and their realization of the fruit of one awakened through causes and conditions.

Sūtra: "Should those still in training attain the emptiness of the four truths, cultivating the path toward cessation, this superior nature about to appear in its full completion, I manifest, before them, the body of a śrāvaka, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their liberation."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a śrāvaka. "Those still in training," within the stage of the path of seeing, through the eight forbearances and eight kinds of knowledge, these sixteen moments of mind, sever the delusions belonging to the four truths — the delusions of views already severed is called "attaining the emptiness of the four truths" — and thereby enter the stage of the path of cultivation, severing the delusions of thought throughout the three realms, eighty-one grades in all, each grade realizing its own portion of the unconditioned through analytical cessation — hence, "cultivating the path toward cessation." "This superior nature" is exactly the unborn nature of the truth of cessation, about to appear in its full completion — like clouds already gathering before the rain itself has fallen. I then manifest, before this one still in training, the body of a śrāvaka, matching their own inclination, expounding the teaching of the truth of cessation, the unborn, bringing about their liberation from every worldly outflow, transcending the three realms, and entering the nirvāṇa of the land with residue still remaining from skillful means.

To Those Seeking the Heavens 午二 應求諸天

Chinese Source

〖經〗若諸眾生，欲心明悟，不犯欲塵，欲身清淨，我於彼前，現梵王身，而為說法，令其解脫。

〖講〗此現梵王身。若諸眾生，指在欲界之眾生。欲心明悟者：對婬欲一事，心得明悟，了知慾為招苦之本，欲由愛生，身因欲有，身為眾苦所集，無非婬欲之所招致，故持戒修身，不犯欲塵，令此欲身，而得清淨。清淨乃是生梵之因，故我於彼眾生之前，現梵王身，為說四無量心，及出欲論，教脩離欲定。此定若成，可以超出欲界，上生色界，不由胎生，乃是化生，身相莊嚴清淨，令得解脫欲界苦麤障，而得色界淨妙離也。

〖經〗若諸眾生，欲為天主，統領諸天，我於彼前，現帝釋身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現帝釋身。若諸眾生，或為人道，或是初二天天人，願為忉利天主。梵語忉利，此云三十三，為六欲天第二天，在須彌山頂，東西南北各八天，帝釋天主，住善見城、居中一天，合成三十三天，不惟統領忉利諸天，兼統四天王。我則應其所求，故於彼前，現帝釋身，即忉利天主，釋提桓因，此云能為主。而為說法者，說上品十善，及種種善論，令其成就帝釋之果。

〖經〗若諸眾生，欲身自在，遊行十方；我於彼前，現自在天身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現自在天身。若諸眾生，指人倫及欲界諸天；欲得此身，逍遙自在，遊行十方，而無阻礙。我於彼前，現他化自在天身[謂此天樂具，他天化作，自在受用，福報超勝，居欲界頂。]，而為說上品十善等法，令其成就自在天福報。或云六天別有魔王宮，亦自在攝。

〖經〗若諸眾生，欲身自在，飛行虛空；我於彼前，現大自在天身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現大自在天身。若諸眾生，指欲界天人，欲得此身自在，而能飛行虛空，上云遊行十方，不過六欲四洲之十方；此云飛行虛空，飛行較遊行為勝。此虛空，乃大千世界之虛空，因大千世界，上覆以四禪天，大自在天，即色界頂天，世間福報最勝，得大自在也。我應其所求，即現大自在天身，而為說上上品十善，四禪四無量心，令其成就最勝果報。有謂此天，即摩醯首羅天王，有三目八臂，騎白牛，執白拂，能飛行虛空者。

〖經〗若諸眾生，愛統鬼神，救護國土；我於彼前，現天大將軍身。而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現天大將軍身。若諸眾生：指人道及八部，心愛統領鬼神，乃八部鬼神。無福德曰鬼，有福德曰神。《正脈》云：「四王主帥，各有八將，韋馱為上首。《金光明經》，散脂為大將，統領二十八部，巡遊世間。」今云愛統鬼神，即欲求天大將軍身也。救護國土者：即巡視世間一切國土，除妖降福，賞善罰惡耳。我則應其所求，即於彼前，現天大將軍身，而為說五戒十善，及秘密神咒，呼召鬼神之法，令其成就威勇，保護蒼生也。

〖經〗若諸眾生，愛統世界，保護眾生；我於彼前，現四天王身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現四天王身。若諸眾生，指人類眾生，及四天天眾，愛統世間四大部洲，保護各國眾生；我則應其所求，即於彼前，現四天王身，為說上品十善，及護國安民之法，令其成就，統領世界之願。四王居須彌山腰，為帝釋外臣，故列天臣。東方持國天王居黃金埵，領乾闥婆、富單那二部；南方增長天王居琉璃埵，領鳩槃荼、薜荔多二部；西方廣目天王居白銀埵，領比舍闍、莫呼落伽二部；北方多聞天王居水晶埵，領藥叉、羅剎二部。

〖經〗若諸眾生，愛生天宮，驅使鬼神；我於彼前，現四天王國太子身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現天王太子身。若諸眾生，指人倫及四天人民，有志愛生四天王宮，為太子，能驅遣使令一切鬼神。鬼即八部，神即四王，各八大將軍之屬；或有鬼神作祟，擾亂人間，四王太子，而能制止。設有違制，則遣大力鬼神，或天大將軍，而降伏之。輔助天王，保護眾生。我則應其所求，即現四天王國太子身，而為說皈依齋戒，十善符咒之法，令得成就其志願也。

《大吉義經》云：「護世四王，各有九十一子，姿貌端正，有大勢力，即那吒之類。」《灌頂疏》云：「唐天寶間，西番五國，來寇西安，國軍莫能禦，玄宗詔不空三藏入內，持念《護國仁王阤羅尼》。方二七遍，忽見神將五百，荷戈殿前。對曰：北方天王，第二子獨健，往救西安；寇進攻，仰見無數天兵天將，布陳空中，寇畏歎曰：中國有聖人，未可犯也！即退兵。隨即表奏，帝喜，因敕諸道府州，各建天王殿以祀之。」海內大剎，皆有天王，即此由來矣。二應求諸天竟。

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings, their own minds clear and awakened regarding desire, not transgress the sense-object of desire, their own desirous body thereby made pure, I manifest, before them, the body of a Brahmā-king, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their liberation."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a Brahmā-king. "Sentient beings" here refers to beings within the desire realm. "Their own minds clear and awakened regarding desire" means exactly this: regarding the one matter of sexual desire, the mind attains clarity and awakening, understanding fully that desire is the very root that invites suffering — desire arising from craving, the body itself existing because of desire, this body being where every suffering gathers, nothing but what desire itself has invited — and so, keeping the precepts and cultivating the body, not transgressing the sense-object of desire, one brings this same desirous body to purity. This purity is exactly the cause of rebirth as a Brahmā, and so I manifest, before these sentient beings, the body of a Brahmā-king, expounding the four immeasurable minds and the treatise on leaving desire behind, teaching the cultivation of the samādhi that is free of desire. Should this samādhi be accomplished, one can transcend the desire realm entirely, rising to birth in the form realm — not through birth from a womb, but through transformative birth, one's own bodily appearance adorned and pure, liberated from the desire realm's own coarse suffering and obstruction, and attaining the form realm's own pure, wondrous freedom.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings wish to be the ruler of the gods, governing over every heaven, I manifest, before them, the body of Śakra, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of Śakra. "Sentient beings" here means either those of the human realm, or the gods of the first two heavens, wishing to become the ruler of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven. The Sanskrit Trāyastriṃśa means "thirty-three" — the second heaven of the six desire-realm heavens, at the summit of Mount Sumeru, eight heavens each to the east, west, south, and north, with Śakra, the ruler of this heaven, dwelling in the city of Sudarśana at the very center, making thirty-three heavens in all — governing not only every heaven of Trāyastriṃśa, but the Four Heavenly Kings as well. I then answer what they themselves seek, manifesting, before them, the body of Śakra — that is, the ruler of Trāyastriṃśa, Śakra Devānām Indra, meaning "able to be lord" — and expound the Dharma, teaching the superior grade of the ten wholesome deeds and every manner of wholesome discourse, bringing about their accomplishment of the fruit of Śakra.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings wish for a body of self-mastery, roaming freely through the ten directions, I manifest, before them, the body of a self-mastery deva, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a self-mastery deva. "Sentient beings" here refers to humankind and the gods of the desire realm, wishing to attain this body, free and at ease, roaming through the ten directions without hindrance. I manifest, before them, the body of a deva of the heaven where enjoyment is transformed by others (meaning that this heaven's own objects of enjoyment are transformed into being by another heaven, freely enjoyed there, its own blessed reward surpassing all others, dwelling at the very summit of the desire realm), and expound the superior grade of the ten wholesome deeds and other such teachings, bringing about their accomplishment of this self-mastery heaven's own blessed reward. Some say this same sixth heaven also holds the palace of the Māra-king within it, likewise included under self-mastery.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings wish for a body of self-mastery, flying through empty space itself, I manifest, before them, the body of a great self-mastery deva, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a great self-mastery deva. "Sentient beings" here refers to the gods of the desire realm, wishing to attain this same self-mastery of body, able to fly through empty space itself — "roaming" above referred to no more than the ten directions within the six desire heavens and four continents, whereas "flying through empty space" here surpasses mere roaming. This "empty space" is exactly the empty space of a great trichiliocosm itself — since a great trichiliocosm is covered above by the four dhyāna heavens, the great self-mastery heaven being exactly the very summit of the form realm, its own worldly blessed reward the most supreme of all, attaining great self-mastery indeed. I answer what they themselves seek, manifesting exactly the body of a great self-mastery deva, and expound the very highest grade of the ten wholesome deeds, together with the four dhyānas and four immeasurable minds, bringing about their accomplishment of this most supreme fruit and reward. Some say this same heaven is exactly the deva-king Maheśvara, having three eyes and eight arms, riding a white ox, holding a white whisk, able to fly through empty space itself.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings love to govern spirits and ghosts, protecting the land, I manifest, before them, the body of a heavenly general, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a heavenly general. "Sentient beings" here refers to humankind and to the eight kinds of spirits themselves, the mind loving to govern spirits and ghosts — that is, the ghosts and spirits of the eight kinds. Those without merit are called ghosts; those with merit are called spirits. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "each of the Four Heavenly Kings' own commanding generals has eight lieutenant generals under him, with Skanda foremost among them; the Suvarṇaprabhāsa Sūtra names Sāṃjñin as a great general, governing twenty-eight divisions, patrolling throughout the world." What is here called "loving to govern spirits and ghosts" means exactly wishing to attain the body of a heavenly general. "Protecting the land" means exactly patrolling every land throughout the world, driving off malevolent spirits and bestowing blessings, rewarding the good and punishing the wicked. I then answer what they themselves seek, manifesting, before them, the body of a heavenly general, and expound the five precepts and the ten wholesome deeds, together with secret mantras and the method of summoning spirits and ghosts, bringing about their accomplishment of a majestic valor, protecting the common people.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings love to govern the world, protecting sentient beings, I manifest, before them, the body of one of the Four Heavenly Kings, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of one of the Four Heavenly Kings. "Sentient beings" here refers to humankind, together with the gods of these four heavens, loving to govern the world's own four great continents, protecting the sentient beings of every land. I then answer what they themselves seek, manifesting, before them, the body of one of the Four Heavenly Kings, and expound the superior grade of the ten wholesome deeds, together with the method of protecting the nation and bringing peace to its people, bringing about their accomplishment of this same vow to govern the world. The Four Kings dwell at the very waist of Mount Sumeru, serving as Śakra's own outer ministers, hence classed here among the heavenly ministers. In the east, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, the Upholder of the Nation, dwells upon a mound of gold, governing the two divisions of gandharvas and pūtanas; in the south, Virūḍhaka, of Increasing Growth, dwells upon a mound of lapis lazuli, governing the two divisions of kumbhāṇḍas and pretas; in the west, Virūpākṣa, of the Broad Eye, dwells upon a mound of silver, governing the two divisions of piśācas and mahoragas; in the north, Vaiśravaṇa, of Wide Hearing, dwells upon a mound of crystal, governing the two divisions of yakṣas and rākṣasas.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings love to be born within a heavenly palace, commanding spirits and ghosts, I manifest, before them, the body of a prince of one of the Four Heavenly Kings, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a heavenly king's own prince. "Sentient beings" here refers to humankind and the people of these four heavens, aspiring and longing to be born within one of the Four Heavenly Kings' own palaces, as a prince, able to command and direct every ghost and spirit. "Ghosts" here are exactly the eight kinds of spirits, and "spirits" are exactly the Four Kings' own generals, each with eight great generals under them; should any ghost or spirit cause disturbance, troubling the human realm, this same prince of the heavenly king is able to restrain and stop it — and should any defy this restraint, he sends forth a mighty ghost or spirit, or one of the heavenly generals, to subdue them, assisting the heavenly king and protecting sentient beings. I then answer what they themselves seek, manifesting the body of a prince of one of the Four Heavenly Kings, and expound the teaching of taking refuge, the fasting precepts, the ten wholesome deeds, and protective mantras, bringing about the accomplishment of this same aspiration.

As the Sūtra of Great and Auspicious Meaning puts it: "each of the Four Heavenly Kings, protectors of the world, has ninety-one sons, upright and handsome in bearing, possessed of great power — of the same kind as Naṭa." The Consecration Commentary records: during the Tianbao era of the Tang dynasty, five western tribal nations invaded Xi'an, and the nation's own army could not repel them; Emperor Xuanzong summoned the Tripiṭaka Master Amoghavajra into the palace, to hold and recite the Dhāraṇī of the Humane King Who Protects the Nation. After only fourteen recitations, five hundred spirit-generals suddenly appeared, bearing halberds before the palace hall, and answered: "the northern heavenly king's second son, Independently Vigorous, is going to Xi'an's own rescue." As the invaders pressed their attack, they looked up and saw countless heavenly soldiers and generals arrayed throughout the sky, and, terrified, exclaimed: "China has a sage among them — this land cannot be invaded!" — and withdrew their own troops at once. This was reported to the throne, and the emperor, delighted, decreed that every province and prefecture build a hall to the heavenly kings, to honor them there. Every great monastery within the four seas holds a hall to the heavenly kings to this very day — and this is exactly where that custom comes from. This closes the second group, responding to those who seek the heavens.

To Those Seeking the Human Realm 午三 應求人趣

Chinese Source

〖經〗若諸眾生，樂為人王，我於彼前，現人王身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現王身。溫陵戒環法師曰：自金輪以至粟散，皆為人王。釋曰，世間人王有五：一、金輪王，即轉輪聖王，亦具三十二相，有七寶隨身，人中最上，無能勝者。即王位時，有金輪寶，從空飛下，而至其前。乘此輪寶，一日能遊行東西南北四大部洲，各國莫不服從，以十善化世，得王[去聲]一四天下。二、象寶有白象王。三、馬寶，名勇疾風。四、將軍寶，又稱主兵臣寶、名離垢眼。五、主藏臣寶，財寶隨身，同輪王出遊，不必帶諸財寶，王欲布施時，但有土地，藏臣令掘，取用不盡。六、寶藏瓶，王隨身攜帶，欲要何物，能如輪王之意，一一出生。七、女寶，即輪王第一夫人，名淨妙德，能輔王化，各國人民，受王之化，悉修十善，世無惡人，此亦福報所感也。

二、銀輪王，即王位時，有銀輪寶，從空飛至，乘之一日能遊行東西南三洲，除北洲，王[去聲]三天下，各國悉皆服從。三、銅輪王，有銅輪寶，能遊行東南二洲，各國歸其統領。四、鐵輪王，有鐵輪寶，能遊行南洲一洲，所有各國，皆歸統轄，如阿育王者是也。五、粟散王，即各國小王，如散佈其粟，遍地皆是。若諸眾生：指人倫；樂為人王：王者往也，懷仁布德，四方歸往，欲為有道之君，治理邦國者也。我於彼前，現人王身，為說五戒十善，以為生貴之因；及說帝王德業，熏成隔生之種，令其成就善因，而獲福果。問：「心懷篡逆，窺竊神器，未知菩薩亦為現身說法否？」答：「菩薩志在利生，豈肯助逆。」

〖經〗若諸眾生，愛主族姓，世間推讓；我於彼前，現長者身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現長者身。我國年高德重，即可稱為長者，西域長者，頗不易稱，須具十德：一、姓貴，謂剎利尊姓，貴族大家。二、位高，謂宰輔丞相，朝中老臣。三、大富，謂豐饒財寶，充裕倉廩。四、威猛，謂霜威嚴重，望之可畏。五、智深，謂心如日月，慧燦珠璣。六、年耆，謂耆年宿德，老當益壯。七、行淨，謂矩範堪親，楷模足式。八、禮備，謂威儀庠序，接物和光。九、上歎，謂上為王者之所稱歎。十、下歸，謂下為人民之所依歸。具此十德，方稱長者，故列宰官之前。

若諸眾生，愛主一族同姓，併願為世間所推重，到處讓居上首者；我於彼前，現長者身，為說博施濟眾，仁民愛物之法，令其成就。

〖經〗若諸眾生，愛談名言，清淨自居；我於彼前，現居士身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現居士身。若諸眾生，愛談古今名人，嘉言典章，可以垂範作則，以訓於世。清淨自居者：身處塵勞，心恆清淨，不染世欲，以道自居。我於彼前，現居士身，為說清心寡慾，潔己脩身之法，令其成就。居士乃以道自居之士，即在俗修行者，有有德有位者，如蘇東坡之類，有有德無位者，如王通、邵雍之流，隱淪不仕者。

〖經〗若諸眾生，愛治國土，剖斷邦邑；我於彼前，現宰官身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現宰官身。若諸眾生，愛治理國家領土，輔佐政治。溫陵謂：三臺輔相是也。能為朝廷，整綱立紀，能為民間，易俗移風。剖斷邦邑者：大者為邦[如省]，小者為邑[即縣]，剖乃剖雪冤屈，斷則決斷是非，如片言折獄，訟簡刑清者也。我於彼前，現宰官身：宰者治也，官者公也，上自三臺輔相，下至州牧縣長，皆名宰官；菩薩現身，為說脩齊治平，護國愛民之法，令其成就。

〖經〗若諸眾生，愛諸數術，攝衛自居；我於彼前，現婆羅門身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現婆羅門身。若諸眾生，愛諸數術，攝衛自居者：謂有一類眾生，愛好天文地理，陰陽度數，曰數；醫卜命相，咒水書符，曰術；調攝身心，節勞靜神，曰攝；保衛生命，固精養氣，曰衛。如前文所云：求太陰精，用和幻藥，可以益壽，可以脩世。以此數術，攝衛自居，不求他學也。

我於彼前，現婆羅門身：婆羅門，此云淨裔，謂此是劫初梵天苗裔，苗裔即後裔。梵者淨也，又云梵志，以彼志在梵天故，菩薩現身，為說世智，調氣煉神之法，令其成就，數術攝衛之學也。

〖經〗若有男子，好學出家，持諸戒律；我於彼前，現比丘身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現比丘身。若有男子，好學出家：此是在俗男子，心厭塵勞，好學佛法，是以割愛辭親，捨俗出家。持諸戒律：初出家所受之沙彌十戒，進受比丘，二百五十戒。比丘名含三義，即五不翻中多含不翻。三義者：一、乞士：謂內乞法以資慧命，外乞食以養身命。二、怖魔：謂受比丘戒，登壇白四羯磨時，地行夜叉讚善，空行夜叉，天行夜叉，亦復讚善，輾轉聲傳六天，魔王聞之生怖，怖懼魔界減少，佛界增多。三、破惡：修持淨戒，能破身口七支之惡。我即現比丘身，為說戒、定、慧學，清白梵行之法，令其成就。

〖經〗若有女人，好學出家，持諸禁戒；我於彼前，現比丘尼身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現比丘尼身。若有女人，既厭塵勞之累，復嫌五障之軀：一者不得作梵天王；二者不得作帝釋；三者不得作魔王；四者不得作轉輪聖王；五者不得作佛。好樂學道脩行，出離世俗之家。持諸禁戒者：諸字包括之辭，尼有三百四十八戒，餘諸八敬等法，皆為佛所制禁戒。尼此云女，比丘尼，女人出家，受具戒者。我現比丘尼身，合其所慕。而為說法：即離染清淨，精脩梵行之法，令其具足三學，成就五德也。上現出家，秉教二眾。

〖經〗若有男子，樂持五戒；我於彼前，現優婆塞身，而為說法，令其成就。若有女子，五戒自居；我於彼前，現優婆夷身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現在家秉教二眾。設若有男子，志慕佛教，不能捨俗出家，但好樂[去聲]受持五戒。五戒為出家、在家，通脩之戒，即殺、盜、婬、妄、酒：一不殺害生命，恆存仁愛慈心；二不偷盜財物，見利自當思義；三不姦婬男女，[在家之眾，惟戒邪婬，夫妻正婬，仍當節欲]，克守世間禮教；四不虛妄語言，履踐信實之道。上四殺、盜、婬、妄是根本戒，又為性業，本性即是罪業，不特受戒者犯之有罪，不受之人犯之，國法亦皆治罪。五不飲酒昏迷，而失本有智性。酒本無罪，飲之昏迷失性，醉後糊塗，能為造罪之因，是為遮業，故佛遮止，不許飲酒。經中具說，飲酒有三十六種過失。

古時夏禹王，有臣名儀狄，制酒佳味，進貢於禹王，禹王飲之，知此酒能誤害於人，遂將儀狄貶於蘇海。又古有優婆塞，因酒而破五戒：因酒戒不能持，一日有酒無餚，鄰舍走來一雞，遂將盜藏。鄰舍覓雞，問曰見否？妄言不見，少頃殺而烹之，以酒燉雞，鄰婦聞氣味生疑，故來探視，遂留同飲同食，醉後調戲成姦，是則因酒，而五戒全破也。

此之男女，身處塵勞；樂持五戒：欲以五戒自居[即自守也]，以修其身。我於彼前，現優婆塞身：此云近事男，可以親近承事三寶之男子；現優婆夷身：即近事女。為說五戒，為人道因，五戒全持，則為上等人，若持四戒，未免美中不足，若持三戒，則為中等之人，如持二戒，則為中人以下。但持一戒，亦可不失人身，此人前生，持戒太少，雖得人身，極為愚癡苦惱，應當持滿分戒[即五戒全持]。此五戒，即儒教仁、義、禮、智、信五常。不殺仁也；不盜義也，不邪婬禮也；不妄語信也；不飲酒智也。為說五戒，令其成就清信士女之身。

〖經〗若有女人，內政立身，以脩家國；我於彼前，現女主身，及國夫人，命婦大家[音姑]，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現婦女身。內政立身者：女正位乎內，故曰內政。家門以內之事，女人主之，孝敬翁姑，教育子女，是為內政。貴而能勤，富而能儉，貞靜幽嫻，以修女德，名曰立身。閨門為萬化之源，關於國家之治亂興衰，曠觀自古及今，上自朝廷，下至家庭，無不皆然。女人又為教育之所係，母教更有力於師教，孟母即其證也。

以脩家國者：即內政立身，而身脩矣。感化家庭，關係國政，如岳武穆之母教，以成精忠報國之志。家通大夫以上，國通諸侯以上。女人如能內政立身，脩身，即為齊家治國之本。

我於彼前，觀其志願，或現女主身、即天子之後；或現國夫人身，即邦君之妻。君稱之曰夫人，國人稱之曰君夫人，一國主君之婦也。命婦，謂受朝廷誥命之婦，妻因夫榮、丈夫為官，女人受誥封也。大家、家訓姑，才德兼備，能為女主之師，如漢扶風[縣名]曹世叔之妻，乃同郡班彪之女和帝常召入宮，令皇后貴人師事之，世稱曹大家也。為說三從四德，端莊淑慎之法，令其成就。

〖經〗若有眾生，不壞男根；我於彼前，現童男身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現童男身。若有保守天真，不染欲塵之男子，天真未洩，男根不壞，有志終身持守，是謂童真。我於彼前，現童男身，為說守真抱璞，固精保元之法，令其成就童真也。

〖經〗若有處女，愛樂處身，不求侵暴；我於彼前，現童女身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗此現童女身。若有處女，亦名處子，乃未出閨門，未嫁之女子，愛樂[去聲好也]處女之身，白圭無玷，不求婚嫁。縱有強施侵暴，迫之令嫁，亦誓所不從，願終身不字，永為處女之身。我於彼前，即現處女身，為說堅貞美德，清淨自居之法，令其成就。上自天主至此，乃是大士以如幻三昧之力，應眾生希求之心，竿木隨身，逢場作戲，同事攝化之妙也。三應求人趣竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings delight in becoming a human king, I manifest, before them, the body of a human king, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a king. Chan Master Wenling Jiehuan said: from the gold-wheel king all the way down to the scattered-grain kings, all alike are called human kings. To explain: the world holds five kinds of human king. First, the gold-wheel king — that is, the wheel-turning sage-king — likewise complete with all thirty-two major marks, the seven treasures accompanying his own person, foremost among humankind, unsurpassed by any other. At the very moment he ascends the throne, a golden wheel-treasure flies down from the sky and arrives before him; riding upon this wheel-treasure, he is able, in a single day, to travel throughout all four great continents to the east, west, south, and north, every land submitting to him without exception, transforming the world through the ten wholesome deeds, and reigning over all four continents beneath the heavens. Second, the elephant-treasure, a white elephant king. Third, the horse-treasure, named Swift as the Wind. Fourth, the general-treasure, also called the minister-treasure who commands the army, named Eye Free of Defilement. Fifth, the treasurer-minister-treasure, wealth and treasure accompanying his own person, journeying together with the wheel-king — there being no need to carry any treasure at all, since, whenever the king wishes to give alms, wherever there is bare earth, the treasurer-minister has only to dig, and its use is inexhaustible. Sixth, the treasure-vase, which the king carries with him always — whatever he desires, it appears at once, exactly according to the wheel-king's own wish. Seventh, the woman-treasure — that is, the wheel-king's own foremost consort, named Pure and Wondrous Virtue, able to assist the king's own transformative rule; every land's own people, receiving the king's own transformative influence, cultivate the ten wholesome deeds without exception, the world entirely free of wicked persons — this, too, being brought about by the very power of blessed reward.

Second, the silver-wheel king: at the moment he ascends the throne, a silver wheel-treasure flies down from the sky, and, riding upon it, he is able, in a single day, to travel throughout the eastern, western, and southern continents (excluding the northern one), reigning over three continents beneath the heavens, every land submitting to him entirely. Third, the copper-wheel king, with a copper wheel-treasure, able to travel throughout the eastern and southern continents, every land coming under his own rule. Fourth, the iron-wheel king, with an iron wheel-treasure, able to travel throughout the southern continent alone, every land there coming under his own governance — King Aśoka being just such a king. Fifth, the scattered-grain kings — that is, every land's own minor kings, scattered about like grains of millet, found everywhere across the earth. "Sentient beings" here means humankind; "delight in becoming a human king" — a king is one to whom all turn: cherishing benevolence and spreading virtue, the four directions turning toward him, wishing to become a ruler of the path, governing the nation and its lands. I manifest, before them, the body of a human king, and expound the five precepts and the ten wholesome deeds, as the cause for a noble birth, and expound the virtuous conduct proper to emperors and kings, habituating the seed that carries across to the next life, bringing about their accomplishment of this wholesome cause, and their attainment of its blessed fruit. One might ask: "should someone harbor thoughts of usurpation and rebellion, scheming to seize the throne itself, would the bodhisattva still manifest a body and expound the Dharma to them?" The answer: "the bodhisattva's own aspiration lies entirely in benefiting beings — how could they ever assist rebellion?"

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings love to head a clan of shared surname, honored and deferred to throughout the world, I manifest, before them, the body of an elder, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of an elder. In our own land, one advanced in years and weighty in virtue may simply be called an elder; in the Western Regions, the title is not so easily granted — one must possess ten virtues in full: noble birth, meaning a distinguished kṣatriya lineage, a great and honored family; high position, meaning a chief minister or chancellor, a senior official of the court; great wealth, meaning abundant riches, granaries filled to overflowing; a commanding bearing, meaning a dignity as forbidding as frost, awe-inspiring to behold; profound wisdom, meaning a mind like the sun and moon, wisdom brilliant as pearls; advanced age, meaning venerable years and long-accumulated virtue, growing only more vigorous with age; pure conduct, meaning a model fit to draw near to, a standard worth emulating; complete propriety, meaning a bearing composed and dignified, meeting others with easy harmony; praised from above, meaning praised by rulers themselves; and relied upon from below, meaning the very refuge the common people turn to. Only one complete with these ten virtues may properly be called an elder — hence listed here even before officials.

"Sentient beings" who love to head a single clan of shared surname, and further wish to be honored throughout the world, deferred to everywhere as foremost — I manifest, before them, the body of an elder, and expound the teaching of giving generously to aid the multitude, benevolence toward the people and care for all things, bringing about their accomplishment.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings love to discuss illustrious sayings, dwelling in purity of themselves, I manifest, before them, the body of a lay scholar, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a lay scholar. "Sentient beings" who love to discuss the illustrious figures of past and present, their fine sayings and canonical writings, fit to serve as a model and standard, instructing the world; "dwelling in purity of themselves" means dwelling amid the toil of the world while the mind remains ever pure, unstained by worldly desire, dwelling by the path alone. I manifest, before them, the body of a lay scholar, and expound the teaching of a pure mind with few desires, keeping oneself unstained and cultivating one's own person, bringing about their accomplishment. A lay scholar is exactly one who dwells by the path alone — that is, one who cultivates practice while remaining within lay life; some hold both virtue and position, as with Su Dongpo; others hold virtue without position, as with Wang Tong or Shao Yong, withdrawn from the world, never taking office.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings love to govern the nation's own lands, adjudicating its provinces and districts, I manifest, before them, the body of an official, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of an official. "Sentient beings" who love to govern the nation's own territory, assisting its administration — Wenling identifies these as the Three Ducal ministers themselves — able to set order and establish discipline for the court, and to transform customs and reform habits among the people. "Adjudicating its provinces and districts": the larger units are called provinces, the smaller ones districts; "adjudicating" means clearing away injustice and grievance, and "deciding" means settling right from wrong — as with a single word that resolves a lawsuit, litigation kept simple and punishment kept clear. I manifest, before them, the body of an official — "governing" meaning exactly to administer, and "official" meaning exactly one who serves the public — from the Three Ducal ministers above, down to a prefect or magistrate below, all alike called officials. The bodhisattva manifests this body, and expounds the teaching of cultivating oneself, ordering one's household, governing the state, and bringing peace to the world, of protecting the nation and cherishing its people, bringing about their accomplishment.

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings love every art of calculation and technique, dwelling by regimens of self-preservation, I manifest, before them, the body of a brahmin, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a brahmin. "Sentient beings" who love every art of calculation and technique, dwelling by regimens of self-preservation: there exists a class of beings who love astronomy and geomancy, the calculations of yin and yang — called "calculation"; medicine, divination, physiognomy, water-charms and talismans — called "technique"; regulating body and mind, conserving effort and stilling the spirit — called "preservation"; and guarding one's own life, securing one's essence and nurturing one's vital energy — called "self-protection." As an earlier passage in this sūtra put it: seeking the essence of the great yin, mixed into an illusion-conjuring elixir, capable of extending life and of transforming the world — dwelling by these very arts and regimens, seeking no other learning at all.

I manifest, before them, the body of a brahmin: "brahmin" here means "pure descendant," referring to a descendant of Brahmā from the very beginning of the kalpa — "descendant" meaning exactly a later generation. "Brahmā" means "pure," also called "one whose aspiration is set on Brahmā," since their own aspiration is fixed on the Brahmā-heaven. The bodhisattva manifests this body, and expounds worldly wisdom, the methods of regulating vital energy and refining the spirit, bringing about their accomplishment in these very arts of calculation and regimens of self-preservation.

Sūtra: "Should any man delight in learning and leaving home, keeping every precept and monastic rule, I manifest, before him, the body of a monk, and expound the Dharma, bringing about his accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a monk. "Any man who delights in learning and leaving home" describes a man still in lay life, his own mind wearied by worldly toil, delighting in learning the Buddha-dharma, and so cutting off attachment and taking leave of his family, forsaking lay life to leave the household. "Keeping every precept and monastic rule": first, upon leaving home, one receives the novice's own ten precepts; advancing further, one receives the monk's own two hundred fifty precepts. The word "bhikṣu" carries three senses, one of the terms among the "five untranslated" left in the original Sanskrit for carrying multiple senses at once. The three senses: first, "one who begs" — begging the Dharma within, to nourish the life of wisdom, and begging food without, to sustain the life of the body. Second, "one who frightens Māra" — for, when one receives the bhikṣu precepts, ascending the platform and completing the fourfold formal proclamation, the earth-treading yakṣas praise this good deed, the sky-traveling and heaven-traveling yakṣas likewise praise it, this word passing on in turn all the way up through the six heavens, until the Māra-king, hearing of it, is struck with dread — dreading that his own realm of Māra will diminish, while the Buddha's own realm increases. Third, "one who destroys evil" — cultivating and upholding pure precepts, able to destroy the seven evils belonging to body and speech. I then manifest the body of a monk, and expound the trainings of precepts, samādhi, and wisdom, the teaching of a pure and undefiled conduct, bringing about his accomplishment.

Sūtra: "Should any woman delight in learning and leaving home, keeping every precept and prohibition, I manifest, before her, the body of a nun, and expound the Dharma, bringing about her accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a nun. Should any woman, already wearied by the burden of worldly toil, further resent the five obstructions bound to her own body — unable to become a Brahmā-king, unable to become Śakra, unable to become a Māra-king, unable to become a wheel-turning sage-king, unable to become a buddha — and delight, instead, in learning the path and cultivating practice, leaving behind her own worldly household. "Keeping every precept and prohibition": the word "every" is inclusive here — a nun holds three hundred forty-eight precepts, together with the eight special rules of respect and other such rules, all alike prescribed and prohibited by the Buddha himself. "Nun" here means "woman" — a bhikṣuṇī being exactly a woman who has left the household and received the full precepts. I manifest the body of a nun, matching what she herself aspires to, and expound the Dharma — that is, the teaching of leaving defilement behind for purity, diligently cultivating a pure conduct, bringing about her completeness in the three trainings, and her accomplishment of the five virtues. The above manifests the two assemblies of those who have left the household, receiving the teaching directly.

Sūtra: "Should any man delight in keeping the five precepts, I manifest, before him, the body of an upāsaka, and expound the Dharma, bringing about his accomplishment; should any woman dwell by the five precepts, I manifest, before her, the body of an upāsikā, and expound the Dharma, bringing about her accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the two lay assemblies, receiving the teaching directly. Suppose there is a man whose aspiration turns toward the Buddhist teaching, yet who cannot leave lay life for the household — he simply delights in receiving and keeping the five precepts. The five precepts are the precepts held in common by monastic and lay practitioners alike: against killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and alcohol. First, not to kill or harm any living creature, holding always to a benevolent, compassionate mind. Second, not to steal another's property, considering what is right whenever gain presents itself. Third, not to engage in sexual license (lay practitioners are enjoined against illicit sexual conduct alone — sexual relations proper to husband and wife remain permitted, though desire itself should still be tempered), upholding the world's own proper conduct. Fourth, not to speak falsely, keeping always to a path of honest, trustworthy speech. These first four — killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech — are the root precepts, and are also called offenses of inherent nature: their very nature is itself already an offense, and so, not only does one who has received the precepts incur an offense by transgressing them, but even one who has never received them incurs a punishment under secular law for the same transgression. Fifth, not to drink alcohol to the point of confusion, thereby losing one's own inherent wisdom-nature. Alcohol itself carries no inherent offense — but drinking it to the point of confusion and losing one's own clarity, growing muddled while drunk, can become the very cause of committing further offenses; this is called an offense of prohibition, and so the Buddha prohibits it, forbidding the drinking of alcohol. This sūtra elsewhere sets out, in full, thirty-six distinct faults that come from drinking alcohol.

In ancient times, King Yu of the Xia dynasty had a minister named Yi Di, who brewed a fine-tasting wine and presented it as tribute to King Yu; King Yu, tasting it, recognized that this wine was capable of leading people astray and doing them harm, and so banished Yi Di to the distant seas of Su. And, in ancient times, there was once an upāsaka who broke all five precepts on account of alcohol: unable to keep his own precept against drinking, one day, having wine but no food to go with it, a neighbor's chicken wandered over, and he stole it away and hid it. The neighbor came looking for the chicken and asked whether he had seen it — he lied and said he had not, and, shortly after, killed and cooked it, stewing it together with his wine. A neighboring woman, smelling the aroma and growing suspicious, came to investigate, and he invited her to stay and share the food and drink with him; once drunk, he made improper advances toward her, and the two committed adultery together. In this way, on account of alcohol alone, all five precepts were broken at once.

These men and women, though dwelling amid worldly toil, delight in keeping the five precepts — wishing to dwell by these five precepts, keeping to them, cultivating their own persons thereby. I manifest, before them, the body of an upāsaka — meaning "a man who draws near and serves," a man able to draw close to and personally serve the Three Jewels — and the body of an upāsikā, meaning exactly the same for a woman. I expound the five precepts, as the cause of the human path: keeping all five precepts in full makes one a person of the highest grade; keeping only four leaves something regrettably wanting; keeping only three makes one a person of the middling grade; keeping only two, a person below the middling grade. Even keeping a single precept is enough not to lose a human body in the next life — though such a person, having kept so few precepts in a past life, though still attaining a human body, remains extremely foolish and beset with suffering; one ought, therefore, to keep the precepts in their full measure (that is, to keep all five in full). These five precepts are exactly the Confucian five constant virtues of benevolence, righteousness, propriety, wisdom, and trustworthiness: not killing is benevolence; not stealing is righteousness; not engaging in sexual misconduct is propriety; not speaking falsely is trustworthiness; not drinking alcohol is wisdom. I expound these five precepts, bringing about their accomplishment as a pure and faithful man or woman.

Sūtra: "Should any woman establish herself through the governance of her own household, cultivating both family and nation thereby, I manifest, before her, the body of a queen, a noblewoman, a titled matron, or a female tutor, and expound the Dharma, bringing about her accomplishment."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a woman. "Establishing herself through the governance of the household": a woman's own rightful place is within — hence, "the governance of the household." Everything within the family's own gate is a woman's own charge: showing filial reverence to her parents-in-law, raising and educating her children — this is exactly the governance of the household. Noble yet diligent, wealthy yet frugal, chaste, calm, and reserved in cultivating her own womanly virtue — this is called "establishing herself." The women's own quarters are the very source of every transformation, bound up with the order or disorder, the rise or fall, of the nation itself — surveying from ancient times to the present, from the royal court above down to the household below, this holds true without exception. Women, moreover, are bound up with education itself — a mother's own teaching carries even greater force than a teacher's, as Mencius's own mother bears witness.

"Cultivating both family and nation": this is exactly establishing herself through the governance of the household, her own person thereby cultivated, moving her family by this same influence, bound up with the nation's own governance — as with the mother of Yue Fei, whose own teaching brought about his resolve of utter loyalty in service of the nation. "Family" extends to the rank of a great officer and above; "nation" extends to the rank of a feudal lord and above. Should a woman be able to establish herself through the governance of her own household, cultivating her own person thereby, this is exactly the very root of an ordered family and a well-governed nation.

I then, before her, observing her own aspiration, manifest either the body of a queen — that is, the consort of the Son of Heaven himself — or the body of a noblewoman — that is, the wife of a feudal lord, called "lady" by her own lord and "the lord's own lady" by the people of the realm, the wife of the one ruler of a single nation. A titled matron is one who has received formal investiture from the court itself, a wife ennobled through her own husband's honor, her husband holding office and she herself receiving this same investiture. A "family tutor," addressed as "aunt," is complete with both talent and virtue, able to serve as a queen's own teacher — as with the wife of Cao Shishu of Fufeng in the Han dynasty, herself the daughter of Ban Biao of the very same commandery, whom Emperor He of Han often summoned into the palace, having the empress and honored ladies study under her as their teacher, known to the age as "Madame Cao, the family tutor." I expound the teaching of the three forms of deference and the four virtues, of dignified, virtuous, and prudent conduct, bringing about her accomplishment.

Sūtra: "If there are those who wish to preserve their innocence as a young man, I manifest before them the body of a youth and expound the Dharma, bringing about their success."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a youth. Should there be a man who preserves his own heaven-given innocence, unstained by the sense-object of desire, this innocence never spent, his own manhood never violated, resolved to hold to this for his whole life — this is called "preserving innocence." I manifest, before him, the body of a youth, and expound the teaching of guarding this innocence and holding to simplicity, securing one's essence and preserving one's original vitality, bringing about his accomplishment of this same innocence.

Sūtra: "If there are maidens who love their unmarried state and do not wish to be violated, I manifest before them the body of a maiden and expound the Dharma, bringing about their success."

Commentary: This manifests the body of a maiden. A "maiden," also called an unmarried girl, is one not yet given in marriage, still within her own family's gate; "loving her unmarried state" means loving to remain a maiden, spotless as unblemished jade, seeking no marriage at all. Even should force be used against her, compelling her toward marriage, she vows never to submit, resolved to remain unbetrothed her whole life through, forever a maiden. I manifest, before her, the body of a maiden, and expound the teaching of steadfast, virtuous chastity, dwelling in purity of herself, bringing about her accomplishment. Everything from the Brahmā-king down to this point is exactly the great being's own use of the illusion-like samādhi's own power, answering the very hopes and longings of sentient beings — carrying, as it were, one's own staff and puppet-stage wherever one goes, performing however the occasion calls for, working alongside beings to gather and transform them, this wondrous skill. This closes the third group, responding to those who seek the human realm.

To Those Wishing Release From the Eight Kinds of Spirits 午四 應離八部

Chinese Source

〖經〗若有諸天，樂出天倫；我現天身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗以上諸科，皆應希求，令得成就，出世入世之願望。以下諸科，皆應厭離，令得成就，出離本位，得生人道。問：「餘趣求生人道，是事不疑，天本勝人，何以反求，轉入人道？」答：「天道雖樂，樂有終盡，五衰相現，畢竟無常。又則天人著樂，不肯脩行。裴相國云：整心慮趣菩提，惟人道為能耳。所以諸佛皆從人中得道，三乘聖人，亦皆人身脩證。」故諸天樂出天倫，得生人道，既得人身，可成四聖也。我現天身，為說無常，苦、空、無我之法，令得成就也。

〖經〗若有諸龍，樂出龍倫，我現龍身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗灌頂云：梵語那伽，此云龍。龍有四類：一守天宮殿、二興雲降雨、三開瀆決江、四守護伏藏。其類不一，故曰諸龍。過去亦曾修福，所住宮殿，亦為寶成，身能變化，多化人形，惟五時不免蛇形：生時、眠時、婬時、瞋時、死時。《長阿含經》明龍有三患：一熱沙炙身、二風壞宮衣、三金翅鳥噉。因有多苦，故樂出離。《阿含經》又云：就因先世多瞋，心曲不端，犯戒鬥諍，故墮龍中，由大行布施福力，故七寶為殿。我現龍身，為說布施持戒，正直柔和，仁慈謙讓等法，令其成就。

〖經〗若有藥叉，樂度本倫；我於彼前，現藥叉身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗梵語藥叉，此云捷疾，其行捷疾故；亦名勇健，勇猛強健故；或云暴惡，其形暴惡故。有地行、空行、飛行三種。《最勝王經》云：是等藥叉悉皆愛樂如來正法，深心護持。樂度本倫者：因受佛法熏習之力，知暴惡為墮緣，故樂度脫本倫。我現藥叉身，為說持戒脩福，柔和善順之法，令其成就人淪；若能兼修慧業，亦可成就聖倫也。

〖經〗若乾闥婆，樂脫其倫；我於彼前，現乾闥婆身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗乾闥婆，此云香陰，在須彌山南，金剛窟住。此神不噉酒肉，惟食諸香，以資五陰，是帝釋樂神。帝釋須樂，燒沉水香，此神尋香而至。好樂脫離本倫，我於彼前，現乾闥婆身，為說離於放逸，及五戒、中品十善之法，令其成就。

〖經〗若阿脩羅，樂脫其倫；我於彼前，現阿脩羅身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗阿脩羅此云非天，有天福無天德故，此指化生阿脩羅，是天趣攝。更有胎生人趣攝，卵生鬼趣攝，濕生畜趣攝。亦云無端正，男丑女美，無端正男故。長阿含經云：南洲金剛山，有脩羅宮，六千由旬，欄楯行樹，每日三時，苦具自至，刀鎗劍戟，從空飛入宮中，脩羅若不躲避，必受殺傷，故生厭離，樂脫其倫。名義集云：由在因時，雖行五常，懷猜忌心，欲勝他故，作下品十善，感脩羅身。我於彼前現阿脩羅身，為說慈忍謙恭，虛心受教，及中品十善之法，令其成就。

按《法華經普門品》，此部之後有迦樓羅，此云金翅鳥，以龍為食，龍求佛救，佛授袈裟，俾纏龍角，乃至纏袈裟一縷者，金翅烏皆不敢食。鳥亦往佛求救，佛曰汝無人食汝，何為求救？鳥曰：我無龍食，則必餓死，佛安可不救？佛曰：汝遵吾教，不可食龍，我每日受食時，布施汝食。現在侍者施食，偈云：「大鵬金翅鳥，曠野鬼神眾，羅剎鬼子母，甘露悉充滿。」故亦樂脫其倫。大士現同類身，為說仁慈愛物之法，令其成就。此經無此部，諒係抄寫之脫漏也。

〖經〗若緊那羅，樂脫其倫；我於彼前，現緊那羅身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗緊那羅此云疑神，其形似人，頭有一角，見者生疑，唐翻歌神，是帝釋唱歌之神，貌丑而音美。《菩薩處胎經》云：須彌山北，十寶山間，有緊那羅，於中治化，由昔布施之力，居七寶殿，壽命極長，天欲奏樂，腋下汗流，便自上天。帝釋請佛，諸天絃歌而頌法門者，即是此神。今樂脫其倫者，大士為現同類身，為說歌詠亂心，欲樂無常，及中品十善之法，令其成就。

〖經〗若摩呼羅伽，樂脫其倫，我於彼前，現摩呼羅伽身，而為說法，令其成就。

〖講〗摩呼羅伽此云大蟒神，亦云地龍，而無神通，乃屬腹行，較之天龍，其苦自倍。此類因心中多癡恚，故所感之報，聾騃無知，常含毒傷生，必更墮落。今樂脫本類，求生人道，大士為現同類身，為說修慧、修慈，忍辱柔和，及中品十善之法，令其成就，四應離八部竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Should any of the gods delight in leaving behind their own kind, I manifest a deva's body, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: Every category above responds to what is hoped for and sought, bringing about accomplishment of an aspiration either toward the transcendent or toward engagement with the world. Every category below, by contrast, responds to a wish for release, bringing about a being's leaving behind their own present station and attaining birth in the human path instead. One might ask: "that beings in every other destiny should wish for rebirth in the human path raises no doubt at all — but the heavens are inherently superior to the human realm, so why would they, in turn, seek to be reborn as humans instead?" The answer: "though the heavenly path is pleasurable, this pleasure eventually comes to its own end, the five signs of decline appearing at last, impermanence unavoidable in the end. Moreover, the gods, attached to their own pleasure, are unwilling to cultivate practice at all. As Chancellor Pei put it: setting one's mind and thought toward bodhi is something only the human path is capable of — and this is exactly why every buddha has attained the path from within the human realm, and every sage of the three vehicles has likewise cultivated and realized this same path within a human body." And so, should the gods delight in leaving their own kind behind, attaining birth in the human path, and, having attained a human body, they may then go on to accomplish any of the four sagely fruits. I manifest a deva's body, and expound the teaching of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, bringing about their accomplishment.

Sūtra: "Should any of the nāgas delight in leaving behind their own kind, I manifest a nāga's body, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: The Consecration Commentary says: the Sanskrit nāga is translated "dragon." There are four kinds of dragon: those guarding heavenly palaces, those raising clouds and bringing down rain, those cutting channels and opening rivers, and those guarding hidden treasures — their kinds not being uniform, hence "the nāgas," plural. Having, in ages past, likewise cultivated merit, the palaces they now dwell in are themselves formed of treasure, their own bodies able to transform, most often taking human form — only at five particular times unable to avoid taking the form of a serpent: at birth, in sleep, in sexual union, in anger, and at death. The Dīrgha Āgama makes plain that dragons suffer three afflictions: scorching sand burning their bodies, wind destroying their palaces and garments, and being devoured by the garuḍa — and, having so much suffering, they long to be free of this state. The Āgama further says: because of excessive anger in a past life, a crooked and dishonest mind, transgressing precepts and engaging in quarrels and strife, one falls into the dragon's own state — yet, through the power of great generosity practiced in the past, their own palaces are formed of the seven treasures. I manifest a dragon's body, and expound the teaching of generosity and upholding the precepts, uprightness and gentleness, benevolence, compassion, and humility, bringing about their accomplishment.

Sūtra: "Should any yakṣa delight in being freed from their own kind, I manifest, before them, the body of a yakṣa, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: The Sanskrit yakṣa is translated "swift" — for their movement is swift indeed; also called "valiant," for their courage and strength; or "fierce," for their fierce appearance. There are three kinds: earth-traveling, sky-traveling, and flying. The Sūtra of the Most Victorious King says: these yakṣas, every one of them, love and delight in the Tathāgata's own true Dharma, guarding and upholding it with a profound mind. "Delight in being freed from their own kind": through the power of the Buddha-dharma's own habituating influence, knowing that fierceness itself is the very condition of their own fall, they long to be freed from this same kind. I manifest a yakṣa's body, and expound the teaching of upholding the precepts and cultivating merit, of gentleness and ready compliance, bringing about their accomplishment of a human birth — and, should they further cultivate the practice of wisdom as well, they may also come to accomplish a sagely birth.

Sūtra: "Should any gandharva delight in being freed from their own kind, I manifest, before them, the body of a gandharva, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: A gandharva is translated "fragrance-aggregate," dwelling in the vajra cave on the southern slope of Mount Sumeru. This spirit eats neither meat nor drinks wine, feeding only on various fragrances to nourish its own five aggregates — it is Śakra's own musician-spirit. Whenever Śakra desires music, aloeswood incense is burned, and this spirit follows the fragrance to arrive. Delighting in being freed from their own kind, I manifest, before them, the body of a gandharva, and expound the teaching of leaving behind indulgence and idleness, together with the five precepts and the middling grade of the ten wholesome deeds, bringing about their accomplishment.

Sūtra: "Should any asura delight in being freed from their own kind, I manifest, before them, the body of an asura, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: Asura is translated "non-deva," possessing the blessings of a deva without a deva's own virtue. This refers to the asuras born by transformation, classed among the heavenly destinies; there are further asuras born from a womb, classed among the human destiny, born from an egg, classed among the ghost destiny, and born from moisture, classed among the animal destiny. They are also called "without proper form," the males ugly and the females beautiful — hence, "without proper form" for the males specifically. The Dīrgha Āgama says: upon the vajra mountain of the southern continent stands an asura palace, six thousand yojanas across, with balustrades and rows of trees; three times each day, instruments of suffering arrive of themselves, swords, spears, and halberds flying down from the sky into the palace, and, should an asura fail to dodge them, they are certain to be wounded or killed — and so they grow weary of this state, and long to be free of their own kind. The Compendium of Names and Meanings says: because, at the causal stage, though practicing the five constant virtues, they harbored a suspicious, jealous mind, wishing to surpass others, and so performed only the lesser grade of the ten wholesome deeds, they came to incur the asura's own body as their result. I manifest, before them, the body of an asura, and expound the teaching of compassionate forbearance and humble respect, an open mind receptive to instruction, together with the middling grade of the ten wholesome deeds, bringing about their accomplishment.

According to the Universal Gate chapter of the Lotus Sūtra, after this same group there should follow the garuḍa, translated "golden-winged bird," which feeds on dragons. The dragons sought the Buddha's own rescue, and the Buddha gave them his own kāṣāya robe, having them wind it about their own horns — and even a dragon wound with no more than a single thread from this robe, the golden-winged bird would not dare devour. The bird, too, came to the Buddha seeking help, and the Buddha asked: "no one is eating you — why do you seek my help?" The bird answered: "without dragons to eat, I am certain to starve to death — how could the Buddha not rescue me as well?" The Buddha said: "follow my own teaching, and eat no more dragons — each day, when I myself receive my meal, I will give alms of food to you." The attendant who now offers food recites this very verse: "great garuḍa, golden-winged bird, and every ghost and spirit of the wilderness, rākṣasas, and Hārītī, mother of ghost-children — may this sweet dew fill you all completely." And so this being, too, longs to be freed from its own kind. The great being manifests a body of this same kind, and expounds the teaching of benevolence and compassion, cherishing every creature, bringing about their accomplishment. This present sūtra text lacks this group entirely — surely nothing more than an omission in copying.

Sūtra: "Should any kiṃnara delight in being freed from their own kind, I manifest, before them, the body of a kiṃnara, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: A kiṃnara is translated "doubtful spirit," its own form resembling a human, with a single horn upon its head, causing whoever sees it to feel uncertain what it is; the Tang dynasty translated it "song-spirit," being Śakra's own singing spirit, ugly in appearance yet beautiful in voice. The Sūtra on Bodhisattvas in the Womb says: north of Mount Sumeru, among the ten treasure-mountains, dwell the kiṃnaras, governing that region — through the power of generosity practiced in ages past, they dwell in palaces of the seven treasures, their own lifespans extremely long; whenever the heavens wish for music, sweat appears beneath their own arms, and they ascend to the heavens of themselves. When Śakra invites the Buddha, and the devas sing and play stringed instruments in praise of the Dharma-gate, it is exactly this same spirit performing. Now, longing to be freed from their own kind, the great being manifests a body of this same kind, and expounds the teaching that song and verse disturb the mind, that pleasure itself is impermanent, together with the middling grade of the ten wholesome deeds, bringing about their accomplishment.

Sūtra: "Should any mahoraga delight in being freed from their own kind, I manifest, before them, the body of a mahoraga, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: A mahoraga is translated "great python spirit," also called "earth-dragon" — possessing no supernatural power at all, moving instead upon its own belly, its own suffering doubly worse than the heavenly dragons. This kind, because of a mind heavy with delusion and hatred, incurs, as its own result, a deaf and witless ignorance, forever carrying poison and injuring living things, certain to fall further still. Now, longing to be freed from their own kind, seeking birth in the human path instead, the great being manifests a body of this same kind, and expounds the teaching of cultivating wisdom and compassion, forbearance and gentleness, together with the middling grade of the ten wholesome deeds, bringing about their accomplishment. This closes the fourth group, responding to those wishing release from the eight kinds of spirits.

To Humans Who Cultivate as Humans, and to Every Non-Human Being 午五 應人修人 · 午六 應離非人

Sūtra: "Should sentient beings delight in remaining human and cultivating as a human, I manifest a human body, and expound the Dharma, bringing about their accomplishment."

Commentary: "Sentient beings" is a name held in common across all six destinies. Set against the phrase that follows, "delight in remaining human and cultivating as a human" refers exactly to the human path — delighting in remaining human, lifetime after lifetime. As this sūtra says elsewhere: a human body is difficult to attain; having already attained one in this present life, one delights in cultivating and upholding the human path, so as not to lose this human body in the life to come — hence, "delight in remaining human and cultivating as a human." Sūtra: "This is called the thirty-two wondrous, pure bodies I manifest to enter into every land, all accomplished freely through the effortless, wondrous power of the samādhi of hearing, its habituation, and its cultivation."

Commentary: The first three lines here point to the bodies manifested; the last four give the very root of this arising function. "This" refers to answering the capacities of all nine Dharma-realms, displaying a bodily form through this universal gate. This closes and lists the overall name given above: called the thirty-two wondrous, pure bodies I manifest to enter into every land — manifesting all at once, each answering its own kind, hence "wondrous"; the bodily forms manifested clinging to no fixed form of their own, hence "pure." And "wondrous" means exactly inconceivable, while "pure" means exactly free of any defiling attachment — this being the very manifest proof that, having already realized perfect penetration, the great being has attained the three inconceivable wheels. First, through the wheel of mind, discerning each capacity with certainty; then, whatever body will bring liberation, that very body is manifested, and whatever dharma will bring liberation, that very dharma is expounded — the three wheels of body, speech, and mind, all alike inconceivable, called "wondrous"; each and every one free of any defiling attachment, called "pure" — and this is exactly these thirty-two wondrous, pure responses. "Entering into every land": that is, throughout every land of the ten directions, there is no realm in which this body does not manifest — with a single body universally entering every realm, performing the Buddha's own work within every realm at once. This wondrous function is exactly what arises from the very essence of perfect penetration, entirely through the power of the samādhi cultivated and realized through the ear-gate. "Through the habituation of hearing and cultivation of hearing": through original awakening's own nature of hearing, habituating from within, giving rise to the wondrous wisdom of initial awakening, carrying out the effort of turning hearing back and taking it up in practice, turning hearing back upon one's own nature at every moment, transforming and habituating away every clinging habit, untying the six knots, breaking through the five aggregates, falseness exhausted and truth laid bare, arising and ceasing themselves extinguished, still cessation appearing of itself. "Accomplished through this effortless, wondrous power": that is, a power and function inconceivable, requiring not the slightest deliberate intention at all — a single body, undivided, manifesting universally; every capacity, without exception, answered without the slightest failure — exactly like a single moon in the sky, its reflection falling upon every body of water at once; does the moon deliberately intend to fall upon the water? Wherever there is water in a thousand rivers, the moon appears in every one of them — even an ordinary, worldly thing behaves this way; how much more so the great being's own wondrous power of perfect penetration? "Accomplished freely": that is, responding spontaneously, wherever a call is felt, an answer follows at once — a thousand places entreating, a thousand places answered, an ever-ready boat ferrying beings across the sea of suffering.

These thirty-two responsive bodies manifested above, and the Universal Gate chapter of the Lotus Sūtra, alternately include and omit one another: as with the heavenly destiny, this sūtra includes the prince of one of the Four Heavenly Kings, which that other sūtra lacks; and, within the human destiny, this sūtra includes the queen and the noblewoman, which that other sūtra likewise lacks. Among the eight kinds of spirits, that other sūtra includes the garuḍa; and, beyond the eight kinds of spirits, that other sūtra includes the vajra-bearing spirit, neither of which this present sūtra lists at all. The great being's own wondrous responsiveness knows no fixed direction, its spiritual transformations beyond all reckoning — these thirty-two responses are themselves no more than a brief account; in truth, this responsive transformation is inexhaustible, and so the two sūtras each select and omit as they see fit, arriving at this same number of thirty-two. 〖經〗一者：由我不自觀音，以觀觀者。令彼十方苦惱眾生，觀其音聲，即得解脫。

〖講〗此苦惱難無畏。八難中此一為總，餘七為別。逼迫外身曰苦，逼迫內心曰惱。上三句出自修之本，下四句彰利他之用。一者由我不自觀音以觀觀者：觀為能觀之智，智光不外照，即不自觀世間之音聲，以觀能觀者是誰？上句是離塵，下句為照性，亦即脫黏內伏，背塵合覺，由此聞熏聞修，金剛三昧，無作妙力，加被眾生；故能令十方苦惱眾生，但能一心稱我名號，我則觀其稱念音聲，尋聲救苦，令其即得解脫苦惱，則苦惱無畏矣！

此即觀世音菩薩，果上得名之因緣。《法華經普門品》，無盡意菩薩問佛：觀世音菩薩以何因緣，名觀世音？佛告無盡意菩薩：善男子，若有無量百千萬億眾生，受諸苦惱，聞是觀世音菩薩，一心稱名[即稱念菩薩名號]，觀世音菩薩，即時觀其音聲，皆得解脫[觀世間稱念菩薩名號音聲，故名觀世音。]。彼經但約菩薩，果上利他而立名，故不說菩薩因中所修自利之行。此經雙約兩利，由得自利行成，方能利他也。

或有以觀其音聲句，作眾生受苦惱時，自己觀其音聲。交師非之當矣！因此經苦惱眾生下，略去一心稱名等字，致有如是錯解，交師加蒙我二字，意即顯矣！眾生在苦惱難中，能有幾人解作觀？即使能作觀得脫，與總標之文，於我身心，獲十四種無畏功德，亦不能合。試詳察焉。

〖經〗二者：知見旋復，令諸眾生，設入大火，火不能燒。

〖講〗此大火難無畏。知見二字，包括六根見、聞、嗅、嘗、覺、知。旋復：即旋妄復真，旋轉緣塵之妄知妄見，復歸自性之真知真見。大士用耳根，反聞入流工夫，旋聞與聲脫，既旋彼妄聞，而與聲塵脫離，則根塵不偶，一根既返源，六根成解脫，故諸根一一皆能旋妄復真。知見旋復，則內見覺之大火既息，而外世間之火，不能為害。

〖經〗三者：觀聽旋復，令諸眾生，大水所漂，水不能溺。

〖講〗此大水難無畏。觀聽旋復：此句是妙力，下是大用。謂由反觀聽聞之性，旋彼妄聞，復歸真聞，聽聞屬水，故聞業交，則見大水，今既旋聞復性，則無聞業，是以水不能溺。令諸眾生，大水所漂，水不能溺：亦即大士自證之力，加被眾生，故大水難無畏矣！

唐岑文本，字景仁，棘陽人，少信佛，嘗誦普門品，一日往吳江舟覆，文本亦溺水中，俄聞有人云：能誦普門品，水難應免。如是者三，遂浮水面，須臾水浪漂送岸邊，為人所救，得免於難。

〖經〗四者：斷滅妄想，心無殺害，令諸眾生，入諸鬼國，鬼不能害。

〖講〗此羅剎難無畏。妄想乃是第六意識，前佛破識非心文云：此非汝心，乃是前塵，虛妄相想，喻之如賊。能殺害眾生法身慧命，如同羅剎能食人，深可怖畏。大士反聞入流，外不緣塵，內不循根，根塵不偶，識心亦滅，故曰斷滅妄想。妄想既滅，心無殺害，全超鬼神心行；以此威力，加被眾生，故能令眾生，諸羅剎鬼國，但能一心稱名，鬼不能害，則羅剎難無畏矣！傳云：師子國有入商賈，一百餘人，航海而來，忽遇惡風[即鬼風]，將船飄墮羅剎鬼國，許多羅剎女來歡迎。內有一商人多智，知入羅剎鬼國，乃號召於眾曰：今者船入鬼國，汝等當一心稱念觀世音菩薩名號，得免於難。眾即依言稱念，忽起大風，將船吹出，速達我國，足證聖言之不虛也。

〖講〗此刀兵難無畏。熏聞成聞者：大士反聞照性，本覺內熏，熏彼妄聞，而成真聞，耳根如是，銷妄復真，六根悉皆銷復。後偈云：一根既返源，六根成解脫；同於聲塵聽聞之性，一一復歸元真，塵亡根盡；根塵既銷，云何覺明，不成圓妙。大士以此自證，金剛三昧，不動不壞之本，加被眾生，能令眾生，臨當被害，刀段段壞，即大士金剛三昧之力，加被被害眾生，身同金剛，故其刀觸身，刀即段段壞。縱然其刀不壞，其身亦無所損，使其兵戈，猶如刀割水，水無斷痕，如風吹光[日光也]，光不息滅。以能觸之刀，色塵即藏性；所觸之身，身根亦藏性；藏性合藏性，即同以空合空，性無搖動，則刀兵難無畏矣！齊書孫敬德，防守北陲，造觀音像，每日禮事，後為賊橫引，判處死刑。有一梵僧，教誦觀音經千遍[即普門品]，後臨刑刀成三段，其首無傷。丞相為奏免。又如六祖，刺客三揮利刃，猶如斬影，刃從頭過，頭無所傷，此乃六祖，自證之力耳。

〖經〗六者：聞熏精明，明遍法界，則諸幽暗，性不能全，能令眾生。藥叉羅剎，鳩槃茶鬼，及毗舍遮，富單那等，雖近其旁，目不能視。

〖講〗此諸鬼難無畏。聞熏精明，明遍法界者：反聞熏修，伏歸本元真精之性。發本明耀，耀性發明，遍周法界，則諸幽隱暗昧為性之鬼神，皆不以自全矣！此明能破暗故。大士以此威光，加被眾生，能令眾生，仗承威光，諸鬼雖近其旁，目不能視，以彼背明向暗，反不堪於光耀。如梟鳥晝盲夜視，羅剎向日不見，視尚不能視，何能加害耶？

藥叉，此云輕捷，有地行、空行、天行三種，是男鬼；羅剎，此云可畏，是女鬼；此二皆食人之鬼，人屍若臭，咒養令鮮食之，乃北方多聞天王管轄。鳩槃茶，此云甕形，是魘魅鬼，能魘魅於人者，乃南方增長天王管轄。毘舍遮，此云噉精氣鬼，能噉人及五穀精氣，乃東方持國天王管轄。富單那，此云主熱病鬼，乃西方廣目天王管轄。等者，以該其餘諸鬼。皆以陰隱為性，故雖近其旁，目不能視，如土地不見洞山者，既不能視，則諸鬼難無畏矣！

〖經〗七者：音性圓銷，觀聽返入，離諸塵妄，能令眾生，禁繫枷鎖，所不能著。

〖講〗此枷鎖難無畏。音性圓銷者：音聲動靜二性，悉皆銷滅，即動靜二相，瞭然不生，故曰圓鎖。觀聽返入者：即觀照能聽聞之性，逆流而入，故曰返入。如是，則入一無妄，不但聲塵銷滅，則色等諸塵，亦隨聲塵以俱滅，故曰離諸塵妄。塵既不緣，根無所偶，見等諸根，亦隨聞以旋復，根塵雙泯。以此妙力，加被眾生，故能令被難人民，而能稱名即成感應，禁繫枷鎖，所不能著其身。因妄塵既離，妄身亦空，故禁閉囹圄，繫縛身體；在頸曰枷，練腳曰鎖，此皆治罪人之刑法；或誤入憲網，或屈遭陷害；若能一心稱念聖號，皆得解脫，則枷鎖難無畏矣！晉竇傳河內人，永和七年，為高昌步卒，呂護所俘。同伴七人，共繫獄中，不久將殺，僧道山時在護營中，與傳相識，乃往視之。傳曰：命在旦夕，能相救乎？山曰：「爾至心念觀世音菩薩，必有感應。」傳遂至心默念三晝夜，械鎖忽然自解[械銷，即腳栲之鎖。]。傳念同伴桎梏，何忍遽捨，復求菩薩神力普濟，乃勸同伴，同心稱念聖號，諸人刑具亦解，遂同遁回鄉裡，自是篤信奉法，一鄉之人，莫不敬事觀音也。

〖經〗八者：滅音圓聞，遍生慈力，能令眾生，經過險路，賊不能劫。

〖講〗此劫賊難無畏。滅音圓聞者：即大士反聞入流時，滅音、而解脫聲塵，圓聞、而證極根性。塵滅、則外無敵對，根圓、則咸歸一心，故能遍生慈力。乃至磣心毒人，亦不能起惡，悉化為慈悲眷屬矣！《法華》云：念彼觀音力，咸即起慈心是也。大士以此慈力，加被眾生，能令眾生，經過危險之路途，或曠野山隘之間，或盜賊衝出之處，皆險路也。但能稱念聖號，賊不能劫，則劫賊難無畏矣！昔尼宗本，高平金鄉人，幼有清信，日誦《普門品》，鄉黨稱之。後出家為尼，被虜所拘，急誦《普門品》，旋即得釋，歸路出冀州，復遇賊所逐，攀上枯樹，誠念觀音聖號，賊尋索不見，得免賊難。初八難無畏竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "First, because I do not turn my own observing back on the sound itself, but instead observe the very one who observes, I cause the suffering, afflicted sentient beings of the ten directions who observe this sound to attain liberation at once."

Commentary: This is fearlessness from the danger of suffering and affliction. Of the eight dangers, this first stands for the whole; the remaining seven are given individually. What presses upon the outer body is called "suffering"; what presses upon the inner mind is called "affliction." The first three lines give the very root of self-cultivation; the four lines that follow make plain the function that benefits others. "First, because I do not turn my own observing back on the sound itself, but instead observe the very one who observes": "observing" here is exactly the wisdom that observes — this light of wisdom no longer illuminating outward — that is, not observing the world's own sounds, but instead observing who, exactly, is the one who observes. The first phrase is leaving the sense-object behind; the second is illuminating this nature directly — exactly slipping loose from what it clings to and subsiding inward, turning one's back on the sense-object while according with awareness. Through this same habituation of hearing and cultivation of hearing turned back, this vajra samādhi, this effortless, wondrous power, blessing sentient beings — I am thereby able to cause the suffering, afflicted sentient beings of the ten directions, should they simply single-mindedly recite my own name, to have me observe this very voice reciting my name, seeking out this voice to rescue them from suffering, bringing about their liberation from suffering and affliction at once — and this is exactly fearlessness from suffering and affliction!

This is exactly the very cause and condition behind Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva's own name, at the fruit stage. In the Universal Gate chapter of the Lotus Sūtra, the Bodhisattva Akṣayamati asks the Buddha: by what cause and condition is Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva so named? The Buddha tells the Bodhisattva Akṣayamati: good son, should there be boundless hundreds of thousands of myriads of sentient beings, suffering every affliction, who hear of this Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva and single-mindedly recite this name (that is, recite the bodhisattva's own name), Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva observes their very voices at that very moment, and all of them attain liberation (observing the world's own voices reciting the bodhisattva's own name — hence, "the one who observes the world's sounds"). That other sūtra names the bodhisattva only in terms of benefiting others at the fruit stage, and so does not speak of the bodhisattva's own self-benefiting practice cultivated at the causal stage. This present sūtra addresses both benefits together, since only once the practice that benefits oneself has been accomplished can one then go on to benefit others as well.

Some take the phrase "observe this sound" to mean sentient beings themselves, while suffering, observing their own voices — but Master Jiao's own refutation of this is entirely correct! Because this present sūtra's own text, following "suffering, afflicted sentient beings," omits the words "single-mindedly recite this name" and the rest, this very misreading has arisen — and Master Jiao's own addition of the two words "call upon me" makes the true meaning plain at once. How many among sentient beings caught in the danger of suffering and affliction would even be capable of carrying out such an observation themselves? And even granting that they could carry out this observation and attain liberation by it, this still would not fit the passage stated at the very outset — "cause sentient beings to attain, through my own body and mind, fourteen kinds of merit and virtue of fearlessness." Examine this carefully.

Sūtra: "Second, knowledge and vision turned back to their own source, I cause sentient beings, should they enter a great fire, not to be burned by it."

Commentary: This is fearlessness from the danger of great fire. The two words "knowledge and vision" include all six sense-faculties' own seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, sensing, and knowing. "Turned back to their own source" means exactly turning false knowledge and false vision back to their own true source, turning back this false knowing and false seeing that conditions sense-objects, and returning to one's own nature's true knowing and true seeing. The great being, using the ear-faculty, the effort of turning hearing back and entering the stream, turns hearing back and becomes free of sound — having already turned back this false hearing, becoming free of the sound-object, sense-faculty and sense-object no longer paired — and, one faculty having already returned to its own source, all six faculties attain liberation together, and so every faculty, one after another, is able to turn back its own falseness and return to its own truth. Knowledge and vision turned back to their own source, the inner fire of seeing and awareness already stilled, the outer fire of the world can no longer do harm.

As Wenling put it: the four elements, inner and outer, constantly interact with one another; seeing and awareness belong to fire, and so, where the karma of seeing intersects with fire, one sees a raging fire before them. Now, knowledge and vision turned back to their own source, there is no longer any karma of seeing, and so fire cannot burn. Causing sentient beings, entering fire, not to be burned — this is exactly the great being's own self-realized power, blessing sentient beings. As the Universal Gate chapter puts it: "through this bodhisattva's own majestic, spiritual power." The Standard Meaning Commentary says: "realizing the Dharma-realm to its very limit, this majestic, spiritual power beyond measure, one who single-mindedly recites this name is thereby gathered within this same light of great compassion's own majesty, and does not fall into the danger of fire — exactly like entering the shade of a mountain, where the summer heat cannot penetrate." This ability to cause sentient beings not to be burned by great fire is exactly fearlessness from the danger of great fire!

That great fire cannot burn is difficult to believe, and so a specific proof is given here. The Accounts of Answered Prayers records: Zhu Changshu, during the Yuankang era of the Jin dynasty, dwelt in a thatched hut in Luoyang; when a great fire spread and was about to reach him, several neighboring households hurried to move their belongings away, while he remained within his own thatched hut, single-mindedly reciting the bodhisattva's own sacred name — and, blessed by the bodhisattva's own majestic, spiritual power, the wind turned and the fire reversed course, extinguishing itself only upon reaching his neighbor's house. Everyone found this remarkable — for even a single spark landing on a thatched hut should have burned it down at once — how, then, could he have remained entirely unharmed? Asked the reason, he answered: I did nothing but recite the sacred name of Avalokiteśvara. There was a certain man in the neighborhood who did not believe this recitation was truly the cause, and so, one dry, windy night, deliberately threw fire at the hut to burn it — once, it would not catch; he threw fire at it three nights running, and still it would not catch — and only then did he come to believe in the bodhisattva's own hidden, spiritual blessing, and went before him to confess this openly.

Sūtra: "Third, observing and listening turned back to their own source, I cause sentient beings, swept away by a great flood, not to drown."

Commentary: This is fearlessness from the danger of great water. "Observing and listening turned back to their own source" is this line's own wondrous power; what follows is its great function. This means: through contemplating this very nature of hearing and listening directly, turning back this false hearing, returning to true hearing — hearing belongs to water, and so, where the karma of hearing intersects with water, one sees a great flood before them; now, hearing already turned back to its own true nature, there is no longer any karma of hearing, and so water cannot drown. Causing sentient beings, swept away by a great flood, not to drown — this, too, is exactly the great being's own self-realized power, blessing sentient beings, and so this is fearlessness from the danger of great water!

Cen Wenben of the Tang dynasty, styled Jingren, a native of Jiyang, believed in the Buddha from youth, and often recited the Universal Gate chapter. One day, traveling on the Wu River, his boat capsized, and Wenben, too, found himself drowning in the water — when, suddenly, he heard a voice say: one who can recite the Universal Gate chapter should be spared this water-danger. This happened three times, and he then found himself floating upon the surface of the water; before long, the waves carried him ashore, where he was rescued by others, and thereby escaped this danger.

Sūtra: "Fourth, severing false thought entirely, the mind free of any wish to harm, I cause sentient beings, entering the land of any ghost, not to be harmed by them."

Commentary: This is fearlessness from the danger of rākṣasas. False thought is exactly the sixth, discriminating consciousness — as the Buddha's earlier refutation of consciousness as the mind put it: this is not your own mind at all, but merely a false image, a thought conjured from the sense-object before you, likened here to a thief, capable of killing off sentient beings' own dharma-body and life of wisdom, exactly as a rākṣasa is capable of devouring a person — deeply to be feared. The great being, turning hearing back and entering the stream, conditioning no sense-object without, following no sense-faculty within, sense-faculty and sense-object no longer paired, the discriminating mind of consciousness likewise extinguished — hence, "severing false thought entirely." False thought already extinguished, the mind holds no wish to harm at all, transcending entirely the very mental conduct of ghosts and spirits — and, through this same majestic power, blessing sentient beings, one is thereby able to cause sentient beings, entering the land of any rākṣasa ghost, simply by single-mindedly reciting this name, not to be harmed by these ghosts — and this is exactly fearlessness from the danger of rākṣasas! A traditional account records: more than a hundred merchants of Siṃhala set sail across the sea, and suddenly encountered a malevolent wind (that is, a ghost-wind), which drove their ship to the land of the rākṣasa ghosts, where many rākṣasa women came out to greet them warmly. Among the merchants was one of great wisdom, who recognized that they had entered the land of the rākṣasa ghosts, and called out to the assembled company: our ship has now entered this land of ghosts — you must all single-mindedly recite the name of Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, and you will be spared this danger. The company followed his words and recited together, and a great wind suddenly rose, blowing their ship free and swiftly bringing them home to their own country — fully bearing out the truth of these sacred words.

Sūtra: "Fifth, habituated hearing become true hearing, all six sense-faculties dissolved and returned to their common source, one and the same as the very nature of hearing itself, I cause sentient beings, facing execution, to have the blade shatter piece by piece — their very weapons of war become like cutting through water, or like a blade swung through beams of light, this nature itself utterly unmoved."

Commentary: This is fearlessness from the danger of the sword and weapons of war. "Habituated hearing become true hearing": the great being, turning hearing back to illuminate this nature directly, original awakening habituating from within, habituating this false hearing until it becomes true hearing — the ear-faculty proceeding exactly this way, dissolving away falseness and returning to truth, all six sense-faculties, without exception, dissolved and returned together. The verse further on puts it: once a single faculty has returned to its own source, all six faculties attain liberation together — one and the same as the very nature of hearing that hears the sound-object, each and every one returning to its own true origin, the sense-object gone and the sense-faculty exhausted; sense-faculty and sense-object both dissolved away, how could this awakened brightness fail to become perfectly wondrous? The great being, through this self-realized root of the vajra samādhi, unmoving and indestructible, blesses sentient beings, and is thereby able to cause sentient beings, facing execution, to have the blade shatter piece by piece — this being exactly the power of the great being's own vajra samādhi, blessing the very being facing harm, their body made one with vajra itself, so that, the moment the blade touches this body, the blade itself shatters piece by piece. And even should the blade itself not shatter, this body suffers no injury at all — their very weapons of war become like a blade cutting through water, the water left with no trace of a cut, or like wind blowing through beams of light (that is, sunlight), this light never once extinguished. Since the blade that touches is itself, as form, exactly the nature of the tathāgatagarbha, and the body that is touched, as the body-faculty, is likewise exactly the nature of the tathāgatagarbha — this same nature meeting this same nature is exactly like emptiness meeting emptiness, this nature itself utterly unmoved — and so this is exactly fearlessness from the danger of the sword and weapons of war! The History of the Qi Dynasty records: Sun Jingde, stationed at a northern border post, made an image of Avalokiteśvara and paid it homage each day; later, falsely implicated by bandits, he was sentenced to death. A monk from India taught him to recite the Avalokiteśvara Sūtra (that is, the Universal Gate chapter) a thousand times, and, when the moment of execution came, the executioner's blade broke into three pieces, and his own neck remained entirely unharmed. The chancellor, on hearing of this, petitioned for his pardon. And, again, as with the Sixth Patriarch — an assassin swung a sharp blade at him three times, yet each blow passed through him as though cutting a mere shadow, the blade passing over his own head without the slightest injury — this, too, being exactly the power of the Sixth Patriarch's own self-realization.

Sūtra: "Sixth, hearing habituated into its own essential brightness, this brightness pervading the Dharma-realm entirely, so that every hidden darkness can no longer sustain its own nature, I cause sentient beings — though yakṣas, rākṣasas, kumbhāṇḍa ghosts, piśācas, pūtanas, and the like draw near beside them — not to be seen by their own eyes."

Commentary: This is fearlessness from the danger of every ghost. "Hearing habituated into its own essential brightness, this brightness pervading the Dharma-realm entirely": turning hearing back, habituating and cultivating, subsiding back into this own original, true, essential nature. Giving rise to this original, bright radiance, this radiant nature giving rise to brightness, pervading the whole Dharma-realm — every hidden, obscure, dark-natured ghost and spirit can no longer sustain its own nature at all! This makes plain that brightness is able to overcome darkness. The great being, through this same majestic light, blesses sentient beings, and is thereby able to cause sentient beings, relying upon this majestic light, that though every ghost draws near beside them, their own eyes cannot see them at all — since these ghosts turn their backs on brightness and face toward darkness, unable, in turn, to withstand this same radiant light. Just as an owl is blind by day and sees only by night, and a rākṣasa cannot see when facing the sun — unable even to see at all, how, then, could they do any harm?

A yakṣa, translated "swift," comes in three kinds — earth-traveling, sky-traveling, and heaven-traveling — and is a male ghost; a rākṣasa, translated "fearsome," is a female ghost; both are ghosts that devour humans — should a human corpse begin to smell, they use incantations to preserve it fresh before eating it — governed by the northern heavenly king, Vaiśravaṇa of Wide Hearing. A kumbhāṇḍa, translated "jar-shaped," is a nightmare-ghost, capable of causing nightmares in people, governed by the southern heavenly king, Virūḍhaka of Increasing Growth. A piśāca, translated "essence-devouring ghost," is capable of devouring the vital essence of both people and the five grains, governed by the eastern heavenly king, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, the Upholder of the Nation. A pūtana, translated "fever-ghost," is governed by the western heavenly king, Virūpākṣa of the Broad Eye. "And the like" includes every other kind of ghost besides these. All of them take concealment and hiddenness as their own nature, and so, though they draw near beside a being, the eye cannot see them at all — exactly like the local earth-spirit unable to see Dongshan — and, since they cannot even see at all, this is exactly fearlessness from the danger of every ghost!

Sūtra: "Seventh, the very nature of sound entirely dissolved, observing and hearing turned back and entering inward, leaving every sense-object and its falseness behind, I cause sentient beings, bound in chains and shackles, to find them unable to hold them fast."

Commentary: This is fearlessness from the danger of chains and shackles. "The very nature of sound entirely dissolved": both natures of sound, of motion and stillness, entirely dissolved away — exactly the two marks of motion and stillness becoming utterly unarisen — hence, "entirely dissolved." "Observing and hearing turned back and entering inward": that is, contemplating this very nature that hears, entering against the stream — hence, "turned back and entering inward." In this way, entering this single, unfalse state, not only is the sound-object dissolved away, but every other sense-object, such as form, is dissolved away together with it — hence, "leaving every sense-object and its falseness behind." The sense-object no longer conditioned at all, the sense-faculty has nothing left to pair with — seeing and every other faculty likewise turn back and return together with hearing, sense-faculty and sense-object both dissolved away together. Through this same wondrous power, blessing sentient beings, one is thereby able to cause people caught in danger, simply by reciting this name, to bring about a response at once, bound in chains and shackles, unable to hold their own bodies fast at all. Because this false sense-object is already left behind, this false body, too, is empty — and so imprisonment within a cell, the binding of the body, a cangue about the neck called a chain and fetters about the feet called a shackle, these being exactly the punishments imposed on the guilty, whether wrongly caught within the law's own net or unjustly framed — should one be able to single-mindedly recite this sacred name, liberation is attained in every case, and this is exactly fearlessness from the danger of chains and shackles! Dou Chuan of Henei, in the Jin dynasty, in the seventh year of the Yonghe era, served as a foot soldier of Gaochang, and was captured by Lü Hu. Together with seven companions, he was bound and held in prison, soon to be executed; a monk named Daoshan, then within Lü Hu's own camp, was acquainted with Chuan, and went to see him. Chuan said: my life hangs by a single thread — can you help save me? Daoshan said: recite Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva's own name with utmost sincerity, and a response is certain to come. Chuan then recited silently, with utmost sincerity, for three full days and nights, and his own shackles suddenly loosened of themselves (the shackles here being exactly the fetters binding his own feet). Chuan, thinking of his companions still bound in these same fetters, could not bear to abandon them, and so further sought the bodhisattva's own spiritual power to save them all together, urging his companions to recite this same sacred name with him, with one mind — and every one of their own instruments of punishment loosened as well, and together they fled back to their own home village. From that time on, he became a devout follower of the Dharma, and no one in his own village failed to honor and serve Avalokiteśvara from then on.

Sūtra: "Eighth, the sound-object extinguished and hearing made perfectly complete, universally giving rise to a power of compassion, I cause sentient beings, passing through a dangerous road, not to be robbed by bandits."

Commentary: This is fearlessness from the danger of robbers and bandits. "The sound-object extinguished and hearing made perfectly complete": at the very moment the great being turns hearing back and enters the stream, the sound-object is extinguished, and one is freed from it; hearing is made perfectly complete, and one realizes the sense-faculty's own nature to its very limit. The sense-object extinguished, there is no longer any adversary without; the sense-faculty made perfectly complete, everything returns together to a single mind — and so this power of compassion is able to arise universally, extending even to the most vicious, poisonous-hearted person, who, too, can no longer give rise to any evil at all, transformed entirely into kin bound by this same compassion! As the Lotus Sūtra puts it: "recollecting this same power of Avalokiteśvara, every one of them gives rise, at once, to a compassionate mind" — exactly this. The great being, through this same power of compassion, blesses sentient beings, and is thereby able to cause sentient beings, passing through a dangerous road — whether through open wilderness or a mountain pass, or a place where bandits might suddenly emerge, every one of these being a dangerous road — simply by reciting this sacred name, not to be robbed by bandits at all — and this is exactly fearlessness from the danger of robbers and bandits! In ancient times, the nun Zongben, a native of Jinxiang in Gaoping, held a pure faith from childhood, reciting the Universal Gate chapter daily, praised throughout her own village for it. Later, leaving the household to become a nun, she was seized and held captive, and urgently recited the Universal Gate chapter, whereupon she was soon released; on her way home, passing through Jizhou, she was again pursued by bandits, and, climbing up into a withered tree, sincerely recited Avalokiteśvara's own sacred name — the bandits searched for her but could not find her, and she thereby escaped this danger from bandits. This closes the first group, the eight kinds of fearlessness from danger.

Sūtra: "Ninth, hearing habituated to leave every sense-object behind, so that no form-object can rob it of its own treasure, I cause every sentient being given to excessive lust to be freed from craving and desire far behind."

Commentary: Craving, aversion, and delusion are called the three poisons — since they are able to poison the very life of the dharma-body and wisdom, hence named "the three poisons," deeply to be feared! Craving, aversion, and delusion are, further, the causes of the three lower destinies: a being of excessive craving falls into hell; a being of excessive aversion falls into the realm of hungry ghosts (aversion belongs to fire, and a hungry ghost thereby receives the retribution of burning by the fire of hunger); a being of excessive delusion falls into the animal realm — this, too, deeply to be feared! What is here called fearlessness from the three poisons does not mean indulging in evil without any fear at all, but rather this: through holding the name, one is freed from these poisons, through this great, majestic, spiritual power, and thereby attains fearlessness.

This is fearlessness from the poison of craving left behind. Among every kind of craving, craving for sexual desire stands foremost. It is exactly this one matter of sexual desire that people transgress most easily of all — the sight of beauty stirring the mind, one is certain to fall into the deep pit of craving and desire, and this is, moreover, the most difficult of all to sever, causing the greatest harm. Should one wish to leave desire behind, the Universal Gate chapter of the Lotus Sūtra says: constantly recollecting and reverently calling upon Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, one thereby attains freedom from desire — relying on the bodhisattva's own majestic, spiritual power, together with the power of one's own recollection in holding this name, using this power of recollection to counteract the mind of lust directly, relying on this majestic, spiritual power to dissolve away this very karmic obstruction.

"Hearing habituated to leave every sense-object behind": this is exactly the effort of turning hearing back to illuminate this nature directly, habituating this false hearing that flows outward until it becomes the true hearing that enters the stream — and, entering the stream, one is certain to let go of what was heard. The knot within the sound-object already untied, the knots within every other sense-object are untied together with it — how, then, could the form-object still rob one of one's own household treasure? "Cause every sentient being given to excessive lust": speaking of "excessive" lust means one whose past habituation runs especially deep, again and again giving rise to thoughts of lust, manifesting as lustful conduct — at its lightest, harming the body and losing one's own virtue; at its worst, ruining one's household and losing one's own life. In its most extreme form — since lust and desire belong to fire — the bodhisattva regards desire exactly as one would avoid a pit of fire.

The bhikṣuṇī Ratnakamala, holding the bodhisattva precepts, secretly engaged in lustful conduct, falsely claiming that lustful conduct was neither killing nor stealing, and so carried no karmic retribution at all. No sooner had she spoken these words than a great, fierce fire arose from her own female organ, and afterward, joint by joint, this same fierce fire burned through her entire body, and she fell into the Avīci hell. A being given to excessive lust who is able to constantly recollect the bodhisattva's own sacred name, blessed by the bodhisattva's own majestic power, is thereby able to be freed from craving and desire far behind — and so, freed from the poison of craving, one may naturally attain fearlessness!

Sūtra: "Twelfth, this form dissolved away and hearing returned to its own source, this very place of practice unmoved, entering into the world without harming the world itself, able to pervade the ten directions, making offerings to every buddha, every Tathāgata, as numerous as motes of dust, and, at each buddha's own side, serving as a son of the Dharma-king — I cause every childless being throughout the Dharma-realm who wishes for a son to give birth to a son endowed with merit, virtue, and wisdom."

Commentary: This is fearlessness in answering the prayer for a son. People of the world without a son fear three things: first, growing old with no one to attend them; second, having no one to entrust their own final affairs to; third, their own family line coming, inevitably, to an end. And so they are certain to pray for a son. As the Universal Gate chapter puts it: "should one wish for a son, worshipping and making offerings to Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, one thereby gives birth to a son endowed with merit, virtue, and wisdom." That other sūtra speaks only of the method for praying for a son, without speaking of the bodhisattva's own blessing power; this present sūtra speaks only of this same blessing, without speaking of the method for praying for a son — read together, the two sūtras' own explanations complete one another fully.

"This form dissolved away and hearing returned to its own source, this very place of practice unmoved": these two lines point to the very cultivation and realization of principle — dissolving away the four elements' own illusory form (exactly "increasing this gradually, both the hearing and what is heard were exhausted"), and turning back to this single, true nature of hearing (exactly "once arising and ceasing were themselves extinguished, still cessation appeared of itself"), realizing and entering this unmoving, unshaken, unborn, unceasing principle-substance — exactly what the Buddha called attaining, altogether, this one vehicle's own ground of still cessation. The next seven lines, "entering into the world without harming the world itself" and what follows, describe this great function arising from this same essence. The bodhisattva's own body is well able to enter every buddha-land as numerous as motes of dust, manifesting boundless bodies from this single body, entering into all three worlds (the world of physical vessels, the world of sentient beings, and the world of true awakening), manifesting a body matched to each kind without harming the world's own appearance in the least — according with principle to accomplish actual affairs, proceeding from truth into the conventional world, this being exactly the wisdom of skillful means; skillful means belongs to the provisional, and the provisional is able to intervene in actual affairs — carrying, within it, the very meaning of giving birth to a son.

"Able to pervade the ten directions, making offerings to every buddha, every Tathāgata, as numerous as motes of dust, and, at each buddha's own side, serving as a son of the Dharma-king": offerings are of two kinds. First, offerings of the body — attending with towel and vessel in hand, offering one's own labor and service — this being the fulfillment of the accumulation of blessings sought. Second, offerings of the mind — constantly following the Buddha's own study, transmitting the wheel of Dharma on the Buddha's own behalf, this mind well according with the Buddha's own mind, bringing the Buddha's own original intention to fulfillment — at each buddha's own side, serving as a true son of the Dharma-king: this being the fulfillment of the accumulation of wisdom sought. Through this same surplus of blessing and surplus of wisdom, blessing sentient beings, one is thereby able to cause every childless being throughout the Dharma-realm, worshipping and making offerings to Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, to give birth to a son endowed with merit, virtue, and wisdom. Possessing merit and virtue, one becomes both wealthy and honored; possessing wisdom, one becomes both upright and discerning. Should one have merit without wisdom, one's own understanding remains mediocre and shallow; should one have wisdom without merit, one's own household remains poor and one's own strength weak. For whoever prays, the bodhisattva causes the birth of a son complete with both merit and wisdom together — and so this is exactly fearlessness in answering the prayer for a son!

I, too, was born through my own parents' prayer to Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva. My own family register is in Gutian County, Fujian, of the Wu clan; my father was a timber merchant, and, at thirty-five years of age, had already had two daughters but no son. My father and mother together prayed to the great being Avalokiteśvara for a son, keeping an image of the great being enshrined at home, my mother worshipping before it each day; afterward, she became pregnant, and worshipped with even greater devotion still. One night, she dreamed she saw the great being, cradling an infant, who said to my mother: this child shall be your son. My mother, receiving the child with joy, awoke from this dream, and told my father and my own grandmother of it. "All six sense-faculties perfectly interfused": "perfect" means perfectly interfused — the six sense-faculties functioning interchangeably, each and every one, whichever is taken up, complete with seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, sensing, and knowing all at once. "Interfused" means penetrating through — the six sense-faculties no longer separated from one another by any obstruction. These two words, "perfectly interfused," govern every line that follows. Because of this penetration, illuminating without any duality: the six sense-faculties' own spiritual, bright illuminating function, without the slightest duality or distinction, and so a great, perfectly round mirror is established, able to accord with and receive every hidden Dharma-gate of every Tathāgata as numerous as motes of dust throughout the ten directions. Because of this interfusion, embracing every world of the ten directions: able to embrace and enclose every buddha-world of the ten directions, and so the empty tathāgatagarbha is established, able to receive every buddha's own Dharma-gate, whether great or small, provisional or real, in its entirety, without the slightest loss. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "according with" is exactly the gentle virtue proper to the earth itself, and "receiving" is exactly the very capability proper to the women's own quarters — and so this is exactly why this line answers the prayer for a daughter.

"Cause every childless being throughout the Dharma-realm who wishes for a daughter": the great being, through this same freely functioning, wondrous power, blesses sentient beings, and is thereby able to cause every being throughout the Dharma-realm without a daughter, wishing to bear one, who is indeed able to worship and make offerings to Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, to give birth thereby to a daughter upright in appearance, blessed with merit, and gentle and compliant — outwardly, of upright appearance and dignified, graceful bearing, this being blessed with merit; inwardly, of a gentle, compliant temperament, chaste, calm, and reserved, this being blessed with virtue. Beholding one blessed with merit, everyone is moved to love her; beholding one blessed with virtue, everyone is moved to honor her — "complete with every fine mark" meaning exactly complete with both the mark of merit and the mark of virtue together. Should she be upright in appearance alone, without gentleness, she may be loved but not honored; should she be gentle alone, without an upright appearance, she may be honored but not loved. Of the two words "merit and virtue," "merit" connects to what comes before, and "virtue" to what follows — complete with both merit and virtue together, she thereby comes to be both loved and honored by all — and so this is exactly fearlessness in answering the prayer for a daughter! This closes the third group, the two kinds of fearlessness in answering these two prayers.

〖講〗此明一名能敵多名。其故何也？由我所得耳根圓通，即是圓通本根。本根有二義：一、為娑婆本利之根，欲取三摩提，實以聞中入。二、為諸圓通中之本根，此是微塵佛，一路涅槃門。上但標名，下出體相；謂此圓通本根，發自妙耳門之中。耳門所以稱妙者，即古觀世音佛，所授從聞、思、修之法，依根中聞性，不生不滅之妙理，起反聞照性之妙智，照破五陰，解除六結，生滅既滅，寂滅現前，證圓通體，發自在用。

然後身心，微妙含容，周遍法界者：然後稱體起用，應化無方，身之微妙也；鑒機不謬，心之微妙也。含容、指妙心，謂心含十方，無量世界眾生；周遍、指妙身，謂身遍聖、凡、染、淨十界。故能令眾生，持我觀世音一名，與彼共持六十二恆河沙數諸法王子名號，二人福德，正等無有別異也。

〖經〗世尊！我一名號，與彼眾多名號無異；由我修習，得真圓通。

Sūtra: "Fourteenth, throughout this great trichiliocosm, its hundred million suns and moons, dwelling now within the world are sons of the Dharma-king as numerous as sixty-two Ganges' sands, each cultivating their own dharma and setting their own example, teaching and transforming sentient beings, according with each being's own capacity through their own skillful wisdom, each one differing from the next."

Commentary: This makes plain fearlessness through holding the name. Since one holds only Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva's own single name, and not the names of every bodhisattva, one might fear that a single name could never match the power of many names combined. "This great trichiliocosm" refers to the Sahā world, holding, in all, a hundred million Mount Sumerus, a hundred million suns and moons, a hundred million sets of four continents. "Dwelling now within the world" means dwelling, at this very moment, within the three realms and six destinies, manifesting a transformation-body matched to each kind, riding upon their own vows to benefit beings; these sons of the Dharma-king number as many as sixty-two Ganges' sands — this being the sage's own direct perception, both known and seen directly.

"Each cultivating their own dharma and setting their own example" and what follows makes plain that these sons of the Dharma-king carry out their own teaching in ways that are by no means uniform — some cultivate an actual practice, a self-benefiting dharma, which may likewise serve as a model held up for sentient beings, an example for them to follow. "Teaching and transforming sentient beings": some cultivate a provisional practice that benefits others, according with each being's own root-nature, manifesting the four means of attraction (giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and working alongside others), using this same skillful, provisional wisdom — each one, again, differing from the next.

Sūtra: "Through this root of perfect penetration I myself attained, this wondrous ear-gate giving rise to it, my own body and mind, afterward, subtly and wondrously embracing and containing, pervading the whole Dharma-realm entirely, I am able to cause sentient beings who hold my own name, together with those who hold the names of these sixty-two Ganges' sands of sons of the Dharma-king, to receive, the two of them, one and the same merit and virtue, exactly equal, without the slightest difference."

Commentary: This makes plain how a single name is able to match the power of many names combined. What is the reason for this? Because this root of perfect penetration I myself attained is exactly the very root of perfect penetration itself. This "root" carries two senses: first, it is the very root of original benefit for this Sahā world — to take hold of samādhi, one truly enters through hearing itself. Second, it is the very root underlying every one of the twenty-five approaches to perfect penetration — this being the single gateway to nirvāṇa shared by buddhas as numerous as motes of dust. The line above names this root; what follows sets out its own substance and characteristics — meaning that this root of perfect penetration arises from within this same wondrous ear-gate. The reason the ear-gate is called "wondrous" is exactly this: the method of hearing, reflecting, and cultivating handed down by the ancient Buddha Avalokiteśvara, relying on the sense-faculty's own nature of hearing, this wondrous, unborn, unceasing principle, gives rise to the wondrous wisdom that turns hearing back to illuminate this nature directly, illuminating through and breaking apart the five aggregates, untying the six knots, arising and ceasing themselves extinguished, still cessation appearing of itself, realizing the very essence of perfect penetration and giving rise to its freely functioning use.

"My own body and mind, afterward, subtly and wondrously embracing and containing, pervading the whole Dharma-realm entirely": afterward, according with this essence, this function arises, responding and transforming without any fixed direction — this being the very subtlety and wonder of the body; discerning each capacity without the slightest error — this being the very subtlety and wonder of the mind. "Embracing and containing" points to this wondrous mind, meaning that this mind embraces the sentient beings of boundless worlds throughout the ten directions; "pervading" points to this wondrous body, meaning that this body pervades all ten Dharma-realms, sagely and ordinary, defiled and pure alike. And so I am able to cause sentient beings who hold my own name, Avalokiteśvara, together with those who hold the names of these sixty-two Ganges' sands of sons of the Dharma-king, to receive, the two of them, one and the same merit and virtue, without the slightest difference at all.

Sūtra: "World-Honored One, my own single name, and those many other names, carry no difference between them at all — for I have, through my own cultivation and practice, attained true perfect penetration itself."

Commentary: As the Zhǐzhǎng commentary puts it: a single name against many names, holding one alone against holding many together — considered in terms of the actual conduct involved for the two persons, these differ as greatly as heaven from earth; yet considered in terms of the merit and virtue attained by the two persons, they do not differ by so much as a single thread. What is the reason for this? The great being says: holding my own single name alone, and holding together the many names of every other bodhisattva, bring exactly the same merit and virtue — and so this is exactly fearlessness through holding the name! This is entirely because I myself have cultivated and practiced the samādhi of the ear-gate, and have thereby attained true and genuine perfect penetration itself. Being complete with the three true realities constantly present within perfect penetration, I am thereby able to surpass all twenty-four other sages and stand wondrously alone — fit to match every son of the Dharma-king without the slightest doubt! Observing these final two lines, one sees that the bodhisattva's own hidden intention has, already, here, selected themself as uniquely foremost among every sage's own perfect penetration. That the Buddha later commands Mañjuśrī to make this same selection once more is only for the sake of those who have not yet grasped this same hidden intention shared by the Buddha and Avalokiteśvara alike — adding, as it were, this further tangle of vines to make the point plain. This closes the second section, setting forth this fearlessness in detail.
