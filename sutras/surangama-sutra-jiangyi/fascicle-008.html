Every Spark Can Burn Down a Forest

Ānanda, still clinging to the causes-and-conditions he first learned, asks the Buddha why both causes-and-conditions and spontaneity are now rejected together. In answer, the sūtra walks through the seven great elements — earth, water, fire, wind, space, seeing, and consciousness — showing not merely that each is true in its own nature, as the four categories already showed, but that each single spark of it pervades the whole Dharma-realm without exception. The fascicle closes the sūtra's entire "refuting falseness, revealing truth" division.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 7 completed the harmonizing of the four categories — aggregates, entrances, fields, and elements — with the tathāgatagarbha. Fascicle 8 opens the next and, in this sūtra's own structure, final movement of the "refuting falseness, revealing truth" division: fully manifesting the seven great elements (七大) — earth, water, fire, wind, space, seeing, and consciousness — as pervading the whole Dharma-realm. Yuanying is careful to mark the difference in depth between the two sections: the four categories showed that each phenomenon is, in its own nature, true; the seven great elements go further, showing that each single instance of that nature — one grain of dust, one spark of fire — carries within it the capacity to pervade everywhere without exception. Space, seeing, and consciousness are added one at a time to the classical four, each addition explicitly merging into a running tally — "five great elements," then "six," then "seven" — until the whole division closes with the discerning mind itself, refuted so thoroughly in the sūtra's opening fascicles, finally welcomed in as fully the tathāgatagarbha as everything else.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. Ānanda's opening objection, and the Buddha's initial rebuke, have already been translated in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 3 ("Ānanda's Objection: What About the Four Great Elements?"), as have the seven great elements' own core demonstrations ("The Seven Great Elements") — that exact English is reused here wherever Yuanying quotes it. The water-and-ice analogy, the detailed clause-by-clause refutations Yuanying's outline requires, and all of his own commentary are translated fresh throughout.

Ānanda's Renewed Doubt, and the Buddha's Response 寅初 阿難轉疑雙非 · 寅二 卯初 責迷許說

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！如來常說，和合因緣，一切世間，種種變化，皆因四大和合發明。云何如來，因緣、自然，二俱排擯？我今不知，斯義所屬！惟垂哀愍，開示眾生，中道了義，無戲論法。爾時世尊，告阿難言：汝先厭離，聲聞、緣覺，諸小乘法，發心勤求，無上菩提，故我今時，為汝開示，第一義諦。如何復將世間戲論，妄想因緣，而自纏繞！汝雖多聞，如說藥人，真藥現前，不能分別，如來說為，真可憐愍！汝今諦聽，我當為汝分別開示；亦令當來，修大乘者，通達實相。阿難默然，承佛聖旨。

〖講〗此由阿難執權疑實。執昔日所聞因緣權教，疑現今所說了義實教。蓋阿難因緣之執，於九番顯見超情科中，佛已與自然而併破矣。阿難祗知妙覺明性，非因緣和合，至於世間諸相，因緣和合之執如故。所以聞佛於四科世間法，一一皆云非因緣，及自然性，所以騰疑復問也。阿難仰白佛言：世尊！如來昔日常說，和合因緣，和合即因緣和合，故並稱焉。佛初立教，以因緣為宗，對破外道自然，所以常說。但是如來一時權巧方便，以因緣正理而破外道邪說，非大乘了義之談，今經為了義教，阿難不達斯旨，是以執權疑實也。

一切世間，種種變化四句，即舉四科諸相。四科諸法乃世間有為之相，細分之，則千差萬別，種種不一，無非因緣和合，虛妄有生，皆自本無，變化而有。故曰：「皆因四大，和合發明」，是則不出因緣矣。上段執昔權義，此文疑今實教。阿難曰：云何如來，因緣、自然，二者俱同排斥，而擯棄之？若單遣自然，原是外計，因無可疑；而併遣因緣，有違自宗。果係何意，而我現今不知，雙非因緣、自然，斯此也義之所歸屬？此句即言不知雙非之旨，屬於何宗何教，故下請佛開示。

惟願如來，垂慈哀愍：哀憐我等惑深，愍念我等智淺，不知非因緣非自然，斯義所屬。今求開示，中道了義，無戲論法。此阿難所請之法，正是四科所說雙非之法，因緣是學者世間戲論，自然是非學者世間戲論，非因緣非自然，即無戲論法，此法亦即中道了義。中道者不偏有無二邊，謂之中道；非因緣，即妙性不滯於有為，非自然，即妙性不墮於無為，有無雙遣，中道現前；正是大乘了義，真實之法，為如來藏，妙真如性。阿難所請之法，即佛已說之法，迷而不知，更為疑請，故佛責以，如說藥人，真藥現前，不能分別者此也。

此如來述其應求施教之意。我昔日因緣之教，乃應求小乘者而說，汝自婬室歸來，悔恨多聞，未全道力，慇懃啟請，十方如來，得成菩提之妙定；是汝先已厭離聲聞、緣覺，諸小乘法，回小向大，已發心勤求，無上菩提之佛道矣。故我今時，為汝開示，第一義諦：即是應汝所求，而施汝大教。今時，即三番破識以後之時，十番顯見，克就根性，直指真心，攝事歸理，會通四科，即性常住，會相歸性。真心者，第一義諦也。

如何復將世間戲論，妄想因緣，而自纏繞：此怪責阿難，吝惜舊聞，因緣權教，而不肯放捨。汝雖多聞，如說藥人，真藥現前，不能分別，如來說為，真可憐愍：此怪責阿難，雖聞中道，第一義諦，不能識取，汝如說藥之人，熟讀本草，口雖能說藥名，而未親採，真藥現前，不能分別是真，反疑棄之。首句誡聽，下則許說。我當為汝，分別開示，非因緣、非自然之了義；不獨為汝，現會大眾，亦令當來之世，修習大乘行者，皆得通達實相，共入如來藏海也。實相與如來藏，名異體同。

如《正脈》所云：四科方談其一一皆是性真，而未嘗言其一一皆周法界，如指香柴煤炭，一一言其是火，而未及言一一皆可洞燒林野。此處標許下，別詳七大，方談其一一皆周法界，如方說出諸火，每一星之火，皆有洞燒林野之極量。四科但顯法法當體真常，七大極顯法法圓融周遍。前後兩科，義理淺深，即在是矣。

實相有三，即三如來藏：一、無相之實相：無一切妄法差別之相，祗有一真平等實相，即空如來藏，空諸一切，虛妄染法之相，併非本體而全無也，乃藏性不變之體。二、無不相之實相：並非無相，而能隨緣現一切相，即不空如來藏，具足十界諸法，非無諸相也，乃藏性隨緣之用。三、無相無不相之實相：若言其無，則不捨一法；若言其有，則不立一塵，即空不空如來藏，真空不礙妙有，妙有不礙真空，乃藏性體用雙彰。阿難默然，承受聖旨者：默然即凝神靜慮，一心入於語義之中，欽承領受，我佛大聖之法旨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata has always taught that all the world's various transformations come about through the combination of causes and conditions — through the combining of the four great elements. Why, then, does the Tathāgata now reject both causes-and-conditions and spontaneity alike? I do not know where this teaching belongs. I beg only that you, in compassion, reveal to sentient beings the middle way, the teaching of complete meaning, free of empty argument." At that time the World-Honored One said to Ānanda: "You once turned away from the teachings of the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, resolving to seek unsurpassed bodhi — and so I now reveal to you the ultimate truth. Why, then, do you again entangle yourself in the world's empty arguments and deluded talk of causes and conditions? Though you are learned, you are like a person who can recite the names of medicines but cannot recognize the real medicine when it is right in front of him — the Tathāgata truly calls this pitiable! You should now listen closely: I will explain and reveal this to you, point by point, so that those who cultivate the Great Vehicle in ages to come may also penetrate through to true reality." Ānanda fell silent, receiving the Buddha's sacred instruction with his whole attention.

Commentary: This arises from Ānanda clinging to the provisional teaching while doubting the real one: clinging to what he once heard of the provisional teaching on causes and conditions, he now doubts the teaching of complete meaning being spoken here. Ānanda's attachment to causes and conditions had already been refuted, together with spontaneity, back in the ninth display of seeing, where seeing was shown to surpass every category. But Ānanda only grasped that the wondrous, enlightened, bright nature is not a matter of causes and conditions combining — his attachment to causes and conditions combining, as an explanation for the appearances of the world, remained exactly as before. And so, hearing the Buddha say, of each of the four categories of worldly phenomena in turn, that none of them is a matter of causes and conditions or of spontaneity, his doubt rose up again, and he asks once more. When the Buddha first established his teaching, he took causes and conditions as its guiding principle, in order to refute the non-Buddhist schools' spontaneity. But this was only the Tathāgata's skillful expedient at the time, using the correct principle of causes and conditions to refute the false teachings of the non-Buddhist schools; it was never the account given by the Great Vehicle's teaching of complete meaning. This sūtra, however, is precisely that teaching of complete meaning — and Ānanda, failing to grasp this point, clings to the provisional teaching while doubting the real one.

The four lines "all the world's various transformations" point back to the appearances covered by the four categories just harmonized — the whole of conditioned, worldly appearance, however finely divided, endlessly various, yet in every case nothing but the combination of causes and conditions, falsely arising from what was originally nothing at all. Hence: "all through the combining of the four great elements" — which, for Ānanda, still comes down to nothing but causes and conditions. The passage above shows him clinging to the old, provisional meaning; this passage shows him doubting the present, real teaching. Ānanda asks: why does the Tathāgata reject both causes-and-conditions and spontaneity together, dismissing both alike? If only spontaneity were set aside, that would simply be the non-Buddhist position being refuted, and no doubt would arise; but to set aside causes-and-conditions as well runs against the Buddha's own established teaching. What, then, could this possibly mean — and to which school, which teaching, does this doubled negation of both causes-and-conditions and spontaneity actually belong? This is why he now begs the Buddha to explain.

"I beg only that the Tathāgata, in his compassion, take pity": pity that our delusion runs so deep, compassion that our wisdom runs so shallow, that we do not know to which teaching this doubled negation belongs; and he now asks to be shown the middle way, the teaching of complete meaning, free of empty argument. What Ānanda asks for here is exactly the doubled negation already given throughout the four categories: causes-and-conditions is the empty argument of those who have studied the teachings; spontaneity is the empty argument of those who have not; neither causes-and-conditions nor spontaneity is precisely the teaching free of empty argument, and this teaching is itself the middle way, the teaching of complete meaning. The "middle way" favors neither extreme, existence nor non-existence, hence "middle"; "not a matter of causes-and-conditions" means the wondrous nature is not caught in the conditioned; "not spontaneous" means the wondrous nature does not fall into the unconditioned either — both existence and non-existence set aside together, the middle way stands revealed of itself. This is exactly the Great Vehicle's teaching of complete meaning, the true Dharma, the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness. What Ānanda now asks for is exactly what the Buddha has already taught — deluded, he fails to recognize it, and asks again out of doubt, which is why the Buddha rebukes him as being like a person who can recite the names of medicines but cannot recognize the real medicine when it is right in front of him.

This shows the Buddha explaining why he taught as he did, in answer to what was actually being asked at the time. My earlier teaching on causes and conditions was spoken in answer to those seeking the Lesser Vehicle. You yourself, returning from the courtesan's chamber, regretted that your great learning had not brought you the full strength of the path, and earnestly begged to hear of the wondrous samādhi by which the Tathāgatas of the ten directions attain bodhi — this already showed that you had turned away from the teachings of the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, turning from the Lesser Vehicle toward the Great, and had resolved to seek the buddha-path of unsurpassed bodhi. "And so I now reveal to you the ultimate truth": this is precisely the Great Vehicle teaching given in answer to what you actually asked for. "Now" means the time after the threefold refutation of consciousness, after the ten displays of seeing directly pointed to the true mind by way of the sense-faculty's own nature, after phenomena were gathered back into principle, and after the four categories were harmonized with the ever-abiding nature, phenomenon gathered back into nature. The true mind is exactly the ultimate truth.

"Why, then, do you again entangle yourself" expresses the Buddha's reproach for hoarding what he once heard of the provisional teaching on causes and conditions, unwilling to let it go. "Though you are learned, you are like a person who can recite the names of medicines but cannot recognize the real medicine when it is right in front of him — the Tathāgata truly calls this pitiable" reproaches Ānanda for hearing the middle way's ultimate truth and yet failing to recognize and take hold of it — like a person who has thoroughly studied a pharmacopoeia, able to recite every medicine's name by mouth, yet who has never gathered the real herbs himself, and so, when the true medicine is right in front of him, cannot recognize it as genuine, and instead doubts and rejects it. The first line here commands attention; what follows promises the explanation. "I will explain and reveal to you, point by point," the complete meaning of neither causes-and-conditions nor spontaneity — not for Ānanda alone, but for the present assembly, and for those in ages to come who cultivate the Great Vehicle, that all may penetrate through to true reality and enter together into the ocean of the tathāgatagarbha. "True reality" and "tathāgatagarbha" are two names for one and the same substance.

As the Zhèngmài commentary observes: the four categories only established that each and every one of them is, in its own nature, true — without yet saying that each and every one of them pervades the whole Dharma-realm — like pointing to incense-wood or coal and saying, of each, "this is fire," without yet going on to say that each is capable, in principle, of burning down an entire forest. What is promised here, in the detailed treatment of the seven great elements that follows, is precisely that each and every one of them pervades the whole Dharma-realm — like now actually setting each fire alight, showing that every single spark carries within it the full capacity to burn down an entire forest. The four categories only reveal that each phenomenon, in its own substance, is true and ever-abiding; the seven great elements reveal, to the fullest extent, that each phenomenon is perfectly interfused and all-pervading. The difference in depth between these two sections, earlier and later, lies exactly here.

True reality is threefold, corresponding to the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha: first, the true reality of no appearance at all — free of every distinguishing mark of false phenomena, nothing but the one true, universally equal true reality; this is the "empty" tathāgatagarbha, empty of every false, defiled phenomenon's appearance, though not empty of its own substance — this is the tathāgatagarbha's unchanging substance. Second, the true reality of no appearance whatsoever being absent — not lacking appearance, but able to manifest every appearance in accordance with conditions; this is the "non-empty" tathāgatagarbha, complete with every phenomenon of the ten dharma-realms — this is the tathāgatagarbha's function, following conditions. Third, the true reality that is neither of these: to call it "absent" would be to abandon not a single phenomenon; to call it "present" would be to establish not a single speck of dust — this is the "empty and non-empty" tathāgatagarbha, true emptiness never obstructing wondrous existence, wondrous existence never obstructing true emptiness — the tathāgatagarbha's substance and function both manifest at once. "Ānanda fell silent, receiving the sacred instruction": falling silent means gathering his spirit and stilling his thoughts, entering wholeheartedly into the meaning of the Buddha's words, reverently receiving our great sage Buddha's instruction.

The General Analogy: Water Becomes Ice, and Ice Becomes Water Again 卯二 總喻性相

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難，如汝所言：四大和合，發明世間，種種變化。阿難，若彼大性，體非和合，則不能與諸大雜和，猶如虛空，不和諸色。若和合者，同於變化，始終相成，生滅相續；生死死生，生生死死，如旋火輪，未有休息。阿難，如水成冰，冰還成水。

〖講〗此以異喻別明，先明大性，非不和合。大性指四大之性，即如來藏性也。諸大指四大之相，相從性起，相不離性，相即性中之相，性即相中之性，性相雖有二名，性相本來一體，此為正義。若謂彼四大之性，體即性體，如非和合者，只許性之不變，不許性能隨緣，則不能與諸大之相雜和，猶如虛空之不和諸色也。此與真如隨緣之用相反，迴異虛空之不和諸色，故非不和合，此名異喻。

此明大性，非是和合。若謂四大之性，是和合者，只許性之隨緣，不許性仍不變，則應同諸大之相，變易遷化，由始而終，因終復始，而始終相成也。從生至滅，既滅復生，而生滅相續也，此約無情世界而說。生死死生，生生死死者，因生有死，至死復生，而遞互相成也。生而復生，死而再死，亦前後相續也，此約有情根身而說。是知內外四大，皆變化相，若大性如此，猶如旋火成輪，未有休息。此亦法固反言，喻亦反顯。與真如不變之體相反，非同火輪之不息，故非是和合，此亦異喻。此二段文，具宗、因、喻三支比量，以法喻皆與大性相反，落於非量。

此單喻顯法，乃以同喻，翻前異喻。喻大性不變隨緣，隨緣不變，翻前二段：一則體非和合，則墮自然，而失隨緣之義；一、則和合，則墮因緣，而失不變之義。今以同喻，而顯真如不變、隨緣二義，故重呼阿難之名，而告之曰：如水成冰，冰還復也成水。上句大性體雖不變，而用能隨緣成相，既不如虛空不和諸相，當如何等乎？當如水能隨緣成冰，無所和合，而能現和合之相，水不離冰，豈一定屬於非和合耶？下句大性用雖隨緣成相，仍然不變本體，既不如火輪之不息，當如何等乎？當如冰還成水，但似和合，終無和合之實，豈一定屬於和合耶？交光法師謂：還字當玩，足顯不變。正因不變，故還為水，正於還為水處，見其非真和合，若真和合則變矣！如青、黃和合，即變為緣，豈能還為青、黃乎？

總立量云：諸大性相是有法，非不和合非和合為宗，因云：隨緣不變故；同喻如水冰冰水，異喻如虛空火輪。此量與諸大性相相合，為真比量，其理極成。阿難前言，四大和合，發明諸相，如來所答，有二意應研究：一、阿難但問四大之相，如來則雙明性相。良以權宗，雖依性說相，性是密意，惟說法相，不說法性。阿難久習權宗，迷性執相，故有斯問。如來則性相雙舉，明相依性起，以性融相，以袪舊見，冀得新悟也。二、阿難但執因緣和合，乃為自宗，因何排擯？如來則兼破和合及不和合，恐阿難和合被破，轉計不和合，故此兼破，以杜轉計也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, as you have said: the four great elements combine to produce all the world's various transformations. Ānanda, if the nature of these great elements is, in its very substance, not a combination, then it could not mix and combine with the various great elements at all — exactly as empty space does not combine with any form. And if it is a combination, then it would be identical with everything that changes — beginning and end perpetually completing each other, arising and ceasing following one upon another without a break; birth becoming death, death becoming birth, birth upon birth, death upon death, like a wheel of fire spinning without ever coming to rest. Ānanda, it is like water becoming ice, and ice becoming water once again."

Commentary: This uses a contrasting analogy to make a specific point: first, that the nature of the great elements is not "not a combination." "The nature of the great elements" means the nature of the four great elements, which is exactly the tathāgatagarbha's own nature; "the great elements" means the appearance of the four great elements — appearance arises from nature and never departs from it; appearance is exactly the appearance found within nature, and nature is exactly the nature found within appearance. Though nature and appearance carry two different names, they are, at root, one and the same substance — this is the correct understanding. If one claims that the nature of the four great elements, being identical with its own substance, is "not a combination," granting the nature only its unchanging aspect while denying that it can also follow conditions, then it could not mix and combine with the appearance of the various great elements at all — exactly like empty space, which does not combine with any form. But this runs contrary to true suchness's own function of following conditions, quite unlike empty space's failure to combine with form — hence it is not "not a combination" either. This is the contrasting analogy.

This shows that the nature of the great elements is not, either, simply a combination. If one claims that the nature of the four great elements, being a combination, grants the nature only its capacity to follow conditions while denying its unchanging aspect, then it ought to be identical with the appearance of the various great elements, forever shifting and changing, moving from beginning to end and from end back to beginning again. From arising to ceasing, and, having ceased, arising once more, arising and ceasing follow one upon another — this said of the inanimate world. Birth becoming death, death becoming birth, birth upon birth, death upon death: because of birth there is death, and, having died, birth comes again, each completing the other in succession — this said of sentient beings' own bodies. From this one knows that the four great elements, within and without, are nothing but ever-changing appearance — and if the nature of the great elements were truly like this, it would be exactly like a wheel of fire spinning, never coming to rest. This, too, runs contrary to true suchness's own unchanging substance, quite unlike a wheel of fire's failure ever to rest — hence it is not simply a combination either. This is the second contrasting analogy. Both of these passages are cast in the classical three-part form of Buddhist logical demonstration — thesis, reason, and example — with both the stated position and its analogy running contrary to the nature of the great elements, and so both fall into the category of a flawed inference.

This uses a single analogy in its own right to illustrate the point, a positive analogy reversing the two contrasting ones just given — illustrating that the nature of the great elements is unchanging while following conditions, and follows conditions while remaining unchanging, reversing the two prior passages: one, where "not a combination" falls into spontaneity and loses the sense of following conditions; the other, where "a combination" falls into causes-and-conditions and loses the sense of remaining unchanging. Now, with a positive analogy, both of true suchness's aspects are shown together: hence, addressing Ānanda by name once more, the Buddha says: it is like water becoming ice, and ice becoming water once again. The first half: though the great elements' nature remains, in substance, unchanging, its function can still follow conditions to form an appearance — since it is not like empty space's failure to combine with any appearance, what, then, is it like? It is like water, which, following conditions, becomes ice without any actual "combining" taking place, and yet manifests exactly the appearance of a combination — water never apart from ice — so how could it be said to belong, fixedly, to "not a combination" at all? The second half: though the great elements' function follows conditions to form an appearance, it still never departs from its own unchanging substance — since it is not like a wheel of fire's failure ever to rest, what, then, is it like? It is like ice becoming water once again — only resembling a combination, with, in the end, no real combination to it at all — so how could it be said to belong, fixedly, to "a combination" either? Master Jiaoguang notes that the word "again" [還] deserves close attention here, since it is exactly what reveals the unchanging aspect: precisely because it is unchanging, it can become water "again" — and it is exactly at the point of becoming water "again" that one sees this was never a true combination in the first place, for if it were a true combination, it would have genuinely changed, the way blue and yellow combined together change into green, which could never become blue or yellow "again."

The overall demonstration, then, runs: the nature and appearance of the great elements — the thesis being that they are neither "not a combination" nor "a combination"; the reason, that they follow conditions while remaining unchanging; the positive analogy, water becoming ice and ice becoming water; the contrasting analogies, empty space and the wheel of fire. This demonstration truly accords with the nature and appearance of the great elements — a sound inference, its reasoning fully established. Ānanda had spoken only of the four great elements combining to produce every appearance; the Buddha's answer carries two points worth studying closely. First, Ānanda asked only about the appearance of the four great elements, while the Buddha addresses both nature and appearance together — since the provisional teaching, though it does speak of appearance in terms of nature, treats nature as a hidden meaning and speaks openly only of the characteristics of phenomena, never of their nature. The Buddha therefore raises nature and appearance together, using nature to dissolve appearance, in order to dislodge Ānanda's old view and bring about new understanding. Second, Ānanda had clung only to the combination of causes-and-conditions as his own established teaching — why, then, does the Buddha reject it? Because the Buddha refutes both combination and non-combination together, fearing that, once combination is refuted, Ānanda would simply shift to claiming non-combination instead — hence refuting both together, to block this very shift.

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Consider the nature of earth: coarse, it is the great earth itself; fine, it is dust. Down to the 'dust bordering on emptiness' — splitting the very edge of the smallest particle of matter, formed of seven parts — split it once more, and it becomes true empty space itself. If this dust bordering on emptiness can be split into empty space, then you should know that empty space is capable of producing the appearance of form. You now ask: is it not through combination that all the world's various transformations come to be produced? Consider, then, this single mote of dust bordering on emptiness — how much empty space combines to produce it? Empty space could hardly combine to form more of this same dust bordering on emptiness. And if this dust bordering on emptiness is, conversely, split apart into empty space, how much of the appearance of form combines to produce that empty space? At the moment form combines, what combines is form, not emptiness; at the moment emptiness combines, what combines is emptiness, not form. Form, at least, can still be split apart — but how could emptiness ever be combined? You have simply never known that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of form is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true form — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know, manifesting in accordance with karma. The unknowing world mistakes this for a matter of causes and conditions or of spontaneity — all of it merely the discriminating, calculating mind; there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all."

Commentary: Ānanda had spoken only of the four great elements combining to produce the appearances of the world; the Buddha now broadens this into the seven great elements because the four alone do not cover every phenomenon — they take in only the aggregate of form, the first eleven of the twelve sense-fields, and the five faculties and six objects among the eighteen elements, but cannot take in the four mental aggregates, the sense-faculties within the six entrances, the mental faculty within the twelve fields and eighteen elements, or the emptiness paired with form. Hence space, seeing, and consciousness are added, gathering in every conditioned phenomenon without exception. Observe, then, that this nature of earth has both a coarse and a fine aspect: coarse, it is the great earth itself; fine, it is dust — down to the dust bordering on emptiness, splitting a mote of dust into seven parts, called an "extremely fine mote"; splitting that extremely fine mote again into seven parts gives what is called "dust bordering on emptiness." This dust bordering on emptiness, formed by splitting an extremely fine mote into seven parts, is itself the very edge of the appearance of form — bordering directly on emptiness, hence its name. It cannot be split any further; split this dust bordering on emptiness again, and it returns to emptiness outright — truly the nature of emptiness now, no longer the nature of form at all.

This moves from splitting form down into emptiness to fixing, conversely, on emptiness combining to form matter. It says: if this dust bordering on emptiness can be split into empty space, then, by the same logic, empty space ought certainly to be capable of producing the appearance of form — yet emptiness producing form is, on its face, no reasonable proposition at all. The Buddha deliberately poses this difficulty to refute the theory of combination, and, in passing, refutes the false position held by this land's own Confucian school as well — that emptiness gives rise to vital energy, and vital energy condenses into physical form, so that the ten thousand things of the world all arise from the combining of yin and yang energies. This asks Ānanda to examine the question himself: since you say the phenomena of the world are all produced through combination, and dust bordering on emptiness is itself one instance of form, it too ought to come about through combination. Form and emptiness are opposites: form has appearance, emptiness has none; the Buddha, knowing full well that emptiness has no measure and cannot, in principle, be combined with anything at all, deliberately raises this point to refute the theory of combination. Dust bordering on emptiness is the very smallest instance of form — beyond this mote, there is no further form left to combine with it, unless it is emptiness itself that combines to produce it. And claiming that dust bordering on emptiness combines to produce more of itself carries three faults: first, the fault of a thing combining with itself to produce itself, for there has only ever been a thing combining with something else to produce itself; second, the fault of one combining to produce one, for there has only ever been many combining to produce one; third, the fault of the fine combining to produce the fine, for there has only ever been the fine combining to produce the coarse.

The Tathāgata here uses Ānanda's own doctrine of splitting form down into emptiness to challenge his claim of combination. Since splitting form down into emptiness is the Lesser Vehicle's own teaching, while all phenomena arising through combination is Ānanda's own claim, the Buddha now follows Ānanda's own teaching to challenge his own claim. The Buddha, knowing full well that form is not produced by emptiness combining, nor emptiness produced by splitting form apart, deliberately poses this challenge. Why can Ānanda offer no reply? Because, following the logic of splitting form apart into emptiness, this could not be a matter of combination at all, contradicting his own claim that all phenomena arise through combination; yet relying on combination, this could not be a matter of splitting apart either, running contrary to his own school's teaching — defeated on both counts, he falls silent, with nothing left to argue. This states the correct conclusion directly. At the moment form combines, combining form can only ever produce more form, never emptiness — and emptiness, having no shape or appearance at all, cannot, in principle, be combined in the first place. Even granting that empty space itself could combine, combining emptiness would still only ever produce more emptiness, never form. From this one sees clearly that neither form nor emptiness comes about through combination at all. Form, at least, can genuinely be split apart — but splitting form apart only ever produces more form, and is not, in truth, capable of producing emptiness at all; emptiness, in the end, admits of no such thing as combination, since it has no shape and no measure. The Buddha's point is: since emptiness cannot be combined, dust bordering on emptiness does not come about through combination — and if dust bordering on emptiness does not come about through combination, how could the countless phenomena of the world, clung to as arising through the combination of causes and conditions, possibly carry any real substance at all?

This shows the complete substance, perfectly interfused. The form belonging to the great element of earth has, as its very substance, the Dharma-realm itself — form and emptiness perfectly interfused, without obstruction, never a matter of combination at all. Ānanda, clinging fast to causes-and-conditions combining, does so because, lacking the wondrous wisdom of original awakening, he has simply never known that, within the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous principle of original enlightenment, there is no phenomenon it does not contain, no phenomenon it does not interfuse with. "Tathāgatagarbha" is another name for the true mind, a name given in terms of principle. "Tathāgata" names the principle by which the true mind, unchanging, follows conditions, and, following conditions, remains unchanging — perfectly interfused, without obstruction, containing and holding every phenomenon. "The nature of form is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true form" points to how, within the tathāgatagarbha, the true nature of form and emptiness interpenetrate each other completely. Each of the seven great elements is introduced, in turn, with the phrase "within the tathāgatagarbha" — these belong to the seven great elements as inherently possessed in principle, unlike the four categories treated earlier, which belong to the seven great elements as already manifested in actual fact.

Question: "Why are the phenomena of the four categories also identified with the seven great elements?" Answer: "The aggregates, entrances, fields, and elements are no different from the seven great elements — only arranged vertically or horizontally, expanded or condensed, in different ways. Taken vertically, the aggregate of form expands into the first six great elements, while the four mental aggregates condense into the great element of consciousness; taken horizontally, the six sense-objects condense into the first five great elements, the six sense-faculties condense into the great element of seeing, and the six consciousnesses condense into the great element of consciousness. When were they ever anything apart from the seven great elements at all?" Question: "This section properly concerns the great element of earth — why does it not speak of 'earth' but substitute the word 'form' instead?" Answer: "The great element of earth belongs to the category of form; substituting the word 'form' states the point in its most general terms." Speaking of "form" as "the nature of form is true form" does not refer to form as already manifested in the world, but to form as inherently possessed in nature within the tathāgatagarbha — form in its true substance, interfused and identical with emptiness. "Nature" here speaks of what is not mere appearance — that is, principle rather than phenomenon; "true" speaks of what is not the ordinary, worldly sense of the word — that is, substance rather than function.

The two words "nature" and "true" name the very substance of principle, the root of both form and emptiness. Different words, one meaning — used here interchangeably; written out in full without this interchange, it would read as four separate clauses: the nature of form is true emptiness, and true emptiness is the nature of form; the nature of emptiness is true form, and true form is the nature of emptiness — together completing the full sense of form and emptiness interfused as one. "Pure and unmoved from the start" points to the tathāgatagarbha's own inherent purity, needing no effort to bring about — not purified by being cleansed, or made clean by being washed, but pure from the very beginning, without the slightest trace of defilement. "Pervading the whole Dharma-realm": the line before this concerns the tathāgatagarbha's own inherently possessed, unchanging substance; this line concerns its inherently possessed function, following conditions. Before this function has yet arisen, it simply pervades the Dharma-realm of principle; once function arises from substance, it comes, of itself, to pervade the Dharma-realm of phenomena as well.

This shows the great function to be without limit. Function arises from substance; what is brought about in actual fact never departs from what is inherently possessed in principle. Still rooted in the tathāgatagarbha's own unchanging substance, its function now arises, following conditions, in response to the conditions of sentient beings across all nine dharma-realms. "Mind" spoken of in terms of the sense-faculties, whose capacities differ, superior or inferior, across the nine realms; "capacity to know" spoken of in terms of understanding, whose measure differs, greater or lesser, across the nine realms — all of this being distinction already arisen out of what is, at root, an equal, undifferentiated nature. If a sentient being calls upon it only with an inferior mind and a small capacity, then what responds to it — a coarse, meager form — nonetheless never fails to match that mind, nor to fill that capacity; if a sentient being calls upon it with a superior mind and a vast capacity, then what responds to it — a vast, wondrous form — likewise never fails to match that mind, nor to fill that capacity.

The phrase "appearing in accordance with karma" is placed in its own section here because this phrase is exactly what gives rise to the two delusions discussed next. Karma divides into defiled and pure; the form belonging to both the direct and circumstantial retribution of beings across the nine realms all appears in accordance with the karma each being carries. Karma is what brings the appearing about; form is what is brought about — like an echo answering a sound, like a shadow following a shape. This holds not only for the deluded, who must accord with defiled karma before anything appears at all, but even for the awakened, who must likewise accord with pure karma before anything can appear. Master Jiaoguang notes that these four characters carry a double sense of not being free to choose either way: first, every pure, wondrous form, mundane or transcendent, cannot appear at all, however much one might wish it to, without according with the corresponding pure karma; second, every suffering, defiled form of the three lower and four evil destinies cannot fail to appear, however much one might wish otherwise, without guarding against the corresponding defiled karma. "The unknowing world" refers to sentient beings lacking true wisdom, who fail to grasp the tathāgatagarbha's twofold nature — unchanging, and following conditions — and so, from the sheer fact of appearing in accordance with karma, come to raise the twin delusions of causes-and-conditions and spontaneity. "All of it merely the discriminating, calculating mind": since both delusions arise entirely from the sixth consciousness's own deluded mind, falsely discriminating and calculating everywhere — exactly the nature of universal conceptual clinging, which is, in itself, empty — hence: "there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all."

Fire, Water, and Wind 火大 · 水大 · 風大

Yuanying applies the identical four-part pattern — standing forth the element with a homely test, refuting every possible source in turn, revealing the substance-and-function of the tathāgatagarbha, then dispelling both the causes-and-conditions and spontaneity misconceptions — to fire, water, and wind. The sūtra's own demonstrations for all three have already been translated in the bare-sūtra project's fascicle 3.

Fire 火大 — Tested through a household lighting its stove with a burning-glass (陽燧) held up to the sun. Before refuting sun, mirror, and tinder in turn, Yuanying adds a distinctive analogy of his own: true combination, he argues, always requires things with genuinely distinct origins that then merge, the way 1,250 monks with 1,250 separate family lineages — Śāriputra of the Brahmin clan, Uruvilvā-Kāśyapa of the Kāśyapa clan, Ānanda himself of the "sugar-cane clan," so named because the Buddha's own ancestor was said to have been born when sunlight fell on a stalk of sugarcane — come together to form a single assembly. Fire, tested the same way, has no such distinguishable origins to combine from at all, "sun and mirror far apart from each other, neither mixed nor joined" — and yet undeniably real.
Water 水大 — Tested through the ritual practice of sorcerers like the sage Kapila, who, under a bright midnight moon, hold up a water-gathering pearl (方諸) to collect "the essence of the moon" for mixing into magical elixirs. Refuted across the pearl, empty space, and the moon itself: if from the moon, the water should flow continuously along the moon's whole path across the sky, not just where the pearl is held; if from the pearl, it should flow constantly, with no need to wait for midnight and a bright moon; if from space, all space everywhere, without limit, would be equally awash — leaving no distinction at all between water, land, and open air.
Wind 風大 — Tested through the everyday moment of straightening one's great robe (僧伽黎, the twenty-five-patch outer robe) before entering the assembly, its corner brushing a neighbor's face with a faint breeze. Refuted across the robe's corner, empty space, and the person's own face — with a pointed final question: if the breeze truly arose from that person's face, it ought to blow back toward them, not toward whoever is straightening their robe. Yuanying closes: seeing and emptiness, moving and still, share no common nature at all — neither combined, nor without any source whatsoever.

Space 空大

〖經〗阿難，空性無形，因色顯發：如室羅城，去河遙處，諸剎利種，及婆羅門，毘舍、首陀、兼頗羅墮、旃陀羅等，新立安居，鑿井求水，出土一尺，於中則有一尺虛空；如是乃至出土一丈，中間還得一丈虛空，虛空淺深，隨出多少。此空為當因土所出？因鑿所有？無因自生？若復此空，無因自生，未鑿土前，何不無礙？惟見大地，迥無通達。若因土出，則土出時，應見空入，若土先出，無空入者，云何虛空，因土而出？若無出入，則應空土，原無異因；無異則同，則土出時，空何不出？若因鑿出，則鑿出空，應非出土？不因鑿出，鑿自出土，云何見空？汝更審諦，諦審諦觀：鑿從人手，隨方運轉，土因地移，如是虛空，因何所出？鑿、空虛實，不相為用，非和非合；不應虛空，無從自出。若此虛空，性圓周遍，本不動搖，當知現前，地、水、火、風，均名五大，性真圓融，皆如來藏，本無生滅。汝全不知：如來藏中，性覺真空，性空真覺，清淨本然，周遍法界。隨眾生心，應所知量。阿難，如一井空，空生一井；十方虛空，亦復如是，圓滿十方，寧有方所。循業發現。世間無知，惑為因緣，及自然性，皆是識心，分別計度。但有言說，都無實義。

〖講〗空性無形：此句指不變之體，性者體性，本無大小方圓之形段，有形則有變，無形則不變。因色顯發：此句指隨緣之用，因隨色塵之緣，顯現發明也。如顯見無礙科，觀一界之大則現大空，一室之小則現小空，方器則現方空，圓器則現圓空，雖隨緣顯發，空體不變。虛空淺深，乃隨鑿出之土，多少而論。此即空性無形，因色顯發之事實。

此破空大無因生。若復此空，無因自然而生，未鑿土前，即應無礙，何以不能無礙，惟見大地，迥然質礙，無有通達之相。鑿土方見虛空，非無因而生也。此破空大因土所出。以未鑿土前，原不見空，因鑿土出，而後成空，遂謂空因土出。則土鑿出之時，應見虛空，從井內周圍土中出來，入於井中。若土先出，無空入者，如何可說，虛空因土所出？此破空無出入。若謂空本在土中，無有從土出，從井入者。則應未鑿土前，空之與土，本無各異之因；同處相依，無異則是同體不分，則土出時，空何以不與土而俱出耶？

此破因鑿不因鑿，兩者俱非。若謂此空，是因鑿而出，則是鑿出虛空，應非出土而成空也。若謂此空，不因鑿出，與鑿無干者，則鑿井之時，但自出土，應不成空，云何隨鑿，隨見虛空耶？此更教以審察諦觀：井因鑿出，鑿從人手，隨其方向，擇地施工，運轉而成，土因地中搬移而出；如是井內虛空，因何所出？以此空，既非無因自出，又非土出，又非鑿出，畢竟從何所出耶？鑿體是實，空性是虛，一虛一實，不相為用，云何可說因緣和合而有？故曰非和非合；不應說言，此空無所從來，自然而出？此即雙破因緣、自然二計。

佛意以此空大，合前四大，同名五大，會歸藏性。諸經多惟說四大，此經點出空大，例彼四大，同名五大。當知非僅虛空，新得大名，兼顯四大，昔日雖稱為大，亦惟其據處處皆有，地水火風言之，而實未顯互不相礙，非真大也。自今融以藏性，圓遍常住，方為真大。性真圓融，皆如來藏，本無生滅者：即言諸大一一，唯性唯真，如前所云，性色真空，乃至性風真空等，以性融大，諸大全性，故曰皆如來藏。

汝全不知，寓有深責之意。我如是為汝種種開示，中道了義，要汝觀空得悟。汝竟全然不知，如來藏中，性具之覺，本是真體之空；性具之空，即是真體之覺。此二句，按前空大，變其文法，將前指性之空，換為覺字，空覺二字，皆指藏性。藏性具有寂、照二義，空是寂義，覺是照義。又復顛倒其詞，將本大性空之句放在下句，若不變其文，則雙句皆是，性空真空，性空真空，無可分別。

此即事以驗，性圓周遍。如一處鑿井，所見之空，空生一井；十方鑿井，所現虛空，亦復如此。一井之空，非出非入者是也。虛空圓滿，周遍十方，寧有一定方所？正由虛空，無所不遍，故成大義。非此有彼無，先無今有，不但具有大義，亦具常義，故前云：「本不動搖，本無生滅」也。

迷位眾生，循其所造有漏之業，則發現染空，如第四禪天人，厭有趣空，則現空無邊處。修位眾生，循其所修無漏之業，則發現淨空，如第四果羅漢，灰身泯智，則現偏空涅槃。世間無知：此指凡、小、外道，惑為空大，是因緣所生，或執空大，乃自然而有，皆是識心分別計度，遍計執性用事，但有言說，都無真實之義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, space has no form of its own; it is revealed only through form. Consider a household far from the river in Śrāvastī — of the warrior caste, the Brahmin caste, the merchant, farmer, artisan, or outcaste castes alike — newly settled, digging a well in search of water. For every foot of earth removed, a foot of empty space appears in its place; and so on, up to removing a full fathom of earth, revealing a full fathom of empty space in turn — the depth of the space keeping exact pace with how much earth is removed. Does this space come from the earth, from the digging, or does it arise spontaneously, with no cause at all? If this space arose with no cause at all, spontaneously, then why, before the well was dug, was it not already unobstructed — why did one see only solid earth, with nothing passing through it at all? If it comes out of the earth, then, at the moment the earth comes out, one ought to see space going in — and if the earth comes out first, with no space going in, how could empty space be said to come out of the earth at all? If there is no coming out or going in, then space and earth would originally share no distinct cause, and, sharing none, would be one and the same thing — in which case, at the moment the earth comes out, why does the space not come out along with it? If it comes out because of the digging, then what the digging produces ought to be space, not earth. And if it does not come out because of the digging, then the digging simply produces earth on its own — so how does one come to see space at all? Examine this yet again, closely, carefully, attentively: the digging comes from the hand, turning this way and that as it is directed; the earth is displaced from the ground. On what basis, then, does this empty space come out at all? Digging and emptiness, one solid and one empty, cannot act upon each other; there is neither a true mixing nor a true combining between them, and yet it would be wrong to say this space simply has no source at all. If this empty space is, in its nature, perfectly complete and all-pervading, never in itself unsettled or moved, then you should know that the earth, water, fire, and wind now before you are, every one, equally to be called among these same five great elements — each, in its true nature, perfectly interfused, each the tathāgatagarbha itself, originally free of arising or ceasing. You do not know at all that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of awareness is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true awareness — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know. Ānanda, as with a single well, opening a single well's worth of space; and so it is, too, with the empty space of the ten directions — vast and complete throughout the ten directions, how could it have any fixed location at all? The unknowing world mistakes this for a matter of causes and conditions or of spontaneity — all of it merely the discriminating, calculating mind; there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all."

Commentary: "Space has no form of its own" points to its unchanging substance: "nature" here means substance itself, which has, from the start, no shape at all — large, small, square, or round; where there is shape, there is change, and where there is no shape, there is no change. "It is revealed only through form" points to its function, following conditions: revealed and manifested through whatever object of form it happens to depend on — as already shown in the display of seeing as unobstructed, where a vast realm shows a vast emptiness, a small room shows a small emptiness, a square vessel shows a square emptiness, a round vessel a round emptiness — however much it is revealed differently through different conditions, the substance of emptiness itself never changes.

This refutes space arising with no cause: if this space arose spontaneously, then, before the well was dug, it ought already to have been unobstructed — why, then, was solid earth all that could be seen, with nothing at all passing through it? Only once the earth was dug did empty space appear — not something arising with no cause at all. This refutes space coming out of the earth: since, before the well was dug, no space was seen at all, and space only came to be once the earth was dug out, one is tempted to say that space comes out of the earth. But, at the moment the earth is dug out, one ought to see empty space coming in from the surrounding earth into the well — yet if the earth comes out first, with no space going in, how could one say that empty space comes out of the earth at all? This refutes there being no coming out or going in at all: if space were already present within the earth, neither coming out of it nor going into the well, then, before the well was dug, space and earth would have to share the very same cause, occupying the same place, depending on each other — sharing no distinction between them, one and the same substance — in which case, at the moment the earth comes out, why would the space not come out along with it?

This refutes both "because of the digging" and "not because of the digging," neither holding. If this space comes out because of the digging, then what the digging produces is empty space, and ought not to be earth at all. And if this space does not come out because of the digging, having nothing to do with it, then digging the well ought simply to produce earth on its own, with no space resulting at all — so how does one come to see empty space appear along with every stroke of digging? This teaches Ānanda to examine and scrutinize yet further: the well comes about through digging, the digging comes from the hand, turning this way and that as it is directed to the chosen spot; the earth is displaced, moved out of the ground. On what basis, then, does this empty space within the well actually come out at all? Since this space arises neither with no cause at all, nor out of the earth, nor because of the digging — where, in the end, does it actually come from? The substance of the digging is solid; the nature of space is empty — one empty and one solid, unable to act on each other at all — how, then, could one say this comes about through the combination of causes and conditions? Hence: "neither a true mixing nor a true combining"; and yet it would be wrong to say this space simply has no source at all, arising spontaneously — this refutes both the causes-and-conditions and the spontaneity misconceptions together.

The Buddha's intent here is to merge this great element of space with the four preceding it, calling them together the "five great elements," drawing them back into the tathāgatagarbha's own nature. Most sūtras speak only of four great elements; this sūtra singles out space as well, showing it equal to the other four, together called the five great elements. One should understand that it is not simply that empty space has newly earned the name "great" — the other four, though called "great" before now, were so called merely on the grounds that earth, water, fire, and wind are found everywhere, without yet showing that they are mutually unobstructed with one another — not yet truly "great" in this fuller sense. Only now, harmonized with the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, perfectly pervading and ever-abiding, do they become truly "great." "Each, in its true nature, perfectly interfused, each the tathāgatagarbha itself, originally free of arising or ceasing" means: each and every one of the great elements is nothing but nature, nothing but truth — as already said, the nature of form is true emptiness, down to the nature of wind is true emptiness, and so on — using nature to interfuse each element, each element entirely nature, hence "each the tathāgatagarbha."

"You do not know at all" carries a note of deep reproach: after all this careful, repeated instruction in the middle way's complete meaning, meant to bring you to awakening through observing emptiness, you still do not know at all that, within the tathāgatagarbha, awareness as inherently possessed in nature is, at root, emptiness in its true substance, and emptiness as inherently possessed in nature is exactly awareness in its true substance. These two lines vary the pattern used for the great element of space itself, substituting the word "awareness" where the earlier pattern would simply repeat "space" — both words referring here to the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, which holds both stillness and illumination together: emptiness names the stillness, awareness names the illumination. The wording is also reversed here — for if left unvaried, both lines would simply read "the nature of space is true space," with nothing left to distinguish between them.

This uses an actual instance to verify that the nature of space is perfectly complete and all-pervading: just as digging a well in one place produces exactly that well's worth of space, digging wells throughout the ten directions produces exactly that same pattern of space wherever they are dug — this is precisely what it means for a single well's space to be neither coming out nor going in. Empty space, vast and complete, pervading the ten directions — how could it have any fixed location at all? It is exactly because empty space is nowhere absent that it earns the name "great" — not present here and absent there, not absent before and present now; it possesses not only the quality of greatness but the quality of permanence as well, hence the earlier description: "never in itself unsettled or moved, originally free of arising or ceasing."

Deluded beings, according with the tainted karma they have created, manifest a defiled emptiness — as when beings of the fourth dhyāna heaven, wearied of existence and drawn toward emptiness, manifest the base of infinite space. Cultivating beings, according with the untainted karma they have cultivated, manifest a pure emptiness — as when fourth-fruit arhats, extinguishing body and wisdom alike, manifest the one-sided emptiness of nirvāṇa. "The unknowing world" refers to ordinary people, Lesser-Vehicle followers, and non-Buddhist schools, who mistake the great element of space for something produced by causes and conditions, or else cling to it as something spontaneously existing — all of it nothing but the discriminating, calculating mind; there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all.

Seeing 見大

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難，見覺無知，因色空有。如汝今者，在祗陀林，朝明夕昏；設居中宵，白月則光，黑月便暗，則明暗等，因見分析。此見為復與明暗相，並太虛空，為同一體？為非一體？或同非同？或異非異？阿難，此見若復與明與暗，及與虛空，元一體者，則明與暗，二體相亡；暗時無明，明時無暗，若與暗一，明則見亡，必一於明，暗時當滅，滅則云何見明見暗？若明暗殊，見無生滅，一云何成？若此見精，與暗與明，非一體者，汝離明暗，及與虛空，分析見元，作何形相？離明離暗，及離虛空，是見元同龜毛、兔角；明、暗、虛空三事俱異，從何立見？明暗相背，云何或同？離三元無，云何或異？分空分見，本無邊畔，云何非同？見暗見明，性非遷改，云何非異？汝更細審，微細審詳，審諦審觀：明從太陽，暗隨黑月，通屬虛空，壅歸大地，如是見精，因何所出？見覺空頑，非和非合；不應見精，無從自出？若見、聞、知，性圓周遍，本不動搖，當知無邊不動虛空，併其動搖地、水、火、風，均名六大，性真圓融，皆如來藏，本無生滅。汝曾不知：如來藏中，性見覺明，覺精明見，清淨本然，周遍法界。隨眾生心，應所知量。如一見根，見周法界；聽、嗅、嘗、觸，覺觸、覺知，妙德瑩然，遍周法界，圓滿十虛，寧有方所？循業發現。世間無知，惑為因緣，及自然性，皆是識心，分別計度，但有言說都無實義。

〖講〗見大統指六根中，見、聞、嗅、嘗、覺、知之性，今單舉見，以例餘五耳。此屬第八識見分，映在六根門頭，緣彼現量六塵者，非取浮、勝二根，故不言根，而言見也。本經立此大，有三義存焉：一、收前文：前十番顯見，顯此見大是真，今更申明見聞等性，即如來藏，則顯見是心之旨愈暢。二、順後文：諸佛異口同音，告阿難言，使汝輪轉，生死結根，唯汝六根，更非他物；令汝速證安樂解脫，寂靜妙常，亦汝六根，更無他物；欲說根性法門，應立此大。三、為圓通故：若無根大，則大勢至念佛圓通，都攝六根，何所依據？故須立此大，具足二十五數也。

見覺無知，因色空有者：乃明見大即藏性，見覺即見性，是靈明洞澈之覺體，一段光明，寂而常照，不立能所，本來無有，能知所知，乃因色空，而有能知，及所知耳。由有色、空之塵，黏湛發見，始有能見之根，與所見之塵，而成能所二知。見覺無知，指不變之體，因色空有，指隨緣之用。此見為復與明暗相，並太虛空，為同一體？為非一體？或同非同？或異非異？佛先徵後破，共有四句：一同、二異，三或同或異，四非同非異。

此破見大，與塵同一體。若復此見，與明與暗，及與虛空，元是一體者，一體則合而不分，則明之與暗，二體更互相亡：暗時無有明，則明亡；明時無有暗，則暗亡。明暗相傾奪，故二體相亡，此見究竟與誰為一耶？若此見與暗為一體者，明生暗滅，則見應當與暗偕亡，云何又能見明？若必此見與明為一體者，暗生明滅，則見亦當與明以俱滅，云何又能見暗？以明來暗去，暗生明滅，明暗雖復差殊，見性本無生滅，一體之義，云何得成？

此破見大與塵非一體。若此見精，與暗與明，非一體者，則見當離塵，別有自體。若離明暗，及與虛空，分析汝見元本體，究竟作何形相？離明離暗，及離虛空，是見元同於龜毛兔角之本無；若明、暗、虛空三事俱異，無塵不能立見，如是云何可說，此見與塵非一體耶？

此破或同或異。明暗互奪兩亡，是為相背，云何或同？離卻明、暗、虛空三者，元無獨立之見，云何或異？此破非同非異。若謂見性，非同於塵，應可分析，各自有體，現今分析所見之空攝色，分析能見之見，本無空、見，邊際界畔可得，云何可說為非同乎？見暗見明，明暗有互相傾奪，見性無遷變改易，一則生滅，一則常住，不得混同，云何可說為非異乎？

此令審觀生信，故謂汝更加細審。當觀所見之明相，從於太陽，暗相隨於黑月，通相屬於虛空，壅相歸於大地，各有從來，汝能見明、暗、通、塞之見精，畢竟因何所出？既無所出，將何和合耶？此破非因緣非自然。見精有知覺，虛空是頑鈍，體性各異，非和非合，何得謂曰，諸法皆由因緣，和合而有？既非因緣，不應見精，無從自然而出。

佛意以此見大，合前五大，同名六大，會歸藏性。五大是無情，見大是有情，合會情與無情共一體。性圓周遍：性即六精之性，本來圓滿，周遍法界，非此有彼無，此無彼有，方是性圓周遍；本不動搖生滅，非先無今有，今有後無，乃是常住義。當知無邊不動虛空，並其動搖地、水、火、風，與此見大均平，以其體性平等，可名六大。

汝曾不知如來藏中，性具之見，即是覺體本明，本覺之精，即是妙明真見。清淨本然二句，謂見等體本清淨，廣大圓滿，周遍法界。此顯大用無限。隨即隨緣起用，能隨九界眾生，勝劣之心，大小之量，一一應之；下則舉例以顯，如一見根，稱體周遍，以例諸根，耳之聽，鼻之嗅，舌之嘗觸，身之覺觸，意之覺知，此等諸根，即妙性之德用，清淨光明，如玉之瑩光皎潔，俱同見根，遍周法界，圓滿十方虛空，寧有一定方所，即無在無所不在也。

循九界眾生之業，發現之見，各有不同。眾生肉眼，不見障外之色；天眼視遠惟明；慧眼見色了空；法眼遍觀塵世，此皆循染淨業，發現勝劣見。世間無知眾生，惑為因緣、自然，皆是意識妄心，妄生分別計度，但有言說，都無真實之義。

問：「十番顯見，已顯見性即是真心，而為諸法總相，今則融入藏性，則見大乃為別相，未審其義云何？」答：「此經推重圓通，悟修證入，皆依六根，故前特顯見性，以為全體，必須悟此不生滅性，為本脩因，然後方可圓成，果地修證。」此七大普融萬法，而如來藏當為總相，萬法皆為別相。若約圓實教旨，法法皆可互為總別，如帝網千珠，一珠含多珠，多珠趣一珠，以一珠為總相，多珠為別相，珠珠皆然，即是互為總別。《正脈》云：良以前之開顯，今之融入，俱有初後二相。前之初相，自根中薦出，及其後相，則會萬法為一體，而根身器界，皆是其中幻影，當即是此中，如來藏也。今之初相，亦從目前明暗辨起，與前根中薦出無異，及其後相，則合會結顯，性真圓融，周遍法界，當亦與前開顯後相，無有異也。又此中七大，皆許同是圓融，又是依圓旨之萬法互含也。而彼中見性，獨許冠於萬法，又是本經之別旨宗要也。當知前欲其巧於悟修，而此欲其圓於見解矣。具眼者辨之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, the awareness of seeing has, in itself, no content of its own — it appears only through brightness and darkness. Just as, now, in the Jeta Grove, it is bright in the morning and dim by evening; and, dwelling through the middle of the night, it is light when the moon is full and dark when the moon is new — brightness, darkness, and the rest are each distinguished only because of seeing. Is this seeing one and the same substance as brightness, darkness, and empty space together, or not the same substance? Is it, perhaps, both the same and not the same, or neither the same nor different? Ānanda, if this seeing were, at root, one and the same substance as brightness, darkness, and emptiness, then brightness and darkness, two substances, would cancel each other out: when it is dark there is no brightness, when it is bright there is no darkness — so if seeing were one with darkness, then, at the moment of brightness, seeing itself ought to vanish along with the darkness; and if it must instead be one with brightness, then, at the moment of darkness, it ought to vanish along with the brightness. But if it vanishes either way, how could it still see brightness, and see darkness too? And if brightness and darkness are, in fact, distinct from each other while seeing itself neither arises nor ceases along with either one, then how could 'one and the same substance' possibly hold at all? If this essence of seeing were not one and the same substance as darkness, brightness, and emptiness, then, apart from brightness, darkness, and emptiness, what shape would this seeing itself, examined on its own, actually have? Apart from brightness, apart from darkness, and apart from emptiness too, this seeing would be exactly like the hair of a tortoise or the horns of a hare — nothing at all. And if brightness, darkness, and emptiness are truly three distinct things, on what basis could seeing itself even be established? Brightness and darkness stand opposed to each other — how, then, could seeing be 'the same' as both, even partly? Apart from these three, there is, to begin with, no seeing at all to speak of — how, then, could it be 'different' from them, even partly? Divide emptiness, divide seeing — neither one has any boundary or edge to be found — how, then, could they be 'not the same'? And seeing darkness, seeing brightness — the seeing-nature itself never shifts or changes — how, then, could it be 'not different' either? Examine this yet more closely, in the finest detail, scrutinizing it with full attention: brightness comes from the sun, darkness follows the moon's dark phase, openness belongs to empty space, obstruction belongs to solid earth — on what basis, then, does this essence of seeing itself come about? Seeing is aware; emptiness is inert — neither a true mixing nor a true combining between them — and yet it would be wrong to say this essence of seeing simply has no source at all. If seeing, hearing, and knowing are, in their nature, perfectly complete and all-pervading, never in themselves unsettled or moved, then you should know that the boundless, unmoving emptiness, together with the shifting, moving earth, water, fire, and wind, are, every one, equally to be called among these same six great elements — each, in its true nature, perfectly interfused, each the tathāgatagarbha itself, originally free of arising or ceasing. You have never known that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of seeing is true awareness and bright, the essence of awareness is true, bright seeing — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know. As with the single faculty of seeing, whose seeing pervades the whole Dharma-realm — so too with hearing, smelling, tasting, the sensation of touch, and cognition: each of these wondrous virtues shines out clear as polished jade, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, perfectly complete throughout the ten directions of emptiness — how could any of them have a fixed location at all? The unknowing world mistakes this for a matter of causes and conditions or of spontaneity — all of it merely the discriminating, calculating mind; there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all."

Commentary: "Seeing" here stands generally for the whole capacity, across the six sense-faculties, to see, hear, smell, taste, sense touch, and know — seeing alone is singled out as representative of all six. This belongs to the eighth consciousness's own seeing-portion, reflected at the gateway of the six sense-faculties, taking hold of the direct perception of the six sense-objects — not the physical, gross sense-organ itself, hence the term "seeing" rather than "faculty." This sūtra establishes seeing as its own great element for three reasons: first, to gather up what came before — the ten displays of seeing already revealed that this great element of seeing is true, and restating here that seeing, hearing, and the rest are exactly the tathāgatagarbha makes the point that seeing is mind ring out even more fully. Second, to prepare for what follows — the Buddhas will tell Ānanda that what binds him to the cycle of birth and death, and what will bring him swiftly to liberation, is nothing but his own six sense-faculties, and nothing else — and to teach this Dharma-gate, this great element must first be established. Third, for the sake of perfect penetration — since, without this great element of seeing established, Mahāsthāmaprāpta's own method of perfect penetration through reciting the Buddha's name, "gathering in all six sense-faculties together," would have nothing to rest on at all; hence this great element must be established, to complete the full count of twenty-five.

"The awareness of seeing has, in itself, no content of its own; it appears only through brightness and darkness" shows that the great element of seeing is exactly the tathāgatagarbha: the awareness of seeing is exactly the seeing-nature, a luminous, thoroughly clear substance of awareness, a single stretch of brightness, still and yet constantly illuminating, establishing no subject or object of its own — originally without any knower or known at all, until, dependent on form and emptiness, a knower and a known come to be. Because of the object of form and emptiness clinging to the still substance and giving rise to seeing, a faculty capable of seeing, and an object seen, first come to be. "The awareness of seeing has, in itself, no content" points to its unchanging substance; "it appears only through brightness and darkness" points to its function, following conditions. The Buddha asks whether this seeing is one and the same substance as brightness, darkness, and empty space together, or not the same substance, or perhaps both, or neither — raising the question first, four possibilities in all, before refuting each in turn.

This refutes seeing being one and the same substance as its object. If seeing were, at root, one and the same substance as brightness, darkness, and emptiness, then, being one substance, undivided, brightness and darkness, two substances, would cancel each other out: when it is dark there is no brightness, so brightness is gone; when it is bright there is no darkness, so darkness is gone. Brightness and darkness overturning each other this way, both substances cancel out — so which, in the end, is seeing supposed to be one with? If seeing were one substance with darkness, then, brightness arising and darkness ceasing, seeing itself ought to vanish along with the darkness — so how could it still see brightness? And if seeing must instead be one substance with brightness, then, darkness arising and brightness ceasing, seeing ought to vanish along with the brightness as well — so how could it still see darkness? Brightness arriving, darkness departing, darkness arising, brightness ceasing — however much brightness and darkness differ from each other, the seeing-nature itself is never subject to arising or ceasing at all — how, then, could the claim of "one and the same substance" possibly hold?

This refutes seeing being a substance distinct from its object. If this essence of seeing were not one and the same substance as darkness and brightness, then seeing ought to stand apart from its object, possessing some separate substance of its own. Apart from brightness and darkness, and apart from emptiness too, examine this seeing's own original substance closely — what shape, in the end, would it actually have? Apart from brightness, apart from darkness, and apart from emptiness, this original seeing would be exactly like the hair of a tortoise or the horns of a hare — things that were never there to begin with. And if brightness, darkness, and emptiness are indeed three wholly distinct things, with no object at all, seeing itself could never be established — so how, then, could one possibly claim that seeing is a substance distinct from its object?

This refutes "partly the same": brightness and darkness overturn each other, standing opposed — how, then, could seeing be "partly the same" as both? This refutes "partly different": apart from these three, brightness, darkness, and emptiness, there is, to begin with, no independent seeing at all to speak of — how, then, could it be "partly different" from them? This refutes "neither the same nor different": if one claims seeing is not the same as its object, it ought to be possible to divide them apart, each with its own separate substance — yet examine the emptiness taking in form that is seen, and examine the seeing capable of seeing it, and neither space nor seeing has any boundary or edge to be found at all — so how could one possibly claim "not the same"? And seeing darkness, seeing brightness — brightness and darkness overturn each other, arising and ceasing, while the seeing-nature itself undergoes no change or alteration at all — one subject to arising and ceasing, the other ever-abiding, the two never to be confused — so how could one possibly claim "not different" either?

This directs Ānanda to scrutinize the matter closely enough to arrive at real conviction: one should observe that the appearance of brightness comes from the sun, the appearance of darkness follows the moon's dark phase, the appearance of openness belongs to empty space, the appearance of obstruction belongs to solid earth — each with its own clear source. But this essence of seeing, capable of seeing brightness, darkness, openness, and obstruction alike — on what basis, in the end, does it itself come about? With no source to be found for it at all, what could it possibly be said to combine with? This refutes both causes-and-conditions and spontaneity together: seeing is aware, while emptiness is entirely inert — their substance and nature simply differ, admitting neither a true mixing nor a true combining — how, then, could one say that all phenomena come about through the combination of causes and conditions? And since it is not a matter of causes-and-conditions, neither should one say that this essence of seeing simply has no source at all, arising spontaneously.

The Buddha's intent here is to merge this great element of seeing with the five preceding it, calling them together the "six great elements," drawing them back into the tathāgatagarbha's own nature — the five great elements before it being insentient, this great element of seeing being sentient, merging the sentient and the insentient together into a single substance. "Perfectly complete and all-pervading" means: the nature of these six essences is, from the start, perfectly complete, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, never present here and absent there, or absent here and present there — this alone is what "perfectly complete and all-pervading" truly means; "never in themselves unsettled or moved, free of arising and ceasing" means: not absent before and present now, nor present now and absent later — this is exactly what "ever-abiding" means. You should know that the boundless, unmoving emptiness, together with the shifting, moving earth, water, fire, and wind, are equal in standing to this same great element of seeing, their substance and nature equal alike — hence they may be called, together, the six great elements.

You have never known that, within the tathāgatagarbha, seeing as inherently possessed in nature is exactly the substance of awakening, originally bright; the essence of original enlightenment is exactly this same wondrous, bright, true seeing. "Pure and unmoved from the start" means that the substance of seeing and the rest is, from the beginning, pure, vast, complete, pervading the whole Dharma-realm. This shows the great function to be without limit — its function arising, following conditions, able to answer the superior or inferior mind, the greater or lesser capacity, of beings across the nine realms, each and every one; as with the single faculty of seeing, whose full substance pervades everywhere, so too with the ear's hearing, the nose's smelling, the tongue's tasting, the body's sensing, and the mind's cognition — each of these faculties is exactly the virtuous function of this same wondrous nature, pure and bright, clear as polished jade, equally pervading the whole Dharma-realm, perfectly complete throughout the ten directions of empty space — how could any of them have any fixed location at all? That is, present nowhere in particular, and absent nowhere at all.

According with the karma of beings across the nine realms, the seeing that appears differs from being to being: an ordinary being's fleshly eye cannot see form behind an obstruction; the heavenly eye sees far and clearly; the wisdom eye sees form and understands emptiness within it; the Dharma eye surveys the whole dusty world at once — all of this simply the appearing of superior or inferior seeing in accordance with defiled or pure karma. The unknowing world mistakes this for a matter of causes-and-conditions or of spontaneity — all of it nothing but the discriminating, deluded sixth-consciousness mind; there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all.

Question: "The ten displays of seeing already revealed that the seeing-nature is exactly the true mind, serving as the general characteristic underlying every phenomenon — but now, harmonized into the tathāgatagarbha, the great element of seeing becomes merely one specific characteristic among others. How should this discrepancy be understood?" Answer: "This sūtra places its whole emphasis on perfect penetration — awakening, cultivation, realization, and entry all rest on the six sense-faculties — hence the earlier passage singled out the seeing-nature specifically, presenting it as the whole substance, so that awakening to this unborn, unceasing nature could serve as the root cause of practice, and only then could realization at the level of the fruit be perfectly accomplished." Here, the seven great elements interfuse the whole of phenomena together, with the tathāgatagarbha itself standing as the general characteristic, and each of the seven as a specific characteristic. But under the perfect, real teaching's own principle, every single phenomenon can equally serve as either general or specific characteristic in turn — like Indra's net of a thousand jewels, where any one jewel contains all the others, and all the others converge in that one jewel; take that one jewel as the general characteristic and the rest as specific, and the same holds true of every jewel in turn.

The Zhèngmài commentary says: the earlier "opening and revealing" and the present "harmonizing and drawing in" each carry an initial phase and a later phase. In the earlier passage's initial phase, the true mind is drawn directly out from within the sense-faculty itself; in its later phase, the ten thousand phenomena are gathered into a single substance, with the sense-faculties, the body, and the material world all revealed as nothing but illusory reflections within that same substance — which is exactly this same tathāgatagarbha spoken of here. In the present passage's initial phase, the argument likewise begins from distinguishing brightness and darkness directly in front of one's eyes, no different from the earlier passage's drawing the truth out from within the sense-faculty; and in its later phase, harmonizing and concluding that the true nature is perfectly interfused, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, is likewise no different from the earlier passage's own later phase. And here, all seven great elements alike are granted to be perfectly interfused — this, too, is the perfect teaching's own principle that every phenomenon mutually contains every other. Yet, in the earlier passage, the seeing-nature alone was granted pride of place over every other phenomenon — this is this sūtra's own distinctive central teaching. One should understand that the earlier passage aimed at skillful awakening and cultivation, while this present passage aims at a perfectly rounded understanding. Let those with discerning eyes judge for themselves.

〖經〗阿難，識性無源，因於六種根塵妄出。汝今遍觀：此會聖眾，用目循歷，其目周視，但如鏡中，無別分析。汝識於中，次第標指，此是文殊，此富樓那，此目犍連，此須菩提，此舍利弗。此識了知，為生於見？為生於相？為生虛空？為無所因，突然而出？阿難，若汝識性，生於見中，如無明暗，及與色空，四種必無，元無汝見，見性尚無，從何發識？若汝識性，生於相中，不從見生，既不見明，亦不見暗，明暗不矚，即無色空，彼相尚無，識從何發？若生於空，非相非見，非見無辨，自不能知，明暗色空；非相滅緣，見、聞、覺、知，無處安立？處此二非，空則同無，有非同物，縱發汝識，欲何分別？若無所因，突然而出，何不日中，別識明月？汝更細詳，微細詳審：見託汝睛，相推前境，可狀成有，不相成無，如是識緣，因何所出？識動、見澄，非和非合，聞、聽、覺、知，亦復如是；不應識緣，無從自出？若此識心，本無所從，當知了別，見、聞、覺、知，圓滿湛然，性非從所，兼彼虛空，地、水、火、風，均名七大，性真圓融，皆如來藏，本無生滅。阿難，汝心麤浮，不悟見聞，發明了知，本如來藏。汝應觀此：六處識心，為同為異？為空為有？為非同異？為非空有？汝元不知：如來藏中，性識明知，覺明真識，妙覺湛然，遍周法界。含吐十虛，寧有方所？循業發現。世間無知，惑為因緣，及自然性，皆是識心，分別計度，但有言說，都無實義。

〖講〗「識性」對下六種根塵，是指前六識，性即了別性，此性無有根源。因於六種根塵為緣，虛妄顯現，乃為塵影，塵有則有，塵無則無，是謂無源。汝今遍觀：此會聖眾，用目循歷者：用眼目循序歷覽。其目周圍巡視，此根塵相對之時，一念未起，正根中見性，取現量性境。但如鏡中現像，元無妍、媸、美、惡，差別之分析也。汝識於中，次第標指者：於此聖眾之中，次第與循歷相照應，眼根循序而觀，意識次第標名指相。此揀明眼識，與隨眼意識之相。

此先徵起，下則逐破。此破因根生。見即根也。離塵無根，從何發識？此破因塵生。相即塵也，彼相亦即塵相，塵尚無有，識從何發？此破因空生。若生於空，既非塵相，又非見根。若非見根，則無能辨之性，自然不能知於明、暗、色、空，是離根無塵；若非塵相，則滅所緣之境，而見、聞、覺、知無處安立，是離塵無根矣！處此非相、非見二非之中，能生之空，則幾同於無。此空等同於無，何能生識耶？若說空是有，又非同於物，亦何能生識？縱然能生汝識，因非相已滅所緣之境，欲將何者為所分別耶？此破無因生。若謂無因，突然而能生出汝識者，何不於白日之中，無有明月，突然特別生出汝識，以了知明月耶？

如前所說，非和合非不和合之義。能見之根，寄託於眼睛之內；所見之塵，推為現前之境；可有形狀者，成為有相之色；不有形相者，成為無相之空；如是根、塵、色、空之中，汝可微細審詳，生識之緣，畢竟因何所出？於根、塵、色、空，既無所出，則非和合明矣！凡言和合，必體性相類，可說和合，現今識有分別屬動，見無分別屬澄。體性各異，非和非合；聞、聽、覺、知五根之性，亦復如是，均非因緣和合矣。不應說此識生緣，乃是無從因也自然而出耶？然必根、塵相對而生，則又非不和合矣！

此合前六大，會歸藏性。謂如我前來所說，此之識心，本無所從，則非和合而有。當知此了別之識，與上見、聞、覺、知之根，同是圓滿湛然，其性非從緣所生。倘不圓滿，可說從緣所生，今既圓滿，此外無法，豈有緣之可從？兼彼無情之虛空，及地、水、火、風悉皆均等，同名七大。性真圓融，皆如來藏，本無生滅者：七大一一皆性皆真，圓融無礙；既唯性唯真，七大本非七大，故皆如來藏，本無生滅，常住不動也。

此警令悟明識大即藏性。謂阿難言：汝心麤浮，麤則不細，浮則不深，無有深細之慧，不能悟明，見聞等根即藏性，不發明能了知之識性，本如來藏者：不字雙用，即不悟不發明根識，同是如來藏性。則教以應當起智觀察，六處識心。若言是同，六用差別；若言是異，元一精明；此識豈可以同異言耶？若言是空，現有了別；若言是有，全無形相；此識豈可以空有言耶？若言非同，元本一體；若言非異，六處用殊；則知此識，又不可以非同非異言矣？若言非空，離於根塵，元無所有；若言非有，現能分別，諸塵境界；則知此識，又未可以空有言矣？若能悟明識大，同異非同異，空有非空有，則可悟明前之見大，與今之識大，非和合非不和合矣！

此結顯識大之體。以識體即是如來藏體，由阿難無有真智，不悟真理，故曰：汝元不知如來藏性之中，性具之識，即是妙明真知；本覺之明，即是性真之識。妙覺湛然，遍周法界者：前云清淨本然，約本元自性說；今此識大，體即妙本明覺，湛然凝然，故直稱妙覺湛然；體中元具遍周法界之用，此亦但理具，而非事造。理具者，全體具足大用，渾涵未發；雖然未發，其本有之力用，毫無欠闕，如火柴具足火之力用，雖然未發，具有焚燒林野之用。事造者，將此火柴一擦，一星之火，便可燎原。

此明大用無限。此科無隨心應量之文者，以心即識心，量亦識量，不復自隨自應，故不列焉。但言識性，能含能吐，含即包藏義，吐即出生義。此即稱性所起之大用，藏性能含裹十方，無際虛空，何況空中，所有世界眾生耶？藏性能顯現十方，無盡虛空，以及空中所有一切諸法，識亦如是。故相宗云，「萬法唯識」也。

循有漏、無漏二種之業，發現六凡有情世間，三乘正覺世間，染淨之識。凡、外無有正知正見之眾，迷惑為因緣和合而生，及自然不和合而生。此等皆妄想識心，分別計度，但有戲論之言說，都無真實之了義耳。

《正脈》問：「此經首先正破識心，如七處曲搜，三迷決了，名義皆妄，畢竟無體；乃至顯見文中，又復旁兼相形而破，未嘗少假寬容。何後於十八界，即已許為如來藏心，妙真如性，至此愈稱其周遍法界，含吐十虛，是即性之全體，而同彼開顯見性之極量，何前乃妄之至，而後乃真之極乎？」答：「前約初心悟修須從方便，決擇真妄，捨生死根本，取涅槃妙心，則識須破盡，決定不用；後約圓解普融，無法不真，無法不如，乃至剎塵念劫，無非一真法界，何況識心，不融法界？懸示中雙具二門，此意詳盡，宜研味之。《指掌》云：識之所以為患者，要在不知是妄，良以不知是妄，必至認以為真，遂不復更求真本：因將如來藏心，日汨沒於情塵之中，從迷積迷，浩劫不返。若果知是妄，不認為真，還須會歸如來藏性，如不然者，必至全體灰泯，反將含吐十虛之妙覺明用，永沉幻果，塵劫莫升。所以妙成二智，斷分於焦芽敗種者，豈曰無故？是知前之所以正破旁破者，務令了識是妄；後之所以會相融性者，務令達妄即真，得旨忘筌，微智者不足與道也」。丑三圓彰七大即性周遍竟。併上大科辛初如來破妄顯真竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, consciousness has no single source; it arises falsely, dependent on the six pairs of sense-faculties and their objects. Look now across this whole assembly of noble ones — as your eye passes over them, roving freely, it sees them at first exactly as a mirror does, without distinguishing anything at all. Then your consciousness, one by one, picks out and names each one in turn: this is Mañjuśrī, this is Pūrṇa, this is Maudgalyāyana, this is Subhūti, this is Śāriputra. Does this act of recognizing consciousness arise from the seeing, from the appearances themselves, from empty space, or does it emerge suddenly, with no cause at all? Ānanda, if this nature of your consciousness arises from within seeing, then, without brightness, darkness, form, or emptiness — these four necessarily absent — there is, to begin with, no seeing of yours at all; and with the seeing-nature itself not even present, from what could consciousness possibly arise? If this nature of your consciousness arises from within appearance, rather than from seeing, then, without seeing brightness, without seeing darkness — brightness and darkness both unwitnessed — form and emptiness alike are absent, and appearance itself, then, is not even present — from what could consciousness possibly arise? If it arises from emptiness, being neither appearance nor seeing — if it is not seeing, it has no capacity to discern at all, and so could not, of itself, know brightness, darkness, form, or emptiness; and if it is not appearance either, then the very object it would depend on has already vanished — where, then, could seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing possibly be established at all? Caught between these two negations, emptiness itself becomes next to nothing at all — and "being," though not identical with any ordinary object, even if it could somehow give rise to your consciousness, would leave nothing left for that consciousness to discern in the first place. And if it arises with no cause at all, springing up quite suddenly, then why does no one, in the middle of a bright day, suddenly happen to perceive the moon shining as well? Examine this yet more closely, in the finest detail, scrutinizing it with full attention: seeing depends on your own eyes; appearance is projected from the object before you — what has a definite shape becomes "existing," what has none becomes "empty" — on what basis, then, does this condition that gives rise to consciousness actually come about? Consciousness is active; seeing is still and clear — neither a true mixing nor a true combining between them — and hearing, listening, sensing, and knowing are each exactly the same — and yet it would be wrong to say this condition that gives rise to consciousness simply has no source at all. If this consciousness, this discerning mind, truly comes from nowhere at all, then you should know that this capacity to discern, together with seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing, is perfectly complete and utterly still, its nature arising from nothing at all — and, together with that boundless emptiness, and earth, water, fire, and wind, is, every one, equally to be called among these same seven great elements — each, in its true nature, perfectly interfused, each the tathāgatagarbha itself, originally free of arising or ceasing. Ānanda, your mind is coarse and unsettled, and you have not awakened to the fact that seeing, hearing, and the capacity to discern which they bring to light are, at root, the tathāgatagarbha. You should observe this for yourself: is the discerning mind, present in each of these six locations, the same in each, or different in each? Empty, or existent? Neither the same nor different? Neither empty nor existent? You have simply never known that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of consciousness is bright knowing, and true consciousness is the brightness of awareness itself — wondrously aware and utterly still, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, containing and giving forth the ten directions of emptiness — how could it have any fixed location at all? The unknowing world mistakes this for a matter of causes and conditions or of spontaneity — all of it merely the discriminating, calculating mind; there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all."

Commentary: "The nature of consciousness" points ahead to the six kinds of sense-faculty and object that follow — meaning the first six consciousnesses, whose nature is exactly the capacity to discern; this nature has no single source of its own. Dependent on the six kinds of sense-faculty and object as its condition, it falsely appears, nothing but a shadow cast by its object — present when the object is present, absent when the object is absent — hence "no source." "As your eye passes over them, roving freely" means the eye moving over them in sequence, taking each one in. As the eye scans across the assembly, at the very moment faculty and object first meet, before a single thought has yet stirred, this is exactly the seeing-nature within the faculty itself, taking hold of its object in direct perception — exactly like an image appearing in a mirror, with, as yet, no distinction at all drawn between beautiful and plain, good and bad. "Then your consciousness, one by one, picks out and names each one in turn" distinguishes the character of consciousness itself.

The question is raised first; what follows refutes each possibility in turn. This refutes arising from the faculty alone: "seeing" here means the faculty itself — apart from its object, the faculty has no existence of its own; from what, then, could consciousness arise? This refutes arising from the object alone: "appearance" here means the object itself; with the object itself not even present, from what could consciousness arise? This refutes arising from emptiness alone: if it arose from emptiness, being neither the object nor the faculty — if not the faculty, it would have no capacity to discern at all, and so could not know brightness, darkness, form, or emptiness, this being a faculty with no object; and if not the object, then the very condition it depends on has already vanished, leaving seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing with nowhere at all to be established, this being an object with no faculty. Caught between these two negations, neither appearance nor seeing, the emptiness that would give rise to consciousness comes to next to nothing at all. This emptiness, next to nothing, how could it give rise to consciousness? And if one insists that emptiness is, after all, something that exists, though unlike any ordinary object, how, still, could it give rise to consciousness? And even granting that it somehow could, since the object it would depend on has already vanished, what would be left for it to discern in the first place? This refutes arising with no cause at all: if consciousness could truly spring up suddenly, with no cause, then why does it not, in the middle of a bright day, with no moon in the sky at all, suddenly and specially give rise to a consciousness recognizing the moon?

This continues the same point made throughout — that these are neither a combination nor not a combination. The faculty capable of seeing rests within the eye itself; the object seen is projected as the scene before it; what has a definite shape becomes an object with form; what has none becomes empty, formless space — and within this faculty, object, form, and emptiness, examine closely and in detail on what, in the end, the condition giving rise to consciousness actually depends. Since it depends on none of these, it is clearly not a matter of combination at all. Whenever one properly speaks of "combination," the substances involved must be of a similar kind — but here, consciousness, being discriminating, belongs to activity, while seeing, being non-discriminating, belongs to stillness — their substance and nature simply differ, and so neither a true mixing nor a true combining holds between them; and hearing, listening, sensing, and knowing, the natures of the remaining five faculties, are each exactly the same — none of them a matter of causes and conditions combining. It would be wrong to say that this condition giving rise to consciousness arises with no cause at all, spontaneously; and yet, since it must always arise from faculty and object meeting each other, it is not simply "not a combination" either!

This merges the sixth great element with the tathāgatagarbha, drawing together all six that came before. As has already been shown, this discerning mind truly comes from nowhere at all, and so is not brought about through combination. One should know that this discerning consciousness, together with the sense-faculties of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing discussed above, is equally perfectly complete and utterly still, its nature arising from no condition at all — for if it were not perfectly complete, one could say it arose from some condition, but, being already perfectly complete, with nothing whatsoever outside it, what condition could it possibly arise from? Together with that same insentient emptiness, and with earth, water, fire, and wind, all equally alike — these are, every one, called the seven great elements. "Each, in its true nature, perfectly interfused, each the tathāgatagarbha itself, originally free of arising or ceasing": each and every one of the seven great elements is, in itself, nature, in itself, truth, perfectly interfused without obstruction. Being nothing but nature, nothing but truth, the seven great elements are, at root, not seven great elements at all — hence all are the tathāgatagarbha, originally free of arising or ceasing, ever-abiding and unmoved.

This warns Ānanda to awaken to the fact that the great element of consciousness is exactly the tathāgatagarbha. It says: your mind is coarse — coarse, and so not fine; unsettled, and so not deep — lacking the deep, fine wisdom needed to awaken to the fact that seeing, hearing, and the other sense-faculties are exactly the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, and failing to bring to light the fact that the very consciousness capable of discerning is, at root, the tathāgatagarbha as well. You are therefore taught to give rise to wisdom and observe directly the discerning mind present in each of these six locations. If one says "the same," its function differs across all six faculties; if one says "different," it is, at root, one single, luminous essence — so how could this consciousness possibly be described in terms of same or different at all? If one says "empty," it plainly discerns things right now; if one says "existent," it has, in itself, no shape whatsoever — so how could it be described in terms of empty or existent either? If one says "not the same," it is, at root, a single substance; if one says "not different," its function varies across the six locations — so this consciousness cannot be described as neither-the-same-nor-different either. If one says "not empty," then, apart from faculty and object, it is, at root, nothing at all; if one says "not existent," it is plainly capable of discerning every object right now — so this consciousness cannot be described as neither-empty-nor-existent either. If one can truly awaken to the fact that the great element of consciousness is beyond same and different, beyond empty and existent, then one can likewise awaken to the fact that the great element of seeing, and this great element of consciousness, are each neither a combination nor not a combination!

This concludes by revealing the substance of the great element of consciousness — since the substance of consciousness is exactly the substance of the tathāgatagarbha, and Ānanda, lacking true wisdom, has failed to awaken to this true principle: you have simply never known that, within the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, consciousness as inherently possessed in nature is exactly wondrous, bright, true knowing, and the brightness of original enlightenment is exactly consciousness in its true nature. "Wondrously aware and utterly still, pervading the whole Dharma-realm": the earlier phrase "pure and unmoved from the start" spoke in terms of the original self-nature; here, with the great element of consciousness, its very substance is exactly wondrous, original, bright awareness, utterly still and settled — within this substance is already inherently contained the function of pervading the whole Dharma-realm, though this, too, is only what is inherently possessed in principle, not yet brought about in actual fact. What is inherent in principle is the whole substance already complete with its great function, folded within, not yet manifest — yet, though not yet manifest, its own inherent power and function lack nothing at all, exactly as a matchstick already possesses within it the full power and function of fire, though not yet manifest, already possessing the capacity to burn down an entire forest. What is brought about in fact is this same matchstick, struck once — a single spark, and the whole plain can be set ablaze.

This shows the great function to be without limit. This section alone lacks the usual phrase "appearing according to mind and capacity," since here mind is itself the discerning mind, and capacity is itself the discerning capacity — it could hardly follow and respond to itself, hence the phrase is simply omitted. It says only that the nature of consciousness can both contain and give forth — "contain" meaning to enclose and hold, "give forth" meaning to bring into being. This is exactly the great function arising in accordance with this very nature: the tathāgatagarbha's own nature can enclose the boundless emptiness of the ten directions — how much more, then, every world and every being within that emptiness? The tathāgatagarbha's own nature can likewise manifest the boundless emptiness of the ten directions, and every phenomenon whatsoever within that emptiness — and consciousness is exactly the same. Hence the Dharma-characteristics school's own saying: "the ten thousand phenomena are consciousness alone."

According with the two kinds of karma, tainted and untainted, the defiled and pure consciousness of the six ordinary destinies, and of the three vehicles' own awakened world, comes to appear. Ordinary people and non-Buddhist schools, lacking correct knowledge and correct view, are deluded into thinking it arises through the combination of causes and conditions, or arises spontaneously without combination — all of it nothing but the deluded, discriminating, calculating consciousness-mind; there is only the empty argument of talk, with no true, complete meaning in it at all.

The Zhèngmài commentary poses a question: "This sūtra, from its very opening, directly refutes the discerning mind — the seven-location search wound its way through every possibility, the threefold confusion was decisively resolved, both name and meaning shown entirely false, with, in the end, no substance to it at all; and even in the passages displaying seeing, consciousness was again refuted alongside, compared against seeing and found wanting, without the slightest concession ever granted it. Why, then, at the eighteen elements, was it already granted to be the tathāgatagarbha mind, the wondrous, true nature of suchness — and why, here, is it praised all the more, as pervading the whole Dharma-realm, containing and giving forth the ten directions, the very whole substance of this same nature, equal to the utmost measure already granted to the revealed seeing-nature itself? How could it be utterly false before, and utterly true, to the fullest degree, now?" Answer: "The earlier passages speak in terms of a beginner's awakening and cultivation, which must proceed by way of skillful means — deciding clearly between true and false, discarding the root of birth and death, and taking hold of the wondrous mind of nirvāṇa — and so consciousness had to be refuted through and through, resolutely set aside, unused. The later passages speak in terms of the perfect understanding that interfuses everything without exception — no phenomenon that is not true, no phenomenon that is not suchness, down to every world-mote and every thought-instant and every kalpa, nothing but the one true Dharma-realm — how, then, could the discerning mind alone fail to be interfused with the Dharma-realm as well? The sūtra's own preliminary outline already holds both approaches together at once — reflect carefully on this, and the point becomes clear.

"The Zhǐzhǎng commentary says: the very reason consciousness becomes a source of harm lies precisely in not knowing it is false — for not knowing it is false, one inevitably comes to take it for true, and so never again seeks the true root beneath it; and so the tathāgatagarbha mind sinks, day after day, into the dust of ordinary feeling, delusion piling upon delusion, through kalpa after kalpa, never to return. But if one truly knows it is false, and does not take it for true, one must then still turn back and harmonize it into the tathāgatagarbha's own nature — for otherwise, one falls into total extinction, and the very wondrous, enlightened, bright function that contains and gives forth the ten directions sinks forever into an illusory, dead result, never to rise again through kalpa after kalpa of dust. This is exactly why those who reach only the wisdom that ends in a fixed destiny, never turning toward the greater path, are likened to scorched sprouts and rotten seeds — is this without reason? One should therefore understand: the earlier passages, refuting consciousness directly and refuting it obliquely alike, aim only to make its falseness fully understood; the later passages, harmonizing appearance and interfusing it with nature, aim only to make it understood that this very falseness is exactly truth — grasping the point and letting go of the means used to reach it. Those of shallow wisdom are simply not equal to walking this path."

"Fully manifesting the seven great elements as the pervading nature" is complete. Together with the sections above, this completes the greater division: "the Tathāgata refutes falseness and reveals truth."

Reading note: This fascicle closes an enormous span of the sūtra — everything from the seven-location search for the mind in fascicle 2 through the ten displays of seeing, the two kinds of false seeing, the four categories, and now the seven great elements — all of it one continuous division Yuanying's outline calls "the Tathāgata refutes falseness and reveals truth" (辛初如來破妄顯真). The closing exchange, quoting the Zhèngmài and Zhǐzhǎng commentaries on why consciousness is refuted so mercilessly at the sūtra's opening and welcomed in so fully here at its close, is Yuanying's own summation of the whole division's method: falseness must first be seen through completely before it can be harmonized back into truth — skip the first step, and what looks like generosity toward the discerning mind becomes, instead, a trap. Fascicle 9 turns to the sūtra's next major division, opening with King Prasenajit's own testimony to the unchanging nature amid old age and impermanence.
