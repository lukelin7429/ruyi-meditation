Ten Displays of Seeing, Part Two

Fascicle 4 completes the sūtra's ten displays of seeing: seeing has nowhere it can be returned to, is never mixed together with the objects it perceives, is unobstructed by distance or walls, and cannot be divided from the world it sees. This fascicle closes Yuanying's own first bound volume of the commentary.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 4 complete Source: bfnn.org / NTU Buddhist Digital Library Covers: 卯五–卯八 — displays five through eight of the ten displays of seeing (nowhere to return, not mixed with objects, unobstructed, undivided)

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 3 covered the first four of the ten displays of seeing. Fascicle 4 completes the next four: that seeing has nowhere it can be sent back to, unlike the body and its surroundings, which all trace back to some external cause; that seeing, though it takes in countless objects, never becomes mixed together with any of them; that seeing is not confined or enlarged by walls, distance, or any physical boundary; and that seeing, examined closely enough, cannot even be pointed to as separate from what it sees — Mañjuśrī's own resolution of "is this" and "is not this" through the image of the second moon. This fascicle closes precisely where Yuanying's own first bound volume of the commentary ends.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. Much of this fascicle's sūtra text has already been translated in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 2 — "The Eight Things That Return," "Is Seeing a Thing?," "Does Seeing Shrink to Fit a Room?," and "Mañjuśrī's Question, and the Second Moon" — and that exact English is reused here. A substantial amount of surrounding text that the bare-sūtra project's excerpts abridge with ellipses is translated fresh in this page.

Fifth Display: Seeing Has Nowhere to Return To 卯五 顯見無還

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難承佛悲救深誨，垂泣叉手而白佛言：「我雖承佛如是妙音，悟妙明心元所圓滿常住心地，而我悟佛現說法音，現以緣心，允所瞻仰；徒獲此心，未敢認為本元心地。願佛哀愍宣示圓音，拔我疑根歸無上道！」

〖講〗上科如來盡心吐露，極顯真心，可惜阿難未能領悟，而反起疑，故向佛求決取捨。悲救深誨者：承佛悲憐愍念，救拔性顛倒苦，深加訓誨，而與正遍知樂，感傷真心不失，顛倒受淪，故致垂泣矣！「我雖承佛如是妙音」：悟妙明心，即指領悟上三科所顯見性，阿難前求發妙明心，佛向阿難眼中指出，顯見性即是妙明心，阿難領悟不動、不滅、不失之見性。「元所圓滿」：包括虛空曰圓，周遍萬法曰滿；「常」指不滅科中，真常不滅之義；「住」指不動科中，安住不動之義。此述聞法雖悟本心，下乃歸功意識。

「而我悟佛現說法音，現以緣心，允所瞻仰」：悟佛法音，是猶但領其文，未諳其旨。阿難以聞解之功，全歸重於聽法緣心，緣心即第六意識，緣慮分別之心，而能聞法領悟，有大功能。允所瞻仰者：謂此緣心，是誠我所瞻依仰慕，而不能捨者，若捨此心，憑誰聞法領悟耶？「徒獲此心，未敢認為本元心地」：眾生日用施為，一一無非承其恩力，咸皆迷而不知，故歸功於緣心，阿難亦復如是。倘若認此，則必捨彼，卻後將何承領佛法？所以躊躇莫決。觀此阿難則真妄雙迷：既是真妄雙迷，何得稱悟？直至三卷末，獲本妙心，常住不滅，方敢認取此心，方是真悟。

「疑根」者：謂致疑之端，乃疑自、疑人、疑法，此疑法也。此疑根之於心，非大雄大力之世尊，莫能拔之。求拔疑根，令到不疑之地，庶可歸無上道；無上道即不生不滅之果覺，須識不生滅之因心，庶可圓成矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Ānanda, receiving the Buddha's own compassionate rescue and profound teaching, wept and, hands clasped, said to the Buddha: "Though I have received this wondrous teaching from the Buddha and understand that the wondrously bright mind is originally the complete and ever-abiding ground of mind, still, my very understanding of the Buddha's present teaching depends on a conditioned, grasping mind, which is what I now rely on to look up in reverence. I have merely obtained this conditioned mind — I do not yet dare recognize it as my own original ground of mind. I beg the Buddha, in compassion, to proclaim the complete teaching and pull out the root of my doubt, so that I may return to the unsurpassed path."

Commentary: Having just poured out his whole heart, thoroughly revealing the true mind, the Buddha finds that Ānanda, unable to grasp it, gives rise instead to further doubt, and so turns back to the Buddha, asking to have the matter settled once and for all — what to take up, what to discard. "Compassionate rescue and profound teaching" describes receiving the Buddha's own pity and concern, being rescued from the suffering of upside-down-ness, deeply instructed, and granted the joy of right and universal knowing — struck by the thought that his own true nature was never truly lost, yet he had suffered as though upside-down all this time, hence these tears. "Though I have received this wondrous teaching, and understand the wondrously bright mind" refers to having grasped what the previous three displays revealed — the nature of seeing itself. Ānanda had earlier asked to have this wondrously bright mind opened up, and the Buddha, pointing directly within Ānanda's own eyes, revealed that the nature of seeing is exactly this wondrously bright mind; Ānanda has now understood the unmoving, unceasing, never-lost nature of this seeing. "Originally the complete and ever-abiding ground" — "complete" refers to the display of "never lost," encompassing all of space and pervading every phenomenon; "ever-abiding" refers to the display of "does not perish," the sense of genuine, constant imperishability; "abiding" refers to the display of "does not move," the sense of settled, unmoving stability. This much describes genuine insight upon hearing the teaching — what follows shows him crediting this insight, mistakenly, to his own discriminating consciousness instead.

"Yet my very understanding of the Buddha's present teaching depends on a conditioned, grasping mind, which is what I now rely on to look up in reverence": understanding the Buddha's teaching in this sense means merely grasping its words, without yet fully absorbing its underlying intent. Ānanda credits his own understanding entirely to the conditioned, grasping mind he used to listen — this conditioned mind is exactly the sixth consciousness, the mind that conditions upon and discriminates its objects, and it does indeed have the real capacity to hear the teaching and gain understanding from it. "Which is what I now rely on to look up in reverence" means: this conditioned mind is genuinely what he looks up to and depends upon, unable to set it aside — for if he set this mind aside, who would be left to hear the teaching and understand it at all? "I have merely obtained this conditioned mind — I do not yet dare recognize it as my own original ground of mind": every sentient being's daily activity relies, without exception, on this same [true mind's own] power, yet all remain deluded about it, unaware — and Ānanda credits this same power, mistakenly, to his conditioned mind instead, exactly as other beings do. Were he to recognize the true mind now, he would have to set this conditioned mind aside — but then how could he go on receiving the Buddha's teaching at all? Hence his hesitation, unable to decide. Seen this way, Ānanda remains doubly deluded, about both the true and the false at once: how, then, could this be called genuine awakening at all? Only at the very end of fascicle 3 — the sūtra's own fascicle, not this commentary's — where the original, wondrous mind is finally grasped as ever-abiding and imperishable, will he dare to take hold of this mind at last; only then is this genuine awakening.

"The root of doubt" refers to the very source from which doubt springs — doubt about oneself, doubt about another person, or doubt about the teaching itself; here it is doubt about the teaching. Such a root of doubt, once lodged in the mind, can be pulled out by no one but a World-Honored One of the greatest heroic strength. Ānanda seeks to have this root of doubt pulled out so that he may reach a state free of doubt entirely, and so return to the unsurpassed path — the unsurpassed path being the fruit of unborn, unceasing enlightenment itself, which can only be perfected once the unborn, unceasing causal mind is first recognized.

〖經〗佛告阿難：汝等尚以緣心聽法，此法亦緣，非得法性。如人以手指月示人，彼人因指，當應看月。若復觀指，以為月體，此人豈惟亡失月輪，亦亡其指，何以故？以所標指，為明月故。豈惟亡指，亦復不識明之與暗。何以故？即以指體，為月明性，明暗二性，無所了故，汝亦如是。

〖講〗如來力為破顯，破者破妄緣心也，顯者顯真見性也。汝等尚以緣心聽法者：以能緣之心，聽我所說法音，則此法亦成所緣之塵，非得法性真理。法音但是能詮，真理方是所詮，聽法能悟真理，方不負我所說，故下喻說，聽法自應觀心。如人以手指月示人：指喻佛之聲教，月喻聽教人之心，彼聽教人，因教自當觀心，猶因指自當看月也。若聽教悟心，則因指見月矣！若復觀指以為月體：此喻執教迷心。此人豈惟亡失月輪不能見，亦亡其所謂指也。何以故？即以所標之指，認為明月故。法合當云：若復執教為心，此聽教人，豈惟不達真心，亦且不知教意，即以所緣聲教，為真心故。

明暗二字，喻中易知，法中難解。喻中不但亡指，並不識明暗，即以指體之暗性，認為月光之明性，自是明暗二性雙迷，無所了知故。法中教以聲、名、句、文為體，無覺照之用，合暗喻；心以靈知不昧為性，有覺照之用，合明喻；學人不解依教觀心，但認聲教為真心，豈獨迷心，亦迷其教，又不但迷教，亦復不達有覺照、無覺照之用。謂汝以緣心聽法，則我所說之法，亦成緣塵，汝則迷失法性，何異執指為月、不識明暗之人？故曰：「汝亦如是」。

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "You are still using a conditioned, grasping mind to listen to the Dharma, and so this Dharma too becomes merely another condition — you have not grasped the true nature of the teaching. It is like a man who points his finger at the moon to show it to someone else: that person ought to look, by means of the finger, at the moon itself. But if instead he looks at the finger and takes it to be the moon's own body, then he has lost not only the moon but the finger as well. Why? Because he has taken the very finger used to point with, for the bright moon itself. Not only has he lost the finger — he has also failed to distinguish brightness from darkness at all. Why? Because he has taken the finger's own dark substance for the moon's own bright nature, confused about both brightness and darkness together, with no understanding of either at all. It is the same with you."

Commentary: The Tathāgata now works with full effort to refute the false and reveal the true — refuting the false, conditioned mind, and revealing the true nature of seeing. "You are still using a conditioned, grasping mind to listen to the Dharma": listening with the mind that conditions upon its objects means that even this very teaching becomes just one more object conditioned upon, and the true nature of the teaching itself is never grasped. The spoken teaching is only the means of expression; the true principle it points to is what is actually to be understood — hearing the teaching in a way that awakens to that true principle is the only way to do justice to what the Buddha has taught, which is exactly why the following analogy insists that hearing the teaching ought to lead one to observe the mind itself.

"It is like a man who points his finger at the moon to show it to someone else": the finger stands for the Buddha's own spoken teaching; the moon stands for the listener's own mind. The listener, upon receiving the teaching, ought to observe their own mind, just as, upon being shown a pointing finger, one ought to look at the moon itself. Hearing the teaching and awakening to the mind is exactly like using the finger to find the moon. "But if instead he looks at the finger and takes it to be the moon's own body": this illustrates clinging to the teaching itself while remaining deluded about the mind. Such a person has lost not only the moon, unable to see it, but has lost even the finger itself, properly understood. Why? Because he has mistaken the very finger used to point, for the bright moon it was pointing toward. Applied to the matter at hand: clinging to the teaching as though it were the mind itself means such a listener not only fails to reach the true mind, but fails even to grasp the teaching's own intent — because he has mistaken the spoken teaching he conditions upon for the true mind itself.

Brightness and darkness are easy enough to follow within the analogy, but harder to unpack in the underlying matter itself. Within the analogy: not only losing the finger, but failing even to distinguish brightness from darkness, means mistaking the finger's own dark substance for the moon's bright nature — doubly confused about both brightness and darkness, with no real understanding of either. Applied to the matter: the teaching itself, made up of sound, name, phrase, and written word, has no illuminating function of its own — this corresponds to darkness; the mind, whose very nature is luminous and unobscured awareness, has real illuminating function — this corresponds to brightness. A student who does not understand how to use the teaching to observe the mind, but instead mistakes the spoken teaching itself for the true mind, is not only deluded about the mind but deluded about the teaching as well — and, further, fails to grasp the very distinction between what has illuminating function and what does not. This is exactly why the Buddha says: since you listen to the Dharma with the conditioned mind, even what I teach becomes merely another condition, and you thereby lose the very nature of the teaching — no different from someone who mistakes the finger for the moon, unable to distinguish brightness from darkness at all. Hence: "It is the same with you."

〖經〗若以分別，我說法音，為汝心者，此心自應，離分別音，有分別性；譬如有客，寄宿旅亭，暫止便去，終不常住，而掌亭人，都無所去，名為亭主。此亦如是：若真汝心，則無所去，云何離聲，無分別性？斯則豈惟聲分別心；分別我容，離諸色相，無分別性。如是乃至，分別都無，非色非空，拘舍離等，昧為冥諦，離諸法緣，無分別性。

〖講〗此正拔不捨緣心之疑根。謂汝雖不以所分別聲教為心，若以能分別法音為汝真心者，此心自應離卻所分別法音，有能分別自性，方許為真。此喻能緣心離塵無性：譬如行客寄宿旅亭，不過暫時止住便去，終不常住，而掌管亭人是為亭主，則無所去。能緣心不住，緣境之時，暫緣便去，終不久緣，故喻如客；若真是汝心，則如亭主常住，並無所去；云何離卻所分別聲，無有能分別之自性？此破意識緣聲之心也。

斯則豈獨緣聲分別之心，離聲無性；即能分別我三十二相之容貌，亦是相有則生，相無則滅，離諸所分別之色相，亦無能分別之自性。如是二字，指上緣聲色二塵，離塵無分別性；乃至二字，超略中間香、味、觸塵，并法處所攝半分生塵，而分別之性，亦復都無，此是六識不緣六塵境界，五俱意亦皆不行，唯留獨頭意識，緣法處半分滅塵，因法塵有生滅之分故。非色非空：即內守幽閒，法處滅塵境界，已離六塵麤相，故非色；猶有寂靜細境，故非空。參禪之士，到此境界，難免被他所誤。

拘舍離等，此云牛舍，乃末伽黎母名，即拘舍離子，六師之一，等餘外道。昧為冥諦者：昧即迷昧，不知此境非真，執為冥初主諦。智論云：「外道通力，能觀八萬劫，八萬劫外，冥然不知」，謂為冥初；從此覺知初立，故名主諦，亦云世性。離諸法緣，無分別性者：《正脈》云：「縱使心之分別都無，亦但離於麤分別耳，微細流注，固所未覺；縱使境之色空都盡，亦但離於麤境耳，滅塵影事，固不能離。」

Sūtra: "If you take the mind that discriminates my spoken teaching to be your own mind, then this mind ought to have its own nature of discrimination apart from the sounds it discriminates. It is like a traveler who lodges for a night at a wayside inn — he stays a while and then departs, never remaining permanently; whereas the innkeeper, who has nowhere else to go, is rightly called the host of the inn. It is the same here: if this were truly your own mind, it would have nowhere to go at all — so why does it have no nature of discrimination apart from sound? And this holds not only for the mind that discriminates sound: the mind that discriminates my own countenance likewise has no nature of discrimination apart from the visible forms it discriminates. And so it goes, on through every kind of discrimination whatsoever, down to that state where all discrimination seems gone entirely — neither form nor emptiness — which Kapila and others like him mistake for the 'obscure principle': even this has no nature of discrimination apart from the very phenomena it conditions upon."

Commentary: This directly pulls out the root of doubt behind Ānanda's inability to let go of the conditioned mind. The Buddha's point: even granting that Ānanda does not take the discriminated teaching itself for mind, if he instead takes the very capacity to discriminate the spoken teaching for his true mind, then this mind ought to retain its own capacity for discrimination even apart from the teaching discriminated — only then could it be accepted as genuinely real. This illustrates that the conditioning mind, apart from its object, has no nature of its own: it is like a traveling guest lodging at a wayside inn, staying only briefly before departing, never remaining permanently, while the innkeeper who manages the inn, having nowhere else to go, is rightly called its host. The conditioning mind does not remain fixed — when it conditions upon some object, it does so only briefly before moving on, never dwelling there for long, hence the analogy of a guest; if it were truly your own mind, it would remain fixed like the innkeeper, the host, with nowhere else to go at all — so why, then, does it have no capacity for discrimination apart from the very sound it discriminates? This refutes the discriminating mind's conditioning upon sound specifically.

And this holds not only for the mind discriminating sound alone, apart from which it has no nature: even the mind capable of discriminating the Buddha's own thirty-two marks arises when the mark is present and ceases when it is absent, having, apart from the visible form it discriminates, no capacity for discrimination of its own either. "And so it goes" refers back to this same point regarding sound and form, that apart from these objects there is no nature of discrimination; "on through" skips over the intervening senses of smell, taste, and touch, together with the portion of mental objects classified as arising phenomena, whose capacity for discrimination is likewise, in every case, entirely absent — this describes the sixth consciousness no longer conditioning upon any of the six sense-objects, the five sense-accompanying instances of consciousness no longer functioning at all, leaving only the isolated discriminating consciousness conditioning upon the remaining, ceasing portion of mental objects, since mental objects alone carry this quality of arising and ceasing. "Neither form nor emptiness" describes the state of inwardly preserving a quiet, secluded stillness — the domain of ceasing mental objects, already free of the six sense-objects' coarser marks, hence "not form"; yet still retaining a subtle, still domain of its own, hence "not emptiness" either. Meditators who reach this state can easily be misled by it, mistaking it for something final.

"Kapila and others like him" — Kapila means "cow-shed," the name of Maskarin's own mother, that is, the son of Kāśyapa, one of the six [heterodox] teachers, together with the various other non-Buddhist schools. "Mistake for the obscure principle" means: confused, not recognizing that this state is not truly real, they cling to it instead as the primordial, governing principle of reality itself. The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom says: "Non-Buddhist practitioners, through their own spiritual powers, can observe as far back as eighty thousand kalpas; beyond eighty thousand kalpas, all is dark and unknown to them" — hence they call this the "primordial" state; and since discernment is said to first arise from this point, it is called the "governing principle," also known as "the nature of the world." As for "even this has no nature of discrimination apart from the very phenomena it conditions upon," the Correct Meridian commentary observes: "Even granting that all discrimination of the mind seems entirely gone, this is only freedom from its coarser forms of discrimination — the subtle, ceaseless undercurrent still flows on, quite unnoticed; even granting that both form and emptiness, as objects, seem entirely exhausted, this is only freedom from coarser objects — the shadow-traces of what has ceased are by no means truly gone."

〖經〗則汝心性，各有所還，云何為主？阿難言：若我心性，各有所還；則如來說，妙明元本也心，云何無還？惟垂哀愍，為我宣說。佛告阿難：且汝見我，見精明元，此見雖非妙精明心，如第二月，非是月影。汝應諦聽！今當示汝，無所還地：

〖講〗此心性，非圓妙明心、寶明妙性，即緣塵分別之心性，亦即上文所謂昏擾擾相以為心性，乃隨塵生滅，各有所還。分別聲者，從聲塵來，還之於聲；乃至分別冥諦者，從冥諦來，還之冥諦。如人影相似，從何人來，還隨何人而去，有來有去，但是暫住之客，不是常住主人，故曰：「云何為主」。

阿難聞說緣心有還，而求示無還也。還者歸還，如世間之物，從誰借來，還之於誰；若是自己之物，則無可還。佛欲示無還之旨，先明見性切近真心，且就阿難日用之見，分明指示，故曰：且汝現前見我之時，此見即是八識精明之體，元者本也；故出其名曰「見精明元」，即二根本中真本，識精元明是也。體即第八識見分，映在六根門頭：在眼曰見精，在耳曰聞精，在鼻曰嗅精，在舌曰嘗精，在身曰覺精，在意曰知精，本具精明之體，而有了境之用；但體受妄熏，而有二種顛倒，見妄未除，精明尚欠於妙，故曰：「雖非妙精明心」。而喻中隨即揚其切近於真，如捏目所見之第二月，雖非真月，而與真月原無別體，但多一捏而已，放手即真；非同水中月影，與真月有虛實之殊，天淵之隔。以第一月喻純真無妄之妙精明心，第二月喻見精明元，水中月影喻緣塵分別之識心。佛欲令人捨彼妄識，取此見精，為本修因也。

問：「阿難求索真心，佛何不指與純真無妄之心，而乃指此帶妄之見精耶？」答：「純真之心，唯佛獨證，等覺菩薩，猶有一分無明未破，真尚未純，而況位居凡、小，離此憑何指示乎？譬如指礦說金，求金之人，若捨於礦，豈有真金可求？十番正示，二見翻顯，如銷礦成金，其金一純，則光明煥發矣！」「汝應諦聽！今當示汝，無所還地」：即本元妙明心地，前阿難所云「悟妙明心，元所圓滿，常住心地」，既圓滿，則無來無去，常住不動，安有所還？是為無所還地。

Sūtra: "So it is that your mind's own nature has, in every case, somewhere it returns to — how, then, could it be called the host?" Ānanda said: "If my own mind's nature has, in every case, somewhere it returns to, then how can the Tathāgata's own wondrously bright, original mind have nowhere to return to at all? I beg only that you, in compassion, explain this for me." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Consider, for now, your own seeing of me — this seeing is the very essence of the eighth consciousness's own luminous clarity. Though this seeing is not yet the wondrous, essential, luminous mind itself, it is like the second moon seen when pressing on the eye — not a mere watery reflection of the moon, but the true moon itself, with one further trace of falseness added. You should listen closely — I will now show you the very ground that has nowhere to return to at all:"

Commentary: This "mind's own nature" is not the perfectly wondrous, bright mind, nor the precious, bright, wondrous nature — it is exactly the discriminating mind that conditions upon dust, exactly what was earlier called the "confused, agitated appearance mistaken for one's own nature of mind," which arises and ceases along with its objects and, in every case, has somewhere it returns to. What discriminates sound came from sound-dust, and returns to sound; and so on, up to what discriminates the "obscure principle," which came from that same obscure principle, and returns to it. It is exactly like a person's shadow: wherever it comes from following that person, it likewise returns, following that same person, when they leave. Having both a coming and a going, it is merely a temporary guest, never the ever-abiding host — hence, "how, then, could it be called the host?"

Hearing that the conditioned mind has somewhere it returns to, Ānanda now asks to be shown what has nowhere to return to at all. "Return" means giving something back — as when a worldly object, borrowed from someone, is returned to that same person; but if a thing is genuinely one's own, there is nothing to return at all. Wishing to reveal this teaching of "nowhere to return," the Buddha first makes clear that the nature of seeing lies extremely close to the true mind, pointing it out plainly through Ānanda's own everyday act of seeing: "Consider now, at this very moment when you see me — this seeing is exactly the essential, luminous substance of the eighth consciousness." "Essence" means the very root — hence he names it "the essence of seeing's own luminous root," exactly the true root among the sūtra's two roots, the essential consciousness, originally bright. Its substance is the seeing-portion of the eighth consciousness itself, reflected at the very gateway of each of the six sense-faculties: in the eye it is called the essence of seeing; in the ear, the essence of hearing; in the nose, the essence of smelling; on the tongue, the essence of tasting; in the body, the essence of sensing; in the mind, the essence of knowing — each inherently possessing this same essential, luminous substance, with the function of comprehending its object. But because this substance has long been permeated by falseness, it carries two kinds of distorted, false seeing; since this false seeing has not yet been removed, its essential luminosity still falls short of being fully "wondrous" — hence, "though this seeing is not yet the wondrous, essential, luminous mind itself." Yet the analogy that follows immediately affirms how close it lies to the truth: like the second moon seen when pressing on the eye — though not the true moon itself, it shares, in substance, no real difference from the true moon at all, carrying only the single added falseness of the pressing finger; release the finger, and it is the true moon entirely — quite unlike a mere reflection of the moon in water, which differs from the true moon as emptiness differs from substance, as far apart as heaven from earth. The first moon here stands for the purely true, undefiled, wondrous, essential, luminous mind; the second moon stands for the essence of seeing's own luminous root; the moon's reflection in water stands for the discriminating consciousness that conditions upon dust. The Buddha wishes to have people set aside this false consciousness and take up instead this essence of seeing, as the very cause and ground of their own cultivation.

One might ask: "Since Ānanda is seeking the true mind, why does the Buddha not point directly to the purely true, undefiled mind itself, rather than to this essence of seeing, which still carries a trace of falseness?" The answer: "The purely true mind is realized by buddhas alone; even bodhisattvas of equal enlightenment still have a portion of ignorance not yet broken through, their truth not yet entirely pure — how much less could it be pointed to directly for those still at the level of ordinary beings or the lesser vehicles? It is like pointing at ore and calling it gold: if one seeking gold discarded the ore entirely, where would any true gold be found at all? The ten displays now being shown directly, together with the later teaching on the two kinds of false seeing, are exactly like smelting ore down into pure gold — once that gold is purified, its own brilliance shines forth on its own." "You should listen closely — I will now show you the very ground that has nowhere to return to at all" refers to the original, wondrously bright ground of mind itself — exactly what Ānanda earlier called "the complete and ever-abiding ground of mind." Being already complete, it has no coming and no going, ever-abiding and unmoving — how, then, could it have anywhere to return to at all? This is exactly the ground that has nowhere to return to.

〖經〗阿難！此大講堂，洞開東方，日輪昇天，則有明曜；中夜黑月，雲霧晦瞑，則復昏暗；戶牖之隙，則復見通；牆宇之間，則復觀壅；分別之處，則復見緣；頑虛之中，遍是空性；鬱垺之象，則紆昏塵；澄霽斂氛，又觀清淨。阿難！汝咸看此諸變化相，吾今各還本所因處。云何本因？阿難！此諸變化，明還日輪；何以故？無日不明，明因屬日，是故還日。暗還黑月，通還戶牖，壅還牆宇，緣還分別，頑虛還空，鬱垺還塵，清明還霽。則諸世間，一切所有，不出斯類。汝見八種見精明性，當欲誰還？何以故？若還於明，則不明時，無復見暗；雖明暗等，種種差別，見無差別。諸可還者，自然非汝；不汝還者，非汝而誰？則知汝心，本妙明淨，汝自迷悶，喪本受淪，於生死中，常被漂溺，是故如來，名可憐愍！

〖講〗此欲示無還之見，先列可還之相。八相共四對：明、暗；通、壅；同、異；清、濁也，各有體相，如日輪是體，明曜是相，乃至澄霽是體，清淨是相。此大講堂，喻妙明元心，不動不變之體，為大總相法門；八相，喻心體隨緣，現一切別相。總言之，隨迷、悟二緣，而現染、淨諸相。明喻智慧善能照了，暗喻無明昏於長夜，通喻六根通達觸處洞然，壅喻二執障蔽頭頭是礙，差別之緣喻善惡，頑虛之狀喻無記，鬱垺喻昏迷之性，清淨喻澂湛之心；正顯種種幻化，皆生當人妙明元心，此心不拒諸相發揮，能為諸相所依，若講堂然，人人皆有此大講堂，試深思之！

此正顯見性無還，乃為真主人。謂汝能見八種之相，此見之精，即是妙明真性，無來無去，不生不滅，當欲從誰以俱還乎？設若見精還於明相，已隨明相而去，則不明時，應無復見暗？汝今不然，雖明去暗來，通去塞來，異滅同生，濁滅清生，所見之相，任從種種千差萬別，而能見之性，湛然盈滿，如明鏡當臺，有物斯鑑，明來見明，乃至清來見清，昭然不昧，凝然不動，無差無別，無往無還。

八相從緣而有，還之於緣，緣有則有，緣無則無；六識因塵而有，還之於塵，塵生則生，塵滅則滅，皆有可還，自然非汝常住不遷之真性，汝當決定棄捨，而不須執吝矣。今此見精明性，明來見明，暗來見暗，不由汝以可還者，自然屬汝自己，非汝之真性，而是誰耶？汝當決定認取，而不可猶豫也。

則知汝之見性真心，雖隨緣而恆不變。本妙、本明、本淨三義，本來現具根中，即生滅門中之本覺心。眾生日用，不離這箇，不為諸相所遷，非同緣心之離塵無體，故曰「本妙」；不為諸相所蔽，非同緣心之昏擾為性，故曰「本明」；不為諸相所染，非同緣心之分別愛著，故曰「本淨」。迷悶者：執吝緣心，無智自解，具足本末不覺，由是喪失本妙明淨之覺心，此非失似失也。法身流轉於五道，枉受淪溺，於生死苦海之中，常被漂流沉溺，如懷珠乞丐，珠本不失，枉受貧窮，為可憐愍者。

Sūtra: "Ānanda, this great lecture hall opens directly to the east: when the sun rises in the sky, there is bright radiance; in the black-moon depth of night, with clouds and fog, there is dimness and dark; through the gaps of doors and windows there is a further sense of openness; between walls there is a further sense of obstruction; where discrimination occurs, there is a further sense of what it takes as its object; in inert emptiness, there is, throughout, the nature of empty space; where dust rises thick, there is a haze of turbid dust; and when the air clears after rain, there is, once more, a sense of purity. Ānanda, you observe all these changing appearances — I will now return each of them to the place that is properly its own cause. What are these proper causes? Ānanda, of all these changes: brightness returns to the sun. Why? Without the sun there is no brightness, and since brightness has its cause in the sun, it returns to the sun. Darkness returns to the black moon. Openness returns to doors and windows. Obstruction returns to walls. The sense of an object returns to discrimination. Inert emptiness returns to empty space. Turbid haze returns to dust. Clarity returns to the clearing sky. Everything that exists in the whole world falls into one of these categories without exception.

"Now, as for the nature of your seeing that perceives these eight kinds of things — to whom should it be returned? Why do I ask this? Because if it could be returned to brightness, then, at a time when there is no brightness, it could no longer see darkness at all. Although brightness, darkness, and all these various things differ from one another, the seeing itself has no such differences. Whatever can be returned to something else is, by its very nature, not you; but that which cannot be returned anywhere — if that is not you, then what is it? You should therefore know that your own mind is, at its root, wondrously bright and pure; it is you yourself who, through delusion and confusion, have lost this original nature and undergone the wheel of rebirth, forever adrift and sinking in birth and death — and this is why the Tathāgata calls you an object of pity."

Commentary: Wishing to reveal the seeing that has nowhere to return, the Buddha first lists what does have somewhere to return. The eight phenomena form four pairs: bright and dark, open and blocked, same and different, clear and turbid — each with its own substance and appearance, as the sun's disc is the substance and brightness its appearance, down to a clearing sky as the substance and purity its appearance. This great lecture hall stands for the wondrously bright, original mind's own unmoving, unchanging substance — the great, all-inclusive gateway underlying every phenomenon; the eight phenomena stand for that same mind's substance manifesting every particular appearance according to conditions. Broadly speaking, following the two conditions of delusion and awakening, it manifests every defiled and pure appearance in turn. Brightness stands for wisdom, skillfully illuminating; darkness for ignorance, dim through the long night; openness for the sense-faculties' own unimpeded reach, touching everything directly; obstruction for the two kinds of clinging obscuring understanding, blocking at every turn; the sense of differing objects stands for good and evil; inert emptiness for moral neutrality; turbid haze for a confused, deluded nature; purity for a settled, clear mind — all of this reveals plainly that every kind of illusory transformation arises from this same wondrously bright, original mind belonging to each person; this mind never refuses to let any of these appearances unfold within it, and can serve as the very ground every appearance depends upon, exactly like this lecture hall itself. Every single person already possesses this same great lecture hall — reflect deeply on this!

This directly reveals that the nature of seeing has nowhere to return, and is thus the true host. The point: you are able to see all eight of these kinds of phenomena — this very essence of seeing is exactly the wondrously bright true nature, coming from nowhere, going nowhere, unborn, unceasing — to whom, then, should it be returned, along with all the rest? Suppose the essence of seeing were returned to brightness, having followed brightness away — then, at a time of no brightness, it ought no longer to be able to see darkness at all. Yet this is not what happens: though brightness departs and darkness arrives, though openness departs and obstruction arrives, though difference ceases and sameness arises, though turbidity ceases and clarity arises, however many thousands of variations appear among the things seen, the very nature capable of seeing them remains full and constant throughout — exactly like a bright mirror set upon its stand, reflecting whatever is placed before it: when brightness comes, it sees brightness; when clarity comes, it sees clarity; clear and unobscured, settled and unmoving, without variation, without difference, going nowhere, returning nowhere at all.

The eight phenomena arise in dependence on conditions, and so return to those same conditions — present when the condition is present, absent when it is absent; the six kinds of consciousness likewise arise in dependence on their objects, and so return to those same objects — arising when the object arises, ceasing when the object ceases. All of these have somewhere to return, and are therefore, by nature, not your own ever-abiding, unchanging true nature — you should resolve, once and for all, to set them aside, without clinging to them any further. But this very essence of seeing's own luminous nature — seeing brightness when brightness comes, seeing darkness when darkness comes — is not something you can send back anywhere; it belongs, by its very nature, to you yourself. If this is not your true nature, then what is? You should resolve, once and for all, to take hold of it, without any further hesitation.

You should therefore know: your own nature of seeing, this true mind, though it follows conditions, remains eternally unchanging. The three qualities — originally wondrous, originally bright, originally pure — are, from the very start, already present within your own sense-faculties; this is exactly the original awakening within the very gateway of birth and death. Sentient beings, in their daily activity, never depart from this very thing. Not shifted by any of these appearances, unlike the conditioned mind, which has no substance apart from its object — hence "originally wondrous." Not obscured by any of these appearances, unlike the conditioned mind, whose very nature is confusion and agitation — hence "originally bright." Not defiled by any of these appearances, unlike the conditioned mind, which discriminates and clings — hence "originally pure." "Confusion" describes clinging to the conditioned mind, lacking the wisdom to understand for oneself, caught in every degree of non-awakening from root to branch, and so losing this originally wondrous, bright, pure, awakened mind — a loss that is not truly a loss, but only seems like one. The Dharma-body, turning through the five destinies, undergoes suffering and sinking needlessly, forever adrift and submerged in the bitter sea of birth and death — exactly like a beggar who carries a priceless jewel within his own robe, the jewel itself never actually lost, yet suffering destitution for nothing — and this is why the Tathāgata calls such a person an object of pity.

Reading note: Yuanying's aside on why the Buddha points to the "essence of seeing" rather than the fully purified true mind directly is worth lingering on: pointing at raw ore and calling it gold is not dishonest, since the gold is genuinely there, waiting to be smelted out — but pointing at nothing at all would leave the seeker with no starting material whatsoever. This image — ore refined into gold, not gold manufactured from nothing — recurs across the commentary as Yuanying's preferred way of explaining why the sūtra spends ten separate displays working with something that "still carries a trace of falseness" rather than simply asserting the pure truth outright.

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Ānanda said: "Though I now recognize that this nature of seeing has nowhere it can be returned to, how can I know that it is my own true nature?" The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Let me ask you this: you yourself have not yet attained the undefiled, pure eye, and it is only through the Buddha's own spiritual power that you are able to see as far as the first dhyāna heaven, without obstruction; while Aniruddha sees the whole of Jambudvīpa as clearly as an āmalaka fruit held in the palm of his hand. Bodhisattvas, in turn, see hundreds and thousands of world-systems; the Tathāgatas of the ten directions see every pure land throughout the whole of space, numerous as dust motes, with nothing left unseen at all; while ordinary sentient beings' own penetrating sight extends no further than an inch or so.

"Ānanda, consider now: you and I, looking upon the palaces where the Four Heavenly Kings dwell, and taking in everything between here and there — every creature of water, land, and air — however dim or bright, however varied in form, none of it is anything but an object in front of us, distinguished and obstructed by its own nature. You should, in all of this, distinguish what belongs to yourself from what belongs to another. Let me now have you sort out, within all that is seen, which is the very substance of your own seeing, and which are merely objects seen."

Commentary: Though Ānanda has now heard that the essence of seeing has nowhere to return, he has not yet grasped this with full certainty, and so still asks how, exactly, he can know it to be his own true nature, rather than belonging to some object. Since the nature of seeing appears so vividly, so brilliantly present right before his eyes, Ānanda still suspects that seeing and its objects are mixed together, unable to decide, and does not yet dare recognize seeing as mind itself — hence the Buddha now reveals his own nature by clearly distinguishing objects from seeing.

This first lists the various kinds of seeing capable of perceiving: that of the śrāvaka, of the bodhisattva, of the buddha, and of the ordinary being. "Aniruddha," in full Aniruddha, means "without poverty," also "unceasing" — because, in a past life, he offered a bowl of coarse millet to a pratyekabuddha, his resulting blessing never ceased, and across ninety-one kalpas, whether reborn among gods or humans, he never once suffered poverty. Aniruddha was the Buddha's own cousin; because he was fond of sleeping during the teachings, the Buddha once rebuked him: "Why do you sleep so, tsk, tsk! Snails and clams sleep for a thousand years at a stretch, never once hearing the Buddha's own name." Struck with shame, Aniruddha resolved on great diligence, and went seven days without sleep, losing his eyesight as a result. The Buddha, taking pity, taught him the vajra samādhi of joyful, illuminating vision, through which he attained a kind of heavenly eye [not dependent on the physical organ] and realized perfect penetration, later saying of himself: "Not depending on the eye at all, I see the ten directions, penetratingly clear, as though viewing a fruit held in my own palm." These first two examples are the seeing of śrāvakas.

"Bodhisattvas see hundreds and thousands of world-systems": a bodhisattva of the first ground sees a hundred world-systems, of the second ground a thousand, and so on up to the tenth ground, which sees countless world-systems without limit. The Tathāgatas of the ten directions see without any limit at all, hence "throughout the whole of space, numerous as dust motes" — an image for a number beyond counting. As for "ordinary sentient beings' own penetrating sight extends no further than an inch or so," this can be understood three ways: first, contrasting the lofty with the lowly — an ordinary being's own clearest sight, set against that of buddhas and sages, extends no further than an inch or so by comparison; second, encompassing every kind of living creature — even the smallest, most limited crawling things have some narrow range of sight of their own; third, describing obstruction and loss of function — unable to see through even a sheet of paper, let alone a wooden board. This passage on seeing, taken as a whole, covers the five eyes across all ten dharma-realms: the ordinary physical eye, Aniruddha's heavenly eye, Ānanda's own wisdom-eye [attained only by relying on the Buddha's power], the bodhisattva's Dharma-eye, and the Tathāgata's own buddha-eye.

This next passage lists only what lies directly within Ānanda's own present view. The palace where the Four Heavenly Kings dwell sits at the waist of Mount Sumeru, forty-two thousand yojanas above the earth: the Eastern King, Guardian of the Nation, dwells upon a golden terrace; the Southern King, Increase and Growth, upon a lapis-lazuli terrace; the Western King, Broad Eyes, upon a silver terrace; and the Northern King, Vaiśravaṇa, upon a crystal terrace, level with the sun and moon. "Taking in everything between here and there" means: beneath the Trāyastriṃśa heaven and above the earth, surveying all around — among sentient creatures, those of water, land, and air; among insentient things, mountains, rivers, the great earth, and empty space, all their various conditions — some dim and dark, some clear and bright, every kind of form differing one from another, all of it nothing but objects distinguished in front of the eyes, obstructed and confined by their own separate natures in every direction.

Objects and seeing were never truly mixed together to begin with; Ānanda only suspected they were, and so the Buddha now has him sort this out for himself, so that his own direct verification will dispel the doubt entirely. The point: within all that you see, distinguish what belongs to yourself from what belongs to another — "yourself" meaning the nature of seeing, "another" meaning the objects seen. Let me now have you sort out, within everything presently before you, carefully distinguishing which is the very substance of your own seeing, and which are the objects you see — once seeing and its objects are clearly, unmistakably separated, you will naturally come to know your own true nature.

〖經〗阿難！極汝見源，從日月宮，是物非汝；至七金山，周遍諦觀，雖種種光，亦物非汝；漸漸更觀，雲騰鳥飛，風動塵起，樹木山川，草芥人畜，咸皆物非汝。阿難！是諸近遠，諸有物性，雖復差殊，同汝見精，清淨所矚，則諸物類，自有差別，見性無殊，此精妙明，誠汝見性。

〖講〗上文佛令自揀物見，惜阿難無此智力，不能辨別分明，故佛此下更以四番展轉發明也。極者盡也，見源即見性，如云盡汝見性能力，從日月宮，此最上所見是物，而非汝見性也；又至七金山，此山圍繞須彌山之外，一重香水海，一重金山，共有七重香水海，七重金山，其體皆金——一、雙持，二、持軸，三、擔木，四、善見，五、馬耳，六、象鼻，七、魚觜。周遍諦觀者：用目循歷，諦實觀察，雖有種種光明，亦是物而非汝見性也。漸漸更觀，自高而卑，自近而遠，乃見雲之騰，鳥之飛，風之動，塵之起，此皆空中所有；以至地上樹木也，山川也，草芥菜類也，人畜也，亦咸是物，而非汝見性也。

此正明見不是物，見性已經擇出，阿難不解，故此承上重示云：阿難是汝所見，若近若遠，若高若低，若大若小，所有物性，雖復種種狀態，差別殊異，列在目前，同是汝之見精，一道清淨，不起分別，圓明照了，矚見也。則諸物類指所見一切物類，自有千差萬別，而汝見性畢竟無殊。此精妙明者，即此見精，本妙本明，與物不雜故妙，遍見諸物故明，即此妙明真心，誠汝自己見性；物見分明，云何於諸物中，而不能揀擇耶？此正酬上文云何得知是我真性之問也。

Sūtra: "Ānanda, trace your own seeing to its very source: from the palaces of the sun and moon — these are objects, not you; to the seven surrounding rings of golden mountains — carefully observing them all, however much light they give off, these too are objects, not you; observing further still, the clouds rising, the birds in flight, the wind stirring, the dust rising, the trees, mountains, and rivers, the blades of grass, the people and animals — all of these, too, are objects, and not you. Ānanda, all these things, near and far, however much they differ from one another, are alike observed by the pure essence of your seeing. The various kinds of things have their own distinctions; the nature of seeing has none. This essential, wondrous brightness is truly your nature of seeing."

Commentary: Having just had Ānanda sort out objects from seeing on his own, the Buddha, aware that Ānanda's own capacity falls short of the task, now unfolds the point further still, through four successive rounds of demonstration. "Trace to its very source" means to trace seeing all the way back — from the palaces of the sun and moon, the very highest thing seen, which is an object, not your own nature of seeing; on to the seven surrounding rings of golden mountains encircling Mount Sumeru — seven rings of fragrant, sweet-water ocean alternating with seven rings of golden mountains, each entirely golden in substance, named in turn Double-Holding, Axle-Holding, Wood-Bearing, Well-Seen, Horse-Ear, Elephant-Trunk, and Fish-Mouth. "Carefully observing them all" means surveying with the eyes, examining truly and thoroughly — however much light these mountains give off, they too are objects, and not your own nature of seeing. Observing further still, from high to low, from near to far: the clouds rising, the birds in flight, the wind stirring, the dust rising — all these belong to the sky above; and further still, on the ground: trees, mountains and rivers, grasses and plants of every kind, people and animals — all of these, too, are objects, and not your own nature of seeing.

This directly demonstrates that seeing is not any of these objects. The nature of seeing has already been sorted out this way, yet Ānanda still does not grasp it, so the Buddha explains once more: Ānanda, everything you see, whether near or far, high or low, large or small — every one of these objects, whatever various states or distinct differences they display before your eyes, is alike perceived by this same essence of your own seeing, a single, unbroken purity, giving rise to no discrimination of its own, perfectly bright, illuminating and beholding all of it at once. "The various kinds of things" refers to everything seen, which naturally differs in a thousand, ten thousand ways from one another, while your own nature of seeing remains, in the end, entirely without difference. "This essential, wondrous brightness" refers to this very essence of seeing itself — inherently wondrous, inherently bright: wondrous because it is never mixed together with any object; bright because it perceives every object equally — this very wondrously bright true mind is truly your own nature of seeing. Objects and seeing being now so clearly distinguished, why, then, can you not sort them out among all these things? This directly answers Ānanda's earlier question — how he might know this to be his own true nature.

〖經〗若見是物，則汝亦可，見吾之見。若同見者，名為見吾，吾不見時，何不見吾，不見之處？若見不見，自然非彼，不見之相；若不見吾，不見之地，自然非物，云何非汝？又則汝今，見物之時，汝既見物，物亦見汝，體性紛雜，則汝與我，並諸世間，不成安立。阿難！若汝見時，是汝非我；見性周遍，非汝而誰？云何自疑，汝之真性，性汝不真，取我求實？

〖講〗此數節文，承上反辨見不是物。上科已將物、見分析明白，物有差別，見性無殊，則見當然不是物。反辨云：若汝執言，見即是物，即當有相，見若有相，則汝見有相，我見亦當有相，則汝亦可見吾之見，畢竟作何形相？前二句防謬，下三句難破。若謂汝我同見萬物之時，我見在於物上，汝見此物時，即名為見吾之見者；則吾收視不見物時，汝亦當見吾不見之處，現今吾不見物時，汝何以不能見吾不見之體，在於何處？既不能見吾不見之處，而說能見吾見物之見者，亦不足信也。

此文欲求義理明白，須知彼字即指物言，我見為能見，彼物為所見。若能見我收視不見之處，自然是我能不見之見體，自然非彼所不見之物相。若不見吾不見之地，則吾之見自然非物。何以故？若是物，收視不見時，必有所在之處，當然令汝可見，既不能見，當然非物。吾之見既非是物，汝之見亦應非物，故末句結曰：云何非汝真性？

此反辨物不是見。當承上云：見若是物者，物亦當是見，則汝現今見物之時，汝既見物，而物亦當見汝矣。體性紛雜者：則無情之物體，與有情之見性，紛然雜亂，而不可辨也。果然如是，則汝與我，並諸世間眾生有情之界，不成安立矣！何以故？物亦能見，則有情無情雜亂，故不成安立。

此轉正意。言若汝見物見我之時，一定是汝見而非我見，汝我有情之與有情，尚不混濫，豈有情之與無情，而至雜亂耶？見性周遍者：謂汝現前，觀四天王宮，以及水陸空行，皆屬汝之見性周遍，此見總不屬於物，亦不屬於我，非汝真性，而是誰耶？物見分明，本不混雜，為何不敢認取？此承上物、見分明不雜。見性是在汝，而不屬於物，云何汝自疑汝之真性。性汝不真者：此性本來是汝，本有家珍，反不敢認以為真，而取我言，以求證實。

Sūtra: "If seeing were itself a thing, then you ought to be able to see my seeing too. If, when we both see the same thing, that counts as your seeing me, then at a time when I am not seeing, why do you not see the place where I am not seeing? And if you could see my not-seeing, that would obviously no longer be the mark of not-seeing at all. But if you cannot see the place where I am not seeing, then it is obviously not a thing — so how could it not be you? And further: at the very time you see some object, since you see the object, does the object also see you? If so, then subject and object would be hopelessly tangled together, and neither you, nor I, nor the world at all could stand established as they are. Ānanda, when you see, it is you who see, not I; the nature of seeing is all-pervading — if it is not you, then who is it? Why do you doubt your own true nature, treating your own nature as not real, and instead seeking something from me to count as real?"

Commentary: This entire passage continues the reversed reasoning showing that seeing is not itself an object. The previous section has already made plain that objects differ endlessly from one another while the nature of seeing has no such differences at all — from which it naturally follows that seeing cannot itself be an object. The reversed argument: if you insist that seeing is itself an object, then it ought to have some visible characteristic; and if your own seeing has some characteristic, then my seeing ought to have one too — meaning you ought to be able to see my own seeing directly. What form, then, would it take? The first two lines here guard against a false answer; the next three press the refutation home. If you claim that, when we both see the same object at once, my seeing rests upon that object and your seeing of that same object counts as "seeing my seeing," then, at a time when I turn my attention inward and see nothing at all, you ought likewise to see the very place where I am not seeing. Yet, right now, when I am not seeing anything, why can you not see where my own not-seeing actually is? Since you cannot see the place of my not-seeing, your earlier claim to see my seeing of an object cannot be trusted either.

To make the reasoning fully clear: the word "that" here refers to the object itself; my own seeing is the subject that sees, that object is what is seen. If you could see the very place where I turn inward and do not see, this would naturally be my own capacity for not-seeing — and naturally not the mark of any object that is itself unseen. But if you cannot see the place of my not-seeing, then my seeing itself is naturally not any kind of object at all. Why? Because if it were an object, then, at the moment I turned my attention inward and ceased seeing, it would have to occupy some definite location, which you certainly ought to be able to see — and since you cannot see it, it is clearly not an object. And if my own seeing is not an object, your seeing cannot be an object either — hence the closing line: how, then, could this not be your own true nature?

This reverses the argument the other way, showing that objects are not seeing either. Continuing the same line: if seeing were an object, then an object ought likewise to be capable of seeing — so that, at the very moment you see some object, since you see it, that object ought equally to see you in return. "Hopelessly tangled together" means: the insentient substance of the object and the sentient nature of seeing become impossibly confused, impossible to distinguish at all. Were this truly so, then you, I, and every sentient being throughout the whole sentient world could no longer be established as we are! Why? Because if objects too could see, then sentient and insentient would be hopelessly jumbled together, and nothing could stand established at all.

This turns back to the correct meaning. The point: when you see an object, or see me, it is definitely you who sees, and not I who sees — even you and I, both sentient beings, are never confused with one another this way; how much less could a sentient being and an insentient object ever become confused together? "The nature of seeing is all-pervading" means: your present observation of the palace of the Four Heavenly Kings, together with every creature of water, land, and air, all belongs to this same all-pervading nature of your own seeing — this seeing belongs neither to any object nor to me — if it is not your own true nature, then whose could it possibly be? Objects and seeing being so clearly, unmistakably distinct, why do you still not dare to take hold of it? This continues directly from the previous demonstration that objects and seeing are clear and unmixed. The nature of seeing resides in you, and belongs to no object at all — why, then, do you still doubt your own true nature? "Treating your own nature as not real" means: this nature has always, truly, been yours — your own inherited family treasure — and yet you still do not dare recognize it as real, seeking instead to have my own words serve as proof of it.

Seventh Display: Seeing Is Unobstructed 卯七 顯見無礙

This display's sūtra passages have already been translated in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 2 ("Does Seeing Shrink to Fit a Room?"); that exact English is reused below, interleaved with Yuanying's commentary.

〖經〗阿難白佛言：「世尊！若此見性必我非餘，我與如來觀四天王勝藏寶殿，居日月宮，此見周圓遍娑婆國；退歸精舍只見伽藍，清心戶堂但瞻簷廡。……此見如是，其體本來周遍一界，今在室中唯滿一室，為復此見縮大為小？為當牆宇夾令斷絕？」

〖講〗此科因當機聞說見性周遍一語，遂生疑惑，以為真性既是周遍，應當一定周遍，自應無礙，何乃動被物礙？故白佛言：若此見性本來周遍，必定是我真性，而非餘物，則應當一定無礙，今何不然？此述見性大小不定。四天王勝藏寶殿，此殿乃殊勝藏寶所成，故稱勝藏寶殿。日月宮亦在須彌山半，與天王宮殿齊。此見周圓遍娑婆國者：自二宮遠觀，此見周遍圓滿，遍娑婆國。退歸精舍祗見伽藍者：從四天退歸精舍，祗見伽藍，此云眾園；清心戶堂，謂講堂，聞法能清淨心地故。安處於戶內堂中，不能遠見，但瞻垂簷與廊廡，先大今小。

此陳疑以請。意謂：見性既云周遍，自當一定常遍，自在無礙，此見今何如是大小不定？其體本來周遍一界，今在室中，忽然成小，唯滿一室？此妄情計度，求決於佛。承上遍界之見，今滿一室，為復此見因室所局，縮一界廣大之見而為一室狹小之見耶？為當牆宇夾斷周遍整個之見，而成內外之二耶？

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if this nature of seeing is truly me and nothing else, then, when the Tathāgata and I look upon the jeweled palace of the Four Heavenly Kings, dwelling amid the palaces of sun and moon, this seeing fills the whole Sahā world-system; yet when we withdraw to the monastery, I see only the monastery grounds, and, with a settled mind indoors, I see only as far as the eaves. ... This seeing's own substance is, from the start, all-pervading throughout a whole world — so why, now, indoors, does it fill only this one room? Has this seeing contracted from large to small? Or have the walls pinched it and cut it off?"

Commentary: Having just heard that the nature of seeing is all-pervading, Ānanda now grows doubtful: if his true nature really is all-pervading, it ought to remain so at all times, entirely unobstructed — so why does it keep being blocked by objects? Hence he asks: if this all-pervading nature of seeing is genuinely his true nature, and nothing else, then it ought to be, without exception, free of any obstruction — so why does this not seem to be the case? This recounts seeing's own apparent inconsistency in size. The jeweled palace of the Four Heavenly Kings, formed of the most excellent treasures, is called for this reason "the jeweled palace of surpassing treasure." The palaces of sun and moon likewise sit at the mid-slope of Mount Sumeru, level with the heavenly kings' own palace. "This seeing fills the whole Sahā world-system" describes viewing from these two distant palaces, where seeing extends fully throughout the whole Sahā world. "Withdrawing to the monastery, I see only the monastery grounds" describes returning from the Four Heavenly Kings' realm to the monastery, seeing only its own grounds — "monastery" here meaning literally "the multitude's garden." "With a settled mind indoors" refers to the lecture hall, where hearing the teaching purifies the mind's own ground. Settled within the hall, unable to see far, one sees only as far as the eaves and the surrounding corridor — large before, small now.

This states the doubt as a formal question. The point: if the nature of seeing is truly all-pervading, it ought to remain constantly so, free and unobstructed at all times — so why does this seeing now appear so inconsistent in size? Its own substance is, from the start, all-pervading throughout an entire world — so why, indoors now, has it suddenly become small, filling only this one room? This is false, calculating speculation, brought to the Buddha to be settled once and for all. Continuing from the seeing that fills a whole world, now filling only one room: has this seeing, confined by the room itself, contracted from a whole world's vast expanse down to this one small room's narrow scope? Or have the surrounding walls pinched off this once-unified, all-pervading seeing, splitting it into an inner and an outer portion?

〖經〗佛告阿難：「一切世間大小、內外諸所事業，各屬前塵，不應說言見有舒縮。譬如方器中見方空，……若如汝問『入室之時縮見令小』，仰觀日時，汝豈挽見齊於日面？若築牆宇能夾見斷，穿為小竇，寧無續迹？是義不然。一切眾生從無始來迷己為物，失於本心，為物所轉；故於是中觀大、觀小。若能轉物，則同如來身心圓明，不動道場，於一毛端遍能含受十方國土。」

〖講〗一切世間大小內外，諸凡所有事相業用，該上下方圓等類，皆屬前塵而有留礙，非關見性也。此以喻明塵大小不定，全由於塵，但得亡塵，自然無礙。今以見性譬虛空，塵相譬器皿，塵相有大小內外，而見性無舒縮斷續，其猶器有方圓，空無定相。

此反難要當機自審，以顯其謬。若如汝先所問，入室之時，縮見令小，則汝仰觀日時，豈能挽引長也見齊於日之面前耶？此乃令審觀日非舒，自知入室非縮矣。若築牆宇，能夾汝見令斷，則將牆宇穿為小竇孔穴也時，寧無接續之跡耶？此亦令審穿無續跡，自知夾無斷痕矣。而疑縮疑斷，俱非正義。

此出成礙之由，由於法執。眾生法執未亡，執心外有法，動成有礙，一切眾生總該凡、外、權、小而言，皆從無始無明住地而來，迷己真心，而為萬物，不了萬物皆己，遂失本來一體之真心，而執心外有實法。迷字即是法執，乃為成礙之由，不達萬法唯心，遂有心、物之分，非惟物不隨心轉，而心反被物礙矣。故於是中，即是於一體之中，觀大觀小，逐境遷移，動被物礙。須知物本是心，迷之為物，則能礙心，亦如冰原是水，結之成冰，則能礙水；果能返迷，自可轉物矣！

轉物之功，須憑妙智，悟圓理，破法執，悟明心外無法，法法唯心，轉萬物為自己，則知天地與我同根，萬物皆吾一體，此由事法界而悟入理法界也。則同如來者，法身如來也。法身以理為身，圓融無礙，隨舉一法，體即法身。身心圓明，不動道場：此明體自在，屬理事無礙法界。眾生迷時，妄認四大為自身相，則被四大留礙而不圓；妄認六塵緣影為自心相，則被六塵障蔽而不明。今既轉物，則身為法性身，圓照周遍，心為妙明心，靈明洞徹，萬物身心，本來一體，即身心即是法界，不動之道場矣。於一毛端，遍能含受十方國土：此明用自在，屬事事無礙法界。毛端即一毛頭上，屬正報之最小者；國土即佛剎大千世界，屬依報之最大者；毛端含國土，即以正攝依；國土在毛端，即以依入正。小大相容，無不自在，即廣狹自在無礙玄門也。

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "All the various matters of large and small, inner and outer, throughout the world, belong to what is in front of you — one should not say that seeing itself expands or contracts. It is like seeing a square shape of space inside a square container. ... If, as you ask, seeing contracts to become small on entering a room, then when you look up at the sun, do you stretch your seeing all the way out to meet the sun's very face? And if building a wall could pinch your seeing and cut it off, then boring a small hole through it should leave some trace of the seeing having been reconnected — but this is not how it works. From beginningless time, all sentient beings have mistaken themselves for things, lost their original mind, and been turned about by things — and so, within that confusion, they perceive things as large or small. But one who is able to turn things, rather than be turned by them, is the same as the Tathāgata, whose body and mind are perfectly bright, who does not move from the place of practice, and who, on the tip of a single hair, can fully contain the lands of the ten directions."

Commentary: Every worldly matter of large and small, inner and outer — every kind of activity and function, covering every category of above and below, square and round — belongs entirely to what stands in front of the eyes, obstructed by its own nature, and has nothing to do with the nature of seeing itself. This uses an analogy to show that it is the object's own size that varies, entirely because of the object itself — remove the object, and there is naturally no obstruction at all. Here the nature of seeing is likened to empty space, and the object to a container: the object has large and small, inner and outer, while the nature of seeing has no expansion or contraction, no cutting off or reconnecting — exactly as a container may be square or round, while space itself has no fixed shape at all.

This reversed challenge is meant to make Ānanda examine his own claim and see its flaw for himself. If, as you first asked, seeing contracts to become small on entering a room, then, looking up at the sun, could you possibly stretch your seeing all the way out to meet the sun's own face? This is meant to have him examine that looking at the sun involves no stretching, and so realize on his own that entering a room involves no contracting either. And if building a wall could pinch off your seeing and cut it in two, then boring a small hole through that same wall ought to leave some trace of the seeing being reconnected — does it not? This, too, is meant to have him examine that boring a hole leaves no trace of reconnection, and so realize on his own that a wall leaves no trace of any true severing either. The suspicion of contraction, and the suspicion of severing, are both, in the end, mistaken.

This reveals the true source of apparent obstruction: clinging to phenomena. So long as sentient beings have not yet abandoned this clinging to phenomena, insisting that things exist genuinely outside the mind, obstruction inevitably follows wherever they turn. Every sentient being — ordinary beings, non-Buddhists, and those of the provisional and lesser vehicles alike — comes, from beginningless ignorance, to mistake their own true mind for mere things, failing to realize that the ten thousand things are nothing but this same mind, and so losing sight of their own originally unified true mind, clinging instead to the belief that real phenomena exist outside the mind. This very delusion is exactly the clinging to phenomena, and is exactly the source of all apparent obstruction — failing to understand that the ten thousand phenomena are mind alone, one comes to divide mind from matter, so that not only do things fail to be turned by the mind, but the mind itself is instead obstructed by things. Hence, "within that confusion," meaning within what was originally one substance, one perceives things now as large, now as small, shifting along with whatever object is encountered, obstructed by things at every turn. One should understand: things were originally nothing but mind itself; deluded into taking them for external things, they become capable of obstructing the mind — exactly as ice was originally water; once frozen solid, it becomes capable of obstructing water's own flow. If one can genuinely turn this delusion around, one can naturally turn things as well.

The power to turn things depends entirely on wondrous wisdom: awakening to the perfect principle, breaking through the clinging to phenomena, understanding clearly that nothing exists outside the mind, that every single phenomenon is mind alone, and so turning the ten thousand things into one's own self — knowing then that heaven and earth share the very same root as oneself, that the ten thousand things and oneself are a single substance. This is awakening, by way of the phenomenal Dharma-realm, into the noumenal Dharma-realm. "The same as the Tathāgata" refers to the Dharma-body Tathāgata: the Dharma-body takes principle itself as its very body, perfectly interfused and unobstructed, so that whatever single phenomenon is raised up, its very substance is the Dharma-body itself. "Body and mind perfectly bright, not moving from the place of practice" describes the substance's own freedom, belonging to the Dharma-realm of unobstructed principle and phenomena together. When sentient beings are deluded, they falsely take the four elements for their own bodily form, and so are obstructed by the four elements, never whole; they falsely take the shadow-traces of the six sense-objects for their own mental form, and so are obscured by the six sense-objects, never bright. Now, having turned things around instead, the body becomes the Dharma-nature's own body, perfectly and universally illuminating; the mind becomes the wondrously bright mind, luminously penetrating everything — body, mind, and the ten thousand things all originally one and the same substance, so that body and mind themselves are the very Dharma-realm, the unmoving place of practice itself. "On the tip of a single hair, fully containing the lands of the ten directions" describes the freedom of function, belonging to the Dharma-realm where every single phenomenon interpenetrates every other without obstruction. The tip of a hair, the very smallest thing belonging to one's own direct retribution; the buddha-lands of a great-thousand world-system, the very largest thing belonging to one's surrounding retribution — the hair's tip containing the land means the direct retribution takes in the surrounding; the land residing within the hair's tip means the surrounding retribution enters the direct. Small and large mutually contain one another, each entirely free — this is exactly the gate of broad and narrow, mutually unobstructed and entirely at ease.

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！若此見精，必我妙性，今此妙性，現在我前？見必我真，我今身心，復是何物？而今身心，分別有實；彼見無別，分辨我身。若實我心，令我今見，見性實我，而身非我？何殊如來，先所難言：物能見我。惟垂大慈，開發未悟。

〖講〗此領前義，以生疑難也。前佛要當機認見為心，多與發明，見性超然獨立，身境無干，阿難遂從分別心中，變現一種昭昭靈靈光景，湛然盈滿目前，喚作見性，似與身心判而為二。阿難前疑見性與萬物混雜，此疑見性與身心各體。故白佛言：若此現前周遍萬物之見精，必定是我妙精明性者，今此妙性，現在我之眼前，則離我身矣；既然是我妙性，豈有反在身外耶？

此恐認見必遺身心。意謂湛然滿前之見，必定是我真性，則我現今之身心，當然非我，究竟復是何物，而今身心分別有實？此約分別以起計。謂現今身心為能分別，有實在作用，彼在前之見，無有分別功能，來分辨我身，我身尚不能辨，況能辨萬物耶？此正同世間凡夫情見，以有分別者為我，無分別者非我。

此約能見以起計。謂在前之見，若實在是我真心，令我現今，此身反為所見，則見性在我之前，如同外物在前；若實在是我，而今此身竟成所見，反非是我矣？此解承上，見性既已離我同物，反能見我身，即同物能見我。何殊異也如來先所難言：「物能見我。」然物能見我，佛先不許，已斥其謬，惟願再垂大慈，開導發明，令未悟者可以得悟。

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if this essence of seeing is truly my own wondrous nature, then this wondrous nature ought to be present right in front of me now. If seeing is truly my own real self, then what, exactly, is this present body and mind of mine? And yet this body and mind genuinely do have the power of discrimination, while that seeing in front of me has no such power to distinguish my own body at all. But if it is truly my own mind, causing me to see as I do now, and this nature of seeing is truly myself, then this body would not be myself at all! How would this differ from what the Tathāgata himself earlier objected to — that an object could see me? I beg only that you, in your great compassion, enlighten what I have not yet understood."

Commentary: This picks up the previous teaching and gives rise to a further doubt. The Buddha has been working hard to have Ānanda recognize seeing as mind, showing at length that the nature of seeing stands independently, entirely unrelated to body or surroundings — and Ānanda, still working from within his own discriminating mind, has conjured up a kind of vivid, luminous impression, filling his field of vision, calling this "the nature of seeing," as though it stood apart from body and mind as a separate thing entirely. Earlier, Ānanda suspected that seeing was mixed together with the ten thousand things; now he suspects that seeing and his own body and mind are two entirely separate substances. Hence he says to the Buddha: if this present, all-pervading essence of seeing, taking in the ten thousand things, is truly my own wondrous, essential, luminous nature, then this wondrous nature ought to be right in front of my own eyes at this very moment — which would mean it stands apart from my own body! But if it is truly my own wondrous nature, how could it possibly stand outside my own body at all?

Here Ānanda fears that recognizing seeing as himself must mean abandoning body and mind altogether. His thought: if this vivid presence, filling his field of vision, is truly his real nature, then his present body and mind must, after all, not be himself — so what, then, are they? And yet this body and mind genuinely do have the power of discrimination. This reasons from the capacity for discrimination: his present body and mind, he thinks, are what actually discriminate, possessing real function; while that seeing in front of him has no such power of discrimination at all, unable even to distinguish his own body — if it cannot even distinguish his own body, how much less could it distinguish the ten thousand things? This is exactly the same view held by ordinary worldly people: taking whatever has the power of discrimination for one's true self, and whatever lacks it for not-self.

This next line reasons from the capacity of seeing itself. His thought: if the seeing in front of him is truly his real mind, causing him to see as he does now, then this present body would instead become merely something seen — the nature of seeing standing in front of him, exactly like some external object standing there. But if that seeing is truly himself, then this present body, having become merely what is seen, would end up not being himself at all! This point continues directly from the one before: if the nature of seeing has already been set apart from himself, treated as though it were an object, and yet is somehow still capable of seeing his own body, this is exactly the same as an object being capable of seeing him — no different at all from what the Tathāgata himself had earlier objected to: "an object seeing me." Yet the Buddha had already rejected this very possibility as mistaken — so Ānanda now begs once more for the Buddha's great compassion, to open and clarify this point further, so that what remains unawakened in him may finally be awakened.

Yuanying's fascicle then contains an extended sequence — the Buddha rejecting Ānanda's premise outright, then twice testing whether seeing can be pointed to among objects (once establishing that no object can be singled out as "seeing," and once establishing that no object can be singled out as "not seeing" either) — before the whole assembly, unable to follow where the teaching is leading, falls into fear, and Mañjuśrī steps forward on their behalf. The sūtra passages for the exchange with Mañjuśrī, and the second-moon analogy that resolves it, have already been translated in the bare-sūtra project's fascicle 2 ("Pointing to What, Exactly, Is Seeing" and "Mañjuśrī's Question, and the Second Moon"); that English is reused in the final translation-table below.

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "What you have just said — that 'seeing is right in front of you' — is not actually true. If seeing were genuinely in front of you, something you genuinely see, then this essence of seeing would have to occupy some definite location — could it really have no way of being pointed to at all? Consider now: you and I are sitting together in the Jeta Grove, taking in the whole view — the groves and streams, the halls and buildings, up to the sun and moon, and, before us, the Ganges itself. Right here, in front of my lion's seat, you could raise your hand and point to any of these: what is shaded is the grove; what is bright is the sun; what obstructs is the wall; what admits passage is open space — and so on, down to the finest blade of grass or twig, however large or small, so long as it has some form, there is nothing that cannot be pointed to directly. If seeing is truly right in front of you in just this way, then you ought to be able to point directly, with your own hand, to exactly what this seeing is. Ānanda, you should know: if empty space were seeing, then, once space had already become seeing, what would be left to be called space? And if an object were seeing, then, once the object had already become seeing, what would be left to be called an object? Try, then, to pick apart every single phenomenon in the finest detail, and extract this pure, essential, wondrous origin of seeing, and point it out to me clearly, the way you would point out any of these other objects, leaving no room for doubt."

Commentary: Ānanda had just mistakenly claimed that seeing and body-and-mind were two entirely separate substances, giving rise to this doubt about seeing standing "in front of him." Rather than resolving this directly by showing that body, mind, and seeing are not two separate substances, the Buddha instead refutes it by showing that all phenomena share a single substance — for two reasons. First, this is easier to argue from: if seeing genuinely stood in front of Ānanda, it ought to be pointed to as clearly as any of the ten thousand things — yet, searching among all these things, there is truly nothing that can be pointed to, either as "seeing" or as "not seeing," so that both possibilities are exhausted, with no such separate mark to be found anywhere at all, and seeing and the ten thousand things naturally turn out to be a single substance. Second, this removes both kinds of clinging at once: body and mind are the intimate basis of clinging to a self; the ten thousand things are the visible domain of clinging to phenomena. Ānanda's real fear is that recognizing seeing as himself must mean abandoning body and mind — meaning his clinging to a self is already burning brightly, while his clinging to phenomena remains, for now, comparatively subtle. Were the Buddha simply to say that seeing, body, and mind form a single substance, Ānanda would only cling further still, treating seeing, body, and mind together as his own self, and the ten thousand things as something entirely other — so that his clinging to phenomena would remain entirely unrefuted, and both kinds of clinging would only grow stronger still.

What follows distinguishes "there being no such thing as 'is seeing'" from "there being no such thing as 'is not seeing'" — this passage uses both directions at once, taking up objects and setting them aside in turn, to single out first the absence of "is seeing." The Tathāgata's question: if this essence of seeing truly stands right in front of your own eyes, something you can genuinely look at and see, then this essence of seeing must occupy some definite location — and having a location, it must have some visible form — could it really have no way of being indicated at all? This settles, in advance, that it ought to be indicable, so that Ānanda can then be asked to point it out among the objects before him. This first uses the fact that every object can be pointed to as a general example. What follows then directs him to point out seeing itself: right now, you and I, sitting together in this Jeta Grove, surveying everything around us — nearby, the groves and flowing streams; further, the halls and buildings; higher still, up to the palaces of the sun and moon; and, in the distance, the Ganges itself, directly before us — anything within sight, right here before my lion's seat, you can raise your hand and point to clearly: what is shaded is the grove, what is bright is the sun, what obstructs is a wall, what admits passage is open space, and so on, down to the finest blade of grass or twig — however large or small, so long as it has some form, there is nothing that cannot be pointed to directly.

"You should know" signals that Ānanda must pay careful attention here — to what, exactly, counts as space, and what counts as an object. The Buddha wants Ānanda to follow the exact format he has laid out, pointing to both object and seeing at once, without destroying the object's own original character in the process. First, regarding space: if space were seeing, then, once space had already become seeing, one must still, without destroying space's own original nature, be able to say what remains, properly, "space." Then, regarding an object: if an object were seeing, then, once the object had already become seeing, one must likewise, without destroying that object's own original nature, be able to say what remains, properly, "an object." This format is like painting directly onto a wall: the wall and the painting must both remain, each in its own right, for there to be any meaningful sense in which object and seeing are each their own separate substance. If "being seeing" simply erased space and erased the object entirely, collapsing everything into a single undifferentiated thing, this would contradict the very point Ānanda is being asked to demonstrate.

The previous passage sought "is seeing" while still keeping the object intact; this passage seeks "is seeing" apart from the object entirely. Seeking it apart from the object means Ānanda must show seeing's own independent substance, standing free of every object — hence the Buddha's instruction: apply yet another round of the finest, most careful effort, picking apart every single phenomenon, and extract this very origin of seeing — this is exactly seeking "is seeing" apart from every object. "The pure, essential, wondrous origin of seeing" — "essential" means what no phenomenon can blend together with it; "clear" means what no phenomenon can obscure; "pure" means what no phenomenon can defile; "wondrous" means what no phenomenon can alter — these four qualities describe the very meaning of this origin of seeing, exactly the inherent, virtuous characteristics belonging to original awakening itself. If seeing and objects are truly each their own separate substance, then seeing's own independent substance, apart from every object, ought to be just as clearly, distinctly pointed to as any of these other objects, without the slightest room for doubt.

〖經〗阿難言：我今於此，重閣講堂，遠洎恆河，上觀日月，舉手所指，縱目所觀，指皆是物，無是見者。世尊！如佛所說，況我有漏初學聲聞，乃至菩薩亦不能於萬物象前剖出精見離一切物別有自性！佛言：如是，如是！

〖講〗此阿難答，即物無是見。謂舉手所指得到，縱目即放眼所觀見也得到，指者皆是物，不能不壞物之本相，更指出何者是見也。故曰：「無是見者」。此阿難答，離物無是見。如佛所說，即指上文佛立格云：「汝可微細，披剝萬象，析出精明，淨妙見元，指陳示我。」況我乃是有漏初學人，那能離物指出是見，即羅漢、辟支佛，亦所不能，乃至菩薩，亦不能於萬象前，剖析出精見，離一切物，別有見之自性也。是離物亦無是見之可指矣！此雙用即離，單遣是見也。

此佛言印證。重言如是者，以阿難所答，即物無是見可指，離物亦無是見可指，二者皆是，故言：「如是如是」。良以見性，量括十方，體周萬法，其與萬法非即非離，惟其非即也，故能靈光獨耀，迥脫根塵，身界無干，生死不繫；惟其非離也，故能塵剎普融，萬物一體，用彌法界，色心不二。

Sūtra: Ānanda said: "Now, in this many-storied lecture hall, as far as the Ganges, up to the sun and moon — everywhere I raise my hand to point, everywhere my eyes freely roam, whatever I point to is an object; there is nothing there that is 'the seeing' itself. World-Honored One, exactly as the Buddha has said — how much less could I, a beginning śrāvaka still with outflows, or even a bodhisattva, manage to extract, from in front of the ten thousand things, a pure seeing that has its own nature apart from every one of those things!" The Buddha said: "Just so, just so!"

Commentary: This is Ānanda's answer showing that, keeping the object intact, there is nothing that "is seeing." His point: wherever he raises his hand to point, wherever his eyes freely roam, whatever he points to is an object — he cannot point out anything that "is seeing" without destroying that object's own original character — hence, "there is nothing there that is 'the seeing' itself." This next passage is Ānanda's answer showing that, apart from the object entirely, there is likewise nothing that "is seeing." "Exactly as the Buddha has said" refers back to the Buddha's own instruction: "Try, then, to pick apart every single phenomenon in the finest detail, and extract this pure, essential, wondrous origin of seeing, and point it out to me clearly." How much less, then, could I — a beginner still subject to outflows — manage to point out "seeing" apart from every object? Even an arhat or a pratyekabuddha could not do this; even a bodhisattva could not, standing before the ten thousand things, extract a pure seeing possessing its own separate nature apart from every one of them. So, apart from the object entirely, there is likewise nothing that can be pointed to as "seeing." This uses both approaches together, keeping the object and setting it aside in turn, to single out the absence of anything that "is seeing."

This is the Buddha's own confirmation. He repeats "just so" twice because Ānanda's answer has shown, in both directions — keeping the object intact, and setting it aside entirely — that nothing can be pointed to as "seeing," both equally correct, hence the double confirmation, "just so, just so." This is because the nature of seeing, in its full measure, encompasses the ten directions, its very substance pervading every phenomenon — and its relation to every phenomenon is neither identity nor separation. Precisely because it is not identical with phenomena, it can shine with its own independent, spiritual light, entirely free of both sense-faculty and sense-object, unrelated to body or world, untouched by birth and death. And precisely because it is not separate from phenomena either, it can perfectly interfuse with every land, forming a single substance with the ten thousand things, its function filling the whole Dharma-realm, form and mind never truly two.

Yuanying then walks through the parallel demonstration that nothing can be pointed to as "not seeing" either — the assembly, hearing both possibilities exhausted at once, falls into confusion and fear, and the Buddha reassures them before Mañjuśrī steps forward to ask, on the whole assembly's behalf, what the essence of seeing and its objects actually, originally are, "in between" is and is not.

〖經〗文殊師利法王子……而白佛言：「世尊！此諸大眾，不悟如來發明二種精見色空、是非是義。世尊！若此前緣，色空等象，若是見者，應有所指，若非見者，應無所矚。而今不知是義所歸，故有驚怖！非是疇昔，善根輕尟，惟願如來大慈發明，此諸物象與此見精元是何物，於其中間無是非是。」

〖講〗前是經家敘述代問之儀。是時文殊師利法王子，愍諸四眾，在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足，合掌恭敬。不悟者：舉其疑端；二種即是義與非是義。精見，即八識精明之見分；色空，總該諸物即相分。文殊意謂：大眾所以惶悚失守者，祗因不悟如來兩番審問，特為發明，精見即見精之與色空，是義與非是義兩種。是義，即無非見之義，以無非曰是故；非是義，即無是見之義。

此述眾疑。若此色空等象若是見者，應當有所指，云何無是見之可指？若非見者，應當無所矚，云何又能見色見空？而今不知是義所歸者：即無是見、無非見二義，所歸趣也。故有驚怖！非是疇昔，善根輕尟音鮮，惟願如來，大慈發明，此諸物象與此見精，元是何物？於中即無是見，又無非是見。佛若發明，則大眾既知是義所歸，而驚怖自息矣。

Sūtra: Mañjuśrī, Dharma-Prince, ... said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, this whole assembly has not understood what the Tathāgata means by asking whether the two things, the essence of seeing and form-and-emptiness, 'are' this or 'are not' this. World-Honored One, if these objects before us — form and emptiness and all such appearances — are themselves seeing, they ought to be something that can be pointed to directly; but if they are not seeing, then nothing at all ought to be perceived. Yet now no one knows where this teaching is truly leading, and so this fear has arisen — not because this assembly's roots of goodness are especially shallow or slight, as in some earlier age, but because of this very uncertainty. I beg only that the Tathāgata, in his great compassion, make clear what these various objects and this essence of seeing actually, originally are, in the space between 'is' and 'is not.'"

Commentary: This opening portion is the compiler's own account of how Mañjuśrī came to ask this question on the assembly's behalf. At that time, Mañjuśrī, Dharma-Prince, taking pity on the fourfold assembly, rose from his seat, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and, with palms joined in reverence, spoke. "Not understood" names the very root of the assembly's confusion; the "two things" are "is" and "is not." "The essence of seeing" is the seeing-portion of the eighth consciousness's own luminous clarity; "form and emptiness" together encompass every object, the perceived-portion. Mañjuśrī's point: the assembly has fallen into fear and confusion only because they have not understood that the Tathāgata's two rounds of questioning were specifically meant to reveal two things — whether the essence of seeing, together with form and emptiness, "is" this, or "is not" this. "Is this" means there is nothing that is not seeing, since nothing being "not seeing" is exactly what is meant by "is"; "is not this" means there is nothing that is seeing at all.

This describes the assembly's own confusion. If form, emptiness, and every such appearance were themselves seeing, they ought to be capable of being pointed to directly — so why can nothing be pointed to as "seeing"? And if they were not seeing at all, then nothing at all ought to be perceived — so why, then, can one still see form, still see emptiness? "No one knows where this teaching is truly leading" means: no one knows the resolution behind these two possibilities — nothing that "is seeing," and nothing that "is not seeing" — hence this fear has arisen. This is not because the assembly's roots of goodness are especially thin or slight — I beg only that the Tathāgata, in great compassion, make plain what these objects and this essence of seeing actually, originally are — in which there is neither anything that "is seeing" nor anything that "is not seeing." Should the Buddha make this clear, the assembly will understand where this teaching truly leads, and their fear will resolve on its own.

〖經〗佛告文殊及諸大眾：「十方如來及大菩薩，於其自住三摩地中，見與見緣并所想相，如虛空花本無所有；此見及緣，元是菩提妙淨明體，云何於中有是非是？文殊！吾今問汝，如汝文殊，更有文殊是文殊者？為無文殊？」「如是，世尊！我真文殊，無是文殊。何以故？若有是者，則二文殊。然我今日非無文殊，於中實無是非二相。」佛言：「此見妙明與諸空塵，亦復如是。本是妙明無上菩提淨圓真心，妄為色空及與聞見，如第二月，誰為是月？又誰非月？文殊！但一月真，中間自無是月、非月。是以汝今，觀見與塵，種種發明，名為妄想，不能於中，出是非是。由是精真妙覺明性，故能令汝出指非指！」

〖講〗此佛酬答文殊之代問。十方如來已證極果之佛，併及大菩薩，有此大字一字，非但二乘絕分，即權教菩薩亦所不能，顯是佛及圓頓菩薩境界。自住三摩地中者：即是以自覺聖智，常住於首楞嚴大定也。見與見緣四句，了妄無體。見即能緣見分，見緣即所緣六塵相分，想即六識妄想，相即六根身相。佛及圓頓菩薩，了知遍計六識本空，依他根塵如幻，喻如病目所見之空華，從緣無性，本無所有，非作故無，本性無故。

此見及緣，元是菩提妙淨明體者：此達妄即真也。緣字並攝根、塵、識三，故以緣字，總該三六十八界，即身、心、世界，依正二報。上明萬象皆妄，故喻空華；此明一性元真，故曰菩提，乃三菩提中真性菩提。元是二字，與前文殊所問元是何物相照應。此能見之見精，及所緣之物象，所以無是非者，何也？元是菩提妙淨明體，不假修為，本來自妙；不用洗滌，本來自淨；不待揩磨，本來自明。妙即法身德，非有非空；淨即解脫德，處染不變；明即般若德，靈光獨耀，乃是三德秘藏之體。既是一體，迥絕是非，故反顯之曰：云何更有是見之與非是見耶？

此假文殊為喻，以明一真法界，本無是非二相。文殊喻一真之體，問云：如汝此身，本來一個真文殊，為是更有文殊，喚作是文殊者，為無有文殊耶？文殊是大智慧，一聞便悟，答曰：世尊！我本一個真文殊，此喻一真之體，於我真文殊分上，並無那個喚作是文殊者，此喻色空無是見也。何以故？若更有一喚作是文殊者，則成二文殊，而一體自不能成，意顯一體分上，更不容說是。然我今日非無文殊，於中實無是非二相者：於真實文殊體中，實無從說是，及與說非；說既叵說，安有是非二相可得耶？此喻一真無是非，真如體中，不剩一法，不少一法，圓同太虛，無欠無餘故也。

此見即能見見精，無始在纏，靈光不昧，故稱妙明。與諸空、塵，即指物象；佛言此見精與物象，但惟一體，所以無是無非，亦復如文殊一樣，但有一真文殊，無是文殊，與無文殊也。本是妙明四句，明妄元是真，於一真總喻而總合，歸真即無是非。不變隨緣曰妙，寂而常照曰明，無上菩提，即第一義諦，真性菩提，為諸法之本源，無有何法能在其上，故曰「無上」。妄為色空，及與聞見者：此明從真起妄，由最初性覺必明，妄為明覺，轉妙明而為無明，起為業識，詐現見、相二分。色、空即所現之相分，及與聞見，即所現之見分。

如第二月三句，別舉真妄二喻。如捏目所見第二月，既有二輪，是非鋒起，於中妄計，誰是真月？誰非真月？合上真心妄為色、空、見相之二分，於中是非自生。文殊但一月真，中間自無是月非月者：此喻真以一月，方出是非，但識得一月為真，則惟一體，本無二輪，中間自無是月非月之可言；自可超出妄擬之戲論。

是以汝今三句，以法各合二喻。乃謂是以上來所說法喻，研究起來，汝今觀見與塵，任從種種發明，無非妄想。觀見即見精，塵即內之身心、外之萬物。承上汝今不悟妙明真心，故觀見與塵，不了元是何物，任從汝種種發明，說是說非，總屬分別計度，名為妄想。於依他法上，起遍計執，縱饒說到驢年，亦不能於中超出是與非是。此合二月妄計是非之喻。

由是真精妙覺明性二句，由是返迷歸悟，會妄歸真，了知見之與塵，元是一真，則能超出是非之外。真精者，無妄離垢之體；妙覺明者，圓照法界之性。悟此體性，則能遠離依他起性及遍計執性，則根、塵、識三，無非圓成實性，悉無自他之別，安有是非可指耶？故能令汝，出指非指者：若悟一真法界，見、相二分俱屬空華，萬物、見性本來一體，故能令汝超出是非，豈復於萬象中，謂何者是見可指，何者非見可指耶？亦如但知一月真，則妄計全消。可見迷、悟、真、妄，惟在一念，若一念迷，則心境紛然，是非鋒起；若一念悟，則妙覺湛然，豈容是非於其間哉！此科以見性不分而論，無是無非，見真妄情自息，是四義中妙義。

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Mañjuśrī and the whole assembly: "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions and the great bodhisattvas, abiding in their own samādhi, see that seeing itself, together with its objects and all conceived appearances, are, like flowers conjured in empty space, originally without any existence at all. This seeing and its objects are, originally, the wondrous, pure, bright substance of bodhi — so how could there be any 'is this' or 'is not this' within it? Mañjuśrī, let me ask you: besides you, Mañjuśrī, is there another Mañjuśrī who 'is' Mañjuśrī — or is there no such Mañjuśrī at all?" "Just so, World-Honored One. I am the true Mañjuśrī; there is no 'Mañjuśrī who is Mañjuśrī.' Why? Because if there were such a thing, there would be two Mañjuśrīs. And yet I am not, today, without a Mañjuśrī either — there is truly, in this matter, no such pair as 'is' and 'is not.'"

The Buddha said: "This wondrous brightness of seeing, together with all the appearances of emptiness and dust, is exactly the same. It is, originally, the wondrous, unsurpassed, pure, perfect, true mind of bodhi, mistakenly taken to be form and emptiness, hearing and seeing — like a second moon: which one, then, is 'the moon,' and which one 'is not the moon'? Mañjuśrī, there is only ever one true moon; in between, there is naturally no such thing as a moon that 'is' or a moon that 'is not.' This is exactly why, no matter how you now examine seeing and its objects, however many explanations you devise, all of it is nothing but false thought — none of it can ever step outside the very question of 'is this' or 'is not this' at all. It is precisely because of this true, essential, wondrously enlightened, bright nature that you are able to move beyond both pointing and not-pointing at all!"

Commentary: The Buddha now answers Mañjuśrī's question on the assembly's own behalf. "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions" refers to buddhas who have already realized the ultimate fruit; "the great bodhisattvas" — note the word "great" here — excludes not only the two lesser vehicles but even provisional-teaching bodhisattvas, showing this to be the domain of buddhas and perfect, sudden-teaching bodhisattvas alone. "Abiding in their own samādhi" means abiding, through their own realized, sagely wisdom, constantly within the great Śūraṅgama samādhi. "Seeing itself, together with its objects" and the following lines show that falseness has no real substance at all: "seeing" is the conditioning, seeing-portion; "its objects" are the six sense-objects conditioned upon, the perceived-portion; "conceived appearances" refers to the sixth consciousness's own false thought; the "appearances" themselves refer to the physical form of the six sense-faculties. Buddhas and perfect, sudden-teaching bodhisattvas understand clearly that the universally clung-to sixth consciousness is, at root, empty, and that the dependently-arisen sense-faculties and objects are illusory — like flowers seen in empty space by a diseased eye: arising from conditions, without any nature of their own, originally without any existence at all — not made nonexistent through some later act, but nonexistent in their very nature from the start.

"This seeing and its objects are, originally, the wondrous, pure, bright substance of bodhi": this reveals falseness as, in itself, nothing other than truth. "Objects" here encompasses sense-faculty, sense-object, and consciousness together — hence this single word takes in the whole eighteen elements, that is, body, mind, and world, both direct and surrounding retribution together. The passage above showed that every phenomenon is false, likened to flowers in space; this passage shows that the single nature underlying them is, originally, true — hence "bodhi," specifically the true-nature bodhi among the three kinds of bodhi. "Originally" here echoes Mañjuśrī's own earlier question, "what are they originally?" Why is it that this seeing-essence and the objects it conditions upon have no such thing as "is" or "is not"? Because they are, originally, the wondrous, pure, bright substance of bodhi — needing no cultivation to bring it about, wondrous from the very start; needing no washing to purify it, pure from the very start; needing no polishing to brighten it, bright from the very start. "Wondrous" corresponds to the virtue of the Dharma-body, neither existent nor empty; "pure" corresponds to the virtue of liberation, unchanged even amid defilement; "bright" corresponds to the virtue of wisdom, its own spiritual light shining independently — together, the very substance of the three virtues' own hidden treasury. Being one and the same substance, utterly beyond any question of "is" or "is not," the Buddha reveals this by turning the question back: how, then, could there still be any "is seeing" or "is not seeing" to speak of at all?

Here the Buddha borrows Mañjuśrī himself as an analogy, to show that the single true Dharma-realm has, from the start, no mark of "is" or "is not" at all. Mañjuśrī stands for the substance of this single truth; the Buddha's question: as for this very body of yours, originally one true Mañjuśrī — is there some further Mañjuśrī who "is" Mañjuśrī, or is there no such Mañjuśrī at all? Mañjuśrī, being of great wisdom, understands the moment he hears it, and answers: World-Honored One, I am originally one true Mañjuśrī — this illustrates the substance of this single truth — and, with regard to my own true self as Mañjuśrī, there is no further one to be called "the Mañjuśrī who is Mañjuśrī" — this illustrates that form and emptiness have no such thing as "is seeing" either. Why? Because if there were some further one called "the Mañjuśrī who is Mañjuśrī," there would be two Mañjuśrīs, and the single substance itself could no longer stand — meaning that, within this single substance, there is no room even to speak of "is" at all. "And yet I am not, today, without a Mañjuśrī either — there is truly, in this matter, no such pair as 'is' and 'is not'" means: within the substance of the true Mañjuśrī himself, there is truly nothing to be called "is," and nothing to be called "is not" either — since neither can even be spoken of, how could there be any pair of marks, "is" and "is not," to be found there at all? This illustrates that the single truth has no "is" or "is not": within the substance of true suchness, not one phenomenon is left over, not one phenomenon is lacking, perfectly identical with the whole of empty space itself, neither deficient nor in excess.

"This seeing" refers to the very seeing-essence capable of seeing, bound within delusion since beginningless time, yet its own spiritual light never dimmed — hence called "wondrously bright." "Emptiness and dust" together refer to every object. The Buddha's point: this seeing-essence and every object are, in truth, one and the same substance, and so have neither "is" nor "is not," exactly as with Mañjuśrī — only ever one true Mañjuśrī, with no "Mañjuśrī who is Mañjuśrī," and yet not without a Mañjuśrī either. This next passage, beginning "it is, originally, wondrous and bright," shows that falseness is, originally, nothing but truth itself, gathering everything into this single truth as one unified analogy — returning to the truth means there is no longer any "is" or "is not" at all. Unchanging while according with every condition is called "wondrous"; still, and yet ever illuminating, is called "bright." "Unsurpassed bodhi" is exactly the ultimate truth, true-nature bodhi, the very source underlying every phenomenon, with nothing whatsoever able to stand above it — hence "unsurpassed." "Mistakenly taken to be form and emptiness, hearing and seeing" reveals falseness arising from truth: from the very first moment when the nature of awakening, necessarily bright of itself, mistakenly became "awareness that is bright," the wondrous brightness was turned into ignorance, giving rise to karmic consciousness, falsely manifesting the seeing-portion and the perceived-portion. Form and emptiness are exactly this manifested, perceived-portion; hearing and seeing are exactly this manifested, seeing-portion.

The three lines beginning "like a second moon" offer separate analogies for both truth and falseness. Like pressing on the eye and seeing a second moon — with two discs now appearing, questions of "is" and "is not" immediately spring up, and one falsely calculates: which, then, is the true moon, and which is not? This corresponds to the true mind falsely becoming the two portions of form, emptiness, seeing, and appearance — within which "is" and "is not" naturally arise. "Mañjuśrī, there is only ever one true moon; in between, there is naturally no such thing as a moon that 'is' or a moon that 'is not'" illustrates truth through a single moon: only once one recognizes the single moon as true does the very question of "is" or "is not" fall away entirely, since it is then recognized as a single, undivided substance, with no second disc at all, and no room left even to speak of "is" or "is not" — one is thereby naturally freed from this whole false, speculative debate.

The three lines beginning "this is exactly why" then apply this teaching, matching each analogy to its corresponding point. The meaning: given everything explained so far, through both direct teaching and analogy, however you now examine seeing and its objects, and however many explanations you devise, none of it is anything but false thought. "Examine seeing" means the seeing-essence itself; "objects" means both the inward body and mind, and the outward ten thousand things. Continuing from the point that you have not yet awakened to the wondrously bright true mind, and so, examining seeing and its objects, fail to grasp what they originally are — however many explanations you devise, declaring this "is" and that "is not," all of it remains mere discrimination and calculation, called false thought. Giving rise to universal clinging upon what is merely dependently arisen, even were you to keep explaining until the end of time, you could never step outside this very question of "is" and "is not." This corresponds to the analogy of the second moon and its false calculation of "is" and "is not."

The two lines beginning "it is precisely because of this" describe turning from delusion back to awakening, dissolving falseness and returning to truth, understanding clearly that seeing and its objects are, originally, one single truth — and so becoming able to step entirely outside the very question of "is" and "is not." "True and essential" describes the substance free of falseness, free of defilement; "wondrously enlightened and bright" describes the nature that perfectly illuminates the whole Dharma-realm. Awakening to this substance and nature, one becomes able to move far beyond both the dependently-arisen nature and the universally-clung-to nature, so that sense-faculty, sense-object, and consciousness alike become nothing but the perfectly accomplished true nature itself, with no distinction whatsoever between self and other remaining — where, then, could there be any "is" or "is not" left to point to at all? "Able to move beyond both pointing and not-pointing" means: once one awakens to the single true Dharma-realm, the seeing-portion and the perceived-portion are both seen to belong to flowers conjured in empty space, the ten thousand things and the nature of seeing originally one and the same substance — and so one becomes able to step entirely beyond this whole question of "is" and "is not," no longer asking, among the ten thousand things, which can be pointed to as "seeing" and which as "not seeing." This is exactly like recognizing the single moon as true, at which point every false calculation dissolves entirely. One can see, then, that delusion, awakening, truth, and falseness all rest on a single thought: let that one thought be deluded, and mind and its objects become a tangled confusion, questions of "is" and "is not" springing up at once; let that one thought be awakened, and wondrous enlightenment stands perfectly clear — how, then, could any question of "is" or "is not" find room to arise at all? This display, discussing the nature of seeing as undivided, shows that where there is neither "is" nor "is not," the discriminating feelings born of true and false dissolve of themselves — among the four meanings, this belongs to the meaning of wondrousness.

Reading note: Yuanying closes his own first bound volume of the commentary exactly here, at the end of this eighth display. Two displays remain — seeing surpasses ordinary conceptual categories altogether, and seeing moves beyond even "seeing" itself as a concept — and these open his second volume, continuing in fascicle 5. Notice how the argument has escalated across all eight displays so far: the first four established that seeing is mind, unmoving, unceasing, and never lost; these last four went further still, showing that seeing has nowhere it can be sent back to (unlike everything else in experience), is never mixed with what it perceives, is not confined by any physical boundary, and, examined closely enough, cannot even be pointed to as a separate thing standing apart from the world it sees at all.
