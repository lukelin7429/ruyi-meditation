Fascicle 6 · 大佛頂首楞嚴經講義卷六 · Complete

Every Illusion, the Same Discovery

Having spent three fascicles pointing directly at the nature of seeing, Yuanying now watches the sūtra widen its lens: the five aggregates and the six sense-entrances, each dismantled by the same method already used on seeing itself, are shown to be nothing other than the tathāgatagarbha — false appearance and true nature turning out to be exactly the same thing, examined closely enough.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 6 complete Source: bfnn.org / NTU Buddhist Digital Library Covers: 丑二 — harmonizing the five aggregates and six sense-entrances with the tathāgatagarbha

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 5 closed the sūtra's entire direct pointing at the sense-faculty's own nature — the ten displays of seeing, and the two kinds of false seeing that strip away its final trace of falseness. Fascicle 6 opens the next movement: rather than stopping with seeing alone, the sūtra now extends the identical method to the five aggregates (form, sensation, perception, formations, consciousness) and the six sense-entrances (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) — showing, category by category, that every one of them, examined closely, has no independent origin at all, and is therefore, in itself, the tathāgatagarbha. Yuanying frames this as moving from "phenomena as seen" to "phenomena as such" — no longer just proving that the act of seeing is stable and unshaken, but that every single phenomenon is.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. The core sūtra text for the five aggregates has already been translated in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 2 ("The Five Aggregates Are the Tathāgatagarbha"), and the six sense-entrances in its fascicle 3 ("The Six Sense-Entrances") — that exact English is reused throughout this page. Yuanying's own commentary, and the framing passages unique to his outline, are translated fresh here.

General Statement: Falseness Itself Is Truth 寅初 總標即妄即真

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難！汝猶未明，一切浮塵，諸幻化相，當處出生，隨處滅盡，幻妄稱相，其性真為妙覺明體。

〖講〗此下會通四科：五陰、六入、十二處、十八界，一一諸法，觀相生滅全妄，論性即妄皆真。從此不復稱見精、見性之名，改稱為如來藏，妙真如性；見性約一根是別，藏性攝六根是總，但是總、別異稱，實無異體耳。

浮塵者，虛浮不實之塵境。無而忽有曰幻，有而俶滅曰化，如空華、鏡像、夢境等。當處出生，生無來處；隨處滅盡，滅無去處；不過徒有幻妄名相而已，全無實體可得。其性真為妙覺明體者：相雖妄而性元真，以浮塵幻化之相，相不離性，其性即是眾生妙覺明體；無相而能現相，故謂之妙。舉此至虛之法為能例，例明下文陰、入、處、界似實有法，令知一一相妄性真，即事即理也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, you still do not understand: every floating, insubstantial, illusory, transformed appearance arises in its own proper place, and ceases entirely in that same place — these are called mere illusory, false appearances, yet their true nature is the very substance of wondrous, enlightened brightness itself."

Commentary: What follows now harmonizes the four categories — the five aggregates, the six sense-entrances, the twelve sense-fields, the eighteen elements — showing, for each and every phenomenon within them, that examined as mere appearance, arising and ceasing are entirely false, while examined as nature, this very falseness is, in every case, true. From this point on, the sūtra no longer speaks of "the essence of seeing" or "the nature of seeing," but instead calls it the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness — "the nature of seeing" names this in terms of a single sense-faculty, a specific instance; "the tathāgatagarbha nature" names this in terms of all six sense-faculties together, the general case — general and specific are simply different names for one and the same substance.

"Floating" describes an insubstantial, unreal object of perception. What did not exist and suddenly appears is called "illusory"; what exists and then abruptly vanishes is called "transformed" — like flowers conjured in space, a reflection in a mirror, or a scene in a dream. "Arises in its own proper place" means it arises from nowhere else at all; "ceases entirely in that same place" means it ceases to nowhere else at all — it is nothing but an illusory, false name and appearance, with no real substance to be found anywhere. As for "yet their true nature is the very substance of wondrous, enlightened brightness itself": though the appearance is false, its nature was, from the start, true — this illusory, transformed appearance never departs from its own nature, and that nature is exactly the sentient being's own substance of wondrous, enlightened brightness; having no fixed appearance of its own, yet able to manifest every appearance, it is therefore called "wondrous." This passage offers this most insubstantial of all phenomena as a general example, to illustrate what follows — the aggregates, entrances, fields, and elements, which seem to be real, substantial phenomena — so that one may recognize, in every single case, that the appearance is false while the nature is true: phenomenon and principle being, in this sense, exactly the same thing.

〖經〗如是乃至：五陰、六入、從十二處、至十八界、因緣和合，虛妄有生；因緣別離，虛妄名滅。殊不能知，生滅去來，本如來藏，常住妙明，不動周圓，妙真如性。性真常中，求於去、來，迷、悟、生死，了無所得。

〖講〗如是指法之辭，緊承上文，如是至虛幻之法，尚且相妄性真，例顯世間所有諸法，乃至陰、入、處、界，皆如是也。諸法雖多，四科之中，科科收無不盡，以一切諸法，總不出心、色二法，因對機開、合故，廣、略有異。

因緣和合，虛妄有生者：即指四科心、色諸法，不離因緣。夫真心絕待，寂湛常恆，不假因緣；而六種染心，虛妄生滅，必藉因緣。夫佛界真善妙色，性本清淨，不假因緣；而九界依、正，虛妄生滅，必藉因緣。因緣別離，虛妄名滅者：若眾生依本覺內熏，發起始覺之智，於所緣境，不執實有，則因緣別離，染心染色皆滅。總論心、色諸法，唯心所現，俱屬依他起性，依他似有，觀相元妄，於本無生滅中妄見生滅。

殊不能知，生滅去來，本如來藏，常住妙明，不動周圓，妙真如性者：以身、心、世界，乃屬依他起性，從緣生滅，虛妄無體；然當知妄不離真，全體即是圓成實性。如來藏，即眾生同具本覺性體，如即本有不變真如之本體，來即真如隨緣遍周之妙用，藏者含藏世出世間，一切善功德，無不具足。常住妙明，不動周圓，即藏性德相：本無去來曰常住；不屬迷悟曰妙明；本無生滅曰不動；無處不遍曰周圓；合此四德，為妙真如性。

性真常中，求於去、來，迷、悟、生死，了無所得者：由不知萬法唯心，宛見差殊，而有彼此去、來，聖、凡、迷、悟，始終生死等相，猶如翳眼，妄見空華。然既知相妄性真，於藏性真常之中，求其去來等相，了無所得；如夢行千里，一經醒寤，不離床枕，豈有去、來之相可得哉？如迷東為西，忽然有人指示令悟，豈有迷、悟之相可得哉？如夢生人間，自少而老，報盡命終，豈有生、死之相可得哉？此中數節之文，與論云：「因緣所生法，我說即是空，亦名為假名，亦名中道義。」若合符節，其意會之自明。

Sūtra: "And so it goes, on through the five aggregates, the six sense-entrances, and, from the twelve sense-fields to the eighteen elements: through the coming together of causes and conditions, they falsely appear to arise; through the separation of causes and conditions, they falsely appear to cease. Yet no one seems able to recognize that this arising and ceasing, this coming and going, is, at root, the tathāgatagarbha itself — ever-abiding, wondrously bright, unmoving, all-pervading, the wondrous, true nature of suchness. Within this true, ever-abiding nature, seeking after coming or going, delusion or awakening, birth or death, one finds, in the end, nothing at all to be had."

Commentary: "And so it goes" is a phrase pointing back to what has just been said, continuing directly: if even the most insubstantial, illusory phenomenon already shows this same pattern — false appearance, true nature — then every phenomenon in the world, extending through the aggregates, entrances, fields, and elements, follows this identical pattern as well. However numerous these phenomena may seem, the four categories together leave nothing out, since every phenomenon whatsoever falls under either mind or matter — the specific groupings simply expand or condense according to the audience's own capacity.

"Through the coming together of causes and conditions, they falsely appear to arise" refers to the mental and material phenomena within these four categories, none of which stands apart from cause and condition. The true mind itself, beyond every opposition, still and constant, needs no cause or condition at all — yet the six kinds of defiled mind, falsely arising and ceasing, must depend on cause and condition to appear. The Buddha-realm's own true, good, wondrous form, its nature originally pure, needs no cause or condition either — yet the defiled being and environment of the nine other realms, falsely arising and ceasing, must likewise depend on cause and condition. "Through the separation of causes and conditions, they falsely appear to cease" means: should a sentient being, relying on the inward permeation of original awakening, give rise to the wisdom of initial awakening, and no longer cling to their conditioned objects as genuinely real, then, causes and conditions having separated, both defiled mind and defiled form alike cease. In sum, every mental and material phenomenon is nothing but a manifestation of mind alone, belonging entirely to the dependently-arisen nature — dependently arisen phenomena only appear to exist; examined as mere appearance, they are, at root, false, falsely seen to arise and cease within what was never, in truth, subject to arising or ceasing at all.

"No one seems able to recognize that this arising and ceasing, this coming and going, is, at root, the tathāgatagarbha itself — ever-abiding, wondrously bright, unmoving, all-pervading, the wondrous, true nature of suchness": since body, mind, and world all belong to this dependently-arisen nature, arising and ceasing in dependence on conditions, false and without substance — one should nonetheless understand that this falseness never departs from truth; its whole substance is exactly the perfectly accomplished, true nature itself. "Tathāgatagarbha" is the substance of original awakening that every sentient being equally possesses: "tathā" (如, "thus") names the true suchness's own unchanging substance; "gata" (來, "come" or "gone") names that same true suchness's wondrous function, following every condition; "garbha" (藏, "womb" or "storehouse") means it contains and holds every wholesome merit and virtue, mundane and transcendent alike, lacking nothing at all. "Ever-abiding, wondrously bright, unmoving, all-pervading" describes exactly the virtuous qualities of this tathāgatagarbha nature: never coming or going is called "ever-abiding"; belonging to neither delusion nor awakening is called "wondrously bright"; never arising or ceasing is called "unmoving"; present everywhere without exception is called "all-pervading" — together, these four virtues make up the wondrous, true nature of suchness.

"Within this true, ever-abiding nature, seeking after coming or going, delusion or awakening, birth or death, one finds, in the end, nothing at all to be had": because one does not understand that the ten thousand phenomena are mind alone, one seems to see all manner of distinctions, and so perceives marks of coming and going, sage and ordinary being, delusion and awakening, birth and death from beginning to end — exactly like a diseased eye falsely seeing flowers conjured in space. Yet once one recognizes that the appearance is false while the nature is true, seeking such marks as coming and going within the tathāgatagarbha's own true, ever-abiding nature yields nothing at all — exactly like a person who dreams of traveling a thousand miles, and, upon waking, finds themselves never to have left their own bed at all: how could there be any mark of coming or going to be found there? Or like a person mistaking east for west, who suddenly, shown the truth by someone else, comes to understand — how could there be any mark of delusion or awakening to be found there? Or like a person who dreams of being born into the human world, growing old from youth, until their allotted span ends and they die — how could there be any mark of birth or death to be found there? This whole passage matches, point for point, the treatise's own verse: "Whatever arises through causes and conditions, I declare to be empty; it is also called a provisional name; it is also called the meaning of the middle way" — reflecting on this makes the point clear on its own.

The Five Aggregates Are the Tathāgatagarbha 卯初 會五陰即藏性

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難！云何五陰，本如來藏，妙真如性？

〖講〗上總以取例，下別為顯示。先顯示五陰，全妄即真。色、受、想、行、識五陰，本是世間有為之法，一旦許即藏性，故須徵起，而釋明耳。陰者蓋覆義，蓋覆真性故；新譯為五蘊，蘊者積聚義，積聚有為故。本如來藏者：謂五陰之相雖妄，其性本真。妙真如性者：不可思議曰妙，以一體具足萬用，萬用不離一體，如下文所謂「一為無量，無量為一」。譬如一金能成眾器，眾器不離一金；一真一切真，五陰皆真；一如一切如，五陰皆如；全相皆性，故曰本如來藏，妙真如性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, how is it that the five aggregates are, at root, the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness?"

Commentary: The passage above offered a general example; what follows now demonstrates each specific case. The Buddha begins with the five aggregates, showing that they are, in their entirety, false appearance that is, at the very same time, true nature. Form, sensation, perception, formations, and consciousness are, on their own terms, ordinary, conditioned phenomena of the world — so, having just declared them to be the tathāgatagarbha, the Buddha must now pose the question directly and explain it clearly. "Aggregate" (陰) carries the sense of "covering," since it covers over the true nature; the newer translation, "heap" (蘊), carries the sense of "accumulation," since conditioned things accumulate together. "At root, the tathāgatagarbha" means: though the appearance of the five aggregates is false, their nature was, from the start, true. "The wondrous, true nature of suchness": what cannot be conceived of in ordinary terms is called "wondrous," since a single substance contains within it ten thousand functions, and none of those ten thousand functions ever departs from that single substance — exactly as stated further on, "one becomes countless, and countless become one." It is like a single measure of gold capable of being shaped into countless different vessels, none of which is ever apart from that same gold: one truth, and every truth follows; the five aggregates are, every one, true. One suchness, and every suchness follows; the five aggregates are, every one, suchness. Every appearance is, in its entirety, this very nature — hence, "at root, the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness."

Form 色陰

〖經〗阿難！譬如有人，以清淨目，觀晴明空，惟一晴虛，迥無所有。其人無故，不動目睛，瞪以發勞，則於虛空，別見狂華，復有一切，狂亂非相。色陰當知，亦復如是。……是諸狂華，非從空來，非從目出。……是故當知：色陰虛妄，本非因緣，非自然性。

〖講〗此喻明色陰即藏性，乃就自法為喻。有人喻一念未動之本來人，清淨眼喻無妄真智，晴明空喻湛寂真理。一念未動以前，以真智照真理，理、智一如，惟是如來藏性，清淨本然，纖塵不立，豈有九界依正可得？其人無故不動目睛，喻最初無端，真如不守自性，不覺念起而有無明。瞪以發勞，喻業識妄為明覺；勞即勞見，喻轉相，轉本有之智光，為能見之見分。則於虛空別見狂華者，空原無華，瞪目妄現，即論中所云，以依能見故，境界妄現。復有一切狂亂非相者：內四大有生老病死，外四大有生住異滅，狂亂不定，畢竟皆空，故曰「非相」。

是諸狂華非從空來非從目出：以此觀察，身心、世界，既如空華，無所從生，無所還滅，則當體虛妄明矣。若空來者既從空來還從空入……空若非空，自不容其華相起滅；若目出者既從目出還從目入……即此華性從目出故當合有見，若有見者，去既華空，旋合見眼？若無見者，出既翳空，旋當翳眼？又見華時目應無翳，云何晴空號清明眼？有見無見，兩者俱非，足徵華非從目出矣。

是故當知：色陰虛妄，本非因緣，非自然性：即喻知法，所有一切色陰，無非業轉之勞相，同彼狂華，由瞪目發勞，虛妄顯現。以本無生體曰虛，由循業偽現曰妄。由本如來藏，常住不動，體恆無變，故非因緣；本如來藏，妙明周圓，用恆隨緣，故非自然；一一即妙真如性，果能善用其心，起智觀察，親見色陰根本，即此色陰，便可通達實相矣。

Sūtra: "Ānanda, it is like a person who, with clear eyes, gazes at a clear, open sky — nothing there but open clarity, utterly empty. If, for no reason, this person holds his eyes fixed and unmoving until they grow strained, he will see, apart from the sky itself, wild flowers appear in the empty space, along with all manner of wild, disordered, unreal shapes. You should know that the aggregate of form is exactly the same. ... If these flowers came from empty space, then, having come from space, they should return into space — but if they truly went in and out like this, then space would no longer be truly empty. ... If they came out of the eye, then, having come from the eye, they should return into the eye. ... You should therefore know that the aggregate of form is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

Commentary: This analogy reveals that the aggregate of form is exactly the tathāgatagarbha, drawn from the same subject matter it illustrates. "A person" stands for the original person before a single thought has yet stirred; "clear eyes" stand for undeluded, true wisdom; "a clear, open sky" stands for the still, silent, true principle itself. Before a single thought has stirred, true wisdom illuminates true principle, principle and wisdom perfectly one — nothing but the tathāgatagarbha nature, pure and undefiled from the very start, without the slightest speck of dust — how, then, could the nine other realms' own being and environment ever be found there at all? "For no reason, this person holds his eyes fixed and unmoving" stands for the very first, groundless moment when true suchness failed to maintain its own nature, and, unaware, a single thought stirred, giving rise to ignorance. "Grow strained" stands for karmic consciousness falsely becoming "bright awareness"; "strain" itself refers to strained seeing, standing for the "turning appearance" — turning the originally possessed light of wisdom into the seeing-portion capable of seeing at all. "He will see, apart from the sky itself, wild flowers appear in the empty space": space itself originally has no flowers in it at all — straining the eyes falsely produces them, exactly as the treatise says, "because there is a capacity for seeing, its objects falsely appear." "All manner of wild, disordered, unreal shapes": the inward four elements bring birth, old age, sickness, and death; the outward four elements bring arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing — wild and disordered, never settled, ultimately all empty — hence, "unreal shapes."

"These flowers came neither from empty space, nor from the eye": examined this way, body, mind, and world, exactly like flowers in space, have no source they arise from, and no place they return to in ceasing — their very substance is thereby shown clearly to be false. If they came from space... space, if not truly empty, could not possibly allow flowers to arise and cease within it. If they came from the eye... this very nature of the flowers, having come from the eye, ought therefore to be capable of seeing; if it could see, then, once departed to become a flower in space, it ought, upon its return, to see the eye itself; if it could not see, then, once departed, it ought to obscure space itself, and, upon its return, ought to obscure the eye itself. And further: at the very moment of seeing the flowers, the eye ought to be entirely free of any obstruction — so why, then, is a clear, unobstructed sky what earns the name "a clear, healthy eye"? Whether one grants it the capacity to see or not, both possibilities fail — proof enough that the flowers do not come from the eye at all.

"You should therefore know that the aggregate of form is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature": by way of this same analogy, one comes to understand the underlying principle — every aggregate of form is nothing but the strained appearance produced by this same turning of consciousness, exactly like these wild flowers, falsely manifested through the eye's own strain. Having no substance from which it truly arises is called "false" [虛]; appearing falsely in accordance with karma is called "illusory" [妄]. Since it is, at root, the tathāgatagarbha, ever-abiding and unmoving, its substance eternally unchanging, it is not a matter of cause and condition; and since it is, at root, the tathāgatagarbha, wondrously bright and all-pervading, its function eternally following conditions, it is not spontaneous either — each and every instance is exactly the wondrous, true nature of suchness. If one is genuinely able to put this mind to good use, giving rise to wisdom and observing directly, personally seeing through to the very root of the aggregate of form, then this aggregate of form alone is already enough to penetrate true reality entirely.

Sensation, Perception, Formations, Consciousness 受、想、行、識陰

Yuanying applies the identical three-part pattern — analogy, detailed refutation of where the sensation truly comes from, and the conclusion that it is "false, originally neither caused nor spontaneous" — to each of the remaining four aggregates. The sūtra's own analogies for these four have already been translated in the bare-sūtra project's fascicle 2.

Sensation 受陰 · 摩掌喻 — rubbing two palms together in empty space produces roughness, smoothness, cold, and heat where nothing existed before. Yuanying: the person at rest, forgetting even that they have a body, stands for the true suchness undisturbed, in which even the aggregate of sensation has not yet arisen; the groundless rubbing together of the two palms stands for ignorance stirring without cause, the unconditioned and the conditioned mingling until the three subtle and six coarse forms of affliction arise together, producing every sensation from roughness to heat.
Perception 想陰 · 說梅喻 — merely talking about a sour plum makes the mouth water; merely imagining a sheer cliff-edge makes the soles of the feet ache. Yuanying notes the classical story of Cao Cao ordering his thirsty troops onward with the promise of a plum grove ahead — the mere thought of sourness produced saliva before any plum was ever reached, proof enough that perception has no real object behind it at all, only the mind's own interfusing, wandering imagination.
Formations 行陰 · 暴流喻 — a rushing torrent's waves follow one upon another, each wave arising and vanishing in its own place, never overtaking the next. Yuanying: this is the subtlest of the five aggregates, corresponding to the seventh consciousness's own constant, deliberating thought-stream, present even where the coarser sensation and perception have already ceased — Confucius's own remark, standing by a river, "that which passes on is like this, never ceasing day or night," is, on this reading, an unwitting description of exactly this same aggregate of formations.
Consciousness 識陰 · 頻伽瓶喻 — a person seals a kalaviṅka-shaped vase full of ordinary air and carries it a thousand miles to another country; the air neither truly leaves its origin nor truly enters its destination. Yuanying: the vase stands for karma, the sealed air within it for consciousness driven along by that karma from one rebirth to the next — yet, examined closely, the air inside the vase and the air of the whole sky were never actually two different things to begin with, exactly as the eighth consciousness and the tathāgatagarbha nature were never, in truth, two different things either.

〖講〗總明五陰虛妄，本無實體，其體即藏性，若以微密觀照，照見五蘊皆空，則破五陰，而超五濁，自可頓入三摩地，與觀音把手共行。又一念中，皆具五陰：一念執著，執則成礙，便是色陰；覺知苦、樂，領納在心，便是受陰；緣慮此境，於中想像，即是想陰；剎那變滅，不得停住，即是行陰；歷歷不昧，了了分明，即是識陰。據此則五陰舉體即是一念，一念舉體即是五陰，若能觀無念者，便是破陰下手工夫也。又奢摩他微密觀照，照此五陰一一相妄：色陰如影像，受陰如陽燄，想陰如夢境，行陰如電光，識陰如幻事。不執實有，則本有如來藏性，不被五陰之所蓋覆，自可親見首楞嚴定體，不生不滅之妙真如性矣。

Commentary: In sum, all five aggregates are false, having no real substance of their own at all — their true substance is exactly the tathāgatagarbha nature. If one applies the subtle, hidden contemplation and sees directly that the five aggregates are empty, this breaks through the aggregates themselves and transcends the five turbidities, allowing direct entry into samādhi, walking hand in hand with Avalokiteśvara's own path. Notice, too, that all five aggregates are already present within a single thought: a single thought's own clinging, once it clings, becomes an obstruction — this is exactly the aggregate of form; being aware of suffering or joy, taking it in and holding it within the mind — this is exactly the aggregate of sensation; conditioning upon this same object and forming an image of it in the mind — this is exactly the aggregate of perception; changing and vanishing in an instant, never remaining still — this is exactly the aggregate of formations; remaining vividly, unmistakably, clearly present throughout — this is exactly the aggregate of consciousness. On this reckoning, the whole substance of the five aggregates is nothing but a single thought, and the whole substance of a single thought is nothing but the five aggregates — so that observing thoughtlessness directly is itself the very starting point for breaking through the aggregates. And through śamatha's own subtle, hidden contemplation, observing each of these five aggregates' own false appearance in turn: the aggregate of form is like a reflected image; sensation, like a mirage; perception, like a dream; formations, like a flash of lightning; consciousness, like a magician's illusion. Ceasing to cling to any of them as genuinely real, the tathāgatagarbha nature one has always possessed is no longer covered over by these five aggregates at all, and one may personally behold the very substance of Śūraṅgama samādhi — the unborn, unceasing, wondrous, true nature of suchness itself.

The Six Sense-Entrances Are the Tathāgatagarbha 卯二 會六入即藏性

Chinese Source

〖經〗復次，阿難！云何六入，本如來藏，妙真如性？

〖講〗梵語缽羅吠奢，此云入，亦云處。入有二義：一為能入，以能入塵取境故；二為所入，以為塵入之處故。按本文吸此塵象，當以吸入為義，即吸入六塵之處，故又名處。他經色心開合無此科，本經以根中不生滅性，即首楞嚴定體，亦即如來成佛之密因，脩證圓通下手所依之處，故特加焉。

本如來藏者：本即根本，一切枝末依之而起，今追究六入枝末之根本，元是如來藏，如即本有不變真如之本體，來即真如隨緣遍周之妙用，藏者含藏世出世間，一切善功德，無不具足，猶如寶故。妙真如性者：即如來藏之性，此性即理即事，全妄全真，不滯一法，不捨一法，故所以稱妙；今乃統萬法惟是一心，一真一切真，無有那一法不真，一如一切如，無有那一法不如：即六入亦妙真如性也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Further, Ānanda: how is it that the six sense-entrances are, at root, the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness?"

Commentary: The Sanskrit āyatana is rendered "entrance," and also "field." "Entrance" carries two senses: the entrance that enters, since it enters and takes hold of its object; and the entrance that is entered, since it is the very place an object enters into. Given that this passage speaks of "taking hold of" its object, the sense here is "the entrance that enters" — the very place through which the six sense-objects are taken in — hence it is also called a "field." Other sūtras' treatments of mind and matter, whether expanded or condensed, do not include this particular category; this sūtra, because it takes the sense-faculties' own unborn, unceasing nature as exactly the substance of Śūraṅgama samādhi, and exactly the hidden cause by which the Tathāgata attains buddhahood, the very starting point relied upon for cultivating and realizing perfect penetration, specifically adds this category for that reason.

"At root, [the entrances are] the tathāgatagarbha": "root" here means the fundamental root from which every branch and leaf arises; tracing the six sense-entrances, as branches and leaves, back to their own fundamental root, one finds it is originally the tathāgatagarbha itself — "tathā" (如) names true suchness's own inherent, unchanging substance; "gata" (來) names that same true suchness's wondrous function, following every condition, pervading everywhere; "garbha" (藏) means it contains and holds every wholesome merit and virtue, mundane and transcendent alike, lacking nothing at all, exactly like a treasury. "The wondrous, true nature of suchness" refers to this same tathāgatagarbha's own nature: this nature is both principle and phenomenon at once, entirely false and entirely true at once, clinging to no single phenomenon, yet abandoning no single phenomenon either — hence it is called "wondrous." Since every phenomenon, gathered together, is nothing but this one mind: one truth, and every truth follows, with no phenomenon whatsoever that is not true; one suchness, and every suchness follows, with no phenomenon whatsoever that is not suchness — so, too, are the six sense-entrances themselves the wondrous, true nature of suchness.

Each of the six sense-entrances follows an identical pattern: a small demonstration engineering a sensation with no real outside object (staring until the eyes strain, plugging the ears until they ring, and so on — the same analogies already translated in the bare-sūtra project's fascicle 3), then a systematic elimination of every possible source — not from the object, not from the sense-faculty alone, not from empty space — leaving only the conclusion that the entrance is, in itself, the tathāgatagarbha.

〖經〗阿難！即彼目精瞪發勞者，兼目與勞，同是菩提瞪發勞相。因于明暗二種妄塵，發見居中，吸此塵象，名為見性。此見離彼明暗二塵，畢竟無體。……若從明來，暗即隨滅，應非見暗。若從暗來，明即隨滅，應無見明。若從根生，必無明暗，如是見精本無自性。若於空出，前矚塵象，歸當見根；又空自觀，何關汝入？是故當知眼入虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

〖講〗此舉前色陰瞪目發勞為能例，發明眼入同一虛妄也。兼目與勞：勞字指空華，謂不特空華是勞相，兼能見之目，與所見之勞相，同是真性菩提心中，瞪發勞相。菩提是本有真心，真原無妄，由最初一念妄動，依動故能見，依能見故境界妄現。由末推本，豈僅色陰虛妄，即眼入同是菩提心中，一念妄動之勞相。

因於明暗二種妄塵，發見居中，吸此塵象，名為見性，此見離彼明暗二塵畢竟無體：以此眼入，因有明、暗二塵，黏湛然之體，發為勞見，居於浮、勝二根之中，吸入此明、暗二塵之象，如磁吸鐵，名為能見之量，此即菩提瞪發勞相，乃屬托塵妄現。倘若離彼明、暗二塵，畢竟無有能入之體可得，是謂離塵無體。

若從明來……若於空出，前矚塵象，歸當見根，又空自觀，何關汝入：若說眼入從空而出，則以空為見之根，出來時前矚塵境萬象，回歸時應當自見其根，當然不能見；縱使能見，乃空自觀，何關汝眼入之事？是故當知眼入虛妄，本非因緣，非自然性：以無實體故虛，無從來故妄，但是幻妄稱相。上句明相妄，下二句顯性真，謂眼入之相雖妄，妄不離真，其性本來不變，非因緣性；又復隨緣而非自然性耳。

Sūtra: "Ānanda, when the eye is stared fixed until it strains, both the eye and the strain alike are marks of bodhi itself straining into fatigue. Dependent on the two false objects of brightness and darkness, seeing arises in between them, taking hold of these objects — this is called the nature of seeing. This seeing, apart from those two objects of brightness and darkness, has, in the end, no substance of its own. ... If it came from brightness, it should vanish along with darkness, and so should not be able to see darkness. If it came from darkness, it should vanish along with brightness, and so should have no seeing of brightness. If it arose from the faculty itself, then, brightness and darkness aside, this essence of seeing would have no nature of its own to begin with. If it emerged from empty space, then, having gazed outward at objects, it should turn and see the faculty itself — and besides, if space itself were doing the observing, what would that have to do with your own entrance of seeing? You should therefore know that the entrance of the eye is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

Commentary: This draws on the earlier example of the strained, fixed stare from the aggregate of form, revealing that the entrance of the eye is likewise, entirely, false. "Both the eye and the strain": "strain" here refers to the flowers seen in space — the point being that not only are the flowers themselves a strained appearance, but the very eye capable of seeing them, together with that strained appearance seen, are, both alike, marks of this same true, bodhi-nature mind straining into fatigue. Bodhi is the inherently possessed true mind, originally free of any falseness at all — from the very first false stirring of a single thought, dependent on that stirring, a capacity to see arose, and, dependent on that capacity to see, its objects falsely appeared. Tracing this from branch back to root: it is not merely the aggregate of form that is false — the entrance of the eye, too, is exactly this same strained appearance, this same single false stirring, within the bodhi-mind itself.

"Dependent on the two false objects of brightness and darkness, seeing arises in between them, taking hold of these objects — this is called the nature of seeing; this seeing, apart from those two objects, has, in the end, no substance of its own": this entrance of the eye, because of the two objects of brightness and darkness, clings to what was originally still and gives rise to strained seeing, situated between the coarse and superior sense-organs, taking hold of these two objects of brightness and darkness the way a magnet draws iron — this is called the capacity for seeing; this is exactly the bodhi-mind's own strained appearance, belonging entirely to a false manifestation dependent on its object. Apart from these two objects of brightness and darkness, there is, in the end, no substance capable of "entering" to be found at all — this is what is meant by "no substance apart from its object."

"If it came from brightness... if it emerged from empty space, then, having gazed outward at objects, it should turn and see the faculty itself — and besides, if space itself were doing the observing, what would that have to do with your own entrance of seeing?": if one claims the entrance of the eye emerges from empty space, then space itself would be the very root of seeing — going outward, it would gaze at every object before it; returning, it ought to see its own root directly — which is plainly impossible; and even granting it could see, this would only be space observing itself, with nothing at all to do with your own entrance of seeing. "You should therefore know that the entrance of the eye is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature": having no real substance is called "false" [虛]; having no true source is called "illusory" [妄] — it is nothing but an illusory, false name and appearance. The first line here states that the appearance is false; the following two lines reveal that the nature is true — meaning that, though the entrance of the eye's own appearance is false, this falseness never departs from truth: its nature, originally unchanging, is not a matter of cause and condition, and, following every condition as it does, is not spontaneous either.

Hearing 耳入 — plugging both ears with the fingers until a ringing sound arises in the head. Yuanying: not merely a demonstration of falseness, but proof that root and object share one and the same origin — hearing itself, and the strained ringing produced without any real outside sound, are equally rooted in the bodhi-mind.
Smelling 鼻入 — inhaling sharply and repeatedly until a sensation of cold arises in the nose, from which arise, in turn, the sense of open or blocked passage and every fragrance and stench. Refuted identically: not from the passage, not from the faculty alone, not from empty space.
Tasting 舌入 — licking the lips until worn, producing bitterness in illness and a faint sweetness in health, against a baseline of blandness present when the tongue is entirely at rest — Yuanying notes this entrance uniquely involves three qualities (sweet, bitter, bland) rather than the usual two, since blandness itself counts as a distinct "flavor of no flavor."
Touching 身入 — a cold hand pressed against a warm one, each shifting toward the other's temperature depending on which is stronger — testing not only contact but its opposite, the sensation felt on parting the two hands again.
Knowing 意入 — falling asleep when exhausted, waking once rested, remembering what is taken in and forgetting what slips away — Yuanying maps the classical fourfold pattern of arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing directly onto this cycle of sleep, waking, memory, and forgetting, showing the mental faculty to be exactly as conditioned and empty of independent substance as any of the other five.

Reading note: Yuanying closes this fascicle by quoting Master Jiaoguang's celebrated observation on the three distinct refutation strategies the sūtra uses across the sense-entrances, sense-fields, and elements: refuting by way of the *condition* itself (removing the fuel rather than fighting the fire directly, since fire has no substance of its own apart from what feeds it) — this is the method used throughout the six entrances, since each is refuted via its paired sense-object; refuting by way of *mutual dependency* (two things propping each other up, so that removing either collapses both, as with a flood and the dragon said to cause it); and refuting by way of the *essential component* (as a bow depends on glue to bind sinew and horn together — remove the glue, and neither remains a bow at all). The entrances rely on the first method throughout; the fields to come, Yuanying notes, will combine the first two; and the eighteen elements will rely on the third.
