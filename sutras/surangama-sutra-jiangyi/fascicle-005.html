Beyond Cause, Beyond Concept, Beyond Seeing

Fascicle 5 completes the ten displays of seeing — surpassing every category of cause, condition, and spontaneity, and moving beyond even "seeing" as a concept — then closes the sūtra's entire direct pointing at the true mind with the two kinds of false seeing: one person's diseased eye, and a whole nation's shared omen.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 4 closed Yuanying's own first bound volume after the eighth display of seeing. Fascicle 5 opens his second volume and completes the sequence: the ninth display shows that seeing cannot be explained as either "spontaneous" or "caused by conditions" — every category ordinary reasoning reaches for simply fails to apply; the tenth shows that seeing moves beyond even the concept of "seeing" itself, closing with the sūtra's famous, compressed formulation, "seeing seeing itself is not seeing." The fascicle then turns to the two kinds of false seeing — one person's own diseased eye, and a whole nation's shared ominous portent — which complete the entire division of the sūtra devoted to pointing directly at the sense-faculty's own nature as the true mind, a division Yuanying's outline calls "帶妄顯真" (revealing truth while still carrying falseness) followed by "剖妄出真" (stripping away that falseness to reveal pure truth).

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. The core sūtra text for both displays, and for the entire section on the two kinds of false seeing, has already been translated in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 2 ("Not Cause and Condition, Not Spontaneity" and "Two Kinds of False Seeing"), and that exact English is reused here. A substantial amount of surrounding argument that the bare-sūtra excerpts abridge with ellipses — the detailed step-by-step refutation of spontaneity and causation, Ānanda's question about the four conditions for eye-consciousness, and the transition into the two kinds of false seeing — is translated fresh in this page.

Ninth Display: Seeing Surpasses Every Category 卯九 顯見超情

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！誠如法王所說，覺緣遍十方界，湛然常住，性非生滅。與先梵志，娑毘迦羅，所談冥諦，及投灰等，諸外道種，說有真我，遍滿十方，有何差別？世尊亦曾於楞伽山，為大慧等，敷演斯義：彼外道等，常說自然，我說因緣，非彼境界。我今觀此：覺性自然，非生非滅，離遠一切虛妄顛倒，似非因緣，與同也彼自然；云何開示，不入群邪，獲真實心，妙覺明性？

〖講〗此一大科，顯見超情。以見性不變隨緣故非自然，隨緣不變故非因緣；自然、因緣，皆是妄情計執，見性兩皆不屬，故曰超情。誠如法王所說：法王所說，指如來上文諸科，所說遍常之義。覺緣指見性，謂親依覺性具有能緣之功能，故稱覺緣。遍十方界者：乃稱性周遍，不分科中，見性遍見一切，同體不分；不雜科中，見性周遍，非汝而誰？此二科俱成遍義。湛然者：如寒潭止水，湛然不動，領不動科中，身境動搖，見無所動；無礙科中，身心圓明，不動道場之義。常住者：領無還科中，若真汝心，則無所去；不失科中，雖稱顛倒，無始不遺之義。性非生滅者：領不滅科中，彼不變者，原無生滅之義。如上諸科，俱成常義。阿難躡之起疑，疑濫外宗，遂計自然。

先梵志是古來梵志也，其人自謂梵天苗裔，志生梵天，即婆羅門種。冥諦，為冥初自諦，是外道二十五諦中第一諦也。彼數論師，計冥性是常，能生大等二十三法，與現今所說湛然常住性非生滅，義似有濫。及投灰等諸外道種：投灰，亦云塗灰，有時以身投灰，有時以灰塗身，而修無益之苦行。等者等餘拔髮、熏鼻、臥刺、自餓，與持雞戒、牛戒諸外道種。此等皆心遊道外；說有真我遍滿十方：外道計我相有三：一、大我；二、小我；三、不定我。此所說覺緣遍十方界，義似有濫大我，如來所說遍常，與外道有何差別？

此舉昔日自宗。楞伽山名，此云不可往，非有神通者不能到故。大慧菩薩，是楞伽會上當機。佛曾在楞伽山，為大慧等，廣辯內教，與外道不同之義。彼外道等常說自然者，清涼云：「無因論師，計一切物，無因、無緣，自然生，自然滅。」此則撥無因果，不立修證，佛說因緣以破之，故曰：「我說因緣，非彼境界。」又因謂種子親因，緣謂資發助緣，內而三乘等性，須假宿生善根種子為因，復假如來教法以為外助之緣，方生諸乘所證之果；外而百穀等物，亦須種子為因，水土為緣，方生芽等，大異於彼外道所說，故曰非彼境界。

首句判同外計。謂我今觀此，覺緣見性，亦成自然。非生非滅三句，謬取如來所顯見性之義，以證成之。非生者，由來本有；非滅者，究竟不壞，即不失不滅科義。若有動、有還、有雜、有礙、有分，俱屬虛妄顛倒，前顯不動、無還、不雜、無礙、不分，故云遠離。此實大乘正理，迥超凡情外計，阿難不達，反取之以證自然，故曰似非因緣即自然，蓋外道自然，佛昔所破，今佛所說同彼自然，此疑違昔宗。云何開示不入群邪者：意謂佛之所說，與外道自然，或同或異，固所未諳，云何開示分明，令知揀擇，不入群邪，頓獲無戲論之真實心，而證妙覺明性之全體大用耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, it is truly as the Dharma-king has said: this awareness and its conditions pervade the ten directions, calm and ever-abiding, its nature beyond birth and death. But how does this differ from what the ancient brahmacārin Kapila taught as the 'obscure principle,' or from the various non-Buddhist schools of ash-smeared ascetics who likewise speak of a true self pervading the ten directions? World-Honored One, you also once, on Mount Laṅkā, expounded this very point to Mahāmati and the others: that those non-Buddhist teachers constantly speak of spontaneity, while you yourself teach causes and conditions — a realm entirely different from theirs. As I now observe it, this nature of awareness seems spontaneous, neither arising nor ceasing, far removed from every falsehood and inversion — it seems to be neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor the 'spontaneity' those others speak of. How, then, can I be shown a way that avoids falling among these various false views, and attains the true mind, the wondrously enlightened, bright nature?"

Commentary: This whole major section shows that seeing surpasses every ordinary category. Because the nature of seeing, unchanging in itself, still follows every condition, it is not "spontaneous"; and because, following every condition, it still remains unchanging, it is not a matter of "cause and condition" either. Both "spontaneous" and "caused" are merely clinging concepts born of false thought — the nature of seeing belongs to neither, hence "surpassing every category." "It is truly as the Dharma-king has said" refers back to everything the Tathāgata has already taught in the previous displays about seeing's pervading, constant nature. "This awareness and its conditions" refers to the nature of seeing itself — since it directly depends on the very nature of awareness, and possesses the capacity to condition upon its objects, it is called "awareness and its conditions." "Pervading the ten directions" describes the nature's own all-pervading quality — established already in the displays showing seeing undivided from all it sees, sharing one substance without division, and seeing unmixed with objects, all-pervading, belonging to no one but oneself; both of these displays together establish this sense of pervasiveness. "Calm" is like a cold, still pool of water, motionless — taken up from the display of seeing unmoving, where body and surroundings stir while seeing itself never moves, and the display of seeing unobstructed, where body and mind, perfectly bright, never leave the place of practice. "Ever-abiding" is taken up from the display of seeing having nowhere to return, where, if this is truly your own mind, it has nowhere to go at all, and the display of seeing never truly lost, where, however much it is called upside-down, it was never actually forfeited from beginningless time. "Its nature beyond birth and death" is taken up from the display of seeing imperishable, where what does not change was, from the very start, beyond birth and death entirely. All these earlier displays together establish this sense of constancy. Ānanda, building on all of this, now gives rise to a new doubt — suspecting that this teaching resembles a non-Buddhist doctrine, and so concluding it must be "spontaneous."

The "ancient brahmacārin" refers to brahmacārins of ages past, who claimed descent from Brahmā himself, resolved to be reborn in the Brahmā heaven — that is, the brahmin caste. The "obscure principle" is the very first of the twenty-five principles taught by non-Buddhist schools, the primordial, self-existing basis of reality. The Sāṃkhya teachers held that this obscure nature is permanent, capable of giving rise to the twenty-three further categories beginning with "the great principle" — a doctrine that seems, on the surface, to resemble what has just been taught here: calm, ever-abiding, its nature beyond birth and death. "The various non-Buddhist schools of ash-smeared ascetics" refers to those who, at times, throw themselves into ashes, and at times smear ashes over their own bodies, practicing austerities that bring no real benefit at all; "and so on" includes other such schools as well — those who pull out their own hair, inhale smoke through the nose, sleep on thorns, starve themselves, or keep the vows of a rooster or an ox — all of them minds wandering outside the true path. As for "speak of a true self pervading the ten directions": non-Buddhist schools hold three kinds of self — the great self, the small self, and the indeterminate self. Since this teaching, "this awareness and its conditions pervade the ten directions," resembles their notion of the great self, Ānanda wonders how the Tathāgata's own teaching of pervasiveness and constancy differs from theirs at all.

This next passage cites the Buddha's own earlier teaching. "Mount Laṅkā" means "the place that cannot be reached" — no one lacking spiritual powers is able to arrive there at all. Mahāmati was the interlocutor at that assembly on Mount Laṅkā. The Buddha once, on that mountain, taught Mahāmati and the others at length the distinction between the Buddhist teaching and non-Buddhist doctrine. As for "those non-Buddhist teachers constantly speak of spontaneity," Master Qingliang observes: "Teachers of the 'no-cause' doctrine hold that all things arise and cease spontaneously, with no cause and no condition at all." This doctrine denies cause and effect altogether and rejects any path of cultivation and realization — which is exactly why the Buddha taught cause and condition, to refute it, hence: "I teach causes and conditions — a realm entirely different from theirs." "Cause" here means the intimate, direct cause, like a seed; "condition" means the supporting, contributing circumstance — inwardly, the nature shared by the three vehicles depends on wholesome seeds accumulated across past lives as its cause, and further depends on the Tathāgata's own teaching as an external, supporting condition, before the fruit realized by any of the vehicles can arise at all; outwardly, even a simple grain of rice depends on a seed as its cause and on water and soil as its condition before a sprout can arise. This is entirely different from what those non-Buddhist teachers claim — hence, "a realm entirely different from theirs."

The opening line here judges Ānanda's own view to match the non-Buddhist position. His thought: as I now observe it, this awareness and its conditions, this nature of seeing, likewise turns out to be spontaneous. The next three lines mistakenly appropriate the very qualities the Tathāgata has just revealed about the nature of seeing, using them as proof of this very claim: "not arising" — because it has always, inherently, existed; "not ceasing" — because it can never, in the end, be destroyed, exactly the meaning already established in the displays of seeing never lost and never perishing. Wherever there is motion, return, mixture, obstruction, or division, all of this belongs to falsehood and inversion; the earlier displays revealed seeing to be free of motion, free of return, unmixed, unobstructed, undivided — hence, "far removed" from all of this. This is, in truth, the correct principle of the Great Vehicle, far surpassing every ordinary, non-Buddhist speculation — yet Ānanda, failing to grasp this, instead seizes upon it as proof of spontaneity, hence, "it seems to be neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor the spontaneity those others speak of." Since spontaneity is exactly the non-Buddhist doctrine the Buddha himself had earlier refuted, and Ānanda now suspects the Buddha's own present teaching resembles that same spontaneity, this is precisely a doubt about whether the present teaching contradicts the Buddha's own earlier teaching. "How, then, can I be shown a way that avoids falling among these various false views" means: Ānanda does not yet know whether the Buddha's present teaching agrees with or differs from the non-Buddhist doctrine of spontaneity — how, then, can it be explained clearly enough for him to distinguish the two properly, avoid falling among these false views, and attain at once the true mind, free of empty argument, thereby realizing the full substance and function of this wondrously enlightened, bright nature?

〖經〗佛告阿難：我今如是，開示方便，真實告汝，汝猶未悟，惑為自然！阿難！若必自然，自須甄明，有自然體。汝且觀此，妙明見中，以何為自，此見為復以明為自？以暗為自？以空為自？以塞為自？阿難！若明為自，應不見暗；若復以空為自體者，應不見塞；如是乃至，諸暗等相，以為自者，則於明時，見性斷滅，云何見明？

〖講〗此破自然。先呼當機之名，而責之曰：我今前來八番，如是開示，費卻許多脣舌，或直顯旁通，或明彰曲示，種種權巧方便，無非將一真實相之道，告知於汝，汝猶未能悟見性為真本，反乃惑為自然，豈不辜負於我哉！若汝必定，以此覺緣周遍不動，性非生滅，以為是自然者，自當甄別明白，有一自然之體方可。

此就中詰問。謂妙明真見，遍見諸緣，汝且觀察，此妙明見中，以見何者為自然之體，此二句為總詰。此見為復下，約明、暗、空、塞四緣，逐一別詰。以明為自者，謂以見明者為自然體。下三例知。此難破。若以見明為自然體者，自然應當不變，既以明為自，只應見明，至於明去暗來，應不隨緣，不能見暗，方成自然？以空為自等，例此可知。如是乃至，超略以塞為自，應不見空？諸暗等相者：子夜黑月，雲霧晦瞑等，皆為暗相，既以暗為自然體，自應不隨明緣，故難以則於明時暗滅，見性應當斷滅，云何明時仍舊見明？既然明來見明，暗來見暗，乃是隨緣，有何自然之義？

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "I have now explained this to you through every skillful means at my disposal, telling you the truth as directly as I can, and yet you still have not awakened, but instead mistake it for spontaneity! Ānanda, if this truly were spontaneous, you ought to be able to identify clearly some definite substance that is spontaneous in itself. Consider now, within this wondrously bright seeing, what would count as this spontaneous self: is it brightness that is this seeing's own self? Is it darkness? Is it emptiness? Is it obstruction? Ānanda, if brightness were this self, then you ought not to be able to see darkness at all; and if emptiness were this very substance, you ought not to be able to see obstruction. And so on: if darkness or any of the rest were taken as this self, then, at the time of brightness, the nature of seeing ought to be entirely cut off — so how could you still see brightness at all?"

Commentary: This refutes spontaneity. The Buddha first addresses Ānanda by name and gently rebukes him: through eight displays now, I have explained this teaching at great length — sometimes directly, sometimes by indirection, sometimes plainly, sometimes obliquely, every kind of skillful means — all of it meant to convey to you a single, true reality; and yet you still have not awakened to seeing as the true root, and instead mistake it for spontaneity — does this not fail everything I have taught you? If you truly insist that this all-pervading, unmoving awareness, whose nature is beyond birth and death, is spontaneous, then you ought to be able to point out clearly some definite substance that is spontaneous in itself.

This next passage questions Ānanda directly on the point. The wondrously bright, true seeing perceives every condition equally — the Buddha asks him to observe: within this wondrously bright seeing, which of these conditions counts as its spontaneous substance? These two lines pose the question generally; what follows then questions him specifically, one by one, regarding brightness, darkness, emptiness, and obstruction. "Brightness as this self" means taking whatever sees brightness as the spontaneous substance — the following three cases follow the same pattern. This is then refuted: if seeing brightness were the spontaneous substance, it ought, by its very nature, to remain unchanging — since it is taken as brightness itself, it ought only ever to see brightness; yet when brightness departs and darkness arrives, it ought not to follow this change at all, and so ought to be unable to see darkness — only then could it truly count as spontaneous. The same logic applies to taking emptiness as this self, and so on; skipping ahead, the same applies to taking obstruction as this self — it ought then to be unable to see emptiness. "Darkness or any of the rest" refers to the black-moon depth of midnight, clouds and fog obscuring the sky, and every other such mark of darkness — if darkness were taken as the spontaneous substance, it ought by nature never to follow the condition of brightness at all, and so the challenge follows: at the time of brightness, darkness having ceased, the nature of seeing itself ought to be cut off entirely — so why, then, does it still see brightness perfectly well at the time of brightness? Since brightness arriving is met with seeing brightness, and darkness arriving is met with seeing darkness, this is exactly following conditions — where, then, is there any true spontaneity in this at all?

〖經〗阿難言：必此妙見，性非自然，我今發明，是因緣生。心猶未明，咨詢如來，是義云何，合因緣性？佛言：汝言因緣，吾復問汝：汝今因見，見性現前，此見為復因明有見？因暗有見？因空有見？因塞有見？阿難！若因明有，應不見暗；如因暗有，應不見明；如是乃至，因空、因塞，同於明暗。復次，阿難！此見又復緣明有見？緣暗有見？緣空有見？緣塞有見？阿難！若緣空有，應不見塞；若緣塞有，應不見空；如是乃至緣明、緣暗，同於空塞。

〖講〗當機聞佛難破自然，轉疑因緣。故曰：必此妙明見性，非是自然，我今發明，必定是從因緣所生。此之發明，不是真智發明真理，乃是對待發明，故心猶未曾明悟，必須咨詢如來，是前來各科所說遍常之義，云何符合因緣性；誠是未明，今教不但不墮自然，兼亦不墮因緣。

上段以真如隨緣義，破其自然，此段以真如不變義，破其因緣。因是親因，如種子；緣是助緣，如水土，故分而破之，此先約因破。佛對阿難言：汝言我今發明，是因緣生。吾再來問汝：汝今因見明、暗、空、塞之境，見性乃得現前，但此性為復因明而有見耶？為復因暗而有見耶？若因明有，即是以明為生見之因，餘可以此例知。若見性因明而有，則明為生見之因，及至暗生明滅，則汝見性，應隨明以俱滅，不應當再見暗；如因暗有，明暗相傾，明時無暗，則汝見性，應與暗以俱亡，不應當再見明。下因空、因塞，與此相同。

此約緣破。復呼當機，謂汝此見性，於明、暗、空、塞四種，究竟以何為發見之緣？若見性緣空而有，則空為發見之緣，及塞時空滅，則汝見性，應隨空以俱滅，不應再見於塞；如緣塞有，塞空相奪，塞時無空，則汝見性，應與塞以俱亡，不應再見於空；下緣明、緣暗，與此相同。

Sūtra: Ānanda said: "Since this wondrous seeing's own nature is surely not spontaneous, I now conclude that it must instead arise from causes and conditions. Yet my mind is still not clear on this — I ask the Tathāgata: how, exactly, does this fit the nature of cause and condition?" The Buddha said: "You speak of cause and condition — let me ask you further: right now, because of seeing, the nature of seeing appears before you — is this seeing present because of brightness? Because of darkness? Because of emptiness? Because of obstruction? Ānanda, if it were present because of brightness, you ought not to be able to see darkness; if it were present because of darkness, you ought not to be able to see brightness — and so it goes for emptiness and obstruction as well, exactly the same as with brightness and darkness. Further, Ānanda: does this seeing instead arise conditioned upon brightness? Conditioned upon darkness? Conditioned upon emptiness? Conditioned upon obstruction? Ānanda, if it arose conditioned upon emptiness, you ought not to be able to see obstruction; if it arose conditioned upon obstruction, you ought not to be able to see emptiness — and so it goes for brightness and darkness as well, exactly the same as with emptiness and obstruction."

Commentary: Having just heard spontaneity refuted, Ānanda now turns his doubt toward causation instead. His thought: since this wondrously bright nature of seeing is surely not spontaneous, I now conclude it must certainly arise from causes and conditions instead. Yet this "conclusion" is not genuine wisdom illuminating true principle — it is merely a conclusion reached by process of elimination — hence his mind is still not truly clear, and he must ask the Tathāgata how everything taught so far about seeing's pervading, constant nature actually fits the nature of cause and condition. He is, in truth, still unclear that the present teaching falls into neither spontaneity nor causation at all.

The previous passage refuted spontaneity by way of true suchness's own capacity to follow conditions; this passage refutes causation by way of true suchness's own unchanging nature. "Cause" is the intimate, direct cause, like a seed; "condition" is the supporting circumstance, like water and soil — hence the refutation proceeds in two separate stages, first by cause. The Buddha says to Ānanda: you claim my present conclusion is that seeing arises from cause and condition — let me ask you further: right now, because of the conditions of brightness, darkness, emptiness, and obstruction, the nature of seeing appears before you — but is this nature present because of brightness, or because of darkness? If it were present because of brightness, then brightness would be the very cause giving rise to seeing — and the same reasoning applies to the rest. If the nature of seeing were present because of brightness, with brightness as the cause giving rise to seeing, then, once darkness arose and brightness ceased, your nature of seeing ought to cease right along with brightness, and you ought no longer to be able to see darkness at all; if it were present because of darkness, then, brightness and darkness displacing one another, at the time of brightness there being no darkness, your nature of seeing ought to vanish right along with darkness, and you ought no longer to be able to see brightness. The same reasoning applies to emptiness and obstruction as cause.

This next passage refutes by way of condition. The Buddha again addresses Ānanda: as for this nature of seeing of yours, among these four — brightness, darkness, emptiness, and obstruction — which, exactly, serves as the condition that gives rise to seeing? If the nature of seeing arose conditioned upon emptiness, with emptiness as the condition giving rise to seeing, then, once obstruction arose and emptiness ceased, your nature of seeing ought to cease right along with emptiness, and you ought no longer to be able to see obstruction; if it arose conditioned upon obstruction, obstruction and emptiness displacing one another, at the time of obstruction there being no emptiness, your nature of seeing ought to vanish right along with obstruction, and you ought no longer to be able to see emptiness. The same reasoning applies to brightness and darkness as condition.

〖經〗當知如是精覺妙明，非因非緣，亦非自然，非不自然，無非不非，無是非是。離一切相，即一切法。汝今云何，於中措心，以諸世間，戲論名相，而得分別？如以手掌，撮摩虛空，祗益自勞，虛空云何，隨汝執捉？

〖講〗當知者：應當起智觀察，了知見性，離四句，絕百非。精覺妙明：即是見性，以不變之覺體，純一無雜曰精覺；以隨緣之妙用，靈明不昧曰妙明，此申其正義。體既不變，故非因緣；用既隨緣，故非自然，此屬第一重遣。

非不自然，無非不非：首句非不自然上，應有非不因緣一句，但是遺脫矣！以因緣自然，皆是妄情計度，故上以非字遣之。恐轉計不因緣、不自然，仍舊未離戲論，故更以非字遣之，此以非遣非，即遣上兩句，此屬第二重遣。無非不非者：無字雙貫非與不非，此為躡遣，以一無字，躡遣前四句，屬第三重遣。

無是非是者，無字亦雙貫是與非是，此為對遣，防其對非計是，對是計非。或聞上文諸非盡遣，仍然計是，故更以無字遣之，屬第四重遣。非是二字，亦即非字，到此重重迭拂，妄情自盡。

此乃情盡法真。相非萬法之自相，乃是妄情計度之心相，即執因緣自然等心。遣之又遣，諸情蕩盡，法法元真；當知法本無差，情計成過，但用亡情，何勞壞相？六祖云：「六塵不惡，還同正覺。」但能離一切妄計之相，即一切諸法，無非全體法界。

云何責怪詞。謂是法非思量分別之所能及，汝今云何於精覺妙明中，措心作意，用諸世間戲論名相，欲得分別妙明真性；此性離名絕相，豈世間戲論名相，而得分別哉！如以手掌撮摩虛空：下喻明無益，祗增自己勞苦，虛空云何隨汝執捉乎？此以手掌撮摩喻戲論，虛空喻真性，虛空既不可措手，而真性又安可措心耶？此科以見性超情論，顯其諸情不墮，越遠外計、權宗，即四義中妙明之義。

Sūtra: "You should know: this essential, wondrously bright awareness is neither caused nor conditioned, and yet it is not spontaneous either; it is not 'not spontaneous' either; there is no 'not-not,' and no 'is' or 'is not' to be found in it at all. Free of every characteristic, it is, at the very same time, every single phenomenon there is. Why, then, do you now try to fix your mind upon it, hoping to grasp it through the idle names and concepts of ordinary worldly debate? It is like trying to grasp a handful of empty space with your own palm — you only exhaust yourself for nothing. How could empty space ever be seized and held by your own hand?"

Commentary: "You should know" means: give rise to wisdom and observe directly, understanding that the nature of seeing stands apart from every one of the four possible positions [is, is not, both, neither], beyond every hundred variations of denial. "Essential awareness, wondrously bright" refers exactly to the nature of seeing: as an unchanging substance of awareness, wholly undivided, it is called "essential awareness"; as a wondrous function that follows every condition, luminously unobscured, it is called "wondrously bright" — this states the correct meaning directly. Since its substance never changes, it is not caused by conditions; since its function follows every condition, it is not spontaneous either — this is the first round of negation.

"Not spontaneous either; there is no 'not-not'": the first line, "not spontaneous either," implies a matching line, "not uncaused either," simply omitted here in the text. Since both "caused" and "spontaneous" are merely calculations of false thought, the passage above negates them with the word "not." But fearing this might simply shift to a new clinging — "not caused," "not spontaneous" — still failing to escape empty argument, the text negates even this with a further "not," negating negation itself, and so canceling both of the previous two lines — this is the second round of negation. "There is no 'not-not'" — the word "no" here governs both "not" and "not-not" together, canceling what came before with a further stroke; this single word "no" cancels all four preceding positions at once — this is the third round of negation.

"No 'is' or 'is not'" — the word "no" here likewise governs both "is" and "is not" together, canceling both directions at once, guarding against clinging to "is" once "is not" has been ruled out, or clinging to "is not" once "is" has been ruled out. Should one, hearing every prior negation exhausted, still cling to some remaining "is," this final "no" cancels that as well — this is the fourth round of negation. "Is" and "is not" together amount to the same negation once more — by this point, negation upon negation has been applied, and false clinging is exhausted entirely on its own.

This describes the exhaustion of clinging and the emergence of true reality. "Characteristic" here does not mean the inherent characteristic of any phenomenon itself, but the mental characteristic produced by false, clinging thought — exactly the mind that clings to "caused" or "spontaneous" and the like. Negating this again and again, every such clinging dissolves entirely, and every phenomenon is revealed, at root, as true. One should understand: phenomena themselves were never truly different from one another to begin with — clinging thought alone creates the appearance of fault; simply let go of the clinging, and there is no need to alter any phenomenon's own characteristic at all. As the Sixth Patriarch said: "The six sense-objects are not themselves evil — they are one and the same with true, correct enlightenment." Simply becoming free of every falsely clung-to characteristic is itself nothing other than every phenomenon there is, the whole substance of the Dharma-realm entire.

"Why, then" carries a tone of gentle rebuke. This teaching is not something ordinary thought and discrimination can ever reach — so why, then, do you fix your mind upon this essential, wondrously bright awareness, applying the idle names and concepts of ordinary worldly debate, hoping thereby to discriminate this wondrously bright, true nature? This nature stands apart from every name, free of every characteristic — how could the idle names and concepts of worldly debate ever succeed in discriminating it at all! "It is like trying to grasp a handful of empty space with your own palm" — what follows illustrates the futility of this: one only exhausts oneself for nothing — how could empty space ever be seized and held in one's own hand? Here, grasping with the palm illustrates idle debate; empty space illustrates the true nature. Just as empty space can never be taken hold of by the hand, how could the true nature ever be taken hold of by the mind either? This display, discussing the nature of seeing as surpassing every category, shows that it falls into none of these ordinary conceptions at all, standing far beyond every non-Buddhist speculation and every provisional teaching alike — among the four meanings, this belongs to the meaning of wondrous brightness.

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！必妙覺性非因非緣，世尊云何常與比丘宣說：見性具四種緣？所謂因空、因明、因心、因眼，是義云何？

〖講〗此科顯見離見，不是常途所謂情見之見，乃指見精自體耳。見精自體，真妄和合，約義可分真、妄二見，並非真妄各體。但約見精帶妄時，則名妄見，離妄時，則名真見。如人在夢時，則名夢人，離夢時則名醒人；夢人醒人無二人，真見妄見無二見。今言離見者，即真見離自體中一分妄見而已，故名為顯見離見。

上科因緣、自然二皆迭遣，此處阿難白佛言：世尊！此妙覺明性，不屬外道自然則可，若謂非因非緣，似乎不可；以因緣是自宗，何得與自宗相違？必此見性非因非緣，世尊云何昔日常與比丘宣說，見性具四種緣，所謂因空、因明、因心、因眼耶？以目前不空、不明，則不能見，無心無眼，更不待言，所以必因四種，方能成見，則見性自屬因緣之義明矣！是義云何者：豈今是而昔非耶？抑或今昔相同，而我未達耶？

當機所引，乃眼根中眼識，如來所顯是眼根中見性，正是根、識不分。八識規矩頌云：「愚者難分識與根。」阿難即其人矣！大乘眼識，九緣方生，所謂：空、明、根、境、作意、分別、染淨、種子、根本；小乘法中略具四緣，缺一不可，即空、明、心、眼四緣，為眼識得生之緣，並未曾說，四緣生妙覺性。良以此性，非生因之所生，乃了因之所了，今說從因緣生，誤之甚矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if this wondrously awakened nature is truly neither caused nor conditioned, then why has the World-Honored One so often taught the monks that seeing requires four conditions — namely, depending on space, depending on light, depending on mind, and depending on the eye? What, then, is the meaning of this?"

Commentary: This display, "seeing moves beyond seeing," does not refer to the ordinary, everyday sense of "seeing" bound up with emotion and opinion, but specifically to the essence of seeing's own substance. This substance is a union of the true and the false — conceptually, it can be divided into a true seeing and a false seeing, though these are not, in fact, two separate substances. When the essence of seeing still carries a trace of falseness, it is called false seeing; once free of that falseness, it is called true seeing — exactly as a person dreaming is called "the dreamer," and, once free of the dream, "the one awake"; the dreamer and the one awake are not two different people, and true seeing and false seeing are not two different kinds of seeing. To speak here of "moving beyond seeing" means exactly this: true seeing becoming free of the single trace of false seeing carried within its own substance — hence the name, "seeing moves beyond seeing."

Having just had both spontaneity and causation refuted in turn, Ānanda now says to the Buddha: World-Honored One, I can accept that this wondrously awakened, bright nature does not belong to the non-Buddhist doctrine of spontaneity — but to say it is neither caused nor conditioned seems harder to accept, since cause and condition is our own school's own teaching — how could it possibly contradict our own school? If this nature of seeing is truly neither caused nor conditioned, then why did the World-Honored One, in the past, so often teach the monks that seeing requires four conditions — namely, depending on space, depending on light, depending on mind, and depending on the eye? Without emptiness or light in front of us, we cannot see at all; and without mind or eye, this goes without saying — so seeing must, by necessity, depend on all four of these conditions before it can occur at all, which clearly shows that the nature of seeing belongs to the category of cause and condition after all! "What, then, is the meaning of this" means: is what is taught now correct, and what was taught before mistaken? Or do the two teachings actually agree, and I simply fail to grasp how?

What Ānanda cites here is actually eye-consciousness, belonging to the eye-faculty; what the Tathāgata has been revealing is the very nature of seeing within the eye-faculty — this is exactly a failure to distinguish sense-faculty from consciousness. The verse on the pattern of the eight consciousnesses says: "The foolish find it hard to distinguish consciousness from the faculty." Ānanda is exactly such a person! In the Great Vehicle's own teaching, eye-consciousness arises only given nine conditions: space, light, the sense-faculty, its object, attentive intention, discrimination, purity or defilement, seed, and root-consciousness; the Lesser Vehicle's teaching gives an abbreviated four conditions, none of which may be lacking — space, light, mind, and eye — as the conditions by which eye-consciousness itself arises. Neither teaching ever claimed that these four conditions give rise to the wondrously awakened nature of seeing itself. This nature, in truth, is not something produced by any generative cause at all, but something realized through a cause of direct understanding alone — to claim now that it arises from cause and condition is a very serious error indeed.

〖經〗佛言：阿難！我說世間，諸因緣相，非第一義。阿難！吾復問汝：諸世間人，說我能見，云何名見？云何不見？阿難言：世人因於日、月、燈光，見種種相，名之為見；若復無此三種光明，則不能見。

〖講〗此明今昔權實不同。佛告阿難，我昔日說四緣生識者，乃一時權巧方便，說世間諸因緣相，引誘小乘學者，免中外道之毒。彼外道妄計自然，我說因緣以破之，非同今日所說第一義諦，修證了義之法，何得取彼而難此耶？

此科辨今所說，非同昔日因緣。故問之曰：諸世間人，說我能見：是以我為能見，物為所見，此乃一切眾生共執。今詰以云何名見，云何不見者：為探其藉緣不藉緣。答以因於日、月、燈三種光明，乃能有見，無光即無見，正述其必定藉緣。此雖單舉明緣，以該空、心、眼三緣，缺一不見，此昔日之權。阿難所述固是，然今日所說，是第一義，如第一番顯見，盲人矚暗，與有眼人，處於暗室，所見無異。見性脫根脫塵，靈光獨耀，又何藉明緣？此即權實不同也。

Sūtra: The Buddha said: "Ānanda, when I speak of causes and conditions among worldly things, that is not the ultimate truth. Ānanda, let me ask you further: ordinary people in the world say, 'I am able to see' — what, exactly, do they mean by 'seeing,' and what do they mean by 'not seeing'?" Ānanda said: "Ordinary people, by means of the light of the sun, the moon, or a lamp, see all sorts of things, and call this 'seeing'; without any of these three kinds of light, they say they cannot see at all."

Commentary: This clarifies that what was taught in the past and what is taught now differ in being provisional versus ultimate. The Buddha tells Ānanda: my earlier teaching that consciousness arises from four conditions was a temporary, skillful expedient, teaching worldly cause-and-condition in order to draw students of the Lesser Vehicle away from the poison of non-Buddhist doctrine. Since those non-Buddhist teachers falsely held to spontaneity, I taught cause and condition specifically to refute that view — this is not at all the same as what is taught today, the ultimate truth, the complete meaning of cultivation and realization. How, then, could that earlier teaching be used to challenge this present one?

This passage distinguishes the present teaching from the earlier one on cause and condition. Hence the question: ordinary people in the world say, "I am able to see" — taking themselves as the one who sees and objects as what is seen, a belief shared by every sentient being. The question that follows — what, exactly, counts as "seeing," and what as "not seeing" — is meant to probe whether this depends on some condition or not. Ānanda's answer — that seeing occurs only by means of the light of sun, moon, or lamp, and that without light there is no seeing at all — states plainly his assumption that seeing necessarily depends on some condition. Though he mentions only the condition of light here, this stands for the conditions of space, mind, and eye as well — none of which, he assumes, can be lacking for seeing to occur. This was the provisional teaching of the past. What Ānanda states is, on its own terms, correct enough — but what is taught today is the ultimate truth: as the very first display of seeing already showed, a blind person perceiving darkness sees no differently from a sighted person sitting in a dark room — the nature of seeing, free of both sense-faculty and sense-object, shines with its own independent light — what need, then, could it possibly have of the condition of light at all? This is exactly the difference between the provisional and the ultimate teaching.

〖經〗阿難！若無明時，名不見者，應不見暗！若必見暗，此但無明，云何無見？阿難！若在暗時，不見明故，名為不見；今在明時，不見暗相，還名不見！如是二相，俱名不見。若復二相，自相陵奪，非汝見性，於中暫無。如是則知，二俱名見，云何不見？

〖講〗此顯無明不是無見。若如汝謂，無明時即謂無見者，應當併暗亦不見，方可謂之無見；但無明便謂無見，乃常情所執，故以應不見暗詰之，斷無是理。若必見暗下，申其正理。謂若無明時，必能見暗者：此但是明無，而見不無，云何汝說無見？

此明、暗相例，以致二皆不見，豈不大謬！此結申正義。上科但是因謬反顯，決無二俱不見之理，故申以正義曰：若復明暗二相，自相侵陵傾奪，明來暗去，暗生明滅，明暗二相，時有時無，非汝見性，於中暫時或無。此句須著眼，明暗有生滅，見性非有無，明來見明，暗來見暗。非暫無即常有，正顯見性常住，不生不滅，不藉因不託緣也。

世間常情，惟許見明有見，不許見暗成見，權教亦須具足空、明、心、眼四緣，方能成見。此經為了義真詮，但取暗中有見，故曰如是則知，見明見暗，二俱名見，云何不見？是則暗中之見，尚不用眼，何假空明，及分別之心耶？是則顯一暗中之見，則四緣俱破矣。

Sūtra: "Ānanda, if the absence of light is what you call 'not seeing,' then you ought not to be able to see darkness either! But if you can indeed see darkness, then this is merely the absence of light — how could you call this 'no seeing' at all? Ānanda, if, in the dark, not seeing brightness is called 'not seeing,' then, in the light, not seeing the mark of darkness ought equally to be called 'not seeing' — so that both conditions alike would have to be called 'not seeing.' But if these two marks, brightness and darkness, simply displace one another in turn, this seeing of yours is never, even for a moment, actually absent in either case. This being so, you should know that both conditions are, in truth, called 'seeing' — how, then, could either be called 'not seeing' at all?"

Commentary: This shows that the absence of light does not mean the absence of seeing. If, as you claim, the absence of light means the absence of seeing, then one ought equally to fail to see darkness itself, before this could truly be called "no seeing" — yet the common assumption is simply that the absence of light means the absence of seeing, and so the Buddha challenges this with "you ought not to be able to see darkness either," a claim that plainly cannot hold. From "but if you can indeed see darkness" onward, the correct principle is stated directly: if, in the absence of light, one can indeed see darkness, then this is merely the absence of light, while seeing itself is by no means absent — how, then, could you claim there is "no seeing" at all?

Applying the same logic to both brightness and darkness leads to the absurd conclusion that neither counts as seeing at all — a serious error indeed! This next passage concludes with the correct principle stated plainly. The previous point merely demonstrated the error by way of reversed reasoning — there is certainly no sense in which both conditions could be called "not seeing" at all — so the correct principle follows: if brightness and darkness, these two marks, simply displace one another in turn — brightness arriving as darkness departs, darkness arising as brightness ceases — these two marks being present at one moment and absent at the next, your own nature of seeing is never, even momentarily, absent in either case. This line deserves close attention: brightness and darkness have their own arising and ceasing, while the nature of seeing itself has neither presence nor absence — brightness arriving is met with seeing brightness, darkness arriving is met with seeing darkness. Never being momentarily absent means being constantly present — this directly reveals that the nature of seeing is ever-abiding, unborn, unceasing, dependent on no cause and reliant on no condition at all.

Ordinary worldly assumption allows only that seeing brightness counts as seeing, never that seeing darkness could count as seeing; even the provisional teaching still requires all four conditions — space, light, mind, and eye — before seeing can occur at all. This sūtra, being the complete and ultimate teaching, takes instead the fact that there is seeing even in darkness as its proof — hence: this being so, you should know that both seeing brightness and seeing darkness are, in truth, called "seeing" — how, then, could either be called "not seeing" at all? This means: seeing in the dark does not even require the eye at all — how much less could it require space, light, or the discriminating mind? Revealing this single instance of seeing in darkness is, by itself, enough to refute all four conditions at once.

〖經〗是故阿難！汝今當知：見明之時，見非是明；見暗之時，見非是暗；見空之時，見非是空；見塞之時，見非是塞。四義成就。汝復應知：見見之時，見非是見；見猶離見，見不能及。云何復說因緣、自然，及和合相？

〖講〗此顯今教為第一義。然有淺、深兩重，先顯離緣第一義。蓋見性離緣之義，自盲人矚暗，顯見是心以來，不動、不滅、不失、無還、不雜、無礙諸科，不分前半，所顯皆是離緣之見，此見即是見精。前文所云：「此見雖非妙精明心，如第二月，非是月影。」喻如第二月者，以其帶妄之故；雖然帶妄，自體離緣，已自超乎因緣宗矣！是故二字，承上是明暗二俱成見之故。汝今當知：見明之時，此之見精，非是因明所有；見暗之時，此之見精，亦非因暗所生；見空見塞，可以例知，則此見精，不屬因緣明矣。

此例顯離見第一義。即顯妙精明見性，喻如天上真月，此節較上更深一層。四義成就者：即指上面見精，非明、暗、空、塞四緣所有之義，已成就離緣第一義，此理極成，無能破者。尚有離見第一義，汝復應知，若但悟見體離緣，而未見見體，尚非圓悟，必見見體離見，乃真見見體矣，故以離緣為例焉。

見見之時，見非是見者：一、三見字，是純真無妄，本體之真見，即妙精明心，如第一月；二、四見字，是所帶一分無明之妄見，即見精明元，如第二月。無始時來，此之真見，常墮妄見之中，不能見妄，如人落水，不見乎水。若觀行力強，脫黏內伏，伏歸元真，發本明耀，則真見現前，即能徹見妄體，正當真見忽見妄見之時，真見即離自體中一分妄見，而不墮在妄中，如人覺夢，夢便不在，即已離夢中矣，此之真見，非是帶妄之見也。

見猶離見，見不能及者：第一見字是真見，二、三見字皆帶妄之見精，言此真見，猶離於見精之自相，見精亦所不能及也。良以有妄見時，真見全隱，及至棄生滅，守真常，常光現前，即真見現前時，則妄見已空，故曰見精所不能及也。

云何復說因緣自然及和合相者：此責其執吝昔宗，不肯放捨，今離見第一義，尚非離緣第一義所可及，況世間戲論因緣、自然耶？此約見性離見論，顯其自相亦離，轉入純真無妄，亦四義中，常住妙明之義。

Sūtra: "This is exactly why, Ānanda, you should now understand: at the moment of seeing brightness, the seeing itself is not brightness; at the moment of seeing darkness, the seeing itself is not darkness; at the moment of seeing emptiness, the seeing itself is not emptiness; at the moment of seeing obstruction, the seeing itself is not obstruction — these four points established. You should further understand: at the very moment of seeing seeing itself, that seeing is not itself an act of seeing either — seeing forever eludes being seen, seeing can never be reached this way. How, then, could one still speak of causes and conditions, of spontaneity, or of any state of 'combination' at all?"

Commentary: This shows that the present teaching is the ultimate truth, in two successive depths — first, the ultimate truth of being free of conditions. The meaning of seeing being free of any condition has already been established, from the very first demonstration that a blind person can still perceive darkness onward — through the displays of seeing unmoving, imperishable, never lost, having nowhere to return, unmixed with objects, and unobstructed — all of the first half of these displays reveal exactly this: seeing free of any condition; and this "seeing" is precisely the essence of seeing itself. As stated earlier: "though this seeing is not yet the wondrous, essential, luminous mind itself, it is like the second moon — not a mere watery reflection of the moon." It is likened to the second moon precisely because it still carries a trace of falseness — yet even carrying this falseness, its own substance already stands free of any condition, already surpassing the entire school of cause and condition! "This is exactly why" picks up directly from the point that both brightness and darkness alike are met with seeing. You should now understand: at the moment of seeing brightness, this very essence of seeing is not something brightness itself possesses as its cause; at the moment of seeing darkness, this same essence of seeing is likewise not something produced by darkness. The same reasoning applies to seeing emptiness and seeing obstruction — from which it follows clearly that this essence of seeing belongs to no cause or condition whatsoever.

This next passage extends the argument to reveal the ultimate truth of moving beyond seeing itself — revealing the wondrous, essential, bright nature of seeing, likened here to the true moon in the sky itself; this section goes a full level deeper than the one before. "These four points established" refers back to the point just made — that the essence of seeing belongs to none of the four conditions, brightness, darkness, emptiness, or obstruction — this establishes the ultimate truth of freedom from condition, a point now fully settled, beyond any possible refutation. Yet there remains a further ultimate truth still: moving beyond seeing itself. You should further understand this: if one has only awakened to the substance of seeing being free of condition, without yet truly seeing that very substance of seeing, this is not yet complete, perfect awakening — one must see that the substance of seeing itself moves beyond seeing, only then truly seeing that substance directly — hence freedom from condition is offered here only as a stepping stone toward this further point.

"At the very moment of seeing seeing itself, that seeing is not itself an act of seeing": the first and third occurrences of "seeing" here refer to the purely true, undefiled seeing, the very substance of the wondrous, essential, luminous mind — the first, true moon; the second and fourth occurrences refer to the seeing that still carries a portion of ignorance, the essence of seeing's own luminous root — the second moon. Since beginningless time, this true seeing has constantly sunk beneath this false seeing, unable to perceive that very falseness — exactly like a person who has fallen into water and cannot see the water itself. But should the strength of one's contemplative practice grow sufficient, freeing itself from clinging and settling inward, returning to its own original truth, its own inherent brightness shining forth once more — then true seeing appears directly before one, able to see through this false substance entirely; and exactly at the moment true seeing suddenly perceives false seeing, true seeing has already become free of this one portion of false seeing carried within its own substance, no longer sunk within that falseness at all — exactly like a person waking from a dream, the dream itself no longer present, already free of the dream entirely. This true seeing, then, is no longer the seeing that still carries falseness.

"Seeing forever eludes being seen, seeing can never be reached this way": the first occurrence of "seeing" here refers to true seeing; the second and third occurrences both refer to the essence of seeing that still carries falseness. This means: this true seeing forever eludes even the essence of seeing's own characteristic — the essence of seeing itself cannot reach it at all. This is because, at the time false seeing is present, true seeing remains entirely hidden; only once one abandons what arises and ceases, holds fast to what is truly constant, and the ever-present light appears — that is, at the very moment true seeing itself appears — does false seeing become entirely empty; hence, "the essence of seeing cannot reach it at all."

"How, then, could one still speak of causes and conditions, of spontaneity, or of any state of 'combination' at all?" This rebukes Ānanda for still clinging to his earlier school of thought, unwilling to let it go. Even this ultimate truth of moving beyond seeing itself already surpasses the ultimate truth of mere freedom from condition — how much more, then, does it surpass the idle worldly debates of cause and condition, or spontaneity? This display, discussing the nature of seeing as moving beyond seeing itself, shows that even seeing's own characteristic is left behind, entering entirely into pure, undefiled truth — this too belongs, among the four meanings, to the meaning of ever-abiding, wondrous brightness.

〖經〗汝等聲聞，狹劣無識，不能通達，清淨實相。吾今誨汝，當善思惟，無得疲怠，妙菩提路。

〖講〗此佛責其滯小，勉其向大。聲聞兼有學無學。謂汝等見狹志劣，無有廣大殊勝之識，執諸法為實有，不了依他如幻，妄起遍計執情，迷於清淨實相之理，故曰不能通達。實相有三：一、如實空義，此為無相之實相；二、如實不空義，此為無不相之實相；三、如實空不空義，此為無相無不相之實相。

吾今誨汝下：如來欲阿難悟明實相之理，故勗以三慧。誨汝者，有誨必聞，聞慧也；善思惟者，正念觀察，非緣心思慮，思慧也；無得疲怠者：無得因循悠忽，自謂疲勞，遂生懈怠，必須精勤進趣，未可半途或廢，修慧也。妙菩提路者：即佛所證，無上菩提之道路，乃從凡至聖，中間所經五十五位，真菩提路也。

Sūtra: "You śrāvakas are narrow in view and limited in insight, unable to penetrate the pure, true reality of things. I now teach you: reflect on this well, and do not grow weary or slack on the wondrous path to bodhi."

Commentary: Here the Buddha rebukes Ānanda for remaining stuck at the lesser vehicle, and urges him instead toward the greater. "Śrāvakas" here includes both those still in training and those beyond it. The point: your own view is narrow, your resolve limited, lacking any vast, excellent insight — clinging to phenomena as though they truly existed, failing to understand that dependently-arisen things are illusory, giving rise instead to universal, clinging attachment, and so remaining deluded about the principle of pure, true reality — hence, "unable to penetrate" it. True reality has three aspects: the meaning of true emptiness, the true reality free of any characteristic; the meaning of true non-emptiness, the true reality with no characteristic it lacks; and the meaning of true emptiness-and-non-emptiness together, the true reality that is neither confined to any characteristic nor lacking any either.

From "I now teach you" onward: the Tathāgata, wishing Ānanda to awaken clearly to the principle of true reality, urges him toward the three kinds of wisdom. "I teach you" implies that, once taught, one must truly listen — this is the wisdom of learning; "reflect on this well" means correct, mindful examination, not mere conditioned, discursive thinking — this is the wisdom of reflection; "do not grow weary or slack" means one must not drift along carelessly, calling oneself tired and so growing lax, but must instead advance with diligent, sustained effort, never abandoning the task halfway — this is the wisdom of cultivation. "The wondrous path to bodhi" refers to the very path of unsurpassed bodhi realized by the Buddha himself — the true path to bodhi, traversing the fifty-five stages from ordinary being all the way to sagehood.

Reading note: Yuanying's closing summary of all ten displays, condensing each into a single line, is worth reproducing in full as a map of the whole sequence: (1) free of both sense-faculty and sense-object, its own spiritual light shining independently; (2) free of both body and surroundings, settled and utterly unmoved; (3) reaching to the very end of future time, ultimately ever-abiding and imperishable; (4) from beginningless time, though seemingly turned upside-down, never actually lost; (5) going nowhere, returning nowhere, standing apart from every object as something ever-abiding; (6) unmixed, unconfused, rising above every appearance as something wholly its own; (7) whether perceiving something vast or something small, turning objects freely and without any obstruction; (8) with neither "is" nor "is not," the discriminating feelings born of true and false dissolving of themselves at the sight of it; (9) falling into none of these ordinary conceptions, standing far beyond every non-Buddhist speculation and every provisional teaching; (10) even its own characteristic left behind, entering entirely into pure, undefiled truth. "Revealing seeing has, by this point," Yuanying writes, "truly reached the very limit of what revealing can accomplish."

Ānanda's Doubled Confusion, and the Buddha's Promise 寅二 剖妄出真

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！如佛世尊，為我等輩，宣說因緣，及與自然；諸和合相，與不和合，心猶未開。而今更聞，見見非見，重增迷悶！伏願弘慈，施大慧目，開示我等，覺心明淨。作是語已，悲淚頂禮，承受聖旨。

〖講〗此述其迷悶。謂世尊為我等輩，於顯見超情科中，宣說因緣及與自然，二者俱非之義，更加重重迭拂，是我已聞；而諸和合相與不和合，是我未聞，心中猶未開通。而今更聞見見非見，重增迷悶者：因未聞和合不和合之義，已是迷悶，而今更聞見見非見，此理不明，重增一重迷悶，急於求示。

上述迷，此請示。伏願如來，發大慈心，賜我大慧。此大慧目，即奢摩他，微密觀照，稱正因之理，所起了因之慧；開示我等覺心，令得明淨。覺心即本覺真心，亦即根中見性。二妄未除，迷悶未釋，則迷雲悶霧，重重籠罩，覺天心地，不得明淨，故求開示。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, though the World-Honored One has taught us about cause and condition and about spontaneity, I have not yet heard anything about combination or non-combination, and my mind remains unopened on this point. And now, hearing further that 'seeing seeing itself is not seeing,' my confusion has only doubled! I beg only that you, in your vast compassion, grant us the eye of great wisdom, and open within us a mind that is awakened, bright, and pure." Having said this, he wept and bowed in reverence, receiving the sacred instruction.

Commentary: This describes Ānanda's own confusion. His point: the World-Honored One, in the display showing seeing surpassing every category, taught that both cause-and-condition and spontaneity are equally mistaken, and negated each repeatedly, layer upon layer — this much I have already heard; but of any teaching on "combination" or "non-combination," I have heard nothing at all, and my mind remains unopened on this particular point. "And now, hearing further that 'seeing seeing itself is not seeing,' my confusion has only doubled" means: not yet having heard the teaching on combination and non-combination was already a source of confusion, and now, hearing further that "seeing seeing itself is not seeing" without fully understanding it, this adds a second layer of confusion on top of the first — hence his urgent request for further instruction.

The passage above describes his confusion; this passage is his actual request. He begs the Tathāgata to bring forth great compassion and grant him great wisdom. This "eye of great wisdom" refers exactly to śamatha's own subtle, hidden contemplation — the wisdom of direct understanding arising from the very principle of correct causation itself — opening within Ānanda and the assembly an awakened mind, bright and pure. "Awakened mind" refers to the true mind of original awakening itself, exactly the nature of seeing within the sense-root. So long as the two kinds of false seeing remain unremoved and this confusion unresolved, clouds of delusion and fog of confusion continue to blanket the very sky and ground of awakened mind, layer upon layer, never allowed to become bright and pure — hence this urgent request for further instruction.

〖經〗爾時世尊，憐愍阿難，及諸大眾，將欲敷演，大陀羅尼，諸三摩提，妙修行路。告阿難言：汝雖強記，但益多聞，於奢摩他，微密觀照，心猶未了，汝今諦聽，吾當為汝，分別開示。亦令將來，諸有漏者，獲菩提果。

〖講〗此經家敘佛，將示妙悟妙修，令得圓證。以當機聞說見見非見，重增迷悶，故於爾時佛生憐愍。憐其智劣，愍其未悟，既然未開圓解，不能遽起妙修。大陀羅尼，此云大總持：總一切法，持無量義。約因，即眾生所具，圓湛不生滅之見性；約果，即首楞嚴王大定，總持百千三昧；約因果同時，即自性天然本定。諸三摩提，乃躡解所起自利之行，即二十五聖圓通法門。妙修行路：乃依行所歷之位，即五十五位真菩提路。

此寄責多聞，未開真智。告阿難言：汝雖有強記之力，但增益多聞而已，於奢摩他自性本定之真理，所起微密觀照，朗然照體之真智，心中猶未了悟，是則真智未開，不能遽示妙修，必先開圓解，為當務之急也。阿難但知見性是真，而不知所以真；復聞見性是妄，而不知所以妄。宜乎迷悶，而心未了。故警之曰：「汝今自當諦實而聽，依教觀心，由言達理，吾當為汝分別真見、妄見，開示奢摩他，微密觀照之義。」

此兼益未來。承上佛為分別見見非見，則真、妄分明。依真智照真理，修微密觀照，不特利益現會，亦令將來有漏凡夫，及有學二乘，依此奢摩他微密觀照之圓解，而起從根解結之圓修，得證圓滿菩提，無上極果。有漏尚然，無漏可知。

Sūtra: At that time the World-Honored One, taking pity on Ānanda and the whole assembly, was about to unfold the great dhāraṇī, the various samādhis, and the wondrous path of cultivation. He said to Ānanda: "Though you have great powers of memory, this has only added to your learning — you have still not fully grasped the subtle, hidden contemplation of śamatha itself. Listen closely now, and I will explain this for you in detail, distinguishing each point clearly — so that, in the future, all those still subject to outflows may likewise attain the fruit of bodhi."

Commentary: This is the compiler's own account of the Buddha now preparing to reveal wondrous awakening and wondrous cultivation, so that Ānanda might attain complete realization. Hearing that "seeing seeing itself is not seeing" only doubled Ānanda's confusion, the Buddha, at that very moment, gives rise to compassion — pitying his limited wisdom, taking pity on his lack of awakening; since he has not yet opened up a complete, perfect understanding, he cannot yet be shown wondrous cultivation directly. "The great dhāraṇī" means "the great, all-encompassing holding": encompassing every phenomenon, holding boundless meaning within it. In terms of cause, this is exactly the perfectly full, unborn, unceasing nature of seeing every sentient being already possesses; in terms of fruit, this is exactly the great Śūraṅgama samādhi, encompassing hundreds and thousands of samādhis within it; in terms of cause and fruit being simultaneous, this is exactly one's own nature's inherent, natural samādhi. "The various samādhis" refers to the self-benefiting practice that arises from this same understanding — exactly the twenty-five sagely methods of perfect penetration. "The wondrous path of cultivation" refers to the stages traversed through this practice — exactly the fifty-five stages of the true path to bodhi.

This gently reproaches Ānanda for relying on broad learning without yet opening genuine wisdom. The Buddha tells Ānanda: though you possess a powerful memory, this has only ever added to your learning — with regard to the true principle of śamatha, one's own naturally settled samādhi, and the subtle, hidden contemplation that arises from it, the genuine wisdom that directly illuminates its own substance, your mind has still not truly awakened to it. Since genuine wisdom has not yet opened within you, you cannot yet be shown wondrous cultivation directly — opening a complete, perfect understanding must come first, as the most urgent matter at hand. Ānanda knows only that the nature of seeing is true, without knowing why it is true; and, hearing further that this same seeing carries falseness, does not know why it is false either — no wonder, then, that confusion remains, and his mind has not yet fully grasped it. Hence the Buddha's warning: you must now listen with complete sincerity, observing your own mind by way of this teaching, reaching the underlying principle through these very words — I will now distinguish for you true seeing from false seeing, and reveal the meaning of śamatha's own subtle, hidden contemplation.

This next passage extends the benefit to future generations as well. Continuing from the point that the Buddha is about to distinguish "seeing seeing itself is not seeing" so that true and false become perfectly clear: relying on genuine wisdom to illuminate genuine principle, cultivating this subtle, hidden contemplation, benefits not only this present assembly, but also every ordinary being still subject to outflows, and every practitioner still in training within the two lesser vehicles, in ages yet to come — that they, too, relying on this same complete understanding of śamatha's subtle, hidden contemplation, may give rise to the complete cultivation that unties the very knot at its root, and thereby realize perfect, complete bodhi, the unsurpassed ultimate fruit. If this holds true even for those still subject to outflows, how much more so for those already free of them.

Individual-Karma False Seeing, and Shared-Karma False Seeing 別業妄見 · 同分妄見

The Buddha now distinguishes the two kinds of false seeing carried within the essence of seeing itself, completing the whole division of the sūtra devoted to pointing directly at the sense-faculty's nature. These sūtra passages have already been translated in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 2 ("Two Kinds of False Seeing"); that exact English is reused below, interleaved with Yuanying's commentary.

〖經〗阿難！一切眾生輪迴世間，由二顛倒分別見妄，當處發生，當業輪轉。云何二見？一者、眾生別業妄見，二者、眾生同分妄見。

〖講〗此示上文所顯，帶妄之見精，乃有二種見妄。一切眾生：不獨六凡，亦兼小乘、權教。輪迴世間：但指依、正二種世間，與二種見妄相合，而同分妄見，只是自惑所現之依、正，不假業招；而別業妄見，不獨惑現，亦兼業招。當處出生句，屬同分；當業輪轉句，屬別業。應知生死大患，其故皆由二種見妄。

由二顛倒者：即是迷真起妄。吾人現在身境，乃親近之依、正，本是惑、業所現，自己別業之虛影，迷而不知，妄生分別，以為心外實有同他共住同見之境，此為別業妄見。所有眾生世界，乃疏遠之依、正，本是自惑所現，與眾同分之虛影，亦迷不知，妄生分別，以為心外實有，與己本身無干之境，此為同分妄見。

當處發生者：不離本處發生也，本處即指真心，謂此見妄無別所依，親依真起，一切妄境皆從妄見所現，妄不離真。當業輪轉者：不離業而輪轉也，即於惑境中，不了心現，妄執實有，依惑起業，依業受報，而成無邊輪轉。

別業者：業別別見，見其自所住持，現得受用，依、正親近之境，不惟惑現，更由業招，但自業發明，還自取著，顛倒分別，視為心外實有，與他同住共見之境，故曰別業妄見。同分者：惑同同見，見其他所住持，非己受用，世界疏遠之境，雖非業招，亦由惑現，但與眾惑同，還同眾見，顛倒分別，視為心外實有，與己本身無干之境，故曰同分妄見。

Sūtra: "Ānanda, all sentient beings revolve through the cycle of the world because of two kinds of inverted, discriminating false seeing, each arising in its own proper place, each turning according to its own karma. What are these two kinds of seeing? First, sentient beings' individual-karma false seeing. Second, sentient beings' shared-karma false seeing."

Commentary: This reveals that the essence of seeing described above, still carrying a trace of falseness, actually contains two distinct kinds of false seeing. "All sentient beings" includes not only ordinary beings of the six destinies, but the lesser vehicle and provisional-teaching practitioners as well. "Revolve through the cycle of the world" refers to both kinds of worldly existence — the being itself and its surrounding environment — corresponding to these same two kinds of false seeing: shared-karma false seeing is simply the being's own surrounding environment as manifested by delusion alone, without any karmic summons involved; individual-karma false seeing, by contrast, involves not only delusion but karmic summons as well. "Each arising in its own proper place" corresponds to shared-karma seeing; "each turning according to its own karma" corresponds to individual-karma seeing. One should understand that the great affliction of birth and death traces back, in every case, to these same two kinds of false seeing.

"Two kinds of inverted [seeing]" means exactly this: being deluded about the truth, one gives rise to falseness. One's own present body and immediate surroundings — the being and environment closest at hand — are originally nothing but the empty projection of one's own individual karma, manifested through delusion and karma together; deluded about this, without realizing it, one falsely gives rise to discrimination, taking it to be something genuinely existing outside the mind, a world shared and jointly witnessed together with others — this is individual-karma false seeing. Every world and every being more broadly — the being and environment further removed — is originally nothing but the empty projection of one's own delusion, shared in common with the whole community; equally deluded about this, without realizing it, one falsely gives rise to discrimination, taking it to be something genuinely existing outside the mind, a world entirely unrelated to one's own person — this is shared-karma false seeing.

"Each arising in its own proper place" means arising nowhere apart from its own true origin — that origin being the true mind itself; this false seeing has no other basis at all, arising directly from truth itself, so that every false appearance manifests from false seeing alone, falseness never actually apart from truth. "Each turning according to its own karma" means turning nowhere apart from karma itself — within this deluded state, failing to recognize that it is mind's own manifestation, one falsely clings to it as genuinely real, gives rise to karma in dependence on this delusion, and receives retribution in dependence on that karma, forming an endless cycle of turning.

"Individual karma" means karma that differs from person to person, producing correspondingly different seeing: each being witnesses the particular environment they themselves inhabit and make use of, both their own being and its immediate surroundings — not merely manifested by delusion, but further summoned by their own individual karma; manifested by their own karma, and then clung to by that same self, discriminated in an upside-down way, taken to be something genuinely existing outside the mind, a world shared and jointly witnessed together with others — hence, individual-karma false seeing. "Shared karma" means karma shared in common, producing correspondingly shared seeing: witnessing the environment inhabited by others, not one's own to make use of, the more distant reaches of the world — though not summoned by one's own individual karma, still manifested through delusion, shared in common with the delusion of the whole community, and so shared, too, in the community's own seeing, discriminated in an upside-down way, taken to be something genuinely existing outside the mind, a world entirely unrelated to one's own person — hence, shared-karma false seeing.

〖經〗云何名為別業妄見？阿難！如世間人目有赤眚，夜見燈光別有圓影五色重疊。……是故當知色實在燈，見病為影；影、見俱眚，見眚非病。……此亦如是，目眚所成，今欲名誰是燈、是見？何況分別非燈、非見！

〖講〗如字舉例之辭；世間人指凡、外、權、小；目有赤眚，例無明見病；夜指迷位；燈指藏性；五色圓影，例眾生五蘊幻軀，世界五塵幻境。因眾生有能見之妄見，故有所見之身、心、世界，見妄若除，身界叵得。此舉別業中之別業，易知之法，例彼別業難知之法，令難知者，亦易知也。

見性本來清淨，從來無病，為無明所熏，黏湛發見，轉本有智光，為能見之妄見，此屬根本見病；見精映色，結色成根，遂有勝義根、浮塵根，赤眚依浮塵根而起，屬枝末見病。是故當知：色實在燈，非燈不現故；見因病為影，非病無影故。影見俱眚者：燈影與見病俱因目眚之故，非但所見燈影是赤眚所生，即能見見病，亦赤眚所成，故合影與見，同是眚為過咎。見眚非病者：此見是真見，不墮在眚妄之中，故能見於眚。既能見眚，即已離眚，如人既已覺夢，即已離夢，所以非病，正由有此無病見體，故前云：「見見非見」。

Sūtra: "What is meant by individual-karma false seeing? Ānanda, it is like a person in the world whose eye has a red inflammation, who at night, looking at a lamp's flame, sees a separate, layered halo of five colors around it. ... You should therefore know that the color truly belongs to the lamp; it is the eye disease that produces the halo. Both the halo and the seeing of it belong to the eye disease — the seeing of the disease is not itself the disease. ... It is the same here: it is produced by the eye's own affliction. Now, what should we call 'the lamp' and what should we call 'the seeing' in this? How much less could we go on to distinguish a 'not-lamp' and a 'not-seeing'!"

Commentary: "It is like" introduces this as an illustrative example; "a person in the world" refers to ordinary beings, non-Buddhists, and provisional or lesser-vehicle practitioners alike; "an eye with a red inflammation" illustrates the disease of ignorance-affected seeing; "at night" stands for the state of delusion; "the lamp" stands for the tathāgatagarbha nature itself; "the layered, five-colored halo" stands for a sentient being's own illusory body of five aggregates, and the world's own illusory realm of five sense-objects. Because sentient beings possess this false, seeing capacity, there comes to be a corresponding body, mind, and world that is seen — remove this false seeing, and neither body nor world can be found at all. This offers the more easily understood case within individual-karma seeing — one person's own case — as an illustration for the harder-to-grasp case of individual-karma seeing more broadly, making what is difficult to understand easy to grasp instead.

The nature of seeing is, from the very start, pure, never actually diseased at all; permeated by ignorance, it clings to what should remain still and gives rise to seeing instead, turning its own originally possessed light of wisdom into a false, seeing capacity — this belongs to the root disease of seeing. The essence of seeing then reflects onto form, congealing that form into the sense-faculty itself, giving rise to both the superior and the coarse sense-organs; the red inflammation, arising in dependence on this coarse sense-organ, belongs to the branch disease of seeing. This is exactly why one should know: the color truly belongs to the lamp, since without the lamp it would not appear at all; the halo is produced because of the eye's own disease, since without that disease there would be no halo at all. "Both the halo and the seeing of it belong to the eye disease" means: both the lamp's own halo and the disease of seeing itself trace back to this same eye affliction — not only is the halo seen produced by this red inflammation, but the very capacity to see it is likewise produced by that same inflammation, so that both the halo and the seeing of it share this same underlying fault. "The seeing of the disease is not itself the disease" means: this act of seeing the disease is itself the true seeing, which never sinks into the disease's own falseness — and it is precisely because it can see the disease that it has, in that very act, already become free of it, exactly as a person who has already awakened from a dream has, in that very waking, already become free of the dream — hence, "not itself the disease." It is precisely because this undiseased substance of seeing exists at all that the earlier teaching could say, "seeing seeing itself is not seeing."

〖經〗云何名為同分妄見？阿難！此閻浮提……若復此中有一小洲，只有兩國。唯一國人同感惡緣，則彼小洲當土眾生，覩諸一切不祥境界，或見二日，或見兩月，……種種惡相，但此國見；彼國眾生，本所不見，亦復不聞。

〖講〗此舉同分中之別分，易知之法，例彼同分難知之法，令難知者，亦易知也。別舉此閻浮提洲中，有一小洲，只有兩國，以有兩國，所見不同，方可驗知同分妄見。惟一國人同感惡緣者：兩國同洲，心行不同，惟獨一國人，同感惡緣。注意「感」字，乃依因感果，感應不忒，由妄惑為能感之惡因，而災象為所感之惡緣。故彼小洲當土眾生，依妄惑妄現，種種不祥境界，為與本國眾生同見，鄰國不見，故知乃由妄惑，妄現咎徵，惟應此國，不應彼國。

但此國見，彼國眾生，本所不見，亦復不聞：同一洲中，天原是一象，分有無，足知非實。前必取兩國者，以一國不足以顯妄，若兩國同見，亦不足以顯妄，故曰：「但此國見，彼國眾生，本所不見。」雖此國惑同同見，畢竟非實。

Sūtra: "What is meant by shared-karma false seeing? Ānanda, in this continent of Jambudvīpa ... suppose there were, within it, a small island with only two countries on it, and the people of just one of those countries, together, had incurred some shared evil condition. Then the beings native to that small island alone would witness all manner of inauspicious signs: some seeing two suns, some seeing two moons ... All these various evil portents would be seen only by that one country; the beings of the other country would, from the start, neither see nor hear anything of the kind."

Commentary: This offers the more easily understood case within shared-karma seeing — a limited, particular instance — as an illustration for the harder-to-grasp case of shared-karma seeing more broadly, making what is difficult to understand easy to grasp instead. It specifically posits, within this continent of Jambudvīpa, a small island containing only two countries — since these two countries see differently from one another, this difference can serve as proof of shared-karma false seeing. "The people of just one of those countries, together, had incurred some shared evil condition": though sharing the same island, the two countries' own conduct and karma differ; only the people of one country, together, incur this shared evil condition. Note carefully the word "incur" — this describes a cause bringing about its corresponding effect, response never failing to match its cause: false delusion serves as the evil cause that incurs this condition, and the ominous portent itself is the evil condition thereby incurred. Hence, the beings native to that small island, manifesting these various inauspicious signs through their own false delusion, are seen in common by the people of their own country, while the neighboring country sees nothing at all — proving that this false delusion, manifesting this ominous portent falsely, applies only to this one country, and not to the other.

"Seen only by that one country; the beings of the other country would, from the start, neither see nor hear anything of the kind": within this same single island, the sky is, in reality, one and the same — yet it is seen by one country and not by the other, proof enough that it is not truly real at all. The reason two countries are specifically required here: a single country alone would not be enough to demonstrate falseness, and yet if both countries saw the same thing, this too would fail to demonstrate falseness — hence, "seen only by that one country; the beings of the other country would, from the start, [see] nothing of the kind." Though this one country shares this same delusion and sees it in common, it is, in the end, by no means truly real at all.

〖經〗阿難！如彼眾生別業妄見……例汝今日，以目觀見山河國土及諸眾生，皆是無始見病所成。見與見緣似現前境，元我覺明見所緣眚。覺見即眚，本覺明心覺緣非眚；覺所覺眚，覺非眚中，此實見見，云何復名覺、聞、知、見？……阿難！如彼眾生同分妄見……例閻浮提三千洲中，兼四大海娑婆世界，并洎十方諸有漏國及諸眾生，同是覺明無漏妙心，見、聞、覺、知虛妄病緣、和合妄生，和合妄死。若能遠離諸和合緣及不和合，則復滅除諸生死因；圓滿菩提不生滅性，清淨本心本覺常住。」

〖講〗此舉所例法。例者同一例也，進前眚目見圓影，易知之別業，例今好眼見身界，難知之別業。今日目觀者，就今眼前親住親見之近境，山河國土及諸眾生，即所見身界，皆是無始根本見病所成之影，與圓影枝末見病之影，同一例也。

見即見分，合上以目觀見；見緣即見所緣之相分，合上國土眾生。此見、相二分，依自證分而起，屬依他起性，非有似有，故曰似現前境。詰其根本，元我真覺墮在妄明之中，以為其咎。見所緣眚者：見即轉相之見分，所緣即現相之相分，皆由無明之力，轉真見成妄見，此見即眚見，遂有所緣依、正之眚影。覺見即眚者：非但所緣是眚，即覺明所發之能見，亦即是眚，以俱依無明而有，妄體本無。本覺明心，覺緣非眚者：此明真體非病，謂本覺妙明真心，遍覺能、所二緣皆妄，此覺體自不墮妄中，實非有眚妄見可比，故曰「非眚」。

覺所覺眚，覺非眚中：一、三覺字是真體，所覺即能緣所緣，由無明所熏，知見妄發見分，發妄不息，勞見發塵相分，悉皆如眚，真覺未覺時，常墮眚中，一覺所覺是眚，則此真覺，即已離眚，非墮眚中矣！此實見見：即前真見見於見精帶妄時，真見非墮於見精妄中，彼此意義相類。云何復名覺、聞、知、見：見性即是寂常心性，所應取為本修因者，云何復將此妙覺明性，名為覺、聞、知、見？覺、聞、知、見，即是六精，體同用異，「元以一精明，分作六和合」。

此對境會釋，真妄二見。是故汝今，見我及汝，並諸世間，十類眾生，皆即見眚，非見眚者：若自惑未除，縱觀如來勝相，猶是見帶眚病，非能見眚者之真見，此判是妄非真。彼見真精，性非眚者，故不名見：彼見即能見眚之見，已離於妄曰真，純一無雜曰精，性非眚者，其性不變隨緣，隨緣不變，不墮於眚妄之中，故曰「非眚」，既非是眚，故不應名見，此判真非妄。

此舉能例法退同合別。若能遠離諸和合緣及不和合，則復滅除諸生死因：緣即別業、同分所緣之境，和合緣即別業境，不但惑現，還加有業，和合而成；不和合緣，即同分境，但由惑現，並無有業和合其中。若能遠離，即微密觀照離妄之功，妄離則真自復。生死因即二種顛倒分別見妄，為輪迴之本，別業妄見屬事識，為分段生死因，同分妄見屬業識，為變易生死因，但所緣同、別二境既離，能緣本、末二妄自息，故曰：「則復滅除諸生死因」。

圓滿菩提，不生滅性，清淨本心，本覺常住：此明即覺，即離妄即真也。前二句圓滿及性字，皆要雙用，不生滅性即是涅槃，謂妄見即離，真覺全顯，即能圓滿三菩提性，及圓滿三涅槃性。清淨本心，本覺常住者：既已圓滿智、斷二果，復本心源，究竟清淨，本覺到此，方得出纏，常住不變，輪迴從此永卸矣！

Sūtra: "Ānanda, just as with that individual's karmic false seeing ... so it is with you today: your eyes' observation of mountains, rivers, lands, and all sentient beings is likewise entirely produced by a beginningless disease of seeing. Seeing, together with what seeing takes as its condition, resembles a scene appearing before us — but it is originally only the affliction that my own bright awareness takes as its condition of seeing. Awareness-in-seeing is itself the affliction; the original, bright mind of awareness, aware of its condition, is not itself afflicted. That which is aware of the afflicted-seeing-as-object is, itself, not within the affliction — this is truly 'seeing the seeing' — so how could it still be called by names like awareness, hearing, knowing, or seeing? ... Ānanda, just as with that shared karmic false seeing ... so it is throughout Jambudvīpa's three thousand continents, together with the four great oceans of this Sahā world, and throughout every impure land of the ten directions and every being within them: all alike are the bright, undefiled, wondrous mind of awareness. Seeing, hearing, awareness, and knowing arise falsely, in combination, from the false, afflicted condition, and perish falsely, in combination, as well. If one is able to remove oneself far from every condition of combination, and from non-combination too, then one thereby also eliminates every cause of birth and death, and fully realizes the unborn, unceasing nature of bodhi — the pure, original mind, original awareness, ever-abiding."

Commentary: This offers the case actually being illustrated. "Just as" signals a shared pattern: from the more easily understood individual-karma seeing of a diseased eye perceiving a halo, to the harder-to-grasp individual-karma seeing of a healthy eye perceiving body and world. "Today's own observation" refers to what stands directly before Ānanda's own eyes, intimately inhabited and directly perceived — mountains, rivers, lands, and every sentient being, that is, the body and world he sees — all of it produced by a root disease of seeing extending back to beginningless time, following exactly the same pattern as the halo produced by a branch disease of seeing.

"Seeing" here is the seeing-portion of consciousness, corresponding to "eyes' observation" above; "what seeing takes as its condition" is the perceived-portion conditioned upon by that seeing, corresponding to "lands" and "sentient beings" above. This seeing-portion and perceived-portion both arise in dependence on the self-verifying portion of consciousness, belonging to the dependently-arisen nature — not truly existing, yet appearing to exist — hence, "resembles a scene appearing before us." Tracing this back to its root: it is originally my own true awareness, having sunk into a state of falsely illuminated awareness, that becomes the very source of this fault. "The affliction that awareness takes as its condition of seeing" means: the seeing-portion, having turned from original awareness, together with the perceived-portion this seeing-portion then manifests, both arise entirely through the power of ignorance, turning true seeing into false seeing — this seeing is exactly the diseased seeing itself, giving rise, in turn, to the diseased halo-images it conditions upon, both the being and its surrounding world. "Awareness-in-seeing is itself the affliction" means: not only is what is conditioned upon afflicted, but the very capacity for seeing that this awareness gives rise to is itself afflicted as well, since both arise entirely in dependence on ignorance, their false substance originally nonexistent. "The original, bright mind of awareness, aware of its condition, is not itself afflicted" clarifies that the true substance itself is never diseased at all — meaning that the original, wondrously bright, true mind, aware that both the conditioning and the conditioned are equally false, never itself sinks into that falseness, truly incomparable to any diseased, false seeing at all — hence, "not itself the affliction."

"That which is aware of the afflicted-seeing-as-object is, itself, not within the affliction": the first and third occurrences of "awareness" here refer to the true substance itself; "aware of...as object" refers to both the conditioning and the conditioned together — permeated by ignorance, knowing and seeing falsely give rise to the seeing-portion, this falseness arising without rest, this strained seeing then giving rise to the dust-like perceived-portion — all of it exactly like the diseased halo; when true awareness has not yet awakened to this, it constantly sinks within this same disease — but the very moment it becomes aware that what it is aware of is itself diseased, this true awareness has, in that very instant, already become free of the disease, no longer sunk within it at all! "This is truly 'seeing the seeing'" refers back to the earlier point: at the very moment true seeing perceives the essence of seeing still carrying falseness, true seeing does not itself sink into that same falseness — the two points share exactly the same meaning. "How could it still be called by names like awareness, hearing, knowing, or seeing?" This very nature of seeing is exactly the still, ever-abiding nature of mind that ought to be taken up as the very cause and ground of one's own practice — why, then, should this wondrously awakened, bright nature still be called merely by the names "awareness," "hearing," "knowing," "seeing"? These four — awareness, hearing, knowing, seeing — are exactly the six essential faculties, one in substance though differing in function: "originally a single essential brightness, divided into six that function together."

This next passage applies the teaching directly to what stands before the assembly, resolving true seeing and false seeing at once. "This is exactly why, when you now see me, and see yourself, and see every one of the ten kinds of beings throughout the world, all of it is exactly seeing the disease itself, not the seeing capable of perceiving that disease": so long as one's own delusion remains unremoved, even beholding the Tathāgata's own excellent marks remains seeing still carrying this same disease, not yet the true seeing capable of perceiving the disease itself — this judges such seeing to be false, not true. "That seeing, essential and true, whose very nature is free of this disease, is for this reason not even properly called 'seeing' at all": this "that seeing" refers to the very seeing capable of perceiving the disease itself — free of falseness, it is called "true"; wholly undivided, it is called "essential"; its nature free of this disease means: its nature, unchanging while following every condition, following every condition while unchanging, never sinks into the disease's own falseness at all — hence, "free of this disease." Being free of the disease in this way, it ought no longer even to be called by the ordinary name "seeing" at all — this judges such seeing to be genuinely true, not false.

This next passage offers the corresponding case for shared-karma seeing, working backward from the previously established individual case. "If one is able to remove oneself far from every condition of combination, and from non-combination too, then one thereby also eliminates every cause of birth and death": "condition" here refers to what is conditioned upon in both individual-karma and shared-karma seeing; "combined condition" refers to the individual-karma environment, manifested not only through delusion but further compounded by karma as well; "non-combined condition" refers to the shared-karma environment, manifested through delusion alone, with no additional karma compounded within it. "Being able to remove oneself far" refers exactly to the power of subtle, hidden contemplation to free itself from falseness — once falseness is removed, truth naturally returns of its own accord. "The cause of birth and death" refers to these same two kinds of inverted, discriminating false seeing, the very root of the wheel of rebirth: individual-karma false seeing belongs to the ordinary discriminating consciousness, the cause of segment-by-segment birth and death; shared-karma false seeing belongs to karmic consciousness itself, the cause of transformational birth and death. But once these two conditioned environments, individual and shared, are both removed, the two conditioning falsenesses, root and branch, naturally cease as well — hence, "one thereby also eliminates every cause of birth and death."

"Fully realizes the unborn, unceasing nature of bodhi — the pure, original mind, original awareness, ever-abiding": this clarifies awakening itself, exactly what is meant by moving beyond falseness into truth. In the first two lines here, both "fully" and "nature" must be read twice over, applying to both terms at once — "unborn, unceasing nature" is itself nirvāṇa: the point is that, the moment false seeing is removed, true awareness appears in its entirety, and one is thereby able to fully realize both the nature of bodhi in its three aspects, and the nature of nirvāṇa in its three aspects as well. "The pure, original mind, original awareness, ever-abiding" means: having already fully realized both wisdom and severance as twin fruits, one returns to the very source of one's own mind, utterly pure — original awakening, having reached this point, is at last freed entirely from entanglement, ever-abiding and unchanging, the wheel of rebirth cast off forever from this point on!

Reading note: Yuanying closes this entire major division of the sūtra here — "克就根性直指真心" ("pointing directly at the sense-faculty's own nature to reveal the true mind"), spanning fascicles 3 through 5 — noting that up to this point the sūtra has only established that the principle itself is "ultimately stable and unshaken." The next major division, beginning in fascicle 6, extends this same argument outward from the sense-faculty to the four categories (five aggregates, six sense-entrances, twelve sense-fields, eighteen elements) and the seven great elements, showing that every single phenomenon, not merely the act of seeing, is equally the tathāgatagarbha — what Yuanying calls proving that "phenomena themselves are ultimately stable and unshaken" as well.
