A Thief Mistaken for a Son

Master Yuanying walks clause by clause through the sūtra's most famous episode: the Buddha's seven-part search for exactly where Ānanda's mind is, and the sharper blow that follows — that the very mind doing the searching was never Ānanda's true mind at all, but "a thief mistaken for a son." Fascicle 2 covers this entire episode, start to finish, exactly as Yuanying's own fascicle division ends it.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 2 complete Source: bfnn.org / NTU Buddhist Digital Library Covers: 癸初–癸三 — settling the judgment, the seven-location search, and the two roots of mind, through Yuanying's concluding verdict

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 1 closed with the Buddha naming the Śūraṅgama samādhi and Ānanda bowing to receive the teaching. Fascicle 2 is where the sūtra's actual line-by-line exposition begins in earnest: the Buddha asks Ānanda what first made him leave home, and, working from that single answer, spends the rest of the fascicle dismantling every place Ānanda tries to locate his own mind — inside the body, outside it, hidden in the eye, correlated with darkness and light, wherever it happens to touch something, suspended between faculty and object, and finally attached to nothing at all. When all seven fail, the Buddha goes further still, exposing the very faculty doing the searching as "a thief mistaken for a son" — not simply misplaced, but never the true mind to begin with. Yuanying's fascicle ends exactly here, with the assembly reduced to silence and the Buddha's verdict restated.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. Wherever Yuanying quotes a sūtra passage Ru-Yi has already translated in the companion bare-sūtra project — nearly this entire fascicle, since fascicle 1 of that project covers the same episode — that exact English wording is reused here, so a reader comparing both projects sees one consistent translation throughout.

What Ānanda Saw That Made Him Leave Home 癸初 子初 問取見相發心

Chinese Source

〖經〗佛告阿難：汝我同氣，情均天倫，當初發心，於我法中，見何勝相，頓捨世間，深重恩愛？

〖經〗阿難白佛：我見如來三十二相，勝妙殊絕，形體映徹，猶如琉璃。常自思惟：此相非是欲愛所生。何以故？欲氣麤濁，腥臊交遘，膿血雜亂，不能發生，勝淨妙明，紫金光聚，是以渴仰，從佛剃落。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "You and I are of one blood, bound as brothers by nature. When you first resolved on the path, what excellent mark did you see in my teaching, that made you cast aside all at once the deep bonds of worldly love and affection?" Ānanda answered the Buddha: "I saw that the Tathāgata's thirty-two marks were so surpassingly fine that his very form seemed translucent, like lapis lazuli. I often reflected: 'Such a form could not have arisen from desire and lust. Why? Because the energy of desire is coarse and turbid — a tangle of foul odors, blood, and pus — and could never give rise to something so pure, wondrous, bright, and radiant as purple-gold light.' It was out of this longing admiration that I followed the Buddha and shaved my head."

Commentary: This section's real purpose, behind Ānanda's request for the Buddha's teaching, is to let the Tathāgata probe exactly how Ānanda first resolved on the path — since the first step of practice depends entirely on that first resolve: if it was grounded in the unborn, unceasing true mind, that is the genuine "first expedient"; if not, it is not. Mind divides into true and false, and it is not yet clear which one Ānanda's own first resolve depended on — hence this question, meant to settle the matter with a single probe. The Buddha's opening words note their kinship first: Ānanda was one of the eight sons of King Śuklodana (Ānanda's father), a first cousin of the Buddha through the same paternal line — hence "of one blood." A father and son, or brothers, are called bound by heaven's own order (天倫); the Buddha invokes this kinship precisely because it is undeniable, "no different from limbs of the same body." Anyone who leaves the household life does so because they have seen something — so the Buddha asks what excellent mark in the Dharma led Ānanda to sever, all at once, the deep debt owed to parents and the heavy bond of a wife and children. Without some vision of the Dharma's own excellence, no one could cut through love and affection to become a monk. Though the Buddha's question is phrased as "what mark did you see," his real aim is to probe the very mind that was capable of seeing it.

Ānanda answers with the plain facts. The "thirty-two marks" are the fruit of the "hundred blessings" of merit accumulated in the causal stage — each single mark the fruit of a hundred distinct merits cultivated, from the crown protuberance down to the flat, full soles of the feet, as detailed at length in the Abhidharma treatises. "Surpassingly fine" (勝妙殊絕): surpassing even a wheel-turning king is called "surpassing"; each mark distinctly, brilliantly manifest is called "fine"; utterly beyond compare is the sense of "surpassingly." His form, inside and out, was luminous through and through — hence "translucent." Lapis lazuli (琉璃) is a blue-colored gem, luminous within and radiant without, and the Buddha's body is likened to it for this reason.

"I often reflected" — this very "reflecting" is itself the sixth consciousness, discriminating thought: precisely the false mind, what is elsewhere called "the root of beginningless birth and death." Ānanda has all along mistaken this for his true mind, and used it constantly — hence "often reflected." The mark in question is the Buddha's thirty-two marks, brought about by the cultivation and permeation of precepts, samādhi, and wisdom — hence "not born of desire and lust." "Coarse and turbid" is then unpacked: "a tangle of foul odors" explains "coarse"; "blood and pus" explains "turbid" — neither could ever give rise to something as adorned as the Buddha's marks. "Pure to the utmost" is "surpassingly pure"; "beyond compare in brightness" is "wondrous and bright"; the whole body the color of jāmbūnada gold, its golden light blazing, massed like a golden mountain — hence "purple-gold light." From this arose Ānanda's longing admiration, and he shaved his head and hair to follow the Buddha, leaving home to study the path. This very faculty that gave rise to that longing is exactly the "grasping, conditioned mind" that the sūtra goes on to refute below.

What is refuted in what follows is not the choice to leave home upon seeing the Buddha's excellence — that choice was not wrong. What is refuted is mistaking the mind that merely reacted to an admired mark for the true mind. As the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment puts it: "using love to abandon love only nourishes love's own root" — how could this alone be entirely good? And if this same reacting mind is mistaken for one's true mind, without knowing that another, genuine true mind exists apart from it, how could the great Śūraṅgama samādhi ever be cultivated from it? The Buddha has already, by this one question, diagnosed the root of Ānanda's affliction — which is exactly why what follows probes and refutes it at length: to clear away the very obstruction blocking the path of śamatha.

The Universal Verdict: All Beings Mistake the False for the True 子二 普判眾生誤認

Chinese Source

〖經〗佛言：善哉！阿難！汝等當知：一切眾生從無始來生死相續，皆由不知常住真心性淨明體，用諸妄想，此想不真故有輪轉。汝今欲研無上菩提真發明性，應當直心詶我所問。十方如來同一道故出離生死，皆以直心！心言直故，如是乃至終始地位，中間永無諸委曲相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: The Buddha said: "Good, Ānanda! You should all know that every sentient being, from beginningless time, has continued through birth and death solely because it does not recognize the ever-abiding true mind, whose nature is pure and bright, and instead makes use of false thinking. Because such thinking is not true, one keeps turning in the wheel of transmigration. Now that you wish to investigate the true nature that reveals unsurpassed bodhi, you must answer my questions with a straightforward mind. The Tathāgatas of the ten directions leave birth and death behind by this same one path — all of them by a straightforward mind. Because mind and word are both straightforward in this way, from beginning to end, through every stage, there is never, in between, any crooked or evasive appearance."

Commentary: "Good, Ānanda" carries two intentions at once: first, delight at having found the very root of the disease, now that it can be treated; second, since a rebuke and refutation is about to follow, a word of comfort offered first. "You" addresses Ānanda directly; "all" addresses the whole assembly. "Should know" means knowing through wisdom, not through the ordinary discriminating consciousness. This passage sets out true and false side by side and renders judgment between them, precisely so that, at this critical juncture, the assembly will take up the true and set aside the false — the very first skillful means of practice.

"Every sentient being" includes ordinary beings, non-Buddhists, and those of the provisional and lesser vehicles alike. "From beginningless time" — ever since the false first stirring of primordial ignorance, beings have, in reliance on delusion, created karma, and, in reliance on karma, received retribution; and within that retribution-body, delusion and karma arise once more, retribution is received once more — birth following death, death following birth, without a single break in the chain. And why? All of it rooted in delusion — so long as one remains deluded, birth and death never cease. "Does not recognize" is exactly this delusion. "The ever-abiding true mind, whose nature is pure and bright" is exactly the truth being missed through that very delusion: pervading vertically through past, present, and future without ever arising or ceasing, this is called "ever-abiding"; one single character throughout the whole Dharma-realm, without any falseness at all, this is called "true mind." Its nature has, from the very start, been pure and undefiled — not "washed clean" only afterward; hence "pure." Its nature has, since time immemorial, been luminously aware, needing no effort to become bright; hence "bright." "Its very substance" is the substance of this originally pure, originally bright true mind — and though a being may be deluded about it, that substance itself is never actually lost.

"Makes use of false thinking": the character "諸" here is merely a grammatical filler; "false thinking" is the clung-to, discriminating false mind of ordinary consciousness — precisely the same mind, mentioned just above, that "saw the mark and reflected on it." Later in the text it is called "phenomena that are mere shadows of conditioned dust." "Because such thinking is not true" ties directly to the passage further on where the Buddha tells Ānanda: "This is nothing but the false, insubstantial appearance of thought conditioned by what is in front of you, and it is deluding your true nature. Ever since beginningless time and continuing into this very life, you have mistaken a thief for your own son, losing your own original, ever-abiding nature — and so you have undergone this turning in the wheel." That is: this mind was never in truth the real mind at all, but Ānanda mistook it for the real one, and, deceived by it in turn, has been swept through the vast ocean of birth and death across kalpas without number, unable to find a way out.

Within this passage, "the ever-abiding true mind" corresponds to the true root among what the sūtra will soon name the two roots — the originally pure substance of bodhi and nirvāṇa; "makes use of false thinking" corresponds to the false root — the very root of beginningless birth and death. Every sentient being, deluded about the truth, clings for this reason to the false, gives rise to false delusion on that basis, creates false karma from that delusion, and receives false retribution from that karma — and so turns, without rest, in the wheel!

Reading note: Yuanying is careful to note that "should know" means knowing through wisdom (智知), not through ordinary consciousness (識知) — a small distinction that anticipates the entire fascicle's argument: the very faculty Ānanda will keep reaching for, throughout the seven-location search, is exactly the "ordinary consciousness" this opening line has just ruled out as a source of real knowing.

The Seven Locations Where Mind Is Sought 丑初 破妄識無處

Yuanying frames what follows carefully: strictly speaking, this is not "seven locations for finding the mind" but seven rounds of refuting the places Ānanda proposes — the sūtra contains only a single actual "asking where the mind is" (徵心), repeated once more later in this same fascicle. What follows are seven attempts by Ānanda to answer that one question, each one collapsing in turn. Because the sūtra's own English wording for these seven exchanges has already been established in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 1, the sūtra passages below reuse that exact translation; only Yuanying's commentary is new to this page.

First: Inside the Body 執心在身內

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！一切世間十種異生，同將識心居在身內；縱觀如來青蓮花眼，亦在佛面。我今觀此浮根四塵，秖在我面；如是識心，實居身內。

〖講〗此執心在身內。一聞徵詰，便白佛言：先稱呼佛為世尊，下敘執，則引十生同計，自己不負責任。一切世間，三界內六凡世間也。十種異生，於十二類眾生中，除去無色、無想二種無心目可言者，其餘十類，業報形體各各差異，故曰異生。浮根，即浮塵眼根，乃色、香、味、觸四塵所成，非清淨四大所成之勝義根。阿難意謂：我今觀此浮根四塵，秖在我面，與佛無異；如是識得我心，實居色身之內，與十類眾生亦無有異。此執心在內，為阿難本計；其餘六處，皆因佛一時破奪，迫成轉計耳。

Sūtra: Ānanda answered the Buddha: "World-Honored One, all the ten kinds of beings in the world alike hold that the discriminating mind dwells inside the body. Even looking at the Tathāgata's own blue-lotus eyes, I see they are set in his face. So too, my own eyes are simply set in my face, and this discriminating mind of mine truly dwells within my body."

Commentary: This is the claim that mind lies inside the body. As soon as Ānanda is questioned, he answers by first addressing the Buddha as World-Honored One, then stating his position — citing the shared assumption of all ten kinds of beings, so as not to bear the responsibility for this claim alone. "All the world" means the six ordinary destinies within the three realms. "Ten kinds of beings" refers to the twelve categories of birth, minus the two — the formless, and those without conception — that have no mind or eyes to speak of; the remaining ten kinds differ from one another in the bodily form their karmic retribution takes, hence "differing kinds." The "coarse sense-faculty" is the ordinary physical eye-organ, formed from the four gross sense-objects of form, smell, taste, and touch — not the "superior faculty" formed from the pure four elements. Ānanda's point: just as his own coarse faculties sit plainly on his face, no different from the Buddha's own blue-lotus eyes, so too his discriminating mind truly dwells within his physical body, no different from the other ten kinds of beings. This claim — mind inside the body — is Ānanda's own original, spontaneous position; the six that follow are all forced transformations of this same claim, each one adopted only after the Buddha has refuted the one before it.

〖經〗佛告阿難：「汝今現坐如來講堂，觀祇陀林今何所在？」……「阿難！汝亦如是。……是故應知，汝言『覺了能知之心住在身內』，無有是處。」

〖講〗此段以阿難執心在內，佛不直破其非，且就現前所見能見中，一一舉問，令其據事直答，不知所以。文有三番問答：第一番例定內外之境，第二番例定先後之見，第三番例定見外之由。佛以能例之法既定，然後依例反難，令阿難無從伸辯，始知如來說法之巧。「頗有眾生先見身中，後觀外物」一句，正脈云：「只此一計，一切眾生所以囚繫胎獄，桎梏肉身，乃至三途苦形，自執妄認，受無量苦，展轉不能自脫者，皆由此計，以為障之深根也。」

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "You are now sitting in the Tathāgata's lecture hall — where, then, is the Jeta Grove?" ... "Ānanda, it is just the same with you. ... You should therefore know that your claim — that the mind which is aware and able to know dwells inside the body — does not hold up."

Commentary: Rather than directly declaring Ānanda's claim wrong, the Buddha instead questions him, point by point, about what he can presently see, forcing him to answer plainly from the facts without yet realizing where the argument is heading. The exchange unfolds in three matched rounds: first, establishing the distinction between what is inside the hall and what is outside it; second, establishing the sequence of what is seen first and what is seen only afterward; third, establishing the actual mechanism by which what is outside comes to be seen at all. Only once these matched examples are firmly established does the Buddha turn them back against Ānanda's own claim, leaving him nothing left to argue. On the line "has any being ever first seen what is inside the body, and only afterward observed things outside it," the Correct Meridian commentary observes: "This single mistaken assumption alone is why every sentient being is imprisoned in the womb, shackled to this fleshly body, and even sunk into the suffering forms of the three lower destinies — clinging to this false belief, suffering boundlessly, turning again and again without ever freeing themselves. All of it traces back to this one assumption as its deepest root of obstruction."

Second: Outside the Body 執心在身外

〖經〗阿難稽首而白佛言：我聞如來如是法音，悟知我心實居身外。……佛告阿難：……是故應知，汝言「覺了能知之心住在身外」，無有是處。

〖講〗此轉計心在身外。阿難見處未真，不免隨語生解，聞佛破內，即便計外。「悟知」者，此悟非真悟：以不見身內，悟知不在身內；以了見身外，悟知必在身外，故決之曰「實居身外」。此當機自負，觀末後一「耶」字，心懷猶豫，還是腳跟不曾點地。佛以食喻破之：諸比丘同來乞食，一人食時，眾人不飽，可證身心若相外，自不相干，則心有所知，身應不覺；而今身心實則相知，故不得在外。

Sūtra: Ānanda bowed his head and said to the Buddha: "Hearing the Tathāgata's teaching, I now realize that my mind must actually dwell outside the body. ..." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "... You should therefore know that your claim — that the mind which is aware and able to know dwells outside the body — does not hold up either."

Commentary: This is Ānanda's transformed claim, now placing mind outside the body. Ānanda's own insight is not yet genuine, and he cannot help generating a new interpretation the moment he hears one refuted — no sooner has the Buddha refuted "inside" than he proposes "outside." This "realization" is not a true realization: from not seeing what is inside, he infers the mind is not inside; from clearly seeing what is outside, he infers it must be outside — and so he declares it settled: "it actually dwells outside." Yet notice his own confidence undercut by the single hesitant word "or" at the very end of his statement — his footing is still not firmly planted. The Buddha refutes this with the analogy of a shared meal: when the monks return from begging together and only one of them eats, the rest are not thereby fed — proving that if body and mind were truly separate, external to one another and unconnected, then whatever the mind knew, the body ought not to feel at all. But body and mind plainly do know each other — so the mind cannot be outside the body.

Third: Hidden in the Eye-Faculty 執心潛眼根

〖經〗阿難白佛言：……此了知心既不知內而能見外，如我思忖，潛伏根裏。猶如有人取瑠璃椀，合其兩眼……佛告阿難：……是故應知，汝言「覺了能知之心潛伏根裏如瑠璃合」，無有是處。

〖講〗此轉計心潛眼根。前二既皆被破，阿難遂計另一處：非內非外，而潛伏於眼根之內，如魚潛於淵，如鳥伏於巢。取琉璃碗喻，以能合之琉璃碗喻眼根，所合之眼根喻心，謂心潛根中，如根潛於琉璃碗中，兩不相礙，故根隨見，心隨即分別。此喻法本不相齊：琉璃籠眼之人，遠見山河時亦能自見其琉璃；今心若潛根中，如根潛於琉璃，則見山河時，理應自見其眼根——而實不能自見其眼，法喻既不相齊，此執遂不能成立。任阿難自恃小慧，善於設喻，終非自性中流出之真知真見，故如來一語便破。

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "... Since this mind that is aware and knows does not know what is inside yet can see what is outside, I now think it must be hidden within the sense faculty itself — just as, if a man takes a crystal bowl and covers both his eyes with it, ..." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "... You should therefore know that your claim — that the mind which is aware and able to know lies hidden within the faculty like crystal fitted over the eye — does not hold up either."

Commentary: With his first two claims both refuted, Ānanda now proposes a third location: neither inside nor outside, but hidden within the eye-faculty itself — like a fish submerged in a deep pool, or a bird settled in its nest. He offers the crystal-bowl analogy: the enclosing crystal bowl stands for the eye-faculty, and the enclosed eye stands for the mind — the mind hidden within the faculty, he claims, just as the eye is hidden within the crystal, the two not obstructing one another, so that the faculty sees and the mind discriminates in the same instant. But the analogy and the claim do not actually match: a person looking through crystal placed over the eyes, gazing at distant mountains and rivers, also sees the crystal itself in the same glance. If the mind were likewise hidden within the eye-faculty the way the eye is hidden within the crystal, then, by the same logic, seeing mountains and rivers should also mean seeing one's own eye-faculty directly — yet in fact one cannot see one's own eye at all. Since the analogy and the claim fail to correspond, this position collapses. However much Ānanda relies on his own cleverness in constructing analogies, none of it flows from the true knowing and seeing of his own nature — which is exactly why the Tathāgata is able to refute it with a single stroke.

Fourth: Darkness Within, Light Without 執心分明暗

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！我今又作如是思惟：是眾生身，府藏在中，竅穴居外；有藏則暗，有竅則明。……佛告阿難：……是故應知，汝言「見暗名見內」者，無有是處。

〖講〗此轉計仍欲曲成「在內」之說。阿難以為：見暗即是見身內之府藏，見明即是見身外之竅穴——藉此挽回第一番已被破之「在內」，並脫第四番「不能見內」之過。府臟者，五臟六腑也；竅穴者，眼、耳、鼻、口七孔，皆在面上。佛破之曰：閉眼所見之暗境，若與眼相對，則此暗仍在眼前，何得名為「見內」？若竟成內，則居於暗室之中，無日、月、燈三種光明，滿室黑暗，豈非亦皆汝之臟腑？若閉眼見暗可名見內，則開眼見明時，何以不能反觀見自己之面？終不能自圓其說。

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I now think of it this way: in the body of any being, the internal organs lie within, while the openings face outward; where there are organs, it is dark, and where there are openings, it is bright. ..." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "... You should therefore know that your claim — that seeing darkness is called seeing what is within — does not hold up either."

Commentary: This transformed claim is really an attempt to rescue the very first position, "inside the body." Ānanda now proposes that seeing darkness is seeing the internal organs, while seeing brightness is seeing the outward-facing openings — hoping thereby to salvage his refuted first claim while also escaping the earlier objection that he cannot actually see what is inside. "Organs" means the five viscera and six bowels; "openings" means the seven apertures of the eyes, ears, nose, and mouth, all located on the face. The Buddha refutes this by asking: the darkness one sees on closing the eyes — if it faces the eyes directly, then this darkness still lies in front of the eyes, so how can it be called "seeing what is within"? And if it really did count as "within," then sitting inside a dark room with no sun, moon, or lamp — filled entirely with blackness — would that blackness, too, all be one's own internal organs? And if closing the eyes to see darkness may be called "seeing within," then why, on opening the eyes to see brightness, can one not turn that same seeing inward and behold one's own face? In the end, the claim cannot be made to hold together on its own terms.

Fifth: Wherever Mind Joins Its Object 執心則隨有

〖經〗阿難言：我常聞佛開示四眾：由心生故種種法生，由法生故種種心生。……佛告阿難：……是故應知，隨所合處心則隨有，無有是處。

〖講〗阿難既四處被破，未敢再逞己見，故引昔教為把柄。昔說心法互生四句，通大小乘：心生法生，明法不自生，從心而起；法生心生，明心不自生，由法而現——正顯心本不生，法無自性，二俱無體，乃心法皆空之旨。阿難卻反用此教，證緣心有體有處，在彼心法偶合之處，可謂迷之甚矣。《正脈》所謂「今阿難失旨，反證緣心有體有處」者是也。佛破之曰：若說「由法生故種種心生」，是心本無自體；無體之物，如何能與他物相合？若謂無體亦能相合，則十八界之外，另有一無體之十九界，六塵之外，另有一無體之七塵，天下豈有此理？

Sūtra: Ānanda said: "I have often heard the Buddha teach the fourfold assembly: 'Because mind arises, all the various dharmas arise; because dharmas arise, all the various minds arise.' ..." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "... You should therefore know that the claim that mind exists wherever it happens to join something does not hold up either."

Commentary: With four locations already refuted, Ānanda no longer dares assert his own view outright, and instead reaches for a teaching he once heard as cover. This teaching on mind and dharmas mutually arising, common to both greater and lesser vehicles, actually means: "mind arises, dharmas arise" shows that dharmas do not arise on their own but are brought forth by mind; "dharmas arise, mind arises" shows that mind does not arise on its own but appears in dependence on dharmas — the whole point being that mind is fundamentally unarisen, dharmas have no nature of their own, and neither has any real substance: both mind and dharmas are empty. Ānanda inverts this very teaching to argue instead that the conditioned mind has a real substance and a real location — namely, wherever mind and its object happen to meet — which is confusion of the deepest kind. As the Correct Meridian commentary puts it: "Here Ānanda has lost the point entirely, turning the teaching around to argue that the conditioned mind has substance and location." The Buddha refutes this by pointing out: if, as claimed, "dharmas arise, and so mind arises," then mind has no substance of its own to begin with — and how could something without any substance join together with anything else at all? And if something without substance really could join with something else, then beyond the eighteen elements there would have to exist some further, substance-less nineteenth element, and beyond the six sense-objects some further, substance-less seventh object — which is simply absurd.

Sixth: Between Faculty and Object 執心在中間

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！我亦聞佛與文殊等諸法王子談實相時，世尊亦言：心不在內，亦不在外。……佛言：……是故應知，當在中間，無有是處。

〖講〗阿難引佛圓頓大教，卻不解真理，反用緣慮之心思惟忖度，附會己意。佛言「心不在內，亦不在外」，本是彰顯真心離一切相、無在無不在的無相實相之義；阿難卻誤解為心必在內外之外的第三處——中間。佛遂追問：中間必有一定之處，畢竟在何處？若說在「身」，則或在邊、或在中，在邊非中，在中同前所破之「內」；若說在「境」，則須問這中間之處有無標記可表？若無所表，等於無中；若有所表，則東西南北，觀者角度不同，所表隨之而異，並無一定之處。阿難見兩途皆被堵死，遂另引「眼色為緣，生於眼識」之教，謂識生於根、塵二者之中，即是心之所在——此仍是誤將眼識(能生於根塵之間的了別作用)與意識(能推度尋逐之心)混為一談，答非所問。

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I have also heard the Buddha say to Mañjuśrī and the other Dharma-prince bodhisattvas, when speaking of true reality, that mind is neither within nor without. ..." The Buddha said: "... You should therefore know that the claim that mind is exactly in between does not hold up either."

Commentary: Ānanda here invokes the Buddha's own perfect and sudden teaching, yet fails to grasp its real meaning, instead applying the very faculty of conditioned, discriminating thought to speculate and force his own interpretation onto it. When the Buddha said "mind is neither within nor without," his actual point was to reveal that the true mind is apart from every characteristic altogether — neither located nor unlocated — the mark-free true reality itself. Ānanda misreads this as meaning the mind must occupy some third location, distinct from both "inside" and "outside": namely, "in between." The Buddha presses him: any genuine middle must have some definite place — where, exactly, is it? If in the body, is it at some edge, or truly central? At an edge is not the middle at all; truly central would mean the same as the "inside" already refuted. If it means some point in the external world, one must ask whether this middle point has any fixed marker — if none, it amounts to no middle at all; if some marker is given, then depending on the observer's own position, east becomes west and south becomes north, so no fixed location can ever be pinned down. Seeing both paths blocked, Ānanda then reaches for yet another teaching — "eye and form, taken as conditions, give rise to eye-consciousness" — proposing that consciousness, arising between faculty and object, is where mind resides. But this once again confuses eye-consciousness (the discerning function that arises between faculty and object) with the discriminating, searching mind actually under discussion — answering a question other than the one that was asked.

〖經〗佛言：汝心若在根、塵之中，此之心體為復兼二？為不兼二？若兼二者，物體雜亂……是故應知，當在中間，無有是處。

〖講〗此就兩義釋之。就阿難自解，眼有分別，屬有知之根；色塵無知，屬無知之物。若此心兼帶根、塵二者，則有知與無知夾雜混亂，敵對兩立，如蝸牛兩角，云何可名為「中」？若不兼帶二者，則此心既非同根之有知，又非同塵之無知，離此根、塵，即無識之體性可得，又有何相可名為「中」？兼二、不兼二，兩途俱墮，「在中間」之說遂不能成立。

Sūtra: The Buddha said: "If your mind lies between faculty and object in this way, does this substance of mind combine both, or not combine both? If it combines both, then object and substance become confused ... You should therefore know that the claim that mind is exactly in between does not hold up either."

Commentary: This can be read two ways. On Ānanda's own understanding: the eye has the capacity for discrimination, and so belongs to the faculty, which has awareness; form as an object has no awareness at all, and so belongs to what lacks it. If this mind combines both faculty and object, then what has awareness and what lacks it become confused together, standing opposed to one another like a snail's two horns — how, then, could this be called a genuine "middle"? And if it combines neither, then this mind is neither the same as the faculty's awareness nor the same as the object's lack of it; apart from faculty and object altogether, no substance of consciousness can be found at all — so what characteristic could still be called "middle"? Whether it combines both or neither, both paths collapse, and the claim of a location "in between" cannot stand.

Seventh: Attached to Nothing At All 執一切無著

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！我昔見佛與大目連、須菩提、富樓那、舍利弗四大弟子共轉法輪，常言「覺知分別心性，既不在內，亦不在外，不在中間，俱無所在；一切無著，名之為心」，則我無著，名為心不？

〖講〗六處計有，悉皆被破，至此乃計無著，仍是引昔教附會己意。佛與四大弟子共轉法輪，常言此心不在內、外、中間，正明大乘無相實相之義，所謂「三際求心心不有，心不有處妄緣空，妄緣空處即菩提」；阿難聞言昧義，遂以己意推度，釋成「俱無所在，一切無著，名之為心」，雖已釋成，尚不敢自決，故又問「則我無著，名為心不」。實因阿難不了：覺知分別之心性，本隨境生滅，原無實體，雖至此技窮，仍認有一「無著」之體，只是不著一切而已——並非真悟「此心本無所有」。

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I once heard the Buddha, together with the four great disciples Mahāmaudgalyāyana, Subhūti, Pūrṇa, and Śāriputra, turning the Dharma wheel, say that the nature of the mind that is aware and discriminates is nowhere at all — not within, not without, not in between — and that attachment to nothing whatsoever is what is called mind. Would my own being attached to nothing, then, count as mind?"

Commentary: With six locations proposed and refuted, Ānanda now proposes non-attachment itself, once again borrowing a remembered teaching and forcing it to fit his own purpose. When the Buddha, together with the four great disciples, taught that this mind is neither within, without, nor in between, he was expounding the Great Vehicle's teaching of mark-free true reality — as the verse has it, "seek the mind through past, present, and future, and it is nowhere to be found; where the false, conditioned mind is empty, that very emptiness is bodhi." Ānanda, hearing the words but missing their meaning, works this into his own interpretation: "nowhere at all, attached to nothing — this is what is called mind." Yet even having worked this out, he does not dare settle it himself, and so asks once more whether his own non-attachment might count as mind. The real trouble is that Ānanda still does not understand: the nature of the mind that is aware and discriminates rises and falls entirely with its objects, and has no real substance of its own to begin with. Even here, at the very end of his resources, he still assumes some substantial "thing" called non-attachment — merely one that attaches to nothing — rather than truly awakening to the fact that this mind, from the start, was never anything at all.

〖經〗佛告阿難：汝言「覺知分別心性俱無在」者，世間虛空、水、陸、飛行諸所物象，名為一切。汝不著者，為在為無？無則同於龜毛、兔角，云何不著？有不著者，不可名無。無相則無，非無則相；相有則在，云何無著？是故應知「一切無著名覺知心」，無有是處。

〖講〗此雙開有、無兩途而破之：若此「不著」之物根本不存在，便與龜毛兔角一般，有名無實，又何須說「不著」？若確有一個「不著」的東西存在，就不能說它「無」；既非無，便是有相；既有相，便有處所——那又如何能叫「無著」？有、無兩途俱墮，阿難七次轉計，至此技窮辭盡，無以自解。

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "You say that the nature of the mind that is aware and discriminates is nowhere at all. Everything in the world — empty space, and every creature of water, land, and air — is called 'everything.' Now, this 'nothing you are attached to' — does it exist, or not? If it does not exist, it is exactly like the fur of a tortoise or the horns of a rabbit — how could there be any attachment to speak of at all? But if there is a 'nothing' that you are not attached to, it cannot then be called nonexistent. What has no characteristic at all is nonexistent; what is not nonexistent must have some characteristic; and whatever has a characteristic must exist somewhere — so how could it be called 'attached to nothing'? You should therefore know that the claim 'the mind that is aware and knows is what is attached to nothing at all' does not hold up either."

Commentary: The Buddha opens both possibilities — existence and nonexistence — and refutes each in turn: if this "thing not attached to" does not exist at all, it is exactly like the fur of a tortoise or the horns of a rabbit, a name with no substance behind it at all — in which case what need is there even to speak of "non-attachment"? But if some real "thing not attached to" genuinely exists, then it cannot be called nonexistent; and if it is not nonexistent, it must have some characteristic; and if it has a characteristic, it must exist somewhere — so how, then, could it still be called "attached to nothing"? Both paths, existence and nonexistence, collapse together. Having transformed his claim seven times over, Ānanda has now exhausted every resource and every word, with nothing left to offer in his own defense.

Reading note: Yuanying closes this whole section (丑初 破妄識無處) by noting that, of the seven, only four are truly distinct "locations" claimed with confidence — inside, outside, hidden in the faculty, and between faculty and object — since the fourth claim (darkness/light) really just tries to rescue the first, and the fifth (wherever it joins) and seventh (nowhere, unattached) both abandon location altogether. He also distinguishes their sources: the first is a claim Ānanda shares with "all ten kinds of beings"; the second, third, and fourth are his own improvised reasoning; the fifth, sixth, and seventh each borrow a remembered teaching and misapply it. As the Correct Meridian commentary puts it, once these seven are refuted, "ordinary false feeling is exhausted, and the place worldly people rely on for 'mind' — nowhere beyond these seven — is left with no foundation left to search for at all."

"This Is Not Your Mind!" 丑二 斥妄識非心

Ānanda's Renewed Plea 阿難責躬請教

〖經〗爾時阿難……而白佛言：我是如來最小之弟，蒙佛慈愛，雖今出家，猶恃憍憐，所以多聞，未得無漏，不能折伏，娑毗羅咒，為彼所轉，溺於淫舍。當由不知，真際所詣！惟願世尊，大慈哀愍，開示我等，奢摩他路，令諸闡提，隳彌戾車。

〖講〗七處被破，阿難未達佛意，仍冀佛為之指出「心之所在」，足見他依舊認識為心，只自恨不知其處，故自責而請益。「真際」即真如實際之異名，亦即本經如來藏、首楞嚴之定體。阿難所求，仍是「求佛指處」，尚未悟徹「無處非心」的深意。「奢摩他路」即天然不生不滅之本定，惟悟真如實際，此路方通；阿難不知，向來所執之妄心，正是壅塞此路的最大障礙——佛下文破妄識、顯真心，正是為他撤去此一障礙。「令諸闡提，隳彌戾車」：一闡提，即斷善根眾生；彌戾車，此云樂垢穢、惡知見。阿難意謂：若蒙開示，不僅自己得成定力，並使邪見深重之人，亦能因此毀滅惡知見而生正信。

Sūtra: At that time Ānanda ... said to the Buddha: "I am the Tathāgata's youngest cousin, and, favored with his kind affection, I left home relying still on his fondness for me — and so, although I have heard much, I have not attained freedom from outflows. I could not overcome the Kapila spell; I was carried off by it and nearly drowned in that house of ill repute, all because I did not know where true reality is actually to be found. I beg only that the World-Honored One, in his great compassion, take pity on us and reveal the path of śamatha, so that even the most incorrigible may be freed from base and mistaken views."

Commentary: Even with all seven of his claims refuted, Ānanda still has not grasped the Buddha's real intent, and continues hoping the Buddha will simply point out where the mind actually is — proof enough that he still takes discriminating consciousness for mind itself, regretting only that he does not yet know its address, and so reproaches himself and begs for further instruction. "True reality" is another name for true suchness, the very substance of the tathāgatagarbha and of the Śūraṅgama samādhi this whole sūtra is building toward. What Ānanda is asking for is still "please point out the place" — he has not yet broken through to the deeper point that no such place exists because it was never mind to begin with. "The path of śamatha" is the naturally unborn, unceasing fundamental samādhi; only by awakening to true suchness does this path ever open. What Ānanda does not realize is that the very false mind he has clung to all along is itself the greatest obstruction blocking that path — which is exactly why what follows, refuting the false mind and revealing the true one, is the Buddha clearing this one obstruction away on his behalf. As for "so that even the most incorrigible may be freed from base and mistaken views": an icchantika is a being who has severed their own roots of goodness, and "base and mistaken views" describes those steeped in filth and wrong knowledge. Ānanda's point is that if the Buddha grants this teaching, it will not only bring him his own power of concentration, but will let even those sunk in the deepest wrong views break free of them and come to genuine faith.

Light from the Buddha's Face 光表破顯諸相

〖經〗爾時世尊，從其面門，放種種光，其光晃曜，如百千日！普佛世界，六種震動，如是十方微塵國土，一時開現。佛之威神，令諸世界，合成一界。

〖講〗此佛放光，以表破妄顯真諸相。破妄，正為顯真：妄不破，則真不顯。故佛從面門放光，以無言之教令人觸境會心。此光非佛獨有，一切眾生皆具，迷之則似失，悟之則顯露。臨濟義玄禪師云：「有一無位真人，在汝諸人六根門頭，放光動地，諸人未證據者，看！看！」正深得此處面門放光之旨。六種震動，正表六處妄識將破；微塵國土一時開現，正表六識既破，常光現前，無量智境，無不朗然照徹。世界合成一界，正表識破根顯，會相歸性，無邊剎土，自他不隔於毫端，遂融合為一真法界。

Sūtra: At that time the World-Honored One sent forth every kind of light from his face, its radiance blazing like a hundred thousand suns! Every buddha-world throughout all directions shook in six ways, and the countless lands of the ten directions, numerous as motes of dust, all opened into view at once. Through the Buddha's own majestic spiritual power, all these worlds fused together into a single world.

Commentary: This burst of light stages, without words, the whole coming movement of refuting the false and revealing the true — for refuting the false is exactly how the true comes to be revealed; unless the false is broken, the true cannot appear. So the Buddha lets light stream from his own face, teaching wordlessly so that beings might touch this vision and meet the mind directly. This light belongs to no single buddha — every sentient being already possesses it; delusion makes it seem lost, and awakening lets it show itself plainly. Chan Master Linji Yixuan once said: "There is a true person of no rank, standing right at the gateways of your own six sense-faculties, sending out light and shaking the earth — look! Look, all of you who have not yet realized it!" — a saying that captures exactly what this face-light passage is pointing at. The six kinds of shaking stand for the imminent breaking of the false mind operating through the six sense-faculties; the countless lands opening into view at once stand for what appears the instant that false consciousness is broken — the ever-present light of true nature, illuminating every immeasurable field of wisdom without exception. The fusing of every world into one stands for what happens once false consciousness is broken and the true faculty is revealed: every phenomenon returned to its own true nature, so that boundless lands, self and other, are no longer separated even by a hair's breadth, but merge into a single, true Dharma-realm.

The Two Roots 發明真妄二本

〖經〗佛告阿難：一切眾生，從無始來，種種顛倒，業種自然，如惡叉聚。諸修行人，不能得成無上菩提，乃至別成聲聞、緣覺，及成外道，諸天魔王，及魔眷屬。皆由不知二種根本，錯亂修習。猶如煮沙，欲成嘉饌，縱經塵劫，終不能得。

〖講〗一切眾生自無始根本不覺以來，迷真執妄，於我、法二種種顛倒分別（惑道），依惑造業，熏成業種（業道），自然感得生死苦果（苦道）——惑、業、苦三，不相捨離，猶如惡叉聚（西域果名，一蒂三果同聚而生）。修行人本期得道證果，卻或滯於化城（聲聞、緣覺），或誤入外道、諸天，甚至墮入魔羅眷屬——皆因不知「二種根本」，將修行之因錯置，猶如煮沙欲成飯，縱經塵劫，終究只是熱沙一堆，不能成飯。

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Every sentient being, from beginningless time, has produced every kind of distorted view, and the seeds of karma arise naturally from this, clustered together like the triple-clustered myrobalan fruit. Practitioners who fail to accomplish unsurpassed bodhi instead become, at best, śrāvakas or pratyekabuddhas, or else fall in with non-Buddhist paths, heavenly demon-kings, and their own demonic retinues — all because they do not recognize the two root causes, and so cultivate practice in confused disorder. It is exactly like cooking sand, hoping it will become a fine meal: though kalpas as numerous as dust motes may pass, it can never be accomplished."

Commentary: Every sentient being, ever since the beginningless root of non-awakening first stirred, has been deluded about the truth and clung to the false, giving rise to every kind of distorted discrimination about self and phenomena — this is the path of delusion. In dependence on delusion, karma is created and its seeds take root — this is the path of karma. And from this karma, the bitter fruit of birth and death naturally follows — this is the path of suffering. Delusion, karma, and suffering never separate from one another, clustering together exactly like the myrobalan fruit of the Western Regions, whose three fruits grow from a single stem. Practitioners who set out hoping to attain the path and its fruit instead often become stalled at some lesser way-station — the vehicles of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha — or wander mistakenly into non-Buddhist paths and heavenly rebirths, or even fall in among the retinue of demonic forces — all because they fail to recognize the "two root causes" and so misplace the very basis of their cultivation, exactly like cooking sand and hoping for rice: however many kalpas pass, it remains nothing but hot sand, never becoming a proper meal.

〖經〗云何二種？阿難：一者無始生死根本，則汝今者，與諸眾生，用攀緣心，為自性者。二者無始菩提涅槃，元清淨體。則汝今者，識精元明，能生諸緣，緣所遺者。由諸眾生，遺此本明，雖終日行，而不自覺，枉入諸趣。

〖講〗第一根本，即妄本：從無始無明妄動以來，浩劫輪迴生死之根本，正是阿難與一切眾生現今所用之「攀緣心」——即第六意識，妄攀所緣諸塵之境，妄起分別，妄生憎愛取捨，塵有則有，塵無則無，虛妄無體，本非真心。一切眾生皆迷認此妄執以為心性，阿難亦然：前云「即思惟體，實我心性」，後云「即能推者，我將為心」，皆是此妄本作祟。

第二根本，即真本：「元清淨體」者，此體一切眾生本來元具，不假造作，不待修成。此體無他，即現今根中所具、圓湛不生滅之性，名為「識精」，元是妙明之心，並非磨之使明，乃本來自明；雖處長夜昏暗，其性不昏。在眼能見，在耳能聞，在鼻嗅香，在舌嘗味，在身覺觸，在意知法，雖分六用和合，元是一精之明，此即真本，修行者當取為因地心。「識精」雖帶少分之妄，究竟全體是真：如捏目所見第二月，全體仍是真月，僅多一捏之妄，放手即全真。「能生諸緣，緣所遺者」：識精即第八識本具之精明體，能攝一切法，能生一切法；然眾生迷此識精元明之體，如眼有見而不能反見自眼——非真遺失，只是迷而不知，故曰「緣所遺者」。

眾生終日承此本明之功能力用——眼見色、耳聞聲、乃至意知法，無非本明照用——而不自覺，即是日用而不知能見能聞者究竟是誰；既已迷卻真本，自必全用妄本攀緣六塵，依之起惑造業，隨業受報，枉入六道，輪迴不息。此正本經一切法門的總樞紐：捨識用根，前之七番破處，正是先破妄本；後之十番顯見，正是為顯真本。

Sūtra: "What are these two? Ānanda, the first is the root of beginningless birth and death: this is exactly the very mind — the grasping, conditioned mind — that you and all sentient beings now take to be your own true nature. The second is the originally pure substance of beginningless bodhi and nirvāṇa: this is exactly your own essential, originally bright consciousness, which is able to give rise to every condition, yet is itself left behind, overlooked, by those very conditions it gives rise to. Because sentient beings overlook this original brightness, they act on it all day long without ever being aware of it, and so wrongly fall into one destiny after another."

Commentary: The first root is the false root: ever since beginningless ignorance first falsely stirred, the very root of the vast, cyclic wheel of birth and death has been exactly this "grasping, conditioned mind" that Ānanda and every sentient being presently rely on — the sixth consciousness, falsely grasping at whatever sense-object it happens to condition itself on, falsely giving rise to discrimination, falsely producing love and hatred, taking and rejecting; present when its object is present, absent when its object is absent, entirely without substance of its own, and never the true mind to begin with. Every sentient being mistakes this false clinging for their own nature of mind — Ānanda no less than any other: his earlier claim that "this very capacity for thought is truly the nature of my mind," and his later claim that "whatever it is in me that is doing the searching — that, I take to be my mind" — both are exactly this false root at work.

The second root is the true root: this "originally pure substance" is what every sentient being has always, inherently, possessed — needing no fabrication, awaiting no cultivation to bring it about. This substance is none other than the perfectly full, unborn, unceasing nature already present within one's own sense-faculties this very moment, called "the essential consciousness" — originally a wondrously bright mind, not brightened through polishing but bright from the very start; even sunk in the long night's darkest hour, its nature is never dimmed. In the eye it sees; in the ear it hears; in the nose it smells fragrance; on the tongue it tastes flavor; in the body it feels touch; in the mind it knows phenomena — though divided into six distinct functions, it is, from the start, a single essential brightness. This is the true root, the very mind practitioners should take up as the causal ground of their practice. "The essential consciousness," though it carries a small trace of falseness within it, is, taken as a whole, entirely true — like the second moon seen when pressing on the eye: the moon itself remains entirely real, with only the added falseness of the pressing finger; release the finger, and it is wholly true once more. "Able to give rise to every condition, yet is itself left behind by those very conditions": the essential consciousness is the inherently luminous substance of the eighth consciousness, able to encompass and give rise to every phenomenon; yet sentient beings remain deluded about this same essential, originally bright substance — like an eye that sees, yet cannot see itself — not truly lost, only unrecognized through delusion, hence "left behind, overlooked."

Sentient beings rely, all day long, on the very function and power of this original brightness — the eye seeing form, the ear hearing sound, and so on up through the mind knowing phenomena, every bit of it nothing but this original brightness at work — yet remain entirely unaware of it, using it daily without ever asking who, exactly, is doing the seeing and the hearing. Having thus lost sight of the true root, one inevitably relies entirely on the false root instead, grasping outward at the six sense-objects, giving rise to delusion and karma on that basis, and receiving retribution accordingly — wrongly falling into the six destinies, turning without rest. This is the very hinge on which the whole sūtra's method turns: setting aside consciousness and relying instead on the sense-faculty's own true nature. The seven refutations already given are precisely the breaking of the false root; the "ten displays of seeing" that follow in fascicle 3 are precisely the revealing of the true root.

"Enough, Ānanda! That Is Not Your Mind!" 正斥妄識非心

〖經〗即時如來舉金色臂，屈五輪指，語阿難言：汝今見不？……阿難言：如來現今徵心所在，而我以心推窮尋逐，即能推者，我將為心。佛言：咄！阿難！此非汝心！

〖講〗如來先以無言之教舉拳驗見，欲令阿難於金拳舉處，回光返照，識得本明；奈阿難鈍根不契，屢問屢答，終究只認「見塵」而不識「見性」，答「同將眼見」，仍是循塵不返，遺卻識精，誤認肉眼為能見。此為第二次徵心（全經僅有兩次真正徵心：一在前文「唯心與目，今何所在」，一即此處「汝目可見，以何為心，當我拳耀」；世所謂「七處徵心」，實則七番破處，並非七次徵心）。阿難仍答「即能推者，我將為心」，依舊認妄為真。佛遂奮起獅子之威，猛喝一聲：「咄！此非汝心！」如金剛王寶劍，直斬命根不斷之處。

Sūtra: At once the Tathāgata raised his golden-hued arm, curled his five fingers into a fist, and said to Ānanda: "Do you see this?" ... Ānanda said: "The Tathāgata is now asking me where the mind is to be found; and since it is by mind that I search everywhere for it, whatever it is in me that is doing the searching — that, I take to be my mind." The Buddha said: "Enough, Ānanda! That is not your mind!"

Commentary: The Tathāgata first teaches wordlessly, raising his fist to test what Ānanda actually sees, hoping that at the very moment the golden fist is raised, Ānanda might turn his own light back on itself and recognize his own original brightness directly. But Ānanda's faculties are too dull to meet the teaching where it is offered: question after question, answer after answer, and still he recognizes only the dust-object seen, never the very nature of seeing itself — answering, at last, "together with the assembly, I see it with the eyes," still chasing after sense-objects rather than turning back to their source, overlooking his own essential consciousness and mistaking the physical eye for what actually sees. This is the sūtra's second true asking of "where is the mind" (the entire sūtra contains only two such genuine askings: the first, earlier, "where, exactly, are this mind and these eyes now"; the second, right here, "what did you take as the mind, that it should be dazzled by my fist" — what is popularly called "the seven-location search for the mind" is, properly speaking, seven rounds of refuting proposed locations, not seven separate askings of the question itself). Ānanda still answers, "whatever it is in me that is doing the searching — that, I take to be my mind," clinging once more to the false as though it were the true. And so the Buddha rouses himself with a lion's full authority and gives a single thunderous shout: "Enough! That is not your mind!" — like the vajra king's own jeweled sword, cutting straight through to the very root of the problem, the one place Ānanda's life-line has never yet been severed.

〖經〗阿難矍然！避座合掌，起立白佛：此非我心，當名何等？佛告阿難：此是前塵，虛妄相想，惑汝真性，由汝無始，至於今生，認賊為子，失汝元常，故受輪轉。

〖講〗阿難被喝，驚懼不安，避座合掌，急問：「此非我心，究竟該名何等？」——足見阿難一向唯知此心，今聞說「非心」，正如人執頑石為美玉，忽聞非玉，頓失至寶，故驚懼失措，急欲索其真名。佛乃指明：此能推之心，實是對現前塵境所起之虛妄相上，又生一層分別想，塵有則有，塵無則無，隨塵生滅，本無實體，卻能惑亂真性。由汝無始劫來，直至今生，都將此妄想認作真心，如同認賊作子，反棄真子而不尋覓；既已認妄，自必遺真，故曰「失汝元常」——元者本也，常者常住真心。此心非真失，只是迷而不知，一切皆由妄想用事，依之起惑造業，依業感報，遂受輪迴生死之苦。

Sūtra: Startled, Ānanda rose from his seat, joined his palms, stood, and said to the Buddha: "If that is not my mind, then what should it be called?" The Buddha said to Ānanda: "This is nothing but the false, insubstantial appearance of thought conditioned by what is in front of you, and it is deluding your true nature. Ever since beginningless time and continuing into this very life, you have mistaken a thief for your own son, losing your own original, ever-abiding nature — and so you have undergone this turning in the wheel."

Commentary: Startled by the shout, Ānanda rises in alarm, joins his palms, and asks urgently: if this is not his mind, then what should it even be called? This alone shows how completely, all along, Ānanda has known only this one mind — hearing now that it is "not mind" is like a man who has always taken a plain stone for a fine jade suddenly learning it is not jade at all, losing his most treasured possession in an instant, and so he scrambles, alarmed, to learn its true name. The Buddha explains directly: this very searching mind is nothing but a further layer of discrimination arising on top of the already-false appearance of whatever object happens to be in front of one — present when its object is present, absent when its object is absent, rising and falling entirely with its object, without any real substance of its own at all — and yet it is fully capable of confusing and deluding one's true nature. From beginningless kalpas up through this very life, Ānanda has taken this false thought for his true mind, exactly like mistaking a thief for one's own son, and so abandoning the real son without ever searching for him. Having once mistaken the false this way, the true is inevitably left behind — hence "losing your own original, ever-abiding nature." "Original" means what was always so; "ever-abiding" refers to the ever-abiding true mind. This mind was never truly lost — only unrecognized through delusion — and it is this very delusion, acting in the false mind's place, that gives rise to affliction, creates karma, draws retribution accordingly, and so brings on the suffering of birth, death, and the turning wheel.

Ānanda's Fear, and the Buddha's Reassurance 丑三 推妄識無體

〖經〗阿難白佛言：世尊！我佛寵弟，心愛佛故，令我出家。我心何獨供養如來，乃至遍歷恆沙國土，承事諸佛，及善知識，發大勇猛，行諸一切難行法事，皆用此心；縱令謗法，永退善根，亦因此心。若此發明，不是心者，我乃無心，同諸土木？離此覺知，更無所有，云何如來，說此非心？我實驚怖！兼此大眾，無不疑惑。惟垂大悲，開示未悟。

〖講〗阿難因聞「非心」之斥，遍歷生平善惡諸事，一一皆謂用此心：供佛、承事善知識、發勇猛心行難行事，是此心之善用；縱令謗法退善根，亦是此心之惡用——此心實為一身善惡之總司令。「若此發明不是心者，我乃無心，同諸土木」，「離此覺知，更無所有」：阿難仍不知，正是這「覺知」之心，如同二祖問初祖安心，初祖曰「將心來，與汝安」，二祖覓心了不可得，方才言下大悟——阿難若能於此當下識得「覓心了不可得」，便可頓悟，免去下文許多周折，可惜他仍執此心必有一「處」可尋，故仍驚怖疑惑，求佛開示。

Sūtra: Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am the Buddha's own favored cousin, and it was love for the Buddha in my heart that led me to leave home. It is with this very mind that I have made offerings to the Tathāgata, and, further still, traveled through lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges to serve every buddha and every good spiritual friend, giving rise to great courage and carrying out every manner of difficult practice — all of this with this same mind. And if I were ever to slander the Dharma and permanently lose my roots of goodness, that too would be because of this same mind. If, as you now reveal, this is not mind at all, then I would have no mind whatsoever, no different from earth or wood. Apart from this very awareness, I have nothing else at all — so how can the Tathāgata say this is not mind? I am truly filled with dread! And this whole assembly, too, is without exception thrown into doubt. I beg only that you, in your great compassion, open a path for those of us not yet awakened."

Commentary: Struck by being told this is "not mind," Ānanda runs back through every good and evil act of his whole life, insisting each one used this very mind: making offerings to the Buddha, serving good spiritual friends, giving rise to courage and carrying out difficult practices — all the good uses of this mind; while slandering the Dharma and permanently losing one's roots of goodness would likewise be its evil use — this mind, in other words, seemingly the supreme commander of good and evil throughout an entire life. "If this is not mind, then I would have no mind at all, no different from earth or wood"; "apart from this very awareness, I have nothing else at all" — Ānanda still does not realize that this very "awareness" he clings to is exactly the same mind at issue when the Second Patriarch once asked the First Patriarch to pacify his mind, and the First Patriarch answered, "Bring me your mind, and I will pacify it for you" — whereupon the Second Patriarch searched for his own mind and found it utterly unobtainable, and was awakened on the spot by those very words. Had Ānanda, right at this moment, recognized that same "searched for and utterly unobtainable," he too might have awakened at once, sparing everything that follows. But instead he still clings to the assumption that this mind must have some "place" yet to be found, and so remains afraid and doubtful, begging the Buddha for further instruction.

〖經〗爾時世尊開示阿難，及諸大眾，欲令心入無生法忍。於師子座摩阿難頂，而告之言：如來常說：諸法所生，唯心所現；一切因果，世界微塵，因心成體。阿難：若諸世界，一切所有，其中乃至草葉縷結，詰其根元，咸有體性，縱令虛空，亦有名貌。何況清淨，妙淨明心，性一切心，而自無體？

〖講〗佛先安慰而後開導，欲令阿難悟見性為真心，得入無生法忍——於一一法，當體如如，不見有少法生、有少法滅。此心真妄二本，妄本無有生滅可言（因根本無實體），真本亦無所謂生滅（因本自不生不滅），一切生滅之相，皆如眼中之翳所見空華，翳眼見空華，華實不曾生，生既不生，滅何所滅？故不見有少法生滅，即是一切事究竟堅固之首楞嚴大定。「如來常說，諸法所生，唯心所現」：一切十界依、正二報，一一皆是因心成體，如《起信論》所云「心為一大總相法門體」。「若諸世界……縱令虛空，亦有名貌」：世間一草一葉一縷一結，追詰根元，尚且各有體性；縱是至大的虛空，亦有「虛空」之名、「通達無礙」之貌——何況那清淨、妙淨明心，作為一切心之體性所依，反倒自己沒有體性嗎？此正是以萬物皆有體，反顯真心必有體，阿難所憂「離此覺知，更無所有」，實屬多慮。

Sūtra: At that time the World-Honored One, wishing to lead Ānanda and the whole assembly to enter the forbearance of the unborn nature of all things, opened this teaching. Resting his hand on Ānanda's crown as he sat on his lion's seat, he said: "The Tathāgata has always taught that all phenomena that arise are manifestations of mind alone; every cause and effect, every world, down to the finest mote of dust, takes on its substance because of mind. Ānanda, in any world, among all that exists — down to a single blade of grass, a leaf, a thread, a knot — trace any of it back to its very root, and each one has its own substantial nature; even empty space itself has a name and a characteristic. How much more, then, should the pure, wondrously pure, bright mind — the very nature underlying every kind of mind — itself be without any substance at all?"

Commentary: The Buddha first offers reassurance, then instruction, hoping to lead Ānanda to awaken to the seeing-nature as his true mind and so enter the forbearance of the unborn — where, with regard to every single phenomenon, one sees it exactly as it truly is, with not the slightest thing arising or ceasing anywhere at all. Both roots of mind, true and false, are in this sense free of arising and ceasing: the false root has no arising or ceasing to speak of, since it has no real substance to begin with; the true root likewise has nothing properly called arising or ceasing, since it was never born and never ceases in the first place. Every appearance of arising and ceasing is exactly like the flowers conjured in empty space by a diseased eye: the diseased eye sees flowers, but the flowers were never truly born — and if never born, then what, exactly, could ever cease? Seeing not the slightest phenomenon truly arise or cease is exactly the Śūraṅgama's great samādhi, in which every single thing is, in the end, utterly stable and unshaken.

"The Tathāgata has always taught that all phenomena that arise are manifestations of mind alone": every one of the ten dharma-realms' rewards, both the being itself and its surrounding world, both ordinary and sacred, both defiled and pure, both cause and effect, takes on its substance because of mind — exactly as the Awakening of Faith puts it, "mind is the substance underlying the one great inclusive gateway of all phenomena." As for "in any world ... even empty space itself has a name and characteristic": even a single blade of grass, one leaf, one thread, one knot in this world, traced back to its very root, still has some substantial nature of its own; even the vast reach of empty space has the name "space" and the characteristic of boundless, unobstructed openness. How much more, then, should the pure, wondrously pure, bright mind — itself the very nature that every kind of mind depends on — be without any substance of its own? This whole passage argues from the fact that even the smallest thing in the world has substance, to show, by contrast, that the true mind must have substance all the more certainly — which means Ānanda's worry, that "apart from this awareness, I have nothing else at all," was never anything to fear in the first place.

〖經〗若汝執吝分別覺觀，所了知性，必為心者，此心即應離諸一切色、香、味、觸諸塵事業，別有全性。如汝今者，承聽我法，此則因聲，而有分別。縱滅一切見、聞、覺、知，內守幽閒，猶為法塵，分別影事。……我非敕汝，執為非心，但汝於心，微細揣摩，若離前塵，有分別性，即真汝心。若分別性，離塵無體，斯則前塵，分別影事。塵非常住，若變滅時，此心則同龜毛兔角；則汝法身，同於斷滅。其誰修證，無生法忍？

〖講〗佛先按定：若阿難執吝不捨、所了知之分別覺觀性，必要認作真心，那麼此心理應能離開一切色、香、味、觸等塵境事務，自有完整獨立的體性存在——這是就理而論。再就事而驗：如阿難現今聽法，正是因有可分別之聲塵，纔有能分別之性，離開聲塵便無此性可言，可見此心離塵畢竟無體。縱然滅去一切見聞覺知，內守於一片幽閒寂靜之境——此正是凡外修定者所common執取的「法性」——實則仍是定中獨頭意識所緣之法塵，仍屬「分別影事」，並非真心。佛又縱破兼施：並非強令阿難認定此心「非心」，只是要他細細揣摩：若這能分別之心真能離開眼前塵境仍有分別之體性，便可承認那就是真心；然若此分別性一旦離塵便無自體可言，那就只是前塵所引發的分別影子罷了。塵境本非常住，一旦變化消滅，此心也將隨之同歸於無，如龜毛兔角，有名無實；若阿難所謂的「法身」竟然也隨塵滅而消滅，那還有誰能修證無生法忍呢？

Sūtra: "If you cling fast to this nature of discriminating, deliberating awareness — this capacity for understanding and knowing — and insist that it must be your mind, then this mind ought to be able to stand apart from every sense-object of form, smell, taste, and touch, possessing its own complete nature all on its own. Yet, as you are right now, listening to my teaching — this very act of discrimination arises only because of sound. Even if you were to extinguish every trace of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing, and hold instead to some inward state of quiet seclusion, that too would still be nothing but a shadow cast by mental phenomena, a further act of discrimination. ... I am not forcing you to accept that this is not mind — only asking you to examine, in the finest detail, your own mind: if, apart from whatever is presently in front of you, some capacity for discrimination genuinely remains, then that would truly be your own mind. But if this capacity for discrimination has no substance at all apart from whatever object is in front of it, then it is nothing but a shadow cast by that very object. Objects do not remain forever — when they shift and vanish, this mind, too, would vanish along with them, becoming nothing but a name with no substance, like the fur of a tortoise or the horns of a rabbit; and if that is so, then your own Dharma-body would likewise come to an end along with them. Who, then, would be left to cultivate and realize the forbearance of the unborn nature of all things?"

Commentary: The Buddha first fixes the terms of the argument: if Ānanda insists, clinging fast and unwilling to let go, that this nature of discriminating, deliberating awareness must be the true mind, then this mind ought, in principle, to be able to stand apart from every sense-object — form, smell, taste, touch — possessing a complete and independent nature entirely on its own. This much follows from the logic of the claim itself. The Buddha then tests it against the facts: right now, as Ānanda listens to the teaching, discrimination arises only because there is a sound to discriminate — apart from that sound, no such capacity exists at all, proving that this mind, apart from its object, has no substance whatsoever. Even if every trace of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing were extinguished, and one held instead to a still, secluded inward state — precisely the state that ordinary and non-Buddhist meditators commonly mistake for "the nature of dharmas" — this, too, is really nothing but a mental object attended to by the isolated discriminating consciousness active even within deep concentration: still a mere "shadow of discrimination," not the true mind at all. The Buddha then both concedes and withdraws the concession in the same breath: he is not forcing Ānanda to accept that this is not mind — only asking him to examine closely whether this discriminating mind can retain any capacity for discrimination once entirely apart from its object. If it can, then it may indeed be called the true mind. But if this capacity for discrimination has no substance whatsoever apart from its object, then it is nothing more than a shadow cast by that very object. Sense-objects themselves are never permanent — once they shift and vanish, this "mind" would vanish along with them, a mere name with no substance, like the fur of a tortoise or the horns of a rabbit. And if what Ānanda calls his own "Dharma-body" would likewise be extinguished the moment its objects vanish, then who could possibly remain to cultivate and realize the forbearance of the unborn at all?

The Assembly Falls Silent, and the Buddha's Concluding Verdict 子二 大眾知非無辯 · 癸三 結歸其判

〖經〗即時阿難，與諸大眾，默然自失！佛告阿難：世間一切諸修學人，現前雖成九次第定，不得漏盡成阿羅漢，皆由執此生死妄想，誤為真實。是故汝今雖得多聞，不成聖果。

〖講〗阿難與大眾聞此推破，已知向來所執之心確實無體，卻又不知何者方為真心，進退失據，故默然無語，如商人忽失至寶。佛乃結歸最初之判：一切修學之人，縱然修成九次第定（四禪、四空八定，加滅盡定），若不得漏盡、不成阿羅漢，皆因執著此生死妄想（第六識分別心）誤以為真實常住真心之故。阿難縱然多聞第一，若仍執妄為真，終究不能證得聖果——此正與篇首所判「一切眾生，皆由不知常住真心，性淨明體，用諸妄想，此想不真，故有輪轉」前後呼應，首尾一貫。

Sūtra: At that moment Ānanda, together with the whole assembly, fell silent, as though they had lost something precious. The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Every practitioner in the world, even one who presently accomplishes the nine successive stages of concentration, if they do not attain freedom from outflows and become an arhat, does so only because they cling to this same false thought bound to birth and death, mistaking it for something real. This is exactly why you, though foremost in learning, have not yet accomplished the fruit of sagehood."

Commentary: Hearing this refutation, Ānanda and the whole assembly now know for certain that the mind they had clung to all along truly has no substance — yet still do not know what the true mind actually is, caught between the two with nowhere to stand, and so fall silent, as though a merchant had suddenly discovered his most treasured possession gone. The Buddha now returns to the very verdict with which this whole section began: every practitioner, however far they progress — even to the nine successive stages of concentration (the four dhyānas and four formless absorptions, plus the cessation-attainment) — if they fail to attain freedom from outflows and become a true arhat, does so for exactly this reason: clinging to this same false thought bound to birth and death (the discriminating sixth consciousness), and mistaking it for the ever-abiding true mind. Ānanda, though foremost among the Buddha's disciples in learning, will never accomplish the fruit of sagehood so long as he continues mistaking the false for the true — a verdict that closes the circle back to where this entire section opened: "every sentient being continues through birth and death solely because it does not recognize the ever-abiding true mind, whose nature is pure and bright, and instead makes use of false thinking. Because such thinking is not true, one keeps turning in the wheel of transmigration." Beginning and end answer one another exactly.

Reading note: Yuanying's fascicle 2 ends exactly here — "初斥破所執妄心，以開奢摩他路竟" ("thus concludes refuting the clung-to false mind, in order to open the path of śamatha") — with the false mind thoroughly dismantled but the true mind not yet positively shown. That positive revelation — the "ten displays of seeing" (十番顯見) that occupy most of the sūtra's own fascicle 2 and Yuanying's fascicle 3 — is deliberately held back. As Yuanying notes in passing above, breaking the false root and revealing the true root are two distinct movements of the text, and this fascicle covers only the first. Readers of the companion bare-sūtra fascicle 1 will recognize this entire episode — it closes that fascicle with "the blind man and the lamp," a first hint, not yet developed, of where the true seeing-nature will be found.
