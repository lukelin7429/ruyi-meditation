Once the Flowers Vanish, They Never Return

Pūrṇa presses a third worry: if a buddha's mind was never different from an ordinary being's, could the whole cycle of delusion simply start over again after enlightenment? The Buddha answers with a string of analogies — a traveler who can never again mistake south for north, flowers that never bloom again once an eye disease is cured — before answering Pūrṇa's second question in full: how the five great elements can each pervade the whole Dharma-realm without destroying one another, and the complete teaching of the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha. The fascicle closes with the sūtra's most famous parable: a man named Yajñadatta, convinced by his own reflection that he has lost his own head.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 9 answered Pūrṇa's first question — how a world, sentient beings, and karma arise and continue — but this answer provokes an immediate worry: if a buddha's own awareness was never actually different from a sentient being's, could delusion simply well up again after enlightenment, the whole cycle starting over? The Buddha spends the opening of this fascicle putting that worry to rest with a run of analogies, before turning to Pūrṇa's still-unanswered second question: how can the five great elements each pervade the whole Dharma-realm without destroying one another? Answering this in full requires the tathāgatagarbha's third and final aspect — empty and non-empty at once — completing the doctrine only partly introduced in fascicle 9. The fascicle closes with Pūrṇa's own remaining doubt (where does delusion first come from, if it has no real cause at all?) and the Buddha's answer: the sūtra's single most famous parable, a man named Yajñadatta who becomes convinced, looking in a mirror, that he has lost his own head.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. Several passages have already been translated, in condensed form, in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 4 — the lost-traveler analogy, the reflected-sun-in-water analogy, the "neither X nor not-X" litany describing the tathāgatagarbha, and the wish-fulfilling jewel sewn into a beggar's own robe — that exact English is reused here wherever it matches. Where the bare-sūtra translation elided material with an ellipsis — including the Yajñadatta story itself, named here for the first time — Yuanying's own clause-by-clause commentary requires the fuller text, translated fresh. All of Yuanying's own commentary is new.

Pūrṇa's Further Doubt: A Beginning Without End? 丑二 兼釋轉難 · 寅初 滿慈執因疑果

Chinese Source

〖經〗富樓那言：若此妙覺，本妙覺明，與如來心，不增不減。無狀忽生，山河大地，諸有為相。如來今得妙空明覺，山河大地，有為習漏，何當復生？

〖講〗當機執眾生因性有始，疑如來果德有終。乃言：若此眾生所具，妙明覺體，本來自妙，無有世界之礙；本來自覺，無有眾生之迷；本來自明，無有業果之昏；與如來返本還元之心，平等一如，佛心比生心，並不增一絲毫；生心比佛心，亦不減一絲毫。

無狀忽生，山河大地，諸有為相者：無狀即無因無故也。眾生最初無故，妄欲加明於覺，遂致從迷入迷，以妄成妄，由妄惑起妄業，依妄業招妄報，忽生山河大地，依報之世界，攝虛空；諸有為相，正報之眾生，兼業果。是由本真之心，忽生種種妄法。

上段執眾生因性有始，此段疑如來果德有終。故難云：如來今得妙空明覺。妙空即妙性真空，彌滿清淨，中不容他，復其無物之本體，如摩尼珠；明覺即妙明本覺，虛靈朗鑑，洞澈法界，還其天然之照體，如大圓鏡。與眾生妙覺明，未生山河等法之前，無二無別。眾生既從真起妄，而如來今者，返妄歸真，山河大地之世界，有為相之眾生，與習漏之業果，何時復當再生耶？此難全同圓覺經中，金剛藏菩薩第三難：「一曰一切眾生，本成佛道，何故復有一切無明？二曰若諸無明，眾生本有，何因緣故，復說本來成佛？三曰十方異生，本成佛道，後起無明，一切如來，何時復生一切煩惱？」難意全同。所異者彼約煩惱苦因，此約依、正苦果，同是從真起妄也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Pūrṇa said: "If this wondrous awareness — originally wondrous, aware, and bright — is no different, neither more nor less, from the Tathāgata's own mind, why would mountains, rivers, and the great earth ever suddenly arise from it at all? And now that the Tathāgata has attained this wondrous, empty, bright awareness, will mountains, rivers, and their habituated afflictions ever arise again?"

Commentary: Pūrṇa, directly addressed, clings to the notion that sentient beings' own causal nature has a beginning, and so doubts whether the Buddha's own fruit-virtue might likewise have an end. He says: if this wondrous, bright substance of awareness possessed by sentient beings is, from the start, of itself wondrous, with no obstruction of any world in it at all; of itself aware, with no confusion of any sentient being in it at all; of itself bright, with no darkness of any karma-fruit in it at all — and is, moreover, perfectly equal, one and the same, with the Tathāgata's own mind, returned to its root and origin — then the Buddha's mind, set against the mind of a sentient being, is not increased by the slightest hair's breadth; and a sentient being's mind, set against the Buddha's mind, is not diminished by the slightest hair's breadth either.

"Why would mountains, rivers, and the great earth ever suddenly arise from it at all": "suddenly, for no reason" means with no cause, no basis at all. Sentient beings, from the very first, with no basis at all, falsely wished to add brightness to awareness, and so fell from confusion into ever-deeper confusion, falseness compounding upon falseness — from false delusion, false karma arose; dependent on false karma, false retribution was summoned — and so mountains, rivers, and the great earth, the circumstantial world of retribution, including empty space, suddenly arose; and every conditioned appearance, the direct retribution of sentient beings, together with karma's own fruit, likewise arose. All of this from the originally true mind, suddenly giving rise to every kind of false phenomenon.

The passage above clings to the notion that sentient beings' own causal nature has a beginning; this passage doubts whether the Buddha's own fruit-virtue might have an end. Hence the challenge: "now that the Tathāgata has attained this wondrous, empty, bright awareness" — "wondrous emptiness" means the wondrous nature's own true emptiness, filling everything, pure, admitting nothing else at all, restored to its own substance where nothing exists at all, like a wish-fulfilling pearl; "bright awareness" means this same wondrous, bright, original enlightenment, empty, spiritual, and radiantly clear, penetrating the whole Dharma-realm through and through, restored to its own naturally illuminating substance, like a great, round mirror. This is no different at all from sentient beings' own wondrous, aware brightness, before mountains and the rest of these phenomena ever arose. Sentient beings have already given rise to falseness out of truth — but now that the Tathāgata has turned falseness back into truth, when will mountains, rivers, and the great earth as a world, sentient beings as conditioned appearance, and karma's own fruit built up from habituated affliction, ever arise again?

This challenge is entirely the same as Bodhisattva Vajragarbha's third challenge in the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment: "First: if every sentient being has, at root, already accomplished the buddha-path, why is there still, even now, every kind of ignorance? Second: if this ignorance is something sentient beings inherently possess, on what basis does the sūtra still speak of them as originally having already accomplished buddhahood? Third: if beings of every kind throughout the ten directions have, at root, already accomplished the buddha-path, and ignorance arises only afterward, then when will every one of the Tathāgatas give rise to every kind of affliction all over again?" The point of the challenge is entirely the same. What differs is only that the earlier challenge speaks in terms of affliction as the cause of suffering, while this one speaks in terms of direct and circumstantial retribution as the fruit of suffering — both, alike, cases of falseness arising out of truth.

The Buddha's Answer: Falseness Never Arises Again 寅二 佛分真妄喻釋 · 卯初 喻妄不復生

Chinese Source

〖經〗佛告富樓那：譬如迷人，於一聚落，惑南為北。此迷為復因迷而有？因悟而出？富樓那言：如是迷人，亦不因迷，又不因悟。何以故？迷本無根，云何因迷？悟非生迷，云何因悟？佛言：彼之迷人，正在迷時，倏有悟人，指示令悟。富樓那！於意云何？此人縱迷於此聚落，更生迷不？不也！世尊。富樓那！十方如來，亦復如是。此迷無本，性畢竟空，昔本無迷，似有迷覺；覺迷迷滅，覺不生迷。

〖講〗此舉喻。迷人、乃迷方之人，喻已起無明之眾生；聚落、乃人煙聚止之村落，喻如來藏；惑南方為北方；南方實不轉為北方，喻迷時從真起妄，真體不變，真不成妄，妄性本空也。

此辨定迷無所從。故問曰：此迷為復因迷而有耶？為復因悟而出耶？富樓那言：如是迷人之迷，亦不因迷有，又不因悟出。何以故下，自行徵釋。迷本無根者：此迷即指最初一念無明妄動，而為諸法之因，諸法皆因無明而有，而無明更無所因也，故曰「無根」；又復無體，自體尚不可得，云何可說，因迷而有？斷無因自生自之理，此答因迷之難。悟非生迷者：悟迷敵體相翻，悟則非迷，迷則非悟，既相翻，自不相生，云何可說，因悟所出？如明暗相背，云何可說，暗因明生耶？此答因悟之難。

問：「法中妄從真起，喻中云何不許，迷因悟出？」答：「法中正不許真能起妄，但說妄依真起，如影依鏡現，終非鏡體自生也。」此文滿慈據喻而答，儼然不錯，就法而言，足證滿慈於法未徹。迷喻無明，悟喻本覺，無明不因無明而有，不因本覺而出，此為正理。迷本無根，云何因迷者：喻無明之先，本無無明，云何可說因無明有？悟非生迷，云何因悟者：喻本覺與無明，一真一妄，真妄相背，云何可說，因本覺出？

此辨無明不復起。佛言彼迷於聚落之人，正在迷南為北之時，倏忽也有悟人，辨明南北之人，指示分明，令得了悟，不至將南作北。故問滿慈，在汝之意，以為云何？此迷人，縱使先迷於此聚落，既經指示令悟之後，更生迷不？答言：「不也，世尊！」佛欲令就喻知法，故作是問。

法合：彼之迷人，合從真起妄，有無明之眾生，正在迷位之時，忽遇於佛；悟人、即大覺悟之佛，為之指示，令得開悟，悟明真本無妄，斷盡無明；於是眾生，返妄歸真，永不復迷也。此法合。十方諸佛如來，三覺圓滿，亦同悟後不復更迷也。

此詳盡前義。此迷無本，即無明無體，同前迷本無根之義。其性徹底元空，亦如迷方之迷，更無所因也。昔本無迷，似有迷覺者：不可作眾生未妄之前，本無無明解，當作昔日在眾生位中，本無無明，不過相似，有迷時妄覺而已。既曰相似，即非實有，以無明無體故。亦如迷方者，正在迷方之時，本來無迷可得，亦不過相似，有迷時妄覺，惑南為北也。覺迷迷滅，覺不生迷者：諸佛在因地之中，遇善知識開示，無明無體，起智觀察，覺得無明是妄，常體即空，則心中無明即滅，以真覺性中，本無無明也。如下文所云，心中達多，狂性自歇，歇即菩提。既覺之後，永不再起無明。亦同迷方之人，倏有悟人，指示令悟，更不生迷也。上二句迷時似有，此二句悟後永無，言正當迷時，已即無迷可得，而況既覺之後，豈復生於迷乎？

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Pūrṇa: "It is like a confused person in a village who mistakes south for north — does this confusion exist because of confusion itself, or does it come out of understanding?" Pūrṇa said: "Such a person's confusion arises neither because of confusion nor because of understanding. Why? Confusion has, at its root, no cause of its own — how could it be 'because of confusion'? And understanding does not produce confusion — how could it be 'because of understanding'?" The Buddha said: "Suppose that, while this confused person is still confused, someone who understands suddenly points out the directions and sets him straight. Pūrṇa, what do you think — even though he was once confused, would he ever become confused about this same village again?" "No, World-Honored One." "Pūrṇa, it is exactly the same with the Tathāgatas of the ten directions. This confusion has no root; its nature is, in the end, empty. It was never truly there to begin with — it only seemed as though awareness had become confused. Once awareness recognizes the confusion, the confusion vanishes; and awareness itself never again produces confusion."

Commentary: This raises the analogy. "The confused person" is one confused about direction, standing for a sentient being in whom ignorance has already arisen; "the village" is a settled place where people dwell together, standing for the tathāgatagarbha; "mistaking south for north" — south never actually turns into north — stands for how, in confusion, falseness arises out of truth, while the true substance itself never changes: truth never actually becomes falseness, and falseness's own nature is, at root, empty.

This determines that confusion comes from nowhere at all. Hence the question: does this confusion exist because of confusion itself, or come out of understanding? Pūrṇa answers that this confused person's confusion arises neither from confusion nor out of understanding — and explains why himself. "Confusion has, at its root, no cause of its own": this confusion refers to that very first single thought of ignorance falsely stirring, which becomes the cause of every phenomenon — every phenomenon exists because of ignorance, while ignorance itself has no further cause behind it at all, hence "no root"; and, further, having no substance of its own — its own substance not even attainable — how could one say it exists "because of confusion"? There is simply no principle by which a thing could cause itself to arise from itself. "Understanding does not produce confusion": understanding and confusion stand as direct opposites — understanding is exactly not confusion, confusion is exactly not understanding; being direct opposites, neither could possibly give rise to the other — how, then, could one say it comes "out of understanding"? Just as brightness and darkness stand opposed, how could one say darkness arises because of brightness?

Question: "In the doctrine itself, falseness arises out of truth — so why, in the analogy, is it not allowed that confusion comes out of understanding?" Answer: "The doctrine itself does not actually allow that truth is capable of giving rise to falseness either — it only says that falseness arises dependent on truth, the way a reflection appears dependent on a mirror, never that the mirror's own substance itself generates the reflection." Pūrṇa's answer here, based strictly on the analogy, is entirely correct — yet, measured against the doctrine itself, it is proof enough that Pūrṇa has not yet fully penetrated the doctrine. Confusion stands for ignorance; understanding stands for original enlightenment. Ignorance does not exist because of ignorance, nor does it come out of original enlightenment — this is the correct principle.

This determines that ignorance, once ended, never arises again. The Buddha says: while that person confused about the village is still mistaking south for north, someone who understands, capable of distinguishing south from north correctly, suddenly points it out clearly, bringing about full understanding, so that south is no longer mistaken for north. Hence the question to Pūrṇa: in your own understanding, even though this confused person was once confused about this same village, would he, once shown clearly and brought to understanding, ever become confused about it again? He answers: "No, World-Honored One!" The Buddha asks this precisely so that the doctrine may be grasped by way of the analogy.

Matched to its meaning: the confused person corresponds to a sentient being in whom ignorance has arisen, falseness arising out of truth; while still in this confused state, he suddenly encounters a Buddha — "the person who understands" being exactly the greatly awakened Buddha — who points things out for him, bringing about awakening: awakening to the fact that truth was never, at root, mixed with falseness, and severing ignorance entirely. And so this sentient being turns falseness back into truth, and is never confused again. This applies the analogy directly: the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, perfect and complete in the three aspects of awakening, are likewise never confused again, once awakened.

This explains the earlier point in full. "This confusion has no root" means ignorance itself has no substance — its nature, examined to the very bottom, is originally empty, exactly like confusion about direction, having no further cause behind it at all. "It was never truly there to begin with — it only seemed as though awareness had become confused": this should not be read as meaning that, before a sentient being ever became false, there was, originally, no ignorance at all — rather, it means that, in the past, while a sentient being remained in that confused state, there was, in fact, originally no ignorance there either — it was merely something that seemed, at the time of confusion, like a falsely-arisen awareness. Being called merely "seeming," it was never genuinely real at all, since ignorance has no substance of its own. It is exactly like confusion about direction: at the very moment of being confused about direction, there was, originally, no confusion to be found there at all — merely something that seemed, at that moment, like a falsely-arisen awareness, mistaking south for north. "Once awareness recognizes the confusion, the confusion vanishes; and awareness itself never again produces confusion": the buddhas, at the causal stage, encountering a good teacher's instruction that ignorance has no substance, give rise to wisdom and observe directly, recognizing that ignorance is false, its constant substance itself empty — and so ignorance within the mind is thereupon dissolved, since true awareness's own nature never, in fact, contained any ignorance at all. As a later passage puts it: "the madness in the mind of Yajñadatta subsides of itself, and this subsiding is itself bodhi." Once awakened, ignorance never arises again — exactly like the person confused about direction, who, once shown clearly by someone who understands, is never confused again. The two lines above describe how, at the moment of confusion, it merely seemed to exist; these two lines describe how, after awakening, it is forever gone — meaning that, even at the very moment of confusion, there was, in fact, no confusion to be found there at all — how much less, then, after awakening, could confusion ever arise again?

Chinese Source

〖經〗亦如翳人，見空中華，翳病若除，華於空滅。忽有愚人，於彼空華所滅空地，待華更生。汝觀是人，為愚為慧？富樓那言：空元無華，妄見生滅，見華滅空，已是顛倒，敕令更出，斯實狂癡，云何更名，如是狂人，為愚為慧？佛言：如汝所解。云何問言：諸佛如來，妙覺明空，何當更出生也，山河大地？

〖講〗《正脈》云：據上文滿慈於萬法，問生續之詳，如來答無明為生續之本。今佛於上科，先以喻明，所答無明本來常空，非研斷始空。而此更以喻明：所問萬法，現今即無；非先無後有，亦非今有後無。《圓覺》答難處，亦有此喻，卻是翳比無明，華比萬法，空比真體。彼文三節平渾，今經前有迷方喻無明，後有木金喻真體，故此空華，單喻萬法耳；即前世界等三也。

此舉喻。如眼有翳病之人，見空中有華。空原無華，翳眼妄見，翳病若得除滅，華於空滅。不特見空華是妄見，即見華滅空，亦是妄見。何以故？翳眼見空華，華本不曾生，生既不生，滅何所滅？故見生見滅，同一妄也。忽有無智之愚人，於彼空華所滅虛空之處，等待空華，何時更生空華。

翳人、喻有無明之眾生，以無明力，轉本有之智光，成能見之妄見，見真空法性之中，有世界、眾生、業果。空喻真空，空華喻山河大地，諸有為相。無明一滅，萬法皆空；當知萬法本空，不待無明滅始空，即有無明，妄見之時，身、心、世界，何嘗實有？亦如空華，不待翳愈華滅，正當翳眼，見空華時，當體即空，何嘗有生？都緣有無明之眾生，於本無之中，妄見似有而已。山河大地，有為習漏，眾生在迷，尚非實有所生，如來既得妙空明覺，無明惑盡，真空理顯，豈復更生耶？故喻忽有愚人，於彼空華，所滅虛空之處，待華更生；滿慈亦如是也。

此辨定。佛令滿慈審觀，待華更生之人，為愚耶？為慧耶？滿慈答言：空元無華，由翳眼妄見生滅；喻真空法性之中，元無山河大地等法，第以無明妄見，迷時有生，悟時有滅。見華滅空，已是顛倒執著，敕令空華更出，斯人實屬狂癡；喻若見諸法，滅妄歸空，已是顛倒分別，問如來何時更生諸法，與待華更生者，何以異耶？

此法合。滿慈於喻，所答不謬，故佛即以反難，如汝所解，已知敕令空華更出，斯實狂癡。云何竟作如是問言：如來今得妙空明覺，何當更出山河大地耶？妙覺明空，與前滿慈所問，妙空明覺，其理無二。覺指本覺照體，屬智德；空指真空寂體，屬斷德；悉皆雙具妙明，故妙覺明覺，妙空明空，隨稱俱可。佛智德究竟，五住皆盡，斷德究竟，二死永亡，方當此稱。又空即如如理，覺即如如智，皆明皆妙也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "It is also like a person with an eye disease, seeing flowers in empty space: once the disease is cured, the flowers vanish from that space. Suppose there were a foolish person who, at that very place in empty space where the flowers had vanished, waited for the flowers to appear again. What do you make of such a person — foolish, or wise?" Pūrṇa said: "Space originally has no flowers in it at all — it was only a falsely perceived arising and ceasing. Seeing the flowers vanish from space is already an inversion; to command them to appear again would be truly, madly foolish. Why, then, ask whether such a madman is foolish or wise?" The Buddha said: "Just as you have understood it. Why, then, do you ask: when will the Tathāgatas' own wondrous, aware emptiness ever again give rise to mountains, rivers, and the great earth?"

Commentary: The Zhèngmài commentary says: as the passage above shows, Pūrṇa asked for the detailed account of how the ten thousand phenomena arise and continue, and the Tathāgata answered that ignorance is the root of this arising and continuing. Now, in the section above, the Buddha first used an analogy to show that this ignorance, already answered as its root, was always empty from the start — not something that only becomes empty once investigated and severed. And here he uses a further analogy to show that the ten thousand phenomena themselves, just asked about, are, even now, simply nonexistent — neither absent before and present now, nor present now and absent later. The Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment's own answer to a similar challenge uses this same analogy, with the eye disease compared to ignorance, the flowers to the ten thousand phenomena, and empty space to the true substance. This sūtra, having already used the analogy of confusion about direction for ignorance, and going on to use gold ore and wood for the true substance, uses this flower-in-space analogy solely for the ten thousand phenomena — that is, exactly the three continuities discussed earlier.

This raises the analogy: a person with a diseased eye sees flowers in empty space. Space originally has no flowers in it at all — the diseased eye falsely perceives them; once the disease is cured, the flowers vanish from that space. Not only is seeing the flowers in space a false perception, but seeing them vanish from space is likewise a false perception. Why? The diseased eye sees flowers in space that were never, in fact, born at all — and, never having arisen, what, then, could possibly cease? Seeing them arise and seeing them cease are, alike, equally false. Suppose, then, an unwise, foolish person, at that very place in empty space where the flowers had vanished, were to wait for those same space-flowers to appear again.

The diseased person stands for a sentient being possessing ignorance, who, by the power of ignorance, converts the inherently possessed light of wisdom into a false perception capable of seeing, and sees, within the very nature of true emptiness, a world, sentient beings, and karma-fruit. Space stands for true emptiness; the flowers in space stand for mountains, rivers, and every conditioned appearance. Once ignorance is extinguished, the ten thousand phenomena are, all of them, empty — but one should understand that the ten thousand phenomena were, in fact, always empty from the start, not waiting for ignorance to be extinguished before becoming empty: even while ignorance and its false perception were present, when were body, mind, and world ever genuinely real at all? Exactly like the flowers in space, not waiting for the eye disease to be cured before vanishing — at the very moment the diseased eye sees these flowers, their very substance is already empty; when were they ever actually born at all? All of this comes down to nothing but a sentient being possessing ignorance falsely seeing something as though it existed, within what was, in truth, never there to begin with. Mountains, rivers, the great earth, and every conditioned, habituated affliction, even while sentient beings remain in confusion, were never, in fact, genuinely produced at all — so, now that the Tathāgata has attained this wondrous, empty, bright awareness, ignorance and its delusion entirely exhausted, the principle of true emptiness fully revealed, how could they possibly arise again? Hence the analogy of a foolish person, at that place in space where the flowers had vanished, waiting for them to appear again — Pūrṇa is doing exactly the same thing.

This determines the point directly: the Buddha has Pūrṇa examine closely whether the person waiting for the flowers to reappear is foolish or wise. Pūrṇa answers that space originally has no flowers in it at all, their arising and ceasing being nothing but the diseased eye's own false perception — standing for the fact that, within the true nature of true emptiness, there were, originally, no such phenomena as mountains and the great earth at all; it was only ignorance's own false perception that made them seem to arise in confusion and cease in awakening. Seeing the flowers vanish from space is already an inversion and a clinging; to command the flowers to appear again shows this person to be truly, madly foolish — standing for the fact that seeing these phenomena as dissolving falseness and returning to emptiness is already an inverted discrimination — so how does asking the Tathāgata when these phenomena will arise again differ at all from waiting for the flowers to reappear?

This applies the analogy directly: since Pūrṇa's own answer to the analogy is not mistaken, the Buddha turns it back on him as a challenge — since you already understand that commanding the flowers to appear again is truly, madly foolish, why do you still go on asking: now that the Tathāgata has attained this wondrous, empty, bright awareness, when will mountains, rivers, and the great earth ever be produced from it again? "Wondrous awareness, bright emptiness" carries the same principle as Pūrṇa's own earlier phrase, "wondrous emptiness, bright awareness" — no difference between them. "Awareness" points to original enlightenment's own illuminating substance, belonging to the virtue of wisdom; "emptiness" points to true emptiness's own still substance, belonging to the virtue of severing — both together always possessing this same wondrous brightness, so "wondrous awareness, bright awareness" and "wondrous emptiness, bright emptiness" may be spoken either way, interchangeably. Only once the Buddha's own virtue of wisdom is ultimate, all five abidings of affliction exhausted, and his virtue of severing ultimate, both kinds of death forever ended, does this title properly apply.

Sūtra: "Further, it is like gold ore, in which pure gold is mixed with dross: once this gold has been made entirely pure, it never again becomes mixed with dross. Or like wood turned to ash: it never again becomes wood. The Tathāgatas' own bodhi and nirvāṇa are exactly the same."

Commentary: The two analogies in the section above illustrate that the false cause and false fruit are, of themselves, never truly produced at all — not something that only ceases once buddhahood is attained. The two analogies in this section illustrate that true wisdom and true severing are, from the start, never subject to change at all — not something that only comes into being once buddhahood is attained. And if even sentient beings, while still in a state of confusion, have a false nature that is, at root, empty, truly unborn — how much more so the buddhas, who have already realized the ultimate fruit, uniquely wondrous, true, and constant: could this possibly still be subject to change?

"Gold ore": since gold is present within the ore, it is called "gold ore." "Mixed" means that pure gold is mixed together with dross within the ore — its own essential, true substance unchanging, standing for the substance of wisdom being unchanging. "Once this gold has been made entirely pure": through the effort of mining and smelting, the dross entirely removed, the gold becomes purely, entirely essential. "It never again becomes mixed with dross": once it has become the substance of pure gold, it never reverts to ore again — standing for the effort put into the virtue of wisdom, cultivating practice to remove delusion; once delusion is purified and wisdom perfected, without duality, without any further mixture, the virtue of wisdom accomplished, ignorance never arises again. Wood stands for affliction; ash stands for nirvāṇa. Wood cannot turn into ash of itself — it must be burned by fire before it becomes ash; fire stands for wisdom — affliction cannot, of itself, realize nirvāṇa either, but must, through wisdom, sever affliction, escape birth and death, and realize nirvāṇa. Once the virtue of severing is accomplished, affliction never arises again — hence the analogy of never again becoming wood.

This applies the analogy directly: the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, having already realized the ultimate fruit of awakening, their realized virtue of wisdom, bodhi, ultimately unchanging, exactly like pure gold no longer mixed with dross — having already transformed affliction into bodhi, they never again have any affliction at all, exactly like pure gold never again reverting to ore. The Tathāgatas' own realized virtue of severing, nirvāṇa, ultimately never subject to arising, exactly like wood burned into ash — having already transformed birth and death into nirvāṇa, they never again undergo birth and death, exactly like wood turned to ash never again becoming wood — hence "exactly the same."

This passage applying the analogy may also be read as a single, combined analogy with a single, combined meaning: gold stands for bodhi and nirvāṇa — though not produced by cultivation, they must nonetheless be revealed through cultivation; once accomplished, they are accomplished forever, never changing again — like pure gold, though gold from the very start, only becomes fully realized once smelted, and, once pure, remains pure forever, never reverting to ore again. Wood stands for affliction and birth and death — though themselves illusory, illusion is used to remove illusion, and, once extinguished, they are extinguished forever, never arising again — like wood turned to ash: wood, burned by fire, is entirely turned to ash, and, once entirely gone, is gone forever, never becoming wood again.

Reading note: Together, these four analogies — the traveler who never again confuses south and north, the space-flowers that never bloom again once the eye disease is cured, pure gold that never reverts to ore, wood-ash that never becomes wood again — answer Pūrṇa's worry completely: awakening is not a temporary suppression that could someday lapse, since the delusion it dispels was never, in truth, anything more than a passing appearance layered over what was already, from the start, true. This completes the teaching of the non-empty tathāgatagarbha, begun in fascicle 9. What follows next turns to Pūrṇa's still-unanswered second question.

The Zhèngmài commentary says: without first clarifying how the various teachings treat the relationship between nature and appearance, delusion and awakening, one cannot appreciate the depth of Pūrṇa's own doubt, nor the subtlety of the Buddha's resolution of it. The two kinds of unobstructed principle are matters that gods, humans, and the Lesser Vehicle alike are, without exception, entirely deluded about — and even the Dharma-characteristics and Emptiness-refuting schools never fully penetrate them either. The Dharma-characteristics school holds that true suchness does not follow conditions, and that appearance is not identical with nature; the Emptiness-refuting school, having refuted appearance, speaks only of the twofold emptiness of appearance and nature — a negation with nothing positively stated, never actually arriving at speaking of appearance as identical with nature. 上各明各現，依他似有，雖屬是妄，未顯宛轉虛妄。俱明俱現，遍計非實，足表宛轉虛妄，故再徵釋，以合相妄之喻。如一水中，現於日影：喻一如來藏性中，具足諸大之性。兩人同觀水中之日，東西各行：喻眾生各依藏性，各循各業不一；如兩人東西各行，則各見有一日，隨二人去，喻各循業感，所現大相不等。一東一西，先無準的者：一東行一西行，其日先無準定的實所現大相。無有定實，即相妄也。

Sūtra: "The true, wondrous, bright awareness is exactly the same: if you take hold of it through emptiness and brightness, then emptiness manifests; if earth, water, fire, and wind each, in turn, bring their own brightness to light, then each, in turn, manifests; and if all are brought to light together, then all manifest together. How could they all manifest together? Pūrṇa, it is like the sun's reflection appearing in a body of water: if two people watch the sun's reflection in the water and then walk off in different directions, east and west, each will find the reflected sun following him — one to the east, one to the west, with no fixed original position to appeal to. One should not then object: 'If this sun is one, how can it go two separate ways? And if the two suns are genuinely two, how did they ever appear as only one?' Such an objection only tangles itself in false premises, with nothing solid to stand on."

Commentary: In the received text, before this passage, there appear two sections — "observing that appearance is, at root, false" and "observing that nature is, at root, true" — which, examined closely, ought properly to follow right after the analogy is given, applying it directly to the doctrine, as is the sūtra's own regular practice elsewhere. As printed, however, these two sections fall between the analogy and its application, awkwardly cutting the flow of thought in two — very likely a copying error introduced sometime after the original translation. Master Jiaoguang, noticing this same problem, moved these two sections to follow directly after "tangles itself in false premises, with nothing solid to stand on," placing the doctrinal application right after the analogy — achieving the best of both. Here, accordingly, the passage applying the analogy to the doctrine is likewise moved forward — at the risk of an unpardonable presumption, for which the reader's understanding is humbly asked.

"The true, wondrous, bright awareness" means exactly this same true suchness, this wondrous, aware, bright mind — matched to the analogy of that great empty space. It, too, is unchanging while following conditions, perfectly interfused, without obstruction — just as space, following the sun and the other seven conditions, manifests brightness and the other seven appearances. The true mind is, originally, none of the five great elements, and yet is able, following conditions, to manifest the appearance of all five.

"If you take hold of it through emptiness and brightness, then emptiness manifests; if earth, water, fire, and wind each, in turn, bring their own brightness to light, then each, in turn, manifests": these five lines describe each, in turn, being brought to light and each, in turn, manifesting — meaning the five great elements manifesting in different places, or at different times. The two lines that follow describe all being brought to light together and all manifesting together — that is, in the same place, at the same time. As for the word "brightness," Master Jiaoguang's own gloss — that it carries the sense of "in accordance with karma" — is the best reading. "You," though addressed to Pūrṇa, is meant to include beings of all nine realms. "If you take hold of it through emptiness and brightness": as when a bodhisattva, in accordance with pure karma tied to emptiness, manifests a body of empty space; or an arhat realizes the one-sided emptiness of nirvāṇa; while an ordinary being, in accordance with defiled karma tied to emptiness, manifests the base of infinite space, and so on. Taking hold of it through earth: a bodhisattva manifests the adorned land of true reward; an ordinary being manifests a tainted, impure land. Taking hold of it through water, fire, or wind: an arhat manifests water pouring from the upper body and fire from the lower; a bodhisattva manifests a compassionate wind sweeping everywhere, to relieve sentient beings of their feverish affliction; an ordinary being manifests the three calamities of water, fire, and wind.

"And if all are brought to light together, then all manifest together": as when a heavenly being sees water as a jeweled ground of lapis lazuli, fit to walk upon; a human being sees water as water, fit to drink; and a hungry ghost sees water as fire, unable to drink it however great the thirst — each and every case nothing but a response summoned in accordance with karma, manifesting simultaneously. What manifests in this way is neither produced by causes and conditions, nor simply possessed by the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, and yet never departs from causes and conditions or from the tathāgatagarbha's own nature either.

The five lines above, describing each in turn being brought to light and manifesting, are dependently arisen, only seeming to exist — though false, they do not yet fully show falseness turning and twisting back on itself. The two lines describing all being brought to light together and manifesting together, universal conceptual construction rather than genuine reality, are what fully demonstrate this self-tangling falseness — hence the question is raised again, to match the analogy of appearance's own falseness. "It is like the sun's reflection appearing in a body of water" stands for a single tathāgatagarbha nature already complete with the nature of every great element. "Two people watch the sun's reflection in the water and then walk off in different directions, east and west" stands for sentient beings, each depending on this same tathāgatagarbha nature, yet each following their own differing karma; just as the two people, walking east and west, each see a sun following them, this stands for each being summoning, in accordance with their own karma, a differing manifestation of the great elements. "With no fixed original position to appeal to": walking east, walking west, the sun has, from the start, no single, fixed, real position that it actually manifests from at all — having no fixed reality is exactly what is meant by appearance's own falseness.

This explains exactly what is meant by falseness turning and twisting back on itself. One should not object — the sun's reflection in the water is one, so why does each of the two see a sun following him? Or — since each, walking separately, sees the reflection as two, why does the water show only a single reflection? This is precisely a case of manifesting simultaneously, with "one" and "two" both unsettled. "Turning and twisting" means exactly this: if one says it is one, each person walking separately sees two — so "one" is false; if one says it is two, the water itself shows only one — so "two" is false as well. Argue it one way or the other, and it comes down to nothing but universal conceptual falseness, with, in the end, no true principle to appeal to as solid ground. Master Guoshan says: watching together shows it to be one, yet knowing it to be two shows this "one" to be false; walking separately shows it to be two, yet this very fact proves that "two" is likewise false.

Sūtra: "Observing appearance, it is, at root, false, with nothing that can be pointed to and definitively stated. This is exactly like inviting flowers in space to bear fruit in space as well — how, then, could one still press the question of whether they destroy one another? Commentary: This concludes and restates the correct meaning: the great elements are, in appearance, false, and, in nature, true. It concludes first in terms of appearance: just as the earlier appearance of brightness was neither the sun's nor space's own, and yet not separate from either, so too, by this same pattern, the appearance of the great elements is, from the start, false — each in turn brought to light and manifesting, or all together brought to light and manifesting, in every case nothing but appearing in accordance with karma, each only seeming to exist, never genuinely real, with nothing that can be pointed to and definitively stated. It is exactly like flowers in empty space, which never existed there at all — a diseased eye sees them as though they existed, while a healthy eye sees they never truly did. To insist there is something here that can be pointed to and definitively stated would be like clinging to these space-flowers as genuinely real — one layer of confusion; and to press further, demanding to know whether they destroy one another, would be like inviting these same space-flowers to bear fruit as well — confusion doubled upon confusion.

Question: "This teaches that the great elements are false in appearance and pose no obstruction — but why, then, does one actually observe, in the world, water and fire destroying one another, and earth failing to hold either space or water?" Answer: "Examined even in terms of actual fact, there is, in truth, neither destruction nor failure to hold one another. Every kind of oil in the world, for instance, properly belongs to the great element of water, and yet each and every kind also possesses the great element of fire within it — set alight, it burns at once. Within the great element of earth, the great element of space is likewise present — dig down a foot into the earth, and a foot's worth of empty space appears; dig down a fathom, and a fathom's worth of empty space appears. And, among the various metals, nothing exceeds gold in earth's own solid, obstructive nature — and yet, smelted, it turns entirely to liquid. What destruction, what failure to hold one another, is there in any of this? Examined in terms of underlying principle, the destruction and mutual exclusion one seems to observe among the great elements is, in every case, nothing but sentient beings' own clinging mind, falsely perceiving — never, in fact, real at all. Just as when clouds race past, the moon seems to move; or when a boat sails on, the shore seems to shift — does the moon, or the shore, actually move at all? Once one understands that the five great elements are, at root, empty, this false clinging, this false perception, is broken through all at once."

This concludes in terms of nature: continuing from the earlier point that empty space's own substance is not any appearance, one now observes directly that the nature of the great elements is, at root, the one true reality, originally without any appearance at all — nothing but this single, wondrous, aware, perfectly bright, true mind, which is exactly the tathāgatagarbha mind. "Never, to begin with, water or fire at all": originally none of the great elements at all; and, the great elements themselves not even present, who is left to speak of destroying or failing to hold one another? This wondrous, aware, bright mind, originally none of the great elements, is nonetheless able to manifest all of them — exactly like a bright mirror, able to manifest every image, while itself, originally, being none of those images at all.

The key move throughout is the same one already used for empty space itself in fascicle 8: an unchanging substance can manifest wildly different, even seemingly contradictory appearances without any of them being genuinely produced, genuinely destroyed, or genuinely in conflict — since none of them was ever more than "appearing in accordance with karma" (循業發現) to begin with. 小中現大，大中現小者：小即小相，大即大相，如一尺之鏡，能現千里之境，鏡子不必放大，境界不要縮小，以鏡望境，小中能現大相；以境望鏡，大中仍現小相；鏡之與境，皆事相也，鏡含境而有餘，境在鏡而如故，彼此不相妨礙，成事事無礙法界，十玄門中，廣陝自在門。

於一毛端，現寶王剎：此下四句，示事事無礙之相，舉依、正二報之事相，交互相涉，以示無礙。一毛，乃正報之最小者；寶王剎，是佛寶法王之剎土，即三千大千一佛世界，乃依報之最大者；在一毛頭上，能現一佛剎土，此以正攝依，以依入正；在毛端望佛剎，而佛剎不小，則小中現大，屬事事無礙，亦即陝不礙廣也。坐微塵裏，轉大法輪者：微塵乃依報之最小者；轉法輪，即現全身而說法，身是正報最大者，以全身坐在微塵之中，開法會轉法輪。此以依攝正，以正入依，由身望塵，而塵包身相，而微塵不大，即大中現小，亦屬事事無礙，亦即廣不礙陝也。

此重結無礙之由，與眾生敵體相翻。眾生則背覺合塵，故發塵勞，有世間相，是以元真之性，轉成元妄之相，所以不礙而礙：而我則滅塵合覺，滅虛妄之塵勞，故發真如，妙淨本覺湛明之性。性字與相字對，眾生迷悶，全真性成妄相，如來修證，融妄相即真性，事事即理，相相皆性，故得理事與事事，二無礙法界，有何諸大陵滅不相容者乎？

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: I, myself, have united this wondrous, unborn, unceasing brightness with the tathāgatagarbha, and the tathāgatagarbha is nothing but wondrous, aware brightness, perfectly illuminating the whole Dharma-realm — and so, within it, one becomes countless, and countless become one; the small appears within the large, the large within the small; unmoving from the place of practice, it pervades the worlds of the ten directions; the body contains the ten directions' boundless empty space; upon the tip of a single hair, a jeweled king's realm appears; seated within a single mote of dust, the great Dharma-wheel turns. Dust extinguished, awareness united — and so true suchness, the wondrous, enlightened, bright nature, comes forth.

Commentary: This addresses Pūrṇa's earlier doubt — why does the Tathāgata alone attain non-obstruction? Here the Buddha shows the very origin of this non-obstruction: true suchness, following pure conditions, gives rise to this. "I" is the Buddha's own self-reference — using true awareness's own wondrous, bright, unborn, unceasing sense-faculty nature as the root cause of practice, turning one's back on the dust and toil of false phenomena, uniting with the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, turning the light inward, illuminating back on itself, freeing oneself from dust and returning to the faculty's own root, subduing falseness and returning to original truth, bringing forth this original, bright radiance — wisdom's own light perfectly illuminating, seeing that every appearance is empty, the one truth alone standing revealed. And the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, no longer hidden by false appearance, is thereby able to interfuse that false appearance entirely — every appearance entirely nature, every falseness exactly truth — nothing but this wondrous, pure, original enlightenment's own utterly still, bright mind, perfectly illuminating the one true Dharma-realm; one returns to this pure, unmoved-from-the-start mind, and attains its whole substance!

Having attained this whole substance, one becomes able, within the one true Dharma-realm, to give rise, in accordance with this substance, to two kinds of unobstructed function: first, the unobstructed interpenetration of principle and phenomena; second, the unobstructed interpenetration of phenomena with one another. "One becomes countless, and countless become one" illustrates the first; "the small appears within the large, the large within the small" illustrates the second. "One" is the one true Dharma-realm's own principle; "countless" is the differentiated phenomena of the ten dharma-realms. Or again: "one" is the one mind; "countless" is the ten thousand phenomena — the one mind capable of giving rise to the ten thousand phenomena is "one becomes countless"; the ten thousand phenomena being nothing but the one mind is "countless become one." "One becomes countless" means phenomena are formed in dependence on principle, principle posing no obstruction to phenomena — the one posing no obstruction to the many. "Countless become one" means principle is revealed through phenomena themselves, phenomena posing no obstruction to principle — the many posing no obstruction to the one — together forming the dharma-realm of principle and phenomena unobstructedly interpenetrating, the gate of "one and many mutually containing each other" among the ten profound gates.

"The small appears within the large, the large within the small": like a foot-wide mirror capable of showing a thousand-mile view — the mirror need not be enlarged, nor the view shrunk down; measured against the view, the mirror, small as it is, is able to show a large appearance within it; measured against the mirror, the view, large as it is, still shows a small appearance — mirror and view alike being nothing but phenomena; the mirror contains the view with room to spare, and the view remains in the mirror exactly as it is, neither obstructing the other at all — forming the dharma-realm of phenomena unobstructedly interpenetrating with one another, the gate of "vastness and narrowness each at ease" among the ten profound gates.

The first four lines show the appearance of principle and phenomena unobstructedly interpenetrating. "Unmoving from the place of practice" is exactly the tathāgatagarbha's own unmoving principle of true suchness. This principle is able to pervade the worlds of the ten directions, including empty space; phenomena themselves extend horizontally across every buddha-land, and vertically encompass all ten dharma-realms. "Unmoving from the place of practice" is this single principle's own whole substance, able to pervade the worlds of the ten directions, present within each and every phenomenon — exactly as a single quantity of gold can be shaped into countless vessels, with not a single vessel that is not entirely gold. Measuring "unmoving from the place of practice" against the worlds of the ten directions is "one becomes countless." "The body contains the ten directions' boundless empty space": the body here is the dharma-body, which takes principle as its very body. "Contains" means to encompass; the ten directions' boundless empty space, including the world itself, is exactly phenomena. This body is likewise this single principle's own whole substance, able to contain the ten directions' boundless empty space — wholly encompassing every phenomenon whatsoever, with nothing left outside it at all, exactly as spring encompasses every flower, a thousand shades of crimson and purple all, alike, spring itself. Measuring the ten directions' own empty space against this single body is "countless become one."

"Upon the tip of a single hair, a jeweled king's realm appears": the four lines that follow show the appearance of phenomena unobstructedly interpenetrating with one another. A single hair is the very smallest instance of direct retribution; the jeweled king's realm is the buddha-land of the treasure of the Dharma-king, that is, a single buddha-world of a great trichiliocosm, the very largest instance of circumstantial retribution — that this buddha-land can appear upon the tip of a single hair takes direct retribution to encompass circumstantial retribution; measured from the hair's own tip against the buddha-land, the buddha-land is not diminished at all — this is "the small showing the large," the narrow posing no obstruction to the vast. "Seated within a single mote of dust, the great Dharma-wheel turns": a mote of dust is the very smallest instance of circumstantial retribution; "turning the Dharma-wheel" means manifesting one's whole body and teaching the Dharma, the body being the very largest instance of direct retribution — that the whole body sits within a single mote of dust, opening a Dharma-assembly and turning the Dharma-wheel, takes circumstantial retribution to encompass direct retribution; measured from the body against the mote of dust, the dust encompasses the body's own form, and yet the mote of dust is not enlarged at all — this is "the large showing the small," the vast posing no obstruction to the narrow.

This concludes, once more, the very origin of this non-obstruction, standing as the direct opposite of what happens with sentient beings. Sentient beings turn their backs on awareness and unite with dust, and so the dust and toil of affliction is drawn forth, and there comes to be the appearance of the world — the originally true nature thereby turned into an originally false appearance, so that what poses no obstruction comes to obstruct after all. But I extinguish dust and unite with awareness, extinguishing this false dust and toil, and so true suchness, this wondrous, pure, original enlightenment's own utterly still, bright nature, comes forth. Sentient beings, confused and troubled, turn the whole of this true nature into false appearance; the Tathāgata, through cultivation and realization, interfuses this false appearance with true nature entirely — phenomenon exactly principle, every appearance entirely nature — and so attains both kinds of unobstructed dharma-realm. What room is there, then, for any of the great elements to destroy or fail to hold one another at all?

Fully Manifesting the Three Aspects of the Tathāgatagarbha: The Empty Aspect 辰二 依本來心圓彰藏性 · 巳初 圓彰空藏一切皆非

Chinese Source

〖經〗而如來藏，本妙圓心。非心、非空、非地、非水、非風、非火、非眼、非耳、鼻、舌、身、意，非色、非聲、香、味、觸、法，非眼識界，如是乃至，非意識界。非明、無明，明無明盡；如是乃至，非老、非死，非老死盡。非苦、非集、非滅、非道、非智、非得。非檀那，非尸羅，非毘梨耶、非羼提、非禪那、非般刺若、非波羅密多。如是乃至，非怛闥阿竭，非阿羅訶，非三耶三菩，非大涅槃，非常、非樂、非我、非淨。

〖講〗此科與上科，所依如來藏心之體固同，而約義有異。上約隨緣義，此約不變義。上依迷悟心，聖凡立判；此依本來心，生佛一如。惟是一真法界，具足十界，即非十界，離即離非，是即非即，一心圓彰三藏，三藏不出一心，圓融極妙，無以復加矣！即佛許說，勝義中真勝義性，亦即一乘寂滅場地，為如來之密因，實眾生之佛性。此心本無迷悟，而為迷悟所依，約本無迷悟，安有聖凡，故十界俱非，而為空如來藏；約迷悟所依，攸分差別，故十界俱即，而為不空如來藏；約雙遮雙照，圓融極妙，而為空不空如來藏。

今先圓彰空藏，而字承接上文，轉語之詞。如云：藏性雖隨染淨二緣，卻不為迷悟所變，而如來藏，依然本妙，妙即不變義。連圓心二字合解，則曰本來元妙，圓滿清淨之心。如摩尼寶珠，本來元妙，圓滿清淨也。此心不立一法，即六祖所云：「本來無一物。」故下十界俱非。

前三句非七大。非心：即非見大識大，見、識二大，皆屬心法故；非空及下二句非五大，五大屬色法；其餘諸句，非十八界，亦即非陰、入、處、界四科。非五根、六塵，即非色陰；非意根第七識為意所依之根，非六識，即非受、想、行、識四陰；又非眼等諸根，即非六入；併色等諸塵，即非十二處；併及六識，即非十八界，是謂非四科。據此則如來藏，非世間法矣。

此文與《心經》，是故空中無色，無受、想、行識，無眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、意，無色、聲、香、味、觸、法，無眼界，乃至無意識界，名相全同，而非字與無字，意義稍異。彼以觀照般若之功，照見真空實相之中，無有世間諸法；此以本來心，不假功用，空如來藏中與空中同，本非七大四科，有為諸法。以上非世間法，即如來藏，非六凡染法矣。

此下非出世法，即如來藏，非四聖淨法矣。四聖淨法，亦佛常說，隨淨緣起所成者。今皆約本來心，未起事用時說，此先非緣覺法，十二因緣十二支，有流轉、還滅二門，先釋名義，後解本文。此佛為緣覺人，所說小乘法，分三世因果，即惑、業、苦三道，遷流不息，輪轉無窮，故曰流轉門；還滅門者，即斷十二支，復還真諦，滅諸生死，故曰還滅門。

十二支：一、無明支，不明我空之理，屬惑；二、行支，即依惑所造之業行，此為過去世二支因；三、識支，即今世投胎時八識；四、名色支，即投胎後心色和合；五、六入支，即出胎後六根為六塵所入處；六、觸支，即少時六根觸對六塵，未成欣戚時；七、受支，即稍長領受外境，能起欣戚時，自識至此為現在世五支果；八、愛支，即受境之後心起愛憎，為現在世惑；九、取支，由愛憎而起取捨；十、有支，即取捨既定而業因已成，愛、取、有為現在世三支因，再感未來世二支果；十一、生支，由現在世惑業因，感來世受生果；十二、老死，指來世從生而至老死也。此十二支，展轉相因，連環鉤鎖，三世因果，流轉不息，曰無明緣行，行緣識，乃至有緣生，生緣老死是也。還滅門是修法：無明滅則行滅；行滅則識滅；乃至生滅則老死滅。闢支利根，一聞佛說，即知無明為生死之根，即從斷無明下手，如砍樹者，直砍其根，根斷而樹自倒，無明斷，而生死自了矣。

此文亦同心經，無字非字如上解。無明上多一明字，即性覺必明之明字，因必欲加明於覺，故成無明，此本來心，一念未動，故非明非無明，如是乃至非老非死。第一非字雙用，又非明非無明盡即滅也，如是乃至，非老死盡，此將流轉，還滅二門分開，各舉因緣之頭，直超因緣之尾，以便易知。此中所云老死盡，但盡分段，未盡變易也。據此如來藏，非緣覺法矣。

次非聲聞法。苦、集、滅、道，四諦法門，是佛為小乘機所說。世間出世間，二種因果，皆是諦實，故稱為諦。苦諦：是世間生死苦果，以逼迫為性。約人間，略說八苦：生苦、老苦、病苦、死苦、愛別離苦，怨憎會苦，求不得苦、五陰熾盛苦。前七為別，後一為總。約三界則分三苦：五趣眾生為苦苦；乃苦中之苦。天趣眾生，三禪以下為壞苦；福樂有盡，久必壞生。四禪以上為行苦；雖苦樂雙亡，難免行陰遷流之苦。佛為說此是苦，汝當知。

集諦：是世間煩惱苦因，我執分別俱生，麤細煩惱，集聚眾生心中，以招感為性，依煩惱惑，造善惡業，招感生死苦果，若無集諦煩惱苦因，當然不受生死苦果，佛為說此是集，汝當斷。滅諦：是出世間涅槃，譯不生不滅，樂果，即二乘所證，方便有餘土，偏真涅槃，揀異究竟無餘涅槃。以可證為性，若能脩道、斷集，自可滅盡諸苦，滅非真諦，因滅會真，故稱滅諦，佛言此是滅，汝當證。道諦：是出世間道品樂因，共有三十七品，四念處、四正勤、四如意足、五根、五力，即五根增長成力、七覺支、八正道，以可修為性。四諦中，此諦最關緊要，若能脩道，自然斷煩惱苦因，滅生、死苦果，證涅槃樂果。佛云：此是道，汝當修。諸弟子聞佛四諦法聲，脩道證果，故稱聲聞。

非智非得者：此文接於四諦之下，不必別作他說。依孤山作小乘所證智理，謂非有我空之智，與非得我空之理，當屬聲聞乘，此亦隨淨緣所成。本來心中，不但有為法當非，即無為法亦非，以藏心不屬有為無為故；據此則如來藏，非聲聞法矣。

此非菩薩法。他經所說檀波羅密等，乃理行因果並舉，此文稍異。依孤山曰：「非檀那等，先非能趨行；非波羅多者，總非所趨理也。」此文全用梵語，前六即六度，後一即到彼岸。檀那此云布施，布施有三：一曰資生施，即以財物布施，資養生命；二曰法施，即以佛法布施，令續慧命；三曰無畏施，即以無畏力，布施於眾生，令離怖畏。尸羅此云持戒，持戒亦三：一曰攝律儀戒，無惡不斷也；二曰攝善法戒，無善不修也。三曰饒益有情戒，無眾生不度也。此大乘戒，不獨制身口，而能攝心也。毘梨耶此云精進，精勤不懈曰精，進趨不退曰進。乃普對諸度萬行，悉皆勇往直前也。

羼提此云忍辱。辱者侮辱，或罵詈，或排斥，或毆打，或殘害。一切逆境，皆謂之辱；忍者忍受，能含忍順受。忍之一事，頗不容易，略說其相有六：一曰力忍，凡辱境之來，忍而不較，退一步，讓三分，由他、任他；二曰反忍，凡遇人加辱，不責人而反責己，總由過去辱他，故今辱我，作還報想，並不尤人；三曰忘忍，雅量寬洪，雖然受辱，毫不介意，處辱如無，此三尚未得理，謂之事忍。理忍亦三：一曰觀忍，凡辱境當前，以智觀察，我身本不有，人相復何存，人我雙亡，辱境安在；二曰喜忍，逢人加辱，心生歡喜，以其能成就我之忍力，如力士逢人試力而喜也；三曰慈忍，對於加辱之人，憐彼愚癡，無有智慧，不知禮義，不明因果，竟起慈心，發願度脫也。如釋迦本師，為歌利王，割截身體，不生瞋恨，發願成佛先度是也。

禪那此云靜慮，此慮非思慮，即正思惟。初脩靜即是止，慮即是觀；修成靜即是定，慮即是慧。或稱禪定，有世間禪，出世間禪，出世上上禪。世間禪：凡夫四禪四空定，外道無心定；出世間禪：小乘禪，大乘禪；出世上上禪：即十方諸佛，得成菩提之定，名大佛頂首楞嚴王是也。

般刺若此云智慧，有文字般若，即一切經典，能詮義理，可以開人智慧；有觀照般若，即起智觀照，三空妙理；有實相般若，即本經，十番所顯之妙淨明心，四科七大所會之如來藏性，平等一如，真實之相。菩薩觀照功深，所得契合真理之智，是為般若妙智。上六是趣果之行，屬因。波羅密多此云到彼岸，是所趣之理屬果。此岸是生死，彼岸是涅槃。涅槃，即佛所證不生滅之果，實教菩薩，得以分證，若權教菩薩，所修諸行，但稱六度，不稱六波羅密，以所修不能離相，未得三輪體空，不到涅槃彼岸。此六波羅密，以般若為先導，必由般若，方能離相，方到彼岸也。此亦屬淨緣起，而本來心，不假脩證，故皆非之。據此，如來藏非菩薩法矣。

如是乃至，非怛闥阿竭，非阿羅訶，非三耶三菩，非大涅槃，非常、非樂、非我、非淨。此非如來法。如是指上所修，六波羅密行，乃能從因至果，非有超略。非怛闥阿竭三句，非能證佛也；非大涅槃三句，非所證法也。怛闥阿竭：此云如來，有法、報、應三身。阿羅訶：此云應供，能應九法界眾生之供。三耶三菩：此云正遍知，正知，知心包萬法，遍知，知萬法唯心。此三即諸佛十號前三號。大涅槃：即佛所證大寂滅海，此云不生滅，二死永亡故；又云圓寂，真無不圓，妄無不寂也。此為佛果之總，下二句，即涅槃所具四德為別。

《正脈》云：常者，非惟二死永亡，無諸生滅，亦且世相常住，究竟堅固也；樂者，非惟遠離諸生死苦，亦且得不思議解脫，受用無量法樂也；我者，非惟證真法身，猶若虛空，亦且山河大地，全露遮那也；淨者，非惟妙淨理體，無諸染著，亦且清淨遍周，無染非淨也。此文似但非涅槃斷果，實亦非菩提智果，正遍知，即三菩提，權、實二智也。

佛，為極果聖人，菩提涅槃是究竟果法，何亦俱非耶？《金剛經》所云：「言佛法者，即非佛法。」又《圓覺經》云：「妙圓覺心，本無菩提，及與涅槃，亦無成佛，及不成佛。」此本來心，與妙圓覺心，無二無別，故佛法亦俱非也。據此則如來藏，又非佛法矣。統上則十界俱非，非六凡法界者，以明空如來藏，非染法之所能染；又非四聖法界者，以明空如來藏，亦非淨法之所能淨；是謂彌滿清淨，中不容他，如實空義也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: And the tathāgatagarbha, this original, wondrous, all-encompassing mind, is neither mind, nor emptiness, nor earth, water, wind, nor fire; neither the eye, the ear, nose, tongue, body, nor mind; neither form, nor sound, fragrance, flavor, touch, nor mental phenomena; neither the element of eye-consciousness, and so on, through neither the element of mind-consciousness. Neither ignorance, nor the ending of ignorance, and so on, through neither old age, nor death, nor the ending of old age and death. Neither suffering, nor its origin, nor its cessation, nor the path, neither wisdom, nor attainment. Neither giving, nor moral discipline, nor diligence, nor patience, nor meditation, nor wisdom, nor the very crossing to the far shore. And so on, through neither the Tathāgata, nor the Worthy One, nor the Perfectly, Universally Awakened One, neither great nirvāṇa, neither permanence, nor bliss, nor self, nor purity.

Commentary: This section and the one before it both depend on the same tathāgatagarbha mind's own substance, yet differ in their emphasis: the section above emphasized the sense of following conditions; this section emphasizes the sense of remaining unchanged. This is nothing but the one true Dharma-realm, complete with all ten dharma-realms, and yet none of the ten dharma-realms at all — departing from both "is" and "is not," being, at once, both "is" and "is not" — a single mind fully manifesting the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha, these three aspects never departing from this single mind, perfectly interfused to the utmost degree of subtlety. This is exactly the true, ultimate nature within the ultimate truth the Buddha promised to teach, exactly the one vehicle's own ground of quiescent extinction, the Tathāgata's own hidden cause, and, in truth, sentient beings' own buddha-nature. This mind is, in itself, neither deluded nor awakened, and yet is what both delusion and awakening depend on: in terms of being, in itself, neither deluded nor awakened, how could sage or ordinary being even exist? — hence all ten dharma-realms are, alike, negated, and it is the "empty" tathāgatagarbha. In terms of being what delusion and awakening depend on, distinctions inevitably follow — hence all ten dharma-realms are, alike, affirmed, and it is the "non-empty" tathāgatagarbha. In terms of doubly negating and doubly illuminating at once, it is the "empty-and-non-empty" tathāgatagarbha.

Now, first fully manifesting the "empty" tathāgatagarbha: though the tathāgatagarbha's own nature follows both defiled and pure conditions, it is never altered by either delusion or awakening — the tathāgatagarbha remains, exactly as before, originally wondrous, "wondrous" meaning exactly unchanging. Read together with "all-encompassing mind," this means: the original, inherently wondrous, perfectly complete, pure mind — exactly like a wish-fulfilling pearl. This mind establishes not a single phenomenon at all — exactly what the Sixth Patriarch meant by "originally, there is not a single thing" — hence what follows negates all ten dharma-realms, one by one.

The first three phrases negate the seven great elements: "neither mind" negates the great elements of seeing and consciousness; "neither emptiness" and the two phrases that follow negate the remaining five great elements, belonging to material phenomena. The remaining phrases negate the eighteen elements — that is, negate the four categories of aggregates, entrances, fields, and elements. Negating the five sense-faculties and six sense-objects negates the aggregate of form; negating the mental faculty, the seventh consciousness, and the six consciousnesses negates the aggregates of sensation, perception, formations, and consciousness; and, further, negating the eye and the other faculties negates the six sense-entrances; together with negating form and the other objects, negates the twelve sense-fields; and together with negating the six consciousnesses, negates the eighteen elements. On this basis, the tathāgatagarbha is not any worldly phenomenon at all.

This passage matches, term for term, the Heart Sūtra's own list — yet the word "neither" here and the word "no" there differ slightly in meaning. That sūtra speaks by way of the power of contemplative prajñā, illuminating and seeing that, within the true emptiness of ultimate reality, there is no worldly phenomenon to be found; this sūtra speaks by way of the original mind itself, needing no effort at all — originally none of the seven great elements or four categories, none of these conditioned phenomena at all. All of this negates worldly phenomena — that is, the tathāgatagarbha is not any of the six ordinary, defiled destinies' phenomena at all.

What follows next negates transcendent phenomena — that is, the tathāgatagarbha is not any of the four sagely, pure paths' phenomena either. This first negates the pratyekabuddha's own teaching — the twelve links of dependent origination, having both a forward-flowing gate and a reverse-cessation gate. The Buddha, teaching this Lesser Vehicle doctrine for pratyekabuddhas, divides cause and effect across three lifetimes — the three paths of delusion, karma, and suffering — flowing onward without cease, turning endlessly through the wheel: hence the "forward-flowing gate." The "reverse-cessation gate" means severing these twelve links, returning to the ultimate truth, extinguishing birth and death.

The twelve links: (1) ignorance, not understanding the principle of the emptiness of self, belonging to delusion; (2) formations, the karmic action created in dependence on this delusion — these two, the causes belonging to a past life; (3) consciousness, the eighth consciousness at the moment of entering the womb in this present life; (4) name-and-form, mind and matter combined after entering the womb; (5) the six entrances, the six sense-faculties as the place the six sense-objects enter, after birth; (6) contact, the six sense-faculties meeting the six sense-objects in early childhood, before any pleasure or displeasure yet arises; (7) sensation, receiving external objects as one grows older, capable now of pleasure or displeasure — from consciousness through here, the five effects belonging to this present life; (8) craving, love and hatred arising in the mind after receiving an object; (9) grasping, taking up or rejecting, arising from this love and hatred; (10) becoming, the karmic cause now fixed, once taking-up and rejecting are settled — craving, grasping, and becoming being the three causes of this present life, in turn summoning two further effects in a future life; (11) birth, summoned by the delusion and karma of this present life into a future rebirth; (12) old age and death, referring to the future life's own passage from birth to old age and death. These twelve links, each causing the next in turn, flow onward without cease — "ignorance conditions formations, formations condition consciousness," and so on, through "becoming conditions birth, birth conditions old age and death." The reverse-cessation gate is the method of practice: ignorance ceasing, formations cease; formations ceasing, consciousness ceases — and so on, through birth ceasing, old age and death cease. A pratyekabuddha of sharp faculties, hearing the Buddha teach this once, understands at once that ignorance is the very root of birth and death, and takes up severing ignorance as the starting point — like someone felling a tree who cuts straight at its root: the root severed, the tree falls of itself.

This passage, too, matches the Heart Sūtra. Note the extra word "brightness" before "ignorance" — this is exactly the same "brightness" as in "the nature of awareness is necessarily bright": because of this very insistence on adding brightness to awareness, ignorance comes to be. This original mind, not a single thought yet stirred, is thus neither brightness nor ignorance, and so on, through neither old age nor death. This takes the forward-flowing and reverse-cessation gates and separates them out, citing only the first link of each chain and leaping directly to its last, for the sake of easy comprehension. Note that "the ending of old age and death" mentioned here means only the ending of segmented birth-and-death, not yet the ending of birth-and-death by transformation. On this basis, the tathāgatagarbha is not the pratyekabuddha's own teaching either.

Next, the sūtra negates the śrāvaka's own teaching: the four noble truths — suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path — a Dharma-gate the Buddha taught for beings of the Lesser Vehicle's own capacity. The truth of suffering: the suffering fruit of birth and death in the world. In human terms, briefly, eight sufferings: the suffering of birth, of old age, of sickness, of death, of separation from what one loves, of meeting with what one hates, of not getting what one seeks, and of the five aggregates blazing fiercely. In terms of the three realms, divided into three sufferings: for beings of the five destinies, the suffering of suffering; for heavenly beings up through the third dhyāna, the suffering of decay; for those in the fourth dhyāna and above, the suffering of formations. The Buddha teaches: this is suffering — you should know it.

The truth of origin: the cause of suffering, affliction, in the world — grasping at a self, both discriminated and innate, gathered and accumulated within sentient beings' own minds, its nature being to summon retribution. The Buddha teaches: this is origin — you should sever it. The truth of cessation: the transcendent fruit of bliss, nirvāṇa, translated "unborn, unceasing" — exactly what the two lesser vehicles realize, distinct from the ultimate nirvāṇa without any residue at all. The Buddha says: this is cessation — you should realize it. The truth of the path: the transcendent cause of bliss, the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment in all — the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of supernatural power, the five roots, the five powers, the seven factors of enlightenment, and the eightfold noble path. Among the four noble truths, this one is the most essential. The Buddha says: this is the path — you should cultivate it. The disciples, hearing the Buddha's own voice teaching the four noble truths, cultivate the path and realize its fruit — hence called "śrāvakas," "hearers."

"Neither wisdom, nor attainment": this refers to the wisdom and principle realized by the Lesser Vehicle — neither the wisdom of the emptiness of self, nor the attainment of the principle of the emptiness of self. Within the original mind, not only are conditioned phenomena to be negated, but unconditioned phenomena as well, since the tathāgatagarbha mind belongs to neither the conditioned nor the unconditioned. On this basis, the tathāgatagarbha is not the śrāvaka's own teaching either.

This negates the bodhisattva's own teaching. This passage uses the Sanskrit terms throughout — the first six are the six perfections, the last is "crossing to the far shore." Dāna translates as "giving," of three kinds: giving of material goods; giving of the Dharma; and giving of fearlessness. Śīla translates as "upholding precepts," likewise of three kinds: gathering in the moral codes; gathering in wholesome practices; and benefiting sentient beings — this Great Vehicle precept-keeping governing not only body and speech, but the mind as well. Vīrya translates as "diligence" — vigorous effort without slackening, advancing without retreat, pressing bravely forward across every perfection and every practice alike.

Kṣānti translates as "patience." "Patience" means to bear, to endure, able to accept and receive submissively — no easy matter. Briefly, its forms number six: patience through sheer strength, enduring an insult without contesting it; patience through reversal, blaming not the other but oneself, thinking of it as repayment for a past life; patience through forgetting, suffering an insult without the slightest concern — these first three not yet grasping the underlying principle, called "patience in practice." Patience in principle likewise has three forms: patience through contemplation, observing with wisdom that one's own self was never truly there to begin with, and so what trace of the insult itself could remain; patience through joy, meeting an insult with a joyful mind, since it perfects one's own capacity for patience; and patience through compassion, pitying the very person giving the insult for their foolishness and blindness to cause and effect, and vowing to save them — as when our own founding teacher Śākyamuni, having his body cut to pieces by King Kalī, felt no anger at all, and vowed that, upon becoming a buddha, he would save this very king first.

Dhyāna translates as "quiet contemplation" — this "contemplation" is not ordinary discursive thought, but right reflection. There is mundane dhyāna, transcendent dhyāna, and the supreme, transcendent-of-the-transcendent dhyāna — exactly the samādhi by which the Buddhas of the ten directions attain bodhi, named the Great Buddha's Crown, King Śūraṅgama.

Prajñā translates as "wisdom" — there is the prajñā of the written word; the prajñā of contemplative illumination; and the prajñā of ultimate reality, exactly the wondrous, pure, bright mind revealed by the ten displays in this sūtra, and the tathāgatagarbha nature harmonized through the four categories and seven great elements. These first six are the practice pursued toward the fruit, belonging to cause. Pāramitā translates as "crossing to the far shore," the principle pursued, belonging to fruit. This shore is birth and death; the far shore is nirvāṇa. Bodhisattvas of the provisional teaching, cultivating these same practices, call them merely "the six perfections" rather than "the six pāramitās," since what they cultivate cannot depart from appearance, never attaining the emptiness of the three wheels — giver, gift, and recipient. These six pāramitās take prajñā as their guide — only through prajñā can one depart from appearance and reach the far shore at all. This, too, belongs to what is brought about through following pure conditions — while the original mind needs no cultivation or realization at all, hence all of it is here negated. On this basis, the tathāgatagarbha is not the bodhisattva's own teaching either.

"And so on, through neither the Tathāgata, nor the Worthy One, nor the Perfectly, Universally Awakened One, neither great nirvāṇa, neither permanence, nor bliss, nor self, nor purity": this negates the Tathāgata's own teaching. "And so on" points back to the six pāramitās just cultivated, capable of leading from cause to fruit — there is no arbitrary leap here, but a direct continuity. The three phrases "neither the Tathāgata, nor the Worthy One, nor the Perfectly, Universally Awakened One" negate the very buddha realized; the four phrases beginning "neither great nirvāṇa" negate the very Dharma realized. Tathāgata translates as "thus come," possessing the three bodies. Arhat translates as "worthy of offerings." Samyaksaṃbuddha translates as "correct, universal knowing." These three are the first three of the buddhas' own ten epithets. Great nirvāṇa is exactly the great ocean of quiescent extinction the Buddha realizes, translated "unborn, unceasing," since both kinds of death are forever ended; also translated "perfect quiescence." This is the general term for the fruit of buddhahood; the two lines that follow name the four virtues nirvāṇa possesses, in particular.

The Zhèngmài commentary says: "permanence" means not only that both kinds of death are forever ended, but also that worldly appearance itself is ever-abiding, ultimately firm and unshaken; "bliss" means not only being far removed from every suffering of birth and death, but also attaining an inconceivable liberation, enjoying boundless dharma-bliss; "self" means not only realizing the true dharma-body, vast as empty space, but also that mountains, rivers, and the great earth are, in their entirety, the manifestation of Vairocana himself; "purity" means not only this wondrous, pure substance of principle, free of every defilement, but also that this purity pervades everywhere. Though this passage appears to negate only nirvāṇa, the fruit of severing, it in fact negates bodhi, the fruit of wisdom, as well.

The Buddha, the sage of the ultimate fruit, bodhi and nirvāṇa the ultimate fruit-dharma — why, then, are these too negated? As the Diamond Sūtra says: "what is called the Buddha-dharma is exactly not the Buddha-dharma." And the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment says: "the wondrous, perfect mind of enlightenment has, at root, neither bodhi nor nirvāṇa, neither becoming a buddha nor not becoming one." This original mind is no different at all from this same wondrous, perfect mind of enlightenment — hence even the Buddha-dharma itself is here negated. On this basis, the tathāgatagarbha is not the Buddha's own teaching either. Taken together, all ten dharma-realms are, alike, negated: negating the six ordinary destinies' own dharma-realms shows that the "empty" tathāgatagarbha cannot be defiled by any defiled phenomenon; negating the four sagely paths' own dharma-realms shows that it cannot be purified by any pure phenomenon either — this is what is meant by "filling everything, pure, admitting nothing else at all" — the meaning of true emptiness.

此經從阿難捨妄求真，求佛發妙明心，即顯發此三藏一心也。佛始從眼根指出，十番極顯其真，二見略剖其妄，復自根中，推而廣之，普會四科，遍融七大。阿難大眾，各各自知，心遍十方，常住不滅；此悟次第空藏，已成頓意，而圓意猶未彰也。復由滿慈，問三種生續之因，如來與答，性覺必明，以為其咎，以致世界、眾生、業果，生續不斷，顯次第不空藏，斯則體用已備，圓意已露，猶未具彰也。復答滿慈，五大圓融之難，以示性相二無礙理，且釋有礙之疑，至於則性之相，無量不思議妙用，即相之性，混融不思議妙體，尚未極顯也。迨依迷悟心，對辨二種緣起，依本來心，圓彰三種藏性，顯理顯到此處，可謂徹法流之底，窮性海之源，顯之極矣。

The Buddha's own mind, the ocean-seal samādhi's light brought forth, this great function now manifest, illuminates and breaks through every falseness, returns to the very source of mind, realizes unsurpassed bodhi to its utmost, and illuminates and sees that beings across all nine realms equally possess this same Buddha-knowledge-and-seeing. "Yet you, the moment you raise a single thought, find the dust and toil of affliction arising first instead": this refers to Pūrṇa, and to ordinary beings and the two vehicles — though equally possessing this tathāgatagarbha mind, containing this wondrous function within it, lacking this wondrous wisdom, this wondrous function is never brought forth; the moment a single thought is raised, every phenomenon discriminated is taken as existing outside the mind, taken as genuinely real — and so the dust and toil of affliction is drawn forth, and there comes to be the appearance of the world.

This traces the cause of why this wondrous function is never brought forth, and the dust and toil of affliction is drawn forth instead: because, at the very start of resolving one's mind, one never gives rise to the mind diligently seeking unsurpassed bodhi. Sanskrit bodhi translates as "the path of awakening," the very buddha-path realized by our great, awakened World-Honored One. But cherishing instead the Lesser Vehicle, easy to cultivate and easy to realize — even should you succeed in exhausting every outflow, beyond further training, complete with the six supernatural powers, attaining a certain omniscience, this still belongs only to the phantom city's own false treasure, a firefly's dim light of lesser wisdom — and you, satisfied with this smallest attainment, thereby lack this wondrous wisdom, unable to bring forth this wondrous function — how does this differ at all from the qín and sè, unable to bring forth their wondrous sound without a skillful hand to play them?

Reading note: Pūrṇa's second question — how the five great elements interfuse without destroying one another — is now fully answered, completing the teaching of the tathāgatagarbha's three aspects begun in fascicle 8. What follows next is Pūrṇa's own remaining doubt: if delusion has no real cause at all, where does it actually come from in the first place — and the Buddha's answer, the sūtra's single most famous parable.

此明無明妄想之體性，本來如是，尚不可得，而欲更索其因，豈可得哉？故曰：「因何為在？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "This being so, the cause of confusion exists only because of confusion itself — and, once one recognizes that confusion has no cause at all, this falseness has nothing left to depend on, and there is not even any arising of it to be found, let alone anything to be extinguished. Those who have attained bodhi are like a person now awake, telling of what happened in a dream: however lucid and clear his mind may be, by what possible means could he ever take hold of an object from within that dream and hand it to someone else? How much more so, then, given that it has no cause at all, originally possessing nothing whatsoever — exactly like that man Yajñadatta in the city: was there ever any real cause or condition behind his terror over his own head, running off in a panic? The moment his madness subsided, his head was found to be exactly where it always was — not obtained from anywhere outside himself at all. And, even while his madness had not yet subsided, when was his head ever actually lost in the first place? Commentary: This explains that the substance of falseness, never truly born, cannot be taken hold of at all. "Exists only because of confusion itself" should not be read as meaning that confusion gives rise to further confusion — confusion does not give rise to confusion, so how could one say it "exists because of confusion itself"? This cause of falseness, precisely because of confusion and delusion, failing to realize it has no cause at all, constantly comes to seem as though it exists — not genuinely real, but merely seeming to exist. As an earlier passage put it: "it was never truly there to begin with — it only seemed as though awareness had become confused." "And, once one recognizes that confusion has no cause at all, this falseness has nothing left to depend on": if one recognizes that confusion, at root, has no cause of arising at all, then the cause of falseness is, at root, nonexistent, and the substance of falseness is likewise empty — hence it has nothing to depend on, and there is not even any cause for falseness's own arising to be found — what, then, would there be left to extinguish? This says that, while sentient beings remain in confusion, this confusion was never, at root, actually born at all; and, when the buddhas cultivate and realize the path, this same confusion is not actually extinguished either, since the substance of falseness is, at root, empty.

"Those who have attained bodhi" refers to the buddhas who have already attained the fruit of unsurpassed bodhi, the long night's dream broken through — like a person now awake from a dream, telling of what happened within it: however lucid and clear his mind may be, by what possible means could he take hold of an actual object from within that dream and hand it to someone else, since the dream's own scene was, from the start, empty, never possessing anything real at all? The Buddha is exactly the same: the five abidings of affliction, his own dream, broken through, like a person now awake, complete with the threefold wisdom. Just as his mind is lucid and clear, teaching sentient beings about ignorance and false thought is exactly like a person now awake describing a dream — however much he may describe it, by what possible means could he take hold of the substance of this falseness and hand it to someone else?

"How much more so" takes up the dream analogy just given: if even an object from within a dream cannot be taken hold of, how much more so this false thought, which, from the start, has no cause at all, this substance of falseness, which, from the start, possesses nothing whatsoever. "That man Yajñadatta in the city: was there ever any real cause or condition behind his terror over his own head, running off in a panic?" — this shows terror arising of itself, with no cause behind it at all; his losing his head and running off in a mad panic matches this same point that the cause of falseness is, at root, empty. "The moment his madness subsided, his head was found to be exactly where it always was — not obtained from anywhere outside himself at all": even while his madness had not yet subsided, at the very moment he was running about in a panic searching for his head, when was his head ever actually lost at all? — this matches the point that the substance of falseness is likewise empty. The head here stands for truth; the madness for falseness. Master Jiaoguang notes: had his head truly ever been lost and regained, this could not properly be called "madness" at all — matched to the doctrine, had wondrous awareness truly ever been lost and regained, this could not properly be called "falseness" at all. Here, its subsiding was never anything obtained from outside himself, and, before it subsided, nothing was actually ever lost either — proof enough that the substance of falseness never, in truth, existed at all.

This explains that the very substance and nature of ignorance and false thought, being exactly as described, cannot even be found to begin with — so how, then, could one possibly go on seeking its cause? Hence: "on what basis could it be said to exist at all?"

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "You need only stop following after discrimination, and the three continuities — world, karma-fruit, and sentient beings — with their three conditions severed, will no longer give rise to their three causes. And then, within your own mind, the very madness of Yajñadatta will subside of itself — and this subsiding is itself bodhi. This supremely pure, bright mind, originally pervading the whole Dharma-realm, is never obtained from anyone else — why, then, should you need such toil and labor, such painstaking effort, to cultivate and realize it?"

Commentary: Pūrṇa, having reached the fourth fruit, has already broken through his clinging to a self, yet his clinging to phenomena still remains — hence his two earlier doubts. The Buddha has answered each in turn, tracing everything back to that very first single thought of ignorance as the fault — and so Pūrṇa now seeks out the cause of this falseness, meaning to strive in cultivation to sever it. The Buddha answers, once more, that the cause of falseness is, at root, empty, and its substance is likewise empty — why, then, painstakingly seek to cultivate its severing at all? And so he tells him: you need only stop following after discrimination, and that alone will suffice!

"Stop following after discrimination" is exactly the very starting point for cultivating the great Śūraṅgama samādhi. This "discrimination" is exactly the false mind capable of discriminating, belonging to the nature of universal conceptual clinging; "the world" and the rest that follow are exactly the false circumstance discriminated, belonging to the dependently-arisen nature, established in dependence on ignorance's own fundamental false phenomena. What is dependently arisen is, like an illusion, empty in its very substance — not only is the dependent phenomenon itself empty, but the ignorance it depends on is, at root, empty as well. Hence simply not following after this false circumstance, and not giving rise to this discriminating, false mind, is exactly emptying out every instance of universal conceptual clinging. Once these three kinds of conditioning mind are severed, present action no longer perfumes the seed, and the very causes capable of giving rise to the three continuities likewise no longer arise — the seed itself no longer sprouts! Exactly like a tree fallen, its root severed, never growing again. Or again, like grain, wheat, and beans: their seeds serve as cause, but water and soil must serve as condition before they can sprout at all — now, with these three conditions severed, it is as though there were no water or soil: though the seed remains, it has no way to sprout.

Earlier, Yajñadatta's own terror over his head, running off in a panic, stood for beginningless ignorance. Ignorance is the cause of every false phenomenon — and now, these three conditions severed, their three causes no longer arising, cause and condition alike cut off entirely, this fundamental ignorance within your own mind will subside of its own accord, its very madness settling to rest. The Zhèngmài commentary says: the word "subside" carries a double sense, both suppression and severance — in the sense of suppression, it means the completed mind of the ten faiths, perfectly suppressing ignorance; in the sense of severance, it means the final mind of equal enlightenment, forever severing ignorance.

The four lines "and this subsiding is itself bodhi" and what follows show falseness extinguished, truth revealed — realization without anything realized, attainment without anything attained. "This subsiding is itself bodhi": note the word "itself" — the very moment the false mind is stilled is exactly the very moment the true mind stands revealed, exactly as clouds part to reveal the moon, original enlightenment emerging from its own entanglement, the threefold wisdom's perfect awakening, this supremely pure, bright mind being exactly the very substance of bodhi — incomparably supreme, pure and undefiled, its light illuminating everywhere, originally pervading the whole Dharma-realm. This mind, while confused, was never truly lost, though it seemed lost; and, once realized, there is nothing gained that was not already had, and yet it counts as gaining — this is one's own inherently possessed family treasure, never obtained from anyone else.

This supremely pure, bright mind attained here carries two senses as well — "initial resolve" and "ultimate completion" — set against the earlier "perfect suppression" and "perfect severance." Perfect suppression of ignorance belongs to the stage of the ten faiths, breaking through one grade of ignorance, ascending to the abiding of initial resolve — this is bodhi at the level of initial resolve. Perfect severance of ignorance belongs to the final mind of equal enlightenment, breaking through all forty-two grades of ignorance entirely, realizing the ultimate fruit of wondrous enlightenment — this is bodhi at the level of ultimate completion.

For an ordinary being of great resolve, possessing sudden faculties, though still at the stage of contemplative practice, perfectly suppressing the five abidings of affliction, personally beholding this bodhi, this supremely pure, bright mind, is no different at all from what is realized at the levels of initial resolve or ultimate completion. As a patriarch said: "simply leave deluded conditions behind, and one is, just as one is, already a buddha" — exactly this mad mind subsiding all at once, this subsiding itself being bodhi. It is only revealed through this subsiding, never produced by it — it is one's own inherently possessed, naturally given truth.

"Why, then, should you need such toil and labor, such painstaking effort, to cultivate and realize it?": this concludes by reproaching the very intention to strive in cultivation. Since the true mind is already inherently possessed, and ignorance is, at root, empty, there is no falseness left to sever, and no truth left to attain — why, then, should such toil and labor be needed at all? The Buddha's own point is: once one can simply understand that falseness is, at root, empty, falseness empty and truth revealed — why should one need to search out the cause of falseness, and toil painstakingly at severing it, hoping thereby to realize entry into truth at all?

This passage belongs to the sudden teaching's own Dharma-gate, pointing directly to the single, topmost step: no cultivation, no realization — this must be well understood, and not misread. There are always those of a reckless, false wisdom who, clinging only to the notion that bodhi is already inherently possessed, that mind itself is exactly Buddha, dismiss cultivation and realization altogether — turning the finest ghee into poison! One should understand: buddhahood, though already so, must still be cultivated; delusion, though originally empty, must still be severed. Cultivation and realization are not, then, simply absent — and yet defilement is, in the end, never actually attained either. Cultivation without cultivation, and yet cultivation is exactly no cultivation; severance without severance, and yet severance is exactly no severance — only this accords with this sūtra's own teaching of complete-meaning cultivation and realization. "Stop following after discrimination" is exactly cultivation without cultivation; "the very madness will subside of itself" is exactly severance without severance; "this supremely pure, bright mind, originally pervading the whole Dharma-realm" is exactly realization without realization.

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "It is like a man who has a wish-fulfilling jewel sewn into the lining of his own robe, without knowing it is there — reduced to poverty, wandering to distant lands, begging and running about. Though genuinely destitute, he never actually lost the jewel. When a wise person suddenly points it out to him, every wish comes true, and he becomes greatly wealthy — only then realizing that this wondrous jewel was never obtained from anywhere outside himself."

Commentary: The man stands for the ordinary being and the Lesser Vehicle; the robe stands for ignorance, both fundamental and derivative; the wish-fulfilling pearl stands for bodhi, this supremely pure, bright mind. "Without knowing it is there": the true mind, covered over layer upon layer by coarse and fine affliction alike, is confused, unaware of itself — never truly lost, though it seems lost. "Reduced to poverty, wandering to distant lands" stands for the phantom city of the three realms: "poverty" meaning destitute, possessing no Dharma-treasure, referring to the two vehicles sunk and stuck within this same phantom city, never bringing forth their own free, wondrous function; "exposed" meaning laid bare, with nowhere to shelter or take refuge, referring to ordinary beings sunk and drowning within the three realms, never finding a place to settle their own body and establish their own life. "Begging and running about": begging for the small benefit of tainted or untainted merit — though genuinely destitute, the pearl was never actually lost, standing for the fact that, though this wondrous function pervading the whole Dharma-realm was never brought forth, this true, bodhi mind was never actually lost either. The first four lines illustrate that, though truth is inherently possessed, it is unrecognized; the last four illustrate that, though truth remains within confusion, it is never actually lost.

"When a wise person suddenly points it out to him, every wish comes true, and he becomes greatly wealthy — only then realizing that this wondrous jewel was never obtained from anywhere outside himself": the wise person stands for the Buddha; pointing out the pearl stands for the Buddha's own teaching, pointing to the true mind. If one can suddenly awaken to this original mind, and, in accordance with this substance, give rise to its function, becoming greatly wealthy — this stands for this supremely pure, bright mind, originally pervading the whole Dharma-realm. "Only then realizing that this wondrous jewel was never obtained from anywhere outside himself" stands for the fact that, though truth is now awakened to, there is nothing actually gained — matching, in the analogy, the pearl, though the man was destitute, never actually being lost; and, since nothing was ever lost, there is nothing gained either — matching, in the doctrine, "never obtained from anyone else."

This section teaching immediate subsidence is exactly the perfect, sudden teaching's own point: knowing that truth is inherently possessed, and understanding that falseness is, at root, empty. Simply subsiding from madness, without any toil or painstaking effort at all, is exactly cultivation without cultivation — entirely the same point as the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration, the complete-meaning method of cultivation and realization, where this same subsiding from madness is concerned. Turning hearing back upon one's own nature, turning one's back on dust and uniting with awareness, is exactly this same "not following after discrimination," the three conditions cut off all at once. Untying the six knots in sequence, from coarse to fine, from shallow to deep, hearing entirely exhausted with nothing left to abide in, emptiness and awareness both perfected to the utmost, arising and ceasing both extinguished, arriving directly at quiescent extinction manifest — this is exactly the three causes no longer arising, the very madness of Yajñadatta within one's own mind subsiding of itself, this supremely pure, bright mind, originally pervading the whole Dharma-realm, functioning in accordance with its own substance, attaining great freedom — matching, in the analogy, the pearl sewn into the man's own robe, found exactly where it always was, every wish coming true, becoming greatly wealthy — the same as Avalokiteśvara attaining the two supreme qualities and bringing forth the three compassionate functions.

As for Pūrṇa's own capacity, one beyond further training, though his clinging to a self is already broken through, his clinging to phenomena remains unsevered — he, too, must begin from this same subsiding of madness. First breaking through the discriminating clinging to phenomena, no longer following after the arising of the three conditions — world, karma-fruit, sentient beings — the innate clinging to phenomena, together with these, ceases to arise, and only then do the three causes fail to arise at all, the very madness of Yajñadatta within his own mind subsiding of itself! The two words "not following" are exactly the effort behind this subsiding of madness — the same meaning as the Buddha's own earlier teaching on practice: "set aside what arises and ceases, and hold fast instead to what is ever-true and constant." The teaching of the empty-and-non-empty tathāgatagarbha, showing why everything interfuses, is complete. Together with the non-empty tathāgatagarbha discussed before it, the direct answer to Pūrṇa is complete.
