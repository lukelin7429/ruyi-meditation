Ten Demons, Ten Philosophies

The aggregate of perception's own ten states depart entirely from the pattern of the first twenty: here, an actual heavenly demon possesses a nearby person and speaks convincing Dharma through them, exploiting one sincere but overly specific craving as its point of entry. Once perception itself is exhausted, the aggregate of formations opens instead onto a different danger altogether — not possession, but philosophy: ten coherent, carefully reasoned conclusions about the nature of existence, each one correct as far as it goes, and each one absolutized into a wrong view at the very last step.

About This Fascicle

This fascicle completes the aggregate of perception's own ten states — the famous heavenly-demon episodes, where an actual outside spirit possesses a nearby person and teaches convincing but corrupt Dharma through them — and then covers the aggregate of formations in full: its own boundary, and the ten wild philosophical interpretations that can crystallize out of a genuine but partial view of it, each one traced back to a specific school of non-Buddhist thought the Buddha's own contemporaries would have recognized by name. The companion bare-sūtra project's fascicles 9 and 10 already translate this same sūtra material in condensed form — the ten heavenly-demon states as a being-grid summary, and the ten philosophical positions the same way — and this fascicle reuses that project's exact wording for the sūtra passages themselves wherever it applies. What this fascicle adds, for the first time, is Yuanying's own full clause-by-clause commentary on every state and every position, together with the complete sūtra text quoted in full rather than condensed.

The Aggregate of Perception: Its Boundary 辛三 想陰魔相 · 癸初 始修未破區宇

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難！彼善男子，修三摩地，受陰盡者，雖未漏盡，心離其形，如烏出籠，已能成就，從是凡身，上歷菩薩，六十聖位，得意生身，隨往無礙。

〖講〗此通明想陰始終之境。彼善男子：躡前透過受陰十境之人，或備經，或不備經，總以不為所惑，透過前境，受陰破，仍復精研，故曰修三摩提；望受陰為終修，望想陰為始修也。受陰盡者：而於前境，一一透過，然猶為想陰所覆，故云雖未漏盡；而雖之云者，亦有似盡意耳。心離其形者：以真心周遍，本來不局於身，由無始迷執，非局而局，縱色陰盡，十方洞開，見聞周遍，亦無離身自在之用，此皆受陰覆之之故。今受陰既盡，方得心離其形，親見離根之體；恐猶未明，故又以如鳥出籠喻之。鳥喻離根之體，籠喻所結之根。離根之體，即是第八本識，既得離根之體，似得漏盡勝用。故云已能成就，從是博地凡夫之身，上歷諸位菩薩，乃至妙覺。六十聖位：此乃指圓頓最利之根，決定能以凡身，上歷聖位也。非同別教，皆實取證故；經生累劫，證得一分，方到一位，豈能以凡身，頓歷諸位哉？六十聖位者：於五十五位，前加三漸次，及乾慧地，後加妙覺，恰成六十。今通名聖者：以從凡入聖，因果理同，以得圓悟故也。得意生身，隨往無礙者：喻如意去，速疾無礙，而有三種：一、人三昧，樂意生身；謂心寂不動，即相似初信至七信，入空行也。二、覺法自性，性意生身；謂普入佛剎，以法為自性，即相似八信，出假位也。三、種類俱生，無作意生身；謂了佛所證法，即九信十信，修中位也。此三種意生身中，應是覺法自性，性意生身。以離根之體，即是第八本識，一切諸法，皆依此識變現，既得此識，即能覺了，一切諸法，自性如幻，以唯識變故。得此身已，不惟能現，且能普入諸剎，故云隨往無礙。初躡前受陰盡相竟。

〖經〗譬如有人，熟寐寱言，是人雖則，無別所知，其言已成，音韻倫次，令不寐者，咸悟其語，此則名為，想陰區宇。

〖講〗熟寐，深睡也；寱言，是自言自語也；非同醒人之言，以有想陰所覆故。是深睡人，昏昏不覺，而於所說之事，雖則無別所知，但其寱言，已成音韻可聽，倫類次序可別，令不寐之人，成皆明悟其語。是想陰未破之人，得意生身，上歷六十聖位，隨往無礙，而於上合下同，實未親證故。雖未親證，而所現不誤；諸佛菩薩，悉知此人，所現之身相可見，位次不紊，如二漸中，言其得通遊界，睹佛聞法，親奉聖旨，則諸佛誰不親知而見耶？此則名為，想陰未破之區宇，初始修未破區宇竟。

〖經〗若動念盡，浮想銷除，於覺明心，如去塵垢，一倫生死，首尾圓照，名想陰盡。是人則能，超越煩惱濁，觀其所由，融通妄想以為其本。

〖講〗《指掌疏》云：動念者，即指第八識所含六識種子；以有微細動相，故以動念稱之。動必有想，即是根本想陰，六識浮想，皆依此想起故。此想既盡，六識中枝末浮想，無所從起，故云浮想銷除。覺明心，即第八本識；以帶妄故，不言妙覺明心。動念既盡，浮想不生，故云如去塵垢，蓋以性識覺明如鏡，六識浮想如塵，微細動相如垢。一倫生死，首尾圓照者：謂三界十二類眾生，一類一類，所有生死，首從卵生，尾至非無想生，皆能圓明照察，生從何來，死向何去，以塵垢既盡，覺心光明既顯，生滅根元，從此披露；如後文所云：見諸十方，十二眾生，畢殫其類是也。然類生，生滅根元，即是行陰；行陰既現，是則超出想陰，故曰名想陰盡。此當圓通，覺所覺空也。《指掌疏》云：前於聞所聞盡時，覺得有箇聞所聞盡，宛然有箇能覺之心，即是第八識中，六識種子，微細動相。今想既盡，微細動相亦無，故能覺與所覺，而俱空矣！所以不復真者，以猶為行陰所覆故。是人則能超煩惱濁者：如前煩惱濁文云，又汝心中，憶識誦習，離塵無相，離覺無性，相織妄成，名煩惱濁。今以動念既盡，浮想銷除，想除識空，故能超越。同觀想陰之所由生，元從融通妄想，交織妄成，以其想陰，能融通質礙，如心想醋梅，口中水出是也。初具示始終竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, once this good person, cultivating samādhi, has exhausted the aggregate of sensation — though not yet free of outflows entirely — the mind can leave the body like a bird escaping its cage, already able to progress, starting from this ordinary body, up through all sixty sagely stages of the bodhisattva path, gaining a mind-made body that travels wherever it wishes without obstruction."

Commentary: This passage, taken as a whole, makes plain the aggregate of perception's own boundary from beginning to end. "This good person" refers to one who has passed through the ten states of the aggregate of sensation described earlier — whether encountering every one of them or not — in every case never misled by them, having passed through this prior territory, so that the aggregate of sensation breaks through; continuing still to refine this research further, this is called "cultivating this samādhi" — with respect to sensation, marking the end of that practice, and, with respect to perception, marking the beginning of this next one.

"Exhausted the aggregate of sensation" — though having passed through every prior state one by one, one still remains covered by the aggregate of perception — hence, "though not yet free of outflows entirely"; and this word "though" itself carries the sense of something nearly, but not quite, exhausted.

"The mind can leave the body" means: since this true mind pervades everywhere, and was never, in truth, confined within the body at all, it is only through beginningless confused clinging that it becomes, though not truly confined, confined all the same. Even once the aggregate of form is exhausted, the ten directions opened up entirely, seeing and hearing pervading everywhere, there is still no free function of leaving the body at all — this, too, is entirely because the aggregate of sensation still covers it over. Now that the aggregate of sensation is exhausted, the mind is, at last, able to leave the body, personally beholding this substance detached from the sense-faculties itself; fearing this may still not be entirely clear, the text further offers the metaphor of a bird escaping its cage. The bird is a metaphor for this substance detached from the faculties; the cage is a metaphor for the very faculty that had bound it. This substance detached from the faculties is precisely the eighth, root consciousness itself; having attained this substance detached from the faculties, one comes close to attaining the very function belonging to one whose outflows are entirely exhausted — hence, "already able to progress, starting from this ordinary body, up through" every stage of the bodhisattva path, all the way to wondrous enlightenment itself.

"All sixty sagely stages" refers specifically to the sharpest faculties of the perfect, sudden teaching, certainly able to progress, with this very ordinary body, straight up through every sagely stage — not the same as the separate teaching, where every stage must be actually, individually realized, requiring lifetime upon lifetime, kalpa upon kalpa, to realize even a single portion and reach a single stage — how, then, could this ordinary body ever progress through every stage all at once? These sixty sagely stages are the fifty-five stages with the three gradual stages and the ground of dry wisdom added before them, and wondrous enlightenment added after — exactly sixty in total. All are called, together, "sagely" here because, in passing from the ordinary to the sagely, cause and fruit share one and the same principle, since this perfect awakening has already been attained.

"Gaining a mind-made body that travels wherever it wishes without obstruction" means: like going wherever the mind wishes, swift and free of any obstruction — and there are three kinds of such a body. First, the mind-made body of one who delights in samādhi entered upon (人三昧樂意生身): meaning that this mind is still and unmoving — corresponding, by resemblance, to the first through seventh of the ten faiths, entering the practice of emptiness. Second, the mind-made body awakened to the nature of phenomena as one's own nature (覺法自性性意生身): meaning entering universally into every buddha-land, taking phenomena themselves as one's own nature — corresponding, by resemblance, to the eighth faith, emerging into the provisional. Third, the mind-made body of every kind arising together, without any contrivance at all (種類俱生無作意生身): meaning fully realizing the very Dharma a buddha has realized — corresponding to the ninth and tenth faiths, cultivating the middle position. Among these three kinds of mind-made body, this passage refers specifically to the second, the mind-made body awakened to the nature of phenomena as one's own nature. Since this substance detached from the faculties is precisely the eighth, root consciousness, and every single phenomenon manifests dependent on this consciousness, having attained this consciousness, one is thereby able to awaken to the fact that every phenomenon's own nature is, like an illusion, nothing but this consciousness's own transformation. Having attained this body, one is not only able to manifest freely, but able, moreover, to enter fully into every buddha-land at once — hence, "travels wherever it wishes without obstruction." This closes the first section, carrying forward the mark of the aggregate of sensation already exhausted.

Sūtra: "It is like a person talking in their sleep, deep in slumber: though this person has, in fact, no separate awareness of what they are saying, their very words have already taken on distinct tones and coherent order, so that anyone not asleep can fully understand what they are saying. This is called the boundary of the aggregate of perception."

Commentary: "Deep slumber" means deep sleep; "talking in one's sleep" means talking to oneself, unprompted — not the same as the speech of one who is awake, precisely because it remains covered by the aggregate of perception. This deeply sleeping person, dim and unaware, has, regarding the matter being spoken of, no separate awareness of it at all; yet their very sleep-talk has already taken on distinct, audible tones, a coherent order that can be discerned, so that anyone not asleep comes to understand fully what they are saying.

This describes one for whom the aggregate of perception has not yet broken through, who has gained this mind-made body, progressing through all sixty sagely stages, traveling wherever they wish without obstruction — and yet, regarding this same accord above with the sagely and unity below with the ordinary, has not, in truth, personally realized it at all. Though not yet personally realized, still, what manifests is not mistaken: every buddha and bodhisattva knows fully well this very person, the bodily marks they manifest able to be seen, their sequence of stages in no way disordered — just as, within the second of the three gradual stages, it is said that one gains the power to roam freely through every realm, beholding buddhas and hearing the Dharma, personally receiving this sagely instruction directly — and which buddha, then, would fail to personally know and see them? This is called the boundary of the aggregate of perception not yet broken through. This closes the first section, the boundary not yet broken through, at the very start of practice.

Sūtra: "Once every stirring thought is exhausted and every floating, superficial idea dissolved, so that the bright, awakened mind is as if cleansed of all dust, and the entire cycle of birth and death, beginning to end, is illuminated in a single perfect view, this is called the exhaustion of the aggregate of perception, and such a person can transcend the turbidity of affliction itself. Examining its cause, this aggregate is rooted in interfusing, false thought."

Commentary: As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: "stirring thought" refers precisely to the seeds of the six consciousnesses contained within the eighth consciousness — since this subtle, fine mark of movement is present, it is spoken of here as "stirring thought." Movement must give rise to thought — this is precisely the very root of the aggregate of perception itself, since every floating, superficial thought of the six consciousnesses arises dependent on this same root thought. Once this root thought is exhausted, the branch-and-twig, floating thought within the six consciousnesses has nothing left from which to arise — hence, "every floating, superficial idea dissolved." "The bright, awakened mind" refers to the eighth, root consciousness itself; since it still carries some trace of falseness, the text does not yet call it the wondrous, awakened, bright mind. Stirring thought already exhausted, floating thought no longer arising — hence, "as if cleansed of all dust": this nature of consciousness, awakened and bright, resembles a mirror; the six consciousnesses' own floating thought resembles dust upon it; this subtle, fine mark of movement resembles a smudge upon it.

"The entire cycle of birth and death, beginning to end, is illuminated in a single perfect view" means: throughout the three realms, class by class, among the twelve categories of sentient beings, every single instance of birth and death — from the very first, egg-born, to the very last, born neither through thought nor its absence — can be illuminated and observed in perfect completeness: whence birth comes, and whither death goes — since this dust and this smudge are now both exhausted, this awakened mind's own light now made manifest, this very root of arising and ceasing is, from this point, laid bare — exactly as a later passage states: "beholding, throughout the ten directions, every one of the twelve kinds of sentient being, their every category exhausted entirely." And this same root of arising and ceasing, class by class, is precisely the aggregate of formations itself; once the aggregate of formations manifests, this is precisely transcending the aggregate of perception — hence, "this is called the exhaustion of the aggregate of perception." This corresponds, within the teaching of perfect penetration, to "awareness, and what is aware of it, both become empty."

As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: earlier, at the moment when "the very hearing of what is heard comes to an end," one was aware of there being such a thing as "the hearing of what is heard coming to an end" — plainly, there remained some awareness capable of this awareness, precisely this subtle, fine mark of movement, these seeds of the six consciousnesses within the eighth. Now that thought itself is exhausted, this subtle, fine mark of movement, too, is gone — and so awareness, and what is aware of it, both alike become empty! The reason this is still not the true return, though, is that it remains covered over by the aggregate of consciousness still.

"Such a person can transcend the turbidity of affliction itself": as the earlier passage on this turbidity states, "and further, within your own mind, recollection, discrimination, recitation, and habituation — apart from this dust, no mark remains; apart from this awareness, no nature remains — interwoven together, this falsely forms what is named the turbidity of affliction." Now that stirring thought is exhausted, and floating thought dissolved, thought itself removed and consciousness made empty, one is thereby able to transcend it. Observing together the very cause from which the aggregate of perception arises, one finds it rooted, at its origin, in interfusing false thought, woven falsely together — since this aggregate of perception is able to interfuse and pass through what is otherwise solid and obstructed, exactly as, when the mind thinks of a sour plum, water rises of itself within the mouth. This closes the first section, showing this aggregate's full course from beginning to end.

The Aggregate of Perception: Ten Heavenly-Demon States 壬二 中間十境

What follows departs entirely from the pattern of the first twenty states, as Yuanying notes throughout: rather than the practitioner's own mind alone producing a mistaken experience, an actual heavenly demon, watching for its opening, sends its own spirit to possess a nearby person entirely, and, speaking through that person, sets up as a teacher specifically to corrupt the genuine practitioner. Every one of the ten follows the same seven-part arc Yuanying identifies explicitly in each case: a particular craving opens the door (定發愛求); the demon finds its opening and sends a spirit to possess someone (魔遣邪附); this possessed host comes and disturbs the practitioner as a guest teacher (客邪投擾 / 邪惑事言); the practitioner, as the true master of their own aggregates, is thrown into confusion (主人惑亂); the demon's teaching is examined for its telltale marks (按其言狀); its true nature — always some aged nature-spirit or ghost — and the harm it brings are named (出名示害); and, last, the practitioner is taught to recognize and guard against it (教悟戒迷).

Chinese Source — State 1: Craving Skillful Means 貪求善巧

〖經〗阿難！彼善男子，受陰虛妙，不遭邪慮，圓定發明，三摩地中，心愛圓明，銳其精思，貪求善巧。

〖講〗此下別示，想陰十種魔境，此第一貪求善巧。彼指透過受陰之男子，是以稱善。虛謂見聞遍周，妙謂離身作用，如鳥出籠，得意生身，隨往無礙。不遭邪慮者：謂不遭遇，受陰邪慮所惑。圓定發明：即圓通妙定，得以發明。是受陰已盡境界，後皆倣此。三摩地中，心愛圓明者：於此禪定心中，忽起一念，愛著圓明，謂愛著圓滿，發明一切妙用故。勇銳其志，精進思惟，貪求變化，更進善巧，將以悚動人心，以行教化，廣作佛事也。

〖經〗爾時天魔，候得其便，飛精附人，口說經法。

〖講〗當爾之時，天魔，即六天魔王，候得其便，有隙可乘，即飛遣精魅，以附他人之身，素受邪惑者。飛精，如軍門飛檄，官府之類。口說相似經法，由魔附之力，資其邪慧，以令聽受，非真能說佛法；若真能說佛法，即非魔矣。蓋受陰盡者，魔不得入其心腑。故假旁人惑之，轉令自亂耳。

〖經〗其人不覺，是其魔著，自言謂得，無上涅槃，來彼求巧善男子處，敷座說法，其形斯須，或作比丘，令彼人見，或為帝釋，或為婦女，或比丘尼，或寢暗室，身有光明。

〖講〗其人：即所附之人；不覺者：雖為魔著，不自覺知。自言謂得，無上涅槃者：以其人一向不能說法，今竟無端能說經法，自己疑成佛道，謂言得無上涅槃，來彼求巧善男子處，敷座說法，蓋欲設計誘惑之耳。說法者：正說善巧方便，示現神通之法。其形斯須者：其形貌於斯須少頃之間，或現作比丘身，以現同類身，投其所好，令彼生信。或現帝釋身，或現婦女身，及比丘尼者：此現異類身也。《起信論》云：座中或現，端正男女等相。又云：或現天像、菩薩像，亦作如來像等。但彼乃魔自來現，此乃附人轉現。又則彼但見所現之像，或可有疑，此則親見其人，斯須變化；如此非有深定妙慧，鮮有不被其惑者。或寢暗室之中，身有種種光明，或時說法，或時現形，或時放光，廣作善巧，鼓動其心，令其自亂耳。

〖經〗是人愚迷，惑為菩薩，信其教化，搖蕩其心，破佛律儀，潛行貪欲。

〖講〗是人愚而無智，迷不自覺，惑為菩薩，三輪應機，身輪現通，口輪說法，意輪必定鑒機，自是傾心，信其教化，將所習定心，咸被搖蕩，所秉戒律，咸被破壞。潛行貪欲者：是魔惑亂行人，徹底主意，凡行人著魔，欲心便起，以魔多貪欲，潛行毀戒，而不解脫者此也。然淨行深，智慧強者，任其善巧莫測，但察誘淫毀戒，便知是魔，決非佛誨，何至迷惑，此反為驗魔之一助耳。

〖經〗口中好言，災祥變異，或言如來，某處出世；或言劫火，或說刀兵，恐怖於人，令其家資，無故耗散。

〖講〗口中常好說言：災，是咎徵，劫火刀兵等，恐怖於人；祥，是休徵，如來某處出世等；變異，即怪誕反常之言。令其家資，無故耗散者：或說咎徵，劫火大三災起、刀兵小三災至，則整家以求救脫；或說休徵，某處有佛，傾資以求接引；故曰家資無故耗散。及至臨時，了無其事。

〖經〗此名怪鬼，年老成魔，惱亂是人，厭足心生。去彼人體，弟子與師，俱陷王難。

〖講〗怪鬼：即遇物成形者。年老成魔者：以為鬼既久，魔王錄為役使，得成魔王伴侶；前飛精附人，即此鬼也。惱亂是修定之人，定力既破，厭足心生，去彼所附人體。魔既不附，即無威德。弟子，即貪求善巧之人等；師，即魔附惑人者。俱陷王難：官廳坐以妖言惑眾，敗壞風俗之罪，此是華報，果報當在地獄。

〖經〗汝當先覺，不入輪迴；迷惑不知，墮無間獄。

〖講〗汝當聽言察理，預先覺知是魔，不為所惑，而超出生死，不入輪迴。倘若迷惑不知，受其惱亂，必墮無間之獄，可不慎哉！初貪求善巧竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, this good person, the aggregate of sensation now empty and wondrous, encountering no deviant apprehension, this perfect samādhi brought to light — within this samādhi, their mind comes to love perfect brightness, sharpening their own refined thinking, craving skillful means."

Commentary: From this point on, the ten distinct demonic states of the aggregate of perception are shown one by one; this is the first, craving skillful means. "This" refers to the good person who has passed through the aggregate of sensation, hence called "good." "Empty" means seeing and hearing pervading everywhere; "wondrous" means this function of having left the body, like a bird escaping its cage, gaining this mind-made body, traveling wherever it wishes without obstruction. "Encountering no deviant apprehension" means not being misled by any deviant apprehension belonging to the aggregate of sensation. "This perfect samādhi brought to light" is precisely this perfect, penetrating, wondrous samādhi, now brought to light — this is the state belonging to the aggregate of sensation already exhausted; everything that follows should be understood along this same pattern. "Within this samādhi, their mind comes to love perfect brightness" means: within this mind of meditative samādhi, a single thought suddenly arises, cherishing attachment to this perfect brightness — that is, cherishing attachment to the perfect, complete manifestation of every wondrous function. Sharpening one's own resolve, applying diligent, refined thought, craving transformation, seeking further skillful means still, wishing to stir and move people's hearts, to carry out teaching and transformation, and to accomplish great Buddhist works widely.

Sūtra: "At that time, a heavenly demon, watching for its opening, sends its own spirit to possess a person, who then speaks the sūtras and their teaching."

Commentary: At exactly this time, this heavenly demon — that is, one of the demon-kings of the sixth heaven — watches for its opening, finding a gap it can exploit, and sends its own spirit forth to possess another person's body, one who has always been susceptible to deviant delusion. "Sends forth its spirit" resembles a military dispatch flying between camp and headquarters. Speaking what resembles the sūtras and their teaching, through the power of this demonic possession, drawing on this borrowed deviant wisdom, so as to make listeners receive it — this is not, in truth, someone genuinely able to expound the Buddhadharma; were they genuinely able to do so, this would not be a demon at all. For one whose aggregate of sensation is already exhausted cannot be entered by a demon directly, in their own inmost heart — and so the demon borrows some other, nearby person instead to mislead them, causing the practitioner to fall into confusion indirectly.

Sūtra: "This person, unaware that they are possessed by this demon, themselves declares that they have attained unsurpassed nirvāṇa, and comes to where this good person seeking skillful means dwells, sets up their own seat, and expounds the Dharma. Their own form, in the briefest moment, may appear as a monk, causing that person to see them so; or as Śakra; or as a woman; or as a nun; or they may sleep within a darkened room, their own body giving off light."

Commentary: "This person" refers to the one who has been possessed; "unaware" means that, though possessed by this demon, they are entirely unaware of it themselves. "Themselves declares that they have attained unsurpassed nirvāṇa" means: since this person has never, before now, been able to expound the Dharma at all, and yet now, unaccountably, is able to speak the sūtras and their teaching, they come to suspect, of themselves, that they have accomplished the path of buddhahood, declaring that they have attained unsurpassed nirvāṇa, and come to where this good person seeking skillful means dwells, setting up their own seat and expounding the Dharma — all of this precisely a scheme to entice and mislead. "Expounding the Dharma" refers specifically to expounding skillful means, manifesting spiritual powers as a kind of teaching. "Their own form, in the briefest moment" means that this bodily form, within the very briefest span of time, may appear, at times, as a monk — manifesting a form of the same kind, catering to what the practitioner already favors, so as to make them believe; or may appear as Śakra, or as a woman, or as a nun — these being forms of a different kind.

As the Awakening of Faith states: within the assembly, it may manifest the marks of an upright man or woman, and so on; and, further, it may manifest the image of a heavenly being, or of a bodhisattva, or even the image of a Tathāgata himself. But there, the demon itself comes directly and manifests these forms; here, it possesses another person and manifests them through that person instead. And further: there, one merely sees the manifested image, and may still harbor some doubt about it; here, one personally witnesses this very person transforming before one's own eyes in the briefest instant — and, absent some deep samādhi and wondrous wisdom, few indeed could avoid being misled by this. Or, sleeping within a darkened room, their own body gives off every kind of light; at times expounding the Dharma, at times manifesting some form, at times emitting light, carrying out these skillful means widely, stirring the practitioner's own heart, and causing them to fall into confusion of their own accord.

Sūtra: "This person, foolish and confused, mistakes them for a bodhisattva, believes in their teaching and transformation, their own mind shaken and unsettled, breaking the Buddha's own monastic discipline, and secretly indulging in desire."

Commentary: This person, foolish and lacking any wisdom, confused and unaware, mistakes the possessed teacher for a bodhisattva — one whose three wheels respond according to capacity: the wheel of the body manifesting spiritual powers, the wheel of speech expounding the Dharma, the wheel of the mind certainly discerning each capacity — and so, of themselves, giving their whole heart over, believes in this teaching and transformation, so that whatever settled mind they had cultivated is, thereupon, entirely shaken and unsettled, and whatever precepts they had upheld are, thereupon, entirely destroyed. "Secretly indulging in desire" points to the demon's own ultimate intent in confusing and disordering the practitioner — for whenever a practitioner falls prey to a demon, a mind of desire is thereby aroused; since demons, for the most part, crave desire themselves, they secretly indulge in it, breaking the precepts, and thereby fail ever to attain liberation. Yet one whose pure conduct runs deep, and whose wisdom is strong, though the demon's own skillful means may be beyond ordinary reckoning, need only observe this same enticement to lust and destruction of the precepts to recognize this as demonic at once, certainly not the Buddha's own teaching — how, then, could one ever be misled by it? This, in fact, becomes one further means of verifying a demon for what it is.

Sūtra: "This mouth loves to speak of calamity and good fortune, of strange and unusual portents — declaring that the Tathāgata has appeared in a certain place, or speaking of coming conflagrations, or of coming warfare, striking terror into people, so that their own family fortune is squandered away for no reason at all."

Commentary: This mouth constantly loves to speak of such things: calamity is an ill omen — coming conflagrations, warfare, and the like, striking terror into people; good fortune is an auspicious omen — the Tathāgata appearing in a certain place, and the like; strange and unusual portents are simply bizarre, contrary-to-nature pronouncements. "Their own family fortune is squandered away for no reason at all" means: either it speaks of some ill omen — the great conflagration of the aeon-ending fires arising, or the lesser calamity of warfare arriving — so that the whole household scrambles to seek deliverance; or it speaks of some auspicious omen — a buddha present in a certain place — so that they exhaust their entire fortune seeking to be received by this buddha; and so the family fortune is squandered away for no reason at all — and, when the appointed time finally arrives, nothing whatsoever has actually happened.

Sūtra: "This is named a strange-object ghost, grown old and become a demon, tormenting this person; and, once its purpose has been served, it departs from that person's body, and both disciple and teacher alike fall into entanglement with the law."

Commentary: A strange-object ghost is one that takes on form by encountering some object. "Grown old and become a demon" means that, having existed as a ghost for a very long time, the demon-king enrolls it into his own service, so that it becomes one of his own retinue — and this same spirit, sent flying to possess a person earlier, is exactly this ghost. Tormenting this practitioner cultivating samādhi, once their own power of samādhi has been broken through, this same sense of its purpose being served arises, and it departs from the body of the person it had possessed. Once the demon no longer possesses them, they are left with no authority or virtue at all. "Disciple" refers to the one craving skillful means and the like; "teacher" refers to the one possessed by the demon and misleading others. "Fall into entanglement with the law" means: the authorities charge them with the crime of misleading the multitude through deviant talk, corrupting public morals — this being the immediately visible retribution alone, its true karmic fruit still awaiting them in hell.

Sūtra: "You should recognize this in advance, so as not to enter the turning wheel of rebirth; confused and unaware of it, you fall into the unrelenting hell."

Commentary: You should listen to what is said and examine the principle behind it, recognizing in advance that this is a demon, not misled by it, and thereby transcend birth and death, not entering the turning wheel of rebirth. But should you remain confused and unaware, suffering this torment, you will certainly fall into the unrelenting hell — how could this not call for the utmost care! This closes the first state, craving skillful means.

〖經〗又善男子，受陰虛妙，不遭邪慮，圓定發明，三摩地中，心愛神通，種種變化，研究化元，貪取神力。爾時天魔，候得其便，飛精附人，口說經法。其人誠不覺知魔著，亦言自得無上涅槃，來彼求通善男子處，敷座說法。是人或復手執火光，手撮其光，分於所聽，四眾頭上，是諸聽人，頂上火光，皆長數尺，亦無熱性，曾不焚燒；或水上行，如履平地；或於空中，安坐不動；或入瓶內，或處囊中，越牖透牆，曾無障礙；惟於刀兵，不得自在。自言是佛，身著白衣，受比丘禮，誹謗禪律，罵詈徒眾，訐露人事，不避譏嫌。口中常說，神通自在。或復令人，旁見佛土，鬼力惑人，非有真實。讚歎行淫，不毀麤行，將諸猥媟，以為傳法。此名天地大力，山精、海精、風精、河精、土精、一切草木，積劫精魅，或復龍魅，或壽終仙，再活為魅，或仙期終，計年應死，其形不化，他怪所附，年老成魔，惱亂是人。厭足心生，去彼人體，弟子與師，多陷王難。汝當先覺，不入輪迴；迷惑不知，墮無間獄。

〖講〗此第八貪求神力，首四句同前。三摩禪定之中，心愛菩薩神通，神妙莫測，通達無礙，種種變化；由是研究變化之元，貪求取得，神通之力。溫陵曰：化元萬化之本也。欲乘之以發神變耳。起心研究，即落邪思；著意貪取，便同有作。安得不為天魔，候得其便哉？其人手執火光下：先現邪惑事，若但說猶不足以取信，今特為現種種神變。是人或手執火光，以手撮取其光，分於所有聽眾頭上，是諸聽人，頂上火光，皆長數尺，不熱不燒。或水上能行，猶如履踐平地，此則水火二者，而得自在。或於空中，安坐不動，宛似空色一如，或入瓶內，或處囊中，幾等大小相容，或牖閉之而可越，或垣隔之而能透，曾無障礙，此亦神異之事。惟於刀兵，不得自在者：以魔未離欲，身見猶存，故不能使刀兵觸之不傷，如風吹光，如刀斷水一樣。自言是佛者：佛為極聖，猶自僭稱。但身著白衣，受比丘禮拜，誹謗禪律者：謗宗門為靜坐狂參，自命頓超生死。謗律學為持戒修身，究竟不出小乘。禪、教、律三者，唯不謗說教，以自己敷座說法，謬稱己說即是佛說，各宜信仰。蓋自命為佛，是壞佛寶；誹謗禪律，是壞法寶；受比丘禮，是壞僧寶。信其邪說，斷三寶種，佈地獄苗矣！罵詈徒眾者：以示無私；訐露人事者：以表正直；不避譏嫌者：發人陰私，譏謗憎嫌，所不避也。口中時常宣說，神通變化，自在無礙，以慰愛求之心故。或復令人，旁見佛土，為證自己是佛，此皆鬼力要迷惑於人，故意妄現，非有真實。讚歎行淫者：以男女交遘，即是法身常住不絕。並不毀麤行，將諸鄙穢猥媟之事，以為傳法，令得佛種不斷故。此將地獄種，以為佛種，吁可嘆也。天地大力四字，通指下之五精，以是天地間，大力精故。五精各有統轄，威權自在，有正有邪，正者為神，邪者為精，以能與正神分權，其力最大。一切奇草異木，受天地之靈秀，盜日月之精華，積劫既久，成為精魅。或復龍魅：如守天宮殿之龍，及守護伏藏之龍，竊天之靈，盜物之精，而為妖魅。或壽終仙，再活為魅者：存想固形，本望長生不死，豈知仙壽，亦有終盡，再活為魅。或仙期終，計年應死，其形不化：以修仙者，燒鉛煉汞，烹養丹砂，應享遐齡，仙期告終，計年應死，神識難留，形骸不化，為他怪所附，可惜已往功行，反資妖孽。不依正覺，修三摩提，而存想固形者，宜知猛省也。八貪求神力竟。

〖經〗又善男子，受陰虛妙，不遭邪慮，圓定發明，三摩地中，心愛入滅，研究化性，貪求深空。爾時天魔，候得其便，飛精附人，口說經法。其人終不覺知魔著，亦言自得無上涅槃，來彼求空善男子處，敷座說法。於大眾內，其形忽空，眾無所見，還從虛空，突然而出，存沒自在，或現其身，洞如琉璃，或垂手足，作旃檀氣。或大小便，如厚石蜜，誹毀戒律，輕賤出家。口中常說，無因無果，一死永滅，無復後身，及諸凡聖。雖得空寂，潛行貪欲，受其欲者，亦得空心，撥無因果。此名日月，薄蝕精氣，金玉芝草，麟鳳龜鶴，經千萬年，不死為靈，出生國土，年老成魔，惱亂是人。厭足心生，去彼人體，弟子與師，多陷王難。汝當先覺，不入輪迴；迷惑不知，墮無間獄。

〖講〗此第九貪求深空。首四句同前。三摩禪定之中，心愛入滅者：《寶鏡疏》云：由彼不知一切法相，本自寂滅，不待更滅，無故欲將有相之法，而入無相之空。非同小乘，滅色歸空也。研究化性，貪求深空者：研究萬化之體性，即是根本想陰，微細動相，精研窮究，欲期於盡，蓋為貪求深空。言深空者，不唯身境俱空，並且存沒自在也。即此一念貪求，便是定心不密，天魔有隙可乘，飛精附人惑亂，固其宜矣。其人至敷座說法，準上可知。於大眾內，其形忽空者：先現邪惑事，雖說空法，恐猶不信，特為示現，堅固信仰耳。正於大眾內，身形忽空，眾無所見：顯是即有而空；正當形空無見，還從虛空，突然而出，顯是即空而有。如是或存或沒，均得自在，略有似於妙有真空，真空玅有也。但魔力詐現，以遂貪求深空者之心也。或現其身，洞如琉璃者：身為眾穢所集，能現洞澈，如琉璃寶。或垂手足，作旃檀氣者：手足垢污不淨，能垂作旃檀香氣。或大小便，如厚石蜜者：大小便臭穢不堪，能現如厚石蜜之甜，石蜜冰糖也，顯是即染而淨。竟於現前身中，炫異惑眾。誹謗戒律者：誹持戒為小乘，自行束縛。輕賤出家：不得身空，自解求脫。此復說邪惑之言以炫世。口中常好說言，無因無果，一死永滅：如波斯匿王，見迦旃延，毘羅胝子，咸言此身，死後斷滅，正同此見。無復後身者：即一死永滅，無復捨生趣生，而受身後苦樂等報；及諸六凡四聖，迷悟差別；即使聖人修證，亦屬無有。蓋以斷滅為得空寂，以行欲為無礙，故曰雖得空寂，潛行貪欲。受其欲者，名持法子，傳授斷滅之法，亦得心空。撥無因果，生大邪見。永嘉云：豁達空，撥因果，莽莽蕩蕩招殃禍，可不懼哉？《指掌疏》云：日月薄蝕者：黑氣相迫曰薄，蔽其精耀曰蝕，即今日月交食是也。然日為陽精，月為陰精，各有光華之氣，周遍散注；當其薄蝕之際，不得散注，直貫於地。地上之金玉芝草，麟鳳龜鶴，得之可以久生，可以毓秀，故云經千萬年，不死為靈。出生國土者，為物仙，為禽仙，為獸仙，與前所稱，積劫精魅者不同也。九貪求深空竟。

〖經〗又善男子，受陰虛妙，不遭邪慮，圓定發明，三摩地中，心愛長壽，辛苦研幾，貪求永歲，棄分段生，頓希變易，細相常住。爾時天魔，候得其便，飛精附人，口說經法。其人竟不覺知魔著，亦言自得無上涅槃，來彼求生善男子處，敷座說法。好言他方，往還無滯，或經萬裡，瞬息再來。皆於彼方，取得其物；或於一處，在一宅中。數步之間，令其從東，詣至西壁，是人急行，累年不到。因此心信，疑佛現前。口中常說，十方眾生，皆是吾子，我生諸佛，我出世界，我是元佛，出世自然，不因修得。此名住世，自在天魔，使其眷屬，如遮文茶，及四天王，毗舍童子，未發心者，利其虛明，食彼精氣，或不因師，其修行人，親自觀見，稱執金剛，與汝長命。現美女身，盛行貪欲，未逾年歲，肝腦枯竭，口兼獨言，聽若妖魅，前人未詳，多陷王難。未及遇刑，先已乾死，惱亂彼人，以至殂殞。汝當先覺，不入輪迴；迷惑不知，墮無間獄。

〖講〗此第十貪求永歲。首四句同前。於三摩禪定之中，心愛長壽，辛苦研究幾微動相，即想陰根本。《指掌疏》云：然此微細動相，不可以有心研究，但依本修，定深自滅，如澄濁水，貯於靜器，靜深不動，沙土自沉。今以辛勤勞苦，研究幾微，已為失計；而又貪求永歲，而棄分段之生，頓希變易，而望細相常住，是為重增妄想。分段生死者：三界內有形生死；六道眾生，依此身根，有壽命長短之分劑，有體質大小之段落，故稱分段。變易生死者：三界外無形生死；三乘聖人，斷界內見思惑盡，生法性土，故受變易，但心念遷變移易而已。今棄分段之生死，頓希變易之生死，而得細相常住也。好言他方下：先現邪惑之事。他方萬裡之遙，或往或還，去來無滯礙也。瞬息時之短也，可以再來。皆於彼方，取得其物為證。此五通中神足通，魔力所為，以至遠而成至近也。昔道教薛道光，修性不修命，但能出陰神，不能遠方取物。張紫陽，性命雙修，能出陽神，兼能遠方取物。由此觀之，縱能遠方取物，仍不出天魔伎倆，又豈能與《法華》，普現色身三昧，及本經圓通殊勝妙用，論其淺深哉？或於一處，在一宅中，不過數步之間，其量至近，令其從東，詣至西壁，是人急行，累年不到。然所明在己，則行遠若近；而明在他，則令近如遠，蓋地之可縮可舒，時之或延或促，皆魔詐現，以惑亂人心。因此心信，疑為是佛現前矣。此復出邪惑之言。口中常說，十方眾生，皆是吾子者：顯未有眾生之前先有也；我生諸佛者：顯未生諸佛之前先生也；我出世界者：顯未出世界之前先出也；是依報正報，諸佛眾生，我是元佛。既是根本元佛，今猶現在，而壽命之長，更無有過者矣；此投其愛求之本念也。出世自然，不因修得者：正顯其徒修無益，撥無修證之事也。住世自在天魔者：即欲界第六天上，別有魔王居處，亦他化自在天攝。使其眷屬者：如飛遣精魅之類。遮文茶，云奴神，即役使鬼，亦云嫉妒女。毘舍童子，即毘舍遮鬼，此云噉精氣，皆隸屬四王天管轄。已發心則護人，未發心則害人，以取利彼行人，定心虛明，資發邪慧，食彼精氣，助養魔軀也。或不因師者：不因魔附之師，其修行人，親自觀見，魔王現身，口稱執金剛堅固之術，與汝長命，身等金剛也。然後現美女身，引誘迷惑，自恃身形強壯，與之盛行貪欲；未逾一年半歲，肝血腦膜，自告枯竭，殊不知長命之說，徒託空言，損身之害，成為實驗。口兼獨言者：乃謂行人，與魔對語，旁人不曉，以為獨言，所言無定，聽若妖魅。前人未詳下：雙明二俱為害。言前來因師，受惑之人，未詳是魔精所附，以致弟子與師，多陷王難，若此之不因師，與美女盛行貪欲，未及遇刑，先已乾死，其為害猶速也。此二者皆足以惱亂行人，以致殂喪殞亡也。《寶鏡疏》云：以上十種，皆由圓定心中，妄起貪求之念，以致然耳。若是如法精進，一念不生，如幻修證，則何善巧之不得？何法界之不歷？何機理之不契？何根本之不析？何感應之不成？何靜謐之不入？何宿命之不知？何神通之不具？何深空之不證？何常住之不獲？而乃忽生心愛貪求。譬如鱗角未成，輒思飛躍；羽毛不足，便擬扶搖；所謂學未優而求仕，丹未成而先吞，其可乎哉？故知招魔成墮者，皆自心妄想之過也。十貪求永歲竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

4. Craving to Trace Things to Their Root 貪求辨析 — This good person's mind comes to love this very root of things, exhaustively surveying the beginning and end of every transformation among physical objects, sharpening their own mind to its keenest edge, craving analysis and discernment. This craving becomes the point at which a heavenly demon finds its opening, sending its spirit to possess a nearby person, who declares attainment of unsurpassed nirvāṇa and comes to expound the Dharma, their own body carrying an intimidating spiritual authority sufficient to subdue any listener even before a word is spoken.

Commentary: This good person, still advancing in samādhi, comes to love this very root of things because, the aggregate of sensation now exhausted, this subtle, fine mark of movement is laid bare, and, not knowing that this is merely the seed of the sixth consciousness, the very root of the aggregate of perception itself, they mistakenly suppose that this movement itself gives rise to birth, taking it to be the very root of all things. And so, in single-minded devotion, they exhaustively survey the beginning and end of every transformation among the myriad things; once this suddenly opens into full comprehension, nothing of the outer and inner, the fine and coarse, of every single thing remains unreached, and the entire substance and function of one's own mind becomes, in every respect, illuminated! Just as this very sūtra has the Buddha say: knowing the root cause of every worldly and transcendent phenomenon — and so every present thing, the pine growing straight and the thorn growing crooked, the swan white and the crow black, each fully understood down to its root cause, even down to a single drop of rain beyond worlds as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, its exact count fully known — this belongs to the very domain of a buddha's own wisdom; a buddha, having transformed the eighth consciousness into the great, perfect mirror-wisdom, is able to know in this way. For a beginner's mind to aspire to this is nothing but a genuine false thought. "Sharpening their own mind to its keenest edge, craving analysis and discernment" means, so to speak, rousing their own spirit, exhausting their own mental strength, craving to discern the principle behind every object, to analyze the very nature of transformation, so that whatever they wish to know appears before them, each and every point made clear — this very single thought becomes, in truth, the point at which the demon is drawn in.

The word "root" here refers to this longing to exhaust the very origin of every transformation. "Their own body carrying an intimidating spiritual authority sufficient to subdue any listener" refers to this possessed body likewise carrying some awe-inspiring, fearsome quality, some spiritual power able to hold and subdue, sufficient to overcome anyone seeking this same root, so that, though those in the assembly have not yet even heard the Dharma spoken, their own hearts are, of themselves, delighted and subdued already.

Sūtra: "Such people declare that the Buddha's own nirvāṇa, bodhi, and dharmakāya are present right here, in this very body of flesh — that father begetting son, and son begetting son again, generation after generation, is itself this same dharmakāya, ever-abiding without cessation, pointing to this present world itself as the very buddha-land, with no separate pure abode or golden-hued form to seek elsewhere. This person, believing and accepting this, loses their own prior mind entirely, offering up their very body and life in reliance upon it, gaining what they had never experienced before. Such people, foolish and confused, mistake this teacher for a bodhisattva, and, their own mind drawn into this same inquiry, break the Buddha's own monastic discipline, and secretly indulge in desire."

Commentary: This makes plain how master and companion alike are equally misled: taking the Buddha's own three kinds of nirvāṇa — specifically, the nirvāṇa of pure nature — taking the true nature of the three kinds of bodhi as this same nirvāṇa, taking the merit of the dharma-body among the three virtues as this same nirvāṇa, and declaring it present right here, in this very body of flesh, father begetting son and son begetting son again, generation after generation, taken as this very root, and this taken to be the dharma-body itself, ever-abiding without cessation — this nature, neither arising nor ceasing, never once apart from what does arise and cease, pointing to this present world itself, taken to be the buddha-land, defilement itself taken to be purity itself, with no separate pure abode or golden-hued form to seek elsewhere — the ordinary itself taken to be the sagely itself.

"This person" refers to the one seeking out this very root, who, believing this deviant teacher, receiving this demonic teaching, loses the very mind they had originally cultivated, and offers up their entire body and life in reliance upon it — taking this base, contemptible matter of bodily procreation as the very root of transformation itself, gaining what they had never experienced before. Such people, foolish and confused, mistake this teacher for a bodhisattva, and, investigating what this teacher's own mind delights in, comply with it in every respect, taking entanglement itself for liberation, breaking the precepts the Buddha established, taking lustful attachment itself for buddha-nature, secretly indulging in desire.

Sūtra: "This mouth loves to speak of the eye, ear, nose, and tongue as themselves the pure land, and of the male and female organs as themselves the true place of bodhi and nirvāṇa — and those without any true understanding believe these filthy words."

Commentary: This mouth loves to speak of the eye, ear, nose, and tongue as themselves the pure land — were this spoken as a provisional teaching's antidote, taking hold of the faculty's own nature, this might, in principle, be acceptable; but to speak this way constantly and exclusively is precisely a non-Buddhist wrong view — how much more so, then, to take the male and female organs, this very root of filth and impurity, as the very root of all transformation, the true place of bodhi and nirvāṇa, profaning the Buddhadharma and throwing the true principle into utter confusion. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: the whole intent here is nothing but enticing people to indulge in lust unchecked and break the precepts, destroying this great samādhi entirely. Those without any true understanding mostly believe these filthy words, and sink down into the demon's own ranks.

Sūtra: "This is named a venom-gu, hex-casting evil ghost, grown old and become a demon."

Commentary: A venom-gu ghost is one that takes on form by encountering some poisonous substance; a hex-casting ghost is one that takes on form by encountering some hidden, obscure influence — the world has an art of hexing and casting spells said to overpower its target, hence also called a "hex-casting" ghost. This closes the fourth state, craving to trace things to their root.

5. Craving Hidden, Responsive Connection 貪求冥感 — This good person's mind comes to love a distant, responsive connection, researching this precisely, in every direction, craving hidden correspondence. A heavenly demon finds its opening and sends its spirit to possess a nearby person, who declares attainment of unsurpassed nirvāṇa and expounds the Dharma, causing listeners to see this person as though they had lived for a hundred or a thousand years, giving rise to a love and attachment they cannot bear to part with, serving this person as a servant, offering the four kinds of support without ever feeling weary — each in the assembly believing this to be their own teacher from a past life, gaining what they had never experienced before.

Commentary: This is the fifth state, craving hidden, responsive connection. The first four clauses match what came before. "Their mind comes to love a distant, responsive connection" — "distant" here means far removed, referring specifically to those sages, connected to oneself across many lifetimes and distant kalpas, who might respond to one's own seeking. "Researching this precisely, in every direction, craving hidden correspondence" means: with a single mind, moving through and researching this precisely in every direction, craving this hidden accord, hoping thereby to move and attain some sagely response.

The moment even a single thought of this craving arises, this settled mind is no longer dense and secure; and so this heavenly demon, watching for its opening, finding a gap it can exploit, a means by which it can ensnare, sends its own spirit flying to possess this person, speaking the sūtras and their teaching, in order to answer this very longing. "This person," the one possessed by the demon, originally unaware that they are possessed by this demon, likewise declares that they have personally realized this wondrous fruit of unsurpassed nirvāṇa, and comes to where this good person seeking such a response dwells, setting up their own seat to expound this teaching of hidden, responsive connection.

What follows falsely presents this matter of hidden, responsive connection: for a time, this possessed person is seen — white-haired yet with a youthful face, appearing exactly as though deeply cultivated and long realized, as though a hundred or a thousand years old — and a mind of love and attachment arises, unable to bear parting with them, serving even as their own servant, offering the four kinds of support without ever feeling weary or fatigued. "Each" refers to those gathered under the demon's own sway; "in the assembly" refers to this same following — their own hearts knowing, or so they believe, that this possessed person was their own teacher from a former life, their own original spiritual guide, and so give rise to a separate kind of attachment to this teaching, sticking fast like glue or lacquer, gaining what they had never experienced before.

Sūtra: "This mouth loves to say, 'In a former life, in a certain birth, I liberated a certain person, who was, at that time, my own wife, concubine, or sibling; and now I have come once more to liberate you, that you might accompany me to a certain world, to make offerings to a certain buddha.' Or it speaks of a further heaven of great light, where a buddha dwells, the very resting place of every Tathāgata — and those without any true understanding believe these false, deceptive words, and lose their own original mind entirely."

Commentary: This person, foolish and lacking any wisdom, mistakes this teacher for a bodhisattva, draws their own mind close to them, tinged and stained more deeply by this teaching day by day, believes this deviant teaching, breaks the precepts the Buddha established, learns this deviant conduct, and secretly indulges in desire. This mouth loves to say: "In my former life, in a certain birth, I liberated a certain person, who was, at that time, my own wife, concubine, or sibling" — falsely recounting some matter of the past, plainly to entice and draw the listener in through desire itself. "Now I and you" continues, likewise, this same karmic bond from a former life, come specially to liberate you once more, to accompany me to a certain world, to make offerings to a certain buddha — this according with this listener's own longing for such a distant, responsive connection. "Or it speaks of a further heaven of great light" and what follows falsely points to the summit-heaven of the desire realm, the demon's own palace there, calling it "the heaven of great light," and falsely calling the demon-king himself a buddha, dwelling there, this being the very resting place of every Tathāgata. Those without any true understanding believe these false, deceptive words, and lose the very mind they had originally cultivated, submitting instead to this demonic teaching. A plague ghost is precisely a pestilence-ghost that takes on form by encountering decline. This closes the fifth state, craving hidden, responsive connection.

6. Craving Deep Seclusion and Stillness 貪求靜謐 — This good person's mind comes to love penetrating deeply, disciplining themselves through hardship and toil, delighting in dwelling in some hidden, secluded place, craving perfect quiet and stillness. A heavenly demon finds its opening and sends its spirit to possess a nearby person, who declares attainment of unsurpassed nirvāṇa, expounds the Dharma, and causes each listener to know their own past karma — telling one, for instance, "you are not yet dead, yet have already become an animal," and having another tread on their tail from behind, so that they find themselves unable to rise at all, the whole assembly thereby won over entirely.

Commentary: This is the sixth state, craving deep seclusion and stillness. The first four clauses match what came before. "Their mind comes to love penetrating deeply" means: within this state of samādhi, the mind comes to love penetrating deeply into union with it, disciplining oneself without regard for hardship or toil, hoping only to penetrate deeply into perfect, penetrating samādhi, delighting in dwelling in some hidden, secluded, solitary place, seeking this cultivation of peace, quiet, and stillness. Little do they know that, within this samādhi, no thought whatsoever ought to be allowed to stir at all — the moment craving enters in, the demon finds its opening, sending its own spirit secretly to possess another person, speaking the sūtras and their teaching. "This person" refers to the one possessed by the demon, originally unaware that they are possessed by this demon, who likewise declares that they have personally realized unsurpassed nirvāṇa, and comes to where this good person seeking such seclusion dwells, setting up their own seat to expound this teaching of perfect penetration.

What follows first presents this deviant, misleading matter: causing every listener to know their own past karma — this being karma from former lives, showing penetration into the past. "Or, in this very place" refers to the place of this teaching, where they say specifically to one person, "you are not yet dead now, yet have already turned into an animal" — and, fearing this person's own heart might not submit, they order another to tread on that person's tail from behind, so that, through the demon's own sustaining power, this person finds themselves suddenly unable to rise at all — and, this proof thus demonstrated, the whole assembled audience gives their hearts over entirely, in reverent submission — this showing penetration into the future. Should someone, further, give rise to a thought, the demon already knows precisely where this thought began — "began" meaning its very starting point — this showing penetration into the present. The demon's own intent is to seem to penetrate all three periods of time at once, as though this were the very supreme function of perfect penetration itself; little do they know that the true, supreme function of perfect penetration is, in truth, far more than this alone.

"Beyond the discipline the Buddha himself established, piling on further austerities" refers to such things as abstaining from every ordinary food, going naked, plucking out their own hair, smoking their own nostrils, lying in ashes or upon thorns — deliberately performing bizarre, unusual conduct, so as to startle the world. "Denouncing monastics" means reproaching them for being unable to endure such austerities themselves. "Reviling their own followers" shows they harbor no private favoritism. "Exposing others' private affairs" means attacking and revealing others' hidden, private matters. "Not avoiding reproach or suspicion" shows their own apparent forthright honesty, concealing nothing. "This mouth loves to speak of calamity and fortune not yet come to pass" describes this deviant, misleading speech further still: this mouth constantly loves proclaiming matters of future calamity and fortune, and, when the time finally arrives, every single prediction proves accurate down to the very last detail — again, a deliberately bizarre, unusual claim, meant to dazzle the world. A being of great power is a ghost possessing great spiritual power, skilled at misleading people — the rest may be understood accordingly. This closes the sixth state, craving deep seclusion and stillness.

7. Craving Knowledge of Past Lives 貪求宿命 — This good person's mind comes to love knowledge and insight, laboring hard in research and inquiry, craving knowledge of past lives. A heavenly demon finds its opening and sends its spirit to possess a nearby person, who declares attainment of unsurpassed nirvāṇa and expounds the Dharma. This person may, for no evident reason, obtain some great, precious pearl at the very place of this teaching — the demon itself, at times, transforming into an animal, carrying this pearl and various other treasures, tablets, talismans, and strange objects in its mouth, bestowing these first upon this listener before finally taking possession of their body.

Commentary: This is the seventh state, craving knowledge of past lives. The first four clauses match what came before. "Their mind comes to love knowledge and insight" means: what ordinary people cannot know, they wish to know; what ordinary people cannot see, they wish to see — and so, sparing no hardship or toil, they research and inquire deeply, craving this knowledge of past lives.

"This person" refers to the one possessed by the demon; this first presents this deviant, misleading matter. "For no evident reason" means: without any apparent cause, obtaining, at this very place of teaching, a great, precious pearl, taken to be an auspicious sign; and, at times, this demon itself transforms into an animal, carrying this pearl in its mouth, together with various colored treasures — vessels such as a jeweled seal or a jeweled vase — and tablets and talismans — bamboo strips called tablets, leather-bound sheets called registers, carved bamboo called talismans, wooden strips called tallies, a talisman serving as a token of trust, made, under Han-dynasty regulation, of bamboo six inches long, carved with an agreed mark within it, each of two people holding half, matched together to confirm mutual trust — and various other strange objects, such as a dragon-light treasure-mirror and the like — bestowing these first upon this listener before finally taking possession of their very body: this plainly the demon's own doing. Or it entices listeners to dig beneath the earth, where a bright pearl illuminates the very spot — every listener personally witnessing this, believing what is said, gaining what they had never experienced before.

Sūtra: "They eat mostly medicinal herbs rather than fine food, sometimes eating but a single grain of sesame or wheat in a day, yet their own form remains full and robust, sustained by the demon's own power; they denounce monastics, revile their own followers, and do not avoid reproach or suspicion. This mouth loves to speak of treasures hidden in distant lands, or of the hidden dwelling places of every sage and worthy of the ten directions, and those who follow after such a person often encounter strange, unusual people."

Commentary: "Eating mostly medicinal herbs rather than fine food" means: avoiding cooked food, eating mostly medicinal herbs such as Solomon's seal or calamus root; or, at times, eating but a single grain of sesame or wheat in a day, their own form remaining full and robust, their blood and vital energy sustained entirely by the demon's own power. Denouncing monastics for not cultivating austerity themselves, reviling their own followers, spending their days in idle satiety. This further recounts more deviant, misleading speech: this mouth constantly loves speaking of hidden treasures in distant lands — misleading through worldly benefit; and of the hidden, secret dwelling places of every sage and worthy of the ten directions — misleading through transcendent benefit. Those who follow after such a person often encounter strange, unusual people — all such matters being of this same hidden, secluded kind, resembling closely this same craving for seclusion and stillness above.

"Mountain forest, land, city moat, river, or mountain" refers to the spirits presiding over these: mountain spirits, forest spirits, local land-spirits, city guardian-spirits — rivers meaning the four great watercourses, mountains meaning the five great peaks, each holding its own particular authority, spirits of blessed merit. "Grown old and become a demon" means that, over a long span of years, the demon-king enrolls it into his own service, hence "becoming a demon." As the Zhizhang Commentary explains this passage on possessing a spirit to mislead people, in three general ways: sometimes openly proclaiming lewdness, breaking the Buddha's own pure precepts, secretly indulging in the five worldly desires together with their own attending disciples — breaking the Dharma of the precepts through desire itself; sometimes practicing some fruitless austerity, foolishly and blindly cultivating, eating nothing but grass and wood exclusively, and inducing others to imitate this same conduct — breaking the Dharma of wisdom through mere foolishness; sometimes given repeatedly to anger and delight, diligence and idleness, faith and doubt, with no fixed pattern of conduct at all — breaking the Dharma of samādhi through this very disorder, simply tormenting the practitioner throughout. This closes the seventh state, craving knowledge of past lives.

8. Craving Spiritual Powers 貪取神力 — This good person's mind comes to love spiritual powers, every kind of transformation, researching this very origin of transformation, craving to seize this spiritual power for themselves. A heavenly demon finds its opening and sends its spirit to possess a nearby person, who declares attainment of unsurpassed nirvāṇa and expounds the Dharma. This person may hold flame in hand, scooping this light and distributing it upon the heads of every listener present, so that every listener's own head bears a flame several feet tall, giving off no heat at all and never burning them; or walk upon water as though upon solid ground; or sit motionless, suspended in mid-air; or enter into a jar, or hide within a sack, passing through window and wall alike without any obstruction whatsoever — only unable to remain unharmed by blade or weapon.

Commentary: This is the eighth state, craving spiritual powers. The first four clauses match what came before. Within this samādhi, the mind comes to love a bodhisattva's own spiritual powers, wondrous beyond all reckoning, penetrating everywhere without obstruction, every kind of transformation — and so researches this very origin of transformation, craving to attain this spiritual power for themselves. As Wenling puts it: this origin of transformation is the very root of every transformation — one wishes to ride upon it, to give rise to spiritual transformations of one's own. The very moment this mind is roused to research it, one has already fallen into deviant thought; the very moment the mind is bent on seizing it, this already amounts to contrived, artificial action — how, then, could the heavenly demon fail to find its opening here?

"This person holding flame in hand" and what follows first present this deviant, misleading matter: mere words alone would not be sufficient to inspire belief, and so this person specially manifests every kind of spiritual transformation. This person may hold flame in hand, scooping this light and distributing it upon the heads of every listener present, so that every listener's own head bears a flame several feet tall, without heat, without burning. Or they walk upon water as though treading solid ground — here, both water and fire alike brought under their own control. Or they sit motionless, suspended in mid-air, as though emptiness and form had become one and the same; or enter into a jar, or hide within a sack, matching, however small the space, their own size to fit it; or a window, though closed, can be passed through, or a wall, though separating, can be penetrated, without any obstruction whatsoever — this, too, a spiritual, extraordinary matter. Only regarding blade and weapon do they remain unable to remain unharmed — since the demon has not yet departed from desire, this view of a fixed self still persisting within it, and so it cannot make itself immune to a blade's touch, unlike the wind blowing against light, or the blade cutting through water.

Sūtra: "They declare themselves to be a buddha, though wearing the white robes of a layperson, receiving the bows even of monastics; they denounce meditation practice and the monastic discipline alike, revile their own followers, expose others' private affairs, and do not avoid reproach or suspicion. Their mouth constantly speaks of spiritual freedom and power, or causes others to glimpse, from the side, a buddha-land — this being nothing but a ghost's own power misleading people, with nothing real about it whatsoever. They praise the practice of lust openly, do not condemn coarse conduct, and take every filthy, obscene act to be the very transmission of the Dharma itself."

Commentary: They declare themselves a buddha — the buddha being the very utmost of sagehood — and yet dare falsely to claim this title for themselves, though wearing the white robes of a layperson, receiving even monastics' own bows of reverence. Denouncing meditation practice and the monastic discipline means: slandering the Chan tradition as mere idle sitting, wild investigation, presuming falsely to have transcended birth and death all at once; slandering the study of the monastic discipline as mere bodily restraint through the precepts, never, in the end, transcending the lesser vehicle. Among Chan, doctrinal study, and monastic discipline, only the teaching-tradition is not slandered — since they themselves set up their own seat to expound the Dharma, falsely claiming their own words to be the Buddha's own words, worthy of everyone's own devotion. To claim buddhahood for oneself destroys the Buddha-jewel; to denounce Chan and the precepts destroys the Dharma-jewel; to receive monastics' own bows destroys the Saṅgha-jewel. Believing this deviant teaching cuts off the very seed of the Three Jewels, and plants, instead, the seeds of hell! Reviling one's own followers displays an apparent absence of favoritism; exposing others' private affairs displays an apparent uprightness; not avoiding reproach or suspicion displays an apparent frank honesty, unafraid to expose others' private matters, mockery, and resentment alike.

This mouth constantly proclaims spiritual freedom and power, without any obstruction whatsoever, so as to console this very craving mind. And causing others to glimpse, from the side, a buddha-land, as proof of their own buddhahood — all of this nothing but a ghost's own power seeking to mislead people, deliberately manifesting falsely, with nothing real about it whatsoever. "Praising the practice of lust" means declaring the union of man and woman to be the dharma-body itself, ever-abiding without cessation; and, moreover, never condemning coarse conduct, taking every filthy, obscene, unclean act to be the very transmission of the Dharma, that the seed of buddhahood might never be cut off. This takes the very seed of hell to be the seed of buddhahood — how truly lamentable!

"The great power of heaven and earth" refers, together, to the five kinds of nature-spirit that follow — since these are the great, powerful spirits of heaven and earth itself. Each of these five kinds of spirit holds its own particular jurisdiction, its own sovereign authority, some upright and some deviant: the upright are called deities, the deviant are called nature-spirits, since they are able to divide authority with the true deities, their power being the very greatest. Every strange plant and unusual tree, receiving the very refined essence of heaven and earth, stealing the very quintessence of sun and moon, accumulated over kalpas, becomes such a nature-spirit turned demon. "Or a dragon-spirit" refers to such creatures as the dragon guarding some heavenly palace, or the dragon guarding some buried treasure, stealing heaven's own numinous power, stealing an object's own essence, and thereby becoming such an uncanny spirit. "Or an immortal, life ended, revived to become such a spirit" refers to one who, through visualization practice, sought to solidify their own bodily form, originally hoping for eternal life without death — yet, unaware that even an immortal's own lifespan has its final end, is revived instead as such a spirit. "Or one whose allotted years as an immortal have come to an end, their years reckoned and death due, yet whose body has not decayed" refers to one who, cultivating immortality, smelting lead and refining mercury, preparing and nourishing cinnabar, ought to have enjoyed a very long lifespan, yet, this immortal span having come to its end, their years reckoned and death due, their spirit and consciousness unable to remain, their bodily form not yet decayed, is possessed instead by some other strange being — how regrettable, that their own past merit and practice should, instead, become fuel for this very demonic affliction! Those who, rather than relying on right awakening, cultivating this samādhi, engage instead in visualization to solidify their own bodily form, ought to know to reflect on this most urgently. This closes the eighth state, craving spiritual powers.

9. Craving Deep Emptiness 貪求深空 — This good person's mind comes to love entering cessation, researching the very nature of transformation, craving profound emptiness. A heavenly demon finds its opening and sends its spirit to possess a nearby person, who declares attainment of unsurpassed nirvāṇa and expounds the Dharma; within the very midst of the assembly, this person's own form may suddenly vanish, unseen by anyone, then reappear, bursting forth once more from empty space, coming and going entirely as they wish.

Commentary: This is the ninth state, craving deep emptiness. The first four clauses match what came before. Within this samādhi, the mind comes to love entering cessation — as the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it, because they do not realize that every phenomenon's own mark is, from the very outset, already quiescent and extinct of itself, requiring no further extinguishing at all, and so, for no evident reason, they wish, instead, to force this phenomenon of form into this markless emptiness — not the same as the lesser vehicle's own extinguishing of form to return to emptiness. "Researching the very nature of transformation, craving profound emptiness" means: researching the very substance and nature of every transformation — that is, precisely the very root of the aggregate of perception, this subtle, fine mark of movement — refining this research to its utmost, hoping thereby to exhaust it entirely, all for the sake of craving this profound emptiness. To speak of this "profound" emptiness means not merely that both body and world alike become empty, but, further, that coming and going becomes entirely free as well.

This single thought of craving alone is already enough to show that this settled mind is no longer dense and secure, and so it stands entirely to reason that the heavenly demon should find an opening here to exploit, sending its own spirit to possess this person and cause confusion. "This person," down through "sets up their own seat and expounds the Dharma," may be understood following the pattern above. "Within the assembly, their own form may suddenly vanish" first presents this deviant, misleading matter: though speaking of this teaching of emptiness, fearing this may still not inspire belief, they specially manifest this proof, so as to make this faith firm and secure. Precisely within the assembly, this bodily form suddenly becomes empty, unseen by anyone — showing precisely that existence itself is emptiness; and precisely at the moment this form is empty, unseen, it bursts forth once more from empty space — showing precisely that emptiness itself is existence. Coming and going in this way, alike attaining this same freedom, this bears some resemblance to wondrous existence and true emptiness, true emptiness and wondrous existence — yet this is merely the demon's own power falsely manifesting this, so as to satisfy the mind of one craving this profound emptiness.

Sūtra: "Or their own body appears transparent, like lapis lazuli; or their hands and feet give off the fragrance of sandalwood; or their excrement and urine taste like thick, refined honey. They denounce the monastic precepts, and hold monastic life in contempt. Their mouth constantly declares that there is no cause and no effect, that a single death is final and permanent extinction, with no further body to follow, and no distinction whatsoever between the ordinary and the sagely. Though claiming to have attained this empty stillness, they secretly indulge in desire, and those who receive this desire from them likewise attain this same empty mind, denying cause and effect altogether."

Commentary: "Or their own body appears transparent, like lapis lazuli" means: a body ordinarily composed of every kind of filth is made to appear translucent, radiant, like the lapis-lazuli treasure itself. "Or their hands and feet give off the fragrance of sandalwood" means: hands and feet, ordinarily unclean and defiled, are made to give off the very fragrance of sandalwood instead. "Or their excrement and urine taste like thick, refined honey" means: excrement and urine, ordinarily foul and unbearable, are made to taste as sweet as thick, refined honey — refined honey being rock candy — showing precisely that defilement itself is purity itself. All of this manifested within their own present body, dazzling and misleading the crowd. "Denouncing the precepts" means slandering the keeping of precepts as belonging to the lesser vehicle, a mere self-imposed restraint; "holding monastic life in contempt" means, having failed to attain emptiness of body themselves, presuming to have found liberation on their own terms instead.

This further recounts more deviant, misleading speech, meant to dazzle the world. This mouth constantly loves declaring that there is no cause and no effect, that a single death is final and permanent extinction — exactly as King Prasenajit, upon meeting Kātyāyana and Vairaṭīputra, was told that this very body, after death, undergoes utter cessation — precisely this same view. "No further body to follow" means: a single death, final and permanent extinction, with no further casting off of one life to take up another, no further receiving of any retribution of suffering or bliss after death; and every distinction between the six ordinary destinies and the four sagely ones, between confusion and awakening, is likewise nonexistent — even a sage's own cultivation and realization is held to be nothing at all. Taking this annihilation itself for the attainment of empty stillness, taking the practice of desire itself for freedom from all obstruction, hence, "though claiming to have attained this empty stillness, they secretly indulge in desire." Those who receive this desire from them are called "sons of the Dharma," transmitted this same teaching of annihilation, likewise attaining this same "empty mind," denying cause and effect altogether, giving rise to this great wrong view. As Yongjia has said: "boundless emptiness, denying cause and effect — reckless and unrestrained, this invites calamity and misfortune" — how could this not be feared!

Sūtra: "This is named a spirit born of the eclipsing essence of sun and moon, or of gold, jade, magic fungus, unicorn, phoenix, tortoise, or crane, surviving for thousands upon thousands of years without dying, becoming numinous, born into this land, grown old and become a demon."

Commentary: As the Zhizhang Commentary explains: "the eclipsing of sun and moon" — dark vapor pressing close together is called eclipsing; obscuring this refined radiance is called an eclipse — precisely what is meant today by a solar or lunar eclipse. Now, the sun is the very essence of yang, and the moon the very essence of yin, each possessing its own radiant vapor, pervading and dispersing everywhere; yet, at the very moment of eclipse, unable to disperse as usual, this vapor pours straight down into the earth instead. The gold, jade, magic fungus, unicorn, phoenix, tortoise, and crane upon the earth, receiving this vapor, are thereby able to live long, and to nurture their own refined essence — hence, "surviving for thousands upon thousands of years without dying, becoming numinous." "Born into this land" refers to becoming an immortal-object, an immortal-bird, or an immortal-beast, differing from the "nature-spirit accumulated over kalpas" spoken of earlier. This closes the ninth state, craving deep emptiness.

10. Craving Long Life 貪求永歲 — This good person's mind comes to love long life, laboring hard through toil and hardship to research this most subtle, imperceptible point, craving eternal years, giving up ordinary, fragmentary birth-and-death in the hope of suddenly attaining a transformational birth-and-death instead, this subtle mark made ever-abiding. A heavenly demon finds its opening and sends its spirit to possess a nearby person, who declares attainment of unsurpassed nirvāṇa and expounds the Dharma, loving to speak of traveling to distant lands and returning without any hindrance, covering ten thousand miles and returning again in the space of a single breath, bringing back some object from that place each time as proof.

Commentary: This is the tenth state, craving long life. The first four clauses match what came before. Within this samādhi, the mind comes to love long life, laboring through hardship and toil to research this most subtle, imperceptible mark of movement — that is, precisely the very root of the aggregate of perception itself. As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: this subtle, fine mark of movement cannot be researched through any deliberate effort of mind at all — one need only rely on this fundamental practice, and, as samādhi deepens, it dissolves of itself, exactly like muddied water, set to rest within a still vessel: once stilled deeply enough, unmoving, the sediment settles of its own accord. Now, to research this most subtle point through such hard, toilsome effort is already a miscalculation; and, further, to crave eternal years, giving up ordinary, fragmentary birth-and-death, hoping suddenly for a transformational birth-and-death instead, hoping for this subtle mark to become ever-abiding — this is nothing but compounding false thought upon false thought.

Fragmentary birth-and-death refers to birth-and-death still possessing form, within the three realms — beings of the six destinies, dependent on this same bodily root, having a fixed span, long or short, to their lifespan, a fixed measure, large or small, to their bodily substance — hence called "fragmentary." Transformational birth-and-death refers to birth-and-death without form, beyond the three realms — noble ones of the three vehicles, having entirely cut off the delusion of view and thought found within the three realms, born instead into the very land of the dharma-nature, and so undergoing this transformational birth-and-death instead, nothing more than this thought itself shifting and changing moment to moment. Now, giving up this fragmentary birth-and-death, hoping suddenly for this transformational birth-and-death instead, hoping thereby to attain this subtle mark, ever-abiding.

"Loving to speak of distant lands" and what follows first presents this deviant, misleading matter: traveling to lands ten thousand miles distant, going and coming without any hindrance or obstruction at all; "the space of a single breath" describes the very shortness of this time, within which one may return once more; bringing back some object from that place each time, as proof. This is precisely the power of unimpeded bodily movement, among the five spiritual powers, brought about through the demon's own power, turning the very farthest distance into the very nearest. In the past, the Daoist Xue Daoguang cultivated nature without cultivating life-force, and so was able only to send forth his own yin-spirit, unable to fetch objects from distant places; Zhang Ziyang cultivated both nature and life-force together, and so was able to send forth his own yang-spirit, and, moreover, was able to fetch objects from distant places as well. Seen in this light, even the ability to fetch objects from distant places never goes beyond this same heavenly demon's own bag of tricks — how, then, could this ever be compared, in depth, to the Lotus Sūtra's own samādhi of manifesting one's own body universally, or to this sūtra's own supreme, wondrous function of perfect penetration?

Sūtra: "Or, within a single place, a single dwelling, over the span of only a few steps, they cause a listener attempting to walk from the east wall to the west wall, however urgently they hurry, to find themselves unable to cross this distance even after years of trying. Because of this, this listener's own mind comes to believe that the Buddha himself now stands present before them."

Commentary: Or, within a single place, a single dwelling, over what ought to be no more than a few steps, this measure being, in truth, exceedingly close, they cause a listener attempting to walk from the east wall to the west wall, however urgently they hurry, to find themselves unable to cross this distance even after years of trying. Yet, should this same clarity be applied to themselves, they can make what is far seem near; while, applied to another, they can make what is near seem far instead — this shrinking or expanding of space, this lengthening or shortening of time, being nothing but the demon's own false manifestation, meant to confuse and disorder people's own minds. Because of this, this listener's own mind comes to believe, mistakenly, that the Buddha himself now stands present before them.

Sūtra: "Their mouth constantly declares that every sentient being of the ten directions is their own child, that they have given birth to every buddha, that they themselves brought forth this world, that they are the primordial buddha itself, appearing in the world spontaneously, not through any cultivation at all."

Commentary: This further gives voice to more deviant, misleading speech. "Every sentient being of the ten directions is my own child" shows that they existed before any sentient being ever did; "I have given birth to every buddha" shows that they existed before any buddha was ever born; "I myself brought forth this world" shows that they existed before this world was ever brought forth — that is, both dependent and direct retribution, every buddha and every sentient being, and "I" am the primordial buddha behind them all. Being, then, this very root, primordial buddha, still present even now, their own lifespan, then, must exceed every other's entirely — this catering precisely to this listener's own original longing. "Appearing in the world spontaneously, not through any cultivation at all" precisely shows that all cultivation elsewhere is useless, denying the very matter of cultivation and realization altogether.

Sūtra: "This is named a heavenly demon abiding at ease within the world, who deploys his own retinue — such as Pañcikā, or the youthful spirits under the Four Heavenly Kings not yet resolved on bodhi — to exploit this practitioner's own empty clarity, and feed upon their vital essence; or, without relying on any such intermediary teacher, this practitioner may personally behold, with their own eyes, this being declaring itself Vajrapāṇi, promising them long life; presenting itself, further, as a beautiful woman, indulging widely in desire with them — and, before even a year has passed, their own liver and brain run dry, this person speaking as though alone, their words sounding, to any listener, exactly like the moaning of one possessed."

Commentary: A heavenly demon abiding at ease within the world refers precisely to a further demon-king dwelling above the sixth heaven of the desire realm, likewise belonging to the heaven of mastery over others' transformations. "Deploys his own retinue" refers to such things as the spirit sent flying to possess someone, described earlier. Pañcikā, translated "servant spirit," is precisely a ghost put to service, also called "the jealous woman." The youthful spirits of Vaiśravaṇa, precisely the piśāca ghosts, translated "devourers of vital essence," all subject to the jurisdiction of the Four Heavenly Kings — those who have already resolved on bodhi protect people, while those not yet resolved on bodhi harm them, seeking benefit from this practitioner's own settled, empty clarity, drawing forth deviant wisdom from it, feeding upon their vital essence, nourishing thereby their own demonic form.

Commentary: This passage, taken as a whole, makes plain the aggregate of formations' own boundary from beginning to end. "This good person" gathers together those who have passed through the ten states of the aggregate of perception described earlier — whether, throughout, never once giving rise to any craving at all, this nature of samādhi remaining constant; or, whenever a demon came, always able to recognize it, never letting it damage their own fundamental practice. Applying their mind in this way, the aggregate of perception breaks through, and, continuing still to refine this research further, this is called "cultivating this samādhi" — with respect to perception, marking the end of that practice, and, with respect to formations, marking the beginning of this next one; this samādhi advancing further still, stirring thought exhausted, floating thought dissolved away, this is called the exhaustion of the aggregate of perception.

"Their ordinary dreaming thoughts dissolve away": as a later passage states, waking is precisely this thinking mind, and sleep gives rise to every dream — now, with neither dream nor thought present at all, waking, too, is free of thought, constantly the same as this same stillness found in sleep; and sleep, too, is free of dreaming, constantly the same as this same awareness found while waking — hence, "waking and sleeping become exactly the same state." "Awakened brightness becomes empty and still" means: this original, wondrous, bright, true mind of awakening, free of every disturbance from dream and thought alike, attains this same purity, emptiness, and stillness! Exactly like a clear, bright sky, utterly without anything within it at all. "No more coarse shadow-images of past objects remaining" means: this coarse, heavy dust-image is precisely a mental object, entirely dependent on the aggregate of perception for its very substance; thought exhausted, this dust-image has nothing left to depend upon — hence, "no more" and so on. Everything up to this point concerns the wondrous quality of the mind's own substance — this "isolated shadow" now, at last, empty.

"Observing" onward concerns the wondrous quality of the mind's own illumination of its objects — this "objective realm" now, too, empty. The mountains, rivers, and great earth of this world are this objective realm; now, this mind illuminating these mountains and rivers becomes exactly like a bright mirror's own thoroughgoing clarity, manifesting according to whatever object appears, without the slightest trace of analysis at all. And so, when an object comes, its reflection appears within the mirror, yet the mirror clings to nothing at all; when an object passes, its reflection vanishes from within the mirror, yet the mirror, too, retains no trace of it at all. Based on this, then, within this objective realm and this consciousness, there is nothing more than an entirely empty receiving and responding. Once the object is gone, within this consciousness, everything is utterly clear, with no trace of any old habitual tendency left to speak of at all — "old" here meaning former, that is, this habitual tendency accumulated in the past. "Only a single, essential truth remains" means only this single essence of consciousness, this true substance — precisely this eighth, root consciousness itself — not yet the final, ultimate truth.

Sūtra: "And it is from this point that the very root of arising and ceasing itself is finally laid bare. One beholds, throughout the ten directions, every one of the twelve kinds of sentient being, their every category exhausted entirely; though not yet penetrating each one's own individual thread of destiny, one sees this same foundation of birth shared by all, exactly like heat-shimmer flickering over open country, this fine, restless glimmer serving as the very hinge underlying every floating sense-faculty and sense-object. This is called the boundary of the aggregate of formations."

Commentary: "The very root of arising and ceasing" refers to the very root cause underlying every instance of arising and ceasing — that is, precisely the seeds of the seventh consciousness contained within the eighth consciousness; since this subtle, fine arising and ceasing is present, it serves as the very root of the aggregate of formations, every instance of arising and ceasing flowing out from it. Formerly, covered by the aggregate of perception, this could not be seen; now, with the aggregate of perception's own stirring thought exhausted, this very root of arising and ceasing belonging to the aggregate of formations is, from this point, laid bare and revealed. And so one comes to behold, throughout the ten directions, the arising and ceasing of every one of the twelve kinds of sentient being, their every category exhausted entirely — "exhausted" here meaning entirely complete.

"Each one's own individual thread of destiny" belongs to the aggregate of consciousness; "this same foundation of birth shared by all" belongs to the aggregate of formations. Now, still covered by the aggregate of formations, one has not yet penetrated the individual thread of destiny behind each of these twelve kinds of birth — hence, "though not yet" — and yet one does see these seeds of the seventh consciousness, this same foundation of birth shared by all, exactly like heat-shimmer flickering, this fine, restless glimmer. Heat-shimmer is not dust in the air, but rather a drifting vapor over open fields, visible, on a clear spring day, hovering close to the ground — its form resembling water, its glimmer resembling flame. Zhuangzi calls this "wild horses"; Buddhist sūtras mostly call it "heat-shimmer" — a thirsty deer chasing after this heat-shimmer, seeing it resemble water from a distance, only to find nothing there upon drawing near. This same aggregate, discussed earlier in fascicle 2, within the section on the five aggregates, was there described, with respect to the position of delusion, taking its coarser mark, likened to a cascading torrent; here, though, it is described with respect to the position of cultivation, taking its subtler mark, likened instead to this same heat-shimmer — used as a metaphor for the aggregate of formations' own hidden, subtle mark of movement. "Fine, restless glimmer" describes a slight brightness, used as a metaphor for this mark of movement within the aggregate of formations, suddenly arising, suddenly vanishing.

"Restless" here distinguishes this from what comes before and after: this restlessness is a light, subtle restlessness, not the same as the heavy turbidity of the aggregate before it; and this restlessness is a stirring restlessness, not the same as the settled clarity of the aggregate after it. "The very hinge underlying every floating sense-faculty and sense-object" means: the floating faculties and their four kinds of object are precisely a sentient being's own bodily faculties; the twelve kinds of birth each differ from one another, and yet the very mechanism governing their ultimate transformation lies, in every case, within the aggregate of formations alone. A "hinge" is the axle of a door; a "socket" is the door's own pivot-hole — because of this hinge and socket, a door is able to open and close; because of this aggregate of formations, the sense-faculties and their objects are able to arise and cease. So long as this mark remains unexhausted, birth and death are difficult to escape — and this is called the boundary of the aggregate of formations. This closes the first section, the boundary not yet broken through, at the very start of practice.

Sūtra: "Once this restless, flickering original nature settles into its own original stillness, and that stillness absorbs its own original habitual tendency, like waves subsiding into still water, this is called the exhaustion of the aggregate of formations, and such a person can transcend the turbidity of sentient existence itself. Examining its cause, this aggregate is rooted in hidden, deeply concealed false thought."

Commentary: Should this light, subtle, restless, flickering original substance of arising and ceasing, through the power of samādhi growing ever deeper, settle, at last, into its own original, clear stillness — precisely what a later passage means by "this place where all is clear and unmoving" — "original nature" refers to this original substance of arising and ceasing, precisely the seventh consciousness; "original stillness" refers to the settling of waves, precisely the eighth consciousness. This settling of this original habitual tendency is precisely the seventh consciousness's own aggregate of formations; once both seed and habitual tendency alike are exhausted, one returns, once more, to this same essential, luminous substance of consciousness — hence, "like waves subsiding into still water."

Since the aggregate of perception is like great, crashing waves, and the aggregate of formations like fine ripples, and the aggregate of consciousness like flowing water free of any waves at all, while this true, awakened substance itself is like a settled, motionless pool — now, transformed into this settled water, this is called the exhaustion of the aggregate of formations. As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: according to the teaching of perfect penetration through the ear-faculty, this corresponds to "even this emptiness, and what is empty, both are extinguished." That is: earlier, at the moment when "awareness, and what is aware of it, both become empty," there still remained, plainly, some mind capable of this very emptiness arising, this object emptied, this awareness itself extinguished — still belonging to a fine current, precisely the seeds of the seventh consciousness within the eighth, named the very root of arising and ceasing. Now, at the moment the aggregate of formations is exhausted, both this nature and this habitual tendency alike are entirely gone, and so this capacity for emptiness, and this object emptied, are, together, extinguished as well! The reason this still cannot be called the true return, though, is that it remains covered over by the aggregate of consciousness still.

"Such a person can transcend the turbidity of sentient existence itself": as the earlier passage on this turbidity states, "knowledge and views constantly wish to remain within the world, while karmic movement constantly shifts them onward into some other land — interwoven together, this falsely forms" this same turbidity. Since the aggregate of formations, arising and ceasing without pause, this karmic movement constantly shifting onward, thereby forms this same knowledge and views belonging to sentient existence, muddying this true nature — now, the aggregate of formations already exhausted, this turbidity of sentient existence is thereby transcended of itself. Having transcended it, one looks back to observe the very source from which the aggregate of formations arises, and finds it rooted, originally, in this deep, hidden, concealed false thought. This closes the first section, showing this aggregate's full course from beginning to end.

The Aggregate of Formations: Ten Wild Philosophical Interpretations 壬二 中間十計

Unlike the aggregate of perception, where an external demon possesses a nearby person, the danger here is purely intellectual, and Yuanying stresses this point at the very outset of each: no external demon or spirit is involved at all — every one of these ten positions is a coherent, carefully reasoned philosophical system, built from a genuine but partial observation of the aggregate of formations itself, going wrong only in the final step of absolutizing this partial view into a wrong metaphysical claim. Each is traced to a specific school of non-Buddhist thought.

Chinese Source — Position 1: Two Theories of No Cause 二種無因

〖經〗阿難當知：是得正知，奢摩他中，諸善男子，凝明正心，十類天魔，不得其便，方得精研，窮生類本。於本類中，生元露者，觀彼幽清，圓擾動元，於圓元中，起計度者，是人墜入，二無因論。

〖講〗此下別示，行陰十計，不言十境，而云十計者，但是想破行現，自緣定中所見，而生種種邪計，非有外境魔事之擾也。此第一、二種無因論。呼阿難而告之曰：當知，警令覺知也。是得正知者：即不遭邪慮也。奢摩他中者：即圓定發明也。凡能到此地位，俱有決定善根，故稱善男子。凝明正心者：凝：不動也，於想陰十境，不起愛求。明：不惑也，於飛精附人，便能覺知。正心：雙承不動定也不惑慧也，定慧均等。十類天魔，不得其便，圓通妙行，乃可增修，方得精心研究，力破想陰。想陰既破，行陰即現，故得窮十二類生，生滅根本。此上歷敘，想破行現。於本類中，生元露者：此下方明，邪解起計，並非外魔，惟是自心作孽，是謂心魔。先窮十二類生，行陰深本，欲於本類中，研求生滅根元顯露者，即行陰顯現也。觀彼幽清，圓擾動元者：乃正觀行陰；彼即指行陰，幽隱輕清，非同前想陰，顯明重濁也。圓者：遍十二類生，一一皆然；即觀行陰遷流，微細動相，為圓擾群動之元，觀同分生基之總相，不起妄計，一味精研；自可進破行陰。今於圓元中，起計度者：於圓擾群動之元，為諸行之本，是生滅之元，如是觀察，執為勝性，故起計度。並不知有不擾不動之真如，亡失本修，以是墜入二無因論。《正脈疏》云：二無因論，乃先世外道，修心邪解，所立違理背正之惡見耳。今行現之解，適與之同，故即墜彼論中，如後車蹈前車之覆轍，故即同墮一坑。後文諸論，皆倣此意。初標由示墜竟。

〖經〗一者、是人，見本無因。何以故？是人既得生機全破，乘於眼根，八百功德，見八萬劫，所有眾生，業流灣環，死此生彼，祗見眾生，輪迴其處，八萬劫外，冥無所觀。便作是解：此等世間，十方眾生，八萬劫來，無因自有。由此計度，亡正遍知，墮落外道，惑菩提性。

〖講〗首二句標定，下徵釋。一者、是人；即於圓元中，起計度之人。計執此圓元為勝性，並不知此但行現，尚未至於盡；行陰盡後，還有識陰，識陰盡後，方是本覺。便謂此行陰，為生滅之元，本來無有因起，故曰見本無因。何以故徵，下釋。是人既得生機全破者：生機：即行陰。破：即顯露義。前文所謂生滅根元，從此披露是也。乘於眼根清淨，獲得八百功德，極盡其見量，能見八萬劫內，所有眾生，業行遷流，灣轉回環，莫能自止。死此生彼者：即捨生趣生也。亦即前文所謂，業運每常遷於國土是也。祗見眾生，隨業行以遷流，輪迴在八萬劫中之處，八萬劫外，冥然莫辨，毫無所觀也。便作如是邪解妄計：此等世間，十方眾生，八萬劫來，無因自有。因八萬劫外，冥然無有所觀，遂計無因。拘舍離等，昧為冥諦，即同此見。儒宗所謂，鴻濛混沌者，皆此類也。殊不知自己見量有限，未至圓極，若如來見量，豎窮三際，橫遍十方，豈以八萬劫為限哉？由此邪計籌度，亡失正知，執八萬劫，無因自有；亡失遍知，墮落外道邪見，惑亂菩提正覺之性矣。初本無因竟。

〖經〗二者、是人，見末無因。何以故？是人於生，既見其根，知人生人，悟鳥生鳥，烏從來黑，鵠從來白，人天本豎，畜生本橫，白非洗成，黑非染造，從八萬劫，無復改移。今盡此形，亦復如是，而我本來，不見菩提，云何更有，成菩提事？當知今日，一切物象，皆本無因。由此計度，亡正遍知，墮落外道，惑菩提性。

〖講〗首二句標定，下徵釋。二者、是人：於圓元中，起計度之人。計執此圓元為勝性，詳推過去，見本無因，例知未來，見末無因也。何以故徵，下釋云：是人於生，既見其根者：謂是人於諸眾生，既見劫前，本無其根，無因而有。蓋即以無因，為十二類生本元，自然而然，由此轉計，成為自然外道。知人還自然生人，悟鳥還自然生鳥，烏從來自然是黑，鵠從來自然是白。白非是洗成而白，黑非是染造而黑。人天二句，當在下，諒抄寫之誤。若是人天，本來是豎形而立，若是畜生，本來是橫行而走，此皆自然而然，本無有因。從八萬劫來，未嘗更改變移。此下釋成，上科全推過去，此科列定未來。今盡此形者：盡，即盡未來際，而此形亦復如是，無有改移。而我本來，八萬劫前，不見十二類生，從菩提性起；云何更有，眾生於劫後，成菩提事乎？當知今日，一切物象，既皆劫前，本無有因，以此驗知劫後，末亦無因也。言八萬劫盡，終成斷滅，無有因果而已。蓋以從無因而起者，還復無因，返於冥初之意而已。由此邪計籌度，亡失正知，皆謂一切自然，亡失遍知，不達循業發現，以致墮落外道惡見，反惑亂菩提正覺之性矣。二詳釋其相竟。是則名為，第一外道，立無因論。初二種無因論竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, you should know: within this śamatha of correct knowledge, this good person's mind, settled and clear, meets no obstruction from any of the ten kinds of heavenly demon, and so is able to research this with full precision, exhausting the very root of these twelve kinds of birth. Within this same root category, this origin of birth now laid bare, they observe this hidden, fine, universally restless root of movement — and, giving rise to speculation regarding this universal root, such a person falls into the two theories of no cause."

Commentary: From this point on, the ten distinct speculations of the aggregate of formations are shown one by one. The text says "speculations" rather than "states" here because these come about only after perception has broken through and formations have manifested, the practitioner giving rise, of themselves, within samādhi, to various wrong speculations based on what they observe — there is no external state or demonic affair disturbing them at all here. This is the first, the two theories of no cause.

Addressing Ānanda, the Buddha tells him: "you should know" here means to warn him into awareness. "Correct knowledge" means encountering no deviant apprehension; "this śamatha" means this perfect samādhi brought to light. Anyone able to reach this stage possesses a definite, wholesome root — hence called a good person. "Settled and clear" means: "settled," unmoving, giving rise to no craving throughout the ten states of the aggregate of perception; "clear," unconfused, able to recognize at once whenever a spirit is sent flying to possess someone. "Mind" carries forward both of these together — the samādhi of being unmoved, and the wisdom of being unconfused — samādhi and wisdom held in equal measure. Meeting no obstruction from any of the ten kinds of heavenly demon, this wondrous practice of perfect penetration can, thereby, be further cultivated, and so one is able to research this with full precision and force through the aggregate of perception. Once the aggregate of perception breaks through, the aggregate of formations manifests, and so one is able to exhaust the very root of arising and ceasing among these twelve kinds of birth. Everything above narrates, in sequence, how perception breaks through and formations manifest.

"Within this same root category, this origin of birth now laid bare" begins to make plain how a wrong interpretation gives rise to speculation — this is, in no way, any external demon, but simply the practitioner's own mind creating this affliction for itself, hence called a mind-demon. First exhausting this deep root of the twelve kinds of birth, the aggregate of formations, one wishes, within this same root category, to research and seek out this very root of arising and ceasing, now laid bare — this being precisely the aggregate of formations made manifest.

"They observe this hidden, fine, universally restless root of movement" refers to directly observing the aggregate of formations itself; "this" refers to the aggregate of formations, hidden and finely subtle, not the same as the aggregate of perception before it, plainly evident and coarse. "Universally" means: throughout all twelve kinds of birth, each and every one is exactly the same — that is, observing the aggregate of formations' own flowing movement, this subtle, fine mark of movement, as the very universal root of restless movement among the entire multitude of beings, observing this general mark of a shared foundation of birth, without giving rise to any wrong speculation, single-mindedly researching this with precision — one is thereby able to advance further and break through the aggregate of formations. But here, instead, giving rise to speculation regarding this universal root — taking this universal root of restless movement among the entire multitude, this very root of every formation, this very origin of arising and ceasing, observed in this way, and clinging to it as some superior nature — one gives rise, thereby, to this speculation, entirely unaware that there exists a suchness that neither stirs nor moves at all, and so loses this fundamental practice, falling, thereby, into the two theories of no cause.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: these two theories of no cause are precisely the wrong interpretations, born of cultivating the mind, held by non-Buddhist practitioners of a former age — evil views established in direct violation of principle, opposed to what is correct. This present interpretation, arising from the manifestation of formations, happens to coincide exactly with theirs, and so one falls, thereby, into this very same theory — exactly like a later cart following the very same ruts as an overturned cart before it, and so falling into one and the same pit together. Every theory discussed hereafter should be understood along this same pattern. This closes the first section, showing the cause and the falling that results.

Sūtra: "First, such a person sees no cause at the very beginning. Why is this? Because this person, having already had this mechanism of birth entirely broken open, and riding upon the eight hundred merits of the eye-faculty, sees, throughout eighty thousand kalpas, every sentient being's own karmic stream circling and curving back upon itself, dying here and being born there — yet sees only sentient beings turning within the wheel of rebirth in this same span; beyond these eighty thousand kalpas, all is dark, with nothing at all to be observed. And so they form this interpretation: that these worlds, and the sentient beings of the ten directions throughout them, have existed, these eighty thousand kalpas, without any cause at all, spontaneously so. Through this speculation, they lose correct knowledge, fall into a non-Buddhist view, and confuse their own very nature of bodhi."

Commentary: The first two clauses establish this position; what follows questions and then answers it. "First, such a person" refers to the one giving rise to speculation regarding this universal root, clinging to and grasping this universal root as some superior nature, entirely unaware that this is merely the manifestation of formations, not yet reaching their exhaustion — for, after the aggregate of formations is exhausted, there still remains the aggregate of consciousness, and only after the aggregate of consciousness is exhausted does one reach original awareness itself. And so they take this aggregate of formations to be the very origin of arising and ceasing, originally without any cause giving rise to it at all — hence, "sees no cause at the very beginning."

"Why is this?" questions this; what follows answers it. "This person, having already had this mechanism of birth entirely broken open" — "this mechanism of birth" is precisely the aggregate of formations; "broken open" carries the meaning of being laid bare — exactly what was meant earlier by "the very root of arising and ceasing itself is finally laid bare." Riding upon this purified eye-faculty, having attained its eight hundred merits, exhausting the very limit of this power of sight, they are able to see, within these eighty thousand kalpas, every sentient being's own karmic conduct flowing onward, circling and curving back upon itself, unable to bring itself to rest. "Dying here and being born there" means casting off one life to take up another — exactly what was meant earlier by "karmic movement constantly shifting them onward into some other land." They see only sentient beings, following this karmic conduct, flowing onward, turning within the wheel of rebirth in this same span of eighty thousand kalpas; beyond these eighty thousand kalpas, all is dark and indistinguishable, with nothing at all to be observed.

And so they form this deviant, false interpretation: that these worlds, and the sentient beings of the ten directions throughout them, have existed, these eighty thousand kalpas, without any cause at all, spontaneously so. Because, beyond these eighty thousand kalpas, all is dark, with nothing to be observed at all, they conclude there is no cause. Such teachers as Maskarī Gośālīputra, mistaking this for the "primal principle," hold exactly this same view; what the Confucian tradition calls "primordial chaos" belongs to this same category as well. Little do they realize that their own power of sight has its own limit, not yet reaching perfect completeness — for the Tathāgata's own power of sight extends vertically through every one of the three periods of time, and horizontally throughout the ten directions at once — how, then, could it ever be confined to a mere eighty thousand kalpas?

Through this deviant speculation and calculation, they lose correct knowledge, clinging to the view that these eighty thousand kalpas exist without any cause, spontaneously so; and they lose complete knowledge as well, falling into this non-Buddhist wrong view, and thereby confusing and disordering this very nature of bodhi, correct and complete awakening itself. This closes the first position, no cause at the very beginning.

Sūtra: "Second, such a person sees no cause at the very end. Why is this? Because this person, regarding birth, has already seen its very root: knowing that a human gives birth to a human, realizing that a bird gives birth to a bird; that the crow has always been black, that the swan has always been white; that humans and heavenly beings are, by their own nature, upright in form, while animals are, by their own nature, horizontal — white was never made white through washing, black was never made black through dyeing — and, from these eighty thousand kalpas onward, nothing has ever been altered or changed. And so, exhausting this present form entirely, they conclude the same will hold true forever: since I myself, originally, never once saw bodhi arise, how, then, could there ever be any further matter of accomplishing bodhi to come? And so you should know: every phenomenon present today is, at root, entirely without cause. Through this speculation, they lose correct knowledge, fall into a non-Buddhist view, and confuse their own very nature of bodhi."

Commentary: The first two clauses establish this position; what follows questions and then answers it. "Second, such a person" refers, again, to the one giving rise to speculation regarding this universal root, clinging to and grasping this same universal root as some superior nature, examining the past in detail and seeing no cause at the very beginning, and, by the same reasoning, inferring the future, and so seeing no cause at the very end either.

"Why is this?" questions this; what follows answers it: "this person, regarding birth, has already seen its very root" means: this person, regarding every sentient being, has already seen that, before this same kalpa, there was, originally, no root cause at all, existing without any cause whatsoever — and so, taking this very absence of cause to be the original root of these twelve kinds of birth, spontaneously so of itself, they turn this speculation further, becoming, thereby, a follower of the non-Buddhist teaching of spontaneity. Knowing that a human, spontaneously, gives birth to a human once more, realizing that a bird, spontaneously, gives birth to a bird once more, that the crow has, spontaneously, always been black, that the swan has, spontaneously, always been white — white was never made white through washing, black was never made black through dyeing. (These two clauses regarding humans and heavenly beings, and animals, ought properly to be read here — most likely a transcription error placed them elsewhere.) Humans and heavenly beings, by their own nature, stand upright in form; animals, by their own nature, move horizontally — all of this, spontaneously so of itself, originally without any cause at all, and, from these eighty thousand kalpas onward, never once altered or changed.

What follows completes this explanation: the section above inferred entirely from the past; this section fixes its conclusion regarding the future. "Exhausting this present form entirely" means: "exhausting" here means to the very end of the future itself — and this present form, too, will remain exactly the same, entirely unaltered and unchanged. "I myself, originally" — that is, eighty thousand kalpas ago — never once saw these twelve kinds of birth arise from this same nature of bodhi; how, then, could there ever be, after this same kalpa has passed, any further matter of sentient beings accomplishing bodhi to come? You should know that every phenomenon present today, being, in every case, already without any cause before this same kalpa, may thereby be inferred to be equally without any cause after this same kalpa as well. That is to say: once these eighty thousand kalpas have run their course, everything, in the end, becomes utter annihilation, with no cause and effect remaining whatsoever. For whatever arose without cause returns, once more, to this same absence of cause — nothing more than a return to this same primordial darkness.

Through this deviant speculation and calculation, they lose correct knowledge, holding that everything, without exception, is spontaneous of itself; and they lose complete knowledge as well, failing to understand that every phenomenon manifests in accordance with karma, and so fall into this non-Buddhist evil view, confusing and disordering, in turn, this very nature of bodhi, correct and complete awakening itself. This closes the second section, detailing this position in full.

This, then, is named the first non-Buddhist school, establishing the theory of no cause. This closes the first position, the two theories of no cause.

〖講〗一者、是人欲窮行陰，但約心之與境二性，以求本元，何自而起。然窮之不遠，以見心境二處，於二萬劫前，無因自有。修習者：即窮心境之修習；能知者：以其見量有限，祗見二萬劫中，十方眾生，所有心境生滅。以心境皆屬行陰，皆有生滅；然生滅正是無常，而彼但見劫內，生滅滅生，咸皆循環，相續不斷，不曾散失。遂計心境二性，以為遍常，不計劫外無因，以為斷滅，與上科異也。

Sūtra: "Ānanda, within this same samādhi, this good person, mind settled and clear, meets no obstruction from any demon, and exhausts the very root of these twelve kinds of birth. Observing this hidden, fine, constantly restless root of movement, and giving rise to speculation regarding a kind of universal permanence, such a person falls into four theories of universal permanence."

Commentary: This is the second position, the four theories of universal permanence. "Mind settled and clear" means: settled and unmoving, throughout the ten states of the aggregate of perception, never once giving rise to craving from beginning to end; clear and illuminating, unconfused, whenever a spirit is sent flying to possess someone, able at once to recognize it — "mind" here carries forward both this samādhi of being unmoved and this wisdom of being unconfused, samādhi and wisdom held in equal measure. "Meets no obstruction from any demon" means this wondrous practice of perfect penetration can, thereby, be further cultivated. The aggregate of perception already broken through, the aggregate of formations now manifest before them, exhausting the very root of arising and ceasing among these twelve kinds of birth, they thereby observe this aggregate of formations' own hidden, fine, constantly restless root of movement.

"Giving rise to speculation regarding a kind of universal permanence" means: seeing this quality pervading everywhere, they call it "universal"; seeing this quality of continuity, they call it "permanent" — little do they realize that this very pervading and this very continuity are nothing but the aggregate of formations' own flowing movement, this mark of arising and ceasing itself. And so, within this very state, they give rise to this false speculation, that this is universally, permanently abiding — such a person falls, thereby, into this non-Buddhist theory, the four theories of universal permanence.

Sūtra: "First, such a person, exhausting the very nature of mind and its objects, finds both to be without cause; through this cultivation, they come to know that, throughout twenty thousand kalpas, every sentient being of the ten directions, in all their arising and ceasing, cycles endlessly without ever being lost, and so concludes this to be permanent. Second, such a person, exhausting the very origin of the four elements, finds their four natures to be ever-abiding; through this cultivation, they come to know that, throughout forty thousand kalpas, every sentient being of the ten directions, in all their arising and ceasing, shares this same constant substance without ever being lost, and so concludes this to be permanent. Fourth, such a person, having already exhausted the very root of the aggregate of perception, and finding no further flowing or resting, no further movement, no further arising-and-ceasing thought remaining, concludes that this thought, now permanently extinguished, has, within this very principle, spontaneously become something free of arising and ceasing altogether — and, through this deliberation of mind, concludes this to be permanent. Commentary: First, such a person, wishing to exhaust the aggregate of formations, examines only these two natures of mind and its objects, seeking their very origin, whence they arose. Yet, in exhausting this, they do not reach very far, seeing only that mind and its objects, twenty thousand kalpas ago, existed without any cause, spontaneously so. "This cultivation" refers to this cultivation of exhausting mind and its objects; "come to know" refers to the fact that their own power of sight has its own limit, seeing only, throughout these twenty thousand kalpas, every sentient being of the ten directions' own arising and ceasing of mind and its objects. Since mind and its objects both belong to the aggregate of formations, both possess arising and ceasing — and this arising and ceasing is, in truth, precisely impermanence itself — yet they see only that, within this span of kalpas, arising ceasing and ceasing arising once more, cycle endlessly, continuing without interruption, never once being lost. And so they conclude that these two natures of mind and its objects are universally permanent, without ever considering that, beyond this span of kalpas, this same absence of cause might equally be taken for utter annihilation instead — this differing from the position above.

Second, such a person, wishing to exhaust the aggregate of formations, relying on their own view and understanding, researches and exhausts the four elements, taking them for the very origin of transformation. Seeing that every phenomenon arises from the combination of these four elements, they conclude that the very nature of these four elements is, originally, ever-abiding. Little do they realize that these four elements, too, belong to nothing but consciousness-only transformation, their own substance originally empty, like a flower conjured in empty space. Relying on this cultivation, they come to know matters within forty thousand kalpas; beyond forty thousand kalpas, all is dark, with nothing to be observed at all. Their own power of sight, compared to the position before, has doubled: every sentient being of the ten directions' own arising and ceasing arises entirely from the combination of these four elements; and, since the very nature of these four elements is ever-abiding, this same substantial nature, they conclude, pervades everywhere, constant and permanent, never once lost — and so they conclude that the nature of these four elements is universally permanent.

Third, such a person, exhausting entirely, among these six sense-faculties, the sixth consciousness they possess, together with the seventh consciousness, manas, ever discerning and deliberating, and the eighth consciousness, which sustains and holds — since it sustains and holds the bodily faculties, the outer world, and every seed — this practitioner, relying on their own view and understanding, exhausts, within the eighth consciousness (mind), the seventh consciousness (intellect), and the sixth consciousness (consciousness proper), the very original root and place from which they arise. Little do they realize that this is precisely the very cause of the aggregate of formations' own continuity, and instead falsely conclude that the very nature of consciousness is constant and unchanging. Relying on this cultivation, they are able to observe that, throughout eighty thousand kalpas, every sentient being, dying here and being born there, turns and cycles endlessly, never once lost — and so they conclude that, from the very beginning, this has pervaded everywhere, permanently abiding; that is, exhausting this very nature that cycles without loss, they conclude this to be universally, permanently abiding. As the Standard Meaning Commentary notes: precisely because the eighth consciousness they have exhausted here is a far deeper, broader, more detailed and precise teaching than the four elements examined before, the span of kalpas they claim to know is, likewise, double that of the position before.

Fourth, such a person concludes that the exhaustion of perception itself is permanence — because, having already exhausted the very root of the aggregate of perception (meaning this root aggregate of perception, precisely this mark of movement within the eighth consciousness), and now, with perception broken through, this mark of movement already exhausted, this very root of arising and ceasing belonging to the aggregate of formations laid bare — "no further flowing or resting" refers to this same root of arising and ceasing, exactly like heat-shimmer, this fine, restless glimmer — this is not, in truth, without any flow at all, yet they mistakenly conclude that there is no further flowing or resting, no further movement or activity whatsoever, meaning that this thought of arising and ceasing has, now, been permanently extinguished, and that, within this very principle, something free of arising and ceasing has spontaneously come to be. Little do they realize that the aggregate of formations is, precisely, the seventh consciousness's own seeds, this subtle, fine, flowing stream — not, in truth, genuinely free of arising and ceasing at all — nothing more than a conclusion reached through mere mental deliberation, taken to be universally, permanently abiding. This closes the second section, detailing this position in full.

For this very reason, then, what is not, in truth, universally permanent is falsely taken to be universally permanent. "They lose correct, complete knowledge" means: failing to understand the aggregate of formations' own flowing movement, correct knowledge is lost; failing to awaken to every phenomenon's own arising and ceasing, complete knowledge is lost. Falling into this non-Buddhist view, clinging to this as permanence, they remain unaware that there exists a further, true permanence — this very nature of bodhi, correct and complete awakening itself. This, then, is named the second, eternalist non-Buddhist school, establishing the theory of universal permanence. This closes the second position, the four theories of universal permanence.

Chinese Source — Position 3: One Part Permanent, One Part Impermanent 一分無常一分常

〖經〗又三摩中，諸善男子，堅凝正心，魔不得便，窮生類本，觀彼幽清，常擾動元，於自他中，起計度者，是人墜入，四顛倒見，一分無常，一分常論。

〖講〗此雙計常與無常也。又三摩中，諸善男子堅凝正心者：前二科云凝明，足顯定慧相資，此云堅凝，似偏屬於定。然雖似偏屬，乃是即慧之定，定力愈深，而堅固凝然不動。想陰十境，始終不起愛求，故曰正心。魔不得便，故得想破行現。研究眾生，十二種類，生滅深本，深本既現，但依耳根妙修，一味反聞照性，自可進破行陰；而乃觀察行陰，幽隱輕清，常時擾動；生滅之根元，既為諸動之根元，則自他依正，皆依之建立。由此於自他法中，起諸妄計籌度者，是人墜入，四顛倒見；顛倒見中，一分無常，一分常義。《合轍》云：平等性中，本無自他，而妄計自他；中道了義，迥絕二邊，而謬執斷常，非倒見而何哉？《正脈》云：四種顛倒，合前二計觀之，二無因，似觀劫外斷處，而計無常；四遍常，似觀劫內續處，而計是常；各皆單計而已。今此乃是雙計，常與無常也。

〖經〗一者、是人，觀妙明心，遍十方界，湛然以為，究竟神我。從是則計，我遍十方，凝明不動，一切眾生，於我心中，自生自死。則我心性，名之為常；彼生滅者，真無常性。二者、是人，不觀其心，遍觀十方，恆沙國土。見劫壞處，名為究竟，無常種性；劫不壞處，名究竟常。三者、是人，別觀我心，精細微密，猶如微塵，流轉十方，性無移改，能令此身，即生即滅，其不壞性，名我性常。一切死生，從我流出，名無常性。四者、是人，知想陰盡，見行陰流，行陰常流，計為常性，色受想等，今已滅盡，名無常性。由此計度，一分無常，一分常故，墮落外道，惑菩提性，是則名為第三外道，一分常論。

〖講〗一者、是人觀彼幽清，常擾動元，此是行陰，生滅根元；而計為妙明真心，遍滿十方世界。且以幽清常擾，不覺妄謂湛然，即不動義，自計最勝曰究竟，名為神我，此即外道二十五諦之最後一諦也。彼有定力，能觀八萬劫；八萬劫外，則冥然莫辨，遂立為冥諦。從冥諦生覺大，覺大生我心，我心生五微，五微生五大，五大生十一根，最後立一神我。謬計冥諦為能生，中間二十三法為所生。神我為能受用，中間二十三法為所受用。如彼論云：神我有知，能思慮故，我以思為性，受用大等二十三法。蓋是以第八識中，所含第七識種子，為神我也。既立神我，從是則計神我，遍滿十方，凝明不動，無生無滅，以故是常。以一切眾生，於我心中，自生自死，故是無常。此於自他處計常無常也。

二者、是人前觀自心，已知是常，故不復觀，又前於眾生，知是無常；而於國土，未知是否常住？故運心遍觀，十方恆沙國土，成壞不一。彼但見劫壞之處，三災起時，壞後見空；不知成住壞空，乃世界之劫運，空後還有成，便名為究竟無常種性。若見劫不壞處，不知暫時而住，不過二十小劫，便名為究竟真常種性。此以國土壞不壞，計為常無常也。《指掌疏》云：要知界性無二，成壞隨緣，若因有壞，而執無常，則菩薩灰嚴土之心；若因不壞，而執真常，則凡夫增戀世之志。邪見誤人，不可不知。

三者、是人捨他觀自，於自己身心二途，分別而觀。別觀我心等：此觀心也。能令此身等：此觀身也。言精細微密者：此想破行現，指行陰根本也。別觀我心，是七識種子故曰精，流注莫見故曰細，虛受照應故曰微，行相幽隱效曰密，猶如微塵，為十二類眾生根本。此即外道所計之微細我也。第一科中神我，即廣大我也。依此微細我，起惑造業，隨業受報，流轉十方，而性無移改，此即大小不定我也。所受之報，有大小不定故。以上觀心，次觀身；能令此身，即生即滅者：前云業運每常遷於國土，能令此身，剎那變滅，故云即生即滅，其不壞性，即無移改之心性，名我性常。至於一切生死之身，從我流出，名無常性。此以自己身心計為常無常也。

四者、是人通觀四陰，知色、受、想三陰已盡，見行陰現在遷流，以行陰相續，不斷而常流者，便計以為常住性。色受想等三陰滅盡，而無有者，故計以為無常性。此於四陰中，計常無常也。二詳釋其相竟。由此計度，自他依正，及陰等。各執不同，總不出一分無常，一分常論。以此雙計，故成顛倒。所以墮落外道，如僧佉論師等。《智論》云：諸法不應執常。常即無罪無福，無所傷殺，亦無施命，無縛無解，則無涅槃；若執無常，即是斷滅，亦無罪福，亦無增損功業，因緣果報亦失；是皆足以惑菩提性，是則名為第三外道一分常論。此偏言一分者，以二分中，以常為勝，顯獨重故。三四種顛倒竟。

Sūtra: "Further, within this same samādhi, this good person, mind firm and settled, meets no obstruction from any demon, and exhausts the very root of these twelve kinds of birth. Observing this hidden, fine, constantly restless root of movement, and giving rise to speculation regarding self and other, such a person falls into four inverted views: one part impermanent, one part permanent."

"Mind firm and settled" — the two positions before this spoke of "settled and clear," fully showing samādhi and wisdom mutually supporting each other; this speaks of "firm and settled," seeming to lean more toward samādhi alone. Yet, though seeming to lean this way, this is samādhi inseparable from wisdom itself — the power of samādhi growing ever deeper, firm and settled, unmoving. Throughout the ten states of the aggregate of perception, never once giving rise to craving from beginning to end — hence, "mind." Meeting no obstruction from any demon, one is thereby able to have perception break through and formations manifest. Researching the deep root of arising and ceasing among these twelve kinds of sentient being, this deep root now manifest, one need only rely on this wondrous cultivation through the ear-faculty, single-mindedly turning the hearing inward to illuminate this nature, and one can, of oneself, advance further and break through the aggregate of formations.

Instead, though, they observe the aggregate of formations, hidden and finely subtle, constantly restless in movement; this very root of arising and ceasing, being the very root of every kind of movement, self and other, dependent and direct retribution, are all, thereby, established upon it. And so, within this very matter of self and other, they give rise to this false speculation and calculation — such a person falls, thereby, into these four inverted views, within which lies this meaning of one part impermanent, one part permanent. As the Hezhe Commentary puts it: within this nature of equality, there was, originally, no self or other at all, and yet self and other are falsely conceived; within this complete meaning of the middle way, both extremes are utterly transcended, and yet annihilation and eternity are wrongly clung to — how could this be anything but an inverted view?

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: comparing these four kinds of inversion against the two speculations before, the two theories of no cause resemble observing the break beyond this span of kalpas, and concluding impermanence; the four theories of universal permanence resemble observing the continuity within this span of kalpas, and concluding permanence — each, in either case, a single, one-sided speculation alone. This present position, though, speculates on both permanence and impermanence together, at once.

Sūtra: "First, such a person, observing this wondrous, bright mind, pervading the worlds of the ten directions, clear and still, takes this to be the ultimate, spiritual self. From this, they conclude: this self of mine pervades the ten directions, settled and bright, unmoving, and every sentient being, within this very mind of mine, is born of itself and dies of itself. And so this nature of my own mind is named permanent, while these beings that arise and cease are of a truly impermanent nature.

"Second, such a person, no longer observing their own mind, instead observes broadly, throughout the ten directions, lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges. Wherever they see a place undergoing the destruction of the kalpa, they name this the ultimate, impermanent kind of nature; wherever they see a place not undergoing this destruction, they name this the ultimate, permanent kind of nature.

"Third, such a person, observing this self of mine separately, finds it refined, fine, and subtle, exactly like a mote of dust, flowing and turning throughout the ten directions, its nature never shifting or changing at all — able to make this body arise and cease in an instant, while its own indestructible nature is named the permanent nature of this self. Every instance of death and birth flows out from this self, and is named the impermanent nature.

"Fourth, such a person, knowing that the aggregate of perception is exhausted, and seeing the aggregate of formations still flowing on, concludes that, since the aggregate of formations flows on constantly, this must be its permanent nature; while form, sensation, and perception, these three aggregates, now already extinguished entirely, are named its impermanent nature.

"Through this conclusion — one part impermanent, one part permanent — they fall into a non-Buddhist view, and confuse their own very nature of bodhi. This, then, is named the third non-Buddhist school, the theory of one part permanent."

Commentary: First, such a person, observing this hidden, fine, constantly restless root of movement — this being precisely the aggregate of formations, this very root of arising and ceasing — instead concludes this to be the wondrous, bright, true mind itself, pervading the worlds of the ten directions. And, moreover, taking this hidden, fine, constant restlessness, unaware of it as such, falsely calling it "clear and still" — meaning, precisely, unmoving — they conclude, of themselves, that this is the very utmost, calling it "ultimate," and name it the spiritual self — this being precisely the very last of the twenty-five categories of the non-Buddhist tradition. Possessing some power of samādhi, able to observe eighty thousand kalpas, yet, beyond these eighty thousand kalpas, all dark and indistinguishable, they establish this as the "primal principle." From this primal principle, "great awareness" is said to arise; from great awareness, this self-mind arises; from this self-mind, five subtle elements arise; from these five subtle elements, five gross elements arise; from these five gross elements, eleven faculties arise; and, at the very last, this single spiritual self is established. They falsely conclude that this primal principle is the very generator, and these twenty-three intervening categories of phenomena its generated products; that this spiritual self is the very enjoyer, and these same twenty-three intervening categories what is enjoyed. As their own treatise states: this spiritual self possesses knowledge, being capable of thought and reflection — this self takes thought itself as its own nature, enjoying these twenty-three categories, "great" and the rest. This is, in fact, nothing but taking the seeds of the seventh consciousness, contained within the eighth consciousness, to be this "spiritual self."

Having established this spiritual self, they then conclude that this spiritual self pervades the ten directions, settled and bright, unmoving, neither arising nor ceasing — and so is permanent; while every sentient being, arising and dying of itself within this very mind of theirs, is thereby impermanent. This, then, speculates on permanence and impermanence in terms of self and other.

Second, this person, having already concluded their own mind to be permanent, no longer observes it further, and, having already concluded sentient beings to be impermanent, turns instead to the question of whether lands themselves remain permanently abiding or not — and so, applying their mind, they observe broadly, throughout the ten directions, lands as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, their formation and destruction never uniform. They see only that, wherever a kalpa's destruction occurs, the three calamities arising, emptiness is seen after this destruction — not realizing that formation, abiding, destruction, and emptiness are simply this same cosmic cycle of the kalpa, and that formation follows once more after emptiness — and so they name this the ultimate, impermanent kind of nature. Wherever they see a place not undergoing this destruction, not realizing that this, too, abides only temporarily, no more than twenty small kalpas at most, they name this the ultimate, truly permanent kind of nature. This, then, speculates on permanence and impermanence in terms of whether a land is destroyed or not. As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: one should know that the very nature of the world-realm is not twofold — its formation and its destruction alike follow conditions. Should one, on account of its destruction, cling to impermanence, this becomes the very mind of a bodhisattva who regards even a jeweled, adorned land as mere ash; should one, on account of its non-destruction, cling to true permanence, this becomes, instead, the ordinary being's own growing attachment to this world. A wrong view misleads people — this cannot but be understood clearly.

Third, this person, setting aside the question of others, observes only themselves, distinguishing separately between their own body and mind in observing each. "Observing this self of mine separately" and so on concerns observing the mind; "able to make this body" and so on concerns observing the body. "Refined, fine, and subtle" refers to this same state where perception has broken through and formations have manifested, pointing to the very root of the aggregate of formations itself. Observing this self of mine separately — since this is the seed of the seventh consciousness, it is called "refined"; since its flowing continuity cannot be seen, it is called "fine"; since it merely receives and reflects emptily, it is called "subtle"; since its own activity remains hidden and concealed, it is called "densely obscure" — exactly like a mote of dust, serving as the very root of these twelve kinds of sentient being. This is precisely the "subtle self" conceived by non-Buddhist teachers; the "spiritual self" in the first position above was, by contrast, the "vast self." Relying on this subtle self, giving rise to delusion and creating karma, receiving retribution according to this karma, flowing and turning throughout the ten directions, while this nature itself never shifts or changes at all — this is precisely the "self of indeterminate size," since the retribution received differs, sometimes vast, sometimes small.

The above concerns observing the mind; next comes observing the body. "Able to make this body arise and cease in an instant" — as it was said earlier, "karmic movement constantly shifts them onward into some other land" — able to make this body change and perish in a single instant, hence, "arise and cease in an instant." "Its own indestructible nature" is precisely this mind-nature that never shifts or changes, named the permanent nature of this self. As for every body subject to birth and death, flowing out from this self, this is named its impermanent nature. This, then, speculates on permanence and impermanence in terms of one's own body and mind.

Fourth, this person, observing all four aggregates together, knows that form, sensation, and perception, these three aggregates, are already exhausted, and sees the aggregate of formations presently flowing on — and, since the aggregate of formations continues in unbroken succession, constantly flowing, they conclude this to be its permanent nature; while form, sensation, and perception, these three aggregates, already extinguished entirely and no longer present, are concluded to be its impermanent nature. This, then, speculates on permanence and impermanence among these four aggregates. This closes the second section, detailing this position in full.

Through this speculation — regarding self and other, dependent and direct retribution, and the aggregates themselves — each clinging to a different position, yet none departing from this same theory, one part impermanent, one part permanent. Through this dual speculation, an inverted view results. And so they fall into this non-Buddhist teaching, such as the followers of the Sāṃkhya treatise and the like. As the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom states: no phenomenon should ever be clung to as permanent — for, were it permanent, there would be no offense and no merit, nothing ever harmed or destroyed, and no giving of life either; no bondage and no liberation, and so no nirvāṇa at all. And should one cling to impermanence instead, this becomes utter annihilation — likewise no offense and no merit, no increase or decrease of any deed, and cause, condition, and karmic fruit alike lost entirely. Both are equally sufficient to confuse this very nature of bodhi. This, then, is named the third non-Buddhist school, the theory of one part permanent. This is spoken of specifically as "one part" because, of the two parts, permanence is taken as superior, showing that it alone is given special weight. This closes the third position, the four kinds of inversion.

The Aggregate of Formations: Positions 4 Through 10

Chinese Source — Position 4: Four Theories of Finitude 四種有邊

〖經〗又三摩中，諸善男子，堅凝正心，魔不得便，窮生類本，觀彼幽清，常擾動元，於分位中，生計度者，是人墜入，四有邊論。一者、是人，心計生元，流用不息。計過未者，名為有邊。計相續心，名為無邊。二者、是人，觀八萬劫，則見眾生，八萬劫前，寂無聞見，無聞見處，名為無邊，有眾生處，名為有邊。三者、是人，計我遍知，得無邊性。彼一切人，現我知中，我曾不知，彼之知性；名彼不得，無邊之心，但有邊性。四者、是人窮行陰空，以其所見，心路籌度，一切眾生，一身之中，計其咸皆半生半滅。明其世界，一切所有，一半有邊，一半無邊。由此計度，有邊無邊，墮落外道，惑菩提性，是則名為，第四外道，立有邊論。

〖講〗又三摩中，文同上可知。於分位中，生計度者：溫陵曰：分位有四，謂三際分位，見聞分位，彼我分位，生滅分位。於四種分位中，生計度者，是人墜入四有邊論。文雖雙計，有邊無邊，理實但是邪計。邊見而已，非真得無邊理體，故以正教判之，但名有邊。一者、是人約分位計有邊無邊也。心計生元者：謂見行陰為十二類生，生滅根元，而現在遷流業用，循環不息。計過去已滅，未來未至，名為有邊；計現在相續，曾無間斷，名為無邊。既取現心續處，為無限際者，為無邊；則必以過未斷處，有限際者，為有邊。然心無限際者，以當念觀心，浩渺無涯岸之謂也。二者、是人約見聞以計有邊無邊也。以定力能觀八萬劫，則見眾生，生滅滅生，輪迴其處，八萬劫前，寂然無有聞見。彼但見無聞見處，冥然莫辨，邈無涯涘；不知惟是自己見聞不及，遂名為無邊之性。又但見有眾生處，生滅相續；不知惟是業緣，虛妄現起，遂名有邊之性。三者、是人約彼我以計有邊無邊也。此由行人，觀己行陰，執為真我。遂計我能周遍了知，於諸法之中，得無邊之性。而彼一切眾生，皆現我知之中，我曾不知，彼知之性；名彼不得，無邊心性，但名有邊心性耳。四者、是人約生滅以計有邊無邊也。窮行陰空者：謂想陰既盡，行陰遷流，今以定力研窮，欲求其空即滅也。以在定中，覺得行陰滅，出定之時，覺得行陰生，不知是定功未至，若定功至，則行陰自空。如波瀾滅，化為澄水。今以其所見，用妄想心路籌度，謬謂一切眾生，一身之中，咸皆半生半滅。以一例諸，明世界所有一切皆是一半有邊，一半無邊，以生時覺得有邊，滅時覺得無邊故。由此妄心計度，有邊無邊，墮落外道，成其伴侶，以其邊則非中，故迷惑中道，菩提正性。是則名為第四外道，立有邊論。四四種有邊竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Further, within this same samādhi, this good person, mind firm and settled, meets no obstruction from any demon, exhausts the very root of these twelve kinds of birth, observes this hidden, fine, constantly restless root of movement, and, giving rise to speculation regarding position and status, such a person falls into the four theories of finitude.

"First, such a person's mind concludes, regarding this very root of birth, that it flows and functions without rest — concluding the past and future to be finite, while concluding this continuous, present mind to be infinite. Second, such a person, observing eighty thousand kalpas, sees that, eighty thousand kalpas before this, sentient beings were utterly still, with no hearing or seeing at all — and, having no place of hearing or seeing, names this infinite, while, having a place where sentient beings exist, names this finite. Third, such a person concludes that this knowing of mine pervades everywhere, attaining an infinite nature; and, since every other person appears within my own knowing, while I myself have never once known their own nature of knowing, this is named their own failure to attain this infinite mind — theirs, therefore, is merely a finite nature. Fourth, such a person, exhausting the aggregate of formations toward emptiness, deliberates, based on what they have observed, that every sentient being, within a single body, is exactly half arising and half ceasing at any moment — and so concludes that everything belonging to this world is likewise half finite and half infinite. Through this conclusion — finite and infinite — they fall into a non-Buddhist view, and confuse their own very nature of bodhi. This, then, is named the fourth non-Buddhist school, the theory of finitude."

Commentary: "Further, within this same samādhi" matches the pattern above. "Giving rise to speculation regarding position and status": as Wenling puts it, there are four such positions — the position of the three periods of time, the position of hearing and seeing, the position of self and other, and the position of arising and ceasing. Giving rise to speculation within these four positions, such a person falls into these four theories of finitude. Though the text speaks of both finite and infinite together, the underlying reasoning, in truth, is nothing but a wrong, one-sided view — not a genuine attainment of the true substance of infinity at all, and so, judged against the correct teaching, this is named, simply, finitude throughout.

First, such a person speculates on finite and infinite in terms of position within time. "This mind concludes, regarding this very root of birth" means: seeing the aggregate of formations as this very root of arising and ceasing among these twelve kinds of birth, its present flowing karmic function cycling without rest. Concluding the past, already gone, and the future, not yet arrived, to be finite; concluding the present, continuing without a single interruption, to be infinite. Having taken this continuous present mind as without any limit, and so infinite, they necessarily take the past and future, cut off, as bounded, and so finite. Yet this "mind without limit" refers only to this present thought's own contemplating mind, boundless and without any visible shore.

Second, such a person speculates on finite and infinite in terms of hearing and seeing. Through the power of samādhi, able to observe eighty thousand kalpas, they see sentient beings, arising and ceasing, ceasing and arising once more, turning within this same span — while, eighty thousand kalpas before this, all is utterly still, with no hearing or seeing at all. Seeing only this place without hearing or seeing, dark and indistinguishable, boundless without any shore, not realizing this is merely the limit of their own hearing and seeing, they name this an infinite nature. And, seeing only this place where sentient beings exist, arising and ceasing in unbroken succession, not realizing this is merely karmic condition, falsely manifesting, they name this a finite nature.

Third, such a person speculates on finite and infinite in terms of self and other. This comes about because the practitioner, observing their own aggregate of formations, clings to it as their own true self, and so concludes that this self of theirs is able to know everything, pervading every phenomenon completely, attaining thereby an infinite nature. Yet every other person, they conclude, appears entirely within their own knowing, while they themselves have never once known these others' own nature of knowing — and so they name this others' own failure to attain this infinite mind-nature, calling it, instead, merely a finite mind-nature.

Fourth, such a person speculates on finite and infinite in terms of arising and ceasing. "Exhausting the aggregate of formations toward emptiness" means: the aggregate of perception already exhausted, the aggregate of formations still flowing on, they now research and exhaust this through the power of samādhi, seeking its very emptiness, that is, its cessation. While in samādhi, they perceive the aggregate of formations as ceased; emerging from samādhi, they perceive it as arising once more — not realizing that this is simply because their own effort in samādhi has not yet reached its full measure; were this effort to reach its full measure, the aggregate of formations would become empty of itself, exactly like waves subsiding into still water. Now, based on what they have observed, deliberating through this same false, calculating mind, they mistakenly conclude that every sentient being, within a single body, is exactly half arising and half ceasing at any moment — and, taking this single instance as an example for every case, conclude that everything belonging to this world is, likewise, half finite and half infinite, since, at the moment of arising, one perceives finitude, and, at the moment of ceasing, one perceives infinity.

Through this false, calculating mind — finite and infinite — they fall into this non-Buddhist teaching, becoming its own companions; since this very finitude is not the middle way, they thereby become confused about this middle way, this true nature of bodhi itself. This, then, is named the fourth non-Buddhist school, the theory of finitude. This closes the fourth position, the four theories of finitude.

Chinese Source — Position 5: Endless Evasive Equivocation 四種矯亂

〖經〗又三摩中，諸善男子，堅凝正心，魔不得便。窮生類本，觀彼幽清，常擾動元，於知見中，生計度者，是人墜入，四種顛倒，不死矯亂，遍計虛論。一者、是人，觀變化元，見遷流處，名之為變。見相續處，名之為恆。見所見處，名之為生。不見見處，名之為滅。相續之因，性不斷處，名之為增。正相續中，中所離處，名之為減。各各生處，名之為有。互互亡處，名之為無。以理都觀，用心別見。有求法人，來問其義，答言我今，亦生亦滅，亦有亦無，亦增亦減，於一切時，皆亂其語，令彼前人，遺失章句。二者、是人，諦觀其心，互互無處，因無得證，有人來問，惟答一字，但言其無，除無之餘，無所言說。三者、是人，諦觀其心，各各有處，因有得證，有人來問，惟答一字。但言其是，除是之餘，無所言說。四者、是人，有無俱見，其境枝故，其心亦亂，有人來問，答言亦有，即是亦無，亦無之中，不是亦有，一切矯亂，無容窮詰。由此計度，矯亂虛無，墮落外道，惑菩提性。是則名為，第五外道，四顛倒性，不死矯亂，遍計虛論。

〖講〗於知見中，生計度者：謂以彼定中，所知所見，不能決擇明了，而妄生周遍計度也。是人墜入四種顛倒之見。不死者：外道計無想天，為不死天。一生不亂答人，死後當生彼天。若實不知，而輒答者，恐成矯亂；故有問時，答言秘密言詞，不應皆說，或不定答。佛法訶云：此真矯亂。《灌頂》云：邪分別性，故名遍計；都無實義，故云虛論。一者、是人以生滅行陰分別，而成八種邪見。觀變化元者：以想盡行現，進觀行陰，為變化之根元，其體即是遷流。既有遷變流轉，故即名為變。雖有遷變，而卻前後相續，有相續則無斷絕，故即名為恆即常也。此變恆一對也。見所見處，名之為生者：於八萬劫內，能見所見之處，似為眾生生，故即名為生。不見見處，名之為滅者：於八萬劫外，不能見所見之處，似是眾生滅，故即名為滅。此生滅一對也。相續之因，性不斷處者：此又於生滅之中，別起有因之計，如前行陰已滅，後行陰未生，中間必有相續之因，然相續即中有身，其體即是識陰，以彼不知，行陰之外，別有識陰，但見其性不斷處，似多出一法，名之為增。正相續中，必有缺乏之處，如出入之息，相續而缺中交是也，缺少即名為減。此增減一對也。又各各生處，名之為有者：因觀眾生，各各生處，以生為有，故遂名為有。見互互無處，以滅為無，故遂名為無。此有無一對也。如上八種，雖則以行陰之理，統而觀之，而行人用心，差別而見，前後不一，無有正知正見。有求法人來問修證之義，答言我今亦生亦滅，亦有亦無，亦增亦減。蓋亦者，兩可之詞，顯其不墮偏執。殊不知，中無主宰，於一切時，皆亂其語。正墮矯亂論議，文少亦變亦恆一對。令彼前人，遺失章句者：指前來問話之人；答者既兩可莫決，而聽者自然遺失章句也。二者、是人諦觀其心者：於生類中，諦觀行陰之心。互互無處，因無得證者：謂悟得一切法皆無也。有人來問，惟答其無，除無之外，無所言說也。三者、因有得證者：念生後必有滅相，滅後必有生相。從此證得，一切皆有；是即有也。餘可知。四者、是人觀察行陰，有無俱見。既見其念念生處，又見其念念滅處；其境如木分為兩枝，其心亦復不定亂也。有人來問，即答，見亦有即是亦無者：以生者必歸於滅。亦無之中不是亦有者：以滅者不定更生。一切矯亂，無容窮詰者：矯者：執拗不順於理。亂者：心無主正。《寶鏡疏》云：此中第一、第四，言皆兩可，亂義為多，而終非順理，亦兼於矯也。第二、第三，言惟一偏，矯義為多，而終非主正，同歸於亂也。故總結云：一切矯亂，無容窮究詰問者也。虛無者：虛妄邪計，無有實義。墮落外道，迷惑菩提真性，是則名為，第五外道。迷正知見，立邪知見，故曰四顛倒性。結名遍計虛論者：此人周遍計度，如執繩為蛇，皆至虛至妄之論也。五四種矯亂竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Further, within this same samādhi, this good person, mind firm and settled, meets no obstruction from any demon, exhausts the very root of these twelve kinds of birth, observes this hidden, fine, constantly restless root of movement, and, giving rise to speculation regarding knowledge and views, such a person falls into these four inverted views: an evasive, undying equivocation, a completely fabricated, empty theory.

"First, such a person, observing the very origin of transformation, sees the place of flowing movement, and names this 'change'; sees the place of continuity, and names this 'constancy.' Seeing the place where something is seen, they name this 'arising'; not seeing the place where something was seen, they name this 'ceasing.' Where the cause of continuity persists without break, they name this 'increase'; where, within this same continuity, some interruption occurs, they name this 'decrease.' Wherever each thing arises, they name this 'existence'; wherever each thing, in turn, vanishes, they name this 'nonexistence.' Surveying all this through principle, yet applying their mind, they see each differently — and so, when someone seeking the Dharma comes to ask them its meaning, they answer: 'I now say both arising and ceasing, both existence and nonexistence, both increase and decrease' — confusing their own words at every turn, so that the one who asked loses track of the very sense of the question entirely.

"Second, such a person, closely observing their own mind, everywhere finds only nonexistence, and, through this nonexistence, gains a kind of confirmation — and so, whenever someone comes to ask, they answer with but a single word, saying only 'nonexistence,' and, apart from this, have nothing further to say at all.

"Third, such a person, closely observing their own mind, everywhere finds only existence, and, through this existence, gains a kind of confirmation — and so, whenever someone comes to ask, they answer with but a single word, saying only 'it is so,' and, apart from this, have nothing further to say at all.

"Fourth, such a person sees both existence and nonexistence together, this realm itself branching in two, and so their own mind, too, becomes confused — and, whenever someone comes to ask, they answer: 'existence is itself nonexistence, yet, within this same nonexistence, it is not the case that existence is present either' — an entirely evasive equivocation, impossible ever to press to any conclusion. Through this conclusion — this evasive, empty confusion — they fall into a non-Buddhist view, and confuse their own very nature of bodhi. This, then, is named the fifth non-Buddhist school, these four inverted natures, an evasive, undying equivocation, a completely fabricated, empty theory."

Commentary: "Giving rise to speculation regarding knowledge and views" means: what is known and seen within this samādhi cannot be resolved and clarified, and so a false, all-encompassing speculation arises instead. Such a person falls into these four inverted views. "Undying" refers to how non-Buddhist practitioners consider the No-Thought heaven the "undying heaven" — one who, throughout this present life, answers no question with any fixed commitment, will, they believe, be born into that heaven after death. Fearing that, should they answer while genuinely not knowing, this might result in an evasive confusion, they therefore, whenever questioned, answer only in secretive, ambiguous language, never stating anything outright, or else giving no fixed answer at all. The Buddhadharma condemns this, saying: this is truly an evasive equivocation. As the Guanding Commentary puts it: because this is a deviant discrimination of one's own nature, it is called "all-encompassing speculation"; because it carries no real meaning whatsoever, it is called an "empty theory."

First, such a person, through discriminating the aggregate of formations' own arising and ceasing, forms eight kinds of wrong view. "Observing the very origin of transformation" means: with perception exhausted and formations now manifest, advancing to observe the aggregate of formations as this very origin of transformation, its own substance being precisely this flowing movement. Since there is this flux and flow, it is named "change"; yet, though there is this flux and flow, it nonetheless continues in unbroken succession before and after — and, since there is continuity, there is no interruption, and so it is likewise named "constancy," meaning permanence. This is the first pair, change and constancy. "Seeing the place where something is seen, they name this arising" means: within these eighty thousand kalpas, wherever something can be seen and is seen, this resembles a sentient being's own birth, and so is named "arising." "Not seeing the place where something was seen, they name this ceasing" means: beyond these eighty thousand kalpas, wherever something can no longer be seen, this resembles a sentient being's own death, and so is named "ceasing." This is the second pair, arising and ceasing.

"Where the cause of continuity persists without break" means: further, within this same arising and ceasing, they give rise to a separate speculation regarding cause: since, when a prior instance of the aggregate of formations has already ceased, and a later instance has not yet arisen, there must, in between, be some cause of continuity — yet this "continuity" is precisely the intermediate-state body, its very substance being, in fact, the aggregate of consciousness itself. Not knowing that, beyond the aggregate of formations, there exists, separately, the aggregate of consciousness, they see only this place where its own nature does not break off, resembling some further phenomenon appearing in addition, and name this "increase." And, within this same continuity, there must also be some place of lack — as with the breath, going out and coming in continuously, yet with some brief interruption between the two — this deficiency is named "decrease." This is the third pair, increase and decrease.

And, further, "wherever each thing arises, they name this existence" means: because they observe sentient beings, each arising in its own place, taking this arising to be existence, they name it, accordingly, "existence." Seeing the place where each, in turn, vanishes, taking this ceasing to be nonexistence, they name it, accordingly, "nonexistence." This is the fourth pair, existence and nonexistence.

Though these eight kinds, above, may all be observed together through this same underlying principle of the aggregate of formations, the practitioner's own application of mind sees each differently, inconsistent from one moment to the next, with no genuine right knowledge or right view present at all. And so, when someone seeking the Dharma comes to ask about the meaning of cultivation and realization, they answer: "I now say both arising and ceasing, both existence and nonexistence, both increase and decrease" — this word "both," here, being a term that hedges between two possibilities, meant to show that they do not fall into any one-sided position. Little do they realize that, having no true master within at all, they confuse their own words at every turn — falling, precisely, into this evasive equivocation. (The text here is somewhat abbreviated regarding the first pair, change and constancy.) "So that the one who asked loses track of the very sense of the question entirely" refers to the very person who came to ask; since the one answering hedges between two possibilities, unable to decide, the listener, naturally, loses track of the very sense of the question itself.

Second, "such a person, closely observing their own mind" means: closely observing, among these kinds of birth, the very mind of the aggregate of formations. "Everywhere finds only nonexistence, and, through this nonexistence, gains a kind of confirmation" means: they believe they have realized that every phenomenon, without exception, is nonexistent. And so, whenever someone comes to ask, they answer only with this nonexistence, and, apart from this, have nothing further to say at all.

Third, "through this existence, gains a kind of confirmation" means: reflecting that, after arising, there must inevitably follow a mark of ceasing, and that, after ceasing, there must inevitably follow a mark of arising once more, from this they confirm that everything, without exception, exists — this, then, is precisely this position of existence. The rest may be understood accordingly.

Fourth, such a person, observing the aggregate of formations, sees both existence and nonexistence together: seeing, on the one hand, the place where each successive thought arises, and, on the other, the place where each successive thought ceases — this realm resembling a tree branching into two, and so their own mind, too, becomes unsettled and confused. Whenever someone comes to ask, they answer accordingly: "seeing existence is itself seeing nonexistence" means that whatever arises must inevitably return to cessation; "yet, within this same nonexistence, it is not the case that existence is present either" means that whatever ceases does not necessarily arise again. "An entirely evasive equivocation, impossible ever to press to any conclusion": "evasive" here means stubbornly resistant, not according with principle; "confusion" means the mind possesses no true master or correctness at all. As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: within these four, the first and the fourth both speak in terms of a double possibility, leaning more toward confusion, yet, in the end, never according with principle, and so partaking of evasiveness as well; the second and third speak in terms of only a single, one-sided position, leaning more toward evasiveness, yet, in the end, never truly mastering or settling anything, and so alike falling into confusion. And so the text concludes, in summary: an entirely evasive equivocation, impossible ever to press to any conclusion through further questioning at all.

"Empty" here means a false, deviant calculation, carrying no real meaning whatsoever. Falling into this non-Buddhist teaching, confusing and disordering this true nature of bodhi, this, then, is named the fifth non-Buddhist school. Confused about right knowledge and right view, they establish, instead, this wrong knowledge and wrong view — hence, "these four inverted natures." Concluding, in the name given, "an all-encompassing, empty theory": this person's speculation reaches everywhere, exactly like mistaking a coiled rope for a snake — every bit of it an utterly empty, utterly false theory. This closes the fifth position, this endless evasive equivocation.

6. Sixteen Forms of "Existence After Death" 死後有相 — Observing this endless flow of the aggregate of formations, without yet exhausting it, such a person falls into holding that some form continues to exist after death — an inversion of thought. This position takes shape in four ways: taking the body as this self; taking this self as vast, encompassing every land, with form belonging to it; taking every prior object as belonging to this self, following it wherever it turns; or taking this self as dwelling within the aggregate of formations' own continuity, so that this self is said to dwell within form. Each of these four positions, applied in turn to all four remaining aggregates, yields sixteen such forms altogether — and from this, they further conclude that affliction remains, always, affliction, and bodhi remains, always, bodhi, the two natures running on forever side by side, never once touching each other.

Commentary: "This endless flow" refers to the aggregate of formations' own unbroken continuity, flowing on without rest. "Giving rise to speculation" means concluding that this aggregate of formations, being the very root of every movement, will, in the future, be able to give rise to every further movement as well — and so they conclude that form, sensation, and perception, these three aggregates, though already ceased in the present, must certainly arise again in the future — hence, "such a person falls into holding that some form continues to exist after death, an inversion of thought." As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: true awakening to the unborn knows that the very first arising already contains its own cessation within it — knowing, thereby, that even birth itself is empty and formless, how much less, then, could one speak of "after death," and falsely conclude that some form continues to exist there!

These four positions correspond to four of the sixty-two views held by non-Buddhist traditions. First: holding firmly, guarding fixedly to this bodily form, declaring that this form composed of the four elements is, entirely, this self — this position takes form itself to be this self. Second: seeing this self's own nature as perfectly fused, encompassing every land of the ten directions, declaring that this self possesses form — this position takes this self as vast and form as small, form contained within this self. Third: taking every object presently before them, following this self wherever it turns and returns — that is, its very function — declaring that form belongs to this self: since form belongs to this self, this plainly makes form something this self possesses, not this self itself — this position takes this self as separate from form. Fourth: taking this self as dwelling within the aggregate of formations' own flowing continuity, declaring that this self dwells within form — since the aggregate of formations' own continuous mark is precisely the aggregate of form itself — this position takes form as vast and this self as small, this self contained within form.

This means: though this physical body has died, this self, they hold, still remains present even now. As with the aggregate of form, so too with the remaining three — and so, applying these same four positions to all four remaining aggregates in turn, cycling through each, this yields, together, sixteen such forms in all. Having, above, seen the aggregate of formations as endless, they conclude, likewise, that the three prior aggregates are, equally, endless, all alike taking on this continued existence. This is further extended still, to conclude that every phenomenon, without exception, is the same: affliction encompasses every defiled phenomenon entirely, and bodhi encompasses every pure phenomenon entirely, and so affliction and bodhi, too, must follow this same principle — affliction remaining, to the very end, affliction, and bodhi remaining, to the very end, bodhi, with absolutely no possibility of change between them. And so these two natures, truth and falseness, are held to run on forever side by side — proceeding together without ever conflicting, never once touching or interfering with each other at all.

Through this conclusion — some form continuing to exist after death — they fall into this non-Buddhist teaching, these sixteen forms: truth remaining, to the very bottom, truth, and falseness remaining, to the very bottom, falseness, truth and falseness each independently established, with no possibility of ever transforming this false phenomenon of affliction into this true nature of bodhi — hence, "confuse their own very nature of bodhi." This, then, is named the sixth non-Buddhist school. "Established among the five aggregates" gathers together all five in this conclusion, though this position, in truth, rests only on the first four; and, though resting on these four, its true meaning lies in the aggregate of formations alone. Establishing this theory on this basis, proceeding from an inverted mind — this being precisely a mind-demon wreaking its own mischief — what, indeed, can be done about it? This closes the sixth position, these sixteen forms of existence after death.

7. Eight Forms of "No Existence After Death" 死後無相 — Having already extinguished form, sensation, and perception, such a person, giving rise to speculation regarding these already-vanished aggregates, falls into holding that no form remains at all after death — an inversion of thought. Seeing form itself already ceased, this bodily form has no cause left to depend upon; observing perception already ceased, this mind has nothing left to bind it; knowing sensation already ceased, there is no further linkage remaining at all — the very nature of these aggregates dissolved and scattered, so that even should some remaining principle of life persist, without sensation or perception present, this would be no different from grass or wood. Since even this present substance cannot, in truth, be found at all, how, then, could there be any further form remaining after death? Verifying this against what has already ceased, they conclude that, after death, no form remains at all — and, cycling through this same reasoning, this yields eight such formless positions altogether, and, from this, they further conclude that nirvāṇa itself, cause and fruit alike, are entirely empty, mere names with nothing real behind them at all, ending, in the end, in utter annihilation.

Commentary: "These already-vanished aggregates" refers to seeing the three prior aggregates already ceased, and giving rise to a false, calculated speculation from this: since these three aggregates existed before and are absent now, by the same reasoning, one infers that the aggregate of formations, present now, must likewise be absent in the future. And so, concluding that after death everything, in the end, returns to utter annihilation, such a person falls into holding that no form remains at all after death. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this position is the exact opposite counterpart of the one before it, turning existence into nonexistence instead: the position above observed the aggregate of formations, not yet ceased, seeing it as endless, and, from this, inferred that the three prior aggregates, and every phenomenon besides, must likewise be endless; this position observes the three prior aggregates, already ceased, seeing them as formless, and, from this, infers that the aggregate of formations, and every phenomenon besides, must likewise be formless. "An inversion of thought" means: this violates the Buddha's own sincere teaching of cultivating cause to realize fruit, and instead establishes this non-Buddhist, empty, annihilationist theory — hence, "an inversion of thought."

Form is the form composed of the four elements; the body is this same single, bodily form. The body depends on form for its existence — seeing this form composed of the four elements ceased, this body, thereby, has no cause left to depend upon. Perception is the perceiving of mind-consciousness; mind is this mind of the mental faculty. This mind is bound by this perception — observing this perception of mind-consciousness ceased, this mind, thereby, has nothing left to bind it. Form comes before sensation, and mind comes after it; with sensation present in between, form and mind can thereby be linked together — knowing this sensation once ceased, form and mind, thereby, are no longer linked at all. Based on this, the very nature of these three prior aggregates, already dissolved and scattered entirely, even should the aggregate of formations still remain, though this be some remaining principle of life, without sensation or perception present, there would be no awareness at all, no different from grass or wood. As Wenling puts it: "the very nature of these aggregates dissolved and scattered" means form, sensation, and perception all ceased; "this remaining principle of life" refers to the aggregate of formations — meaning that, without sensation or perception, formations, too, must likewise cease.

"This substance" refers to the present aggregates of form and mind together, not the aggregate of form alone. Now, within this samādhi, seeing that all four aggregates, in the present, have no form whatsoever to be found, how, then, could there be any further form remaining after death? Verifying, through this, what has already ceased, and knowing what has not yet ceased in the present, comparing this against the future, they conclude that these aggregates' own form, after death, must certainly be entirely absent. Cycling through this same reasoning, back and forth, examining each in turn, every one of the four aggregates is found formless both before birth and after death — and so form, sensation, perception, and formations, these four aggregates together, yield eight such formless positions in all.

Since even this present substance is empty, with no cause of cultivation to be found, and since this form after death is likewise empty, with no fruit of realization to be found, they conclude, from this, that nirvāṇa itself, cause and fruit alike — every worldly and transcendent phenomenon — is entirely empty, nothing more than an empty name, with no real substance behind it at all, ending, in the end, in utter annihilation. Annihilation is a great wrong view, denying cause and effect entirely — for every single phenomenon, without exception, is never apart from cause and effect. As it is said: "boundless emptiness, denying cause and effect — reckless and unrestrained, this invites calamity and misfortune." This closes the seventh position, these eight formless positions.

8. Eight Forms of "Neither Existence nor Non-Existence" 死後俱非 — Since the aggregate of formations still remains present while sensation and perception have both already ceased, such a person speculates on both existence and nonexistence at once, each undermining the other's own substance — and so falls into holding that death is neither one thing nor the other, giving rise to an inverted theory. Within form, sensation, and perception, they see an existence that is not, in truth, existence; within the aggregate of formations' own flowing continuity, they observe a nonexistence that is not, in truth, nonexistence. Cycling through this same reasoning, exhausting the very boundary of every aggregate, this yields eight such neither-nor positions in all, so that, whichever aggregate is taken as the point of reference, they conclude, in every case, that death is neither some form's existence nor its nonexistence. And, further, concluding that the very nature of every formation is subject to this same shifting confusion, their mind gives rise to a kind of penetrating awakening — existence and nonexistence both alike denied, with no way left to settle what is true or what is false.

Commentary: As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "the aggregate of formations still remains present" means: seeing that the aggregate of formations has not yet ceased, its own boundary still plainly present; "sensation and perception have both already ceased" means: seeing that the three prior aggregates have already ceased, their substance and mark alike entirely empty. "Speculating on both existence and nonexistence at once" means: taking what remains present as existence, and what has ceased as nonexistence. "Each undermining the other's own substance" means: using the aggregate of formations' own existence to undermine the three prior aggregates' own nonexistence, and using the three prior aggregates' own nonexistence to undermine the aggregate of formations' own existence. In the end, falling into this neither-nor position means: undermining nonexistence results in "not nonexistence"; undermining existence results in "not existence" — and so this inverted theory arises.

This directly explains this meaning of each undermining the other's own substance: since the three prior aggregates have already ceased, within form, sensation, and perception, they see the aggregate of formations' own existence as, likewise, ceased along with them — and so, "not existence." Since this last aggregate still remains, within the aggregate of formations' own flowing continuity, they observe these three aggregates' own nonexistence as, likewise, present along with it — and so, "not nonexistence."

"Cycling through this same reasoning, exhausting the very boundary of every aggregate" means: cycling in this way, observing what came before from what comes after, and what comes after from what came before, exhausting the very boundary of form, sensation, perception, and formations, these four aggregates, they arrive at this neither-existence-nor-nonexistence position, yielding eight such neither-nor positions in all — that is, neither the existence nor the nonexistence of form, sensation, perception, and formations. "Whichever aggregate is taken as the point of reference" means: whichever single aggregate is raised up as the object of reference, they conclude, in every case, that death holds neither existence nor nonexistence of form.

"Every formation" here refers to every phenomenon generally, not the aggregate of formations alone — what follows extends this example to every phenomenon: the very nature of every phenomenon is, without exception, subject to this same shifting, confused change — existence is not, in truth, existence, and nonexistence is not, in truth, nonexistence either — and, regarding this, they claim to know and see it completely. "Their mind gives rise to a kind of penetrating awakening" is not a genuine penetrating awakening at all, but merely an increase of wrong knowledge and wrong understanding, existence and nonexistence both alike denied. Should someone then ask: which, then, is false, and which is true? — they would be unable to answer this either, hence, "with no way left to settle what is true or what is false."

Through this conclusion, they take what holds true before life to hold equally after death, both, in every case, neither existent nor nonexistent. "With no way left to see clearly what lies beyond" means precisely this same darkness and obscurity, with no correct principle left to state at all — unable to say existence, unable to say nonexistence either. This makes plain that this neither-nor position, in every case, cannot, in truth, be spoken of at all, and so they fall into this non-Buddhist teaching, confusing this true nature of bodhi. This, then, is named the eighth non-Buddhist school. Established among the five aggregates, this neither-nor position after death is, in truth, a theory established entirely from an inverted mind. This closes the eighth position, these eight neither-nor positions.

9. Seven Theories of Annihilation 七際斷滅 — Speculating regarding what comes after what comes after — that is, what ultimately, finally, becomes nothing at all — such a person falls into these seven theories of annihilation: concluding that the body itself ceases entirely; or that desire, once exhausted, ceases entirely; or that suffering, once exhausted, ceases entirely; or that the very utmost bliss ceases entirely; or that the very utmost equanimity ceases entirely. Cycling through this same reasoning, exhausting all seven of these boundaries, they conclude that everything presently existing is, in the end, extinguished — and, once extinguished, never to return again.

Commentary: "What comes after what comes after — that is, what ultimately becomes nothing at all" — as Wenling puts it: seeing the place where the aggregate of formations ceases, thought by thought, they name this "what ultimately becomes nothing at all," and, from this, falsely conclude that the seven realms of birth among humans and heavenly beings all, in the end, undergo this same annihilation. This position differs from the seventh, the formless position, discussed above: that one reasoned from the three prior aggregates; this one reasons from the aggregate of formations itself. And that one inferred backward into the past, in order to settle the matter of what comes after death; this one observes forward into the future, thought by thought becoming extinguished — and so concludes that every single place, everywhere, holds some point of final annihilation.

These seven realms of annihilation are: concluding that the body itself ceases entirely — referring to the four continents and the first two of the six desire-heavens; or that desire, once exhausted, ceases entirely — referring to the first dhyāna, the ground free of birth, possessed of joy and bliss, since desire and its taint are already exhausted there, having already departed from birth within the desire realm; or that suffering, once exhausted, ceases entirely — referring to the second dhyāna, the ground where samādhi gives rise to joy and bliss, its joy at its very utmost, free of any anxiety; or that the very utmost bliss ceases entirely — referring to the third dhyāna, the ground free of joy, possessed of wondrous bliss, since even bliss must eventually come to its own end; or that the very utmost equanimity ceases entirely — referring to the fourth dhyāna, the ground of letting go of thought, purity, cultivating this letting-go samādhi, both suffering and bliss alike let go of and gone — together with the four formless heavens, since even the very obstruction of material substance is let go of there as well.

Cycling through this same reasoning, pressing this investigation to its very utmost limit across these seven realms, they conclude that everything presently existing returns, in every case, to annihilation — these seven realms all displaying this same annihilation — and so they know that, after death, in the future, there can never be any further matter of rebirth at all. This closes the ninth position, these seven realms of annihilation.

10. Five Theories of "Present" Nirvāṇa 五現涅槃 — Speculating regarding what comes after what comes after — that is, what continues to exist — such a person falls into these five theories of nirvāṇa: taking the desire realm as the true basis of return, since observing this perfect brightness there gives rise to a longing devotion for it; or taking the first dhyāna as such, since its very nature is free of anxiety; or the second dhyāna, since its mind is free of suffering; or the third dhyāna, since its very utmost delight accords perfectly with every wish; or the fourth dhyāna, since both suffering and bliss are, there, both alike gone, no longer subject to the very nature of rebirth's arising and ceasing at all. Mistaking these heavens, still subject to outflow, for the unconditioned itself, they conclude that these five realms offer perfect security, the very basis relied upon by the most supreme and pure of all — and, cycling through this same reasoning, conclude that these five realms represent the very ultimate end of the path.

Commentary: "What comes after what comes after — that is, what continues to exist" is the exact reverse of the position discussed before it: the position above held that, since the aggregate of formations flows on, thought by thought, never remaining fixed, what comes after must therefore inevitably cease; this position holds that, since the aggregate of formations continues, thought by thought, without a single interruption, what comes after must therefore inevitably continue to exist — hence, "what comes after what comes after — that is, what continues to exist," bound, they believe, to yield some real fruit, certainly never extinguished into nothingness. "Such a person falls into these five theories of nirvāṇa" means: rather than waiting to reduce the body to ash and extinguish the intellect entirely in order to enter nirvāṇa, they take these five presently conceived realms themselves to be nirvāṇa already.

This position takes these five realms, wherever one is presently born, to be nirvāṇa itself — showing plainly that this is not held by a single person alone, but that different people, speculating across all five realms, each follow their own particular view, some concluding this of only a single realm alone. "The desire realm" here refers to the six desire-heavens generally, not any single heaven in particular. "The true basis of return" means falsely taking this to be the true realm of nirvāṇa, since nirvāṇa is the very name, within the Buddha's own teaching, for turning from birth-and-death to rely upon nirvāṇa instead — this resembling how the Daoist immortal traditions hold that, above the six desire-heavens, there is no birth and death at all. This clause ought to apply to all four of the remaining realms that follow as well, each of them holding this same view in turn.

Why is the desire realm taken as this true basis of return? Because, observing this perfect brightness there, a longing devotion for it arises. Since, formerly, the aggregate of perception has already broken through, this perfect samādhi brought to light, and, having newly attained the heavenly eye, they behold everywhere this heavenly light, pure and adorned, transcending utterly the brightness of sun and moon, and, moreover, departed entirely from the filth and turbidity of the human world — and so a mind of longing devotion arises, and they conclude this realm to be nirvāṇa, present here and now. Or they take the first dhyāna, the ground free of birth, possessed of joy and bliss, where suffering and affliction do not press upon them, to be nirvāṇa, present here and now. Or they take the second dhyāna, the ground where samādhi gives rise to joy and bliss, where anxiety does not press upon them, to be nirvāṇa, present here and now. (The present text saying the first dhyāna is "free of anxiety" and the second "free of suffering" is most likely a transposition error in translation.) Or they take the third dhyāna, the ground free of joy, possessed of wondrous bliss, its very utmost delight according perfectly with every wish, meaning this utmost joy and delight, this great accord attained, to be nirvāṇa, present here and now. Or they take the fourth dhyāna, the ground of letting go of thought, purity, cultivating this letting-go samādhi, both suffering and bliss alike let go of and gone, and so conclude that the three calamities cannot reach here, no longer subject to rebirth's very nature of arising and ceasing at all, to be nirvāṇa, present here and now.

All five of these realms lie, in fact, within heavens still subject to outflow; now, mistaking these heavens still subject to outflow, they take them, instead, to be the unconditioned itself, nirvāṇa itself. "These five realms offer perfect security" means: having mistakenly taken these five realms to be nirvāṇa, the unconditioned, they thereupon take them to be their own secure home-country! "The very basis relied upon by the most supreme and pure of all" means: taken to be the very basis relied upon by the most supreme, purest being of all — that most supreme, pure being being, precisely, the Buddha, since the Buddha relies upon nirvāṇa itself. Proceeding in this way, from the lowest realm upward, reasoning through each in turn, there is a cyclical quality to this reasoning. "These five realms represent the very ultimate end of the path" means: having reasoned through these five realms, they mistakenly conclude that each represents the very utmost, ultimate fruit, unsurpassed by anything else at all.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: first, regarding the six desire-heavens, having only just departed from the dust and filth of the human world, they falsely take this to be true purity. Next, regarding the first and second dhyānas, having only just departed from the anxiety and suffering of the lower realm, they falsely take this to be true bliss. Next, regarding the third dhyāna, having only just attained this accord and freedom, they falsely take this to be a true self. Next, regarding the fourth dhyāna, having attained, for a time, freedom from the destruction of the three calamities, they falsely take this to be true permanence, free of arising and ceasing. This is precisely mistaking impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self, and impurity for permanence, bliss, self, and purity instead.

"Five theories of present nirvāṇa" means: concluding that these five realms are, each, already nirvāṇa in the present moment, already presently receiving the very bliss of quiescent cessation, with nothing further left to wait for in the future at all. "Falling into a non-Buddhist teaching, confusing this very nature of bodhi" means: bodhi and nirvāṇa, these two, are precisely the Tathāgata's own twin fruits of wisdom and severance — now, having mistaken this falseness for truth, they will inevitably come to mistake this truth for falseness as well, hence, "confuse this very nature of bodhi." This, then, is named the tenth non-Buddhist school. Established among the five aggregates, this theory of five present nirvāṇas is, in truth, a theory established entirely from an inverted mind, this false speculation. This closes the second section, these ten intervening speculations.

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these ten wild interpretations in meditation are all produced by the interaction of effort and the aggregate of formations, and so these particular realizations appear."

Commentary: As Wenling puts it: earlier, this text spoke of "states appearing in meditation" — that is, heavenly demons watching for their opening; here, it speaks instead of "wild interpretations in meditation" — that is, a mind-demon giving rise to its own deep affliction of its own accord. Whenever seeing the path is not yet genuine, and one strays down some forked path into false speculation, this is exactly such a wild interpretation — this being what is called a mind-demon, most urgently to be guarded against.

"All produced by the interaction of effort and the aggregate of formations" means: this all comes about because perception has broken through and formations have manifested, using the power of samādhi and wisdom to advance toward truth and cut off falseness, precisely at the very moment truth and falseness contend against each other, each in turn gaining the upper hand — and so these particular realizations appear. "These particular realizations" refers precisely to these wild interpretations themselves.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: surveying these ten wild interpretations as a whole, none of them go beyond these four terms — annihilation, permanence, emptiness, and existence. The first five belong to annihilation and permanence; the last five belong to emptiness and existence. The first is the view of annihilation; the second, the view of permanence; the third, a double affirmation of both; the fourth and fifth both extend and broaden this same double affirmation further still.

Question: why is there no double negation among them? Answer: annihilation and permanence are both, alike, faults; were there a double negation of both, this would constitute a correct view free of fault, not a non-Buddhist position at all. The sixth clings to existence; the seventh clings to emptiness; the eighth is a double negation of both. Question: why, then, is this eighth position not itself a correct view free of fault? Answer: existence and emptiness are not, in themselves, necessarily faults — a fault arises only through one-sided clinging to either; and this eighth position's own double negation refers, in fact, to this same confusion and obscurity regarding the aggregate that follows, an indecision between existence and nonexistence — not the middle way's own genuine double negation at all, and so this is not a correct view. The ninth extends this to a final, complete annihilation into emptiness; the tenth extends this to a final, complete clinging to existence — and, were emptiness and existence, too, gathered back into annihilation and permanence, this would leave, in the end, nothing more than these same two views, annihilation and permanence, after all.

Sūtra: "Beings, stubborn and confused, fail to measure themselves honestly; meeting these states appearing before them, they mistake confusion for genuine understanding, declaring themselves to have ascended to sagehood — committing the offense of great false speech, and falling into the unrelenting hell for it."

Commentary: "Stubborn and confused" means possessing a nature of stubborn foolishness by its very constitution, difficult to bring to genuine awakening. Confusion and delusion cover over the mind, easily giving rise to such wild interpretations, precisely because they fail to reflect and measure themselves honestly — who, in truth, am I? Meeting these states appearing before them, they mistake this very confusion for genuine understanding, claiming realization not yet actually attained, declaring themselves to have ascended to sagehood, falsely claiming some sagely fruit — and, this offense of great false speech thereby committed, they will certainly fall into the unrelenting hell — how truly regrettable!

For a practitioner to cultivate all the way to the point where perception has broken through and formations have manifested, with external demons entirely powerless against them, requires who knows how many years and months of effort — exactly like one removing layer after layer of clothing: having already shed the topmost three layers, only two remain, this effort already more than halfway complete — and yet, at this very point, to speak falsely, claiming to have ascended to sagehood, and thereby bring about one's own downfall — how could this not be truly lamentable!

Sūtra: "You must take these very words of the Tathāgata and, after my passing, transmit and proclaim them throughout the age of the Dharma's decline, so that every sentient being everywhere may awaken to this teaching. Do not let a self-generated demon of the mind take root and grow — instead, protect and guide practitioners, and dissolve wrong views at their source."

Commentary: This charges Ānanda to act as a spiritual guide breaking down wrong views, so that what has not yet arisen never arises, and what has already arisen is swiftly extinguished. Charging Ānanda: you, and everyone else gathered in this assembly, must take these very words of the Tathāgata and, after the Tathāgata's own passing, transmit and proclaim them throughout the age of the Dharma's decline, since, as the age of the sages recedes ever further into the past, demons grow strong and the Dharma grows weak.

"So that every sentient being everywhere may awaken to this teaching" means: awakening clearly and thoroughly to the meaning of these ten wild interpretations — once one is able to awaken to this clearly, one is naturally able to discern it, deviant and correct plainly distinguished; the very moment a false thought first sprouts, one ought, at once, to resist it with correct samādhi. "Do not let" here carries the sense of a prohibition. "A self-generated demon of the mind" refers precisely to these ten wild interpretations themselves — meaning that, though external demons cannot disturb a practitioner at this stage, one must not allow this demon of one's own mind to take root and grow of its own accord. "Affliction" here means the very sprouting of calamity, since this is able to become the very cause of hell. As the Book of Documents states: "calamity brought by heaven may still be escaped; calamity brought by oneself cannot be survived." To speak here of it arising "of its own accord" is precisely this same calamity brought upon oneself. Everything above teaches that what has not yet arisen should never be allowed to arise. "Protect and guide practitioners, and dissolve wrong views at their source" teaches that what has already arisen should be swiftly extinguished — preserving and protecting this samādhi, guiding and shielding this continued cultivation, dissolving this deviant, wrong view at its very root.

Sūtra: "Teach them, in both body and mind, to awaken to the true meaning, so that they meet no forked path on the unsurpassed way. Do not let their own hearts settle for some small attainment as though it were complete — hold up instead the pure standard of the great, awakened king himself."

Commentary: This charges Ānanda to act as a teacher guiding others toward the truth, teaching them to give rise to right wisdom where it has not yet arisen. Personally investigating, in both body and mind, this true, real meaning, turning away entirely from belonging to annihilation, permanence, emptiness, or existence — simply letting not a single thought arise, turning this light of awareness back to illuminate this nature, flowing straight into the very center of the middle way, meeting no forked path at all upon this unsurpassed path of buddhahood. A branch growing sideways from a tree is called a "fork"; a road dividing crookedly is called a "byway" — neither being the true, straight root or path at all. Do not let their own hearts settle, within, for some small attainment, taken as though it were already complete. For to meet such a forked path is, precisely, to fall into a non-Buddhist teaching; and to settle for some small attainment is, precisely, to fall into the lesser vehicle instead. And so Ānanda, the one to whom this teaching is directly addressed, is charged to hold up the pure standard of the great, awakened king himself. Not a single thought arising is called purity; swiftly hastening toward unsurpassed bodhi, becoming this great, awakened king, is held up as the very standard for both humans and heavenly beings alike, showing the very compass-point toward buddhahood itself. This closes the fourth section, the demonic states of the aggregate of formations.
