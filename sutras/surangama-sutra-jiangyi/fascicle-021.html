From Human Debt to the Summit of the Formless Realm

The seven-destinies teaching runs to its conclusion: humans returning from the animal realm still marked by their old habits, the ten kinds of immortals who cultivate long life instead of true awakening (with Yuanying's own telling of how Lü Dongbin, the immortal, came to bow before Chan Master Huanglong), every heaven of the desire, form, and formless realms named and defined in turn, and the four kinds of asuras. The Buddha then closes the entire teaching with a single reversal: none of these seven destinies, for all their exhaustive detail, has ever had any real, independent existence at all.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 20 closed with hell, hungry ghosts, and animals — the three destinies a being falls into through the heavier grades of emotion over thought. Fascicle 21 completes the remaining four: humans returning from the animal realm to reclaim old debts, still marked by the same ten habits that sent them there in the first place; the ten kinds of immortals, who cultivate concentration and diet rather than true awakening in pursuit of a longer, not truly deathless, life (closing with Yuanying's own telling of how the immortal Lü Dongbin came to bow before Chan Master Huanglong, having tried and failed to behead him); every heaven of the desire realm, the eighteen heavens of the form realm across four levels of dhyāna, and the four formless heavens at the very summit of conditioned existence, each named and defined by its own precise degree of remaining desire or subtlety of concentration; and the four kinds of asuras, classified not by any single realm but by the very manner of their birth. The fascicle then closes the entire seven-destinies teaching, begun back in fascicle 20, with a single reversal: after cataloguing every destiny in this exhaustive, near-bureaucratic detail, the Buddha states plainly that none of it was ever anything more than a single false thought, self-summoned and self-received, with no more independent reality than a flower conjured in the sky.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. This fascicle's sūtra text overlaps substantially with material the companion bare-sūtra project already translated in its own fascicles 8 and 9 — the ten immortals, the six desire-realm heavens, the eighteen form-realm heavens, the four formless heavens, the four asuras, and the closing "all seven destinies are self-created" passage. Wherever Yuanying quotes the identical CBETA wording already translated there, that existing translation is reused verbatim; because that companion project condenses the eighteen form-realm heavens (and, to a lesser extent, the immortals) to summary grids, this fascicle supplies Yuanying's own considerably fuller clause-by-clause commentary throughout.

The Human Destiny: Reclaiming an Old Debt 辛四 諸人趣 · 壬初 躡前警起

Chinese Source

〖經〗復次，阿難！從是畜生，酬償先債，若彼酬者，分越所酬，此等眾生，還復為人，反徵其剩。

〖講〗《寶鏡疏》云：人者忍也，謂於世間，違順情境，能安忍故。若論人趣，亦有多種，約而言之，有三差別：一是五戒，及中品為因，直克其果。二是從勝類中來，如聖賢示現，天仙謫降之類。三是惡道中來，如地獄、鬼畜及阿修羅。今且單約從畜復形者，以詳明之。《正脈》云：以人趣為修進通途，諸趣皆願為之，求轉身之速也。故諸佛但於人中成佛，裴公序《圓覺》云：整心慮，趣菩提，唯人道為能耳！且勝劣無量差別，富貴慈善者似天，聰明者似仙，剛暴者似修羅，愚癡者似畜，貧賤者似鬼，囚繫者似獄。夫相似既多，則知來處必多。今亦順序而談，故偏取從畜來者。夫此文頗似畜生趣餘文，再四研求，前趣之文已結，當是躡前警起之文，故以復次阿難起之。觀文中徵剩索命二意，乃諸畜所以潤生為人也。言警起者，是我佛欲人警悟，於諸畜生，不可鞭策，過用其力，瞋怒枉殺其身也。負債應分兩種：一負物債，反復徵償。二負命債，殺食不已。從是畜生，酬償先債者：此負物債也；酬償先世物債，債畢即停，兩不相干矣。若彼酬者，分越所酬：越超過也，超過所應償之分。此等眾生，還復為人，反徵其剩者：即乘此因緣，反復為人，索還其前生餘剩之債矣。《正脈》問：酬滿壽終，或轉別生，則無可越。若酬滿，而彼不死不轉，則凡心何以知其當止乎？答：餵養不到，非禮苦役，鞭策過度，則必越分，於此切宜存心，至於死轉，必有冥冥主宰，不足慮也。

〖經〗如彼有力，兼有福德，則於人中，不捨人身，酬還彼力，若無福者，還為畜生，償彼餘直。

〖講〗此償物債，而分勝劣。勝者：人中償還；劣者：為畜償還。如彼有力，兼有福德者：彼指被徵之人；有力指有善業之力，兼有福而仁，有德而賢，不必捨命，則於人趣之中，不捨人身，酬還彼索債者，前世過用之力也。如被盜賊劫奪，親友負欠等皆是。此約勝者說。若無福者下：此約劣者說。若無善業之力，及無福德者，則難保人身，還為畜生，或被驅役，或被售賣，償彼前世餘剩之債，故曰餘值。《指掌疏》云：則現前過用得意，即當來反償之不得意，普願舉世仁人君子，當其得意時，須防有不得意時耳。初負債反復徵償竟。

〖經〗阿難當知：若用錢物，或役其力，償足自停。

〖講〗此重明剩債易償，言反徵可停。阿難當知者：借阿難以警世人也。若用錢物者：言被徵之人，若有善業之力，不捨人身，而反徵者，不過用其錢物而已。若無福者，則為畜生，而反徵之人，或應役其身力，故曰或役其力。是皆可以償足，自然停止，但不至分越所酬而已。

〖經〗如其中間，殺彼身命，或食其肉，如是乃至，經微塵劫，相食相誅，猶如轉輪，互為高下，無有休息。

〖講〗此正明負命難解。設或為其中間，殺彼身命，食其血肉，如是生生世世，經微塵劫數，怨習難忘。以肉還肉，故互來相食；以命還命，故互來相誅。遞相報復，無有已時；猶旋轉車輪一般，互為人畜，循環高下，無有休息。

〖經〗除奢摩他，及佛出世，不可停寢。

〖講〗此唯許佛法能止。奢摩他，為自性本定，法也；佛出世，為照世明燈，佛也；除此佛法之外，不可停寢。奢摩他開解照了，此自性天然本定，返妄契真，能止生死業苦，明自力得解也。遇佛出世，為眾生說法，能解歷劫冤愆，明他力得解也。佛意警誡，不宜恣意殺生食肉也。初躡前警起竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Furthermore, Ānanda, once such beings, having become animals, have repaid their former debt — should the one repaid receive more than what was actually owed, these same beings, in turn, become human once more, and reclaim this same excess."

Commentary: As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: "human" means to endure — that is, able to bear patiently whatever favorable or adverse circumstance arises in this world. Speaking broadly of this human destiny, there are, in fact, several kinds; briefly, three distinctions: first, those who take the five precepts, together with a middling grade of goodness, as their direct cause, straightforwardly bringing about this same fruit; second, those who come from some superior class, such as a sage or worthy manifesting in this form, or an immortal or deva sent down in exile; and third, those who come from some evil destiny, such as hell, ghosts, animals, or asuras. This present passage, though, speaks specifically and solely of those who return to human form from the animal realm, unpacking this in detail.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: the human destiny serves as the common thoroughfare for advancing in cultivation, and every destiny alike wishes to attain it, seeking this swift transformation of the body. And so it is that every buddha accomplishes buddhahood only in the human realm. As Pei Xiu's own preface to the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment puts it: settling the mind and its every thought, advancing toward bodhi — only the human path is truly capable of this! And, further, superior and inferior differ without limit: the wealthy, honored, and compassionate resemble the heavens; the intelligent resemble immortals; the harsh and violent resemble asuras; the foolish and deluded resemble animals; the poor and lowly resemble ghosts; and the imprisoned and bound resemble hell. Since there are so many resemblances as these, one thus knows that the places beings come from must, likewise, be many. This passage, too, follows this same order, and so specifically takes up those coming from the animal realm.

This passage rather resembles the remaining text of the animal-destiny section above; examining it again and again, since the account of the prior destiny has already been concluded, this ought properly to be this same passage carrying forward what precedes, giving this warning — hence it begins with "furthermore, Ānanda." Observing, within the text, these two meanings of reclaiming an excess and demanding a life, this is exactly how animals come to nourish life and become human once more. As for this word "warning": our own Buddha wishes to warn people not to whip and drive animals, using their strength to excess, harming and needlessly killing their bodies in anger. This matter of debt properly divides into two kinds: first, owing a debt of goods, reclaimed and repaid in turn; second, owing a debt of life, killed and eaten without end. "Once such beings, having become animals, have repaid their former debt" refers to this same debt of goods: once this former debt of goods is repaid, the debt is settled, and the two parties are no longer entangled with one another at all.

"Should the one repaid receive more than what was actually owed" — "excess" means surpassing; surpassing the portion actually owed. "These same beings, in turn, become human once more, and reclaim this same excess" means that, riding upon this same cause and condition, they, in turn, become human once more, reclaiming this excess left over from a former life. As the Standard Meaning Commentary asks: once the debt is repaid in full and this life comes to its end, or is transformed into some other kind of birth, then there is nothing left to exceed. But should the debt be repaid in full, and yet that being neither dies nor is transformed, how, then, could an ordinary mind ever know when to stop? The answer: should the animal not be properly fed, forced into improper labor, or driven and whipped to excess, this will certainly exceed what is owed — and so one ought, in this matter, take the greatest care; as for death and transformation, there is certainly some hidden, unseen ruler over this, and there is no need to trouble oneself over it.

Sūtra: "Should the one so reclaiming possess strength and, further, possess merit and virtue, then, remaining human, without giving up their human body at all, they repay that same strength owed; should they lack merit, though, they become an animal once more, to repay this same remaining debt."

Commentary: This speaks of repaying a debt of goods, distinguished according to whether one is superior or inferior. The superior repay it while remaining human; the inferior repay it by becoming an animal. "Should the one so reclaiming possess strength and, further, possess merit and virtue" — "that one" refers to the person being reclaimed from; "strength" refers to the power of wholesome karma, together with merit that makes one kind, and virtue that makes one worthy — such a person need not give up their own life at all, but, remaining within the human destiny, without giving up their human body, repays that same strength once claimed from them — this same excessive strength used in some former life. This is as when one is robbed by a thief, or a friend fails to repay some debt owed, and the like — this speaks in terms of the superior. "Should they lack merit" below speaks in terms of the inferior: lacking the power of wholesome karma, and lacking merit and virtue, one is unable to preserve one's own human body, and becomes an animal once more, whether driven into labor or sold away, repaying this same remaining debt from a former life — hence, "this same remaining debt."

As the Detailed Commentary puts it: this present excess, used to one's own satisfaction, becomes, in time to come, this same repayment, to one's own dissatisfaction — may every benevolent man and gentleman throughout this world, at the very moment they find themselves satisfied, take care to guard against a time of dissatisfaction yet to come. This closes the first section, reclaiming an excess owed.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, you should know: whether repaid in money or goods, or through labor demanded of them, once the debt is repaid in full, this naturally comes to a stop."

Commentary: This further makes plain that an excess debt is easily repaid, and that this reclaiming may readily come to a stop. "Ānanda, you should know" means borrowing Ānanda as a means of warning the people of this world. "Whether repaid in money or goods" means that, should the person being reclaimed from possess the power of wholesome karma, and not give up their human body, then this reclaiming amounts to no more than the use of their money or goods. Should they lack merit, though, they become an animal, and this same person now reclaiming from them makes use of their own bodily strength — hence, "or through labor demanded of them." Either way, this debt can be repaid in full, and this reclaiming will naturally come to a stop of itself, so long as it does not exceed what is actually owed.

Sūtra: "But should one, in the meantime, kill that same being's own life, or devour its own flesh, then, in just this way, across kalpas as numerous as motes of dust, they devour one another and slaughter one another in turn, exactly like a turning wheel, rising and falling in turn, without end."

Commentary: This makes plain, directly, how difficult it is to resolve a debt of life. Should one, in the meantime, kill that being's own life, and devour its own flesh and blood, then, in this way, life after life, across kalpas as numerous as motes of dust, this habitual grudge is difficult ever to forget. Flesh repaid with flesh, and so they come, in turn, to devour one another; life repaid with life, and so they come, in turn, to slaughter one another — this retribution repaid again and again, with no end in sight at all — exactly like a turning cart-wheel, becoming, in turn, human and animal, cycling higher and lower without any rest at all.

Sūtra: "Only śamatha, or a buddha's own appearance in the world, can ever bring this to a stop."

Commentary: This grants that only the Buddha-dharma is able to bring this to a halt. Śamatha is this same samādhi inherently possessed by one's own nature — the Dharma itself; and a buddha's own appearance in the world is this same bright lamp illuminating the world — the Buddha himself. Apart from this Dharma and this Buddha, nothing else can ever bring this to a stop. Śamatha opens this understanding, illuminating it fully — this same naturally, inherently possessed samādhi, turning falseness back into accord with truth, is able to bring this suffering karma of birth and death to a stop, making plain that liberation is attained through one's own power. Encountering a buddha's own appearance in the world, expounding the Dharma for the sake of sentient beings, is able to resolve this grudge accumulated across kalpas, making plain that liberation is attained through another's power. This is the Buddha's own intent in giving this warning: that one ought not indulge freely in killing life and eating flesh. This closes the first section, carrying forward what precedes with this warning.

The Ten Classes of Humans Returning from the Animal Realm 壬二 詳列十類 · 壬三 結嘆可憐

Chinese Source

〖經〗汝今應知：彼梟倫者，酬足復形，生人道中，參合頑類。

〖講〗汝今應知者：此警告阿難，以及世人，應當以智而知，今畜報既盡，幸得為人，須及早省悟，不可仍依餘習，旋得旋失；直貫諸科，寄居於此。彼梟倫者：昔因惑習，為怪為梟者，今雖酬債已足，恢復本形；可見原從人道，墮落三途，現三途罪畢，生於人道之中；猶帶餘習，參雜混合於頑類。頑謂惡而且愚，不可化為一毫之善者也。參合頑類者：非彼一類，皆梟所化也。餘準此可知。

〖經〗彼咎徵者，酬足復形，生人道中，參合異類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼咎徵者：昔因淫習，為風魃為咎徵者。今雖酬債已足，恢復本形；可見原從人道，墮落三途，現三途罪畢，生於人道之中；猶帶餘習，參雜混合於異類。異類者，謂妖異之類，如身具二形，六根反常者是也。

〖經〗彼狐倫者，酬足復形，生人道中，參於庸類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼狐倫者：昔因誑習，為魅為狐者。今雖酬債已足，恢復本形；可見原從人道，墮落三途，現三途罪畢，生於人道之中；猶帶餘習，參雜混合於庸類。庸類者：無超拔之氣，媚世求榮，甘為庸鄙之類。

〖經〗彼毒倫者，酬足復形，生人道中，參合狠類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼毒倫者：昔因瞋習，為蠱鬼為毒物者，今雖酬債已足，復形人道；猶帶餘習，參雜混合於狠類。狠類者，剛暴自用，毫無仁慈之行也。

〖經〗彼蛔倫者，酬足復形，生人道中，參合微類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼蛔倫者：昔因冤習，為癘為蛔者。今雖酬債已足，復形人道；猶帶餘習，參合微類。微類者：卑微下賤，倡優婢僕，雖親附人，人不介意是也。

〖經〗彼食倫者，酬足復形，生人道中，參合柔類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼食倫者：昔因慢習，為餓鬼為食類者。今雖酬債已足，復形人道；猶帶餘習，參合柔類。柔類者：柔弱懦性，被世欺凌，不能卓立之類也。

〖經〗彼服倫者，酬足復形，生人道中，參合勞類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼服倫者：昔因枉習，為魘為服者。今雖酬債已足，復形人道；猶帶餘習，參合勞類。勞類者：勞苦不息，碌碌營生者是也。

〖經〗彼應倫者，酬足復形，生人道中，參於文類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼應倫者：昔因見習，為魍魎為應類者。今雖酬債已足，復形人道；猶帶餘習，參於文類。文類者：合轍云：小有才能，通文合禮，與人應接，不失其序，非經天緯地之大文也。

〖經〗彼休徵者，酬足復形，生人道中，參合明類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼休徵者：昔因詐習，為役使鬼，為休徵者。今雖酬債已足，復形人道；猶帶餘習，參合明類。明類者：乃世智辯聰，堪為世用，非仰觀俯察之大明也。

〖經〗彼諸循倫，酬足復形，生人道中，參於達類。

〖講〗汝今應知，彼諸循倫者：昔因訟習，為傳送鬼，為循類者。今雖酬債已足，復形人道，猶帶餘習，參於達類。達類者：《灌頂》云：諳練世故，了達人情，非博古窮今之大達也。二詳列十類竟。

〖經〗阿難！是等皆以宿債酬畢，復形人道，皆無始來，業計顛倒，相生相殺，不遇如來，不聞正法，於塵勞中，法爾輪轉，此輩名為，可憐愍者。

〖講〗此警示，既得為人，當念人身，難得而易失，切宜珍重愛惜！是等皆以宿債酬畢，復形人道者：是等，指上十類人倫，皆以宿生之債，酬償已畢，恢復人道本形。皆無始來，業計顛倒，相生相殺者：此指多生，皆從無始以來，惡業妄計，種種顛倒，不忘債與命也。為徵債而相生，如人死為羊，羊死為人也。為索命而相殺，如汝負我命，我還汝債也。不遇如來出世，不聞諸佛正法，悔過自新，無緣解脫。於塵勞之中，法爾輪轉者：然塵屬惑，有惑必有業，有業必有苦，如惡叉聚，自然不離，故云法爾輪轉。此輩名為可憐憫者：此輩幸得為人，凶多吉少，苦多樂少，若乃一念不慎，轉眼便成墮落；仙天無路可升，佛道從茲永隔，故名為可憐憫者。四諸人趣竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "You should know this now: those of the owl-like kind, once their debt is repaid in full, return to human form, born within the human path, mixed in among the obstinate kind."

Commentary: "You should know this now" warns Ānanda, and everyone in this world besides, that one ought to know this clearly through wisdom: now that this animal retribution is exhausted, and one is fortunate enough to become human once more, one ought to awaken to this early, and not simply continue on relying on this same remaining habit, only to gain it and lose it again just as quickly — this phrase governs every section that follows, situated here at the start. "Those of the owl-like kind" refers to those who, in the past, because of this habit of confused delusion, became the strange ghost and then the owl-like kind: now, though their debt is already repaid in full, restoring their original human form — one thus sees that they originally came from the human path, fell into the three lower destinies, and, this offense in those three destinies now settled, are born once more within the human path — still carrying this same remaining habit, mixed in among the obstinate kind. "Obstinate" means evil and, further, foolish, unable to be transformed toward even the slightest good. "Mixed in among the obstinate kind" does not mean that these alone, exclusively, are all transformed from owls — the rest should be understood the same way.

Sūtra: "Those who were once ill-omened creatures, once their debt is repaid in full, return to human form, born within the human path, mixed in among the strange kind."

Commentary: You should know this now: those who were once ill-omened creatures, because, in the past, of this habit of lust, became the drought-demon ghost and then an ill-omened creature. Now, though their debt is already repaid in full, restoring their original human form — one thus sees that they originally came from the human path, fell into the three lower destinies, and, this offense in those three destinies now settled, are born once more within the human path — still carrying this same remaining habit, mixed in among the strange kind. This closes the fourth section, the destiny of humans.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, there are, further, those who, coming from the human realm, do not rely on true awakening to cultivate samādhi, but cultivate instead some separate, false notion, fixing their own form through sheer concentration alone, roaming through mountains and forests, in places beyond human reach — there are ten kinds of such immortals."

Commentary: The Sanskrit word maṛci is here translated "immortal"; an immortal is one who shifts and moves on. Hence the Buddha tells Ānanda: there are, further, those coming from the human destiny — not these same ten classes described above, but those who, upon first attaining a human body, still carry these same remaining habits of these ten habitual causes. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: only from this destiny of immortals onward do beings have no further connection with these ten habits at all! Wishing to seek the way of the immortal, dreading impermanence, imagining the body to abide forever, they withdraw into the mountains and forests. "Not relying on true awakening to cultivate samādhi" means not relying on this original mind of awareness, giving rise to this initial wisdom of awakening, and cultivating this great Śūraṅgama samādhi, thereby realizing this ultimate, indestructible principle — but, instead, separately cultivating some false notion, manipulating one's own vital essence and spirit, fixing one's own form through sheer concentration, seeking a long life that never dies. Little do they realize that, while seeking a long life may indeed be possible, seeking never to die at all is, in truth, nothing but empty talk — even should one live a thousand or ten thousand years, this amounts to no more than dying somewhat later than others.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these beings, firm and unceasing in their reliance on ingested elixirs, perfect this path of diet — this is named the earth-walking immortal."

Commentary: "These beings" refers to those who separately cultivate this false basis; this phrase governs all ten sections together, situated here at the start. In the first five sections, this word "walking" is read in the level tone, referring to walking and moving on foot; in the last five sections, this same word is read in the departing tone, referring to the depth of one's own accomplished practice. "Firm and unceasing in their reliance on ingested elixirs" means fixing one's own bodily form firmly in concentration, relying on ingested medicinal elixirs — refining and preparing various substances into pills or cakes, ingesting them without cease, and thereby gaining their effect, called perfecting this path of diet. Not only does the whole body grow strong and healthy, and one's own years extended, but the body itself becomes light and one's own movement swift — hence, the earth-walking immortal, unable yet to rise into the sky. This, together with the four sections that follow, are distinguished according to how light or heavy, near or far, high or low, one's own walking becomes, thereby distinguishing superior from inferior.

Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in their reliance on herbs and plants, they perfect this path of medicine — this is named the flying immortal."

Commentary: "Firm and unceasing in their reliance on herbs and plants" means fixing one's own bodily form firmly in concentration, relying on ingested herbs and plants. This differs from the ingested elixirs above only in whether the substance is raw or cooked; here, one does not eat cooked food at all, but ingests only the purple mushroom, the yellow essence root, pine needles, and cypress leaves, ingesting them without cease, and thereby gaining their effect, called perfecting this path of medicine. Walking as though flying, rising to great heights and crossing over ravines, the body lighter than before — hence named the flying immortal.

Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in their reliance on metals and minerals, they perfect this path of transformation — this is named the wandering immortal."

Transcending the world to roam beyond it, and, through benefiting and rescuing others, roaming throughout the world besides — hence named the wandering immortal.

Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in their reliance on movement and stillness, they perfect this path of vital energy and essence — this is named the sky-walking immortal."

Commentary: "Firm and unceasing in their reliance on movement and stillness" means fixing one's own bodily form firmly in concentration, relying on this same movement and stillness — movement to circulate the breath and regulate the body, stillness to nourish this essence and settle the spirit; moving and resting in due season, one's own daily conduct carefully observed. Through this circulation and nourishment, unceasing, one first refines this essence back into vital energy, then refines this vital energy back into spirit, and finally refines this spirit back into emptiness itself — and so both vital energy and essence are, together, transformed, form and spirit both made wondrous, called perfecting this path of vital energy and essence. Riding upon clouds and driving dragons, roaming through the empty sky — hence named the sky-walking immortal.

Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in their reliance on bodily fluids, they perfect this path of moistening virtue — this is named the heaven-walking immortal."

As the Detailed Commentary puts it: of these five immortals above, the first two resemble, in their own tradition, the lesser vehicle, since they know only how to fix their own form firmly through concentration, entirely without any thought of benefiting others at all — distinguished from one another merely by whether cooked food is eaten or not. The latter three resemble, in their own tradition, the greater vehicle, since they, too, harbor this thought of benefiting others — yet, in their wandering, are still unable to forget the body itself, and so cannot reach far or rise to great heights. As for the sky-walking immortal, though form and spirit are both made wondrous, and one is able to rise to great heights and reach far distances, some measure of limitation still remains. But as for the heaven-walking immortal, this accords with the very virtue of heaven and earth themselves, this same function joined with the six vital forces, benefiting all things without ever ruling over them, dwelling within the very midst of the world while leaving no trace at all.

Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on refined essence, they perfect this path of absorbing this essence — this is named the communicating immortal."

Commentary: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on refined essence" means fixing one's own mind firmly, seeking this refined essence — gathering the very essence of the sun and moon, ingesting the colored radiance of clouds and mist, practicing this without cease, until this pure vital energy secretly penetrates throughout — this purity being exactly this same essence of the sun, moon, clouds, and mist — called perfecting this absorption of this pure essence. Form transformed together with vital energy, spirit communicating together with all things, passing through metal and stone, treading through water and fire, moving freely without obstruction, communicating and interacting with the very process of creation itself — hence named the communicating immortal. As the Inner Biography of Emperor Wu of the Han records: when Dongfang Shuo encountered the yellow-browed elder, the elder said to him: "I abstain from grain and ingest this refined vital energy, turning once every three thousand years, reversing my own bones and washing my own marrow; turning once every three thousand years, stripping away my own skin and refining my own hair — in my own life, I have already washed my marrow three times, and refined my hair three times! Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on mantras and restraints, they perfect this method of technique — this is named the way-walking immortal."

Commentary: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on mantras and restraints" means fixing one's own mind firmly, holding to mantras and restraints — holding mantras, one extends one's own life and lengthens one's own years, protecting the nation and blessing its people; observing restraints, one halts one's own craving and restrains one's own desire, subduing demons and expelling evil spirits — practicing this without cease, and thereby gaining its effect, called perfecting this method of technique. Mantra refers to incantations and techniques; method refers to restraining practices — reciting mantras over dates or writing talismans to cure illness, restraining poison and driving out demons to benefit sentient beings. Taking up this path to nourish one's own body, and extending this same path to aid the world — hence named the way-walking immortal.

Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on thought and recollection, they perfect this same thought and recollection — this is named the illuminating immortal."

Commentary: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on thought and recollection" means fixing one's own mind firmly, sunk in deep contemplation and still recollection, visualizing the crown of the head so that the spirit may emerge from it, and fixing the mind on the navel-wheel so as to refine the vital energy — that is, fixing the mind on the navel-wheel below, passing through the coccyx gate, rising to the double gates along the spine, all the way through to the very palace at the crown, thereby sending the spirit out through the very crown of the head; practicing this without cease, and thereby gaining its effect, called perfecting this same thought and recollection. The spirit moves freely in and out at will, this vital energy communicating both above and below, form and spirit reflecting and answering one another — hence named the illuminating immortal.

Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on sexual union, they perfect this response through it — this is named the essence-cultivating immortal."

Commentary: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on sexual union" means fixing one's own mind firmly, in order to accomplish this same union. As the Book of Changes puts it: the way of Qian forms the male, and the way of Kun forms the female; male and female unite their essence, and the ten thousand things are transformed and born. As the Consecration Commentary puts it: this takes the kidney-water as the trigram Kan, the male, and the heart-fire as the trigram Li, the female, drawing out Kan to fill in Li, sending fire downward and drawing water upward, causing them to unite, in order to form this immortal embryo; practicing this without cease, and thereby gaining its effect, called perfecting this response. This same response is exactly this meaning of union — here, through Kan and Li, uniting their essence to accomplish this practice — hence named the essence-cultivating immortal.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: as for using a woman as one's own vessel, drawing out and aiding oneself through defiled and impure means, our own Buddhist teaching plainly condemns this as a demonic doctrine, and even the way of the immortals itself regards it with contempt, as belonging to the lowest grade. This caters to human desire, and the deluded and infatuated are, for the most part, misled by it — a true, upright gentleman ought to sever it from his own speech entirely, and keep himself far from it.

Sūtra: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on transformation, they perfect this awakened understanding — this is named the transcendent immortal."

Commentary: "Firm and unceasing in fixing the mind on transformation" means fixing one's own mind firmly, exhaustively studying the principle of transformation, investigating this technique and researching deeply into this principle of transformation, practicing this without cease, and thereby gaining its effect. The mind, fixed upon this principle of transformation, over time comes to a kind of awakened understanding, and thereby communicates with the very process of creation itself, called perfecting this awakened understanding. Moving mountains and overturning seas, reversing the very seasons themselves — hence named the transcendent immortal. This closes the second section, listing each of these immortals in turn.

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, all of these refine the mind through purely human means, without cultivating true awakening, but attain instead a separate principle of life, living a thousand or ten thousand years, resting deep in the mountains or upon some island in the great sea, cut off entirely from human company — yet this, too, is nothing but this same wheel of rebirth, this same false thought turning on and on. Without cultivating true samādhi, once this same reward is exhausted, they return once more, and scatter, once again, among the various destinies."

Commentary: This concludes by showing this to be false thought, and urges most earnestly toward true cultivation. "All of these" refers to these ten classes of immortals described above, all of whom, dwelling among human beings, dread the impermanence of birth and death, and so seek this long life that never dies. As the Standard Meaning Commentary asks: how could one ever call it awakening, to say merely that longer surpasses shorter, or that birth, in the end, only awaits its own extinction — never realizing this ultimate principle of the unborn, this same wondrous substance, originally constant?

"Refine the mind" means separately cultivating some false notion, not cultivating this true mind of awakening at all. "Attain instead a separate principle of life" means fixing one's own form through visualization, separately attaining this extended life — this false principle corresponding to this same false effort, and so able to live a thousand or ten thousand years; yet this is merely dying later, not truly never dying at all — like the pine and cypress, which merely wither later than other trees, not truly never withering at all. Resting the mind, dwelling deep in the mountains or upon some island in the sea, cut off from every trace of human habitation — this is exactly what is meant by Penglai and the Weak Water, which only a flying immortal can ever cross: beyond the seven golden mountains, this water is so weak that no boat or oar can ever reach it, and even feathers sink within it — this being the very dwelling-place of these divine immortals; yet this is by no means some land where one never dies at all, and this, too, remains within this same wheel of rebirth. Since even what they depend upon has never transcended the three realms, how could this same dependent self ever escape birth and death at all? Now, the Dharma-body is truly constant, while this physical form is empty and illusory; dreading a swift death and seeking a long life is itself nothing but false thought, and depending on false thought, one falls into arising and ceasing — this, precisely, is this same turning on and on.

"Without cultivating true samādhi" urges, in the most earnest terms, toward true cultivation: that is, wishing to escape this wheel of rebirth, one must sever false thought; and wishing to sever false thought, one must rely on this same samādhi. This samādhi is exactly this great Śūraṅgama samādhi itself. Within this causal stage, relying on this unborn, unceasing nature as the very root of one's own cultivation, one then perfectly accomplishes cultivation and realization at the fruit-stage; yet, should one, within this causal stage, rely instead on arising and ceasing as one's own cause, wishing thereby to attain this unborn, unceasing nature, there could be no such possibility at all. Should one fail to cultivate this Śūraṅgama samādhi with all urgency, then, once this reward of immortality is exhausted, one must, as before, change one's own head and face once more, scattering, once again, among the various destinies — how could this fail to grieve one deeply!

As the Standard Meaning Commentary asks: those who cultivate the way of the immortal falsely claim that the Buddhist teaching cultivates the nature alone, without cultivating this same life-force, and so, across ten thousand kalpas, this shadowy, numinous spirit can never enter sagehood — many are misled by this claim; may this be addressed here directly, to awaken those caught in this deep confusion. The answer: what they speak of as nature and life-force are, both alike, not truly real at all — for they take this spirit and soul within the body as this nature, and this vital energy, knotted within the body, this very root of life, as this life-force. And so they say that one who cultivates the nature alone attains merely a shadowy, ghostly immortal, without any lasting bodily form; while one who cultivates both together attains a light, wondrous, long-lived body, and so boasts of form and spirit both made wondrous. Yet how could they ever know that what the Buddha speaks of as this nature is this same true suchness, this ocean of nature, inherently possessed by every single person — the very substance underlying boundless heavens and earths, and boundless myriad things besides? Whoever realizes this nature is thereby able not only to manifest boundless, wondrous bodies, but also to manifest boundless heavens, earths, and myriad things as well. And what such a person thereby manifests is by no means limited merely to lasting a hundred thousand or ten thousand years — even across kalpas as numerous as motes of dust or grains of sand, this, too, may be sustained. And, wishing to gather it in, they gather it in, without a single mote of dust remaining; and, wishing to manifest it, they manifest it, every dharma fully displayed — attaining this great freedom, this great function — only this may truly be called the buddha-nature of true suchness itself. Should this claim still fail to be believed, let one read closely the earlier passage revealing this nature, and one will naturally awaken to how ignorant and how false that earlier slander truly was.

In the past, Lü Yan traveled to the capital three times, seeking office without success, and returned home each time despondent and gloomy. Han Zhongli, wishing to lead Lü Dongbin — that is, Lü Yan — to awakening, waited for him partway along the road, at an inn. When Lü Yan arrived, he sighed long and deep, lamenting bitterly. Han said to him: "Why does the honored gentleman carry on so? Could it be that fame and office have eluded you?" Fame and rank, he continued, are exactly like a dream: gaining them brings no true joy, and losing them ought bring no true sorrow — why, then, does this weigh so heavily on your heart? Lü Yan heard this and said nothing. Han then produced a pillow, and said to him: "You have traveled a hard road today — rest a while, and take a nap." Lü Yan accepted it and fell asleep at once.

In this dream, he found himself entering some household, taking on a new life there; born clever and handsome, his mother cherished him as a pearl held in the palm of her hand. As a boy, he studied diligently, possessed of talent surpassing others; once grown to maturity, he passed every examination with ease, entered official service, and rose steadily in his career, advancing at last to Minister of Personnel. Loyal and upright by nature, he was, in the end, framed by a treacherous official and condemned to death. His whole life had unfolded in one favorable circumstance after another, until this sudden calamity of execution befell him; bound and led to the execution ground, the sharp blade fell, his own head dropped — and he awoke with a start, drenched in a cold sweat from head to foot.

Han then said to him: "And what, in the end, of fame, rank, and riches?" Better, by far, to follow me at once in cultivating the Way. Lü Yan reflected on this, and, realizing that everything in this human world is nothing but impermanence, followed him, thereafter cultivating a separate path, and changing his own name to Dongbin. He forged a pair of twin swords, male and female, cutting down demons and driving out evil spirits, his whole intent set on benefiting living beings.

One day, passing beneath Mount Huanglong, he saw a canopy of purple clouds hovering above it, and, knowing that some exalted person must dwell there, entered the mountain to pay his respects. It happened that Chan Master Huanglong was, at that very moment, ascending the hall to expound the Dharma; Dongbin listened in from the side. Once the master had finished speaking, Chan Master Huanglong said: "Today, someone here has come to steal the Dharma." Dongbin at once stepped forward from the crowd and said: "I already carry, in my own pouch, the elixir of eternal life — what use would I have for stealing the Dharma?" Chan Master Huanglong rebuked him, saying: "You — nothing but a corpse-guarding ghost!" Dongbin at once sent his own precious sword flying, meaning to sever the Chan master's own head; yet, as the sword reached his neck, it gave a single ringing sound and fell to the ground, burying itself in the earth, and would not fly back to his hand at all. Dongbin exhausted every effort of his own mind, and still it would not return — and Huanglong's own head remained exactly as it had been.

It was then that Dongbin came to believe, in truth, how lofty this master's own attainment of the Way truly was, and, turning at once toward repentance, begged to be instructed, bowing to become his disciple. There remains a verse in testimony of this:

"I crush and cast aside this floating gourd, and lay down my own lute as well;
from this day on, I no longer play with mercury's own gold.
Ever since that day I first beheld Huanglong,
I have, at last, regretted where my own heart was so wrongly spent before."

One can thus see, in this, that the way of the immortal falls short of the way of the Buddha itself. This closes the fifth section, this destiny of immortals.

〖經〗阿難！諸世間人，不求常住，未能捨諸妻妾恩愛。

〖講〗此標示生天之因。諸世間人，有欲離苦得樂，不求常住，不生不滅，圓湛之心，但依有為事相，而求有漏果報。是依妄本，而不依真本而修，為三界總因也。未能捨諸妻妾恩愛者：別為六欲天之因，以欲界未離三欲，皆有淫欲，飲食睡眠，今稱欲界。以淫為諸貪之首，故未能捨諸妻妾，深恩重愛，以修出世，戒定之業，而證無漏之果也。

梵語提婆，此翻為天。灌頂引論云：天者，清淨光潔，最勝最尊；或謂天者，天然自在，首出庶物。以上品十善，及世間禪定為因。《正脈》云：天趣與仙趣不同，世人仙天不分，而學仙者，濫附於天，且謂諸天，皆彼祖仙。今略辯之：仙以人身，而戀長生，最怕捨身受身；諸天皆捨前身，而受天身。豈其類哉？又仙處海山，如蓬萊崑崙，皆非天上。四王忉利，曾無卜居，況上界乎？是知天趣，最為界內尊勝之流，迥非仙與鬼神之類也。

〖經〗於邪淫中，心不流逸，澄瑩生明，命終之後，鄰於日月。如是一類，名四王天。

〖講〗己色為正淫，他色為邪淫。此人遵守五戒，於邪淫中，不唯身無有犯，即心中亦無一念思想；是正淫雖有，邪淫已無，故不奔流縱逸，而得愛水，澄淨瑩潔，心地光明也。今獨約淫欲一事，以是生死根本，故約之而分勝劣耳。此人命終之後，捨人身而受天身，生須彌山腰，鄰於日月宮。如是一類，名四天王天，為帝釋外臣：東持國天王，居黃金埵；南增長天王，居琉璃埵；西廣目天王，居白銀埵；北多聞天王，居水晶埵。此四天離人間地，四萬二千由旬，身長拘盧舍四分之一，當此間周尺，七十五丈，壽五百歲。以人間五十年，為一晝夜，合計九百萬年也。

〖講〗此比前更進一步，不但無有邪淫，即於自己正妻，房幃之間，淫欲愛念，尚且微少淡薄，所謂有時有節也。然於清淨獨居之時，間有淫念生起，不得全其淨味。命終之後，超日月之光明，生須彌之極頂（即人間頂）。再上，即空居天，與人間不接矣。如是一類，名忉利天。梵語利忉，此云三十三，在須彌山頂，四方各八天，中有一天，為善見城，乃帝釋所居。此天離地，八萬四千由旬。身長半盧舍，當周尺，一百五十丈；帝釋身長，一拘盧舍；由其過去，遍修敬恭業故。以人間百年為一晝夜，壽命一千歲，合人間數，乃三十六萬年也。以上二天，名地居天，以未離地故也。然此二天，形交成淫，與人間同；但風氣一洩，欲漏便除，非有不淨流溢也。《智論》云：昔有婆羅門，姓憍尸迦，與知友三十二人，發心修塔，以此功德，命終皆生須彌山頂。僑尸迦為天主，三十二人為輔臣。《淨明疏》云：昔迦葉佛滅後，有一女人，發心起塔，報為天主；三十二人助修，報為輔臣。二緣不同者，以既在輪迴，前後更易，各引一緣耳。

〖講〗逢欲境現前，暫時相交，過去之後，竟無追思回憶；於人間世，非有深染，故曰動少靜多。此於淨居，而得全味，漸近清虛；故命終之後，於虛空中，朗然安住。《正脈》問：空居諸天，宮殿池樹，皆何所踞？答：七寶琉璃，與大地無異，但欲下之時，即虛豁無礙，例如人間大地，賢聖天仙鬼神，皆能出入自在。當知萬法本空，由業力轉，虛實並現，而昧者未達也。

〖經〗無世間心，同世行事，於行事交，瞭然超越，命終之後，遍能出超，化無化境，如是一類，名他化自在天。

〖講〗無世間心，同世行事者：厭離世間，淫欲不淨，全無樂著，心希上界，權同世間。行夫婦事，於行事相交之際，不但無味，而且瞭然超越，神遊境外，毫無欲想。命終之後，超出化無化境：化，即第五；無化，即下四天。如是一類，名他化自在天：凡五塵欲境，不勞自化，皆他天之所變化，自己得自在受用也。但此天，以眼暫視，便為淫事。《正脈》引偈云：「四王忉利欲交抱，夜摩執手兜率笑，化樂熟視他暫視，此是六天真快樂」。離地一百二十八萬由旬，有地如雲，安住其上。身長周尺四百五十丈。以人間一千六百年為一晝夜，壽一萬六千歲。

問：欲界頂天，為魔王天是否？答：《瑜伽論》云：第六天上，別有魔羅，所居天宮，即他化自在攝。是知魔天，別是一類，說攝或可；說即，則不可也。二欲分輕重竟。

〖經〗阿難！如是六天，形雖出動，心跡尚交，自此已還，名為欲界。

〖講〗此結成欲界之名。欲性躁動，世人無有節制；其動也，若瀑流，若野火，莫能鎮壓。如是六天，漸能節制，而向於靜，因中欲心漸漸輕，生天層層高。形雖出動，心跡尚交者：如初天，且止外動；二天，內動亦微；三天，遇境方動；四天，境迫不違；五天，交中無味；六天，形合心超。然此等形雖合，而心超出，似離於動，但其心跡，尚然猶交。如前四天，不唯跡交，尚兼心交，以有味故。後二天，雖無心交，猶有跡交，以應事故。此雖欲有重輕，總未離於男女愛情。自此已還，直至阿鼻地獄，皆欲界攝。以諸趣雖異，而欲是同，故名五趣雜居地也。

問：天台云：六天果報，十善為本。兼護法生四王；兼慈化生忉利；兼不惱眾生，善巧純熟，生夜摩；兼修禪定麤細，生兜率；兼欲界定，生化樂；兼未到地定，生他化。是知六天，通以十善為因。前三天，各兼功行；後三天，各兼禪定。今何惟約欲事輕重，分六天耶？答：功行禪定為緣，通修十善為因，但十善之中，斷欲為要，設不斷欲，十善何成？如來為人從要故，惟約欲輕欲重，而分勝劣。本經修楞嚴大定，以淫欲為生死冤家，故此中惟約欲。六天以寡欲而上昇，四禪以絕欲而高蹈。故知，不斷淫欲，上界猶不可望，況欲證無上菩提耶？所以欲修三摩地，而出生死海者，必須先斬此一關也。初六欲天竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, people throughout the world, not seeking this constantly abiding mind, are yet unable to give up the deep love and affection they hold for their own wives and consorts."

Commentary: This marks out the very cause of rebirth in the heavens. People throughout the world, wishing to leave suffering behind and attain happiness, yet not seeking this same constant, unborn, unceasing, perfectly still mind, rely instead on conditioned marks and phenomena, seeking only some fruit of retribution still subject to outflow. This is relying on a false root, not a true one, in their cultivation — and this serves as the total cause of all three realms. "Yet unable to give up the deep love and affection they hold for their own wives and consorts" specifically gives the cause of these six heavens of desire, since the desire realm has not yet departed from these three desires — sexual desire, the desire for food, and the desire for sleep, all still present — and so is named the desire realm. Since lust stands foremost among every craving, such a person is unable to give up this deep love and profound affection for their own wives and consorts, and so cultivate this unconditioned karma of precepts and samādhi that transcends the world, thereby realizing this fruit free of outflow.

The Sanskrit word deva is here translated "heaven." As the Consecration Commentary, citing a treatise, puts it: a heavenly being is pure, bright, and radiant, most excellent and most honored of all; or, some say, a heavenly being is one who is naturally at ease, foremost among all created things. Taking the superior grade of the ten wholesome deeds, together with worldly meditative concentration, as its cause. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: the destiny of the heavens differs from the destiny of the immortals — yet people of this world often fail to distinguish immortal from heavenly being, and those who study the way of the immortal wrongly attach themselves to the heavens, even claiming that every heavenly being is, in fact, their own ancestral immortal. Let this now be briefly disputed: an immortal, retaining a human body, clings to long life, most fearing to give up this body and take on another; every heavenly being, though, gives up this former body entirely, and receives, instead, a heavenly body — how, then, could these ever be the same kind of being? And, further, immortals dwell upon the sea or in the mountains, such as Penglai or Kunlun, none of them truly in the heavens above at all. Even the Four Heavenly Kings and the Trāyastriṃśa heaven have no such dwelling-place at all — how much less, then, the realms above them? One thus knows that this destiny of the heavens is the most honored, most excellent class within these bounds, utterly unlike the class of immortals, ghosts, or spirits at all.

Sūtra: "One whose mind, amid deviant sexual union, does not stream outward and indulge freely, this mind settling clear and radiant, bright — such a person, at the end of life, dwells close to the sun and moon. This class is named the heaven of the Four Heavenly Kings."

Commentary: Union with one's own spouse is called proper union; union with anyone else is called deviant union. Such a person upholds the five precepts, and, amid deviant sexual union, not only commits no transgression with the body, but gives rise to not a single thought of it within the mind either; that is, though proper union may still occur, deviant union is already absent, and so one no longer streams outward, indulging freely, and this water of love becomes settled, clear, and pure, the ground of mind bright. Here, this speaks specifically in terms of this one matter of sexual desire alone, since this is the very root of birth and death, and so distinguishes superior from inferior in these terms alone. Such a person, at the end of life, gives up this human body and receives a heavenly body, born at the very waist of Mount Sumeru, dwelling close to the palaces of the sun and moon. This class is named the heaven of the Four Heavenly Kings, ministers under Śakra's own command: to the east, the Heavenly King who Upholds the Nation, dwelling upon a golden mound; to the south, the Heavenly King of Growth, dwelling upon a lapis-lazuli mound; to the west, the Heavenly King of Broad Eyes, dwelling upon a silver mound; and to the north, the Heavenly King of Wide Learning, dwelling upon a crystal mound. These four heavens are forty-two thousand yojanas removed from the human realm, their bodies a quarter of a krośa in length — that is, seventy-five zhang by this land's own measure — with a lifespan of five hundred years, fifty years of the human realm counting as a single day and night there, amounting, altogether, to nine million years.

Sūtra: "For those whose desire toward their own spouse has grown faint, not fully present even in a time set aside for it — at death, they rise beyond the light of sun and moon, dwelling at the very summit of the human realm. This class is named the Trāyastriṃśa heaven."

Commentary: This is a further step beyond what precedes: not only is deviant union absent, but even toward one's own proper spouse, within the private chamber, this love of sexual union has grown faint and diminished, occurring, as it were, only at fitting times and seasons. Yet, even in these times of pure solitude, some thought of desire still occasionally arises, and so this pure savor is not yet complete. At the end of life, such a person rises beyond the very brightness of the sun and moon, born at the very summit of Mount Sumeru — that is, the very summit of the human realm. Rising higher still, one enters the heavens dwelling in the empty sky, no longer connected to the human realm at all. This class is named the Trāyastriṃśa heaven. The Sanskrit word trāyastriṃśa is here translated "thirty-three": at the very summit of Mount Sumeru, eight heavens stand in each of the four directions, and, at the center, one further heaven, the City of Excellent View, where Śakra himself dwells. This heaven is eighty-four thousand yojanas removed from the earth; its beings' bodies measure half a krośa in length — one hundred and fifty zhang by this land's own measure; and Śakra's own body measures a full krośa in length, owing to his own past cultivation of the karma of reverence throughout every former life. A hundred years of the human realm count as a single day and night there, with a lifespan of a thousand years, amounting, when reckoned against the human realm's own count, to three hundred and sixty thousand years.

These two heavens above are named the earth-dwelling heavens, since they have not yet departed from the earth. Yet, in these two heavens, physical union still produces desire, exactly as in the human realm; only, once the very breath of this union is released, this same desire is at once dispelled, without any impure discharge flowing forth at all. As the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom puts it: in the past, there was a brāhmaṇa surnamed Kauśika, who, together with thirty-two companions, resolved to build a stūpa; through this same merit, all, at the end of life, were born at the very summit of Mount Sumeru. Kauśika became the lord of that heaven, and the other thirty-two became his ministers. As the Pure Light Commentary puts it: in the past, after the buddha Kāśyapa's own passing, a certain woman resolved to build a stūpa, and was, in reward, reborn as this lord of heaven; thirty-two others assisted her in this same work, and were, in reward, reborn as her ministers. These two accounts of the causal condition differ from one another because, being still within this cycle of rebirth, earlier and later circumstances shift and change, each drawing upon a different condition of its own.

Sūtra: "For those who meet desire only briefly, with no lingering thought once it passes, their waking life more often still than stirred — at death, they abide luminously within empty space itself, their own light no longer needing the sun and moon, which cannot reach that high. This class is named the Yāma heaven."

Commentary: When some occasion for desire presents itself, such a person unites only briefly, and, once it has passed, gives rise to no further thought or recollection of it at all; throughout human life, there is no deep attachment to it at all — hence, "stillness more often than stirring." Attaining, in this way, this same pure savor complete within this pure solitude, one draws gradually near to this same pure emptiness; and so, at the end of life, one dwells luminously, at ease, within the empty sky itself.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary asks: for these heavens dwelling in the empty sky, upon what, then, do their palaces, ponds, and trees rest? The answer: these are, alike, of the seven precious substances and lapis lazuli, no different from the great earth itself — only that, when descending toward the desire realm below, this same emptiness offers no obstruction whatsoever, exactly as, in the human realm, the great earth itself allows sages, worthies, devas, immortals, ghosts, and spirits alike to pass freely in and out. One should know that every dharma is, at root, empty, transformed only through the power of karma, emptiness and solidity both alike manifest together — it is only the deluded who fail to penetrate this.

"Their own light no longer needing the sun and moon, which cannot reach that high" means that, though the prior heaven had already risen beyond the sun and moon, their light could still reach it; this heaven, rising higher still, finds that this same light of the sun and moon can no longer reach it at all. "These beings possess their own light" means that, through both dependent and direct retribution together, the body and the palace mutually illuminate one another, and so require no sun or moon at all. This class is named the Yāma heaven — here translated "skillful in dividing time," since, the light of the sun and moon being unable to reach it, there is no distinguishing day from night there at all; instead, this heaven, through the opening and closing of a lotus flower, skillfully knows the passage of time, receiving its pleasures accordingly, each in its proper measure. Most sūtras refer to this heaven by the name Yāma, and so this present section follows that same name. Here, only the clasping of hands constitutes sexual union — there is no bodily union at all. This heaven lies one hundred and sixty thousand yojanas removed from the earth, resting upon a ground like cloud, dwelling there at ease. Its beings' bodies measure, by this land's own measure, two hundred and twenty-five zhang. Two hundred years of the human realm count as a single day and night there, with a lifespan of two thousand years.

Sūtra: "For those at rest at every moment, who cannot yet fully refuse desire when it presents itself — at death, they rise to a subtle, refined realm entirely disconnected from the lower heavens and the human world, untouched even by the three calamities that destroy a world-cycle. This class is named the Tuṣita heaven."

Commentary: Such a person, at every moment, is able to remain still, unmoved; yet, should some occasion for contact present itself, forcing this upon them, they are not yet able to refuse or resist it, and so still go along with it — this being somewhat superior to what precedes. At the end of life, such a person rises to this fine and subtle realm, no longer connected to the human or heavenly realms below. Yet this heaven possesses both an inner court and an outer court: the outer court belongs to ordinary heavenly beings, and contains a lesser maṇi-jewel palace; the inner court is where bodhisattvas dwell, with Maitreya Bodhisattva as its own lord, and contains a great maṇi-jewel palace, where he constantly abides, expounding the Dharma and teaching beings. At times, this bodhisattva goes to the outer court's own lesser maṇi-jewel palace, expounding the Dharma to teach ordinary beings there; and should their own capacity prove ripe enough to awaken, he draws them onward, leading them into the inner court. And this inner court is a place the outer court itself cannot even know of — hence, "no longer connected to the lower heavens and the human world below" — hence, "refined." And, extending even through the destruction of the third dhyāna heaven at the end of a kalpa, this bodhisattva's own dwelling-place remains untouched by any of the three calamities of water, fire, or wind — hence, "subtle." This is exactly what the Lotus Sūtra means in saying: "this land of mine is never destroyed, though all perceive it consumed by the fire at the end of the kalpa." Examining this passage's own wording closely, the first four clauses give the cause belonging to the outer court, lacking their own fruit; and "rises to this subtle, refined realm" onward gives the fruit belonging to the inner court, lacking its own cause — the translator, it would seem, having abbreviated the text somewhat here. This class is named the Tuṣita heaven, here translated "the heaven of contentment," since it is able, toward the objects of desire, to give rise to contentment — here, only a smile constitutes sexual union, with no need even for the clasping of hands at all. This heaven lies three hundred and twenty thousand yojanas removed from the earth, resting upon a ground like cloud above. Its beings' bodies measure, by this land's own measure, three hundred zhang. Four hundred years of the human realm count as a single day and night there, with a lifespan of four thousand years.

Sūtra: "For those with no desiring mind of their own, who merely go along with the world's ways when the occasion arises, finding it as tasteless as chewing wax — at death, they are born into a realm beyond the transformed. This class is named the Heaven of Bliss through Transformation."

Commentary: "I myself hold no thought of desire at all, but go along with your own wish, carrying out this matter" — this shows, indeed, a deep resistance to it. Yet, unable to resist it entirely, and pressed by this great matter — that is, the continuation of the family's own line — one, in the end, reluctantly goes along with this wish. Yet, encountering the object of desire, laid bare before them, this tastes to them exactly as tasteless as chewing on wax. "Laid bare" — as Sima Xiangru's own rhapsody puts it: her flowery countenance offered of itself, her jade-like body laid bare — meaning that the body itself is laid out, offered before another. At the end of life, such a person is born into a realm beyond the transformed — "beyond" means surpassing the heaven below; "transformed" means transforming the five desired objects at will, for one's own use and enjoyment. This class is named the Heaven of Bliss through Transformation. Other sūtras call it, simply, the heaven of transformed bliss, meaning that it transforms these five sense-objects for itself, and enjoys this bliss on its own — this present section follows that same name. Here, only a fixed gaze constitutes sexual union, with no need even for a smile at all. This heaven lies six hundred and forty thousand yojanas removed from the earth, resting upon a ground like cloud above. Its beings' bodies measure, by this land's own measure, three hundred and seventy-five zhang. Eight hundred years of the human realm count as a single day and night there, with a lifespan of eight thousand years.

Sūtra: "For those with no thought of the world at all, yet who outwardly go along with worldly affairs, transcending them completely even in the moment of doing so — at death, they are able to pass entirely beyond both the transformed and the untransformed realms alike. This class is named the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations."

Commentary: "With no thought of the world at all, yet who outwardly go along with worldly affairs" means that such a person, wearied of the world, finds this same impurity of sexual desire entirely without any attachment or pleasure at all, the mind aspiring toward the higher realms, only outwardly, provisionally, going along with worldly ways. Carrying out this matter between husband and wife, at the very moment of this same union, not only finds it without the slightest savor at all, but, further, transcends it entirely, clearly and completely, the spirit roaming beyond this realm altogether, without the slightest thought of desire remaining. At the end of life, such a person surpasses both the transformed realm and the untransformed realm below it: "transformed" refers to the fifth heaven above; "untransformed" refers to the four heavens below it. This class is named the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations: since every object of the five sense-desires, without troubling to transform them oneself, is transformed instead by another heaven, and one enjoys them freely, at one's own ease. Here, only a single glance constitutes this matter of sexual desire. As the Standard Meaning Commentary cites in verse: "the Four Heavenly Kings and Trāyastriṃśa embrace in desire; Yāma clasps hands, and Tuṣita smiles; the Heaven of Transformed Bliss gazes fixedly, and this heaven glances but once — these are the true pleasures of these six heavens." This heaven lies one million two hundred and eighty thousand yojanas removed from the earth, resting upon a ground like cloud above. Its beings' bodies measure, by this land's own measure, four hundred and fifty zhang. One thousand six hundred years of the human realm count as a single day and night there, with a lifespan of sixteen thousand years.

One might ask: is the very summit of the desire realm the heaven of the demon-king? The answer: as the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra puts it: above this sixth heaven, there is, further, a separate Māra, whose own heavenly palace belongs to this same Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations. One thus knows that this heaven of Māra is a separate class of its own; to say that it is included within this heaven may perhaps be said, but to say that it is identical with it cannot be said at all. This closes the second section, distinguishing these degrees of desire.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, in all six of these heavens, though the body has left coarse activity behind, traces of the mind still intermingle with it. From this point downward, all is called the desire realm."

Commentary: This concludes, establishing this name of the desire realm. This nature of desire is restless and agitated, and people of this world hold no restraint over it at all; when it stirs, it is like a cascading torrent, or a wildfire on the plain, impossible to suppress. These six heavens, though, are gradually able to restrain it, turning toward stillness — since, within this same causal stage, this mind of desire grows gradually lighter, the heavens attained rise, layer upon layer, ever higher. "Though the body has left coarse activity behind, traces of the mind still intermingle with it": in the first heaven, only outward activity ceases; in the second heaven, even inward activity grows faint; in the third heaven, it stirs only upon encountering some occasion; in the fourth heaven, though pressed by circumstance, it does not resist; in the fifth heaven, though united, there is no savor in it; and in the sixth heaven, though the body still unites, the mind transcends it entirely. Yet, though the body still unites in this way, the mind transcends it, seeming to stand apart from this activity — yet these very traces of mind still intermingle with it nonetheless. As in the first four heavens, not only do bodily traces intermingle, but the mind, too, still intermingles with it, since some savor of it still remains. In the latter two heavens, though the mind no longer intermingles with it, bodily traces still intermingle with it nonetheless, since some occasion must still be answered. Though desire here differs in weight, none of these six heavens has yet departed from this same attached love between male and female. From this point on — extending all the way down to the Avīci hell itself — every one is included within this same desire realm. Since, though these destinies differ from one another, this same desire runs common to them all, this whole span is named the ground where these five destinies dwell mixed together.

One might ask: Tiantai holds that the fruit of retribution in these six heavens takes the ten wholesome deeds as its root, joined, further, with protecting the Dharma to be born among the Four Heavenly Kings; joined with compassionate transformation to be born in Trāyastriṃśa; joined with not troubling sentient beings, and skillful means brought to full maturity, to be born in Yāma; joined with the cultivation of coarse and subtle meditative concentration to be born in Tuṣita; joined with concentration belonging to the desire realm to be born in the Heaven of Transformed Bliss; and joined with concentration not yet reaching the very ground of dhyāna to be born in the Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations. One thus knows that these six heavens, in general, take the ten wholesome deeds as their cause — the first three heavens each joined with their own particular conduct, and the latter three each joined with their own particular meditative concentration. Why, then, does this present passage distinguish these six heavens solely in terms of the weight of desire alone? The answer: conduct and meditative concentration serve as the condition, while the general cultivation of the ten wholesome deeds serves as the cause — yet, among these ten wholesome deeds, severing desire is the essential point; and, should desire not be severed, how could these ten wholesome deeds ever be accomplished at all? The Tathāgata, addressing people according to what is essential, distinguishes superior from inferior solely in terms of desire, whether light or heavy.

This sūtra cultivates this great Śūraṅgama samādhi, taking sexual desire as the very enemy of birth and death, and so this passage speaks solely in terms of desire alone. These six heavens rise through diminished desire; the four dhyānas ascend through desire entirely severed. One thus knows that, without severing sexual desire, even these higher realms remain beyond hope — how much less, then, the realization of unsurpassed bodhi itself? And so whoever wishes to cultivate samādhi, and thereby emerge from this sea of birth and death, must first cut through this one barrier before anything else. This closes the first section, the six heavens of desire.

〖經〗阿難！世間一切，所修心人，不假禪那，無有智慧。但能執身，不行淫欲，若行若坐，想念俱無，愛染不生，無留欲界；是人應念，身為梵侶。如是一類，名梵眾天。

〖講〗前明六天，雖求離欲，未能絕無，以形雖出動，心跡尚交，故名欲界。而此上十八天，雖離欲染，尚有色質，貌如童子，身白銀色，衣黃金衣，惟男無女，純是化生，色身勝故，故名色界。又名梵世，梵者淨也，已離欲染，而得清淨，通號四禪，已離欲界散動也。非但離欲，併離食睡，三欲俱亡，稍涉飢倦，即入禪定，而出定時，則精神飽滿，但以禪悅為食、為息，稍離麤重身心矣。略分四重，詳分十八重，但疑有同處，而區分勝劣為類者，未必十八，皆上下為次也。

此明色界，初禪三天；首言世間者，顯非出世；而言一切，所修心人者：謂不知本有寂常心性，而錯亂修習也。禪那，此云靜慮；不假禪那，無有智慧者：謂不修首楞嚴大定，無漏之靜慮，即無有出世間，真正之智慧。顯彼惟有漏靜慮，六事行耳！六行者：厭欲界是苦、是麤、是障；欣色界是淨、是妙、是離。此則凡夫伏惑，超欲界道也；非無漏真三摩地，妙圓通矣！此是總論，應通下文。

但能執身，不行淫欲，方入本天；此天獨顯戒德，未彰定慧。先言執身者：表異六欲，不免身犯。而此天非但執身不犯；若行若坐，想念之心，亦復俱無。由是身心，俱得清淨，愛染不生，故欲界無可卜居，臨終之時。應念化生上之色界，身為梵世伴侶。如是一類，乃是梵天之民，故名梵眾天。壽二十小劫，身長半由旬。

〖講〗欲界淫習，既已伏除，離欲淨心，已得顯現。初二句，指前天，但顯戒德；下謂本天，戒與定俱。於諸律儀，愛樂隨順者：於諸梵行戒品，愛樂則悅豫，隨順則輕安，即與定共之相；無有強行之苦，乃有安行之樂，自有不期然而然者矣。是人應時，能行梵德者：顯是本天轉升，故云應時，非同前天，離下生上，猶待異時，此不但清淨身心，而且弘揚德化，已超梵眾。如是一類，名梵輔天。《正脈》云：既輔化，即天臣矣，壽四十小劫，身長一由旬。

〖經〗身心妙圓，威儀不缺，清淨禁戒，加以明悟，是人應時，能統梵眾，為大梵王。如是一類，名大梵天。

〖講〗此天乃顯戒與慧俱。初天，由執身而攝心；次天，由心現而行德；此天，身心得一如無二之妙，滿足分量之圓。行、住、坐、臥，四威儀之中，悉皆不缺，有威可畏，有儀可仰，禁戒自然清淨；不唯清淨，加以明悟，此即戒與慧俱也。是人應時，能統梵眾，猶體仁足以長人，君之道也。如是一類，名大梵天；位超梵輔，故稱為大。壽六十小劫，身長一由旬半。初示三天別相竟。

〖經〗阿難！此三勝流，一切苦惱，所不能逼，雖非正修，真三摩地。清淨心中。諸漏不動，名為初禪。

〖講〗此上三天，稱為勝流者：以身勝樂勝，能勝下界諸趣之流。一切苦惱，所不能逼者：指欲界八苦，所不能逼，雖非依真本，而起正修，得證真三摩地者，然於持戒清淨心中，而欲界諸漏，所不能動，名為初禪；於九地中，第二名離生喜樂地，離欲界諸惡趣生，而得清淨喜樂也。劫盡火災得至，由其內有覺觀火故。初初禪天竟。

〖經〗阿難！其次梵天，統攝梵人，圓滿梵行，澄心不動，寂湛生光。如是一類，名少光天。

〖講〗此明色界，二禪三天也。前天定力，尚假戒扶，此則不假戒扶，而自不動；定深發光，以光之勝劣為次。阿難！其次梵天，統攝梵人：似是大梵所為；梵眾天：戒德顯著；梵輔天：戒與定俱；大梵天：戒與慧俱；具足戒、定、慧，故曰圓滿梵行，而升此天。環師亦謂，躡大梵之行，升進者是也。統攝既久，則化他功深，自行益純。澄心下，方入此天；澄心不動，寂湛生光者：謂心水澄凝不動，寂然發用，湛然生光，但心光尚劣，未極其量，如是一類，名少光天。壽二大劫，身長二由旬。

〖經〗光光相然，照耀無盡，映十方界，遍成琉璃。如是一類，名無量光天。

〖講〗溫陵曰：定力轉明，妙光迭發，境隨光發，遍成琉璃。由定力轉更增明，身光心光，展轉相然，如以火傳火，光光迭發，照耀於無盡也。所有依報外境，隨光而發，亦同正報身心，內外明徹，遍成琉璃。映十方界者：約其定光，隨所受用十方言之，非遍一切十方也。按二禪覆以小千世界，遍小千之十方也。如是一類，名無量光天。壽四大劫，身長四由旬。

〖經〗吸持圓光，成就教體，發化清淨，應用無盡。如是一類，名光音天。

〖講〗吸，即取也；持，即執持；圓光，謂前天依正，光明交映，至此定深，而至圓滿，故曰圓光成就。教體者：《正脈》云：以此光明，代其言音，以宣彼梵行教化，如世間以文字，代其言音，而亦以宣諸教化，與用光明作佛事者，同也。有謂二禪以上，無有語言，恐未必然。《法華》云：光音及遍淨，乃至有頂天，言語之音聲，悉皆得聞之；此可為證。發化清淨，應用無盡者：發宣梵行教化，清淨無著，應用無有窮盡。如是一類，名光音天。壽八大劫，身長八由旬。初示三天別相竟。

〖經〗阿難！此三勝流，一切憂懸，所不能逼，雖非正修，真三摩地。清淨心中，麤漏已伏，名為二禪。

〖講〗此三勝流，非但勝於欲界，猶復勝於初禪，以後後勝於前前也。一切憂懸，所不能逼者：以初禪，乍離欲苦，恐其復墜，故一切憂愁懸掛，竟有不安之貌，時時以覺觀拒之。今至二禪，離欲漸遠，恐墜心息，故憂懸所不能逼，而入無覺無觀矣。然雖無覺觀，仍屬有漏，而非依寂常之真心，而修實證真三摩地也。初禪方得漏心不動，而未能伏；此天於清淨梵行心中，麤漏已伏。麤漏，指初禪愛，無所由起，故曰已伏。能以定力，伏前五識，不起現行，故名為二禪。於九地中第三，名定生喜樂地，以定力功德，發生喜樂也。火災不到，而有水災，由其內心有喜水故。二二禪天竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, people throughout the world who cultivate the mind, without relying on true dhyāna, possess no wisdom at all. Yet, should such a person be able to hold the body itself in check, never engaging in sexual conduct, whether walking or sitting, with no thought or notion of it arising at all, no love or defilement given rise to, so that nothing binds them to the desire realm — such a person, the very moment life ends, becomes a companion of Brahmā. This class is named the Retinue of Brahmā."

Commentary: The prior passage made plain these six heavens: though seeking to leave desire behind, they were not yet able to eliminate it entirely, since, though the body had left coarse activity behind, traces of the mind still intermingled with it — hence named the desire realm. These eighteen heavens above it, though free of the defilement of desire, still possess material substance — their appearance like that of a child, their bodies the color of white silver, clothed in garments of gold, male alone, without any female at all, entirely transformation-born, their form-bodies superior — hence named the form realm. Also called the Brahmā-world: Brahmā means pure — having already departed from the defilement of desire, and attained this same purity — generally named the four dhyānas, having already departed from the scattered activity of the desire realm.

Not only free of desire, but free, further, of both food and sleep, all three desires entirely gone; when even slightly hungry or weary, one enters samādhi at once, and, upon emerging from samādhi, the spirit is full and satisfied — taking the joy of dhyāna itself as both food and rest, having departed somewhat from this coarse, heavy body and mind. Briefly divided, this makes four levels; in detail, eighteen — yet there is some question as to whether beings dwelling in the same place, distinguished only by superior or inferior class, must necessarily correspond exactly to these eighteen, level by level, in strict order.

This makes plain the form realm's first dhyāna, its first three heavens. Beginning with the word "world" shows that this is not yet transcending the world; and speaking of "all people who cultivate the mind" means those who do not know that this same still, constant nature of mind is inherently already possessed, and so cultivate it in confused disorder. Dhyāna is here translated "stillness and reflection"; "without relying on true dhyāna, possess no wisdom at all" means that, without cultivating this great Śūraṅgama samādhi, this stillness and reflection free of outflow, there is no transcendent, true, correct wisdom at all — showing that such people possess only this stillness and reflection still subject to outflow, this practice of the six activities alone. These six activities are: regarding the desire realm as suffering, as coarse, as an obstruction; and delighting in the form realm as pure, as wondrous, as freedom. This is exactly how an ordinary being subdues affliction, transcending the path of the desire realm — not this same true samādhi, free of outflow, this wondrous, perfect penetration at all! This is spoken in general terms here, applicable to everything that follows below.

"Yet, should such a person be able to hold the body itself in check, never engaging in sexual conduct" only then does one enter this heaven proper; this heaven displays the virtue of the precepts alone, not yet manifesting samādhi or wisdom. Speaking first of holding the body in check shows this to differ from the six desire-heavens, which could not avoid some bodily transgression. Yet this heaven not only holds the body in check, never transgressing; even walking or sitting, this mind of thought and notion, too, is entirely absent. And so, body and mind both alike attaining purity, no love or defilement is given rise to — hence, nowhere to dwell within the desire realm; at the very moment of death, such a person is transformed at once, ascending to this same form realm above, becoming a companion of this Brahmā-world. This class, then, are the very citizens of this Brahmā heaven — hence named the Retinue of Brahmā. Their lifespan is twenty small kalpas, their bodies half a yojana in length.

Sūtra: "Once this habit of desire is already removed, this mind free of desire manifests; toward every rule of monastic conduct, such a person delights in it and readily accords with it. This person, at the fitting time, is thereby able to carry out this same virtue of Brahmā. This class is named the Ministers of Brahmā."

Commentary: This habit of lust belonging to the desire realm, already subdued and removed, this pure mind free of desire has already come to manifest. The first two clauses point to the prior heaven, displaying only the virtue of the precepts; what follows refers to this present heaven, where precepts and samādhi are both present together. "Toward every rule of monastic conduct, such a person delights in it and readily accords with it" means that, toward every precept belonging to this pure conduct, delighting in it brings joy, and according with it brings ease — this being exactly the mark of samādhi accompanying it; free of the suffering of forced practice, one instead enjoys the happiness of practice at ease, spontaneously so, without ever intending it. "This person, at the fitting time, is thereby able to carry out this same virtue of Brahmā" shows that this is properly this same heaven's own advancement — hence, "at the fitting time," unlike the prior heaven, which, departing from what is below to be born above, still had to wait for some other time. This heaven, not only pure in body and mind, but, further, spreading and proclaiming this same transformative virtue, already surpasses the Retinue of Brahmā. This class is named the Ministers of Brahmā. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: already assisting in this transformation, this is exactly a minister of heaven. Their lifespan is forty small kalpas, their bodies a full yojana in length.

Sūtra: "Body and mind wondrously complete, dignity of bearing unbroken, these pure precepts joined, further, with clear understanding; this person, at the fitting time, is thereby able to govern this same retinue of Brahmā, becoming Great Brahmā himself. This class is named Great Brahmā."

Commentary: This heaven displays precepts and wisdom both present together. In the first heaven, one holds the body in check, thereby gathering in the mind; in the next heaven, this mind manifests, thereby carrying out this virtue; in this present heaven, body and mind attain this same wondrous state, without any duality remaining, this measure fulfilled completely and entirely. Throughout walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, these four postures, nothing at all is lacking; possessed of a dignity that inspires awe, and a bearing worthy of reverence, these precepts naturally pure. Not only pure, but, further, joined with clear understanding — this being exactly precepts and wisdom both present together. "This person, at the fitting time, is thereby able to govern this same retinue of Brahmā" — exactly as embodying benevolence is sufficient to lead others, this being the very way of a true ruler. This class is named Great Brahmā, this position surpassing the Ministers of Brahmā — hence called "great." Their lifespan is sixty small kalpas, their bodies a yojana and a half in length. This closes the first section, showing the distinct marks of these three heavens.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these three superior classes cannot be pressed upon by any suffering or affliction at all. Though this is not yet this true, direct cultivation, this true samādhi, still, within this pure mind, no outflow stirs at all. This is named the first dhyāna."

Commentary: These three heavens above are called superior classes because their bodies are superior and their joy is superior, able to surpass every destiny of the lower realms. "Cannot be pressed upon by any suffering or affliction at all" refers to how the eight sufferings of the desire realm cannot press upon them at all; though this is not yet relying on this true root, giving rise to this direct cultivation and thereby realizing this true samādhi, still, within this same pure mind of upheld precepts, every outflow of the desire realm is unable to stir them at all — this is named the first dhyāna. Among the nine grounds, this is the second, named the ground of joy and bliss arisen from departure — departing from birth among the evil destinies of the desire realm, and thereby attaining this same pure joy and bliss. This heaven is reached by the fire-calamity that destroys a kalpa, since it still harbors within it this same fire of coarse and subtle mental activity. This closes the first dhyāna heaven.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, next, from Brahmā's own heaven, governing these people of Brahmā, this same conduct of Brahmā brought to perfect fulfillment — this mind, settled and unmoving, gives rise, in its own stillness, to light. This class is named Lesser Light."

Commentary: This makes plain the form realm's second dhyāna, its three heavens. In the prior heaven, this power of samādhi still relied on the precepts for support; here, though, no longer relying on the precepts for support, one is, of oneself, unmoving; samādhi grows deep and gives rise to light, and these heavens are ordered according to whether this light is superior or inferior. "Ānanda, next, from Brahmā's own heaven, governing these people of Brahmā" seems to describe what Great Brahmā himself has done: the Retinue of Brahmā display the virtue of the precepts; the Ministers of Brahmā are precepts and samādhi both present together; and Great Brahmā is precepts and wisdom both present together — complete with precepts, samādhi, and wisdom all at once, hence, "this same conduct of Brahmā brought to perfect fulfillment," and so rising to this present heaven. As the Standard Meaning Commentary likewise holds, this carries forward Great Brahmā's own conduct, advancing further still. Having governed for so long, the merit of transforming others grows deep, and one's own practice becomes ever more pure. "This mind" onward now enters this present heaven proper: this mind, settled and unmoving, this mind's own water settled and congealed, unmoving, gives rise, in its own stillness, to function, and, in its own clarity, to light — yet this light of mind remains inferior still, not yet reaching its own fullest measure. This class is named Lesser Light. Their lifespan is two great kalpas, their bodies two yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "This light kindles further light in turn, illuminating without end, casting its reflection throughout the ten directions, until everything becomes, entirely, like lapis lazuli. This class is named Boundless Light."

Commentary: As Wenling puts it: the power of samādhi grows ever brighter, this wondrous light arising in turn, this realm arising according with this light, becoming, entirely, like lapis lazuli. As the power of samādhi turns ever brighter still, the body's own light and the mind's own light kindle one another in turn, exactly as fire is passed from one torch to the next, light after light arising in succession, illuminating without end. Every outer realm belonging to this same dependent retribution follows this light and arises as well, just as this same direct retribution of body and mind, inward and outward alike, becomes thoroughly bright, becoming, entirely, like lapis lazuli. "Casting its reflection throughout the ten directions" speaks in terms of this same light of samādhi, according with whatever is received and made use of throughout these ten directions — not pervading literally every direction there is. According to tradition, this second dhyāna covers a small-thousand world, and so this light pervades the ten directions of this small-thousand world alone. This class is named Boundless Light. Their lifespan is four great kalpas, their bodies four yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "Drawing in and holding fast to this perfect light, this becomes the very substance of this teaching, proclaiming this transformation, pure, its function inexhaustible. This class is named Light-Sound."

Commentary: "Drawing" means to take up; "holding" means to grasp and sustain; "perfect light" refers to the prior heaven's own dependent and direct retribution, this light mutually reflecting one another — reaching this point, this samādhi grows deep, arriving at this same perfection, hence, "perfect light accomplished." "The very substance of this teaching" — as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this same light substitutes for this same speech and sound, thereby proclaiming this pure conduct, this teaching and transformation — exactly as, in this world, written words substitute for speech and sound, likewise proclaiming every teaching and transformation, no different from making use of light itself to carry out the Buddha's own work.

Some hold that, above the second dhyāna, there is no spoken language at all — yet this may not necessarily be so. As the Lotus Sūtra puts it: the Light-Sound heaven, and the Universal Purity heaven, all the way up to the very summit of existence, every sound and voice there is heard there in full — this may serve as proof. "Proclaiming this transformation, pure, its function inexhaustible" means proclaiming and expounding this pure conduct, this teaching and transformation, pure and unattached, its function never coming to an end. This class is named Light-Sound. Their lifespan is eight great kalpas, their bodies eight yojanas in length. This closes the first section, showing the distinct marks of these three heavens.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these three superior classes cannot be pressed upon by any anxious apprehension at all. Though this is not yet this true, direct cultivation, this true samādhi, still, within this pure mind, every coarse outflow is already subdued. This is named the second dhyāna."

Commentary: These three superior classes surpass not only the desire realm, but the first dhyāna as well, since each later stage surpasses the one before it. "Cannot be pressed upon by any anxious apprehension at all": in the first dhyāna, having only just left the suffering of desire behind, fearing to fall back into it once more, every anxious apprehension still hung heavy, an unsettled appearance still present, constantly resisted through discursive thought and reflection. Reaching this second dhyāna now, one has departed ever further from desire, no longer fearing to fall, and so no anxious apprehension can press upon it at all, entering this state free of both discursive thought and reflection. Yet, though free of discursive thought and reflection, this still belongs to outflow, not yet relying on this same constant, true mind to cultivate and genuinely realize this true samādhi. The first dhyāna attains only this unmoving mind subject to outflow, not yet able to subdue it; this heaven, within this pure mind of this Brahmā-conduct, has already subdued this coarse outflow. This coarse outflow refers to the attachment of the first dhyāna, having nowhere left to arise from — hence, "already subdued." Through the power of samādhi, one is able to subdue these first five consciousnesses, so that they no longer manifest in present activity — hence named the second dhyāna. Among the nine grounds, this is the third, named the ground of joy and bliss born of samādhi, since this merit and virtue of samādhi's own power gives rise to this joy and bliss. This heaven is not reached by the fire-calamity, yet is reached by the water-calamity, since this inward mind still harbors this same water of joy within it. This closes the second dhyāna heaven.

〖經〗阿難！如是天人，圓光成音，披音露妙，發成精行，通寂滅樂。如是一類，名少淨天。

〖講〗此明色界，三禪三天也。《持地論》目第三禪為樂俱禪，此定功德，與遍身樂俱發故。前二禪雖有樂支，為喜支所障，今滅喜純樂，故得其名。阿難！如是天人：躡前光音天，圓滿光明，成就音聲，而為教體；披發音聲，顯露妙理。此天，即依妙理，發成精行，離前喜動，而生淨樂，恬然安靜，而通寂滅之樂。寂滅樂，亦定清心安所發，不可濫於本性寂體，以名同體異也。以初入此境，故言通，而未能成，名少淨天。初伏第六意識，非真寂滅也。壽十六大劫，身長十六由旬。

〖經〗淨空現前，引發無際，身心輕安，成寂滅樂。如是一類，名無量淨天。

〖講〗淨空現前者：前天通寂滅樂，以其淨境未亡，至此定力轉深，並淨亦空，故曰淨空現前。引發無際者：以空引淨，淨與空發，虛空無有邊際，淨境亦無邊際。自覺正報之身心，猶若太虛，無累無礙，故曰輕安，成寂滅樂。如是一類，名無量淨天，淨空無際，故無有量也。壽三十二大劫，身長三十二由旬。

〖經〗世界身心，一切圓淨，淨德成就，勝託現前，歸寂滅樂。如是一類，名遍淨天。

〖講〗前天淨境，只遍身心，至此定力轉深，並依報之世界，正報之身心，泯同一體，皆得虛寂，故曰一切圓淨。《正脈》云：此但定力所使，所謂境隨定變而已，非唯心觀力所使也。淨德成就：接上句，既一切圓淨，則純淨之德成就，妙樂無窮；有漏之樂，至此已極。自覺殊勝歸託之處現前，將謂真實安身立命處所，清淨極樂家鄉矣。不知尚屬有漏，即便歸託於此，故云歸寂滅樂。如是一類，名遍淨天，以其觸處，皆成淨樂，故名遍淨天。壽六十四大劫，身長六十四由旬。初示三天別相竟。

〖經〗阿難！此三勝流，具大隨順，身心安隱，得無量樂，雖非正得，真三摩地。安隱心中，歡喜畢具，名為三禪。

〖講〗阿難！此三天，又勝二禪之流。初禪，苦惱不逼，未得隨順；二禪，憂懸不逼，五識已伏，但得隨順；至此三禪，已得寂滅，六識已伏，名具大隨順。身心安隱，得無量樂者：前二禪，有喜心動念，故身心不得安隱，今喜心既離，身心安隱，自在受樂。樂稱無量者，以所具妙樂，周遍無量故。界內，以三禪為極樂處也。雖非正得真修實證之三摩地，然安隱心中，歡喜畢具。安隱心，即三禪心。初、二禪離障增勝，故曰清淨心。三禪得樂增勝，故曰安隱心。《正脈》云：仍言歡喜畢具者；以有安隱心中，四字揀之，良以喜是動心所發，樂是靜心所融，若在飛動心，縱說樂，而仍是喜支；若在安隱心，縱說喜，而仍是樂支，故通名為三禪。於九地中第四，名為離喜妙樂地。以離前初、二禪之喜，得三禪之妙樂也。水火二災不到，風災得至，由其未離出入息故。三三禪天竟。

〖經〗阿難！復次天人，不逼身心，苦因已盡，樂非常住，久必壞生。苦樂二心，俱時頓捨，麤重相滅，淨福性生。如是一類，名福生天。

〖講〗此四禪九天。溫陵曰四禪報境，但有三天，第四無想，乃第三廣果別開，此外復有五不還天，乃聖賢別修靜慮，與凡夫不同。但以同入捨禪，故於捨心同分中，安立居處。阿難！復次天人：是指三禪中來者，謂初禪離苦惱，二禪盡憂懸，故云不逼身心。苦惱憂懸是苦因，既皆不逼，是已苦盡則樂生。三禪身心安隱，得無量樂；此天則明樂亦不當受，何以故？樂非常住之法，福業盡時，久必壞生，由是起雙捨苦樂之行，苦樂二心，並已捨除，故曰俱時頓捨。正以二心俱捨，名麤重相滅，以壞則苦生，今苦壞雙超，捨心不動，即淨福性。此定既發，即名為生；故曰如是一類，名福生天。壽一百二十五大劫，身長一百二十五由旬。

〖經〗捨心圓融，勝解清淨，福無遮中，得妙隨順，窮未來際。如是一類，名福愛天。

〖講〗前天苦樂二心，俱時頓捨，尚未圓融；至此捨定益深，功行純熟，故曰捨心圓融。勝解清淨者：即決定勝解，任持捨定，能所雙亡，不為異緣所轉，是為清淨。然既捨心圓融，無有遮限，而所感淨福，亦應無遮，於此無遮福中，得妙隨順，窮未來際。妙隨順者，大自在也；所有願求，亦無遮限，得大自在，窮未來際，愛樂隨順也。如是一類，名福愛天，此天之福，於有為界中，最為可愛。壽二百五十大劫，身長二百五十由旬。

〖經〗阿難！從是天中，有二歧路，若於先心，無量淨光，福德圓明，修證而住。如是一類，名廣果天。

〖講〗阿難！從是天中，是福愛天中，有二種歧路：一直往道，趣廣果天；一迂僻道，趣無想天；心念行業各別也。若於先心：即福愛天，妙隨順心，能令所求如意，今無量淨光，即捨俱禪，定深而發光也。於此光中，以四無量心，熏禪福德，增盛圓明，離下地染，修證而住。如是一類，名廣果天，謂廣大福德，所感之果也。壽五百大劫，身長五百由旬，水火風三災，皆不能到。於九地中，名捨念清淨地，謂雙捨苦樂二念，而得清淨也。

〖經〗若於先心，雙厭苦樂，精研捨心，相續不斷，圓窮捨道，身心俱滅，心慮灰凝，經五百劫。是人既以生滅為因，不能發明，不生滅性，初半劫滅，後半劫生。如是一類，名無想天。

〖講〗先心是福愛天中，雙厭苦樂之心，至廣果天，增修福德，令得圓明；至無想天，增修捨定，令期究竟；此二天之分歧也。精研捨心三句，即指增修捨定，深搜細索，相續無有間斷。圓窮者：圓滿窮究，必求其究竟；捨道者：即以捨定，為涅槃道也。身心俱滅，心慮灰凝者：謂以捨定，滅除捨心，定中渾成一空，故心身俱成泯滅，令其心思緣慮，有若寒灰，凝然不動，但如夾冰之魚。魚譬六識，心心所法；冰譬捨定，冬天水結成冰，而魚夾在冰中，而不能動。此定亦復如是，祗伏六識現行，經五百大劫，定力攝持，報形不壞也。五百劫後，依舊散入輪迴。然無想報盡，多皆墮獄，因其在定，未證謂證，以為證阿羅漢，已出生死。後出定毀謗三寶，以佛狂妄說法。說阿羅漢，所作已辦，不受後有，以故墮入地獄。是人既以生滅為因，不能發明，不生滅性者：《正脈》云：出其無常之故，正由向二種根本，錯亂修習耳。前二句明其錯依六識，生死根本，為本修因，強令灰凝。後二句明其反迷識精元明，圓湛不生滅性，而全不知用故也。初半劫滅者：初生此天，習定半劫，始得想滅，無想定成。後半劫生者：於四百九十九劫半，而想心復生，此定仍壞。有成有壞，終非究竟。如是一類，名無想天。此天依於捨禪，滅除六識，心心所法，令不起現行，故名無想天。但與廣果，同修捨禪，故同一處，以是外道故，分二種名耳。初四天別相竟。

〖經〗阿難！此四勝流，一切世間，諸苦樂境，所不能動。雖非無為，真不動地，有所得心，功用純熟，名為四禪。

〖講〗阿難！此四勝流者：並超下地故。一切世間：通指欲界至三禪也，欲界為苦樂所動；初禪苦惱不逼，二禪憂懸不逼，均不為苦動；三禪得無量樂，雖不為苦動，尚為樂動；至此四禪，苦樂雙亡，而住捨定，故曰諸苦樂境，所不能動。溫陵曰：四禪不為三災所動，名不動地。然彼器非真常，情俱生滅，雖非無為真境，而有為功用，至此已純熟矣。問：不為三災所動，何言器非真常，情俱生滅？答：他經明此天，天人生時，宮殿園林，隨之而生；死時，隨之而滅。器即器世間，宮殿園林，而非真常之境；情即有情世間，與之俱生俱滅。雖非無為真常之境：如十地之第八不動地。有所得心者：即修習捨定，認為涅槃，期其必得，名有所得心。正顯有為功用純熟，不加勉強，任運不動也。名為四禪。《正脈》云：初禪共戒，戒德增上；二禪喜俱，光明增上；三禪樂俱，淨樂增上；四禪捨俱，於前三天，福德增上，後一天，捨定增上，此其別也。初四勝流天竟。

〖經〗阿難！此中復有，五不還天，於下界中，九品習氣，俱時滅盡，苦樂雙亡，下無卜居，故於捨心，眾同分中，安立居處。

〖講〗此中，即四禪天中，復有五種不還天。梵語阿那含，此云不還，三果聖人，寄居之處。以其於下界中，即欲界中，九品思惑習氣，已經斷盡，證三果聖位，再不還來欲界受生也。習氣者，思惑種子也。乃貪、瞋、癡、慢，任運而起，微細之惑，與生俱生，非同分別中麤惑也。由彼惑難除，故分八十一品，於三界九地，地地各分九品。今斷欲界，五趣雜居地中九品，於上、中、下，復各分三品，天上人間，七次往返受生斷之，乃習氣與現行皆滅，故云俱時滅盡，證三果也。此乃明聖人斷惑之事，有異四禪伏惑不斷也。苦樂雙亡，下無卜居者：斷欲界地九品之惑盡，故苦亡，欲界無卜居；於初、二、三禪之樂，亦復不受，故樂亡，於色界三禪，亦無卜居；以非其同分也。而所入之定，乃苦樂雙亡，捨俱禪故，於四禪捨心，眾同分中，安立居處，住此以斷七十二品思惑，求證阿羅漢果也。然安立居處，確在同分，而雜修靜慮，另有別業。《俱舍》云：雜修靜慮，有五品不同，故生五淨居天。雜修者，初起無漏觀，次起有漏觀，後復起無漏觀，以有漏無漏，間雜而修故。靜慮者，定慧均等之謂；五品者，下、中、上、上勝、上極也。問：何故名為淨居？答：三果聖人，於下界中，九品思惑，俱時滅盡，故名為淨。淨者，所居之處，故名淨居也。

〖經〗阿難！苦樂兩滅，鬥心不交。如是一類，名無煩天。

〖講〗按五品，此應屬下品。苦樂兩滅，即雙亡義。鬥心即欣厭二心，當苦樂未滅時，則厭苦欣樂二心，交戰胸中，互為勝負，故曰鬥心。今既兩滅，是以不交。如是一類，名無煩天。《正脈》云：盛熱曰煩，亦狀其內心，鬱陶熱中之象，有鬥心者，所不能免，此方不交，初得清涼，故名無煩。壽一千大劫，身長一千由旬。

〖經〗機括獨行，研交無地。如是一類，名無熱天。

〖講〗此屬中品。機，即發動之機，狀念之放也。括，即收括之括，狀念之收也。獨行，言其唯一捨念，或放或收，更無餘念間雜。研交無地者：若有一念，不可言無，今更以雜修靜慮，研究此一念，了不可得，則不唯不交，即欲交亦無地矣。如是一類，名無熱天。微煩曰熱，並熱亦無，故曰無熱天。壽二千大劫，身長二千由旬。

〖經〗十方世界，妙見圓澄，更無塵象，一切沉垢。如是一類，名善見天。

〖講〗此屬上品。由修靜慮，發天眼通，既與四禪同分，亦應同見大千。十方世界：即大千之十方；妙見：即天眼通，此是修得，而非報得，更勝四禪；圓，即圓滿大千；澄，即澄清朗徹；更無塵象，外境之障隔，故圓；亦無一切沉垢，內念之留滯，故澄。內外虛融，妙見無礙。如是一類，名善見天。體淨用周，所見皆善也。壽四千大劫，身長四千由旬。

〖經〗精見現前，陶鑄無礙。如是一類，名善現天。

〖講〗此屬上勝品。精妙之見，既已現前，此句指前天，見體清淨，見用周遍，此天增修靜慮，體用兼勝。陶鑄無礙者：如陶師之範土為瓦，鑄匠之鎔金造像，能以定慧力，任運成就，隨心自在，變現無礙。如是一類，名善現天。壽八千大劫，身長八千由旬。

〖經〗究竟群幾，窮色性性，入無邊際。如是一類，名色究竟天。

〖講〗此屬上極品。究竟，即研窮之義；群幾，即群動之微也。《正脈》謂：研窮多念，至於一念，故曰究竟群幾。以雜修五品，初用多念無漏，熏多念有漏，乃至最後，用一念無漏，熏一念有漏，名上極品。窮色性性者：性是體性，上性字指色體，下性字指空體；謂窮諸色之性，而至於空性也。又色依空現，空性即色性之性故；即所謂心既熏多至少，色亦窮麤至微是也。至此身雖尚在，而境界全空，故云入無邊際，無邊即虛空，際即界畔，入空無邊處之界畔。如是一類，名色究竟天；色界至此，是為極頂，故稱色究竟天。壽一萬六千大劫，身長一萬六千由旬。二示五天別相竟。

〖經〗阿難！此不還天，彼諸四禪，四位天王，獨有欽聞，不能知見。如今世間，曠野深山，聖道場地，皆阿羅漢，所住持故，世間麤人，所不能見。

〖講〗此結示四天不見。阿難！此五不還天，彼諸第四禪天中，四位天王：指正天三，外道一，故成為四，不可指初、二、三、四禪也，因上中隔絕故。唯識謂：二禪以上，不稱王臣，此言四王，或推尊上首，略似如王也。獨有欽聞，不能知見者：以第四禪天，係有漏凡夫，但能伏惑；而五不還天，是無漏聖人，而能斷惑，聖凡有異，伏斷懸殊，故但仰嘉名，不知其受用，不見其依正也。問：彼之天王，是菩薩遊三摩地，為何不能知見？答：惟其菩薩，示跡凡夫，當與凡夫，同其知見也。如今世間下：取例發明。曠野深山，皆絕無人煙之地，皆是羅漢別境，稱為聖道場地。而世間麤人，所不能見，以不修無漏業故。二五不還天竟。

〖經〗阿難！是十八天，獨行無交，未盡形累，自此已還，名為色界。

〖講〗孤山曰：獨行無交，俱無情慾故。未盡形累，尚有色質故。以上是十八梵天，清淨無侶，故曰獨行無交。雖然離欲，依舊有化生身，未盡身形之累。自此色究竟天以還，至梵眾天，同一色界。二四禪竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, such heavenly beings — this same perfect light having already become sound — unfold this sound to reveal this wondrous principle; giving rise, thereby, to this refined conduct, they penetrate through to this bliss of quiescent cessation. This class is named Lesser Purity."

Commentary: This makes plain the form realm's third dhyāna, its three heavens. The Treatise on the Stages calls this third dhyāna the dhyāna of bliss accompanying, since this merit of samādhi arises together with a bliss that pervades the entire body. In the first two dhyānas, though a factor of bliss was present, it remained obstructed by the factor of joy; only here, with joy extinguished and bliss alone remaining pure, does this heaven receive its name. "Ānanda, such heavenly beings" carries forward the prior Light-Sound heaven, where this perfect, full light had already become accomplished as sound, serving as the very substance of the teaching; unfolding and giving voice to sound, this wondrous principle is thereby revealed. This heaven, relying on this same wondrous principle, gives rise to this refined conduct, departing from the prior heaven's agitation of joy, and giving rise instead to this pure bliss, calm and tranquil, at ease, penetrating through to this bliss of quiescent cessation. This bliss of quiescent cessation, too, is only what this settled, pure mind of samādhi gives rise to — not to be confused with the quiescent substance of the true nature itself; the name is shared, though the substance differs. Since this is only the first entry into this state, the text says "penetrate through," not yet "accomplish." This is named Lesser Purity — only the sixth consciousness has, as yet, been subdued here; this is not yet true quiescent cessation at all. Their lifespan is sixteen great kalpas, their bodies sixteen yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "This pure emptiness manifests before them, drawn forth and extended without limit; body and mind become light and at ease, accomplishing this bliss of quiescent cessation. This class is named Boundless Purity."

Commentary: "This pure emptiness manifests before them": the prior heaven's bliss of quiescent cessation still had not let go of this notion of purity itself; reaching this point, the power of samādhi grows deeper still, and even this purity, too, becomes empty — hence, "this pure emptiness manifests before them." "Drawn forth and extended without limit": emptiness draws forth purity, and purity, together with emptiness, unfolds; just as empty space itself has no boundary or limit, so too this realm of purity has no boundary or limit. One becomes aware, of oneself, that this body and mind of direct retribution are, themselves, like the great void — free of any burden, free of any obstruction — hence, "light and at ease, accomplishing this bliss of quiescent cessation." This class is named Boundless Purity, since this purity and emptiness, without limit, are therefore without any measure at all. Their lifespan is thirty-two great kalpas, their bodies thirty-two yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "This world, and this body and mind, become, all alike, perfectly pure; this virtue of purity, fully accomplished, appears before them as this same superior refuge, and they return to this bliss of quiescent cessation. This class is named Universal Purity."

Commentary: In the prior heaven, this state of purity pervaded only body and mind; reaching this point, the power of samādhi grows deeper still, and even this outer world of dependent retribution, together with this body and mind of direct retribution, dissolve into one and the same substance, both alike attaining this same emptiness and stillness — hence, "become, all alike, perfectly pure." As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this is only what the power of samādhi itself brings about — this realm changing according to samādhi, so to speak — not brought about through the power of mind-only contemplation. "This virtue of purity, fully accomplished" continues the prior clause: since everything has, alike, become perfectly pure, this virtue of pure purity is thereby accomplished, this wondrous bliss inexhaustible; this bliss subject to outflow reaches, at this point, its very utmost. One becomes aware, of oneself, that this supreme place of refuge appears before them, taking it to be the true place where one may settle body and life, the pure, blissful home-country itself. Not realizing that this still belongs to outflow, one simply returns, thereupon, to take refuge in it — hence, "returns to this bliss of quiescent cessation." This class is named Universal Purity, since, wherever one turns, all becomes this same pure bliss — hence the name. Their lifespan is sixty-four great kalpas, their bodies sixty-four yojanas in length. This closes the first section, showing the distinct marks of these three heavens.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these three superior classes are endowed with this great accord; body and mind at peace and secure, they attain boundless bliss. Though this is not yet the true attainment of this true samādhi, still, within this mind at peace and secure, joy is present here in full completion. This is named the third dhyāna."

Commentary: Ānanda, these three heavens surpass, again, the flow of the second dhyāna. In the first dhyāna, suffering and affliction do not press upon them, yet accord has not yet been attained; in the second dhyāna, anxious apprehension does not press upon them, the five consciousnesses already subdued, yet only accord alone has been attained; reaching this third dhyāna now, quiescent cessation has already been attained, the sixth consciousness already subdued — this is named being endowed with great accord. "Body and mind at peace and secure, they attain boundless bliss": in the prior two dhyānas, this mind of joy still stirred with thought, and so body and mind could not attain peace and security; now, this mind of joy having already departed, body and mind are at peace and secure, enjoying this bliss freely and spontaneously. This bliss is called boundless because this wondrous bliss they possess pervades everywhere, without limit. Within the three realms, the third dhyāna is held to be the very place of utmost bliss. Though this is not yet the true attainment, through genuine cultivation and genuine realization, of this true samādhi, still, within this mind at peace and secure, joy is present here in full completion. This mind at peace and secure is exactly the mind of the third dhyāna. In the first and second dhyānas, obstructions are departed from and superiority increases — hence called the pure mind. In the third dhyāna, bliss is attained and superiority increases — hence called the mind at peace and secure. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: the text still speaks of "joy present here in full completion" — the four words "within this mind at peace and secure" distinguish this from mere agitation; joy, properly, is what an agitated mind gives rise to, while bliss is what a settled mind dissolves into. If joy belongs to an agitated mind, then, even called bliss, it remains, in fact, a factor of joy; if it belongs to a settled mind, then, even called joy, it remains, in fact, a factor of bliss — hence this is named, together, the third dhyāna. Among the nine grounds, this is the fourth, named the ground of wondrous bliss free of joy, since this joy of the first and second dhyānas has been departed from, and this wondrous bliss of the third dhyāna attained instead. Neither the water-calamity nor the fire-calamity reaches here, yet the wind-calamity reaches here still, since the in-breath and out-breath have not yet been departed from. This closes the third dhyāna heaven.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, next, these heavenly beings — body and mind no longer pressed upon at all, the very cause of suffering already exhausted — recognize that bliss, too, is not a permanent abode, and must, in time, decay and give rise to suffering once more. And so, both minds of suffering and of bliss are, at once, entirely let go of together; this coarse, heavy quality is thereby extinguished, and the nature of pure merit is given rise to. This class is named Birth of Merit."

Commentary: This covers the fourth dhyāna's nine heavens. As Wenling puts it: the fourth dhyāna's own retribution-realm comprises only three heavens; the fourth, No-Thought, is a further branch opened out from the third, Vast Fruit. Beyond these, there are, further, five non-returner heavens, cultivated separately by noble sages through their own stillness and reflection, differing from the ordinary practice — yet, since all alike enter this same letting-go dhyāna, they are established, together, within this same shared class of the letting-go mind. "Ānanda, next, these heavenly beings" refers to one coming from within the third dhyāna: the first dhyāna departs from suffering and affliction, the second dhyāna exhausts anxious apprehension — hence, "body and mind no longer pressed upon." Suffering and affliction, anxious apprehension, are the very cause of suffering; since neither presses upon them any longer, suffering has already been exhausted, and so bliss is given rise to. The third dhyāna, body and mind at peace and secure, attains boundless bliss; this heaven, though, makes plain that even this bliss ought not to be accepted either — why so? Because bliss, too, is not a permanent thing; when the merit that sustains it is exhausted, it must, in time, decay and give rise to suffering once more. And so, one gives rise to this practice of letting go of both suffering and bliss together; both minds of suffering and of bliss are, alike, already let go of and removed — hence, "at once, entirely let go of together." Precisely because both minds are let go of together, this is named "this coarse, heavy quality extinguished" — since decay gives rise to suffering, and now both suffering and its decay are, together, transcended, this letting-go mind unmoving is exactly this nature of pure merit. Once this samādhi has already arisen, this is named birth — hence, "this class is named Birth of Merit." Their lifespan is one hundred twenty-five great kalpas, their bodies one hundred twenty-five yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "This letting-go mind becomes perfectly fused, this superior understanding pure; within this merit free of any hindrance, they attain this wondrous accord, extending to the very furthest bounds of the future. This class is named Beloved Merit."

Commentary: In the prior heaven, both minds of suffering and bliss were, at once, entirely let go of together — yet not yet perfectly fused; reaching this point, this letting-go samādhi grows deeper still, this merit and practice ripened and mature — hence, "this letting-go mind becomes perfectly fused." "This superior understanding pure" refers to this determined, superior understanding, sustaining and upholding this letting-go samādhi, subject and object both forgotten, not turned aside by any differing condition — this is what is meant by pure. And so, this letting-go mind being perfectly fused, without any hindrance or limit, the pure merit it gives rise to, too, ought to be without hindrance; within this merit free of hindrance, they attain this wondrous accord, extending to the very furthest bounds of the future. This wondrous accord means great sovereign freedom; whatever is wished for or sought is, likewise, without hindrance or limit — attaining this great sovereign freedom, extending to the furthest bounds of the future, delighting in and readily according with it throughout. This class is named Beloved Merit, since the merit of this heaven, within the entire conditioned realm, is the most beloved of all. Their lifespan is two hundred fifty great kalpas, their bodies two hundred fifty yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, from within this heaven, there are two forking paths. Should one, building upon this prior mind, take this boundless, pure light, this merit and virtue perfectly bright, and cultivate and realize it, thereby dwelling in it — this class is named Vast Fruit."

Commentary: "Ānanda, from within this heaven" — that is, from within the Beloved Merit heaven — there are two forking paths: one, the direct path, leads onward to the Vast Fruit heaven; the other, a deviant, roundabout path, leads onward to the No-Thought heaven — the very thought and karmic activity of each differing entirely. "Should one, building upon this prior mind" — that is, building upon this same wondrous, according mind of the Beloved Merit heaven, able to grant whatever one seeks according to one's wish — take, now, this boundless, pure light — that is, this same letting-go dhyāna, samādhi grown deep and giving rise to light. Within this light, using the four boundless minds to further cultivate and enrich the merit and virtue of this dhyāna, causing it to increase and become perfectly bright, departing from every defilement of the lower grounds, cultivating and realizing it, thereby dwelling in it — this class is named Vast Fruit, meaning the fruit brought about by this vast, great merit and virtue. Their lifespan is five hundred great kalpas, their bodies five hundred yojanas in length; none of the three calamities of water, fire, or wind can reach here. Among the nine grounds, this is named the ground of letting go of thought, purity — meaning that both thoughts of suffering and bliss are, together, let go of, and purity thereby attained.

Sūtra: "Should one, though, building upon this prior mind, reject both suffering and bliss together, refining and researching this letting-go mind without ever ceasing, thoroughly exhausting this path of letting-go until body and mind alike are extinguished, this thinking mind congealed like cold ash — and remain so for five hundred kalpas: such a person, since they have taken arising-and-ceasing itself as their very cause, is unable to bring to light this nature that neither arises nor ceases; for the first half-kalpa there is cessation, and for the latter half-kalpa there is arising once more. This class is named No-Thought."

Commentary: This "prior mind" refers, again, to this mind, within the Beloved Merit heaven, rejecting both suffering and bliss together; reaching the Vast Fruit heaven, one further cultivates merit, causing it to become perfectly bright; reaching the No-Thought heaven, one further cultivates this letting-go samādhi, seeking it to its very utmost — this is exactly where these two heavens fork apart. "Refining and researching this letting-go mind," the three clauses that follow, refer to this further cultivation of letting-go samādhi, searching deeply and finely, continuing without a single interruption. "Thoroughly exhausting" means exhausting it completely, to its very utmost, seeking its final consummation; "this path of letting-go" means taking this letting-go samādhi itself to be the path of nirvāṇa. "Body and mind alike extinguished, this thinking mind congealed like cold ash" means that, through this letting-go samādhi, one extinguishes this letting-go mind itself, this samādhi collapsing everything into a single undifferentiated emptiness — hence, body and mind alike dissolve away, and this thinking, reflecting mind becomes, as it were, cold ash, congealed and unmoving, like a fish trapped within ice. The fish here is a metaphor for the sixth consciousness, this mind and its mental factors; the ice is a metaphor for this letting-go samādhi — just as, in winter, water freezes into ice, and a fish trapped within the ice cannot move at all. This samādhi, too, is exactly the same: it merely subdues the present activity of the sixth consciousness, and, through the sustaining power of this samādhi, for five hundred great kalpas, this retribution-body remains unimpaired. After these five hundred kalpas, though, it disperses once more, scattering back into the cycle of rebirth. And, once the retribution of No-Thought is exhausted, such beings mostly fall into hell — since, while still in this samādhi, not having genuinely realized it, they took it to be realization, believing themselves to have attained arhatship and already left birth-and-death behind. Later, emerging from this samādhi, they slander the Three Jewels, calling the Buddha's teaching a mad deception, for having taught that the arhat's work is done, with no further existence to be received — and, on this account, they fall into hell. "Such a person, since they have taken arising-and-ceasing itself as their very cause, is unable to bring to light this nature that neither arises nor ceases" — as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this explains the reason for its impermanence, precisely because they have, in confused disorder, cultivated according to those two mistaken roots spoken of earlier. The first two clauses make plain that they have mistakenly relied on the sixth consciousness, this root of birth-and-death, taking it to be the very basis of their cultivation, forcibly congealing it into this ash-like state; the latter two clauses make plain that they have, in turn, been confused about this essential, bright purity of consciousness itself, this perfect, all-pervading nature that neither arises nor ceases, entirely failing to make any use of it at all. "For the first half-kalpa there is cessation" means that, at the very beginning of birth into this heaven, cultivating this samādhi for half a kalpa, thought first comes to cessation, and the samādhi of no-thought is thereby accomplished. "For the latter half-kalpa there is arising once more" means that, in the four-hundred-ninety-ninth and a half kalpa, this thinking mind arises once again, and this samādhi, too, thereby collapses. Having both an arising and a collapse, this can never, in the end, be any true consummation at all. This class is named No-Thought. This heaven relies on this letting-go dhyāna to extinguish the sixth consciousness, this mind and its mental factors, causing them not to arise in present activity — hence named the No-Thought heaven. Yet, since it is cultivated by means of this same letting-go dhyāna shared with the Vast Fruit heaven, the two dwell, together, in the very same place; it is only because this is a non-Buddhist practice that it receives a separate name of its own. This closes the first section, showing the distinct marks of these four heavens.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these four superior classes cannot be moved at all by any realm of suffering or bliss found throughout the entire world. Though this is not yet the true, unconditioned ground of true unmovability, still, this mind that has attained something has, in its function, grown fully ripened and mature. This is named the fourth dhyāna."

Commentary: "Ānanda, these four superior classes" — because they all alike surpass the grounds below them. "The entire world" refers, generally, to everything from the desire realm up through the third dhyāna: the desire realm is moved by suffering and bliss both; the first dhyāna, suffering and affliction do not press upon it, the second dhyāna, anxious apprehension does not press upon it — neither is moved by suffering; the third dhyāna attains boundless bliss, and though not moved by suffering, is still moved by bliss. Reaching this fourth dhyāna now, both suffering and bliss are, together, gone, dwelling instead in this letting-go samādhi — hence, "cannot be moved at all by any realm of suffering or bliss." As Wenling puts it: the fourth dhyāna is not moved by any of the three calamities — hence named the ground of unmovability. Yet its vessel-world is not truly constant, and sentient beings there still undergo arising and ceasing; though this is not yet the true, unconditioned, truly constant ground — such as the eighth ground of immovability among the ten grounds of the bodhisattva path — still, this conditioned function has, by this point, grown fully ripened and mature. Question: if it is not moved by the three calamities, how can it be said that the vessel is not truly constant, and sentient beings there undergo arising and ceasing both? Answer: other sūtras explain that, for the beings of this heaven, when they are born, their palaces and gardens and groves are born together with them; and when they die, these, too, perish together with them. "Vessel" refers to the vessel-world itself, the palaces and gardens and groves — and this is not a truly constant realm; "sentient" refers to the world of sentient beings, arising and ceasing together with it. "This mind that has attained something" refers to cultivating this letting-go samādhi, taking it to be nirvāṇa itself, expecting with certainty to attain it — this is named the mind that has attained something. This makes plain, precisely, that this conditioned function has, by this point, grown ripened and mature, no longer requiring any forced effort, operating spontaneously, unmoving of itself. This is named the fourth dhyāna. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: the first dhyāna is joined with the precepts, the virtue of the precepts predominating; the second dhyāna is joined with joy, this brightness of light predominating; the third dhyāna is joined with bliss, this pure bliss predominating; the fourth dhyāna is joined with letting-go — in the first three of its heavens, merit and virtue predominate, and in its last heaven, this letting-go samādhi predominates; this is the distinction between them. This closes the first section, the four superior-class heavens.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, within this same fourth dhyāna, there are, further, five non-returner heavens. For beings there, the nine grades of habitual tendency belonging to the lower realms have, all at once, been utterly extinguished; both suffering and bliss are, together, gone, and so there is nowhere left below for them to dwell. And so, within this same shared class of the letting-go mind, a dwelling-place is established for them."

Commentary: "Within this" refers to within this fourth dhyāna heaven; there are, further, these five kinds of non-returner heavens. The Sanskrit anāgāmin is translated "non-returner" — this is the dwelling-place of third-fruit noble ones. Because, within the lower realms — that is, within the desire realm — the nine grades of the habitual tendency of thought-delusion have already been cut off entirely, they have realized this third-fruit noble stage, and never again return to the desire realm to take birth there. "Habitual tendency" refers to the seeds of thought-delusion — that is, greed, anger, delusion, and pride, arising spontaneously, this subtle, fine delusion born together with life itself, not the same as the coarser delusion cut off through discrimination. Because this delusion is so difficult to remove, it is divided into eighty-one grades: throughout the nine grounds of the three realms, each ground is, in turn, divided into nine grades. Here, one cuts off the nine grades belonging to the ground of the five destinies mingled together — that is, the desire realm — each of the upper, middle, and lower divided, again, into three further grades, requiring seven cycles of birth and death, back and forth between the heavens and the human realm, to cut them off completely — at which point both the seeds and the present activity of this delusion are extinguished together — hence, "all at once, been utterly extinguished," realizing this third fruit. This makes plain, then, the matter of a noble one cutting off delusion — differing from the mere subduing, without cutting off, found in the fourth dhyāna. "Both suffering and bliss are, together, gone, and so there is nowhere left below for them to dwell": having cut off entirely the delusion belonging to the nine grades of the desire-realm ground, suffering is thereby gone, and there is nowhere within the desire realm left for them to dwell; and, no longer accepting the bliss of the first, second, or third dhyānas either, bliss, too, is thereby gone, and there is nowhere within the third dhyāna of the form realm left for them to dwell either — since they no longer share the same class as those there. And the samādhi they enter is exactly this letting-go samādhi in which both suffering and bliss are gone together; and so, within this same shared class of the letting-go mind belonging to the fourth dhyāna, a dwelling-place is established for them, where they dwell in order to cut off the remaining seventy-two grades of thought-delusion, and seek to realize the fruit of arhatship. Yet, though this dwelling-place is established within this same shared class, this mingled cultivation of stillness and reflection follows its own separate course of karmic activity. As the Abhidharmakośa puts it: this mingled cultivation of stillness and reflection comes in five distinct grades, and so gives rise to birth among these five pure-dwelling heavens. "Mingled" means that one first gives rise to an outflow-free contemplation, then gives rise to an outflow-subject contemplation, and afterward gives rise, once more, to an outflow-free contemplation — cultivating outflow-free and outflow-subject contemplation alternately, mingled together. "Stillness and reflection" refers to samādhi and wisdom held in equal balance; the five grades are: lower, middle, upper, superior-upper, and utmost-upper. Question: why are these called the pure dwellings? Answer: these third-fruit noble ones have, within the lower realms, already utterly extinguished the nine grades of thought-delusion all at once — hence named pure; and the place where they dwell is, accordingly, named the pure dwellings.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, both suffering and bliss extinguished, this contending mind no longer clashes. This class is named No Vexation."

Commentary: According to the five grades, this belongs to the lower grade. "Both suffering and bliss extinguished" carries the very same meaning as "both suffering and bliss, together, gone." "Contending mind" refers to these two minds of longing and aversion: while suffering and bliss have not yet been extinguished, this mind, wearied of suffering and longing for bliss, contends and battles within the breast, each in turn gaining the upper hand — hence called a contending mind. Now that both have, together, been extinguished, this contention, accordingly, no longer clashes. This class is named No Vexation. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: intense heat is called vexation, likewise describing the appearance of an inner mind burning and agitated within — something a contending mind can never avoid. Here, this contention no longer clashes, and one first attains this coolness and clarity — hence named No Vexation. Their lifespan is one thousand great kalpas, their bodies one thousand yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "This trigger-mechanism operates alone; researching where this clash might occur, there is no ground for it left at all. This class is named No Heat."

Commentary: This belongs to the middle grade. "Trigger-mechanism" refers to this mechanism of arousal, describing this thought as it releases; "held-in" describes this same thought as it is drawn back and restrained. "Operates alone" means that only this single letting-go thought, whether released or restrained, has, with it, no further thought intermingled at all. "Researching where this clash might occur, there is no ground for it left" means that, were even a single thought still present, one could not call it entirely absent; here, though, one researches this same single thought further still, through this mingled cultivation of stillness and reflection, and finds it, in the end, utterly unobtainable — so that not only does no clash occur, but were one even to wish for a clash to occur, there would be no ground left for it at all. This class is named No Heat. Slight vexation is called heat; here, even this slightness is gone — hence named No Heat. Their lifespan is two thousand great kalpas, their bodies two thousand yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "The worlds of the ten directions, this wondrous seeing perfectly clear and still, no longer harbor any trace of dust, any settled defilement at all. This class is named Good Views."

Commentary: This belongs to the upper grade. Through this cultivation of stillness and reflection, the heavenly eye is given rise to; and, sharing this same class as the fourth dhyāna, one likewise sees the great-thousand world entire. "The worlds of the ten directions" refers to the ten directions of this great-thousand world; "wondrous seeing" refers to the heavenly eye — cultivated and attained here, not received merely as retribution, and so surpassing even the fourth dhyāna itself. "Perfectly" means encompassing this great-thousand world in full; "clear" means limpid and thoroughly transparent — no dust or trace obstructing it from outside, hence perfect; no settled defilement of thought lingering within, hence clear. Inward and outward, empty and fused, this wondrous seeing is free of any obstruction at all. This class is named Good Views — the very substance pure, its function pervading everywhere, so that whatever is seen is entirely good. Their lifespan is four thousand great kalpas, their bodies four thousand yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "This refined seeing manifests before them, molding and casting without any obstruction at all. This class is named Good Manifestation."

Commentary: This belongs to the superior-upper grade. This refined, wondrous seeing has already manifested before them — this clause refers to the prior heaven, this substance of seeing pure, this function of seeing pervading everywhere; this heaven, further cultivating this stillness and reflection, has both substance and function superior together. "Molding and casting without any obstruction" is like a potter molding clay into a vessel, or a founder casting metal into an image — through the power of samādhi and wisdom, accomplishing spontaneously, freely, according to the mind, manifesting and transforming without any obstruction at all. This class is named Good Manifestation. Their lifespan is eight thousand great kalpas, their bodies eight thousand yojanas in length.

Sūtra: "Thoroughly exhausting every subtlety of movement, they arrive at the very nature of the nature of form itself, entering into boundlessness without limit. This class is named the Summit of Form."

Commentary: This belongs to the utmost-upper grade. "Thoroughly exhausting" carries the meaning of researching to the very utmost; "the subtlety of movement" refers to the subtlest trace of movement within the crowd of thoughts. As the Standard Meaning Commentary holds: researching many thoughts down to a single thought — hence, "thoroughly exhausting the subtlety of movement." Through this mingled cultivation across the five grades, one begins by using many thoughts free of outflow to permeate many thoughts subject to outflow, until, at the very last, a single thought free of outflow permeates a single thought subject to outflow — this is named the utmost-upper grade. "Arrive at the very nature of the nature of form itself": "nature" here means substantial nature; the first "nature" refers to the substance of form, the second "nature" refers to the substance of emptiness — meaning that one exhausts the nature of every form, arriving, at last, at the nature of emptiness itself. And form, too, depends on emptiness to manifest — this nature of emptiness being, precisely, the nature of the nature of form itself; that is to say, as the mind is permeated down from many thoughts to few, form, too, is exhausted down from coarse to subtle. At this point, though the body still remains, this bounded realm has become, entirely, empty — hence, "entering into boundlessness without limit." Boundlessness refers to empty space itself; limit refers to the very boundary of a realm — entering into the boundary of the realm of the base of boundless space. This class is named the Summit of Form — the form realm, reaching this point, comes to its very utmost peak; hence called the Summit of Form. Their lifespan is sixteen thousand great kalpas, their bodies sixteen thousand yojanas in length. This closes the second section, showing the distinct marks of these five heavens.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these non-returner heavens are known, among the four kings of the fourth dhyāna itself, only by report, revered from afar — they are unable to truly know or see them at all. It is exactly as in this present world, where the wilderness and deep mountains hold sites of sacred practice, sustained entirely by arhats dwelling there unseen — such places remain invisible to coarse, ordinary people of the world."

Commentary: This closes by showing why these four kings cannot see them. "Ānanda, these five non-returner heavens" — among the four kings of that same fourth dhyāna — refers to three true kings, together with one heterodox king, making four in all; this cannot refer to the kings of the first, second, or third dhyānas, since these are separated by intervening grounds. According to Consciousness-Only teaching, from the second dhyāna upward there are, properly speaking, no kings or ministers spoken of at all; this reference to "four kings" here, then, is perhaps only an honorific term for whoever stands foremost there, loosely resembling a king. "Known only by report, revered from afar, unable to truly know or see them" — because the fourth dhyāna heaven remains an ordinary being subject to outflow, able only to subdue delusion, while these five non-returner heavens are outflow-free noble ones, able to cut delusion off entirely; noble and ordinary differ, subduing and cutting off are worlds apart, and so these kings can only look up with reverence at their exalted name, without knowing what they enjoy there, or seeing their dependent and direct retribution at all. Question: if those heavenly kings are bodhisattvas roaming in samādhi, why can they not know or see them? Answer: precisely because these bodhisattvas manifest traces as ordinary beings, they share, together with ordinary beings, the very same knowledge and views. "It is exactly as in this present world" onward draws an illustration to make this plain: the wilderness and deep mountains are, all of them, places entirely empty of human habitation, and yet are exactly the private domain of arhats, called sites of sacred practice — and yet coarse people of the world are unable to see them, since they have not cultivated the outflow-free path themselves. This closes the second section, the five non-returner heavens.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these eighteen heavens proceed each alone, without any intermingling among them; they have not yet exhausted the burden of form. From here downward, all of this is named the form realm."

Commentary: As Guhshan puts it: "proceed each alone, without any intermingling" means that all alike are free of sexual desire; "not yet exhausted the burden of form" means that material substance still remains. Above, these are the eighteen Brahmā heavens, pure and without companionship — hence, "proceed each alone, without any intermingling." Though free of desire, they still possess a transformation-born body, and have not yet exhausted the burden of form. From this Summit of Form heaven downward, all the way to the Retinue of Brahmā heaven, belong, together, to this same form realm. This closes the fourth dhyāna.

〖經〗復次，阿難！從是有頂，色邊際中，其間復有，二種歧路，若於捨心，發明智慧，慧光圓通，便出塵界，成阿羅漢，入菩薩乘。如是一類，名為回心大阿羅漢。

〖講〗溫陵曰：自此而上，明無色界四天，無業果色，有定果色，依正皆然。四天皆依偏空修進：初、厭色依空；二、厭空依識；三、色空識三都滅，而依識性；四、依識性，以滅窮研，而不得真滅；是皆有為增上善果，未出輪迴，不成聖道者也。此將明無色界四天，先簡回心超出也。阿難！從是色究竟天，居色界之頂，是為有頂；與無色為鄰，故云色邊際中。其間上進，復有二種歧路。因三果人，根有利鈍，其利根者，即於色究竟，捨定心中，發無漏人空智慧，慧光圓通，斷盡思惑，即便超出塵界，離分段生，證偏空理，成阿羅漢；不以小果為足，進修大因，故云入菩薩乘。其鈍根者，在色究竟天中，復由定心，欣上厭下，滅色歸空，生無色界，此其差別也。如是一類，即利根一類。回心，即回其欣上厭下之心，頓出三界。大阿羅漢，即回其捨大取小心，速入大乘；但據跡猶在聲聞，故仍稱羅漢，而以大字揀之。初標歧除聖竟。

〖經〗若在捨心，捨厭成就，覺身為礙，銷礙入空。如是一類，名為空處。

〖講〗此正示無色界，四空天也。若在捨心：長水曰：捨心有二：一者、若於有頂，用無漏道，斷惑入空，即樂定那含也。二者、若於廣果，用有漏道，伏惑入定，即凡夫外道也。捨厭成就者：即厭有趣空也。覺得有身，尚是有礙，於是銷除形礙，以入於空也。溫陵曰：厭己形礙，堅修空觀。滅身歸無，即厭色依空者也。名空處定，故報生空處也。如是一類，名為空處，壽二萬大劫。四空天無業果色，故不言身形之長短也。

〖經〗諸礙既銷，無礙無滅，其中惟留阿賴耶識，全於末那，半分微細。如是一類，名為識處。

〖講〗溫陵曰：諸礙既銷而無，則不依於色；無礙之喜亦滅，則不依於空。不依於色，躡前天果相；不依於空，明本天功行與果相，即是雙厭色空，惟依於識也。其中惟留阿賴耶識，全於末那，半分微細者：其中於所存識中，惟留第八阿賴耶識，於全分末那，第七識中，半分微細猶存，即是厭空依識也。阿賴耶，此云藏識，但是常言而已，意顯末那識，言末那以阿賴耶為體故；末那，即第七識，此云染污依，為第六意識所依之根。末那託六識，緣六塵，行相麤顯，托塵似有，離塵實無；今既色空俱滅，識已無托。外緣麤顯者，半分已滅唯留內緣八識。微細者，半分獨存，如是一類，名為識處定，故報生識處也。以末那為能緣心，賴耶為所緣境耳。壽四萬大劫。

〖經〗空色既亡，識心都滅，十方寂然，回無攸往。如是一類，名無所有處。

〖講〗初天滅色歸空，次天滅空歸識，故曰空色既亡。識心，即指末那半分微細猶存。此天以緣識心散，反能破定故。憑深定力，將幽微精細之半分，亦伏不行，故謂都滅。是知此滅，非如羅漢，種現俱斷也。下即定中所證境界，半分微細既盡，唯有賴耶獨存；賴耶是無分別，惟覺十方，寂然冥然，迥無攸往，攸即所也，不復前進矣。此為外道，昧為冥諦之處也。如是一類，名無所有處，色、空、識三者，皆無所有也。壽六萬大劫。

〖經〗識性不動，以滅窮研，於無盡中，發宣盡性，如存不存，若盡非盡。如是一類，名為非想、非非想處。

〖講〗賴耶獨存，稱為識性。識性即是藏性，藏性由來不動，如本經云：常住妙明，不動周圓是也。惜彼凡天，不達斯理，但見賴耶堅固，不可動搖，以滅定之力，深窮研習，於無盡中，強以發宣；欲盡其性，然識性不盡有二義：一者、識性即是藏性，凡聖皆無可盡之理。二者、若約種子，則凡外未秉如來，斷種法門，終不可盡。那含，復是鈍根，亦非盡時。此天既欲盡其性，由定力所逼，識性雖存，而不起現行。故曰如存不存，似殘燈之半滅也。雖見識性盡，其實未盡，故曰若盡非盡，似殘燈之半明也。如是一類，名為非想，非非想處天。《正脈》云：末後環師，承如存不存，以結非想；承若盡非盡，以結非非想；得其語脈矣！蓋非想，即非有想，非非想，即非無想耳。壽八萬大劫。二正列四天竟。

〖經〗此等窮空，不盡空理，從不還天，聖道窮者。如是一類，名不回心，鈍阿羅漢。若從無想，諸外道天，窮空不歸，迷漏無聞，便入輪轉。

〖講〗此辨聖凡，出墜各異。此等，指四空處；窮空者：正脈云：初天，窮色令銷；二天，窮空令無；三天，窮識令滅；四天，窮性令盡。蓋前二窮境，後二窮心；欲令心境俱空，故總謂窮空。不盡空理者：凡外未了人空之理，小聖不達法空之理，何況圓頓之旨，心境本空，豈待銷滅哉？故總斷其不了耳。此下方明聖凡出墜，今先明聖人超出三界，言彼住非想天中，若從五不還天，修習聖道，窮空而來者，以窮空力，經歷四天，斷四地惑，三十六品盡，證我空理，成阿羅漢，即出三界；如是一類，名不回心，鈍阿羅漢。言不回心有二義：一者、於色界頂，不早回厭有趣空之心，速成羅漢。二者，沉空滯寂，不早回捨小取大之心，向菩薩乘。名為鈍阿羅漢，乃對前利根而說。利根者，乃不經四天，少修二十萬大劫也。次明凡夫墜入輪迴，言彼住非想天中。若從無想，諸外道天，窮空不歸者；若從無想，廣果諸天中，但惟修習有漏禪定。窮空來者，則迷有漏天，作無為想；無多聞性，不知三界之內，無有安身立命處。所以八萬劫滿，無所歸託，即當下墜，而入輪迴；隨其宿業；依然流轉諸趣；即所謂：饒經八萬劫，終是落空亡。問：後經謂無想，妄執涅槃，而臨終興謗墮獄，何得而入四天耶？答：但興謗墮獄，間或有之，無想同分，根性萬殊，未必各各皆然，無想天中，壽五百大劫，初半劫滅，後半劫生，滅時似無，生時還有，以還有故，厭有趣空，所以修四空定，何足疑也。

〖經〗阿難！是諸天上，各各天人，則是凡夫，業果酬答，答盡入輪。彼之天王，即是菩薩，遊三摩地，漸次增進，回向聖倫，所修行路。

〖講〗此明諸天，王民不同也。是諸天上者：通指六欲，四禪、四空，惟除五不還天，是聖人故。不還以外，各各天人，皆是凡夫，業果酬答。答盡入輪者：業果，對業因言，此等天人，因中唯修有漏善業，十善八定等，為實功業；而六欲、四禪、四空業果，以酬答之；縱獲勝福，不過酬答前因而已。天福既盡，散入諸趣，如鬱頭藍弗天人，因中在山中習定，眾鳥爭喧，嫌其闠鬧，曾動一念瞋心，最好有一飛狸，將其食盡。因中起此一念，後修定生天，生到非想非非想天；天報既畢，墮落飛狸之身。問：上界無惡，何以墮落三途？答：藏識種子，歷劫不亡，隨彼熟種，任運而墮，豈揀三途，寧不可惜！所以如來苦勸：老實念佛，帶業往生，橫超三界，疾出生死也。彼之天王，即是菩薩，遊三摩地者：彼等之天王，非是凡夫，即是大乘菩薩，住於三摩地，遊戲神通，寄位天王之位，濟物利生，成就己德。漸次增進，回向聖倫，所修行路者：行漸次而增，位漸次而進，無非借此天位，同向無上菩提之果，以是悟後之修，一一修行，悉皆回事向理，以入聖人之倫類。所修者，乃楞嚴大定，即所謂妙修行路者是也。所以不落輪迴。

〖經〗阿難！是四空天，身心滅盡，定性現前，無業果色，從此逮終，名無色界。

〖講〗此結四空之名也。阿難！是四空天，前二天，身境全空，是身滅盡；後二天，識亦不起，是心滅盡；身心既已俱滅，定性現前，則在定時，所有定果色，定中隨化依正身境，自在受用，《顯揚論》說，為定自在所生色者，是也。無業果色者：約出定時，無欲色二界所感依正之業果色。《正脈》云：身心俱寂，依正皆空，聖眼觀之，三尺識神，似中有也。從此逮終者：逮，到也，從初空處，而到非想處，是謂三界終極於此，名無色界。但憑定力，暫無依正，非真蘊空，永絕業果也。初正列諸天竟。

〖經〗此皆不了，妙覺明心，積妄發生，妄有三界，中間妄隨，七趣沉溺，補特伽羅，各從其類。

〖講〗此總結三界，皆是虛妄。自四王以至非想，皆由不了自己本妙覺性，本明真心，此性本來自妙，寸絲不掛，此心本來自明，靈光獨耀，圓陀陀，光灼灼，清淨本然，而覓三界依正，本不可得。由不了故，所以從迷積迷，以妄起妄，遂有業轉現，三種細相發生；由三細，即有六麤；惑、業、苦三，悉皆俱備，故妄有三界。中間妄隨七趣沉溺者：中間，即三界之中間，復隨妄業，沉溺七趣，以受其報。八識田中，無量劫業種俱有，隨何種業成熟，即趣何趣受報。補特伽羅：此翻有情，又云數取趣；謂諸有情，起惑造業，於三界中，數數取著，諸趣受生。各從其類者：謂各從自己之業因，而受同類之業果；故文中每言，如是一類也。六諸天趣竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Further, Ānanda: from this Summit of Existence, at the very edge of form, there are, again, two forking paths within it. Should one, within this letting-go mind, bring forth wisdom, this light of wisdom perfectly penetrating, they thereby transcend this realm of dust altogether, become an arhat, and enter the bodhisattva vehicle. This class is named a great arhat who has turned their mind around."

Commentary: As Wenling puts it: from this point upward, this makes plain the four formless heavens — possessing no form born of karma, yet possessing a form born of samādhi, both dependent and direct retribution alike being so. All four heavens rely on a one-sided emptiness for their advancement: the first rejects form and relies on emptiness; the second rejects emptiness and relies on consciousness; the third, form, emptiness, and consciousness all three extinguished, relies on the very nature of consciousness itself; the fourth, relying on this nature of consciousness, exhausts it through further research, yet never attains any true cessation at all. All of these are, alike, conditioned, superior-good fruits, not yet transcending the wheel of rebirth, not yet accomplishing the noble path. This is about to make plain the four formless heavens; first, though, it distinguishes the case of one who turns their mind around and transcends altogether. Ānanda, from this Summit of Form heaven, dwelling at the very summit of the form realm, this is named the Summit of Existence; and, bordering upon the formless realm, this is named the very edge of form. Advancing further from here, there are, again, two forking paths. Because among third-fruit noble ones, faculties differ between the sharp and the dull: those of sharp faculties, right within this letting-go samādhi at the Summit of Form, bring forth this outflow-free wisdom of the emptiness of self, this light of wisdom perfectly complete, cutting off entirely the delusion of thought, and thereby transcend this realm of dust altogether, departing from fragmentary birth-and-death, realizing the truth of one-sided emptiness, and becoming an arhat; yet, not content to rest in this lesser fruit, they advance further to cultivate this great cause — hence, "enter the bodhisattva vehicle." Those of dull faculties, remaining within the Summit of Form heaven, rely, once more, upon this same samādhi-mind, delighting in what lies above and wearied of what lies below, extinguishing form and returning to emptiness, and are thereby born into the formless realm — this being the very point where the two paths diverge. This class refers to the class of sharp faculties. "Turned their mind around" means turning around this very mind that delighted in what lies above and wearied of what lies below, transcending the three realms all at once. "Great arhat" means turning around this mind that grasped at the lesser and cast aside the great, swiftly entering the great vehicle — yet, since their outward trace still remains among the śrāvakas, they are still called arhat, distinguished only by the word "great." This closes the first section, distinguishing the case of the noble one who transcends.

Sūtra: "Should one, though, remain within this letting-go mind, this rejection and this weariness both brought to completion, becoming aware that this very body itself is an obstruction, and dissolving this obstruction to enter into emptiness — this class is named the Base of Emptiness."

Commentary: This now properly shows the four formless heavens. "Should one remain within this letting-go mind" — Changshui says: this letting-go mind is of two kinds. One: should this be at the Summit of Form, using the outflow-free path to cut off delusion and enter emptiness — this is the non-returner who delights in samādhi. Two: should this be at the Vast Fruit heaven, using the outflow-subject path to subdue delusion and enter samādhi — this is the ordinary, non-Buddhist practitioner. "This rejection and this weariness both brought to completion" means being wearied of existence and delighting in emptiness. Becoming aware that even having a body remains itself an obstruction, one thereupon dissolves this bodily obstruction, entering into emptiness instead. As Wenling puts it: wearied of the very obstruction of one's own form, one firmly cultivates the contemplation of emptiness; extinguishing the body and returning to nothingness — this is exactly rejecting form and relying on emptiness. This is named the samādhi of the Base of Emptiness, and so one's retribution is birth into the Base of Emptiness. This class is named the Base of Emptiness. Their lifespan is twenty thousand great kalpas. The four formless heavens possess no form born of karma, and so their bodily height is not spoken of at all.

Sūtra: "Every obstruction having already been dissolved, this freedom from obstruction, too, is extinguished; within this, only the ālaya-consciousness alone remains, together with a subtle half-portion still remaining of the entirety of manas. This class is named the Base of Consciousness."

Commentary: As Wenling puts it: every obstruction having already been dissolved and become empty, one no longer relies upon form; and this joy in this very freedom from obstruction, too, is extinguished, one no longer relying upon emptiness either. Not relying upon form carries forward this prior heaven's fruit-characteristic; not relying upon emptiness makes plain this heaven's own cultivated practice as against this same fruit-characteristic — that is, rejecting both form and emptiness together, relying only upon consciousness instead. "Within this, only the ālaya-consciousness alone remains, together with a subtle half-portion still remaining of the entirety of manas" means that, among the consciousnesses that still remain, only the eighth consciousness, the ālaya, remains; and, of the entirety of the seventh consciousness, manas, only a subtle half-portion still remains — this is exactly wearied of emptiness and relying upon consciousness instead. Ālaya is translated "storehouse consciousness" — this is only its common name; the intent here is to point to manas itself, since manas takes the ālaya as its very substance. Manas is precisely the seventh consciousness, translated "the defiled support," being the very root upon which the sixth consciousness, mano-vijñāna, depends. Manas relies upon the sixth consciousness, taking hold of the six kinds of sense-object, its activity coarse and evident, appearing to exist by clinging to these objects, yet, apart from these objects, having no real existence at all; now that form and emptiness alike have both been extinguished, this consciousness has nothing left to cling to at all. Its coarse, evident outward conditioning — this half-portion has already ceased; its subtle, fine quality — this remaining half-portion still persists alone. This class is named the samādhi of the Base of Consciousness, and so one's retribution is birth into the Base of Consciousness — manas being the mind that takes hold of the object, and the ālaya being the very object it takes hold of. Their lifespan is forty thousand great kalpas.

Sūtra: "Form and emptiness both already gone, this discriminating mind of consciousness, too, is entirely extinguished; the ten directions become utterly still, with nowhere left at all to turn toward or go. This class is named the Base of Nothing-at-All."

Commentary: The first heaven extinguishes form and returns to emptiness; the next heaven extinguishes emptiness and returns to consciousness — hence, "form and emptiness both already gone." "This discriminating mind" refers to this subtle half-portion of manas still remaining. This heaven regards this very conditioning by this discriminating mind as itself able to disrupt samādhi; relying on the deep power of this samādhi, this fine, subtle half-portion, too, is subdued and kept from operating — hence called "entirely extinguished." One should understand that this cessation is not the same as an arhat's cessation, where both seed and present activity are cut off together. What follows describes the realm realized within this samādhi: this subtle half-portion having now come to its end, only the ālaya remains, alone. The ālaya is without discrimination at all; it perceives only the ten directions, still and remote, with nowhere at all left to turn toward — "toward" here meaning "a place to go." One no longer advances any further at all. This is precisely the place where non-Buddhist practitioners mistake this for the "primal principle," shrouded in darkness. This class is named the Base of Nothing-at-All, since form, emptiness, and consciousness — all three — are, alike, nowhere to be found at all. Their lifespan is sixty thousand great kalpas.

Sūtra: "This very nature of consciousness remains unmoved; and yet, through this power of cessation, one researches it to its very utmost, striving, within this boundless nature, to bring forth and proclaim its exhaustion — so that it seems to exist, yet does not exist; seems exhausted, yet is not exhausted. This class is named the Base of Neither Perception nor Non-Perception."

Commentary: The ālaya remaining alone is called the nature of consciousness. This nature of consciousness is exactly the nature of the treasury itself; the nature of the treasury has, from the very beginning, never been moved at all — as this sūtra itself puts it: constantly abiding, wondrously bright, unmoving, perfect and complete. It is only that these ordinary heavenly beings, failing to penetrate this principle, and seeing only that this ālaya is firm and unshakable, use, instead, the power of this samādhi of cessation to research and study it deeply, striving, within this boundless nature, to forcibly bring it forth and proclaim it, wishing to exhaust this very nature. Yet this nature of consciousness cannot, in fact, be exhausted, for two reasons: first, this nature of consciousness is exactly the nature of the treasury itself, and there is, for noble and ordinary alike, no principle by which it could ever be exhausted at all. Second, considered in terms of its seeds, then, for any ordinary being or non-Buddhist practitioner who has not yet taken hold of the Tathāgata's own method for cutting off these seeds, it can, in the end, never be exhausted at all — and the non-returner, being, moreover, of dull faculties, is likewise not yet at the time for its exhaustion. This heaven, wishing, nonetheless, to exhaust this very nature, is, through the pressure of the power of samādhi, brought to a state where, though the nature of consciousness still exists, it no longer manifests in present activity — hence, "it seems to exist, yet does not exist," resembling a lamp half-extinguished. Though it appears that this nature of consciousness has been exhausted, it has not, in fact, been exhausted at all — hence, "seems exhausted, yet is not exhausted," resembling a lamp half-lit still. This class is named the Base of Neither Perception nor Non-Perception. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: at the very end, Master Huan, taking up "seems to exist, yet does not exist," concludes with "neither perception"; and taking up "seems exhausted, yet is not exhausted," concludes with "nor non-perception" — this captures precisely the thread of the text. That is to say: "neither perception" means it is not that perception exists; "nor non-perception" means it is not that perception is entirely absent either. Their lifespan is eighty thousand great kalpas. This closes the second section, listing in full these four heavens.

Sūtra: "All of these beings, exhausting emptiness itself, have not yet exhausted the very principle of emptiness. Should one, from the non-returner heavens, exhaust this same noble path all the way through — this class is named a dull arhat who has not turned their mind around. Should one, though, from the No-Thought heaven, or from any of the various non-Buddhist heavens, exhaust emptiness itself without ever truly returning — deluded by outflow, without any real learning — such a one falls, instead, straight back into the turning wheel of rebirth."

Commentary: This distinguishes how the noble one and the ordinary being, alike, emerge or fall — each by an entirely different path. "All of these" refers to these four formless bases; "exhausting emptiness" — as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: in the first heaven, form is exhausted and made to vanish; in the second heaven, emptiness is exhausted and made nonexistent; in the third heaven, consciousness is exhausted and made to cease; in the fourth heaven, this very nature is exhausted and made complete. The first two exhaust the outer realm, the latter two exhaust the mind itself; wishing to make both mind and realm, together, empty, this is called, generally, exhausting emptiness. "Have not yet exhausted the principle of emptiness" means that ordinary and non-Buddhist practitioners have not yet understood the principle of the emptiness of self, and lesser noble ones have not yet penetrated the principle of the emptiness of phenomena — let alone the perfect, sudden teaching, where mind and realm are, originally, already empty of themselves, with no need for any dissolving or extinguishing at all! And so all are, together, judged as failing to understand. What follows now makes plain how the noble one and the ordinary being, alike, emerge from or fall back into the three realms. First, this makes plain how the noble one transcends the three realms: that is, one dwelling within the Base of Neither Perception nor Non-Perception who, coming from among the five non-returner heavens, cultivates the noble path, exhausting emptiness along the way — through the power of exhausting emptiness, passing through these four heavens, cutting off the delusion belonging to these four grounds, all thirty-six grades entirely, realizing the truth of the emptiness of self, and thereby transcending the three realms. This class is named a dull arhat who has not turned their mind around. To say "not turned their mind around" carries two meanings: first, at the very summit of the form realm, not turning around early enough this mind wearied of existence and delighting in emptiness, to swiftly become an arhat. Second, sinking into and clinging to this quiescent emptiness, not turning around early enough this mind that grasped at the lesser and cast aside the great, toward the bodhisattva vehicle. Hence named a dull arhat — spoken of here in contrast to the sharp-faculty one spoken of earlier. The sharp-faculty one, without passing through these four heavens at all, needs only a little more than two hundred thousand great kalpas of cultivation. Next, this makes plain how the ordinary being falls back into the cycle of rebirth: that is, one dwelling within the Base of Neither Perception nor Non-Perception who, coming from the No-Thought heaven, or from any of the various non-Buddhist heavens, having cultivated only outflow-subject dhyāna, exhausts emptiness without ever truly returning: mistaking this outflow-subject heaven for something unconditioned, harboring the notion that it is unconditioned; lacking any real learning at all, not knowing that, within the three realms, there is, nowhere, any place at all where body and life can truly settle. And so, once these eighty thousand kalpas are exhausted, with nowhere left to turn to or rely upon, they fall, then and there, straight down, into the turning wheel of rebirth — following, once more, according to their prior karma, drifting on through the various destinies just as before; exactly as it is said: though one may pass through eighty thousand kalpas, in the end, one still falls back into emptiness and is lost. Question: a later passage states that the being of the No-Thought heaven, falsely clinging to it as nirvāṇa, slanders the teaching at the moment of death and falls into hell — how, then, can it be said that such beings enter these four heavens instead? Answer: slandering the teaching and falling into hell does, indeed, occur from time to time — yet, among those who share this same class of No-Thought, their underlying faculties differ in countless ways, and it need not be that every single one of them follows the very same course. Within the No-Thought heaven, the lifespan is five hundred great kalpas: cessation for the first half-kalpa, arising once more for the latter half-kalpa — at the moment of cessation, thought seems entirely absent, yet, at the moment of arising, it is present once more; and, precisely because thought is present once more, one becomes wearied of existence and delights in emptiness instead, and so cultivates these four formless samādhis — there is nothing at all strange in this.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, throughout all these heavens, each and every heavenly being there is simply an ordinary being, receiving the fruit of karma in due repayment; once this repayment is exhausted, they, too, enter the turning wheel of rebirth once more. Yet the very kings of these heavens are themselves bodhisattvas, roaming freely within samādhi, advancing gradually, step by step, turning their practice toward the very ranks of the noble ones — this being precisely the path along which they cultivate."

Commentary: This makes plain how the kings and the common people of these heavens differ from one another. "Throughout all these heavens" refers, generally, to the six desire-heavens, the four dhyānas, and the four formless heavens — excepting only the five non-returner heavens, since these belong to noble ones alone. Apart from the non-returner heavens, each and every heavenly being elsewhere is simply an ordinary being, receiving the fruit of karma in due repayment. "Once this repayment is exhausted, they enter the turning wheel of rebirth once more" — "fruit of karma," spoken in relation to "cause of karma," means that these heavenly beings, throughout their causal stage, cultivated only outflow-subject wholesome karma — the ten wholesome deeds, the eight dhyānas, and so forth — taking these as their genuine merit and practice; and the fruits of the six desire-heavens, the four dhyānas, and the four formless heavens repay them accordingly. Even should they attain superior blessings, this is nothing more than repayment for their prior causes. Once the blessings of heaven are exhausted, they scatter back down into the various destinies once more — just as, in the case of the heavenly being Udraka Rāmaputra, who, while cultivating samādhi deep in the mountains during his causal stage, was disturbed by a flock of contending, noisy birds, and, resenting the disturbance, gave rise to a single thought of anger, wishing that a flying fox might devour them all. Having given rise to this single thought during his causal stage, he later cultivated samādhi and was born into heaven, reaching the very Base of Neither Perception nor Non-Perception; and when this heavenly retribution came to its end, he fell, accordingly, into the body of a flying fox. Question: the upper realms are free of evil — how, then, could such a being fall into the three evil destinies? Answer: the seeds stored within consciousness are never lost, however many kalpas may pass; and, following whatever seed has ripened, one falls accordingly, without regard for whether this leads to the three evil destinies or not — how could this not be a matter for the deepest regret! This is exactly why the Tathāgata so earnestly urges: recite the Buddha's name in all sincerity, be born, carrying karma still unresolved, into the Pure Land, and transcend the three realms directly, swiftly leaving birth-and-death behind altogether. "Yet the very kings of these heavens are themselves bodhisattvas, roaming freely within samādhi" means that the kings of these heavens are not, in fact, ordinary beings at all, but great-vehicle bodhisattvas, abiding within samādhi, roaming freely through spiritual powers, taking temporary residence in the rank of a heavenly king, to bring benefit and salvation to beings there, while accomplishing their own virtue at the same time. "Advancing gradually, step by step, turning their practice toward the very ranks of the noble ones — this being precisely the path along which they cultivate" means that their practice advances by gradual stages, and their rank, too, advances by gradual stages, using this heavenly rank as nothing more than a temporary support, all alike turning toward the very fruit of unsurpassed bodhi; this being precisely the cultivation that follows enlightenment, every single practice, alike, turned from phenomena toward principle, thereby entering into the very ranks of the noble ones. What they cultivate is precisely this great Śūraṅgama samādhi — that is, exactly this same wondrous path of cultivation spoken of throughout this sūtra. And so they never fall back into the turning wheel of rebirth at all.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, in these four formless heavens, body and mind alike have been extinguished; the very nature of samādhi manifests before them, with no form born of karmic fruit remaining at all. From here all the way to their very end, this is named the formless realm."

Commentary: This closes by naming these four formless heavens. Ānanda, in these four formless heavens: in the first two heavens, both body and its outer realm are entirely empty — this is body extinguished; in the latter two heavens, consciousness, too, no longer arises — this is mind extinguished. Body and mind alike having already been extinguished, the very nature of samādhi manifests before them; and so, while within samādhi, whatever form arises through samādhi — the dependent and direct retribution freely manifested and enjoyed while within samādhi, transformed at will — is precisely what the Elucidation Treatise calls form produced by the sovereign freedom of samādhi. "No form born of karmic fruit" means that, upon emerging from samādhi, there remains no form born as the karmic fruit of the desire or form realms, dependent or direct. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: body and mind both utterly still, dependent and direct retribution both alike empty — in the eyes of a noble one, this is like a mere three-inch spark of consciousness, resembling the intermediate state between death and rebirth. "From here all the way to their very end" — "reaching" means arriving; from the very first, the Base of Emptiness, all the way to arriving at the Base of Neither Perception nor Non-Perception, this is said to be where the three realms come to their utmost end — this is named the formless realm. Yet this is only through the power of samādhi that, for a time, there is no dependent or direct retribution at all — this is not a truly emptying-out of the aggregates in reality, nor a permanent severing of all karmic fruit whatsoever. This closes the first section, listing in full all of these heavens.

Sūtra: "All of this comes about simply because beings fail to understand this wondrous, enlightened, bright mind of theirs; falseness accumulates upon falseness and gives rise to all of this, so that these three realms come to exist falsely, and, in the midst of them, beings falsely follow along, sinking down into these seven destinies — each such being, each such person, following, accordingly, their own particular kind."

Commentary: This closes by summing up that the three realms, altogether, are simply false. From the Four Heavenly Kings all the way up to the Base of Neither Perception nor Non-Perception, all of this comes about simply because beings fail to understand their own wondrous, enlightened nature, this originally bright, true mind. This nature is, from the very outset, inherently wondrous, without a single thread attached to it at all; this mind is, from the very outset, inherently bright, its spiritual light shining alone, perfectly round and utterly luminous, pure and unconditioned from the very beginning — and, seeking within it for any dependent or direct retribution of the three realms, none whatsoever can, in truth, ever be found. Simply because beings fail to understand this, confusion accumulates upon confusion, falseness arises from falseness, and so the three subtle marks — karmic activity, the perceiving subject, and the perceived realm — come to arise; and from these three subtle marks there come, in turn, the six coarse marks; delusion, karma, and suffering, all three, are thereupon present in full — hence, these three realms come to exist falsely. "In the midst of them, beings falsely follow along, sinking down into these seven destinies": "in the midst" refers to the very midst of these three realms; beings, following, further, their own false karma, sink down into these seven destinies, there to undergo their retribution. Within the field of the eighth consciousness lie stored the karmic seeds of countless kalpas; whichever karma happens to ripen, that same destiny is entered, to receive its corresponding retribution there. "Pudgala" is translated "sentient being," also rendered "one who repeatedly takes hold of a destiny" — meaning that every sentient being, giving rise to delusion and creating karma, repeatedly clings and attaches, again and again, within these three realms, taking birth, over and over, among these various destinies. "Each such being, each such person, following, accordingly, their own particular kind" means that each being follows their own particular karmic cause, and receives, accordingly, the corresponding karmic fruit belonging to that same kind — hence why the text, throughout, so often says, "this class." This closes the sixth destiny, the heavens.

Sūtra: "Further, Ānanda: within these three realms there are, again, four kinds of asura."

Commentary: Sanskrit asura, also transliterated asūra, these being merely regional variants of the same Sanskrit pronunciation. The Yogācārabhūmi translates this "not-heaven," the ancients explaining: possessing the good fortune of the heavens, without the very virtue of the heavens. Older translations render it "without proper form" — the Dīrgha Āgama states that, when the asuras give birth to daughters, they are beautiful, but when they give birth to sons, they are mostly ugly, and so the name is drawn from the male. According to the order in which the Buddha first set out the seven destinies, each proceeds from the inferior toward the superior; the asuras are placed here, at this point, precisely because they, alone, possess all four modes of birth at once. "Further, Ānanda: within these three realms" — according to the various sūtras and treatises, the dwelling-place of the asuras would seem confined to the desire realm alone; yet the text here says "within these three realms" because, according to the earlier section, these three realms exist only falsely, and beings, in their midst, falsely follow along, sinking down into these seven destinies — hence, "within these three realms." "There are, again, four kinds" means that, divided by lineage, there are four: egg-born, womb-born, moisture-born, and transformation-born — and, receiving birth accordingly, they are divided among the heavens, humans, ghosts, and animals. Though their lineages differ in these four ways, their nature is, alike, given to great anger, and their conduct, alike, given to great jealousy — nature and conduct, in this, closely resembling one another throughout.

Sūtra: "One who, dwelling among the ghosts, gains spiritual penetration into emptiness through the power of protecting the Dharma is an asura born from an egg, classified under the destiny of ghosts."

Commentary: "Dwelling among the ghosts" refers to this class's own prior cause — that is to say, they came originally from the destiny of ghosts, and, through a good vow and a good mind, upheld and protected the Buddhadharma — whether protecting the sūtras, protecting mantras, protecting the precepts, protecting meditative practice, or protecting those who cultivate in accordance with the Dharma — all such beings may be called protectors of the Dharma. Through the power of this wholesome karma, they leave behind that destiny of ghosts and enter into this one instead; with protecting the Dharma as their cause, their fruit is, accordingly, superior still, and so they are able to gain spiritual penetration and dwell within the realm of emptiness. This asura is born from an egg; egg-born, they fly through the open sky, their cause and fruit both resembling that of ghosts — hence classified under the destiny of ghosts.

Sūtra: "One who, having fallen from virtue among the gods and been demoted, comes to dwell close to the sun and moon is an asura born from a womb, classified under the destiny of humans."

Commentary: "Having fallen from virtue among the gods and been demoted" refers to this class's own prior cause: as the Zhizhang Commentary puts it, the heavens of form sustain themselves through pure conduct, while the heavens of desire take having few desires as their very virtue; should one's pure conduct grow the least bit deficient, or one's desire grow the least bit heavier, this is, in every case, called a falling from virtue. Demoted from their heavenly rank, they fall into the asura realm, their blessed reward resembling that of the gods still, their dwelling-place, too, much the same. Where they come to dwell is close by the palaces of the sun and moon, bordering below upon the human realm. As the Sūtra of the Right Recollection of the True Dharma states: there are asuras dwelling at the flank of Mount Sumeru, able, within the desire realm, to transform their bodies large or small, entirely as they wish — this is exactly this same class. This asura emerges from a womb, classified under the destiny of humans; their desire being heavy, they are, accordingly, demoted, and so come to be born from a womb, since a womb comes about through desire. And, since their desire resembles that of humans, they are classified, accordingly, under the destiny of humans.

Sūtra: "There are also asura kings who hold whole worlds in their grasp, their power penetrating and utterly fearless, contesting authority with Brahmā, with Śakra, and with the Four Heavenly Kings — such an asura exists through transformation, classified under the destiny of the heavens."

Commentary: "There are asura kings" — this distinguishes them from the ordinary multitude of asuras. This class's own prior cause is not stated in the text; reasoning from its fruit, though, it must come from the human realm — among humans, some establish great deeds and accomplishments, others scheme after kingship and hegemony, and, when such things do not turn out as their hearts desired, anger and jealousy arise accordingly; and so they come to be reborn as asura kings, their blessed reward resembling that of the heavenly beings. "Hold whole worlds in their grasp": as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it, they are able, further, to command spirits and ghosts, bringing fortune or misfortune upon the human world — as the Peacock Sūtra speaks of certain punishments meted out by asuras — this much may be seen from it. "Their power penetrating and utterly fearless": "power" here refers to the power of spiritual penetration; "penetrating" means thoroughly reaching — able to thoroughly reach every heaven, without any fear at all. And so they are able to contend for authority with Brahmā, with Śakra the lord of the gods, and with the Four Heavenly Kings. As the Guanding Commentary puts it: Brahmā is lord of a small-thousand world; Śakra is the most revered among the thirty-three heavens; the Four Kings together govern the four continents, each with their own particular charge — the asuras, not being counted among them, give rise to jealousy and contention, wishing to seize their authority, and come, from time to time, to make war upon them. Originally contesting authority with Śakra, with the Four Kings serving as the vanguard; when the Four Kings could not prevail, they would report to Śakra, and when Śakra, too, could not prevail, they would, in turn, beg for aid from the other heavens, even from Brahmā himself, who would then lend his strength to the battle; this asura exists through transformation. "Classified under the destiny of the heavens": this asura, their power of merit and virtue vast, does not undergo birth through the womb at all, but exists through transformation instead, able to transform their body large or small — at its largest, transforming to a hundred sixty-eight thousand yojanas, so that, when it shakes Mount Sumeru with its hand, the mountain itself is truly moved, and Śakra's own palace, at the very summit of Sumeru, is shaken and unsettled, giving rise to fear in his heart. Afterward, when Śakra's troops went out to do battle, he had each of them recite the name of the Mahāprajñāpāramitā, and thereby won a great victory; and the asura, then, transformed into a tiny body, and hid within the hollow of a single lotus fiber. This makes plain, then, this matter of transformation, and so this asura is classified under the destiny of the heavens.

Sūtra: "And, Ānanda, there is a further, lesser kind of asura, born at the very heart of the great ocean, sinking down to dwell at the mouth of some watery cavern, roaming the sky by day and returning to sleep in the water by night — such an asura exists through moisture, classified under the destiny of animals."

Commentary: "Ānanda, there is a further, lesser kind of asura" refers to a further portion, that is, those whose power of blessed reward does not prevail, belonging to this lesser, inferior class, spoken of here in contrast to those above: those born through transformation resemble a king; those born through the womb resemble a minister; those born from an egg resemble the common people; and those born through moisture resemble servants and slaves. They are born at the very heart of the great ocean, sinking down beneath the mouth of some watery cavern — this watery cavern perhaps referring to Weilü, said to lie east of the deep blue sea, where there is a rock forty thousand li across, lying beneath the tail-end of every river and stream, and so called Weilü, "tail-gathering" — this being, precisely, a cavern of the sea, a mouth through which its waters drain away. "Roaming the sky by day" means roaming through the open sky during daylight hours, there to be put to use in service; "returning to sleep in the water by night" means returning to the water by night, there to rest from their labors. This asura exists through moisture, classified under the destiny of animals: born at the very heart of the great ocean, at the mouth where its waters drain away, and so existing through moisture; their blessed reward being of the lowest kind, though they may think of food and, indeed, obtain it, at first it tastes fine, yet, by the very last mouthful, it turns, of a sudden, to the taste of blue mud — and so this class is classified under the destiny of animals. This closes the second section, setting out these destinies in full.

The Seven Destinies: All Self-Created, None Inherent 己三 結妄勸離 · 己四 判決邪正

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難！如是地獄、餓鬼、畜生，人及神仙，天洎修羅，精研七趣，皆是昏沉，諸有為相，妄想受生，妄想隨業，於妙圓明。無作本心，皆如空華，元無所著，但一虛妄，更無根緒。

〖講〗此總明七趣，虛妄因果。如是指前來所說，地獄鬼畜，人及神仙，天洎修羅；精細研究，昇沉往返，若因若果，不出虛妄情想而已。皆是下：即指妄因妄果。昏沉惑也，有為業也，無非隨妄想以受生，隨妄業以受報苦也；即所謂惑、業、苦三，如惡叉聚也。於妙圓明下：欲明其妄，先舉其真，以反顯之。空有不羈曰妙，本來周遍曰圓，無所障蔽曰明，無作無為，本有真心；此七趣，皆如空華；以虛空喻真心，以狂華喻七趣；空原無華，病目妄見。原無所著下三句：正當見華時，當處出生，隨處滅盡，豈有著落耶？是知七趣，但一虛妄名相而已，更無根本頭緒，可研究也。《指掌疏》云：前阿難問云：此道為復本來自有，為是眾生妄習生起？今云妄想受生，妄想隨業，是答以妄習生起也。又云：但一虛妄，更無根緒，是答以非本來有也。

〖經〗阿難！此等眾生，不識本心，受此輪迴，經無量劫，不得真淨，皆由隨順，殺、盜、淫故。反此三種，又則出生，無殺、盜、淫，有名鬼倫，無名天趣，有無相傾，起輪迴性。

〖講〗溫陵曰：前問妙心遍圓，何有獄鬼，人天等道？故此結示，由殺、盜、淫三為根本也。《正脈》云：上言更無根緒，而此又指病根者；蓋妄雖無體，起固無因，而妄理相應，續非無故，故復指也。阿難！此等七趣，一切眾生：據前阿難疑云：佛體真實，云何復有地獄、餓鬼、畜生、修羅、人、天等道？今如來以不識本心等釋之；本心者，本有妙明真心，亦即佛體真實，由不識佛體真實，故受此七趣輪迴，經無量劫，惡道固為不淨，而善道淨亦非真，以思惑種子，眠伏藏識之中，伏而不斷，故非真淨也。又不識即惑道，受此輪迴，即業苦二道。皆由下：申明隨業，言所以不得真淨者，皆由隨順殺盜淫故，則成三惡；反此三種者，知其為惡道之因，而欲違之也。又則出生，無殺、盜、淫，則成四善，三惡固非可隨，四善亦當捨離，以同為繫縛三界之根本也。有名鬼倫，無名天趣者：有則成三惡，墮落三途，地獄亦餓鬼之倫類也。無則成四善，得生四善道。今名天趣，以天趣，攝仙人阿修羅故。有無相傾，起輪迴性者：傾者奪也，奪有成無，從下升上，則成四善道；奪無成有，從上墜下，則成三惡道。由是互奪不已，顛倒輪迴，譬如井輪之高下，無有休息，故稱輪迴性；是有為生滅性故。

〖經〗若得妙發，三摩提者，則妙常寂，有無二無，無二亦滅，尚無不殺、不偷、不淫，云何更隨殺盜淫事？

〖講〗此勸修大定。以定藥能除病之深根。若得妙發三摩提者：指妙耳門圓通也。從妙理發妙智，依妙智照妙理，即反聞聞自性，背塵合覺也。三摩提，本經佛告阿難，有三摩提，名大佛頂首楞嚴王，具足萬行，十方如來，一門超出，妙莊嚴路，此勸修大定也。妙常寂者：聞性三種德相，無七趣之繫縛曰妙，無七趣之生滅曰常，無七趣之流轉曰寂，但能反聞如是聞性，則有之三惡，無之四善，二俱無有，故云有無二無。此則迥超凡外分段生死，先得人空，證有餘涅槃，而出三界矣。若能將無二之性，亦皆銷滅，正所謂斷性亦無，故云無二亦滅。此則迥超權小變易生死，乃空性圓明，成法解脫，以至俱空不生，證無餘涅槃，安住首楞嚴大定矣！當此之時，尚無權小不殺、不偷、不淫，云何更隨凡外，作殺、盜、淫之事乎？是正道尚不屑為，況邪道豈肯為之耶？初藥病雙舉竟。

〖經〗阿難！不斷三業，各各有私，因各各私，眾私同分，非無定處，自妄發生，生妄無因，無可尋究。

〖講〗此正答前問地獄，為有定處，為復自然。彼彼發業，各各私受。前阿難，但問地獄受果時，同受耶？私受耶？此通結七趣也。三業即殺、盜、淫，由不斷此三種惡業，則各各有私造別業，因各各有私造別業，在眾私同分之中，非無定處，即別業同報也。此前二句：佛結答造業各私也。次三句：結答受報，有同分地也。後三句：總結畢究虛妄也。而言七趣果報，但由一念妄動而發生，並非心外。妄之一字，即指最初一念生相無明，無明本空，如前所云，妄性無體，非有所依，故曰生妄無因，無可追尋窮究，如虛空華，本無所有也。

〖經〗汝勗修行，欲得菩提，要除三惑。不盡三惑，縱得神通，皆是世間有為功用，習氣不滅，落於魔道。雖欲除妄，倍加虛偽，如來說為，可哀憐者，汝妄自造，非菩提咎。

〖講〗此正勸須除三惑。勗是勉也，汝勉力真實修行。欲得無上菩提者，要漸次伏斷三惑，即殺、盜、淫，因迷惑不了，是輪迴根本，故即名為惑。不但只除三惡之有，亦復更除四善之無，是謂有無二無也，又不止此，甚至無二亦滅，無二之性，亦復滅除，此即斷性亦無也。不盡三惑，但除世惡，而不除世善，則對待不盡，至若善惡皆除，而斷性存在，猶未盡三惑種子，縱以禪定之力，得發相似神通，亦不能超出世間，成就無作妙力，究竟皆是世間有為功用，有漏而已。習氣不滅，對境復發，縱能上昇，終必落於天魔外道。雖欲除妄，倍加虛偽。《寶鏡疏》云：以凡夫天人，不達真修，向外求心，已是虛偽。而況外道天魔全無正念，而欲除妄者，正是以暴易暴，以妄除妄，則虛偽中，更加一倍虛偽者矣。以故如來說此七趣，真為可哀而可憐者也。《正脈疏》云：倍加虛偽者，如各自謂得無上道，實假世智以妄研，乘神通而造業，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，枉費功力，翻成惡因，故如來深憫之也。且汝前問，佛體真實。云何復有人天等道者，故答之曰：汝妄自造，非菩提咎。故明現前種種諸妄，皆由汝自心所造，實非菩提之過咎；菩提即佛體也。三結妄勸離竟。

〖經〗作是說者，名為正說；若他說者，即魔王說。

〖講〗若使能作是說，勸人欲得菩提，要盡除三惑者，此人即是代佛宣揚，名為正說。若讚歎殺、盜、淫，不礙真修，無須斷絕，此人即是魔王之邪說矣！亟宜著眼辨識，勿為所惑。古德云：修禪不持戒，即是魔羅業，以妄修於妄，真實可憐憫。是知我正說經畢，重說七趣，不但三惡當斷，縱使四善，亦復要離，是以戒助定而已。大科談七趣勸離，以警淹留竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, examined closely, hell, hungry ghosts, animals, humans, immortals, the heavens, and asuras — all seven destinies together — are nothing but confused, conditioned appearances. Beings are born through false thought and follow karma through false thought; against this wondrous, perfect, bright, effortless original mind, all of this is exactly like flowers conjured in empty space, with nowhere at all that they are truly attached. It is nothing but a single falseness, with no root or thread to it beyond that."

Commentary: This sums up, in general, that these seven destinies are, throughout, false in both cause and fruit. "Examined closely" refers to closely researching everything spoken of above — hell, ghosts, animals, humans, immortals, the heavens, and asuras — examined closely, whether in their rising or their falling, coming or going, whether as cause or as fruit, none of it ever departs from false feeling and false thought at all. "All of this is nothing but" points, precisely, to this false cause and this false fruit: confusion is delusion; conditioned activity is karma; none of it is anything but being born through false thought, and undergoing retribution and suffering through false karma — that is to say, precisely delusion, karma, and suffering, these three, clinging together like the fruit of the akṣa tree.

"Against this wondrous, perfect, bright" onward: wishing to make plain this falseness, the text first raises up what is true, in order to set it against this falseness by way of contrast. Free of both emptiness and existence, and yet not bound by either, this is called wondrous; originally pervading everywhere, this is called perfect; free of all obstruction and covering, this is called bright; without any conditioned activity or contrivance at all, this is one's own original, true mind. This seven destinies is, throughout, exactly like flowers conjured in empty space: emptiness itself is taken as a metaphor for the true mind, and these wildly conjured flowers as a metaphor for these seven destinies; space, in itself, never truly holds any flower at all — it is only a diseased eye that falsely perceives one there. The three clauses that follow — "with nowhere at all that they are truly attached" — mean, precisely, that, exactly at the moment such a flower appears, it arises from out of nowhere in particular, and, wherever it appears, it likewise vanishes there again — how could there be any real place where it is truly attached at all? And so one knows that these seven destinies are nothing more than a single false name and appearance — there is no true root or thread to them at all, worth any further research. As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: Ānanda asked earlier — is this path something originally, inherently possessed of itself, or is it something that arises through the false habitual tendencies of sentient beings? The text here answers that it is born through false thought, and follows karma through false thought — this is the answer that it arises through false habitual tendencies. And it further says: it is nothing but a single falseness, with no root or thread to it beyond that — this is the answer that it was never, truly, originally there at all.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these beings fail to recognize this original mind of theirs, and so undergo this same wheel of rebirth, throughout countless kalpas, never attaining any true purity at all — and this is entirely because they follow along after killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct. Turning against these same three, on the other hand, gives rise, in turn, to a further kind of existence: the presence of killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct is named the class of ghosts; their absence is named the destiny of the heavens. And so presence and absence tilt back and forth against each other, giving rise to the very nature of this turning wheel itself."

Commentary: As Wenling puts it: the earlier question asked how, if this wondrous mind pervades everywhere, there could still be such things as hell, ghosts, humans, and heavens at all — and so this passage now makes plain, in closing, that killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct, these three, are precisely the very root of it. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: earlier it was said that there is no root or thread to be found at all — and yet here, once more, the very root of this disease is pointed to; for although this falseness has no true substance of its own, and its arising is, in the end, without any true cause, still, as a matter of false principle corresponding to false effect, this continuation is never truly without any basis at all — hence it is pointed to once again here.

"Ānanda, these seven destinies, all sentient beings" — this refers back to Ānanda's earlier doubt: if the Buddha's own body is true and real, how can there still be such destinies as hell, hungry ghosts, animals, asuras, humans, and heavens? The Tathāgata now answers this by explaining that it is because beings fail to recognize this original mind. "Original mind" refers to this wondrous, bright, true mind, originally possessed by all — which is, precisely, the very body of the Buddha, true and real. Precisely because beings fail to recognize this true and real body of the Buddha, they undergo this wheel of rebirth through these seven destinies, throughout countless kalpas; the evil destinies, certainly, are impure, yet even the good destinies, though called pure, are not truly pure either, since the seeds of thought-delusion still lie dormant, hidden within the storehouse-consciousness, subdued but never cut off — and so, too, this can never be called true purity. And, again, precisely because they fail to recognize this, they undergo the path of delusion, and thereby undergo this same wheel of rebirth, which is precisely the path of karma and of suffering.

"Entirely because" that follows makes plain how this follows upon karma: that is to say, the very reason true purity is never attained is entirely because beings follow along after killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct, and so come to form these three evil paths; turning against these same three shows that these are recognized as the very cause of the evil destinies, and one wishes, accordingly, to turn against them. "Gives rise, in turn, to a further kind of existence: without killing, stealing, or sexual misconduct" forms, accordingly, these four kinds of good — yet these three evils, certainly, are not to be followed, while these four goods, too, ought to be relinquished as well, since both alike serve equally as the very root binding one to these three realms. "The presence of these is named the class of ghosts; their absence is named the destiny of the heavens": their presence forms these three evils, and one falls, accordingly, into the three evil paths — hell, too, being simply a class within the very lineage of ghosts. Their absence forms these four goods, and one is able, accordingly, to be born into these four good destinies — here named, generally, the destiny of the heavens, since the destiny of the heavens includes, within it, both immortals and asuras as well.

"Presence and absence tilt back and forth against each other, giving rise to the very nature of this turning wheel itself": to tilt means to overturn — overturning presence into absence, rising from below to what lies above, forms, accordingly, these four good destinies; overturning absence into presence, falling from above to what lies below, forms, accordingly, these three evil destinies. And so, through this ceaseless mutual overturning, this upside-down turning wheel goes on and on — resembling, precisely, the ceaseless rising and falling of a well-wheel, never once coming to rest — and so this is called the very nature of the turning wheel, this being, precisely, a conditioned nature of arising and ceasing.

Sūtra: "But for one who has genuinely brought forth this wondrous samādhi, this wondrous nature is constantly still — existence and nonexistence, both gone; and even 'both gone' itself dissolves away. At that point there is not even a 'not killing,' a 'not stealing,' or a 'not engaging in sexual misconduct' left to speak of at all — how, then, could one still follow after killing, stealing, or sexual misconduct at all?"

Commentary: This urges the cultivation of the great samādhi, since the medicine of samādhi alone is able to remove this deep root of disease. "Should one genuinely bring forth this wondrous samādhi" refers to this wondrous gateway of the ear, this perfect penetration: bringing forth this wondrous wisdom from this wondrous principle, and, relying on this wondrous wisdom, illuminating this wondrous principle in turn — precisely turning the hearing inward to hear one's own nature, turning away from the dust of the senses to unite with awakened enlightenment. This samādhi is exactly what the Buddha himself told Ānanda earlier in this sūtra: there is a samādhi named the Great Buddha's Crown Śūraṅgama King, complete with ten thousand practices, the single gateway through which every Tathāgata of the ten directions alike transcends, this wondrous, adorned path — this, precisely, is what urges the cultivation of this great samādhi.

"This wondrous nature is constantly still": among the three virtuous marks of this hearing-nature, free of the bondage of these seven destinies, this is called wondrous; free of the arising and ceasing of these seven destinies, this is called constant; free of the drifting flow of these seven destinies, this is called still. Simply by turning the hearing to hear this very nature of hearing in this way, then these three evils that exist, and these four goods that do not exist, both alike become nonexistent — hence, "existence and nonexistence, both gone." At this point, one transcends, once and for all, the fragmentary birth-and-death of both the ordinary being and the non-Buddhist practitioner alike, first attaining the emptiness of self, and realizing the nirvāṇa with residue still remaining, thereby departing from the three realms altogether. And should one, further, be able to dissolve away even this very nature of "both gone" itself — precisely what is meant by "even the nature of cessation itself is gone" — hence, "even 'both gone' itself dissolves away." At this point, one transcends, once and for all, the transformational birth-and-death of both the provisional and the lesser vehicle alike; the very nature of emptiness now perfectly bright, one accomplishes liberation through phenomena, arriving, at last, at a state where even emptiness itself no longer arises, realizing the nirvāṇa without residue remaining, abiding securely within this great Śūraṅgama samādhi. At such a time as this, there is not even a "not killing," a "not stealing," or a "not engaging in sexual misconduct" — belonging to the provisional or lesser vehicle — left to speak of at all; how, then, could one still follow after the ordinary being's or the non-Buddhist practitioner's own killing, stealing, or sexual misconduct at all? That is to say: the right path itself is not even worth clinging to — how much less, then, would one ever be willing to follow the wrong path at all! This closes the first section, raising up together both the disease and its remedy.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, so long as these three karmic acts are not severed, each being has their own private share of them; and, because of these countless private shares, this shared class held commonly in common is, itself, not without its own fixed place. All of this arises simply from falseness, and falseness, once arisen, has no real cause behind it at all — nothing further to be traced or investigated."

Commentary: This answers, directly, the earlier question of whether hell has a fixed location, or occurs entirely of its own accord, each and every being's own karma bringing about their own particular, private retribution. Earlier, Ānanda asked only whether the fruit of hell is received in common, or received privately by each; this passage now closes the matter for all seven destinies together. These three karmic acts are killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct; because these three evil karmic acts are not severed, each being creates their own separate, particular karma accordingly; and, because each being has their own separate, particular karma, then, within this shared class held commonly among these many private karmas, this is, itself, not without its own fixed place — this being precisely the principle that separate karma nonetheless yields a common retribution-ground. The first two clauses close the matter of how karma is created, each privately, by the Buddha's own answer; the next three clauses close the matter of how retribution is received, in this same shared ground; and the last three clauses sum up, altogether, that all of this is, in the end, simply false. And so it is said that the fruits of retribution belonging to these seven destinies arise from nothing more than a single deluded thought, and are, in no way, anything apart from the mind itself. This word "falseness" points, precisely, to this very first thought, this arising of subtle ignorance itself; ignorance is, originally, empty of itself — as was said earlier, this false nature has no substance of its own, is not something truly relied upon at all — hence, it is said that this falseness arises without any real cause, nothing further to be traced back or investigated at all, exactly like a flower conjured in empty space, originally possessing nothing at all.

Sūtra: "I urge you: should you wish to attain bodhi through this cultivation, you must remove these three delusions entirely. Without exhausting these three delusions, even should you attain spiritual powers, all of it remains nothing more than a conditioned, worldly function; the underlying habitual tendencies are not extinguished, and one falls, instead, onto the path of demons. Even wishing to remove this falseness, one only compounds falseness upon falseness all the more — the Tathāgata calls such a person truly pitiable indeed. You yourself create this falseness; it is no fault of bodhi itself."

Commentary: This urges, directly, that these three delusions must be removed. "Urge" means to exhort with effort — I exhort you to genuine, true cultivation. Should you wish to attain unsurpassed bodhi, you must, by gradual stages, subdue and cut off these three delusions — that is, killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct — since, through confusion and failure to understand, these become the very root of the wheel of rebirth, and are, accordingly, named delusions. And it is not only these three evils, which exist, that must be removed, but, further, these four goods, which do not exist, must likewise be removed as well — this being, precisely, what was meant by "existence and nonexistence, both gone." And it does not stop even there: even this very nature of "both gone" must, in the end, be dissolved away as well — this being, precisely, "even the nature of cessation itself is gone."

Should these three delusions not be exhausted — removing only the worldly evil, while not removing the worldly good — then this opposition between the two is never brought to an end; and, even should both good and evil alike be removed, while the very nature of cessation itself still remains, the seeds of these three delusions have still not been exhausted at all. Even should one, through the power of samādhi, bring forth something resembling spiritual powers, one is still unable to transcend the world, or accomplish this wondrous, uncontrived power — in the end, all of it remains nothing more than a worldly, conditioned function, subject to outflow after all. The underlying habitual tendency is not extinguished, and, when conditions arise once more, it manifests again; and, though such a one may, for a time, ascend, in the end, they must fall back, inevitably, onto the path of heavenly demons and non-Buddhist practitioners.

Though wishing, still, to remove this falseness, one only compounds falseness upon falseness all the more. As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: an ordinary being or a heavenly person, failing to reach genuine cultivation, seeking the mind outward instead, is already false to begin with — how much more so a non-Buddhist practitioner or a heavenly demon, entirely lacking any right mindfulness at all, and yet wishing, still, to remove falseness! This is precisely using violence to replace violence, using falseness to remove falseness — and so, within this very falseness, a further falseness is compounded upon it, doubled over again. And so the Tathāgata declares that these seven destinies are, truly, a thing to be pitied, and to be grieved over, indeed. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "compounds falseness upon falseness all the more" — for each, in their own way, claims to have attained the unsurpassed path, when, in truth, they merely borrow worldly cleverness to falsely investigate it, and make use of spiritual powers to create karma instead, straying ever further from perfect penetration, turning their backs on the very city of nirvāṇa, wasting their effort entirely in vain, and turning it, instead, into the very cause of evil — and this is precisely why the Tathāgata grieves for them so deeply.

And, further, recall that you asked earlier: the Buddha's own body is true and real — how, then, can there still be such destinies as humans and heavens? And so the answer given here is: you yourself create this falseness; it is no fault of bodhi itself. This makes plain that every kind of falseness now appearing before you is created entirely by your own mind, and is, in truth, no fault whatsoever of bodhi itself — bodhi being, precisely, the very body of the Buddha. This closes the third section, urging that this falseness be removed.

Sūtra: "Whoever explains it in this way is giving a correct explanation; whoever explains it in any other way is giving Māra's own explanation instead."

Commentary: Should one be able to explain it in just this way, urging others to seek bodhi by removing these three delusions entirely, such a person is speaking on the Buddha's own behalf, proclaiming the teaching — this is named a correct explanation. Should one, instead, praise killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct as no obstacle to genuine cultivation, requiring no severing at all, such a person's words are, precisely, the deviant teaching of Māra the king himself — and this must, most urgently, be seen clearly and discerned, lest one be misled by it. As an ancient worthy has said: to practice meditation without upholding the precepts is exactly Māra's own karma — cultivating falseness by means of falseness, a thing, indeed, most truly to be pitied. And so we know that, now that this sūtra's own explanation is finished, and the seven destinies have been spoken of once more, it is not only these three evils that must be cut off, but even these four goods, too, must be relinquished as well — this being simply a matter of the precepts assisting samādhi, nothing more. This closes the great section expounding the seven destinies, urging that they be relinquished, as a warning against lingering long within them.

A note on this closing turn. After twenty-four dense sections cataloguing every destiny with almost bureaucratic precision — exact ratios of desire, exact lifespans, exact cosmological measurements — Yuanying's commentary follows the sūtra in reversing course entirely: none of it, he insists, has any real, independent existence at all. The taxonomy was never offered as cosmology for its own sake. It exists so that a practitioner can recognize exactly where each thread of karma leads and let it go — at which point the entire structure of seven destinies "dissolves," in Yuanying's own words, rather than being escaped from as though it were a real place one had to travel out of. This closes the great teaching on the seven destinies that has run continuously since fascicle 20, and with it, this fascicle.
