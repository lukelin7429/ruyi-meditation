A Thousand Places Entreating, a Thousand Places Answered

Ānanda, having only ever received the Śūraṅgama Mantra's protection secondhand, asks to hear it proclaimed in full. The Buddha emits light from his own crown, revealing a transformation-buddha who speaks this mantra in five parts. Yuanying then unpacks, clause by clause, everything the sūtra claims for it — ten uses for every buddha of the ten directions, and a long, vivid catalogue of what it does for ordinary practitioners: dissolving even grave karma, guarding against fire, water, poison, and demonic affliction, granting rebirth free of a lowly destiny. The fascicle closes with the entire assembly of protective deities — vajra-spirits, Brahmā and Śakra, the eight kinds of spirits, the sun and moon, mountain and water spirits — each vowing in turn to guard whoever practices it.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 17 closed with the ritual platform's own construction, built around the Śūraṅgama Mantra without ever quoting the mantra itself. This fascicle supplies it. Ānanda admits that, though he owes his own rescue from Mātaṅgī to this mantra, he has only ever received its protection secondhand, spoken by Mañjuśrī on his behalf — and now asks to hear the Buddha proclaim it directly. The Buddha emits light from his own crown; within this light appears a transformation-buddha, who speaks the mantra itself, in five distinct parts Yuanying names in turn: Vairocana's own true-dharma assembly, Śākyamuni's own responsive-transformation assembly, Avalokiteśvara's own united assembly, Vajragarbha's own restraining-and-gathering assembly, and Mañjuśrī's own assembly of propagation — 2,620 characters in all, everything before the word "tadyathā" being homage paid to every buddha, bodhisattva, and sage, and everything from "tadyathā" onward being the mantra's own working heart. Yuanying then unpacks, in careful, clause-by-clause detail, everything the sūtra itself claims for this mantra: ten distinct uses it serves for every buddha of the ten directions, and a long, vivid catalogue of benefits for ordinary practitioners — protection from fire, water, poison, and demonic affliction; the dissolving of karma from broken precepts, even grave and long-accumulated karma; rebirth free of a lowly destiny; protection for entire nations, down to favorable weather and freedom from plague. The fascicle closes with the entire assembly of protective deities — vajra-spirits, Brahmā, Śakra, the Four Heavenly Kings, the eight kinds of spirits, the sun and moon, mountain and water spirits, and finally the vajra-bodhisattvas themselves — each vowing in turn to guard whoever holds and recites it, bringing to a close the sūtra's entire section on skillful, direct and auxiliary practice, begun back in fascicle 17.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. Per this project's own established convention (see the bare-sūtra project's translation notes), the mantra's own syllables are never given a semantic English "translation" — they are reproduced in transliteration only, and only the sūtra's surrounding prose about the mantra's origin and power is translated. The companion project's own fascicle 7 already carries the mantra's own opening lines in transliteration, with a link to the complete text on CBETA; this fascicle reuses that same excerpt and reference rather than re-transcribing all 427 phrases here, since the bfnn.org source used throughout this project shows visible transcription gaps in the mantra's own text (a handful of rare characters lost in the original scan) that would need correction against CBETA in any case. That same fascicle 7 also already translated several of the very sūtra passages on the mantra's benefits that Yuanying quotes again here; that existing translation is reused verbatim wherever the clauses match exactly, and Yuanying's own commentary — including the account of the mantra's five-part structure and every clause of its benefits not already covered there — is translated fresh throughout.

The Assembly's Renewed Request 辛二 重請說神咒 · 壬初 會眾重請

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難頂禮佛足，而白佛言：自我出家，恃佛憍愛，求多聞故，未證無為。

〖講〗此重請說神咒。阿難雖蒙其益，未克親聞；故頂禮佛足，而白佛言：自我捨俗出家，猶恃佛之憍憐寵愛，惟求多聞，不務真修，但得初果，所以道力微弱，故未證四果無為之道。

〖經〗遭彼梵天，邪術所禁，心雖明了，力不自由，賴遇文殊，令我解脫，雖蒙如來，佛頂神咒，冥獲其力，尚未親聞。

〖講〗遭者遇也，彼指摩登伽，為愛女之故，用先梵天咒，邪術之所禁制。然在彼時，心雖明了，而未全道力，無自由分。賴遇文殊，將咒往護，令我解脫淫難，始將歸來。今但云賴遇文殊得脫者：舉能持人，略所持咒，故雖蒙如來佛頂神咒，而文殊但是密誦，破邪除淫，而我冥獲其力；真言密語，尚未親聞。

〖經〗惟願大慈，重為宣說，悲救此會，諸修行輩，末及當來，在輪迴者，承佛密音，身意解脫。

〖講〗此請重說，為利今後。惟願世尊大慈，重為宣說，大悲救拔現住此會諸修行輩，此指三乘聖眾，多學少成，難進易退者，均望咒力救拔也。不唯現會獲益，末指當來，佛法展轉流通，即在輪迴者，亦得均霑法利，以承佛密咒音聲，身意俱得解脫；以遠離魔難，身得解脫，破惑證真，意得解脫也。

〖經〗於時會中，一切大眾，普皆作禮，佇聞如來秘密章句。

〖講〗作是請已，於時會中，一切大眾，普皆頂禮於佛。佇者待也，佇聞如來秘密神咒，微妙章句。初會眾重請竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: 'Ever since I left the household life, relying on the Buddha's own fond, doting love for me, I sought broad learning above all else, and so have not yet realized the unconditioned.'"

Commentary: This is the renewed request to hear this mantra. Though Ānanda had already received its benefit, he had not yet been able to hear it directly himself — and so he bows at the Buddha's feet, and says to the Buddha: ever since I left lay life for the household, still relying on the Buddha's own fond, doting, favored love for me, I sought broad learning alone, never applying myself to true cultivation, and attained only the first fruit — and so my own power on the path remains weak, and I have not yet realized the unconditioned path of the fourth fruit.

Sūtra: "I encountered that ancient Brahmā-heaven spell, restrained by this deviant art — my mind, though clear, had no freedom of its own. Relying on encountering Mañjuśrī, I was thereby freed — and though I received the blessing of the Tathāgata's own Buddha-crown mantra, its power reaching me invisibly, I have still not yet heard it directly myself."

Commentary: "Encountered" means came upon. "That" points to Mātaṅgī, who, for the sake of her own beloved daughter, used the ancient Brahmā-heaven spell, this deviant art's own restraint. Yet, at that time, though my own mind remained clear, my own power on the path was not yet complete, and I had no freedom of my own. Relying on encountering Mañjuśrī, who took this mantra and went to my protection, I was thereby freed from this danger of lust, and only then was able to return. What is here called simply "relying on encountering Mañjuśrī and being freed" names the person who held this mantra, while leaving out the mantra itself that was held — and so, though I received the blessing of the Tathāgata's own Buddha-crown mantra, Mañjuśrī merely recited it secretly, breaking through this deviance and removing this lust, and I received its power invisibly — this true, secret utterance itself I have still not yet heard directly.

Sūtra: "I wish only that, out of this same great compassion, you would proclaim it once more, compassionately saving every practitioner in this present assembly, and, into the age to come, every being still caught in the wheel — that, receiving the Buddha's own secret sound, they might attain liberation of both body and mind."

Commentary: This requests that it be spoken again, for the benefit of both present and future beings. I wish only that the World-Honored One, out of this same great compassion, would proclaim it once more, with great compassion rescuing every practitioner presently gathered in this assembly — this referring to the sagely assembly of the three vehicles, many still learning yet few having accomplished, easily advancing yet just as easily retreating, all alike hoping for this mantra's own power to rescue them. Not only would those present in this assembly benefit — "into the age to come" points to the Buddha-dharma's own continued transmission, so that even beings still caught in the wheel of birth and death might equally share in this same benefit of the Dharma, receiving the Buddha's own secret mantra's sound, both body and mind attaining liberation together — freed from demonic danger, the body attains liberation; breaking through delusion to realize truth, the mind attains liberation.

Sūtra: "At that time, everyone in the assembly, without exception, made obeisance together, awaiting the chance to hear the Tathāgata's own secret verses."

Commentary: Having made this request, at that time, everyone in the assembly, without exception, bowed before the Buddha together. "Awaiting" means waiting expectantly, awaiting the chance to hear the Tathāgata's own secret, sacred mantra, these wondrous verses. This closes the first section, the assembly's own renewed request.

The Transformation-Buddha Speaks 壬二 如來重說 · 癸初 正說神咒

Chinese Source

〖經〗爾時世尊，從肉髻中，涌百寶光，光中涌出，千葉寶蓮，有化如來，坐寶華中。

〖講〗此將說神咒，先現化佛。然此科乃顯中之密，以從如來自身所現也。爾時世尊，從肉髻中：準《無上依經》云：乃是頂骨涌起，自然成髻，其色紅赤，在青螺紺髮之中，即無見頂相；表如來藏，法身理體也。光從頂放，表般若妙智也；光涌百寶，表解脫大用也。光中涌出，千葉寶蓮者：表依妙智，而發勝因也。有化如來，坐寶華中：表因果一契也。

〖經〗頂放十道，百寶光明，一一光明，皆遍示現，十恆河沙，金剛密跡，擎山持杵，遍虛空界。

〖講〗此乃密中之密，以從佛頂之佛頂，放寶光之寶光，表斯咒，尊中之尊，妙中之妙也。由一頂髻放十道光，具百寶色者，表如來藏性，圓含萬法，其體清淨本然，周遍法界也。金剛密跡，擎山持杵，遍虛空界者：內秘菩薩之德，外現金剛之跡，擎山制外，持杵降魔，密行化導，故曰金剛密跡。遍滿空界，顯現威力。金剛遍界者，表循業發現，生善滅惡，隨緣妙用也。初光相表顯竟。

〖經〗大眾仰觀，畏愛兼抱，求佛哀祐，一心聽佛，無見頂相，放光如來，宣說神咒。

〖講〗法會大眾，見佛從頂，放光之中化佛，化佛復從頂放光，光中示現，金剛密跡，事出希有，惟有景仰觀察。畏者畏其威，愛者愛其德；既折、攝並行，而畏愛兼抱；謂二者兼具，令人不能去諸懷抱也。求佛哀祐者：哀憐而攝授之，護祐而帡幪之。一心聽佛，無見頂相，放光如來，宣說神咒者：謂佛從無見頂相放光，光中化現如來，宣說神咒；正表無為心佛，無上心咒，以顯藏心微妙，不可思議也。咒者咒願，佛說此咒，正咒願眾生，令其斷惑證真，革凡成聖，具諸不測之威神，而有難思之妙用。二大眾欽聽竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "At that time the World-Honored One, from within his own fleshy crown-protrusion, emitted a hundred kinds of treasure-light surging forth; and, within this light, a thousand-petaled, jeweled lotus surged forth, upon which sat a transformation-Tathāgata."

Commentary: About to speak this mantra, the Buddha first manifests this transformation-buddha. Yet this section is exactly the secret found within the manifest, arising from the Tathāgata's own body directly. "At that time the World-Honored One, from within his own fleshy crown-protrusion": according to the Sūtra on the Unsurpassed Basis, this is exactly the crown-bone rising up, naturally forming this same protrusion, its own color a reddish crimson, set amid his blue-black, curling hair — this being exactly this same unseen crown-mark, representing the tathāgatagarbha, the very principle-substance of the dharma-body itself. Light emitted from the crown represents the wondrous wisdom of prajñā; this light surging forth in a hundred kinds of treasure represents the great function of liberation. "Within this light, a thousand-petaled, jeweled lotus surged forth" represents this wondrous wisdom giving rise to this same supreme cause. "Upon which sat a transformation-Tathāgata" represents cause and fruit in perfect accord with one another.

Sūtra: "From this crown, ten rays of a hundred kinds of treasure-light shone forth, and, within each and every ray of this light, appeared, everywhere at once, secret vajra-traces, as numerous as ten Ganges' sands, holding up mountains and grasping vajra-clubs, filling the whole realm of empty space."

Commentary: This is exactly the secret within the secret — from this Buddha-crown of a Buddha-crown, this treasure-light of a treasure-light emitted, representing this mantra as the very most honored among the honored, the very most wondrous among the wondrous. That a single crown-protrusion emits these ten rays of light, complete with a hundred kinds of treasure-color, represents the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha, perfectly containing the ten thousand dharmas, its own substance originally pure of itself, pervading the whole Dharma-realm. "Secret vajra-traces, holding up mountains and grasping vajra-clubs, filling the whole realm of empty space": inwardly concealing a bodhisattva's own virtue, outwardly manifesting a vajra-spirit's own trace — holding up mountains to restrain what is external, grasping vajra-clubs to subdue demons, this secret conduct guiding beings — hence, "secret vajra-traces." Filling the whole realm of empty space displays this same majestic power. These vajra-spirits filling this whole realm represent appearing in accordance with karma, giving rise to what is wholesome and extinguishing what is evil, this wondrous function according with conditions. This closes the first section, this light and its mark displayed.

Sūtra: "The whole assembly gazed upward in reverence, holding both awe and affection together, seeking the Buddha's own compassionate protection, listening single-mindedly as the Tathāgata, light streaming from this unseen crown-mark, proclaimed this sacred mantra."

Commentary: The assembly gathered for this Dharma-teaching, seeing this transformation-buddha within the light emitted from the Buddha's own crown, and this transformation-buddha, in turn, emitting light from his own crown, within which these secret vajra-traces appeared — this being so rare an occurrence — could do nothing but gaze up at it in reverent contemplation. "Awe" is awe of his own majesty; "affection" is affection for his own virtue — both restraint and gathering-in carried out together, and so awe and affection held together at once, meaning both present together, leaving people unable to set aside either feeling. "Seeking the Buddha's own compassionate protection": taking pity and gathering them in, protecting and sheltering them like a canopy overhead. "Listening single-mindedly as the Tathāgata, light streaming from this unseen crown-mark, proclaimed this sacred mantra": this means the Buddha, light streaming from this unseen crown-mark, this Tathāgata manifesting within this light, proclaiming this sacred mantra — precisely representing this unconditioned mind-buddha, this unsurpassed mind-mantra, displaying the tathāgatagarbha's own subtle, inconceivable nature. "Mantra" means a spoken vow — the Buddha speaking this mantra precisely as a vow spoken on behalf of sentient beings, that they might sever delusion and realize truth, transforming the ordinary into the sagely, complete with every unfathomable, spiritual power, and possessed of this inconceivable, wondrous function. This closes the second section, the assembly's own reverent listening.

The Mantra in Five Parts 子三 五會神咒

Śākyamuni's own transformation-buddha now speaks the mantra itself. Its own transliterated syllables are, per this project's established convention, left untranslated — reproduced here only in their opening lines, as already given in the companion project's fascicle 7:

南牟薩怛他蘇伽哆耶（歸命一切諸佛）阿囉訶帝三藐三菩陀耶（歸命一切如來應正等覺）娜牟薩婆勃陀（敬禮一切諸佛）勃地薩哆吠弊（歸命菩薩）娜牟颯哆喃三藐三菩陀俱胝喃（敬禮正遍知）薩失囉皤迦僧伽喃（敬禮辟支佛及四果人）娜牟嚧雞阿囉喝哆喃（歸命羅漢等眾）……娜牟薄伽筏帝（歸命世尊）嚧陀囉耶（大自在天）……娜牟婆伽筏帝怛他揭多俱囉耶（如來族）娜牟鉢頭摩俱囉耶（歸命蓮華族菩薩等）娜牟筏折囉俱囉耶（歸命金剛族）娜牟摩尼俱囉耶（歸命寶族）娜牟伽闍俱囉耶（歸命眾族）……〔the mantra continues at length〕……跢姪他、唵、阿那隸、毘舍提，鞞囉跋闍囉陀唎，槃陀槃陀你、跋闍囉、謗尼泮、虎𤙖都嚧甕泮、莎婆訶

〖講〗上來神咒今依紹興海眼諸古本勘對考正。以上神咒，凡有五會，至一百三十七句止，是毘盧真法會；至一百七十八句止，是釋尊應化會；至二百七十二句止，是觀音合同會；至三百三十二句止，是剛藏折攝會；至四百十八句止，是文殊弘傳會；哆侄他起至咒終，共四百二十七句。長水云：哆姪他前，但是歸命諸佛、菩薩聖賢等，及敘咒願，加被、滅惡離難。至唵字下，方說咒心，即是秘密首楞嚴也。

此咒是如來藏心所成，故曰心咒；而全咒是如來藏心，又曰咒心。今五會併此，共計二千六百二十字。末三字娑婆訶，或云薩婆訶，唐翻速成，令我所作速成就也。然此段既稱心咒，則是五會中，精要之義。如有力者，通前俱持，固為盡善；設或無力，則但持此段，確信功極，自收成效。然眾生之機，顯密不定，以有眾生，應從顯說，而獲益者；亦有眾生，應從密說，而獲益者。雖顯密互陳，然皆發明，大佛頂如來藏心，不思議體用也。諸經神咒，例皆不翻，即五種不翻中，秘密不翻。而於四例中，即翻字不翻音之例；字是華文，音是梵語，此方人不曉，而天竺之人，日用所共曉解者也。至於秘咒，非但常人不知，理應下位聖賢，不達上位之咒，故於顯部之外，另曰密部也。然在西域秘密部，類分東、西、南、北、中，凡有五部，此咒正當中央，遮那灌頂部也。

天如曰：孤山所引天台四悉檀，悉遍也，檀施也。諸聖以四法，遍施眾生也。初世界悉檀者：隨方異說，令生歡喜益也。二、為人悉檀者：生善益也。三、對治悉檀者：破惡益也。四、第一義悉檀者：入理益也。

此四悉普益也。如上根之人，誦之可得入理益；其次以誦咒除惡習，得破惡益；其次以誦咒，遠離雜念，得生善益；其次以誦咒，而能摧邪輔正，令生歡喜益也。《正脈》云：大端聖賢宏化，例有顯密二教，如醫療病，率有二途：一者授方，則顯說病源、藥性，及炮治之法，如佛顯教。二者授藥，則都不顯說，但惟與藥，令服愈病而已，不必求知何藥何治，如佛密教。故今授藥，不必求解，若解生則咒喪矣。持咒之法，但當深信不疑，一味持之，身、口、意三密相應，不要起心求解，如參話頭相似，只許參，不許解，一念不生，自能得益。

如昔日有一浪人，遠奔他國，詐稱王子。彼國因以公主妻之，食時多瞋難事，其公主欲探其意，願善順之，一日往詢彼國商人，能通語言者。彼商人多智，知彼詐稱王子，未便與言，有關國恥。遂用本國語，作一偈曰：若當瞋時，則含笑念偈云：無親往他國，欺誑一切人，麤食是常食，何勞復作瞋？囑云：須學言音，不必求解。說是偈時，彼知事露，從此不敢生瞋。神咒之功，亦復如是。《正脈》云：秘咒少分應知，三義略盡：一、理法力：謂以一字含無邊妙理，而稱陀羅尼，謂總一切法，持無量義，斯之威力，全具所詮之理；如此方元、亨、利、貞，亦可避凶致祥矣。二者、威德力：謂諸佛菩薩，一切權實聖賢，威德深重，具大勢力，稱其名號，隨願如意；如今世間，有勢力人，亦可假其名聲，伏惡脫難也。三者、實語力：佛菩薩，一切聖賢，起大悲心，愍眾生故，出誠實語，咒願眾生，離苦得樂，革凡成聖，故誦之可以隨言成益；如世之實修行人，尚可咒願吉凶，隨言成就，何況證理，入位聖賢，真慈誓願，安可測度？略述由此三義，故持之得不測之神功。然須確信，專持功滿，方收成效，若猶豫間斷中輟，或壇戒不能如法，而謗斯咒無功者，招大罪苦也。

須知放光如來，說此神咒，正為近下根機，最初方便，持之自可遠魔脫難，斷惑證真，轉煩惱以得菩提，出生死而入涅槃也。初正說神咒竟。

This mantra, taken as a whole, falls into five distinct parts: up to phrase one hundred and thirty-seven is Vairocana's own true-dharma assembly; up to phrase one hundred and seventy-eight is Śākyamuni's own responsive-transformation assembly; up to phrase two hundred and seventy-two is Avalokiteśvara's own united assembly; up to phrase three hundred and thirty-two is Vajragarbha's own restraining-and-gathering assembly; up to phrase four hundred and eighteen is Mañjuśrī's own assembly of propagation; and from "tadyathā" to the very end of the mantra comes to four hundred and twenty-seven phrases in all. As Changshui put it: everything before "tadyathā" is nothing but homage paid to every buddha, bodhisattva, and sage, together with the very statement of this mantra's own vow — its blessing, its dissolving of evil, its freeing from calamity. Only from the syllable "oṃ" onward does it actually speak this mantra's own working heart — this being the secret Śūraṅgama itself.

This mantra is formed entirely from the very mind of the tathāgatagarbha, hence called the "mind-mantra"; and this entire mantra is itself this same mind of the tathāgatagarbha, hence also called the "mantra-mind." Taken together, these five parts come to two thousand six hundred and twenty characters in all. Its final three characters, svāhā (sometimes rendered "sa-po-he"), translate, in the Tang dynasty's own rendering, "swift accomplishment" — may what I have done be swiftly accomplished. Since this closing portion alone is already called the "mind-mantra," it is exactly the essential meaning distilled from all five parts together. Should one have the capacity for it, holding the entire mantra, from beginning to end, is certainly the most excellent practice; but, lacking such capacity, holding this closing portion alone, with genuine, unwavering confidence, brings its own effect to completion of itself. Yet sentient beings' own capacities differ, not fixed to either the manifest or the secret teaching alone — for there are sentient beings who benefit precisely through what is manifestly taught, and there are sentient beings who benefit precisely through what is secretly taught. Though the manifest and the secret are thus set forth side by side, both alike reveal this same inconceivable substance and function of the Great Buddha's Crown, the mind of the tathāgatagarbha itself. Every sūtra's own sacred mantra is, as a rule, left untranslated — this being exactly the "untranslated for secrecy" case among the five reasons a term is left untranslated. And, among the four categories of translation, this belongs to the case of "translating the script but not the sound": the script is Chinese, but the sound is Sanskrit, unintelligible to people of this land, yet something the people of India understand in their own everyday use. As for this secret mantra specifically, it is not only ordinary people who fail to understand it — by rights, even a sage of a lower rank cannot fully grasp a mantra belonging to a higher rank — and so, beyond the manifest division of teachings, a further, secret division is named as well. Within the esoteric tradition of the Western Regions, this secret division is itself classed into east, west, south, north, and center, five divisions in all — and this present mantra belongs precisely to the central one, the division of Vairocana's own consecration.

As Tianru put it: the fourfold siddhānta Gushan cites from Tiantai — siddha meaning "universal," and dāna meaning "bestowal" — every sage bestows these four methods universally upon sentient beings. First, the siddhānta of the world: speaking differently according to place, giving rise to the benefit of joy. Second, the siddhānta for the person: giving rise to the benefit of wholesome growth. Third, the siddhānta of counteraction: giving rise to the benefit of overcoming evil. Fourth, the siddhānta of ultimate truth: giving rise to the benefit of entering principle itself.

These four siddhāntas bestow benefit universally. One of superior capacity, reciting this mantra, may attain the benefit of entering principle itself; next, reciting this mantra to remove evil habits, one attains the benefit of overcoming evil; next, reciting this mantra to leave scattered thoughts far behind, one attains the benefit of wholesome growth; next, reciting this mantra, able thereby to overturn deviance and support what is correct, one attains the benefit of joy. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: in general, when sages and worthies spread their own teaching widely, there are, as a rule, both a manifest and a secret teaching — exactly as, in treating illness, there are, in general, two paths: first, prescribing a remedy, whereby the source of the illness, the nature of the medicine, and the method of its preparation are all explained openly — this is like the Buddha's own exoteric teaching; second, administering the medicine directly, whereby nothing at all is explained openly, only the medicine itself given, so that, taking it, the illness is cured, with no need to seek to understand what medicine it is or how it treats the illness — this is like the Buddha's own esoteric teaching. And so, administering medicine in this way requires no seeking after understanding at all — should such understanding arise, this mantra's own efficacy would be lost. The method of holding a mantra is simply this: believe deeply, without the slightest doubt, and hold it single-mindedly, the three mysteries of body, speech, and mind brought into full accord — never giving rise to a mind that seeks to understand it, exactly like investigating a huatō: only investigation is permitted, never understanding; let not a single thought arise, and benefit follows of itself.

As in the old story of a wanderer who traveled far to another country, falsely claiming to be a prince. That country, believing him, gave him their own princess in marriage — yet, at mealtimes, he grew easily angry and difficult to manage, and the princess, wishing to learn the reason for this, resolved to accommodate him gently, and one day went to consult a merchant of that country who understood his own native language. This merchant, being of great intelligence, recognized that the man had falsely claimed to be a prince, and, unwilling to speak of this openly, for fear of shaming his own nation, composed instead a verse in the man's own native language, saying: whenever anger is about to arise, recite this verse instead, smiling as you do: "with no kin, I journeyed to another land, deceiving everyone I met; coarse food has always been my ordinary food — why trouble myself with anger now?" And he instructed her: you need only learn to pronounce these sounds, with no need to seek to understand their meaning. When she recited this verse to him, he knew at once that his own deception had been discovered, and, from that time on, no longer dared give way to anger. The efficacy of this sacred mantra works in exactly the same way. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: a small portion of what ought to be understood about this secret mantra may be summarized in three points. First, the power of principle and dharma: a single syllable containing boundless, wondrous principle within it, called a dhāraṇī, meaning that it gathers together every dharma, holding boundless meaning — this same power fully containing the very principle it expresses, exactly as, in our own land, the four qualities of origination, prevalence, advantage, and constancy are likewise able to avert misfortune and bring about good fortune. Second, the power of majestic virtue: every buddha and bodhisattva, every sage of the provisional and real teachings alike, possessing a majestic virtue profound and weighty, complete with great power — invoking their own name accords with one's own wish, exactly as, in the present world, one may likewise borrow a powerful person's own reputation to subdue evil and escape danger. Third, the power of truthful speech: every buddha, bodhisattva, and sage, giving rise to this same mind of great compassion, out of pity for sentient beings, utters this truthful speech, this vow spoken on their behalf, that they might leave suffering behind for happiness, transforming the ordinary into the sagely — and so reciting it brings benefit according to its own words; exactly as, in the world, even a genuine practitioner may pronounce a vow regarding good or ill fortune, and see it accomplished according to their own words — how much more so, then, a sage who has realized this same principle directly, whose true, compassionate vow could never be fathomed at all? Briefly stated, it is through these same three points that holding this mantra brings about this unfathomable, spiritual achievement. Yet one must hold to it with genuine confidence, single-mindedly, until this same effort reaches its own completion, and only then does it bring about its own effect — should one waver, interrupt, or abandon this practice partway through, or should the platform and precepts fail to be carried out properly, and one then slanders this mantra as being without effect, this brings upon oneself a grave offense and suffering indeed.

One should know that the Tathāgata, emitting this light, speaking this sacred mantra, does so precisely for the sake of those of a lesser, more middling capacity, as their very first skillful means — holding it, one may naturally keep demonic influence far away and escape calamity, sever delusion and realize truth, turning affliction into bodhi itself, leaving birth and death behind to enter nirvāṇa. This closes the first section, directly speaking this sacred mantra.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these subtle and wondrous verses of the Buddha-crown's gathered light, this secret Sitātapatra dhāraṇī —"

Commentary: This displays the full name of this mantra. "The Buddha-crown's gathered light" means that this mantra originally arose from within the light of the Buddha's own crown, spoken by this transformation-buddha. Calling this light "gathered" means that its own blazing net, crossing and interwoven, is exactly like a great mass of fire gathered together. "Crown" represents this mantra's own supreme honor; "light" represents its own majestic, spiritual power; "gathered" is exactly its own spiritual function. "Sitātapatra": preceded by the two words mahā, translated "great"; sitā, translated "white"; and tapatra, translated "canopy" — these being simply this mantra's own name. Some hold that the seven phrases from "tadyathā, oṃ" onward are this mantra's own "mind" — this is mistaken, for that portion is exactly the very place where all five parts of this mantra converge together, its own crystallized essence. Examined closely, this entire mantra is formed wholly from the mind of the tathāgatagarbha, hence called the "mind-mantra"; and this entire mantra is itself this same mind of the tathāgatagarbha, hence named the "mantra-mind." Yet this mind never departs from these three meanings of substance, characteristic, and function: its substance, free of every counterpart, is called "great" — that is, the tathāgatagarbha's own original, wondrous, perfectly complete mind, the meaning of true emptiness itself; its characteristic, free of every defiling stain, is called "white" — that is, the tathāgatagarbha's own original, bright, wondrous mind, the meaning of true non-emptiness itself; and its function, covering everything at once, is called "canopy" — that is, the tathāgatagarbha's own wondrous, bright mind's own origin, the meaning of true emptiness-and-non-emptiness together. "This secret dhāraṇī, these subtle and wondrous verses": hidden and profoundly secret, meaning that this mantra, too, contains gāthā verses within it; refined, subtle, and wondrous. "Verses" refers to its own major divisions, such as these same five parts; and its finer divisions are called "phrases." Each and every one of these is, alike, inconceivable.

Sūtra: "This mantra gives rise to every buddha of the ten directions — the Tathāgatas of the ten directions rely on this mantra-mind to accomplish unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening."

Commentary: This makes plain that every one of the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, in benefiting both themselves and others, does so entirely through the function of this same secret mantra-mind. Why is it that every buddha of the ten directions appears in the world, manifesting birth and accomplishing buddhahood? Entirely because every Tathāgata of the ten directions relies on this mantra-mind as their own hidden cause, cultivating and realizing complete meaning, every bodhisattva's own myriad practices, and only afterward perfectly accomplishing cultivation and realization at the fruit stage — and this is exactly why they accomplish unsurpassed bodhi. "Unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening" is exactly the very fruit of bodhi's own wisdom. Understanding clearly that mind gives rise to the ten thousand dharmas is "correct knowledge"; understanding that the ten thousand dharmas are nothing but mind alone is "perfect knowledge" — and so every buddha's own causal-stage mind and fruit-stage awakening are, from beginning to end, never two different things at all.

Sūtra: "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions wield this mantra-mind to subdue every demon and restrain every non-Buddhist path."

Commentary: This makes plain this function of subduing demons and restraining outside paths. Every Tathāgata of the ten directions, about to realize truth directly, is, in many cases, disturbed by demons and outside teachers, unable to accomplish this swiftly — and so wields this same secret mantra-mind as a vajra king's own precious sword, subduing every demon of the five aggregates, and restraining every outside teacher holding the views of annihilationism or eternalism.

Sūtra: "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions ride upon this mantra-mind, seated upon a jeweled lotus, answering lands as numerous as motes of dust."

Commentary: This makes plain this function of universally manifesting a cloud of bodies. Every Tathāgata of the ten directions, having already realized truth, manifests Vairocana's own body, traveling throughout the Flower-Treasury World — and so rides upon this same secret mantra-mind; and, since this mantra-mind is itself exactly every buddha's own true dharma-body, seated upon a jeweled lotus, they travel throughout lands as numerous as motes of dust, manifesting a body matched to each kind of being.

Sūtra: "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions hold this mantra-mind within themselves, turning the great wheel of the Dharma throughout lands as numerous as motes of dust."

Commentary: This makes plain this function of expounding the Dharma matched to each capacity. Every Tathāgata of the ten directions, wishing to turn the wheel of the Dharma, must, within this same unobstructed wisdom, hold this same secret mantra-mind within themselves; and, since this mantra-mind is itself exactly the treasury of the true Dharma-eye, taken as the very root of virtue, they are thereby able, throughout lands as numerous as motes of dust, through the eight qualities of the Buddha's own voice and the four kinds of eloquence, to turn this great, unsurpassed, root wheel of the Dharma.

Sūtra: "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions hold this mantra-mind, and are thereby able, throughout the ten directions, to touch the crown and bestow a prophecy — and, their own fruit not yet accomplished, they, too, throughout the ten directions, receive this same prophecy from a buddha."

Commentary: This makes plain this function of bestowing this same prophecy, both on oneself and on others. Every Tathāgata of the ten directions is spoken of here in terms of their own origin manifesting this same trace, this response and transformation-body. Holding this same secret mantra-mind, since this mantra-mind is itself able to serve as the very root of accomplishing buddhahood, once having accomplished the path themselves, they are thereby able, throughout the ten directions, to gather beings in with compassion, touching every bodhisattva's own crown, bestowing on them the prophecy of their own future buddhahood to come. And, should their own fruit not yet be perfectly accomplished, they, too, are able, throughout these same ten directions, to receive this same prophecy of bodhi directly from a buddha.

Sūtra: "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions depend on this mantra-mind to rescue every kind of suffering throughout the ten directions — the sufferings of the hell-realms, hungry ghosts, and animals; of blindness, deafness, and muteness; of being bound to what one hates, of separation from what one loves, of not obtaining what one seeks, of the five aggregates blazing with affliction — every misfortune, great or small, dissolved at once; the calamities of thieves, of war, of unjust punishment, of imprisonment, of wind, water, and fire, and of hunger, thirst, and poverty, all dispersing the instant this mind recalls the mantra."

Commentary: This makes plain this function of drawing beings out of suffering and rescuing them from calamity. Every Tathāgata of the ten directions, depending on this same secret mantra-mind, is thereby able, throughout the ten directions, to draw beings out of every kind of suffering — whether teaching them to hold it themselves, or reciting this vow on their behalf — relying on this mantra's own majestic power, they attain freedom from suffering and calamity. "So-called" is a word introducing an explanation, meaning: first, hell; second, hungry ghosts; third, animals; fourth, the northern continent; fifth, blindness, deafness, and muteness; sixth, being born before or after a buddha's own appearance; seventh, worldly wisdom and eloquence; eighth, the heavens of long life. These eight conditions are unable to receive this teaching, finding it difficult to enter the Buddha-dharma — hence called "difficulties." The suffering of being bound to what one hates, the suffering of separation from what one loves, the suffering of not obtaining what one seeks, the suffering of the five aggregates blazing with affliction, together with the four sufferings of birth, old age, sickness, and death, together make these eight sufferings. Speaking here in terms of "difficulties," the earlier ones are named, encompassing the later; speaking in terms of "suffering," the later ones are named, encompassing the earlier — this is how these sufferings and difficulties come to be distinguished from one another. As for these misfortunes, great and small: according to the Bhaiṣajyaguru Sūtra, there are nine great ones, and countless small ones, too many to describe in full. Every one of these difficulties described above, the moment this same secret mantra-mind is recalled, dissolves away and disperses.

〖講〗此下至無生忍，皆明助道成智。前說離難遠魔，固是神王之力所及，此明助道須假菩薩。咒是諸佛心印，咒在之處，即為佛在，故常有聖眷隨侍；故囑當知：是佛頂神咒，常有八萬四千那由他，恆河沙俱胝，金剛藏王菩薩：那由他，此云萬億，俱胝，此云百億。言菩薩之數，百億不足以盡之，復以恆河沙計之；恆河沙百億不足以盡之，復以萬億計之。萬億恆河沙百億，不足以盡之，復以八萬四千計之；極言數目有如許之多。現威勇之形，具降魔之力，故名金剛。蘊秘密之德，攝金剛之眾，故稱藏王。種族者：謂此唯一類相聚，故曰種族。一一皆有諸金剛眾，而為眷屬，聽其驅策，如火頭金剛，青面金剛等。此諸聖眾，為護持咒故，併護衛持咒之人，晝夜恭隨侍衛，如慈母之護嬰兒，時刻不離也。設若有眾生，於散心亂心之時，非有正定，常住三摩地，但能散心思憶神咒，亂心口持神咒。雖非定心憶持，而金剛王亦常隨從於彼，為報佛恩，格外施護也。通稱男子者，以能憶神咒，即具善根故也。何況反顯辭，彼邪定聚，及不定聚眾生，尚蒙金剛隨護，何況此等決定發菩提心者，是回小向大，修習耳根圓通，志在無上菩提也。此諸金剛菩薩藏王，執金剛杵，持秘密藏，或稱金剛菩薩，或稱菩薩藏王，或稱金剛藏王菩薩，或稱金剛菩薩藏王，或稱金剛王，或稱金剛藏，隨稱皆可。以此菩薩，既得如來藏心，去惑純精，而與行人，現具藏心，本來無二。聖凡雖異，心精通吻，故能以同體心精。陰者冥也，速者疾也，冥冥之中，如兩鏡相對，以心印心，速疾啟發彼持者之神識，應時得大開悟也。此神識通明。是人，即指精心陰速之人，應時心能記憶八萬四千恆河沙劫前事。一切妙理，周遍了知，得無疑惑，似涉三明。況河沙紀劫，迥超小乘八萬故能獲宿命，而得天眼也。溫陵曰：即所謂成就眾生出世間智也。初資發通明竟。

〖經〗從第一劫，乃至後身，生生不生，藥叉羅剎，及富單那，迦吒富單那，鳩槃茶，毗舍遮等，並諸餓鬼，有形無形，有想無想，如是惡處。

〖講〗此永離惡處。從第一劫：謂從初發心持咒時起，乃至超略中間，直至最後身。生生謂捨生趣生；不生下之諸趣，因得通明，有助道故。藥叉或云夜叉，捷疾鬼也。羅剎此云可畏，食人鬼也。富單那此云臭惡鬼，主熱病鬼也。迦吒富單那，此云奇臭惡鬼，主熱病之甚者。鳩槃茶此云甕形，魘魅鬼也。毘舍遮或云毗舍闍，此云噉精氣，以能噉人精氣，及噉五穀精氣。並諸餓鬼：如大腹、臭毛、針咽、炬口等。有形：即是有色，如休咎精明。無形：即是無色，如空散銷沉。有想：即靈通怪變，如鬼神精靈。無想：即凝滯堅頑，如精神化為土木金石等。如上諸處，各有自苦，遮障聖道，故曰惡處。然既生生不生，則聖道可修，宜乎因圓果滿，而至最後身矣！

〖經〗是善男子若讀若誦，若書若寫，若帶若藏，諸色供養。劫劫不生，貧窮下賤，不可樂處。此諸眾生，縱其自身，不作福業，十方如來，所有功德，悉與此人。由是得於恆河沙阿僧祇，不可說不可說劫，常與諸佛同生一處。無量功德，如惡叉聚，同處熏修，永無分散。

〖講〗是善男子：指決定發菩提心人。不唯不生雜趣，即於人中，不可樂處，亦復不生。若果將此神咒：對本曰讀；背本曰誦；敬寫為書；便書為寫；身佩名帶；器貯名藏。諸色供養：如香、花、燈、塗、果之類。劫劫不生，貧窮下賤：貧賤皆妨道業，為不可樂處。既不生此，則定生可樂處。以持尊勝頂法，故感尊勝果報也。此為生佛界之由。若果欲生佛界者，定必多修福德因緣方可。今此人自身不作福業，但能持咒，而十方如來，所有功德，悉與此人者，咒力使然也。由是下，得生佛前。且得於恆河沙阿僧祇，不可說不可說劫，與佛同生一處，所謂常在佛前是也。無量功德下，方是生後功德，既常在佛前，必常隨佛學，了知諸佛為眾生心內諸佛，眾生乃諸佛心內眾生，心佛及眾生，是三無差別。如惡叉聚之果，三果一蒂，不相捨離，同處熏煉修習，永無分別離散矣！

〖經〗是故能令破戒之人，戒根清淨；未得戒者，令其得戒；未精進者，令其精進；無智慧者，令得智慧；不清淨者，速得清淨；不持齋戒，自成齋戒。

〖講〗《指掌疏》云：承上神咒，以有如是利益，是故能令眾行成就。破戒之人：如阿難八萬行中，祗毀一戒。戒根清淨者：如阿難心清淨故，尚未淪溺。未得戒者：如登伽宿為淫女；令其得戒者：如登伽今為性尼。未精進者：如阿難專好多聞，登伽偏於淫愛。令得精進者：如阿難慇懃請定，登伽成精進林。無智慧者：如登伽纏眠貪愛，不知為苦。令得智慧者：如登伽與羅侯母，同悟宿因。不清淨者：如登伽未蒙咒前，欲燄飛揚；速得清淨者：如登伽蒙咒後，淫心頓歇。設或此人，縱有宿愆，不能持齋與持戒者，但能持此咒，日久月深，消除業障，自然成就，能令齋戒圓成。如上破戒之人，能令戒根清淨等，總指未持咒前，有種種諸事，自持咒之後，仗神咒功勳，自能成就。但舉阿難登伽二人，為證甚善。末後不持齋戒，自成齋戒者：並非謂不要持齋戒，自然能成齋戒，此種惡習，自當誡之。須知戒為無上菩提本，斯言信不誣也。四眾行成就竟。

〖經〗阿難！是善男子，持此咒時，設犯禁戒，於未受時，持咒之後，眾破戒罪，無問輕重，一時銷滅。縱經飲酒，食噉五辛，種種不淨，一切諸佛，菩薩金剛，天仙鬼神，不將為過。設著不淨，破弊衣服，一行一住，悉同清淨。縱不作壇，不入道場，亦不行道，誦持此咒，還同入壇，行道功德，無有異也。若造五逆，無間重罪，及諸比丘、比丘尼，四棄八棄，誦此咒已，如是重業，猶如猛風，吹散沙聚，悉皆滅除，更無毫髮。阿難！若有眾生，從無量無數劫來，所有一切輕重罪障，從前世來，未及懺悔，若能讀誦，書寫此咒，身上帶持，若安住處，莊宅園館，如是積業，猶湯消雪。

〖講〗此明持咒能滅現在諸罪也。阿難！是善男子，持此咒時，自應嚴持禁戒，以佛有明誨，故設有犯一切禁戒，於未受持此咒時，乃屬往昔。今既持咒自新，誓不更犯，則從前所有眾破戒罪，無問所犯輕重，一時消滅。重謂小乘初篇，大乘十重；輕謂小乘餘篇，大乘四十八輕。《指掌》云：羯磨云「前心作惡，如雲覆日，後心起善，似炬銷暗」。今既似炬銷暗，乃何罪不滅？故無問輕重也。縱使往日，曾經飲酒，酒雖非葷物，而無性命，然酒為起罪因緣，故宜禁戒。食噉五辛，如蔥、蒜、韭、薤、興渠，此五辛，熟食發淫，生噉增恚，以至種種葷腥，不淨之物。今既持咒之後，則諸佛菩薩，金剛天仙鬼神，不將為過，以既往不咎也。準前文，佛教著新淨衣，然香誦咒，又教六時行道，三七不寐。今云設著不淨者，則不局於淨衣；又云破弊者，則不局於新衣；又云凡一行動者，則不局於六時行道也；凡一住此者，則不局於三七不寐也。此為行頭陀行，清貧不備者，恐失誦咒利益，故特開許，悉同清淨也。準前文，如法建壇，依教行道，方合如來清淨軌則。今者此人既乏資財，縱不作壇，又不能入楞嚴道場，亦不能隨眾行道，所有功德，等無有異。此極重罪滅。設若未誦咒前，造此五逆：殺父、殺母、殺阿羅漢、破和合僧、出佛身血，當墮五無間地獄，故云無間重罪。及諸比丘四棄：即殺、盜、淫、妄，四波羅夷罪。梵語波羅夷，此云棄，謂犯此罪者，永棄佛法海外，猶如大海，不納死屍也。比丘尼八棄者，前四根本戒，與比丘同，再加觸、八、覆、隨；第五觸，謂與染心男子，身相觸，第六八，謂與染心男子，捉手捉衣，屏處共坐，共語、共行、相倚、相期等八事；第七覆，謂覆他重罪；第八隨，謂隨大僧供給衣食，即為僧所舉，未作共住法者，不得隨彼也。如是等罪，皆極重而不可懺者。若是知慚悔過，誦咒求懺，仗咒力故，應念消滅。如是重業，猶如沙聚，言沙已成聚，非猛風不能吹散。而密咒具不測之威神，故如猛風，一吹便散，悉皆消滅，更無毫髮之少留也。此極遠罪滅。阿難！若有眾生，從於無量無數劫來，無量無數，乃西域十大數之一二。百洛叉百洛叉，為一無數；無數無數，為一無量。所有一切輕重罪障：罪之所以稱障者，有礙正修故。從前世來，未及懺悔：懺具云懺摩，此翻悔過，懺是梵音，悔是華言，故稱華梵兼舉。懺者懺其前愆，悔者悔其後過，既未及懺悔，必障正修，欲修三摩，須假咒力，若能讀誦，書寫此咒，身上帶持：讀誦者，起坐不忘；書寫者，觸目驚心。若安住處，莊宅園館：莊宅，常時安住處也；園館，暫時遊憩處也。如是積業，猶湯銷雪：如是無量無數劫來，所有積聚罪業，仗神咒之功力，速能除滅，猶如沸湯銷雪相似。五諸罪消滅竟。

〖經〗不久皆得，悟無生忍。

〖講〗《正脈》云：圓實初住，便證此忍，別教當在地上，詳前發通，乃是定心成就所發。今悟無生，乃是慧心成就所發。前但了知不昧，未必實證，萬法無生，應知後位，深於前位，況得此忍之後，罪福皆空，聖凡情盡，可以魔佛一如，方能遍涉惡苦諸趣，而無礙無擇。故知此科，超前諸科，而為助道之成功矣！又所謂出世間智者，發通成始，而無生成終也。按六科，前四即為人生善悉檀，第五對治破惡，第六第一義入理也。而前之救難，後之應求，乃世界歡喜悉檀也，餘可類知。《指掌疏》云：宿障既銷，三摩得成，故云不久得忍。準前俱空不生。按後乃當第三漸次，且不可作八地釋之。二助成道業竟。

Sūtra: "Ānanda, you should know: this mantra is constantly accompanied by eighty-four thousand nayutas of Ganges-sand koṭis of the race of Vajragarbha bodhisattva-kings, each and every one of them attended by their own multitude of vajra-beings as retinue, in constant attendance day and night. Should there be sentient beings who, with a scattered mind, not yet in samādhi, hold this mantra in mind and recite it with the mouth, these same vajra-kings still constantly follow after them, these good sons — how much more, then, one whose mind is resolutely set on bodhi! These same vajra bodhisattva-kings of this treasury, with utmost sincerity, in hidden swiftness, open and awaken their spiritual awareness. Such a person, at that very moment, becomes able to recall, in mind, events across eighty-four thousand Ganges-sand kalpas, understanding them completely, without the slightest doubt remaining."

Commentary: Everything from here down to "attain no-birth patience" makes plain how this mantra aids the path and brings forth wisdom. What was said earlier, of escaping calamity and standing apart from demons, is certainly within the power reached by these spirit-kings; this passage makes plain that aiding the path further requires the help of bodhisattvas. This mantra is the very seal of every buddha's own mind, and wherever this mantra is present, the Buddha is, in effect, present there as well — hence it is constantly accompanied by this same sagely retinue in attendance. Hence the charge to know: this Buddha-crown mantra is constantly accompanied by eighty-four thousand nayutas of Ganges-sand koṭis of Vajragarbha bodhisattva-kings — a nayuta here translated as ten thousand koṭi, and a koṭi as a hundred million; that is, the number of these bodhisattvas cannot be exhausted even by a hundred million, and so is reckoned again by Ganges-sands; not exhausted even by a hundred million Ganges-sands, it is reckoned again by ten-thousand-koṭis; not exhausted even by ten-thousand-koṭi hundred-million Ganges-sands, it is reckoned once more by eighty-four thousand — this states, in the most emphatic terms, just how vast this number truly is. Manifesting a form of majestic courage, complete with the power to subdue demons, hence called "vajra." Harboring the virtue of this secret treasury, gathering in this vajra multitude, hence called "king of this treasury." "Race" means that these alone, all of a single kind, gather together — hence, "race." Each and every one of them, alike, has its own multitude of vajra-beings as retinue, obeying their command, such as Vajra of the Fiery Head, or Vajra of the Blue Face. This host of sages, in order to protect this mantra, and, together with it, to protect whoever holds this mantra, reverently attends and guards them day and night, exactly as a loving mother guards her infant, never departing from them for a single moment. Should there be sentient beings who, at a time of scattered, disordered mind, without yet abiding in true, fixed samādhi, are merely able to recall this mantra with a scattered mind, or recite this mantra with a disordered mind — though this is not yet holding it with a settled mind, these same vajra-kings still constantly follow after them, in order to repay the Buddha's own kindness, extending this protection all the more generously. Calling them, generally, "good sons" is because their very ability to recall this mantra is itself proof they possess this same root of goodness. "How much more" introduces a statement by contrast: if even beings of deviant or undetermined disposition still receive this protection and following from these vajra-beings, how much more, then, one whose mind is resolutely set on bodhi — turning from the lesser toward the greater, cultivating this perfect penetration of the ear-faculty, aspiring toward unsurpassed bodhi itself. These same vajra bodhisattva-kings of this treasury, wielding the vajra pestle, upholding this secret treasury, are called, variously, vajra bodhisattvas, bodhisattva-kings of this treasury, vajra bodhisattva-kings of this treasury, vajra kings of this treasury bodhisattvas, vajra kings, or vajra treasuries — any of these names may be used alike. Since these bodhisattvas have already attained the tathāgatagarbha mind, having removed delusion down to its own pure essence, and since this is, with the practitioner's own inherently complete tathāgatagarbha mind, originally never two different things at all — though sage and ordinary being differ, mind and essence are mutually attuned, and so are able, through this same shared essence of mind, to act as one. "Hidden" means unseen; "swiftly" means without delay — in this hidden, unseen manner, exactly as two mirrors face each other, sealing mind with mind, swiftly opening and bringing forth this same practitioner's own spiritual awareness, so that, at the very moment, great awakening is attained. This is spiritual awareness opened into understanding. "Such a person" refers exactly to this person whose spiritual awareness has been swiftly, hiddenly opened. At that very moment, their mind becomes able to recall events from eighty-four thousand Ganges-sand kalpas before — every subtle principle understood completely, without the slightest doubt remaining, resembling, in this, the three kinds of higher knowledge. Moreover, reckoning across Ganges-sand kalpas far surpasses even the eighty-thousand-kalpa limit of the lesser vehicle, and so is able to attain knowledge of past lives, and the heavenly eye as well. As Wenling puts it: this is exactly what is meant by "bringing sentient beings to accomplish transcendent wisdom." This closes the first section, bringing forth understanding and clarity.

Sūtra: "From the very first kalpa, all the way to one's own final body, life after life, one is never again born among the yakṣas, rākṣasas, pūtanas, kaṭapūtanas, kumbhāṇḍas, piśācas, and the rest, nor among the hungry ghosts of every kind, whether possessed of form or formless, whether possessed of thought or thoughtless — none of these evil places."

Commentary: This is forever standing apart from evil places. "From the very first kalpa" means from the very moment one first sets the mind on holding this mantra, all the way, skipping over every interval, to one's own final body. "Life after life" means casting off one life and taking up the next; "never again born" among the realms that follow refers to every realm below, since, having attained this understanding and clarity, one has the aid of the path. A yakṣa, or yaksha, is a swift ghost. A rākṣasa, here translated "the fearsome one," is a ghost that devours people. A pūtana, here translated "the foul ghost," governs fevers. A kaṭapūtana, here translated "the extremely foul ghost," governs the most severe fevers. A kumbhāṇḍa, here translated "jar-shaped," is a ghost that induces nightmares. A piśāca, here translated "devourer of vital essence," is able to devour both a person's own vital essence and the essence of the five grains. And every kind of hungry ghost besides — bloated of belly, foul of hair, needle-throated, torch-mouthed, and the rest. "Possessed of form" means possessed of matter, such as the luminous spirits of good and ill omen; "formless" means without matter, such as those scattered and dissolved into emptiness. "Possessed of thought" means uncannily transformed and strange, such as ghosts, spirits, and haunting essences; "thoughtless" means congealed and utterly inert, such as spirit turned to earth, wood, metal, or stone. Every one of these realms above has its own particular suffering, obstructing the sagely path — hence called "evil places." Yet, since one is no longer reborn among them, life after life, the sagely path may be cultivated freely, and it is fitting that the cause be perfected and the fruit fulfilled, arriving at last at this final body.

Sūtra: "Should such a good son read this mantra, recite it, copy it out, write it down, wear it, or keep it stored away, offering to it every kind of ornament, kalpa after kalpa, they will never be born into poverty, low station, or any unwelcome place. And though such a being performs no meritorious deeds of their own, the Tathāgatas of the ten directions bestow on this person every merit and virtue they themselves possess — and so, across Ganges-sand asaṃkhyeyas of kalpas beyond all reckoning, this being is constantly born together with every buddha, in the very same place, this immeasurable merit and virtue clustered together like an akṣa-seed cluster, cultivated together in that same place, never once scattered or parted."

Commentary: "Such a good son" refers to one whose mind is resolutely set on bodhi. Not only is such a person never reborn among the mixed and lower realms, but even among human beings, no unwelcome place is ever their birth again either. Should such a person truly, with this mantra — facing the text is called "reading"; reciting from memory is called "reciting"; writing it out reverently is called "copying"; writing it for convenience is called "writing down"; wearing it on the body is called "wearing"; and storing it in a vessel is called "keeping." "Every kind of ornament of offering" refers to things such as incense, flowers, lamps, unguents, and fruit. "Kalpa after kalpa, never born into poverty or low station": poverty and low station both hinder the path's own accomplishment, and are thus "unwelcome places." Since one is never born into these, one is certainly born, instead, into welcome places — for, by upholding this most honored, supreme dharma of the crown, one thereby draws forth this same most honored fruit of retribution. This explains why such a being comes to be born in the very presence of the buddhas. Truly wishing to be born within the buddha-realm requires, as a rule, that one must cultivate many meritorious causes and conditions. Yet here, though this person performs no meritorious deeds of their own, and is merely able to hold this mantra, the Tathāgatas of the ten directions nonetheless bestow on this person every merit and virtue they themselves possess — this being exactly what this mantra's own power brings about. From "and so" onward, such a being comes to be born in the very presence of the buddhas. Moreover, across Ganges-sand asaṃkhyeyas of kalpas beyond all reckoning, they are born together with every buddha in the very same place — which is exactly what is meant by "constantly in the presence of the buddhas." Everything from "this immeasurable merit and virtue" onward describes, then, the merit and virtue that follows upon this birth: being constantly in the buddhas' own presence, one necessarily and constantly follows the buddhas in study, coming to understand clearly that every buddha is a buddha within sentient beings' own minds, and that sentient beings are, likewise, sentient beings within every buddha's own mind — mind, buddha, and sentient being being, these three, without any difference at all. Like the fruit of the akṣa-seed cluster, three fruits on a single stem, never letting go of one another, cultivated and refined together in this same place, never once scattered or parted.

Sūtra: "And so this mantra is able to cause one who has broken the precepts to become pure again at their very root; one who has not yet received the precepts to receive them; one lacking diligence to become diligent; one without wisdom to attain wisdom; one impure to swiftly become pure; and one who upholds no fast or precept at all to spontaneously accomplish them."

Commentary: As the Detailed Commentary puts it: continuing from the benefits of this mantra described above, this mantra is thereby able to bring every practice to accomplishment. "One who has broken the precepts" is like Ānanda, who, among his eighty thousand practices, transgressed only this one precept; "becomes pure again at their root" is like how Ānanda's own mind remained pure at heart, and so had not yet truly fallen. "One who has not yet received the precepts" is like Mātaṅgī, once a woman of lust; "to receive them" is like how she has now become a bhikṣuṇī of pure conduct. "One lacking diligence" is like Ānanda, devoted solely to hearing much; or like Mātaṅgī, given wholly over to lustful craving; "to become diligent" is like how Ānanda earnestly requested this teaching on samādhi, and Mātaṅgī became a very forest of diligence. "One without wisdom" is like Mātaṅgī, bound in sleep and greedy craving, not knowing this as suffering; "to attain wisdom" is like how Mātaṅgī, together with Rāhula's own mother, awakened together to their shared karmic cause from the past. "One impure" is like Mātaṅgī, before receiving this mantra, her own flame of desire still blazing; "to swiftly become pure" is like how Mātaṅgī, once she had received this mantra, saw her own lustful mind cease at once. Should such a person, though burdened still with karma accumulated in the past, be unable to keep either fast or precept, yet be able only to hold this mantra, then, as the days and months pass, their own karmic hindrances are dissolved, and this same accomplishment comes about naturally, causing fast and precept alike to be perfectly fulfilled. Everything above — "one who has broken the precepts becomes pure again at their root," and the rest — refers, throughout, to the state before holding this mantra, when all these various matters existed; once this mantra is held, relying on this mantra's own merit, one naturally comes to this same accomplishment. Citing simply these two figures, Ānanda and Mātaṅgī, serves as excellent proof of this. As for the final clause, "one who upholds no fast or precept at all spontaneously accomplishes them" — this by no means says that one need not keep the fast and precepts at all, and that they will simply come about of themselves; such an evil habit must certainly still be guarded against. One must understand that the precepts are the very root of unsurpassed bodhi — this saying is, indeed, no empty claim. This closes the fourth section, every practice brought to accomplishment.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, when this good son holds this mantra, should any precept-breaking sin have occurred before receiving it, then, once this mantra is held, every such sin of broken precepts, grave or light, is dissolved all at once. Even should one, in the past, have drunk wine or eaten any of the five pungent roots, or any manner of impure thing, no buddha, bodhisattva, vajra-being, deity, or spirit will hold this against such a person. Even should one wear soiled or ragged clothing, whether walking about or dwelling in one place, all alike is equally pure. And even without constructing a platform, without entering the place of practice, without walking the ritual path at all, simply reciting and holding this mantra is no different at all from the merit of entering the platform and walking this same path. Should one have committed the five heinous crimes bringing uninterrupted retribution, or should monks or nuns have committed the four or eight offenses of expulsion, yet, having recited this mantra, such grave karma, too, like a heap of sand scattered by a fierce wind, is entirely eliminated, without the slightest trace remaining. Ānanda, should sentient beings, across countless kalpas beyond all reckoning, carry every manner of sin and karmic hindrance, light or grave, from lives before this one, not yet repented — should they be able to read, recite, copy, or write out this mantra, wear it on the body, or keep it in the place where they dwell, whether their own household or a garden pavilion, then such accumulated karma is dissolved like snow in boiling water."

Commentary: This makes plain that holding this mantra is able to dissolve every present sin. Ānanda, this good son, holding this mantra, ought naturally to keep the precepts with utmost strictness, since the Buddha has given this clear instruction; yet should there be any transgression of any precept, occurring before this mantra was ever received or held, this belongs to the past. Now that this mantra is held and one begins anew, vowing never to transgress again, every sin of broken precepts previously incurred, grave or light alike, is dissolved all at once. "Grave" refers, in the lesser vehicle, to the first section of offenses, and, in the greater vehicle, to the ten grave offenses; "light" refers, in the lesser vehicle, to the remaining sections, and, in the greater vehicle, to the forty-eight lesser offenses. As the Detailed Commentary puts it, the karman formula itself says: "An evil deed done with an earlier mind is like clouds covering the sun; a good deed arisen with a later mind is like a torch dispelling darkness." Now that it is, indeed, like a torch dispelling darkness, what sin could fail to be dissolved? Hence, "regardless of how light or grave." Even should one, in the past, have drunk wine — though wine itself is not among the meat-and-blood offerings, and has no life of its own, it is nonetheless a cause and condition giving rise to transgression, and is therefore rightly forbidden. Eating any of the five pungent roots — onion, garlic, chive, scallion, and asafoetida — these five, cooked, arouse lust, and, eaten raw, increase anger, extending to every manner of meat, blood, and impure thing. Yet now, once this mantra has been held, no buddha, bodhisattva, vajra-being, deity, or spirit will hold this against such a person, since what is past is no longer counted. Even should one wear soiled clothing or dress in rags — this passage, then, does not restrict one to pure, fresh clothing; and calling it "ragged" does not restrict one to new clothing either; and "walking about" does not restrict one to the six periods of walking the path; nor does "dwelling in one place" restrict one to the three weeks without sleep. This is spoken for the sake of one practicing austere, itinerant conduct, too poor to be fully equipped, lest such a one lose the benefit of reciting this mantra — hence this special allowance, that all alike is equally pure. Following the earlier text, building the platform according to the Dharma and practicing according to this teaching is what accords with the Tathāgata's own pure regulations. Yet here, this person, lacking the means, though unable to build a platform, unable to enter the Śūraṅgama place of practice, and unable to walk this path together with the assembly — the merit gained is nonetheless no different at all. This is the dissolving of even the gravest of sins. Should one, before ever reciting this mantra, have committed these five heinous crimes — killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, causing schism within the harmonious saṃgha, or drawing a buddha's blood — one would fall into the five realms of uninterrupted hell, hence called "crimes bringing uninterrupted retribution." And the four offenses of expulsion for monks — killing, stealing, lust, and false speech — the four pārājika offenses. The Sanskrit word pārājika is here translated "expulsion," meaning that whoever commits such an offense is forever expelled beyond the very ocean of the Buddha-dharma, just as the great ocean itself never harbors a corpse within it. The eight offenses of expulsion for nuns include these same four root precepts shared with monks, together with four more: touching, the eight matters, concealment, and following. Fifth, touching, means physical contact with a man of impure mind; sixth, the eight matters, means, with a man of impure mind, holding hands, holding clothing, sitting together in a secluded place, speaking together, walking together, leaning against one another, and making an appointment together — these eight matters; seventh, concealment, means concealing another's own grave offense; and eighth, following, means that, once expelled by the assembly of monks for having failed to undergo this same procedure of communal dwelling, one may not follow along with them for support of clothing and food. Sins such as these are, every one, of the gravest kind, ordinarily beyond any possibility of repentance. Yet should such a person know shame, repent this wrongdoing, and recite this mantra seeking to confess it, then, relying on this mantra's own power, the sin dissolves the very instant it is recalled. "Such grave karma, like a heap of sand" — sand, once gathered into a heap, cannot be scattered except by a fierce wind; yet this secret mantra possesses an unfathomable, majestic spiritual power, and so, like a fierce wind, scatters it the very instant it blows, eliminating it entirely, without the slightest trace remaining. This is the dissolving of even the most distant sin. Ānanda, should sentient beings, across countless kalpas beyond all reckoning — "countless" and "beyond all reckoning" are among the ten great numbers of the Western Regions; a hundred lakhs of lakhs make one "beyond reckoning," and a beyond-reckoning of beyond-reckonings makes one "countless" — carry every manner of sin and karmic hindrance, light or grave: sin is called a "hindrance" because it obstructs true cultivation. "From lives before this one, not yet repented" — repentance, in full, is kṣamā, here translated "confessing one's fault"; "confessing" is the Sanskrit sound, and "fault" is its Chinese meaning, hence cited together, both Chinese and Sanskrit. To confess is to confess one's own past transgression; to acknowledge fault is to acknowledge one's own later wrongdoing. Since this has not yet been repented, it must necessarily obstruct true cultivation; and wishing to cultivate this same samādhi, one must rely on this mantra's own power. Should one be able to read and recite, copy and write out this mantra, wearing it on the body — reading and reciting it so that, rising or sitting, one never forgets it; copying and writing it out so that, meeting the eye, it strikes the heart — or keep it in the place where one dwells, whether household or garden pavilion — a household being where one dwells constantly, a garden pavilion being where one rests temporarily — then such karma, accumulated across these countless kalpas beyond all reckoning, relying on this mantra's own meritorious power, is swiftly eliminated, exactly like snow dissolved by boiling water. This closes the fifth section, every sin dissolved.

Sūtra: "Before long, all such beings attain the patient acceptance of the non-arising of all dharmas."

Commentary: As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: in the perfect, real teaching, this patience is already realized at the very first abiding; in the gradual teaching, it belongs only to the bodhisattva grounds. Examined against the bringing-forth of understanding described earlier, that was brought forth through the accomplishment of a settled mind; this awakening to non-arising is brought forth through the accomplishment of a wise mind. The earlier passage spoke only of understanding without confusion, not necessarily of true realization that the ten thousand dharmas are without arising — one should understand that this later stage surpasses the earlier one, and, all the more, that once this patience is attained, both offense and merit alike are empty, every distinction between sage and ordinary being exhausted, demon and buddha become one and the same — only then can one travel freely through every evil and suffering realm, without obstruction and without discrimination. One thus knows that this section surpasses every section before it, and stands as the true accomplishment of aiding the path! And what is called "transcendent wisdom" is brought to its beginning by this bringing-forth of understanding, and brought to its very completion by this awakening to non-arising. Examining these six sections: the first four belong to the siddhānta of bringing forth goodness for beings; the fifth belongs to the siddhānta of counteracting and destroying evil; and the sixth belongs to the siddhānta of entering ultimate truth itself. And the earlier rescue from calamity, together with the later fulfillment of wishes, belong to the siddhānta of worldly delight — the rest may be understood by the same pattern. As the Detailed Commentary puts it: once past hindrances are dissolved, this samādhi is accomplished — hence, "before long, this patience is attained." Compare this with the earlier passage on both being empty and without arising. And, checking against the later text, this properly corresponds to the third of the gradual stages, and should by no means be read as referring to the eighth ground. This closes the second section, aiding the accomplishment of the path.

Fulfilling What Is Wished For, and Blessing the Nation 巳三 稱遂願求 · 辰二 約國土以顯普益

Chinese Source

〖經〗復次阿難！若有女人，未生男女，欲求孕者；若能至心，憶念斯咒，或能身上帶此悉怛多般怛囉者，便生福德智慧男女。求長命者，即得長命；欲求果報，速圓滿者，速得圓滿；身命色力，亦復如是。命終之後，隨願往生十方國土，必定不生邊地下賤，何況雜形？

〖講〗此稱遂願求。因不諳佛法，但求世間福樂。設若有女人，未曾生男育女，欲求孕者，孕即懷胎。若能以至誠心，憶念斯咒。憶，則常時記憶；念，則常時持念；自應所求皆遂。設或秉性稍鈍，不能憶念，祗能身上帶持此悉怛多般怛囉者，亦得同樣功效。昔以未生男女，恐為丈夫所棄，今則便生福德智慧男女。有福德，富而仁；有智慧，聰而賢；此遂男女求也。求長命者：貪生惡死，人之常情，無論富貴貧賤，皆求百年無病，永享遐齡。能持誦神咒者，即得長命。欲求果報，速圓滿者：果報有福利修行，二種分別，此中似是福利。而修行，上科中資發通明，速得無生，修行已得圓滿。身命色力，亦復如是：此福利果報。如謂身體健康，壽命延長，形色端正，筋力強壯。亦復如是者：指同上果報，速得圓滿也。此鈍根劣機，不知持咒，為當來成佛之因，惟求色身利益，故亦滿其願也。此求身後往生。命終者，前陰已壞，後陰未成也。隨願往生十方諸佛淨土，以持咒功力，得滿所願。決定不生邊地下賤，邊地下賤，尚且不生，何況地獄餓鬼等，雜形異報耶？《指掌疏》云：《大悲經》云：誦持大悲神咒，若不生諸佛國者，我誓不成正覺。蓋彼兼菩薩願力，此兼諸佛心力，以此咒是諸佛心咒，持此咒者，必為諸佛護念故。初約眾生以顯各益竟。

〖經〗阿難！若諸國土，州縣聚落，飢荒疫癘，或復刀兵，賊難鬥諍，兼餘一切，厄難之地。寫此神咒，安城四門，並諸支提，或脫闍上，令其國土，所有眾生，奉迎斯咒，禮拜恭敬，一心供養，令其人民，各各身佩，或各各安所居宅地，一切災厄，悉皆銷滅。

〖講〗此正顯此咒，能護國安民，轉凶為吉也。若諸一國領土之內，州縣者國土之分治；聚落者，人煙聚集之村落，此又為州縣所轄也。飢荒者：即五穀草菜不生，為饑饉難；疫癘者：癘乃四時不正之氣，人得之而成疾疫，為疫癘難；刀兵者：邊疆不靖；賊難者：家國不寧；鬥諍者：群小為亂，皆刀兵難也。兼餘一切厄難之地者：如久雨為患，旱魃成災，蝗蟲肆虐，風雹時至，凡有損於眾生者皆是。寫此神咒，安城四門：眾人往來，必經之處。並諸支提：此云可供養處，即清淨佛剎之都稱。或脫闍上，脫闍此云幢，如尊勝幢，陀羅尼幢之類。令其國土，所有眾生，禮拜恭敬。皆同一心，至誠供養。令其人民，各各身上，佩帶此咒，或各各安供於而居宅地；一切災害危厄之事，悉皆銷滅，自可化暴戾為祥和也。

〖經〗阿難！在在處處，國土眾生，隨有此咒，天龍歡喜，風雨順時。五穀豐殷，兆庶安樂。

〖講〗隨在隨處，國土所有眾生，皆宜有咒，隨有此咒之地方，能信受奉持，方感天龍歡喜，則風雨順時。如古之盛時，五風十雨也。由是則五穀收成，必豐盛而殷實也。兆，十億為兆；庶，眾也，儒典以民為庶。今云兆庶者，即指民眾之多，非局定數也。安樂者，謂各安居樂業也。

〖經〗亦復能鎮，一切惡星，隨方變怪，災障不起，人無橫夭，杻械枷銷，不著其身，晝夜安眠，常無惡夢。阿難！是娑婆界，有八萬四千，災變惡星，二十八大惡星，而為上首；復有八大惡星，以為其主。作種種形，出現世時，能生眾生種種災異。有此咒地，悉皆消滅。十二由旬，成結界地，諸惡災祥，永不能入。

〖講〗鎮者壓也，以此神咒，亦復能鎮壓一切惡星。星各有方，故曰隨方。變怪者，反常曰變，異餘曰怪，如前彗孛飛流，隨其所感之方而現；彼國眾生，本所不見，亦復不聞；蓋人事作於下，天道應乎上，然變怪既已不興，災障自然不起。災不起，則各盡天年，故曰人無橫夭；障不起，則相安無事，故杻械枷鎖，不著其身。晝夜安眠，常無惡夢者：晝為覺時，夜為臥時，安眠者，無有夢想也，謂災障既無，自然臥安覺安，夢想亦安矣！娑婆界者，五濁惡世，故有惡星，淨土無之。八萬四千者，乃周列宿之總數，乃應眾生煩惱之數。災變惡星者，惡不自惡，因災而變，故云災變惡星。二十八者，為四方之經，八大星者，為循經之緯也。二十八星，佛經世典，大同小異，如東方七星，依《孔雀》，謂：昴、畢、觜、參、井、鬼、柳。而世典，則角、亢、氐、房、心、尾、箕也。南方七星，依《孔雀》，謂：星、張、翼、軫、角、亢、氐。而世典，則井、鬼、柳、星、張、翼、軫也。西方七星，依《孔雀》，謂：房、心、尾，箕、斗、牛、女。而世典，則奎、婁、胃、昴、畢、參、觜也。北方七星，依《孔雀》，謂：虛、危、室、壁、奎、婁、胃。而世典，則斗、牛、女、虛、危、室、壁也。或《孔雀》約災變，而世典約常度；言地方清寧，則四七各住本位，若災難將起，則四七遞互交錯，能統領八萬四千，而為上首。復有八大惡星。長水謂：金、木、水、火、土、羅侯、計都、彗，有謂五星與羅、計、孛也。以為其主者：以為八萬四千之主，二十八大惡星。以為其帥，上列天象，下應人事，順則為福應，逆則為災應。儒書所謂惠迪吉，從逆凶，作種種形，或商羊舞水，石燕飛風，如為小兒，幻出歌謠等。出現世時，能生一切眾生種種患變災異，非常不測之事。有此咒地，悉皆消滅，十二由旬，成結界地。溫陵曰：以咒力葉乎百順，故惡變悉滅於天，災祥不入其境。有此秘咒之地，則能反凶為吉，所有災象，悉皆消滅。由旬者，正云踰善那，此云合，猶此之驛站也。乃輪王巡狩，一停之舍。由旬有三種：大者八十里，中者六十里，小者四十里。成結界地者：百靈呵護。諸惡災祥，永不能入：祥者，吉凶之先兆也；災祥當是凶兆，不取吉祥之義。二詳伸護生助道以出由竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Furthermore, Ānanda, should any woman who has not yet borne a son or daughter wish to conceive, and be able, with utmost sincerity, to recall this mantra in mind, or else simply wear this Sitātapatra mantra on the body, she will bear a son or daughter blessed with both merit and wisdom. Should one seek long life, long life is attained; should one wish this same fruit of retribution to be swiftly fulfilled, it is swiftly fulfilled; body, life, complexion, and strength likewise follow in just this way. And after this life ends, one is reborn, according to one's own wish, in any land of the ten directions, certainly never born in any border region or low station — how much less, then, in any misshapen form at all!"

Commentary: This is the fulfillment of what is wished for. Because such a person, unfamiliar with the Buddha-dharma, seeks only worldly blessing and happiness. Should any woman, never yet having borne a son or raised a daughter, wish to conceive — conceiving means becoming pregnant. Should she be able, with a heart of utmost sincerity, to recall this mantra in mind — recalling means constantly keeping it in memory, and reciting means constantly holding it in mind — what she seeks will naturally all be fulfilled. And should her own disposition be somewhat dull, unable to recall it in mind, and she is only able to wear this Sitātapatra mantra on the body, this, too, gains the very same effect. In the past, not having borne a son or daughter, she might have feared being abandoned by her husband; now, she will bear a son or daughter blessed with both merit and wisdom — blessed with merit means wealthy and kind; blessed with wisdom means clever and worthy — and this is the fulfillment of this wish for a son or daughter. "Should one seek long life" — clinging to life and fearing death is the common disposition of every person, whether noble or humble, rich or poor, all wishing to live a hundred years free of illness, forever enjoying a long life span. One able to recite and hold this sacred mantra thereby attains long life. "Should one wish this fruit of retribution to be swiftly fulfilled" — such fruits of retribution divide into two kinds, worldly benefit and cultivation of the path; what is meant here resembles worldly benefit. As for cultivation of the path, the section above, on bringing forth understanding and clarity, on swiftly attaining non-arising, already describes this cultivation as fulfilled. "Body, life, complexion, and strength likewise follow in just this way" — this refers to this same worldly fruit of benefit, meaning bodily health, extended lifespan, an upright and radiant complexion, and vigorous physical strength. "Likewise follow in just this way" refers to this same fruit of retribution described above, swiftly fulfilled. This is spoken for one of dull faculties and lesser capacity, who does not know that holding this mantra is itself the cause of buddhahood yet to come, and seeks only the benefit of this physical body — and so this wish, too, is fulfilled. This is the wish to be reborn after this present life. "This life ends" means that the prior aggregate-body has already dissolved, and the next has not yet formed. One is reborn, according to one's own wish, in any pure land of the buddhas of the ten directions, this wish fulfilled entirely through the power of holding this mantra. Certainly never born in a border region or low station — if one is not even born there, in a border region or low station, how much less, then, in hell, as a hungry ghost, or in any other misshapen, differently retributed form at all? As the Detailed Commentary puts it: the Great Compassion Sūtra states, "Whoever recites and holds the Great Compassion Mantra, should they not be reborn in a buddha's own land, I vow never to attain true awakening." That vow, however, is joined to a bodhisattva's own power of vow; this one is joined to every buddha's own power of mind, since this mantra is every buddha's own mind-mantra, and whoever holds this mantra will certainly be held in mind and protected by every buddha. This closes the first section, showing these benefits for beings, each in turn.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, should any land — its provinces, counties, and villages — suffer famine, plague, warfare, banditry, strife and contention, or any other place of calamity and hardship, write out this mantra and place it at the four gates of the city, and also at every shrine, or atop every banner-pillar, so that the beings of that land may welcome and receive this mantra, bow to it in reverence, and, with a single mind, make offering to it — causing the people, each and every one, to wear it on the body, or to place it, each and every one, in the place where they dwell — every calamity and hardship will thereby be entirely dissolved."

Commentary: This makes plain, directly, that this mantra is able to protect the nation and give the people peace, turning misfortune into fortune. Should any land, within its own territory — "provinces" and "counties" being subdivisions of a nation's own governance, and "villages" being settlements where people gather, themselves governed under these provinces and counties — suffer famine, meaning that the five grains and every plant fail to grow, this being the calamity of famine; or plague, an epidemic being the untimely energy of the four seasons, which, when contracted, becomes illness, this being the calamity of plague; or warfare, meaning unrest at the borders; or banditry, meaning a household or nation without peace; or strife and contention, meaning chaos stirred up by petty troublemakers — these all being calamities of warfare; or any other place of calamity and hardship besides, such as prolonged rain bringing disaster, drought bringing famine, locusts running rampant, wind and hail arriving in season — anything, in short, that brings harm to sentient beings. Write out this mantra and place it at the four gates of the city — the very places through which everyone must pass; and also at every shrine, meaning, in general, every place fit for offering, that is, every pure buddha-realm; or atop every banner-pillar — a banner-pillar being what is meant by a banner such as the Supreme Banner or the Dhāraṇī Banner. Causing the beings of that land to bow in reverence, all alike of one mind, offering with utmost sincerity. Causing the people, each and every one, to wear this mantra on the body, or to place it, each and every one, in offering in the place where they dwell — every calamity, hardship, and danger will thereby be entirely dissolved, and cruelty and violence may naturally be transformed into auspicious harmony.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, in every place, in whatever land, wherever this mantra is present, nāgas and devas rejoice, wind and rain arrive in their proper season, the five grains flourish in abundance, and the multitudes of the people dwell in peace and happiness."

Commentary: In whatever place, in whatever land, wherever the beings of that land ought to have this mantra — wherever this mantra is present, and its people are able to receive and uphold it in faith — this moves the nāgas and devas to rejoice, and wind and rain then arrive in their proper season, just as in the flourishing ages of antiquity, five days of wind and ten of rain. From this, the five grains are gathered in, abundant and full. "Multitudes," a myriad meaning ten billion, and "the people," meaning the multitude — the classics of this land use "the multitude" to mean the common people; here, "the multitudes of the people" simply refers to how numerous the people are, not to any fixed number. "Dwell in peace and happiness" means that each dwells settled in their own occupation, content in their own work.

Sūtra: "This mantra is further able to subdue every evil star, whatever direction its calamitous transformation may appear in, so that disaster and hardship never arise, no one meets an unnatural death, no one is bound in shackles, fetters, or chains, and all sleep peacefully, day and night, forever free of evil dreams. Ānanda, in this sahā world there are eighty-four thousand calamitous, evil stars, with twenty-eight great evil stars foremost among them, and, further, eight great evil stars as their own chief rulers — taking on every manner of form, and, whenever they appear in the world, able to give rise to every kind of calamity and strange misfortune among sentient beings. Wherever this mantra is present, all such things are entirely dissolved. Within a radius of twelve yojanas, this becomes a bounded, protected ground, and every such calamity and inauspicious sign can never enter it at all."

Commentary: "Subdue" means to press down — this sacred mantra is further able to press down and subdue every evil star. Each star governs its own direction, hence "whatever direction." "Calamitous transformation" means that departing from the ordinary is called "transformation," and differing from the rest is called "calamitous" — such as comets and shooting stars appearing according to whatever direction they are drawn toward; the beings of that land, who would ordinarily neither see nor hear of such things at all. For human affairs unfold below, while the way of heaven answers from above — yet, once such calamitous transformations no longer arise, disaster and hardship naturally cease to arise as well. Since disaster does not arise, each person fulfills their own natural years — hence, "no one meets an unnatural death"; and since hardship does not arise, all remain at peace with one another, without incident — hence, "no one is bound in shackles, fetters, or chains." "All sleep peacefully, day and night, forever free of evil dreams" — day is the time of waking, and night is the time of lying down; sleeping peacefully means free of troubling dreams. Once disaster and hardship are both absent, one naturally lies down in peace and wakes in peace, and even one's dreams, too, are at peace. "Sahā world" means this world of the five turbidities, and so it has these evil stars; a pure land has none. "Eighty-four thousand" is the total number of stars arrayed around the heavens, corresponding to the number of afflictions of sentient beings. "Calamitous, evil stars" — these stars are not evil of themselves; it is because of calamity that they undergo this transformation, hence, "calamitous, evil stars." The twenty-eight correspond to the four directions taken as the warp, and the eight great stars correspond to what follows this same warp as the weft. As for these twenty-eight stars, Buddhist scripture and worldly classics largely agree, with minor differences of naming, region by region. It may be that Buddhist reckoning follows calamitous transformation, while the worldly classics follow ordinary, fixed positions — meaning that when a land is peaceful and settled, each group of seven, across all four directions, remains in its own fixed place; but when disaster is about to arise, these four groups of seven shift and cross one another, coming together to command all eighty-four thousand stars as their foremost leaders. There are, further, eight great evil stars, variously identified with the five visible planets together with the lunar nodes and the comet. These serve as chief rulers over the eighty-four thousand, with the twenty-eight great evil stars serving as their commanders — arrayed above as celestial signs, answering below in human affairs; when followed, they answer with good fortune, and when defied, with misfortune — exactly what the Confucian classics mean in saying, follow the way and gain good fortune, defy it and meet with misfortune. Taking on every manner of form — such as strange portents in water or sky, or omens appearing as a child chanting some prophetic rhyme, and so on — whenever they appear in the world, they are able to give rise to every kind of calamity and extraordinary, unpredictable misfortune among all sentient beings. As Wenling puts it: since this mantra's power accords with every good fortune, every calamitous transformation is entirely dissolved in the heavens themselves, and neither disaster nor inauspicious sign can ever enter this same territory. Wherever this secret mantra is present, misfortune may thus be turned back into fortune, and every calamitous sign is entirely dissolved. A yojana, here translated "a stage," resembles a relay station in this land — the distance a wheel-turning king would cover in one day's royal progress, before resting for the night. A yojana comes in three sizes, greater, middle, and lesser. "Becomes a bounded, protected ground" means that a hundred numinous beings shelter and protect it. "Every calamity and inauspicious sign can never enter it" — an "omen," here, is a portent, whether of good fortune or ill; and "inauspicious sign" here properly means an ill omen, not taking the sense of a good one. This closes the second section, explaining at length the reasons this mantra protects life and aids the path.

Sūtra: "For this very reason, the Tathāgata proclaims this mantra, that it might, in ages yet to come, protect beginning students, every practitioner, so that, entering samādhi, body and mind become at ease, attaining great peace and stability."

Commentary: Continuing from how holding this mantra brings about every benefit described above, for this very reason the Tathāgata proclaims this mantra, leaving it for ages yet to come, to protect those newly setting their minds on this study. Not speaking of the present age is because, in the present age, while the Buddha still lives in the world, demons cannot gain any opening; nor does it speak of those long in practice, since one long in practice already has the strength to subdue demons. "Entering samādhi" means entering true, fixed absorption through the perfect penetration of the ear-faculty. "Body and mind become at ease" means that both inner and outer, body and mind, are liberated and at ease, attaining great peace and stability.

Sūtra: "Never again will any demon or ghost or spirit, or any beginningless enmity, calamity, misfortune, old karma, or outstanding debt, come to trouble or harm this person."

Sūtra: "You, and every one among this assembly still in training, and every practitioner in ages yet to come, relying on my own platform of practice, keeping the precepts according to the Dharma, whatever precept-master one receives them from, encountering a pure monastic, holding this mantra-mind, will give rise to no doubt or regret."

Commentary: "You, and every one among this assembly still in training" refers to the assembly below the third fruit. "And every practitioner in ages yet to come" — those yet to come still require this protection, and this present assembly, all the more, ought to receive it in faith — relying on my own platform of practice, according to the ritual regulations I have set forth, keeping the precepts according to the Dharma, spotless as ice and frost. Whatever precept-master one receives them from, encountering a pure monastic, holding this mantra-mind, will give rise to no doubt or regret. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: not committing these four faults — first, an improper platform; second, a lapse in the precepts; third, an impure teacher; fourth, doubt and regret — should even one of these four be committed, it becomes difficult to attain realization in this very life, and only a distant cause is planted instead.

Sūtra: "Should this good son, within this very body born of father and mother, fail to attain this mind's own penetration, then everything the Tathāgatas of the ten directions have taught would amount to nothing but false speech."

Commentary: Should this good son, committing none of these four faults, fail, within this very body of the five aggregates now present, born of father and mother — not waiting for some later body — to attain this ground of mind, understanding clearly this tathāgatagarbha mind and thereby attaining perfect penetration, then everything the Tathāgatas of the ten directions have taught would amount to nothing but false speech. As the Detailed Commentary puts it: this "mind's own penetration" means precisely this ground of mind opened and penetrated. Should one truly accomplish what is described above, committing none of these four faults, this ground of mind naturally opens and penetrates; and should one's own strength prove not yet sufficient, one will certainly still receive the Buddha's own direct assistance — exactly as described earlier, in the brief showing of samādhi and wisdom: "I will myself manifest a body, come before that person, touch their crown, and comfort them, causing them to awaken" — this is exactly what is meant. I would say, further: should one attain realization through one's own strength, one will certainly attain the perfect understanding, greatly opened — exactly as described earlier, after the three weeks of samādhi: "seated upright, dwelling in stillness, across a hundred days, one of sharp faculties, without even rising from the seat, attains the fruit of stream-entry." Having attained this first fruit, one is able to sever views and confusion — exactly as Ānanda, having attained the position of the first fruit, by the end of the third fascicle of this very sūtra, attained a vast and perfect understanding, said to attain the dharma-body without passing through countless kalpas — meaning that, even though the sagely fruit itself is not yet accomplished in body and mind, one knows with certainty, beyond any doubt, that buddhahood will be attained. And should one's own mind and this essence become fully attuned, one will certainly attain knowledge of past lives as well — exactly as described earlier, in the perfect realization of understanding and clarity: "such a person, at that very moment, becomes able to recall, in mind, events across eighty-four thousand Ganges-sand kalpas, understanding them completely, without the slightest doubt remaining." This closes the second section, broadly displaying every power of this mantra. This, together with the larger section above, closes the second section, the Tathāgata speaking once more.

〖講〗此明內聖，金剛藏王菩薩，發願護咒。《正脈》云：證究竟堅固之理，故稱金剛；秘跡護持，故稱藏；慈威特勝，折攝並行，故稱王。菩薩是其常儀，降魔則現持杵忿怒金剛之相。《寶鏡疏》云：金剛藏王者，依究竟堅固，如來藏心，全體大用，以立名也。蓋首楞嚴定，亦名金剛三昧，所以地上菩薩，破除無明，皆用金剛正智，以其根本無明，最細最堅，若非金剛正智，則不能破故。此咒心亦稱金剛者，以從如來金剛心內，所流出也。故能除宿障，破無明，翻轉塵勞，而成護法，即有八萬四千，金剛正智，常隨守護也。世尊！如我等輩，指同行菩薩，所修功業，歷位斷證久矣！當成菩提，現在不取菩提，而證涅槃，而實圓住三秘密藏，常隨此咒，救護末世，修三摩提，指修習圓通之人。正修行者：謂持戒誦咒，方得遠魔離障，為正修行。此表其不捨眾生，帶果行因，以酬護生之本願也。

〖經〗世尊！如是修心，求正定人，若在道場，及餘經行，乃至散心，遊戲聚落。我等徒眾，常當隨從，侍衛此人。縱令魔王，大自在天，求其方便，終不可得。諸小鬼神，去此善人，十由旬外；除彼發心，樂修禪者。世尊！如是惡魔，若魔眷屬，欲來侵擾，是善人者，我以寶杵，殞碎其首，猶如微塵；恆令此人，所作如願。

〖講〗世尊！如是修心，修三摩提，求得楞嚴正定之人，若使在道場靜坐，專修反聞工夫。及於餘處經行，持誦秘密咒心，此是正定聚者；乃至散心，遊戲聚落，謂欲求正定，不能攝心，乃至散心，雖然散心，不忘誦咒，此是不定聚者；閒遊嬉戲，於人煙集聚，村落之中，此是邪定聚者；我等徒眾，指所統領眷屬。如前云：一一菩薩，皆有諸金剛眾，而為眷屬是也。又前云：設有眾生，於散亂心，此不定聚也；非三摩地，此邪定聚也。心憶口持，是金剛王，常隨於彼，何況決定菩提心者，此正定聚也。此則直言俱護耳，故曰常當隨從侍衛此人，亦顯為法為人之深心也。縱令魔王：欲界頂天；大自在天：即色界頂天。摩醯首羅天王，三目八臂、騎白牛，執白拂。此二魔王天，貪戀塵勞，惱亂正修。求其方便，終不可得者：無隙可乘也，有菩薩救護故。諸小鬼神者：指魔民魔女等，去者離也，離此善人，十由旬外；前云，有此咒地，十二由旬，成結界地。除彼發心，樂修禪者：諸魔鬼神，亦有發菩提心，好樂修禪之者，願隨行人，親近修習，故不袪除，準許隨從也。世尊！如是惡魔：指上二魔王天，常欲惱害正修，故以惡魔稱之。若魔眷屬：指上諸小鬼神，是其伴侶，故以眷屬稱之。欲來侵惱擾害，修三摩提之善人者：我以寶杵，殞碎其首，猶如微塵：殞滅也，寶杵擬之，其首即碎，不待觸擊也。恆令此人，所作如願者：恆者常也，常時救護此人，凡所修為，悉皆如意，離諸障難，故云如願也。《指掌疏》云：問：菩薩以慈悲為本，十重以殺戒為先，況復四種明誨，言猶在耳，遽發是願，應為佛呵，若果杵碎其首，犯殺違慈，其如菩薩行何？答：如父母之於兒女，笑罵皆為教道，菩薩之於眾生，折攝俱屬拔濟。故孤山云：若涅槃殺闡提，仙預誅淨行，皆住無緣慈，得一子地，乃能如是。是知此之杵碎其首，本無瞋怒，而其恆令如願，亦非喜愛；必以如是之人，乃可發如是願，如來不呵，非為無故，幸勿以大鵬比鷃也。《正脈》云：自初華屋請修，以至此文，當為巧修正助周。夫二修雖皆最初方便，而耳門深入、是為正修，而道場持咒，皆為助行。是知方便修人，自分利鈍二根。根稍利者，固不必道場等助，而自脫纏以入圓通，如阿難是也。根稍鈍，而不能促入者，方用後門助之。問：「此既一周，何無證悟之人？」答：「證悟在正行之末，此但助行，故無證悟。其於經題，四實法中，正屬修證了義耳」。辛二重請神咒竟。併上大科冥授以選本根竟。再併前正宗大科，己三說三摩修法，令從耳根一門深入竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Having spoken these words, the vajra-beings within the assembly, numbering in the countless hundreds of thousands, all at once, before the Buddha, joined their palms and bowed their heads to the ground, saying to the Buddha: 'Exactly as the Buddha has said, we will, with utmost sincerity, protect all those who cultivate bodhi in just this way.'"

Commentary: "Vajra-beings" are exactly those who wield the vajra pestle in hand, this host of mighty warriors. "Countless hundreds of thousands" states, in the most emphatic terms, just how numerous they are. All at once, before the Buddha, they joined their palms and bowed their heads to the ground, saying to the Buddha: exactly as the Buddha has said, this mantra carries such benefit as this; we will, with utmost sincerity, protect and uphold all those who cultivate bodhi in just this way — meaning those who cultivate the perfect penetration of the ear-faculty, thereby attaining this true, fixed samādhi of bodhi. Through protecting this mantra, one protects, as well, whoever holds it — these, too, belong to the same category as the retinue of this treasury-king, not the bodhisattvas spoken of in the text further on.

Sūtra: "At that time, Brahmā, together with Śakra, lord of the devas, and the four great heavenly kings, likewise bowed together before the Buddha, saying to the Buddha: 'Should there truly be such a good person, cultivating and studying in just this way, we will exert our utmost sincerity, protecting and guarding them, so that, throughout this one lifetime, everything they undertake is fulfilled according to their own wish.'"

Commentary: Brahmā and Śakra, these two heavenly rulers of highest rank, together with the four heavenly kings, ministers under Śakra's own command, likewise bowed together before the Buddha at that time, saying to the Buddha: "should there truly be" — truly, meaning genuinely — such a person truly holding this mantra, cultivating and studying the perfect penetration of the ear-faculty, this is exactly a person of pure and complete goodness; we will exert our utmost, most sincere effort, upholding and protecting them, so that, throughout this one lifetime, whatever they undertake is entirely fulfilled according to their own wish. As the Detailed Commentary puts it: "throughout this one lifetime, everything fulfilled according to their own wish" means attaining perfect bodhi within this very body, exactly what is meant by attaining the dharma-body without passing through countless kalpas. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this fulfillment within one lifetime means bringing about realization and this mind's own penetration within this present life — this belongs to the siddhānta of entering ultimate truth. Should this instead be read as fulfillment in every worldly matter, then it would belong to the remaining three siddhāntas.

Sūtra: "There were, further, countless great generals among the yakṣas, kings of the rākṣasas, kings of the pūtanas, kings of the kumbhāṇḍas, kings of the piśācas, Vināyaka, every great king among the ghosts, and every commander among the ghosts, who likewise, before the Buddha, joined their palms and bowed their heads to the ground, saying: 'We, too, vow to protect and uphold this person, so that their own mind of bodhi swiftly comes to perfect fulfillment.'"

Commentary: Calling them "great general" or "king" here, in every case, carries this same meaning of a chief ruler. They likewise bowed before the Buddha, giving rise to this vow, to protect and uphold this same person reciting and cultivating this mantra. "So that their own mind of bodhi swiftly comes to perfect fulfillment" means that this mind seeking buddhahood above swiftly comes to perfect accomplishment.

Sūtra: "There were, further, countless sons of heaven of the sun and moon, the lord of rain, the lord of clouds, the lord of thunder, together with the elder of lightning, the officials who patrol the years, and the retinues of every star, who likewise, within the assembly, bowed their heads at the Buddha's feet, saying to the Buddha: 'We, too, protect this practitioner, establishing this place of practice, so that they attain freedom from every fear.'"

Commentary: The sun and moon are the very rulers illuminating this world — each single world of four continents has its own pair of sun and moon, hence "countless." Wind, rain, cloud, and thunder are each called a "lord," and lightning is called an "elder," meaning an elder in rank — these, too, are titles of a chief ruler. "The officials who patrol the years" are those who govern the years and oversee the months, surveying every good and evil deed among humankind, resembling the twelve palace-spirits. They, too, within the assembly, bowed their heads at the Buddha's feet, saying to the Buddha: seeing this virtue, they, too, wished to emulate it, likewise vowing to protect this practitioner cultivating the perfect penetration of the ear-faculty — establishing this Śūraṅgama place of practice, so that this person may take realization within the appointed time, their mind at peace as the sea itself, attaining freedom from every fear.

Sūtra: "There were, further, countless mountain spirits, sea spirits, and every spirit of the earth, of water, land, and sky, and every essence-spirit of the ten thousand things, together with the king of the wind-spirits, and the heavens of the formless realm, who, before the Tathāgata, all at once bowed their heads, saying to the Buddha: 'We, too, protect this practitioner, so that they accomplish bodhi, forever free of demonic affairs.'"

Commentary: "Countless mountain spirits" means the spirits ruling over mountains, such as those of the five great peaks; "sea spirits" means the spirits ruling over the sea, such as those of the four great rivers; "spirit of the earth" means the spirit ruling over the earth, such as the earth-spirit of unshakable firmness. "Of water, land, and sky" names these three realms, so as to encompass every spirit within them. "Every essence-spirit of the ten thousand things" means such spirits as those of medicinal herbs, forest trees, or sprouting crops. Everything above belongs to the visible kind; what follows belongs to the invisible kind. "The king of the wind-spirits" means the spirit ruling over the wind; "the heavens of the formless realm" means the four heavens without form. "Before the Tathāgata, all at once bowed their heads, saying to the Buddha" — one might ask: how could the king of the wind-spirits, or the heavens of the formless realm, bow their heads at all? The answer is: these formless deities, reflected momentarily within the Tathāgata's own light, are thereby made to appear for a time, and thereby, too, receive this same joy. As the Nirvāṇa Sūtra states: were the heaven of neither-thought-nor-non-thought and the rest truly without form, how could they possibly come and go, advance and stop at all? And the Āgama states: when Śāriputra passed into extinction, the heavens of the formless realm wept tears like rain — all of this making plain that they are without coarse form, yet possess a subtle form nonetheless. Hence the Antarābhava Sūtra states that the heavens of the formless realm bow in worship before the World-Honored One — and this same bowing of the head here is exactly this same worship. "We, too, protect this practitioner, so that they accomplish unsurpassed bodhi, forever free, throughout, of any demonic affair." This closes the first section, protection upheld by this outer assembly.

Sūtra: "At that time, the eighty-four thousand nayutas of Ganges-sand koṭis of Vajragarbha bodhisattva-kings, within this great assembly, at once rose from their seats, bowed their heads at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: 'World-Honored One! As for beings such as ourselves, our own accomplished merit and work, we have long since accomplished bodhi; yet we do not take up nirvāṇa, and instead constantly follow this same mantra, rescuing and protecting every true practitioner of the final age who cultivates this samādhi.'"

Commentary: This makes plain that these inner sages, the Vajragarbha bodhisattva-kings, vow to protect this mantra. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: having realized the truth of ultimate indestructibility, they are called "vajra"; concealing this secret trace, upholding and protecting it, they are called "treasury"; their compassionate majesty especially supreme, both subduing and gathering in practiced side by side, they are called "king." Appearing as a bodhisattva is their ordinary manner; when subduing demons, they manifest, instead, this same wrathful form of a vajra-being wielding the pestle. As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: these "Vajragarbha kings" take their very name from this same tathāgatagarbha mind of ultimate indestructibility, its whole substance and great function. For this Śūraṅgama samādhi is also named the vajra samādhi, and so it is that bodhisattvas of the grounds, in breaking through fundamental ignorance, all employ this same correct wisdom of the vajra, since this root ignorance is the subtlest and most obstinate of all, and nothing but vajra's own correct wisdom is able to break through it. This mantra-mind, too, is called "vajra" for this same reason, since it flows forth from within the Tathāgata's own vajra mind. Hence it is able to remove past hindrances, break through ignorance, and turn back the toil of the senses, thereby becoming this same protector of the Dharma — and so these eighty-four thousand beings of vajra correct wisdom constantly follow and guard it. "World-Honored One, as for beings such as ourselves" refers to these fellow-practicing bodhisattvas. "Our own accomplished merit and work" — having, through every stage, long since severed delusion and attained realization, we ought, by rights, to have accomplished bodhi already; yet, at present, we do not take up bodhi and enter nirvāṇa, but instead truly abide within the three secret treasuries, constantly following this mantra, rescuing and protecting the final age, cultivating this same samādhi — referring to those who cultivate this perfect penetration. "True practitioners" means those who keep the precepts and hold this mantra, thereby truly standing apart from demons and obstruction — this being true practice. This shows that they do not abandon sentient beings, but, carrying this fruit while still practicing this cause, fulfill this same original vow to protect all beings.

Sūtra: "World-Honored One! Such a one, cultivating this mind, seeking true samādhi, whether seated within the place of practice, or walking about elsewhere, or even with a scattered mind, wandering and playing about the village — our own multitude of followers will constantly follow after them, attending and guarding this person. Even should Māra, lord of the highest heaven of the desire realm, or the deva of great sovereignty, seek some opening against them, in the end, none can ever be found. Every lesser demon and spirit stays ten yojanas distant from this good person — except those who have themselves set their minds on delighting in the cultivation of meditation. World-Honored One! Should such evil demons, or any demon's own retinue, wish to come and encroach upon or trouble this good person, we will, with our own precious pestle, shatter their heads to dust, and will constantly cause this person's every undertaking to be fulfilled according to their own wish."

Commentary: World-Honored One, such a one, cultivating this mind, seeking to attain this true Śūraṅgama samādhi, whether seated in stillness within the place of practice, devoted wholly to this practice of reverse-hearing, or, elsewhere, walking the path, reciting and holding this secret mantra-mind — this is a being of the truly settled group; or even with a scattered mind, wandering and playing about the village — meaning one who wishes to attain true samādhi yet is unable to gather in the mind, and so, even with a scattered mind, does not forget to recite this mantra — this is a being of the undetermined group; or one idly wandering and playing about, amid the gathered smoke of human dwellings, in the village itself — this is a being of the wrongly settled group. "Our own multitude of followers" refers to these same retinues under our own command, as described earlier: "each and every one of them has its own multitude of vajra-beings as retinue." And, as described earlier as well: "should there be sentient beings, with a scattered mind" — this is the undetermined group; "not yet in samādhi" — this is the wrongly settled group; "hold this mantra in mind and recite it with the mouth, these same vajra-kings still constantly follow after them — how much more, then, one whose mind is resolutely set on bodhi" — this is the truly settled group. This states plainly that all alike receive this same protection — hence, "will constantly follow after them, attending and guarding this person" — this, too, showing this same profound intent, undertaken both for the sake of the Dharma and for the sake of this person. "Māra, lord of the highest heaven of the desire realm" and "the deva of great sovereignty, at the summit of the form realm" — this Maheśvara deva-king, three-eyed, eight-armed, riding a white bull, holding a white whisk. These two demon-kings, attached to worldly toil, delight in disturbing true cultivation; yet, seeking some opening, in the end, none can ever be found — for no gap can be exploited, since these bodhisattvas rescue and protect against it. "Every lesser demon and spirit" refers to demon-subjects, demon-consorts, and the like; "stays distant" means to keep apart — apart from this good person, by ten yojanas — as described earlier: wherever this mantra is present, twelve yojanas become a bounded, protected ground. "Except those who have themselves set their minds on delighting in the cultivation of meditation" — even among these demons and spirits, some, too, have set their minds on bodhi, delighting in the cultivation of meditation, wishing to draw near this practitioner and cultivate alongside them — and so these are not driven away, but are, instead, permitted to follow along. World-Honored One, such evil demons refers to these two demon-kings described above, constantly wishing to trouble and harm true cultivation, hence called "evil demons." "Any demon's own retinue" refers to these lesser demons and spirits described above, their own companions, hence called "retinue." Should they wish to come and encroach upon or trouble this good person, cultivating this samādhi — we will, with our own precious pestle, shatter their heads to dust: to shatter is to destroy; struck with this pestle, their heads are shattered at once, without even needing to touch them. "And will constantly cause this person's every undertaking to be fulfilled according to their own wish" — "constantly" means at all times; at all times rescuing and protecting this person, so that whatever they cultivate and undertake is entirely fulfilled as wished, free of every hindrance and difficulty — hence, "fulfilled according to their own wish." As the Detailed Commentary raises this question: bodhisattvas take compassion as their very root, and the ten grave precepts place the precept against killing foremost among them — how much more, then, given that the Four Clear Instructions are still fresh in our ears — how could such a vow as this be raised so suddenly? Should this not draw the Buddha's own reproach? And should this pestle truly shatter their heads, would this not itself be killing, a violation of this same compassion — how, then, could this accord with a bodhisattva's own conduct at all? The answer: just as a parent, toward their own children, expresses both laughter and scolding alike as forms of teaching and guidance, so, too, a bodhisattva, toward sentient beings, expresses both subduing and gathering-in alike as forms of rescue and salvation. One should understand, then, that this shattering of heads with the pestle carries, at root, no anger at all; and this constant fulfillment of wishes carries, likewise, no ordinary fondness either — only toward a person such as this could such a vow as this ever be raised, and the Tathāgata does not reproach it, not without reason. Let none, then, mistake this great roc for some small quail. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it, in final summary: from the very beginning, this request to build the platform of flowers, all the way to this present passage, together constitute what may be called the complete cycle of skillful direct and auxiliary practice. Now, though both are, at root, the most preliminary of skillful means, this direct entry through the gate of the ear is the true, direct practice, while this building of the platform and holding of the mantra are, both alike, auxiliary practice. One thus knows that, among practitioners of skillful means, there are, by nature, those of sharper and those of duller faculty: one somewhat sharper need not rely on this platform and the rest as auxiliary aid at all, but is naturally able to cast off entanglement and enter perfect penetration directly, exactly as Ānanda himself did; while one somewhat duller, unable to enter so swiftly, must then make use of this latter approach as an aid. One might ask: since this constitutes one complete cycle, why is there no account here of anyone actually attaining awakening or realization? The answer: awakening and realization belong properly to the end of the direct practice itself; this passage speaks only of auxiliary practice, and so no account of awakening or realization appears here. As for its place within the sūtra's own title, among these four true dharmas, this passage belongs, properly, to cultivation and realization of complete meaning alone. This closes this second major section, the request to speak this mantra once more. Together with the larger section above, this closes the very use of the hidden bestowal in selecting this root faculty. And, joined further with the larger section of the main body of the teaching before it — the third of Master Yuanying's own three sections, expounding the practice of this samādhi, causing entry through this one single gate of the ear-faculty — this, too, now comes to its close.

Reading note: Master Yuanying closes this fascicle by naming exactly what these two fascicles, taken together, have accomplished within the sūtra's own larger architecture: the "complete cycle of skillful direct and auxiliary practice" (巧修正助周). The direct practice is the ear-faculty's own reverse-hearing, entered without any external aid; the auxiliary practice is everything surrounding it — the platform, the precepts, and this mantra. A practitioner of sharp faculties, like Ānanda himself once his own understanding opened, needs only the direct practice; a practitioner of duller faculties leans on this same auxiliary support. Both converge on the identical realization — this fascicle's own closing question-and-answer makes a point of clarifying that no new account of awakening appears here only because this whole section was, from the start, describing this auxiliary help rather than the moment of realization itself, which belongs to the direct practice already unfolding in the fascicles before it.
