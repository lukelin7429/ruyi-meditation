The Clouds Part, the Moon Shows

Ānanda's mind grows entirely open, and he speaks the sūtra's most famous verse of gratitude — chanted independently in monasteries to this day. Then Pūrṇa, foremost in eloquence among the Buddha's disciples, raises two doubts: if everything is originally the pure tathāgatagarbha, why does a whole world of mountains, rivers, and suffering beings ever arise at all — and how can the five great elements interfuse without destroying one another? The Buddha's answer to the first question traces the sūtra's own account of cosmogony step by step, from a single mistaken thought to mountains, oceans, sentient beings, and the endless cycle of killing, stealing, and desire.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 8 closed the sūtra's entire "refuting falseness, revealing truth" division. Fascicle 9 opens the next: Ānanda and the assembly, having heard everything harmonized into the tathāgatagarbha, find their minds thrown entirely open — and Ānanda speaks the sūtra's celebrated verse of thanks, "Wondrously still, all-upholding, unmoved World-Honored One." Pūrṇa, foremost among the Buddha's disciples in eloquence, then raises two doubts of his own: if the world is originally the pure tathāgatagarbha, why does an entire world of mountains, rivers, and suffering beings arise from it at all — and how can the five great elements interfuse without destroying one another? The Buddha's full answer to the first question occupies the rest of this fascicle, tracing the whole chain from a single mistaken thought ("the nature of awareness is necessarily bright") through the three subtle and six coarse marks to the three continuities of world, sentient beings, and karma. The second question — and the teaching of the tathāgatagarbha's third and final aspect it requires — is taken up in fascicle 10.

Ānanda's Mind Becomes Clear, and Faith Arises 辛二 阿難明心生信 · 壬初 承示開悟

Chinese Source

〖經〗爾時阿難，及諸大眾，蒙佛如來，微妙開示，身心蕩然，得無罣礙，是諸大眾，各各自知，心遍十方，見十方空，如觀手中，所持葉物。一切世間，諸所有物，皆即菩提，妙明元心。心精遍圓，含裹十方。反觀父母，所生之身，猶彼十方虛空之中，吹一微塵，若存若亡。如湛巨海，流一浮漚，起滅無從，瞭然自知，獲本妙心，常住不滅。

〖講〗此結集經家，敘述阿難，悟明本有真心，發起大乘正信，先敘承示，以為開悟之大本也。爾時，乃如來破妄顯真，已竟之時。微者隱微，昔日權宗，未曾顯說，六識是生死根本，根性是涅槃正因。妙者奧妙，今日實教，廣談了義，直指生滅身中有佛性，一切諸法是真如：此即上三卷之微妙開示。《正脈》云：通承破妄顯真科中，諸文為言，良以此大開解，功夫非近，今當總前，撮其大要，令知微妙之實。破妄心有三：一、七破以密示無處；二、重徵以顯呵非心；三、縱奪以決指無體。是所以破妄心者，極微細而盡精妙矣。顯真心文中亦三：一示見等，而克就根性，直指其實體。二、示陰等，而廣融諸相，以明其一體。三、示地等，而極顯圓融，以彰其全體。是所以顯真心者，亦可謂極微細而盡精妙矣。

阿難開悟，即是已開見道之眼。自第一卷阿難認識為心，三迷被破，遂即捨妄求真，求如來為發妙明心，為開道眼。如來即為指見是心，顯見不動、不滅、不失、無還、不雜、無礙、不分、超情、離見，經歷十番，可謂發妙明心，而至微細矣！更為會四科，全事即理，融七大全相皆性，普融萬法，可謂發妙明心，而極奧妙矣！由是阿難明心生信，頓開道眼，故有此悟。

身心蕩然，得無罣礙者：當以二義釋之：一、達妄本空：昔在迷時，妄認四大假合，為自身相；六塵緣影，為自心相；乃為六根所局，六識所錮，皆成罣礙。今者了達妄身、妄心，本自空寂，蕩然如大水漂蕩無存，故得無礙，解脫自在也。二、知真本具：昔在迷時，將本有法性身，妙明心，埋沒於塵勞煩惱之中，幻妄身心之內，今者知法身清淨，遍一切處，則身蕩然；妙心圓明，遍周法界，則心蕩然；無形無相，蕩然寬廓，得無罣礙，得大受用也。

是諸大眾，各各自知，心遍十方者：以既蒙如來，微妙開示，不獨當機阿難得悟，是諸同聞大眾，亦多各各自知；自知即悟也。其中大眾，位有淺、深不同，悟有解、證不等，所悟為何？乃悟真心，遍滿十方，包含萬法。較之昔日，認識為心，執心在身內；捨妄求真文云：「不知寂常心性。」不失科中云：「不知色身外洎山河，虛空大地，咸是妙明真心中物。」不雜科中則曰：「云何得知，是我真性？」不分科中又謂：「茫然不知，是義終始！」四科總標文云：「殊不能知，生滅去來，本如來藏，常住妙明，不動周圓，妙真如性。」此皆不自知也。

今聞四科，全事即理，七大全相皆性，到此云散月明，瓜熟蒂落，如人飲水，冷煖自知。心遍十方，見十方虛空，乃依報世界之最大者。在真心中，如觀手中，所持葉物，此則觀大同小也。葉即貝葉，物如菴摩羅果。

此悟萬法唯心。妙明元心，即如來藏心。一切包括之詞，世間即情、器二世間，身心世界是也。此皆領上顯見科中，第四顯見不失文云：「色心諸緣，及心所使，諸所緣法，唯心所現；汝身汝心，皆是妙明，真精妙心中所現物。」又領第八顯見不分文云：「此見及緣即身心世界，元是菩提，妙淨明體。」又領陰等四科，皆即如來藏性，而成此悟，至此則斥破妄心，顯示真心之旨，方以極領，更不再認緣塵分別為心矣。

此悟心包萬法。嚮者誤認妄想為真心，惑在色身之內，身在界中，界在空中，重重含裹，遺真認妄，迷已為物，曾無超越；今者圓悟真心，惟精惟一，惟是一真法界，純一無雜，周遍圓滿，故曰：心精遍圓。含容萬法，包裹十方，無往而非常住真心，無處不是性淨明體，此則轉小成大也。乃領上七大文中，性真圓融，皆如來藏，而成此悟。以上正敘心蕩然，得無罣礙。

此悟正報法身。嚮者妄認四大假合，以為遍身，亦復認物為己，六根各局，四支質礙，處在空界之中，不能超越；今則悟明本有法身，虛廓曠蕩，猶若太虛，反觀父母所生血肉之身，高不過七尺，壽不上百年，總屬幻妄不實，猶彼十方虛空之中，風吹所起一點微塵，至渺小，極幻妄，似有似無。太虛喻法身廣大，微塵喻生身渺小。此身在法性空中，如草露風燈，雖然暫存，終非永存，故曰若存；又如幻事夢境，雖未即亡，終歸滅亡，故曰若亡。此則轉麤為細也。

向者誤執此身堅實，今悟法性身，如湛然澄清不動之巨大海；生身如海中所流一浮漚，倏起倏滅，起無所從來，滅無所從去，常處出生，隨處滅盡，乃屬幻妄無常。此則轉實為虛也。

瞭然自知，獲本妙心，常住不滅者：此結身蕩然也。前敘開悟之文，總標身心蕩然，得無罣礙，身即法性身，心即妙明心，因悟法身妙心，故得蕩然無礙。下則先敘心蕩然。阿難與大眾，自知妙心，遍滿十方，萬法唯心，心包萬法，澈悟依報，法法全真。

後敘身蕩然，因悟法身廣大，常住不滅，故反觀父母所生之身，如空中一塵，海中一漚，是以結曰：「瞭然自知」。瞭然明白，纖毫不昧，親證實到，自知自信，不由他悟也。獲本妙心者：即得親見，本覺妙心。本覺妙心，是本有法身，一向迷時，法身埋沒在五蘊身中；今已開悟，親見本來面目，雖然曰獲，既屬本有，實非新得。此身常無始終，住無去來，無始無終，無去無來，則永劫不滅，故曰「常住不滅」。此亦領前，不滅、不失、無還三科，及釋迷悶科末，清淨本心，本覺常住，會四科一一非因緣，非自然，融七大一一皆如來藏，本無生滅，諸微妙開示，而成此悟也。

《正脈》交光法師云：經家於佛說之後，偈讚之前，特詳敘此者，正以示奢摩他，秘奧觀體，令行人於此著眼；蓋通前三卷工夫，全為揭露，此至妙至密之觀體也。良以眾生常流轉，權乘不究竟者，皆緣未見此體，猶如生盲故也。行人若能於斯所敘，心境一如，不犯思惟，物物頭頭，瞭然在目，渾是妙心自體，亦不費纖毫功力，身心本來，廓周沙界；但不馳散，積之歲月，而不心開者，未之有也。當知本惟一體，若語正因本性，即空如來藏，以一味真如，更無餘物故。若兼了因，即奢摩他秘密觀照，以親見自心，非作意思惟故。若更不避彌天過犯，則西來直指，正法眼藏，即此而已。但彼直入無分別，此由方便分別，至此無分別處，其歸一也。

問：此似意盡無餘，然奢摩他未竟，後二藏未談，彼是何意？答：徵密觀照，此方了其密字，以體屬秘奧故也。彼乃兼用，盡其精細，始屬微字，宜酌分之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: At that time Ānanda and the whole assembly, receiving the Tathāgata's subtle and wondrous instruction, felt body and mind grow entirely open, free of every obstruction. Each one of them came to know, for themselves, that mind pervades the ten directions, and saw the empty space of the ten directions the way one sees a leaf held in the palm of the hand. Everything that exists in the whole world is nothing but the wondrous, bright, original mind of bodhi; the essence of mind, perfectly full and complete, encloses and contains the ten directions within itself. Looking back at the body born of their own parents, it seemed like a single speck of dust blown about somewhere within the boundless space of the ten directions, now appearing, now vanishing; like a single bubble floating on a vast, calm ocean, arising and ceasing with no origin to be found. Clearly, of themselves, they knew they had recovered their own original, wondrous mind, ever-abiding and never perishing.

Commentary: This is the sūtra-compiler's own account, narrating how Ānanda awakened to his own inherently possessed true mind and gave rise to genuine Great Vehicle faith — first narrating the instruction received, as the great foundation for this awakening. "At that time" means the moment just after the Tathāgata had completed refuting falseness and revealing truth. "Subtle" means hidden and faint: the provisional teaching, in earlier days, never openly stated that the sixth consciousness is the root of birth and death, and the sense-faculty's own nature the true cause of nirvāṇa. "Wondrous" means profound: today's real teaching broadly expounds the complete meaning, pointing directly to how buddha-nature exists within this very body of arising and ceasing, and how every phenomenon whatsoever is true suchness — this is exactly the subtle, wondrous instruction of the three fascicles just concluded.

The Zhèngmài commentary says: taken together, everything in the "refuting falseness, revealing truth" division amounts to this — since this great unfolding of understanding was no small undertaking, it is worth now gathering up what came before and drawing out its main point, so that its subtle reality may be clearly grasped. The refutation of the false mind had three parts: first, the seven-location search, secretly showing it has no location at all; second, the repeated challenge, openly rebuking it as not the mind; third, the granting-and-withdrawing argument, decisively showing it has no substance. This is why the refutation of the false mind may be called utterly fine-grained and exhaustively subtle. The revelation of the true mind likewise had three parts: first, showing seeing and the rest, taking hold of the sense-faculty's own nature to point directly to its real substance; second, showing the aggregates and the rest, broadly harmonizing every appearance to reveal their single substance; third, showing earth and the rest, fully displaying their perfect interfusion to manifest their complete substance. This, too, is why the revelation of the true mind may be called utterly fine-grained and exhaustively subtle.

Ānanda's awakening here is exactly the opening of the eye of the path. From the very first fascicle, where Ānanda mistook consciousness for the mind and his threefold confusion was refuted, he thereupon set aside falseness to seek the true, asking the Tathāgata to bring forth his own wondrous, bright mind and open his eye of the path. The Tathāgata then pointed out that seeing is the mind, revealing seeing as unmoving, unceasing, never lost, having nowhere to return to, unmixed with objects, unobstructed, undivided, surpassing every category, and moving beyond seeing itself — running through ten displays in all — which may be called bringing forth the wondrous, bright mind down to its very finest grain. He then further harmonized the four categories, phenomenon entirely principle, and interfused the seven great elements, every appearance entirely nature, universally interfusing the whole of phenomena — which may be called bringing forth the wondrous, bright mind to its utmost, most profound depth. Through all this, Ānanda's mind became clear and faith arose, the eye of the path opening at once — hence this awakening.

"Body and mind grow entirely open, free of every obstruction" should be understood in two senses: first, penetrating through to the fact that falseness is, at root, empty — in earlier days of confusion, one falsely took the four elements' provisional combination for one's own bodily form, and the shadow cast by the six sense-objects for one's own mental form, thereby confined by the six sense-faculties and imprisoned by the six consciousnesses, everything becoming an obstruction. Now, having penetrated through to the fact that this false body and false mind are, from the start, empty and still, one is swept entirely open, like a great flood carrying everything away, leaving nothing behind — hence "free of obstruction," liberated and at ease. Second, knowing that truth is already inherently possessed — in earlier days of confusion, one's own inherently possessed dharma-nature body and wondrous, bright mind lay buried beneath the dust and toil of affliction, within this illusory, false body and mind; now, knowing that the dharma-body is pure, pervading every place, the body grows entirely open; and the wondrous mind, perfectly bright, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, the mind grows entirely open — formless and without any fixed appearance, vast and boundlessly open, one attains freedom from every obstruction, and comes into this great functioning.

"Each one of them came to know, for themselves, that mind pervades the ten directions": having received the Tathāgata's subtle, wondrous instruction, it was not Ānanda alone, the one directly addressed, who awakened — the whole assembly listening alongside him likewise, for the most part, came to know this for themselves; knowing for oneself is exactly awakening. Among this assembly, standing differed, shallow or deep, and awakening differed, understanding or realization, in unequal measure — but what, exactly, did they awaken to? They awakened to the fact that the true mind pervades and fills the ten directions, containing every phenomenon within it. Compare this to their earlier state: mistaking consciousness for the mind, clinging to the mind as located within the body; the passage on setting aside falseness to seek the true says, "not knowing the still, ever-constant nature of mind"; the section on seeing never truly lost says, "not knowing that the physical body, and, beyond it, the mountains, rivers, empty space, and the great earth, are all objects within the wondrous, bright, true mind"; the section on seeing not mixed with objects asks, "how, then, could one know this is one's own true nature?"; the section on seeing not divided says, "utterly at a loss, not knowing where this meaning begins or ends"; and the general statement introducing the four categories says, "no one seems able to recognize that this arising and ceasing, this coming and going, is, at root, the tathāgatagarbha" — every one of these describing a failure to know for oneself.

Now, hearing that the four categories are, in their entirety, phenomenon exactly principle, and the seven great elements, every appearance exactly nature, one arrives, at this point, at the clouds parting to reveal the moon, the melon ripening until it falls from its own stem — exactly like a person drinking water, knowing for himself whether it is warm or cool. "Mind pervades the ten directions, and saw the empty space of the ten directions" — this empty space is the very largest object within the circumstantial world of retribution; yet, within the true mind, it is seen the way one sees a leaf held in the palm of the hand — this is seeing something vast as though it were something small. "Leaf" here means a palm-leaf; "object" is compared to an āmalaka fruit.

This is awakening to the fact that the ten thousand phenomena are mind alone. "The wondrous, bright, original mind" is exactly the tathāgatagarbha mind. "Everything" is an inclusive term: "the world" means both the sentient world and the material world — body, mind, and the world itself. All of this receives what was shown earlier, in the fourth display of seeing, seeing never truly lost: "Form, mind, every condition, and everything the mind directs — every phenomenon taken as an object — is nothing but what mind itself manifests; your own body and your own mind are both objects manifesting within this wondrous, bright, true, essential, wondrous mind." It likewise receives the eighth display, seeing not divided: "This seeing, and what it takes as its condition — that is, body, mind, and world — are, at root, bodhi's own wondrous, pure, bright substance." And it likewise receives the teaching that the aggregates and the other three categories are, every one, the tathāgatagarbha's own nature — bringing this awakening to completion. Only now is the whole point of refuting the false mind and revealing the true mind fully received — no longer ever again mistaking the discriminating mind, conditioned by its object, for the mind itself.

This is awakening to the fact that mind contains the ten thousand phenomena. Before, one mistakenly took deluded thought for the true mind, confined within the physical body; the body within the world, the world within space, layer upon layer enclosing one within the next — abandoning the true and clinging to the false, mistaking oneself for a mere object, never once transcending this confinement. Now, perfectly awakened to the true mind — essential, single, nothing but the one true Dharma-realm, pure and unmixed, perfectly complete and all-pervading — hence: "the essence of mind, perfectly full and complete." Containing and holding the ten thousand phenomena, enclosing the ten directions, nowhere that is not the ever-abiding true mind, no place that is not this pure, bright substance of nature — this is turning the small into the great. This receives what was shown earlier, in the passage on the seven great elements, that each, in its true nature, is perfectly interfused, each the tathāgatagarbha — bringing this awakening to completion. The above narrates directly the mind growing entirely open, free of every obstruction.

This is awakening to the direct retribution body, the dharma-body. Before, one falsely took the four elements' provisional combination as one's own body throughout, and mistook this object for oneself, each of the six sense-faculties confined to its own domain, the four limbs obstructed by solid matter, situated within the bounds of space, unable to transcend it. Now, awakened to the inherently possessed dharma-body — vast, open, and boundless, exactly like great space itself — one looks back at this flesh-and-blood body born of one's own parents, no more than seven feet tall, living no more than a hundred years, altogether illusory and unreal — exactly like a single mote of dust stirred up by the wind somewhere within the boundless space of the ten directions: utterly tiny, thoroughly illusory, seeming to exist, and seeming not to. Great space is the analogy for the dharma-body's vastness; the mote of dust, for this born body's tininess. This body, within the emptiness of the dharma-nature, is like dew on grass or a lamp in the wind — though it lingers for a while, it is never truly lasting, hence "seeming to exist"; and like a magical illusion or a scene in a dream — though not yet gone, it inevitably comes to an end, hence "seeming not to." This turns the coarse into the fine.

Before, one mistakenly clung to this body as solid and real; now, one awakens to the dharma-nature body as like a vast, still, clear, unmoving ocean; and this born body as like a single bubble floating upon that ocean, appearing and vanishing in an instant, arising from nowhere, ceasing to nowhere, arising in its own proper place and ceasing entirely in that same place — nothing but illusory, false, impermanent. This turns the substantial into the empty.

"Clearly, of themselves, they knew they had recovered their own original, wondrous mind, ever-abiding and never perishing" concludes the body's own becoming entirely open. The earlier narration of this awakening set out, in general, body and mind growing entirely open, free of every obstruction — the body being exactly the dharma-nature body, the mind being exactly the wondrous, bright mind; because of awakening to this dharma-body and this wondrous mind, one attains this entirely open, unobstructed state. What follows narrates first the mind growing entirely open: Ānanda and the assembly, knowing for themselves that the wondrous mind pervades and fills the ten directions, that the ten thousand phenomena are mind alone, and mind contains the ten thousand phenomena — thoroughly awakening to the circumstantial retribution, every single phenomenon entirely true.

Afterward it narrates the body growing entirely open: because of awakening to the dharma-body's vastness, ever-abiding and never perishing, one looks back at this body born of one's own parents as like a single speck of dust in space, a single bubble on the ocean — hence the conclusion, "clearly, of themselves, they knew." "Clearly" means fully understood, without the slightest obscurity; personally realized, arrived at in fact; known for oneself, believed for oneself, not awakened to through anyone else. "Recovered their own original, wondrous mind" means personally beholding the wondrous mind of original enlightenment. This wondrous mind of original enlightenment is the inherently possessed dharma-body; all along, in confusion, this dharma-body lay buried within the body of the five aggregates — now, awakened, one personally beholds one's own original face. Though called "recovered," since it was already inherently possessed, it is, in truth, nothing newly gained. This body is ever without beginning or end, abiding without coming or going — hence never perishing through eternal kalpas, hence "ever-abiding and never perishing." This, too, receives the earlier sections on seeing never ceasing, never lost, and having nowhere to return to, together with the close of the section resolving confusion — the pure, original mind, original enlightenment ever-abiding — and the harmonizing of the four categories, each in turn neither a matter of causes-and-conditions nor of spontaneity, and the interfusing of the seven great elements, each in turn the tathāgatagarbha, originally free of arising and ceasing — all this subtle, wondrous instruction bringing this awakening to completion.

Master Jiaoguang's Zhèngmài commentary says: the sūtra-compilers, after the Buddha's own teaching and before the verse of praise, take special care to narrate this passage in such detail precisely to point to śamatha's own hidden, secret substance of contemplation, so that practitioners may fix their attention here — for the whole effort of the three preceding fascicles has been entirely devoted to uncovering this same, most wondrous, most secret substance of contemplation. It is precisely because sentient beings forever drift in transmigration, and the provisional vehicle never reaches the ultimate, that neither has ever truly seen this substance — exactly like someone born blind. If a practitioner can, in this same manner narrated here, make mind and its object one and the same, without falling into discursive thought, then every single thing, examined closely, stands clearly before the eyes, nothing but this wondrous mind's own substance — costing not the slightest exertion at all, body and mind, from the very start, as vast as the sands of the Ganges. Only let one not let the mind scatter and run outward; sustained month after month, year after year, there has never yet been a case where the mind failed, in the end, to open.

One should understand that this is, at root, a single substance: spoken of as the correct cause, the original nature, it is exactly the "empty" tathāgatagarbha, a single flavor of true suchness, with nothing else beyond it at all. Spoken of together with the complete cause, it is exactly śamatha's own secret contemplation, personally beholding one's own mind, not a matter of deliberate, discursive thought at all. And if one dares risk incurring every possible objection by saying so, this is exactly the direct pointing brought from the West, the true Dharma-eye treasury itself — nothing more, nothing less. Only, that tradition enters directly into non-discrimination, while this sūtra proceeds by way of skillful discrimination, arriving, in the end, at this same place beyond discrimination — the two converging into one and the same destination.

Answer: "What has been shown so far fulfills only the word 'secret' in 'secret contemplation,' since the substance itself belongs to what is hidden and secret; the remaining two aspects bring the function to bear as well, exhausting every fine detail, and only then fulfill the word 'subtle' — the two should be carefully distinguished."

Commentary: This praises and thanks both the Buddha and the Dharma: the first line praises the Buddha; of the second line, the first four characters praise the Dharma, the last three praise Buddha and Dharma together. "Wondrously still" praises the Buddha's dharma-body. The dharma-body is the principle-substance every phenomenon depends on — called dharma-nature among the insentient, buddha-nature among the sentient; it is exactly every sentient being's own inherently possessed buddha-nature, wondrously aware and utterly still, pervading the whole Dharma-realm. Sentient beings, in delusion, have this dharma-body buried beneath the mountain of the five aggregates; our Buddha, at the causal stage, awakened to this wondrously aware, utterly still true mind — this is exactly the virtue of the dharma-body. The sūtra elsewhere says: "the subtle, wondrous, pure dharma-body, utterly still, reflecting everything" — hence the praise, "wondrously still."

"All-upholding" praises the Buddha's reward-body. The reward-body takes wisdom as its very body — this wisdom being prajñā's own wondrous wisdom, the virtue of prajñā, which every single being already possesses. The Buddha says: every being upon this great earth already possesses the Tathāgata's own wisdom and virtuous marks — pointing exactly to this virtue of prajñā. Sentient beings, deluded about this, turn wisdom entirely into consciousness; our Buddha, at the causal stage, awakened to this, and, relying on suchness-wisdom illuminating suchness-principle, turned the light inward, illuminating the very source of mind, until delusion was exhausted and wisdom perfected, turning consciousness into wisdom, attaining fundamental wisdom, and becoming the reward-body enjoyed by himself alone. Relying on this fundamental wisdom, subsequently attained wisdom arises as well, becoming the reward-body enjoyed together with others. Upholding boundless virtue, adorned with every excellent mark, capable of serving sentient beings as an external condition, permeating them from without — hence the praise, "all-upholding."

"Unmoved" praises the Buddha's manifestation-body. The manifestation-body is the body shown forth in answer to sentient beings' own capacities: observing that a being will be liberated by a superior manifested body, the Buddha shows a superior manifested body; by an inferior manifested body, an inferior manifested body — manifesting in answer to each capacity, all the while never stirring from its own fundamental ground, universally answering the ten directions, free and liberated, functioning without obstruction — this is exactly the virtue of liberation. As when the Buddha first taught the Avataṃsaka, and, this great teaching not suiting lesser capacities, he thereupon showed two aspects at once, never stirring from the place of stillness even while wandering to the Deer Park, manifesting a body sixteen feet tall to teach the five mendicants — hence the praise, "unmoved." This explains the three virtues and three bodies together, since the Buddha realizes the three virtues and thereby accomplishes the three bodies, and is praised accordingly. As for the word "Honored One": because the Buddha, proceeding from cause to fruit, has exhausted all five abidings of affliction, forever ended both kinds of death, and become complete in both merit and wisdom, he is honored above all nine realms.

"King Śūraṅgama" is exactly the very Dharma Ānanda originally requested, and the World-Honored One realized and taught. Ānanda had asked after the samādhi by which the Tathāgatas of the ten directions attain bodhi — first requesting the teaching on wondrous śamatha, samādhi, and dhyāna, separate names for this same samādhi; the Buddha later told him of a samādhi called the Great Buddha's Crown, King Śūraṅgama — the single, overarching name for all three. Śūraṅgama translates as "every phenomenon's ultimate firmness" — the Buddha, having realized this samādhi of ultimate firmness, has just finished explaining how the four categories and seven great elements, every phenomenon and appearance whatsoever, are, each and every one, harmonized back into the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, originally unmoving, originally free of arising and ceasing — this self-nature's own inherent, wondrous samādhi being exactly the Śūraṅgama samādhi. This samādhi is the king among all samādhis, capable of governing a hundred thousand samādhis at once. Ānanda, having heard this and awakened to it, praises it by the very name of the samādhi he has awakened to. The three characters "rarest of all in this world" praise Buddha and Dharma together, as something hard to encounter, hard to meet. As the Lotus Sūtra says: "Buddhas appear in the world only after a vast, remote span of time — hard indeed to encounter; and even when they do appear, teaching this Dharma is harder still — like the udumbara flower, blooming only once in a great while." How could this not be "rarest of all in this world"?

This shows the benefit gained: the first line, the benefit of ending falseness; the second, the benefit of manifesting truth. "Melted" means dissolved and removed; "a billion kalpas" means countless kalpas, not a fixed number; "inverted thinking" means being deluded about truth and taking falseness for it, clinging to falseness as truth, truth and falseness turned upside down. One's own inherently possessed dharma-body, deluded and unaware of itself, is thus not truly lost, though it seems lost; and once, in a single moment, one awakens and personally beholds one's own original face, then there is nothing gained that was not already had, and yet it counts as gaining! Now, having heard the Tathāgata lead him along step by step, refuting the shadow-conditioned mind as not the true mind, pointing to the buddha-nature within the sense-faculty, ten displays given directly, two kinds of false seeing shown in turn, harmonizing the four categories so that falseness is exactly truth, and further interfusing the seven great elements so that every appearance is entirely nature — the inherently possessed dharma-body has been laid out entirely in plain view, like clouds parting to reveal the sun, like ice melting into water: the inverted thinking of a billion kalpas is dissolved in a single moment!

"Without passing through countless ages, I have gained the Dharma-body": the Sanskrit asaṃkhyeya-kalpa translates as "an uncountable kalpa." The Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā says: "Three asaṃkhyeya-kalpas cultivating the six perfections, then a hundred kalpas planting the causes of the excellent marks, and only then is the fivefold dharma-body attained." The Vijñaptimātratā-siddhi says: "Before the first ground, one asaṃkhyeya-kalpa passes; from the first ground to the seventh, a second is completed; from the eighth ground to equal enlightenment, a third — and only then is the ultimate dharma-body reached." Ānanda's own claim here, that he has attained the dharma-body without needing to pass through three great asaṃkhyeya-kalpas, has become a source of controversy. Three doubts have been raised about it: first, that it contradicts the provisional teaching's own account, and so doubts this sūtra's own perfect, real Great Vehicle principle; second, that Ānanda still hopes to have his subtle delusions removed and swiftly ascend to wondrous enlightenment — apparent proof that he has not yet attained the dharma-body at all; third, that Ānanda still remains at the first fruit — so how could he suddenly claim to have gained the dharma-body without passing through the asaṃkhyeyas at all?

A detailed explanation is now called for. First, one should examine the word "gained" closely: "gain" means "attain," and attainment differs as attainment through awakening or attainment through realization. Ānanda's own hindrance of affliction runs heavy, while his hindrance of the knowable runs light — and the Buddha has, layer upon layer, revealed to him directly his own inherently possessed true mind: this mind, unborn, unceasing, unmoving, is exactly the pure dharma-body every single being already possesses, formerly covered over by inverted, deluded thought. Now that this inverted thought has been dissolved, exactly as clouds parting naturally reveal the sun, he personally beholds his own inherently possessed buddha-nature — what fault, then, is there in saying he has "awakened to and gained the dharma-body"? Second, one should examine the distinction between the provisional and the real teaching: the provisional teaching mostly emphasizes phenomena, which must be brought about through cultivation; the real teaching emphasizes what is inherently possessed in nature, valuing understanding above all. Even before awakening, the dharma-body is, of itself, already fully present — as the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment says: "every sentient being has, at root, already accomplished the buddha-path; if only one can, in a single thought, turn the light around, one becomes at once identical with what was already attained — simply leave deluded conditions behind, and one is, just as one is, already a buddha." How could this be the same as the provisional, gradual teaching, which insists one must pass through the asaṃkhyeyas before the dharma-body can be gained at all? Third, one should examine the perfect, real teaching's own principle: the graduated stages do not obstruct the perfect interfusion, and the perfect interfusion does not obstruct the graduated stages. As Master Jiaoguang's Zhèngmài commentary puts it: according to the perfect teaching's own principle, the graduated stages do not obstruct the perfect interfusion — so, even without yet having brought every delusion to a final end, there is nothing to prevent one from fully gaining the dharma-body, the whole substance already exactly buddhahood.

And the perfect interfusion, in turn, does not obstruct the graduated stages — so, even having fully gained the dharma-body, there is nothing to prevent removing subtler delusions still further, passing through the remaining fruits, and going on to become the ultimate king of treasures. This sūtra says, further on: "in principle, awakening is sudden, and, riding on that awakening, delusion dissolves all at once; but in actual practice, nothing is removed all at once — it is exhausted only stage by stage" — clear proof of this very point. So it is that, though one still passes through the asaṃkhyeyas afterward, to accomplish the ultimate fruit of buddhahood, this poses no obstruction whatsoever to having already gained the dharma-body beforehand, without passing through them at all. How much more so, given that cultivation after a perfect, sudden awakening is buddhahood in every single thought — even while going on to sever the pervasive delusions, it already outpaces the provisional, gradual practitioner by a ratio of a single day to a whole kalpa.

The five turbidities: first, the turbidity of the aeon — it has no separate substance of its own; it is exactly the convergence of the four turbidities below. It belongs to the declining aeon, when human life-span has fallen to twenty thousand years and beings already possess the four turbidities below.

Second, the turbidity of views — its substance is the five sharp fetters, and every view blazing fiercely is its characteristic. The five sharp fetters are five kinds of false view: first, the view of self — clinging to the body as self, falsely giving rise to craving and attachment, failing to realize that the four elements' provisional combination is, in every case, impermanent. Second, extreme views — clinging to existence or to emptiness, failing to realize that a one-sided understanding has already lost the middle way. Third, clinging to precepts and rituals as such — mistaking what is not a true cause for a true cause, cultivating every kind of austerity, failing to realize that steaming sand to make rice can never succeed, however many kalpas of dust pass by. Fourth, clinging to views as such — mistaking what is not a true fruit for a true fruit, claiming realization before it is actually reached, failing to realize that, within the tainted realm, nothing is ever truly ultimate. Fifth, wrong view — denying cause and effect altogether, falling into a reckless, nihilistic emptiness, failing to realize that this poison, once mixed into the mind, misleads both oneself and others. These five kinds of false view cloud and confuse one's own nature — hence the turbidity of views.

Third, the turbidity of affliction — its substance is the five dull fetters, and the three calamities called down by them are its characteristic. First, greed — craving toward agreeable circumstances. Second, anger — hatred toward disagreeable circumstances. Third, delusion — confusion toward neutral circumstances, neither agreeable nor disagreeable, unable to perceive clearly. Fourth, arrogance — a haughty mind toward other beings, unable to remain humble. Fifth, doubt — a wavering mind toward wholesome teachings, unable to reach a settled judgment. These five kinds of deluded mental state disturb and afflict, cloud and confuse one's own nature — hence the turbidity of affliction.

Fourth, the turbidity of beings — its substance is grasping the five aggregates together with view and conceit; a debased, ignoble name is its characteristic. Sentient beings accumulate the five aggregates into a body: the outward body, the provisional combination of the four elements, belongs to the aggregate of form; the inward mind's first five consciousnesses, receiving the objects of the five sense-fields, form the aggregate of sensation; the sixth consciousness imagining its object and casting off an impression forms the aggregate of perception; the seventh consciousness's constant, deliberating thought, moment following moment like a rushing torrent, forms the aggregate of formations. The eighth consciousness holds together breath, warmth, and life-span throughout a single lifetime: while consciousness remains within the body, there is warmth; once consciousness leaves, a cold sensation arises and life is cut off — this consciousness, holding the body together, forms the aggregate of consciousness. This is what is meant by "grasping the five aggregates." "View" is the delusion that willfully posits a ruling self, taken as "the self that sees"; "conceit" is the innate delusion positing a ruling self as well — karmic actions from a past life, experienced as retribution in this one, form its substance. "A debased, ignoble name" means: sentient beings' own name is base and lowly, form and mind alike degraded and impure, cycling through birth and death, beset by every kind of suffering — hence the turbidity of beings.

Fifth, the turbidity of life-span — its substance is form and mind linked and held together; the hastening of years and diminishing of life-span is its characteristic. A human life grasps the four elements as its body; the four elements are, in themselves, inanimate, yet, because the eighth consciousness's seeing-portion is present, they are made to seem aware — originally a single, luminous essence, dividing into the six sense-faculties combined together. Form and mind, linked and held together without scattering apart, are sufficient to serve as the very root of life. A human's longest life-span does not exceed a hundred years, its length utterly unfixed, birth and death unpredictable — hence "the turbidity of life-span." Together, these five make up the evil world of the five turbidities. As human life-span falls from twenty thousand years, losing one year for every hundred years that pass, down to a hundred years, the five turbidities blaze at their fiercest, and their suffering grows correspondingly severe — this is called the evil world of the five turbidities. Sentient beings, stubborn and unyielding, are hard to tame, hard to subdue; it is at exactly such a time that our Buddha appeared in the world. Ānanda, too, now vows to enter this turbid world first, to save beings — wishing to follow in his own teacher's footsteps.

"If a single sentient being has not yet become Buddha, I will not, for that reason alone, take final nirvāṇa here": this is the vast mind, sustained without interruption. He vows to save every sentient being without exception — exactly like Bodhisattva Kṣitigarbha, whose vow and practice are unshakably firm: only once every sentient being is saved will he realize bodhi; only once the hells are emptied does he vow to become a buddha. "Not taking" nirvāṇa carries two senses: first, not taking the nirvāṇa the two lesser vehicles attain alone for themselves; second, not taking even the buddhas' own ultimate nirvāṇa. That is: only once every sentient being is saved is his own vow fulfilled — and only once every sentient being is saved does he, too, realize bodhi.

The first line here praises the Buddha's virtue; the second seeks the severing of delusion. Since the Buddha possesses the virtue of wisdom, able to break through delusion down to its subtlest, deepest level, he is called "great hero"; able to uproot ignorance down to its deepest root, "great strength"; able to bring sentient beings ultimate happiness, "great kindness"; able to uproot sentient beings' suffering of birth and death, "great compassion." Hence he hopes the Buddha will, further still, carefully remove even the subtlest delusions — falling into two kinds: thought-delusions within the three realms, and ignorance beyond the three realms. Though Ānanda has awakened to and gained the dharma-body, both delusions still remain — hence he wishes to apply further effort in practice, seeking the Buddha's further instruction to remove them carefully. Ānanda, having awakened, asks how to practice — and only by the close of fascicle 8 does he sever the beginningless, accumulated, empty habituation held within the sense-faculty's own root. The text there says: "having severed the six grades of subtle affliction cultivated away within the three realms, and advanced to the second fruit, there still remain seventy-five grades unsevered." The ignorance beyond the three realms is subtler than subtle, finer than fine.

This half-verse continues the half before it: with the Buddha's great heroism and great strength removing his subtle delusions, he may naturally, and swiftly, ascend to the path of unsurpassed awakening; this relies, too, on the Buddha's great compassion, uprooting both kinds of the suffering of birth and death, so that, realizing nirvāṇa without remainder, he may ascend to the throne of unsurpassed, great awakening. He further hopes for the Buddha's great kindness, granting the joy of both adornments, merit and wisdom, so that, throughout the worlds of the ten directions, answering each capacity as it appears, he may manifest, sit at the place of practice, and teach the Dharma for the benefit of beings.

Śūnyatā translates as "emptiness"; vajra translates as "adamantine, indestructible." Ānanda has now given rise to seeking awakening above and transforming beings below, the vast mind, the foremost mind, the constant mind, and the mind free of inversion — all four minds arising together, the four great, profound vows. The conclusion: even should the very nature of emptiness itself be brought to an end, his own adamantine mind will never waver — exactly what is meant by "though empty space may come to an end, my vow is without limit." From the main body of the teaching up to this point, the exposition of the Dharma forms one complete cycle, called the cycle of refuting falseness and revealing truth.

"Hearing the Tathāgata's own subtle, wondrous voice of the Dharma just now" refers generally to the three preceding fascicles: the ten displays pointing directly to the true mind, the four categories entirely phenomenon exactly principle, the seven great elements perfectly interfused and all-pervading — all of it this same fine-grained, profound, subtle, wondrous voice of the Dharma. That mosquito's or gnat's very shape, moreover, he could not even see to begin with — how, then, could he ever hear its sound? "Awakened" refers to Ānanda and his companions, who, hearing the Buddha's wondrous instruction, suddenly awakened to the wondrous mind, all-pervading and ever-abiding — meaning, though awakened, Pūrṇa fears this awakening may not be genuine. 〖經〗世尊！若復世間，一切根塵，陰、處、界等，皆如來藏，清淨本然，云何忽生，山河大地，諸有為相？次第遷流，終而復始？

〖講〗此於萬法以起疑。前五句牒佛語，後五句舉疑情。根塵指十番顯見，根塵對辨。陰、處、界等，等六入、七大，總括上三卷之文。顯見不分科中云：「此見及緣，元是菩提，妙淨明體即如來藏。」陰等四科，科科皆云：「本如來藏。」七大文中，一一皆云：「如來藏，清淨本然。」因聞如上妙示，遂而起二疑，此於萬法起生續疑。世間法是有為，如來藏是無為，既皆無為，應當無相，云何忽生山河大地諸有為相？此疑、始之忽生。山河大地，屬無情之世界；諸有為相，屬有情之眾生，及與業果。意謂既是如來藏，清淨本然，以清淨故，不應更有染法之相，云何忽生山河大地等，染法之相耶？

次第遷流。終而復始者：此疑終之相續，次第上，若再加云何二字，其意更顯。此次第遷流，即上世界、眾生、業果等三，終而復始，即是相續之意。世界有成、住、壞、空，空已復成；眾生有生、老、病、死，死後再生，是謂終而復始。以既是本然，故不應更有生滅之相，云何次第遷流，終而復始耶？此問乃求佛為說，始生終續之詳，非直怪問其不當生也。滿慈但執空如來藏，不變之體；不達不空如來藏，隨緣之用。故佛後分始生終續，說不空藏以答之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "World-Honored One, if all the faculties, objects, aggregates, sense-fields, and elements of the world are, originally and purely, the tathāgatagarbha, why do mountains, rivers, and the great earth — all these conditioned appearances — suddenly arise, flowing onward in succession, ending only to begin again?"

Commentary: This raises a doubt about the ten thousand phenomena. The first five lines restate the Buddha's own words; the next five raise the actual doubt. "Faculties and objects" points to the ten displays of seeing, where faculty and object were examined against each other; "aggregates, sense-fields, and elements" and so on includes the six sense-entrances and the seven great elements, gathering together the whole of the three preceding fascicles. The section on seeing not divided says: "this seeing, and what it takes as condition, are, at root, bodhi's own wondrous, pure, bright substance" — that is, the tathāgatagarbha; the four categories, each in turn, say "at root, the tathāgatagarbha"; the seven great elements, each in turn, say "within the tathāgatagarbha, pure and unmoved from the start." Having heard this wondrous instruction, Pūrṇa now raises two doubts — this one concerning how the ten thousand phenomena could ever arise and continue. Worldly phenomena are conditioned; the tathāgatagarbha is unconditioned — being entirely unconditioned, it ought to have no appearance at all, so why do mountains, rivers, and the great earth, every conditioned appearance, suddenly arise from it? This doubts how it could ever begin, suddenly arising in the first place. Mountains, rivers, and the great earth belong to the insentient world; every conditioned appearance belongs to sentient beings themselves, and to karma and its fruit. The sense is: since this is the tathāgatagarbha, pure and unmoved from the start, and, being pure, there ought to be no further appearance of any defiled phenomenon at all — why, then, do mountains, rivers, and the great earth, defiled appearances all, suddenly arise from it?

"Flowing onward in succession, ending only to begin again" doubts how it could ever continue once begun — if the words "why" were added again just before "flowing onward," the sense would be even clearer. This "flowing onward in succession" refers to the world, sentient beings, and karma's own fruit, each in turn; "ending only to begin again" means exactly this same continuity. The world passes through formation, abiding, decay, and emptiness, and, once empty, forms again; sentient beings pass through birth, old age, sickness, and death, and, having died, are born again — this is what "ending only to begin again" means. Since this is originally so, pure and unmoved from the start, there ought to be no further appearance of arising and ceasing at all — so why does it flow onward in succession, ending only to begin again? This question asks the Buddha to explain, in detail, both how it begins to arise and how it continues once begun — not simply to object that it ought never to arise at all. Pūrṇa clings only to the "empty" tathāgatagarbha, its unchanging substance, without yet grasping the "non-empty" tathāgatagarbha, its function, following conditions — hence the Buddha, in what follows, divides this into its beginning and its continuation, answering with the teaching on the non-empty tathāgatagarbha.

〖經〗又如來說：地、水、火、風，本性圓融，周遍法界，湛然常住。世尊！若地性遍，云何容水？水性周遍，火則不生，復云何明，水火二性，俱鄰虛空，不相陵滅？世尊！地性障礙，空性虛通，云何二俱，周遍法界？而我不知，是義攸往。惟願如來，宣流大慈，開我迷雲，及諸大眾。作是語已，五體投地，欽渴如來，無上慈誨。

〖講〗此於五大以起疑。先牒佛語，空大文云：「若此虛空，性圓周遍，本不動搖，當知現前地、水、火、風，均名五大。」蓋此之本性圓融，乃圓真實，即前之性圓；此之周遍法界，乃通真實，即前之周遍；此之湛然常住，乃常真實，即前之本不動搖。既均名五大，則地、水、火、風，亦應具此三真實，何以現見，地水相陵，水火相剋，地空相礙也？

此求佛釋疑。惟願如來發宣流布大慈風，掃開我等之迷雲，令得慧日圓明，照澈本性圓融，周遍法界，湛然常住之義，而到究竟無疑惑地。作如是請法之語已，五體投拜於地，欽承渴仰如來，無上慈悲之教誨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "And the Tathāgata teaches that earth, water, fire, and wind, in their basic nature, interfuse perfectly and pervade the whole Dharma-realm, calm and ever-abiding — but World-Honored One, if the nature of earth is all-pervading, how can it make room for water? And if the nature of water is all-pervading, how could fire ever arise within it? How, further, can the natures of water and fire both pervade empty space without destroying one another? World-Honored One, the nature of earth is obstructive, while the nature of space is open and unobstructed — how can both pervade the Dharma-realm at once? I do not know where this teaching is meant to lead. I beg only that the Tathāgata, in great compassion, dispel this cloud of confusion, for me and for this whole assembly!" Having spoken these words, he prostrated himself, his five limbs touching the ground, reverently thirsting for the Tathāgata's own unsurpassed, compassionate instruction.

Commentary: This raises a doubt about the five great elements. It first restates the Buddha's own words — the passage on the great element of space says: "if this empty space is, in its nature, perfectly complete and all-pervading, never in itself unsettled or moved, then you should know that the earth, water, fire, and wind now before you are, every one, equally to be called among these same five great elements." Since they are equally called the five great elements, earth, water, fire, and wind ought equally to possess this same perfection, pervasiveness, and constancy — so why, then, does one actually observe earth encroaching on water, water overcoming fire, earth obstructing space?

This states the doubt itself. The doubt concerns the five great elements as a whole, and not seeing or consciousness, since these latter two, being formless, pose no obstruction, and so raise no doubt. The first two lines doubt whether earth and water can hold each other; the middle six lines doubt whether water and fire can hold each other, since water overcomes fire; the last four lines doubt whether earth and space can hold each other — earth's nature belonging to what has form, meaning obstruction, space's nature belonging to what has no form, meaning open passage, one open, one obstructed, their natures following entirely different courses. And Pūrṇa, of shallow wisdom, does not know where this teaching — of how the ten thousand phenomena arise and continue, and how the five great elements interfuse — is ultimately meant to lead.

This seeks the Buddha's own resolution of his doubt. He begs only that the Tathāgata send forth and spread the wind of great compassion, sweeping away the cloud of confusion, so that the sun of wisdom may shine perfectly bright, illuminating fully the meaning of nature interfusing perfectly, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, calm and ever-abiding — arriving, at last, at a place entirely free of doubt. Having spoken these words requesting the Dharma, he prostrated himself, his five limbs touching the ground, reverently receiving, thirsting after, the Tathāgata's own unsurpassed, compassionate instruction.

〖經〗爾時世尊，告富樓那，及諸會中漏盡無學，諸阿羅漢：如來今日，普為此會，宣勝義中，真勝義性。令汝會中，定性聲聞，及諸一切未得二空，回向上乘。阿羅漢等。皆獲一乘，寂滅場地，真阿練若，正修行處。汝今諦聽，當為汝說。富樓那等，欽佛法音，默然承聽。

〖講〗此標為滿慈，一類之機。此明所為之機有三：一定性，二回心，三眾等。如來自言普為者，即是以平等大慈，而說殊勝了義，不獨為滿慈一人而說也。勝義中真勝義性者。法相宗，勝義諦有四種：一、世間勝義：謂蘊、處、界等；二、道理勝義：謂苦、集、滅、道四諦；三、證得勝義：謂二空真如；四、勝義勝義：謂一真法界。此經所云：「如來藏，清淨本然。」即一真法界，不變之理體，能起隨緣之事用。前阿難執和合、因緣，是執權疑實，迷藏性不變之體；今滿慈執清淨本然，是執實難權，昧藏性隨緣之用。故佛為說後二藏，以窮生妄之深源，成礙之幽本，答萬法生續，不離性本二覺，答五大圓融，歸極三藏一心，為勝義中，真勝義性。

令汝會中，定性聲聞下，示所被之機。定性聲聞，指沉空滯寂，得少為足，鈍根阿羅漢。聲聞是阿羅漢之別名，以聞四諦聲，入涅槃道故。不肯回小向大，涉俗利生，故名定性。及諸一切，未得二空，回向上乘阿羅漢者：未得二空，是但證我空，未曾兼得法空。然雖未得法空，已能回小乘之心，向大乘之道，不甘永閉化城，願趨寶所，是為回向最上一乘，大阿羅漢。等者等在會闢支，以及有學之眾。

皆獲一乘，寂滅場地，真阿練若，正修行處。佛先許說真勝義，此許得殊勝益。一乘即上乘，乃最上一佛乘也；即法華經之大白牛車。令在會皆獲者，佛慈平等普益也。寂滅場地：即不生不滅之因地心，亦即如來密因。佛說奢摩他，令悟妙心本具圓理，十方如來，皆依此因心，而成果覺，入大寂滅海，即涅槃果海。上二句切勿作果地解，連三、四兩句皆是因心。

真阿練若：有云阿蘭若，譯為無諠雜，即寂靜處，無有喧譁雜鬧，寂靜可脩行處。若但境靜，非真寂滅場地，非真阿練若，必以本來不生滅不動搖之真心，方是寂滅場地，真阿練若，與境無干。此心即首楞嚴之定體，乃為十方婆伽梵，一路涅槃門，故曰正修行處；下文所說，三如來藏心是也。若悟此心，是為開圓解，始可起圓脩，得圓證矣。汝今諦聽，當為汝說者：佛囑以諦實而聽，望其勿再執理迷事。藏心體雖不變，用能隨緣，隨染緣則三種相續，五大相陵；隨淨緣，則滅塵合覺，故發真如，妙覺明性。此科先說隨染之用。富樓那等，欽仰佛之法音，默然承聽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: At that time the World-Honored One said to Pūrṇa, and to all those in the assembly who had exhausted every outflow, the arhats beyond further training: "Today the Tathāgata will proclaim, universally, for this whole assembly, the true, ultimate nature within the ultimate truth itself — so that those of you in this assembly who are fixed-nature śrāvakas, together with all those who have not yet attained the twofold emptiness but have turned their resolve toward the supreme vehicle, arhats and the rest alike, may all attain the one vehicle's own ground of quiescent extinction, the true, tranquil forest, the very place of correct practice. Listen closely now: I will explain this for you." Pūrṇa and the others, reverently receiving the Buddha's own voice of the Dharma, fell silent and listened closely.

Commentary: This marks out the capacity Pūrṇa belongs to, and those like him — showing three kinds of capacity being addressed: fixed-nature śrāvakas, those who have turned their minds toward the Great Vehicle, and the assembly generally. The Tathāgata's own word "universally" shows that he speaks this supreme teaching of complete meaning out of equal, great compassion, not for Pūrṇa's sake alone. As for "the true, ultimate nature within the ultimate truth itself": the Dharma-characteristics school distinguishes four kinds of ultimate truth — worldly ultimate truth, meaning the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements; rational ultimate truth, meaning the four noble truths; realized ultimate truth, meaning the true suchness of the twofold emptiness; and ultimate ultimate truth, meaning the one true Dharma-realm. What this sūtra calls "the tathāgatagarbha, pure and unmoved from the start" is exactly this one true Dharma-realm — the unchanging substance of principle, capable of giving rise to the function of phenomena, following conditions. Earlier, Ānanda clung to combination and causes-and-conditions, clinging to the provisional while doubting the real, deluded about the tathāgatagarbha's own unchanging substance; now Pūrṇa clings to "pure and unmoved from the start," clinging to the real while finding the provisional difficult, unaware of the tathāgatagarbha's own function, following conditions. Hence the Buddha now teaches the remaining two aspects of the tathāgatagarbha, to trace delusion back to its deepest source, and obstruction back to its most hidden root — answering how the ten thousand phenomena arise and continue by way of the two aspects of awakening inherent in nature itself, and answering how the five great elements interfuse by way of the ultimate convergence of the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha in a single mind: this is the true, ultimate nature within the ultimate truth itself.

"So that those of you in this assembly who are fixed-nature śrāvakas" and what follows shows the capacities being addressed. "Fixed-nature śrāvakas" refers to those sunk in emptiness, mired in stillness, satisfied with a lesser attainment — arhats of dull faculties, unwilling to turn from the Lesser Vehicle toward the Great, to engage with the world for the benefit of beings. "Together with all those who have not yet attained the twofold emptiness but have turned their resolve toward the supreme vehicle, arhats and the rest": not yet attaining the twofold emptiness means having realized only the emptiness of self, not yet also the emptiness of phenomena — yet, though not yet attaining the emptiness of phenomena, they are already able to turn the Lesser Vehicle's own mind toward the Great Vehicle's own path, unwilling to remain forever shut within the phantom city, resolved instead to press on toward the true treasure. "And the rest" includes the pratyekabuddhas present in the assembly, as well as the multitude still in training.

"May all attain the one vehicle's own ground of quiescent extinction, the true, tranquil forest, the very place of correct practice": the Buddha first promises to teach the true ultimate truth; this promises the supreme benefit gained from it. "The one vehicle" is the supreme vehicle, the single, highest buddha-vehicle — exactly the great white ox-cart of the Lotus Sūtra. That everyone in the assembly is to attain it shows the Buddha's own equal, universally benefiting compassion. "The ground of quiescent extinction" is exactly the unborn, unceasing mind at the causal stage — exactly the Tathāgata's own hidden cause. The Buddha's teaching on śamatha brings about awakening to the fact that the wondrous mind already possesses the complete principle inherently — the Tathāgatas of the ten directions all rely on this same causal-stage mind to accomplish the awakening of the fruit, entering the great ocean of quiescent extinction, the fruit-ocean of nirvāṇa. These first two lines should by no means be read as describing the level of the fruit — together with the third and fourth lines, all four describe the causal-stage mind.

"The true, tranquil forest" translates as "free of clamor and confusion," a place of stillness, without noise or disturbance, a still place fit for practice. But if this stillness refers only to an outward place, it is not the true ground of quiescent extinction, not the true, tranquil forest — only the original, unborn, unceasing, unmoving true mind itself is the true ground of quiescent extinction, the true, tranquil forest, having nothing to do with any outward place at all. This mind is exactly the very substance of Śūraṅgama samādhi, the single path through the gate of nirvāṇa for the Bhagavats of the ten directions — hence "the very place of correct practice"; what follows describes exactly the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha mind. If one awakens to this mind, this is opening perfect understanding — only then can one give rise to perfect cultivation, and attain perfect realization. "Listen closely now: I will explain this for you" — the Buddha instructs them to listen with full attention, hoping they will no longer cling to principle alone while remaining confused about actual phenomena. Though the tathāgatagarbha mind's own substance is unchanging, its function can follow conditions: following defiled conditions, the three continuities arise, and the five great elements seem to encroach on one another; following pure conditions, dust is extinguished and awareness is united, and true suchness, the wondrous, enlightened, bright nature, comes forth. This section first explains the function that follows defiled conditions. Pūrṇa and the others, reverently looking up to the Buddha's own voice of the Dharma, fell silent and listened closely.

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: The Buddha said: "Pūrṇa, as for your question — 'if originally pure, why do mountains, rivers, and the great earth suddenly arise?'"

Commentary: The Zhèngmài commentary says: this section teaches the non-empty tathāgatagarbha, to show the origin of continuity. Set against the "empty" tathāgatagarbha discussed above — which addresses the gate of mind as true suchness, gathering falseness back into truth to reveal the tathāgatagarbha mind's own unchanging substance — this addresses the gate of mind as arising and ceasing, tracing how falseness arises from truth, to reveal the tathāgatagarbha mind's own function, following conditions. This function, properly speaking, has two aspects: following defiled conditions, it gives rise to the function of the six ordinary destinies; following pure conditions, it gives rise to the function of the four sagely paths. Though the restated words are brief, the fuller meaning is necessarily still implied throughout.

Within the gate of arising and ceasing, there are two aspects, awakening and non-awakening — awakening is truth, non-awakening is falseness. "The nature of awareness" and "original enlightenment" are exactly the truth that the gate of arising and ceasing depends on. The Awakening of Faith says: "dependent on original enlightenment, non-awakening comes to be; and, further, from this non-awakening ignorance, the three subtle marks and six coarse marks arise, and so there come to be the three — world, sentient beings, and karma-fruit — suddenly arising and continuing." The Buddha now raises this same true awakening, which everything depends on, to test Pūrṇa: has he mistaken it for something else?

"The nature of awareness is inherently bright, and original enlightenment is inherently subtle and bright" is a teaching the Buddha has always given to the bodhisattvas — Pūrṇa, present in the assembly, has naturally heard it often. Hence the question: have you not always heard the Tathāgata himself proclaim this teaching? "The nature of awareness" and "original enlightenment" are, originally, one and the same true awakening: "nature" speaks in terms of the one true substance of principle; "original" expresses what is inherently, naturally possessed — neither touches on phenomena or function, nor concerns cultivation, being exactly the true source of the ten thousand phenomena. "Inherently bright" and "inherently subtle and bright" mean: still, and yet constantly illuminating; illuminating, and yet constantly still. "Subtle" is the unchanging substance, called "stillness"; "bright" is the function, following conditions, called "illumination" — and this "illumination" here is only the illuminating function inherent in principle, not yet brought about in actual fact. The Buddha raises these two phrases with a deep purpose in mind: first, to show that ignorance and the ten thousand phenomena have nothing to depend on apart from this; second, to show that stillness and illumination are already inherently possessed — how, then, could they ever need some artificially added brightness?

"Pūrṇa said: Just so, World-Honored One." "Just so" is a word of assent, meaning "yes, indeed." "I have always heard the Buddha proclaim this very meaning" — exactly the meaning of "the nature of awareness is inherently bright, and original enlightenment is inherently subtle and bright." Though Pūrṇa has indeed often heard this, what he has heard is only the words, not their meaning — as becomes clear from what follows. Master Jiaoguang notes: this question closely resembles the Buddha's very first question to Ānanda, asking what first moved him to resolve on the path — both cases drawing on something already seen or heard, to open the way into what is about to be revealed.

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: The Buddha said: "When you speak of 'awareness that is bright,' do you mean a brightness that is, in its own nature, called awareness — or an awareness that, lacking brightness, must be given brightness to become 'bright awareness'?" Pūrṇa said: "If what lacks brightness were still called awareness, then there would be nothing at all that it illuminates." The Buddha said: "But if there is nothing it illuminates, there is no 'bright awareness' either. Having an object is not itself awareness; having no object is not itself brightness — and 'no-brightness' besides is not the calm, bright nature of awareness itself. The nature of awareness is necessarily bright; mistakenly, this becomes 'awareness that is bright.'"

Commentary: Pūrṇa, foremost in teaching the Dharma, having already often heard this same meaning, must surely also have often taught it himself. The Buddha therefore asks: when you speak of "awareness that is bright," what, exactly, do you mean by it in your own mind? What follows raises both the true and the false possibility together, to test whether Pūrṇa truly recognizes the truth, or has mistaken falseness for it. The question: do you mean that this nature is, of itself, already bright, called "awareness" — inherently possessed, spiritual brightness, needing no brightness added to it at all? Or do you mean that awareness is, in itself, not bright, and brightness must be added to awareness before it can be called a "bright awareness"? Here, "inherently possessed, spiritual brightness" is true awareness, true brightness; "brightness that must be added" is false awareness, false brightness. Raising both possibilities together to test which one Pūrṇa takes and which he rejects is exactly like the earlier questioning of Ānanda — where is the mind? what is taken as the mind? — both aimed at drawing out what has been mistaken all along, so it can be refuted directly.

Here Pūrṇa, in fact, takes hold of the false option, answering: if this substance of awareness, without any brightness further added to it, is still called "awareness," then it is simply named "awareness" with, in truth, nothing at all that it illuminates! Examining these words closely, he rejects true awareness outright, and, looking closely at what he actually means, deeply clings to false awareness — this is a case of having heard the name while remaining unaware of its meaning. The Zhèngmài commentary says: this answer closely resembles Ānanda's own earlier dispute with the Buddha — "if this capacity to bring things to light is not the mind, then I have no mind at all, no different from earth or wood" — both cases drawn out by the Buddha from a long-held, mistaken attachment the speaker himself fails to recognize as mistaken. But where Ānanda's attachment was to the sixth consciousness, the deluded mind, Pūrṇa's attachment is to fundamental ignorance itself — coarse versus fine, shallow versus deep, entirely different in kind.

"The Buddha said: But if there is nothing it illuminates, there is no 'bright awareness' either" — the first two lines restate Pūrṇa's own claim; what follows refutes it. The Buddha says, as you have claimed: if nothing is added to brightness in awareness, then there is no brightness at all, only bare awareness — so, in your own understanding, something must necessarily be added to brightness in awareness before the two together can be called "bright awareness." Yet you fail entirely to realize that, the moment anything is added, both "awareness" and "brightness" are lost together! Why? Because brightness added from outside the substance itself is no longer the inherently possessed brightness — it arises at one moment and ceases at another. "Having an object is not itself awareness; having no object is not itself brightness" explains exactly why, the moment anything is added, both are lost together: if a thought arises adding something to be illuminated, this is no longer the inherently bright, true awareness; and if that thought is forgotten, with nothing added, then this awareness, too, is no longer bright at all. Because Pūrṇa has not yet awakened to the fact that true awareness already inherently possesses this wondrous brightness, he insists on adding brightness to awareness — not realizing that, once brightness is added, it exists at one moment and not at another, and can never be ever-abiding at all!

"And 'no-brightness' besides is not the calm, bright nature of awareness itself": because Pūrṇa insists on adding brightness to awareness, both "awareness" and "brightness" are lost together, falling entirely into ignorance. This resulting "no-brightness" is likewise not the true awareness — that is, the nature's own two aspects of awakening, calm and utterly still, wondrously bright; this wondrous brightness is ever-still and ever-illuminating — how could it possibly exist at one moment and not at another? The phrases "having an object is not itself awareness" and "having no object is not itself brightness" are somewhat difficult to grasp at first; let an analogy make it clear: true awareness inherently possesses this wondrous brightness, like a wish-fulfilling pearl, which inherently possesses its own light and illuminating function — the pearl and its light never separate from each other, the pearl itself already being the light, needing nothing further added to illuminate it. False awareness, by contrast, is, in its own nature, not bright at all — like an electric light bulb, shaped somewhat like the pearl, yet requiring electric current added to it before it can shine. "Having an object is not itself awareness": having something added to illuminate, it is no longer true awareness — like the light bulb, needing a switch: flipped on, it shines, and though it now shines, it is not the true wish-fulfilling pearl. "Having no object is not itself brightness": having nothing added to illuminate, there is no brightness at all — like the light bulb with its switch left off, giving no light whatsoever. These two phrases together show both "awareness" and "brightness" lost at once — the fault lying entirely in adding brightness in the first place.

This concludes by establishing the very root of falseness. "Necessarily bright" here means exactly ignorance — ignorance being the root that seals falseness in place. Most commentators read these two characters, "necessarily bright," as meaning "necessarily possessing true brightness"; but, following the passage above, where the Buddha raised true and false awareness together to test Pūrṇa, and Pūrṇa insisted that brightness must be added to awareness before it can be called "bright awareness," and the Buddha directly rebuked this insistence on adding brightness as mistaken — these two lines conclude the point. "The nature of awareness is necessarily bright; mistakenly, this becomes 'awareness that is bright'": the nature of awareness is exactly the nature's own two aspects of awakening, inherently possessing wondrous brightness, needing no brightness added to make it bright at all. Your own insistence that brightness must be added to awareness before it can be called "bright awareness" — this single thought insisting on adding brightness is exactly this "mistaken doing," doing what ought never to be done at all. It thereby turns wondrous brightness into ignorance, and true awareness into false awareness! This word "necessarily" in "necessarily bright" is exactly the same as the word "establish" in the later passage, "establishing knowing upon knowing and seeing" — one's own mind already inherently possesses true knowing and true seeing, with no need to establish any further knowing or seeing at all, hence the Buddha's own later teaching: "establishing knowing upon knowing and seeing is exactly the root of ignorance." This "necessarily bright" is likewise exactly this same root of ignorance.

The Zhèngmài commentary says: ignorance depends directly on the true mind, original enlightenment, standing alone before the nine subtle marks, three subtle marks, and six coarse marks, given the separate name "the solitary arising-mark," fundamental non-awakening, called "delusion," called "confusion." It has two capacities: first, the capacity to conceal the substance of true awareness; second, the capacity to give rise to the appearance of the ten thousand things — as becomes clear in what follows.

Question: "This arising-mark of ignorance is something even a bodhisattva of equal enlightenment has not yet fully understood — so why is it now spoken of, so accessibly, as merely 'adding brightness to awareness'?" Answer: "This delusion lies prior to even the three subtle marks, something no bodhisattva can properly know at all — only a Buddha directly perceives it. The Tathāgata, having a superior skillful means, is able to let a beginner understand it by way of inference instead. Speaking of it, provisionally, as 'adding brightness to awareness' is exactly this same mark. Without this skillful means, it would be like a mute person who has seen a thief but cannot cry out to give the alarm! Could the sovereign Dharma-king possibly leave things at that?"

Question: "If this expression is only provisional, not literally true, would it not risk misleading people?" Answer: "Far from misleading anyone, it brings great benefit besides. The Tathāgata directly perceives that a bodhisattva of equal enlightenment has exhausted every thought except this one alone — and, so long as this one thought remains, the Buddha-dharma cannot fully manifest; once this thought, too, is exhausted, one enters the fruit-ocean of wondrous enlightenment itself. Hence he lets beings of sudden capacity, simply by understanding the emptiness of phenomena, the mind purified, not a single thought arising, distantly unite with the Tathāgata's own wondrous mind of nirvāṇa — already possessing, of itself, the very substance of illumination, with no need to raise any further act of illuminating scrutiny at all; to raise such an act would be exactly the same as this very 'adding brightness to awareness' spoken of here. Master Yongjia says: 'should one so much as glance back at it, this at once produces a subject and an object.' The word 'glance' here is exactly this same word 'brightness': subject and object — originally nothing but the one true, original awakening — falsely become a brightness that illuminates and an awareness that is illuminated, and, once split into subject and object, neither is true any longer! The Buddha and the patriarchs speak with one and the same voice here, matching perfectly, like two halves of a tally — those who hope for sudden entry would do well to investigate this point closely."

Sūtra: Awareness itself has nothing that it illuminates; because of this need for brightness, an object comes to be posited. Once this object is falsely posited, it gives rise to your own false capacity to perceive it. And, in what was originally without sameness or difference, difference blazes fiercely into being; differing from that very difference which has just arisen, sameness comes to be posited as well; sameness and difference, thus brought to light, give rise, on this basis, to what is neither the same nor different.

Commentary: This shows how falseness arises dependent on truth. Ignorance is the root of falseness, and here gives rise to the first two of the three subtle marks — what follows is the subtle object itself. The first line addresses truth; the following two show falseness arising: "awareness" is the nature's own two aspects of awakening, the truth everything depends on; true awareness is not itself any object it illuminates, since it inherently possesses this wondrous brightness already, never falling into subject and object at all. Only because of this insistence on adding brightness does wondrous brightness turn into an ignorance capable of illuminating, and true awareness come to posit a false awareness that is illuminated. This is like the Awakening of Faith's own statement: "because of dependence on original enlightenment, there is non-awakening; and because of dependence on non-awakening, mind stirs — this stirring is called karma." This sūtra's own account brings out more than the treatise does: the treatise merely says mind stirs, without explaining why it stirs; here it is explained — it is precisely because brightness is added to original enlightenment that this stirring of mind is drawn forth.

"Once this object is falsely posited, it gives rise to your own false capacity to perceive it": the first half is exactly the karmic mark's own object, falsely posited; the second half is exactly the turning mark. Because the karmic mark's object has already been falsely posited, the power of ignorance, in turn, converts the inherently possessed light of wisdom into your own false capacity to see, capable of seeing. This, too, brings out more than the treatise: the treatise does not explain how dependence on stirring gives rise to a capacity to see at all — here it is explained: it is precisely because the karmic mark's own false object has already been posited that this false capacity is drawn forth.

"In what was originally without sameness or difference" means: within the karmic mark of the eighth consciousness itself. Because of this very first single thought of ignorance falsely stirring, the whole of the tathāgatagarbha's own true emptiness is transformed into a dim, obscured realm of emptiness. Emptiness is the mark of sameness; the world is the mark of difference. Since the world's own mark of difference has not yet formed, empty space's own mark of sameness has nothing to reveal it by — for it is only against difference that sameness can be revealed at all. "Difference blazes fiercely into being": this arising of difference has its cause in the false capacity — this false capacity being exactly the second subtle mark, the turning mark, the seeing-portion. Once there is a capacity to see, and yet no phenomena have yet formed, there is nothing at all to be seen — so the karmic mark itself is taken as the object seen, though the karmic mark is nothing but a single, dim, obscured emptiness, with not a single thing in it to be seen at all. Yet the seeing-portion is bent on seeing something, and, after seeing for long enough, an appearance of circumstance is finally brought forth. This is exactly what the section on seeing never truly lost describes: "this dim obscurity becomes emptiness, and, within this dim, obscured emptiness, darkness congeals into form." It is likewise exactly what the verse further on says: "through delusion, empty space comes to be; dependent on space, a world is established." It is like a person staring fixedly into empty space: staring long enough, the eyes grow strained, and flowers appear to bloom in the empty space.

"Blazing fiercely" describes a flourishing appearance: darkness has already congealed into the form of the four elements, like a fire's light flaring up out of the darkness of night, blazing forth conspicuously. Though this circumstance has now appeared, it is still entirely within the fundamental consciousness itself.

"Differing from that very difference which has just arisen, sameness comes to be posited as well": the first "different" here functions as a verb, meaning "not the same as"; the "difference" that follows is a noun, meaning the circumstance marked by difference. This says: differing from that circumstance marked by difference which has just blazed into being, and set against this world marked by difference, an emptiness marked by sameness comes to be posited in turn. "Sameness and difference, thus brought to light, give rise, on this basis, to what is neither the same nor different": these three lines should be taken as referring to sentient beings — continuing from the world's mark of difference and empty space's mark of sameness just described, this sameness and that difference, brought clearly into view against each other, give rise, on this basis, to the further circumstance of sentient beings, neither the same nor different. Sentient beings' outward forms all differ from one another, hence "neither the same" — yet their capacity for awareness is, at root, one and the same, hence "nor different."

Question: "Here, empty space, the world, and sentient beings are said to be subtle circumstances — how do they differ from the coarse circumstances discussed later?" Answer: "These exist only within the fundamental consciousness itself, form congealed out of darkness, serving merely as the embryonic beginning of what will become the coarse circumstances." These three subtle marks match what was already said in fascicle 2: "this dim obscurity becomes emptiness, and, within this dim, obscured emptiness, darkness congeals into form; form mingles with false thought, and the appearance of this thought becomes a body" — and likewise the verse further on: "through delusion, empty space comes to be; dependent on space, a world is established; thought, settling, becomes a land; awareness itself becomes sentient beings." In every case, falseness arises dependent on truth, and this falseness produces emptiness, world, and sentient beings.

The Zhèngmài commentary poses a question: "Reading through the three marks explained above, in their proper sequence, one point still needs clarifying: this false brightness already takes the karmic mark as its object to be illuminated, and the turning mark also takes the karmic mark as its object to be seen — what distinguishes the two? And why does the turning mark not instead take circumstance itself as its object to be seen?" Answer: "This false brightness arises, at first, dependent on original enlightenment itself, falsely taking original enlightenment as its object to be illuminated — it never originally intended the karmic mark at all. But, given that original enlightenment is, in fact, never something that can be illuminated as an object, original enlightenment ends up, in the end, impossible to illuminate — and this false brightness merely ends up dragging the karmic mark along in its wake as a kind of object to be illuminated instead. Hence the Buddha's own words: 'awareness itself has nothing that it illuminates; because of this need for brightness, an object comes to be posited' — the point is plain enough. As for the turning mark taking the karmic mark as its object to be seen — this much is correct: the turning mark arises dependent on the karmic mark, falsely taking the karmic mark as its object to be seen — it never originally intended circumstance itself at all. But, given that the karmic mark is, in fact, never something that can be seen as an object either, the karmic mark ends up, in the end, impossible to see — and it merely ends up dragging circumstance along in its wake as a kind of object to be seen instead. Hence the Buddha's own words: 'once this object is falsely posited, it gives rise to your own false capacity to perceive it, and, in what was originally without sameness or difference, difference blazes fiercely into being' — the point becomes even clearer still." Hence, in the sūtra's own text, the single word "object" links two different "capacities" at once, one hidden above, one revealed below — like a grandfather and a grandson: how could one say there is no distinction between them? Likewise the single word "capacity" links two different "objects" — again like grandfather and grandson: how could the two possibly be confused as one and the same?

Sūtra: Stirred and disturbed in just this way, and set against each other, weariness is produced. This weariness, sustained, gives rise to dust; and, mutually entangled, a murky turbidity results. From this, the dust and toil of affliction are drawn forth. Where this settles into activity, it becomes "world"; where it settles into stillness, it becomes empty space. Empty space is "sameness"; world is "difference." And that which is neither the same nor different is the true realm of conditioned phenomena.

Commentary: "Stirred and disturbed in just this way" points back to what has just been described — the mark of circumstance, arising from nothing into being, sameness posited against difference, from which emptiness and world suddenly arose; and further, from sameness and difference brought to light against each other, sentient beings, neither the same nor different, arose in turn as well — in just this way, within the ocean of the storehouse-consciousness, the wind of circumstance stirs it into agitation. "Set against each other" means mutually opposed: the false circumstance drawing forth the false mind serves as its condition, this mark of circumstance becoming the image that gives rise to the seven evolving consciousnesses.

"Weariness is produced" means: the seventh consciousness is drawn forth, first raising the mental factor of discernment, distinguishing defiled from pure with respect to its object, clinging to it as genuinely existing outside the mind, failing to realize it is nothing but one's own mind falsely manifesting — this arising of discerning wisdom turns into wearying deliberation, hence "weariness is produced." This is exactly the first coarse mark, the mark of intellect, belonging to the innate clinging to phenomena. "This weariness, sustained" means: the seventh consciousness's own constant, deliberating thought, continuing without a break, this wearying deliberation persisting over time. This is exactly the second coarse mark, the mark of continuity, belonging to the discriminated clinging to phenomena.

"Gives rise to dust" means: the sixth consciousness, calculating and discriminating everywhere, its clinging growing ever deeper, positing "self" and "what belongs to self," giving rise to defiled, clinging, dust-laden thoughts — hence "gives rise to dust." This is exactly the third coarse mark, the mark of grasping and clinging, belonging to the innate clinging to a self. "A murky turbidity results" means: the sixth consciousness, building upon the already-inverted, clung-to mark just described, further establishes a mark of provisional names and words, following the name to cling to the mark, this inversion growing especially severe, until the very water of the mind becomes murky and unclear — hence "a murky turbidity results." This is exactly the fourth coarse mark, the mark of conceptualized names, the discriminated clinging to a self.

"From this, the dust and toil of affliction are drawn forth": "this" points, at a distance, back to ignorance, the three subtle marks, and the four coarse marks just described, delusion compounding upon delusion, falseness piled upon falseness; and, nearer at hand, to the mark of conceptualized names just described, from which every karmic action is now drawn forth — this is exactly the fifth coarse mark, the mark of initiating karma. "Dust and toil" is exactly affliction, carrying the double sense of defilement and disturbance, likened here to dust and to toil. There are eighty-four thousand of these in all; before body and speech act, they belong to delusion; once body and speech have acted, they belong to karma — and now, delusion having drawn forth action of body and speech, every kind of karmic action is created; once the karmic cause is formed, its karmic fruit inevitably follows, with no escaping it at all.

Answer: "Though the account speaks of a sequence from the very beginning, the underlying principle is, in truth, beginningless — surely it cannot mean that, before the coarse circumstance forms, there is truly no body or speech at all? This directly answers Pūrṇa's own question. The first four lines answer "why do mountains, rivers, and the great earth suddenly arise?"; the last two answer "every conditioned appearance," meaning sentient beings and karma's own fruit. Starting from "the nature of awareness is necessarily bright," the account has traced, step by step, the whole chain back to its source: dependent on the nature of awareness, ignorance falsely arose; because of this ignorance, the three subtle marks came to be; further conditioned by circumstance, the dust and toil of affliction arose; the false causes of delusion and karma having thus been formed, the suffering fruit of direct and circumstantial retribution now appears. Hence: where it settles into activity, taking on form, there is "world," mountains, rivers, and the great earth; where it settles into stillness, without form, vast and open, there is "empty space." Emptiness does not stir, hence "stillness" — and this circumstantial world of retribution never actually departs from the subtle circumstance described earlier: this empty space is exactly the actualized manifestation of that earlier mark of sameness; this world is exactly the actualized manifestation of that earlier mark of difference. Your question — why do mountains, rivers, and the great earth suddenly arise? — is answered: it is precisely from this that they arise.

"And that which is neither the same nor different is the true realm of conditioned phenomena": this answers "every conditioned appearance," likewise showing it never departs from the subtle circumstance described earlier. Taking, from within that subtle circumstance, sameness and difference brought to light against each other, giving rise to the mark that is neither the same nor different, and, from this, forming the true, conditioned phenomena of sentient beings and karma's own fruit. This direct retribution of sentient beings and karma's own fruit is likewise the actualized manifestation of that earlier subtle mark — exactly the sixth coarse mark, the mark of suffering bound by karma. Bound by karma, one falls into the five aggregates, the three realms, and the two hells, with no way to escape, never free at all. Your question — why does every conditioned appearance suddenly arise? — is answered: it is precisely from this that it arises.

Master Jiaoguang says: this account, cast as a single cycle of the ten thousand phenomena first coming to be, speaks only in terms of their sudden arising. It is also cast, with respect to the reverse order of practice — severing delusion — to display this same sequence in reverse: provisionally stating the sequence of first arising lets one observe the sequence of forward arising, making awakening easier, without falling into confusion; while understanding the sequence of reverse severing makes cultivation and realization easier, without falling into disorder. One should also understand that, from ignorance down to the coarse circumstance, the sūtra's own account repeatedly uses "capacity" and "object," each linked in turn to the one before and after, so that one may recognize capacity and object as exactly the starting point from which the ten thousand things arise. Once a practitioner has clearly distinguished true from false, a single thought, cutting off capacity and object all at once, is enough to hold the ten thousand things firmly in check, and sit back down into what is pure and unmoved from the start — exactly what is meant by "simply leave deluded conditions behind, and one is, just as one is, already a buddha."

"And, in the karma thus received, following it upward or downward accordingly": discussing the karmic retribution received by the four kinds of birth, there is, in truth, no separate ruling agent behind it at all — "following" simply means "in accordance with": the karmic fruit that answers is always in accordance with its karmic cause; wholesome karma rises upward, unwholesome karma sinks downward. Emotion, thought, union, and separation each carry their own share of wholesome or unwholesome, and so the fruit of rising or sinking necessarily follows its wholesome or unwholesome cause. The last two lines involve both a distant and a near cause and condition: distantly, ignorance is the cause, karmic consciousness the condition; nearer at hand, emotion, thought, union, and separation are the cause, and parents, one's own karma, warmth, and moisture the condition. The four kinds of birth turning one into another, rising and sinking through the three realms, birth and death binding on and on, the wheel turning without rest — hence sentient beings continue on without end.

Sūtra: Pūrṇa, thought and desire bind together, and desire cannot be severed — and so, in every world, parents and children go on being born from one another without cease; this has craving and desire as its root. Craving and desire nourish each other, and craving cannot be stopped — and so egg-born, transformation-born, moisture-born, and womb-born beings, throughout the world, devour one another according to their relative strength; this has killing and desire as its root. Because a person eats a sheep, the sheep is reborn a person, and the person, in turn, is reborn a sheep. And so it goes, on through the ten kinds of birth as well: dying and dying again, being born and born again, devouring one another in turn — unwholesome karma arising together with them, without end, into the boundless future. This has stealing and greed as its root.

Commentary: This shows that karma and its fruit are rooted in one's own mind's own greed. Deluded craving stands foremost among the afflictions, and is likewise the very root of every karmic action; though the scope of craving is broad, desire for sex is foremost among them. "Thought and desire bind together, and desire cannot be severed": this means that sameness in thought becoming attraction is exactly the cause of this binding — why? Because, once thought and attraction run deep, sticking together like glue and lacquer, unable to let go, they bind the condition of rebirth ever more tightly; and so, throughout the world, parents and children go on being born from one another, generation after generation without cease — all of this rooted in desire and craving. "Desire and craving" refers exactly to the thought and attraction present at the moment of rebirth: sameness in thought becomes attraction; attraction gives rise to desire; and desire brings about the receiving of a body. Every person in the whole world is exactly the same.

Wherever there is craving and attachment, there must be body and life; and, having body and life, one must equally seek to nourish it. Each and every being wishes to nourish its own body and life, and so this craving cannot be stopped — knowing only to nourish itself, giving no thought to cruelty, it inevitably kills other living creatures, feeding on their very flesh. And so, throughout the world, every kind of the four births, according to its own strength, bullies the weak — the weak becoming food for the strong: large birds eat small birds, large beasts eat small beasts, large fish eat small fish, large insects eat small insects. "Devour one another according to their relative strength" — as when, in summer, a snake eats a mouse, and, in winter, a mouse eats a snake. All of this is rooted in killing and greed.

Wenling says: "taking what is not given, and taking covertly, are both theft." Hence a person eating a sheep is "taking what is not given"; the sheep dying to be reborn a person, and devouring one another in turn, is "taking covertly" — both belonging to stealing and greed. "Because a person eats a sheep": continuing from craving nourishment above, a person eats a sheep — but does the sheep ever willingly consent to be eaten by that person? Once its old karmic debt is exhausted, it dies and is reborn a person; and does the person who ate the sheep remain a person life after life forever? Once this unwholesome karma is formed, that person, too, dies and is reborn a sheep — person and sheep exchanging places, repaying this old debt, devouring one another in turn — exactly as the saying goes, "eat eight ounces of another's flesh, and you will repay half a pound in return." And this holds not only between person and sheep, but on through the ten kinds of birth as well: dying and dying again, being born and born again, retaliating back and forth, devouring one another in turn. This unwholesome karma, born together with life itself, meeting its rival again and again, without end, into the boundless future, retaliation never ceasing — all of this is rooted in stealing and greed.

The Zhèngmài commentary poses a question: "Ordinary worldly teaching, discussing killing, treats killing a person out of rage or conflict as the gravest offense; discussing theft, treats robbing another of their property or life as the gravest offense — with eating meat not counted among these at all, which seems a reasonable weighing of severity. Why, then, does this sūtra dwell precisely on this lighter matter, while apparently leaving aside the graver ones?" Answer: "This carries two senses. First, using the lighter case to imply the graver one: ordinary worldly teaching in this land is chiefly concerned with stopping present disorder and providing for the people's welfare, and so forbids only actual, present violence, without prohibiting the eating of meat. This sūtra, however, aims to cut off birth and death entirely, and so must sever the very condition of birth itself — hence it extends even to prohibiting the eating of meat, cutting off everything along the way. If even this lighter matter is understood to already be a condition of birth and death, the graver matters go without saying. Moreover, true compassion, being universal and equal, regards every taking of life alike — what room is there for degrees of severity at all? Second, cutting off the root to stop the branch: nearly every instance of killing and theft, traced back to its deepest root, arises from eating meat in the first place. When the eighty thousand members of the Śākya clan met their slaughter at Virūḍhaka's hands, ordinary people knew only the immediate cause — insults exchanged — without knowing the more distant cause: a grudge incurred long before, from eating fish. This is exactly why lasting peace never holds in this land — for, in times of peace, people indulge freely in eating meat; those who enjoy such fortune eventually see their fortune end and calamity begin, while the animals who are owed their debt of retribution, once that debt is repaid by being reborn human, still carry the grudge of having been killed — and so disorder inevitably follows, even to the killing of countless people. This is exactly why the Buddha prohibits eating meat — a matter of the sage's own deep, far-reaching wisdom, uprooting the cause and stopping it at its very source. The sūtra says elsewhere: 'if the world wishes to be free of the calamity of war, sentient beings must stop eating meat.' Followers of other teachings, who have not yet come to believe and understand this, should not lightly disparage it!"

Chinese Source

〖經〗富樓那，如是三種，顛倒相續，皆是覺明，明了知性，因了發相，從妄見生，山河大地；諸有為相，次第遷流，因此虛妄，終而復始。

〖講〗此文躡前三種相續，結答相續無別法，即續彼三種忽生；又躡前三種忽生，結答忽生無別法，即生此三種相續，意乃雙關。但忽生中，先生虛空，次世界，後眾生，未曾明言業果，乃合業果於眾生中，具足三種。相續中明言世界、眾生、業果，未曾明言虛空，乃合虛空於世界中，亦全無缺漏也。無論忽生相續，皆不出世界、眾生、業果三法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Pūrṇa, these three kinds of inverted continuity are, every one, nothing but this same "awareness made bright," this nature of clear knowing; and, because of this clear knowing, marks are brought forth, and, from this false seeing, mountains, rivers, and the great earth arise; every conditioned appearance flows onward in succession — and, because of this very falseness, ending only to begin again.

Commentary: This passage follows on from the three kinds of continuity just described, concluding that continuity is nothing apart from these three — continuing exactly the same three that first suddenly arose; and it likewise follows on from the three kinds of sudden arising, concluding that sudden arising is nothing apart from these three either — giving rise to exactly these same three continuities. The meaning is thus doubled, pointing both ways at once. But note: in the account of sudden arising, empty space arose first, then the world, then sentient beings, without karma-fruit ever being explicitly mentioned — karma-fruit being folded into the account of sentient beings, so that all three are, in fact, fully present there. In the account of continuity, world, sentient beings, and karma-fruit are all explicitly mentioned, without empty space ever being explicitly mentioned — empty space being folded into the account of the world, so that nothing at all is actually left out here either. Whether sudden arising or continuity, in every case, nothing lies beyond these same three: world, sentient beings, and karma-fruit.

The first two lines restate the continuity described above; the next four trace the false cause back to its source; the five lines beginning with "mountains, rivers" explain the actualized manifestation of sudden arising and continuity together. The Buddha, addressing Pūrṇa, says: these three continuities just described — world, sentient beings, and karma-fruit — are exactly this same mark of inversion, arising from truth as falseness. Hence: "every one, nothing but this same 'awareness made bright'" — that is, arising from true awareness as false brightness; "this nature of clear knowing" points to the ignorance within this false brightness, this nature of clear knowing being exactly this falsely-possessed capacity to know. Dependent on this falsely-knowing ignorance, the three marks — karmic, turning, and manifesting — are brought forth: these two phrases are exactly "ignorance, non-awakening, gives rise to the three subtle marks."

"From this false seeing" means: proceeding from the subtle toward the coarse, forming coarse delusion and coarse circumstance — this false seeing being exactly the coarse delusion, and "mountains, rivers" and what follows being exactly the coarse circumstance. The word "arise" links both what comes before and after: linked to what precedes, coarse delusion arises; linked to what follows, circumstance arises dependent on this delusion. Your question — why do mountains, rivers, and the great earth, every conditioned appearance, come to arise? — is answered: it is precisely from this that they arise. "Flows onward in succession": the world passes through formation, abiding, decay, and emptiness; sentient beings transform into one another; karma-fruit repays back and forth between beings — all of this likewise arising from this same falseness, falsely continuing on, ending only to begin again, circling round and round without ever coming to rest. "Because of this very falseness": "this" points back to "awareness made bright, this nature of clear knowing" — meaning that not only do these three continuities arise from this same source, but your further question — why does it flow onward in succession, ending only to begin again? — is likewise answered: it is precisely because of this same falseness that it continues on.

Reading note: The direct answer to Pūrṇa's first question — how the ten thousand phenomena arise and continue — is now complete, tracing the whole chain from a single mistaken thought ("the nature of awareness is necessarily bright") through the three subtle and six coarse marks to the three continuities of world, sentient beings, and karma. Fascicle 9 closes here. Fascicle 10 takes up Pūrṇa's second question — how the five great elements can each pervade the whole Dharma-realm without destroying one another — and, with it, the teaching of the third and final aspect of the tathāgatagarbha: empty and non-empty at once.
