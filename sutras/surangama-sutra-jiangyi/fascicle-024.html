Five Layers, One Falseness

The last of the fifty demonic states — ten deviant final attainments awaiting even an advanced practitioner at the very threshold of the aggregate of consciousness — closes the entire teaching on the five aggregates, and with it, the path opens straight through to wondrous enlightenment itself. Ānanda then asks one final question: what, exactly, is the false thought underlying each of the five aggregates, and in what order are they removed? The Buddha's answer — five layers of false thought, nested one inside the next, all ultimately a single falseness with no real cause at all — closes the sūtra's entire teaching on cultivation. A closing comparison of merit, and the assembly's joyful departure, brings Master Yuanying's twenty-four-fascicle commentary, and this translation of it, to an end.

About This Fascicle

This is the final fascicle of Master Yuanying's twenty-four-fascicle commentary. It completes the fifty states of demonic interference with the aggregate of consciousness's own ten deviant final attainments — where the danger is no longer wild philosophy but an advanced practitioner mistaking one genuine, partial insight for the completed path itself, and stopping there. It then follows the sūtra through to its very close: the path from consciousness exhausted straight through to wondrous enlightenment, Ānanda's final question about the five layers of false thought underlying all five aggregates and the order in which they are removed, and the sūtra's own closing comparison of merit between material giving and teaching even a single person this understanding. The companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 10 already translates this same closing material in condensed form, and this fascicle reuses that project's exact wording for the sūtra passages themselves. What this fascicle adds, for the first time, is Yuanying's own full clause-by-clause commentary throughout, together with the complete sūtra text quoted in full.

The Aggregate of Consciousness: Its Boundary 辛五 識陰魔相 · 癸初 始修未破區宇

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難！彼善男子，修三摩地，行陰盡者，諸世間性，幽清擾動，同分生機，倏然隳裂，沉細綱紐，補特伽羅，酬業深脈，感應懸絕。

〖講〗此通明識陰，始終之相。彼善男子：即是行陰已盡之人，或始終不起狂解，或妄念便能覺知，常住圓定，故仍稱修三摩地；此中望行陰為終修，望識陰為始修也。行陰盡者，諸世間性，即行陰生滅性也；諸世間一切有為之法，無非流變生滅故。幽清擾動，同分生機者：觀幽隱輕清，擾動之根元，即十二類生。同分生機，即同生基也。基：表生之處；機：明動之始；其意則一而已。倏然隳裂，沉細綱紐者：倏然即忽然也。隳壞裂破，沉細綱紐，沉是深沉，細即微細，綱為網上之大綱，紐為衣中之紐扣；此狀生機之綱紐，為網衣之樞要也。功夫至此，忽然隳裂，沉細綱紐，則補特伽羅，此云數取趣，即中有身，眾生由此，能數數取著於諸趣，而受生也。且受生，所以酬答宿業，而酬業之深潛脈絡，即是行陰，為生滅根元也。感應懸絕者：感應即因果也，而言懸絕者，以行陰既盡，深潛脈絡已斷，因亡果喪，不復受生，故云懸絕。

〖經〗於涅槃天，將大明悟，如雞後鳴，瞻顧東方，已有精色。六根虛靜，無復馳逸，內外湛明，入無所入。深達十方，十二種類，受命元由；觀由執元，諸類不召，於十方界，已獲其同；精色不沉，發現幽秘。此則名為，識陰區宇。

〖講〗於涅槃性天，為五陰所覆，輪轉生死，昏如長夜，將大明悟。言將者，謂纔有明悟之前兆耳。故下以喻顯之。前三陰盡，如雞初、二、三鳴，未色見精。今行陰既盡，如雞最後啼鳴；瞻顧性天，亦若東方已有精明之色，但未大明白，以其尚為識陰所覆故。工夫至此，行陰已盡，識陰已現時也。六根虛靜，無復馳逸四句，指掌疏約前四陰盡相言之。以受盡故虛，無領納故；想盡故靜，離分別故；行盡故無復馳逸，絕遷流故。內之六性，既已湛然明淨，況夫色陰先盡，而外之六塵，豈更昏擾乎？如是則內根外境，同歸湛明之一體也。既唯一體，內外相盡，故曰：入無所入。《正脈》云：亦即漸次中，塵既不緣，根無所偶，反流全一，六用不行之時也。內外湛明：言根塵化為一味，湛明之境。入無所入者：謂初心亡所，故言入流；既盡根塵，更何所入？深達十方，十二種類，受命元由者：至此根塵既銷，綱紐自破，則第八識，從此顯現。環師謂：受命元由，即是識陰，然亦即是類生別相；所謂各命由緒，顯異前之總相而見，故曰深達也。十方世界，十二種類，眾生投胎，受命根本元由，若知此是無明幻力所起，離於依他起性，則識陰自可頓超。其奈行人至此，觀此受命元由，執此為本元真心，故曰觀由執元，遂致識陰終不可破。雖不可破，而行陰已盡，果報不牽，故曰諸類不召，則盡十二類，皆不能牽引受生矣。唯見十方世界，同一識性，同是唯識，一體變現，更無別法之可得，故曰已獲其同。此即已得六銷，猶未亡一。精色不沉，發現幽秘者：謂識精元明，常得現前，如初見性天，已有精明之色，不復更沉。雖未大明，然已東方發白，顯現幽暗隱秘之處，言其具見暗中之物也。即是行盡識現，如脫外衣，方見最內貼體汗衫，此則名為識陰，未破之區宇。

〖經〗若於群召，已獲同中，銷磨六門，合開成就，見、聞通鄰，互用清淨。十方世界，及與身心，如吠琉璃，內外明徹，名識陰盡。是人則能，超越命濁，觀其所由，罔象虛無，顛倒妄想，以為其本。

〖講〗群召，謂群生果報，皆能牽召受生，故曰群召。已獲其同者：行陰已破，諸類不召，識陰現前，故觀十方唯識，一體變現，更無別法，是謂已獲同中，同即一體也。銷磨六門，合開成就者：即於同中，加功用行，銷鎔磨煉，六根門頭，合之則一，開之則六。以六根為一根用，以眼能見，耳、鼻、舌、身、意皆能見，名合成就；以一根為六根用，以眼不獨見色，而能聞、嗅、嘗、觸、覺、知，名為開成就；一根如是，根根合開皆然。見聞通鄰，互用清淨者：《正脈》云：不唯情界脫纏，亦以情器交徹也。見聞者：略舉六根之二；通鄰者：其結已解，其體不隔也；互用者：體既無隔，用可互通也；謂眼家，作耳家佛事等。以其回脫浮塵、勝義，二種根結，無障無礙，故曰清淨。此即情界脫纏。下則情器交徹。十方世界，此器世界；及與身心，此有情世界也。外器內根，全是自己心光，世界身心，蕩然不復更有，故云如吠琉璃，內外明徹。即前所謂山河大地，應念化成無上知覺是也。至此一念不生，不生不滅，與生滅和合之相滅，故云名識陰盡。按耳圓通，此當寂滅現前。《指掌疏》云：前於空所空滅時，宛爾有箇能滅心，即是第八本識，然此乃是對生言滅，滅非真滅，正是識陰，今於識陰盡處，對生言滅之滅，亦復俱滅，故是寂滅現前時也。是人則能超越命濁：此經命濁，以一性六用，同異相織為體，如前命濁文云：汝等見聞，元無異性一性也，眾塵隔越，無狀異生六用也。性中相知，用中相背，同異失準，相織妄成，名為命濁。今以合開成就，一六俱亡，無復相織，故能超越。至此反觀識陰之所由來，莫非幻妄，非有謂之罔；非無謂之象；體性空寂，故名虛無；迷背真性，名為顛倒；元無別法，惟此妄想，以為識陰之根本耳。但能達妄本空，則妄想尚無，識陰何有？故曰識陰虛妄，本如來藏也。初具示始終竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, once this good person, cultivating samādhi, has exhausted the aggregate of formations, the hidden, subtle, restless hinge underlying every being's shared basis of life suddenly splits apart and falls away — the deep, fine web of thread by which each individual being responds to and receives the fruit of its own karma is abruptly severed."

Commentary: This passage, taken as a whole, makes plain the aggregate of consciousness's own mark, from beginning to end. "This good person" refers precisely to the one who has already exhausted the aggregate of formations — whether never once giving rise, from beginning to end, to any wild interpretation, or, whenever a false thought arose, always able at once to recognize it, this samādhi remaining constant throughout — and so is still called one "cultivating this samādhi"; with respect to formations, this marks the end of that practice, and, with respect to consciousness, the beginning of this next one.

"The aggregate of formations exhausted, every worldly nature" refers precisely to this aggregate of formations' own nature of arising and ceasing — every conditioned phenomenon throughout the world being nothing but this same flux, this same arising and ceasing. "The hidden, subtle, restless hinge underlying every being's shared basis of life" refers to observing this hidden, fine, restless very root of movement — that is, precisely these twelve kinds of birth. This "shared basis of life" is exactly this same shared foundation of birth described earlier: "basis" indicating the very place where birth occurs; "hinge" making plain the very starting point of movement — the two meanings, in the end, one and the same. "Suddenly splits apart and falls away, this deep, fine web of thread" means: "suddenly" here means all at once; splitting apart and falling away this deep, fine web of thread — "deep" meaning profoundly hidden, "fine" meaning subtle — "web" refers to the main cord of a net, "thread" to the fastening button of a garment; this describes this very hinge of life's own mechanism as the pivotal, essential point of this net-like garment.

This effort, reaching this point, suddenly splits apart and falls away this deep, fine web of thread — and so this pudgala, translated "one who repeatedly takes hold of a destiny," precisely this intermediate-state body, by which sentient beings are able, again and again, to cling and attach themselves to one destiny after another, receiving birth thereby. And this receiving of birth is precisely how one repays one's own former karma — and the deep, hidden vein along which this karma is repaid is precisely the aggregate of formations itself, this very root of arising and ceasing. "Response and correspondence abruptly severed" means: response and correspondence are precisely cause and effect themselves; and to say "abruptly severed" means that, the aggregate of formations now exhausted, this deep, hidden vein has already been cut off, cause gone and fruit lost, no longer receiving birth at all — hence, "abruptly severed."

Sūtra: "At this point, on the very verge of nirvāṇa's own dawning, it is like the moment just before daybreak: looking toward the east, one already glimpses the first traces of color. The six sense-faculties become empty and still, no longer racing outward; both within and without, everything becomes translucent, entering a state where there is nothing further left to enter, and one comes to understand deeply, throughout the ten directions, the root cause behind all twelve categories of birth receiving their particular span of life."

Commentary: Within this heavenly realm of nirvāṇa's own nature, covered over by the five aggregates, turning within birth and death, as dim as an endless night, one now stands on the very verge of a great awakening dawning. To speak of standing "on the verge" means only that the very first sign of this awakening has just now appeared — hence, what follows illustrates this with a metaphor. The three prior aggregates exhausted resemble a rooster's first, second, and third crow, when no color can yet be seen at all; now, with the aggregate of formations, too, exhausted, this resembles the rooster's very last crow — looking toward this heavenly realm of one's own nature is likewise as though the east already showed the very first traces of color, though not yet fully bright, since it remains still covered over by the aggregate of consciousness. This effort, reaching this point, is exactly the moment the aggregate of formations has been exhausted and the aggregate of consciousness has just now appeared.

"The six sense-faculties become empty and still, no longer racing outward" — these four clauses, as the Zhizhang Commentary explains, describe, together, the mark of the four prior aggregates already exhausted: sensation exhausted, hence empty, since there is no more receiving and taking in; perception exhausted, hence still, since there is no more discriminating; formations exhausted, hence no longer racing outward, since this flowing movement is entirely cut off. These six natures within, already translucent, bright, and pure, and, moreover, the aggregate of form already exhausted before them — how, then, could the six kinds of dust-object without still cause any dimness or disturbance at all? And so both the faculty within and the object without alike return to this same one substance, translucent and bright. Being, then, this single substance alone, both inner and outer marks are exhausted — hence, "entering a state where there is nothing further left to enter." As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this is precisely the moment, within the three gradual stages, when the sense-object no longer serves as any condition, and the faculty has nothing left to pair with, this reversed flow returning entirely to unity, these six functions no longer operating at all. "Both within and without, everything translucent" means that faculty and object have fused into this single flavor, this translucent, bright state. "Entering a state where there is nothing further left to enter" means: at the very outset, the object was forgotten — hence, "entering the stream"; now, faculty and object both alike exhausted, what further "entering" could remain at all?

"One comes to understand deeply, throughout the ten directions, the root cause behind all twelve categories of birth receiving their particular span of life" means: reaching this point, faculty and object both dissolved, this web of thread broken of itself, the eighth consciousness thereby becomes manifest. As Master Huan puts it: "the root cause behind receiving their span of life" is precisely the aggregate of consciousness itself, and, moreover, precisely the particular mark distinguishing each kind of birth — that is, this same "individual thread of destiny" spoken of earlier, shown here in distinction from the general mark seen before it — hence, "understand deeply."

Throughout the worlds of the ten directions, these twelve kinds of sentient being taking up rebirth — should one recognize this very root cause behind receiving their span of life to be nothing but the illusory power of ignorance itself, departed from this nature of dependent arising, the aggregate of consciousness could, thereby, be transcended all at once. Yet, unable to help it, the practitioner, reaching this point, observing this very root cause behind receiving life, clings to it instead as the very root, true mind itself — hence, "observing this root cause, they cling to it as the very root" — and so the aggregate of consciousness, in the end, cannot be broken through at this stage. Though it cannot yet be broken through, still, the aggregate of formations already exhausted, karmic fruit no longer draws them onward — hence, "none of these kinds any longer summon them," meaning that none of these twelve kinds can any longer draw them onward into further birth at all. They see only that the worlds of the ten directions share, alike, this single nature of consciousness, alike nothing but consciousness-only, this one substance manifesting through transformation, with no further, separate phenomenon to be found at all — hence, "already attaining this same unity." This is precisely having already dissolved these six, yet not yet forgotten this one remaining unity.

"This refined color no longer sinks away, revealing what lies hidden and secret" means: this essential, original brightness of consciousness constantly manifests before them, exactly as, upon first glimpsing this heavenly realm of one's own nature, this refined, bright color has already appeared, no longer sinking away as before. Though not yet fully bright, still, the east has already begun to whiten, revealing what lies hidden, dark, and secret — meaning that they are already able to see objects within darkness fully. This is exactly formations exhausted and consciousness manifest, like removing an outer garment to reveal, at last, the innermost undershirt clinging to the very skin. This is called the boundary of the aggregate of consciousness not yet broken through.

Sūtra: "Once, amid this shared origin common to every summoned kind of being, the six sense-gates are ground down until opening and closing become one and the same, seeing and hearing interchange and interpenetrate in perfect purity, and the worlds of the ten directions, together with one's own body and mind, become like clear glass, transparent within and without — this is called the exhaustion of the aggregate of consciousness, and such a person can transcend the turbidity of life-span itself. Examining its cause, this aggregate is rooted in formless, empty, inverted false thought."

Commentary: "Every summoned kind of being" refers to the karmic fruit of every kind of being, each able to draw and summon them into further birth — hence, "summoned." "This shared origin already attained" means: the aggregate of formations already broken through, none of these kinds any longer summoning them, the aggregate of consciousness now manifest before them, and so they observe that the ten directions are nothing but consciousness-only, this one substance manifesting through transformation, with no further, separate phenomenon at all — this is what is meant by "amid this shared origin already attained," "shared" here meaning this same single substance.

"The six sense-gates are ground down until opening and closing become one and the same" means: within this very unity, applying further diligent effort, grinding and refining these six sense-gates until, closed together, they become one, and, opened apart, they become six. Taking these six faculties as functioning through a single faculty — the eye able to see, and the ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind able to see as well — this is named "closed together, accomplished"; taking a single faculty as functioning through all six — the eye not only seeing form, but able to hear, smell, taste, touch, feel, and know as well — this is named "opened apart, accomplished." As with one faculty, so with every other, each equally able to close together or open apart.

"Seeing and hearing interchange and interpenetrate, functioning together in perfect purity" — as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this is not merely the realm of sentient feeling freed from entanglement, but the realm of sentient feeling and the realm of physical objects, too, interpenetrating each other fully. "Seeing and hearing" briefly names two of the six faculties; "interchange and interpenetrate" means that their own knot has already been untied, their own substance no longer separated; "functioning together" means that, their substance no longer separated, their function, too, can interchange freely — meaning, for instance, that the eye's own faculty performs the ear's own function, and so on. Since they have, thereby, cast off the two knots of the coarse and the essential sense-faculty, without any obstruction whatsoever, this is called "pure." This is precisely the realm of sentient feeling freed from entanglement.

What follows concerns the realm of sentient feeling and physical objects interpenetrating each other. "The worlds of the ten directions" refers to this world of physical objects; "together with one's own body and mind" refers to this world of sentient feeling. The outer world of objects and the inner faculties alike are entirely this same light of one's own mind; world, body, and mind alike vanish entirely, with nothing further remaining at all — hence, "like clear glass, transparent within and without." This is precisely what was meant earlier: mountains, rivers, and the great earth transforming, the very moment this thought occurs, into unsurpassed awareness. Reaching this point, not a single thought arises, and this mark of arising-and-ceasing, merged together with what neither arises nor ceases, is itself extinguished — hence, "this is called the exhaustion of the aggregate of consciousness." Corresponding to the teaching of perfect penetration through the ear-faculty, this is precisely the moment "quiescent cessation manifests before them." As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: earlier, at the moment "even this emptiness, and what is empty, both are extinguished," there still remained, plainly, some mind capable of this very extinguishing — precisely the eighth, root consciousness itself; yet this "cessation" was spoken only in relation to "arising," not a true cessation at all, being, in fact, precisely the aggregate of consciousness itself. Now, at the very point the aggregate of consciousness is exhausted, even this same "cessation," spoken only in relation to "arising," is itself, together with that arising, extinguished as well — and so this is precisely the moment quiescent cessation manifests before them.

"Such a person can transcend the turbidity of life-span itself": this sūtra's own turbidity of life-span takes this single nature and its six functions, sameness and difference interwoven together, as its very substance — as the earlier passage on this turbidity states: "your own seeing and hearing are, at root, without any difference in nature" — this is sameness; "yet every sense-object separates and divides them, giving rise, for no evident reason, to these different functions" — this is difference; "this nature knows itself internally, yet each function turns away from the others outwardly, sameness and difference both losing their own true measure — interwoven together, this falsely forms what is named the turbidity of life-span." Now, having accomplished this closing-together and opening-apart, both this one substance and these six functions alike are gone, no longer interwoven at all — and so one is able to transcend it.

Reaching this point, looking back to observe the very source from which the aggregate of consciousness arises, one finds it nothing but illusion and falseness throughout: not truly existent, hence called "formless"; not truly nonexistent either, hence called "an image"; its own substance and nature empty and quiescent, hence named "empty"; deluded, turning away from this true nature, named "inverted" — originally there is no separate phenomenon at all, only this false thought itself, serving as the very root of the aggregate of consciousness. One need only penetrate the fact that this falseness is, at root, empty, and this false thought itself is, then, nowhere to be found — how, then, could the aggregate of consciousness exist at all? Hence it is said: the aggregate of consciousness, false and empty, is, at root, nothing but the tathāgatagarbha itself. This closes the first section, showing this aggregate's full course from beginning to end.

The Aggregate of Consciousness: Ten Deviant Final Attainments 壬二 中間十執

At this final threshold, Yuanying stresses, the danger is no longer wild philosophical theory but something subtler still: a genuine, advanced practitioner mistaking one specific insight — real as far as it goes — for the completed path itself, and stopping there. Each follows the same three-part pattern: the aggregate of formations exhausted and consciousness manifest (行盡識現); a wrong interpretation resulting in fault (謬解成咎); and the position named as a warning (出名警覺). Each is traced to a specific school of non-Buddhist practice, or, in the last two cases, to a permanently fixed śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who never turns toward the greater vehicle at all.

Chinese Source — Position 1: Taking a "Cause" as the Source 因所因執

〖經〗阿難當知：是善男子，窮諸行空，於識還元，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。

〖講〗此示識陰十種邪執，第一因所因執也。先教以當知，以示警覺之意。是修圓通之善男子，窮諸行空者；謂研窮行陰，而至於空，即行陰已盡。於識還元者：指行盡識現，如前云：熠熠元性，性入元澄，一澄元習，如波瀾滅，化為澄水是也。於八識反本還元矣！已滅七識遷流生滅之性，而於識陰，寂滅之性，精妙未圓。《指掌疏》云：寂即常德，離生死故；滅即淨德，滅煩惱故；精即我德，有真體故；妙即樂德，具神用故。總為一涅槃，蓋必透過識陰，始得圓滿也。《寶鏡疏》云：而於識陰，寂滅之性，雖已現前，但其真精妙明，尚未發光，猶為識陰所覆，未能圓照於法界也。《正脈疏》云：所言寂滅者，即圓通中解結，末後之滅結也。不帶纖毫生滅曰精，惟餘一味寂常曰妙，始是純真性體。此而未圓，正明識陰未破，尚為所覆，似一似常未精未妙也。

〖經〗能令己身，根隔合開，亦與十方，諸類通覺，覺知通吻，能入圓元。若於所歸，立真常因，生勝解者，是人則墮，因所因執，娑毗迦羅，所歸冥諦，成其伴侶，迷佛菩提，亡失知見。

〖講〗《正脈疏》云：能令下，先舉起執之由。大凡起執，必睹大定中，殊勝之象，以發端耳。首二句：能令自己之身，六根隔礙銷鎔，合六根為一根之用，開一根為六根之用，躡前銷六入一之境。次三句：亦與十方，十二類生，通一見聞覺知，此為群心，通同吻合之境。覺知既已通同吻合，能入圓元。圓元：即識陰也。圓表諸類遍含，元表萬化託始。其言能入者，意明四陰蕩盡，歸宿於此而已。下方是所起法執。若於所歸：即是能入圓元；由不達即是識陰，遂妄立真常，堪可依住，因即依也，以為極果，生殊勝之解者。是人則墮因所因執者：本非可依，而謬執能依之心，所依之境，故曰因所因執。《正脈》云：識乃無明幻影，罔象虛無，畢竟無實，如人夢見依歸得托之地，妄生慶幸，豈有真實哉？下明所墮同類，娑毘迦羅，此云黃髮外道，師事梵天，以我為能歸，以冥諦為所歸，前云非色非空，拘舍黎等，昧為冥諦；然計此非只一人，前後異出耳。成其伴侶者，即同類也。迷佛菩提，亡失知見者：迷了佛果菩提無得之真道，亡失自己因地知見之妙心矣。

〖經〗是名第一，立所得心，成所歸果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生外道種。

〖講〗識陰十相，此居其首，故名第一。前能令此身，根隔合開，遂立此識有所得之心；亦與諸類，覺知通吻，遂執此識成所歸之果。殊不知，圓滿菩提，歸無所得。今以心有所得，果有所歸，如人夢見拾得金寶，歸於家中，所得所歸，皆非真實也。違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生外道種：《寶鏡疏》云：嚮者，行盡識現，根隔合開，圓通將近；今立能立所，則違而遠矣。嚮者，於涅槃性天，將大明悟；今則背之，而入生死路矣。向者，覺心欲發，堪為佛子，今則反生外道，斷佛種矣，可不悲哉！後皆倣此。初因所因執竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, you should know: this good person, having exhausted the aggregate of formations into emptiness, returns, through consciousness, toward this very root, and, this arising and ceasing already extinguished, has not yet perfected this same quiescent cessation in its full, essential subtlety."

Commentary: This shows the ten deviant clingings of the aggregate of consciousness; this is the first, taking a cause as the very thing depended upon. "You should know" is spoken first, to convey this sense of warning. This good person cultivating perfect penetration, "having exhausted the aggregate of formations into emptiness," means researching and exhausting the aggregate of formations until it reaches emptiness — that is, the aggregate of formations already exhausted. "Returns, through consciousness, toward this very root" refers precisely to formations exhausted and consciousness manifest — exactly as it was said earlier: "this restless, flickering original nature settles into its own original stillness, and that stillness absorbs its own original habitual tendency, like waves subsiding into still water." Within the eighth consciousness, one returns, thereby, to this very original root!

The seventh consciousness's own nature of flowing arising and ceasing already extinguished, this quiescent, ceased nature belonging to the aggregate of consciousness has not yet perfected this full, essential subtlety. As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: "quiescent" is precisely the virtue of constancy, departed from birth and death; "cessation" is precisely the virtue of purity, afflictions extinguished; "essential" is precisely the virtue of true self, possessing this real substance; "subtle" is precisely the virtue of bliss, endowed with this spiritual function — together forming this single nirvāṇa, which can be perfected only by passing entirely through the aggregate of consciousness. As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: though this quiescent, ceased nature of the aggregate of consciousness has already appeared before them, this true, essential, wondrous brightness has not yet dawned into its own light, still covered over by the aggregate of consciousness, not yet able to illuminate the entire dharma-realm perfectly. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this "quiescent cessation" spoken of here is precisely the very final untying of the knot within the teaching of perfect penetration. Carrying not the slightest trace of arising or ceasing is called "essential"; retaining only this single flavor of quiescent constancy is called "subtle" — only this is the pure, true substance of this nature. Not yet perfected here makes plain precisely that the aggregate of consciousness has not yet broken through, still covered over, only resembling unity, only resembling constancy, not yet truly essential, not yet truly subtle at all.

Sūtra: "Able to make their own body's sense-faculties close together or open apart at will, and, moreover, share this same awareness with every one of the twelve kinds of being throughout the ten directions, this awareness now in complete accord, they become able to enter this perfect, original root. Should they, regarding what they have thus returned to, establish it as the very cause of true permanence, and give rise to a superior understanding of it, such a person falls into taking a cause as the very thing depended upon — falling in with Kapila's followers and their 'returned-to' dark principle, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view."

Commentary: As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "able to make" and what follows first raises the very cause from which this clinging arises. Generally speaking, whenever such a clinging arises, it is invariably because one has beheld some especially excellent sign within this great samādhi, and this becomes the very starting point.

The first two clauses mean: able to make their own body's six sense-faculties, their own obstruction dissolved away, close these six faculties together into the function of a single faculty, or open a single faculty apart into the function of all six — carrying forward this same state, described earlier, of dissolving the six into one. The next three clauses mean: sharing, moreover, this same seeing, hearing, awareness, and knowing with all twelve kinds of being throughout the ten directions — this being the state where this crowd of minds comes into complete, shared accord. This awareness now in complete accord, they become able to enter this perfect, original root. "Perfect, original root" refers precisely to the aggregate of consciousness itself: "perfect" expresses how it encompasses every kind of being universally; "original" expresses how every transformation takes its very starting point from it. To speak of being "able to enter" here means, precisely, that the four prior aggregates have all been swept away entirely, and one returns, at last, to rest here alone.

What follows is precisely where this clinging to phenomena arises. "Regarding what they have thus returned to" refers precisely to this same ability to enter this perfect, original root; failing to understand that this is nothing but the aggregate of consciousness itself, they falsely establish it instead as this true, permanent reality, something fit to be relied upon and dwelt within — "cause" here meaning precisely "reliance" — taking it to be the very utmost fruit, giving rise to a superior understanding of it. "Such a person falls into taking a cause as the very thing depended upon" means: this was never, in truth, anything fit to be relied upon at all, and yet they wrongly cling to this mind that relies upon it, and this realm relied upon — hence, "taking a cause as the very thing depended upon." As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: consciousness is nothing but ignorance's own illusory reflection, formless and empty, ultimately without any real substance at all — exactly like a person who, in a dream, sees some place of refuge to return to and rely upon, and falsely rejoices at it — how could there be anything real in this at all?

What follows makes plain the kind of companion into which they fall: Kapila, translated "yellow-haired non-Buddhist teacher," took Brahmā as his own master, taking this self as the very agent of return, and this dark principle as the very object returned to — as was said earlier, Maskarī Gośālīputra and others, mistaking neither form nor emptiness for the "primal principle," held exactly this same view; though this position is held not by a single person alone, but appears, in different forms, across different times. "Becoming their own companions" means belonging to this same class. "Confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view" means: confused about the Buddha's own fruit of bodhi, this true path beyond any attainment, and losing this own wondrous mind of right knowledge and right view belonging to the causal ground.

Sūtra: "This is named the first: establishing a mind that believes something has been attained, taking this as the very fruit one has returned to — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of a non-Buddhist teacher."

Commentary: Among the ten marks of the aggregate of consciousness, this stands first, hence "the first." Earlier, able to make this body's own sense-faculties close together or open apart at will, they thereby establish this mind believing that this consciousness has been attained; sharing, moreover, this same awareness and knowing with every other kind of being, they thereby cling to this consciousness as the very fruit they have returned to. Little do they realize that perfect, complete bodhi returns, in the end, to a place where nothing at all is attained. Now, taking this mind to have attained something, this fruit to have somewhere returned to, is exactly like a person who, in a dream, finds gold and treasure, and returns home with it — what is "attained" and what is "returned to" are, in neither case, real at all.

"Straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of a non-Buddhist teacher": as the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: just now, formations exhausted and consciousness manifest, sense-faculties closing together or opening apart at will, perfect penetration was nearly at hand; now, establishing this subject and this object, they stray from it, far away instead. Just now, standing on the very verge of a great awakening dawning within this heavenly realm of nirvāṇa's own nature; now, they turn their back on it, entering, instead, the very road of birth and death. Just now, this awakened mind on the very verge of unfolding, fit to become a child of the Buddha; now, instead, they give rise to this seed of a non-Buddhist teacher, cutting off the very seed of buddhahood — how could this not be lamented! Everything that follows should be understood along this same pattern. This closes the first position, taking a cause as the very thing depended upon.

Chinese Source — Positions 2 Through 10

〖經〗阿難！又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。若於所歸，覽為自體，盡虛空界，十二類內，所有眾生，皆我身中，一類流出。生勝解者，是人則墮，能非能執，摩醯首羅，現無邊身，成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第二，立能為心，成能事果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生大慢天，我遍圓種。

〖講〗此第二，能非能執也，準前可知。若於所歸者：躡前章也。但前章，有乍現勝相之文，已將識陰，為所歸之果。今仍躡所歸二字，前但執為歸托之處，此則覽為自體，是其差別也。覽為自體者：即忘身觀識，久久觀成，唯見識體無邊，不見有身故，即以識心為自體也；遂謂盡虛空界，十二種類，所有眾生，皆我身中，一類流出。生殊勝解者，將必以我為能生彼，而彼非能生我，故曰是人則墮，能非能執。摩醯首羅，即色界頂天，乃三目八臂，騎白牛，執白拂，大自在天是也。竺法蘭云：西域梵志，常修梵行，事首羅天，以為天尊。今云現無邊身者：謂大自在天，自計於身中，能現無邊眾生之身；而此識陰行人，計我生彼十二類生，與彼計同，故云成其伴侶。不信別有因果，自應迷佛所證菩提之果覺，妄計彼天為勝，自應亡失正知正見之因心。立能為心，成能事果者：因見識陰，能攝能生，遂立為能為因心，十方眾生，皆我流出，遂謂成就能事之果。違遠圓通者：則失因地心；背涅槃城者：則亡果地證也。生大慢天，即大自在天，自計能生一切，起祖先慢，故以大慢為名。又計自體，周遍虛空，圓含一切；今行人謬計，既同於彼，成彼種類，故曰我遍圓種也。《正脈》問：此計識為自體，流出一切，何異佛說，萬法唯識？答：佛說萬法唯識，緣生如幻，生即無生；此計實生，安得一轍？又唯識正明無他心外之法，此計能生他法，宛爾顛倒，何疑之有！二能非能執竟。

〖經〗又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。若於所歸，有所歸依，自疑身心，從彼流出，十方虛空，咸其生起，即於都起，所宣流地，作真常身，無生滅解。在生滅中，早計常住，既惑不生，亦迷生滅，安住沉迷。生勝解者，是人則墮，常非常執；計自在天，成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第三，立因依心，成妄計果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生倒圓種。

〖講〗此第三，常非常執，準上可知。若於所歸，有所歸依者：此與上科差別也。上科於所歸識元，覽為自體。此科於所歸識元，為我所歸依處。自疑我之身心，從彼流出。不僅彼能生我，即十方虛空，咸其生起，況空中所有一切耶？即於都起，所宣流地者：地即處也，然識陰即為虛空等，所都起處，為身心等，所宣流處也。作真常身，無生滅解者：於彼所歸識陰，始終不見生滅，故即作真常之身，無有生滅解也。在生滅中，早計常住者：識陰，由真如不生不滅，與生滅和合，名和合識，非是不生不滅，要必破和合識，滅相續心，方為常住真心。今識陰未盡，在生滅中，早計常住者，不亦惑乎？此正不了真如為不生滅性，亦兼不了識陰猶屬微細生滅，故曰既惑不生，猶迷生滅。且真妄雙迷，安心住著，故曰沉迷。反生勝解者：是以識陰，而作常住解；我及萬物，皆是非常；故曰則墮常非常執。計自在天，即欲界頂，魔王天。涅槃經迦旃延說：一切眾生悉是自在天作。《楞伽經》塗灰外道說：計自在天，為萬物因。今以識陰，為身心從彼流出，及虛空萬物，皆其生起，正與此天相似，故曰成其伴侶。然既惑不生滅性，則必迷佛果菩提之道，亦迷生滅識陰，則必亡失，正知正見，而墮邪知邪見也。立因依心者：計識能生我身心為因，又計是我歸依之處為依。成妄計果者：謂以生滅識陰，作真常身，成就妄計之果。亡失本修，自與圓通相違遠，流入外道，正與涅槃，相反背矣。此與上科，同一識陰，而所執則異。前執我圓，能生萬物；此執彼圓，生我身心；故曰倒圓種。三常非常執竟。

〖經〗又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。若於所知，知遍圓故，因知立解，十方草木，皆稱有情，與人無異。草木為人，人死還成十方草樹，無擇遍知。生勝解者，是人則墮，知無知執，婆吒、霰尼，執一切覺，成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第四，計圓知心，成虛謬果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生倒知種。

〖講〗此第四，知無知執，準上可知。溫陵曰：所知，即所觀識陰也。謂識有知，而一切法，由知變起，因計知體，圓遍諸法，遂立異解，謂無情皆有知也。十方草木，本屬無情，以異解故，皆稱有情，與人無異。草木為人，人死還成十方草樹，互為輪轉，無所揀擇，有情無情，而遍皆有知也。《正脈》問：此與佛說，山河化為無上知覺，有何簡別？答：今詳內所明，見、相二分，本惟一心，迷之為二，故妄見無情，不通知覺，大悟復歸一心，則通一知覺，更無外物，非謂各各有知，同他心量也。今以無情有知，生勝解者，是人則墮，謬計無情有知，實本無知，故曰知無知執。婆吒、霰尼二外道名；婆吒，名義集云：跋闍，此云避去，善見律云：初為牧童，毘舍離王，未登位時，共同遊戲，童為王蹋，泣訴父母。父母曰：汝應避去，因此立名。霰尼或云先尼，此翻有軍，立名之意未詳。執一切覺者：二外道執一切有情無情，皆有知覺，此計遍知，竟同於彼，故曰成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第四，計圓遍一切有知為因心，成虛無謬誤之果，則違遠圓通之因地心，迷背涅槃之果地覺，生倒知種；以無情無知為有知，謂倒知種。問：世有依草附木，以顯靈異，詎非有知耶？答：是能依能附之精靈有知，非彼無情之草木有知也。四知無知執竟。

〖經〗又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。若於圓融，根互用中，已得隨順，便於圓化，一切發生，求火光明，樂水清淨，愛風周流，觀塵成就，各各崇事，以此群塵，發作本因，立常住解，是人則墮，生無生執。諸迦葉波。並婆羅門，勤心役身，事火崇水，求出生死，成其伴侶，迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第五，計著崇事，迷心從物，立妄求因，求妄冀果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生顛化種。

〖講〗此第五生無生執，準上可知。環師謂：識陰盡者，消磨六門，諸根互用，今識陰未盡，則纔得隨順而已。若於圓滿無礙，融通不隔，六根互用之中，觀中一字，足見互用之妙，含而未發也。已得隨順者：雖然互用之妙，含而未發而已；得隨心順意，略無隔礙而已。即便於此，圓融化理，妄生計度，一切諸法，莫不由此四大發生。於是求火之光明，樂水之清淨，愛風之周流，觀塵之成就，塵即地大，以地大能成就諸事故。各各崇事：崇即尊崇，事即供事，或尊供於火，或尊供於水等，各隨所見，而偏執也。為欲增進其圓化之妙，以此群塵，發作本因者，群塵即指四大，如前云：四大和合，發明世間，種種諸相，以此為發生造作本因。一切所作，皆屬無常，惟此常住，故立常住解，以為常司造化之真宰也。並謬計能生聖果，一切聖凡因果，莫不由之而生；實則不能生，故曰生無生執。諸迦葉波，別姓也，如優樓頻螺迦葉等，婆羅門總姓，共有十八，迦葉其一也，乃指一類，事四大之外道。勤勞其心，役使其身，供事於火，尊崇於水，事風崇土，可以例知。以此崇事，求出生死，以求真常之果，成其伴侶。所立既非真常，所修寧有實果？故曰迷佛菩提。崇事無情，立常住解，故曰亡失知見。《正脈》云：計著：邪惑也。崇事：邪業也。迷心者：迷己一真靈覺之心；從物者：從四大無知之物。妄求因者：非因計因也；妄冀果者：非果望果也。認無情之物為真因，故曰違遠圓通；將有漏之果為實證，故曰背涅槃城。生顛化種者：謂生顛倒化理之種類，佛說應觀法界性，一切唯心造。是心為能造，而一切為所造，此是生物之正理。今既顛倒化理，故墮外道種類。五生無生執竟。

〖經〗又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。若於圓明，計明中虛，非滅群化，以永滅依，為所歸依。生勝解者，是人則墮，歸無歸執，無想天中，諸舜若多，成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第六，圓虛無心，成空亡果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生斷滅種。

〖講〗此第六歸無歸執，準上可知。圓明，即是識陰，見前四陰皆盡，露出識陰，圓遍湛明，猶如止水，而不知仍是罔象，虛無妄想，即便計此，圓明中虛無體性，為究竟地。非滅群化者：非即毀也，要毀滅群塵所化，一切身土，即欲灰身滅土，纖塵不立。以永滅依，為所歸依者：以永滅群化所依之空，為所歸之處，常處虛空，永為依托，更不前進。豈知乃是虛無不實之境，非是實有可歸之處也。以此生勝解者，是人則墮歸無歸執，以此為托之處，實無可歸托也。無想天中，諸舜若多，成其伴侶者：《正脈》云：無想天，略舉非非想，以該四空，非取四禪無想也。諸舜若多，總舉趣空天眾，為同類也。既以斷滅為果，自應迷佛菩提，以虛無為因，自應亡失知見。於圓明中，以虛無之心為因，成就空亡之果，謂縱使修到非非想天，八萬劫終是落空亡。違遠圓通，以不生不滅為因地心；背涅槃城，以不生不滅為果地覺也。現在既以斷滅居心，將來必墮斷滅之種。《正脈》問：此與後二何別？答：棄有取空，見解志願皆同，但先心各別，此凡外種伏惑取空，彼聖性種斷惑取空也。六歸無歸執竟。

〖經〗又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。若於圓常，固身常住，同於精圓，長不傾逝。生勝解者，是人則墮，貪非貪執；諸阿斯陀，求長命者，成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第七，執著命元，立固妄因，趣長勞果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生妄延種。

〖講〗此第七貪非貪執，準上可知。《正脈》云：圓常亦識陰區宇。歷觀上來，於此一境，稱圓元、圓融、圓明、圓常，義各有表。元表諸法統歸，融表萬化含蓄，明表徹體虛朗，常表究竟堅牢。各與本文關涉，細尋可見。由彼觀見，前四陰盡滅，而識陰現前，識體精明，湛不搖動，而妄計為圓滿常住也。又見識為一身之主，有欲堅固此身，令得常住，同於識精圓明，長不傾逝。傾逝即死也，即所謂長生不死也。不知此身，終是無常生滅，妄生貪著。以此生勝解者，是人則墮，貪非貪執，謂妄生貪著長生，而實非可貪也。諸阿斯陀，此云無比，即長壽仙也。言諸者，以仙非一人，凡求長命者，成其伴侶。不知更求真常之果，故曰迷佛菩提；但知堅固幻妄之軀，故曰亡失知見。執著命元者：執著識陰，為受命之根元也。立固妄因者：立堅固幻妄之色身，以圓常識陰為因心。趣長勞果者：趣向長戀塵勞之果報，而不求出離之道也。亡失本修，故曰違遠圓通；不出生死，故曰背涅槃城。生妄延種者：謂不達自性真實常住，存想固形，妄冀延長壽命，以成長壽仙之種類也。七貪非貪執竟。

〖經〗又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。觀命互通，卻留塵勞，恐其銷盡，便於此際，坐蓮華宮，廣化七珍，多增寶媛，恣縱其心。生勝解者，是人則墮，真無真執，吒枳、迦羅，成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第八，發邪思因，立熾塵果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生天魔種。

〖講〗此第八真無真執，準上可知。觀命：即觀受命元由，乃是識陰，與諸類通覺，故曰互通。是知一切身命，咸以識陰為本，一切塵勞，悉與命元有關；於是卻留住世間塵勞，祗恐其銷盡，則我之身命，無所依託。且行盡識現，一切圓融變化，莫不隨心自在。便於此際，正卻留塵勞之相。塵勞以貪欲為本，即於此際，現坐蓮華宮，取其莊嚴美麗，微妙香潔。廣化七珍，多增寶媛者：七珍即七寶，媛是美女也。使七寶羅列於華宮，俾美女橫陳於左右，窮奢極欲，恣縱其心，以為妙樂。生勝解者，是人則墮，真無真執，妄執業識，命元為真常，而實非真常。吒枳迦羅，即天魔之異名，吒枳此云結縛，迦羅此云我所作，此魔自謂三界結縛，惟我所作，以能變化欲境，結縛眾生故。今卻留塵勞，略同彼計，故云成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見，不知更有無上菩提，亡失正知正見也。發邪思因者：謂發邪思縱欲，以為因心。立熾塵果者：謂立熾盛塵勞，以為果覺也。違遠圓通，本修之因心；背涅槃城，寂滅之果覺也。不斷欲而修禪，必落魔道，故曰天魔種。八真無真執竟。

〖經〗又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。於命明中，分別精麤，疏決真偽，因果相酬，惟求感應，背清淨道：所謂見苦斷集，證滅修道，居滅已休，要不前進。生勝解者，是人則墮定性聲聞。諸無聞僧，增上慢者，成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第九，圓精應心，成趣寂果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城。生纏空種。

〖講〗此第九定性聲聞，準上可知。於命明中者：謂識陰顯露，已能通達，各命由緒，瞭然明白也。便知此中，識陰遍含，漏無漏種子，為一切凡聖所依。由是分別精麤者：分析辨別，而聖位則精，謂變易精微故；凡位則麤，謂分段麤顯故。疏決真偽者：疏通決擇，而聖道則真，謂修證真實故；外道則偽，謂斷常偽妄故。因果相酬者：世出世間，皆是依因感果，自相酬答。而欲易麤為精，捨偽從真，故惟求感應。感即是修，應即是證，惟求真修實證，速出三界也。背清淨道者：謂背棄一乘實相，清淨之道，以雙離二邊垢故。所謂見苦、斷集、證滅、修道者：躡解惟求感應之事。苦、集，是世間因果。滅、道，是出世間因果。見世間苦諦苦果，皆由集諦煩惱苦因所招；見出世間滅諦樂果，皆由道諦樂因所感。厭苦斷集，慕滅修道。居滅已休，更不前進者：居滅諦涅槃之樂，則心滿意足，自謂所作已辦，生死已了，更不求進大乘之道，中止化城，不求寶所，得少為足。生勝解者：於菩薩所修大乘，不生一念好樂之心，是人則墮，定性聲聞。定性：即不發回小向大之心，鈍阿羅漢是也。諸無聞僧者：四禪無聞比丘，及增上慢者，略同此計，故曰成其伴侶。沉空滯寂，故迷佛果，菩提之道，灰身泯智，是以亡失大乘知見。圓精應心者：圓滿易麤為精，求應之因心，成就沉空趣寂，定性之小果。不得圓通之因心，故曰違遠。莫獲涅槃之極果，故稱迷背。生纏空種：謂永纏於空，而無超脫之志。九定性聲聞竟。

〖經〗又善男子，窮諸行空，已滅生滅，而於寂滅，精妙未圓。若於圓融，清淨覺明，發研深妙，即立涅槃，而不前進。生勝解者，是人則墮，定性闢支。諸緣獨倫，不回心者，成其伴侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見。是名第十，圓覺吻心，成湛明果，違遠圓通，背涅槃城，生覺圓明，不化圓種。

〖講〗此第十定性闢支，準上可知。六根互用曰圓融，諸塵不染曰清淨，照見命元曰覺明。發研深妙者：發心研究有二種：一獨覺，寂居觀化，無師自悟。二緣覺，觀察因緣，悟明無性，此二人俱得深妙之悟，即以悟境，立為涅槃，以為歸息之處。不知更求真如不動，寂滅場地，及性海圓融，緣起無礙，故云而不前進。以是生勝解者，是人則墮入定性闢支，闢支梵語，具含緣、獨二覺義。定性，以不復前進，亦即不回心者，成其同伴等侶。迷佛菩提，亡失知見者：迷了佛果，無上菩提之道，此寶所也；亡失本修，正知正見之心，此因心也。圓遍諸類，覺知通吻，此是識陰境界，即以悟境為因心，成湛明果。寂靜名湛，成獨覺，得自然慧之果；洞徹曰明，成緣覺，究竟無生之果。違遠圓通，背涅槃城者：謂圓通依不生滅為因地心，然後圓成果地修證。今乃依識陰悟境，即立涅槃，得少為足，中止化城，故曰違遠，曰背。生覺圓明，不化圓種者：覺，即獨覺、緣覺，生此二種果位；圓明即悟證之境。獨覺無師自悟，緣覺緣生無性，遂計其理圓，其智明，即認為涅槃真境；而不能融化透過，所悟所執，空淨圓影，依然為一定性種耳。問：識陰十境，前八是外道、天、仙、魔王，錯修妄本，貪戀塵勞，不出三界，枉受生死，判屬為魔，固其宜矣；而聲聞、緣覺、斷惑證真，已出三界，而了生死，何亦判屬魔境，令人不敢修習；倘遇鈍根，有失接引，豈慈悲心者之所為耶？答：準《華嚴經》，忘失菩提心，修諸善法，皆為魔業。況此經乃修楞嚴大定，圓滿無上菩提，聲聞、緣覺，得少為足，中止化城，非魔業而何哉？二中間十執竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

2. Taking the "Capable" Self as Universal 能非能執 — Regarding what one has thus returned to, one takes it, instead, as one's own very self, concluding that every sentient being throughout the entire realm of empty space, within all twelve kinds of being, flows outward from within this same body of one's own, each kind its own separate stream. Giving rise to a superior understanding of this, such a person falls into taking oneself as the capable generator of what is, in fact, incapable of being generated this way — falling in with Maheśvara and his own manifestation of a boundless body, becoming his own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the second: establishing this mind as capable of generating all things, taking this as the very fruit of this capable act — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of the heaven of great pride, this self-as-universal-and-perfect kind.

Commentary: This is the second, taking the "capable" self as universal — understood following the pattern above. "Regarding what one has thus returned to" carries forward the section before it, yet, where that section spoke of some especially excellent sign momentarily appearing, already taking the aggregate of consciousness as the very fruit returned to, this present position, though carrying forward these same two words "returned to," differs in that, before, it was merely clung to as some place of refuge, while here it is, instead, regarded as one's own very self — this is the distinction between them.

"Regarding it as one's own very self" means: forgetting the body to observe consciousness alone, and, this observation cultivated for a long time, seeing only this substance of consciousness as boundless, no longer seeing any body present at all — and so taking this mind of consciousness to be one's own very self. And so they conclude that every sentient being, throughout the entire realm of empty space, within all twelve kinds of being, flows outward from within this same body of theirs, each kind its own separate stream. Giving rise to this superior understanding, they inevitably conclude that this self of theirs is what generates all others, while these others are, in no way, capable of generating this self — hence, "such a person falls into taking oneself as the capable generator of what is, in fact, incapable of being generated this way."

Maheśvara is precisely the deva at the very summit of the form realm — possessing three eyes and eight arms, riding a white bull, holding a white whisk, this Great Sovereign Deva himself. As Zhu Falan states: ascetics of the Western Regions constantly cultivate brahmacarya, serving this Śvara-deva as their own heavenly lord. "Manifesting a boundless body" here refers to how this Great Sovereign Deva concludes, of himself, that he is able to manifest, within his own body, the bodies of boundless sentient beings; and this practitioner within the aggregate of consciousness, concluding that they themselves generate all twelve kinds of sentient being, holds exactly this same view — hence, "becoming his own companions." Not believing that there exists any separate cause and effect, they inevitably become confused about the Buddha's own realized fruit of bodhi, falsely concluding this deva to be superior, and inevitably lose this causal mind of right knowledge and right view.

"Establishing this mind as capable of generating all things, taking this as the very fruit of this capable act" means: seeing that the aggregate of consciousness is able to encompass and able to generate, they establish it, thereby, as this capable, causal mind, concluding that every sentient being of the ten directions flows out from themselves, and so declare this to be the accomplished fruit of this capable act. "Straying far from perfect penetration" means losing this causal mind; "turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa" means losing this fruit-ground realization. "The heaven of great pride" is precisely this Great Sovereign Deva, who, concluding of himself that he is able to generate everything, gives rise to an ancestral pride befitting this claim — hence named "great pride." He further concludes that his own substance pervades empty space entirely, perfectly encompassing everything; now, this practitioner's own wrong conclusion, being exactly the same as his, becomes, thereby, this same kind — hence, "this self-as-universal-and-perfect kind."

As the Standard Meaning Commentary asks: this position takes consciousness as one's own self, flowing outward to generate everything — how does this differ from the Buddha's own teaching that every phenomenon is nothing but consciousness-only? Answer: the Buddha's teaching that every phenomenon is nothing but consciousness-only means that everything arises dependent on conditions, like an illusion — this very arising being, itself, unarisen; this present position, though, takes this generation to be genuinely real — how could the two ever be reconciled? And, further, consciousness-only teaching makes plain, precisely, that there is no phenomenon whatsoever apart from mind itself; this present position, though, holds that this self is capable of generating other, separate phenomena — plainly an inversion of the very teaching itself. What doubt could remain! This closes the second position, taking the capable self as universal.

3. Taking Dependence as Permanence 常非常執 — Regarding what one has thus returned to, one takes it, instead, as something to be relied upon and returned to, suspecting one's own body and mind to flow outward from it, and that all of empty space throughout the ten directions arises from it as well — and so, regarding this very ground from which everything arises and flows, they take it to be a permanent, true body, free of arising and ceasing. Yet, still in the very midst of arising and ceasing, already concluding this to be permanently abiding, they remain confused about what is truly unborn, and equally deluded about what does, in fact, arise and cease — dwelling, thereby, in this deep confusion. Giving rise to a superior understanding of this, such a person falls into taking dependence itself for permanence — falling in with the followers of the Freely-Abiding Deva, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the third: establishing this mind of causal reliance, taking this as the fruit of this false speculation — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of this inverted-perfection kind.

Commentary: This is the third, taking dependence as permanence — understood following the pattern above. "Regarding what one has thus returned to, taking it as something to be relied upon and returned to" differs from the position before it: that position, regarding this consciousness returned to, regarded it as one's own very self; this position, regarding this same consciousness returned to, takes it, instead, as the very place upon which oneself relies and returns. They suspect that their own body and mind flow outward from it — and not only is it able to generate this self, but all of empty space throughout the ten directions arises from it as well, how much more so, then, everything found within that space? "Regarding this very ground from which everything arises and flows" — "ground" here means "place": that is, the aggregate of consciousness is taken to be the very place from which all of empty space and the rest universally arise, and the very place from which body and mind and the rest flow outward.

"Taking it to be a permanent, true body, free of arising and ceasing" means: regarding this same aggregate of consciousness returned to, never once seeing, from beginning to end, any arising or ceasing within it, they take it, thereby, to be a permanent, true body, free of arising and ceasing. "Still in the very midst of arising and ceasing, already concluding this to be permanently abiding" means: the aggregate of consciousness, since suchness itself, unborn and unceasing, is merged together with arising and ceasing, is named this merged consciousness — this is not, in itself, unborn and unceasing; only by breaking through this merged consciousness, extinguishing this continuing mind, can one attain the truly permanent, true mind. Now, the aggregate of consciousness not yet exhausted, and, still in the very midst of arising and ceasing, already concluding this to be permanently abiding — is this not confusion indeed? This is precisely failing to understand that suchness itself is what neither arises nor ceases, while, at the same time, failing to understand that the aggregate of consciousness still belongs to this same subtle arising and ceasing — hence, "confused about what is truly unborn, and equally deluded about what does, in fact, arise and cease." Confused, moreover, about both truth and falseness together, settling their own mind into this attachment, they dwell, thereby, in this deep confusion.

Giving rise, instead, to this superior understanding: this is taking the aggregate of consciousness and forming, from it, an understanding of permanent abiding; taking this self and every phenomenon to be, thereby, not truly permanent at all — hence, "falls into taking dependence itself for permanence." The Freely-Abiding Deva refers precisely to the demon-king's own heaven at the very summit of the desire realm. As Kātyāyana states in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra: every sentient being is, entirely, the very creation of this Freely-Abiding Deva. As the ash-smearing non-Buddhist teachers state in the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra: this Freely-Abiding Deva is held to be the very cause of every phenomenon. Now, taking the aggregate of consciousness as that from which body and mind flow outward, and empty space and every phenomenon likewise arise, this position closely resembles this same heavenly teaching — hence, "becoming their own companions." And yet, confused about this nature that neither arises nor ceases, they inevitably become confused about the Buddha's own fruit-path of bodhi as well; and, deluded, too, about the arising and ceasing of the aggregate of consciousness itself, they inevitably lose this right knowledge and right view, falling instead into wrong knowledge and wrong view.

"Establishing this mind of causal reliance" means: concluding that consciousness is able to generate this body and mind of theirs as its very cause, and, further, concluding that this is the very place upon which they themselves rely. "Taking this as the fruit of this false speculation" means: taking this arising-and-ceasing aggregate of consciousness and forming, from it, this permanent, true body, accomplishing, thereby, the fruit of this same false speculation. Losing this fundamental practice, they stray, of themselves, far from perfect penetration, flowing instead into this non-Buddhist teaching, running directly counter to nirvāṇa itself. This position and the one before it both concern this same aggregate of consciousness, yet differ in what is clung to: the position before this clung to this self as universal, capable of generating everything; this position clings to that consciousness as universal, generating this self's own body and mind — hence, "this inverted-perfection kind." This closes the third position, taking dependence as permanence.

4. Taking Universal Knowing as Absolute 知無知執 — Regarding what is known, concluding that this knowing pervades everywhere, universally and perfectly, and, from this conclusion, forming this understanding: that grass and trees of the ten directions are all, alike, sentient, no different from human beings; that grass and trees become human, and, when a human dies, they become, once more, the grass and trees of the ten directions — knowing applied indiscriminately, universally, without any distinction whatsoever. Giving rise to a superior understanding of this, such a person falls into taking universal knowing as absolute — falling in with the followers of Vasiṣṭha and Śaṇḍilya, clinging to universal sentience throughout, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the fourth: concluding this mind of perfect knowing, taking this as the fruit of this false, mistaken view — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of this inverted-knowing kind.

Commentary: This is the fourth, taking universal knowing as absolute — understood following the pattern above. As Wenling puts it: "what is known" refers precisely to the aggregate of consciousness itself as the object observed. Concluding that consciousness possesses this knowing, and that every phenomenon arises through the transformation of this same knowing, they conclude, from this, that this very substance of knowing pervades every phenomenon universally, and so establish this deviant understanding: that even what is insentient possesses knowing.

Grass and trees of the ten directions, originally insentient, are, through this deviant understanding, declared, instead, to be sentient, no different from human beings. Grass and trees become human; and, when a human dies, they become, once more, the grass and trees of the ten directions — turning into each other endlessly, with no distinction whatsoever, sentient and insentient alike, they conclude, possessed of knowing universally. As the Standard Meaning Commentary asks: how does this differ from the Buddha's own teaching that mountains and rivers transform into unsurpassed awareness? Answer: examined in detail, what this refers to internally are the seeing-portion and the appearing-portion, originally one and the same mind, divided into two only through delusion — and so one falsely sees what is insentient as cut off from awareness entirely; upon great awakening, returning once more to this same single mind, one comes to share this one, same awareness throughout, with no external object remaining at all — this is not at all the same as saying that each and every thing, individually, possesses its own knowing, sharing the very same measure of mind as another sentient being.

Now, giving rise to this superior understanding — that even what is insentient possesses knowing — such a person falls into this wrong conclusion, that what is insentient possesses knowing, when, in truth, it possesses no knowing at all — hence, "taking universal knowing as absolute." Vasiṣṭha and Śaṇḍilya are the names of two non-Buddhist teachers. Regarding Vasiṣṭha, the Compendium of Names and Meanings states: Vārṣagaṇya, translated "one who flees" — the Sudarśana-vinaya states: originally a cowherd boy, who, before King Vaiśālī had ascended the throne, once played together with him, and, being trampled by the future king, wept and complained to his own parents, who told him, "you ought to flee" — and so this name was given. Śaṇḍilya, also rendered Senika, translated "possessing an army" — the intent behind this name is not recorded. "Clinging to universal sentience" means: these two non-Buddhist teachers cling to the view that everything, sentient and insentient alike, possesses knowing and sentience — this present conclusion of universal knowing being exactly the same as theirs — hence, "becoming their own companions." Confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view: this is named the fourth: concluding this mind of universal, perfect knowing to be the very cause, accomplishing this empty, mistaken fruit — straying, thereby, far from this causal mind of perfect penetration, confused and turned away from this fruit-ground awareness of nirvāṇa, giving rise to this inverted-knowing kind — taking what is insentient and without knowing to possess knowing, hence, "inverted knowing."

Question: the world does hold that spirits sometimes rely upon grass or attach themselves to trees, manifesting numinous, uncanny effects — is this not proof that these things possess knowing? Answer: it is the very spirit capable of this reliance and attachment that possesses knowing — not that the insentient grass or tree itself possesses any knowing at all. This closes the fourth position, taking universal knowing as absolute.

5. Taking Worship of the Elements as the Path 生無生執 — Having gained, within this perfect fusion, this free interchange among the sense-faculties, already according with this freely, they proceed, within this same perfect transformation, to conclude that everything, without exception, arises from this source — and so they seek fire's own brightness, delight in water's own purity, love wind's own constant circulation, and contemplate earth's own accomplishment, each in turn revering and serving one of these, taking this whole multitude of elements as the very root cause from which everything arises, establishing an understanding of permanent abiding upon it. Such a person falls into taking worship of the elements as the very path to what cannot, in truth, be generated this way at all — falling in with the various fire-worshipping Kāśyapas and other brahmins, laboring in mind and pressing their own bodies into service, serving fire and revering water, seeking to escape birth and death through this, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the fifth: clinging to and revering these elements, this mind confused and following after mere objects, establishing this false cause and seeking this false fruit — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of this inverted-transformation kind.

Commentary: This is the fifth, taking worship of the elements as the path — understood following the pattern above. As Master Huan puts it: for one whose aggregate of consciousness is truly exhausted, the six sense-gates are ground down until every faculty interchanges freely; here, though, the aggregate of consciousness not yet exhausted, they have merely attained this same accord for the moment.

"Within this perfect fusion, without any obstruction, this free interchange among the sense-faculties" — the character "within" here shows plainly this wondrous quality of free interchange, present yet not fully unfolded. "Already according with this freely" means: though this wondrous quality of free interchange remains present yet not fully unfolded, they have already gained a certain accord with their own mind and wish, with almost no obstruction at all. And so, right within this perfect fusion and transformation, they give rise, falsely, to this speculation: that every phenomenon, without exception, arises from these four elements. And so they seek fire's own brightness, delight in water's own purity, love wind's own constant circulation, and contemplate earth's own accomplishment — "dust" here referring to the element of earth, since the element of earth is able to accomplish every undertaking. "Each in turn revering and serving one of these": "revering" means holding in high esteem, "serving" means making offerings to — whether revering and offering to fire, or to water, and so on, each following their own particular view, and clinging one-sidedly to it.

Wishing, further, to advance this wondrous quality of perfect transformation, they take this whole multitude of elements as the very root cause from which everything arises — "this whole multitude of elements" referring precisely to the four elements, exactly as was said earlier: "the four elements, combining together, give rise to every kind of appearance found throughout the world" — taking this to be the very root cause of every arising and creation. Yet everything so created belongs, without exception, to impermanence; only this alone, they conclude, is permanently abiding, and so they establish this understanding of permanent abiding, taking it to be the true sovereign that constantly governs creation and transformation. They further falsely conclude that this is able to generate the sagely fruit itself, that every cause and effect, sagely and ordinary alike, arises, without exception, from it — yet, in truth, it cannot generate anything of the kind at all — hence, "taking worship of the elements as the path to what cannot, in truth, be generated this way at all."

The various Kāśyapas are a family name, as in Uruvilvā-Kāśyapa and others; brahmin is a general clan-name, comprising eighteen in total, of which Kāśyapa is one — referring here to a class of non-Buddhist teachers who serve the four elements. Laboring their own mind, pressing their own body into service, offering to fire, revering water, serving wind, honoring earth — each may be understood by this same pattern. Through this reverence and service, they seek to escape birth and death, seeking this fruit of true permanence, becoming their own companions. Yet what they have established is not, in truth, true permanence at all — how, then, could what they cultivate ever yield any real fruit? Hence, "confused about the Buddha's own bodhi." Revering and serving what is insentient, establishing this understanding of permanent abiding — hence, "losing this right knowledge and right view."

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "clinging to and revering" is this deviant delusion; "revering and serving" is this deviant karma. "This mind confused" means confused about this single, true, spiritually aware mind that is one's own; "following after mere objects" means following after these insentient objects, the four elements. "Establishing this false cause" means taking what is not a cause to be a cause; "seeking this false fruit" means hoping for what is not a fruit as though it were one. Taking an insentient object to be the true cause — hence, "straying far from perfect penetration"; taking a fruit still subject to outflow to be genuine realization — hence, "turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa." "Giving rise to the seed of this inverted-transformation kind" means giving rise to this kind that has inverted the true principle of transformation itself — for the Buddha teaches that one ought to observe the very nature of the dharma-realm as, entirely, created by mind alone. This mind is the very creator, and everything else the very thing created — this is the correct principle governing how things come to be. Now, having inverted this very principle of transformation, they fall, thereby, into this non-Buddhist kind. This closes the fifth position, taking worship of the elements as the path.

6. Taking Emptiness Itself as the Final Refuge 歸無歸執 — Regarding this perfect brightness, they conclude that, within this brightness, there lies an emptiness — not the extinguishing of this whole multitude of transformations, but taking this permanent extinction itself as their very refuge, this being what they return to. Giving rise to a superior understanding of this, such a person falls into taking as a refuge what offers, in truth, no refuge at all — falling in with the beings of the No-Conception Heaven, the Śūnyapuṣpas, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the sixth: taking this perfectly empty mind as the cause, accomplishing this fruit of utter, empty loss — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of this annihilationist kind.

Commentary: This is the sixth, taking emptiness itself as the final refuge — understood following the pattern above. "This perfect brightness" refers precisely to the aggregate of consciousness itself: seeing the four prior aggregates all already exhausted, and this aggregate of consciousness laid bare, perfect, pervading, translucent, and bright, exactly like still water. Not realizing that this remains, still, nothing but this same formless, empty false thought, they conclude, instead, that, within this perfect brightness, there lies an empty, substanceless nature, taking this to be the very ultimate ground.

"Not the extinguishing of this whole multitude of transformations" means: "not" here means to destroy — that is, wishing to destroy and extinguish this whole multitude of transformations produced by these elements, every body and land alike, wishing, that is, to reduce the body to ash and extinguish the land entirely, with not even the finest speck of dust remaining. "Taking this permanent extinction itself as their very refuge, this being what they return to" means: taking this very emptiness upon which this whole multitude of transformations permanently depends as the very place they return to, dwelling constantly within this emptiness, taking it as their permanent refuge, advancing no further at all. Little do they know that this is nothing but an empty, unreal realm, not truly a real place to which one could return at all.

Giving rise to this superior understanding on this basis, such a person falls into taking as a refuge what offers, in truth, no refuge at all — taking this as the place they rely upon, when, in truth, there is nothing here to rely upon or return to whatsoever. "Falling in with the beings of the No-Conception Heaven, the Śūnyapuṣpas, becoming their own companions": as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it, "the No-Conception Heaven" here briefly names the base of neither perception nor non-perception, meant to encompass all four formless heavens, not referring specifically to the No-Thought heaven of the fourth dhyāna. "The Śūnyapuṣpas" generally names the multitude of heavenly beings inclined toward emptiness, taken here as belonging to this same class. Having already taken annihilation as their own fruit, they inevitably become confused about the Buddha's own bodhi; taking emptiness as their own cause, they inevitably lose this right knowledge and right view.

Within this perfect brightness, taking this empty, substanceless mind as the cause, they accomplish this fruit of utter, empty loss — meaning that, even should one cultivate all the way to the base of neither perception nor non-perception, after these eighty thousand kalpas, one still, in the end, falls back into this same empty loss. "Straying far from perfect penetration" means taking what neither arises nor ceases as the causal mind falsely; "turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa" means taking what neither arises nor ceases as the fruit-ground awareness falsely. Presently, already settling their own mind upon annihilation, they will inevitably, in the future, fall into this same annihilationist kind. As the Standard Meaning Commentary asks: how does this differ from the two positions that follow it? Answer: casting aside existence and grasping at emptiness, their view and their aspiration are, in each case, the same — yet the mind from which each begins differs: this present position belongs to the ordinary and non-Buddhist kind, merely subduing delusion to grasp at emptiness; the two that follow belong to the noble-nature kind, actually cutting off delusion to grasp at emptiness instead. This closes the sixth position, taking emptiness itself as the final refuge.

7. Clinging to Bodily Permanence 貪非貪執 — Regarding this perfect constancy, they wish to make this body itself permanently abiding, identical to this essential perfection, never to decline or pass away for long. Giving rise to a superior understanding of this, such a person falls into craving what, in truth, offers nothing worth craving at all — falling in with the immortal Asita and every other seeker of long life, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the seventh: clinging to this very root of life itself, establishing this false cause of bodily permanence, seeking this fruit of prolonged, worldly toil — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of this falsely-extended kind.

Commentary: This is the seventh, clinging to bodily permanence — understood following the pattern above. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this "perfect constancy," too, belongs to the boundary of the aggregate of consciousness. Surveying this same single state discussed throughout, called, in turn, "perfect origin," "perfect fusion," "perfect brightness," and "perfect constancy," each name carries its own particular sense: "origin" expresses how every phenomenon returns, together, to it; "fusion" expresses how every transformation is held within it; "brightness" expresses how its very substance is thoroughly, emptily clear; "constancy" expresses its very utmost firmness and solidity — each connected to the text at hand, discoverable through careful examination.

Through this observation, seeing the four prior aggregates all exhausted, and the aggregate of consciousness manifest before them, this substance of consciousness essential and bright, translucent, unmoving, they falsely conclude this to be perfect, permanent abiding. And, further, seeing consciousness as the very master of this one body, they wish to make this body firm and solid, so that it, too, might attain this same permanent abiding, identical to this essential, perfect brightness of consciousness, never to decline or pass away for long — "decline and pass away" meaning precisely death, that is, this very notion of eternal life without death. Not realizing that this body, in the end, remains subject to impermanence, to arising and ceasing, they falsely give rise to this craving attachment.

Giving rise to this superior understanding on this basis, such a person falls into craving what, in truth, offers nothing worth craving at all — meaning that they falsely give rise to this craving attachment to eternal life, when, in truth, this is nothing worth craving in the least. The immortal Asita, translated "without compare," refers precisely to a long-lived immortal — spoken of in the plural, since such immortals are not a single person alone; every seeker of long life becomes their own companion. Not knowing to seek, instead, this truly permanent fruit, they inevitably become confused about the Buddha's own bodhi; knowing only to strengthen this illusory, false body, they inevitably lose this right knowledge and right view.

"Clinging to this very root of life itself" means clinging to the aggregate of consciousness as the very root cause of receiving life. "Establishing this false cause of bodily permanence" means establishing this firm, illusory, false physical body, taking this perfectly constant aggregate of consciousness as its causal mind. "Seeking this fruit of prolonged, worldly toil" means seeking after this fruit of long attachment to worldly toil, rather than seeking this path of transcendence and liberation. Losing this fundamental practice — hence, "straying far from perfect penetration"; never transcending birth and death — hence, "turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa." "Giving rise to the seed of this falsely-extended kind" means: failing to understand this own nature's true, permanent abiding, visualizing, instead, to solidify their own bodily form, falsely hoping to extend their own lifespan, becoming, thereby, this same kind as the long-lived immortals. This closes the seventh position, clinging to bodily permanence.

8. Indulging Worldly Pleasure Out of Fear of Its Loss 真無真執 — Observing life to be interconnected everywhere, they deliberately retain this worldly entanglement instead, fearing that, once it is entirely exhausted, they will have nothing left — and so, at exactly this juncture, they sit within a lotus palace of their own conjuring, manifesting the seven treasures widely, multiplying jeweled consorts around themselves, indulging their own mind without any restraint at all. Giving rise to a superior understanding of this, such a person falls into taking as truth what is, in fact, no truth at all — falling in with Ṭaki and Kāla, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the eighth: giving rise to this deviant, indulgent thought as the cause, establishing this fruit of blazing worldly entanglement — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of a heavenly demon.

Commentary: This is the eighth, taking as truth what is, in fact, no truth at all — understood following the pattern above. "Observing life" means observing this very root cause of receiving life — that is, precisely the aggregate of consciousness, sharing this same awareness with every other kind of being — hence, "interconnected everywhere." Knowing, thereby, that every body and life, without exception, take the aggregate of consciousness as their very root, and that every worldly entanglement, entirely, bears some relation to this same root cause of life, they thereupon deliberately retain this worldly entanglement instead, fearing only that, once entirely exhausted, this body and life of theirs will have nowhere left to rely upon at all. And, moreover, with formations exhausted and consciousness manifest, every kind of perfect fusion and transformation follows the mind freely, entirely at ease — and so, at exactly this juncture, they deliberately retain this very mark of worldly entanglement. This worldly entanglement takes craving desire as its very root, and so, at exactly this juncture, they manifest, sitting within a lotus palace, chosen for its solemn beauty, its wondrous fragrance and purity. "Manifesting the seven treasures widely, multiplying jeweled consorts": the seven treasures are precisely the seven kinds of treasure, and "consorts" here means beautiful women. Arraying the seven treasures throughout this lotus palace, and beautiful women reclining to either side, indulging in every luxury and desire to its very utmost, giving free rein to their own mind, taking this to be wondrous bliss.

Giving rise to this superior understanding on this basis, such a person falls into taking as truth what is, in fact, no truth at all — falsely clinging to this karmic consciousness, this very root of life, as true permanence itself, when, in truth, it is nothing of the kind. Ṭaki-Kāla is another name for the heavenly demon himself: Ṭaki means "bondage," Kāla means "my own doing" — this demon declares, of himself, that the very bondage of the three realms is entirely his own doing, since he is able to transform every object of desire, thereby binding sentient beings to it. Now, deliberately retaining this worldly entanglement, they hold much the same view as he does — hence, "becoming their own companions." Confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view: not knowing that there exists, still, this further, unsurpassed bodhi, they lose this right knowledge and right view.

"Giving rise to this deviant, indulgent thought as the cause" means giving rise to this deviant thought of unrestrained desire, taking it as the causal mind. "Establishing this fruit of blazing worldly entanglement" means establishing this fruit of blazing, worldly entanglement, taking it as the fruit-ground awareness. "Straying far from perfect penetration" concerns this causal mind of fundamental practice; "turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa" concerns this fruit-ground awareness of quiescent cessation. Cultivating meditation without cutting off desire inevitably leads to falling onto the path of demons — hence, "the seed of a heavenly demon." This closes the eighth position, taking as truth what is, in fact, no truth at all.

9. Stopping at the Śrāvaka's Own Cessation 定性聲聞 — Within this bright understanding of life itself, they distinguish refined from coarse, resolving true from false; taking cause and effect to correspond to one another, they seek only this direct response, turning their back on the pure path — that is, precisely: seeing suffering, cutting off its origin, realizing cessation, cultivating the path — and, dwelling in this cessation, they simply rest there, unwilling to advance any further at all. Giving rise to a superior understanding of this, such a person falls among fixed-nature śrāvakas — falling in with monks of little learning and those caught in overreaching pride, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the ninth: this perfect, essential mind responding directly, accomplishing this fruit of resting content in quiescence — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to the seed of this emptiness-bound kind.

Commentary: This is the ninth, fixed-nature śrāvakas — understood following the pattern above. "Within this bright understanding of life itself" means: the aggregate of consciousness now laid bare, one is already able to penetrate this individual thread behind each being's own life, perfectly, clearly understood. And so they come to know that, within this aggregate of consciousness, both outflow-subject and outflow-free seeds alike are contained entirely, serving as the very basis for every ordinary being and noble one alike. And so, distinguishing refined from coarse: what is refined belongs to the noble stages, since these are subtle, transformational; what is coarse belongs to the ordinary stages, since these are plainly fragmentary. Resolving true from false: what is true belongs to the noble path, since it is genuinely cultivated and realized; what is false belongs to the non-Buddhist path, since it rests on annihilation or eternalism, both false. "Cause and effect correspond to one another" means: both worldly and transcendent phenomena, without exception, rely on cause to bring about effect, each corresponding to the other in turn. Wishing, then, to exchange this coarseness for this refinement, to relinquish this falseness and follow this truth, they seek, accordingly, only this direct response — "response" here meaning cultivation, "correspondence" meaning realization — seeking only this genuine cultivation and true realization, so as to transcend the three realms swiftly.

"Turning their back on the pure path" means turning away from the one-vehicle's own true reality, this pure path — since it doubly departs from the defilement of both extremes. "That is, precisely: seeing suffering, cutting off its origin, realizing cessation, cultivating the path" carries forward this same matter of seeking only this direct response: suffering and its origin are the cause and effect of the mundane world; cessation and the path are the cause and effect transcending it. Seeing that the mundane truth of suffering, this fruit of suffering, is entirely summoned by the truth of its origin, this cause of afflictive suffering; seeing that the transcendent truth of cessation, this fruit of bliss, is entirely brought about by the truth of the path, this cause of bliss — wearied of suffering, they cut off its origin; longing for cessation, they cultivate the path.

"Dwelling in this cessation, they simply rest there, advancing no further" means: dwelling in this bliss of the truth of cessation, nirvāṇa itself, their mind becomes fully satisfied, declaring to themselves that their work is done, birth and death already ended, no longer seeking to advance toward the great vehicle's own path at all — halting midway at this conjured city, never seeking the true treasure beyond it, satisfied with the smallest attainment. Giving rise to this superior understanding: toward the great vehicle cultivated by bodhisattvas, they give rise to not a single thought of delight or longing at all — such a person falls among fixed-nature śrāvakas. "Fixed-nature" means never giving rise to the mind that turns from the lesser toward the great — this is precisely the dull-faculty arhat. "Monks of little learning" refers to the fourth dhyāna's monk of little learning, and those caught in overreaching pride hold roughly this same view — hence, "becoming their own companions." Sinking into emptiness, mired in quiescence, they thereby become confused about the Buddha's own fruit, this path of bodhi; reducing the body to ash and extinguishing the intellect, they thereby lose this knowledge and vision belonging to the great vehicle.

"This perfect, essential mind responding directly" means: perfecting this exchange of coarseness for refinement, this causal mind seeking direct response, accomplishing this small fruit of sinking into emptiness, resting content in quiescence, this fixed nature. Failing to attain this causal mind of perfect penetration — hence, "straying far"; failing to attain this ultimate fruit of nirvāṇa — hence, "confused and turned away." "Giving rise to the seed of this emptiness-bound kind" means being permanently bound within emptiness, with no aspiration left to transcend it at all. This closes the ninth position, fixed-nature śrāvakas.

10. Stopping at the Pratyekabuddha's Own Tranquil Clarity 定性闢支 — Regarding this perfect fusion, pure awareness and brightness, refined and researched to its most profound, wondrous point, they establish this, at once, as nirvāṇa itself, and advance no further. Giving rise to a superior understanding of this, such a person falls among fixed-nature pratyekabuddhas — falling in with solitary realizers and those dependent on conditions who never turn their minds toward the greater vehicle, becoming their own companions, confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view. This is named the tenth: this perfect awakening in complete accord with the mind, accomplishing this fruit of translucent brightness — straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa, and giving rise to this awakened, perfectly bright, yet unconverted kind.

Commentary: This is the tenth, fixed-nature pratyekabuddhas — understood following the pattern above. Free interchange among the six sense-faculties is called "perfect fusion"; untouched by any defilement is called "pure"; illuminating this very root of life is called "aware and bright." "Refined and researched to its most profound, wondrous point" refers to two kinds of practitioners who apply their minds to this same research: the solitary realizer, dwelling alone in stillness, observing transformation, awakening without any teacher at all; and the one dependent on conditions, observing cause and condition, awakening to and understanding this nature's very absence of any fixed self — both of these attain a profound, wondrous awakening, and, on the basis of this very state of awakening, establish it as nirvāṇa itself, taking it to be the very place of return and rest.

Not knowing to seek, further, this truly unmoving ground of suchness, this site of quiescent cessation, and this perfectly fused ocean of nature, this unobstructed arising through conditions — hence, "advance no further." Giving rise to this superior understanding on this basis, such a person falls into fixed-nature pratyekabuddha-hood — "pratyekabuddha," a Sanskrit term, encompassing both the sense of "dependent on conditions" and "solitary realization." "Fixed-nature" refers to never advancing any further, precisely never turning their mind around — becoming their own equal companions and fellows. Confused about the Buddha's own bodhi, and losing this right knowledge and right view: confused about the Buddha's own fruit, this path of unsurpassed bodhi — this true treasure itself — and losing this fundamental mind of right knowledge and right view — this causal mind itself.

Pervading every kind universally, this awareness and knowing in complete accord — this is precisely the very realm of the aggregate of consciousness itself; taking this very state of awakening as the causal mind, they accomplish this fruit of translucent brightness. Stillness and quiet are named "translucent," accomplishing the solitary realizer's own fruit of spontaneous wisdom; thorough penetration is named "bright," accomplishing the one dependent on conditions' own fruit of ultimate non-arising. "Straying far from perfect penetration, turning one's back on the very city of nirvāṇa" means: perfect penetration relies on what neither arises nor ceases as its own causal mind, only afterward perfecting this fruit-ground cultivation and realization; here, though, relying instead on this state of awakening belonging to the aggregate of consciousness, they establish nirvāṇa at once, satisfied with the smallest attainment, halting midway at this conjured city — hence, "straying" and "turning one's back."

"Giving rise to this awakened, perfectly bright, yet unconverted kind": "awakened" refers precisely to the solitary realizer and the one dependent on conditions, giving rise to these two kinds of fruit-position; "perfectly bright" refers to this very realm of awakening and realization. The solitary realizer awakens without any teacher; the one dependent on conditions awakens to this arising through conditions, without any fixed self — and so they conclude that their own principle is perfect, their own wisdom bright, taking this to be the true realm of nirvāṇa itself; yet, unable to fuse through and pass entirely beyond it, what they have awakened to and cling to remains, in the end, nothing but an empty, pure, perfect reflection — still, in the end, nothing but this same fixed-nature kind.

Question: among the ten states of the aggregate of consciousness, the first eight belong to non-Buddhist teachers, heavenly beings, immortals, and demon-kings, mistakenly cultivating a false root, craving and clinging to worldly entanglement, never transcending the three realms, undergoing birth and death in vain — it stands to reason that these should be judged demonic. But śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, having cut off delusion and realized truth, have already transcended the three realms and put an end to birth and death — why, then, are they, too, judged demonic states, causing people to be afraid to cultivate at all? Should a dull-faculty practitioner encounter this teaching and lose the chance to be guided forward, how could this be the act of one possessed of true compassion? Answer: according to the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, forgetting the mind of bodhi while cultivating every wholesome practice is, in every case, Māra's own karma. How much more so, then, in this sūtra, which teaches the cultivation of this great Śūraṅgama samādhi, this perfect, complete, unsurpassed bodhi — for śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, satisfied with the smallest attainment, to halt midway at this conjured city — is this not, precisely, Māra's own karma indeed? This closes the second section, these ten intervening clingings.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, in each of these ten states, meditation goes astray midway through, deluded by relying on some particular condition, giving rise to a false sense of completion at a point that is not yet complete at all — all of it produced by the interaction of effort and the aggregate of consciousness, and so these particular positions arise."

Commentary: "These ten states in meditation" refers to passing through the ten states of the aggregate of formations without ever giving rise to any wrong clinging within the aggregate of consciousness — belonging, throughout, to this same wholesome state of meditation. "Meditation goes astray midway through, deluded by relying on some particular condition" means: not yet reaching perfect penetration, one strays midway down some forked path, as with the first eight positions above, each giving rise to its own wild interpretation, relying on this very inverted, wild view without any awareness of it — hence, "deluded." "Giving rise to a false sense of completion at a point that is not yet complete at all" means: not yet reaching quiescent cessation's own manifestation, one already gives rise to a sense of complete realization, resting content with this fruit, advancing no further, declaring oneself already satisfied — as with the latter two positions, the fixed-nature śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha. Examining the true reason for this, though, it is, in truth, no external demon coming to disturb them at all — it is entirely because the aggregate of consciousness, on the very verge of breaking through yet not yet broken through, has this mind applied impurely, false thought and right thought arising, interacting, one against the other. Whenever false thought happens to prevail, these particular positions arise — "these positions" being precisely these ten clingings described above.

Sūtra: "Beings, stubborn and confused, fail to measure themselves honestly; meeting these states appearing before them, each settles into whatever confused mind their own prior inclination favors, taking it as the ultimate resting place, and declaring themselves to have fulfilled the unsurpassed bodhi — committing the offense of great false speech. Whether drawn by non-Buddhist teachings or deviant demonic influence, once their allotted karma is exhausted, they fall into the unrelenting hell; śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas caught here likewise fail to advance any further."

Commentary: This is a warning, making the harm plain. Sentient beings, stubborn and without any real knowledge, confused and without any true understanding, fail to reflect and measure themselves honestly, and, meeting this state appearing before them, each takes hold of whatever they themselves favor. "Whatever confused mind their own prior inclination favors" refers to habituation accumulated over kalpas, a one-sided fondness for some deviant seed, this confused darkness of their own mind — and now, within samādhi, the state that manifests happens closely to resemble this same prior inclination, striking precisely at this old, festering root, provoking this same chronic affliction, and so they delightedly take hold of it, and rest content there. In the first eight positions, regarding the fruit they themselves have conceived, they take it to be the ultimate resting place, believing this to be the very place where body and life may finally settle — as with the first, this fruit of returning to a refuge, and the eighth, this fruit of blazing worldly entanglement. In the latter two positions, regarding the fruit they themselves have realized, they declare themselves to have already fulfilled unsurpassed bodhi, falsely proclaiming this to be the very ultimate realization — as with the ninth, this fruit of resting content in quiescence, and the tenth, this fruit of translucent brightness.

Yet all of these, claiming what has not been attained, claiming realization not actually reached, are said, thereby, to commit the offense of great false speech. Though all alike constitute false speech, the resulting harm differs in severity: the eight non-Buddhist and demonic positions, their karma of outflow-subject meditative merit eventually exhausted, will certainly fall into the unrelenting hell — this speaks of the graver harm. The two positions of the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha, what they presently realize being brought about by outflow-free cultivation, will certainly never fall into hell at all; they merely fail to advance any further, permanently shut within this conjured city, never reaching the true treasure beyond it — this speaks of the lesser harm. Though this harm differs in severity, still, within the teaching of perfect penetration, both alike constitute a demonic obstruction.

Sūtra: "You must hold fast to the Tathāgata's own path, and, after my passing, transmit this teaching throughout the age of the Dharma's decline, so that every being everywhere awakens to this meaning. Do not let a view-generated demon take root in one's own mind — instead protect, comfort, and rescue practitioners, dissolving deviant conditions at their source, and lead them, in both body and mind, into the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, so that they reach completion from the very start without meeting a single forked path."

Commentary: "You" — holding fast to this mind of great compassion, saving the world, upholding the Tathāgata's own path of awakening others — take this very teaching of distinguishing demons and, after the Tathāgata's own passing, when the true Dharma gradually declines and deviant teachings grow ever more fierce, transmit and proclaim it throughout this age of the Dharma's decline, so that every sentient being everywhere may awaken to this very meaning — "this meaning" being precisely this meaning of these ten distinct positions, meditation going astray midway through, and all the rest. Having already awakened to this clearly, this wild, inverted knowledge and view naturally subsides of itself.

The first seven belong to view; the eighth carries both view and craving together, since it deliberately retains worldly entanglement. The two vehicles, though their own view and craving within the three realms have been exhausted, still retain view and craving beyond the three realms: regarding nirvāṇa, they are confused about the truth, clinging instead to a mere resemblance of it; regarding the truths themselves, they are wearied of existence and attached to emptiness, failing to understand the emptiness of phenomena, seeking only their own benefit, and the like — all of this belonging to this same inverted, discriminating view. With none of these views, above, allowed to arise, hence, "do not let a view-generated demon take root of its own accord." "Of its own accord" makes plain that this is, in no way, an external demon coming to disturb them, but simply this view-demon within one's own mind, arising of its own accord. "Taking root" refers to a heavy, weighty offense — as with the non-Buddhist demonic teachers, their retribution finally exhausted and falling into hell; or the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha, permanently shut within this conjured city — both belong to this same case.

"Protect, comfort, and rescue practitioners, dissolving deviant conditions at their source": "comfort" here means to bring peace — preserving and protecting this samādhi, comforting and rescuing the practitioner, dissolving and extinguishing this very condition giving rise to wrong views. This deviant condition is precisely this same inverted, discriminating view and craving described above, entirely obstructing this true, correct knowledge and view. Now, this having been extinguished, this obstruction is exhausted and this principle made manifest, causing them, in both body and mind, naturally to enter into the Buddha's own knowledge and vision — and, from this point on, accomplishing perfect penetration, flowing straight into the very ocean of omniscience, all the way to buddhahood itself, meeting no forked path at all — precisely what is meant by: from beginning to end, throughout every stage, there is, in between, never any twisting or turning at all. This closes the second section, distinguishing the marks of these five demonic realms in detail — and, with it, this entire major division.

Sūtra: "This very teaching is the one by which countless buddhas, numerous as dust motes across kalpas as many as the sands of the Ganges in ages past, opened their own minds and attained the unsurpassed path."

Commentary: "This very" is a term pointing to this teaching. "This teaching" refers, immediately, to this teaching of distinguishing demons within the aggregate of consciousness, and, more broadly, encompasses the four aggregates before it as well. "In ages past, across kalpas as many as the sands of the Ganges" points, distantly, to many kalpas indeed. "Countless buddhas, numerous as dust motes" speaks, to the very utmost, of how many such buddhas there truly are — as it was said earlier: "this is the single path of nirvāṇa, walked by buddhas numerous as dust motes." "Opened their own minds through this" means: whenever any single aggregate has not yet broken open, one must rely on this very teaching to distinguish it properly, so as not to be misled by any of these ten demonic states — only thereby is one able to pass through. As with a single aggregate, so with every aggregate; and, once the aggregate of consciousness, too, is exhausted, one attains the unsurpassed path of buddhahood spontaneously, of its own accord!

Sūtra: "Once the aggregate of consciousness is exhausted, all your sense-faculties presently before you interchange freely with one another. From this free interchange, you become able to enter the bodhisattvas' own vajra-like dry wisdom. This perfect, bright, essential mind then unfolds its own transformation from within."

Commentary: "Once the aggregate of consciousness is exhausted" is about to bring this teaching to its very close, making plain this transcendent realization. All your sense-faculties presently before you interchange freely with one another — the very substance of these six faculties being, of course, perfectly fused, and the very function of these six faculties, too, no longer separated, each single faculty carrying within it the function of the other five as well — this corresponds to the perfect teaching's own first ground of abiding, the very stage of perfect penetration; the five aggregates already exhausted, all six knots untied, the three empty realizations transcended, this mark of arising-and-ceasing itself now extinguished, quiescent cessation manifesting before them.

"From this free interchange" means: precisely from within this first ground of abiding, one becomes, thereby, able to enter the bodhisattvas' own vajra-like dry wisdom — this "dry wisdom" being precisely this same ground of dry wisdom spoken of earlier, now already realized at this first abiding. The ear-faculty's own perfect penetration is named this vajra samādhi, this dry wisdom directly relying upon and being established through it — hence specially named "vajra." This is precisely transcending everything in a single leap, straight into the very final mind of equal enlightenment — briefly raising up, together, the very beginning and the very end. What follows, "this perfect, bright, essential mind unfolds its own transformation from within," briefly indicates what lies in between: pervading the entire dharma-realm is called "perfect"; illuminating without any boundary is called "bright" — as an earlier verse states, "every bodhisattva presently existing now each enters this same perfect brightness" — this being precisely the very substance of perfect penetration, hence called this "essential mind," meaning this pure, true, wondrous mind free of any falseness whatsoever. "From within" means: from within this same span between the first ground of abiding and equal enlightenment, spiritual powers and transformations are given rise to — exactly as Avalokiteśvara attained these two supreme excellences, and gave rise to these three wondrous functions.

Sūtra: "Like a precious moon held within pure, clear lapis lazuli. In just this way you pass beyond the ten faiths, the ten abidings, the ten practices, the ten transferences, the four aids to penetration, the bodhisattvas' own vajra-like ten grounds, and equal enlightenment's own perfect brightness."

Commentary: The first two clauses illustrate this with a metaphor: lapis lazuli is a metaphor for this perfect, bright, essential mind; the moon held within it is a metaphor for this transformation unfolding from within. That is: this perfect, bright, essential mind, its very nature endowed with spiritual transformation, unhindered in giving full expression to its own function, illuminating both near and far thoroughly. Speaking in terms of awakening and realization, this is already equal to the fruit of buddhahood itself.

"In just this way you pass beyond the ten faiths, the ten abidings" and so on means: this first ground of abiding is what does the transcending, and every intervening stage is what is transcended; what follows, "entering the Tathāgata's own ocean of wondrous adornment," likewise takes this first ground of abiding as what enters, and this ocean of wondrous adornment as what is entered. That the ten faiths, too, are transcended here shows plainly that, throughout this sūtra, these ten faiths are, in fact, opened out from this very first ground of abiding itself. Speaking specifically of "what bodhisattvas practice" at the level of the grounds indicates that entering the grounds is precisely the truly cultivated, noble stage itself. All are cultivated and severed through this same sharp, vajra-like wisdom — hence called the "vajra-like ten grounds," distinct from the "vajra" spoken of earlier at the level of dry wisdom: that earlier reference pointed to the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration, the common reliance of every stage, and so was placed at the very beginning of all the stages; this present reference makes plain this vajra-like, firm, sharp wisdom found at the level of the grounds themselves, able to cut off even the subtlest, finest ignorance — hence placed at the very head of the ten grounds.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: why, at equal enlightenment, does the text again speak of "perfect brightness"? Because, from beginning to end, this is nothing but this same single mind throughout, and, reaching all the way to equal enlightenment, this is the very utmost point of this unfolding transformation. According to Tiantai teaching, those of sharp faculties within the perfect teaching can, in a single lifetime, transcend directly into the tenth ground; according to Qingliang, understanding and practice, both present within oneself, can, in a single lifetime, perfect the fruit of countless kalpas — both transcending from this same first ground of abiding. For this first ground of abiding is named "the abiding where the mind is first given rise to" — and, by this same reasoning, from the very first arising of this mind, perfect, correct awakening is already accomplished. Some older commentaries hold that this transcending begins from the seventh faith instead — this I would not presume to accept.

On the whole, examining the perfect teaching in detail, there are, in truth, only two stages: first, cutting off the general delusion shared with the stages before it, from the very completion of contemplative practice, transcending in a single leap directly into this first ground of abiding, with no further realization sought in between; second, cutting off the further, particular delusion that follows, from entering this first ground of abiding, transcending in a single leap all the way to equal enlightenment, likewise with no further realization sought in between. Yet the Buddha, within the perfect teaching, still lists out these many separate stages — for two reasons: first, to draw in those of gradual capacity, so that they might come to delight in and follow this sudden, perfect teaching instead; second, because the Buddha's own eye, so bright and penetrating, is able to see and analyze even what is most swift and sudden in its own right. It is like a falcon soaring up a mountainside — however swift its ascent may be, it nonetheless passes, of itself, from lower to higher in sequence; only one whose own eye is dull could never see this clearly enough to analyze it — hence the need for a buddha's own eye to do so.

Sūtra: "Entering at last the Tathāgata's own ocean of wondrous adornment — perfect, complete bodhi, arriving finally at a place where there is nothing further to attain at all!"

Commentary: Entering, at last, this ultimate fruit of the Tathāgata himself, perfect to the very utmost. "This ocean of wondrous adornment" means: this ocean of fruit, adorned with ten thousand virtues, each brought to its own utmost wondrous perfection — this being the very consummation of blessed merit. "Perfect, complete bodhi" means: realizing this wisdom of all modes, perfect and complete, with nothing whatsoever left over — this being the very consummation of wisdom. "Arriving finally at a place where there is nothing further to attain at all" means: according perfectly with this true nature, originally already possessed, never attained from anything outside oneself at all — this being the very consummation of principle itself.

The two clauses above show what is accomplished through cultivation; the last clause shows what is inherent in this nature itself — for cultivation arises out of this nature, and this cultivation, in turn, accords with this same nature once more; apart from this true nature, there is not the slightest phenomenon left to be attained at all! As it was said earlier: "this wild nature subsides of itself; and this very subsiding is bodhi itself, this supreme, pure, bright mind, never once attained from any other person at all." This closes the first section, showing this transcendent realization.

Sūtra: "This is the detailed analysis, made by every past buddha and World-Honored One within śamatha and vipaśyanā, of these subtle demonic affairs."

Commentary: This raises up the ancient buddhas' own teaching of distinguishing demons, this essential heart of the teaching handed down and received by every past buddha, cultivating and advancing this great samādhi, progressing through every sagely stage. Within this śamatha of stilled samādhi, applying this contemplative wisdom of vipaśyanā, samādhi and wisdom held in equal measure, this beginning-awakening wisdom, within the causal stage, growing bright, they analyze these subtle demonic affairs. These past buddhas are those who have already traveled this same road before — as the ancient saying goes: wishing to know the road down the mountain, one need only ask someone who has already traveled it — and one will naturally never go astray. What is spoken here, then, follows nothing but the very model set down by these past buddhas themselves.

Sūtra: "When a demonic state appears, if you are able to recognize it clearly, the defilements of your mind are washed away, and you do not fall into wrong views. The demons rooted in your own aggregates dissolve; heavenly demons are shattered; powerful ghosts and spirits lose their nerve and flee; mountain goblins and nature-spirits find no further opening to arise at all. You proceed straight on to bodhi, lacking nothing whatsoever. Even those of lesser capacity continue to advance, their minds never confused or troubled regarding the great nirvāṇa."

Commentary: Should one genuinely be able to act as I have taught, then, whenever every demonic state appears before you, you will be able, thereby, to recognize it clearly. Though the summoning of a demon comes about through samādhi, its true cause lies, in fact, within the mind. One need only ensure that this mind never gives rise to any superior understanding of it, and this mind's own defilement is thereby washed away — the master no longer confused, this demonic affair can do nothing to you at all, and you will, of yourself, never fall into this net of wrong views. This directly instructs how to clearly recognize every demon.

"The demons rooted in your own aggregates" are precisely these two demons of mind and view; through this mind's own defilement washed away, these aggregate-demons dissolve. This inner demon already dissolved, the heavenly demon without, too, ought naturally to have its own courage shattered. Powerful ghosts and spirits, seeing that even their own king has been shattered thus, ought naturally to lose their own nerve and flee, twelve yojanas away; mountain goblins and nature-spirits, every lesser spirit besides, ought naturally to vanish and hide their own traces, finding no further opening to arise at all.

Everything above first raises up the ancient buddhas' own teaching of distinguishing demons — thus, with both inner and outer demons alike dissolved together, one is able to transcend every stage, straight through to the very fruit of unsurpassed bodhi's own wisdom. And though able to transcend in this way, still, regarding the merit belonging to every intervening stage, nothing at all remains lacking — precisely what is meant by: awakening to one truth is awakening to every truth at once; realizing one truth is realizing every truth at once. Even those of the lesser two vehicles are able to turn from the lesser toward the great, and press forward with resolve to advance. Regarding the Tathāgata's own realized fruit, this great nirvāṇa free of any remainder, their mind is no longer confused or troubled — they, too, reach this true treasure at last!

Sūtra: "But if, in the final age, there are beings dull and slow of understanding, who do not yet recognize true meditation or know how to explain the Dharma, yet still love to cultivate samādhi — fearing they may fall into deviant paths without realizing it, urge them with your whole heart to hold the dhāraṇī of the Buddha's own crown. Even if they cannot yet recite it, let them write it out and keep it in the meditation hall, or wear it on the body — and no demon whatsoever will be able to move them."

Commentary: This, in turn, instructs practitioners to rely on the mantra for protection, specifically because, in this age of the Dharma's decline, the age of the sages recedes ever further into the past, giving ample cause for deep concern. How much more so, then, for beings foolish, confused, dim, and dull, their wisdom unclear and their faculties unsharp: through this unclear wisdom, they fail to recognize the distinct states found within meditation; through these unsharp faculties, they fail to know the Buddha's own essential teaching for distinguishing demons — both their own strength and the strength of the Dharma alike lacking. And yet they still love to cultivate this samādhi of the ear-faculty, directly cultivating this samādhi of turning the hearing inward.

"Fearing they may fall into deviant paths" means: fearing that, unable to recognize a demonic state clearly, they may, by mistake, enter this deviant net, becoming, thereby, one with this deviance itself. And so Ānanda ought, of himself, to urge them, with his whole heart, to hold this dhāraṇī of the Buddha's own crown — needing no other technique at all, but only to devote themselves to holding this mantra alone. Since this mantra is called the "Buddha's crown," it is the very most venerable, most supreme teaching of all; and, further called a "dhāraṇī," it gathers together every phenomenon entirely, holding within it boundless meaning — every phenomenon and every meaning alike contained within it.

Should someone lack the disposition for reciting it aloud, and be unable to recite it, let them write it out instead, keeping it in the meditation hall, or wherever they dwell, or wear it upon their own body — "and no demon whatsoever will be able to move them," since this mantra is constantly accompanied by the lineage of Vajragarbha bodhisattva-kings, together with their own retinue, attending day and night without fail.

Sūtra: "You should hold in the deepest reverence this final teaching, the ultimate guidance for cultivation and advancement handed down by every Tathāgata of the ten directions."

Commentary: As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: having already shown this teaching for distinguishing demons, and, further, extended this concluding exhortation, the Buddha, at the very last, teaches, once more: "you should hold this in deepest reverence" — the Tathāgata's own repeated, earnest exhortation could not be more deeply felt than this! Teaching this reverence carries two meanings: first, this is the ultimate teaching for cultivation and advancement, handed down by every Tathāgata of the ten directions, from beginning to end, and ought to be reverently received and cultivated — this charges Ānanda to rely on this teaching, and thereby accomplish this practice benefiting himself. Second, this is the very model left behind by every Tathāgata of the ten directions, out of compassion for this final age yet to come, and ought to be reverently received and proclaimed widely — this charges Ānanda to rely on this instruction, and thereby accomplish this practice benefiting others.

As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: "this final teaching" means: since Ānanda, the one to whom this teaching is directly addressed, so earnestly and sincerely requested it, every Tathāgata of the ten directions, in accomplishing bodhi, relies on this wondrous śamatha, samādhi, and dhyāna as their very first, initial expedient means — and so the Tathāgata caused him to give rise to faith and open his own understanding, all the way through showing him the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha, and the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration, correct cultivation and auxiliary cultivation practiced together, giving rise to actual practice and entering into realization — and, with this, the task belonging to this first, initial expedient means was already accomplished. Yet the Buddha turned back once more, drawing near to his own seat again, detailing once more the seven destinies, so as to spur on this diligent cultivation, and, further, distinguishing these five demonic states, so as to guard against confusion and disorder — this being, with respect to samādhi belonging to one's own nature, shown to the beginning practitioner as this very model for cultivation and practice, coming, then, at the very last, after everything else — hence, "this final teaching," corresponding directly to this "first, initial expedient means" spoken of earlier. This closes the first major division, the Buddha's unprompted, unasked teaching on the demonic states of the five aggregates.

The Buddha tells Ānanda: is there truly no reason at all why I say that all five aggregates, without exception, take false thought as their own root? "The essential, true, wondrously bright original awakening, perfectly pure of itself" means: pure and unmixed, utterly true and free of any falseness — this refers to this pure, true mind's own very substance. "Wondrous" here means quiescence; "bright" means illumination — this refers to this quiescence and illumination fused together as one, this very function. Named "original awakening" precisely to distinguish it from anything cultivated into being; specifically called "perfectly pure" meaning filled entirely with purity, admitting nothing else within it at all.

"Retaining nothing of birth and death" means having no fragmentary birth-and-death within the three realms at all; "any worldly defilement" means having no transcendent nirvāṇa realized beyond the three realms either — as it was said earlier: "the mark of thought is dust; consciousness is defilement; depart from both entirely, and your own Dharma-eye ought, at that very moment, to become perfectly clear." "Even empty space itself" abbreviates and skips over worlds and sentient beings, reaching, in the end, all the way to empty space itself — as it was said earlier: "once the bubble bursts, this emptiness was, itself, never truly there at all — how much less, then, these three states of existence!" In sum: birth and death, nirvāṇa, every sentient being and world, all the way to empty space itself, arise, in every case, because of false thought. For fragmentary birth-and-death within the three realms is precisely sensation, perception, and formations, these three aggregates; transformational nirvāṇa beyond the three realms is precisely the aggregate of consciousness; and dependent and direct retribution, all the way to empty space itself, is precisely the aggregate of form. Yet, within this original, wondrously bright mind of awakening, there was, originally, never any of these five aggregates at all — as the Heart Sūtra states: "within emptiness there is no form, no sensation, perception, formations, or consciousness" — originally absent within this true emptiness, this real mark of things.

Sūtra: "It is this same original awakening, wondrously bright, essentially true, that falsely gives rise to every world of physical containers, exactly as Yajñadatta, in his confusion, mistook his own reflection for his lost head."

Commentary: This offers a metaphor showing that this falseness has no real existence at all. "This same" refers precisely to these five aggregates, all arising from false thought alone. "Original awakening" refers precisely to this originally possessed nature of awakening; "wondrously bright, essentially true" refers precisely to this single, true dharma-realm — meant to make plain that substance and function are fused together entirely. Since this is, originally, nothing but this single truth, how, then, could these five aggregates exist at all?

It is only that a single thought moves falsely, and becomes this karmic consciousness; depending on this movement, this becomes able to perceive, giving rise to this seeing-portion; depending on this ability to perceive, this objective realm falsely manifests, giving rise to this appearing-portion; and, once these two portions, seeing and appearing, are present, this gives rise, in turn, to both the world of sentient beings and the world of physical containers. In just this way, emptiness and seeing, undistinguished, give rise, together, to the aggregate of form and the turbidity of the aeon; this nature grasping at the four elements gives rise, together, to the aggregate of sensation and the turbidity of view; faculty and object, mutually perceived, give rise, together, to the aggregate of perception and the turbidity of affliction; knowledge and views wishing to remain, karmic movement constantly shifting, give rise, together, to the aggregate of formations and the turbidity of sentient existence; this nature knowing itself within, this function turning away from itself without, give rise, together, to the aggregate of consciousness and the turbidity of life-span. All of this, in sum, because of failing to understand that everything is mind alone, applying, instead, this false thought again and again, falsely forming, in turn, these five aggregates. Hence: exactly as Yajñadatta, in his confusion, mistook his own reflection for his lost head.

Yajñadatta's confusion about his own head serves as a metaphor for sentient beings failing to understand that everything is mind alone; his madly chasing after this mere reflection serves as a metaphor for sentient beings applying this false thought throughout — for, in the end, truth is originally, always present, while falseness is originally, always empty. Because sentient beings fail to know that truth is originally present, they are confused and believe it lost — exactly as Yajñadatta's own head was, in fact, always there, yet he falsely panicked at its supposed loss. Failing to penetrate the fact that falseness is, at root, empty, they mistakenly take it to be real — exactly as Yajñadatta's own reflection was never real at all, yet he mistook it for his true head. This is meant to show that these five aggregates are, from the very root, false and unreal. This closes the first section, tracing this falseness back to its very origin, empty from the start.

Sūtra: "Falseness has no cause of its own to begin with; it is only within false thought that the very principle of causation is established. And one who, in turn, remains confused about causation itself calls whatever remains simply 'spontaneous.' Yet even that quality of emptiness is itself already something falsely conjured — both 'caused' and 'spontaneous' are nothing but calculations made by the deluded minds of sentient beings."

Commentary: As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: the reason these two positions must, necessarily, both be refuted here is this — these five aggregates, from the very outset, arise from false thought, and, though seemingly constant, are, in truth, without any real existence at all; yet, in the end, they persist by relying on this same inverted speculation, and, though seemingly nonexistent, are, in truth, constantly present all the same — precisely what is meant by arising from utter nothingness, yet becoming, in the end, a kind of real existence. And so, unless these two speculations are set aside, these five aggregates can never be broken through at all — this is exactly why the Tathāgata, wishing to dig out the very root of this false thought, first refutes the very speculation upon which it depends.

"Falseness has no cause of its own to begin with" carries forward what came before, this false arising: originally possessing nothing at all, originally without any cause whatsoever — as it was said earlier, "were there truly some cause behind it, how could it still be called false?" And so one knows that these five aggregates, present before us now, are nothing but false thought itself. Since this falseness arises, one from another, in turn, each becoming, successively, the seed of the next, those who study within the Buddhist tradition establish, on this basis, this principle of cause and condition — hence, "it is only within false thought that the very principle of causation is established" — yet this, too, is nothing more than a skillful means, without any ultimate, real meaning of its own; how much more so, then, this wrong view held by non-Buddhist traditions, denying cause and effect entirely — and, further confused about causation itself, calling these five aggregates, instead, spontaneous by nature.

In truth, both "caused" and "spontaneous" are nothing but idle theorizing, and so the text raises up the metaphor of empty space itself, to illustrate this by comparison: this very nature of empty space, though it seems unmoving and indestructible, is, in fact, itself something illusory and false, arising from delusion — as it was said earlier, "delusion falsely gives rise to empty space; depending on this emptiness, a world is established." How much more so, then, both "caused" and "spontaneous," these two speculations, both nothing but the deluded minds of sentient beings, this inverted discrimination, this false, arbitrary calculation, without any real meaning whatsoever.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, one who knows the very place where falseness arises may speak of this falseness in terms of cause and condition. But should falseness, in fact, have no such origin at all, then to speak of this falseness in terms of cause and condition is, itself, already speaking of what is entirely nonexistent — how much more so, then, one who, not even knowing this much, goes further still, and infers mere spontaneity instead?"

Commentary: And further, Ānanda: should you, in truth, be able to know the very place from which this false thought arises, you might, then, speak of this false thought in terms of cause and condition — this is granted, provisionally, for the sake of argument. But should this false thought, in fact, have no place of arising to be found at all, its very substance entirely empty, then to speak of this false thought's own cause and condition is, itself, already speaking of what is entirely nonexistent to begin with — this, then, takes away even this provisional grant entirely. Causation itself, within the Buddhist tradition, belongs to the lesser vehicle, held to be an incomplete teaching, not the ultimate truth — how much more so, then, an outside tradition, which does not even understand causation in the first place, and wrongly infers mere spontaneity instead — this being falseness compounded upon falseness, all the more so still. This closes the second section, judging this inverted speculation to be mistaken.

Sūtra: "This is exactly why the Tathāgata reveals to you that the root cause of all five aggregates alike is false thought."

Commentary: Because both of these speculations, causation and spontaneity, are alike mistaken, the Tathāgata reveals to you that the very root cause underlying the covering weight of these five aggregates, though showing five distinct differences — solid, empty-bright, and the rest — is, in every case, nothing but false thought, with nothing else behind it at all. This is precisely the Tathāgata's own intent: wishing sentient beings to understand that this falseness has no real cause at all, and to know that this truth does, indeed, have its own real basis.

For the Buddha, ever since fascicle 1, in generally judging sentient beings' own mistaken recognition, stated: "every sentient being, since beginningless time, has, in every case, failed to know this constantly abiding, true mind, this substance of nature, pure and bright" — this being confusion about truth — "and applies, instead, this false thought throughout" — this being clinging to falseness — "this thought not being true, and so the turning wheel of rebirth results." And so, taking up these same two words, "false thought," and unfolding them layer upon layer, reaching, at last, to these five aggregates, expounding these five kinds of false thought as their very root, dividing this into five distinct layers, explained one by one in full detail — one thereby sees this single, grandmotherly heart of compassion, teaching others, this utmost intent carried through from beginning to end. And, further, one sees, throughout this sūtra's own ten fascicles of text, this thread connecting its opening to its close, its very momentum like a dragon curving back upon itself, beginning and end attending to one another throughout. This closes the first section, setting out the very cause of speaking of this false thought.

The Five Layers of False Thought 辛二 詳示五重妄想

Chinese Source — First: The Aggregate of Form 堅固第一妄想

〖經〗汝體先因，父母想生，汝心非想，則不能來，想中傳命。

〖講〗此別示五重妄想，先明色陰妄想，五根六塵，皆為色陰，今特顯其與妄想相應者，且就內身五根而言。我雖然為汝發明，五陰本因，汝猶不知，色陰為堅固妄想。故云，汝之形體，有質礙可見者，先因父母，俱動愛欲妄想而生。愛欲妄想動，而後有赤白二渧，若汝中陰心中，非有憎愛之想，自不能攬為自體。前云流愛為種，納想成胎，務必父母與己，三想感應和合，而來想中，傳續命根也。

〖經〗如我先言：心想醋味，口中涎生；心想登高，足心酸起；懸崖不有，醋物未來，汝體必非，虛妄通倫，口水如何，因談醋出？

〖講〗此引喻顯妄。如我先於想陰文中，曾言二喻，今復引之也。心想醋味，口中涎生；心想登高，足心酸起；然懸崖不有，醋物未來。但憑虛想，而口水足酸，虛妄而應。而汝現前身體，必非與虛妄，通為一類者，口水如何因談醋出？足酸如何因思崖生？此句影略也。是知口水足酸，既然由想而生，汝體虛妄，亦應同於口水足酸也。現前色陰既爾，而前之根本色陰，例此可知。

〖經〗是故當知，汝現色身，名為堅固，第一妄想。

〖講〗是故者：是汝體口水，虛妄通倫之故。應當得知，汝現在色身之體，名為堅固，第一妄想：謂取著有力，堅固而不可解也。如父母交遘，欲愛妄想，與自己投胎，流愛妄想，其堅固有力，自不待言矣！《正脈》問：內根固然，若兼外器，何關妄想？答：如前世界相續中，言堅明立礙，及堅覺寶成等，亦堅固妄想也。初色陰妄想竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Your body first arose because of your parents' own false thought; had your own mind not itself been a kind of thought, it could never have come to receive this life within that same thought at all."

Commentary: This shows each of these five layers of false thought individually, beginning with the aggregate of form's own false thought. The five sense-faculties and six sense-objects together compose the aggregate of form; here, though, the text specifically brings out its correspondence with false thought, speaking, for now, only in terms of the five faculties within the body. Though I have already revealed to you the very root cause of these five aggregates, you still do not know that the aggregate of form is precisely this solid false thought. And so it is said: your own bodily form, possessing material substance, visible to the eye, first arose because your parents' own false thought of desire and craving stirred into motion. This false thought of desire and craving having stirred, the red and white drops then came to be; had your own mind, within the intermediate state, not itself harbored this thought of aversion and attraction, it could never have grasped hold of this as its own body at all. As it was said earlier: this flowing desire becomes the very seed, and this thought received becomes the very embryo — necessarily requiring these three thoughts, belonging to both parents and oneself, to respond to and merge with one another, and it is precisely within this thought that this life-root comes to be transmitted and continued.

Sūtra: "Just as I have said before: thinking of something sour brings saliva to the mouth; thinking of standing at a height makes the sole of the foot ache — even though no cliff-edge and no actual sour fruit are anywhere present. If your own body were not, in truth, of this same insubstantial, false kind, how could mere talk of something sour produce real saliva in the mouth?"

Commentary: This offers a metaphor to make this falseness plain. Just as I once spoke, earlier, within the text on the aggregate of perception, of these same two metaphors — now they are raised once more. Thinking of something sour brings saliva to the mouth; thinking of standing at a height makes the sole of the foot ache — and yet no cliff-edge and no actual sour fruit are anywhere present at all. Relying on nothing but this empty thought alone, this saliva and this ache in the foot respond nonetheless, this false thought producing a real effect. And your own present body must, therefore, be of this exact same insubstantial, false kind — otherwise, how could mere talk of something sour produce real saliva in the mouth at all? How could merely thinking of a cliff-edge produce this real ache in the foot? (This clause is left implicit, echoing the one before it.) One thereby knows that, since this saliva and this ache in the foot both arise from mere thought, your own body, too, being equally insubstantial and false, ought to be exactly the same as this saliva and this ache in the foot. Since this holds true for the present aggregate of form, the very root, original aggregate of form, too, may be understood by this same example.

Sūtra: "For this very reason, you should know that your own present body of form is named the solid, first false thought."

Commentary: "For this very reason" refers to this same reason — your own body being of this same insubstantial, false kind as this saliva. You should, thereby, know that your own present body of form is named the solid, first false thought — meaning that this clinging attachment is forceful, solid, and impossible to dissolve. Just as the parents' own union arises from this false thought of desire and craving, and one's own entry into the womb arises from this false thought of flowing desire — how solid and forceful these truly are needs hardly be said at all!

As the Standard Meaning Commentary asks: this is understandable enough for the inner faculties — but what has this to do with false thought regarding the outer, physical world as well? Answer: as was said earlier, within the passage on the world's own continuity, "this solidity, this brightness, establishes obstruction" and "this solid awareness accomplishes treasure" and the like — these, too, are exactly this same solid false thought. This closes the first section, the aggregate of form's own false thought.

Chinese Source — Second Through Fifth

〖經〗即此所說，臨高想心，能令汝形，真受酸澀。由因受生，能動色體，汝今現前，順益違損，二現驅馳，名為虛明，第二妄想。

〖講〗此明受陰，即虛明妄想也。即此色陰中所說前喻，臨高虛想之心，以發起受陰者。想以取像為義，受以領納為義，前色陰即五根，此受陰即五識，而想陰即第六意識。臨高想心，即六識，曾經懸崖險處，落卸影子，在想心中。今聞懸崖，則起臨高虛想之心，能令汝之形體，真受酸澀。受酸澀處，即受陰，於三受中，是苦受，所謂轉想成受也。懸崖不有，想雖無實，而酸澀忽形，受乃是真；如是諸受，皆可例知其妄也。由想心為因，所以受陰生起，能動色陰之形體，真受酸澀之妄境。此由因受生句，合臨高想心；能動色體之句，合能令汝形，真受酸澀。汝今現前，正示受陰也。所謂順之則益，即樂受；違之則損，即苦受。二現驅馳者：即苦樂二者，現在能驅役自心，馳流不息。文中不言不苦不樂受者，因捨受不顯，苦樂二受，現前分別，容易明白也。溫陵曰：臨高空想，而酸澀真發，違順皆妄，而損益現馳，則受陰無體，虛有所明，故名為虛明，第二妄想。二受陰妄想竟。

〖經〗由汝念慮使汝色身，身非念倫，汝身何因，隨念所使，種種取像，心生形取，與念相應。寤即想心，寐為諸夢，則汝想念，搖動妄情，名為融通，第三妄想。

〖講〗此明想陰，即融通妄想，全以想陰之虛，能使色身之實，虛實相應，以見想陰之妄也。由汝念慮者：念慮，即想陰也，亦即前所謂，浮想是也。謂由於汝之第六意識，想念與思慮，所以能使役汝之現前色身。而身是色法，念是心法，色法本非心法同一倫類；既非念類，不應隨念，故難以汝身之色法，何因緣故，隨心法之念所使耶？即今現前，根塵相對，種種取像，想陰以取像為義，然皆因想念心生，而後諸根之形方取；則知根身，所取之像，必定與想念而相應也。此約體通五識，故能令色身，時時與念慮相應；前難云：隨念所使者此也。《寶鏡疏》取喻如歌舞工伎之人，隨他拍轉，拍緩則步緩，拍急則步急，而身與念，亦若是矣。寤即想心，寐為諸夢者：寤即醒也，寐即睡也。醒時即是想心，乃通於散位獨頭；睡時即為亂夢，又通於夢中獨頭。不言定中獨頭者，以此經三摩，非思惟影像之定，獨頭不起現行故。則汝身汝想，無時無處，而不相應；良由汝之想念不息，以故搖動妄情，或寤或寐，紛燃無間也。則知想陰，與前五根，並五識，互融互通，為第三妄想。不惟互融色身，隨念所使，而且互通夢寐，搖動妄情。則知此陰，雙融色心兩處，俱通寤寐兩境，故名融通妄想也。三想陰妄想竟。

〖經〗化理不住，運運密移，甲長髮生，氣銷容皺，日夜相代，曾無覺悟。阿難！此若非汝，云何體遷，如必是真，汝何無覺？則汝諸行，念念不停，名為幽隱，第四妄想。

〖講〗此明行陰，即幽隱妄想也。化理不住者：指行陰為變化之理體，有遷流之事用，體屬生滅，故云不住。運運密移者：謂念念遷變，秘密推移，表其幽深隱微之動相。《莊子》喻以夜壑負舟，正此密移之意；但彼謂造化，此言行陰也。此下乃釋密移之事，能遷實體。意謂行陰，生、住、異、滅，四相遷流，念念不停，能遷實體；如初生之時，甲漸漸長，及其壯也，髮漸漸生，逮乎垂老之年，氣漸漸銷，容漸漸皺，其誰使之耶？殆行陰為之。日夜相代，曾無覺悟者：此皆日夜生、住、異、滅，無有少停，互相更代，從古及今，曾無一人，能覺悟也。波斯匿王云：變化密移，我誠不覺者此也。此雙詰是非，顯體虛妄。故呼阿難，此遷流之行陰，若果非汝心者，云何能遷變汝之實體耶？以見不非汝也。如必此遷流之行陰，是真汝心者，汝何不念念覺知耶？以見不即汝也。是汝非汝，兩不可定，足知虛妄非真矣！幽隱者：幽深隱微。則汝諸行：指現在行陰，念念遷流，不得停住。《仁王般若經》云：一念之中，有九十剎那，一剎那中，有九百生滅，其幽隱難知，是非莫辨，徹體虛妄，故以妄想名之。又以密移不覺故，冠幽隱之號。《正脈》云：大抵受、想、行之三陰，雖皆屬心，而文中皆顯與色身通貫；受則能令色身領境，想則能驅使於身，行則能遷變乎體。又雖說三陰通貫色身，而實要顯身為念倫，非真實有也。四行陰妄想竟。

〖經〗又汝精明，湛不搖處，名恆常者，於身不出，見、聞、覺、知，若實精真，不容習妄。何因汝等，曾於昔年，睹一奇物，經歷年歲，憶忘俱無？於後忽然，覆睹前異，記憶宛然，曾不遺失，則此精了，湛不搖中，念念受熏，有何籌算？阿難當知：此湛非真，如急流水，望如恬靜，流急不見，非是無流，若非想元，寧受妄習。非汝六根，互用開合，此之妄想，無時得滅。故汝現在，見、聞、覺、知，中串習幾，則湛了內，罔象虛無，第五顛倒，微細精想。

〖講〗此明識陰，即微細精想也。純一無雜，橫豎洞照，曰精明，所謂似一也。浮想已盡，遷擾俱停，日湛不搖處，所謂似常也。即此似一似常，元是根本識陰，體通如來藏性，眾生迷位之中，離此無別真體可得，第一卷云：識精元明是也。若以此精明不搖之識，為恆常不變之性者，於身不出見、聞、覺、知，所謂元依一精明，分成六和合也。此固以根本難知，指出現前六用，令凡小之人，知現前見、聞、覺、知，與識精同體故。若實精一無雜，真實無妄者，自不容習種之妄染；譬如真金，不應混雜泥沙也。何因反難之詞。既云若實精真，不容習妄，下正明習妄之事，故反難云：何因緣之故，汝等曾於疇昔之年，睹一奇異之物，既見奇物，必先留心，經歷年深歲久，初時猶憶，久則斯忘，久之又久，則憶忘俱無。於後忽然，覆睹前異者：於後來忽然之間，覆睹從前奇異之物，而記憶宛然如昔日。由前六熏習之力，熏成種子，在八識田中，曾不遺失，故以何因反難之。六識如聚斂之吏，七識似出納之官，八識猶庫藏之使，故知此論收執不忘，惟約第八也。理實此識，尚能憶持多劫，無量種習，次第成熟，豈止現生之睹物耶？則此下，顯妄非真，精了即精明也，亦即現前見、聞、覺、知，同一無分別性，名湛不搖中。念念受熏，有何籌算者：前念後念，受彼妄習所熏，無有停息，分劑頭數，無量無邊，有何可籌量計算也。阿難！應當了知，此八識，湛不搖者，熏之以真則真，熏之以妄則妄，亦非真常不可動搖之性也。但如急流水，《正脈》云：須取無波，平流之急水。望如恬安也靜者：以其無波之參差，無飛湍之上下也。次二句，明其正因流急，故不可見，非真無流也。嘗驗其流，拋一草藥，於其水面，草藥迅疾而去，方覺其流之最急，非無流也。若非想元者：謂此若非前四陰，妄想根元，寧受妄習所熏，此決言其猶有微細妄想故也。然此微細妄想，直待何時，方得銷滅？除非汝之六根，互用之時，根隔開合之際，此之妄想，亦無時而得滅也。但經用反言以顯，故云：若非根解入圓通，此妄終無滅時也。六根互用開合，正當寂滅現前時也。此承上文，未得六根互用，未盡想元，故汝現在，見、聞、嗅、嘗、覺、知六精之性，即第八識中，念念受熏，互相串穿。雖習氣幾微，令不散失，則湛然了知之內，即精明湛不搖中，一分無明為能串，而六根習幾為所串耳。故罔象虛無，若無不無，似有非有，凡夫計為命根，二乘認作涅槃，虛而作實，無而為有，是為顛倒，微細精想。謂迷真執似，迷真如藏性，執相似藏識，豈非顛倒乎？前四麤，顯此識微細，又此識雖非妙精明心，如第二月，故名精想，但多一捏而已，放手即是真月矣！二詳示五重妄想竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second: The Aggregate of Sensation 虛明第二妄想

Sūtra: "This same thought of standing at a height, just described, is able to make your own body genuinely undergo an aching soreness. Because this sensation arises through this thought, it is able to move this very body of form; and this same driving back and forth, right now, between what feels beneficial when accorded with and harmful when opposed, is named the empty, bright, second false thought."

Commentary: This makes plain the aggregate of sensation — precisely this empty, bright false thought. Entirely on the basis of this emptiness belonging to the aggregate of perception, able to move this substance belonging to the aggregate of form, empty and substantial responding to one another, one thereby sees the falseness of the aggregate of sensation as well. This same earlier metaphor, given within the discussion of the aggregate of form — this thought of standing at a height, empty in itself — gives rise, here, to the aggregate of sensation. "Perception" carries the meaning of taking on an image; "sensation" carries the meaning of receiving and taking something in. The aggregate of form, discussed before, is precisely these five sense-faculties; this aggregate of sensation is precisely these five sense-consciousnesses; and the aggregate of perception is precisely the sixth consciousness, mano-vijñāna. This "thought of standing at a height" is precisely this sixth consciousness, having, at some point, cast down within this thinking mind the mere shadow-image of some perilous cliff-edge. Now, hearing of a cliff-edge, this thought of standing at a height, empty in itself, arises, able to make your own body genuinely undergo this aching soreness. This place where this aching soreness is received is precisely the aggregate of sensation — among the three kinds of sensation, this being the sensation of suffering — precisely what is meant by "perception transformed into sensation." Though no cliff-edge is present at all, and this thought, then, has no real object behind it, still, this ache in the sole of the foot suddenly takes shape, and this sensation itself is real — and so, by this same example, one may understand every other sensation to be, likewise, false.

Because this thinking mind serves as the cause, the aggregate of sensation is thereby given rise to, able to move the very form of the aggregate of form's own body, this false realm of genuinely felt aching soreness. This clause, "because this sensation arises," matches "this thought of standing at a height"; "able to move this very body of form" matches "able to make your own body genuinely undergo this aching soreness." "Right now" directly points to the aggregate of sensation itself. What is meant here is that, when accorded with, benefit results — this being pleasant sensation; when opposed, harm results — this being unpleasant sensation. "This same driving back and forth" means that these two, suffering and pleasure, presently drive one's own mind about, coursing on without rest. The text here does not mention neutral sensation, neither suffering nor pleasure, since this neutral sensation is not readily apparent, while these two, suffering and pleasure, are, at present, easily distinguished and understood.

As Wenling puts it: this thought of standing at a height is entirely empty, and yet this ache in the sole of the foot genuinely arises; whether accorded with or opposed, all alike is false, and yet this harm and this benefit presently drive one about — and so the aggregate of sensation, having no real substance of its own, is nonetheless empty and yet manifest — hence named the empty, bright, second false thought. This closes the second section, the aggregate of sensation's own false thought.

Third: The Aggregate of Perception 融通第三妄想

Sūtra: "It is because of your own thought and reflection that your body of form is set in motion — yet this body itself does not belong to this same category as thought. Why, then, is your own body driven along by this thought at all, taking on so many different images, this mind giving rise to an image and this body taking on its very form, according with this thought exactly? Awake, this is precisely this same thinking mind; asleep, it becomes every dream instead. And so, this thought and reflection of yours, stirring this false sense-feeling into motion, is named the interfusing, third false thought."

Commentary: This makes plain the aggregate of perception — precisely this interfusing false thought. Entirely on the basis of this emptiness belonging to the aggregate of perception, able to move this solid substance belonging to the body of form, empty and substantial responding to one another, one thereby sees the falseness of the aggregate of perception as well. "Because of your own thought and reflection" — this thought and reflection is precisely the aggregate of perception itself, precisely this same "floating, superficial thought" spoken of earlier. This means that, because of your own sixth consciousness, this thought and this reflection, your own present body of form is thereby set in motion. Yet the body belongs to the dharma of form, while thought belongs to the dharma of mind — the dharma of form and the dharma of mind are, by their own nature, not of the same category at all; since the body does not belong to the same category as thought, it ought not, then, to be driven along by thought at all — and so it is asked, by way of challenge: for what reason, then, is this bodily form of yours driven along by this mental thought at all?

Right now, faculty and object confronting each other, taking on so many different images — perception carries precisely this meaning of taking on an image — yet all of this comes about only because this thought first arises in the mind, and only afterward does each sense-faculty take on its corresponding form; one thereby knows that whatever image the body and its faculties take on must certainly correspond exactly to this same thought. This concerns the very substance shared in common with the five sense-consciousnesses, and so is able to make the body of form correspond, moment by moment, with this thought and reflection — precisely what was challenged earlier: "driven along by this thought." As the Precious Mirror Commentary illustrates it: like a performer of song and dance, following the beat exactly — when the beat slows, the steps slow; when the beat quickens, the steps quicken — and the body, together with thought, follows exactly this same pattern.

"Awake, this is precisely this same thinking mind; asleep, it becomes every dream instead": waking means being roused from sleep; sleeping means being asleep. While waking, this is precisely this same thinking mind, extending, too, to the independent mental activity occurring in a scattered, undirected state; while sleeping, this becomes confused dreaming instead, extending, too, to the independent mental activity occurring within dreams. Independent mental activity occurring within samādhi is not mentioned here, since this sūtra's own samādhi is not a samādhi of merely contemplating mental images, and so independent mental activity does not arise in present activity there at all.

And so your own body and your own thought, at no time and in no place, ever fail to correspond to one another — entirely because this thought and reflection of yours never ceases, and so stirs this false sense-feeling into motion, whether waking or sleeping, restless and never interrupted. One thereby knows that the aggregate of perception, together with the five sense-faculties before it and the five sense-consciousnesses, interfuses and interpenetrates throughout — hence, this third false thought. Not only does it interfuse with the body of form, driving it along, but it interpenetrates, too, both waking and sleeping, stirring this false sense-feeling into motion in either state. One thereby knows that this aggregate fuses together both body and mind at once, and interpenetrates both waking and sleeping alike — hence named this interfusing false thought. This closes the third section, the aggregate of perception's own false thought.

Fourth: The Aggregate of Formations 幽隱第四妄想

Sūtra: "This process of transformation never rests; it operates continuously, secretly shifting things along — nails grow, hair lengthens, vital energy is spent, the face wrinkles, day replacing night without one's ever quite noticing it happen. Ānanda, if this were not truly you, how could your own body be changing this way? And if it truly is you, why are you not aware of it happening? This ceaseless flow of your own formations, moment after moment never stopping, is called the hidden, fourth false thought."

Commentary: This makes plain the aggregate of formations — precisely this hidden false thought. "This process of transformation never rests" refers to the aggregate of formations as this very substantial principle of transformation itself, possessed of this actual function of flowing movement, its own substance belonging to arising and ceasing — hence, "never rests." "It operates continuously, secretly shifting things along" means that, thought by thought, this changes and shifts, secretly moving things along, expressing this hidden, deeply concealed, subtle mark of movement. Zhuangzi's own metaphor of a valley bearing a boat away in the night captures exactly this same sense of secret shifting — though he speaks of it in terms of cosmic creation and transformation generally, while here it refers specifically to the aggregate of formations.

What follows explains this matter of secretly shifting things along, able to change this real substance itself. This means that the aggregate of formations, through these four marks — arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing — flows on, thought by thought never stopping, able to change this real substance: as, at the very start of life, the nails gradually grow; as one reaches maturity, the hair gradually lengthens; and, arriving at old age, the vital energy gradually is spent, and the face gradually wrinkles — who, then, is doing this? None other than the aggregate of formations itself. "Day replacing night without one's ever quite noticing it happen" means: all of this, day and night, is this same arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing, without the slightest pause, replacing one another in turn, from ancient times to the present, with never a single person able to notice it happening at all. As King Prasenajit himself said: "this secret shifting of transformation — I, in truth, never notice it at all" — exactly this same point.

This poses this pair of questions, whether this is or is not truly oneself, to reveal that this very substance is false. And so the Buddha calls upon Ānanda: should this flowing aggregate of formations, in fact, not be truly you, then how could it change your own real substance at all? — this showing that it is not, therefore, separate from you either. Should this flowing aggregate of formations, though, necessarily be your own true mind, then why are you not aware of it, thought by thought? — this showing that it is not, therefore, identical to you either. Whether it is you or not you cannot be settled either way — this is enough to show that it is false, and not truly real at all!

"Hidden" means deeply, subtly concealed. "Your own formations" refers to the present aggregate of formations, flowing on, thought by thought, never able to come to rest. As the Sūtra of Humane Kings' Wisdom states: "within a single thought there are ninety instants; within a single instant there are nine hundred arisings and ceasings" — so hidden and difficult to know is this, impossible to discern as true or false, false throughout its very substance — hence named "false thought." And, further, because this secret shifting goes unnoticed, it bears the additional name "hidden."

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: on the whole, though sensation, perception, and formations, these three aggregates, all belong to mind, the text, throughout, shows each connected through and pervading the body of form as well: sensation is able to make the body of form receive its objects; perception is able to drive the body along; formations are able to change this very substance itself. And though these three aggregates are said to pervade the body of form throughout, the true intent, in fact, is to show that the body itself belongs to this same category as thought, not truly, independently real at all. This closes the fourth section, the aggregate of formations' own false thought.

Fifth: The Aggregate of Consciousness 罔象虛無第五顛倒細微精想

Sūtra: "And further: this essential, bright, seemingly unmoving stillness of yours, which you take to be your own constant nature, never actually goes beyond the functions of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing at all. If this were, in truth, essentially real, it ought to admit no trace of habituated falseness whatsoever. Why is it, then, that some object glimpsed once, years ago, can be entirely forgotten, both remembered and forgotten alike gone — and yet, on suddenly beholding it once more later on, is recognized at once, this memory perfectly intact, with nothing ever actually lost at all? Within this seemingly still, unmoving essence, then, every single moment is quietly receiving this same habituation — who could possibly reckon and count them all?"

Commentary: This makes plain the aggregate of consciousness — precisely this subtle, essential thought. Pure and unmixed, illuminating thoroughly in every direction, this is called "essential and bright" — that is, resembling unity. Every floating thought already exhausted, this shifting disturbance entirely stilled, this daily, translucent, unmoving state — that is, resembling constancy. This very resemblance to unity and to constancy is, originally, the root aggregate of consciousness itself, its own substance pervading the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha; within a sentient being's own deluded state, apart from this, there is no separate, real substance to be found at all — exactly what fascicle 1 called "this essential, original brightness of consciousness."

Should one take this essential, bright, unmoving consciousness to be a nature constantly, permanently unchanging, this never goes beyond, within the body, the functions of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing — precisely what was meant earlier by "originally relying on this single, essential brightness, divided into these six, united together." This is precisely because this root itself is difficult to know, and so these six present functions are pointed to instead, so that ordinary and lesser practitioners alike might know that this present seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing shares the very same substance as this essential consciousness itself. Were this, in truth, essentially unified, without any admixture, truly real and free of any falseness, it would, of itself, admit no trace of habituated, false defilement at all — exactly as true gold ought never to be mixed together with mud and sand.

"Why is it" poses this challenge, by way of contrast. Having just said that, were this essentially real, it would admit no habituated falseness at all, what follows now directly makes plain this very matter of habituated falseness — and so it is asked, by way of challenge: for what reason is it that you yourselves, in years now past, once beheld some strange, unusual object, and, as the years passed by, at first still remembered it, and then, in time, forgot it, and, in time still further, both remembering and forgetting alike were entirely gone? "And yet, on suddenly beholding it once more later on" means: afterward, suddenly, beholding once more this same strange object from before, this memory returns exactly as it was in former days. This comes about through the power of habituation from these six consciousnesses earlier, forming this seed, stored within the field of the eighth consciousness, never once actually lost — hence the challenge, asking for what reason this could be.

The sixth consciousness resembles an official who gathers taxes; the seventh consciousness resembles an official who manages disbursement and receipt; the eighth consciousness resembles the very keeper of the treasury itself — and so one knows that this discussion, of gathering and retaining without loss, concerns the eighth consciousness alone. In truth, this consciousness is able to hold in memory countless kalpas' worth of innumerable seeds and habituated tendencies, ripening in due sequence — how could this be limited merely to beholding some object within this present life alone?

What follows reveals this falseness, showing it is not truly real. "Essential understanding" is precisely this same "essential and bright" spoken of before — that is, precisely this present seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing, sharing this one, same nature free of discrimination, named this translucent, unmoving state. "Every single moment quietly receiving this same habituation — who could possibly reckon and count them all" means: thought after thought, receiving this habituation from false thought, without any pause whatsoever, the sheer number and measure of these moments boundless and beyond counting — how could this ever be reckoned or calculated at all?

Sūtra: "Ānanda, you should know: this apparent stillness is not truly real at all. It is like a swiftly flowing river that looks, from a distance, perfectly calm — simply because the current moves too fast to see, not because there is no current there at all. If this were not, itself, the very root of thought, how could it still be susceptible to habituation by thought at all? Unless your six sense-faculties open and close, interchanging freely with one another, this false thought will never, at any point, be extinguished. This is why what churns, unnoticed, within your present seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing, is called the formless, empty, fifth, inverted, most subtle and essential false thought."

Commentary: Ānanda, you ought to understand clearly: this eighth consciousness, translucent and unmoving as it seems, becomes true when tinged by truth, and becomes false when tinged by falseness — it is not, itself, a truly permanent, unshakable nature at all. It is only like a swiftly flowing river — as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it, one must take specifically a swift current free of waves, flowing evenly. "Looks, from a distance, perfectly calm" means: because it lacks any uneven waves, any leaping torrent rising and falling. The next two clauses make plain that it is precisely because this current flows so swiftly that it cannot be seen at all — not because there is truly no current there. Testing this current once, by throwing a blade of grass onto the water's surface, one sees the grass swept swiftly away, and only then becomes aware of how truly swift this current is — not that there was no current there at all.

"If this were not, itself, the very root of thought" means: were this not, in truth, this very root of false thought belonging to the four prior aggregates, how could it ever be susceptible to habituation by this same false thought at all? This decisively states that some subtle, fine false thought still remains present here.

Yet this subtle, fine false thought — precisely when might it finally be extinguished? Unless your own six sense-faculties, at the very moment of their free interchange, at the very point where the faculty's own separation opens and closes, this false thought, too, will find no moment at all in which to be extinguished. Yet the sūtra here expresses this in reverse, to make it plain: that is, unless the sense-faculty is untied and one enters perfect penetration, this falseness will, in the end, never find a moment to be extinguished at all. This free interchange among the six sense-faculties, this opening and closing, is precisely the very moment quiescent cessation manifests before them.

This carries forward what came before: not yet having attained this free interchange among the six sense-faculties, not yet having exhausted this very root of thought — and so, in your present seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, sensing, and knowing, these six essential natures, precisely within the eighth consciousness, thought by thought, receiving this habituation, threading through one after another. Though this habitual energy is exceedingly subtle, causing nothing at all to be lost or scattered, still, within this translucent, thoroughly understanding consciousness — that is, within this essential, bright, unmoving translucence — a single portion of ignorance serves as the very thread doing this threading, while the six faculties' own habituated, subtle traces serve as what is threaded through.

And so this is formless and empty — seemingly absent, yet not truly absent; seemingly present, yet not truly present either. The ordinary being takes this to be their very life-root itself; the practitioner of the two lesser vehicles mistakes it for nirvāṇa itself — taking what is empty to be real, taking what is absent to be present — this is precisely this inverted, subtle, essential thought. This means being confused about truth and clinging, instead, to a mere resemblance of it — confused about the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha itself, clinging instead to this resemblance, the ālaya-consciousness — how could this not be an inversion indeed?

The four aggregates before this are shown to be coarse, in order to show that this consciousness is subtle by comparison; and, further, though this consciousness is not, itself, this wondrous, essential, bright mind, it is like the second moon seen when the eye is pressed — hence named this "essential thought" — merely one press of the finger too many; release the hand, and this is, at once, the very true moon itself! This closes the second section, showing each of these five layers of false thought in detail.

〖經〗阿難！是五受陰，五妄想成。汝今欲知，因界淺深：惟色與空，是色邊際。惟觸及離，是受邊際。惟記與忘，是想邊際。惟滅與生；是行邊際。湛入合湛，歸識邊際。

〖講〗此總結五陰，即五種妄想所成也。此五種，即是眾生所受報法。受此五種，蓋覆真性，故名五受陰，又名五取蘊。一切眾生，莫不取此以為自體，故名此身為五蘊幻軀，又名五陰身也。由是而觀，五陰，雖淺深麤細之不同，而要之皆妄想所成，悉非真心本有也。《寶鏡疏》云：良以眾生，自迷如來藏性，而有妄色妄心，依此色心，而成五陰，故有世間凡、聖差別也。若以眾生知見，執此五陰，為實有者，即世間法。若以二乘知見，執此五陰，為空寂者，即出世法。若在諸佛菩薩，善得中道，了色即空，達空即色，即為出世上上法，乃第一義諦，不思議境界也。設若離此五陰之法，則五乘聖教，亦無安立之處也。以故，迷之則凡，悟之則聖，皆不出此。凡修定者，苟能於是，用金剛觀智，蕩滌空有情計，掃除斷常知見，了一真之本具，達諸妄以本空，則其五陰妄想之心，當體清淨，即是本如來藏，妙真如性矣！初答生起妄想竟。

〖講〗阿難前第三問，如是五重，詣何為界？佛今於第二超答云：汝現今欲知，因界淺深者，此一科之文，披閱諸家疏釋，惟交光法師，研究有得於心，詳釋邊際之淺深，超千佔而獨最，故悉依之。交光法師云：今考古訓，十八界，乃云界者因義；謂出生諸法，如地生物，而地為物因也。今五陰即界之開合，故名因界，但是陰之別名而已。淺深，即是邊際之淺深，歷五陰而各有也。如色陰中，有相為色，無相為空，若離諸色相，而棲心空淨；祖家，謂之一色邊，唯識，謂為空一顯色，是知盡色，而不盡空；皆未出乎色陰邊際，而一切空忍，皆非究竟也。受陰中，取著曰觸，厭捨曰離，斷諸取著，而不忘厭捨，是猶住捨受之中。故佛於離幻之後，復教離離，是知盡觸，而不盡離；亦未出乎受陰邊際，而一切背捨，皆非究竟也。想陰中，有念為記，無念為忘，除諸念而不忘無念，是仍住於靜念之中。故佛言：有念無念，同歸迷悶。祖云：莫謂無心便是道，無心猶隔一重關。是知盡記，而不盡忘，亦未出乎想陰邊際，而一切無想，皆非究竟也。行陰中，以迷位，散心麤行為生相，如二卷喻如瀑流者是也。以修位，定心細行為滅相，如此卷喻如野馬者是也。然此細行，似滅非滅，仍是清擾細遷，如定中人，不免爪生髮長，足以驗之。是知盡生，而不盡滅，亦未出乎行陰邊際，而一切滅定，皆非究竟也。識陰中，以有入為湛入，蓋泯行流，而滅歸識海。經云：性入元澄，一澄元習，如波瀾滅，化為澄水是也。以無入為合湛，經云：內外湛明，入無所入是也。蓋合字有不動之意，即流急不見其流也。然此合湛境界，分劑非淺，良以始言湛入，特表行陰方消，識海初入；按位已當七信，齊於四果。而圓通正在聞所聞盡，終言合湛，更名識海久停，湛明淨極，雖視湛入有加，居然仍在識境，咸不免於最細四相所遷，是知盡湛入，而不盡合湛，終未出乎識陰邊際；所謂清光照眼，猶似迷家，而一切明白法身，猶未究竟也。問：識陰盡時，畢竟何位？答：入初住，證圓通也。經云：非汝六根，互用開合，此之妄想，無時得滅；是其明徵也。問：此之識陰，既惟第八，即是業識，而別經論，皆謂無明生相，等覺後心方盡，今言初住即盡，而後位依何住持耶？答：彼是漸教所談，初住等覺，尚隔天淵，豈遽說盡？此是圓頓之旨，經文從互用中，頓超諸位，能入金剛乾慧，非等覺後心而何？應知勝義中，真勝義性，大不思議，不應以漸而難圓也。然以此總較，因界之淺深者，若但知色為色，而不知空亦是色者，知色界之淺者也；知空色之皆色者，知色界之深者也。如是乃至但知湛入為識，而不知合湛亦識者，知識界之淺者也；知湛入合湛皆識者，知識界之深者也。是則發揮五重妄想，可謂極盡其境界矣！

〖經〗此五陰元，重疊生起，生因識有，滅從色除。理則頓悟，乘悟併銷；事非頓除，因次第盡。我已示汝，劫波巾結，何所不明，再此詢問？

〖講〗阿難前第二問云：又此五陰，為併銷除，為次第盡？故如來在此第三，而追答之。此五陰生滅次第，即六根結解次第，故先明五陰，生起滅除，二重次第。此五陰元，是從細向麤，一重疊一重，次第生起也。生則從細向麤，因迷藏性，以為識性，故曰生因識有。由識而行，由行而想，由想而受，由受而色；如人著衣，必自內向外，而漸著故。滅則從麤向細﹒須從色陰先除。則受、想、行、識，次第漸除；如人脫衣，必自外向內，而漸脫也。此單陳滅除次第，而仍兼始悟則無次第，而終修須次第也。蓋此五陰，以理推究，如前所云：五陰本因，同是妄想。既唯妄想，妄性本空，一念頓悟，乘此心開，則五重妄想，如紅爐點雪，一併銷除，有何淺深次第之可得耶？《正脈》舉喻，夜暗驚杌為鬼，奔馳荒越，一被他人說破，鬼想全銷。若就事相而論，事，謂修斷之事，色心諸法，非能頓除，務必自淺而深，因五陰之次第而漸除以盡也。《正脈》云：鬼想雖已全銷，而馳途豈能遽返，要須歷返前途，方歸舊處矣！總是頓悟，漸修之意而已。我已示汝，劫波巾結，何所不明，再此詢問？此乃斥問。取第五卷，綰巾以示倫次之文云。六結不同，一巾所造，令其雜亂，終不得成；是結解，定有倫次，故云：我已示汝，劫波巾結。前阿難亦云：是結本以次第綰生，今日當須次第而解。於彼既知，於此即應理會。故責云：何所不明，再此詢問。此處破陰之文，與解六結而入圓通比之，破五陰而登初住，法數五六參差，如何會合？答：初於聞中，入流亡所解動結，所入既寂，動靜二相，瞭然不生解靜結，初解動靜二結，破色陰。如是漸增，聞所聞盡，解根結，破受陰。盡聞不住，覺所覺空，解覺結破想陰。空覺極圓，空所空滅，解空結，破行陰。生滅既滅，寂滅現前，解滅結，破識陰。如是解結破陰，若合符節矣。《寶鏡疏》問：如何是頓悟漸除之義乎？答：如大海猛風頓息，是頓悟也；波浪漸停，是漸除也；如嬰兒諸根頓生，是頓悟也；力量漸備，是漸除也；如太陽頓出，是頓悟也；霜露漸消，是漸除也；如春筍頓長，即與母齊，是頓悟也；枝葉分敷，節節而上，是漸除也。若但頓悟，而不漸除，則有解無行，執理迷事；若但漸除，而不頓悟，則有行無解，執事迷理；均非正修真三摩地也。二具答三問竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, these five aggregates one clings to are all formed from these five false thoughts. If you now wish to know the relative depth of each one's own domain: form and emptiness alone mark the outer edge of the aggregate of form; contact and its absence alone mark the edge of sensation; remembering and forgetting alone mark the edge of perception; ceasing and arising alone mark the edge of formations; and returning into stillness, merging fully with that stillness, marks the edge of consciousness."

Commentary: This sums up, in general, that these five aggregates are, entirely, formed from these five kinds of false thought. These five kinds are precisely the very phenomena of retribution received by sentient beings; receiving these five, they cover over this true nature — hence named the five aggregates one clings to, also called the five categories of grasping. Every sentient being, without exception, grasps these as their own very self — hence this body is named this illusory form composed of the five aggregates, also called this body of the five aggregates. Seen in this light, though these five aggregates differ in their relative depth, coarseness, and subtlety, all of them, in the end, are nothing but the product of false thought, none of them ever truly, originally possessed by the true mind at all.

As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: it is entirely because sentient beings, confused about their own nature of the tathāgatagarbha, come to possess this false form and this false mind; and, depending on this form and mind, these five aggregates come to be formed — and so there arise these distinctions, within this world, between the ordinary and the sagely. Should one, through the knowledge and view of an ordinary sentient being, cling to these five aggregates as truly, really existent, this is a worldly dharma. Should one, through the knowledge and view of the two lesser vehicles, cling to these five aggregates as empty and quiescent, this is a transcendent dharma. Should one, though, as with every buddha and bodhisattva, skillfully attain the middle way, understanding that form is itself emptiness, and penetrating that emptiness is itself form, this is the very highest of transcendent teachings — precisely this truth of the foremost meaning, this inconceivable realm itself. Were one to set these five-aggregate teachings aside entirely, the five vehicles' own sagely teaching would, likewise, have nowhere at all to be established. And so: confusion makes one ordinary, and awakening makes one a sage — neither ever departing from this same ground. Should any practitioner of samādhi be able, in this very place, to apply this vajra-like contemplative wisdom, sweeping away every calculation of emptiness and existence, sentiment and reckoning alike, clearing away every knowledge and view of annihilation and eternalism, understanding that this single truth is already, fully possessed, and penetrating that every falseness is, at root, empty — then this very mind of false thought belonging to these five aggregates becomes, in its own very substance, pure, and is, itself, precisely this original tathāgatagarbha, this wondrous, true suchness itself! This closes the first section, answering the question on the arising of false thought.

Ānanda's third question, earlier, asked: "where, exactly, does the boundary lie between each of these five layers?" The Buddha now answers this, out of sequence, as the second of his three replies: "if you now wish to know the relative depth of each one's own domain." Examining the various commentaries on this particular section of text, only Master Jiaoguang's own research reached genuine insight here, his detailed explanation of the relative depth of each boundary surpassing a thousand others by far — and so this commentary follows him entirely throughout.

Master Jiaoguang states: examining the ancient teaching now, the eighteen elements — "element" here carries the meaning of "cause": that is, giving rise to every phenomenon, exactly as earth gives rise to things, earth itself being the very cause of these things. Now, these five aggregates are precisely these same elements, opened out or gathered together — hence called "causal domains," being nothing more than another name for these same aggregates. "Relative depth" refers precisely to the relative depth of each boundary, present within every one of the five aggregates in turn.

Within the aggregate of form: what has form is called "form"; what has no form is called "emptiness." Should one depart from every mark of form, and settle one's own mind within this pure emptiness, the patriarchal tradition calls this "the boundary of form alone"; consciousness-only teaching calls this "the manifest color of emptiness alone" — one thereby knows that, though form itself may be exhausted, emptiness has not yet been exhausted at all: this still has not gone beyond the very boundary of the aggregate of form, and every attainment of the forbearance of emptiness alone is, thereby, not yet the ultimate at all.

Within the aggregate of sensation: clinging attachment is called "contact"; wearied rejection is called "departure." Cutting off every clinging attachment, yet not forgetting this wearied rejection either, one still dwells, thereby, within this equanimous sensation. And so, after teaching departure from illusion, the Buddha further teaches departing from this very departure as well — one thereby knows that, though contact itself may be exhausted, this departure has not yet been exhausted at all: this, too, has not gone beyond the very boundary of the aggregate of sensation, and every attainment of turning one's back and rejecting is, thereby, not yet the ultimate at all.

Within the aggregate of perception: having a thought is called "remembering"; having no thought is called "forgetting." Removing every thought, yet not forgetting this very state of no-thought either, one still dwells, thereby, within this quiet, thoughtless state. And so the Buddha states: "having thought and having no thought both alike return to confusion and distress." As the patriarchs have said: "do not say that having no mind is itself the path — even having no mind still lies one further barrier away" — one thereby knows that, though remembering itself may be exhausted, forgetting has not yet been exhausted at all: this, too, has not gone beyond the very boundary of the aggregate of perception, and every attainment of no-thought is, thereby, not yet the ultimate at all.

Within the aggregate of formations: at the stage of delusion, this scattered mind's own coarse activity is its mark of arising — exactly as fascicle 2 likened it to a cascading torrent; at the stage of cultivation, this settled mind's own fine activity is its mark of ceasing — exactly as this present fascicle likens it to heat-shimmer instead. Yet this fine activity, though seemingly ceased, is not, in truth, truly ceased — still remaining this same light, restless, subtle shifting, exactly as a person deep in samādhi still cannot help but have their nails grow and their hair lengthen, sufficient proof of this. One thereby knows that, though arising itself may be exhausted, ceasing has not yet been exhausted at all: this, too, has not gone beyond the very boundary of the aggregate of formations, and every attainment of the samādhi of cessation is, thereby, not yet the ultimate at all.

Within the aggregate of consciousness: entering into some further state is called "returning into stillness"; entering into no further state at all is called "merging fully with that stillness." As the sūtra states: "this nature enters its own original stillness, and that stillness absorbs its own original habitual tendency, like waves subsiding into still water" — this is precisely "returning into stillness." As the sūtra states: "both within and without, everything translucent, entering a state where there is nothing further left to enter" — this is precisely "merging fully with that stillness." For this character "merging" carries the sense of unmoving — that is, this current flowing so swiftly that its very flow can no longer be seen at all.

Yet this realm of "merging fully with that stillness" is, in truth, no shallow measure at all. For, at first, to speak of "returning into stillness" specifically expresses that the aggregate of formations has only just dissolved, and one has only just entered this ocean of consciousness — corresponding, in terms of stage, already to the seventh faith, equal to the fourth fruit; and this very perfect penetration lies precisely at "the very hearing of what is heard coming to an end." At the very last, to speak of "merging fully with that stillness" renames this same ocean of consciousness, now long settled, this translucent brightness reaching its very purest extreme — though, compared to "returning into stillness," this has advanced further still, it plainly remains, even so, within the very realm of consciousness itself, unable to avoid being shifted along by even the subtlest of these four marks. One thereby knows that, though "returning into stillness" may be exhausted, "merging fully with that stillness" has not yet been exhausted at all: this, in the end, has not gone beyond the very boundary of the aggregate of consciousness — precisely what is meant by: this pure light shining in the eye still resembles a traveler lost, still confused about their own home; and this perfectly clear, bright dharma-body has not yet reached its own ultimate completion at all.

Question: at the very point the aggregate of consciousness is exhausted, what stage, in the end, is this? Answer: entering this first ground of abiding, realizing perfect penetration itself. As the sūtra states: "unless your six sense-faculties open and close, interchanging freely with one another, this false thought will never, at any point, be extinguished" — this being clear proof of it.

Question: since this aggregate of consciousness is nothing but this eighth consciousness alone, precisely this karmic consciousness itself, while other sūtras and treatises all hold that this subtle mark of ignorance's own arising is exhausted only at the very final mind of equal enlightenment — why, then, does the text here say it is exhausted already at the first ground of abiding? And upon what, then, does the stage following it depend and rest? Answer: those other teachings are spoken within the gradual teaching, where the first ground of abiding and equal enlightenment remain separated by a gulf as wide as heaven from earth — how, then, could its exhaustion ever be spoken of so hastily there? This present sūtra, though, expresses the very intent of the sudden, perfect teaching: the text states that, from within this free interchange, one transcends every stage all at once, becoming able to enter this vajra-like dry wisdom — how could this be anything other than the very final mind of equal enlightenment itself? One should know that, within this truth of ultimate meaning, this true, ultimate nature of meaning is vast and inconceivable indeed — one ought never to measure this sudden teaching by the standard of the gradual.

Now, comparing all of this together, regarding the relative depth of each of these causal domains: one who knows only form as form, without knowing that emptiness, too, is itself form, knows only the shallower reach of the boundary of form; one who knows that both emptiness and form alike are form knows the deeper reach of this same boundary. And so it continues, all the way to: one who knows only "returning into stillness" as consciousness, without knowing that "merging fully with that stillness," too, is itself consciousness, knows only the shallower reach of the boundary of consciousness; one who knows that both "returning into stillness" and "merging fully with that stillness" alike are consciousness knows the deeper reach of this same boundary. This, then, may truly be said to bring out these five layers of false thought to their very fullest extent!

Sūtra: "These five aggregates arise layered, one upon another — their arising is rooted in consciousness, and their cessation must proceed by removing form first. In principle, this can be awakened to all at once, and, riding that single awakening, all five may dissolve together; but in actual practice, they are not removed all at once — they are exhausted one by one, in sequence. I have already shown you, in the parable of the knotted cloth, exactly this same point — what remains unclear, that you should ask again?"

Commentary: Ānanda's second question, earlier, asked: "are these five aggregates removed all at once, or exhausted one after another in sequence?" And so the Tathāgata, here, in this third reply, answers this in turn. This sequence in which the five aggregates arise and cease is precisely the same sequence in which the six knots are tied and untied — and so, first, this makes plain these two sequences, the arising and the ceasing of the five aggregates.

This original source of the five aggregates proceeds from subtle to coarse, layer upon layer, arising in this order in succession. Their arising, then, proceeds from subtle to coarse, since, confused about this treasury-nature, one takes it, instead, to be this very nature of consciousness — hence, "their arising is rooted in consciousness." From consciousness comes formations; from formations comes perception; from perception comes sensation; from sensation comes form — exactly as a person putting on clothing must necessarily dress from the inside outward, layer by layer. Their cessation, then, proceeds from coarse to subtle, and must begin by removing the aggregate of form first — sensation, perception, formations, and consciousness then removed, one after another, in sequence — exactly as a person removing clothing must necessarily undress from the outside inward, layer by layer.

This alone sets forth the sequence of their removal, while, at the same time, holding together both the fact that, at the very start, this awakening admits of no such sequence at all, and that, in the final cultivation, this same sequence must, nonetheless, still be followed. For these five aggregates, examined through principle, are, as was said earlier, "the very root cause of these five aggregates alike is nothing but false thought" — being, then, nothing but false thought, this false nature is, at root, empty; and, the very moment a single thought awakens to this fully, riding upon this same opening of the mind, these five layers of false thought, exactly like snow scattered onto a red-hot furnace, dissolve away entirely, all at once — what relative depth or sequence could possibly remain to be found here at all? As the Standard Meaning Commentary offers by way of illustration: startled, in the dark of night, by what one takes to be a ghost, one flees in a wild panic across the wilderness; the very moment someone else explains the truth of it, this notion of a ghost dissolves away entirely, all at once.

Should one, though, discuss this in terms of actual phenomena — "phenomena" here meaning the actual matter of cultivation and severance — form and mind, every such dharma, cannot be removed all at once; one must, necessarily, proceed from the shallower to the deeper, gradually removing them, exhausting them entirely, in accordance with the sequence proper to these five aggregates. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: though this notion of a ghost has already dissolved away entirely, how could one's own headlong flight be reversed all at once as well? One must, necessarily, retrace this same path traveled before, step by step, before finally returning to one's original place. In sum, this is nothing but this same meaning: sudden awakening, gradual cultivation.

"I have already shown you, in the parable of the knotted cloth, exactly this same point — what remains unclear, that you should ask again?" This is a rebuke, in the form of a question. This refers back to fascicle 5, where the knotted cloth was tied to illustrate this very sequence, the text stating: these six knots, though differing from one another, are all made from one and the same cloth; should they be tangled together all at once, they could never, in the end, be untied at all — this shows that both the tying and the untying of these knots necessarily follow a fixed sequence. Hence: "I have already shown you, in the parable of the knotted cloth." Ānanda himself, earlier, likewise said: "since these knots were originally tied on in sequence, today they ought, likewise, to be untied in this same sequence." Having already understood this there, he ought, then, to grasp this same point here as well — hence the rebuke: "what remains unclear, that you should ask again?"

Comparing this present text, on breaking through the aggregates, against untying these six knots to enter perfect penetration: breaking through the five aggregates corresponds to entering this first ground of abiding — yet these two numbers, five and six, do not match exactly. How, then, are they to be reconciled? Answer: at first, within hearing, entering the stream and forgetting the object, this unties the knot of movement; what has entered thereby growing still, these two marks of motion and stillness becoming, thereupon, utterly unarising — this unties the knot of stillness: these first two knots untied, movement and stillness, breaks through the aggregate of form. Increasing gradually further still, the very hearing of what is heard comes to an end — this unties the knot of the faculty itself, breaking through the aggregate of sensation. This hearing exhausted, no longer dwelling anywhere, awareness, and what is aware of it, both become empty — this unties the knot of awareness itself, breaking through the aggregate of perception. This emptiness and this awareness both reaching their very utmost perfection, even this emptiness, and what is empty, both are extinguished — this unties the knot of emptiness itself, breaking through the aggregate of formations. This mark of arising and ceasing itself now extinguished, quiescent cessation manifesting before them — this unties the knot of cessation itself, breaking through the aggregate of consciousness. In just this way, the untying of these knots and the breaking through of these aggregates match one another exactly, like the two halves of a single tally.

As the Precious Mirror Commentary asks: what, exactly, is meant by "sudden awakening, gradual removal"? Answer: it is like a great sea's own fierce wind suddenly ceasing — this is sudden awakening; while its waves gradually settle — this is gradual removal. It is like an infant's every faculty suddenly being born, complete at once — this is sudden awakening; while their own strength gradually develops — this is gradual removal. It is like the sun suddenly rising — this is sudden awakening; while the frost and dew gradually melt away — this is gradual removal. It is like a spring bamboo shoot suddenly growing to match its parent stalk's own height — this is sudden awakening; while its branches and leaves gradually unfold, joint by joint, rising higher still — this is gradual removal. Were one to have sudden awakening alone, without this gradual removal, one would possess understanding without practice, grasping principle while remaining confused about actual phenomena; were one to have gradual removal alone, without this sudden awakening, one would possess practice without understanding, grasping phenomena while remaining confused about the principle behind them — neither, in either case, being the true, correct cultivation of this real samādhi at all. This closes the second section, answering these three questions in full.

〖講〗此明流通分。流者：流傳後世；通者：通達十方。經分三分，譬如一人：初序分如首，五官具存，觀之便知善惡，亦如經中序分，一觀便知，是大乘小乘。二正宗分如身，五臟全在，以為一身之要。三流通分如足，以便行走。經有流通分，能成法益，流傳無盡，通達無邊也。此以財施較定供佛之福。阿難！設若復有一人，遍滿十方虛空，則虛空無盡可知；如是虛空，盈滿七寶，則七寶無邊可知；以此滿空七寶，供養一佛二佛，則財施之勝，福田之廣可知；何況持以奉上微塵數諸佛，一一悉皆，欽承奉事。心無虛度者：即無有一佛，而空過者，則福田之廣，愈可知矣！又以滿空七寶供養，是廣大心；供養諸佛，是第一心；微塵諸佛，心無空過，是常時心；如是三心並發，在汝阿難之意，以為云何？是人以此施佛，殊勝因緣，得福多不多耶？

〖講〗欲顯滅惡之功，先示真實之語。因以下：弘經之時至少，而滅惡獲福甚多，恐難生信。故先舉諸佛如來，所有語言，無有虛而不實，妄而不真，汝當諦信也。若復有人，身具小乘，淫、殺、盜、妄，四根本重罪；十波羅夷：大乘十種重罪。波羅夷：義當極惡，亦譯名棄。謂若犯此罪之一者，即應墮獄，況復俱犯？即應速墮；故云瞬息即經此方他方，阿鼻地獄等。瞬息時之極短，猶言臨墮迅速也。先墮此方阿鼻地獄，具足一劫；更墮他方，阿鼻地獄，具足一劫，乃至展轉，窮盡十方無間。無間者：即阿鼻之華言也。靡即無也不經歷者：顯十方俱經，諸獄備歷也。此舉暫爾弘經，即指將要墮獄之人。能以一念者：謂能以一念，回光返照，背塵合覺，頓悟圓通法門；深入一門，彼六知根，一時清淨。既頓悟已，又將此法門，於末劫中，開示未學，修禪那者，令其各得開悟，續佛慧命，紹隆佛種。然而弘經之功雖少，惟以一念，其所得之益甚多，不可為喻。是人罪障，應念消滅者：是人罪障，即小乘四重、大乘十棄。應念者：應其弘經之一念，頓悟真如本有，妄想本空，則何罪不消，何障不滅？所謂千年暗室，一燈能破；又如一星之火，便可燎原，頓成灰燼。變其所受地獄苦因，成安樂國者：不惟只消罪障，且能變苦為樂，其故何也？以能將此法門，於末劫中，開示未學。此法門，即圓頓法門，圓彰法界，極顯一心，此一念心，即十方如來，成佛真體，故能變苦因成樂國，化魔界成佛界矣！此不僅離苦得樂，仍當得福無量，超越無比。前之施人者：即超越前以盈空珍寶，奉上塵數諸佛之人。超越百倍、千倍、千萬億倍，如是展轉乃至算數譬喻，所不能及倍。總之前人之福，任說無有邊際，回不能及於此福之少分耳。超越前人，略有三義：一、約佛，不以財施為重，惟以法施為重，以能一念，悟此圓頓法門故。二、約行，不以自行為要，惟以化他為要，以能於末劫中，開示未學故。三、約福，前是有漏之福，此是無漏之福，以能出離生死，永脫輪迴故。譬如摩尼一顆，勝似海寶千般，阿伽陀藥，壓倒醫方萬品，則超越前福，固其宜矣！

〖經〗阿難！若有眾生，能誦此經，能持此咒，如我廣說，窮劫不盡，依我教言，如教行道，直成菩提，無復魔業。

〖講〗弘經利益，轉苦為樂，已如上說。今舉無惡，誦經持咒之人言之。能誦此經者：能誦持顯文，一心不亂，若文若義，了解分明。能持此咒者：能加持密咒，三業相應，有正有助，自能得益。如我廣說，窮劫不盡者：此誦持之福，如我以四無礙辯才，廣為宣說，窮劫說其所得之福，所受之報，尚不能盡。然而自利之行，尚且如此，何況更能利他乎？依我教言，如教行道者：如前文言，汝等必須，將如來語，於我滅後，傳示末法，遍令眾生，覺了斯義。此首二句，即依我教言。下於我滅後，宣傳開示，遍令末法，一切未學眾生，覺瞭如斯辨魔之義，此即如教而行利他之道。誦經持咒，既是顯密雙修，而自行化他，仍復自他兩利也。直成菩提，無復魔業者：況無上菩提，必須兩利圓滿，方克證入。從初發心住，一超直入，妙覺果海，圓滿菩提，於其中間，中中流入，更無魔業肆擾，諸委曲相。《指掌疏》云：此經顯密雙修，自他兩利，如風帆揚於順水，若獅絃奏於群音，自然直成菩提，無復魔業。此功德所以無盡，而稱讚所以靡窮也。

〖經〗佛說此經已。比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷，一切世間，天人阿修羅，及諸他方，菩薩二乘，聖仙童子，並初發心，大力鬼神，皆大歡喜，作禮而去。

〖講〗佛說此經已者：自阿難請定之後，如來初示佛定總名，令知諸佛，修因剋果；次說奢摩他路，令悟密因，大開圓解；三說三摩修法，令依耳根，一門深入；四說禪那證位，令住圓定，疾趣菩提；正說妙定始終已竟。復詳初心緊要，初談七趣勸離，以警淹留；次辨五魔令識，以護墮落；三請重明五陰，生滅之相，正宗已竟。復說流通，較量持福，滅惡之功，兩利之勝，至此所應說者，皆已說竟。比丘、比丘尼、優婆塞、優婆夷，解見在前。序分，唯列比丘，餘三或是後來，今則俱列，以如來凡說法處，必有四眾，為內護故。一切世間，天、人、阿修羅、八部列三，餘亦應有；以如來說法處，必有八部，為外護故。序分不列，非所急也。及諸他方，菩薩、二乘者：他方菩薩，序分中，咨決先來眾，及音感後至眾。他方二乘，序分中，唯列闢支無學，並未列羅漢，或亦後來，故以俱列。聖仙童子者：內修聖道，外現仙身，不壞童真，故稱聖仙童子。或因聞法而來，或因護咒而至，故序分不列。並初發心，大力鬼神者：鬼神特加發心；大力者：表其立志護法，具大神力，可以降魔制外，而為外護之眾。皆大歡喜者：合會大眾，法喜充滿，既聞正宗，而獲本妙圓心；復聞流通，而知究竟弘益。依如來之密因，起了義之修證，發菩薩之萬行，一超直入，妙莊嚴海；自慶成佛有分，皆大歡喜也。作禮而去者：進而聞法於師，退而修法於己；作禮：表謝法之儀；而去：為自修之行。又去不徒去，要休去，歇去，冷湫湫地去，正所謂狂性自歇，歇即菩提，勝淨明心，本週法界，不從人得也。全部正文，講解已竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "You should take this understanding of the very root of false thought and open it up fully in your own mind, then transmit it to every practitioner of the age of the Dharma's decline yet to come, so that they recognize falseness for what it is, grow deeply weary of its arising within themselves, and, knowing that nirvāṇa exists, no longer cling with longing to the three realms."

Commentary: This closes, in general, this teaching on the five aggregates, urging that it be transmitted to future generations. And so Ānanda is charged: you should take these five aggregates' own root cause of false thought and research each in turn — the aggregate of form's own solid false thought, the aggregate of sensation's own empty, bright false thought, the aggregate of perception's own interfusing false thought, the aggregate of formations' own hidden false thought, the aggregate of consciousness's own subtle, essential thought. "Open it up fully in your own mind" means: though these names of false thought differ in relative depth and sequence, understanding thoroughly that all alike are, equally, false, with no real root or thread to them at all — this urges Ānanda toward benefiting himself, remaining unconfused. "Transmit it to" and what follows urges him toward benefiting others, spreading this benefit universally.

Speaking of this universally, "throughout the age of the Dharma's decline," is because sentient beings' own faculties, at that time, will be shallow and weak, their own clinging mind exceedingly heavy, these five aggregates difficult to remove. Should any practitioner not even consider cultivating this practice at all, there is, of course, nothing further to say. But should they wish to cultivate this practice, they ought to be made to recognize these five aggregates' own substance and nature, this very root cause of falseness — since this is false, how could these five aggregates ever be truly real at all? Mistaking falseness for truth is precisely why this turning wheel of rebirth exists. "Grow deeply weary of its arising within themselves" means: having already penetrated the fact that these five aggregates are entirely false, their own substance originally empty, this deep, earnest resolve to depart from them arises naturally, of itself! "Knowing that nirvāṇa exists" means: knowing that this originally possessed nature, neither arising nor ceasing, this true nature complete in every respect, relying on this as the very causal mind of this fundamental practice, one thereafter perfects this fruit-ground cultivation and realization, no longer clinging with longing to the three realms, this cause and effect of birth and death still subject to outflow. The character for "longing" stands in direct opposition to the character for "weariness" — having already grown weary of it, how could one still long for it at all?

This closes the second section, this renewed request to explain the arising and ceasing of the five aggregates — and, with it, this entire major division, expounding the five demonic states so that they may be recognized, so as to guard against falling away. From the point where Ānanda first requested the teaching on the seven destinies, all the way to this present teaching, this constitutes one further complete cycle, named the cycle of transcending existence and departing from demonic influence.

As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: Śūraṅgama translates "ultimate firmness" — taking, as its very meaning, being unmoving and indestructible. This sūtra's own close examination of the seven destinies shows them to be summoned entirely by one's own karma alone; its detailed distinguishing of the five demonic states traces them entirely to deviant thought; and its renewed teaching on the five aggregates takes false thought, throughout, as their very root. Summoned by one's own karma — the seven destinies form, and this hidden cause is thereby destroyed; caused by deviant thought — the five demons arise, and this complete meaning is thereby lost; rooted in false thought — the five aggregates cover one over, and every practice thereby collapses. This hidden cause destroyed, right faith cannot remain firm; this complete meaning lost, right understanding cannot remain solid; every practice collapsed, true cultivation must inevitably fall away — this being precisely why, from beginning to end, nothing remains firm, and everything is subject to collapse.

Now, knowing that this is summoned by one's own karma, should this karma never be created, these seven destinies become, thereby, empty; knowing that this is caused by deviant thought, should this thought never turn deviant, these five demons flee, thereby, of their own accord; knowing that this is rooted in false thought, should this thought depart from falseness, these five aggregates dissolve, thereby, of themselves. These seven destinies emptied, this hidden cause is thereby fully possessed, and right faith becomes firm indeed! These five demons fled, this complete meaning thereby manifests before them, and right understanding becomes solid indeed! These five aggregates dissolved, every practice proceeds without any obstruction, and true cultivation is thereby accomplished! True cultivation accomplished, this wondrous realization must inevitably follow — and so, based on this, giving rise to faith, unfolding understanding, initiating practice, and realizing this fruit, from beginning to end, remain ultimately firm, unmoving and indestructible throughout — and this same meaning, expressed by the very title's own word "Śūraṅgama," is summed up entirely in this. From the point Ānanda first requested this samādhi, all the way through this teaching to this present point, this closes the sūtra's own main body of teaching.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, suppose someone were to fill the whole of empty space throughout the ten directions with the seven treasures, and offer it all up to buddhas as numerous as dust motes, honoring and making offerings to each one without a single moment wasted — what do you think? Would such a person, through this cause of giving to buddhas, gain great merit?"

Commentary: This makes plain the sūtra's own concluding, transmissive section. "Transmission" means passing this teaching down to later generations; "circulation" means spreading it throughout the ten directions. A sūtra is divided into three sections, comparable to a single person: the opening, introductory section is like the head — every one of the five sense-organs present there, one glance sufficient to know whether it shows good or ill; likewise, glancing once at this sūtra's own opening section, one knows at once whether it belongs to the great vehicle or the lesser. The main body of teaching is like the torso — every one of the five internal organs present there, serving as the very essence of the body itself. This concluding, transmissive section is like the feet, enabling one to walk forward. A sūtra possessing this concluding section is thereby able to accomplish this benefit of the Dharma, transmitted without end, spreading throughout without any bound at all.

This compares the merit of giving material wealth against the merit of making offerings to buddhas. Ānanda, suppose there were a further person who filled the whole of empty space throughout the ten directions — one thereby knows that this empty space is itself endless; and filled this same space with the seven treasures — one thereby knows that these treasures are themselves boundless; and, with these treasures filling this empty space, made offerings to a single buddha, or two buddhas — one thereby knows already how great this material giving would be, how vast this field of merit. How much more so, then, were this person to offer it all up to buddhas as numerous as dust motes, honoring and serving every single one of them entirely! "Without a single moment wasted" means that not a single buddha among them was ever passed over without an offering being made — this field of merit's own vastness made all the more evident still! And, further, making this offering of treasures filling all of empty space is itself a vast, expansive mind; making offerings to every buddha is itself the very foremost mind; and never once passing over a single buddha, numerous as dust motes though they are, is itself a constant, unceasing mind — these three minds arising together at once, what, Ānanda, do you make of this? Would such a person, through this excellent cause of giving to buddhas, gain great merit or not?

Sūtra: "Ānanda answered: 'Empty space itself is endless, and its treasures beyond number. In the past, there was a sentient being who, having offered the Buddha a mere seven coins, obtained, upon giving up this present life, the very position of a wheel-turning king — how much more so, then, this present person, who has exhausted the whole of empty space and filled every buddha-land throughout it entirely, offering all these treasures up. Even were one to ponder this for a whole kalpa, one could never reach the full measure of it — how, then, could such merit have any limit at all?'"

Commentary: Ānanda answers: empty space pervades the ten directions entirely — hence, "endless"; treasures fill this same empty space entirely — hence, "beyond number." "In the past" and what follows offers an example, by way of comparison: in the past, there was a sentient being who, offering the Buddha a mere seven coins, obtained the very position of a wheel-turning king — according to the Damo Xianzong Treatise, the venerable Amoghavajra, that is, Aniruddha, once, amid the most excellent of all fields of merit, offered the Buddha, this most excellent field of merit himself, seven coins' worth of food, and, through the later ripening of this same karmic fruit, was reborn, seven times over, into the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, and seven times more, among humans, becoming a wheel-turning king; and, at the very last, was reborn into the great, noble Śākya clan itself — the rest recorded in the Āgamas and elsewhere, not repeated here. A wheel-turning sage-king governs every land throughout all four great continents, the very foremost blessed reward found among humans, complete with the seven treasures, surrounded by a thousand sons. These seven treasures are: the golden wheel-treasure, the horse-treasure, the elephant-treasure, the minister-of-war treasure, the minister-of-the-treasury treasure, the woman-treasure, and the treasure-vase.

How much more so, then, this present person, who has exhausted the whole of empty space, and, moreover, filled every buddha-land of the ten directions entirely with treasures, offering it all up to buddhas as numerous as dust motes — even should one ponder and deliberate over this for a whole kalpa, using this sixth consciousness alone, one could never reach the full measure of it. How, then, could such a blessed reward have any limit at all?

Sūtra: "The Buddha said to Ānanda: 'The words of every buddha and Tathāgata are never false. Suppose, instead, that someone guilty of the four gravest offenses and the ten pārājika transgressions were, in a single instant, to pass through the Avīci hells of this world and of every other world, exhausting every unrelenting hell throughout the ten directions without exception — yet were able, with but a single thought, to explain this very teaching to someone not yet trained in it, sometime in the final kalpa. That person's own karmic obstructions would dissolve the very instant of that thought, transforming the causes of hellish suffering they were to undergo into a land of peace and joy, and the merit gained would surpass that of the person who gave all those treasures by a hundred times, a thousand times, a thousand million times over — beyond anything that number or comparison could ever measure.'"

Commentary: Wishing to bring out the very power of dispelling evil, the Buddha first establishes the truth of his own words. "Because" and what follows: since the time spent proclaiming this sūtra is so very brief, and yet the merit gained through dispelling evil is so very great, this may be difficult to believe — and so the Buddha first raises up the fact that every buddha and Tathāgata's own words are never false, never untrue — you ought, thereby, to believe this with full sincerity.

"Suppose, instead, that someone" possesses the lesser vehicle's own four root offenses — sexual misconduct, killing, stealing, and false speech — and, further, the ten pārājika offenses of the great vehicle. Pārājika carries the meaning of "the very utmost evil," also translated "expulsion" — meaning that, should one commit even a single one of these offenses, one ought, thereby, to fall into hell — how much more so, then, having committed all of them at once? One ought, then, to fall there with the very greatest speed — hence, "in a single instant, pass through the Avīci hells of this world and of every other world," and so on. "A single instant" describes the very shortest span of time — as though saying that this falling is exceedingly swift. Falling first into this world's own Avīci hell, remaining there for a full kalpa; then falling into another world's own Avīci hell, remaining there, too, for a full kalpa; and so, turning onward, exhausting every unrelenting hell throughout the ten directions entirely — "unrelenting" being precisely the Chinese rendering of "Avīci" itself. "Without exception" means none at all left unexperienced — showing that every hell throughout the ten directions, without exception, has been passed through and fully experienced.

This raises up the case of one who, however briefly, proclaims this sūtra — referring precisely to a person on the very verge of falling into hell. "With but a single thought" means: able, with but this single thought, to turn this light of awareness back to illuminate this nature, turning away from the dust of the senses to unite with awakened enlightenment, suddenly awakening to this teaching of perfect penetration, entering deeply into this single gateway, so that these six knowing faculties become, all at once, pure. Having already suddenly awakened to this, and, further, taking this very teaching and explaining it, in this final kalpa, to someone not yet trained in it, causing them, each in turn, to attain this same awakening, continuing the Buddha's own wisdom-life, upholding and glorifying the very seed of buddhahood. And yet, though the effort spent proclaiming this sūtra is so very brief, this alone, through but a single thought, brings a benefit so very great that no comparison could ever capture it.

"That person's own karmic obstructions would dissolve the very instant of that thought" means: this person's own karmic obstructions — precisely the lesser vehicle's four root offenses, the great vehicle's ten offenses of expulsion. "The very instant of that thought" means: corresponding to this same single thought of proclaiming this sūtra, suddenly awakening that suchness is, itself, originally present, and that false thought is, itself, originally empty — what offense, then, would fail to dissolve? What obstruction would fail to be extinguished? Precisely as a single lamp is able to dispel a thousand years of darkness within a sealed room; or as a single spark is able to set an entire field ablaze, reducing it, all at once, to ash entirely.

"Transforming the causes of hellish suffering they were to undergo into a land of peace and joy" means: not merely dispelling this karmic obstruction, but, further, able to transform suffering itself into bliss — and why is this so? Because they were able to take this very teaching and explain it, in this final kalpa, to someone not yet trained in it. This teaching is precisely this sudden, perfect teaching, fully manifesting the entire dharma-realm, bringing this single mind to its very utmost expression — and this single thought of mind is precisely the very true substance by which every Tathāgata of the ten directions accomplishes buddhahood. And so it is able to transform this cause of suffering into a land of bliss, transforming this realm of Māra into the very realm of buddhahood itself!

This is not only freedom from suffering and the attainment of bliss, but, further, boundless merit gained, surpassing all comparison. "The person who gave all those treasures" refers precisely to that person, surpassed here, who earlier filled empty space with treasures and offered them up to buddhas numerous as dust motes. Surpassing them by a hundred times, a thousand times, a thousand million times over, and, turning onward still, beyond anything that number or comparison could ever measure — in sum, however boundless that earlier person's own merit might be said to be, it could never, in the end, reach even the smallest fraction of this present merit at all.

Surpassing that earlier person carries, roughly, three distinct meanings: first, with respect to the Buddha himself — he does not hold material giving to be of the greatest weight, but holds giving the Dharma to be of the greatest weight instead, since one is able, through but a single thought, to awaken to this sudden, perfect teaching. Second, with respect to practice itself — he does not hold self-benefiting practice to be the most essential, but holds benefiting others to be the most essential instead, since one is able, in this final kalpa, to explain this teaching to those not yet trained in it. Third, with respect to merit itself — the merit gained before is merit still subject to outflow, while this present merit is merit free of outflow entirely, since one is able, thereby, to transcend birth and death, permanently escaping the turning wheel of rebirth. It is exactly like a single wish-fulfilling jewel surpassing a thousand kinds of ocean treasure combined, or a single dose of the universal medicine surpassing ten thousand kinds of ordinary remedy — that this present merit should surpass the merit before it stands, then, entirely to reason!

Sūtra: "Ānanda, if there are beings able to recite this sūtra and hold this mantra, were I to explain their merit in full, I could speak for a whole kalpa and never exhaust it. Rely on my teaching, and practice the path exactly as taught, and you will proceed straight to bodhi, with no further demonic hindrance at all."

Commentary: The benefit of proclaiming this sūtra, transforming suffering into bliss, has already been explained above, exactly as stated. Now this speaks, further, of one free of any evil at all, one who recites this sūtra and holds this mantra. "Able to recite this sūtra" means able to recite and uphold this explicit text, single-mindedly, without distraction, understanding both its wording and its meaning clearly and distinctly. "Able to hold this mantra" means able to hold and empower this esoteric mantra, one's own three karmic actions in accord with it, both direct and auxiliary practice combined, naturally able to gain this benefit. "Were I to explain their merit in full, I could speak for a whole kalpa and never exhaust it" means: this same merit of reciting and upholding this teaching, were I to explain it in full, using my own fourfold unobstructed eloquence, speaking of it throughout an entire kalpa, could never exhaust the merit gained and the reward received through it, however long I spoke. And yet, if even this self-benefiting practice alone is already so great as this, how much more so, then, being able, further, to benefit others as well?

"Rely on my teaching, and practice the path exactly as taught" means: as was said in the passage before, "you must take these very words of the Tathāgata and, after my passing, transmit and proclaim them throughout the age of the Dharma's decline, so that every sentient being everywhere may awaken to this teaching" — this first pair of clauses being precisely this same "relying on my teaching." What follows, after the Tathāgata's own passing, proclaiming and teaching this, so that every sentient being everywhere throughout this age of the Dharma's decline, not yet trained in it, comes to understand fully this same meaning of distinguishing demons — this is precisely practicing this path of benefiting others exactly as taught. Reciting this sūtra and holding this mantra is, together, cultivating both the explicit and the esoteric teaching at once; and, cultivating oneself while transforming others as well, this is, again, benefiting both self and other together.

"You will proceed straight to bodhi, with no further demonic hindrance at all" means: how much more so, then, this unsurpassed bodhi, which can only be realized once this twofold benefit, self and other together, is entirely perfected. From the very first ground of abiding, where this mind is first given rise to, one transcends all at once, entering directly into this ocean of wondrous, complete enlightenment, this perfect, complete bodhi — and, throughout this entire span in between, flowing straight through it entirely, no demonic karma is ever again permitted to run rampant and disturb it, no twisting or turning ever again to be found at all. As the Zhizhang Commentary puts it: this sūtra cultivates both the explicit and the esoteric teaching together, benefiting both self and other at once — exactly like a sail hoisted before a following wind, or a lion's own roar sounding above every other creature's cry — and so, naturally, one proceeds straight to bodhi, with no further demonic hindrance at all. This is precisely why this merit is inexhaustible, and why this praise, too, can never come to its own end.

Sūtra: "When the Buddha had finished speaking this sūtra, the monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen; every being of the world — devas, humans, and asuras; bodhisattvas and śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas from other worlds; sages, immortals, and youths; and the powerful ghosts and spirits newly resolved on bodhi — all rejoiced greatly, bowed in reverence, and departed."

Commentary: "When the Buddha had finished speaking this sūtra" means: from the point Ānanda first requested this samādhi, the Tathāgata first showed the buddhas' own general name for this samādhi, causing him to know that every buddha cultivates this cause to achieve this fruit; next explained the path of śamatha, causing him to awaken to this hidden cause, opening widely this perfect understanding; third, explained the practice of samādhi, causing him to enter deeply through this single gateway of the ear-faculty; fourth, explained the realization belonging to dhyāna, causing him to abide in this perfect samādhi, swiftly hastening toward bodhi — and so this true teaching on this wondrous samādhi, from beginning to end, was already complete. He then further detailed what is most essential for the beginning practitioner: first speaking of the seven destinies, urging that they be relinquished, as a warning against lingering long within them; next distinguishing these five demons, so that they might be recognized, to guard against falling away; and, third, at this renewed request, explaining once more the marks of these five aggregates' own arising and ceasing — and so the main body of this teaching, too, was already complete. He then further spoke this concluding, transmissive section, comparing the merit of upholding this teaching, this power of dispelling evil, and this supreme benefit of this twofold gain — and, reaching this point, everything that ought to be spoken has, at last, all been spoken in full.

Monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen have already been explained earlier. In the opening section, only monks were listed; the remaining three, perhaps, arrived only later — now, though, all four are listed together, since, wherever the Tathāgata expounds the Dharma, these four assemblies must, without exception, be present, serving as its own inner protectors. "Every being of the world — devas, humans, and asuras" lists three of the eight kinds of beings; the rest, too, ought equally to be present, since, wherever the Tathāgata expounds the Dharma, all eight kinds must, without exception, be present, serving as its own outer protectors — not listed in the opening section, since this was not yet urgent there.

"Bodhisattvas and śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas from other worlds": bodhisattvas from other worlds were, in the opening section, listed among those who had come earlier to consult and resolve their doubts, and those who arrived later, drawn by the sound of the teaching. Śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas from other worlds were, in the opening section, listed only as pratyekabuddhas beyond further training, arhats not included among them — these, too, perhaps, arriving only later, and so listed together here now. "Sages, immortals, and youths" refers to those who cultivate the sagely path within while manifesting an immortal's own form without, their own childlike purity never once destroyed — hence called sagely immortal-youths; arriving, perhaps, drawn by this teaching, or coming to protect this mantra — hence not listed in the opening section either. "And the powerful ghosts and spirits newly resolved on bodhi": ghosts and spirits are specially noted here as having newly given rise to this resolve; "powerful" expresses how they have set their own will on protecting the Dharma, possessed of great spiritual power, able to subdue demons and restrain outside forces, serving, thereby, as this outer protecting assembly.

"All rejoiced greatly" means: this whole gathered assembly, filled entirely with the very joy of the Dharma — having already heard the main body of this teaching, and thereby attained this originally possessed, wondrous, perfect mind; and, further, having heard this concluding, transmissive teaching, and thereby come to know this ultimate, far-reaching benefit — relying on the Tathāgata's own hidden cause, giving rise to this cultivation and realization of complete meaning, bringing forth the bodhisattva's own ten thousand practices, transcending all at once, entering directly into this ocean of wondrous adornment — rejoicing, of themselves, in having their own share in buddhahood at last, all rejoicing greatly indeed.

"Bowed in reverence, and departed" means: advancing, they had heard the Dharma from their own teacher; withdrawing, they would cultivate this Dharma within themselves. "Bowing in reverence" expresses this rite of gratitude for the Dharma received; "departing" is this very practice of their own further cultivation. And this departure is not an idle departure either — it is a departure of rest, a departure of cessation, a departure into cool, quiet stillness — precisely what is meant by: this wild nature subsides of itself, and this very subsiding is bodhi itself, this supreme, pure, bright mind, pervading the entire dharma-realm, never once attained from any other person at all. With this, the full explanation of this sūtra's own complete text comes, at last, to its end.

On completing this translation. With this fascicle, Ru-Yi Meditation Center's translation of Master Yuanying's twenty-four-fascicle Śūraṅgama Sūtra Jiǎngyì — as far as we are aware, the first complete English translation of this commentary specifically, distinct from the better-known English edition that draws on Master Hsuan Hua's own teaching instead — is complete. Yuanying began writing this commentary at sixty-eight, after more than forty years of study, and finished it at seventy-four, the year of his death, regarding it as the central teaching project of his life. This translation project has run alongside the companion bare-sūtra translation, reusing that project's own wording wherever Yuanying quotes the sūtra directly, and adding, for the first time in English, his own complete clause-by-clause commentary throughout — from the tenfold preliminary analysis in fascicle 1, through the seven-location search for the mind, the ten displays of seeing, the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha, all twenty-five accounts of perfect penetration, the Śūraṅgama Mantra, the fifty-five stages of the path, the seven destinies, and the fifty states of demonic interference across all five aggregates, to this final teaching on the five layers of false thought underlying them all.
