A Crooked Cause Brings a Twisted Result

Ānanda, now understanding the theory of perfect penetration, asks a starkly practical question: how does one actually set up a place of practice safe from demonic interference? The Buddha's answer begins with the three decisive principles — gathering the mind is precepts, from precepts samādhi arises, from samādhi wisdom is born — and unfolds into the four clear instructions against sexual misconduct, killing, stealing, and false claims of spiritual attainment, each with its own vivid analogy for why partial effort cannot work. The fascicle closes with the Śūraṅgama Mantra recommended as help for lingering habits from past lives, and the elaborate, symbol-laden building of the ritual platform itself, culminating in a hundred days of practice.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 16 closed the sūtra's entire twenty-five approaches to perfect penetration, ending with Mañjuśrī's verse and the assembly's own awakening. This fascicle turns from theory to practice. Ānanda, understanding the method itself, now asks a different question entirely: for beings of the final age, encountering false teachers "as numerous as the sands of the Ganges," how does one actually set up a place of practice, safe from demonic interference? The Buddha's answer opens with the three decisive principles underlying all Buddhist practice — precepts, samādhi, wisdom — and then unfolds into the sūtra's four clear instructions in full: severing sexual desire, killing, stealing, and false claims of spiritual attainment, each following the same pattern Yuanying draws out clause by clause — a stated principle, the specific kind of demonic or spirit-realm rebirth that results from ignoring it, and a vivid analogy for why partial effort cannot succeed. The Buddha then recommends the Śūraṅgama Mantra as help for habits carried over from past lives too deep for precepts alone to clear, illustrated by Ānanda's own case with Mātaṅgī. The fascicle closes with the Buddha's detailed, richly symbolic instructions for building the ritual platform itself — the white bull's dung mixed with sandalwood, the ten kinds of incense, the eight mirrors, the offerings, the images of Vairocana, Śākyamuni, and Avalokiteśvara — and the resulting three-week and hundred-day schedule of practice, said to bring even a beginning practitioner to the stream-enterer's own realization.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. The companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 6 already carries Ānanda's opening request and a condensed rendering of the four clear instructions; where the exact same clauses recur here, that existing translation is reused, keeping the refrain "this is what the Buddha teaches; anyone who teaches otherwise is Māra's own teaching" fixed throughout, as that project's own notes require. Because Yuanying quotes the sūtra here clause by clause, in far finer grain than that earlier condensed rendering, most of the sūtra text quoted in this fascicle — and all of Yuanying's own commentary, including the full account of the ritual platform's construction — is translated fresh.

Ānanda's Request 庚二 道場加行 · 壬初 阿難請

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難整衣服，於大眾中，合掌頂禮，心跡圓明，悲欣交集，欲益未來諸眾生故，稽首白佛：大悲世尊！我今已悟，成佛法門，是中修行，得無疑惑。

〖講〗此道場加行科，乃為圓根近下者設也。然上科為初方便，此科更為最初方便，亦助修圓通而已。阿難整理衣服，於大眾中，合十指爪，頂禮於佛。心跡圓明，悲欣交集者：心即根中圓湛不生不滅之真心；跡即旋流反聞，次第解結之事跡；不達本有之心，則曠劫積行，徒勞無益；不達修證之事，則塵埋寶藏，莫救貧窮。今性修畢達，故曰圓明，即上明了其家所歸道路之義；得路未歸故悲，到家有望故欣，一時悲欣交集於一心也。

自解已圓，愍後未悟，現欲利益未來末法諸眾生等。稽首白佛，稱曰大悲世尊：因如來悲心無盡故。我今已悟，成佛法門者：言我求成菩提最初方便，而今相似解發，已悟成佛法門。是中修行，得無疑惑者：是中指耳根之中，修行即離塵照性，旋流反聞工夫，得無有疑惑，不勞重建道場也。

〖經〗常聞如來，說如是言：自未得度，先度人者，菩薩發心，自覺已圓，能覺他者，如來應世。我雖未度，願度末劫，一切眾生。

〖講〗此顯道場，專為心跡，未得圓明者請也。常聞如來所說如是言，即指下之所說：無論因人果人，皆以利他為事業。自未得度先度人者：此指因人；無明未盡，猶非得度，但捨己利他，此是菩薩發心，以度人為先，先盡生界，後取菩提也。自覺已圓，能覺他者：此指果人；自心三覺已經圓滿，萬德畢備，而能覺悟法界眾生，此如來十號，以應世為本，倒駕慈航果後興悲也。我雖未度三句，願學菩薩，發利他之心，我雖未度，願度末劫，一切眾生；此為地藏菩薩，眾生度盡方證菩提是也。

〖經〗世尊！此諸眾生，去佛漸遠，邪師說法，如恆河沙，欲攝其心，入三摩地，云何令其安立道場，遠諸魔事？於菩提心，得無退屈？

〖講〗此請道場，遠離魔事。復稱世尊，此諸眾生，指末劫時候，去佛漸遠，根機愈薄，邪說競興，魚目難辨，蠱惑初心，彼彼皆是，如恆河沙之多。且邪必亂正，有欲初心後學，收攝其心，從聞、思、修，入三摩地，豈不難哉？敢請如來，云何令其初心後學，安立道場，使諸魔事，自然遠離，不能惑亂，於菩提心，念念增長，得無有退屈也？初阿難請竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: Ānanda straightened his robes, and, in the midst of the assembly, joined his palms and bowed. His mind and its own outward trace both now perfectly clear, sorrow and joy mingling within him, wishing to benefit sentient beings still to come, he bowed his head and said to the Buddha: "Greatly compassionate World-Honored One, I have now awakened to the gateway of Buddhahood, and, in this matter of practice, am free of doubt."

Commentary: This section, on the auxiliary practice of the place of practice, is set up specifically for those of a lesser, more middling capacity for the perfect faculty. The section above already gave the very first skillful means; this section gives, further, the most initial skillful means of all, likewise nothing more than an aid to cultivating perfect penetration. Ānanda straightens his own robes, and, in the midst of the assembly, joins his own ten fingers together, bowing at the Buddha's feet. "His mind and its own outward trace both now perfectly clear, sorrow and joy mingling within him": "mind" is exactly this true mind, perfectly calm, unborn, unceasing, found within the sense-faculty itself; "trace" is exactly the actual course of practice — turning the stream back, turning hearing back upon itself, untying the knot stage by stage. Failing to reach this already-possessed mind, one's practice, however long accumulated across kalpas, remains wasted effort, of no benefit at all; failing to reach the actual matter of this cultivation and realization, this treasury lies buried in dust, unable to relieve one's own poverty. Now, both this nature and this cultivation reached at once, hence, "perfectly clear" — exactly the same sense as "coming to understand clearly one's own home, and the road that leads back to it," described earlier. Having found the road, yet not yet home, hence sorrow; having real hope of reaching home, hence joy — sorrow and joy mingling together, all at once, within this single mind.

His own understanding already complete, he feels compassion for those still to come who have not yet awakened, and now wishes to benefit every sentient being of the future, the age of the Dharma's decline. He bows his head and addresses the Buddha, calling him "greatly compassionate World-Honored One" — because the Tathāgata's own compassionate mind is inexhaustible. "I have now awakened to the gateway of Buddhahood": that is, what I myself sought, this very first skillful means toward accomplishing bodhi, has now, a resembling understanding having arisen, been awakened to. "In this matter of practice, am free of doubt": "this" points to within the ear-faculty itself; "practice" is exactly the effort of leaving the sense-object behind to illuminate this nature directly, turning the stream back, turning hearing back upon itself — free of any doubt at all, requiring no further establishing of a place of practice at all.

Sūtra: "I have often heard the Tathāgata say words such as these: 'one who has not yet crossed over, yet first brings others across, is a bodhisattva's own resolve; one whose own awakening is already complete, and who is able to awaken others, is a Tathāgata appearing in the world.' Though I myself am not yet fully crossed over, I vow to bring across every being of this final age."

Commentary: This displays how this place of practice is requested specifically for those whose own mind and its outward trace have not yet been made perfectly clear. "I have often heard the Tathāgata say" points exactly to what follows: whether a being still at the causal stage or one already at the fruit stage, benefiting others is, in every case, one's own life's work. "One who has not yet crossed over, yet first brings others across" points exactly to a being at the causal stage — ignorance not yet exhausted, not yet truly crossed over, yet setting aside one's own benefit to benefit others first: this is exactly a bodhisattva's own resolve, placing the crossing-over of others first, exhausting the realm of sentient beings before finally taking up bodhi for oneself. "One whose own awakening is already complete, and who is able to awaken others" points exactly to a being at the fruit stage — one's own three kinds of awakening already perfectly complete, every virtue fully possessed, and thereby able to awaken sentient beings throughout the Dharma-realm — this is exactly the Tathāgata's own ten titles, taking appearing in the world as their own root, turning the ship of compassion back around once more, giving rise to compassion even after the fruit is already attained. The three lines "though I myself am not yet fully crossed over" express a wish to learn this same bodhisattva's own resolve to benefit others — though I myself am not yet fully crossed over, I vow to bring across every being of this final age — exactly as with Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva, who vows not to realize bodhi until every sentient being has been brought across first.

Sūtra: "World-Honored One, as these beings grow further removed from the Buddha's own time, false teachers will proclaim their teachings as numerous as the sands of the Ganges. If beings wish to gather their minds and enter samādhi, how should they set up a place of practice, so as to stay far from demonic interference, and never retreat from the mind of bodhi?"

Commentary: This requests a place of practice, that it might stay far from demonic interference. Addressing the Buddha again, "these beings" refers to beings of the final age, growing ever further removed from the Buddha's own time, their own capacities growing ever weaker, false teachings arising in ever greater competition, fish-eyes and pearls scarcely distinguishable, bewitching beginning practitioners — such teachers everywhere, as numerous as the sands of the Ganges. And falseness is certain to disturb what is true — should a beginning practitioner, a later student, wish to gather in the mind and enter samādhi through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating, would this not prove exceedingly difficult? And so he ventures to ask the Tathāgata: how might one cause such beginning practitioners, such later students, to set up a place of practice, so that every demonic affair might naturally stay far away, unable to bewitch or disturb them, and so that, regarding the mind of bodhi, they might grow only stronger, moment after moment, never retreating from it at all? This closes the first section, Ānanda's own request.

The Three Decisive Principles 壬二 如來說

Chinese Source

〖經〗爾時世尊，於大眾中，稱讚阿難：善哉！善哉！如汝所問，安立道場，救護眾生，末劫沉溺，汝今諦聽：當為汝說。阿難大眾，唯然奉教。

〖講〗此讚其請問之當，而許說也。《正脈》云：善哉有二意：一、善其發利他心，得菩薩正行。二、善其請道場意，得利他法要也。意謂如汝所問，祗恐邪師說法，擾亂定心，必須安立道場，方可遠離魔事；此誠救度一切眾生，末劫之時，免於沉淪陷溺，極要之法也。汝今諦聽，吾當為汝分別解說。阿難大眾唯然奉教者：唯然應諾之辭，奉命承教，而諦聽也。

〖經〗佛告阿難：汝常聞我毘奈耶中，宣說修行，三決定義，所謂：攝心為戒，因戒生定，因定發慧，是則名為，三無漏學。

〖講〗此佛正說道場助行。《寶鏡疏》云：以持戒誦咒，為助修之行也。蓋前佛對當機，一往發明如來藏心，由耳根圓通，悟理直入者，乃為正修也。今當機憐愍末劫，恐多障難，故遠魔辯異，設立道場，所以如來，有帶事兼修之說，名為助行，謂助正修之行也。然助有二：一、惑重者，先持禁戒，以制斷發業無明；二、習重者，兼持秘咒，令熏斷俱生無明。故知末世修禪定者，非此助行，則不免於邪說所惑，而成魔魅也。

佛告阿難：汝尋常豈不聞我所宣說，《毘奈耶中》，有三種修行，決定義耶？毘奈耶，大小乘律藏之通名，故佛常說，阿難常聞。毘尼，此翻善治，謂自治淫、怒、癡，亦能治人淫、怒、癡，亦云調伏，謂調練三業，制伏過非也。毘奈耶，正詮戒學，兼詮定、慧，故成三決定義，謂決定依此修證也。

攝心為戒者：檢異權小，多約身、口事相為戒；今約大乘，但收攝其心，一念不生，眾戒具足，所以攝心，便名為戒。因戒生定者：心既攝已，寂然不動，漸生於定，如風止息，波浪自停。《圓覺經》云：「尸羅不清淨，三昧不現前。」因定發慧者：定心成就，本明漸發，照涵萬象，如風停水湛，自體生光。《圓覺經》云：「無礙清淨慧，皆從禪定生。」則名為三無漏學：以能修三無漏學，不漏落於生死輪迴故。

《正脈》云：今此所修，仍是耳根圓通，但為最初近下之根，特加戒律、道場、持咒之三事，故大科云加行也。至於因戒所生之定慧，仍是亡塵盡根妙定，及彼定所得三空慧耳。觀經文但惟詳戒，而略於定慧可見也。問：「前門何不用此？」答：「中根煩惱輕微，無自起淫等四念，且彼於亡塵時，防護有力，世俗曲屈聲，已不容入，安有自起四念，何況道理徑直等聲，一併止絕哉？至於阿難初果，已能不入色聲等六塵，但加反聞，尚不多費亡塵之力，安有自起婬等之念耶？故知斯門，特為塵勞素重，不待聲引而頻舉，自發淫等四念，乃至身口，亦所未免者，加四戒以為反聞之前方便；加道場持咒，以為正反聞時之助行也。舊解全不知此，杳無一字，豈自此後，別為一法門哉？此非一小失，故特為警示學人珍重。」結名三無漏者，戒中已自不容一念漏於諸非，何況定慧？然此非但不漏落於三有而已，以注心反聞，兼不漏落空有二邊，所以為真無漏，而非小乘比也。二總舉三學竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: At that time the World-Honored One, in the midst of the great assembly, praised Ānanda: "Excellent, excellent! Just as you have asked, setting up a place of practice, so as to rescue and protect sentient beings from sinking and drowning in this final age — listen closely now, and I shall explain this for you." Ānanda and the assembly said, "Yes indeed," and received this teaching.

Commentary: This praises the fitness of this request, and grants permission to explain it. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "excellent" carries two senses — first, approving his having given rise to this mind of benefiting others, attaining a bodhisattva's own true conduct; second, approving the very intention behind requesting this place of practice, attaining the essential method of benefiting others. This means: exactly as you have asked, fearing only that false teachers' own teaching will disturb the settled mind, one must set up a place of practice, and only then can demonic interference be kept far away — this is, indeed, the most essential method for rescuing every sentient being, in this final age, from sinking and drowning. Listen closely now, and I shall explain this for you, distinguishing each point in turn. "Ānanda and the assembly said, 'Yes indeed,' and received this teaching": "yes indeed" is a word of assent, receiving this command and this teaching, and listening closely to it.

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "You have often heard me teach, in the monastic discipline, the three decisive principles of practice — that gathering the mind is precepts; that from precepts, samādhi arises; and that from samādhi, wisdom is born. These are called the three undefiled trainings."

Commentary: This is the Buddha's own direct teaching of this auxiliary practice for the place of practice. As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: upholding the precepts and reciting the mantra serve as this auxiliary practice of cultivation. For the Buddha's own earlier teaching, addressed to this same capacity, went straight to revealing the mind of the tathāgatagarbha, entering directly through awakening to this principle by way of the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration — this being exactly the true, direct cultivation itself. Now, this same capacity, out of compassion for beings of this final age, fearing many obstacles and difficulties for them, seeks to keep demonic influence at bay and distinguish it from what is genuine, setting up this place of practice — and so the Tathāgata gives this teaching involving actual ritual matters, cultivated alongside the main practice, called this auxiliary practice, that is, a practice that assists the true, direct cultivation itself. Yet this assistance is of two kinds: first, for one whose own delusion runs heavy, first upholding the prohibitive precepts, to restrain and cut off the ignorance that initiates karma; second, for one whose own habituated tendencies run heavy, further upholding the secret mantra, so as to habituate away the innate ignorance born together with oneself. And so one knows that a practitioner of meditative absorption in this final age, without this same auxiliary practice, cannot avoid being bewitched by false teachings, and falling prey to demonic delusion.

The Buddha told Ānanda: have you not, in the ordinary course of things, often heard me teach, within the monastic discipline, these three decisive principles of practice? "Vinaya" is the general name for the treasury of monastic discipline shared by both the greater and lesser vehicles — hence the Buddha constantly taught it, and Ānanda constantly heard it. "Vinaya" is translated "skillfully governing," meaning governing one's own lust, anger, and delusion, and being able, further, to govern others' lust, anger, and delusion as well; also translated "taming and subduing," meaning disciplining and training the three karmic activities, restraining and subduing every fault and wrong. The Vinaya itself directly expresses the training in precepts, while also expressing, alongside it, the trainings in samādhi and wisdom — and so it forms these three decisive principles, meaning that cultivation and realization must decisively rely upon them.

"Gathering the mind is precepts": this distinguishes it from the provisional and lesser teachings, which mostly define precepts in terms of the outward marks of bodily and verbal conduct; here, in terms of the greater vehicle, one simply gathers in the mind itself — not a single thought arising, and every precept is thereby complete in full — and so gathering the mind is itself called precepts. "From precepts, samādhi arises": the mind already gathered in, still and unmoving, samādhi gradually arises from it — exactly as, once the wind stops, the waves settle of themselves. As the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment puts it: "where moral discipline is not pure, samādhi does not appear." "From samādhi, wisdom is born": the mind of samādhi accomplished, this original brightness gradually arises, illuminating and embracing the ten thousand forms — exactly as, once the wind stills and the water grows calm, this same water gives rise to its own reflected light of itself. As the Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment puts it: "unobstructed, pure wisdom is, in every case, born from meditative absorption." These are called the three undefiled trainings: since cultivating these three undefiled trainings prevents one from falling, through any outflow, back into the wheel of birth and death.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: what is cultivated here remains, throughout, the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration — only, for this most initial, lesser capacity, three particular matters are specially added: moral discipline, the place of practice, and holding the mantra — hence this whole section is called the auxiliary practice. As for the samādhi and wisdom that arise from these precepts, these remain, throughout, exactly this wondrous samādhi of letting go of the sense-object and exhausting the sense-faculty, and the threefold emptiness of wisdom attained through this same samādhi. One can see, observing the sūtra's own text, that it dwells in detail on precepts alone, and treats samādhi and wisdom only briefly. One might ask: why was this not used in the earlier gate? The answer: a middling capacity's own affliction runs light, giving rise to no spontaneous thought of lust and the rest of these four — and, further, at the very moment of letting go of the sense-object, their own guarding power is already strong, such that even worldly, twisted sounds can no longer enter at all — how, then, could any spontaneous thought of these four ever arise, how much less any sound of straightforward doctrine and the like, all alike already cut off entirely? As for Ānanda himself, already at the first fruit, already able to keep the six sense-objects of form, sound, and the rest from entering at all, needing only to add this turning of hearing back upon itself, scarcely even expending much effort at letting go of the sense-object — how, then, could any spontaneous thought of lust and the rest ever arise in him? One thereby knows that this present gate is set up specifically for those whose own worldly toil runs habitually heavy, who need no sound to draw them on before these four thoughts arise repeatedly and spontaneously, extending even to body and speech, unable to avoid them entirely — and so these four precepts are added as the preliminary skillful means before turning hearing back upon itself, and the place of practice and holding the mantra are added as the auxiliary practice accompanying this same turning of hearing back, properly carried out. Earlier commentaries entirely fail to grasp this, not a single word given on it — how could this possibly be some separate, distinct Dharma-gate, set apart from everything before it? This is no small oversight, and so I specially flag it here, that students might treasure this point. As for being named the three undefiled trainings: within the precepts alone, not even a single thought is already permitted to leak into any wrongdoing — how much more so, then, samādhi and wisdom? Yet this is not merely freedom from falling, through any outflow, into the three states of existence alone — for, fixing the mind on turning hearing back upon itself, one is, further, kept from falling, through any outflow, into either extreme of emptiness or existence as well — and this is exactly why it is called truly undefiled, not to be compared with the lesser vehicle's own trainings at all. This closes the second section, giving, in summary, these three trainings.

First: Sever Sexual Desire 卯初 斷淫

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難！云何攝心，我名為戒？

〖講〗心為戒本，若攝之則名持戒，若放之則為破戒，故以攝心，名為戒也。

〖經〗若諸世界，六道眾生，其心不淫，則不隨其生死相續，

〖講〗此明戒為三學之首。以初卷至四卷半，皆明生信開解，即是慧學。自舉喻華屋，請示修門，至此乃是定學。以故向下為根機稍下者，正明戒學。則知此經，俱詮三學。然唯說四重，以是大小二乘，共具之根本戒也。溫陵曰：諸經戒殺居首，謂設化以慈悲為先；此經婬戒居首，為真修以離欲為本。蓋欲氣麤濁，染污妙明，欲習狂迷，易失正受，續生死喪真常，莫甚於此，故須首戒也。

若諸世界，六道眾生：皆因淫欲，而正性命，是以纏縛生死。其心不婬，則不隨其生死相續者：而於婬欲一事，非但不動身口，亦不生一念思想之心，方為不犯，方與攝心為戒相應。餘三仿此，既心戒清淨，則樹倒根斷，枝葉不生，自不隨其生死相續。如後乾慧地文云：「欲愛乾枯，根境不偶，現前殘質，不復續生。」可證斯義。

〖經〗汝修三昧，本出塵勞，淫心不除，塵不可出。

〖講〗汝修耳根三昧，本思出離塵勞，塵勞即界內見思二惑。淫心不除，塵勞必不能出，淫欲為塵勞上首故。

〖經〗縱有多智，禪定現前，如不斷淫，必落魔道：上品魔王，中品魔民，下品魔女。

〖講〗此承上文，帶淫修禪，塵不可出。縱有多智者：世智辯聰，善說諸法。禪定現前者：世間禪定，善入住出，而得現前。縱有二字，雙貫多智、禪定二種。如不斷淫者：於修三昧時，不捨絕淫念，皆為魔業，必落魔道，魔樂欲境。今既不斷淫欲，上品則墮魔王，以魔界欲勝、福強、心靈，非禪智不能便生；今以禪智而助淫心，使淫增熾。如陰魔中，行空禪者，卻留塵勞，廣化七珍，多增寶媛是也。設無世智禪定，則直墮地獄無疑矣！中品則墮魔民，下品則墮魔女，上、中、下三品，則隨福厚薄，以為階降耳。

〖經〗彼等諸魔，亦有徒眾，各各自謂，成無上道。

〖講〗彼等諸魔，其徒實繁；以淫欲為傳法，遞相傳授，令不斷絕，各各自謂成無上道。我慢成習，非果計果。如受魔文云：其人亦不覺知魔著，自言謂得無上涅槃是也。初首陳持犯利害竟。

〖經〗我滅度後，末法之中，多此魔民，熾盛世間，廣行貪淫，為善知識，令諸眾生，落愛見坑，失菩提路。

〖講〗我者佛自稱。滅五住煩惱，度二種生死，即去世之別稱也。正像已過，末法之中；去聖時遙，魔強法弱，故云多此魔民，熾盛世間。此多魔教，生受其惑，死為魔眷；如後受魔文云：「命終之後，必為魔民」是也。如火熾然盛發，世間盡受其焚；如後想魔文云：「如是邪精，魅其心腑，近則九生，多踰百世，令真修行、總成魔眷」是也。

廣行貪淫者：即先世帶淫修禪之輩，來為教師，現通現慧，密教行淫，以為佛事，遞相傳授。為善知識者：自以為善知行淫之事，善識行淫之理；如後想魔文云：「讚歎行淫，不毀麤行，將諸猥媟，以為傳法」是也。令諸眾生，落愛見坑，失菩提路：愛即思惑，見即見惑，而以淫欲，為教化之端，則墮愛坑；而以淫欲，為真修之法，則墮見坑；不能自出，而失菩提正路矣！

〖經〗汝教世人，修三摩地，先斷心淫。是名如來，先佛世尊，第一決定，清淨明誨。

〖講〗此囑阿難，轉教末世。初心修三摩地者：即耳根圓通。淫欲為大定冤賊，不唯身不行淫事，且要心不起淫念方可。此正防微杜漸，臨深履薄之誡。果能如是，方名如來，先佛世尊，第一決定，清淨明誨。如來指今佛，先佛世尊，指過去諸佛，今昔之佛，皆作是說，定可信從也。第一者，居四重之首，決定斷除心淫，清淨脩學，究竟明誨也。

〖經〗是故阿難！若不斷淫，修禪定者，如蒸沙石，欲其成飯，經百千劫，只名熱沙。何以故？此非飯本，沙石成故。

〖講〗此承上是佛教斷淫之故。阿難！若不斷淫心，修禪定者，如蒸沙石，欲其成飯，經百千劫，祗名熱沙，此非飯本。而以禪定薰修，喻之以蒸；以不生滅心，為本修因，然後圓成果地修證，喻如蒸米成飯。

〖經〗汝以淫身，求佛妙果，縱得妙悟，皆是淫根，根本成淫，輪轉三途，必不能出；如來涅槃，何路修證？

〖講〗設汝不斷淫欲，是以淫身，求佛所證妙覺極果；此因果不孚，如蒸沙成飯相似。縱得妙悟者：謂縱經多劫修習，得開妙悟，合上經百千劫，只名熱沙；經劫修習如蒸，得開妙悟如熱相。皆是淫根五句：合上此非飯本，沙石成故。根本成淫者：有淫欲種子，觸處便發，由此惡業日臻，必至輪轉三途，必不能出；人身尚不可得，況欲修佛果耶？如來涅槃者：佛所證之如如理；何路修證者：沙石成故，決難成飯也。

〖經〗必使淫機，身心俱斷，斷性亦無，於佛菩提，斯可希冀。

〖講〗機者，發動之由，如弩牙也。謂身之淫機，由心使作，心之淫機，由念弛放，必使一念不生，身心二途，方得俱斷。若使能斷之性猶存，而所斷緣影仍在，終非杜絕，必使斷性亦無，方稱藥病雙除，始為無病人也。正如息機歸寂然，諸幻成無性矣！即此無幻之性，方合於佛果菩提，菩提佛所證之如如智，斯可希冀矣！此與上科涅槃，乃理智一如，互影說故。《寶鏡疏》云：身斷淫，律儀戒也；心斷淫，定共戒也；斷性亦無，道共戒也。初曲分損益之相竟。

〖經〗如我此說，名為佛說；不如此說，即波旬說。

〖講〗此判決邪正。以當機深防，未來邪說亂正，魔佛難辨，而求說辨魔規則，佛則以四根本戒，以辨驗之，令末世修學，永為龜鑑。如我此說，修三摩地，先斷心淫者，是則名為佛說，急宜從之。不如此說，或行淫不礙真修，是即波旬說，速當絕之。波旬訛也，正云波卑夜，此云惡者。能傷法身，而害慧命者也，此釋迦出世，魔王名也。魔字從石，梁武帝謂，此惱害於人，易之為鬼，今從梁易也。初斷淫竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, how is gathering the mind itself what I call precepts?"

Commentary: The mind is the very root of the precepts — gather it in, and this is called upholding the precepts; let it loose, and this becomes breaking the precepts. And so gathering the mind itself is called precepts.

Sūtra: "Should the sentient beings of the six destinies, throughout every world, hold no lust in their minds, they would not follow the continuity of birth and death."

Commentary: This makes plain that the precepts stand foremost among these three trainings. From fascicle one all the way to the middle of fascicle four, the sūtra makes plain giving rise to faith and opening understanding — this being exactly the training in wisdom. From the analogy of the jeweled hall onward, requesting to be shown the gate of practice, all the way to this point, has been the training in samādhi. And so, from this point on, for those of a somewhat lesser capacity, the training in precepts is now made plain directly. One thereby knows that this sūtra expresses all three trainings throughout. Yet it speaks only of these four precepts, since these are the root precepts shared in common by both the greater and lesser vehicles. As Wenling put it: in every other sūtra, the precept against killing stands first, since compassion is placed foremost in setting up any teaching; in this sūtra, the precept against sexual misconduct stands first, since true cultivation takes leaving desire behind as its own root. For the very energy of desire is coarse and turbid, defiling this wondrous brightness; the habit of desire is wild and confused, easily losing correct absorption — nothing continues birth and death, and loses this true, constant nature, more than this alone — and so it must be given first among the precepts.

"Should the sentient beings of the six destinies, throughout every world": every one of them, because of sexual desire, fixes their own life and destiny upon it, and so is bound fast to birth and death. "Hold no lust in their minds, they would not follow the continuity of birth and death": that is, regarding this one matter of sexual desire, not only does the body and speech remain unmoved, but not even a single thought or notion of it arises in the mind — only this counts as no transgression at all, only this truly accords with gathering the mind as precepts. The remaining three precepts follow this same pattern. Once the mind's own precept is thus purified, the tree falls and its root is severed, no branch or leaf able to grow from it again — and one naturally no longer follows this same continuity of birth and death. As a later passage, on the ground of dry wisdom, puts it: "the craving for desire dried up entirely, sense-faculty and sense-object no longer paired, this present remaining substance no longer continuing to be born again" — this bears out exactly this same meaning.

Sūtra: "Your cultivation of samādhi is meant, at root, to escape worldly affliction — but if the mind of lust is not removed, that affliction cannot be escaped."

Commentary: Your cultivation of the ear-faculty's own samādhi is meant, at root, to escape worldly affliction — "worldly affliction" being exactly the two afflictions of views and thought, still within the three realms. Should the mind of lust not be removed, this same worldly affliction can never be escaped at all — since desire for sexual union stands foremost among every worldly affliction.

Sūtra: "However much wisdom you may have, however much samādhi may appear before you, if lust is not severed, you will certainly fall into the way of demons — at best, a demon-king; in the middle, one of the demon-king's own people; at worst, a demon-woman."

Commentary: This follows directly from what came above: cultivating Chan while still carrying lust, worldly affliction cannot be escaped. "However much wisdom you may have": worldly wisdom and eloquence, skilled at expounding every teaching. "However much samādhi may appear before you": worldly meditative absorption, skillfully entering, abiding in, and emerging from it, brought fully to appear. The word "however" governs both "wisdom" and "samādhi" together. "If lust is not severed": at the very time of cultivating samādhi, failing to set aside and cut off every thought of lust, all of it becomes the very work of Māra, certain to fall into the way of demons — for demons delight in the very realm of desire. Now, lust not yet severed, at best one falls to become a demon-king — for, within the realm of Māra, desire prevails, blessing runs strong, and the mind is sharp, none of which can arise apart from meditative absorption and wisdom; and now, using this same absorption and wisdom to assist the mind of lust instead, causing this lust to blaze only more fiercely. As with those, among the fifty states of demonic interference, who cultivate an absorption in emptiness, yet retain worldly affliction within it, broadly manifesting the seven treasures, and multiplying their own consorts of jeweled beauty. Lacking any such worldly wisdom or absorption, one would fall directly into hell, without a doubt! In the middle, one falls to become one of the demon-king's own people; at worst, one falls to become a demon-woman — these three grades, best, middling, and worst, following according to how thick or thin one's own blessing happens to be.

Sūtra: "Every one of these demons has, in turn, their own followers, each and every one of them claiming to have accomplished the unsurpassed path."

Commentary: These demons' own followers are, in truth, numerous indeed — taking sexual union itself as the very transmission of the Dharma, passed on from one to the next without ever breaking off, each and every one claiming to have accomplished the unsurpassed path. Arrogance become habit, mistaking what is not the fruit for the fruit itself — exactly as the passage on being possessed by demons, further on, puts it: this person, too, remains entirely unaware of being possessed by a demon, declaring themselves to have attained unsurpassed nirvāṇa. This closes the first section, first setting forth the benefit and harm of keeping or breaking this precept.

Sūtra: "After I have passed away, in the age of the Dharma's decline, many such demonic followers will flourish in the world, indulging openly in lust while posing as spiritual guides, causing beings to fall into the pit of love and false view, losing the path to bodhi."

Commentary: "I" is the Buddha's own self-reference. Extinguishing the five kinds of settled affliction, crossing over both kinds of birth and death — this is exactly a distinct term for the Buddha's own departure from the world. The ages of the true and semblance Dharma already past, within the age of the Dharma's decline, the time of the sages grown ever more distant, demons strong and the Dharma weak — hence, "many such demonic followers will flourish in the world." Under this same demonic teaching, taught while alive, one comes under its delusion; dying, one becomes one of the demon-king's own kin — exactly as a later passage, on being possessed by demons, puts it: "once this life ends, one is certain to become one of the demon-king's own people." Exactly like a fire blazing up fiercely, consuming everything in the world — as a later passage, on delusion by demons, puts it: "this same deviant essence bewitches the very organs of the heart — at its closest, within nine lifetimes, at its most extreme, across more than a hundred generations, turning even a genuine practitioner entirely into one of the demon-king's own kin."

"Indulging openly in lust": that is, exactly this same kind who, in a past life, carried lust while cultivating Chan, now come as teachers, manifesting spiritual powers, manifesting wisdom, secretly teaching sexual union as though it were the Buddha's own work, passing this on from one to the next in turn. "Posing as spiritual guides": taking themselves to be skilled in the practice of sexual union, well versed in its own underlying principle — exactly as a later passage, on delusion by demons, puts it: "praising the practice of sexual union, never condemning coarse conduct, taking every kind of lewdness itself as the very transmission of the Dharma." "Causing beings to fall into the pit of love and false view, losing the path to bodhi": "love" is exactly the affliction of thought, and "view" is exactly the affliction of views — and, taking sexual desire as the very starting point of their own teaching and transformation, one falls into the pit of love; taking sexual desire as the very method of true cultivation itself, one falls into the pit of false view — unable to climb out of either, and so losing the true path to bodhi entirely!

Sūtra: "In teaching people of the world to cultivate samādhi, first sever the mind's own lust — this is the Tathāgata's, and every past buddha's, first decisive and pure instruction."

Commentary: This charges Ānanda with passing this teaching on to the beings of this final age. A beginning practitioner cultivating samādhi refers exactly to the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration. Sexual desire is the very enemy and thief of this great samādhi — not only must the body refrain from any act of sexual union, but the mind, too, must refrain from giving rise to even a single thought of lust, and only then does this suffice. This is exactly the warning to guard against the subtlest beginning, to nip it in the bud, to tread as though on thin ice above a deep abyss. Only if one is truly able to do this can this be called the Tathāgata's, and every past buddha's, first decisive and pure instruction. "The Tathāgata" points to the present Buddha; "every past buddha" points to every buddha of the past — buddhas of the present and of ages past alike, all having taught exactly this, certainly to be believed and followed. "First" means standing at the very head of these four precepts — decisively severing this lust of the mind, this ultimate, pure instruction for cultivation and practice.

Sūtra: "Therefore, Ānanda, cultivating meditative absorption without severing lust is like steaming sand and stone, hoping it will become rice — though a hundred thousand kalpas may pass, it remains nothing but hot sand, since sand and stone were never rice to begin with."

Commentary: This follows from the reason given above for the Buddha's own teaching to sever lust. Ānanda, should one cultivate meditative absorption without severing the mind of lust, this is exactly like steaming sand and stone, hoping it will become rice — though a hundred thousand kalpas may pass, this remains nothing but hot sand, never the true basis for rice at all. Cultivating and habituating through meditative absorption is likened here to this same steaming; taking the unborn, unceasing mind as the very root cause of cultivation, and thereby perfectly accomplishing cultivation and realization at the fruit stage, is likened to steaming rice until it becomes cooked rice itself.

Sūtra: "Taking this body still caught in lust, and seeking thereby the Buddha's own wondrous fruit — even should you attain some wondrous awakening, it remains, throughout, rooted in lust; this very root having become lust itself, you will turn in the wheel through the three lower destinies, and can never escape it. What path, then, could ever lead to the Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa, cultivated and realized?"

Commentary: Should you fail to sever sexual desire, taking this same body still caught in lust, and seeking thereby the Buddha's own realized, wondrous, ultimate fruit — this cause and this fruit fail to correspond to one another at all, exactly like steaming sand, hoping it will become rice. "Even should you attain some wondrous awakening": that is, even should one cultivate and practice across many kalpas, and attain some opening of wondrous awakening — corresponding to "though a hundred thousand kalpas may pass, it remains nothing but hot sand" above; cultivating and practicing across kalpas corresponds to this steaming, and attaining this opening of wondrous awakening corresponds to this same heat. The five lines "it remains, throughout, rooted in lust" correspond to "since sand and stone were never rice to begin with" above. "This very root having become lust itself": the very seed of sexual desire still present, it sprouts forth at whatever it touches, and, through this same evil karma accumulating day by day, one is certain to turn in the wheel through the three lower destinies, unable ever to escape it — even a human body itself becomes impossible to attain, how much more so, then, the Buddha's own fruit one wishes to cultivate? "The Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa" is exactly this same principle of thusness the Buddha has realized; "what path, then, could ever lead" means exactly this: sand and stone having become what they are, it is decidedly impossible for them ever to become rice.

Sūtra: "One must bring this very mechanism of lust, in both body and mind, to a complete severing — and even this severing itself must, in the end, be nowhere to be found — only then may one hope for the Buddha's own bodhi."

Commentary: "Mechanism" is exactly the very trigger that sets something in motion, like the trigger of a crossbow. This means: the body's own mechanism of lust is set in motion by the mind; the mind's own mechanism of lust is set loose by thought — one must bring it about that not a single thought arises at all, and only then are both body and mind severed together. Should the very nature capable of severing still remain, while the shadow-trace of what was severed still lingers on, this is, in the end, no true eradication at all — one must bring it about that even this severing itself is nowhere to be found, and only then may this be called both the medicine and the sickness removed together, only then is one truly free of illness at all. This is exactly like "still that mechanism, and stillness returns; every illusion then has no nature of its own," described earlier — and it is exactly this same nature, free of illusion, that alone accords with the Buddha's own fruit of bodhi; bodhi being exactly the wisdom of thusness the Buddha has realized — only then may one hope for it! This corresponds to nirvāṇa in the section above, principle and wisdom being one and the same, each explained in terms reflecting the other. As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: severing lust in the body is exactly the precept of moral discipline itself; severing lust in the mind is exactly the precept accompanying samādhi; and even this severing itself being nowhere to be found is exactly the precept accompanying the path itself. This closes the first section, distinguishing, at length, the marks of harm and benefit.

Sūtra: "As I have taught here, this is what the Buddha teaches; anyone who teaches otherwise is Māra's own teaching."

Commentary: This decisively distinguishes what is deviant from what is correct. Since this present capacity deeply guards against future ages' own deviant teachings disturbing what is correct, demon and buddha difficult to tell apart, and seeks a fixed rule for distinguishing demonic influence, the Buddha therefore uses these four root precepts as the very standard for testing and verifying it, so that practitioners of this final age might forever hold them as a fixed mirror to judge by. "As I have taught here" — that cultivating samādhi requires first severing the mind's own lust — this is exactly what is called the Buddha's own teaching, urgently to be followed. "Anyone who teaches otherwise" — that engaging in sexual union poses no obstacle to true cultivation — this is exactly what is called Māra's own teaching, swiftly to be cut off. "Māra" is a corrupted transliteration; properly rendered pāpīyas, translated "the evil one" — one capable of harming the dharma-body and injuring the very life of wisdom itself — this being the name of the demon-king in the age Śākyamuni himself appeared in the world. The character for "demon" (魔) originally used the radical for "stone" (石); Emperor Wu of the Liang dynasty, holding that this being brings vexation and harm upon people, changed it to the radical for "ghost" (鬼) instead — the character used today follows this same Liang-dynasty change. This closes the first precept, severing sexual desire.

〖經〗阿難！又諸世界，六道眾生，其心不殺，則不隨其生死相續。

〖講〗此殺戒，亦注重在心，非但身不行殺，乃至其心，亦不起殺念。殺念尚且不起，何況身臨殺事！是則可為真持戒者，可以真修三昧也。其心苟無殺念，則不負他命，不欠他債，自然不隨其生死相續。何以故？如本經云：汝負我命，我還汝債，以是因緣，經百千劫，常在生死，今命債既無，生死自離矣！

〖經〗汝修三昧，本出塵勞，殺心不除，塵不可出。

〖講〗汝修三昧本來求出塵勞生死；如若殺心不能除去，則與眾生結怨連禍，塵勞不可出矣！

〖經〗縱有多智，禪定現前，如不斷殺，必落神道：上品之人，為大力鬼；中品則為飛行夜叉，諸鬼帥等；下品當為地行羅剎。

〖講〗《指掌疏》云：恐謬辨云，多智之人，了知殺性本空，禪定得力，殺過縱使現前，似無關於塵勞；聰明長者，作家居士，喫得肉已飽，來尋僧說禪者，類多此計，故如來以縱有多智等防之。有多智，指多世智辯聰；禪定，謂有漏禪定；現前，謂智能通達，善說諸法，禪能出入三昧境界。今以禪智助殺，必定墮落神道。鬼神福報，雖分勝劣，均為惡趣；故從人道而入者，謂之墮落也。上品之人，禪智勝於殺業，神通既大，福德又勝，故為大力之鬼；應是天行夜叉，及諸鬼主，如川嶽等；人間尊奉，稱帝稱天者。中品之人，禪智與殺業正等，神通福德，既次於上，則為飛行夜叉，諸鬼帥等；應是空行夜叉，如山林城隍等。下品之人，禪智劣於殺業，神通既微，福德亦劣，則為地行羅剎，噉人精氣，亦如大海邊，羅剎鬼國是也。

〖經〗彼諸鬼神，亦有徒眾，各各自謂，成無上道。

〖講〗未證謂證，未得謂得，各各自己謬稱成無上道。初首陳持犯利害竟。

〖經〗我滅度後，末法之中，多此鬼神，熾盛世間，自言食肉得菩提路。

〖講〗此言滅後，末法之中，世衰道微，邪魔惡鬼，盛行世間；倡言食肉，當得菩提正路。乃云：「酒肉穿腸過，佛在心頭坐。」真鬼語也。

〖經〗阿難！我令比丘，食五淨肉，此肉皆我神力化生，本無命根。汝婆羅門，地多蒸濕，加以沙石，草菜不生，我以大悲神力所加，因大慈悲，假名為肉，汝得其味。奈何如來滅度之後，食眾生肉，名為釋子！

〖講〗此預防問難。食肉既是鬼教，佛應嚴戒，何故如來亦許比丘食五淨肉耶？故呼阿難而告之曰：我令比丘食五淨肉者：不見殺、不聞殺、不疑為我殺、及自死、鳥殘，此為五淨肉。此肉皆我神力之所化生，本無命根：命根者，識、煖、息三，連持色心者，以為命根。下又訪問難，既是如來神力，何不化穀菜，而乃化淨肉，反令後之食肉者，得以藉口，豈慈悲者之所為耶？乃曰：汝婆羅門國，地多蒸濕，過炕謂之蒸，過潤謂之濕，加以沙石，不唯不產五穀，且草菜亦不能生；設若不化五淨肉，而化稻梁蔬菜，事出反常，人以為怪，我以大悲神力所加，愍其饑虛，而化成五種淨肉。因大慈悲，假名為肉，令汝暫得其味，聊以支身，非有心性命根之真肉也。奈何如來滅後，有實食眾生肉者，而更欲名為釋子耶？西域四姓出家，同稱釋子。《涅槃經》云：「善男子！從今日始，不聽食肉，應觀是食，如子肉想，夫食肉者，斷大悲種。」

〖經〗汝等當知，是食肉人，縱得心開，似三摩地，皆大羅剎，報終必沉生死苦海，非佛弟子。如是之人，相殺相吞，相食未已，云何是人得出三界？

〖講〗此令知必墮。汝等當知者：欲其將如來語，傳示末法，急為救正也。當以智知，是食肉人，縱使修學，暫得心開，亦不過相似三摩地，終非真實三昧也。夫殺貪不除，現生皆大羅剎，報盡壽終，必沉生死苦海，永為鬼類，非佛弟子。如是之人，命債不了，互相殺害，互相吞嗜，互相食噉，未有已時，無由解脫，云何得出三界？

〖經〗汝教世人，修三摩地，次斷殺生。是名如來，先佛世尊，第二決定，清淨明誨。

〖講〗此佛敕轉教末世之人。修三摩地，即耳根圓通。次斷殺生者：對淫能障定，殺乃違慈，故次斷之。是名先佛世尊，第二種決定，清淨脩學之明誨也。

〖經〗是故阿難！若不斷殺，脩禪定者，譬如有人：自塞其耳，高聲大叫，求人不聞，此等名為欲隱彌露。

〖講〗此承上是佛教斷殺之故。阿難！若不斷殺，脩禪定者，不得解脫。譬如有人，喻帶殺修禪之人。自塞其耳者：謂以手塞耳，避人聽聞，而復高聲大叫，謂殺生食肉，無有罪報，求人不聞者，此正欲隱而彌露也。

〖經〗清淨比丘，及諸菩薩，於歧路行，不蹋生草，況以手拔？云何大悲，取諸眾生，血肉充食。

〖講〗首二句，指二乘持戒人。歧路者，分歧小路也。生草易長，清淨二乘之眾經行，足倘不蹋，況以手拔耶？仁慈之心，猶及草木，云何大悲，取眾生血肉，以充滋養，任意食噉耶？

〖經〗若諸比丘，不服東方，絲、綿、絹、帛，及是此土，靴、履、裘、毳，乳、酪、醍醐，如是比丘，於世真脫，酬還宿債，不遊三界。

〖講〗此勸斷殺，必須深斷，方可脫離生死。若諸比丘六句，明服有二種：一服用，如東方絲、綿、絹、帛，及是此土靴履裘毳。二服食，如乳、酪、醍醐。然雖此中，一概遮止，唯經律論所明，不無輕重。如絲綿絹帛，則大小乘，並皆嚴禁，以其害多命也。靴履裘毳，小乘一向聽許，大乘亦不全遮，以其非專為此而害命也。乳、酪、醍醐，大小並許。今云不服者，以能不服，則彌善耳。如是比丘，子縛已斷，於諸世間，真得解脫；然果縛尚存，不過酬還宿債而已。現前殘質既盡，無復續生，故不再遊三界。

〖經〗何以故？服其身分，皆為彼緣；如人食其地中百穀，足不離地。必使身心，於諸眾生，若身身分，身心二途，不服不食我說是人真解脫者。

〖講〗何以故，於世真脫？服眾生身分，指絲綿等。雖非身肉，然亦是彼身之一分，用之皆為彼結不離之緣。如劫初之人，體有飛光，足有躡雲，因食地肥，不能輕舉，然後食其地中百穀，足不離地。百穀者，《物理論》云：粱者黍稷之總名，稻者粳糯之總名，菽者眾豆之總名，三穀各二十，合為六十；蔬果之實，各二十種，為百穀也。

必使身心，於諸眾生，若身身分：必使身心，指比丘之身心；於諸眾生，指有情眾生；若身，謂肌肉骨髓；身分，謂裘毳乳酪等。身心二途，不服不食者：謂非但身之於眾生身，及身分，不服不食；而心亦無一念貪求服食而已，是身心二途俱斷。我說是人，真解脫者：既無負命索債，則三界之生緣斷絕，故說是人，真解脫者。初曲分損益之相竟。

〖經〗如我此說，名為佛說；不如此說，即波旬說。

〖講〗如我此說，脩三摩提，次斷殺生者，是則名為佛說，急宜從之。不如此說，食肉不礙菩提，是即波旬說，速當遠之。卯二斷殺竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, should the sentient beings of the six destinies, throughout every world, hold no thought of killing in their minds, they would not follow the continuity of birth and death."

Commentary: This precept against killing likewise places its own emphasis on the mind — not only must the body refrain from any act of killing, but the mind, too, must refrain from giving rise to even a single thought of killing. Should even the thought of killing fail to arise, how much more so, then, any actual encounter with killing itself! Only this may be called a true keeper of the precepts, only this makes true cultivation of samādhi possible. Should the mind genuinely hold no thought of killing, one owes no life to another, and incurs no debt to another, and so naturally no longer follows this same continuity of birth and death. Why is this so? As this very sūtra puts it elsewhere: "you owe me my life, and I repay you your debt" — and, through this same cause and condition, across a hundred thousand kalpas, one remains constantly caught in birth and death; now, this debt of life no longer present, birth and death are naturally left behind!

Sūtra: "Your cultivation of samādhi is meant, at root, to escape worldly affliction — but if the mind of killing is not removed, that affliction cannot be escaped."

Commentary: Your cultivation of samādhi is meant, at root, to seek release from worldly affliction and birth and death — should the mind of killing fail to be removed, one forms enmity and incurs disaster with sentient beings, and worldly affliction can never be escaped at all!

Sūtra: "However much wisdom you may have, however much samādhi may appear before you, if killing is not severed, you will certainly fall into the way of spirits — at best, a spirit of great power; in the middle, a flying yakṣa or one of the ghost-generals; at worst, an earth-treading rākṣasa."

Commentary: As the Zhǐzhǎng Commentary puts it: fearing this mistaken reasoning — that one of great wisdom, fully understanding that the very nature of killing is empty, samādhi already effective, might commit the fault of killing even so, this seeming to have no bearing on worldly affliction at all — clever elders and household laypeople, having eaten their fill of meat, who then come seeking out monks to discuss Chan with, mostly hold to this same reasoning — and so the Tathāgata guards against this with "however much wisdom you may have" and the rest. "Wisdom" points to worldly wisdom and eloquence; "samādhi" means a defiled meditative absorption; "appear before you" means this wisdom able to penetrate through, skilled at expounding every teaching, this samādhi able to enter and emerge from its own state of absorption. Now, using this same absorption and wisdom to assist killing, one is certain to fall into the way of spirits. Though the blessed reward of ghosts and spirits differs, superior or inferior, both alike belong to an evil destiny — and so, entering from the human path, this is called a fall indeed. One of the best grade, this absorption and wisdom exceeding their own karma of killing, their own spiritual powers already great, their own blessed virtue superior as well, and so becomes a spirit of great power — corresponding to a heaven-traveling yakṣa, or one of the ghost-lords, such as those presiding over rivers and mountains, honored and worshipped among people, called "emperor" or "god." One of the middling grade, this absorption and wisdom exactly equal to their own karma of killing, their own spiritual powers and blessed virtue already inferior to the grade above, becomes a flying yakṣa, one of the ghost-generals — corresponding to a sky-traveling yakṣa, such as a spirit of mountain forests or city moats. One of the lowest grade, this absorption and wisdom inferior to their own karma of killing, their own spiritual powers already faint, their own blessed virtue inferior as well, becomes an earth-treading rākṣasa, devouring the very vital essence of people — exactly like the land of the rākṣasa ghosts, at the edge of the great ocean.

Sūtra: "Every one of these ghosts and spirits has, in turn, their own followers, each and every one of them claiming to have accomplished the unsurpassed path."

Commentary: Claiming realization not yet realized, claiming attainment not yet attained, each and every one falsely proclaiming for themselves the accomplishment of the unsurpassed path. This closes the first section, first setting forth the benefit and harm of keeping or breaking this precept.

Sūtra: "After I have passed away, in the age of the Dharma's decline, many such ghosts and spirits will flourish in the world, claiming that eating meat leads to bodhi."

Commentary: This speaks of the age after the Buddha's own passing, the age of the Dharma's decline, when the world grows corrupt and the path grows faint, and deviant demons and evil ghosts flourish throughout the world, proclaiming that eating meat leads to the true path of bodhi. As the saying goes: "wine and meat may pass through the gut, yet the Buddha still sits within the heart" — truly the words of a ghost!

Sūtra: "Ānanda, I permit monks to eat only the five kinds of pure meat, and even this meat is manifested by my own spiritual power, without any true life in it at all. Your own land of the brahmins, its soil heavy with steam and damp, sand and stone besides, where even grass and vegetables cannot grow — I have, out of great compassion, manifested this through my own spiritual power, giving it, in this same great compassion, the mere name of 'meat,' so that you might have this taste to sustain yourselves. How, then, after the Tathāgata's own passing, could there be those who eat the actual flesh of living beings, and still wish to be called disciples of Śākyamuni!"

Commentary: This anticipates and forestalls an objection. Since eating meat is itself this same teaching of ghosts, should not the Buddha strictly forbid it — why, then, did the Tathāgata himself permit monks to eat the five kinds of pure meat at all? And so he calls to Ānanda and tells him directly: "I permit monks to eat the five kinds of pure meat" — meat neither seen killed, nor heard to have been killed, nor suspected of having been killed for oneself, together with meat from an animal already dead of itself, or the leftover kill of a bird of prey — these are the five kinds of pure meat. "This meat is manifested by my own spiritual power, without any true life in it at all": the very life-root itself is made up of three things joined together — consciousness, warmth, and breath, sustaining form and mind together — this being what is called the life-root. What follows anticipates a further objection: since this is the Tathāgata's own spiritual power, why not manifest grain and vegetables instead, rather than this same pure meat, which only gives later meat-eaters an excuse to point to — how could this be the act of one who is truly compassionate? And so it says: your own land of the brahmins, its soil heavy with steam and damp — excessive heat is called "steam," and excessive moisture is called "damp" — sand and stone besides, unable not only to produce the five grains, but even grass and vegetables as well; and, were I not to manifest these five kinds of pure meat, but rice, grain, and vegetables instead, this would be so contrary to the ordinary course of things that people would find it strange indeed — and so, out of great compassion, through my own spiritual power, taking pity on your own hunger and emptiness, I manifested these five kinds of pure meat instead. Through this same great compassion, giving it the mere name of "meat," allowing you to have this taste for a time, enough to sustain your own bodies — this is by no means true meat, possessing any actual mind-nature or life-root at all. How, then, after the Tathāgata's own passing, could there be those who genuinely eat the flesh of living beings, and still wish to be called disciples of Śākyamuni? Those who left the household life from any of the four castes of the Western Regions are alike called disciples of Śākyamuni. As the Nirvāṇa Sūtra puts it: "good sons, from this very day forward, I no longer permit the eating of meat — one should regard this same food exactly as one would regard the flesh of one's own child. Whoever eats meat severs the very seed of great compassion itself."

Sūtra: "You should all know: whoever eats meat, even should their own mind open, resembling samādhi, is, in every case, nothing but a great rākṣasa, and, this same retribution exhausted, is certain to sink into the bitter sea of birth and death — no disciple of the Buddha at all. Such people kill one another, devour one another, feed upon one another without end — how could such a person ever escape the three realms?"

Commentary: This makes plain that such a fall is certain. "You should all know": wishing him to transmit and proclaim the Tathāgata's own words throughout the age of the Dharma's decline, urgently correcting this same error. One ought to know, with genuine wisdom, that whoever eats meat, even should they cultivate and study, temporarily attaining some opening of the mind, this amounts to nothing more than a resembling samādhi, never true and genuine samādhi at all. For, this greed for killing not yet removed, one is, in this very life, nothing but a great rākṣasa — and, this same retribution exhausted, this life ended, one is certain to sink into the bitter sea of birth and death, forever counted among the ghosts, no disciple of the Buddha at all. Such people, their debt of life never settled, kill one another, devour one another, feed upon one another without end, with no time when this ever ceases, no way to attain liberation from it — how, then, could such a person ever escape the three realms?

Sūtra: "In teaching people of the world to cultivate samādhi, next sever killing — this is the second decisive and pure instruction."

Commentary: This is the Buddha's own charge, to pass this teaching on to the beings of this final age. Cultivating samādhi refers exactly to the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration. "Next sever killing": set against lust, which obstructs samādhi directly, killing itself violates compassion — and so it is severed next in turn. This is called the Tathāgata's, and every past buddha's, second decisive and pure instruction for cultivation and practice.

Sūtra: "Therefore, Ānanda, cultivating meditative absorption without severing killing is like a person who plugs their own ears and then shouts loudly, hoping others will not hear — trying to hide what only becomes more exposed."

Commentary: This follows from the reason given above for the Buddha's own teaching to sever killing. Ānanda, should one cultivate meditative absorption without severing killing, one cannot attain liberation. "Like a person": likened here to one who cultivates Chan while still carrying killing. "Plugs their own ears": that is, covering the ears with one's own hands, avoiding the hearing of others, while shouting loudly at the same time — claiming that killing living beings and eating meat carries no karmic retribution at all, hoping others will not hear — this is exactly trying to hide what only becomes all the more exposed.

Sūtra: "Pure monastics and bodhisattvas, walking a forest path, will not even step on living grass, much less pull it up by hand — how, then, could one of great compassion take the flesh and blood of sentient beings for food?"

Commentary: The first two lines here point to a keeper of the precepts within the two lesser vehicles. "A forest path" is a small, forking path. Living grass grows back easily, and yet the pure assembly of the two vehicles, walking along it, will not even set foot on it, how much less pull it up by hand? If even a benevolent, compassionate mind extends this far, even to grass and trees, how, then, could one of great compassion take sentient beings' own flesh and blood for nourishment, eating it freely at will?

Sūtra: "Should monks refrain even from wearing eastern silk, cotton, damask, or brocade, or the leather boots, shoes, furs, and wool of this land, or from taking milk, cream, or ghee, such monks are truly liberated from this world — repaying their own past debts in full, they no longer wander through the three realms."

Commentary: This urges that severing killing must be carried out thoroughly, and only then can one truly be liberated from birth and death. The six lines "should monks" make plain two kinds of "wearing" or "taking": first, wearing for use — such as eastern silk, cotton, damask, and brocade, and this land's own leather boots, shoes, furs, and wool; second, taking as food — such as milk, cream, and ghee. Yet, though these are all, without exception, restricted here, the sūtras, vinaya, and treatises still make plain that they are not without their own degree of relative weight. Silk, cotton, damask, and brocade are strictly forbidden by both the greater and lesser vehicles alike, since producing them harms many lives. Leather boots, shoes, furs, and wool have always been permitted by the lesser vehicle, and are not entirely forbidden by the greater vehicle either, since these are not produced specifically by taking life for this purpose alone. Milk, cream, and ghee are permitted by both vehicles alike. What is here called "refrain from wearing" means: should one be able to refrain from them even so, this is all the more excellent. Such a monk, the fetter of the child already severed, is truly liberated within this world — yet the fetter of the fruit still remains, amounting to nothing more than repaying this same past debt. This present, remaining substance exhausted, with no further continuation of birth to follow, and so one no longer wanders through the three realms again.

Sūtra: "Why is this so? Wearing any part of another being's own body forms, in every case, a karmic bond with that being — exactly like a person who eats the hundred grains grown from the earth, whose own feet can never leave that same earth. One must bring it about that, regarding every sentient being, whether their own body or any part of their own body, both body and mind alike refrain from wearing or taking it — I declare that such a person is truly liberated."

Commentary: Why is one truly liberated from this world? "Wearing... another being's own body" points to silk, cotton, and the rest. Though not the flesh of the body itself, these are, nonetheless, some part of that same body — and using them forms, in every case, an inescapable karmic bond with that being. Exactly as, in the very beginning of this kalpa, people's own bodies carried a flying radiance, their own feet treading upon clouds — until, through eating the earth's own rich substance, they lost the ability to rise lightly, and afterward ate the hundred grains grown from the earth instead, their own feet no longer able to leave it. As for the "hundred grains": the Treatise on the Principles of Things says: liang refers, in general, to millet and broomcorn millet; dao refers, in general, to non-glutinous and glutinous rice; shu refers, in general, to every kind of bean — these three kinds of grain, twenty varieties each, making sixty in all; and vegetables and fruits, twenty kinds each as well, together forming these hundred grains.

"One must bring it about that, regarding every sentient being, whether their own body or any part of their own body": "bring it about that... body and mind" points to a monk's own body and mind; "every sentient being" points to sentient beings possessed of feeling; "their own body" means their own flesh, bones, and marrow; "any part of their own body" means fur, wool, milk, cream, and the like. "Both body and mind alike refrain from wearing or taking it": this means not only refraining, in body, from wearing or taking any sentient being's own body or any part of it, but the mind, too, holding not even a single thought of craving to wear or take it — this being both body and mind alike severed together. "I declare that such a person is truly liberated": already free of owing any life or demanding repayment of any debt, the very cause of rebirth within the three realms is thereby cut off entirely — and so such a person is declared to be truly liberated. This closes the first section, distinguishing, at length, the marks of harm and benefit.

Sūtra: "As I have taught here, this is what the Buddha teaches; anyone who teaches otherwise is Māra's own teaching."

Commentary: "As I have taught here" — that cultivating samādhi requires severing killing next — this is exactly what is called the Buddha's own teaching, urgently to be followed. "Anyone who teaches otherwise" — that eating meat poses no obstacle to bodhi — this is exactly what is called Māra's own teaching, swiftly to be kept far away. This closes the second precept, severing killing.

〖經〗縱有多智，禪定現前，如不斷偷，必落邪道；上品精靈，中品妖魅，下品邪人，諸魅所著。

〖講〗此防謬云：多智之人，了知塵勞本空，禪定現前，彈指可超無學，雖不斷偷，塵勞莫繫，時節既至，要出便出，何言不可出耶？無恥禪流，往往以此飾非，故以縱有多智，禪定現前警之。謂縱有世智辯聰，有漏禪定現前，如若不斷偷心，必定落於邪道。

《指掌疏》云：邪道潛取，以偷心為墮緣，邪通炫世，非禪智不能發，今以禪智助偷，必當墮落。其類上品精靈者：盜日月之精華，竊天地之靈秀，附山托水，惑人祭祀者，是也。中品妖魅者：盜人物之津液，竊山林之氣潤，為魍為魎，伺便作孽者是也。下品邪人者：賦性險曲，居心邪僻。諸魅所著者：為精靈之所附，被妖魅之所迷，妄言欺世，詭異惑俗之類。此等亦由禪智盜業，互相勝劣，而感上、中、下異。

〖經〗彼等群邪，亦有徒眾，各各自謂，成無上道。

〖講〗此指帶偷修禪，其類不一，故曰彼等群邪。亦有徒眾，成慢不歸，邪知邪見，不自覺知，端坐受供，居然如佛，故云各各自謂，成無上道；亦非果計果矣！初首陳持犯利害竟。

〖經〗我滅度後，末法之中，多此妖邪，熾盛世間，潛匿奸欺，稱善知識，各自謂己，得上人法炫惑無識，恐令失心，所過之處，其家耗散。

〖講〗此預言滅後末法之中，佛教陵夷，妖邪得便，故言其多。熾盛世間，潛匿奸欺者：熾然惑眾，盛行世間，潛蹤詭秘，匿詐藏奸，欺世盜名。如想魔文云：潛行貪欲，口中好言災祥變異，即潛匿奸欺也。信其教化，搖蕩其心，妖言妖行，或現妖通，詐稱得道善知識。各自謂己，得上人法者：上人即是菩薩，以居聲聞上故。此未得謂得，未證謂證，各謂自己，已得菩薩法，此邪師自負也。炫惑無識，恐令失心者：嚇詐誘亂，世間無識者，恐令其人，失於本心，亡其知見。如想魔文云：或言如來，某處出世，或言劫火，或說刀兵，即炫惑無識之事。所過之處，其家耗散者：聞某處佛出，則求其接引，聞劫火刀兵，則望其救濟，傾家奉之，所至之處，令其家財耗散無餘。

〖經〗我教比丘，循方乞食，令其捨貪，成菩提道。諸比丘等，不自熟食，寄於殘生，旅泊三界，示一往還，去已無返。

〖講〗此先以己教相形顯。循者順也，方者處也，我教比丘，循順地方處所，而行乞食之法，趣得支身，不妄求不多求，令其捨貪，為菩提道。貪為煩惱根本，根本既捨，枝末自枯，煩惱離而菩提可成。豈同妖邪，炫異惑眾，貪他利養，造生死業耶！諸比丘等下，釋出捨貪之故。我教比丘，不自炊爨熟食者：令其知身是幻，悟世無常，不過寄居於此，以了殘生而已。旅泊三界者：陸宿曰旋，水宿曰泊；無非借此三界，為旅店客船，以示一度往還，自此已去，無復再返，如此行持，方為佛子。

〖經〗云何賊人，假我衣服，裨販如來，造種種業，皆言佛法？卻非出家，具戒比丘，為小乘道，由是疑誤，無量眾生，墮無間獄。

〖講〗云何是痛斥語，賊人謂內心竊取利養，外貌假借僧儀。稗販如來者：稗者助也，販即販賣。謂著如來衣服，以助販賣也。良以不著佛衣，不能取信於人，販法為難故。造種種業，皆言佛法者：謂非法說法也；詭言異行，炫惑人心，造種種業，無所不至，皆言是最上一佛乘之正法。

卻非出家，具戒比丘，為小乘道者：謂法說非法也，若以前乞食，正教責之，彼必自是他非。卻乃毀謗真實出家，具戒比丘，正修行人，為小乘道；反自妄稱，我是大乘菩薩之行。由是疑誤，無量眾生，墮無間獄者：由是法說非法之故，疑惑無量眾生。以正教為小乘道。而從妄謗，由是非法說法之故，賺誤無量眾生，以業因為真佛法，而遵妄行，一盲引眾盲，皆墮五種無間地獄，則妖邪之害，可勝言哉？初潛匿炫惑即妖教竟。

〖經〗若我滅後，其有比丘，發心決定，脩三摩提，能於如來形像之前，身然一燈，燒一指節，及於身上，爇一香炷。

〖講〗此教捨身微因。若我滅後，其有比丘，發大乘心，決定棄捐諸有為相，修三摩提，即耳根圓通，如幻三摩提也。此句下義含慮有宿生盜業，為障勝脩，故教修如是微因。能於如來，形像之前者：為供養佛故，亦為仗承佛力，銷宿障故，又為求佛證明，除現執故。然燈燒指，及爇香炷，此為捨行，一切難捨，無過己身，今則難捨能捨，信其定消宿業也。

〖經〗我說是人無始宿債，一時酬畢，長揖世間，永脫諸漏，雖未即明無上覺路，是人於法，已決定心。

〖講〗我說是然燈燒指，及爇香炷之人，無始宿業盜債，一時酬畢。何以捨身微因，即獲如是善果耶！盜者，取他身外之財物，利養此身，今則捨難捨之自身，供養於佛。一念捨心，即是大智慧光明，無始宿債，即是黑暗，明能破暗，理固然也。

長揖即永辭也，世間即有漏三界。永脫諸漏者：謂永遠脫離，見思諸漏，而出生死。無上覺路者：正謂無上妙覺，菩提之路，即五十五位圓通已後事，此人雖未即明圓通法門，信知必得，決知無有疑心也。

〖經〗若不為此，捨身微因，縱成無為，必還生人，酬其宿債，如我馬麥，正等無異。

〖講〗設若不為此捨身酬債微因，以求懺悔滅罪，則縱成佛，雖得無漏無為，必定還來生於人間，酬其宿債，以其宿業未盡故。如我在毘蘭邑中食馬麥之報，無有異也。

按《興起經》：昔舍衛國毘蘭邑，阿耆達王，請佛與五百比丘，三月供齋，時有魔惑王心，入宮耽荒五欲，供六日止。又值邑內饑饉，乞食不得。時有馬師，減馬麥半，供佛及僧，至九十日，王乃醒悟，向佛求懺。舍利詢問因緣，佛言：過去毘婆尸佛時，有王請佛及僧，佛僧食已，為病比丘請，一分食。過梵志山，梵志聞香，詬曰：此髡頭沙門，應食馬麥，何與甘饌，所教五百童子，亦如是說。爾時梵志者，今我身是，五百童子者，今五百羅漢是。然此但因宿詬佛僧，尚不免報，何況盜他財物，侵彼身分，自應酬債。要知化佛不屬業生，真佛離諸根量，喚誰受報，蓋為造業眾生，示有果報，令生恐懼，故今引之，以證宿債必酬，抑亦因此，以見捨行之不可不修也。

〖經〗汝教世人，修三摩地，後斷偷盜，是名如來，先佛世尊，第三決定，清淨明誨。

〖講〗此敕轉教末世之人，修三摩地，即耳根圓通。後斷偷盜者：對淫能障定，殺乃違慈，理應先斷故，此居後也。是名如來，先佛世尊，第三種決定，清淨修學之明誨也。

〖經〗是故阿難！若不斷偷，修禪定者，譬如有人，水灌漏卮，欲求其滿，縱經塵劫，終無平復。

〖講〗此承上是佛教斷偷之故。阿難！若不斷除偷盜之心，脩禪定者，譬如有人，帶偷修禪人；卮是酒器，漏卮喻帶偷之心；水喻定慧，注水入於帶偷心中，望其成就圓通，如以水灌漏卮，欲求其滿，隨灌隨漏，縱經塵劫，終無平復。

〖經〗若諸比丘，衣缽之餘，分寸不蓄，乞食餘分，施餓眾生。

〖講〗若諸末世，清淨比丘，三衣一缽之外，分寸不許蓄積，此止持不貪也。循方乞食，趣得支身，設有餘分，盡施饑餓眾生，此作持不慳吝也。合之，則斷貪吝心。

〖經〗於大集會，合掌禮眾，有人捶詈，同於稱讚。

〖講〗上句斷慢心，於大集會之中，合掌禮拜四眾；以必觀實相平等，見人是佛故。下二句斷瞋心，有人嫌其煩瀆，捶打罵詈，歡喜領受，同於稱讚；以必觀怨親一相，榮辱無關故。

〖經〗必使身心，二俱捐捨；身肉骨血，與眾生共。

〖講〗上二句斷癡心，必使若身若心二者，俱能捐捨，則斷我、法二執，即斷癡心。下二句，轉貪、瞋、癡、慢，而成同體大悲。身肉骨血，俱皆可捨，則身心寧復有遺哉？以必觀此苦、空、無常、無我、不淨之物，施作佛事耳。此皆成就無偷之心，以上則於依正二報，斷除偷心矣！

〖經〗不將如來，不了義說，迴為己解，以誤初學，佛印是人，得真三昧。

〖講〗此於佛法上，亦除去偷心。不了義說，如《阿含》云：蓄物可以資身進道；如《婆多》論，許百物各可蓄一，但禁餘二者是也。不將如來權乘不了義說，以此回護過犯，以為自己解釋。因其自己，不能奉行大乘，反曲引權小教中，不了義事，以自誤誤人，皆偷心不死之故。果能如是，偷心盡絕，一念純真，即證圓通體。以是而修，名真三昧，佛印是人即得者，良有以也。初曲分損益之相竟。

〖經〗如我所說，名為佛說，不如此說，即波旬說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, should the sentient beings of the six destinies, throughout every world, hold no thought of stealing in their minds, they would not follow the continuity of birth and death."

Commentary: This severs stealing. Not only stealing another's own property, but even deceiving others through strange words and conduct, dazzling and confusing the ignorant, causing them to lose their own right mind, together with even a single thought seeking gain through improper means — all of this belongs here as well. Not only must the body refrain from stealing, but the mind, too, must hold no thought of stealing at all — only this makes one a true keeper of the precepts, only this makes true cultivation of samādhi possible. That this passage does not even mention the body refraining from stealing is because, the mind not stirring at all, how much less, then, the body? Left unsevered, this debt is repaid back and forth without end, continuing birth and death on and on; severed, one no longer follows this same continuity.

Sūtra: "Your cultivation of samādhi is meant, at root, to escape worldly affliction — but if the mind of stealing is not removed, that affliction cannot be escaped."

Commentary: As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "only one who grows weary of the sense-object can escape the sense-object — how could one who still craves the world ever transcend the world?"

Sūtra: "However much wisdom you may have, however much samādhi may appear before you, if stealing is not severed, you will certainly fall into a deviant path — at best, an elemental spirit; in the middle, a bewitching demon; at worst, a deviant person possessed by such spirits."

Commentary: This guards against this mistaken reasoning: one of great wisdom, fully understanding worldly affliction to be empty at root, samādhi already appearing before them, able, in the snap of a finger, to transcend even the rank of no further training — even without severing stealing, worldly affliction could hardly bind them, and, the proper time arriving, they would simply leave whenever they wished — why, then, say this cannot be escaped at all? Shameless practitioners of Chan often gloss over their own faults with reasoning exactly like this — and so the Tathāgata warns against it with "however much wisdom you may have, however much samādhi may appear before you." This means: even with worldly wisdom and eloquence, even with a defiled meditative absorption appearing before them, should the mind of stealing fail to be severed, one is certain to fall into a deviant path.

As the Zhǐzhǎng Commentary puts it: a deviant path takes things in secret, this mind of stealing itself being the very condition of this fall; deviant spiritual powers dazzle the world, and could never arise apart from meditative absorption and wisdom — and now, using this same absorption and wisdom to assist stealing, one is certain to fall as a result. Among this kind, the best grade, an elemental spirit, steals the very essence of the sun and moon, pilfers the very spiritual excellence of heaven and earth, attaching itself to a mountain or relying on a body of water, bewitching people into offering sacrifice to it. The middling grade, a bewitching demon, steals the very fluid essence of people and things, pilfers the very vital moisture of forests and mountains, becoming a wangliang or a wangxiang spirit, watching for its own chance to work mischief. The lowest grade, a deviant person, is one whose own nature is treacherous and crooked, whose own mind dwells in deviance and perversity. "Possessed by such spirits" means possessed by an elemental spirit, deluded by a bewitching demon, speaking falsely to deceive the world, a strange and deceptive kind that bewilders the common people. These, too, arise through this same absorption and wisdom assisting the karma of stealing, differing from one another, superior or inferior, and so incurring these three grades, best, middling, and worst.

Sūtra: "Every one of these deviant spirits has, in turn, their own followers, each and every one of them claiming to have accomplished the unsurpassed path."

Commentary: This points to those who cultivate Chan while still carrying stealing — this kind is by no means uniform, hence, "these deviant spirits," plural. They, too, have their own followers, arrogance become fixed, never turning back, deviant knowledge and deviant views, entirely unaware of their own state, sitting upright to receive offerings, seemingly no different from a buddha — hence, "each and every one of them claiming to have accomplished the unsurpassed path"; this, too, is exactly mistaking what is not the fruit for the fruit itself. This closes the first section, first setting forth the benefit and harm of keeping or breaking this precept.

Sūtra: "After I have passed away, in the age of the Dharma's decline, many such deviant spirits will flourish in the world, hiding their true nature, claiming to be spiritual guides, each declaring themselves to have attained a superior state, deceiving the ignorant, until, wherever they pass, the households they touch are ruined."

Commentary: This foretells that, in the age of the Dharma's decline after the Buddha's own passing, the Buddha's own teaching will fall into decay, and deviant spirits will find their own opportunity — hence, "many." "Flourish in the world, hiding their true nature": bewitching the multitude fiercely, flourishing widely throughout the world, concealing their own tracks in secret, hiding their own deceit and treachery, deceiving the world and stealing a false reputation. As a later passage, on delusion by demons, puts it: "secretly indulging in greed and desire, their own mouths fond of speaking of calamitous and auspicious portents" — this is exactly what is meant by "hiding their true nature." Believing in this teaching and transformation, their own minds shaken and unsettled, this deviant speech and deviant conduct, sometimes manifesting a deviant spiritual power, falsely claiming to be a spiritual guide who has attained the path. "Each declaring themselves to have attained a superior state": a "superior person" is exactly a bodhisattva, standing above the śrāvaka. This is claiming attainment not yet attained, claiming realization not yet realized, each one declaring themselves to have already attained a bodhisattva's own dharma — this deviant teacher's own arrogant self-regard. "Deceiving the ignorant, until... they lose their own right mind": frightening, deceiving, and leading astray those in the world without understanding, causing such people to lose their own original mind, forfeiting their own knowledge and vision. As a later passage, on delusion by demons, puts it: sometimes claiming the Tathāgata has appeared in some particular place, sometimes speaking of a kalpa-ending fire, sometimes speaking of war and bloodshed — this is exactly this same matter of deceiving the ignorant. "Wherever they pass, the households they touch are ruined": hearing that a buddha has appeared somewhere, people seek to be received and guided there; hearing of a kalpa-fire or of war, people hope to be rescued and saved — emptying their own households to offer everything up, and, wherever such a teacher passes, causing that household's own wealth to be exhausted entirely, without remainder.

Sūtra: "I teach monks to beg for food from house to house without discrimination, precisely so as to let go of greed and accomplish the bodhisattva path. Monks do not cook their own food, but merely lodge here to complete what remains of this present life, like a traveler passing through the three realms, showing a single journey there and back, once gone, never to return."

Commentary: This first sets my own teaching against theirs in contrast, to make the difference plain. "From house to house" means following along, place by place; I teach monks to follow along, place by place, carrying out this method of begging for food, obtaining just enough to sustain the body, neither improperly seeking nor seeking too much, so as to let go of greed, this being the very path to bodhi. Greed is the very root of affliction — this root already let go of, its branches and leaves wither away of themselves, affliction thereby left behind, and bodhi may thereby be accomplished. How could this ever be the same as a deviant spirit, dazzling the multitude with strange claims, greedy for others' own offerings, creating karma that continues birth and death instead! What follows, "monks," explains the reason for this same letting go of greed. "I teach monks not to cook their own food": so that they might know the body to be illusory, awaken to the impermanence of the world, understanding themselves to be doing nothing more than lodging here temporarily, to complete what remains of this present life alone. "Like a traveler passing through the three realms": resting on land is called "lodging," and resting on water is called "anchoring" — nothing more than borrowing these three realms as a mere inn or passing boat, showing a single journey there and back, once departed from this point on, never to return again — only conduct carried out exactly this way makes one a true disciple of the Buddha.

Sūtra: "How, then, could a thief, borrowing my very robes, peddle the Tathāgata's own teaching for profit, creating every kind of karma while claiming it to be the Buddha-dharma, and even denouncing properly ordained monks as merely following the Lesser Vehicle? By this they mislead countless beings into the Avīci hell."

Commentary: "How, then" is a word of severe rebuke. "A thief" means one who, inwardly, steals profit and gain for themselves, while, outwardly, borrowing the monastic appearance falsely. "Peddle the Tathāgata's own teaching for profit": "peddle" means to assist, and "for profit" means to sell — that is, wearing the Tathāgata's own robes, so as to assist in this same selling. This is precisely because, without wearing the Buddha's own robes, one cannot win people's own trust, making it difficult to peddle the Dharma at all. "Creating every kind of karma while claiming it to be the Buddha-dharma": that is, speaking what is not the Dharma as though it were the Dharma — deceptive words and strange conduct, dazzling and confusing people's own minds, creating every kind of karma, stopping at nothing, all the while claiming this to be the true Dharma of the very highest, single Buddha-vehicle itself.

"And even denouncing properly ordained monks as merely following the Lesser Vehicle": that is, speaking the Dharma as though it were not the Dharma — should one rebuke them with the correct teaching on begging for food described earlier, they will certainly claim themselves to be right and others wrong, going so far as to denounce truly ordained monks, complete in the precepts, genuine practitioners, as merely following the Lesser Vehicle — while falsely claiming, for themselves, to be practicing the Greater Vehicle's own bodhisattva conduct instead. "By this they mislead countless beings into the Avīci hell": because of this speaking what is not the Dharma as though it were the Dharma, countless sentient beings are led into doubt, taking the correct teaching itself for the Lesser Vehicle, and following this same false denunciation instead; and, because of this speaking the Dharma as though it were not the Dharma, countless sentient beings are cheated and misled, taking the very cause of this same karma for the true Buddha-dharma, and following this false conduct as a result — one blind person leading a crowd of the blind, all of them falling into the five kinds of Avīci hell together — how, then, could the harm of these deviant spirits ever be fully told? This closes the first section, hiding one's true nature and dazzling others being exactly this same teaching of deviant spirits.

Sūtra: "Should there be, after I have passed away, any monk who resolves decisively to cultivate this samādhi, able, before an image of the Tathāgata, to burn a single lamp upon their own body, to burn a single finger-joint, or to burn a single stick of incense upon their own body."

Commentary: This teaches this same small act of giving up the body as a cause. Should there be, after I have passed away, any monk who gives rise to this mind of the greater vehicle, decisively casting aside every conditioned mark, cultivating this samādhi — that is, exactly the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration, this illusion-like samādhi. This line, below, carries the sense of one concerned that karma of stealing carried over from a past life might obstruct this excellent cultivation, and so teaches the cultivation of this same small cause. "Before an image of the Tathāgata": to make an offering to the Buddha; also to rely upon the Buddha's own power, dissolving away this past obstruction; also to seek the Buddha's own confirmation, removing one's own present attachment. Burning a lamp, burning a finger, and burning incense are all acts of giving something up — and, of everything difficult to give up, nothing surpasses one's own body itself; and now, giving up even what is hardest of all to give up, one may be confident this dissolves away past karma indeed.

Sūtra: "I declare that this person's own beginningless, past debt is thereby repaid all at once, taking their leave of the world forever, permanently freed of every outflow — and, though not yet directly clear about the unsurpassed path of awakening, this person has already resolved their own mind decisively regarding this same Dharma."

Commentary: I declare that this person, burning a lamp, burning a finger, and burning incense, has their own beginningless, past karmic debt of theft repaid all at once. Why should this small cause of giving up the body bring about so excellent a fruit? A thief takes another's own wealth, external to the body, to nourish this same body — and now, instead, giving up this very body, hardest of all to give up, as an offering to the Buddha. A single thought of giving up is itself the very light of great wisdom; this beginningless, past debt is itself darkness — and brightness is naturally able to overcome darkness, this being simply the way things are.

"Taking their leave" means bidding farewell forever; "the world" means the three realms, still subject to outflow. "Permanently freed of every outflow" means permanently freed from every outflow of views and thought, and thereby escaping birth and death. "The unsurpassed path of awakening" refers exactly to this unsurpassed, wondrous awakening, the very path of bodhi — that is, matters belonging to after the fifty-five stages of perfect penetration; though this person is not yet directly clear about the gate of perfect penetration itself, they may be confident that they will certainly attain it, decisively knowing this without the slightest doubt at all.

Sūtra: "Should one fail to carry out this small cause of giving up the body, even should one accomplish the unconditioned, one is certain to return, reborn as a human, to repay this same past debt — exactly equal, without the slightest difference, to my own eating of horse-fodder."

Commentary: Should one fail to carry out this small cause of giving up the body to repay this same debt, seeking thereby to confess and dissolve this karma, then, even should one accomplish buddhahood, attaining the undefiled, unconditioned itself, one is certain to return, reborn among humans, to repay this same past debt — since this past karma is not yet exhausted. Exactly like my own retribution of eating horse-fodder in the town of Vairañcā, no different at all.

According to the Sūtra on the Rise of Past Causes: in ages past, in the town of Vairañcā in the kingdom of Śrāvastī, King Agnidatta invited the Buddha and five hundred monks to receive offerings for three months. At that time, Māra confused the king's own mind, and he retreated into his own palace, indulging in the five desires, and the offerings ceased after only six days. This same town, further, was struck by famine, and food could not be obtained through begging. At that time, a horse-groom set aside half of his own horses' fodder to offer to the Buddha and the monastic assembly, and this continued for ninety days, until the king finally came to his senses, and went before the Buddha to confess and repent. Śāriputra inquired into the cause and condition behind this, and the Buddha said: in the past, during the age of the Buddha Vipaśyin, a king invited the Buddha and the monastic assembly to a meal; the Buddha and the assembly, having already eaten, set aside a further portion for a sick monk. Passing by a mountain where a brahmin ascetic dwelt, this ascetic, smelling the fragrance of this food, cursed them, saying: "these shaven-headed monks ought to eat horse-fodder — why give them such delicious food instead?" — and the five hundred young disciples he taught said exactly the same. That very brahmin ascetic is myself, in this present life; and those five hundred young disciples are these same five hundred arhats, now. Yet, if merely cursing the Buddha and his monastic assembly in a past life could not, even so, escape this same retribution, how much more so, then, one who steals another's own wealth, encroaching upon some part of their own body — such a person must certainly repay this same debt in turn. One should understand that a transformation-body buddha is not itself subject to karmic birth, and a true buddha is free of every measure of sense-faculty entirely — who, then, could there be to receive such retribution at all? This was shown entirely for the sake of beings who create karma, displaying that karmic retribution truly exists, so as to give rise to a wholesome fear of it — and so it is cited here, to bear out that a past debt is certain to be repaid, and, further, to show that this practice of giving up the body cannot but be cultivated.

Sūtra: "In teaching people of the world to cultivate samādhi, sever stealing next — this is the third decisive and pure instruction."

Commentary: This charges Ānanda with passing this teaching on to the beings of this final age. Cultivating samādhi refers exactly to the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration. "Sever stealing next": set against lust, which obstructs samādhi directly, and killing, which violates compassion, both of which ought, in principle, to be severed first — and so stealing takes its place after them. This is called the Tathāgata's, and every past buddha's, third decisive and pure instruction for cultivation and practice.

Sūtra: "Therefore, Ānanda, cultivating meditation without severing stealing is like a person trying to fill a leaking vessel by pouring water into it — though kalpas as numerous as dust motes may pass, it will never be filled."

Commentary: This follows from the reason given above for the Buddha's own teaching to sever stealing. Ānanda, should one cultivate meditative absorption without severing the mind of stealing, this is exactly like a person — likened here to one who cultivates Chan while still carrying stealing; a vessel is a wine-cup, and a leaking vessel is likened to this same mind that still carries stealing; water is likened to samādhi and wisdom, poured into this mind that still carries stealing, hoping thereby to accomplish perfect penetration — exactly like pouring water into a leaking vessel, hoping it will fill, only to leak out as fast as it is poured in — though kalpas as numerous as dust motes may pass, it will never be filled.

Sūtra: "Should monks, beyond their own robes and alms bowl, hoard nothing at all, however slight — whatever remains over from begging for food, given to beings still hungry."

Commentary: Should a pure monk, in this final age, beyond their own three robes and single alms bowl, be forbidden to hoard even the slightest amount — this is refraining from greed. Begging for food from house to house, obtaining only enough to sustain the body, and, should anything remain over, giving it entirely to beings still hungry — this is actively practicing freedom from stinginess. Taken together, this severs the mind of both greed and stinginess.

Sūtra: "Within a great assembly, joining the palms to bow to everyone present — even should someone strike or curse them, receiving this exactly as though it were praise."

Commentary: The first line here severs the mind of arrogance — within a great assembly, joining the palms to bow to all four groups present, since one must contemplate the true mark of equality itself, seeing every person as a buddha. The next two lines sever the mind of anger — even should someone, resenting their own persistence, strike or curse them, receiving this joyfully, exactly as though it were praise — since one must contemplate enemy and kin as a single, undivided mark, honor and disgrace alike having nothing to do with it.

Sūtra: "One must bring it about that both body and mind alike are given up entirely — one's own flesh, bones, and blood shared in common with sentient beings."

Commentary: The first two lines here sever the mind of delusion — one must bring it about that both body and mind, the two together, are able to be given up entirely, thereby severing both attachments, to self and to dharmas, this being exactly severing the mind of delusion. The next two lines transform greed, anger, delusion, and arrogance into this same great compassion of shared substance. One's own flesh, bones, and blood, all alike able to be given up — how, then, could body or mind still hold anything back at all? This requires contemplating this same suffering, empty, impermanent, selfless, impure thing, offering it up to carry out the Buddha's own work. All of this together accomplishes a mind free of stealing — everything above severs the mind of stealing with regard to both the circumstantial and direct retributions.

Sūtra: "Never taking the Tathāgata's own teachings of incomplete meaning and turning them into an interpretation of one's own, to mislead beginning students — the Buddha confirms that such a person attains true samādhi."

Commentary: This severs the mind of stealing with regard to the Buddha-dharma itself. Teachings of incomplete meaning are such as the Āgama's own saying that keeping possessions may sustain the body and advance one on the path, or the Vaibhāṣika treatise's own permitting each of a hundred kinds of item to be kept singly, forbidding only a second or third of each. "Never taking the Tathāgata's own provisional-vehicle teachings of incomplete meaning" — that is, using these to shield one's own transgression, turning them into an interpretation of one's own. This is precisely because, unable to carry out the greater vehicle's own teaching themselves, one instead twists and cites matters of incomplete meaning from the provisional, lesser teaching, misleading both oneself and others — all of this because the mind of stealing has not yet been put to death. Should one truly be able to do this, the mind of stealing entirely eradicated, this single thought entirely pure and genuine, one thereby realizes the very essence of perfect penetration itself. Cultivating on this basis is called true samādhi — and that the Buddha confirms such a person attains it is, indeed, well founded. This closes the first section, distinguishing, at length, the marks of harm and benefit.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, should the sentient beings of the six destinies, throughout every world, though their body and mind already free of killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct, these three practices already complete, engage in great false speech, this same samādhi cannot become pure; they become a demon of love and view, and lose their very Buddha-heritage entirely."

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: false speech is of two kinds — speaking words that are untrue is called minor false speech; falsely claiming to have realized a fruit of attainment is called great false speech. What is severed here is exactly this great false speech. Not only must the mouth refrain from uttering either kind of false speech, but the mind, too, must hold no thought of false speech at all — only this may be called pure keeping of the precepts, only this makes cultivation of samādhi possible. And so he calls to Ānanda: should the sentient beings of the six destinies, throughout every world, though their body and mind already free of killing — this being the accomplishment of compassionate conduct; free of stealing — this being the accomplishment of giving conduct; free of sexual misconduct — this being the accomplishment of pure conduct — hence, "these three practices already complete." Should one fail to work alongside such beings, this would be to twist against their own minds, unable to accord with them at all — and so working alongside them is meant to win their own good will, so as to then praise, to them, this very highest, single Buddha-vehicle, able to turn sentient beings back from delusion toward awakening, leaving suffering behind for happiness, turning their own deviant mind toward correct samādhi instead — hence, "bringing their minds into samādhi."

Sūtra: "Never once declaring, 'I am a true bodhisattva, a true arhat,' never revealing the Buddha's own hidden purpose or speaking carelessly to those not yet ready to hear it."

Commentary: In the end, never willing to declare, of themselves, "I am a true bodhisattva, a true arhat," revealing the Buddha's own intention, this hidden cause. "Speaking carelessly to those not yet ready to hear it" means carelessly revealing this to those still young in their own learning.

Sūtra: "Except at the very moment of death, when a hidden final transmission may quietly be left behind. How, then, could such a person deceive and confuse beings through great false speech?"

Commentary: "Except at the very moment of death" means displaying the relinquishing of one's own retribution-body. "A hidden final transmission may quietly be left behind" means a final message and charge, quietly conveyed. Remaining in the world, one does not reveal it; revealing it, one no longer remains — as Chan Master Dushun's own verse puts it: "the traveler wanders on and on, climbing mountains, bowing to mounds of earth — Mañjuśrī is exactly this, right here — where else would you go looking for Amitābha?" "How, then" is a word of rebuke — "such a person" points to one possessed by the demon of love and view, using exactly this same demonic teaching of claiming attainment not yet attained, claiming realization not yet realized, to deceive and confuse beings of later ages still young in their own learning, committing this same great false speech.

Sūtra: "In teaching people of the world to cultivate samādhi, sever great false speech last of all — this is the Tathāgata's fourth decisive and pure instruction."

Commentary: The Buddha charges bodhisattvas and arhats to manifest their bodies exactly this way; you should teach later beginning practitioners, wishing to cultivate samādhi, that though they may first be able to sever lust, killing, and stealing, they must, further, also sever this same great false speech afterward — this is called the Tathāgata, of present and past alike, giving this fourth decisive, pure instruction for cultivation and practice, severing the precept against false speech. This closes the third section, passing on this teaching of every past buddha.

Sūtra: "Therefore, Ānanda, anyone who does not sever great false speech is like someone carving a shape out of human excrement and calling it sandalwood, hoping it will smell fragrant — there is no such possibility."

Commentary: This follows from the reason given above for the Tathāgata's own teaching to sever great false speech. Calling again to Ānanda: should one fail to sever great false speech, this is likened to carving dried human excrement into the shape of sandalwood — cause and fruit simply do not match. The mind and conduct of false speech is likened here to dried excrement; true cultivation through meditative absorption is likened to a sandalwood image of the Buddha — and, taking this same cause of false speech, this filth of excrement, and seeking thereby the fruit of the dharma-body attained through meditative absorption, how could this ever be possible? Hence, "hoping it will smell fragrant — there is no such possibility."

Sūtra: "I teach monks to hold a straightforward mind as their very place of practice; in every posture, in every act, they should have no falseness in them at all — how, then, could they falsely claim attainment of a superior state?"

Commentary: And so I teach monks that, without severing false speech, bodhi can never be accomplished — relying, instead, only on this same upright, straightforward mind, this itself becomes the very place of accomplishing the path. As the Avataṃsaka Sūtra puts it: "the wondrous tree of bodhi's own Dharma grows from the very ground of a straightforward mind." As the Vimalakīrti Sūtra puts it: "a straightforward mind is itself the place of practice" — since it holds no falseness at all. Within these four postures — walking, standing, sitting, and lying down — and even in speech and silence, in every single act, one must remain upright and straightforward, holding no falseness at all — how, then, could one falsely claim, of oneself, to be honored, declaring attainment of the greater or lesser vehicle, or of a superior person's own dharma?

Sūtra: "It is like a beggar falsely proclaiming himself emperor, bringing about his own execution — how much more so one who would falsely usurp the very throne of the Dharma-king! A crooked cause brings a twisted result; seeking the Buddha's bodhi this way is like a man trying to bite his own navel — who could ever succeed at that?"

Commentary: A beggar is likened here to samādhi not yet accomplished, this nature's own virtue not yet manifest. "Falsely proclaiming himself emperor" is exactly this same great false speech, claiming attainment not yet attained, claiming realization not yet realized. An emperor is the ruler of an entire nation — falsely proclaiming oneself such brings about, of itself, execution of one's entire clan, the ruin of one's entire household. The two lines "how much more so" carry the sense of arguing from the lesser to the greater — meaning that even the worthies and sages of the three vehicles cannot be falsely usurped, how much less, then, the Buddha, this transcendent Dharma-king himself — how could this ever be falsely usurped? "A crooked cause brings a twisted result": taking this same great false speech as one's own cause, this cause itself is not true, and so the matter of the fruit-stage cannot avoid being crooked and twisted as well — seeking to advance, one instead retreats; seeking to rise, one instead falls — and seeking the Buddha's own fruit of bodhi this way naturally cannot succeed at all. "Like a man trying to bite his own navel": "bite" means to gnaw with the mouth; "navel" is exactly the navel of the belly — meaning that mouth and navel remain too far apart from one another, never able to reach each other at all — and so, having committed this same false speech oneself, extinguishing the very seed of buddhahood, who, then, could one possibly hope to accomplish this through?

Sūtra: "But if monks keep their minds as straight as a taut string, entirely true and real in everything, they will enter samādhi forever free of demonic interference — I myself attest that such a person accomplishes the bodhisattva's unsurpassed knowledge and awareness."

Commentary: This deeply affirms that severing great false speech is certain to accomplish unsurpassed bodhi. Should monks keep their minds as straight as a bowstring, holding no mark of flattery or crookedness at all — the mind is the very master of these four postures; the mind straight, then, within all four postures, every act, every practice, is, without exception, entirely true and genuine, forever free of falseness, and so is able to enter directly into samādhi. "Forever free of demonic interference": demonic conduct is deviant and treacherous — the mind now straight and conduct now genuine, Māra can find no opportunity at all — and so I myself attest that such a person accomplishes the bodhisattva's own unsurpassed knowledge and awareness. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: this bodhisattva's own unsurpassed knowledge and awareness is exactly the true, causal-stage mind of perfect penetration itself — yet, taking this cause to determine this fruit, this is, indeed, unsurpassed bodhi as well! This closes the first section, distinguishing the precept and its exhortation at length.

Sūtra: "As I have taught here, this is what the Buddha teaches; anyone who teaches otherwise is Māra's own teaching."

Commentary: As I have taught here — that, having already severed lust, killing, and stealing, one must, further, sever great false speech as well — this is exactly what is correct, exactly called the Buddha's own teaching, urgently to be followed. Should this not be so taught — that, these first three already severed, great false speech poses no obstacle to true cultivation — this is exactly what is deviant, exactly Māra's own teaching, to be deeply cut off. This is exactly the Tathāgata wishing Ānanda to transmit and proclaim the Tathāgata's own words throughout the age of the Dharma's decline, protecting beginning, genuine practitioners, so that they might recognize the heavenly demon, and not be deceived by it. This closes the fourth precept, severing false speech — and, together with it, closes these three sections above, opening and explaining these four grave precepts in full.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, should these four matters never once be lost, the mind would no longer even condition form, fragrance, taste, or touch — how, then, could any demonic affair ever arise at all?"

This closes the first section, correctly teaching the upholding of the precepts.

Sūtra: "Should there be any habits carried over from past lives that cannot be dissolved away, teach such a person to recite, single-mindedly, my own unsurpassed sacred mantra of the Buddha-crown's own light, Mahā-sitātapatra."

Commentary: This directly urges holding this mantra. "Past" means past lives; "habits" means delusion and karma, the seed of habituated tendency. As Wenling put it: karma in the present life has already been restrained, and one's own conduct can be changed accordingly; but habits from past lives are difficult to dissolve away, and require, without fail, the assistance of spiritual power. Now, a practitioner may love what is correct, yet find themselves firmly caught in what is deviant; may wish for purity, yet find themselves inclined toward defilement — as though, unseen, something were driving them on, unable to stop themselves — this being exactly what habits from past lives do.

Only the inconceivable power of this sacred mantra is able to dissolve them away — and so it says: teach such a person to recite, single-mindedly, my own sacred mantra of the Buddha-crown's own light. Reciting a mantra "single-mindedly" admits of both an actual and a principled sense. Single-mindedness in actual practice: the mind capable of holding it holds the mantra held, subject and object clearly distinguished, continuing on without a break. Single-mindedness in principle: the mind capable of holding it is entirely this mantra itself, and the mantra held is entirely this mind itself — subject and object both gone, mind and mantra not two different things at all, the mantra being itself the mind, and the mind being itself the mantra. This mantra was spoken by a transformation-buddha manifested from the Buddha's own crown-light, precisely so as to represent the very most honored among the honored, the very most secret among the secret. A buddha is, by nature, already the most honored being in the world — and now, this being a transformation-buddha manifested from the Buddha's own crown, this is indeed the most honored among the honored. The crown cannot be seen, and is the very most secret thing in the world — and now, this being a transformation-buddha, its teaching arising in radiance from this same crown, this is indeed the most secret among the secret — and this, too, is borrowed here to represent the very meaning of dissolving away these habitual tendencies. "Mahā-sitātapatra" translates "the great, white canopy" — "great" represents the very substance of the tathāgatagarbha's own mind, cut off from every counterpart; "white" represents the very mark of the tathāgatagarbha's own mind, free of every false defilement; "canopy" represents the very function of the tathāgatagarbha's own mind, universally covering everything at once. Being exactly this same mind of the tathāgatagarbha, it is therefore called unsurpassed, this spiritual, wondrous mantra beyond all measure.

Sūtra: "This is exactly the sacred mantra spoken by the Tathāgata's own unseen crown-mark, this unconditioned mind-buddha, arising in radiance from this same crown, seated upon a jeweled lotus."

Commentary: "This" points to this same sacred mantra, saying that this mantra belongs to the Tathāgata's own unseen crown-mark — that is, the very first of the Buddha's own thirty-two major marks, called the fleshy-crown mark. And it is called "unseen" because, as the Avataṃsaka Sūtra records, even a bodhisattva of the ninth ground, serving as the Buddha's own wet-nurse, holding him up and gazing closely, could still not see the very top of his own crown — showing that this crown-dharma cannot ever be seen by ordinary seeing at all. "This unconditioned mind-buddha": arising from the World-Honored One's own fleshy-crown mark, a hundred kinds of treasure-light surging forth — hence, "arising in radiance from this same crown." Within this light, a thousand-petaled, jeweled lotus surges forth, upon which a transformation-Tathāgata sits — hence, "seated upon a jeweled lotus." The Buddha is called "unconditioned" because this is exactly the Tathāgata entering samādhi, manifesting from within this same unconditioned mind. Emitting this light once more, and speaking this mind-mantra — since this is exactly every buddha's own mind-seal, its own very substance being exactly the tathāgatagarbha itself. This closes the first section, directly urging the holding of this mantra and praising its excellence.

Sūtra: "And you yourself, further, throughout kalpas of cause and condition with Mātaṅgī across past lives, this same habituated tendency of love and desire was not the work of a single life, or even a single kalpa — yet, the moment I proclaimed this mantra, her own mind of love was forever released, and she attained arhatship."

Commentary: This displays the inconceivable power of this mantra. Taking up, for a moment, this recent matter as an example: you and the woman Mātaṅgī share a cause and condition across past lives, this same affection and love, this seed of habituated tendency, not the work of a single life or even a single kalpa — its own origin lies far back indeed! Difficult to be freed from it, exactly as was said earlier: "you loved my mind, and I was drawn to your beauty — and, through this same cause and condition, across a hundred thousand kalpas, we remained constantly bound to one another." According to the Mātaṅgī Sūtra: once Ānanda had returned, this woman would not stop weeping, continuing to think of Ānanda without end. She followed him all the way to Jetavana, and the Buddha had her summoned and brought before him, asking her directly: what is it you seek, pursuing Ānanda this way? She answered: I love Ānanda's eyes, ears, mouth, nose, and his whole bodily form as well. The Buddha said: Ānanda's eyes hold nothing but tears; his ears hold nothing but wax; his mouth holds nothing but saliva; his nose holds nothing but mucus; his body holds nothing but urine and excrement. Should you become husband and wife, this brings with it every kind of impurity; and this impurity brings children; and children bring death in turn; and once there is death, there is weeping and wailing besides — what benefit, then, could this body ever bring you, and what is there left in it for you to love? Hearing these words, the woman corrected her own mind at once, and thereby entered the path directly. "The moment I proclaimed this mantra, her own mind of love was forever released": this refers to the very moment the Buddha first proclaimed this mantra, commanding Mañjuśrī to take it and go to her protection — this evil spell dissolved away at once, and, the very moment she heard this sacred mantra, through this same hidden power of habituation and blessing, her own past habituated tendency was dissolved away entirely; and, upon seeing the Buddha and hearing the Dharma, this river of love dried up completely, and she directly realized the third fruit at once — hence, "her own mind of love was forever released." "Attained arhatship" refers to her attaining the fourth fruit, described earlier, after hearing Mañjuśrī's own selection of perfect penetration. This is precisely because, first receiving the blessing of this mantra's own power, dissolving away this same past habituated tendency, and then, further, relying on the sound of the Dharma, awakening to and realizing this sagely fruit — had it not been for this same power of the mantra, removing this past habituated tendency, how could her own mind of lust ever have been so swiftly dissolved away, according with the unconditioned Dharma itself?

Sūtra: "Even she, still a woman given to lust, without any mind set on cultivation at all, was swiftly brought, through this same spiritual power working invisibly, to realize the rank beyond further training. Commentary: And, further, Mātaṅgī's own habituated tendency ran heavy, still counted a woman given to lust, without any mind set on cultivation at all — yet, simply through this same mantra's own unfathomable power, this same effort of habituating blessing, she was, even so, swiftly brought to realize the fruit beyond further training. "How, then" is a word of deep astonishment, meaning: how could you, whose own delusion and habituated tendency run so much lighter, present in this assembly as śrāvakas — including both those still in training and those beyond it: those still in training having severed the affliction of views, and those beyond training having severed the affliction of thought — how could you be any different from Mātaṅgī, still counted a woman given to lust? And, further, what you yourselves seek is nothing less than this very highest, single vehicle, resolved decisively, with genuine confidence, upon the very path of buddhahood itself — how, then, could this be any different from Mātaṅgī's own lack of any mind set on cultivation at all?

"Scattering dust": dust is likened here to habituated tendency, and a following wind is likened to this same mantra. This closes the first section, setting forth in detail the strict precepts to be upheld beforehand.

Sūtra: "Should there be one, in this final age, wishing to sit within a place of practice, first upholding a monk's own pure, prohibitive precepts — one must select a monastic of pure precepts, foremost among monks, to serve as one's own teacher; should one fail to encounter a genuinely pure monastic, one's own monastic discipline can never be accomplished."

Commentary: This makes plain how samādhi arises from precepts, this diligent cultivation of monastic discipline forming its own very root. Should there be, in this final age, one cultivating the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration, resolving to sit within this same place of cultivating the path, they must first uphold these same four grave, pure, prohibitive precepts belonging to a monk — "pure" at the source meaning purifying its very origin, and "pure" in its flow meaning purifying its own continuation — exactly as described above: first upholding these four kinds of monastic discipline, as clear as ice and frost, and thereby naturally unable to give rise to any further branch or leaf of transgression. At the very time of receiving the precepts, one must select the teacher who confers them — for this teacher is the very model for the student, and, should this model itself be improper, few could ever rely on it to become a proper vessel of the Dharma. One must, without fail, select a monastic whose own precept-root is pure, foremost among monks, to serve as this same teacher. "Foremost" means one whose own years in the Dharma and years of ordination are both long, esteemed by the assembly — only such a monastic is able to bring this formal proceeding to completion in harmony; only by using such a one as one's own teacher can this karmic procedure be properly accomplished. Should one fail to encounter a genuinely pure, great monastic, then, though one may receive the precepts formally, this amounts to nothing more than an empty name — the true, undefiled substance of the precepts themselves will, without fail, never be accomplished at all. Only upon encountering a genuine monastic, pure in the precepts, complete in both virtue and years of ordination, are the precepts truly accomplished.

Sūtra: "The precepts thus accomplished, wearing new, clean robes, burning incense in quiet dwelling, one recites this mind-mantra spoken by the Buddha one hundred and eight times, and only then marks off the boundary and establishes the place of practice."

Commentary: The precepts thus accomplished, this vessel fit for the path complete, Māra can find no opportunity at all — and, according to one's own means, whether abundant or slight, one either wears new robes, or washes existing ones until clean, and, wearing these same robes, both outward and inward alike are made pure. Burning incense to fill this same room, dwelling quietly to gather in this same mind, mind and place habituating one another together — reciting this mantra in this state, its own spiritual effect is easily achieved, and this same past habituated tendency is easily removed. Reciting this mantra one hundred and eight times represents the very number of afflictions it treats — every sentient being's own afflictions numbering one hundred and eight — and so, through the precepts opposing present karma, and this mantra removing past habituated tendency, Māra can find no opportunity at all. Only afterward may one select a day and establish this same place of cultivating the path. As the Zhǐzhǎng Commentary puts it: marking off this ritual platform requires reciting this mantra first, for two reasons: first, treating habituated tendency and purifying this same root, this ritual form is thereby made manifest; second, relying on this mantra's own spiritual efficacy, keeping every demonic affair far away. Whoever aspires to practice the path cannot but know this.

Sūtra: "Seeking, throughout the ten directions, from every land now present, the unsurpassed Tathāgatas, to emit their own light of great compassion, coming to bathe one's own crown."

Commentary: This seeks the Buddha from within samādhi itself. The two sections above dealt with the intent behind the precepts; this present passage is exactly the samādhi that arises from them. This is precisely because, once a sentient being's own mind, like water, grows clear, every buddha's own reflection appears within it — this same water of the mind grown calm and clear is itself exactly samādhi, and so one knows that this passage is already within samādhi itself. "Seeking, throughout the ten directions, from every land now present": that is, presently seated within this place of practice; "the unsurpassed Tathāgatas" means those who transcend all nine Dharma-realms, standing alone in honor — this belongs to what is sought. "Emit their own light of great compassion": this light emitted from this same mind of great compassion, coming to bathe one's own crown, body and mind alike benefiting from it — this belongs to what responds. Stimulus and response meeting one another, one may practice the path without further worry! This closes the first section, samādhi arising from the precepts.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, should such pure monks, in this final age, or nuns, laymen, or laywomen, their minds free of craving and lust, uphold the Buddha's own pure precepts, and, within this place of practice, give rise to the bodhisattva's own vows."

Commentary: The section above dealt with wisdom arising from samādhi; this present section deals with wisdom arising from samādhi. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: what comes earlier does not include what comes later, but what comes later must, without fail, include what comes earlier. The earlier section merely cited precepts and moved on to samādhi; this present section names samādhi as the cause in summary, while its own text cites both precepts and samādhi together as this same cause.

Ānanda, in this final age, marking off this boundary and establishing this platform is permitted only to pure monks; yet, once this ritual form is already established, its actual practice is not restricted to any one of the four assemblies — hence, "or nuns, laymen, or laywomen." Both assemblies who have left the household life, their own precepts kept consistently, are together called "pure"; the two lay assemblies are together called "laymen and laywomen" — a term combining Chinese and Sanskrit together, "dāna" translated "giving," and "beyond" because giving is able to carry one beyond the bitter sea of poverty. This permission is now extended universally because every sentient being alike possesses the buddha-nature, and every one alike is capable of this same practice. Though all four assemblies share this same platform, each must, without fail, follow their own proper order — an upāsaka following behind the monks, an upāsikā following behind the nuns — this being a fixed and settled order. "Their minds free of craving and lust" points to the laity, their minds free of every thought of craving, delusion, lust, and desire. "Uphold the Buddha's own pure precepts" points to those who have left the household, strictly upholding every pure precept and rule set down by every buddha, body and mind never transgressing them — only this truly suffices. Should this not be so, one must not permit indiscriminate participation, lest it obstruct the very work of the path. And, within this same place of cultivating the path, one gives rise, throughout, to the bodhisattva's own four great vows, this being the very occasion for stirring the Buddha's own response.

Sūtra: "Leaving and returning to bathe, practicing the path throughout all six periods of the day and night, remaining thus without sleep for three full weeks — I myself will manifest my own body, appearing before this person, touching their crown to comfort and reassure them, bringing them to awakening."

Commentary: As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: whenever one leaves this place of practice and then returns to it, one must, without fail, bathe — purifying the body outwardly. Practicing the path throughout all six periods means fixing one's attention, throughout, on turning hearing back upon itself, walking in meditation to dispel any drowsiness — gathering in the mind inwardly. Throughout the twelve double-hours of day and night, six periods spent practicing the path in walking meditation, and six periods spent sitting still, evenly balancing drowsiness and restlessness — as with practicing the path during the hour of the rat, sitting still during the hour of the ox, practicing the path during the hour of the tiger, sitting still during the hour of the hare, and so on. Yet, whether walking or sitting, what is practiced throughout is nothing but turning hearing back to hear one's own nature, entering the stream and letting go of what is heard. The two lines that follow close this with a fixed period of samādhi, entered decisively within this same span of time. "Without sleep" carries two senses: first, removing any drowsy unawareness; second, guarding against forgetting to turn hearing back upon itself. This section directly cites samādhi, making plain these two words, "arising from"; the section that follows deals with wisdom being born from it.

This mind of samādhi is the very direct cause of wisdom being born. "I myself will manifest my own body, appearing before this person" — touching their crown represents this same intent of gathering them in and conferring this teaching upon them; comforting and reassuring them bestows the power of fearlessness — this being the supporting condition. This cause and this condition both now complete, one is brought to a sudden, thorough understanding, a bright, great awakening. As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: yet this awakening admits of every shade, shallow and deep alike, not fixed to any single mark — should one attain the state where the two marks of motion and stillness become unarisen, this gives rise to the wisdom of the stream-enterer's own path of seeing; should one attain the exhaustion of both the hearing and what is heard, this gives rise to the arhat's own wisdom of the emptiness of self; should one attain the extinguishing of both emptiness and what was emptied, this accomplishes the wisdom of the emptiness of dharmas; should one attain the appearing of still cessation itself, this gives rise to the unsurpassed wisdom of knowledge and awareness belonging to perfect penetration itself. As Master Gushan put it: should one behold such an image, one ought to contemplate it as empty and still — if it is truly the Buddha, this will be plainly evident; if it is a demon, it will vanish away at once. This closes the first section, the initial, brief account of the place of practice.

Sūtra: "The Buddha told Ānanda: should one, in this final age, wish to set up a place of practice, first take dung from a bull of great strength, white in color, from the Snowy Mountains, feeding on the rich, fragrant grass of that mountain, drinking only the pure water of the Snowy Mountains — its dung is fine and delicate — and one may take this same dung, mixed together with sandalwood, to plaster the ground of this place."

Commentary: This shows how a place of practice is to be built. For a sage's own arrangements make manifest both principle and actual event together — the event accomplished in dependence on principle, and principle made manifest through the event; this is by no means merely a ritual form set up for the sake of outward appearance alone. The Buddha told Ānanda: sentient beings of this final age, their own obstructions deep and their own karma heavy, find it difficult to cultivate practice without setting up a place of practice — hence, "wish to set up." Fearing that the ground where this platform is set up might be impure, one must, without fail, apply a fresh coating of plaster to it. Every school of symbolic interpretation has its own particular strength; only the Precious Mirror Commentary interprets every symbol, from beginning to end, in a way that accords with the very method of cultivating perfect penetration — and so it is relied upon throughout here. The Snowy Mountains represent the very unchanging principle-substance of true suchness, this dharma-nature itself. The bull of great strength, white in color, represents this same nature's own inherent purity, the very root and correct wisdom itself. It is taken first because it eats and drinks exactly as this method prescribes, and one takes its dung, mixing it with fragrant sandalwood, to plaster this same ground. "Feeding on the rich, fragrant grass of that mountain": the Snowy Mountains hold a grass named "forbearance," eating which one may obtain ghee, rich and fatty — its dung, correspondingly, pure and fragrant. This bull drinking only the pure water of the Snowy Mountains — this fragrant grass represents the three kinds of wisdom, and this pure water represents correct samādhi. That water and grass alone govern this bull represents exactly a practitioner's own habituation, samādhi and wisdom held in perfect balance. This dung, fine and delicate, represents exactly samādhi arising from precepts, and wisdom arising from samādhi. "One may take this same dung, mixed together with sandalwood, to plaster the ground of this place": removing the old ground and covering it with this fresh plaster represents the three undefiled trainings, able to habituate the dharma-body, forming this true, causal ground itself.

Sūtra: "Should such a bull, from the Snowy Mountains, not be available, other cattle's own dung is foul and unclean, unfit for plastering the ground. Choose, instead, a level plain, and dig away the surface soil to a depth of five feet, taking the pure, yellow earth beneath."

Commentary: "Choose a level plain": choosing, instead, a level plain — for a raised mound might have impurities gathered upon it, and a low hollow might collect filth flowing into it. Digging away the surface soil to a depth of five feet, taking this same, inherently pure, yellow earth beneath, and using it instead. Should this white bull of the Snowy Mountains not be available, this represents taking up, instead, a middling capacity. "Dig away the surface soil to a depth of five feet" represents clearing away every outward concern, cultivating this turning of hearing back upon itself exclusively, from untying the knot through to breaking through the five aggregates. "Taking this same, pure, yellow earth" represents this: yellow, the color occupying the very center, represents the very center of the six sense-faculties, this middle-way buddha-nature itself.

Sūtra: "Mix with this earth sandalwood, aloeswood, storax, frankincense, saffron, benzoin, costus root, spikenard, valerian, and clove — these ten kinds of incense, finely ground into powder, mixed with this earth to form a paste, and used to plaster the ground of this place, sixteen feet across, whether square or round, forming an octagonal platform."

Commentary: Mixing in these ten kinds of incense, sandalwood and the rest, represents the ten perfections. "Finely ground into powder" represents these ten perfections broadly expanded into ten thousand practices. "Mixed with this earth to form a paste" represents these ten thousand practices being nothing but mind itself. "Used to plaster the ground of this platform" represents accomplishing this true, causal ground. "Sixteen feet across, whether square or round, forming an octagonal platform": this fixes the very form of this platform, representing the eightfold correct path gathering in the eight deviant paths. An octagon is square yet also round, round yet also square — representing substance and function not being two different things at all, event and principle alike converging on the same point.

The white bull described above represents, precisely, one of the very highest capacity, able to remain free of discriminating thought, the three conditions cut off at once, the three causes no longer arising, this deranged nature coming to rest of itself — and this very rest is itself bodhi; likened here to using nothing but this bull's own dung, mixed together with sandalwood alone. Should one be unable to bring this deranged nature to rest directly, at once, one must, instead, know how to cultivate practice through the two decisive meanings, breaking through the five aggregates at the very gateway of the six sense-faculties, attaining this original, bright awakening's own unborn, unceasing nature as the causal-stage mind, further adding these four kinds of monastic discipline and the five-part sacred mantra, and only then accomplishing this wondrous cultivation — likened here to mixing in this pure, yellow earth, adding, beyond sandalwood alone, these nine further kinds of incense.

The Sanskrit candana translates "giving happiness," that is, white sandalwood incense. Zhu Fazhen records that white sandalwood trees grow on Mount Luo, in a foreign land. This sūtra uses it to plaster the ground here — not the same as the sandalwood "on this shore," described in the Lotus Sūtra, six shu in weight, worth an entire Sahā world. Aloeswood: the Record of Strange Things records that it comes from the kingdom of Rinan; whoever wishes to gather it fells the tree and lays it upon the ground, where it decays for a long while, until its own heartwood becomes utterly solid, sinking when placed in water. Storax: the Continued Book of Han records that it comes from the great kingdom of Da Qin; combining various fragrances and boiling their sap together is called storax — or, some say, the people of the kingdom of Storax itself gather flowers, press out their sap, boil it into a paste, and sell it to traveling merchants. Frankincense: the Record of Plants and Trees of the South records that it comes from a great tree by the seashore in the kingdom of Da Qin, growing amid the sand; in high summer, this tree's own resin flows out over the sand, its nature able to relieve pain, and is gathered and sold. Saffron: the Shuōwén Dictionary records that this fragrant offering-wine is made from the finest of a hundred kinds of flower, brought as tribute from distant lands; the officials in charge of it pound and boil it, mixing it with wine to invoke the descent of the spirits. Benzoin: the Materia Medica records that it is found in abundance in the region between Shang and Luo, its tree resembling a white poplar, its fruit as large as a bird's egg, able to dispel foul vapors and clear up skin eruptions — this being exactly the resin of the sweetgum tree. Costus root: the Record of Strange Things of the Southern Regions records that it is the root of an herb, able to rouse one from nightmares, resembling licorice root in appearance, found in Jiaozhou. Spikenard: the Record of the Southern Yue records that it is also called "swallow-grass," or "fragrant-smoke grass," found among the mountain valleys of Lingling commandery, its leaves resembling sweet basil, able to clarify the essence and brighten the eyes. Valerian: found among the mountains of Liangzhou, able to harmonize and blend every fragrance together, able to relieve bloating of the abdomen, and able, further, to bring down excess energy. Clove: the Record of Strange Things records that it comes from Boluozhou, said to be a withered form of a certain herb, held in the mouth by Secretariat officials of the Han dynasty while reporting matters to the throne, for, taken over a long period, it is said to make one's own body and speech both fragrant — this being exactly the clove itself. "The ground of this place" is exactly what the esoteric teachings call marking out the ground to form a ritual space. Yet, wishing to cultivate samādhi, one must, without fail, select a day, mark off this boundary, and build this platform; and, once this practice is complete, one must dissolve this boundary and dismantle this platform at once, never leaving it standing until the first light of dawn appears — this being a fixed rule. As the ancients put it: raised earth is called an altar, and cleared earth is called a shan (a cleared, swept space). The Discourses of the States records: what an altar clears away is called a "field." "Raised earth" means exactly earth that has been built up. This present "ground of this place" may be understood exactly as this same cleared shan — and yet, since it is already called an altar here, earth must, without fail, be raised up as well; and so the ground is first cleared to form this same field, and afterward this pure, yellow earth is specially taken, mixed with incense, and, upon this same field, plastered and raised up, forming the shape of this altar, set at the very center of the room — this displays the very method of the Śūraṅgama's own ritual platform. This closes the first section, the very form of the platform to be built.

Commentary: "At the very center, facing the sun" means at the very center, in the position of honor. The Sanskrit Vairocana, given in full as Mahāvairocana, translates in three senses: first, pervading every place — the dharma-body; second, pure and complete — the body of self-enjoyment; third, light shining everywhere — the body of enjoyment for others. These three bodies share this single name because they are, alike, this same true body. Standing for this single, true Dharma-realm, the very true master of this still place, this is exactly the wisdom of the reward-body. The Sanskrit Śākyamuni translates "the capable and benevolent one, calm and silent," the teacher of this entire Sahā trichiliocosm, this being exactly the compassion of the response-body. The Sanskrit Maitreya translates "the compassionate one," the very teacher of the age yet to come. The Sanskrit Akṣobhya translates "the unmoving one," dwelling foremost among the multitude of motion in the east, representing an unmoving wisdom present even amid motion itself. The Sanskrit Amitābha translates "boundless life," dwelling in the western land of ultimate bliss, representing this same bestowal of happiness, a compassion that excludes nothing whatsoever. This image of Avalokiteśvara, manifesting these same great transformations — as made plain in an earlier passage, these many heads, arms, and eyes and the rest — this being the master of perfect penetration within the exoteric teaching. Vajragarbha, who constantly leads the vajra-holding spirits, protecting and upholding those who hold this mantra, subduing demons and removing obstructions, is exactly the master of perfect penetration within the esoteric teaching. Setting these at either side represents the exoteric and esoteric teachings both displayed together, equally.

Sūtra: "And, further, Śakra, Brahmā, Ucchuṣma, together with Nīlakaṇṭha, every one of the Kuṇḍalīs, and Bhṛkuṭī, and each of the Four Heavenly Kings, together with Vināyaka and Gaṇapati, arranged at the sides of the door, set both left and right."

Commentary: As Wenling put it: these figures set at either side of the door, Śakra, Brahmā, and the rest, are these same powerful, external protectors. In practicing this method in the age of the Dharma's decline, should any of these figures be missing, this practice can never be accomplished. Śakra means "able to be lord," exactly the ruler of Trāyastriṃśa. Brahmā, not transgressing the sense-object of desire, is exactly the ruler of the first dhyāna heaven. Ucchuṣma is exactly the Vajra of the Fire-Head. Nīlakaṇṭha is exactly the Blue-Faced Vajra. Kuṇḍalī, translated "the one who resolves bonds of enmity," is likewise another name for a vajra-spirit. Bhṛkuṭī is exactly Vairocana's own spiritual transformation — as the sūtra elsewhere describes: "to the right stands Bhṛkuṭī, a string of prayer beads hanging from her hand, three eyes, hair bound up in a topknot, her venerable form pure white, a halo of light around her beyond compare" — exactly this figure. The Four Heavenly Kings are exactly the kings of the east, south, west, and north — that is, the Upholder of the Nation, of Increasing Growth, of the Broad Eye, and of Wide Hearing. Vināyaka is exactly the pig-headed emissary; Gaṇapati is exactly the elephant-trunked emissary. From Śakra on down, these same external protectors, all alike, represent the very function of overturning what is deviant and manifesting what is correct. This closes the fourth section, the venerated images to be housed.

Sūtra: "Take, further, eight mirrors, and suspend them, facing downward, in the empty space above; and, together with the mirrors already set within the platform, arrange them so that each pair faces the other directly, allowing their reflected forms to interpenetrate one another, layer upon layer."

Commentary: Taking, further, these eight round mirrors, suspending them facing downward in the empty space above, represents every buddha's own fruit-stage attainment, transforming the eighth consciousness into this great, perfectly round mirror of wisdom, from this empty tathāgatagarbha universally illuminating the lands of the ten directions, as numerous as motes of dust. Together with the mirrors already set within this platform, surrounding this same lotus and bowl, one to each direction, facing outward, and these outer mirrors above, one to each direction, facing inward — hence, "each pair facing the other directly" — this represents how the mind of a sentient being is exactly the Buddha's own mind, the Buddha's own mind no different at all from a sentient being's own mind. "Allowing their reflected forms to interpenetrate one another, layer upon layer" represents sentient being and buddha mutually interfused, sage and ordinary being no different at all, this being exactly this inconceivable state itself. For these eight mirrors within the platform, gazing upward toward these mirrors set in the empty space above, represent this causal stage already containing this ocean of the fruit-stage within it. Should a practitioner, at any moment throughout the twelve double-hours of day and night, happen to set their own eyes upon this ritual arrangement even once, and come thereby to understand this principle clearly, they would know that mind, buddha, and sentient being are, all three, without the slightest difference — and that their entire being already dwells, throughout, within this same Dharma-realm of the unobstructed interpenetration of phenomenon with phenomenon! This closes the first section, the building of this place of practice.

Sūtra: "Throughout the first week, bow with utmost sincerity, invoking the names of the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, every great bodhisattva, and every arhat, continuously, throughout all six periods of the day; reciting this mantra while circling the platform, walking the path with a wholly sincere mind, completing, within each such period, one hundred and eight full circuits."

Commentary: This is the sequence of cultivation and realization. In this first week, one bows, recites, and walks the path. "Throughout the first week, bow with utmost sincerity": bowing described as "utmost sincerity" means fixing the mind wholly upon it, this same sincere earnestness undivided. Whenever a person cultivates the path, they must, without fail, take refuge in and revere the Three Jewels, this being the very first skillful means. The Tathāgatas of the ten directions are the Buddha Jewel; the bodhisattvas and arhats are the Saṅgha Jewel; holding and reciting this mind-mantra is the Dharma Jewel. Continuously, throughout all six periods of the day and night, holding and reciting the Buddha-crown's own mind-mantra while circling this platform, walking this path within the place of practice with a wholly sincere mind, completing, within each such period of circling the platform, one hundred and eight full circuits. This does not refer to the mantra in its entirety, but to the mind-mantra alone: tadyathā, oṃ, anale, viśade, vīra-vajra-dhāre, bandha bandhani, vajra-pāṇi phaṭ, hūṃ trūṃ phaṭ, svāhā. One need only bring the three karmic activities — of body, speech, and mind — into full accord, and this practice will naturally take effect of itself: six periods spent walking the path, six periods spent bowing, relying upon this same inconceivable power of blessing to assist the path.

Sūtra: "Throughout the second week, single-mindedly, without interruption, give rise to the bodhisattva's own vows — my own monastic discipline already teaches these same vows."

Commentary: In this second week, one gives rise to vows exclusively. Since this is called "exclusively," it is not restricted to these same six periods; and since this is called "single-mindedly," it is no longer mixed with bowing and recitation — only this single-minded giving rise to the bodhisattva's own vows. One must, without fail, carry this on from thought to thought, moment after moment, without a single break; this guards against the mind growing weary and retreating, strengthening it, instead, with this same firm resolve. "My own monastic discipline already teaches these same vows": such as the Sūtra of Brahmā's Net's own ten great kingly vows, and the bodhisattva's own four great vows — carrying out these vows firmly, one attains this same great courage.

Sūtra: "Throughout the third week, without interruption, hold this Śūraṅgama mantra continuously, all twelve double-hours of day and night; and, reaching the seventh day, every Tathāgata of the ten directions appears at once, exactly where the light of these mirrors crosses one another, and one receives the Buddha's own hand, touching one's own crown."

Commentary: In this third week, one holds this mantra exclusively. As Wenling put it: with no interval of rest across this entire time, and no fixed limit on how many times this mantra is recited, reciting and holding it exclusively, this same essential sincerity is thereby able to move and reach this same response, this same effort bringing about its own effect at last. Reaching the very last day of this third week, every Tathāgata of the ten directions appears all at once, exactly where the light of these mirrors, within this room housing the platform, crosses one another — the Buddha's own body already appearing within these mirrors, and one's own body appearing within them as well. Personally receiving every buddha's own hand, touching one's own crown, sage and ordinary being no longer separated at all. This interpenetration, layer upon layer, without end, is exactly the illuminating wisdom of sentient being and buddha meeting one another, stimulus and response meeting on the very same path.

Sūtra: "Then, within this place of practice, one cultivates samādhi — able to cause such a practitioner, even in this final age, to have body and mind both bright and pure, exactly like lapis lazuli."

Commentary: This is the accomplishment of this mind of samādhi. Cultivating, within this place of practice, this same effort of turning hearing back upon itself exclusively — hence, "one cultivates samādhi," that is, exactly the Śūraṅgama's own ear-faculty perfect penetration. Never allowing even a single thought to leak into the realm of the sound-object; illuminating this nature moment after moment, dwelling in samādhi thought after thought, nothing but this same turning of hearing back upon itself, without a single break.

This is the accomplishment of this mind of wisdom. "Able to cause": taking this mind of samādhi as the direct cause, and this place of practice, holding this mantra, and the rest, as the supporting condition — through the power of this same cause and condition together, this is thereby made able to happen. Such a person, though studying and practicing in this final age, is likewise able to give rise to wisdom and open to awakening — and so sense-faculty, sense-object, and the discriminating mind of consciousness are transformed, the moment they are attended to, into unsurpassed knowledge and awareness — hence, "body and mind both bright and pure." Inner and outer alike reflecting one another with perfect clarity, exactly like lapis lazuli — for every single dharma, without exception, is manifest within it! This closes the first section, the first three weeks accomplishing samādhi and wisdom.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, should even one among this monk's own original precept-conferring teacher, or the ten fellow monks gathered together with him, prove impure, such a place of practice mostly fails to succeed."

Commentary: This makes plain that, even once this ritual form is established, should teacher or companions prove impure, this practice mostly fails to succeed. "The ten fellow monks gathered together with him": according to the Vaipulya Dhāraṇī Sūtra, when carrying out this method, ten people or fewer — "or fewer" meaning that any of the six or seven assemblies may suffice. Should no like-minded companions be found, one should not force the matter, and may rather practice alone instead. Impurity here is of two kinds: first, the precept-conferring teacher being impure; second, one's fellow practitioners being impure — should even one among these prove impure, such a place of practice mostly fails to succeed, no buddha or sage descending upon it, no dragon or heavenly spirit protecting it, correct samādhi difficult to cultivate, this wondrous awakening difficult to give rise to — and so one knows that teacher and companions cannot but be chosen with the greatest care.

Sūtra: "From this same third week onward, sitting upright and dwelling in stillness, for a full hundred days — one of sharp capacity, without ever rising from this seat, will attain the fruit of stream-entry. And, even should body and mind not yet accomplish this sagely fruit, one will know, decisively, of oneself, that buddhahood is certain, beyond any error."

Commentary: This makes plain that these first three weeks of study and practice have not yet, in themselves, brought samādhi and wisdom to full completion. From this third week onward, sitting upright, in correct posture, dwelling in still stillness, for a full hundred days, applying oneself with bold, vigorous effort, cultivating this same effort of letting go of the sense-object to illuminate this nature directly. One of sharp capacity, their own delusion and obstruction alike light, this power of samādhi continuous and unbroken, this illuminating wisdom clear and distinct, samādhi and wisdom flowing together at once, across these same hundred days, without ever rising from this seat. This "without ever rising from this seat" for a hundred days means that this same effort has become fully effective, and, within this same samādhi, one does not even notice the passing of this length of time at all — nor is this the same as an ordinary practice of sitting for one period and walking for the next; for the text states plainly: across these hundred days, without ever rising from this seat, one attains the fruit of stream-entry directly. The Sanskrit srota-āpanna translates "entering the stream," no longer entering form, sound, fragrance, taste, touch, or mental objects, severing the discriminating affliction of views, this being the very stage of the path of seeing. Borrowing this same name from the lesser vehicle's own fruit, yet, considered in terms of the realization itself, this is, in truth, identical to the very first of the ten faiths within the perfect teaching — Ānanda himself, awakening perfectly to the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha, attained the dharma-body directly, at once, in exactly this same way.

As the Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: even should one's own body and mind still give rise to subtle afflictions, difficult to sever all at once, and the discriminating affliction of thought still remain in full — even should the sagely fruit beyond further training not yet be immediately accomplished — one will, nonetheless, know decisively, of oneself, that this matter of accomplishing buddhahood is no empty error at all. This is what is meant by a single moment of faith becoming faith forever — believing that one's own mind is itself the Buddha, that one's own nature is itself the Buddha, never again to be doubted. This closes the second section, the sequence of cultivation and realization.

Sūtra: "You asked how a place of practice should be built — it is built exactly this way."

Commentary: This closes and answers this entire request as a whole. Ānanda's own earlier question asked: "for practicing in the age of the Dharma's decline, in setting up a place of practice, how should one mark off its boundary?" I have already explained marking off this boundary and building this platform, every method involved, together with this same sequence of study, practice, and realization. Every such method has, one by one, been shown in detail — and so this closes, answering his own request, saying: "you asked how a place of practice should be built — it is built exactly this way."

As the Zhǐzhǎng Commentary puts it: "exactly this way" means exactly as described above. Yet this same method of building this platform, described above, is, in truth, no easy matter to carry out in this final age — and so a later passage on reciting this mantra permits doing so even without entering a place of practice at all. And the passage above, that even a single impurity causes this place of practice to fail, one thereby knows: those with the means should, indeed, build this platform exactly according to this method; those without such means need only keep the precepts purely. Should one have a platform without the precepts, this is, in fact, worse than having the precepts without a platform at all. This closes the first section, the renewed request to explain this place of practice.
