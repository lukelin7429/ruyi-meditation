A Mansion Without a Door

Ānanda, hearing the Buddha say that once the three conditions are severed the mind's madness simply "subsides of itself," objects that this looks like abandoning causation for the rival teaching of spontaneity. The Buddha answers with his final word on Yajñadatta — dismantling causation and spontaneity together as equally idle theorizing — then turns the force of this teaching on Ānanda himself: decades of erudition, without real practice, accomplished less than Mātaṅgī's and Yaśodharā's single night of genuine cultivation. With the assembly's doubts resolved, Ānanda formally requests the actual method of practice, comparing himself to a traveler suddenly given a mansion who still cannot find the door in. The Buddha answers by teaching the two decisive meanings that must be settled before cultivation begins — cause matching fruit, and resolving the knot exactly where it was tied — closing with a full tally of what each of the six sense-faculties is actually capable of.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 10 closed the Buddha's answer to Pūrṇa with the teaching that truth never changes and falseness never truly arises again. This fascicle opens with Ānanda, listening in, seizing on the Buddha's own words for a third and final doubt about causation: if the mind's madness merely "subsides of itself" once its conditions are severed, and this resting is itself bodhi "not obtained from anyone else," doesn't this abandon causation for the very "spontaneity" taught by the Buddha's rivals? The Buddha's answer returns one last time to Yajñadatta, now dismantling both causation and spontaneity together, in the sūtra's most demanding round of negation yet — arriving at freedom from idle theorizing altogether. He then turns this teaching's full force back on Ānanda: decades of hearing and retaining the Dharma, without a single day of real practice, could not save him from Mātaṅgī's spell — while Mātaṅgī herself, and Yaśodharā, attained more through one genuine act of practice than Ānanda's whole career of listening. This closes the sūtra's second great teaching cycle, "the cycle without arising, without obstruction." Ānanda, his doubts resolved at last, formally requests the actual entry point for practice, likening his own situation to a traveler suddenly given a magnificent house by a king, still unable to find the door in. The Buddha's answer opens the sūtra's third major division — teaching the practice of samādhi through a single sense-faculty — beginning with the two decisive meanings that must be settled before any cultivation starts at all: first, that the mind taken up at the causal stage must match the awakening sought at the fruit-stage, introducing the five turbidities and the teaching on clarifying them; second, that the very knot which binds beings must be untied exactly where it was tied, in the six sense-faculties themselves. The fascicle closes with the Buddha's full accounting of what each sense-faculty is functionally capable of — the groundwork for Ānanda's choice, in the fascicles ahead, of which single faculty to cultivate through.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. Ānanda's Third Doubt: Clinging to Causation 癸二 兼示阿難 · 子初 阿難躡佛語而執因緣

Chinese Source

〖經〗即時阿難，在大眾中，頂禮佛足，起立白佛：世尊，現說殺、盜、婬業，三緣斷故，三因不生，心中達多，狂性自歇，歇即菩提，不從人得。斯則因緣，皎然明白，云何如來，頓棄因緣？我從因緣，心得開悟。世尊！此義何獨我等年少，有學聲聞；今此會中，大目犍連，及舍利弗，須菩提等，從老梵志，聞佛因緣，發心開悟，得成無漏。今說菩提，不從因緣。則王舍城，拘舍梨等，所說自然，成第一義。唯垂大悲，開發迷悶。

〖講〗前四句經家敘儀。世尊下，阿難略牒佛語。現說殺、盜、婬業，此牒業果相續之文。以三貪為本，生死不了，則攝世界、眾生在內。究三種生續之因，因於無明，滿慈求索妄因，意擬修斷，佛示無因，何勞肯綮？即教以圓頓下手工夫，但不隨妄心分別，世間、業果、眾生，三種能緣之心不起，即是三緣頓斷，遍計執性既空，依他起性，亦不可得，現行不熏，種子不發，故三因亦復不生。

心中達多，狂性自歇者：由因緣俱斷之故，而心中無明狂性，自然歇息。前從真起妄，則無明忽生，生本不曾生，今返妄歸真，無明頓歇，歇亦無所歇，以無明本空故。又無明實性即佛性，故曰歇即菩提。妄空真露，乃現出本有家珍，不從人得也。

斯則因緣，皎然明白，云何如來，頓棄因緣？上二句，因聞佛對阿難，累排因緣，對滿慈常說因緣，前云以是因緣，世界相續；以是因緣，眾生相續；以是因緣，業果相續。今又言三緣斷故，三因不生，阿難重執因緣。下二句疑佛自語相違。上言識迷無因，妄無所依；又言歇即菩提，何勞修證；云何如來，既說因緣，又頓棄因緣耶？此阿難第三次疑因緣也。第一於顯見超情科中，疑見性不由因緣；第二於圓彰七大科前，疑萬法不由因緣；今第三疑證果成道，有果必定有因，何以亦不屬因緣？佛既久排因緣，而語中又帶因緣，此是致疑之端也。

我從因緣，心得開悟：此敘昔教因緣之益。我阿難實從因緣之法，心得開悟，而入見道位，得成初果。世尊！此因緣之義，乃屬正理，能令眾生，返邪歸正，何獨我等年少，有學之人，聞佛因緣聲教而得益？今此會中上首，大採菽氏，及鶖子、空生等，諸長老皆從老梵志：別約舍利、目連，先事沙然梵志學道，為上首弟子。沙然歿世之後，由聞因緣之教，發明心地，反邪歸正，從佛出家，開悟四諦法門，依之斷見思惑，得成無漏道，即阿羅漢所證之道。無欲漏、有漏、無明漏，三漏俱盡，得出三界，得證無生，則因緣之教，能令返邪歸正，了生脫死，超凡入聖，豈不大有益乎？

今說菩提，不從因緣：此敘今教頓棄因緣，不唯有背自宗，兼恐反濫邪教。今說，即指現今所說，無明無因，其體本空，狂心若歇，歇即菩提，何藉劬勞修證，皆頓棄因緣也。若是則王舍城，拘舍梨外道等，所說八萬劫後，自然成道，猶如縷丸，極處停止，不假修證者，翻成為第一義諦矣！即使如來今教，不落因緣一邊，亦墮自然一邊，如何得成中道了義無戲論法？心實迷悶，惟願大悲，開示發明，掃蕩我迷雲悶霧，令得朗耀性天也。初阿難躡佛語而執因緣竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: At that moment Ānanda, in the midst of the great assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, rose, and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, you have just taught that when the three conditions — killing, stealing, and sexual desire — are severed, the three causes no longer arise, and the madness in the mind of Yajñadatta subsides of itself, and this subsiding is itself bodhi — not something obtained from anyone else. This makes causation perfectly clear and evident — so why does the Tathāgata now suddenly reject causation altogether? It was, in fact, through a causal chain of reasoning that my own mind came to awaken. World-Honored One, was the benefit of this teaching confined only to us, the young ones still in training among the śrāvakas? Even now, in this very assembly, Mahāmaudgalyāyana, Śāriputra, Subhūti, and others, having come from an elder wandering ascetic, heard your teaching on causation, gave rise to the resolve for awakening, and attained the undefiled path. Now you teach that bodhi does not come from causation at all. If this is so, then the 'spontaneity' taught by Maskarin Gośālīputra and the other wanderers of Rājagṛha would turn out to be the ultimate truth after all! I beg only that you, in great compassion, dispel this confusion."

Commentary: The first four lines are the sūtra compiler's own scene-setting. From "World-Honored One" on, Ānanda briefly cites the Buddha's own words back to him. "You have just taught that when the three conditions — killing, stealing, and sexual desire — are severed" cites the earlier teaching on karma-continuity: with the three cravings as its root, birth and death never comes to an end, and this same account covers world-continuity and sentient-being-continuity as well. Tracing all three continuities back to their common root, one arrives at ignorance. Pūrṇa had gone looking for a cause behind this falseness, intending to cultivate its severance, and the Buddha showed him there is no such cause at all — why labor over it? This is exactly the instruction for the sudden, perfect approach: simply do not let the deluded mind go on discriminating, and the three minds that condition the world, karma-fruit, and sentient beings never even arise — this is itself the sudden severing of the three conditions. Once the nature that grasps at what is merely conceived is empty, the dependent nature has nothing left to attach to either; present activity no longer perfumes the seeds, and the seeds no longer sprout — hence the three causes likewise cease to arise.

"The madness in the mind of Yajñadatta subsides of itself": once the conditions are all severed together, the mind's own ignorant madness naturally comes to rest. Earlier, falseness arose out of truth, and ignorance suddenly appeared — but this arising was never truly an arising at all. Now, turning falseness back into truth, ignorance is suddenly at rest — but this resting, too, is not really a resting of anything, since ignorance was empty to begin with. And because ignorance's true nature is itself none other than buddha-nature, this resting is itself bodhi. Once falseness is empty, truth stands revealed — the treasure of one's own household, always already possessed, simply comes into view; it was never obtained from anyone else.

"This makes causation perfectly clear and evident — so why does the Tathāgata now suddenly reject causation?" These lines follow from Ānanda's having repeatedly heard the Buddha invoke causation with Pūrṇa — teaching that it is by causation that the world continues, by causation that sentient beings continue, by causation that karma-fruit continues — and now, hearing the Buddha say instead that once the three conditions are severed the three causes no longer arise, Ānanda clings once again to causation. The next two lines suspect the Buddha of contradicting himself: earlier the Buddha said that confusion has no cause and falseness has nothing to depend on, and also that resting is itself bodhi, needing no toilsome cultivation at all — so why does the Tathāgata sometimes speak of causation and other times suddenly reject it? This is Ānanda's third doubt about causation. The first arose in the section on how seeing surpasses every category, doubting that the nature of seeing does not depend on causation; the second arose just before the section fully proclaiming the seven great elements, doubting that the ten thousand phenomena do not depend on causation; and now, this third doubt concerns the realization of the fruit and the accomplishment of the path — surely, where there is a fruit, there must be a cause; how could this too fall outside of causation? Since the Buddha has for so long been dismantling causation, and yet his own words still carry causation within them, this is exactly what gives rise to Ānanda's doubt.

"It was, in fact, through a causal chain of reasoning that my own mind came to awaken": this recounts the benefit of the earlier teaching on causation. I, Ānanda, truly did awaken my mind through the teaching of causation, and thereby entered the path of seeing and attained the first fruit. World-Honored One, this teaching of causation belongs to the correct principle, capable of turning sentient beings back from error toward the right path — how could its benefit be confined only to us, the young ones still in training? The foremost elders in this very assembly — the great one surnamed Kolita, together with Śāriputra, Subhūti, and the rest — all came by way of an elder wandering ascetic first: Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana, in particular, had originally served the wandering ascetic Sañjaya as their teacher, becoming his foremost disciples; after Sañjaya's death, it was upon hearing the teaching of causation that they opened their minds, turned from error back to the right path, left the household life under the Buddha, awakened to the teaching of the four noble truths, and, relying on it, severed the afflictions of views and thought, attaining the undefiled path — the very path realized by an arhat. With the three defilements of desire, existence, and ignorance all exhausted, they escaped the three realms and realized the unborn. The teaching of causation, then, is exactly what turns beings from error back to the right path, frees them from birth and death, and lifts them from the ordinary into the ranks of the sagely — how could this not be of the greatest benefit?

"Now you teach that bodhi does not come from causation": this recounts how the present teaching's sudden rejection of causation not only seems to run against the Buddha's own earlier teaching, but risks being confused with the very heterodox teachings the Buddha has always opposed. "Now you teach" refers to what has just been taught: that ignorance has no cause, its substance originally empty; that once the mad mind is at rest, this resting is itself bodhi, needing no toilsome cultivation at all — all of this suddenly setting causation aside. If this is so, then the teaching of Maskarin Gośālīputra and the other wanderers of Rājagṛha — that after eighty thousand aeons the path is accomplished spontaneously, like a ball of thread unwinding to its natural stopping point, requiring no cultivation or realization at all — would turn out to be the ultimate truth! And even if the Tathāgata's present teaching does not fall to the extreme of causation, it would still fall to the extreme of spontaneity — how, then, could it ever amount to the middle way's teaching of complete meaning, free of idle theorizing? My mind is genuinely confused; I beg only that you, in your great compassion, open and reveal the truth, sweeping away the clouds and mist of my confusion, and let the sky of my own nature shine clear." This closes the first part: Ānanda seizes on the Buddha's own words and clings to causation.

The Buddha's Final Word on Yajñadatta: Neither Causation Nor Spontaneity 子二 如來拂深情而責執吝 · 丑初 就喻拂情伸意 · 二 迭拂諸情令盡

Chinese Source

〖經〗佛告阿難：即如城中，演若達多，狂性因緣，若得除滅，則不狂性，自然而出，因緣、自然，理窮如是。阿難！演若達多，頭本自然，本自其然，無然非自，何因緣故，怖頭狂走？若自然頭，因緣故狂；何不自然，因緣故失？本頭不失，狂怖妄出，曾無變易，何藉因緣？本狂自然，本有狂怖；未狂之際，狂何所潛？不狂自然，頭本無妄，何為狂走？若悟本頭，識知狂走，因緣、自然，俱為戲論。是故我言：三緣斷故，即菩提心。

〖講〗即如，乃即就前喻，以推阿難所執，因緣、自然之情。達多狂性，喻無明，以為能障菩提之因緣；若得除滅，合狂心若歇。不狂性，喻菩提，以狂性若得除滅，則不狂性，自然而出，合歇即菩提。在汝所謂因緣、自然之理，研窮起來，必定如是。汝實未知，我說三緣斷故，三因不生，狂性因緣，若得除滅，此本非因緣。又說狂心若歇，歇即菩提，此亦非自然。以下約頭狂雙拂，因緣、自然二計皆非。

此約頭拂自然。頭喻菩提之真，前二句標定，頭為自然。謂演若達多之頭，若本來是自然。三、四二句，本自其然，無然非自者：即是既本來自然，牒上句意，即應常時自然，無時而不自然也。末二句反難，以何因緣之故，忽怖無頭，而狂走覓頭耶？既然狂怖妄出，則頭不得謂為自然矣！法合，謂眾生真性，若本來自然，無有那一時，不是自然，何因緣故，復起無明，迷真逐妄，今欲返妄歸真耶？既有無明妄動，則真性不得謂之自然矣！此中破自然，只破自然，不是以因緣，對破自然。若對破則成矯亂，何因緣故句，則何故也。

此約頭拂因緣。文中雖有自然，及狂字，但惟帶言而已。恐聞前喻，自然被破，轉計因緣，故獨約頭辨，以明非因緣也。若自然本有之頭，由照鏡因緣之故，狂怖無頭；下二句反難，何不以自然之頭，由照鏡因緣之故，遂真失耶？法合：謂本來真性，由必欲加明因緣，故起無明，何不以本來真性，由無明因緣故，而遂真失耶？

此結明非因緣，申其正義。以本有之頭，雖由照鏡因緣狂走，其頭依然不失。設有人，見達多之狂走，問云：「何為狂走？」答曰：「我要覓頭。」彼人以手摩其頭曰：「這是甚麼？」達多始覺，頭還不失，狂怖無端妄出，既狂之時，頭原無失，歇狂之後，頭亦無得。而狂起狂歇，不關本頭之事，其頭非但無失，曾無絲毫變易，則何所藉因緣耶？法合：真性常住不失，無明忽然妄起，無明雖起，真性不變，妄起之時，雖迷不失；妄滅之後，雖證無得。妄起妄滅，與真性本不相干，則真性亦不屬因緣矣！

此約狂拂自然。狂喻無明之妄，若謂本來狂是自然，即應本來常有狂怖。下二句難云：既是常有，而未發狂之際，其狂潛藏何處？難道身心之中，有潛狂所在耶？既無潛狂之所，則狂非自然矣！法合：無明若是自然，則本有無明，當一念未動之時，清淨本然心中，無明何所潛藏？以真元無妄故，何得謂無明為自然？

此約狂拂因緣。首句反言，不是狂出於自然，即是翻成因緣。頭本無妄者：謂狂怖之時，頭本不失，即當常無狂怖，有何因緣，而狂走耶？頭本無妄，則狂非因緣矣！法合：謂無明不是自然，是因緣者，畢竟以何為因？然真性宛在，不曾遺失，為何因緣，而背覺合塵耶？若真性有失，可說因緣，真性不失，則非因緣矣！以上喻明真性與無明，俱不屬因緣、自然矣。阿難一向溺於權宗，不知衣裏神珠宛在，輾轉他方求食，因緣之見，固不能忘也。

滿慈執無明有因，阿難疑真性同自，皆由不悟本頭不失，狂走無端，所以墮入因緣、自然之二計。若悟本頭，雖狂不失，則頭非自然；依然宛在，則頭非因緣；若知狂走，未狂元無，則狂非自然；頭本無妄，則狂非因緣。若明斯義，則因緣、自然，俱為戲論，全無實義。此正銷阿難之現疑，兼防滿慈之又執。是因緣、自然，俱屬戲論之故，我先言三緣斷故，即菩提心；三種分別之緣斷故，則菩提非自然；妄離真顯，當下即是，則菩提非因緣矣！此佛重伸自己所說，歇即菩提之意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Consider that very Yajñadatta in the city: if his madness — a matter, you say, of causes and conditions — could be brought to an end, then his sane nature would have to emerge 'spontaneously.' Causation and spontaneity, examined to their very limit, both come down to exactly this same problem. Ānanda, if Yajñadatta's head were spontaneous by nature — of itself simply so, with nothing about it that was not simply so — then by what cause or condition did he become terrified that he had no head, and run about in madness? And if it were his natural head that, because of some cause or condition, went mad, then why did it not equally lose itself, because of some cause or condition, in the first place? His original head was never lost; his mad terror arose out of nothing at all; the head itself never underwent any change whatsoever — what, then, could there possibly be for causation to explain? If his madness were spontaneous by nature, then he would have possessed this mad terror from the very start — but before he ever went mad, where was this madness hiding? And if his madness were not spontaneous, then his head was never truly lost at all — so why, then, did he run about in madness? Once one truly understands one's own head, and recognizes his running about in madness for what it was, causation and spontaneity are both seen to be nothing but idle theorizing. This is exactly why I said: once the three conditions are severed, this is itself the mind of bodhi."

Commentary: "Consider that very [Yajñadatta] in the city" takes up the earlier analogy directly, to examine what Ānanda still clings to under the names "causation" and "spontaneity." Yajñadatta's madness stands for ignorance, taken as the very cause and condition capable of obstructing bodhi; its being brought to an end corresponds to the mad mind coming to rest. His sane nature stands for bodhi; once his madness is brought to an end, his sane nature emerges "spontaneously," corresponding to this resting being itself bodhi. Examined by the logic of what you call causation and spontaneity, this must indeed be the necessary conclusion. But you have not actually understood: when I say that once the three conditions are severed the three causes no longer arise, and that once his madness — his so-called "causation" — is brought to an end, this was never truly a matter of causation to begin with; and when I say that once the mad mind is at rest, this resting is itself bodhi, this too was never truly a matter of spontaneity. What follows sweeps away both positions at once, by way of the head and the madness in turn — neither causation nor spontaneity survives.

This first sweeps away spontaneity, by way of the head. The head stands for the truth of bodhi. The first two lines posit that the head is spontaneous — that Yajñadatta's head, that is, was spontaneous by its very nature from the start. The next two lines, "of itself simply so, with nothing about it that was not simply so," draw out what this would mean: if it were spontaneous from the start, it should have remained spontaneous at every moment, with never a time when it was not simply so. The last two lines turn this into a challenge: by what cause or condition, then, did he suddenly become terrified that he had no head, and run about in madness? Since this terror and this madness arose out of nothing at all, the head cannot, after all, be called "spontaneous." Applied to the doctrine: if sentient beings' own true nature were spontaneous from the start, there could never be a single moment when it was not simply so — so by what cause or condition did ignorance ever arise at all, mistaking truth and chasing after falseness, such that one would now need to turn falseness back into truth? Since ignorance did, in fact, arise and stir falsely, the true nature cannot, after all, be called "spontaneous." Note that this passage refutes spontaneity on its own terms, purely by spontaneity's own logic — it is not refuting spontaneity by counterposing causation against it. Were it doing that, the argument would collapse into confusion; "by what cause or condition" here simply means "for what reason," nothing more.

This next sweeps away causation, likewise by way of the head. Though the words "spontaneous" and "mad" still appear in this passage, they are carried along only incidentally. Fearing that, once spontaneity has just been refuted, one might switch instead to clinging to causation, the Buddha addresses the head alone once more, to show that causation, too, fails. If it were his natural, original head that, by the cause and condition of looking in the mirror, became terrified that it had no head — then the next two lines turn this into a challenge: why did this same natural head not, by that very same cause and condition of looking in the mirror, actually lose itself for real? Applied to the doctrine: if it was by the cause and condition of wishing to add brightness to awareness that the original true nature gave rise to ignorance — then why did that same original true nature not, by the cause and condition of ignorance, actually lose itself for real?

This closes by making plain that none of this was ever a matter of causation, and states the correct meaning directly. Although his original head, by the cause and condition of looking in the mirror, was the occasion for his mad running, the head itself remained, the whole time, never actually lost. Suppose someone, seeing Yajñadatta running about in madness, had asked him: "Why are you running like this?" and he had answered: "I am looking for my head" — and that person had then reached out, touched his head, and said: "What is this?" Only then would Yajñadatta have realized that his head had never been lost at all. His mad terror arose out of nothing at all: at the very moment of his madness, the head was never actually lost; and once his madness came to rest, there was likewise nothing newly gained. The arising and the resting of his madness, then, had nothing whatsoever to do with the head itself — the head was not only never lost, it never underwent the slightest change at all — so what, then, could there possibly be for causation to explain? Applied to the doctrine: the true nature abides eternally, never lost; ignorance arises suddenly and falsely, yet even while ignorance is present the true nature never changes — at the very moment falseness arises, though one is deluded, nothing is actually lost; and once falseness ceases, though one has realized the truth, nothing is actually newly gained. Falseness arising and falseness ceasing have, in fact, nothing whatsoever to do with the true nature — so the true nature, too, cannot be said to belong to causation.

This sweeps away spontaneity, by way of the madness itself. The madness stands for the falseness of ignorance. If one claimed that this madness was spontaneous by nature from the start, it should have been present, as mad terror, at every moment from the beginning. The next two lines press the challenge: if it were present at every moment, then, before the madness had ever broken out, where was this madness hiding? Was there some hidden reservoir of madness lodged somewhere in his body or mind? Since there was no such hidden place at all, the madness cannot, after all, be called "spontaneous." Applied to the doctrine: if ignorance were spontaneous, then, at the very moment before a single thought had yet stirred, within the originally pure and clear mind, where could ignorance possibly have been hiding? Since the true and original nature never contained any falseness at all, how could ignorance ever be called spontaneous?

This sweeps away causation, by way of the madness itself. The first line reverses the claim: if his madness did not arise spontaneously, this turns the explanation over to causation instead. "His head was never truly lost" means: at the very time of his mad terror, the head was, in fact, never lost at all — so it ought to have remained, at every moment, entirely free of mad terror; by what cause or condition, then, did he run about in madness? Since the head was never truly lost, the madness cannot, after all, be attributed to causation either. Applied to the doctrine: if one claims ignorance is not spontaneous but rather a matter of causation, then what, in the end, could possibly serve as its cause? The true nature remains present the whole time, never actually lost — by what cause or condition, then, would one turn one's back on awareness to unite with the dust of the senses? Only if the true nature had actually been lost could one speak of a cause for this at all; since the true nature is never actually lost, this too cannot be a matter of causation. All of the above, by way of the analogy, makes plain that neither the true nature nor ignorance belongs to causation or spontaneity at all. Ānanda has all along been submerged in the provisional teachings, unaware that the wondrous pearl sewn into his own robe was there the whole time, wandering from place to place begging for food instead — his attachment to the view of causation has, understandably, been difficult to let go of.

Pūrṇa clung to the notion that ignorance must have a cause; Ānanda now doubts that the true nature must be identical to spontaneity — both errors come from the same failure to realize that the original head was never lost, and that the mad running arose out of nothing at all, and so both fall into the twin traps of causation and spontaneity. Once one truly understands one's own head — that though mad, it was never lost, the head is thus not spontaneous; that it remained present the whole time, the head is thus not a matter of causation; that the mad running, before it ever arose, was originally nothing at all, madness is thus not spontaneous; that the head was never truly lost, madness is thus not a matter of causation — once this meaning is understood, causation and spontaneity are both seen to be nothing but idle theorizing, with no real substance to either at all. This directly dissolves Ānanda's present doubt, while also guarding against Pūrṇa's own attachment recurring. It is precisely because causation and spontaneity are both nothing but idle theorizing that I earlier said: once the three conditions are severed, this is itself the mind of bodhi. Once the three conditioning minds of discrimination are severed, bodhi is thus not a matter of spontaneity; once falseness departs and truth stands revealed, this being so at once and directly, bodhi is thus not a matter of causation either. This is the Buddha restating, once more, the meaning of his own earlier teaching: that resting is itself bodhi.

Yet however much you can memorize and retain, the world only calls you foremost in learning; you yourself know full well that this has not made your own power of the path complete.

"This could never equal a single day spent cultivating the undefiled path": this undefiled path must not be understood as the practice of the two lesser vehicles — it points instead to the sudden, perfect method of cultivation, and should be read in connection with the surrounding passages, both before and after. Since mere learning falls so far short of actual practice, hence "could never equal a single day": the mad mind suddenly at rest, no longer following the three conditioning minds that discriminate the world, karma-fruit, and sentient beings — this is exactly turning one's back on the dust of the senses to unite with awareness, going against that beginningless stream of desire that drives birth and death, and thereby escaping the world's twin sufferings of aversion and craving. Aversion and craving are the two causes of suffering; birth and death are their two resulting fruits. Aversion and craving need no further elaborate explanation: seeing something as different from oneself gives rise to aversion, seeing it as the same gives rise to craving. If one is able to stop following after discrimination, then, the senses no longer being engaged at all, how could aversion or craving ever arise from them? Once the cause of suffering is severed, its fruit of suffering falls away of itself — this is exactly what cultivating the undefiled path means, and shows the man of great learning the way back home along the very road he is already on: not by exhausting the flow outward toward the dust of sound, but by turning that flow backward to illuminate one's own nature. As a verse later in the sūtra puts it: "Taking up hearing to hold onto every buddha's teaching — why not instead turn hearing back to hear your own hearing itself?" — this corresponds exactly to what is taught here.

Commentary: Here Mātaṅgī is cited to spur Ānanda on. Having long been a woman given to desire, she was bound by all three obstructions at once: lustful mind is the obstruction of affliction; her lifelong habit of desire is the obstruction of karma; and her present birth in a woman's body is the obstruction of retribution. Relying on the Śūraṅgama Mantra's own awesome power, she dissolved her craving and desire — that is, the fire of lust suddenly subsided and her river of craving ran dry — and so the obstruction of affliction was already removed. "Within this teaching" — within the Buddha-dharma itself — she became "a forest of diligent practice," and so the obstruction of karma was severed as well. Bearing now the name Bhikṣuṇī Prakṛti, counted among the ranks of the sangha jewel, the obstruction of retribution too had already been transformed.

Rāhula is the Buddha's own son — not conceived through desire, but said to have entered the womb merely by the pointing of a finger, remaining there for six years, hence his name, meaning "hindrance" or "veil." Yaśodharā's name means "bearer of renown" — a woman of renown among women, and the Buddha's own wife. When the Buddha was still a prince, he married at seventeen, though husband and wife shared no worldly passion between them and were never physically united. The prince left the household at nineteen and attained buddhahood at thirty; Yaśodharā, together with the Buddha's own aunt, then resolved to leave the household life as well. Bhikṣuṇī Prakṛti and Yaśodharā came to the very same realization of their past causes, understanding that, throughout every life across the ages, nothing but craving had ever been their suffering — understanding that the retribution of a woman's body carries especially heavy craving and desire, and that their own suffering, across so many lives, had always come down to this same craving.

"Through a single moment of undefiled, wholesome practice, one attains release from bondage, another receives a prediction of buddhahood": this is the very point of the Buddha's spurring exhortation. Bhikṣuṇī Prakṛti and Yaśodharā, though both born in a woman's body, a lesser vessel by worldly reckoning, nevertheless attained this simply through a single moment of undefiled, wholesome practice. And what was this practice? Simply this: realizing that craving alone had been their suffering across every past life, and, with a single thought, damming the very flow of that craving so it could no longer run outward — and because it no longer ran outward, it turned back and returned to its own source, and the power of samādhi was thereby accomplished. The next two lines make plain the fruits they realized. "One attains release from bondage": this refers to Bhikṣuṇī Prakṛti, whose river of craving ran dry, severing the nine grades of the affliction of thought throughout the mingled realms of the five destinies, and thereby escaping the bondage of birth and death within the desire realm. It also refers to Yaśodharā, who had already realized the fourth fruit, her work accomplished, freed from birth-and-death by segments, and thereby escaping the bondage of birth and death within the three realms altogether. "Another receives a prediction of buddhahood": this means that, should these two women turn from the lesser vehicle toward the greater, setting aside the śrāvaka path entirely, they would finally attain the Tathāgata's own nirvāṇa without remainder, following the very path of their original resolve onward toward bodhi itself — and would then receive the Buddha's own prediction of their future buddhahood, an attainment whose full measure cannot yet even be reckoned.

"How, then, can you go on deceiving yourself, still clinging to mere seeing and hearing?" — these are words of direct rebuke. Yaśodharā's own woman's body was already reckoned a lesser vessel; Mātaṅgī, a woman given to desire, was of an even lower capacity still — and yet both attained such advancement simply through a single moment of undefiled, wholesome practice. How, then, can you, Ānanda, in the fine and dignified form of a man, born of the illustrious royal house itself, cling only to mere learning, content to remain in so lowly a position — when the benefit of genuine practice now stands plainly before your own eyes, and still you refuse to press forward and practice? How can you go on deceiving and demeaning yourself in this way, still lingering in attachment to mere seeing and hearing and discrimination? This points directly at giving rise to grasping upon seeing appearances, and clinging upon hearing sounds — inevitably following after the senses outward, and thereby drawing one's own continued transmigration upon oneself.

Master Jiaoguang observes: one should understand that this careful distinction Ānanda raises here is of the utmost importance. For having just finished dismantling causation entirely, the Buddha is now about to speak of cultivation and realization once again. If cultivation and realization are said to definitely exist, this would seem to contradict what was just said; yet if causation and conditions are said to definitely not exist at all, this would undercut the very cultivation and realization about to be taught — the sage's own words seem, on the surface, to contradict themselves, and this cannot be left unexamined. What is now made clear is that the true nature never actually changes — just as the head was never actually lost — and yet this hardly means there is no genuine cultivation or realization at all; it was never simply a matter of falling entirely into causation to begin with. And it is likewise made clear that falseness's present appearance as delusion is exactly like the madness's present arising — and yet this hardly means there is, in the end, no cultivation or realization either; it was never simply a matter of falling entirely into spontaneity to begin with. From this one comes to understand: in this sūtra, "no cultivation, no realization" never actually obstructs there being genuine cultivation and realization; and "genuine cultivation and realization" likewise never obstructs there being, at the same time, no cultivation and no realization at all. The passages before and after this point no longer present any real contradiction to debate — and how subtle and wondrous this teaching truly is!

Sūtra: Ānanda and the whole assembly, hearing the Buddha's own instruction, found their doubts entirely dissolved; their minds awakened to true reality itself, and body and mind together grew light and at ease — an attainment never known before. Once again they wept, moved to tears, bowed at the Buddha's feet, knelt with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Unsurpassed in great compassion, pure and precious king — you have so skillfully opened our minds, guiding us with every kind of causal circumstance and every skillful means, drawing all who are sunk and lost in darkness out of the ocean of suffering."

Commentary: This passage draws together, in one summary, the two great sections answering Pūrṇa directly and, alongside it, instructing Ānanda — since no closing summary had appeared right after the answer to Pūrṇa alone, this passage now closes both together. It first recounts their awakening, which can only follow from the Buddha's own instruction, hence "Ānanda and the whole assembly, hearing the Buddha's own instruction" — Pūrṇa's own name is not mentioned separately here, since he is included within "the whole assembly," this passage closing out both his and Ānanda's doubts together, not Ānanda's alone. "Found their doubts entirely dissolved": there were five doubts in all — four belonging to Pūrṇa, one to Ānanda — every one of them a deep and subtle confusion. Pūrṇa's doubts: first, doubting how, if originally pure, the three continuities could suddenly arise from it at all — the Buddha showed that the arising and continuing of the ten thousand phenomena begins with a single thought of ignorance, and so every worldly appearance follows. Second, doubting how the five great elements, each with a nature that does not interfere with the others, could nonetheless pervade one another without obstruction — the Buddha showed that every appearance is, in its entirety, identical with the nature itself, one single, unchanging, wondrous substance, and so can follow conditions freely and without hindrance. Third, doubting whether the Buddhas and Tathāgatas might, at some future time, give rise to falseness once again — the Buddha showed that falseness was never truly produced to begin with, exactly like a diseased eye seeing flowers in space: the space-flowers were never truly born, and truth, in the end, never actually changes, exactly like ore, once smelted into gold, never reverting to ore again. Fourth, doubting by what cause sentient beings ever came to have falseness at all, obscuring their own wondrous brightness — the Buddha showed that, since it is called "falseness" in the first place, how could it possibly have a real cause? For if it truly had a cause, how could it still be called "falseness" at all? Ānanda's doubt was that the Buddha's sudden rejection of causation might risk being confused with spontaneity — the Buddha showed that once one knows truth is, from the start, always already possessed, and understands that falseness is, at root, empty, then causation and spontaneity are both seen to be nothing but idle theorizing. Having heard these repeated, wondrous demonstrations, their doubts and confusion were thus entirely dissolved.

Pūrṇa clung to the notion that the cause must exist and doubted the fruit; he also doubted whether falseness itself had a cause. Ānanda clung stubbornly to his former teaching, and doubted that truth might be confused with spontaneity — both men, alike, were indulging in idle theorizing. The Buddha further taught them that idle theorizing bears no fruit: unless one lets go of idle theorizing, then even aeons of labor come, in the end, to no real realization at all; but if one is able to let go of idle theorizing, the mad mind is at once brought to rest, and this resting is itself bodhi. Though these words were addressed to Ānanda alone, their meaning was intended for Pūrṇa as well — one might say a single stroke of ink, and two dragons take shape at once.

Hence they came, together, to awaken their minds to true reality itself. This "true reality" is none other than the non-empty tathāgatagarbha, the empty-and-non-empty tathāgatagarbha, and the supremely pure and bright mind of bodhi. The empty tathāgatagarbha had already been awakened to in the three fascicles before this one, and so is not counted among what is meant here. In fascicle 2, at the close of the ten displays of seeing, the Buddha rebuked the assembly for being narrow, inferior, and lacking real understanding, unable to penetrate to pure true reality — this was the first time "true reality" was spoken of, and everything that follows it — the truth stripped bare of falseness, the nature harmonized across the four categories — is likewise true reality. In fascicle 3, just before the section fully proclaiming the seven great elements, the Buddha promised that future practitioners of the greater vehicle would come to penetrate true reality — this was the second time it was spoken of, and everything discussed from there on, the seven great elements' pervading fullness, and the pervasive, constant mind Ānanda and the assembly awakened to, is likewise true reality. And now, this third time, as recounted here by the sūtra's own compiler, that they have "awakened to true reality" may likewise be understood in the same sense. Everything the Buddha has just taught concerning the latter two aspects of the tathāgatagarbha, directly answering Pūrṇa and, alongside it, instructing Ānanda, has been, in its entirety, nothing but the unfolding of this same true reality. "Body and mind together grew light and at ease — an attainment never known before": at the close of fascicle 3, when Ānanda and the assembly awakened to the empty tathāgatagarbha, it was said that "body and mind became utterly cleared away, free of all obstruction" — awakening to the empty aspect meant that the false body and false mind were utterly cleared away, leaving nothing behind, and so obstruction vanished; while the true body and true mind, utterly cleared, spacious and vast, never had any obstruction to begin with. Now, awakening to the latter two aspects, body and mind grow light and at ease: fully realizing that ignorance and the ten thousand phenomena are, at root, empty, no coarse and heavy view of body or mind remains — hence "light"; and knowing for themselves that the true mind of bodhi is, from the start, already possessed, needing no toilsome labor to attain — hence "at ease." All of this, never before attained in the past, is now attained at last — hence "an attainment never known before."

The first four lines set the scene of their reverence; the middle three praise the Buddha's goodness; the last give thanks for the benefit received. "Once again they wept": this is the fifth time Ānanda has been moved to tears, hence "once again." The first time, after being rescued from the binding spell and brought back, he bowed and wept in grief, resenting that, from beginningless time, he had relied only on learning and never made his own power of the path complete. The second time, after his three delusions had been refuted, he wept once more, confessing that he had relied on the Buddha's own awesome presence rather than diligently cultivating samādhi himself, so that though his body had left the household life, his mind had never truly entered the path. The third time, in the section on seeing having nowhere to return to, he wept and clasped his hands, saying that although he had received the Buddha's own voice and awakened to the wondrous, bright mind, he still dared not recognize it as his own original ground of mind. The fourth time, after the ten displays of seeing, in the section stripping away falseness to reveal pure truth, hearing that "seeing seeing itself is not seeing," his confusion only deepened further, and he wept and bowed, begging the Buddha to bestow the great eye of wisdom upon him. This may be understood in two ways: first, that with regard to the hidden, causal-stage mind by which all the Buddhas and Tathāgatas cultivate and realize unsurpassed bodhi, though this assembly had already awakened to it, they had not yet found the gate of practice and so could not actually enter and realize it — hence their minds had not yet found freedom; second, that with regard to the causal mind of bodhi itself, though Ānanda and Pūrṇa alike had already awakened to it — each, at the close of the three fascicles before this one, coming to know for themselves that the true mind pervades all and is ever-constant, and then, upon receiving the Buddha's own teaching, coming to know that the tathāgatagarbha's own wondrous, aware, bright mind pervades the ten directions, containing and nurturing the Tathāgata's own lands — this complete and perfect understanding remains difficult to hold fast to, and, true practice not yet begun, they still belonged to the class of indeterminate nature, unable to avoid regressing the moment they meet an unfavorable condition, without any freedom of their own, not yet at ease. The Buddha therefore urges them not to grow weary, and shows them the two decisive meanings, so as to turn this indeterminate nature into a truly settled one.

"And, further, for sentient beings of the degenerate age to come, after his own final nirvāṇa, who would seek to give rise to the mind of bodhi": "further" means "together with, and also" — the Buddha's own compassion is inexhaustible, wishing to benefit beings present and future alike; "to come" means the future. When the Buddha enters final nirvāṇa, the five abodes of affliction are extinguished, both kinds of birth-and-death transcended — birth-and-death by mutation already exhausted, birth-and-death by segments likewise left behind — and he returns to the great ocean of utter stillness. After his final nirvāṇa, the true Dharma abides in the world for a thousand years, the semblance Dharma for a thousand years more, and the degenerate Dharma for ten thousand years after that; that sentient beings of this degenerate age, with their shallow roots and inferior wisdom, should still be able to give rise to the mind of bodhi is truly a rare and precious thing. "Opened the wondrous path of practice belonging to the unsurpassed vehicle": the unsurpassed vehicle is the single, highest buddha-vehicle itself — even the shared teaching's "one vehicle" still admits something higher above it, and only the distinct teaching's own "one vehicle" may properly be called unsurpassed; this is the fruit toward which one presses forward, namely the nirvāṇa without remainder Ānanda has just requested. The line that follows names the cause by which one presses toward it, namely the path of one's own original resolve mentioned just before. This "wondrous path of practice" points, in hidden fashion, to the perfect penetration through the ear-faculty: from the wondrous principle of the nature of hearing, giving rise to the wondrous wisdom of hearing turned back upon itself, and, by means of this wondrous wisdom, illuminating this wondrous principle — hearing, reflecting, cultivating, and realizing in turn. "Opened," in the first line, corresponds to "path" in the second: once the path of this original resolve, this wondrous practice, has been opened, then nirvāṇa without remainder and unsurpassed bodhi may at last be hoped for.

This first teaches the two decisive meanings in general outline. The Buddha announces to Ānanda, to the whole assembly now present, and, equally, to sentient beings yet to come, that they are all resolved to seek unsurpassed bodhi, unwilling to rest content within the phantom city forever, determined instead to press onward toward the true treasure. "With regard to the Buddhas' own wondrous samādhi": adding the word "wondrous" to "samādhi" points to the very samādhi Ānanda requested at the very start — the second of the three names he asked about. This is likewise the very cultivation of "the teaching of complete meaning" named in the sūtra's own title, and likewise the very samādhi Avalokiteśvara entered through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating — the method all the Buddhas cultivate in common. "Unwilling to grow weary or discouraged" is admirable indeed! — but one must first understand clearly whether the very first, causal-stage mind that gives rise to this resolve for awakening is the true mind, or the false mind. If one relies on the false mind, the causal stage is not genuine, and the fruit it brings will be crooked and twisted, unable to attain unsurpassed bodhi — like trying to steam sand into rice. If one relies on the true mind instead, taking what is unborn and unceasing as the causal-stage mind, then one can go on to perfectly accomplish cultivation and realization at the fruit-stage. Hence, with regard to these two decisive meanings, one cannot but understand them clearly in advance. The Buddha now explains these two decisive meanings himself, first posing the question in general: what are these two decisive meanings governing the first mind?

Sūtra: "Within your own body, solidity is earth, moisture is water, warmth is fire, and movement is wind; bound by these four, your own calm, perfect, wondrous, bright awareness becomes divided into seeing, hearing, sensing, and discerning — and, from beginning to end, five layers of turbidity result."

Commentary: This directly identifies the substance of the five turbidities. This sūtra uses the same term as other scriptures but with a different meaning: what is meant here is simply that once the earth of the four elements is thrown into the perfectly calm water of the mind, the water loses its own clarity, so that seeing and the rest of the faculties are no longer perfect and calm — this loss is exactly what "turbidity" refers to as its substance. Master Jiaoguang observes: although the outer five great elements and the inner four elements alike serve as this turbid substance, it is the inner four elements above all that press most urgently upon birth-and-death and most thoroughly obstruct nirvāṇa — hence this passage speaks mostly of the four elements within the body itself. First, the four elements within the body are explained: within your own present body, whatever is solid — flesh, sinew, and bone — is the element earth; whatever is moist — bodily fluids, essence, and blood — is the element water; whatever is warm — heat and temperature — is the element fire; and whatever moves — the breath and its circulation — is the element wind.

"Bound by these four": the four elements, combined together as a temporary aggregate, bind and entangle one another, weaving themselves into a body. Once there is the appearance of a body, the six sense-faculties falsely come to be, dividing what is one single, calm, perfectly pervading, wondrous, aware, bright true mind — unborn, unceasing — so that it combines with arising-and-ceasing and becomes the ālaya-consciousness. This consciousness's own essential, original brightness, reflected at the threshold of the six sense-faculties, becomes seeing — that is, the eye-faculty's own essence of seeing; becomes hearing — the ear-faculty's own essence of hearing; becomes sensing — the nose-faculty's essence of smelling, the tongue-faculty's essence of tasting, and the body-faculty's essence of feeling; and becomes discerning — the mind-faculty's own nature of discernment, distinguished from mental consciousness's own separate work of discrimination and understanding. This is exactly form and mind combining together to become this turbid substance — as a later passage in the sūtra puts it: "originally relying on this one, essential brightness, it divides itself into six combined faculties." "From beginning to end" means from the aggregate of consciousness at the start to the aggregate of form at the end, since arising proceeds from consciousness outward — and these five layers, muddled together and unclear, form the appearance of turbidity; this closes by establishing their number and their name.

"Why is this called turbidity? Ānanda, it is like clear water: pure and clean in its own nature — while dust, ash, and sand belong to a different kind of thing altogether, their own substance obstructing and clouding whatever they touch; the two substances simply are as they are, their natures never following after one another. But suppose someone were to take that dust and throw it into the clear water: the dust loses its own power to obstruct, the water loses its own clarity, and the whole appearance grows murky and disturbed — this is what is called turbidity. Your own five layers of turbidity are exactly the same." This analogy shows the appearance of the five turbidities as a whole. The first sentence poses the question; what follows makes it plain through the analogy. The first two lines about clear water stand for the pure, undefiled mind, clean and clear by its own nature; the dust, ash, and sand stand for the four elements. "A different kind of thing" — their own substance obstructing and clouding whatever they touch — means that the substance of the four elements is itself something that clogs and obstructs, capable of obscuring the true nature. "The two substances" are the substance of clear water and the substance of the dust; "simply are as they are" means this is how they are from the start — one pure and clean, the other obstructing and clouding — their natures never following after one another, standing for how the pure, undefiled mind and the four elements, one true and one false, differ utterly in their own natures. "Someone" stands for a sentient being caught in delusion; taking the dust and throwing it into the water stands for falseness arising and disturbing what is true, so that truth and falseness combine, form and mind interweave — comparable to how the dust loses its power to obstruct and the water loses its clarity, truth and falseness no longer distinguishable, hence "murky and disturbed," a chaotic, confused appearance called turbidity. The water of your own mind, Ānanda, has exactly these same five layers of turbidity.

"Ānanda, you see empty space pervading the ten directions, indistinguishable from seeing itself: space has no substance, seeing has no independent awareness of its own, and the two weave falsely together — this is the first layer, called the turbidity of the aeon." What follows in the next five sections shows each of the five turbidities by name in turn, each name formed by the combination of its own substance and appearance. This first shows the turbidity of the aeon, which depends on the aggregate of form — both the four elements within the body and the five great elements without belong to the aggregate of form, while the nature of the six essences within belongs to mind as such. Whenever turbidity is spoken of, it is because the water of the mind, originally calm, becomes turbid once the various elements are thrown into it, exactly as the analogy above makes clear; this particular turbidity of the aeon depends on the aggregate of form, formed by the weaving-together of the outer five elements' own space-element with the six essences' own essence of seeing. The first line, "you see empty space," raises space to stand in also for earth and the other three elements, and raises seeing to stand in also for hearing and the other four essences; space and seeing alone are singled out because both, alike, pervade the ten directions, making the false weaving-together between them easiest to see. "Indistinguishable from seeing itself": pervading together at once, with no way to mark out where the boundary of seeing ends and the boundary of space begins — these three lines identify the substance of the turbidity of the aeon. "Space has no substance, seeing has no independent awareness of its own": if there were only space and no seeing, space would have no substance that could ever be found — for without seeing, who could ever make space's own substance known? And if there were only seeing and no space, seeing would have nothing to be aware of at all — for without any object, the faculty itself could never be made manifest. "The two weave falsely together": seeing and space interweave, like warp and weft, densely woven, indistinguishable. As it is with seeing and space, so it is with seeing and form; as it is with the eye-faculty, so it is with every other faculty — faculty and object confront one another, clouding the true nature and obscuring its own wondrous brightness, and so the appearance of the turbidity of the aeon takes shape.

"Your own body, formed by combining the four elements, obstructs seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing, so that water, fire, wind, and earth are made to circulate as if aware — this weaving-together falsely produced is the second layer, called the turbidity of views." Next, the turbidity of views is shown. This turbidity depends on the aggregate of sensation, formed by the weaving-together of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing with the four elements within, the six faculties of sensory reception taking in their various objects. "Your body, formed by combining the four elements" — these two lines identify the substance of the turbidity of views, meaning that your own present body has taken the four elements, combined as a temporary aggregate, as its own substance. Once there is the appearance of a body, the six sense-faculties follow, and so a single essential brightness divides itself into the six essences of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing. Originally a single essential brightness, once obstructed and partitioned by the four elements of earth, water, fire, and wind, it becomes the six sense-faculties, divided into six combined functions — what originally had no obstruction now becomes obstructed! The eye can only see, and so on up through the mind, which can only know; while water, fire, wind, and earth, originally things without awareness at all, are made to circulate as though aware — "made to circulate" meaning turned about, revolved by the very nature of these six essences, so that what was without awareness becomes, as it were, endowed with awareness. "This weaving-together falsely produced": awareness and its absence weave together, like warp and weft, densely woven, indistinguishable, disturbing the true nature and falsely producing the appearance of the turbidity of views — comprising, in all, the sixty-two views, with the view of a self foremost among them, sensitive to pain at even the prick of a needle's point.

"Further, in your mind, memory, discrimination, and habituated learning give rise to knowledge and views, manifesting the six sense-objects; apart from these objects there is no appearance, and apart from this awareness there is no nature to be found — this falsely woven result is the third layer, called the turbidity of affliction." Third, the turbidity of affliction is shown. This turbidity depends on the aggregate of perception: with the six faculties already established in the previous section, and now confronting the six sense-objects, the six kinds of perception naturally form — that is, the sixth consciousness imagining the objects of the six sense-fields — hence "further, in your mind, memory, discrimination, and habituated learning." This means that, within the sixth consciousness's own false imagining, one recalls past objects once taken hold of, fixing them firmly in memory without forgetting; one discriminates present objects now being taken hold of, clinging to them with attachment; and one rehearses future objects not yet encountered, planning for them in advance — these two lines identify the substance of the turbidity of affliction. "Giving rise to knowledge and views, manifesting the six sense-objects": "nature" here means the very nature of the sixth consciousness that does the imagining, which, relying on the six faculties, gives rise to the six kinds of false imagining — seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, sensing, and knowing. The first five occur together with the corresponding sense-consciousness arising at the same moment; "knowing" alone is the independently operating mental consciousness. "Knowledge and views" names two to stand for all six. "Manifesting" refers to the appearance of the six sense-objects as imagined, the objects of the six sense-fields now present before it. "Apart from these objects there is no appearance, and apart from this awareness there is no nature to be found": if the six consciousnesses were separated from the six sense-objects, then the objects taken as conditions would vanish and the consciousness taking them as conditions would dissolve, with no appearance of consciousness to be found at all; and if the six sense-objects were separated from the six consciousnesses' own false awareness, then what takes hold would never arise and what is taken hold of would likewise be empty, with no nature of an object to be found at all. "This falsely woven result": false awareness and false objects weave together, like warp and weft, densely woven, indistinguishable, and so the endless conditioning of objects and grasping after thoughts, craved and clung to without rest, brings about the appearance of the turbidity of affliction.

"Further, your own arising and ceasing, morning and night, never stops; your own knowledge and views ever wish to remain fixed in the world, while your own karmic momentum ever shifts you onward from one land to the next — this falsely woven result is the fourth layer, called the turbidity of beings." Fourth, the turbidity of beings is shown. This turbidity depends on the aggregate of formations: with the previous three sections already establishing a world, and now a body and mind as well, once world, body, and mind are all in place, arising and ceasing naturally follow. The seventh consciousness is the very root of this arising and ceasing, flowing on moment after moment to form the aggregate of formations — hence "further, within your own manas, from morning to night, false thoughts follow one after another without a moment's rest," arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing in endless succession, and so the world shifts onward and body and mind continue, giving rise to boundless birth and death. "Your own knowledge and views ever wish to remain fixed in the world": since no ordinary being fails to crave life and fear death, clinging to the view and knowledge of a self, one ever wishes to remain fixed in the world forever — having reached a hundred years, still wishing for a hundred and twenty — this speaks in terms of the mind. Yet, powerless against it, the aggregate of formations secretly shifts on, karmic momentum ever pressing forward, with no freedom of one's own, casting off one life to take up the next, shifting from one land to another — this speaks in terms of the body. "This falsely woven result": the false body and false mind, ever shifting yet ever wishing to remain, weave together, like warp and weft, densely woven, indistinguishable, disturbing the true nature and falsely producing the appearance of the turbidity of beings.

"Your seeing and hearing have, originally, no differing nature at all, yet the various sense-objects divide them apart, producing difference where none belongs. In their own nature they remain mutually aware of one another; in their own function they stand opposed to one another; sameness and difference lose all fixed measure — this falsely woven result is the fifth layer, called the turbidity of life-span." Fifth, the turbidity of life-span is shown. This turbidity depends on the aggregate of consciousness, pointing to the eighth consciousness in particular — the seventh consciousness belongs to the aggregate of formations, the sixth to the aggregate of perception. The eighth consciousness, within a sentient being, is the very master that departs last and arrives first; and one's life-span bears a direct, connected relation to this eighth consciousness — so long as it has not yet left the body, one's life is not yet exhausted, and the very moment it departs the body, one's life-span comes to its end; hence the turbidity of life-span depends on the aggregate of consciousness. "Your own seeing and hearing have, originally, no differing nature at all": your own seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing were originally one single substance, with no differing nature between them at all. "The various sense-objects divide them apart": the various sense-objects — brightness and darkness, motion and stillness, and the rest of the six — are taken hold of, forming the sense-faculties, each opening its own separate gateway, and thereby dividing what was one single substance into six essences, producing difference where none belongs. "In their own nature they remain mutually aware of one another; in their own function they stand opposed to one another": considered in terms of their own nature, these six functions remain, at root, one single substance, all belonging to a single household, their awareness communicating freely with one another — the same, not different; but considered in terms of their own function, this one substance, having become six separate functions, cannot help but stand, in some sense, opposed to one another — different, not the same. "Sameness and difference lose all fixed measure, and this falsely woven result": "measure" means a fixed standard — sameness is not fixedly the same, difference is not fixedly different, hence "sameness and difference lose all fixed measure." This single sameness and this single difference weave together, like warp and weft, densely woven, indistinguishable, disturbing the true nature and falsely producing the appearance of the turbidity of life-span.

Master Jiaoguang observes: taking the whole of the above together, the wondrously aware, bright mind is nothing but a single, calm, perfect wholeness, with not even an inside or an outside to it at all — how, then, could there ever be any turbidity in it whatsoever? It is only because it obscures itself into what appears as empty space, and, within this darkened obscurity, congeals this very darkness into form, that, from without, it becomes muddled by the five great elements of the material world, and so the turbidity of the aeon results; drawing further inward, it becomes muddled by the four elements forming a bodily appearance, and so the turbidity of views results; drawing further inward still, it becomes muddled by the shadowy traces of the six sense-objects as conditions, and so the turbidity of affliction results, from which body and mind, broken and yet continuing, shift onward from land to land; and it becomes further muddled by birth and death themselves, and so the turbidity of beings results — taking these four appearances together, both within and without alike become one single, muddled turbidity, entirely losing the very meaning of "calm." And from this, further still, the various sense-objects knot and congeal, so that the six sense-faculties can no longer freely interpenetrate one another, and so the turbidity of life-span results — taking this one appearance alone, the very meaning of "perfect wholeness" is entirely lost as well. Hence, wishing to restore this original calm, perfect wholeness, one must seek the method for clarifying this turbidity — and this is exactly what the passage that follows goes on to teach.

Sūtra: "Ānanda, you now wish for your own seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing to accord fully, at last, with the Tathāgata's own constancy, bliss, true self, and purity. You must first choose the very root of birth and death, and, relying instead on what is unborn and unceasing, bring the perfectly calm nature to completion."

Commentary: The section above made plain that, though sentient beings are entirely furnished with the five turbidities, they still possess, at root, this same calm, perfect nature, its four inherent virtues merely hidden and unmanifest; this section now teaches the method for clarifying this turbidity and restoring that clarity. This method requires, first, distinguishing the true causal mind from the false, taking up the true and setting aside the false as the very starting point of practice — only then can the turbidity be clarified and the calm, perfect nature be restored. Hence the Buddha tells Ānanda: the very nature of seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing you now possess within your own sense-faculties is itself none other than the mind of original enlightenment; and this is, at root, one and the same substance — with no duality, no distinction whatsoever — as the virtues of constancy, bliss, true self, and purity realized by all the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, which is itself none other than final, complete awakening. It is, from the start, calm and pure; it is, from the start, perfectly complete and all-pervading — and it is only because of the five turbidities that these four virtues are lost. Matched one against the other: because of the turbidity of beings, one flows on through birth and death, and so loses true constancy, becoming instead impermanence; because of the turbidity of affliction, bound and entangled by the root afflictions and their followers, one loses true bliss, becoming instead suffering; because of the turbidity of views and the turbidity of life-span, faculty and consciousness combined together, one loses the true self, becoming instead a false self; and because of the turbidity of the aeon, worldly toil and defilement, one loses true purity, becoming instead impurity. This is only speaking in terms of their particular correspondences; considered more generally, every one of the five turbidities causes the loss of all four virtues at once — though this "loss" is never a true loss, exactly as muddy water loses its own clarity, and yet, examined closely, the clear water remains present within the very mud itself. Though the four virtues are, in this sense, never truly lost, the five turbidities remain, even now, unremoved — and so one remains, by one's own reckoning, worlds apart from the Buddha's own realized virtue. You now wish for the four natures bound up within the five turbidities to accord fully with the Tathāgata's own four virtues — this cannot be accomplished without the actual work of clarifying this turbidity! And this resolve to clarify the turbidity cannot itself bear fruit without first choosing the true, causal-stage mind — hence the need to decisively choose between them, taking up the one and setting aside the other.

This is exactly choosing decisively between the two roots, true and false. If this choice is not made clearly, and taking-up and setting-aside are reversed, then the five turbidities can never be clarified, and nirvāṇa can never be realized by any method at all — hence the Buddha specifically instructs Ānanda that he must choose the true, causal-stage mind. "The very root of birth and death" is exactly the grasping, conditioned mind of the sixth consciousness — in the Buddha's earlier distinction between the two roots, this was named the false root. Ordinary beings, those outside the teaching, and those of the provisional and lesser vehicles alike, failing to realize that this mind is not the true cause at all, take it up regardless and cultivate in confused disorder, and so can never attain unsurpassed bodhi — this is exactly why the Buddha so thoroughly refuted this false mind, three times over: because it is a cause of arising and ceasing, it cannot accord with the fruit-virtue of nirvāṇa, and so must be decisively set aside and abandoned first of all. "Relying instead on what is unborn and unceasing, bring the perfectly calm nature to completion": this is exactly the unborn, unceasing, perfectly complete, all-pervading, calmly abiding buddha-nature of original enlightenment already possessed within the sense-faculties — in the Buddha's earlier distinction between the two roots, this was named the true root, the very originally pure substance of bodhi and nirvāṇa itself — hence the Buddha's tenfold, thorough revealing of its truth. Near at hand, it is fully present within every sense-faculty; far-reaching, it encompasses the ten thousand phenomena entirely — yet ordinary beings, those outside the teaching, and those of the provisional and lesser vehicles alike remain entirely unaware of it, using it every single day without ever knowing it is there. This must now be decisively understood, and taken up and put to use. The first two lines here mean setting aside consciousness; the next two mean making use of the sense-faculty's own nature. Setting aside consciousness and making use of the sense-faculty's nature is the very essential point of this entire sūtra: unless the discriminating mind of consciousness is set aside, how could the great samādhi ever be cultivated at all? And unless the sense-faculty's own nature is taken up and put to use, how could nirvāṇa ever be realized? Hence, in answering Ānanda's request for the method of practice, the Buddha's teaching is exactly this: set aside consciousness, and make use of the sense-faculty's nature. The passage further on, "if you set aside what arises and ceases, and hold fast instead to what is ever-true and constant," carries this very same meaning. The word "completion" here means: relying on this unborn, unceasing, perfectly calm nature of the sense-faculty, bringing it to completion as the true, causal-stage mind — and once the cause is true, the fruit can be realized, and so one comes to perfectly accomplish cultivation and realization at the fruit-stage, which is itself the completion of the fruit-stage awakening.

Sūtra: "Every sentient being's own falseness weaves itself together in just this way: within the body, this constant exchange and flux, and space and time, mutually interpenetrate one another. And though this quality of space may be posited as having ten directions, in fact the world only clearly names four — east, west, south, and north; above and below have no fixed position of their own, and the intermediate directions have no single, determinate direction either. Commentary: Every sentient being's own body is woven together, falsely, out of the interweaving of the four elements and the six essences. A sentient being's own bodily faculties: the body itself is a temporary aggregate of the four elements, and the four elements belong to insentient matter — it is only because of the presence of the essences of seeing, hearing, and the rest, turning what is without awareness into what has awareness, that it comes to be called a sentient being's world at all. Once there are bodily faculties, there is a world; and within these bodily faculties, this constant exchange and flux proceeds exactly like a merchant trading his goods, in constant flux without pause. The quality of space here is this very flux, unfixed — as when front becomes back, or left becomes right; the quality of time is this same flowing onward without pause — as when present becomes past, or future becomes present. "Space and time mutually interpenetrate one another": time interpenetrating space, and space interpenetrating time, each entering into the other in turn — this is the very foundation for the tally of merit built up, fold upon fold, in what follows.

This first settles the true nature of "space." Though this quality of space may be posited as having directions, and called "the ten directions" — note the word "may be posited": these ten are not fixedly ten at all. Examined for what can actually, firmly be named as a fixed direction, the world names only the four directions, east, west, south, and north. The next two lines, "above and below have no fixed position of their own, and the intermediate directions have no single, determinate direction," explain why only these four are properly named: "above" and "below" are simply the above and below belonging to these same four directions — apart from the four directions, there is no separate "above" or "below" at all, hence "no fixed position of their own." "The intermediate directions have no single, determinate direction" means that the intermediate points, where two of the four directions meet — each corner formed by the combination of two adjoining directions — likewise have no single, fixed direction of their own either.

The next lines take up only the count of the four directions — east, west, south, and north — as what can be firmly, definitely named, and show how this interpenetrates with the three periods of past, present, and future. "Three multiplied by four, or four multiplied by three": taking the three periods as interpenetrating the four directions, three times four makes twelve; taking the four directions as interpenetrating the three periods, four times three likewise makes twelve — hence "alike unfold into twelve," turning it now this way, now that, however one turns it, it always comes to twelve. This is the first folding, and the basis for the two foldings that follow.

"Flowing onward" means flowing from this root outward to its branches, from the first folding onward to the third; "multiplying" means growing from a smaller number into a larger one, from twelve multiplying up to twelve hundred. The phrase "ten, a hundred, a thousand" simply means ten, as people commonly say "one ten," "one hundred," "one thousand." The three foldings are as follows: the first folding, above, takes space as interpenetrating time — each of the three periods possessing all four directions, three times four becoming twelve; the second folding takes time as interpenetrating space — each of the four directions possessing all ten periods in turn, becoming a hundred and twenty; the third folding has each of these ten periods, in turn, possessing all ten directions, becoming twelve hundred. The text speaks only of "ten, a hundred, a thousand" because it gives only the round numbers, omitting the remainder. This takes "three times four" as the first folding; taking "four times three" as the first folding instead follows exactly the same pattern, as one can infer. Master Ouyi offers a separate explanation of the three foldings: the first folding is three times four, or four times three, making twelve; the second folding takes the three periods within the four directions, each period possessing all ten dharma-realms in turn, so that twelve becomes a hundred and twenty; the third folding has each of these dharma-realms, in turn, possessing all ten realms as well, so that a hundred and twenty becomes twelve hundred. This is exactly why the passage below, concerning the mind-faculty, says that it silently takes in every worldly and transcendent phenomenon throughout the ten directions and the three periods of time.

"Taken altogether, from beginning to end": the first folding is the beginning, the third folding is the end; "taken altogether" means gathering together everything from beginning to end — that each and every one of the six sense-faculties possesses, in its own functional merit, twelve hundred units in all. This means that each of the six kinds of sense-faculty a sentient being possesses pervades body and mind alike; and since body and mind themselves are formed by exactly this interpenetration of space and time, already resolved into twelve hundred parts, each of the six kinds of faculty likewise comes, in turn, to possess this same twelve hundred units of functional merit.

The Zhǐzhǎng commentary raises a question: is this merit of the sense-faculties' own nature the same as, or different from, the merit of the six sense-faculties spoken of in the Lotus Sūtra? The answer: this passage speaks in terms of what is inherent in principle — meaning what the nature already possesses of itself; that passage speaks in terms of what is brought about in fact — meaning what is manifested through the merit of reciting the sūtra. Were that merit not achieved through the power of recitation, it too would be nothing but what is inherent in principle alone; and were this merit, once the sense-faculty's own knot is resolved, to be actually realized, it too would come to equal what is brought about in fact. They are, in this sense, the same even in their difference, and different even in their sameness — one cannot simply call them the same, nor simply call them different. This bears further reflection.

Smelling with the nose: it perceives the breath going out and coming in, but lacks awareness at the very midpoint between the two — the nose is thus missing one part in three; know that the nose has only eight hundred units of merit. Speaking with the tongue: it can proclaim the whole of worldly and transcendent wisdom — its words may be bounded, but the principles it expresses are inexhaustible — know that the tongue-faculty is fully complete, with twelve hundred units of merit. Feeling with the body: it senses contact, whether agreeable or not, only when touching something, and knows nothing when separated from it — the body is thus missing one part in three; know that the body has only eight hundred units of merit. Knowing with the mind: it silently takes in every phenomenon, worldly and transcendent, throughout the ten directions and the three times, holding sage and ordinary being alike within its full scope — know that the mind-faculty is fully complete, with twelve hundred units of merit."

Commentary: The nature of the sense-faculties is, in itself, equal — every faculty's merit is likewise equal, with, at root, nothing to distinguish between them — but because sentient beings across different regions have faculties of differing strength, and this Sahā world is no exception, three of the six faculties turn out superior and three inferior: possessing the full twelve hundred units of merit makes a faculty superior, eight hundred makes it inferior. And among the three superior faculties, the ear-faculty is superior still above the other two — hence the Buddha's own question: "You should now further determine, among the six sense-faculties, which is superior and which is inferior." What follows, beginning "seeing with the eye," shows each faculty's merit as complete or lacking in turn — complete making it superior, lacking making it inferior. "Seeing with the eye": "seeing" here is exactly the eye-faculty's own merit of its nature of seeing; the body's front, back, left, and right correspond to south, north, east, and west. "Dark behind" means the north, behind, cannot be seen, hence dark; "bright in front" means the south, in front, can be seen, hence bright. It further says "fully bright to the front," meaning due south, together with the two adjoining corners southeast and southwest, can all be fully seen; and "fully dark to the rear," meaning due north, together with northeast and northwest, cannot be seen at all. "Visible to either side" means due east and due west, glancing left and right, surveying both sides, can likewise be fully seen.

"Two-thirds": this speaks of the fraction of merit involved — the front, together with its two adjoining corners, counts as one portion; the two sides count as one portion; the rear, together with its two adjoining corners, counts as one portion. Each of the four cardinal directions carries two hundred units of merit, making eight hundred in all; each of the four corners carries one hundred units, making four hundred in all — combined, twelve hundred units of merit in total. The eye-faculty attains only two of these three portions, since the rear is entirely dark, missing this one portion entirely. Taken altogether, then, the eye-faculty's own merit is incomplete — of the three portions spoken of, one portion, the north together with its two rear corners, is entirely without merit; know, then, that the eye-faculty has only eight hundred units of merit, and is inferior.

This explains the ear-faculty's own merit of its nature of hearing. Hearing with the ear takes in sound pervasively from every one of the ten directions, without any omission at all — as a later passage puts it: "were drums struck at once in all ten directions, all ten would be heard at the very same moment." The nature of hearing is perfect and complete — this is its perfect reality. Sound has two aspects, motion and stillness: where there is sound, this is called motion; where there is no sound, this is called stillness. When hearing a sound in motion, there may seem to be some difference of near and far — "seem" meaning "as if," since sound as an object is itself false, never truly real to begin with, hence "as if" there were such a difference — yet whether the sound merely seems near or far, both are heard without any obstruction at all; this is its pervasive reality. And when hearing stillness, in the utter quiet where no sound is present at all, the nature of hearing extends even further, without any boundary or limit whatsoever — motion and stillness are both heard, present at every single moment; this is its constant reality. Know, then, that the ear-faculty is fully complete, with twelve hundred units of merit — the most superior of all six.

This explains the nose-faculty's own merit of its nature of smelling. Smelling with the nose can take in both the outgoing breath and the incoming breath. Taking in the outgoing breath carries four hundred units of merit, and taking in the incoming breath likewise carries four hundred — but at the very midpoint between the two, where the breath briefly pauses, its function does not manifest at all, and so four hundred units are lacking there. Examined for its actual merit, the nose-faculty is thus missing one of its three portions; know, then, that the nose has only eight hundred units of merit, and is inferior.

This explains the nature of the tongue-faculty. Only speech is taken into account here, not the tasting of flavors — for although the tongue's nature possesses both these functions, were tasting taken into account it would prove inferior, since taste can only be known through direct contact; whereas speech, taken into account instead, proves superior, since it can proclaim worldly teachings, exhausting conventional wisdom, and can proclaim transcendent teachings as well, exhausting ultimate wisdom. "Its words may be bounded, but the principles it expresses are inexhaustible": this may mean bounded by a particular language — as when an Indian dharma-master, arriving in China without knowing the local tongue, could nonetheless expound the Buddhist principles he taught without any limit at all; or it may mean bounded merely in extent — as when a single verse can encompass boundless, subtle meaning. There is a further reading as well: "whether the words are coarse or refined, all alike return to the ultimate truth." As the Lotus Sūtra says: "Every word spoken in governing the world, every worldly trade and livelihood, accords, without exception, with true reality itself" — this is the capacity to exhaust transcendent wisdom; and, as when Confucius, hearing a child's song, was moved to reflect upon his own conduct, this is the capacity to exhaust worldly wisdom as well. Know, then, that the tongue-faculty is fully complete, with twelve hundred units of merit, and is superior.

This explains the body-faculty's own merit of its nature of sensing. When the body-faculty meets the object of touch it senses, it discerns whether this contact is agreeable or disagreeable — disagreeable as when wearing padded clothing in summer, or a single thin layer in winter; agreeable as when eating fine food while hungry, or drinking sweet nectar while thirsty. But it can only sense agreeable or disagreeable contact while actually in contact with something, and knows nothing at all once separated from it — separation counting as one portion of merit, contact counting as two portions, hence "missing one when separated, doubled when in contact." Each portion carries four hundred units of merit; examined for its actual merit, the body-faculty is thus missing one of its three portions; know, then, that the body has only eight hundred units of merit, and is inferior.

This explains the mind-faculty's own merit of its nature of knowing. "Silently takes in" is exactly this merit of its nature of knowing — knowing within, without the mouth speaking a word, is called "silent"; "takes in" means holding everything within its scope. What follows, "the ten directions and the three times," and then "every phenomenon, worldly and transcendent," together name time, place, persons, and dharmas, to display the full extent of what it takes in: in terms of place, it takes in the ten directions, which is its meaning of pervading horizontally; in terms of time, it takes in the three periods, which is its meaning of extending vertically through every age — this is one pairing, of time and place. "Every worldly phenomenon" means the defiled dharmas of the six ordinary realms; "every transcendent phenomenon" means the pure dharmas of the four sagely realms — this is a second pairing, of persons and dharmas. There is not a single dharma it fails to take in — whether persons or dharmas, in every single case it exhausts their entire scope and boundary completely. Know, then, that the mind-faculty illuminates all things perfectly, without the slightest omission, and so is fully complete, with twelve hundred units of merit, and is superior.

Eye — 800. Sees the front and both sides, but nothing behind: missing one part in three.
Ear — 1200. Hears sound from every direction, near or far, moving or still, without any gap at all.
Nose — 800. Smells the in-breath and the out-breath, but nothing at the still midpoint between them.
Tongue — 1200. Can proclaim every worldly and transcendent teaching without any limit at all.
Body — 800. Feels touch only in actual contact, and nothing at all once separated from it.
Mind — 1200. Silently takes in every phenomenon, in every direction and every age, without exception.

Reading note: This tally is not idle numerology — it is exactly the groundwork Ānanda will need in the fascicles ahead, when twenty-five disciples each testify to their own path to awakening and Ānanda must choose a single faculty to cultivate through. Three faculties reach the full twelve hundred because nothing about their own structure leaves a gap in what they can take in — sound from any direction, meaning without limit, every phenomenon whatsoever; the other three lose a third of their merit to a structural blind spot each carries built in — no rear vision, no awareness at the breath's still turning point, no sensation without contact. This closes the section fully displaying the six sense-faculties' own merit, and, with it, the second decisive meaning's own foundation: the very knot that binds is now identified precisely enough to be untied.
