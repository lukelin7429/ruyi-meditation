Fascicle 7 · 大佛頂首楞嚴經講義卷七 · Complete

No Fixed Place to Be Found

Having harmonized the five aggregates and six sense-entrances with the tathāgatagarbha in fascicle 6, Yuanying now completes the pattern: the twelve sense-fields, each a faculty paired with its object, and the eighteen elements, each pairing joined by the consciousness it gives rise to, are shown in turn to have no fixed location anywhere — not in the faculty, not in the object, not in their combination — and are therefore, every one of them, nothing other than the tathāgatagarbha.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 7 complete Source: bfnn.org / NTU Buddhist Digital Library Covers: 卯三 · 卯四 — harmonizing the twelve sense-fields and eighteen elements with the tathāgatagarbha

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 6 harmonized the first two of Yuanying's four categories — the five aggregates and the six sense-entrances — with the tathāgatagarbha. Fascicle 7 completes the pattern with the remaining two: the twelve sense-fields (十二處, each of the six sense-faculties paired with its own object) and the eighteen elements (十八界, each of those twelve pairings joined by the consciousness it gives rise to). The method is now familiar. For the sense-fields, the Buddha tests each faculty-object pair by asking which one produces the other, or whether either one travels toward the other. For the elements, he asks after the consciousness that supposedly arises between them, refuting the faculty, the object, and their combination in turn. Every single case ends the same way: no fixed location (處) can be found for it anywhere, and it is therefore, at root, the tathāgatagarbha.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. The core sūtra text for both categories has already been translated in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 3 ("Every Category, the Same Discovery") — that exact English is reused here wherever Yuanying quotes it. Two short framing questions that open each category ("how is it that the twelve sense-fields are, at root, the tathāgatagarbha..." and the same for the eighteen elements) were not separately quoted in that earlier translation and appear here in fresh English for the first time. Yuanying's own commentary, and the more granular clause-by-clause quotations his outline requires, are translated fresh throughout.

The Twelve Sense-Fields Are the Tathāgatagarbha 卯三 會十二處即藏性

Chinese Source

〖經〗復次，阿難！云何十二處，本如來藏，妙真如性？

〖講〗此總標十二處即藏性。處者方所也，定在也。以權教相宗說，根一定在內，塵一定在外，眼唯對色，耳唯對聲等，內外各六處，故名十二處，亦名十二入；謂取境，則以根入塵，受境，則以塵入根，通為能入，亦通為所入，故名為入。六科中，前二眼、色、耳、聲四處，約根塵互破，身、觸二處，獨約根破，餘六處皆約塵破，循循善誘，巧為開發，一一顯其相妄性真，全事即理也。

本科十二處，為如來對迷色重迷心輕者，開色合心：開一色法，為六塵、五根，合受、想、行、識四陰心法為一意根，故說十二處。根、塵互相依倚，下文云：根塵同源，縛脫無二，迷晦即無明，發明便解脫。今為融歸藏性者，正欲今發明也。初總徵竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Further, Ānanda: how is it that the twelve sense-fields are, at root, the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness?"

Commentary: This is the general statement that the twelve sense-fields are the tathāgatagarbha. "Field" (處) means a fixed place, a determinate location. In the provisional teaching of the Dharma-characteristics school, the sense-faculty is said to reside fixedly within and its object fixedly without — the eye faces only form, the ear only sound, and so on — six locations within and six without, making twelve fields, also called the twelve entrances: taking hold of an object, the faculty "enters" the object; receiving an object, the object "enters" the faculty — so it is called, indifferently, both the entrance that enters and the entrance entered into, hence "entrance." Among these six categories, the first two — eye and form, ear and sound, four locations in all — are refuted by way of the faculty and its object mutually depending on each other; body and touch, the two locations there, are refuted solely by way of the faculty; the remaining six locations are all refuted by way of the object alone — leading the listener along step by step, skillfully drawing out the point, so that each and every one of them is shown, in turn, to be false in appearance while true in nature: phenomenon, in its entirety, being exactly principle.

This section on the twelve fields is spoken by the Tathāgata for those whose delusion about matter runs deep while their delusion about mind runs shallow: it expands matter while condensing mind — expanding the single category of matter into the six sense-objects and five sense-faculties, and condensing the four mental aggregates of sensation, perception, formations, and consciousness into the single mental faculty — hence "twelve fields." Faculty and object depend on each other; a later passage says: "faculty and object share a single source; bondage and liberation are not two; confusion is exactly ignorance, and understanding is exactly liberation." What is now being drawn back and harmonized into the tathāgatagarbha nature is meant precisely to bring about this same understanding. The general question is complete.

Seeing and Form 眼、色二處

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難！汝且觀此祗陀樹林及諸泉池，於意云何？此等為是色生眼見？眼生色相？阿難！若復眼根生色相者，見空非色，色性應銷，銷則顯發一切都無；色相既無，誰明空質？空亦如是。若復色塵生眼見者，觀空非色，見即銷亡，亡則都無，誰明空、色？是故當知：見與色、空，俱無處所。即色與見，二處虛妄，本非因緣，非自然性。

〖講〗此標舉眼、色二處，觀即眼處，餘為塵處。此約權宗，心、法相生為問。由法生故，種種心生；由心生故，種種法生。故徵云：在汝之意以為如何？還是色塵生出眼見耶？還是眼根生出色相耶？雙開兩途為問，下乃分破。

此破眼生色相。故呼阿難，而告之曰：若復內之眼根，生出外之色相者，則眼具生色之性，設或見空之時，而非色相，是所生之色相既無，而能生之色性指眼見，應即銷亡！銷則顯發一切都無者：銷字即能生之見銷亡，而所生之色亦無，色相既無，空相亦復叵得？故曰顯發一切都無。色相既無，誰明顯也空質者：言空、色相形而顯，色相既無，空應不顯矣。反言無色，將誰顯空？此二句，即解釋顯發一切都無之義。空亦如是者：例破眼生空相，空、色俱為眼根所對之境，故須例破云：若復眼根生空相者，見色非空之時，空性應銷，銷則顯發一切都無，空相既無，誰明色質？為避文繁，故總例之曰：空亦如是。

此破色生眼見。若復外之色塵，能生內之眼見者，觀空之時，而非色相，是能生見之色塵已滅，而所生之見，即應銷亡！然見既銷亡，則一切都無；既無能見，則將誰來明是空是色，故曰：誰明空色？則見與色、空，一總都無矣。準上亦應有空亦如是句。

此科結云：由是義故，應當起智觀察，了知能見之眼根，與所見之色、空，俱無定在之處所。以眼不生色，則色無處所；色不生眼，則見無處所，此且明其無內、外二處耳。即色與見，二處虛妄者：即色塵與眼見，二處之體，亦屬虛妄，以無體曰虛，偽現曰妄。前文云：「見與見緣，如虛空華，本無所有。」本非因緣，非自然性者：亦如前文云：其性真為妙覺明體，本非權乘所計之因緣性，及外道所計之自然性；以因緣、自然，俱為戲論矣。初眼色處竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, look now at this Jeta Grove with its trees and springs and pools — what do you think? Is it that the form gives rise to the eye's seeing, or that the eye gives rise to the appearance of form? Ānanda, if the eye-faculty gives rise to the appearance of form, then, seeing empty space, which is not form, this capacity for form ought to vanish — and once it vanishes, everything manifest becomes nothing at all; with the appearance of form gone, what is left to reveal the substance of emptiness? And so it is with empty space too. And if the object of form gives rise to the eye's seeing, then, observing emptiness, which is not form, this seeing itself ought to vanish — and once it vanishes, everything becomes nothing at all; what, then, is left to reveal either emptiness or form? You should therefore know that seeing and form, and empty space besides, have no location at all — the two fields of form and seeing are false, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

Commentary: This sets out the two fields of eye and form: "observe" points to the field of the eye; everything else observed points to the field of form. The question is posed in the terms of the provisional teaching, where mind and phenomena are said to give rise to each other: because of phenomena, all manner of mental states arise; because of mind, all manner of phenomena arise. Hence the question: what do you think — does the object of form give rise to the eye's seeing, or does the eye-faculty give rise to the appearance of form? Both possibilities are opened together as the question; what follows refutes them one at a time.

This refutes the claim that the eye gives rise to the appearance of form. Addressing Ānanda directly, the Buddha says: if the eye-faculty within gives rise to the form without, then the eye possesses, as its own nature, the capacity to produce form — and so, at the moment it sees empty space, which is not form at all, this very capacity to produce form ought to vanish along with it. "Everything manifest becomes nothing at all" means: the seeing capable of producing form vanishes, and the form it would have produced vanishes with it — and if the appearance of form is gone, how could empty space itself, its counterpart, still be shown to exist? Hence, "everything manifest becomes nothing at all." "With form gone, what is left to reveal the substance of emptiness" means: emptiness and form show themselves only in contrast to each other; with form gone, emptiness too ought to have nothing left to reveal it. Turned the other way: with no form at all, what is left to reveal emptiness? These two lines simply explain what "everything manifest becomes nothing at all" means. "And so it is with empty space too" extends the same refutation to the claim that the eye gives rise to the appearance of emptiness — since emptiness and form are equally objects the eye faces, the same refutation must apply to both: if the eye-faculty gives rise to the appearance of emptiness, then, at the moment it sees form, which is not emptiness, this capacity for emptiness ought to vanish, and everything manifest becomes nothing at all — with emptiness gone, what is left to reveal the substance of form? To avoid needless repetition, the text simply extends the point: "and so it is with empty space too."

This refutes the claim that form gives rise to the eye's seeing. If the object of form without gives rise to the seeing within, then, at the moment of observing empty space, which is not the appearance of form, this same object of form capable of producing seeing has already ceased — and so the seeing it produced ought to vanish along with it. But once seeing itself has vanished, everything becomes nothing at all; with no capacity to see remaining, who is left to declare this emptiness, that form? Hence: "what is left to reveal either emptiness or form?" Seeing, form, and emptiness are, every one, reduced to nothing whatsoever. By the same pattern as before, one should also supply here: "and so it is with empty space too."

This concludes: for this reason, one should give rise to wisdom and observe directly, coming to understand that the eye-faculty which sees, and the form and emptiness which are seen, have, between them, no fixed location at all. Since the eye does not give rise to form, form has no location; since form does not give rise to the eye, seeing has no location — this shows only that there is no location either within or without. "The two fields of form and seeing are false": the very substance of these two fields, form and seeing, is likewise false — having no substance is called "empty" [虛]; falsely appearing is called "illusory" [妄]. An earlier passage already said: "seeing, and what conditions seeing, are like flowers conjured in space — originally there is nothing there at all." "Originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature" likewise echoes an earlier passage: their true nature is exactly the substance of wondrous, enlightened brightness — never a matter of the causes-and-conditions the provisional vehicle imagines, nor of the spontaneity the non-Buddhist schools imagine; cause-and-condition and spontaneity alike are nothing but empty argument. The field of eye and form is complete.

Hearing and Sound, Smelling and Fragrance, Tasting and Flavor, Touching and Object, Mind and Mental Phenomena 耳聲 · 鼻香 · 舌味 · 身觸 · 意法

Yuanying applies the identical pattern — standing forth the pair, posing the question both ways, refuting each source in turn, concluding that both are false in appearance and true in nature — to the remaining five sense-fields. The sūtra's own demonstrations for these five have already been translated in the bare-sūtra project's fascicle 3.

Hearing and Sound 耳、聲二處 — Refuted by the same three-way test (sound coming to the ear, the ear going to the sound, or neither), with a memorable added move: a listener objects that if neither coming nor going explains it, how can hearing happen at all? Yuanying answers by appeal to modern technology, citing wireless radio broadcasting as proof that a nature can be all-pervading and "displayed through a condition without being produced by it" — a foreign broadcast is heard the instant it is tuned in, exactly as if speaker and listener shared one room, with no coming or going involved, since the hearing-nature, like the sound-nature, is already pervasive everywhere, needing only a condition to reveal it, never a condition to produce it.
Smelling and Fragrance 鼻、香二處 — Tested against the sūtra's image of a single grain of rare incense scenting the whole of Śrāvastī, forty li around, all at once. Yuanying glosses this incense as "ox-head sandalwood," citing the Lotus Sūtra's own valuation of a mere six zhū of it as worth an entire world-system, and adds a historical case from the Bówùzhì: Emperor Wu of Han once received four sticks of a foreign incense so rare that the court's standing weight-limit on tribute incense had to be set aside for it; a piece no larger than a bean, hung above the palace gate, scented the whole of Chang'an for forty li for a full month, and, years later, during a plague, burning a single stick of the same incense was said to cure the entire afflicted population. The refutation itself rules out the nose (flesh is not sandalwood, and could not contain its scent), empty space (constant and everywhere, it would make fragrance permanent and universal rather than dependent on burning anything at all), and the wood itself (smoke drifting straight upward could not reach forty li in every direction the instant the incense was lit).
Tasting and Flavor 舌、味二處 — Refuted across tongue, food, and empty space, with a vivid conclusion to the space-alternative: if flavor arose from space itself, and space were salty, then everyone alive would be steeped in salt like fish in the sea, permanently unable to taste even plainness, since salt and bland are known only by contrast to each other. Yuanying closes by naming the four alternatives this whole refutation has now worked through — arising from the tongue itself, from something else (food), from no cause at all (space), or jointly — mapping the classical fourfold negation of arising explicitly onto the sūtra's own argument.
Touching and Object 身、觸二處 — Uniquely tested through a single body touching itself (a hand rubbing the head each morning) rather than two separate objects, since touch requires one thing capable of knowing and one thing merely known, joined together. Yuanying notes the monastic precept behind the sūtra's example: the Buddha instructed disciples to touch their own head three times daily while silently reciting a verse — "guard the mouth, gather the mind, let the body commit no offense; trouble no sentient being at all; leave behind every fruitless austerity; one who practises so crosses over the world" — a discipline meant to keep a monk, especially one converted from a non-Buddhist order, mindful of his own shaved head and chosen path. The refutation runs through both hand-versus-head (if only the hand is capable of touching, the head — which also plainly feels the touch — would have to be entirely without awareness, and vice versa) and one-body-versus-two (if hand and head share one nature, no separation remains for touch to occur across at all; if they are two, then which one is the true site of touching — the toucher or the touched? Space itself cannot serve either role).
Mind and Mental Phenomena 意、法二處 — The mental object here is not a real, external sense-object like the other four, but what Yuanying identifies technically as an "isolated shadow-object" [獨影境]: a residue cast off by the five real sense-objects and taken in by mind-consciousness alone, sorted into wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral "shadows." The refutation proceeds by asking whether this mental object is identical to mind or separate from it, and, if separate, whether it is itself something that knows or something inert — every branch ending the same way: no fixed location can be found for it, either within mind or apart from mind.

Reading note: This category combines two of the three refutation strategies named in fascicle 6's closing note. Eye-form and ear-sound are refuted by way of the faculty and object mutually depending on each other (更互破); body-touch is refuted solely by way of the faculty, a variant of refuting by the condition (約緣破); and the remaining three — smell, taste, and mind — are refuted solely by way of the object alone. As fascicle 6 anticipated, the twelve sense-fields draw on a combination of methods rather than any single one throughout.

The Eighteen Elements Are the Tathāgatagarbha 卯四 會十八界即藏性

Chinese Source

〖經〗復次，阿難！云何十八界，本如來藏，妙真如性？

〖講〗此總徵十八界即藏性。梵語馱都，此云界。界者界限，六根、六塵、六識，各有界限。以內之能緣者，屬根之界限；外之所緣者，屬塵之界限；中間能了別者，屬識之界限，三六十八，各有界限故。又界者因義，因即是依，以根、塵、識，互相依也。又界者種族義，以根、塵、識三，各有種子族類故。

十八界，佛為心、色二迷俱重者說，心色俱開：色法開為六塵、五根；心法開為意根、及六識，合成十八。此中破意，乃三法從要破、根、塵、識三，識為其要，故獨約識破。識界既破，則根、塵二界，自不成立，如三間之屋，但拆去中間牆壁，左右二間之界，亦自不成矣！雖則獨約識破，實則根、塵兼破，但破其相妄，妄相既破，真性自顯，故曰：「本如來藏，妙真如性。」初總徵竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Further, Ānanda: how is it that the eighteen elements are, at root, the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness?"

Commentary: This is the general question, asking how the eighteen elements are the tathāgatagarbha. The Sanskrit dhātu is rendered "element." "Element" means a boundary: the six sense-faculties, six sense-objects, and six consciousnesses each have their own boundary — the inward capacity that takes hold of an object belongs to the faculty's boundary; the outward object taken hold of belongs to the object's boundary; and the discernment arising in between belongs to consciousness's boundary — three sets of six, eighteen in all, each with its own boundary. "Element" also carries the sense of "cause," cause meaning "dependence," since faculty, object, and consciousness all depend on one another. And "element" also carries the sense of "lineage," since faculty, object, and consciousness each have their own seed and their own family line.

The eighteen elements are taught by the Buddha for those whose delusion runs equally deep about both mind and matter: both mind and matter are expanded here — matter expanded into the six sense-objects and five sense-faculties; mind expanded into the mental faculty and the six consciousnesses — eighteen in all together. The refutation here works by way of the essential component among the three, faculty, object, and consciousness — consciousness being the essential one, so the refutation is directed at consciousness alone. Once the element of consciousness is refuted, the elements of faculty and object no longer stand on their own — like a house of three rooms: tear down only the middle wall, and the boundaries of the two side rooms on either side no longer hold either. Though the refutation is directed at consciousness alone, faculty and object are, in effect, refuted along with it — only their false appearance is refuted; once that false appearance is refuted, the true nature shows itself of its own accord, hence: "at root, the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness." The general question is complete.

Eye-Consciousness 眼識界

Chinese Source

〖經〗阿難！如汝所明，眼色為緣，生於眼識。此識，為復因眼所生，以眼為界？因色所生，以色為界？阿難！若因眼生，既無色空，無可分別；縱有汝識，欲將何用？汝見又非青、黃、赤、白，無所表示，從何立界？若因色生，空無色時，汝識應滅？云何識知，是虛空性？若色變時，汝亦識其色相遷變，汝識不遷，界從何立？從變則變，界相自無；不變則恆，既從色生，應不識知虛空所在？若兼二種，眼、色共生，合則中離，離則兩合，體性雜亂，云何成界？是故當知：眼色為緣，生眼識界，三處都無。則眼與色，及色界三，本非因緣，非自然性。

〖講〗此佛就阿難，昔聞因緣權教，所明者為問。眼根與色塵，根、塵相對，互相為緣故；根為增上緣，塵為所緣緣，二緣相合，識生其中，故曰：「生於眼識」，而成三界矣。根、塵、識三，惟徵於識，即三法從要破也；識既被破，三界自無。承上徵云：「此識為復因眼根所生，即以眼為識之界，名為眼識界耶？為復因色塵所生，即以色為識之界，名為色識界耶？」良以根、塵各有別名，皆隨自法為名，而識則無之，若不係以根、塵，則無所分別。諸經多係屬於根，曰眼識、耳識等，今以權教，根、塵皆能生識，故雙約以徵之。

此破識單從眼生。若謂此識單因眼根生，即以眼為界者，則不藉乎色、空。既無色空，無有可分別之塵，單根則不能生識；縱使有能生汝之識，若無色、空，汝識亦將何所施其用乎？識以了別塵、境為用，無塵則不用識也。此承上汝眼根之見，無形、無色，既無長、短、方、圓之形，又非有青、黃、赤、白之色，無所表示，汝根之相。前段無色、空則塵無，此段無表示則根無，則中間之識，從何處可以立其界限乎？

此破識單從色生。若謂此識，單因色塵生，即以色為界者。至於見空無色之時，則色塵已滅，汝識應當隨色而滅，識滅則無所識知，云何見空之時，又識知是虛空性耶？此言色塵遷變之時，汝亦識其色相已經遷變，是汝識不隨色遷變矣？一存一亡，無有對待，則識界從何可以成立耶？此與上段文，有二意：上乃從變不識空，此乃不變不成界。

此段上二句，因聞識不從色變，應不成界，乃轉計識從色變。遂破云：若識從色變，則色與識，二俱變滅，界相自然無矣。此從變不成界，乃對上不變不成界，成為交互之文。下四句，因聞識從色變，應不識空，乃轉計識不從色變。亦破云：若識不從色變，則識性恆常，雖然恆常不變，此識既因色塵所生，自屬無知，應當不能識知虛空所在！今能知空，足證識非從色生矣。此不變不識空，乃對上從變不識空，成為交互文。此種經文，是為交床（牒）法，兩頭俱到，文法極妙。

此以上單根獨塵，皆不能生識，轉計和合而生。遂破云：若言兼眼根、色塵二種，共生汝識者，且問還是眼色相合而生耶？還是眼色相離而生耶？此二句，上合離兩字指眼色，下離合兩字指眼識。若謂眼色，合併而生，則汝識半從根生，半從塵生，中間必有離縫，以不是整個生成。如二物相合，合處有離縫也；若謂眼色，離開而生，則汝識半是有知，半是無知，有知者合於眼，無知者合於色，遂成兩合矣。體性雜亂者：指識之體性。雜對合則中離說，半從根半從塵，和雜而生故曰雜；亂對離則兩合說，半合根半合塵，則成動亂故曰亂。既是雜亂，云何能成識之中界乎？

是各生共生，俱不得成之故，應當了知。此知含二義：一者應當知妄，至三處都無止，結相之妄；二者應當知真，後四句歸性之真。以上所言，眼、色二種為緣，生於眼識者，現前推究，眼識既不從眼生，又不從色生，亦非雙兼眼色共生，則中間所生眼識，既不成界，內外眼色，何得為緣？中界既無，內外叵得，故曰三處都無。又三處都無，處即界也，所謂眼色為緣生識，今眼、色、識三界，俱不可得；應知此是相宗權教，順世之談，都無實義。則眼與色，及色界三：色界即色識界，係塵為名；此眼、色、識三，本非因緣，非自然性，乃是如來藏，妙真如性。初眼色識界竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Ānanda, as you understand it, eye and form, taken as conditions, give rise to eye-consciousness — but does this consciousness arise dependent on the eye, taking the eye as its basis, or dependent on form, taking form as its basis? Ānanda, if it arises from the eye, then, without form or emptiness present, there is nothing to be discerned at all — even granting that this consciousness of yours exists, of what use could it possibly be? And your seeing itself is not blue, yellow, red, or white — it has nothing to display — so on what basis could an element be established from it? If it arises from form, then, at the moment emptiness is present with no form in it, your consciousness ought to cease — so how could it still discern that this is the nature of emptiness? And if, when form changes, you likewise discern that its appearance has changed, while your consciousness itself does not change along with it, on what basis could an element be established at all? If consciousness changes along with form, then the mark of any boundary vanishes of itself; if it remains constant while arising from form, then, form having changed, it ought no longer to discern where emptiness itself is to be found. And if you claim it arises from both together, eye and form jointly producing it, then, whether joined or separate, its very substance becomes a confused mixture — so how, then, could any element be established from it at all? You should therefore know that eye-consciousness, said to arise from eye and form as conditions, has no location in any of the three — eye, form, and the resulting element of consciousness are, all three, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

Commentary: The Buddha poses this question in terms of what Ānanda had previously heard from the provisional teaching on causes and conditions: the eye-faculty and the object of form stand opposite each other, mutually serving as each other's condition — the faculty as the dominant condition, the object as the object-condition — and when the two conditions come together, consciousness arises between them, hence "eye-consciousness arises," and thus the three elements come to be formed. Of the three — faculty, object, and consciousness — the question is posed only about consciousness, since this is the refutation by way of the essential component: once consciousness is refuted, all three elements fall away on their own. Continuing the question: does this consciousness arise dependent on the eye-faculty, taking the eye as consciousness's own boundary, called the element of eye-consciousness — or does it arise dependent on the object of form, taking form as consciousness's own boundary, called the element of form-consciousness? The faculty and the object each already have their own distinct name, named after their own respective category, but consciousness alone has none of its own — without attaching it to either faculty or object, there would be no way to distinguish it at all. Most sūtras attach it to the faculty, speaking of "eye-consciousness," "ear-consciousness," and so on; here, in terms of the provisional teaching, since both faculty and object are held capable of giving rise to consciousness, the question is posed with reference to both together.

This refutes the claim that consciousness arises from the eye alone. If this consciousness arises solely from the eye-faculty, taking the eye alone as its boundary, then it would need no form or emptiness at all to depend on. But with no form or emptiness present, there is no object at all to be discerned — the faculty alone cannot give rise to consciousness; and even granting that such a consciousness could arise, without form or emptiness present, what use could it possibly serve? Consciousness functions by discerning its object; with no object present, consciousness has nothing to do. This continues: your own eye-faculty's seeing has, in itself, no shape and no color — it has neither long, short, square, nor round form, nor blue, yellow, red, or white color — nothing at all by which the faculty's own appearance could be displayed. The previous point showed that with no form or emptiness present, the object itself has no location; this point shows that with nothing to display, the faculty itself has no location either — so on what basis could the consciousness arising between them establish any boundary at all?

This refutes the claim that consciousness arises from form alone. If this consciousness arises solely from the object of form, taking form alone as its boundary, then, at the moment of seeing emptiness with no form present, the object of form has already ceased, and your consciousness ought to cease along with it — and once consciousness has ceased, there is nothing left to discern. How, then, at the very moment of seeing emptiness, could it still discern that this is the nature of emptiness? This says that, at the moment form itself changes, you likewise discern that its appearance has already changed — meaning your own consciousness does not change along with form. One persists while the other passes away, with no correspondence between them at all — so on what basis could the element of consciousness be established? This point and the one before it make two distinct points: the previous one shows that if consciousness changes, it could not discern emptiness; this one shows that if it does not change, no boundary could be established.

The first two lines here respond to the point that if consciousness does not change along with form, no boundary could be established, by shifting to the claim that consciousness does change along with form — which is then refuted: if consciousness changes along with form, then form and consciousness both change and cease together, and the mark of any boundary is naturally gone. This — "if it changes, no boundary is established" — is set against the earlier point — "if it does not change, no boundary is established" — the two forming an interlocking pair. The next four lines respond to the point that if consciousness changes along with form, it could not discern emptiness, by shifting to the claim that consciousness does not change along with form — which is likewise refuted: if consciousness does not change along with form, then its nature is constant; yet, constant and unchanging as it may be, this consciousness, having arisen from the object of form, properly has no capacity to know at all, and so ought to be unable to discern where emptiness itself is to be found. Yet it can, in fact, know emptiness — proof enough that consciousness does not arise from form. This — "if it does not change, it cannot discern emptiness" — is set against the earlier point — "if it changes, it cannot discern emptiness" — the two again forming an interlocking pair. This kind of passage is what is called the "interlocking" method [交床法], reaching both ends of the argument at once — a most skillful piece of composition.

Since neither the faculty alone nor the object alone can give rise to consciousness, the argument shifts to claiming that the two arise in combination — which is refuted in turn: if you say eye-faculty and form together give rise to your consciousness, then is it that eye and form arise joined together, or arise separately? [The first pair of words, "joined" and "separate," refer to eye and form; the second pair, "separate" and "joined," refer to eye-consciousness.] If eye and form give rise to consciousness by being joined, then your consciousness is half produced by the faculty and half by the object, with an inevitable seam between the two, since it is not formed as a single whole — like two objects joined together, with a seam where they meet. If eye and form give rise to consciousness by being separate, then your consciousness is half aware and half unaware — the aware half belonging to the eye, the unaware half belonging to form — and this becomes a case of two things merely set side by side. "Its very substance becomes a confused mixture" refers to the substance and nature of consciousness itself: "mixture" answers to the case of joining, where it is half from the faculty and half from the object, blended together, hence "mixture"; "confusion" answers to the case of separating, where it is half joined to the faculty and half joined to the object, forming an unstable jumble, hence "confusion." Being, either way, a confused mixture, how could it possibly form the boundary lying between faculty and object?

Since arising from either alone, or arising from the two together, both fail to hold, one should now understand this in two senses: first, one should understand the falseness — everything up through "has no location in any of the three" concludes that the appearance is false; second, one should understand the truth — the final four lines conclude that the nature is true. As for what has just been said — that eye and form, taken as conditions, give rise to eye-consciousness — examined directly, eye-consciousness arises neither from the eye alone, nor from form alone, nor from the two together; and so the consciousness that would arise between them establishes no boundary at all — how, then, could eye and form outside it still serve as its conditions? With no boundary in the middle, and nothing to be found on either side, hence: "has no location in any of the three." Here "the three" means the three boundaries — since it was said that eye and form, as conditions, give rise to consciousness, and now none of the three elements, eye, form, and consciousness, can be found at all, one should recognize this as nothing but the provisional Dharma-characteristics teaching's own worldly account, with no real substance to it at all. "Eye, and form, and the resulting element of consciousness": the element of consciousness here is named after the object, form. These three — eye, form, and consciousness — are, originally, neither a matter of causes and conditions nor of any spontaneous nature; they are exactly the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous, true nature of suchness. The element of eye-consciousness is complete.

Ear-, Nose-, Tongue-, Body-, and Mind-Consciousness 耳識界 · 鼻識界 · 舌識界 · 身識界 · 意識界

Yuanying applies the identical pattern — standing forth the triad, refuting the faculty alone, the object alone, and their combination, then concluding that all three are false in appearance and true in nature — to the remaining five elements. The sūtra's own demonstrations for eye- and ear-consciousness have already been translated in the bare-sūtra project's fascicle 3; the same refutation extends to the remaining four senses there as well.

Ear-Consciousness 耳識界 — Refuted through the same three sources (the ear, sound, or their combination), with an added distinction between the fleshy ear — described, per the sūtra's own simile, as "shaped like a rolled leaf" — and the faculty's actual hearing-nature; a merely fleshy organ, Yuanying notes, belongs to the body-faculty and its object of touch, not to hearing at all. The refutation of the sound-alone alternative runs through a chain of four absurdities: if hearing cannot hear the arising of consciousness itself, sound provides no boundary at all; if it can, then hearing becomes identical with sound, indistinguishable from what is heard; if consciousness itself is what gets heard, nothing is left to know that the hearing occurred; and if nothing knows at all, a person would be no different from grass and trees.
Nose-Consciousness 鼻識界 — Opens by asking what "the nose" even refers to: the fleshy organ, shaped per the sūtra "like a pair of drooping claws," or the faculty's actual capacity to perceive the stirring of air. Flesh alone is ruled out as belonging to the body-faculty and its object of touch, not smelling; and if empty space itself is credited with knowing scent, then, by the same logic, all space anywhere would equally be "you" — leaving "today's Ānanda" nowhere in particular at all. Yuanying illustrates the fragrance-versus-nose question with the classical image, drawn via the Zhǐzhǎng commentary from the Contemplation of Buddha Samādhi Sūtra, of the eranda tree — reeking like a corpse for forty yojanas around — within whose very thickets sandalwood saplings grow unnoticed until, come autumn, they mature and give off a fragrance so complete it entirely overpowers the eranda stench for good. If a single nose could equally smell both fragrance and stench as its own fixed nature, Yuanying presses, a single person would need two noses — "facing me and asking the way, there would be two Ānandas: which one is your real body?" — and if there is truly only one nose, fragrance and stench simply cancel each other out, leaving no fixed quality from which any boundary of consciousness could be established at all.
Tongue-Consciousness 舌識界 — Tested across tongue, flavor, empty space, and their combination, closing with the sūtra's most explicit tetralemma of the whole chapter: not arisen from itself (the tongue), not arisen from another (flavor), not arisen without cause (space), and not arisen jointly (root and object combined, which would lose each ingredient's own nature the way water and earth mixed together lose the properties of water and earth alike) — the four alternatives matching, term for term, the classical fourfold negation of arising.
Body-Consciousness 身識界 — Refuted across the body alone (with no combining or separating felt against anything, there is simply nothing for the body to discern), the object of touch alone ("who, apart from a body, has ever known a joining or a separating?"), and their combination, worked through a careful four-line tetralemma: knowing the body means the knowing itself came from touch; knowing touch means the knowing itself came from the body; so touch cannot belong to the body, and the body cannot belong to touch — each locked to one side, never both together. Master Jiaoguang's own summary names the underlying pattern precisely: "what is produced carries no mark shared by both" [所生無兼相], and "what produces it carries no mark of mutual correspondence" [能生無對相].
Mind-Consciousness 意識界 — Refuted first through the mental faculty alone (the seventh consciousness, manas, constant deliberating thought): without a present object of thought, the faculty has no form to be found at all, since it exists only correlated with its object — remove the object, and even the faculty vanishes with it. A further test asks whether mind-consciousness and this deliberating faculty are the same thing or different: if the same, consciousness cannot be said to be "produced by" the faculty, since producer and produced would collapse into one; if different, consciousness would belong to the same inert, unknowing category as any ordinary object, contradicting its own defining function of knowing. Refuted next through the mental object alone: every real phenomenon in the world already belongs to one of the five sense-objects, each displayed distinctly to its own faculty and none of them "mental" in itself — so when asked what shape a purely mental object would have, apart from all five sense-objects together, the sūtra finds nothing left to point to at all.

Reading note: As fascicle 6 anticipated, the eighteen elements apply the third refutation strategy throughout: refutation by way of the essential component (從要破). Of the three — faculty, object, and consciousness — consciousness alone is targeted directly in every case, since once it falls, neither faculty nor object can stand on its own either, the way tearing down a house's central wall collapses the boundary of both side rooms at once.

Master Jiaoguang's closing observation on the whole passage: "This section may serve as the very gateway into the dharma-realm of the unobstructed interpenetration of principle and phenomena [理事無礙法界] — though it does not, by itself, contain every one of that dharma-realm's own approaches, once one awakens to this, one can naturally reach the meaning of all those approaches as well, hence 'gateway.' This is because ordinary people cling to phenomena and fail entirely to see principle, while the provisional teaching keeps principle and phenomena apart, unable to bring the two into harmony — and so neither is able to enter the dharma-realm of their unobstructed interpenetration. This sūtra, here, takes phenomena themselves and, one by one, draws them back, harmonized into principle — this is exactly the gate, among the ten approaches, where every phenomenon is entirely principle. Having reached the point where every phenomenon is exactly principle, the gate to every wondrous approach thereafter opens of its own accord, one after another — what could this be, if not exactly that gateway?" Together with the two categories treated in fascicle 6, this completes "harmonizing the four categories with the ever-abiding nature" (會通四科即性常住) — the whole span of fascicles 6 and 7. Fascicle 8 turns to the next movement in Yuanying's outline: "fully manifesting the seven great elements as the pervading nature" (圓彰七大即性周遍), extending the identical method to earth, water, fire, wind, space, seeing, and consciousness.
