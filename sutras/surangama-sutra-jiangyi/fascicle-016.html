Turn That Hearing Back Upon Your Own Hearing

Avalokiteśvara's testimony closes with four inconceivable virtues — countless heads, arms, and eyes; boundless mantras; beings moved of themselves to give; every kind of offering to buddha and sentient being alike. The Buddha's own light then confirms all twenty-five accounts at once, and commands Mañjuśrī to judge among them. Mañjuśrī's long reply in verse examines and sets aside every other method in turn, arriving at its central teaching: why not turn that hearing which retains the Buddha's own words back upon your own hearing? This closes the sūtra's entire twenty-five approaches to perfect penetration.

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 15 carried the opening of Avalokiteśvara's own testimony — the ancient Buddha's teaching, the six-stage untying of the knot, the thirty-two manifested bodies, and the fourteen kinds of fearlessness. This fascicle carries the rest: the four inconceivable, effortless, wondrous virtues (countless heads, arms, and eyes; boundless secret mantras; beings moved of themselves to give up their own treasures; every kind of offering to buddha and sentient being alike) and the closing lines of the testimony itself, in which Avalokiteśvara receives, from the ancient Buddha, the very name carried into this present age. The Buddha then manifests a great sign — light pouring from his own body to every buddha and bodhisattva present, answered in turn by light from every one of them — confirming that every one of the twenty-five accounts, different as their methods are, reach one and the same essence. The Buddha then commands Mañjuśrī to judge among all twenty-five, for Ānanda's sake and for the sake of beings still to come. Mañjuśrī's reply, delivered in a long verse, is one of the most frequently studied and chanted passages in Chinese Buddhism: examining and setting aside each of the other twenty-four methods in turn — why sight is blocked front to back, why smell is not constant, why the body-faculty has no fixed boundary — before arriving at its own central teaching, addressed directly to Ānanda: why not turn that hearing which retains the Buddha's own words back upon your own hearing? The fascicle closes with the assembly's own response — Ānanda's class awakening in understanding, and Mātaṅgī's class (already an arhat, from the mantra's rescue back in fascicle 1) advancing further still upon hearing this same verse.

Quoted passages from the sūtra itself are marked Sūtra:; Yuanying's own explanation is marked Commentary:. Nearly every sūtra passage quoted in this fascicle — the four inconceivable virtues, the closing testimony line, Mañjuśrī's entire verse in full — was already translated, word for word, in the companion bare-sūtra project's fascicle 6, and that existing translation is reused verbatim throughout. A handful of connecting sūtra lines that fascicle 6 had abridged or omitted — Avalokiteśvara receiving the prophecy of this name, the Buddha's own manifestation of light, the command to Mañjuśrī, and the assembly's closing response — are translated fresh here for the first time. All of Yuanying's own commentary, including his full clause-by-clause unpacking of Mañjuśrī's verse, is new.

The Four Inconceivable Virtues 辰三 四不思議

Chinese Source

〖經〗世尊！我又獲是圓通，修證無上道故，又能善獲四不思議無作妙德。

〖講〗此重標自證圓通體用。上三句證體，下三句起用；前上合下同，帶果行因，歷位進脩，皆標從三昧。此重標，我又得是真實圓通：獲者得也；修證無上道故：當是功行漸深，修證將滿，得成無上之佛道故，溫陵多約等覺釋之。

又能善獲下，稱體起用；而言善獲者，即不假作意，任運自在，至妙至神，不可思惟，不可擬議，言思所不能及，口欲談而辭喪，心欲緣而慮亡也。四種皆是無作無為不可思議之德用，妙即不可思議之謂也。

〖經〗一者：由我初獲，妙妙聞心，心精遺聞，見、聞、覺、知，不能分隔，成一圓融，清淨寶覺，故我能現，眾多妙容，能說無邊，秘密神咒。

〖講〗此敘得體現用之來由。一者，四種居首，即第一不思議。推其原由，即由我蒙彼觀世音如來，教我從聞、思、修入三摩地，初於聞中，入流照性，從本覺妙理，起始覺妙智，以妙智，照妙理，理智雙妙，故曰妙妙聞心。又六根之性，為妙性耳根超諸根而獨妙，是謂妙中之妙。初得此心，為本修因地，故曰初獲妙妙聞心。

心精遺聞：遺者脫也，即聞所聞盡，惟一心精，根塵雙脫也。見、聞、覺、知：即六精，舉四攝六故。不能分隔者：見等不為六根分離隔礙，以塵亡根盡，反流全一，六用不行，即屬此根初解，先得人空。成一圓融，清淨寶覺者：即不住化城，漸次增進，而得法空，俱空。直透末後牢關，生滅既滅，寂滅現前，伏歸本元真體，成一圓融無礙，清淨本然，寶覺真心。六根互用曰圓融，諸塵不染曰清淨。證入三如來藏心，本覺理體，猶如摩尼寶珠，故曰寶覺。

寶覺真心，由來清淨，本無一法可得，即空如來藏，如摩尼珠之體，清淨無染：真心隨緣，而成十界諸法，如摩尼珠，隨方現色；真心雖隨緣，而本體不變，如摩尼珠，雖現眾色，珠體不變。

故我能現，眾多妙容下：大用現前。一身能現多容，即現首、現臂、現目。能說無邊秘密神咒：咒而稱秘密者，乃諸聖密言，下位不知，上位密咒。持之得益，具有神妙力用，故又稱神咒也。

〖經〗其中或現：一首、三首、五首、七首、九首、十一首，如是乃至一百八首，千首、萬首、八萬四千爍迦羅首。

〖講〗此明現首。其中即眾多妙容之中。首為五根都聚之處，一身以首為尊，以首為貴，菩薩故先現之。首以一數為本數，遞加至八萬四千，以應法門之數，對治塵勞煩惱。六祖曰：「我此法門，從一般若，生八萬四千智慧。何以故？為世有八萬四千塵勞煩惱，若無塵勞，智慧常現，何用轉染成淨？」爍迦羅此云堅固不壞也。

或問：「菩薩現八萬四千首，是事奚足信？」答：「此是菩薩不思議境界，詎可以凡情測度哉？普賢菩薩，於一毛孔，不可說佛剎次第入，則現首更無足疑，即如人之一身，有八萬四千毛孔，無足異也。」

〖經〗二臂、四臂、六臂、八臂、十臂、十二臂、十四、十六、十八、二十、至二十四，如是乃至一百八臂，千臂、萬臂、八萬四千母多羅臂。

〖講〗此明現臂。臂即手臂。一身二臂為本數，遞增至八萬四千，以表法門如前說。母多羅：此云印，謂臂各有手，手各結印故。

〖經〗二目、三目、四目、九目、如是乃至一百八目、千目、萬目、八萬四千清淨寶目。

〖講〗此明現目。人但二目，摩醯首羅天王，則有三目，故以雙數單數，互增至八萬四千。清淨寶目：即法眼圓明清淨，無礙無染，如佛紺目澄清是也。

〖經〗或慈、或威，或定、或慧，救護眾生，得大自在。

〖講〗此之慈、威、定、慧，對上首、臂、目言。或是不定之意，或現慈容可親，或現威容可畏；或垂臂提攜則慈，或擎拳勇健則威；或慈眼眷顧為慈，或怒目猙獰為威；或結印安靜則定，或摩頂授記則慧；慈是愛念相，威是雄勇相，定是寂靜相，慧是開朗相。所現諸相，皆為救護眾生，救是救拔，令其離苦，護是護念，令其得樂。又慈以護其善根，威以救其惡性，定以護其散亂，慧以救其昏迷。得大自在：即由不思議妙德，不勞作意，任運示現，自在成就，無往而不適其宜也。形既如是，咒可例知，亦必救護自在矣！

〖經〗二者：由我聞思，脫出六塵，如聲度垣，不能為礙，故我妙能現一一形，誦一一咒，其形其咒，能以無畏，施諸眾生。是故十方，微塵國土，皆名我為施無畏者。

〖講〗上科同體形咒，一身能現眾多妙容，能說無邊神咒。此科異體形咒，鑑機不一，現形亦多，應現何形，一一現之，各為說咒，故不同上科。亦不同三十二應，各隨所求，各應同類，此不拘類求，此能施無畏；亦不同十四無畏，彼但冥加，此則顯應。

前四句明證體，中明起用，是故下述得名。二者，由我聞思脫出六塵：即從聞、思、修，入三摩地，諸根圓拔，六塵迥脫，所以塵不能礙。此二句是法，下二句是喻：如音聲能度垣牆之外，雖有垣牆，不能為礙。因是證體，故我能現妙用，任運隨緣，能現一一身形，能誦一一神咒。誦者，即誦古佛所說之咒，如過去千光王靜住佛，所說《廣大圓滿無礙大悲心大陀羅尼》等，所現之形，所說之咒，皆能以無畏力，布施於眾生，是由實德咸孚，令聞廣譽，十方世界，微塵國土眾生，皆名我為能施無畏者。

〖經〗三者：由我修習，本妙圓通，清淨本根，所遊世界，皆令眾生，捨身珍寶，求我哀愍。

〖講〗前三句為能感，後四句為所感。由我修習本妙圓通者：圓通乃眾生人人本具，迷而不發，雖處迷位，依然不失，故曰本妙圓通。清淨本根：是指耳根，根性即是如來藏性，清淨本然，為此方對機之根，是為本根。上句在迷本具，此句修證離染，故得清淨。由證清淨之體，一切無有染著，故令眾生，能捨慳吝執著之心。而我所遊化世界，皆能令眾生，破除慳貪，發心喜捨，捨自身所有珍珠寶物，求我哀愍，受之而為施作佛事也。

《正脈》云：眾生慳[不捨也]心最難破，捨心最難發，求心不可強致。今所過之處，即感眾生，破慳施捨哀求，是誠不可思議，威神所使然也。孰知其洪源，但由修習耳根，清淨無著，故能類感如此。此事《法華普門品》可證，無盡意菩薩聞佛所說觀世音菩薩，遊化諸國，利益娑婆，即白佛言：「世尊！我今當供養觀世音菩薩，即解頸眾寶珠瓔珞，價值百千兩金，而以與之，作是言：仁者！受此法施，珍寶瓔珞[瓔珞雖然貴重，本是財施，而曰法施者，一解供之時，了達三輪體空；二不為己施，乃為眾施，求作佛事，饒益眾生，故成法施。]。時觀世音菩薩，不肯受之。無盡意復白觀世音菩薩言：仁者！愍我等故，受此瓔珞[觀世音不受有二意：一不供佛，而供我，佛不命受，故不敢受；二現從頸解，受之非義，故不宜受。無盡意重請，亦有二意：一表誠懇，望佛轉致，令其必受；二為眾而施，為眾而受，乃無傷於義。]。爾時，佛告觀世音菩薩，當愍此無盡意菩薩，及四眾八部，受此瓔珞。即時觀世音菩薩，愍諸四眾八部，受其瓔珞，分作二分，一分奉釋迦牟尼佛，一分奉多寶佛塔[既為眾而受，亦為眾而供，奉供二尊，佛為無上福田，以此轉供，回施一切眾生，令植佛因，旁引法華，以見菩薩，言之有徵也。]。

〖經〗四者，我得佛心，證於究竟，能以珍寶，種種供養，十方如來，傍及法界，六道眾生。

〖講〗前二句述所證體，下明所起用。我得佛心者：即得諸佛如來，秘密之因地心。依此因心，而起了義修證，從妙耳門，反聞照性，證得一切事究竟堅固首楞嚴定，即證入如來藏，則世出世法，一一無不含藏其中，故能具足珍寶，供養佛生，以見佛生不二，等與供養也。證於究竟：莫作妙覺極果解，以菩薩示居等覺因位故也。

〖經〗求妻得妻，求子得子，求三昧得三昧，求長壽得長壽，如是乃至，求大涅槃得大涅槃。

〖講〗上則佛、生等供，此則財、法二施。求妻得妻，求子得子：此對人道，以外財布施，國城妻子，皆外財也；求長壽得長壽：此對人道天仙，以內財布施，壽命色力，皆內財也。此應在三昧前。人間，有求長年益壽，仙道有求堅固形骸，壽千萬歲；天道，亦有長壽天，有求皆得，上皆以財布施。

求三昧得三昧：此對上二界諸天，及三乘聖眾，或求四禪，四空，有漏禪定，或求聲聞、緣覺、三賢、十聖，無漏三昧，皆能令得三昧；或求無餘大涅槃，究竟斷德，即令得大涅槃；此皆以法布施。蓋由大士，證得藏心，一真法界，平等真如，故能生佛等供，財法二施，其無作妙力，豈可得而思議哉？二承演三科竟，併上科寅三詳演稱體起用竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "World-Honored One, I have, further, attained this perfect penetration, cultivating and realizing the unsurpassed path thereby, and am, further, able to skillfully attain four inconceivable, effortless, wondrous virtues."

Commentary: This restates, once more, the very essence and function of self-realized perfect penetration. The first three lines here establish this essence; the next three give rise to its function — accordant above, one with below, this fruit already carried forward into further causal practice, advancing stage by stage — all of this stands upon the same samādhi described above. This restatement, "I have, further, attained this true perfect penetration" — "attained" means exactly this: obtained. "Cultivating and realizing the unsurpassed path thereby": this marks the gradual deepening of this same merit and practice, cultivation and realization nearing their own completion, and so attaining the unsurpassed buddha-path itself — Wenling mostly reads this in terms of the stage of equal enlightenment.

"Further able to skillfully attain," what follows: according with this essence, giving rise to function; and calling this "skillful attainment" means exactly this: requiring no deliberate intention at all, spontaneous and free, utterly wondrous, utterly spiritual, beyond thought, beyond comparison, beyond what words or thought could ever reach — the mouth wishing to speak of it finds words fail; the mind wishing to grasp it finds thought itself vanishes. All four of these virtues are effortless, unconditioned, inconceivable functions of virtue — "wondrous" here means exactly this same inconceivability.

Sūtra: "First, since I first attained this wondrous, wondrous hearing-mind, its very essence transcending hearing itself, seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing can no longer be kept separate from one another, forming a single, perfectly interfused, pure, precious awareness — and so I am able to manifest countless wondrous forms and proclaim boundless sacred mantras."

Commentary: This recounts how this same essence, once attained, gives rise to this function. This "first" stands foremost of the four, being the first inconceivable virtue. Tracing its own origin: because I received, from that Tathāgata Avalokiteśvara, the teaching of entering samādhi through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating, at first, within hearing itself, entering the stream to illuminate this nature directly, giving rise, from original awakening's own wondrous principle, to the wondrous wisdom of initial awakening — this wisdom illuminating this principle, both principle and wisdom alike wondrous — hence, "wondrous, wondrous hearing-mind." And, further, among the six sense-faculties' own natures, all wondrous natures, the ear-faculty alone surpasses every other faculty in its own wonder — this being exactly the wonder within this same wonder. First attaining this mind as the very root cause of cultivation, hence, "I first attained this wondrous, wondrous hearing-mind."

"Its very essence transcending hearing itself": "transcending" means exactly slipping free — that is, both the hearing that hears and what is heard exhausted, only this single essence of mind remaining, sense-faculty and sense-object both slipped free together. "Seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing" are exactly the six essences, four named standing for all six. "Can no longer be kept separate" means exactly this: seeing and the rest are no longer separated or obstructed by the six sense-faculties — the sense-object gone and the sense-faculty exhausted, turned back against the stream into a single whole, all six functions no longer operating separately — this belongs exactly to this faculty's own first untying, first attaining the emptiness of self. "Forming a single, perfectly interfused, pure, precious awareness": that is, no longer resting within any conjured city along the way, advancing gradually further still, and attaining the emptiness of dharmas, and the emptiness of both together — penetrating straight through to that very last, most stubborn barrier, arising and ceasing themselves extinguished, still cessation appearing of itself, subsiding back into this own original, true substance, forming this single, perfectly interfused, unobstructed, originally pure, precious, awakened, true mind. The interchangeable use of all six sense-faculties is called "perfectly interfused"; unstained by any sense-object is called "pure." Realizing and entering the mind of the three aspects of the tathāgatagarbha, this principle-substance of original awakening, exactly like a wish-fulfilling jewel — hence, "precious, awakened."

This precious, awakened, true mind has, from its very origin, always been pure, with not a single dharma to be found within it at all — this is exactly the empty tathāgatagarbha, exactly like the wish-fulfilling jewel's own substance, pure and unstained. This true mind, according with conditions, forms every dharma of the ten Dharma-realms — exactly like the wish-fulfilling jewel manifesting whatever color is placed before it; and this true mind, though according with conditions, its own essential substance never changes — exactly like the wish-fulfilling jewel, though manifesting every color, its own jewel-substance never changing at all.

"And so I am able to manifest countless wondrous forms" and what follows: this great function now appears. A single body able to manifest many forms — that is, manifesting heads, manifesting arms, manifesting eyes. "Proclaim boundless sacred mantras": calling a mantra "sacred" means exactly this: these are the sages' own secret words, unknown to those of lower rank, the secret mantras of those of higher rank. Holding and reciting them brings benefit, complete with a spiritual, wondrous power and function — hence also called "sacred mantras."

Sūtra: "Among these, I may manifest one head, three heads, five, seven, nine, or eleven heads, and so on, up to a hundred and eight heads, a thousand heads, ten thousand heads, or eighty-four thousand vajra-firm heads."

Commentary: This makes plain the manifestation of heads. "Among these" means among these countless wondrous forms. The head is where the five sense-faculties all gather together; of a single body, the head is honored above all, the head is held most precious of all — and so the bodhisattva manifests it first. Heads begin from a single one as their base number, increasing successively all the way to eighty-four thousand, matching the number of Dharma-gates, so as to counteract worldly toil and affliction. As the Sixth Patriarch put it: "this Dharma-gate of mine gives rise, from a single prajñā, to eighty-four thousand kinds of wisdom. Why is this? Because the world holds eighty-four thousand kinds of worldly toil and affliction — were there no such toil, wisdom would shine forth constantly of itself; what need, then, would there be to transform defilement into purity?" "Vajra-firm" is translated "solid and indestructible."

One might ask: "the bodhisattva manifests eighty-four thousand heads — how could such a thing be believed?" The answer: "this is exactly the bodhisattva's own inconceivable state — how could it ever be measured by an ordinary being's own understanding? Samantabhadra Bodhisattva, within a single pore, enters buddha-lands beyond number, one after another — manifesting many heads raises no more doubt than this at all; it is no different, indeed, from an ordinary human body, which itself already has eighty-four thousand pores."

Sūtra: "Two arms, four, six, eight, ten, twelve, fourteen, sixteen, eighteen, twenty, up to twenty-four, and so on, up to a hundred and eight arms, a thousand arms, ten thousand arms, or eighty-four thousand mudrā-arms."

Commentary: This makes plain the manifestation of arms. "Arms" are exactly the arms of the body. A single body's two arms serve as the base number, increasing successively all the way to eighty-four thousand, representing the Dharma-gates as explained above. "Mudrā" is translated "seal," meaning that each arm has its own hand, and each hand forms its own mudrā.

Sūtra: "Two eyes, three, four, nine, and so on, up to a hundred and eight eyes, a thousand eyes, ten thousand eyes, or eighty-four thousand pure, precious eyes."

Commentary: This makes plain the manifestation of eyes. An ordinary person has only two eyes; the deva-king Maheśvara has three — and so, alternating between even and odd numbers, these increase all the way to eighty-four thousand. "Pure, precious eyes" are exactly the Dharma-eye, perfectly bright and pure, unobstructed and unstained — exactly like the Buddha's own eyes, deep blue and utterly clear.

Sūtra: "Sometimes compassionate, sometimes majestic, sometimes in samādhi, sometimes full of wisdom, rescuing and protecting sentient beings, attaining great freedom."

Commentary: This compassion, majesty, samādhi, and wisdom are spoken here in relation to the heads, arms, and eyes described above. "Sometimes" carries the sense of no fixed pattern: sometimes manifesting a compassionate countenance, approachable and warm; sometimes a majestic countenance, awe-inspiring; sometimes lowering an arm to lift a being up — this is compassion; sometimes raising a fist, bold and vigorous — this is majesty; sometimes a compassionate eye, caring and attentive — this is compassion; sometimes a wrathful eye, fierce and forbidding — this is majesty; sometimes forming a mudrā in calm stillness — this is samādhi; sometimes touching a crown to bestow a prophecy — this is wisdom. Compassion is the mark of loving concern; majesty is the mark of bold valor; samādhi is the mark of still calm; wisdom is the mark of open clarity. Every mark manifested is entirely for the sake of rescuing and protecting sentient beings — "rescuing" means drawing them out, freeing them from suffering; "protecting" means holding them in mind, bringing them to happiness. Compassion, further, protects a being's own wholesome roots; majesty rescues a being from their own evil nature; samādhi protects against a being's own scatteredness; wisdom rescues a being from their own confusion. "Attaining great freedom": that is, through this same inconceivable, wondrous virtue, requiring no deliberate intention at all, manifesting spontaneously, freely accomplished, never once failing to suit whatever occasion arises. The forms being exactly this way, the mantras may be understood by the same example — certain, likewise, to rescue and protect just as freely!

Sūtra: "Second, since my hearing and reflection have escaped the six sense-objects entirely, like sound passing through a wall unobstructed, I can manifest any form and recite any mantra, bestowing fearlessness on beings, so that I am called, throughout countless lands, 'the Giver of Fearlessness.'"

Commentary: The previous section, on the same-substance forms and mantras, described a single body able to manifest countless wondrous forms, able to proclaim boundless mantras. This present section, on different-substance forms and mantras, discerns each capacity individually, manifesting many different forms as well — whatever form should be manifested in answer, that very form is manifested, each accompanied by its own mantra — and so it differs from the section above. It differs, too, from the thirty-two responses, where each response matches what is sought, each answering a being of the same kind — here, not restricted to any one kind sought, this section bestows fearlessness directly. It differs, further, from the fourteen kinds of fearlessness, where that blessing works invisibly — here, the response is manifest and direct.

The first four lines make plain this realized essence; what follows makes plain the function arising from it, and so, after this, its own name is given. "Second, since my hearing and reflection have escaped the six sense-objects entirely": that is, through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating, entering samādhi, every sense-faculty entirely uprooted, the six sense-objects slipping entirely loose — and so these sense-objects can no longer obstruct at all. These first two lines are the dharma itself; the two that follow are the analogy: like sound able to pass beyond a wall — though the wall stands there, it cannot obstruct the sound at all. Because this essence is already realized, I am thereby able to manifest this wondrous function, spontaneously, according with conditions, able to manifest any form, able to recite any mantra. "Reciting" means exactly reciting the mantras spoken by the ancient buddhas themselves — such as the Great, Perfect, Unobstructed Dhāraṇī of Great Compassion, spoken by the past Buddha Thousand-Radiance King, Calm Abiding, and others besides. Whatever form is manifested, whatever mantra is recited, all alike are able to bestow the power of fearlessness upon sentient beings — and it is entirely because this genuine virtue reaches so many that this broad renown has spread, so that sentient beings of every land, as numerous as motes of dust throughout the ten directions, all alike call me the Giver of Fearlessness.

Sūtra: "Third, through cultivating this original, wondrous, perfect penetration and pure root-nature, wherever I travel, beings are moved to offer their own bodies and treasures, seeking my compassion."

Commentary: The first three lines here are the moving cause; the four lines that follow are the moved effect. "Through cultivating this original, wondrous, perfect penetration": perfect penetration is exactly what every single sentient being already possesses in full — deluded, it fails to manifest, yet, even while caught within delusion, it is never actually lost — hence, "original, wondrous perfect penetration." "Pure root-nature" points exactly to the ear-faculty — the sense-faculty's own nature being exactly the tathāgatagarbha's own nature, originally pure of itself, the very root-faculty matched to this particular world's own capacity, hence "root-nature." The line above speaks of this nature already possessed while still deluded; this present line speaks of cultivating and realizing it, leaving defilement behind, and so attaining purity. Through realizing this pure essence, free of any defiling attachment whatsoever, one is thereby able to bring sentient beings to let go of their own stingy, grasping mind. And wherever I myself travel and teach, I am able to bring sentient beings, everywhere, to break through their own stinginess and greed, resolving with joy to give freely, offering up their own pearls and treasures, seeking my own compassion — and I receive these offerings, using them to carry out the Buddha's own work.

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: among sentient beings, a stingy mind (that is, one unwilling to give) is hardest of all to break through, a mind of giving is hardest of all to resolve upon, and a mind that longs to give cannot be forced into being. Yet wherever this bodhisattva passes, sentient beings are moved of themselves to break through their own stinginess, to give freely, pleading for the bodhisattva's compassion — this is, indeed, truly inconceivable, brought about entirely through this same majestic, spiritual power. And who would know the great source of this power? It is entirely because this same ear-faculty has been cultivated, pure and free of any attachment, that it is able to move beings in this way. This very matter is confirmed in the Universal Gate chapter of the Lotus Sūtra: hearing the Buddha describe how Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva travels and teaches throughout every land, benefiting this Sahā world, the Bodhisattva Akṣayamati said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I shall now make an offering to Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva" — and, undoing the string of precious pearls about his own neck, worth a hundred thousand taels of gold, he offered it, saying: "Venerable one, please accept this offering of the Dharma, this precious pearl necklace" (though a pearl necklace is a precious thing, in substance an offering of wealth, it is called here an offering of the Dharma for two reasons: first, at the very moment of untying it to offer, he penetrated fully to the emptiness of all three wheels of this giving; second, this offering was made not for his own sake, but on behalf of every being, seeking to carry out the Buddha's own work and benefit sentient beings — and so it became an offering of the Dharma). At that time, Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva was unwilling to accept it. Akṣayamati said again to Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva: "Venerable one, out of compassion for us, please accept this necklace" (Avalokiteśvara's refusal carried two senses: first, this offering was not made to the Buddha but to the bodhisattva directly, and, the Buddha not having commanded its acceptance, the bodhisattva did not presume to accept it; second, since it had just been untied from around the giver's own neck, accepting it in this manner seemed improper, and so it was declined. Akṣayamati's renewed request likewise carried two senses: first, to express his own sincere earnestness, hoping the Buddha himself would relay this request, so that it might certainly be accepted; second, this offering was made on behalf of every being, and so its acceptance, too, would be on behalf of every being — and so no impropriety was involved after all). At that time, the Buddha told Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva: you should, out of compassion for this Bodhisattva Akṣayamati, and for the four assemblies and the eight kinds of spirits, accept this necklace. Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva then, out of compassion for the four assemblies and the eight kinds of spirits, accepted the necklace, and divided it into two portions — one portion offered to the Buddha Śākyamuni, and the other offered to the stūpa of the Buddha Prabhūtaratna (having accepted it on behalf of every being, the bodhisattva likewise made this offering on behalf of every being as well, offering it up to these two honored ones — the Buddha being the unsurpassed field of merit — and, through this same relayed offering, returning its benefit to every sentient being, so that they might plant the very seed of buddhahood. This citation, drawn from the Lotus Sūtra, shows this bodhisattva's own words to carry real proof behind them).

Sūtra: "Fourth, having attained the Buddha's own mind and realized it to its very limit, I am able to make every kind of offering to the Tathāgatas of the ten directions and, at the same time, to every being of the six destinies throughout the Dharma-realm."

Commentary: The first two lines here recount this realized essence; what follows makes plain the function that arises from it. "I have attained the Buddha's own mind": that is, attained exactly the same hidden, causal-stage mind held by every buddha, every Tathāgata. Relying on this same causal mind, giving rise to the cultivation and realization of complete meaning, through this wondrous ear-gate, turning hearing back to illuminate this nature directly, realizing the Śūraṅgama samādhi, the ultimate, solid firmness underlying every single event — that is, realizing and entering the tathāgatagarbha directly, within which every mundane and transcendent dharma, without exception, is contained in full — and so I am able to be complete with every treasure, making offerings to both buddhas and sentient beings alike, seeing buddha and sentient being as not two different things at all, offering to both equally. "Realized it to its very limit": this should not be read as the ultimate fruit of wondrous enlightenment itself, since the bodhisattva is shown here still standing at the causal stage of equal enlightenment.

Sūtra: "Those who seek a spouse obtain one, those who seek a child obtain one, those who seek samādhi obtain it, those who seek long life obtain it, and so on, up to those who seek great nirvāṇa, who obtain that as well."

Commentary: What came above was offering equally to buddhas and to sentient beings; what follows here is the two kinds of giving, of wealth and of Dharma. "Those who seek a spouse obtain one, those who seek a child obtain one": this is spoken in relation to the human path, an offering of outer wealth — kingdoms, cities, spouses, and children all being exactly outer wealth. "Those who seek long life obtain it": this is spoken in relation to the human path and to the heavenly immortals, an offering of inner wealth — lifespan, form, and strength all being exactly inner wealth. This line should properly come before "seek samādhi": in the human realm, there are those who seek to extend their own years and add to their own lifespan; in the way of the immortals, there are those who seek a solid, indestructible body, a lifespan of a thousand or ten thousand years; and, in the heavenly path, too, there are heavens of long life — whoever seeks any of these obtains it, all through this same offering of wealth.

"Those who seek samādhi obtain it": this is spoken in relation to the gods of the upper two realms, and to the sagely assemblies of the three vehicles — whether seeking the four dhyānas and four formless absorptions, defiled meditative states, or seeking the undefiled samādhi of the śrāvaka, the pratyekabuddha, the three grades of worthy, or the ten grades of sage, in every case, samādhi is thereby attained; or whether seeking the great nirvāṇa with nothing further remaining, the ultimate virtue of severance itself, this too is thereby attained — all of this through this same offering of the Dharma. This is entirely because the great being, having realized this very mind of the tathāgatagarbha, this single, true Dharma-realm, this equal, true suchness, is thereby able to offer equally to both sentient beings and buddhas alike, this twofold giving of wealth and Dharma — how, then, could this effortless, wondrous power ever be fully grasped by thought? This closes the second section, elaborating these three categories in detail, and, together with it, closes the previous section as well, elaborating in detail how wondrous function arises from this same essence.

〖經〗佛問圓通，我從耳門，圓照三昧，緣心自在，因入流相，得三摩地，成就菩提，斯為第一。

〖講〗佛前問我等，悟十八界，誰為圓通，從何方便，入三摩地？我觀世音乃從妙耳門，依本根圓湛不生滅性，起智觀照，是謂圓照，即反聞照性，照到一心本源，即得三昧，是首楞嚴王三昧。此圓照三昧四字，上二修因，下二證果。緣心自在句，即果後脩因，又曰帶果行因，包括上三科。緣字，即三十二應，十四無畏，四不思議，隨緣應化，所起妙用，心得自在。上結答佛問悟十八界誰為圓通，我悟妙耳門，即是圓通根。

因入流相，得三摩地者：此結答佛問從何方便，入三摩地，我因入流相，得三摩地。因入流相四字，即從聞、思、修，解六結，破五陰，所有行相。因者依也，依耳根，最優勝，最圓滿之根；又因者由也，由耳根聞性，起始覺智。入流者，逆生死流，入涅槃流，由淺入深，入到生滅既滅，寂滅現前，得三摩地，即如幻聞薰聞修，金剛三昧，名得耳根圓通。此上所答五句，該攝一經總題，亦即該攝全經要義。我從妙耳門，圓湛不生滅性，為因地心，即如來密因。照字是起智觀照，做入流照性，背塵合覺之工夫，即修證了義。緣心自在，隨緣普應，或冥應，或顯應；心得自在，任運利生，即諸菩薩萬行。得三摩地，即得首楞嚴大定。

成就菩提者：此成無上極果之菩提，圓得三菩提也。證真性菩提之妙理，滿實智菩提之妙智，起方便菩提之妙用，故曰成就菩提。上我從耳門圓照，即得元明覺，無生滅性，為因地心。此句即然後圓成果地修證。

斯為第一者：斯者此也，此耳根圓通，最為第一，即結答法門殊勝。此耳根一門，即佛首告阿難，有三摩提，名大佛頂，首楞嚴王，具足萬行，十方如來，一門超出，妙莊嚴路之一門；亦即阿難請入華屋之門；亦即十方薄伽梵，一路涅槃門：故為第一。

又此成就菩提，斯為第一者：即觀世音代佛明答阿難所問，十方如來得成菩提，妙奢摩他、三摩、禪那，最初方便。謂我從耳門，悟圓湛不生滅性，大開圓解，即妙奢摩他，起智觀照，依耳根，為本修因，反聞照性，而起圓修，即妙三摩；隨緣應化，心得自在，安住圓定，萬行繁興，即妙禪那。具足三定，圓成首楞嚴大定，成就無上菩提，惟此耳根第一。悟根性為因地心，是最初方便；從根修證，是初方便[為入初發心住之方便。]；獲二勝、發三用，隨緣利生，是方便。阿難能問耳根圓通，悟徹得成菩提之法，改偏聞之轍，向耳根門頭，旋妄復真，即是具足三慧，速證圓通，何待佛敕文殊再選也。

〖經〗世尊！彼佛如來，歎我善得，圓通法門，於大會中，授記我為觀世音號。

〖講〗此結自利殊勝。彼佛如來，即古觀世音如來，教我從聞、思、修入三摩地，我即秉教修習，而得圓通。歎我善得圓通法門，即讚歎於我；善得者，費力少，而收效速，得循圓根，與不圓根，日劫相倍也。於大會中，授記我為觀世音號，此即依修證自利行，而授因位之名，應在證圓通時授之，由其師資道合，故授同名之記。

〖經〗由我觀聽，十方圓明，故觀音名，遍十方界。

〖講〗上約人得名，此約法得名。法即修證之法，由我觀照耳根，聽聞之性，圓照法界，惟妙覺明，十方眾生，念我名號，無不圓明了知，不因心念，不假作意，與樂拔苦，自在成就，由是十方眾生，皆稱我名，故觀音名，遍十方界。十方眾生，皆與菩薩有緣，禮念供養，甲於其他菩薩也。二觀音廣陳竟，併上諸聖異說，子二眾說本因竟。

Sūtra: "Since you ask about perfect penetration: through the ear-faculty, by the perfectly illuminating samādhi, my conditioned mind became free, and, through the mark of entering the stream, I attained samādhi and accomplished bodhi. This is foremost."

Commentary: The Buddha earlier asked us all: awakening to the eighteen elements, which one brings you to perfect penetration? And by what skillful means did you enter samādhi? I, Avalokiteśvara, proceeded exactly from this wondrous ear-gate, relying on this root-faculty's own perfectly calm, unborn, unceasing nature, giving rise to wisdom's own contemplation — this being called "perfectly illuminating," that is, turning hearing back to illuminate this nature directly, illuminating all the way to the single mind's own original source, and thereby attaining samādhi — this being the very king of Śūraṅgama samādhi itself. These four words, "perfectly illuminating samādhi," the first two describe the causal-stage cultivation, and the last two describe the realized fruit. The line "my conditioned mind became free" is exactly this fruit carried forward into further causal practice — also called "carrying the fruit forward into causal practice" — encompassing all three sections described above. The word "conditioned" points exactly to the thirty-two responses, the fourteen kinds of fearlessness, and the four inconceivable virtues, each transforming and responding according to conditions, this wondrous function thereby arising, the mind attaining freedom. This closes the answer to the Buddha's question of which, among the eighteen elements, brings one to perfect penetration — I awakened to this wondrous ear-gate, exactly this root of perfect penetration.

"Through the mark of entering the stream, I attained samādhi": this closes the answer to the Buddha's question of by what skillful means one enters samādhi — through the mark of entering the stream, I attained samādhi. These four words, "through the mark of entering the stream," are exactly the entire course of practice belonging to hearing, reflecting, and cultivating, untying the six knots, breaking through the five aggregates. "Through" means exactly relying upon — relying on the ear-faculty, the most excellent, most perfectly complete faculty of all; and "through" also means exactly by means of — by means of the ear-faculty's own nature of hearing, giving rise to the wisdom of initial awakening. "Entering the stream" means going against the stream of birth and death, and entering the stream of nirvāṇa instead, proceeding from the shallow into the deep, entering all the way to arising and ceasing themselves extinguished, still cessation appearing of itself, thereby attaining samādhi — that is, exactly this illusion-like habituation of hearing and cultivation of hearing turned back, this vajra samādhi — named exactly attaining the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration. These five lines given in answer above encompass this entire sūtra's own title, and encompass, too, the essential meaning of the whole sūtra. "I proceeded from this wondrous ear-gate," this perfectly calm, unborn, unceasing nature, taken as the causal-stage mind — this is exactly the Tathāgata's own hidden cause. The word "illuminating" is exactly giving rise to wisdom's own contemplation, carrying out the effort of entering the stream to illuminate this nature directly, turning one's back on the sense-object while according with awareness — this is exactly the cultivation and realization of complete meaning. "My conditioned mind became free," responding universally according to conditions, whether responding invisibly or responding manifestly, the mind attaining freedom, spontaneously benefiting beings — this is exactly the bodhisattvas' own myriad practices. "I attained samādhi" is exactly attaining the Śūraṅgama's own great samādhi.

"Accomplished bodhi": this is accomplishing bodhi as the unsurpassed, ultimate fruit itself, perfectly attaining the three kinds of bodhi together — realizing the wondrous principle of the bodhi of true nature, fulfilling the wondrous wisdom of the bodhi of actual wisdom, and giving rise to the wondrous function of the bodhi of skillful means — hence, "accomplished bodhi." "I proceeded from the ear-gate, by the perfectly illuminating samādhi," above, is exactly attaining this original, bright awakening's own unborn, unceasing nature, taken as the causal-stage mind; this present line is exactly the subsequent, perfect accomplishment of cultivation and realization at the fruit stage.

"This is foremost": "this" means exactly this ear-faculty's own perfect penetration, foremost of all — this closes the answer, affirming the surpassing excellence of this Dharma-gate. This single gate of the ear-faculty is exactly the very gate the Buddha first told Ānanda of — the samādhi named the Great Buddha's Crown, the king of Śūraṅgama, complete with every practice, the single gate through which every Tathāgata of the ten directions has transcended, the one gate along this wondrously adorned path; it is also exactly the very gate Ānanda requested, to enter the jeweled hall; and it is also exactly the single gateway to nirvāṇa shared by every Bhagavān of the ten directions — hence, foremost.

And this "accomplished bodhi, this is foremost" is exactly Avalokiteśvara answering, on the Buddha's own behalf, Ānanda's original question — by what initial skillful means did every Tathāgata of the ten directions come to accomplish bodhi, through wondrous śamatha, samādhi, and dhyāna? This means exactly: I, proceeding from the ear-gate, awakened to this perfectly calm, unborn, unceasing nature, opening at once to this perfect understanding — this is exactly wondrous śamatha; giving rise to wisdom's own contemplation, relying on the ear-faculty as the very root of cultivation, turning hearing back to illuminate this nature directly, giving rise thereby to perfect cultivation — this is exactly wondrous samādhi; responding and transforming according to conditions, the mind attaining freedom, abiding securely within this perfect samādhi, every practice flourishing forth at once — this is exactly wondrous dhyāna. Complete with these three forms of samādhi together, perfectly accomplishing the Śūraṅgama's own great samādhi, accomplishing unsurpassed bodhi itself — it is exactly this ear-faculty alone that is foremost. Awakening to the sense-faculty's own nature as the causal-stage mind is the very first skillful means; cultivating and realizing through the sense-faculty is the initial skillful means (that is, the skillful means for entering the first abiding of the initial resolve); attaining these two supreme qualities and giving rise to these three functions, benefiting beings according to conditions, is skillful means as well. Ānanda, able to ask after the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration, awakening thoroughly to this method of accomplishing bodhi, changing course from his own one-sided reliance on mere hearing, turning instead toward the very gate of the ear-faculty, turning back falseness and returning to truth — this is exactly being complete with these three kinds of wisdom, swiftly realizing perfect penetration — what further need is there, then, to wait for the Buddha to command Mañjuśrī to select once more?

Sūtra: "World-Honored One, that Buddha, that Tathāgata, praising me for so excellently attaining this Dharma-gate of perfect penetration, conferred on me, in the midst of the great assembly, the prophecy of my own name, Avalokiteśvara."

Commentary: This closes this account of the surpassing excellence of self-benefiting practice. "That Buddha, that Tathāgata" is exactly the ancient Tathāgata Avalokiteśvara, who taught me to enter samādhi through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating — and I, receiving this teaching, took up the practice, and thereby attained perfect penetration. "Praising me for so excellently attaining this Dharma-gate of perfect penetration" is exactly this same praise given to me — "excellently attaining" means exactly this: little effort spent, yet swift results gained, since attaining the perfect faculty, compared with an imperfect one, differs as much as a single day from a whole kalpa. "Conferred on me, in the midst of the great assembly, the prophecy of my own name, Avalokiteśvara": this is exactly conferring, upon this same self-benefiting practice of cultivation and realization, the very name belonging to this causal stage — properly conferred at the moment perfect penetration is itself realized, and, teacher and disciple's own paths thus in perfect accord, conferring, too, this very same name in common.

Sūtra: "Because I observe and listen, becoming perfectly bright throughout the ten directions, this name Avalokiteśvara has spread throughout the worlds of the ten directions."

Commentary: What came above named this bodhisattva in terms of the person; this line names this bodhisattva in terms of the dharma itself. This dharma is exactly the dharma of cultivation and realization — through my own contemplating and illuminating the ear-faculty, this nature of hearing, perfectly illuminating the whole Dharma-realm, nothing but this wondrous, awakened brightness — sentient beings of the ten directions who recollect my own name are, without exception, perfectly and brightly known by me, not through any deliberate thought or intention on my part at all, bestowing happiness and drawing out suffering, freely accomplished — and so sentient beings of the ten directions, every one of them, call upon my own name, and this name Avalokiteśvara has thereby spread throughout the worlds of the ten directions. Sentient beings of the ten directions all share a karmic affinity with this bodhisattva, worshipping, recollecting, and making offerings more than to any other bodhisattva at all. The Buddha Manifests an Auspicious Sign 子三 佛現瑞應

〖經〗爾時世尊，於師子座，從其五體，同放寶光，遠灌十方，微塵如來及法王子，諸菩薩頂。

Sūtra: At that time the World-Honored One ... said to Mañjuśrī, Dharma-Prince: "You have now heard these twenty-five great bodhisattvas and arhats, beyond further training, each describe the means by which they first attained the path — all of them cultivating genuine perfect penetration, their practices, in truth, without any real difference of superior or inferior, earlier or later."

Commentary: This is the Buddha commanding Mañjuśrī to make this selection. Earlier, in the section on awakening to and entering perfect penetration, it was already said: "attaining the perfect faculty, compared with an imperfect one, differs as much as a single day from a whole kalpa," and further: "select carefully, among these, whichever one you find yourself able to enter through, and I shall make this plain for you, so that you may advance." Because Ānanda did not know which faculty was truly the most perfect one, the Buddha, though quietly indicating the ear-faculty, found Ānanda still failing to grasp this — and so Ānanda requested that the Tathāgata give one final, direct instruction, withdrawing his own quiet hint, hoping instead for the Buddha's own explicit teaching. And so the Buddha commanded every sage to give their own account of their causal-stage cultivation and realization, each one declaring this to be foremost. Ānanda, once again, remained at a loss — and so the Buddha commanded Mañjuśrī to select among these approaches to perfect penetration. Mañjuśrī, being the very great wisdom at the very root, and, in ages past, the teacher of seven buddhas, receives this command to make this selection for two reasons: first, though the Buddha had earlier had everyone enter deeply through a single gate, he had, in the end, never yet explicitly stated which gate this was — and now, with every gate set forth together, it is fitting, in principle, that a single gate be determined once and for all. Second, though every sage's own account describes a genuine skillful means, it is a pity that not every one of them matches this particular world's own capacity and wisdom — only Avalokiteśvara's own method precisely suits this occasion — and so this one selection determines, once and for all, to set aside every other gate, and take up the ear-gate alone.

And so the Tathāgata specially addressed Mañjuśrī, Dharma-Prince: you now behold these twenty-five bodhisattvas and arhats — all called "beyond further training," since, for one of the perfect teaching, cultivation itself is identical with there being nothing further to cultivate. "Each describe the means by which they first attained the path, all of them cultivating genuine perfect penetration": each one describes their own initial resolve, all the way to attaining the path itself — nothing other than the eighteen elements of sense-faculty, sense-object, and consciousness, together with the seven great elements, taken up as their own respective starting point, this skillful means — each one declaring that, relying on this same cultivation, genuine perfect penetration was ultimately attained in every case, each calling their own method foremost.

"Their practices, in truth, without any real difference of superior or inferior, earlier or later": the practice each of these sages cultivated, and the perfect penetration each of them realized, are, in the final analysis, one and the same, without any real distinction of superior or inferior, without any real difference at all. Though returning to this same single origin admits of no duality, in actual practice these skillful means are many-gated indeed — and the particular gate each one entered through does differ, in skill or clumsiness, in swiftness or slowness. This is exactly like a thousand paths and nine great roads, all alike converging upon the royal capital. Judged by this alone, these twenty-five gateways should admit of no selection at all!

元明照生所，所立照性亡：此二句能依妄源，即最初根本不覺，亦即獨頭生相無明，一切妄法，皆依他而生，故此句元明二字，躡上文而來。元明，不妄生照用，則是本明，毫無一物，今於彼元明性上，妄生照用，此照是妄照，即經前所云：「性覺必明，妄為明覺。」生所者，因妄照而妄所生，即前所云：「覺非所明，因明立所」。以所照之妄境既立，而真照之性遂亡，即所謂有相當情，無相即隱，如迷雲起，必障蔽於慧日也。遂將本有如來藏，轉成無明藏識也。

〖講〗此明從真起妄，妄成世界、眾生、併業果，三種相續之相。以其妄所既已成立，則轉本有之智光，而成能見之妄見，欲見本識，不知本識，卒不可見，遂迷性空，而妄成頑空，故曰迷妄有虛空，即前晦昧為空是也；親依無明，虛空先現耳。次句依空立世界者：以本識既不可見，而定欲見之，空見相對，堅執欲緣，如瞪目發勞，依虛空晦昧，結暗境而成四大之色法，故曰，依空立世界；即前空晦暗中，結暗為色是也。

想澄成國土，知覺乃眾生者：上句，即溫陵所謂：妄想凝結，成無情國土也。下句，謂妄想知覺，成有情眾生也。此依無明藏識，生起依、正二報，而有見、相二分。《指掌疏》云：且約本識中細相言之，而不言麤相者，以其示迷途未遠，見歸源之易也。

〖經〗空生大覺中，如海一漚發，有漏微塵國，皆依空所生，漚滅空本無，況復諸三有？

〖講〗前四句，明諸法忽生；後二句，明諸法還滅，即返妄歸真之意。前迷妄有虛空，則虛空生於大覺心中，僅如海之一漚發現而已。以覺心，喻之如海；虛空，喻之如漚，其大小為何如耶？有漏微塵國，皆依空所生者：有漏，即前之有情世界，具足欲漏、有漏、無明漏也。微塵國，即前之器世界，皆依虛空之所生，不出空外，益見其微劣與虛妄矣！此四句，即前引起塵勞煩惱，起為世界，靜成虛空，虛空為同，世界為異，彼無同異，真有為法。

漚滅空本無，況復諸三有者：空性如漚，有生必滅，究之生滅，亦屬妄見；漚滅，虛空本無所有，況復空中，諸三有世間耶？三有即三界，謂欲有、色有、無色有也。妙理無端，妄成三界，如水結成冰，物而不化，故謂之曰有。又取中三界九地，則為九有：廣開四洲四惡趣，六欲併梵天，四禪四空定，無想阿那含，則為二十五有也。又依空同體，安危事一，故滅則俱滅。是知漚性不實，滅之仍歸於海，則知空性本無，滅之仍歸如來藏性也。

〖經〗歸元性無二，方便有多門。聖性無不通，順逆皆方便，初心入三昧，遲速不同倫。

〖講〗上明可以還滅，已引歸元之路；此明圓通順逆之意，意謂：但能滅除妄識境界，自可復歸本來元有如來藏性；既歸藏性，其理則無有二。設若欲入此無二之理，其方便自有多門；如京畿是一，入路多歧也。

聖性無不通，順逆皆方便者：諸聖證入此性，則無有不通；以三科七大，或順修而入，或逆修而入，二十五門，皆為方便之門，如千逕九逵，皆達帝京也。孤山曰：「觀音耳根則順，餘聖諸根則逆。對此方之機為順，不對此方之機為逆也。」《正脈》云：「順塵識流，宛轉達道曰順入，則六塵六識，火大至識大圓通也。如順背京之路，繞遠方到者也。逆根性之外流，而旋返入性曰逆入，即六根及根大圓通也。如逆背京之路，回身即到者也。請觀諸根圓通，俱有旋反字面可見矣！

初心入三昧，遲速不同倫者：此二句，正見須選；以上所云，順逆皆方便，所謂十方如來，於一切法門，皆能證得，何須選擇！但初發心人，欲入三昧，指楞嚴大定，得循圓根，與不圓根，日劫相倍。以不得圓根，則艱難而遲，雖修數劫，不及一日，以得圓根則容易而速，但修一日，可當數劫。因此遲速難易，自不同倫，又不可以不選也。初發源開選竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "The nature of the sea of awareness is calm and perfectly complete; perfectly complete, this same calm awareness is originally wondrous; this original brightness gives rise to illumination, and to what is illuminated; and, this illuminated object established, the very nature that illuminates is thereby lost."

Commentary: The first two lines here describe the true source relied upon — exactly this original, true mind already possessed, exactly the tathāgatagarbha's own nature. This is exactly like an earlier passage, where, before distinguishing brightness from its absence, the sūtra first named this nature as originally twofold awareness, and, before refuting the conditioned and the unconditioned, first named these two words, "true nature." "Sea of awareness" should not be read as an analogy at all, but rather as directly naming the very nature of awareness itself as a sea — exactly as the Avataṃsaka Sūtra speaks of a "sea of buddha-lands," a "sea of kalpas," and so on. Were this taken as an actual sea of water, one would fall into the fault of an analogy that does not truly match what it illustrates — for this sea of awareness has no boundary at all, in any direction, and no bottom at all, in any depth, unlike a sea of water, which still has both a shore and a bottom. Of "the nature of the sea of awareness," the word "nature" points to the great aspect of substance; "calm and perfectly complete," "perfectly complete, calm," refer to the great aspect of characteristics; and "originally wondrous," "original brightness," speak of the great aspect of function. "Calm and perfectly complete" means that this sea of awareness, calm and unmoving, embraces the ten thousand things within it completely — this being exactly stillness that is nonetheless illuminating, unchanging while constantly according with conditions. "Perfectly complete, this same calm awareness" means that, though this sea of awareness embraces the ten thousand things completely, it remains, in the end, calm and unmoving — this being exactly illumination that is nonetheless still, according with conditions while constantly unchanging. "Wondrous" means originally wondrous of itself, not through any act of construction; "bright" means originally bright of itself, requiring no exertion of effort at all — and, being originally wondrous, originally bright, not a single thought arises, everything already complete exactly as it is, of itself. Within this, the word "wondrous" already carries the sense of "bright" within it — set against "calm and perfectly complete" above, "originally wondrous" carries this same sense, and "perfectly complete, calm" carries this same sense of "original brightness."

"This original brightness gives rise to illumination, and to what is illuminated; and, this illuminated object established, the very nature that illuminates is thereby lost": these two lines describe the false source relied upon — exactly the very first, most fundamental non-awakening, exactly this independent, arising mark of ignorance, from which every false dharma, without exception, arises in dependence. And so these two words, "original brightness," in this line, follow directly from the same words above. "Original brightness," not falsely giving rise to any illuminating function at all, is simply this original brightness itself, not a single thing present within it — yet now, upon this very nature of original brightness, a false illuminating function arises, and this illumination is itself a false illumination — exactly what the sūtra said earlier: "the nature of awareness is necessarily bright; mistakenly, this becomes 'awareness that is bright.'" "Gives rise to... what is illuminated" means exactly this: because of this false illumination, a false object illuminated arises — exactly what was said earlier: "awareness is not something to be illuminated; because of this illumination, an object is established." Once this false realm illuminated is thus established, this true, illuminating nature is thereby lost — exactly what is meant by: where a mark is present, feeling attaches to it; where no mark is present, it remains hidden — exactly as confused clouds, once they arise, are certain to obscure the very sun of wisdom. And so this original tathāgatagarbha, already possessed, is thereby transformed into the storehouse-consciousness of ignorance itself.

Sūtra: "Deluded by this falseness, empty space came to be; depending on this empty space, world-systems were established; thought, settling, formed these lands; and knowing and awareness became sentient beings."

Commentary: This makes plain how, from truth, falseness first arises, and this falseness comes to form world-systems, sentient beings, and the continuity of karmic fruit, three continuities in all. Because this false object had already been established, the original light of wisdom is thereby transformed into a false seeing capable of seeing — wishing to see this original consciousness, yet unable to know it, this original consciousness could never actually be seen at all, and so one grows deluded regarding the nature of emptiness, falsely forming, instead, a mere blank void — hence, "deluded by this falseness, empty space came to be," exactly what was called, earlier, "darkened and dulled into emptiness"; relying directly upon ignorance, empty space appears first of all. The next line, "depending on this empty space, world-systems were established": since this original consciousness could not be seen at all, and yet one remained fixed on wishing to see it, emptiness and this seeing stood opposed to one another, this stubborn attachment seeking something to condition — exactly like straining the eyes until they grow weary, staring fixedly — and, depending on this same darkened, dulled emptiness, congealing this dark state into the physical dharmas of the four elements — hence, "depending on this empty space, world-systems were established," exactly what was called, earlier, "within this darkness, congealing darkness into form."

"Thought, settling, formed these lands; and knowing and awareness became sentient beings": the first of these two lines is exactly what Wenling called false thought congealing to form the insentient lands; the second means false thought and awareness forming sentient beings, possessed of feeling. This arises in dependence on the storehouse-consciousness of ignorance, giving rise to both the circumstantial and direct retributions, and thereby to both the seeing-portion and the appearing-portion. As the Zhǐzhǎng commentary puts it: this speaks specifically of the subtle marks within this original consciousness, not of its coarser marks, so as to show that the path of delusion has not strayed far at all, and that finding one's way back to this same source remains easy.

Sūtra: "This empty space arose within the great sea of awareness, like a single bubble forming upon the ocean; every land, defiled, as numerous as motes of dust, arises depending on this same empty space; once the bubble bursts, this empty space itself was never truly there at all — how much less, then, the three states of existence themselves?"

Commentary: The first four lines make plain how every dharma suddenly arises; the last two make plain how every dharma returns to cessation — that is, the sense of returning from falseness to truth. In the passage above, "deluded by this falseness, empty space came to be" — this empty space arises within the great sea of the mind of awareness itself, merely like a single bubble appearing upon the ocean. The mind of awareness is likened here to the ocean; empty space is likened to the bubble — how could their size ever be compared? "Every land, defiled, as numerous as motes of dust, arises depending on this same empty space": "defiled" points exactly to the world of sentient beings described above, complete with the outflow of desire, the outflow of existence, and the outflow of ignorance. "Lands as numerous as motes of dust" point exactly to the world of physical vessels described above, each and every one arising in dependence on this same empty space, never once stepping outside it — making all the more plain just how minute and insubstantial, how utterly false, this all truly is! These four lines are exactly what was described earlier as giving rise to worldly toil and affliction, arising to form world-systems, settling to form empty space — empty space being what is shared in common, and world-systems being what differs from one place to the next; apart from these, there is neither sameness nor difference, only the true, unconditioned dharma itself.

"Once the bubble bursts, this empty space itself was never truly there at all — how much less, then, the three states of existence themselves?": the nature of emptiness is exactly like this bubble — whatever arises is certain to cease, and, examined closely, this very arising and ceasing is itself nothing but a false view. Once the bubble bursts, empty space itself was never truly there at all — how much less, then, the three states of existence within this same empty space? The three states of existence are exactly the three realms — existence within desire, existence within form, and existence within formlessness. This wondrous principle, having no beginning at all, falsely forms these three realms — exactly like water congealing into ice, a thing that no longer melts away, hence called "existence." Taking, further, the middle three realms' own nine grounds, these become nine states of existence; broadly expanding this to the four continents, the four evil destinies, the six desire heavens together with the Brahmā heaven, the four dhyānas, the four formless absorptions, the heaven of no-thought, and the non-returners' own heavens, this becomes twenty-five states of existence in all. And, depending on this same emptiness, sharing one and the same substance, safety and danger are, in the end, a single matter — and so, once this ceases, all of it ceases together. Knowing, then, that the bubble's own nature was never real to begin with, its cessation returning it, in the end, to the very ocean itself, one thereby knows that the nature of emptiness, too, was never truly there to begin with, its cessation returning it, in the end, to the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha itself.

Sūtra: "Returning to this single origin, this nature admits of no duality — yet the skillful means for doing so are many-gated indeed. Every sage's own realized nature is, without exception, fully penetrated — whether entered with the stream or against it, each is itself a skillful means; yet, for one just beginning to enter samādhi, swiftness and slowness are by no means the same."

Commentary: What came above made plain that returning to this cessation is indeed possible, already pointing the way back to this same original source; this present passage makes plain the meaning of perfect penetration's own accordance and reversal, meaning exactly this: simply extinguish the false, discriminating state of consciousness, and one may, of oneself, return to this same original, already-possessed nature of the tathāgatagarbha. And once this nature of the tathāgatagarbha is returned to, its principle admits of no duality at all — yet, should one wish to enter this same non-dual principle, the skillful means for doing so are many-gated indeed, exactly as a single capital city may still be reached by many different roads.

"Every sage's own realized nature is, without exception, fully penetrated — whether entered with the stream or against it, each is itself a skillful means": every sage, realizing and entering this nature, finds nothing whatsoever left unpenetrated — whether through the three categories and seven great elements, entered by cultivating with the stream, or entered by cultivating against it, all twenty-five gateways alike serve as gateways of skillful means, exactly like a thousand paths and nine great roads, every one of them reaching the imperial capital. As Master Gushan put it: "Avalokiteśvara's own ear-faculty is entered with the stream; every other sage's own faculty is entered against it — matched to this particular world's own capacity is 'with the stream'; not matched to this particular world's own capacity is 'against the stream.'" As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: "following the flow of sense-object and consciousness, winding one's way, in the end, to the path itself, is called entering with the stream — that is, perfect penetration through the six sense-objects and six consciousnesses, and through the great elements of fire up through consciousness; exactly like following the road leading away from the capital, circling far around before finally arriving there. Going against the sense-faculty's own outward flow, and turning back to enter this nature directly, is called entering against the stream — that is, perfect penetration through the six sense-faculties and through the great element of the faculties; exactly like going against the road leading away from the capital, turning around and arriving there at once." Observe how every one of the sense-faculties' own accounts of perfect penetration carries this same language of turning and reversing, and this distinction becomes plain at once!

"Yet, for one just beginning to enter samādhi, swiftness and slowness are by no means the same": these two lines make plain exactly why a selection is needed at all. Given what was said above — that whether entered with the stream or against it, each is itself a skillful means, and that every Tathāgata of the ten directions is able to realize any Dharma-gate whatsoever — what need, then, could there possibly be for any selection at all? But for one just beginning to resolve on practice, wishing to enter samādhi (referring here to the Śūraṅgama's own great samādhi), attaining the perfect faculty, as against an imperfect one, differs as much as a single day from a whole kalpa. Failing to attain the perfect faculty, one finds the path difficult and slow — though one may cultivate for several kalpas, this falls short of even a single day's progress through the perfect faculty; attaining the perfect faculty, one finds the path easy and swift — cultivating for a single day alone may equal several kalpas of progress otherwise. Because of this same difference in swiftness and slowness, in difficulty and ease, these paths are by no means equal — and so a selection cannot but be made. This closes the first section, opening this selection at its very source.

Sūtra: "Form, born of thought, congeals into a sense-object; the essence of mind can never see through it directly. How, then, could something never clearly seen through bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: The Buddha said earlier that these sages' own practices carry no real difference of superior or inferior at all — this was spoken in terms of every sage alike having realized the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha. But now, for a beginning practitioner seeking to cultivate and realize samādhi, there is indeed a real difference of swiftness and slowness, of difficulty and ease — and so a selection cannot but be made! This section examines and sets aside the six sense-objects' own accounts of perfect penetration. First, Upaniṣada, awakening through contemplating the emptiness of form's own nature. This means: form relies on nothing but false thought, congealing to form itself, becoming an obstructing sense-object — should one attempt to see through it directly with the very essence of mind, one could never bring it to full clarity in the end; for the very substance of this form-object is, at its root, formed from congealed darkness — how, then, could a beginning practitioner ever rely on so unclear, so impenetrable a thing, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Sound itself is mixed together with language; it carries nothing but names, phrases, and their flavor of meaning; a single one of these can never contain every other. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Second, Ajñātakauṇḍinya, awakening through hearing the sound of the Dharma. "Sound" here means unmediated sound itself; "language" means sound bent into speech — and this one thing, sound, cannot avoid being mixed together with language and written words. "It carries nothing but names, phrases, and their flavor of meaning": a name expresses a thing's own essential nature — a single word taken directly as a label, such as saying "vase," without specifying which vase; a phrase expresses some particular distinction — such as adding further words, "flower vase"; and a sentence expresses a connected whole, many words joined together. What is here called "flavor" is exactly the flavor of meaning expressed through this same phrasing and structure — carrying nothing more than this same name, phrase, and flavor of meaning. And a single name can never contain every other name within it, a single phrase can never contain every other phrase within it, a single meaning can never contain every other meaning within it — how, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so limited a thing, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Fragrance is known only in contact; apart from contact, it was never there to begin with; the awareness of it can never be constant. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Third, Gandhavyūha, the Fragrance-Adorned Youth, awakening through the sense-object of fragrance. Fragrance can only be known through the nose-faculty when the two make contact; should they be apart, not in contact, there is originally no fragrance there at all. And the sense-faculty capable of awareness can never remain constantly in contact with the sense-object it is aware of — when the sense-object makes contact, this awareness is present; when the sense-object departs, this awareness disappears along with it entirely — how, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so inconstant a thing, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "The very nature of taste is not something inherent of itself; it appears only at the moment of tasting; this awareness can never remain constantly one with it. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Fourth, Bhaiṣajyarāja and Bhaiṣajyasamudgata, awakening through the sense-object of taste. Yet the very substance and nature of this taste-object is not inherent of itself, knowing that it has flavor on its own. "Inherent of itself" means spontaneous, self-existing — only when this taste-object makes contact with the tongue-faculty does one come to know that it has flavor at all. The tongue-faculty's own nature, capable of this awareness, can never remain constantly one with what it is aware of — how, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so inconstant a thing, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Touch is made plain only through what is touched; without something touched, touch cannot be made plain at all; whether in contact or apart, this nature is never fixed. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Fifth, Bhadrapāla, awakening through the sense-object of touch. Yet this touch-object carries no fixed nature of its own — only when the body-faculty, capable of awareness, makes contact with some external, unaware object does one come to feel cold, warmth, roughness, smoothness, and every other such favorable or unfavorable mark, and only then is this properly called "touch." The first "touch" here refers to the touch-object itself; the second "touch" refers to whatever external object the body-faculty comes into contact with — and so this means: the touch-object can only be made plain and manifest through some object the body-faculty comes into contact with. "Without something touched, touch cannot be made plain at all": lacking any object touched, the mark of this touch-object could never be made plain at all. This nature of contact or separation is, by its own nature, never fixed at all — how, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so inconstant a thing, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Mental objects are called an inner sense-object; relying on this object, there must always be something it depends on; subject and object here can never fully interpenetrate one another. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Sixth, Mahākāśyapa, awakening through the mental object. This mental object is not the same substantial reality as the outer five sense-objects — it is exactly the shadow-remainder left behind by these five sense-objects, settling within the mental sphere alone, conditioned by the mind alone; combined with the outer five sense-objects, this belongs entirely to mental objects, an isolated shadow-object — hence called an inner sense-object. "Relying on this object, there must always be something it depends on": relying on the outer five sense-objects' own shadow-remainder, there is always some remaining shadow it depends upon — the outer sense-object being what leaves this remainder behind, and the inner sense-object being this remainder itself. But there are five outer sense-objects, and their remainders must arise in some sequence, one before another; there are, correspondingly, five inner sense-objects, whose shadows are likewise not without their own order. When mental conditioning first arises, it fixes upon only a single object at a time, releasing one to take up the next — subject and object here can never fully interpenetrate or pervade one another at once — how, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so limited a thing, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "The nature of seeing, though utterly clear, sees what is in front, but not what is behind; of the four intermediate directions, it lacks fully half. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Of the six sense-faculties, only five are named here, since the ear-faculty alone remains, held back to be selected. First, Aniruddha, awakening through the nature of seeing. Yet the nature of this seeing-faculty, that is, the eye-faculty, though utterly clear and penetrating, if considered in terms of the four cardinal directions, sees only what lies in front, and to the left and right, but not what lies behind — hence, "sees what is in front, but not what is behind." Considered, further, in terms of the four intermediate directions, it sees only the two in front, not the two behind — hence, "of the four intermediate directions, it lacks fully half": three parts in ten hold merit, one part in ten holds none. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so imperfect a faculty, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "The breath through the nose passes out and in; yet, at the very moment before you, there is no breath crossing at all; broken apart this way, it cannot fully interpenetrate. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Second, Kṣudrapanthaka, awakening through contemplating the nose-faculty. This "breath" is exactly the air passing through the nose — going out, it passes to the outside; coming in, it passes to the inside — hence, "passes out and in," exactly what was said earlier: there is an outward breath and an inward breath. "At the very moment before you, there is no breath crossing at all": at the very moment the outward breath is exhausted, it cannot immediately turn inward; at the very moment the inward breath is exhausted, it cannot immediately turn outward — and, at this brief pause between the outward and inward breath, called "the very moment before you," there is no breath crossing over at all, and hence no merit present there — exactly what was said earlier: "lacking any crossing point in between." Judged by this, three parts in ten hold merit, and one part is lacking entirely. "Broken apart this way, it cannot fully interpenetrate": and, further, broken into separate parts, each with its own separate basis — an outward breath without an inward one, an inward breath without an outward one — and, at this very point where they break apart, they cannot interpenetrate one another at all. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so imperfect a faculty, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "The tongue does not enter without some cause; through taste alone does its awareness arise; taste gone, this awareness is nowhere to be found. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Third, Gavāṃpati, awakening through the tongue-faculty. As Wenling put it: the tongue is not able to become aware apart from taste on its own accord — this is exactly what is meant by "without some cause." "Without some cause" means exactly without any cause at all — that is, this tongue-entrance does not, without cause, come to know flavor; only because the taste-object makes contact with the tongue-faculty does this aware knowing then arise. Should the taste-object vanish away, this aware knowing is originally nowhere to be found at all — how, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so inconstant a faculty, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

The Standard Meaning Commentary raises a question: the ear, apart from sound, still hears stillness, and this is said to be its own basic nature — why, then, is the tongue, apart from taste, tasting blandness, not likewise said to be constant? The answer: the ear's own capacity for awareness apart from its object is constant and universal throughout — apart from sound, hearing stillness, this being even more perfectly complete than hearing sound itself, something people easily grasp, as the sūtra says elsewhere: "whether moving, near or far, stillness has no boundary at all." The tongue-faculty's own awareness of taste, by contrast, extends no further than these three inches of tongue — even its awareness in contact is already inferior, and its awareness apart from contact, this bland flavor, is fainter and dimmer still — how, then, could it ever be compared to the ear's own constant nature, so plainly manifest?

Sūtra: "The body-faculty is exactly the same as what it touches; neither one, on its own, is a perfect awareness or a perfect contemplation; each has its own bounded limit, unable to merge with the other in the dark. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Fourth, Pilindavatsa, awakening through contemplating the truth of suffering by way of the body-faculty. This body-faculty is exactly the same as the touch-object it possesses — as an earlier stanza said: "touch is made plain only through what is touched; without something touched, touch cannot be made plain at all." Now the body-faculty stands in exactly this same position: the body's own capacity for awareness likewise depends on what it touches to be made manifest; lacking any touch-object, it cannot be made manifest at all. "Neither one, on its own, is a perfect awareness or a perfect contemplation": the single word "perfect" governs both "awareness" and "contemplation" together — "perfect awareness" is an independent, complete substance; "perfect contemplation" is an absolute, complete wisdom. This body-faculty and the touch-object described earlier are, neither one, this perfect awareness or this perfect contemplation. This is precisely because the knowing that arises only in contact depends on sense-faculty and sense-object meeting one another to be manifest at all — and so the touch-object described earlier, apart from this body-faculty, has its own mark hidden away entirely, certainly no independent, complete substance, nor any absolute, complete wisdom either. And this body-faculty, apart from the touch-object described earlier, likewise has its own knowing dissolved away entirely, likewise no independent, absolute complete substance or complete wisdom. These two lines together make plain that knowing arises only once contact occurs. "Each has its own bounded limit, unable to merge with the other in the dark": further, the body-faculty and the touch-object, one belonging to what has knowing, the other to what lacks it, each has its own bounded limit, each its own measure, unable, once separated, to merge together in hidden accord — this line makes plain that, once separated, there is no longer any knowing at all. Present in contact, absent apart — this nature is never fixed at all. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so inconstant a faculty, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "The mind-faculty is mixed together with scattered, disordered thought; a state of calm, clear knowing can never actually be found there at all; thought and its notions cannot be shed. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Fifth, Subhūti, awakening through the mind-faculty. "The mind-faculty" refers exactly to the faculty of mental knowing, that is, the mind-faculty itself. The mind-faculty is exactly the seventh, manas consciousness; the sixth consciousness relies on this seventh as its own root, hence "mind-faculty." "Mixed" means intermingled; "scattered, disordered thought" is exactly the sixth, discriminating consciousness — this consciousness being, among every consciousness, the most disordered, the strongest, and the hardest of all to tame. "A state of calm, clear knowing can never actually be found there at all": this means that, even shedding this discriminating consciousness entirely, a state of calm, clear knowing can, in the end, never actually be found there at all — and so thought and its notions cannot all be shed away at once. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so mixed and disordered a faculty, never fully shed, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Seeing-consciousness is mixed together from three things meeting at once; inquire into its own root, and it is found to have no real mark at all; its own substance was never fixed to begin with. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: This examines and sets aside the six consciousnesses. At the very start of this sūtra, the six consciousnesses were named as the very root of birth and death, not something to be mistakenly relied upon in cultivation — and so discarding consciousness and relying on the sense-faculty became this sūtra's own central purpose. Yet each sage's own account of perfect penetration still included all six consciousnesses among the twenty-five, showing that every sage's own realized nature is, without exception, fully penetrated, and that whether entered with the stream or against it, each is itself a skillful means. Mañjuśrī now sets these aside once more, precisely because, for one just beginning to enter samādhi, swiftness and slowness are by no means the same. First, Śāriputra, awakening through the eye-consciousness. "Seeing-consciousness" should properly read "seeing, consciousness" — "seeing" belongs to the eye, that is, this is exactly eye-consciousness. "Mixed together from three things meeting at once": sense-faculty and sense-object meet one another, and consciousness arises between them — subject and object spoken of together. This is exactly the eye's own instantaneous, moment-by-moment discrimination — outwardly facing the form-object, inwardly facing the eye-faculty, and, arising falsely in between, this instantaneous, coarse discrimination is exactly the eye-consciousness. Since it depends on its own substance, and relies, in turn, on sense-faculty and sense-object both, hence, "mixed together from three things meeting at once." "Inquire into its own root, and it is found to have no real mark at all": that is, inquiring into the very root of this consciousness thus arisen, it is found to come from nowhere at all. First, it does not arise from the sense-faculty alone — for the sense-faculty alone, without any sense-object, cannot give rise to it by itself. Second, it does not arise from the sense-object alone — for the form-object, itself without any knowing, cannot be the cause that gives rise to consciousness. Third, it does not arise jointly from sense-faculty and sense-object combined — for the sense-faculty possesses knowing, while the sense-object lacks it entirely, and so any consciousness thus jointly arisen ought to be half with knowing and half without — yet this is not in fact the case at all. And so, examined as a whole, this consciousness proves entirely false and unreal, and is therefore said to have no real mark at all. Its own substance was never fixed to begin with — how, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so inconstant a consciousness, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "This mind's own hearing penetrates the ten directions completely — yet this arises from a great, causal power built up over ages; a beginning practitioner cannot enter through it. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Second, Samantabhadra Bodhisattva, awakening through the ear-consciousness. "This mind's own hearing" is exactly the ear-consciousness, able to penetrate the ten directions completely, hearing perfectly without any obstruction at all — yet all of this arises from cultivating the practice of the Dharma-realm, accomplished entirely through this same great, causal power built up over ages; it is by no means the case that the ear-consciousness itself is naturally capable of such a function — this requires, rather, a bodhisattva already at a profoundly advanced stage. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so distant, so long-accumulated a cause, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "This contemplation through the nose was, from the start, a provisional skillful means, meant only to gather in and settle a scattered mind; once settled, this becomes a mind that dwells on something. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Third, Sundarananda, awakening through the nose-consciousness. "Contemplation through the nose" means holding, at the very tip of the nose, this contemplation of whiteness. "From the start, a provisional skillful means": this was, from the very start, a skillful, provisional method, set up matched to a particular capacity, not something the nose-consciousness itself inherently possesses — the nose-consciousness's own function being simply to discriminate fragrant from foul. The next line describes exactly the intent behind this provisional method: meant only to gather in this scattered mind, bringing it to a temporary rest, nothing more. The third line says: once there is a mind capable of dwelling, this whiteness at the tip of the nose becomes exactly the object it dwells upon. But the true mind dwells upon nothing at all — how, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on this mind that dwells upon something, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Expounding the Dharma plays with sound and written words alone; Pūrṇa's own great awakening was already accomplished before this; names and phrases are not, themselves, undefiled dharmas. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Fourth, Pūrṇa, awakening through the tongue-consciousness. The tongue-consciousness, in expounding the Dharma, does nothing more than play with sound, spoken language, and written words. And Pūrṇa's own great awakening, his own accomplishment of arhatship, came, in fact, from a power of eloquence already built up across boundless kalpas beforehand — this being exactly how he was able to accomplish this at all, not through any single moment's function of the tongue-consciousness itself. Names, phrases, and written words belong to the category of factors dissociated from mind, classed among conditioned dharmas, not undefiled dharmas at all. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so conditioned a dharma, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Keeping or breaking the precepts governs only the body; whatever is not of the body, this cannot restrain at all; from the start, it never reaches every dharma. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Fifth, Upāli, awakening through the body-consciousness. "Keeping or breaking the precepts" means keeping the precepts against sexual misconduct, killing, and stealing, wishing to remain pure, never allowing these to be transgressed — yet this restrains only the body-consciousness, nothing beyond its own scope. As for false speech, idle chatter, harsh speech, and divisive speech, the body-consciousness has no way to restrain these at all — this does not even fully extend to the two karmic acts of speech and mind, how much less, then, to a bodhisattva's own pure moral discipline, all the way to the eighty-thousand subtle practices, every Dharma-gate there is? Hence, "from the start, it never reaches every dharma." How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so limited a Dharma-gate, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it? The Zhǐzhǎng commentary raises a question: Upāli held to the body first, and then, in sequence, held to the mind as well, attaining mastery of both alike — why, then, does this verse speak only of restraining the body? The answer: this is spoken specifically in terms of a beginning practitioner. In general, when a beginner first takes up the precepts, sexual misconduct, killing, stealing, and false speech must be severed first — false speech may still be dealt with somewhat more gradually, but sexual misconduct, killing, and stealing are the most urgent of all. Yet holding to the mind directly is indeed difficult — and so one first uses the body-consciousness to restrain the body. Once the body-consciousness no longer gives rise to any deviant impulse, free of any evil contact, sexual misconduct, killing, and stealing naturally cease to occur at all — this is exactly restraining the body. But as for the mind stirring with the impulse toward sexual misconduct, killing, or stealing, or the mouth uttering false or idle speech, it is by no means the case that these are not meant to be severed as well — it is only that the body-consciousness alone cannot hold these in check, and not everyone succeeds at this in the same way. How, then, could Upāli have entered perfect penetration by relying on this alone?

Sūtra: "Spiritual powers themselves have their root in causes accumulated in past lives; what has this to do with mental discrimination? Thought that conditions an object is never apart from that same object. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Sixth, Mahāmaudgalyāyana, awakening through the mind-consciousness. What cannot be fathomed is called "spiritual"; what moves freely, without obstruction, is called "power." Though Mahāmaudgalyāyana's own spiritual powers arose from turning consciousness back to its own calm, clear source, the light of his own mind bursting forth — tracing this to its own deep root, this came, in fact, from causes accumulated and long cultivated across past lives. And so, upon encountering the Buddha and hearing the Dharma, he attained accomplishment at once — one thereby knows this had its own long history behind it, having nothing whatsoever to do with the mind-consciousness itself! "Mental discrimination" is exactly the mind-consciousness — the discrimination that arises facing the mental object, conditioning it thought after thought; present when joined to this mental object, absent when apart from it — hence, "thought that conditions an object is never apart from that same object," "object" here being exactly this mental object itself. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so conditioned, so grasping a consciousness, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Should one contemplate the nature of earth, it is a solid, obstructing thing, not something that penetrates through anything at all; it is a conditioned thing, not the sage's own true nature. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: This examines and sets aside the seven great elements' own accounts of perfect penetration — the first five great elements paired with sense-objects, the sixth paired with consciousness, and the seventh paired with the sense-faculties. First, Dharaṇiṃdhara Bodhisattva, awakening through the great element of earth, through leveling roads. Should one contemplate the very nature of the great element of earth, this state proves to be a solid, congealed, obstructing thing, not a mark that penetrates through anything at all. Dharaṇiṃdhara's own leveling and filling of roads still belongs to conditioned activity, not yet the true, undefiled nature itself — "sage" here meaning exactly "true" — only afterward, encountering the teaching of leveling the mind itself, did he return to this true, sagely nature. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so conditioned a dharma, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Should one contemplate the nature of water, it is formed entirely through thought and mental notions, not the true, real, suchness-nature of unmoving thusness; and thusness itself is never reached through discriminating awareness or contemplation. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Second, Candraprabha, the Youth of Moonlight, awakening through contemplating the great element of water. Should one take the very nature of the great element of water as the object of contemplation, this state is formed entirely through thought and mental notions, not the true, real principle of unmoving thusness itself. To accord with this principle of unmoving thusness, one must attain the very wisdom of unmoving thusness itself — yet giving rise to a discriminating mind, to awareness and contemplation, fails to accord with it at all — hence, "thusness" can never be entered through the discriminating awareness and contemplation belonging to the six consciousnesses. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on this discriminating, contemplating mind, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Should one contemplate the nature of fire, revulsion at existence is not the same as true freedom from desire; this is no skillful means for a beginning practitioner. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Third, Ucchuṣma, awakening through contemplating the great element of fire. Should one take the very nature of the great element of fire as the object of contemplation — Ucchuṣma's own revulsion at the fire of lust, seeking thereby to leave desire behind, only reached true freedom from desire once body and mind were both entirely severed, and even this very severing itself no longer remained. But not every beginning practitioner is caught by so heavy a habit of lust — some have little desire, others none at all — why, then, should they necessarily rely on this same method as their own skillful means? How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so unsuited a dharma, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Should one contemplate the nature of wind, motion and stillness are never without some counterpart opposing them; and whatever stands opposed to a counterpart is not the unsurpassed awakening itself. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Fourth, Ratnaprabha, Dharma-Prince, awakening through contemplating the great element of wind. Should one take the very nature of the great element of wind as the object of contemplation — though he contemplated everywhere, within and without, and found all of it turned by nothing but the power of this same false, conditioned wind, this great element of wind still has both motion and stillness, and so belongs to the cycle of arising and ceasing, an impermanent dharma, never without some counterpart opposing it. And, having some counterpart opposing it, it can hardly be the very substance of unsurpassed awakening itself. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so impermanent a dharma, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

The Standard Meaning Commentary raises a question: does not the Dharma-gate of turning hearing back likewise enter through motion and stillness — how, then, does it differ from this? The answer: that method gradually sheds both sense-objects of motion and stillness, so as to take up the very nature of hearing, itself free of motion and stillness, as its own skillful means for a beginning practitioner. This present method, by contrast, takes up the very nature of wind, which still has both motion and stillness, as its own gate of entry — and so the two differ greatly indeed; how, then, could one ever use this same objection against the other?

Sūtra: "Should one contemplate the nature of emptiness, its own dull benightedness is, from the start, no awareness at all; and what lacks awareness differs entirely from bodhi itself. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Fifth, Ākāśagarbha Bodhisattva, awakening through contemplating the great element of space. Should one take the very nature of the great element of space as the object of contemplation, closely observing how the four elements have nothing to depend upon, how false thought itself arises and ceases, how empty space admits of no duality, how every buddha-land is, at root, the same — this very empty space itself, though, is exactly what darkened dullness produces: "dull" means exactly obscured, and "benighted" means exactly inert. Taking this obscured inertness as its own mark, its own substance is, from the start, not this same function of a spiritually bright, aware knowing at all. "What lacks awareness differs entirely from bodhi itself": lacking this function of spiritually bright, aware knowing, it differs, of itself, entirely from bodhi. The Sanskrit bodhi translates as "the path of awakening" — exactly this unsurpassed path of awakening realized through perfect penetration itself. To now seek this same bright, aware fruit through a cause of dull benightedness is no different at all from drilling into ice to get fire. How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so unaware a thing, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Should one contemplate the nature of consciousness, this consciousness observed is never something constantly abiding; and the very mind that holds it in mind to observe is itself already false. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Sixth, Maitreya Bodhisattva, awakening through contemplating the great element of consciousness. Should one take the very nature of consciousness as the object contemplated, closely observing how the ten directions are nothing but consciousness alone — yet this very nature of consciousness, arising and ceasing moment after moment without pause, is, in truth, never a constantly abiding nature at all. And the very mind that holds this in mind to observe it is already false to begin with — how much more so, then, this great element of consciousness observed, which could hardly fail to be false as well? How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on so inconstant a mind, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

Sūtra: "Every conditioned activity is impermanent; the very nature of this recollection is, from the start, subject to arising and ceasing; cause and fruit here, at this present moment, do not correspond to one another. How, then, could this bring one to perfect penetration?"

Commentary: Seventh, Mahāsthāmaprāpta, Dharma-Prince, through the great element of the faculties. According to the usual order, the great element of the faculties ought properly to come sixth — yet here Maitreya's great element of consciousness is placed ahead of it, and Mahāsthāmaprāpta's great element of the faculties comes last of all, showing plainly how this bodhisattva, knowing full well that he wished to cultivate the Śūraṅgama's own great samādhi, deliberately yielded the final, most honored place to Avalokiteśvara alone — seeking, instead, the one method able to reach beings of sharp and dull capacities alike, benefiting all three roots of capacity together: this very method of recollecting the Buddha. Whatever involves movement and change belongs entirely to conditioned activity, and every conditioned activity is impermanent. Yet Mahāsthāmaprāpta himself said: gathering in all six sense-faculties together at once, this pure recollection continuing without a break — and, being this same "gathering in together," this belongs exactly to the great element of the faculties. One can see, from this, that recollecting the Buddha is neither mere recitation with the mouth, nor recollection with the sixth, discriminating mind of consciousness, but rather the mind-faculty gathering in all six sense-faculties together to recollect. Though called "pure recollection," this remains, in the end, a recollection with something still present in it; and, being called "continuing," it can hardly avoid arising and ceasing — hence, "the very nature of this recollection is, from the start, subject to arising and ceasing." Taking this recollection of the Buddha as the cause, and rebirth in the Pure Land as the fruit, cause and fruit correspond to one another perfectly, and so of ten thousand who cultivate it, ten thousand attain rebirth there. But should one take this same cause, subject to arising and ceasing, and seek thereby to directly realize perfect penetration, which is itself unborn and unceasing, cause and fruit would stand at odds with one another, stimulus and response no longer corresponding — hence, "cause and fruit here, at this present moment, do not correspond to one another." How, then, could a beginning practitioner rely on this mind, subject to arising and ceasing, and hope to swiftly attain perfect penetration through it?

As the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it: considered as a whole, all twenty-four sages, judged by what they themselves realized, must stand equal to Avalokiteśvara — yet, tracing the reason their own gateways of entry were not taken from this same root faculty, four conditions may be given in brief for why they must be set aside here: first, not matched to this particular world's own capacity; second, not well suited to a beginning practitioner; third, requiring some further support besides; fourth, not something that can be cultivated as an ordinary, sustained practice. This, in reverse, shows the ear-faculty to be exactly matched to this world's own capacity, well suited to a beginning practitioner, requiring no further support, and cultivable as an ordinary, sustained practice for all. This same intention appears again in the verse that follows, anticipated here in advance. This closes the second section, examining and setting aside these gateways in turn.

The Faculty Matched to This World 辰初 根隨方利

Chinese Source

〖經〗我今白世尊：佛出娑婆界，此方真教體，清淨在音聞。欲取三摩提，實以聞中入。

〖講〗上來餘門，既經揀去，此則應選耳根，故云：我現今重白世尊言：佛出娑婆世界，此云堪忍，以娑婆眾苦具足，眾生堪能忍受諸苦故。如來必隨一方機宜，而立教體。設教不應機，化則不勝。準維摩經云：或有佛土，以光明而作佛事；或有佛土，以佛菩提樹而作佛事；乃至或以園林臺觀，或以虛空影響，或以音聲語言，或以寂無言說，各隨機宜，教體不同。今佛世聞聲得益者多，即末世亦從此方眾生耳根偏利，能由聞性，遍達無量差別理事。故對此一方機宜，以音聲施作佛事，所以透彼聞根之利也。如來在世，聞教得益者多，即末世亦從開示，而得悟入者眾；意言常途但以音聲為教體，是隨相假體。而此方真實教體，清淨本然，周遍法界者，惟在聽音之聞性而已。

欲取三摩提，實以聞中入者：良以教詮藏性，而聞性最切近藏性者也。然領悟雖以雙託音聞，而修定但宜單取聞性，故凡欲取證三摩提，即楞嚴大定，實在要以耳根聞性得入。如觀音自敘：彼佛教我，從聞、思、脩，入三摩地。於是依教，初於聞中，做入流照性工夫，解六結而越三空，破五陰而獲二勝，是則從說選根以來，直至此處，方以決定指出耳根，為圓通本根，至妙之法門矣！

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "I now say to the World-Honored One: where the Buddha appears, in the Sahā world, the true substance of teaching in this land lies in purity found through sound and hearing. To take hold of samādhi, one truly enters through hearing itself."

Commentary: Every other gate having already been examined and set aside above, this present passage now selects the ear-faculty itself, and so it says: I now say again to the World-Honored One: the Buddha appears in the Sahā world — translated "able to endure," since this Sahā world is complete with every kind of suffering, and its sentient beings are able to endure and bear all of it. The Tathāgata, wherever he appears, is certain to establish his own teaching according to that particular world's own capacity — for should the teaching fail to match the capacity, its transformative power falls short. As the Vimalakīrti Sūtra puts it: in some buddha-lands, light itself carries out the Buddha's own work; in others, the Buddha's own bodhi tree carries it out; in others, gardens, groves, terraces, and pavilions; in others, empty space and echoing sound; in others, spoken sound and language; in others, silence, without any speech at all — each matched to its own particular capacity, the very substance of teaching differing from world to world. Now, in the Buddha's own age, those who benefited from hearing his teaching were many, and, even in this final age, it is likewise from this same world's own sentient beings, whose ear-faculty is especially sharp, that one is able, through the very nature of hearing, to reach every boundless distinction of principle and phenomenon. And so, matched to this particular world's own capacity, sound itself is used to carry out the Buddha's own work — this being exactly how the sharpness of this hearing-faculty is brought fully to bear. While the Tathāgata remained in the world, those who benefited from hearing his teaching were many; and, even in this final age, it is likewise through his own teaching that many attain awakening and entry. This means: in the ordinary course of things, sound alone serves as the substance of teaching — yet this is merely a substance borrowed according to outward appearance. The true substance of teaching in this particular world, originally pure of itself, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, lies in nothing but this very nature of hearing that hears sound.

"To take hold of samādhi, one truly enters through hearing itself": this is precisely because the teaching expresses the very nature of the tathāgatagarbha, and the nature of hearing lies closest of all to this same nature of the tathāgatagarbha. Yet, though understanding itself may rest on both sound and hearing together, cultivating samādhi should properly take up the nature of hearing alone — and so, whoever wishes to take hold of and realize samādhi, that is, the Śūraṅgama's own great samādhi, must, in truth, enter through the ear-faculty's own nature of hearing. As Avalokiteśvara recounted directly: that buddha taught me to enter samādhi through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating. And so, relying on this same teaching, at first, within hearing itself, carrying out the effort of entering the stream to illuminate this nature directly, untying the six knots and passing beyond the three emptinesses, breaking through the five aggregates and attaining these two supreme qualities — and so, from the very moment this selection among the sense-faculties began, all the way to this very point, the ear-faculty is now decisively pointed out as the very root of perfect penetration, the most wondrous Dharma-gate there is!

This particular world's own sentient beings already have a sharp ear-faculty, and the Tathāgata already carries out his own work through sound — and so whoever hears this sound ought, by rights, to be freed from turning endlessly in the wheel! Yet, helplessly, sentient beings remain deluded regarding this original hearing — "sentient beings" here refers specifically to those given to mere intellectual cleverness, deluded and unawakened, unable to grasp the path through mere words, unable to hear the teaching and contemplate the mind directly; the very hearing capable of hearing is exactly this wondrous, bright mind-nature itself — yet they simply follow along with whatever sound-teaching they hear, only adding further idle theorizing, and so bring this endless turning upon themselves. "Ānanda, for all his own strength of memory, could not avoid falling into deviant thought": this cites an actual case as proof — Ānanda himself, for all his own prodigious memory, holding every Tathāgata's own twelve divisions of scripture throughout the ten directions, this pure, wondrous principle as numerous as the sands of the Ganges, still could not avoid falling into deviant thought, turned about by mere objects, nearly drowning within a brothel, entirely without freedom of his own. "Is it not exactly this: following what one sinks into, or turning the stream back to attain freedom from falseness?": the words "is it not" pick up, decisively, everything said above; the eight words that follow name the affliction and its remedy together, coming down to nothing more than being deluded regarding this original hearing, or not being deluded regarding it. Deluded, one follows what one sinks into — "following" being exactly this same "following after," "what" being exactly the sound-object, and "sinks into" being exactly this endless turning — meaning that following after sound inevitably leads to this same endless turning, exactly as every buddha, speaking with one and the same voice, has declared: "Ānanda, should you wish to know it, this innate ignorance, arising together with you, is exactly what turns you in this wheel — the very root of the knot of birth and death is nothing but your own six sense-faculties, nothing else at all." Not deluded, one turns the stream back instead — turning back this same faculty of hearing, turning hearing back to hear its own nature. "Attain freedom from falseness" means attaining this true, ever-abiding nature, never turning in this wheel at all — exactly as every buddha has further declared: "and should you further wish to know it, this unsurpassed bodhi, which brings you to swift realization, peaceful, blissful liberation, the still, wondrous, ever-abiding — this, too, is nothing but your own six sense-faculties, nothing else at all." These two lines are, again, exactly what another verse elsewhere puts: "delusion and obscurity are exactly ignorance itself; bringing this to light is exactly liberation itself." Should one be able to bring this to light, turning the stream back, turning hearing back upon itself, one thereby attains this same great, unobstructed liberation. For a beginning practitioner, at every moment, in every place, turning solely toward the ear-faculty, turning hearing back to hear one's own nature — there is nothing else to it at all — and, of itself, news of one's own arrival home follows naturally. How could this not be both swift and easy to cultivate? This closes the first section, fully displaying the wonder of this gate.

〖經〗聞非自然生，因聲有名字，旋聞與聲脫，能脫欲誰名？

〖講〗此科法說。當修反聞，如何修、如何證？先明超越情界，次示超越器界。聞非自然生者：聞指妄聞，乃攬塵所結之根，非是自然而生也。因聲有名字者：因於動、靜二種妄塵，黏起湛然一精明之體，發為聽精，聽精映聲，捲聲成根，由此無始來，遂有耳根之名字，即根元；目為清淨四大，因名耳體，乃聚聞結滯為根之體。旋聞二句，言既因攬聲而結，必待脫聲而解。旋觀能聞之聞性，而與所聞之聲塵脫離，即入流亡所。乃至動靜二相，瞭然不生，旋倒聞根，反聞自性，而與聲塵脫離也。能脫即指聞根，前因攬塵而結，茲因旋根而脫；欲誰名者，言塵已脫，而根隨盡，更有誰名為根也？此二句即當如是漸增聞所聞盡，併能聞耳根之名亦失，惟一妙性而已。《正脈》云：此四句喻水本因寒而結冰，故冰須脫寒而還水矣！

〖經〗一根既返源，六根成解脫。

〖講〗此如後三漸文云：「塵既不緣，根無所偶。」亦即返流全一，六用不行，人疑但解一根，餘根尚結，不知根雖有六，體本是一；此橫論六根，結則同結，解則齊解，六根同一成壞也。正同豎論六結，每一根，約迷真起妄，從性至塵。由細向麤，皆有次第六結；約返妄歸真，從塵至性，由麤向細，亦有次第六結；如一結成時，六根同成一結，乃至六結皆然；或一結解時，六根齊解，一結乃至六結皆然。解結者，但從耳根，逆流而入。六結：一動、二靜、三根、四覺、五空、六滅。此處，一根既已返本還源，覺性現前，不復循塵結根，於六結，但解動、靜、根三結，則六根俱成解脫。是則一根返源，豈不至簡耶？六根齊脫，豈不至要耶？夫至六根皆脫，則眾生世界，已自不能纏縛矣！

Sūtra: "Hearing does not arise on its own — it is given its very name because of sound. Turn hearing back, and free it from sound; once freed, what name would even apply to it?"

"Hearing does not arise on its own": "hearing" here refers to false hearing, exactly this sense-faculty congealed by grasping at sense-objects, not something that arises spontaneously of itself at all. One should know that, although there are six faculties, their own substance is, at root, one and the same — considered horizontally, across all six faculties, when knotted, they are knotted together, and when untied, they are untied together, all six faculties sharing one and the same formation and dissolution. The illusion performed is exactly this conjuring trick, likened here to the ignorance that brings about actual events. The men and women conjured are exactly these figures the illusionist creates, likened here to the six sense-faculties. Yet, among these various gates, there are cases where the Buddha's own majestic blessing is plainly shown — as when Aniruddha lost his sight, and the Buddha showed him the light of joyful seeing; or when Kṣudrapanthaka lacked the ability to recite, and the Buddha taught him to regulate the breath and gather in the mind. This differs from the "body and mind bright and clear" described after the verse in fascicle five — there, body and mind were made bright and clear specifically regarding the sequence of the knot's own formation and the sequence of its own untying, yet it was not yet known which single gate was the very gate to enter through, nor by what method one ought to cultivate. This present "clear" carries the sense of a sudden, thorough understanding. Having heard Mañjuśrī's own verse of selection, plainly pointing out — why not turn that hearing which retains the Buddha's teaching back upon your own hearing? and — for accomplishing the mind of nirvāṇa, Avalokiteśvara's way is best — they now understand clearly that the very gate to enter through lies in the ear-faculty alone, and that cultivation and realization lie solely in turning the stream back. And, further, understanding that, once the ear-faculty is untied, the sense-faculties of all six locations are untied together with it — this is exactly "body... clear," since the sense-faculty is itself the body; and understanding that, once the nature of hearing is restored, the nature of all six locations is restored together with it — this is exactly "mind... clear," since this nature is itself the mind. "Received this great instruction": because they had not yet known, before this, which was the very gate to enter through, hoping instead for the Buddha's own hidden transmission of it — and so the Tathāgata commanded every sage to give their own account of their own gate, and then had Mañjuśrī make this plain selection among them, striking obliquely, borrowing indirect words to convey it — and, reaching this point at last, they attained this sudden, thorough understanding, this achievement entirely due to receiving this great instruction.

"Beholding the Buddha's own bodhi and great nirvāṇa": the explanation for this has already appeared earlier and need not be repeated. Now, having received this great instruction, beholding the Buddha's own twin fruits of wisdom and severance, this method of cultivation and realization, clear and no longer obscured — and so an analogy is given to make this plain. "Exactly like a person": likening Ānanda and the whole assembly, before a single thought had yet stirred, dwelling securely in their own home village of bodhi and nirvāṇa — until, suddenly, ignorance and non-awakening, a single thought stirring at once, the three subtle marks arising vigorously, the six coarse marks contending with one another — turning their backs on awareness to join with sense-objects, flowing outward and forgetting the way back — exactly like traveling far away on some matter, journeying back and forth through the six destinies, month after month, year after year, not only failing to return home, but even the very road back to this home village nearly forgotten entirely. Now, fortunate enough to encounter the Tathāgata's own every kind of instruction, and to hear Mañjuśrī's own commanded selection, though not yet attaining perfect penetration directly, a resembling understanding has nonetheless arisen — already knowing the sequence of untying the knot at the ear-faculty, that turning the stream back attains freedom from falseness at once — exactly like coming to understand clearly one's own home, and the road that leads back to it. Though not yet dressed for the triumphant journey home, they have, nonetheless, already come to know this very road home — exactly this unsurpassed vehicle's own wondrous path of practice!

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtra: "Throughout the whole assembly gathered there — the eight kinds of heavenly and dragon spirits, those still in training within the two lesser vehicles, and every bodhisattva newly resolved on bodhi, numbering, in all, as many as ten Ganges' sands — every one of them realized their own original mind, leaving sense-objects far behind and departing from defilement, attaining the pure Dharma-eye."

Commentary: This speaks specifically of Mātaṅgī's own class of capacity, their own afflictive obstruction light, and so able to realize this directly, at once. Yet, for a practitioner of the perfect and sudden teaching, awakening itself reaches all the way through, though realization still admits of shallow and deep degrees. Mātaṅgī, having earlier heard the mantra and been sustained by its own inconceivable power, realized the third fruit directly, at once — and here, hearing this verse, awakening at once, and, riding this same awakening, severing her own remaining habits of thought directly, at once, and so is classed here together with this same group. Whenever this sūtra recounts a realization, it generally proceeds from the deeper to the shallower — and these three groups here, considered specifically in terms of the perfect teaching's own stages, are: first, the first of the ten abidings; second, the seventh of the ten faiths; third, the first of the ten faiths. "Throughout the whole assembly gathered there" names, in summary, every capacity present within this assembly. "The eight kinds of heavenly and dragon spirits" ought to be sharp-facultied ordinary beings; "those still in training within the two lesser vehicles" ought to be beginning practitioners not yet fixed in their own vehicle; and "every bodhisattva newly resolved on bodhi" ought to be beginners of the perfect teaching itself. These beings' own past causes run genuinely deep, and though their present stage remains shallow, all alike belong to those with a light afflictive obstruction, and so are able, riding this same awakening, to realize this directly — their number reaching as many as ten Ganges' sands, one may well say how many, indeed, whose capacity had already ripened! "Every one of them realized their own original mind": every one of them awakened to and realized this same, already-possessed, true mind — this mind being exactly this perfectly calm, unborn, unceasing nature found at the very gateway of the six sense-faculties themselves; the same mark of mind Ānanda and the others attained at the close of fascicle three, called their own original, wondrous mind — only, there, they merely awakened to it, while here, it is genuinely realized. This is precisely because, for one of the perfect teaching, the initial and the final mind are, in the end, no different at all — and so, at the very first abiding, one already realizes this same, already-possessed, true mind directly! "Leaving sense-objects far behind and departing from defilement, attaining the pure Dharma-eye": as the Standard Meaning Commentary puts it, this is exactly what fascicle four called the marks of perception as sense-object, and the sentiments of consciousness as defilement, both left far behind together — the awareness and what it was aware of becoming empty leaves sense-objects far behind; emptiness and what was emptied being extinguished departs from defilement. "Attaining the pure Dharma-eye": the Adornment Treatise explains the pure Dharma-eye as the stage of the path of seeing at the first ground — and, according to the perfect teaching, this is exactly the very first of the ten abidings. For, once arising and ceasing are themselves extinguished, still cessation appearing of itself, this is exactly the pure Dharma-eye itself. This stage is exactly realizing, all the way through, the causal-stage mind of perfect penetration, forming the very first step of the fruit stage — exactly what the close of fascicle four called: "your own Dharma-eye will, at that very moment, grow clear and bright."

Sūtra: "The bhikṣuṇī Prakṛti, upon hearing this verse spoken, attained arhatship."

Commentary: Prakṛti is exactly Mātaṅgī herself — "left the household," in our own language, named bhikṣuṇī, "nun" meaning exactly "woman." Upon hearing Mañjuśrī speak this verse, she advanced further still, attaining arhatship — that is, exactly the seventh of the ten faiths of the perfect teaching, borrowing this lesser vehicle's own title to name this greater, ordinary stage. Considered in terms of perfect penetration, this corresponds exactly to this same faculty's own first untying, first attaining the emptiness of self — the text here corresponding precisely to "both the hearing and what is heard were exhausted."

Sūtra: "And boundless sentient beings, every one of them, resolved upon this unequaled, equal, unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening."

Commentary: This describes bodhisattvas newly resolved on bodhi, exactly the first of the ten faiths of the perfect teaching. Their number, called "boundless," makes their own inconceivability all the more plain. As the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom puts it: asama, translated "unequaled, equal," since the fruit of buddhahood, bodhi itself, is precisely unequaled — meaning that no dharma whatsoever could ever stand equal to it. Now, since every one of them has been able to resolve upon this very mind of accomplishing buddhahood, this is called: regarding what admits of no equal at all, now able to equal it — hence, "unequaled, equal." Anuttarā-samyak-saṃbodhi, joined together, forms exactly the single word "buddha" — this very title standing alone, above and beyond all nine Dharma-realms. Explained from the last term upward: "saṃbodhi" translates "correct awakening," surpassing the six ordinary destinies' own non-awakening; "samyak" translates "correctly equal," surpassing the two lesser vehicles' own inability to hold self and other equally, seeking only their own benefit, unwilling to benefit others; "anuttarā" translates "unsurpassed," surpassing even a bodhisattva's own remaining superior — for even a bodhisattva of equal enlightenment still has wondrous enlightenment above them, still counted a "superior being with something above them still" — only the Buddha, this one alone, stands worthy of this title in full. The Precious Mirror Commentary puts it: what this present capacity had sought, up to this point, was called only a road, or a gate, or, at most, a bestowed dwelling — not yet knowing that there was, all along, an original home of one's own. Now, after hearing of the ear-faculty's own perfect penetration, the sūtra's own compiler speaks directly of a "home" — displaying precisely how this present assembly has come to understand their own mind clearly, something never received from another at all, its own meaning exactly like an original home, not to be compared with any merely bestowed dwelling. And so this very turning of hearing back through the ear-faculty is exactly the path and gate by which one returns to nirvāṇa's own homeland, back to bodhi's own home. This closes the first great section, selecting the sense-faculty and entering directly.
