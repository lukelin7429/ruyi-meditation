The Avataṃsaka Sūtra Complete

Samantabhadra's great gāthā closes with four last similes: the ocean filled with jewels in which every being's image appears, just as every being's reflection appears in the ocean of a buddha's own merit; the sun that lights the ten directions without ever moving, just as a buddha's light removes the world's darkness without ever coming or going; and the nāga king's rain, which arises from neither body nor mind and yet drenches everywhere, just as the Tathāgata's own rain of dharma opens every mind without arising from body or mind at all. The gāthā turns at last to the dharma-body itself, neither existent nor non-existent, beyond every likeness the three realms could offer, displayable only by such ultimate terms as emptiness, suchness, and nirvāṇa; and it closes where the whole sūtra closes, with the declaration that no one could ever finish speaking a buddha's own merit, and Samantabhadra's own final word, that whoever hears of this ocean of merit with a joyful and trusting mind will surely attain all that has been said of it, and should never doubt it.

The last four similes of the great gāthā — the ocean, the sun, the nāga king's rain, and the dharma-body itself — and Samantabhadra's own closing word.

Chinese Source

譬如大海寶充滿，
清淨無濁無有量；
四洲所有諸眾生，
一切於中現其像。

佛身功德海亦爾，
無垢無濁無邊際；
乃至法界諸眾生，
靡不於中現其影。

譬如淨日放千光，
不動本處照十方；
佛日光明亦如是，
無去無來除世暗。

譬如龍王降大雨，
不從身出及心出，
而能霑洽悉周遍，
滌除炎熱使清涼。

如來法雨亦復然，
不從於佛身心出，
而能開悟一切眾，
普使滅除三毒火。

如來清淨妙法身，
一切三界無倫匹；
以出世間言語道，
其性非有非無故。

雖無所依無不住，
雖無不至而不去；
如空中畫夢所見，
當於佛體如是觀。

三界有無一切法，
不能與佛為譬諭；
譬如山林鳥獸等，
無有依空而住者。

大海摩尼無量色，
佛身差別亦復然；
如來非色非非色，
隨應而現無所住。

虛空真如及實際，
涅槃法性寂滅等；
唯有如是真實法，
可以顯示於如來。

剎塵心念可數知，
大海中水可飲盡，
虛空可量風可繫，
無能盡說佛功德。

若有聞斯功德海，
而生歡喜信解心，
如所稱揚悉當獲，
慎勿於此懷疑念。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as the great ocean is filled with jewels,
pure and unclouded, measureless in its vastness,
and every being of the four continents
has their image appear within it,

so too is the ocean of merit that is a buddha's own body,
stainless and unclouded, without any boundary;
and there is no being of the whole dharma-realm
whose reflection does not appear within it.

Just as the pure sun sends forth a thousand rays of light,
without moving from its own place, illuminating the ten directions,
so too is the light of the buddha's own sun:
without going and without coming, it removes the darkness of the world.

Just as a nāga king sends down a great rain,
which comes forth neither from his body nor from his mind,
and yet is able to moisten and drench everywhere entirely,
washing away the burning heat and bringing coolness,

so too is the rain of dharma of the Tathāgata:
it comes forth neither from the buddha's body nor from his mind,
and yet is able to open and awaken every assembly,
universally causing the fire of the three poisons to be extinguished.

The Tathāgata's own pure and wondrous dharma-body
has no equal in all the three realms;
because it follows the path of language that transcends the world,
its nature is neither existent nor non-existent.

Though it has nothing on which it depends, there is nothing in which it does not abide;
though there is nowhere it does not reach, it never goes anywhere;
like a painting in the sky, or what is seen in a dream,
one should regard the buddha's own body in just this way.

No dharma at all, whether existent or non-existent, within the three realms,
is able to serve as a likeness for the buddha;
just as, among the mountains, the forests, the birds, and the beasts,
there is none that abides by depending on empty space.

The measureless colors of the maṇi jewels within the great ocean,
and the distinctions of a buddha's body, are likewise the same;
the Tathāgata is neither form nor non-form,
and appears according to what is fitting, abiding nowhere.

Empty space, suchness, and the true limit,
nirvāṇa, the nature of dharmas, and quiescence—
only such true dharmas as these
are able to display something of the Tathāgata.

The motes of dust of a land, or the thoughts of a mind, could be counted and known;
the water within the great ocean could be entirely drunk;
empty space could be measured, and the wind could be bound;
yet none is able to speak the whole of a buddha's own merit.

If there is one who hears of this ocean of merit
and gives rise to a joyful mind of faith and understanding,
they shall surely attain all that has been extolled;
be careful never to harbor any doubting thought concerning this."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

」 — the closing bracket at the very end of these last stanzas closes Samantabhadra's own speech, and with it the sūtra's own narrative comes to an end: no further prose follows before the fascicle's colophon. The gāthā that fills nearly the whole of fascicle eighty, beginning in Part 1336 with a single drop from the ocean of a buddha's merit, closes on its own image made explicit at last, an ocean whose merit could never be spoken in full even were every mote of dust counted, every drop of the ocean drunk, and the wind itself bound.

剎塵心念可數知，大海中水可飲盡，虛空可量風可繫 — the four impossibilities named in the second-to-last stanza, counting the dust of a land or the thoughts of a mind, drinking the ocean dry, measuring empty space, and binding the wind, are given as a rising scale of the unthinkable, each more impossible than the last, before the stanza's own conclusion: that even so, none of it could exhaust the speaking of a buddha's merit. Samantabhadra's very last words, addressed to the assembly and, through it, to whoever reads this translation, are a warning against doubt rather than a further teaching.
