Ten Past Buddhas, Ten Named Palaces

Relying on the Buddha's own power, the King of the Tuṣita Heaven recollects wholesome roots planted in the presence of buddhas long past, and speaks a verse of ten identical stanzas — each naming a different buddha who once entered a differently named palace, and each entry alone declared the very reason that particular place became the most auspicious of all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 194 — the king's verse of ten past buddhas Source: Taishō T279 (Śikṣānanda, Tang, 695–699 CE)

Auspiciousness Traced to a Single Visit

The king's verse follows one unvarying template ten times over: a named buddha of the past, called "most supreme among every auspicious sign," who once entered a specific, differently named palace — and it is that single visit alone, the verse insists each time, that made the place auspicious. Ten buddhas, ten palaces, one identical claim repeated with only the names changing.

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the King of the Tuṣita Heaven, relying upon the Buddha's own awesome power, at once recollected the wholesome roots he himself had planted in the presence of buddhas of the past, and spoke a verse, saying:

"In the past there was a Tathāgata named Unobstructed Moon, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Adorned Palace, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all. In the past there was a Tathāgata named Vast Wisdom, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Golden-Colored Palace, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all.

In the past there was a Tathāgata named Universal Eye, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Lotus Palace, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all. In the past there was a Tathāgata titled Coral, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Treasury Palace, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all.

In the past there was a Tathāgata, the Lion Among Expounders, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Palace of the King of Mountains, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all. In the past there was a Tathāgata named Sun's Illumination, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Palace of Many Flowers, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all.

Ru-Yi Translation

In the past there was a Buddha titled Boundless Light, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Tree-Adorned Palace, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all. In the past there was a Tathāgata named Dharma Banner, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Jeweled Palace, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all.

In the past there was a Tathāgata named Lamp of Wisdom, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Fragrant Mountain Palace, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all. In the past there was a Buddha titled Light of Merit, most supreme among every auspicious sign; he once entered this very Maṇi Palace, and for this reason this place is the most auspicious of all."

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

Each stanza names a different palace — the Adorned Palace, the Golden-Colored Palace, the Lotus Palace, the Treasury Palace, and so on through ten — suggesting either ten distinct halls within the same vast complex or ten successive names this same site has borne across the visits of ten different buddhas. Either reading fits the chapter's own logic: auspiciousness here is not an inherent property of a place but a trace left behind by a buddha's own presence, accumulating one visit at a time until a single palace carries the memory of ten.
