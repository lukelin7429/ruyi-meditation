Monks Who Would Rather Talk of Kings

Her origin story opens the same way the last teacher's did — an ancient eon, a world, a buddha, a wheel-turning king. But this account moves faster into decline: a true dharma failing not through persecution but through what its own monks would rather talk about.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: Part 1137 — an eon named Free of the Stain of Light Source: Taishō T279 (Śikṣānanda, Tang, 695–699 CE)

A World Shaped Like a Lotus

Good-Wealth asks the question every teacher in this chapter eventually gets asked, and the answer opens onto an eon and a world built, this time, in the shape of a flower.

Chinese Source

時，善財童子白夜神言：「奇哉！天神！此解脫門如是希有！聖者證得，其已久如？」夜神言：「善男子！乃往古世，過世界轉微塵數劫，有劫名：離垢光明，有世界名：法界功德雲，以現一切眾生業摩尼王海為體，形如蓮華，

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, Good-Wealth said to the night-spirit: "How marvellous, heavenly spirit! This liberation-gate is so rare indeed! How long is it since the noble one attained it?" The night-spirit said: "Good son, in an age long past, more eons ago than there are fine motes of dust in the turnings of worlds, there was an eon named Free of the Stain of Light, and a world named Cloud of the Merit of the Dharma-Realm, having for its very substance an ocean of maṇi kings manifesting the deeds of all beings, and shaped like a lotus flower.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

過世界轉微塵數劫 — more eons ago than there are fine motes of dust in the turnings of worlds. This exact measure occurs only once in the whole of T279's eighty fascicles. Every other origin story in this fascicle has counted back in fine motes of dust of buddha-lands or of a single world; this one alone counts by 世界轉, the turnings or revolutions of worlds — the cycles of forming and dissolving this sūtra elsewhere calls 成壞. A hapax like this is easy to read past, but it marks the one place in the whole chapter where the unit of counting is not a piece of matter but a completed cycle of cosmic time.

Nets of Fragrant Maṇi, Cities Beyond Counting

The world's construction continues, layered the way every such world in this sūtra is layered — held up by something, ringed by something, and populated past any hope of a final count.

Chinese Source

住四天下微塵數香摩尼須彌山網中，以出一切如來本願音蓮華而為莊嚴，須彌山微塵數蓮華而為眷屬，須彌山微塵數香摩尼以為間錯，有須彌山微塵數四天下，一一四天下有百千億那由他不可說不可說城。

Ru-Yi Translation

It rested within a net of Sumerus of fragrant maṇi as numerous as the fine motes of dust of four continents, adorned with lotus flowers that gave out the sound of every tathāgata's original vow, with lotus flowers as numerous as the fine motes of dust of Mount Sumeru as its retinue, and fragrant maṇi jewels as numerous as the fine motes of dust of Mount Sumeru set between them. It held sets of four continents as numerous as the fine motes of dust of Mount Sumeru, and each single set of four continents had a hundred thousand koṭis of nayutas of ineffably ineffable cities.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

百千億那由他不可說不可說城 — a hundred thousand koṭis of nayutas of ineffably ineffable cities, in each single one of an already Sumeru-dust-many sets of four continents. This is nesting on top of nesting, and it is worth pausing on the fact that a single city, in a fascicle so far concerned with buddhas, kings and night-spirits, is about to be found and named among all of this. The vastness is not decorative; it is the scale the story has to be told at before the single particular case — one continent, one capital, one buddha — can be picked out of it.

One Continent, One King

Out of that uncountable field, the story settles on a single set of four continents, a single capital, and the first of Sumeru-dust-many buddhas to appear there.

Chinese Source

「善男子！彼世界中，有四天下，名為：妙幢；中有王都，名：普寶華光；去此不遠，有菩提場，名：普顯現法王宮殿。須彌山微塵數如來於中出現，其最初佛，名：法海雷音光明王。彼佛出時，有轉輪王，名：清淨日光明面，於其佛所，受持一切法海旋修多羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son, within that world there was a set of four continents named Wondrous Banner. In it there was a royal capital named Light of Universal Jewelled Flowers; and not far from there was an awakening-site named Palace of the Dharma-King Universally Manifest. Tathāgatas as numerous as the fine motes of dust of Mount Sumeru appeared there, and the first of those buddhas was named King of the Light of the Thunder-Sound of the Dharma-Ocean. At the time that buddha appeared, there was a wheel-turning king named Face of the Light of the Pure Sun, who at that buddha's place received and upheld the sūtra Whirlpool of the Ocean of Dharma.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

普顯現法王宮殿 — Palace of the Dharma-King Universally Manifest. Good-Wealth's fourth question to this teacher, back in Part 1133, was 云何近法王位, how does one draw near to the throne of a dharma-king. The awakening-site where her whole story is about to unfold is named for exactly that palace, before any king or teacher in the story has done anything to earn it. The setting answers the question before the narrative does.

What Happens After a Buddha Dies

The buddha passes into nirvāṇa, and the king who once held his sūtra becomes its guardian — until the guarding itself begins to fail, not from outside attack but from within.

Chinese Source

佛涅槃後，其王出家，護持正法。法欲滅時，有千部異眾千種說法。近於末劫，業惑障重；諸惡比丘多有鬪諍，樂著境界，不求功德，樂說王論、賊論、女論、國論、海論，及以一切世間之論。

Ru-Yi Translation

After the buddha's parinirvāṇa, that king went forth from home and safeguarded the true dharma. When the dharma was about to perish, there arose a thousand divergent communities teaching a thousand different doctrines. As the eon drew near its end, the hindrance of deeds and afflictions grew heavy; the evil monks among them were given to much contention, delighted in sense-objects, and sought no merit, preferring instead to talk of kings, to talk of thieves, to talk of women, to talk of the state, to talk of the sea, and every other kind of worldly talk.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

樂說王論、賊論、女論、國論、海論，及以一切世間之論 — preferring instead to talk of kings, of thieves, of women, of the state, of the sea, and every other kind of worldly talk. This is a fixed and ancient list, the same category of idle discourse condemned across the earliest Buddhist literature as unworthy of a renunciant's time. Its appearance here, inside a Mahāyāna sūtra otherwise occupied with oceans of dust and buddhas beyond counting, is a small shock of the plain and the recognisable. The dharma does not fail here because Māra attacks it. It fails because monks would rather talk about the news.

有千部異眾千種說法 — there arose a thousand divergent communities teaching a thousand different doctrines. The schism is quantified before the moral decline is even described — a thousand groups, a thousand teachings — and the fragmentation of doctrine is named as the first symptom, with worldliness following as the second. A dharma that has split into a thousand mutually disagreeing schools is already dying before anyone in it starts gossiping about kings.

The King-Monk's Protest

One person in the story refuses to let this pass without saying so — and what he does about it is not argument but a display that buys the true dharma another sixty-five thousand years.

Chinese Source

「時，王比丘而語之言：『奇哉！苦哉！佛於無量諸大劫海集此法炬，云何汝等而共毀滅？』作是說已，上昇虛空，高七多羅樹，身出無量諸色焰雲，放種種色大光明網，令無量眾生除煩惱熱，令無量眾生發菩提心。以是因緣，彼如來教，復於六萬五千歲中而得興盛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"At that time the king who had become a monk said to them: 'How strange, how sorrowful! The Buddha, through measureless oceans of great eons, gathered together this torch of dharma — how can you, together, destroy it?' Having said this, he rose up into the sky to the height of seven tāla trees, and from his body came forth measureless clouds of flame of every colour, sending out a great net of light of every colour, causing measureless beings to be free of the heat of affliction, and causing measureless beings to give rise to the mind of awakening. By this cause and condition, that tathāgata's teaching flourished once more, for sixty-five thousand years.

Reading note — Ru-Yi Meditation Center

奇哉！苦哉 — how strange, how sorrowful! Two exclamations, and the second is the more unusual choice. A sūtra could easily have given the king-monk anger at the destruction of the teaching; instead the first honest word out of him is grief. 苦, the word for suffering itself, is what he calls watching a true dharma get talked away by its own custodians.

以是因緣，彼如來教，復於六萬五千歲中而得興盛 — by this cause and condition, that tathāgata's teaching flourished once more, for sixty-five thousand years. The rescue is not doctrinal. Nobody refutes the thousand competing schools or out-argues the monks who would rather discuss the sea. One person rises into the air and burns with light, and that alone is what buys the teaching sixty-five thousand more years. Across this whole fascicle, a display has repeatedly done the work an argument could not — Samantabhadra outshone a proud city in fascicle seventy without a word of doctrine, and here a single body of light does what none of the debate could.
