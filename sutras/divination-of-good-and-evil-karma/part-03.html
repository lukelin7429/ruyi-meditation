One Hundred and Eighty-Nine Fates

This page completes the sūtra's first fascicle. It carries the third and final wheel-mark — six wheels, eighteen numbered faces, cast three times and summed to reach one of one hundred and eighty-nine possible readings spanning present circumstance, past origin, and future destiny — together with the sūtra's own warning against practicing this method for profit, and the closing instruction for anyone whose every cast comes back unfavorable.

Six Wheels, and Why They All Come From One

Chinese Source

「善男子！若欲占察三世中受報差別者，當復刻木為六輪。於此六輪，以一二三、四五六、七八九、十十一十二、十三十四十五、十六十七十八等數，書字記之。一數主一面，各書三面，令數次第不錯不亂。當知如此諸數，皆從一數而起，以一為本。如是數相者，顯示一切眾生六根之聚，皆從如來藏自性清淨心一實境界而起，依一實境界以之為本。所謂依一實境界故，有彼無明。不了一法界，謬念思惟，現妄境界，分別取著，集業因緣，生眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、意等六根。以依內六根故，對外色、聲、香、味、觸、法等六塵，起眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、意等六識。以依六識故，於色、聲、香、味、觸、法中，起違想、順想、非違非順等想，生十八種受。

「若未來世佛諸弟子，於三世中所受果報，欲決疑意者，應當三擲此第三輪相，占計合數，依數觀之，以定善惡。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Good son! If you wish to divine the distinctions in retribution received across the three times, you should further carve six wheels of wood. On these six wheels, inscribe the numbers one, two, three; four, five, six; seven, eight, nine; ten, eleven, twelve; thirteen, fourteen, fifteen; and sixteen, seventeen, eighteen. Each number governs one face; each wheel has three faces so inscribed, so that the numbers proceed in order without error or confusion. Know that all these numbers arise from the number one, taking one as their root. This pattern of numbers shows that the whole cluster of the six faculties of every sentient being arises from the one real realm of the pure, intrinsically clean mind of the tathāgatagarbha, and takes this one real realm as its root. That is to say: because of this one real realm, there is that ignorance which fails to understand the single dharma-realm, gives rise to mistaken thought, manifests a false and deluded realm, discriminates and grasps at it, and accumulates the causes and conditions of karma, thereby producing the six faculties of eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. Because these inner six faculties depend on this, they meet the outer six objects of form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and mental phenomena, giving rise to the six consciousnesses of eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. Because these depend on the six consciousnesses, in the midst of form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and mental phenomena, there arise the perceptions of what is contrary, what is agreeable, and what is neither contrary nor agreeable, giving rise to eighteen kinds of feeling.

"If disciples of the Buddha in ages to come wish to settle their doubts about the retribution received across the three times, they should cast this third wheel-mark three times, tally the total of the numbers cast, and, according to that total, observe and determine good or evil."

Reading note — after Zhixu

This is the one place in the whole sūtra where the divination method and its philosophical foundation are stated in a single breath, and Zhixu's Xuanyi leans on this passage more than any other to prove the two are not really two separate teachings joined by convenience. He treats the sūtra's own claim — that every number on these six wheels ultimately traces back to one — as a literal argument, not a decorative flourish: "一與一為二，二與一為三，三與一為四，四與一為五，乃至展轉無窮，無不從一起也" ("one and one make two, two and one make three, three and one make four, four and one make five, and so on without end — there is nothing that does not arise from one"), and then reads the sūtra's own account of how ignorance first divides the one real realm into a seer and a seen — "so that one becomes two" — as the same operation described abstractly: number itself, not just karma, is what delusion does to what was never actually divided.

He takes open aim at a rival cosmology in passing: the classical Chinese correlative reading of numbers, in which "Heaven's one gives birth to water, and Earth's six brings it to completion," mistakes as an external fact about the universe what is really a description of one's own mind — "不知吾心一念妄動，故不覺從一有五，非心外果有天也" ("not realizing that it is one's own mind's single deluded stirring that brings five out of one without their noticing, they fail to see that there is no 'Heaven' apart from the mind that could produce it"). On his reading, the wheel's eighteen numbered faces are not an arbitrary numerological convention borrowed from folk practice; they are the sūtra's own diagram of exactly how six faculties, six objects, six consciousnesses, and eighteen kinds of feeling — the entire machinery of ordinary experience — unfold, in strict order, from a single undivided root the sūtra names the tathāgatagarbha, the one real realm this whole collection turns to directly in Part 4.

Reading note — after Zhixu

Zhixu's Shu divides these one hundred and eighty-nine readings into exactly the three groups the sūtra itself signals by its next two sections: one hundred and sixty numbers covering retribution in the present life (readings 1–160, immediately above), eleven covering the realm one has come from in a past life (161–171, the next section), and eighteen covering the destiny awaiting one after death (172–189, the section after that). He treats the sheer breadth of the first group — spanning everything from which vehicle one is fit to practice, through mundane concerns of wealth, office, marriage, and companionship, to the success of a specific medical treatment — as itself a piece of doctrine: the method makes no distinction in principle between a question about final liberation and a question about whether a particular physician can cure a particular illness. Both are simply retribution, arising from the same causes and conditions, and both are equally proper matters for the same wheel.

"Good son! These are called the one hundred and eighty-nine kinds of mark distinguishing good and evil karmic retribution. In this method of divination, whatever matter one has fixed one's mind on observing, if the total cast corresponds to one's intention, there is no discrepancy or error. But if the total cast and summed does not correspond to the matter one had fixed one's mind on observing, it means one was not utterly sincere, and this is called a false and mistaken casting. And whoever, having cast three times, finds nothing at all appears, this person is called one who has already attained the attainment of nothing to attain."

Reading note — after Zhixu

Zhixu addresses directly the question this closing line seems to invite: if someone has already "attained the attainment of nothing to attain" — a description he identifies with an arhat's realization — why would such a person ever need to consult a wheel at all? His answer distinguishes two kinds of liberation: one who has attained the unconditioned wisdom of full liberation (俱解脫) genuinely has no further use for divination, but one who is liberated by wisdom alone (慧解脫) has attained cessation without the same depth of meditative mastery, and can, under sufficiently adverse conditions, still temporarily regress — he cites the case, reported elsewhere in the canon, of an arhat who came so close to falling back from his attainment seven times over that he finally took his own life rather than risk an eighth. Even a very high attainment, on this reading, is not immune to needing a genuine diagnostic tool; the wheel's value does not taper off once a practitioner has made real progress; if anything, what taper off are the more common causes of a false reading, since a mind that has already broken through self-grasping has correspondingly less left to obstruct its own sincerity.

Divining for Others, and the Sūtra's Warning Against Fortune-Tellers

Chinese Source

「復次，善男子！若自發意，觀於他人所受果報，事亦同爾。若有他人不能自占，而來求請欲使占者，應當籌量觀察自心，不貪世間，內意清淨，然後乃可如上，歸敬修行供養，至心發願而為占察。不應貪求世間名利，如行師道，以自妨亂。又若內心不清淨者，設令占察而不相當，但為虛謬耳。

「復次，若未來世諸眾生等，一切所占，不獲吉善，所求不得，種種憂慮，逼惱怖懼時，應當晝夜常勤誦念我之名字。若能至心者，所占則吉，所求皆獲，現離衰惱。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Furthermore, good son, if one sets one's own intention to observe the retribution received by another person, the matter is handled just the same way. If another person, unable to divine for themselves, comes and asks to have it divined for them, one should first weigh and examine one's own mind — not coveting worldly gain, one's inner intention pure — and only then may one, as described above, take refuge, practice, make offerings, and with utmost sincerity make one's vow before divining for them. One must not covet worldly fame and profit, acting like one who plies a trade as a fortune-teller, and thereby hinder and confuse oneself. And if one's inner mind is not pure, then even if one performs the divination, it will not correspond to anything, and will simply be false and mistaken.

"Furthermore, if sentient beings of the ages to come find that everything they divine turns out unfavorable, that what they seek is not obtained, and they are beset by every kind of anxiety, distress, and fear, they should constantly and diligently call and recite my name, day and night. If they are able to do so with utmost sincerity, then what they divine will turn favorable, what they seek will all be obtained, and they will presently be freed from misfortune."

Reading note — after Zhixu

Zhixu reads the warning against "acting like one who plies a trade as a fortune-teller" (如行師道) as the sūtra closing the loop it opened at the very start of Part 1: a method built to dissolve doubt collapses into exactly the worldly practice it explicitly disowns the moment it is performed for a fee or for reputation — "邪人行正法，正法亦成邪故" ("when a corrupted person practices even a correct Dharma, that correct Dharma itself becomes corrupted by it"). The wheel-marks do not carry their own immunity to misuse; what keeps them from becoming ordinary fortune-telling is entirely the state of mind — free of worldly craving, inwardly clean — that the person casting them brings to the rite that precedes every throw.

On the closing instruction — recite the name until an unfavorable reading turns favorable — Zhixu draws the conclusion that follows from taking this seriously in the other direction as well: "准此，則所占雖吉，若恣情放逸，不更修善，亦當不久還受衰惱矣" ("by the same logic, even a favorable reading, if one grows indulgent and lax and stops cultivating good, will before long give way to misfortune again"). Neither a good cast nor a bad one is ever the end of the matter; both are simply information that returns the practitioner to practice — favorable results a summons to keep going, unfavorable ones a summons to sincere recitation and repentance, exactly as Part 1 said of the very first wheel.

Part 3 Complete — Fascicle One Closes

This closes Part 3, and with it the sūtra's first fascicle: the third and final wheel-mark, whose one hundred and eighty-nine possible readings the sūtra explicitly grounds in the same "one real realm" its own six-faculty cosmology derives from a single undivided root; its warning that this method curdles into ordinary fortune-telling the instant it is practiced for profit rather than sincerity; and its closing promise that a mind sincere enough can turn even a run of unfavorable readings around. Part 4 opens the sūtra's second fascicle and its true center of gravity — the "one real realm" the third wheel has already named, unfolded here as the sūtra's own account of mind-nature itself.
