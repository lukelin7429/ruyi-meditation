Fascicle 3 · 大佛頂如來密因修證了義諸菩薩萬行首楞嚴經卷第三

Every Category, the Same Discovery

Fascicle 3 applies the method of fascicle 2 systematically: the six sense-entrances, the twelve sense-fields, the eighteen elements, and the seven great elements — earth, water, fire, wind, space, seeing, and consciousness — each shown, category by category, to be nothing other than the tathāgatagarbha. The fascicle ends with Ānanda's awakening and his verse of praise to the Buddha.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 3 complete Source: Taishō T19, no. 945 Covers: the entire fascicle — six entrances through Ānanda's verse of praise

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 2 ended by showing that each of the five aggregates is, at root, the tathāgatagarbha. Fascicle 3 runs the same argument through three more classical categories of Buddhist analysis — the six sense-entrances (六入), the twelve sense-fields (十二處, each a faculty paired with its object), and the eighteen elements (十八界, each faculty-object pair together with the consciousness it gives rise to) — before turning to a final, more expansive analysis of seven "great elements" (七大): earth, water, fire, wind, space, seeing, and consciousness. Each item, in turn, is shown to have no independent origin — not from itself, not from its apparent cause, not from empty space, not spontaneously — and is therefore, in the sūtra's central refrain, "the nature of true emptiness, and true emptiness's own nature," pervading the whole Dharma-realm as the tathāgatagarbha.

This page is Ru-Yi's own translation, made directly from the Chinese text preserved by CBETA (public domain). It does not reproduce or paraphrase the Buddhist Text Translation Society's existing English edition; see the translation notes for terminology, and the companion Jiǎngyì project for Master Yuanying's commentary.

The Six Sense-Entrances 六入本如來藏妙真如性

For each of the six sense-entrances, the Buddha applies the same test: this "entrance" is neither the sense-faculty itself, nor its object, nor empty space, nor does it appear from nowhere at all — it is, each time, the tathāgatagarbha.

Seeing 眼入

Chinese Source

阿難！即彼目精瞪發勞者，兼目與勞，同是菩提瞪發勞相。因于明暗二種妄塵，發見居中，吸此塵象，名為見性。此見離彼明暗二塵，畢竟無體。……若從明來，暗即隨滅，應非見暗。若從暗來，明即隨滅，應無見明。若從根生，必無明暗，如是見精本無自性。若於空出，前矚塵象，歸當見根；又空自觀，何關汝入？是故當知眼入虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda, when the eye is stared fixed until it strains, both the eye and the strain alike are marks of bodhi itself straining into fatigue. Dependent on the two false objects of brightness and darkness, seeing arises in between them, taking hold of these objects — this is called the nature of seeing. This seeing, apart from those two objects of brightness and darkness, has, in the end, no substance of its own. ... If it came from brightness, it should vanish along with darkness, and so should not be able to see darkness. If it came from darkness, it should vanish along with brightness, and so should have no seeing of brightness. If it arose from the faculty itself, then, brightness and darkness aside, this essence of seeing would have no nature of its own to begin with. If it emerged from empty space, then, having gazed outward at objects, it should turn and see the faculty itself — and besides, if space itself were doing the observing, what would that have to do with your own entrance of seeing? You should therefore know that the entrance of the eye is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Hearing, Smelling, Tasting, Touching, Knowing 耳、鼻、舌、身、意入

阿難！譬如有人，以兩手指急塞其耳，耳根勞故頭中作聲……是故當知耳入虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！譬如有人急畜其鼻，畜久成勞，則於鼻中聞有冷觸……是故當知鼻入虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！譬如有人以舌舐吻，熟舐令勞……是故當知舌入虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！譬如有人，以一冷手觸於熱手……是故當知身入虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！譬如有人，勞倦則眠，睡熟便寤，覽塵斯憶，失憶為忘……是故當知意入虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

Ānanda, it is like a person who presses two fingers firmly into his ears: the ear-faculty, strained, produces a ringing sound inside the head. Both the ear and the strain alike are marks of bodhi itself straining into fatigue — hearing arises dependent on the two false objects of motion and stillness ... You should therefore know that the entrance of the ear is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, it is like a person who inhales sharply through the nose until, over time, it becomes strained, so that he perceives a sensation of cold within the nose ... You should therefore know that the entrance of the nose is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, it is like a person licking his own lips: licking them until worn and strained, if the person is ill he tastes bitterness, and if well, a faint sweetness ... You should therefore know that the entrance of the tongue is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, it is like a person touching a cold hand to a hot one: if the cold is stronger, the hot hand turns cold; if the heat is stronger, the cold hand turns warm ... You should therefore know that the entrance of the body is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, it is like a person who, wearied, falls asleep, and, once well-rested, wakes; who, taking in an object, remembers it, and, losing that memory, forgets — this arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing, taken in through habituation, returns without ever overstepping its own bounds, and this is called the faculty of mind-cognition ... You should therefore know that the entrance of the mind is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Reading note: Each of the six entrances gets a small, homely demonstration first — pressed fingers in the ears, rubbing the tongue against the lips, a cold hand against a warm one — engineering a sensation without any real outside object, precisely so that the "sense experience" it produces can be shown to have no independent origin: not the faculty, not the object, not space, not spontaneous. The method is identical to the five-aggregates analogies that closed fascicle 2, now run across all six senses in turn.

The Twelve Sense-Fields 十二處本如來藏妙真如性

The Buddha now takes each faculty together with its object — sight and form, hearing and sound, smell and fragrance, taste and flavor, touch and object touched, mind and mental phenomena — and asks, in each case, which one produces the other, or whether the sense travels to the object or the object travels to the sense.

Seeing and Form 色、眼

Chinese Source

阿難！汝且觀此祇陀樹林及諸泉池，於意云何？此等為是色生眼見，眼生色相？……是故當知見與色、空俱無處所，即色與見二處虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda, look now at this Jeta Grove with its trees and springs and pools — what do you think? Is it that the form gives rise to the eye's seeing, or that the eye gives rise to the appearance of form? ... You should therefore know that seeing and form, and empty space besides, have no location at all — the two fields of form and seeing are false, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Hearing and Sound, Smelling and Fragrance, Tasting and Flavor, Touching and Object, Mind and Mental Phenomena 聲、耳 · 香、鼻 · 味、舌 · 觸、身 · 法、意

阿難！汝更聽此祇陀園中食辦擊鼓，眾集撞鐘，鐘鼓音聲前後相續。……是故當知聽與音聲俱無處所，即聽與聲二處虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！汝又嗅此鑪中栴檀，此香若復然於一銖，室羅筏城四十里內同時聞氣。……是故當知香、鼻與聞俱無處所，即嗅與香二處虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！汝常二時眾中持鉢，其間或遇酥酪醍醐，名為上味。……是故當知味、舌與甞俱無處所，即甞與味二俱虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！汝常晨朝以手摩頭，於意云何？此摩所知，誰為能觸？……是故當知覺、觸與身俱無處所，即身與觸二俱虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！汝常意中所緣善、惡、無記三性，生成法則；此法為復即心所生？為當離心別有方所？……是故當知法則與心俱無處所，則意與法二俱虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。

Ānanda, listen again to the drum sounding in this Jeta Grove as the meal is prepared, and the bell struck to gather the assembly — the sounds of bell and drum following one another in succession. Does the sound come to your ear, or does your ear go out to where the sound is? ... You should therefore know that hearing and sound have no location at all — the two fields of hearing and sound are false, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, smell again the sandalwood burning in the censer: if a single grain of it is lit, the scent is smelled throughout the forty li around Śrāvastī all at once. Does this fragrance arise from the sandalwood, from your nose, or from empty space? ... You should therefore know that fragrance, the nose, and the smelling of it have no location at all — the two fields of smelling and fragrance are false, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, at your two daily meal-times, holding your bowl among the assembly, you sometimes encounter ghee or clarified butter, called a superior flavor. Does this flavor arise in empty space, in the tongue, or in the food itself? ... You should therefore know that flavor, the tongue, and the tasting of it have no location at all — the two fields of tasting and flavor are equally false, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, every morning you rub your head with your hand — what do you think? In this rubbing and its being felt, what is it that does the touching? Is the capacity to touch in the hand, or in the head? ... You should therefore know that the sensation of touch, the object touched, and the body have no location at all — the two fields of body and touch are equally false, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, whatever your mind takes hold of among wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral phenomena, giving rise to their patterns as mental objects — do these mental objects arise from mind itself, or do they have some location apart from mind? ... You should therefore know that mental objects and mind have no location at all — the two fields of mind and mental phenomena are equally false, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

The Eighteen Elements 十八界本如來藏妙真如性

For each sense-field, the Buddha now asks after the consciousness that arises from it: does eye-consciousness, for instance, come from the eye, or from form? Each of the six is shown to be groundless in exactly the same way.

Eye-Consciousness, Ear-Consciousness 眼識界 · 耳識界

Chinese Source

阿難！如汝所明眼、色為緣生於眼識，此識為復因眼所生、以眼為界？因色所生、以色為界？……是故當知眼、色為緣生眼識界，三處都無，則眼與色及色界三，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！又汝所明耳、聲為緣生於耳識，此識為復因耳所生、以耳為界？因聲所生、以聲為界？……是故當知耳、聲為緣生耳識界，三處都無，則耳與聲及聲界三，本非因緣、非自然性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda, as you understand it, eye and form, taken as conditions, give rise to eye-consciousness — but does this consciousness arise dependent on the eye, taking the eye as its basis, or dependent on form, taking form as its basis? ... You should therefore know that eye-consciousness, said to arise from eye and form as conditions, has no location in any of the three — eye, form, and the resulting element of consciousness are, all three, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, as you further understand it, ear and sound, taken as conditions, give rise to ear-consciousness — but does this consciousness arise dependent on the ear, taking the ear as its basis, or dependent on sound, taking sound as its basis? ... You should therefore know that ear-consciousness, said to arise from ear and sound as conditions, has no location in any of the three — ear, sound, and the resulting element of consciousness are, all three, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Nose-, Tongue-, Body-, and Mind-Consciousness 鼻識界 · 舌識界 · 身識界 · 意識界

阿難！又汝所明鼻、香為緣生於鼻識……是故當知鼻、香為緣生鼻識界，三處都無，則鼻與香及香界三，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！又汝所明舌、味為緣生於舌識……是故當知舌、味為緣生舌識界，三處都無，則舌與味及舌界三，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！又汝所明身、觸為緣生於身識……是故當知身、觸為緣生身識界，三處都無，則身與觸及身界三，本非因緣、非自然性。

阿難！又汝所明意、法為緣生於意識……是故當知意、法為緣生意識界，三處都無，則意與法及意界三，本非因緣、非自然性。

Ānanda, as you further understand it, nose and fragrance, taken as conditions, give rise to nose-consciousness ... You should therefore know that nose-consciousness has no location in any of the three — nose, fragrance, and the resulting element, are, all three, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, as you further understand it, tongue and flavor, taken as conditions, give rise to tongue-consciousness ... You should therefore know that tongue-consciousness has no location in any of the three — tongue, flavor, and the resulting element, are, all three, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, as you further understand it, body and touch, taken as conditions, give rise to body-consciousness ... You should therefore know that body-consciousness has no location in any of the three — body, touch, and the resulting element, are, all three, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Ānanda, as you further understand it, mind and mental phenomena, taken as conditions, give rise to mind-consciousness ... You should therefore know that mind-consciousness has no location in any of the three — mind, mental phenomena, and the resulting element, are, all three, originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature.

Reading note: Together, the six entrances, twelve fields, and eighteen elements make up the three classical Abhidharma schemes for cataloguing the whole of experience — from the narrowest (just the six internal faculties) to the widest (faculty, object, and consciousness, eighteen items in all). Running the identical refutation across all three schemes, rather than just one, forecloses the objection that some other, uncovered category might still harbor a real, independent origin.

Ānanda's Objection: What About the Four Great Elements? 阿難執因緣和合以請

Chinese Source

阿難白佛言：「世尊！如來常說和合因緣，一切世間種種變化，皆因四大和合發明。云何如來因緣、自然二俱排擯？我今不知斯義所屬，唯垂哀愍，開示眾生中道了義無戲論法！」

爾時，世尊告阿難言：「汝先厭離聲聞、緣覺諸小乘法，發心勤求無上菩提，故我今時為汝開示第一義諦；如何復將世間戲論、妄想因緣而自纏繞？汝雖多聞，如說藥人，真藥現前不能分別，如來說為真可憐愍！」

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, the Tathāgata has always taught that all the world's various transformations come about through the combination of causes and conditions — through the combining of the four great elements. Why, then, does the Tathāgata now reject both causes-and-conditions and spontaneity alike? I do not know where this teaching belongs. I beg only that you, in compassion, reveal to sentient beings the middle way, the teaching of complete meaning, free of empty argument."

At that time the World-Honored One said to Ānanda: "You once turned away from the teachings of the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, resolving to seek unsurpassed bodhi — and so I now reveal to you the ultimate truth. Why, then, do you again entangle yourself in the world's empty arguments and deluded talk of causes and conditions? Though you are learned, you are like a person who can recite the names of medicines but cannot recognize the real medicine when it is right in front of him — the Tathāgata truly calls this pitiable!"

The Seven Great Elements 七大本如來藏妙真如性

The Buddha now answers Ānanda's challenge directly, taking each of the four classical "great elements" — earth, fire, water, wind — together with space, seeing, and consciousness, and showing, through a vivid analogy for each, that none of them is produced by "combination" in the way Ānanda assumes. Each is, instead, the tathāgatagarbha itself.

Earth: Splitting a Dust Mote Down to Emptiness 地大

Chinese Source

汝觀地性，麁為大地，細為微塵；至隣虛塵，析彼極微色邊際相，七分所成；更析隣虛，即實空性。……汝元不知如來藏中性色真空、性空真色，清淨本然周遍法界，隨眾生心應所知量。循業發現，世間無知，惑為因緣及自然性；皆是識心分別計度，但有言說，都無實義！

Ru-Yi Translation

Consider the nature of earth: coarse, it is the great earth itself; fine, it is dust. Down to the "dust bordering on emptiness" — splitting the very edge of the smallest particle of matter, formed of seven parts — split it once more, and it becomes true empty space itself. ... You have simply never known that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of form is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true form — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know, manifesting in accordance with karma. The unknowing world mistakes this for a matter of causes and conditions or of spontaneity — all of it merely the discriminating, calculating mind; there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all.

Fire: The Drill and the Burning-Glass 火大

阿難！火性無我，寄於諸緣。汝觀城中未食之家，欲炊爨時，手執陽燧日前求火。……此火為從鏡中而出？為從艾出？為於日來？……日、鏡相遠，非和非合，不應火光無從自有。汝猶不知如來藏中性火真空、性空真火，清淨本然周遍法界，隨眾生心應所知量。

Ānanda, fire has no self of its own; it depends on various conditions to appear. Observe a household in the city that has not yet cooked its meal, wishing to light the stove, holding a burning-glass up to the sun to kindle fire. ... Does this fire come out of the mirror, out of the tinder, or from the sun itself? ... The sun and the mirror are far apart from one another; there is neither a true mixing nor a true combining between them, and yet it would be wrong to say the fire's light simply has no source at all. You still do not know that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of fire is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true fire — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know.

Water: The Moon-Pearl That Gathers Dew 水大

阿難！水性不定，流息無恒。如室羅城迦毘羅仙……求太陰精用和幻藥。是諸師等，於白月晝，手執方諸，承月中水。此水為復從珠中出？空中自有？為從月來？……月珠相遠，非和非合，不應水精無從自有。汝尚不知如來藏中性水真空、性空真水，清淨本然周遍法界，隨眾生心應所知量。

Ānanda, the nature of water is unfixed: it flows or rests without constancy. The great sorcerers of Śrāvastī — such as the sage Kapila — seeking the essence of the moon to mix into their magical potions, hold up a moon-pearl by day under the bright moon, catching the water that gathers from the moon. Does this water come out of the pearl, exist already in empty space, or come from the moon itself? ... The moon and the pearl are far apart from one another; there is neither a true mixing nor a true combining between them, and yet it would be wrong to say the water's essence simply has no source at all. You still do not know that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of water is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true water — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know.

Wind: A Corner of a Monastic Robe 風大

阿難！風性無體，動靜不常。汝常整衣入於大眾，僧伽梨角動及傍人，則有微風拂彼人面。此風為復出袈裟角？發於虛空？生彼人面？……風、空性隔，非和非合；不應風性無從自有。汝宛不知如來藏中性風真空、性空真風，清淨本然周遍法界，隨眾生心應所知量。

Ānanda, wind has no fixed substance; its moving and stillness are not constant. When you straighten your robe to enter the assembly, the corner of your outer robe brushes against the person beside you, and a faint breeze touches that person's face. Does this wind come out of the corner of the robe, arise from empty space, or come from that person's own face? ... Wind and space are, by nature, separate from each other; there is neither a true mixing nor a true combining between them, and yet it would be wrong to say this nature of wind simply has no source at all. You still do not know that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of wind is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true wind — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know.

Space: Digging a Well 空大

阿難！空性無形，因色顯發。如室羅城去河遙處……鑿井求水。出土一尺，於中則有一尺虛空；如是乃至出土一丈，中間還得一丈虛空。虛空淺深，隨出多少。此空為當因土所出？因鑿所有？無因自生？……鑿、空虛實不相為用，非和非合，不應虛空無從自出。……汝全不知如來藏中性覺真空、性空真覺，清淨本然周遍法界，隨眾生心應所知量。

Ānanda, space has no form of its own; it is revealed only through form. Consider a household far from the river in Śrāvastī, newly settled, digging a well in search of water. For every foot of earth removed, a foot of empty space appears in its place; and so on, up to removing a full fathom of earth, revealing a full fathom of empty space in turn — the depth of the space keeping exact pace with how much earth is removed. Does this space come from the earth, from the digging, or does it arise spontaneously, with no cause at all? ... Digging and space, one solid and one empty, cannot act upon each other; there is neither a true mixing nor a true combining between them, and yet it would be wrong to say this space simply has no source at all. ... You do not know at all that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of awareness is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true awareness — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know.

Seeing: Neither the Same as Light and Space, Nor Different 見大

阿難！見覺無知，因色空有。……此見為復與明、暗相并太虛空為同一體？為非一體？或同非同？或異非異？……見暗、見明性非遷改，云何非異？……汝曾不知如來藏中性見覺明、覺精明見，清淨本然周遍法界，隨眾生心應所知量。

Ānanda, the awareness of seeing has, in itself, no content of its own — it appears only through brightness and darkness. ... Is this seeing one and the same substance as brightness, darkness, and empty space together, or not the same substance? Is it, perhaps, both the same and not the same, or neither the same nor different? ... Seeing darkness and seeing brightness — this seeing-nature itself never changes — so how could it be called "different" from them? ... You have never known that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of seeing is true awareness and bright, the essence of awareness is true, bright seeing — pure and unmoved from the start, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, appearing according to each sentient being's mind and capacity to know.

Consciousness: Recognizing the Assembly 識大

阿難！識性無源，因於六種根塵妄出。汝今遍觀此會聖眾，用目循歷，其目周視，但如鏡中無別分析；汝識於中次第標指，此是文殊、此富樓那、此目犍連、此須菩提、此舍利弗。此識了知，為生於見？為生於相？為生虛空？為無所因突然而出？……汝元不知如來藏中性識明知、覺明真識，妙覺湛然，遍周法界，含吐十虛，寧有方所？循業發現，世間無知，惑為因緣及自然性；皆是識心分別計度，但有言說，都無實義。」

Ānanda, consciousness has no single source; it arises falsely, dependent on the six pairs of sense-faculties and their objects. Look now across this whole assembly of noble ones — as your eye passes over them, roving freely, it sees them at first exactly as a mirror does, without distinguishing anything at all. Then your consciousness, one by one, picks out and names each one in turn: this is Mañjuśrī, this is Pūrṇa, this is Maudgalyāyana, this is Subhūti, this is Śāriputra. Does this act of recognizing consciousness arise from the seeing, from the appearances themselves, from empty space, or does it emerge suddenly, with no cause at all? ... You have simply never known that, in the tathāgatagarbha, the nature of consciousness is bright knowing, and true consciousness is the brightness of awareness itself — wondrously aware and utterly still, pervading the whole Dharma-realm, containing and giving forth the ten directions of emptiness — how could it have any fixed location? The unknowing world mistakes this for a matter of causes and conditions or of spontaneity — all of it merely the discriminating, calculating mind; there is only talk, with no real substance in it at all."

Reading note: The refrain "the nature of X is true emptiness, and the nature of emptiness is true X" is the fascicle's structural key. It is not saying that earth, fire, water, and the rest simply dissolve into nothingness — that would be the "denying" extreme fascicle 1's terminology already ruled out. It says that each element's true nature already is empty in the specific sense of having no fixed, independent origin of its own — and it is exactly this absence of any fixed origin that lets each element appear, without contradiction, "wherever karma calls it forth," pervading the whole Dharma-realm rather than being locked into one particular place. Seeing and consciousness are added to the traditional four great elements (plus space) precisely to make the point unmistakable: mind itself is not somehow exempt from this analysis, standing outside it as a special case — it is one more of the seven, subject to exactly the same discovery.

Ānanda's Awakening, and His Verse of Praise 阿難大眾蒙示得悟 · 說偈讚佛

Chinese Source

爾時，阿難及諸大眾，蒙佛如來微妙開示，身心蕩然得無罣礙。是諸大眾，各各自知心遍十方，見十方空如觀掌中所持葉物。一切世間諸所有物，皆即菩提妙明元心，心精遍圓含裹十方。反觀父母所生之身，猶彼十方虛空之中吹一微塵，若存若亡；如湛巨海流一浮漚，起滅無從。了然自知獲本妙心，常住不滅。禮佛合掌，得未曾有，於如來前說偈讚佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Ānanda and the whole assembly, receiving the Tathāgata's subtle and wondrous instruction, felt body and mind grow entirely open, free of every obstruction. Each one of them came to know, for themselves, that mind pervades the ten directions, and saw the empty space of the ten directions the way one sees a leaf held in the palm of the hand. Everything that exists in the whole world is nothing but the wondrous, bright, original mind of bodhi; the essence of mind, perfectly full and complete, encloses and contains the ten directions within itself. Looking back at the body born of their own parents, it seemed like a single speck of dust blown about somewhere within the boundless space of the ten directions, now appearing, now vanishing; like a single bubble floating on a vast, calm ocean, arising and ceasing with no origin to be found.

Clearly, of themselves, they knew they had recovered their own original, wondrous mind, ever-abiding and never perishing. Bowing to the Buddha, palms joined, having gained what they had never had before, they spoke this verse of praise before the Tathāgata:

Wondrously still, all-upholding, unmoved World-Honored One —
King Śūraṅgama, rarest of all in this world!
It has melted away my inverted thinking of a billion kalpas;
without passing through countless ages, I have gained the Dharma-body.

I vow now to attain the fruit, become a king among treasures,
and return to save these beings, numerous as the sands of the Ganges.
I offer this profound resolve to lands as many as motes of dust —
this alone is what it means to repay the Buddha's kindness.

I humbly beg the World-Honored One to bear witness for me:
I vow to enter first into this evil world of the five turbidities.
If a single sentient being has not yet become Buddha,
I will not, for that reason alone, take final nirvāṇa here.

Great hero, great strength, great compassion —
I beg you, further, to help me remove even the subtlest delusions,
so that I may quickly ascend to unsurpassed enlightenment
and sit at the place of practice throughout the worlds of the ten directions.

Let the very nature of empty space itself be brought to an end —
this vajra-like mind of mine will never waver.

大佛頂萬行首楞嚴經卷第三 — End of Fascicle Three

Reading note: This verse — often chanted on its own in Chinese monasteries independently of the sūtra — marks the turning point of the whole work. Fascicles 1–3 have been almost entirely dialectical: refuting false locations for the mind, and then showing category after category to be groundless in the same way. Ānanda's vow here ("if a single sentient being has not yet become Buddha, I will not take final nirvāṇa") is the bodhisattva vow proper, and signals that the sūtra is about to turn from analysis to practice — fascicle 4 takes up Pūrṇa's questions about how the world of delusion arises in the first place, and the path toward the Buddha's answer in the famous teaching on the twenty-five methods of perfect penetration.
