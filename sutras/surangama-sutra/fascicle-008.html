Fascicle 8 · 大佛頂如來密因修證了義諸菩薩萬行首楞嚴經卷第八

Three Stages, Fifty-Five Rungs, and the Roots of the Six Destinies

The Buddha sets out three gradual stages of practice, then the complete fifty-five-stage path from dry wisdom to equal enlightenment, and gives the sūtra its five titles. Ānanda then asks the question the rest of the fascicle answers: if the mind is originally pure, why do hell, hungry ghosts, animals, immortals, and the heavens exist at all? The Buddha traces every destiny back to the same root — the ratio of clinging emotion to rising thought in a single mind.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 8 complete Source: Taishō T19, no. 945 Covers: the entire fascicle — three gradual stages through the six heavens of the desire realm

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 7 closed by deriving all twelve categories of birth from twelve kinds of confused thought. Fascicle 8 opens by noting that each of those twelve categories in turn contains all twelve distortions folded into it — a warning that the coming taxonomy of destinies is not a neutral zoology but a map of delusion. The Buddha then gives Ānanda three gradual stages of practice, walks through the entire fifty-five-stage path from the first glimmer of dry wisdom to the threshold of Buddhahood, and, when Mañjuśrī asks what to call this sūtra, gives it five titles at once. The fascicle's long second half answers Ānanda's own question — posed after hearing of a nun and two others who fell straight into the hells despite outward practice — by tracing hell, hungry ghosts, animals, immortals, and the six heavens of the desire realm all back to a single mechanism: the shifting ratio, in any one mind, of "emotion" (clinging, downward-pulling attachment) to "thought" (aspiration, upward-lifting clarity).

This page is Ru-Yi's own translation, made directly from the Chinese text preserved by CBETA (public domain). It does not reproduce or paraphrase the Buddhist Text Translation Society's existing English edition; see the translation notes for terminology, and the companion Jiǎngyì project for Master Yuanying's commentary.

The Three Gradual Stages 三漸次

Chinese Source

「阿難！如是眾生一一類中，亦各各具十二顛倒。猶如揑目，亂花發生。顛倒妙圓真淨明心，具足如斯虛妄亂想。汝今修證佛三摩提，於是本因元所亂想，立三漸次方得除滅。如淨器中除去毒蜜，以諸湯水并雜灰香洗滌其器，後貯甘露。云何名為三種漸次？一者、修習除其助因。二者、真修刳其正性。三者、增進違其現業。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Ānanda, within each and every one of these categories of birth, each also possesses all twelve distortions folded into it — like pressing on the eye until scattered flowers appear out of nowhere. It is this same wondrously perfect, true, pure, bright mind, turned upside down, that takes on this whole mass of empty, false, confused thought. Now that you are cultivating and realizing the Buddha's samādhi, you must work from this same root — the original confused thought itself — and establish three gradual stages, only then to remove it: like cleansing a vessel that once held poisoned honey, first scouring it with hot water mixed with ash and fragrant herbs, and only afterward storing sweet dew inside it. What are these three gradual stages? First, cultivation to remove the aiding cause. Second, true cultivation to scour out the very root-nature of desire. Third, advancing further to reverse present habitual activity."

First: Removing the Aiding Cause 除其助因

The Buddha explains that all twelve categories of beings depend on four kinds of nourishment to survive — food eaten in portions, food taken in through contact, food taken in through intention, and food taken in through consciousness — and that "all beings live by nourishment" for exactly this reason: "beings live by what is sweet, and die by what is poison." Anyone seeking samādhi must therefore first sever the five pungent roots. Cooked, these five foods stir up lust; eaten raw, they inflame anger. Even a person in this world who eats them and can still expound all twelve divisions of the scriptures will be shunned by every heavenly immortal of the ten directions, who find the smell repugnant; hungry ghosts, meanwhile, lick at such a person's lips whenever they eat, so that they dwell constantly in the company of ghosts, their store of merit wasting away with no benefit at all. Bodhisattvas, heavenly immortals, and good spirits of the ten directions will not come to protect someone who cultivates samādhi while still eating these five roots — and a powerful demon-king, finding his opening, will appear in the very form of a buddha to expound the Dharma to such a person, denouncing the precepts and praising lust, anger, and delusion instead, so that at life's end they themselves become one of that demon-king's own retinue, and, once that demonic merit is exhausted, fall into the unrelenting hell. "Ānanda, one who seeks bodhi must permanently sever the five pungent roots — this is called the first gradual stage of advancing practice."

Second: Scouring Out the True Nature 刳其正性

Entering samādhi requires first strictly upholding the pure precepts: permanently severing the mind of lust, abstaining from wine and meat, and taking only food purified by fire, never anything raw. There is no possibility, the Buddha says, of leaving the three realms without severing lust and killing — one should regard sexual desire exactly as one would a venomous snake, or a hated enemy encountered on the road. First hold the śrāvaka precepts against the four and the eight gravest offenses, so that the body itself stays still; then carry out the bodhisattva's own pure rules of conduct, so that even the mind no longer stirs toward transgression. Once the precepts are truly accomplished, one is forever free, in this world, from any karma of mutual generation [through desire] or mutual killing; without theft, there is no mutual debt, and no old debt left owing to the world at all. A person purified in this way, cultivating samādhi, needs no heavenly eye to see, with this very body born of father and mother, the worlds of the ten directions with perfect clarity — beholding buddhas, hearing the Dharma, personally receiving the sacred instruction, gaining great spiritual powers, and roaming the ten directions with a past entirely clean of obstruction. "This is called the second gradual stage of advancing practice."

Third: Reversing Present Karma 違其現業

For a person who keeps the precepts purely in this way, the mind holds no lust at all, and no longer streams outward toward the six sense-objects; because it no longer streams outward, it turns back and returns to its own source. With no sense-object left to fasten onto, the sense-faculties have nothing to pair with; the reversed stream becomes wholly unified, and the six functions cease their separate operation. The lands of the ten directions become bright and pure, like the moon suspended within lapis lazuli, body and mind at ease, wondrously perfect and equal throughout, gaining great peace and stability — and every Tathāgata's hidden, perfect, pure wonder appears within this very state. Such a person then attains forbearance of the unborn nature of all phenomena, and, cultivating gradually from there, according to whatever practice arises, becomes established in the sagely stages. "This is called the third gradual stage of advancing practice."

The Fifty-Five Stages of the True Path to Bodhi 五十五位真菩提路

Having set out the three gradual stages, the Buddha walks Ānanda through the entire path in a single continuous sweep — from the first glimmer of "dry wisdom," not yet joined to the Tathāgata's own stream of Dharma, all the way to the threshold of Buddhahood itself.

Chinese Source

「阿難！是善男子，欲愛乾枯，根境不偶，現前殘質不復續生。執心虛明，純是智慧。慧性明圓，瑩十方界。乾有其慧，名乾慧地。「欲習初乾，未與如來法流水接。即以此心中中流入，圓妙開敷。從真妙圓，重發真妙，妙信常住。一切妄想滅盡無餘，中道純真，名信心住。……」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Ānanda, once this good person's craving desire has dried up entirely, the sense-faculties and their objects no longer pair with one another, and the residual matter of this present body no longer continues into further birth. The mind that holds to this is empty and bright, purely wisdom itself — its very nature of wisdom bright and full, shining clear throughout the ten directions. Wisdom alone, still dry [not yet moistened by the Dharma-stream], is called the ground of dry wisdom.

"With this habitual desire only just dried up, not yet joined to the Tathāgata's own flowing stream of Dharma, this same mind now enters directly into that very stream, and its perfect wonder begins to unfold. From true wondrous perfection, a further true wonder arises, and wondrous faith abides constantly; every trace of false thought is extinguished without remainder, and the middle way becomes wholly genuine — this is called abiding in the mind of faith."

The Ten Abidings of Faith 十信

The sūtra continues in the same compressed style through all ten: once faith is genuine and clear, understanding penetrates everywhere, and the [five] aggregates, [twelve] sense-fields, and [eighteen] elements can no longer obstruct it — this is the mind of mindfulness, in which all habitual tendencies accumulated across countless past and future kalpas of taking on and laying down bodies appear before this person, remembered without any loss. Wondrous perfection, wholly genuine, gives rise to true transformation, so that beginningless habitual tendency is unified into a single, essential brightness, advancing only toward true purity — this is the mind of vigor. The mind's essence appears, purely wisdom itself — the mind abiding in wisdom. Holding to this bright wisdom, pervading and still everywhere, constantly settled in its own wondrous stillness — the mind abiding in concentration. The light of concentration grows bright, its nature penetrating ever deeper, advancing only, never retreating — the mind of non-retreat. Advancing at ease, preserved without loss, in communion with the very breath of the Tathāgatas of the ten directions — the mind that guards the Dharma. Preserving this bright awareness, able through its wondrous power to turn the Buddha's own compassionate light back toward the Buddha and abide there — like two mirrors facing each other, their wondrous reflections entering one another layer upon layer — the mind of dedication. The mind's light secretly turned back, gaining the Buddha's own constant, unsurpassed, wondrously pure stillness, abiding in the unconditioned without any loss — the mind abiding in precepts. Settled at ease within the precepts, able to roam the ten directions, going wherever one wishes — the mind of aspiration.

The Ten Abidings 十住

Through genuine skillful means, these ten minds give rise, the sūtra says, to a single unified mind in which all ten qualities interpenetrate — the abiding of arousing the mind [of bodhi]. Within this mind, understanding appears as if pure lapis lazuli held refined gold within it, this prior wondrous mind now serving as the very ground one walks upon — the abiding of cultivating the ground. The mind and the ground come into mutual contact and understanding, both becoming perfectly clear, roaming the ten directions without any obstruction — the abiding of practice. Practice now the same as the Buddha's own, receiving the Buddha's very breath, like a being in the intermediate state seeking out its own parents, its hidden affinity secretly communicating and entering the Tathāgata's lineage — the abiding of noble birth. Having entered this way into the "womb" of the path, personally receiving the awakened heritage, complete in every mark like a fetus already formed with nothing lacking — the abiding of fully equipped skillful means. Countenance now like the Buddha's own, the marks of mind likewise the same — the abiding of the rectified mind. Body and mind fused into one, growing daily further — the abiding of non-retreat. All ten bodily, spiritual marks at once complete — the abiding of the youth of true purity [Dharma-prince in embryo]. Form now complete and emerging from the womb, one's own person now truly a child of the Buddha — the abiding as a Dharma-prince. Outwardly now fully formed, as when a great king delegates the affairs of state to his crown prince, and that prince, once grown, is formally anointed — the abiding of anointment.

The Ten Practices 十行

Having become a true child of the Buddha, fully endowed with the Tathāgata's countless wondrous virtues, according with all beings of the ten directions — the practice of joy. Skillfully benefiting every sentient being — the practice of beneficial action. Awakening oneself and awakening others without any resistance between the two — the practice free of resentment. Giving rise to every kind of being across the furthest reaches of future time, the three periods equal and the ten directions penetrated — the practice without limit. Every gateway of the Dharma brought into concord, without the slightest confusion or error — the practice free from confusion and disorder. Revealing, within sameness itself, every kind of difference, and finding sameness present within each and every difference — the practice of skillful manifestation. Down to filling the whole of the ten directions' empty space with motes of dust, and within each single mote manifesting the worlds of the ten directions, the manifested motes and manifested worlds never obstructing one another — the practice free of attachment. Every manifestation, in every form, being the very foremost perfection — the practice of veneration. Interfused and perfect in just this way, able to bring about the very standard followed by every buddha of the ten directions — the practice of good dharma. Each and every one of these wholly pure and undefiled, the single true unconditioned, since this is the nature's own original state — the practice of truth and reality.

The Ten Transferences of Merit 十迴向

Having perfected spiritual powers and accomplished the Buddha's own work, purely genuine and true, far from every remaining trouble, one now turns to liberate beings while dissolving the very mark of "liberating," turning the unconditioned mind toward the path of nirvāṇa — the transference of rescuing all beings while leaving behind the mark of "being". Destroying what can be destroyed, and leaving behind even the leaving-behind itself — the indestructible transference. Original awakening, still and clear, its awakening equal to the Buddha's own awakening — the transference equal to all buddhas. True essence brought to light, one's own ground now like the Buddha's own ground — the transference reaching every place. Worlds and Tathāgatas mutually interpenetrating without obstruction — the transference of the treasury of boundless merit. Within this same ground as the Buddha's own, each individually giving rise to a pure cause, and, relying on that cause, taking up the path to nirvāṇa — the transference according with the equal, good root. The true root now accomplished, all beings of the ten directions are one's own original nature, that nature perfectly fulfilled without losing a single being — the transference according with the equal contemplation of all beings. Taking hold of every phenomenon while leaving every mark behind, attached neither to the taking-hold nor to the leaving-behind — the transference of the mark of suchness. Truly attaining suchness itself, unobstructed throughout the ten directions — the transference of unbound liberation. The virtue of one's own nature perfectly accomplished, the very measure of the Dharma-realm dissolved — the transference of the boundlessness of the Dharma-realm.

The Four Aids to Penetration 四加行

Having completed all forty-one of these pure minds, one next accomplishes four kinds of wondrous, perfect, additional practice. Taking the Buddha's own awakening as one's own mind, as though about to emerge yet not yet emerged — like drilling wood to kindle fire, the wood on the verge of catching flame — this is the ground of warmth. Taking one's own mind as what the Buddha himself has walked upon, as though depending on it and yet not quite depending on it — like climbing a high mountain, one's body entering empty space while some slight obstruction still remains below — this is the ground of the summit. Mind and Buddha now the same, having genuinely found the middle way — like someone bearing an injury who neither holds it in nor lets it fully out — this is the ground of forbearance. Every trace of measurement and number dissolved, confusion and awakening in the middle way both no longer objects to fix upon — this is the ground of being foremost in the world.

The Ten Grounds 十地

Having thoroughly penetrated great bodhi, understanding now reaching the entire domain of a Tathāgata's own awakening — the ground of joy. Difference merging into sameness, and sameness itself then also dissolving — the ground free of defilement. Purity brought to its utmost, brightness arising — the ground of emitted light. Brightness at its utmost, awakening fully complete — the ground of blazing wisdom. Every kind of sameness and difference alike unable to reach it — the ground difficult to overcome. The unconditioned true suchness, its pure nature laid bare — the ground of manifestation. The very limit of true suchness exhausted — the ground of traveling far. The single, true, suchness mind — the ground of immovability. The functioning of true suchness brought forth — the ground of skillful wisdom.

From here on, these bodhisattvas' cultivation and practice reach their completion, their merit and virtue perfectly fulfilled; this ground is also called the "ground of accomplished cultivation" — a shading cloud of compassion covering the sea of nirvāṇa — the ground of the dharma-cloud.

Equal Enlightenment, and the Culmination 等覺 · 妙覺

The Tathāgata moves against the stream [outward, to save beings]; these bodhisattvas move with the stream [inward, toward the Buddha] until they arrive at exactly the same point — where awakening and its very edge intersect and meet: this is called equal enlightenment.

"Ānanda, from the mind of dry wisdom all the way to equal enlightenment, this awakening only then attains what was originally the very first dry-wisdom ground within the vajra mind [of ultimate realization] — folded over, again and again, single then doubled, twelve times in all, before wondrous enlightenment is finally reached, and the unsurpassed path accomplished. Every one of these various grounds is entered gradually and deeply through the vajra-like contemplation, within the illusion-like ten profound analogies of śamatha, using the Tathāgatas' own pure vipaśyanā cultivation and realization. Ānanda, all of this, through these three kinds of advancement, is what makes it possible to accomplish these fifty-five stages of the true path to bodhi. Whoever contemplates it this way contemplates rightly; whoever contemplates it any other way contemplates wrongly."

Reading note: The traditional count of "fifty-five" stages is reached by setting the ground of dry wisdom aside as a preliminary threshold (not yet joined to the Dharma-stream) and wondrous enlightenment aside as the destination beyond even equal enlightenment, leaving ten faiths, ten abidings, ten practices, ten transferences, four aids to penetration, ten grounds, and equal enlightenment itself — 10+10+10+10+4+10+1 = 55 — as the stages of the path proper. Commentators (including Master Yuanying; see the companion Jiǎngyì project) devote a great deal of care to exactly how each stage's one- or two-character description packs in a specific technical claim; this page keeps the sūtra's own extremely compressed phrasing rather than expanding it into looser paraphrase, since later fascicles and the commentary both return to these exact terms.

The Sūtra's Five Titles 十方如來清淨海眼

Chinese Source

爾時，文殊師利法王子，在大眾中即從座起，頂禮佛足而白佛言：「當何名是經？我及眾生云何奉持？」佛告文殊師利：「是經名『大佛頂、悉怛多般怛囉無上寶印、十方如來清淨海眼』，亦名『救護親因、度脫阿難及此會中性比丘尼得菩提心入遍知海』，亦名『如來密因修證了義』，亦名『大方廣妙蓮華王十方佛母陀羅尼呪』，亦名『灌頂章句、諸菩薩萬行首楞嚴』，汝當奉持。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Mañjuśrī, Dharma-Prince, rose from his seat in the midst of the assembly, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said to the Buddha: "What should this sūtra be called? How should I and all beings uphold it?"

The Buddha said to Mañjuśrī: "This sūtra is named The Great Buddha's Crown, the Unsurpassed Jeweled Seal of Sitātapatra, the Pure Ocean-Eye of the Tathāgatas of the Ten Directions; it is also named Rescuing the True Cause, Liberating Ānanda and the Bhikṣuṇī of the Nature [Prakṛti] in This Assembly to Attain the Mind of Bodhi and Enter the Ocean of Omniscience; it is also named The Tathāgata's Hidden Cause, Cultivation and Realization of Complete Meaning; it is also named The Dhāraṇī of the Great, Vast, Wondrous Lotus King, Mother of the Buddhas of the Ten Directions; and it is also named The Verses of Crown-Anointment, the Myriad Practices of All Bodhisattvas, Śūraṅgama. You should uphold it under all these names."

Reading note: As the commentary's own opening exegesis explains (see the Jiǎngyì project's fascicle 1), the sūtra's actual working title on this site — the nineteen characters compressed from the first three of these five full names — is itself a deliberate condensation of exactly this list. Naming the sūtra five different ways at once is not decorative; each name foregrounds a different one of its major themes: the mantra and its protective power, the rescue of Ānanda and Prakṛti specifically, the "hidden cause" and "complete meaning" of its title phrase, its function as a dhāraṇī, and the myriad bodhisattva practices under the name Śūraṅgama.

Ānanda's Question: Why Do the Six Destinies Exist? 阿難請問七趣

Chinese Source

……阿難……而白佛言：「大威德世尊！慈音無遮，善開眾生微細沈惑，令我今日身心快然，得大饒益。世尊！若此妙明真淨妙心本來遍圓，如是乃至大地草木、蠕動含靈本元真如，即是如來成佛真體，佛體真實，云何復有地獄、餓鬼、畜生、修羅、人、天等道？世尊！此道為復本來自有？為是眾生妄習生起？世尊！如寶蓮香比丘尼，持菩薩戒，私行婬欲，妄言行婬非殺、非偷，無有業報。發是語已，先於女根生大猛火，後於節節猛火燒然，墮無間獄。瑠璃大王、善星比丘；瑠璃為誅瞿曇族姓，善星妄說一切法空，生身陷入阿鼻地獄。此諸地獄，為有定處？為復自然彼彼發業、各各私受？唯垂大慈，發開童蒙，令諸一切持戒眾生聞決定義，歡喜頂戴，謹潔無犯！」

Ru-Yi Translation

... Ānanda ... said to the Buddha: "Greatly awesome and virtuous World-Honored One, your compassionate voice holds nothing back, and has opened up the subtle, deeply buried confusions of sentient beings, so that my own body and mind today feel light and clear, greatly benefited. World-Honored One, if this wondrous, bright, true, pure, wondrous mind is originally all-pervading and perfectly complete — extending even to the great earth, the grasses and trees, and every crawling, sentient thing, their original true suchness itself being the very true substance by which the Tathāgata becomes Buddha, that substance of buddhahood being real and genuine — then how can there still be the destinies of hell, hungry ghosts, animals, asuras, humans, and gods? World-Honored One, do these destinies exist inherently, from the very beginning, or do they arise from beings' own false habitual tendencies? World-Honored One: the bhikṣuṇī Ratnakamalā, who held the bodhisattva precepts yet secretly engaged in sexual misconduct, falsely claiming that such conduct was neither killing nor stealing and so carried no karmic retribution at all — no sooner had she spoken these words than a great fierce fire arose first from her own female organ, and then burned joint by joint through her entire body, until she fell into the unrelenting hell. So too King Virūḍhaka [Ruby], and the monk Sunakṣatra: Virūḍhaka, for slaughtering the Gautama clan, and Sunakṣatra, for falsely claiming that all phenomena are empty [and so denying cause and effect], both sank, in their very living bodies, into the Avīci hell. Are these hells, then, fixed in some definite location? Or does each being's own karma arise naturally, each receiving it individually and privately? I beg only that you, in your great compassion, open the eyes of the untaught, so that all beings who keep the precepts may hear this settled teaching, and, joyfully upholding it, remain scrupulously pure and free of transgression."

Internal and External Tendencies 內分 · 外分

Chinese Source

佛告阿難：「快哉此問！令諸眾生不入邪見。汝今諦聽，當為汝說。阿難！一切眾生實本真淨，因彼妄見有妄習生，因此分開內分、外分。阿難！內分即是眾生分內。因諸愛染發起妄情，情積不休能生愛水。……此名內分。阿難！外分即是眾生分外。因諸渴仰，發明虛想，想積不休能生勝氣。……此名外分。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda: "Excellent, this question! It will keep beings from falling into wrong views. Listen carefully now, and I will explain. Ānanda, all beings are, in their true nature, originally pure — it is only because of false seeing that false habitual tendency arises, and from this, an internal tendency and an external tendency divide off from one another.

"The internal tendency is what belongs within a being itself: because of every kind of attached love, false emotion arises, and once this emotion accumulates without rest, it gives rise to a kind of 'water of love.' And so a being's mind, recalling some delicacy, produces water in the mouth; recalling a former companion with either affection or resentment, fills the eyes with tears; craving after wealth, produces a covetous drool, the whole body glossy with it; the mind fixed on sexual union, and fluid flows naturally from both the male and female organs. Ānanda, though these attachments differ, this flowing and congealing is the same in every case — being wet and heavy, it does not rise, but naturally falls. This is called the internal tendency.

"The external tendency is what belongs beyond a being itself: because of every kind of yearning aspiration, empty thought comes to light, and once this thought accumulates without rest, it gives rise to a kind of superior vital energy. And so a being's mind, holding to the precepts, feels the whole body light and clear; holding to mantras and mudrās, looks about with a bold, vigorous bearing; wishing to be born in the heavens, dreams of soaring flight; keeping the buddha-lands in mind, finds sacred realms secretly appearing; serving a good spiritual friend, holds their own body and life lightly. Ānanda, though these thoughts differ, this lightness and rising is the same in every case — buoyant and unsinking, it naturally transcends. This is called the external tendency."

How Emotion and Thought Decide a Destiny 情想輕重

Chinese Source

「阿難！一切世間生死相續，生從順習，死從變流。臨命終時，未捨暖觸，一生善惡俱時頓現。死逆、生順，二習相交。純想即飛，必生天上。……情想均等，不飛、不墜，生於人間，想明斯聰，情幽斯鈍。情多想少，流入橫生；重為毛群，輕為羽族。七情、三想，沈下水輪，生於火際……身為餓鬼，常被焚燒……九情一想，下洞火輪……輕生有間、重生無間二種地獄。純情即沈，入阿鼻獄。……」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Ānanda, the continuity of birth and death throughout the world is such that birth follows the stream of habitual tendency, and death is a reversal of that same stream. At the moment of death, before the body's last warmth has faded, an entire lifetime's good and evil appears all at once, in a single instant. Death runs against the ordinary current of life, and life runs with it — and it is exactly at this crossing point that the two habitual tendencies, emotion and thought, meet and interact.

"One who is pure thought takes flight, and is certain to be born in the heavens above — and if that rising mind is joined with merit, with wisdom, and with a pure vow as well, the mind opens of itself and the buddhas of the ten directions appear, so that one is reborn in any pure land according to one's own vow. Where there is little emotion and mostly thought, one rises but not far, becoming a flying immortal, a powerful ghost-king, a flying yakṣa, or an earth-walking rākṣasa, roaming freely through the four heavens without obstruction — and among these, any who hold a good vow and good intention to protect this Dharma of mine, whether guarding the precepts and accompanying those who keep them, or guarding mantras and accompanying those who hold them, or guarding meditative concentration and preserving the forbearance of the Dharma, dwell in close attendance right beneath the Tathāgata's own seat.

"Where emotion and thought are equal, one neither rises nor falls, and is born among human beings — clarity of thought bringing intelligence, and murkiness of emotion bringing dullness. Where there is much emotion and little thought, one flows down into the realm of creatures born sideways [animals] — heavier, becoming one of the furred kind; lighter, one of the feathered kind. Where there are seven parts emotion to three parts thought, one sinks down through the water-wheel [beneath the earth], reborn at the very edge of fire, taking on the nature of fierce flame, the body becoming that of a hungry ghost, forever scorched by burning, harmed even by water, without food or drink for a hundred thousand kalpas. Where there are nine parts emotion to one part thought, one sinks down through the fire-wheel entirely, the body passing through the place where wind and fire cross — lighter, into one of the interrupted hells; heavier, into the unrelenting hell itself. And where there is pure emotion alone, one sinks straight down into the Avīci hell — and if, within that sinking mind, there is also slander of the Great Vehicle, violation of the Buddha's own precepts, false preaching of the Dharma for gain, empty consumption of offerings made in good faith, or unearned acceptance of reverence, along with any of the five heinous or ten grave offenses, one is reborn again and again in Avīci hells throughout the ten directions. However each being's own evil karma may bring this about individually, all who share the same karma also share, in common, the very ground of that same destiny."

Reading note: This is the sūtra's governing mechanism for everything that follows: not a court handing down sentences from outside, but a single continuous variable — the ratio, within one's own mind at the moment of death, of downward-pulling emotion (情, rooted in attached love) to upward-lifting thought (想, rooted in aspiration) — that determines, with mathematical precision (seven-to-three, nine-to-one, and so on), exactly which destiny a being falls into. "Emotion" and "thought" are not simply "bad" and "good" in a moral sense; they are two directions a mind can lean, and even a mind leaning the right way (toward "thought") still needs merit, wisdom, and vow to reach a pure land rather than merely a heaven.

Ten Habitual Causes and Six Cross-Referenced Retributions of Hell 十習因 · 六交報

The Buddha now explains that all of this is self-induced: "these are all what these very beings have brought about through their own karma, creating ten habitual causes and receiving six cross-referenced retributions."

Chinese Source (first cause, in full)

「一者、婬習交接，發於相磨，研磨不休，如是故有大猛火光於中發動；如人以手自相磨觸，暖相現前。二習相然，故有鐵床、銅柱諸事。是故十方一切如來，色目行婬同名欲火，菩薩見欲如避火坑。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"First, the habit of lust, in its mutual contact, arises from mutual friction; this grinding, unceasing, gives rise to a great fierce blaze of fire stirring within it — just as, when a person rubs their own hands together, a sensation of warmth appears. The two [the habit and its fruit] kindle one another, and so there come to be beds of iron and pillars of copper [that the lustful embrace in hell, mistaking them for their former lover]. This is why every Tathāgata of the ten directions, in describing sexual misconduct, alike calls it 'the fire of desire' — and why bodhisattvas regard desire exactly as they would a pit of fire, to be avoided at all costs."

2. Greed 貪習 — arises from mutual grasping, an unceasing pull like sucking in cold air, giving rise to accumulated ice; the Tathāgatas call excessive craving "the water of greed," and bodhisattvas see it as a miasmic sea to be avoided.
3. Arrogance 慢習 — arises from mutual self-reliance, a restless surging like water racing and piling into waves; the Tathāgatas call self-conceit "drinking the water of delusion," and bodhisattvas see it as a great flood to be avoided.
4. Anger 嗔習 — arises from mutual clashing, an unresolved friction that heats the mind into fire and forges vapor into metal, producing hills of blades and forests of swords; the Tathāgatas call anger "a sharpened sword," and bodhisattvas see it as execution itself, to be avoided.
5. Deceit 詐習 — arises from mutual manipulation, drawing another in without let-up, like water seeping into a field and making weeds grow; the Tathāgatas call cunning fraud "a slandering thief," and bodhisattvas fear deceit as they would a wolf.
6. Fraud 誑習 — arises from mutual deception, unceasing false accusation, the mind flying about to scheme, giving rise to dust, filth, and excrement, just as dust carried on the wind leaves nothing visible; the Tathāgatas call deceit and fraud alike "robbery and murder," and bodhisattvas see fraud as they would treading on a viper.
7. Grudges 怨習 — arises from mutual resentment, a nursed hatred like a person of hidden poison secretly harboring malice; the Tathāgatas call an enemy held in the heart "a harm-dealing ghost," and bodhisattvas see grudges as they would drinking poisoned wine.
8. Wrong views 見習 — the views of a fixed self, of clinging to precepts wrongly held, and every deviant realization, arise from mutual opposition, giving rise to interrogation and cross-examination, like travelers meeting and questioning one another on the road; the Tathāgatas call evil views alike "a pit of views," and bodhisattvas see every false, universalized clinging as entering a poisoned ravine.
9. False accusation 枉習 — arises from mutual imposition, unjust slander, like a slanderous person forcing the innocent into wrong, giving rise to crushing and grinding, like millstones grinding grain; the Tathāgatas call resentful slander "a tiger that spreads lies," and bodhisattvas see false accusation as being struck by lightning.
10. Litigation 訟習 — arises from mutual concealment, hiding what one has done, as one cannot hide one's shadow at noon, giving rise to evil companions, the karma-mirror, and the fire-pearl that expose one's past deeds for cross-examination; the Tathāgatas call concealment alike "a hidden thief," and bodhisattvas regard concealment as carrying a mountain on their back while wading through a vast sea.

Each of the ten habitual causes then ripens through six cross-referenced retributions — one for each of the six senses through which the dying mind's karma "crosses over" into its next rebirth. A representative selection, translated in fuller detail:

Chinese Source (first retribution, in full)

「一者、見報招引惡果。此見業交，則臨終時，先見猛火滿十方界。亡者神識飛墜乘煙，入無間獄。發明二相：一者、明見，則能遍見種種惡物，生無量畏。二者、暗見，寂然不見，生無量恐。如是見火：燒聽，能為鑊湯、洋銅；燒息，能為黑烟、紫焰；燒味，能為燋丸、鐵糜；燒觸，能為熱灰、鑪炭；燒心，能生星火迸灑，煽鼓空界。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"First, the retribution of sight draws forth its own evil fruit. When this karma of sight comes due, at the moment of death, one first sees fierce fire filling the ten directions; the departed consciousness, falling as though riding on smoke, plunges into the unrelenting hell. Two aspects then appear: first, bright seeing, in which one sees every kind of evil thing everywhere, giving rise to boundless dread; second, dark seeing, utterly unseeing, giving rise to boundless terror. This same fire of seeing, crossing into hearing, becomes cauldrons of boiling water or molten copper; crossing into breath, becomes black smoke and violet flame; crossing into taste, becomes scorched pellets and iron gruel; crossing into touch, becomes hot ash and burning coals; crossing into the mind itself, scatters sparks that fan and churn the whole of empty space."

2. Hearing 聞報 — one first sees waves and floods engulfing heaven and earth; the departed consciousness sinks and is swept along the current into the unrelenting hell. Open hearing brings a chaos of noise that scatters the spirit; closed hearing brings a silent, sunken numbness. Crossing into the other senses, this water becomes interrogation and rebuke, thunder and poisonous vapor, rain and mist swarming with venomous creatures, pus and blood and every kind of filth, beasts and ghosts and excrement, and lightning and hail that shatter the very mind.
3. Smell 嗅報 — one first sees poisonous vapor filling every distance; the departed consciousness rises up out of the ground into the unrelenting hell. Open smelling chokes the mind with foul vapor; blocked smelling suffocates one to collapse. Crossing into the other senses, this becomes solid obstruction, fire and torches, drowning and boiling, spoiled and rancid flesh, a great mountain of flesh with countless eyes devouring endlessly, and ash and pestilential sand that batter the body to pieces.
4. Taste 味報 — one first sees a net of iron, fiercely blazing, covering the whole world; the departed consciousness plunges downward, hung by the head from the net, into the unrelenting hell. Inhaling freezes into ice that splits flesh and bone; exhaling flies out as fierce fire that scorches marrow. Crossing into the other senses, this becomes endurance under torment, molten metal and stone, sharp blades, a great iron cage covering the land, bows and arrows, and burning iron raining down from the sky.
5. Touch 觸報 — one first sees a great mountain closing in from all four sides, with no way out; the departed consciousness sees a great iron city, fire-snakes and fire-dogs, tigers, wolves, and lions, ox-headed and horse-headed wardens driving one with spears through the gate toward the unrelenting hell. Contact by pressing crushes flesh and bone against the closing mountain; contact by cutting slices open the heart and organs with sword and blade. Crossing into the other senses, this becomes roads, watchtowers, halls, and benches that burn; blows, stabs, and shots; bindings, floggings, and confinement; plowing, tearing, and dismemberment; falling, flying, frying, and roasting.
6. Thought 思報 — one first sees an evil wind destroying the land; the departed consciousness, blown up into the air, spins and falls, riding the wind down into the unrelenting hell. Unawareness brings a frantic, ceaseless wandering; awareness brings unbearable, deep suffering. Crossing into the other senses, this becomes fixed places of confinement, mirrors of judgment and testimony, great stones, ice, frost, mud, and fog, fiery carts, ships, and cages, wailing and weeping, and a single day containing ten thousand deaths and ten thousand rebirths.

Reading note: The logic here is exact rather than metaphorical: each of the ten causes and six sense-based retributions "crosses" (交) with the others, so that (as the sūtra spells out just after this passage) a being whose karma is complete across all six senses and all ten causes falls into Avīci itself; one whose evil act involves only a single sense and a single cause falls into one of the "one hundred and eight" lesser hells; and so on by exact combinatorics. Each hell-image is not an arbitrary punishment invented from outside but, the text insists throughout, "self-summoned and self-received" (自業所感) — the same faculty that committed the act becomes, quite literally, the very organ through which its consequence is felt.

Hungry Ghosts 十種鬼

Once a being's hell-karma is exhausted, if other unresolved offenses remain — breaking monastic discipline, violating the bodhisattva precepts, or defaming the Buddha's own nirvāṇa — that being takes on the form of a ghost, sorted by which particular craving drove the original offense:

1. Craving for objects 怪鬼 — takes shape by attaching to an object; "the strange ghost."
2. Craving for sex 魃鬼 — takes shape by attaching to wind; "the drought-demon ghost."
3. Craving to deceive 魅鬼 — takes shape by attaching to an animal; "the charm ghost."
4. Craving for resentment 蠱毒鬼 — takes shape by attaching to an insect; "the venom-gu ghost."
5. Craving to brood 癘鬼 — takes shape by attaching to decline; "the plague ghost."
6. Craving for pride 餓鬼 — takes shape by attaching to vital energy; "the hungry ghost" proper.
7. Craving to deceive by scheming 魘鬼 — takes shape by attaching to darkness; "the nightmare ghost."
8. Craving for [false] insight 魍魎鬼 — takes shape by attaching to a spirit; "the shade ghost."
9. Craving for accomplishment 役使鬼 — takes shape by attaching to brightness; "the servant ghost."
10. Craving for faction 傳送鬼 — takes shape by attaching to a person; "the courier ghost."

"Ānanda, all of these beings fall this way through pure emotion, their karmic fire burning them dry, so that they rise up out of the hells to become ghosts — every one of them self-summoned by their own false, deluded karma. Awaken to bodhi, and this wondrous, perfect brightness was never, in truth, anything at all."

Animals: Repaying Old Debts, and Return to the Human Realm 畜生 · 復形人道

Once a ghost's karma is likewise exhausted, both emotion and thought become empty, and such a being meets, in this world, the very person to whom it owes a debt — taking the body of an animal to repay it. Each of the ten kinds of ghosts above, once its ghost-karma ends, is reborn in the world most often as a corresponding kind of creature: the object-ghost as an owl-like bird; the wind-ghost as an ill-omened creature; the animal-charm ghost as a fox; the venom-gu ghost as a poisonous creature; the plague ghost as a tapeworm-like parasite; the vital-energy ghost as livestock bred for food; the darkness ghost as a creature valued for its hide or plumage; the spirit-shade ghost as an auspicious creature valued as an omen; the brightness ghost as a creature marked as a good omen; and the person-attached ghost as a creature that merely follows human company.

If an animal's repayment exceeds the actual debt, such a being is reborn again as a human, to reclaim the excess — and if the original creditor had both strength and merit, they need not give up the human body at all, but simply repay in kind; without merit, they return once more to animal form to pay off what remains. "Ānanda, you should know: if payment is made in money or in labor, once the debt is settled it naturally stops. But if, in the meantime, one kills the debtor and eats its flesh, then through kalpas as numerous as motes of dust, both will go on devouring and killing one another, endlessly rising and falling in turn like a wheel, with no rest at all — unless śamatha is cultivated, or a buddha appears in the world, there is no way to bring it to a stop." Each of the ten kinds of creature, once its debt is fully repaid, returns to human form once more, and takes its place among a corresponding human temperament — stubborn, foolish, willful, mediocre, petty, gentle, laborious, cultured, perceptive, or capable — "all of them, having exhausted an old debt, returning once more to the human path: all of them, from beginningless time, through the distorted accounting of karma, generating and destroying one another. Not meeting a Tathāgata, not hearing the true Dharma, turning naturally in this dust and toil — these are truly beings to be pitied."

Ten Kinds of Immortals 十種仙

Apart from these, the Buddha explains, there are humans who, without relying on true awakening to cultivate samādhi, cultivate instead a separate path of false thought, fixing their form through sheer concentration, and roam the mountains and forests, in places beyond human reach — ten kinds of immortals:

1. Earth-walking immortal 地行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing reliance on diet and elixirs.
2. Flying immortal 飛行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing reliance on herbs and medicines.
3. Wandering immortal 遊行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing reliance on transmuting metals and minerals.
4. Sky-walking immortal 空行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing practice of movement and stillness [breath and exercise].
5. Heaven-walking immortal 天行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing cultivation of the bodily fluids.
6. Communicating immortal 通行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing absorption of essences [solar and lunar energies].
7. Way-walking immortal 道行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing practice of mantras and spells.
8. Illuminating immortal 照行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing concentration of thought and recollection.
9. Essence-cultivating immortal 精行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing sexual union [alchemical practices of this kind].
10. Transcendent immortal 絕行仙 — perfected through firm, unceasing transformation and realization.

"Ānanda, all of these refine the mind through purely human means, without following true awakening, but attain instead a separate principle of life, living a thousand or ten thousand years, resting deep in the mountains or on some island in the great sea, cut off entirely from human company. Yet this too is merely a further turning of false thought within the wheel of rebirth; without cultivating true samādhi, once the reward is exhausted, they return, and scatter once more among the various destinies."

The Six Heavens of the Desire Realm 欲界六天

The fascicle closes with the six heavens still within the desire realm, each defined by how much residual desire for sensual union remains:

1. The Heaven of the Four Heavenly Kings 四天王天 — for those who cannot yet give up their spouses' love, but whose mind does not stray into illicit union, and grows clear and bright of itself — at death, they dwell close to the sun and moon.
2. The Trāyastriṃśa Heaven 忉利天 — for those whose desire toward their own spouse has grown faint, not fully present even in a time set aside for it — at death, they rise beyond the light of sun and moon, dwelling at the very summit of the human realm.
3. The Yāma Heaven 須焰摩天 — for those who meet desire only briefly, with no lingering thought once it passes, their waking life more often still than stirred — at death, they abide luminously within empty space itself, their own light no longer needing the sun and moon, which cannot reach that high.
4. The Tuṣita Heaven 兜率陀天 — for those at rest at every moment, who cannot yet fully refuse desire when it presents itself — at death, they rise to a subtle, refined realm entirely disconnected from the lower heavens and the human world, untouched even by the three calamities that destroy a world-cycle.
5. The Heaven of Bliss through Transformation 樂變化天 — for those with no desiring mind of their own, who merely go along with the world's ways when the occasion arises, finding it as tasteless as chewing wax — at death, they are born into a realm beyond the transformed [where desire's objects are conjured rather than sought].
6. The Heaven of Mastery over Others' Transformations 他化自在天 — for those with no thought of the world at all, yet who outwardly go along with worldly affairs, transcending them completely even in the moment of doing so — at death, they are able to pass entirely beyond both the transformed and the untransformed realms alike.

"Ānanda, in all six of these heavens, though the body has left coarse activity behind, traces of the mind still intermingle with it. From this point downward [including these six], all is called the desire realm."

大佛頂萬行首楞嚴經卷第八 — End of Fascicle Eight

Reading note: Notice the single variable running through all six heavens, exactly continuous with the "emotion versus thought" ratio that organized the fall into the lower destinies: not the presence or absence of desire as a binary, but its diminishing residue, heaven by heaven, until even the outward performance of worldly affairs becomes something done "as tasteless as chewing wax." Fascicle 9 continues upward from here, into the form and formless realms that lie beyond desire altogether, before turning to the sūtra's other great set-piece: the fifty states of demonic interference that can arise even in genuine meditation.
