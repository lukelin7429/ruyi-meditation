What in You Never Grows Old?

Fascicle 2 turns from refuting where the mind is to directly pointing at the unmoving, unborn nature of seeing itself — through King Prasenajit's aging body, the "eight things returned to their origins," and the teaching that the five aggregates are, each one, the tathāgatagarbha.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 2 complete Source: Taishō T19, no. 945 Covers: the entire fascicle — King Prasenajit's dialogue through the five aggregates

About This Fascicle

Fascicle 1 ended with Ānanda unable to say what actually does the seeing, once the eye and the lamp have both been ruled out. Fascicle 2 answers that question — not by argument alone, but by turning the whole assembly's attention toward something none of them had thought to doubt: the nature of seeing itself, which, unlike the body, never ages, and, unlike any object, cannot itself be turned into an object of sight.

This page is Ru-Yi's own translation, made directly from the Chinese text preserved by CBETA (public domain). It does not reproduce or paraphrase the Buddhist Text Translation Society's existing English edition; see the translation notes for terminology choices, and the companion Jiǎngyì project for Master Yuanying's classical commentary on this same material.

What Never Grows Old 波斯匿王請示不生滅性

Chinese Source

爾時，阿難及諸大眾聞佛示誨，身心泰然。念無始來失却本心，妄認緣塵分別影事，今日開悟，如失乳兒忽遇慈母。合掌禮佛，願聞如來顯出身心真妄虛實，現前生滅與不生滅二發明性。

波斯匿王起立白佛：「我昔未承諸佛誨勅，見迦旃延、毘羅胝子，咸言：『此身死後斷滅，名為涅槃。』我雖值佛，今猶狐疑。云何發揮，證知此心不生滅地？今此大眾諸有漏者，咸皆願聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time Ānanda and the whole assembly, hearing the Buddha's instruction, felt their bodies and minds grow calm and at ease. Reflecting that, from beginningless time, they had lost their own original mind and mistakenly clung to the shadow-play of conditioned, discriminating thought, they now felt awakened — like an infant who has lost its milk and suddenly meets its loving mother again. Joining their palms, they bowed to the Buddha, wishing to hear the Tathāgata reveal, of body and mind alike, what is true and what is false, what is empty and what is real, and to make plain the nature of what before us arises and ceases and what does not.

King Prasenajit rose and said to the Buddha: "In the past, before I received the guidance of the buddhas, I heard Kātyāyana and Vairaṭīputra both claim that after this body dies it is utterly annihilated, and that this is what is called nirvāṇa. Though I have now met the Buddha, I still harbor this doubt. How can it be shown and proven that this mind reaches a state beyond birth and death? All those with outflows still remaining in this assembly wish equally to hear the answer."

佛告大王：「汝身現存，今復問汝：汝此肉身為同金剛常住不朽？為復變壞？」「世尊！我今此身終從變滅。」佛言：「大王！汝未曾滅，云何知滅？」「世尊！我此無常變壞之身雖未曾滅，我觀現前念念遷謝，新新不住；如火成灰，漸漸銷殞，殞亡不息，決知此身當從滅盡。」

佛言：「如是，大王！汝今生齡已從衰老，顏貌何如童子之時？」「世尊！我昔孩孺，膚腠潤澤，年至長成血氣充滿，而今頹齡迫於衰耄，形色枯悴，精神昏昧，髮白面皺，逮將不久，如何見比充盛之時！」

The Buddha said to the great king: "Your body still exists here before me — let me ask you: is this fleshly body of yours as everlasting and indestructible as vajra, or does it change and decay?" "World-Honored One, this body of mine will, in the end, change and perish." The Buddha said: "Great king, it has not yet perished — how do you know it will perish?" "World-Honored One, although this impermanent, decaying body of mine has not yet perished, I observe that, moment after moment, it is passing away, never for an instant staying the same — like a fire that gradually reduces itself to ash, extinguishing itself without rest. I know for certain that this body must, in the end, come to a complete end."

The Buddha said: "Just so, great king. Your years now bring you toward old age — how does your present countenance compare to when you were a child?" "World-Honored One, in my infancy my skin was moist and supple; when I reached my prime, my blood and vitality were full; but now, in this declining age, drawing near to senility, my form and complexion are withered, my spirit dull, my hair white, my face lined, and I have not much time left — how could this compare to the fullness of my prime!"

佛言：「大王！汝之形容應不頓朽？」王言：「世尊！變化密移，我誠不覺，寒暑遷流，漸至於此。何以故？我年二十雖號年少，顏貌已老初十年時；三十之年又衰二十；于今六十又過于二，觀五十時宛然強壯。世尊！我見密移，雖此殂落其間流易且限十年。若復令我微細思惟：『其變寧唯一紀二紀，實為年變；豈唯年變，亦兼月化；何直月化，兼又日遷。』沈思諦觀，剎那剎那、念念之間不得停住，故知我身終從變滅。」

佛言：「大王！汝見變化遷改不停，悟知汝滅；亦於滅時，知汝身中有不滅耶？」波斯匿王合掌白佛：「我實不知！」

The Buddha said: "Great king, surely your appearance did not decay all at once?" The king said: "World-Honored One, the change has crept along in secret; I truly never noticed it happening — the cold and the heat passing, little by little, until it came to this. Why do I say so? At twenty, though called young, my face was already older than at ten; at thirty it had declined further from twenty; and now, past sixty-two, looking back on fifty I seem to have still been vigorous then. World-Honored One, I see this secret change proceeding, marked off, roughly, in units of ten years. But if I reflect on it more minutely still: the change is not confined to a decade or two at a time — it is truly a change from year to year; and not only from year to year, but month to month; and not merely month to month, but day by day as well. Reflecting deeply and observing closely, moment after moment, instant after instant, it never once stays still — and so I know for certain that this body of mine must, in the end, undergo change and perish."

The Buddha said: "Great king, you see this unceasing change and transformation and thereby recognize that you will perish — but at the very time of that perishing, do you know whether there is, within your body, something that does not perish?" King Prasenajit, joining his palms, said to the Buddha: "I truly do not know."

佛言：「我今示汝不生滅性。大王！汝年幾時見恒河水？」王言：「我生三歲，慈母携我謁耆婆天，經過此流，爾時即知是恒河水。」佛言：「大王！如汝所說，二十之時衰於十歲，乃至六十，日月歲時念念遷變；則汝三歲見此河時，至年十三其水云何？」王言：「如三歲時宛然無異；乃至于今年六十二，亦無有異。」

佛言：「汝今自傷髮白面皺，其面必定皺於童年；則汝今時觀此恒河，與昔童時觀河之見，有童耄不？」王言：「不也，世尊！」

佛言：「大王！汝面雖皺，而此見精性未曾皺！皺者為變，不皺非變；變者受滅，彼不變者元無生滅，云何於中受汝生死？而猶引彼末伽梨等，都言此身死後全滅！」王聞是言，信知身後捨生趣生，與諸大眾踊躍歡喜，得未曾有。

The Buddha said: "I will now show you the nature that neither arises nor ceases. Great king, at what age did you first see the water of the Ganges?" The king said: "When I was three years old, my loving mother took me to pay homage at the shrine of the god Jīva, and on the way we passed this river — that was when I first knew it as the Ganges." The Buddha said: "Great king, as you have said, your body declined from twenty to a mere ten years' vigor, and on to sixty, changing moment by moment with the days, months, and years — so when you saw this river at age three, and again at age thirteen, how was the water then?" The king said: "It was exactly the same as when I was three — and even now, at sixty-two, it is no different at all."

The Buddha said: "You now grieve over your white hair and wrinkled face — your face is certainly more wrinkled now than in childhood. But when you look at this Ganges now, compared to how you looked at it as a child, is the seeing itself young in one case and old in the other?" The king said: "No, World-Honored One."

The Buddha said: "Great king, though your face is wrinkled, this essence of seeing has never once been wrinkled! What wrinkles is subject to change; what does not wrinkle is not subject to change. What changes is subject to perishing; what does not change is, from the very beginning, beyond birth and death — how could it undergo your birth and death? And yet you still cite the likes of Maskarin Gośālīputra, who claim that after this body dies everything is utterly annihilated!" Hearing this, the king came to believe and know that after this body is given up, one goes on to another rebirth, and he, together with the whole assembly, leapt for joy, having gained what they had never had before.

Reading note: The Buddha does not argue King Prasenajit out of his fear of annihilation with logic alone — he gets the king to notice something directly, in his own experience. The face in the mirror keeps changing; the river seen at three and the river seen at sixty-two look exactly the same, seen by exactly the same seeing. This "essence of seeing" (見精) that never wrinkles is fascicle 2's first concrete demonstration of something in Ānanda's own experience that is not subject to birth and death — and it is offered before any argument, as something the king can simply check for himself.

Upside-Down, or Right-Side Up? 垂手喻倒

Chinese Source

阿難即從座起，禮佛合掌，長跪白佛：「世尊！若此見聞必不生滅，云何世尊名我等輩遺失真性，顛倒行事？願興慈悲洗我塵垢！」

即時如來垂金色臂，輪手下指，示阿難言：「汝今見我母陀羅手為正為倒？」阿難言：「世間眾生以此為倒，而我不知誰正誰倒。」佛告阿難：「若世間人以此為倒，即世間人將何為正？」阿難言：「如來竪臂，兜羅綿手上指於空，則名為正。」

佛即竪臂，告阿難言：「若此顛倒首尾相換，諸世間人一倍瞻視，則知汝身與諸如來清淨法身比類發明；如來之身名正遍知，汝等之身號性顛倒。隨汝諦觀汝身、佛身，稱顛倒者，名字何處號為顛倒？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda then rose from his seat, bowed to the Buddha with palms joined, knelt at length, and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if this seeing and hearing are truly beyond birth and death, why does the World-Honored One say that we have lost our true nature and act in an upside-down way? I beg you, in your compassion, to wash away the dust that clouds us!"

At once the Tathāgata lowered his golden-hued arm, turned his hand palm-downward, and, pointing it toward the ground, said to Ānanda: "Do you see this hand of mine, marked with the wheel-sign, as right-side up or upside down?" Ānanda said: "Ordinary beings in the world would call this upside down; but I myself do not know which way is right and which is upside down." The Buddha said to Ānanda: "If people in the world call this upside down, what, then, would they call right-side up?" Ānanda said: "If the Tathāgata raised his arm and pointed his cotton-soft hand upward toward the sky, that would be called right-side up."

The Buddha then raised his arm and said to Ānanda: "If this is all it takes — head and tail simply switching places — for ordinary people in the world to see it doubly, one way and then the other, then you should understand, comparing your own body to the Tathāgatas' pure Dharma-body in just this way: the Tathāgata's body is called that of right and universal knowing, while your bodies are called, by their very nature, upside down. Look closely, then, at your own body and at the Buddha's body, and consider exactly where, in what you call 'upside down,' the name 'upside down' is actually to be found."

Reading note: The joke buried in this exchange is the point: a hand pointing down and a hand pointing up are, physically, the very same hand — "upside down" and "right-side up" are just two ways of describing an orientation, not two different kinds of hand. The Buddha uses this to needle at Ānanda's assumption that "true nature" and "upside-down, deluded existence" must be two different things happening to two different beings, rather than two ways of looking at the very same nature.

The Finger and the Moon, the Guest and the Host 法喻顯心：標指喻月 · 客塵喻

Chinese Source

于時阿難與諸大眾，瞪矒瞻佛目精不瞬，不知身心顛倒所在。佛興慈悲，哀愍阿難及諸大眾，發海潮音遍告同會：「諸善男子！我常說言：『色心諸緣及心所使、諸所緣法，唯心所現。』汝身、汝心，皆是妙明真精妙心中所現物。……譬如澄清百千大海，棄之、唯認一浮漚體目為全潮，窮盡瀛渤。汝等即是迷中倍人！如我垂手等無差別，如來說為可憐愍者。」

Ru-Yi Translation

At this, Ānanda and the whole assembly stared fixedly at the Buddha, their eyes unblinking, unable to find where in body or mind this "upside-down-ness" actually was. The Buddha, moved by compassion, taking pity on Ānanda and the assembly, sent forth a voice like the ocean tide and proclaimed to the whole gathering: "Good sons! I have always taught that form, mind, all their conditions, the mental factors that mind employs, and all the objects these take hold of, are nothing but what mind itself manifests. Your body and your mind are both things manifested from within the wondrous, bright, true, essential, wondrous mind. ... It is like abandoning the whole vast, clear ocean and recognizing only a single floating bubble as the entire tide, thinking it exhausts the whole sea. You are, in this delusion, doubly lost — just as with my hand turning one way or the other, no real difference at all — and this is why the Tathāgata calls you objects of pity."

阿難承佛悲救深誨，垂泣叉手而白佛言：「我雖承佛如是妙音，悟妙明心元所圓滿常住心地，而我悟佛現說法音，現以緣心，允所瞻仰；徒獲此心，未敢認為本元心地。願佛哀愍宣示圓音，拔我疑根歸無上道！」

佛告阿難：「汝等尚以緣心聽法，此法亦緣，非得法性。如人以手指月示人，彼人因指當應看月，若復觀指以為月體，此人豈唯亡失月輪，亦亡其指。……汝亦如是，若以分別我說法音為汝心者，此心自應離分別音、有分別性。譬如有客寄宿旅亭，暫止便去，終不常住；而掌亭人都無所去，名為亭主。……則汝心性各有所還，云何為主？」

Ānanda, receiving the Buddha's compassionate rescue and profound teaching, wept and, hands clasped, said to the Buddha: "Though I have received this wondrous teaching from the Buddha and understand that the wondrously bright mind is originally the complete and ever-abiding ground of mind, still, my very understanding of the Buddha's present teaching depends on a conditioned, grasping mind, which is what I now rely on to look up in reverence. I have merely obtained this conditioned mind — I do not yet dare recognize it as my own original ground of mind. I beg the Buddha, in compassion, to proclaim the complete teaching and pull out the root of my doubt, so that I may return to the unsurpassed path."

The Buddha said to Ānanda: "You are still using a conditioned, grasping mind to listen to the Dharma, and so this Dharma too becomes merely another condition — you have not grasped the true nature of the teaching. It is like a man who points his finger at the moon to show it to someone else: that person ought to look, by means of the finger, at the moon itself. But if instead he looks at the finger and takes it to be the moon's own body, then he has lost not only the moon but the finger as well. ... It is the same with you: if you take the mind that discriminates my spoken teaching to be your own mind, then this mind ought to have its own nature of discrimination apart from the sounds it discriminates. It is like a traveler who lodges for a night at a wayside inn — he stays a while and then departs, never remaining permanently; whereas the innkeeper, who has nowhere else to go, is rightly called the host of the inn. ... In just this way, your mind's own nature has, in every case, somewhere it returns to and departs from — how, then, could it be the host?"

Reading note: Two images do a great deal of work here. "The finger and the moon" warns against mistaking the teaching itself — even the Buddha's own spoken words — for the reality it only points toward. "The guest and the host" introduces the criterion the rest of the fascicle will use to sort the false from the true: whatever comes and goes, arising with a condition and departing when that condition ends, is a "guest," not the "host." The discriminating mind that only ever arises in response to what it hears is shown to be exactly this kind of guest.

The Eight Things That Return, and the One That Does Not 八還辨見

Chinese Source

佛告阿難：「……阿難！此大講堂，洞開東方，日輪昇天則有明耀；中夜黑月，雲霧晦暝，則復昏暗；戶牖之隙則復見通；牆宇之間則復觀擁；分別之處則復見緣；頑虛之中遍是空性；欝𡋯之象則紆昏塵；澄霽斂氛又觀清淨。阿難！汝咸看此諸變化相，吾今各還本所因處。云何本因？阿難！此諸變化，明還日輪；何以故？無日不明，明因屬日，是故還日。暗還黑月；通還戶牖；擁還牆宇；緣還分別；頑虛還空；欝𡋯還塵；清明還霽。則諸世間一切所有，不出斯類。汝見八種見精明性，當欲誰還！何以故？若還於明，則不明時無復見暗。雖明暗等種種差別，見無差別，諸可還者自然非汝；不汝還者非汝而誰？則知汝心本妙明淨，汝自迷悶喪本受輪，於生死中常被漂溺，是故如來名可憐愍。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda: "... Ānanda, this great lecture hall opens directly to the east: when the sun rises in the sky, there is bright radiance; in the black-moon depth of night, with clouds and fog, there is dimness and dark; through the gaps of doors and windows there is a further sense of openness; between walls there is a further sense of obstruction; where discrimination occurs, there is a further sense of what it takes as its object; in inert emptiness, there is, throughout, the nature of empty space; where dust rises thick, there is a haze of turbid dust; and when the air clears after rain, there is, once more, a sense of purity.

"Ānanda, you observe all these changing appearances — I will now return each of them to the place that is properly its own cause. What are these proper causes? Ānanda, of all these changes: brightness returns to the sun. Why? Without the sun there is no brightness, and since brightness has its cause in the sun, it returns to the sun. Darkness returns to the black moon. Openness returns to doors and windows. Obstruction returns to walls. The sense of an object returns to discrimination. Inert emptiness returns to empty space. Turbid haze returns to dust. Clarity returns to the clearing sky. Everything that exists in the whole world falls into one of these categories without exception.

"Now, as for the nature of your seeing that perceives these eight kinds of things — to whom should it be returned? Why do I ask this? Because if it could be returned to brightness, then, at a time when there is no brightness, it could no longer see darkness at all. Although brightness, darkness, and all these various things differ from one another, the seeing itself has no such differences. Whatever can be returned to something else is, by its very nature, not you; but that which cannot be returned anywhere — if that is not you, then what is it? You should therefore know that your own mind is, at its root, wondrously bright and pure; it is you yourself who, through delusion and confusion, have lost this original nature and undergone the wheel of rebirth, forever adrift and sinking in birth and death — and this is why the Tathāgata calls you an object of pity."

Reading note: This is one of the sūtra's most quoted teaching devices. Every quality that shows up in seeing has an external cause it can be traced back to and "returned" to — brightness traces back to the sun, darkness to the absence of the moon's light, and so on. Once everything returnable has been returned, what is left over — the seeing itself, which is present whether the scene is bright or dark, open or blocked — has nowhere left to be sent back to. Yuanying's commentary calls this "the one thing that cannot be returned, because it is you."

Is Seeing a Thing? 辨見非物

Chinese Source

阿難言：「我雖識此見性無還，云何得知是我真性？」佛告阿難：「……阿難！是諸近遠諸有物性，雖復差殊，同汝見精清淨所矚。則諸物類自有差別，見性無殊。此精妙明，誠汝見性。若見是物，則汝亦可見吾之見。若同見者名為見吾，吾不見時，何不見吾不見之處？若見不見，自然非彼不見之相。若不見吾不見之地，自然非物，云何非汝？又則汝今見物之時，汝既見物，物亦見汝；體性紛雜，則汝與我并諸世間不成安立。阿難！若汝見時，是汝非我；見性周遍，非汝而誰？云何自疑汝之真性，性汝不真，取我求實！」

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda said: "Though I now recognize that this nature of seeing has nowhere it can be returned to, how can I know that it is my own true nature?" The Buddha said to Ānanda: "... Ānanda, all these things, near and far, however much they differ from one another, are alike observed by the pure essence of your seeing. The various kinds of things have their own distinctions; the nature of seeing has none. This essential, wondrous brightness is truly your nature of seeing.

"If seeing were itself a thing, then you ought to be able to see my seeing too. If, when we both see the same thing, that counts as your seeing me, then at a time when I am not seeing, why do you not see the place where I am not seeing? And if you could see my not-seeing, that would obviously no longer be the mark of not-seeing at all. But if you cannot see the place where I am not seeing, then it is obviously not a thing — so how could it not be you? And further: at the very time you see some object, since you see the object, does the object also see you? If so, then subject and object would be hopelessly tangled together, and neither you, nor I, nor the world at all could stand established as they are. Ānanda, when you see, it is you who see, not I; the nature of seeing is all-pervading — if it is not you, then who is it? Why do you doubt your own true nature, treating your own nature as not real, and instead seeking something from me to count as real?"

Reading note: The argument turns on a simple test: things can be seen; seeing itself cannot be. If seeing were just one more object among others, it would have to be visible the way objects are — and then Ānanda ought to be able to see the Buddha's seeing, or see the "gap" where the Buddha isn't seeing anything, both of which are absurd. Whatever necessarily escapes being turned into an object in this way is exactly what is doing the seeing — and that, the Buddha insists, simply is Ānanda, not some third thing to go looking for.

Does Seeing Shrink to Fit a Room? 辨見無舒縮

Chinese Source

阿難白佛言：「世尊！若此見性必我非餘，我與如來觀四天王勝藏寶殿，居日月宮，此見周圓遍娑婆國；退歸精舍只見伽藍，清心戶堂但瞻簷廡。……此見如是，其體本來周遍一界，今在室中唯滿一室，為復此見縮大為小？為當牆宇夾令斷絕？」

佛告阿難：「一切世間大小、內外諸所事業，各屬前塵，不應說言見有舒縮。譬如方器中見方空，……若如汝問『入室之時縮見令小』，仰觀日時，汝豈挽見齊於日面？若築牆宇能夾見斷，穿為小竇，寧無續迹？是義不然。一切眾生從無始來迷己為物，失於本心，為物所轉；故於是中觀大、觀小。若能轉物，則同如來身心圓明，不動道場，於一毛端遍能含受十方國土。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, if this nature of seeing is truly me and nothing else, then, when the Tathāgata and I look upon the jeweled palace of the Four Heavenly Kings, dwelling amid the palaces of sun and moon, this seeing fills the whole Sahā world-system; yet when we withdraw to the monastery, I see only the monastery grounds, and, with a settled mind indoors, I see only as far as the eaves. ... This seeing's own substance is, from the start, all-pervading throughout a whole world — so why, now, indoors, does it fill only this one room? Has this seeing contracted from large to small? Or have the walls pinched it and cut it off?"

The Buddha said to Ānanda: "All the various matters of large and small, inner and outer, throughout the world, belong to what is in front of you — one should not say that seeing itself expands or contracts. It is like seeing a square shape of space inside a square container. ... If, as you ask, seeing contracts to become small on entering a room, then when you look up at the sun, do you stretch your seeing all the way out to meet the sun's very face? And if building a wall could pinch your seeing and cut it off, then boring a small hole through it should leave some trace of the seeing having been reconnected — but this is not how it works. From beginningless time, all sentient beings have mistaken themselves for things, lost their original mind, and been turned about by things — and so, within that confusion, they perceive things as large or small. But one who is able to turn things, rather than be turned by them, is the same as the Tathāgata, whose body and mind are perfectly bright, who does not move from the place of practice, and who, on the tip of a single hair, can fully contain the lands of the ten directions."

Reading note: The square-container analogy makes the point cleanly: the shape of space you see inside a square box is a fact about the box, not about space itself — space was never actually square, and removing the box doesn't require space to "expand" back to its true shape. In the same way, apparently seeing "more" outdoors and "less" indoors is a fact about the walls, not about seeing contracting and expanding. The closing line — "turn things, rather than be turned by them" — becomes a recurring standard the sūtra uses for what liberation actually looks like in practice.

Pointing to What, Exactly, Is Seeing 責令指陳非見

Chinese Source

佛告阿難：「今汝所言『見在汝前』，是義非實。……阿難當知，若空是見，既已成見，何者是空？若物是見，既已是見，何者為物？汝可微細披剝萬象，析出精明淨妙見元，指陳示我，同彼諸物分明無惑。」

阿難言：「我今於此重閣講堂，遠洎恒河、上觀日月，舉手所指、縱目所觀，指皆是物，無是見者。世尊！如佛所說，況我有漏初學聲聞，乃至菩薩亦不能於萬物象前剖出精見離一切物別有自性！」佛言：「如是，如是！」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha said to Ānanda: "What you have just said — that 'seeing is right in front of you' — is not actually true. ... Ānanda, you should know: if empty space were seeing, then, once space had already become seeing, what would be left to be called space? And if an object were seeing, then, once the object had already become seeing, what would be left to be called an object? Try, then, to pick apart every single phenomenon in the finest detail, and extract this pure, essential, wondrous origin of seeing, and point it out to me clearly, the way you would point out any of these other objects, leaving no room for doubt."

Ānanda said: "Now, in this many-storied lecture hall, as far as the Ganges, up to the sun and moon — everywhere I raise my hand to point, everywhere my eyes freely roam, whatever I point to is an object; there is nothing there that is 'the seeing' itself. World-Honored One, exactly as the Buddha has said — how much less could I, a beginning śrāvaka still with outflows, or even a bodhisattva, manage to extract, from in front of the ten thousand things, a pure seeing that has its own nature apart from every one of those things!" The Buddha said: "Just so, just so!"

Mañjuśrī's Question, and the Second Moon 文殊致問 · 第二月喻

Chinese Source

文殊師利法王子……而白佛言：「世尊！此諸大眾，不悟如來發明二種精見色空、是非是義。……唯願如來大慈發明，此諸物象與此見精元是何物，於其中間無是非是。」

佛告文殊及諸大眾：「十方如來及大菩薩，於其自住三摩地中，見與見緣并所想相，如虛空花本無所有；此見及緣，元是菩提妙淨明體，云何於中有是非是？文殊！吾今問汝，如汝文殊，更有文殊是文殊者？為無文殊？」「如是，世尊！我真文殊，無是文殊。何以故？若有是者，則二文殊。然我今日非無文殊，於中實無是非二相。」

佛言：「此見妙明與諸空塵，亦復如是。本是妙明無上菩提淨圓真心，妄為色空及與聞見，如第二月，誰為是月？又誰非月？文殊！但一月真，中間自無是月、非月。……由是精真妙覺明性，故能令汝出指非指！」

Ru-Yi Translation

Mañjuśrī, Dharma-Prince, ... said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, this whole assembly has not understood what the Tathāgata means by asking whether the two things, the essence of seeing and form-and-emptiness, 'are' this or 'are not' this. ... I beg only that the Tathāgata, in his great compassion, make clear what these various objects and this essence of seeing actually, originally are, in the space between 'is' and 'is not.'"

The Buddha said to Mañjuśrī and the whole assembly: "The Tathāgatas of the ten directions and the great bodhisattvas, abiding in their own samādhi, see that seeing itself, together with its objects and all conceived appearances, are, like flowers conjured in empty space, originally without any existence at all. This seeing and its objects are, originally, the wondrous, pure, bright substance of bodhi — so how could there be any 'is this' or 'is not this' within it? Mañjuśrī, let me ask you: besides you, Mañjuśrī, is there another Mañjuśrī who 'is' Mañjuśrī — or is there no such Mañjuśrī at all?" "Just so, World-Honored One. I am the true Mañjuśrī; there is no 'Mañjuśrī who is Mañjuśrī.' Why? Because if there were such a thing, there would be two Mañjuśrīs. And yet I am not, today, without a Mañjuśrī either — there is truly, in this matter, no such pair as 'is' and 'is not.'"

The Buddha said: "This wondrous brightness of seeing, together with all the appearances of emptiness and dust, is exactly the same. It is, originally, the wondrous, unsurpassed, pure, perfect, true mind of bodhi, mistakenly taken to be form and emptiness, hearing and seeing — like a second moon: which one, then, is 'the moon,' and which one 'is not the moon'? Mañjuśrī, there is only ever one true moon; in between, there is naturally no such thing as a moon that 'is' or a moon that 'is not.' ... It is precisely because of this true, essential, wondrously enlightened, bright nature that you are able to move beyond both pointing and not-pointing at all!"

Reading note: "The second moon" refers to the old image of pressing a finger against the eye and seeing one moon appear as two — the second moon is not simply nonexistent (something really does appear), yet it is not a second real moon either (there is, and always was, only one). The Buddha uses it, together with Mañjuśrī's own name, to dissolve the very question Ānanda kept trying to force an answer to: whether seeing "is" or "is not" its objects. The category itself, he suggests, does not apply here.

Not Cause and Condition, Not Spontaneity 辨非因緣非自然

Chinese Source

阿難白佛言：「世尊！誠如法王所說，覺緣遍十方界湛然常住，性非生滅；與先梵志娑毘迦羅所談冥諦，及投灰等諸外道種說有真我遍滿十方，有何差別？……我今觀此覺性自然，非生非滅，遠離一切虛妄顛倒，似非因緣與彼自然；云何開示，不入群邪，獲真實心妙覺明性？」

……佛言：「阿難！我說世間諸因緣相，非第一義。……見明之時，見非是明；見暗之時，見非是暗；見空之時，見非是空；見塞之時，見非是塞，四義成就。汝復應知，見見之時，見非是見，見猶離見，見不能及，云何復說因緣自然及和合相？」

Ru-Yi Translation

Ānanda said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, it is truly as the Dharma-king has said: this awareness and its conditions pervade the ten directions, calm and ever-abiding, its nature beyond birth and death. But how does this differ from what the ancient brahmacārin Kapila taught as the 'obscure principle,' or from the various non-Buddhist schools of ash-smeared ascetics who likewise speak of a true self pervading the ten directions? ... As I now observe it, this nature of awareness seems spontaneous, neither arising nor ceasing, far removed from every falsehood and inversion — it seems to be neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor the 'spontaneity' those others speak of. How, then, can I be shown a way that avoids falling among these various false views, and attains the true mind, the wondrously enlightened, bright nature?"

... The Buddha said: "Ānanda, when I speak of causes and conditions among worldly things, that is not the ultimate truth. ... At the moment of seeing brightness, the seeing itself is not brightness; at the moment of seeing darkness, the seeing itself is not darkness; at the moment of seeing emptiness, the seeing itself is not emptiness; at the moment of seeing obstruction, the seeing itself is not obstruction — these four points established. You should further understand: at the very moment of seeing seeing itself, that seeing is not itself an act of seeing either — seeing forever eludes being seen, seeing can never be reached this way. How, then, could one still speak of causes and conditions, of spontaneity, or of any state of 'combination' at all?"

Reading note: Ānanda's worry is a real one for any Buddhist audience: an unchanging, all-pervading awareness sounds uncomfortably close to the "self" (ātman) taught by rival schools like Sāṃkhya (Kapila's tradition). The Buddha's answer refuses both of the categories Ānanda offers — "arising from causes and conditions" and "existing spontaneously, on its own" — as equally beside the point. The closing line, "seeing seeing itself is not seeing" (見見之時，見非是見), is one of the sūtra's most compressed and most quoted formulations: awareness can never turn itself into one more object for itself to look at, which is exactly why no philosophical category built for objects — caused, uncaused, one, many, self, not-self — ever quite fits it.

Two Kinds of False Seeing 別業妄見 · 同分妄見

Chinese Source

「阿難！一切眾生輪迴世間，由二顛倒分別見妄，當處發生，當業輪轉。云何二見？一者、眾生別業妄見，二者、眾生同分妄見。

「云何名為別業妄見？阿難！如世間人目有赤眚，夜見燈光別有圓影五色重疊。……是故當知色實在燈，見病為影；影、見俱眚，見眚非病。……此亦如是，目眚所成，今欲名誰是燈、是見？何況分別非燈、非見！

「云何名為同分妄見？阿難！此閻浮提……若復此中有一小洲，只有兩國。唯一國人同感惡緣，則彼小洲當土眾生，覩諸一切不祥境界，或見二日，或見兩月，……種種惡相，但此國見；彼國眾生，本所不見，亦復不聞。」

Ru-Yi Translation

"Ānanda, all sentient beings revolve through the cycle of the world because of two kinds of inverted, discriminating false seeing, each arising in its own proper place, each turning according to its own karma. What are these two kinds of seeing? First, sentient beings' individual-karma false seeing. Second, sentient beings' shared-karma false seeing.

"What is meant by individual-karma false seeing? Ānanda, it is like a person in the world whose eye has a red inflammation, who at night, looking at a lamp's flame, sees a separate, layered halo of five colors around it. ... You should therefore know that the color truly belongs to the lamp; it is the eye disease that produces the halo. Both the halo and the seeing of it belong to the eye disease — the seeing of the disease is not itself the disease. ... It is the same here: it is produced by the eye's own affliction. Now, what should we call 'the lamp' and what should we call 'the seeing' in this? How much less could we go on to distinguish a 'not-lamp' and a 'not-seeing'!

"What is meant by shared-karma false seeing? Ānanda, in this continent of Jambudvīpa ... suppose there were, within it, a small island with only two countries on it, and the people of just one of those countries, together, had incurred some shared evil condition. Then the beings native to that small island alone would witness all manner of inauspicious signs: some seeing two suns, some seeing two moons ... All these various evil portents would be seen only by that one country; the beings of the other country would, from the start, neither see nor hear anything of the kind."

「阿難！如彼眾生別業妄見……例汝今日，以目觀見山河國土及諸眾生，皆是無始見病所成。見與見緣似現前境，元我覺明見所緣眚。覺見即眚，本覺明心覺緣非眚；覺所覺眚，覺非眚中，此實見見，云何復名覺、聞、知、見？……

「阿難！如彼眾生同分妄見……例閻浮提三千洲中，兼四大海娑婆世界，并洎十方諸有漏國及諸眾生，同是覺明無漏妙心，見、聞、覺、知虛妄病緣、和合妄生，和合妄死。若能遠離諸和合緣及不和合，則復滅除諸生死因；圓滿菩提不生滅性，清淨本心本覺常住。」

"Ānanda, just as with that individual's karmic false seeing ... so it is with you today: your eyes' observation of mountains, rivers, lands, and all sentient beings is likewise entirely produced by a beginningless disease of seeing. Seeing, together with what seeing takes as its condition, resembles a scene appearing before us — but it is originally only the affliction that my own bright awareness takes as its condition of seeing. Awareness-in-seeing is itself the affliction; the original, bright mind of awareness, aware of its condition, is not itself afflicted. That which is aware of the afflicted-seeing-as-object is, itself, not within the affliction — this is truly 'seeing the seeing' — so how could it still be called by names like awareness, hearing, knowing, or seeing?

"Ānanda, just as with that shared karmic false seeing ... so it is throughout Jambudvīpa's three thousand continents, together with the four great oceans of this Sahā world, and throughout every impure land of the ten directions and every being within them: all alike are the bright, undefiled, wondrous mind of awareness. Seeing, hearing, awareness, and knowing arise falsely, in combination, from the false, afflicted condition, and perish falsely, in combination, as well. If one is able to remove oneself far from every condition of combination, and from non-combination too, then one thereby also eliminates every cause of birth and death, and fully realizes the unborn, unceasing nature of bodhi — the pure, original mind, original awareness, ever-abiding."

Reading note: The two kinds of false seeing scale the same diagnosis up from the individual to the whole shared world. A diseased eye sees a halo around a lamp that isn't really there — but the disease is in the eye, not the seeing itself, which remains untouched by what it mistakenly perceives. In just the same way, an entire population can share a "disease" of perception (their collective karma) that makes them see mountains, rivers, and each other as solid, external, and separate from mind — while the awareness underneath that shared affliction remains, in itself, no more diseased than the healthy seeing hidden beneath any one person's blurred vision.

The Five Aggregates Are the Tathāgatagarbha 五陰本如來藏妙真如性

The fascicle closes with the Buddha taking each of the five aggregates (form, sensation, perception, formations, and consciousness) in turn, and showing, with a different everyday analogy for each, that none of them truly arises from any cause, or from itself, or from nowhere at all — each is, at root, nothing other than the tathāgatagarbha, described here for the first time by name.

Form: Flowers Conjured in Empty Space 色陰 · 空花喻

「阿難！譬如有人，以清淨目觀晴明空，唯一晴虛，逈無所有。其人無故不動目睛、瞪以發勞，則於虛空別見狂花，復有一切狂亂非相。色陰當知亦復如是。……若空來者，既從空來還從空入，若有出入即非虛空。……若目出者，既從目出還從目入；……是故當知色陰虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。」

"Ānanda, it is like a person who, with clear eyes, gazes at a clear, open sky — nothing there but open clarity, utterly empty. If, for no reason, this person holds his eyes fixed and unmoving until they grow strained, he will see, apart from the sky itself, wild flowers appear in the empty space, along with all manner of wild, disordered, unreal shapes. You should know that the aggregate of form is exactly the same. ... If these flowers came from empty space, then, having come from space, they should return into space — but if they truly went in and out like this, then space would no longer be truly empty. ... If they came out of the eye, then, having come from the eye, they should return into the eye. ... You should therefore know that the aggregate of form is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

Sensation: Rubbing the Hands Together 受陰 · 摩掌喻

「阿難！譬如有人，手足宴安，百骸調適，忽如忘生，性無違順。其人無故以二手掌於空相摩，於二手中妄生澁、滑、冷、熱諸相。受陰當知亦復如是。……若空來者，既能觸掌何不觸身？……若從掌出，應非待合。……是故當知受陰虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。」

"Ānanda, it is like a person whose hands and feet are perfectly at ease, whose whole body is comfortable, who has all but forgotten the sensation of being alive, with no feeling of anything pleasant or unpleasant at all. If, for no reason, this person rubs his two palms together in empty space, all sorts of sensations — roughness, smoothness, cold, heat — arise falsely between the two hands. You should know that the aggregate of sensation is exactly the same. ... If these sensations came from empty space, since space could touch the palms, why would it not also touch the rest of the body? ... And if they came from the palms alone, they should not need to wait for the two hands to come together at all. ... You should therefore know that the aggregate of sensation is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

Perception: The Thought of a Sour Plum 想陰 · 說梅喻

「阿難！譬如有人，談說醋梅口中水出，思踏懸崖足心酸澁。想陰當知亦復如是。……若梅生者，梅合自談何待人說？若從口入，自合口聞，何須待耳？……是故當知想陰虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。」

"Ānanda, it is like a person who, on hearing someone talk about sour plums, finds water welling up in his mouth, or who, thinking of stepping to the edge of a sheer cliff, feels his feet grow weak and tense. You should know that the aggregate of perception is exactly the same. ... If this sourness arose from the plum itself, the plum ought to be able to talk about itself — why would it need a person to speak of it? And if it entered through the mouth, the mouth itself ought to hear the words — why would it need to wait for the ears to hear them first? ... You should therefore know that the aggregate of perception is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

Formations: A Rushing Torrent 行陰 · 暴流喻

「阿難！譬如暴流波浪相續，前際後際不相踰越。行陰當知亦復如是。……若因空生，則諸十方無盡虛空成無盡流，世界自然俱受淪溺。若因水有，則此暴流性應非水，……是故當知行陰虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。」

"Ānanda, it is like a rushing torrent, its waves following one upon another in unbroken succession, each earlier wave never overtaking the one that comes after. You should know that the aggregate of mental formations is exactly the same. ... If this flowing nature arose from empty space, then all the boundless empty space of the ten directions would become a boundless torrent, and every world-system would naturally be submerged and drowned. If it arose from water, then this rushing flow could not itself be identical with water... You should therefore know that the aggregate of formations is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

Consciousness: A Sealed Vase of Air 識陰 · 頻伽瓶喻

「阿難！譬如有人，取頻伽瓶塞其兩孔，滿中擎空，千里遠行用餉他國。識陰當知亦復如是。阿難！如是虛空，非彼方來，非此方入。……是故當知識陰虛妄，本非因緣、非自然性。」

大佛頂萬行首楞嚴經卷第二

"Ānanda, it is like a person who takes a kalaviṅka-shaped vase, plugs both its openings, fills it entirely with ordinary air, and carries it a thousand miles to give as a gift in another country. You should know that the aggregate of consciousness is exactly the same. Ānanda, this air inside the vase did not come from that other place, nor does it, on arrival, actually enter this new place. ... You should therefore know that the aggregate of consciousness is false — originally neither a matter of causes and conditions, nor of any spontaneous nature."

大佛頂萬行首楞嚴經卷第二 — End of Fascicle Two

Reading note: Each analogy isolates the same structural point: the illusory flowers are neither in the sky nor in the eye; the false sensations are neither in space nor in either hand alone; the watering mouth is caused neither by the plum nor by the ear alone; the torrent is identical with neither empty space nor with water as such; the air in the sealed vase neither truly "comes from" its origin nor truly "enters" its destination. Each aggregate, examined this closely, turns out not to be an independently existing thing that was caused into being at all — which is exactly why it can be, without contradiction, identical with the tathāgatagarbha (如來藏, the "womb" or "matrix" of Buddhahood) rather than some obstruction standing in front of it. Fascicle 3 goes on to apply this same method to the twelve sense-fields and eighteen elements.
