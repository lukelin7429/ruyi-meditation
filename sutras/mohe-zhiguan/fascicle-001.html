Fascicle 1 · 摩訶止觀卷第一

No Earlier Age Had Heard Its Like

This page begins Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Mohe Zhiguan (摩訶止觀, "The Great Calming and Contemplation"), the Tiantai founder's longest and most difficult treatise, and one of the three great works of Chinese Buddhism traditionally grouped as the "Tiantai Three Great Works" (天台三大部). We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Status: fascicle 1 complete Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T46, no. 1911 Covers: the transmission lineage behind the teaching, the three kinds of calming-and-contemplation, and the opening of topic one, the great intent

Project Background

Zhiyi (智顗, 538–597 CE) is the founder of the Tiantai school and one of the great systematizers of Chinese Buddhism. The Mohe Zhiguan is the last and longest of his three major works on meditation, delivered as oral lectures at Yuquan Monastery (玉泉寺) in Jingzhou in 594 CE and recorded directly by his disciple Guanding (灌頂) — unlike Zhiyi's other treatises, this one was never revised into a second, polished pass, and the lecture series itself was cut short before its announced outline could be completed. It runs to ten fascicles in the Taishō edition and treats calming-and-contemplation (止觀, śamatha-vipaśyanā) not as a technique but as the single teaching underlying every buddha's path, worked out through Zhiyi's own tenfold outline: the great intent, the name, the essence, the scope, the relation to partial and complete teachings, the preliminary expedients, the actual practice, its fruits, the teachings that arise from it, and where it all ultimately leads.

This treatise has an existing English translation, Neal Donner and Daniel B. Stevenson's The Great Calming and Contemplation (University of Hawaii Press, 1993), which covers this same first chapter in scholarly depth. This translation is made independently, directly from the Chinese source, without reference to that published translation — the same principle already followed for Zhiyi's other treatise on this site, the Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen, and for Dharmamitra's translations of Zhiyi's shorter meditation manuals.

Translation Method

  • Made directly from the Chinese source text preserved by CBETA, which is in the public domain — Zhiyi died in 597 CE, well over a millennium ago.
  • CBETA's edition splits fascicle 1 into "upper" and "lower" halves (之上 / 之下) for print-volume reasons; we treat both halves as one continuous fascicle, matching how the content is actually organized, and matching this site's existing convention for the Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen.
  • Zhiyi's own tenfold outline (十章) — see the translation notes for the running glossary — is kept as the structure for the whole treatise; fascicle 1 covers only the first of the ten, the great intent (大意), itself broken into five brief points that this fascicle covers in full.
  • Every completed section can be revised after review by Dharma teachers and readers familiar with the Chinese text.

Opening the Treatise 卷第一上

Chinese Source

摩訶止觀卷第一上
隋天台智者大師說
門人灌頂記

Ru-Yi Translation

The Great Calming and Contemplation, Fascicle One, Upper Part
Spoken by the Great Master Zhiyi of Tiantai, of the Sui dynasty
Recorded by his disciple Guanding

The Origin of This Teaching 緣起

Chinese Source

止觀明靜，前代未聞。智者，大隋開皇十四年四月二十六日，於荊州玉泉寺，一夏敷揚、二時慈霔，雖樂說不窮，纔至「見境」，法輪停轉，後分弗宣。然挹流尋源，聞香討根。論曰：「我行無師保。」經云：「受莂於定光。」書言：「生知者上，學而次良。」法門浩妙，為天真獨朗？為從藍而青？行人若聞《付法藏》，則識宗元。

Ru-Yi Translation

Calming-and-contemplation, luminous and still — no earlier age had heard its like. In the fourteenth year of the Kaihuang era of the great Sui, on the twenty-sixth day of the fourth month, the Master, at Yuquan Monastery in Jingzhou, spread this teaching through a single summer's retreat, its rain of compassion falling morning and evening. Though he delighted in teaching without exhaustion, he had reached only the discussion of "the objects contemplated" when the wheel of the Dharma stopped turning, and its latter portion was never proclaimed. Yet one may still trace the stream back to its source, and follow the fragrance back to its root. A treatise says: "In my own practice, I had no teacher to rely on." A sūtra says: "He received his prediction of buddhahood from Dīpaṃkara." A classical text says: "Those born already knowing are of the highest kind; those who attain knowledge through study come next." Is this vast and wondrous Dharma-gate, then, a light shining from heaven-born nature alone, needing nothing else — or is it "indigo drawn out from the indigo plant," a blue deeper than the blue it came from? If the practitioner hears the Account of the Transmission of the Dharma-Treasury, they will come to recognize this teaching's own lineage and source.

大覺世尊積劫行滿，涉六年以伏見，舉一指而降魔。始鹿苑，中鷲頭，後鶴林，法付大迦葉。迦葉八分舍利，結集三藏，法付阿難。阿難河中入風三昧，四派其身，法付商那和修。修手兩甘露、現五百法門，法付毱多。多在俗得三果，受戒得四果，法付提迦多。多登壇得初果，三羯磨得四果，法付彌遮迦。迦付佛馱難提。提付佛馱蜜多。多授王三歸，降伏算者，法付脇比丘。比丘出胎髮白，手放光取經，法付富那奢。奢論勝馬鳴，剃髮為弟子。鳴造《賴吒和羅妓》，妓音演無常、苦、空，聞者悟道，法付毘羅。羅造《無我論》，論所向處邪見消滅，法付龍樹。樹生生身、龍成法身，法付提婆。婆鑿天眼施萬肉眼，法付羅睺羅。羅識鬼名書，降伏外道，法付僧佉難提。提說偈試羅漢，法付僧佉耶奢。奢遊海見城說偈，法付鳩摩羅馱。馱見萬騎，記馬色、得人名、分別衣，法付闍夜那。那為犯重人作火坑，令入懺悔，坑成池，罪滅，法付盤馱。馱付摩奴羅。羅分恒河為二分，自化一分，法付鶴勒夜那。那付師子。師子為檀彌羅王所害，劍斬流乳。付法藏人，始迦葉，終師子，二十三人。末田地與商那同時取之，則二十四人。諸師皆金口所記，並是聖人，能多利益。昔王不立厩於寺，立厩於屠，況好世值聖，寧無益耶？又婆羅門貨髑髏，孔達者、半者、不者，達者起塔禮供得生天。聞法之要，功德若此，佛為此益，付法藏也。

The Great Enlightened World-Honored One, his practice over many kalpas fulfilled, passed through six years subduing wrong views, and with a single raised finger vanquished Māra. Teaching first at the Deer Park, then at Vulture Peak, and last beneath the twin śāla trees, he entrusted the Dharma to Mahākāśyapa. Kāśyapa divided the relics into eight shares, compiled the Tripiṭaka, and entrusted the Dharma to Ānanda. Ānanda, in the middle of a river, entered the wind-samādhi and divided his body into four streams; he entrusted the Dharma to Śāṇakavāsa. He, with both hands, poured forth two streams of ambrosia and manifested five hundred dharma-gates; he entrusted the Dharma to Upagupta. He, while still a layman, attained the third fruit, and upon receiving ordination attained the fourth; he entrusted the Dharma to Dhītika. He, upon ascending the ordination platform, attained the first fruit, and through the threefold formal procedure attained the fourth; he entrusted the Dharma to Miccaka. He entrusted it to Buddhanandi. He entrusted it to Buddhamitra. He gave the three refuges to a king and subdued a heretical diviner; he entrusted the Dharma to the Venerable Pārśva. He was born from the womb with hair already white, and light shone from his hand as he took up the sūtras; he entrusted the Dharma to Puṇyayaśas. He, in debate, defeated Aśvaghoṣa, who then shaved his head to become his disciple. Aśvaghoṣa composed the play Rāṣṭrapāla, whose melody proclaimed impermanence, suffering, and emptiness, so that those who heard it awakened to the Way; he entrusted the Dharma to Kapimala. He composed the Treatise on No-Self, and wherever it reached, wrong views dissolved of themselves; he entrusted the Dharma to Nāgārjuna. His name itself tells his story — the "tree" naming his birth in the flesh, the "dragon" naming the completion of his Dharma-body; he entrusted the Dharma to Āryadeva. He opened the heavenly eye within himself and bestowed its sight upon ten thousand eyes of flesh; he entrusted the Dharma to Rāhulabhadra. He could read the very script of ghosts, and subdued non-Buddhist rivals; he entrusted the Dharma to Saṅghānandi. He spoke a verse to test an arhat; he entrusted the Dharma to Saṅghayaśas. He, journeying by sea, beheld a city and spoke a verse upon it; he entrusted the Dharma to Kumāralāta. He, upon seeing ten thousand horsemen pass, recorded the color of every horse, the name of every rider, and the cut of every garment; he entrusted the Dharma to Jayata. He, for a man who had committed a grave offense, conjured a pit of fire and had him enter it in repentance — the pit became a pool, and the offense was extinguished; he entrusted the Dharma to Vasubandhu the Elder. He entrusted it to Manorhita. He divided the Ganges into two streams, transforming one of them by his own power alone; he entrusted the Dharma to Haklenayaśas. He entrusted it to Siṃha. Siṃha was put to death by King Damira; when the sword fell, milk, not blood, flowed from the wound. Those who transmitted the Dharma-treasury, beginning with Kāśyapa and ending with Siṃha, number twenty-three; counting Madhyāntika, reckoned together with Śāṇakavāsa as his contemporary, makes twenty-four. Every one of these masters was foretold by the Buddha's own golden mouth, and every one was a sage, able to bring great benefit to others. Of old, a king would not build his stables at a monastery, choosing to build them by a slaughterhouse instead — how much more, then, in a good age, does one who actually encounters a sage fail to benefit? And there was once a brāhmaṇa who traded in skulls, testing each by whether a probe passed cleanly through it, halfway through, or not at all — the skull it passed cleanly through belonged to one who, in life, had built stūpas, paid reverence, and made offerings, and so had gained rebirth in heaven. The merit of hearing the essential Dharma is like this. It was for this very benefit that the Buddha caused the Dharma-treasury to be transmitted at all.

Reading note: This lineage passage is drawn from the Account of the Transmission of the Dharma-Treasury (付法藏因緣傳), a text recounting twenty-four successive Indian patriarchs from Mahākāśyapa to Siṃha Bhikṣu. Each vignette is a single compressed clause — often, as with Nāgārjuna, a pun on the master's own name — and would have been immediately recognizable to Zhiyi's original monastic audience the way a string of one-line Sunday-school references might be to a churchgoing one; most are left here in their compressed, allusive form rather than expanded into full legends we cannot verify with confidence. The point of citing all twenty-four is to answer the question the opening paragraph poses: is this teaching self-illuminating "heaven-born" genius, needing no lineage — or something handed down, "indigo drawn from indigo," each teacher exceeding the one before? The answer given is the latter, and this passage supplies the receipts before naming Zhiyi's own two immediate teachers next.

此之止觀，天台智者說己心中所行法門。智者生，光滿室，目現雙瞳。行法華經懺，發陀羅尼，代受法師講金字《般若》。陳、隋二國宗為帝師。安禪而化，位居五品。故經云：「施四百萬億那由他國人，一一皆與七寶，又化令得六通，不如初隨喜人百千萬倍。」況五品耶？文云：「即如來使，如來所使，行如來事。」《大經》云：「是初依菩薩。」

As for this Calming-and-Contemplation: the Tiantai Master Zhiyi expounded the dharma-gate practiced within his own mind. When the Master was born, light filled the room, and his eyes showed a double pupil in each. Practicing the Lotus Repentance, he gave rise to dhāraṇī, and, standing in for his own teacher, lectured on the gold-lettered Prajñāpāramitā. Both the Chen and the Sui courts honored him as Teacher to the Emperor. He passed away seated in meditation, his own attainment resting, by his own account, at no more than the Five Grades of Disciple. Yet the sūtra says: "One who gives to four hundred myriad koṭis of nayutas of people, bestowing on each of them the seven treasures, and moreover leads them all to attain the six supernormal powers — this does not equal one hundred-thousand myriad koṭi-th part of the merit of the very first person who simply rejoices on hearing the sūtra." How much more, then, the Five Grades? The text also says: "Such a one is the Tathāgata's own messenger, sent by the Tathāgata, carrying out the Tathāgata's work." And the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "This is a bodhisattva of the first reliance."

智者師事南岳，南岳德行不可思議，十年專誦，七載方等，九旬常坐，一時圓證，大小法門朗然洞發。南岳事慧文禪師，當齊高之世獨步河淮，法門非世所知，履地戴天莫知高厚。文師用心一依《釋論》，論是龍樹所說，《付法藏》中第十三師。智者《觀心論》云：「歸命龍樹師。」驗知龍樹是高祖師也。疑者云：「《中論》遣蕩，《止觀》建立，云何得同？」然天竺注論凡七十家，不應是青目而非諸師。又《論》云：「因緣所生法，我說即是空，亦為是假名，亦是中道義。」云云。

The Master served as disciple to Nanyue [Huisi]. Nanyue's own virtue and practice were beyond conception: ten years devoted wholly to recitation of the Lotus Sūtra, seven years given to the Vaipulya repentance rite, ninety days of unbroken sitting — and in a single moment he perfectly realized the Dharma, the gates of the teaching, both lesser and great, opening forth in luminous clarity all at once. Nanyue in turn served as disciple to the Chan Master Huiwen, who, in the era of Northern Qi's Emperor Gao, walked alone and unrivaled across the region of the Yellow River and the Huai — his Dharma-gate unknown to his own age, one who trod the earth and bore up the sky with none able to fathom its height or depth. Master Huiwen's own use of mind relied entirely on the Śāstra — a treatise spoken by Nāgārjuna, the thirteenth master named in the Account of the Transmission of the Dharma-Treasury. The Master's own Treatise on the Contemplation of Mind says: "I take refuge in the Master Nāgārjuna." From this it can be confirmed that Nāgārjuna is Tiantai's own high patriarch and teacher. One who doubts this may object: "The Middle Treatise sweeps everything away, while the Calming-and-Contemplation builds a positive teaching up — how can the two be called the same?" Yet in India, commentators on that treatise numbered some seventy schools in all; it would hardly do to follow only Piṅgala's reading of it and set every other master's aside. And the Treatise itself says: "Whatever arises through causes and conditions, this I declare to be empty; it is also called a provisional name; and it is also the meaning of the middle way" — and so on.

Reading note: Zhiyi's own teacher was Nanyue Huisi (南岳慧思); Huisi's teacher was Huiwen (慧文), whose method Zhiyi traces to Nāgārjuna's Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra (大智度論) — a work already a separate translation project on this site at sutras/mahaprajnaparamita-sastra. The doubter's objection and its answer set up everything that follows in this fascicle: the "Three Truths verse" quoted here (因緣所生法，我說即是空，亦為是假名，亦是中道義) — empty, provisional, and middle, three ways of describing one and the same thing that arises through conditions — is the seed from which Zhiyi's entire system grows, including this fascicle's own closing analysis of the four kinds of Four Noble Truths and its treatment of a single thought arising as, at once, empty, provisional, and middle. The lineage passage was not idle piety: it is establishing that this "unprecedented" teaching (前代未聞) is nonetheless not invented from nothing.

The Three Kinds of Calming-and-Contemplation 三種止觀

Chinese Source

天台傳南岳三種止觀：一、漸次，二、不定，三、圓頓，皆是大乘，俱緣實相，同名止觀。「漸」則初淺後深，如彼梯隥；「不定」前後更互，如金剛寶置之日中；「圓頓」初後不二，如通者騰空。為三根性，說三法門、引三譬喻。略說竟，更廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tiantai transmits from Nanyue three kinds of calming-and-contemplation: first, the gradual; second, the indeterminate; third, the perfect-and-sudden. All three are Mahāyāna, all three take the true mark of reality as their object, and all three alike bear the name "calming-and-contemplation." The gradual is shallow at first and deep at the end, like climbing a stairway rung by rung. The indeterminate moves back and forth between earlier and later, like a vajra jewel set out in the sunlight, flashing now this color, now that. The perfect-and-sudden makes no two of beginning and end, like one who has mastered flight simply rising into the sky. These three are spoken for three kinds of capacity, taught as three dharma-gates, and illustrated by three similes. Having stated this in brief, we now explain it at greater length.

「漸」，初亦知實相，實相難解，漸次易行。先修歸戒，翻邪向正，止火血刀，達三善道。次修禪定，止欲、散網，達色、無色定道。次修無漏，止三界獄，達涅槃道。次修慈悲，止於自證，達菩薩道。後修實相，止二邊偏，達常住道。是為初淺後深漸次止觀相。

As for the gradual: from the very first, it too takes the true mark of reality as what is known — but the true mark of reality is hard to understand, while the gradual approach is easy to carry out. One first cultivates the refuges and the precepts, turning from wrong to right, halting the paths of fire, blood, and the sword, and reaching the three wholesome destinies. Next one cultivates dhyāna-concentration, halting desire and the net of distraction, and reaching the path of the form and formless concentrations. Next one cultivates the uncontaminated, halting the prison of the three realms, and reaching the path of nirvāṇa. Next one cultivates compassion, halting at merely one's own realization, and reaching the bodhisattva path. Last one cultivates the true mark of reality itself, halting the two one-sided extremes, and reaching the path of what abides forever. This is the mark of the gradual calming-and-contemplation, shallow at the start and deep at the end.

「不定」者，無別階位，約前漸後頓，更前更後，互淺互深；或事或理，或指「世界」為「第一義」，或指「第一義」為「為人」、「對治」；或息觀為止，或照止為觀，故名不定止觀。疑者云：「教境名同，相頓爾異。」然同而不同，不同而同。漸次中六，善惡各三，無漏總中三，凡十二不同，從多為言，故名不定。此章同大乘、同實相，同名止觀，何故名為辯差？然同而不同，不同而同。漸次中九不同，不定中四不同，總有十三不同，從多為言，故名不同耳。「一切聖人皆以無為法而有差別」即其義也。

As for the indeterminate: it has no separate ladder of stages. Taking the gradual as what comes before and the sudden as what comes after, it may move further forward or further back, now shallower, now deeper — sometimes phenomenal conduct, sometimes principle; sometimes pointing to "the worldly" and calling it "ultimate meaning," sometimes pointing to "ultimate meaning" and calling it "for the individual" or "the antidote"; sometimes taking the stilling of contemplation itself as calming, sometimes taking the illumination within calming itself as contemplation. Hence it is called the indeterminate calming-and-contemplation. One who doubts this may say: "If the teaching and its objects share the same names [across gradual, indeterminate, and sudden], how can their marks turn out so suddenly different?" Yet they are the same yet not the same, not the same yet the same. Within the gradual there are six; wholesome and unwholesome each further divide into three; within the uncontaminated, taken as a whole, there are three more — twelve differences in all — and, speaking from the greater number, it is called indeterminate. [One may further ask:] this chapter is the same Mahāyāna, the same true mark of reality, the same name "calming-and-contemplation" throughout — why, then, speak of distinguishing differences at all? Yet again, they are the same yet not the same, not the same yet the same. Within the gradual there are nine differences, within the indeterminate four, thirteen in all — and, speaking from the greater number, it is called "different." This is the very meaning of "every sage alike takes the unconditioned Dharma as their basis, and yet differs."

Reading note: These three are not three different teachings but three different routes through the same one — the distinction is pedagogical and experiential, not doctrinal. All three "take the true mark of reality as their object" (俱緣實相); what differs is only whether a given practitioner's own path to seeing it runs stairway-fashion (漸, gradual), flickers unpredictably between shallow and deep (不定, indeterminate), or opens the whole thing at once with no "before" and "after" at all (圓頓, perfect-and-sudden). This threefold scheme is one of Zhiyi's best-known contributions to Chinese Buddhist thought, and this fascicle is where he first states it. Watch for it to resurface throughout this fascicle: the entire discussion of the Four Noble Truths later in this same section is explicitly organized as gradual, indeterminate, and perfect-and-sudden versions of "revealing what is right."

The Perfect-and-Sudden, in Full 圓頓止觀

Chinese Source

「圓頓」者，初緣實相，造境即中，無不真實。繫緣法界，一念法界，一色一香無非中道。己界及佛界、眾生界亦然。陰入皆如，無苦可捨；無明、塵勞即是菩提，無集可斷；邊、邪皆中正，無道可修；生死即涅槃，無滅可證。無苦、無集，故無世間；無道、無滅，故無出世間。純一實相，實相外更無別法。法性寂然名「止」，寂而常照名「觀」。雖言初後，無二無別，是名「圓頓止觀」。「漸」與「不定」置而不論，今依經更明圓頓。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the perfect-and-sudden: from the very first it takes the true mark of reality as its object — every object one arrives at is already the middle, nothing about it not already real. Binding the mind to the dharma-realm, a single thought is itself the dharma-realm; a single color, a single scent — none of it is anything other than the middle way. One's own realm, the buddha-realm, and the realm of sentient beings are exactly the same. The aggregates and sense-bases are all thusness itself, with no suffering left to be abandoned; ignorance and the dust of affliction are themselves bodhi, with no accumulation left to be severed; the extreme and the deviant are themselves the correct middle, with no path left to be cultivated; birth-and-death is itself nirvāṇa, with no cessation left to be realized. Since there is no suffering and no accumulation, there is no "mundane world"; since there is no path and no cessation, there is no "world-transcending" either. It is purely and simply the one true mark of reality, with nothing else apart from that true mark at all. The Dharma-nature, still and silent, is called "calming"; that same stillness, ceaselessly illuminating, is called "contemplation." Though beginning and end are spoken of, there is no two of them, no separateness at all — this is called the perfect-and-sudden calming-and-contemplation. Setting the gradual and the indeterminate aside for now without further discussion, we turn to the sūtras themselves to clarify the perfect-and-sudden further.

如了達甚深妙德賢首曰：「菩薩於生死，最初發心時，一向求菩提，堅固不可動。彼一念功德，深廣無崖際，如來分別說，窮劫不能盡。」此菩薩聞圓法、起圓信、立圓行、住圓位、以圓功德而自莊嚴、以圓力用建立眾生。

As the bodhisattva Sudṛḍhādhyāśaya, of thoroughly penetrated and profound wondrous virtue, has said: "When a bodhisattva, amid birth-and-death, first sets the mind on enlightenment, resolved single-mindedly to seek bodhi, that resolve is firm and cannot be shaken. The merit of that one single thought is deep and vast, without any shore or boundary — were the Tathāgata to explain it in full detail, he could not exhaust it though the kalpas ran out." Such a bodhisattva hears the perfect Dharma, gives rise to perfect faith, establishes perfect practice, abides in the perfect stage, adorns themself with perfect merit, and, by perfect power and function, establishes sentient beings [in the same].

云何「聞圓法」？聞「生死即法身」、「煩惱即般若」、「結業即解脫」。雖有三名而無三體，雖是一體而立三名，是三即一相，其實無有異。法身究竟，般若解脫亦究竟。般若清淨，餘亦清淨。解脫自在，餘亦自在。聞一切法亦如是，皆具佛法，無所減少，是名「聞圓法」。

What is meant by "hearing the perfect Dharma"? Hearing that birth-and-death is itself the Dharma-body; that affliction is itself prajñā; that binding karma is itself liberation. Though these are three names, they have no three separate essences; though there is one essence, three names are set up for it — these three are one and the same mark, with, in truth, no difference between them at all. If the Dharma-body is ultimate, then prajñā and liberation are equally ultimate. If prajñā is pure, the others are equally pure. If liberation is free and unobstructed, the others are equally free and unobstructed. Hearing of any and every dharma is exactly the same: each is complete with the whole of the Buddhadharma, with nothing whatsoever lacking. This is called "hearing the perfect Dharma."

云何「圓信」？信一切法即空即假即中，無一二三而一二三，無一二三是遮一二三，而一二三是照一二三，無遮無照皆究竟、清淨、自在。聞深不怖，聞廣不疑，聞非深非廣意而有勇，是名「圓信」。

What is "perfect faith"? Believing that every dharma is at once empty, at once provisional, and at once the middle; that there is no "one, two, three" of them, and yet there is "one, two, three"; that the absence of "one, two, three" is a negating of "one, two, three," while the presence of "one, two, three" is an illuminating of "one, two, three" — and that this negating and this illuminating are both alike ultimate, pure, and free. Hearing of the profound, one is not terrified; hearing of the vast, one does not doubt; hearing that it is neither profound nor vast in any fixed sense, the mind still finds courage in it. This is called "perfect faith."

云何「圓行」？一向專求無上菩提，即邊而中，不餘趣向，三諦圓修。不為無邊所寂、有邊所動，不動不寂直入中道，是名「圓行」。云何「入圓位」？入初住時，一住一切住，一切究竟，一切清淨，一切自在，是名「圓位」。

What is "perfect practice"? Single-mindedly and exclusively seeking unsurpassed bodhi, taking the very extreme itself as the middle, turning toward nothing else, cultivating all three truths at once and in full. Neither stilled by the extreme of non-being nor moved by the extreme of being, neither stilled nor moved, one enters the middle way directly — this is called "perfect practice." What is "entering the perfect stage"? At the moment of entering the first abiding-stage, one abiding is every abiding; everything is at once ultimate, everything pure, everything free — this is called the "perfect stage."

云何「圓自在莊嚴」？彼經廣說自在相，或於此根入正受，或於彼根起出說，或於一根雙入出，或於一根不入出，餘一一根亦如是。或於此塵入正受，或於彼塵起出說，或於一塵雙入出，或於一塵不入出，餘一一塵亦如是。或於此方入正受，或於彼方起出說，或於一方雙入出，或於一方不入出。或於一物入正受，或於一物起出說，或於一物雙入出，或於一物不入出。若委說者，秖於一根一塵即入即出、即雙入出、即不入出，於正報中一一自在，於依報中亦如是，是名「圓自在莊嚴」。譬如日光周四天下，一方中，一方旦，一方夕，一方夜半，輪迴不同，秖是一日而四處見異，菩薩自在亦如是。

What is "perfect, free adornment"? That sūtra explains the mark of this freedom at length: one may enter absorption through this sense-faculty and rise from it, expounding the Dharma, through that one; one may, through a single faculty, both enter and rise at once; or, through a single faculty, neither enter nor rise at all — and every other faculty, each in turn, is exactly the same. One may enter absorption through this sense-object and rise through that one; enter and rise at once through a single object; or neither enter nor rise through a single object — and every other object, each in turn, is exactly the same. One may enter absorption in this direction and rise, expounding the Dharma, in that direction; enter and rise at once in a single direction; or neither enter nor rise in a single direction. One may enter absorption through one single thing, rise through that same one thing, enter and rise at once through it, or neither enter nor rise through it. To state it in full: within a single sense-faculty, a single sense-object, there is entering, there is rising, there is entering-and-rising-at-once, there is neither-entering-nor-rising — each and every one free, within one's own direct retribution, and exactly the same within one's environmental retribution as well. This is called "perfect, free adornment." It is like sunlight encompassing the four continents at once: in one quarter it is dawn, in another dusk, in another midnight — a single wheeling motion appearing differently in four places, though it is only ever the one sun. The bodhisattva's own freedom is exactly the same.

云何「圓建立眾生」？或放一光，能令眾生得即空即假即中益，得入出、雙入出、不入出益，歷行住坐臥語默作作亦如是。有緣者見，如目覩光；無緣不覺，盲瞽常闇。故舉龍王為譬，竪遍六天、橫亘四域，興種種雲、震種種雷、耀種種電、降種種雨，龍於本宮不動不搖，而於一切施設不同。菩薩亦如是，內自通達即空即假即中，不動法性而令獲種種益、得種種用，是名「圓力用建立眾生」。初心尚爾，況中、後心？如來殷勤稱歎此法，聞者歡喜。常啼東請，善財南求，藥王燒手，普明刎頭，一日三捨恒河沙身，尚不能報一句之力，況兩肩荷負百千萬劫，寧報佛法之恩？一經一說如此，餘經亦然。

What is "perfect power and function establishing sentient beings"? A single beam of light, once released, can grant sentient beings the benefit of the empty, the provisional, and the middle all at once, the benefit of entering, of rising, of entering-and-rising-together, of neither entering nor rising — and it is exactly the same across walking, standing, sitting, lying down, speaking, keeping silent, every action whatsoever. Those with the karmic connection to see it are like eyes beholding light itself; those without it perceive nothing, as blind eyes remain forever in the dark. Hence the simile of the dragon king is raised: reaching vertically through the six heavens and spanning horizontally the four continents, it raises clouds of every kind, sends thunder of every kind, flashes lightning of every kind, and sends down rain of every kind — and yet the dragon, in its own palace, never moves or stirs, even as its manifestations differ endlessly. The bodhisattva is exactly the same: inwardly penetrating the empty, the provisional, and the middle all at once, without ever moving the Dharma-nature itself, they still cause beings to obtain every kind of benefit and every kind of function. This is called "perfect power and function establishing sentient beings." If this is already so at the very first thought of enlightenment, how much more at the middle and final stages? This is why the Tathāgata praises this Dharma so earnestly, and why those who hear it rejoice. Sadāprarudita begged in the east; Sudhana sought throughout the south; Bhaiṣajyarāja burned his own arm; Samantaprabhāsa cut off his own head — giving up bodies as numerous as the sands of the Ganges three times a day would still not repay the power of even a single phrase of this teaching, much less shouldering it on both shoulders through a hundred thousand myriad kalpas repay the debt owed to the Buddhadharma itself. This is so of this one sūtra's own account, and every other sūtra says exactly the same.

Reading note: This is the fascicle's first sustained account of what "perfect-and-sudden" actually amounts to in practice, not just in slogan — six successive terms (聞圓法, 圓信, 圓行, 圓位, 圓自在莊嚴, 圓力用建立眾生: hearing, believing, practicing, attaining, being freely adorned by, and finally acting through this teaching) that move from how the teaching is first understood to how a bodhisattva who has fully realized it acts in the world without effort. The dragon-king simile at the end is worth sitting with: the dragon "never moves or stirs" in its own palace even as it produces every kind of weather across the sky — an image for how realizing emptiness-provisionality-and-the-middle "all at once" does not mean withdrawing from activity, but the opposite: acting without the activity ever disturbing what has been realized. The roll call of bodhisattva self-sacrifice that closes this passage (Sadāprarudita, Sudhana, and the rest, all figures from the Perfection of Wisdom and Lotus literatures) is a rhetorical flourish common to this genre — establishing the stakes before the text pauses, in the next section, to actually prove all of this from scripture.

Scriptural Proof for the Three Teachings 引證

Chinese Source

疑者云：「餘三昧願聞誠證。」然經論浩博，不可委引，略舉一兩。《淨名》云：「始坐佛樹力降魔，得甘露滅覺道成，三轉法輪於大千，其輪本來常清淨，天人得道此為證，三寶於是現世間。」此即漸教之始也。又云：「佛以一音演說法，眾生隨類各得解，或有恐怖或歡喜，或生厭離或斷疑，斯則神力不共法。」此證不定教也。又云：「說法不有亦不無，以因緣故諸法生，無我無造無受者，善惡之業不敗亡。」此證頓教也。

Ru-Yi Translation

One who doubts this may say: "We would wish to hear genuine scriptural proof for the other two as well." The sūtras and treatises are vast beyond measure and cannot be cited in full — only one or two examples are given for each. The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "First seated beneath the bodhi tree, by his power he subdued Māra; attaining the deathless cessation, the path of enlightenment was accomplished; three times he turned the Dharma-wheel throughout the great trichiliocosm — and that wheel, from the very first, was always pure. That gods and men attained the Way was itself the proof of this, and the Three Jewels thereupon appeared in the world." This is the very beginning of the gradual teaching. It also says: "The Buddha, with a single voice, expounds the Dharma, yet sentient beings, each according to their own kind, understand it differently — some feel fear, some rejoice, some give rise to weariness, some have their doubts severed. This is indeed a power belonging to him alone." This proves the indeterminate teaching. It also says: "The Dharma he expounds is neither being nor non-being; through causes and conditions all dharmas arise; there is no self, no doer, and no receiver — and yet wholesome and unwholesome karma is never lost." This proves the sudden teaching.

《大品》云：「次第行、次第學、次第道。」此證漸也。又云：「以眾色裹摩尼珠，置之水中，隨物變色。」此證不定也。又云：「從初發心即坐道場，轉法輪度眾生。」此證頓也。《法華》云：「如是之人應以此法漸入佛慧。」此證漸也。又云：「若不信此法，於餘深法中示教利喜。」此證不定也。又云：「正直捨方便，但說無上道。」此證頓也。

The Larger Sūtra says: "Sequential practice, sequential study, sequential path." This proves the gradual. It also says: "Wrap a maṇi-jewel in cloths of every color and place it in water — it takes on the color of whatever surrounds it." This proves the indeterminate. It also says: "From the very first arising of the resolve, one already sits at the seat of enlightenment, turning the Dharma-wheel and bringing sentient beings across." This proves the sudden. The Lotus Sūtra says: "Such a person should, by means of this Dharma, gradually enter the Buddha's wisdom." This proves the gradual. It also says: "If they do not yet believe this Dharma, then within some other profound dharma, they are shown, taught, benefited, and delighted." This proves the indeterminate. It also says: "Directly, setting every expedient means aside, only the unsurpassed path is taught." This proves the sudden.

《大經》云：「從牛出乳，……乃至醍醐。」此證漸也。又云：「置毒乳中，乳即殺人，乃至置毒醍醐，醍醐殺人。」此證不定也。又云：「雪山有草名曰忍辱，牛若食者即得醍醐。」此證頓也。《無量義》云：「佛轉法輪，微渧先墮，淹諸欲塵，開涅槃門，扇解脫風，除世熱惱，致法清涼。次降十二因緣雨，灑無明地，掩邪見光。後澍無上大乘，普令一切發菩提心。」此證漸也。

The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "From the cow comes milk ... and so on, up to ghee." This proves the gradual. It also says: "Place poison in milk, and the milk alone can kill a person; place poison even in ghee, and the ghee kills a person exactly the same." This proves the indeterminate. It also says: "On the Snow Mountains there grows a grass called forbearance; should a cow eat it, ghee is obtained directly." This proves the sudden. The Sūtra of Immeasurable Meanings says: "When the Buddha turns the Dharma-wheel, fine droplets fall first, settling the dust of desire, opening the gate to nirvāṇa, fanning the wind of liberation, removing the world's own feverish affliction, and bringing the Dharma's coolness. Next he sends down the rain of the twelve links of dependent origination, sprinkling the ground of ignorance and dimming the light of wrong views. Last he pours down the unsurpassed Mahāyāna, causing every being everywhere to give rise to the bodhi-mind." This proves the gradual.

《華嚴》曰：「娑伽羅龍車軸雨海，餘地不堪。」為上根性說圓滿修多羅，二乘如聾如瘂。《淨名》曰：「入瞻蔔林不嗅餘香，入此室者但聞諸佛功德之香。」《首楞嚴》曰：「擣萬種香為丸，若燒一塵具足眾氣。」《大品》曰：「以一切種智知一切法，當學般若波羅蜜。」《法華》曰：「合掌以敬心，欲聞具足道。」《大經》曰：「譬如有人在大海浴，當知是人已用諸河之水。」《華嚴》曰：「譬如日出，先照高山，次照幽谷，次照平地。」平地，不定也；幽谷，漸也；高山，頓也。

The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "The dragon-king Sāgara pours down rain thick as cart-axles over the ocean alone — no other ground could bear it." This is spoken for those of the highest capacity, expounding the perfect and complete sūtra directly; before it, the two lesser vehicles are as if deaf, as if mute. The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "One who enters a grove of campaka trees smells no other fragrance; one who enters this room smells only the fragrance of the buddhas' own merit." The Śūraṅgama Sūtra says: "Pound ten thousand kinds of incense into a single pellet — burn even a speck of it, and every one of those scents is present at once." The Larger Sūtra says: "To know all dharmas by means of the wisdom that knows all things in every one of their modes, one should study the perfection of wisdom." The Lotus Sūtra says: "With palms joined and a reverent mind, they wish to hear the complete and perfect path." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "It is like a person bathing in the great ocean — know that such a person has already made use of the water of every river." The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "It is like the sun rising: it shines first on the high mountains, next on the shadowed valleys, and next on the level ground." The level ground is the indeterminate; the shadowed valleys are the gradual; the high mountains are the sudden.

上來皆是金口誠言，三世如來所尊重法。過去過去，久遠久遠，邈無萠始；現在現在，無邊無際；未來未來，展轉不窮。若已、今、當不可思議，當知止觀諸佛之師。以法常故，諸佛亦常，樂、我、淨等亦復如是。如是引證，寧不信乎？

Everything cited above is the Buddha's own true golden word, the Dharma honored by every buddha of the three times. Past beyond past, distant beyond distant, stretching back with no discernible beginning at all; present beyond present, boundless and without limit; future beyond future, unfolding on without end. If what has already been, what now is, and what is yet to come are all alike beyond conception, then know that calming-and-contemplation is the very teacher of every buddha. Because the Dharma itself is eternal, the buddhas too are eternal — and bliss, self, and purity are exactly the same. With proof cited like this, how could one still fail to believe?

Reading note: This whole passage is a scholastic set piece — proof-texting the same threefold scheme, gradual/indeterminate/sudden, across nine different sūtras in turn (Vimalakīrti, the Larger Sūtra, the Lotus, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, the Sūtra of Immeasurable Meanings, the Avataṃsaka, the Śūraṅgama, and others), each supplying its own memorable image: milk ripening into ghee for the gradual, a jewel taking on whatever color surrounds it for the indeterminate, a cow eating a special mountain grass and getting ghee directly for the sudden. The final image, of sunrise touching mountain peaks before valleys before the plain, is the one Zhiyi's later interpreters would take furthest, eventually formalizing it into the doctrine of the Five Periods (五時) that orders the Buddha's entire teaching career — though that later development is not what this passage itself is arguing; here the point is only that all three teaching-styles are equally scriptural, equally the Buddha's own words, not that they occurred in a strict chronological sequence.

The Three Written Versions, and Why Teach in Words at All 三種文

Chinese Source

既信其法，須知三文：《次第禪門》合三十卷，今之十軸，是大莊嚴寺法慎私記；不定文者，如《六妙門》，以不定意歷十二禪、九想、八背、觀練熏修、因緣、六度，無礙旋轉縱橫自在，此是陳尚書令毛喜請智者出此文也；圓頓文者，如灌頂荊洲玉泉寺所記十卷是也。雖有三文，無得執文而自疣害。論云：「若見若不見般若，皆縛皆脫。」文亦例然。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having come to believe this teaching, one should further know its three written versions. The text for the gradual is the Sequential Dharma-Gates of Dhyāna, thirty fascicles in all, surviving today in ten scrolls — the private record made by Fashen of Great Adornment Monastery. The text for the indeterminate is the Six Wondrous Gates, which, with an indeterminate intent, passes through the twelve dhyānas, the nine contemplations, the eight liberations, observing-refining-perfuming-and-mastering, causation, and the six pāramitās, turning without obstruction and moving freely in every direction — this is the text the Chen dynasty's Left Assistant Director Mao Xi requested the Master to bring forth. The text for the perfect-and-sudden is the very ten fascicles recorded by Guanding at Yuquan Monastery in Jingzhou — this text itself. Though there are these three written versions, none should cling to the words themselves and so do injury to themselves, as by a wart or a growth. The Treatise says: "Whether one sees prajñā or does not see it, one is equally bound, equally freed, by that very seeing or not-seeing." The written word is exactly the same.

Reading note: This one paragraph maps Zhiyi's three great meditation treatises onto the threefold scheme just proven from scripture — and, remarkably for a reader of this site, all three are now translation projects here. The gradual is the Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen (次第禪門), already complete in Ru-Yi's translation. The indeterminate is the Six Wondrous Gates (六妙門), already available in Bhikshu Dharmamitra's published English translation — see the source-and-copyright note in the translation notes regarding why this site translates independently rather than consulting it. The perfect-and-sudden is this very text, the Mohe Zhiguan, now being translated fascicle by fascicle on this page. Zhiyi's own closing warning is worth taking to heart on a translation project like this one: the written word is a way in, not the thing itself — "whether one sees prajñā or does not see it, one is equally bound, equally freed," meaning that clinging to any of the three texts as if it were the destination, rather than a gate toward it, defeats the purpose of all three alike.

An Objection: Isn't Reality Beyond Words? 言、默

Chinese Source

疑者云：「『諸法寂滅相，不可以言宣。』《大經》云：『生生不可說，乃至不生不生不可說。』若通若別，言語道斷，無能說無所說。身子云：『吾聞解脫之中無有言說，故吾於此不知所云。』《淨名》云：『其所說者無說無示，其聽法者無聞無得。』斯人不能說，斯法不可說，而言示人？」然但引一邊，不見其二。《大經》云：「有因緣故，亦可得說。」《法華》云：「無數方便，種種因緣，為眾生說」又云：「以方便力故，為五比丘說」。若通若別，皆可得說。

Ru-Yi Translation

One who doubts this may say: "'All dharmas bear the mark of quiescent extinction, and cannot be conveyed in words.' The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: 'Arising-of-arising cannot be spoken of, down to not-arising-of-not-arising, which also cannot be spoken of.' Whether taken generally or in each particular, the path of language is cut off — there is no one able to speak, and nothing spoken of. Śāriputra himself said: 'I have heard that within liberation there is no speech at all — hence, as to this, I do not know what to say.' The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: 'What is spoken has no speaking and no showing; what is heard has no hearing and no attaining.' If this person cannot speak, and this Dharma cannot be spoken, how can it still be said to instruct anyone at all?" Yet this cites only one side, and fails to see the other. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Because there are causes and conditions, it can indeed be spoken." The Lotus Sūtra says: "Through countless expedient means and manifold causes and conditions, it is taught for the sake of sentient beings" — and also: "By the power of skillful means, it was taught for the five bhikṣus." Whether generally or in particular, it can indeed be spoken.

《大經》云：「有眼者為盲人說乳。」此指真諦可說。《天王般若》云：「總持無文字，文字顯總持。」此指俗諦可說。又如來常依二諦說法。《淨名》云：「文字性離，即是解脫。」即說是無說。《大經》云：「若知如來常不說法，是即多聞。」此指不說而是說也。《思益》云：「佛及弟子常行二事，若說若默。」《法華》云：「去來坐立，常宣妙法，如注大雨。」又云：「若欲求佛道，常隨多聞人；善知識者是大因緣，所謂化導令得見佛。」《大經》云：「空中雲雷生象牙上華。」何時一向無說？

The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "One with eyes describes milk for the blind." This points to how the ultimate truth can indeed be spoken. The Prajñāpāramitā Spoken for the Heavenly King says: "The dhāraṇī itself has no written words, yet written words reveal the dhāraṇī." This points to how the conventional truth can indeed be spoken. And indeed, the Tathāgata always teaches the Dharma by way of the two truths together. The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "The nature of written words is itself free — this is precisely liberation." Speaking, here, is itself no speaking at all. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "If one knows that the Tathāgata never actually speaks the Dharma, this itself is true learning." This points to how not-speaking is itself speaking. The Sūtra of Assured Resolve says: "The Buddha and his disciples constantly carry out two things: speaking, and keeping silent." The Lotus Sūtra says: "Going, coming, sitting, standing, he constantly proclaims the wondrous Dharma, like a great rain endlessly falling." It also says: "If one wishes to seek the buddha-path, always follow those of great learning; a good spiritual friend is a great causal condition — meaning, one who guides and transforms us so that we come to see the Buddha." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Clouds thunder in the empty sky, and flowers bloom on an elephant's ivory tusk." When, then, is there ever a single, fixed absence of speech at all?

若競說默，不解教意，去理逾遠。離說無理，離理無說，即說無說，無說即說，無二無別，即事而真。大悲憐愍一切無聞，如月隱重山，舉扇類之；風息太虛，動樹訓之。今人意鈍，玄覽則難；眼依色入，假文則易。若封文為害，須知文非文，達一切文非文非不文，能於一文得一切解。為此義故，以三種文作達一門也。已略說緣起竟。

If one merely competes over speaking versus silence, failing to grasp the teaching's actual intent, one only strays further from the principle. Apart from speaking there is no principle; apart from the principle there is no speaking. Speaking is itself no-speaking; no-speaking is itself speaking — there is no two of them, no separateness at all; the very phenomenon of speech is itself already truth. Out of great compassion, pitying all who have never heard it, the Buddha teaches the way one might raise a fan to represent a moon hidden behind layered mountains, or shake a tree to instruct someone about a wind stilled in the vast, empty sky. People today are dull of mind, and direct, wordless contemplation is difficult for them; the eye takes things in by way of form and color, and so the written word offers an easier way in. But if one takes the written word itself as an obstacle, one must know that the written word is not merely the written word — one must penetrate through to how every piece of writing is neither the written word nor not the written word, so that through a single piece of writing, one may gain understanding of everything at once. For this very reason, the three written versions of this teaching are offered as three ways into one and the same gate. This concludes the brief account of this teaching's origin.

Reading note: This closes the whole opening section on the teaching's origin (緣起) — the lineage, the three kinds of calming-and-contemplation, their scriptural proof, and the three written texts — with a defense of teaching in words at all, against the standard Mahāyāna objection that ultimate reality is strictly ineffable. Zhiyi's answer is not to deny the objection but to relativize it: every "silence" quotation is answered with a "speech" quotation, until speaking and silence collapse into each other ("speaking is itself no-speaking; no-speaking is itself speaking"). The two closing similes — raising a fan to indicate a moon hidden behind the mountains, shaking a tree to indicate an unseen wind — describe exactly what this translation project is trying to do: the English words on this page are not identical with what Zhiyi realized, any more than a fan is the moon, but for readers who cannot yet see directly, they are how a teacher already inside a tradition still manages to point.

Opening the Treatise into Ten Sections 開章為十

Chinese Source

今當開章為十：一、大意，二、釋名，三、體相，四、攝法，五、偏圓，六、方便，七、正觀，八、果報，九、起教，十、旨歸。十是數方，不多不少。始則標期在茶，終則歸宗至極，善始令終總在十章中矣。生起者，專「次第」十章也。至理寂滅，無生無生者、無起無起者，有因緣故，十章通是生起。別論前章為生，次章為起。緣由、趣次亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

The treatise is now opened into ten sections: first, the great intent; second, explaining the name; third, the essence and its marks; fourth, the scope of the teachings it gathers in; fifth, its relation to partial and complete teachings; sixth, the preliminary expedients; seventh, the actual practice; eighth, its fruits and rewards; ninth, the teachings that arise from it; tenth, where it ultimately leads. Ten is a squared, settled number — neither too many nor too few. Marking out its own destination from the very start, and returning at the end to the utmost of its own source, a good beginning and a fitting end are both held together within these ten sections. As for their generative order: this concerns specifically how the ten sections are sequenced. At the level of ultimate principle, all is quiescent extinction — there is no arising and no one who arises, no generating and no one who generates. Yet because there are causes and conditions, the ten sections as a whole do have a generative order: taken individually, the earlier section is the "generating," and the section that follows is its "arising." Their underlying cause and their sequence of aim are exactly the same.

所謂無量劫來癡惑所覆，不知無明即是明，今開覺之，故言「大意」。既知無明即明，不復流動，故名為「止」；朗然大淨，呼之為「觀」。既聞名得體，體即攝法，攝於偏圓，以偏圓解，起於方便。方便既立，正觀即成。成正觀已，獲妙果報。從自得法，起教教他，自他俱安，同歸常寂。秖為不達無生無起，是故生起。既了無生無起，心行寂滅，言語道斷，寂然清淨。

That is to say: covered over for measureless kalpas by delusion and confusion, one does not know that ignorance is itself already illumination — and this is now opened up and awakened to. Hence it is called "the great intent." Once one knows that ignorance is itself illumination, the mind no longer flows and drifts — hence this is named "calming." Luminous and utterly pure, this is called "contemplation." Having heard the name, one grasps the essence; the essence gathers in every teaching, gathered into the partial and the complete; through understanding the partial and the complete, the preliminary expedients arise. Once the preliminary expedients are established, the actual practice is accomplished. Once the actual practice is accomplished, its wondrous fruits and rewards are obtained. From what is attained for oneself, teachings arise to teach others; self and other are both put at ease, alike returning to what is ever-quiescent. All of this generative order exists only because one has not yet penetrated to no-arising and no-generating at all. Once no-arising and no-generating are fully understood, the mind's own activity is itself quiescent extinction, the path of language cut off, still and utterly pure.

Classifying the Ten Sections 分別

Chinese Source

分別者，十章功德如囊中有寶，不探示人，人無見者。今十章，幾真、幾俗、幾非真非俗？幾聖說、聖默、非說非默？幾定、幾慧、幾非定慧？幾目、足、幾非目足？幾因、果、非因果？幾自、他、非自他？幾共、不共、非共非不共？幾通、別、非通別？幾廣、略、非廣略？幾橫、竪、非橫竪？如是等種種，應自在作問。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for classifying them: the merit within these ten sections is like a treasure hidden inside a sack — unless it is drawn out and shown, no one will ever see it. Of these ten sections: how many belong to the ultimate truth, how many to the conventional, how many to neither? How many are the sage's speech, how many the sage's silence, how many neither? How many are concentration, how many wisdom, how many neither? How many are the eye, how many the foot, how many neither? How many are cause, how many fruit, how many neither? How many concern oneself, how many others, how many neither? How many are shared, how many unshared, how many neither? How many are general, how many particular, how many neither? How many are extensive, how many concise, how many neither? How many run vertically, how many horizontally, how many neither? Questions of every such kind may freely be raised.

初八章即俗而真，果報一章即真而俗，旨歸章非真非俗。正觀聖默，餘八章聖說，旨歸非說非默。正觀一分是定，餘八章及一分是慧，旨歸非定非慧。大意至正觀是因，果報是果，旨歸非因非果。前八章自行，起教化他，旨歸非自非他。大意至起教是目，方便至果報是足，旨歸非目非足。大意至正觀共，果報、起教不共，旨歸非共非不共。大意一通，八章別，旨歸非通非別。大意略，八章廣，旨歸非廣非略。體相竪，餘八橫，旨歸非橫非竪。

The first eight sections are conventional even as they are already ultimate; the section on fruits and rewards is ultimate even as it is already conventional; the section on where it all leads is neither ultimate nor conventional. The actual practice is the sage's silence; the remaining eight sections are the sage's speech; where it all leads is neither speech nor silence. The actual practice is, in part, concentration; the remaining eight sections, together with part of the actual practice, are wisdom; where it all leads is neither concentration nor wisdom. From the great intent through the actual practice is cause; fruits and rewards is fruit; where it all leads is neither cause nor fruit. The first eight sections are practice for oneself; the teachings that arise transform others; where it all leads is neither for oneself nor for others. From the great intent through the teachings that arise is the eye [of understanding]; from the preliminary expedients through fruits and rewards is the foot [of practice]; where it all leads is neither eye nor foot. From the great intent through the actual practice is shared with the other vehicles; fruits and rewards and the teachings that arise are unshared; where it all leads is neither shared nor unshared. The great intent alone is general; the other eight sections are particular; where it all leads is neither general nor particular. The great intent is concise; the other eight sections are extensive; where it all leads is neither extensive nor concise. The essence and its marks run vertically [i.e., toward depth]; the remaining eight run horizontally [i.e., toward breadth]; where it all leads is neither horizontal nor vertical.

Reading note: This ten-by-ten grid is a set piece of scholastic classification, sorting the treatise's own ten sections along ten different axes at once (truth-level, speech/silence, concentration/wisdom, and so on) — and in every single axis, the tenth section, "where it all leads" (旨歸), is the one that resolves into neither pole. That is the entire point of putting a section like this so early: it previews, in miniature, the treatise's most basic habit of mind, run again and again throughout this fascicle — take a pair of opposites, show that each of the first nine sections falls to one side or straddles both, and then show that the whole scheme itself finally collapses into a "neither" that is not a third alternative but the ground the first two were both standing on all along. The paired terms "eye" and "foot" (目 / 足) are a common classical-Chinese image for understanding and practice taken together — knowing the way and walking it — used again later in this fascicle's own treatment of the ten sections.

Clarifying Questions 料簡

Chinese Source

料簡者，問：略指、大意，同異云何？答：通則名異意同，別則略指三門，大意在一頓。問：約顯教論顯觀，亦應約祕教論密觀？答：既分顯祕，今但明顯，不說祕。問：分門可爾，任論得不？答：或得或不得。教是上聖被下之言，聖能顯祕兩說，凡人宣述秖可傳顯不能傳祕。聽者因何作觀？或得者，六根淨位，能以一妙音遍滿三千界，隨意悉能至，則能傳祕教。若修觀者，發所修顯法，不發不修者，發宿習人得論密觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the clarifying questions: Question: How do the brief pointer given just above and the great intent, the treatise's own first section, differ, and how are they the same? Answer: In general, their names differ but their sense is the same; in particular, the brief pointer concerns the three gates of gradual, indeterminate, and sudden, while the great intent rests wholly in the one, sudden approach. Question: If the manifest teaching is used to discuss manifest contemplation, should not the esoteric teaching likewise be used to discuss esoteric contemplation? Answer: Since manifest and esoteric are indeed distinguished, this treatise clarifies only the manifest, and does not discuss the esoteric. Question: Dividing the gates this way is well enough — but on its own terms, can the esoteric be spoken of at all, or not? Answer: In some cases it can, in some it cannot. The teaching is the sage's own word, extended from above to those below; the sage alone is capable of expounding both the manifest and the esoteric together, while an ordinary person, in expounding it, can transmit only the manifest and cannot transmit the esoteric. By what means, then, does the listener come to practice contemplation at all? Where it can be done: one who has reached the stage of the six purified faculties can, through a single wondrous sound, fill the whole trichiliocosm at once, reaching wherever intended — such a one can indeed transmit the esoteric teaching. As for one who cultivates contemplation: manifest dharmas already cultivated are brought forth; those not yet cultivated are not; but a person who brings forth what was habituated in past lives can, through this treatise, attain the esoteric contemplation as well.

問：初淺後深是漸觀，初深後淺是何觀相？答：是不定觀。問：初後俱淺是何觀相？答：小乘意，非三止觀相也。問：小乘亦是佛說，何意言非？若言非者，不應言漸。答：既分大小，小非所論。今言漸者，從微至著之漸耳。小乘初後俱不知實相，故非今漸也。問：示三文者，文是色，色是門、為非門？若是門者，色是實相，更何所通？若非門者，云何而言「一色一香皆是中道」？答：文、門並是實相，眾生多顛倒，少不顛倒，以文示之，即於文達文、非文、非文非不文。文是其門，於門得實相故。文是其門，門具一切法，即門、即非門、即非門非不門。

Question: Shallow at first and deep at the end is the mark of the gradual contemplation — what, then, is the mark of a contemplation deep at first and shallow at the end? Answer: This is the mark of the indeterminate contemplation. Question: What is the mark of a contemplation shallow both at the start and at the end? Answer: This is the intent of the Hīnayāna, not the mark of any of the three kinds of calming-and-contemplation at all. Question: But the Hīnayāna, too, is the Buddha's own word — on what grounds is it excluded? And if it is excluded here, should it not also be excluded from ever being called "gradual"? Answer: Since Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna are indeed distinguished, the Hīnayāna is simply not what this treatise is discussing. What is called "gradual" here means only the gradual progress from the subtle to the manifest within the Mahāyāna itself. In the Hīnayāna, neither the beginning nor the end ever knows the true mark of reality — hence it is not the "gradual" spoken of here. Question: As for the three written versions just shown — writing is a form, and is form a gate, or not a gate? If it is a gate, then form is already the true mark of reality itself, and what further is left for it to lead through to? If it is not a gate, how can it then be said that "a single color, a single scent, are none other than the middle way"? Answer: Both writing and gate are alike the true mark of reality. Sentient beings are mostly inverted in their view, and only rarely not inverted; writing is shown to them so that, by means of the writing itself, they may penetrate through to the writing, to what is not writing, and to what is neither writing nor not-writing. Writing is itself the gate, because it is through the gate that the true mark of reality is attained. Writing is the gate; the gate is complete with every dharma — it is the gate, it is not the gate, and it is neither the gate nor not the gate.

Reading note: The closing exchange here — is writing a "gate" (門) into reality, or a barrier against it? — answers the same way the previous section on the three written versions did: writing is a gate precisely because "through the gate the true mark of reality is attained," yet writing is never identical with what it leads to, hence "it is the gate, it is not the gate, and it is neither the gate nor not the gate." This fourfold move (is / is not / neither / — implicitly, both) is the tetralemma pattern that recurs constantly across Zhiyi's works, including the extended fourfold analysis of "contaminated dharma" in fascicle 1 of the Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen. Having now finished the whole outer frame — the ten-section outline, its classification, and these clarifying questions — the text turns at last to actually expounding the ten sections themselves, beginning with the first, the great intent.

I. The Great Intent, in Five Brief Points 大意

Chinese Source

解釋者，釋十章也。初釋大意，囊括始終，冠戴初後，意緩難見。今撮為五，謂發大心、修大行、感大果、裂大網、歸大處。云何發大心？眾生昏倒不自覺知，勸令醒悟，上求下化。云何行大行？雖復發心，望路不動，永無達期，勸牢強精進，行四種三昧。云何感大果？雖不求梵天，梵天自應，稱揚妙報，慰悅其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the exposition: this expounds the ten sections themselves. The first, expounding the great intent, gathers in the whole treatise from start to finish, crowning both its beginning and its end at once — its sense is so expansive that it is hard to take in all at once. Here, then, it is drawn together into five points: giving rise to the great mind, cultivating great practice, evoking a great fruit, tearing apart the great net, and returning to the great abode. What is "giving rise to the great mind"? Sentient beings are confused and inverted, unaware of their own condition; here they are urged to awaken, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below. What is "cultivating great practice"? Though the mind has already been given rise to, one who merely looks toward the road without moving will never in the end arrive; hence one is urged to firm, vigorous diligence, practicing the four kinds of samādhi. What is "evoking a great fruit"? Though one does not seek rebirth in the Brahmā heavens for its own sake, the Brahmā heavens respond of themselves — this wondrous reward is proclaimed here to console and gladden the practitioner's own heart.

云何裂大網？種種經論開人眼目，而執此疑彼、是一非諸，聞雪謂冷乃至聞鶴謂動。今融通經論，解結出籠。云何歸大處？法無始終，法無通塞。若知法界，法界無始終、無通塞，豁然大朗，無礙自在。生起五略，顯於十廣云云。就發心更為三：初、方言，次、簡非，後、顯是。

What is "tearing apart the great net"? The manifold sūtras and treatises open people's eyes, and yet they cling to this one and doubt that one, affirming a single position while rejecting every other — like one who, on merely hearing of snow, insists it must be cold, or, on merely hearing of a crane, insists it must be moving. Here the sūtras and treatises are brought into harmony with one another, every knot untangled, freeing the mind from its own cage. What is "returning to the great abode"? The Dharma has no beginning and no end; the Dharma has no passage and no obstruction. If one knows the dharma-realm, the dharma-realm itself has no beginning or end, no passage or obstruction — one opens out into vast clarity, unobstructed and free. These five brief points generate what will be shown at greater length across the ten sections that follow, and so on. As for giving rise to the mind specifically: this is further divided into three — first, terminology; second, rejecting what is wrong; third, revealing what is right.

Reading note: The five brief points (五略) are Zhiyi's own compressed preview of topic 1 before he expounds it at length, and this fascicle in fact only ever reaches the first of the five in full detail: giving rise to the great mind (發大心). The remaining four — practice, fruit, the net of doubt, and the final abode — are named here only in outline; watch for this fascicle's closing status note on how much of "the great intent" fascicle 1 actually completes. The image behind "tearing apart the great net" is worth pausing on: not every sūtra saying something different means the sūtras disagree, any more than "snow is cold" and "a crane moves" are competing claims about the same thing — the appearance of contradiction across scripture is, for Zhiyi, almost always a failure to see how partial teachings fit together, not a real conflict in the Dharma itself.

Giving Rise to the Mind, Part One: Terminology, and Rejecting What Is Wrong 方言 · 簡非

Chinese Source

「菩提」者，天竺音也，此方稱「道」。「質多」者，天竺音，此方言「心」，即慮知之心也；天竺又稱「污栗馱」，此方稱是草木之心也；又稱「矣栗馱」，此方是積聚精要者為心也。今簡非者，簡積聚、草木等心，專在慮知心也。道亦有通有別，今亦簡之，略為十。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Bodhi" is a Sanskrit sound; in this land it is called "the Way." "Citta" is a Sanskrit sound; in this land it is called "mind," meaning specifically the mind that deliberates and knows. In Sanskrit it is also called hṛd, which in this land names the mind in the sense of plants and trees; it is also called hṛdaya, which in this land names mind as that which gathers together what is essential. Here, in ruling out what is not meant, the mind of mere aggregation and the mind of plants and trees are both set aside, and only the deliberating, knowing mind is intended. "The Way," too, has both a general sense and a particular sense; this too must here be narrowed down, briefly, into ten kinds.

Reading note: This short terminology note does real work: it fixes, at the outset, exactly which "mind" is meant by "the bodhi-mind" — not mind as mere aggregation, and not the loose sense of hṛd found in expressions like "the heart of a tree," but citta, the mind that actively deliberates and knows. Everything that follows — the ten deviant minds, and the whole later discussion of giving rise to the mind correctly — is about that mind specifically.

Ten Deviant Minds, Mapped to the Ten Dharma-Realms 簡非

Chinese Source

若其心念念專貪瞋癡，攝之不還、拔之不出，日增月甚，起上品十惡如五扇提羅者，此發地獄心、行火途道。若其心念念欲多眷屬，如海吞流、如火焚薪，起中品十惡如調達誘眾者，此發畜生心、行血途道。若其心念念欲得名聞，四遠八方稱揚欽詠，內無實德，虛比賢聖，起下品十惡如摩犍提者，此發鬼心、行刀途道。

Ru-Yi Translation

1. If the mind, thought after thought, is wholly given over to greed, anger, and delusion — gathered in but never turning back, pulled at but never coming loose, growing worse day by day and month by month — giving rise to the highest grade of the ten evils, like the figures the text names Wǔ Shàntíluó (五扇提羅, an obscure transliterated name whose precise referent is uncertain) — this gives rise to a mind bound for the hells, walking the path of fire.
2. If the mind, thought after thought, longs for many followers, like the ocean swallowing every stream, like fire consuming kindling — giving rise to the middle grade of the ten evils, like Devadatta luring away the assembly — this gives rise to a mind bound for the animal realm, walking the path of blood.
3. If the mind, thought after thought, longs to gain fame, to be praised and celebrated in every direction, far and near, while inwardly possessing no real virtue and falsely comparing itself to the sages and worthies — giving rise to the lowest grade of the ten evils, like Māgandika — this gives rise to a mind bound for the realm of ghosts, walking the path of the sword.

若其心念念常欲勝彼，不耐下人、輕他珍己，如鴟高飛下視，而外揚仁義禮智信，起下品善心、行阿脩羅道。若其心念念欣世間樂，安其臭身、悅其癡心，此起中品善心、行於人道。若其心念念知三惡苦多、人間苦樂相間、天上純樂，為天上樂關六根不出、六塵不入，此上品善心、行於天道。

4. If the mind, thought after thought, constantly wishes to surpass others, unable to bear being beneath anyone, belittling others while prizing itself — like a kite flying high and looking down — while outwardly displaying benevolence, righteousness, propriety, wisdom, and good faith, giving rise to the lowest grade of wholesome mind, this walks the path of the asuras.
5. If the mind, thought after thought, delights in worldly pleasures, content with this foul-smelling body, gratified by its own deluded state — this gives rise to the middle grade of wholesome mind, walking the human path.
6. If the mind, thought after thought, knows that the three evil destinies hold much suffering, that the human realm mixes suffering and pleasure together, and that the heavens above hold pleasure alone — and, for the sake of that heavenly pleasure, shuts the six faculties so nothing goes out and the six sense-objects so nothing comes in — this is the highest grade of wholesome mind, walking the heavenly path.

若其心念念欲大威勢，身口意纔有所作，一切弭從，此發欲界主心、行魔羅道。若其心念念欲得利智辯聰，高才勇哲，鑒達六合，十方顒顒，此發世智心、行尼犍道。若其心念念五塵六欲，外樂蓋微，三禪樂如石泉，其樂內重，此發梵心、行色無色道。若其心念念知善惡輪環，凡夫耽湎，賢聖所呵，破惡由淨慧，淨慧由淨禪，淨禪由淨戒，尚此三法如飢如渴，此發無漏心、行二乘道。

7. If the mind, thought after thought, longs for great authority and power, so that the moment body, speech, or mind acts at all, everyone and everything submits and follows — this gives rise to the mind of a ruler of the desire realm, walking the path of Māra.
8. If the mind, thought after thought, longs to gain sharp wisdom and eloquent cleverness, lofty talent and bold understanding, seeing clearly through the six directions, admired and looked up to on every side — this gives rise to a mind of worldly cleverness, walking the path of the non-Buddhist ascetics.
9. If the mind, thought after thought, finds the outward pleasure of the five sense-objects and six desires grown faint, while the pleasure of the third dhyāna, like a spring welling up from within rock, weighs ever heavier from within — this gives rise to the mind of Brahmā, walking the path of the form and formless realms.
10. If the mind, thought after thought, knows that wholesome and unwholesome karma turn in an endless wheel, that the ordinary person is sunk in indulgence and the sages and worthies condemn this, and knows too that breaking through unwholesomeness depends on pure wisdom, pure wisdom on pure dhyāna, and pure dhyāna on pure precepts, and so prizes these three dharmas as one starving prizes food or one parched prizes water — this gives rise to an uncontaminated mind, walking the path of the two lesser vehicles.

若心若道其非甚多，略言十耳。或開上合下，或開下合上，令十數方足而已。舉一種為語端，強者先牽。如論云：「破戒心墮地獄，慳貪心墮餓鬼，無慚愧心墮畜生。」即其義也。或先起非心，或先起是心，或是非並起，譬象、魚、風並濁池水，象譬外，魚譬內，風譬並起。又象譬諸非，自外而起；魚譬內觀羸弱，為二邊所動；風譬內外合雜，穢濁混和。

As for both mind and Way, the ways either can go wrong are very many; only ten are given here, in brief. Sometimes what is opened out above is folded together with what lies below, sometimes what is opened out below is folded together with what lies above, simply so that the number ten comes out square and complete. A single kind is raised as the leading example in each case, the strongest tendency named first. As the Treatise says: "A mind that breaks the precepts falls into the hells; a mind of stinginess and greed falls among the hungry ghosts; a mind without shame or conscience falls among the animals" — this is exactly that same sense. Sometimes the wrong mind arises first, sometimes the right mind arises first, and sometimes right and wrong arise together — as when an elephant, a fish, and the wind all alike muddy the water of a pool. The elephant stands for what comes from without, the fish for what comes from within, and the wind for the two arising together. Again: the elephant stands for every wrong that arises from outside oneself; the fish stands for inner contemplation grown weak and frail, moved by the two extremes; the wind stands for inner and outer mixing together, muddying and clouding everything as one.

Reading note: These ten deviant minds map directly onto the ten dharma-realms (十法界) — hell, animal, hungry ghost, asura, human, heaven, Māra, non-Buddhist paths, form-and-formless realm, and the two lesser vehicles — a scheme this treatise uses constantly elsewhere, and worth comparing with the different but overlapping ten wrong motivations opening topic 1 of the Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen. Notice the same grading principle at work in both lists: motives that look obviously bad (hell, animal, ghost, asura) shade into motives that look ordinary or even admirable (human contentment, heavenly bliss, worldly intelligence, Brahmā's inward joy) before ending on the subtlest failure of all — the tenth mind, wanting only one's own escape from suffering, which is the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha's own motivation, ruled out not because it is evil but because, as the closing passage explains, it lacks great compassion. The elephant/fish/wind simile for how a "muddied," wrong-headed mind can arise from without, from within, or from both at once is a small, vivid piece of natural imagery inside an otherwise highly abstract passage.

Why the Tenth Mind Still Falls Short 俱非雙簡

Chinese Source

又九種是生死，如蠶自縛；後一是涅槃，如麞獨跳，雖得自脫，未具佛法，俱非故雙簡。前九是世間，不動不出；後一雖出，無大悲，俱非，雙簡也。有為無為、有漏無漏、善惡、染淨、縛脫、真俗等種種法門亦如是。又九法約世間苦諦，後一非苦諦，雖非苦諦，曲拙灰近，故雙非，簡却。次、有為有漏約集諦，後一非集諦，雖非集諦，曲近灰拙，亦雙非，簡也。次、善惡染淨約道諦，後一是道諦，雖是道諦亦如前簡。次、縛脫真俗約滅諦，後一雖是滅諦亦如前簡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Again: the first nine are birth-and-death, like a silkworm binding itself in its own cocoon; the last is nirvāṇa, like a musk deer bounding off alone — though it wins its own release, it is not yet complete in the Buddhadharma; both, then, are wrong, and both alike are ruled out. The same holds for every such pair of dharma-gates — conditioned and unconditioned, contaminated and uncontaminated, wholesome and unwholesome, defiled and pure, bound and freed, ultimate and conventional. Again: the nine correspond to the truth of suffering as understood in the mundane sense; the last does not correspond to it in that sense — yet even so it remains crooked, clumsy, and close to mere ash, so both are wrong, and it too is ruled out. Next, the conditioned and the contaminated correspond to the truth of accumulation; the last does not correspond to it in that sense — yet even so it remains close to crooked, clumsy ash as well, and it too is ruled out. Next, the wholesome and unwholesome, the defiled and the pure, correspond to the truth of the path; the last does indeed correspond to the truth of the path — yet even so it is ruled out exactly as before. Next, the bound and the freed, the ultimate and the conventional, correspond to the truth of cessation; the last, though it does correspond to the truth of cessation, is likewise ruled out exactly as before.

若得此意，歷一切根塵、三業、四儀，生心動念皆此觀察，勿令濁心得起。設起速滅，如有明眼人能避險惡道，世有聰明人能遠離眾惡。初心行者若見此意，堪為世間而作依止。

If one grasps this sense, then across every sense-faculty and sense-object, the three karmic actions, and the four bodily postures, wherever a thought arises or the mind stirs, this same contemplation should be applied throughout, so that no muddied mind is ever allowed to arise. Should it arise regardless, let it be extinguished at once — just as a person with clear eyes can avoid a perilous road, and a person of true intelligence in this world can keep far from every evil. A beginning practitioner who grasps this sense is already fit to become a refuge for the world.

Reading note: This is the same move the Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen makes with its own tenth wrong motivation, run here with even more scholastic precision: the musk deer bounding off "alone" is a pointed image, since it captures exactly what disqualifies the śrāvaka/pratyekabuddha motivation without calling it evil — it is a real escape, just a solitary one. The fourfold-truth mapping that follows (worked through suffering, accumulation, path, and cessation in turn) is doing something specific: showing that the tenth mind partially satisfies two of the Four Noble Truths (path and cessation) in a way the other nine never do, while still falling short on all four when measured against the bodhisattva's own standard — a more exacting rejection than simply lumping it in with the other nine.

How the Resolve Actually Arises 感應發心

Chinese Source

問：行者自發心、他教發心？答：自、他、共、離，皆不可，但是感應道交而論發心耳。如子墮水火，父母騷擾救之。《淨名》云：「其子得病，父母亦病。」《大經》云：「父母於病子，心則偏重。」動法性山、入生死海，故有病行、嬰兒行，是名感應發心也。《禪經》云：「佛以四隨說法，隨樂、隨宜、隨治、隨義。」將護彼意，說悅其心；附先世習，令易受行；觀病輕重，設藥多少；道機時熟，聞即悟道。豈非隨機感應利益？

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Does the practitioner give rise to the resolve by their own power, or is it given rise to through another's teaching? Answer: Self, other, both together, and neither — none of these four alone will do; giving rise to the resolve can only be spoken of in terms of the mutual resonance between response and appeal. It is like a child falling into fire or water, and the parents, in their alarm, rushing to the rescue. The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "When the child falls ill, the parents fall ill as well." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Toward a sick child, a parent's own heart leans especially heavy." Stirring the very mountain of the Dharma-nature, entering the very ocean of birth-and-death — hence there is the conduct of the sick, and the conduct of the infant. This is what is called giving rise to the resolve through mutual resonance. The Dhyāna Sūtra says: "The Buddha teaches the Dharma by four kinds of accordance: according with what is enjoyed, according with what is fitting, according with what treats the ailment, and according with ultimate meaning." Guarding the listener's own intent, he teaches in a way that gladdens their heart; matching what they were habituated to in past lives, he makes it easy for them to accept and practice; observing the gravity of their affliction, he prescribes medicine in the right measure; and when the occasion for the Way has ripened, on merely hearing it, they awaken to the path at once. Is this not exactly benefit through resonance according with capacity?

《智度》論「四悉檀」，世法間隔名「世界」，隨其堪能名「為人」。兩悉檀與四隨同，亦是感應意也。更引論「五復次」：一、明菩薩種種行故說《般若波羅蜜經》；二、令菩薩增念佛三昧故；三、說跋致相貌故；四、拔弟子惡邪故；五、說第一義故說《般若波羅蜜經》。此五復次與四隨、四悉皆不異，又與五因緣同。

The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom speaks of "four siddhāntas": where worldly dharmas stand apart from one another, this is named "the worldly [siddhānta]"; according with what each person is capable of, this is named "[the siddhānta] for the individual." These two siddhāntas correspond to the four accordances above, and are likewise the sense of mutual resonance. The Treatise further gives "five further reasons" for teaching the Perfection of Wisdom: first, to clarify the bodhisattva's manifold practices; second, to increase the bodhisattva's own buddha-recollection samādhi; third, to explain the marks of non-retrogression; fourth, to pull disciples free of evil and deviant views; fifth, to explain ultimate meaning. These five further reasons are, every one, no different from the four accordances and the four siddhāntas, and are likewise the same as a further scheme of five causal conditions.

若不隨機，惱他故說，於彼無益。若大悲雷雨，得從微之著。論云：「真法及說者，聽眾難得故。」如是則生死非有邊非無邊，實相非難非易、非有非無，此名「真法」；能如此說聽，名「真說聽」。有三悉檀益，名「有邊」；第一義益名「非有邊非無邊」。故知緣起能辦大事，則感應意也。然四隨、四悉、五緣名異，意義則同。今說之四隨是大悲應益，悉檀是憐愍遍施，蓋左右之異耳。

If the Buddha did not accord with capacity, and taught in a way that only vexed the listener, there would be no benefit to them at all. It is instead like great compassion's own thunder and rain, able to move from the subtle to the manifest. The Treatise says: "The true Dharma, one able to teach it, and an assembly able to hear it — such things are hard to come by." On this view, birth-and-death is neither bounded nor unbounded, and the true mark of reality is neither difficult nor easy, neither being nor non-being — this is called "the true Dharma"; and being able to teach and to hear in just this way is called "true teaching and true hearing." Having the benefit of the three siddhāntas — worldly, individual, and antidote — is called "bounded"; having the benefit of the siddhānta of ultimate meaning is called "neither bounded nor unbounded." Hence it is known that dependent origination alone can accomplish this great matter — and this, again, is the very sense of mutual resonance. Now the four accordances, the four siddhāntas, and the five conditions differ only in name; in sense and meaning they are the same. To state it here: the four accordances are great compassion responding with benefit; the siddhāntas are compassion bestowed universally — these differ only as left differs from right.

言「因緣」者，或「因於聖、緣於凡」，或「因於凡、緣於聖」，則感應道交。當知三法，言味相符則意同。隨「樂欲」偏語，修因所尚；「世界」偏語，受報間隔，蓋因果之異耳。「便宜」者，選法以擬人；「為人」者，觀人以逗法，此乃欣赴不同耳。又五因緣者，眾生信樂為因，佛說一法一切法，大菩提心也，於經是「樂欲」，於論是「世界」；眾生有大精進勇猛，佛說一行一切行，則四三昧於經是「便宜」，於論是「為人」；眾生有平等大慧為因，感佛說一破一切破，獲勝果報及通經論，於經論俱是「對治」；眾生有佛智眼為因，感佛說一究竟一切究竟，得說旨歸寂滅，於經論俱是「第一義」也。

As for "cause and condition": sometimes the sage is the cause and the ordinary being the condition; sometimes the ordinary being is the cause and the sage the condition — either way, response and appeal meet on the same path. One should know that these three schemes, in the flavor of their own wording, correspond, and so agree in sense. Speaking in terms of "what is enjoyed" emphasizes what cultivating the cause values; speaking in terms of "the worldly" emphasizes how the karmic result received stands apart — these differ only as cause differs from fruit. "What is fitting" means selecting the Dharma to match the person; "[the siddhānta] for the individual" means observing the person to fit the Dharma to them — these differ only in which direction the meeting runs. Again, as to the five causal conditions: when sentient beings have faith and delight as their cause, the Buddha teaches one dharma that is every dharma — the great bodhi-mind; in the sūtras this is "what is enjoyed," in the treatises this is "the worldly." When sentient beings have great diligence and courage, the Buddha teaches one practice that is every practice — the four samādhis; in the sūtras this is "what is fitting," in the treatises this is "for the individual." When sentient beings have equanimous great wisdom as their cause, the Buddha, moved by this, teaches one refutation that is every refutation, obtaining thereby the excellent fruit and mastery of sūtra and treatise alike; in both this is "the antidote." When sentient beings have the buddha's own eye of wisdom as their cause, the Buddha, moved by this, teaches one ultimate that is every ultimate, able to speak of where it all leads, quiescent extinction itself; in both this is "ultimate meaning."

又五緣、五復次者，菩提心是諸行本，論舉種種行，蓋枝本之異耳；四三昧是通修，念佛是別修，蓋通別之異耳；勝報備說「依正」、「習果」、「報果」，跋致偏舉「習果」入位之相，蓋雙隻之異耳；除經論疑滯者，經論是起疑執處，拔弟子惡邪者是起過人，人處異耳；「本末究竟等」與「第一義」名同易見所以不異，是為義同。又聖說多端，或次說或不次說，或具說或不具說，或雜說或不雜說；眾生稟益不同，或次益不次益，或具益不具益，或雜益不雜益。或四悉檀成五緣、五緣成四悉；或四悉成一因緣、一因緣成一悉；或一一因緣皆具四悉、四悉具五緣。如是等種種互相成顯，還以三止觀結之，可以意知。

Again, as to the five conditions and the five further reasons: the bodhi-mind is the root of every practice, while the Treatise names manifold practices — these differ only as branch differs from root. The four samādhis are cultivated in general, while buddha-recollection is cultivated in particular — these differ only as general differs from particular. The excellent reward is explained in full, covering direct and environmental retribution, the habituation-fruit and the retribution-fruit, while non-retrogression singles out only the mark of the habituation-fruit entering its stage — these differ only as the paired differs from the single. Removing doubt and obstruction from sūtra and treatise addresses where doubt and clinging arise; pulling disciples free of evil and deviance addresses where transgression arises in a person — these differ only in where the problem is located. "Root and branch alike ultimate" and "ultimate meaning" plainly share the same sense under different names — this, then, is where the meaning is the same throughout. Again, the sage's own teaching takes many forms — sometimes in sequence, sometimes not; sometimes complete, sometimes not; sometimes mixed together, sometimes not — and sentient beings receive its benefit just as variously. Sometimes the four siddhāntas together make up the five conditions, and the five conditions the four siddhāntas; sometimes the four siddhāntas make up a single causal condition, and a single causal condition a single siddhānta; sometimes each causal condition is itself complete with all four siddhāntas, and each siddhānta complete with all five conditions. All such things bring each other out and reveal each other in turn, and can, once again, be resolved through the three kinds of calming-and-contemplation — this much may be understood by reflection alone.

又以一止觀結之，發菩提心即是觀，邪僻心息即是止。又五略秖是十廣，初五章秖是發菩提心一意耳；方便、正觀秖是四三昧耳；果報一章秖明違順，違即二邊果報，順即勝妙果報；起教一章轉其自心利益於他，或作佛身，施權實，或作九界像，對揚漸頓、轉漸頓、弘通漸頓；旨歸章秖是同歸大處祕密藏中。故知略廣意同也。

Again, resolving it through a single calming-and-contemplation: giving rise to the bodhi-mind is itself contemplation; the stilling of the deviant mind is itself calming. Again, the five brief points are simply the ten sections stated at greater length: the first five sections are simply this one sense of giving rise to the bodhi-mind; the preliminary expedients and the actual practice are simply the four samādhis; the section on fruits and rewards simply clarifies what goes against and what accords — going against yields the fruit of the two extremes, according yields the excellent, wondrous fruit; the section on the teachings that arise turns what was attained in one's own mind to benefit others, whether by manifesting a buddha's own body and bestowing the provisional and the real together, or by manifesting a form within the other nine dharma-realms, setting forth and propagating the gradual and the sudden alike; the section on where it all leads is simply the shared return to the great abode, the hidden treasury itself. Hence it is known that the brief and the extended agree in sense throughout.

Reading note: This long digression — occasioned by a single question, "does the resolve arise from the practitioner or from a teacher?" — answers with the tetralemma again (self/other/both/neither all rejected) in favor of "mutual resonance" (感應道交), the same principle underlying every act of Buddhist teaching in this framework: a parent doesn't decide unilaterally to save a drowning child, any more than the child unilaterally saves itself — need and response arise together. What follows is a demonstration, not just an assertion, that five different classificatory schemes scattered across the canon (the four accordances, the four siddhāntas, the five further reasons, cause-and-condition, and the five conditions) are all, at bottom, restatements of this one principle under different names — Zhiyi's characteristic move of using scholastic cross-referencing to argue for underlying unity rather than mere coincidence of vocabulary. The section closes by folding the whole of topic 1's five brief points back into calming-and-contemplation itself: giving rise to the bodhi-mind simply is contemplation, and the stilling of every deviant mind just examined simply is calming — the entire elaborate apparatus of this fascicle, in other words, is nothing other than what its own title names.

Revealing What Is Right, First Approach: The Four Kinds of Four Noble Truths 顯是 · 四諦

Chinese Source

顯是更為三：初、四諦，次、四弘，後、六即。四諦名相出《大經．聖行品》，謂生滅、無生滅、無量、無作。「生滅」者，苦集是世因果，道滅出世因果。苦則三相遷移，集則四心流動，道則對治易奪，滅則滅有還無。雖世、出世，皆是變異，故名「生滅四諦」也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Revealing what is right is further divided into three: first, by way of the Four Noble Truths; next, by way of the four universal vows; last, by way of the six identities. The names and marks of the Four Noble Truths come from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's chapter on noble conduct: they are called the truths of generation-and-extinction, of no generation, of the immeasurable, and of the unconditioned. As for generation-and-extinction: suffering and its accumulation are the mundane cause and effect; the path and cessation are the world-transcending cause and effect. Suffering is the shifting of its three marks; accumulation is the flowing and drifting of the four states of mind; the path is treated by an antidote that displaces it; cessation is the extinguishing of what exists, returning it to non-being. Whether mundane or world-transcending, all of this is change and alteration — hence this is called "the Four Noble Truths of generation-and-extinction."

「無生」者，苦無逼迫，一切皆空，豈有空能遣空？即色是空，受想行識亦復如是，故無逼迫相也。「集無和合相」者，因果俱空，豈有因空與果空合？歷一切貪瞋癡亦復如是。道不二相，無能治所治，空尚無一，云何有二耶？法本不然，今則無滅，不然不滅，故名「無生四諦」也。

As for no generation: suffering has no real oppression to it, since everything is already empty — how could emptiness ever need to be dispelled by emptiness? Form is itself already empty, and sensation, perception, formations, and consciousness are exactly the same — hence there is no mark of oppression at all. As for accumulation having no mark of combination: cause and effect are both alike empty — how could an empty cause combine with an empty effect? The same holds throughout every instance of greed, anger, and delusion. The path has no mark of duality — there is no treating subject and no treated object, for if there is not even a single one, how could there be two? The Dharma was never, in the first place, kindled, and so now there is nothing to extinguish; neither kindled nor extinguished — hence this is called "the Four Noble Truths of no generation."

「無量」者，分別校計苦有無量相，謂一法界苦尚復若干，況十法界？則種種若干，非二乘若智若眼所能知見，乃是菩薩所能明了。謂地獄種種若干差別，鈹剝、割截、燒煮、剉切，尚復若干不可稱計，況復餘界種種色、種種受想行識，塵沙海渧寧當可盡？故非二乘知見，菩薩智眼乃能通達。又集有無量相，謂貪欲瞋癡，種種心，種種身口，集業若干。身曲影斜、聲喧響濁，菩薩照之不謬耳。又道有無量相，謂析體、拙巧、方便、曲直、長短、權實，菩薩精明而不謬濫。又滅有無量相，如是方便能滅見諦，如是方便能滅思惟，各有若干正助，菩薩洞覽無毫差也。

As for the immeasurable: distinguishing and reckoning them out, suffering has immeasurable marks. Even within a single dharma-realm, suffering already comes in many varieties — how much more across all ten dharma-realms? Such manifold varieties lie beyond what the wisdom and vision of the two lesser vehicles can know or see; only a bodhisattva can clearly understand them. Consider the manifold varieties of the hells alone — flaying, cutting, burning, boiling, chopping — already too numerous to reckon; how much more, then, the manifold forms, and manifold sensations, perceptions, formations, and consciousnesses, of every other realm — could these ever be exhausted, any more than the sands of the desert or the drops of the sea? Hence they lie beyond the knowledge and vision of the two lesser vehicles; only the bodhisattva's own wisdom-eye can penetrate them. Again, accumulation has immeasurable marks: craving, anger, and delusion, manifold states of mind, manifold acts of body and speech, accumulate karma in manifold ways. As a bent body casts a crooked shadow, and a discordant sound produces a muddled echo, the bodhisattva illuminates all of it without ever going astray. Again, the path has immeasurable marks: analytical and embodying approaches, clumsy and skillful means, expedients crooked and straight, long and short, provisional and real — the bodhisattva discerns all of these with precision, never confusing one for another. Again, cessation has immeasurable marks: this expedient can extinguish what is severed by seeing the truths, that expedient can extinguish what is severed by sustained cultivation, each with its own manifold principal and auxiliary methods — the bodhisattva surveys all of it through and through, without the slightest discrepancy.

又即空方便正助若干皆無若干；雖無若干而分別若干，無謬無亂。又如是方便能析滅四住；又如是方便能體滅四住；如是方便能滅塵沙；如是方便能滅無明。雖種種若干，彼彼不雜。又三悉檀分別故有若干，第一義悉檀則無若干。雖無若干，從多為論，故名若干，稱「無量四諦」也。「無作四諦」者，皆是實相，不可思議，非但第一義諦無復若干，若三悉檀及一切法無復若干，此義可知，不復委記。

Again, once these manifold principal and auxiliary expedients are themselves seen as empty, their manifoldness itself is no longer manifold; yet even though it is no longer manifold, it is still distinguished as manifold, without confusion and without disorder. Again, this expedient can sever the four abodes of affliction analytically; that expedient can sever the four abodes of affliction by directly embodying their emptiness; this expedient can sever the dust-and-sand-like afflictions; that expedient can sever ignorance itself. Though manifold and various, each remains distinct from the rest, without mixing. Again, distinguished through the three siddhāntas, they are manifold; through the siddhānta of ultimate meaning, they are not manifold at all. Yet even though not manifold, speaking from the greater number, they are still called manifold — hence this is named "the Four Noble Truths of the immeasurable." As for the Four Noble Truths of the unconditioned: every one of them is simply the true mark of reality itself, inconceivable — and it is not only the ultimate-meaning truth that is no longer manifold, but the three other siddhāntas, and indeed every dharma whatsoever, that are no longer manifold either. This sense may be understood of itself, and need not be recorded in further detail.

若以四諦竪對諸土，有增有減，同居有四，方便則三，實報則二，寂光但一。若橫敵對者，同居生滅，方便無生滅，實報無量，寂光無作云云。又總說名四諦，別說名十二因緣。苦是識、名色、六入、觸、受、生、老死七支；集是無明、行、愛、取、有等五支；道是對治、因緣、方便；滅是無明滅乃至老死滅。故《大經》開四「四諦」，亦開四「十二因緣」。下智觀故得聲聞菩提，中智觀故得緣覺菩提，上智觀故得菩薩菩提，上上智觀故得佛菩提。

If the Four Noble Truths are matched vertically against the Buddha-lands, the count rises and falls: in the Land of Common Dwelling there are all four; in the Land of Expedient Means, three; in the Land of True Reward, two; in the Land of Eternally Tranquil Light, only one. If matched horizontally, one for one: the Land of Common Dwelling corresponds to generation-and-extinction; the Land of Expedient Means, to no generation; the Land of True Reward, to the immeasurable; the Land of Eternally Tranquil Light, to the unconditioned — and so on. Again, spoken of as a whole, this is called the Four Noble Truths; spoken of in its particulars, it is called the twelve links of dependent origination. Suffering corresponds to the seven links of consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-bases, contact, sensation, birth, and old-age-and-death; accumulation corresponds to the five links of ignorance, formations, craving, grasping, and becoming; the path corresponds to the antidote, to causation, and to expedient means; cessation corresponds to the extinguishing of ignorance, down to the extinguishing of old-age-and-death. Hence the Nirvāṇa Sūtra opens the Four Noble Truths into four kinds, and likewise opens the twelve links of dependent origination into four kinds. Contemplating with lesser wisdom, one attains the bodhi of the śrāvaka; contemplating with middling wisdom, one attains the bodhi of the pratyekabuddha; contemplating with higher wisdom, one attains the bodhi of the bodhisattva; contemplating with the highest wisdom of all, one attains the bodhi of the buddha.

又《中論》偈云「因緣所生法」即是生滅；「我說即是空」是無生滅；「亦名為假名」是無量；「亦名中道義」是無作。又解：「因緣」即集，「所生」即苦，「滅苦方便」是道，「苦集盡」是滅。又偈言因緣：「因緣即無明，所生法即行、名色、六入等。」故文云：「為利根弟子說十二因緣不生不滅相」，指前二十五品；「為鈍根弟子說十二因緣生滅相」，指後兩品。當知論偈總說，即四種四諦；別說，即四種十二因緣也。已分別四「四諦」竟。

Again, in the Middle Treatise's own verse: "whatever arises through causes and conditions" corresponds to generation-and-extinction; "this I declare to be empty" corresponds to no generation; "it is also called a provisional name" corresponds to the immeasurable; "it is also the meaning of the middle way" corresponds to the unconditioned. Another reading: "causes and conditions" corresponds to accumulation, "what arises" corresponds to suffering, "the expedient means that extinguish suffering" is the path, and "accumulation exhausted" is cessation. The verse also speaks in terms of dependent origination: "causes and conditions" means ignorance itself; "what arises" means formations, name-and-form, the six sense-bases, and the rest. Hence the text says: "For disciples of sharp faculties, the twelve links of dependent origination are taught in their mark of neither arising nor ceasing" — referring to the preceding twenty-five chapters of the Middle Treatise; "for disciples of dull faculties, the twelve links of dependent origination are taught in their mark of arising and ceasing" — referring to the two chapters that follow. One should know, then, that the Treatise's verse, taken as a whole, gives the four kinds of Four Noble Truths; taken in its particulars, it gives the four kinds of the twelve links of dependent origination. This concludes the exposition of the four kinds of Four Noble Truths.

Reading note: This is one of the most consequential passages in the whole treatise: the "Fourfold Four Noble Truths" (四種四諦) became a cornerstone of how later Tiantai scholasticism classifies the entire Buddhist canon into Four Teachings, matched here to the four kinds of practitioner wisdom in the passage's own closing lines — lesser wisdom to the śrāvaka, middling to the pratyekabuddha, higher to the bodhisattva, highest to the buddha. Notice how the scheme nests the three truths from the "Three Truths verse" (already introduced earlier in this fascicle via Nāgārjuna) inside a fourfold structure: generation-and-extinction treats suffering and its causes as literally real events requiring real remedies; no-generation empties that picture out entirely; the immeasurable restores manifold particularity, but now seen with a bodhisattva's comprehensive vision rather than mistaken for solid, separate facts; the unconditioned collapses the distinction between this teaching and the true mark of reality itself, with nothing left to count at all. Each is a legitimate, complete teaching in its own register, not a cruder approximation discarded once you reach the next — which is exactly what licenses reading one and the same verse of Nāgārjuna's four ways at once, as the passage does in its final rows.

Giving Rise to the Mind by Reasoning Through the Four Truths 推理發心

Chinese Source

諸經明種種發菩提心，或言推種種理發菩提心，或覩佛種種相發菩提心，或覩種種神通，或聞種種法，或遊種種土，或覩種種眾，或見修種種行，或見種種法滅，或見種種過，或見他受種種苦而發菩提心。略舉十種為首廣說云云。「推理發心」者：法性自天而然，集不能染，苦不能惱，道不能通，滅不能淨。如雲籠月，不能妨害；却煩惱已，乃見法性。經言：「滅非真諦，因滅會真。」滅尚非真，三諦焉是？煩惱中無菩提，菩提中無煩惱。是名「推生滅四諦上求佛道下化眾生發菩提心」。

Ru-Yi Translation

The various sūtras explain many different ways of giving rise to the bodhi-mind: some speak of doing so by reasoning through various principles; some by beholding the Buddha's various marks; some by beholding various miraculous transformations; some by hearing various teachings; some by journeying through various lands; some by beholding various assemblies; some by seeing various practices being cultivated; some by seeing various dharmas come to an end; some by seeing various transgressions; and some by seeing others undergo various sufferings. Ten such ways are named here as the leading examples, to be explained at length, and so on. As for giving rise to the mind through reasoning: the Dharma-nature is what it is of itself, spontaneously so — accumulation cannot stain it, suffering cannot afflict it, the path cannot make it pass through anything, cessation cannot purify it any further. It is like the moon veiled by clouds: the clouds cannot actually harm the moon itself; only once affliction is set aside is the Dharma-nature seen. A sūtra says: "Cessation itself is not the ultimate truth; it is by means of cessation that the ultimate truth is met." If even cessation is not, in itself, the ultimate, how much less the other three? There is no bodhi within affliction, and no affliction within bodhi. This is called "giving rise to the bodhi-mind, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below, by reasoning through the Four Noble Truths of generation-and-extinction."

「推無生四諦發心」者：法性不異苦集，但迷苦集失法性。如水結為水，無別水也。達苦集無苦集，即會法性。苦集尚是，何況道滅？經言：「煩惱即是菩提，菩提即是煩惱。」是名「推無生四諦上求下化發菩提心」。「推無量」者：夫法性者名為實相，尚非二乘境界，況復凡夫？出二邊表，別有淨法，如《佛藏經》十喻云云。是名「推無量四諦上求下化發菩提心」。

As for giving rise to the mind by reasoning through the Four Noble Truths of no generation: the Dharma-nature is no different from suffering and accumulation; it is only through being deluded about suffering and accumulation that the Dharma-nature is lost. It is like ice congealing into water — there is no separate water apart from it. To penetrate suffering and accumulation and find no suffering and accumulation there is precisely to meet the Dharma-nature. If this holds even for suffering and accumulation, how much more for the path and cessation? A sūtra says: "Affliction is itself bodhi; bodhi is itself affliction." This is called "giving rise to the bodhi-mind, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below, by reasoning through the Four Noble Truths of no generation." As for reasoning through the immeasurable: the Dharma-nature is what is called the true mark of reality — it lies beyond even the domain of the two lesser vehicles, let alone that of the ordinary person. Standing beyond the mark of both extremes, there is a distinct pure Dharma, as in the ten similes of the Buddha-Treasury Sūtra, and so on. This is called "giving rise to the bodhi-mind, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below, by reasoning through the Four Noble Truths of the immeasurable."

「推無作」者：夫法性與一切法無二無別，凡法尚是，況二乘乎？離凡法更求實相，如避此空，彼處求空。即凡法是實法，不須捨凡向聖。經言：「生死即涅槃，一色一香皆是中道。」是名「推無作四諦上求下化發菩提心」。若推一法，即洞法界，達邊到底，究竟橫竪，事理具足，上求下化備在其中，方稱發菩提心。菩提名「道」，道能通到橫竪彼岸，名「發心波羅蜜」。故於推理委作淺深，事理周遍。下去，法法例爾。

As for reasoning through the unconditioned: the Dharma-nature and every dharma whatsoever are neither two nor separate. If this holds even for the dharmas of the ordinary person, how much more for those of the two lesser vehicles? To leave the dharmas of the ordinary person behind and seek the true mark of reality elsewhere is like fleeing this emptiness only to go seeking emptiness somewhere else. The very dharmas of the ordinary person are themselves already the real Dharma; there is no need to abandon the ordinary in order to turn toward the sagely. A sūtra says: "Birth-and-death is itself nirvāṇa; a single color, a single scent, are none other than the middle way." This is called "giving rise to the bodhi-mind, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below, by reasoning through the Four Noble Truths of the unconditioned." If, in reasoning through even a single dharma, one thereby penetrates the whole dharma-realm, reaching every extreme to its very bottom, exhaustive in both breadth and depth, complete in both phenomenal conduct and principle, with seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below both already contained within it — only then does this deserve to be called giving rise to the bodhi-mind. Bodhi is named "the Way," because the Way can carry one through to the far shore in both breadth and depth alike — hence it is named "the perfection of giving rise to the mind." This is why, in reasoning through principle, the treatment has been made shallow to deep in careful order, phenomenal conduct and principle both covered throughout. From here on, every dharma follows this same pattern.

Reading note: Of the ten scriptural ways of giving rise to the bodhi-mind just listed, this fascicle only ever develops the first in full — reasoning through principle (推理) — and even that only through the lens of the Four Noble Truths just explained. Watch the same fourfold pattern recur exactly: generation-and-extinction reasons from real suffering to a Dharma-nature it cannot touch (the cloud-and-moon image); no-generation identifies affliction and bodhi outright ("ice congealing into water" — not two substances, one changing state); the immeasurable insists this pure Dharma still exceeds even the two lesser vehicles' domain; the unconditioned drops the distinction between ordinary and sage entirely ("a single color, a single scent, are none other than the middle way," the treatise's own favorite line, already used earlier in this fascicle to describe the perfect-and-sudden). This is where the upper half of fascicle 1 (卷第一上) ends in the source text — the lower half continues by developing the second and third of the ten ways, beholding the Buddha's marks and hearing the Dharma, before returning to conclude "revealing what is right" by way of the four universal vows and the six identities.

Revealing What Is Right, Second Approach: Beholding the Buddha 顯是 · 見佛相好

Chinese Source

「觀佛相好發心」者：若見如來父母生身，身相昞著，明了得處，輝麗灼爍，毘首羯磨所不能作，勝轉輪王相好纏絡，世間希有，天上天下無如佛，十方世界亦無比。願我得佛，齊聖法王，我度眾生，無數無央。是為「見應佛相好，上求下化，發菩提心」。若見如來，知如來無如來；若見相好，知相好非相好。如來及相皆如虛空，空中無佛，況復相好？見如來非如來，即見如來；見相非相，即見諸相。願我得佛齊聖法王，我度眾生無數無央。是為見勝應相好，上求下化，發菩提心。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for "contemplating the Buddha's auspicious marks and giving rise to the mind": if one sees the Tathāgata's body born of father and mother, its bodily marks radiantly manifest, clearly grasped wherever seen, brilliant and dazzling — a form even Viśvakarman himself could never fashion, surpassing even a wheel-turning king's own array of marks, rare in all the world, with none in heaven or on earth equal to the Buddha, and nothing in the ten directions to compare with him — and thereupon vows: "May I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king, and bring sentient beings across, numberless, without limit" — this is called "seeing the response-body buddha's auspicious marks, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below, and giving rise to the bodhi-mind." If, on seeing the Tathāgata, one knows that there is no Tathāgata there to see; if, on seeing his auspicious marks, one knows that those marks are not truly marks — the Tathāgata and his marks are both like empty space, and within emptiness there is no buddha, much less any auspicious mark. To see the Tathāgata as not the Tathāgata is precisely to see the Tathāgata; to see the marks as not marks is precisely to see every mark. "May I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king, and bring sentient beings across, numberless, without limit." This is called seeing the superior response-body's auspicious marks, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below, and giving rise to the bodhi-mind.

若見如來身相一切靡所不現，如明淨鏡覩眾色像，一一相好凡聖不得其邊、梵天不見其頂、目連不窮其聲。論云：「無形第一體，非莊嚴莊嚴。」願我得佛齊聖法王。是為見報佛相好，上求下化，發菩提心。若見如來，知如來智，深達罪福相，遍照於十方；微妙淨法身，具相三十二，一一相好即是實相，實相法界具足無減。願我得佛齊聖法王。是為見法佛相好，上求下化，發菩提心云云。

If one sees that the Tathāgata's bodily marks manifest absolutely everything without exception, like a bright, clear mirror reflecting every form and image — each and every mark such that neither ordinary being nor sage can find its boundary, Brahmā himself cannot see its crown, and even Maudgalyāyana cannot exhaust the range of its voice. The Treatise says: "The formless is the foremost body — adorned, yet without adornment." "May I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king." This is called seeing the reward-body buddha's auspicious marks, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below, and giving rise to the bodhi-mind. If one sees the Tathāgata and knows the Tathāgata's own wisdom — deeply penetrating the marks of offense and merit alike, illuminating the ten directions everywhere at once; the subtle and wondrous pure Dharma-body, complete with the thirty-two marks, each and every one of them itself the true mark of reality, the true mark of reality itself the dharma-realm, complete and lacking nothing — "May I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king." This is called seeing the Dharma-body buddha's auspicious marks, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below, and giving rise to the bodhi-mind, and so on.

Reading note: This is the second of the ten scriptural ways of giving rise to the bodhi-mind named at the end of the upper half — beholding the Buddha's marks — worked through four buddha-bodies of increasing subtlety: the response-body seen in the ordinary, literal sense (dazzling physical splendor); the "superior response-body," seen only once one recognizes that even those literal marks are themselves empty ("to see the Tathāgata as not the Tathāgata is precisely to see the Tathāgata"); the reward-body, whose marks are boundless, without edge or limit; and the Dharma-body, where every single mark simply is the true mark of reality, the dharma-realm itself. The same vow — "may I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king, and bring sentient beings across" — is repeated after each, unchanged; what changes each time is only how deeply the practitioner has understood what is actually being beheld.

Beholding Miraculous Transformations 見神變發心

Chinese Source

云何「見佛種種神變發菩提心」？若見如來依根本禪，一心作一，不得眾多；若放一光，從阿鼻獄上至有頂，火光晃耀，天地洞明，日月戢重輝，天光隱不現。願我得佛，齊聖法王云云。若見如來依如來無生理，不以二相應諸眾生，能令眾生各各見佛獨在其前。願我得佛，齊聖法王云云。若見如來依如來藏三昧正受，十方塵剎起四威儀，而於法性未曾動搖。願我得佛，齊聖法王云云。若見如來與諸神變無二無異，如來作神變、神變作如來；無記化化、化復作化，不可窮盡，皆不可思議，皆是實相而作佛事。願我得佛，齊聖法王云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by "seeing the Buddha's various miraculous transformations and giving rise to the bodhi-mind"? If one sees the Tathāgata, resting in the fundamental dhyānas, with a single mind produce a single thing without producing many at once; or release a single beam of light that reaches from the Avīci hell below up to the Peak of Existence above, its fiery radiance blazing forth, heaven and earth utterly illuminated, sun and moon folding their own light away, the light of every heaven hidden and no longer visible — "May I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king," and so on. If one sees the Tathāgata, resting in the Tathāgata's own principle of no-arising, respond to every sentient being without any duality of aspect at all, and yet cause each and every being to see the Buddha standing alone directly before them — "May I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king," and so on. If one sees the Tathāgata, resting in the correct absorption of the tathāgatagarbha-samādhi, rise up in the four bodily postures across every dust-mote-like buddha-land in the ten directions, and yet the Dharma-nature itself never once stirs or moves — "May I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king," and so on. If one sees that the Tathāgata and his every miraculous transformation are neither two nor different — the Tathāgata performing the transformation, and the transformation itself performing the Tathāgata; indeterminate transformation transforming transformation, transformation again performing further transformation, inexhaustibly, all of it beyond conception, all of it the true mark of reality carrying out the work of a buddha — "May I attain buddhahood, equal to this sagely Dharma-king," and so on.

Reading note: The third of the ten ways, and again given in four stages of increasing depth: a single, ordinary act of miraculous power; power exercised without division yet perceived individually by each being ("cause each and every being to see the Buddha standing alone directly before them" — an image also found in this fascicle's earlier account of the dragon king's rain, unmoved in its own palace while producing every kind of weather at once); power exercised across countless world-systems while the Dharma-nature itself never stirs; and finally the collapse of any distinction between the one who transforms and the transformation itself.

Revealing What Is Right, Third Approach: Hearing the Dharma 顯是 · 聞法發心

Chinese Source

云何「聞種種法發菩提心」？或從佛及善知識，或從經卷，聞生滅一句，即解世、出世法，新新生滅、念念遷移；戒慧解脫，寂靜乃真。願我得佛，能說淨道云云。或聞生滅，即解四諦皆不生不滅，空中無刺，云何可拔？誰苦、誰集、誰修、誰證？畢竟清淨，能所寂然。願我得佛，能說淨道云云。或聞生滅，即解生滅對不生滅為二，非生滅非不生滅為中。中道清淨，獨拔而出生死、涅槃之表。願我得佛，能為眾生說最上道，獨拔而出，如華出水、如月處空云云。或聞生滅，即解生滅、不生滅、非生滅非不生滅，雙照生滅、不生滅。即一而三；即三而一，法界祕密，常樂具足。願我得佛，能為眾生說祕密藏，如福德人執石成寶、執毒成藥云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by "hearing various dharmas and giving rise to the bodhi-mind"? Whether from the Buddha and a good spiritual friend, or from the sūtras themselves, on hearing a single phrase about generation-and-extinction, one thereby understands the mundane and world-transcending dharmas alike, ever newly generated and extinguished, shifting thought after thought; precepts, wisdom, and liberation are true only in their very stillness — "May I attain buddhahood, able to teach the pure path," and so on. Or, on hearing of generation-and-extinction, one thereby understands that the Four Noble Truths are themselves neither generated nor extinguished — within emptiness there is no thorn at all, so what is there left to pull out? Who suffers, who accumulates, who cultivates, who realizes? Ultimately pure, subject and object both alike quiescent — "May I attain buddhahood, able to teach the pure path," and so on. Or, on hearing of generation-and-extinction, one thereby understands that generation-and-extinction set against no-generation-and-extinction makes two, while neither generation-and-extinction nor no-generation-and-extinction is the middle. The middle way, pure in itself, stands alone, pulled clear above both birth-and-death and nirvāṇa — "May I attain buddhahood, able to teach the utmost path for sentient beings, standing alone, pulled clear, like a lotus rising out of the water, like the moon set in open sky," and so on. Or, on hearing of generation-and-extinction, one thereby understands generation-and-extinction, no-generation-and-extinction, and neither generation-and-extinction nor no-generation-and-extinction, illuminating both at once — one that is three, three that is one, the dharma-realm's own hidden treasury, ever blissful and complete — "May I attain buddhahood, able to teach sentient beings the hidden treasury, as a person of great merit turns a stone to treasure by grasping it, or turns poison to medicine by grasping it," and so on.

若聞無生，謂二乘無三界生，菩薩未無生。若聞無生，謂三乘皆無三界生。若聞無生，二乘非分，但在菩薩。菩薩先無分段生，次無變易生。若聞無生，一無生一切無生。若聞無量一句，例如此。若聞無量，謂二乘方便道、四諦、十六諦等，以為無量。若聞無量，二乘自用伏惑，不能化他；菩薩用此無量，自去惑，亦化他。若聞無量，謂二乘無分，但在菩薩。菩薩用斷界內塵沙，亦伏界外塵沙。若聞無量，謂二乘無分，但在菩薩。菩薩用斷界內外塵沙，亦伏無明。若聞無量，但在菩薩。菩薩用伏斷無明。若聞無作一句，例亦如此。

If one hears of "no-birth" and takes it to mean that the two lesser vehicles have no birth within the three realms, while the bodhisattva has not yet reached no-birth — this is one reading. If one hears of "no-birth" and takes it to mean that all three vehicles alike have no birth within the three realms — this is another. If one hears of "no-birth" and takes it to mean that it is not the portion of the two lesser vehicles at all, but belongs to the bodhisattva alone, who first has no birth-and-death of limited span, and next has no birth-and-death of unlimited transformation — this is another still. If one hears of "no-birth" and understands that one instance of no-birth is every instance of no-birth — this is the deepest reading. The same pattern holds for hearing the single phrase "the immeasurable." If one hears of "the immeasurable" and takes it to mean the two lesser vehicles' own expedient path, the Four Noble Truths, the sixteen aspects of the truths, and so forth. If one hears of "the immeasurable" and understands that the two lesser vehicles use it only to subdue their own affliction, unable to transform others, while the bodhisattva uses this same immeasurable both to remove their own affliction and to transform others. If one hears of "the immeasurable" and takes it to mean that it belongs to the bodhisattva alone, who uses it to sever the dust-and-sand afflictions within the three realms, and also to subdue those beyond them. If one hears of "the immeasurable" and takes it to mean that it belongs to the bodhisattva alone, who uses it to sever the dust-and-sand afflictions both within and beyond the three realms, and also to subdue ignorance itself. If one hears of "the immeasurable" as belonging to the bodhisattva alone, who uses it to subdue and sever ignorance itself — this is the deepest reading. The same pattern holds again for hearing the single phrase "the unconditioned."

若聞無作，謂非佛、天、人、修羅所作；二乘證此無作。《思益》云：「我等學於無作，已作證得，而菩薩不能證得云云」。若聞無作，謂三乘皆能證得。若聞無作，謂非二乘境界，況復凡夫？菩薩破權無作，證實無作。若聞無作，謂即權無作證實無作。若得此意，隨聞一句，通達諸句，乃至一切句、一切法而無障礙云云。

If one hears of "the unconditioned" and takes it to mean simply that which is not made by buddha, deva, human, or asura, and that the two lesser vehicles realize this unconditioned — the Sūtra of Assured Resolve says: "We ourselves study the unconditioned and have already realized it, while the bodhisattva is unable to realize it," and so on. If one hears of "the unconditioned" and takes it to mean that all three vehicles alike can realize it. If one hears of "the unconditioned" and takes it to mean that it lies beyond even the domain of the two lesser vehicles, let alone the ordinary person — the bodhisattva breaks through the provisional unconditioned and realizes the real unconditioned. If one hears of "the unconditioned" and understands that the very provisional unconditioned is itself the real unconditioned realized — this is the deepest reading. If one grasps this sense, then on hearing any single phrase, one penetrates every other phrase, down to every phrase and every dharma whatsoever, without any obstruction at all, and so on.

Reading note: This whole passage runs the same fourfold pattern (generation-and-extinction / no-generation / immeasurable / unconditioned) a third time, now applied not to reasoning or beholding but to sheer hearing — the same single phrase, understood four progressively deeper ways depending on the hearer's own capacity. "One instance of no-birth is every instance of no-birth" and its parallel readings of "the immeasurable" and "the unconditioned" are worth sitting with: Zhiyi is showing that the very same scriptural sentence, taken at face value, supports at least four defensible readings ranked from Hīnayāna-adjacent to fully bodhisattva-scope — which is exactly the "tearing apart the great net" promised back in the five brief points: apparent scriptural disagreement dissolved by recognizing depth of reading, not correcting the text.

The Verse of Dependent Origination, Read Four Ways 重說論偈

Chinese Source

夫一說眾解，是義難明，更約《論》偈重說之。若言「因緣所生法，我說即是空」者，既言「因緣所生」，那得即空？須析因緣盡，方乃會空，呼方空為即空。「亦名假名」者，有為虛弱，勢不獨立，假眾緣成；賴緣故假，非施權之假。「亦名中道義」者，離斷常名「中道」，非「佛性中道」。若作如此解者，雖三句皆空，尚不成即空，況復即假、即中？此生滅四諦義也。

Ru-Yi Translation

That one single statement can be understood in so many ways is a point difficult to make clear; let it be restated once more by way of the Treatise's own verse. If one takes "whatever arises through causes and conditions, this I declare to be empty" literally: since it says "arises through causes and conditions," how could this already be empty in any immediate sense? One would first have to analyze the causes and conditions away entirely before emptiness is ever met — calling that resulting, analyzed emptiness "empty." As for "it is also called a provisional name": what is conditioned is weak and frail, unable to stand on its own, provisionally formed by the coming-together of manifold conditions; it is "provisional" because it depends on conditions, not the "provisional" of skillfully bestowed expedient means. As for "it is also the meaning of the middle way": departing from the extremes of annihilation and permanence is named "the middle way" — not the "middle way of buddha-nature." Taken in this way, though all three clauses speak of emptiness, this does not even amount to true emptiness, much less the provisional or the middle — this is the sense proper to the Four Noble Truths of generation-and-extinction.

若因緣所生法，不須破滅，體即是空，而不得即假、即中。設作假、中，皆順入空。何者？諸法皆即空，無主我故；假亦即空，假施設故；中亦即空，離斷常二邊故。此三番語雖異，俱順入空。退，非二乘析法；進，非別非圓，乃是三獸渡河，共空之意耳。若謂即空、即假、即中者，三種邐迤，各各有異。三語皆空者，無主故空、虛設故空、無邊故空；三種皆假者，同有名字故假；三語皆中者，中真、中機、中實，故俱中。此得別失圓云云。

If, instead, what arises through causes and conditions need not be broken apart and extinguished, its very substance already being empty, and yet the provisional and the middle are still not attained — even where one speaks of the provisional and the middle, both still tend back into emptiness alone. Why is this? Every dharma is empty, since there is no master-self; the provisional, too, is empty, since it is only a provisional designation; the middle, too, is empty, since it departs from the two extremes of annihilation and permanence. Though these three ways of speaking differ, all three alike tend back into emptiness. Falling short of the bodhisattva's own path, this is not the two lesser vehicles' analytical approach to dharmas; yet failing to reach further, it is neither the distinct teaching nor the perfect teaching either — it is simply the shared crossing of the river by the three animals, the sense of a merely shared emptiness. If, instead, one takes it as "empty," "provisional," and "the middle" all at once, but understood as three distinct stages unfolding one after another, each with its own separate character: the three ways of speaking of emptiness are empty because there is no master, empty because it is a mere provisional designation, and empty because it is without any edge; the three ways of speaking of the provisional are provisional because each alike possesses a mere name; the three ways of speaking of the middle are the truth of the middle, the capacity of the middle, and the reality of the middle, and are hence alike the middle. This attains the distinct teaching, but falls short of the perfect.

若謂即空、即假、即中者，雖三而一，雖一而三，不相妨礙。三種皆空者，言思道斷故；三種皆假者，但有名字故；三種皆中者，即是實相故。但以空為名，即具假、中；悟空，即悟假、中。餘亦如是。當知聞於一法，起種種解，立種種願，即是種種發菩提心。此亦可解。其淨土、徒眾、修行、法滅、受苦、起過等，發菩提心，例前可解，不復委記。

If, instead, one takes it as "empty," "provisional," and "the middle" all at once, three yet one, one yet three, none obstructing any other: the three all being empty is because the path of speech and thought is cut off; the three all being provisional is because there is only a name; the three all being the middle is because it is simply the true mark of reality itself. Speaking of emptiness alone is already complete with the provisional and the middle; awakening to emptiness is already awakening to the provisional and the middle. Every other term is exactly the same. One should know, then, that on hearing a single dharma, giving rise to various understandings of it and setting forth various vows upon it is itself already the manifold ways of giving rise to the bodhi-mind. This much, too, may be understood. As for giving rise to the bodhi-mind through a pure land, an assembly of followers, cultivated practice, a dharma's own ending, undergoing suffering, or witnessing transgression — these follow the same pattern as what has already been shown, and need not be recorded in further detail.

Reading note: This is the fascicle's most technical single passage, and worth slowing down for: the same "Three Truths verse" quoted twice already in this fascicle (empty / provisional / middle) is now read four distinct ways, matched one-to-one with the Four Noble Truths just explained. Read literally and sequentially (you must first analyze conditions away to find emptiness), it only reaches the crude, Hīnayāna-adjacent teaching. Read so that everything — the provisional and the middle included — collapses back into a single, undifferentiated emptiness, it reaches only "the shared crossing of the river by the three animals": a classic simile (the hare swims across barely touching the water, the horse deeper, the elephant walking the riverbed) for how the same single teaching of emptiness is experienced with different depth by the three vehicles, none of them yet distinguishing the provisional and the middle as their own, equally real registers. Read as three separate, sequential stages — first empty, then provisional, then middle — it reaches the "distinct teaching" (別教), a real advance, but Zhiyi still marks it as falling short. Only the final reading — empty, provisional, and middle simultaneously, "three yet one, one yet three, none obstructing any other" — is the perfect teaching (圓教) this whole fascicle has been building toward since its first mention of the perfect-and-sudden. The same one sentence, unchanged, carries all four teachings at once; what differs is only how completely the reader lets it collapse.

Resolving It All Through the Three Kinds of Contemplation 結歸三止觀

Chinese Source

上來所說既多，今以三種止觀結之。然法性尚非一法，云何以三、四推之？今言一二三四，說法性是所迷，苦集是能迷；能迷有輕重，所迷有即離。約界內外分別，即有四種苦集；約根性取理，即有一二三四不同云云。若界內鈍人迷真重，苦集亦重；利人迷真輕，苦集亦輕。界外利鈍輕重亦如是。法性是所解，道滅是能解，所解有「即」、「離」，能解有「巧」、「拙」。界內鈍人所解「離」，能解則「拙」；利人所解「即」，能解亦「巧」。界外利鈍，即、離、巧、拙，亦如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since what has been said above is now considerable, let it be resolved here by way of the three kinds of calming-and-contemplation. Yet the Dharma-nature itself is not even a single, fixed thing — how, then, could it be reckoned by threes and fours? What is meant here by "one, two, three, four" is this: the Dharma-nature is what is deluded about, and suffering and its accumulation are the deluding itself; the deluding has its lighter and heavier grades, and what is deluded about has its "identity with" and its "separation from." Sorted by whether it lies within or beyond the three realms, there are four kinds of suffering and accumulation; sorted by capacity and how principle is grasped, there is a difference of one, two, three, and four, and so on. Within the three realms, a person of dull faculties is deluded about the truth heavily, and so their suffering and accumulation are heavy as well; a person of sharp faculties is deluded about it lightly, and so their suffering and accumulation are light as well. Beyond the three realms, sharp and dull faculties are heavier and lighter in exactly the same way. The Dharma-nature is what is understood, and the path and cessation are the understanding itself; what is understood has its "identity with" and "separation from," and the understanding has its "skillful" and its "clumsy." Within the three realms, the dull person's understanding is one of "separation," and clumsy; the sharp person's understanding is one of "identity," and skillful. Beyond the three realms, sharp and dull faculties are identical, separate, skillful, and clumsy in exactly the same way.

所以者何？事理既殊，昏惑亦甚。譬如父子，兩謂路人，瞋打俱重。瞋以譬集，打以譬苦。若謂煩惱即法性，事理相即，苦集則輕；實非骨肉，兩謂父子，瞋打則薄。麁細、枝本、通別、遍不遍、難易等，亦如是。或云界內苦集底滯為重；界外升出為輕。或界內皮惑故為淺；界外肉惑故為深。或言界內隨他意故為拙；界外隨自意故為巧。或言界內稱機故為巧；界外不稱機故為拙。或言界內有能所故為麁；界外無能所故為細。或言界內小道極在化城故為細；界外大道極在寶所故為麁。

Why is this? Where phenomenal conduct and principle are held apart as distinct, confusion and delusion run deep. It is like a father and son who each mistake the other for a stranger on the road: their anger and their blows are both severe. Anger here stands for accumulation, and blows stand for suffering. But if affliction is understood as itself the Dharma-nature, phenomenal conduct and principle held as identical, then suffering and accumulation grow light — as when two people who are not truly kin nonetheless mistake each other for father and son, and their anger and blows are then slight indeed. The same holds for every such further pair: coarse and subtle, branch and root, general and particular, pervading and not pervading, difficult and easy. Some say: within the three realms, suffering and accumulation lie stagnant at the bottom, and so are heavy; beyond the three realms, they rise up and out, and so are light. Some say: within the three realms, delusion lies at the skin's surface, and so is shallow; beyond, it lies within the flesh, and so is deep. Some say: within the three realms, teaching follows another's own intent, and so is clumsy; beyond, it follows one's own intent, and so is skillful. Some say: within the three realms, it matches the hearer's capacity, and so is skillful; beyond, it does not match the hearer's capacity, and so is clumsy. Some say: within the three realms, there remains a subject and object of understanding, and so it is coarse; beyond, there is no subject or object, and so it is subtle. Some say: within the three realms, the lesser path's own limit lies at the phantom city, and so it is subtle; beyond, the great path's own limit lies at the treasure-place itself, and so it is coarse.

或言界內客塵故為枝；界外同體故為本。或言界內在初故為本；界外在後故為枝。或言界內小、大共故為通；界外獨在大故為別。或言界內偏故為小、淺，故為別；界外圓故為大，無隔故為通。或言界內短故為不遍；界外周法界故為遍。或言界內在一切賢聖共故為遍；界外獨在大緣故為不遍。或言界內用二乘方便故為難斷；界外但依無礙慧故為易斷。如是等種種互說，今若結之，則易可解。若作淺深輕重者，漸次觀意也。若作一實四諦不分別者，圓觀意也。若作更互輕重者，不定觀意也。皆是大乘法相，故須識之。若見此意，即知三種：漸次顯是、不定顯是、圓頓顯是云云。

Some say: within the three realms, afflictions are adventitious dust, and so are branch; beyond, they are one with the very substance itself, and so are root. Some say: within the three realms, they come first, and so are root; beyond, they come after, and so are branch. Some say: within the three realms, lesser and great vehicles share the same affliction, and so it is general; beyond, it belongs to the great vehicle alone, and so it is particular. Some say: within the three realms, it is partial, and so lesser and shallow, and so particular; beyond, it is perfect, and so great, and unobstructed, and so general. Some say: within the three realms, it is limited, and so does not pervade; beyond, it fills the whole dharma-realm, and so pervades. Some say: within the three realms, it is shared among every sage and worthy alike, and so pervades; beyond, it belongs to the great vehicle's own causal condition alone, and so does not pervade. Some say: within the three realms, it is severed by means of the two lesser vehicles' own expedients, and so is hard to sever; beyond, it relies only on unobstructed wisdom, and so is easy to sever. All such things are argued back and forth in various ways — yet if resolved now, they are easily understood. Taken as matters of shallow-and-deep, light-and-heavy, this is the sense of the gradual contemplation. Taken as the one, real Four Noble Truths without any such distinctions at all, this is the sense of the perfect contemplation. Taken as shifting back and forth between light and heavy, this is the sense of the indeterminate contemplation. All of these are marks of the Mahāyāna Dharma, and so must be recognized for what they are. Grasping this sense, one thereby knows the three kinds: revealing what is right by the gradual, by the indeterminate, and by the perfect-and-sudden, and so on.

問：集既有四，苦果何二？答：惑隨於解，集則有四；解隨於惑，但感二死。例如小乘，惑隨於解，則有見諦思惟；若解隨於惑，但是一分段生死耳。問：苦集可是因緣所生法，道滅何故爾？答：苦集是所破，道滅是能破。能破從所破得名，俱是因緣生法。故《大經》云：「因滅無明，則得熾然三菩提燈。」亦是因緣也。問：法性是所迷，何故二，何故四？答：法性隨權實，是故二；法性隨根緣，是故四。若見此意，例見相、聞法，乃至起過，例作四種，分別廣說云云。

Question: Since accumulation has four kinds, why does the fruit of suffering have only two? Answer: When affliction follows understanding, accumulation has four kinds; when understanding follows affliction, only the two kinds of death are incurred. It is exactly as in the Hīnayāna: when affliction follows understanding, there are the afflictions severed by seeing the truths and those severed by sustained cultivation; when understanding follows affliction, there is only the single birth-and-death of limited span. Question: Suffering and accumulation may well be dharmas that arise through causes and conditions — but why should the path and cessation be so as well? Answer: Suffering and accumulation are what is broken through; the path and cessation are what does the breaking. What does the breaking takes its very name from what it breaks through, and so both alike are dharmas arisen through causes and conditions. Hence the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "By means of extinguishing ignorance, the blazing lamp of the threefold bodhi is obtained" — this, too, is dependent origination. Question: The Dharma-nature is what is deluded about — why, then, is it sometimes spoken of as two, and sometimes as four? Answer: The Dharma-nature, following the provisional and the real, is spoken of as two; the Dharma-nature, following capacity and condition, is spoken of as four. Grasping this sense, the same fourfold pattern applies equally to beholding the Buddha's marks, hearing the Dharma, and even witnessing transgression — each, following the same example, may be worked out into four kinds and explained at length, and so on.

Reading note: This closes "revealing what is right by way of the Four Noble Truths" (the whole stretch of this fascicle running from before the fascicle break through here) by explicitly re-sorting everything already covered into the gradual, the indeterminate, and the perfect-and-sudden — the three kinds of calming-and-contemplation first named early in the upper half. The long list of paired terms — heavy/light, skillful/clumsy, coarse/subtle, branch/root, general/particular, pervading/not-pervading, difficult/easy, each argued both ways depending on whether affliction lies "within the three realms" or "beyond" them — is not indecision on Zhiyi's part; it is the point. Which side of each pair counts as "deeper" depends entirely on which axis you're measuring by, and the text deliberately lets contrary answers stand side by side ("some say... some say...") rather than adjudicating between them, because the real resolution is structural, not a matter of picking a winner: sorted by fixed shallow-to-deep stages, it's gradual contemplation; taken as one undifferentiated truth, it's perfect contemplation; taken as shifting unpredictably between registers, it's indeterminate contemplation.

Revealing What Is Right, Fourth Approach: The Four Universal Vows 顯是 · 四弘誓願

Chinese Source

中、約弘誓顯是者，前推法性、聞法等，其義已顯，為未了者更約四弘。又四諦中，多約「解」明上求下化；四弘中，多約「願」明上求下化。又四諦中，通約「三世佛」明上求下化；四弘中，多約「未來佛」明上求下化。又四諦中，多約「諸根」明上求下化；四弘中，專約「意根」明上求下化。如此分別令易解，得意者不俟也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, revealing what is right by way of the four universal vows: what was already reasoned above through the Dharma-nature, through hearing the Dharma, and the rest, has already made this sense clear; for those who have not yet fully grasped it, it is explained once more by way of the four vows. Also: within the Four Noble Truths, seeking enlightenment above and transforming beings below is mostly explained in terms of "understanding"; within the four vows, it is mostly explained in terms of "vowing." Within the Four Noble Truths, this is explained generally in terms of "the buddhas of the three times"; within the four vows, it is mostly explained in terms of "the buddha yet to come." Within the Four Noble Truths, this is mostly explained in terms of "the various faculties"; within the four vows, it is explained specifically in terms of "the mind-faculty" alone. Distinguishing it this way makes it easier to understand; one who has already grasped the point need not wait for this.

夫心不孤生，必託緣起。意根是因，法塵是緣，所起之心是所生法。此根、塵，能、所，三相遷動，竊起竊謝，新新生滅，念念不住，睒爍如電耀，遄疾若奔流。色泡、受沫、想炎、行城、識幻，所有依報——國土、田宅、妻子、財產，一念喪失，倏有忽無。三界無常，一篋偏苦，四山合來，無逃避處，唯當專心戒定智慧，竪破顛倒，橫截死海，超度有流。經言：「我昔與汝等不見四真諦，是故久迴轉。」火宅如此，云何耽湎縱逸嬉戲？是故慈悲起四弘誓，拔苦與樂。如釋迦之見耕墾，似彌勒之觀毀臺，即其義也。以明了四諦，故非九縛；起四弘誓，故非一脫。是為非縛非脫發真正菩提心，顯是義明矣。

Mind never arises alone; it must always depend on causes and conditions. The mind-faculty is the cause, mental objects are the condition, and the mind that arises from them is the generated dharma. This faculty and object, this subject and object, all three marks shifting and moving — stealing into being, stealing out of being, ever newly generated and extinguished, never resting for even a single thought-moment, flashing like a bolt of lightning, rushing swift as a torrent. Form is a bubble, sensation a patch of foam, perception a mirage, formations a phantom city, consciousness an illusion — and every environmental retribution one possesses, one's land, house, fields, spouse, children, and wealth, can be lost in a single thought, here one moment and gone the next. The three realms are impermanent, this whole box of existence nothing but suffering; the four mountains close in together, with nowhere left to flee — one should therefore give the whole mind, undivided, to precepts, concentration, and wisdom, breaking through delusion vertically and cutting across the sea of death horizontally, crossing beyond the stream of existence altogether. A sūtra says: "I, together with all of you, failed in the past to see the Four Noble Truths — and so we have wandered in circles for so long." Since the burning house is like this, how could one still indulge, drift, and play? For this very reason, out of compassion, the four universal vows are set forth, to pull beings out of suffering and give them happiness — just as Śākyamuni, on seeing a farmer plowing the earth, or Maitreya, on contemplating a ruined tower, were each moved to renounce the world — this is exactly that same sense. Through clearly understanding the Four Noble Truths, one is freed from the nine kinds of bondage; through setting forth the four universal vows, one is freed from the single, merely personal liberation as well. This is what is called giving rise to the true and correct bodhi-mind, neither bound nor freed — and with this, the sense of revealing what is right is made clear.

Reading note: This opens the fourth and final way of "revealing what is right," now approached through the four universal vows rather than the Four Noble Truths — and immediately narrows the whole cosmic analysis down to the single mind-faculty meeting a single mental object, a single thought arising. The catalogue of similes for the five aggregates (form a bubble, sensation foam, perception a mirage, formations a phantom city, consciousness an illusion) is a well-known scriptural set; what follows will apply the same three-pass method used earlier in this fascicle — first empty, then provisional, then all three truths at once — but now to this one arising thought itself, rather than to the Four Noble Truths in the abstract.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, simply contemplating faculty and object meeting each other, a single thought arises — and neither the arising itself nor what it generates is anything other than empty. To mistakenly suppose that mind "arises" at all: this arising has no self-nature, no other-nature, no shared nature, and no causeless nature. When it arises, it does not come from self, other, both, or neither; when it departs, it does not go toward east, west, south, or north. This mind is not located within, without, or in between; nor does it exist fixedly on its own — it has only a name, and is called "mind" by that name alone. Even that name does not abide, nor does it "not abide," since it cannot be found at all. Its arising is itself no "arising," and also no "non-arising" — being and non-being are both alike quiescent. The ordinary, foolish person supposes it exists; the wise know it does not — like the moon reflected in water: gaining it brings joy, losing it brings grief, to the foolish; but for one who has gone beyond, there is neither grasping nor letting go, and so neither joy nor grief at all. A mirror's image, a magical illusion, are exactly the same.

《思益》云：「苦無生，集無和合，道不二，滅不生。」《大經》云：「解苦無苦，而有真諦，乃至解滅無滅，而有真諦。」集既即空，不應如彼渴鹿，馳逐陽焰。苦既即空，不應如彼癡猴，捉水中月。道既即空，不應言：「我行即空、不行不即空。」如筏喻者，法尚應捨，何況非法？滅既即空，不應言：「眾生壽命，誰於此滅而證彼滅。」生死即空，云何可捨？涅槃即空，云何可得？

The Sūtra of Assured Resolve says: "Suffering has no arising, accumulation has no combining, the path is not two, cessation does not arise." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "To understand suffering as without suffering is itself to have the ultimate truth — and so too, understanding cessation as without cessation is itself to have the ultimate truth." Since accumulation is already empty, one should not be like the thirsty deer chasing after a mirage. Since suffering is already empty, one should not be like the foolish monkey grasping at the moon reflected in water. Since the path is already empty, one should not say: "My practice is empty, but non-practice is not empty" — as with the simile of the raft, even the Dharma itself should be let go of, how much more what is not the Dharma? Since cessation is already empty, one should not say: "Some sentient being's own lifespan — whose cessation is it, that realizes that cessation?" If birth-and-death is itself empty, what is there left to abandon? If nirvāṇa is itself empty, what is there left to attain?

經言：「我不欲令無生法中有修道，若四念處乃至八聖道。我不欲令無生法中有得果，若須陀洹乃至阿羅漢。」依例亦應言：「我不欲令無生法中有色受想行識，我不欲令無生法中有貪欲瞋恚癡。」但愍念眾生，興誓願，拔兩苦，與二樂。以達苦集空，故非九縛；達道滅空，故非一脫。是為非縛非脫發真正菩提心，顯是義明矣。

A sūtra says: "I do not wish there to be, within the dharma of no-arising, any cultivation of the path — such as the four foundations of mindfulness, down to the eightfold noble path. I do not wish there to be, within the dharma of no-arising, any fruit attained — such as stream-entry, down to arhatship." Following this same pattern, one should also say: "I do not wish there to be, within the dharma of no-arising, any form, sensation, perception, formations, or consciousness. I do not wish there to be, within the dharma of no-arising, any craving, anger, or delusion." One merely feels compassion for sentient beings, and so raises the vow, to pull them free of the two sufferings and grant them the two joys. Through penetrating suffering and accumulation as empty, one is freed from the nine kinds of bondage; through penetrating the path and cessation as empty, one is freed from the single, merely personal liberation as well. This is what is called giving rise to the true and correct bodhi-mind, neither bound nor freed — and with this, the sense of revealing what is right is made clear.

Second Pass: The Arising Thought Is Provisional 觀根塵即假

Chinese Source

秖觀根塵，一念心起，心起即假。假名之心，為迷解本，謂四諦有無量相。三界無別法，唯是一心作。心如工畫師，造種種色。心構六道，分別校記，無量種別，謂如是見、愛是界內輕重集相，界外輕重集相。如是生死是分段輕重苦相，界外輕重苦相。還翻此心而生於解。譬如畫師洗蕩諸色、塗以墡彩。所謂觀身不淨，乃至觀心無常，如是道品紆通化城。觀身身空，乃至觀心心空。空中無無常，乃至無不淨。如是道品直通化城。觀身無常，無常即空，乃至觀身法性非常非無常、非空非不空，乃至觀心亦如是。如是道品紆通寶所。觀身法性非淨非不淨，雙照淨不淨；乃至觀心法性常無常、雙照常無常。如是道品直通寶所。

Ru-Yi Translation

Simply contemplating faculty and object, a single thought arises — and that arising is itself the provisional. This provisionally named mind is the very root of both delusion and understanding, and is said to give the Four Noble Truths their immeasurable marks. The three realms hold no separate dharma of their own — they are made by this one mind alone. The mind is like a skilled painter, fashioning every kind of form. The mind constructs the six destinies, distinguishing and reckoning out immeasurable kinds of difference — such as: this view and this craving are the lighter and heavier marks of accumulation within the three realms, and the lighter and heavier marks of accumulation beyond them; this birth-and-death is the lighter and heavier mark of suffering of limited span, and the lighter and heavier mark of suffering beyond the three realms. One then turns this very mind back around and, from it, brings forth understanding — just as a painter washes away the old colors and applies fresh pigment in their place. That is to say: contemplating the body as impure, down to contemplating the mind as impermanent — practices of this kind lead, by a winding path, through to the phantom city. Contemplating the body's own body as empty, down to contemplating the mind's own mind as empty — within that emptiness there is no impermanence at all, nor any impurity — practices of this kind lead directly through to the phantom city. Contemplating the body as impermanent, and that impermanence as itself empty, down to contemplating the body's own Dharma-nature as neither permanent nor impermanent, neither empty nor not empty — and contemplating the mind exactly the same way — practices of this kind lead, by a winding path, through to the treasure-place itself. Contemplating the body's own Dharma-nature as neither pure nor impure, illuminating both purity and impurity at once, down to contemplating the mind's own Dharma-nature as both permanent and impermanent, illuminating both at once — practices of this kind lead directly through to the treasure-place itself.

是人見諦滅，名須陀洹；是人思惟滅，名三果。是人見滅，名見地；是人思滅，名薄、名離、名已辦，乃至侵習，名辟支佛。是人見思滅，名十住；塵沙滅，名十行、十迴向；無明滅，名十地、等覺、妙覺。是人見思、塵沙滅，名十信；無明滅，名十住、十行、十迴向、十地、等覺、妙覺。分別十六門道滅不同及一切恒沙佛法，分別校計，不可說、不可說，如觀掌果無有僻謬，皆從心生，不餘處來。觀此一心能通不可說心；不可說心能通不可說法；不可說法能通不可說非心非法。觀一切心，亦復如是。

For such a person, the extinguishing of what is severed by seeing the truths is called stream-entry; the extinguishing of what is severed by sustained cultivation is called the third fruit. For such a person, the extinguishing of what is seen is called the ground of vision; the extinguishing of what is cultivated is called "thinned," "freed," or "accomplished," and, carried further still, is called pratyekabuddhahood. For such a person, the extinguishing of what is severed by seeing and by cultivation together is called the ten abidings; the extinguishing of the dust-and-sand afflictions is called the ten practices and the ten dedications; the extinguishing of ignorance is called the ten grounds, virtual enlightenment, and wondrous enlightenment. In the perfect teaching, for such a person, the extinguishing of what is severed by seeing and cultivation is called the ten faiths; the extinguishing of ignorance is called the ten abidings, the ten practices, the ten dedications, the ten grounds, virtual enlightenment, and wondrous enlightenment. Distinguishing the sixteenfold differences of how the path extinguishes affliction, and the whole of the Buddhadharma numerous as the sands of the Ganges, reckoned out and distinguished — this cannot be spoken of, cannot be spoken of at all; yet, like observing a fruit resting in one's own palm, there is nothing mistaken in it, for all of it arises from mind alone, and comes from nowhere else. Contemplating this one mind, one can penetrate through to the unspeakable mind; the unspeakable mind can penetrate through to the unspeakable dharma; the unspeakable dharma can penetrate through to what is unspeakably neither mind nor dharma. Contemplating every mind whatsoever is exactly the same.

九縛凡夫不覺不知，如大富盲兒坐寶藏中，都無所見；動轉罣礙，為寶所傷。二乘熱病，謂諸珍寶是鬼虎龍蛇，棄捨馳走，竛竮辛苦五十餘年。雖縛脫之殊，俱貧如來無上珍寶。起大慈悲誓願，拔苦與樂，是為非縛非脫發真正菩提心，顯是義明矣。

The ordinary being, bound by the nine kinds of bondage, neither perceives nor knows any of this — like a wealthy blind child sitting in the midst of a treasure-store, seeing nothing at all, stumbling and catching on things at every turn, even wounded by the very treasure around them. The two lesser vehicles suffer a kind of fever: mistaking every one of these jewels for a ghost, a tiger, a dragon, or a snake, they abandon it all and flee, wandering destitute and in hardship for fifty-some years. Though bondage and liberation differ between them, both alike are poor, lacking the Tathāgata's own supreme treasure. Hence great compassion gives rise to the vow, to pull beings free of suffering and grant them happiness. This is what is called giving rise to the true and correct bodhi-mind, neither bound nor freed — and with this, the sense of revealing what is right is made clear.

Reading note: The blind child sitting in a treasure-store, wounded by the very jewels around him, and the two lesser vehicles fleeing those same jewels for fifty years mistaking them for monsters, are two halves of a single point: both the ordinary person and the arhat/pratyekabuddha are, in different ways, poor in the midst of wealth — one because he cannot see what surrounds him, the other because what he does glimpse of it terrifies him into flight. The "fifty-some years" alludes to the Lotus Sūtra's parable of the wayward son, wandering destitute before discovering he was the rich man's heir all along. Note too how this pass differs from the first: where the empty-level analysis dissolved suffering and its causes into "no arising," here the provisional-level analysis restores the whole map of Buddhist attainment — stream-entry, the ten abidings, the ten grounds, the sixteenfold distinctions of how the path extinguishes affliction — as real, distinguishable stages, all of it "arising from mind alone."

Third Pass: Empty, Provisional, and the Middle at Once 觀根塵即空即假即中

Chinese Source

次、根塵相對，一念心起，即空即假即中者，若根若塵，並是法界，並是畢竟空，並是如來藏，並是中道。云何即空？並從緣生，緣生即無主，無主即空。云何即假？無主而生即是假。云何即中？不出法性，並皆即中。當知一念即空即假即中，並畢竟空，並如來藏，並實相。非三而三，三而不三；非合非散，而合而散，非非合非非散；不可一異而一異。譬如明鏡，明喻即空，像喻即假，鏡喻即中。不合不散，合散宛然；不一二三，二三無妨。此一念心，不縱不橫，不可思議。非但己爾，佛及眾生亦復如是。《華嚴》云：「心、佛及眾生，是三無差別。」當知己心具一切佛法矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next: where faculty and object meet and a single thought arises, at once empty, at once provisional, and at once the middle — both faculty and object alike are the dharma-realm, alike ultimately empty, alike the tathāgatagarbha, alike the middle way. How are they empty? Because both alike arise from conditions, and what arises from conditions has no master; having no master, they are empty. How are they provisional? Because, having no master, they nonetheless arise — this very arising is itself the provisional. How are they the middle? Because neither ever departs from the Dharma-nature — both alike are the middle. One should know, then, that a single thought is at once empty, at once provisional, and at once the middle: alike ultimately empty, alike the tathāgatagarbha, alike the true mark of reality. Not three, and yet three; three, and yet not three; neither combined nor separate, and yet combined, and yet separate, and yet neither not-combined nor not-separate; unable to be called simply one or simply different, and yet one, and yet different. It is like a bright mirror: its brightness is a likeness for the empty, its reflected image a likeness for the provisional, the mirror itself a likeness for the middle — neither combined nor separate, and yet combination and separation both entirely present; neither one, two, nor three, and yet two and three are no obstacle at all. This single thought of mind is neither vertical nor horizontal, utterly beyond conception. Nor is this true of one's own mind alone — the Buddha, and every sentient being, are exactly the same. The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "Mind, Buddha, and sentient beings — these three are without any difference." One should know, then, that one's own mind is already complete with the whole of the Buddhadharma.

《思益》云：「愚於陰界入，而欲求菩提，陰界入即是，離是無菩提。」《淨名》曰：「如來解脫，當於眾生心行中求。」「眾生即菩提，不可復得；眾生即涅槃，不可復滅。」「一心既然，諸心亦爾，一切法亦爾。」《普賢觀》云：「毘盧遮那遍一切處。」即其義也。當知一切法即佛法，如來法界故。若爾，云何復言「遊心法界如虛空」？又言「無明、明者，即畢竟空」？此舉「空」為言端，空即不空，亦即非空非不空。又言「一微塵中有大千經卷」；「心中具一切佛法，如地種，如香丸」者，此舉「有」為言端。有即不有，亦即非有非不有。又言「一色一香無非中道」，此舉「中道」為言端。即中而邊，即非邊非不邊，具足無減。勿守語害圓、誣罔聖意。

The Sūtra of Assured Resolve says: "The foolish, amid the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements, seek bodhi elsewhere — yet the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements are themselves already bodhi; apart from these there is no bodhi to be found." The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "The Tathāgata's own liberation should be sought precisely within the very mental activity of sentient beings." "Sentient beings are themselves bodhi, and cannot be attained all over again; sentient beings are themselves nirvāṇa, and cannot be extinguished all over again." "If this is so of the one mind, it is exactly so of every other mind, and of every dharma whatsoever." The Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra says: "Vairocana pervades every place" — this is exactly that same sense. One should know, then, that every dharma whatsoever is itself already the Buddhadharma, because it is itself already the Tathāgata's own dharma-realm. If this is so, how can it also be said, "let the mind roam through the dharma-realm as through empty space"? Or that "ignorance, and illumination too, are themselves ultimately empty"? These raise "emptiness" as their leading term: emptiness is itself not-empty, and is also neither empty nor not-empty. Again it is said, "within a single mote of dust there are sūtra-scrolls as numerous as the great trichiliocosm"; "the mind is complete with the whole Buddhadharma, as the earth already holds every seed, or as a single incense-pellet holds every fragrance" — these raise "being" as their leading term: being is itself not-being, and is also neither being nor not-being. Again it is said, "a single color, a single scent, are none other than the middle way" — this raises "the middle way" as its leading term: the very middle is itself the extreme, and is also neither extreme nor not-extreme, complete and lacking nothing. One must not cling to the words themselves and so do violence to the perfect teaching, slandering and misrepresenting the sage's own intent.

若得此解，根塵一念心起，根即八萬四千法藏；塵亦爾，一念心起，亦八萬四千法藏。佛法界、對法界、起法界，無非佛法。生死即涅槃，是名苦諦。一塵有三塵，一心有三心；一一塵有八萬四千塵勞門，一一心亦如是。貪瞋癡亦即是菩提，煩惱亦即是菩提，是名集諦。翻一一塵勞門，即是八萬四千諸三昧門，亦是八萬四千諸陀羅尼門，亦是八萬四千諸對治門，亦成八萬四千諸波羅蜜。無明轉即變為明，如融氷成水，更非遠物，不餘處來，但一念心普皆具足。如如意珠，非有寶，非無寶。若謂無者即妄語；若謂有者即邪見。不可以心知，不可以言辯。眾生於此不思議、不縛法中，而思想作縛；於無脫法中，而求於脫。是故起大慈悲，興四弘誓，拔兩苦，與兩樂，故名「非縛非脫發真正菩提心」。

If one grasps this understanding, then when faculty and object meet and a single thought arises, the faculty itself is the eighty-four thousand dharma-treasuries; the object is exactly the same — a single thought arising is likewise the eighty-four thousand dharma-treasuries. The dharma-realm of buddhahood, the dharma-realm one is faced with, and the dharma-realm that arises — none of it is anything other than the Buddhadharma. Birth-and-death is itself nirvāṇa: this is what is called the truth of suffering. A single mote of dust holds three dust-motes; a single mind holds three minds; each and every mote of dust holds eighty-four thousand gates of worldly affliction, and each and every mind is exactly the same. Craving, anger, and delusion are themselves already bodhi; affliction itself is already bodhi: this is what is called the truth of accumulation. Turning each and every gate of worldly affliction around, it becomes precisely the eighty-four thousand gates of samādhi, and precisely the eighty-four thousand gates of dhāraṇī, and precisely the eighty-four thousand gates of the antidote, and it likewise accomplishes the eighty-four thousand pāramitās. Ignorance, once turned, transforms directly into illumination — like ice melting into water, nothing brought in from far away, nothing arriving from anywhere else — for a single thought of mind is already, universally, complete with all of it. It is like a wish-fulfilling jewel: neither possessing treasure, nor lacking it. To say it has none is a false statement; to say it has some is a wrong view. It cannot be known by the discriminating mind, nor argued out in words. Sentient beings, in the midst of this inconceivable Dharma that was never bound to begin with, imagine bondage into being through their own thinking; and within a Dharma that was never in need of liberation, they go seeking liberation. For this very reason, great compassion arises, and the four universal vows are raised, to pull beings free of the two sufferings and grant them the two joys — hence this is called "giving rise to the true and correct bodhi-mind, neither bound nor freed."

Reading note: This third pass is the fascicle's clearest statement of what would become one of Tiantai's signature doctrines: "a single thought contains the three thousand [realms]" (一念三千) is not spelled out by that later name here, but its logic is fully present — a single arising thought, empty, provisional, and the middle simultaneously, is already "complete with the whole of the Buddhadharma," already "the eighty-four thousand dharma-treasuries." The bright-mirror simile (brightness for empty, reflected image for provisional, the mirror itself for the middle) gives concrete shape to what "three yet one, one yet three" actually means: a mirror's brightness, its reflected images, and its physical substance are not three separate things you could pull apart, yet they are also not simply identical — exactly the relationship claimed for emptiness, provisionality, and the middle. The warning against clinging to whichever single term a given scriptural passage happens to lead with ("emptiness," "being," "the middle way") — recognizing that each, taken as the leading term, still secretly contains the other two — is Zhiyi's direct answer to the "great net" of apparently conflicting scripture promised back in the five brief points.

Why Everything Together Is Called "Right" 併是 · 一大事因緣

Chinese Source

前三皆約四諦為語，今約法藏、塵勞、三昧、波羅蜜，其義宛然。問：前簡非，併言非；今顯是，何故併言是？答：所言併是者，皆非縛非脫，故言併是，通皆上求故。又次第漸入到實，故言併是。又實難知，借權顯實，故言併是。此三番擬世界悉檀，言併是也。又權不攝實，實則攝權，欲令攝顯易見，故言併是。此一番擬為人悉檀，故言是也。又一菩提心，一切菩提心，若不說者，不知一切，故言併是。此一番擬對治悉檀明是。若究竟而論，前三是約權，後一約實。譬如良醫有一祕方總攝諸方，阿伽陀藥功兼諸藥，如食乳糜更無所須，一切具足，如如意珠。權實顯是，其義可知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The three passes just given all spoke in terms of the Four Noble Truths; this present one speaks in terms of the dharma-treasuries, worldly afflictions, samādhis, and pāramitās — yet the sense is plainly the very same throughout. Question: Earlier, in rejecting what is wrong, every one of the ten was together called wrong; now, in revealing what is right, why is everything together called right? Answer: What is meant by calling everything together "right" is that all of it is alike neither bound nor freed, and so all is called right, since all alike seek enlightenment above. Again, progressing in sequence, gradually entering into what is real, everything together is called right. Again, since the real is hard to know directly, the provisional is borrowed to reveal the real, and so everything together is called right — these three ways of putting it correspond to the worldly siddhānta. Again, the provisional does not contain the real, while the real does contain the provisional; wishing to make this containment easy to see, everything together is called right — this way of putting it corresponds to the siddhānta for the individual. Again, the one bodhi-mind is every bodhi-mind; if this were not stated, the whole would not be known through the one, and so everything together is called right — this way of putting it corresponds to the siddhānta of the antidote. Considered to the very end, the first three passes are stated in terms of the provisional, and the last in terms of the real. It is like a skilled physician possessing a single secret formula that already contains every other formula within it, an agada elixir whose power already includes the power of every other medicine — like eating a bowl of rich milk-gruel, needing nothing further at all, complete and lacking nothing, like the wish-fulfilling jewel itself. The sense of revealing what is right through the provisional and the real together may be understood from this.

又一是者，一大事因緣故。云何為「一」？一實不虛故，一道清淨故，一切無礙人，一道出生死故。云何為「大」？其性廣博多所含容，大智、大斷，大人所乘，大師子吼，大益凡聖，故言為大。「事」者，十方三世佛之儀式，以此自成佛道，以此化度眾生，故名為事。「因緣」者，眾生以此因感佛，佛以此緣起應，故言因緣。又「是」者，不可言三，不可言一，不可言非三非一，而言三一，故名「不可思議是」也。又「是」者，非作法，非佛、非天、人、脩羅所作。常境無相，常智無緣。以無緣智緣無相境；無相之境相無緣之智。智境冥一，而言境智，故名「無作」也。

Again, as for everything together being "one": this is because of the one great cause and condition. What is meant by "one"? Because it is the one reality, not false; because it is the one path, pure in itself; because it is the one path by which every unobstructed person goes beyond birth-and-death. What is meant by "great"? Because its nature is vast and all-containing — great wisdom, great severance, that which great beings ride upon, the great lion's roar, of great benefit to ordinary being and sage alike — hence it is called "great." "Matter" means the very protocol of every buddha of the three times and ten directions, by which they themselves accomplish the buddha-path and by which they transform and bring across sentient beings — hence it is named "matter." "Cause and condition" means that sentient beings, by this cause, move the Buddha, and the Buddha, by this condition, arises in response — hence it is called "cause and condition." Again, as for its being called "right": it cannot be called three, cannot be called one, cannot be called neither three nor one — and yet it is called three, and called one — hence it is named "the inconceivable right." Again, as for its being called right: it is not something made — not made by buddha, deva, human, or asura. Its eternal object has no mark; its eternal wisdom has no object to grasp. Wisdom without an object to grasp takes as its object the object that has no mark; the markless object gives its own mark to the wisdom that has no object to grasp. Wisdom and object merge into one, and yet "object" and "wisdom" are still spoken of — hence this is named "the unconditioned."

又「是」者，如《文殊問經》云：「破一切發，名發菩提心；常隨菩提相，而發菩提心。」又「無發而發，無隨而隨。」又「過一切破，過一切隨，雙照破隨，名發菩提心。」如此三種不一不異，如理如事，如非理非事，故名為是。若例此義，無作、不可思議、一大事因緣等諸法門，皆言破，皆言隨，皆言非破非隨，雙照破隨。又前三是上中下智所觀；後一是上上智所觀。前三是共；後一是不共。前三淺、近、曲；後一深、遠、直云云。前三是小中大；後一是大中大、上中上、圓中圓、滿中滿、實中實、真中真、了義中了義、玄中玄、妙中妙、不可思議中不可思議。若能如此簡非顯是，體權識實而發心者，是一切諸佛種。

Again, as for its being called right: as the Sūtra of Mañjuśrī's Questions says, "breaking through every wrong arising is what is called giving rise to the bodhi-mind; constantly following the mark of bodhi is also giving rise to the bodhi-mind." It also says: "arising without arising, and following without following." It also says: "going beyond every breaking-through, going beyond every following, illuminating both breaking-through and following at once — this is called giving rise to the bodhi-mind." These three are neither one nor different — as principle, as matter, and as neither principle nor matter — hence this is called "right." Following this same pattern, every dharma-gate — the unconditioned, the inconceivable, the one great cause and condition, and the rest — can equally be spoken of as breaking-through, as following, and as neither breaking-through nor following, illuminating both at once. Again, the first three approaches are contemplated by lesser, middling, and higher wisdom; the last is contemplated by the very highest wisdom of all. The first three are shared with other vehicles; the last is unshared. The first three are shallow, near, and roundabout; the last is deep, far-reaching, and direct, and so on. The first three are lesser-within-great; the last is great-within-great, highest-within-highest, perfect-within-perfect, complete-within-complete, real-within-real, true-within-true, definitive-within-definitive, profound-within-profound, wondrous-within-wondrous, inconceivable-within-inconceivable. One who can, in this way, reject what is wrong and reveal what is right, embodying the provisional and recognizing the real, and thereby gives rise to the mind — such a one is the very seed of every buddha there is.

Reading note: "The first three" being ranked below "the last" is a small but important signal: this closes the discussion of giving rise to the mind by way of the Four Noble Truths, the four universal vows, and the empty/provisional/middle analysis of a single thought — three genuine approaches, but still, by the treatise's own account, only "lesser-within-great" compared to what is coming next. "One great cause and condition" (一大事因緣) quietly invokes the Lotus Sūtra's own famous phrase for why a buddha appears in the world at all, folded here into the analysis of what it means for the bodhi-mind itself to be "one." The section closes by naming, without yet explaining, the fourth and final approach still to come — the six identities — which the text has just ranked as alone "unshared," "direct," and "inconceivable-within-inconceivable."

Revealing What Is Right, Fifth and Final Approach: The Six Identities 顯是 · 六即

Chinese Source

約六即顯是者，為初心是，後心是？答：如《論》焦、炷，非初不離初，非後不離後。若智信具足，聞一念即是。信故不謗，智故不懼，初後皆是。若無信，高推聖境，非己智分；若無智，起增上慢，謂己均佛，初後俱非。為此事故，須知六即，謂理即、名字即、觀行即、相似即、分真即、究竟即。此六即者，始凡終聖。始凡故，除疑怯；終聖故，除慢大云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

Revealing what is right by way of the six identities: is it the initial mind that is right, or the final mind? Answer: as with the Treatise's own simile of the wick and the flame — it is not the beginning alone, yet it is never apart from the beginning; it is not the end alone, yet it is never apart from the end. If wisdom and faith are both complete, then on hearing of it, a single thought is already right. Through faith, one does not slander it; through wisdom, one is not afraid of it — beginning and end are both alike right. Without faith, one places the sage's own state too high above oneself, as though it had no part in one's own capacity; without wisdom, arrogance and conceit arise, supposing oneself already equal to the Buddha — beginning and end are then both alike wrong. For this very reason, one must know the six identities: identity in principle, identity in name, identity in contemplative practice, identity in resemblance, identity in a portion of the truth, and identity in the ultimate. These six identities run from the ordinary being at the start to the sage at the end. Because it begins with the ordinary being, doubt and timidity are removed; because it ends with the sage, arrogance and conceit are removed, and so on.

理即者，一念心即如來藏理。「如」故即空，「藏」故即假，「理」故即中；三智一心中具，不可思議。如上說「三諦一諦、非三非一」；一色一香、一切法、一切心亦復如是。是名「理即是菩提心」，亦是「理即止觀」。即寂名「止」；即照名「觀」。名字即者，理雖即是，日用不知，以未聞三諦，全不識佛法，如牛羊眼，不解方隅。或從知識，或從經卷，聞上所說一實菩提，於名字中通達解了，知一切法皆是佛法。是為「名字即菩提」，亦是「名字止觀」。若未聞時，處處馳求。既得聞已，攀覓心息，名「止」；但信法性，不信其諸，名為「觀」。

Identity in principle: a single thought of mind is itself already the principle of the tathāgatagarbha. Because of "thus," it is already empty; because of "treasury," it is already provisional; because of "principle," it is already the middle — the three wisdoms complete within the one mind, inconceivably so. As explained above, "three truths, one truth, neither three nor one"; a single color, a single scent, every dharma, every mind — all are exactly the same. This is called "identity in principle is the bodhi-mind," and also "identity in principle is calming-and-contemplation." Its very stillness is named "calming"; its very illumination is named "contemplation." Identity in name: though principle is already right in this way, one uses it day after day without knowing it, since one has not yet heard of the three truths and does not recognize the Buddhadharma at all — like the eye of an ox or sheep, unable to make out direction. But then, whether from a good spiritual friend or from the sūtras themselves, one hears what has been explained above, the one real bodhi, and comes to understand it within the realm of names, knowing that every dharma whatsoever is itself the Buddhadharma. This is called "identity in name is bodhi," and also "identity in name is calming-and-contemplation." Before one has heard it, one runs about everywhere seeking; once one has heard it, the seeking, grasping mind comes to rest — this is named "calming." Simply believing in the Dharma-nature, without yet believing in anything further beyond it, is named "contemplation."

觀行即是者，若但聞名口說，如蟲食木，偶得成字，是蟲不知是字、非字，既不通達，寧是菩提？必須心觀明了，理慧相應，所行如所言，所言如所行。《華首》云：「言說多不行，我不以言說，但心行菩提。」此心口相應，是觀行菩提。《釋論》四句評聞慧具足，如眼得日，照了無僻。觀行亦如是，雖未契理，觀心不息。如《首楞嚴》中「射的喻」。是名「觀行菩提」，亦名「觀行止觀」。恒作此想名「觀」；餘想息名「止」云云。相似即是菩提者，以其逾觀逾明，逾止逾寂，如勤射隣的，名「相似觀慧」。一切世間治生產業不相違背，所有思想籌量，皆是先佛經中所說，如六根清淨中說。圓伏無明名「止」；似中道慧名「觀」云云。

Identity in contemplative practice: if one has only heard the name and speaks it with the mouth, like an insect gnawing at wood that happens to form the shape of a written character — the insect itself does not know whether this is a character or not — then, without real penetration, how could this be bodhi? One must bring the mind to contemplate clearly, principle and wisdom corresponding to each other, so that one's practice matches one's own words, and one's words match one's own practice. The Sūtra of the Head Grove says: "Much speech without practice is not what I mean — I mean the mind itself practicing bodhi." Where mind and mouth correspond in this way, this is bodhi at the level of contemplative practice. The Treatise's own fourfold assessment says that when learning and wisdom are both complete, it is like the eye receiving sunlight, illuminating everything without error. Contemplative practice is exactly the same: though principle has not yet been directly met, the contemplating mind does not cease — as in the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's simile of the archer aiming at a target. This is called "bodhi at the level of contemplative practice," also named "calming-and-contemplation at the level of contemplative practice." Constantly holding to this contemplation is named "contemplation"; the ceasing of every other thought is named "calming," and so on. Identity in resemblance is bodhi: as one's contemplation grows ever clearer, and one's calming grows ever more still, like an archer's practiced aim drawing ever nearer the target's own center — this is named "wisdom of contemplation in resemblance." Every worldly occupation and productive undertaking no longer runs contrary to the Dharma; every thought and deliberation is already exactly what the Buddha's own sūtras have taught, as explained in the passage on the purification of the six faculties. Perfectly subduing ignorance, without yet severing it, is named "calming"; a wisdom resembling the middle way is named "contemplation," and so on.

分真即者，因相似觀力入銅輪位，初破無明，見佛性，開寶藏，顯真如，名「發心住」；乃至「等覺」，無明微薄，智慧轉著，如從初日至十四日，月光垂圓，闇垂盡。若人應以佛身得度者，即八相成道；應以九法界身得度者，以普門示現，如經廣說。是名「分真菩提」，亦名「分真止觀」、「分真智斷」。究竟即菩提者，等覺一轉，入於妙覺，智光圓滿不復可增，名「菩提果」。大涅槃斷，更無可斷，名「果果」。等覺不通，唯佛能通，過茶無道可說，故名「究竟菩提」，亦名「究竟止觀」。總以譬譬之，譬如貧人，家有寶藏而無知者，知識示之，即得知也；耘除草穢而掘出之，漸漸得近；近已藏開，盡取用之。合六喻可解云云。

Identity in a portion of the truth: through the power of resemblance-level contemplation, one enters the stage of the copper wheel — first breaking through ignorance, seeing the buddha-nature, opening the treasure-store, and manifesting suchness itself — this is named "the abiding of the arising resolve"; and onward through "virtual enlightenment," ignorance growing ever thinner, wisdom growing ever more manifest, like the moon from its first day to its fourteenth, its light approaching fullness as the darkness approaches its end. Should someone be liberated by means of a buddha's own body, the eight aspects of accomplishing the path are shown; should someone be liberated by means of a body from any of the other nine dharma-realms, the universal gate manifests accordingly, as the sūtra explains at length. This is called "bodhi as a portion of the truth," also named "calming-and-contemplation as a portion of the truth," "wisdom and severance as a portion of the truth." Identity in the ultimate is bodhi: with a single further turning beyond virtual enlightenment, one enters wondrous enlightenment — the light of wisdom perfectly full, with nothing left to increase — this is named "the fruit of bodhi." The great severance of nirvāṇa has nothing further left to sever — this is named "the fruit of the fruit." Virtual enlightenment does not yet pass through to this; only the Buddha can pass through to it — beyond the very last syllable, there is no further path left to speak of. Hence this is named "ultimate bodhi," also "ultimate calming-and-contemplation." To sum it all up in a single further simile: it is like a poor person whose own house already holds a hidden treasure, though they do not know it — a friend points it out, and only then do they come to know of it; weeding away the undergrowth, they dig it out, drawing gradually nearer; once near, the treasure-store opens, and they take from it and use it without limit. Taking these six similes together, the whole may be understood, and so on.

Reading note: The six identities became, alongside the fourfold Four Noble Truths already explained in this fascicle, one of Tiantai's most quoted teaching devices, precisely because it resolves the tension raised at the very top of this section: is any given moment of practice already "it," or not yet? Both, in a graduated sense — hence "identity" (即) paired with six distinct stages, rather than a flat yes/no. Each of the six recurs constantly through this site's other Zhiyi translations; watch especially for the progression from a truth already fully present but entirely unrecognized (identity in principle — true of every being, right now, including the reader), through hearing about it and merely repeating the words (identity in name — the "insect gnawing at wood" that happens to trace the shape of a character, without understanding what a character is), through actually practicing with it, then a resemblance to real realization convincing enough that "worldly occupation no longer runs contrary to the Dharma," then a first genuine breakthrough, and only at the very end, complete buddhahood itself. On 荼 ("the very last syllable"): this is the final letter of the Siddhaṃ syllabary used in Chinese Buddhist exegesis as a symbol for the utmost limit, beyond which nothing further can be said — the same image, in fact, that this fascicle's own opening line quietly invoked in describing the treatise's own destination "marked out from the start."

Comparison with the Five Kinds of Bodhi 五菩提

Chinese Source

問：《釋論》五菩提意云何？答：《論》竪判別位，今竪判圓位。會之，「發心」對「名字」；「伏心」對「觀行」；「明心」對「相似」；「出到」對「分真」；「無上」對「究竟」。又用彼名，名「圓位」，「發心」是「十住」；「伏心」是「十行」。問：住已斷，行云何伏？答：此用真道伏。例如小乘破見名「斷」；思惟名「伏」。「明心」是「十迴向」；「出到」是「十地」；「無上」是「妙覺」。又從十住具五菩提，乃至妙覺究竟五菩提。故《地義》云：「從初一地具諸地功德。」即其義也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: What is meant by the Treatise's own five kinds of bodhi? Answer: The Treatise ranks them vertically according to the distinct teaching's own stages; here they are ranked vertically according to the perfect teaching's own stages instead. Matched together: "the bodhi of the arising resolve" corresponds to identity in name; "the bodhi of the subdued mind" corresponds to identity in contemplative practice; "the bodhi of the illuminated mind" corresponds to identity in resemblance; "the bodhi of emergence and arrival" corresponds to identity in a portion of the truth; "the unsurpassed bodhi" corresponds to identity in the ultimate. Using those same names again for the perfect teaching's own stages: "the arising resolve" is the ten abidings; "the subdued mind" is the ten practices. Question: If affliction is already severed at the abidings, how can it still be spoken of as merely "subdued" at the practices? Answer: This "subduing" is carried out by means of the true path itself. It is exactly as in the Hīnayāna, where breaking through views is called "severing," and dealing with what is cultivated through sustained practice is called "subduing." "The illuminated mind" is the ten dedications; "emergence and arrival" is the ten grounds; "the unsurpassed" is wondrous enlightenment. Moreover, from the ten abidings onward, each stage is already complete with all five kinds of bodhi, all the way up to wondrous enlightenment's own ultimate five kinds of bodhi. Hence the Treatise on the Grounds says: "From the very first ground onward, the merits of every ground are already complete within it" — this is exactly that same sense.

問：何意約圓說六即？答：圓觀諸法，皆云「六即」。故以圓意約一切法，悉用六即判位。餘不爾，故不用之。當其教用之，胡為不得？而淺近非教正意也。

Question: Why is it specifically in terms of the perfect teaching that the six identities are explained? Answer: The perfect teaching's own contemplation of every dharma is expressed, throughout, in terms of "the six identities." Hence, taking the perfect teaching's own intent as the standard for every dharma, the six identities are used everywhere to rank its stages. The other teachings do not work this way, and so this scheme is not used for them. Within their own teaching, could it not still be applied there too? It could — yet doing so shallowly and superficially is not that teaching's own proper intent.

Reading note: This closing exchange folds the six identities back into an existing, older classification — the Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra's own five kinds of bodhi — while insisting on a real difference: the Treatise ranks them along the distinct teaching's stages, while Zhiyi re-ranks the very same five names along the perfect teaching's own stages instead, mapping them onto the bodhisattva's ten abidings, ten practices, ten dedications, and ten grounds. The final answer — that the six identities happen to be explained here specifically in terms of the perfect teaching, though nothing stops another teaching from using the same scheme less exactingly — is a small, characteristic moment of Zhiyi qualifying his own system's claims rather than overstating them.

Closing Topic One 結成

Chinese Source

然上來簡非，先約苦諦，升沈世間簡耳；次約四諦，智曲拙淺近簡耳；次約四弘行願；次約六即位，展轉深細，方乃顯是。故知明月神珠，在九重淵內驪龍頷下，有志、有德，方乃致之。豈如世人，麁淺浮虛，競執瓦、石、草、木，妄謂為寶。末學膚受，太無所知！

Ru-Yi Translation

As for everything rejected as wrong throughout what has come before: first, taken in terms of the truth of suffering, only the rising and sinking of the mundane world was rejected; next, taken in terms of the Four Noble Truths, only crooked, clumsy, and shallow wisdom was rejected; next, taken in terms of the four vows and their conduct; and next, taken in terms of the stages of the six identities, turning ever deeper and finer — only then is what is right finally, fully revealed. Hence it is known: the bright moon-pearl lies in the ninth-deep abyss, beneath the black dragon's very chin — only one of true resolve and true virtue can ever retrieve it. How unlike the ordinary person of the world, coarse, shallow, and hollow, who competes to seize on tile, stone, grass, and wood, falsely taking them for treasure! Those of shallow learning, skimming only the surface, understand nothing at all!

Reading note: This is the closing line of fascicle 1 as CBETA's text has it — a deliberately sharp final note, and worth pausing on rather than reading past. The image of a pearl beneath a black dragon's chin, in the deepest abyss, comes from the Zhuangzi: only a diver of real nerve dares retrieve it, and only while the dragon sleeps. Zhiyi's application of it here retroactively reframes the entire, exhausting apparatus of this fascicle — the twenty-four patriarchs, the three kinds of calming-and-contemplation, the fourfold Four Noble Truths worked four separate ways, the three passes through empty/provisional/middle, the six identities — as itself the depth required to reach something genuinely precious, in contrast to those who settle for "tile, stone, grass, and wood" mistaken for treasure: teachings grasped only at their most superficial, literal level. It is a fitting note to end fascicle 1's account of giving rise to the bodhi-mind on: the entire point of insisting on this much depth and this much rigor was never rigor for its own sake, but making sure the reader does not mistake something cheap for what the fascicle's own opening line called, at the very start, unprecedented.
