Fascicle 9 · 摩訶止觀卷第九

The Object of Meditative States

This page continues Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Mohe Zhiguan (摩訶止觀, "The Great Calming and Contemplation"). Fascicle 8 moved from the aggregates through four further objects of contemplation in compressed form. Fascicle 9 is devoted entirely to a single object, the sixth, meditative states (禪定境), and treats it at very great length: ten kinds of dhyana are distinguished, the causes and conditions of their arising explained, and the manifestation-marks of each described in exhaustive practical detail, before the ten modes of contemplation are finally applied to the object as a whole. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Status: fascicle 9 in progress Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Source: Taishō T46, no. 1911 Covers: topic seven, continued — object six of the ten objects of contemplation

Continuing from Fascicle 8

Fascicle 8 moved past the first object of contemplation (the aggregates, entries, and elements, completed across fascicles 5–7) to treat four further objects — affliction, illness, the marks of karma, and demonic affairs — each in compressed form. Fascicle 9 (again combining CBETA's upper and lower halves into a single continuous page, per this site's existing convention) is devoted in its entirety to the sixth object, meditative states (禪定境). Unlike fascicle 8's four compressed objects, this one object receives an extended, four-part treatment of its own: first, distinguishing and classifying ten kinds of dhyana; second, the causes and conditions of their arising; third, the actual manifestation-marks of each, described in very great practical detail — including the physical "eight sensations," the nine contemplations of a corpse's decay, the eight liberations, and an extended treatment of the contemplation on dependent origination that itself runs through all ten modes of contemplation in miniature; and fourth, the final application of the ten modes of contemplation to meditative states as a whole. The Sixth Object: Contemplating Meditative States 第六、觀禪定境

Chinese Source

第六、觀禪定境者：夫長病、遠行是禪定障。《立世阿毘曇》云：「多諫諍、多營事亦是禪定障；復有多讀誦亦是禪定障。」《文殊問菩提經》云：「禪定有三十六垢。」垢即是障。上諸境得入，到清涼池；入流竟，則不須觀禪境。若魔事雖過而真明未發，雖無別修，以通修故，發過去習，諸禪紛現，當置魔事觀於諸禪。所以者何？禪樂美妙，喜生耽味，垢膩日增。若謂是道，墮增上慢；若呵棄者，全失方便。如此等過不可具記。雖免魔害，更為定縛；如避火墮水，無益三昧。為是義故，須觀禪境。但禪支諸定，助道有力，大小乘經皆共稱美。若四禪八定，《毘曇》、《成實》明之委細；自性九禪，《地持》、《十地》甚為分明。今亦略示其發相，粗為四意：一、明開合，二、發因緣，三、明發相，四、修止觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sixth, contemplating the object of meditative states: prolonged illness and distant travel are both obstacles to meditative concentration. The *Lokasthiti-abhidharma* says: "Frequent contention, frequent involvement in worldly affairs, are likewise obstacles to meditative concentration; excessive reading and recitation, too, is an obstacle to meditative concentration." The *Sūtra of Mañjuśrī's Questions on Bodhi* says: "Meditative concentration has thirty-six defilements" — defilement is precisely obstruction. Once one has gained entry through the objects treated above, one arrives at the pool of coolness; once one has entered the very stream, there ought to be no further need to contemplate the object of meditative states at all. Yet should demonic affairs have already passed, while genuine clarity has not yet arisen, then, though no separate cultivation of meditative states is undertaken, the general cultivation already underway stirs up habituation accumulated in the past, and every manner of meditative state arises in confused profusion — at just this point one ought to set aside the contemplation of demonic affairs and take up the contemplation of these meditative states instead. Why is this so? The bliss of meditative absorption is so exquisite that delight readily gives rise to indulgent attachment, and this very defilement grows daily thicker. Should one take it for the path itself, one falls into overweening pride; should one instead rebuke and cast it entirely aside, one loses every skillful means it might have offered. Faults of this kind cannot be recorded in full. Though one may thereby escape the harm of demons, one becomes bound instead by meditative concentration itself — like fleeing fire only to fall into water, gaining nothing at all from such a samādhi. For this reason, one must contemplate the object of meditative states. The branch-factors and various concentrations belonging to dhyāna are of great assistance to the path, and both the greater and lesser vehicles' scriptures alike praise them without exception. As for the four dhyānas and the eight concentrations, the *Abhidharma* and the *Satyasiddhi-śāstra* explain them in exhaustive detail; as for the nine dhyānas of inherent nature, the *Bodhisattva-bhūmi* and the *Daśabhūmika* treat them with great clarity. Here, too, their manifestation-marks shall be briefly indicated, treated roughly in four parts: first, clarifying their opening and closing classification; second, the causes and conditions of their arising; third, clarifying their manifestation-marks; fourth, cultivating calming-and-contemplation.

Ten Gates of Dhyāna, and Their Five- and Fifteen-Fold Groupings 一明開合

Chinese Source

初、明開合者，禪門無量且約十門：一、根本四禪，二、十六特勝，三、通明，四、九想，五、八背捨，六、大不淨，七、慈心，八、因緣，九、念佛，十、神通。此十門與五門、十五門，云何同異？但有開合之異耳。開五為十者：開數息出特勝、通明；開不淨出背捨、大不淨；慈心、因緣守本；念佛門，《毘曇》名界方便，《禪經》稱念佛，此亦守本；神通約九禪上發，不專據一法。合十五門為十者：數息、不淨各有三，則不合。慈心有三，但合為一，即眾生慈也。沒二名者，禪是門戶詮次事法，法緣是二乘入理觀，無緣是大乘入理觀，沒理去二，存事唯一。若開者，即屬二乘、菩薩兩境中攝。因緣亦三門，三世輪轉麁，果報、一念明義細，細故附理，麁故屬事；今沒細存麁，但稱三世門也。念佛亦三，但取念應佛耳。神通但取五通。若但取五門，有所不收；若取十五，義濫於理，是故簡理開事。雖開合不同，各有其意。次、明漏、無漏：若依《毘曇》判此十禪，皆名有漏；緣諦智修，名無漏禪。不爾，但緣事修，名有漏禪。《成論》亦爾，根本等是有漏；空無相心修，名無漏。今小異彼，當十禪體相是有漏，通是事禪。若胡瓜能為熱病作因緣者，小當分別。四禪，世間本有，凡夫、外道共；專修此者祇發有漏。自行，十二門；化他，讚法讚者。《大經》云「所謂四十八年」，即此意也。十六特勝、通明，佛不出世，利根凡夫亦修此禪，而不發無漏；如來若說，亦發無漏。比於餘禪，其力雖弱，交勝根本。為是義故稱「亦有漏亦無漏」。九想等是出世客法，雖是事法，能防欲過；不俟諦智，能發無漏。如迦絺那五百羅漢，人人七遍為說四諦不能悟道，佛說不淨即發無漏。厭患力強，故判屬無漏。若言非無漏者，不應稱為聖戒、定、慧。聖之言正，正豈過無漏？《大經》云：「聖行者，諸佛境界，非二乘所知……佛說此法，二乘奉行，故名聖行。」今佛說聖法，二乘行之，何得非無漏？又《大品》云：「根本是世間法施；不淨等是出世法施。」既言出世，豈非無漏？又云：「九想開不淨；不淨開身念處；身念處開三念處；三念處開三十七品；三十七品開涅槃。」涅槃初緣豈非無漏？若言事禪應是有漏者，譬服二石，一熱一冷，雖同事禪，應漏、無漏異。若無漏緣稱無漏者，六地斷見、七地斷思，此亦是緣，亦應無漏。六地、七地斷見思者，終不單用根本，會須諦智，寄此位發。單用根本，非無漏緣。不淨等不爾，直以不淨能為作緣云云。所以不取十想者，前三見諦、中四思惟、後三無學，皆屬理攝，故不取。不取八念者，有人修九想無怖，又念佛門已攝，故不取。慈心觀兩屬，若依根本起慈，屬有漏；若依不淨等起慈，屬無漏。慈無地位，約地階級，依根本成眾生緣；依背捨成法緣。因緣亦無他位，念佛、五通皆約他階級，例如慈心兩屬云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, clarifying opening and closing classification: the gates of dhyāna are without measure, but here they shall be treated in terms of ten gates: first, the four root dhyānas; second, the sixteen special excellences; third, the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna; fourth, the nine contemplations; fifth, the eight liberations; sixth, the great impurity contemplation; seventh, loving-kindness; eighth, dependent origination; ninth, buddha-recollection; tenth, spiritual powers. How do these ten gates relate to a fivefold and a fifteenfold grouping — are they the same or different? They differ only in how they are opened apart or closed together. As for opening five into ten: the counting of the breath opens out into the special excellences and the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna; impurity opens out into the eight liberations and the great impurity contemplation; loving-kindness and dependent origination each keep to their own root category; the buddha-recollection gate — named "the expedient means of the realm" in the *Abhidharma*, and "buddha-recollection" in the *Dhyāna Sūtra* — likewise keeps to its own root category; spiritual powers arise upon the foundation of the nine dhyānas taken together, and are not confined to any single method alone. As for closing fifteen into ten: counting the breath and impurity each have three subdivisions, which are not further combined; loving-kindness has three subdivisions, but these are combined into one, namely loving-kindness toward sentient beings. As for the two names dropped: dhyāna is the gateway explaining the sequence of concrete practice; conditioned loving-kindness is the two vehicles' entry into principle-contemplation; unconditioned loving-kindness is the Mahāyāna's entry into principle-contemplation — dropping the principle-aspect and removing these two, only the concrete-practice aspect remains as a single category. Were one to open them apart instead, they would belong, respectively, within the objects of the two vehicles and of the bodhisattva. Dependent origination likewise has three gates: the coarse turning through the three time-periods, and the fine clarification of retributive result and of a single thought-moment — the fine being fine because it adjoins principle, the coarse being coarse because it belongs to concrete practice; here the fine is dropped and the coarse retained, so it is simply called "the three-time-period gate." Buddha-recollection likewise has three, but here only recollection of the response-body Buddha is taken up. Spiritual powers here take up only the five mundane powers. Were one to take up only the fivefold grouping, something would be left uncollected; were one to take up the full fifteen, the sense would overflow into principle — hence principle is set aside and concrete practice alone is opened out. Though the opening and closing differ, each has its own proper sense. Next, clarifying the contaminated and the uncontaminated: according to the *Abhidharma*'s judgment, all ten of these dhyānas are named "contaminated"; cultivation taken up with the wisdom of the truths is named "uncontaminated dhyāna" — otherwise, cultivation taken up with mere concrete practice is named "contaminated dhyāna." The *Satyasiddhi* holds likewise: the root dhyānas and the rest are contaminated; cultivation with the mind of emptiness and marklessness is named uncontaminated. Here the position differs slightly: the very substance and marks of these ten dhyānas are themselves contaminated, being uniformly dhyānas of concrete practice — though, should a bitter cucumber prove capable of serving as a cause and condition for feverish illness, some slight distinction must then be drawn. The four dhyānas exist inherently in the world, shared alike by ordinary beings and non-Buddhist practitioners; one who cultivates them exclusively produces only the contaminated. Practiced for oneself, they comprise the twelve gates; used to convert others, they are praised by both the teaching and the one who teaches it. The Great Sūtra's phrase "the so-called forty-eight years" carries precisely this same sense. The sixteen special excellences and the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna may be cultivated even by sharp-faculty ordinary beings in an age when no buddha has appeared in the world, though without producing the uncontaminated; should the Tathāgata himself expound them, they do produce the uncontaminated. Compared to the other dhyānas, their power, though weaker, still exceeds that of the root dhyānas — for this reason they are called "both contaminated and uncontaminated." The nine contemplations and the rest are supramundane guest-dharmas: though themselves concrete practices, they are able to guard against the fault of desire, and, without needing to wait upon the wisdom of the truths, are able to produce the uncontaminated. As when the five hundred arhats under Kāṭyāyana, each taught the Four Noble Truths seven times over, were unable to awaken to the path, yet the Buddha's teaching of impurity produced the uncontaminated at once — the power of world-weariness being so strong, they are judged to belong to the uncontaminated. Were they said not to be uncontaminated, they could not rightly be called noble precepts, noble concentration, and noble wisdom — "noble" (聖) means "correct" (正), and how could correctness ever exceed the uncontaminated? The Great Sūtra says: "Noble conduct is the very domain of the buddhas, not something the two lesser vehicles can know... yet the Buddha taught this very dharma, and the two lesser vehicles undertook it in practice, hence it is named noble conduct." Since the Buddha here taught a noble dharma, and the two lesser vehicles practiced it, how could it fail to be uncontaminated? Further, the *Larger Prajñāpāramitā* says: "The root dhyānas are mundane dharma-giving; impurity and the rest are supramundane dharma-giving." Since it is called supramundane, how could it fail to be uncontaminated? It further says: "The nine contemplations open out into impurity; impurity opens out into the foundation of mindfulness of the body; the foundation of mindfulness of the body opens out into the three foundations of mindfulness; the three foundations of mindfulness open out into the thirty-seven factors; the thirty-seven factors open out into nirvāṇa." Could this very first condition leading to nirvāṇa possibly fail to be uncontaminated? Were one to insist that concrete-practice dhyāna must be contaminated, this would be like two kinds of medicinal stone, one heating and one cooling — though both alike belong to concrete-practice cures, contaminated and uncontaminated must still differ between them. Were cultivation taken up with an uncontaminated object called uncontaminated, then the sixth ground's severing of views and the seventh ground's severing of habituated thought, these too being objects taken up, ought likewise to be uncontaminated — yet those who sever views and habituated thought at the sixth and seventh grounds never employ the root dhyānas alone, but must necessarily draw upon the wisdom of the truths, this arising precisely at that very stage; employing the root dhyānas alone is not an uncontaminated object. Impurity and the rest are not so — impurity itself, directly, is able to serve as the very condition, and so on. As for why the ten contemplations are not taken up here: the first three pertain to the truths as directly seen, the middle four to habituated reflection, and the last three to the stage beyond further learning — all belonging to principle, and hence not taken up here. As for why the eight recollections are not taken up: some cultivate the nine contemplations without dread already, and, further, the buddha-recollection gate already gathers this in, hence they are not taken up separately. The contemplation of loving-kindness belongs to both categories at once: cultivated on the foundation of the root dhyānas, loving-kindness belongs to the contaminated; cultivated on the foundation of impurity and the rest, it belongs to the uncontaminated. Loving-kindness itself has no fixed stage, but, in terms of gradations by stage, loving-kindness founded on the root dhyānas becomes loving-kindness toward sentient beings, while loving-kindness founded on the liberations becomes loving-kindness founded on the Dharma. Dependent origination likewise has no separate stage of its own; buddha-recollection and the five spiritual powers are likewise graded according to another's stage, exactly parallel to loving-kindness's belonging to both categories at once, and so on.

How This Differs from the Sequential Dharma-Gates and the Antidotes, and the Order of Depth 來意不同 · 明深淺不同

Chinese Source

次、來意不同。問：此中十門與《次第禪門》及對治，云何同異？答：《次第禪門》為成禪波羅蜜，禪善根利，故禪門先發，後驗善惡；此中為成般若，禪善根鈍，先阻煩惱，遇業遭魔，後始發禪。對治中，為破遮障，修成助道；今此任運自發，仍為觀境。禪門雖同，各有其意云云。次、明深淺不同者：四禪是根本、闇證、味禪，凡聖通共，薄修即得。特勝少有觀慧，不味、不闇證，橫對念處，竪對根本，故先味、次淨也。通明觀慧，證相深細，細次於總也。此三同是根本實觀，治惑力弱。九想正是假想初門，前鋒伏欲，故次列也。九想但厭患外境，未治其心，故次八背捨也。背捨雖破內外貪欲，總而未別，緣中不得自在，故次明大不淨，破依正貪也。雖總別治貪，未修大福德，故次慈心。雖復內治重貪，外修福德，不入因緣則非世間正見，故次因緣；三世輪轉無主、無我，成世正見也。雖世正見，緣底下因人，福力微弱，次緣上果，福力廣大。雖前來諸定，未有力用轉變自在，故次神通云云。次、互發不同：其次第互發，凡有八種，例陰界境可知云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, how the reason for treating them here differs from elsewhere. Question: how do these ten gates here relate to the *Sequential Dharma-Gates of Dhyāna* and to the antidotes treated in the preliminary expedients — are they the same or different? Answer: the *Sequential Dharma-Gates* aims at accomplishing the pāramitā of dhyāna, where the wholesome root of dhyāna is itself sharp, so the gates of dhyāna arise first, and good and evil are only tested afterward; here, by contrast, the aim is accomplishing prajñā, where the wholesome root of dhyāna is dull, so that affliction obstructs first, one encounters karma and meets with Māra, and only afterward does dhyāna finally arise. Within the antidotes of the preliminary expedients, the aim is breaking through obstruction so as to cultivate an accomplished auxiliary to the path; here, by contrast, these arise of their own accord, spontaneously, and are then themselves taken up as the object of contemplation. Though the gates of dhyāna are the same in each case, each context carries its own proper sense, and so on. Next, clarifying the differing order of depth: the four dhyānas are the root, darkly realized, savored dhyānas, shared in common by ordinary beings and sages alike, obtainable with only slight cultivation. The special excellences possess a small measure of contemplative wisdom, neither savored nor darkly realized, matched horizontally to the foundations of mindfulness and vertically to the root dhyānas — hence the savored is treated first and the pure afterward. The penetrating-and-clear dhyāna's own contemplative wisdom realizes its object with still greater depth and subtlety, this subtlety following upon the more general treatment before it. These three are alike root, genuine contemplations, whose power to overcome affliction remains weak. The nine contemplations are precisely the first gate of visualized practice, a vanguard subduing desire — hence they are listed next. The nine contemplations merely produce world-weariness toward the outer object, without yet treating the mind itself — hence the eight liberations follow next. Though the liberations break through desire both inward and outward, they do so only in general, not yet in particular detail, and freedom is not yet attained with respect to the object — hence the great impurity contemplation is clarified next, breaking through attachment to both direct and dependent retribution. Though desire is treated both generally and particularly by this point, great merit has not yet been cultivated — hence loving-kindness follows next. Though heavy desire is treated inwardly and merit cultivated outwardly by this point, without entering dependent origination this would fall short of correct mundane view — hence dependent origination follows next, the turning of the three time-periods without master or self accomplishing correct mundane view. Though correct mundane view is thereby attained, its object remains the lowly causal person, whose power of merit is slight — hence the higher fruit is taken as object next, whose power of merit is vast and expansive. Though every meditative state treated up to this point exists, none yet possesses the power to transform and transmute at will — hence spiritual powers follow next, and so on. Next, how their mutual triggering differs: their sequential mutual triggering comes, in all, to eight distinct kinds, understood by the same pattern as in the object of the aggregates, entries, and elements, and so on.

Causes and Conditions for the Arising of Dhyāna 二明發禪因緣

Chinese Source

○二、明發禪因緣者，《大經》云：「一切眾生皆有初地味禪；若修、不修，必定當得。」近情而望，劫盡不修；久遠推之，亦曾離蓋。譬以誦經，廢近則易習，廢久則難習。當知昔有次第習即次第發，乃至事修事發等云云。如彼大地，種類具足，得雨潤氣，各各開生。生亦前後，結果不俱，梅四、桃七、梨九、柿十，雨緣雖同，成實有異。宿習如種，止觀如雨，禪發如果熟參差，總言八種耳。是名「內因緣發」也。又雖有應生之善，必假威神方乃開發。地雖有種，非日不芽；佛無憎愛，隨緣普益。若次第緣即次第加，乃至事修緣即事加。鴻鍾任擊，巨細由桴。加常平等，淺深聽習。《大論》云：「池華不得日，翳死無疑；善不被加，沈溺未顯。」《淨度經》云：「眾生自度耳，佛於其無益。淨度菩薩言：『眾生若不聞佛十二部經，云何得度？』」二言相乖，共成一意，是名「外緣發」也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the causes and conditions for the arising of dhyāna: the Great Sūtra says: "Every sentient being, without exception, already possesses the savored dhyāna of the first ground — whether cultivated or not, it will certainly, eventually, be attained." Looking at this from the standpoint of one's immediate, ordinary feeling, it seems that one has never cultivated it even to the very end of an eon; but tracing it back across the far, distant past, one has indeed, at some point, already left the hindrances behind. It is like reciting a sūtra: set aside only recently, it is easily taken up again; set aside for a very long time, it becomes difficult to take up again. One should understand that whatever was cultivated sequentially in the past will likewise arise sequentially now, extending even to concrete practice cultivated and concrete practice arising, and so on. It is like the great earth, complete with every kind of seed: receiving the moistening vapor of rain, each seed opens and puts forth growth in its own way. This growth, too, occurs at different times, and the bearing of fruit is not simultaneous — the plum in four months, the peach in seven, the pear in nine, the persimmon in ten — the condition of rain being the same for all, yet the ripening of the fruit differs among them. Habituation accumulated in past lives is like the seed; calming-and-contemplation is like the rain; the arising of dhyāna, ripening at different paces like these fruits, comes, in sum, to the eight kinds already mentioned. This is named "arising from internal causes and conditions." Further, though there is present a wholesome factor bound to arise, it must still borrow the majestic spiritual power of the Buddha before it can actually open forth and manifest — the earth may possess the seed, but without sunlight it will not sprout; the Buddha holds neither aversion nor favoritism, but universally benefits all beings according to their conditions. Should the condition be sequential, the Buddha's empowerment is sequential in turn; extending even to concrete-practice conditions, the empowerment, too, is concrete in turn. A great bell responds however it is struck, whether with a large or a small mallet; the Buddha's empowerment is likewise constant and impartial, while the shallowness or depth of its effect depends on how it is received in practice. The Great Treatise says: "A lotus in a pond that receives no sunlight is certain, beyond doubt, to wither and die; a wholesome factor that receives no empowerment remains submerged and fails to manifest." The *Sūtra on Deliverance through Purity* says: "Sentient beings deliver themselves alone — the Buddha is of no benefit to them in this." Third, clarifying the manifestation-marks of the various dhyānas: in the constantly-walking samādhi, the root dhyānas arise only rarely; in the constantly-sitting samādhi and the rest, they arise more often. Here they shall be treated chiefly in terms of sitting practice. Should the body be upright, the mind gathered in, and the breath harmonized, and one perceives this very path of mind grow dissolved and tranquil, calm and secure, entering by degrees, the mind remaining upon its object without scattering into distraction — this is named "coarse abiding." Following this state of mind, a further calm surpassing what came before is named "fine abiding." Between these two states, before and after, there necessarily occurs a certain method by which the body is sustained. When this method arises, the body naturally becomes upright and straight, free of fatigue and pain, as though something were supporting and assisting the body's own strength. Should the sustaining be faulty, it arrives tense, taut, and painful, and departs slack, loose, and exhausting — this is a coarse and faulty method of sustaining. Should the method of sustaining be good, it sustains both coarse and fine abiding without any excess of slackness or tension. Whether after an hour or two, a day or two, or a month or two, one gradually perceives a deepening subtlety, and the very ground of the mind suddenly opens into a measure of clarity — the body becomes as though cloud or shadow, gleaming and luminously pure, in accord with the dharma of concentration, the mind held unmoving, the whole being purified and cleansed, refreshingly cool and clear. Following further into emptiness and purity, while still perceiving the marks of body and mind, without yet possessing the accompanying meritorious qualities — this is named "the desire-realm concentration." The *Satyasiddhi* names this "the mind in accord with the ten wholesome deeds," flickering and unsteady, not fit to be dwelt in for long. Here it is said that the desire-realm concentration is embryonic, weak, and unstable, and is therefore called "flickering" — it is not a fixed concentration like a steady lamp-flame. It is also called "lightning-flash," of which that treatise says: "Setting aside these seven dependent stages, is there any further concentration able to produce the uncontaminated? Answer: there is a desire-realm concentration able to produce the uncontaminated — the uncontaminated, once produced, arises with the suddenness of a lightning-flash; should it fail to produce the uncontaminated, it may instead persist for a long duration." The *Bequeathed Teaching Sūtra* says: "Should one glimpse the lightning-flash, one may momentarily behold the path." As when Ānanda, striving with his mind yet failing to attain it, relaxed his effort and reached for his pillow, and at that very instant entered the lightning-flash — the lightning-flash being itself the vajra. The vajra does not stand alone: through the desire-realm concentration one enters the uncontaminated, the uncontaminated arising with such suddenness that it is likened to a lightning-flash — it is not the desire-realm concentration itself that bears this name. Abiding in the desire-realm concentration, whether for a year or a month, the dharma of concentration sustains the mind without either slackness or pain, and one may remain within it continuously for days on end without emerging. Following this state of mind, a further, sudden transformation dissolves it entirely: one no longer perceives, within the desire-realm concentration, the head, the body, the clothing, or the bed, as though empty space itself, gleaming and utterly secure. The body is itself an obstruction of concrete matter; this obstruction of concrete matter has not yet come; once the obstruction departs and the body itself becomes empty, what has not yet come is then able to arise — this is named "the mark of the stage of approach." One without proper understanding, attaining this concentration, supposes it to be the very forbearance of the unborn — yet the obstruction of one's own nature still remains, and one has not yet even entered the first dhyāna; how, then, could one possibly, in error, claim it to be the concentration of the unborn? It is like ashes covering a fire, which a fool treads upon carelessly, unaware. According to the *Satyasiddhi*, there is no "future dhyāna" as such, hence it says: "What you call the future dhyāna — is this not simply my own desire-realm concentration?" The *Abhidharma*, by contrast, does affirm it — the Venerable Ghoṣaka and the *Great Treatise* both set it forth in full. The Buddha himself gave both explanations, and it is only the authors of these treatises who each emphasize one side alone. Here, then, the matter is judged according to the individual practitioner: some, having attained the desire-realm concentration, dwell for months in the stage of approach before soon entering the first dhyāna — such a person is properly called to be in "the desire realm," not yet "the stage of approach." Others dwell only briefly in the desire realm before passing into the stage of approach, there remaining for ten days or more — such a person is properly said to be in "the stage of approach," not "the desire realm." Still others remain for a long time within both states at once, and are properly said to hold both concentrations — this cannot be judged according to one side alone. Here, following the *Great Treatise*, both are set forth in full. As for the fine, point-by-point distinction between deviant and correct marks at each stage, this is explained in full detail within the *Sequential Dharma-Gates*' own cultivation-and-verification manual. But since the first dhyāna lies close to the desire realm, like a border region beset with many dangers, one ought to have at least a summary understanding of it here.

The phase of retreat itself has two kinds: spontaneous retreat, and retreat triggered by some condition. Conditions may be either external or internal — mistakes among the twenty-five external expedient methods of exhaling and inhaling constitute "external conditioned retreat"; the arising, within a still mind, of worry and attachment triggered by the three obstructions or four māras constitutes "internal conditioned retreat." Afterward, one may cultivate further and regain it, or may fail to regain it — such cases are exceedingly common. As for the phase of protection: skillfully applying both internal and external expedient means to guard and protect the concentrated mind so that it suffers no loss. As for the phase of abiding: whether through such guarding and protection it remains secure and undiminished, or abides spontaneously of its own accord — either case constitutes the phase of abiding. As for the phase of advance: whether advancing spontaneously, or advancing through diligent effort — each admits of both a horizontal and a vertical dimension, and each of these, in turn, admits of both gradual and sudden modes. Should one advance through the twelve gates one by one in sequence, this is named "gradual advance"; should they all be perfected at a single stroke, this is named "sudden advance." The special excellences and the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna arise category by category — this is named "horizontal gradual"; should they all arise together at a single stroke, this is named "horizontal sudden." The obstruction of concrete matter is coarse and obstructive, and is unable to manifest; now, as the cultivating mind grows gradually keener, and the obstruction of one's own nature is thereby removed, subtle dharmas accordingly arise of themselves — why, then, should they need to come from without at all? Why is this so? The counting of the breath is able to transform the mind; the mind transforms fire; fire transforms wind; wind transforms water; water transforms earth — the four elements, transformed into subtler and subtler form, thereby produce the eight sensations. It is like wheat transformed into leaven, leaven transformed into mash, mash transformed into wine — the mash is likened to the desire-realm concentration, the wine to the first dhyāna, wheat serving throughout as their common root, none of it arriving from without. Should one insist that this concentration is produced strictly either from itself or from without, this falls into the fault of self-nature or other-nature respectively; here, following the *Madhyamaka-śāstra*'s own refutation of the four kinds of production, the discussion is simply of "arising from within" versus "arriving from without" in this looser, conventional sense. Further, the eight sensations are themselves the four great elements: motion and lightness are wind; itching and warmth are fire; cold and smoothness are water; heaviness and roughness are earth. Their essence and function combining and compounding together, the eight sensations thereby result.

Should the motion sensation arise, it may begin at the head, the back, the waist, the ribs, or the feet, gradually spreading throughout the entire body. One perceives this motion within the body itself, with no corresponding mark of motion visible outwardly, resembling a wind stirring subtly, only barely perceptible. Brightness: gleaming, pure, and exquisite, brilliantly clear beyond any comparison. Concentration: the mind, single and secure, free of any scattering or distraction. Wisdom: no longer clouded in confusion or caught within the net of doubt, the mind understanding with calm sharpness. Should the eight sensations arise within the desire-realm concentration itself, they are, in every case, deviant sensations, diseased and afflicted sensations, explained in full within the *Sequential Dharma-Gates*' own cultivation-and-verification manual, and are not discussed here. Here, in the eight sensations proper to the first dhyāna, deviant must be carefully distinguished from correct. Why is this so? First, because this stage is a border region, lying close to the desire realm; second, because the mind still carries a residue of the desire realm within it, through which what is deviant may readily gain entry. It is like a door left open, through which a thief may then enter — a ghost entering into dhyāna does not thereby make dhyāna itself a ghost. Should one fail to recognize this, the correct sensation is destroyed, and only the deviant and evil remains. As for deviant sensations: examining, once again, the eight sensations and the ten merits, one clarifies whether each shows excess or deficiency. As when the motion sensation arises with a direct, gentle stirring, neither slow nor hasty, moving quietly within the body — should it instead be forced, urgent, and hasty, with the hands and feet twitching in agitation, this is gross excess; should there be no motion at all, as though bound and fettered, this is deficiency. The remaining sensations of cold, warmth, and the rest are exactly the same. Further, taking up the ten qualities of emptiness and brightness accompanying the motion sensation, one likewise clarifies excess and deficiency in each. The emptiness spoken of here is simply an open, unobstructed openness — this is correct emptiness. Should it become an utter, total cessation with no awareness whatsoever remaining, this is gross excess; should it instead feel dense and obstructed like a solid block, this is deficiency. Brightness: like a mirror or the moon, clear and luminous — should it become like the blazing sun, or manifest every manner of colored light, this is gross excess; should nothing whatsoever be perceived, this is deficiency. Concentration: simply a single, tranquil, settled mind — should it become bound and rigidly fixed, unmoving, this is gross excess; should the mind instead scatter and race across every manner of object, this is deficiency. Correspondence, and the rest, follow this same pattern. These constitute twenty kinds of deviant mark within the motion sensation alone; combined with the remaining seven sensations, this yields one hundred and sixty deviant dharmas in all. Correct dhyāna ought not, properly, to contain anything deviant at all — the reason it nonetheless does is comparable to taking the herb calamus, which, just as its medicinal power is about to take effect, produces excessive anger, or taking Solomon's seal, which, just as its power is about to take effect, produces excessive desire — it is not the medicine itself that causes this, but rather that the medicine is expelling some coarse, underlying condition, and this coarse condition, on the verge of departing, flares up in intensity. Within the desire realm alone, only deviant sensations occur, increasing illness and increasing the hindrances, with no correct merit present at all. Once one enters the form-realm concentration, the eight sensations along with the ten merits of emptiness and brightness are stirred into motion — but there also exist these one hundred and sixty deviant forms, which cannot be left unrecognized. The Great Treatise says: "There is a wind that is able to bring rain, and there is a wind that is able to destroy rain" — clouds gather in the northeast and disperse in the southwest; dhyāna is exactly the same. The eight sensations and ten merits, once recognized, accomplish dhyāna; the one hundred and sixty deviant forms, once given rein, destroy dhyāna. Should even a single dharma among these prove deviant, every other dharma present becomes correspondingly tainted — just as, should one member of a party turn out to be a thief, the rest of the party is implicated as an accomplice band; but should the very first sensation prove free of deviance, every other dharma present is wholesome accordingly.

正禪五支者：若初觸觸身，在緣名「覺」；細心分別八觸及十眷屬，名為「觀」；慶昔未得而今得故，名為「喜」；恬愉名為「樂」；寂然名「一心」。《毘曇》二十三心數一時而發，取其強者判為五支。五支悉是定體，體前方便如上說。《成論》明五支前後相次而起。四支為方便，一心支為定體。《大集》以第六默然心為定體。有人言五支在欲界第九心；或言在欲界定前。此則非五支也。今辨覺觀俱禪，正就初禪判。那得爾耶？五支同起而有強弱相翳，取成就者以判五支。如一槌撞鐘，初麁、中細之異，五支亦爾。初緣覺相盛，不妨已有觀等四支。覺強，觀未了；覺息，觀方明。初已有喜，觀息喜支成。初已有樂，樂未暢；喜息，則樂成。初已有一心，四支所動；今樂謝，一心成。如初開寶藏，覺是寶物，亦知珍貴，喜、樂、定想，但未知是何等寶。次分別金銀，別已，領納生喜。喜故受樂，安快一心。如人飽食，無所復須。亦如對五欲極睡，故論云：「如人得寶藏。」云云。若四禪同以一心支為體，云何四異？今分初禪，是覺、觀家一心，故有四別。若進二禪，但呵覺、觀，初禪即壞，別義轉明。若通者，同用一心為體。釋五支名義相等，具在《修證》云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the five branch-factors of correct dhyāna: the very first sensation touching the body, remaining upon its object, is named "applied thought"; the mind's fine discernment of the eight sensations and their ten accompanying merits is named "sustained thought"; rejoicing that what was never attained before is now attained is named "joy"; the resulting serene contentment is named "bliss"; and the stillness beyond this is named "one-pointedness." The *Abhidharma* holds that twenty-three mental factors arise all at once, and, taking up only the strongest among them, judges these to constitute the five branch-factors — the five branch-factors together comprising the very substance of concentration itself, with the preliminary expedients preceding this substance as already explained above. The *Satyasiddhi* holds that the five branch-factors arise one after another in sequence: the first four constitute the preliminary expedient, and only the one-pointedness branch constitutes the true substance of concentration. The *Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra* takes the sixth, silent mind itself as the very substance of concentration. Some hold that the five branch-factors occur at the ninth mind-moment of the desire realm; others hold they occur before the desire-realm concentration itself — but these views do not properly correspond to the five branch-factors at all. Here, distinguishing the dhyāna possessing both applied and sustained thought together, the judgment is made properly with respect to the first dhyāna specifically — how, then, could this be otherwise? The five branch-factors arise together, yet with varying strength eclipsing one another in turn, and it is by taking up whichever proves dominant that the five branch-factors are properly judged. It is like striking a single bell with a mallet: an initial coarseness followed by a gradually finer resonance — the five branch-factors are exactly the same. At the very outset, the mark of applied thought is dominant, though this does not preclude the other four branch-factors — sustained thought and the rest — from already being present as well. While applied thought remains dominant, sustained thought has not yet fully matured; once applied thought subsides, sustained thought then becomes clear and manifest. At the very outset, joy is already present as well, though only once sustained thought subsides does the joy branch-factor come to full completion. At the very outset, bliss is already present as well, though not yet fully unfolded; once joy subsides, bliss then comes to completion. At the very outset, one-pointedness is already present as well, though still stirred by the other four branch-factors; only once bliss, too, subsides does one-pointedness come to completion. It is like first opening a treasure-house: applied thought is the treasure itself, and one already knows it to be precious, with joy, bliss, and the intention toward concentration all already present — yet one does not yet know precisely what kind of treasure it is. Next, one distinguishes gold from silver — this distinguishing accomplished, one receives it with rising joy; because of this joy, one experiences bliss; and one settles, at ease, into one-pointedness. It is like a person, having eaten to satisfaction, requiring nothing further at all — or like one overcome with the deepest sleep after indulging every one of the five desires. Hence the treatise says: "It is like a person who has found a treasure-house," and so on. Should all four dhyānas alike take the one-pointedness branch as their very substance, how, then, do the four differ from one another? Here, the first dhyāna is distinguished as the one-pointedness proper to applied and sustained thought — hence the four dhyānas are distinguished accordingly. Should one advance to the second dhyāna, one simply rebukes applied and sustained thought, and the first dhyāna is thereby dissolved, its own distinguishing sense growing correspondingly clearer. Considered generally, however, all four dhyānas alike employ one-pointedness as their substance. 復次，初動八觸，功德猶麁；若數數發，則轉深利。品或言三，或言九，或無量品，更互娛樂；功德叢鬧，不得一心。如恒奏妓，似多人客，󰉵對一已，一已復來。出散暫無，薄斂復現。若欲去之，但呵覺、觀；初禪謝已，即發中間單定，亦名「轉寂心」，亦名「退禪地」，亦名「篾屑心」。於此單靜心中，既失下，未發上，若生憂悔，此心亦失。若不悔者，內淨即發，無復八觸受納分別，故名「一識定」；混四大色成一淨色。照心轉淨，與喜俱發，無魔邪相，以非邊境故。喜已生樂，樂謝入一心。此禪喜，動樂不安，當呵喜。喜謝，入未到。忽發三禪，與樂俱起，還是色法轉妙，不倚喜生樂。此正樂遍身受，聖人能捨，凡夫捨為難。此有五支，謂：捨、念、慧、樂、一心。經論出之，或前、或後，皆是修行小異耳。此樂對苦，呵樂即謝，亦有未到。未到謝已，發不動定，還是色法轉妙。不為苦樂所動，名「不動定」。定法安隱，出入息斷，不苦不樂、捨、念清淨、一心支。雖爾，猶是色法。呵三種色，滅三種色；緣空得定，不復見色，心得脫色，如鳥出籠，是名「空定」。此定謝已，亦入未到。緣識生定，名為「識處」。此定謝已，緣無所有，入無所有法相應，名「不用處」，舊云「緣少許識」。若爾，即是所有處，亦是用處，何謂「不用」、「無所有」耶？此定過已，忽發非想非非想。此定不緣識處，故非想；不緣不用處，故非非想。更無上法可攀，三界頂禪。世為極妙，外道計為涅槃，實是闇證。具足苦集，垂盡三有，還墮三途。委悉明根本禪，往《修證》中尋之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the eight sensations, when they first stir, produce merit that remains still coarse; should they recur again and again, this merit grows correspondingly deeper and sharper. Their grades may number three, or nine, or an immeasurable number, alternating and interweaving in delight — and, growing crowded and clamorous, this very abundance of merit prevents the mind from settling into one-pointedness. It is like the constant playing of musicians, resembling a crowd of many guests: one departs only for another to arrive in its place; the crowd disperses for a moment, then, scarcely thinned, gathers again. Should one wish to be rid of this, one need only rebuke applied and sustained thought — the first dhyāna thereupon dissolves, and there arises in its place a solitary intermediate concentration, also named "the mind turned to quiescence," also named "the ground of dhyāna's own withdrawal," also named "the winnowed-chaff mind." Within this solitary, still mind, having already lost the lower stage yet not yet produced the higher, should regret and misgiving arise, this very state of mind is lost as well. Should no such regret arise, inner purity then manifests of itself, with no further reception or discrimination of the eight sensations — hence it is named "the concentration of unified consciousness," in which the four great elements' own coloration is fused into a single, pure coloration. The illuminating mind, growing correspondingly purer, arises together with joy, free of any demonic or deviant mark, since this is no longer a border region. Joy having already given rise to bliss, bliss in turn subsides into one-pointedness. Yet the joy of this dhyāna still carries with it an unsettling agitation of bliss — one must therefore rebuke joy as well. Joy having subsided, one enters the stage of approach once more, and there suddenly arises the third dhyāna, arising together with bliss — this, too, being form-dharma transformed into ever greater subtlety, bliss here no longer depending on joy for its arising. This is the true bliss felt throughout the entire body — sages are able to relinquish it, while ordinary beings find such relinquishment exceedingly difficult. This dhyāna has five branch-factors: equanimity, mindfulness, wisdom, bliss, and one-pointedness. The scriptures and treatises set these forth sometimes in one order, sometimes in another — this reflects only slight variation in the actual practice, nothing more. This bliss stands opposed to suffering — once rebuked, bliss too subsides, and a corresponding stage of approach likewise occurs here. Once this stage of approach has subsided, there arises the unmoving concentration, again form-dharma transformed into still greater subtlety, no longer moved by either suffering or bliss — hence it is named "the unmoving concentration." This dharma of concentration is secure and settled, the in-breath and out-breath entirely ceased, with neither-suffering-nor-bliss, equanimity, purity of mindfulness, and one-pointedness as its branch-factors. Even so, this remains, nonetheless, form-dharma. Rebuking these three kinds of form and extinguishing these three kinds of form, one takes emptiness as one's object and attains concentration, no longer perceiving form at all — the mind thus freed from form, like a bird escaping its cage — this is named "the concentration of emptiness." Once this concentration subsides, one likewise enters a corresponding stage of approach. Taking consciousness as one's object, concentration arises, named "the sphere of infinite consciousness." Once this concentration subsides, taking nothing-whatsoever as one's object, one enters correspondence with the dharma of nothing-whatsoever, named "the sphere of nothingness" — anciently called "the sphere taking a mere trace of consciousness as its object." If this is so, is it not then, in fact, the sphere of something, the sphere of use — why, then, is it called "no-use," "nothing-whatsoever"? Once this concentration has passed, there suddenly arises neither-perception-nor-non-perception. This concentration does not take the sphere of consciousness as its object, hence "neither perception"; nor does it take the sphere of no-use as its object, hence "nor non-perception." There remains no higher dharma to reach for beyond this — it is the very summit dhyāna of the three realms. The world regards it as utterly sublime, and non-Buddhist practitioners take it to be nirvāṇa itself, yet it is, in reality, nothing but darkly realized attainment, still fully possessed of suffering and its origin, and, though the three modes of existence are on the very verge of exhaustion here, one falls back once more into the three lower paths from this very peak. The Sixteen Special Excellences 次明特勝發相

Chinese Source

○次、明特勝發者：若依律教，應在不淨後；依行，在不淨前。如律云：「佛為比丘說不淨觀，皆生厭患，不能與臭身共住，衣鉢雇鹿杖自害。佛令放不淨，修特勝。」大黃、巴豆瀉人太過，身力弱者即便弊之；更以餘藥並下、並補。補故是愛；下故是策。策勝根本；愛勝不淨。有觀，名「亦無漏」；對治力弱，名「亦有漏」。如廉食人噉猪䐗鄙貯屎物，而猶可強食之；若六月臭猪，蟲蠅所集，不復可食。特勝是實觀，猶可從容；不淨是假想，不須可耐云云。特勝發者：忽見氣息出、入、長、短，知來無所從，去無所至；入不積聚，出不分散。若約根本，即是麁、細住。若見息來去遍身，若約根本是未到地。而根本闇證，謂無身、床鋪等者，非實無也。如灰覆火上，愚者輕蹈之；如夜噉食、如盲觸婦，皆不暢其情。今有觀慧，見息遍身而定心明淨安隱，故異闇證也。又見身中三十六物，如開倉見󰝿、粟、麻、豆。若對根本，即初禪位。前八觸觸身倉，心眼不開，不見內物。特勝既有觀慧，觸開身倉，心眼即見三十六物：肝如菉豆；心如赤豆；腎如烏豆；脾如粟；大小腸道更相應通，血脈灌注如江河流。內有十二物：肝、心、痰、癊等。中有十二：膜、膚、肪、膏等。外有十二：髮、毛等。出入息統致其間，不淨、無常、苦、空、無我，一切身行皆休，終不為身而造諸惡。是名「除諸身行」。若對道品，是身念處；若對根本，即是覺、觀兩支。心眼初開，是覺支；分別三十六物無謬，是觀支。心受喜，對喜支。前喜名隱沒、有垢、味；今喜不隱沒、無垢、味，即是「法喜」，非是「受喜」也。心受樂者亦如是，非「受樂」樂；知樂中三受皆無樂，名樂支。受諸心行是一心支。知眾心是一心，不同根本計實一心也。若對道品，皆受念處。心作喜、心作攝者：前喜從三十六物生，此直就心作喜，故知對二禪。《大集》明二禪但三支，無內淨。今心作喜，意似於此。作攝者：喜動則散，若作攝，得入一心。根本但內淨受喜；特勝有觀慧，恒攝喜心。心作解脫者，此對三禪。根本之樂，猗喜遍身受，凡夫捨為難；特勝有觀慧，則無愛味，故言「解脫」。從心作喜至心作解脫，皆是心念處也。從觀無常者，對第四禪。餘處亦觀無常，未是別治；得不動定，味之為常。今有觀慧，知離苦樂而終是色法，猶是無常，不應生染，故稱無常。從觀出散對空處。滅三種色，如鳥出籠，故言「出」；緣空，故言「散」。雖緣空，亦有觀慧。觀離欲是對識處。緣空多則散，散名為「欲」；特勝觀慧離是散心，故名「離欲」。觀滅對無所有處。特勝觀慧觀識若多若少皆無，故名「觀滅」。觀棄捨對非想非非想處。棄識處及無所有處，更有妙定名為「非想非非想」，凡夫妄謂涅槃。佛弟子知其雖無麁煩惱而有細煩惱，而無愛味，故稱「淨禪」。從無常至棄捨，皆名「法念處」。此十六法，橫竪對治法，節節皆異。根本闇證功德則薄，如食無鹽；特勝功德則重，如食有鹽。委論發相具如《修證》中云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the manifestation of the special excellences: according to the vinaya teaching, this practice properly follows the impurity contemplation; according to its actual conduct, it properly precedes it. As the vinaya says: "The Buddha taught the monks the contemplation of impurity, and all fell into such world-weariness that they could no longer bear to remain alongside their own malodorous bodies, hiring others to slay them with a deer-hunter's staff by way of their robes and bowl. The Buddha thereupon had them set impurity aside, and instead cultivate the special excellences." Rhubarb root and croton seed purge the body to excess, and one whose bodily strength is already weak is thereby further debilitated by them; one must then apply a further medicine that both purges and restores at once — the restoring corresponds to attachment, the purging to exertion; exertion surpasses the root dhyānas, while attachment surpasses impurity. Since this practice involves contemplative insight, it is named "also uncontaminated"; yet since its power as an antidote remains comparatively weak, it is also named "also contaminated." It is like a person accustomed to plain food who is able to force himself to eat even a base dish containing pig entrails still holding excrement, yet, presented with a pig six months rotten and swarming with flies, finds it no longer possible to eat at all — the special excellences, being a genuine, realized contemplation, can still be approached with relative composure, whereas the impurity contemplation, being a visualized practice, cannot be so readily endured, and so on. As for the manifestation of the special excellences: one suddenly perceives the breath's own going out, coming in, its length and shortness, knowing that it comes from nowhere and goes nowhere, that what enters does not accumulate and what exits does not disperse. Mapped onto the root dhyānas, this corresponds to coarse and fine abiding. Perceiving the breath moving throughout the entire body corresponds, mapped onto the root dhyānas, to the stage of approach. Yet the root dhyānas' own dark realization, in which one perceives no body, no bed, and the rest, does not mean these things are genuinely absent — it is like ashes covering a fire, which a fool treads upon carelessly, unaware; or like eating in the dark of night, or a blind man touching his wife — in neither case does one's true feeling find full expression. Here, by contrast, possessing contemplative insight, one perceives the breath pervading the entire body while the concentrated mind remains bright, pure, and secure — hence this differs from dark realization. One further perceives the thirty-six impure substances within the body, as though opening a granary and beholding rice, millet, hemp seed, and beans. Mapped onto the root dhyānas, this corresponds to the stage of the first dhyāna: there, the eight sensations merely touch the body's own storehouse, without opening the mind's own eye, and one does not perceive the substances within. The special excellences, possessing contemplative insight, open the body's storehouse through this very touch, and the mind's eye then directly perceives the thirty-six substances: the liver like green mung beans, the heart like red beans, the kidney like black beans, the spleen like millet, the large and small intestines interconnected and communicating with one another, blood and vessels flowing through them like a great river. Twelve substances lie within: the liver, heart, phlegm, bile, and the rest; twelve lie in the middle layer: membrane, skin, fat, grease, and the rest; twelve lie without: hair, body-hair, and the rest. The in-breath and out-breath govern and pervade throughout this whole domain — impurity, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self — every bodily activity thereby comes to rest, and one never again commits any evil for the sake of this very body. This is named "eliminating every bodily activity." Mapped onto the branches of the path, this corresponds to the foundation of mindfulness of the body; mapped onto the root dhyānas, this corresponds to the two branch-factors of applied and sustained thought — the mind's eye first opening corresponds to applied thought, and the flawless discernment of the thirty-six substances corresponds to sustained thought. The mind receiving joy corresponds to the joy branch-factor — the earlier joy [of the root dhyānas] is characterized as concealed, defiled, and savored, while this present joy is unconcealed, undefiled, and unsavored, being precisely "Dharma-joy" rather than "the joy of mere sensation." The mind receiving bliss is likewise so, being not "the bliss of mere sensation" — knowing that none of the three kinds of feeling within this bliss are themselves truly blissful is named the bliss branch-factor. The reception of every mental activity is the one-pointedness branch-factor — knowing every state of mind as itself this very one-pointedness, unlike the root dhyānas' mistaken supposition of a genuinely singular, substantial mind. Mapped onto the branches of the path, all of these correspond to the foundation of mindfulness of feeling. As for the mind producing joy and the mind producing restraint: the earlier joy arose from the thirty-six substances, whereas here joy is produced directly by the mind itself, and this is known to correspond to the second dhyāna — the *Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra* explains that the second dhyāna has only three branch-factors, lacking inner purity; here, the mind producing joy carries a sense resembling this very account. As for producing restraint: joy, once stirred, tends toward scattering, but through this restraint one is able to enter one-pointedness — the root dhyānas merely receive joy through inner purity, whereas the special excellences, possessing contemplative insight, constantly restrain and gather in this joyful mind. As for the mind producing liberation: this corresponds to the third dhyāna. The bliss of the root dhyānas, felt throughout the body by way of joy, is difficult for an ordinary being to relinquish; the special excellences, possessing contemplative insight, are free of any craving-attachment to it, and hence this is called "liberation." From the mind producing joy through the mind producing liberation, all of these belong to the foundation of mindfulness of mind. As for contemplating impermanence: this corresponds to the fourth dhyāna. Elsewhere, too, one contemplates impermanence, but this does not yet constitute a distinct antidote there, since one who attains the unmoving concentration tends to savor it as though permanent. Here, by contrast, possessing contemplative insight, one knows that, even freed from suffering and bliss alike, this remains, in the end, form-dharma, still impermanent, and one ought not give rise to attachment toward it — hence it is termed "contemplating impermanence." As for contemplating dispersal and departure: this corresponds to the sphere of emptiness. Extinguishing the three kinds of form, like a bird escaping its cage, is termed "departure"; taking emptiness as one's object is termed "dispersal" — though emptiness is here the object, contemplative insight nonetheless remains present. Contemplating freedom from desire corresponds to the sphere of consciousness — taking emptiness excessively as one's object tends toward scattering, and this scattering is itself named "desire"; the special excellences' contemplative insight is free of this very scattered mind, hence it is named "freedom from desire." Contemplating cessation corresponds to the sphere of nothingness — the special excellences' contemplative insight recognizes that consciousness, whether much or little, is in either case without genuine substance, hence it is named "contemplating cessation." Contemplating relinquishment corresponds to the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — relinquishing both the sphere of consciousness and the sphere of nothingness, there remains a further subtle concentration named "neither-perception-nor-non-perception," which ordinary beings mistakenly suppose to be nirvāṇa itself; the Buddha's disciples recognize that, though free of coarse affliction, it still harbors subtle affliction, yet, being free of craving-attachment to it, it is nonetheless termed "the pure dhyāna." From contemplating impermanence through relinquishment, all of these are named "the foundation of mindfulness of dharmas." These sixteen dharmas, applied as antidotes both horizontally and vertically, differ at every single stage from one another. The merit of the root dhyānas' own dark realization remains comparatively meager, like food without salt; the merit of the special excellences is correspondingly weightier, like food properly salted. ○次、通明禪發相者：上特勝修時，觀慧猶總；見三十六物，證相亦總。通明修時細妙；證時分明。《華嚴》亦有此名；《大集》辨寶炬陀羅尼，正是此禪也；《請觀音》亦是此意。修時三事通修，能發三明、六通。又修寶炬時，乃至入滅受想定。當知此門具八解脫、三明、六通，故名「通明」也。《大集》辨此五支名目，謂：如心覺、大覺、思惟、大思惟、觀於心性，是名「覺支」。觀心行、大行、遍行，是為「觀支」。如實知、大知、心動至心喜，是為「喜支」。身安、心安、受於樂觸，是為「安支」。心住、大住、不亂於緣，是名「定支」。初觀三事皆融，證時三事皆一，故名「如心覺」。覺於真諦，色、息、心泯一無異。又識俗諦，皮、肉、骨等皆有九十九重；覺五藏生五氣；亦見身中蟲戶，行來言語，無細不了。覺託胎初陰，過去無明業是蠟，現在父母精血是泥。過去業不住，故名「印壞」；現在託識，名色具足，故名「文成」。住在生藏之下、熟藏之上，子腸中，形甚微細；唯有一念妄想，色心相依，如有、如無、如夢。業行力故，自然能起一念思心，感召其母。母便思青色、呼聲、臊氣、酢味；因此念力，生一毫氣，氣變為水，水變為血，血變為肉；母氣出入以相資潤，便得成肝藏；向上成眼，向下成手足大指。若思白色、哭聲、腥氣、辛味，便成肺藏；上向為鼻，下向為手足第二指。若思赤色、語聲、焦氣、苦味，便成心藏；上向為口，下向為手足第三指。若思黃色、歌聲、香氣、甜味，便成脾藏；上向為舌，下向為手足第四指。若思黑色、吟聲、臭氣、鹹味，便成腎藏；上向為耳，下向成手足第五指。覺身分細微，例皆如此。思惟、大思惟者，即是思惟真、俗也。觀於心性者，即是空也；若真、若俗同入心性。《請觀音》云：「一一入於如實之際。」如此覺支與上倍異。心行、大行者，上覺支是解，今心行去是觀行。心行於世諦，故名「行」；行真諦，故名「大行」；三事俱行，故名「遍行」。心住者，於俗諦得一心。大住者，於真諦得一心。不亂於緣者，雖見真、俗無量境界，而於心不謬也。具明其相，備如通明觀中廣說。發此定時，見身、息、心，同如芭蕉相，無有堅實，是未到地相。見此三事同如泡沫相，是初禪。見三事同如浮雲相，是二禪。見三事同如影相，是三禪。見三事同如鏡像，是四禪。滅此三事皆空；滅空緣識；滅識緣無所有；滅無所有緣非想非非想；滅非想非非想三種受想，而身證滅受之法，以成解脫。有俗觀故，名「亦有漏」；有真觀故，名「亦無漏」。此禪事理既備，階位具足。《成論》人應用此明道定，入八解脫，於義為便而不肯用。《阿毘曇》約八背捨得有事理，俱異外道，成俱解脫人。《成論》但有理無事，便無俱解脫人。約外道禪為事禪，亦應約十善為戒、世智為慧。戒、慧既異外道，定何意同？是則客醫無客定，八術不成。委論其相，具在《修證》中說云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the manifestation of the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna: when the special excellences are being cultivated, their contemplative insight remains still general in nature, and the perceiving of the thirty-six substances, too, remains a comparatively general realization. When the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna is cultivated, it is subtle and refined throughout, and its realization is precise and distinct. This name appears also in the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*; the *Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra*'s discussion of the "jeweled-torch dhāraṇī" is precisely this same dhyāna; the *Sūtra of Petitioning Avalokiteśvara* carries this same sense as well. In its cultivation, the three matters — form, breath, and mind — are cultivated together as one, and this is able to give rise to the three insights and the six spiritual powers. Further, in cultivating the jeweled-torch, one proceeds all the way to entering the concentration of the cessation of feeling and perception. One should recognize that this gate is complete with the eight liberations, the three insights, and the six spiritual powers all at once — hence it is named "the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna." The *Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra* sets forth this dhyāna's five branch-factors by name as follows: as-the-mind awareness, great awareness, reflection, great reflection, and contemplating the nature of mind — these together are named "the applied-thought branch-factor." Contemplating the activity of mind, great activity, and pervasive activity — these are "the sustained-thought branch-factor." Genuine knowing, great knowing, and the mind's movement all the way to the mind's own joy — these are "the joy branch-factor." The body at ease, the mind at ease, and the reception of blissful contact — these are "the ease branch-factor." The mind abiding, greatly abiding, and undisturbed with respect to its object — this is named "the concentration branch-factor." At the outset, one contemplates the three matters as all fused into one, and, at the moment of realization, the three matters are all recognized as one and the same — hence it is named "as-the-mind awareness." Awakening to the real truth, form, breath, and mind dissolve into a single undifferentiated whole. Further, one discerns the conventional truth: skin, flesh, bone, and the rest are each recognized as possessing ninety-nine distinct layers; one perceives the five organs generating the five vital energies; one likewise perceives the worm-nests within the body, their comings and goings, their speech and utterance, nothing however subtle left unclear. One perceives the very first aggregate at the moment of conception: the karma of past ignorance is the wax, and the sexual fluids of one's present parents are the clay. The past karma does not remain fixed, hence it is named "the seal broken"; the present consciousness, taking rebirth, is complete with name-and-form, hence it is named "the imprint formed." Dwelling below the stomach and above the intestines, within the small intestine's own coil, its form is exceedingly minute — there is present only a single thought-moment of deluded conception, form and mind mutually dependent, existing as though present, as though absent, as though within a dream. Through the very power of this karmic activity, one is naturally able to give rise to a single thought-moment's reflective mind, which calls forth its mother in turn. The mother then conceives a thought of blue-green color, a calling sound, a rank odor, a sour taste; through the power of this very thought, a subtle vital energy is generated, this energy transforms into water, the water transforms into blood, the blood transforms into flesh; the mother's own vital energy, entering and departing, nourishes and moistens this in turn, and thus the liver organ comes to be formed — extending upward it becomes the eyes, extending downward it becomes the thumbs of the hands and feet. Should one instead conceive of white color, a wailing sound, a fishy odor, a pungent taste, the lung organ is thereby formed — extending upward it becomes the nose, extending downward the second toes and fingers. Should one conceive of red color, a speaking sound, a scorched odor, a bitter taste, the heart organ is thereby formed — extending upward it becomes the mouth, extending downward the third toes and fingers. Should one conceive of yellow color, a singing sound, a fragrant odor, a sweet taste, the spleen organ is thereby formed — extending upward it becomes the tongue, extending downward the fourth toes and fingers. Should one conceive of black color, a chanting sound, a foul odor, a salty taste, the kidney organ is thereby formed — extending upward it becomes the ears, extending downward the fifth toes and fingers. Perceiving the subtle formation of the body's every part follows this same pattern throughout. Reflection and great reflection are precisely the reflection upon the real and the conventional truths. Contemplating the nature of mind is precisely emptiness itself — the real and the conventional alike entering together into this very nature of mind. The *Sūtra of Petitioning Avalokiteśvara* says: "Each and every one enters into the very ultimate of genuine reality." This applied-thought branch-factor thus differs by twofold depth from that of the excellences described above. Mental activity and great activity: the applied-thought branch-factor above was a matter of understanding, whereas mental activity here proceeds as contemplative practice. Mental activity with respect to the conventional truth is named "activity"; activity with respect to the real truth is named "great activity"; the three matters activated together are named "pervasive activity." Abiding of mind is attaining one-pointedness with respect to the conventional truth; great abiding is attaining one-pointedness with respect to the real truth. Being undisturbed with respect to one's object means that, though one perceives immeasurable objects belonging to both the real and the conventional, the mind is nowhere led astray by them. The full clarification of these marks is set forth at length within the treatment of the penetrating-and-clear contemplation itself. When this concentration arises, one perceives body, breath, and mind alike as resembling a plantain trunk, possessing no solid substance whatsoever — this is the mark of the stage of approach. Perceiving these three matters alike as resembling foam is the first dhyāna. Perceiving the three matters alike as resembling floating clouds is the second dhyāna. Perceiving the three matters alike as resembling a shadow is the third dhyāna. Perceiving the three matters alike as resembling a reflection in a mirror is the fourth dhyāna. Extinguishing these three matters, all become empty; extinguishing emptiness, one takes consciousness as one's object; extinguishing consciousness, one takes nothing-whatsoever as one's object; extinguishing nothing-whatsoever, one takes neither-perception-nor-non-perception as one's object; extinguishing the three kinds of feeling and perception belonging to neither-perception-nor-non-perception, one bodily realizes the dharma of the cessation of feeling, thereby accomplishing liberation. Since this dhyāna involves conventional contemplation, it is named "also uncontaminated." Since this dhyāna's own matters and principle are both thus fully complete, its stages, too, are complete in full. Masters of the *Satyasiddhi* ought properly to apply this account in clarifying the concentration proper to the path, entering the eight liberations — this would be convenient to their own doctrine, and yet they are unwilling to make use of it. The *Abhidharma*, treating the eight liberations, holds that they possess both matter and principle together, thereby differing entirely from non-Buddhist practitioners, and constitute the "both-liberated" practitioner. The *Satyasiddhi*, possessing only principle without matter, thus has no place at all for the both-liberated practitioner. Were non-Buddhist dhyāna to be treated as a merely material dhyāna, one ought equally to treat the ten wholesome deeds as mere precept and worldly wisdom as mere intellectual understanding — yet if precept and wisdom are properly held to differ from those of non-Buddhist practitioners, why, then, should concentration alone be held to be the same? This would leave the guest-physician with no guest-remedy of his own, and his eightfold art of healing would fail to be accomplished at all. The exhaustive treatment of these marks is set forth in full within the *Sequential Dharma-Gates*' own cultivation-and-verification manual, and so on.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the manifestation of the impurity dhyāna: this is treated first in terms of the nine contemplations, which themselves apply to two kinds of practitioner: the dharma-destroying practitioner, and the dharma-preserving practitioner. Should a dharma-destroying practitioner cultivate the nine contemplations, these are: first, the contemplation of bloating; second, the contemplation of decay; third, the contemplation of blood-smeared disfigurement; fourth, the contemplation of festering pus; fifth, the contemplation of bluish-green discoloration; sixth, the contemplation of devouring by scavengers; seventh, the contemplation of scattering apart; eighth, the contemplation of bare bones; ninth, the contemplation of cremation. Such a person seeks merely to sever suffering, burning away even the very skeleton itself, hastening urgently toward the stage beyond further learning, taking no delight in concrete contemplative practice for its own sake. Since there remains no skeleton left for him to contemplate, he thereby possesses no meditative concentration, no spiritual powers or transformations, and no dhyāna of aspiration, wisdom, or the summit. Though he speaks of "burning away" the body entirely, his body in fact remains — comparable, for instance, to bodily realizing the cessation of feeling and perception while the body itself still remains present, and so on. This kind of person is prone to retreat, as the *Abhidharma* itself describes such a mark of retreat — the fourth fruit likened here to sand resting at the bottom of a well. The *Āgama* says: "A practitioner of the third fruit retreated from the precepts and returned to lay life, forsaking and losing his monastic discipline, yet without losing his shared attainment of the path. Laypeople, seeing this, raised slander, saying there was no noble dharma at all. The Buddha said: 'Having grown sated with desire, world-weariness arose in him — before long he shall return once more, seeking to go forth again.' None of the monks would receive him back, so the Buddha himself received him, and he thereupon attained arhatship. Ānanda asked: 'Great Worthy, was this the retreat of one still in training, or the retreat of one beyond further training?' The Buddha answered: 'The retreat of one still in training.'" If this is so, then it refers precisely to a worldly-wisdom-based practitioner who severs affliction and is liberated through wisdom alone, and such a person may indeed retreat — it is not that one with uncontaminated wisdom, having severed one grade of affliction and advanced one grade of understanding, is thereby subject to retreat as well. Should one manifest these nine contemplations while possessing none of the meritorious qualities of genuine dhyāna, this is precisely the dharma-destroying practitioner. As for the nine contemplations of the dharma-preserving practitioner: proceeding from the initial contemplation of bloating up through the contemplation of bare bones, without advancing to the contemplation of cremation, one attains the streaming light, the liberations, the bases of mastery, contemplation, refinement, permeation, cultivation, and spiritual powers and transformations, every meritorious quality complete in full, thereby becoming the "both-liberated" practitioner. In its cultivation, one whose craving predominates contemplates externally; one whose wrong views predominate contemplates the body itself; one in whom views and craving are evenly matched contemplates both internally and externally at once. As for their manifestation, this may be understood according to the very same pattern.

於坐禪中忽見死屍在地。言說方爾，奄便那去？氣盡身冷，神逝色變；無常所遷，不簡豪賤；老少端醜，無逃避處；慈父孝子，無相代者。屍腥在地，風吹日暴，與本永異。或見一屍、多屍，是大不淨觀相；或滿一聚落、一國土；或一屍色變，或多屍色變。死屍雖非九數，是諸想之本，故先說之。是等死屍顏色黯黑；身體洪直，手足葩花；膖脹𦠇鄧如韋囊盛風；九孔流溢甚為穢惡。行者自念：「我身如是，未離未脫；觀所愛人，亦復如是。」是相發時，得一分定心，黮黮安快。須臾之間，見此脹屍風吹日暴，皮肉破壞，身體坼裂，形色改異了不可識，是名「壞相」。又見坼裂之處，血從中出，散溜塗漫，處處斑駁，灌溢於地，臭處蓬勃，是為血塗相。又見膿爛流潰，𭱨𭱨滂沱，如蠟得火，是名膿爛相。又見殘皮餘肉風日乾炙，臭敗黮黵，半青半瘀，𥀰𥀰𤿽𤿽，是為青瘀相。又見此屍而為狐狼鴟鷲之所噉食，紛葩鬪競，爴裂拽挽，是為噉相。又見頭手異處、五藏分張不可收斂，是為散相。又見二種骨，一帶膿膏，一純白淨；或見一具骨，或遍聚落。如是諸相轉時，定心隨轉，𪒠𪒠沈寂，愉愉靜妙，安快之相說不可貲。不壞法人所觀齊此。

Ru-Yi Translation

During seated meditation, one suddenly perceives a corpse lying upon the ground. Only just now speaking, how has it so abruptly departed? Its breath exhausted, its body cold, its spirit gone and its complexion altered — subject to impermanence, sparing neither the noble nor the base, neither the old nor the young, neither the comely nor the plain, with no place left for any of them to flee to; not even a loving father or a filial son can take its place. The corpse lies rank upon the ground, exposed to wind and sun, utterly transformed from what it was before. One may perceive a single corpse, or many corpses — this being the mark of the great impurity contemplation — or an entire village, or an entire country, filled with them; one corpse's discoloration, or the discoloration of many corpses at once. Though the mere corpse itself is not counted among the nine contemplations proper, it is the very foundation upon which all of them rest, and is therefore described first. Such a corpse's coloration turns dark and ashen; the body grows swollen and rigid, the hands and feet splayed outward like flower petals; it bloats and distends like a leather sack filled with wind, its nine orifices overflowing with utterly foul discharge. The practitioner reflects to himself: "My own body is exactly so, not yet freed, not yet delivered from this; and the person I love is likewise exactly so." When this mark first arises, one attains a measure of concentrated mind, dark and hushed, secure and at ease. In but a moment, one perceives this bloated corpse, exposed further to wind and sun, its skin and flesh breaking apart, its body splitting and cracking open, its form and color so altered as to become entirely unrecognizable — this is named "the mark of decay." One further perceives, at the very places where it has split open, blood issuing forth from within, spreading and smearing about, mottled here and there, pooling and flowing across the ground, its stench rising thick and heavy — this is "the mark of blood-smearing." One further perceives it festering and dissolving into pus, oozing and streaming in great quantity, like wax melting under fire — this is named "the mark of festering pus." One further perceives the remaining skin and flesh scorched dry by wind and sun, foul and blackened, half bluish-green and half discolored, cracked and fissured throughout — this is "the mark of bluish-green discoloration." One further perceives this very corpse devoured by foxes, wolves, owls, and vultures, contending and struggling over it in confusion, clawing, tearing, and dragging it apart — this is "the mark of devouring." One further perceives the head and hands scattered to different places, the five organs strewn apart beyond any possibility of gathering back together — this is "the mark of scattering." One further perceives two kinds of bone: one still bearing traces of pus and grease, the other purely white and clean; one may perceive a single complete skeleton, or skeletons scattered throughout an entire village. As these various marks pass through their successive stages, the concentrated mind transforms along with them, growing ever more deeply hushed and still, serene and subtly exquisite — a state of secure ease beyond any possible price. This is as far as the contemplation of the dharma-preserving practitioner extends.

Ru-Yi Translation

Before perceiving this mark, one's craving-attachment remains exceedingly strong; but once one has perceived it, desire is entirely brought to a halt, and one finds oneself utterly unable to bear its recurrence any longer — like one who, not yet seeing excrement, can still eat rice with ease, but who, catching a sudden whiff of its stench, vomits at once. It is likewise like the brahmin devoted to purity who, having eaten a cake smeared with the pus and marrow of an abscess, strikes his own head in self-reproach, crying: "Now I understand at last!" Should one realize this mark fully, then even arched brows and gleaming eyes, gleaming teeth and crimson lips, appear as though covered over by a mound of urine and filth, or as a rotting corpse merely draped in fine silk brocade — one can no longer even bear to look upon it, let alone draw physically near it. One who once would even hire another to slay oneself with a deer-hunter's staff rather than continue in this state — how much less, then, would one embrace and caress it in search of sensual pleasure? A contemplation of this kind is precisely the great rhubarb-root purgative for the disease of lustful desire. It is like a person fond of food who, once he genuinely understands that pig entrails are simply a vessel filled with excrement, can still force himself to eat it regardless — yet, confronted with the sight of a rotting, worm-infested, foul-smelling pig, is he still able to eat it at all? Earlier, the power of the special excellences remained weak, unable to eliminate desire with any real certainty; here, by contrast, the power of this contemplation is strong, and the fire of lust is swiftly extinguished. Hence it is said: "Once the contemplation of the nine perceptions is accomplished, the six thieves are thereby already largely removed." One further comes to recognize love as a form of enmity and deception, and comes to know the distinction between the false and the genuinely real. World-weariness of this kind not only eliminates desire but is also able to produce the uncontaminated, and is also able to accomplish the Mahāyāna itself. Having finished expounding the contemplation of the corpse's transformation, the *Great Treatise* goes on immediately to discuss the six pāramitās and the four immeasurable minds — every commentator alike has declared this a corrupted, erroneous passage. Here, by contrast, it is made clear that the bodhisattva, cultivating even this very first contemplation alone, is already thereby complete with the entire Mahāyāna — hence, having set forth every dharma at length, the text then immediately says: "And so it is, extending even to the contemplation of cremation as well." How, then, could this possibly be dismissed as a corrupted, dropped-out passage?

Next, clarifying the manifestation of the eight liberations: of the ten dhyāna gates named earlier, the first three sets — the root dhyānas, the special excellences, and the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna — are savored and pure, while the nine contemplations, extended through the ten totality-bases, are together named "contemplation"; the nine sequential concentrations are "refinement"; the lion's swift-leaping concentration is "permeation"; and the transcendent concentration is "cultivation" — these last four together constitute this set of concentrations. Here, first, clarifying the liberations: the liberations themselves admit of both a general and a particular sense — the general shared alike by the two lesser vehicles, the particular belonging distinctively to the bodhisattva. The liberations are further not fixed in their naming: sometimes, speaking of the fruit in terms of the cause, "liberation" is called by the name "turning away"; and, conversely, sometimes, speaking of the cause in terms of the fruit, "turning away" is called by the name "liberation." Judged strictly, where affliction is ultimately severed and both matter and principle are fully complete, this is properly termed "liberation"; where affliction is not yet exhausted and concentration not yet fully perfected, this is termed merely "turning away." "Turning" (背) means turning away in world-weariness from the pure and refined five desires of the lower stage and of one's own present stage; "away" (捨) means relinquishing the very mind of attachment itself — hence together they are named "turning away" (背捨). Where craving predominates in breaking through desire, the outer mark manifests, as explained above [in the nine contemplations]; where wrong view predominates in breaking through desire, the inner mark manifests instead — this inner mark is precisely the eight liberations. The first is: possessing form within, contemplating form without; and so on up to the eighth, the liberation of the cessation of feeling and perception. As for "possessing form within, contemplating form without": one neither breaks nor destroys the inner form, but contemplates the white bones, skin, and flesh within, while contemplating the corpse and the rest without. As for the actual method of cultivating this, it is given in full within the *Dhyāna Gates*. Here, its manifestation shall be indicated only briefly. The practitioner suddenly perceives his own toes, the skin swelling up like a blister, gradually spreading to the calf, to the waist, and throughout the entire body up to the crown of the head. In but a moment, the body grows swollen and rigid, the whole form distended and taut, the five toes splayed like flower petals, the two legs like pillars, the waist and belly like a great jar, the head like a basin, swollen and distended everywhere, like a leather sack filled with wind. When this mark arises, whether spreading from the feet to the crown, or from the crown to the feet, filling an entire meditation-couch, the skin grows taut and the flesh splits open, on the very verge of bursting. Once it bursts, pus flows forth, soaking and dissolving everything around it. Further, from the crown down to the feet, the skin and flesh fall away of themselves, leaving only the bare white bones — the joints and limbs held together, propped one against another, rigid and unmoving. The fallen skin and flesh gather together in a single heap, swarming with worms, filthy and utterly repulsive. Should this mark manifest, one comes to feel a deep aversion toward one's own body, regarding it with the same disgust as excrement — how much more so, then, toward one's wife, children, and possessions, that one should feel any reluctance to give them up? Both the bodhisattva who sacrificed his own body [to feed the starving tigress] and the one slain by the deer-hunter's staff had both attained precisely this same contemplation. Inwardly, one no longer conceives of a self; outwardly, one no longer cherishes any possession; one lowers one's head in shame and remorse, this very sense of world-weariness continuing on without interruption, and so on. The Great Sūtra says: "Removing away the skin and flesh, one contemplates carefully the bare white bones, fixing one's mindfulness upon every single joint in turn, observing them forward and backward until the bones appear utterly pure and clean." This is named "the inner mark of possessing form." As for contemplating form without: one perceives, outwardly, a corpse bloated and rotting with pus, filling an entire village, or an entire country — this being the very same impurity contemplated in the nine contemplations described earlier, hence it is called "contemplating form without." This first liberation stands at the stage of the desire-realm concentration.

As this practice advances further, one perceives the skeleton emitting four colors — blue-green, yellow, white, and dove-gray — glowing and shimmering, on the very verge of manifesting fully yet not quite manifest. The blue-green color's blue-green light, and likewise for each of the others down to the dove-gray color's dove-gray light, resembles a flowing stream, its light enveloping the skeleton as though seen through mist or a hazy mirror in sunlight. Should the mind attend to the feet, the light correspondingly moves downward; should the mind attend to the head, the light correspondingly moves upward. Through the very power of the blue-green light, it illuminates and pervades the ten directions, so that one perceives blue-green everywhere, as though Mount Sumeru itself, however one turns to face it, showed but a single color throughout — and likewise for the dove-gray color as well. While this colored light remains on the verge of manifesting yet not quite manifest, one stands at the stage of the concentration of approach. Persisting in this way for a long while, the light ought eventually to manifest of its own accord; should it fail to do so, one should gather the mind and contemplate carefully the point between the eyebrows, and, releasing one's attention there, it will then manifest. It appears like smoke issuing from a bamboo pipe: small and slight at first, then gradually dispersing and expanding; the four colors, unfolding one after another, issue forth from between the eyebrows, illuminating the ten directions everywhere at once, opening suddenly into vast brilliance. Each single color, too, carries its own ten merits, eight sensations, five branch-factors, and marks of correctness or deviance, exactly as before. The initial arising of the color is named "applied thought"; distinguishing the eight colors is named "sustained thought." Though one previously already knew that bone lay within the flesh, one did not yet know of these eight colors within the bone itself — rejoicing at what one has never before perceived, mingled with sorrow and shame, is named "the joy branch-factor." When this color first arises, one experiences a profound bliss in the dharma, the very ground of the mind serene and joyful — this is named "the bliss branch-factor." The concentrated mind grows deep and clear, abiding securely and unmoving, its darkness deepening ever further; empty, luminous, wise, and settled, filled with faith, reverence, shame, and remorse, giving rise to no slander or ill will, in accord with freedom from every hindrance. Whether cold, warmth, or the rest, all arise together in thick clusters, entirely free of confusion or error — hence this is termed "a thicket-forest" of accompanying qualities. Yet the motion, itching, emptiness, brightness, five branch-factors, and other marks found here differ from the root dhyānas, for the mind's eye has opened into brightness and the dharma here is deep and its bliss weighty; they differ, too, from the special excellences and the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna, for those still retain the skin and flesh, and their contact remains not fully unobstructed, whereas here one makes contact directly with the bare skeleton itself, this very dharma being correspondingly deep and subtle. As for the deviant marks that may enter among these eight colors: should one, for instance, perceive the blue-green color only indistinctly, mottled and unattractive, this is precisely a deviant mark — and likewise for the remaining seven colors. Dark realization, lacking contemplative insight, is like a night filled with many thieves; this dhyāna, possessing contemplative insight, is like daylight, in which deception is scarce, and, should it arise at all, is readily dispelled. Should the Tripiṭaka teaching hold that "these eight colors are dharmas of the form realm, coming into contact with the skeleton of the desire realm, thereby producing these various merits," this represents an account given strictly according to the root dhyānas' own contaminated nature. The Mahāyāna's clarification of precept, concentration, and wisdom holds that none of these can ever be entirely exhausted — why is this so? For though this present life expires and the precepts taken for it thereby lapse, the "non-action" merit generated by them does not itself perish. Concentration, though it may subdue affliction, will inevitably sever it before long, like a parasite that has entered the body, damaging the organs and threatening life — though not yet dead, its condition cannot long endure. The path of wisdom suffers no such loss — the stream-enterer's own seven remaining rebirths, and the uncontaminated itself, remain constant and unbroken throughout. One should understand, then, that precept and concentration are themselves uncontaminated dharmas. If this is so, then the very light of these eight colors is itself a dharma beyond the three realms. Should this mark manifest, the first liberation is thereby accomplished, standing at the stage of the first dhyāna. The *Satyasiddhi* says: "The first two liberations belong to the desire realm; the pure liberation belongs to the form realm; the four liberations [of formlessness] belong to the formless realm; the liberation of cessation transcends all three realms." The *Abhidharma* says: "The first two liberations pertain jointly to the desire realm and the second dhyāna; the pure liberation lies within the fourth dhyāna; as for the third dhyāna, since its bliss so predominates there, no liberation is established for it." Still others hold: "The third dhyāna has no basis of mastery; the fourth dhyāna has no liberation." These three accounts differ from one another. Here, following the *Great Treatise*, the first liberation and the second basis of mastery both belong to the first dhyāna — since they already possess five branch-factors, this confirms that they belong to the first dhyāna. Second, possessing no form within, one contemplates form without with a mind of impurity: the skeleton is itself formed from sexual fluid and blood, and ought properly to be rebuked and dissolved, its bones analyzed down to the four subtle elements. The Mahāyāna's own embodying method recognizes that the skeleton arises from the mind itself, the mind being like an illusory transformation — the skeleton is thus empty and unreal, and dissolves of its own accord. It is like a fine horse that follows its rider's every intention, or like a capable person engaged in some joint task, coming and going without the slightest resistance. Once the skeleton has departed, and the new dharma has not yet arrived, one is prone to excessive joy and consequent retreat. Here, with a mind of impurity, one contemplates only form without — "without" referring to the corpse and the rest external to oneself, and also referring to the eight colors emitted by the skeleton itself. The reason for contemplating form without at this stage is that one still remains comparatively close to the desire realm, and must therefore contemplate outer impurity as well. As for the actual method of destroying the skeleton, this is treated separately elsewhere; here only its manifestation is discussed. One suddenly perceives the skeleton dissolving away of its own accord, leaving only the eight colors and the outer impurity remaining. At the moment the skeleton itself dissolves, one stands at an intermediate stage; one further perceives the eight colors arising together, simultaneously, with the inner purity, the blue-green and other colors undergoing a further round of intensification. Inner purity, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness, these four branch-factors' merit now surpassing what came before — this is the second liberation, standing at the stage of the second dhyāna.

三、淨背捨身作證者：初禪、二禪非遍身樂，四禪無樂何所為證？《成論》人四禪共淨背捨。今以兩禪共淨背捨。既言三禪有遍身樂，可以為證，即是其初，成就在四禪，能具足勝處。故知淨背捨位在三禪也。淨者，《釋論》云：「緣淨故淨。」八色已是淨法，而未被淨緣瑩練。淨色極在四禪，此色起時瑩於八色，更轉明淨，故言「緣淨故淨」。遍身受者，樂之極在三禪，故總此二禪為淨背捨也。淨有四義：不淨不淨者，欲界之身已是不淨，而今膖脹，故言「不淨不淨」；不淨淨者，除却皮肉，諦觀白骨，無腹筋血，如珂如貝，故言「不淨淨」；淨不淨者，是眉間所出八色光明，光明是淨，未被練治，故言「淨不淨」也；淨淨是第三背捨，更被淨緣練治也，故言「淨淨」。四、空背捨者：過一切色，滅有對色，不念種種色。「一切色」是欲界內、外色；「有對」是五根所對；此兩色前三背捨已滅。但有八色隨心轉變，故言「種種色」。呵色緣空，更無別法，但入空定。若凡夫多染，保著空定；聖人深心、智慧利，直去不迴，故名「背捨」。若緣空多則散，虛誑不實，捨空緣識，識法相應，名「識處背捨」。又識生滅無常虛誑，無復所緣，但有能緣，故言「無所有處」。識處如癰，無所有處如瘡；捨識、無識，即是「非想非非想」。此無想猶有細煩惱。今捨能緣非想之受想，亦無復能滅之想，定法持身泯然無想，如水魚、蟄蟲。若以所滅為名，與攀上厭下何異？今從能滅自地亦滅他地得名，故言「滅受想背捨」。具如《修證》中說。《毘曇》明得滅定是「俱解脫」；不得此一定但名「慧解脫」。《成論》得電光名「慧解脫」；具得世間禪名「俱解脫」。《成論》後四更無別法，以無漏心修此可然。前三何意無別法而約外道禪耶云云？若過去曾得八定，故發宿習；而滅定一種，不得無漏，修則不成，故不論宿習也。九次第定、超越等，約三藏者，無有凡人修於此定，故不論發宿習也。若約大乘亦應有此義，今所不論。

Third, the pure liberation realized bodily: neither the first nor the second dhyāna involves bliss felt throughout the entire body, while the fourth dhyāna has no bliss at all — with what, then, could this liberation possibly be realized? Masters of the *Satyasiddhi* hold that the fourth dhyāna together constitutes the pure liberation. Here, instead, two dhyānas together constitute the pure liberation: since it is said that the third dhyāna already involves bliss felt throughout the entire body, this may serve as its realization — this being its very starting point, its accomplishment occurring in the fourth dhyāna, where it is able to become fully complete with the bases of mastery. One should therefore understand that the pure liberation stands at the stage of the third dhyāna. As for "pure": the *Great Treatise* says, "made pure through a pure object." The eight colors are already a pure dharma, yet not yet refined and polished by a pure object. Pure form reaches its culmination in the fourth dhyāna, and, once this form arises, it further refines and polishes the eight colors, rendering them still more luminous and pure — hence it is said, "made pure through a pure object." As for "felt throughout the entire body": the culmination of bliss lies in the third dhyāna, hence these two dhyānas together are named the pure liberation. Purity itself carries a fourfold sense: "impure-impure" refers to the body of the desire realm, already impure in itself, now further bloated and distended — hence it is called "impure-impure"; "impure-pure" refers to removing away the skin and flesh to contemplate carefully the bare white bones, free of belly, sinew, and blood, resembling mother-of-pearl or shell — hence it is called "impure-pure"; "pure-impure" refers to the eight-colored light issuing from between the eyebrows — this light is itself pure, yet not yet refined and treated — hence it is called "pure-impure"; and "pure-pure" is precisely this third liberation, further refined and treated by a pure object — hence it is called "pure-pure." Fourth, the liberation of emptiness: transcending every form, extinguishing form possessing resistance, no longer conceiving of any form whatsoever of any kind. "Every form" refers to the internal and external forms of the desire realm; "possessing resistance" refers to what the five faculties encounter as their objects — both these kinds of form have already been extinguished by the first three liberations. There remain only the eight colors, transforming along with the mind, hence these are called "form of every kind." Rebuking form and taking emptiness as one's object, with no further separate dharma involved, one simply enters the concentration of emptiness. Ordinary beings, being heavily attached, tend to cling fast to this concentration of emptiness; sages, whose minds run deep and whose wisdom is sharp, proceed straight onward without turning back — hence this is named "turning away." Should one take emptiness excessively as one's object, this leads to scattering, an unreal and deceptive state; relinquishing emptiness, one takes consciousness as one's object instead, corresponding to the dharma of consciousness — this is named "the liberation of the sphere of consciousness." Further, consciousness, arising and ceasing, is itself impermanent, unreal, and deceptive, with no further object left to grasp, only the grasping faculty itself remaining — hence this is called "the sphere of nothingness." The sphere of consciousness is like an abscess; the sphere of nothingness is like an open sore. Relinquishing both consciousness and its very absence is precisely "neither-perception-nor-non-perception" — yet this state of no-perception still harbors subtle affliction within it. Here, one relinquishes even the very grasping of the feeling and perception belonging to this neither-perception state, and no further perception of even this act of relinquishing remains — the dharma of concentration sustaining the body in utter, dissolved non-perception, like a fish submerged in water or a hibernating insect. Were this liberation named merely after what it extinguishes, how would it differ from grasping upward while merely feeling aversion toward what lies below? Here, instead, it is named from the very fact that it extinguishes not only its own stage but every other stage as well — hence it is called "the liberation of the cessation of feeling and perception." This is explained in full within the *Sequential Dharma-Gates*' own cultivation-and-verification manual. The *Abhidharma* holds that one who attains the concentration of cessation is "both-liberated"; one who fails to attain this single concentration is named merely "liberated-through-wisdom." The *Satyasiddhi* holds that one who attains the lightning-flash is named "liberated-through-wisdom," while one who is fully complete with the mundane dhyānas is named "both-liberated." The *Satyasiddhi* holds that the last four liberations involve no further separate dharma of their own, since it is possible to cultivate these with an uncontaminated mind — yet what, then, is the reason that the first three should involve no separate dharma, being treated strictly according to non-Buddhist dhyāna? Should one have already attained the eight concentrations in a past life, this produces the arising of habituation from that past life — but the concentration of cessation alone does not produce the uncontaminated, and cultivation of it alone cannot be accomplished, hence habituation from past lives is not discussed with respect to it. As for the nine sequential concentrations, the transcendent concentration, and the rest, treated according to the Tripiṭaka teaching, there exists no ordinary being who cultivates these concentrations at all, hence the arising of habituation from past lives is likewise not discussed with respect to them. Treated according to the Mahāyāna, this same sense ought properly to apply as well, though it is not discussed here.

○次、明大不淨觀發者，亦名「大背捨」。前所觀、所發，除欲皮肉，諦觀骨人、死屍不淨，或一屍、兩屍，城邑聚落不淨流溢等，但約自、他正報，故言「小不淨」也。約此而論厭背，故名「背捨」，亦是總、別相云云。若大不淨觀，何但正報流溢不淨？依報：宅宇、錢財、穀米、衣服、飲食、山河、園林、江淮、池沼，絓是色法，悉皆不淨。蟲膿流出，臭處腥臊，舍如丘墓，錢如死蛇，羹如屎汁，飯如白蟲，衣如臭皮，山如肉聚，池如膿河，園林如枯骨，江海如汪穢。《大經》云：「美羹作穢汁想。」即此觀也。於坐禪中忽如上見，見此大地無一好處，依正不復可貪，是名「大不淨發」也。如初然火，加功攢發，烟炎蓋微；火既成勢，不復擇薪，乃至江河亦能乾竭。初觀不淨，止一屍、一國，婬心乍興乍廢。今定力已成，厭惡亦盛，一切依正無非不淨，欲心永息。復次，諸物有何定相？隨人果報感見不同，善業感淨色，惡業感不淨色。如諸天寶地、寶宮；人中富樂，執諸瓦石變成金銀。善力所招，依正俱淨。如《僧護經》所說地獄，獄相不同：或見身肉為地，為他所耕；或見身如樹林，眾所摧折；或身如山，如屋、如衣，凡一百二十種，皆惡業所感招不淨色。若執淨色，保愛堅固，以大觀力破大著心，翻大顛倒，成大不淨觀也。何以故？夫幻術法多是欺誑；神通法得其道理，凡一切物皆可轉變。如蘇、蠟、金、鐵，遇煖流變如水；遇冷成地。此得解觀，契轉變之道，定力爾故。若根本，但除下地著，不能除自地；若小、大背捨，未是無漏，但除下地、自地著；若無漏緣通，則下、自、上皆除著也。若人發大不淨，入背捨亦大，初禪攝。若內無骨人，外觀八色及依正兩報，緣境大故，名第二大背捨，二禪攝。若以大不淨入淨背捨亦大，乃至滅背捨亦如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the manifestation of the great impurity contemplation, also named "the great turning away." What was contemplated and manifested earlier — setting aside the skin and flesh, contemplating carefully the impurity of the skeleton and the corpse, whether one corpse or two, the impurity overflowing throughout an entire city or village, and the rest — pertained strictly to the direct retribution of oneself and others, and was therefore called "the lesser impurity." It was in terms of this that world-weariness and turning away were discussed, hence it was named "turning away" — this too involving both a general and a particular sense, and so on. As for the great impurity contemplation, why should it be confined merely to the overflowing impurity of direct retribution alone? Dependent retribution, too — dwellings, wealth, grain, clothing, food and drink, mountains and rivers, gardens and groves, rivers and lakes, ponds and pools — being, every one of them, form-dharmas, are likewise, without exception, impure. Worms and pus flow forth, foul and rank odors arise: a dwelling is like a burial mound, coins are like dead snakes, broth is like a filthy fluid, cooked rice is like white maggots, clothing is like foul skin, a mountain is like a heap of flesh, a pond is like a river of pus, a garden or grove is like a pile of bare bones, and rivers and seas are like pools of filth. The Great Sūtra says: "Regard even a delicious broth as though it were a filthy fluid" — this is precisely this very contemplation. During seated meditation, one suddenly perceives things exactly as described above, perceiving this entire earth as possessing not a single good place anywhere, so that neither direct nor dependent retribution can any longer be craved at all — this is named "the manifestation of the great impurity." It is like a fire first kindled: with effort applied and fuel gathered, the smoke and flame remain at first faint and slight; but once the fire has gathered full force, it no longer discriminates among the fuel it consumes, and can eventually dry up and exhaust even a great river itself. The initial contemplation of impurity was confined to a single corpse, a single country, and lustful desire still rose and subsided by turns; but now that the power of concentration is already fully accomplished, world-weariness, too, grows correspondingly powerful, so that every form of direct and dependent retribution alike is recognized as nothing but impurity, and desire is thereby forever stilled. Furthermore, do these various things possess any fixed mark of their own at all? Each is perceived differently according to the particular retribution of the person perceiving it — wholesome karma perceives pure form, unwholesome karma perceives impure form. As with the jeweled ground and jeweled palaces of the various heavens; or with wealth and pleasure among humankind, where one grasps mere tile and stone and finds them transformed into gold and silver — these being summoned forth by the very power of wholesome karma, both direct and dependent retribution alike appearing pure. As the *Sūtra of Saṃgharakṣa* describes the hells, whose very marks differ one from another: some perceive their own flesh as the very ground itself, plowed over by others; some perceive their own body as a forest, broken and felled by the crowd; some perceive their body as a mountain, or as a house, or as clothing — one hundred and twenty kinds in all, every one of them impure form summoned forth by unwholesome karma. Should one grasp firmly at pure form, cherishing and clinging to it with unshakable tenacity, one must apply the power of this great contemplation to break through this very great attachment, overturning this great inversion, thereby accomplishing the great impurity contemplation. Why is this so? For illusionist tricks are, for the most part, mere deception and fraud, whereas the methods of spiritual power grasp the genuine principle of the matter, whereby every object whatsoever, without exception, is capable of being transformed. It is like butter, wax, gold, or iron, which, meeting warmth, flow and transform into liquid, and, meeting cold, solidify into a hardened mass — this understanding-based contemplation accords precisely with this very principle of transformation, through the power of concentration acting in just this way. As for the root dhyānas, they remove only attachment to the lower stage, without being able to remove attachment to one's own present stage; as for the lesser and greater liberations, not yet being uncontaminated, they remove attachment to both the lower stage and one's own present stage; but only an uncontaminated object, penetrating fully, removes attachment to the lower, one's own, and the higher stages all at once. Should a person's great impurity contemplation manifest, the liberation he thereby enters is likewise great, belonging to the first dhyāna. Should there be no skeleton remaining within, while one contemplates the eight colors together with both direct and dependent retribution without, this object being correspondingly vast, this is named the second great liberation, belonging to the second dhyāna. Should the great impurity lead one into the pure liberation, this too is correspondingly great, and likewise extending all the way to the liberation of cessation.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the great bases of mastery: these further ripen the liberations, so that one gains complete freedom to transform one's very object at will. The *Great Treatise* explains that a person of dull faculties, having completed the eight liberations, only then cultivates the bases of mastery and the totality-bases; a person of middling faculties, having completed the first three liberations, cultivates the bases of mastery within the four dhyānas; a person of superior faculties, having cultivated only the first liberation, thereby cultivates every dharma of this kind at once. Here the matter shall be treated as pertaining to a middling case. As for "many" or "few": this, too, is treated in terms of direct and dependent retribution — a single corpse is "few," two corpses are "many," and this pattern may be extended indefinitely; a single garment, a single meal, a single mountain or river is "few," while immeasurable garments, meals, mountains, and rivers are "many." As the initial cultivation proceeds from few to many, so too ought its later manifestation to proceed in just the same way. As for "beautiful" or "ugly": form that is upright and comely through wholesome karma is "beautiful," form that is base and unsightly through unwholesome karma is "ugly." Alternatively, whatever appears pleasing to oneself is "beautiful," whatever appears displeasing to oneself is "ugly." Alternatively, whatever possesses wisdom is "beautiful," whatever possesses folly is "ugly." Alternatively, wealth and nobility are "beautiful," poverty and lowliness are "ugly." Yet, whether beautiful or ugly in any of these senses, both alike remain impure. Mountains and rivers, lands and countries, clothing and food, houses and dwellings — whether beautiful or ugly, both alike remain impure. Further, both direct and dependent retribution alike are ugly, while the eight colors emitted by the skeleton are beautiful; yet these eight colors, too, are in another sense ugly, while once refined and polished they become beautiful — beautiful and ugly alike, in every case, remain impure. These first two bases of mastery belong to the first dhyāna. As for possessing no mark of form within while contemplating form without, whether many or few, whether beautiful or ugly, with mastery of knowledge and mastery of vision: this refers to the skeleton dissolved away within, while the eight colors remain without, together with direct and dependent retribution, many or few, beautiful or ugly, exactly as explained above, and so on.

勝知見者：此心勝色，不為色所縛，心能轉色，故言「勝知」也；勝見者，淨不淨等皆於己心自在，觀解成就，故言「勝見」也。此兩，二禪攝。若勝處成時，身尚不惜，況財物、他身耶？上古賢人推位讓國、還牛洗耳，皆是昔生經修此觀，自然成性，無復愛染。不得此意，貪之至死，何能忽榮棄位耶？後四勝處，在四禪中成就。三禪樂多，不能轉變。就聲聞法，謂言如此；於菩薩法，禪禪轉變，何得無耶？《大論》云「青、黃、赤、白」，此從實法；《瓔珞》云「地、水、火、風」，此從假名，互得相攝。此四勝處，內外色盡，但有八色；唯有多少轉變，無有好醜轉變也。十一切處在四禪中。初禪覺觀多，二禪喜動，三禪樂動，不得廣普，遍一切處；唯不動念慧則能廣普。以青遍十方，十方皆青；餘色亦爾，故名「一切處」。若「一切入」者：以青遍一切時，黃來入青，亦遍一切處。青黃本相不失，相入又不相濫。餘色相入亦如是。是名「一切入」。此乃內心放色遍一切處，那得以外樹葉為緣遍一切處耶？內心無法，安能轉變外樹葉耶？先能變心，方能變葉耳。《大論》取優鉢羅華者，恐人不解，借外喻內，不可執喻為正義。若通明觀，內無骨人，不放八色；修勝處時當借外緣，或可應爾。不壞法人，內自放，不須外也。復次，菩薩修勝處具眾行者：若不達依正，可起貪、慳；此觀若明，身尚欲捨，況惜己物而貪他財？是則名「檀」。得如此觀，不為財色而破於戒，害彼財主，引物自歸，欺詐百端而求全濟，決無此理，是則名「尸」。得此觀時，若他觸惱及以侵奪，終不生瞋，諍於糞穢，是則名「忍」。是觀成時，不倚不淨屍身、不淨國土間退定心，是名「精進」。此觀能具觀練熏修、神通變化、願智頂等，是則名「禪」。得此觀時，一切法能所皆不可得，不生不滅畢竟清淨，是則名「慧」。一切道定法門，皆於勝處轉變成就。心定自在，迴轉去住，作諸法門，隨心即成。如快馬破陣，亦自制住。是時明淨，無復魔事。心使於魔，魔不能破心也。行四三昧人，若發得此法，多轉入五品弟子位。何故爾？助道力大，能疾近清涼池。齊此，是發觀禪，亦是發摩訶衍禪相。若練熏修，凡夫尚不得學，無發可論。若別出經論，故不俟言也。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for mastery of knowledge: this mind masters form, no longer bound by form, the mind itself now able to transform form at will — hence it is named "mastery of knowledge." As for mastery of vision: purity, impurity, and every such distinction are now entirely within one's own mind's free command, one's contemplative understanding fully accomplished — hence it is named "mastery of vision." These two together belong to the second dhyāna. Once the bases of mastery are accomplished, one no longer even cherishes one's own body — how much less, then, wealth, possessions, or another's body? The sages of ancient times who relinquished the throne and yielded the kingdom, or who washed their ears at the mere mention of high office, all attained this same contemplation through cultivation in past lives, its nature becoming spontaneous and second nature to them, free of any further craving-attachment whatsoever. Without grasping this very sense, one clings to such things even unto death — how, then, could one possibly relinquish glory and abandon high office so readily? The remaining four bases of mastery are accomplished within the four dhyānas — yet, since the third dhyāna's bliss so predominates there, transformation cannot be accomplished within it. This holds true according to the śrāvaka's own teaching; but within the bodhisattva's teaching, transformation occurs within every single dhyāna without exception — how, then, could this one alone be excepted? The *Great Treatise* speaks of "blue-green, yellow, red, and white" in terms of the real dharma; the *Yingluo Sūtra* speaks of "earth, water, fire, and wind" in terms of conventional designation — the two accounts are mutually inclusive of one another. In these four bases of mastery, both internal and external form are entirely exhausted, leaving only the eight colors remaining — involving only transformation in quantity, many or few, without any transformation in quality, beautiful or ugly. The ten totality-bases occur within the four dhyānas. In the first dhyāna, applied and sustained thought predominate; in the second, joy remains in motion; in the third, bliss remains in motion — in none of these can the totality-base extend broadly and universally, pervading every place at once; only the unmoving wisdom of pure thought is able to extend broadly and universally in this way. Taking blue-green to pervade the ten directions, the ten directions become entirely blue-green; and likewise for every other color — hence this is named "the totality-base." As for "the totality-entry": taking blue-green to pervade every place, yellow then enters into this very blue-green, itself likewise pervading every place at once — the original marks of blue and yellow neither lost nor confusedly intermingled with one another. The remaining colors' mutual entry follows this same pattern — this is named "the totality-entry." This, then, is a matter of the inner mind projecting color to pervade every place — how, then, could this possibly be accomplished by taking some outer leaf of a tree as one's object, so as to pervade every place through it? Without the inner mind's own capacity, how could one possibly transform an outer leaf at all? Only once the mind itself can be transformed is one then able to transform the leaf as well. The *Great Treatise*'s reference to the blue lotus flower is meant only to aid understanding for those who might otherwise fail to grasp it, borrowing an outer illustration to clarify what is inward — one must not cling to this illustration as the true, primary meaning itself. In the penetrating-and-clear contemplation, there is no skeleton within, and no eight colors are emitted — when cultivating the bases of mastery in this context, borrowing an external condition may then indeed be appropriate. For the dharma-preserving practitioner, however, the projection occurs from within, requiring nothing external at all. Furthermore, the bodhisattva's cultivation of the bases of mastery is complete with every one of the practices: should one fail to penetrate direct and dependent retribution, greed and stinginess may readily arise — but once this contemplation is clear, one is willing to relinquish even one's own body, how much less, then, cling stingily to one's own possessions or covet another's wealth? This alone is named "generosity." Attaining this contemplation, one no longer breaks the precepts for the sake of wealth or desire, no longer harms the owner of some property, seizing it for oneself, nor practices deception in a hundred different ways merely to secure one's own survival — such conduct becomes altogether impossible for one who has attained it — this is named "moral discipline." Attaining this contemplation, should another cause one vexation or even seize one's belongings by force, one never gives rise to anger, no more than one would quarrel over a heap of filth — this is named "forbearance." Once this contemplation is accomplished, one no longer relies upon impure corpses or impure lands as an excuse to let the concentrated mind retreat — this is named "diligence." This contemplation is able to encompass contemplation, refinement, permeation, cultivation, spiritual powers and transformations, and the dhyānas of aspiration, wisdom, and the summit, all at once — this is named "dhyāna." Attaining this contemplation, every dharma, whether agent or object, proves altogether unobtainable, neither arising nor ceasing, ultimately pure — this is named "wisdom." Every dharma-gate of the path and of concentration is accomplished through transformation within these very bases of mastery. The mind, settled and free, turns about at will, departing and remaining as it pleases, producing every dharma-gate in accordance with its own intention the very instant it is willed — like a swift horse breaking through the enemy's ranks yet also able to rein itself in of its own accord. At this stage, the mind is bright and pure, free of any further demonic affair — the mind itself now commands Māra, and Māra is no longer able to break through the mind at all. Should a practitioner of the four kinds of samādhi manifest this very dharma, he is, for the most part, thereby carried directly into the stage of the five grades of disciple. Why is this so? Because the power of this auxiliary practice is so great that it swiftly approaches the very pool of coolness. Up to this point, what has manifested is the dhyāna of contemplation, and is likewise the manifestation of the very mark of Mahāyāna dhyāna itself. As for refinement, permeation, and cultivation, even an ordinary being is not permitted to study these at all — hence there is nothing further here to discuss regarding their manifestation; these are treated separately elsewhere in the scriptures and treatises, and need not be dwelt upon further here.

Loving-Kindness: Wishing All Beings Happiness 七明慈心發相

○七、明慈心發者：慈倚根本前後云云。忽緣一切眾生，取其樂相，無怨無惱，悅心適意；或見得人中樂，或見得天上樂。善修得解，定心分明，無一眾生不得樂者。初躡躡細靜，後轉深定。但所緣有三：若緣親人得樂，名「廣」；中人，名「大」；怨人，名「無量」。又緣一方眾生得樂，名「廣」；四維，名「大」；十方，名「無量」。此定有隱沒、不隱沒。若心緣眾生，決定作得樂想，心甚分明，而所緣處不見眾生得受於樂，是內不隱沒而外隱沒。復有內心明淨、外見得樂，是為內、外俱不隱沒。若先得此定，後發五支功德者：初覺眾生悉皆得樂，心與定合，自心亦樂，善修得解，名「覺支」。分別得樂，或人中、天上無量差品，皆悉明了，名「觀支」。怨親平等，無復畏怨憂親之苦，名「喜支」。喜支動息，心神愉懌，亦如所緣得樂之相，名「樂支」。定法轉深，持心不動，名「一心支」。此名同根本，而法味永異。如糖蜜和水，冷同味別。若發單根本，報止梵眾、梵輔；若得慈定，則報為梵王。其果既勝，因亦大矣。若先得根本，後加慈定，根本益深也。又於慈定中發二禪，內淨四支成就；又發三禪，樂具五支成就；又發四禪，一一與諸禪相應，支林具足而法味倍增，如前喻。但慈心本緣他得樂，內受樂定、外見他樂，此相齊三禪。四禪但見他得樂，內無樂受，以捨苦樂故，是為小乘如此分別。佛或時破執為緣，言：「慈心，福至遍淨；悲心，福至空處；喜心，福至識處；捨心，福至不用處。」但菩薩恒與慈悲俱，何地而無慈悲？慈悲熏一切善，豈止齊三禪耶？此一往語耳。若先發根本，後發慈定亦如是。然皆闇證隱沒；或內不隱沒而外隱沒云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, clarifying the manifestation of loving-kindness: loving-kindness may depend upon the root dhyānas, arising either before or after them, and so on. One suddenly extends this attention to every sentient being, apprehending their happiness directly, free of any enmity or vexation, the mind joyful and at ease — perceiving some attaining human happiness, others attaining heavenly happiness. Skillfully cultivated and understood, the concentrated mind grows clear and distinct, until there remains not a single sentient being who fails to attain happiness. At first this proceeds gradually, subtle and still; afterward it deepens into a fuller concentration. Its object admits of three degrees: extending to a beloved person's attainment of happiness is named "broad"; to a neutral person, "great"; to an enemy, "immeasurable." Alternatively, extending to the happiness of sentient beings in a single direction is named "broad"; to the four intermediate directions as well, "great"; to the ten directions entire, "immeasurable." This concentration admits of both a concealed and an unconcealed mode. Should the mind, extending to sentient beings, form a firm conviction of their attaining happiness, this conviction being altogether clear and distinct, and yet the very place one attends to shows no visible sign of beings actually receiving happiness — this is unconcealed within, yet concealed without. There is, further, a mode in which the inner mind is bright and pure while the attainment of happiness is also visibly perceived without — this is unconcealed both within and without. Should one first attain this concentration, and only afterward manifest its five accompanying branch-factors: the initial awareness that every sentient being, without exception, has attained happiness, the mind united with this concentration, one's own mind thereby joyful as well, skillfully cultivated and understood, is named "the applied-thought branch-factor." Distinguishing this attainment of happiness — whether among humans or in the heavens, in whatever immeasurable variety of grades — with complete clarity, is named "the sustained-thought branch-factor." Enemy and beloved regarded with perfect equanimity, free of any further suffering born of dread toward an enemy or anxious concern for a loved one, is named "the joy branch-factor." This joy, once stirred, subsiding into stillness, the mind and spirit made joyfully content, resembling the very mark of happiness attended to as one's object, is named "the bliss branch-factor." The dharma of concentration deepening further, sustaining the mind unmoving, is named "the one-pointedness branch-factor." Though these branch-factors share the same names as those of the root dhyānas, the flavor of their dharma differs permanently from them — like sugar-syrup mixed with water compared to plain water, cold in the same way yet utterly different in flavor. Should one manifest the root dhyānas alone, unaccompanied, their retribution is confined merely to the Brahmā-multitude or Brahmā-ministers heavens; but should one attain the concentration of loving-kindness, its retribution becomes rebirth as the very Great Brahmā king himself. Since its fruit is thus superior, its cause, too, is correspondingly greater. In the fourth dhyāna, one merely perceives another's attainment of happiness, without any inward reception of bliss oneself, since suffering and bliss are both alike relinquished there — this is how the Hīnayāna makes this particular distinction. The Buddha, at times, breaks through this very attachment as an object of teaching, saying: "Loving-kindness's merit reaches as far as the heaven of universal purity; compassion's merit reaches the sphere of emptiness; sympathetic joy's merit reaches the sphere of consciousness; equanimity's merit reaches the sphere of nothingness" — yet the bodhisattva is constantly accompanied by loving-kindness and compassion at every stage; in what stage, then, could loving-kindness and compassion possibly be absent? At the very outset of the treatise, causes and conditions are contemplated in general; then, in the "Chapter on Defilement and the Defiled" and elsewhere, the links of craving and grasping are refuted specifically; in the "Chapter on the Sense-Faculties," the link of suffering is refuted specifically; and, extending to the final two chapters, the śrāvaka's own contemplation of dependent origination is set forth specifically. Whether treated generally or specifically, in every case it is dependent origination that is being contemplated — how, then, could it fail to constitute the treatise's own central doctrine? Northern masters, taking up the corrective argument found within these final chapters, hold that the six causes and four conditions constitute the treatise's central doctrine — yet this pertains merely to the arising-and-ceasing form of dependent origination, and the sense of these final two chapters does not represent the treatise's own true, central doctrine. 今既發因緣法，故約之明止觀，例為十意云云。思議境者：過去無明心中作於黑業諸不善行，成三途界；作諸白業及不動業，成三善界。若轉無明為生滅明，名「下智觀」，得聲聞菩提。轉有漏行為出世助道行，七種學人殘業未盡，猶生善界。若無學用無漏業及著真諦，愛與根本無明合，生方便土，受彼名色，於彼愛、瞋而起取、有，是聲聞界。若翻無明為不生不滅之明，是則中智，得緣覺菩提。《請觀音》云：「觀十二因緣如夢、幻、芭蕉，成緣覺道。」意在於此。轉有漏行為無漏助道，結業盡、不盡，同前，是為緣覺界。若轉無明為般若、轉不善行為五度，以未發真，猶具界內十二因緣，是六度界。若轉無明為空慧，轉行為六度，六、七地前斷惑未盡，皆同前。斷盡生彼，福慧小勝耳，是名「中智觀」，得通教菩提。若轉無明為次第明，轉行為歷別行，十信、住斷未盡，十行、向斷盡，皆同前。是名上智觀，故得別教菩提。若轉無明為佛智明，從初發心知十二緣是三佛性。若通觀十二緣真如實理是正因佛性；觀十二因緣智慧是了因佛性；觀十二緣心具足諸行是緣因佛性。若別觀者：無明、愛、取，即了因佛性；行、有，即緣因佛性；識等七支，即正因佛性。何以故？苦道是生死，變生死身即法身；煩惱是闇法，轉無明為明；業行是縛法，變縛成解脫。即三道是三德。性得因時不縱不橫，名「三佛性」；修得果時不縱不橫，如世伊字，名「三德涅槃」。《淨名》云：「一切眾生即大涅槃，即是佛，即是菩提。」乃此意也。是名「上上智觀」，得佛菩提。若五品未斷，同學人；鐵輪長別苦海，同無學。雖復變易，五根生福逈異。《釋論》云：「二乘受法性身，諸根闇鈍，以其於佛道紆迴故。」若別、圓，能破無明，直開苦道如實之法，從實法得實報，直於行、有，具足諸行，感得依正無有罣礙。根利福深，不同中下。若三賢、十聖，住於果報，悉成就彼十二因緣。等覺，餘有一生因緣在。若最後窮無明源，愛取畢竟盡，故名「究竟般若」；識等七果盡，故名「究竟法身」；行、有盡，名「究竟解脫」。雖言斷盡，無所可斷，不思議斷。不斷無明、愛、取而入圓淨涅槃；不斷名、色七支而入性淨涅槃；不斷行、有善惡而入方便淨涅槃。《淨名》云：「以五逆相而得解脫，亦不縛不脫。」如此而推，十二因緣即是一切無量佛法，是名「不可思識境」也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now that this very dharma of dependent origination has manifested, calming-and-contemplation shall be clarified in terms of it, treated according to the same pattern in ten parts, and so on. As for the discursible object: within the mind of past ignorance, one performs dark, unwholesome acts, thereby forming the realms of the three lower paths; or performs pure acts and unshakable acts, thereby forming the realms of the three wholesome paths. Should one transform ignorance into the light of arising-and-ceasing, this is named "inferior wisdom's contemplation," attaining the bodhi of the śrāvaka. Transforming contaminated formation into supramundane auxiliary practice of the path, the seven kinds of trainee, their remaining karma not yet exhausted, are still born within the wholesome realms. Should one beyond further training employ uncontaminated karma while still clinging to the real truth, craving combines with root ignorance, giving rise to birth in the land of skillful means, receiving there a corresponding name-and-form, and, through craving and aversion arising there, giving rise once more to grasping and existence — this is the realm of the śrāvaka. Should one transform ignorance into the light of the neither-arising-nor-ceasing, this is middling wisdom, attaining the bodhi of the pratyekabuddha. The *Sūtra of Petitioning Avalokiteśvara* says: "Contemplating the twelve links of dependent origination as a dream, an illusion, a plantain trunk, one accomplishes the path of the pratyekabuddha" — this carries precisely this same sense. Transforming contaminated formation into uncontaminated auxiliary practice, whether the fetters of karma are exhausted or not exhausted, following the same pattern as before, this is the realm of the pratyekabuddha. Should one transform ignorance into prajñā, and transform unwholesome formation into the five pāramitās, since the real truth has not yet been realized, the twelve links of dependent origination proper to the three realms remain still fully present — this is the realm of the six pāramitās. Should one transform ignorance into the wisdom of emptiness, and transform formation into the six pāramitās, then, prior to the sixth and seventh grounds, affliction not yet fully severed, this follows the same pattern as before; once severance is complete, one is born into that very land, one's merit and wisdom only slightly superior — this is named "middling wisdom's contemplation," attaining the bodhi of the shared teaching. Should one transform ignorance into the light of sequential progression, and transform formation into distinctly graded practice, then, through the ten stages of faith and the ten abidings, severance not yet complete, and through the ten practices and the ten transferences, severance complete, this follows the same pattern as before. This is named "superior wisdom's contemplation," attaining the bodhi of the distinct teaching. Should one transform ignorance into the light of the Buddha's own wisdom, then, from the very first arising of the bodhi-mind, one knows the twelve links to be the three buddha-natures themselves. Contemplated generally: the true suchness, the real principle of the twelve links, is the correct-cause buddha-nature; the wisdom that contemplates the twelve links is the complete-cause buddha-nature; the mind, complete with every formation, that contemplates the twelve links is the conditioning-cause buddha-nature. Contemplated distinctly: ignorance, craving, and grasping are precisely the complete-cause buddha-nature; formation and existence are precisely the conditioning-cause buddha-nature; consciousness and the remaining seven links are precisely the correct-cause buddha-nature. Why is this so? The path of suffering is birth-and-death itself, and transforming the body of birth-and-death is precisely the Dharma-body; affliction is a dark dharma, and transforming ignorance produces light; karmic action is a binding dharma, and transforming this binding accomplishes liberation — the three paths are thus precisely the three virtues. When these natures are obtained as cause, they are neither vertically sequential nor horizontally separate — this is named "the three buddha-natures"; when these are cultivated to fruition as result, they are likewise neither vertically sequential nor horizontally separate, like the worldly letter *i* — this is named "the nirvāṇa of the three virtues." The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* says: "Every sentient being is itself the great nirvāṇa, is itself the Buddha, is itself bodhi" — this carries precisely this same sense. This is named "the supreme wisdom's contemplation," attaining the very bodhi of the Buddha himself. Should the five grades of disciple not yet have achieved severance, this remains the same as one still in training; the iron-wheel stage, permanently separated from the sea of suffering, is the same as one beyond further training. Though there is, further, transformation-birth involved, the merit generated by the five faculties differs vastly from stage to stage. The Great Treatise says: "The two lesser vehicles receive only the Dharma-nature body, their faculties remaining dim and dull, since they take so circuitous a route toward the Buddha's own path." The distinct and perfect teachings, by contrast, are able to break through ignorance directly, opening at once the very real dharma of the path of suffering, obtaining a genuine retributive result directly from this real dharma, proceeding straight through formation and existence, complete with every practice, thereby attaining both direct and dependent retribution entirely unobstructed — their faculties sharp and their merit deep, differing altogether from the middling and lower capacities. The three worthies and ten sages, abiding within retributive result, all alike accomplish this very twelve-link dependent origination; at the stage of equal enlightenment, only a single further lifetime's worth of dependent origination remains. Only at the very final exhaustion of the source of ignorance, craving and grasping ultimately exhausted, is this named "ultimate prajñā"; consciousness and the remaining seven fruit-links exhausted, this is named "ultimate Dharma-body"; formation and existence exhausted, this is named "ultimate liberation." Though this is spoken of as "exhaustion" and "severance," there is, in truth, nothing whatsoever actually severed — this is an inconceivable severance. One enters the perfect, pure nirvāṇa without severing ignorance, craving, or grasping; one enters the nature-pure nirvāṇa without severing name-and-form and the remaining seven links; one enters the expedient-means pure nirvāṇa without severing the good and evil of formation and existence. The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* says: "Through the very marks of the five heinous transgressions one attains liberation, yet is neither bound nor liberated at all." Reasoning in this way, the twelve links of dependent origination are themselves precisely the whole of the Buddha's own immeasurable Dharma — this is named "an object beyond what discriminating consciousness alone can encompass."

The Twelve Links Correlated with the Lotus Sūtra's Ten Suchnesses and the Ten Objects of Contemplation 十二緣對十如 · 對十境

Chinese Source

復次，十二緣對《法華》中十如者：如是性對無明，《淨名》云：「若知無明性即是明性。」如是相對行；體對識等七支；力對愛、取；作對有因，又是無明、愛、取之習因；緣對行、有；果對無明生智慧習果；報對行、有五種涅槃；本對三道三種佛性；末對三德涅槃。復次對十境者：十法界陰入、病患兩境對識等七支；煩惱、見、慢等境對無明、愛、取；業、魔、禪、二乘、菩薩等對行、有等支。復次，十二因緣、十如、十境，在異心中是生滅思議；在一念心中是不生不滅、不可思議。《華嚴》云：「十二因緣在一念心中。」《大集》云：「十二因緣，一人一念悉皆具足。」此猶存略，若一人一念悉皆具足十界、十如、十二因緣，乃可稱為摩訶衍不可思議十二因緣耳。問：《十二門論》云：「緣法實無生。若謂為生者，為在一心中？為在眾心中？」亦可得言在一念耶？答：《華嚴》云：「一中無量；無量中一。」《大品》云：「一切法趣無明，是趣不過；乃至一切法趣老死。」今說一心具十二因緣，當有何咎？復次，言一念，不同世人取著一異定相一念，乃是非一非異而論一耳。譬如泯法覆心，一念之中夢無量世，事如《法華》云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, correlating the twelve links with the Lotus Sūtra's ten suchnesses: such-a-nature corresponds to ignorance — the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* says, "should one know the very nature of ignorance, this is itself the nature of light"; such-a-mark corresponds to formation; such-a-substance corresponds to consciousness and the remaining seven links; such-a-power corresponds to craving and grasping; such-a-function corresponds to the causal factor of existence, this being likewise the habituated cause of ignorance, craving, and grasping; such-a-condition corresponds to formation and existence; such-a-fruit corresponds to the habituated fruit of wisdom generated from ignorance; such-a-retribution corresponds to the five kinds of nirvāṇa generated from formation and existence; such-a-beginning corresponds to the three buddha-natures of the three paths; such-an-end corresponds to the nirvāṇa of the three virtues. Furthermore, correlating with the ten objects of contemplation: among the ten dharma-realms, the objects of the aggregates and of illness correspond to consciousness and the remaining seven links; the objects of affliction, views, and pride correspond to ignorance, craving, and grasping; the objects of karma, demons, meditative states, the two lesser vehicles, and the bodhisattva correspond to formation, existence, and the remaining links. Furthermore, the twelve links of dependent origination, the ten suchnesses, and the ten objects of contemplation, considered across separate, differing moments of mind, are discursible in terms of arising-and-ceasing; considered within a single thought-moment, they are neither arising nor ceasing, inconceivable. The *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* says: "The twelve links of dependent origination are present within a single thought-moment." The *Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra* says: "The twelve links of dependent origination are entirely complete within a single person's single thought-moment." Even this remains somewhat general — only when a single person's single thought-moment is recognized as entirely complete with the ten realms, the ten suchnesses, and the twelve links of dependent origination all at once may this properly be called the Mahāyāna's own inconceivable twelve-link dependent origination. Question: the *Twelve Gate Treatise* says: "Dharmas arising through conditions have, in truth, no genuine arising at all. Were one to speak of their arising, would this occur within a single mind, or within many minds?" — could one, then, still properly speak of this occurring within a single thought-moment? Answer: the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* says: "The immeasurable is within the one; the one is within the immeasurable." The *Larger Prajñāpāramitā* says: "Every dharma tends toward ignorance, this tendency reaching no further — extending even to every dharma tending toward old age and death." Here, then, to speak of a single mind complete with the twelve links of dependent origination — what fault could this possibly carry? Furthermore, in speaking of "a single thought-moment," this is not the same as the world's own attachment to some fixed, determinate mark of oneness or difference in a single thought-moment — it is, rather, that, being neither one nor different, one nonetheless speaks provisionally of "oneness." It is like a dissolving dharma covering the mind, within a single thought-moment dreaming of countless ages — a matter treated just as in the *Lotus Sūtra*, and so on.

Giving Rise to the True Bodhi-Mind, and Skillfully Pacifying the Mind Through the Four Universal Vows 真正發菩提心 · 善巧安心

Chinese Source

真正發菩提心者：若依生滅、無生滅、假名等十二因緣而起慈悲、誓願者，此非真正。故《華嚴》云「菩提心魔」，即此意也。若依不思議十二因緣起慈悲，覆度一切，是名真正。拔苦有二：一、拔十法界無明、愛、取、行、有五種因苦；二、拔十法界識、名色七種果苦。慈與樂亦爾。謂與十法界觀無明、愛、取，成慧行正道；轉行、有成行行助道，是名「與樂因」。觀十界名色七支皆安樂性，即大涅槃，不可復滅，名「與樂果」。約此四義起四弘誓：未度令度，度十界七支生死之苦；未解令解，解十界無明、愛、取、行、有五支之集；未安令安，安十界無明、愛、取、行、有，正助之道；未得涅槃令得，識等七支安樂涅槃也云云。善巧安心者：巧觀十界識等七支即是法性，不起無明、愛、取八倒迷惑，名為「觀」；十法界行、有等種種顛倒息，故名為「止」云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for giving rise to the true bodhi-mind: should one give rise to compassion and vows founded merely upon the arising-and-ceasing, the neither-arising-nor-ceasing, or the provisionally-named account of the twelve links of dependent origination, this is not yet the true and genuine bodhi-mind — hence the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* speaks of "the bodhi-mind that is itself a demon," carrying precisely this same sense. Only should one give rise to compassion founded upon the inconceivable twelve links of dependent origination, universally delivering all beings without exception, is this properly named true and genuine. Extracting suffering has two aspects: first, extracting the ten dharma-realms' own suffering of the five causal links — ignorance, craving, grasping, formation, and existence; second, extracting the ten dharma-realms' own suffering of the seven fruit-links — consciousness, name-and-form, and the rest. Loving-kindness in granting happiness likewise has two corresponding aspects: contemplating, on behalf of the ten dharma-realms, ignorance, craving, and grasping, thereby accomplishing the direct path of wisdom-conduct, and transforming formation and existence into the auxiliary path of conduct-in-action, this is named "granting the cause of happiness." Contemplating the ten realms' name-and-form and the remaining seven links as themselves possessing the very nature of peace and happiness, this being precisely the great nirvāṇa, never again to be extinguished, is named "granting the fruit of happiness." Founded upon these four senses, the four universal vows arise: those not yet delivered shall be delivered — delivering the ten realms' seven fruit-links from the suffering of birth-and-death; those not yet released shall be released — releasing the ten realms from the accumulation of ignorance, craving, grasping, formation, and existence, these five causal links; those not yet at peace shall be brought to peace — bringing the ten realms' ignorance, craving, grasping, formation, and existence to peace, through the direct and auxiliary paths together; those not yet attained to nirvāṇa shall attain it — attaining, through consciousness and the remaining seven links, the very peace and happiness of nirvāṇa itself, and so on. As for skillfully pacifying the mind: skillfully contemplating the ten realms' consciousness and the remaining seven links as themselves precisely the Dharma-nature, not giving rise to the eight inversions of delusion and confusion belonging to ignorance, craving, and grasping, is named "contemplation"; the ten dharma-realms' every manner of inverted delusion belonging to formation, existence, and the rest thereby coming to rest, is named "calming," and so on.

Comprehensively Breaking Through, and Distinguishing Blockage from Passage 破法遍 · 善知通塞

Chinese Source

破法遍者：橫破十界十二因緣悉是一念，一念不自、不他、不共、不無因，當知十界悉無生也；竪破十界行、有、見思、塵沙、無知、無明不生，乃至四十二品不生不生，名「大涅槃」。善知通塞者：達因緣真名「通」；起見思著為「塞」。沈真為不通；達因緣事為不塞。於三道起法愛為塞；達因緣中理名為通。若於番番起無明、愛、取、行、有為失；若於番番悉有智慧名得。或直就有作等四種苦、集論塞；四種道、滅為通。或直就三假故為塞；破三假無生為通。通惑既爾，別惑亦然。或直就四見起十使為塞；破見為通云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for comprehensively breaking through every dharma: horizontally, breaking through the twelve links of dependent origination across all ten realms as entirely a single thought-moment, this thought-moment being neither self-caused, nor other-caused, nor jointly caused, nor uncaused — one should recognize thereby that all ten realms are entirely unborn. Vertically, breaking through, across all ten realms, formation, existence, view-and-thought affliction, dust-and-sand affliction, ignorance-of-particulars, and fundamental ignorance as themselves unborn, extending through all forty-two grades as unborn upon unborn — this is named "the great nirvāṇa." As for skillfully distinguishing blockage from passage: penetrating to the very truth of causes and conditions is named "passage"; giving rise to attachment through view-and-thought affliction is "blockage." Sinking into the real truth alone constitutes non-passage; penetrating the concrete practice of causes and conditions constitutes non-blockage. Giving rise to Dharma-attachment with respect to the three paths constitutes blockage; penetrating the underlying principle within causes and conditions constitutes passage. Should ignorance, craving, grasping, formation, and existence arise anew at each successive round, this is failure; should wisdom be present at each successive round instead, this is attainment. Or, treated strictly in terms of the four kinds of active, artificial suffering and origin, these constitute blockage, while the four kinds of path and cessation constitute passage. Or, treated strictly in terms of the threefold provisional, this constitutes blockage, while breaking through the threefold provisional into the unborn constitutes passage. As the shared afflictions are thus, so too are the distinct afflictions. Or, treated strictly in terms of the ten proclivities arising from the four views, these constitute blockage, while breaking through view constitutes passage, and so on.

The Four Foundations of Mindfulness Mapped onto the Twelve Links, and the Three Gates of Liberation 善修道品 · 三解脫門

Chinese Source

善修道品者：若通論十界因緣中色法皆名為「身」；一切受法皆名為「受」；一切識法皆名為「心」；一切想、行皆名為「法」。若別論：名色支中取色，六入中取五入，觸中取五觸、五受，生死支各取色分，皆名身念處攝。名色支中取識分，六入中取意入，生死支各取識分，皆名心念處攝。無明、行、名色支中取想、行，觸支中取法觸，愛支、取支、有支、生支中取想、行，死支中亦取想、行，皆法念處攝。或時云：無明是過去愛，愛是污穢五陰。若現在論：無明，法念處攝；行，法攝；識，心攝；名色，身、心兩攝；六入緣六塵，塵，法攝；入，身攝；觸，法攝；受，還受攝；愛，污穢，身心兩攝；取，法攝；有，行攝；生是色起，死是色滅，法攝。問：數人說生死皆是不相應行，秖應法念處攝，云何通三念處？答：《大經》云：「此五陰滅，彼五陰續生，如蠟印印泥，印壞文成。」故知生死之法不離五陰，得作此說云云。若通別因緣諸色，非垢非淨，能雙照垢淨，名「身念處」。觀諸因緣通別諸受，非苦非樂，雙照苦樂，名「受念處」。觀諸因緣通別心識，非常非無常，雙照常、無常，是心念處。觀諸因緣通別想行，非我非無我，雙照我、無我，是法念處。此四能破十二因緣中八種顛倒，八顛倒轉成四枯四榮，亦是非枯非榮，中間入涅槃，見佛性也。勤觀此四，名「正勤」；乃至八道，如前說。觀根本無四句，不生不滅，即畢竟空。此空具十八空；十八空祇是一空。《方等》云：「小空、大空皆歸一空。」《大品》云：「一獨空。」是名「空解脫門」。皆入此空，不取法性四相，不受、不著、不念、不分別新、舊、內、外云云。若心無依倚，以無所見見真佛性，以不住法住大涅槃，是名「無相解脫門」。是大涅槃，非修非作，非自故非因，非他故非緣，不共故非合，非無因故非離，無修無得，名「無作解脫門」。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for skillfully cultivating the branches of the path: treated generally, among the ten realms' own causes and conditions, every form-dharma is named "body"; every dharma of feeling is named "feeling"; every dharma of consciousness is named "mind"; every dharma of perception and formation is named "dharmas." Treated specifically: the form taken up within the name-and-form link, the five sense-fields taken up within the six sense-fields link, the five contacts and five feelings taken up within the contact link, and the form-portion taken up within each of the birth and death links, are all together classed under the foundation of mindfulness of the body. The consciousness-portion taken up within the name-and-form link, the mental sense-field taken up within the six sense-fields link, and the consciousness-portion taken up within each of the birth and death links, are all together classed under the foundation of mindfulness of the mind. The perception and formation taken up within the ignorance, formation, and name-and-form links, the mental contact taken up within the contact link, the perception and formation taken up within the craving, grasping, existence, and birth links, and the perception and formation likewise taken up within the death link — all of these are classed under the foundation of mindfulness of dharmas. It is sometimes said that ignorance is craving from a past life, and craving is itself the defiled five aggregates. Treated in terms of the present alone: ignorance is classed under the foundation of mindfulness of dharmas; formation likewise under dharmas; consciousness under mind; name-and-form under both body and mind; the six sense-fields, taking the six sense-objects as their condition, the objects classed under dharmas and the fields themselves under body; contact classed under dharmas; feeling classed, naturally, under feeling; craving, being itself defilement, classed under both body and mind; grasping classed under dharmas; existence classed under formation; birth being the arising of form and death being the ceasing of form, both classed under dharmas. Question: masters of numerical categories hold that birth and death are both alike formations dissociated from mind, and hence ought to be classed under the foundation of mindfulness of dharmas alone — how, then, can they pertain to all three foundations of mindfulness at once? Answer: the Great Sūtra says: "As these five aggregates cease, those five aggregates continue to arise, like a wax seal pressed into clay — the seal itself broken even as the impression it leaves is formed." One thus knows that the dharma of birth and death is never apart from the five aggregates, and this account may accordingly be given. Contemplating the general and particular forms belonging to causes and conditions as neither defiled nor pure, able to illuminate both defilement and purity at once, is named "the foundation of mindfulness of the body." Should one be able to bear with adversity here, one is thereby able to accomplish the Tathāgata's own meritorious qualities of formation and existence — namely, the retributive mark of the purification of the six faculties. As for the manifestation of the obstruction of affliction: this refers to greed, anger, false calculation, the deep and sharp forms of wrong view, pride, and the wisdom-conduct of the two lesser vehicles and of the bodhisattvas of the shared, distinct, and Tripiṭaka teachings — all of these manifest from within the links of ignorance, craving, and grasping. Should one be able to penetrate this thoroughly and bear with it, the Buddha's own knowledge and vision are thereby opened. As for the manifestation of the obstruction of retribution: this refers to every manner of the aggregates, entries, and elements, every manner of the eight winds, and every manner of illness — these manifest precisely within the seven fruit-links. Should one recognize these as themselves the very buddha-nature, neither moved to grasp them nor to reject them, remaining like empty space throughout, this is precisely not severing birth-and-death while yet entering nirvāṇa, not destroying the aggregates and entries while yet manifesting the true and genuine Dharma-body. Able to penetrate thoroughly in this way, one thereby meets with no obstruction from any of the three obstructions. Abiding in the ground of forbearance, gentle, compliant, and never rash or hasty, the mind, too, remains unstartled — this is named "the accomplishment of the mind of bearing with adversity." As the śrāvaka, once abiding in the dharma of forbearance, never again retreats into committing the five heinous transgressions or becoming an icchantika, so too does the bodhisattva, abiding in the ground of forbearance, never again give rise to a grave transgression obstructing the path. As for freedom from attachment to the resemblance of the path: this has two aspects, the resemblance and the genuine. The bodhisattva, proceeding from the initial subduing forbearance into the compliant forbearance, manifests the iron-wheel stage's own resemblance-understanding merit, yet must remain untainted by attachment to these three dharmas — namely, resemblance-wisdom, resemblance-merit, and resemblance-Dharma-nature. Since this wisdom still involves ignorance, craving, and grasping; since this merit still involves the karma of formation and existence; and since this Dharma-nature still involves name-and-form and birth-and-death — one ought not become attached to any of these three. Should craving arise toward these three dharmas, one neither enters the bodhisattva's own stage nor falls back into the two lesser vehicles — this is named "falling at the summit," also named "compliance with the path." Contemplating ignorance, craving, and grasping is compliance with the path of wisdom-conduct; contemplating formation and existence is compliance with the path of conduct-in-action; contemplating consciousness and the rest is compliance with the path of the Dharma-nature. Through compliance with these three paths, one does not fall into the stage of the śrāvaka; yet, through craving toward these same three paths, one likewise fails to enter the stage of the bodhisattva. How, then, does this craving arise? It is like entering a forest of campaka blossoms and no longer smelling any other fragrance at all — the bodhisattva craves only the merit of the buddhas, no longer giving thought to the two lesser vehicles or to any other expedient path — this is named "craving." Because of this very craving, one remains unable to transform ignorance, craving, and grasping into true light; unable to transform formation and existence into wondrous conduct; unable to manifest consciousness and form as the very Dharma-body. Should these three paths remain untransformed, how could one possibly enter the bodhisattva's own stage at all? Should one instead remain free of attachment to these three resemblance-dharmas, free of craving for mere compliance with the path, then: "every immeasurable transgression is removed, the pure mind constantly unified; such a person, honored and sublime, is then able to behold prajñā itself." Since even prajñā itself is not clung to, how much less, then, any lesser dharma? "Entering the very principle of prajñā is named 'abiding'" — this is precisely "at the very moment the first abiding of the resolve is attained, perfect enlightenment is thereby accomplished, knowing the true and genuine nature of every dharma, complete with the very body of wisdom, awakened not through any other's instruction." Beholding prajñā means genuinely beholding the three paths as the three kinds of prajñā themselves. From this point onward, thought after thought grows quiescent, flowing naturally into the very ocean of omniscience — immeasurable ignorance is thereby broken through of its own accord. The *Great Treatise* says: "'Why is it that the breaking-through of ignorance's own samādhi is spoken of at every single stage?' Answer: 'The categories of ignorance are exceedingly numerous — from the very first arising of the resolve all the way to the vajra-summit, ignorance is broken through and the Dharma-nature revealed at every stage, with only a single remaining category left at the very end. Once this final category, too, is removed, one is then named a buddha.'" The body of the Tathāgata is the very substance of the vajra itself — every evil already severed, every good universally gathered together, the three virtues brought to their ultimate completion, passing beyond even the letter "ṭha," beyond which there remains nothing further that words can express — this is named "riding this very jeweled vehicle directly to the place of enlightenment," "arriving to abide within the very ocean of omniscience itself." The rest is as explained above, and so on.

Tenth, clarifying the manifestation of spiritual powers, treated briefly in five kinds: the heavenly eye, knowledge of others' minds, the heavenly ear, knowledge of past lives, and the power over the body. The uncontaminated power belongs properly to a lower-numbered object treated elsewhere. Here, only dhyāna is able to give rise to spiritual power; spiritual power is never able to give rise to dhyāna. Why is this so? Every dhyāna is itself a dharma of concentration, and dhyānas are mutually able to trigger one another. Every dhyāna is the very substance of spiritual power; spiritual power is the very function of dhyāna. Function arises from substance, hence spiritual power attaches itself to substance and thereby arises; function never arises in isolation on its own, so how could it possibly give rise to substance in turn? The sūtra says: "Deeply cultivating meditative concentration, one attains the five spiritual powers" — this carries precisely this same sense. Treated generally, each and every dhyāna is able to give rise to all five powers. Treated specifically, in terms of what most readily and conveniently arises: the root dhyānas, for the most part, are unable to give rise to spiritual power at all, and, should they do so, the result is rarely sharp or effective. The special excellences and the penetrating-and-clear dhyāna, for the most part, give rise to the bodily power of light, effortless movement. The liberations and the bases of mastery, for the most part, give rise to the bodily power of transformation at will. Should one cultivate the root dhyānas exclusively, this merely increases rebirth among humans and devas, with no prospect of ever escaping this cycle at all — as in the time of the Buddha Mahābhijñā-jñānābhibhū, when the Brahmā gods themselves declared: "One hundred and eighty kalpas have passed in vain, with no buddha appearing in the world, and the three evil paths filled to overflowing" — not a single being, in all that time, managed to escape birth-and-death. Should one cultivate the impurity contemplation, the liberations, and the rest exclusively, these are able to produce the uncontaminated without needing to wait upon the wisdom of the truths at all, thereby accomplishing the dharma-realm of the śrāvaka. Should one contemplate these various dhyānas as able to break through the six coverings — the coverings being themselves the origin of suffering, this origin inviting the very fruit of suffering; the breaking-through being itself the path, this path being able to reach cessation — this, too, is the dharma-realm of the śrāvaka, and is likewise the dharma-realm of the bodhisattva of the six pāramitās. Further, dhyāna necessarily involves the relinquishing of desire, and this is precisely dāna; should one fail to uphold the precepts, samādhi fails to manifest at all, and hence this is precisely śīla; attaining dhyāna, one is thereby free of anger, and this is precisely kṣānti; attaining dhyāna, one is thereby free of scattered thought, and this is precisely vīrya; this very dharma of dhyāna is itself named dhyāna proper; and knowing that every dharma is impermanent is named prajñā. This is named the dharma-realm of the bodhisattva of the six pāramitās arising through dhyāna. Further, contemplating this very dhyāna as a dharma produced through causes and conditions — should one contemplate the various dhyānas as themselves the link of "existence," this existence arising from grasping, extending through to old age and death, exactly as explained earlier — this is the dharma-realm of the pratyekabuddha. Further, contemplating the various dhyānas as dharmas produced through causes and conditions, themselves precisely empty — this arising-dharma's very emptiness being the truth of the path of the unborn — this is the dharma-realm of the śrāvaka and bodhisattva of the shared teaching. Further, contemplating this very dhyāna as a dharma produced through causes and conditions, itself precisely emptiness, precisely the provisional, and precisely the middle all at once — the ten dharma-realms themselves arising from dhyāna and ceasing from dhyāna. Why is this so? Should dhyāna, as cause, give rise to the dharmas of the three lower paths and the six paths generally, this is precisely increasing the twenty-five modes of existence, generating six dharma-realms while extinguishing four; should dhyāna, as cause, give rise to the dharmas of the liberations and the rest, subduing the twenty-five modes of existence, this is likewise suppressing and obscuring six dharma-realms. Should one contemplate the liberations and the rest as impermanent, this employs the clumsy method of arising-and-ceasing to break through the twenty-five modes of existence, extinguishing six dharma-realms and generating one; should one contemplate dhyāna as a dharma produced through causes and conditions, precisely empty, this employs the skillful method of the unborn to break through the twenty-five modes of existence, extinguishing seven dharma-realms and generating one; should one contemplate dhyāna as precisely the provisional, this employs the immeasurable clumsy method to break through the twenty-five modes of existence together with adventitious affliction, extinguishing eight dharma-realms and generating one; should one contemplate dhyāna as a dharma produced through causes and conditions, precisely the middle, this employs the single, genuine, skillful method to break through the twenty-five modes of existence together with the affliction of ignorance itself, extinguishing nine dharma-realms and generating one.

Ru-Yi Translation

Accomplishing the king-samādhi, which gathers in every samādhi universally, the root dhyānas and the liberations all enter into it entirely, like streams returning to the sea; the root dhyānas and liberations are thereby transformed, becoming entirely the Mahāyāna itself. Loving-kindness alone, identified with lesser good, wishing to grant happiness, is "the conduct of the infant." The Inconceivable Object: A Single Thought-Moment of Concentration Is Neither Sentient Being nor Not 不思議觀者

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the inconceivable contemplation: should a single thought-moment's concentrated mind manifest — whether savored or pure, extending even to spiritual power itself — one recognizes at once that this very mind is precisely the Dharma-nature of ignorance, the Dharma-realm itself. The hundred dharmas of the ten realms, every manner of concentration and distraction alike, are entirely complete within this single thought-moment. Why is this so? Through delusion regarding the Dharma-nature, every manner of scattered, evil dharma comes to be; through understanding of the Dharma-nature, every manner of dharma of concentration comes to be. Since concentration and distraction are thus themselves precisely ignorance, ignorance itself is likewise precisely the Dharma-nature. Delusion and understanding, concentration and distraction — their nature is not two, subtle and profound beyond ordinary conception, cut off from every path of words; conceptual thought and calculation applied to it only exhaust themselves in vain — how, then, could this possibly be the domain of an ordinary being or of the two lesser vehicles? Though transcending ordinary experience entirely, it is nonetheless never apart from the multitude of existing beings. The sūtra says: "Every sentient being is itself the very concentration of cessation." Though this mind itself is thus named "concentration," sentient beings have never, from the very outset, simply been this concentration, nor have sentient beings ever, from the very outset, simply not been this concentration. Why is this so? Were one to depart from sentient beings entirely, where else could this concentration possibly be sought? Hence sentient beings have never, from the outset, simply not been it. Yet were sentient beings themselves simply identical with this concentration, this concentration would then not be sentient beings at all — hence sentient beings have never, from the outset, simply been it either. Since they have never simply been it, they are not identical with it; since they have never simply not been it, they are not separate from it either — this very non-identity and non-separation is where the subtlety lies. Difficult to fathom, as though empty itself, only buddha and buddha together are able to exhaust its full depth. Since a single thought-moment of meditative concentration is thus, so too is every other object of contemplation exactly the same. Should one, through this very contemplation, suddenly attain awakening, then the very moment one hears this teaching spoken, the sickness of affliction is cured at once, with no need to proceed through the remaining nine modes at all. Should this contemplation fail to bring awakening, one gives rise once more to compassion. This underlying principle is itself quiescent, and yet sentient beings, giving rise to delusion regarding it, allow the frivolous discourse of ignorance to obscure the very treasury of the Tathāgata, forming a dense thicket-forest of affliction — hence compassion arises, extracting this fundamental, heavy suffering. Further, ignorance is itself precisely the Dharma-nature, and affliction is itself precisely bodhi — wishing to lead sentient beings to manifest the very truth and the very Dharma-body within their own concrete circumstances just as they are, loving-kindness thereby arises, granting ultimate happiness. Vows of this kind, pure and genuinely true, seeking the Buddha's own path above while transforming sentient beings below, uncontaminated by any poison, free of any partiality or deviation, resting upon no support whatsoever, apart from both extremes — this is named "giving rise to the bodhi-mind." The very moment this mind arises, one may suddenly attain awakening, like a swift horse that needs only glimpse the shadow of the whip to arrive directly upon the correct road. Should one not yet arrive there, one ought then to pacify the mind through calming-and-contemplation, skillfully turning and adapting, cultivating through skillful means either calming or contemplation as suits the case. Contemplating a single thought-moment of meditative concentration as the quiescence of both extremes is named "embodying-the-real calming"; illuminating the Dharma-nature's own purity, free of every obstruction and hindrance, is named "the contemplation of emptiness." Further, contemplating the mind of dhyāna as itself precisely emptiness and precisely the provisional, illuminating the two truths at once while remaining unmoved from the real limit itself, is named "according-with-conditions calming"; penetrating the correspondence of remedy to illness, precisely fitting each occasion, is named "the contemplation of the provisional." Further, contemplating the mind of dhyāna still more deeply, this mind of dhyāna being precisely emptiness, precisely the provisional, and precisely the middle, without any duality or distinction among them, is named "non-discriminating calming"; penetrating to true reality, the treasury of the Tathāgata, the ultimate truth, without any duality or distinction, is named "the contemplation of the middle." These three kinds of calming and three kinds of contemplation exist within a single thought-moment of mind, neither before nor after, neither one nor different. To break through the two extremes, they are named "one" and "the middle"; to break through partial attachment and mere arising-and-ceasing, they are named "perfect" and "quiescent"; to break through the sequential, step-by-step version of the three kinds of calming and contemplation, they are named "the three contemplations in a single mind." In truth, there exists no fixed mark whatsoever of "middle," "perfect," or "a single mind" — it is simply by means of this very calming-and-contemplation that the mind is pacified, and so on.

Ru-Yi Translation

Should these two dharmas, calming and contemplation, refine the mind and yet still fail to gain entry, one should recognize that, prior to the manifestation of the genuine, everything remains delusion and disorder. Through the single mind's three contemplations, comprehensively breaking through every delusion and disorder, both horizontal and vertical, delusion departs and wisdom manifests, disorder subsides and concentration is accomplished. Should one still fail to awaken, this indicates blockage rather than passage — one ought then to contemplate further which aspect remains blocked and which remains unblocked. Where there is no blockage, this indicates passage; where there is blockage, further contemplation is required. Knowing whether a character is truly a character, one distinguishes gain from loss within the Four Noble Truths. Should one still fail to awaken, this indicates a failure to understand the proper coordination of the branches of the path. Why is this so? A single thought-moment of dhyāna-mind is already complete with the five aggregates of all ten realms — these aggregates being precisely empty, one breaks through the four inversions within the three realms, forming the four withered trees; these aggregates being precisely the provisional, one breaks through the four inversions beyond the three realms, forming the four flourishing trees; these aggregates being precisely the middle, neither internal nor external, neither flourishing nor withered, one enters parinirvāṇa in the very midst between them. Contemplations of this kind, the four foundations of mindfulness, open the very gate of the branches of the path; the branches of the path open the three gates of liberation; entering nirvāṇa, the path and its concentration are thereby both complete. Why, then, does one still fail to awaken? This must be on account of obstruction accumulated in past lives, one's present attachment to the very savor of dhyāna proving impossible to relinquish. Past and present reinforcing one another, they together form the covering of stinginess — how, then, could the path possibly manifest? One ought to repent with utmost earnestness, relinquishing body, life, and wealth, relinquishing craving for the savor of dhyāna, cultivating the pāramitā of generosity so as to assist in overcoming the obstruction of stinginess. Further, craving-attachment to the various dhyānas itself breaks the precepts that accord with the path, extending even to breaking the complete precepts themselves — past and present reinforcing one another, they together form the covering of precept-violation. One ought to repent with utmost earnestness, making one's concrete conduct scrupulous and pure, so as to assist in overcoming the obstruction of moral discipline. Further, just as even Kālodāyin and the Brahmā gods themselves still harbor anger, how, then, could one who has newly manifested a mere concrete dhyāna possibly be free of anger? Further, whatever meditative concentration one possesses, it remains, in truth, neither unborn nor genuinely quiescent, since it lacks the twofold forbearance — hence anger arises of its own accord regardless. Past and present reinforcing one another, they together form the covering of anger. One ought to repent with utmost earnestness, further cultivating concrete loving-kindness so as to assist in overcoming the obstruction of forbearance. Further, craving-attachment to the savor of dhyāna is itself a blindness born of heedless folly, scattered and disordered, intermixed with confusion — past and present reinforcing one another, they together form laziness. One ought to sustain diligence with utmost earnestness, without interruption, so as to assist in overcoming the obstruction of diligence. Further, the marks of karma that manifest within dhyāna itself disturb and vex the very mind of dhyāna, preventing it from settling into deep unity. Should the two lesser vehicles merely sever affliction, departing while still owing their karmic debt, without addressing the severing of karma itself; while the bodhisattva, severing affliction, receives the Dharma-nature body, yet finds certain dharma-gates open to him and others still closed — one should recognize that this is due to obstruction by karma, requiring one to cultivate every wholesome karmic action with utmost earnestness. If even the Dharma-nature body remains thus affected, how much more so the body of birth-and-death — how could it possibly be free of karma at all? Cultivating goodness thus assists in overcoming the obstruction of concentration. Further, one who savors dhyāna in this way entirely fails to understand impermanence and arising-and-ceasing — how much less, then, does he understand the savoring-attachment to the unborn and unceasing itself? Past and present reinforcing one another, they together form the obstruction of folly. One ought to repent with utmost earnestness, treating this concrete delusion and deviation directly. This has clarified the antidotes only in brief outline — treated in full, they could never be exhausted. The practitioner's own contemplative method reaches its very limit at this point; should one still fail to awaken even here, this indicates an exceedingly dull faculty, a grave obstruction of transgression. Fearing that further transgression and obstruction might arise from this very failure, the remaining three modes are once more clarified below in brief. Knowing one's own stage guards, from within, against overweening pride; bearing with adversity guards, from without, against the eight winds; freedom from Dharma-attachment guards against falling at the very summit. With these ten modes thus accomplished, one swiftly enters the unborn, attains the single great cart, and travels through all four directions directly to subtle enlightenment itself, breaking through the twenty-five modes of existence and realizing the king-samādhi — practice for oneself and the transformation of others both complete, from beginning to end. The rest is as explained above, and so on.

Reading note: The scale of this fascicle — devoted entirely to a single one of the ten objects of contemplation, where fascicle 8 compressed four objects into the same span — reflects how much practical, technical material the tradition had accumulated around meditative states specifically by Zhiyi's own time: pulse-like physical sensations, corpse-decay stages, colored lights, and a catalog of deviant marks to test against genuine ones. Yet the fascicle's own governing warning, stated at its very opening and repeated at its close, is that none of this technical apparatus is the point — dhyāna itself is "like a bitter cucumber," equally capable of serving as the seed for any of the ten dharma-realms depending on what mind meets it. The embedded ten-mode application to the causal contemplation (因緣), tracing the twelve links across the discursible, the inconceivable, the four teachings' own stages, and finally a single thought-moment at once, functions as a complete demonstration in miniature of the very method this whole treatise exists to teach — proof, offered from within the object of meditative states itself, that the method does not depend on any one particular object to do its work.
