Fascicle 8 · 摩訶止觀卷第八

Beyond the Aggregates: Four Further Objects

This page continues Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Mohe Zhiguan (摩訶止觀, "The Great Calming and Contemplation"). Fascicles 5 through 7 completed the full ten-mode treatment of the first of the ten objects of contemplation, the five aggregates. Fascicle 8 moves on to the second, third, fourth, and fifth objects — affliction, illness, the marks of karma, and demonic affairs — treating each in turn through its own governing outline before applying the ten modes of contemplation to it, now considerably compressed, since much of what the ten modes require has already been laid out in full for the aggregates. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Continuing from Fascicle 7

Fascicles 5, 6, and 7 together completed the full ten-mode treatment of the first of the ten objects of contemplation, the five aggregates, entries, and elements. Fascicle 8 (again combining CBETA's upper and lower halves into a single continuous page, per this site's existing convention) moves on to four further objects in turn: the second, affliction (煩惱境); the third, illness (病患境); the fourth, the marks of karma (業相境); and the fifth, demonic affairs (魔事境). Each object receives its own governing outline — for affliction and for the marks of karma, a fourfold structure of characteristics, causes and conditions, methods of treatment, and the cultivation of calming-and-contemplation; for illness and for demonic affairs, a fivefold structure adding an account of benefit and harm, or of the harm demons inflict. In every case the final step, applying the ten modes of contemplation proper, is now considerably compressed compared to the extended treatment given the aggregates in fascicles 5 through 7, since the treatise itself often simply refers back to what was already established there. The Second Object: Contemplating Affliction 第二、觀煩惱境

Chinese Source

第二、觀煩惱境者：上陰界入不悟，則非其宜，而觀察不已，擊動煩惱，貪瞋發作；是時應捨陰入觀於煩惱。前呵五欲，知其過罪；棄蓋是捨平常；陰入觀於果報，於中求解。今觀發作隆盛，起重貪瞋。如鐵不與火合，但黑；若與火合，赫然。又報法尋常，無時不有，呵棄為易；若欻起煩惱，控制則難。何者？生來雖瞋，諫曉則息；今所發者，咆勃可畏。生來倒想，乍起乍滅；今所發者，欝然不去。生來欲色，抑制可停；今所發者，不簡死馬，況其匹類？此惑內發，強梁熾盛；若見外境，心狂眼闇。譬如流水，不覺其急；概之以木，漣漪𭱯起。亦如健人，不知有力，觸之怒壯。煩惱臥伏，如有如無；道場懺悔，觀陰界入，如觸睡師子哮吼震地。若不識者，則能牽人作大重罪。非唯止觀不成，更增長惡業，墜黑闇坑，無能勉出。為是義故，須觀煩惱境也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, contemplating the object of affliction: when contemplation of the aggregates, entries, and elements above has not yet brought awakening, this alone is no longer the right remedy, and continued unceasing observation instead stirs and agitates affliction, so that greed and anger break forth. At just this moment one ought to set aside contemplation of the aggregates and entries and contemplate affliction directly. Earlier, rebuking the five desires made known their fault and their guilt; casting aside the hindrances was the casting-aside of what is ordinary and constant; contemplating the aggregates and entries in terms of retributive result sought understanding within that very retribution. Now, by contrast, one contemplates affliction breaking forth in full flood, giving rise to greed and anger grown heavy and severe. It is like iron: left apart from fire, it is merely black; brought into contact with fire, it blazes red-hot. And whereas the ordinary dharmas of retribution are constant, present at every moment without exception, so that rebuking and casting them aside is easy — should affliction instead suddenly surge forth, controlling it becomes difficult indeed. Why is this so? Anger one is born with, though present, subsides once admonished and made to understand; but what now breaks forth roars and rages, terrible to behold. Inverted thought one is born with arises and vanishes by turns; but what now breaks forth remains clotted and will not depart. Desire for form one is born with can be checked and halted by restraint; but what now breaks forth makes no distinction even before a dead horse, let alone its living kind. This affliction, breaking forth from within, is violent and blazing in its intensity; and should it then encounter an outer object as well, the mind runs wild and the eyes go dark. It is like a flowing stream, whose swiftness one does not perceive — but obstruct it with a piece of wood, and eddies and ripples surge up violently at once. It is also like a powerful man unaware of his own strength — touch him, and his fury and force erupt. Affliction, lying dormant, seems now present, now absent; but repentance in the place of practice, and contemplation of the aggregates, entries, and elements, is like touching a sleeping lion, which roars forth and shakes the very earth. Should one fail to recognize this for what it is, it is capable of dragging a person into grave and weighty transgression — not only will calming-and-contemplation fail to be accomplished, affliction will further increase evil karma, casting one down into a pit of utter darkness from which there is no escaping by any effort at all. For this reason, one must contemplate the object of affliction.

The Fourfold Outline, and Clarifying the Marks of Affliction 觀此為四 · 一略明其相

Chinese Source

觀此為四：一、略明其相，二、明因緣，三、明治異，四、修止觀。初、明相者，先釋名。煩惱是昏煩之法，惱亂心神。又與心作煩，令心得惱，即是見思利鈍。此一往分數，五鈍何必是貪瞋？如諸蠕動，實不推理，而舉𧑃張鬐，怒目自大。底下凡劣，何甞執見？行、住、坐、臥，恒起我心，故知五鈍非無利也。五利豈唯見惑，何甞無恚、欲耶？當知利、鈍之名，通於見、思。今約位分之，令不相濫。若未發禪來，雖有世智，推理辯聰，見想猶弱，所有十使同屬於鈍。從因定發見，見心猛盛，所有十使從強受名，皆屬於利。如兩學人，一得法意，為諍則強；一得語言，為諍則弱。得語如無禪，得意如發定。若發定已而起見惑，如下所觀；若未發定而起煩惱，正是今所觀也。若利中有鈍，見諦但斷於利，鈍猶應在。《毘曇》人謂利上之鈍，名「背上使」；見諦斷時，正利既去，背使亦去。思惟亦如是。若開此利鈍，為八萬四千；今但束為四分。三毒偏發為三分；若等緣三境名「等分」；三毒偏起是覺觀而非多；三毒等起名「覺觀多」。若少、若多悉名「散動」，俱能障定；無記是「報散動」，則不障定。經云：「從滅定出，入散心中；散心中還入諸定。」散不障定，即此義也。《成論》人云：「散兼無知，癡能障定。」若爾，散兼瞋、欲，何不障定耶？今釋別有意，如上棄蓋中說。但煩惱之相廣不可盡，若具分別，妨於觀門。《法華》云：「二十年中常令除糞。」糞即煩惱，污穢法也。棄之若盡，得一日之價；若住分別多少，終不得直。今觀煩惱糞，求智慧錢，非欲分別見、思相也。若爾，五百羅漢何以分別？為持佛法，作眾導首，通種種難，須廣分別。今正入道，力所未暇，亦於觀非急，但總知四分糞穢，勤而棄之。若從空入假時，當委悉分別。復次，利鈍合各束為四分，同是界內，共二乘斷，名「通煩惱」也。若界外四分，二乘不斷，名「別煩惱」。若作相關，何得離通有別？通惑為枝，別惑為本；得真智斷枝，得中智斷本。若作不思議者，祇界內煩惱即是菩提，何得非是別惑？已如前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

This contemplation is treated in four parts: first, briefly clarifying its marks; second, clarifying its causes and conditions; third, clarifying the different modes of treatment; fourth, cultivating calming-and-contemplation. First, clarifying the marks: begin by explaining the name. Affliction (煩惱) is a dharma of murky vexation, disturbing and troubling the mind and spirit; it also makes trouble together with the mind, causing the mind to become vexed — this is precisely the sharp and dull of view and habituated thought. Taken in one general division, why must the five dull afflictions be identified only with greed and anger? Just as every crawling creature, though genuinely incapable of reasoning, still rears its head and bristles its mane, glaring with eyes and puffing itself up — even the lowest and most benighted of ordinary beings, walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, constantly gives rise to the thought of self; hence it is known that the five dull afflictions are not without a sharp component of their own. And the five sharp afflictions, likewise, are hardly confined to the delusion of view alone — how could they ever be without anger and desire as well? One should understand that the names "sharp" and "dull" run in common through both view and habituated thought; here, they are divided according to spiritual stage, so that the two do not become confused with one another. Prior to the arising of meditative absorption, though one may possess worldly wisdom, reasoning ability, and eloquence, the delusion of view remains weak, and all ten fundamental afflictions belong, together, to the dull category. Once view arises from within concentration attained through causes and conditions, the mind of view grows fierce and dominant, and all ten fundamental afflictions, now named for their strength, belong, together, to the sharp category. It is like two students: one who has grasped the very meaning of the teaching is strong in debate; one who has grasped only its wording is weak in debate. Grasping the wording is like being without meditative absorption; grasping the meaning is like having attained concentration. Should view-delusion arise only after concentration has already been attained, this is as will be contemplated further below; should affliction arise before concentration has been attained, this is precisely what is contemplated here and now. Should there be a dull component within the sharp, the truth of seeing severs only the sharp, while the dull still remains. Masters of the *Abhidharma* call the dull riding atop the sharp the "affliction riding the back" — when the truth of seeing is severed, once the sharp itself has departed, the affliction riding its back departs along with it; the same holds for habituated thought as well. Should one expand this sharp and dull fully, it comes to eighty-four thousand kinds; here, however, they are simply bound together into four divisions. The three poisons arising unevenly make three divisions; should they engage the three objects evenly, this is named "even division"; the three poisons arising unevenly are called "coarse and subtle discursive thought" rather than "many," while the three poisons arising evenly are called "many coarse and subtle discursive thought." Whether few or many, all of this is named "scattered agitation," and both alike are capable of obstructing concentration; the indeterminate is "retributive scattered agitation," which does not obstruct concentration. The sūtra says: "Emerging from cessation-concentration, one enters the scattered mind; from within the scattered mind, one re-enters the various concentrations" — scattering that does not obstruct concentration is precisely this very sense. Masters of the *Satyasiddhi* say: "Scattering combined with unknowing — delusion is able to obstruct concentration." If this is so, then scattering combined with anger and desire, why would these not obstruct concentration as well? The explanation here has its own separate sense, as already explained above within the discussion of casting aside the hindrances. But the marks of affliction are so broad they cannot be exhausted; were one to distinguish them fully in detail, this would only hinder the very gateway of contemplation. The Lotus Sūtra says: "For twenty years he was constantly made to remove the dung." The dung is precisely affliction, a defiling dharma; casting it aside completely earns the wage of a single day, whereas dwelling on distinguishing how much or how little never earns the wage at all. Here, one contemplates the dung of affliction only in order to seek the coin of wisdom, not in order to distinguish the marks of view and habituated thought in fine detail. If this is so, then why did the five hundred arhats make such fine distinctions? Because, upholding the Buddha's Dharma and serving as guides for the assembly, penetrating every manner of difficulty, they required broad and detailed distinction. Here, by contrast, one has only just entered the path, one's strength not yet equal to such a task, and it is likewise not urgent for the purposes of this contemplation — one need only know, in general terms, the four divisions of defiling filth, and diligently cast them aside. Once one enters the provisional from emptiness, one ought then to distinguish them exhaustively in full detail. Furthermore, the sharp and the dull, each bound together into their own four divisions, both alike belong within the three realms, severed in common by the two lesser vehicles — these are named "shared afflictions." Should there be four divisions beyond the three realms, not severed by the two lesser vehicles, these are named "distinct afflictions." Were one to draw a correlation between them, how could there fail to be a distinct apart from the shared? The shared delusions are the branches, the distinct delusions are the root; attaining the wisdom of the real severs the branches, attaining the wisdom of the middle severs the root. Were one to treat this in terms of the inconceivable, then affliction within the three realms itself is precisely bodhi — how could it fail to be, at the same time, a distinct delusion as well? This has already been explained above.

Clarifying the Causes and Conditions of Affliction's Arising 二明因緣

Chinese Source

二、明煩惱起之因緣。因緣有三，如後說。起相有四：深而不利、利而不深、亦深亦利、不深不利。第四句即屬通途果報惑相，尋常相係，故言「非深非利」也。三句起動異常，即屬煩惱發相也。發時深重不可禁止；觸境彌增無能遮制，是為深相。數數發起，起輒深重，故名為「利」。利而不深、深而不利，準此可知。因緣者：一、習因種子，二、業力擊作，三、魔所扇動。習者：無量劫來煩惱重積，種子成就，薰習相續。如駛水流，順之不覺其疾，概之則知奔猛。行人任煩惱流沿生死海都不覺知；若修道品泝諸有流，煩惱嵬起。唯當勤勉特出，曉夜兼功耳。業者：無量劫來惡行成就，如負怨責，那得令汝修道出離？故惡業卓起，破壞觀心，使善法不立。如河湉靜不覺流浪；暴風卒至波如連山。若放擲帆柁，壞在斯須；一心正前後，行船得免。魔者：若作魔行，是其民屬，故不動亂。若行道出界，去此投彼，十軍攝擒，故深利之惑，欻然而至。如大海水，雖無風流，摩竭吸水，萬物奔趣，不可力拒；專稱佛名，乃得脫耳。若就火為譬者，抖擻如習，風扇如業，膏投如魔。魔、業如下說；觀習動煩惱是今所觀也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the causes and conditions of affliction's arising. There are three causes and conditions, as will be explained below. The marks of its arising are four: deep but not sharp; sharp but not deep; both deep and sharp; neither deep nor sharp. This fourth proposition belongs to the ordinary marks of delusion tied to retributive result, bound together in their constant, everyday fashion, and is hence said to be "neither deep nor sharp." The other three propositions, arising and stirring in an unusual manner, belong instead to the marks of affliction's own outbreak. When it breaks forth, it is deep and heavy, impossible to restrain; the more it encounters its object, the more it increases, with nothing able to check or control it — this is the deep mark. Arising again and again, and whenever it arises, arising with grave severity — this is named "sharp." Sharp but not deep, and deep but not sharp, may be understood accordingly. As for the causes and conditions: first, the seed of the habituated cause; second, the striking blow of karmic force; third, the fanning instigation of demons. As for habituation: through immeasurable kalpas, affliction has accumulated heavily, its seeds fully formed, its habituation continuing without pause. It is like a swift-flowing stream — following along with it, one does not perceive its speed, but obstruct it, and one at once recognizes its violent rush. The practitioner, drifting along the current of affliction down the sea of birth-and-death, remains entirely unaware of it; but should one cultivate the branches of the path, swimming upstream against every current of existence, affliction rears up violently in response. One ought only to strive with diligence to break decisively free of it, applying oneself doubly, by day and by night alike. As for karma: through immeasurable kalpas, evil conduct has become fully accumulated, like an unpaid debt demanding repayment — how could it possibly allow you to cultivate the path and win release? And so evil karma rises up abruptly, destroying the very mind of contemplation, so that wholesome dharmas cannot establish themselves. It is like a river, calm and still, whose undulation one does not perceive — until a sudden violent storm arrives, and the waves rise like a chain of mountains; should one then let go of both sail and rudder, ruin follows in an instant, but should one keep the mind fixed and correct, both fore and aft, the vessel may yet be saved. As for demons: should one engage in demonic conduct, one belongs already to their own subjects, and so they cause no disturbance. But should one practice the path so as to escape their domain, leaving this realm to take refuge in another, the ten armies close in to seize and capture — and so deep and sharp delusion arrives with sudden, violent force. It is like the waters of the great ocean: though there be no wind or current, the makara-fish, drawing in the water, draws every creature rushing helplessly along with it, unable to resist by any force of its own — only by single-mindedly invoking the Buddha's name can one win release from it. Were one to draw a comparison in terms of fire: the shaking-loose is like habituation, the fanning wind is like karma, the pouring-on of oil is like demons. Demons and karma will be discussed further below; contemplating habituation as it stirs affliction is what is contemplated here and now.

Clarifying the Different Modes of Treatment 三明治異

Chinese Source

三、治法不同者：小乘治有五：對、轉、不轉、兼、具。此五共治四分煩惱。障道起，如下業境云云。對治者：一分煩惱即有三種，合成十二；對此亦有十二，如對寇設陣，是名「對治」。轉治者：如不淨是貪欲對治，而非其宜，應以淨觀得脫，轉修慈心，念以淨法安樂，豈加穢辱？是名「轉治」。若瞋人教不淨，癡人教思惟邊、無邊，掉散教用智慧分別，此是病不轉而治轉，皆名「轉治」。若藥病俱轉，亦名「轉治」，亦是「對治」。不轉治者：病雖轉，治終不轉。宜修此法，但以此治，治轉不轉病，故名「不轉治」。兼治者：病兼藥亦兼。如貪欲兼瞋，不淨須帶慈心；病兼一二，藥亦兼一二，是名「兼治」。具治者：具用上法共治一病。是名小乘先用五治，後用諦智，乃得入真。大乘明治，非對、非兼等，名「第一義治」。如阿竭陀藥能治眾病。小乘多用三悉檀為治；大乘多用第一義悉檀為治也。空無生中，誰是煩惱？誰是能治？尚無煩惱，何物而轉？既無所轉，亦不兼、具，但以無生一方遍治一切也。此極略，須善取意也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, the different modes of treatment: the Hīnayāna's own treatment is fivefold — direct antidote, transformative treatment, non-transformative treatment, combined treatment, and complete treatment. These five together treat the four divisions of affliction. When they arise to obstruct the path, this is as will be discussed below within the object of karmic marks. As for the direct antidote: each single division of affliction has three kinds, which together make twelve; and set against these there are likewise twelve antidotes — like setting up a battle-line against an invading enemy — this is named "the direct antidote." As for transformative treatment: unattractiveness is the direct antidote to desire, and yet, should this not be the fitting remedy, one ought instead to gain release through the contemplation of purity, transforming one's practice into loving-kindness, contemplating the ease and happiness found within the pure dharma — how could this then add further defilement and disgrace? This is named "transformative treatment." Should one teach unattractiveness to a person given to anger, or teach the contemplation of finite and infinite to a person given to delusion, or teach the discernment of wisdom to a person given to restlessness and distraction, this is a case where the illness itself does not transform but the treatment transforms instead — all of this, too, is named "transformative treatment." Should both remedy and illness transform together, this too is named "transformative treatment," and is likewise "the direct antidote." As for non-transformative treatment: though the illness itself transforms, the treatment, in the end, does not transform — one ought to cultivate this same dharma regardless, using this one treatment alone to treat both the transformed and the untransformed illness alike; hence it is named "non-transformative treatment." As for combined treatment: the illness is combined, and the remedy is likewise combined. As when desire is combined with anger, unattractiveness must be paired together with loving-kindness; the illness combines one or two elements, and the remedy likewise combines one or two — this is named "combined treatment." As for complete treatment: all of the above methods are used together to treat a single illness. This describes how the Hīnayāna first employs these five treatments, and only afterward employs the wisdom of the truths, in order finally to enter the real. The Mahāyāna's own clarification of treatment is neither the direct antidote nor the combined treatment nor any of the rest, but is named "treatment according to the ultimate truth" — like the universal remedy agada, capable of curing every illness without exception. The Hīnayāna, for the most part, employs the three siddhāntas as its treatment; the Mahāyāna, for the most part, employs the siddhānta of the ultimate truth as its treatment. Within emptiness and the unborn, what could possibly be affliction? What could possibly be the very treatment that treats it? Since there is not even affliction to begin with, what is there left to transform? And since there is nothing to transform, there is likewise no combining or completing — one simply uses the unborn as the single, universal remedy that treats everything without exception. This account is extremely brief, and one must grasp its underlying sense with care.

四、修止觀者，還為十意。初、簡思議境者：一念欲覺，初起甚微，不即遮止，遂漸增長；為欲事故，貪引無道，乃至四重、五逆，是名「煩惱生地獄界」。為欲因緣不知慚恥，魯扈觝突，無復禮義，亡失人種，是名「貪欲生畜生界」。又為欲因緣慳惜守護，亦慳他家，是名「貪欲生餓鬼界」。為欲因緣而生嫉妬，猜忌防擬，常欲勝他，百方鴆陷，令彼退負，是名「貪欲生修羅界」。又欲因緣深愛現樂，以禮婚娉，每存撙節，符順仁義，為未來欲樂而持五戒，是名「貪欲生人界」。又欲起時，鄙人欲麁，希求天欲，勤修十善，防止純熟任運不起，是觀貪欲生六天界。又觀欲心棄呵清淨，能發禪定，是色天、無色天界。又觀欲是集，集方招苦，厭此苦集而修出要，是聲聞界。若觀欲是無明，為無明欲而造諸行，輪環無際；若止於欲，無明、行等皆止，是為緣覺界。若觀欲是蔽，而起慈悲，而行於捨，怖畏無常，乃至觀欲是癡等，是六度界。若觀欲本自不起，今亦不住，將亦不滅，欲即是空，空即涅槃，是為通教界。又觀欲心有無量相，集既非一，苦亦無量，知根、欲、性皆因欲心，分別具足，是為別教界。其餘三分煩惱出生諸法，亦復如是。次第生一切法，是名「思議境」也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, cultivating calming-and-contemplation, again treated in ten parts. First, simplifying to the discursible object: a single thought of desire, awakened, is at its first arising exceedingly subtle; should one fail to check and halt it immediately, it gradually grows and increases — and, for the sake of desire's own object, greed draws one onward without restraint, all the way to the four grave offenses and the five heinous crimes; this is named "affliction generating the realm of hell." For the sake of desire's own causes and conditions, knowing no shame, coarse and violent, defying every restraint, abandoning all propriety and righteousness, losing even the very seed of humanity — this is named "desire generating the realm of animals." Further, for the sake of desire's own causes and conditions, being stingy, hoarding, and guarding one's own, while coveting the households of others as well — this is named "desire generating the realm of hungry ghosts." For the sake of desire's own causes and conditions, giving rise to jealousy and envy, harboring suspicion and scheming, constantly wishing to surpass others, plotting by every means to poison and entrap them, forcing them into retreat and defeat — this is named "desire generating the realm of asuras." Further, for the sake of desire's own causes and conditions, deeply cherishing present pleasure, observing the proprieties of betrothal and marriage, always preserving moderation and restraint, according with benevolence and righteousness, upholding the five precepts for the sake of future pleasure and desire yet to come — this is named "desire generating the realm of humans." Further, when desire arises, recognizing that the desire of the vulgar and the lowly is coarse, one seeks instead the desire of the heavens, diligently cultivating the ten wholesome acts, guarding against and restraining desire until it grows fully mature and no longer arises even spontaneously — this is contemplating desire generating the realm of the six heavens. Further, contemplating the mind of desire, casting it off and rebuking it as impure, one is thereby able to give rise to meditative absorption — this is the realm of the heavens of form and the formless. Further, contemplating desire as the very origin of suffering, since this origin necessarily invites suffering as its result, and, wearied by this suffering and its origin, cultivating the path of renunciation — this is the realm of the śrāvaka. Should one contemplate desire as ignorance, creating every formation for the sake of desire born of ignorance, circling round without end — and should one then halt this very desire, ignorance and formation and all the rest are halted along with it — this is the realm of the pratyekabuddha. Should one contemplate desire as a covering affliction, and thereupon give rise to compassion, practicing generosity, dreading impermanence, and so on, contemplating desire as delusion and the like — this is the realm of the six pāramitās. Should one contemplate desire as, at root, never itself arising, not now abiding, nor ever ultimately ceasing — desire itself being emptiness, and emptiness itself being nirvāṇa — this is the realm of the shared teaching. Further, contemplating the mind of desire as possessing immeasurable characteristics, since its origin is by no means single, its resulting suffering is likewise immeasurable, and knowing that faculty, desire, and nature all arise from the very mind of desire, distinguished and complete in every particular — this is the realm of the distinct teaching. The remaining three divisions of affliction likewise give rise, each in turn, to every dharma in just the same way. Generating every dharma in this successive sequence is what is named "the discursible object."

The Inconceivable Object: Desire Itself Is the Path 不思議境 · 貪欲即是道

Chinese Source

不思議境者：如《無行》云：「貪欲即是道，恚、癡亦如是；如是三法中，具一切佛法。」如是四分雖即是道，復不得隨；隨之，將人向惡道；復不得斷，斷之成增上慢。「不斷癡愛，起諸明脫」，乃名為道。不住調伏；不住不調伏。住不調伏，是愚人相；住於調伏，是聲聞法。所以者何？凡夫貪染隨順四分，生死重積狠戾難馴，故名「不調」。二乘怖畏生死，如為怨逐，速出三界。阿羅漢者，名為「不調」；三界惑盡，無惑可調，如是不調，名之為「調」。焦種不生，根敗無用。菩薩不爾，於生死而有勇；於涅槃而不味。勇於生死，無生而生，不為生法所污；如花在泥，如醫療病。不味涅槃，知空不空，不為空法所證；如鳥飛空，不住於空。不斷煩惱而入涅槃，不斷五欲而淨諸根，即是不住調伏、不住不調伏意。今末代癡人聞菴羅果甘甜可口，即碎其核，甞之甚苦。果種甘味一切皆失。無智慧故，刻核太過，亦復如是。聞「非調伏非不調伏，亦不礙調伏亦不礙不調伏」，以不礙故名「無礙道」；以無礙故，灼然淫泆，公行非法，無片羞恥，與諸禽獸無相異也。此是噉鹽太過，鹹渴成病。經云：「貪著無礙法，是人去佛遠，譬如天與地。」大經云：「言我修無相，則非修無相。」此人行於非道，欲望通達佛道，還自壅塞，同於凡鄙。是住不調，非不住也。復有行人聞「不住調伏，不住不調伏」，怖畏二邊，深自競持，欲修中智，斷破二邊。是人不能即貪欲是道，斷貪欲已，方云是道。此乃住調伏心，非不住也。北方備此兩失。又初學中觀，斷於貪欲，不能得益，放心行不調事。初一行之，薄得片益。自此已後常行不息，亦無復益，行之不改。以己先益，化他令行，又引經為證。受化之徒但貪欲樂，無纖芥道益。崩騰耽湎遂成風俗，污辱戒律，陵穢三寶。周家傾蕩佛法，皆由此來。是住不調及住於調，何關不住調與不調？是名大礙，何關無礙？是增長非道，何關佛道？如是調與不調，皆名不調。何以故？悉是凡情，非賢聖行。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the inconceivable object: as the *Sūtra on the Practice of Non-Action* (無行經) says, "Desire is itself the path; anger and delusion are likewise so — within these three dharmas is contained the whole of the Buddha's Dharma." Although these four divisions are thus themselves the path, one still may not simply follow along with them — to follow along with them would only lead a person toward the evil paths of rebirth — nor may one simply sever them either — to sever them would only produce overweening pride. "Not severing delusion and craving, yet giving rise to every manner of clarity and liberation" — only this is truly named the path. One neither abides in the tamed nor abides in the untamed. To abide in the untamed is the mark of the foolish ordinary being; to abide in the tamed is the dharma of the śrāvaka. Why is this so? The ordinary being, greedily defiled, follows along in compliance with the four divisions of affliction, accumulating birth-and-death heavily, fierce and intractable, difficult to tame — hence this is named "untamed." The two lesser vehicles, dreading birth-and-death as though pursued by an enemy, swiftly flee the three realms altogether. The arhat is named "untamed" in this sense: with the delusions of the three realms exhausted, there is no longer any delusion left to tame, and this very absence of anything left to tame is itself named "tamed" — like a seed scorched so it can never sprout again, or a faculty ruined beyond all use. The bodhisattva is not like this at all: courageous within birth-and-death, yet unattached to the taste of nirvāṇa. Courageous within birth-and-death, being born though there is, in truth, no birth, he is not defiled by the dharma of birth — like a flower rooted in mud, like a physician treating illness. Unattached to the taste of nirvāṇa, knowing emptiness as not merely empty, he is not confined within the very dharma of emptiness he has realized — like a bird flying through open sky, never abiding fixedly within that sky. Not severing affliction, yet entering nirvāṇa; not severing the five desires, yet purifying every faculty — this is precisely the sense of neither abiding in the tamed nor abiding in the untamed. Now, foolish people of this degenerate age, hearing that the āmra fruit is sweet and delicious, at once crack open its pit and taste it, finding it exceedingly bitter — and thereby lose entirely the sweet flavor of the fruit itself. Lacking wisdom, they crack the pit far too eagerly, and this is exactly the same error. Hearing "neither tamed nor untamed, yet obstructing neither the tamed nor the untamed," and taking this freedom from obstruction to be named "the unobstructed path," they thereby, precisely because of this "freedom from obstruction," indulge openly in lust and licentiousness, brazenly practicing what is against the Dharma, without the slightest trace of shame, no different at all from beasts. This is eating salt to gross excess, until the resulting thirst itself becomes an illness. The sūtra says: "One who clings with attachment to the unobstructed dharma is a person far from the Buddha, as far as heaven is from earth." The Great Sūtra says: "To say, 'I cultivate signlessness,' is precisely not to cultivate signlessness at all." Such a person, practicing what is not the path, hoping thereby to attain penetration of the buddha-path, only obstructs himself all the more, becoming no different from the vulgar and the base. This is abiding in the untamed — it is not "non-abiding" at all. There are, further, practitioners who, hearing "neither abiding in the tamed nor abiding in the untamed," dread both extremes, and so hold themselves rigidly in check, wishing to cultivate the wisdom of the middle by severing and breaking through both extremes at once. Such a person is unable simply to take desire itself as the path — only after severing desire does he then call this "the path." This, in fact, is abiding in the very mind of the tamed — it is not "non-abiding" either. The northern school is guilty of both these faults at once. There are, further, those who, in their initial study of the contemplation of the middle, find that severing desire brings them no benefit, and so let the mind loose to practice untamed conduct instead. At first this brings a slight, partial benefit; but continuing thereafter without cease, no further benefit ever comes, and yet the practice is never abandoned. Taking their own initial benefit as proof, they transform others into practicing the same, even citing scripture as their evidence. Those they thereby convert find only the pleasure of desire, without the faintest trace of benefit to the path. This decadence and indulgence eventually becomes an entire social custom, defiling the moral precepts and disgracing the Triple Gem — the collapse of the Buddha's Dharma under the Zhou dynasty arose entirely from just this cause. This is abiding in the untamed, and also abiding in the tamed — how could this have anything at all to do with neither abiding in the tamed nor the untamed? This is a great obstruction — how could it have anything to do with the unobstructed? This only increases what is not the path — how could it have anything to do with the buddha-path? Both this "tamed" and this "untamed" alike must be named "untamed." Why is this so? All of it belongs entirely to ordinary feeling, and is not the conduct of sages and worthies at all.

Neither Abiding, Yet Truly Abiding: The Fourfold Contemplation of Desire 不住調伏不住不調伏 · 喜根勝意

Chinese Source

今言：不住調伏、不住不調伏、不住非調伏非不調伏、不住亦調伏亦不調伏；亦住調伏、亦住不調伏、亦住非調伏非不調伏、亦住亦調伏亦不調伏。何以故？煩惱即空故，不住不調伏；煩惱即假故，不住調伏；煩惱即中故，不住亦調伏亦不調伏；雙照煩惱故，不住非調伏非不調伏。雖不住調、不調等，而實住調、不調等；雖實住調、不調等，而實不住調、不調等。何以故？不偏觀一句故，一句即諸句；一切法趣貪欲故，貪欲是諸法所都故。用此意歷一切句，所謂：計貪欲是有，名住不調伏；計之為無，住於調伏。如是等自在說云云。如是體達，名為「無礙道」；「一切無礙人，一道出生死」。云何出耶？有時體達貪欲畢竟清淨，無累無染，猶如虛空，豁出生死，是名「住調伏得益」。或時縱心觀此貪欲本末因緣，幾種是病？幾種是藥？如和須蜜多入離欲際度脫眾生，作是觀時，豁出生死，是名「住不調得益」。或時二俱非故得益；或時俱觀得益。如是善巧應住、不應住，自他俱益，於菩薩法無所損減，以四悉檀而自斟酌。如喜根為諸居士說巧度法，皆得無生忍。勝意比丘行拙度法，無所克獲。後遊聚落，聞貪欲即道而瞋喜根云何為他說障道法？作擯未成，喜根為說偈，即便身陷。菩薩知其不信，會墮地獄，是故強說，作後世因。巧觀悉檀，若自、若他、若近、若遠，住調伏、不調等，皆當無失；不住調、不調等，亦皆無失。若不得四悉檀意，若住、不住，自織愛網，起他譏慢，自礙礙他，非無礙也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, then, one says: not abiding in the tamed, not abiding in the untamed, not abiding in neither-tamed-nor-untamed, not abiding in both-tamed-and-untamed; and equally, abiding in the tamed, abiding in the untamed, abiding in neither-tamed-nor-untamed, abiding in both-tamed-and-untamed. Why is this so? Because affliction is itself emptiness, one does not abide in the untamed; because affliction is itself the provisional, one does not abide in the tamed; because affliction is itself the middle, one does not abide in both-tamed-and-untamed; because one illuminates affliction from both sides at once, one does not abide in neither-tamed-nor-untamed. Though one does not abide in the tamed, the untamed, and the rest, one in truth abides in the tamed, the untamed, and the rest; though one in truth abides in the tamed, the untamed, and the rest, one in truth does not abide in the tamed, the untamed, and the rest. Why is this so? Because one does not fixedly contemplate any single proposition alone — a single proposition is itself already every proposition; because every dharma tends toward desire, desire itself being the very capital city to which every dharma belongs. Applying this same sense throughout every proposition: to calculate desire as existent is named abiding in the untamed; to calculate it as non-existent is abiding in the tamed — and so on, freely, in whatever manner suits the case. Embodying and penetrating in this way is named "the unobstructed path" — "every person free of obstruction escapes birth-and-death by a single path." How does one escape it? At times, embodying and penetrating desire as ultimately pure, free of all entanglement and defilement, like empty space itself, one breaks decisively free of birth-and-death — this is named "gaining benefit by abiding in the tamed." At other times, letting the mind range freely to contemplate desire's own root and branch, its causes and conditions — how many aspects are illness, how many are remedy? — as when Vasumitrā, having entered the very state of freedom from desire, delivers sentient beings by this very means, one, making this contemplation, breaks decisively free of birth-and-death — this is named "gaining benefit by abiding in the untamed." At still other times, one gains benefit precisely because neither of the two applies; at other times, one gains benefit through contemplating both together. Skillfully judging in this way when one should abide and when one should not, benefiting both oneself and others alike, without the slightest diminishment to the bodhisattva's own Dharma, one weighs and applies the four siddhāntas according to one's own discernment. Thus the bhikṣu Priyaṅkara, expounding the skillful method of liberation to the assembled householders, brought them all to the forbearance of the unborn; while the bhikṣu Sthiramati, practicing the clumsy method, gained nothing at all by it. Later, wandering into a village and hearing it taught that "desire is itself the path," he grew furious at Priyaṅkara — how could he teach others a dharma that obstructs the path? Before the formal expulsion could be completed, Priyaṅkara spoke a verse for him, and Sthiramati's body was at once swallowed into the earth. The bodhisattva, knowing full well that Sthiramati would not believe him and would certainly fall into hell regardless, spoke forcefully anyway, so as to plant a cause for his benefit in a future life. Skillfully contemplating the siddhāntas, whether for oneself or for another, whether for what is near or what is far, abiding in the tamed, the untamed, and the rest is entirely without fault, and not abiding in the tamed, the untamed, and the rest is likewise entirely without fault. But should one fail to grasp the very sense of the four siddhāntas, then whether one abides or does not abide, one only weaves one's own net of attachment, provokes mockery and contempt from others, and obstructs both oneself and others alike — this is not the unobstructed path at all.

Light within Darkness, Darkness within Light 闇中有明 · 明中有闇

Chinese Source

若一念煩惱心起，具十界百法，不相妨礙。雖多不有，雖一不無；多不積，一不散；多不異，一不同；多即一，一即多。經云：「闇中樹影，闇故不見，天眼能見。」是為闇中有明。智障甚盲闇，是為明中有闇。亦如初燈與闇共住。如是明闇不相妨礙，亦不相破。何以故？世間現見，室內然燈，不知向闇去至何處；若燈滅者，闇法復來。來無本源，去無足跡。闇既如此，明亦復然。求闇無闇，明無所破；求明無明，闇無所蓋。雖無明闇，破蓋宛然。不受、不著、不念、不分別。新起者名「不受」；舊起者名「不著」；不內取名「不念」；不外取名「不分別」。妙慧朗然，以是義故，名「不思議」、「不相妨」、「不相除」。若世智燈滅，闇惑更來。若中道智光常住不動，如神珠常照，闇則不來。觀煩惱闇即大智明，顯佛菩提，惑則不來也。準上陰入境可知。如是觀時，追傷己過，廣愍眾生。何以故？理非明闇，以迷惑故，起苦集闇；解治法故，有道滅明。約闇故悲，約明故慈，大誓之心與境俱起。為滿願故，須立要行；行之要者，莫先止觀。四分煩惱，體之即空，名「體真止入空觀」也。觀諸煩惱藥病等法，名「隨緣止入假觀」。觀諸煩惱同真際，名「息二邊止入中道觀」。善巧安心修此三止三觀，成一心三眼三智也。若眼、智未開，破障令遍。觀四分煩惱念念三假，非自、他、共、離、單、複、具足，見思不生；知病識藥，無知不生；非真、非緣，無明不生。橫竪破遍。於即空中翻構苦集，是名「知塞」；於苦集中達即是空，是名「知通」。於諸法藥翻構為病，是名「知塞」；於諸病法即能知藥，是名「知通」。翻法性為無明，名之為「塞」；無明轉即變為明，名之為「通」。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a single thought-moment of afflicted mind arise, it is already complete with the ten dharma-realms and the hundred dharmas, none obstructing any other. Though many, they are not truly existent; though one, they are not truly non-existent; the many do not accumulate, and the one does not disperse; the many are not different from one another, and the one is not identical with itself; the many are precisely the one, and the one is precisely the many. The sūtra says: "The shadow of a tree within darkness — because of the darkness, it cannot be seen, yet the divine eye is able to see it." This is light present within darkness. Where the obstruction of wisdom is at its most blind and dark — this is darkness present within light. It is likewise like a newly lit lamp coexisting together with darkness. Light and darkness, in just this way, neither obstruct one another nor destroy one another. Why is this so? It is plainly seen, in ordinary experience, that when a lamp is lit within a room, one does not know where the darkness has gone in departing; and should the lamp be extinguished, the darkness returns once more. In coming, it has no point of origin; in going, it leaves no trace behind. Just as darkness is so, light too is exactly the same. Seeking darkness, there is no darkness to be found, yet light is not thereby destroyed; seeking light, there is no light to be found, yet darkness is not thereby covered over. Though there is, in truth, neither light nor darkness, the breaking-through and the covering-over remain nonetheless vividly present. One neither receives nor clings, neither dwells upon nor discriminates: what newly arises is named "not receiving"; what has already arisen is named "not clinging"; not grasping inwardly is named "not dwelling upon"; not grasping outwardly is named "not discriminating." Subtle wisdom shines forth luminously — for this very reason it is named "the inconceivable," "that which does not obstruct," "that which does not eliminate." Should the lamp of worldly wisdom be extinguished, the darkness of delusion returns once more; but should the light of the wisdom of the middle way abide constantly, unmoving, like a divine pearl shining forever, darkness will never return at all. Contemplating the very darkness of affliction as itself the great light of wisdom, manifesting the Buddha's own bodhi, delusion will never return — this may be understood by the same standard as the object of the aggregates and entries above. Contemplating in this way, one grieves over one's own past faults, and extends compassion broadly to all sentient beings. Why is this so? Because the underlying principle is neither light nor darkness — it is precisely because of delusion that the darkness of suffering and its origin arises, and precisely because of understanding the method of treatment that the light of the path and cessation is present. In terms of darkness, one gives rise to compassion; in terms of light, one gives rise to loving-kindness — and the mind of the great vow arises together with its very object. To fulfill this vow, one must establish the essential practice; and of every essential practice, none precedes calming-and-contemplation. Embodying the four divisions of affliction as themselves empty is named "the calming that embodies the real, entering the contemplation of emptiness." Contemplating every affliction as itself the dharma of remedy and illness alike is named "the calming that follows conditions, entering the contemplation of the provisional." Contemplating every affliction as identical with the very boundary of the real is named "the calming that stills both extremes, entering the contemplation of the middle way." Skillfully pacifying the mind by cultivating these three calmings and three contemplations accomplishes the single mind's three eyes and three forms of wisdom. Should the eye and the wisdom not yet be opened, one must break through every obstruction comprehensively. Contemplating the four divisions of affliction as, moment by moment, the threefold provisional — neither self, other, both, nor apart, whether single, compound, complete, or wordless — the delusion of view and habituated thought does not arise; knowing illness and recognizing remedy, unknowing does not arise; being neither the real nor the conditioned, ignorance does not arise. This is comprehensive breaking-through, both horizontal and vertical. Within emptiness itself, to construct suffering and its origin once more is named "knowing blockage"; within suffering and its origin, to penetrate through to emptiness itself is named "knowing passage." Within every dharma of remedy, to construct illness once more is named "knowing blockage"; within every dharma of illness, to be able to recognize the remedy is named "knowing passage." To overturn the Dharma-nature into ignorance is named "blockage"; ignorance, once transformed, itself becomes light — this is named "passage."

Reading note: The lamp-and-darkness simile resolves the same puzzle the fascicle keeps circling back to under different figures — how affliction and enlightenment can be neither identical nor separable. Neither light nor darkness has "a point of origin" or "leaves a trace behind" when it departs, which is exactly why seeking to abolish affliction outright, or to indulge it outright, both miss the point equally: the treatise is not describing a substance to be destroyed but a mode of illumination that either obtains or does not.

This clarifies the sense of real wisdom illuminating every object, understood by the same pattern as before. Next is clarified: prajñā both arises and does not arise, opening into a further four propositions — this clarifies the wisdom of the paths and real wisdom together illuminating the four objects, and so on. Next is clarified: prajñā neither arises nor does not arise, the wisdom of the middle way illuminating the four objects, understood accordingly. This makes sixteen propositions, which, joined to the root four, together make thirty-six propositions. Question: what, then, of the Dharma-body? Answer: prajñā, already being identical with the Dharma-body, what need is there to ask further at all? Should one wish to distinguish it further still, this may be grasped through one's own reflection, without troubling the text with further exposition. Further, the Dharma-body, the reward-body, the response-body, and the transformation-body serve as the root of these four bodies. Within each single body, four bodies arise in turn — that is, from the Dharma-body arise the reward-body, the response-body, the transformation-body, and the complete arising of all three together; the remaining bodies are each the same. This makes sixteen bodies. And, further, from the four bodies entering into a single body, body by body likewise, there are again sixteen. Joined to the prior root, this makes thirty-six bodies in all. Every body, body by body, is itself the Dharma-realm — hence each is equally able to arise, and each is equally able to enter, and so on.

Reading note: This thirty-six-and-forty-proposition combinatorics — first for severing/entering nirvāṇa, then re-run identically for prajñā's arising, then again for the four bodies — is not scholastic ornamentation for its own sake. Zhiyi is demonstrating that the same fourfold logical grid (the tetralemma of affirmation, negation, both, and neither, doubled against a second variable) applies uniformly across every major category of Buddhist doctrine — liberation, wisdom, and embodiment alike — because all three are, at root, one and the same "single mark." The exhaustive repetition is itself the argument: if the grid produces coherent, non-arbitrary distinctions everywhere it is applied, the underlying unity it maps is real, not merely asserted.

The Third Object: Contemplating Illness 第三、觀病患境

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, contemplating the object of illness: to possess a body at all is already to possess illness. The four snakes differ in their very nature; water and fire oppose one another. The owl and the goshawk roost together; the python and the rat share a single den. A vessel of poison, a heavy burden — this is the very thicket of every suffering. The four kingdoms are neighbors, forever encroaching upon and ruining one another; when their strength is evenly matched they hold a brief peace, but the moment weakness shows, one swallows the other whole. The four great elements' own flourishing and decline may be understood by exactly this same simile. When the buddhas greet one another, the Dharma says: "Are your illnesses few, your afflictions few?" The Buddha, sharing the human condition, since human beings do indeed have illness, cannot, even as a matter of skillful means, be entirely without it — he simply says "few." Illness carries two distinct senses: first, genuine illness arising from actual cause; second, provisional illness assumed as skillful means resulting in benefit. As when he lay down at Vaiśālī, feigning illness to establish the teaching, using his own bodily illness as the very occasion to instruct ordinary and worldly people, rebuking the lesser vehicle and exhorting the greater — conversing there together with Mañjuśrī, broadly clarifying the three kinds of regulation appropriate to illness arising from cause, and broadly clarifying the four kinds of consolation appropriate to illness resulting as fruit. And as the Tathāgata, resting in cessation, discourses on the constant, or, by means of illness, expounds the very power of the Dharma — all of this belongs to the skillful gateway of entering illness, deliberately drawing forth illness and affliction as a means. Provisional illness of this kind is not what is contemplated here. What is contemplated here is the body born of karmic retribution. The stirring and agitation of the four snakes lays waste to the cultivation of the sagely path; should one be able to contemplate it properly, this only increases the benefit of one's own effort of mind. Those of superior wisdom and sharp faculty, having already understood the bearing-with-adversity discussed above, are thoroughly versed in the object of illness, and need no further extended discussion. For those who do not yet understand, this shall now be distinguished further. It is like felling a great tree — ten thousand strokes of the axe, and it finally falls; like boring through a massive boulder — a hundred million strikes, and it is finally pierced through — hence this further, repeated explanation. Prolonged illness and distant travel are the two great obstacles to meditative concentration. Should the body become afflicted with illness, the merit one has cultivated is lost, and immeasurable transgression arises instead. The sūtra says: "Puncturing the floating raft, dismantling the very bridge, losing correct mindfulness altogether." Illness thus destroying the precepts is like puncturing the floating raft; destroying meditative concentration is like dismantling the bridge; giving rise to a perverse, inverted mind, cherishing this fetid body of pus and blood, thereby destroying the pure Dharma-body — this is named "losing correct mindfulness." For this reason, one ought to contemplate the object of illness. Furthermore, there are those who, in ordinary health, drift along idly, indolent and unfocused; yet when illness strikes urgently, they redouble the exertion of their mind, and are thereby able to accomplish a great many things. Moreover, since the capacities and dispositions of beings differ, awakening may fittingly occur precisely through illness. This is exactly the causes and conditions of the four siddhāntas, and hence the necessity of the object of illness.

Causes and Conditions of Illness's Arising: The Four Elements and Diet 二病起因緣 · 四大 · 飲食

Second, clarifying the causes and conditions of illness's arising, which are six: first, illness from imbalance of the four great elements; second, illness from immoderate diet; third, illness from improper regulation in seated meditation; fourth, illness gained through the opportunity taken by ghosts and spirits; fifth, illness caused by demons; sixth, illness arising from karma. Ginger, cinnamon, and other pungent foods increase fire; sugarcane, honey, and other sweet, cooling foods increase water; pears increase wind; rich, greasy foods increase earth; the cucumber serves as a cause and condition for feverish illness — that is, it is an unsettling food to consume. One who eats must distinguish the very nature of what is eaten. Once food is eaten, it enters the belly and is broken down through digestion: the coarse portion becomes stool and urine, the fine portion is dissolved and, flowing from the three orifices at the waist, seeps into the four limbs, transforming into pure blood that moistens and nourishes the entire body, as dust is moistened by water. Should the body's blood not be sufficiently full, one becomes withered, wasted, and diminished. The turbid portion transforms into fat, so that as the aging faculties diminish they turn to grime, while the newly formed faculties congeal into flesh. Further, the body's fire, residing below, digests the stomach's raw contents, causing food and drink to be broken down and circulated, transformed throughout the entire body. A worldly proverb says: "To attain longevity, keep the feet warm and the head exposed." Knowing that the five organs may thus be harmed, one ought to restrain what diminishes them and partake of what increases them, weighing this with careful discernment.

Next, improper regulation of the counted breath commonly gives rise to shivering fits and cramping contraction of the sinews and vessels. As for the eight sensations: mind conjoined with the four great elements produces four primary sensations, together with four dependent sensations, making eight sensations in all — heaviness, as though sinking downward; lightness, as though rising upward; cold, as in an icy chamber; heat, as in a burning house; roughness, as though pulling against resistance; smoothness, as though rubbing against oil; softness, as though boneless; coarseness, as though the skin were made of bran. These eight sensations divide four to the ascending, four to the descending. The inhaled breath, following the earth element, brings heaviness; the exhaled breath, following the wind element, brings lightness. Again, the inhaled breath, following the water element, brings cold; the exhaled breath, following the fire element, brings heat. Again, the inhaled breath, following the earth element, brings roughness; the exhaled breath, following the wind element, brings smoothness. Again, the inhaled breath, following the water element, brings softness; the exhaled breath, following the fire element, brings coarseness. Should the sensation of heaviness arise, and one repeatedly exhales, this runs contrary to the sensation itself, and illness results at once; the remaining cases may be understood by the same pattern. There is, further, illness produced by applying calming alone, without skillful method: constantly fixing the mind below tends to stir up illness of the earth element; constantly fixing the mind above tends to stir up illness of the wind element; constantly fixing the mind with tense urgency tends to stir up illness of the fire element; constantly fixing the mind with loose slackness tends to stir up illness of the water element. Next, there is illness produced by applying contemplation improperly, in a skewed and one-sided manner: at the moment of first entering the womb, a thought-mind arises and calls forth the mother's own response — the mother then thinks of the five: form, sound, fragrance, flavor, and touch. A single thread of stirring vital energy becomes water, water becomes blood, blood becomes flesh, and flesh forms the five faculties and five organs. Now, should the meditator engaged in seated contemplation think and observe to excess, this damages the five organs and produces illness. Excessive engagement with form stirs the liver; with sound, the kidney; with fragrance, the lung; with flavor, the heart; with touch, the spleen. Furthermore, the eye's excessive engagement with blue stirs the liver; with red, the heart; with white, the lung; with black, the kidney; with yellow, the spleen. The ear's excessive engagement with calling and shouting stirs the liver; with speech, the heart; with weeping, the lung; with moaning, the kidney; with singing, the spleen. The nose's excessive engagement with a rank odor stirs the liver; with a scorched odor, the heart; with a fishy odor, the lung; with a foul odor, the kidney; with a fragrant odor, the spleen. The tongue's excessive engagement with sourness stirs the liver; with bitterness, the heart; with pungency, the lung; with saltiness, the kidney; with sweetness, the spleen. The body's excessive engagement with hardness stirs the liver; with warmth, the heart; with lightness, the lung; with cold, the kidney; with heaviness, the spleen. This is the mutual generation among the five organs — excessive engagement with it results in illness. As for mutual overcoming: excessive engagement with white overcomes the liver; with black, the heart; with red, the lung; with yellow, the kidney; with blue, the spleen — the remaining cases, of sound and the rest, may be understood by the same pattern. Should illness of the five organs lie hidden and difficult to discern, one may divine it through seated meditation and through dreams. Should one, in meditation or in dreams, frequently see the color blue-green — blue-green people, beasts, lions, tigers, and wolves — and give rise to dread, this is illness of the liver. Should one, in meditation or in dreams, frequently see the color red — fire arising, red people or beasts, red blades or weapons, a red young man or woman embracing one closely, or parents, brothers, and the like, giving rise either to joy or to dread — this is illness of the heart. The remaining organs may be verified by their respective colors, following the same pattern below. There is, further, illness produced by contemplation improperly stirring the four elements: should the object of contemplation remain unfixed, now engaging this, now engaging that, the mind itself falls into contention; and from this contention, disordered wind arises, becoming illness of the wind element. It is like guiding an infant's own walking — one must simply let it proceed at its own pace; forcing it along, hoping for swift arrival, only brings about harm instead. Further, fixedly holding to a single object, giving rise to a mind of anxious expectation for its result, the force of wind and heat never exhausted, becomes illness of the fire element. Further, contemplating the object such that, at the moment it arises one takes it as ceasing, and at the moment it ceases one takes it as arising, the mind thereby thrown into contradiction, bringing about itching and pain, becomes illness of the earth element. Further, failing to savor the object contemplated, yet forcing oneself to continue regardless, the water element increases, becoming illness of the water element.

Fourth, ghost-sickness: the four elements and five organs are not, in themselves, ghosts, and ghosts are not, in themselves, the four elements and five organs; but should a ghost enter into the four elements and five organs, this is named "ghost-sickness." Were one to claim there is no such thing as ghost-sickness, a heterodox shaman treating it purely as a matter of ghosts sometimes still succeeds in curing it; were one to claim there is no such thing as illness of the four elements, a physician treating it purely with decoctions and medicine sometimes still succeeds in curing it. There was once a king whose ghost-sickness resided in an empty place within the body, repeatedly killed by needle-treatment there in vain; but once the ghost-king himself came and took up residence over the heart, the needle-practitioner could only fold his hands, powerless. Hence it is known that ghost-sickness genuinely exists. Nor do ghosts trouble a person entirely at random — it is, in truth, on account of a person's own perverse thoughts about various matters, or a wish to know fortune and misfortune in advance, that the wandering hungry-ghost spirit performs its various transformations, its blue-green, yellow, and other colors entering through the five faculties, so that the very ground of one's own mind forms perverse understanding, becoming able to know fortune and misfortune. It may come to know the fortune and misfortune of a single person, a single household, a single village, or an entire country — but this is not the knowledge of a sage. Should it go untreated, in time it will kill the person entirely. Fifth, demon-sickness: this is not, in fact, different from ghost-sickness. But whereas a ghost merely afflicts the body, or kills the body, a demon instead destroys the very mind of contemplation, destroys the life-force of wisdom within the Dharma-body, gives rise to perverse thought, and robs a person of their merit — this is how it differs from a ghost. This, too, arises on account of the practitioner, within seated meditation, harboring perverse thoughts of gain — the demon then manifests all manner of clothing, food and drink, the seven treasures, and various other objects, which, once received with delight, enter the mind and become illness. This illness is difficult to cure, as will be explained further below within the discussion of treatment. Sixth, karma-sickness: this may be entirely the result of karma from a former life, or the present life's own violation of the precepts stirring up karma from a former life. Karmic force, becoming illness, may again be correlated with the five faculties, so that one may know precisely what transgression has been committed. Karma of the transgression of killing produces illness of the liver and the eye; karma of the transgression of drinking alcohol, illness of the heart and the mouth; karma of the transgression of sexual misconduct, illness of the kidney and the ear; karma of the transgression of false speech, illness of the spleen and the tongue; karma of the transgression of stealing, illness of the lung and the nose. Violating the five precepts as karma gives rise to illness in the five organs and five faculties correspondingly, and only once that karma has been dissipated does the illness abate. Should one, in the present life, uphold the precepts strictly, this too may stir up karma and produce illness, hence it is said: "Should there be grave transgression, a headache may remove it — what ought to be suffered heavily in hell is instead repaid lightly among human beings." This is illness arising precisely because karma is on the verge of dissipating. Karma-sickness is of many kinds — swelling and fullness, sallowness and depletion. In every case of illness, one must search out its cause with meticulous care, know the very root of the illness, and only then apply treatment.

Third, clarifying methods of treatment, each suited to its own particular case. Should illness result from overwork or from diet, this requires only proper regulation through medicinal prescriptions to be cured. Should illness result from improper regulation in seated meditation, this instead requires seated meditation with the skillful regulation of the breath and contemplation to be cured — decoctions and medicine are not the fitting remedy here at all. Should the illness be of the ghost or demon variety, this requires the power of profound contemplative practice, together with great sacred mantras, to be cured. Should the illness be karma-sickness, one must apply the power of inner contemplation together with outer repentance to be cured. These various treatments differ from one another, and one ought to grasp the sense proper to each with care — one must not seize the blade by its edge and wound oneself in the process. Here, in terms of seated meditation, six treatments are briefly indicated: first, the calming-focus method; second, the breath-sound method; third, the respiration method; fourth, the visualization method; fifth, the mind-contemplation method; sixth, the medical-arts method. As for the calming-focus method, Master Wen says: "Fix the mind at the navel, the size of a bean; loosen the robe, examine it carefully, and grasp its image clearly; then close the eyes, close the lips and teeth, raise the tongue to the palate, and let the breath settle into calm regularity. Should the mind race outward, gather it back in. Should the image become unclear, again loosen the robe and look at it, familiarizing oneself thoroughly with its appearance, then proceed as before." This method is able to cure every illness, and is likewise able to give rise to every meditative absorption. In making this contemplation, countless sensations may also arise: pain as though pierced by a needle, tension as though pulled by a rope, itching as though gnawed by insects, cold as though drenched with water, heat as though scorched by fire. As sensations of this kind arise, one should apply oneself with single-minded diligence, never allowing oneself to retreat or fall back. Should one pass safely through these sensations, one becomes able to give rise to every meditative absorption; and should the spirit and mind become tranquil and still, this is itself the mark of the concentration of lightning-flash. If this method is able even to attain absorption, how much more, then, is it able to cure illness? The reason the mind is fixed at the navel is that the breath emerges from the navel and returns again to the navel — taking the navel as the boundary of its coming and going readily allows one to awaken to impermanence. Furthermore, at the moment a person enters the womb, consciousness and spirit first unite with the blood at a point fixed to the navel; the navel is able to sustain and connect the body, and is, moreover, the very source of the intestines and stomach — tracing this source, one is able to perceive impurity, and thereby able to halt desire. Should one, within the four foundations of mindfulness, contemplate the navel, one is able to accomplish the gate of mindfulness of the body; should one practice the six wondrous gates, the navel is the gate of calming, and is likewise able to lead one into the path — hence it is so frequently employed. As for its direct application in treating illness: the elixir field is the sea of vital energy, able to lock in and swallow up ten thousand illnesses. Should one fix the mind at the elixir field, the breath and vital energy become harmonized, and illness is thereby cured — this is precisely the sense involved. There is, further, a master who says: "Rising energy filling the chest, pain in both flanks, tightness across the back and spine, pain in the shoulder-well points, feverish oppressive pain in the heart, distress preventing eating, swelling about the heart, cold below the navel, heat above and cold below, disharmony of yin and yang, and coughing with phlegm — these twelve illnesses on the right side are all treated by fixing the mind at the elixir field." The elixir field lies two and a half inches below the navel; should the pain be especially acute, shift the focus of mind toward the Zusanli point; should the pain still not be relieved, shift it further to the crease above the great toenail of both feet, taking relief as the measure of success. Headache, redness and pain in the eyes, heat in the lips and mouth, swelling encircling the base of the nose, sudden abdominal pain, deafness in both ears, and stiffness of the neck — these six illnesses on the right side require that a boundary-point be established between the two feet, upon which the mind is then focused. Should sudden, urgent, watery abdominal distension and pain arise, simply fix the mind single-mindedly upon this point. Should the mind grow oppressed, allow it a brief rest; once slightly recovered, resume, applying the prior method with renewed emphasis. Should relief begin to be felt, one ought to apply the treatment method all the more vigorously. Should acute pain in the lower back and legs result from this practice, visualize a pit ten feet deep beneath both feet, shift the prior boundary-point to the bottom of this pit, and fix the mind upon it as its focus — relief will naturally follow. This is best practiced in a quiet room. Further, constantly fixing the mind at the feet is able to cure every illness. Why is this so? The five forms of consciousness reside in the head, and the mind tends to focus upward; the mind drives the wind, the wind stirs the fire, the fire melts the water, the water moistens the body — hence the upper portion becomes regulated while the lower portion falls into disorder, giving rise to every manner of illness, or to cramping and paralysis of the legs and feet. Further, the five organs are like a lotus flower, drooping downward — when consciousness tends too often upward, the vital energy grows overly strong, striking against the internal organs and thereby overturning them into illness. Should the mind instead focus downward, it drives the fire downward to flow freely, so that food and drink are properly broken down, and the five organs remain in harmony. Fixing the mind at the feet is the very best of treatments — it is constantly employed, and has repeatedly brought profound benefit; treating others by this method has time and again proven effective — Jiang, Wu, Mao, and others are examples of exactly this. Further, fixing the mind attentively upon whatever point the illness occupies, within no more than three days, provided there is no other unusual condition, relief is invariably obtained. Why is this so? It is like a door: opened, it lets in the wind; closed, all is still. The mind engaging an external object is like opening the door; fixing the mind upon the point of pain is like closing it — this follows a natural principle. Further, the mind is like a sovereign, and illness is like a bandit — should the mind settle securely in its proper place, the bandit is scattered and destroyed. Yet it is not always the case that the mind must be fixed directly upon the site of the illness itself. As the *Yellow Emperor's Secret Methods* says: "Heaven and earth's two vital forces intermingle, each possessing the five phases — metal, wood, water, fire, and earth — circulating as in a cycle, so that metal transforms and water is generated, water flows and wood flourishes, wood stirs and fire brightens, fire blazes and earth is made firm — this is mutual generation. Fire, meeting water, has its light extinguished; water, meeting earth, is unable to flow; earth, meeting wood, develops swelling sores; wood, meeting metal, is broken and injured — this is mutual overcoming." As when metal overcomes wood, the lung grows strong while the liver grows weak — one should then fix the mind at the lung, drawing in its white vital energy, and the liver's illness will then be cured. The remaining four organs may be understood by the same pattern. Further, as for using the calming-focus method to treat the four elements: urgent, tight focus treats water; loose, relaxed focus treats fire; focus at the crown treats earth; focus at the feet treats wind.

Apply this exhaling and inhaling with careful attention, never carrying it to excess. Should one be skilled in weighing and applying it, increasing and diminishing as fitting, one is able not only to cure one's own illness, but to aid others as well.

The Respiration Method: Four Kinds of Breath and Twelve Special Breaths 三用息為治

三、用息為治者：夫色心相依而息，譬樵、火相藉而煙。瞻煙清濁，知樵臊濕；察息強軟，驗身健病。若身行風橫起，則痛痒成病，何暇用心？須急治之！先須識息有四伴：有聲曰風，守之則散；結滯曰氣，守之則結；出入不盡曰喘，守之則勞；不聲、不滯，出入俱盡曰息，守之則定。當求靜處結跏，平身正直，縱任身體，散誕四支，布置骨解。當令關節相應，不倚不曲。緩帶，轉側調適，以左手置右手上，大指纔令相詣。縱放頰車，小小開口，四、五過長吐氣。次漸平頭，徐徐閉目，勿令眼臉太急，常使籠籠，然後用息也。用息治八觸相違病者：若因重觸成地大病，偏用出息治之。若發輕觸成風病，偏用入息治之。若發冷觸成水病，偏用出息治之。若發熱觸成火病，偏用入息治之。餘亦如是。若得調和正等，隨意而用。此用常所數息，非作別息也。次、別運十二息者，謂：上、下、焦、滿、增長、滅壞、冷、煖、衝、持、和、補。此十二息帶假想心。所以者何？若初念入胎，即有報息。隨母氣息兒漸長大，風路滑成，兒息出入不復隨母；生在異處，各各有息，名「報息」。依息者，依心而起，如瞋欲時氣息隆盛，此名「依息」也。前六氣，就報息帶想；今十二息，就依息帶想，故不同前也。前明緣五色為五藏病者，此則依藏為病故，用今依息治之。上息治沈重地病；下息治虛懸風病；焦息治脹滿；滿息治枯瘠；增長息能生長四大。外道服氣，祇應服此生長之氣耳。滅壞息散諸癊膜；冷息治熱；煖息治冷；衝息治癥結腫毒；持息治掉動不安；補息補虛乏；和息通融四大。作諸息時，各隨心想，皆令成就。細知諸病用諸息，勿謬用也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, as for the respiration method: form and mind depend upon one another through the breath, just as firewood and flame depend upon one another to produce smoke. Observing whether the smoke is clear or turbid, one knows whether the wood is rank or damp; examining whether the breath is forceful or gentle, one verifies whether the body is healthy or ill. Should wind stir wrongly within the body's own conduct, pain and itching become illness — how could one afford to delay applying the mind to it? One must treat it urgently. First, one must recognize that breath has four accompanying states: audible, this is called "wind" — dwelling on it causes dispersal; congested and obstructed, this is called "qi" — dwelling on it causes further congestion; entering and leaving without ever being fully exhausted, this is called "panting" — dwelling on it causes exhaustion; inaudible and unobstructed, entering and leaving each fully exhausted, this is called "breath proper" — dwelling on it brings settled calm. One should seek out a quiet place, sit cross-legged, keep the body level and upright, let the body relax freely, allow the four limbs to loosen, and arrange the joints properly. See that the joints correspond correctly with one another, neither leaning nor bent. Loosen the belt, shift and adjust the posture for comfort, place the left hand atop the right, the thumbs barely touching one another. Relax the jaw, open the mouth slightly, and exhale slowly at length four or five times. Then gradually level the head, slowly close the eyes, taking care not to shut the eyelids too tightly, keeping them always loosely closed — only then apply the breath. As for using the breath to treat illness arising from sensations applied contrary to the eight sensations: should heaviness of sensation produce illness of the earth element, treat it by emphasizing the exhaled breath; should lightness of sensation produce illness of the wind element, treat it by emphasizing the inhaled breath; should coldness of sensation produce illness of the water element, treat it by emphasizing the exhaled breath; should heat of sensation produce illness of the fire element, treat it by emphasizing the inhaled breath — the remaining cases follow the same pattern. Once harmony and proper balance are attained, apply the breath freely as needed. This employs the ordinary counted breath, not any special or separate breath. Next, as for separately applying the twelve special breaths: these are the ascending, the descending, the parching, the filling, the growth-increasing, the dissolving, the cooling, the warming, the piercing, the sustaining, the harmonizing, and the replenishing breaths. These twelve breaths are each accompanied by visualization. Why is this so? At the very first moment of entering the womb, there is already the retributive breath. Following the mother's own breath, the child gradually grows; once the channel of wind becomes smooth and formed, the child's own breath, entering and leaving, no longer follows the mother's — born into a separate place, each possesses its own breath, named "the retributive breath." The dependent breath arises dependent upon the mind — as when, at a moment of anger or desire, the breath grows vigorous and forceful — this is named "the dependent breath." The prior six breath-sounds are accompanied by visualization in terms of the retributive breath; these twelve breaths are accompanied by visualization in terms of the dependent breath, and hence differ from what came before. What was explained earlier, of the five colors as causes of illness in the five organs, was illness in terms of the organ itself; these twelve breaths, by contrast, treat illness in terms of the dependent breath. The ascending breath treats illness of heavy sinking earth; the descending breath treats illness of hollow, suspended wind; the parching breath treats distension and fullness; the filling breath treats wasting and emaciation; the growth-increasing breath is able to nourish and increase the four elements — non-Buddhist practitioners who ingest vital energy ought properly to ingest only this very growth-increasing breath. The dissolving breath disperses every manner of phlegm-membrane; the cooling breath treats heat; the warming breath treats cold; the piercing breath treats hardened masses and swollen poison; the sustaining breath treats restless agitation; the replenishing breath replenishes depletion and exhaustion; the harmonizing breath brings the four elements into mutual accord. In producing each of these breaths, one accompanies it, in every case, with its corresponding visualization, so that it may be brought fully to completion. One must know each illness in fine detail and apply the corresponding breath accordingly, never applying it mistakenly.

Visualization, Mind-Contemplation, and the Medical Arts 四假想治 · 五觀心治 · 六方術治

Chinese Source

四、假想治者：前氣息中，兼帶用想，今專以假想為治。如辯師治癭法；如患癥人用針法；如《阿含》中用煖蘇治勞損法；如吞蛇法云云。五、觀心治者：不帶想息，直觀於心，內外推求，心不可得，病來偪誰？誰受病者？六、方術治者：術事不知則遠，知之則近。如治咽法、如治齒法、如捻大指治肝等云云。術事淺近，體多貢幻，非出家人所須，元不須學，學須急棄。若修四三昧，泡脆之身損增無定，借用治病，身安道存，亦應無嫌。若用邀名射利、喧動時俗者，則是魔幻魔偽，急棄！急棄！三十六獸嬈人者，應三遍誦呪曰：「波提陀　毘耶多　那摩那　吉利波　阿違婆　推摩陀　難陀羅　憂陀摩　吉利摩　毘利吉　遮陀摩」初得細心，外境觸心驚擲，於是氣上，腹滿胸煩、頭痛悶。此是六神遍身遊戲，因驚擲失守，外有惡神入身，奪其住處，故使如此。若治之法：閉口蹙鼻不令氣出，待氣遍身，然後放氣令長遠；從頭至足遍身皆作出想，牽之令盡。如是三遍，然後誦呪：「支波晝　烏蘇波晝　浮流波晝　牽氣波晝」三遍竟，然後調息，從一至十，命出入息言：「阿那波那，阿晝波晝。」病即差也。若赤痢、白痢、卒中惡、面青、眼反、脣黑，不別人者，以手痛捻丹田，須臾即差。又隨身上有痛處，手杖痛打病處，至四、五十。此復何意？夫諸病無非心作，心有憂愁思慮，邪氣得入。今以痛偪之，則不暇橫想，邪氣去，病除也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, the visualization method: whereas the prior breath methods incorporated visualization as an accompaniment, here visualization alone serves as the treatment. As in the method used by masters skilled in treating goiter; as in the needling method used for those afflicted with hardened masses; as in the method, found in the Āgamas, of using warmed ghee to treat exhaustion and depletion; as in the method of "swallowing the snake," and so on. Fifth, the mind-contemplation method: without any accompanying visualization or breath, one contemplates the mind directly, searching for it within and without, finding the mind itself unobtainable — upon whom, then, does the illness press? Who is it that suffers the illness at all? Sixth, the medical-arts method: matters of technique, unknown, seem remote; known, they become near at hand. As in methods for treating the throat, methods for treating the teeth, or pressing the thumb to treat the liver, and so on. These technical matters are shallow and superficial in themselves, and their substance often verges on mere trickery — they are not what a renunciant genuinely requires, and ideally ought never to be studied at all; once studied, they ought to be cast aside without delay. Should one, in cultivating the four kinds of samādhi, find this fragile, bubble-like body's own decline and increase unpredictable, borrowing such methods to treat illness so that the body remains at ease and the path is preserved is likewise unobjectionable. But should one use them to court fame, seek profit, or stir up worldly commotion, this is demonic illusion and demonic falsehood — cast it aside at once! Cast it aside at once! For the thirty-six beast-spirits that disturb a person, one should recite three times the mantra: "Batidha, Piyeduo, Namona, Jilibo, Aweipo, Tuimotuo, Nantuoluo, Youtuomo, Jilimo, Piliji, Zhetuomo." When one first attains a refined, subtle mind, an outer object may strike the mind with a sudden, startling jolt, whereupon vital energy rises, the belly grows distended, the chest oppressed, and the head aches and throbs. This occurs because the six spirits, roaming freely throughout the body, lose their proper posts through this very startled jolt, and an evil spirit from outside enters the body, seizing their vacated dwelling — hence this condition results. As for the method of treatment: close the mouth, pinch the nose, and prevent the breath from escaping; wait until the vital energy has pervaded the entire body, then release the breath, letting it extend outward at length, visualizing it exiting from head to foot over the whole body, drawing it out until fully exhausted. Repeat this three times, then recite the mantra: "Zhibozhou, Wusubozhou, Fuliubozhou, Qianqibozhou." Once this is repeated three times, then regulate the breath, counting from one to ten, directing the in-breath and out-breath while saying: "Ānāpāna, āzhoubozhou." The illness will then be cured. Should there be red or white dysentery, sudden collapse from noxious influence, a bluish complexion, rolled-back eyes, or blackened lips, such that the person can no longer recognize others, press firmly and painfully upon the elixir field with the hand, and relief comes shortly after. Further, wherever pain occurs upon the body, strike the painful site forty or fifty times with a stick. What is the sense of this? All illness, without exception, is produced by the mind — where the mind holds worry, sorrow, and anxious thought, perverse vital energy is able to enter. By pressing this pain upon the person now, one leaves no leisure for wayward thought, the perverse energy departs, and the illness is thereby removed.

Benefit and Harm, and the Ten Conditions for Successful Treatment 四損益 · 十法必有良驗

Chinese Source

四、明損益：損益皆有漸頓。若用息太過，五藏頓翻者：即雖未翻，漸就增劇，以至頓翻者。若人巧修，豁然頓益者：即雖與病相持，後當漸愈者。如服湯藥，年月將漸，乃得其益。內治亦然。若心利病輕、心利病重、心鈍病輕、心鈍病重，致有漸頓不同也。夫世間醫藥費財用工，又苦澁難服，多諸禁忌，將養惜命者死計將餌。今無一文之費，不廢半日之功，無苦口之憂，恣意飲噉，而人皆不肯行之。庸者不別貨，韻高和寡，吾甚傷之！能具十法，必有良驗。一、信，二、用，乃至第十、識遮障。信是道元，佛法初門。如治癩人信血是乳；敬駱駝骨是真舍利。決信此法能治此病，不生狐疑。信而不用，於己無益，如執利劍不用擬賊，翻為彼害。不用亦爾。何意須勤？初、中、後夜，朝暮專精，以得汗為度。鑽火中息，火難可得。不勤亦爾。何謂為恒？恒用治法，念念在緣而不動亂。何謂別病？別病因起，如上所說。若不識病，浪行治法，不相主對，於事無益。何謂方便？善巧用治，吐納得所，運想成就，不失其宜。如琴弦緩急，輾轉軫柱，輕重手指，聲韻方調。何謂為久？若用未益，不計日月，習不休廢。何謂知取捨？益則勤用；損則改治。何謂知將護？善識禁忌，行來飲食，不使觸之。何謂識遮障？用益勿𠹕說；未益勿疑謗。向人說者，未差不差，差已更發，更治不差，設差倍功。若能十法具足，用上諸治，益定無疑。我當為汝保任此事終不虛也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, clarifying benefit and harm: both benefit and harm occur either gradually or suddenly. Should one apply the breath methods to excess, the five organs may overturn suddenly — though not yet overturned, they gradually worsen, step by step, until at last they overturn all at once. Should a person practice skillfully, sudden benefit may open up all at once — though the illness still contends for a time, it will thereafter gradually be cured. It is like taking a decoction of medicine, whose benefit accrues only gradually, month by month and year by year; internal treatment is exactly the same. Whether the mind is keen with a light illness, keen with a severe illness, dull with a light illness, or dull with a severe illness, the resulting benefit or harm arrives gradually or suddenly according to each case. Ordinary worldly medicine costs money and labor, and is, besides, bitter and difficult to swallow, hedged about with many prohibitions — those who nurture their health and cherish their lives regard even their meals as a kind of calculated remedy. Here, by contrast, there is not a single coin of expense, not half a day's labor lost, no bitterness to endure in the mouth — one may eat and drink freely as one pleases — and yet people, without exception, refuse to practice it. The vulgar cannot distinguish true value from false, and what is elevated in tone finds few who can appreciate it — I grieve deeply over this. Should one be able to fulfill ten conditions in full, good results are certain to follow. First, faith; second, application; and so on through the tenth, recognizing obstruction. Faith is the very root of the path, the first gateway of the Buddha's Dharma. It is like one afflicted with leprosy believing that blood is milk, or venerating camel-bone as a genuine relic of the Buddha — one must firmly believe that this very method is able to cure this very illness, without giving rise to the slightest doubt or hesitation. Should one believe, yet fail to apply it, this brings no benefit to oneself whatsoever — like grasping a sharp sword yet failing to use it against a bandit, so that it becomes, instead, a source of harm to oneself. Failure to apply the method is exactly the same. Why must one apply diligence? In the first, middle, and last watches of the night, and morning and evening alike, one must apply oneself with single-minded intensity, taking the appearance of sweat as the measure of success — it is like drilling for fire and stopping midway, so that the fire can never be obtained; failure to apply diligence is exactly the same. What is meant by constancy? Constantly applying the method of treatment, keeping the mind fixed upon its object at every single thought-moment, without wavering or disorder. What is meant by distinguishing the illness? Distinguishing the causes from which the illness arises, as explained above — should one fail to recognize the illness, and apply the treatment method at random, without properly matching remedy to illness, this brings no benefit to the matter at all. What is meant by skillful means? Skillfully and adeptly applying the treatment, exhaling and inhaling exactly as suited, carrying the visualization through to completion, never losing what is fitting to the case — it is like the strings of a lute, tightened or loosened, the pegs and bridge adjusted this way and that, the fingers pressing lightly or heavily, until the tone and pitch are finally brought into harmony. What is meant by persistence? Should application not yet show benefit, one must not count the days and months that have passed, but continue the practice without ever ceasing. What is meant by knowing what to take up and what to set aside? Where benefit results, apply the method diligently; where harm results, change the treatment. What is meant by knowing how to safeguard the treatment? Skillfully recognizing what is prohibited, and, in one's daily coming and going, eating and drinking, taking care never to violate it. What is meant by recognizing obstruction? Where benefit has resulted, one must not speak of it carelessly and boastfully; where benefit has not yet resulted, one must not give rise to doubt or slander. Speaking of it to others prematurely, whether the illness has not yet been cured, or has been cured only to break out again, or, treated further, still resists cure, or, even once cured, requires double the effort thereafter. Should one be able to fulfill these ten conditions completely, and apply the treatments described above, benefit is certain to follow without the slightest doubt. I myself hereby vouch to you that this matter shall never prove empty or false.

Reading note: The extended medical section in this fascicle — pulse diagnosis, organ pathology, the six breath-sounds, the twelve special breaths, mantric and pressure-point remedies — reflects a genre convention of early Chinese meditation manuals, in which the physical discipline of prolonged seated practice was understood to carry real somatic risk, requiring the same systematic, empirically minded treatment as any other domain of the treatise. Zhiyi's own closing personal guarantee ("I myself hereby vouch to you that this matter shall never prove empty or false") is unusually direct for the treatise as a whole, and signals that this material was drawn from his own observed clinical experience guiding students through illness in retreat, not merely inherited from textual sources.

Cultivating Calming-and-Contemplation: The Power of the Three Kinds of Samādhi 五修止觀

Chinese Source

若善修四三昧，調和得所，以道力故必無眾病；設小違返，冥刀扶持，自當銷愈。假令眾障峯起，當推死殉命，殘生餘息，誓畢道場，捨心決定，何罪不滅？何業不轉？陳鍼、開善云云。豈有四大、五藏而不調差？如帝釋堂，小鬼敬避；道場神大，無妄侵撓。又城主剛，守者強；城主恇，守者忙。心是身主，同名、同生天，是神能守護人，心固則強。身神尚爾，況道場神耶？如《大論》釋精進，鬼黏五處云云。但一心修三昧，眾病銷矣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should one skillfully cultivate the four kinds of samādhi, harmonized and properly applied, then, by the very power of the path, illness of every kind ought never to arise at all; and should some slight deviation still occur, an unseen, sword-like power sustains and supports one, so that it naturally dissolves and heals of itself. Should every manner of obstruction rise up like a mountain peak, one ought to resolve to stake one's very life upon it, offering what remains of one's own breath and being, vowing to complete the practice in the place of enlightenment — with the mind thus resolved and unwavering, what transgression could fail to be dissolved? What karma could fail to be transformed? Chen Zhen and Kaishan, and so on, are examples of this. How, then, could the four elements and five organs possibly remain unregulated and uncured? It is like the hall of Śakra, where lesser spirits dare not trespass out of reverence — the great spirit of the place of enlightenment likewise permits no wanton intrusion. Further, when the lord of a city is resolute, its guards are strong; when the lord of a city is timid, its guards grow flustered. The mind is the very sovereign of the body — the spirits that share one's name and share one's birth in the heavens are able to guard and protect a person accordingly, and, the mind being firm, they grow strong in turn. If even the body's own attendant spirits are thus, how much more so the spirit of the place of enlightenment itself? As the Great Treatise explains in its discussion of diligence, a demon clinging to the five points of the body, and so on. Simply cultivate samādhi with a single, undivided mind, and every illness will be dissolved.

The Discursible Object: Ten Dharma-Realms from Illness, and the Chain of Dependent Origination 思議境 · 十二因緣

Chinese Source

五、修止觀，例前為十云云。先簡思議者，病因緣故，生十法界。如為病故退失本心、棄廢禪定、誹謗三寶，不惟先罪招禍，而言修善無福，起大邪見。又惜身養命，魚肉辛酒，非時無度。或病差身壯，五欲恣情。善心都盡，惡業熾盛，起上、中、下罪。是為因病造三惡法界。若人自念：此病困苦，皆由往日不善折致，深生慚愧，不敢為非；雖嬰困篤，而善心無改，起上、中、下善。是為因病造三善法界。若遭疾病，因怖畏生死，知此病身酬於前業。若構生死，將來流轉復何窮極？苦集危脆，世世相隨，為之受惱；當求寂滅無相涅槃。是為因病起聲聞法界。又觀此病，病我色心，因於此病而致老死。死由於生，生由昔有，有從取生，取從愛生，愛從受生，受從觸生，觸從六入生，六入從名、色生。色即四大、五根；名即四心。觀此根、大，復從何生？青色從木生，黃色從地生，赤色從火生，白色從風生，黑色從水生。又觀木從水生，水從風生，風從地陽氣生，地從火生，火從木生，木還從水生。如是追逐，周而復始。無自生者。觀外五行既爾，內五藏色亦復如是。肝從青氣生，心從赤氣生，肺從白氣生，腎從黑氣生，脾從黃氣生。此之肝藏為自體生？為從他生？即知肝藏從腎生，腎從肺生，肺從脾生，脾從心生，心從肝生。肝不自生，還從腎生。如是內求四大、五藏，既其無體，何故不壞？四心持之：識心持地，想心持風，受心持火，行心持水，是故不壞。此之四心為自生？為不自生？即知行心從受生，受心從想生，想心從識生，識從過去行生，過去行從無明生，無明從妄想生，妄想還從妄想生。經云：「妄想生妄想，輪迴十二緣。」如狂渴人，見焰為水，南向逐之，逐之不得，大喚言水，空中響應，謂：「己大南，水應在北。」迴頭北走。如是四方皆逐不得。遂大懊惱，謂水入地，爮地吼喚，身體疲極，轉更至闇，亦復不得。南走喻舌逐味；北走喻耳逐聲；西走喻鼻逐香；東走喻眼逐色；爮地喻身逐觸；到闇喻意逐無明。如是六根，遍走諸塵，無一可得，亦不得因緣和合之相，但自疲苦。既覺知已，不復更走。以不走故，身心定住。心定住故，豁爾悟解，發得因緣正智，知此色、心等，從本已來體性寂靜，非生非滅；妄想顛倒，謂有生滅。若不隨妄想，則無明滅乃至老死滅，畢故不造新，如不然火，是則無煙。既不得無明、老死，病為病誰？是名觀病起緣覺法界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, cultivating calming-and-contemplation, following the same pattern as before, in ten parts. First, simplifying to the discursible: illness, through its own causes and conditions, generates the ten dharma-realms. As when, on account of illness, one loses one's original resolve, abandons meditative concentration, and reviles the Triple Gem — not only does one's prior transgression bring calamity upon oneself, but one further declares that cultivating good brings no reward at all, giving rise to a grave and perverse view. ○第四、觀業相境者：行人無量劫來所作善惡諸業，或已受報，或未受報。若平平運心，相則不現；今修止觀，能動諸業，故善惡相現。疑者言：「大乘平等，何相可論？」今言不爾，祇由平等鏡淨，故諸業像現。《光明》云：「將證十地，相皆前現。」《阿含》云：「將證初果，八十八頭蛇於其前死。」大小兩乘相文甚多。又《法華》云：「深達罪福相，遍照於十方。」罪福祇是善惡業耳。《淨名》云：「於第一義而不動，善能分別諸法相。」故汝難非也。明業相為四：一、相發因緣，二、正發相，三、料簡，四、止觀。因緣者，有內、有外。內者，止觀研心，心漸明淨，照諸善惡。或可以止止惡，惡方欲滅；以觀觀善，善方欲生。或可以止止惡，惡因靜生；以觀觀善，善因觀滅。無量業相出止觀中，如鏡被磨，萬像自現。外者，諸佛慈悲常應一切；眾生無機不能得覩。以止觀力，能感諸佛示善惡禪，諸業則現。如持花鬘示於大眾。是名「內外因緣」。若得此意，細判罪福，皂白無濫，堪為方等師，調伏於他。今但研心止觀，令業謝行成，一心取道，何用曲辨相耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, contemplating the object of the marks of karma: the practitioner has, throughout immeasurable kalpas, produced every kind of wholesome and unwholesome karma, some already having borne its retributive result, some not yet having done so. Should one apply the mind in an ordinary, even manner, its marks fail to manifest at all; but now, cultivating calming-and-contemplation, one is able to stir these very karmic deeds into motion, and so the marks of good and evil manifest. Some may doubt this, saying: "The Mahāyāna teaches universal equality — what marks could there possibly be to discuss?" Here it is said that this is not so at all — it is precisely because the mirror of equality itself is perfectly clear that the images of every karmic deed manifest within it. The Sūtra of Golden Light says: "As one is about to realize the ten grounds, every mark manifests beforehand." The Āgama says: "As one is about to realize the first fruit, eighty-eight-headed serpents perish before one's very eyes." Passages of this kind, in both the greater and lesser vehicles, are exceedingly numerous. Further, the Lotus Sūtra says: "Deeply penetrating the marks of transgression and merit, illuminating the ten directions everywhere at once." Transgression and merit are simply unwholesome and wholesome karma. The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* says: "Unmoved within the ultimate truth, yet skillfully able to distinguish the marks of every dharma." Hence your objection does not hold. Clarifying the marks of karma is treated in four parts: first, the causes and conditions of these marks' manifestation; second, the marks' actual manifestation; third, discriminating analysis; fourth, calming-and-contemplation. As for causes and conditions: there are both inner and outer. As for the inner: calming-and-contemplation refines the mind, the mind growing gradually clear and pure, illuminating every wholesome and unwholesome deed. It may be that calming stills what is evil, evil then on the verge of extinguishing; and contemplation illuminates what is good, good then on the verge of arising. Or it may be that calming stills what is evil, evil arising precisely through this very stillness; and contemplation illuminates what is good, good extinguished precisely through this very illumination. Immeasurable marks of karma emerge from within calming-and-contemplation, just as, once a mirror is polished, ten thousand images manifest of their own accord. As for the outer: the compassion of every buddha constantly responds to all beings without exception, yet sentient beings, lacking the proper occasion, are unable to perceive it. Through the very power of calming-and-contemplation, one is able to move the buddhas to manifest wholesome and unwholesome forms of meditative vision, and karmic deeds thereupon manifest — like holding forth a garland of flowers before the assembled multitude. This is named "the inner and outer causes and conditions." Should one grasp this very sense, one may discriminate transgression from merit in fine detail, black from white without the slightest confusion, and thereby become fit to serve as a master of the Vaipulya teachings, regulating others accordingly. Here, however, one need only refine the mind through calming-and-contemplation, so that karma dissolves and practice is accomplished, seizing the path with a single, undivided mind — what need is there, then, for such elaborate discrimination of these marks?

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the manifestation of karmic marks: their manifestation follows no fixed order, and shall simply be explained here as convenient. First, clarifying the manifestation of wholesome marks, which have six patterns: first, the mark of retributive fruit manifesting; second, the mark of the habituated cause manifesting; third, the retributive mark manifesting first, the habituated mark manifesting later; fourth, the habituated mark manifesting first, the retributive mark manifesting later; fifth, the habituated and retributive marks manifesting simultaneously; sixth, no fixed order between the two. When these karmic marks manifest, they do so in a bewildering variety of forms — recognizing these six patterns, one may distinguish them without error. What is meant by "habituated cause" and "habituated fruit"? Masters of the *Abhidharma* say: "The habituated cause is a cause belonging to its own category; the habituated fruit is its corresponding, dependent result." Habituation is also named "habituated continuity" — a seed of one's own category generating its like, so that a later thought-moment, arising, continues the habituation of the moment before; the earlier thought-moment serves as cause, the later as fruit. This sense applies across all three moral natures. Treatise-masters, however, confine it to the wholesome and unwholesome alone — the morally indeterminate has no habituated continuity. As for "retributive cause" and "retributive fruit": these pertain to a different lifetime. The habituated cause and habituated fruit of a former life are both together named the retributive cause, since this cause draws forth a later fruit, and is hence designated by the term "retribution," named "the retributive cause"; the body received thereafter, in one of the five destinies, is precisely "the retributive fruit." Within this present retributive body, wholesome and unwholesome deeds again arise, continuing in habituation as habituated cause and habituated fruit. Considered wholly in relation to the former life, this habituated continuity is itself a fruit; considered in relation to the life to come, this same habituated continuity is itself a cause. Masters of numerical categories explain that obtaining the body of a pigeon or sparrow is a retributive fruit, while excessive lust is a habituated fruit; treatise-masters, by contrast, hold that both the pigeon's body and the excessive lust are alike retributive fruits, since lust itself arises from greed, and greed is the habituated fruit. Further, affliction arising in the present life is named "habituated cause"; the karma thereby formed is "retributive cause." Affliction arising in a later life is named "habituated fruit"; the suffering and pain thereof is named "retributive fruit." Should one, in seated meditation, merely see various marks appear, this is named "the mark of retributive fruit manifesting" — since it arises from a cause in a former life, it may equally be called "retributive cause." And since the very cause capable of arising also draws forth a later retributive result, the two terms are, in effect, interchangeable — here, however, it is simply classified as the mark of retributive fruit. Should one, within meditation, see no such marks at all, but instead find the mind suddenly stirred to action, this is the manifestation of "habituated cause" — since it, too, is able to draw forth a future fruit, it may equally be called "habituated fruit"; since it repays a cause from the past, the two terms are again interchangeable — here, however, it is simply classified as habituated cause. Wholesome marks are exceedingly numerous; here they shall be treated briefly in terms of the six pāramitās. As for the manifestation of the mark of dāna: one may, in meditation, suddenly see a supreme field of merit, images of the Triple Gem, the sagely assembly and great worthies, one's own parents and teachers, or virtuous persons, receiving one's own offering; or one may see the field of compassion receiving one's offering; or one may see both fields together, though not actually receiving the offering, nonetheless rejoicing in it; or one may not see any field at all, whether receiving or not, but see only the offered goods arrayed and spread out in abundance; or one may not see the offered goods at all, but see only a field of pure ground; or this may represent the mark of giving's own retribution in the present life, or represent the mark of giving's retribution in a former life; or one may see a person accomplished in the practice of dāna approach and praise the very act of giving. Occurrences of this kind are all marks of retributive fruit manifesting. Next, should one see no such marks at all, but the mind simply grows stirred, wishing to practice generosity, reverently making offerings to the Triple Gem, to parents, and to teachers; or grieving over the poor and suffering and wishing to relieve them; or becoming, of one's own accord, especially clear and penetrating in the very dharma-gate of dāna — states of mind of this kind are all marks of habituated cause manifesting. Whether this mind of giving arises first and the retributive mark appears afterward, or the retributive mark appears first and this mind of giving arises afterward, or both arise together, or arise in no fixed order, may be understood according to the particular case. When the mark of śīla manifests, there are likewise six patterns. One may see the ten preceptors, the robe and bowl, the ordination platform, the formal proceedings, and feel joyful reverence toward them; or, though not seeing this mark directly, one may see one's own robes clean and pure, one's own deportment surpassing the assembly; or one may see a person of constant precept-observance, his countenance radiant, his bearing calm and dignified, coming to praise the precepts. Marks of this kind are all the manifestation of the retributive fruit of upholding the precepts. At other times, none of these marks appear at all, but the mind spontaneously grows stirred to uphold the precepts, one's own conviction is that the categories of pure precepts are insufficient in themselves and ought to be upheld more fully still; or one wishes to correct and rectify those who violate the precepts, bringing them all into accord with the Dharma; or one comes, of one's own accord, to understand the texts of the vinaya, thoroughly versed in its every category — this is the manifestation of habituated cause. Whether this arises first, later, together, or in mixed order, may be understood according to the particular case. As for the manifestation of the mark of kṣānti: one may see a person capable of forbearance; or see oneself actually practicing an act of forbearance; or see one's own body upright and pure, hands and feet perfectly proportioned, rare indeed in this world; or see an upright person of forbearance come and praise the practice of forbearance — these are marks of the retributive fruit of forbearance. Or one may directly give rise to the mind of forbearance, and further understand the dharma-gate of forbearance — this is named the manifestation of the habituated cause of forbearance. Whether prior, subsequent, simultaneous, or mixed, may be understood accordingly. As for the mark of vīrya: one may see a person of diligence; or see one's own deeds of diligence; or see one's own body possessed of great vigor and heroic strength; or see a person of constant diligence, unceasing by day and by night, coming to praise diligence itself — these are marks of the retributive fruit of diligence. Or one may see no such mark at all, but simply give rise to the mind of diligence, sparing not one's own body through the first, middle, and last watches of the night; or come to penetrate thoroughly the dharma-gate of diligence — this is named the mark of the habituated cause of diligence. Whether prior, subsequent, simultaneous, or mixed, may be understood accordingly. The marks of dhyāna will be explained at length within the object of meditative states below; the marks of prajñā will be explained at length within the object of the bodhisattva. Since the habituated and retributive marks of the six pāramitās already number six kinds each, every other wholesome dharma follows exactly the same pattern. Should one search out this method in fine detail, the longer one pursues it the clearer it becomes — there is no need to elaborate further here, nor would it even be proper to do so at excessive length; it is a matter to be received directly, face to face, and transmitted orally, to be discussed at whatever length suits the occasion.

Debt to the Triple Gem: "Accomplishment" and "Establishment" 負三寶物 · 成立

Chinese Source

諸方等師相傳云：「負三寶物，其相現時，決應須償。」南岳師云：「若自有物，償者善；若自無物，欲廢行法，四方馳求。」此有二義。眾生昔罪何量！負貸三寶非止一條，如羅漢先直取道，未遑償業，故名「觝責」。行者若廢道場而行乞匃，紛動數年，豈非魔事？今且未償，但決志修行諸佛實法，展我「成」、「立」。成者，待破煩惱入無生忍，於法身地廣供養一切三寶，還入生死以償眾生；菩薩爾時不名「觝責」。立者，待功夫著滿、名行竪立、果報自至時，當償三寶，非是觝負不作償心；小乞申延，期於展立耳。此豈非好事？若廢行法，出於道場，此決須償。不得讀誦聽學、營私眾務，決應方便求財償之。此釋與《優婆塞戒經》同。經云：「若負三寶物人，正事修道，欲求須陀洹乃至阿羅漢者，則不須償也；不學道，應急償也。」阿羅漢人若用佛物，此則無罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Masters of the Vaipulya teachings have handed down the saying: "When the mark of a debt owed to the Triple Gem manifests, one must certainly repay it." The master of Nanyue says: "Should one possess the means oneself, repayment is the better course; should one lack the means, and yet abandon one's own practice to rush about in every direction seeking it, this carries two distinct senses." The transgressions sentient beings have accumulated in past lives are beyond any measure — debts owed to the Triple Gem are hardly confined to a single item. As when an arhat, seizing the path directly without delay, has no leisure to repay such karma beforehand, this is named "the outstanding obligation." Should a practitioner, however, abandon the place of practice in order to go begging for funds, disturbing himself for years on end in the process, is this not itself already the very work of Māra? Here, then, though the debt is not yet repaid, one ought simply resolve firmly to cultivate the genuine Dharma of every buddha, thereby carrying forward one's own "accomplishment" and "establishment." As for accomplishment: once affliction is broken through and the forbearance of the unborn is entered, one makes vast offerings to the entire Triple Gem from the very ground of the Dharma-body itself, and returns again into birth-and-death in order to repay sentient beings — the bodhisattva, at that time, is no longer named one under "outstanding obligation." As for establishment: once one's own effort reaches its full completion, one's reputation and conduct firmly established, and the fruit of one's practice arrives of its own accord, one then repays the Triple Gem in full — this is not at all the same as an outstanding debtor who forms no intention whatsoever of repayment; it is, rather, a small request for an extension, made in expectation of this very future establishment. How could this not be regarded as a genuinely good course? Should one, however, abandon one's own practice and leave the place of cultivation entirely for this purpose, the debt must then certainly be repaid at once — one may not continue reading, reciting, listening, and studying, or attending to private, worldly affairs, but must resolutely seek out the means by whatever expedient necessary and repay it. This explanation accords with the *Upāsaka-śīla Sūtra*, which says: "Should a person owing a debt to the Triple Gem be genuinely engaged in cultivating the path, seeking the fruit of stream-entry or even up to arhatship, he need not repay it; should he not be studying the path at all, he ought to repay it urgently." Should an arhat make use of the Buddha's own property, this, moreover, carries no transgression at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying evil marks: evils are exceedingly numerous; here they shall be treated in terms of the six coverings, each of the six likewise possessing its own six patterns. As for the mark of the covering of stinginess: one may see the Triple Gem, one's teachers, or one's parents appearing gaunt and haggard, or exposed and unclothed, or dressed in ragged, tattered garments; or afflicted with hunger and starvation, the monastery halls standing empty and desolate; or one may see every object carefully guarded, sealed and locked away — differing from what came before. Where before one rejoiced at the very sight of an object, now, seeing a beggar, one reacts to the very same object with anger and abuse. Where objects before represented instruments of generosity, they now represent instruments of stinginess. Or one may see a stingy person approach directly before oneself. These are named the marks of the retributive fruit of the covering of stinginess, likewise possessing six patterns understood by the same prior scheme. As for the mark of precept-violation: one may see images of the Triple Gem, one's teachers, elders, and parents with their heads severed, or sunk helplessly into the ground; or their bodies torn and mutilated, beaten and afflicted with suffering; or their heads separated from their bodies, monastery buildings fallen into ruin; or see one's parents cursing and reviling, the Triple Gem rebuking and condemning; or see a person delighting in slaughter, a butcher, approaching directly before oneself; or evil birds and venomous creatures crawling upon one's own head and body — these are all marks of the retributive fruit of the covering of anger, likewise possessing six patterns understood by the same prior scheme. Should one see impure filth — excrement, urine, corpses, foul objects blocking the road, or deep water crossing the path, preventing further passage; or see oneself coupling with a former sexual partner, exhibiting impure marks, filthy and shameful; or see one's own body giving off a foul odor; or see a person of excessive lust come and speak of licentious matters; or see beasts and humans copulating — all of this is the mark of the retributive fruit of the transgression of sexual misconduct, likewise possessing six patterns. Should one see the very place where an object was stolen in a past life, its rightful owner approaching to curse and angrily bind that very object; or see a person skilled in theft come and urge one toward the act of stealing — these are all marks of the retributive fruit of stealing, likewise understood by the same prior scheme of six patterns. Should one see one's parents, teachers, and others contending in dispute, harboring venomous anger, scheming in various ways to sow discord and slander oneself; or see a person of much verbal transgression approaching — this is precisely the mark of the retributive fruit of the four verbal transgressions, likewise sixfold. Or one may see a drunken person vomiting and lying in disarray; or see one's own body sunk in stupor and confusion — these are all marks of the retributive fruit of drinking alcohol, likewise sixfold. All of these together are the marks of the retributive fruit of the covering of precept-violation. The remaining four coverings may be understood by the same pattern, and hence are not recorded here in full detail. Furthermore, inward pain in the mind is the habituation of killing; inward heaviness in the mind is the habituation of stealing; inward agitation in the mind is the habituation of sexual misconduct; and a mind possessing all three together is the habituation of even division among them.

三、料簡善惡相現為障不同：或非障而障、障而非障、障非障俱障、障非障俱不障。非障而障者：若人先發善相，當時歡喜，後起愛慢，輕忽於他；恃此證相作貢高本，漸染名利，過患轉生；心退法壞，捨戒還俗，無惡不造。豈非初因不障之善，後致大障之惡耶？障而非障者：如先發惡相，慚愧怖畏，勤懺此惡，斷相續心永不起罪；勤行眾善，至辦大事。豈非初因於障，後致不障耶？俱障、俱不障，例可知云云。若非障而障者：此是善將滅而相現，此善滅表惡生。若障非障者：此是惡將滅而相現，此惡表善生。若障不障俱不障者：此相表善不滅、惡不生。若障不障俱障者：此表惡不滅、善不生。此約初善為語，謂善不障、惡為障，如上分別。若約真諦為言者，上諸善惡悉皆是障。故《淨業障經》云：「一切惡障，一切善障」。若約假為語，真諦善惡，俱皆是障。若約中為語，假上善惡，俱皆是障，故障不可盡。復次，善惡習因心起，是則易知；善惡報果相起，是則難知。若善報相扶，善習因心起，或前、或後現者，多是性善相；孤然起者，多是無作善相。惡報果相扶，惡習因心起，或前、或後，多是性惡；不扶習起，多是違無作惡。復次，善惡報果孤然起者，雖以無作往判，理復難明，多好雜魔。若欲分別，須細意撿挍，用空、明、善、惡等十法往驗。若過、不及，則是魔相；異此乃是無作也。又三相往驗，所謂：久久住、數數來、又壞禪心，此三是魔相也。無此三是無作。復次，諸惡相現時，初現瞋怒，再來平平，三現歡喜；或人諫曉，或人驅逐，當知皆是惡欲滅相也。夫發心真正、慧解分明，善識諸相一一無謬，不為諸障所惑，打心入理，更增其明。行有餘力，分別業門；雖通達自在，兼以化他。若分別業相不能縷碎，但總知是障，無所取著，直打心觀理，業不能礙。若本無解心，又發意邪僻，見此相已而生愛著，魔得其便入示吉凶，更相因倚，貿易財食，死墮鬼道；此非鬼禪更謂誰耶？若自正正他，須得其意。親自行證，又師氏口決，方可彰言。莫輒媒衒，妄作寒熱，禍則大矣！深囑！深囑！後生慎之！問：道場神護，怨責那得擾動？答：實爾。如世遊軍虞候，但覘非防惡，責主切物所不能遮。業來責報準此可解。復次，諸業名、教、體、相，具如《毘曇》、《成實論》；若作觀破業，具如《中論》。彼二家者互有長短。今意異彼，但明善惡不濫，於事即足。若廣分別，妨於正道；若直破而已，全不識道品、正助調停，方法未具。今之止觀，明業相不足，觀法有餘。

Third, discriminating analysis of how good and evil marks manifest differently in terms of obstruction: there is what is not obstruction and yet becomes obstruction; what is obstruction and yet becomes not obstruction; what is both obstruction and not obstruction together as obstruction; and what is both obstruction and not obstruction together as not obstruction. As for what is not obstruction and yet becomes obstruction: a person may first manifest a wholesome mark, rejoicing at the time — but afterward gives rise to attachment and arrogance, treating others with contempt; relying on this very proof as grounds for self-conceit, gradually becoming stained by fame and profit, so that faults and calamities arise in turn — the mind retreats, the Dharma is ruined, the precepts are abandoned, one returns to lay life, and there is no evil left uncommitted. Is this not, then, a case where what was at first a wholesome, unobstructing cause later brings about a gravely obstructing evil? As for what is obstruction and yet becomes not obstruction: as when one first manifests an evil mark, and, filled with shame and dread, diligently repents this evil, severing the very continuity of the mind so that transgression never arises again — diligently practicing every wholesome deed, until the great matter is finally accomplished. Is this not, then, a case where what was at first an obstruction later brings about what is no obstruction at all? Both-obstruction and both-not-obstruction may be understood by the same pattern. As for what is not obstruction and yet becomes obstruction: this is a case of a wholesome state on the verge of extinguishing, its mark manifesting precisely as it does so — this extinguishing of the wholesome signals the arising of evil in its place. As for what is obstruction and yet becomes not obstruction: this is a case of an evil state on the verge of extinguishing, its mark manifesting precisely as it does so — this evil signals the arising of good in its place. As for what is both obstruction and not obstruction together as not obstruction: this mark signals that the wholesome does not extinguish and the evil does not arise. As for what is both obstruction and not obstruction together as obstruction: this signals that the evil does not extinguish and the wholesome does not arise. This account is framed in terms of the initial wholesome state, taking the wholesome as not-obstruction and the evil as obstruction, as distinguished above. Were one, however, to speak in terms of the real truth, every one of these wholesome and evil states without exception is itself obstruction — hence the *Sūtra on Purifying Karmic Obstruction* says: "Every evil is obstruction; every good is likewise obstruction." Were one to speak in terms of the provisional, the good and evil of the real truth are, both alike, obstruction. Were one to speak in terms of the middle, the good and evil found even within the provisional are, both alike, obstruction — hence obstruction can never truly be exhausted. Furthermore, the arising of the mind of good and evil's own habituated cause is comparatively easy to recognize; the arising of the mark of good and evil's own retributive fruit is, by contrast, difficult to recognize. Should the mark of wholesome retributive fruit be accompanied by the mind of wholesome habituated cause, whether appearing before or after it, this is, for the most part, a mark of innate goodness; should it arise in isolation, unaccompanied, this is, for the most part, a mark of goodness not innately determined. Should the mark of unwholesome retributive fruit be accompanied by the mind of unwholesome habituated cause, whether before or after, this is, for the most part, innate evil; should it arise unaccompanied by habituation, this is, for the most part, evil contrary to what is innately determined. Furthermore, should the mark of good or evil retributive fruit arise entirely in isolation, though one may judge it provisionally as "not innately determined," its underlying principle remains, in truth, difficult to discern, and is frequently intermixed with demonic influence. Should one wish to distinguish it properly, one must examine it with meticulous care, applying the ten dharmas of emptiness, clarity, good, evil, and the rest, to test it thoroughly. Should it show either excess or deficiency, this is a demonic mark; anything other than this is not innately determined in the ordinary sense. There are, further, three marks by which to test it: namely, whether it persists for a very long time, whether it recurs again and again, and whether it further damages the very mind of meditative concentration — these three marks together indicate a demonic influence; the absence of all three indicates that it is not innately determined. Furthermore, when evil marks manifest, they first appear as anger and fury, then, on a second occasion, appear neutral and even, then, on a third occasion, appear as joy; or a person may admonish and instruct one, or a person may drive one away — one ought to recognize all of this as the very mark of the evil's own imminent extinguishing. Whoever gives rise to true and correct resolve, whose wisdom and understanding are clear and distinct, recognizes every one of these marks without the slightest error, is not confused by any of these obstructions, and, striking directly into the underlying principle with the mind, only increases in clarity all the more. Should one have strength to spare, one may then further distinguish the various gateways of karma; though thoroughly versed and free in this discernment, one applies it, additionally, to the transformation of others. Should one be unable to distinguish the marks of karma down to their finest detail, one need only recognize, in general, that this is obstruction, cling to nothing whatsoever, and strike directly into the underlying principle with contemplation, and karma will then be unable to obstruct one at all. Should one, however, lack any understanding to begin with, and further give rise to perverse intention, seeing such marks and thereby giving rise to attachment and craving, a demon seizes precisely this opportunity to enter and display omens of fortune and misfortune, the two thereafter reinforcing one another, trading goods and food between them, until one dies and falls into the realm of ghosts — is this not precisely what is meant by "demonic meditation," if not this? Should one wish to correct oneself, or correct others, one must genuinely grasp the proper sense of the matter — only through one's own personal practice and verification, together with the oral instruction received directly from a genuine teacher, may one then speak of it openly. One must never carelessly flaunt or peddle such things, falsely claiming heat or cold where none exists — the resulting calamity would be grave indeed! I urge this most earnestly! I urge this most earnestly! Let those who come after take the greatest care! Question: the spirits guarding the place of practice — how, then, could a resentful creditor still manage to cause disturbance there? Answer: this is indeed so. It is like a patrolling officer of the worldly army, whose duty is merely to observe and report, not to actively prevent every wrong — a creditor pressing his claim against an object cannot, by such patrol alone, be entirely barred. Karma arriving to press its claim for repayment may be understood by exactly this same principle. Furthermore, the names, teachings, substance, and marks of the various forms of karma are set forth in full within the *Abhidharma* and the *Satyasiddhi-śāstra*; as for the contemplation that breaks through karma, this is set forth in full within the *Madhyamaka-śāstra*. These two schools each have their own strengths and shortcomings. The sense given here differs from both — it aims merely to clarify good and evil without confusion, and this alone suffices for the present purpose. Were one to distinguish it too broadly, this would only hinder the correct path; were one merely to break through it directly without further distinction, one would fail entirely to recognize the branches of the path, or how direct and auxiliary practice ought to be coordinated together, the method thereby remaining incomplete. The calming-and-contemplation given here, then, treats the clarification of karmic marks only briefly, while its treatment of the actual method of contemplation is given in fuller measure.

Cultivating Calming-and-Contemplation: Ten Dharma-Realms from Karma 四修止觀 · 思議業境

四、修止觀者，即為十意。云何思議業境？若業能招三惡道報，有上、中、下；若業能招三善道報，謂上、中、下；不動業招色、無色報。如是等業招於色心，還迷色心起四顛倒，生死不絕良由於此。今觀業無業，倒惑不生，以至漏盡，是名「聲聞觀業」也。若觀業由無明，無明故業，業故名色乃至老死。若知無明，不起取、有；無明滅故諸行滅，是「緣覺觀業」。若觀業行幻化，幻化即空，空即涅槃，是名「通教觀業」。若觀業如大地，能生種種芽，十法界法皆從業起，是名「別教觀業」。悉是思議境，非今所用也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, cultivating calming-and-contemplation, treated in ten parts. What, then, is the discursible object of karma? Karma capable of inviting retribution in the three evil paths comes in superior, middling, and inferior grades; karma capable of inviting retribution in the three wholesome paths likewise comes in superior, middling, and inferior grades; unshakable karma invites retribution in the realms of form and formlessness. Karma of this kind invites retribution in both form and mind, and this very form and mind, in turn deluded, give rise to the four inversions — the unbroken continuity of birth-and-death arises precisely from this very cause. Now, contemplating karma as having no karma, inverted delusion does not arise, extending all the way to the exhaustion of every contaminant — this is named "the śrāvaka's contemplation of karma." Should one contemplate karma as arising from ignorance — ignorance thus producing karma, karma thus producing name-and-form, extending all the way to old age and death — and should one recognize this ignorance, grasping and existence no longer arise; ignorance extinguished, every formation is thereby extinguished — this is "the pratyekabuddha's contemplation of karma." Should one contemplate karmic activity as illusory transformation, illusory transformation being itself emptiness, emptiness being itself nirvāṇa — this is named "the shared teaching's contemplation of karma." Should one contemplate karma as like the great earth, able to give rise to every manner of sprout, the dharmas of all ten dharma-realms arising from karma — this is named "the distinct teaching's contemplation of karma." All of this belongs to the discursible object, and is not what is employed here and now.

The Inconceivable Object: Deeply Penetrating the Marks of Transgression and Merit 不思議境 · 深達罪福相

不思議境者：如經云「深達罪福相」者，罪即三惡，福即三善。但解三惡業相，不達人天三善業相，則非深達；達惡、達善乃為深達。若達善惡業相但是善惡，不名深達。又善惡俱是惡，離善、離惡皆是善，是為深達。又達人天善惡，是生死邊；達二乘離善離惡，涅槃空邊，但是二邊，不名深達。又二邊皆是惡，亦不名深達。別教菩薩能達二邊之淺，漸漸深達，故名深達。又別教漸深，亦非深達；圓教即於淺業達於深業，方乃得名「深達罪福相，遍照於十方」。如是深達，實不曲辨於三界，亦不徑侹而入空，即此意也。觀一念起即具十界，名「十方」。十方是依報，十界是正報。若無依報，亦無正報；既有正報，即有性、相、本末等百法，亦名「百方」。如是等法即一念業，故名「一業一切業」。《華嚴》云：「『佛子，心性是一，云何能生種種諸業？』答云：『譬如大地一，能生種種芽。』」地若得雨，毒、藥眾芽一時沸發。今法性地得行道雨，善惡業芽一念競起。業名「法界」，諸法之都，故稱「不思議境」。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the inconceivable object: as the sūtra says, "deeply penetrating the marks of transgression and merit" — transgression is precisely the three evil paths, and merit is precisely the three wholesome paths. To understand only the marks of the three evil paths' karma, without penetrating the marks of the three wholesome paths' karma belonging to humans and devas, is not yet deep penetration; only penetrating both evil and good together is truly deep penetration. Yet should one penetrate the marks of good and evil karma merely as good and evil in themselves, this, too, is not named deep penetration. Further, both good and evil alike are, in this deeper sense, evil, while being apart from both good and evil is itself the true good — this is deep penetration. Further, penetrating the good and evil of humans and devas is merely the extreme of birth-and-death; penetrating the two lesser vehicles' being apart from good and evil is merely the extreme of nirvāṇa's own emptiness — but this remains only two extremes, and is not named deep penetration. And since both extremes are, in fact, alike evil, this too is not named deep penetration. The bodhisattva of the distinct teaching is able to penetrate the shallow aspect of both extremes, penetrating gradually ever deeper, and hence is named "deep penetration" in this qualified sense. Yet the distinct teaching's own gradual depth, too, is not yet true deep penetration; only the perfect teaching, penetrating from shallow karma directly to deep karma in a single stroke, may rightly be named "deeply penetrating the marks of transgression and merit, illuminating the ten directions everywhere at once." Deep penetration of this kind, in truth, neither elaborately distinguishes the three realms one by one, nor proceeds directly into emptiness alone — this is precisely its own sense. Contemplating a single thought-moment's arising as already complete with the ten realms is named "the ten directions." The ten directions are the dependent retribution; the ten realms are the direct retribution. Without dependent retribution, there could be no direct retribution either; and, direct retribution being present, there is likewise present the hundred dharmas of nature, mark, root, and branch, and so on — also named "the hundred directions." Dharmas of this kind are precisely a single thought-moment's own karma — hence it is named "one karma is every karma." The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "'Son of the Buddha, mind-nature is one — how, then, is it able to generate every manner of differing karma?' The answer: 'It is like the great earth, which, though one, is able to generate every manner of differing sprout.'" Should the earth receive rain, poisonous and medicinal sprouts alike burst forth all at once; here, likewise, should the ground of the Dharma-nature receive the rain of practicing the path, the sprouts of wholesome and unwholesome karma compete to arise within a single thought-moment. Karma is named "the Dharma-realm," the very capital of every dharma — hence it is called "the inconceivable object."

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thereby deeply penetrated the object of karma, good and evil alike gathered into a single capital, one thereupon gives rise to compassion. The underlying principle of transgression and merit is itself neither violation nor compliance — violating it produces transgression, complying with it produces merit. It is like the conventional truth's own names, forms, and every obstructing substance, likewise neither violation nor compliance in themselves — should one steal and thereby produce transgression, the unwholesome karma of the three lower paths results; should one give and thereby produce merit, the karma of the three wholesome paths results. The bodhisattva, deeply penetrating this very neither-violation-nor-compliance, gives rise to compassion in the face of violation, and to loving-kindness in the face of compliance. The real truth of emptiness itself, beyond every path of words, is likewise neither violation nor compliance — to violate it produces the contaminated karma of the six paths; to comply with it produces the uncontaminated karma of the three vehicles. The bodhisattva, deeply penetrating this emptiness-identity's own neither-violation-nor-compliance, gives rise to compassion in the face of violation, and to loving-kindness in the face of compliance. The truth of the middle way is likewise neither violation nor compliance — to violate it produces the karma of the two extremes, contaminated and uncontaminated; to comply with it produces the karma of the middle way, neither contaminated nor uncontaminated. The Lotus Sūtra's "attained through long cultivation of karma" is precisely this very karma. The bodhisattva, deeply penetrating the true reality of the middle way's own neither-violation-nor-compliance, gives rise to compassion in the face of violation, and to loving-kindness in the face of compliance. For one who has truly penetrated deeply, it is, in every case, simply a single thought-moment's mind that is neither violation nor compliance, with no threefold distinction remaining at all — and this, too, is a single thought-moment's compassion, neither before nor after — hence it is named "the true and correct mind of bodhi." Pacifying the mind through karma's emptiness, the good then complies while the evil is stilled — the evil being stilled is named "calming"; the good complying is named "contemplation." Pacifying the mind through karma's provisional nature, evil is stilled and good complies. Pacifying the mind through karma's middle nature, evil is stilled and good complies — the complying is named "contemplation," the stilling is named "calming." This is named "contemplating karma through skillful pacification of the mind." As for comprehensively breaking through every dharma: the *Abhidharma* says, "Karma, once dissipated, enters the past, bound as though by a rope, and the practitioner receives its retribution in the future"; the *Satyasiddhi* says, "Karma passes from the present into the future, where its retribution is received." Now, contemplating this very karma: should karma belong to the past, the past having already dissipated, how could karma still exist there at all? Should karma belong to the future, the future not yet having come into being, how could karma exist there? Should karma belong to the present, the present itself never abides even a single thought-moment — should a thought-moment have already passed, it belongs to the past; should it not yet have arrived, it belongs to the future; arising and ceasing in the very same instant, which portion of it could possibly be "present" at all? Were one to say that karma exists precisely at the moment of passing, calling this "the present," is it the passing itself that constitutes the karma, or the thing that passes? Is it the passing of the moment of passing, or the passing of the thing that passes? Since the present itself cannot ultimately be found, karma too remains unobtainable. Examined across the three time-periods, searched both horizontally and vertically, every wholesome and unwholesome karma alike proves unobtainable, ultimately pure. To speak, then, of "wholesome" and "unwholesome" karma at all is merely a distinction made through worldly words and provisional names — one must not, upon hearing the name, mistake it for something genuinely real. Why is this so? One seeks, at root, the very truth of the principle itself, not an empty name. An empty name has no nature of its own — however forcefully one might try to distinguish it, this is like pointing at empty space. Karma possesses neither an agent nor a recipient, and the three truths are, all alike, quiescent — hence this is named "comprehensively breaking through every dharma." As for distinguishing blockage from passage: with respect to karma, non-karma, both karma and non-karma, and neither karma nor non-karma, one clarifies and recognizes, within every single proposition, suffering and its origin; within every single thought, one fully comprehends the path and cessation. Verified and genuinely accomplished, this never mistakes a mere insect's trail for a true character — hence it is named "distinguishing blockage from passage." As for cultivating the branches of the path: masters of the *Satyasiddhi* say: "Mental karma arising alone does not yet constitute complete karma — the mind grasping the real dharma, perception grasping the provisional name, and volition then acting accordingly upon this same object, only then does mental karma become fully constituted; this, then, gives the three foundations of mindfulness — and, as for the two karmas of body and speech, being themselves form, these constitute the foundation of mindfulness of the body." Masters of the *Abhidharma* say: "The mental factors and the mind-king arise together, simultaneously" — the mind-king is precisely the foundation of mindfulness of mind; the mental factor of feeling is precisely the foundation of mindfulness of feeling; perception and the remaining mental factors are all the aggregate of formation, precisely the foundation of mindfulness of dharmas; the mind-king and its factors, arising dependent upon form, constitute the foundation of mindfulness of the body. Whether simultaneous or successive, all four foundations of mindfulness are present in either case. Now, contemplating this karma as complete with the five aggregates of all ten dharma-realms is itself being complete with every one of the four foundations of mindfulness. The form belonging to the same category as every karmic deed is the foundation of mindfulness of the body — this body being neither pure nor impure. The four aggregates of the same category constitute the remaining three foundations of mindfulness — these being neither suffering nor pleasure, neither self nor non-self, neither permanent nor impermanent — this is precisely the nirvāṇa of the twin trees, neither flourishing nor withered, extending to the three gates of liberation. This is named "the branches of the path." As for auxiliary remedies: one ought to be mindful of the response-body Buddha's thirty-two marks and the rest, mindful of the reward-body Buddha's immeasurable merit, together breaking through the unwholesome karma of habituated cause. Mindfulness of the dharma-gate Buddha breaks through habituated cause; mindfulness of the thirty-two marks breaks through retributive fruit, and so on. Mindfulness of the dharma-gate Buddha aids in breaking through the unwholesome karma of retributive fruit; through the very power of mindfulness of the Buddha, the obstruction of unwholesome karma is thereby transformed, and one enters the very gate of nirvāṇa. Making this contemplation, one must not falsely claim the stage of the higher sages. One must, further, bear with adversity, allowing every inner and outer obstruction to become unobstructed. Should one attain the resemblance of the path, this is not yet genuine understanding, and one must not give rise to Dharma-attachment. Should Dharma-attachment fail to arise, one proceeds freely and without hindrance, flowing naturally into the very ground of coolness. This, the Mahāyāna's ten contemplations, attains immeasurable, uncontaminated, pure retributive fruit, attaining the supreme reward, attaining unimpeded karma, deeply penetrating transgression and merit, ultimately free of defilement — hence named "purity, which is itself the Dharma-body"; returning to the very root and source, wisdom's illumination perfectly complete — hence named "the supreme, which is itself the reward-body"; manifesting form throughout the nine paths, appearing universally through every gate — hence named "freedom, which is itself the response-body." These three bodies together are precisely the Mahāyāna's own loftiness and breadth, arriving directly at the very place of enlightenment. The rest is as explained above, and so on.

Next, clarifying Māra proper: in order to break through two kinds of good and increase two kinds of evil, he delights in striking through the five faculties with both strong and gentle assaults alike. The Great Treatise says: "Māra is named 'the flower-arrow,' also named 'the five arrows' — each striking one of the five faculties, together destroying the very faculty of mind." Each of the five faculties acts within a single instant; should that instant turn awry, it belongs already to the faculty of mind — and should the faculty of mind itself be destroyed, how could the five faculties possibly still remain intact? The eye seeing a desirable form is named "the flower-arrow," the gentle bandit; seeing a fearful form is named "the poison-arrow," the fierce bandit; seeing a neutral form is the bandit neither fierce nor gentle. The remaining four faculties are exactly the same. Together these make eighteen arrows, also named "the eighteen feelings." For this very reason, one ought never to receive or cling to them — clinging to them produces illness, and illness thus produced is difficult to cure, permanently hindering meditative concentration and, at death, casting one down into the path of Māra. Māra himself, in truth, understands nothing of the two lesser vehicles — he merely feigns their practice, so as to prevent entry into the Mahāyāna. It is like an ignorant, unlearned person, first led to feign such practice, abandoning the Mahāyāna to study the lesser vehicle instead — once great effort has already been invested, regret thereafter brings no benefit at all. Rigidity: the mind hardened and unyielding, unable to move freely in or out, like tile or stone, difficult to alter or transform, out of accord with the wholesome path. Softness: the will weak and yielding, easily corrupted, like soft mud, unfit to be shaped into any vessel. Should any of these show either excess or deficiency, all alike are named "wrong marks." Each single faculty admits of three kinds of feeling; each single feeling admits of twenty wrong dharmas; the three feelings together yield sixty wrong dharmas; across the five faculties, this yields three hundred wrong dharmas in all. Though ninety-five kinds of heterodox teaching exist, each different in its own wrongness, their point of entry, in every case, is necessarily through these same five faculties — examining these three hundred carefully, one finds they invariably correspond to those very heterodoxies. The startling ghost, for the most part, causes the loss of meditative contemplation; the seasonal spirit, for the most part, causes a person to acquire wrong views; Māra proper is capable of both these forms of harm at once.

Methods of Treatment for the Three Kinds of Demon 四明治法

Chinese Source

四、明治法。若治𢟋惕者：須知拘那含佛末法比丘，好惱亂眾僧，僧擯驅之，即生惡誓：「常惱坐禪人。」此是源祖之鬼，報或已謝；而同業生者，亦能惱亂，今呵其宗祖，聞即羞去。呵云：「我識汝名字，汝是𢟋惕惡夜叉，拘那含佛時破戒偷臘吉支，貪食嗅香。我今持戒，不畏於汝。」如是呵已，即應去；若其不去，當密誦戒序及戒，戒神還守，破戒鬼去。治時媚鬼者：須善識十二時、三十六時獸，知時唱名，媚即去也。隱士頭陀人多畜方鏡，挂之座後，媚不能變鏡中色像，覽鏡識之，可以自遣。此則內外兩治也。治魔羅有三：一、初覺呵，如守門人遮惡不進。如佛告比丘：「一切他物不受。」不受之術能治一切自他魔事。二、若已受入，當從頭至足一一諦觀，求魔叵得，又求心叵得。魔從何來？欲惱何等？如惡人入舍，處處照檢，不令得住。三、觀若不去，強心抵捍，以死為期，不共爾住。善巧迴轉如是三治，不須多說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, clarifying methods of treatment. As for treating the startling ghost: one must know that, in the final age of Kanakamuni Buddha's Dharma, a certain monk delighted in disturbing the assembly of monks, and, once expelled by the saṅgha, uttered a malicious vow: "I shall forever torment those who sit in meditation." This is the very ancestral ghost, its own retribution perhaps already dissipated — yet those born of the same karmic lineage are likewise able to cause disturbance. Rebuking this very ancestral lineage now, it will, upon hearing itself named, depart in shame. Rebuke it thus: "I know your very name — you are the evil yakṣa of the startling ghost, who, in the time of Kanakamuni Buddha, broke the precepts and stole incense-fragrant offerings out of greed. I now uphold the precepts, and do not fear you." Having rebuked it in this way, it ought to depart at once; should it fail to depart, one should then silently recite the preamble to the precepts and the precepts themselves — the precept-guardian spirits then return to their post, and the precept-breaking ghost departs. As for treating the seasonal seductive spirit: one must be thoroughly versed in the twelve double-hours and the thirty-six seasonal beasts, and, knowing the proper hour, call out the beast's own name — the seductive spirit will then depart at once. Hermits and ascetics commonly keep a square mirror, hanging it behind their seat — the seductive spirit is unable to alter its own reflected image within the mirror, and, examining the mirror, one may thereby recognize and dispel it oneself. This constitutes both an inner and an outer treatment together. As for treating Māra proper, there are three methods: first, rebuking it the very instant one becomes aware of it, like a gatekeeper barring evil from entering at all — as the Buddha told the monks, "Receive nothing whatsoever belonging to another" — this very technique of non-receiving is able to treat every demonic affair, whether from oneself or from another. Second, should it have already been received and entered in, one should then contemplate carefully from head to foot, point by point, seeking the demon and finding it unobtainable, seeking the mind itself and finding it likewise unobtainable — from where, then, could the demon possibly have come? What, then, could it possibly trouble? It is like an evil person entering a house — one searches and examines every corner, allowing it no place to remain. Third, should it still fail to depart even after this contemplation, one should strengthen the mind and resist it directly, resolved even unto death rather than continue dwelling together with it. Skillfully applying these three methods in turn requires no further elaboration here.

Cultivating Calming-and-Contemplation: The Demon-Realm Is Itself the Buddha-Realm 五止觀 · 魔界即佛界

Chinese Source

五、止觀者，例為十法。思議境者：若魔事起，隨順魔行，作諸惡業，成三途法。若隨魔起善，所謂他屬而行布施；雖生善道，世世相染，或時附著，倚託言語。若捨身命，即受彼報；設欲修道，遮障萬端。經云：「有菩薩，有魔、無魔。」即此意也。是為三善法界。魔又化令自入涅槃，眾生何預汝事？唐受辛苦，不如取證！是名二乘法界。魔又令人紆迴拙度，不速入菩提道。如是淺深歷別，皆是思議境也。若即此魔事，具十界百法在一念中；一切法趣魔，如一夢法具一切事。一魔一切魔，一切魔一魔，非一非一切。亦是一魔一切魔，一佛一切佛，不出佛界即是魔界，不二不別。如此觀者，降魔是道場。上根利智，治魔顯理，以魔為侍，於魔不怖，如薪益火。緣修不能寂照，持世不覺魔謀，謂言「善來」。真修寂照，不待觀而後鑑，即知是魔，非帝釋也。別教不耐非法，故云「非我所宜」；圓教安之實際，故言「如我應受」。不畏非人，於生死有勇，是名「不思議境」也。魔界即佛界，而眾生不知，迷於佛界，橫起魔界，於菩提中而生煩惱，是故起悲。欲令眾生於魔界即佛界、於煩惱即菩提，是故起慈。慈無量佛，悲無量魔，無量慈悲即無緣一大慈悲也。欲滿此願、顯此理，應降魔作道場。八十億眾不能動心，名「止」；達魔界即佛界，名「觀」，但以四悉，止觀安心。隨魔事起，即以四句破之；橫、竪、單、複，破悉無滯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, calming-and-contemplation, treated in ten parts, following the same pattern as before. As for the discursible object: should demonic affairs arise, and one follow along in compliance with demonic conduct, producing every manner of evil karma, this accomplishes the dharma of the three lower paths. Should one instead follow the demon in producing something wholesome — as when, belonging to another's domain, one nonetheless practices generosity — though this gives rise to birth in a wholesome path, one remains tainted by this association life after life, at times still possessed, still relying on demonic speech. Should one give up body and life itself in this state, one receives precisely that demon's own retribution; and should one then wish to cultivate the path, obstruction of every kind arises to hinder it. The sūtra says: "There is the bodhisattva possessed of Māra, and the bodhisattva free of Māra" — this is precisely this same sense. These together constitute the three wholesome dharma-realms. Māra may, further, transform himself so as to induce a person to enter nirvāṇa for himself alone, saying: "What concern of yours are these sentient beings? Why suffer such hardship in vain — better simply to attain realization for yourself!" This is named the dharma-realm of the two lesser vehicles. Māra may, further, cause a person to take a roundabout, clumsy path, failing to enter swiftly upon the path of bodhi. Distinctions of shallow and deep of this kind are all, alike, the discursible object. Should one instead take this very demonic affair as itself complete with the hundred dharmas of the ten realms within a single thought-moment — every dharma tending toward Māra, just as a single dream-dharma is complete with every event within it — then one demon is every demon, every demon is one demon, neither one nor every. And likewise, one demon is every demon, and one buddha is every buddha — not stepping outside the buddha-realm, this is itself already the demon-realm, neither two nor separate. Contemplating in this way, subduing Māra is itself the very place of enlightenment. Those of superior faculty and sharp wisdom, treating Māra so as to reveal the underlying principle, take Māra himself as an attendant, feeling no dread before him at all — like firewood only feeding the flame the higher. Cultivation through mere conditions, unable to achieve quiescent illumination, fails to perceive Māra's own scheme, and so declares, "Welcome, good friend"; but genuine cultivation through quiescent illumination, needing no further contemplation before discerning it, recognizes at once that this is Māra, not truly Śakra at all. The distinct teaching, unable to bear what is not the Dharma, therefore says, "This is not what is fitting for me"; the perfect teaching, settling securely into ultimate reality itself, therefore says, "This, too, is exactly what I ought to receive." Fearing nothing not fully human, and courageous within birth-and-death itself, this is named "the inconceivable object." The demon-realm is itself the buddha-realm, yet sentient beings, not knowing this, remain deluded with respect to the buddha-realm, wrongly giving rise to the demon-realm instead, generating affliction from within bodhi itself — hence compassion arises. Wishing to lead sentient beings to recognize the demon-realm as itself the buddha-realm, and affliction as itself bodhi — hence loving-kindness arises. Loving-kindness toward immeasurable buddhas, compassion toward immeasurable demons — this immeasurable compassion and loving-kindness is itself the single, great, unconditioned compassion. Wishing to fulfill this very vow and manifest this very principle, one ought to subdue Māra and thereby make of it the very place of enlightenment. The eighty koṭis of Māra's host, unable to move the mind, is named "calming"; penetrating the demon-realm as itself the buddha-realm is named "contemplation" — one simply pacifies the mind through calming-and-contemplation by means of the four siddhāntas. Whenever demonic affairs arise, one breaks through them at once by means of the four propositions, whether horizontal, vertical, single, or compound — every form of breaking-through proceeds without the slightest hindrance.

How Each of the Four Teachings Overcomes the Māras 四教破魔

Chinese Source

三藏初伏四魔，坐道場，破煩惱魔，得菩提道；又得法性身，破陰入魔；此兩共破死魔。道樹下得不動三昧，變三玉女，破八十億兵，冠、蓋、劍各墮者，是破天子魔。通教初得無生忍，至六地得菩提道如前；八地道觀雙流，是不動三昧，破天子魔。兩處聲聞，止破三魔。笈多恒為所惱，後得神通，伏而非破云云。別教十住已破界內四魔；登地分得菩提道破煩惱魔；分得法身破陰魔；分得赤色三昧破天子魔。若《瓔珞》云：「等覺，三魔已盡，唯一分死魔在。」三不應前盡，一不應獨餘，此乃別教方便說耳。圓教初住俱破八魔，得菩提道，破煩惱魔云云。乃至妙覺，八魔究竟永盡。雖初住破非初住破；雖後覺破非後覺破；而不離初住、後覺。是為破法遍也。於上一一破魔法中，皆識苦集、無明、蔽度，知字非字。道品者：魔界具一切色。色即是空，色即不淨；色即是假，此名為淨；色即是中，非淨非不淨。餘四陰亦如是。是名「一念處一切念處」，乃至三解脫門。門若未開，必由事障。久遠劫來為魔所使，起於魔檀，為有報故；持於魔戒，要利養故；行於魔忍，為畏他故；習魔精進，求名聞故；得於魔禪，昧於鬼法；樂於魔慧，分別見網。如是六法雖名為善，其實是魔。由此邪蔽，蔽三脫門。今用正度對治六蔽；蔽去度成，如油多明盛。若雜煩惱，當用前四分觀助治；雜業，借念二佛助治。若小乘伏道，遍名為「聞慧」；乃至圓教五品，是聞慧位。此尚未成，豈可濫真起增上慢？若欲入真，當一心安忍，勿更為魔之所動亂。窮微觀照，強心呵抵。若入似位，得法賞賜，勿生高心、愛心。譬如大勳，黜為小縣，或失祿，或失命。若起法愛，是犯罪。但發似解如小縣，失似解如失祿；墮二乘地如失命，大乘家業宗社滅故。若無法愛，從相似入真實，調魔為侍，直至道場。復次，退慧如失勳；退定如失祿；俱退如失命。復次，通用一意為觀者，行人根鈍，先解通意，度曲入別。《中論》品品別意，而俱會無生。通、別互舉，得意相成也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tripiṭaka teaching first subdues the four māras by sitting at the very place of enlightenment, breaking through the affliction-māra and attaining the path of bodhi; further attaining the Dharma-nature body, it breaks through the aggregate-māra; these two together break through the death-māra. Beneath the bodhi-tree, attaining the unshakable samādhi, transforming the three jade maidens sent against him, breaking the eighty koṭis of Māra's soldiers, their crowns, canopies, and swords all falling away — this breaks through the deva-māra. The shared teaching first attains the forbearance of the unborn; upon reaching the sixth ground it attains the path of bodhi as before; at the eighth ground, the contemplation of the path flowing in twin streams is itself the unshakable samādhi, breaking through the deva-māra. In both these accounts of the śrāvaka, only three of the māras are broken through — Gupta, for instance, remained constantly troubled by Māra, later attaining spiritual powers sufficient only to subdue him, not to break through him entirely, and so on. The distinct teaching, by the ten abidings, has already broken through the four māras within the three realms; upon ascending to the grounds, it partially attains the path of bodhi, breaking through the affliction-māra; partially attains the Dharma-body, breaking through the aggregate-māra; partially attains the crimson samādhi, breaking through the deva-māra. The *Yingluo Sūtra* says: "At the stage of equal enlightenment, three of the māras are already exhausted, with only a portion of the death-māra still remaining" — yet it would be inconsistent for three to be already exhausted while only one remains uniquely behind; this, then, is merely the distinct teaching's own explanation through skillful means. The perfect teaching, at the very first of the ten abidings, breaks through all eight māras together, attaining the path of bodhi and breaking through the affliction-māra, and so on, extending all the way to subtle enlightenment, where all eight māras are ultimately and permanently exhausted. Though broken through already at the first abiding, this is not merely broken through at the first abiding alone; though broken through only at the final enlightenment, this is not merely broken through at the final enlightenment alone — yet it remains, at the same time, never apart from either the first abiding or the final enlightenment. This is comprehensively breaking through every dharma. Within each and every one of these methods of breaking through Māra above, one recognizes, throughout, suffering and its origin, ignorance, and the coverings to be overcome through the pāramitās, knowing whether a character is truly a character. As for the branches of the path: the demon-realm is complete with every form. Form is itself emptiness, form is itself impurity; form is itself the provisional, and this is named purity; form is itself the middle, neither pure nor impure — the remaining four aggregates are exactly the same. This is named "a single foundation of mindfulness is every foundation of mindfulness," extending to the three gates of liberation. Should this gate remain unopened, it is necessarily on account of some concrete obstruction. Through long, distant kalpas, employed in Māra's own service, one gives rise to Māra's own generosity, for the sake of some hoped-for reward; upholds Māra's own precepts, for the sake of gain; practices Māra's own forbearance, out of fear of others; cultivates Māra's own diligence, seeking fame and renown; attains Māra's own meditative absorption, obscured within demonic dharmas; delights in Māra's own wisdom, caught within the net of discriminating views. Dharmas of this sixfold kind, though named "wholesome," are, in reality, entirely demonic. Through these very perverse coverings, the three gates of liberation are themselves covered over. Here, one applies the genuine pāramitās as antidote to these six coverings — once the coverings are removed, the pāramitās are accomplished, like oil abundant and the flame correspondingly bright. Should affliction be intermixed with this, one ought to apply the four-division contemplation explained earlier as auxiliary treatment; should karma be intermixed, one borrows mindfulness of the two Buddhas as auxiliary treatment. In the Hīnayāna's own stage of subduing, this is entirely named "the wisdom of hearing"; extending even to the perfect teaching's five grades of disciple, this too remains the stage of the wisdom of hearing. Since even this stage is not yet accomplished, how could one possibly presume upon the genuine and give rise to overweening pride? Should one wish to enter the genuine, one ought to bear with adversity with a single, undivided mind, never again allowing oneself to be stirred and disturbed by Māra. Exhausting subtlety through contemplative illumination, one strengthens the mind and rebukes him directly. Should one enter the stage of resemblance, attaining the reward of the Dharma there granted, one must not give rise to an arrogant or attached mind. It is like a great achievement demoted to a petty district — one may thereby lose one's stipend, or even one's very life. Should Dharma-attachment arise, this is itself already a transgression. Merely giving rise to the resemblance of understanding is like the petty district; losing that resemblance of understanding is like losing one's stipend; falling into the stage of the two lesser vehicles is like losing one's very life, since the entire household enterprise and ancestral line of the Mahāyāna is thereby extinguished. Should one be free of Dharma-attachment, one proceeds from the resemblance stage into the genuine reality itself, subduing Māra so that he becomes one's own attendant, arriving directly at the very place of enlightenment. Furthermore, retreating in wisdom is like losing one's achievement; retreating in concentration is like losing one's stipend; retreating in both together is like losing one's very life. Furthermore, as for applying a single, shared sense throughout the contemplation: since the practitioner's own faculties are dull, one first understands the shared sense, and only then, by a winding path, enters the distinct teaching. The *Madhyamaka-śāstra*, chapter by chapter, treats each its own distinct sense, yet all alike converge upon the unborn — the shared and the distinct mutually raised together, each completing the other's own sense.

Closing the Fascicle: Skin for a Single Verse 結勸 · 剝皮為紙

Chinese Source

問：魔動竟，好法後起，為是法爾，寒過春來耶？答：未必併然。自有過難，好法亦不發。魔是惡緣所感；善是心力所致。《釋論》云：「釋迦往昔在惡世，世無佛，求法精進了不能得。魔變化作婆羅門，詭言：『有佛一偈，汝能皮為紙、骨為筆、血為墨，當以與汝。菩薩樂法，即自剝皮，曝令乾，擬書偈，魔即隱去。』佛知其心，從下方涌出，為說深經，得無生忍。」可以為證云云。
摩訶止觀卷第八下

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: once Māra's disturbance has run its course, does the wholesome dharma arise afterward as a matter of natural necessity, the way spring inevitably follows the passing of winter's cold? Answer: this is not necessarily always so together in every case. There are, in fact, cases where the very difficulty encountered proves so severe that the wholesome dharma fails to arise even afterward. Māra is called forth by evil conditions; the wholesome is brought about by the power of the mind itself. The Great Treatise says: "In a former life, in an evil age when no Buddha was present in the world, Śākyamuni sought the Dharma with diligence, yet was entirely unable to obtain it. Māra, transforming himself into a brahmin, spoke deceitfully: 'I possess a single verse of the Buddha's own teaching — should you be able to make paper of your own skin, a pen of your own bone, and ink of your own blood, I shall give it to you.' The bodhisattva, delighting in the Dharma, at once flayed his own skin, exposing it to dry in the sun, and prepared to write down the verse — whereupon Māra vanished from sight at once. The Buddha, knowing the sincerity of his heart, emerged forth from beneath the earth, expounded a profound sūtra for him, and he thereby attained the forbearance of the unborn." This may serve as confirmation of the point, and so on.
The Great Calming and Contemplation, Fascicle Eight, Lower Part, ends here.

Reading note: The skin-for-a-verse story closes the fascicle on a note that reframes everything that came before it: demonic obstruction is not evidence that the practice has failed, but frequently the very occasion through which the deepest teaching becomes available. Māra's own vanishing the instant the bodhisattva actually begins the sacrifice — rather than at the moment of the bargain, or at its completion — suggests that what demons cannot survive is not resistance but genuine indifference to the cost being asked. The Buddha's arrival "from beneath the earth" immediately afterward answers the fascicle's own opening question about why objects two through five of contemplation were necessary at all: affliction, illness, karma, and demonic affairs are not obstacles standing between the practitioner and the ninth and tenth objects yet to come, but the very terrain through which realization itself becomes possible.
