Fascicle 3 · 摩訶止觀卷第三

Four Teachings, One Reality

This page continues Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Mohe Zhiguan (摩訶止觀, "The Great Calming and Contemplation"). Fascicle 3 is unusually dense: it covers four of the treatise's ten sections at once — explaining the name, the essence and its marks, the scope of the teachings it gathers in, and its relation to partial and complete teachings — and is where Zhiyi lays out, in full, the Four Teachings (藏/通/別/圓) scheme that organizes the rest of the treatise and much of later Tiantai scholasticism. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

II. Explaining the Name, First Approach: Mutual Opposition 釋名 · 相待

Chinese Source

第二、釋止觀名者，大途梗概，已如上說。復以何義立止觀名？略有四：一、相待，二、絕待，三、會異，四、通三德。一、相待者，止觀各三義：息義、停義、對不止止義。息義者，諸惡覺觀、妄念思想寂然休息。《淨名》曰：「何謂攀緣？謂緣三界。何謂息攀緣？謂心無所得。」此就所破得名，是止息義。停義者，緣心諦理，繫念現前，停住不動。《仁王》云：「入理般若名為住。」《大品》云：「以不住法住般若波羅蜜中。」此就能止得名，即是停止義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Topic two, explaining the name of calming-and-contemplation: the broad outline of this has already been given above. By what further sense, then, is the name "calming-and-contemplation" established? There are, in brief, four: first, mutual opposition; second, the absolute, beyond all opposition; third, reconciling different names; fourth, pervading the three virtues. First, mutual opposition: calming and contemplation each have three senses. For calming: the sense of ceasing, the sense of stilling, and the sense of calming as opposed to not-calming. As for the sense of ceasing: every unwholesome discursive thought, every deluded notion and idea, becomes quiescent and comes to rest. The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "What is meant by clinging and pursuing? It means clinging to the three realms. What is meant by ceasing clinging and pursuing? It means the mind attains nothing at all." This takes its name from what is broken through — this is the sense of calming as ceasing. As for the sense of stilling: the mind, taking hold of the truth as its object, fixes its attention directly before it, stilled and unmoving. The Sūtra of Benevolent Kings says: "Entering the prajñā of principle is called abiding." The Larger Sūtra says: "By means of a dharma of non-abiding, one abides within the perfection of wisdom." This takes its name from the subject that does the calming — this is the sense of calming as stilling.

對不止以明止者，語雖通上，意則永殊。何者？上兩止對生死之流動，約涅槃論止息；心行理外，約般若論停止。此約智斷通論相待。今別約諦理論相待。無明即法性，法性即無明，無明亦非止非不止而喚無明為不止；法性亦非止非不止而喚法性為止。此待無明之不止，喚法性而為止。如經：「法性非生非滅而言法性寂滅；法性非垢非淨而言法性清淨。」是為對不止而明止也。觀亦三義：貫穿義、觀達義、對不觀觀義。貫穿義者，智慧利用，穿滅煩惱。《大經》云：「利钁斵地，磐石、砂礫，直至金剛。」《法華》云：「穿鑿高原猶見乾燥土，施功不已，遂漸至泥。」此就所破得名，立貫穿觀也。觀達義者，觀智通達，契會真如。《瑞應》云：「息心達本源，故號為沙門。」《大論》云：「清淨心常一，則能見般若。」此就能觀得名，故立觀達觀也。

As for calming clarified in opposition to not-calming: though the wording resembles what came before, the sense is, in the end, entirely different. Why is this? The two senses of calming above are opposed to the flowing motion of birth-and-death, discussed in terms of nirvāṇa as ceasing; and opposed to the mind's own activity straying outside principle, discussed in terms of prajñā as stilling. This discusses mutual opposition generally, in terms of wisdom and severance together. Here, mutual opposition is discussed specifically, in terms of the truth of principle itself. Ignorance is itself already the Dharma-nature, and the Dharma-nature is itself already ignorance; ignorance is neither calming nor not-calming, and yet is called "not-calming"; the Dharma-nature is likewise neither calming nor not-calming, and yet is called "calming." This is opposing the "not-calming" of ignorance, and calling the Dharma-nature "calming" on that basis. As the sūtra says: "The Dharma-nature is neither arising nor ceasing, and yet the Dharma-nature is said to be quiescent extinction; the Dharma-nature is neither defiled nor pure, and yet the Dharma-nature is said to be pure." This is calming clarified in opposition to not-calming. Contemplation likewise has three senses: the sense of piercing through, the sense of penetrating understanding, and the sense of contemplation as opposed to not-contemplating. As for the sense of piercing through: wisdom's own sharp function pierces through and extinguishes affliction. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "A sharp hoe breaking the ground, through massive rock and gravel, reaching straight down to the vajra layer beneath." The Lotus Sūtra says: "Digging into a high plateau, one sees only dry, parched earth at first; applying the effort without ceasing, one gradually reaches damp soil." This takes its name from what is broken through — this establishes contemplation as piercing through. As for the sense of penetrating understanding: contemplative wisdom penetrates through and meets suchness itself. The Sūtra of Auspicious Manifestations says: "Stilling the mind and penetrating to its very source — this is why one is called a śramaṇa." The Treatise says: "The pure mind, ever one, is then able to see prajñā." This takes its name from the subject that does the contemplating — hence this establishes contemplation as penetrating understanding.

對不觀觀者，語雖通上，意則永殊。上兩觀亦通對生死彌密而論貫穿；迷惑昏盲而論觀達。此通約智斷相待明觀。今別約諦理，無明即法性，法性即無明，無明非觀非不觀而喚無明為不觀；法性亦非觀非不觀而喚法性為觀。如經云：「法性非明非闇而喚法性為明。」「第一義空非智非愚而喚第一義空為智。」是為對不觀而明觀也。是故止觀各從三義得名。

As for contemplation clarified in opposition to not-contemplating: though the wording resembles what came before, the sense is, in the end, entirely different. The two contemplations above are likewise generally opposed to the dense entanglement of birth-and-death, discussed in terms of piercing through; and opposed to confusion and blind delusion, discussed in terms of penetrating understanding. This discusses mutual opposition generally, in terms of wisdom and severance. Here, in terms of the truth of principle specifically: ignorance is itself already the Dharma-nature, and the Dharma-nature is itself already ignorance; ignorance is neither contemplating nor not-contemplating, and yet is called "not-contemplating"; the Dharma-nature is likewise neither contemplating nor not-contemplating, and yet is called "contemplating." As the sūtra says: "The Dharma-nature is neither bright nor dark, and yet the Dharma-nature is said to be bright." "Ultimate emptiness is neither wise nor foolish, and yet ultimate emptiness is said to be wisdom." This is contemplation clarified in opposition to not-contemplating. Hence calming and contemplation each take their name from three senses.

Reading note: Topic two opens by naming four approaches to the treatise's own title, of which "mutual opposition" is the most accessible: calming and contemplation are each given three ordinary senses (ceasing, stilling, and calming-against-not-calming), the third of which already anticipates everything that follows — ignorance and the Dharma-nature turn out to be identical, and "calming" is simply the name given to that identity from the side that has stopped mistaking one for the other. The next three approaches will progressively dissolve the very opposition this first approach still relies on.

Second Approach: The Absolute, Beyond All Opposition 釋名 · 絕待

Chinese Source

二、絕待明止觀者，即破前三相待止觀也。先橫破，次竪破。若止息止從所破得名者，照境為正，除惑為傍，既從所離得名，名從傍立，即墮他性。若停止止從能破得名，照境為正，除惑為傍，既言能照，名從智生，即墮自性。若非妄想息故止、非住理故止，智斷因緣故止，名從合生，即墮共性。若非所破、非能破而言止者，此墮無因性。故龍樹曰：「諸法不自生，亦不從他生，不共，不無因，是故說無生。」無生止觀豈從四句立名？四句立名是因待生，可思、可說；是結惑生，可破、可壞。起滅流動之生，何謂停止？迷惑顛倒之生，何謂觀達耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying calming-and-contemplation as the absolute, beyond all opposition: this breaks through the three kinds of mutually-opposed calming-and-contemplation just given. First a horizontal refutation, then a vertical one. If "calming as ceasing" takes its name from what is broken through: illuminating the object is the primary function, removing delusion the secondary one; since the name is taken from what is departed from, the name is established from the secondary function, and so falls into the nature of "other." If "calming as stilling" takes its name from the subject that does the breaking-through: illuminating the object is the primary function, removing delusion the secondary one; since it is said to illuminate, the name arises from wisdom itself, and so falls into the nature of "self." If calming is named because it is neither the ceasing of deluded thought nor the abiding in principle, but because wisdom and severance come together through causes and conditions, the name arises from their combination, and so falls into the nature of "both together." If calming is spoken of as neither what is broken through nor what does the breaking-through, this falls into the nature of "without cause." Hence Nāgārjuna says: "No dharma arises from itself, nor from another, nor from both together, nor without cause — for this reason it is said that nothing arises at all." How, then, could calming-and-contemplation, itself unborn, ever be named by way of these four alternatives? A name established by way of the four alternatives is a name arisen through mutual dependence — something that can be thought about, something that can be spoken of; it is a name arisen from the very knot of affliction — something that can be broken through, something that can be destroyed. How could the arising and ceasing, flowing motion of such a "birth" ever be called "stilling"? How could the confusion and delusion, the inversion of such a "birth" ever be called "penetrating understanding"?

又竪破者，若從四句生者，即是生生，非止觀也。若能止息見、思，停住真諦，此乃待生生說生不生之止觀耳。若以空心入假，止息塵沙，停住俗理，此乃待生不生說不生生之止觀耳。若止息無明，停心中理，此是待生死涅槃二邊不止而論止觀耳。皆是待對，可思議；生結惑，可破壞。尚未是止，何況不止？猶自非觀，何況不觀？何以故？遣執不盡故，言語道不斷故，業果不絕故。今言「絕待止觀」者，絕橫竪諸待，絕諸思議，絕諸煩惱、諸業、諸果，絕諸教、觀、證等。悉皆不生，故名為止；止亦不可得。觀冥如境，境既寂滅，清淨尚無清淨，何得有觀？止觀尚無，何得待不止觀說於止觀；待於止觀說不止觀；待止、不止說非止非不止？故知止、不止皆不可得；非止非不止亦不可得。待對既絕，即非有為；不可以四句思，故非言說道，非心識境。既無名相，結惑不生，則無生死，則不可破壞。滅絕、絕滅，故名「絕待止」；顛倒想斷，故名「絕待觀」。亦是絕有為止觀，乃至絕生死止觀。

Again, as for the vertical refutation: if calming-and-contemplation arises through any of the four alternatives, this is "birth born of birth," and not truly calming-and-contemplation at all. If one is able to still the views and habituated afflictions, abiding fixedly in the ultimate truth, this is merely a calming-and-contemplation of "no-birth" spoken of in opposition to "birth born of birth." If, with an empty mind, one enters the provisional, stilling the dust-and-sand afflictions, abiding fixedly in the conventional principle, this is merely a calming-and-contemplation of "birth born of no-birth" spoken of in opposition to that same "no-birth." If one stills ignorance itself, fixing the mind on the principle of the middle, this is merely calming-and-contemplation spoken of in opposition to the two extremes of birth-and-death and nirvāṇa, neither yet truly calmed. All of these are still matters of mutual opposition, still something that can be thought about; still a birth arisen from the very knot of affliction, still something that can be broken through and destroyed. Not yet even truly calming — how much less the absolute beyond calming and not-calming? Still not truly contemplation — how much less the absolute beyond contemplating and not-contemplating? Why is this? Because clinging has not been fully dispelled; because the path of language has not been cut off; because karmic result has not been brought to an end. What is now spoken of as "calming-and-contemplation as the absolute" cuts off every opposition, horizontal and vertical alike; cuts off every kind of discursive thought; cuts off every affliction, every karma, every fruit; cuts off every teaching, contemplation, and realization as well. Since none of it arises at all, it is named "calming"; yet even "calming" cannot be found. Contemplation merges with its object exactly as it is; and since the object itself is quiescent extinction, even purity itself has no "purity" left to speak of — how, then, could there be any "contemplation" at all? Since even calming-and-contemplation itself does not exist, how could one, in opposition to "not-calming-and-contemplation," speak of "calming-and-contemplation"; or, in opposition to "calming-and-contemplation," speak of "not-calming-and-contemplation"; or, in opposition to "calming" and "not-calming," speak of "neither calming nor not-calming"? Hence it is known that neither calming nor not-calming can be found; and neither "neither calming nor not-calming" can be found either. Once opposition itself is cut off, this is no longer anything conditioned; it cannot be thought through by way of the four alternatives, and so is neither the path of language nor an object of consciousness. Since it has, then, no name or mark at all, the knot of affliction does not arise; and since it does not arise, there is no birth-and-death; and since there is no birth-and-death, there is nothing that can be broken or destroyed. Extinguished beyond extinction, cut off beyond cutting-off — hence it is named "calming as the absolute"; every inverted notion severed — hence it is named "contemplation as the absolute." This is likewise cutting off every conditioned calming-and-contemplation, on up to cutting off calming-and-contemplation with respect to birth-and-death itself.

絕待止觀則不可說，若有四悉檀因緣故亦可得說。若有世界因緣則會異而說；若有為人因緣則通三德而說；若有對治因緣則相待而說；若有第一義因緣則絕待而說，說為止觀。此之名字，不在內、外、兩中間，亦不常自有。是字不住、亦不不住。是字不在橫四句、竪四句中，故言是字不住；亦不在無橫無竪中，故言亦不不住。是字不可得故，故名「絕待止觀」，亦名「不思議止觀」，亦名「無生止觀」，亦名「一大事止觀」。故如此大事，不對小事。譬如虛空，不因小空名為大也。止觀亦爾，不因愚亂名為止觀，無可待對，獨一法界，故名「絕待止觀」也。世人約種種語，釋絕待義，終不得絕。何以故？凡情馳想，種種推畫分別，悟與不悟、心與不心，凡聖差別；絕則待於不絕，不思議待思議，輪轉相待，絕無所寄。若得意亡言，心行亦斷，隨智妙悟，無復分別；亦不言悟不悟、聖不聖、心不心、思議不思議等。種種妄想，緣理分別皆名為待。真慧開發，絕此諸待，絕即復絕，如前火木，名為「絕待」。故《淨名》云：「諸法不相待，乃至一念不住故。」即此意也。若爾，絕待乃是聖境，初心無分。今以六即望之，初心無所失，聖境無所濫。

Calming-and-contemplation as the absolute cannot, then, be spoken of at all — yet, given the causal conditions of the four siddhāntas, it can indeed be spoken of nonetheless. Given the causal condition of the worldly siddhānta, it is spoken of by way of reconciling different names; given the causal condition of the siddhānta for the individual, it is spoken of by way of pervading the three virtues; given the causal condition of the siddhānta of the antidote, it is spoken of by way of mutual opposition; given the causal condition of the siddhānta of ultimate meaning, it is spoken of by way of the absolute — and, spoken of in any of these ways, it is called "calming-and-contemplation." This very name is not located within, without, or in between; nor does it exist fixedly on its own. This name does not abide, and yet also does not "not abide." This name is not found among the four horizontal alternatives, nor among the four vertical ones — hence it is said not to abide; nor is it found where there is neither horizontal nor vertical — hence it is said also not to "not abide." Because this name can never be found at all, it is called "calming-and-contemplation as the absolute," also called "inconceivable calming-and-contemplation," also called "calming-and-contemplation of no-birth," also called "calming-and-contemplation of the one great matter." Such a great matter as this is not opposed to any lesser matter — just as empty space is not called "great" merely by contrast with some lesser emptiness. Calming-and-contemplation is exactly the same: it is not named "calming-and-contemplation" by contrast with foolish confusion, having nothing at all to be opposed to, the one and only dharma-realm alone — hence it is named "calming-and-contemplation as the absolute." Ordinary people, using every kind of language to explain the sense of "the absolute," never in the end actually attain it. Why is this? The ordinary mind runs after its own notions, constructing and distinguishing in every kind of way — awakened versus unawakened, mind versus no-mind, ordinary being distinguished from sage — and so "the absolute" itself ends up opposed to "the not-absolute," "the inconceivable" opposed to "the conceivable," turning round and round in mutual opposition, with the absolute itself finding nowhere to rest. If one grasps the sense and lets go of the words, the mind's own activity, too, is cut off; following wisdom into wondrous awakening, there is no more discrimination at all — no longer even speaking of "awakened" versus "unawakened," "sage" versus "not-sage," "mind" versus "no-mind," "conceivable" versus "inconceivable." Every kind of deluded notion, every discrimination made in relation to principle, is called "opposition." Once true wisdom opens and unfolds, it cuts off every one of these oppositions; and that very cutting-off is, in turn, itself cut off — like the earlier image of fire and wood — and this is what is named "the absolute, beyond opposition." Hence the Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "No dharma stands in opposition to any other... down to: not even a single thought abides." This is exactly that same sense. If this is so, is "the absolute" then a state belonging only to the sage, with no part for the beginner at all? Viewed now through the six identities, the beginner loses nothing by this, and the sage's own state is in no way diminished either.

Reading note: This is a masterclass in the treatise's tetralemma method, applied here to its own title. The four ways calming could be named (from what it removes, from the wisdom that removes it, from their combination, from neither) map directly onto Nāgārjuna's four rejected modes of arising — self, other, both, and neither — and every single one, including the sophisticated "vertical" refutations that follow (opposing "no-birth" to "birth," then a subtler no-birth to that no-birth, then the extremes themselves to each other), is shown to still be a name arisen through opposition, and so still short of the absolute. The closing question is worth sitting with: if "the absolute" belongs only to the sage, does the beginning practitioner have any part in it at all? The answer points forward to the six identities already explained in fascicle 1 — the beginner "loses nothing," because identity in principle is already true of them, even unrecognized.

Third Approach: Reconciling Different Names 釋名 · 會異

Chinese Source

三、會異者，如此絕待止觀，亦名「不可思議」，亦名為「大」。《大經》云：「大名不可思議也。」諸餘經論或名「遠離」，或名「不住」、「不著」、「無為」、「寂滅」、「不分別」、「禪定」、「棄」、「除」、「捨」等，如是一切皆是「止」之異名。止既絕大不可思議，遠離等皆絕大不可思議。餘處或名「知見」、「明識」、「眼覺」、「智慧」、「照了」、「鑒達」等，如是一切皆是「觀」之異名。觀既絕大不可思議，知見等皆絕大不可思議。所以者何？般若是一法，佛說種種名；解脫亦爾，多諸名字。亦如虛空，無所有、不動、無礙。當知三德秖是一法，隨眾生類，為之立異字。若聞絕待，慎莫驚畏；若聞會異，慎莫疑惑而自毀傷也。又止觀自相會者，止亦名觀，亦名不止；觀亦名止，亦名不觀，即前釋名意同也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, reconciling different names: this very calming-and-contemplation as the absolute is also named "inconceivable," also named "the great." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "The name 'great' means inconceivable." Every other sūtra and treatise variously names it "departing far," "non-abiding," "non-attachment," "the unconditioned," "quiescent extinction," "non-discrimination," "dhyāna-concentration," "relinquishing," "removing," "letting go," and so forth — every one of these is simply another name for "calming." Since calming itself is already the absolute, great, and inconceivable, "departing far" and every other such name is likewise, absolutely, great and inconceivable. Elsewhere it is variously named "knowledge and vision," "clear discernment," "the eye's own awareness," "wisdom," "illuminating understanding," "discerning penetration," and so forth — every one of these is simply another name for "contemplation." Since contemplation itself is already the absolute, great, and inconceivable, "knowledge and vision" and every other such name is likewise, absolutely, great and inconceivable. Why is this? Prajñā is a single dharma, and the Buddha teaches it under manifold names; liberation is exactly the same, with many names of its own. It is likewise like empty space, possessing nothing, unmoving, unobstructed. One should know that the three virtues are simply a single dharma, given different names according to the different kinds of sentient beings addressed. On hearing of the absolute, take care not to be alarmed or afraid; on hearing of these reconciled names, take care not to fall into doubt and so injure oneself. Again, calming and contemplation reconcile with each other directly: calming is also named contemplation, and also named not-calming; contemplation is also named calming, and also named not-contemplating — exactly the same sense as explaining the name given earlier.

Reading note: Having pushed the name "calming-and-contemplation" all the way to the absolute, the treatise now defuses a worry the reader might reasonably have: if so many other scriptures use entirely different vocabulary — "departing far," "non-abiding," "quiescent extinction" for calming; "knowledge and vision," "illuminating understanding" for contemplation — does this mean they are describing something different? The answer is the same move fascicle 1 made repeatedly with apparently conflicting scripture: one reality, addressed under manifold names suited to different audiences, "as empty space" is one thing however many words are used to point at it. The closing line even folds calming and contemplation back into each other — "calming is also named contemplation... contemplation is also named calming" — collapsing the very distinction the whole section began with.

Fourth Approach: Pervading the Three Virtues 釋名 · 通三德

Chinese Source

四、通三德者，若眾經異名皆是止觀者，名則無量，義亦無量；何故但以三義釋止觀耶？為對三德作此釋耳。諸法無量，何故獨對三德？《大論》云：「菩薩從初發心，常觀涅槃行道。」《大經》云：「佛及眾生，皆悉安置祕密藏中。」祕密即是涅槃，涅槃即是三德，三德即是止觀。自、他，初、後，皆得修入，故用對之耳。若用兩字共通三德者，止即是斷，斷通解脫；觀即是智，智通般若；止觀等者，名為捨相，捨相即是通於法身。又止即奢摩他；觀即毘婆舍那；他、那等故。即憂畢叉。通三德如前。問：止觀是二法，豈得通不思議三德耶？答：還以不思議止觀，故得通耳。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, pervading the three virtues: if the manifold different names found across the sūtras are all names for calming-and-contemplation, then the names are measureless, and their meanings measureless too — why, then, explain calming-and-contemplation by way of only three senses? This explanation is given specifically in correspondence with the three virtues. Since dharmas are measureless, why match them specifically to the three virtues alone? The Treatise says: "From the very first arising of the resolve, the bodhisattva constantly contemplates nirvāṇa as they walk the path." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Both buddhas and sentient beings are alike placed within the hidden treasury." The hidden treasury is itself nirvāṇa; nirvāṇa is itself the three virtues; the three virtues are themselves calming-and-contemplation. Self and other, beginning and end, can all alike cultivate and enter it — hence it is matched to this scheme specifically. If the two words together are matched to the three virtues jointly: calming is itself severance, and severance corresponds to liberation; contemplation is itself wisdom, and wisdom corresponds to prajñā; calming and contemplation held in equal balance is named "relinquishing marks," and relinquishing marks corresponds to the Dharma-body. Again, calming is śamatha; contemplation is vipaśyanā; and the two held equal is upekṣā. Their correspondence to the three virtues is exactly as above. Question: Calming and contemplation are two distinct dharmas — how could they possibly correspond to the inconceivable three virtues? Answer: It is precisely by means of inconceivable calming-and-contemplation that this correspondence is possible at all.

又《大品》明十八空釋般若；百八三昧釋禪。雖前後兩釋，豈可禪無般若，般若無禪？特是不二而二，二則不二。不二即法身；二即定、慧。如此三法未曾相離。是故《大經》云：「佛性有五種名，或名首楞嚴，或名般若。」今非止非觀，或名為止，或名為觀，即是不思議止觀，通於不思議三德。復次，止、觀各通三德者，止中有觀，觀中有止。如止息止是止善，屬定門攝，即通解脫；停止止是行善，屬觀門攝，即通般若；非止止屬理攝，即通法身。其義可見也。貫穿觀是止善，定門攝，即通解脫；觀達觀是行善，觀門攝，即通般若；非觀觀，理攝，即通法身，意亦可見。

Again, the Larger Sūtra explains prajñā by way of the eighteen kinds of emptiness, and explains dhyāna by way of the hundred and eight samādhis. Though these are two separate explanations given one after the other, how could dhyāna truly lack prajñā, or prajñā truly lack dhyāna? This is precisely "not two, and yet two; two, and yet not two." The "not two" is itself the Dharma-body; the "two" is concentration and wisdom. These three dharmas have never once been separate from each other. Hence the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "The buddha-nature has five names — it is named the Śūraṅgama, and it is named prajñā." Here, what is neither calming nor contemplation is nonetheless named calming, and nonetheless named contemplation — this is precisely inconceivable calming-and-contemplation, pervading the inconceivable three virtues. Furthermore, calming and contemplation each pervade the three virtues individually: within calming there is contemplation, and within contemplation there is calming. Thus: calming as ceasing is a wholesome practice of calming, gathered under the gate of concentration, and so corresponds to liberation; calming as stilling is a wholesome practice of conduct, gathered under the gate of contemplation, and so corresponds to prajñā; calming as neither-calming-nor-not is gathered under principle itself, and so corresponds to the Dharma-body — the sense of this is plain to see. Contemplation as piercing through is a wholesome practice of calming, gathered under the gate of concentration, and so corresponds to liberation; contemplation as penetrating understanding is a wholesome practice of conduct, gathered under the gate of contemplation, and so corresponds to prajñā; contemplation as neither-contemplating-nor-not is gathered under principle, and so corresponds to the Dharma-body — the sense of this, too, is plain to see.

復次，止觀共通三德者，止息止、貫穿觀皆從所離得名，即通解脫；停止止、觀達觀皆從能緣之智得名，即通般若；非止止、非觀觀皆名法性，即通法身。復次，三德通於止觀者，還以三德共通兩字；又應三德各通兩字。三德共通者，解脫通止，般若通觀，法身通非止非觀。三德各通止觀者，夫解脫者具足解脫具有三種：方便淨解脫通止息止、圓淨解脫通停止止、性淨解脫通非止止。夫般若者，具足般若。具有三種：道慧般若，通貫穿觀；道種慧般若，通觀達觀；一切種慧般若，通非觀觀。具足法身，亦有三種：色身，通一止一觀；法門，身通一止一觀；實相身，通一止一觀。其義可見也。

Furthermore, calming and contemplation jointly correspond to the three virtues: calming as ceasing and contemplation as piercing through both take their name from what is departed from, and so correspond to liberation; calming as stilling and contemplation as penetrating understanding both take their name from the wisdom that takes hold of the object, and so correspond to prajñā; calming as neither-nor and contemplation as neither-nor are both named the Dharma-nature, and so correspond to the Dharma-body. Furthermore, the three virtues correspond to calming and contemplation from the other direction as well: the three virtues jointly correspond to the two words together; and the three virtues also each correspond to the two words individually. As for the three virtues jointly corresponding: liberation corresponds to calming, prajñā corresponds to contemplation, and the Dharma-body corresponds to neither-calming-nor-contemplating. As for the three virtues each corresponding to calming and contemplation individually: liberation, complete in itself, has three kinds — liberation through the purity of skillful means corresponds to calming as ceasing; liberation through perfect purity corresponds to calming as stilling; liberation through the purity of one's own nature corresponds to calming as neither-nor. Prajñā, complete in itself, likewise has three kinds — the wisdom of the path corresponds to contemplation as piercing through; the wisdom of the seeds of the path corresponds to contemplation as penetrating understanding; the wisdom that knows all things in every mode corresponds to contemplation as neither-nor. The Dharma-body, complete in itself, likewise has three kinds — the physical body corresponds to one calming and one contemplation; the body of dharma-gates corresponds to one calming and one contemplation; the body of the true mark of reality corresponds to one calming and one contemplation. The sense of this is plain to see.

若信三德絕大不思議，通義既明，須信止觀絕大不思議。若信涅槃三法具足，名祕密藏；亦信三止具足，名大寂定，名祕密藏；亦信三觀具足，名大智慧，名祕密藏；亦信非止非觀三法具足，名祕密藏。若信三德不縱不橫，不並不別，如三點、三目者，亦信三止三觀不縱不橫，不並不別也。而諸經赴緣，偏舉一法，以示義端。如《首楞嚴》偏舉止邊；止具一切法，不減少，亦名祕密藏。《智度》、《法華》偏舉觀邊；觀具一切法，不減少。《涅槃》舉三法具足，法亦不多，亦名祕密藏。止觀亦爾，若開若合，開亦不多，合亦不少，一一皆是法界，攝一切法，悉名祕密藏。偏舉尚爾，況圓舉耶？止觀通三德既爾，通諸異名，遠離、知見等，亦如是。又通諸三名，所謂三菩提、三佛性、三寶等，一切三法，亦如是。

If one believes that the three virtues are the absolute, great, and inconceivable, then, once their correspondence with calming-and-contemplation is made clear, one must likewise believe that calming-and-contemplation is the absolute, great, and inconceivable. If one believes that nirvāṇa's three dharmas, complete together, are named the hidden treasury, one must likewise believe that the three kinds of calming, complete together, are named great quiescent concentration, and named the hidden treasury; and that the three kinds of contemplation, complete together, are named great wisdom, and named the hidden treasury; and that the three dharmas of neither-calming-nor-contemplating, complete together, are named the hidden treasury. If one believes that the three virtues are neither vertically arranged nor horizontally arranged, neither juxtaposed nor separate, like three dots or like three eyes, one must likewise believe that the three kinds of calming and the three kinds of contemplation are neither vertically nor horizontally arranged, neither juxtaposed nor separate. Yet the various sūtras, addressing different occasions, each raise up only one side of it as the leading term. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra, for instance, raises up calming's own side specifically; yet calming is already complete with every dharma, lacking nothing at all, and is likewise named the hidden treasury. The Treatise and the Lotus Sūtra raise up contemplation's own side specifically; yet contemplation is likewise already complete with every dharma, lacking nothing at all. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra raises up the three dharmas together, complete as one; yet even this is no more numerous a set of dharmas, and is likewise named the hidden treasury. Calming-and-contemplation is exactly the same: whether opened out into many or gathered into one, the opening-out is no more numerous, and the gathering-in no less complete — each and every one of them is already the dharma-realm itself, gathering in every dharma, all alike named the hidden treasury. If this holds even when only one side is raised up, how much more when the whole is raised up together? Since calming-and-contemplation pervades the three virtues in just this way, it likewise pervades every other name as well — "departing far," "knowledge and vision," and the rest, exactly the same. And it likewise pervades every other threefold name — the three kinds of bodhi, the three buddha-natures, the three jewels, and every other threefold dharma whatsoever, exactly the same.

Reading note: This fourth approach recasts the treatise's threefold structure (a "ceasing/piercing" pair, a "stilling/penetrating" pair, a "neither-nor" pair) as corresponding, term by term, to the three virtues from fascicle 2's closing section: liberation, prajñā, and the Dharma-body. What makes the correspondence more than decorative is the double move that follows — not only do the three virtues jointly correspond to calming-and-contemplation, but calming alone and contemplation alone each already contain all three virtues within themselves, and each virtue in turn already contains its own threefold structure. This is the same fractal logic already seen throughout this fascicle and the last, now demonstrated at the level of the treatise's own title.

Neither Vertical nor Horizontal: The Letter I 不縱不橫 · 伊字三點

Chinese Source

問：云何字義縱橫？云何字義不縱、不橫？答：諸小乘師說：「般若種智已圓，果縛尚在；解脫未具，身猶雜食；又帶無常，一優二劣，譬之橫川走火。」又云：「先有相好之身；次得種智般若；後滅身智，方具解脫。」既有上下、前後之義，譬之縱三點水。若入滅定，有身而無智；羅漢在無色，有智而無身；若入無餘，但有孤調解脫。此義各各不相關，並之則橫，累之則縱，分之則異。諸大乘師說：「法身是正體，有佛無佛本自有之，非適今也。了因般若、無累解脫，此二當有，隔生跨世，彌亘淨穢。此字義縱也。」又言：「三德無前後，一體具足；以體從義，而有三異。蓋乃體橫而義縱耳。」又言：「體、義俱不殊，而有隱、顯之異。俱不異，未免橫；隱顯異，未免縱。」眾釋如此，寧與經會？

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: In what sense are these three names arranged vertically or horizontally? In what sense are they neither vertical nor horizontal? Answer: Various Hīnayāna masters say: "Prajñā, as the wisdom of every mode, is already perfect, and yet the bondage of retribution still remains; liberation is not yet complete, and the body still depends on ordinary food; moreover it still carries impermanence with it — one factor superior, two inferior, like a fire running sideways across a river." They also say: "First there is the body with its auspicious marks; next the wisdom of every mode, prajñā, is attained; only afterward, once body and wisdom are both extinguished, is liberation finally complete." Since this involves a sense of higher and lower, earlier and later, it is likened to the vertical arrangement of the three dots of the character for water. On entering the concentration of cessation, there is body but no wisdom; the arhat, dwelling in the formless realm, has wisdom but no body; on entering nirvāṇa without remainder, there is only solitary, self-tamed liberation, and nothing more. On this view, each of these three has nothing to do with the other two: set side by side, they are horizontal; piled one atop another, they are vertical; divided apart, they are different. Various Mahāyāna masters say: "The Dharma-body is the true substance itself, existing of itself whether or not a buddha appears in the world — it is not something that only comes to be now. The illuminating cause, prajñā, and freedom from all encumbrance, liberation — these two are yet to come, separated across lifetimes and spanning different ages, extending through both the pure and the defiled. In this sense, the arrangement of these names is vertical." They also say: "The three virtues have no before or after — one substance, complete in itself; yet, following that substance out into its distinct meanings, three differences arise. This is substance horizontal, but meaning vertical." They also say: "Substance and meaning are, in fact, no different from each other — yet there remains a difference between the hidden and the manifest. If they are truly no different, this hardly escapes being horizontal; if hidden and manifest differ, this hardly escapes being vertical." Given how various these explanations are, how could they possibly accord with the sūtras?

今明三德皆不可思議，那忽縱？皆不可思議，那忽橫？皆不可思議，那忽一？皆不可思議，那忽異？此約理藏釋。身常、智圓、斷具，一切皆是佛法，無有優劣，故不縱。三德相冥，同是一法界；出法界外，何處更別有法？故不橫。能種種建立，故不一；同歸第一義，故不異。此約行因釋也。即一而三故不橫；即三而一故不縱。不三而三故不一；不一而一故不異。此約字用釋也。真「伊」字，義為若此。問：三德、四德，其意云何？答：通論三德，一一皆常樂我淨。《大經》云：「諸佛所師，所謂法也。以法常故，諸佛亦常。」法即法身，佛即般若、解脫，故作通解也。《大經》云：「因滅是色，獲得常色；受想行識，亦復如是。」則法身皆常、樂、我、淨；二德亦然。若依一種，轉「色」成法身，法身「常」、「樂」；轉「識」想成般若，般若即「淨」；轉「受」行成解脫，解脫則「我」。又依念處，轉「識」成「常」，轉「受」成「樂」，轉「想」、「行」成「我」，轉「色」成「淨」。是則通、別各有二解：依圓是頓義，依別是漸義云云。

Here it is explained that the three virtues are all alike inconceivable — how, then, could they suddenly be vertical? All alike inconceivable — how, then, could they suddenly be horizontal? All alike inconceivable — how, then, could they suddenly be one? All alike inconceivable — how, then, could they suddenly be different? This is explained in terms of the hidden treasury of principle itself: the body eternal, wisdom perfect, severance complete — every one of these is already the Buddhadharma, with no superior or inferior among them, hence they are not vertical. The three virtues merge into one another, all alike the one and only dharma-realm; outside the dharma-realm, where else could there possibly be any separate dharma? Hence they are not horizontal. They are able to be established in manifold different ways, hence they are not one; they alike return to the same ultimate meaning, hence they are not different. This is explained in terms of the cause of practice. Being one and yet three, they are not horizontal; being three and yet one, they are not vertical. Not being three and yet three, they are not one; not being one and yet one, they are not different. This is explained in terms of how the term itself functions. The true character i has exactly this same sense. Question: What is the relation between the three virtues and the four virtues of nirvāṇa? Answer: Discussed generally, the three virtues each and every one possess permanence, bliss, self, and purity alike. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "What every buddha takes as their own teacher is the Dharma. Because the Dharma is eternal, the buddhas too are eternal." The Dharma here is the Dharma-body; the Buddha is prajñā and liberation — hence this is explained as applying to all three together. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "By means of the extinguishing of this form, an eternal form is obtained; and sensation, perception, formations, and consciousness are exactly the same." Thus the Dharma-body possesses permanence, bliss, self, and purity all together; and the other two virtues are exactly the same. According to one account: transforming form accomplishes the Dharma-body, and the Dharma-body is "permanence" and "bliss"; transforming consciousness and perception accomplishes prajñā, and prajñā is "purity"; transforming sensation and formations accomplishes liberation, and liberation is "self." According to another account, based on the foundations of mindfulness: transforming consciousness accomplishes "permanence"; transforming sensation accomplishes "bliss"; transforming perception and formations accomplishes "self"; transforming form accomplishes "purity." Thus the shared and the distinct teachings each have their own two explanations: following the perfect teaching gives the sudden sense of it; following the distinct teaching gives the gradual sense of it, and so on.

問：三障及三道皆障三德。三障開通至極，三道、四倒亦應開通至極？答：例。何者？業有三種，謂漏業、無漏業、非漏非無漏業。感於三報，謂分段、方便、實報。報由三種煩惱，謂取相、塵沙、無明也。又約三種報，一一開三道。約三種煩惱，一一開四倒。

Question: Both the three obstructions and the three paths obstruct the three virtues. The three obstructions have been opened out and traced through to their utmost extent — should not the three paths and the four inversions likewise be opened out and traced through to their utmost extent? Answer: Yes, by the same pattern. Why is this? Karma is of three kinds: contaminated karma, uncontaminated karma, and karma neither contaminated nor uncontaminated. This gives rise to three kinds of retribution: birth-and-death of limited span, of the expedient means, and of true reward. This retribution arises from three kinds of affliction: grasping at marks, the dust-and-sand afflictions, and ignorance. Moreover, in terms of the three kinds of retribution, each in turn opens out into the three paths; and in terms of the three kinds of affliction, each in turn opens out into the four inversions.

Reading note: This is one of the most famous images in all of Tiantai literature: the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own simile of the Siddhaṃ letter i (伊), written not as three dots stacked vertically (like the classical graph for "water," implying a strict before-and-after sequence) nor spread horizontally side by side (implying three unrelated, independent things), but as three points arranged so that none precedes the others and none stands apart from the others — a single character that is visibly triangular rather than linear. Zhiyi uses it to reject both rival positions just surveyed: the Hīnayāna masters' sequential "body first, then wisdom, then liberation, only complete at the very end," and the Mahāyāna masters' various attempts to rescue simultaneity while still smuggling in some residual hierarchy ("substance horizontal but meaning vertical"). The three virtues — like the three kinds of calming and the three kinds of contemplation examined throughout this whole section — are neither ranked nor separable, and the letter itself, rather than any argument, is offered as proof that such an arrangement is not just abstractly conceivable but can be seen. This closes topic two, explaining the name, in its entirety.

Third, explaining the essence and marks of calming-and-contemplation: having already grasped the great intent, thoroughly understood as given above, and the name, ranged over as widely as just shown, one must now come to recognize the essence itself, profound and deep. The Teaching-Mark: What Falls Outside This Treatise's Concern 教相 · 簡非

Chinese Source

教相顯者，夫止觀名教，通於凡聖；不可尋通名，求於別體，故用相簡之。若凡夫止善所治是止相；行善所生是觀相。又四禪、四無量心是止相；六行是觀相。此等皆未免生死，即有漏為相。故《大論》云：「除摩黎山，餘無出栴檀；除三乘智慧，餘無真智慧。」故非今所論也。若二乘以九想、十想、八背捨、九次第定，多是事禪，一往止相；有作四諦慧是觀相。此之止觀雖出生死，而是拙度，滅色入空。此空亦得名「止」，亦得名「非止非不止」，而不得名「觀」。何以故？灰身滅智，故不名觀。但是析法，無漏為相，非今所論也。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for showing it by the mark of the teaching: "calming-and-contemplation," as a name for a teaching, is shared in common between the ordinary being and the sage alike; one cannot simply search for the shared name and expect to find, through it, the specific essence — hence "mark" is used to distinguish them. For the ordinary person, the wholesome states that calming works to remedy are the mark of calming; and the wholesome states that contemplative practice gives rise to are the mark of contemplation. Again, the four dhyānas and the four immeasurable minds are the mark of calming; the six practices of renunciation are the mark of contemplation. All of these still fail to escape birth-and-death — their mark is simply the contaminated. Hence the Treatise says: "Apart from Mount Malaya, no sandalwood grows anywhere else; apart from the wisdom of the three vehicles, there is no true wisdom anywhere else." Hence this level is not what is discussed here. As for the two lesser vehicles: the nine contemplations, the ten contemplations, the eight liberations, and the nine sequential concentrations are mostly phenomenal dhyāna, and, taken broadly, are the mark of calming; the wisdom of the constructed Four Noble Truths is the mark of contemplation. Though this calming-and-contemplation does escape birth-and-death, it is the clumsy method, extinguishing form to enter emptiness. This emptiness, too, may be named "calming," and may be named "neither calming nor not-calming" — but it cannot be named "contemplation." Why is this? Because the body is reduced to ash and wisdom itself extinguished, it cannot be named "contemplation." This is merely the analytical method, its mark uncontaminated — and this, too, is not what is discussed here.

Reading note: Before defining what this treatise's own calming-and-contemplation actually is, Zhiyi first rules out three lower registers that share the same name but not the same substance: ordinary wholesome states (still fully within birth-and-death), and the Hīnayāna's own analytical practice (which does escape birth-and-death, but by extinguishing form and consciousness rather than seeing through them — hence it earns the name "calming" but never "contemplation," since there is no illuminating wisdom left once the practitioner has, in effect, extinguished themselves). What follows is specifically the Mahāyāna's "skillful method" (巧度), as opposed to this "clumsy method" (拙度) just excluded.

The Three Kinds of Calming, Across the Teachings 教相 · 三止

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The skillful method's calming has three kinds: first, the calming that embodies the real; second, the calming of skillful means that follows conditions; third, the calming that stills discrimination between the two extremes. First, the calming that embodies the real: every dharma arises from conditions, and causes and conditions are themselves empty, without any master. "Stilling the mind and reaching its very source — this is why one is called a śramaṇa." Knowing that causes and conditions are only a provisional combination, their nature illusory and empty, this is why it is called "embodying." The mind's own clinging and pursuing, and its deluded thoughts, once found to be empty, are thereby stilled — this emptiness is itself the real — hence it is called "the calming that embodies the real." Second, the calming of skillful means that follows conditions: if the three vehicles alike, by means of the wordless path, sever affliction and enter the real, that real is then no different between them — the only difference spoken of is whether affliction together with its habituated traces is fully exhausted or not yet exhausted. If the two lesser vehicles embody the real, they have no need of this calming of skillful means; the bodhisattva, entering the provisional, must precisely put it into practice. Knowing that emptiness is not simply empty is why it is called "skillful means"; discriminating between medicine and illness is why it is called "following conditions"; the mind settling upon the conventional truth is why it is named "calming." A sūtra says: "In motion or in stillness, the mind remains ever one" — this too may serve as confirmation of this same sense. Third, the calming that stills discrimination between the two extremes: the flowing motion of birth-and-death and the fixed assurance of nirvāṇa are both alike a partial practice, a partial function, neither meeting the middle way. Here, knowing that the conventional is not simply conventional, the extreme of the conventional is stilled; and also not accepting that it is not-conventional, the extreme of emptiness is stilled — this is named "the calming that stills the two extremes." Though these three names for calming are not themselves found stated in any sūtra or treatise, they are named according to their sense by analogy with the three contemplations to come. The Treatise says: "The bodhisattva, relying on the sūtras' own teaching, coins names for things — this is called the giving of Dharma." There is no fault in coining such names. If one is able to trace them back and find their sense already present in the sūtras, then this naming already tacitly accords with that same sense.

詳此三止，與前釋名，名髣髴同，其相則異。同者，止息止似體真；停止止似方便隨緣；非止止似息二邊。其相則別，所謂三諦相也。前三成次三，後一具前三。何以故？如體真止時，達因緣假名，空無主，流動惡息，是名止息義；停心在理，正是達於因緣，是停止義；此理即真，真即本源，本源不當止與不止，是非止止。此三義共成體真止相。若方便止時，照假自在，散亂無知息，是止息義。停心假理，如《淨名》入三昧，觀比丘根性，分別藥病，是停止義。假理不動，是非止止。如是三義，共成方便隨緣止相也。息二邊時，生死、涅槃二相俱息，是止息義。入理般若名為住，緣心中道，是停止義。此實相理非止、不止，是不止止義。如此三義，共成息二邊止相。故與前永異也，亦非今所用也。

Examining these three closely against the three senses given earlier in explaining the name, the names resemble each other but their marks differ. What is similar: calming as ceasing resembles embodying the real; calming as stilling resembles skillful means following conditions; calming as neither-nor resembles stilling the two extremes. What differs is their own mark — namely, the mark of the three truths themselves. The earlier three general senses together accomplish the next three teaching-specific kinds; and the last of these three contains the first two within it. Why is this? When embodying the real in calming, one penetrates that causes and conditions are but a provisional name, empty and without any master, and the flowing motion of unwholesomeness is thereby stilled — this is the sense of ceasing; the mind settles upon principle, which is precisely penetrating causes and conditions — this is the sense of stilling; this very principle is itself the real, and the real is itself the very source, and the source itself is properly neither calming nor not-calming — this is calming as neither-nor. These three senses together accomplish the mark of calming that embodies the real. In the calming of skillful means, illuminating the provisional freely, scattered confusion and ignorance are thereby stilled — this is the sense of ceasing. The mind settles upon the principle of the provisional, as when Vimalakīrti entered samādhi and observed the bhikṣus' own capacities, discriminating between medicine and illness — this is the sense of stilling. The principle of the provisional itself unmoved is calming as neither-nor. These three senses together accomplish the mark of the calming of skillful means that follows conditions. In stilling the two extremes, the marks of both birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are alike stilled — this is the sense of ceasing. Entering the prajñā of principle is named abiding, the mind taking hold of the middle as its object — this is the sense of stilling. This very principle of the true mark of reality, neither calming nor not-calming, is the sense of calming-not-calming. These three senses together accomplish the mark of the calming that stills the two extremes. Hence these are, in the end, entirely different from what came before, and are also not what is used here in this treatise.

Reading note: These three kinds of calming — embodying the real, skillful means following conditions, and stilling the two extremes — are, though not yet named as such, the Tripiṭaka/shared teaching's practice, the distinct teaching's own bodhisattva practice entering the provisional, and a practice addressed to the middle way respectively; watch for these same three to be formally matched to the Four Teachings once the three contemplations below are introduced. Zhiyi's own admission that these names appear nowhere in scripture verbatim, only "coined" by analogy — followed immediately by a defense of coining names at all — is a small, candid moment about how much of this treatise's own technical vocabulary is Zhiyi's original systematization rather than direct quotation.

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the mark of contemplation. Contemplation has three kinds: entering emptiness from the provisional, named "the contemplation of the two truths"; entering the provisional from emptiness, named "the contemplation of equality"; and these two contemplations serving as the expedient path by which one enters the middle way, illuminating both truths at once, thought after thought quiescent and extinct, flowing naturally into the ocean of omniscience — named "the contemplation of the middle, the ultimate truth." This naming comes from the Yingluo Sūtra. As for what is meant by "the two truths": contemplating the provisional serves as the expression by which one enters emptiness, and emptiness is met by means of that expression — subject and object discussed together — hence it is called "the contemplation of the two truths." Moreover, on the very day emptiness is met, one does not merely see emptiness alone, but also comes to recognize the provisional as well — like clouds clearing to reveal what had been obscured, above made manifest and below made bright at once. Because both the real and the provisional are thereby made manifest, this may be called the contemplation of the two truths. Now, since it is by means of the provisional that the real is met, why should this not also be called the contemplation of the two truths? Again: the conventional is what is broken through; the real is what is put to use. If named from what is broken through, it should be called "the contemplation of the conventional truth"; if named from what is put to use, it should be called "the contemplation of the real truth." Discussed jointly, as both breaking-through and putting-to-use together, it is hence called "the contemplation of the two truths." Again, this is further distinguished into three: in terms of the teaching, there is the contemplation of the two truths according with sentiment; in terms of practice, there is the contemplation of the two truths according with sentiment-and-wisdom together; in terms of realization, there is the contemplation of the two truths according with wisdom. Though the merit of the first contemplation has not yet met the real directly, it can still be discussed as the contemplation of the two truths, according with the teaching or according with practice. Question: The first contemplation takes its name jointly from breaking-through and putting-to-use; the second contemplation likewise involves breaking-through and putting-to-use — should it not likewise be called "the two truths"? Answer: The former has already received the name "the two truths"; though the latter likewise involves breaking-through and putting-to-use, it instead takes its name, from the more excellent side, as "equality." Question: The third contemplation, too, involves breaking-through and putting-to-use — why is it not likewise named from the more excellent side? Answer: The first two contemplations still involve some residue, and so require further breaking-through and further putting-to-use; the third contemplation has no residue at all, and so takes its name simply from its use alone — it cannot be treated by the same rule.

問：前二觀俱觀二諦，亦應俱入二諦？答：初為破病故觀假；為用真故觀真，是故俱觀。一用、一不用，故不俱入。問：真及中，俱得稱諦。界內外俗，俗則非理，云何稱諦？答：《地持》明二法性：一、事法性，性差別故；二、實法性，性真實故。即二諦之異名。既俱得稱法性，何意不得俱稱諦？問：若爾，俱稱涅槃？答：經云：「貧人得寶」乃至「獼猴得酒」。又非想定即世俗涅槃。即其義也。問：若爾，俱無漏耶？答：論云：「世間正見，出世正見。」問：若爾，俱無生耶？答：經云：「異相互無。」問：從假入空，必須破假而入空耶？答：通途應有四句：不破入、破入、破不入、不破不入，乃至三十六句，如後說。

Question: The first two contemplations both contemplate the two truths — should they not both enter into the two truths equally? Answer: The first contemplates the provisional in order to break through illness, and contemplates the real in order to put it to use — hence both are contemplated. But one is put to use and the other is not, hence they do not both "enter" equally. Question: Both the real and the middle can be called "truth" — but the conventional, whether within or beyond the three realms, is not itself principle — how can it be called "truth"? Answer: The Bodhisattva-bhūmi explains two kinds of dharma-nature: first, the phenomenal dharma-nature, since its nature is differentiated; second, the real dharma-nature, since its nature is truly real. These are simply different names for the two truths. Since both can already be called "dharma-nature," why should they not also both be called "truth"? Question: If this is so, should both then also be called "nirvāṇa"? Answer: The sūtra says: "A poor person obtaining treasure" ... down to "a monkey obtaining wine" — and also, the concentration of neither-perception-nor-non-perception is itself called "worldly nirvāṇa." This is exactly that same sense. Question: If this is so, are both then uncontaminated? Answer: The Treatise says: "worldly right view, world-transcending right view." Question: If this is so, are both then without arising? Answer: The sūtra says: "each distinct mark is, of the other, absent." Question: In entering emptiness from the provisional, must the provisional necessarily be broken through before emptiness is entered? Answer: In the general sense, there should be a fourfold pattern: entering without breaking through, entering by breaking through, breaking through without entering, and neither breaking through nor entering — extending even to thirty-six such patterns, as explained further on.

從空入假，名「平等觀」者，若是入空，尚無空可有，何假可入？當知此觀為化眾生，知真非真，方便出假，故言從空分別藥病而無差謬，故言「入假」。「平等」者，望前稱平等也。前觀破假病，不用假法，但用真法；破一、不破一，未為平等。後觀破空病，還用假法，破、用既均，異時相望，故言「平等」也。今當譬之，如盲初得眼開，見空、見色；雖見於色，不能分別種種卉木、根莖、枝葉、藥毒種類。從假入空隨智之時，亦見二諦而不能用假。若人眼開後，能見空、見色，即識種類，洞解因緣，麁、細、藥、食，皆識、皆用，利益於他。此譬從空入假，亦真真俗，正用於假，為化眾生故，名為「入假」。復言平等，意如前說。

As for "entering the provisional from emptiness," named "the contemplation of equality": if one has already entered emptiness, there is not even emptiness itself left to possess — what provisional, then, is there left to enter? One should know that this contemplation, for the sake of transforming sentient beings, knows the real as not simply real, and, through skillful means, emerges into the provisional — hence it is said that, from emptiness, one discriminates medicine from illness without error, and this is called "entering the provisional." "Equality" is a name given by comparison with what came before. The earlier contemplation broke through the illness of the provisional, making no use of provisional dharmas, but using only real dharmas — breaking through one side while not breaking through the other, this is not yet "equality." This later contemplation breaks through the illness of emptiness, and, in turn, makes use of provisional dharmas — breaking-through and putting-to-use now being equally balanced, compared across the two different moments — hence it is called "equality." Let this now be illustrated: it is like a blind person whose eyes have just been opened, seeing empty space and seeing form — yet, though seeing form, unable to distinguish the manifold varieties of plants and trees, roots and stems, branches and leaves, medicinal and poisonous kinds. When entering emptiness from the provisional according with wisdom, one likewise sees the two truths but is not yet able to make use of the provisional. Once such a person's eyes have opened further, able to see both emptiness and form, they come to recognize the various kinds, thoroughly understanding causation, coarse and subtle, medicinal and edible, recognizing and making use of all of it, bringing benefit to others. This illustrates entering the provisional from emptiness — the real truly the real, and the conventional truly the conventional, now genuinely put to use through the provisional, for the sake of transforming sentient beings — hence it is named "entering the provisional." It is again called "equality," with the same sense as explained before.

Reading note: The blind-person simile is worth pausing on: seeing emptiness for the first time is genuine progress, but the newly sighted still cannot yet distinguish "medicinal from poisonous" among the provisional forms now visible — that finer discrimination, needed to actually help anyone, is exactly what "entering the provisional from emptiness" adds. Note too the careful naming logic threaded through the questions and answers: why the second contemplation earns the more prestigious name "equality" rather than reusing "the two truths" (because it has, by this point, no residue left to resolve) is a small but telling window into how deliberately this treatise's terminology is constructed, not merely inherited.

The Contemplation of the Middle, and the Perfect-and-Sudden Mark 教相 · 中道觀 · 圓頓相

「中道第一義觀」者，前觀假空，是空生死；後觀空空，是空涅槃；雙遮二邊，是名「二空觀」為方便道，得會中道，故言：「心心寂滅，流入薩婆若海。」又初觀用空，後觀用假，是為雙存方便；入中道時能雙照二諦。故經言：「心若在定，能知世間生滅法相。」前之兩觀為二種方便，意在此也。問：《大經》云：「定多、慧多，俱不見佛性。」此義云何？答：次第三觀，二乘及通菩薩有初觀分；此屬定多慧少，不見佛性。別教菩薩有第二觀分；此屬慧多定少，亦不見佛性。二觀為方便，得入第三觀，則見佛性。問：經言：「十住菩薩以慧眼故，見不了了。」非全不見。初觀是慧眼位，第二觀是法眼位，云何而言兩眼全不見耶？答：彼次第眼偏定、偏慧，佛之所呵，不可言其見也。所言慧眼見者，其名乃同，實是圓教十住之位。三觀現前，入三諦理，名之為「住」；呼「住」為慧眼耳。故《法華》云：「願得如世尊，慧眼第一淨。」如斯慧眼，分見未了，故言：「如夜見色、空中鵝雁。」非二乘慧眼得如此名。故《法華》中：「譬如有人穿鑿高原，唯見乾土；施功不已，轉見濕土；遂漸至泥，後則得水。」乾土譬初觀；濕土譬第二觀；泥譬第三觀；水譬圓頓觀。又譬於教，三藏教不詮中道，如乾土；通教如濕土；別教如泥；圓教詮中道，如水。二教之所不詮，二行之所不到，偏空慧眼寧得見性？若見性者，無有是處。

As for "the contemplation of the middle, the ultimate truth": the earlier contemplation contemplates the provisional as empty — this empties birth-and-death; the later contemplation contemplates emptiness itself as empty — this empties nirvāṇa; negating both extremes at once, this is named "the twofold contemplation of emptiness," serving as the expedient path by which the middle way is met — hence it is said: "thought after thought quiescent and extinct, flowing into the ocean of omniscience." Moreover, the first contemplation makes use of emptiness, and the second makes use of the provisional — this is the twofold preservation of skillful means; on entering the middle way, one is able to illuminate both truths at once. Hence a sūtra says: "When the mind abides in concentration, it is able to know the marks of arising and ceasing throughout the world." The two contemplations that came before serve as two kinds of expedient means for exactly this reason. Question: The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Too much concentration, too much wisdom — either way, one does not see the buddha-nature." What is the sense of this? Answer: Within the sequential three contemplations, the two lesser vehicles and the shared teaching's own bodhisattva possess a portion of the first contemplation — this belongs to too much concentration and too little wisdom, and does not see the buddha-nature. The distinct teaching's own bodhisattva possesses a portion of the second contemplation — this belongs to too much wisdom and too little concentration, and likewise does not see the buddha-nature. The two contemplations serve as expedient means, and, on entering the third contemplation, the buddha-nature is then seen. Question: A sūtra says: "The bodhisattvas of the ten abidings, by means of the eye of wisdom, see it, though not yet with full clarity" — this is not the same as not seeing it at all. The first contemplation is the stage of the eye of wisdom, and the second contemplation is the stage of the Dharma-eye — how, then, can it be said that these two eyes see nothing at all? Answer: Those sequential eyes are each partial, one toward concentration, the other toward wisdom, and are precisely what the Buddha criticizes — one cannot say that they truly "see" at all. What is here called "seeing by means of the eye of wisdom," though the name is the same, in fact refers to the ten abidings of the perfect teaching. Once the three contemplations stand present, entering the principle of the three truths, this is named "abiding"; and this "abiding" is called the eye of wisdom. Hence the Lotus Sūtra says: "I vow to attain, like the World-Honored One, the eye of wisdom, foremost in its purity." An eye of wisdom of this kind sees only in part, not yet with full clarity — hence it is said: "like seeing forms at night, like geese flying across the sky." This is not the same name given to the two lesser vehicles' own eye of wisdom. Hence in the Lotus Sūtra: "It is like a person digging into a high plateau, seeing at first only dry earth; applying effort without ceasing, they gradually see damp earth; and so, gradually, reach mud, and afterward finally obtain water." Dry earth is a simile for the first contemplation; damp earth, for the second; mud, for the third; water, for the perfect-and-sudden contemplation. Likewise, as a simile for the teachings: the Tripiṭaka teaching does not expound the middle way, and is like dry earth; the shared teaching is like damp earth; the distinct teaching is like mud; the perfect teaching expounds the middle way, and is like water. What the first two teachings do not expound, what the first two practices do not reach — how could the eye of wisdom, one-sidedly fixed on mere emptiness, ever manage to see the buddha-nature? Were it to see the buddha-nature, this would be an impossibility.

此三觀與前三觀，名一往似同，義、相則異。同者，前是貫穿，觀諸虛妄，似從假入空也。前觀達觀，達理理和，達事事和，似入假平等觀也。前不觀觀，似中道也。其相異者，前是一諦相，今是三諦相。又前三觀通成後三，後三具前三。所以者何？如從假入空，破四住磐石，此豈非貫穿義？所入之空，空即是理，智能顯理，即觀達義；此之空理，即是非觀觀義。如此三義，共成入空觀相也。從空入假亦具三義，何以故？識假名法，破無知障，即是貫穿義；照假名理，分別無謬，即觀達義；假理常然，即不觀觀義也。此三義共成假觀相。中道之觀亦具三義：空於二邊即貫穿義；正入中道即觀達義；中道法性即不觀觀義。如此三義共成中道觀相。此依摩訶衍明三止三觀之相；以義隨相，條然各別。若論三觀，則有權實、淺深；若論三智，則有優劣、前後；若論三人，則有諸位大小。此則次第分張，非今所用也。

These three contemplations and the earlier three contemplations resemble each other in name, on first approach, but differ in sense and mark. What is similar: the earlier "piercing through," contemplating every falsehood, resembles entering emptiness from the provisional. The earlier "penetrating understanding," penetrating principle so that principle harmonizes, penetrating phenomena so that phenomena harmonize, resembles the contemplation of equality entering the provisional. The earlier "contemplation-that-is-not-contemplation" resembles the middle. What differs in their mark: the earlier ones bear the mark of a single truth; these here bear the mark of the three truths. Moreover, the earlier three general senses together accomplish the latter three teaching-specific contemplations, and the last of the latter three contains the first two within it. Why is this? As when entering emptiness from the provisional, breaking through the massive rock of the four abodes of affliction — is this not precisely the sense of piercing through? The emptiness thereby entered, that emptiness being itself principle, wisdom able to reveal principle — this is precisely the sense of penetrating understanding; and this very principle of emptiness is itself the sense of contemplation-that-is-not-contemplation. These three senses together accomplish the mark of the contemplation that enters emptiness. Entering the provisional from emptiness likewise contains all three senses: why is this? Recognizing the provisional as dharma, breaking through the obstruction of not-knowing — this is the sense of piercing through; illuminating the provisional as principle, discriminating it without error — this is the sense of penetrating understanding; the principle of the provisional, ever thus — this is the sense of contemplation-that-is-not-contemplation. These three senses together accomplish the mark of the provisional contemplation. The contemplation of the middle likewise contains all three senses: emptying both extremes is the sense of piercing through; correctly entering the middle way is the sense of penetrating understanding; the Dharma-nature of the middle itself is the sense of contemplation-that-is-not-contemplation. These three senses together accomplish the mark of the contemplation of the middle. This, based on the Mahāyāna, explains the mark of the three kinds of calming and three kinds of contemplation — following their sense according to their own distinct mark, each clearly separate from the other. If the three contemplations are discussed this way, there is a difference of provisional and real, shallow and deep; if the three wisdoms are discussed this way, there is a difference of superior and inferior, earlier and later; if the three kinds of person are discussed this way, there is a difference of greater and lesser stage. This, then, is the sequential, divided version — and it is not what is used here in this treatise.

圓頓止觀相者，以止緣於諦，則一諦而三諦；以諦繫於止，則一止而三止。譬如三相在一念心，雖一念心，而有三相。止、諦亦如是；所止之法，雖一而三；能止之心，雖三而一也。以觀觀於境，則一境而三境；以境發於觀，則一觀而三觀。如摩醯首羅面上三目，雖是三目，而是一面。觀境亦如是，觀三即一，發一即三，不可思議。不權不實，不優不劣，不前不後，不並不別，不大不小。故《中論》云：「因緣所生法，即空、即假、即中。」又如《金剛般若》云：「如人有目，日光明照，見種種色。」若眼獨見，不應須日；若無色者，雖有日、眼，亦無所見。如是三法，不異時、不相離。眼喻於止；日喻於觀；色喻於境。如是三法不前不後，一時論三。三中論一，亦復如是。若見此意，即解圓頓教止觀相也。

As for the mark of the perfect-and-sudden calming-and-contemplation: since calming takes the truth as its object, one truth is thereby three truths; since the truth binds itself to calming, one calming is thereby three kinds of calming. It is like the three marks present within a single thought of mind: though a single thought, it possesses three marks. Calming and truth are exactly the same: the dharma that is calmed, though one, is three; the mind that calms, though three, is one. Since contemplation contemplates its object, one object is thereby three objects; since the object brings forth contemplation, one contemplation is thereby three kinds of contemplation. It is like Maheśvara's three eyes upon his one face: though three eyes, it is a single face. Contemplation and object are exactly the same: contemplated as three, it is yet one; brought forth as one, it is yet three — inconceivable. Neither provisional nor real, neither superior nor inferior, neither earlier nor later, neither juxtaposed nor separate, neither great nor small. Hence the Middle Treatise says: "What arises through causes and conditions is at once empty, at once provisional, and at once the middle." Again, as the Diamond Sūtra says: "It is like a person with eyes: when the sun's light shines brightly, they see every kind of form." If the eye alone could see, it would have no need of the sun; and if there were no form at all, then even with both sun and eye, still nothing would be seen. These three dharmas are never at different times, never separate from one another. The eye is a simile for calming; the sun, for contemplation; form, for the object. These three dharmas, neither earlier nor later, are discussed all at once, as three. Discussed as three within the one, it is exactly the same. Grasping this sense, one thereby understands the mark of the perfect-and-sudden teaching's own calming-and-contemplation.

何但三一、一三？總前諸義，皆在一心。其相云何？體無明顛倒即是實相之真，名「體真止」。如此實相遍一切處，隨緣歷境，安心不動，名「隨緣方便止」。生死涅槃，靜散休息，名「息二邊止」。體一切諸假悉皆是空，空即實相，名「入空觀」。達此空時，觀冥中道，能知世間生滅法相，如實而見，名「入假觀」。如此空慧即是中道，無二無別，名「中道觀」。體真之時，五住磐石、砂礫，一念休息，名「止息義」。心緣中道，入實相慧，名「停止義」。實相之性，即「非止非不止義」。又此一念能穿五住，達於實相；實相非觀，亦非不觀。如此等義但在一念心中，不動真際，而有種種差別。經言：「善能分別諸法相，於第一義而不動。」雖多名字，蓋乃「般若之一法，佛說種種名」。眾名皆圓，諸義亦圓。相待、絕待，對「體」不可思議；不可思議故，無有障礙；無有障礙故，具足無減。是圓頓教相顯止觀體也。

But why stop at merely three-and-one, one-and-three? Every sense discussed above is, in fact, all contained within a single mind. What is its mark? Embodying ignorance and every inversion as itself the very real of the true mark of reality is named "the calming that embodies the real." This true mark of reality, pervading every place, following conditions through every object encountered, the mind settled and unmoved, is named "the calming of skillful means that follows conditions." Birth-and-death and nirvāṇa, agitation and dispersal, both alike at rest, is named "the calming that stills the two extremes." Embodying every provisional thing as, every one, empty, that emptiness itself the true mark of reality, is named "the contemplation that enters emptiness." Penetrating this emptiness, contemplation merging with the middle way, able to know the marks of arising and ceasing throughout the world, seeing them exactly as they truly are, is named "the contemplation that enters the provisional." This very wisdom of emptiness is itself the middle way, with no two of them, no separateness at all, and is named "the contemplation of the middle." At the moment of embodying the real, the massive rock and gravel of the five abodes of affliction, in a single thought, come to rest — this is named "the sense of ceasing." The mind taking the middle way as its object, entering the wisdom of the true mark of reality, is named "the sense of stilling." The very nature of the true mark of reality is itself "the sense of neither calming nor not-calming." Moreover, this very single thought is able to pierce through the five abodes of affliction, penetrating to the true mark of reality; and the true mark of reality is itself neither contemplation nor not-contemplation. All such senses as these are contained entirely within a single thought of mind, never moving from the very brink of the real, and yet manifesting every kind of distinction. A sūtra says: "Skillfully able to distinguish the marks of every dharma, and yet, with regard to the ultimate meaning, never moving at all." Though there are many names, this is simply "prajñā, a single dharma, which the Buddha teaches under manifold names." Every name is already perfect; every sense is likewise already perfect. Mutual opposition and the absolute, taken together against the essence, are alike inconceivable; and, being inconceivable, they are free of any obstruction; and, being free of obstruction, they are complete and lack nothing. This is how the perfect-and-sudden teaching's own mark reveals the essence of calming-and-contemplation.

Reading note: This is one of the treatise's most important passages, and worth reading twice: it takes the sequential three-teaching account of calming and contemplation just built up in painstaking detail — dry earth, damp earth, mud, water — and then reveals that the "water" stage, the perfect-and-sudden, was never actually a fourth rung on the same ladder at all. Maheśvara's three eyes on one face, and the eye-sun-form simile from the Diamond Sūtra (none of the three useful without the other two, none reducible to the others, none temporally prior), both make the same point: truth, calming, and contemplation are "neither earlier nor later," present together in a single thought from the very start. The whole elaborate sequential apparatus just constructed — three kinds of calming, three kinds of contemplation, each analyzed at length — turns out, in the treatise's own words, to be "all contained within a single mind." This closes the teaching-mark subsection of topic three.

二、明眼智者，體則非知非見、非因非果，說之己自難，何況以示人？雖叵知見，由於眼智，則可知見；雖非因果，由因果顯。止觀為因，智眼為果。因是顯體之遠由，果是顯體之近由。其體冥妙，不可分別；寄於眼智，令體可解。今先明「次第眼智」者，三止三觀為因，所得三智三眼為果。三止者，若體真止，妄惑不生，因止發定，定生無漏；慧眼開故，見第一義，真諦三昧成。故止能成眼，眼能見體，得真體也。若隨緣止，冥真出假，心安俗諦；因此止故，得陀羅尼；陀羅尼分別藥病，法眼豁開，破障通無知，常在三昧，不以二相見諸佛土，則俗諦三昧成。是則止能發眼，眼能得體，得俗體也。若息二邊止，則生死、涅槃，空、有雙寂；因於此止，發中道定，佛眼豁開，照無不遍，中道三昧成。故止能得眼，眼能得體，得中道體也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying eyes and wisdom: the essence itself is neither knowing nor seeing, neither cause nor fruit; to speak of it is already difficult enough for oneself, let alone to show it to another. Though it cannot be known or seen directly, by means of eyes and wisdom, it can indeed be known and seen; though it is neither cause nor fruit, it is made manifest by means of cause and fruit. Calming-and-contemplation serves as the cause; wisdom and eyes serve as the fruit. The cause is the distant basis for revealing the essence; the fruit is the near basis for revealing the essence. The essence itself is hidden and subtle, beyond all discrimination — hence it is expressed by way of eyes and wisdom, so that the essence may be understood. Here, first, "the sequential eyes and wisdom" are explained: the three kinds of calming and three kinds of contemplation serve as the cause, and the three eyes and three wisdoms thereby obtained serve as the fruit. As for the three kinds of calming: in the calming that embodies the real, deluded affliction does not arise; through this calming, concentration is brought forth, and that concentration gives rise to the uncontaminated; the eye of wisdom thereby opens, seeing ultimate meaning, and the samādhi of the real truth is accomplished. Hence calming is able to accomplish the eye; the eye is able to see the essence; the real essence is thereby attained. In the calming that follows conditions, merging with the real while emerging into the provisional, the mind settles upon the conventional truth; through this calming, dhāraṇī is obtained; dhāraṇī discriminates medicine from illness, the Dharma-eye opens wide, breaking through obstruction and penetrating not-knowing, constantly abiding in samādhi, seeing every buddha-land without any duality of aspect — and the samādhi of the conventional truth is accomplished. Hence calming is able to bring forth the eye; the eye is able to attain the essence; the conventional essence is thereby attained. In the calming that stills the two extremes, birth-and-death and nirvāṇa, emptiness and existence, are both alike stilled together; through this calming, the concentration of the middle way is brought forth, the buddha-eye opens wide, illuminating everywhere without exception, and the samādhi of the middle way is accomplished. Hence calming is able to attain the eye; the eye is able to attain the essence; the essence of the middle way is thereby attained.

三觀者，若從假入空，空慧相應，即能破見思惑，成一切智。智能得體，得真體也。若從空入假，分別藥病，種種法門，即破無知，成道種智。智能得體，得俗體也。若雙遮二邊，為入中方便，能破無明，成一切種智。智能得體，得中道體也。是則三止三觀共成三眼三智，各得三體。是故顯體而談眼智，即此意也。問：眼見、智知，知、見異耶？答：此應四句分別：知而非見，見而非知，亦知亦見，不知不見。凡夫不證故不見，不聞故不知；二乘人證故亦見，聞故亦知；支佛證故是見，不聞故不知；方便道人聞故是知，未證故不見。復次，信行人因聞故有慧，因慧故發無漏，得一切智。此智因聞，故稱智知；法行人思惟得定，因定發無漏，成慧眼。此眼因禪，故稱眼見。然知見同證真諦，從所因處仍本受名，故言知見也。此就慧眼一切智作此分別，餘二眼二智例爾。若一心眼智則不如此。

As for the three kinds of contemplation: in entering emptiness from the provisional, wisdom corresponding to emptiness, one is thereby able to break through the afflictions of views and habituated thought, accomplishing the wisdom that knows all things generally. This wisdom is able to attain the essence — the real essence is thereby attained. In entering the provisional from emptiness, discriminating medicine from illness across every dharma-gate, one thereby breaks through not-knowing, accomplishing the wisdom of the seeds of the path. This wisdom is able to attain the essence — the conventional essence is thereby attained. In negating both extremes at once, serving as the expedient means for entering the middle, one is able to break through ignorance, accomplishing the wisdom that knows all things in every mode. This wisdom is able to attain the essence — the essence of the middle way is thereby attained. Thus the three kinds of calming and three kinds of contemplation together accomplish the three eyes and three wisdoms, each attaining its own essence in turn. This is exactly the sense in which revealing the essence is discussed by way of eyes and wisdom. Question: The eye sees, wisdom knows — do knowing and seeing differ? Answer: This should be distinguished by four alternatives: knowing but not seeing; seeing but not knowing; both knowing and seeing; neither knowing nor seeing. The ordinary being, not yet having realized it, does not see, and, not yet having heard of it, does not know. The two lesser vehicles, having realized it, do see, and, having heard of it, do know. The pratyekabuddha, having realized it, does see, but, not having heard it taught, does not know. The practitioner of the expedient path, having heard of it, does know, but, not yet having realized it, does not see. Furthermore, the practitioner of faith-conduct, through hearing, gains wisdom; through wisdom, brings forth the uncontaminated; and thereby attains the wisdom that knows all things generally. This wisdom, arising through hearing, is called "wisdom-knowing." The practitioner of dharma-conduct, through contemplation, attains concentration; through concentration, brings forth the uncontaminated; and thereby accomplishes the eye of wisdom. This eye, arising through dhyāna, is called "eye-seeing." Yet knowing and seeing both alike realize the real truth — it is only from the basis through which each was reached that the original name is retained, hence they are spoken of as "knowing" and "seeing" respectively. This distinction is made here specifically in terms of the eye of wisdom and the wisdom that knows all things generally; the other two eyes and two wisdoms follow this same pattern. Where eyes and wisdom are of a single mind, however, this distinction does not apply.

若明「不次第止觀眼智」者，如前所說，止即是觀，觀即是止，無二無別。得體近由亦如是，眼即是智，智即是眼。眼故論見，智故論知；知即是見，見即是知。佛眼具五眼，佛智具三智。王三昧，一切三昧悉入其中；首楞嚴定，攝一切定。《大品》云：「欲得道慧、道種慧、一切智、一切種智，當學般若。」問：《釋論》云：「三智在一心中。」云何言：「欲得道慧等，當學般若？」答：實爾！三智在一心中。為向人說，令易解故，作如此說耳。《金剛般若》云：「如來有肉眼不？答云：有」乃至「如來有佛眼不？答云：有。」雖有五眼，實不分張；秖約一眼，備有五用，能照五境。所以者何？佛眼亦能照麁色，如人所見，亦過人所見，名「肉眼」；亦能照細色，如天所見，亦過天所見，名「天眼」；達麁細色空，如二乘所見，名「慧眼」；達假名不謬，如菩薩所見，名「法眼」；於諸法中皆見實相，名「佛眼」。當知佛眼圓照無遺。故經云：「五眼具足成菩提，永與三界作父母。」而獨稱「佛眼」者，如眾流入海，失本名字，非無四用也。

As for clarifying "the non-sequential eyes and wisdom of calming-and-contemplation": as explained earlier, calming is itself contemplation, and contemplation is itself calming, with no two of them, no separateness at all. The near basis for attaining the essence is exactly the same: the eye is itself wisdom, and wisdom is itself the eye. Because it is an eye, seeing is spoken of; because it is wisdom, knowing is spoken of — yet knowing is itself seeing, and seeing is itself knowing. The buddha-eye is complete with all five eyes; the buddha's wisdom is complete with all three wisdoms. The king of samādhis, every samādhi enters within it entirely; the Śūraṅgama concentration gathers in every concentration. The Larger Sūtra says: "Wishing to attain the wisdom of the path, the wisdom of the seeds of the path, the wisdom that knows all things generally, and the wisdom that knows all things in every mode, one should study prajñā." Question: The Treatise says: "The three wisdoms are within a single mind." How, then, can it be said, "wishing to attain the wisdom of the path and the rest, one should study prajñā"? Answer: This is indeed so! The three wisdoms are within a single mind — it is only for the sake of addressing people, to make it easier to understand, that it is spoken of in this way. The Diamond Sūtra says: "Does the Tathāgata have the fleshly eye? — Answer: he does" ... down to "Does the Tathāgata have the buddha-eye? — Answer: he does." Though there are five eyes, they are, in fact, not truly divided apart from one another; it is simply that a single eye is complete with all five functions, able to illuminate five different objects. Why is this? The buddha-eye can also illuminate coarse form, seeing as an ordinary person sees, and yet surpassing what an ordinary person sees — this is named the "fleshly eye." It can also illuminate subtle form, seeing as a deva sees, and yet surpassing what a deva sees — this is named the "heavenly eye." Penetrating that both coarse and subtle form are empty, as the two lesser vehicles see — this is named the "eye of wisdom." Penetrating the provisional name without error, as a bodhisattva sees — this is named the "Dharma-eye." Seeing the true mark of reality within every dharma whatsoever — this is named the "buddha-eye." One should know that the buddha-eye illuminates everywhere perfectly, leaving nothing out. Hence a sūtra says: "Complete with the five eyes, one accomplishes bodhi, and becomes forever the father and mother of the three realms." That it is called, singly, the "buddha-eye" alone is like how every stream, flowing into the sea, loses its own original name — not that the other four functions are thereby absent.

佛智照空，如二乘所見，名「一切智」；佛智照假，如菩薩所見，名「道種智」；佛智照空假中，皆見實相，名「一切種智」。故言「三智一心中得」，故知一心三止所成三眼，見不思議三諦；此見從止得，故受「眼」名。一心三觀所成三智，知不思議三境；此智從觀得，故受「智」名。境之與諦，左右異耳。見之與知，眼目殊稱，不應別說。今將境來顯智，令三觀易明；用諦來目眼，使三止可解。雖作三說，實是不可思議一法耳。用此一法眼智，得圓頓止觀體也。如此解釋，本於觀心；實非讀經，安置次比。為避人嫌疑，為增長信，幸與修多羅合，故引為證耳。

The buddha's wisdom illuminating emptiness, as the two lesser vehicles see, is named "the wisdom that knows all things generally"; the buddha's wisdom illuminating the provisional, as a bodhisattva sees, is named "the wisdom of the seeds of the path"; the buddha's wisdom illuminating emptiness, the provisional, and the middle together, seeing the true mark of reality in every case, is named "the wisdom that knows all things in every mode." Hence it is said, "the three wisdoms are attained within a single mind." Hence it is known that the single mind's three kinds of calming accomplish the three eyes, seeing the inconceivable three truths; this seeing is attained through calming, hence it receives the name "eye." The single mind's three kinds of contemplation accomplish the three wisdoms, knowing the inconceivable three objects; this knowing is attained through contemplation, hence it receives the name "wisdom." "Object" and "truth" differ only as left differs from right; "seeing" and "knowing" differ only as "eye" differs from "sight" in name — they should not be discussed as fundamentally separate things. Here, "object" is brought in to reveal wisdom, making the three contemplations easier to understand; and "truth" is used to name the eyes, making the three kinds of calming easier to grasp. Though stated in three different ways, it is, in reality, a single, inconceivable dharma alone. Making use of this single dharma of eyes and wisdom, the essence of the perfect-and-sudden calming-and-contemplation is attained. This explanation is grounded in the contemplation of mind itself — it is not, in truth, simply reading scripture and arranging citations in sequence. It is offered only to avoid arousing others' suspicion, and to strengthen faith, being fortunate enough to accord with the sūtras — hence it is cited here as confirming evidence, and nothing more.

Reading note: "Eyes and wisdom" gives the essence — which cannot itself be directly known or seen — its practical fruit: three eyes and three wisdoms, matched one-to-one with the three kinds of calming and three kinds of contemplation just explained. The buddha-eye passage is a striking piece of reasoning: the five eyes named across scripture (fleshly, heavenly, wisdom, Dharma, buddha) are not five separate organs but one eye's five functions, "as every stream, flowing into the sea, loses its own original name" — the highest name absorbs, rather than discards, the ones beneath it. The closing methodological aside is worth reading carefully in its own right: Zhiyi states outright that this whole account is "grounded in the contemplation of mind itself," with scriptural citations added afterward only "to avoid arousing others' suspicion, and to strengthen faith" — a rare moment where the treatise explains its own relationship to the texts it quotes, closer to independent realization confirmed by scripture than exegesis derived from it.

三、明境界者，若得「能顯眼智」中意，無俟「所顯諦聽」之說。為未解者，更此一科。夫信行尚多聞，因此分別，以會圓妙；法行宗深觀，緣此思惟，以見正境耳。就此為二：一、明說境意，二、明諸境離合。經云：「為諸眾生，開佛知見。」若無中境，智無所知，眼無所見；當知應有佛眼境也。《經》云：「世孰有真天眼者？不以二相見諸佛土。」若無俗境，此眼不應見於佛土。經云：「天眼開闢，慧眼見真。」故知應有慧眼境也。此三諦理不可思議，無決定性，實不可說。若為緣說，不出三意：一、隨情說，即隨他意語；二、隨情智說，即隨自他意語；三、隨智說，即隨自意語。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying objects: if one has already grasped the sense within "eyes and wisdom that reveal," there is no need for this further account of "the objects thereby revealed." For those who have not yet fully understood, this one further section is given. Practitioners of faith-conduct still rely on extensive learning, and by means of this discrimination come to meet the perfect and wondrous; practitioners of dharma-conduct take deep contemplation as their guiding principle, and by means of this reflection come to see the correct object directly. This is divided into two: first, clarifying the purpose of discussing objects; second, clarifying the convergence and divergence of the various objects. A sūtra says: "For the sake of sentient beings, the Buddha opens the Buddha's own knowledge and vision." If there were no object corresponding to the middle, wisdom would have nothing to know, and the eye nothing to see — one should know, then, that there must indeed be an object for the buddha-eye. A sūtra says: "Who in the world truly has the heavenly eye? One who sees every buddha-land without any duality of aspect." If there were no conventional object, this eye could not see any buddha-land at all. A sūtra says: "The heavenly eye opens wide; the eye of wisdom sees the real." Hence one should know that there must indeed be an object for the eye of wisdom as well. This principle of the three truths is inconceivable, without any fixed, determinate nature, and, in reality, cannot be spoken of at all. Yet, spoken for the sake of some condition or occasion, it does not exceed three senses: first, spoken according with sentiment, that is, language following another's own intent; second, spoken according with sentiment-and-wisdom together, that is, language following both one's own and another's intent; third, spoken according with wisdom, that is, language following one's own intent alone.

According with Sentiment: The Blind and the Color of Milk 隨情說

Chinese Source

云何隨情說三諦？如盲不識乳，便問他言：「乳色何似？」他人答言：「色白如貝、粖、雪、鶴等。」雖聞此說，亦不能了乳之真色。是諸盲人各各作解，競執貝、粖而起四諍。凡情愚翳亦復如是。不識三諦大悲方便而為分別，或約有門明三諦，如盲聞貝；或約空門明三諦，如盲聞粖；或作空有門明三諦，如盲聞雪；或作非空非有門明三諦，如盲聞鶴。雖聞此說，未即諦理。是諸凡夫終不能見常樂我淨真實之相。雖未得見，各執空有，互相是非。所以常途解二諦者，二十三家，家家不同，各各異見；皆引經論，莫知孰是。若言併是，理則無量；若言併非，悉有所據。為此義故，執自非他，雖飲甘露，傷命早夭。經稱文殊、彌勒未悟之時，共諍二諦，兩墮地獄。今世凡情偏執一文，鏗然固著；雖謂為能，恐乖佛旨。如是等人，皆未識隨情三諦。若識此意，聞種種說，即知如來俯逐根情；根情既多，說不一種。此即是隨他意而說三諦也。

Ru-Yi Translation

How is it that the three truths are spoken of according with sentiment? It is like a blind person who does not know what milk is, and so asks another: "What does the color of milk resemble?" The other person answers: "Its color is white, like a conch shell, like rice-flour, like snow, like a crane." Though hearing this explanation, the blind person is still unable to understand milk's own true color. Each of these blind people forms their own understanding, competing to hold to "shell" or "rice-flour," and so four disputes arise among them. Ordinary sentiment, clouded by delusion, is exactly the same. Not recognizing the three truths, the Buddha, out of great compassion, employs skillful means and makes distinctions accordingly: sometimes explaining the three truths by way of the gate of being, like the blind person hearing "shell"; sometimes by way of the gate of emptiness, like hearing "rice-flour"; sometimes by way of the gate of both emptiness and being, like hearing "snow"; sometimes by way of the gate of neither emptiness nor being, like hearing "crane." Though hearing these explanations, one has still not reached the truth of principle itself. Every one of these ordinary beings, in the end, is still unable to see the true mark of permanence, bliss, self, and purity. Though not yet able to see it, each clings to their own view of emptiness or being, each declaring the other mistaken. This is precisely why, among those who explain the two truths in the ordinary way, there are twenty-three schools, no two schools the same, each holding a different view — every one of them citing sūtras and treatises, with no way to know which is correct. If it is said that all are equally right, then the principle would be endlessly multiplied; if it is said that all are equally wrong, each still has genuine scriptural grounds. For this very reason, clinging to one's own view and rejecting another's, though drinking the ambrosia of the Dharma itself, one's own life is cut short prematurely. The sūtras tell how Mañjuśrī and Maitreya, before their own awakening, once disputed the two truths together, and both fell into hell for it. People of the present age, clinging one-sidedly and rigidly to a single passage of text, though they suppose themselves capable, are likely straying from the Buddha's own actual intent. People of this kind have, every one, failed to recognize the three truths spoken according with sentiment. If one recognizes this sense, then, hearing every kind of differing explanation, one knows that the Tathāgata is simply bending down to accommodate the sentiment of each different capacity; and since capacities and sentiments are so manifold, the explanations given are not all of one kind. This is exactly what is meant by the three truths spoken according with another's own intent.

Reading note: This parable — plainly a relative of the more famous blind-men-and-the-elephant story — makes a sharper and more specific point than the general "reality is bigger than any one description" moral usually drawn from that better-known tale. Here, every one of the four descriptions offered (shell, rice-flour, snow, crane) is true of milk's actual color; the blind listeners' error is not that they were told something false, but that they mistook one true, partial description for a complete grasp of the thing itself, and then went to war with each other over it. Zhiyi's target is explicit and dates his own moment precisely: twenty-three rival schools of Two Truths exegesis in the Chinese Buddhism of his day, each citing genuine scripture, each unable to see that the apparent conflict was a symptom of their own unopened eyes rather than a real contradiction in the teaching.

According with Sentiment-and-Wisdom, and According with Wisdom Alone 隨情智說 · 隨智說

Chinese Source

隨情智說三諦者，就情說二，就智說一。若爾，不得一所論三。此就凡情，凡情悉是方便，雖即一而三，但束為二。若就聖智，聖智皆是實得，雖即一而三，但束為一。情、智相望，故言三諦，如相似位人，六根淨時，猶未發真見於中道。雖觀三諦，約位往明，但破四住及塵沙惑，既證方便道；但束為二諦。若入初住，破無明、見佛性，雙照二諦，方稱為智；亦具三諦，但束為中道第一義諦。情、智合論，即是隨自他意語也。隨智說三諦者，從初住去，非但說中，絕於視聽，真俗亦然。三諦玄微，唯智所照，不可示、不可思，聞者驚怪。非內非外，非難非易，非相非非相，非是世法，無有相貌，百非洞遣，四句皆亡，唯佛與佛乃能究盡；言語道斷，心行處滅。不可以凡情圖想，若一若三，皆絕情望。尚非二乘所測，何況凡夫？如乳真色，眼開乃見；徒費言語，盲終不識。如是說者，名為隨智說三諦相也，即是隨自意語。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the three truths spoken according with sentiment-and-wisdom together: in terms of sentiment, two are spoken of; in terms of wisdom, one is spoken of. If this is so, does this not fail to account for the "three" under a single "one"? This is discussed from the side of ordinary sentiment: ordinary sentiment is entirely a matter of skillful means, and though it is, in reality, one truth that is three, it is here bundled together as two. Discussed from the side of the sage's own wisdom: the sage's wisdom is entirely a matter of genuine attainment, and though it too is one truth that is three, it is here bundled together as one. Sentiment and wisdom viewed against each other are why "three truths" are spoken of — as with a person of the stage of resemblance, at the point of the six purified faculties, who has not yet brought forth the true seeing of the middle way. Though such a person contemplates the three truths, discussed in terms of their own stage, they have only broken through the four abodes of affliction and the dust-and-sand afflictions, having only realized the expedient path — hence this is bundled together merely as the two truths. Once one enters the first abiding-stage, breaking through ignorance and seeing the buddha-nature, illuminating both truths at once, only then is this properly called "wisdom"; and this, too, is complete with the three truths, yet bundled together as the single "truth of the middle, the ultimate meaning." Sentiment and wisdom discussed jointly together is exactly what is meant by language following both one's own and another's intent. As for the three truths spoken according with wisdom alone: from the first abiding-stage onward, it is not only the middle that is beyond seeing and hearing altogether — the real and the conventional are exactly the same. The three truths, profound and subtle, are illuminated by wisdom alone, unable to be shown, unable to be thought about — those who hear of them are startled and bewildered. Neither internal nor external, neither difficult nor easy, neither a mark nor not a mark, not any worldly dharma at all, possessing no describable appearance whatsoever, every one of the hundred negations swept entirely away, every one of the four alternatives utterly gone — only a buddha, together with another buddha, can fully fathom it; the path of language is cut off, the place where the mind moves extinguished. It cannot be pictured or imagined by ordinary sentiment — whether spoken of as one or as three, both are equally beyond the reach of sentiment. This is not even within the two lesser vehicles' own power to fathom — how much less the ordinary being? It is like milk's own true color: only once the eyes are opened is it seen; words spent on it otherwise are wasted, for the blind, in the end, never come to know it. An explanation given in this way is named the mark of the three truths spoken according with wisdom, which is exactly language following one's own intent alone.

Confirmed from the Two Truths, and the Four Siddhāntas Within Each 二諦例成 · 四悉檀

Chinese Source

今更引經中所明二諦文，顯成三諦之說。若言：「凡夫人即能體達因緣，生於觀解。」豈非隨情說俗？「體因緣即空」，豈非隨情說真？若如此者，即是隨情說二諦也。若言：「凡夫心所見，名為俗諦；聖人心所見，名為真諦。」如此說者，豈非隨情智說二諦也？若言：「凡夫行世間，不知世間相。」凡夫尚不知世間之俗，那得知真？故知二諦皆非凡情所識。如此說者，豈非隨智說二諦？二諦既有三番說，三諦例此可解。疑者若言：「佛常依二諦說法，故有三番二諦意。」今亦例此，佛常好中道，降胎、出生、出家、成道、入滅皆在中夜。一色一香無非中道；若說中道，豈不三意赴緣耶？又一一說各具四悉檀意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Now, citing further from the sūtras' own passages explaining the two truths, this reveals and completes the account of the three truths. If it is said: "The ordinary person is indeed able to embody and penetrate causes and conditions, giving rise to contemplative understanding" — is this not the conventional spoken of according with sentiment? "Embodying causes and conditions as empty" — is this not the real spoken of according with sentiment? If this is so, this is precisely the two truths spoken according with sentiment. If it is said: "What the ordinary mind sees is named the conventional truth; what the sage's mind sees is named the real truth" — is a statement of this kind not precisely the two truths spoken according with sentiment-and-wisdom together? If it is said: "The ordinary person walks through the world, yet does not know the world's own mark" — since the ordinary person does not even know the conventional truth of the world, how could they know the real? Hence it is known that neither truth is recognized by ordinary sentiment at all. Is a statement of this kind not precisely the two truths spoken according with wisdom alone? Since the two truths already have these three ways of being spoken of, the three truths may be understood by the same pattern. Should a doubter say: "The Buddha always teaches the Dharma by means of the two truths — hence there are these three ways of understanding the two truths" — this too follows the same pattern: the Buddha always favored the middle way as well — his descent into the womb, his birth, his going forth from home, his accomplishment of the path, and his entry into extinction all occurred at the middle of the night. A single color, a single scent — none of it is other than the middle way; if the middle way is spoken of, would this not, too, address different occasions in three ways? And each single instance of teaching, in turn, is complete with the sense of all four siddhāntas.

隨情中四意者，夫諦理不可說，說必寄言，言必契情，情必欣悅。或聞真歡喜；或聞俗歡喜；或聞中歡喜。此即是隨情中用世界悉檀意也。夫眾生便宜不同，或聞說無，戒慧增長；或聞說有，戒慧增長；或聞說中，戒慧增長。此即隨情中用為人悉檀意也。夫行者破惡不同，或聞有法，能破睡眠、覺觀等；或聞無法，能破睡、散等；或聞中法，能破睡、散等。此即隨情中用對治悉檀意也。夫眾生入悟不同，或聞無開解；或聞有超悟；或聞中發徹。乃至觀心亦爾，或說有觀，恍如雲影；或作無觀，泯失身心；或作中觀，神智明白。如是等種種不同，應在一不在二；應在二不在一。故云：「佛說生法，於無生法得度，佛說無生法，於生法得度。」此即是用第一義悉檀意也。故《法華》云：「佛知眾生種種欲、種種行、種種性、種種憶想。」即此四意。何故爾？種種欲，是隨世界；種種性，是生善；種種行，是對治；種種憶想，是第一義。何故「性」屬生善，「行」屬對治破惡耶？若通論，性善有冥有顯，行惡亦有冥有顯。今從義便，善是冥伏，惡是彰露。如佛未出時，三乘善根冥伏不現，故言善性冥也。若聞三諦，此善發生，故知種種性應屬生善，可對為人悉檀也。又佛未出時，諸眾生惡行彰顯，邪非僻倒，過失現前；佛為破此惡故，說於三諦。故知種種行屬破惡，即對治悉檀也。種種憶想是第一義者，想是慧數，僻故成心倒、見倒等；若遇知識，正此想慧，即成三不倒。佛欲正其此慧，故說三諦，即第一義也。

As for the fourfold sense within "according with sentiment": the truth of principle cannot be spoken of at all, and yet, when spoken, it must rely on words, and words must accord with sentiment, and sentiment must find delight in them. Some, hearing of the real, rejoice; some, hearing of the conventional, rejoice; some, hearing of the middle, rejoice — this is exactly the use, within "according with sentiment," of the worldly siddhānta. Sentient beings' own capacities differ: some, on hearing of non-being, grow in precepts and wisdom; some, on hearing of being, grow in precepts and wisdom; some, on hearing of the middle, grow in precepts and wisdom — this is exactly the use, within "according with sentiment," of the siddhānta for the individual. Practitioners break through evil differently: some, on hearing of the dharma of being, are able to break through drowsiness, discursive thought, and the like; some, on hearing of the dharma of non-being, are able to break through drowsiness, distraction, and the like; some, on hearing of the dharma of the middle, are able to break through drowsiness, distraction, and the like — this is exactly the use, within "according with sentiment," of the antidote siddhānta. Sentient beings enter awakening differently: some, on hearing of non-being, gain understanding; some, on hearing of being, awaken all at once; some, on hearing of the middle, break through completely. This holds equally for contemplating the mind: some describe a contemplation of being, indistinct as a cloud's own shadow; some practice a contemplation of non-being, in which body and mind alike dissolve away; some practice a contemplation of the middle, spirit and wisdom made bright and clear. Given such manifold differences as these, sometimes the truth should rest in the "one" and not the "two"; sometimes it should rest in the "two" and not the "one." Hence it is said: "The Buddha teaches the dharma of birth so that beings may be liberated through the dharma of no-birth; the Buddha teaches the dharma of no-birth so that beings may be liberated through the dharma of birth." This is exactly the use of the siddhānta of ultimate meaning. Hence the Lotus Sūtra says: "The Buddha knows sentient beings' manifold desires, manifold conduct, manifold natures, manifold recollections and notions" — this is exactly these same four senses. Why is this? Manifold desires correspond to according with the worldly; manifold natures correspond to generating the good; manifold conduct corresponds to the antidote; manifold recollections and notions correspond to ultimate meaning. Why should "nature" belong to generating the good, and "conduct" belong to the antidote that breaks through evil? Discussed generally, wholesome nature has both a hidden and a manifest aspect, and unwholesome conduct likewise has both a hidden and a manifest aspect. Here, for convenience of meaning, the good is taken as hidden and latent, and evil as manifest and exposed. Thus, before a buddha appears in the world, the wholesome roots of the three vehicles lie hidden and latent, not yet manifest — hence it is said that wholesome nature is "hidden." On hearing the three truths, this wholesomeness is brought forth and generated — hence it is known that "manifold natures" should properly belong to generating the good, matched to the siddhānta for the individual. Again, before a buddha appears in the world, sentient beings' unwholesome conduct lies manifest and exposed, deviance and error and inversion, fault and transgression standing plainly before them; the Buddha, in order to break through this evil, teaches the three truths — hence it is known that "manifold conduct" belongs to breaking through evil, that is, the antidote siddhānta. As for "manifold recollections and notions" corresponding to ultimate meaning: "notion" is a mental factor of discernment, and, when deviant, becomes the inversion of mind and the inversion of view; should one meet a good spiritual friend, this discerning faculty is corrected, and the three non-inversions are thereby accomplished. The Buddha, wishing to correct this discerning faculty, teaches the three truths — this is exactly ultimate meaning.

隨情說三諦既具四意；隨情智、隨智說三諦，例此可解。是則三四十二種，說三諦不同，豈可以凡情局聖，謂唯一種，執諍自毀耶？若知聖說無崖，終不是此非彼、起增上慢、高舉稜層。如有智盲人，莫諍乳色；勤行方便，慚愧有羞。以三止證三眼，見三法，獲三智，知三諦；見中分明，雙照曉了。如雲除發障，上顯下明。爾時乃可諦審是非，決定師子吼也。私謂：「隨情是併與；隨情智是半與半奪；隨智是併奪。何者？如聖語凡云：『汝今心想，即是俗；能體達俗虛，即是真。』豈非併與相？『汝今所知百千推畫，皆是俗；唯聖所知，乃是真。』豈非半與半奪相？夫『二諦者，凡人併不識，上聖獨能知。』此豈非併奪？此釋易解，故錄之。」

Since the three truths spoken according with sentiment are already complete with these four senses, the three truths spoken according with sentiment-and-wisdom, and according with wisdom alone, may be understood by this same pattern. This gives three ways multiplied by four senses — twelve kinds in all — of speaking the three truths differently. How, then, could one confine the sage's own teaching within the narrow bounds of ordinary sentiment, supposing there to be only a single correct way of putting it, clinging and disputing to one's own ruin? If one knows that the sage's own teaching has no fixed boundary, one will, in the end, never affirm this and deny that, never give rise to overweening pride, never hold oneself arrogantly aloof. Like an intelligent person who happens to be blind, one should not dispute over the color of milk, but diligently practice skillful means, with shame and a proper sense of humility. By means of the three kinds of calming, realizing the three eyes, seeing the three dharmas, attaining the three wisdoms, knowing the three truths — seeing the middle clearly, illuminating both truths at once, fully understood — it is like clouds clearing away to reveal what had been obscured, above made manifest and below made bright at once. Only then can one truly examine what is right and what is wrong, and only then may one rightly give the lion's roar of certainty. A personal note: "According with sentiment" is wholly granting; "according with sentiment-and-wisdom" is half granting and half withholding; "according with wisdom" is wholly withholding. Why is this? When the sage speaks to the ordinary person, saying: "What your mind now conceives is itself the conventional; being able to embody and penetrate the emptiness of the conventional is itself the real" — is this not the mark of wholly granting? "What you now know, through a hundred thousand constructions and distinctions, is all merely the conventional; only what the sage knows is truly the real" — is this not the mark of half granting and half withholding? As for "the two truths: ordinary people, without exception, fail to recognize them; the sage alone is able to know them" — is this not wholly withholding? This explanation is easy to grasp, and so it is recorded here.

Reading note: Zhiyi's own signed aside at the very end — flagged in the source text itself as a personal note, distinct from the received exegesis around it — is worth noticing simply because it is rare: most of this treatise speaks in an impersonal, systematizing voice, and this small moment of "here is my own gloss, and I include it because it's easy to grasp" gives a brief glimpse of the author stepping half out from behind the system. His three-part scheme (wholly granting / half granting, half withholding / wholly withholding) offers a tidy way to hold in mind why the same teaching can sound generous in one sūtra passage and severe in another: it depends on how much of the ordinary person's own understanding is being validated versus corrected. This closes "the purpose of discussing objects"; the far longer second half of this subsection — the convergence and divergence of the two, three, and four truths across the Four Teachings — begins next.

The Essence, Continued: Convergence and Divergence Across the Four Teachings 境界 · 境智離合

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the convergence and divergence of objects and wisdom: first the object, then the wisdom. The manifold sūtras speak of the truths sometimes as four, sometimes three, sometimes two, sometimes one, converging and diverging differently — this shall now be explained in a general way. The Tripiṭaka teaching is a teaching of expedient means, and explains only the two truths. In the bodhisattva's initial mind, the mind takes hold of the real as its object, subduing the four abodes of affliction, causing the very fat of affliction to melt away; through three incalculable eons cultivating the practice of the six pāramitās, the body of merit is made full; over a hundred kalpas planting the causes for the auspicious marks, the five supernormal powers are obtained, and the Dharma-eye attained, illuminating the conventional truth, discriminating the various capacities and dispositions, ripening sentient beings and carrying out the work of a buddha. In the final mind, seated at the seat of enlightenment, the thirty-four mind-moments sever the afflictions of views and habituated thought completely. These thirty-four mind-moments are the eight forbearances, eight wisdoms, nine unobstructed states, and nine liberations, together making up the thirty-four. A sūtra also says: "A single thought contains six hundred arisings and cessations." The Satyasiddhi masters say: "A single thought contains sixty instants." It is simply within this single thought that, entering emptiness from the provisional, the eye of wisdom is obtained, illuminating the real truth, and buddhahood is thereby attained. Having already illuminated the conventional beforehand, and now illuminating the real as well, the two truths are both made manifest at once, differing here from the disciples. The bodhisattva illuminates only the conventional and not the real; the two lesser vehicles illuminate only the real and not the conventional; the Buddha alone is able to do both together, and, moreover, adds the truth of the middle, the ultimate meaning, on top of them. The Tripiṭaka teaching's two truths are already themselves expedient means; upon these two truths, the middle is further added — expedient means built upon expedient means. Illuminating and seeing this further truth, the buddha-eye is further added; knowing this further truth, the wisdom that knows all things in every mode is further added. Divided apart, there are two; taken together, there are three. This is the mark of the convergence and divergence of the two truths and three truths within the Tripiṭaka teaching's own Dharma.

次、三乘人同以無言說道斷煩惱。論諦離合者，俗諦則同，真諦則異。《大論》云：「空有二種：一、但空，二、不但空。」《大經》云：「二乘之人但見於空，不見不空。智者非但見空，能見不空；不空即大涅槃。」二乘但空，智如螢火；菩薩之人，智慧如日。既空異智別，則有兩諦之殊，而今合為一真諦。二乘體假入真，秖入但空，不能從但空入假，無化他之用。菩薩體假入但真，能從但空入假，化度眾生，淨佛國土。上根菩薩體假入真，前入但空，次入不但空，則破無明，見佛性，與前真永別，豈可同為一真諦耶？昔莊嚴家云：「佛果出二諦外。」得此片意，而作義不成。不知佛智別照何境，別斷何惑。若得今意，出外義則成。開善家云：「佛果不出二諦外，不能動異二乘。」作義復不成。若得此意，不出義亦成。古來名此為「風流二諦」，意在此。但空、不但空合時秖是一真諦；離時成兩真諦，與三藏家異。彼三藏第三諦，但有中道名，無別體。眼無別見，智無別知。今則不爾。第三諦亦名「真諦」，亦名「中道第一義諦」，有別體、別見、別知。是為通教二諦、三諦離合之相也。

Next, as for the shared teaching, where the three vehicles alike sever affliction by means of the wordless path: discussing the convergence and divergence of the truths, the conventional truth is the same across the three, but the real truth differs. The Treatise says: "Emptiness is of two kinds: first, mere emptiness; second, not-mere emptiness." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "The people of the two lesser vehicles see only emptiness, and do not see the not-empty. The wise see not merely emptiness, but are able to see the not-empty as well; the not-empty is itself the great nirvāṇa." The two lesser vehicles' mere emptiness, their wisdom is like a firefly's glow; the bodhisattva's own wisdom is like the sun. Since the emptiness differs and the wisdom differs, there ought to be a real distinction of two truths — and yet here they are combined together as a single real truth. The two lesser vehicles, embodying the provisional to enter the real, enter only mere emptiness, and are unable to proceed from mere emptiness into the provisional again, lacking any function for transforming others. The bodhisattva, embodying the provisional to enter what is not merely real, is able to proceed from mere emptiness into the provisional, transforming and delivering sentient beings, purifying the buddha-land. The bodhisattva of superior faculties, embodying the provisional to enter the real, first enters mere emptiness, and next enters what is not merely emptiness, thereby breaking through ignorance and seeing the buddha-nature — this is, in the end, entirely distinct from the "real" spoken of before — how, then, could they possibly be treated as one and the same real truth? Long ago, the Zhuangyan masters said: "The buddha-fruit lies outside the two truths." They grasped a fragment of this sense, but their argument did not succeed, since they could not say what distinct object the Buddha's wisdom illuminates, nor what distinct affliction it severs. Grasping the present sense, the argument that it "lies outside" does succeed. The Kaishan masters said: "The buddha-fruit does not lie outside the two truths, and cannot be moved to differ from the two lesser vehicles." Their argument, too, did not succeed. Grasping this present sense, the argument that it does "not lie outside" also succeeds. In earlier times, this was named the "elegant two truths" — the sense lies exactly here. Mere emptiness and not-mere-emptiness, taken together, form simply the one real truth; taken apart, they form two real truths — and this differs from the Tripiṭaka school's own account. In that Tripiṭaka school, the third truth has only the name "the middle," with no separate essence of its own — the eye has no separate seeing of it, and wisdom no separate knowing of it. Here, this is not so: this third truth is also named "the real truth," and is also named "the truth of the middle, the ultimate meaning" — it has its own separate essence, its own separate seeing, its own separate knowing. This is the mark of the convergence and divergence of the two truths and three truths within the shared teaching.

次、別教明二諦，與前求異。前之真、俗合為別家之俗。俗者，是世界隔別。俗有，真無。凡夫為俗諦所攝，二乘為真諦所攝。既有、無之異，故稱為俗。《勝鬘》名二乘作「空亂意眾生」。《大經》云：「我與彌勒共論世諦，五百聲聞謂說真諦。」若論二諦，俗諦不開；若作三諦，開有為俗，開無為真，對不但空為第一義諦。是為別教離合之相也。次、圓教，但明一實諦。《大經》云：「實是一諦，方便說三。」今亦例此，實是一諦，方便說三。《法華》云：「更以異方便，助顯第一義耳。」是為圓教二諦、三諦、一諦離合之相也。

Next, as for the distinct teaching's own account of the two truths, one must seek what differs from what came before. What was, before, the real and the conventional together are, in the distinct teaching's own house, both now simply the conventional. The conventional here means the world's own manifold differentiation. The conventional is being; the real is non-being. The ordinary being is gathered under the conventional truth; the two lesser vehicles are gathered under the real truth. Since there is this distinction of being and non-being between them, both together are called the "conventional." The Śrīmālā Sūtra names the two lesser vehicles "sentient beings confused in mind regarding emptiness." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "When I and Maitreya together discussed the worldly truth, the five hundred śrāvakas present took it to be a discussion of the real truth." If the two truths alone are discussed, the conventional truth is left unopened; if the three truths are constructed, being is opened out as the conventional, and non-being is opened out as the real, opposed to the not-merely-empty taken as the truth of ultimate meaning. This is the mark of convergence and divergence within the distinct teaching. Next, as for the perfect teaching: it explains only the one real truth. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "In reality it is one truth; as an expedient means, it is spoken of as three." Here, too, it follows this same pattern: in reality it is one truth; as an expedient means, it is spoken of as three. The Lotus Sūtra says: "By means of yet other expedient means, this further helps to reveal the ultimate meaning." This is the mark of convergence and divergence among the two truths, three truths, and one truth within the perfect teaching.

Reading note: This is the treatise's first fully systematic statement of how the Two Truths and Three Truths function differently in each of the Four Teachings — the classification scheme that organizes the rest of this treatise. In the Tripiṭaka teaching, the "third truth" (the middle) is barely a truth at all, just a name tacked onto the other two with no independent eye or wisdom of its own. In the shared teaching, it becomes real, contested territory: the "elegant two truths" debate between the Zhuangyan and Kaishan schools, both partly right and partly wrong on Zhiyi's account, turns on whether the buddha-fruit lies "outside" the two truths — resolved by recognizing that the third truth here does have its own separate object, its own separate wisdom. The distinct teaching redraws the whole map, folding what was previously "real" (the two lesser vehicles' insight) back down into a subordinate "conventional." Only the perfect teaching collapses the whole apparatus into "one real truth," everything else "an expedient means" for revealing it.

Convergence and Divergence of the Four Noble Truths, and of Wisdom 四諦離合 · 智離合

Chinese Source

次、明四諦離合者，前三諦、二諦、一諦皆豎辯；四諦則橫論。則有四種四諦，謂生滅、無生滅、無量、無作等。生滅四諦即是橫開三藏二諦也。無生四諦即是橫開通教二諦也。無量四諦即是橫開別教二諦也。無作四諦即是橫開圓教一實諦也。今將《中觀論》合此四番四諦。論云：「因緣所生法」者，即生滅四諦也；「我說即是空」，即無生四諦也；「亦為是假名」，即無量四諦也；「亦名中道義」，即無作四諦也。二、明智離合者，諸經或說一智，二三四乃至十一智等。若說三智，可用觀三諦；如其增、減，當云何觀？一、智者，經云：「一切諸如來，同共一法身；一心一智慧，力無畏亦然。」唯一佛智即一切種智，一相寂滅相；種種行類、相貌皆知，名「一切種智」。此智觀三諦者，若言「一相寂滅相」，即是觀於中道；若言「種種行類、相貌皆知」者，即是雙照二諦也。若二智者，所謂權實。權即一切智、道種智，觀於有、無兩諦也。實即一切種智，觀於中道諦也。三智觀三諦，可解，不說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the convergence and divergence of the Four Noble Truths: the three truths, two truths, and one truth discussed above are all discussed vertically; the Four Noble Truths, by contrast, are discussed horizontally. There are, accordingly, four kinds of Four Noble Truths: generation-and-extinction, no generation, the immeasurable, and the unconditioned. The Four Noble Truths of generation-and-extinction are simply the Tripiṭaka teaching's own two truths opened out horizontally. The Four Noble Truths of no generation are simply the shared teaching's own two truths opened out horizontally. The Four Noble Truths of the immeasurable are simply the distinct teaching's own two truths opened out horizontally. The Four Noble Truths of the unconditioned are simply the perfect teaching's own one real truth opened out horizontally. Here, the Middle Treatise is brought in to match these four kinds of Four Noble Truths. The Treatise says: "whatever arises through causes and conditions" — this is the Four Noble Truths of generation-and-extinction; "this I declare to be empty" — this is the Four Noble Truths of no generation; "it is also called a provisional name" — this is the Four Noble Truths of the immeasurable; "it is also the meaning of the middle way" — this is the Four Noble Truths of the unconditioned. Second, clarifying the convergence and divergence of wisdom: the various sūtras sometimes speak of a single wisdom, sometimes two, three, four, on up to eleven kinds of wisdom. If three wisdoms are spoken of, they can be used to contemplate the three truths — but what of when the number increases or decreases? As for a single wisdom: a sūtra says: "Every one of the Tathāgatas shares alike a single Dharma-body; a single mind, a single wisdom, and their powers and fearlessnesses are exactly the same." This one, single buddha-wisdom is itself the wisdom that knows all things in every mode — one mark, the mark of quiescent extinction, and yet knowing every kind of conduct, class, and appearance — this is named "the wisdom that knows all things in every mode." As for how this wisdom contemplates the three truths: when it is said "one mark, the mark of quiescent extinction," this is contemplating the middle way; when it is said "knowing every kind of conduct, class, and appearance," this is illuminating both truths at once. As for two wisdoms: these are the provisional and the real. The provisional is the wisdom that knows all things generally and the wisdom of the seeds of the path, contemplating the two truths of being and non-being. The real is the wisdom that knows all things in every mode, contemplating the truth of the middle. Three wisdoms contemplating the three truths may be understood without further explanation.

四智者，如《大品》明道慧、道種慧、一切智、一切種智。《釋論》解此有多種，或因中但有理體，名為道慧、道種慧；果上事理皆滿，名一切智、一切種智。或言因中權實，故言道慧、道種慧；入空為實慧，入假為權慧。或言果上權、實，故言一切智、一切種智。直緣中道，名一切智；雙照二諦，名一切種智。或言因中總別，果上總別。或言道慧、道種慧，是單明權實；一切智、一切種智，是複明權實。如是等種種釋四智，四智秖是照三諦也。若經中有明五諦，六七八九乃至無量者，但得此意釋之，使入三諦也。十一智者，世智、他心智，兩種照俗諦；八智，觀真諦；如實智，觀中道。是名「智有離合而三諦不動」。復次，智、諦俱開者，隨其多少自相攝，如三諦即有三智，二諦即有二智，此義可解。又智、諦俱不開者，且據一諦一智，不增不減，此亦可解。若智雖開合，終是實智，能顯實體也。

As for four wisdoms: such as the Larger Sūtra's own account of the wisdom of the path, the wisdom of the seeds of the path, the wisdom that knows all things generally, and the wisdom that knows all things in every mode. The Treatise explains this in several ways. In one account: within the cause, there is only the substance of principle, named the wisdom of the path and the wisdom of the seeds of the path; upon the fruit, both phenomenon and principle are complete, named the wisdom that knows all things generally and the wisdom that knows all things in every mode. In another account: within the cause there is the provisional and the real, hence "the wisdom of the path" and "the wisdom of the seeds of the path" are spoken of — entering emptiness is the real wisdom, entering the provisional is the provisional wisdom. In another account: upon the fruit there is the provisional and the real, hence "the wisdom that knows all things generally" and "the wisdom that knows all things in every mode" are spoken of — taking the middle way directly as one's object is named "the wisdom that knows all things generally"; illuminating both truths at once is named "the wisdom that knows all things in every mode." In another account: within the cause there is the general and the particular, and upon the fruit there is likewise the general and the particular. In another account: the wisdom of the path and the wisdom of the seeds of the path singly clarify the provisional and the real; the wisdom that knows all things generally and the wisdom that knows all things in every mode doubly clarify the provisional and the real. Explanations of the four wisdoms of this and every such kind are simply illuminating the three truths. Where the sūtras speak of five truths, or six, seven, eight, nine, on up to measureless numbers of truths, one need only grasp this same sense to explain them, bringing them all into the three truths. As for the eleven wisdoms: worldly wisdom and the wisdom that knows others' minds, these two illuminate the conventional truth; the eight wisdoms contemplate the real truth; the wisdom that knows things exactly as they are contemplates the middle way. This is what is meant by "wisdom having its own convergences and divergences, while the three truths themselves remain unmoved." Furthermore, where both wisdom and truth are opened out together, each corresponds to the other according to however many there are — as when the three truths correspond to three wisdoms, and the two truths to two wisdoms — this sense may be understood. And where both wisdom and truth are left unopened, resting simply on one truth and one wisdom, neither increased nor decreased, this too may be understood. Whether wisdom is opened out or gathered together, in the end it is always the same real wisdom, able to reveal the real essence.

Reading note: The fourfold Four Noble Truths — already the centerpiece of fascicle 1's own treatment of giving rise to the bodhi-mind — reappear here explicitly identified as the horizontal counterpart to the two/three/one truths' vertical scheme, and matched, clause by clause, to the same "Three Truths verse" from the Middle Treatise already cited twice in fascicle 1. Readers of that fascicle will recognize the same four terms (generation-and-extinction, no generation, the immeasurable, the unconditioned) now given their formal place within the Four Teachings apparatus this fascicle has been building. Whatever the count — one wisdom, two, three, four, eleven, or any number in between — the governing principle stays fixed: "wisdom has its own convergences and divergences, while the three truths themselves remain unmoved," meaning the scheme can always be run in reverse to recover the same underlying three (or one).

Truth and Wisdom Combined, and Using the Distinct Teaching to Graft onto the Shared 諦智合辯 · 以別接通

Chinese Source

次、約諦、智合辯者，三藏真諦發一眼一智；俗諦發一眼一智；兩諦共發一眼一智。慧眼、一切智，緣真諦；法眼、道種智，緣俗諦；佛眼、一切種智，共緣真俗兩諦。不得道雙照，秖得道前後共照耳。通教，真諦發二眼二智；俗諦發一眼一智。一切智、一切種智共緣真諦；道種智緣俗諦。若作別接通者，俗諦發一眼一智；真諦發一眼一智；開真出中發一眼一智。智緣諦，亦如是。別教三諦，一一諦各發一眼一智。智緣諦，亦如是。若別教作二諦者，俗中空，發一眼一智；俗中有，發一眼一智；真諦，發一眼一智。智緣諦，亦如是。圓教者，一實諦發三眼三智；智緣諦，亦如是。問：云何以別接通？答：初空假二觀破真俗上惑盡，方聞中道；仍須修觀破無明，能八相作佛。此佛是果，仍前二觀為因，故言以別接通耳。不以此佛果接三阿僧祇、百劫種相之因，故不接三藏；不將此果接十地之因，故不接別；不將此果接十住斷無明，故不接圓。唯得以別接通，其義如此。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, discussing truth and wisdom combined together: in the Tripiṭaka teaching, the real truth brings forth one eye and one wisdom; the conventional truth brings forth one eye and one wisdom; the two truths together bring forth one eye and one wisdom. The eye of wisdom and the wisdom that knows all things generally take the real truth as their object; the Dharma-eye and the wisdom of the seeds of the path take the conventional truth as their object; the buddha-eye and the wisdom that knows all things in every mode take both the real and conventional truths together as their object. One cannot say they illuminate both at once — only that they illuminate them together, one after the other. In the shared teaching, the real truth brings forth two eyes and two wisdoms; the conventional truth brings forth one eye and one wisdom. The wisdom that knows all things generally and the wisdom that knows all things in every mode together take the real truth as their object; the wisdom of the seeds of the path takes the conventional truth as its object. If the distinct teaching is used to graft onto the shared teaching, the conventional truth brings forth one eye and one wisdom; the real truth brings forth one eye and one wisdom; and opening the real to reveal the middle brings forth a further one eye and one wisdom. Wisdom taking the truth as its object follows the same pattern. In the distinct teaching's own three truths, each single truth brings forth its own one eye and one wisdom; wisdom taking the truth as its object follows the same pattern. If the distinct teaching is instead constructed as two truths, emptiness within the conventional brings forth one eye and one wisdom; being within the conventional brings forth one eye and one wisdom; the real truth brings forth one eye and one wisdom; wisdom taking the truth as its object follows the same pattern. In the perfect teaching, the one real truth brings forth three eyes and three wisdoms; wisdom taking the truth as its object follows the same pattern. Question: What is meant by "using the distinct teaching to graft onto the shared teaching"? Answer: The first two contemplations, of emptiness and of the provisional, break through every affliction resting upon the real and conventional truths completely, and only then is the middle way heard of at all; one must still further cultivate contemplation to break through ignorance, and thereby be able to accomplish buddhahood through the eight aspects. This buddhahood is the fruit, with the two preceding contemplations serving as its cause — hence this is called "using the distinct teaching to graft onto the shared teaching." This buddha-fruit is not grafted onto the cause of the three incalculable eons and the hundred kalpas of planting the marks, and so it is not grafted onto the Tripiṭaka teaching; nor is this fruit grafted onto the cause of the ten grounds, and so it is not grafted onto the distinct teaching itself; nor is this fruit grafted onto the ten abidings' own severing of ignorance, and so it is not grafted onto the perfect teaching. It can only be that the distinct teaching is grafted onto the shared teaching — the sense of this is exactly as given.

Reading note: "Using the distinct teaching to graft onto the shared teaching" (以別接通) is one of Tiantai scholasticism's more technical doctrines, worth flagging even in summary: it addresses practitioners of the shared teaching whose own two contemplations (of emptiness, then of the provisional) have already exhausted what that teaching alone can offer, yet whose capacity is sharp enough to continue directly into the distinct teaching's contemplation of the middle, without needing to restart within the distinct teaching's own lengthy causal path (the ten grounds). It is a doctrine about second wind — for practitioners who outgrow their starting teaching's own resources partway through, without discarding the progress already made.

Gain and Loss 體相 · 得失

Chinese Source

四、明得失者，「失」即思議；「得」即不思議也。若言智由心生，自然照境。如炬照物，若照、未照，此物本有；若觀、不觀，境自天然，諦智不相由藉。若言智不自智，由境故智；境不自境，由智故境。如長短相待。此是相由而有。若言境不自境，亦不由智故境，境智因緣故境；智亦例然。此是共合得名。若言皆不如上三種，但自然而爾，即是無因境智。此四解皆有過。所以者何？有四取則有依倚；依倚則是非；是非則愛恚；愛恚生一切煩惱；煩惱生故，戲論諍競生；諍競生故，起身口意業；業生故，輪迴苦海，無解脫期。當知四取是生死本，故龍樹伐之。諸法不自生，那得自境智？無他生，那得相由境智？無共生，那得因緣境智？無無因生，那得自然境智？若執四見著，愚惑紛綸，何謂為智？今以不自生等破四性；性破故，無依倚，乃至無業、苦等；清淨心常一，則能見般若。以是義故，自境智苦集不生，即是生生不可說。故身子默然。乃至無因境智苦集不生，即是不生不生不可說。故《淨名》杜口，言語道斷，心行處滅。雖不可說，有四悉檀因緣故，亦可得說。或說自生境智，乃至或說無因境智。雖作四說，性執久破，如前。但有名字，名字無性，無性之字是字不住，亦不不住，是為不可思議。故《金光明》云：「不可思議智境，不可思議智照。」即此意也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, clarifying gain and loss: "loss" means what is still conceivable; "gain" means the inconceivable. Suppose one says wisdom arises from mind of itself, illuminating its object spontaneously — like a torch illuminating an object: whether illuminated or not yet illuminated, the object exists there already of itself; whether contemplated or not contemplated, the object stands there of its own natural accord, truth and wisdom depending on nothing at all outside themselves. Or suppose one says wisdom is not wisdom of itself, but is wisdom because of its object; and the object is not the object of itself, but is the object because of wisdom — like "long" and "short," each existing only relative to the other. This is "having existence through mutual dependence." Or suppose one says the object is not the object of itself, nor is it the object because of wisdom, but is the object through the causal conditions of both object and wisdom together; and wisdom, likewise, in exactly the same way. This is "taking a name through combination." Or suppose one says it is none of the above three, but simply so of itself, spontaneously — this is "object and wisdom without any cause." Every one of these four explanations has its own fault. Why is this? Wherever there is grasping at one of these four, there is dependence on something else; wherever there is dependence, there is right-and-wrong; wherever there is right-and-wrong, there is craving and aversion; craving and aversion give rise to every affliction; once affliction arises, idle theorizing and contentious dispute arise; once dispute arises, karmic action of body, speech, and mind arises; once karma arises, one turns in the ocean of suffering, with no time of liberation in sight. One should know that these four kinds of grasping are the very root of birth-and-death — hence Nāgārjuna cuts them down. No dharma arises from itself — how, then, could there be object and wisdom arising from themselves? None arises from another — how, then, could there be object and wisdom arising through mutual dependence? None arises from both together — how, then, could there be object and wisdom arising through causal combination? None arises without cause — how, then, could there be object and wisdom arising spontaneously, without any cause? If one clings to any of these four views, tangled in confusion and delusion, what could this possibly be called "wisdom"? Here, by means of "not arising from itself" and the rest, the four natures are refuted; and once these natures are refuted, there is no dependence at all, down to no karma, no suffering, and the rest — "the pure mind, ever one, is then able to see prajñā." For this reason, object-and-wisdom-from-itself, suffering and its accumulation, do not arise — this is "birth-of-birth, that cannot be spoken of" — hence Śāriputra fell silent. And on to object-and-wisdom-without-cause, suffering and its accumulation likewise do not arise — this is "no-birth-of-no-birth, that cannot be spoken of" — hence Vimalakīrti shut his mouth, the path of language cut off, the place where the mind moves extinguished. Though it cannot be spoken of, given the causal conditions of the four siddhāntas, it can indeed be spoken of nonetheless. Sometimes object-and-wisdom-from-itself is spoken of, and so on down to object-and-wisdom-without-cause being spoken of. Though these four accounts are given, the clinging to any fixed nature has long since been refuted, exactly as before. There is only the name, and the name has no fixed nature; and a name without fixed nature neither abides nor "does not abide" — this is what is meant by the inconceivable. Hence the Sūtra of Golden Light says: "The inconceivable object of wisdom, the inconceivable illumination of wisdom" — this is exactly that same sense.

若破四性境智，此名「實慧」。若四悉檀赴緣說四境智者，此名「權慧」。如是境智，凡夫兩失，二乘一得一失，菩薩兩得。何以故？凡夫有四性，自行為失；無四悉檀，化他為失。二乘破四性入第一義，自行為得；不度眾生，化他為失。菩薩具足，是故兩得。又凡夫兩失，是思議失；二乘一得一失，俱是思議；菩薩兩得，俱不思議。此約通教辯得失。若別望通，通教兩得，俱是思議；別教兩得，俱不思議。若圓望別，別教教、道兩得，俱是思議。何以故？教門方便，或言無明生一切法；或言法性生一切法；或言緣修顯真修；或言真自顯。執此還成性過，墮可思議中也。若證道者，即不思議也。若圓教教、證，俱不思議。何故爾？至理無說，為緣四說；但有假名，假名之名，名即無生。故教、證俱不可思議也。無思無念，故無依倚、戲論、結業，無業故無生死，是名自行為得。得於實體，能以不可說說，化導眾生，令出生死，得於實體，是為自、他俱得體也。

Refuting the four fixed natures of object and wisdom is named "the real wisdom." Speaking of object and wisdom in these four ways, addressing different occasions by way of the four siddhāntas, is named "the provisional wisdom." As for object and wisdom of this kind: the ordinary being fails on both counts; the two lesser vehicles gain on one count and fail on the other; the bodhisattva gains on both counts. Why is this? The ordinary being holds to the four fixed natures — a failure in practice for oneself; and lacks the four siddhāntas — a failure in transforming others. The two lesser vehicles refute the four fixed natures and enter ultimate meaning — a gain in practice for oneself; yet fail to deliver sentient beings — a failure in transforming others. The bodhisattva is complete in both — hence gaining on both counts. Moreover, the ordinary being's double failure is a conceivable failure; the two lesser vehicles' gain-and-failure are both alike conceivable; the bodhisattva's double gain is, in both respects, inconceivable. This discusses gain and loss in terms of the shared teaching. Viewed from the distinct teaching against the shared: the shared teaching's double gain is, in both respects, still conceivable; the distinct teaching's double gain is, in both respects, inconceivable. Viewed from the perfect teaching against the distinct: the distinct teaching's own double gain, in both its teaching and its path, is, in both respects, still conceivable. Why is this? The teaching's own expedient gateway: sometimes it is said that ignorance gives rise to every dharma; sometimes that the Dharma-nature gives rise to every dharma; sometimes that conditioned cultivation reveals true cultivation; sometimes that the true reveals itself. Clinging to any of these still amounts to the fault of fixed nature, falling back into the conceivable. Only the path of realization itself is inconceivable. In the perfect teaching, both its teaching and its realization are, in both respects, inconceivable. Why is this so? Ultimate principle has nothing to be said of it at all, and is spoken of only for the sake of some occasion, in these four ways; there is only the provisional name, and the name of that provisional name is itself, in its very naming, unborn. Hence both the perfect teaching's teaching and its realization are alike inconceivable. Without thought, without conception, there is no dependence, no idle theorizing, no binding karma; and, without karma, no birth-and-death — this is what is meant by "gain in practice for oneself." Having attained the real essence, one is able, by means of the inexpressible, still to express it, guiding and transforming sentient beings, causing them to escape birth-and-death, they too attaining the real essence — this is "both self and other alike attaining the essence."

Reading note: "Gain and loss" closes topic three by running the tetralemma one final time — now against object-and-wisdom themselves, refusing all four ways they might be said to arise (from itself, from another, from both, from neither) — and then re-grading every level of the Four Teachings by how completely each still depends on some fixed account of how understanding relates to its object. Even the distinct teaching's own doctrine, on close inspection, "still amounts to the fault of fixed nature" at the level of its teaching, however inconceivable its actual realization; only the perfect teaching is inconceivable in both respects at once, teaching and realization together. This closes topic three, the essence and its marks, in its entirety — the treatise now turns to topic four, the scope of the teachings it gathers in.

IV. The Scope of the Teachings It Gathers In 攝法

Chinese Source

第四、明攝法者，疑者謂：「止觀名略，攝法不周。」今則不然。止觀總持，遍收諸法。何者？止能寂諸法，如灸病得穴，眾患皆除；觀能照理，如得珠王，眾寶皆獲，具足一切佛法。《大品》有百二十條及一切法，皆言當學般若。般若秖是觀智，觀智已攝一切法。又止是王三昧，一切三昧悉入其中。今更廣論攝法，即為六意：一、攝一切理，二、攝一切惑，三、攝一切智，四、攝一切行，五、攝一切位，六、攝一切教。此六次第者，有佛無佛，理性常住；由迷理故，起生死惑；順理而觀，是故論智；解故立行；由行故證位；位滿故教他。事理、解行、因果、自他等次第，皆止觀攝盡也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, clarifying the scope of the teachings gathered in: a doubter might say, "The name 'calming-and-contemplation' is brief — the scope of teachings it gathers in cannot be complete." This is not so. Calming-and-contemplation is a total retention, gathering in every dharma without exception. Why is this? Calming is able to still every dharma, like finding the exact point to cauterize an illness, so that every affliction is removed at once; contemplation is able to illuminate principle, like obtaining the king of jewels, so that every treasure is thereby gained, complete with the whole of the Buddhadharma. The Larger Sūtra names one hundred and twenty items, and every dharma besides, all of which it says one should study through prajñā. Prajñā is simply the wisdom of contemplation, and the wisdom of contemplation already gathers in every dharma. Moreover, calming is the king of samādhis, and every samādhi enters entirely within it. Here, the scope of the teachings gathered in is discussed further, at greater length, under six senses: first, gathering in every principle; second, gathering in every affliction; third, gathering in every wisdom; fourth, gathering in every practice; fifth, gathering in every stage; sixth, gathering in every teaching. As for the sequence of these six: whether or not a buddha appears in the world, the nature of principle abides forever; because one is deluded about principle, the affliction of birth-and-death arises; contemplating in accordance with principle, wisdom is thereby discussed; through understanding, practice is established; through practice, stage is realized; once the stage is fulfilled, others are taught. The sequence of phenomenon and principle, understanding and practice, cause and fruit, self and other, is entirely gathered in by calming-and-contemplation.

1–2. Gathering in Every Principle and Every Affliction 攝一切理 · 攝一切惑

一、以三止三觀攝一切理者，理是諦法，如上開合，偏圓不同；權實之外，更無別理。除摩黎山，餘無栴檀；若更有者，即是妄語。既以止觀顯體，即攝一切理也。二、止觀攝一切惑者，以迷諦故，起生死惑。迷，即無明。若迷權理，則有界內相應、獨頭等無明。與見思諸使合者，名「相應」；不相應者名「獨頭」。是事不知，故起貪。不知者是「無明」；起貪是「行」；貪者是「識」；識共四陰起是「名色」；色動諸根是「六入」；六入所著是「觸」；觸隨順塵是「受」；受所喜樂是「愛」；愛俱生纏是「取」；造當來生業是「有」；未來陰起是「生」；陰熟是「老」；捨陰是「死」。是十二輪更互為因果：煩惱通業；業通苦；苦通煩惱，故名「三道」。《成論》云：「前行後三中，後行前七中。七是業，復是道，能通後世；後三非業，而能通七，亦得是道。」經中亦呼為「十二牽連」、「十二輪」。束縛不窮，故名為「輪」；三世間隔，故名「分段」。覆真諦理，不得解脫，此即是病。說病即知藥，藥即從假入空止觀。觀藥即知病，故此惑為入空止觀所攝也。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, gathering in every principle by means of the three kinds of calming and three kinds of contemplation: principle is the dharma of the truths, opened out and gathered together as explained above, partial and complete differing accordingly; beyond the provisional and the real, there is no other, separate principle at all. Apart from Mount Malaya, no other sandalwood grows; were there any elsewhere, this would simply be a false statement. Since calming-and-contemplation has already been used to reveal the essence, it thereby gathers in every principle. Second, calming-and-contemplation gathering in every affliction: because one is deluded about the truths, the affliction of birth-and-death arises. This delusion is ignorance itself. If one is deluded about the provisional principle, there is ignorance both associated and solitary, within the three realms. That which combines with the afflictions of views and habituated thought is named "associated"; that which does not combine with them is named "solitary." Not knowing this matter, craving arises: not-knowing is "ignorance"; the arising of craving is "formations"; the craver is "consciousness"; consciousness arising together with the four remaining aggregates is "name-and-form"; form stirring the various faculties is "the six sense-bases"; what the six sense-bases become attached to is "contact"; contact following after its sense-object is "sensation"; what sensation delights in is "craving"; craving arising together with entanglement is "grasping"; the creation of karma for a life yet to come is "becoming"; the arising of the aggregates in the future is "birth"; the ripening of the aggregates is "old age"; the relinquishing of the aggregates is "death." These twelve links turn, each in turn cause and effect of the next: affliction leads to karma; karma leads to suffering; suffering leads back to affliction — hence they are named "the three paths." The Satyasiddhi says: "The former link is contained within the latter three; the latter is contained within the first seven. The seven are karma, and are also the path, able to carry one through to the next life; the latter three are not karma, yet are able to carry one through the seven, and so may also be called the path." The sūtras also call this "the twelve interlinked connections," "the twelve wheels." Binding without end, hence named "wheel"; the three periods of time kept apart from each other, hence named "birth-and-death of limited span." Covering over the truth of the real, unable to attain liberation — this is the illness itself. To speak of the illness is already to know the medicine; the medicine is precisely the calming-and-contemplation of entering emptiness from the provisional. To contemplate the medicine is already to know the illness — hence this affliction is gathered in by the calming-and-contemplation that enters emptiness.

若迷實理，則有界外相應、獨頭等無明。所以者何？界內雖斷相應、獨頭，而習氣猶在。小乘中，習非正使；大乘實說，習即別惑，是界外無明也。故《寶性論》云：「二乘之人，雖有無常、苦、空、無我等對治，於佛法身猶是顛倒。」顛倒即是「無明」獨頭；無漏智業為「行」；三種意生身，亦是五種意生身——意即是「識」；身即「名色」、「六入」、「觸」、「受」；無明細惑、戲論未究竟滅，即是「愛」、「取」；煩惱染、業染、生染未究竟，即是「有」；三種意因移，即是「生」；其果變易，即是「老死」。束此十二，是無漏界中四種障，謂：緣、相、生、壞。緣即煩惱道；相即業道；生、壞即苦道。故知界外有十二因緣。所以者何？降佛已下，皆有無明；無明潤業；業既被潤，那得無苦？此十二輪雖不退界墮下，不妨從無明輪至老死，從老死輪至無明。障於實理，良由此惑。此惑為「入假」、「入中」兩觀所治。更料簡之。何以故？三種意生身，凡有多種。若析、體二乘及通菩薩等，先斷界內惑盡，而未曾修習假、中者，生於界外，界外惑全未被伏，其根則鈍。若於彼習觀時，必須次第歷劫修行，學恒沙佛法，先破塵沙。塵沙雖不潤生，能障化道，故須前斷。斷此惑者，止是調心方便，伏界外惑；進斷三道，相應、獨頭，枝本皆去。故知假觀正攝得塵沙，亦攝得無明。若別圓二人，通惑先盡，別惑被伏，生彼界者神根即利；但修中觀，治彼三道。從於初地乃至後地，地地中皆有三道。地地無明分分滅，業滅、苦滅。地地相應去時，獨頭亦去。地地雖有智，智與無明雜；雜故，亦得呼為智障，障上分智故。唯佛心中無無明，則煩惱道盡；煩惱道盡，故業盡；業盡，故苦盡。三道究竟，唯在如來。是故中觀攝得界外惑也。

If one is deluded about the real principle, there is ignorance both associated and solitary, beyond the three realms. Why is this? Though the associated and solitary ignorance within the three realms is severed, its habituated trace still remains. In the Hīnayāna, this habituated trace is not counted as an active affliction proper; in the Mahāyāna's own true account, this habituated trace is itself a distinct affliction — this is ignorance beyond the three realms. Hence the Ratnagotravibhāga says: "The people of the two lesser vehicles, though they possess antidotes such as impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, remain, with regard to the Buddha's own Dharma-body, still inverted." This inversion is itself "ignorance," solitary; uncontaminated wisdom-karma is "formations"; the three kinds of mind-made body, also called the five kinds of mind-made body — mind itself is "consciousness"; the body is "name-and-form," "the six sense-bases," "contact," "sensation"; the subtle affliction of ignorance, its idle theorizing not yet ultimately extinguished, is itself "craving" and "grasping"; the defilement of affliction, the defilement of karma, and the defilement of birth, not yet ultimately ended, are "becoming"; the shifting of the three kinds of mental cause is "birth"; the transformation of its fruit is "old age and death." Gathering these twelve together, they are the four obstructions within the uncontaminated realm, namely: condition, mark, generation, and dissolution. Condition is the path of affliction; mark is the path of karma; generation and dissolution are the path of suffering. Hence it is known that the twelve links of dependent origination exist beyond the three realms as well. Why is this? From below the level of the Buddha, ignorance is present in every case; ignorance moistens karma; and once karma is thus moistened, how could suffering fail to follow? Though these twelve wheels do not regress and fall back into the three realms, this does not prevent turning from the wheel of ignorance to old-age-and-death, and from the wheel of old-age-and-death back to ignorance. What obstructs the real principle is precisely this affliction. This affliction is treated by the two contemplations of entering the provisional and entering the middle. Let this be further clarified. Why is this? The three kinds of mind-made body are, in general, of many varieties. If the analytical and embodying two lesser vehicles, and the bodhisattva of the shared teaching, first sever every affliction within the three realms completely, without ever having cultivated the provisional or the middle, and are then born beyond the three realms, the affliction beyond the three realms remains entirely unsubdued, and their own faculties are dull. Should they cultivate contemplation at that point, they must necessarily proceed sequentially, cultivating practice across many kalpas, studying the Buddhadharma numerous as the sands of the Ganges, first breaking through the dust-and-sand afflictions. Though the dust-and-sand afflictions do not themselves moisten karmic birth, they are able to obstruct the path of transforming others, and so must be severed first. Severing this affliction is merely the expedient means of regulating the mind, subduing the affliction beyond the three realms; advancing further, one severs the three paths, both associated and solitary, root and branch alike removed. Hence it is known that the contemplation entering the provisional properly gathers in the dust-and-sand afflictions, and gathers in ignorance as well. If, on the other hand, practitioners of the distinct and perfect teachings first exhaust the afflictions shared with the other vehicles and subdue the distinct affliction as well, then, born within that realm, their spiritual faculties are already sharp; they need only cultivate the contemplation of the middle to treat those same three paths. From the first ground on through the later grounds, each and every ground contains the three paths within it. At each and every ground, ignorance is extinguished portion by portion, karma extinguished, suffering extinguished. As the associated affliction departs at each ground in turn, the solitary affliction departs along with it. At each and every ground, though there is wisdom, that wisdom remains mixed together with ignorance; and, being mixed, it too may be called an obstruction of wisdom, since it obstructs the higher portion of wisdom still to come. Only within the Buddha's own mind is there no ignorance at all — hence the path of affliction is exhausted; the path of affliction being exhausted, karma is exhausted; karma being exhausted, suffering is exhausted. The ultimate end of the three paths lies with the Tathāgata alone. This is why the contemplation of the middle gathers in the affliction beyond the three realms.

Reading note: "Gathering in every affliction" doubles the classical twelve links of dependent origination: the familiar version, operating within the three realms and ending in the "birth-and-death of limited span" every Buddhist knows, is matched by a second, subtler twelve links operating beyond the three realms, ending instead in a variety Zhiyi calls "the birth-and-death of unlimited transformation" — the residual, barely perceptible ignorance the Hīnayāna doesn't even count as a real affliction, which the Mahāyāna insists must still be uprooted before buddhahood. This is the same distinction fascicle 1 introduced as the bodhisattva's "first no birth-and-death of limited span, next no birth-and-death of unlimited transformation" — here given its full twelvefold structure, matched to the two remaining contemplations (entering the provisional treats the dust-and-sand afflictions; entering the middle treats ignorance itself, ground by ground, all the way to buddhahood).

Third, calming-and-contemplation gathering in every wisdom: the various convergences and divergences of wisdom have already been explained above; the three contemplations, applied to gather them in, leave nothing whatsoever out. Worldly wisdom, which does not illuminate principle, has already been gathered in among the eleven wisdoms. Were the twenty wisdoms explained at length, these too would be gathered in by the three contemplations. Fourth, calming-and-contemplation gathering in every practice: the wisdom discussed above is understanding, and understanding without practice, in the end, arrives nowhere. Practice is of two kinds: wisdom-practice and auxiliary practice. This holds from the Tripiṭaka teaching's own wisdom-practice and auxiliary practice, on up to the perfect teaching's wisdom-practice and auxiliary practice. Wisdom-practice is the primary practice; auxiliary practice is the supporting practice. Vipaśyanā is able to break through affliction, yet still requires the power of śamatha to assist correct knowledge and vision. The primary and supporting practices turn together, following wisdom, as the feet follow the eyes. In the Tripiṭaka teaching, contemplating impermanence and analysis are the wisdom-practice; contemplating impurity, loving-kindness, and the like are the auxiliary practice — these two practices follow analytical wisdom into emptiness. In the shared teaching, embodying dharmas as like illusion and transformation is the wisdom-practice; going through every dharma with counting the breath, the foundations of mindfulness, and calming-and-contemplation taking phenomena as their object is the auxiliary practice — these two practices follow the wisdom that embodies dharmas into emptiness. For the sake of transforming sentient beings, cultivating the wisdom of the seeds of the path, taking conventional principle as one's object belongs to wisdom-practice; taking conventional phenomena as one's object belongs to auxiliary practice — these two practices follow the wisdom of the seeds of the path into the provisional. If the middle way takes the true mark of reality as its object, the one path pure and undefiled, this is wisdom-practice; going through every dharma-gate, every pāramitā, all of it the Mahāyāna — the twelve links of dependent origination themselves the buddha-nature, the foundations of mindfulness themselves sitting at the seat of enlightenment, and so forth — this is auxiliary practice. These two practices follow the wisdom of the middle into the true mark of reality.

復次，根本四禪，定慧等故，兩攝。欲界，定少慧多，觀攝。中間亦爾。四空，定多慧少，止攝。四無量心：前三心，觀攝；捨心，止攝。九想、八念、十想，觀攝。八背捨：前三背捨，觀攝；後五，止攝。九次第定、師子奮迅、超越等，是止攝。四念處是慧性，觀攝。若作四意止說者，作心記錄不淨等，此屬止攝；而終是觀為主。四正勤，為成念處，一往觀攝。若兩惡不生，止攝；兩善為生，觀攝。四如意足，從四因緣得定，即果為名，止攝。五根：信、進、慧三根，觀攝；念、定，止攝。又，信、念兩屬。五力亦如是。七覺分：擇法、喜、進等，觀攝；除捨、定，止攝；念，通兩處。八正：正見、正思惟，觀攝；正業、正語、正命，屬戒，即止攝；正念、正定、正精進，止攝。四諦：三諦是有為行，屬觀門；滅諦是無為行，屬止門。十六行，皆是觀門。四弘誓，依四諦起，如彼。十八不共法：三業，隨智慧行，觀攝；三無失，止攝；知三世，觀攝；餘可知。四無畏者：一切智無畏，屬觀攝；漏盡，止攝；至處道，觀攝；障道，止攝。三三昧門，止攝。三解脫門，觀攝。六度者：前三是功德，止攝；後三是智慧，觀攝。又，五度功德，止攝；般若，觀攝。又，六度皆是功德莊嚴，止攝。乃至九種大禪、百八三昧，皆屬止攝。十八空、十喻、五百陀羅尼，皆觀攝。如是等一切慧行、行行，無不為止觀所攝。當知止觀名略，攝義則廣。

The four formless states, with much concentration and little wisdom, are gathered under calming. Of the four immeasurable minds, the first three are gathered under contemplation, and equanimity under calming. The nine contemplations, eight recollections, and ten contemplations are gathered under contemplation. Of the eight liberations, the first three are gathered under contemplation, and the last five under calming. The nine sequential concentrations, the lion's-sport samādhi, the transcendence samādhi, and the rest are gathered under calming. The four foundations of mindfulness, being of the nature of wisdom, are gathered under contemplation; if spoken of as the four foundations of stopping, mentally noting and recording impurity and the like belongs to calming, though contemplation still remains the governing principle throughout. The four right efforts, serving to accomplish the foundations of mindfulness, are, broadly, gathered under contemplation; where the two unwholesome states are prevented from arising, this is gathered under calming, and where the two wholesome states are made to arise, this is gathered under contemplation. The four bases of supernormal power, attaining concentration through four causal conditions, are named from their fruit, and gathered under calming. Of the five faculties, faith, diligence, and wisdom are gathered under contemplation, and mindfulness and concentration under calming — and faith and mindfulness each belong to both. The five powers are exactly the same. Of the eightfold path, correct view and correct thought are gathered under contemplation; correct action, correct speech, and correct livelihood, belonging to moral discipline, are gathered under calming; correct mindfulness, correct concentration, and correct diligence are gathered under calming. Of the six pāramitās, the first three are matters of merit, gathered under calming; the last three are matters of wisdom, gathered under contemplation. The eighteen kinds of emptiness, the ten similes, and the five hundred dhāraṇīs are all gathered under contemplation. Every wisdom-practice and auxiliary practice of this and every such kind is, without exception, gathered in by calming-and-contemplation. One should know, then, that though the name "calming-and-contemplation" is brief, the meaning it gathers in is vast.

Reading note: This catalog — sorting virtually the entire received vocabulary of Buddhist practice (the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, the six pāramitās, the eighteen unshared qualities, the nine kinds of great dhyāna, and more) into either "calming" or "contemplation" — is less an argument than a demonstration: page after page, every named practice in the tradition finds a place, and none is left over. The governing rule, stated early here and worth keeping in mind while reading the list, is that concentration-heavy practices are gathered under calming and wisdom-heavy practices under contemplation, "as the feet follow the eyes" — auxiliary practice supporting, but never substituting for, the primary wisdom-practice that actually does the seeing.

Fifth, gathering in every stage: some might say, "If the first ground is itself the second ground, and the second ground is itself the third, then, within quiescent extinction and suchness itself, what stages could there possibly be?" — in that sense, there is indeed no sequence of stages at all. And yet, throughout the Mahāyāna sūtras, every kind of stage and ground is spoken of everywhere. This is precisely because, though no-arising and no-ceasing, correct wisdom itself has nothing to be attained, it is nonetheless able to treat affliction, karma, and suffering. Once the three paths are purified, distinctions can indeed still be found even within the unconditioned dharma — what objection, then, could there be to speaking of sequential stages? If, entering emptiness through analysis, the three paths are severed by way of the two gates of being and non-being, as the Abhidharma explains through the seven worthies, seven sages, and four śramaṇa-fruits; and as the Satyasiddhi explains through the twenty-seven worthies and sages — these differentiated stages and marks, on up through the gates of neither-being-nor-non-being, are all gathered in by the contemplation that analyzes into emptiness. If, entering emptiness through embodying dharmas across the four gates, the three paths are severed, as the Larger Sūtra explains the stages shared across the three vehicles, from dry wisdom on up through the eighth ground, all alike gathered in by the calming-and-contemplation that enters emptiness. If one proceeds from emptiness into the provisional, cultivating distinct practices in sequence, and has not yet grasped the deeper sense, this accomplishes the stage of the thirty minds that subdue affliction, gathered in by the two contemplations of emptiness and the provisional together. If one has grasped the deeper sense, able to break through the three paths, this accomplishes the ten grounds, gathered in by the third contemplation. Or, using only the contemplation of the provisional throughout, this too holds across all four gates in the same way. As for the perfect teaching's own faith, understanding, and practice: the phenomenal is itself already the real; proceeding from the stage of contemplative practice into the stage of resemblance, advancing to break through ignorance, opening, showing, awakening to, and entering the Buddha's own knowledge and vision — forty-two stages in all, riding together in the one jeweled vehicle, straight to the seat of enlightenment. Likewise, the fourteenth day is likened to prajñā, the stage of cause; the fifteenth is likened to wondrous enlightenment, the stage of fruit. All of these are gathered in by the contemplation of the middle. This holds across all four gates in the same way.

Question: The Mahāyāna does not explain stages and grounds — how, then, can calming-and-contemplation be said to gather them in? Answer: The Mahāyāna sūtras and treatises do, in fact, all explain stages and grounds. If you fear stages and grounds, and take refuge instead in "no stages and grounds," you still cannot escape bondage — for it is precisely the freedom of the written word's own nature from itself that is liberation. Though stages and grounds are spoken of, this is itself already "no stages and grounds." The Middle Treatise says: "It is like the outsider who refutes the worldly cause and effect, and so is left with no present life and no future life; and refutes the world-transcending cause and effect, and so is left with no Three Jewels, no Four Noble Truths, no fourfold śramaṇa-fruit." What Three Jewels are thereby left absent? Since wrong views are not yet extinguished, there is indeed no Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths, or fourfold śramaṇa-fruit as accounted for within the Tripiṭaka teaching — if one cannot even attain the fruit of the clumsy method's own path, how could there possibly be the further three rounds of Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths, and fourfold śramaṇa-fruit beyond it? This passage rebukes the non-Buddhist outsider for lacking all four rounds of the Three Jewels entirely. If it instead rebukes the clumsy method, then only the Tripiṭaka teaching's own Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths, and fourfold śramaṇa-fruit are present, with no further three rounds of the path's own fruit beyond it. As for what I myself refute here: the Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths, and fourfold śramaṇa-fruit are indeed present. Why is this? Analytically breaking through affliction, karma, and suffering within the three realms yields the Tripiṭaka teaching's own Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths, and fourfold śramaṇa-fruit; embodying and breaking through them directly yields the further three rounds of Three Jewels, Four Noble Truths, and fourfold śramaṇa-fruit. Marking this single point clarifies: treating what lingers within, refuting what is deviant without, removing the deviance and pettiness of both extremes — the correct Three Jewels and Four Noble Truths are thereby established. How, then, could it be said that there is none of it at all? It is only a matter of whether there is a stage or no stage — this cannot be settled without direct realization. Here, one simply trusts the teaching: where the teaching speaks of stages, the sequence of stages stands plainly before us; where the teaching speaks of no stages, this opens out entirely into emptiness and purity alike. "No phrase's own meaning" is itself the bodhisattva's own phrase and meaning — marking emptiness while still discussing stages, the stage itself can never be pinned down, and no dispute should arise over this. Again, by way of the Middle Treatise's own fourfold verse, the sense of stages and grounds may likewise be found. The verse says: "whatever arises through causes and conditions, this I declare to be empty" — this breaks through affliction, karma, and suffering, and thereby yields both the wisdom and the severance of stream-entry, which is the bodhisattva's own forbearance regarding the non-arising of dharmas. The sixth ground is equal to the two lesser vehicles; the seventh ground is the expedient means; the tenth ground is as if buddhahood itself — these stages are self-evidently present; how, then, could it be said there are none? The verse's "it is also called a provisional name" is the gradual, sequential breaking-through of the three paths beyond the three realms, and thereby yields the forty-two stages of worthies and sages — how, then, could it be said there are none? The verse's "it is also the meaning of the middle way" is the perfect breaking-through of the five abodes of affliction, and thereby yields the stages of the six identities — how, then, could it be said there are none? Simply by means of these four clauses, every stage is gathered in; every stage does not exceed these four clauses, and these four clauses do not exceed calming-and-contemplation — hence this is called "gathering in stages."

Reading note: The opening objection here is a real tension worth taking seriously, not a straw man: if ultimate reality admits no distinctions, doesn't speaking of "stages" and "grounds" already betray it? Zhiyi's answer refuses to collapse the paradox: "though stages and grounds are spoken of, this is itself already 'no stages and grounds'" — the sequence is real at the level where affliction, karma, and suffering are actually treated, even though, at the level of quiescent extinction, none of it can be pinned down as ultimately real either. The closing return to the Middle Treatise's own four-clause verse — the same verse cited throughout this fascicle and fascicle 1 — ties the whole edifice of Buddhist stages (stream-entry, the ten grounds, the forty-two worthy-and-sage stages, the six identities) back to that one root text one final time.

Sixth, gathering in every teaching: the Vibhāṣā says: "The mind alone is able to give names to every dharma; without mind, there would be no names at all." One should know that every name, whether mundane or world-transcending, arises entirely from mind. If one contemplates the mind wrongly, following the current of ignorance, every kind of evil teaching arises accordingly — such as the Sāṃkhya, the Vaiśeṣika, and the ninety-five kinds of deviant view thereby generated. Various wholesome teachings likewise arise: the five phases and the sexagenary cycle, yin-yang and the eight trigrams, the five classics and the philosophers and historians. Worldly wisdom's own teachings, without the Way, all arise from mind. How, then, do the world-transcending teachings likewise all arise from mind? Sthiramati's Ratnagotravibhāga says: "There is a great sūtra-scroll, as vast as the trichiliocosm itself, recording every matter of the trichiliocosm; and, like it, one of middling size, recording the matters of the four continents, the three realms, and every such expanse — every one of these is recorded within a single mote of dust. As this holds for one mote of dust, so it holds for every mote of dust. A person appears in the world, and, with the pure heavenly eye, sees this great sūtra-scroll, and thinks: 'How can this great scroll lie hidden within a mote of dust, and yet fail to benefit sentient beings at all?' And so, by skillful means, breaks open the dust to release this scroll, for the benefit of others." The Tathāgata's own unobstructed wisdom, a sūtra-scroll complete within the very body of every sentient being — covered over by inversion, unbelieved and unseen. The Buddha teaches sentient beings to cultivate the eightfold noble path, breaking through every falsehood, so that they see their own wisdom to be equal to the Tathāgata's own. This, taking the mote of dust as its vehicle, is a simile grounded in what exists. Again, taking emptiness as the vehicle for a simile: the Treatise on Giving Rise to the Bodhi-Mind says: "It is like a person who, seeing the Dharma about to be lost, writes out the Tathāgata's own twelvefold division of the sūtras, facing upward into empty space, complete and entire — and yet no sentient being knows of it at all. After a very long time, another person, wandering through that same emptiness, comes upon the sūtra and cries out in astonishment: 'How can sentient beings neither know of it nor see it?' And so copies it down, showing and guiding sentient beings with it." What is meant by "copying down the sūtra"? It means causing sentient beings to cultivate the eightfold noble path, breaking through falsehood, and so on.

Reading note: These two similes — a scroll as vast as an entire universe hidden inside a single mote of dust, and a complete set of scriptures written invisibly across empty space, seen only by whoever happens to notice — are among the most striking images in the whole treatise, and both make the identical point already familiar from this fascicle's account of the buddha-eye and the letter i: the whole is not built up from the parts, but already fully present within each one, waiting only to be recognized rather than assembled. The Buddha's own teaching, on this account, does not add anything to sentient beings that was not already there — it simply "breaks open the dust" to release what was hidden inside all along.

Reading note: The Five Flavors (milk, cream, curdled butter, clarified butter, ghee — a real dairy-refining sequence, each stage genuinely produced from the one before it) became one of the most influential organizing schemes in later Tiantai historiography, eventually developing into the full doctrine of the Five Periods that classifies the Buddha's entire fifty-year teaching career. Here it is used more narrowly, mapping five ways of contemplating mind onto five bodies of scripture. The startling image that follows — "killing" present in milk just as much as in ghee — answers a natural worry about a graduated scheme like this: does the first, most elementary teaching actually accomplish anything, or is it just a warm-up for the real thing later on? Zhiyi's answer, borrowed from the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own image of poison surviving intact through every stage of dairy refinement, is that wherever the buddha-nature teaching is genuinely present, it "kills" — severs the two kinds of death — with the same full potency, whether encountered at the very first stage or the very last. This closes topic four, the scope of the teachings gathered in, in its entirety.

Fifth, clarifying partial and complete: the practitioner, having already come to know that calming-and-contemplation gathers in every dharma without exception, and that the dharmas thereby gathered in are so numerous, must now come to recognize great and small, shared and unshared, provisional and real, conceivable and inconceivable — hence partial and complete are distinguished here. This is divided into five: first, clarifying great and small; second, clarifying half and full; third, clarifying partial and perfect; fourth, clarifying gradual and sudden; fifth, clarifying the provisional and the real. Ultimate principle itself is neither great nor small, nor even provisional nor real — great, small, provisional, and real cannot, any of them, truly be spoken of at all. Yet, given the right causal conditions, great, small, and the rest can indeed all be spoken of. By the power of a lesser expedient means, the small was taught to the five bhikṣus; by the power of a greater expedient means, the great was taught to the bodhisattvas. Though great and small are both alike expedient means, one must still recognize the reason for each — hence these five paired distinctions are used, so that nothing is confused or conflated.

"Small" means the Hīnayāna. Its wisdom-power is weak, and it is capable only of cultivating the analytical calming-and-contemplation, analyzing form and mind apart. As the Treatise, explaining the pāramitā of giving, refutes the non-Buddhist doctrine of the atom, says: "Is this mote of dust existent, or non-existent? If the ultimate particle of form is not analyzed all the way through, this yields the view of permanence, the view of being; if the ultimate particle is analyzed all the way through, this yields the view of annihilation, the view of non-being. This is the non-Buddhist's own analysis of form. The analysis of mind is exactly the same: reckoning either that mind exists or that mind does not exist, both alike fall into annihilation or permanence. All of this is the non-Buddhist outsider's own analysis of form and mind. The Treatise's text, however, goes on to explain the Tripiṭaka teaching's own analytical contemplation, saying: "Form, whether coarse or subtle, taken together and contemplated as a whole, is impermanent, without any self." Why is this? Coarse and subtle form alike arise from ignorance; ignorance is unreal, and so both coarse and subtle form are alike provisional. Being provisional, they are impermanent and without fixed nature, and emptiness is thereby entered. Again, the very slightest arising of mind necessarily relies on faculty and sense-object; there is not a single dharma that does not arise from conditions. What arises from conditions is, without exception, impermanent. Some say "a single thought of mind contains sixty instants"; some say "three hundred billion instants" — an instant never abides, thought after thought impermanent. Impermanent, without any master, affliction is thereby broken at its very root; without karma, without suffering, birth-and-death extinguished — this is named nirvāṇa. This is what is meant by "the intent of the contemplation that analyzes form and mind." This term "analysis" has its origin with the non-Buddhist outsiders, and correct analysis is here explained specifically in opposition to their own deviant analysis. But it is not only outsider deviance that requires this correct analysis — should even the Buddha's own disciples cling to the Buddha's own teaching-gate and give rise to attachment through view, this too requires correct analysis. Namely: the Tripiṭaka teaching's own four gates give rise to their own fourfold attached view, on up even to the perfect teaching's own four gates giving rise to their own fourfold attached view — idle theorizing and contentious dispute, affirming oneself and denying others, every one of them drinking the ambrosia of the Dharma and yet cutting their own life short by it, binding themselves in golden chains and turning in birth-and-death — all of this requires correct analysis. Hence the Treatise says: "To refute nirvāṇa does not mean refuting the nirvāṇa the sage has actually attained; it means only that, for students who have not yet attained nirvāṇa but cling to it, turning it into idle theorizing, it is therefore said that nirvāṇa is refuted." If this is so, the expedient means of analysis is used throughout to refute such clinging. There are, in general, four gates, and within each single gate, ten factors are complete: recognizing correct causation, down to not giving rise to attachment even to the Dharma itself, one is able, within any of these gates, to see the ultimate meaning. Hence it is known that the Tripiṭaka teaching's own fourfold-gated analytical calming-and-contemplation is, decisively and without doubt, the Hīnayāna.

Next, clarifying the great: this is the Mahāyāna. Its wisdom is deep and sharp, cultivating the calming-and-contemplation that embodies dharmas as neither arising nor ceasing. This is what a great person practices — hence it is named the "Great Vehicle." When the Middle Treatise explains "at once empty," it extends the Mahāyāna's own teaching; the Mahāyāna is itself the great. Within that Vehicle it is said, "Wishing to attain śrāvaka-hood, one should study prajñā" — this is, at root, a bodhisattva's own dharma; the great is able to embrace the small as well, incidentally including the śrāvaka within it. It is like the Vermilion Bird Gate, established by the imperial household, properly for the passage of royal affairs, and yet not preventing the common crowd from also passing through it. Though it is passed through by common people, it remains, in the end, the emperor's own gate. The Mahāyāna is exactly the same: properly intended for the bodhisattva who embodies dharmas to enter emptiness, though the Hīnayāna, too, is present within it, it remains, in the end, named the great. This is exactly like the Tripiṭaka teaching's own analytical method: though the Buddha and bodhisattvas, too, are present within it, it remains, in the end, the Hīnayāna. What is meant by the Mahāyāna's own contemplation that embodies dharmas differs from the Tripiṭaka teaching's own. In the Tripiṭaka teaching, the name is provisional but the dharma is real, and the real is analyzed away to reach emptiness — like breaking apart a pillar to make it empty space. In the Mahāyāna's own embodying approach, both name and dharma are alike provisional; their very own mark is already emptiness, void and quiescent from the very start. It is like the pillar reflected in a mirror, which was never truly a pillar to begin with, needing no destruction of any actual pillar to become empty — the very sky, as its own reflection, is already empty, neither arising nor ceasing, quite unlike a real pillar. Again, the Treatise, explaining the Mahāyāna practitioner's own contemplation that embodies dharmas, cites the Buddha, standing upon a single square of wood, telling the bhikṣus: "It is like a bhikṣu who, having attained dhyāna-concentration, transforms earth into gold, and transforms gold into earth — in reality, neither gold nor earth, simply a transformation brought about." Form and mind are exactly the same: neither arising nor ceasing, simply the transformations of ignorance. Never, from the very start, truly arisen — how, then, could they now be truly extinguished? Again, the Treatise cites contemplating a single length of cotton cloth as already complete with the eighteen kinds of emptiness — this is what is named "the contemplation that embodies dharmas."

The real thereby entered is not truly the buddha-nature, and does not meet the real principle — it is merely "the real" according with sentiment alone. The Mahāyāna's own embodying of dharmas is named "contemplating form and mind according with principle" — like tracing an illusion back to find the illusionist, and tracing the illusionist back to find the very art of illusion; or like tracing a dream back to find sleep, and tracing sleep back to find the mind itself. Tracing illusory form and mind back, one finds ignorance; tracing ignorance back, one finds the buddha-nature. Embodying dharmas penetrates through to principle — hence it is named "contemplation according with principle." The calming-and-contemplation that embodies dharmas has, in general, four gates, and within each single gate, all ten factors are complete, forming the contemplation. This contemplation not only embodies the form and mind of the non-Buddhist outsider's own karmic retribution, but touches every kind of clinging whatsoever — reckoning that whoever has not yet entered through the Tripiṭaka teaching's own four gates, on up even to the perfect teaching's own four gates, and clings to their gate, thereby forming a view, is, every one, embodied as like an illusion. This is, decisively and without doubt, what is named "Mahāyāna calming-and-contemplation." If one grasps the intent of this contemplation as used here, then even attachment arising within any of the Mahāyāna's own gates must still be emptied and refuted. This is never the same as those worldly Dharma-masters and Chan-masters who claim that Laozi's own "Way and Virtue" or Zhuangzi's own "Free and Easy Wandering" are equal to the Buddhadharma — this claim does not hold. Even attachment arising within the perfect teaching's own gate is still refuted by the Tripiṭaka teaching's own initial gate, and does not even reach the level of the Hīnayāna — how much less, then, the ordinary, base view of the mind untrained at all? A firefly and the sun are worlds apart; a mountain and a single hair's tip are utterly incomparable — those who declare their own way to be the true one, disparaging the Buddha himself, how could they possibly escape refutation?

Reading note: The distinction Zhiyi draws here between "analysis" (析法) and "embodying" (體法) is one of the treatise's most important technical contrasts, recurring constantly: the Hīnayāna reaches emptiness by breaking a real thing down into its parts until nothing solid remains — "like breaking apart a pillar to make it empty space" — while the Mahāyāna recognizes that the thing was never solid to begin with, "like a pillar reflected in a mirror, which was never truly a pillar in the first place." Both arrive at emptiness, but one treats emptiness as a destination reached by demolition, the other as already true of the thing from the start. Zhiyi's closing aside — explicitly rejecting the common move of equating Daoist philosophy with the Buddhadharma, popular among some contemporary "worldly Dharma-masters and Chan-masters" — is a rare direct polemical moment naming a live controversy of his own era, not just an abstract doctrinal point.

Second, clarifying half and full. "Half" refers to the nine-part division of the teachings; "full" refers to the twelve-part division. It is commonly transmitted that only the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own eternal abiding is truly "full," and everything else is merely "half." Third, clarifying partial and perfect: "partial" means one-sided and skewed; "perfect" means round and complete. In the general, broad sense, calling the small "partial" is unobjectionable — but distinguished in the particular sense, this will not do. The two names "half" and "small" are fixed and confined to a narrow scope, unable to be extended any further; the sense of "partial," by contrast, runs through the whole range, from the small all the way to the great. It is like the half-moon, aligned exactly with the first or last quarter; the waxing moon, by contrast, is not so fixed — from the very first crescent all the way to the fourteenth night, every phase is called "gradual," and only the fifteenth night alone is called the full, perfect moon. Reading note: "Half and full" is dispatched quickly because Zhiyi treats it as parasitic on the great/small distinction already made — but "partial and perfect" earns its own careful treatment because it cuts differently than either pair before it. "Small" is a fixed, narrow category (only the Hīnayāna), but "partial" stretches across almost the entire scheme, taking in the Tripiṭaka teaching, the shared teaching, and the distinct teaching alike — everything short of the perfect teaching itself. The waxing-moon simile makes the point vividly: "half-moon" names one exact phase, but every night from the first sliver through the fourteenth counts as "gradual," with only the fifteenth, full moon earning the name "perfect" — a much larger and more continuous category than "half" ever was.

Fourth, clarifying gradual and sudden: "gradual" names what proceeds in sequence, relying on the shallow to reach the deep; "sudden" names what is complete all at once, reaching the utmost immediately. This, too, has no separate sense of its own — it, too, supports and completes the distinction of partial and perfect. The three teachings' own calming-and-contemplation are, every one, gradual; the perfect teaching's own calming-and-contemplation alone is named sudden. This much follows directly from the names themselves, and the sense is already plain. Here, this shall be further clarified at greater length, so that nothing is left unresolved. The first two teachings' own calming-and-contemplation are gradual and not sudden, their power unable to reach far, meeting only the partial real. The perfect teaching's own calming-and-contemplation is sudden and not gradual, walking the great, direct path, taking the very extreme itself as the middle. The distinct teaching's own calming-and-contemplation is both gradual and sudden at once. Why is this? At the very first mind, it already knows the middle — hence it is called sudden as well; yet it proceeds by way of expedient means to enter it — hence it is called gradual as well. Furthermore: in the first two teachings, contemplation, teaching, practice, and realization are all, every one, named gradual. In the distinct teaching, the teaching, contemplation, and practice are all named gradual; the path of realization alone is sudden. In the perfect teaching, teaching, contemplation, practice, and realization are all, every one, named sudden. Why is this? The first two teachings' own contemplations are spoken by way of expedient means — a thatched hut, a winding path — and so all four aspects, teaching and contemplation alike, are gradual together. The distinct teaching's own contemplation carries expedient means within its own speaking: relying on this expedient practice, it first breaks through the shared affliction, and so these three aspects are all gradual; only afterward, breaking through ignorance and seeing the buddha-nature, is the path of realization itself sudden. The perfect teaching's own contemplation "directly and without deviation, setting aside all expedient means, teaches only the unsurpassed path"; "this alone, this one matter, is real; the other two are then not truly real"; "teaches the most real matter of all" — this is named "the real of the teaching." "Practicing the Tathāgata's own practice," "entering the Tathāgata's own room, robe, and seat," and the rest, "and there being, moreover, a single practice that is the Tathāgata's own practice" — this is named "the real of practice." The middle way thereby seen is itself the one and only ultimate, identical with the very Dharma-body the Tathāgata himself has attained, with no difference, no separateness at all — this is named "the real of realization."

In the first two teachings, within the cause, there is teaching, practice, realization, and a person; but upon the fruit, there is only the teaching, with no practice, realization, or person. Why is this? The person within the cause reduces the body to ash and enters quiescent stillness, sunk in emptiness and utterly extinguished, unable to accomplish the buddhahood at the very head of the fruit — this is simply the speaking of expedient means, and so there is the teaching, but no practice, realization, or person. Within the cause of the distinct teaching, there is teaching, practice, realization, and a person; but with regard to the fruit, there is only the teaching, with no practice, realization, or person. Why is this? Should one break through ignorance and ascend to the first ground, this is already the perfect teaching's own first abiding-stage, and no longer the distinct teaching's own first-ground stage at all. If the first ground is already this way, how much more the later grounds and the later fruit? Hence it is known that the person within the cause never actually reaches the fruit — hence it is said, "no person at the very head of the fruit." In the perfect teaching, teaching, practice, realization, and person within the cause all proceed, every one, from cause through to fruit, all alike genuinely real — hence it is said, "there is truly a person there." Furthermore: before the first three teachings' own teaching, practice, realization, and person have been brought into convergence, one still does not even know the perfect teaching — how much less enter it? Should the Buddha bring the essential teaching into convergence, opening the gradual to reveal the sudden, every one of them is thereby brought through and entered. Though this is not the sudden as such, it is "gradual-and-sudden." Hence the Lotus Sūtra says: "What you have practiced is itself already the bodhisattva path." "Each riding the jeweled carriage, matching exactly what was, from the very start, their own original wish." "Definitively clarifying the śrāvaka's own dharma, this is the king of all sūtras." Opening and bringing through the gradual dharma, causing every one of them to enter, grafting them onto it by means of the distinct principle. Hence, within the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, those who have attained the fruit of the two lesser vehicles' own path are not thereby cut off from the perfect, eternal teaching; cultivating and studying by means of this very attainment, all of them shall become buddhas in the end. This is precisely entering the perfect teaching from the gradual, and is likewise called the sense of "opening the gradual to reveal the sudden."

Reading note: "No person at the very head of the fruit" (果頭無人) is a striking and somewhat severe claim: a practitioner of the first three teachings who follows their own path all the way to its own stated final fruit never actually arrives as the same person who set out, because genuine breakthrough at any point along the way already means having quietly graduated into the perfect teaching's own scheme instead. Only the perfect teaching offers a path where "teaching, practice, realization, and person... proceed, every one, from cause through to fruit," continuous and undivided. The Lotus Sūtra citations that follow soften this severity considerably: "opening the gradual to reveal the sudden" means the other three teachings were never dead ends, only routes that eventually merge into the same one road — the śrāvaka's own path is retroactively declared to have been "the bodhisattva path" all along.

Furthermore: entry into the perfect teaching by way of the four kinds of calming-and-contemplation does not necessarily wait for all four practices to be completed together before entering the perfect; nor does it necessarily wait for the gradual to be opened and the sudden revealed together before entering — such entry is, in fact, indeterminate. Why is this? Every sentient being's own mind-nature, as the proper cause, may be likened to milk; hearing the illuminating cause's own dharma may be likened to placing poison within it. The proper cause is never severed, just as milk's own four subtle elements remain — though the five flavors change in succession, these four elements are ever present. Hence the poison follows these four elements, and, at every one of the five flavors in turn, still kills. Sentient beings' own mind-nature is exactly the same: the proper cause never destroyed, the poison of the illuminating cause, following the pace, quick or slow, of the proper cause itself, can be triggered anywhere, at any point. Sometimes triggered through principle, sometimes through teaching, sometimes through practice, and coming to fruition through realization. It is like the pratyekabuddha, of sharp wisdom and ripened wholesome roots, appearing in an age without a buddha, and awakening entirely of their own accord — triggering through principle is exactly the same: wholesome roots long ago planted, though in this present life the perfect teaching is not even heard, the poison of the illuminating cause is triggered spontaneously, of its own accord — this is triggering through principle. Should one hear the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, the sun shining first upon the high mountains, and thereby attain awakening, this is triggering through teaching. Having heard it, one reflects, and, through reflection, awakens — this is triggering through the stage of contemplative practice. If it is at the stage of the six purified faculties, advancing to break through ignorance, this is triggering through the stage of resemblance-realization. If one further increases the path and diminishes rebirth, this too is triggering through realization. This discusses indeterminate entry from within the perfect teaching's own house. If a practitioner of the first three teachings is triggered while still at the ordinary being's own stage, this is triggering through principle. If triggered on hearing the teaching, this is triggering through teaching. If triggered through cultivating the expedient means, this is triggering through the stage of contemplative practice. If triggered within the stage of a worthy or sage, this is triggering through realization. This discusses indeterminate entry from within the three other houses. There is, furthermore, an indeterminate triggering that is not truly "killing" — as when, while cultivating the uncontaminated, the contaminated is triggered spontaneously without being sought at all, severing neither of the two kinds of death entirely. If, cultivating the middle way, the uncontaminated is triggered and attained, one is thereby separated forever from the ocean-wheel of suffering of the three realms — this is severing only one of the two kinds of death, not both — and this, too, is called indeterminate.

Furthermore: each of the four teachings' own calming-and-contemplation is, within its own scope, both perfect and gradual. Within the Tripiṭaka teaching, there is a path from the very first mind's own expedient means through to entering the real stage — this is named gradual. The thirty-four mind-moments severing every fetter and accomplishing the fruit — is this not, in its own way, named perfect? Within the shared and distinct teachings, from the first mind through to the final mind — is there not likewise gradual and perfect within each? Within the perfect teaching, considered in its own essence, principle at its very utmost is called perfect; and yet there is also the first mind through to the forty-first ground — is this not likewise gradual? Wondrous enlightenment, the ultimate — is this not likewise perfect? The "perfect" within "perfect" is not the same as the "perfect" within "gradual"; the "gradual" within "perfect" is not the same as the "gradual" within "gradual." Hence it is known that, within its own proper scope, each teaching is complete with both senses. This should be explained at greater length in the Lotus Commentary. Yet the "gradual" within "gradual" is not the "gradual" within "perfect," though it can come to accomplish the "gradual" within "perfect"; the "gradual" within "perfect" is not the "perfect" within "perfect," and can never come to accomplish the "perfect" within "perfect." Why is this? The Lotus Sūtra says: "What you have practiced is itself already the bodhisattva path" — hence the "gradual" within "gradual" comes to accomplish the "gradual" within "perfect." The "gradual" within "perfect" is the fruit provisionally established by the three lesser teachings, and can never come to accomplish the buddha of wondrous enlightenment itself. By analogy: the "small" within "small" is not the "small" within "great," though it can come to accomplish the "small" within "great"; the "great" within "small" is not the "great" within "great," and can never come to accomplish the "great" within "great." The "provisional" within "provisional" is not the "real" within "provisional," though it can come to accomplish the "real" within "provisional"; the "real" within "provisional" is not the "real" within "real," and can never come to accomplish the "real" within "real." Why is this? At the very head of the fruit of the three lesser teachings, there is teaching but no person — hence the provisional and the real can never come to accomplish the "real" within "real." Half and full, gradual and sudden, should each be distinguished following this same pattern, and need not be belabored further in writing.

Contemplating the mind and reasoning it through, the mark of the Dharma should indeed be exactly this — and yet people mostly do not believe it. Here, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own five similes are used to confirm this sense. The sixth fascicle says: "The ordinary being is like milk; the stream-enterer is like cream; the once-returner is like curdled butter; the non-returner is like clarified butter; the arhat, the pratyekabuddha, and the Buddha are like ghee." The Treatise says: "Within the śrāvaka scriptures, the arhat is named 'the stage of buddhahood.'" Hence all three — arhat, pratyekabuddha, and Buddha — are alike named ghee. Does this simile not explain the five flavors within the Tripiṭaka teaching itself? The sense of "gradual within perfect" can be established by analogy with this. The thirty-second fascicle says: "Sentient beings are like milk mixed with blood; the stream-enterer and once-returner are like pure milk; the non-returner is like cream; the arhat is like curdled butter; the pratyekabuddha and the bodhisattva are like clarified butter; the Buddha is like ghee." Does this simile not explain the five flavors within the shared teaching itself? The pratyekabuddha, having encroached upon habituated traces, is slightly superior to the śrāvaka, and so is grouped together with the bodhisattva as clarified butter alike; the Buddha, both the affliction proper and its habituated trace exhausted, is named ghee. Borrowing this to illustrate the shared teaching's own proper scope, the sense of "gradual within perfect" is thereby made manifest. The ninth fascicle says: "Sentient beings are like a newborn calf, blood and milk not yet distinguished; the śrāvaka is like milk; the pratyekabuddha is like cream; the bodhisattva is like curdled and clarified butter; the Buddha is like ghee." Is this simile not the sense of the five flavors within the distinct teaching itself? The beginning and middle of the ten abidings, able to sever completely the views and habituated thought shared with the other vehicles, is named milk, likened generally to the śrāvaka. The latter mind of the ten abidings, slightly deeper, is likened to the pratyekabuddha, cream. The ten practices and ten dedications are like curdled and clarified butter. The very first of the ten grounds is already named buddhahood, and so is like ghee. Borrowing this passage reveals the distinct teaching's own contemplation, within its proper scope, as complete with the sense of "gradual within perfect." The twenty-seventh fascicle says: "On the Snow Mountains there grows a grass called forbearance; should a cow eat it, it becomes ghee directly." The grass is a simile for the correct path; if one is able to cultivate the correct path, the buddha-nature is seen directly. Is this simile not the very sense of the perfect teaching? Without passing through the four intervening flavors, ghee is accomplished directly. Borrowing this passage, the stages of "gradual within perfect" and the rest are shown by analogy. The eighth fascicle says: "Place poison in milk, and it pervades every one of the five flavors, each alike able to kill." Is this simile not precisely the simile for the indeterminate? This is exactly how the four kinds of triggering — through principle, teaching, practice, and realization — come to enter the perfect teaching. This clarification has now been made in terms of gradual and sudden; the first three sections, and this final one, should likewise be understood the same way. It is only that great-and-small, half-and-full, are each fixed to their own precise, narrow scope, and cannot be treated identically.

Reading note: The milk-and-poison image returns from topic four's own "killing at every stage" passage, now doing different work: there, it showed the buddha-nature teaching retains full potency at every one of the five flavors; here, it explains why awakening cannot be scheduled — the "poison," once present, may be triggered by principle, by hearing a teaching, by practice, or by formal realization, in any order, at any stage, for any given person. The four Nirvāṇa Sūtra citations that follow are worth noticing as a set: the same five-flavor sequence (milk, cream, curdled butter, clarified butter, ghee) is mapped independently onto all three of the lesser teachings' own internal stages, while the perfect teaching alone gets the different image of forbearance-grass, which "becomes ghee directly" — no intervening stages at all. This closes "gradual and sudden," the fourth of the five sub-sections; only "the provisional and the real" remains to close topic five, and with it, this entire fascicle.

5. The Provisional and the Real 明權實

五、明權實者，權是權謀，暫用還廢；實是實錄，究竟旨歸。立權略為三意：一、為實施權，二、開權顯實，三、廢權顯實。如《法華》中蓮華三譬。諸佛即一大事出世，元為圓頓一實止觀，而施三權止觀也。權非本意，意亦不在權外；祇開三權止觀，而顯圓頓一實止觀也。為實施權，實今已立；開權顯實，權即是實，無權可論；是故廢權顯實，權廢實存。暫用釋名，其義為允。問：何意用此權實？答：佛知眾生種種性欲，以四悉檀而成熟之。若人欲聞正因緣，為說三藏；欲聞因緣即空，為說通觀；欲聞歷劫修行，為說別觀；欲聞即中，為說圓觀。是名隨「世界悉檀」，亦名隨「樂欲」，為實施權說權實止觀也。為生扶真之事善，說三藏觀；為生扶真之理善，為說通觀；為生扶中之事善，為說別觀；為生扶中之理善，為說圓觀。是名隨「為人悉檀」，亦名隨「便宜」而說權實止觀也。為破邪因緣、無因緣，說三藏觀；為破拙度，故說通觀；為破共法，故說別觀；為破帶方便，故說圓觀。是為「對治悉檀」說權實止觀也。為思議鈍根，拙度令入真諦，說三藏觀；為思議利根，巧度令入真諦，故說通觀；為不思議鈍根，拙度令入見中，故說別觀；為不思議利根，巧度令入見中，故說圓觀。是名為一實而施三權。權實相對，則有四種止觀。為實施權，意齊此也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, clarifying the provisional and the real: the provisional is a provisional stratagem, used for a time and then set aside; the real is the true record, where everything ultimately leads. Establishing the provisional serves, in brief, three purposes: first, establishing the provisional for the sake of the real; second, opening the provisional to reveal the real; third, setting aside the provisional to reveal the real. This is like the Lotus Sūtra's own three similes of the lotus flower. Every buddha appears in the world for the sake of the one great matter, originally intending the perfect-and-sudden, one real calming-and-contemplation — and yet bestows the three provisional kinds of calming-and-contemplation instead. The provisional was never the original intent, yet that intent does not lie outside the provisional either; it is simply that the three provisional kinds of calming-and-contemplation are opened up, and thereby the perfect-and-sudden, one real calming-and-contemplation is revealed. Establishing the provisional for the sake of the real: the real is now, thereby, established. Opening the provisional to reveal the real: the provisional is itself the real, with no provisional left to speak of at all — hence, setting aside the provisional reveals the real, the provisional set aside and the real alone remaining. Used for a time to explain the name, this sense holds true. Question: For what reason is this scheme of the provisional and the real employed at all? Answer: The Buddha knows sentient beings' manifold natures and desires, and ripens them by means of the four siddhāntas. If a person wishes to hear of correct causation, the Tripiṭaka teaching is taught for them; if they wish to hear that causation is itself empty, the shared teaching's own contemplation is taught; if they wish to hear of cultivation practiced across many kalpas, the distinct teaching's own contemplation is taught; if they wish to hear of the middle directly, the perfect teaching's own contemplation is taught. This is named according with the worldly siddhānta, and also named according with "what is enjoyed" — establishing the provisional for the sake of the real, teaching the provisional-and-real calming-and-contemplation. To generate the phenomenal wholesomeness that supports the real, the Tripiṭaka teaching's own contemplation is taught; to generate the principled wholesomeness that supports the real, the shared teaching's own contemplation is taught; to generate the phenomenal wholesomeness that supports the middle, the distinct teaching's own contemplation is taught; to generate the principled wholesomeness that supports the middle, the perfect teaching's own contemplation is taught. This is named according with the siddhānta for the individual, and also named according with "what is fitting," teaching the provisional-and-real calming-and-contemplation. To break through deviant causation and causeless views, the Tripiṭaka teaching's own contemplation is taught; to break through the clumsy method, the shared teaching's own contemplation is taught; to break through what is shared in common with other vehicles, the distinct teaching's own contemplation is taught; to break through what still carries expedient means, the perfect teaching's own contemplation is taught. This is teaching the provisional-and-real calming-and-contemplation by way of the antidote siddhānta. For the conceivable dull faculty, the clumsy method causing entry into the real truth, the Tripiṭaka teaching's own contemplation is taught; for the conceivable sharp faculty, the skillful method causing entry into the real truth, the shared teaching's own contemplation is taught; for the inconceivable dull faculty, the clumsy method causing entry into seeing the middle, the distinct teaching's own contemplation is taught; for the inconceivable sharp faculty, the skillful method causing entry into seeing the middle, the perfect teaching's own contemplation is taught. This is what is meant by bestowing three provisional teachings for the sake of the one real. Provisional and real set against each other yield the four kinds of calming-and-contemplation. Establishing the provisional for the sake of the real has exactly this same intent.

權實既興，良由悉檀；權實可廢，亦由悉檀。何者？眾生煩惱結使厚，利智善根薄，故興初觀，生其事善；事善若生，煩惱伏薄，即廢三藏觀。為生理善，興於通觀；理善已生，即廢通觀。為生界外事善，即興別觀；界外事善已生，即廢別觀。為生界外理善，即興圓觀。是為興廢因緣，故說於權實止觀也。餘三悉檀興廢可解。若約五味教論興廢者，華嚴為大行人，廢兩權，興一權一實；三藏廢兩權一實，但興一權；方等四種俱興；般若廢一權，興兩權一實；法華廢三權，興一實；涅槃還興四種，皆入佛性，無所可隔。是故如來巧用悉檀，興廢適時，順機而作，皆益眾生。是故如來不空說法，為度人故，應興、應廢也。對三權說一實，實存權廢，已如前說。今更料簡。四種止觀皆實不虛。所以者何？若不開決，則無入理。今決了聲聞法，是諸經之王，開方便門，示真實相；一一止觀皆得入圓。如快馬見鞭影，即得正路。故四種皆實也。又四種皆權。何以故？四理皆不可說。權不可說，故非權；實不可說，故非實。非權而強說為權；非實而強說為實。等是強說，何意不名權為實耶？以有說故，故皆是權。又此權實，悉是非權非實。何以故？皆不可說故。此非權非實，不得異於向實。向以見理為實，實秖是非權非實，此義不異。若異者，應有別慧，應照別理。理惑既同，不可使異。對權故說實，廢教故說理，故言非權非實。即教而理，權實即非權非實，無二無別，不合不散。非權非實，理性常寂，名之為「止」；寂而常照，亦權亦實，名之為「觀」。觀故稱「智」、稱「般若」；止故稱「眼」、稱「首楞嚴」。如是等名不二不別，不合不散，即不可思議之止觀也。此非但開實是非權非實；開權亦是非權非實，猶屬開權顯實意耳。

The provisional and real thus arising is precisely on account of the siddhāntas; and the provisional and real thus being set aside is likewise on account of the siddhāntas. Why is this? When sentient beings' own afflictions and fetters lie thick, and their sharp wisdom and wholesome roots lie thin, the first contemplation is raised up, generating their phenomenal wholesomeness; once that phenomenal wholesomeness is generated, and their afflictions subdued and thinned, the Tripiṭaka teaching's own contemplation is set aside. To generate their principled wholesomeness, the shared teaching's own contemplation is raised up; once that principled wholesomeness is generated, the shared teaching's own contemplation is set aside. To generate their phenomenal wholesomeness beyond the three realms, the distinct teaching's own contemplation is raised up; once that phenomenal wholesomeness beyond the three realms is generated, the distinct teaching's own contemplation is set aside. To generate their principled wholesomeness beyond the three realms, the perfect teaching's own contemplation is raised up. This is the causal condition of raising up and setting aside — hence the provisional-and-real calming-and-contemplation is spoken of in this way. The raising up and setting aside of the remaining three siddhāntas may be understood by the same pattern. If raising up and setting aside are discussed in terms of the five-flavor teaching periods: the Avataṃsaka, taught for great practitioners, sets aside two of the provisional teachings, raising up one provisional and the one real; the Tripiṭaka period sets aside two provisional and the one real, raising up only the one remaining provisional; the Vaipulya period raises up all four together; the Perfection of Wisdom period sets aside one provisional, raising up two provisional and the one real; the Lotus sets aside all three provisional, raising up the one real alone; the Nirvāṇa Sūtra raises up all four once again, every one of them entering the buddha-nature, with nothing left set apart at all. This is precisely why the Tathāgata skillfully employs the siddhāntas, raising up and setting aside according to the occasion, acting in accordance with capacity, every one of them benefiting sentient beings. This is why the Tathāgata never teaches the Dharma emptily — for the sake of delivering beings, raising up and setting aside are each done exactly as fitting. Setting the one real against the three provisional, the real remaining and the provisional set aside, has already been explained above. Here, this shall be further clarified. All four kinds of calming-and-contemplation are alike real, not false. Why is this? Without this opening and resolving, there would be no entry into principle at all. Here, definitively clarifying the śrāvaka's own dharma, this is the king of every sūtra, opening the gate of expedient means and showing the true mark of reality; each and every one of the four kinds of calming-and-contemplation is thereby able to enter the perfect. As a swift horse, seeing merely the shadow of the whip, arrives at once on the correct road. Hence all four are alike real. And yet all four are also alike provisional. Why is this? All four principles are, in themselves, inexpressible. Since the provisional cannot truly be spoken of, it is not truly "provisional"; since the real cannot truly be spoken of, it is not truly "real." What is not truly provisional is nonetheless forcibly called "provisional"; what is not truly real is nonetheless forcibly called "real." Since both are equally forced descriptions, why should the provisional not also be named "real"? Because both are, in fact, spoken of at all, both are, in that very sense, provisional. Moreover, this provisional and this real are, both alike, neither provisional nor real. Why is this? Because both are, in truth, inexpressible. This "neither provisional nor real" is no different from the "real" spoken of just before. Earlier, seeing principle directly was called "real"; and this "real" is simply "neither provisional nor real" — the sense is not actually different. Were it different, there would have to be some separate wisdom, illuminating some separate principle — but since principle and delusion are already the same, they cannot be made to differ here. Speaking of "the real" in opposition to "the provisional," and speaking of "principle" in setting aside "the teaching," is why it is said to be "neither provisional nor real." The teaching is itself already principle; the provisional and the real are themselves already neither provisional nor real, with no two of them, no separateness at all, neither combined nor separate. Neither provisional nor real, the nature of principle ever quiescent, is named "calming"; that same quiescence, ever illuminating, at once provisional and at once real, is named "contemplation." Because it illuminates, it is called "wisdom," called "prajñā"; because it is calm, it is called "the eye," called "the Śūraṅgama." Names of this kind are not two, not separate, neither combined nor apart — this is precisely the inconceivable calming-and-contemplation itself. This is not only true of opening the real as itself neither provisional nor real; opening the provisional, too, is itself neither provisional nor real, still belonging to the very sense of "opening the provisional to reveal the real."

Reading note: The five-flavor mapping here gives the treatise's own compressed account of what would become the Five Periods doctrine, tracking not chronological sequence but a rhythm of "raising up and setting aside" particular teachings as different audiences ripen — Vaipulya alone raising all four together, the Lotus alone setting aside every provisional teaching entirely, the Nirvāṇa Sūtra reuniting them all once more. The closing tetralemma is this fascicle's real punchline, arrived at after a whole fascicle spent painstakingly distinguishing provisional from real, teaching from teaching: even "provisional" and "real" themselves, examined closely, are only "forced" descriptions of something ultimately inexpressible — which does not cancel the distinction (it was worth building in full detail) but relativizes it one final time, in exactly the pattern this fascicle has run again and again since its very first section on "mutual opposition" versus "the absolute."

Closing Questions: Grafting, and Whether the Provisional Teachings Know the Real 問答料簡 · 三權知實

Chinese Source

問：為一實施三權，唯有四種止觀。若以別接通止觀者，為權、為實？復何意不預四數？何意但言接通？何位被接？接入何位？答：接得入教，此則屬權；接得入證，此則屬實也。四教論其始終，接但終而無始，故不入四數。諸教皆接，亦應有之。此義不用者，二教明界內理，二教明界外理，兩處交際，須安一接，故但以別接通。若齊通為言，不論破無明，八地名支佛地，從此被接，知有中道。九地伏無明，十地破無明，即名為佛。但一品破，那得是極？故知接入別也。若望別教，是入初地位行也。若就諦論接者，通教真諦，空、中合論；從初已來，但觀真中之空。破見思惑盡到第八地，方為說真內之中。故云：「智者見空及與不空。」被接方聞，聞已見理，即是入別位也。三藏菩薩明位不爾，故不論接。別、圓發心已知中道，更將何接？故知接但在通也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: For the sake of the one real, three provisional teachings are bestowed — this yields exactly four kinds of calming-and-contemplation. If the distinct teaching is used to graft onto the shared teaching's own calming-and-contemplation, is this provisional, or is it real? And why is it not counted among the four to begin with? Why is it said only that the distinct grafts onto the shared? At what stage is the grafting done, and into what stage does it enter? Answer: Where the grafting reaches into the teaching, this belongs to the provisional; where the grafting reaches into realization, this belongs to the real. Discussing the Four Teachings from beginning to end, the grafting occurs only at the end, never at the beginning — hence it is not counted among the four. Should every teaching be grafted onto in this way, this ought to hold generally as well — yet this scheme is not applied elsewhere, because it is specifically the shared teaching, explaining principle within the three realms, and the distinct teaching, explaining principle beyond the three realms, that meet exactly at the boundary between the two, requiring a single graft to be set there — hence it is only the distinct teaching grafted onto the shared. Speaking strictly in terms of the shared teaching alone, without yet discussing the breaking through of ignorance, the eighth ground is named the pratyekabuddha's own ground; from this point, one is grafted on, and comes to know that the middle way exists. The ninth ground subdues ignorance; the tenth ground breaks through ignorance, and is thereupon named buddhahood. But breaking through only a single portion of ignorance — how could this possibly be the ultimate? Hence it is known that the grafting enters the distinct teaching. Viewed against the distinct teaching itself, this corresponds to entering the practice of its own first ground. If the grafting is discussed in terms of the truths themselves: in the shared teaching's own real truth, emptiness and the middle are discussed together; from the very first, only the emptiness within the real-and-middle is contemplated. Once the afflictions of views and habituated thought are broken through completely, reaching the eighth ground, only then is the middle within the real actually taught. Hence it is said: "The wise see both emptiness and the not-empty." Only once grafted on does one come to hear of this; having heard it, one sees principle — this is precisely entering the distinct teaching's own stage. The Tripiṭaka teaching's own bodhisattva, in explaining its stages, does not work this way, and so grafting is not discussed there. The distinct and perfect teachings, from the very arising of the resolve, already know the middle way — what further grafting, then, could be applied to them? Hence it is known that grafting occurs only within the shared teaching.

問：三權皆得知實不？答：別教初知；通教後知；三藏初後俱不知。問：若知，何意名權？若不知，二經相違。答：別雖初知，帶方便聞，教猶是權。通雖後知，可接者知，教終是權。其意可見。若言三藏不知，違二經者。《大經》云：「阿羅漢不知三寶常住不變者，所有禁戒亦不具足，不能得聲聞之道。」此義今當通，任羅漢自力，不應知見常住。譬如天眼未開，不見障外；不聞他說，亦不能知。羅漢佛眼未開，又不聞佛說，那得自知常住？故《法華》云：「於自所得功德，生滅度想；若遇餘佛，便得決了。」又云：「聲聞、緣覺、不退菩薩，亦不能知。」當知不聞則不知也。經稱知者，齊知己理。真諦無為，亦是於常，一相無變。若人分別真諦二相遷動者，不能發真；要須觀空，方入無漏。如須菩提觀空，憍陳如證無生智。又律儀不具足者，若能觀空得道共戒，此是具足戒也。故《華嚴》云：「諸法實性相，常住不變異，二乘亦皆得，而不名為佛。」故知常住語通，得作此釋。

Question: Do all three provisional teachings come to know the real? Answer: The distinct teaching knows it from the beginning; the shared teaching knows it only at the end; the Tripiṭaka teaching, neither at the beginning nor at the end, never knows it at all. Question: If they do know it, why are they still called "provisional"? And if they do not know it, this would seem to contradict itself across the two sūtras. Answer: Though the distinct teaching knows it from the beginning, this knowledge still comes wrapped in expedient means — the teaching remains, even so, provisional. Though the shared teaching knows it only at the end, it is only those capable of being grafted on who come to know it at all — the teaching remains, in the end, provisional as well. The sense of this may be seen clearly enough. As for the claim that the Tripiṭaka teaching does not know it at all, seeming to contradict the two sūtras: the Nirvāṇa Sūtra says, "The arhat, not knowing that the Three Jewels abide forever, unchanging, is also incomplete in every precept and restraint, and cannot attain the śrāvaka's own path." This sense should now be reconciled: left to the arhat's own power alone, they ought not to know or see the eternal at all. It is like the heavenly eye not yet opened, unable to see beyond any obstruction; and, not hearing another speak of it, likewise unable to know it. The arhat's own buddha-eye is not yet opened, and, moreover, has not heard the Buddha teach it directly — how, then, could they know the eternal by themselves? Hence the Lotus Sūtra says: "Toward the merit they themselves have already attained, they form the notion of final extinction; should they then encounter another buddha, they thereupon come to understand it definitively." It also says: "Even the śrāvaka, the pratyekabuddha, and the non-retrogressing bodhisattva are, likewise, unable to know it." One should know, then, that not hearing it means not knowing it. Where the sūtra speaks of "knowing," this means knowing only so far as their own principle extends. The real truth, unconditioned, is indeed itself a form of the eternal, one mark without change — yet if a person distinguishes the real truth as having two shifting marks, they cannot bring forth the real at all; they must contemplate emptiness before they can enter the uncontaminated. As Subhūti contemplated emptiness, and Kauṇḍinya realized the wisdom of no-arising. As for the claim that their moral restraint is incomplete: if one is able to contemplate emptiness and thereby attain the precept conascent with the path, this is itself already the complete precept. Hence the Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "The true nature and mark of every dharma abides forever, unchanging — the two lesser vehicles, too, attain this alike, and yet are not thereby named buddhas." Hence it is known that the language of "eternal abiding" is used generally, and this explanation may indeed be given.

若不作此釋，三藏不說大乘常住，聲聞那得具聲聞道、具禁戒耶？若作此釋，道共戒無失，彌益其美。又舉例釋者，如《大品》云：「婬欲障生梵天，何況菩提？」為生梵天，須斷欲；欲得菩提，斷二邊欲。欲名雖同，其意則異。此義亦爾。欲入真諦，須知無為常不變易；欲入實相，亦知常住一相不變。知常語同，大小則異。故三藏止觀不知圓、實，不違經。《勝鬘》云：「若不知常住，所有三歸皆不成就。」此云何通？遠尋根本，三乘初業不愚於法。若取四念處聞慧為初者，此初知真諦常住，不起六十二見；以無倚著心，賢聖成就。此釋同前意也。若古昔為初業者，先發菩提心，早知常住；畏怖生死，退大取小。法才王子及《涅槃》中退轉菩薩，從初已來，歸依一體三寶，熏修戒善。有受法，無捨法；心無盡故，戒亦無盡；一切戒善為此所熏。譬如大地冥益樹木；樹木萌芽悉得成就。小乘歸戒不離菩薩戒，菩薩戒力能成就之，即此義也。若不作初業知常，三藏歸戒羯磨悉不成就。若作此釋，於大小兩經義無相違。

Were this explanation not given, since the Tripiṭaka teaching itself never speaks of the Mahāyāna's own eternal abiding, how could the śrāvaka possibly be complete in the śrāvaka's own path and complete in its own precepts and restraints at all? Given this explanation, the precept conascent with the path suffers no defect, and its own excellence is only further enhanced. Again, citing a further example to clarify: as the Larger Sūtra says, "Lustful desire obstructs rebirth in the Brahmā heavens — how much more, then, bodhi itself?" To be reborn in the Brahmā heavens, desire must be severed; to attain bodhi, desire toward both extremes must be severed. Though the word "desire" is the same in both cases, the sense differs. This present matter is exactly the same: to enter the real truth, one must know the unconditioned as eternal, unchanging; to enter the true mark of reality, one likewise must know the eternal abiding of the one mark, unchanging. The language of "knowing the eternal" is the same in both cases, yet the lesser and the great differ. Hence the Tripiṭaka teaching's own calming-and-contemplation, not knowing the perfect and the real, does not thereby contradict the sūtras. The Śrīmālā Sūtra says: "If one does not know the eternal abiding, none of the three refuges can be truly accomplished." How is this to be reconciled? Tracing this back to its very root: the three vehicles' own beginning practice is not, in fact, foolish with regard to the Dharma. If the hearing-wisdom of the four foundations of mindfulness is taken as the beginning, this beginning already knows the real truth's own eternal abiding, and does not give rise to any of the sixty-two views; with a mind free of any dependence or attachment, the accomplishment of worthy and sage alike is achieved. This explanation accords with the sense given before. As for those who, in ancient times, were at the very beginning of practice: they first gave rise to the bodhi-mind, and thereby already knew the eternal abiding early on; but, fearing birth-and-death, they retreated from the great and took up the small instead. Prince Dharmatiḥ, and the retrogressing bodhisattvas spoken of in the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, from the very first, took refuge in the single essence of the Three Jewels, cultivating and perfuming precepts and goodness. There is a dharma of receiving, but no dharma of relinquishing it; since the mind itself is inexhaustible, the precepts, too, are inexhaustible; every wholesome precept is perfumed by this same mind. It is like the great earth, imperceptibly nourishing trees, so that their sprouts and shoots all come to accomplishment. The Hīnayāna's own refuge and precepts are never truly apart from the bodhisattva's own precepts — the bodhisattva precept's own power is able to bring them to accomplishment as well — this is exactly that same sense. Were one not to grant that the beginning of practice already knows the eternal, then the Tripiṭaka teaching's own refuge, precepts, and formal ordination procedures would, every one, fail to be truly accomplished at all. Granting this explanation, the sense of both the Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna sūtras is left without any contradiction between them.

Reading note: The fascicle's final stretch takes up a genuinely delicate question: does an arhat, who has attained real liberation by the Tripiṭaka teaching's own lights, actually know that the Three Jewels abide eternally — the Mahāyāna's own central claim about ultimate reality? Answering "no" risks making the arhat's attainment sound worthless; answering "yes" risks collapsing the very distinction between Hīnayāna and Mahāyāna the whole fascicle has spent its length constructing. Zhiyi's resolution — the arhat "knows only so far as their own principle extends," reaching real but partial truth through their own power, unable to know the further eternal truth without direct instruction — lets both scriptures stand without contradiction, and closes the fascicle on a note of reconciliation rather than final rupture: the Hīnayāna's own refuge and precepts are, in the end, "never truly apart from the bodhisattva's own precepts," nourished by the same single root all along, "like the great earth, imperceptibly nourishing trees." This closes topic five, partial and complete teachings, and with it, the whole of fascicle three.
