The Great Cart: Completing the Ten Modes

This page continues Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Mohe Zhiguan (摩訶止觀, "The Great Calming and Contemplation"). Fascicle 6 completed the fourth of the ten modes of contemplation, comprehensively breaking through every dharma, for the first object of contemplation. Fascicle 7 finishes the remaining six modes for that same first object — the five aggregates — in full: distinguishing blockage from passage, cultivating the branches of the path, auxiliary remedies, knowing one's own stage, bearing with adversity, and freedom from attachment to the Dharma. It closes by showing that these ten modes together are exactly the Lotus Sūtra's own parable of the great cart, then extends the entire method from seated contemplation into the six ordinary activities and six sense-doors of daily life. By the end of this fascicle, the complete ten-mode treatment of the first of the ten objects of contemplation is finished. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Mode Five: Distinguishing Blockage from Passage 識通塞

Chinese Source

第五、識通塞者，亦名「知得失」，亦名「知字非字」。如上破法遍應通入無生；若不入者，當尋得失，必滯是非，不得一向作解。何者？若同外道，愛著觀空智慧，宜以四句遍破，能破如所破，令眾塞得通。若不執觀空智慧，則能破不如所破，但破塞、存通。如除膜養珠，破賊護將。若爾，即大導師善知通塞，將導眾人能過五百由旬。舊云：「六地見思盡為三百，七地、八地為四百，九地、十地為五百」。此義與《釋論》乖，論以二乘為四百；二乘之道非七地、八地。《攝大乘》人以三界為三百，方便、因緣兩生死足為五百。則攝義不盡，更有有後生死、無後生死，屬何百耶？地人以十信、住、行、向、地為五百。此與《法華》乖。《法華》過三百由旬作化城，此則二百由旬作化城。復有人解三界為三百，二乘足為五百。此義三失：一、出三界外立化城，云何二乘出三界外不入城，更行四百、五百？四百、五百之外更無化城，何所可入而稱二乘？二者滅化城方可得進，城猶未滅，而輒進四百、五百？三者二乘共入化城，云何聲聞為四百，支佛為五百？有人以五住煩惱為五百。然二乘已斷四住，應是四百由旬外立化城。有人以斷三界思為三百，塵沙為四百，無明為五百。此亦不然，由旬本譬煩惱，云何見多而不數，思少為三百？此之名義，本出《法華》。《法華》舉五百為譬，本以生死險道，導師觀知合之，應作三番明五百，乃會經耳：一就生死處所，二就煩惱，三就智慧。諸師之釋，方圓動息，不與文會，如持一孔之匙開三須之鑰。初家約通位就四百立化城；攝家約生死割二種於荒外；地家約別位在界內立化城；次家徑侹不待開權即自顯實。人師過如此，《釋論》意云何？論有二文，初以二乘為四百而止，不作五百；後文以二乘為一百。今通之，論明通意，通家以真諦為極，過三界已，未破化城，但入涅槃，即指涅槃為四百耳。而復以二乘為一百者，更明出假菩薩從空出假，非涅槃為一百，入三界為三百。作如此消文，於經論無妨也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, distinguishing blockage from passage: also named "knowing gain and loss," also named "knowing whether a character is truly a character." As explained above, comprehensively breaking through every dharma ought to lead directly through to the unborn; should one fail to pass through in this way, one must then search out gain and loss, since one will otherwise inevitably remain blocked in disputes of right and wrong, unable to form a single, unified understanding. Why is this? Should one, like the non-Buddhist paths, cling with attachment to the very wisdom that contemplates emptiness, one ought to use the four propositions to break through comprehensively, so that what breaks through and what is broken through both alike become blockage turned to passage. Should one not cling fixedly to the wisdom that contemplates emptiness, then what breaks through is unlike what is broken through, and one merely breaks through the blockage while preserving the passage. It is like removing the membrane while still nurturing the pearl within, or breaking through the bandit while still protecting the general. If this is so, then the great guide, skillfully knowing blockage and passage, is able to lead the assembly through, passing five hundred yojanas. It has been said, in the past, that "the sixth ground, views and habituated thought exhausted, is three hundred; the seventh and eighth grounds are four hundred; the ninth and tenth grounds are five hundred." This sense conflicts with the Treatise, which takes the two lesser vehicles as four hundred — yet the path of the two lesser vehicles is not the seventh or eighth ground at all. Masters of the *Compendium of the Mahāyāna* take the three realms as three hundred, with the two kinds of birth-and-death — of skillful means, and of causes and conditions — together making up five hundred; yet this fails to gather in the full sense, since there is, further, birth-and-death with a subsequent birth and birth-and-death without one — to which hundred would these belong? Masters of the Dilun school take the ten faiths, abidings, practices, dedications, and grounds as the five hundred — but this conflicts with the Lotus Sūtra, in which the phantom city is built beyond three hundred yojanas, whereas this account would build it at merely two hundred. There are, further, those who explain the three realms as three hundred, with the two lesser vehicles making up the full five hundred — but this account has three faults: first, if the phantom city is established beyond the three realms, how could the two lesser vehicles, already beyond the three realms, fail to enter that very city, and instead continue on for a further four hundred, five hundred? Beyond four hundred, five hundred, there is no further phantom city at all — into what, then, could they possibly enter, to be called "the two lesser vehicles"? Second, only once the phantom city is dissolved can one advance further — yet if the city has not yet been dissolved, how could one abruptly advance to four hundred, five hundred? Third, the two lesser vehicles enter the very same phantom city together — how, then, could the śrāvaka be four hundred and the pratyekabuddha five hundred? Some take the five abodes of affliction as the five hundred — yet the two lesser vehicles have already severed four of these abodes, and so the phantom city ought to be established beyond four hundred, not five. Some take the severing of habituated thought across the three realms as three hundred, dust-and-sand as four hundred, and ignorance as five hundred — but this, too, is mistaken, since a yojana is fundamentally a simile for affliction: how could views, though more numerous, go uncounted, while habituated thought, though fewer, be reckoned as the full three hundred? This very name and sense originates, at root, from the Lotus Sūtra itself. The Lotus Sūtra raises five hundred as a simile, originally likening it to the perilous road of birth-and-death, which the guide surveys and understands, matching it accordingly — this ought to be explained through three separate passes in order to actually accord with the sūtra's own text: first, in terms of the very location of birth-and-death; second, in terms of affliction; third, in terms of contemplative wisdom. Every prior master's own explanation, whether too broad or too narrow, whether moving or resting, fails to accord with the text itself — like holding a single-holed key and trying to open a lock that needs three. The first school, in terms of the shared teaching's own stage, establishes the phantom city at four hundred; the Compendium school, in terms of birth-and-death, divides it into two kinds set beyond the wilderness; the Dilun school, in terms of the distinct teaching's own stages, establishes the phantom city within the three realms; the next school proceeds directly, without waiting to open the provisional, manifesting the real of itself. Since every one of these masters has erred in this way, what, then, is the actual sense of the Treatise? The Treatise gives two passages: the first takes the two lesser vehicles only as far as four hundred, without reaching five hundred; the latter passage takes the two lesser vehicles as merely one hundred. Reconciling these now: the Treatise clarifies the shared teaching's own sense, in which the shared teaching takes the real truth as its own ultimate limit — once one has passed beyond the three realms, the phantom city not yet broken through, one simply enters nirvāṇa, and it is this very nirvāṇa that is pointed to as the four hundred mark. And as for further taking the two lesser vehicles as merely one hundred, this clarifies, further, the bodhisattva who emerges into the provisional, emerging into the provisional from emptiness — nirvāṇa itself not being the one hundred mark, but rather entering the three realms being the three hundred mark. Reading the text in this way brings no difficulty at all, whether for the sūtra or for the treatise.

今明五百由旬者，一、約生死處所，謂三界果報為三百，方便有餘土、實報無礙土是為五百由旬處所；次、約煩惱者，所謂見諦惑為一百，五下分為二百，五上分為三百，塵沙為四百，無明為五百；次、約觀智者，空觀智知三百，假觀智知四百，中觀智知五百。此與文會，無前諸師過也。又諸師判位遼遠，初心行人尚未斷見，何由超過五百由旬乎？今論由旬，有橫通塞、有竪通塞。橫者具約三法：苦集為塞，道滅為通；無明十二因緣為塞，無明滅為通；六蔽覆心為塞，六度為通。竪通塞者：見思分段生死為塞，從假入空觀為通；無知方便生死為塞，從空入假觀為通；無明因緣生死等為塞，中道正觀為通。今當以橫織竪檢校通塞。如從假入空破諸見思，單、複、具足、無言等見，九九八十一思。如斯諸惑本是污穢，增長煩惱遮塞行人，那忽取著謂是謂非？起諸結業漏落生死，唯見苦集不見道滅。既不識見思中四諦，是事不知，名為「無明」，乃至老死。但構因緣，無明不滅。不滅故，堅著叵捨，唯在此岸，不到彼岸。《大經》云：「童子飢時取糞中果，智人呵之，赧然有愧。」失於淨法，是名為「塞」。若於諸見，介爾起心，知無性實、無常、無主；倒破則無業，無業則無果，是名為「道」，道故有滅。若識四諦，則無無明，亦無老死。因緣壞故，則捨諸有到於彼岸。當用此意歷一一心、歷一一能、歷一一所。若起三塞，破之令通；若是三通，養令成就。復次，體見即空，能體亦即空。如羅漢之心尚名「無漏五陰」，我觀未真，那得非陰？若計陰實，則結業生死。若不識陰四諦，即是無明。若愛觀空智慧，則不能捨；用即空意歷一一心、歷一一能、歷一一所。若有三塞，破之令通；若是三通，養令成就。則巧過見思之塞，善通三百由旬也。

Here, then, is clarified the sense of the five hundred yojanas: first, in terms of the very location of birth-and-death — the three realms' own retribution is three hundred, while the land of skillful means with residue and the land of real reward without obstruction together make up the five hundred yojanas' own location; second, in terms of affliction — the affliction of seeing the truth is one hundred, the five lower fetters are two hundred, the five higher fetters are three hundred, dust-and-sand is four hundred, and ignorance is five hundred; third, in terms of contemplative wisdom — the wisdom of the contemplation of emptiness knows three hundred, the wisdom of the contemplation of the provisional knows four hundred, and the wisdom of the contemplation of the middle knows five hundred. This accords with the text itself, and is free of every fault found in the prior masters' own accounts. Further, since every prior master places these stages so distant, and a beginning practitioner has not yet even severed view, how could he possibly pass beyond five hundred yojanas at all? Discussing yojanas here, there is both horizontal blockage-and-passage and vertical blockage-and-passage. The horizontal, taken fully in terms of the threefold dharma: suffering and its origin are blockage, the path and cessation are passage; ignorance and the twelve links of dependent origination are blockage, the extinguishing of ignorance is passage; the six coverings of the mind are blockage, the six pāramitās are passage. As for vertical blockage-and-passage: the segmented birth-and-death of views and habituated thought is blockage, the contemplation of entering emptiness from the provisional is passage; the unmanifest birth-and-death of skillful means is blockage, the contemplation of entering the provisional from emptiness is passage; the birth-and-death caused by ignorance and its conditions is blockage, the direct contemplation of the middle way is passage. Here, then, one should weave the horizontal together with the vertical to examine blockage and passage. Thus, entering emptiness from the provisional breaks through every view — the single, compound, complete, and wordless views — and the eighty-one categories of habituated thought, nine-times-nine. Afflictions of this kind are, at root, defilement itself, increasing affliction and blocking the practitioner — how, then, could one suddenly cling to them, calling one thing right and another wrong? Giving rise to every binding karma, one leaks and falls into birth-and-death, seeing only suffering and its origin, never the path and cessation. Since one does not recognize the Four Noble Truths within view and habituated thought, this very not-knowing is named "ignorance," extending all the way to old age and death. Simply constructing cause and condition after cause and condition, ignorance is never extinguished; and being unextinguished, one clings to it firmly, unable to let go, remaining forever on this shore, never reaching the other. The Great Sūtra says: "A child, hungry, takes fruit from a pile of dung — the wise rebuke him for it, and he blushes, ashamed." Losing the pure Dharma in just this way is named "blockage." Should one, with respect to every view, give rise to even the slightest thought recognizing that it has no fixed nature, no permanence, no master, then inversion is thereby broken through, and where inversion is broken there is no karma, and where there is no karma there is no fruit — this is named "the path," and, being the path, it brings cessation. Should one recognize the Four Noble Truths, there is then no ignorance, and no old age and death either. Since cause and condition are thereby destroyed, one abandons every mode of existence and reaches the other shore. One should apply this very sense across each and every mind, each and every faculty, each and every object. Should the threefold blockage arise, one breaks through it so that it becomes passage; should this same threefold passage already be present, one nurtures it so that it comes to fruition. Furthermore, embodying view is itself already emptiness, and the very faculty that embodies it is likewise already empty. Even the arhat's own mind is still named "the uncontaminated five aggregates" — my own contemplation, not yet genuine, how could it fail to be an aggregate as well? Should one calculate the aggregates as real, this results in binding karma and birth-and-death; should one fail to recognize the Four Noble Truths within the aggregates, this is itself already ignorance. Should one cling with attachment to the very wisdom that contemplates emptiness, one is then unable to let go of it — one applies, instead, the very sense of emptiness-identity across each and every mind, each and every faculty, each and every object. Should the threefold blockage be present, one breaks through it so that it becomes passage; should this same threefold passage already be present, one nurtures it so that it comes to fruition. This, then, skillfully passes beyond the blockage of view and habituated thought, and skillfully passes through the full three hundred yojanas.

Weaving Horizontal and Vertical: Entering the Provisional and the Middle 橫織竪檢校 · 從空入假 · 中道正觀

Chinese Source

次、用橫織竪檢校從空入假觀通塞者，此則易解。於病法、藥法、授藥法，於一一法、一一能、一一所，明識諦、緣、度。若起三塞，破之令通；若有三通，養令成就。則過無知之塞，通四百由旬。次、用橫織竪檢校中道正觀者：於無明、法性、真緣等，一一法、一一能、一一所，明識諦、緣、度。若起三塞，破之令通；若有三通，養令成就。則過無明之塞，通五百由旬。若作如此論通塞者，次第竪論六地、初地，動經劫數塞乃得通。《大經》云：「須陀洹者八萬劫到，乃至支佛十千劫到。」到菩薩初發心住。此論聖位，何益初心行人者乎？復次，約橫別論通塞者，如《大品經》云「有菩薩從初發心即與薩婆若相應」者，與空相應也。若初未相應，當用諦、緣、度檢一一心。若有三塞，破之令通；若有三通，養令成就，得過三百由旬。又云：「有菩薩從初發心即能遊戲神通、淨佛國土。」此是出假之意。若初發心修假，亦用諦、緣、度檢一一心，破塞養通，過四百由旬。又云：「有菩薩從初發心即能坐道場成正覺。」此即中意。若初發心修中，亦用諦、緣、度檢一一心，破塞養通，過五百由旬也。如此說者，雖初心得論通塞，而三法各別。《大論》引三喻，一則步涉，二則乘馬，三則神通。步、馬兩行，須知通塞；神通無礙，塞不能遮。山壁皆虛，何通可擇？初觀喻步，次觀喻馬，後觀喻飛。三義分張，亦非今所用也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, weaving the horizontal together with the vertical to examine blockage and passage in the contemplation of entering the provisional from emptiness: this is readily understood. With respect to the dharma of illness, the dharma of medicine, and the dharma of administering medicine, across each and every dharma, each and every faculty, each and every object, one clarifies and recognizes the truth, the causal condition, and the perfection involved. Should the threefold blockage arise, one breaks through it so that it becomes passage; should this same threefold passage already be present, one nurtures it so that it comes to fruition. This, then, passes beyond the blockage of unknowing, and passes through the full four hundred yojanas. Next, weaving the horizontal together with the vertical to examine the direct contemplation of the middle way: with respect to ignorance, the Dharma-nature, and the real and the conditioned, across each and every dharma, each and every faculty, each and every object, one clarifies and recognizes the truth, the causal condition, and the perfection involved. Should the threefold blockage arise, one breaks through it so that it becomes passage; should this same threefold passage already be present, one nurtures it so that it comes to fruition. This, then, passes beyond the blockage of ignorance, and passes through the full five hundred yojanas. Should one discuss blockage and passage strictly in this sequential, vertical manner — the sixth ground, the first ground — this would take kalpa after kalpa before blockage could ever become passage. The Great Sūtra says: "The stream-enterer arrives after eighty thousand kalpas; the pratyekabuddha arrives after ten thousand kalpas" — arriving, that is, at the bodhisattva's own first abiding, where the resolve first arises. This discusses only the sagely stages — what benefit could this possibly bring to the beginning practitioner? Furthermore, discussing blockage and passage horizontally, taken separately: as the Larger Sūtra says, "There is a bodhisattva who, from the very first resolve, is already corresponding with omniscience itself" — this is corresponding with emptiness. Should one not yet correspond with it at the very outset, one should apply the truth, the causal condition, and the perfection to examine each and every mind. Should the threefold blockage be present, one breaks through it so that it becomes passage; should this same threefold passage already be present, one nurtures it so that it comes to fruition, thereby passing beyond the full three hundred yojanas. It further says: "There is a bodhisattva who, from the very first resolve, is already able to roam freely in spiritual powers and purify buddha-lands" — this is the sense of emerging into the provisional. Should one, at the very first resolve, cultivate the provisional, one likewise applies the truth, the causal condition, and the perfection to examine each and every mind, breaking through blockage and nurturing passage, passing beyond the full four hundred yojanas. It further says: "There is a bodhisattva who, from the very first resolve, is already able to sit at the seat of enlightenment and accomplish correct awakening" — this is precisely the sense of the middle. Should one, at the very first resolve, cultivate the middle, one likewise applies the truth, the causal condition, and the perfection to examine each and every mind, breaking through blockage and nurturing passage, passing beyond the full five hundred yojanas. Explained in this way, though the beginning practitioner is indeed able to discuss blockage and passage, the three dharmas still remain separate from one another. The Great Treatise cites three similes: the first is traveling on foot; the second is riding on horseback; the third is spiritual power. Both traveling on foot and riding on horseback require knowing blockage from passage; but spiritual power meets with no obstruction at all, and blockage is powerless to obstruct it — mountains and cliffs are all alike insubstantial to it, so what "passage" is there left to choose? The first contemplation is likened to traveling on foot; the second contemplation, to riding on horseback; the third contemplation, to flight. Yet these three senses remain divided from one another, and this, too, is not what is used here.

若竪論三觀，兩觀當地為通，望上為塞；若後一觀勝下為通，隔小為塞。橫論三觀，當分為通，不相收為塞。法相淺深任有通塞，況復於中起苦、集、無明、蔽等？是故皆塞，無復有通。若一心三觀法相，即破竪中之通塞；三觀一心破橫中之通塞。空即三觀，故破步涉山壁，三百之通塞；假即三觀，破乘馬四百之通塞；中即三觀，破神通之通塞。良以一心能即空、假、中者，一切山河石壁、眾魔群道皆如虛空。一心三觀遊之無礙，終不去下陵高，避山從谷。觸處諸塞皆通無礙，能過五百由旬到於寶所。是名為「通」。通本對塞，既觸處如空，則無復有塞；無塞則無通。若於無塞、無通起苦、集、無明、障蔽者，非但失於神通，亦失馬、步。能破如所破，字為非字，如彼蟲道偶得三觀之名，是蟲不知是字非字。若於一一法、一一能、一一所，皆即空、即假、即中，具諦緣度，是名無通、無塞，雙照通塞。是為智者識字非字；亦名良醫知得、知失。於無生門明識通塞者，於餘法門亦如是。是為初心過五百由旬，應明六即義云云。

Should one discuss the three contemplations vertically, the two earlier contemplations, taken each on its own ground, are passage, while measured against what lies above them, they are blockage; and should the final contemplation surpass what lies beneath it, this is passage, while being cut off from the lesser is blockage. Should one discuss the three contemplations horizontally, each taken within its own proper division is passage, while their failure to gather one another in is blockage. The marks of the dharma, shallow or deep, admit of blockage and passage in whichever way one takes them — how much more so, then, once suffering, its origin, ignorance, and covering affliction arise within them? This being so, all of it is blockage, with no passage left at all. Should the mark of the dharma be understood as the three contemplations in a single mind, this itself breaks through the blockage-and-passage found within the vertical account; the three contemplations as a single mind breaks through the blockage-and-passage found within the horizontal account. Emptiness is itself the three contemplations, and hence breaks through the blockage-and-passage of traveling on foot across mountains and cliffs, the three hundred mark; the provisional is itself the three contemplations, and breaks through the blockage-and-passage of riding on horseback, the four hundred mark; the middle is itself the three contemplations, and breaks through the blockage-and-passage of spiritual power. Precisely because a single mind is able to be at once empty, provisional, and the middle, every mountain, river, and cliff of stone, every demon and every band of the non-Buddhist paths, are all alike as insubstantial as empty space itself. The single mind's three contemplations roam through this without any obstruction whatsoever, never once needing to descend from the low to climb the high, or avoid the mountain to follow the valley instead. Wherever one turns, every blockage there becomes passage without obstruction, able to pass the full five hundred yojanas and arrive at the treasure-place itself. This is named "passage." Passage is, at root, set against blockage — but once wherever one turns is already like empty space itself, there is no longer any blockage left at all; and where there is no blockage, there is, correspondingly, no passage either. Should one, within this very absence of blockage and passage, still give rise to suffering, its origin, ignorance, and obstructing affliction, this is not merely the loss of spiritual power, but the loss of the horse and of one's own feet as well. What breaks through being identical with what is broken through, a character being no longer a mere character at all — it is like that worm's own trail, which happened by chance to form the shape of the three contemplations' own name, and yet the worm itself knows nothing of whether this is truly a character or not. Should one, with respect to each and every dharma, each and every faculty, each and every object, find them all at once empty, at once provisional, at once the middle, complete with the truth, the causal condition, and the perfection, this is named "neither passage nor blockage," illuminating both blockage and passage at once. This is exactly what is meant by the wise recognizing whether a character is truly a character; also named the skilled physician knowing gain from loss. As it is when clarified through the gate of the unborn, distinguishing blockage from passage, so it is through every other Dharma-gate as well. This is the beginning practitioner passing the full five hundred yojanas, and one ought now to clarify the sense of the six identities accordingly.

Reading note: The four-round demolition of rival readings of the Lotus Sūtra's "five hundred yojanas" — each accused of a specific structural failure (mismatched boundaries, doubled cities, uncounted afflictions) — is characteristic of how this treatise handles inherited exegesis: not by picking a winner among existing schools but by showing that only a genuinely new three-pass structure (location, affliction, wisdom) actually fits the text throughout. The closing point that "spiritual power meets with no obstruction at all... mountains and cliffs are all alike insubstantial to it" answers the chapter's real question: blockage and passage are not properties of the terrain but of the traveler's own mode of movement, so a single mind's three contemplations simply have no terrain left capable of blocking them.

Question: blockage-and-passage, gain-and-loss, and whether-a-character-is-truly-a-character — are these one and the same, or different? Answer: these are a single sense, spoken of in various different ways, though a certain distinction still remains: blockage-and-passage is discussed in terms of understanding; gain-and-loss is discussed in terms of practice; whether-a-character-is-truly-a-character is discussed in terms of the teaching itself. The Sūtra of Golden Light says: "Correct hearing, correct listening, correct discernment, correct understanding of conditions, correct and complete awakening." Knowing whether a character is truly a character is correct hearing and correct listening; knowing gain and loss is correct discernment and correct understanding of conditions; knowing blockage and passage is correct and complete awakening. Though this distinction remains, all three alike reveal one and the same underlying sense. Question: does horizontal blockage block vertical passage? Does vertical blockage block horizontal passage? Does horizontal passage open vertical blockage? Does vertical passage open horizontal blockage? Answer: taken one way, yes; taken a second way, no. As for the "yes": since ignorance is itself already view and habituated thought, how could this fail to be a horizontal obstruction as well? And since the wisdom of the middle heals everything without exception, how could it fail to open both horizontal and vertical blockage alike? This is the sense in which the answer is, taken one way, "yes." Explained a second way, however: horizontal blockage is a near obstruction, and is unable to block vertical passage; horizontal passage is of weak strength, and is unable to open vertical blockage; vertical blockage lies deep and far, and does not constitute a horizontal obstruction; and vertical passage corresponds only to its own proper counterpart, and does not open horizontal blockage either.

Reading note: The closing "yes and no" is not evasion but precision: the treatise routinely gives a sweeping first answer and then immediately qualifies it, because the sweeping answer is true at the level of ultimate principle (ignorance and views are never, in the end, separate obstructions) while the qualified answer is true at the level of how practice actually proceeds (a near, shallow obstruction genuinely cannot block a distant, deep passage, whatever their ultimate identity). Mode Five is now complete; the fascicle turns next to the sixth mode, cultivating the branches of the path — the thirty-seven factors traditionally associated with the Hīnayāna, about to receive the same one-mind-three-truths treatment.

Sixth, clarifying the cultivation and regulation of the branches of the path: the branches of the path admit of four approaches: first, taken on their own terms; second, mutually gathering one another in; third, mapped to stage; fourth, generating one another in sequence. First, clarifying what is taken on its own terms: one need not have every branch complete before the path can be attained. Whether three, four, two, five, a single seven, or merely eight — taken on its own terms, each is already the path, hence it is said, "One ought to attain the path relying on the foundations of mindfulness alone." It is also said, "This alone is the seat of enlightenment"; and, "This alone is the Great Vehicle." Since this holds for the foundations of mindfulness, it likewise holds for every other branch. This is the branches of the path taken on their own terms, not yet their mutual regulation. Second, clarifying their mutual gathering-in: the single dharma of the foundations of mindfulness already gathers in every other branch. Citing the Treatise's own text: "Since the foundations of mindfulness already gather in every other branch, every other branch, in turn, gathers in the foundations of mindfulness as well." This is the branches of the path mutually gathering one another in, likewise not yet their mutual regulation. Third, mapped to stage: the foundations of mindfulness correspond to their own proper stage; the four right efforts are the stage of warmth; the four bases of supernormal power are the stage of the summit; the five faculties are the stage of forbearance; the five powers are the stage of the supreme mundane dharma; the eightfold path is the stage of seeing the truth; the seven factors of awakening are the stage of the path of cultivation. This is mapping them to stage, likewise not yet their mutual regulation. Fourth, generating one another in sequence: cultivating the foundations of mindfulness is able to generate the four right efforts; the four right efforts bring forth the four bases of supernormal power; the four bases of supernormal power generate the five faculties; the five faculties generate the five powers; the five powers generate the seven factors of awakening; the seven factors of awakening enter the eightfold path — this is their skillful cultivation and regulation. The precepts, concentration, wisdom, and the rest are all named "correct." "A mind that is pure and constantly unified is thereby able to see prajñā itself" — this is their generating one another in sequence, and is likewise their regulation. As for why this is needed: though comprehensively breaking through every dharma and distinguishing blockage from passage have already been discussed above, without regulating the branches of the path, how could one possibly correspond swiftly with the genuine Dharma at all? The genuine Dharma is named "the uncontaminated"; the branches of the path are "the contaminated" — yet the contaminated is able to serve as the very expedient means for the uncontaminated. Should this expedient means be misapplied, the genuine principle becomes difficult to reach. It is like the method of brewing wine: should the yeast and warmth be applied just rightly, water is transformed into wine; should the leaven be misjudged, the flavor fails to form at all. The Great Treatise says: "The thirty-seven factors are the very dharma of practicing the path; the city of nirvāṇa has three gates." The three gates are the near cause; the branches of the path are the distant cause. For this very reason, the branches of the path must be cultivated and regulated.

Not Merely a Hīnayāna Teaching: Contaminated and Uncontaminated Across the Four Teachings 道品非小乘 · 有漏無漏

Chinese Source

問：道品是二乘法，云何是菩薩道耶？答：《大論》呵此問：「誰作是語？三藏、摩訶衍皆不作是說，那得獨云是小乘法？」《淨名》云：「道品善知識，由是成正覺；道品是道場，亦是摩訶衍。」《涅槃》云：「能修八正道者，即見佛性，名得醍醐。」《大集》云：「三十七品是菩薩寶炬陀羅尼。」如此等經皆明道品，何時獨是小乘？若《大經》云「三十七品是涅槃因，非大涅槃因；無量阿僧祇助菩提法是大涅槃因」者，道品之外無別有道品，如四諦外無第五諦。一種苦集如爪上土，分別苦集有無量相，如十方土。直明一三十七品是涅槃因，復有無量三十七助道品名大涅槃因。云何無量？有四種道諦故，有十六門故。又有漏道品：欲界二十二、未到三十六、初禪三十七，皆有漏道品，如乳；三藏道品如酪；通教道品如生酥；別教如熟酥；圓教如醍醐。《大經》之文義合於此，非道品外別有助法也。或言三十七品是助道；或言是正道。《大論》云：「是菩薩道。」此文似正也。《淨名》云：「道品善知識，由是成正覺。」此文似助也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: the branches of the path are the dharma of the two lesser vehicles — how, then, could they be the bodhisattva's own path? Answer: the Great Treatise rebukes this very question: "Who says such a thing? Neither the Tripiṭaka teaching nor the Mahāyāna itself ever makes such a claim — how, then, could one insist, one-sidedly, that this is merely the Hīnayāna's own dharma?" The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "The branches of the path are one's own good spiritual friend, and it is through them that correct awakening is accomplished; the branches of the path are themselves the seat of enlightenment, and are themselves the Great Vehicle." The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "One who is able to cultivate the eightfold path thereby sees the buddha-nature directly, and is named one who has attained the ghee itself." The Great Collection Sūtra says: "The thirty-seven factors are the bodhisattva's own precious-torch dhāraṇī." Sūtras of this kind all, without exception, clarify the branches of the path — when, then, could they ever be exclusively the Hīnayāna's own? As for the Great Sūtra's own saying that "the thirty-seven factors are the cause of nirvāṇa, but not the cause of the great nirvāṇa; it is the boundless, incalculable dharmas that assist bodhi that are the cause of the great nirvāṇa" — there is, in truth, no separate set of branches of the path beyond these same branches, just as there is no fifth truth beyond the Four Noble Truths themselves. A single instance of suffering and its origin is like the dirt beneath a single fingernail; distinguishing suffering and its origin into their boundless further aspects is like the dirt of the ten directions entire. Directly clarifying a single instance of the thirty-seven factors as the cause of nirvāṇa, there are, further, boundless further instances of the thirty-seven branches assisting the path, named the cause of the great nirvāṇa. How could there be boundless instances? Because there are four kinds of the truth of the path, and because there are sixteen gates. There are, further, contaminated branches of the path: the desire realm's own twenty-two, the not-yet-arrived state's own thirty-six, and the first dhyāna's own thirty-seven — all of these are contaminated branches of the path, likened to milk; the Tripiṭaka teaching's own branches of the path are likened to cream; the shared teaching's own, to curdled butter; the distinct teaching's own, to clarified butter; the perfect teaching's own, to ghee itself. The Great Sūtra's own text and sense accord exactly with this, and there is no separate assisting dharma to be found beyond the branches of the path themselves. Some say the thirty-seven factors are merely an auxiliary to the path; others say they are the correct path itself. The Great Treatise says, "This is the bodhisattva's own path" — this text seems to favor the latter. The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says, "The branches of the path are one's own good spiritual friend, and it is through them that correct awakening is accomplished" — this text seems to favor the former.

又若言三十七品是有漏者，云何言七覺是修道？《法華》云：「無漏根、力、覺、道之財。」云何八正在七覺前？此應三句分別：一、三十七品皆有漏，二、皆是無漏，三、亦有漏亦無漏。如《大論》云：「修八正道得初善有漏五陰。」善有漏五陰即是煖法，煖法之前尚得修於八正道。云何修邪？初從師受法，繫心憶念名「念處」；為求此法，勤而行之名「正勤」；一心中修名「如意足」；五善根生名「根」；根增長名「力」；分別道用名「七覺」；安隱道中行名「八正道」。能如是修，得善有漏五陰。當知道品皆是有漏。皆是無漏者，即是見諦思惟所行道品，一向是無漏。《法華》之文意在此也。從來雖言有漏中得修八正、七覺等，未有文證。而《毘婆沙》云：「若八正在七覺後，亦得是有漏，亦得是無漏。」何以故？依八正入見諦，即是亦無漏；「若八正在七覺前，一向是無漏。」此則可解。引《婆沙》文證成二意。又亦漏無漏即是對位意也。諸道諦三十七品今不具記，但明無作道諦三十七品，成於一心三觀義也。

Further, should one say the thirty-seven factors are contaminated, how, then, could it be said that the seven factors of awakening belong to the path of cultivation? The Lotus Sūtra says, "The uncontaminated wealth of the faculties, powers, awakening-factors, and path" — how, then, could the eightfold path come before the seven factors of awakening? This ought to be distinguished through three propositions: first, that the thirty-seven factors are, every one, contaminated; second, that they are, every one, uncontaminated; third, that they are both contaminated and uncontaminated at once. As the Great Treatise says: "Cultivating the eightfold path attains the initial, wholesome, contaminated five aggregates." The wholesome, contaminated five aggregates are precisely the dharma of warmth, and even before the dharma of warmth is reached, one is still able to cultivate the eightfold path. How, then, does one cultivate it in this way? At the very outset, receiving the dharma from a teacher, binding the mind in constant recollection, is named "the foundations of mindfulness"; seeking this dharma and diligently carrying it out is named "the four right efforts"; cultivating it with a single, unified mind is named "the four bases of supernormal power"; the five wholesome roots being brought forth is named "the faculties"; these faculties growing in strength is named "the powers"; distinguishing the function of the path is named "the seven factors of awakening"; and proceeding along the calm and settled path is named "the eightfold path." Cultivated in exactly this way, one attains the wholesome, contaminated five aggregates. One should thus know that the branches of the path can indeed be, every one, contaminated. As for their being, every one, uncontaminated: this refers to the branches of the path as practiced within the seeing of the truth and habituated reflection upon it, which are, without exception, uncontaminated throughout. This is precisely the sense intended by the Lotus Sūtra's own text. Though it has long been said that the eightfold path and the seven factors of awakening and the rest can be cultivated even within the contaminated, there has, until now, been no textual proof of this. Yet the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā says: "Should the eightfold path come after the seven factors of awakening, it may indeed be either contaminated or uncontaminated." Why is this? Because, relying on the eightfold path to enter the seeing of the truth, this is indeed also uncontaminated; "should the eightfold path come before the seven factors of awakening, it is, without exception, uncontaminated throughout." This, then, is readily understood. Citing the Vibhāṣā's own text proves both senses at once. And this contaminated-or-uncontaminated distinction is itself precisely the sense of mapping them against stage. The thirty-seven factors belonging to every kind of truth of the path are not recorded here in full; only the thirty-seven factors belonging to the unconditioned truth of the path are clarified here, accomplishing the very sense of the three contemplations in a single mind.

The fingernail-dirt and ten-directions-dirt simile makes the chapter's real point vivid: the thirty-seven factors most readers know are only the fingernail's worth: the perfect teaching's own reading of the identical thirty-seven factors is the "boundless, incalculable" version of the very same list.

The Four Foundations of Mindfulness: Body, Feeling, Mind, and Dharma, in One Mind 四念處 · 一心三觀

Since the Dharma-nature is the middle, it is neither one nor every, illuminating both the one and the every at once — also named neither empty nor provisional, illuminating both emptiness and the provisional at once — and thus everything, neither empty nor provisional, illuminates both emptiness and the provisional at once. The form of the nine dharma-realms, at once empty, at once provisional, at once the middle, proceeds in exactly the same way. This is named "the foundation of mindfulness of the body."

若觀法性受，法性因緣生故，一種一切種，一受一切受。法性受空故，一切受一受，一空一切空。法性受假名故，一受一切受，一假一切假。法性受中故，非一受非一切受，非空非假雙照空假，則一切非空非假雙照空假。九法界受即空、即假、即中，亦復如是。是名「受念處」。若觀法性心因緣生法，一種一切種，一心一切心。法性空故，一切心一心，一空一切空。法性假故，一心一切心，一假一切假。法性中故，非一非一切，非空非假雙照空假。九法界心亦復如是。是名「心念處」。若觀法性想、行兩陰因緣生法，一種一切種，一行無量行。法性空故，一切行一行，一空一切空。法性假故，一行一切行，一假一切假。法性中故，非一非一切，非空非假雙照空假，一切非空非假雙照空假。九法界行皆即空、即假、即中，亦復如是。是名「法念處」。

Should one contemplate the Dharma-nature of feeling: since the Dharma-nature arises through causes and conditions, one kind is every kind, one feeling is every feeling. Since the Dharma-nature of feeling is empty, every feeling is one feeling, one emptiness is every emptiness. Since the Dharma-nature of feeling is a provisional name, one feeling is every feeling, one provisional is every provisional. Since the Dharma-nature of feeling is the middle, it is neither one feeling nor every feeling, neither empty nor provisional, illuminating both emptiness and the provisional at once — and thus everything, neither empty nor provisional, illuminates both emptiness and the provisional at once. The feeling of the nine dharma-realms, at once empty, at once provisional, at once the middle, proceeds in exactly the same way. This is named "the foundation of mindfulness of feeling." Should one contemplate that the Dharma-nature of mind is a dharma arisen through causes and conditions, one kind is every kind, one mind is every mind. Since the Dharma-nature is empty, every mind is one mind, one emptiness is every emptiness. Since the Dharma-nature is the provisional, one mind is every mind, one provisional is every provisional. Since the Dharma-nature is the middle, it is neither one nor every, neither empty nor provisional, illuminating both emptiness and the provisional at once. The mind of the nine dharma-realms proceeds in exactly the same way. This is named "the foundation of mindfulness of mind." Should one contemplate that the two aggregates of perception and formation, as dharmas of the Dharma-nature arisen through causes and conditions — one kind is every kind, one formation is every boundless formation. Since the Dharma-nature is empty, every formation is one formation, one emptiness is every emptiness. Since the Dharma-nature is the provisional, one formation is every formation, one provisional is every provisional. Since the Dharma-nature is the middle, it is neither one nor every, neither empty nor provisional, illuminating both emptiness and the provisional at once — and thus everything, neither empty nor provisional, illuminates both emptiness and the provisional at once. The formations of the nine dharma-realms, every one, at once empty, at once provisional, at once the middle, proceed in exactly the same way. This is named "the foundation of mindfulness of dharmas."

如是念處力用廣博，義兼大小，俱破八倒，雙顯榮枯，雙非榮枯，即於中間入般涅槃，亦名「坐道場」，亦名「摩訶衍」，亦名「法界」。兼廣之義，其相云何？法性之色實非是淨，而凡夫橫計為淨，是名顛倒；實非不淨，二乘之人橫計不淨，是名顛倒。今觀色種即空，一切即空，空中無淨，云何染著？是名「凡夫計淨倒破，枯念處成」。色種即假，一切皆假，分別名相不可窮盡；假智常淨，不為無知塵惑所染，云何滯空而取灰滅，言色不淨？是名二乘不淨倒破，榮念處成。是名八倒俱破，枯榮雙立。觀色本際非空非假，則一切非空非假。非空故非不淨倒；非假故非淨倒。非淨倒故則非榮樹；非不淨倒故則非枯樹。非枯非榮則非二邊；無邊無中乃名「中間」。佛會此理故名「涅槃」，亦是非淨非不淨。八倒不生名為「涅槃」，如是涅槃名「祕密藏」。安置諸子祕密藏中，佛自住中，故言「入」也。

The power and function of these foundations of mindfulness are vast and expansive in this way, their sense embracing both the lesser and greater vehicles at once, breaking through all eight inversions together, revealing both flourishing and withering at once, negating both flourishing and withering at once, entering thereby into parinirvāṇa at their very midpoint — also named "sitting at the seat of enlightenment," also named "the Great Vehicle," also named "the dharma-realm." What, then, is the mark of this sense embracing both at once? The Dharma-nature of form is, in truth, not purity itself, and yet the ordinary being falsely calculates it to be pure — this is named "inversion." It is, in truth, not impurity either, and yet the person of the two lesser vehicles falsely calculates it to be impure — this, too, is named "inversion." Contemplating now that the seed of form is itself empty, and everything is itself empty, within this very emptiness there is no purity at all — how, then, could one become defiled and attached to it? This is named "the ordinary being's inverted calculation of purity broken through, the withering foundation of mindfulness accomplished." The seed of form is itself the provisional, and everything is entirely the provisional; distinguishing its names and marks can never be exhausted; the wisdom of the provisional is constantly pure, never defiled by the taint of unknowing — how, then, could one remain fixed in emptiness, grasping at ashen extinction, and claim that form is impure? This is named "the two lesser vehicles' own inverted calculation of impurity broken through, the flourishing foundation of mindfulness accomplished." This is named "both inversions broken through together, withering and flourishing both established at once." Contemplating the very ultimate boundary of form as neither empty nor provisional, then everything is neither empty nor provisional. Being not empty, it is not the inversion of impurity; being not provisional, it is not the inversion of purity. Not being the inversion of purity, it is thus not the flourishing tree; not being the inversion of impurity, it is thus not the withering tree either. Being neither withering nor flourishing, it is thus neither of the two extremes; being without extreme and without middle, this is precisely named "the very midpoint." The Buddha, converging upon this very principle, is thus named "nirvāṇa," which is likewise neither purity nor impurity. The eight inversions no longer arising is what is named "nirvāṇa"; a nirvāṇa of this kind is named "the secret treasury." Placing every one of his own children within this secret treasury, the Buddha himself abides within it as well — hence it is said, he "enters" it.

法性之受本非是樂，而凡夫之人橫計為樂，是名顛倒。實非是苦，二乘之人橫計為苦。今觀受種即空，一切皆空；空中無樂，云何生染？則凡夫倒破，枯念處成。受種即假，一切皆假，以無所受而受諸受；名聞分別不生厭畏，云何棄之沈空灰斷？二乘倒破，榮念處成。是名「二倒雙破，枯榮雙立」。觀受本際即非空非假；非空故非枯，非假故非榮。邊倒不生名為「涅槃」；中間理顯名「祕密藏」，皆如上說云云。法性之心本非是常，凡夫橫計，是名「常倒」；法性實非無常，二乘橫計無常。今觀心種即空，一切即空；空中非常，云何謂心念念相續？是名凡夫常倒破，枯念處成。心即假名，一切悉假；心若無常，那得分別無量心相？是名「二乘無常倒破，榮念處成」。又心即非空非假，非空故非無常，非假故非於常。非榮非枯邊倒不生名「入涅槃」；中道理顯名「祕密藏」。安置諸子，自亦入中云云。法性之法本非有我，凡夫之人橫計有我。本非無我，二乘之人橫計無我。今觀法性即空，一切皆空；空中無我，是名「凡夫倒破，枯念處成」。法性即假，一切皆假施設，自在不滯，我義具足，是名「二乘倒破，榮念處成」。觀法本際即非空非假。非空故非無我；非假故非於我。邊倒不生名「入涅槃」；中間理顯名「祕密藏」。治倒法藥其數有四；法性觀智名之為「念」；一諦三諦名之為「處」。一切即空，諸倒榮枯無不空寂；一切即假，二邊雙樹無不成立；一切即中，無非法界。祇一念心，廣遠若此。若能深觀念處，是坐道場，是摩訶衍，是雙樹間入般涅槃；始終具足，不須更修餘法。

The Dharma-nature of feeling is, at root, not bliss itself, and yet the ordinary being falsely calculates it to be bliss — this is named "inversion." It is, in truth, not suffering either, and yet the person of the two lesser vehicles falsely calculates it to be suffering. Contemplating now that the seed of feeling is itself empty, and everything is entirely empty — within this very emptiness there is no bliss at all, so how could defilement possibly arise? This, then, is the ordinary being's own inversion broken through, the withering foundation of mindfulness accomplished. The seed of feeling is itself the provisional, and everything is entirely the provisional, receiving every feeling by way of receiving nothing at all; fame and its discrimination give rise to neither aversion nor fear — how, then, could one abandon them and sink into the ashen extinction of mere emptiness? This is the two lesser vehicles' own inversion broken through, the flourishing foundation of mindfulness accomplished. This is named "both inversions broken through together, withering and flourishing both established at once." Contemplating the very ultimate boundary of feeling as neither empty nor provisional: being not empty, it is not withering; being not provisional, it is not flourishing. The inversion of either extreme no longer arising is what is named "nirvāṇa"; the principle of the very midpoint made manifest is named "the secret treasury" — all exactly as explained above. The Dharma-nature of mind is, at root, not permanence itself, and yet the ordinary being falsely calculates it to be so — this is named "the inversion of permanence"; the Dharma-nature is, in truth, not impermanence either, and yet the two lesser vehicles falsely calculate it to be impermanent. Contemplating now that the seed of mind is itself empty, and everything is itself empty — within this very emptiness there is no permanence at all, so how could mind be said to continue on, thought-moment after thought-moment? This is the ordinary being's own inversion of permanence broken through, the withering foundation of mindfulness accomplished. Mind is itself a provisional name, and everything is entirely provisional; were mind truly impermanent, how could one possibly distinguish its boundless different marks at all? This is named "the two lesser vehicles' own inversion of impermanence broken through, the flourishing foundation of mindfulness accomplished." Further, mind is itself neither empty nor provisional; being not empty, it is not impermanence; being not provisional, it is not permanence either. Neither flourishing nor withering, the inversion of either extreme no longer arising, is named "entering nirvāṇa"; the principle of the middle way made manifest is named "the secret treasury." Placing every one of his own children within it, the Buddha himself, too, enters into the middle. The Dharma-nature of dharmas is, at root, not the possession of a self, and yet the ordinary being falsely calculates it to possess a self. It is, at root, not the absence of a self either, and yet the two lesser vehicles falsely calculate it to lack a self. Contemplating now that the Dharma-nature is itself empty, and everything is itself empty — within this very emptiness there is no self at all — this is named "the ordinary being's own inversion broken through, the withering foundation of mindfulness accomplished." The Dharma-nature is itself the provisional, and everything is entirely a provisional construction, free and unfixed, the very sense of "self" complete within it — this is named "the two lesser vehicles' own inversion broken through, the flourishing foundation of mindfulness accomplished." Contemplating the very ultimate boundary of dharmas as neither empty nor provisional: being not empty, it is not the absence of self; being not provisional, it is not the possession of self either. The inversion of either extreme no longer arising is named "entering nirvāṇa"; the principle of the very midpoint made manifest is named "the secret treasury." The dharma-medicine that cures inversion comes, in number, to four; the contemplative wisdom of the Dharma-nature is what is named "mindfulness"; the one truth and the three truths together are what is named "the foundation." Everything at once empty, every inversion, whether flourishing or withering, is nowhere not empty and quiescent; everything at once the provisional, the twin trees of both extremes are nowhere not fully established; everything at once the middle, nothing whatsoever is other than the dharma-realm itself. It is simply a single thought-moment of mind, vast and far-reaching to exactly this degree. Should one be able to contemplate the foundations of mindfulness this deeply, this is itself sitting at the seat of enlightenment, is itself the Great Vehicle, is itself entering parinirvāṇa between the twin trees — complete, beginning to end, in itself, requiring no further cultivation of any other dharma at all.

Reading note: The "eight inversions" — ordinary beings mistaking the impermanent, suffering, selfless, and impure for permanent, blissful, self, and pure; the two lesser vehicles overcorrecting into the opposite four mistakes about nirvāṇa itself — are broken through here by an identical structural move applied four times: the "withering" contemplation of emptiness cures the ordinary being's excess, the "flourishing" contemplation of the provisional cures the lesser vehicles' opposite excess, and their negation together reveals "the very midpoint" that was never actually one of the two trees. The image of the Buddha "placing every one of his own children within this secret treasury" and "abiding within it as well" makes explicit what the whole passage has been building toward: this is not a technique reserved for advanced practitioners but the literal content of what the historical parinirvāṇa between the twin sāla trees is said to mean.

The Remaining Six Branches: One Mind, Three Truths 正勤 · 如意足 · 五根 · 五力 · 七覺 · 八正道

Chinese Source

若不入者，更研餘品。勤觀念處，名「正勤」。見思本起，名「已生惡」；觀於即空，令已生不生，故勤精進。塵沙無明，名「未生惡」；觀即假、即中，令未生不生，故勤精進。竭力盡誠，行四三昧，遮此二惡。一切智名「已生善」；此善易生，故言「泥洹道易得」也。道種智、一切種智名「未生善」；此分別智難生。空智已生，勤加增長；中智未生，令得開發。三觀無間，祇為生此二智耳。是四正勤亦能悟道，故言「一心勤精進故得三菩提」，不須餘法。若不入者，當是不勤，心過散動；須入善寂，審觀心性，名為「上定」。於上定中修如意足：欲、精進、心、思惟。欲者，專向彼法，亦名「莊嚴彼法」。定中觀智，如密室中燈，照物則了。以照了故，斷行成就修如意足。精進者，成就彼法。法性不動而寂然精進，無間無雜。斷行成就修如意足。心者，正住觀察彼法一心中緣。制之一處無事不辦，斷行成就修如意足。思惟者，善能分別彼法方便。如此思惟不令動散。定思惟故，斷行成就修如意足。能如此修，定心而入，不須餘法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should one not yet enter thereby, one further cultivates the remaining branches. Diligently contemplating the foundations of mindfulness is named "the four right efforts." The original arising of view and habituated thought is named "evil already arisen"; contemplating this as itself empty causes what has already arisen to become unborn, and this is diligent effort. Dust-and-sand and ignorance are named "evil not yet arisen"; contemplating these as at once the provisional and the middle causes what has not yet arisen to remain unborn, and this too is diligent effort. Exhausting one's strength and sincerity in full, one carries out the four samādhis, warding off these two evils. Omniscience is named "wholesomeness already arisen" — this wholesomeness is easily brought forth, and hence it is said, "the path to nirvāṇa is easily attained." The wisdom of the path in every kind, and the wisdom of all modes, are named "wholesomeness not yet arisen" — this discerning wisdom is difficult to bring forth. The wisdom of emptiness, already arisen, one diligently increases further; the wisdom of the middle, not yet arisen, one causes to be opened and brought forth. The three contemplations, without interruption, exist for the sole purpose of bringing forth these two wisdoms. These four right efforts are likewise able to bring about awakening, hence it is said, "through diligent effort with a single mind, the threefold bodhi is attained" — requiring no further dharma at all. Should one not yet enter thereby, this is because one's diligence is lacking, the mind excessively scattered and agitated — one must enter into wholesome quiescence, examining and contemplating the mind's own nature, named "the higher concentration." Within this higher concentration, one cultivates the four bases of supernormal power: aspiration, diligence, mind, and reflection. Aspiration means turning wholly toward that very dharma, also named "adorning that very dharma." The contemplative wisdom within concentration is like a lamp within a sealed chamber, illuminating whatever it touches with perfect clarity — and by virtue of this very clarity, the cutting-off practice is accomplished, cultivating the base of supernormal power. Diligence means bringing that very dharma to accomplishment. The Dharma-nature, unmoving and yet quiescently diligent, without interruption, without admixture — the cutting-off practice is accomplished, cultivating the base of supernormal power. Mind means abiding correctly, observing that very dharma, its object gathered within a single mind. Fixing it in a single place, there is nothing whatsoever left unaccomplished — the cutting-off practice is accomplished, cultivating the base of supernormal power. Reflection means being skillfully able to distinguish that very dharma's own skillful means. Reflecting in this way, one does not allow the mind to become agitated or scattered — through this concentrated reflection, the cutting-off practice is accomplished, cultivating the base of supernormal power. Able to cultivate in this way, entering with a concentrated mind, no further dharma at all is needed.

若不入者，當修五根。信三諦理是三世佛母，能生一切十力、無畏、解脫、三昧。但念處修，不求餘法，是名「信根」。進者，以信攝於諸法。信諸法故，倍策精進。念者，但念正助之道，不令邪妄得入。又此法者為精進所修，是法不忘，故名「念根」。定者，一心寂定而行精進。又此法為念所攝，是法不忘、不動，故名「定根」。慧者，念處之慧為定法所攝，內性自照不從他知，是名「慧根」。但修五根亦能入道成摩訶衍。若不入者，進修五力，令根增長，遮諸煩惱名之為「力」。信破諸疑，無能動者。精進除懈怠，如本所願，皆得成就。念破邪想，不為煩惱所壞。定破散亂，遠離憒鬧。雖有所說，不礙初禪；善住覺觀，不礙二禪；心生歡喜，不礙三禪；教化眾生，不礙四禪。妨四禪法不妨諸定，亦不捨定，亦不隨定，是名「定力」。慧破邪執，一切執、一切慧雙照具足，是名「慧力」。如是五力名「摩訶衍」。若不入者，用七覺均調。心浮動時，以除覺除身口之麁；以捨覺捨於觀智；以定心入禪。若心沈時，精進、擇、喜起之。念通緣兩處。修此七覺即得入道。《大論》云：「若離五蓋專修七覺，不得入者無有是處。」若不入者，修八正道。更以出世上上正見觀三諦理，以正思惟發動此觀。如法相說自他俱益，即是正語。若黑業得黑報，白業得白報，雜業得雜報，非白非黑業得非白非黑報；約小乘作可解。今言沈空是黑業，出假是白業，兩兼是雜業，中道是非白非黑業，皆名「邪命」。若業能盡業，名為「正業」；依此而行，名為「正業」。正業不為二邊所牽。見他得利心不惱熱，而於己利常知止足，是為「正命」。善入正諦，名「正精進」。心不動失，正直不忘，名「正念」。正住決定，名「正定」。因是八正道，即得入理。《大經》云：「若有能修八正道者，即得醍醐。」如是道品非是對位，但於初心觀法性理，即得具足。

Should one not yet enter thereby, one ought to cultivate the five faculties. Believing that the principle of the three truths is the mother of the buddhas across all three times, able to give rise to every one of the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, liberation, and samādhi — simply cultivating the foundations of mindfulness, seeking no other dharma — this is named "the faculty of faith." Diligence means gathering in every dharma by means of faith; because one believes in every dharma, one redoubles one's own diligent effort. Mindfulness means simply bearing in mind the correct path and its auxiliary supports, never allowing deviance or falseness to enter in; and, since this very dharma is cultivated by diligence, and this dharma is never forgotten, it is named "the faculty of mindfulness." Concentration means a single mind, quiescent and settled, carrying out diligent effort; and, since this very dharma is gathered in by mindfulness, and this dharma is never forgotten, never disturbed, it is named "the faculty of concentration." Wisdom means the wisdom belonging to the foundations of mindfulness, gathered in by the dharma of concentration, its own inward nature self-illuminating, known not through any other — this is named "the faculty of wisdom." Simply cultivating these five faculties is, likewise, already able to enter the path and accomplish the Great Vehicle. Should one not yet enter thereby, one advances to cultivate the five powers, causing the faculties to grow in strength, warding off every affliction — this is what is named "power." Faith breaks through every doubt, so that nothing at all is able to shake it. Diligence removes laziness, so that whatever one has originally vowed is, every bit of it, brought to accomplishment. Mindfulness breaks through deviant thought, so that one is never destroyed by affliction. Concentration breaks through scattered distraction, remaining far removed from noise and confusion. Though something is being spoken, this does not obstruct the first dhyāna; skillfully abiding in reflection and examination does not obstruct the second dhyāna; the mind giving rise to joy does not obstruct the third dhyāna; and transforming sentient beings does not obstruct the fourth dhyāna. Obstructing the dharma of the four dhyānas does not obstruct any of the other concentrations, and one neither abandons concentration nor follows blindly after it — this is named "the power of concentration." Wisdom breaks through deviant attachment, every attachment and every wisdom alike illuminated together and complete — this is named "the power of wisdom." Powers of this kind, five in all, are named "the Great Vehicle." Should one not yet enter thereby, one applies the seven factors of awakening to bring balance. When the mind grows agitated and unsettled, one uses the factor of removal to remove the coarseness of body and speech; the factor of relinquishment to relinquish attachment to the very contemplative wisdom itself; and the factor of concentration to enter dhyāna. When the mind grows sunken and dull, one uses the factors of diligence, discrimination, and joy to rouse it. The factor of mindfulness pervades both conditions at once. Cultivating these seven factors of awakening, one thereby enters the path directly. The Great Treatise says: "Should one, apart from the five hindrances, cultivate the seven factors of awakening exclusively, and still fail to enter thereby — this is simply impossible." Should one not yet enter thereby, one cultivates the eightfold path. Applying, further, the supreme supramundane correct view to contemplate the principle of the three truths, one uses correct thought to rouse and set this contemplation into motion. Explaining it as the mark of the dharma, benefiting both oneself and others, this is precisely correct speech. Should black karma bring a black retribution, white karma a white retribution, mixed karma a mixed retribution, and karma that is neither white nor black a retribution that is neither white nor black — this is readily understood with reference to the Hīnayāna. Here, it is said instead that sinking into emptiness is black karma; emerging into the provisional is white karma; embracing both together is mixed karma; and the middle way is karma that is neither white nor black — every one of these, misapplied, is named "deviant livelihood." Should this karma itself be able to exhaust karma, this is named "correct action"; carrying this out in practice is named "correct action." Correct action is not dragged along by either extreme. Seeing another attain some benefit, the mind is not vexed or inflamed by it; and, with respect to one's own benefit, one constantly knows when to stop and be content — this is "correct livelihood." Skillfully entering the correct truth is named "correct diligence." The mind neither wavering nor forgetting, upright and unforgetting, is named "correct mindfulness." Correctly abiding in resolute settledness is named "correct concentration." Through this very eightfold path, one thereby enters principle directly. The Great Sūtra says: "Should there be one able to cultivate the eightfold path, he thereby attains the very ghee itself." Branches of the path explained in this way are not merely mapped against a fixed stage, but, cultivated at the very first thought, are already complete in full.

The Great Treatise says: "Among the four foundations of mindfulness, the four kinds of diligence are named 'the four right efforts'; the four kinds of concentrated mind are named 'the four bases of supernormal power'; the arising of the five wholesome roots is named 'the faculties'; their growth in strength is named 'the powers'; distinguishing the four foundations of mindfulness' own function of the path is named 'the awakening-factors'; and proceeding along the calm and settled path of the four foundations of mindfulness is named 'the eightfold path.'" One should therefore know that the beginning practitioner, cultivating the path, uses these thirty-seven factors to nourish and cultivate the four kinds of samādhi within calming-and-contemplation, thereby entering the bodhisattva's own stage. Branches of the path in this sense are the near cause of the great nirvāṇa; every other branch of the path is named its distant cause. Here, then, a simile is offered to reveal this very sense: planting a seed in the earth, its sprout-tip first opens, sending its root downward, spreading its branches and leaves upward, its flower unfolding in bloom, and finally bearing fruit to full ripeness. The Dharma-nature and the dharma-realm are the great earth; the contemplation of the foundations of mindfulness is the seed itself; the four right efforts are like the sprout pushing forth; the five faculties are like the root taking hold; the five powers are like the stem and leaves growing in strength; the seven factors of awakening are like the flower opening in bloom; and the eightfold path is like the fruit finally born. This bearing of fruit is precisely entering the bronze-wheel stage, realizing the forbearance of the unborn — also named "arriving at the treasure-place," also named "entering the secret treasury," also named "attaining the ghee itself," also named "seeing the buddha-nature directly," also named "the Dharma-body manifest, the eight aspects accomplishing buddhahood." "The branches of the path are one's own good spiritual friend, and it is through them that correct awakening is accomplished" — this is exactly that same sense. As for the common explanation of the tree of the path: as the Larger Sūtra clarifies, leaving behind the three evil destinies is named "the benefit of the leaf"; attaining a human or heavenly body is named "the benefit of the flower"; and attaining the fruit of the fourfold path is named "the benefit of the fruit" — this explanation, however, is one-sidedly given in terms of emptiness alone. Escaping the ground of the two lesser vehicles is the benefit of the leaf; attaining a body of transformative spiritual power is the benefit of the flower; and being complete with the wisdom of the path in every kind is the benefit of the fruit — this explanation is one-sidedly given in terms of the provisional alone. Escaping the bondage of both extremes is the benefit of the leaf; receiving the Dharma-nature body is the benefit of the flower; and directly realizing the buddha-nature is the benefit of the fruit — this explanation is one-sidedly given in terms of the middle alone. Should one instead take this together, in terms of all three contemplations at once: emptiness itself is named "the benefit of the leaf"; the provisional itself is named "the benefit of the flower"; and the middle itself is named "the benefit of the fruit."

Reading note: The seed-to-fruit simile mapping each of the seven remaining branches onto a stage of a plant's growth is more than decorative — it explains why the treatise insists these thirty-seven factors need never be left behind for something more advanced: a seed does not stop being connected to the fruit once the stem appears, and "the branches of the path are the near cause of the great nirvāṇa" precisely because each stage literally grows out of, rather than replaces, the one before it. The three one-sided readings of "leaf, flower, fruit" (through emptiness alone, the provisional alone, the middle alone) followed immediately by their synthesis is the same rhetorical move used throughout this fascicle: name the partial readings first, by name, so the reader can recognize why the whole point was never to choose among them.

According to the shared teaching: clarifying that suffering and its origin are, every one, like illusion and transformation, is itself already the gate of emptiness. The old edition of the Treatise reads: "Should one contemplate the most subtle particle of form, there are eighteen kinds of emptiness"; the current edition reads: "Should one contemplate a single length of felt cloth, there are eighteen kinds of emptiness." Felt cloth is a provisional name, while the subtlest particle is a real dharma — this is the difference between the two editions. Should one truly grasp the sense of it, both the provisional and the real are alike empty. Though the mark of emptiness is itself empty, one may still calculate upon the very contemplative wisdom involved — but since there is neither a contemplating subject nor a contemplated object at all, who, then, is it that performs this contemplation of emptiness? The Great Treatise says: "The śrāvaka, taking emptiness as the object, cultivates the three liberations; the bodhisattva, taking the true mark of every dharma as the object, cultivates the three liberations." The wise see both emptiness and not-emptiness at once, and this emptiness-and-not-emptiness together is also named "the middle way." "A single gate of liberation is itself already the three gates of liberation" recapitulates, at the level of practice, the very structure the whole fascicle keeps returning to: names that appear to multiply into a sequence of three or four turn out, examined closely, to have been describing one thing from different angles all along.

Further, the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, like the sun rising and first illuminating the high mountains, leans heavily toward the four "flourishing" inversions; the Tripiṭaka teaching of the Deer Park leans heavily toward the four "withering" inversions; the Vaipulya and Prajñāpāramitā sūtras, for the most part, temper the withering to bring it toward flourishing, drawing the lesser vehicle toward the greater; and the teaching given beneath the crane-forest, its transformative work already complete, enters nirvāṇa at the very midpoint between withering and flourishing. The Six Pāramitās as Remedy for the Six Coverings of the Mind 六蔽 · 六度為治

若人修四三昧，道品調適，解脫不開而慳貪忽起，激動觀心，於身、命、財守護保著。又貪覺緣想，須欲念生，雖作意遮止而慳貪轉生。是時當用檀捨為治。修三昧時破戒心忽起，威儀麁獷，無復矜持，身口乖違，觸犯制度。淨禁不淳，三昧難發。是時當用尸羅為治。修三昧時瞋恚悖怒，常生忿恨，惡口兩舌、諍計是非。此毒障於三昧，是時當修忍為治。修三昧時放逸懈怠，恣身口意，縱蕩閑野，無慚無愧，不能苦節。如鑽火未熱數數而止。事𠐳之人尚不辦世務，況三昧門？是時應用精進為治。修三昧時散亂不定，身如獨落，口若春蛙，心如風燈。以散逸故法不現前，是時應用禪定為治。修三昧時愚癡迷惑，計著斷常，謂有人、我、眾生、壽命；觸事面牆，進止常短，不稱物望。意慮頑拙，非智黠相，是時當用智慧為治。諸蔽覆心亦有厚薄，薄者心動，身口不必動；厚者身口動，心必先動。內病既強，其相外現。若用對治得去，是病所宜；若對治不除，當依四隨迴轉助道。如治一慳，或樂修檀，或不樂修檀；或善心生，或不善生；或修檀慳破，或不破；或修檀助開，或不開。當善巧斟酌，或對、或轉、或兼、或第一義云云。修餘治亦如是。於助六度，但作一事解，不能助道；當觀此助不思議，攝一切法，如後說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a person cultivating the four samādhis, having already regulated the branches of the path, find that liberation still fails to open while stinginess and craving suddenly arise, stirring and disturbing the contemplating mind, guarding and clinging to body, life, and property — or should craving's own thought and conditioned imagination give rise to a sudden desire, and, though one applies deliberate effort to check it, stinginess and craving only arise all the more — at such a time, one ought to apply giving and relinquishment as the remedy. Should a mind that breaks the precepts suddenly arise while cultivating samādhi, one's own deportment growing coarse and unrestrained, no longer holding to any discipline, body and speech at odds with one another, violating every regulation — the purity of one's own restraint no longer intact, samādhi becomes difficult to bring forth. At such a time, one ought to apply the precepts as the remedy. Should anger and rage arise while cultivating samādhi, constantly giving rise to resentment and hatred, harsh speech and slander, disputing over right and wrong — this very poison obstructs samādhi itself. At such a time, one ought to cultivate patience as the remedy. Should laxity and laziness arise while cultivating samādhi, indulging body, speech, and mind, dissolute and unrestrained, without any sense of shame before oneself or before others, unable to bear even the slightest discipline — like striking flint that never grows hot before one gives up again and again — a person absorbed in mere activity cannot even manage worldly affairs, how much less the very gate of samādhi? At such a time, one ought to apply diligence as the remedy. Should scattered distraction and instability arise while cultivating samādhi, the body like a lone falling leaf, the mouth like a spring frog's constant croaking, the mind like a lamp flickering in the wind — because of this very scattering and dissipation, the dharma fails to manifest before one. At such a time, one ought to apply meditative concentration as the remedy. Should foolish delusion and confusion arise while cultivating samādhi, calculating and clinging to either annihilation or eternalism, claiming there to be a person, a self, a sentient being, a life-span — meeting every matter as though facing a blank wall, one's advance and retreat always falling short, never meeting what is actually called for, one's own thought clumsy and dull, lacking any trace of sharp discernment — at such a time, one ought to apply wisdom as the remedy. Every one of these coverings of the mind, too, comes in both a thicker and a thinner form: in the thinner form, the mind alone is stirred, without body or speech necessarily being stirred as well; in the thicker form, body and speech are stirred, and the mind is necessarily stirred first of all. Once the inward illness has grown strong, its own mark manifests outwardly. Should the direct antidote succeed in removing it, this is exactly the illness it was suited for; but should the antidote fail to remove it, one should then rely on the four modes of adaptation, turning the remedy in a different direction to assist the path instead. Thus, in treating a single instance of stinginess: one may find delight in cultivating giving, or one may not find delight in it; a wholesome mind may arise, or it may not arise; cultivating giving may break through stinginess, or it may not break through it; cultivating giving may help open the way, or it may not open it. One should skillfully weigh and judge accordingly — whether by direct antidote, by turning it in a different direction, by combining approaches, or by the ultimate meaning itself. Cultivating every other remedy proceeds in exactly the same way. With respect to assisting through the six pāramitās, understanding this merely as a single, isolated matter cannot actually assist the path at all — one must contemplate that this very assistance is itself inconceivable, gathering in every dharma without exception, as explained further below.

Reading note: The precise, almost clinical symptom-lists here — the mouth "like a spring frog's constant croaking," striking flint that "never grows hot before one gives up again and again" — read less like abstract doctrine than a meditation teacher's own field notes, cataloguing exactly what stinginess, precept-breaking, anger, laziness, distraction, and folly actually look like once they surface mid-practice. The four-mode adaptation closing the passage (direct antidote, redirection, combination, ultimate meaning) prevents this from becoming a rigid formula: giving is the textbook cure for stinginess, but "should the antidote fail to remove it," the treatise insists on flexibility over insisting the textbook is wrong.

Giving and the Precepts, as Remedy — Theory and Practice United 檀度 · 尸羅 · 事理雙修

有人言：「說六是通教，說十是通宗。」此不應爾！《大經》明「六度是佛性」，《大品》云「是摩訶衍」。一度尚攝諸法，何況六耶？若得開合之意，則無去取。如禪有願、智、力，開出泥滓波羅蜜；有神通力，開出婆羅波羅蜜，定守禪度也。般若有道種智，開出漚和俱舍羅；又有一切種智，開出闍那波羅蜜；一切智守本，受般若之稱。離則為十，束即為六，豈得以廣略而判大小耶？今明六度助道攝諸法盡，略明攝諸道品、調伏六根、十力、四無所畏、十八不共法、六通、三明、四攝、四辯、陀羅尼、三十二相、八十隨形好等及一切法。云何攝諸道品？諸道品中各有捨覺分，正為檀攝。若三藏捨覺分，雖不入理，亦是捨身、命、財。《大論》云：「慈、悲、喜於眾生有益，捨何所益？」捨能具足六度，廣利眾生，是名「大益」。又捨如膏油，能增五度光明。故知檀度攝捨覺分也。若通教捨覺分，捨身、命、財如幻如化，三事皆空，此捨覺分亦為檀度所攝也。若別教捨覺分，捨身、命、財中無知，此捨亦為檀度所攝也。若圓教捨覺分，捨十法界色身，捨十法界連持之命，捨十法界依報，如是身、命、財皆不入二邊。何以故？財名「六塵」，若計六塵可捨，有前人可與，己身能施，如此施者即入六塵有邊；若三事皆空，即墮無邊。今觀財即空，不入有；觀財即假，不入空。不二之捨，與生死後際等，離老病死，得不壞常住。有邊是生死，屬前際；空邊是涅槃，屬後際。是二皆空，悉不可得，故稱為「等」。離老死者，前際空故，離分段老死；後際空故，離變易老死；二死永免故言「離」也。得不壞常住者，即是中道法性，諸佛所師；以法常故，諸佛亦常。此常住財無能毀損，常住之身無能繫縛，常住之命不可斷滅，成就究竟檀波羅蜜以自莊嚴。故《金剛般若》云：「初、中、後日分，悉以恒河沙身布施，不如受持般若一四句偈。」當知理觀圓捨乃會道品，檀度所攝也。

Ru-Yi Translation

It has been claimed that "speaking of six pāramitās is the shared teaching, while speaking of ten is the shared teaching's own fuller doctrine" — but this cannot be right. The Great Sūtra clarifies that "the six pāramitās are themselves the buddha-nature"; the Larger Sūtra says they are "the Great Vehicle itself." If a single pāramitā already gathers in every dharma, how much more so, then, the full six? Grasping the sense of opening and gathering, there is nothing here to prefer or reject. Thus dhyāna contains vow, wisdom, and power, opened out further as the pāramitā of stilling the sediment; and it contains spiritual power, opened out further as the pāramitā of purification — while the pāramitā of dhyāna itself still holds its own settled ground. Prajñā contains the wisdom of the path in every kind, opened out as skillful strategy; and it contains the wisdom of all modes, opened out as the pāramitā of true knowledge — while all-encompassing wisdom itself still holds to its own root, receiving the name of "prajñā." Separated out, this becomes ten; gathered together, this becomes six — how, then, could breadth or brevity alone be used to judge which is the greater vehicle and which the lesser? Here, then, it is clarified that the six pāramitās, as the auxiliary path, gather in every dharma entirely — briefly clarifying how they gather in every branch of the path, the regulation of the six faculties, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the eighteen dharmas not shared with others, the six spiritual powers, the three insights, the four modes of embrace, the four unimpeded eloquences, the dhāraṇīs, the thirty-two marks, the eighty subsidiary excellent features, and every other dharma as well. How, then, do they gather in every branch of the path? Within every branch of the path there is, in each case, the awakening-factor of relinquishment, which is precisely what giving gathers in. Even in the Tripiṭaka teaching's own awakening-factor of relinquishment, though it does not yet enter principle directly, it is still the relinquishing of body, life, and property. The Treatise says: "Loving-kindness, compassion, and sympathetic joy already benefit sentient beings — what further benefit does relinquishment bring?" Relinquishment is able to bring the six pāramitās to completion, broadly benefiting sentient beings — this is named "the great benefit." Relinquishment, further, is like refined oil, able to increase the light of the remaining five pāramitās. One should therefore know that the pāramitā of giving gathers in the awakening-factor of relinquishment. In the shared teaching's own awakening-factor of relinquishment, relinquishing body, life, and property as though illusion, as though transformation, all three matters being alike empty — this awakening-factor of relinquishment, too, is gathered in by the pāramitā of giving. In the distinct teaching's own awakening-factor of relinquishment, relinquishing the very unknowing found within body, life, and property — this relinquishment, too, is gathered in by the pāramitā of giving. In the perfect teaching's own awakening-factor of relinquishment, relinquishing the physical body of the ten dharma-realms, relinquishing the life that sustains and connects the ten dharma-realms, relinquishing the dependent retribution of the ten dharma-realms — body, life, and property of this kind enter neither of the two extremes at all. Why is this? "Property" is named "the six sense-objects" — should one calculate that the six sense-objects can be relinquished, with a recipient present to receive them and one's own person able to give them, a giving of this kind falls into the extreme of the six sense-objects' own existence; and should all three matters be considered alike empty, this instead falls into the extreme of nonexistence. Contemplating now that property is itself empty, one does not fall into existence; contemplating that property is itself the provisional, one does not fall into emptiness. A relinquishment free of this duality is equal to the very final boundary of birth-and-death, free of old age, illness, and death, attaining the unbroken and constant abiding. The extreme of existence is birth-and-death, belonging to the prior boundary; the extreme of emptiness is nirvāṇa, belonging to the final boundary. Both of these being alike empty, neither can ever be found at all — hence they are named "equal." As for being "free" of old age and death: the prior boundary being empty, one is free of segmented old age and death; the final boundary being empty, one is free of transformational old age and death; both kinds of death permanently escaped, this is exactly why it is said to be "free." As for "attaining the unbroken and constant abiding": this is precisely the Dharma-nature of the middle way, the very teacher of every buddha; because the Dharma is constant, every buddha, too, is constant. This constantly abiding property cannot be destroyed by anything at all; this constantly abiding body cannot be bound by anything at all; this constantly abiding life cannot be severed or extinguished by anything at all — accomplishing the ultimate perfection of giving, adorning oneself with it. Hence the Diamond Prajñā Sūtra says: "Giving away, in the morning, at midday, and in the evening, bodies as numerous as the sands of the Ganges — this still does not equal receiving and upholding a single four-line verse of prajñā." One should therefore know that the perfect relinquishment of contemplative principle is exactly what accords with the branches of the path, gathered in by the pāramitā of giving.

如此道品捨覺分，理觀深微，而不存事行；三藏中，事施雄猛，剜燈、救貿、國城、妻子，而理觀全無毫末。兩皆有過。今明事檀，助破慳蔽，進成理觀，豈可相離？若人雖解實相圓捨之觀，撫臆論行，涉事慳克；保護財物一毫不捨；辭憚勞苦稱筋計力，不能屈己成他；貪惜壽命，豈能諍死讓生？觸事悋著鏗然不動，但解無行，如是重蔽何由可破？三解脫門何由可開？今於道場苦到懺悔，生決定心起大誓願，捨身命財決無愛惜。自行此檀，又以教他，讚歎檀法，隨喜檀者。立此誓已，稱十方佛為證為救。心若真實，無欺誑者，能感如來放檀光明，照除慳蔽。《思益》等云云。以蒙光故，與諸道品捨覺相應，須一一釋出之。事理既圓，能畢竟檀捨，財同糞土、身比毒器、命若行雲，棄三如唾。慳障既破，治道義成，便得解脫。若無因緣，寄之行道；應有利益，捨若遺芥。是為事油助增道明，開三脫門，得見佛性。若不能爾，無助治之益。若如上修，即應得悟；設不悟者，應自思惟，理觀道品有正業、正語、正命。此屬尸羅所攝。若三藏正業等，乃是慎護威儀，不破、不缺、不穿、不雜。通教正業等，不得身口，即事而真，乃是隨道、無著等戒。別教正業等，乃是智所讚、自在等戒。圓教正業等，皆觀法性，即是具足等戒。《淨名》云：「其能如此，是名奉律。」即此意也。理觀之戒即心而備，雖作此解身口多虧。或今生麁獷，或先世遮障未得懺悔，覆我三昧，脫門不開。思是事已，當自悲愍，深生改革：從今日始斷相續心，誓持禁戒事無瑕玷，護持愛惜如保浮囊，終不全身而損戒也。毒龍輸皮全蟻；須陀摩王失國獲偈。自戒化他，讚法讚者，大誓不動，稱佛名字為證為救。心誠感佛放淨戒光，能令毀禁者淨戒光觸時二世罪滅，即與理觀正業相應，一一須釋出之。事理既圓，畢竟持戒，入三脫門，見於佛性。是名「助油以增道明」。

This account of the awakening-factor of relinquishment through the branches of the path, though profound and subtle in contemplative principle, does not by itself preserve any actual practice; within the Tripiṭaka teaching, by contrast, phenomenal giving is heroic and fierce — gouging out one's own eye for a lamp, exchanging oneself to redeem another, giving away kingdom, city, wife, and child — and yet its own contemplative principle contains not the slightest trace of insight. Both approaches, taken alone, have their fault. Here, then, it is clarified that phenomenal giving assists in breaking through the covering of stinginess, advancing to bring contemplative principle to completion — how, then, could the two ever be separated? Should a person understand the contemplation of true reality's own perfect relinquishment in theory alone, yet, laying hand to chest, discuss only practice in the abstract while, in actual phenomenal affairs, remaining stingy and grasping — guarding his own property without relinquishing so much as a hair's breadth of it, shirking hardship, tallying up his own strength and effort, unable to bend himself to benefit another, cherishing his own life so dearly that he could never contend for another's death by yielding his own life instead — clinging to every matter he touches, unmoving as struck bronze, understanding alone but never practicing — how could a heavy covering of this kind ever be broken through? How could the three gates of liberation ever be opened at all? Now, at the place of practice, repenting with painstaking sincerity, giving rise to a resolute mind and raising a great vow, one relinquishes body, life, and property with utterly no regret at all. Carrying out this very giving oneself, and further teaching it to others, praising the dharma of giving, and rejoicing in those who give — having established this vow, one invokes the buddhas of the ten directions as witness and as rescue. Should the mind be genuinely sincere, free of all deceit, one is able to move the Tathāgata to emit the very light of giving, illuminating and removing the covering of stinginess — as explained in the Sūtra of Excellent Golden Light and elsewhere. Receiving this very light, one thereby corresponds with the awakening-factor of relinquishment found within every branch of the path, and this must be explained out, point by point. Phenomenal practice and contemplative principle both perfected, one is able to relinquish giving to the very ultimate — property treated like dung and dirt, body compared to a poisonous vessel, life like a passing cloud, casting all three away like spittle. The obstruction of stinginess thereby broken, the sense of the remedial path is accomplished, and liberation is thereby attained. Even should there be no immediate cause or condition for it, one still relies on it in carrying out the path; and should benefit indeed arise, one relinquishes it as easily as casting away a mustard seed. This is the oil of phenomenal practice assisting and increasing the brightness of the path, opening the three gates of liberation, and thereby seeing the buddha-nature directly. Should one fail to do this, there is no benefit of assistance and remedy at all. Should one cultivate exactly as explained above, one ought thereby to attain awakening; but should one fail to awaken even so, one ought to reflect that the contemplative principle of the branches of the path contains correct action, correct speech, and correct livelihood, and these belong to what the precepts gather in. In the Tripiṭaka teaching's own account of correct action and the rest, this is precisely careful guarding of one's own deportment, unbroken, undiminished, unperforated, unmixed. In the shared teaching's own account, correct action and the rest do not fix upon body or speech at all, but are true precisely within phenomenal action itself — this is precisely the precept that accords with the path, free of attachment. In the distinct teaching's own account, correct action and the rest are precisely the precept praised by the wise, self-mastering. In the perfect teaching's own account, correct action and the rest all contemplate the Dharma-nature directly, this being precisely the precept complete in every respect. The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "One who is able to do this is named one who upholds the discipline" — this is exactly that same sense. The precept of contemplative principle is already complete within the mind itself; and yet, though one may understand this, body and speech still frequently fall short of it. Whether because of coarseness and lack of restraint in this present life, or because of hindrances from a former life not yet repented of, this covers over one's own samādhi, and the gate of liberation fails to open. Reflecting on this matter, one ought to feel deep grief and compassion for oneself, and give rise to profound reform: from this very day, one severs the continuing mind of transgression, vowing to uphold the precepts without the slightest flaw, guarding and cherishing them as one would guard a floating bladder crossing the sea, never once giving up the precepts merely to preserve one's own body intact. The poison-dragon surrendered his own skin to spare the ants; King Sudāna lost his own kingdom yet gained the verse. Upholding the precepts oneself, and transforming others by it, praising the Dharma and praising those who uphold it, one's great vow unmoving, one invokes the Buddha's own name as witness and as rescue. Should the mind be genuinely sincere, one moves the Buddha to emit the very light of pure precepts, so that, at the very moment this light of pure precepts touches one who has broken the precepts, the offenses of both this life and the last are extinguished, thereby corresponding with the correct action of contemplative principle — and this, too, must be explained out, point by point. Phenomenal practice and contemplative principle both perfected, upholding the precepts to the very ultimate, one enters the three gates of liberation and sees the buddha-nature directly. This is named "the oil that assists and increases the brightness of the path."

如上修戒若不入者，當復思惟：是諸道品各有念根、念力、念覺分、正念等，即是忍義，羼提所攝。若三藏正念等，是伏忍。通教正念等，是柔順忍。別教正念等，是無生忍。圓教正念等，是寂滅忍。若人念力堅強，瞋恚之賊則不得入。而得入者，或因無念，或念不強而瞋蔽得起。或今世起，或前世起，或瞋同行、外護；或瞋現事，或追緣昔嫌；或初起屑屑，或初即隆盛。若恣瞋毒，傾蕩無遺，設不自在，如蛇自齧。瞋障百千法門，豈得恣之而不呵責？當知但有理解，未有忍力。既知是已，深生改悔發大誓願，卑如江海穢濁歸之，屈如橋梁人馬踐之。當耐勞苦，猶如射垛眾箭湊之，無恨無怨。如富樓那被罵，喜免手；乃至被刃，喜疾滅。無辜惱者忍力轉盛，如揩金磨鏡。羼提仙人強軟俱安。自忍化他，讚法讚者，大誓不動，稱十方佛為證為救。佛放忍光，二世瞋障重罪銷滅，得與事理諸念相應，於諸違境忍力成就。是為事油助增道明。

Should one, having cultivated the precepts as described above, still fail to enter thereby, one ought further to reflect that within every one of these branches of the path there is, in each case, the faculty of mindfulness, the power of mindfulness, the awakening-factor of mindfulness, and correct mindfulness — and these are exactly the sense of patience, gathered in by the pāramitā of patience. In the Tripiṭaka teaching's own account, correct mindfulness and the rest are the forbearance of subduing. In the shared teaching's own account, they are the forbearance of gentle compliance. In the distinct teaching's own account, they are the forbearance of the unborn. In the perfect teaching's own account, they are the forbearance of quiescent cessation. Should a person's own power of mindfulness be firm and strong, the very bandit of anger cannot gain entry at all. Yet should it gain entry nonetheless, this is either because mindfulness was altogether absent, or because mindfulness was too weak, and the covering of anger was thereby able to arise. It may arise in this very life, or it may arise from a former life; it may be anger toward a companion in practice or an outward protector; it may be anger at some present matter, or the pursuit of some old resentment recalled from the past; it may begin trivially, or it may begin already at full intensity. Should one indulge this very poison of anger unchecked, it overturns and sweeps away everything without remainder, and, unable to master oneself, one becomes like a snake biting its own body. Anger obstructs a hundred thousand Dharma-gates — how, then, could one possibly indulge it without rebuking it directly? One should know that merely having a theoretical understanding is not yet having the actual power of patience. Having recognized this, one gives rise to profound remorse and raises a great vow: as low as the rivers and seas, into which every defilement flows; as yielding as a bridge, trodden underfoot by every person and horse that crosses it. One ought to endure hardship, like a target absorbing every arrow shot at it, without resentment, without complaint. Like Pūrṇa, who, when reviled, rejoiced at having merely escaped a slap of the hand; and, even when struck by a blade, rejoiced at his own swift extinction. One who bears such affliction without any fault of his own finds his own power of patience only growing stronger, like gold polished, like a mirror ground smooth. The patience-sage Kṣāntivādin remained at peace whether treated harshly or gently. Cultivating patience oneself, and transforming others by it, praising the Dharma and praising those who practice it, one's great vow unmoving, one invokes the buddhas of the ten directions as witness and as rescue. The Buddha emits the very light of patience, and the grave offense of anger's own obstruction across both this life and the last is dissolved, thereby corresponding with every mindfulness of both phenomenal practice and contemplative principle, the power of patience accomplished amid every adverse circumstance. This is the oil of phenomenal practice assisting and increasing the brightness of the path.

Even so brief a moment can form so grave a karma — how much more, then, the evil thoughts accumulated across the long night of countless lifetimes, whose karma is boundless without limit? Karma is like a creditor demanding repayment, forever watching for its opportunity against a person. Should one directly repay this particular debt, no other karma drags one along in the meantime; but as this repayment nears completion, other karmic debts compete to seize hold, with no fixed term for either departure or continuance. The murderous demon of impermanence spares neither the mighty nor the worthy; how precarious and fragile, how unreliable a thing to place one's trust in! How, then, could one calmly expect to reach a hundred years of life? Racing in every direction, accumulating and hoarding wealth — before this accumulation is even complete, one suddenly departs forever. Whatever property and goods one possessed become, in the end, merely another's, while one departs alone into the darkness — who is left even to inquire whether this was right or wrong? Even a monk, however overflowing his own learning and understanding, or however diligently he extinguishes his own afflictions, may still fail to awaken to impermanence. As the proverb says: "Pitiable is one without even the five charms of beauty, and diligent effort without the mind of the path" — this is exactly that same sense. Should one recognize that impermanence surpasses even a raging flood, a fierce wind, flashing lightning, or the vastness of mountain, sea, and marketplace, with nowhere at all to escape it — contemplating in this way, the mind grows greatly fearful, unable to rest easy even in sleep, unable to find any food palatable, as though rushing to save one's own head from flame. Like a swift white colt, like a startled crow, racing day and night to seek the way out — how, then, could one still cling with greed to worldly wealth, entangling oneself in every mode of existence, performing deeds of no benefit at all, constructing further karma of birth-and-death? Abruptly severing every fetter, transcending it all and going straight forward, like a wild fox breaking free and leaping clear, racing to escape the burning house, seeking rescue and deliverance at the very earliest moment. This is breaking through the inversion of permanence.

Reading note: The five-stage decomposition of the body — from conception through decay to a corpse that offends "worse even than a dead dog" — is one of the most viscerally graphic passages in the entire treatise, and deliberately so: the treatise is explicit that "these five kinds are, every one, a genuine, realized contemplation, not merely an intellectual understanding," meaning the point is not to be read once but to be held in mind until the body's own habitual claim on affection genuinely loosens. The proverb about diligence "without the mind of the path" is a pointed aside aimed at monastics themselves — learning and even ascetic effort are no guarantee of having actually confronted impermanence, which is exactly the gap this whole passage exists to close.

The eye and form being empty, one is neither foolish nor wise; and so on, all the way through, mind and dharmas being empty, one is likewise neither foolish nor wise — this accords with the branches of the path's own tenfold wisdom, and this is named "the pāramitā of wisdom regulating the six faculties." This is the shared teaching's own account of regulating the six faculties, complete with the six pāramitās in full. Should the eye and form both be complete with the ten dharma-realms, the ten dharma-realms each having their own differing retribution, superior or inferior, and each cultivating their own cause at differing depths — cause and fruit being boundless, never to be exhausted — setting aside unknowing, distinguishing the marks of every dharma, with nothing at all received or clung to; and so on, all the way through, mind and dharmas being complete with the ten dharma-realms, distinguished without attachment — this accords with the branches of the path's own awakening-factors of removal and relinquishment, and this is named "the pāramitā of giving regulating the six faculties." Distinguishing the boundless marks and appearances of the eye, of form, and, all the way through, of mind and dharmas, never once mismatching a given capacity, never injuring another's own wholesome root, and never, oneself, being injured by boundless faculties and their objects — this accords with the branches of the path's own correct action, speech, and livelihood, and this is named "the pāramitā of the precepts regulating the six faculties." Further, amid the faculties and objects of the ten dharma-realms, whether in opposition or accordance, the mind remains unmoved, abiding securely within the middle of the provisional, able to bear with the very matter of accomplishing the path — this accords with the branches of the path's own mindfulness, and this is named "the pāramitā of patience regulating the six faculties." Further, distinguishing every faculty and object, should a thought of difficulty or hardship arise, one still does not retreat midway, showing courage amid birth-and-death itself — this accords with the branches of the path's own diligence, and this is named "the pāramitā of diligence regulating the six faculties." Further, distinguishing every faculty and object, the mind neither disturbed nor confused, neither shaken nor deviant — this accords with the branches of the path's own concentrations, and this is named "the pāramitā of dhyāna regulating the six faculties." Further, distinguishing every faculty and object, the power of the wisdom of the path in every kind administering medicine exactly as suited, skillful and adept in its means, and yet without any defiled attachment at all — this accords with the branches of the path's own wisdoms, and this is named "the pāramitā of wisdom regulating the six faculties." This, then, is the distinct teaching's own account of regulating the six faculties, complete with the six pāramitās in full.

復次，若如《殃掘摩羅經》云「所謂彼眼根，於諸如來常，具足無減修，了了分明見」者，「彼」是九法界眼根也。「於如來常」者，九界自謂各各非真；如來觀之，即佛法界，無二無別。「無減修」者，觀諸眼即佛眼，一心三諦，圓因具足，無有缺減也。「了了分明見」者，照實為了了，照權為分明；三智一心中，五眼具足圓照，名為「了了見佛性」也。見論圓證，修論圓因。又「具足修」者，觀於眼根，捨二邊漏，名為「檀」；眼根不為二邊所傷，名為「尸」；眼根寂滅，不為二邊所動，名為「羼提」；眼根及識自然流入薩婆若海，名為「精進」；觀眼實性，名為「上定」；以一切種智照眼中道，名為「智慧」。是為眼根具足無減修。無減故，了了分明見眼法界；乃至彼意根，於諸如來常，具足無減修，了了分明知。於一一根，即空、即假、即中，三觀一心，名「無減修」。證慧眼、法眼、佛眼，一心中得，名「了了見」。皆如上說。根既如此，塵亦復然；一切諸法亦復如是。是為圓教調伏諸根滿足六度。此則究竟調伏，究竟滿足；如是助道，助究竟道。當知六度遍能調伏一切諸根也。《大品》云：「施者、受者、財物不可得故，具足檀波羅蜜。」亡三事，無所著，正當檀體。應是具足者，行於財法二施，檀名具足，事理二圓。自他俱益，故名「具足」。事則破其慳法而能捨財；理則破其慳心而能捨法。二破二捨，體用具足，名「波羅蜜」也。

Furthermore, as the Aṅgulimāla Sūtra says, "This eye-faculty of theirs, with respect to every tathāgata's own constancy, is cultivated completely without deficiency, and sees with perfect clarity" — "theirs" refers to the eye-faculty of the nine dharma-realms. "With respect to every tathāgata's own constancy" means that the nine realms, taken each on their own, believe themselves not to be genuinely real; yet the Tathāgata, contemplating them, finds them to be precisely the buddha-dharma-realm itself, without duality, without distinction. "Cultivated completely without deficiency" means contemplating that every eye is itself already the buddha-eye, the three truths in a single mind, the perfect cause complete in full, with nothing whatsoever lacking. "Sees with perfect clarity" means illuminating the real is "perfect," and illuminating the provisional is "clarity"; the three wisdoms within a single mind, the five eyes complete and perfectly illuminating — this is named "seeing the buddha-nature with perfect clarity." Discussed in terms of seeing, this is the perfect realization; discussed in terms of cultivation, this is the perfect cause. Further, as for "cultivated completely": contemplating the eye-faculty, relinquishing the contamination of both extremes, is named "giving"; the eye-faculty not being injured by either extreme is named "the precepts"; the eye-faculty quiescent, unmoved by either extreme, is named "patience"; the eye-faculty and its consciousness flowing spontaneously into the very ocean of omniscience is named "diligence"; contemplating the eye's own true nature is named "the higher concentration"; and illuminating the eye's own middle way with the wisdom of all modes is named "wisdom." This is the eye-faculty cultivated completely without deficiency. Being without deficiency, one sees the dharma-realm of the eye with perfect clarity; and so on, all the way through, that same mind-faculty, with respect to every tathāgata's own constancy, is cultivated completely without deficiency, and knows with perfect clarity. Within each and every faculty, at once empty, at once provisional, at once the middle, the three contemplations in a single mind — this is named "cultivated without deficiency." Realizing the eye of wisdom, the Dharma-eye, and the buddha-eye, all attained within a single mind, is named "seeing with perfect clarity." All exactly as explained above. As it is for the faculty, so it is, likewise, for its object; and every other dharma proceeds in exactly the same way. This, then, is the perfect teaching's own account of regulating the six faculties, complete with the six pāramitās in full. This, then, is the ultimate regulation, the ultimate completeness; auxiliary practice of this kind assists the ultimate path itself. One should therefore know that the six pāramitās are able to regulate every faculty everywhere, without exception. The Larger Sūtra says: "Since the giver, the receiver, and the property given can none of them be found, this is precisely the perfection of giving complete in full." Extinguishing these three matters, with nothing at all clung to — this is exactly the very substance of giving itself. As for what makes it "complete": carrying out both giving of property and giving of Dharma, giving is thereby named "complete," both phenomenal practice and contemplative principle perfected together. Benefiting both oneself and others at once, this is exactly why it is named "complete." Phenomenally, one breaks through the very dharma of stinginess and is thereby able to relinquish property; in terms of principle, one breaks through the very mind of stinginess and is thereby able to relinquish Dharma. These two forms of breaking-through, these two forms of relinquishing, their substance and function both complete — this is named "pāramitā."

Reading note: The Aṅgulimāla Sūtra's claim that "this eye-faculty of theirs... is cultivated completely without deficiency" is a striking reversal: rather than the eye needing to be purified of its ordinary, deluded functioning, it is already, examined correctly, "itself already the buddha-eye" — the perfect teaching's own reading finds no separate object to cultivate toward, only a mark to see with the clarity that was always structurally available. This closes the mapping of the six pāramitās onto the regulation of the six faculties across all Four Teachings; the fascicle now turns to how the same six pāramitās gather in everything else that constitutes a buddha's own attainment.

云何六度攝佛威儀？佛以十力、無畏、不共法等為威儀。一心中修四道品，名「修佛威儀」；證佛眼、佛智，名「得佛威儀」。今逐語便，約道品明攝十力者：若四種道品，即是四種四諦智決定因果。知生滅之集，決受三界之苦，斯有是處；生滅之集，若至無餘涅槃，斯無是處。若生滅之道，能盡苦入涅槃，斯有是處；生滅之道，若至三界，斯無是處。乃至無作之集，通至變易，斯有是處；若通至無上涅槃，無有是處。若無作道、滅，通至一切種智，斯有是處；若通至二乘，無有是處。是四因果一心中知，決判明斷，名「是處非處力」。故如來於佛法中作師子吼：「獨我法中有四沙門果！」即此義也。業報智力者，知四種集是知業，知苦是知報，道滅亦爾。分別四種業報淺深不謬，是二力也。知禪定力者，四種道諦中八定，分別深淺，照了不差，是三力也。知根、欲、性力者，知過去苦集不同名根力；知現在苦集樂欲不同名欲力；知未來苦集得失不同名性力。是四五六力也。知至處道力者，知四道諦所至之處，是七力也。知宿命、天眼力者，照過去一世多世、種性好惡、壽命長短名宿命力；照未來生處好醜名天眼力。是名八九力也。漏盡力者，四種滅諦所證無漏心慧等解脫也。是一法門而有四種者，如王密語，智臣解意。佛說十力赴四種機，不令小者謗大，傷其功德；不令大者得小，抑其善根。彼彼獨聞，各各獲利，無謀權巧，故號「能仁」。菩薩智臣深解密語，知意在三藏，即問生滅，鄭重諮詢，令有緣疾悟；乃至知意在圓，或頌無作，或問無作，令他得解。一音殊唱，萬聽咸悅，口密無邊，義不可盡。上作四釋，何足致疑耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

How, then, do the six pāramitās gather in the Buddha's own dignified bearing? The Buddha takes the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, the dharmas not shared with others, and the rest as his very dignified bearing. Cultivating the four branches of the path within a single mind is named "cultivating the Buddha's own dignified bearing"; realizing the buddha-eye and buddha-wisdom is named "attaining the Buddha's own dignified bearing." Here, then, following the text's own natural order, clarifying how the branches of the path gather in the ten powers: the four branches of the path are themselves already the four kinds of Four Noble Truths' own wisdom, determining cause and fruit decisively. Knowing that the origin belonging to generation-and-extinction decisively brings about the suffering of the three realms — this is "a valid case"; should the origin belonging to generation-and-extinction lead all the way to nirvāṇa without remainder — this is "not a valid case." Should the path belonging to generation-and-extinction be able to exhaust suffering and enter nirvāṇa — this is "a valid case"; should the path belonging to generation-and-extinction instead lead back into the three realms — this is "not a valid case." And so on, all the way through: should the origin belonging to the unconditioned lead to transformational birth-and-death — this is "a valid case"; should it lead to the unsurpassed nirvāṇa itself — this is "not a valid case." Should the path and cessation belonging to the unconditioned lead to the wisdom of all modes — this is "a valid case"; should they lead merely to the two lesser vehicles — this is "not a valid case." Knowing these four pairs of cause and fruit within a single mind, judging and distinguishing them with perfect clarity, is named "the power of knowing what is and is not a valid case." Hence the Tathāgata, within the Buddha's own Dharma, roars the lion's own roar: "Only within my own Dharma are there the four fruits of the śramaṇa!" — this is exactly that same sense. As for the power of knowing karma and its retribution: knowing the four kinds of origin is knowing karma; knowing suffering is knowing retribution; and the same holds for the path and cessation. Distinguishing the shallow and deep degrees of the four kinds of karma and retribution without error is the second power. As for the power of knowing meditative concentration: distinguishing the depth of the eightfold concentration found within the four kinds of the truth of the path, illuminating it without the slightest deviation, is the third power. As for the power of knowing faculty, aspiration, and disposition: knowing that past suffering and its origin differ from one being to the next is named the power of faculty; knowing that present suffering, its origin, and delight and aspiration differ from being to being is named the power of aspiration; knowing that future suffering, its origin, gain, and loss differ from being to being is named the power of disposition. These are the fourth, fifth, and sixth powers. As for the power of knowing the destination of every path: knowing the destination reached by each of the four kinds of the truth of the path is the seventh power. As for the powers of knowing past lives and the divine eye: illuminating one's own or another's past life, or many past lives, their lineage's own excellence or fault, their life-span long or short, is named the power of knowing past lives; illuminating the excellence or fault of future places of rebirth is named the power of the divine eye. These are named the eighth and ninth powers. As for the power of the exhaustion of contamination: this is the uncontaminated mind, wisdom, and liberation realized through the four kinds of the truth of cessation. As for why this single Dharma-gate should have four distinct versions: it is like a king's own secret message, which only his wise minister can decode. The Buddha teaches the ten powers to meet four distinct capacities, so that the lesser may not slander the greater and thereby injure their own merit, and so that the greater may not settle for the lesser and thereby suppress their own wholesome root. Each capacity, hearing only its own version, gains its own benefit, without any contrivance or artifice at all — hence he is named "the Capable One." The bodhisattva, as wise minister, deeply understands this secret message: knowing the intent lies with the Tripiṭaka teaching, he asks specifically about generation-and-extinction, inquiring earnestly and respectfully, so that those with the affinity for it may swiftly awaken; and so on, all the way through, knowing the intent lies with the perfect teaching, he either recites or inquires about the unconditioned, so that others may attain understanding. A single voice, uttered uniquely, and ten thousand listeners, every one delighted — the secret held within the spoken word is boundless, its sense inexhaustible. Given these four explanations above, what room could there possibly be left for doubt?

問：十力是佛威儀，初心云何能學？云何能得？答：《大論》云：「菩薩行般若，十力、無畏不應住。若佛於佛法無有過失，是則應住；若菩薩無佛法，何所論住？」釋云：菩薩修佛功德多生重著；破此重心，故言「不應住」；又菩薩分得十力、無畏，既未究竟，故不應住。若爾，前雖修而未得，後語入位，何關初心？若依《華嚴．十住品》云：「菩薩因初發心得十力分。」正念天子問法慧云：「初心大士修十力方便，云何知家非家，出家學道？云何方便修習梵行，具十住道，速成菩提？」答云：「菩薩先當分別十種之法，謂三業及佛、法、僧、戒。若身是梵行，梵行渾濁，八萬戶蟲；若身業是梵行，四儀顧眄，舉足下足。若口是梵行，音聲觸心，脣齒舌動；若口業是梵行，則是語言。乃至戒是梵行，戒場十眾問清淨，戒師白四羯磨，剃髮、乞食等皆非梵行。梵行為在何處？誰有梵行？三世平等猶如虛空，是名方便。又更修習增上十法，所謂十力，甚深無量。如是觀者，疾得一切諸佛功德，初發心時便成正覺，知一切法真實之性，具足慧身，不由他悟。」如此明文，豈非初心修證十力？又《地持》云：「菩薩知如來藏，聞思前行，修自性禪，得入一切禪。一切禪有三種：一、現法樂，現法樂故稱歡喜地；二、出生十力種性三摩跋提及二乘除入；三、利益眾生禪也。」十住名聞慧，十行名思慧；此聞思前，以修自性入一切禪，得具三法。豈非初心有修有證？三據明矣。道品、六度及佛十力宛轉相攝，皆如上說。若修道品、六度，即是修佛十力；若調伏諸根，滿足六度，即是滿足十力，住佛威儀無異也。《十住毘婆沙》云：「力名扶助，氣力不可窮盡。」《地持》云：「得勝、堪能，名為『力』。」於十處悉如實，離虛妄，勝於魔，自行故，名「得勝」；能以方便利益眾生，故言「堪能」。然佛力無量，何止言十？實是一智緣十事，故言「十」。此十化眾生足。舉十，餘亦可知。《殃掘》云：「十力是聲聞宗，非摩訶衍；大乘有無量力。」此二釋彌顯四種十力意。

Question: the ten powers are the Buddha's own dignified bearing — how could a beginning practitioner possibly study them? How could he possibly attain them? Answer: the Great Treatise says: "The bodhisattva, practicing prajñā, ought not to abide in the ten powers or the fearlessnesses. Should the Buddha have no fault at all within the Buddha's own Dharma, then he ought indeed to abide there; but should the bodhisattva have no such Buddha-dharma yet, what abiding could possibly even be discussed?" Explained thus: the bodhisattva cultivating the Buddha's own merit tends readily to grow heavily attached to it; breaking through this heavy attachment is exactly why it is said he "ought not to abide" there; and further, the bodhisattva attains the ten powers and fearlessnesses only in part, not yet the ultimate, and this too is why he ought not to abide there. If this is so, though he cultivates them beforehand without yet attaining them, and only later, described as entering the proper stage, actually attains them — what relevance could this have to the beginning practitioner at all? According to the Avataṃsaka Sūtra's own "Chapter on the Ten Abidings": "The bodhisattva, on account of the very first arising of the resolve, already attains a portion of the ten powers." The heavenly son Correct Mindfulness asks Dharma-Wisdom: "How does the great being of the very first resolve cultivate the skillful means of the ten powers, knowing the household to be no true household, and leaving home to study the path? How does he cultivate the practice of pure conduct through skillful means, complete with the path of the ten abidings, swiftly accomplishing bodhi?" The answer is given: "The bodhisattva ought first to distinguish ten kinds of dharma, namely the three karmas together with the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṃgha, and the precepts. Should the body itself be pure conduct, pure conduct would then be turbid, harboring eighty thousand households of worms within it; should bodily karma itself be pure conduct, this would be merely the four postures, a glance, the lifting and setting down of a foot. Should speech itself be pure conduct, this would be merely sound touching the mind, lip, tooth, and tongue moving; should verbal karma itself be pure conduct, this would be merely language itself. And so on, all the way through: should the precepts themselves be pure conduct, the ten-person assembly at the ordination platform inquiring after purity, the precept-master proclaiming the fourfold formal procedure, the shaving of the head, the begging for alms, and the rest, would all fail to be pure conduct at all. Where, then, does pure conduct actually reside? Who, then, actually possesses pure conduct? The three times, equal throughout, like empty space itself — this is named 'skillful means.' Further cultivating the ten superior dharmas, namely the ten powers, profound and boundless — one who contemplates in this way swiftly attains every merit of every buddha, and, at the very moment the resolve first arises, already accomplishes correct awakening, knowing the true nature of every dharma, complete with the very body of wisdom, awakened not through any other." Given so clear a text as this, how could this fail to be the beginning practitioner cultivating and realizing the ten powers? Further, the Bodhisattvabhūmi says: "The bodhisattva, knowing the tathāgatagarbha, with hearing and reflection as its own preceding practice, cultivates the dhyāna of inherent nature, and thereby enters every dhyāna. Every dhyāna is of three kinds: first, the bliss of the present dharma, and, on account of this present bliss, it is named the ground of joy; second, the very seed-nature giving birth to the ten powers, and samāpatti, together with what the two lesser vehicles set aside; third, the dhyāna that benefits sentient beings." The ten abidings are named the wisdom of hearing; the ten practices are named the wisdom of reflection; and it is this very hearing and reflection, prior to all else, cultivating the dhyāna of inherent nature and entering every dhyāna, that attains all three dharmas complete. How could this fail to be the beginning practitioner both cultivating and realizing at once? These three sources of evidence make this entirely clear. The branches of the path, the six pāramitās, and the Buddha's own ten powers mutually gather one another in, turn by turn, exactly as explained above. Cultivating the branches of the path and the six pāramitās is itself already cultivating the Buddha's own ten powers; regulating the six faculties and completing the six pāramitās is itself already completing the ten powers, abiding in the Buddha's own dignified bearing without any difference at all. The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā says: "'Power' is named for its support and assistance, its own vital strength inexhaustible." The Bodhisattvabhūmi says: "Attaining superiority and capability is what is named 'power.'" Being genuinely real throughout the ten domains, free of falsehood, surpassing Māra himself, through one's own practice — this is named "attaining superiority"; being able to benefit sentient beings through skillful means — this is named "capability." Yet the Buddha's own power is boundless — why, then, stop at merely ten? In truth, it is a single wisdom taking ten matters as its object, and this is exactly why it is spoken of as "ten." These ten alone already suffice to transform sentient beings; given these ten, the rest may likewise be known. The Aṅgulimāla Sūtra says: "The ten powers belong to the śrāvaka's own tradition, not the Great Vehicle; the Great Vehicle possesses boundless powers." These two accounts together make the sense of the fourfold ten powers all the more manifest.

云何道品攝四無所畏？一切智無畏者，即是備知四種苦諦，為他分別，明示過患，決定師子吼無微畏相，無能難言是法非法。障道無畏者，四種集諦障四道滅，決定師子吼無微畏相，無能難言此非障道。盡苦道無畏者，四種道諦能行是道，得盡苦出世間，決定師子吼無微畏相。無漏無畏者，即四種滅諦各有所證、各有所滅，決定師子吼無微畏相。道品、無畏，宛轉相攝。若修道品、六度，即是修無畏，住佛威儀也。《大論》云：「內心具足名為『力』；外用無怯名『無畏』。」《十住毘婆沙》云：「一法名無畏，云何言四？於四事中無疑，故名『四』。」佛應於一切法無畏，云何但四？舉大要，開事端，餘亦無畏也。攝十八不共法者：初「身、口無失」，此二是四種道品正業、語、命也。得供不高，逢毀不下，名「無不定心」。四威儀恒在定，名「無不知已捨」。此二法是四種道品中八種定也。修身戒心慧不可盡，名「欲無減」。慈悲度人，安住寂滅，不增不減，名「精進無減」。無量劫為一切眾生受苦，不疲不厭，名「念無減」。此三法是四種道品中八種精進也。常照三世眾生心，不須更觀而為說法，不失先念，名「慧無減」。憶三世事不忘，名「解脫無減」。自然覺悟，不同二乘，名「解脫知見無減」。一切身業，智慧為本，得無礙智說不可盡，名「身業共智慧行」；「口、意共智慧」亦如是。凡十一法，是四種道品中十種之慧。結成攝法，意如上說。攝四無礙智者：法無礙是四種四諦名字之法，名字從心分別；若無心者，誰為作名？既達一心無量心，亦知一名無量名，名不可盡是名「法無礙」。義無礙者，諸法諸名皆歸一義，所謂如實義，名「義無礙」。辭無礙者，十法界眾生言辭不同，皆悉解了。十界音辭入一音辭，知一界即解十界，無有罣礙，名「辭無礙」。又法是四諦法門，義是四種道諦，辭是四種苦諦云云。樂說無礙者，以四種四諦巧赴機緣，旋轉交絡說不可盡，令他樂聞。於一字中說一切字、一切義，赴一切音，當其根性各沾利益。結攝意如上說。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, then, do the branches of the path gather in the four fearlessnesses? The fearlessness of omniscience: this is being fully equipped to know the four kinds of the truth of suffering, distinguishing them for others, clearly showing their faults and failings, roaring the lion's own roar with resolute certainty, with not the slightest trace of fear, so that none can challenge whether this is truly the Dharma or not. The fearlessness of knowing what obstructs the path: the four kinds of the truth of origin obstruct the four kinds of the path and cessation, and one roars the lion's own roar with resolute certainty, with not the slightest trace of fear, so that none can challenge that this is indeed what obstructs the path. The fearlessness of the path that exhausts suffering: the four kinds of the truth of the path are able to be carried out, attaining the exhaustion of suffering and transcendence of the world, and one roars the lion's own roar with resolute certainty, with not the slightest trace of fear. The fearlessness of the uncontaminated: the four kinds of the truth of cessation, each with its own realization, each with its own extinguishing, and one roars the lion's own roar with resolute certainty, with not the slightest trace of fear. The branches of the path and the fearlessnesses gather one another in, turn by turn. Cultivating the branches of the path and the six pāramitās is itself already cultivating fearlessness, abiding in the Buddha's own dignified bearing. The Great Treatise says: "Being complete within one's own inward mind is named 'power'; its outward function, free of any timidity, is named 'fearlessness.'" The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā says: "This is, in truth, a single dharma named fearlessness — why, then, is it spoken of as four? Because there is no doubt with respect to four distinct matters, it is hence named 'four.'" The Buddha ought to be fearless with respect to every dharma without exception — why, then, only four? This raises only the essential outline, opening up the matter's own starting-points; the rest, too, is likewise fearless. As for gathering in the eighteen dharmas not shared with others: first, "body and speech without fault" — these two are precisely the four branches of the path's own correct action, speech, and livelihood. Receiving offerings without growing proud, meeting slander without growing dejected, is named "the mind without any instability." The four postures constantly abiding in concentration is named "no lack of full awareness." These two dharmas are the eightfold concentration found among the four branches of the path. Cultivating body, precepts, mind, and wisdom inexhaustibly is named "aspiration without diminishment." Compassion delivering beings, abiding securely in quiescent cessation, neither increasing nor decreasing, is named "diligence without diminishment." Bearing suffering for the sake of every sentient being across boundless kalpas, without weariness, without aversion, is named "mindfulness without diminishment." These three dharmas are the eightfold diligence found among the four branches of the path. Constantly illuminating the minds of sentient beings across the three times, needing no further contemplation before teaching the Dharma, never losing one's own prior recollection, is named "wisdom without diminishment." Recollecting the affairs of the three times without forgetting is named "liberation without diminishment." Spontaneous awakening, unlike the two lesser vehicles, is named "the knowledge and vision of liberation without diminishment." Every bodily action, taking wisdom as its very root, attaining unimpeded wisdom, its own expression inexhaustible, is named "bodily action accompanied by wisdom in its function"; and "verbal and mental action accompanied by wisdom" proceed in exactly the same way. These eleven dharmas, in all, are the tenfold wisdom found among the four branches of the path. Gathering these together completes the account of what is gathered in, the sense exactly as explained above. As for gathering in the four unimpeded understandings: unimpeded understanding of dharma is the very naming of the four kinds of Four Noble Truths, names distinguished from the mind itself — but were there no mind at all, who would be there to give rise to any name? Having already penetrated a single mind's own boundless minds, one likewise knows a single name's own boundless names, names inexhaustible — this is named "unimpeded understanding of dharma." Unimpeded understanding of meaning: every dharma, every name, converges upon a single meaning, namely the meaning of reality as it truly is — this is named "unimpeded understanding of meaning." Unimpeded understanding of language: the ten dharma-realms' own sentient beings speak in differing languages, every one of them fully understood. The language of the ten realms entering into a single language, knowing one realm is thereby understanding all ten, with no obstruction at all — this is named "unimpeded understanding of language." Further, dharma here is the Dharma-gate of the Four Noble Truths; meaning is the four kinds of the truth of the path; language is the four kinds of the truth of suffering, and so on accordingly. Unimpeded understanding of eloquent expression: using the four kinds of Four Noble Truths, skillfully meeting each capacity and condition, turning and interweaving endlessly in expression, causing others to delight in hearing it. Within a single word, expressing every word, every meaning, meeting every sound, so that each capacity and nature receives its own benefit accordingly. The sense of gathering this together is exactly as explained above.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for gathering in the six spiritual powers: the divine eye, divine ear, and mind-made body are as explained within the regulation of the six faculties; knowing others' minds, past lives, and the exhaustion of contamination are as explained within the ten powers. As for gathering in the three insights, this is as explained within the six spiritual powers. As for gathering in the four means of embrace: giving as embrace is precisely the awakening-factors of removal and relinquishment found among the four branches of the path; loving speech is precisely the correct action, speech, and livelihood found among the four branches of the path; beneficial conduct and working alongside others are precisely the eightfold concentration found among the four branches of the path — since concentration possesses spiritual power, it is able to accomplish beneficial conduct and working alongside others accordingly. As for gathering in the dhāraṇīs: upholding every wholesome dharma is like a whole, unbroken vessel holding water; warding off every unwholesome dharma is like a thorn hedge guarding a fruit orchard. This is precisely the four right efforts found among the four branches of the path, diligently warding off the two evils and diligently generating the two forms of wholesomeness. Hence the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā verse says: "Severing evil already arisen is like removing a venomous snake; severing evil not yet arisen is like guarding beforehand against a flood; increasing wholesomeness already arisen is like watering a sweet fruit sapling; bringing forth wholesomeness not yet arisen is like drilling wood to bring forth fire." As for gathering in the thirty-two marks: the Vibhāṣā says: "Within the 'Chapter on Marks' of the Abhidharma, each and every mark is distinguished threefold, namely the mark's own substance, the mark's own karma, and the mark's own fruit." The Great Treatise says: "Across a hundred kalpas, the thirty-two marks were planted as seed" — this is exactly that same sense. Applying this back to the Tripiṭaka teaching's own branches of the path and six pāramitās, one never departs, in the end, from giving, the precepts, wisdom, and the rest. The text is too elaborate to record in full here, but the sense of gathering may be known accordingly. As for the shared teaching's own account of the mark's substance, karma, and fruit, this differs from the account given above. Should one seek the Buddha by way of marks alone, the wheel-turning sage-king would then be the Tathāgata himself — one who does this walks a deviant path. The Buddha teaches that the thirty-two marks are, in truth, not the thirty-two marks at all — each and every one of them entirely purified through the mind of emptiness, corresponding with emptiness itself, is what is properly named "a mark." The Vibhāṣā, too, says: "The bodhisattva, with a single mind, cultivates the karma of the thirty-two marks, taking wisdom, throughout, as the very root" — this is precisely the wisdom of emptiness. If this is so, the thirty-two marks are, every one, gathered in by the tenfold wisdom of the branches of the path and the pāramitā of wisdom — this is exactly the shared teaching's own sense. Furthermore, the two prior teaching-gates of the branches of the path clarify only the cause, attaining the cultivation of the mark's own karma; discussed in terms of fruit, they attain only the mark's own substance. But this mark is only slightly superior to the wheel-turning king's own marks, since even Māra is able to conjure an imitation of it, and it is hence nothing truly extraordinary; and, upon entering nirvāṇa without remainder, the mark itself is extinguished forever. It is like obtaining bronze, which cannot reflect one's own face. The two lesser vehicles, together with the Tripiṭaka teaching's own buddha, alike attain only the real truth, without any image of the dharma-realm itself. One should therefore know that the two prior branches of the path are not the dharma that cultivates the mark; only the latter two branches of the path are the dharma that truly cultivates the mark. The Lotus Sūtra says: "Deeply penetrating the mark of offense and merit, illuminating the ten directions everywhere at once, the sublime and pure Dharma-body, complete with the thirty-two marks." Should one realize the middle way, the middle way is itself already complete with this very mark. As in the Lotus Sūtra, where the two lesser vehicles are opened, shown, awakened, and enter into the sublime accord with the middle way, they are thereby granted the prediction of buddhahood with the eight aspects. It is like obtaining a mirror, in which ten thousand images are bound to take form. The Great Vehicle, attaining the middle, has nothing whatsoever that fails to manifest; the mark of the Dharma-body is what is properly named "the true mark." The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "Already relinquished every mark and excellent feature found in the mundane world." The wheel-turning king and Māra both adorn themselves with mundane marks, all of them alike illusory and false, and this is exactly why it is said, "already relinquished." The bright mirror of the middle way is, at root, without any mark at all; and being markless and yet still marked, its beauty or ugliness follows from what it reflects, appearing everywhere, in whatever measure the condition calls for, manifesting the very form of a body — this is precisely the true mark. The Contemplation of Amitāyus Sūtra says: "Why does the Buddha Amitābha have eighty-four thousand marks, and each and every mark eighty-four thousand excellent features?" The Mahābherī Sūtra and the Avataṃsaka Sūtra both say: "The marks form a great ocean of marks; the excellent features form a lesser ocean of excellent features." Since it is already spoken of as "an ocean of marks," how could this possibly be confined to merely thirty-two? Since conditions differ from one being to the next, their number, too, varies accordingly. This true and real mark is gathered in by the distinct and perfect teachings' own branches of the path, and the sense of this may be known of itself, not recorded here in full. One should therefore know that the six pāramitās, as the auxiliary path, gather in every wholesome dharma without limit, without boundary. The twelve items raised above are given only to show the very starting-point of this sense; the rest, too, may be known to be gathered in accordingly. If even the auxiliary path is thus, how much more so, then, the correct path itself, and so on accordingly.

Reading note: The closing move on the thirty-two marks — "the Buddha teaches that the thirty-two marks are, in truth, not the thirty-two marks at all" — extends the Diamond Sūtra's own famous negation to the very iconography most associated with buddhahood itself: a wheel-turning king can display the same physical marks, and even Māra can counterfeit them, so the "true mark" cannot be the marks as such but only the Dharma-body's own markless mirror, which "beauty or ugliness follows from what it reflects." With this, Mode Seven, auxiliary remedies, closes: the six pāramitās, run outward from six specific antidotes to six specific coverings, have been shown to gather in literally everything a buddha possesses — the regulation of the six faculties, the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, the unshared dharmas, the unimpeded understandings, the spiritual powers, the means of embrace, the dhāraṇīs, and the marks themselves.

○第八、明次位者：夫真、似二位，有解脫知見，朱紫分明。終不謬謂未得謂得，計四善根以為初果，初果為無學，自知所斷證、未斷證。雖四門名位有殊，斷及諦理孱然不異。二乘多論一生斷結，時節既促，教門所明大同小異，不過迭動。菩薩教門非但時長、行遠，智斷亦別，徑路乃殊，歸途一也。六度初僧祇未知作佛；二僧祇知而不說；三僧祇自知亦說；百劫種大人相，具五功德名「不退地」，皆似位也。坐道場成佛，方名「真位」。此教初淺尚有次位，豈有凡夫造心即言上位？此非增上慢，推與誰乎？通教二乘真、似之位，智異三藏，斷位不殊。若菩薩位，條然不同。簡名義通、別，如《法華玄》云云。別教惑斷智位，二乘聾啞非其境界，故名為「別」。一往望《攝論》、《華嚴》所明地位，即是其義。但別義多途，赴機異說：橫則四門不同，竪則階降深淺，不可定執一經而相是非。又菩薩或造通論釋經，或造別論釋經。如龍樹作千部論；天親及諸菩薩論復何量？度此者少，那得苦專一意，非撥餘門？若苟且抑揚，失佛方便，自招毀損；欲望通途，翻成哽塞。今明別位，四門異說，種種不同。雖阡陌經緯，其致一也。此方雖未有多論，而前四門推之。若通教說種種位，知其同是真諦；別教說種種位，知其同是中道。經言：「雖說種種道，其實為一乘」；「其所說法，皆悉到於一切智地」。得此意者，狐疑易息，鬪諍不生。上破思假中已略說諸位，若欲知者往彼尋看云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

Eighth, clarifying one's own stage: the genuine and the resembling stages both possess the knowledge and vision of liberation, distinguished as clearly as crimson from purple. One must never mistakenly claim to have attained what has not yet been attained, calculating the four wholesome roots as though they were already the first fruit, or the first fruit as though it were already beyond further training — one must, rather, know for oneself precisely what has and has not yet been severed and realized. Though the four gates differ in the names given to their stages, the actual severance and the principle of the truth involved remain, in substance, no different at all. The two lesser vehicles, for the most part, discuss severing bondage within a single lifetime, and since their own time-frame is so compressed, what their teaching-gates clarify is largely the same, with only minor variation, amounting to little more than a shift in emphasis. The bodhisattva's own teaching-gate, by contrast, is not merely a matter of far greater time and far longer practice — its wisdom and severance differ as well, its direct path taking an altogether different route, though its final destination remains one and the same. Within the six pāramitās' own account: in the first incalculable eon, one does not yet know one will become a buddha; in the second incalculable eon, one knows it but does not yet speak of it; in the third incalculable eon, one both knows it and speaks of it; and across a hundred further kalpas, one plants the seed of the great being's own marks, complete with the five merits, named "the stage of non-regression" — all of these are, nonetheless, merely resembling stages. Only sitting at the seat of enlightenment and accomplishing buddhahood is properly named "the genuine stage." Even this teaching, shallow as it is at the outset, still retains its own sequence of stages — how, then, could an ordinary being, the very moment a thought first arises, possibly claim to have already reached the highest stage? If this is not overweening pride, then to what, exactly, could it be attributed? In the shared teaching's own account, the genuine and resembling stages of the two lesser vehicles differ in wisdom from the Tripiṭaka teaching, yet do not differ in the stage of severance itself. As for the bodhisattva's own stage, this differs distinctly and completely. Distinguishing the sense of "shared" and "distinct" by name is explained in the *Lotus Sūtra's Profound Meaning*, and so on accordingly. In the distinct teaching's own account of the stages of severing affliction and of wisdom, the two lesser vehicles, deaf and mute to it, are altogether outside its own domain, and this is exactly why it is named "distinct." Taken in one respect, this corresponds to the very stages clarified in the *Compendium of the Mahāyāna* and the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* — this is exactly that same sense. Yet the distinct teaching's own sense branches out in many directions, meeting each capacity with a differing account: taken horizontally, its four gates differ from one another; taken vertically, its stages descend through differing depths — one cannot fix rigidly upon a single sūtra alone and judge every other account right or wrong by it. Bodhisattvas, further, at times compose general treatises explaining the sūtras, and at times compose specific treatises explaining them instead. Nāgārjuna alone composed a thousand treatises; how, then, could Vasubandhu's own treatises, and those of every other bodhisattva besides, ever be fully reckoned? Those who have truly gone beyond this are few indeed — how, then, could one bitterly cling to a single, exclusive sense, dismissing every other gate outright? Should one carelessly favor one account and disparage another, one loses the Buddha's own skillful means and brings ruin upon oneself; wishing, instead, to seek the general, common path, one instead ends up choked and blocked by one's own contrivance. Here, then, the distinct teaching's own stages are clarified, its four gates giving differing accounts, various and unalike — yet, though crisscrossing like the paths and furrows of a field, their ultimate direction remains one and the same. This land may not yet possess many treatises of its own, but the four gates discussed earlier already allow this to be inferred. Should the shared teaching speak of various different stages, one knows they are, alike, the very same real truth; should the distinct teaching speak of various different stages, one knows they are, alike, the very same middle way. The sūtra says: "Though I speak of every kind of path, in truth it is all for the sake of the one vehicle alone"; and, "Whatever Dharma is spoken, it all, without exception, arrives at the very ground of omniscient wisdom." Grasping this sense, doubt and suspicion are easily put to rest, and no contention arises at all. Above, in the treatment of breaking through habituated thought, the various stages have already been briefly explained — should one wish to know them in detail, one may go there to look into it accordingly.

Reading note: "If this is not overweening pride, then to what, exactly, could it be attributed?" is the treatise's own sharpest warning yet against a specific failure mode this fascicle is building toward — mistaking resemblance for realization. That even the six-pāramitā path's own "stage of non-regression," reached only after a hundred kalpas of merit, still counts as merely "resembling" rather than "genuine" sets an exceptionally high bar, precisely so that no ordinary practitioner mistakes early insight for arrival. The insistence that rival treatises by Nāgārjuna, Vasubandhu, and others need not be adjudicated against one another — "their ultimate direction remains one and the same" — extends the fascicle's running refusal to treat doctrinal plurality as doctrinal conflict.

又今有十意融通佛法。一、明道理寂絕亡離，不可思議。即是四諦、三、二、一、無，隨情智等，或開或合。若識此意，權實道理冷然自照。二、教門綱格匡骨盤峙，包括密、露，涇渭大小。即是漸、頓、不定、祕密，藏、通、別、圓。若得此意，聲教開合，化道可知。三、經論矛盾，言義相乖，不可以情通，不可以博解。古來執諍連代不消；若得四悉檀意，則結滯開融，懷抱瑣析，拔擲自在，不惑此疑彼也。四、若知謬執而生塞著，巧破盡淨。單、複、具足、無言窮逐，能破如所破，有何所得耶？五、結正法門，對當行位。修有方便，證有階差。權實、大小、賢聖不濫，增上慢罪從何而生？六、於一法門縱橫無礙，綸緒次第亹亹成章。七、開章科段，鉤鎖相承，生起可愛。八、帖釋經文，婉轉繡媚。總用上諸方法，隨語消釋，義順而文當。九、翻譯梵漢，名數兼通，使方言不壅。十、一一句偈如聞而修，入心成觀。觀與經合，觀則有印；印心作觀，非數他寶。唯翻譯名數，未暇廣尋九意，不與世間文字法師共，亦不與事相禪師共。一種禪師，唯有觀心一意，或淺、或偽，餘九全無。此非虛言，後賢有眼者當證知也。次位者，十意之一也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are, here, ten senses that bring the Buddha's own Dharma into thorough accord. First, clarifying principle itself, quiescent, cut off, utterly beyond all grasping, inconceivable — this is precisely the Four Noble Truths, the three, the two, the one, and the none, following sentiment and wisdom variously, at times opened out, at times gathered together. Should one recognize this sense, the principle of the provisional and the real illuminates itself with perfect clarity. Second, the very framework and skeleton of the teaching-gates, standing coiled and firm, encompassing the esoteric and the exoteric, distinguishing the greater from the lesser as clearly as the Jing and Wei rivers — this is precisely the gradual, the sudden, the indeterminate, the secret, and the Tripiṭaka, shared, distinct, and perfect teachings. Grasping this sense, the opening and gathering of the verbal teaching, and the very path of transformation itself, may both be known. Third, apparent contradictions between sūtra and treatise, where word and sense seem to conflict with one another — these cannot be reconciled through mere sentiment, nor resolved through mere broad learning alone. Clinging disputes over this have persisted, unresolved, across the generations since antiquity; but should one grasp the sense of the four siddhāntas, every knot and obstruction opens and dissolves, every fragment held within one's own breast is freed and cast loose at will, and one is no longer confused by this account or doubtful of that one. Fourth, should one recognize that mistaken clinging gives rise to blockage and attachment, one skillfully breaks through it entirely, leaving nothing behind. Pursuing the single, compound, complete, and wordless views to their very limit, what breaks through and what is broken through — what, in the end, is actually gained by any of it? Fifth, drawing together the correct Dharma-gate, matched precisely against the proper stage of practice — cultivation has its own skillful means, and realization has its own graduated distinctions. The provisional and the real, the greater and the lesser, the worthy and the sage, none confused with one another — from where, then, could the offense of overweening pride possibly arise? Sixth, moving through a single Dharma-gate freely, both horizontally and vertically, without any obstruction at all, its own thread and sequence woven together, diligently forming a coherent whole. Seventh, opening chapters and dividing sections, each link connected to the next in proper succession, arising in a manner both engaging and clear. Eighth, glossing and explaining the sūtra's own text, its phrasing graceful and refined — applying every method described above together, resolving each passage according to its own wording, so that sense and text accord with one another precisely. Ninth, translating between Sanskrit and Chinese, thoroughly conversant with both names and numerical categories, so that regional dialect never obstructs understanding. Tenth, taking each and every phrase and verse exactly as one has heard it and cultivating it in practice, bringing it into the mind to form actual contemplation. Contemplation, in accord with the sūtra, is thereby validated by it; validating the mind through the very contemplation one forms, not merely reckoning up another's own treasure as though it were one's own. It is only translation of names and numerical categories that has not yet had leisure to be broadly explored among these nine senses — and it is precisely this comprehensive command that neither the mundane textual scholar nor the mere phenomenal meditation-teacher shares. A meditation teacher of the ordinary kind possesses only the single sense of contemplating the mind, and even this, whether shallow or counterfeit, while the remaining nine are entirely absent. This is no empty claim — future worthies with discerning eyes will bear witness to it and know it for themselves. Knowing one's own stage is, itself, one among these ten senses.

Reading note: This list is a rare moment of the treatise stepping back to describe its own method rather than applying it — and read this way, it doubles as an implicit self-portrait of everything the fascicle has done so far: the four siddhāntas dissolving apparent contradiction (sense three), the refusal to let mistaken clinging harden into permanent attachment (sense four), and the insistence that stage and realization must never be confused (sense five) are all principles this very fascicle has been enacting rather than merely stating. The closing jab — that an ordinary meditation teacher possesses only "the single sense of contemplating the mind... while the remaining nine are entirely absent" — is a pointed claim about what actually qualifies someone to teach at all.

Chinese Source

若圓教次位者，於菩薩境中應廣分別。但彼證今修，故須略辨。若四種三昧修習方便，通如上說，唯《法華》別約六時五悔，重作方便。今就五悔明其位相。先知逆順十心而繫緣實相是第一懺。常懺悔，無不懺時。但心理微密，觀用輕疎，黑惡覆障卒難開曉；重運身口助發意業，使疾相應，更加五悔耳。懺名「陳露先惡」；悔名「改往修來」。佛智遍照，佛慈普攝，我以身口投佛足下：願世間眼證我懺悔！我無始無量遮佛道罪，無明所偪，不識正真；從三界繫，動身口意、起十惡罪；三寶、六親、四生、五道，作不饒益事；破發三乘心人，造五、七逆；自作教他，見作隨喜；應現生後，受諸苦惱。如三世菩薩求佛道時懺悔，我亦如是！傷已昏沈、無智慧眼！發是語時，聲淚俱下，至誠真實，五體投地；如樹崩倒，摧折我人，眾惡傾殄，是名「懺悔」。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the perfect teaching's own stages, this ought properly to be explained at length within the context of the bodhisattva's own object of contemplation; but since that discussion belongs to realization, while what is at issue here is cultivation, only a brief account is given. The skillful means for cultivating the four kinds of samādhi has already been generally explained above; only the Lotus Sūtra specifically discusses the sixfold daily practice of the five kinds of repentance as a further, additional skillful means. Here, then, the very marks of stage are clarified in terms of these five kinds of repentance. First, knowing the ten minds that flow downstream and the ten minds turned upstream, and binding one's own object to true reality itself, is the first repentance. One repents constantly, with no time at which one is not repenting. Yet since the principle of mind is so subtle and concealed, and the application of contemplation still light and sparse, dark evil covering and obstructing it, difficult to open and illuminate all at once — one must, further, mobilize body and speech together to assist in bringing forth mental karma, so as to swiftly bring about correspondence — and this is exactly why the five kinds of repentance are added here. "Repentance" means "openly disclosing one's own former evil"; "remorse" means "reforming what has passed and cultivating what is to come." The Buddha's own wisdom illuminates everywhere; the Buddha's own compassion embraces universally. I cast my own body and speech down before the Buddha's own feet: may the eye of the world bear witness to my own repentance! From beginningless time, I have accumulated boundless offenses obstructing the buddha-path, driven by ignorance, unable to recognize the true and correct; bound within the three realms, stirring body, speech, and mind, giving rise to the ten evil offenses; toward the three treasures, my six kinds of kin, the four modes of birth, and the five destinies, I have done what brings no benefit at all; I have destroyed the resolve of those who had given rise to the mind of the three vehicles, committed the five and the seven heinous transgressions; done these things myself and taught others to do them, and rejoiced upon seeing others do them; and, whether in this present life or manifest hereafter, I shall receive every manner of suffering and affliction for it. Just as the bodhisattvas of the three times repented at the very moment they sought the buddha-path, so, too, do I repent in exactly the same way! I grieve for my own confusion and torpor, for lacking the very eye of wisdom! Uttering these words, voice and tears flow together at once, in the utmost sincerity and truth, the five limbs of the body cast prostrate to the ground — as though a great tree were toppling and crashing down, crushing and breaking apart the very notion of self and other, every evil overturned and utterly destroyed. This is named "repentance."

The Five Kinds of Repentance: Entreaty, Rejoicing, Dedication, and Vow 五悔 · 勸請 · 隨喜 · 迴向 · 發願

Chinese Source

勸請者，名為「祈求」。聲聞自度，直懺己罪；菩薩愍眾，故行道，故須勸請。我今知罪尚不得脫；眾生不知，歷劫流轉。我無力救，請十方佛。佛愍眾生，不簡巨細，必冀從願。《大論》明請、不請云云。請轉法輪謂：勸、示、證，令於四諦生眼、智、明、覺，是名「三轉」。有人言：「請說三乘，名『三轉』。」佛若說法，眾生得涅槃證；設未得者，且令受世間樂。佛若普許，則一切得安；我預一切，罪苦亦除。如遍請雨，我有少田，自霑甘潤。請住世者：夫命隨業得住，變化隨心得住，心止化滅。我今請佛饒益眾生，如大炬火，莫止變化之心，久住安隱度脫一切，是名「勸請」。隨喜者，名為「慶彼」。佛既三轉法輪，眾生得三世利益，我助彼喜。又我應勸化令其生善；其善自生，是故我喜。喜三世眾生福德善、三世三乘無漏善、三世諸佛從初心至入滅一切諸善，我皆隨喜，亦教他喜。如買、賣香、傍觀，三人同熏；能化、受化及隨喜者，三善均等。觀眾生惑甚可悲傷；觀眾生善應大恭敬，心常不輕。深知眾生具正、緣、了，即雖未發，會必應生；毒鼓遠近，為要當死，故敬之如佛。何者？未來諸世尊，其數無有量也。此深是隨喜意也。《法華》隨喜法、《大品》隨喜人，人、法互舉耳。

Ru-Yi Translation

Entreaty means "beseeching and seeking." The śrāvaka, delivering only himself, simply repents his own offense alone; the bodhisattva, out of compassion for sentient beings, carries out the path for their sake, and hence entreaty is also needed. I know now that even my own offense has not yet been released; sentient beings, not even knowing this, revolve on through kalpa after kalpa. Lacking the power to rescue them myself, I entreat the buddhas of the ten directions. The buddhas, pitying sentient beings, make no distinction between the great and the small among them, and are certain to grant what is asked of them. The Great Treatise clarifies both what is entreated and what is not, and so on accordingly. Entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel means: urging, demonstrating, and verifying, so that eye, wisdom, illumination, and awakening are all given rise to with respect to the Four Noble Truths — this is named "the threefold turning." Some say: "Entreating the teaching of the three vehicles is itself named 'the threefold turning.'" Should the Buddha teach the Dharma, sentient beings attain the realization of nirvāṇa; and should some not yet attain it, they are, at the very least, granted worldly bliss in the meantime. Should the Buddha grant this universally, then everyone attains peace and security; and, since I myself am included among everyone, my own offense and suffering are thereby removed as well. It is like entreating rain for everyone at once — I myself possess only a small field, yet I, too, receive my own share of its sweet moisture. As for entreating the Buddha to remain in the world: life abides according to karma, and transformation abides according to mind — should the mind cease, transformation, too, is extinguished. I now entreat the Buddha to benefit sentient beings, like a great blazing torch, urging him never to still his own mind of transformation, but to abide long in the world, safely and securely, delivering everyone without exception. This is named "entreaty." Rejoicing means "celebrating another's own good fortune." The Buddha, having already turned the Dharma-wheel three times, sentient beings attain benefit across all three times, and I assist in their own rejoicing over it. Further, I ought to encourage and transform others so that wholesomeness arises within them; and once that wholesomeness arises of itself, this is exactly why I rejoice. I rejoice in the merit and wholesomeness of sentient beings across the three times; in the uncontaminated wholesomeness of the three vehicles across the three times; and in every wholesomeness of every buddha across the three times, from the very first arising of the resolve all the way through to entering final nirvāṇa — in all of this, without exception, I rejoice, and I teach others to rejoice as well. It is like the buyer of incense, the seller of incense, and a bystander nearby, all three alike scented by it — the one who transforms, the one transformed, and the one who rejoices in it, all three forms of wholesomeness alike, in equal measure. Contemplating sentient beings' own affliction, one ought to feel deep grief and sorrow for them; contemplating sentient beings' own wholesomeness, one ought to show them the greatest reverence, the mind never once regarding them lightly. Deeply knowing that sentient beings are complete with the correct cause, the conditional cause, and the revealing cause of buddha-nature, then, even should this not yet have manifested, it is bound, in time, to arise — just as the poison-drum, whether heard from near or far, is certain to bring death to whoever hears it, one reveres them exactly as one would revere a buddha. Why is this? Because the buddhas of the future are boundless in number. This is the deep sense of rejoicing itself. The Lotus Sūtra speaks of rejoicing in the Dharma; the Larger Sūtra speaks of rejoicing in the person — person and Dharma are simply raised interchangeably here.

迴向者，迴眾善向菩提。一切賢聖功德廣大，我今隨喜，福亦廣大。眾生無善，我以善施；施眾生已，正向菩提。如迴聲入角，響聞則遠；迴向為大利。正迴向者：斷三界道，滅諸戲論，乾煩惱泥，滅棘刺林，捨除重檐；不取、不念、不見、不得、不分別。能迴向者、所迴向處，諸法皆妄想和合故有，一切法實不生。無已、今、當生；無已、今、當滅，諸法如是。我順諸法，隨喜迴向，如三世諸佛所知、所見、所許。是名「真實正迴向」，亦名「最上具足大迴向」。則不謗佛、無過咎、無所繫、無毒、無失。何但迴向如此，前三後一亦然。《毘婆沙》云：「罪應如是懺，勸請隨喜福，迴向於菩提。」發願者，誓也。如許人物，若不分券，物則不定；施眾生善，若不要心，或恐退悔，加之以誓。又無誓願，如牛無御不知所趣；願來持行，將至所在。亦名陀羅尼，持善遮惡；如坯得火，堪可盛物。二乘生盡故不須願；菩薩生生化物，須總願、別願。四弘是總願，法藏、《華嚴》所說一一善行、陀羅尼，皆有別願。今於道場，日夜六時行此懺悔，破大惡業罪；勸請，破謗法罪；隨喜，破嫉妬罪；迴向，破為諸有罪。順空無相願，所得功德不可限量，譬算校計亦不能說。若能勤行五悔方便，助開觀門，一心三諦豁爾開明，如臨淨鏡遍了諸色；於一念中圓解成就，不加功力任運分明，正信堅固無能移動。此名「深信隨喜心」，即初品弟子位也。

Dedication means dedicating every wholesomeness toward bodhi itself. The merit of every worthy and sage is vast and expansive, and my own rejoicing here is, correspondingly, vast in its own merit as well. Should sentient beings lack any wholesomeness of their own, I bestow wholesomeness upon them; and, having bestowed it upon sentient beings, I direct it precisely toward bodhi. It is like sound turned back through a horn, its echo carrying all the farther for it — dedication, likewise, brings about the greatest benefit. As for correct dedication: severing the path of the three realms, extinguishing every idle discourse, drying up the mire of affliction, clearing away the thorny thicket, casting off the heavy burden one carries — not grasping, not conceiving, not seeing, not attaining, not discriminating at all. That which dedicates, and that toward which it is dedicated — every dharma exists only through the coming-together of false imagination, and, in truth, no dharma whatsoever ever actually arises. There is no past, present, or future arising; there is no past, present, or future ceasing — every dharma is exactly like this. I accord with every dharma in this way, rejoicing and dedicating accordingly, exactly as known, seen, and sanctioned by the buddhas of the three times. This is named "the genuine and correct dedication," also named "the supreme and complete great dedication." Thus one neither slanders the Buddha, incurs any fault, remains bound by anything, is poisoned by anything, nor suffers any loss whatsoever. And this holds not only for dedication itself — the first three repentances, and the final one as well, proceed in exactly the same way. The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā says: "Offenses ought to be repented in exactly this way; entreaty and rejoicing bring merit in exactly this way; dedicate this, then, toward bodhi itself." Raising the vow is the very act of solemn oath itself. It is like granting a person some possession — should one not divide the deed of grant, the possession remains unsettled; bestowing wholesomeness upon sentient beings, should one not resolve one's own mind firmly, one may fall into regret and retreat afterward, and it is exactly for this reason that the vow is added. Further, without a vow, one is like an ox with no one to drive it, not knowing where it ought to go; the vow, once made, sustains the practice, carrying it through to its very destination. It is also named a dhāraṇī, upholding wholesomeness and warding off evil — like unfired clay meeting fire, becoming thereby fit to hold whatever is placed within it. The two lesser vehicles, their own births exhausted, have no need of such a vow; the bodhisattva, taking birth after birth to transform beings, requires both a general vow and specific vows. The four universal vows are the general vow; every individual wholesome practice and dhāraṇī spoken of in the Ratnagotra scriptures and the Avataṃsaka Sūtra each has its own specific vow. Now, at the place of practice, carrying out this fivefold repentance six times daily, day and night: repentance breaks through the offense of great evil karma; entreaty breaks through the offense of slandering the Dharma; rejoicing breaks through the offense of jealousy; dedication breaks through the offense of grasping at every mode of existence. According with the vow of emptiness and marklessness, the merit thereby attained cannot be measured or limited at all — even calculation and reckoning could never fully express it. Should one be able to diligently carry out the skillful means of these five kinds of repentance, assisting in opening the very gate of contemplation, the single mind's own three truths open up suddenly, luminous and clear, like standing before a clean mirror that reflects every form entirely; within a single thought-moment, perfect understanding is accomplished, manifest of itself without any exertion of effort at all, correct faith firm and resolute, moved by nothing whatsoever. This is named "the mind of deep faith and rejoicing," and is precisely the stage of the first grade of disciple.

Reading note: The incense simile for rejoicing — buyer, seller, and bystander all "alike scented by it" — makes a genuinely counterintuitive claim: rejoicing in another's merit is not a lesser, secondhand version of that merit but a full and equal share of it, which is exactly why the fivefold repentance can function as a complete practice on its own rather than a mere preliminary. The image of the poison-drum, fatal to anyone within earshot regardless of intent, reframes reverence for other beings' buddha-nature as something closer to a physical law than a moral stance: their eventual awakening is not a possibility to be hoped for but a near-certainty to be reckoned with now.

The Five Grades of Disciple, and On to the Purification of the Six Faculties 五品位 · 六根清淨 · 初住

Chinese Source

〈分別功德品〉云：「其有眾生聞佛壽長遠，乃至能生一念信解，所得功德不可限量，能起如來無上之慧。若聞是經而不毀呰，起隨喜心，當知已為深信解相。」即初品文也。又以圓解觀心修行五悔，更加讀誦，善言妙義與心相會，如膏助火；是時心觀益明，名「第二品」也。文云：「何況讀誦、受持之者，斯人則為頂戴如來。」又以增品勝心修行五悔，更加說法，轉其內解，導利前人；以曠濟故，化功歸己，心更一轉，倍勝於前，名「第三品」也。文云：「若有受持、讀誦、為他人說，自書、教人書、供養經卷，不須復起塔寺供養眾僧。」又以增進心修行五悔，兼修六度；福德力故，倍助觀心更一重深進，名「第四品」也。文云：「況復有人能持是經，兼行六度，其德最勝，無量無邊，譬如虛空，至一切種智。」又以此心修行五悔，正修六度，自行化他，事理具足；心觀無礙，轉勝於前，不可比喻，名「第五品」也。文云：「能為他人種種解說，清淨持戒，忍辱無瞋，常貴坐禪，精進勇猛，利根智慧，當知是人已趣道場，近三菩提。」若爾，五品之位在十信前。若依《普賢觀》，即以五品為十信五心。但佛意難知，赴機異說；借此開解，何勞苦諍云云。復次，今此一章是觀陰、界、入境，須約陰、入而判次位。所謂：黑陰入界即三惡道位；白陰入界即三善道位；善方便陰入界即小乘似位；無漏陰界入即二乘真位；變易陰界入即五種人位；法性常色、常受、想、行、識陰界入即佛位云云。又假名五品既轉明淨，豁入聞慧，通達無滯，深信難動，即信心也。如此次第，念、進、慧、定、陀羅尼、戒、護、迴向、願等十信具足，名「六根清淨相似之位」，四住已盡，《仁王般若》云：「十善菩薩發大心，長別三界苦輪海。」即此意也。次入初住，破無明見佛性。《華嚴》云：「初發心時便成正覺，真實之性不由他悟。」即此意也。如是次第四十二位，究竟妙覺，無有叨濫。是名「知次位」。

Ru-Yi Translation

The "Chapter on Distinguishing Merit" says: "Should there be sentient beings who, hearing that the Buddha's own life-span is so vast and far-reaching, are able to give rise to even a single thought-moment's faith and understanding, the merit thereby attained cannot be measured or limited at all, able to give rise to the Tathāgata's own unsurpassed wisdom. Should one hear this sūtra and not disparage it, giving rise to a mind of rejoicing instead, one should know this to already be the mark of deep faith and understanding." This is precisely the text for the first grade. Further, cultivating the fivefold repentance through the perfect understanding of contemplating the mind, and adding, further, reading and reciting the sūtra, so that its excellent words and sublime meaning meet and correspond with the mind itself, like oil helping a flame — at this point, the mind's own contemplation grows still further luminous, and this is named "the second grade." The text says: "How much more so, then, for one who reads, recites, receives, and upholds it — such a person is thereby one who carries the Tathāgata upon his own head." Further, cultivating the fivefold repentance with an even more excellent mind, having advanced through this grade, and adding, further, the teaching of the Dharma to others, turning one's own inward understanding outward to guide and benefit others — and, on account of this broad deliverance of others, the merit of transformation returns to oneself as well, the mind turning further still, doubly excelling what came before — this is named "the third grade." The text says: "Should there be one who receives, upholds, reads, recites, and expounds it for others, copies it himself or teaches others to copy it, and makes offerings to the sūtra-scroll itself, he need not, further, erect stūpas or temples, or make offerings to the assembly of monks." Further, cultivating the fivefold repentance with an ever more advancing mind, and combining this with the cultivation of the six pāramitās — through the power of this merit, doubly assisting the contemplating mind, advancing yet one further degree in depth — this is named "the fourth grade." The text says: "How much more so, then, should there be a person able to uphold this sūtra while also practicing the six pāramitās — his own merit is most excellent, boundless, without limit, like empty space itself, reaching all the way to the wisdom of all modes." Further, with this same mind, cultivating the fivefold repentance while directly cultivating the six pāramitās, practicing for oneself and transforming others at once, phenomenal practice and contemplative principle both complete, the mind's own contemplation unobstructed, surpassing what came before to a degree beyond all comparison — this is named "the fifth grade." The text says: "Able to expound this Dharma to others in every manner of way, purely upholding the precepts, patient and free of anger, constantly valuing seated meditation, diligent and courageous, of sharp faculty and wisdom — one should know that such a person has already drawn near to the very seat of enlightenment, close to the threefold bodhi itself." If this is so, then the stage of the five grades lies before the ten degrees of faith. According to the Contemplation of Samantabhadra Sūtra, the five grades are themselves taken as the five minds of the ten degrees of faith. Yet the Buddha's own intent is difficult to fathom fully, meeting each capacity with a differing account — borrowing this simply to open up understanding, why should one labor so bitterly over such disputes? Furthermore, this entire chapter is contemplating the object of the aggregates, elements, and entries, and so the stages, too, must be judged specifically in terms of the aggregates and entries themselves. Namely: the aggregates and entries of the dark kind are the stage of the three evil destinies; the aggregates and entries of the bright kind are the stage of the three wholesome destinies; the aggregates and entries of skillful means are the resembling stage of the Hīnayāna; the uncontaminated aggregates, elements, and entries are the genuine stage of the two lesser vehicles; the transformational aggregates, elements, and entries are the stage of the five kinds of person; and the aggregates, elements, and entries of the Dharma-nature's own constant form, constant feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are the stage of buddhahood itself, and so on accordingly. Further, once the provisionally named five grades have grown ever more luminous and pure, opening freely into the wisdom of hearing, penetrating through without obstruction, deep faith unmoved by anything at all — this is precisely the mind of faith itself. Proceeding in this same sequence — mindfulness, diligence, wisdom, concentration, dhāraṇī, the precepts, guarding, dedication, and vow — the ten degrees of faith complete in full is named "the stage resembling the purification of the six faculties," the four abodes of affliction already exhausted. The Humane Kings Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra says: "The bodhisattva of the ten wholesome ways, giving rise to the great mind, is forever separated from the great ocean-wheel of suffering across the three realms" — this is exactly that same sense. Next, one enters the first abiding, breaking through ignorance and seeing the buddha-nature directly. The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "At the very moment the resolve first arises, one already accomplishes correct awakening, the true nature awakened to not through any other" — this is exactly that same sense. Proceeding in this sequence through the forty-two stages in full, reaching the very culmination of wondrous enlightenment, there is nothing whatsoever excessive or presumptuous in any of it. This is named "knowing one's own stage."

Reading note: Mapping the five grades directly onto this fascicle's own opening object of contemplation — dark aggregates as the three evil destinies, the Dharma-nature's own constant aggregates as buddhahood itself — closes a structural loop the fascicle has been building since Mode Five: every one of the ten modes has been worked through for exactly the same first object named back in fascicle 5, and Mode Eight's own account of stage is itself indexed to that same object, not to some separate, abstract ladder. "Why should one labor so bitterly over such disputes?" — dismissing the question of whether the five grades precede or overlap the ten degrees of faith — is the treatise's characteristic move: a scriptural discrepancy is evidence of differing skillful means, not grounds for sectarian argument. With this, Mode Eight, knowing one's own stage, is complete.

Ru-Yi Translation

Ninth, bearing with adversity: being able to bear with the very matter of accomplishing the path, unmoved and never retreating — a mind of this kind is named "a great being." From the very beginning of contemplating the aggregates and entries, through to knowing one's own stage, these eight dharmas have already turned obstruction into the opening of wisdom — whether one has not yet entered a grade, or has already entered the first grade, one's spiritual wisdom grows sharp and keen. Like a blade so keen it severs even flying frost the instant it touches anything, the beginning practitioner's own sharpness and cleverness may exceed even this. Without having formally studied, one is already able to understand every sūtra and treatise; surveying another's own commentary, one penetrates directly to the very heart of its doctrine; wishing to explain but a single passage, one's own eloquence proves inexhaustible. Should one hold this treasure and jewel within, concealing one's own understanding and hiding one's own name, quietly and diligently pressing forward, one is certain to enter a grade, or to advance into an even deeper one. Resolve and intent firm and unmoving, incapable of being shifted by anything — this is by far the superior approach. But an awl does not stay hidden within a bag; it is difficult to conceal and easy to let slip into view. Perhaps one sees a lecturer whose teaching fails to accord with principle, or a practitioner whose conduct fails to match the proper track — and, out of compassion, one offers a word of correction, and is at once surrounded by others. Once made to lecture or expound, or urged on for the sake of sentient beings, an inward itch stirs an outward movement, and one speaks a phrase or two of the Dharma, or demonstrates a section or two of meditation; beginning with a single listener, word spreads and gradually widens, until it can no longer be stopped. What one first supposed would be beneficial turns out to benefit others only slightly, while one's own practice is abandoned and diminished — and it is not merely that one's own grade fails to advance, but that the very obstruction of the path rises up all the more! The elephant calf's own strength being slight, its body falls beneath the blade and arrow; scooping up boiling water and pouring it upon ice, one only adds, in the end, to the mass of ice itself. The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā names this "the ruined bodhisattva." In the past, there were meditation teachers of Ye and Luo whose fame spread across river and sea alike — wherever they went, crowds gathered like clouds from every direction; wherever they left, hundreds and thousands followed in throngs — yet what benefit, in the end, did all this rumbling commotion actually bring? At the moment of their death, every one of them was filled with regret! The Master of Wujin lamented, saying: "All my life I hoped to enter the bronze-wheel stage, yet I led the assembly far too early, and what I sought was never accomplished." In his own final testament, he wrote simply: "Choose! Choose! Choose! Choose!" This lofty and excellent example, handed down to us, may well serve as our own mirror. Should one's own practice, having reached this point, examine and weigh itself carefully, and find one's own strength of wisdom already robust and strong, one ought indeed to broadly benefit others, like a great elephant leading its own herd. But should this not yet be the case, one ought instead to bear with adversity and continue cultivating samādhi more deeply — once practice is accomplished and one's own strength made manifest, it will not be too late, even then, to transform others. The Great Treatise says: "The bodhisattva takes delivering others as his very own task — why, then, does he dwell alone in the deep mountains, cultivating only himself? Answer: it is like taking medicine to nurse one's own body back to health, so that its constitution may be restored to its own proper task — though the body withdraws far away, the mind never withdraws at all." Once one reaches the purification of the six faculties, this is named "one who serves as the first refuge for others," and whatever Dharma one then teaches may indeed be trusted and accepted. A single voice reaching everywhere at once, and every listener rejoicing to hear it — this is the very stage of transforming others. Should one, at this very point, fail to emerge and teach, then neither the strong bandit nor the soft bandit has any power over one at all — one's own practice, thereby perfected, becomes of genuine use to others as well. The great elephant standing its ground, blade and arrow rendered useless against it; the sunlight illuminating the world, the longest ice melting of itself before it — this, precisely, is the very power of bearing with adversity. Should one be caught by the net of fame and reputation, the cord of gain and support, or the thicket of gathered disciples, moths and worms boring inward while the very branches and leaves are consumed from without — one ought to push these away at the earliest moment, neither accepting them nor clinging to them; and should pushing them away fail to remove them, and one finds oneself instead stuck fast and bound by them, one ought to draw in one's own merit and conceal its very brilliance, feigning derangement to hide what is genuine within, secretly covering over one's own gold and treasure so that no thief may catch sight of it. And should even concealment fail to bring escape, one ought, at once, to depart ten thousand leagues, to some remote and isolated land, where, unknown to anyone, one may swiftly attain the study of the path — as did Guṇavarman, and so on accordingly. Should fame, gain, or disciples come from without to break one's own practice, one recalls these three techniques and grits one's teeth in patient endurance. Though invited a thousand, ten thousand times over, one remains firm and immovable — yield! withdraw! depart! Should affliction, karma, concentration, view, pride, and the rest come from within to break one's own practice, one likewise recalls three techniques: at once empty, at once provisional, at once the middle. Even should one's own flesh be butchered and ground to powder, the mind remains undisturbed, unscattered; even should the whole earth press down and crush one, one is not thereby buried beneath its weight. The great wind at the end of the kalpa cannot make it lighter; the coldest ice cannot make it cold; the fiercest blaze cannot make it hot. Holding the mind upright, contemplating correctly — how, then, could one possibly take some slight attainment, some fragment of dhyāna, as cause for delight? How could one, the moment some slight evil appears, immediately take it as cause for worry? Unfired clay is easily piled up in a heap; but a flower shaped from wet clay is difficult to bring to genuine fruit. The Larger Sūtra says: "Boundless numbers of people give rise to the bodhi-mind, and yet, for the most part, fall back into the stage of the two lesser vehicles." For the sake of accomplishing this great task, bearing with adversity is needed all the more. Should one grasp this sense, there is no need even for the remaining nine objects of contemplation; should one not yet fully understand it, this ought to be explained further, at greater length.

Reading note: "The Master of Wujin" lamenting on his deathbed that he "led the assembly far too early" is a specific, named cautionary tale rather than an abstract warning — the treatise is willing to cite a real teacher's own regret to make the point that early sharpness of insight is precisely what makes premature teaching so tempting, and so dangerous. The three techniques for internal disturbance (empty, provisional, middle) and three for external disturbance (push away, conceal, flee) are notably asymmetric: affliction arising from within is met with philosophical resolve, while fame and disciples arising from without are met with concrete evasive action, up to and including emigrating "ten thousand leagues" away — the treatise treats social entanglement as a genuinely different order of threat than internal affliction, not simply a variant of it.

Mode Ten: Freedom from Attachment to the Dharma 無法愛

Chinese Source

○第十、無法愛者：行上九事，過內外障應得入真；而不入者，以法愛住著而不得前。《毘曇》云：「煖法猶退，五根若立，上忍發真，則不論退。頂法若生愛心，應入不入，退為四重、五逆。」通、別皆有頂墮之義。既不入位，又不墮二乘。《大論》云：「三三昧是似道位。未發真時，喜有法愛，名為『頂墮』。」今人行道萬不至此，至此善自防護。此位無內外障，唯有法愛。法愛難斷，若有稽留，此非小事，譬如同帆一去一停，停即住著。又雖不著沙，亦不著岸，風息故住。不著沙喻無內障，岸喻外障。而生法愛，無住風息，不進不退，名為「頂墮」。若破法愛，入三解脫，發真中道，所有慧身不由他悟，自然流入薩婆若海，住無生忍，亦名「寂滅忍」。以首楞嚴遊戲神通，具大智慧如大海水，所有功德唯佛能知。今止觀進趣方便，齊此而已；入住功德，今無所論，後當重辨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tenth, freedom from attachment to the Dharma: having practiced the nine matters explained above, having passed beyond both internal and external obstruction, one ought thereby to enter the real directly — and should one still fail to enter it, this is on account of a lingering attachment to the very Dharma itself, unable to advance any further. The Abhidharma says: "The dharma of warmth may still regress; but once the five faculties are firmly established, the supreme forbearance brings forth the real directly, and regression is no longer even discussed. Should the dharma of the summit give rise to a mind of attachment, one who ought to enter fails to enter, and instead regresses into the four grave offenses or the five heinous transgressions." Both the shared and distinct teachings alike have this very sense of "falling at the summit." Neither having entered the proper stage, nor yet having fallen back into the two lesser vehicles, the Great Treatise says: "The three samādhis are the resembling stage of the path. Before the real has yet been brought forth, one is prone to a delight that becomes attachment to the very Dharma itself — this is named 'falling at the summit.'" People who practice the path today fall short of even reaching this point by a vast measure, yet, should one reach it, one ought to guard and protect oneself against it with the greatest care. This stage has neither internal nor external obstruction remaining — only attachment to the Dharma itself. This attachment to the Dharma is difficult to sever, and should it linger even briefly, this is no small matter — it is like a sailboat that alternately advances and then stops; the moment it stops, it becomes fixed and stuck in place. And though it may not yet be caught on the sandbar, nor yet moored at the shore, it remains stopped simply because the wind itself has died down. Not being caught on the sandbar is a simile for being free of internal obstruction; the shore is a simile for external obstruction. But giving rise to attachment to the Dharma, with no wind at all to move it onward, neither advancing nor retreating, is exactly what is named "falling at the summit." Should one break through this very attachment to the Dharma, one enters the three gates of liberation, bringing forth the real, the middle way directly — the very body of wisdom one thereby possesses, awakened to not through any other, flows spontaneously into the very ocean of omniscience itself, abiding in the forbearance of the unborn, also named "the forbearance of quiescent cessation." With the Śūraṅgama samādhi, one roams freely in spiritual powers, complete with great wisdom vast as the very ocean itself — whatever merit this brings, only a buddha could ever fully know it. The skillful means for calming-and-contemplation's own advance and pursuit reach, here, exactly this far; the merit of entering and abiding within this stage is not discussed further here, and will be treated again, at greater length, later on.

Reading note: The becalmed sailboat is a precise image for a genuinely subtle failure state: "falling at the summit" is not falling backward into the two lesser vehicles, nor failing to advance through any active obstruction — it is simply drifting, stuck exactly because "the wind itself has died down," with no internal or external force left to blame. That even attachment to the Dharma itself — not craving, not pride, but delight in one's own genuine progress — can become the final obstruction is the fascicle's last and subtlest warning. With this, all ten modes of contemplation, applied to the first of the ten objects of contemplation, are complete in full.

The Ten Modes as the Great Vehicle: The Lotus Sūtra's Parable of the Cart 大乘觀 · 大車喻

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

These ten modes, taken together, are named "the contemplation of the Great Vehicle"; and one who studies this vehicle is named "one who studies the Great Vehicle itself." What, then, is this "Great Vehicle"? As the Lotus Sūtra says: "He bestowed upon each of his own children, equally, a single great cart. This cart was tall and broad, adorned with every kind of jewel, its railings running all around, bells hanging from all four sides. Above it, further, a canopy was spread, likewise adorned with rare and precious jewels of every kind. Jeweled cords were interwoven throughout, hung with garlands of flowers; layered cushions were spread within it, and a crimson pillow set in place. It was drawn by a white ox, fat and sturdy, of great strength, its skin full and lustrous, its very form graceful and fine, possessed of great physical power, its gait even and steady, swift as the wind itself. And, further, a great many attendants accompanied it, guarding it on every side." The Great Vehicle of calming-and-contemplation is exactly the same. Contemplating that thought after thought is nothing other than the true mark of the Dharma-nature itself is named "bestowing, equally, a single great cart." Within each and every mind, at once empty, at once provisional, at once the middle, is named "bestowing this cart upon each of his own children." Penetrating to the very source of the three truths is named "tall." Gathering in the ten dharma-realms is named "broad." The boundless branches of the path are named "adorned with every kind of jewel." The four right efforts, warding off evil and upholding wholesomeness, together with the vow that sustains this very practice, firm and secure as a nail or clasp, is named "the railings running all around." The eloquence of Dharma and meaning, proclaimed and expounded so as to awaken understanding, is named "bells hanging from all four sides." Compassion covering everywhere, with nothing whatsoever left out or excluded, is named "the canopy spread above." What the branches of the path gather in — the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, and the eighteen dharmas not shared with others — shared with no one else at all, is named "adorned with rare and precious jewels." The four universal vows, the mind resolved never to retreat, is named "jeweled cords interwoven throughout." The four means of embrace, gathering in every being, with nothing whatsoever left undelighted, is named "hung with garlands of flowers." Every dhyāna and samādhi, giving rise to the six spiritual powers, is named "layered cushions spread within." The four gates converging upon their single source, every practice brought finally to rest, is named "a crimson pillow set in place." The wisdom of the four foundations of mindfulness, breaking through the very darkness of the eight inversions, is named "drawn by a white ox." The four right efforts increasing the two forms of wholesomeness is named "fat and sturdy, of great strength." Warding off and severing the two evils, these two evils exhausted and purified entirely, is exactly why it is said, "its skin full and lustrous." The four bases of supernormal power, the four eloquences functioning with perfect freedom, is named "its very form graceful and fine." The five faculties, coiled firm and unable to be shifted, is named "physical power." The five powers, growing in strength and warding off every unwholesome dharma, is named "great strength." The seven factors of awakening, discriminating and selecting, is named "its gait." The eightfold path, calm and secure, is named "even and steady." Auxiliary remedies broadly gathering in every dharma is named "a great many attendants accompanying it." Breaking through attachment to the Dharma and ignorance, flowing into the very ocean of omniscience, bringing forth the real with utmost swiftness, is named "swift as the wind itself." Carrying all of his own children along, in joyful and delightful play. This contemplation of the Great Vehicle, its every Dharma-gate complete with its own measure, accords exactly with that very sūtra passage — this is exactly why it is named "the contemplation of the Great Vehicle."

Furthermore, since every dharma is entirely the one vehicle, anyone possessing a mind at all is already complete with this very sublime dharma — this is named "the vehicle in principle." Closing the Fascicle: Three Similes on Mastery, Broad and Narrow 結勸 · 三譬

Chinese Source

○若能如上勤而行之，於一生中必不空過。雖聞不用，如黑蚖懷珠，何益於長蛇者乎？今以三譬譬於得失。匹夫隻勇，修治一刀一箭，破一寇兩寇，獲賜一金一銀，祿潤一妻一子。如此之人但利器械，負戟前驅以命博貨，何用廣知兵法耶？若欲為國，麴糵、舟楫、鹽梅、霖雨者，須善文武，計在帷帳，折衝萬里。所學處深，所破亦大；獲賞既重，祿潤甚多。雖知而不用，用而屢北；尚不能濟身，澤豈及人乎？學禪觀者亦如是。唯知一法，或止、或觀，擬破少惡，寂心行道，得少禪定，攝少眷屬，便以為足，如匹夫鬪耳。欲作大禪師，破大煩惱，顯無量善法，益無量緣，當學十法止觀，洞達意趣。於六緣、六受行用相應。煩惱卒起，即便有觀；觀過惑表，勇健難事，解髻得珠。若解而不用，用而不當，而反師惑心，道安克乎？又如野巫，唯解一術，方救一人，獲一脯柈，何須學神農本草耶？欲為大醫，遍覽眾治，廣療諸疾，轉脈轉精，數用數驗，恩救博也。學禪者亦如是。但專一法，治惑即去，當時微益，終非大途包括之意，亦不能破煩惱入無生忍。雖善醫藥，不依方服，病豈差乎？讀誦止觀甚利，心不行用，無生終不現前。又如學義，止欲一問一答，衒耀一時，何須廣尋經論？欲作法主，當善異部。雖諳解處多而不曾出眾，怯弱不任酬往。若無怯怖，臨機百轉，以無方之答，答縱橫之問，是為大法師。觀行人亦如是。觀行若明，能歷緣對境觸處得用；若不如是，魔軍何由可破？煩惱重病何由可除？法性深義何由可顯？三事不辦，區區困役，秖是生死凡夫，非為學道方便也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should one be able to practice diligently in exactly this manner, one is certain not to pass one's own life in vain. Yet should one hear this and fail to put it into practice, one is like the black serpent hoarding a jewel — what benefit could this possibly bring even a great python? Here, then, three similes are offered to illustrate what is gained and what is lost. Take an ordinary man of solitary courage, who trains himself with only a single blade, a single arrow, defeating one bandit, then two, and is thereby granted a small reward of gold or silver, sufficient to support a single wife and child. Such a person merely sharpens his own weapon, charging ahead into battle, wagering his very life for mere payment — what need has he for any broad knowledge of the art of war at all? But should one wish to serve an entire kingdom — the fermenting of wine, the building of boats, the seasoning of food, the timing of the rains — one must be thoroughly skilled in both civil and military affairs, planning strategy from within the very command-tent itself, repelling the enemy from ten thousand leagues away. What one has studied runs deep, and what one thereby breaks through is correspondingly great; the reward one attains is correspondingly weighty, and the benefit that follows correspondingly vast. Yet should one know this and fail to apply it, or apply it only to be repeatedly defeated, one cannot even save oneself — how, then, could one's own beneficial influence possibly reach others at all? One who studies meditative contemplation is exactly the same. Knowing only a single dharma, whether calming or contemplation alone, intending merely to break through some minor evil, stilling the mind to practice the path, attaining some slight measure of meditative concentration, gathering a small circle of disciples, and considering this already sufficient — this is no different from that solitary soldier's own single combat. Should one wish, instead, to become a great meditation-master, breaking through great affliction, manifesting boundless wholesome dharmas, benefiting boundless conditions and beings, one ought to study the ten modes of calming-and-contemplation, penetrating thoroughly to their very intent, corresponding in actual practice with the six conditions and six forms of receiving encountered in daily life. Should affliction suddenly arise, contemplation is immediately brought to bear upon it; contemplation surpassing the very affliction it observes, courageous and capable of the most difficult tasks, one thereby loosens the topknot and retrieves the very jewel hidden within it. But should one understand this and fail to apply it, or apply it wrongly, thereby instead becoming a servant to one's own deluded mind, how could the path ever possibly be mastered? It is also like a rustic folk-healer, versed in only a single remedy, able to save but a single patient, receiving in return only a single portion of dried meat — what need has he to study the whole of Shennong's own materia medica at all? But should one wish to become a great physician, surveying every remedy without exception, broadly treating every illness, refining one's own diagnostic pulse-reading again and again, applying and verifying one's own methods time after time, the benefit and rescue thereby brought about is vast and far-reaching indeed. One who studies meditation is exactly the same. Devoted exclusively to a single method alone, though this may indeed remove a given affliction at that particular moment, bringing only a slight, momentary benefit, this is, in the end, not the intent of the great and comprehensive path at all, and is likewise unable to break through affliction and enter the forbearance of the unborn. Even the finest medicine, if not taken according to its proper prescription, could never actually cure the illness at all. Reading and reciting the treatise on calming-and-contemplation may be exceedingly sharp and keen, yet, should the mind fail to put it into actual practice, the unborn will, in the end, never manifest before one at all. Further, it is like a scholar who wishes only to answer a single question, showing off for a single moment — what need has he, then, for any broad study of the sūtras and treatises at all? But should one wish to become a true master of the Dharma, one must be thoroughly skilled across every rival school of doctrine. Though one's own understanding may run deep in certain areas, should one never once have faced a genuine audience, one remains timid and weak, unable to bear the burden of actual exchange. But should one be entirely free of such timidity and fear, meeting every occasion with a hundred different turns, answering every manner of question, however far it ranges, with an answer suited to no fixed formula at all — this alone is a true master of the Dharma. One who practices contemplation is exactly the same. Should contemplative practice be genuinely clear and luminous, one is able, in every activity, meeting every object encountered, to put it to actual use at every turn; but should this not be so, how could the very army of Māra ever possibly be defeated? How could the grave illness of affliction ever possibly be removed? How could the profound meaning of the Dharma-nature ever possibly be made manifest? Should these three matters remain unaccomplished, one merely toils and struggles in vain, remaining nothing more than an ordinary being caught in birth-and-death — this is not, in any way, the skillful means of studying the path at all.

Reading note: The three closing similes — the lone soldier versus the strategist, the folk-healer versus the great physician, the one-question debater versus the true master — all make the identical structural argument the fascicle has been building since Mode Six's insistence that the branches of the path gather in literally everything: narrow competence and comprehensive mastery are not different in kind but in scope, and the narrow version, however genuinely effective within its own limits, cannot be scaled up simply by trying harder within it. The final line — "an ordinary being caught in birth-and-death... not, in any way, the skillful means of studying the path at all" — is a blunt closing verdict on exactly the failure mode Mode Nine warned against: technique without the comprehensive ten-mode structure behind it is not a smaller version of the path, but no path at all.

Status of this page: Fascicle 7 of the Mohe Zhiguan is now translated in full, spanning both the upper and lower halves of the fascicle (卷第七上 and 卷第七下). This fascicle completes the remaining six of the ten modes of contemplation for the first object of contemplation, the aggregates, entries, and elements — finishing what fascicles 5 and 6 began. It covers: distinguishing blockage from passage (識通塞, the fivefold-hundred-yojana parable resolved through location, affliction, and wisdom, and horizontal-and-vertical blockage checked against all three movements of comprehensively breaking through every dharma); cultivating the branches of the path (道品調適, the thirty-seven factors mapped onto one mind and three truths, the four foundations of mindfulness defeating the eight inversions, and the three gates of liberation across all four teachings); auxiliary remedies (助道對治, the six pāramitās as antidote to the six coverings of the mind — including the extended contemplation of the body's five kinds of impurity and of impermanence — and how the six pāramitās gather in the regulation of the six faculties, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the eighteen unshared dharmas, the four unimpeded understandings, the six spiritual powers, the four means of embrace, the dhāraṇīs, and the thirty-two marks); knowing one's own stage (明次位, the ten hermeneutical principles, the five kinds of repentance, and the five grades of disciple through the purification of the six faculties to the first abiding); bearing with adversity (安忍, including the cautionary tale of the "ruined bodhisattva"); and freedom from attachment to the Dharma (無法愛, "falling at the summit"). The fascicle then shows all ten modes together as the Lotus Sūtra's own parable of the great cart, and closes by extending the entire method from seated contemplation into the six ordinary activities and six sense-doors of daily life (歷緣對境), worked through in full for walking and for the eye, then a final threefold simile on the difference between narrow competence and comprehensive mastery. With this fascicle, the complete ten-mode treatment of the first of the ten objects of contemplation is finished. The remaining nine objects of contemplation — affliction, illness, the marks of karma, demonic affairs, meditative states, views, overweening pride, the two lesser vehicles, and the bodhisattva — along with topics eight through ten of the treatise's own ten-section outline, remain to be translated, beginning with fascicle 8 of the CBETA source.
