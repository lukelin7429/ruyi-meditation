The Four Kinds of Samādhi

This page continues Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Mohe Zhiguan (摩訶止觀, "The Great Calming and Contemplation"). Fascicle 2 covers the treatise's celebrated fourfold scheme of practice — constantly sitting, constantly walking, half walking and half sitting, and neither walking nor sitting — and closes out topic one, the great intent, begun in fascicle 1. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Second Brief Point: Cultivating Great Practice, Through the Four Kinds of Samādhi 勸進四種三昧

Chinese Source

二、勸進四種三昧入菩薩位。說是止觀者，夫欲登妙位，非行不階；善解鑽搖，醍醐可獲。《法華》云：「又見佛子，修種種行，以求佛道。」行法眾多，略言其四：一、常坐，二、常行，三、半行半坐，四、非行非坐。通稱「三昧」者，調直定也。《大論》云：「善心一處住不動，是名三昧。」法界是一處，正觀能住不動，四行為緣，觀心藉緣調直，故稱三昧也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second: encouraging entry into the bodhisattva stage through the four kinds of samādhi. In expounding this calming-and-contemplation: to ascend to the wondrous stage, there is no climbing it without practice; skillfully churning and shaking the cream, ghee may be obtained. The Lotus Sūtra says: "And I see the Buddha's disciples cultivating manifold practices, seeking the buddha-path." The practices are many; stated briefly, there are four: first, constantly sitting; second, constantly walking; third, half walking and half sitting; fourth, neither walking nor sitting. What all four are alike called "samādhi" means a well-regulated and direct concentration. The Treatise says: "A wholesome mind abiding in one place, unmoving — this is called samādhi." The dharma-realm is that one place; correct contemplation is what can abide there unmoving; the four practices serve as its condition, the contemplating mind, by means of that condition, regulated and made direct — hence it is called samādhi.

Reading note: The Four Kinds of Samādhi (四種三昧) is likely the single most historically influential passage in this entire treatise — the practical framework behind centuries of Tiantai monastic practice, and, through the second of the four, a foundational text for East Asian Pure Land devotion as well. All four are explicitly one and the same samādhi wearing four different postures: what matters is not walking versus sitting as such, but the mind "regulated and made direct" toward the dharma-realm. Each of the four gets its own method (方法, covering body, speech, and mind) and its own encouragement to practice (勸修).

I. Constantly-Sitting Samādhi 常坐三昧

Chinese Source

一、常坐者，出《文殊說》、《文殊問》兩般若，名為一行三昧。今初明方法，次明勸修。方法者，身論開遮，口論說默，意論止觀。身開常坐，遮行住臥。或可處眾，獨則彌善。居一靜室，或空閑地，離諸喧鬧，安一繩床，傍無餘座。九十日為一期，結跏正坐，項脊端直，不動、不搖、不萎、不倚，以坐自誓。肋不拄床，況復屍臥、遊戲、住立？除經行、食、便利，隨一佛方面，端坐正向，時刻相續，無須臾廢。所開者，專坐；所遮者，勿犯。不欺佛，不負心，不誑眾生。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, constant sitting: this comes from the two Prajñāpāramitā sūtras, the Sūtra Spoken by Mañjuśrī and the Sūtra of Mañjuśrī's Questions, where it is named the one-practice samādhi. Here, first, the method is explained; next, encouragement to cultivate it. As for the method: for the body, what is permitted and what is forbidden is discussed; for speech, what is spoken and what is kept silent; for the mind, calming and contemplation. For the body, sitting constantly is permitted; walking, standing, and lying down are forbidden. One may practice among an assembly, though practicing alone is even better. Dwell in a single quiet room, or an open, unoccupied place, apart from every clamor and disturbance; set out a single rope-seat, with no other seat beside it. Ninety days make one period: sit cross-legged and upright, neck and spine straight, without moving, without swaying, without slumping, without leaning — holding to the vow of sitting itself. The ribs should not even rest against the bed-frame, much less should one lie down flat, wander about, or stand up. Except for walking meditation, eating, and relieving oneself, one should sit facing directly toward a single buddha's own direction, upright and correctly oriented, continuing hour after hour without a moment's lapse. What is permitted: single-minded sitting. What is forbidden: any violation of this. One does not deceive the Buddha, does not betray one's own resolve, does not deceive sentient beings.

口說默者，若坐疲極，或疾病所困，或睡蓋所覆，內外障侵，奪正念心，不能遣却，當專稱一佛名字，慚愧懺悔，以命自歸，與稱十方佛名，功德正等。所以者何？如人憂、喜、欝、怫，舉聲歌哭，悲笑則暢。行人亦爾。風觸七處成身業，聲響出脣成口業，二能助意成機，感佛俯降。如人引重，自力不前；假傍救助，則蒙輕舉。行人亦爾。心弱不能排障，稱名請護，惡緣不能壞。若於法門未了，當親近解般若者，如聞修學，能入一行三昧，面見諸佛，上菩薩位。誦經、誦呪尚喧於靜，況世俗言語耶？

As for speech, spoken and silent: if, while sitting, one grows utterly exhausted, or is beset by illness, or overcome by the hindrance of drowsiness, and inner and outer obstructions invade, seizing hold of the correct mindful state so that it cannot be dispelled, one should single-mindedly call out the name of one buddha, with shame and repentance, entrusting one's own life — and this is of exactly equal merit to calling out the names of the buddhas of the ten directions. Why is this? It is like a person's grief, joy, gloom, or indignation: raising the voice in song or wailing brings relief through sorrow or laughter. The practitioner is exactly the same. Wind striking the seven places forms the karmic act of body; the sound issuing from the lips forms the karmic act of speech; both together assist the mind and complete the occasion, moving the Buddha to bend down and descend. It is like someone hauling a heavy load: by their own strength alone they cannot advance, but with the help of others at their side, they find it lifted with ease. The practitioner is exactly the same: when the mind is weak and cannot repel obstruction, calling on the name and beseeching protection keeps evil conditions from doing harm. If one has not yet fully understood this dharma-gate, one should draw close to those who understand the Perfection of Wisdom, and, learning and cultivating as one has heard, be able to enter the one-practice samādhi, behold the buddhas face to face, and ascend to the bodhisattva stage. Even reciting sūtras or reciting mantras still disturbs the stillness — how much more, then, ordinary worldly speech?

意止觀者，端坐正念，蠲除惡覺，捨諸亂想，莫雜思惟，不取相貌，但專繫緣法界，一念法界。繫緣是止，一念是觀。信一切法皆是佛法，無前無後，無復際畔，無知者，無說者。若無知、無說，則非有、非無，非知者、非不知者。離此二邊，住無所住，如諸佛住，安處寂滅法界。聞此深法，勿生驚怖！此法界亦名「菩提」，亦名「不可思議境界」，亦名「般若」，亦名「不生不滅」。如是等一切法，與法界無二無別。聞無二無別，勿生疑惑！能如是觀者，是觀如來十號。觀如來時，不謂如來為如來，無有如來為如來，亦無如來智。能知如來者，如來及如來智無二相、無動相、不作相、不在方、不離方、非三世、非不三世、非二相、非不二相、非垢相、非淨相。此觀如來，甚為希有，猶如虛空，無有過失，增長正念。

As for mind, calming and contemplation: sit upright with correct mindfulness, sweeping away every unwholesome thought, letting go of every scattered notion, not mixing in discursive thinking, not grasping at any mark or appearance — simply binding the mind's attention wholly to the dharma-realm, a single thought the dharma-realm itself. Binding the attention is calming; the single thought is contemplation. Believe that every dharma whatsoever is itself already the Buddhadharma, with no before and no after, with no boundary or edge left at all, with no knower and no speaker. If there is no knower and no speaker, then it is neither being nor non-being, neither one who knows nor one who does not know. Departing from these two extremes, abiding nowhere at all, one abides just as every buddha abides, dwelling securely within the quiescent dharma-realm. On hearing this profound Dharma, do not be alarmed or afraid! This dharma-realm is also named "bodhi"; it is also named "the inconceivable state"; it is also named "prajñā"; it is also named "neither arising nor ceasing." Every dharma of this kind is, with the dharma-realm, neither two nor separate. On hearing that they are neither two nor separate, do not give rise to doubt! One who can contemplate in this way is contemplating the Tathāgata's own ten epithets. In contemplating the Tathāgata, one does not take the Tathāgata to be the Tathāgata; there is no Tathāgata to serve as "the Tathāgata," nor any wisdom of the Tathāgata either. The very wisdom able to know the Tathāgata — the Tathāgata and the Tathāgata's wisdom — has no mark of duality, no mark of motion, no mark of being made, is not located in any direction and does not depart from any direction, is not the three times and not other than the three times, is not a dual mark and not other than a dual mark, is not a mark of defilement and not a mark of purity. This contemplation of the Tathāgata is exceedingly rare, like empty space itself, without any fault, and it increases correct mindfulness.

見佛相好，如照水鏡，自見其形。初見一佛，次見十方佛。不用神通往見佛，唯住此處見諸佛。聞佛說法，得如實義。為一切眾生見如來，而不取如來相。化一切眾生向涅槃，而不取涅槃相。為一切眾生發大莊嚴，而不見莊嚴相。無形無相，無見聞知。佛不證得，是為希有。何以故？佛即法界，若以法界證法界，即是諍論。無證無得！觀眾生相如諸佛相；眾生界量如諸佛界量。諸佛界量不可思議，眾生界量亦不可思議。眾生界住如虛空住，以不住法、以無相法住般若中。不見凡法，云何捨？不見聖法，云何取？生死、涅槃，垢、淨亦如是不捨、不取，但住實際。如此觀眾生，真佛法界。

Seeing the Buddha's own auspicious marks is like a reflection in still water: one sees one's own form there. First one sees a single buddha; next one sees the buddhas of the ten directions. One does not need supernormal power to go and see the Buddha — simply abiding in this very place, one sees every buddha. Hearing the Buddha expound the Dharma, one attains its meaning exactly as it truly is. One sees the Tathāgata for the sake of all sentient beings, and yet does not grasp at any mark of the Tathāgata. One transforms every sentient being toward nirvāṇa, and yet does not grasp at any mark of nirvāṇa. One sets forth great adornment for the sake of every sentient being, and yet sees no mark of adornment at all. Formless and markless, beyond seeing, hearing, or knowing — the Buddha does not "realize" or "attain" any of it, and this is what is so exceedingly rare. Why is this? The Buddha is himself already the dharma-realm; were the dharma-realm to realize the dharma-realm, this would itself be a kind of contention. There is no realizing, no attaining! Contemplate the mark of sentient beings as the mark of every buddha; the measure of the realm of sentient beings as the measure of the realm of every buddha. The measure of the buddhas' realm is inconceivable, and the measure of sentient beings' realm is exactly as inconceivable. The realm of sentient beings abides as empty space abides — abiding within prajñā by means of a dharma that abides nowhere, a markless dharma. If one does not see any dharma of the ordinary being, what is there to abandon? If one does not see any dharma of the sage, what is there to take up? Birth-and-death and nirvāṇa, defilement and purity, are exactly the same: neither abandoned nor taken up, abiding only in true reality itself. Contemplating sentient beings in this way is the true dharma-realm of the Buddha himself.

觀貪欲瞋癡諸煩惱，恒是寂滅行，是無動行，非生死法，非涅槃法。不捨諸見，不捨無為，而修佛道。非修道，非不修道，是名「正住煩惱法界」也。觀業重者，無出五逆。五逆即是菩提，菩提、五逆無二相。無覺者、無知者、無分別者。逆罪相、實相相，皆不可思議，不可壞，本無本性。一切業緣皆住實際，不來不去，非因非果。是為觀業即是法界印。法界印，四魔所不能壞，魔不得便。何以故？魔即法界印，法界印云何毀法界印？以此意歷一切法，亦應可解。

Contemplate craving, anger, delusion, and every affliction as forever the very conduct of quiescent extinction, conduct that never moves at all — neither a dharma of birth-and-death nor a dharma of nirvāṇa. Without abandoning any view, and without abandoning the unconditioned, one cultivates the buddha-path — neither cultivating the path nor not cultivating it. This is called "correctly abiding within the dharma-realm of affliction." Contemplate that among heavy karma, none is heavier than the five heinous crimes. The five heinous crimes are themselves already bodhi; bodhi and the five heinous crimes bear no mark of duality between them. There is no one who is aware of this, no one who knows it, no one who distinguishes it. The mark of the crime of rebellion and the mark of the true mark of reality are both alike inconceivable, indestructible, originally without any fixed nature of their own. Every karmic condition abides within true reality itself, neither coming nor going, neither cause nor fruit. This is what is meant by contemplating karma as itself the seal of the dharma-realm. The seal of the dharma-realm cannot be destroyed by any of the four demons; the demon can gain no advantage over it. Why is this? Because the demon is itself already the seal of the dharma-realm — how could the seal of the dharma-realm destroy the seal of the dharma-realm? Applying this same sense across every dharma whatsoever, the rest should likewise be understood.

Reading note: The one-practice samādhi's method is deceptively simple to state — ninety days of unbroken sitting, facing one direction, with nothing permitted but sitting itself — and radical in what its "mind" instructions actually ask: not a technique of concentration on some object, but binding the mind directly to the dharma-realm itself, so that "a single thought" already is what is being sought. The contemplation of the Tathāgata's ten epithets that follows systematically empties out every possible way of pinning the Buddha down as an object — not located in any direction, not the three times, no mark of purity even — and then, remarkably, applies the identical treatment to sentient beings, to affliction, and to the five heinous crimes (the most severe karma recognized in the tradition), all "the seal of the dharma-realm" alike. This is the same "even this is included" logic already familiar from fascicle 1's six identities and fourfold Four Noble Truths, now given as an actual sitting instruction rather than a scholastic analysis.

Encouragement to Cultivate It 勸修

Chinese Source

上所說者，皆是經文。勸修者，稱實功德，獎於行者。法界法，是佛真法，是菩薩印。聞此法不驚不畏，乃從百千萬億佛所久植德本。譬如長者失摩尼珠，後還得之，心甚歡喜。四眾不聞此法，心則苦惱；若聞信解，歡喜亦然。當知此人即是見佛，已曾從文殊聞是法。身子曰：「諦了此義，是名菩薩摩訶薩。」彌勒云：「是人近佛座，佛覺此法故。」故文殊云：「聞此法不驚，即是見佛。」佛言：「即住不退地，具六波羅蜜，具一切佛法矣。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Everything stated above is the sūtra's own text. As for encouragement to cultivate it: praising its true merit, to spur the practitioner onward. The dharma of the dharma-realm is the Buddha's own true Dharma, the bodhisattva's own seal. One who hears this Dharma without alarm and without fear has already, over a hundred thousand myriad koṭis of buddhas, long planted the very root of virtue. It is like a wealthy elder who lost a maṇi-jewel and later recovered it, his heart filled with great joy. If the fourfold assembly does not hear this Dharma, the heart suffers; if they hear it and believe and understand it, the joy is exactly the same. One should know that such a person has already seen the Buddha, and has already once heard this very Dharma from Mañjuśrī. Śāriputra said: "To clearly understand this meaning is what is called a bodhisattva-mahāsattva." Maitreya said: "Such a person sits near the Buddha's own seat, because the Buddha has awakened to this very Dharma." Hence Mañjuśrī says: "To hear this Dharma without alarm is itself already to see the Buddha." The Buddha said: "Such a one abides at once in the stage of non-retrogression, complete with the six pāramitās, complete with the whole of the Buddhadharma."

若人欲得一切佛法、相好威儀、說法音聲、十力無畏者，當行此一行三昧，勤行不懈，則能得入。如治摩尼珠，隨磨隨光，證不可思議功德。菩薩能知，速得菩提；比丘、比丘尼聞不驚，即隨佛出家；信士、信女聞不驚，即真歸依。此之稱譽，出彼兩經云云。

If a person wishes to obtain the whole of the Buddhadharma — every auspicious mark and dignified bearing, the very sound of the Buddha's own voice expounding the Dharma, the ten powers and the fearlessnesses — they should practice this one-practice samādhi, diligently and without slackening, and will thereby be able to enter it. It is like the polishing of a maṇi-jewel: with each polishing it grows brighter, and one comes to realize inconceivable merit. A bodhisattva who can know this swiftly attains bodhi; a bhikṣu or bhikṣuṇī who hears it without alarm has already, in that very moment, gone forth from home in the Buddha's own following; a layman or laywoman who hears it without alarm has already taken true refuge. This praise comes from those same two sūtras, and so on.

II. Constantly-Walking Samādhi 常行三昧

Chinese Source

二、常行三昧者，先方法，次勸修。方法者，身開遮，口說默，意止觀。此法出《般舟三昧經》，翻為「佛立」。「佛立」三義：一、佛威力，二、三昧力，三、行者本功德力。能於定中，見十方現在佛在其前立。如明眼人清夜觀星，見十方佛，亦如是多，故名「佛立三昧」。《十住婆沙》偈云：「是三昧住處，少中多差別；如是種種相，亦應須論議。」住處者，或於初禪，二、三、四、中間發是勢力，能生三昧，故名「住處」。初禪「少」；二禪「中」；三、四「多」。或少時住名「少」，或見世界少，或見佛少，故名「少」。「中」、「多」亦如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, constantly-walking samādhi: first the method, next the encouragement. As for the method: for the body, what is permitted and forbidden; for speech, what is spoken and kept silent; for the mind, calming and contemplation. This method comes from the Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra, and is rendered "the Buddha standing present." "The Buddha standing present" has three meanings: first, the Buddha's own majestic power; second, the power of the samādhi itself; third, the power of the practitioner's own root merit. Within concentration, one is able to see the buddhas of the ten directions, present now, standing directly before one. It is like a person with clear sight gazing at the stars on a clear night: seeing the buddhas of the ten directions is exactly as manifold as this — hence it is named "the samādhi of the Buddha standing present." The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā's own verse says: "As for where this samādhi abides, there is a difference of little, middling, and much; distinctions of this kind should also be discussed." As for "where it abides": whether in the first dhyāna, or in the second, third, fourth, or the intermediate stage, this power is brought forth, able to give rise to the samādhi — hence it is called "where it abides." The first dhyāna is "little"; the second dhyāna is "middling"; the third and fourth are "much." Or: abiding for a short time is called "little"; or seeing few world-systems, or seeing few buddhas, is called "little" — and "middling" and "much" are exactly the same.

身開常行，行此法時，避惡知識及癡人、親屬、鄉里，常獨處止。不得希望他人，有所求索；常乞食，不受別請。嚴飾道場，備諸供具、香餚、甘果。盥沐其身，左右出入，改換衣服。唯專行旋，九十日為一期。須明師，善內外律，能開除妨障。於所聞三昧處，如視世尊，不嫌不恚，不見短長。當割肌肉供養師，況復餘耶？承事師，如僕奉大家。若於師生惡，求是三昧終難得。須外護，如母養子；須同行，如共涉險。須要期誓願：「使我筋骨枯朽，學是三昧不得，終不休息。」起大信，無能壞者；起大精進，無能及者；所入智，無能逮者。常與善師從事。終竟三月，不得念世間想欲，如彈指頃。三月終竟，不得臥出，如彈指頃。終竟三月行，不得休息，除坐食左右。為人說經，不得希望衣食。《婆沙》偈云：「親近善知識，精進無懈怠，智慧甚堅牢，信力無妄動。」

For the body, constant walking is permitted. In practicing this method, avoid bad companions, foolish people, relatives, and fellow-villagers; dwell constantly alone. Do not hope for anything from others, nor seek anything from them; always beg for food, and do not accept private invitations. Adorn the practice-place with care, and provide every offering-implement, fragrant food, and sweet fruit. Bathe the body, and, whether coming or going, change one's robes. Devote oneself solely to walking in a circuit; ninety days make one period. One must have an enlightened teacher, well versed in both the monastic and the broader moral discipline, able to remove every obstruction. Toward the place from which one has heard of this samādhi, regard it as one would regard the World-Honored One himself — without resentment, without anger, without noting any fault or merit in it. One should even cut one's own flesh to make offering to one's teacher — how much less anything lesser than that? Serve one's teacher as a servant serves the head of a great household. Should one give rise to any ill will toward one's teacher, this samādhi will, in the end, be very hard to attain. One must have an outer protector, as a mother rears her child; one must have companions in practice, as those who cross danger together. One must set a fixed vow: "Though my very sinews and bones wither and crumble, if I fail to attain this samādhi, I will still never rest." Give rise to great faith, that nothing can destroy; give rise to great diligence, that nothing can equal; and the wisdom one enters will be such that nothing can surpass it. Constantly devote oneself to a good teacher. Throughout the full three months, one must not entertain any worldly thought or desire, not even for the space of a finger-snap. Throughout the full three months, one must not lie down to rest, not even for the space of a finger-snap. Throughout the full three months of walking, one must not cease, except for sitting, eating, and relieving oneself. In expounding the sūtra for others, one must not hope for robes or food in return. The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā's own verse says: "Draw close to a good spiritual friend, diligent without any slackening; let wisdom be exceedingly firm and secure, the power of faith never moved by falsehood."

口說默者，九十日，身常行，無休息；九十日，口常唱阿彌陀佛名，無休息；九十日，心常念阿彌陀佛，無休息。或唱念俱運，或先念後唱，或先唱後念，唱念相繼，無休息時。若唱彌陀，即是唱十方佛功德等，但專以彌陀為法門主。舉要言之，步步、聲聲、念念，唯在阿彌陀佛。意論止觀者，念西方阿彌陀佛，去此十萬億佛剎，在寶地、寶池、寶樹、寶堂、眾菩薩中央坐說經。三月常念佛。云何念？念三十二相，從足下千輻輪相，一一逆緣念諸相，乃至無見頂；亦應從頂相順緣，乃至千輻輪，令我亦逮是相。

As for speech, spoken and silent: for ninety days the body walks constantly, without rest; for ninety days the mouth constantly chants the name of Amitābha Buddha, without rest; for ninety days the mind constantly holds Amitābha Buddha in mind, without rest. Sometimes chanting and mindful recollection proceed together; sometimes recollection comes first and chanting after; sometimes chanting comes first and recollection after — chanting and recollection following one upon the other, with no time of rest at all. Chanting the name of Amitābha is itself already chanting the merit of the buddhas of the ten directions equally; it is only that Amitābha alone is taken as this dharma-gate's own presiding figure. To state the essential point: with every step, with every sound, with every thought — all rest in Amitābha Buddha alone. As for mind, calming and contemplation: recollect Amitābha Buddha in the west, ten myriad koṭis of buddha-lands from here, seated amid a jeweled land, jeweled pools, jeweled trees, and a jeweled hall, at the center of a great assembly of bodhisattvas, expounding a sūtra. Throughout the three months, constantly hold the Buddha in mind. How does one hold him in mind? Recollect the thirty-two marks, beginning from the thousand-spoked wheel mark on the soles of the feet, dwelling on each mark in reverse order one by one, up to the crown of the head, invisible even to the highest gaze; one should likewise proceed from the crown mark in forward order, down again to the thousand-spoked wheel, so that oneself, too, might attain these very marks.

又念：我當從心得佛，從身得佛？佛不用心得，不用身得。不用心得佛色，不用色得佛心。何以故？心者，佛無心；色者，佛無色，故不用色心得三菩提。佛色已盡，乃至識已盡。佛所說盡者，癡人不知，智者曉了。不用身口得佛，不用智慧得佛。何以故？智慧，索不可得；自索「我」了不可得，亦無所見。一切法本無所有，壞本、絕本其一。如夢見七寶，親屬歡樂；覺已追念，不知在何處。如是念佛。又如舍衛有女名須門，聞之心喜，夜夢從事；覺已念之，彼不來、我不往，而樂事宛然。當如是念佛。如人行大澤飢渴，夢得美食；覺已腹空，自念一切所有法皆如夢。當如是念佛。數數念，莫得休息；用是念，當生阿彌陀佛國。是名「如相念」。

One further reflects: shall I attain the Buddha through the mind, or attain the Buddha through the body? The Buddha is attained neither through the mind nor through the body. One does not attain the Buddha's own form through the mind, nor attain the Buddha's own mind through form. Why is this? As to mind — the Buddha has no mind; as to form — the Buddha has no form; hence perfect bodhi is attained neither through form nor through mind. The Buddha's own form is already exhausted, and consciousness, too, is already exhausted. What the Buddha teaches by "exhausted," the foolish do not understand, while the wise fully comprehend it. The Buddha is attained neither through body nor speech, nor attained through wisdom. Why is this? Sought for, wisdom cannot be found; seeking for a "self," in the end, it too cannot be found, and nothing at all is seen. Every dharma is, from the very root, without anything at all — its very root destroyed, its very root cut off. This is the first simile. It is like dreaming of the seven treasures, and of relatives rejoicing together; on waking and recalling it, one does not know where it has gone. One should hold the Buddha in mind in just this way. Again, it is like a woman of Śrāvastī named Sumana, of whom, on merely hearing her name, one's heart delights, and that night one dreams of being with her; on waking and recalling it, she did not come, nor did one go to her, and yet the pleasure of it remains vivid and entire. One should hold the Buddha in mind in just this way. It is like a person crossing a great wilderness, hungry and thirsty, who dreams of obtaining fine food; on waking, the stomach is still empty, and one reflects that every dharma whatsoever that exists is exactly like this dream. One should hold the Buddha in mind in just this way. Recollect again and again, without ever resting; by means of this recollection, one shall be born in Amitābha Buddha's own land. This is called "recollection according to its true mark."

如人以寶倚瑠璃上，影現其中。亦如比丘觀骨，骨起種種光，此無持來者，亦無有是骨，是意作耳。如鏡中像不外來、不中生，以鏡淨故，自見其形。行人色清淨，所有者清淨，欲見佛，即見佛；見即問，問即報，聞經大歡喜其二。自念：佛從何所來？我亦無所至，我所念即見。心作佛，心自見心，見佛心；是佛心，是我心見佛。心不自知心，心不自見心；心有想為癡，心無想是泥洹。是法無可示者，皆念所為。設有念，亦了無所有，空耳其三。偈云：「心者不知心，有心不見心，心起想即癡，無想即泥洹。」「諸佛從心得解脫，心者無垢名清淨，五道鮮潔不受色，有解此者成大道。」是名「佛印」，無所貪、無所著、無所求、無所想，所有盡、所欲盡，無所從生、無所可滅、無所壞敗。道要、道本，是印二乘不能壞，何況魔邪？云云。

It is like a jewel resting against a slab of lapis lazuli, and its reflection appearing within it. It is also like a monk contemplating bones, from which every kind of light arises — no one brought this light in from outside, nor is there truly any bone there at all; it is the mind's own doing, and nothing more. It is like the image in a mirror, which comes from nowhere outside and is not generated within — because the mirror itself is clear, one simply sees one's own form there. When the practitioner's own form is pure, and everything they possess is pure, then, wishing to see the Buddha, one sees the Buddha at once; seeing him, one asks a question; asking, one receives an answer; hearing the sūtra, one is filled with great joy. This is the second simile. One further reflects: from where has the Buddha come? Nor have I myself gone anywhere at all — what I hold in mind is precisely what I see. Mind makes the Buddha; mind itself sees mind; seeing the Buddha is mind — this very mind is the Buddha's mind, and it is my own mind that sees the Buddha. Mind does not know itself as mind; mind does not see itself as mind. Mind possessed of conceptual thought is delusion; mind free of conceptual thought is nirvāṇa. This Dharma has nothing that can be pointed to or shown — all of it is simply what recollection itself has done. Even granting that recollection occurs, in the end it, too, has nothing at all to it — sheer emptiness, and nothing more. This is the third simile. A verse says: "Mind does not know mind; possessing mind, one does not see mind. Mind giving rise to conceptual thought is itself delusion; mind free of conceptual thought is itself nirvāṇa." "Every buddha attains liberation through mind; mind, being without stain, is called pure. The five destinies, bright and unsullied, take on no form at all — one who understands this accomplishes the great path." This is called "the seal of the Buddha": nothing craved, nothing clung to, nothing sought, nothing conceived; every possession exhausted, every desire exhausted, nothing from which anything arises, nothing that can be extinguished, nothing that can be broken or destroyed. This is the very essence and root of the path, a seal that even the two lesser vehicles cannot break, much less any demon or deviant force, and so on.

《婆沙》明新發意菩薩，先念佛色相，相體、相業、相果、相用，得下勢力；次念佛四十不共法，心得中勢力；次念實佛，得佛上勢力，而不著色法二身。偈云：「不貪著色身，法身亦不著，善知一切法，永寂如虛空。」

The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā explains that a bodhisattva newly resolved on enlightenment first holds in mind the Buddha's own formal marks — the substance of the marks, their function, their fruit, their use — and thereby attains the lower power; next holds in mind the Buddha's own forty unshared qualities, and thereby the mind attains the middling power; next holds in mind the real Buddha, and thereby attains the highest power of the Buddha, no longer attached to either the form-body or the Dharma-body. A verse says: "Not craving or clinging to the form-body, nor clinging even to the Dharma-body — skillfully knowing every dharma whatsoever, forever quiescent, like empty space itself."

Reading note: This is the fascicle's most historically consequential passage: the constantly-walking samādhi, drawn from the Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra, is the earliest systematic source in East Asian Buddhism for continuous recitation of Amitābha's name paired with visualization of his physical marks — a practice that would go on to shape the entire subsequent history of Pure Land devotion, even though this treatise, characteristically, refuses to let the practice rest at the level of visualization alone. Notice the structure of the whole passage: strict, demanding external method (ninety days, no lying down, no private invitations, chanting and recollection running continuously) gives way to three similes for why none of it should be understood as literally fetching the Buddha from somewhere else — a dream, a lamp reflected in lapis lazuli, an image in a mirror — culminating in "mind makes the Buddha; mind itself sees mind." The famous line "the Buddha is attained neither through the mind nor through the body" answers a question the practice itself seems to invite: if visualizing the Buddha's marks in such loving physical detail, wouldn't one risk mistaking the image for the reality? The treatise's answer is not to abandon the visualization but to hold it inseparably together with knowing that "every dharma whatsoever is, from the very root, without anything at all."

Encouragement to Cultivate It 勸修

Chinese Source

勸修者，若人欲得智慧如大海，令無能為我作師者，於此坐不運神通，悉見諸佛、悉聞所說、悉能受持者，常行三昧於諸功德最為第一。此三昧是諸佛母、佛眼、佛父、無生大悲母；一切諸如來，從是二法生。碎大千地及草木為塵，一塵為一佛剎，滿爾世界中寶用布施，其福甚多；不如聞此三昧不驚不畏，況信、受持、讀誦、為人說？況定心修習，如𤛓牛乳頃？況能成是三昧？故無量無量。《婆沙》云：「劫火、官賊、怨毒、龍獸、眾病侵是人者，無有是處。此人常為天龍八部、諸佛皆共護念、稱讚。皆共欲見，共來其所。」若聞此三昧，如上四番功德皆隨喜，三世諸佛菩薩皆隨喜，復勝上四番功德。若不修如是法，失無量重寶，人天為之憂悲。如齆人，把栴檀而不嗅；如田家子，以摩尼珠博一頭牛云云。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for encouragement to cultivate it: if a person wishes to gain wisdom as vast as the great ocean, such that no one could any longer serve as their teacher; if, seated right here, without exercising any supernormal power, they can see every buddha, hear everything taught, and hold it all in mind — the constantly-walking samādhi, among every kind of merit, is foremost of all. This samādhi is the mother of every buddha, the eye of the Buddha, the father of the Buddha, the mother of unborn great compassion; every one of the Tathāgatas is born from these two dharmas. Were one to grind the whole earth of the great trichiliocosm, together with every plant and tree, into dust, and take each single mote of that dust as one buddha-land, and fill every one of those world-systems with treasure and give it all away — the merit of this would already be very great; yet it does not equal merely hearing of this samādhi without alarm and without fear, much less believing it, upholding it, reciting it, or explaining it to others; much less still fixing the mind and cultivating it, even for as long as it takes to milk a cow; much less actually accomplishing this samādhi. Hence it is measureless, measureless indeed. The Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā says: "That the fire at the end of an eon, officials, bandits, poisoned enmity, dragons, wild beasts, or manifold illness should ever assail such a person — there is no such possibility. This person is constantly protected, held in mind, and praised together by the eightfold host of gods and dragons and by every buddha alike; all alike wish to see them, and all come together to where they are." If one but hears of this samādhi, the four kinds of merit described above are all matters for rejoicing; the buddhas and bodhisattvas of the three times all rejoice in it as well, surpassing even the four kinds of merit described above. If one fails to cultivate a Dharma such as this, one loses a measureless, weighty treasure, and gods and humans alike grieve over it on one's behalf. It is like a person with a blocked nose holding sandalwood and never smelling it; like a farmer's child trading a maṇi-jewel for a single head of cattle, and so on.

III. Half-Walking-Half-Sitting Samādhi 半行半坐三昧

Chinese Source

三、明半行半坐，亦先方法，次勸修。方法者，身開遮，口說默，意止觀。此出二經。《方等》云：「旋百二十匝，却坐思惟。」《法華》云：「其人若行、若立，讀誦是經；若坐，思惟是經，我乘六牙白象，現其人前」故知俱用半行半坐為方法也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, explaining half walking and half sitting: again, first the method, next the encouragement. As for the method: for the body, what is permitted and forbidden; for speech, what is spoken and kept silent; for the mind, calming and contemplation. This comes from two sūtras. The Vaipulya Dhāraṇī Sūtra says: "Circle one hundred and twenty times, then sit back and contemplate." The Lotus Sūtra says: "Should that person, whether walking or standing, recite this sūtra; or, sitting, contemplate this sūtra, I shall mount the six-tusked white elephant and appear before that person." From this it is known that both together make use of half walking and half sitting as their method.

By Way of the Vaipulya Dhāraṇī Sūtra 方等三昧

Chinese Source

方等至尊，不可聊爾，若欲修習，神明為證。先求夢王，若得見一，是許懺悔。於閑靜處，莊嚴道場，香泥塗地，及室內外作圓壇彩畫，懸五色幡，燒海岸香，然燈，敷高座，請二十四尊像，多亦無妨。設餚饌，盡心力。須新淨衣、鞵、屩，無新浣故；出入著脫，無令參雜。七日長齋，日三時洗浴。初日供養僧，隨意多少。別請一明了內外律者為師，受二十四戒及陀羅尼呪。對師說罪要用月八日、十五日。當以七日為一期，決不可減；若能更進，隨意堪任。十人已還，不得出此。俗人亦許，須辦單縫三衣，備佛法式也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Vaipulya rite is utterly exalted, and must not be undertaken carelessly; if one wishes to cultivate it, the divine and numinous stand as its witnesses. First seek a sign in dream, the King of dreams; should one obtain a single such vision, this grants permission for the repentance to proceed. In a quiet and secluded place, adorn the practice-place with care: smear fragrant paste on the ground, and, both within and outside the room, paint a round altar; hang banners of five colors; burn incense of aloeswood; light lamps; set out a high seat; and invite images of the twenty-four venerable ones — more than this is also no hindrance. Prepare offerings of food, exerting the utmost of one's own effort. New, clean robes, sandals, and slippers are required — never new mixed with old; whether putting them on or taking them off, never let them be mixed together with anything else. Observe a full fast for seven days, bathing three times each day. On the first day, make offerings to the saṅgha, in whatever quantity one is able. Separately invite one teacher who is thoroughly versed in both the monastic and the broader moral discipline, and receive from them the twenty-four precepts and the dhāraṇī-mantra. Confessing one's offenses before this teacher should be done on the eighth and fifteenth days of the month. Seven days should be taken as one period, which must never be shortened; should one be able to continue further, this may be done according to one's own capacity. No more than ten people should take part in this. Lay practitioners, too, are permitted to undertake it, provided they prepare a single-layered set of the three robes, in keeping with the Buddha-dharma's own proper form.

口說默者，預誦陀羅尼呪一篇使利。於初日分，異口同音，三遍召請三寶、十佛、方等父母、十法王子。召請法在《國清百錄》中。請竟，燒香運念，三業供養。供養訖，禮前所請三寶。禮竟，以志誠心，悲泣雨淚，陳悔罪咎。竟，起旋百二十匝，一旋一呪，不遲不疾，不高不下。旋呪竟，禮十佛、方等、十法王子。如是作已，却坐思惟；思惟訖，更起旋呪；旋呪竟，更却坐思惟，周而復始。終竟七日，其法如是。從第二時，略召請，餘悉如常。

As for speech, spoken and silent: first recite the dhāraṇī-mantra through once, until it is fluent. In the first watch of the first day, with every voice in unison, invoke the Three Jewels, the ten buddhas, the father and mother of the Vaipulya rite, and the ten Dharma-princes, three times over. The method of invocation is found in the Guoqing Bailu. Once the invocation is complete, burn incense and set the mind in motion, making offering through the three karmic actions. Once the offering is complete, pay reverence to the Three Jewels just invoked. Once the reverence is complete, with a mind of utmost sincerity, weeping tears of grief, set forth one's confession of every offense and fault. Once this is complete, rise and circle one hundred and twenty times, one mantra-recitation for each circuit, neither too slow nor too fast, neither too loud nor too soft. Once the circling and chanting are complete, pay reverence to the ten buddhas, the Vaipulya rite, and the ten Dharma-princes. Having done this, sit back and contemplate; once the contemplation is complete, rise again to circle and chant; once the circling and chanting are complete, sit back once more to contemplate — round and round, beginning again each time. Throughout the full seven days, this same method is followed. From the second watch onward, the invocation may be abbreviated; everything else remains exactly as before.

意止觀者，經令思惟，思惟「摩訶袒持陀羅尼」，翻為「大祕要遮惡持善」。祕要，秖是實相、中道、正空。經言：「吾從真實中來。真實者，寂滅相；寂滅相者，無有所求，求者亦空。得者、著者、實者、來者、語者、問者悉空。寂滅、涅槃亦復皆空；一切虛空分界亦復皆空其一。」「無所求中，吾故求之，如是空空真實之法，當於何求？六波羅蜜中求其二。」此與《大品》十八空同。《大經》迦毘羅城空、如來空、大涅槃空，更無有異。以此空慧歷一切事，無不成觀。

As for mind, calming and contemplation: the sūtra instructs one to contemplate — to contemplate the "Mahā-dhāraṇī of Grasping," rendered as "the great secret essential that restrains evil and upholds the good." This secret essential is nothing other than the true mark of reality, the middle way, correct emptiness. The sūtra says: "I come from within the truly real. The truly real is the mark of quiescent extinction; the mark of quiescent extinction is that there is nothing to be sought, and the seeker, too, is empty. The one who attains, the one who clings, the real, the one who comes, the one who speaks, the one who asks — all alike are empty. Quiescent extinction and nirvāṇa, too, are both alike empty; every division and boundary of empty space, too, is likewise empty." This is the first. "Within this state where nothing is sought, I nonetheless still seek it — such an empty, truly real dharma of pure emptiness, where, then, is it to be sought? It is to be sought within the six pāramitās." This is the second. This is the same as the eighteen kinds of emptiness in the Larger Sūtra. In the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, the emptiness of Kapilavastu, the emptiness of the Tathāgata, and the emptiness of the great nirvāṇa are, again, no different from this. Applying this wisdom of emptiness across every single matter, there is nothing that does not become a genuine contemplation.

方等者，或言「廣平」；今言「方」者，「法」也。般若有四種方法，謂四門入清涼池，即「方」也。所契之理，平等大慧，即「等」也。令求夢王，即二觀前方便也。道場，即清淨境界也。治五住糠、顯實相米，亦是定慧用莊嚴法身也。香塗者，即無上尸羅也。五色蓋者，觀五陰免子縛，起大慈悲，覆法界也。圓壇者，即實相不動地也。繒旛，即翻法界上迷，生動出之解。旛、壇不相離，即動出、不動出不相離也。香燈，即戒慧也。高座者，諸法空也。一切佛皆栖此空。二十四像者，即是逆順觀十二因緣，覺了智也。餚饌者，即是無常、苦酢，助道觀也。新淨衣者，即寂滅忍也。瞋惑重積，稱「故」；翻瞋起忍，名為「新」。七日，即七覺也。一日，即一實諦也。三洗，即觀一實、修三觀、蕩三障、淨三智也。一師者，即一實諦也。二十四戒者，逆順十二因緣發道共戒也。呪者，囑對也。《瓔珞》明十二因緣有十種，即有一百二十支；一呪一支。束而言之，秖是三道，謂苦、業、煩惱也。今呪此因緣，即是呪於三道而論懺悔。事懺，懺苦道、業道；理懺，懺煩惱道。文云：「犯沙彌戒，乃至大比丘戒，若不還生，無有是處。」即懺業道文也。「眼耳諸根清淨」，即懺苦道文也。「第七日見十方佛，聞法得不退轉」，即懺煩惱道文也。三障去，即十二因緣樹壞，亦是五陰舍空。思惟實相，正破於此，故名「諸佛實法懺悔」也。

As for "Vaipulya": it may also be rendered "vast and level." What is here called "vast" means "the Dharma." The Perfection of Wisdom has four kinds of method, namely the four gates entering the cooling pool — this is what is meant by "vast." The principle thereby engaged, the great wisdom of equality, is what is meant by "level." Seeking a sign in dream, the King, is the preliminary expedient means for the two contemplations. The practice-place is the pure and clear state itself. Removing the husk of the five abodes of affliction to reveal the rice-grain of the true mark of reality is likewise concentration and wisdom adorning the Dharma-body in their function. The fragrant paste is the unsurpassed moral discipline itself. The five-colored canopy stands for contemplating the five aggregates, freeing them from the snare that binds the hare, and giving rise to great compassion that covers over the whole dharma-realm. The round altar is the true mark of reality's own unmoving ground. The silk banners turn the delusion resting atop the dharma-realm into the understanding that both moves outward and emerges from it; banner and altar are never separate from each other, just as the moving-and-emerging and the unmoving-and-emerging are never separate from each other. The fragrance and the lamp are precepts and wisdom. The high seat is the emptiness of every dharma — every buddha alike dwells within this emptiness. The twenty-four images are the wisdom of awakened understanding, contemplating the twelve links of dependent origination both forward and backward. The food offerings are the contemplation, aiding the path, of impermanence and the bitterness of suffering. The new, clean robes are the forbearance of quiescent extinction — where anger and delusion have long accumulated, this is called "old"; where anger is turned around and forbearance arises, this is called "new." The seven days are the seven factors of enlightenment. The single day is the one real truth. The threefold bathing is contemplating the one real truth, cultivating the three contemplations, sweeping away the three obstructions, and purifying the three wisdoms. The single teacher is the one real truth itself. The twenty-four precepts are the path-conascent precepts brought forth by contemplating the twelve links of dependent origination both forward and backward. The mantra-recitation is an entrusting correspondence: the Yingluo Sūtra explains that the twelve links of dependent origination each have ten aspects, making one hundred and twenty branches in all — one mantra-recitation for each branch. Gathered together, these are simply the three paths: suffering, karma, and affliction. The mantra recited here upon this causal chain is thus a mantra upon these three paths, discussed here in terms of repentance. Repentance through phenomenal conduct repents the path of suffering and the path of karma; repentance through principle repents the path of affliction. The text says: "Should one who has violated the novice's precepts, down to the fully-ordained monk's own precepts, fail to be restored to that standing, there is no such possibility" — this is the text repenting the path of karma. "The eyes, ears, and every faculty made pure" is the text repenting the path of suffering. "On the seventh day, seeing the buddhas of the ten directions, hearing the Dharma, and attaining non-retrogression" is the text repenting the path of affliction. Once the three obstructions are gone, the very tree of the twelve links of dependent origination collapses, and the house of the five aggregates, too, stands empty. To contemplate the true mark of reality is precisely to break through all of this — hence this is named "repentance through the buddhas' own real Dharma."

勸修者，諸佛得道皆由此法，是佛父母，世間無上大寶。若能修行，得全分寶；但能讀誦，得中分寶；華香供養，得下分寶。佛與文殊說下分寶所不能盡，況中上耶？若從地積寶至梵天，以奉於佛，不如施持經者一食充軀，如經廣說云云。

As for encouragement to cultivate it: every buddha's own attainment of the path comes by means of this Dharma; it is the father and mother of every buddha, the unsurpassed great treasure of the world. One able to practice it in full obtains the whole portion of this treasure; one able only to recite it obtains the middle portion of this treasure; one who makes offering with flowers and incense obtains the lesser portion of this treasure. The Buddha, in conversation with Mañjuśrī, said that even the lesser portion of this treasure could not be exhausted in the telling — how much less, then, the middle or the whole? Were one to pile up treasure from the earth all the way up to the Brahmā heaven and offer it all to the Buddha, this would still not equal giving a single meal to sustain the body of one who upholds this sūtra, as the sūtra explains at length, and so on.

Reading note: The Vaipulya rite's method is the treatise's most elaborate ritual instruction so far — a demanding seven-day retreat of alternating circumambulation and seated contemplation, with strict rules for dress, bathing, and confession. What makes this passage distinctive is what follows: a line-by-line symbolic reading of every ritual element just described, from the round altar (the unmoving ground of the true mark of reality) to the twenty-four images (the twelve links of dependent origination read forward and backward) to the confession schedule itself, mapped onto the three paths of suffering, karma, and affliction. Nothing in the ritual is merely decorative — every object and gesture is treated as already teaching the doctrine it enacts, which is itself a working example of this fascicle's own recurring claim that "phenomenal conduct" and "principle" are never really separable.

By Way of the Lotus Sūtra 法華三昧

Chinese Source

約《法華》，亦明方法、勸修。方法者，身開遮，口說默，意止觀。身開為十：一、嚴淨道場，二、淨身，三、三業供養，四、請佛，五、禮佛，六、六根懺悔，七、遶旋，八、誦經，九、坐禪，十、證相。別有一卷，名《法華三昧》，是天台師所著，流傳於世，行者宗之。此則兼於說、默，不復別論也。

Ru-Yi Translation

In terms of the Lotus Sūtra, the method and the encouragement to cultivate it are likewise explained. As for the method: for the body, what is permitted and forbidden; for speech, what is spoken and kept silent; for the mind, calming and contemplation. For the body, ten things are permitted: first, adorning and purifying the practice-place; second, purifying the body; third, offering through the three karmic actions; fourth, inviting the Buddha; fifth, paying reverence to the Buddha; sixth, repentance through the six faculties; seventh, circumambulation; eighth, reciting the sūtra; ninth, sitting in meditation; tenth, verifying the resulting mark. There exists a separate single fascicle, named Fahua Sanmei (Lotus Samādhi), composed by the Tiantai Master himself, circulated widely in the world, and taken as authoritative by practitioners. This already covers both speaking and silence together, and need not be discussed separately here.

意止觀者，《普賢觀》云：「專誦大乘，不入三昧，日夜六時，懺六根罪。」〈安樂行品〉云：「於諸法無所行，亦不行、不分別。」二經本為相成，豈可執文拒競？蓋乃為緣，前後互出，非碩異也。〈安樂行品〉護持、讀誦、解說、深心禮拜等，豈非事耶？《觀經》明無相懺悔：「我心自空，罪福無主」、「慧日能消除」，豈非理耶？南岳師云「有相安樂行」、「無相安樂行」，豈非就事、理得如是名？持是行人，涉事修六根懺，為悟入弄引，故名「有相」；若直觀一切法空為方便者，故言「無相」。妙證之時，悉皆兩捨。若得此意，於二經無疑。

As for mind, calming and contemplation: the Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra says: "Single-mindedly reciting the Mahāyāna, even without entering samādhi, throughout the six periods of day and night, repent the offenses of the six faculties." The "Course of Ease and Bliss" chapter says: "With regard to every dharma, there is nothing to be done, no doing, and no discriminating." These two sūtras were originally meant to complete each other — how could one cling to the words of one and reject the other in competition? They were simply spoken according to differing conditions, appearing at different points, and are not truly, fundamentally at odds. Does not the "Course of Ease and Bliss" chapter's own guarding, upholding, reciting, explaining, and bowing with deep sincerity all count as phenomenal conduct? Does not the Contemplation Sūtra's own explanation of markless repentance — "my own mind is itself empty, offense and merit alike without any master," "the sun of wisdom is able to dissolve them away" — count as principle? When the Nanyue Master spoke of "the course of ease and bliss with marks" and "the course of ease and bliss without marks," was this not precisely a matter of naming things according to phenomenal conduct and principle respectively? A practitioner upholding this practice, engaging with phenomenal conduct to cultivate repentance through the six faculties as a way of coaxing oneself toward awakening, is why it is called "with marks"; one who takes contemplating the emptiness of all dharmas directly as the expedient means is why it is called "without marks." At the moment of wondrous realization, both alike are set aside entirely. Grasping this sense, there is no cause for doubt between these two sūtras.

今歷文修觀，言「六牙白象」者，是菩薩無漏六神通。牙有利用，如通之捷疾；象有大力，表法身荷負；無漏、無染，稱之為白。頭上三人，一持金剛杵，一持金剛輪，一持如意珠，表三智居無漏頂云云。杵擬象能行，表慧導行；輪轉表出假；如意表中。牙上有池，表八解，是禪體；通是定用，體用不相離故。牙端有池，池中有華，華表妙因，以神通力淨佛國土，利益眾生，即是因。因從通生，如華由池發。華中有女，女表慈。若無無緣慈，豈能以神通力促身令小，入此娑婆？通由慈運，如華擎女。女執樂器，表四攝也。慈修身口，現種種同事、利行、財法二施，引物多端；如五百樂器音聲無量也。示喜見身者，是普現色身三昧也。隨所宜樂而為現之，未必純作白玉之像。語言陀羅尼者，即是慈熏口說種種法也，皆「法華三昧」之異名，得此意於象身上，自在作法門也。

Now, working through the text to cultivate this contemplation: what is meant by "the six-tusked white elephant" is the bodhisattva's own uncontaminated six supernormal powers. The tusks have their own sharp function, likened to how swift and unimpeded these powers are; the elephant possesses great strength, standing for the Dharma-body's own capacity to bear every burden; uncontaminated and undefiled, it is called "white." The three figures upon its head — one holding a vajra pestle, one holding a vajra wheel, one holding a wish-fulfilling jewel — stand for the three wisdoms dwelling atop the uncontaminated, and so on. The pestle, aimed so the elephant may proceed, stands for wisdom guiding practice; the turning wheel stands for emerging from the provisional; the wish-fulfilling jewel stands for the middle. Above the tusks is a pool, standing for the eight liberations, the very substance of dhyāna; the supernormal powers are the function of concentration, and substance and function are never separate from each other. At the tip of the tusks is the pool, and within the pool is a lotus flower; the flower stands for the wondrous cause — purifying the buddha-land and benefiting sentient beings by supernormal power is itself the cause. The cause is born from the supernormal power, just as the flower issues from the pool. Within the flower is a woman; the woman stands for loving-kindness. Without objectless loving-kindness, how could one, by supernormal power, contract the body and make it small enough to enter this world of Sahā? The supernormal power is set in motion by loving-kindness, just as the flower bears up the woman. The woman holds a musical instrument, standing for the four means of attraction. Loving-kindness cultivates body and speech, manifesting every kind of shared endeavor, beneficial conduct, and the two forms of giving, material and Dharma, drawing beings in through manifold means — like the five hundred musical instruments, their sounds beyond number. Manifesting the body of joyful appearance is the samādhi of universally manifesting a physical form, appearing according to whatever is fitting and pleasing, not necessarily always taking the form of a pure white jade image. The dhāraṇī of language is the manifold dharmas spoken by loving-kindness's own perfuming of speech — all of these are simply other names for the Lotus Samādhi, and, grasping this sense, one may act freely and without hindrance through the whole dharma-gate found within the elephant's own body.

Reading note: The reconciliation of the Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra's elaborate six-faculties repentance ritual with the Lotus Sūtra's own "Course of Ease and Bliss" chapter — which insists there is "nothing to be done, no doing, and no discriminating" — repeats the treatise's basic move against apparent scriptural conflict: not a contradiction, but Nanyue Huisi's own "with marks" and "without marks" courses of ease and bliss, phenomenal conduct and principle addressing the same reality from two directions. The reading of the six-tusked white elephant that follows is a small masterpiece of the treatise's allegorical method: every visual detail of the Lotus Sūtra's own vision — the elephant's tusks, the pool between them, the lotus flower, the woman within it holding an instrument — is treated as encoding a specific element of bodhisattva practice, so that the entire, elaborate image becomes, in Zhiyi's phrase, "the whole dharma-gate found within the elephant's own body."

As for encouragement to cultivate it: the Samantabhadra Contemplation Sūtra says: "Should any of the seven assemblies have broken the precepts, and wish, in the space of a single finger-snap, to remove and extinguish the offense of birth-and-death accumulated over a hundred thousand myriad koṭis of incalculable kalpas..." "Should one wish to give rise to the bodhi-mind, without severing affliction yet entering nirvāṇa, without departing from the five desires yet purifying every faculty and seeing beyond every obstruction..." "Should one wish to see the divided bodies, Prabhūtaratna, and Śākyamuni Buddha..." "Should one wish to attain the Lotus Samādhi and the dhāraṇī of every language..." "To enter the Tathāgata's own room, wear the Tathāgata's own robe, and sit upon the Tathāgata's own seat..." "To expound the Dharma in the midst of the eightfold host of gods and dragons..." "Should one wish that Mañjuśrī, Bhaiṣajyarāja, and the other great bodhisattvas hold flowers and incense and stand in attendance in the sky..." — "one should cultivate this Lotus Sūtra, recite the Mahāyāna, and hold the matters of the Mahāyāna in mind." "Let this wisdom of emptiness correspond with the mind." "Recollect that every bodhisattva's own mother, the unsurpassed and supreme expedient means, is born from contemplating the true mark of reality." So the sūtra explains at length. Who, hearing a Dharma such as this, would fail to give rise to the bodhi-mind? Excepting only those unworthy people, benighted in delusion and without wisdom.

IV. Neither-Walking-Nor-Sitting Samādhi 非行非坐三昧

Chinese Source

四、非行非坐三昧者，上一向用行、坐，此既異上，為成四句，故名「非行非坐」；實通行、坐及一切事。而南岳師呼為「隨自意」，意起即修三昧。《大品》稱「覺意三昧」，意之趣向，皆覺識明了。雖復三名，實是一法。今依經釋名。「覺」者，照了也；「意」者，心數也；「三昧」如前釋。行者心數起時，反照觀察，不見動轉、根原、終末、來處去處，故名「覺意」。諸數無量，何故對意論覺？窮諸法源，皆由意造，故以意為言端。對境覺知，異乎木石，名為「心」；次心籌量，名為「意」；了了別知，名為「識」。如是分別，墮心想見倒中，豈名為「覺」？

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, neither-walking-nor-sitting samādhi: the practices above made exclusive use of walking and sitting; since this one differs from those, and is needed to complete the fourfold pattern, it is named "neither walking nor sitting" — though in truth it extends through walking, sitting, and every activity whatsoever. The Nanyue Master called it "following one's own intent," since the samādhi is cultivated the moment any intent arises. The Larger Sūtra calls it "the samādhi of awakened intent," since wherever the intent turns, it is at once awakened and clearly understood. Though it bears three names, it is, in truth, a single dharma. Here the name is explained according to the sūtra. "Awakened" means illuminated and understood; "intent" means a mental factor; "samādhi" is explained as before. When the practitioner's own mental factor arises, turning the light of contemplation back upon it, one finds no motion, no root or origin, no beginning or end, no place it comes from or goes to — hence it is called "awakened intent." The mental factors are without number — why, then, is "awakened" spoken of specifically in relation to "intent"? Because, tracing every dharma back to its very source, all of it is fashioned by intent alone — hence "intent" is taken as the leading term. Awareness directed toward an object, distinct from wood or stone, is named "mind"; the deliberation that follows upon mind is named "intent"; clear, distinct discernment is named "consciousness." Distinctions made in this way still fall within the inverted view of mind, conception, and view — how could this be called "awakened"?

「覺」者，了知心中非有意，亦非不有意；心中非有識，亦非不有識；意中非有心，亦非不有心；意中非有識，亦非不有識；識中非有意，亦非不有意；識中非有心，亦非不有心。心、意、識非一，故立三名；非三，故說一性。若知名非名，則性亦非性。非名故不三，非性故不一；非三故不散，非一故不合；不合故不空，不散故不有；非有故不常，非空故不斷。若不見常、斷，終不見一、異。若觀意者，則攝心、識，一切法亦爾。若破意，無明則壞，餘使皆去。故諸法雖多，但舉意以明三昧。觀則調直，故言「覺意三昧」也。「隨自意」、「非行非坐」準此可解。

"Awakened" means understanding that within mind there is neither intent nor the absence of intent; within mind there is neither consciousness nor the absence of consciousness; within intent there is neither mind nor the absence of mind; within intent there is neither consciousness nor the absence of consciousness; within consciousness there is neither intent nor the absence of intent; within consciousness there is neither mind nor the absence of mind. Mind, intent, and consciousness are not one — hence three names are established for them; they are not three — hence a single nature is spoken of. If one knows that the name is not truly a name, then the nature, too, is not truly a nature. Because it is not a name, it is not three; because it is not a nature, it is not one. Because it is not three, it is not scattered; because it is not one, it is not combined. Because it is not combined, it is not empty; because it is not scattered, it is not existent. Because it is not existent, it is not permanent; because it is not empty, it is not annihilated. If one does not see permanence or annihilation, one will, in the end, never see oneness or difference. If one contemplates intent, mind and consciousness are thereby included as well, and every dharma is exactly the same. If intent is broken through, ignorance itself collapses, and every remaining affliction departs along with it. Hence, though the dharmas are many, intent alone is raised to clarify this samādhi. Contemplation is thereby made regulated and direct — hence it is called "the samādhi of awakened intent." "Following one's own intent" and "neither walking nor sitting" may be understood along these same lines.

Reading note: The fourth samādhi completes the set by naming what the previous three had already been doing without saying so: every moment, in any posture, can be the occasion for this same practice, the instant intent (意) arises. The dense little argument that mind, intent, and consciousness are "not one" yet "not three" is another instance of the tetralemma pattern this treatise never tires of — and note the closing logical chain: not-three leads to not-scattered, not-one leads to not-combined, not-combined leads to not-empty, not-scattered leads to not-existent, and so on, each step following necessarily from the last, refusing every stopping point along the way. This section is by far the longest and richest of the four samādhis, dividing into four further approaches: by way of the sūtras, by way of wholesome states, by way of unwholesome states, and by way of indeterminate states.

1. By Way of the Sūtras: the Sūtra of Invoking Avalokiteśvara 約諸經

As for the practices given in the sūtras: whatever is not already included in the three practices above belongs to "following one's own intent." Take, for instance, the Sūtra of Invoking Avalokiteśvara for how this looks in practice: in a quiet place, adorn the practice-place with banners, canopies, incense, and lamps; invite an image of Amitābha, and images of the two bodhisattvas Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta, set in the west; and prepare a willow branch and pure water. 經云：「眼與色相應，云何攝住？」乃至「意與攀緣相應，云何攝住者？」《大集》云：「如心住。」如，即空也。此文一一皆入如實之際，即是「如」、「空」之異名耳。地無堅者，若謂地是有，有即實，實是堅義；若謂地是無，是亦有亦無、非有非無，是事實，皆是堅義。今明畢竟不可得，亡其堅性也。水性不住者，謂水為有，有即是住，乃至謂水是非有非無，亦即是住。今不住有四句，亦不住無四句中，亦不住不可說中，故言水性不住。風性無礙者，觀風為有，有即是礙，乃至謂風非有非無，亦無無四句，故言風性無礙。火大不實者，火不從自生，乃至不從無因生；本無自性，賴緣而有，故言不實。觀色既爾，受、想、行、識一一皆入如實之際。觀陰既爾，十二因緣如谷響，如芭蕉堅、露、電等，一時運念，令空觀成，勤須修習，使得相應。觀慧之本，不可闕也。

The sūtra says: "The eye, corresponding to form — how is it to be gathered in and made to abide?" — down to "intent, corresponding to whatever it clings to and pursues — how is it to be gathered in and made to abide?" The Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "Like mind, it abides" — "like" here means "empty." Every clause of this text, one by one, enters into the very brink of true reality itself — "like" and "empty" are simply different names for the same thing. As for "the earth has no solidity": if one holds the earth to be existent, existence would mean it is real, and reality would mean solidity; if one holds the earth to be non-existent, or both existent and non-existent, or neither existent nor non-existent, this too, in fact, amounts to the very same claim of solidity. What is explained here is that, in the end, it can never be found at all — its very solidity is thereby lost. As for "water's nature does not abide": to hold water to be existent is already to make it "abide"; and so too holding water to be neither existent nor non-existent is likewise to make it "abide." Here it abides in none of the four alternatives of existence, none of the four alternatives of non-existence, nor in the inexpressible either — hence it is said that water's nature does not abide. As for "wind's nature is unobstructed": to contemplate wind as existent is already to make it an obstruction, down to holding wind as neither existent nor non-existent — this too is no different, under any of the four alternatives — hence it is said that wind's nature is unobstructed. As for "the element of fire is unreal": fire does not arise from itself, down to it does not arise without cause; fundamentally without any fixed nature of its own, it exists only by relying on conditions — hence it is said to be unreal. Contemplating form in this way, sensation, perception, formations, and consciousness, too, each and every one, enter into the very brink of true reality. Contemplating the aggregates in this way, the twelve links of dependent origination are like an echo in a valley, like the pith of a plantain tree, like dew, like lightning — setting the mind in motion toward all of this at once, so that the contemplation of emptiness is accomplished, one must diligently cultivate it until it truly corresponds. This is the very root of contemplative wisdom, and cannot be left out.

銷伏毒害陀羅尼能破報障，毘舍離人平復如本。破惡業陀羅尼能破業障，破梵行人蕩除糞穢，令得清淨。六字章句陀羅尼能破煩惱障，淨於三毒根，成佛道無疑。六字即是六觀世音，能破六道三障。所謂大悲觀世音破地獄道三障；此道苦重，宜用大悲。大慈觀世音破餓鬼道三障；此道飢渴，宜用大慈。師子無畏觀世音破畜生道三障；獸王威猛，宜用無畏也。大光普照觀世音破阿脩羅道三障；其道猜、忌、嫉、疑，偏宜用普照也。天人丈夫觀世音破人道三障；人道有事、理，事伏憍慢，稱「天人」，理則見佛性，故稱「丈夫」。大梵深遠觀世音破天道三障；梵是天主，標主得臣也。廣六觀世音即是二十五三昧，大悲即是無垢三昧；大慈即是心樂三昧；師子即是不退三昧；大光即是歡喜三昧；丈夫即是如幻等四三昧；大梵即是不動等十七三昧。自思之可見云云。此經通三乘人懺悔，若自調自度，殺諸結賊，成阿羅漢；若福厚根利，觀無明、行等，成緣覺道；若起大悲，身如瑠璃，毛孔見佛，得首楞嚴，住不退轉。諸大乘經有此流類，或七佛八菩薩懺，或虛空藏八百日塗廁，如此等，皆是隨自意攝云云。

The Dhāraṇī That Dissolves and Subdues Poisonous Harm can break through the obstruction of karmic retribution — the people of Vaiśālī were restored to health exactly as before. The Dhāraṇī That Breaks Unwholesome Karma can break through the obstruction of karma — one who has violated the pure conduct of brahmacarya is cleansed of that filth and made pure again. The Dhāraṇī of the Six-Syllable Passage can break through the obstruction of affliction, purifying the very root of the three poisons, so that the accomplishment of the buddha-path is beyond doubt. The six syllables are the six forms of Avalokiteśvara, each able to break through the three obstructions of one of the six destinies. Namely: Avalokiteśvara of Great Compassion breaks the three obstructions of the hell-destiny — this destiny's suffering is severe, and so great compassion is fitting for it. Avalokiteśvara of Great Loving-Kindness breaks the three obstructions of the hungry-ghost destiny — this destiny suffers hunger and thirst, and so great loving-kindness is fitting for it. Avalokiteśvara the Fearless Lion breaks the three obstructions of the animal destiny — the king of beasts is majestic and fierce, and so fearlessness is fitting for it. Avalokiteśvara of Great Light Universally Shining breaks the three obstructions of the asura destiny — this destiny is given to suspicion, envy, jealousy, and doubt, and so universal illumination is especially fitting for it. Avalokiteśvara, Man Among Gods and Humans, breaks the three obstructions of the human destiny — the human destiny has both phenomenal conduct and principle: in phenomenal conduct, arrogance is subdued, hence "man among gods and humans"; in principle, the buddha-nature is seen, hence "man" in the sense of a true adept. Avalokiteśvara of Great Brahmā, Profound and Far-Reaching, breaks the three obstructions of the heavenly destiny — Brahmā is the lord of the heavens, and naming the lord implies gaining the ministers beneath him as well. Extended in full, the six forms of Avalokiteśvara are the twenty-five samādhis: great compassion is the stainless samādhi; great loving-kindness is the samādhi of a joyful mind; the lion is the samādhi of non-retrogression; great light is the samādhi of delight; the man among gods and humans is the fourfold samādhi of illusion and the rest; great Brahmā is the seventeenfold samādhi of the unmoved and the rest. Reflecting on this for oneself, the rest may be seen, and so on. This sūtra provides repentance for practitioners of all three vehicles alike: one who tames and delivers only oneself, slaying the bandits of the fetters, accomplishes arhatship; one of thick merit and sharp faculties, contemplating ignorance, formations, and the rest, accomplishes the pratyekabuddha's own path; one who gives rise to great compassion, the body like lapis lazuli, seeing the Buddha through every pore, attains the Śūraṅgama and abides in non-retrogression. Other Mahāyāna sūtras belong to this same class of practice as well — such as the Repentance of the Seven Buddhas and Eight Bodhisattvas, or Ākāśagarbha's eight hundred days of cleaning latrines — all such things belong to the category of following one's own intent, and so on.

Reading note: The Sūtra of Invoking Avalokiteśvara's ritual gives this fourth samādhi its one worked example of external method, immediately followed by the same treatment already seen for the Vaipulya rite: every element of the ritual's own doctrinal content — the four elements analyzed as neither existent nor non-existent, the six-syllable dhāraṇī matched one-to-one against the six destinies and their fitting antidote (compassion for hell's suffering, fearlessness for the animal realm's ferocity, and so on) — is drawn out and shown to already contain the whole of the treatise's own teaching. This closes the "by way of the sūtras" approach; the three approaches that follow turn from named scriptural rituals to a general method — the fourfold moment of a thought's arising — applied in turn to wholesome, unwholesome, and indeterminate states of mind.

2. Going Through Wholesome States: the Fourfold Moment and the Six Pāramitās 歷諸善

二、歷諸善，即為二：先、分別四運，次、歷眾善。初明四運者，夫心識無形，不可見，約四相分別，謂未念、欲念、念、念已。未念，名心未起；欲念，名心欲起；念，名正緣境住；念已，名緣境謝。若能了達此四，即入一相無相。問：未念未起，已念已謝，此二皆無心，無心則無相，云何可觀？答：未念雖未起，非畢竟無。如人未作作，後便作作，不可以未作作故，便言無人。若定無人，後誰作作？以有未作作人，則將有作作。心亦如是，因未念故，得有欲念；若無未念，何得有欲念？是故未念雖未有，不得畢竟無念也。念已雖滅，亦可觀察。如人作竟，不得言無；若定無人，前誰作作？念已心滅亦復如是，不得言永滅；若永滅者，則是斷見，無因、無果。是故念已雖滅，亦可得觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, going through wholesome states: this is divided into two — first, distinguishing the fourfold moment; next, going through the various wholesome states themselves. First, explaining the fourfold moment: mind and consciousness are formless and cannot be seen, and so are distinguished by way of four marks — not-yet-thought, about-to-think, thinking, and having-thought. Not-yet-thought means the mind has not yet arisen; about-to-think means the mind is about to arise; thinking means it correctly abides, fastened upon its object; having-thought means its attention to that object has already passed away. If one is able to fully penetrate these four, one thereby enters the one mark that is no mark. Question: "Not-yet-thought" has not yet arisen, and "having-thought" has already passed away — both of these are without any mind at all, and if there is no mind, there is no mark; how, then, can they be contemplated? Answer: Though "not-yet-thought" has not yet arisen, it is not, in the end, simply nothing at all. It is like a person who has not yet acted, but will act later — one cannot, simply because they have not yet acted, say there is no person there at all. If there were truly no person, who would act later? Because there is a person who has not yet acted, there is bound to be an act to come. Mind is exactly the same: because of "not-yet-thought," there comes to be "about-to-think"; if there were no "not-yet-thought," how could "about-to-think" ever arise? Hence, though "not-yet-thought" does not yet exist, it cannot be said to be, in the end, simply no thought at all. Though "having-thought" has ceased, it too can be contemplated. It is like a person who has finished acting — one cannot say there is no one there; if there were truly no person, who acted just before? "Having-thought," the mind having ceased, is exactly the same — one cannot say it has been permanently extinguished; were it permanently extinguished, this would be the view of annihilation, with no cause and no effect. Hence, though "having-thought" has ceased, it too can still be contemplated.

問：過去已去，未來未至，現在不住；若離三世，則無別心，觀何等心？答：汝問非也。若過去永滅，畢竟不可知；未來未起，不可知；現在無住，不可知。云何諸聖人知三世心？鬼神尚知自他三世，云何佛法行人起斷滅、龜毛兔角見？當知三世心雖無定實，亦可得知。故偈云：「諸佛之所說，雖空亦不斷，相續亦不常，罪福亦不失。」若起斷滅，如盲對色，於佛法中無正觀眼，空無所獲。行者既知心有四相，隨心所起善惡諸念，以無住著智反照觀察也。次、歷善事。善事眾多，且約六度。若有諸塵，須捨六受；若無財物，須運六作。捨、運共論，有十二事。

Question: The past has already gone, the future has not yet arrived, and the present does not abide — if one departs from the three times, there is no separate mind left at all; what mind, then, is there to contemplate? Answer: Your question is mistaken. If the past were permanently extinguished, it could never, in the end, be known; if the future had not yet arisen, it could not be known; if the present had no abiding, it could not be known. How, then, could the sages know the mind across all three times? Even spirits and ghosts know their own and others' three times — how, then, could a practitioner of the Buddhadharma give rise to the view of annihilation, as absurd as a tortoise's fur or a hare's horn? One should know that, though the mind across the three times has no fixed, real existence, it can nonetheless still be known. Hence a verse says: "What all the buddhas teach: though empty, it is not annihilated; though continuous, it is not permanent; wholesome and unwholesome karma, too, is never lost." If one gives rise to the view of annihilation, it is like a blind person facing color — within the Buddhadharma, one has no eye of correct contemplation, and gains nothing but emptiness. Once the practitioner knows that mind has these four marks, then, whatever wholesome or unwholesome thought arises in the mind, they turn the light of unattached, non-abiding wisdom back upon it in contemplation. Next, going through wholesome matters: wholesome matters are many; here, take the six pāramitās for example. Where there are sense-objects present, one must relinquish the six kinds of receiving; where there is no material wealth, one must exercise the six kinds of action. Taken together, relinquishing and exercising make twelve matters in all.

Reading note: The fourfold moment (未念/欲念/念/念已: not-yet-thought, about-to-think, thinking, having-thought) is this section's basic analytical tool, applied across everything that follows. The two objections raised and answered here matter for what comes next: against the worry that a not-yet-arisen or already-past thought is simply nothing to contemplate, the treatise insists that neither annihilation nor fixed permanence is right — a position it will now apply concretely to the six pāramitās, first across the twelve matters of relinquishing the six kinds of sense-experience and exercising the six kinds of active giving.

First, discussing the eye receiving form: not-yet-seeing, about-to-see, seeing, and having-seen — the mind at each of these four moments cannot be seen, nor can it be said not to be seen. Turning the light of contemplation back upon the very mind that is aware of form, it does not come from outside — were it to come from outside, it would have nothing to do with me; nor does it emerge from within — were it to emerge from within, it would not need to depend on causes and conditions. Since it is neither outside nor inside, nor is it in between, nor does it exist fixedly on its own. One should know: the mind aware of form is, in the end, quiescent and empty. The form contemplated is the equal of emptiness; the one who contemplates form is the equal of a blind person. Down to the mind taking a mental object: not-yet-taking, about-to-take, taking, and having-taken — the mind at each of these four moments cannot be found either. Turning the light of contemplation back upon the very mind that is aware of a mental object, it does not come from outside, nor does it emerge from within — there is no mental object there at all. Where there is no dharma, everything is the equal of emptiness. This is the contemplation of the six kinds of receiving as awareness, and so on. The eye-faculty, the visible sense-object, space, and light — each and every one of these has no seeing of its own, nor any discrimination. The coming together of causes and conditions gives rise to eye-consciousness; eye-consciousness, through causes and conditions, gives rise to mind-consciousness; and once mind-consciousness has arisen, it is able to discriminate. Depending on mind-consciousness, there then comes to be eye-consciousness; eye-consciousness is able to see, and, having seen, gives rise to craving; craving defiles the form seen, and violates the precepts one has undertaken — this is the fourfold moment leading to hell. The mind truly loves the form but conceals and denies it, saying it does not — this is the fourfold moment leading to the ghost-realm. Giving rise to attachment toward the form, and reckoning it as "I" and "mine" — this is the fourfold moment leading to the animal realm. "My" form against "another's" form, "I" superior and "the other" inferior — this is the fourfold moment leading to the asura realm. Another freely gives me a form, neither given nor taken by force, and upon this form the five precepts and the ten wholesome deeds of benevolence, deference, fidelity, trustworthiness, and clarity arise — this is the fourfold moment leading to humans and gods. Contemplating the fourfold-moment mind itself, its mark arising and ceasing, thought after thought never abiding, thought after thought bound to the three kinds of feeling, thought after thought never free, thought after thought belonging to causes and conditions alone — this is the fourfold moment of the two lesser vehicles. Having contemplated the faults of one's own fourfold moment in this way, one contemplates the fourfold moment of others exactly the same way, and thereupon gives rise to compassion and practices the six pāramitās. Why is this? The sense-object of the six kinds of receiving has, by its very nature and mark, always been just this — and for measureless kalpas, stubborn folly has clung to it and been unable to let it go, clinging without ever being able to make it disappear. Now, contemplating the sense-object as no sense-object, there is no receiving of it; contemplating the faculty as no faculty, there is no attachment to oneself; contemplating the person as unfindable, there is no receiver either. All three matters being empty, this is named the pāramitā of giving.

《金剛般若》云：「若住色、聲、香、味、觸、法布施，是名住相布施。如人入闇，則無所見。不住聲、味布施，是無相施。如人有目，日光明照，見種種色。」直言不見相，略猶難解。今不見色有相、無相、亦有無相、非有無相；若處處著相，引之令得出，不起六十二見，乃名「無相檀」，到於彼岸。一切法趣檀，成摩訶衍，是菩薩四運。又觀四運與虛空等，即「常」；不受四運，即「樂」；不為四運起業，即「我」；四運不能染，即「淨」。是佛法四運。如是四運雖空，空中具見種種四運，乃至遍見恒沙佛法，成摩訶衍。是為假名四運。若空，不應具十法界；法界從因緣生，體復非有。非有故空，非空故有，不得空有，雙照空有，三諦宛然，備佛知見，於四運心具足明了。觀聲、香、味、觸、法五受四運心，圓覺三諦不可思議，亦復如是。準前可知，不復煩記。

The Diamond Sūtra says: "If one gives while dwelling on form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental objects, this is called giving while dwelling on marks — like a person entering darkness, seeing nothing at all. Giving without dwelling on sound or taste is giving without marks — like a person with eyes, when sunlight shines brightly, seeing every kind of form clearly." To simply say "not seeing any mark" remains, in brief, still hard to understand. Here, one does not see form as having a mark, as having no mark, as both having and having no mark, or as neither having nor having no mark; if one clings to a mark wherever one turns, one is drawn out of it until none of the sixty-two views arise — only then is it called "markless giving," reaching the far shore. Every dharma whatsoever tends toward giving, accomplishing the Mahāyāna — this is the bodhisattva's own fourfold moment. Again: contemplating the fourfold moment as the equal of empty space is itself "permanence"; not being subject to the fourfold moment is itself "bliss"; not generating karma on account of the fourfold moment is itself "self"; the fourfold moment being unable to defile it is itself "purity" — this is the fourfold moment of the Buddhadharma. Though this fourfold moment is thus empty, within that very emptiness every kind of fourfold moment is seen in full, extending even to seeing the whole of the Buddhadharma, numerous as the sands of the Ganges, accomplishing the Mahāyāna — this is the fourfold moment as provisional name. If it were merely empty, it could not be complete with the ten dharma-realms; the dharma-realm arises from causes and conditions, and its very substance, again, is not existent. Because it is not existent, it is empty; because it is not empty, it is existent; not grasping at either emptiness or existence, illuminating both emptiness and existence at once, the three truths stand out vividly, complete with the Buddha's own knowledge and vision, fully and clearly present within the fourfold-moment mind. Contemplating the fourfold-moment mind of the other five kinds of receiving — sound, smell, taste, touch, and mental objects — the perfect awakening to the three truths, inconceivable, is exactly the same. This may be understood following the pattern above, and need not be recorded in further detail.

次、觀六作行檀者，觀未念行、欲行、行、行已，四運遲速皆不可得，亦不見不可得。反觀覺心，不外來、不內出、不中間、不常自有，無行、無行者，畢竟空寂。而由心運役，故有去來，或為毀戒，或為誑他，或為眷屬，或為勝彼，或為義讓，或為善禪，或為涅槃。或為慈悲，捨六塵、運六作。方便去來，舉足下足皆如幻化，怳𭝏虛忽，亡能亡所，千里之路不謂為遙，數步之地不謂為近。凡有所作不唐其功，不望其報。如此住檀，攝成一切恒沙佛法，具摩訶衍，能到彼岸。又觀一運心十法具足。一不定一，故得為十；十不定十，故得為一。非一、非十，雙照一十，一念心中具足三諦。住、坐、臥、語、默，作作亦復如是，準前可知。故《法華》云：「又見佛子，名衣上服以用布施，以求佛道。」即此義也。

Next, contemplating giving through the six actions: contemplate not-yet-acting, about-to-act, acting, and having-acted — the fourfold moment, whether slow or fast, can never be found, nor can it even be said that it "cannot be found." Turning the light of contemplation back upon the very mind aware of this, it does not come from outside, does not emerge from within, is not in between, and does not exist fixedly on its own — there is no action and no one who acts, only quiescent emptiness in the end. And yet, because the mind is set in motion, there is a coming and going: sometimes for the sake of breaking a precept, sometimes to deceive another, sometimes for one's followers, sometimes to surpass another, sometimes out of righteous deference, sometimes for skillful dhyāna, sometimes for nirvāṇa itself, and sometimes out of compassion, relinquishing the six sense-objects and exercising the six actions. This coming and going by skillful means, every raising and setting-down of the foot, is all like a magical illusion, indistinct and unreal, with no agent and no object lost either — a thousand-mile road is not called far, and a few steps of ground are not called near. Whatever action is undertaken, its merit is never wasted, and no reward is ever hoped for. Abiding in giving in this way, it gathers together the whole of the Buddhadharma, numerous as the sands of the Ganges, complete with the Mahāyāna, able to reach the far shore. Again, contemplating a single arising thought, the ten dharma-realms are already complete within it: the one is not fixed as one, and so can be ten; the ten are not fixed as ten, and so can be one. Neither one nor ten, illuminating both one and ten at once — the three truths are complete within a single thought of mind. Abiding, sitting, lying down, speaking, keeping silent, every action whatsoever, is exactly the same, and may be understood following the pattern above. Hence the Lotus Sūtra says: "And I see the Buddha's disciples give away fine garments and upper robes, seeking the buddha-path." This is exactly that same sense.

Reading note: Giving (檀, dāna) gets by far the fullest treatment of the six pāramitās, worked through twice — once as passive receiving (the eye receiving a visible form, and so on through all six senses) and once as active giving (a step taken, a robe handed over). The ten-dharma-realms ladder that appears mid-passage — the very same act of seeing a form, depending on what response follows, leading toward hell, the ghost realm, the animal realm, the asura realm, or the human and heavenly realms — repeats fascicle 1's own move of grading motivations, but now applied to a single sensory event rather than a whole life's practice. The four virtues of nirvāṇa (常/樂/我/淨: permanence, bliss, self, purity) reappearing here, mapped onto the fourfold moment itself, is characteristic of how thoroughly this treatise refuses to leave any technical term sitting in only one place in its system.

前約十二事共論檀，今約一一事各各論六。行者行時，以大悲眼觀眾生，不得眾生相，眾生於菩薩得無怖畏，是為行中檀。於眾生無所傷損，不得罪福相，是名尸。行時心想不起，亦無動搖，無有住處，陰入界等亦悉不動，是名忍。行時不得舉足下足，心無前思後覺，一切法中無生、住、滅，是名精進。不得身、心、生死、涅槃，一切法中無受念著，不味不亂，是名禪。行時頭等六分如雲如影，夢幻響化，無生滅斷常，陰界入空寂，無縛無脫，是名般若。具如《首楞嚴》中廣說。又行中寂然有定相，若不察之，於定生染，貪著禪味。今觀定心，心尚無心，定在何處？當知此定從顛倒生。如是觀時，不見於空及與不空，即破定相，不生貪著，以方便生，是菩薩解。行者未悟，或計我能觀心，謂是妙慧；著慧自高，是名智障。同彼外道，不得解脫。即反照能觀之心，不見住處，亦無起滅，畢竟無有觀者及非觀者。觀者既無，誰觀諸法？不得觀心者，即離觀想。《大論》云：「念想觀已除，戲論心皆滅，無量眾罪除，清淨心常一，如是尊妙人，則能見般若。」《大集》云：「觀於心心。」即此意也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Above, giving was discussed jointly across all twelve matters; now each single matter is discussed in terms of all six pāramitās in turn. When the practitioner walks, contemplating sentient beings with the eye of great compassion, finding no mark of "sentient being" at all, so that sentient beings, in relation to the bodhisattva, feel no fear — this is giving within walking. Doing no harm at all to sentient beings, finding no mark of offense or merit — this is called moral discipline. When walking, no thought arises in the mind, nor any agitation; there is no place where it abides; the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements, too, are all alike unmoved — this is called patience. When walking, there is no raising or setting-down of the foot as such, the mind has no thought before and no awareness after; within every dharma there is no arising, abiding, or ceasing — this is called diligence. Finding no body, no mind, no birth-and-death, no nirvāṇa, no receiving or clinging within any dharma whatsoever, neither savoring it nor disturbed by it — this is called dhyāna. When walking, the head and the other five parts of the body are like clouds, like shadows, like a dream, an illusion, an echo, a magical transformation — without arising or ceasing, without permanence or annihilation, the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases quiescent and empty, with nothing bound and nothing freed — this is called prajñā. This is explained in full detail in the Śūraṅgama Sūtra. Again, within walking, there is a still, concentrated mark of its own; if one does not examine this closely, one gives rise to defilement even within concentration, craving and clinging to the very flavor of dhyāna. Now, contemplating this concentrated mind — the mind itself has no fixed mind at all, so where could this concentration even be located? One should know that this concentration arises only from an inverted view. Contemplating in this way, one sees neither emptiness nor non-emptiness, and thereby breaks through the mark of concentration itself, giving rise to no craving or clinging, arising instead through skillful means — this is the bodhisattva's own understanding. A practitioner not yet awakened may suppose that they themselves are able to contemplate the mind, taking this to be wondrous wisdom; clinging to this wisdom and growing self-important is called an obstruction of wisdom, no different from a non-Buddhist practitioner, and liberation is not attained. Rather, one turns the light of contemplation back upon the very mind that is doing the contemplating, and finds no place where it abides, nor any arising or ceasing at all — in the end, there is neither a contemplator nor a non-contemplator. Since the contemplator itself does not exist, who is there to contemplate any dharma at all? Not grasping at a "contemplating mind" is itself departing from every notion of contemplation. The Treatise says: "Thought, conception, and contemplation already removed, the mind of idle theorizing entirely extinguished, measureless offenses removed, the pure mind ever one — such a person, honored and wondrous, is then able to see prajñā itself." The Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "Contemplate mind's very own mind" — this is exactly that same sense.

如是行中具三三昧。初觀破一切種種有相，不見內外，即空三昧；次觀能壞空相，名無相三昧；後觀不見作者，即無作三昧。又破三倒、三毒，越三有流，伏四魔怨，成波羅蜜，攝受法界，增長具足一切法門，豈止六度、三三昧而已矣！若於行中具足一切法者，餘十一事亦復如是。次、更歷六塵中競持謹潔，如擎油鉢，一渧不傾。又於六作中威儀肅肅，進退有序，但名持戒。持戒果報升出受樂，非是三昧，不名波羅蜜。若得觀慧，於十二事尸羅自成。謂觀未見色、欲見、見、見已四運心，種種推求，不得所起之心，亦不得能觀之心，不內外，無去來，寂無生滅其一。能如是觀，身口七支淨若虛空，是持不缺、不破、不穿三種律儀戒。破四運諸惡覺觀，即持不雜戒也。不為四運所亂，即持定共戒也。四運心不起，即持道共戒也。分別種種四運無滯，即持無著戒也。分別四運不謬，即持智所讚戒也。知四運攝一切法，即持大乘自在戒也。識四運四德，即持究竟戒其二。心既明淨，雙遮二邊，正入中道，雙照二諦，不思議佛之境界，具足無減其三。

In this way, walking is already complete with the three samādhis: the initial contemplation that breaks through every kind of mark of existence, seeing neither inside nor outside, is the samādhi of emptiness; the next contemplation, able to break even the mark of emptiness itself, is named the samādhi of marklessness; the final contemplation, in which no agent at all is found, is the samādhi of the unconditioned. Moreover, this breaks the three inversions and the three poisons, crosses beyond the three streams of existence, subdues the four demon-enemies, accomplishes the pāramitās, gathers in the dharma-realm, and increases and completes every dharma-gate whatsoever — how could it be limited to merely the six pāramitās and the three samādhis alone! If walking alone is already complete with every dharma, the remaining eleven matters are exactly the same. Next, further, going through the six sense-objects, striving to uphold them with careful purity, like carrying a bowl of oil without spilling a single drop; and, within the six actions, maintaining a solemn and dignified bearing, orderly in every advance and retreat — this alone is merely called "upholding the precepts." The fruit and reward of upholding the precepts in this way is rebirth into pleasure, but this is not samādhi, and cannot be called a pāramitā. If one attains contemplative wisdom, the precepts, across all twelve matters, are accomplished of themselves. Namely: contemplating the fourfold-moment mind of not-yet-seeing form, about-to-see it, seeing it, and having-seen it, seeking it out in every way, one finds neither the mind that arose nor the mind that contemplates it, neither inside nor outside, no coming or going, quiescent and without arising or ceasing. This is the first. Able to contemplate in this way, the seven karmic branches of body and speech become as pure as empty space itself — this is upholding the threefold monastic discipline that is unbroken, unimpaired, and unpunctured. Breaking through every unwholesome discursive thought across the fourfold moment is upholding the precept free of contamination. Not being disturbed by the fourfold moment is upholding the precept conascent with concentration. The fourfold-moment mind not arising at all is upholding the precept conascent with the path. Distinguishing every kind of fourfold moment without any obstruction is upholding the precept of non-attachment. Distinguishing the fourfold moment without error is upholding the precept praised by the wise. Knowing that the fourfold moment gathers in every dharma is upholding the precept of Mahāyāna freedom. Recognizing the fourfold moment's own four virtues is upholding the ultimate precept. This is the second. The mind, once clear and pure, negates both extremes at once, entering correctly into the middle way, illuminating both truths at once — the inconceivable state of the Buddha, complete and lacking nothing. This is the third.

色者、色法、受者不可得，三事皆亡，即「檀」。於色、色者安心不動，名「忍」。色、色者無染、無間，名「毘梨耶」。不為色、色者所亂，名「禪」。色、色者如幻如化，名「般若」。色、色者如虛空，名「空三昧」。不得此空，名「無相三昧」。無能無所，名「無作三昧」。何但三諦、六度、三空？一切恒沙佛法皆例可解。觀色塵既爾，餘五塵亦然，六受、六作亦如是。《法華》云：「又見佛子，威儀具足，以求佛道。」即此義也。次、歷忍善者，還約作、受，皆有違順。順是可意，違不可意。於違不瞋，於順不愛。無見、無見者，無作、無作者，皆如上說。次、歷精進善。舊云：「精進無別體，但篤眾行。」義而推之，應有別體。例無明通入眾使，更別有無明。今且寄誦經，勤策其心，以擬精進；晝夜不虧乃得滑利。而非三昧、慧。今觀氣息，觸七處，和合出聲如響，不內、不外，無能誦、所誦；悉以四運推檢，於塵不起受者，於緣不生作者，煩惱不間誦說，念念流入大涅槃海，是名「精進」云云。

Form, the dharma of form, and the one who receives it, all being unfindable, and all three matters vanishing together — this is "giving." Settling the mind, unmoved, toward form and the one aware of form — this is named "patience." Form and the one aware of form being free of defilement and without interruption — this is named "vīrya." Not being disturbed by form or the one aware of form — this is named "dhyāna." Form and the one aware of form being like an illusion, like a magical transformation — this is named "prajñā." Form and the one aware of form being like empty space — this is named "the samādhi of emptiness." Not grasping even at this emptiness — this is named "the samādhi of marklessness." Neither an agent nor an object — this is named "the samādhi of the unconditioned." Why stop at only the three truths, the six pāramitās, and the three emptinesses? The whole of the Buddhadharma, numerous as the sands of the Ganges, may all likewise be understood following this same pattern. Contemplating the sense-object of form in this way, the other five sense-objects are exactly the same, and the six kinds of receiving and the six kinds of action are exactly the same as well. The Lotus Sūtra says: "And I see the Buddha's disciples, complete in dignified bearing, seeking the buddha-path." This is exactly that same sense. Next, going through the wholesome state of patience: this again is discussed in terms of both acting and receiving, each having what goes against and what accords. What accords is agreeable; what goes against is disagreeable. Toward what goes against, no anger arises; toward what accords, no craving arises. No seeing and no one who sees, no acting and no one who acts — all exactly as explained above. Next, going through the wholesome state of diligence: the old teachers said, "Diligence has no separate substance of its own — it is simply the thoroughness of every other practice." Reasoning it through, however, it should have its own separate substance — just as ignorance, though it pervades and enters every other affliction, is nonetheless itself a distinct thing. Here, taking sūtra recitation as an example, diligently spurring on the mind serves as a model for diligence — reciting without fail day and night, one eventually attains fluency. Yet this alone is not samādhi or wisdom. Now, contemplating the breath itself, touching the seven places, blending together to produce a sound like an echo — neither inside nor outside, with no one reciting and nothing recited — examining all of this thoroughly through the fourfold moment, no receiver arises toward any sense-object, no agent arises toward any condition, affliction never interrupts the recitation, and thought after thought flows into the great ocean of nirvāṇa. This is called "diligence," and so on.

次、歷諸禪。根本、九想、背捨等，但是禪，非波羅蜜。觀入定四運，尚不見心，何處有定？即達禪實相，以禪攝一切法。故《論》第五，解八想竟，明十力、四無所畏、一切法。諸論師不達玄旨，咸謂《論》誤，未應說此。此是論主明八想作摩訶衍相，故廣釋諸法耳云云。次、歷智慧者，《釋論》八種解般若。今且約世智，用觀六受、六作。四運推世智叵得，皆如上說。約餘一切善法亦如是。問：若一法攝一切法者，但用觀即足，何須用止？一度即足，何用五度耶？答：六度宛轉相成，如被甲入陣，不可不密云云。觀如燈，止如密室；浣衣、刈草等云云。又般若為法界，遍攝一切，亦不須餘法；餘法為法界，亦攝一切，亦不須般若。又般若即諸法，諸法即般若，無二無別云云。

Next, going through the various dhyānas: the root dhyānas, the nine contemplations, the eight liberations, and the rest are merely dhyāna, not yet a pāramitā. Contemplating the fourfold moment of entering concentration, one finds not even a mind there at all — where, then, could any concentration be found? This is directly penetrating the true mark of reality within dhyāna, so that dhyāna gathers in every dharma. Hence the Treatise, in its fifth fascicle, having finished explaining the eight contemplations, goes on to explain the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, and every dharma whatsoever. Various commentators, failing to penetrate this profound intent, all claimed the Treatise had erred, and that this should not have been discussed there. But this is the Treatise's own author showing that the eight contemplations already constitute a Mahāyāna mark, and so explaining every dharma at length precisely for that reason, and so on. Next, going through wisdom: the Śāstra explains prajñā in eight different ways. Here, taking only worldly wisdom as an example, apply it to contemplating the six kinds of receiving and the six kinds of action. Examined through the fourfold moment, worldly wisdom, too, cannot be found, exactly as explained above. The same holds for every other wholesome dharma as well. Question: If a single dharma already gathers in every dharma, then contemplation alone should suffice — why is calming needed as well? A single pāramitā should suffice — why are all five others needed? Answer: The six pāramitās turn upon and complete one another in sequence, like donning armor before entering battle — one cannot afford to leave any part unsecured, and so on. Contemplation is like a lamp; calming is like a sealed room; it is like washing a robe, or cutting grass, and so on. Again: prajñā, as the dharma-realm, pervades and gathers in everything, and so needs no other dharma; every other dharma, as the dharma-realm, likewise gathers in everything, and so needs no prajñā either. Again: prajñā is itself every dharma, and every dharma is itself prajñā, with no two of them, no separateness at all, and so on.

Reading note: Having established the pattern fully with giving, the treatise now moves faster through the remaining five pāramitās, showing that every one of them, examined through the same fourfold moment, dissolves the same way into the three samādhis of emptiness, marklessness, and the unconditioned. Two moments are worth flagging: the warning against mistaking concentration itself for wisdom ("clinging to this wisdom and growing self-important is called an obstruction of wisdom, no different from a non-Buddhist practitioner") is a check against a real occupational hazard of contemplative practice, not a merely theoretical point; and the closing question — if one pāramitā already contains all six, why practice all six at all? — gets an answer built entirely from ordinary household similes (armor before battle, a lamp and a sealed room, washing a robe, cutting grass), grounding a highly abstract argument in the most concrete possible terms. This closes "going through wholesome states" in full.

3. Going Through Unwholesome States: "Craving Is Itself the Path" 歷諸惡

Chinese Source

三、以「隨自意」歷諸惡事者，夫善惡無定，如諸蔽為惡，事度為善，人天報盡，還墮三塗，已復是惡。何以故？蔽度俱非動出，體皆是惡。二乘出苦，名之為善。二乘雖善，但能自度，非善人相。《大論》云：「寧起惡癩野干心，不生聲聞、辟支佛意。」當知生死涅槃俱復是惡。六度菩薩慈悲兼濟，此乃稱善。雖能兼濟，如毒器貯食，食則殺人，已復是惡。三乘同斷，此乃稱好。而不見別理，還屬二邊，無明未吐，已復是惡。別教為善，雖見別理，猶帶方便，不能稱理。《大經》云：「自此之前，我等皆名邪見人也。」邪豈非惡？唯圓法名為善。善順實相名為道；背實相名非道。若達諸惡非惡皆是實相，即行於非道通達佛道。若於佛道生著，不消甘露，道成非道。如此論善惡，其義則通。今就「別」明善惡，事度是善，諸蔽為惡。善法用觀，已如上說；就惡明觀，今當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, going through every kind of unwholesome matter by way of "following one's own intent": good and evil, in truth, have no fixed nature. Take, for instance, how the various afflictions are evil while the practices of giving and the rest are good — yet once the reward of rebirth among gods and humans is exhausted, one falls back into the three evil destinies, and this, again, is evil. Why is this? Because neither affliction nor the mundane pāramitās actually move beyond birth-and-death — their very substance is, in either case, evil. The two lesser vehicles' escape from suffering is called good. Though the two lesser vehicles are good in this sense, they are able to deliver only themselves, and so do not have the mark of a truly good person. The Treatise says: "I would rather give rise to the mind of an evil, mangy jackal than give rise to the resolve of a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha." One should know that birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are, in this sense, both alike evil. The bodhisattva of the six pāramitās, with compassion, benefits both self and other together — this, then, deserves to be called good. Yet though able to benefit both, it is like storing food in a poisoned vessel: the food itself will kill anyone who eats it — and this, again, is evil. That the three vehicles alike sever this — this, then, deserves to be called good. Yet without seeing the distinct principle, this still belongs to one of the two extremes, ignorance not yet expelled — and this, again, is evil. The distinct teaching counts as good — though it sees the distinct principle, it still carries expedient means with it, and cannot yet fully correspond to principle. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Before this point, we were all, every one of us, called people of wrong view." Is wrongness not itself evil? Only the perfect teaching's own Dharma may be called truly good. What accords with the true mark of reality is called the path; what turns its back on the true mark of reality is called not-the-path. If one penetrates that every evil, not truly evil, is itself already the true mark of reality, then one walks upon not-the-path and thereby penetrates through to the buddha-path itself. But should one become attached even to the buddha-path, then even ambrosia cannot be digested, and the path itself turns into not-the-path. Discussed in this way, the sense of good and evil runs through everything alike. Here, taking them in their particular sense, the practices of giving and the rest are good, and the various afflictions are evil. How to contemplate the wholesome dharmas has already been explained above; how to contemplate evil will now be explained.

前雖觀善，其蔽不息，煩惱浩然，無時不起。若觀於他，惡亦無量。故修一切世間不可樂想時，則不見好人、無好國土，純諸蔽惡而自纏裹。縱不全有蔽，而偏起不善，或多慳貪，或多犯戒，多瞋、多怠、多嗜酒味。根性易奪，必有過患，其誰無失？出家離世，行猶不備；白衣受欲，非行道人，惡是其分。羅漢殘習，何況凡夫？凡夫若縱惡蔽，摧折俯墜，永無出期，當於惡中而修觀慧。如佛世時，在家之人帶妻挾子，官方俗務，皆能得道。央掘摩羅彌殺彌慈；祇陀末利唯酒唯戒；和須蜜多婬而梵行；提婆達多邪見即正。若諸惡中一向是惡，不得修道者，如此諸人永作凡夫。以惡中有道故，雖行眾蔽，而得成聖，故知惡不妨道。又道不妨惡。須陀洹人婬欲轉盛；畢陵尚慢；身子生瞋，於其無漏有何損益？譬如虛空中「明暗不相除，顯出佛菩提」，即此意也。

Even though wholesome states were contemplated above, one's own afflictions do not thereby cease — affliction surges on vast and unbroken, arising at every moment without exception. If one contemplates others as well, their evils, too, are without measure. Hence, when one cultivates the perception that nothing in the entire world is truly pleasurable, one finds no good person and no good land anywhere — everyone wrapped up purely in every kind of affliction and evil. Even granting that one does not possess every affliction all at once, one still gives rise, in some skewed way, to what is unwholesome — excessive stinginess and greed in some, frequent violation of the precepts in others, much anger, much sloth, much fondness for the taste of wine. Character and disposition are easily overturned, and fault is bound to follow — who, then, is entirely without failing? One who has gone forth from home, leaving the world behind, still has practice yet incomplete; a layperson still receiving the pleasures of the senses is not one truly walking the path, and evil is simply their lot. Even an arhat retains residual habituation — how much more, then, an ordinary person? Should an ordinary person indulge every evil affliction without restraint, they will be broken and cast down, with never any escape in sight — hence, it is precisely within evil itself that contemplative wisdom must be cultivated. It was exactly this way in the Buddha's own time: laypeople with wives and children, engaged in official duties and worldly affairs, were still able to attain the path. Aṅgulimāla, ever more given to killing, was ever more given to compassion; Jetavana and Mallikā were entirely given to wine, and entirely given to the precepts alike; Vasumitrā practiced licentiousness and yet lived the pure conduct of brahmacarya; Devadatta's own wrong views were themselves precisely correct. If it were true that, within every evil, evil alone is present, so that the path could never be cultivated there, then every one of these people would have remained an ordinary being forever. Because there is a path present even within evil, though they carried out every kind of affliction, they nonetheless attained sagehood — hence it is known that evil does not obstruct the path. And likewise, the path does not obstruct evil. A stream-enterer's own desire may grow even stronger; Piṇḍola still had pride; Śāriputra still gave rise to anger — what loss or gain could this possibly bring to their own freedom from contamination? It is like how, within empty space, "brightness and darkness do not cancel each other out, and buddha-bodhi is thereby made manifest" — this is exactly that same sense.

若人性多貪欲，穢濁熾盛，雖對治折伏，彌更增劇，但恣趣向。何以故？蔽若不起，不得修觀。譬如綸釣，魚強繩弱不可爭牽，但令鉤餌入口，隨其遠近任縱沈浮，不久收獲。於蔽修觀亦復如是。蔽即惡魚；觀即鉤餌。若無魚者，鉤餌無用。但使有魚，多、大唯佳，皆以鉤餌隨之不捨，此蔽不久堪任乘御。云何為觀？若貪欲起，諦觀貪欲有四種相：未貪欲、欲貪欲、正貪欲、貪欲已。為當未貪欲滅，欲貪欲生？為當未貪欲不滅，欲貪欲生？亦滅亦不滅，欲貪欲生？非滅非不滅，欲貪欲生？若未滅欲生，為即為離？即滅而生，生滅相違；若離而生，生則無因。未貪不滅而欲生者，為即為離？若即，即二生相並，生則無窮；若離，生亦無因。若亦滅亦不滅而欲生者，若從滅生，不須亦不滅；若從不滅生，不須亦滅。不定之因，那生定果？若其體一，其性相違；若其體異，本不相關。若非滅非不滅而欲貪生，雙非之處為有為無？若雙非是有，何謂雙非？若雙非是無，無那能生？如是四句不見欲貪欲生，還轉四句不見未貪欲滅。欲貪欲，生、不生、亦生亦不生、非生非不生，亦如上說。觀貪欲蔽畢竟空寂，雙照分明，皆如上說。是名「鉤餌」。

If a person's own nature is given heavily to craving, its filth and turbidity blazing fiercely, and yet, though treated with antidotes and subdued by force, it only grows more severe still — then simply let it run its own course. Why is this? If the affliction does not arise at all, there is nothing for contemplation to work on. It is like fishing with a line: when the fish is strong and the line weak, one cannot fight and haul it in by force — one need only let the hook and bait enter its mouth, and, following it near or far, letting it sink or rise freely, before long it will be caught. Cultivating contemplation upon affliction is exactly the same. The affliction is the fierce fish; contemplation is the hook and bait. Were there no fish at all, the hook and bait would be useless. But given that there is a fish, the larger and more numerous the better — following it constantly with the hook and bait, never letting go, this affliction will, before long, be entirely brought under rein. How, then, does one contemplate it? Should craving arise, contemplate closely that craving has four such marks: not-yet-craving, about-to-crave, actively craving, and having-craved. Does about-to-crave arise from not-yet-craving having ceased, or from not-yet-craving not having ceased, or from both having ceased and not having ceased, or from neither having ceased nor not having ceased? If about-to-crave arises from cessation, is this arising identical with that cessation, or separate from it? If identical, then arising and ceasing contradict each other; if separate, then the arising has no cause at all. If not-yet-craving has not ceased, and about-to-crave nonetheless arises, is this identical or separate? If identical, then two arisings stand side by side, and arising would be endless; if separate, then again there is no cause for it. If it arises from both having ceased and not having ceased, then, if it arises from the ceased aspect, the not-ceased aspect is unnecessary; if it arises from the not-ceased aspect, the ceased aspect is unnecessary — how could an indeterminate cause ever produce a determinate effect? If the two are one and the same in substance, their natures contradict each other; if they are different in substance, they have nothing to do with each other in the first place. If about-to-crave arises from neither having ceased nor not having ceased, is this place of double-negation itself existent or non-existent? If this double-negation is existent, in what sense is it "double-negation" at all? If this double-negation is non-existent, how could non-existence give rise to anything? In none of these four alternatives is the arising of about-to-crave to be found; turning the same four alternatives around, the cessation of not-yet-craving is not to be found either. Whether craving arises, does not arise, both arises and does not arise, or neither arises nor does not arise, may likewise be examined exactly as above. Contemplating the affliction of craving as, in the end, quiescent and empty, while illuminating both aspects clearly at once, all exactly as explained above — this is what is called "the hook and bait."

若蔽恒起，此觀恒照；亦不見起，亦不見照；而起，而照其一。又觀此蔽因何塵起？色耶？餘耶？因何作起？行耶？餘耶？若因於色，為未見、欲見、見、見已？若因於行，未行、欲行、行、行已？為何事起？為毀戒耶？為眷屬耶？為虛誑耶？為嫉妬耶？為仁讓耶？為善禪耶？為涅槃耶？為四德耶？為六度？為三三昧耶？為恒沙佛法耶其二？如是觀時，於塵無受者，於緣無作者；而於塵、受、根、緣雙照分明。幻化與空及以法性不相妨礙。所以者何？若蔽礙法性，法性應破壞；若法性礙蔽，蔽應不得起。當知蔽即法性，蔽起即法性起，蔽息即法性息。《無行經》云：「貪欲即是道，恚癡亦如是，如是三法中，具一切佛法。若人離貪欲，而更求菩提，譬如天與地，貪欲即菩提。」《淨名》云：「行於非道，通達佛道。」「一切眾生即菩提相，不可復得；即涅槃相，不可復滅。」「為增上慢，說離婬怒癡，名為解脫；無增上慢者，說婬怒癡性，即是解脫。」「一切塵勞是如來種。」山海色味無二無別。即觀諸惡不可思議理也其三。常修觀慧與蔽理相應，譬如形影，是名「觀行位」。能於一切惡法、世間產業皆與實觀不相違背，是「相似位」。進入銅輪，破蔽根本；本謂無明，本傾枝折，顯出佛性，是「分證真實位」。乃至諸佛盡蔽源底，名「究竟位」。於貪蔽中竪具六即、橫具諸度，一切法例如上云云。

If the affliction constantly arises, this contemplation constantly illuminates it; one sees neither the arising nor the illuminating, and yet there is arising, and there is illuminating. This is the first. Again, contemplate through what sense-object this affliction arises — form? or something else? Through what action does it arise — walking? or something else? If through form, is it not-yet-seeing, about-to-see, seeing, or having-seen? If through action, is it not-yet-acting, about-to-act, acting, or having-acted? For what matter does it arise — for breaking a precept? for one's followers? for deceit? for jealousy? for benevolence and deference? for skillful dhyāna? for nirvāṇa? for the four virtues? for the six pāramitās? for the three samādhis? for the whole of the Buddhadharma, numerous as the sands of the Ganges? This is the second. Contemplating in this way, there is no receiver toward any sense-object, no agent toward any condition; and yet sense-object, receiving, faculty, and condition are all illuminated clearly at once. Magical illusion, emptiness, and the Dharma-nature do not obstruct one another at all. Why is this? If affliction obstructed the Dharma-nature, the Dharma-nature would have to be destroyed by it; if the Dharma-nature obstructed affliction, affliction should never be able to arise at all. One should know that affliction is itself already the Dharma-nature: affliction arising is itself the Dharma-nature arising, and affliction ceasing is itself the Dharma-nature ceasing. The Sūtra on the Practice of Non-Action says: "Craving is itself the path; anger and delusion are exactly the same; within these three dharmas, the whole of the Buddhadharma is complete. Should a person leave craving behind and seek bodhi elsewhere, this is like heaven separated from earth — craving is itself bodhi." The Vimalakīrti Sūtra says: "Walking upon not-the-path, one penetrates through to the buddha-path." "Every sentient being is itself already the mark of bodhi, and cannot be attained all over again; is itself already the mark of nirvāṇa, and cannot be extinguished all over again." "For those of overweening pride, leaving behind lust, anger, and delusion is taught as liberation; for those without overweening pride, the very nature of lust, anger, and delusion is itself taught as liberation." "Every worldly affliction is itself the very seed of the Tathāgata." Mountain and sea, form and flavor, are neither two nor separate. This is contemplating every evil as the inconceivable principle itself. This is the third. Constantly cultivating this contemplative wisdom, corresponding to the principle within affliction, like a form and its shadow, is named "the stage of contemplative practice." Being able, in every unwholesome dharma and every worldly occupation, to remain never at odds with true contemplation, is "the stage of resemblance." Advancing into the copper-wheel stage, breaking through the very root of affliction — its root being ignorance — the root toppled and its branches broken, the buddha-nature manifested, is "the stage of partially realized true reality." And on up to the buddhas, who exhaust affliction to its very source and depth, is named "the ultimate stage." Within the affliction of craving, the six identities are already complete vertically, and every pāramitā complete horizontally — every dharma follows this same pattern, as explained above, and so on.

Reading note: This is the most provocative passage in the fascicle, and Zhiyi knows it — hence the very careful framing before and after. The opening move is crucial and easy to miss on a fast read: "good and evil have no fixed nature" is not a claim that ethical distinctions don't matter, but the reverse — every level up to and including the bodhisattva's own six pāramitās is shown to still count as "evil" relative to something deeper, right up until only the perfect teaching remains. Only after this ladder is fully built does the treatise dare quote the Sūtra on the Practice of Non-Action's notorious line "craving is itself the path." The named exemplars (Aṅgulimāla the mass murderer turned compassionate, Vasumitrā the courtesan who was secretly a bodhisattva, Devadatta the Buddha's own schismatic cousin) are not endorsements of ordinary vice; they are extreme test cases for a claim about how deeply the true mark of reality penetrates. The very next section exists because Zhiyi anticipated exactly how this teaching would be twisted by the unscrupulous or unready — and that warning follows immediately.

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, contemplating neither good nor evil, that is, the indeterminate, drowsy, and dulled kind of dharma. The reason this too must be contemplated is that there are people whose very disposition neither does good nor does evil — such a person has no causal condition at all for world-transcending practice through following one's own intent; what, then, is to be done with such a person? The Treatise says: "One who has prajñāpāramitā even within the indeterminate is thereby able to cultivate this contemplation." Contemplating this indeterminate state — is it different from good and evil, or the same? If the same, it would not be indeterminate at all. If different: does the indeterminate arise from the determinate having ceased? From the determinate not having ceased? From the determinate having both ceased and not ceased? From the determinate neither having ceased nor not having ceased? Even the determinate itself, sought for, cannot be found — how much less, then, could sameness or difference between the determinate and the indeterminate be found? Because they are not the same, they are not joined together; because they are not different, they are not scattered apart. Because they are not joined, nothing arises from them; because they are not scattered, nothing ceases either. Again, going through the twelve matters: in which of them does the indeterminate arise? For whose sake does the indeterminate arise? Who is it that is indeterminate? Contemplating in this way, it takes on the mark of empty space itself. Again, this one dharma of the indeterminate gives rise to the ten dharma-realms and to every dharma whatsoever. Again, the indeterminate is itself already the Dharma-nature; the Dharma-nature's own constant quiescence is the meaning of "calming"; that same quiescence, constantly illuminating, is the meaning of "contemplation." Within the indeterminate, though not-the-path, one penetrates through to the buddha-path; the indeterminate, as the dharma-realm, gathers in every dharma horizontally and every one of the six stages vertically, lofty and vast, complete and lacking nothing, exactly as explained above. Furthermore: to explain "following one's own intent" only in terms of the final, wholesome state alone is the gradual sense of it. To explain "following one's own intent" through good and evil together at once is the sudden sense of it. To explain "following one's own intent" through wholesome states folded and layered one upon another is the indeterminate sense of it, and so on.

復次，四種三昧方法各異，理觀則同。但三行方法，多發助道法門，又動障道；隨自意既少方法，少發此事。若但解方法，所發助道，事相不能通達；若解理觀，事無不通。又不得理觀意，事相助道亦不成；得理觀意，事相三昧任運自成。若事相行道，入道場得用心，出則不能；隨自意則無間也。方法局三；理觀通四云云。問：上三三昧皆有勸修，此何獨無？答：六蔽非道即解脫道。鈍根障重者，聞已沈沒，若更勸修，失旨逾甚。淮河之北有行大乘空人，無禁捉蛇者，今當說之。其先師於善法作觀，經久不徹；放心向惡法作觀，獲少定心、薄生空解，不識根緣、不達佛意，純將此法一向教他。教他既久，或逢一兩得益者，如蟲食木偶得成字，便以為證，謂是事實，餘為妄語。笑持戒修善者，謂言非道。純教諸人遍造眾惡。盲無眼者不別是非，神根又鈍，煩惱復重，聞其所說，順其欲情，皆信伏隨從。放捨禁戒，無非不造，罪積山岳。遂令百姓忽之如草，國王大臣因滅佛法，毒氣深入于今未改。

Furthermore: though the methods of the four kinds of samādhi each differ from one another, the contemplation of principle within them is the same throughout. It is only that the three other practices' own methods often bring forth dharma-gates that assist the path, while also stirring up obstructions to the path; "following one's own intent" has, by comparison, few external methods, and so brings forth few such matters. If one understands only the method, then whatever assists the path in the phenomenal sense cannot be fully penetrated; if one understands the contemplation of principle, then there is nothing in the phenomenal sense that is not penetrated. Moreover, without grasping the sense of contemplating principle, the phenomenal assistance to the path will not succeed either; grasping the sense of contemplating principle, the phenomenal samādhi is accomplished of itself, spontaneously. If one practices the path only in its phenomenal form, one can apply the mind while within the practice-place, but not once outside it; "following one's own intent," by contrast, knows no such interruption. The method is confined to three of the four; the contemplation of principle runs through all four alike, and so on. Question: Each of the three samādhis above comes with its own encouragement to cultivate it — why does this one alone lack any such encouragement? Answer: The six afflictions, as not-the-path, are themselves already the path of liberation. Those of dull faculties and heavy obstruction, on merely hearing this, sink into confusion — and were encouragement added on top of that, the true intent would be lost all the more severely. North of the Huai River there lived practitioners of a supposed Mahāyāna emptiness who handled snakes without any restraint — let this now be told. Their founding teacher had, for a long time, practiced contemplation upon wholesome dharmas without ever penetrating through; releasing the mind instead toward contemplation upon unwholesome dharmas, he gained a slight measure of concentrated mind and a thin taste of understanding emptiness, yet, without recognizing capacity or condition, and without penetrating the Buddha's own actual intent, he took this one method alone and taught it to everyone without exception. Having taught it this way for a long time, he happened upon one or two people who genuinely benefited — like an insect gnawing at wood that happens to trace the shape of a character — and thereupon took this as proof, declaring it the real truth, and dismissing everything else as false speech. He mocked those who uphold the precepts and cultivate wholesome practice, calling it not-the-path, and taught everyone, purely and simply, to commit every kind of evil without restraint. Blind, sightless people, unable to distinguish right from wrong, their own spiritual faculties dull, their afflictions heavy besides, heard what he taught, found it accorded with their own desires, and all believed and submitted, following along. They abandoned every precept and restraint, leaving nothing undone, until their offenses piled up like mountains. This eventually caused the common people to regard the Dharma as worthless as weeds, and the king and his ministers, because of this, destroyed the Buddhadharma itself — a poison whose effect runs deep, and has not been undone even to this day.

《史記》云：「周末有被髮袒身不依禮度者，遂犬戎侵國，不絕如綖，周姬漸盡。」又阮籍逸才，蓬頭散帶，後公卿子孫皆斆之。奴狗相辱者，方達自然；撙節兢持者，呼為田舍，是為司馬氏滅相。宇文邕毀廢，亦由元嵩魔業。此乃佛法滅之妖怪，亦是時代妖怪，何關隨自意意？何以故？如此愚人，心無慧解，信其本師，又慕前達，決謂是道。又順情為易，恣心取樂而不改迷。譬如西施本有心病，多喜嚬呻，百媚皆轉，更益美麗。隣女本醜而斆其嚬呻，可憎彌劇。貧者遠徙，富者杜門，穴者深潛，飛者高逝。彼諸人等亦復似是，狂狗逐雷，造地獄業，悲哉可傷！既嗜欲樂，不能自止，猶如蒼蠅為唾所粘。浪行之過，其事略爾。

The Records of the Grand Historian says: "At the end of the Zhou, there were those with disheveled hair and bared bodies who no longer followed ritual propriety — and so the Rong barbarians invaded the state, its fortunes hanging on like a single thread, and the house of Zhou gradually came to its end." Again, Ruan Ji, a man of untethered talent, went about with tousled hair and loosened robes, and afterward the sons and grandsons of ministers and nobles all imitated him. Those who insulted one another like dogs and slaves were then said to have attained the natural and spontaneous; those who kept themselves restrained and disciplined were mocked as rustic and provincial — and this was the very mark of the House of Sima's own downfall. Yuwen Yong's destruction of the Dharma, too, came about through Wei Yuansong's own demonic karma. All of this is the specter that destroys the Buddhadharma, and likewise the specter of the age itself — but what does any of it have to do with the true intent of "following one's own intent"? Why is this? Such foolish people, their minds without wisdom or understanding, trust in their own founding teacher, and, admiring those who came before, decide firmly that this is the path. Moreover, following one's own desires feels easy, and indulging the mind in pleasure never corrects the delusion. It is like Xi Shi, who originally suffered from a heart ailment and often winced and sighed — every one of her hundred captivating expressions only made her more beautiful still. A plain-looking neighbor girl, imitating her wincing and sighing, only became all the more unbearable to look at. The poor moved far away, the rich shut their doors, burrowing creatures sank deep underground, and flying creatures soared far off. These people are exactly the same: like a mad dog chasing after thunder, they build up the karma of hell — how sorrowful, how pitiable! Having grown addicted to sensual pleasure, they cannot restrain themselves, just like a fly stuck fast to spit. This, in brief, is the fault of reckless, undisciplined practice.

其師過者，不達根性，不解佛意。佛說「貪欲即是道」者，佛見機宜，知一種眾生底下薄福，決不能於善中修道。若任其罪，流轉無已；令於貪欲修習止觀，極不得止，故作此說。譬如父母見子得病，不宜餘藥，須黃龍湯，鑿齒瀉之，服已病愈。佛亦如是，說當其機。快馬見鞭影，即到正路。貪欲即是道，佛意如此。若有眾生不宜於惡修止觀者，佛說諸善名之為道。佛具二說，汝今云何呵善就惡？若其然者，汝則勝佛。公於佛前灼然違反。復次，時節難起，王事所拘，不得修善，令於惡中而習止觀。汝今無難、無拘，何意純用乳藥毒他慧命？故《阿含》中放牛人善知好濟，令牛群安隱。若好濟有難，急不獲已，當從惡濟。惡濟多難，百不全一。汝今無事，幸於好濟，善道驅牛，何為惡道自他沈沒？破壞佛法、損失威光、誤累眾生，大惡知識不得佛意，其過如是。

As for the teacher's own fault: he failed to penetrate his students' actual capacity and disposition, and failed to understand the Buddha's own actual intent. When the Buddha teaches that "craving is itself the path," the Buddha, seeing what was fitting to the occasion, knew of one kind of sentient being, of the lowest capacity and thinnest merit, who could, in the end, never cultivate the path through wholesome means at all. Left to their own karma, they would drift on and on without end; hence, only because there was truly no other way to make them stop, he had them cultivate calming-and-contemplation within craving itself, and taught it in this way for that reason alone. It is like parents who, seeing their child fall ill, find no other medicine will serve, and must resort to a decoction of realgar and dragon's blood, breaking the child's teeth open to pour it in — and once taken, the illness is cured. The Buddha is exactly the same: he taught according to the occasion. A swift horse, seeing merely the shadow of the whip, arrives at once on the correct road. "Craving is itself the path" — this is the Buddha's own actual intent. Where there are sentient beings for whom it is not fitting to cultivate calming-and-contemplation through evil, the Buddha teaches the various wholesome practices, and calls these the path instead. The Buddha provided both teachings — how, then, can you now condemn the good and embrace evil instead? Were this truly right, you would then have to be greater than the Buddha himself — a flagrant contradiction, right in the Buddha's own presence. Furthermore: in times of hardship and difficulty, constrained by the affairs of kings, unable to cultivate the good, one might have to practice calming-and-contemplation within evil itself instead. But you, now, face no such hardship, no such constraint — why, then, do you take what should be nourishing milk and turn it into poison, destroying others' own wisdom-life? The Āgama tells of a cowherd who knew well the good fording-place, and so kept his herd of cattle safe. Should the good fording-place become dangerous, then, only when there was truly no other choice, one would have to cross by the dangerous ford instead. The dangerous ford holds many perils, and not one in a hundred crosses it safely. You, now, facing no such circumstance, are fortunate enough to have the good fording-place available — why, then, drive the cattle by the evil path, sinking both yourself and others together? Destroying the Buddhadharma, diminishing its own majestic light, leading multitudes of sentient beings astray — this is the fault of the great evil counselor who fails to grasp the Buddha's own actual intent.

復次，夷險兩路皆有能通，為難從險。善惡俱通，審機入蔽。汝棄善專惡，能通達非道，何不蹈躡水火、穿逾山壁？世間險路尚不能通，何況行惡而會正道，豈可得乎？又不能知根緣，直是一人即時樂善，即時樂惡，好樂不定，何況無量人邪？而純以貪欲化他？淨名云：「我念聲聞，不觀人根，不應說法。」二乘不觀，尚自差機；況汝盲瞑，無目師心者乎？自是違經，不當機理，何其愚惑，頓至於此！若見有人不識機宜，行說此者則戒海死屍；宜依律擯治，無令毒樹生長者宅云云。復次，檢其惡行，事即偏、邪。汝謂貪欲即是道，陵一切女而不能瞋恚，即是道。害一切男，唯愛細滑觸是道，畏於打拍苦澁觸，則無有道。行一不行一，一有道一無道，癡闇如漆，偏行污損。譬如死屍穢好花園云云。難其偏行如前，或將水火刀杖向之，其即默然。或答云：「而汝不見，我常能入。」此乃違心，無慚、愧語，亦不得六即之意。所以須說此者，上三行法，勤策事難，宜須勸修。隨自意和光入惡，一往則易，宜須誡忌，如服大黃湯，應備白飲而補止之云云。

Furthermore: both the level road and the perilous one can lead through — one takes the perilous road only when facing genuine difficulty. Good and evil can both lead through, but one enters affliction only after carefully discerning the occasion. You abandon the good and devote yourself exclusively to evil, claiming to penetrate through by not-the-path — why, then, do you not also tread through water and fire, or pass straight through mountains and cliffs? If even the perilous roads of this ordinary world cannot be crossed by you in this way, how much less could practicing evil ever meet with the correct path — how could this possibly be attained? Moreover, unable even to know a single person's own capacity and condition — even one single person delights in the good at one moment and delights in evil the next, their likes and dislikes never fixed — how much less, then, could this be known for countless different people, and yet you teach them all, purely and simply, through craving alone? Vimalakīrti said: "I recall that the śrāvakas, without examining a person's own capacity, ought not to teach the Dharma to them." Even the two lesser vehicles, without examining capacity, already err in matching the occasion — how much more, then, you, blind and benighted, a teacher with no eyes of your own, guided only by your own arbitrary mind? This is, of itself, already a violation of the sūtras, failing to match capacity to principle — how has such foolish delusion come to this?! Should one see a person who fails to recognize what is fitting to the occasion, and yet acts and teaches in this way, they are a corpse floating in the ocean of the precepts; they should be expelled and dealt with according to the Vinaya, lest a poisonous tree be allowed to take root and grow within the elder's own household, and so on. Furthermore, examining this evil practice closely, its own conduct is itself already partial and deviant. You claim that craving is itself the path — yet can you violate every woman without ever giving rise to anger, and call this the path? Can you harm every man, loving only smooth and pleasant sensation as the path, while fearing beatings and bitter, harsh sensation, with no path found there at all? Practicing one and not the other, with a path in one case and no path in the other, is delusion as thick as pitch, a partial and defiling practice — like a corpse befouling a beautiful garden, and so on. Challenged on this partiality as above, some fall silent; some answer, "But you fail to see that I can always enter it regardless" — but this itself already contradicts their own mind, a statement free of any shame or conscience, and one that has failed to grasp the sense of the six identities as well. The reason this whole matter must be discussed here is this: the three practices explained above are demanding and difficult to carry out diligently, and so encouragement to cultivate them is genuinely needed; "following one's own intent," by contrast, blending its own light with evil to enter it, is, at first approach, easy — and so what is genuinely needed here is caution and restraint instead, like taking a purgative decoction of rhubarb, which must be followed with a bland gruel to settle and restore the body afterward, and so on.

Reading note: This long digression is Zhiyi's own answer to why this fourth samādhi comes with a warning instead of praise — and it is worth reading as a genuine historical document, not just doctrine. The Huai River story, naming a real antinomian sect that used "craving is itself the path" to justify licentiousness and contributed, in Zhiyi's account, to actual state persecution of Buddhism (the reference is almost certainly to Emperor Wu of Northern Zhou's suppression of Buddhism, here blamed partly on the monk-turned-critic Wei Yuansong), is treated as continuous with the same social pattern behind the fall of the Zhou dynasty and the license of Ruan Ji's circle at the end of the Wei-Jin period — three separate historical collapses, in Zhiyi's telling, from the identical root cause of mistaking indulgence for freedom. The argument against the false teacher is precise, not merely indignant: he failed to match the teaching to the hearer's actual capacity, failed to test whether his own "path" held up symmetrically (violating without anger, but not enduring the reverse), and offered no exit strategy — "a purgative decoction of rhubarb... followed with a bland gruel," meaning even a legitimately radical teaching still needs careful aftercare, not indefinite indulgence.

問：中道正觀以一其心，行用即足，何須紛紜四種三昧、歷諸善惡、經十二事？水濁珠昏，風多浪鼓，何益於澄靜耶？答：譬如貧窮人得少便為足，更不願好者。若一種觀心，心若種種，當奈之何？此則自行為失。若用化他，他之根性舛互不同；一人煩惱已自無量，何況多人？譬如藥師集一切藥擬一切病。一種病人須一種藥治一種病，而怪藥師多藥。汝問似是。煩惱心病無量無邊，如為一人，眾多亦然。云何一人？若人欲聞四種三昧，聞之歡喜，須遍為說，是為世界。以聞四種，次第修行能生善法，即具說四，是各各為人。或宜常坐中治其諸惡，乃至隨自意中治其諸惡，是名對治。是人具須四法，豁然得悟，是第一義。秖為一人尚須四說，云何不用耶？若為多人者，一人樂常坐，三非所欲；一人欲常行，三非所樂。遍赴眾人之欲，即世界悉檀也。餘三悉檀亦如是。又約一種三昧亦具四悉檀意。若樂行即行，樂坐即坐。行時若善根開發入諸法門，是時應行；若坐時心地清涼喜悅安快，是時應坐。若坐時沈昏，則抖擻應行；行時散動疲困，是則應坐。若行時怳焉虛寂，是則應行；若坐時湛然明利，是時應坐。餘三例爾云云。

Question: If correct contemplation of the middle way already unifies the mind as one, practicing with it alone should suffice — why is such a bewildering array of four kinds of samādhi needed, going through every wholesome and unwholesome state, across twelve separate matters? When water is muddied and a jewel dimmed, when the wind blows hard and the waves are churned up — how could any of this benefit stillness and clarity? Answer: It is like a poor person, content with the little they have, who no longer wishes for anything better. But if the mind that contemplates is only ever one kind, while the mind itself is actually manifold — what is to be done about that? To leave it this way would already be a failure in one's own practice. And if one uses this to transform others, the capacities and dispositions of others are mismatched and varied — a single person's own afflictions are already without measure, how much more, then, many people together? It is like a physician who gathers together every kind of medicine to treat every kind of illness — a single kind of patient needs a single kind of medicine to treat a single kind of illness, and yet one finds it strange that the physician keeps so many medicines on hand. Your question seems reasonable, but is not. The afflictions of the mind, as illnesses, are measureless and boundless — even for a single person, let alone for many people together. How is this so even for a single person? If a person wishes to hear of the four kinds of samādhi, and rejoices on hearing of them, all four must be explained to them in full — this is the worldly siddhānta. Having heard of all four, and practicing them in sequence, able to give rise to wholesome dharmas — explaining all four together in this way is the siddhānta for the individual. Whether it is fitting to treat their various evils within constant sitting, or within following one's own intent, and so on — this is called the siddhānta of the antidote. Such a person, needing all four methods together, suddenly attains awakening — this is the siddhānta of ultimate meaning. If even a single person still needs all four explained, how could they possibly be dispensed with? And if teaching many people at once: one person delights in constant sitting, and finds no desire for the other three; another wishes for constant walking, and finds no delight in the other three. Meeting the desires of every person in the assembly is itself the worldly siddhānta; the other three siddhāntas apply in exactly the same way. Moreover, even within a single one of the four samādhis, all four siddhāntas are already present: if one delights in walking, walk; if one delights in sitting, sit. If, while walking, wholesome roots open and unfold, entering various dharma-gates, this is the moment to walk; if, while sitting, the very ground of the mind grows cool, joyful, and at ease, this is the moment to sit. If, while sitting, one grows sunk in dullness, one should rouse oneself and walk instead; if, while walking, one becomes scattered, agitated, and exhausted, one should then sit. If, while walking, one feels vividly open and empty, this is the moment to walk; if, while sitting, one feels lucid, clear, and sharp, this is the moment to sit. The other three siddhāntas follow this same pattern, and so on.

問：善扶理，可修止觀；惡乖理，云何修止觀？答：《大論》明根，遮有四：一、根利無遮，二、根利有遮，三、根鈍無遮，四、根鈍有遮。初句上品。佛世之時身子等是其人也。行人於善法中修止觀者，以勤修善法，未來無遮，常習止觀令其根利。若過去具此二義，今生薄修即得相應，從觀行位入相似、真實。今生不得入者，昔無二義。今約善修，令未來疾入。次句得道根利而罪積障重。佛世之時闍王、央掘示其人也。逆罪遮重，應入地獄；見佛聞法，豁爾成聖，以根利故，遮不能障。今時行人於惡法中修止觀者，即此意也。以起惡故，未來有遮；修止觀故，後世根利。若遇知識鞭入正道，云何而言惡法乖理，不肯修止觀耶？次、根鈍無遮者，佛世之時周利槃特示是其人。雖三業無過，根性極鈍，九十日誦鳩摩羅偈：「智者身口意，不造於諸惡，繫念常現前，不樂著諸欲，亦不受世間，無益之苦行。」今時雖持戒行善，不學止觀，未來無遮而悟道甚難。後句者，即一切行惡之人，又不修止觀者是也。不修止觀，故不得道；根鈍，千遍為說，兀然不解。多造罪惡，遮障萬端，如癩人身痺，針刺徹骨，不知不覺；但以諸惡而自纏裹。以是義故，善雖扶理，道由止觀；惡雖乖理，根利破遮。唯道是尊，豈可為惡而廢止觀？《大經》云：「於戒緩者不名為緩，於乘緩者乃名為緩。」應具明緩、急四句，合上根遮義也云云。又經云：「寧作提婆達多，不作欝頭藍弗。」即其義也。應勤聽思修，初無休息，如醉婆羅門剃頭、戲女披袈裟云云。

Question: The good supports principle, and so calming-and-contemplation may be cultivated through it; evil goes against principle — how, then, could calming-and-contemplation be cultivated through it? Answer: The Treatise explains capacity and hindrance as fourfold: first, sharp capacity without hindrance; second, sharp capacity with hindrance; third, dull capacity without hindrance; fourth, dull capacity with hindrance. The first is the highest grade — Śāriputra and others in the Buddha's own time were people of this kind. A practitioner who cultivates calming-and-contemplation within wholesome dharmas, through diligently cultivating the good, incurs no hindrance in the future, and, constantly practicing calming-and-contemplation, makes their own capacity sharper still. If, in past lives, one was already complete in these two qualities, then, in this present life, even slight cultivation brings correspondence at once, moving from the stage of contemplative practice into the stages of resemblance and true reality. Those in this present life who fail to enter these stages simply lacked these two qualities in the past. Cultivating the good now causes swift entry in the future. The second case is sharp capacity for attaining the path, yet with heavy, accumulated karmic hindrance — King Ajātaśatru and Aṅgulimāla, in the Buddha's own time, were people of this kind. Though their heinous crimes carried heavy hindrance, deserving rebirth in hell, on seeing the Buddha and hearing the Dharma they suddenly became sages — because their capacity was sharp, the hindrance could not obstruct them. A practitioner of the present day who cultivates calming-and-contemplation within unwholesome dharmas is acting on exactly this same principle: because evil is generated, hindrance arises for the future; yet because calming-and-contemplation is cultivated, one's capacity in a later life is sharpened. Should such a person meet a spiritual friend who drives them onward into the correct path, how could one still say that unwholesome dharmas go against principle, and refuse to cultivate calming-and-contemplation through them? Next, dull capacity without hindrance: Cūḍapanthaka, in the Buddha's own time, was a person of this kind — though his three karmic actions carried no fault, his own disposition was extremely dull, and he spent ninety days simply reciting the verse of Kumāra: "The wise, in body, speech, and mind, commit none of the evils; mindfulness constantly present before them, they take no delight in clinging to the desires, nor do they undertake the useless austerities of the world." A person of the present day who, though upholding the precepts and practicing the good, does not study calming-and-contemplation, incurs no hindrance for the future, but finds awakening to the path exceedingly difficult all the same. The last case is every person who practices evil, and who, moreover, does not cultivate calming-and-contemplation at all. Not cultivating calming-and-contemplation, they do not attain the path; their capacity being dull, though taught a thousand times over, they remain stubbornly uncomprehending. Committing much evil, their hindrances arise in ten thousand forms, like a leper whose body has grown numb — a needle pierces through to the bone, and they neither know nor feel it — wrapped up purely in their own manifold evils alone. For this reason: though the good supports principle, the path comes by way of calming-and-contemplation; though evil goes against principle, sharp capacity can still break through its hindrance. Only the path itself is to be honored — how, then, could calming-and-contemplation ever be set aside merely on account of evil? The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "One lax in the precepts is not, on that account alone, called lax; one lax in the vehicle itself, this alone is truly called lax." The full fourfold pattern of laxity and urgency should be explained, matching the sense of capacity and hindrance given above, and so on. A sūtra also says: "I would rather be Devadatta than Udraka Rāmaputra" — this is exactly that same sense. One should diligently listen, reflect, and cultivate, without ever resting from the start — like a drunken brahmin shaving his head, or an actress dressed as a nun in a play, and so on.

Reading note: This closes the four kinds of samādhi in full. The final fourfold scheme — sharp capacity without hindrance, sharp capacity with hindrance, dull capacity without hindrance, dull capacity with hindrance — is the treatise's real answer to the whole "craving is itself the path" question: it was never a claim that evil is harmless, but that sharp capacity can sometimes break through the hindrance evil creates, the way Aṅgulimāla and King Ajātaśatru did on meeting the Buddha directly. For everyone else — which is to say, almost everyone — the ordinary route through wholesome practice remains what actually works, and the treatise says so without hedging: "though evil goes against principle, sharp capacity can still break through its hindrance... only the path itself is to be honored." The closing images (a drunken brahmin shaving his head, an actress playing a nun) are droll rather than solemn — a reminder that even after this much intensity, sustained ordinary effort, not any one dramatic technique, is what the treatise actually asks of most readers.

第五、歸大處。諸法畢竟空故，說是止觀者，夫膠手易著，寱夢難惺；封文齊意，自謂為是；競執瓦礫，謂瑠璃珠。近事顯語，猶尚不識，況遠理密教，寧當不惑？為此意故，須論旨歸。旨歸者，文旨所趣也。如水流趣海、火炎向空；識密達遠無所稽滯。譬如智臣解王密語；聞有所說皆悉了知，到一切智地。得此意者，即解旨歸。「旨」者，自向三德；「歸」者，引他同入三德，故名「旨歸」。又自入三德名「歸」；令他入三德名「旨」，故名「旨歸」。今更總、別明旨歸。諸佛為一大事因緣出現於世，示種種像，咸令眾生同見法身；見法身已，佛及眾生俱歸法身。又佛說種種法，咸令眾生究竟如來一切種智；種智具已，佛及眾生俱歸般若。又佛現種種方便神通變化解脫諸縛，不令一人獨得滅度，皆以如來滅度而滅度之；既滅度已，佛及眾生俱歸解脫。《大經》云：「安置諸子祕密藏中，我亦不久自住其中。」是名「總相旨歸」。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, returning to the great abode. Because every dharma is, in the end, empty, this calming-and-contemplation is taught: glue on the hand is easily stuck fast, but a dream once spoken is hard to wake from; clinging to the letter as though it matched one's own intent, one takes oneself for right, competing to grasp tile and rubble while calling it lapis lazuli. If even close-at-hand matters and plainly stated words still go unrecognized, how much more, then, might distant principles and hidden teachings lead one astray? For this very reason, where it all leads must now be discussed. "Where it leads" means where the text's own intent tends toward. As water flows toward the sea, and flame rises toward the sky, so too does deep understanding penetrate far without ever getting stuck along the way. It is like a wise minister who alone understands the king's own secret code: hearing whatever is said, he grasps it all at once, and thereby reaches the ground of all-knowing wisdom. One who grasps this sense has thereby understood where it all leads. "Where" means turning toward the three virtues oneself; "it leads" means drawing others in to enter the three virtues together — hence it is called "where it leads." Or: entering the three virtues oneself is called "leading"; causing others to enter the three virtues is called "where" — hence, again, "where it leads." Here, "where it all leads" will be explained both as a whole and in its particulars. Every buddha appears in the world for the sake of the one great causal condition, manifesting every kind of form, so that sentient beings alike come to see the Dharma-body together; once the Dharma-body is seen, both buddhas and sentient beings alike return to the Dharma-body. Again, the Buddha teaches every kind of dharma, so that sentient beings alike come to the Tathāgata's own ultimate wisdom that knows all things in every one of their modes; once that wisdom is complete, both buddhas and sentient beings alike return to prajñā. Again, the Buddha manifests every kind of skillful means, supernormal transformation, and liberation from every bond, never causing a single person alone to attain extinction, but bringing every being to extinction by means of the very same extinction the Tathāgata himself has attained; once that extinction is attained, both buddhas and sentient beings alike return to liberation. The Nirvāṇa Sūtra says: "I have placed all my children within the hidden treasury, and before long I too shall dwell within it myself." This is called "where it all leads, as a whole."

別相者，身有三種：一者色身，二者法門身，三者實相身。若息化論歸者，色身歸解脫，法門身歸般若，實相身歸法身。般若說有三種：一說道種智，二說一切智，三說一切種智。若息化論歸，道種智歸解脫，一切智歸般若，一切種智歸法身。解脫有三種：一、解無知縛，二、解取相縛，三、解無明縛。若息化歸真，解無知縛歸解脫，解取相縛歸般若，解無明縛歸法身。以是義故，別相旨歸亦歸三德祕密藏中。

As for the particulars: the body has three kinds — first, the physical body; second, the body of dharma-gates; third, the body of the true mark of reality. Once transformative activity is set to rest and where it leads is discussed, the physical body returns to liberation, the body of dharma-gates returns to prajñā, and the body of the true mark of reality returns to the Dharma-body. Prajñā, too, is spoken of as three kinds — first, the wisdom that knows the seeds of every path; second, the wisdom that knows all things generally; third, the wisdom that knows all things in every one of their modes. Once transformative activity is set to rest and where it leads is discussed, the wisdom of the seeds of every path returns to liberation, the wisdom that knows all things generally returns to prajñā, and the wisdom that knows all things in every mode returns to the Dharma-body. Liberation, too, is of three kinds — first, freedom from the bond of not-knowing; second, freedom from the bond of grasping at marks; third, freedom from the bond of ignorance. Once transformative activity is set to rest and one returns to the real, freedom from the bond of not-knowing returns to liberation, freedom from the bond of grasping at marks returns to prajñā, and freedom from the bond of ignorance returns to the Dharma-body. For this reason, where it all leads, in its particulars, likewise returns to the three virtues' own hidden treasury.

復次，三德非三非一，不可思議。所以者何？若謂法身直法身者，非法身也。當知法身亦身、非身、非身非非身，住首楞嚴，種種示現作眾色像，故名為「身」；所作辦已，歸於解脫。智慧照了諸色非色，故名「非身」；所作辦已，歸於般若。實相之身非色像身、非法門身，是故非身非非身；所作辦已，歸於法身。達此三身無一異相是名為「歸」；說此三身無一異相是名為「旨」。俱入祕藏，故名「旨歸」。若謂般若直般若者，非般若也。當知般若亦知、非知、非知非非知。道種智般若遍知於俗，故名為「知」；所作辦已，歸於解脫。一切智般若遍知於真，故名「非知」；所作辦已，歸於般若。一切種智般若遍知於中，故名「非知非非知」；所作辦已，歸於法身。達三般若無一異相是名為「歸」；說三般若無一異相是名為「旨」。俱入祕藏，故名「旨歸」。若謂解脫直解脫者，非解脫也。當知解脫亦脫、非脫、非脫非非脫。方便淨解脫調伏眾生不為所染，故名為「脫」；所作辦已，歸於解脫。圓淨解脫不見眾生及解脫相，故名「非脫」；所作辦已，歸於般若。性淨解脫則非脫非非脫；所作辦已，歸於法身。若達、若說，如此三脫非一異相，俱入祕藏，故名「旨歸」。

Furthermore: the three virtues are neither three nor one, and are inconceivable. Why is this? If one holds that the Dharma-body is simply and only the Dharma-body, this is not truly the Dharma-body at all. One should know that the Dharma-body is likewise body, non-body, and neither body nor non-body: abiding in the Śūraṅgama, it manifests every kind of form in various ways — hence it is named "body"; once this task is accomplished, it returns to liberation. Wisdom, illuminating that every form is not truly form, is hence named "non-body"; once this task is accomplished, it returns to prajñā. The body of the true mark of reality is neither a body of physical form nor a body of dharma-gates, and is hence neither body nor non-body; once this task is accomplished, it returns to the Dharma-body. Penetrating that these three bodies bear no mark of difference between them is called "leading"; expounding that these three bodies bear no mark of difference between them is called "where." Both alike enter the hidden treasury — hence it is called "where it all leads." If one holds that prajñā is simply and only prajñā, this is not truly prajñā at all. One should know that prajñā is likewise knowing, non-knowing, and neither knowing nor non-knowing. The wisdom of the seeds of every path, as prajñā, knows the conventional universally — hence it is named "knowing"; once this task is accomplished, it returns to liberation. The wisdom that knows all things generally, as prajñā, knows the ultimate universally — hence it is named "non-knowing"; once this task is accomplished, it returns to prajñā. The wisdom that knows all things in every mode, as prajñā, knows the middle universally — hence it is named "neither knowing nor non-knowing"; once this task is accomplished, it returns to the Dharma-body. Penetrating that these three kinds of prajñā bear no mark of difference between them is called "leading"; expounding that these three kinds of prajñā bear no mark of difference between them is called "where." Both alike enter the hidden treasury — hence it is called "where it all leads." If one holds that liberation is simply and only liberation, this is not truly liberation at all. One should know that liberation is likewise freedom, non-freedom, and neither freedom nor non-freedom. Liberation through the purity of skillful means tames sentient beings without being defiled by them — hence it is named "freedom"; once this task is accomplished, it returns to liberation. Liberation through perfect purity sees no mark of sentient beings and no mark of liberation itself — hence it is named "non-freedom"; once this task is accomplished, it returns to prajñā. Liberation through the purity of one's own nature is neither freedom nor non-freedom; once this task is accomplished, it returns to the Dharma-body. Whether penetrated or expounded, these three kinds of liberation bear no mark of difference, and both alike enter the hidden treasury — hence it is called "where it all leads."

復次，三德非新非故，而新而故。所以者何？三障障三德：無明障法身；取相障般若；無知障解脫。三障先有，名之為「故」；三德破三障，今始得顯，故名為「新」。三障即三德；三德即三障。三障即三德，三障非故；三德即三障，三德非新。非新而新，則有發心所得之三德，乃至究竟所得之三德；非故而故，則有發心所治之三障，乃至究竟所治之三障。新非新，故非故，則有理性之三德。若總達三德非新、非故、而新而故，無一異相；為他亦然，即是旨歸祕密藏中。又說者，無明先有，名為「故」；法身是明，破於無明，名為「新」。無明即明；明即無明。無明即明，無明非故；明即無明，明則非新。取相先有，名之為「故」；無相破相，無相名「新」。相即無相，無相即相，何新何故？無知先有，名之為「故」；知破無知，知名為「新」。無知即知，知即無知，何新何故？若達總別，新故無一異相；若為他說，亦復如是，是名「旨歸」，入祕密藏。縱橫、開合、始終等，例皆如是。

Furthermore: the three virtues are neither new nor old, and yet are both new and old. Why is this? The three obstructions obstruct the three virtues: ignorance obstructs the Dharma-body; grasping at marks obstructs prajñā; not-knowing obstructs liberation. The three obstructions, having existed already, are named "old"; the three virtues, once the three obstructions are broken through, are only now made manifest — hence they are named "new." The three obstructions are themselves already the three virtues; the three virtues are themselves already the three obstructions. The three obstructions being themselves the three virtues, the three obstructions are not truly "old"; the three virtues being themselves the three obstructions, the three virtues are not truly "new." Not new, and yet new, there is the three virtues attained at the arising of the resolve, on up to the three virtues attained at the ultimate stage; not old, and yet old, there is the three obstructions treated at the arising of the resolve, on up to the three obstructions treated at the ultimate stage. Neither new nor not-new, neither old nor not-old, there is the three virtues as inherent principle itself. If one fully penetrates that the three virtues are neither new, nor old, and yet both new and old, bearing no mark of difference at all — and, expounding this for others, it is exactly the same — this is precisely where it all leads, within the hidden treasury. Again, to state it further: ignorance, having existed already, is named "old"; the Dharma-body is illumination, and breaks through ignorance, hence named "new." Ignorance is itself illumination; illumination is itself ignorance. Ignorance being itself illumination, ignorance is not truly "old"; illumination being itself ignorance, illumination is not truly "new." Grasping at marks, having existed already, is named "old"; marklessness breaks through marks, and marklessness is named "new." Marks are themselves marklessness, and marklessness is itself marks — what, then, is truly new, and what truly old? Not-knowing, having existed already, is named "old"; knowing breaks through not-knowing, and knowing is named "new." Not-knowing is itself knowing, and knowing is itself not-knowing — what, then, is truly new, and what truly old? If one penetrates both the whole and the particulars, new and old bear no mark of difference; and expounding this for others is exactly the same — this is called "where it all leads," entering the hidden treasury. Vertical and horizontal, opening and closing, beginning and end, and every such pair, all follow this same pattern.

復次，旨歸亦復如是。謂旨、非旨、非旨非非旨，歸、非歸、非歸非非歸，一一悉須入祕密藏中，例上可解。旨，自行故；非旨，化他故；非旨非非旨，無自他故，旨歸三德寂靜若此，有何名字而可說示？不知何以名之，強名「中道」、「實相」、「法身」、「非止非觀」等；亦復強名「一切種智」、「平等大慧」、「般若波羅蜜觀」等；亦復強名「首楞嚴定」、「大涅槃」、「不可思議解脫止」等。當知種種相、種種說、種種神力，一一皆入祕密藏中。何等是旨歸？旨歸何處？誰是旨歸？言語道斷，心行處滅，永寂如空，是名「旨歸」。至第十重中，當廣說也。

Furthermore, "where it all leads" is likewise exactly the same: it is "where," "not-where," and "neither where nor not-where"; it is "leads," "does not lead," and "neither leads nor does not lead" — each and every one of these must enter into the hidden treasury alike, and may be understood following the pattern above. "Where" is because of practice for oneself; "not-where" is because of transforming others; "neither where nor not-where" is because there is no self or other at all. Where it all leads, the three virtues being quiescent just as this — what name could possibly be spoken or shown for it? Not knowing what else to call it, it is forcibly named "the middle way," "the true mark of reality," "the Dharma-body," "neither calming nor contemplation," and so on; it is likewise forcibly named "the wisdom that knows all things in every mode," "the great wisdom of equality," "the contemplation of the perfection of wisdom," and so on; it is likewise forcibly named "the Śūraṅgama concentration," "great nirvāṇa," "the inconceivable calming of liberation," and so on. One should know that every kind of mark, every kind of teaching, every kind of supernormal power, each and every one, enters into this same hidden treasury alike. What, then, is "where it all leads"? Where does it lead to? Who is it that leads there? The path of language is cut off, the place where the mind moves is extinguished, forever quiescent like empty space itself — this is what is named "where it all leads." This will be explained at length when the tenth section is reached.

Reading note: This closes topic one, the great intent, in its entirety — and does so by introducing the Three Virtues (三德: the Dharma-body, prajñā, and liberation), a scheme that will recur throughout the rest of this treatise as its own account of what buddhahood ultimately consists in. The pattern is by now familiar from this fascicle and the last: each of the three virtues, examined closely, turns out to already contain all three within itself (the Dharma-body is "body, non-body, and neither body nor non-body"), and the three obstructions that block them turn out to be, on analysis, identical with the virtues they obstruct — ignorance "is itself" illumination, not merely overcome by it. What is new here is the wise minister simile opening the section: understanding "where it all leads" is likened to a minister who alone can decode a king's private cipher, grasping instantly what everyone else must labor over — an image for how, once this treatise's central pattern is truly internalized, its dense reasoning becomes almost transparent rather than harder to follow. The section's own closing line — "the path of language is cut off... forever quiescent like empty space itself" — deliberately returns to the same territory as fascicle 1's debate about whether ultimate reality can be spoken of at all, now stated as the treatise's own resting point rather than a live question.
