Fascicle 10 · 摩訶止觀卷第十 · The Treatise's Final Fascicle

The Object of Views

This page continues — and, for now, concludes — Ru-Yi's own English translation of Zhiyi's Mohe Zhiguan (摩訶止觀, "The Great Calming and Contemplation"). Fascicle 10 treats the seventh of the ten objects of contemplation, views (諸見境): the varieties of heterodox teacher and doctrine, how a given view manifests through dhyāna or through hearing, the faults views produce, and the ten modes of contemplation finally applied to views by way of the single exemplary case of nihilistic emptiness-view. The CBETA source text ends mid-topic at the close of this fascicle — this is the actual point at which Zhiyi's own lecture series was cut short, not merely the current limit of this site's translation. We translate slowly, section by section, with the Chinese source beside the English so readers can study the meaning, not merely read around it.

Continuing from Fascicle 9 — and Where the Treatise Itself Ends

Fascicle 9 was devoted entirely to the sixth object of contemplation, meditative states, across all ten kinds of dhyāna. Fascicle 10 (again combining CBETA's upper and lower halves into a single continuous page, per this site's existing convention) moves to the seventh object, views (諸見境) — and this is as far as the historical record goes. Guanding's transcription of Zhiyi's lectures breaks off partway through this seventh of the ten objects, before ever reaching the remaining three (overweening pride, the two lesser vehicles, the bodhisattva) or the treatise's own final three topics (the fruits of practice, establishing the teaching, and the ultimate aim). This page therefore does not merely mark where this site's translation currently stands — it translates the treatise through to its own actual, historical end. The object of views receives a four-part treatment: the varieties of heterodox person and doctrine, the causes and conditions by which a given view manifests, the faults views produce, and, finally, the ten modes of contemplation applied to views by way of a single exemplary case, nihilistic emptiness-view. The Seventh Object: Contemplating Views 第七、觀諸見境

Chinese Source

第七、觀諸見境者：非一曰「諸」；邪解稱「見」。又解知是見義。推理不當而偏見分明，作決定解，名之為「見」。夫聽學人，誦得名相，齊文作解；心眼不開，全無理觀。據文者生，無證者死。夫習禪人，唯尚理觀，觸處心融；闇於名相，一句不識。誦文者守株，情通者妙悟，兩家互闕，論評皆失。若見解無滯，名字又諳，以見解問他，意無窮盡，如曲射繞鳥，飛走失路。若解釋難問綽有餘工，如射太虛，箭去無礙。當知非由學成，必是見發。此見或因禪發、或因聞發。例如無漏起時，藉於信、法聞思。因聞發者，本聽不多廣能轉悟，見解分明、聰辯問答；因禪發者，初因心靜，後觀轉明，翻轉自在有如妙達。南方習禪者寡，發見人微；北方多有此事。盲瞑不識，謂得真道，謂得陀羅尼。闇於知人，高安地位。或時不信，撥是狂惑。今言非狂、非聖。夫鬼著能語，鬼去則癡；其既不爾，故知非狂。尋其故惑，貪、瞋尚在；約其新惑，更增煩惱，八十八使繫縛浩然，故知非聖。乃是見慧發耳。通論見發，因聞、因禪，而多因禪。或禪已見發；或禪、見俱發；見已得禪又少，兩義則多。例如諸禪通發無漏，而未到發者少，六地、九地發者多。為是義故，次禪定境而論諸見也。若人見發，利智根熟能自裁正，或尋經論勘知己過者，此人難得；若不能自正，遇善知識明示是非，破其見心，此亦難得。故云：「真法及說者，聽眾難得故。」既不自覺，又不值師，邪畫日增，生死月甚，如稠林曳曲木，何得出期？今觀諸見境為四：一、明諸見人法，二、明諸見發因緣，三、明過失，四、明止觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, contemplating the object of views: "not one" is called "manifold"; "heterodox understanding" is termed "view." Further, understanding itself is what the word "view" means — reasoning improperly, yet with one's own partial understanding rendered vividly clear, and forming a fixed, settled interpretation from it, this is named "view." Now, those who study by way of listening recite and acquire names and categories, forming their understanding strictly according to the text — but their own mind's eye remains unopened, entirely without principle-contemplation. One who relies on the text alone lives by it; one without genuine realization dies by it. Those who practice meditation, by contrast, value principle-contemplation alone, the mind dissolving freely wherever it turns — yet they remain in the dark regarding names and categories, unable to recognize even a single phrase. The one who merely recites the text is like the farmer guarding his stump, waiting for another rabbit to run into it; the one whose feeling has genuinely penetrated the matter attains subtle awakening — yet each of these two schools lacks what the other has, and every assessment made by either alone proves deficient. Should one's understanding through view remain unobstructed, and the terminology, too, already familiar, then, questioning another by way of this very understanding, one's own sense proves inexhaustible — like an archer shooting a curved arrow to encircle a bird, so that the bird's own flight loses its way. Should one instead find refuting difficult questions to be a matter of ample skill and surplus effort, this is like shooting an arrow into the vast emptiness of the sky, its flight meeting no obstruction at all. One should understand, then, that this is not something accomplished merely through study — it must instead be the manifestation of a genuine view. This view may manifest either through dhyāna or through hearing — comparable, for instance, to how the arising of the uncontaminated itself relies upon the practitioner of faith's hearing-and-reflection or the practitioner of dharma's own hearing-and-reflection. One in whom it manifests through hearing, though his own listening has not been extensive, is nonetheless able to turn this into broad awakening, his understanding through view clear and distinct, his eloquence sharp in question and answer. One in whom it manifests through dhyāna, at first through a stilled mind, only afterward finds his own contemplation growing correspondingly clear, turning and adapting with complete freedom, as though possessed of subtle penetration. In the south, where few practice meditation, those in whom views manifest are correspondingly rare; in the north, this occurs far more often. Those blind and benighted, failing to recognize this for what it is, suppose they have attained the genuine path itself, suppose they have attained a dhāraṇī — and, remaining in the dark as to how to judge such a person, they elevate him to an exalted rank. At other times, people refuse to believe it at all, dismissing it outright as sheer madness and delusion. Here, it is said that this is neither madness nor sagehood. A person possessed by a spirit is able to speak eloquently — but once the spirit departs, foolishness returns; since this is plainly not the case here, one knows this is not madness. Examining the person's own prior afflictions, greed and anger still remain present in him; and, considering his newly arisen afflictions, they have only increased further still, the eighty-eight proclivities binding him as vastly as ever — hence one knows this is not sagehood either. It is, rather, simply the manifestation of view-based intelligence, nothing more. Discussing the manifestation of views generally, they arise either through hearing or through dhyāna, though for the most part through dhyāna. Sometimes dhyāna manifests first and views manifest afterward; sometimes dhyāna and views manifest together; cases in which views manifest first and dhyāna is only attained afterward are comparatively rare, while the other pattern is far more common. This is comparable, for instance, to how every dhyāna is generally capable of producing the uncontaminated, yet those in whom it is produced at the mere stage of approach are few, while those in whom it is produced at the sixth or ninth ground are many. For this very reason, the object of views is discussed here immediately following the object of meditative states. Should a person in whom a view manifests possess sharp intelligence and mature faculties, able to correct himself, or examine the sūtras and treatises and thereby recognize his own fault — such a person is difficult indeed to find. Should he be unable to correct himself, yet encounter a good friend able to demonstrate clearly what is right and wrong, breaking through his very mind of view — this, too, is difficult indeed to find. Hence it is said: "The genuine Dharma, and one able to expound it, and an audience able to receive it, are all alike difficult to come by." Failing to recognize his own condition, and failing, further, to encounter such a teacher, his heterodoxy grows daily, his cycle of birth-and-death intensifies monthly, like a man dragging a crooked log through a dense thicket — how could he possibly find his way out? Contemplating the object of views is here treated in four parts: first, clarifying the persons and doctrines involved in views; second, clarifying the causes and conditions of their manifestation; third, clarifying the faults they produce; fourth, clarifying calming-and-contemplation.

Three Kinds of Heterodox Person: Outsiders, Those Attached to Buddhism, and Those Who Cling Their Way Out of It 一明諸見人法 · 三種邪人

Chinese Source

第一、明諸見人法又二：一、邪人不同，二、邪人執法不同。邪人不同又為三：一、佛法外外道，二、附佛法外道，三、學佛法成外道。一、外外道。本源有三：一、迦毘羅外道，此翻黃頭，計因中有果；二、漚樓僧佉，此翻休睺，計因中無果；三、勒沙婆，此翻苦行，計因中亦有果亦無果。又《入大乘論》云：「迦毘羅所說有計一過，作者與作一，相與相者一，分與有分一，如是等名為計一。優樓僧佉計異，迦羅鳩馱計一、異，若提子計非一非異。一切外道及摩迦羅等計異，皆不離此四。」從三四外道派出枝流，至佛出時有六大師，所謂：富蘭那迦葉，迦葉，姓也，計不生不滅；末伽梨拘賒梨子，計眾生苦樂無有因緣，自然而爾；刪闍夜毘羅胝子，計眾生時熟得道，八萬劫到縷丸數極；阿耆多翅舍欽婆羅，欽婆羅，麁衣也，計罪報之苦以投巖、拔髮代之；迦羅鳩馱迦旃延，計亦有亦無；尼揵陀若提子，計業所作定不可改。此出《羅什疏》，名與《大經》同，所計三同、三異，或翻誤，或別有意，今所未詳。而大體祖承迦毘羅等，依本為三，或可為四，謂四見也。二、附佛法外道者：起自犢子、方廣，自以聰明，讀佛經書而生一見；附佛法起，故得此名。犢子讀《舍利弗毘曇》，自制別義，言：「我在四句外，第五不可說藏中。」云何四句？外道計色即是我，離色有我，色中有我，我中有色；四陰亦如是，合二十身見。《大論》云：「破二十身見成須陀洹。」即此義也。今犢子計我異於六師，復非佛法，諸論皆推不受，便是附佛法邪人法也。或云三世及無為法為四句也。又方廣道人自以聰明，讀佛十喻，自作義云：「不生不滅，如幻如化，空幻為宗。」龍樹斥云：「非佛法！」方廣所作，亦是邪人法也。三、學佛法成外道：執佛教門而生煩惱，不得入理。《大論》云：「若不得般若方便，入阿毘曇，即墮有中；入空，即墮無中；入昆勒，墮亦有亦無中。」《中論》云：「執非有非無，名愚癡論。」倒執正法，還成邪人法也。若學摩訶衍四門即失般若意，為邪火所燒，還成邪人法。故《百論》正破外外道。今大乘論師炎破《毘曇》、《成實》，謂是計有、無外道。然《成論》云：「三藏中實義，空是。」此乃似無意。又同百家之是，異百家之非，捉義出沒，又似因中亦有果亦無果意，又似昆勒意。當時論起，人皆得道；今時執者，乃是人失，何關法非？此應從容，不可雷同迦毘羅等。若以大破小，如《淨名》所斥，取其不見中理與外道同，非是奪其方便之意。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, clarifying the persons and doctrines involved in views, treated in two parts: first, the varieties of heterodox person; second, the varieties of doctrine such heterodox persons hold. The varieties of heterodox person are, in turn, threefold: first, outsiders entirely outside the Buddha's own teaching; second, outsiders attached to the Buddha's teaching; third, those who study the Buddha's teaching yet become outsiders through it. First, outsiders entirely outside the teaching: their root sources are three. First, the Kāpila school, whose name translates as "yellow-head," holding that the effect already exists within the cause; second, the Aulūkya-Sāṃkhya school, whose name translates as "owl," holding that the effect does not exist within the cause; third, the Ulūka school, whose name translates as "ascetic practice," holding that the effect both exists and does not exist within the cause. Further, the *Treatise on Entering the Mahāyāna* says: "What Kapila taught holds a single fault of positing oneness — the agent and the act are one, the mark and that which bears the mark are one, the part and the whole possessing that part are one, and so on — all such positions are termed 'positing oneness.' Aulūkya-Sāṃkhya posits difference; Kāla-kaudra-kātyāyana posits both oneness and difference; the son of Jñātṛ posits neither oneness nor difference. Every non-Buddhist school, including the followers of Kapila, positing difference, never departs from these four positions." From these three or four root schools, branch lineages spread forth, so that, by the time of the Buddha's own appearance, there were six great teachers: Pūraṇa Kāśyapa — Kāśyapa being his clan name — who held that nothing arises and nothing ceases; Maskarin Gośālīputra, who held that sentient beings' suffering and happiness have no cause or condition at all, occurring spontaneously of themselves; Sañjaya Vairaṭīputra, who held that sentient beings attain the path only once their own time has ripened, extending across eighty thousand kalpas until the very count of a ball of thread wound and unwound to its very end; Ajita Keśakambala — kambala meaning "coarse woolen garment" — who held that the suffering of retributive transgression could be substituted for by throwing oneself from a cliff or plucking out one's own hair; Kakuda Kātyāyana, who held both existence and non-existence at once; and Nirgrantha Jñātṛputra, who held that karma, once performed, is fixed and can never be altered. This account derives from Kumārajīva's own commentary — the names agree with those in the Great Sūtra, though the three points of agreement and three of divergence in what each is said to hold may reflect either a translation error or some separate intended sense not yet fully clear here. In their general substance, however, these all trace their lineage back to Kapila and the rest, and, according to their root sources, may be reckoned as three, or, alternatively, as four — namely, the four views. Second, as for outsiders attached to the Buddha's own teaching: this arises with the Vātsīputrīya and Vaitulya schools, who, relying on their own cleverness, read the Buddha's own scriptures and thereby give rise to a view of their own — arising, as it does, attached to the Buddha's teaching, this school receives this very name. The Vātsīputrīyas, reading the *Śāriputra-abhidharma*, fashioned a separate doctrine of their own, saying: "I exist outside these four propositions, within a fifth, inexpressible category." What are these four propositions? Non-Buddhists hold that form itself is the self, that the self exists apart from form, that the self exists within form, or that form exists within the self — and the remaining four aggregates are treated the same way, yielding twenty views of a self in all. The *Great Treatise* says: "Breaking through these twenty views of a self accomplishes stream-entry" — this carries precisely this same sense. Here, the Vātsīputrīyas' own positing of a self differs from that of the six teachers described above, yet it is likewise not genuine Buddhist teaching either — every treatise alike rejects and refuses to accept it — hence this is precisely a heterodox doctrine attached to the Buddha's teaching. Some instead say the four propositions here concern the three time-periods together with unconditioned dharmas. Further, the Vaitulya adherents, relying on their own cleverness, read the Buddha's ten similes and fashioned a doctrine of their own, saying: "Neither arising nor ceasing, like an illusion, like a transformation — emptiness and illusion are our very core teaching." Nāgārjuna rebuked this, saying: "This is not the Buddha's own teaching!" — what the Vaitulya adherents fashioned is likewise a heterodox doctrine. Third, as for those who study the Buddha's own teaching yet become outsiders through it: clinging to the very gateway of the Buddha's teaching, one gives rise to affliction through it, unable to penetrate to its underlying principle. The *Great Treatise* says: "Should one fail to grasp prajñā's own skillful means, entering the Abhidharma leads one to fall into existence; entering emptiness leads one to fall into non-existence; entering the Kaurilya leads one to fall into both existence and non-existence at once." The *Madhyamaka-śāstra* says: "Clinging to neither-existence-nor-non-existence is named the treatise of utter folly." Inverting one's grasp of the correct teaching in this way likewise results in a heterodox doctrine. Should one study the Mahāyāna's own four gates yet thereby lose the very sense of prajñā, one is burned by this same heterodox fire, likewise becoming a heterodox doctrine. Hence the *Hundred Treatise* directly refutes those entirely outside the teaching. In the present day, certain Mahāyāna treatise-masters fiercely refute the *Abhidharma* and the *Satyasiddhi*, calling them heterodox schools positing existence or non-existence. Yet the *Satyasiddhi* itself says: "The genuine sense within the Tripiṭaka is emptiness itself" — this hardly seems to carry the sense these critics impute to it. It is, further, in agreement with what a hundred schools hold to be correct, and in disagreement with what a hundred schools hold to be mistaken, its own position shifting in and out of view — resembling, at times, the sense of the effect both existing and not existing within the cause, and, at other times, the sense of the Kaurilya school. When this treatise was first composed, everyone who studied it attained the path through it; that people today merely cling to it is a failing of the people themselves — what fault does this reflect upon the teaching itself? This matter ought to be approached with due composure, and not simply lumped together indiscriminately with Kapila and the rest. Should one use the Mahāyāna to refute the Hīnayāna, as the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* itself does, this is done on the grounds that the Hīnayāna fails to perceive the truth of the middle, making it comparable in this one respect to the non-Buddhist schools — it is not meant to deny the genuine value of its own skillful means.

Three Ways of Attaining a Doctrine: Omniscience, Spiritual Power, and Vedic Learning 二明邪人執法不同

Chinese Source

二、明邪人執法不同者，《關中疏》云：「一師各有三種法：一、得一切智法，二、得神通法，三、得韋陀法。」一切智者，各於所計生一種見，解心明利，將此見智通一切法，故名「一切智外道」。神通法者，發得五通，變城為鹵、轉釋為羊、停河在耳、捫摸日月，此名「神通外道」。韋陀法者，世間文字、星醫兵貨，悉能解知，是為「韋陀外道」。一師則有三種得法不同也。犢子、方廣亦如是。若望執佛法邪，約三藏四門，一門有三：一、直發理解，智性生見；二、得諸神通；三、解《四阿含》文字。如是四門則有十二種得法不同也。若得意者，一一門中初有三種念處：一、性念處，二、共念處，三、緣念處。「性」是直緣諦理；「共」是事理合修；「緣」是遍緣一切境法，亦是緣三藏教法。後證果時成三種解脫——慧解脫、俱解脫、無疑解脫。故結集法藏時選取千人，悉用無疑解脫，遍解內外經書，擬降外敵。《毘曇婆沙》云：「煩惱障解脫、禪定障解脫、一切法障解脫。」慧解脫人得初解脫，共解脫人得第二解脫，唯佛得第三解脫，總名「無疑解脫」也。執摩訶衍通、別、圓四門失意者，例有三十六種得法不同。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the varieties of doctrine-attainment method among heterodox persons: the *Guanzhong Commentary* says: "Each single teacher possesses three kinds of doctrine: first, the doctrine of attaining omniscience; second, the doctrine of attaining spiritual power; third, the doctrine of attaining Vedic learning." As for omniscience: each teacher, with respect to his own particular position, gives rise to a single view, his understanding sharp and clear, and, taking this very view and this very intelligence, applies it universally to every dharma — hence such a one is named "the omniscience-attaining outsider." As for the doctrine of spiritual power: one manifests the five mundane powers, transforming a city into a salt marsh, turning a monk into a sheep, stopping a river within one's own ear, or reaching out to touch the sun and moon — such a one is named "the spiritual-power-attaining outsider." As for the doctrine of Vedic learning: one is able to understand and know worldly writings of every kind — astrology, medicine, military strategy, and commerce — such a one is named "the Vedic-learning-attaining outsider." Each single teacher thus has three distinct ways of attaining his doctrine. The Vātsīputrīya and Vaitulya schools are exactly the same. As for those who cling erroneously to the Buddha's own teaching, treated in terms of the Tripiṭaka teaching's four gates: each single gate has three aspects — first, directly manifesting understanding through principle, one's own intellectual nature giving rise to a view; second, attaining various spiritual powers; third, understanding the very text of the four Āgamas. These four gates thus yield twelve distinct ways of attaining a doctrine in all. Grasping this sense more precisely, within each single gate there are, at the outset, three kinds of foundation of mindfulness: the foundation of mindfulness of nature, the foundation of mindfulness of shared cultivation, and the foundation of mindfulness of the object. "Nature" refers to taking the truth of the principle itself directly as one's object; "shared cultivation" refers to cultivating concrete practice and principle together; "object" refers to taking every object and dharma universally as one's object, this being likewise taking the Tripiṭaka teaching's own doctrine as one's object. Once the fruit is later realized, this accomplishes three kinds of liberation — liberation through wisdom alone, both-liberation, and liberation free of doubt. Hence, at the time the Dharma treasury was first compiled, a thousand men were selected, every one of them possessing liberation free of doubt, thoroughly versed in both Buddhist and non-Buddhist scriptures alike, so as to be prepared to subdue any outside challenger. The *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā* says: "Liberation from the obstruction of affliction, liberation from the obstruction of meditative concentration, liberation from the obstruction of every dharma." One liberated through wisdom alone attains the first liberation; one both-liberated attains the second liberation; only the Buddha attains the third liberation — together these are all named "liberation free of doubt." Those who cling erroneously to the Mahāyāna's own shared, distinct, and perfect teachings' four gates, thereby losing the true sense of each, likewise have, by the same pattern, thirty-six distinct ways of attaining their doctrine.

How Views Manifest: Through Dhyāna or Through Hearing 二明諸見發因緣

As the *Zhuangzi* says: "Noble and base, suffering and joy, right and wrong, gain and loss — all alike are simply nature taking its own spontaneous course." Should this be understood to affirm the very fruit itself, then this is not refuting the fruit; yet its failure to address prior karma is itself a refutation of the cause. The rites establish benevolence and righteousness, protecting the body and securing the state; should these not be practiced and applied, one's clan is destroyed and one's household ruined. Yet what, precisely, have you attained? You do not even recognize the desire proper to the desire realm, nor the subtlety of the first dhyāna — how much less, then, the subtlety that lies beyond even these? All of this pertains to holding the view of spontaneous nature while practicing good. There is, further, holding this same view of spontaneous nature while simply following one's own impulses at will, neither actively directing oneself toward good, nor actively engaging in evil either — should one disturb this very spiritual harmony, one thereby fails to accord with nature's own spontaneity. Though such a person makes no active choice between the two, this itself constitutes morally indeterminate conduct — and, this karmic action not yet exhausted, how could receiving its corresponding retribution possibly be in doubt? Should one instead hold this view of spontaneous nature while committing evil, holding that all things follow nature's own spontaneous course, and thereby indulging oneself freely in evil, on the grounds that it, too, ultimately returns to this same nature — this is turning one's back on freedom from desire and indulging desire instead, departing from subtlety to embrace coarseness in its place. It is like Zhuang Zhou's own rebuke of benevolence and righteousness: though this may guard against the petty thief, it fails to anticipate the great thief, who seizes upon benevolence and righteousness themselves as the very means of seizing the state. What was originally meant, through spontaneous nature, to still desire, becomes instead a pretext, through this same spontaneous nature, for committing evil — this sense may readily be understood.

This emptiness-view itself divides into three: first, refuting the cause without refuting the fruit, or refuting the fruit without refuting the cause; second, refuting both cause and fruit together, without refuting every other dharma; third, refuting cause, fruit, and every other dharma all at once — "every other dharma" here referring to the three unconditioned dharmas. How, then, does this third position differ from the Buddha's own teaching? The *Great Treatise* clarifies that the greater and lesser vehicles' respective emptiness differ in being reached through embodying versus through analysis. Yet non-Buddhist schools, too, employ both embodying and analysis — how, then, do these differ? Non-Buddhist schools proceed from a heterodox causal condition, or from no causal condition at all, whether by analysis, by embodying, or by ultimate emptiness. The Buddha's own disciples, by contrast, know that this proceeds from the causal condition of craving, whether by analysis, by embodying, or by ultimate emptiness. Some hold: "Mere refutation through words is not the same as embodying." Here, however, it should be noted: the *Madhyamaka-śāstra*, from beginning to end, uses refutation as its very governing method, chapter by chapter — how, then, could such refutation possibly differ from embodying itself? Hence the distinction between heterodox and correct, or between the lesser and greater vehicles, is not properly drawn along these lines at all — rather, following the *Great Treatise*, analysis applied to the correct causal condition differs from the non-Buddhist schools, while embodying applied to the correct causal condition differs from the Hīnayāna. One in whom this emptiness-view arises no longer contends over wealth, position, or retributive result — emptiness itself becomes the sole point of contention instead. His mind, emptied of all fear, retains no regard for propriety whatsoever, indulging his feelings and giving free rein to his desires, destroying correct view, proper deportment, and pure livelihood alike — upon death, such a person inevitably falls into one of the three evil paths. Though he claims to be entirely unobstructed, he nonetheless dares not defy his own ruler or show contempt toward the empress, cherishing his own person all the while — and in this alone, then, he plainly remains obstructed after all. Ru-Yi Translation

Converting others admits of four modes: first, being evil oneself while urging others to practice good; second, extolling good conduct in oneself while urging others toward evil; third, urging both oneself and others alike toward evil; fourth, urging both oneself and others alike toward good. As for being evil oneself while urging others toward good, one says: "I myself am able to penetrate the underlying principle, and thus evil poses no obstacle for me; you, however, are still at a shallow stage of practice, and must first cultivate good" — the proper way of converting others, in this view, is to lead them first through goodness. As for being good oneself while urging others toward evil, one says: "I myself am the very master of conversion, and must, in blending my light with the world, remain good; you, however, are merely practicing for your own sake, and ought properly to practice evil instead." Urging both oneself and others toward evil together is done on the grounds that this constitutes the genuine, real path; urging both oneself and others toward good together is done on the grounds that this constitutes the provisional path. Though these four modes differ from one another, all alike take evil as their very root, sinking ever further in accordance with the karma thus accumulated — what path, then, could possibly be followed through any of them? Further, as for emptiness-view combined with the practice of good: holding that emptiness itself is free of good and evil, and yet that good must nonetheless be practiced — for should one fail to practice good, the god Viṣṇu grows angry, and sentient beings suffer affliction as a result; this affliction, in turn, forms karma; this karma, arising in the past, is received as retribution in the present. By upholding the precepts and practicing austerities in the present, one thereby blocks this present evil fruit, and attains the exhaustion of every contaminant. If this is so, then, precisely because goodness is required, one upholds the precepts, restrains the body, reduces desire, remains content, wears coarse clothing, and eats simple fare. Yet, cultivating this very conduct for the sake of emptiness, one gives rise to joy and anger regardless — emptiness itself remaining the very locus of contention, attachment, and calculation. Should one attain dhyāna and then have this view manifest, the dhyāna is thereby dissolved and the view grows correspondingly intense; should the view manifest and dhyāna follow afterward, this dhyāna, for the most part, proves to be nothing but demonic dhyāna, demonic spiritual power. Such an emptiness-view, in its own practice, follows only this single path — while its conversion of others still admits of the same four modes described above. Both one's own practice and the conversion of others alike, in this case, remain simply subject to karma — and rising or sinking according to karma, what does this have to do with the genuine path at all? Next, as for clinging to an emptiness-view without practicing either good or evil, remaining aloof and evenly poised: though this is called "even poise," one still praises what one favors and condemns what one dislikes, taking this very evenness as grounds for self-elevation. One should recognize, then, that this very evenness itself becomes a source of affliction. Should dhyāna be attained and the view manifest, this follows the same pattern as before; and should Vedic learning likewise be mastered, covertly absorbing the Buddha's own teaching to embellish this indeterminate position, snarling and biting, baying and barking, immeasurable fetters arising from this very moral indeterminacy. This person's own practice, again, follows only this single path, while conversion of others still admits of the same four modes. Should dhyāna fail to manifest, karma drags such a person down into an evil path; should dhyāna manifest, he is instead reborn in accordance with this dhyāna. Should this particular karma not yet have ripened, whichever karma from a former life proves strongest draws him onward first. One should recognize, then, that none of these views is yet able to subdue affliction at all — how, then, could affliction possibly be severed through them? The corresponding marks of merit and fault for the remaining position, both-existence-and-non-existence, may be understood according to this same pattern.

Next, the shared teaching's own four gates: their very substance is correct dharma, standing near the conjured city on the way toward the ultimate destination. Where the earlier gates were roundabout, this one proceeds directly — though skillful and clumsy in different measure, the point of passage through them is, in the end, no different at all. It is like the heavenly gate opening directly onto splendor while every other gate remains circuitous and cramped — yet, provided one does not become fixed on either gate exclusively, one is able to pass through and advance regardless; whereas, should one instead spend one's time counting tiles and timber, both gates alike become mere sources of delay and obstruction. Should one not linger and become fixed within any single dharma-gate, and remain free of contention and attachment with respect to either cause or fruit, this is properly named "the mind free of clinging" — not even clinging to this very freedom-from-clinging as a dharma in itself — and heterodox view thereby fails to arise. Furthermore, should one cling with a clinging mind even to this direct gate, heterodox view arises here as well — whether for the sake of reputation, for the sake of a following, for the sake of feeling superior, or for the sake of material gain, one distinguishes the marks of the gates from one another, and anger, craving, and the fetter of pride thereby arise from this very distinction. It is like mixing poison into a genuinely good medicine — how could this fail to result in death? Taking up the poison of view and carrying it into the correct Dharma itself, one only increases suffering and its origin thereby — though this is no fault of the Tathāgata's own teaching. A sharp-faculty outsider, entering the correct mark by way of a heterodox mark, is thereby led from clinging into freedom-from-clinging, becoming a genuine disciple of the Buddha; a dull-faculty adherent from within, entering into heterodoxy by way of the correct mark, is thereby led from freedom-from-clinging back into clinging, becoming instead a heterodox disciple — is this not truly lamentable? The distinct and perfect teachings' own four gates, whether skillful or clumsy, sharp or dull, all alike lead through to ultimate nirvāṇa. Where the cause remains free of clinging, the fruit remains free of contention; but should one shut oneself up within a single gate and give rise to view there, affliction arises all the same, no different from the outsider Aulūkya's own school. Considering the matter in this light, it is like a person of clear sight standing at the very confluence of the Jing and Wei rivers — how could such a person possibly mistake the name of one river for the other, or fail to distinguish the clear water from the muddy? In brief outline, the manifestation of views comes to five successive rounds; each round yields four; this yields, in turn, twenty gates; each gate yields seven — together amounting to one hundred and forty distinct ways in which a view may be attained. Discussed in full, these are without measure, each arising through its own particular causes and conditions. It is precisely because calming is cultivated in general that the various dhyānas are able to manifest; and precisely because contemplation is cultivated in general that the various views are able to manifest. The general condition for their cultivation lies in calming-and-contemplation itself, while the specific root cause underlying each must necessarily trace back to some former life — whether one studied among the outsiders entirely outside the teaching, or studied as a disciple of the Buddha within either the greater or lesser vehicle, or once manifested such views through hearing the characteristics of the Dharma, or manifested these very views through the practice of seated meditation. Separated by rebirth, this is forgotten and set aside, and the corresponding understanding fails to manifest again. Now, cultivating a stilled mind, or hearing the sūtras and treatises, one's own habituation from the past is thereby stirred, and the corresponding view or dharma arises once more. What was already ripe in a former life manifests easily now; what remained raw and unripe in a former life manifests only with difficulty now. The more distant the intervening rebirths, the more difficult; the nearer, the easier. Should the outer, heterodox view be both ripe and recent, it manifests first; should the inner view be both ripe and recent, it manifests first instead. Since spiritual power and Vedic learning are themselves concrete, external matters, they are easily forgotten across rebirth, and manifest only with difficulty; view, by contrast, is of the very nature of intelligence itself, difficult to forget, and manifests easily. It is like a person long separated from another, who remembers the other's name yet forgets their face — the relative ease and difficulty between concrete matters and underlying principle is exactly the same. Should there have existed, in a former life, some demonic connection from without, a demon then adds its own influence, manifesting demonic dhyāna and demonic view; should there have existed a sagely connection from without instead, a sage then adds his own influence, manifesting correct dhyāna and correct view.

Furthermore, should one not yet have recognized the harm views produce, one might grow fearful at the mere manifestation of a view and rush to sever it in haste; but now, having recognized its heterodox mark, one ought to take care never to sever it too abruptly — allowing it instead to reach its own full development, and using it as an auxiliary support to the path, will bring immense benefit. It is like a tapeworm dwelling in the belly, which one ought first to nurture and allow to grow, only later purging it out entirely, whole and intact, like a string of pearls. Why is this so? The foolish people of this world, obtuse as cattle or horses, gain no benefit at all even from thunderous proclamations of the Dharma or lavish displays of embroidered brocade — hearing and seeing such things leaves them entirely unmoved. Their indulgent attachment to the five desires is like one afflicted with a tapeworm; and should views then manifest in them, these views' own devouring of dull proclivities may be likened to this same tapeworm. Yet the wisdom of view stands close beside correct contemplation itself — hearing the Dharma taught, such a person awakens easily, like that very tapeworm dissolved by a broth of ground pearl. For this very reason, one must nurture the view and refine the mind through it, letting it serve as a vanguard opening the way toward genuine understanding. Should one thereby enter the two lesser vehicles, the branches of the path are cultivated by moving through this very view; should one enter the Mahāyāna instead, the branches of the path are cultivated without being moved by this view at all. Only once the invading bandit has been confronted and defeated is the true achievement accomplished. This is nurturing an outer view so as to make of it one's own attendant. Should the Tripiṭaka teaching's own clumsy four-gate view manifest, or the shared teaching's own skillful four-gate view, though the view itself remains an obstruction, its assistance to the path proves correspondingly deep. As for meritorious dharmas generally, ascending to the heavens through them is quite easy, while attaining the path through them proves difficult; view, by contrast, being of the very nature of intelligence, one may sink through it just as easily, yet awaken to the path through it with equal speed. The *Great Treatise* says: "Even the three evil paths contain those who attain the path — it is simply that, being so few, this is not commonly spoken of. A white person with a single black mole is still not, on that account, called a black person." Once one recognizes this as merely a view, delusion is thereby prevented from arising further, and one may allow it free rein to develop and discriminate. It is like the various outsiders who already possessed a mind of view before being converted by the Buddha — the very instant they glimpsed the shadow of his whip, like swift horses, they attained awakening at once. Were there no such view present at all, ten thousand blows of the axe could not break through to them — it would be like preaching the Dharma to cattle or horses, who could never comprehend it at all; a savage tribesman who understands not a word of the language — how could one possibly discuss profound matters with him? Hence the Buddha does not even appear in the world for such a person, but instead divides his own form and scatters his substance, becoming teacher and friend alike, guiding such a person by way of whatever view he already holds. The very instant the Buddha's own sun first rises, the provisional serves to draw one toward the real — hearing the Dharma, one awakens at once. The *Lotus Sūtra*'s own phrase, "secretly dispatching two men," refers, in terms of doctrine, to the two provisional teachings serving as skillful means; in terms of persons, it refers to provisional manifestations equal in status to the two lesser vehicles. If even the assembled sages, in all their own circuitous skillful means, still teach by way of a person's existing view, how, then, could one possibly rush to eliminate a view the very moment it manifests? Should one, in a former life, have already cultivated the distinct and perfect teachings' own eight gates without yet having severed the shared afflictions, and should such a view now manifest, its fault would be comparable to that of the three outer schools; should one, in a former life, have already broken through the shared afflictions without yet having awakened to the distinct teaching's own principle, this might instead be comparable to the two lesser vehicles. If even these earlier views are still to be nurtured, how much more so this present one? The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* takes up the two lesser vehicles' own faulty aspect and assigns it to the category of the outsiders; yet it also takes up their auxiliary aspect and employs it as an attendant. One must understand this both ways, advancing and withdrawing in turn, not fixing upon a single interpretation exclusively. Should the mind of view manifest in the course of one's present practice of the path, genuine principle may then be anticipated in due course; should no view manifest at all, the domain of the sages remains difficult indeed to encounter.

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the discursible object: emptiness-view generates the dharmas of all ten realms. A bitter cucumber is not itself hot, and yet it is able to serve as the cause of feverish illness; emptiness itself is not any of the ten realms, and yet it is able to serve as their very cause and condition. The *Satyasiddhi* says: "The very instant a partial view arises in the mind, this is already unwholesome." The *Abhidharma*, by contrast, clarifies that the instantaneous arising of a partial view in the mind is not, in itself, either wholesome or unwholesome, and is named "morally indeterminate." Yet it is precisely from this initiating mind that every wholesome and unwholesome state subsequently arises. Here, this emptiness-view likewise carries both senses at once. Contemplated specifically, it is precisely from this initiating mind that the ten dharma-realms themselves arise. Why is this so? Formerly, before this emptiness-view had arisen, no corresponding conduct had yet occurred; now that emptiness-view has manifested, three distinct modes of conduct immediately follow, exactly as explained above. As for committing evil on account of emptiness: practicing a doctrine of no obstruction whatsoever, one no longer perceives, above, the reverent field of the sūtras and the Buddha as worthy of honor, nor, below, the very virtue of one's parents' kindness; habituating oneself to conduct fit for a naked beast, one thereby annihilates every wholesome quality, both worldly and supramundane alike. Even the icchantika, evil as he is, still retains some residue of compassionate, loving good within him — emptiness-view, by contrast, retains none whatsoever, its evil purely of the highest three grades. Rebellion, murder, and injury constitute the hell-realm; utter shamelessness and lack of remorse constitute the animal realm; stinginess, greed, breaking of the fast, and living by impure means constitute the hungry-ghost realm — breaking the fast brings constant hunger, and impurity brings the devouring of filth. As for practicing good on account of emptiness: upholding the precepts, practicing austerities, adorning oneself with the ten wholesome deeds, the three karmic actions thereby growing pure and mature, this constitutes the three wholesome paths; and, further manifesting the root dhyānas, this constitutes the form realm. Further, as for the śrāvaka realm arising on account of emptiness: one who merely speaks of "emptiness" in this way, in truth, fails to recognize the Four Noble Truths as they exist within genuine emptiness at all. Why is this so? Should one realize the Dharma-nature itself, this is both emptiness and purity together; but this illusory, false emptiness-view necessarily still depends upon a retributive result, and this retributive result is itself defiled, polluted form. The *Larger Prajñāpāramitā* says: "Whether form is held to be permanent or impermanent, both positions alike still depend upon form itself — receiving this into oneself as 'emptiness' is precisely what is meant here, and nothing else." Grasping at the mere appearance of emptiness, distinguishing it from the dharma of existence, taking emptiness as one's object and giving rise to the three modes of conduct described above, discriminating this very mind of emptiness as superior to every other dharma — this itself is precisely "the five aggregates." Emptiness taken as an object corresponding to the mental faculty constitutes the second of the sense-fields; adding mental consciousness to this yields the three realms in miniature. The elements, sense-fields, and aggregates thus constituted are themselves precisely the truth of suffering. Emptiness-view becomes, in turn, the very locus of anger, of craving, and of pride. Should one whose view of existence proves comparatively weak encounter this emptiness-view, it claws apart his dharma of existence and drags his own reasoning over into emptiness, so that doubt is prevented from arising at all in him; but should this clawing fail to break through, and this dragging-over fail to succeed, snarling contention gives rise to doubt instead. And, further, even where no doubt is present now, great doubt is bound to arise later. Why is this so? Should emptiness genuinely be the underlying truth itself, it ought properly to be equal to the sages' own realization — and, since it plainly is not equal to it, how could doubt possibly fail to arise eventually? Who, then, is it that conceives of emptiness in this way? It is the self that conceives of it. Yet the self is, in reality, not emptiness, and emptiness, in turn, is not the self — and yet, on the basis of this very emptiness, a self is nonetheless generated: "my own practice," "my own understanding," praising oneself, condemning oneself. Clinging to this very extreme of emptiness, one finds oneself unable to relinquish it. Holding that this very path of emptiness leads through to nirvāṇa, one takes emptiness itself as one's moral precept, distinct from the precepts against killing, theft, and the rest observed by ordinary people. Positing a cause where none exists, this is grasping after precepts and rituals as a cause where none belongs; conceiving of emptiness as "emptiness" in this fixed way is, in truth, not genuine, principled emptiness at all — positing a fruit where none exists, this is grasping after view as a fruit where none belongs. Emptiness-view, thus skewed and one-sided, is itself precisely wrong view. From this very emptiness-view arise these ten proclivities in the following distribution: beneath the truth of suffering in the desire realm, all ten are present in full; beneath the truth of origin, seven are present, excluding the view of self, the extreme views, and the grasping at precepts and rituals; beneath the truth of the path, eight are present, excluding the view of self and the extreme views; beneath the truth of cessation, seven are present, excluding the view of self, the extreme views, and the grasping at precepts and rituals — thirty-two in total within the desire realm. In the form and formless realms, each excludes the four instances of anger as well, yielding twenty-eight in each — together amounting to the full eighty-eight proclivities. This is named "the truth of origin."

集迷苦起，苦由集生，苦集流轉，長爪不識。復有一鬼頭上火然，非想已來，尚自未免，何得於空不識苦集？若識空見苦集，苦集皆依於色，一切色法名「身」。身色污穢，污穢是不淨，智者所惡，破於淨倒，名「身念處」。若受空見，是受不受，受第二句。順空即樂受；違空即苦受；不違不順即不苦不樂受。三受即三苦，計苦為樂，是名顛倒。若知無樂，破樂顛倒，名「受念處」。空塵對心而生意識。此心生滅新新流動；有緣思生，無緣思不生。生滅無常而謂是常，即是顛倒。識識無常，即破常倒，名「心念處」。取空像貌而行善惡，行中計我。行若是我，行有好惡、行有興廢，我亦應爾。諸行無量，我若遍者我則無量；若不遍者，則一行無我、眾行亦無我。強計有我，即是顛倒。若知無我，則破想、行，名「法念處」。但諸陰，通計四倒。於想、行，計我強；於色，計淨強；於心，計常強；於受，計樂強，名「別念處」。若總念處則不爾也。是為空見生念處觀。勤破倒觀，即見「正勤」；定心中修，名「如意足」；五善根生，名為「根」；破五惑，名為「力」；安隱道用，名「七覺」；安隱道中行，名「八正道」。是為空見能生道諦。四倒除故，是癡滅；癡滅故，愛滅；愛滅故，瞋滅；瞋滅故，知空非道，慚愧低頭，則是慢滅；無復所執，則疑滅。空見既具苦集，苦集非畢竟空，執空心破故求我叵得。我叵得故，則身見破；身見破故，則我見破；我見破故，邊見破；空見非道，戒取破；空非涅涅，見取破；空不當理，邪見破。十使破故，八十八使破；八十八使破故，子縛破；子縛破故，能發初果進成無學；果縛破，入無餘涅槃。是為空見生滅諦，即聲聞法界也。若於空見明識四諦，則知盡苦真道；真道伏斷，得成賢聖。乃至一百四十種見，單、複、具足、無言等見，皆識真道；於諸見中能動能出。若不爾者，不見四真諦，是故久流轉生死大苦海。若能見四諦，則得斷生死。生有既盡已，更不受諸有。即此意也。

Ru-Yi Translation

This truth of origin, deluded regarding suffering, gives rise to suffering, and suffering, in turn, arises from origin — suffering and origin thus flowing onward together in transmigration, something the long-nailed Brahmin himself never recognized. There is, further, a story of a ghost with fire blazing atop its head — even reaching all the way up to the peak of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, one still cannot escape this fate; how, then, could one possibly fail to recognize suffering and its origin merely on account of clinging to emptiness? Should one recognize the suffering and origin present within this very emptiness-view, one recognizes that suffering and origin both alike depend upon form — every form-dharma being named "body." The body's own form, being polluted, and pollution being itself impurity, is something the wise abhor, breaking through the inversion of taking it for pure — this is named "the foundation of mindfulness of the body." As for the feeling proper to emptiness-view, this is a feeling that is, properly speaking, neither straightforwardly a feeling nor entirely without feeling, occupying the second of these two propositions. Compliance with emptiness produces pleasant feeling; opposition to it produces painful feeling; neither compliance nor opposition produces feeling that is neither painful nor pleasant. These three feelings are themselves the three forms of suffering — taking suffering to be pleasure is itself precisely an inversion. Recognizing that there is, in truth, no pleasure here at all breaks through this inversion of pleasure — this is named "the foundation of mindfulness of feeling." Emptiness, taken as an object corresponding to the mind, gives rise to mental consciousness. This very mind arises and ceases, ever freshly flowing onward — arising when there is an object to think upon, not arising when there is none. Taking this arising and ceasing, impermanent as it is, to be permanent is itself precisely an inversion. Recognizing consciousness as itself impermanent breaks through this inversion of permanence — this is named "the foundation of mindfulness of mind." Grasping at the mere appearance of emptiness while engaging in good or evil conduct, one conceives of a self within this very conduct. Were this conduct itself the self, then, since conduct admits of good and bad, of arising and ceasing, the self, too, would have to admit of the same. Yet formations are themselves without measure — were the self to pervade them all, the self, too, would be without measure; were it not to pervade them all, then any single formation would be without self, and every formation, taken together, would likewise be without self. Forcibly conceiving of a self under these conditions is itself precisely an inversion. Recognizing that there is no self breaks through this inversion with respect to perception and formation — this is named "the foundation of mindfulness of dharmas." Now, with respect to the aggregates generally, all four inversions apply throughout — yet, with respect to perception and formation specifically, the conception of self predominates; with respect to form, the conception of purity predominates; with respect to mind, the conception of permanence predominates; with respect to feeling, the conception of pleasure predominates — this distinction is named "the particular foundations of mindfulness," as distinct from "the general foundation of mindfulness," where this distinction does not apply. This, then, is emptiness-view giving rise to the contemplation of the foundations of mindfulness. Diligently breaking through this inverted view through contemplation is itself "right diligence"; cultivated within a settled mind, it is named "the bases of supernormal power"; the five wholesome roots thereby generated are named "the faculties"; breaking through the five kinds of affliction is named "the powers"; the secure and settled function of the path is named "the seven factors of enlightenment"; and conduct proceeding along this secure and settled path is named "the eightfold noble path." This is emptiness-view giving rise to the truth of the path. Because the four inversions are thereby removed, folly is extinguished; folly extinguished, craving is extinguished; craving extinguished, anger is extinguished; anger extinguished, one recognizes that emptiness is not itself the true path, and, bowing one's head in shame and remorse, pride, too, is thereby extinguished; with nothing further left to cling to, doubt, too, is extinguished. Since this emptiness-view already contains suffering and origin within it, and suffering and origin are not, in fact, ultimate emptiness at all, once the very mind clinging to emptiness is broken through, the self one sought is found to be entirely unobtainable. Since the self proves unobtainable, the view of self is thereby broken through; the view of self broken through, the very conceit of "I" is thereby broken through; the conceit of "I" broken through, extreme views are broken through; recognizing that emptiness is not itself the path, grasping at precepts and rituals is broken through; recognizing that emptiness is not itself nirvāṇa, grasping at views is broken through; recognizing that emptiness does not, in fact, correspond to the true principle, wrong view itself is broken through. These ten proclivities thus broken through, the full eighty-eight proclivities are broken through; the eighty-eight proclivities broken through, the bondage of the seed is broken through; the bondage of the seed broken through, one is thereby able to manifest the first fruit and advance to the stage beyond further training; the bondage of the fruit then broken through, one enters nirvāṇa without remainder. This is emptiness-view giving rise to the truth of cessation — this being precisely the dharma-realm of the śrāvaka. Should one, by way of emptiness-view, come to a clear understanding of the Four Noble Truths, one thereby knows the genuine path that exhausts suffering; this genuine path, subduing and severing affliction, brings one to the accomplishment of noble sagehood. Extending even to all one hundred and forty kinds of view — single, compound, complete, or beyond-words — every one of them, in this same way, may come to recognize the genuine path; within these very views themselves, one is able both to be moved by them and to escape through them. Should this not occur, one fails to perceive the Four Noble Truths at all, and thus continues to flow onward, for a long time, within the great sea of suffering that is birth-and-death. Should one be able to perceive the Four Noble Truths, one thereby attains the severing of birth-and-death — the very existence one is born into now already exhausted, one no longer receives any further mode of existence at all. This carries precisely this same sense.

Emptiness-View Giving Rise to the Pratyekabuddha and the Bodhisattva of the Six Pāramitās 空見生支佛 · 空見生六度

次、明空見生支佛者：空見非空，妄謂是空，顛倒分別，倒即是無明。無明故，取著空見；若知無明，何所取著？若知無明，不起取、有，畢故不造新；不造新、不起取、有，畢故是不起無明；若無無明，則成智明。故有智慧時則無煩惱；無煩惱時則無明滅；無明滅則諸行滅，乃至老死滅。《中論》云：「云何聲聞觀十二因緣義？乃說常、無常等六十二見。」問答殆不相應。今秖此是答。常、無常等見皆是無明；知無明，不起取、有，即是聲聞法中十二因緣觀。《法華》云：「樂獨善寂，求自然慧。」此慧善寂六十二見也。又觀剎那空見既具四諦，此空見心為有、為無？剎那心起，便具五陰，云何言無？此即有支。有即含果，亦是因中有果義；若作無果者，有支有因，因義具足，有從何生？若無取者，有則不生。取即五見：執空是邊、於空計我、謂空為道、為涅槃、為正，是為取支。取從愛生，愛喜、違瞋、慢彼、疑此，此名愛支。愛因受生，受故愛起。如受一法，愛味追求。知受因觸，以有意根空塵得觸。經云：「觸因緣故，生諸受。」觸由於入，塵觸諸根，故得於入。入由名色，歌羅邏三事：色有五胞，命能連持，識有四陰之名。又三事名色：由初託胎識，識由往業，業由無明。無明是過去顛倒，謂有、謂無一切諸見，故能成辦今世色軀。《經云》：「識種、業田、愛水，無明覆蔽，生名色芽。」今復顛倒，迷於空見，起善惡行，種於未來名色之芽。顛倒又顛倒，無明又無明，更相因緣無有窮已。若知無明顛倒，不須推畫若有、若無，達其體性本自不實，妄想因緣和合故有；既知顛倒，無明即寢；寢故，諸行老死皆寢。空見無明、老、死寢者，一百四十諸見無明、老、死皆寢。寢故，是破二十五有、侵除習氣，是名「空見生支佛法界」。若於空見，識是無明，無明可滅。若不識者，尚不出空見。為見造業，如蠶作繭，何得成支佛耶？鼻隔禪師發得空見，多墮網中不能自拔；散心法師雖分別諸使，亦不自知空見過患。闇證凡龜，盲狗穭吠，自行化他全無道氣。空見生六度菩薩法者：既識空見諦、緣，即是知病、識藥。識藥故自欣，知病故愍彼，欲共眾生離苦求樂。空見陰界是苦，十使等是集，念處等是道，四倒破是滅；約此起誓。如一空見，一日一夜凡生幾許百千億陰，一一五陰即是眾生。日夜既爾，何況一世？何況無量世？空見既爾，餘見亦然。能生之見既多，所生之陰則不可數。一人尚爾，何況多人？是為「眾生無邊誓願度」。如一空見念念八十八使；餘三見、六十二等，亦八十八使。一人尚爾，何況多人？是名「煩惱無量誓願斷」。如一空見修念處道品，餘一切見正助之道無量無邊。一人尚爾，多人亦然。是為「法門無盡誓願知」。如一空見煩惱滅；無量見，無量煩惱亦滅。一人既爾，諸人亦然。是名「無上佛道誓願成」。若眾生苦集是性實者，則不可度；以苦集從因緣生，無有自性故，苦海可乾，集源易竭，故言「度」耳。觀空起願如上說。約空起行者：若執空見而行布施者，乃是魔施；知空見諦緣無常、無我等過，則捨空見；亦愍於他，勸捨空見而行布施。若執空見而持戒者，與持雞、狗等戒何異？知空見無常等過，不為空見所傷，慈愍於他，令防空見。若執空見為瞋處、愛處強行忍者，是力不足，畏他故忍。今知空見無量過患，能伏空見及六十二；亦勸於他安忍空見。若不除空見而精進者，雜見非精；退入三途非進。今知空見，空見不起為「精」；空見業破而得升出名「進」。亦勸於他修此精進。若不破空見得禪者，多是鬼法。今知空過，不為空見所動，成正禪、正通，不為諂媚憍利；以此神通勸化眾生，令捨見散入禪。若執空見而修智慧，愚癡世智。今識空見諦緣，以無常狼怖空見羊，煩惱脂銷；廣起願行，功德身肥，悲愍眾生，令除脂長肉。若有緣機熟，即坐道場、斷結作佛。是名「空見生六度法界」。

Next, clarifying emptiness-view giving rise to the pratyekabuddha realm: emptiness-view is not genuine emptiness at all, but is mistakenly supposed to be so — this inverted discrimination is itself precisely ignorance. Because of this ignorance, one grasps and clings to emptiness-view; yet, should one recognize this as ignorance, what would there be left to grasp and cling to? Should one recognize this ignorance, one no longer gives rise to grasping and existence, and, this cycle exhausted, produces nothing new; producing nothing new, no longer giving rise to grasping and existence, and this cycle exhausted, ignorance itself no longer arises; and, ignorance being absent, wisdom's own light is thereby accomplished. Hence, at the moment wisdom is present, affliction is absent; affliction being absent, ignorance is thereby extinguished; ignorance extinguished, every formation is thereby extinguished, extending all the way through to the extinguishing of old age and death. The *Madhyamaka-śāstra* asks: "How does the śrāvaka contemplate the sense of the twelve links of dependent origination?" — and yet proceeds to speak of the sixty-two views concerning permanence, impermanence, and the rest, so that question and answer seem almost entirely unrelated. Here, however, it should be noted: this is, in fact, precisely the answer. Views of permanence, impermanence, and the rest are all, themselves, ignorance; recognizing this ignorance, and no longer giving rise to grasping and existence, is precisely the contemplation of the twelve links of dependent origination within the śrāvaka's own teaching. The *Lotus Sūtra* says: "Delighting in solitary quiescence, seeking spontaneous wisdom" — this very wisdom being the quiescence of these same sixty-two views. Further, contemplating this instantaneous emptiness-view as already complete with the Four Noble Truths — is this mind of emptiness-view itself existent, or non-existent? Since this instantaneous mind arises already complete with the five aggregates, how could it possibly be called non-existent? This, then, is precisely the link of "existence." Existence, in turn, contains its own fruit within it — this being likewise the sense of the effect already existing within the cause; were one instead to hold that it contains no fruit, then, existence already possessing its cause, this causal sense being already complete, from where, then, would existence itself have arisen? Were there no grasping present, existence itself would fail to arise at all. Grasping is itself these five views: clinging to emptiness as an extreme, conceiving of a self within emptiness, holding emptiness to be the path, to be nirvāṇa, to be correct — this constitutes the link of "grasping." Grasping arises, in turn, from craving — delighting in what accords with it, resenting what opposes it, disdaining one thing, doubting another — this is named the link of "craving." Craving arises, in turn, from feeling, since it is on account of feeling that craving arises — as when, receiving a single dharma into oneself, one savors it with craving and pursues it further. One recognizes that this feeling arises, in turn, from contact — since it is through the presence of the mental faculty and the object of emptiness together that contact comes to be. The sūtra says: "Through the causal condition of contact, every feeling arises." Contact arises, in turn, from the sense-fields, since it is through objects coming into contact with the faculties that the sense-fields themselves come to be. The sense-fields arise, in turn, from name-and-form — the three constituent factors of the *kalala* stage: form possessing its five nascent protuberances, life-force sustaining and connecting them, and consciousness bearing the name of the four mental aggregates. And these same three factors constitute name-and-form: arising from the very first consciousness taking rebirth in the womb, consciousness arising, in turn, from past karma, karma arising, in turn, from ignorance. This ignorance is precisely the inverted view carried over from the past, positing existence, positing non-existence, and every other view besides — and it is precisely this that is able to bring about the very physical body of the present life. The sūtra says: "The seed of consciousness, the field of karma, and the water of craving — all obscured over by ignorance — thereby give rise to the very sprout of name-and-form." Here, once again, inverted, deluded by this same emptiness-view, one gives rise to good and evil conduct, thereby planting the very sprout of name-and-form for a future life. Inversion upon inversion, ignorance upon ignorance, this mutual dependency continuing on without any end whatsoever. Should one recognize this ignorance as itself an inversion, there is no need to calculate further whether it is existent or non-existent — one need only penetrate to its own underlying nature, recognizing that it was, from the very start, never genuinely real at all, arising only through the coincidence of causes and conditions bound up with delusive thought. Once this inversion is thus recognized, ignorance itself is thereby put to rest; and, being put to rest, every formation, and old age and death as well, are all likewise put to rest. Emptiness-view's own ignorance, old age, and death being thus put to rest, the ignorance, old age, and death belonging to all one hundred and forty kinds of view are, in this same way, likewise put to rest. Being thus put to rest, this breaks through the twenty-five modes of existence, and removes even their underlying habituation — this is named "emptiness-view giving rise to the dharma-realm of the pratyekabuddha." Should one, within emptiness-view itself, recognize this as ignorance, this very ignorance becomes capable of extinguishing; should one fail to recognize it as such, one fails even to escape emptiness-view itself. Producing karma through this very view is like a silkworm spinning its own cocoon around itself — how, then, could one possibly attain pratyekabuddhahood through it? Meditation masters closed off from proper guidance who manifest this emptiness-view for the most part fall into its very net, unable to extricate themselves; and lecture-masters given to scattered discursiveness, though they may skillfully distinguish every proclivity in theory, likewise fail to recognize the harm of emptiness-view in their own case. Darkly realized, like an ordinary tortoise, or a blind dog barking at nothing — both their own practice and their conversion of others alike are entirely devoid of the very spirit of the path. As for emptiness-view giving rise to the dharma of the bodhisattva of the six pāramitās: having already recognized the truth and the causal condition underlying emptiness-view, this is itself precisely knowing the illness and identifying the remedy. Knowing the remedy, one rejoices in oneself; knowing the illness, one feels compassion for others, wishing to join with every sentient being in departing from suffering and seeking happiness together. Emptiness-view's own aggregates and elements are suffering; its ten proclivities and the rest are origin; its foundations of mindfulness and the rest are the path; the breaking-through of its four inversions is cessation — and it is on the basis of these very four truths that the vow is given rise to. Consider a single instance of emptiness-view: within a single day and night, how many hundreds of thousands, even hundreds of millions, of aggregates does it give rise to — each single set of five aggregates being itself a sentient being? Since this holds even within a single day and night, how much more so across an entire lifetime — how much more so, further, across immeasurable lifetimes? As this holds for emptiness-view, so too does it hold for every other view. Since the views capable of generating such aggregates are themselves so numerous, the aggregates thereby generated are utterly beyond counting — and if this holds even for a single person, how much more so for the multitude of all persons together? This is precisely "sentient beings are numberless, I vow to deliver them all." As a single instance of emptiness-view carries, thought after thought, the full eighty-eight proclivities, so too do the remaining three views, and the sixty-two views, and the rest, each likewise carry these same eighty-eight proclivities — and if this holds even for a single person, how much more so for the multitude? This is named "afflictions are inexhaustible, I vow to sever them all." As a single instance of emptiness-view cultivates the branches of the path through the foundations of mindfulness, so too does every other view generate its own immeasurable, boundless path of direct and auxiliary practice — and if this holds even for a single person, so too does it hold for the multitude. This is precisely "the dharma-gates are boundless, I vow to master them all." As a single instance of emptiness-view has its affliction extinguished, so too do the immeasurable views have their immeasurable afflictions likewise extinguished — and if this holds even for a single person, so too does it hold for every person alike. This is named "the supreme Buddha-path, I vow to accomplish it." Were the suffering and origin of sentient beings possessed of some fixed, inherent reality, they could never be delivered at all — but since suffering and origin arise entirely through causes and conditions, possessing no inherent nature of their own, the very sea of suffering can be dried up, the very source of origin easily exhausted — hence one properly speaks of "delivering" them at all. Contemplating emptiness and thereby giving rise to the vow proceeds exactly as described above. As for giving rise to actual conduct on the basis of emptiness: should one cling to emptiness-view while practicing generosity, this is nothing but demonic generosity; recognizing the faults of impermanence, selflessness, and the rest present within the truth and causal condition of this very emptiness-view, one relinquishes emptiness-view instead, and, further, feels compassion for others, urging them likewise to relinquish emptiness-view and practice generosity in its place. Should one cling to emptiness-view while upholding the precepts, how would this differ from a dog or a chicken observing its own peculiar restrictions? Recognizing the faults of impermanence and the rest present within emptiness-view, one is no longer harmed by it, and, with compassion for others, guards them against it as well. Should one cling to emptiness-view as the very locus of anger and craving, forcing oneself merely to endure it, this is a case of insufficient inner strength, enduring only out of fear of others' judgment. Here, by contrast, recognizing the immeasurable faults of emptiness-view, one is able to subdue emptiness-view together with all sixty-two related views, and further urges others toward this same genuine forbearance with respect to emptiness-view. Should one fail to remove emptiness-view while claiming to practice diligence, this is a diligence adulterated by view, not genuine diligence at all — retreating into the three lower paths is no genuine advance either. Here, by contrast, recognizing emptiness-view for what it is, preventing emptiness-view from arising at all is properly named "refined diligence"; and the karma of emptiness-view, once broken through, allowing one to rise up and escape it entirely, is properly named "advancing diligence" — and one further urges others to cultivate this same diligence. Should one attain dhyāna without having broken through emptiness-view, this dhyāna, for the most part, proves to be demonic dharma. Here, by contrast, recognizing the fault of emptiness, one remains unmoved by emptiness-view, thereby accomplishing genuine dhyāna and genuine spiritual power, free of flattery, arrogance, or self-interested gain — and, by means of this very spiritual power, one exhorts and converts sentient beings, leading them to relinquish view and enter into dhyāna instead. Should one cling to emptiness-view while cultivating wisdom, this remains merely foolish, worldly intelligence. Here, by contrast, recognizing the truth and causal condition underlying emptiness-view, one uses the wolf of impermanence to frighten off the very sheep of emptiness-view, so that the fat of affliction melts away; broadly giving rise to vows and practice, the very body of one's merit thereby grows correspondingly full — with compassion for sentient beings, one leads them likewise to remove this fat and grow genuine flesh in its place. Should the conditions and capacity prove ripe, one thereupon sits in the very place of enlightenment, severs every fetter, and accomplishes buddhahood itself. This is named "emptiness-view giving rise to the dharma-realm of the six pāramitās."

觀空見即是無明，無明即空，從無明生一切苦集，皆不可得。何者？四倒是橫計，寧有性實？所治之倒非有，能治念處云何可得？乃至覺道皆悉不生，故不可得。故《大品》云「習應苦空」等云云。二乘知即空，斷苦入滅；菩薩即空，慈悲願行，誓度眾生。雖度眾生，如度虛空；雖滅煩惱，如與空共鬪；雖生法門，如虛空生；雖滅眾生，實無眾生得滅度者。是智、是斷，是菩薩無生法忍。是名「空見生通教菩薩法界」也。觀此空見有無量相。所謂四諦，分別校計不可窮盡。此無盡者從空見生；空見從無明生。所生無量，能生亦無量。能生既假名，所生亦是假名。推此無明從法性生，譬如尋夢，知由於眠。觀此空見而識實相，實相即如來藏。無量客塵覆此藏理，修恒沙法門顯清淨性。是名「空見生別教法」也。空見生圓教法，如前、如後。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contemplating emptiness-view as itself precisely ignorance, and ignorance as itself precisely emptiness, one recognizes that every instance of suffering and its origin arising from this ignorance proves, in every case, entirely unobtainable. Why is this so? The four inversions are themselves mere arbitrary conception — how could they possibly possess any fixed, inherent reality? The very inversion to be corrected is not truly existent, so how could the corrective foundations of mindfulness themselves possibly be obtained either? Extending even to the path of enlightenment itself, nothing whatsoever genuinely arises, and hence nothing is ultimately obtainable at all. Hence the *Larger Prajñāpāramitā* says: "One should train oneself in suffering as itself emptiness," and so on. The two lesser vehicles, recognizing this very emptiness, sever suffering and enter cessation; the bodhisattva, recognizing this same emptiness, gives rise to compassion, vows, and practice instead, vowing to deliver every sentient being. Though he delivers sentient beings, this is like delivering empty space itself; though he extinguishes affliction, this is like contending against emptiness itself; though he generates dharma-gates, this is like empty space itself generating them; though he brings sentient beings to extinction, there is, in truth, no sentient being whatsoever who is actually brought to extinction. This very wisdom, this very severance, is precisely the bodhisattva's own forbearance of the unborn. This is named "emptiness-view giving rise to the dharma-realm of the bodhisattva of the shared teaching." Contemplating this same emptiness-view as possessing immeasurable aspects — namely, the Four Noble Truths, whose distinctions and calculations prove utterly inexhaustible. This very inexhaustibility arises from emptiness-view; emptiness-view, in turn, arises from ignorance. What is thereby generated is immeasurable, and what generates it is likewise immeasurable. Since what generates it is itself but a provisional designation, what is thereby generated is likewise but a provisional designation. Tracing this ignorance back further still, one finds it arises from the Dharma-nature itself — comparable to searching within a dream, only to recognize that it arises, in truth, from sleep. Contemplating this very emptiness-view, one comes to recognize true reality itself, true reality being precisely the treasury of the Tathāgata. Immeasurable adventitious dust obscures this very treasury-principle — cultivating dharma-gates as numerous as the sands of the Ganges reveals its own pure nature in turn. This is named "emptiness-view giving rise to the dharma of the distinct teaching." Emptiness-view giving rise to the dharma of the perfect teaching follows the same pattern as what has already been explained, both before and after this point.

Four Medicines for One Illness: The Four Teachings' Own Foundations of Mindfulness, and the Burning House 四教四念處 · 三車喻

Chinese Source

復次，見惑浩浩，如四十里水；思惑殘勢，如一渧水。前諸方便共治見惑，惑盡名為「入流」，任運不退。見惑難除，巧須方便。《成論》云：「以空治惑。」若空治得入，不俟餘法；若不入者，更設何治？如水中生火，水不能滅；空見起過，空何能治？今知空見苦集之病，然後用諦智治之。三藏無常智、通家即空智，皆前除見。別亦前除見入空，次善巧出假，如空中種樹。圓雖不作意除見，見自前除。除堅牢見，種種方治，云何直言但以空治邪？云何諸治共治一見？如患冷用四種藥：服薑桂者，去病復力；服五石者，病去益色；服重婁者，加壽能飛；服金丹者，成大仙人。病同一種，藥法為異，得力亦異。四教治見，見盡解異。治見既爾，治餘亦然。此四治者，即是四念處。遺教令依四念處修道，得出火宅。所以者何？一空見心即三界。「三界無別法，唯是一心作。」空見生六道業，受六道身，居六道處。處即火宅，身居即苦具，業即鬼神。競共推排，三車自運，乃得出耳。三車，即是三藏中三乘念處；亦是通中三人共一念處；又是別方便中三種念處，真實一種念處；又圓一實念處。略說，九種四念處；中說，九種道品；廣說，九種四諦。是諸念處皆能治見，得出火宅。遺囑之意，義在於此。但釋迦初出，先示三人，各用四念處。此如《法華》，羊、鹿、牛車，各出火宅。次說三人同修一念處。此如《大品》「是乘從三界出，到薩婆若中住。」亦如《大集》「三乘之人同以無言說道斷煩惱。」次說菩薩修次第念處。此如《大品》「不共般若」，諸念處乘，別而未合。後說一切小大同一念處。此如《法華》「同乘大車，直至道場。」約此空見明諸惑、明諸治，與諸經論不相違背。「一微塵中有大千經卷」，即此意也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the affliction of view is vast and turbulent, like a body of water forty miles across; the residual force of habituated thought, by comparison, is like a single drop of water. The various preliminary expedients discussed earlier all treat the affliction of view together — once this affliction is entirely exhausted, this is named "entering the stream," proceeding thereafter spontaneously without ever retreating. Yet the affliction of view proves difficult to remove, and skillful means are required for it. The *Satyasiddhi* says: "Emptiness is used to treat affliction." Should this treatment through emptiness prove effective, no further method is needed; but should it fail to prove effective, what further treatment could one possibly apply? It is like trying to kindle fire within water itself — water alone could never extinguish it; and, similarly, should emptiness-view itself give rise to fault, how could emptiness alone possibly serve to treat it? Here, one must first recognize the illness of suffering and origin present within emptiness-view itself, and only then apply the wisdom of the truths to treat it properly. The Tripiṭaka teaching's wisdom of impermanence, and the shared teaching's wisdom of emptiness-as-such, both alike remove view first, as a preliminary step. The distinct teaching, likewise, first removes view and enters emptiness, and only afterward skillfully emerges into the provisional, like planting a tree within empty space itself. The perfect teaching, though it makes no deliberate effort to remove view as such, finds view removed of its own accord beforehand regardless. Removing a firmly entrenched view thus requires every manner of differing treatment — how, then, could one simply declare that emptiness alone suffices to treat it in every case? And how, indeed, could every one of these different treatments possibly treat one and the same view identically? It is like treating a cold affliction with four different medicines: taking ginger and cinnamon removes the illness and restores strength; taking the five minerals removes the illness and enhances one's complexion; taking heavy asparagus root extends one's lifespan and grants the ability to fly; taking the golden elixir accomplishes the state of a great immortal. The illness is one and the same in every case, yet the medicine and method applied differ, and the resulting benefit differs correspondingly. As the Four Teachings treat view, so too does view, once exhausted, yield a correspondingly different understanding in each case — and as this holds for treating view, so too does it hold for treating every other affliction. These four treatments are themselves precisely the four foundations of mindfulness. The Buddha's own bequeathed teaching instructs disciples to rely upon the four foundations of mindfulness in cultivating the path, so as to escape the burning house. Why is this so? A single instance of emptiness-view's own mind is itself already the three realms in their entirety — "the three realms possess no separate dharma of their own; they are, in every case, simply the fabrication of a single mind." Emptiness-view generates the karma of the six paths, receiving thereby a body within one of the six paths, dwelling within one of their respective domains. This very domain is the burning house itself; the body dwelling within it is the very instrument of its suffering; and the karma involved is itself the very demon or spirit driving it onward. Contending and pushing against one another, the three carts move of their own accord, and it is only by way of them that one is finally able to escape. These three carts are precisely the three vehicles' own foundations of mindfulness within the Tripiṭaka teaching; they are likewise the single, shared foundation of mindfulness held in common by the three kinds of persons within the shared teaching; they are, further, the three kinds of foundation of mindfulness found within the distinct teaching's own preliminary expedients, together with its single, genuine foundation of mindfulness; and they are, further still, the perfect teaching's own single, real foundation of mindfulness. Stated briefly, this yields nine kinds of the fourfold foundation of mindfulness; stated at middling length, nine kinds of the branches of the path; stated at length, nine kinds of the Four Noble Truths. Every one of these foundations of mindfulness is able to treat view, and thereby bring one to escape the burning house — this is precisely the sense contained within the Buddha's own bequeathed instruction. When Śākyamuni first appeared in the world, he first demonstrated to the three kinds of persons, each in turn, their own respective use of the four foundations of mindfulness — this is comparable to the *Lotus Sūtra*'s own goat-cart, deer-cart, and ox-cart, each escaping the burning house separately. He next taught these same three kinds of persons to cultivate a single, shared foundation of mindfulness together — this is comparable to the *Larger Prajñāpāramitā*'s own teaching that "this very vehicle emerges from the three realms, and arrives to abide within the very ocean of omniscience." It is likewise comparable to the *Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra*'s own teaching that "practitioners of the three vehicles alike sever affliction by means of the very path beyond all verbal expression." He next taught the bodhisattva to cultivate the sequential foundations of mindfulness — this is comparable to the *Larger Prajñāpāramitā*'s own "prajñā not shared with the two lesser vehicles," where the various vehicles proper to the foundations of mindfulness remain distinct, not yet unified together. Finally, he taught that every being, lesser and greater vehicle alike, shares this same single foundation of mindfulness in common — this is comparable to the *Lotus Sūtra*'s own teaching of "riding together upon the great cart, directly to the very place of enlightenment." Treated in terms of this very emptiness-view, clarifying every affliction and clarifying every treatment for it in this way, one finds this in no way at odds with any of the sūtras or treatises elsewhere — "within a single mote of dust there exists the entire canon of the great chiliocosm's own scriptures" carries precisely this same sense.

The Inconceivable Object: A Single Thought of Emptiness-View Is Itself the Dharma-Nature 次明不思議境者

Chinese Source

次、明不思議境者：一念空見具十法界，即是法性；法性更非遠物，即是空見心。《淨名》云：「諸佛解脫當於眾生心行中求，當於六十二見中求。」三法不異，故宛轉相指。一切眾生即是菩提，不可復得，即圓淨解脫；五陰即是涅槃，不可復滅，即方便淨解脫；眾生如即佛如，是性淨解脫。佛解脫者，即是色解脫等五種涅槃。空見心即是污穢五陰。五陰即有眾生，眾生即有五陰，名色、眾生更互相縛，不得相離。觀此五陰即是涅槃，不可復滅；本無繫縛，即是解脫。本有解脫攝一切法，故言「解脫即心而求」。又觀見心五陰即是法性，便無復見心五陰。因滅是色，獲得常色等法性五陰；因滅眾生，獲得常住法性眾生。能一色一切色、一識一切識、一眾生一切眾生，不相妨礙，如明鏡淨，現眾色像，是名「性淨」。三種解脫不得相離，不縱不橫，不可思議，圓滿具足，空見中求，是名「不可思議境」。此境無明、法性宛然具足，傷已昏沈，今始覺知，一切眾生亦復如是。既是法性，那不起慈？既是無明，那不起悲？觀此空見本性空寂、淨若虛空。善巧安心，研此二法。見陰、見假，四句不生。單、複諸句，句句有苦集、無明蔽塞；句句有道滅等通。觀空見，一陰一切陰，三諦不動則了「法身」。觀不動陰，非淨非不淨等，雙樹涅槃亦是道場，是觀名「般若」。八倒破，名「解脫」。於一念處起一切念處，調伏眾生。如是三法非因非果。非因而因，念處是道場；非果而果，雙樹中間而入涅槃。於空見不動而修不思議三十七品。如是遍破，不得空見，名「空三昧」；不見空相，名「無相三昧」；如是三昧不從真緣生，名「無作三昧」。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, clarifying the inconceivable object: a single thought-moment of emptiness-view is already complete with all ten dharma-realms — this being precisely the Dharma-nature itself; and the Dharma-nature is, moreover, no distant thing at all, but precisely this very mind of emptiness-view. The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* says: "The liberation of every buddha is to be sought precisely within the mental activity of sentient beings — to be sought precisely within these sixty-two views themselves." These three dharmas are, in fact, not different from one another, and hence mutually point toward one another in turn. Every sentient being is itself already bodhi, never again to be attained anew — this is the perfect, pure liberation; the five aggregates are themselves already nirvāṇa, never again to be extinguished anew — this is the expedient-means, pure liberation; sentient beings' own suchness is itself the Buddha's own suchness — this is the nature-pure liberation. The Buddha's own liberation is precisely this fivefold nirvāṇa of form-liberation and the rest. The very mind of emptiness-view is precisely the polluted five aggregates; the five aggregates already imply sentient beings, and sentient beings already imply the five aggregates — name-and-form and sentient being thus mutually binding one another, unable to be separated from each other at all. Contemplating these very five aggregates as themselves already nirvāṇa, never again to be extinguished — originally free of any binding whatsoever, this is itself already liberation. This inherent liberation gathers in every dharma without exception — hence it is said, "liberation is to be sought precisely within this very mind." Further, contemplating the very mind and five aggregates of view as themselves the Dharma-nature, there ceases to be any further mind and five aggregates of view left to speak of at all. Through the very extinguishing of this form, one attains the constant form and the rest belonging to the Dharma-nature's own five aggregates; through the very extinguishing of sentient being, one attains the constantly abiding sentient being belonging to the Dharma-nature. One is thereby able to hold a single form as every form, a single consciousness as every consciousness, a single sentient being as every sentient being, without any of these obstructing one another in the slightest — like a bright, clear mirror manifesting every kind of image at once — this is named "nature-purity." These three kinds of liberation cannot be separated from one another, neither sequential nor separate, inconceivable, perfectly complete in every respect, sought precisely within emptiness-view itself — this is named "the inconceivable object." Within this very object, ignorance and the Dharma-nature are both, in fact, already fully present together — one has merely been overcome by one's own confusion until now, only now beginning to awaken and recognize this fact; and every sentient being, without exception, is exactly the same. Since this is already the Dharma-nature itself, how could loving-kindness fail to arise from it? Since this is already ignorance itself, how could compassion fail to arise from it as well? Contemplating this very emptiness-view as, in its own fundamental nature, empty and quiescent, pure as empty space itself — one skillfully pacifies the mind, refining these two very dharmas together. The aggregates as viewed, and the provisional as viewed, through the fourfold proposition, prove unborn — in every single one of these propositions, whether taken singly or in combination, there is present, at once, both the blockage of suffering, origin, and ignorance, and the passage of the path, cessation, and the rest. Contemplating emptiness-view in this way, a single aggregate is every aggregate, the three truths remaining unmoved — this alone is truly understanding "the Dharma-body." Contemplating these unmoved aggregates as neither pure nor impure, and so on, the very nirvāṇa beneath the twin trees is itself already the place of enlightenment — this very contemplation is named "prajñā." The eight inversions thereby broken through is named "liberation." Within a single foundation of mindfulness, every other foundation of mindfulness thereby arises, subduing and taming sentient beings accordingly. These three dharmas are, in fact, neither cause nor fruit — being, without being a cause, nonetheless a cause, the foundation of mindfulness is itself the very place of enlightenment; being, without being a fruit, nonetheless a fruit, one enters nirvāṇa precisely between the twin trees. With respect to this very emptiness-view, remaining unmoved, one cultivates the inconceivable thirty-seven factors of enlightenment. Breaking through in this comprehensive way, failing to obtain emptiness-view as a fixed object at all is named "the samādhi of emptiness"; failing to perceive any mark of emptiness at all is named "the samādhi of marklessness"; and this very samādhi, not arising from any genuine, fixed causal condition, is named "the samādhi of non-fabrication."

The Great Vow, and the Treatise's Own Final Words 發大誓願

Chinese Source

若不入者，發大誓願，內捨執見，外棄命財。空見乖理，戒不清淨，誓令空見不犯法身，守護七支不撓含識。若空見喧動，中忍不成；今誓苦到安心空見，如橋地海總集我身，心終不動。若空見間雜，誓純一專精，念念流入。又空見擾動不能安一，至誠懺悔，息二攀緣。一切種智不開者，無明未破；誓觀空見，法性現前，剛決進勇，不證不休。如是對治，助開涅槃。深識位次，不濫上地。內外風塵不能破壞。順道法愛不生，故無頂墮。心心寂滅流入薩婆若海；乘一大車，遊於四方，直至道場，成得正覺。餘如上說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should one still fail to enter through this very gate, one should give rise to the great vow: inwardly relinquishing one's own clinging to view, outwardly relinquishing even one's own life and wealth. Since emptiness-view runs contrary to true principle, and the precepts thereby remain impure, one vows that emptiness-view shall never again transgress against the Dharma-body, guarding the seven branches of bodily and verbal conduct so as never to disturb any sentient being. Should emptiness-view stir up clamor and disturbance, the forbearance one holds within remains incomplete — here, then, one vows to pacify the mind with respect to emptiness-view through the utmost, earnest effort, as though gathering one's entire being together like a bridge spanning the very earth and sea, the mind remaining, in the end, entirely unmoved. Should emptiness-view intrude and intermix itself among one's practice, one vows to remain pure and single-minded, wholly devoted, each successive thought flowing steadily onward into this very practice. And should emptiness-view stir up disturbance such that one cannot settle into unity at all, one repents with utmost sincerity, bringing both forms of grasping-at-objects to rest. Should the wisdom of all modes still fail to open forth, this is because ignorance has not yet been broken through — one vows, then, to contemplate emptiness-view until the Dharma-nature itself manifests directly before one, advancing with firm resolve and courage, never resting until this is genuinely realized. Applying this antidote in this way assists in opening the very gate of nirvāṇa. Deeply recognizing one's own stage, one does not falsely presume upon a higher one; inner and outer winds of disturbance prove unable to destroy this resolve. Attachment to mere compliance with the path fails to arise, and hence there is no falling away at the very summit either. Thought after thought growing ever more quiescent, one flows naturally into the very ocean of omniscience itself; riding upon this single, great cart, one travels through all four directions, arriving directly at the very place of enlightenment, and thereby accomplishes true and perfect enlightenment. The rest is as already explained above.
The Great Calming and Contemplation, Fascicle Ten, Lower Part, ends here.

Reading note: These are the final words of the Mohe Zhiguan as it has come down to us — the text simply ends here, mid-treatise, at "the rest is as already explained above." Zhiyi never delivered the remaining three objects of contemplation (overweening pride, the two lesser vehicles, the bodhisattva) or the treatise's own final three topics (the fruits of practice, establishing the teaching, and the ultimate aim); Guanding's transcription breaks off at exactly this point, and no later continuation exists in the tradition. That the treatise ends on a vow — to pacify the very mind that clings to emptiness itself, and to travel "through all four directions" toward enlightenment regardless of how far the map has actually been drawn — reads, in hindsight, as an unintentionally fitting close to a work that was itself never finished.

Status of this page: Fascicle 10 of the Mohe Zhiguan is now translated in full, spanning both the upper and lower halves of the fascicle (卷第十上 and 卷第十下) — and, with it, this page completes Ru-Yi's translation of the entire Mohe Zhiguan as it survives: the treatise's own text ends partway through this fascicle, at the close of the seventh of the ten objects of contemplation, views (諸見境). Fascicle 10 treats views in four parts: the varieties of heterodox person (outsiders entirely outside the Buddha's teaching, outsiders attached to it, and those who cling their way out of it from within) and the varieties of doctrine-attainment method among them (omniscience, spiritual power, Vedic learning); the causes and conditions by which a given view manifests, through dhyāna or through hearing, correlated in detail across the six Indian teachers, the Vātsīputrīya and Vaitulya schools, and the Tripiṭaka teaching's four gates extended through all Four Teachings, yielding one hundred and forty distinct combinations in all; the faults views produce, opening with a critique of Zhuangzi and Laozi's own "three profundities" before turning to the Indian emptiness-view's threefold conduct — evil, good, and morally indeterminate — and closing with a comparative judgment of true and false doctrine, including the counsel to nurture rather than hastily sever a view already manifest; and, finally, the ten modes of contemplation applied to views by way of a single exemplary case, nihilistic emptiness-view, tracing it through the discursible object across all six lower realms and the inconceivable object where a single thought of view is itself already the Dharma-nature, closing with the great vow that is the treatise's own final line. The remaining four objects of contemplation — overweening pride, the two lesser vehicles, and the bodhisattva — along with topics 8 through 10 of the treatise's own ten-section outline, were never delivered by Zhiyi and do not exist to be translated.
