The Real Mark and the Sixteen Gates

Fascicle 8 closes Explaining the Name (釋名) through the word "sūtra" itself, then opens Clarifying the Essence (顯體): the real mark as the sūtra's single essence, six ways of distinguishing the false, one dharma under many names, and the Tripiṭaka, shared, and distinct teachings' own gates and contemplations for entering the real mark.

Explaining the Common Name: "Sūtra" 釋通名

Chinese Source

第二、釋通名者，「經」一字也，具存胡音，應云「薩達磨分陀利脩多羅」。薩達磨，此翻妙法；分陀利，此翻蓮華，已如上釋；脩多羅，或云脩單蘭，或云脩妬路。彼方楚夏，此土翻譯不同，或言無翻，或言有翻。釋此為五：一、明無翻。二、明有翻。三、和融有無。四、歷法明經。五、觀心明經。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, explaining the common name: "sūtra" is a single word. Preserving the foreign pronunciation in full, it ought to be rendered "saddharma-puṇḍarīka-sūtra." "Saddharma" is rendered here as "wondrous dharma"; "puṇḍarīka" is rendered here as "lotus flower," already explained above; "sūtra" is sometimes rendered "sūtrānta," sometimes "sūtra-patha." Just as the various regions of that foreign land have their own dialects, so too, in translating into this land, renderings differ: some say it is untranslatable, some say it is translatable. This is explained under five headings: first, clarifying its untranslatability; second, clarifying its translatability; third, reconciling the translatable and the untranslatable; fourth, explaining "sūtra" through the various dharmas; fifth, explaining "sūtra" through the contemplation of mind.

Untranslatability 明無翻

Chinese Source

言無翻者，彼語多含，此語單淺，不可以單翻複，應留本音。而言「經」者，開善云：非正翻也，但以此代彼耳。此間聖說為經，賢說子史；彼聖稱經，菩薩稱論；既不可翻，宜以此代彼，故稱經也。既不可翻而含五義：一、法本，亦云出生。二云微發，亦云顯示。三云涌泉。四云繩墨。五云結鬘。今秖作五義不可翻，今於一中作三，三五十五義：一、教本。二、行本。三、義本。今廣釋之。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for saying it is untranslatable: the foreign word carries many senses at once, while the word in this land is single and shallow — one cannot use a single, simple word to translate something so manifold, and the original sound ought to be retained instead. As for calling it "sūtra": Kaishan says this is not a proper translation at all, but merely uses this word to stand in place of that one. In this land, what a sage teaches is called a "canon," and what a worthy teaches is called the "masters and histories"; in that foreign land, what a sage teaches is called "sūtra," and what a bodhisattva teaches is called a "treatise" — since it cannot be properly translated, it is fitting to use this word to stand in place of that one, and hence it is called "sūtra." Since it cannot be translated, it carries five senses: first, dharma-source, also called "giving rise to birth"; second, subtle arising, also called "manifestation"; third, gushing spring; fourth, marking-line; fifth, binding a garland. Here, these are simply the five untranslatable senses — and within each single one of these five, a further three senses are found, making fifteen senses in all: first, the source of teaching; second, the source of practice; third, the source of meaning. This is now explained at length.

言法本者，一切皆不可說，以四悉檀因緣則有言說：世界悉檀說，則為教本；為人、對治，則為行本；第一義悉檀，則為義本。所言教本者，金口所說一言為本，流出無量言教。若通、若別，當時被物，聞即得道。故經言：一一脩多羅，復有無量脩多羅以為眷屬。

若後人不解，菩薩以佛教為本，作通論、別論，申通、別經，令佛意不壅，尋者得道，良由其論有本故也。諸外道等，雖有所說，不與脩多羅合，戲論無本，不能得道。經是行本者，示人無諍法，導達通塞，開明眼目，救治人病，如教脩行，則起通別諸行，從此至彼，入清涼池，至甘露地泥洹真法寶，眾生從種種門入，故知經是行本。經是義本者，尋一句詮於一義，尋無量句詮無量義，或尋一句詮於無量義，尋無量句詮於一義，若通、若別尋詮會入，故經是義本。束此三種為法門者，教本即是聞慧，行本即是思慧，義本是脩慧，見真法本之義，尚已多含，故不可翻也。或言出生，例此可知。

As for "dharma-source": all things without exception are, in themselves, inexpressible, and it is only by the causes and conditions of the four siddhāntas that there comes to be any expression at all — teaching by the mundane siddhānta is the source of teaching; teaching by the individual and therapeutic siddhāntas is the source of practice; teaching by the siddhānta of ultimate meaning is the source of meaning. As for what is called the source of teaching: a single word spoken by the Buddha's own golden mouth serves as the source, from which immeasurable further words and teachings flow forth — whether common or particular, meeting beings at the appropriate time, so that, upon hearing it, they attain the path. Hence the sūtra says: each and every sūtra has, further, immeasurable further sūtras as its own retinue.

If later persons do not understand it, bodhisattvas take the Buddha's own teaching as their source and compose common treatises and particular treatises, expounding both common and particular sūtras, so that the Buddha's own intent is never obstructed, and whoever seeks it out attains the path — precisely because their treatises have this same source. The various non-Buddhist outsiders, though they too have their own teachings, do not accord with the sūtra, and their idle theorizing, having no such source, is unable to bring anyone to the path. As for the sūtra being the source of practice: it shows people the dharma free of contention, guides them through what is passable and what is blocked, opens and clarifies the eye, and heals and remedies the sickness of the person — cultivating practice according to this teaching, one gives rise to every common and particular practice, proceeding from this shore to that one, entering the cooling pond, arriving at the ground of sweet dew, the true Dharma-jewel of nirvāṇa, sentient beings entering through many different gates — hence one knows the sūtra is the source of practice. As for the sūtra being the source of meaning: seeking out a single phrase, one meaning is expounded; seeking out immeasurable phrases, immeasurable meanings are expounded; or seeking out a single phrase, immeasurable meanings are expounded; seeking out immeasurable phrases, a single meaning is expounded — whether common or particular, seeking out what is expounded, one converges upon it — hence the sūtra is the source of meaning. Gathering these three together as the Dharma-gate: the source of teaching is precisely the wisdom of hearing; the source of practice is precisely the wisdom of reflection; the source of meaning is the wisdom of cultivation. Seeing the sense of this true dharma-source is already so manifold that it cannot be translated. It is also said to mean "giving rise to birth," which may be understood by this same pattern.

二、含微發者，佛以四悉檀說，言辭巧妙，次第詮量初、中、後善，圓滿具足，如大海水漸漸轉深。聞教之者，初聞世界悉檀，次第領受，分別法相，微有解生，漸漸增長，明練通達。又遍讀諸異論，廣知智者意，多聞強識，以至成佛，就此即約教論微發。初聞為人、對治，即能起行，始人天小行，次戒定慧入無漏行，見道修道，遂證無學。從小入大，終于妙覺，是約行論微發。初聞第一義悉檀，展轉增廣，入於聞、思，煗、頂、世第一。次、入見諦，得真第一義；次、入脩道至無學，從小入大，見似真中道，起自毫末，終成合抱也。束此三發為法門者，依小乘，即三種解脫發；依大乘初住中，有教發是般若，行發即如來藏，理發是實相。微發已自多含，故不可翻也。

Second, containing "subtle arising": the Buddha teaches by means of the four siddhāntas, his words skillful and wondrous, expounding and measuring, in due sequence, what is good at the beginning, in the middle, and at the end, perfectly complete — like the great ocean's own water, growing gradually ever deeper. One who hears this teaching first hears the mundane siddhānta, receiving it in due sequence, distinguishing the marks of dharmas, a subtle understanding beginning to arise, growing gradually greater, becoming clear, refined, and penetrating. Further reading through every differing treatise, broadly knowing the wise ones' own intent, of vast learning and firm memory, one arrives at last at buddhahood — this, in terms of the teaching, discusses subtle arising. First hearing the individual and therapeutic siddhāntas, one is thereby able to give rise to practice — beginning with the small practices of humans and devas, next entering the untainted practice through precepts, concentration, and wisdom, the path of seeing and the path of cultivation, until at last realizing the stage beyond further training — proceeding from the small into the great, ending at last in wondrous enlightenment: this, in terms of practice, discusses subtle arising. First hearing the siddhānta of ultimate meaning, turning and turning, ever broadening, one enters the wisdom of hearing and of reflection, then warmth, summit, and the supreme worldly dharma; next one enters the path of seeing the truths, attaining the true, ultimate meaning; next one enters the path of cultivation, arriving at the stage beyond further training — proceeding from the small into the great, seeing the resemblance and then the true middle way, arising from the very tip of a hair and ending at last as a tree one's arms cannot encircle. Gathering these three "arisings" together as the Dharma-gate: according to the Hīnayāna, these are the three kinds of liberation's own arising; according to the Mahāyāna's own initial abiding, the arising of teaching is prajñā, the arising of practice is the tathāgatagarbha, and the arising of principle is the real mark. "Subtle arising" is already so manifold in itself that it cannot be translated.

三、含涌泉者，從譬為名也。佛以四悉檀說法，文義無盡，法流不絕。若聞世界，說一句解無量句，月四月至歲，如風於空中，自在無障礙，說初心解，已如涌泉，何況後心！何況如來！猶如石泉流潤遍益也！若聞為人、對治，起無量行，恒沙佛法，種種法門，一行無量行，入善境界，登八正直道。若聞第一義，理若虛空，虛空之法，不可格量、遍一切處，是名義涌泉。束此為法門者，教泉是法無礙辯，行泉即辭無礙辯，義泉即義無礙辯。樂說通三處，涌泉已自多含，故不可翻也。

四、含繩墨者，佛以四悉檀說。初聞世界，裁愛見之邪教，不為邪風倒惑，得入正轍，即教繩墨也。若聞為人、對治，遠離非道，入好正濟道品之路，即行繩墨也。若聞第一義，裁愛見此岸，得至彼岸，不保生死亦不住無為，即義繩墨。束此為法門者，教裁邪即是正語；行裁邪即正業、正精進、正念、正定等；義裁邪即正見、正思惟等。繩墨已自多含，故不可翻。

Third, containing "gushing spring": this name is taken from a metaphor. The Buddha teaches the Dharma by means of the four siddhāntas, its text and meaning inexhaustible, the flow of the Dharma never ceasing. If one hears the mundane siddhānta, expounding a single phrase resolves immeasurable further phrases, a month, four months, up to a year — like the wind moving through open space, sovereign and unobstructed; the understanding of the very first mind, already like a gushing spring — how much more so the later mind! How much more so the Tathāgata himself! Just like a spring within stone, its flowing moisture benefiting everywhere pervasively! If one hears the individual and therapeutic siddhāntas, immeasurable practices arise, every buddha-dharma numerous as the sands of the Ganges, every kind of Dharma-gate, a single practice being immeasurable practices, entering the good state, ascending the eightfold correct and upright path. If one hears the siddhānta of ultimate meaning, its principle is like empty space itself — the dharma of empty space cannot be measured or bounded, pervading every place — this is named the gushing spring of meaning. Gathering this as the Dharma-gate: the spring of teaching is the unobstructed eloquence of dharma; the spring of practice is the unobstructed eloquence of words; the spring of meaning is the unobstructed eloquence of meaning. Eloquence in teaching pervades all three of these places — "gushing spring" is already so manifold in itself that it cannot be translated.

Fourth, containing "marking-line": the Buddha teaches by means of the four siddhāntas. First hearing the mundane siddhānta, one cuts away the deviant teaching of craving and views, no longer overturned and deluded by the deviant wind, and thereby enters the correct track — this is the marking-line of teaching. If one hears the individual and therapeutic siddhāntas, one departs far from the wrong path, entering the good, correct road of the factors that ferry one across — this is the marking-line of practice. If one hears the siddhānta of ultimate meaning, one cuts away craving and views on this shore, and is thereby able to arrive at the far shore, neither clinging to birth-and-death nor abiding in the unconditioned — this is the marking-line of meaning. Gathering this as the Dharma-gate: the teaching's own cutting-away of the deviant is precisely correct speech; the practice's own cutting-away of the deviant is precisely correct action, correct diligence, correct mindfulness, and correct concentration, and the rest; the meaning's own cutting-away of the deviant is precisely correct view and correct intention, and the rest. "Marking-line" is already so manifold in itself that it cannot be translated.

五、含結鬘者，結教行理，如結華鬘，令不零落。世界悉檀結佛言教不零落，為人、對治結眾行不零落，第一義結義理不零落。束此為法門者，結教成口無失，結行即身無失，結義即意無失，亦是三種共智慧行，亦是三陀羅尼。教不零落是聞持陀羅尼，行不零落行陀羅尼，義不零落即總持陀羅尼。若作嚴身釋者，即是約教名智慧莊嚴，約行名福德莊嚴，約義即是所莊嚴。所莊嚴即是法身，為定慧莊嚴也。一切眾生皆有法身，法身體素，天龍之所忽劣，若修學定慧，莊嚴法身，則一切見敬也。

舊云：經含五義，今則經含十五義，豈可單漢翻胡，名含之釋如此(云云)。

訓者訓常，今釋其訓。天魔外道不能改壞，名為教常。真正無雜，無能踰過，名為行常。湛然不動，決無異趣，名為理常。又訓法者，法可軌，行可軌，理可軌。今直釋訓已含六義，況胡言重複，而可單翻耶？

Fifth, containing "binding a garland": binding together teaching, practice, and principle, just as binding a garland of flowers keeps its petals from scattering and falling away. The mundane siddhānta binds the Buddha's own spoken teaching so that it does not scatter and fall away; the individual and therapeutic siddhāntas bind every practice so that it does not scatter and fall away; the siddhānta of ultimate meaning binds meaning and principle so that they do not scatter and fall away. Gathering this as the Dharma-gate: binding the teaching accomplishes the mouth's freedom from fault; binding practice accomplishes the body's freedom from fault; binding meaning accomplishes the mind's freedom from fault — these are also the three kinds of practice shared in common with wisdom, and are also the three dhāraṇīs. The teaching not scattering and falling away is the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard; practice not scattering and falling away is the dhāraṇī of practice; meaning not scattering and falling away is precisely the dhāraṇī that holds all things together. Taken as an explanation of "adornment of the body": in terms of teaching, this is named the adornment of wisdom; in terms of practice, this is named the adornment of merit; in terms of meaning, this is precisely what is adorned. What is adorned is precisely the Dharma-body, adorned by concentration and wisdom. Every sentient being possesses the Dharma-body, yet the Dharma-body's own substance remains plain and unadorned, disregarded and belittled even by the devas and the nāgas; if one cultivates and studies concentration and wisdom, adorning the Dharma-body, then it is seen and honored by all.

The old teaching says: "sūtra" carries five senses; here, it is shown that "sūtra" carries fifteen senses — how, then, could a single word of this land possibly translate the foreign word? The explanation of what is "carried" within the name is as given here, and so on.

As for glossing "sūtra": it is glossed as "constant" — this now explains that gloss. That the heavenly demons and the non-Buddhist outsiders are unable to alter or destroy it is named the constancy of the teaching. That it is true and correct, without any admixture, and that none can surpass it, is named the constancy of practice. That it is still and unmoving, and certainly admits of no divergent direction, is named the constancy of principle. Again, glossing it as "what can be modeled": the dharma can be modeled upon, practice can be modeled upon, and principle can be modeled upon. Now, simply explaining these two glosses already yields six senses — how much more so, then, the foreign word itself, layered with meaning upon meaning — how could it possibly be rendered by any single, simple translation?

Reading note

The fifteen-meaning scheme is this treatise's own signature move applied to a single word: five base senses (dharma-source, subtle arising, gushing spring, marking-line, binding a garland) are each run through the teaching/practice/principle triad that has structured the whole treatise since Fascicle 1, so that "sūtra" is shown to be untranslatable not because it is vague but because it is *precisely* fifteen-fold — a single Chinese word could only ever capture one of the fifteen strands at a time. The gushing-spring image is worth noting for how it scales without limit: "the understanding of the very first mind, already like a gushing spring — how much more so the later mind! How much more so the Tathāgata himself!" is the same a fortiori pattern this treatise has used throughout the origin gate, now applied to a single sūtra's own inexhaustibility.

The Case for Translatability 明有翻

Chinese Source

二、言有翻者，亦為五：一、翻為經，經由為義，由聖人心口故。今亦隨而釋之，謂教由、行由、理由。一切脩多羅、一切通別論、一切疏記等，皆由聖人心口，是名教由。一切契理行、一切相似行、一切信行法行，皆由聖人心口，故以行為由。一切世間義、一切出世義、一切方便義、一切究竟義，皆由聖人心口，故以義為由。教由世界，行由為人、對治，義由第一義悉檀。

又言經者緯義，如世絹經，以緯織之，龍鳳文章成。佛以世界悉檀說經，菩薩以世界緯織，經緯合故賢聖文章成。又約行論經緯，慧行為經，行行為緯，經緯合故，八正文章成。又約理論經緯者，詮真為經，詮俗為緯，經緯合故，二諦文章成。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, as for saying that it is translatable, this too has five senses. First, rendering it as "sūtra": "sūtra" takes "source" as its own sense, since it derives from the sage's own mind and mouth. Here, too, this is explained accordingly, in terms of the teaching's own source, practice's own source, and principle's own source. Every sūtra, every common and particular treatise, every commentary and record, all derive from the sage's own mind and mouth — this is named the teaching's own source. Every practice that accords with principle, every practice of resemblance, every practice of the one who follows through faith and the one who follows through the dharma, all derive from the sage's own mind and mouth — hence practice, too, has this same source. Every mundane meaning, every world-transcending meaning, every meaning of skillful means, every meaning of the ultimate, all derive from the sage's own mind and mouth — hence meaning, too, has this same source. The teaching's own source is the mundane siddhānta; practice's own source is the individual and the therapeutic siddhāntas; meaning's own source is the siddhānta of ultimate meaning.

Again, "sūtra" also carries the sense of "weft": just as, in ordinary silk-weaving, the warp is woven together with the weft, and thereby the pattern of the dragon and the phoenix is formed — the Buddha, by means of the mundane siddhānta, teaches the sūtra as the warp, and the bodhisattva, by means of the mundane siddhānta, weaves in the weft; warp and weft joined together, the pattern of the worthy and the sage is thereby formed. Again, discussed in terms of practice, warp and weft: the practice of wisdom is the warp, and the practice of conduct is the weft; warp and weft joined together, the pattern of the eightfold correct path is thereby formed. Again, discussed in terms of principle, warp and weft: expounding the real is the warp, and expounding the conventional is the weft; warp and weft joined together, the pattern of the two truths is thereby formed.

二、翻為契者，契緣、契事、契義。世界說是契緣；隨宜說是契生善；隨對治說是契破惡，是為契事；隨第一義說，是契義。

三、翻法本者，即教、行、理本，如前釋(云云)。

四、翻線者，線貫持教、行、理，令不零落，嚴身等義如前釋。又線能縫義，縫教使章句次第，堪可說法。如支佛不值十二部線，不能說法，世智辯聰亦不得經線，正語不成。又線能縫行，依經則行正，違經則行邪。又縫理者，理所不印，墮六十二邪；理所印者，會一究竟道也。

五、翻善語教，亦是善行教，亦是善理教。世界悉檀說即善語教；為人、對治即善行教；第一義說即善理教。是名脩多羅有五種翻也。

Second, rendering it as "according with": according with conditions, according with the matter at hand, according with meaning. Teaching by the mundane siddhānta is according with conditions; teaching according to what is fitting is according with generating good; teaching according to the antidote is according with breaking through evil — these two together are according with the matter at hand; teaching according to the siddhānta of ultimate meaning is according with meaning.

Third, rendering it as "dharma-source": this is precisely the source of teaching, of practice, and of principle, exactly as explained before, and so on.

Fourth, rendering it as "thread": the thread strings together and holds fast the teaching, practice, and principle, so that they do not scatter and fall away — the sense of "adornment" and the rest is as explained before. Again, the thread is able to stitch together meaning: stitching the teaching gives its chapters and verses their proper sequence, fit to teach the Dharma — just as the pratyekabuddha, not having encountered the thread of the twelve divisions of scripture, is unable to teach the Dharma, and worldly wisdom and eloquence, too, without obtaining the sūtra's own thread, cannot achieve correct speech. Again, the thread is able to stitch together practice: relying on the sūtra, practice is correct; departing from the sūtra, practice becomes deviant. Again, as for stitching together principle: what principle does not seal falls into the sixty-two views; what principle does seal converges upon the one, ultimate path.

Fifth, rendering it as "the teaching of good words," which is likewise the teaching of good practice, and likewise the teaching of good principle: teaching by the mundane siddhānta is the teaching of good words; teaching by the individual and therapeutic siddhāntas is the teaching of good practice; teaching by the siddhānta of ultimate meaning is the teaching of good principle. This is what is meant by saying that "sūtra" has these five translatable senses.

Reading note

Where the untranslatability camp reads "sūtra" as a single word too dense with meaning for any one Chinese term to capture, the translatability camp reads it the other way around: "sūtra" itself already carries at least five distinct native senses — source, weft-partner-to-warp, according-with, thread, and the teaching of good words — each of which maps cleanly onto the teaching/practice/principle triad this treatise has used constantly since Fascicle 1. The two positions are not yet reconciled here; the treatise stages them as genuinely competing accounts before turning, in the next section, to show that both are right in their own way.

Reconciling the Translatable and the Untranslatable 和融有無

Chinese Source

三、和融有無者，昔佛法初度，胡漢未明，言無翻者，乃是河西群學所傳。晚人承用，加以此代彼，今傳譯煥爛，方言稍通，豈苟執無翻，猶以多含為解？若多含者，何局五耶？若有翻者，以何為正？義寧種種，翻那得多？若翻脩多羅為經者，脩多羅有九，謂通別脩多羅藏等，何不見周正十二部中經部，三藏中有經藏耶？若翻為契法本等者，亦應改正十二部中有契部、法本部、線部、善語、教部，三藏中應有線等藏，彼諸處皆不從此翻，何獨通脩多羅耶？

《釋論》云：「般若尊重，智慧輕薄，何得用輕翻重？」若爾，即是無翻家證。夫實相尊重不可說，遂得作胡言而說。何不得作漢語而翻？若不可翻，亦不可說，此即有翻家證。舊云：涅槃含三德，不可用滅度為翻。又梁武云：滅度小乘法，不可用翻大涅槃。此未必爾。經言有涅槃、大涅槃，亦應有滅度、大滅度。此經云：「如來滅度」，豈非大滅度？既以小滅度翻小涅槃，何得不以大滅度翻大涅槃？

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, reconciling the translatable and the untranslatable: in the past, when the Buddhadharma was first brought across, the foreign and Chinese languages were not yet mutually clear, and the claim of untranslatability was what the scholars of Hexi handed down. Later persons, inheriting this usage, added the practice of using this word to stand in place of that one. Now, however, translation has become abundant and refined, and the various dialects have become somewhat mutually intelligible — how, then, could one simply cling to untranslatability, still taking "manifold meaning" as the explanation? If it is truly manifold in meaning, why limit it to only five senses? And if it is translatable, then which rendering is the correct one? If the sense is various in so many ways, how could the translations possibly be so numerous? If "sūtra" is rendered as "canon," note that the Sanskrit word itself has nine forms — the common and particular sūtra-collections and the rest — why, then, does one not find, within the twelve divisions of scripture properly reckoned, a "canon division," or, within the Tripiṭaka, a "canon-treasury"? And if it is rendered as "according with," "dharma-source," and the rest, then, by the same logic, the twelve divisions of scripture ought likewise to be corrected to include an "according-with division," a "dharma-source division," a "thread division," and a "teaching-of-good-words division," and the Tripiṭaka ought to include a "thread-treasury" and the rest — yet none of these other places ever follow any of these renderings; why, then, should "sūtra" alone be singled out for this treatment?

The Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: "Prajñā is weighty and revered, while 'wisdom' is light and shallow — how could the light word be used to translate the weighty one?" If this is so, this is itself evidence for the untranslatability camp. Yet the real mark itself, weighty and revered, is inexpressible — and if it can nonetheless be expressed in the foreign tongue, why could it not equally be expressed by translation into Chinese? If it truly cannot be translated, then it likewise cannot be spoken at all — and this, in turn, is evidence for the translatability camp. The old teaching says: nirvāṇa carries the three virtues within it, and so "cessation and passing away" cannot be used as its translation. Emperor Wu of Liang, too, says: "cessation and passing away" is a Hīnayāna dharma, and so cannot be used to translate "great nirvāṇa." Yet this need not be so. The sūtra speaks of both "nirvāṇa" and "great nirvāṇa" — so too there ought to be both "cessation and passing away" and "great cessation and passing away." This present sūtra says, "the Tathāgata's cessation and passing away" — how could this fail to be "great cessation and passing away"? Since the lesser "cessation and passing away" is already used to translate the lesser "nirvāṇa," why could the greater "cessation and passing away" not likewise be used to translate "great nirvāṇa"?

若謂滅度偏，不含三德者，今作含釋，滅者即解脫，解脫必有其人，人即法身，法身不直身，必有靈智，靈智即般若。又大即法身，滅即解脫，度即般若。但標滅度，含三宛然，何得無翻耶？若執一言，則彼彼相是非，不達佛意，已如上說。

今和融有無，虛豁義趣。若言無翻，名含五義，於一一義更含三義，彌見其美；若作有翻，於一一翻亦具三義，轉益旨深；任彼有無，何所乖諍？《大經》云：「我終不與世間共諍。」世智說有，我亦說有；世智說無，我亦說無。如此通融，於二家無失，而有理存焉。復次，圓義無方，處處通入，乃如上說。

若正翻名，世諦不得混濫，今且據一名以為正翻，亦不使二家有怨。何者？從古及今，譯胡為漢，皆題為經。若餘翻是正，何不改作契線？若傳譯僉然，則經正明矣！若等是無翻，何不標微發、涌泉等？今正用經，於多含義強，含三法本、三微發、三涌泉、諸繩墨、結鬘等義，亦含契、線、善語教、訓法、訓常等，無不攝在經一字中，餘句亦如是。諸大小乘教，皆以經為通名故，不用餘句也。

If it is said that "cessation and passing away" is one-sided and does not carry the three virtues within it, here an explanation is given showing that it does: "cessation" is precisely liberation; liberation must have its own person, and that person is precisely the Dharma-body; the Dharma-body is not a mere body alone, and must possess numinous wisdom, and that numinous wisdom is precisely prajñā. Again, "great" is the Dharma-body, "cessation" is liberation, and "passing away" is prajñā. Simply setting forth "cessation and passing away," the three virtues are thereby vividly present within it — how, then, could it be untranslatable? If one clings to a single word alone, then one side and the other endlessly dispute right and wrong, failing to penetrate the Buddha's own intent, exactly as already explained above.

Here, then, the translatable and the untranslatable are reconciled, and their sense and aim opened up freely. If one speaks of untranslatability, the name carries five senses, and within each single one of these five, three further senses are carried, so that its beauty is seen all the more clearly; if one takes up translatability, then each single translation likewise possesses these same three senses, its aim growing ever deeper still — whichever of the two, translatable or untranslatable, one takes, what is there left to dispute? The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra says, "I have never, in the end, disputed with the world." When worldly wisdom says something exists, I too say it exists; when worldly wisdom says it does not exist, I too say it does not exist. Reconciled in this way, neither position suffers any loss, and yet reason is preserved throughout. Again, the perfect sense knows no fixed direction, penetrating and entering everywhere, exactly as explained above.

As for settling on the correct translation of the name: worldly convention should not be thrown into confusion, and so, here, a single name is taken as the correct translation, without thereby causing either side any grievance. Why is this? From ancient times to the present, wherever the foreign tongue has been translated into Chinese, it has, in every case, been titled "sūtra." If some other translation were the correct one, why has it never instead been changed to "according with" or "thread"? Since the transmitted translation is thus unanimous, "sūtra" is plainly shown to be the correct one. And if it were, on the other hand, equally untranslatable, why has it never instead been marked as "subtle arising" or "gushing spring"? Here, then, "sūtra" is correctly adopted, strong in its manifold sense, carrying within it the three senses of dharma-source, the three of subtle arising, the three of gushing spring, and every sense of marking-line and binding a garland, and likewise carrying within it "according with," "thread," "the teaching of good words," the gloss of "modeling," the gloss of "constancy," and the rest — there is nothing that is not gathered within this single word "sūtra," and every other candidate word is exactly the same. Every Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna teaching alike takes "sūtra" as its own common name, and hence no other word is used.

Reading note

The historical argument here is unusually concrete for this treatise: rather than resolving the dispute on doctrinal grounds alone, Zhiyi points to the plain fact of translation practice — every generation of translators, without exception, has settled on "sūtra" and never once switched to "according with" or "thread," which is itself evidence that "sūtra" is the correct rendering, not an arbitrary placeholder. The "cessation and passing away" example (translating nirvāṇa) is a small masterclass in this treatise's own method: rather than picking a side in an existing scholarly dispute, Zhiyi shows that the three virtues (Dharma-body, liberation, prajñā) can all be found packed into the supposedly "one-sided" Hīnayāna term itself, dissolving the objection rather than refuting it. The closing move — worldly wisdom says it exists, I too say it exists; worldly wisdom says it does not exist, I too say it does not exist — is the same non-contentious stance the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra* itself models, applied here to a purely philological dispute about translation.

Explaining "Sūtra" Through the Various Dharmas 歷法明經

四、歷法明經者。若以經為正翻，何法是經？舊用三種：一用聲為經，如佛在世，金口演說，但有聲音詮辯，聽者得道，故以聲為經。《大品》云：「從善知識所聞也。」二、用色為經，若佛在世，可以聲為經，今佛去世，紙墨傳持，應用色為經。《大品》云：「從經卷中聞。」三、用法為經，內自思惟，心與法合，不由他教，亦非紙墨，但心曉悟，即法為經，故云：「脩我法者，證乃自知(云云)。」三塵為經，施於此土。耳識利者，能於聲塵分別取悟，則聲是其經，於餘非經。若意識利者，自能研心思惟取決，法是其經，於餘非經。眼識利者，文字詮量而得道理，色是其經，於餘非經。此方用三塵而已。餘三識鈍：鼻臭紙墨，則無所知；身觸經卷，亦不能解；舌噉文字，寧別是非？若他土亦用六塵，亦偏用一塵，如《淨名》曰：「以一食施於一切……於食等者，於法亦等；於法等者，於食亦等。」此即偏用舌根所對為經。或有國土，以天衣觸身即得道，此偏用觸為經。或見佛光明得道，此偏用色為經。或寂滅無言，觀心得道，此偏用意為經。如眾香土，以香為佛事，此偏用香為經。他方六根識利，六塵得為經。此土三根識鈍，鼻不及驢狗鹿等，云何於香味觸等能得通達？

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, explaining "sūtra" through the various dharmas: if "sūtra" is taken as the correct translation, what dharma, then, is sūtra? Formerly, three kinds were used. First, taking sound as sūtra: since, while the Buddha was in the world, his own golden mouth expounded the teaching, and it was solely by the sound of his voice, expounded and discerned, that the hearers attained the path, sound is thereby taken as sūtra. The Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says, "heard from a good spiritual friend." Second, taking form as sūtra: while the Buddha was in the world, sound could indeed be taken as sūtra; now that the Buddha has departed the world, and the teaching is transmitted and upheld through paper and ink, form ought to be taken as sūtra. The Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says, "heard from within a sūtra-scroll." Third, taking dharma as sūtra: reflecting inwardly upon oneself, the mind uniting with the dharma, not depending upon any other's teaching, and not upon paper and ink either, but simply the mind's own awakening — this alone is dharma taken as sūtra, hence it is said, "one who cultivates my own dharma realizes it and knows it for oneself, and so on."

These three sense-objects are taken as sūtra as they are applied within this land. One of sharp auditory consciousness is able to distinguish and grasp awakening from the sense-object of sound, and so, for such a person, sound is their own sūtra, and nothing else is. One of sharp mental consciousness is able, of themselves, to hone the mind's own reflection and grasp the decisive resolution, and so, for such a person, dharma is their own sūtra, and nothing else is. One of sharp visual consciousness attains the principle of the path through the discernment of written characters, and so, for such a person, form is their own sūtra, and nothing else is. This land makes use of only these three sense-objects. The other three faculties are dull here: the nose, smelling paper and ink, gains no knowledge from it at all; the body, touching a sūtra-scroll, likewise cannot understand it thereby; the tongue, tasting the written characters, how could it possibly distinguish right from wrong by this? If other lands, too, make use of all six sense-objects, or make one-sided use of a single one of them — as the Vimalakīrti Sūtra says, "By means of a single meal, giving to all... equality with respect to food is likewise equality with respect to the dharma; equality with respect to the dharma is likewise equality with respect to food" — this takes one-sided use of what the tongue faculty encounters as sūtra. Or there may be a land where touching a heavenly garment to the body itself brings attainment of the path — this takes one-sided use of touch as sūtra. Or seeing the Buddha's own radiant light brings attainment of the path — this takes one-sided use of form as sūtra. Or quiescent extinction, wordless, contemplating mind, brings attainment of the path — this takes one-sided use of mind as sūtra. As in the land of manifold fragrances, where fragrance itself serves as the buddha's own undertaking — this takes one-sided use of fragrance as sūtra. In other lands, the six faculties' own consciousness is sharp, and so all six sense-objects are able to serve as sūtra; in this land, the three faculties' own consciousness is dull — the nose does not even equal that of a donkey, a dog, or a deer — how, then, could fragrance, taste, touch, and the rest possibly be penetrated here?

問：
根利故於塵是經，鈍者塵則非經耶？

答：
六塵是法界，體自是經，非根利取方乃是經。

Question: If it is because the faculty is sharp that a sense-object becomes sūtra, does this mean that, for one of dull faculty, the sense-object is not sūtra at all?

Answer: The six sense-objects are themselves the dharma-realm — their very substance is, of itself, sūtra; it is not the case that sūtra exists only once a sharp faculty grasps it.

何者？《大品》云：「一切法趣色，是趣不過。」此色能詮一切法。如黑墨色：一畫詮一，二畫詮二，三畫詮三，竪一畫則詮王，足右畫則詮丑，足左畫則詮田，出上詮由，出下詮申，如是迴轉，詮不可盡。或一字詮無量法，無量字共詮一法，無量字詮無量法，一字詮一法，於一黑墨小小迴轉，詮量大異，左迴詮惡，右迴詮善，上點詮無漏，下點詮有漏，殺活與奪、毀譽苦樂，皆在墨中，更無一法出此墨外。略而言之，黑墨詮無量教、無量行、無量理，黑墨亦是教本、行本、理本。黑墨從初一點至無量點，從點至字，從字至句，從句至偈，從偈至卷，從卷至部。又從點一字句中，初立小行，後著大行。又從點字中，初見淺理，後到深理，是名黑色教、行、義三種微發。又從黑色涌出點，出字、句、偈，不可窮盡。涌出諸行無盡，涌出義無盡，是名黑色具三涌泉。又約黑色裁教、行、義邪。又約黑色結教、行、義鬘，又以嚴身。又色是由，由色故縛，有六道生死；由色故脫，有四種聖人。又色訓法，法色故，能成教、行、理。又色是常，色教不可破，色行不可改，色理不可動。又色不可翻，色義多含故。又色可翻，名色為經。故見色經時，知色愛見，知色因緣生法，知色即空即假即中，色即法界，總含諸法。法界文字，文字即空，無點、無字、無句、無偈。句偈文字，畢竟不可得，是名知字非字，非字亦字。

Why is this? The Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says, "Every dharma tends toward form — this tendency has no exception." Form itself is able to expound every dharma. Take the color black ink, for instance: a single stroke expounds "one"; two strokes expound "two"; three strokes expound "three"; a single vertical stroke forms the character for "king"; adding a stroke to the right forms the character for "shame"; adding a stroke to the left forms the character for "field"; extending the top stroke upward forms the character for "from"; extending the bottom stroke downward forms the character for "extend" — turning it about in this way, its expounding is inexhaustible. Sometimes a single character expounds immeasurable dharmas; immeasurable characters together expound a single dharma; immeasurable characters expound immeasurable dharmas; a single character expounds a single dharma — within this single black ink, the smallest turn of the brush yields vastly different meanings expounded: turning left expounds evil, turning right expounds good; a dot above expounds the untainted, a dot below expounds the tainted; killing and sparing life, giving and taking away, disparaging and praising, suffering and joy — all of these lie within the ink itself, and there is not a single dharma that lies outside this ink. Stated briefly: black ink expounds immeasurable teaching, immeasurable practice, and immeasurable principle; black ink is likewise the source of teaching, the source of practice, and the source of principle. Black ink proceeds from the very first single dot to immeasurable dots, from a dot to a character, from a character to a phrase, from a phrase to a verse, from a verse to a fascicle, from a fascicle to a whole division. Again, within a single dot, character, or phrase, small practice is first established, and great practice is set forth only afterward. Again, within a dot or character, shallow principle is seen first, and deep principle is arrived at only afterward — this is named black color's own threefold subtle arising, of teaching, of practice, and of meaning. Again, from black color, dots gush forth, and characters, phrases, and verses issue from it, inexhaustibly; the practices that gush forth from it are without end, and the meanings that gush forth from it are without end — this is named black color's own possession of the threefold gushing spring. Again, in terms of black color, teaching, practice, and meaning are cut away from what is deviant. Again, in terms of black color, teaching, practice, and meaning are bound together as a garland, and thereby serve as adornment as well. Again, form is itself a source: by means of form, one is bound, and there come to be the six destinies' own birth-and-death; by means of form, one is liberated, and there come to be the four kinds of sage. Again, form is glossed as "dharma": because dharma has form, it is able to accomplish teaching, practice, and principle. Again, form is itself constant: form's own teaching cannot be destroyed, form's own practice cannot be altered, form's own principle cannot be moved. Again, form cannot be translated, since form's own sense is so manifold; and form can indeed be translated, being named "form" as sūtra. Hence, when one sees form as sūtra, one thereby knows form's own craving and views; knows that form is a dharma born of causes and conditions; knows that form is at once empty, at once provisional, and at once the middle; form is itself the dharma-realm, gathering in every dharma entirely. The dharma-realm's own written characters — the written character is itself empty; there is no dot, no character, no phrase, no verse. The phrase, the verse, the written character — in the end, none of these can be found at all — this is named knowing that the character is not a character, and yet the non-character, too, is a character.

Reading note

The claim that "the six sense-objects are themselves the dharma-realm" resolves the opening question about sharp versus dull faculties the same way this treatise resolves most such asymmetries: sūtra-ness is not something a sharp faculty adds to an otherwise inert object, but something already fully present in the object itself, waiting only to be recognized. The demonstration that follows — building an entire teaching/practice/principle, arising, gushing-spring, and constancy account entirely out of brush-stroke variations in black ink — is this treatise's most literal possible illustration of "one dharma contains all dharmas": the same handful of strokes that form ordinary characters for "king," "field," or "shame" are shown to carry, in principle, everything the fifteen-fold untranslatability scheme already claimed for the word "sūtra" itself.

Black ink and color as sūtra, taken as dharma-source: if, in reaction to the ink and its characters, anger arises, cutting short another's own life; if, in reaction to the ink and its characters, craving arises, leading to theft or sexual misconduct; or even if, in reaction to the ink, delusion arises, giving rise to wrong views — one should know that the ink and its characters are the source of the four evil destinies. If, in reaction to the ink and its characters, loving-kindness or equanimity arises, or correct view arises, one should know that the ink and its characters are the source of the human and heavenly realms. If one knows that the ink and its characters are themselves the unrecorded fruit of retribution — the unrecorded being the truth of suffering; that defilement arising in reaction to this resultant form is precisely the truth of arising; that knowing the character to be formed through causes and conditions, suffering, empty, and without self, is the truth of the path; and that, once one knows the character is not a character, no inverted view of the character arises, and every affliction is thereby extinguished — this is precisely the truth of cessation; knowing the character through these Four Noble Truths, one is able to give rise to warmth and summit, whether approaching the fruit or abiding in the fruit, the liberation of the worthy and the sage — one should know that the ink and its characters are the source of the śrāvaka.

If one fails to understand the character, this is named ignorance; giving rise to craving and aversion in reaction to the character is formations; discriminating the character as attractive or ugly is consciousness; consciousness taking hold of the character is named name-and-form; the character passing before the eye is named the six sense-fields; the character as sense-object meeting the faculty is named contact; taking it in and clinging to it with defilement is precisely sensation; entangled attachment, unable to let go, is craving; exerting every effort to pursue it is grasping; grasping then becomes karma, named becoming; becoming, able to draw forth its own fruit, is named birth, old age, sickness, and death — the wheel of suffering never resting — this, then, is the source of the twelve links of dependent origination.

How could sentient beings possibly, in delusion, give rise to grasping and rejecting? Giving rise to compassion and the vow, practicing the six pāramitās, delivering sentient beings, entering the very brink of true reality, and yet finding no sentient being who has thereby attained cessation and passing away — one should know that this character is the source of the bodhisattva.

If one knows that the character is not a character, and is neither a character nor a non-character, freedom from both extremes' own inverted views is named purity; purity, being free of karma, is named self; self, being free of suffering, is named bliss; freedom from suffering, being free of birth-and-death, is named permanence. Why is this? The character is the conventional truth; the non-character is the real truth; neither character nor non-character is the one real truth. The one truth is precisely the three truths, and the three truths are precisely the one truth — this is named the source of the object.

若知墨字，從紙、筆、心、手，和合而成，一一字推不得一字，一一點推亦不得字，則無所不得，心手即不得能，無能無所，知能所誰？是一切智本。字雖非字，非字而字，從心故有點，從點有字，從字有句，從句有偈，從偈有行，從行有卷，從卷有帙，從帙有部，從部有藏，從藏有種種分別，是道種智本。雖非字非非字，而雙照字非字，是為一切種智本。

雪山為八字，捨所愛身，是為行本。我解一句乃至半句，得見佛性，入大涅槃，即是位本。我得三菩提，皆由聞經，及稱善哉，字即乘本。若忘失句逗，還令通利，與其三昧，及陀羅尼，即感應本。依文學通即神通本。依字故得語，即說法本。說字教他即眷屬本。勤學此字，祿在其中，即利益本。

If one knows that the ink and its characters are formed through the coming-together of paper, brush, mind, and hand, then, tracing each single character, not a single character can be found; tracing each single dot, likewise no character can be found — and thus there is nothing that cannot be found. Mind and hand themselves cannot be found as the agent — with neither agent nor object, who, then, is it that knows agent and object? This is the source of the wisdom that knows all things. Though the character is not a character, it is, as non-character, still a character: from mind there comes to be a dot; from a dot there comes to be a character; from a character there comes to be a phrase; from a phrase there comes to be a verse; from a verse there comes to be a chapter of practice; from a chapter there comes to be a fascicle; from a fascicle there comes to be a case; from a case there comes to be a division; from a division there comes to be a treasury; from a treasury there come to be every kind of distinction — this is the source of the wisdom of the paths. Though neither a character nor a non-character, and yet illuminating both character and non-character at once — this is the source of the wisdom knowing all modes.

For the sake of the eight-character verse, the Himalayan ascetic relinquished his own beloved body — this is the source of practice. "I, understanding a single phrase, or even half a phrase, come to see buddha-nature, and enter great nirvāṇa" — this is precisely the source of stage. "I attained the threefold bodhi entirely by hearing the sūtra, and by exclaiming 'excellent!'" — the character is itself the source of the vehicle. If one forgets a phrase or a pause and is caused, once again, to master it fluently, granted samādhi and dhāraṇī for this — this is the source of sympathetic response. Relying on the study of the text to attain penetration is the source of spiritual powers. Relying on the character, one obtains speech — this is the source of teaching the Dharma. Teaching the character to others is the source of the retinue. Diligently studying this character, one's own reward already lies within it — this is the source of merit and benefit.

Reading note

This sequence runs a single written character through nearly every doctrinal scheme the treatise has built since Fascicle 1 — the ten dharma-realms (a character provoking anger, craving, or delusion is the source of the four evil destinies; provoking loving-kindness, the human and heavenly realms), the Four Noble Truths, the twelve links of dependent origination, the three truths, and the three kinds of wisdom — each keyed to how the perceiving mind reacts to the character, not to any property of the ink itself. The closing run correlating the ten wondrous aspects to a single character's own source (practice, stage, vehicle, sympathetic response, spiritual powers, and the rest) is the same move already used for the lotus flower's own anatomy back in the immediately preceding section, now applied to the smallest possible unit of the sūtra's own text: a single dot of ink.

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, explaining "sūtra" as the contemplation of mind: this, too, follows the four categories given above.

The first category, untranslatability: mind contains within it every mental factor, good and evil alike, and one should know that this mind is the very capital of every dharma — how, then, could it be fixed and judged as any one thing? If evil is mind, then mind would not contain good and every other mental factor within it; if good is mind, then mind would not contain evil and every other mental factor within it. Not knowing quite how to designate "mind," the particular is here used to stand for the general — hence one knows that "mind," even in this abbreviated sense, is able to contain the ten thousand dharmas within it, how much more so, then, these same five senses! The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says, "Within a single mote of dust there is a sūtra-scroll as vast as a great trichiliocosm" — this is precisely this same sense.

As for mind being the dharma-source: the Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says, "Within the whole of the world, there is nothing that is not fashioned by mind." Without mind, there is no reflection or awareness; without reflection or awareness, there is no speech — one should therefore know that mind is itself the source of speech.

As for mind being the source of practice: the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says, "the practice of mind, the great practice, the pervading practice." Mind is itself the mental factor of intention, and the mental factor of intention belongs to the aggregate of formations; every formation is established by means of the mind's own intention, and hence mind is the source of practice.

As for mind being the source of principle: without mind, by whom could principle be contained at all? By honing principle with the very first mind, one arrives, hazily, at the point of awakening, gradually entering resemblance, and thereby realizing the truth itself — this is the source of principle.

心含微發者，初剎那心，微微而有；次心若存若亡，次漸增長，後則決定，暢心而發口，是語微發。初心習行，行猶微弱，次少樹立，後成大行，即行微發。初觀心不見心理，更脩髣髴，乃至相似真實，即理微發。

心含涌泉者，心具諸法，障故不流，如土石壓泉，去壅涌溜，若不觀心，心闇不明，所說不長。若觀心明徹，則宣辯無方，流溢難盡，豈非語涌泉？若不觀心，行則有間，以觀心故，念念相續，翻六蔽成六度，六度攝一切行，是行涌泉。若能觀心，如利钁斵地，磐石沙鹵，理水清澄，滔滔無竭，即義涌泉。

The mind, first habituating itself to practice, finds its practice still faint and weak; it then establishes itself a little; and, afterward, becomes great practice — this is practice's own subtle arising. Contemplating mind at first, one does not yet see the principle of mind; cultivating further, one approaches something resembling it, and, at last, arrives at the resemblance of the true itself — this is principle's own subtle arising.

As for mind containing the gushing spring: mind is complete with every dharma, yet obstructed, and so it does not flow — like a spring pressed down beneath earth and stone; once the blockage is removed, it gushes forth in a torrent. If one does not contemplate mind, mind remains dark and unclear, and what is taught does not extend far. If one contemplates mind with clarity and penetration, then one's own expounding and eloquence know no fixed bound, overflowing without end — how could this fail to be speech's own gushing spring? If one does not contemplate mind, practice has its own gaps and interruptions; because one contemplates mind, thought follows thought without a break, turning the six coverings into the six pāramitās, the six pāramitās gathering in every practice — this is practice's own gushing spring. If one is able to contemplate mind, it is like a sharp mattock breaking through the earth, through solid rock and salt-flat sand alike, until the water of principle, clear and pure, flows on and on without ever running dry — this is meaning's own gushing spring.

All of this may be understood accordingly.

Again, mind is itself the marking-line: if, by contemplating mind, one attains correct speech, one departs from deviant and inverted teaching. Contemplating mind correctly, one is thereby spared from deviant practice; the mind free of any view to cling to thereby enters correct principle. Practice, taken as a matter of fact, is like the string; practice, taken as a matter of principle, is like the ink — snapping the string against the wood of craving and views, the vessel of the correct Dharma is thereby formed.

This is how the mind, taken as sūtra, is so manifold in its own meaning — the fifteen senses are here briefly indicated, and so on.

Mind's own vertical conditioning upon principle is the warp, mind's own horizontal conditioning upon principle is the weft, weaving together meaning and principle, and so on. Again, the object contemplated is the warp, the contemplating wisdom is the weft, and the turning and returning of contemplation weaves together every kind of pattern.

Again, mind is itself "according with" contemplation: wisdom according with its object is according with conditions, according with delight and desire; mind as according with teaching is according with what is fitting and with the antidote; mind as according with practice is according with the mind of ultimate meaning, according with principle.

Mind as dharma-source, and mind as thread, are exactly as explained before, and so on.

As for mind being "the teaching of good words": dharma and speech alike may be either good or evil, and here they are settled specifically as good dharma and good speech; mind and contemplation likewise may be either good or evil, and here they are settled specifically as good mind and good contemplation — this is precisely the teaching of good words, of good practice, and of good principle, and so mind is thereby complete with these same three senses.

三類、和融有無明觀者，可解(云云)。

四類、歷法為觀者，若小乘明惡中無善、善中無惡，事理亦然。此則惡心非經，則無多含之義，隘路不受二人並行。若大乘觀心者，觀惡心非惡心，亦即惡而善，亦即非惡非善；觀善心非善心，亦即善而惡，亦非善非惡。觀一心即三心，以此三心歷一切心，歷一切法，何心、何法而不一三，一切法趣此心，一切心趣此法。如此觀心，為一切語本、行本、理本，有翻五義，無翻五義，一一於心解釋無滯。遍一切心無不是經，大意可領，不俟多記也。

As for mind being "what can be modeled": without contemplation there is no rule or measure; by means of contemplation, the sovereign mind is made correct, and, the sovereign mind being correct, its mental factors, too, are made correct. Practice and principle are exactly the same: the sovereign mind according with principle, its factors, too, accord with principle — hence this is named "what can be modeled." As for mind being "constant": the mind's own nature is eternally settled, just like empty space itself — who could possibly destroy it? Again, a deluded thought cannot destroy a wholesome thought; deviant practice does not interfere with correct practice; deviant principle does not destroy correct principle — hence mind is named "constant."

Explaining every matter accordingly, turning each single one toward mind as its own object of contemplation, the wisdom of contemplation is thereby ever more perfected, never at odds with any matter — like fire fed by more fuel, matter and principle alike suffer no loss. This is precisely the written character being, at the same time, no written character at all — not that one discards the written character to practice some separate contemplation instead.

Third category, reconciling the translatable and the untranslatable through contemplation: this may be understood accordingly, and so on.

Fourth category, contemplation through the various teachings: if the Hīnayāna clarifies that there is no good within evil, and no evil within good, then the same holds for matter and principle alike — in this case, an evil mind is not sūtra, and there is no sense of manifold meaning at all, a narrow path that admits no two persons walking abreast. If one contemplates mind according to the Mahāyāna, one contemplates the evil mind as not the evil mind — it is, at once, evil and yet good, and, at once, neither evil nor good; one contemplates the good mind as not the good mind — it is, at once, good and yet evil, and, at once, neither good nor evil. Contemplating the one mind as the three minds, and, by means of these same three minds, passing through every mind and every dharma, what mind or what dharma would fail to be, at once, one and three? Every dharma tends toward this very mind, and every mind tends toward this very dharma. Contemplating mind in this way is the source of every word, the source of every practice, and the source of every principle; the five translatable senses and the five untranslatable senses are each, one by one, explained without any obstruction in terms of mind. Pervading every mind, there is nothing that is not sūtra — the general sense may be grasped from this, and it need not be recorded in further detail.

Reading note

The contemplation-of-mind treatment runs the identical fifteen-fold and five-fold schemes already applied to sound, form, and the black-ink character, but relocates the source of all fifteen senses to mind itself rather than to any external medium — a move consistent with how this treatise has closed nearly every other topic since Fascicle 2. The fourth category's Hīnayāna-versus-Mahāyāna contrast is the section's real payoff: where the Hīnayāna's strict good/evil partition would make an evil mind simply not-sūtra at all ("a narrow path that admits no two persons walking abreast"), the Mahāyāna's contemplation of the one mind as the three minds at once dissolves the partition entirely, so that literally no mental state — however apparently unwholesome — falls outside what can be contemplated as sūtra. This closes explaining "sūtra" through the contemplation of mind, and, with it, Explaining the Common Name (釋通名) in its entirety — and, with that, Explaining the Name (釋名) as a whole, the first of the treatise's Five Sections, complete at last after occupying the treatise since its very first fascicle. The treatise continues with the second of the Five Sections, Clarifying the Essence (顯體).

Reading note

The dharma-seal argument answers the "point of the essence" question directly: a seal's whole function is to certify authenticity against forgery, and a Mahāyāna teaching's single seal — the real mark alone, rather than the Hīnayāna's three — is possible only because birth-and-death and nirvāṇa are no longer being distinguished as two separate things requiring two separate certifications. The beam-and-pillar illustration that follows is one of this treatise's clearest statements of what "essence" has meant throughout Clarifying the Essence: not a further ingredient added to cause and fruit, but the very capacity that makes cause and fruit hold together as anything at all — present precisely as an absence, the way empty space is what makes a house habitable rather than a heap of timber.

一、就凡簡者，《釋論》云：世典亦稱實者，乃是護國治家稱實也。外道亦稱實者，邪智僻解，謂為實也。小乘稱實者，厭苦蘇息，以偏真為實也。如是等，但有實名，而無其義。何者？世間妖幻道術亦稱為實，多是鬼神魅法，此法入心，迷醉狂亂，自衒善好，謂勝真實，立異動眾，示奇特相。或髑髏盛屎，約多人前張口大咽，或生魚臭肉，增狀餔食，或裸形弊服，誇傲規矩，或直來直去，不問不答，種種譎詭，詃誘無智，令信染惑著。著已求脫叵得，內則病害其身，外則誅家滅族，禍延親里，現受眾苦，後受地獄長夜之苦，生生障道，無解脫期。此乃世間現見，何實可論，鈍使愛論攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, broadly distinguishing the false: the correct essence is profound and cut off from ordinary reach, difficult to know at a single glance; and, further, names belonging to the deviant and the lesser become mixed up with the correct and the great — like fish-eyes mingled together with genuine pearls — hence the false must be distinguished out. This is done under six senses: first, distinguishing in terms of the ordinary; second, distinguishing in terms of the outsider; third, distinguishing in terms of the lesser vehicle; fourth, distinguishing in terms of the one-sided; fifth, distinguishing by way of illustration; sixth, distinguishing in terms of awakening.

First, distinguishing in terms of the ordinary: the Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: worldly canons, too, speak of "the real," meaning by this the protection of the nation and the governance of the household. Non-Buddhist outsiders, too, speak of "the real," meaning by this their own deviant wisdom and twisted understanding, which they take to be real. The Hīnayāna, too, speaks of "the real," meaning by this the weariness of suffering and the rest it seeks, taking the one-sided real truth as "the real." All such cases as these possess the name "real" alone, without its actual sense. Why is this? The world's own delusive sorcery and magical arts, too, are called "real," yet these are, for the most part, the workings of ghosts and spirits and their bewitchments — once this teaching enters the mind, one becomes deluded, intoxicated, and deranged, boasting of one's own excellence, claiming to surpass the truly real, setting up strange, unheard-of displays to stir the crowd, exhibiting bizarre and extraordinary marks. Some fill a skull with excrement and, before a great crowd, open their mouth wide and gulp it down; some devour raw fish and rotting flesh, heaping their plate ever higher; some go about naked or in tattered clothing, boasting arrogantly of their disregard for propriety; some come and go abruptly, neither asking nor answering — every kind of deceit and trickery, luring in the ignorant, causing them to believe, be defiled, and become attached. Once attached, seeking release proves impossible: inwardly, sickness afflicts their own body; outwardly, their household is executed and their clan destroyed, calamity extending even to their own kin; in this present life they undergo every kind of suffering, and, in the life to come, undergo the suffering of a long night in hell — life after life, obstructing the path, with no hope of ever attaining liberation. This is something plainly seen in the world — what "real" could there possibly be in it? It falls under the category of dull afflictions clinging to their own theories.

若周孔經籍，治法禮法，兵法醫法，天文地理，八卦五行，世間墳典，孝以治家，忠以治國，各親其親，各子其子，敬上愛下，仁義揖讓，安于百姓，霸立社禝；若失此法，彊者陵弱，天下焦遑，民無聊生，鳥不暇栖，獸不暇伏；若依此法，天下太平，牛馬內向，當知此法，乃是愛民治國，而稱為實。《金光明》云：「釋提桓因種種勝論。」即其義也。蓋十善意耳，修十善上符天心，諸天歡喜，求天然報，此法為勝，故言勝論耳。又大梵天王，說出欲論，即是修定出欲淤泥，亦是愛論攝耳。世又方術，服藥長生，練形易色，飛仙隱形者，稱此藥方祕要真實，此亦愛論鈍使攝耳。

If the classics of Zhou and Confucius — the laws of governance, the laws of ritual propriety, the arts of war, the arts of medicine, astronomy and geography, the eight trigrams and the five phases, the world's own ancient canons — filial piety for governing the household, loyalty for governing the state, each cherishing their own kin, each caring for their own children, respecting those above and cherishing those below, benevolence and righteousness, courteous deference, bringing peace to the common people, establishing the altars of the hegemon's own state — were this teaching lost, the strong would trample the weak, the whole world thrown into anxious turmoil, the people unable to find any peace in their own lives, the birds given no time to roost, the beasts given no time to lie down; were this teaching relied upon, the whole world would be at great peace, oxen and horses turned inward toward home — one should know that this teaching, being precisely what cherishes the people and governs the state, is accordingly called "the real." The Suvarṇaprabhāsa Sūtra says, "Śakra, Lord of the Gods, teaches various supreme treatises" — this is precisely this same sense. This refers simply to the sense of the ten wholesome actions: cultivating the ten wholesome actions accords, above, with the heart of heaven, delighting every deva, and seeking the natural reward of heaven itself — this teaching, being supreme, is accordingly called a "supreme treatise." Again, the great Brahmā king teaches the treatise on transcending desire — this is precisely cultivating concentration to rise out of the mire of desire, and this, too, falls under the category of theories still bound by affection. The world, further, has its own esoteric arts: taking elixirs for long life, refining the body and transforming its appearance, becoming a flying immortal or rendering oneself invisible — those who call this secret, essential medicinal recipe "true and real" likewise fall under the category of dull afflictions clinging to their own theories.

二就外簡者，即是外道典籍也。若服藥求知，聰利明達，推尋道理，稱此藥方為勝為實者，藥力薄知，不能鑒遠，觸藥則失，藥歇則失，亦非實也。若此間莊老，無為無欲，天真虛靜，息諸誇企，棄聖絕智等，直是虛無其抱，尚不出單四見外，何關聖法？縱令出單四見外，尚墮複四見中，見網中行，非解脫道。若外國論力受梨昌募，撰《五百明難》，其一云：「瞿曇為一究竟道，為眾多究竟道？」佛言：「但一究竟道。」論力云：「云何諸師各各說究竟道？」佛指鹿頭：「汝識其不？」論力言：「識，究竟道中，其為第一。」佛言：「若其得究竟道，云何自捨其道為我弟子耶？」論力即悟，歎佛法中，獨一究竟道。又如長爪云：「一切論可破，一切語可轉，觀諸法實相，于久不得一法入心。」《釋論》云：「長爪執亦有亦無見。」又云：「亦計不可說見。」

如斯流類，百千萬種，虛妄戲論，為惑流轉，見網浩然，邪智瀾漫，觸境生著。或時襵揲，有無為有，無有無無為無，有非有非無為有，無非有非無為無，百千番牒，悉皆見倒，生死諸邊，非真實也。《大經》云：「被無明枷，繫生死柱，遶二十五有不能得脫。」即此義也。

Second, distinguishing in terms of the outsider: this refers to the canonical texts of the non-Buddhist outsiders. If one takes medicine seeking knowledge, becoming sharp, keen, and clear-sighted, reasoning out principle, and calls this medicinal recipe supreme and real — the power of the medicine is shallow knowledge, unable to perceive anything far-reaching; once one comes into contact with the drug it is lost, and once the drug's effect wears off it is lost as well — this, too, is not "the real." As for the Zhuangzi and Laozi of this land, with their non-action and freedom from desire, their heavenly, natural quiescence, resting from every boastful ambition, discarding sagehood and cutting off cleverness, and the rest — this is simply an empty, vacuous embrace of nothingness, not even escaping the single fourfold tetralemma of views, let alone having anything to do with the sagely Dharma. Even granting that it escapes the single fourfold tetralemma, it still falls into the compound fourfold tetralemma — walking within the net of views is not the path of liberation. As for the foreign debater Lunli, commissioned by King Lichang, who composed the Five Hundred Points of Difficulty — one of these asks: "Is Gautama's own path a single, ultimate path, or are there many ultimate paths?" The Buddha answers: "There is only the single, ultimate path." Lunli asks: "Why, then, do the various teachers each speak of their own ultimate path?" The Buddha points to a deer-headed ascetic: "Do you recognize him?" Lunli says: "I recognize him — among those on the ultimate path, he is counted foremost." The Buddha says: "If he had truly attained the ultimate path, why would he abandon his own path to become my own disciple?" Lunli, at this, was immediately awakened, and praised the Buddhadharma as alone possessing the single, ultimate path. Again, as Dīrghanakha says: "Every theory can be refuted, every statement can be overturned — contemplating the real mark of all dharmas, for a long while, not a single dharma entered my mind." The Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: "Dīrghanakha clung to the view of both existence and non-existence." And again: "He also reckoned the view of the inexpressible."

Kinds and classes such as these — hundreds, thousands, tens of thousands of varieties — are idle theorizing born of delusion, carried along by confusion, the net of views vast and sprawling, deviant wisdom spilling over without bound, giving rise to attachment the moment it meets any object. Sometimes folding and unfolding: existence taken as existence; non-existence taken as non-existence; existence that is neither existence nor non-existence taken as existence; non-existence that is neither existence nor non-existence taken as non-existence — turned over a hundred, a thousand times, all of it, without exception, an inverted view, every one of them merely another edge of birth-and-death, none of them the truly real. The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Bound by the yoke of ignorance, tied to the pillar of birth-and-death, circling the twenty-five states of existence, unable to attain release" — this is precisely this same sense.

三、就小簡者，聲聞法中亦云：「離有離無名聖中道。」《大集》云：「拘隣如沙門，最初獲得真實之知見。」然小乘不運大悲，不濟眾生，功德力薄，不求作佛，不深窮實相，則智慧劣弱，雖云離有離無名聖中道，乃以斷常二見為二邊，真諦為中道。真無漏慧名為見，證涅槃法名為知，雖斷見思，除滅分段，而住草庵，非究竟理。對前生死有邊，即是涅槃無邊，二俱可破可壞，非真實道，故不名實相也。

Third, distinguishing in terms of the lesser vehicle: within the śrāvaka's own dharma, too, it is said: "Departing from both existence and non-existence is named the noble middle way." The Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "The śramaṇa Kauṇḍinya was the very first to attain the true and real knowledge and vision." Yet the Hīnayāna does not carry forth great compassion, does not deliver sentient beings, its own power of merit being weak; it does not seek to become a buddha, nor does it deeply fathom the real mark, and so its wisdom remains inferior and weak. Though it, too, speaks of departing from both existence and non-existence as the noble middle way, it takes the two views of annihilation and eternalism as the two extremes, and the real truth as the middle way. True, untainted wisdom is named "seeing"; realizing the dharma of nirvāṇa is named "knowing" — though it severs views and thought, and eliminates segmented birth-and-death, it still dwells within the thatched hut, not the ultimate principle. Set against the prior extreme of birth-and-death, this is simply the extreme of nirvāṇa — both alike can be broken and destroyed, neither being the truly real path, and hence this is not named the real mark.

四、就偏簡者，諸大乘經，共二乘人帶方便說者，名字既同，義須分別。如《摩訶衍》中云：「三乘之人，同以無言說道斷煩惱。」《中論》云：「諸法實相，三人共得」者，二乘之人雖共稟無言說道，自求出苦，無大悲心，得空則止，鈍根菩薩亦爾。利根菩薩大悲為物，深求實相。共實相者，智如螢火，是故非實；不共實相，智如日光，是故為實。《大經》云：「第一義空名為智慧。」二乘但空，空無智慧；菩薩得不但空，即中道慧。此慧寂而常照，二乘但得其寂，不得寂照，故非實相。菩薩得寂，又得寂照，即是實相。

見不空者，復有多種：一、見不空，次第斷結，從淺至深，此乃相似之實，非正實也。二、見不空，具一切法，初阿字門則解一切義，即中、即假、即空，不一不異，無三無一。二乘但一即；別教但二即；圓具三即，三即真實相也。《釋論》云：「何等是實相？謂菩薩入於一相，知無量相，知無量相又入一相。」二乘但入一相，不能知無量相；別教雖入一相，又入無量相，不能更入一相。利根菩薩即空故入一相，即假故知無量相，即中故更入一相。如此菩薩，深求智度大海，一心即三，是真實相體也。

華嚴不共二乘，但約菩薩：三智次第，得亦非正實；不次第得者，是正實也。若方等中，四人得三智，三人為虛；一人為實。大品三慧說三智屬三人，前二不深求，淺而非實；後一人深求一心三智，是故是實。此經「汝實我子」，無復四三之人，十方諦求，更無餘乘，但一實相智，決了聲聞法，但說無上道，純是一實體也。

Fourth, distinguishing in terms of the one-sided: among the various Mahāyāna sūtras, wherever the two lesser vehicles are addressed together, accompanied by skillful means, the names used are identical, yet their sense must be distinguished. As the Mahāyāna teaching says: "The persons of the three vehicles alike sever affliction by means of the path without verbal expression." The Mūlamadhyamakakārikā says, "the real mark of all dharmas is attained jointly by persons of all three vehicles" — though the two lesser vehicles' own persons alike receive this path without verbal expression, they seek only their own release from suffering, without the mind of great compassion, and, once attaining emptiness, they come to rest — the dull-faculty bodhisattva is exactly the same. The sharp-faculty bodhisattva, moved by great compassion for the sake of beings, seeks the real mark deeply. The real mark shared in common with the two lesser vehicles has a wisdom like the light of a firefly, and hence is not "the real"; the real mark not shared in common with them has a wisdom like the light of the sun, and hence is "the real." The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra says, "The emptiness of ultimate meaning is named wisdom." The two lesser vehicles attain only bare emptiness, and bare emptiness is without wisdom; the bodhisattva attains an emptiness that is not bare, which is precisely the wisdom of the middle way. This wisdom is quiescent, yet constantly illuminating; the two lesser vehicles attain only its quiescence, not its quiescent illumination, and hence theirs is not the real mark. The bodhisattva attains quiescence, and, further, attains quiescent illumination as well — this is precisely the real mark.

As for seeing non-emptiness, there are, further, many kinds. First, seeing non-emptiness by severing the fetters in sequence, proceeding from the shallow to the deep — this is merely the real of resemblance, not the correct real. Second, seeing non-emptiness as complete with every dharma: the very first "a" syllable-gate already resolves every meaning at once — at once the middle, at once the provisional, at once emptiness, neither one nor different, neither three nor one. The two lesser vehicles attain only the single "at-once"; the distinct teaching attains only two of the "at-onces"; the perfect teaching is complete with all three "at-onces" — these three "at-onces" together are the true and real mark. The Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: "What is the real mark? It is the bodhisattva entering into the one mark, knowing the immeasurable marks, and, knowing the immeasurable marks, entering once more into the one mark." The two lesser vehicles enter only the one mark, unable to know the immeasurable marks; the distinct teaching, though it enters the one mark and further enters the immeasurable marks, is unable to enter once more into the one mark. The sharp-faculty bodhisattva, being at once empty, enters the one mark; being at once provisional, knows the immeasurable marks; being at once the middle, enters once more into the one mark. Such a bodhisattva, seeking deeply into the great ocean of the perfection of wisdom, finds the single mind to be, at once, the three — this is the essence of the true and real mark.

The Avataṃsaka is not shared in common with the two lesser vehicles at all, being addressed solely to the bodhisattva: attaining the three kinds of wisdom in sequence is likewise not the correct real; attaining them not in sequence is the correct real. Within the vaipulya teachings, four kinds of persons attain the three kinds of wisdom — three of these persons attain it emptily, and only one attains it as real. The Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra's own three wisdoms are spoken of as belonging to three kinds of persons: the first two do not seek deeply, and, being shallow, are not the real; the last of these three persons alone seeks deeply into the single mind's own three kinds of wisdom, and hence alone is the real. This present sūtra's own "you are truly my own children" admits no longer any of these various persons, whether four or three — sought throughout the ten directions, no other vehicle whatsoever remains, only the one wisdom of the real mark, decisively resolving the śrāvaka's own dharma, teaching only the unsurpassed path — purely and solely the one, real essence.

《大經》云：「一實諦者，則無有二，無有二故名一實諦；又、一實諦名無虛偽；又、一實諦無有顛倒；又、一實諦非魔所說；又、一實諦名常樂我淨。」常、樂、我、淨，無空、假、中之異。異則為二，二故非一實諦；一實諦即空即假即中，無異無二，故名一實諦。若有三異，則為虛偽，虛偽之法，不名一實諦；無三異故，即一實諦。若異即是顛倒未破，非一實諦；無三異故無顛倒，無顛倒故名一實諦。異者，不名一乘；三法不異，具足圓滿，名為一乘。是乘高廣，眾寶莊挍，故名一實諦。

魔雖不證別異空、假，而能說別異空、假；若空、假、中不異者，魔不能說，魔不能說，名一實諦。若空、假、中異者名顛倒，不異者名不顛倒，不顛倒故無煩惱，無煩惱故名為淨；無煩惱則無業，無業故名為我；無業故無報，無報故名樂；無報則無生死，無生死則名常；常樂我淨，名一實諦。

The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra says: "The one real truth has no second — and, having no second, it is named the one real truth. Again, the one real truth is named free of falsehood; again, the one real truth has no inverted view; again, the one real truth is not what a demon speaks; again, the one real truth is named permanence, bliss, self, and purity." Permanence, bliss, self, and purity have no distinction of emptiness, the provisional, and the middle. Were there such a distinction, this would make two, and, being two, it would not be the one real truth; the one real truth is at once empty, at once provisional, and at once the middle, with no distinction and no duality, and hence it is named the one real truth. If there were three distinct things, this would be falsehood, and a false dharma cannot be named the one real truth; being without these three distinctions, it is precisely the one real truth. If there were such a distinction, this would be an inverted view not yet broken through, and not the one real truth; being without the three distinctions, there is no inverted view, and, being without any inverted view, it is named the one real truth. Where there is distinction, it cannot be named the one vehicle; the three dharmas being undistinguished, complete and perfectly full, this is named the one vehicle. This vehicle, lofty and broad, adorned with every kind of jewel, is accordingly named the one real truth.

Though Māra cannot realize distinct and separate emptiness and the provisional, he is nonetheless able to speak of distinct and separate emptiness and the provisional; but if emptiness, the provisional, and the middle are undistinguished, Māra is unable to speak of this at all — and it is precisely because Māra is unable to speak of it that it is named the one real truth. If emptiness, the provisional, and the middle are distinguished, this is named an inverted view; if undistinguished, this is named free of inverted views. Being free of inverted views, there is no affliction; being free of affliction, this is named purity. Being free of affliction, there is no karma; being free of karma, this is named self. Being free of karma, there is no retribution; being free of retribution, this is named bliss. Being free of retribution, there is no birth-and-death; being free of birth-and-death, this is named permanence. Permanence, bliss, self, and purity — this is named the one real truth.

一實諦者，即是實相；實相者，即經之正體也。如是實相，即空、假、中：即空故，破一切凡夫愛論，破一切外道見論；即假故，破三藏四門小實，破三人共見小實；即中故，破次第偏實，無復諸顛倒小偏等因果四諦之法，亦無小偏等三寶之名，唯有實相因果，四諦、三寶，宛然具足。亦具諸方便因果、四諦、三寶。何以故？實相是法界海故，唯此三諦，即是真實相也。

又、開次第之實，即是圓實，證道是同故。又、開三人共得實，深求即到底故。又、開三藏之實，決了聲聞法。又、開諸見論實，於見不動而修道品故。又、開諸愛論實，魔界即佛界故，行於非道，通達佛道。一切諸法中，悉有安樂性。即絕待明實，是經體也。

The one real truth is precisely the real mark; the real mark is precisely the sūtra's own correct essence. This same real mark is at once empty, at once provisional, and at once the middle: being at once empty, it breaks through every ordinary being's own theories born of attachment, and breaks through every non-Buddhist outsider's own theories born of views; being at once provisional, it breaks through the Tripiṭaka teaching's own fourfold gate of lesser reality, and breaks through the shared reality that the three kinds of persons alike see as lesser; being at once the middle, it breaks through the sequential, one-sided reality, so that no inverted, lesser, one-sided dharma of cause, fruit, or the Four Noble Truths remains, nor any lesser, one-sided name of the Three Jewels — there remains only the real mark's own cause and fruit, the Four Noble Truths, and the Three Jewels, vividly and completely present. It is likewise complete with every expedient means' own cause and fruit, Four Noble Truths, and Three Jewels. Why is this? Because the real mark is itself the ocean of the dharma-realm — these three truths alone are precisely the true and real mark.

Again, opening the sequential reality is precisely the perfect reality, since the realization of the path is identical either way. Again, opening the reality jointly attained by the three kinds of persons, since seeking it deeply, one arrives, either way, at the very same bottom. Again, opening the Tripiṭaka teaching's own reality decisively resolves the śrāvaka's own dharma. Again, opening every theory born of views' own reality, since, without being moved from views, one nonetheless cultivates the factors of the path. Again, opening every theory born of attachment's own reality, since Māra's own realm is itself already the buddha-realm — walking amid what is not the path, one thoroughly penetrates through to the buddha-path. Within every dharma without exception, the nature of peace and bliss is entirely present. This is clarifying the real in the absolute sense, and this is the sūtra's own essence.

Reading note

The "one real truth" passage closes item four by escalating from the relative reading (the real mark distinguished from what the two lesser vehicles or the distinct teaching attain) to the absolute reading already familiar from this treatise's own treatment of "wondrous": permanence, bliss, self, and purity have no internal distinction of empty/provisional/middle at all, and it is precisely this seamlessness — the fact that "Māra is unable to speak of it" — that qualifies it as the one real truth. The five-fold "opening" that follows (the sequential reality, the reality shared by three kinds of persons, the Tripiṭaka's own reality, theories of view, theories of attachment) reprises, one level up, the very simplifications just carried out across items one through four: every one of the false or partial "reals" already distinguished away is now reopened and shown to have been the real mark all along, differently expressed — "Māra's own realm is itself already the buddha-realm."

The third proposition, name and sense the same while essence differs: taking "wondrous being" as the name and "wondrous form" as the sense, with "ultimate emptiness" as the essence — here the two are the same while the one differs, hence it is said that name and sense are the same while essence differs. Again, taking "emptiness" as the name and "suchness" as the sense, with "wondrous being" as the essence — this, too, is a case of the two being the same while the one differs. The remaining two gates are exactly the same, hence it is said that name and sense are the same while essence differs.

The fourth proposition, name and sense differing while essence is the same: as with "wondrous being" and the rest, each name differs from the others; and "true, good form" and the rest, each sense differs from the others; and yet they converge together upon the identical single essence, with no second direction at all — hence it is said that name and sense differ while essence is the same. The remaining three gates are exactly the same.

In the first three propositions, name and sense are never fully merged. In the first proposition, seeking a single name, one attains a single sense and a single essence — within that particular gate, all is perfectly merged, with no bearing on any other matter. In the second proposition, seeking differing names, one recognizes differing senses and differing essences — essence, sense, and name are here least merged of all, and this is easily understood. In the third proposition, the essence itself is not merged, and though name and sense are the same, they never truly come together in the end — each is simply a particular gate clarifying its own sense. For one who fails to grasp this, disputes arise from exactly this point — sometimes the lesser vehicle encroaching upon the great, sometimes the great vehicle wresting something away from the lesser. Why is this? The Hīnayāna, wishing to sever birth-and-death, hears of "ultimate" emptiness as not being bare emptiness, and, following its own inclination and desire, insists that this must simply mean bare emptiness, clinging to this and giving rise to dispute. Again, the Hīnayāna, wishing to sever birth-and-death, holds it is "not existent"; and, breaking through the affliction of clinging to nirvāṇa, holds it is "not non-existent" — hearing the middle way described as neither existent nor non-existent, and bolstering its own lesser inclination with this, it insists this must simply be its own prior teaching of neither existent nor non-existent, and so, over these two gates especially, dispute frequently arises. If it hears of the mind of the middle, real principle, which is plainly at odds with the lesser vehicle, no dispute arises at all — why is this? The two lesser vehicles delight in emptiness, yet now hear of existence; the two lesser vehicles reduce body and extinguish wisdom to ashes, yet now hear of mind and wisdom — being so plainly at odds with their own inclination, they do not cling to this to make a dispute of it. It is precisely because the lesser encroaches by pilfering from the great that dispute arises. As for the great wresting something from the lesser: Mahāyāna students, seeing the emptiness-gate and the neither-empty-nor-existent gate shared in common with the three vehicles, sharing an identical name with the two lesser vehicles, fail to see the deeper intent, and so dismiss them as belonging to some deceptive appearance, not the true doctrine. Taking up only the two gates of "wondrous being" and "both empty and existent," and citing these as the perfect and eternal dharma, whether they concede these two gates to the lesser vehicles or not — this dispute is comparatively minor. But if one knows that emptiness here means not bare emptiness, and that "neither existent nor non-existent" here means excluding both extremes, then all four gates alike are claimed away from the lesser vehicle, and the lesser vehicle bitterly disputes over these same two gates.

Furthermore, even among the Mahāyāna's own four gates, name and sense are not fully merged, and gate disputes with gate, each devouring the other — how much more so, then, the Hīnayāna, a mere jackal daring to encroach upon the lion — could it possibly fail to be devoured by it?

三句生諍，非今經體也。第四句名義異而體同，體有眾義，功用甚多。四門隨緣，種種異稱，以體融故，圓應眾名，法體既同，異名異義，而不諍也。其相云何？今當略說。《無量義》云：「無量義者，從一法生。其一法者所謂實相。」實相之相，無相不相，不相無相，名為實相，此從不可破壞真實得名。又此實相諸佛得法，故稱妙有。妙有雖不可見，諸佛能見，故稱真善妙色。實相非二邊之有，故名畢竟空。空理湛然，非一非異，故名如如。實相寂滅，故名涅槃。覺了不改，故名虛空佛性。多所含受，故名如來藏。寂照靈知，故名中實理心。不依於有，亦不附無，故名中道。最上無過，故名第一義諦。如是等種種異名，俱名實相，種種所以，俱是實相功能，其體既圓，名義無隔，蓋是經之正體也。

復次，諸法既是實相之異名，而實相當體；又實相亦是諸法之異名，而諸法當體。妙有不可破壞，故名實相。諸佛能見，故名真善妙色。不雜餘物，名畢竟空。無二無別，故名如如。覺了不變，故名佛性。含備諸法，故名如來藏。寂滅靈知，故名中實理心。遮離諸邊，故名中道。無上無過，名第一義諦。隨以一法當體，隨用立稱，例此可知。《大經》云：「解脫之法多諸名字，百句解脫，秖一解脫。」《大論》云：「若如法觀佛，般若與涅槃，是三則一相，其實無有異。」若得此意，知種種名，皆名實相，亦名般若，亦名解脫。三法亦是諸法名，諸法亦是三法體(云云)。

The first three propositions all give rise to dispute, and are not this present sūtra's own essence. The fourth proposition — name and sense differing while essence is the same — has an essence possessing many senses, its function and use exceedingly abundant. The four gates, following conditions, take up various differing designations, and, because the essence itself is fully merged, it perfectly responds to every name; the dharma's own essence being identical throughout, differing names and differing senses give rise to no dispute at all. What, then, is its own mark? This shall now be briefly stated. The Sūtra of Immeasurable Meanings says: "Immeasurable meanings arise from a single dharma. That single dharma is what is called the real mark." The real mark's own mark is markless, yet not without mark; without mark, yet not markless — it is accordingly named "the real mark," this name obtained from its own indestructible, true reality. Again, this real mark is the very dharma every buddha has attained, and hence it is called "wondrous being." Though wondrous being cannot ordinarily be seen, every buddha is able to see it, and hence it is called "true, good, wondrous form." The real mark is not "being" in the sense of either of the two extremes, and hence it is named "ultimate emptiness." The principle of emptiness, still and unmoving, neither one nor different, and hence it is named "suchness." The real mark is quiescent and extinguished, and hence it is named "nirvāṇa." Awakened understanding, unchanging, and hence it is named "the buddha-nature of empty space." Containing and receiving so much within it, and hence it is named "the tathāgatagarbha." Quiescent illumination and numinous knowing, and hence it is named "the mind of the middle, real principle." Depending neither upon existence nor attaching to non-existence, and hence it is named "the middle way." Supreme, with nothing surpassing it, and hence it is named "the truth of ultimate meaning." Every one of these various, differing names is, alike, named "the real mark"; their various reasons are, alike, the real mark's own functions and capacities. Its essence being already perfectly merged, name and sense present no barrier between them at all — this, then, is the sūtra's own correct essence.

Again, since every dharma is itself a differing name for the real mark, the real mark is their own proper essence; and, again, the real mark is itself a differing name for every dharma, so every dharma is its own proper essence. Wondrous being cannot be destroyed, and hence it is named "the real mark." Every buddha is able to see it, and hence it is named "true, good, wondrous form." Unmixed with any other thing, it is named "ultimate emptiness." Without duality and without distinction, it is named "suchness." Awakened understanding, unchanging, it is named "buddha-nature." Containing and providing for every dharma, it is named "the tathāgatagarbha." Quiescent extinction and numinous knowing, it is named "the mind of the middle, real principle." Excluding and departing from every extreme, it is named "the middle way." Unsurpassed, with nothing beyond it, it is named "the truth of ultimate meaning." Taking up any single dharma as its own proper essence, and establishing a designation according to its function, may be understood by this same pattern. The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra says: "The dharma of liberation carries many names — a hundred phrases for liberation, and yet only the one liberation." The Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: "If one contemplates the Buddha in accordance with the dharma, prajñā and nirvāṇa — these three are but a single mark; in reality, there is no difference among them." If one grasps this same sense, one knows that every various name is, alike, named "the real mark," and is also named "prajñā," and is also named "liberation." The three dharmas are themselves, too, names for every dharma, and every dharma is, too, the essence of these three dharmas, and so on.

三、譬顯者，譬如一人名金師，能鍛金，其體黃，譬初句法也。譬如一人名青，而能作漆，其身白淨。又、一人名烏，能研朱，其身則紫。如是等無量百千，名技身異，譬第二句。譬如百人，同姓同名，同解一技，而其身各異，譬第三句。譬如一人，遭亂家禍，處處換姓、處處變名，如張儀、范蠡之類，涉多官職，身備眾位。若從多技得名，書畫金鐵等師；若從文官，儒林、中散；若從武官，熊渠、次飛。隨處換名，譬名異；隨技得稱，譬義異；而體是一，更非異人。經言：「王家力士，一人當千」，此人未必力敵於千，直以種種技藝能勝千故，故稱當千。工遍眾技，無技不通，仕具眾位，無官不歷。是不可壞人，妙技術人，有體氣人，無過患人，遍通達人能破敵人，上族姓人，富財技人，多知人，中庶信直人，頂蓋人，譬第四句法也。譬顯冷然，故知前三句屬別意，後一句屬圓意也。

Third, illustrating by way of comparison: it is like a person named Goldsmith, able to forge gold, whose body itself is yellow — this illustrates the first proposition's own dharma. It is like a person named Blue, who is also able to make lacquer, whose body is pure white. Again, a person named Crow, able to grind cinnabar, whose body is purple. Cases of this kind, immeasurable in the hundreds and thousands, with differing names, skills, and bodies, illustrate the second proposition. It is like a hundred persons sharing the same surname and given name, all understanding the identical single skill, and yet each of their bodies differs — this illustrates the third proposition. It is like a single person who, meeting with the calamity of a household thrown into chaos, changes his surname wherever he goes, alters his given name wherever he goes — of the kind of Zhang Yi or Fan Li — passing through many official posts, his own single body furnished with every rank. If named according to his many skills, he is called a master of calligraphy, painting, metalworking, and the rest; if named according to civil office, a scholar of the Confucian forest or a court attendant; if named according to military office, Xiong Qu or Cifei. Changing his name according to where he is illustrates differing names; being designated according to his skill illustrates differing senses; and yet the essence is one and the same — never a different person at all. The sūtra says, "A mighty warrior of the royal household, a single man worth a thousand" — this person need not actually match the strength of a thousand men in combat; it is simply that, by means of every kind of skill and art, he is able to surpass a thousand, and hence he is called "worth a thousand." Skilled in every craft, there is no skill he does not master; serving in every office, there is no post he has not held. He is the indestructible person, the person of wondrous skill and art, the person of robust constitution, the person without fault or flaw, the person of pervasive penetration able to defeat every enemy, the person of noble lineage, the person rich in wealth and skill, the person of great knowledge, the person honest and upright among commoners, the person crowned above all — this illustrates the fourth proposition's own dharma. The illustration, cool and clear, thereby makes known that the first three propositions belong to the particular sense, while the last alone belongs to the perfect sense.

四、約四隨者。

問：
實相一法，何故名義紛然？

答：
隨彼根機，種種差別，赴欲、赴宜、赴治、赴悟。例如世人，學數則捨大，修衍則棄小，習空則惡有，善地則彈中。既不欲聞，聞之不悅，無心信受，不滅煩惱，不發道心。各於己典，偏習成性，得作未來聞法根緣。如來于時，以佛眼觀其信等諸根，以若干言辭隨應方便而為說法。為有根性，說妙有、真善妙色，不違不逆，信戒忍進，蕩除空見，即能悟入，契於實相。為空根性說畢竟空、如如、涅槃等，諦聽諦受，以善攻惡，無相最上。為亦空亦有根性，說虛空佛性、如來藏、中實理心，欣然起善，離非心淨。為非空非有根性者，即說非有非無中道，遮於二邊，不來不去，不斷不常，不一不異等。欲得聽聞，欣如渴飲，信樂修習，眾善發生，執見皆祛，無惡不盡，第一義理豁然明發。

隨此四根故四門異說，說異故名異，功別故義異，悟理不殊，體終是一。故求那跋摩云：「諸論各異端，修行理無二，偏執有是非，達者無違諍。」故四隨殊唱，是一實之異名耳。

Fourth, in terms of the four accommodations.

Question: If the real mark is a single dharma, why are its names and senses so profuse?

Answer: It is accommodated to the various differences of each hearer's own faculties — accommodating their desire, accommodating what is fitting, accommodating the antidote, accommodating their awakening. It is comparable to ordinary people in the world: one who studies the Abhidharma's own enumerations sets aside the great vehicle; one who cultivates the Mahāyāna abandons the lesser; one habituated to emptiness detests existence; one who favors the bodhisattva grounds dismisses the middle. Not wishing to hear something, one who hears it takes no delight in it, has no mind to believe and accept it, does not extinguish affliction by it, and does not arouse the aspiration for the path from it. Each, habituated one-sidedly to their own particular canon, forms this into their own settled disposition, which then becomes the condition for their own future hearing of the Dharma. The Tathāgata, at the appropriate time, observes with the buddha-eye their faith and every other faculty, and, with a certain number of words, teaches the Dharma according to whatever skillful means suits them. For those of the faculty for existence, he teaches wondrous being, true, good, wondrous form — neither contrary nor resistant to their own inclination — faith, precepts, forbearance, and diligence sweeping away their own view of emptiness, so that they are thereby able to awaken and enter, according with the real mark. For those of the faculty for emptiness, he teaches ultimate emptiness, suchness, nirvāṇa, and the rest — listened to and received with full attention, using good to attack evil, the markless being supreme. For those of the faculty for both emptiness and existence, he teaches the buddha-nature of empty space, the tathāgatagarbha, the mind of the middle, real principle — joyfully giving rise to goodness, departing from what is not real, the mind made pure. For those of the faculty for neither emptiness nor existence, he teaches precisely the middle way of neither existence nor non-existence, excluding both extremes — neither coming nor going, neither annihilated nor eternal, neither one nor different, and the rest. Wishing to hear it, they delight in it as one thirsty delights in drink; believing and taking joy in cultivating it, every kind of good arises, every clinging view is thereby dispelled, no evil remains unexhausted, and the principle of ultimate meaning bursts forth, radiantly clear.

Accommodated to these same four faculties, the four gates are taught differently; because the teaching differs, the names differ; because the function differs, the senses differ — yet the principle awakened to is no different, and the essence, in the end, is one and the same. Hence Guṇavarman says: "Every treatise has its own differing point of departure, yet in cultivating practice, the principle admits of no second; those who cling one-sidedly raise up disputes of right and wrong, while the one who has truly penetrated it holds no contention at all." Hence the four accommodations' own differing proclamations are simply differing names for the one real thing.

Reading note

The four-proposition scheme (name/sense/essence all same; all differing; name-and-sense same but essence differing; name-and-sense differing but essence the same) diagnoses sectarian dispute as a category error: the first three propositions all treat some partial coincidence of vocabulary as though it settled the question of essence, which is exactly how "the lesser encroaches on the great" and "the great wrests something from the lesser" both arise — real terminological overlap mistaken for, or willfully denied as, real doctrinal identity. Only the fourth proposition — differing names, identical essence — actually describes the real mark's own relationship to "wondrous being," "ultimate emptiness," "the tathāgatagarbha," and the rest: eleven synonyms surveyed here, each simply naming the same single reality by way of a different one of its own functions. The itinerant-official illustration makes the same point through a human life rather than a doctrine: Zhang Yi or Fan Li changing names and titles with every post held is not eleven different people but one, and disputing over which title is his "real" one misses that every title was equally real, equally his, equally partial. The four-accommodations closing move is this treatise's most direct statement yet of why the Buddha teaches multiple, seemingly incompatible names at all: not confusion or inconsistency on his part, but a single physician offering different medicine to different patients — "accommodating desire, accommodating what is fitting, accommodating the antidote, accommodating awakening." This closes one dharma under many names (一法異名) in its entirety, the third of the seven headings under Clarifying the Essence. The treatise continues with the fourth heading, the gates that enter the essence (入實相門).

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, clarifying the gates that enter the real mark: the real mark is profound and subtle, its principle deep and unfathomable, like ascending a sheer chasm, which necessarily requires a flying ladder; to reach the true source, it is necessary to rely on teaching and practice, and hence teaching and practice are taken as the gates. The text below says, "By means of the Buddha's own teaching-gate, one departs from the suffering of the three realms," and, "Once the buddha's child has practiced the path, in a future life he shall attain buddhahood" — "gate" is named for its capacity to lead through, precisely this same sense. This is briefly treated under four senses: first, briefly showing the marks of the gates; second, showing the contemplation that enters the gates; third, showing coarse and wondrous; fourth, showing opening and revealing.

As for showing the marks of the gates: the Buddhadharma cannot be directly displayed as such, and, taught according to conditions, it must necessarily be expounded through four propositions concerning principle, able to lead the practitioner into the ground of true reality. The Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: "Concerning a dharma of this kind, teaching the siddhānta of ultimate meaning: namely, that all is real; that all is not real; that all is both real and not real; that all is neither real nor not real." All of these, alike, are named the real mark of all dharmas. Yet the real mark is not even a single thing to begin with — how, then, could it be spoken of as four? One should know that these four are simply "the gates that enter the real mark."

It further says: "the four gates enter the cooling pond" — these gates are unobstructed, admitting not only the sharp-faculty but the dull-faculty as well; not only those settled in concentration, but those of scattered mind, single-minded resolve, or diligent striving, are equally admitted. It further says: "Prajñā has four kinds of marks, namely the mark of existence, the markless mark, and even the marks of neither existence nor non-existence." Prajñā, too, is not even a single mark to begin with — how, then, could it have four marks? One should know that these, too, are simply the gates that enter prajñā. It further says: "The perfection of wisdom is like a great blazing flame — it cannot be grasped from any of its four sides, since deviant views are themselves consumed by its fire." If one does not touch the fire, it warms the body and cooks food; if one touches the fire, the fire itself burns the body — the body, once burned, renders the warming of food useless. The four gates fundamentally lead through to prajñā, removing affliction and accomplishing the great task; but if one clings and becomes attached to them, this becomes a deviant view, burning the Dharma-body itself. Once the Dharma-body is burned, what could the four gates possibly lead through to? Only by not touching the fire can the gate truly lead through.

若以佛教為門者，教略為四(云云)。若於一教，以四句詮理，即是四門，四四合為十六門。若以行為門者，稟教修觀，因思得入，即以行為門。藉教發真，則以教為門。若初聞教，如快馬見鞭影即入正路者，不須修觀。若初修觀，如夜見電光即得見道者，不更須教。並是往昔善根習熟，今於教門得通，名為信行；於觀門得通，名法行。若聞不即悟，應須修觀；於觀悟者，轉成法行。若修觀不悟，更須聽法；聽法得悟，轉名信行。教即為觀門，觀即為教門。

聞教而觀，觀教而聞，教觀相資，則通入成門。教觀合論，則有三十二門，此語其大數耳。細尋於門，實有無量。五百身因，三十二不二門，善財遊法界，值無量知識，說無量教門，無量觀行。如喜見城，千二百門，實相法城，豈唯一轍？經云：「說種種法門，宣示於佛道。」今且約四教，明十六門相。

If the Buddha's own teaching is taken as the gate, the teaching, in brief, is fourfold, and so on. If, within a single teaching, principle is expounded through the four propositions, this is precisely the four gates; four teachings times four propositions together make sixteen gates. If practice is taken as the gate, receiving the teaching and cultivating contemplation, entering by means of reflection — this is practice taken as the gate. Relying on the teaching to arouse the real is teaching taken as the gate. If, upon first hearing the teaching, one is like a swift horse that enters the correct road the very moment it sees the shadow of the whip, one need not further cultivate contemplation. If, upon first cultivating contemplation, one is like one who, seeing a flash of lightning by night, thereby immediately sees the path, one need not further rely on the teaching. Both cases are due to wholesome roots from former lives, already ripened and habituated; one who now gains penetration through the gate of teaching is named "the one who follows through faith"; one who gains penetration through the gate of contemplation is named "the one who follows through the dharma." If, upon hearing, one is not immediately awakened, one ought to cultivate contemplation further; and if awakened through contemplation, one is thereby transformed into "the one who follows through the dharma." If, cultivating contemplation, one is not awakened, one ought to further listen to the teaching; and if awakened through listening to the teaching, one is thereby renamed "the one who follows through faith." Teaching is itself the gate of contemplation, and contemplation is itself the gate of teaching.

Hearing the teaching and thereby contemplating, contemplating and thereby hearing the teaching — teaching and contemplation mutually assisting one another, one thereby leads through and forms the gate. Discussed jointly, teaching and contemplation together yield thirty-two gates — this figure states only their general number. Examined closely, the gates are, in reality, immeasurable. The five hundred bodily causes, the thirty-two non-dual gates; Sudhana, wandering throughout the dharma-realm, encountering immeasurable spiritual friends, is taught immeasurable teaching-gates and immeasurable contemplative practices. As with the City of Gladly Seen, with its twelve hundred gates — the city of the Dharma of the real mark, how could it possibly have only a single track? The sūtra says, "Teaching every kind of Dharma-gate, proclaiming and revealing the buddha-path." Here, then, taking up the Four Teachings, the marks of the sixteen gates shall be clarified.

The Tripiṭaka Teaching's Four Gates 三藏四門

Chinese Source

三藏四門者。初、明有門，謂生死法，本非世性、微塵、父母所作，乃是無明正因緣法，出生諸行煩惱、業、苦，三道悉皆是有。一切有為，無常、苦、空、無我，能發得煗、頂、世第一法，發真無漏因，用真修道。此則道諦，亦是有。子果既斷，得有餘、無餘涅槃。故《大集》云：「甚深之理不可說，第一實義無聲字，陳如比丘於諸法，獲得真實之知見。」此則因滅會真，真亦是有。此是諸阿毘曇論之所申，見有得道，即有門也。

二、空門者，即是彼教析正因緣，無明老死，苦集二諦，三假浮虛，破假實，悉入空平等，發真無漏，因空見真，空即第一義之門也。故須菩提在石室，觀生滅無常入空。因空得道，見佛法身，恐此是《成實論》之所申也。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the Tripiṭaka teaching's own four gates: first, clarifying the gate of existence — namely, that the dharma of birth-and-death is, at root, made neither by some worldly primal nature, nor by atomic particles, nor by one's own parents, but is rather the correct causal dharma of ignorance, giving rise to every formation, affliction, karma, and suffering — the three paths, every one of them, are existence. Every conditioned thing is impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, able to bring forth warmth, summit, and the supreme worldly dharma, arousing the true, untainted cause, and, by means of the true, cultivating the path. This, then, is the truth of the path, and this, too, is existence. Once the causal and resultant fetters are severed, one attains nirvāṇa with residue remaining and nirvāṇa with no residue remaining. Hence the Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra says: "The exceedingly profound principle cannot be spoken of; the truth of ultimate meaning has no sound or syllable — the monk Kauṇḍinya, with respect to every dharma, attained the true and real knowledge and vision." This, then, is the cause extinguished and the truth converged upon, and this truth, too, is existence. This is what every Abhidharma treatise sets forth: seeing existence, one attains the path — this is precisely the gate of existence.

Second, the gate of emptiness: this is precisely that same teaching's own analysis of the correct causal dharma — ignorance, old age and death, the two truths of suffering and arising — the threefold provisional, fleeting and unsubstantial, breaking through the provisional and the real, entering entirely into the equality of emptiness, arousing the true and untainted, seeing the truth by means of emptiness — emptiness is itself the gate of ultimate meaning. Hence Subhūti, within his stone chamber, contemplated arising-and-ceasing's own impermanence and entered emptiness; by means of emptiness he attained the path, and beheld the Buddha's own Dharma-body — this, it is suspected, is what the Tattvasiddhi sets forth.

三、明有、空門者，即是彼教明正因緣生滅，亦有亦空。若稟此教，能破偏執有無之見，見因緣有空，發真無漏，因有無見真，有無即是第一義之門也。此是迦旃延因門入道，故作《昆勒論》，還申此門也。

四、非有非無門者，即是彼教明正因緣生滅，非有非無之理。若稟此教，能破有無邊邪執見，見因緣非有非無，發真無漏，因非有非無見真，非有非無即第一義之門也。惡口車匿，因此入道，未見論來。有人言犢子阿毘曇申此意，彼論明我在第五不可說藏中，我非三世，故非有我；非無為，故非無我。此恐未可定用也。

Third, clarifying the gate of both existence and emptiness: this is precisely that same teaching's own clarification of the correct causal dharma's arising and ceasing as both existent and empty at once. If one receives this teaching, one is able to break through the one-sided clinging view of existence or non-existence, seeing the causal dharma as both existence and emptiness, arousing the true and untainted, seeing the truth by means of both existence and non-existence — existence and non-existence together are themselves the gate of ultimate meaning. This is Kātyāyana's own entry into the path through this gate, and hence he composed the Piṭaka Treatise, likewise setting forth this same gate.

Fourth, the gate of neither existence nor non-existence: this is precisely that same teaching's own clarification of the correct causal dharma's arising and ceasing as the principle of neither existence nor non-existence. If one receives this teaching, one is able to break through the deviant, clinging view at the extremes of existence and non-existence, seeing the causal dharma as neither existence nor non-existence, arousing the true and untainted, seeing the truth by means of neither existence nor non-existence — neither existence nor non-existence is itself the gate of ultimate meaning. Foul-Mouthed Chandaka entered the path by means of this gate, though no treatise setting this forth has yet been found. Some say the Vātsīputrīya Abhidharma sets forth this same sense: that treatise clarifies the self as residing within a fifth, inexpressible category — the self is not encompassed by the three time-periods, and hence is not "existent"; nor is it unconditioned, and hence is not "non-existent" either. This, it is feared, cannot yet be definitively adopted.

Reading note

The fire-and-warmth image opening this heading sets the terms for everything that follows across all sixteen gates: a gate is genuinely useful right up until it is clung to, at which point the very same teaching that "removes affliction and accomplishes the great task" burns the Dharma-body instead. The Tripiṭaka teaching's own four gates are each paired with a specific historical figure's own path to realization — Kauṇḍinya through existence, Subhūti through emptiness, Kātyāyana through both at once, and the more tentative case of Chandaka through neither — grounding what could otherwise read as a purely scholastic fourfold scheme in four distinct, remembered enlightenment stories.

The Shared Teaching's Four Gates 通教四門

Chinese Source

二、明通教四門相者，此是摩訶衍門，通通通別，不可偏取。

今約通通論四門者，上三藏四門，皆滅色入空，如析實人頭等六分，求人不得，故名為空。通教四門皆即色是空，如觀鏡像，六分即空，不待析盡為空。《大論》云：「佛告比丘：觀空即疊，觀疊即空。」此是體門異析門也。

三藏觀生空得道，三藏觀生空得道已，又更觀法空，生、法二境不融。今通門生空即法空，法空即生空，無二無別。《大品》云：色性如我性，我性如色性，此二皆如幻化。

有人言：三藏破計實性，約實法求我不得，但是觀性空；大乘明相自性是空，不須撿已為空。此乃一往之言。《大品》云：常性空，無不性空時，曉了諸法，如幻化水月鏡像，豈止相空而已！

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, clarifying the marks of the shared teaching's own four gates: these are the Mahāyāna's own gates — common-within-the-common, and common-within-the-particular — and cannot be taken up one-sidedly.

Discussing here the four gates in terms of common-within-the-common: the Tripiṭaka's own four gates discussed above all extinguish form to enter emptiness, like analyzing a real person into their six parts — head and the rest — and finding no "person" to be had, and hence it is named emptiness. The shared teaching's own four gates all take form itself as already empty, like observing a reflection in a mirror — the six parts are themselves already empty, without needing to be analyzed away to exhaustion before becoming empty. The Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: "The Buddha told the monks: contemplate emptiness as itself the folded cloth; contemplate the folded cloth as itself emptiness." This is the embodying gate, distinct from the analytical gate.

The Tripiṭaka teaching contemplates the emptiness of the person and attains the path; having attained the path through the emptiness of the person, it then further contemplates the emptiness of dharmas — the two objects, person and dharma, remaining unmerged. Here, within the shared teaching's own gate, the emptiness of the person is itself the emptiness of dharmas, and the emptiness of dharmas is itself the emptiness of the person, with no duality and no distinction between them. The Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says: "The nature of form is like the nature of self, and the nature of self is like the nature of form — both alike are like a magical illusion."

Some say: the Tripiṭaka teaching breaks through the conceived real nature, seeking the self in terms of real dharmas and finding it nowhere, this being simply the contemplation of the emptiness of nature; the Mahāyāna clarifies that the mark's own inherent nature is itself empty, requiring no further inspection before being recognized as empty. This, however, is only a partial statement. The Larger Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra says: "Emptiness of nature is constant — there is no time at which it is not empty of nature — clearly understanding every dharma to be like a magical illusion, like the moon in water, like a reflection in a mirror" — how could this possibly stop at the mere emptiness of the mark alone!

秖約此幻化即判四門。論云：「一切實一切不實，一切亦實亦不實，一切非實非不實。」佛於此四句，廣說第一義悉檀。一切實為有門者，若業若果善惡等法，乃至涅槃皆幻化。譬鏡中像，雖無實性，而有幻化頭等六分，為有門也。

諸法既如幻化，幻化本自無實，無實故空，乃至涅槃亦如幻化，如鏡中像，假有形色，求不可得，是為空門。

諸法既如幻，故名為有。幻不可得，故名為空。如鏡中像，見而不可見，不可見而見，是亦空亦有門。

幻有尚不可得，況復幻空而當可得？即是兩捨為門。是通教即空之四門也。若三乘共稟而根性不同，各於四句入第一義，故此四句皆名為門。故青目注論云：「諸法實相有三種。」今是三乘人同入此門，見第一義者，是即空之一種也。

Judging the four gates simply in terms of this same magical illusion: the Treatise says, "All is real; all is not real; all is both real and not real; all is neither real nor not real." The Buddha, within these same four propositions, broadly teaches the siddhānta of ultimate meaning. As for "all is real" being the gate of existence: whether karma or fruit, good or evil dharmas, even nirvāṇa itself — all are a magical illusion. It is like a reflection within a mirror: though it has no real nature, it nonetheless possesses the illusory six parts, head and the rest — this is the gate of existence.

Since every dharma is thus like a magical illusion, and a magical illusion is, in itself, fundamentally without reality, being without reality it is empty — even nirvāṇa itself is likewise like a magical illusion, like a reflection within a mirror, provisionally possessing form and appearance, yet, when sought, nowhere to be found — this is the gate of emptiness.

Since every dharma is thus like an illusion, it is named "existent"; since the illusion cannot be found, it is named "empty." Like a reflection within a mirror — seen, and yet not to be seen; not to be seen, and yet seen — this is the gate of both emptiness and existence.

If even illusory existence cannot be found, how much less could illusory emptiness be found either? This, then, is the gate of setting both aside together. These are the shared teaching's own four gates of "at once empty." Though the three vehicles alike receive this same teaching, their faculties differ, and each enters the truth of ultimate meaning through one of these same four propositions — hence these same four propositions are, every one, named a gate. Hence Piṅgala's own commentary on the Treatise says: "The real mark of all dharmas is of three kinds." Here, it is the three vehicles' persons alike entering this same gate and seeing the truth of ultimate meaning that constitutes one single kind of this "at-once-empty."

The Distinct and Perfect Teachings' Four Gates 別教・圓教四門

Chinese Source

三、明別教四門者，若用《中論》偈：「亦名為假名」也而辨四門者，即如《大論》四句，亦是此四句意。

所言別者，下異藏通，有七義故別；上異圓教，又歷別入中，故言別。此意正出《大經》，但多散說，今約乳等喻，即顯別四門也。

文云：佛性如乳有酪，石中有金，力士額珠，即是有門。若明石無金性，乳無酪性，眾生佛性，猶如虛空。大般涅槃空、迦毘羅城空，即是空門。又云：佛性亦有亦無。云何為有？一切眾生悉皆有故。云何為無？從善方便而得見故。又譬乳中亦有酪性，亦無酪性，即是亦有亦無門也。若明佛性即是中道，雙非兩遣。又譬乳中非有酪性，非無酪性，即是非空非有門。

別教菩薩，稟此四門之教，因見佛性住大涅槃，故此四句即是別教之四門。一往用擬別門經文，或時為圓門，此義在下料簡(云云)。

圓教四門相者，此門明入佛性第一義，一往與別門名義是同，細尋意趣，別有多途。分別同異，在下委論(云云)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, clarifying the distinct teaching's own four gates: if one uses the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā's own verse, "also named the provisional designation," to distinguish the four gates, this is exactly like the Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa's own four propositions, carrying this same fourfold sense.

As for what is meant by "distinct": below, it differs from the Tripiṭaka and shared teachings, possessing seven distinguishing senses of its own, and hence is called "distinct"; above, it differs from the perfect teaching, in that it proceeds sequentially, stage by stage, into the middle, and hence, too, is called "distinct." This sense properly derives from the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, though there scattered across many separate passages; here, taking up the milk-and-the-rest illustration, the distinct teaching's own four gates are thereby revealed.

The text says: buddha-nature is like milk containing cream within it, like gold within a stone, like the jewel embedded in the strongman's own forehead — this is the gate of existence. Clarifying that the stone has no inherent gold-nature, and milk has no inherent cream-nature, sentient beings' own buddha-nature being likewise just like empty space; great final nirvāṇa itself empty, the city of Kapilavastu itself empty — this is the gate of emptiness. Again, it says: buddha-nature is both existent and non-existent. How is it existent? Because every sentient being, without exception, possesses it. How is it non-existent? Because it is only seen by means of skillful, wholesome means. Again, illustrated as milk both containing cream-nature and not containing cream-nature — this is the gate of both existence and non-existence. Clarifying that buddha-nature is precisely the middle way, doubly negating and setting aside both extremes at once; again, illustrated as milk neither containing cream-nature nor not containing cream-nature — this is the gate of neither emptiness nor existence.

The distinct teaching's bodhisattva, receiving the teaching of these same four gates, and, by means of them, seeing buddha-nature and abiding in great nirvāṇa — hence these same four propositions are precisely the distinct teaching's own four gates. Taken at face value, they are used to model the distinct teaching's own scriptural text; yet, at times, they may also serve as the perfect teaching's own gate — this sense is resolved further below, and so on.

As for the perfect teaching's own four gates: these gates clarify entering buddha-nature, the truth of ultimate meaning; taken at face value, their names and senses are the same as the distinct teaching's own gates, yet, examined closely, their own aim differs in many ways. Distinguishing their sameness and difference is treated in full detail further below, and so on.

Reading note

The city-with-four-gates image, run twice — once for the Tripiṭaka versus the shared teaching, once for the distinct versus the perfect — makes the sixteen-gate scheme's own logic visually explicit: "clumsy" and "skillful" gates differ only in how directly they lead through, never in what they lead to, "the governor is one and the same." The milk-and-cream illustration borrowed from the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra* gives the distinct teaching's own four gates a concreteness the earlier three-vehicles-and-magical-illusion account lacked: existence (cream already present in the milk), emptiness (no such nature actually findable in stone or milk alike), both (present and absent depending on causal ripening), and neither (the middle way's own double negation) are four ways of describing the identical relationship between buddha-nature and the ordinary being who has not yet manifested it. That the perfect teaching's own four gates share identical vocabulary with the distinct teaching's, differing "in many ways" only on close inspection, previews the much sharper distinction the treatise draws out further below — a warning against assuming identical terminology guarantees identical doctrine, the same warning already sounded in item 3's account of sectarian dispute.

Showing the Contemplation That Enters the Gates 示入門觀

Chinese Source

二、示入門觀，即為二：先、略示入門處。二、略示入門觀。

略示入門處者，能通教門，大為十六，所通之理，但是偏圓兩真，前八門同入偏真，後八門同入圓真。何故偏真理一門八耶？三藏四門，紆迴隘陋，名為拙度。通教四門是摩訶衍，寬直巧度。門有巧拙之殊，能通為八，真理無二，所通唯一。譬如州城，開四面門。四面偏門，以譬三藏；四面直門，以譬通教；偏直既殊，能通為八，使君是一，所通不二也。

別教四門，偏而未融；圓四門，圓而且融。偏圓既殊，能通為八，圓真不二，所通唯一。譬如帝城，開四面門，四面偏門，以譬別教；四面直門，以譬圓教，偏直既殊，能通為八，帝尊不二所通唯一(云云)。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, showing the contemplation that enters the gates: this is, in turn, treated in two parts: first, briefly showing where the gates lead; second, briefly showing the contemplation that enters the gates.

As for briefly showing where the gates lead: the teaching-gates able to lead through number, in total, sixteen, yet the principle they lead to is only the one-sided real or the perfect real — the first eight gates alike lead into the one-sided real, and the latter eight gates alike lead into the perfect real. Why is it that a single principle, the one-sided real, requires eight gates? The Tripiṭaka's own four gates are circuitous and cramped, named the clumsy method; the shared teaching's own four gates are the Mahāyāna's own, broad, direct, and skillful method. The gates differ in being skillful or clumsy, and hence the ones able to lead through number eight, though the real principle admits of no duality, and what is led to is only the one. It is like a walled city with gates opened on all four sides: the four indirect gates illustrate the Tripiṭaka teaching; the four direct gates illustrate the shared teaching. Indirect and direct being distinct, the ones able to lead through number eight, and yet the governor is one and the same, and what is led to is not two.

The distinct teaching's own four gates are one-sided and not yet fully merged; the perfect teaching's own four gates are perfect and, moreover, fully merged. One-sided and perfect being distinct, the ones able to lead through number eight, and yet the perfect real admits of no duality, and what is led to is only the one. It is like an imperial city with gates opened on all four sides: the four indirect gates illustrate the distinct teaching; the four direct gates illustrate the perfect teaching. Indirect and direct being distinct, the ones able to lead through number eight, and yet the emperor is unique, and what is led to is only the one, and so on.

問：
小乘一種四門，摩訶衍何故三種四門？

答：
小乘淺近，一生斷結，喻如小家。大乘深遠，通處則長，譬如大家，須千門萬戶也，三四何足為多耶？

問：
摩訶衍門那得三人見真？

答：
此門正意通大，傍通於小，譬如王國有通門別門，別門通朝士，通門通朝市，不可以民庶登踐，謂為民門。摩訶衍通門亦如是，正通實相，傍通真諦，故三乘灰斷，兼由此門，不可以兼通偏真，而名小乘門也。

Question: The Hīnayāna has only a single kind of fourfold gate — why does the Mahāyāna have three kinds of fourfold gates?

Answer: The Hīnayāna is shallow and near at hand, severing the fetters within a single lifetime — comparable to a small household. The Mahāyāna is deep and far-reaching, and, where it leads through, extends over a long course — comparable to a great household, which requires a thousand gates and ten thousand doors. How, then, could three sets of four gates possibly be considered too many?

Question: How is it that the Mahāyāna's own gate is where all three kinds of persons come to see the truth?

Answer: This gate's own correct intent leads through to the great, while, incidentally, it also leads through to the lesser — comparable to a royal state having both a common gate and a particular gate: the particular gate leads only the court officials through, while the common gate leads through to the court and the marketplace alike, and cannot be climbed by the common people alone and thereby called "the common people's gate." The Mahāyāna's own common gate is exactly the same: it correctly leads through to the real mark, while, incidentally, it also leads through to the real truth — hence the three vehicles' own reduction to ashes and severing of the fetters, too, proceeds by means of this very gate, yet it cannot, on that account, be said to lead through only to the one-sided real, and thereby be named "the Hīnayāna's own gate."

Reading note

The small-household/great-household image answers a question the sixteen-gate scheme raises just by existing: if the destination is one, why sixteen doors rather than one? The answer is scale rather than redundancy — a small building needs one door, a vast one needs many, and needing more gates is itself evidence of how much further the Mahāyāna's own house extends. The common-gate/particular-gate image resolves the more delicate puzzle of why śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas can pass through a Mahāyāna gate at all without that gate becoming, thereby, merely "the Hīnayāna's own gate": a gate built to reach the capital does not stop being the capital's gate just because a passing farmer also finds it useful, and using it for a lesser errand does not entitle the farmer to rename it after himself.

The Tripiṭaka Teaching's Gate of Existence, in Ten Senses 三藏有門觀十意

Chinese Source

略示入門觀者，先、明三藏有門觀，彼有門中，具於信法、信行，聞說即悟，此心疾利，得道方法難可示人。且約法行觀門，即為十意：一、識所觀境。二、真正發心。三、遵修定慧。四、能破法遍。五、善知通知塞。六、善用道品。七、善用對治。八、善知次位。九、善能安忍。十者、法愛不生。阿毘曇中具此十意，其文間散，論師設欲行道，不知依何而修，如惑岐路，莫識所從。今撮其要意，通冠始終，則識有門入道之觀也。

一、明所觀境者，即是識正無明因緣，生一切法也。若謂世間苦樂之法，從毘紐天生，或言從世性生、微塵生，皆邪因緣生。若言自然法爾，無誰作者，此無因緣生。無因緣生是破因不破果，邪因緣亦是破正因果，是等悉非正因緣境，所不應觀。數存隣虛，論破隣虛，此與邪無相濫，殆非正因緣境。何者？隣虛有無，未免二見，猶是無明顛倒，倒故是集，集故感麁細等色。無明顛倒，既其不實，所感苦果報，那得定計有無？故《大論》云：「色若麁若細，總而觀之，無常無我。」無我故無主，若麁若細，若因若緣，若苦若集，若依若正，皆無常無主，悉是無明顛倒所作。如阿毘曇門廣說。是名識正因緣所觀之境，不同外道邪無因緣也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Briefly showing the contemplation that enters the gates: first, clarifying the Tripiṭaka teaching's own gate-of-existence contemplation. Within that gate of existence, both the one who follows through faith and the one who follows through the dharma are provided for — one who, upon hearing the teaching, is immediately awakened has a mind swift and sharp, and the method for attaining the path in such a case is difficult to demonstrate to others. Taking up, then, the gate of contemplation for the one who follows through the dharma, this is treated under ten senses: first, recognizing the object contemplated; second, the true and correct arousing of the aspiration; third, cultivating concentration and wisdom; fourth, thoroughly breaking through the dharma; fifth, skillfully knowing what is passable and what is blocked; sixth, skillfully applying the factors of the path; seventh, skillfully applying the antidote; eighth, skillfully knowing the sequence of stages; ninth, skillfully able to remain patient; tenth, not giving rise to attachment to the Dharma. The Abhidharma contains all ten of these senses, though its text is scattered here and there — a commentator wishing to practice the path, not knowing which passage to rely upon for cultivation, is left as confused as one at a fork in the road, unable to recognize which way to follow. Here, their essential sense is gathered together, spanning from beginning to end throughout, so that the contemplation for entering the path through the gate of existence may be recognized.

First, clarifying the object contemplated: this is precisely recognizing the correct causal dharma of ignorance, giving rise to every dharma. If one holds that the world's own dharmas of suffering and joy arise from the god Viṣṇu, or says they arise from some primal worldly nature, or from atomic particles — these are all births from a deviant causal dharma. If one says they are simply spontaneous, self-so, with no maker at all, this is birth with no causal dharma whatsoever. Birth with no causal dharma refutes the cause but not the effect; a deviant causal dharma likewise refutes the correct cause and effect — objects of this kind are, every one, not the correct causal object, and ought not to be contemplated. The Abhidharma's own enumeration posits the neighboring-void, the atomic particle; the Treatise refutes the neighboring-void — this risks being confused with the deviant "no-cause" position, and is likewise almost not the correct causal object. Why is this? Whether the neighboring-void exists or does not exist, neither escapes the two views, and remains simply an inverted view born of ignorance; being inverted, it is arising; being arising, it draws forth coarse and fine form alike. Ignorance's own inverted view, being itself unreal, how could the resultant fruit of suffering it draws forth possibly be fixed as either existing or not existing? Hence the Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: "Form, whether coarse or fine, contemplated altogether, is impermanent and without self." Being without self, it is without any master; whether coarse or fine, whether cause or condition, whether suffering or arising, whether the dependent or the direct — all alike are impermanent and without any master, all of it made by ignorance's own inverted view, exactly as broadly explained through the Abhidharma's own gate. This is named recognizing the object contemplated through the correct causal dharma, unlike the non-Buddhist outsiders' own deviant "no causal dharma."

二、發心真正者，既識無明顛倒，流轉行識，乃至老死，如旋火輪，欲休息結業，正求涅槃，發二乘心，出離見愛，不要名利，但破諸有，不增長苦集，唯志無餘，其心清淨，不雜不偽，此心真正，名正發心，不同外道天魔也。

三、遵修定慧者，行人既誓求出，有依波羅提木叉住修道，但罪障紛馳，心不得安，道何由剋？為修四念處，學五停心，破五種障。

五停事觀即是定，定生念處即慧，慧定均停，故名安心。又定慧調適，故名停心。若無定慧，若單定慧，若不均調定慧，皆不名賢人。如世間賢人，智德具足，智則靡所不閑，德則美行無缺，許由、巢父，乃可稱賢。若多智寡德名狂人，多德寡智名癡人，狂癡皆非賢也。賢名賢能，亦名賢善，善故有德，能故有智，智德具足，故稱賢人。行者亦爾，修四念處慧，學五停心定，定慧具足。

Second, the true and correct arousing of the aspiration: having already recognized ignorance's own inverted view, flowing on through formations and consciousness, even to old age and death, like a spinning wheel of fire, one wishes to bring the accumulated karma to rest, correctly seeking nirvāṇa, arousing the mind of the two lesser vehicles, departing from views and craving, seeking neither fame nor profit, only breaking through every state of existence, not further increasing suffering and its arising, aspiring only toward the state with no residue remaining — this mind, pure, unmixed, and unfeigned, this mind true and correct, is named the correct arousing of the aspiration, unlike the non-Buddhist outsiders' or the heavenly demon's own aspiration.

Third, cultivating concentration and wisdom: the practitioner, having already vowed to seek release, relies upon the prātimokṣa and abides in cultivating the path — yet, offenses and obstructions scattering him in every direction, the mind cannot find peace, so how could the path possibly be attained? For this reason, one cultivates the four applications of mindfulness, studies the five methods of stilling the mind, and breaks through the five kinds of obstruction.

The five stilling-methods' own contemplation of phenomena is itself concentration; concentration giving rise to the applications of mindfulness is itself wisdom; wisdom and concentration held in equal balance is accordingly named "settling the mind." Again, concentration and wisdom brought into harmonious adjustment is accordingly named "stilling the mind." Without concentration and wisdom, or with only one of the two alone, or with concentration and wisdom unbalanced, none of these can be named a worthy person. Just as the worthy persons of the world are complete with both wisdom and virtue — wisdom leaving nothing unmastered, virtue leaving no fine conduct wanting — as with Xu You or Chao Fu, only then may they properly be called "worthy." One of much wisdom but little virtue is named a madman; one of much virtue but little wisdom is named a fool — madness and folly are, neither one, worthiness. "Worthy" means capable and worthy, and also means good and worthy: goodness gives rise to virtue, capability gives rise to wisdom, and, wisdom and virtue both complete, one is accordingly called a worthy person. The practitioner is exactly the same: cultivating the wisdom of the four applications of mindfulness, studying the concentration of the five stilling-methods, concentration and wisdom both complete.

云何數息具足定慧？制諸覺散，從一至十，知息及數，無常生滅，念念不停。又若觀不淨，當深厭穢惡，能觀所觀，無常生滅，速朽虛誑，誑諸眾生，厭觀起恚，須慈定相應，見他得樂，亦知此定，及彼樂相，無常生滅。因緣觀時，橫觀四生，悉是因緣生法；竪觀三界，亦是因緣生法。從緣生者，悉是無常無我。諸障起者，應須念佛，亦如是。是名五停具修定慧。

有定故不狂，有慧故不愚，依此安心，為眾行基址，發生煗頂，入苦忍真明，隣聖為賢，義在於此。不同外道，不知鑽搖，漿猶難得，況復酪蘇等也！

How is it that mindfulness of the breath is complete with both concentration and wisdom? Restraining every scattering thought, counting from one to ten, knowing the breath and its count as impermanent, arising and ceasing, thought after thought never still — this is concentration and wisdom both present at once. Again, if one contemplates impurity, one ought to feel deep revulsion toward the foul and unclean; yet the very contemplation, and what it contemplates, are themselves impermanent, arising and ceasing, swiftly decaying and hollow, deceiving every sentient being — and, revulsion arising into aversion, one requires the concentration of loving-kindness to correspond with it, seeing another attain happiness, and likewise knowing that this very concentration and that very mark of happiness are themselves impermanent, arising and ceasing. In the contemplation of causes and conditions, contemplated horizontally across the four modes of birth, all alike are dharmas born of causes and conditions; contemplated vertically through the three realms, likewise all are dharmas born of causes and conditions. Whatever is born from conditions is, without exception, impermanent and without self. When any obstruction arises, one ought likewise to be mindful of the Buddha in this same way. This is named the five stilling-methods, each complete with the cultivation of both concentration and wisdom.

Having concentration, one is not driven mad; having wisdom, one is not foolish — settling the mind by means of these, as the very foundation for every practice, one brings forth warmth and summit, entering the true clarity of the forbearance of suffering, drawing near to the sages as a worthy one — the sense lies precisely here. Unlike the non-Buddhist outsiders, who, not even knowing how to drill and shake to draw forth the sap, can scarcely obtain even the raw juice, let alone cream or butter!

四、破法遍成，見有得道，其安心定慧。若五停心後，修共念處時，帶不淨等，遍破諸法，事理悉成。若五停心後，單修性念處時，一向理觀，以無常之慧，遍破諸見。破見之觀，如《中論》下兩品所明也。

佛初轉法輪，不說餘法，但明無常，遍破一切外道，若有若無，乃至非有非無，神及世間，常無常等，六十二見，使得清淨。今阿毘曇師受他破云：無常是小乘，常是大乘，常得破無常，無常不得破常。若得前意，此不應然。未得道前，執心所計，常、無常、亦常亦無常、非常非無常等。法塵對意根而生諸見，見從緣生，從緣生者，悉是無常。云何外道有常、樂、我、淨？如是四倒，悉用無常破之。故五百比丘語達兜言：「但修無常，可以得道，可以得通。」如六群比丘為他說法，純說無常。當知見無深淺，悉為無常所破，不同舊醫，純用乳藥也。

Fourth, thoroughly breaking through the dharma is accomplished: seeing existence, one attains the path, this being the settling of the mind through concentration and wisdom. If, after the five stilling-methods, one cultivates the shared applications of mindfulness, accompanied by the contemplation of impurity and the rest, thoroughly breaking through every dharma, both phenomena and principle are accomplished together. If, after the five stilling-methods, one cultivates solely the applications of mindfulness upon nature, wholly a contemplation of principle, one uses the wisdom of impermanence to thoroughly break through every view. The contemplation that breaks through views is exactly as clarified in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā's own final two chapters.

When the Buddha first turned the wheel of the Dharma, he taught no other dharma but clarified impermanence alone, thereby thoroughly breaking through every non-Buddhist outsider's own view — whether of existence or non-existence, or even neither existence nor non-existence, whether of the soul or of the world, whether permanent or impermanent, and the rest — the sixty-two views, causing them to be purified. Now, Abhidharma masters, refuted by others, say: impermanence is the Hīnayāna's own teaching, permanence is the Mahāyāna's own teaching — permanence is able to refute impermanence, but impermanence cannot refute permanence. If one grasps the sense already given above, this ought not to be so. Before attaining the path, the clinging mind conceives of permanence, impermanence, both permanence and impermanence, and neither permanence nor impermanence, and the rest — mental objects meeting the mind-faculty give rise to every such view, and views, being born from conditions, are, without exception, impermanent. How, then, could the non-Buddhist outsiders possibly possess permanence, bliss, self, and purity? These same four inverted views are, every one, broken through by means of impermanence. Hence the five hundred monks said to Dhṛtarāṣṭra: "Simply cultivate impermanence, and one may thereby attain the path, and thereby attain penetration." As when the group of six monks taught others the Dharma, teaching solely impermanence. One should know that views, whether shallow or deep, are, every one, broken through by impermanence — unlike the old physician who relies solely on the remedy of milk.

五、知通塞者，前雖遍破諸見之過，未見其德。過即是塞，德即是通。若有見中八十八使，乃至非有非無不可說見中八十八使，悉從緣生，名之為塞，塞故須破。復識其通者，所謂有見中道滅，乃至非有非無不可說見中道滅，如是道滅，從因緣生，名之為通，通何須破？若不識諸見，謂是事實、餘妄語，執見成業，愛潤感果，豈非塞耶？能於諸見，一一皆知無常顛倒，不生計著，不執則無業，無業則無果。如是達者，則有道滅，豈不名通？不同外道，如蟲食木，是蟲不知是字非字也。

六、善修道品者，豈唯識此通塞而已？當修道品，進諸法門。謂觀此有見，乃至不可說見，皆依於色，污穢不淨，即身念處。若受有受，乃至受不可說受，皆依三受，受即是苦，名受念處。觀於諸見所起想行，悉是無我，名法念處。觀諸見之心，念念無常，名心念處。觀此四觀，名有為法中得正憶念，得是念故，四倒則伏，是名念處。勤修四觀，名四正勤。定心中修，名四如意。五善根生，故名五根。五根增長，遮諸惡法，故名五力。定慧調停，名七覺分。

安隱道中行，名八正道。今非約位道品，但就通脩論三十七耳。若一停心門作三十七品，餘停心亦如是。阿毘曇道諦中，應廣分別(云云)。

此三十七品是行道法，將至涅槃城有三門，所謂苦下二行，為空解脫門。集道各四，苦下有二，是無作解脫門。滅下有四，是無相解脫門。若涅槃門開，即得入也。故佛於《須跋陀羅經》中，決定師子吼，唯我法中有八正道，外道法中，尚無一道，何況八道耶。

Fifth, knowing what is passable and what is blocked: though what came before thoroughly broke through the faults of every view, its own virtue has not yet been seen. Fault is precisely obstruction; virtue is precisely passage. The eighty-eight fetters within the view of existence, even down to the eighty-eight fetters within the inexpressible view of neither existence nor non-existence — all, without exception, are born from conditions, and are named "obstruction," and, being obstruction, must be broken through. One must, further, recognize their own passage: namely, the path and cessation present within the view of existence, even down to the path and cessation present within the inexpressible view of neither existence nor non-existence — this same path and cessation, too, being born from causes and conditions, is named "passage" — and what need is there to break through passage? If one fails to recognize the various views, taking them to be the real matter and everything else as false speech, one's clinging view becomes karma, moistened by craving and drawing forth its own fruit — how could this fail to be obstruction? Being able, with respect to every view, to know each and every one as an impermanent, inverted view, one gives rise to no fixed attachment; without attachment there is no karma; without karma there is no fruit. One who has thus penetrated it possesses, thereby, the path and cessation — how could this fail to be named passage? Unlike the non-Buddhist outsiders, like an insect gnawing through wood, this same insect never knowing whether the shape it traces is a character or not.

Sixth, skillfully cultivating the factors of the path: how could this consist merely of recognizing what is passable and blocked, and nothing more? One ought to cultivate the factors of the path, advancing through every Dharma-gate. That is: contemplating this view of existence, even down to the inexpressible view, as, every one, depending upon form, defiled and impure — this is the application of mindfulness upon the body. If sensation possesses existence, even down to the inexpressible sensation, all depending upon the three kinds of sensation, and sensation itself being suffering — this is named the application of mindfulness upon sensation. Contemplating the perception and formation arisen from every view as, without exception, without self — this is named the application of mindfulness upon dharmas. Contemplating the mind of every view, thought after thought impermanent — this is named the application of mindfulness upon mind. Contemplating these same four contemplations is named attaining correct mindfulness within conditioned dharmas; having attained this mindfulness, the four inverted views are thereby subdued — this is named the applications of mindfulness. Diligently cultivating these four contemplations is named the four correct exertions. Cultivated within a settled mind, this is named the four bases of miraculous power. The five wholesome roots arising, hence named the five faculties. The five faculties increasing, warding off every evil dharma, hence named the five powers. Concentration and wisdom brought into balance, named the seven factors of awakening.

Walking securely along the path, this is named the eightfold correct path. Here, this is not discussed in terms of stage-specific factors of the path, but only in terms of the thirty-seven factors as generally cultivated. If a single stilling-method already yields all thirty-seven factors, every other stilling-method is exactly the same. Within the Abhidharma's own treatment of the truth of the path, this ought to be broadly distinguished, and so on.

These same thirty-seven factors are the dharma of practicing the path, and, on the way to the city of nirvāṇa, there are three gates: namely, the two practices under suffering constitute the liberation-gate of emptiness; the four each under arising and the path, together with the two under suffering, constitute the liberation-gate of the unconditioned; the four under cessation constitute the liberation-gate of the markless. Once the gate of nirvāṇa is opened, one thereby gains entry. Hence the Buddha, within the Subhadra Sūtra, decisively roars the lion's roar: "Only within my own Dharma is there the eightfold correct path — within the non-Buddhist outsiders' own dharma, there is not even a single path, how much less eight paths!"

七、善脩對治者，若利人即入，若不入者當修助道。故論云：十二禪等悉是助開門法，正慧既弱，遮障得起，修助道為援。論云：「貪欲起，教修不淨，背捨等；緣中不自在，當教勝處；緣中不廣普，當教一切處。若少福德，當教無量心；若欲出色，當教四空。」如是等悉是助道，助開門法，不同外道，於根本禪，起愛見慢也。

八、善識次位者，雖修如此正助等法，不得即言我是聖人，叨濫真似，不識賢聖。今明識真似階差，自知非聖，增上之慢則不得生。不同外道，戒取見取，計生死法，以為涅槃也。

Seventh, skillfully cultivating the antidote: if one is of sharp faculties, one enters directly; if one does not enter, one ought to cultivate the auxiliary path. Hence the Treatise says: the twelve dhyānas and the rest are, every one, dharmas that assist in opening the gate — when correct wisdom is weak, obstructions are able to arise, and cultivating the auxiliary path serves as their support. The Treatise says: "If craving and desire arise, one is taught to cultivate impurity, the liberations, and the rest; if one lacks mastery over the object, one is taught the bases of mastery; if one's object is not broad and pervasive, one is taught the totalities. If one has little merit, one is taught the immeasurable minds; if one wishes to rise beyond form, one is taught the four formless attainments." Dharmas of this kind are, every one, the auxiliary path, dharmas that assist in opening the gate — unlike the non-Buddhist outsiders, who, within the root dhyānas themselves, give rise to craving, views, and pride.

Eighth, skillfully recognizing the sequence of stages: though one cultivates correct and auxiliary dharmas of this kind, one must not, on this account, say "I am a sage" — presumptuously confusing the true with the merely resembling, failing to recognize the worthy from the sage. Here, clarifying the recognition of the distinction between the true and the resembling stages, one knows for oneself that one is not yet a sage, and overweening pride is thereby unable to arise. Unlike the non-Buddhist outsiders, who, clinging to precepts and clinging to views, conceive of the dharma of birth-and-death itself as nirvāṇa.

九、善修安忍。別相念處力弱，未甚通泰。轉修總相念處，或總一、總二乃至總四，是時應須安忍，使諦觀成就，轉入煗法，似道煙生。《大經》云：「煗雖有漏有為，還能破壞有漏有為。」「我弟子有，外道則無。」又，若安忍即成頂法，頂法成名忍，到傍邊，如其不忍，則退還此邊。故云：頂法退為五逆，煗法退為闡提。是故此中善須安忍內外諸障，不同外道不能安忍細微遮法也。

十、法愛不生者，上來既得四善根生，若起法愛，雖不退為五逆、闡提，而不得入見諦。是則三番縮觀，進成上忍，世第一法發苦忍真明，十六剎那得成初果，或成超果；或重用觀，斷五下五上，得成無學。若利人用觀，節節得入；若鈍用觀，具來至十。阿毘曇中雖復廣解，不出十意。

五百阿羅漢作《毘婆沙》，正申有門得道，云何而言是調心方便？四門調適，俱能得道。若生取著，俱不得道。若但云見有得道，見空不得道，云何異於外人？故《大論》云：「若不得般若方便，則墮有無。」今以十法為方便，直入真門，永異外道也。是為有門入真之觀也。餘空門、亦空亦有門、非空非有門，入真之觀始終方便，比於有門，各各不同。然俱會偏真，斷三界惑，更無異也。其三門準有，例應十觀，大同小異，可以意得，今不能煩記(云云)。

Ninth, skillfully cultivating patience: the particular applications of mindfulness, weak in their own power, are not yet fully penetrating. One then turns to cultivate the general applications of mindfulness, whether taken generally as one, as two, or as all four together — at this time, one ought to remain patient, so that the contemplation of the truths is accomplished, turning to enter the dharma of summit, the smoke of the path-to-come already rising. The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra says: "Warmth, though tainted and conditioned, is nonetheless able to break through what is tainted and conditioned." "My own disciples possess it; the non-Buddhist outsiders do not." Again, if one remains patient, one thereby accomplishes the dharma of summit; the dharma of summit accomplished is named forbearance, arriving at the very brink — should one fail to remain patient, one falls back from this same brink. Hence it is said: falling back from the dharma of summit becomes the five heinous deeds; falling back from the dharma of warmth becomes the icchantika. For this reason, one must here skillfully remain patient amid every inward and outward obstruction, unlike the non-Buddhist outsiders, who are unable to remain patient before even the subtlest obstructing dharma.

Tenth, no attachment to the Dharma arising: having already, through what came before, brought forth the four wholesome roots, should attachment to the Dharma arise, though one does not fall back into the five heinous deeds or the icchantika, one still fails to enter the path of seeing the truths. This, then, is the threefold contracting of the contemplation, advancing to accomplish the higher forbearance; the supreme worldly dharma brings forth the true clarity of the forbearance of suffering, and, within sixteen mental instants, the first fruit is accomplished, or the leaping-over fruit is accomplished; or, further applying the contemplation, one severs the five lower and five higher fetters, accomplishing the state beyond further training. If one of sharp faculties applies the contemplation, one enters node by node; if one of dull faculties applies it, all ten senses are required in full. Though the Abhidharma explains this at length, it does not go beyond these same ten senses.

The five hundred arhats composed the Vibhāṣā, correctly setting forth attaining the path through the gate of existence — how, then, could this be called merely a skillful means for regulating the mind? All four gates, adjusted and applied, are equally able to attain the path; yet, should attachment arise, none of them attain the path at all. If one simply says that seeing existence attains the path while seeing emptiness does not, how would this differ from the non-Buddhist outsiders? Hence the Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa says: "Without attaining prajñā's own skillful means, one falls into either existence or non-existence." Here, these same ten dharmas are taken as the skillful means, entering directly through the gate of the real, forever distinct from the non-Buddhist outsiders. This, then, is the contemplation that enters the real through the gate of existence. As for the remaining gates — of emptiness, of both emptiness and existence, of neither emptiness nor existence — their own contemplation entering the real, from beginning to end, in its skillful means, differs, respectively, from the gate of existence in each case. Yet all alike converge upon the one-sided real, severing the afflictions of the three realms, with no further difference at all. These same three gates, measured against the gate of existence, likewise each call for their own ten contemplations, largely the same with only minor differences, which may be grasped by the same sense — this shall not be recorded here in full detail, and so on.

Reading note

This ten-item scheme is Zhiyi's own attempt to rescue the Abhidharma from itself: its own ten-part method for actually walking through the gate of existence to realization is, in his account, "scattered here and there" across the canon, leaving a sincere practitioner "as confused as one at a fork in the road" despite the material all being present. Gathering it into a single sequence — from correctly identifying the causal object, through settling the mind, breaking through views, distinguishing obstruction from passage, cultivating the thirty-seven factors, applying antidotes, tracking one's own stage honestly, remaining patient at the threshold, and finally releasing even attachment to the Dharma itself — turns a badly organized library into a usable path. The closing point is worth underlining: any of the four gates can fail if clung to, and none of them are inherently superior to the others — "if one simply says that seeing existence attains the path while seeing emptiness does not, how would this differ from the non-Buddhist outsiders?" The gates are genuinely equal doors; what differs is only which door a given practitioner's own faculties are already facing.

It embodies and understands every dharma as, alike, a magical illusion. Though the three kinds of persons alike arouse the aspiration, there are, too, minor differences between them, and so on. A certain Mādhyamika commentator says: this refers to the Mahāyāna's own śrāvaka. Here, I say this is not so. The sūtra says, "Wishing to attain śrāvaka-hood or pratyekabuddha-hood, one ought to study prajñā." The Treatise says, "The śrāvaka's and the pratyekabuddha's own path of liberation, of nirvāṇa, are both alike obtained from prajñā." Neither the sūtra nor the treatise says these are identical — this is simply the commentator's own error. Though one knows that concentration and wisdom cannot themselves be found, the two dharmas that settle the mind, by means of the wisdom of magical illusion, thoroughly break through the four views, the sixty-two views, and every dharma whatsoever, knowing suffering and its arising, within this same magical illusion, to be named "obstruction," and knowing the path and cessation, within this same magical illusion, to be named "passage." By means of an ungraspable mind, one cultivates the thirty-seven factors; by means of what has nothing to be remedied, one studies every antidote, recognizing the ground of dry wisdom even up to the ground of buddhahood; the wisdom of magical illusion is neither moved by outer demons nor driven back by inner obstructions. Every dharma does not arise, and yet prajñā itself arises, without giving rise to any craving or attachment either, and one thereby enters the real; whether by wisdom or by severance, the forbearance of the dharma of no-generation, compared with what came before, is more skillful — this may be understood by the same pattern, and shall not be recorded here in full detail. The remaining three gates' own ten senses are largely the same with only minor differences, which may likewise be grasped by the same sense, and shall not be recorded in the text here either.

次、明別教有門觀，即為十意(云云)。

一、觀境者，超出凡夫四見四門外，亦非二乘四門法，亦非通教四門法。諸四門法為境，不名實相，非生死涅槃；如來藏者，乃名為妙有，有真實法。如此妙有為一切法而作依持，從是妙有出生諸法，是為所觀之境也。

二、明發心者，菩薩深觀實相妙有，不為生死所遷，金藏草穢，額珠鬪沒，貧窮孤露，甚可愍傷。菩薩為此起大慈悲，四弘誓願。《思益》有三十二大悲。《華嚴》云：「不為一人、一國、一界微塵人，乃為法界眾生，發菩提心。」如是發心有大勢力，如師子吼。既發心已，安心進行，如前所說種種定慧，如是時中，宜應修如是定；如是時中，宜應修如是慧。定愛慧策，安心修道，依止二法，不餘依止，是為安心法也。還以妙有之慧，遍破生死一切諸見，六十二等，功德黑闇，皆悉不受。遍破涅槃，沈空取證，猶如大樹，不宿怨鳥。於一一法中，明識通塞，如雪山中，備有毒草，亦有藥王。

Next, clarifying the distinct teaching's own gate-of-existence contemplation: this, too, is treated under ten senses, and so on.

First, contemplating the object: this transcends the ordinary being's own four views and four gates, and is likewise not the two lesser vehicles' own four-gate dharma, nor the shared teaching's own four-gate dharma. The dharma of every one of these four gates, taken as an object, is not properly named "the real mark," being neither birth-and-death nor nirvāṇa; the tathāgatagarbha alone is properly named "wondrous being," possessing a truly real dharma. This same wondrous being serves as the very support upon which every dharma depends, every dharma issuing forth from this wondrous being — this is the object here contemplated.

Second, clarifying the arousing of the aspiration: the bodhisattva, deeply contemplating the real mark's own wondrous being, is not carried along by birth-and-death — the gold buried beneath grass and filth, the jewel embedded in the forehead lost in the struggle, poverty-stricken and forsaken — this is truly a matter for the deepest pity and sorrow. The bodhisattva, for this reason, arouses great compassion and the four universal vows. The Viśeṣacintibrahma-paripṛcchā Sūtra speaks of thirty-two kinds of great compassion. The Avataṃsaka Sūtra says: "Not for the sake of a single person, a single nation, or beings as numerous as motes of dust in a single realm, but for the sake of the sentient beings of the entire dharma-realm, is the aspiration for bodhi aroused." An aspiration aroused in this way possesses great power, like the lion's own roar. Having already aroused the aspiration, one settles the mind and advances in practice, cultivating whichever concentration and wisdom, as explained before, is appropriate to whatever time it may be — at such a time, one ought to cultivate such-and-such concentration; at such a time, one ought to cultivate such-and-such wisdom. Concentration cherished, wisdom spurred onward, settling the mind and cultivating the path, relying upon these same two dharmas and no other — this is the dharma of settling the mind. Turning, further, to the wisdom of wondrous being, one thoroughly breaks through every view of birth-and-death, the sixty-two and the rest, and their own dark obscuring of merit, accepting none of them at all. Thoroughly breaking through nirvāṇa as well, sinking into emptiness to seek realization is like a great tree that harbors no resentful bird. Within each and every dharma, one clearly recognizes passage and obstruction, as within the Himalayas, where both poisonous herbs and the king of medicinal herbs alike are found in abundance.

菩薩須知，如此心起，即是六道苦集，名為塞；如是心起，即是二乘道滅，名為通。又如是心起是二乘苦集，名為塞；如是心起是菩薩道滅，名為通。如是心起，名為菩薩苦集；如是心起，名佛道滅。於苦集中，能知非道，通達佛道；能知佛道，起於壅塞，了了無滯，是為識通塞。

善修道品者，夫三十七品是菩薩寶炬陀羅尼。破倒念處，勤行定心，五善根生，能排五惡，定慧調適，安隱道中行。離十相故名空三昧。亦不見空相，名無相三昧。不作願求，名無作三昧。是行道法，近涅槃門。若修諸法對治之門，所謂常無常，恒非恒，安非安，為無為，斷不斷，涅槃非涅槃，增上非增上，常樂觀察諸對治門，助開實相也。

從初十信、十住、十行、十迴向、十地、等覺、妙覺，聖位深淺悉知無謬，終不謂我叨極上位，內忍善惡兩覺，違從二賊；外忍八風，以忍力故，不為傾動。設證相似之法，法愛不起，不墮菩薩，頂生名法愛，無是愛故，即入菩薩位。破無明穢草，顯出妙有金藏，得見佛性，入於實相。是為有門修入實觀也。

餘空門，亦空亦有門，非空非有門，入實之觀，例亦為十。諸門方便，雖各不同，俱會圓真，理無差二，三門觀法，準有可知，不復委記(云云)。

The bodhisattva must know: when such-and-such a mind arises, this is precisely the suffering and arising of the six destinies, named "obstruction"; when such-and-such a mind arises, this is precisely the two lesser vehicles' own path and cessation, named "passage." Again, when such-and-such a mind arises, this is the two lesser vehicles' own suffering and arising, named "obstruction"; when such-and-such a mind arises, this is the bodhisattva's own path and cessation, named "passage." When such-and-such a mind arises, this is named the bodhisattva's own suffering and arising; when such-and-such a mind arises, this is named the buddha's own path and cessation. Within suffering and arising, being able to know what is not the path, one thoroughly penetrates the buddha-path; being able to know the buddha-path, one recognizes where obstruction still arises, clearly and without any lingering hindrance — this is recognizing passage and obstruction.

As for skillfully cultivating the factors of the path: the thirty-seven factors are the bodhisattva's own jeweled-torch dhāraṇī. Breaking through the inverted applications of mindfulness, diligently practicing the settled mind, the five wholesome roots arise, able to dispel the five evils; concentration and wisdom brought into balance, one walks securely along the path. Departing from the ten marks, this is named the samādhi of emptiness. Further, not even seeing any mark of emptiness itself, this is named the samādhi of the markless. Making no vow or seeking at all, this is named the samādhi of the unconditioned. This is the dharma of practicing the path, drawing near the gate of nirvāṇa. As for cultivating the various dharmas' own gate of antidote — namely, permanence versus impermanence, constancy versus inconstancy, ease versus unease, the conditioned versus the unconditioned, severance versus non-severance, nirvāṇa versus non-nirvāṇa, the superior versus the non-superior — constantly delighting in examining every one of these antidote-gates assists in opening the real mark.

From the very beginning — the ten faiths, the ten abidings, the ten practices, the ten dedications, the ten grounds, equal enlightenment, and wondrous enlightenment — one knows, without any error, the depth or shallowness of every one of these sagely stages, never once claiming, presumptuously, that one has already reached the very highest stage; inwardly, one remains patient before the two thieves of good and evil discursive thought contending against and complying with one; outwardly, one remains patient before the eight winds — and, by the very power of this patience, one is not swayed or overturned. Should one realize a dharma of mere resemblance, no attachment to the Dharma arises, and one does not fall — attachment to the Dharma arising at the very summit is named "attachment to the Dharma," and, being free of this attachment, one thereby enters the bodhisattva's own stage. Breaking through the weeds of ignorance's own defilement, the golden treasury of wondrous being is thereby manifested, buddha-nature is seen, and one enters the real mark. This is cultivating and entering the real contemplation through the gate of existence.

As for the remaining gates — of emptiness, of both emptiness and existence, of neither emptiness nor existence — their own contemplation entering the real likewise follows this same pattern of ten senses. Though each gate's own skillful means differs, all alike converge upon the perfect real, the principle admitting of no difference at all; these same three gates' own contemplation method, measured against the gate of existence, may thereby be understood, and shall not be recorded here in full detail, and so on.

Reading note

The distinct teaching's own version of the ten-item scheme keeps the Tripiṭaka's structure but changes what fills every slot: the object contemplated is no longer bare impermanence but the tathāgatagarbha itself, described through the gold-buried-in-filth and lost-forehead-jewel images already used earlier in Clarifying the Essence; the compassion aroused is explicitly universal rather than self-directed ("not for the sake of a single person... but for the sake of the sentient beings of the entire dharma-realm"); and the stages climbed are the full bodhisattva path through wondrous enlightenment rather than the four fruits. The corrected misreading — that the shared teaching's śrāvaka is somehow "the Mahāyāna's own śrāvaka" — matters because it protects the whole sixteen-gate architecture: if the shared teaching's gates were secretly just the Hīnayāna's own gates renamed, the careful distinction between "clumsy" and "skillful" methods leading to the identical one-sided real would collapse.
