Fascicle 37 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十七 · The Chapter on Power Opens

The Buddhas' and Bodhisattvas' Power, and Why Every Object Is Mind-Only

With the chapter on true meaning complete, the treatise opens 威力品, the chapter on power — the fourth of the seven fields of the factors of awakening — beginning with three kinds of power a bodhisattva possesses: the power of holiness resting on mastery of concentration, the power inherent in the Dharma's own teachings, and the power present from birth.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 37 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Chapter on Power Opens — Four Ways Buddhas Save Beings, and Elder Dàochuò's Seven Similes for a Single Name's Power

Fascicle 37 opens the chapter on power (威力品), the fourth of the seven fields structuring the factors of awakening, following directly after the chapter on true meaning just completed. Before defining a bodhisattva's own power directly, the text surveys four ways buddhas save beings — through teaching, through their radiant physical presence, through miraculous power, and through their very names — and lingers on the last of these with seven vivid similes explaining how reciting a single name can outweigh a lifetime of accumulated karma.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處威力品第五

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the Ground of the Bodhisattva, the first division on yogic practice: the fifth chapter, on power.

Chinese Source

云何諸佛菩薩威力？當知略有三種。一者、聖威力。謂佛菩薩得定自在，依定自在，隨其所欲一切事成。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the power of buddhas and bodhisattvas? It should be understood that, in brief, there are three kinds. The first is the power of holiness: buddhas and bodhisattvas attain mastery over concentration, and resting on this mastery, everything they wish for comes to pass.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by situating this new chapter within the seven fields already introduced back in fascicle 35 (self-benefit, benefiting others, true meaning, power, ripening beings, ripening one's own buddha-qualities, and unsurpassed awakening): having completed true meaning, the bodhisattva has fully transformed from ordinary being into noble one for their own benefit, and now turns outward, using post-realization wisdom to cultivate miraculous power in service of ripening other beings — which is precisely why power follows true meaning here. He frames this against the *Mahāsaṃnipāta-sūtra*'s own four-fold account of how buddhas rescue beings: through spoken teaching, dispelling ignorance and opening the eye of wisdom; through their physical radiance and marks, so that merely fixing one's attention on a buddha's form — even a mere painted image, as in the story of a wayward young Śākyamuni whose heart turned at the sight of one — plants the seed of eventual awakening; through miraculous power, this chapter's own topic; and through their names, where sincere recitation is said to dissolve karmic obstruction across countless past lives.

He recounts at length the seven similes the Tang-dynasty Pure Land teacher Dàochuò offered to answer a natural objection — how could reciting a single name possibly outweigh lifetimes of accumulated karmic wrongdoing? A mountain of firewood, stacked by a hundred people over a hundred years higher than the Himalayas, still burns to nothing from a single bean-sized flame, just as a name's merit consumes vast accumulated karma; a crippled man who can barely walk three steps unaided crosses a thousand miles in a day riding a favorable wind, just as a name carries a struggling practitioner swiftly toward the Pure Land; a poor man who unearths a thousand-year-old treasure and presents it to the king is instantly wealthy, just as an ordinary being's severe karmic burden is instantly outweighed once brought to a buddha's own field; a bedridden invalid unable even to mount a donkey rides effortlessly through the sky astride a wheel-turning king's flying wheel, just as a name carries the powerless along regardless of their own strength; a person bound many times over by rope is freed in one stroke by a child's sword, just as a name's power severs every karmic bond at once; a poisonous bird's touch kills fish in the water, yet a rhinoceros horn's touch revives the dead, illustrating that the world already contains inexplicable reversals of fortune, which a name's power resembles; and the legend of Yellow Crane Tower, where a crane calls a dead immortal's name for a thousand years until, astonishingly, he returns to life — evidence, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, that the world holds wonders beyond ordinary reckoning, and a buddha's power exceeds even these. He closes by noting the chapter's own dual purpose: to deepen confidence in the buddhas' virtues for those practicing through faith and recitation, and to establish the correct view — that every object is mind-only — for those walking the path of direct insight, the two approaches meant to reinforce rather than compete with one another.

2. Milestone: Four Kinds of Wisdom Establishing "Consciousness-Only, No Object" — Why a Bodhisattva's Mind Can Reshape What It Perceives

The power of holiness rests entirely on one insight: every object of experience is mind-only. The treatise draws on the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s own four kinds of wisdom to establish this — the same object appearing differently to different beings, cognition arising with no object actually present, sages perceiving without the distorted effort ordinary beings require, and, most strikingly, masters of mind and concentration reshaping objects at will, right up to a fully awakened one for whom no object appears at all.

Chinese Source

成就相違識相智。如餓鬼傍生及諸天人，同於一事，見彼所識有差別故。成就無所緣識現可得智。如過去未來夢影緣中有所得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Accomplishing the wisdom of contradictory cognized appearances: as with hungry ghosts, animals, and heavenly beings, who, regarding one and the same thing, perceive it with differing cognitions. Accomplishing the wisdom that cognition can arise with no object actually present: as with past events, future events, and dream-images, where cognition is nevertheless obtained regarding them.

Chinese Source

得心自在一切菩薩，得靜慮者，隨勝解力諸義顯現。得奢摩他修法觀者，纔作意時諸義顯現。已得無分別智者，無分別智現在前時，一切諸義皆不顯現。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who have attained mastery over mind, and those who have attained meditative absorption, can make any object appear simply through the power of resolute intention. Those who have attained calm-abiding and cultivate contemplation on phenomena can make any object appear the very moment attention is directed toward it. For those who have already attained non-discriminating wisdom, when that non-discriminating wisdom is manifest before them, no object whatsoever appears at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all four wisdoms as a cumulative case for "consciousness-only, no [independently real] object" (唯識無義) — not a denial that experience occurs, but a demonstration that what appears to experience never was the fixed, mind-independent thing it seemed to be. The first wisdom is the "one water, four minds" teaching already met in fascicle 36: a human sees ordinary water, a heavenly being sees lapis lazuli, a fish sees its own house, a hungry ghost sees pus and blood — the identical location yielding entirely different experienced objects depending purely on each viewer's own karma, which could never happen if the water possessed one single, fixed, independent nature everyone equally perceived. The second wisdom points to dreaming, remembering the past, and anticipating the future: in every case a vivid cognition arises with no corresponding object actually present anywhere to be found, proving cognition does not require a genuine external object in order to occur.

The third wisdom, which the text takes up separately, contrasts the ordinary being's confidence in their own perception with the sage's very different situation: an unenlightened person assumes what they see is simply how things are and feels no need to question it, while only a noble one perceives without distortion and without needing any effort to correct a mistaken view — the ordinary confidence turns out to be exactly backward, since it is the ordinary view that is habitually mistaken, not the sage's. Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates this with the plain observation that no one questions why cultivating precepts, concentration, and wisdom should be necessary at all if ordinary perception were already accurate.

The fourth wisdom, drawn out through three concrete levels, is the most vivid: a bodhisattva who has attained mastery over mind, or accomplished meditators generally, can transform an object the instant they resolve to — wishing a teacup to become a balloon, or a small thing to become large, is simply accomplished, exactly as Vimalakīrti's famously tiny room held countless visitors without any sense of crowding, impossible if the room's smallness were genuinely fixed. Someone cultivating calm-abiding can make a contemplated object — impermanence, suffering, or any other topic of meditation — appear vividly the instant attention turns to it, in stark contrast to an ordinary being's scattered mind, which cannot even sustain continuous recitation of a single Buddha's name without wandering. And most tellingly of all, one who has actually attained non-discriminating wisdom finds that when this wisdom is genuinely present, no object appears whatsoever — exactly like waking from a dream and finding the entire dreamed world simply gone, which could never happen if that world had been genuinely, independently real in the first place. Together, these four wisdoms are what the treatise means by the power of holiness resting on mastery of concentration — a bodhisattva's mind can reshape what it perceives precisely because nothing perceived was ever fixed to begin with.

3. The Power of Holiness Completed — A Mind Made Utterly Pliant Through Three Incalculable Eons

The power of holiness closes with what makes it possible in the first place: a mind rendered completely pliant — like water taking the shape of whatever vessel holds it — through the sustained cultivation of no-self and no-fixed-phenomena across three incalculable eons, free of affliction and thus free to move however compassion and wisdom together require.

Chinese Source

心調柔故，善修心故，是名聖威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because the mind has become pliant, and because the mind has been well cultivated, this is called the power of holiness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that an afflicted mind is inherently rigid — anger, craving, and confusion each pull thought, speech, and action into fixed, predictable grooves — while a mind that has genuinely subdued self-clinging and clinging to fixed phenomena becomes soft in the way water is soft: poured into a round vessel it is round, poured into a square one it is square, taking whatever shape compassion and wisdom call for without resistance, able to answer a thousand simultaneous appeals exactly where each is made. This pliancy is not a natural gift — no buddha or bodhisattva was born already awakened — but the fruit of uninterrupted cultivation across the provisions, preparation, seeing, cultivation, and final stages of the path, sustained across three full incalculable eons. He cites the *Discourse Records of the Yogācārabhūmi*'s own gloss identifying the power of holiness with the six superknowledges — the first five shared even with accomplished outsiders to the teaching, the sixth, the extinction of contaminants, belonging to noble ones alone — noting that even a bodhisattva who retains subtle habitual traces of past affliction, without the affliction itself any longer active, still counts as having attained this sixth superknowledge in its own partial, genuine sense. This closes the power of holiness (聖威力), the first of the three kinds of power; the power inherent in the Dharma itself (法威力) opens next.

4. The Power of the Dharma Opens — Vast Fruit from Supreme Practices

The second kind of power belongs not to the practitioner directly but to the teachings and practices themselves: the six perfections, cultivated from long before non-discriminating wisdom is ever attained and continuing all the way to buddhahood, carry within them the capacity to produce vast fruit and supreme benefit simply by virtue of what they are.

Chinese Source

二者、法威力。謂諸勝法有廣大果，有大勝利，是名法威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

The second is the power of the Dharma: it is that these supreme practices carry vast fruit and great benefit — this is called the power of the Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies these "supreme practices" as the six perfections — giving, moral discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom — cultivated continuously from well before a bodhisattva ever attains non-discriminating wisdom, through the entire path, and onward without interruption even after buddhahood itself is reached. What distinguishes this from the power of holiness just described is where the power is located: the power of holiness is a capacity possessed by the practitioner's own mastered mind, while the power of the Dharma is, so to speak, built into the practices themselves — vast fruit and supreme benefit follow from genuinely cultivating giving or patience or wisdom regardless of which specific individual carries it out, the way a seed of a particular kind reliably produces its own particular fruit. The treatise now turns to explaining this power's own precise nature in more detail.

5. The Third Power — Innate Power Carried Over From Accumulated Merit, Completing All Three Kinds

The third kind of power needs no present cultivation at all: it is simply carried forward, already complete, from merit accumulated across countless past lives — appearing as an inborn marvel the moment a buddha or bodhisattva is born, closing out the three basic kinds of power before the treatise expands them into a fuller fivefold account.

Chinese Source

此中法者，即是六種波羅蜜多，所謂布施乃至般若。如是諸法有大威力，名法威力。三者、俱生威力。謂佛菩薩先集廣大福德資糧，證得俱生甚希奇法，是名俱生威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

The "Dharma" spoken of here refers to the six perfections — giving, and so on up through wisdom. These practices possess vast power; this is called the power of the Dharma. The third is innate power: buddhas and bodhisattvas, having previously gathered vast provisions of merit, thereby attain innate and truly marvelous qualities — this is called innate power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates innate power with several concrete examples: certain arhats are said to have been born with a lump of gold appearing in their open palm, needing never to beg for support, a direct fruit of generosity practiced in earlier lives; the monk known as "Honey-Bowl" always found his alms bowl spontaneously filled with honey, since in a past life as a monkey he had joyfully offered honey to the Buddha; and the Buddha's own birth was marked by walking seven steps and declaring his own supremacy above all realms, among many other extraordinary signs detailed elsewhere in the treatise. He notes this differs from both prior kinds of power precisely in requiring no present mastery of concentration or present practice of the perfections — it simply ripens automatically, the way a planted seed eventually bears its own fruit without needing to be replanted. This completes the three basic kinds of power (聖威力, 法威力, 俱生威力); the treatise now expands this into a fuller five-fold classification.

6. Milestone: Five Kinds of Power, and the Six Superknowledges That Constitute Miraculous Power

The treatise refines its three-fold account into five: miraculous power, the power of the Dharma, innate power, power shared with hearers and solitary buddhas, and power unique to buddhas and bodhisattvas alone — beginning with miraculous power's own six classical constituents, from moving through solid matter to the extinction of every contaminant.

Chinese Source

又佛菩薩如是威力品類差別，復有五種。一者、神通威力，二者、法威力，三者、俱生威力，四者、共諸聲聞、獨覺威力，五者、不共聲聞、獨覺威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, the distinct kinds of power belonging to buddhas and bodhisattvas may further be classified into five: first, the power of miraculous ability; second, the power of the Dharma; third, innate power; fourth, power shared in common with hearers and solitary buddhas; and fifth, power unique to buddhas and bodhisattvas, not shared with hearers or solitary buddhas.

Chinese Source

云何諸佛菩薩神通威力？謂六神通。一者、神境智作證通，二者、隨念宿住智作證通，三者、天耳智作證通，四者、見死生智作證通，五者、知心差別智作證通，六者、漏盡智作證通。是名神通威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the miraculous power of buddhas and bodhisattvas? It is the six superknowledges: first, the superknowledge of miraculous ability; second, the superknowledge of recollecting past lives; third, the superknowledge of the divine ear; fourth, the superknowledge of seeing death and rebirth; fifth, the superknowledge of knowing others' minds; and sixth, the superknowledge of the extinction of contaminants. This is called miraculous power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi runs through the six briefly: the superknowledge of miraculous ability transforms one thing into many or many into one, reaches bodily even into the form realm's own first dhyāna heaven, and underlies everything the chapter is about to detail; recollecting past lives recovers one's own and others' names, food, and circumstances across countless former births; the divine ear hears speech at any distance without the ordinary ear's limitation; seeing death and rebirth observes precisely where a being goes after dying; knowing others' minds reads another's mental state with a precision ordinary familiarity only crudely approximates; and the extinction of contaminants — attained only by noble ones, unlike the first five, which even accomplished non-Buddhist practitioners can develop — is the direct fruit of having actually exhausted every affliction through the supramundane path. He notes each of these six ultimately rests on the same wisdom already established: since every object is mind-only, a mind that has genuinely mastered itself can display, recall, hear, see, and know across boundaries an ordinary unliberated mind takes to be fixed and impassable. The treatise now turns to the first and most elaborately detailed of the six — the superknowledge of miraculous ability — beginning with its two basic modes.

7. Miraculous Ability's Two Modes — Transforming What Exists, Conjuring What Did Not

The superknowledge of miraculous ability divides into two basic modes: transformation, which takes something already existing and changes it into something else, and conjuring, which brings into being something that was not there at all — together said to comprise eighteen distinct varieties, several of which the treatise now details one by one.

Chinese Source

謂佛菩薩神境智通略有二種。一者、能變通，二者、能化通。如是二種品類差別，各有多種。

Ru-Yi Translation

The superknowledge of miraculous ability possessed by buddhas and bodhisattvas is, in brief, of two kinds: first, the power of transformation; second, the power of conjuring. Each of these two kinds has, in turn, many further distinct varieties.

Chinese Source

謂十八變。一者、震動，二者、熾然，三者、流布，四者、示現，五者、轉變，六者、往來，七者、卷，八者、舒，九者、眾像入身，十者、同類往趣，十一者、顯，十二者、隱，十三者、所作自在，十四者、制他神通，十五者、能施辯才，十六者、能施憶念，十七者、能施安樂，十八者、放大光明。

Ru-Yi Translation

These are the eighteen transformations: quaking, blazing, pervading, showing, transmuting, coming and going, contracting, expanding, gathering every form into one's own body, going forth in the same form as one's audience, revealing, concealing, freely accomplishing whatever is willed, restraining another's miraculous power, bestowing eloquence, bestowing memory, bestowing ease, and radiating great light.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two modes precisely: transformation changes an already-existing thing's own reference point — a teacup becomes a washbasin — while conjuring produces something out of nothing that was not there before — a teacup appears where none existed. He notes an earlier scholastic dispute the treatise's own Mahāyāna position resolves clearly: some schools held that only transformation counts as a genuine "miraculous power" while conjuring is merely its fruit, and that a conjuring mind is karmically neutral rather than wholesome; the Mahāyāna view taken here treats conjuring as equally a form of miraculous power, and treats a buddha's own conjuring as always wholesome, since it exists solely to benefit beings, though an ordinary conjuring act might remain karmically neutral if no such intention is present. With both modes and all eighteen varieties named, the treatise proceeds to explain several of the most vivid ones individually, beginning with the first three.

8. Milestone: Quaking Worlds, Blazing Fire and Water, and Light That Fills Every Realm

The first three of the eighteen transformations announce a buddha's presence before a single word is spoken: making entire world-systems quake, producing fire and water simultaneously from different parts of the body in every color imaginable, and radiating light that fills every dwelling and world without exception.

Chinese Source

震動者，謂佛菩薩得定自在……普能震動寺館舍宅、村邑聚落、城郭國土、那落迦世界、傍生世界、祖父世界、人世界、天世界……乃至無量無數三千大千世界皆能震動。是名震動。

Ru-Yi Translation

Quaking: buddhas and bodhisattvas, having attained mastery over concentration ... are universally able to make temples, dwellings, villages, towns, walled cities and countries, the hell realms, the animal realm, the ghost realm, the human realm, and the heavenly realm quake ... up to an immeasurable, uncountable number of great trichiliocosms, all of them able to quake. This is called quaking.

Chinese Source

熾然者……從其身上發猛焰火，於其身下注清冷水；從其身下發猛焰火，於其身上注清冷水。……是名熾然。流布者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，流布光明，遍滿一切寺館舍宅，乃至無量無數世界無不充滿……是名流布。

Ru-Yi Translation

Blazing: ... fierce flames issue from the upper body while cool water pours from the lower body; fierce flames issue from the lower body while cool water pours from the upper body ... this is called blazing. Pervading: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, radiate light pervading every temple and dwelling, filling even an immeasurable, uncountable number of worlds without exception ... this is called pervading.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lingers on quaking's own cosmology, since the treatise measures its scope in specific units: a single world with four continents; a "small thousand world," containing a thousand such systems, each with its own sun, moon, and Mount Sumeru, reaching up to the first dhyāna heaven; a "medium thousand world," a thousand small-thousand worlds, reaching the second dhyāna; and a "great trichiliocosm," a thousand medium-thousand worlds, reaching the third dhyāna heaven — with the fourth dhyāna standing above even this. He notes a curious etymological aside the treatise flags: the ghost realm is called the "grandfather world" (祖父世界) because, according to the *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā*, the very first being ever to hoard resources selfishly and then steal was also the very first to fall into that realm as a consequence — everyone born there since is, in a sense, a descendant of that original ancestor. He stresses that a buddha's quaking never causes the harm an earthquake does; its entire purpose, as Elder Miàojìng noted, is to shake loose beings' own dormant ignorance and afflictions, functioning almost like an announcement before a teaching begins, drawing attention the way a temple bell calls people to assembly. Blazing similarly displays water and fire moving in perfect, uncontradictory coexistence through a single body, in the seven colors of light the treatise names, demonstrating mastery over even the four elements themselves; pervading simply fills every space touched with light, so vivid that a buddha teaching indoors needs no lamp at all — together, these three transformations announce a buddha's presence and inspire confidence in an audience before a single word of teaching is even spoken.

9. Milestone: Showing Every Realm and Every Pure Land — With a Story of a Monk Humbled by His Own Lay Disciple

The fourth transformation lets an assembled audience see, on request, exactly what samsara and liberation actually look like: every realm of rebirth from hell to the heavens, and every buddha's pure land across universes beyond counting — a vision meant to inspire both renunciation of suffering and aspiration toward genuine purity.

Chinese Source

示現者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，如其所樂，示彼一切諸來會眾……令悉現見下諸惡趣、上諸人天。復令現見諸餘佛土，及於其中諸佛菩薩……悉令現見；亦為宣說彼佛土名，及如來名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Showing: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, show to the entire assembly, as they wish, whatever they take delight in ... causing them all to directly see the miserable realms below and the human and heavenly realms above. They further cause them to directly see other buddhas' lands, and the buddhas and bodhisattvas dwelling within them ... causing them all to directly see this, and also proclaiming the name of that buddha-land, and the name of its Tathāgata.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this transformation reaches every kind of being present at a teaching assembly — monastics, learned laypeople, hearers, bodhisattvas, and the full array of celestial and semi-divine beings named in the treatise's own list — each shown, according to what genuinely benefits them, the lower realms' suffering and the higher realms' relative ease, alongside distant buddhas' pure lands, so that renunciation and aspiration both arise from direct sight rather than mere description.

He shares a story illustrating why sincerity in practice matters more than outward skill: two monks with beautiful voices for chanting nonetheless performed the morning and evening liturgy carelessly, as if merely singing a song, without genuine reverence. A lay visitor, deeply moved by their chanting despite this, practiced with real sincerity alongside it and, in time, attained the first stage of awakening. After this disciple's death and rebirth among the heavens, he encountered the same two monks — reborn not as gods but as a music-deity and a song-deity, entertaining the heavenly assembly, a direct karmic fruit of their fondness for chanting without the devotion that should have accompanied it. Greeting them with familiar warmth, he was met with their own embarrassed admission: "once you came to hear us chant — now we sing for you, and you have attained realization while we still have not." Stung into genuine effort, the two are said to have gone on to attain the fruit themselves not long after. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson plainly: a student can sometimes outpace the very teacher who first inspired them, and talent without sincerity, however impressive, only postpones — never replaces — the real work of practice.

10. Milestone: Transmuting the Four Elements — Mind Creates the World It Stands On

The fifth transformation, transmuting, takes any one of the four elements — earth, water, fire, or wind — and turns it genuinely into any other, precisely as resolved and never by accident, revealing the same truth behind every transformation in this chapter: nothing perceived was ever fixed, only ever mind's own display.

Chinese Source

轉變者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，若於其地起水勝解，即令成水如實非餘；火、風勝解亦復如是。……若於一切起餘勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Transmuting: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration — if they form the resolute conviction that earth is water, it genuinely becomes water and nothing else; the same holds for resolving it into fire or wind. ... Whatever resolute conviction is formed regarding any of these, it genuinely becomes exactly that and nothing else.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi grounds this directly in the mind-only teaching already established: ordinary beings stand on solid ground because collective karmic seeds project a shared, seemingly fixed earth-experience, yet even among ordinary beings the same ground never appears quite identically to any two perceivers. A buddha or bodhisattva, never mistaking this collectively-projected ground for something genuinely independent of mind, can simply resolve it into water — and genuinely drink it, not merely display an illusion of water the way a stage magician's trick water cannot actually be swallowed — precisely because "earth" was never more solid than mind's own conviction to begin with. He cites the *Avataṃsaka-sūtra*'s own famous verse — "if you wish to know all buddhas of past, present, and future, contemplate the nature of the Dharma-realm: everything is mind alone" — as the doctrinal heart of every transformation this chapter describes, including a mustard seed containing an entire Mount Sumeru without either one changing size, since "large" and "small" were never fixed properties to begin with.

He adds a curious clarification from elsewhere in the treatise: if two bodhisattvas of equal power simultaneously will opposing transformations onto the same object — one resolving earth into water, the other into fire — the equal, opposing convictions cancel out and neither succeeds; if their power is unequal, the stronger resolve prevails; and if their power is equal but one commits first with full attention, that one succeeds. The lesson he draws is a practical one about ordinary effort as much as miraculous power: single-minded resolve accomplishes what divided or hesitant intention cannot, in worldly practice no less than supernormal display.

11. Transmuting Ordinary Materials into Treasure, Trained Through the Eight Mastery-Bases

The same power that transmutes one element into another extends to their derivative qualities and to ordinary materials themselves — grass and dung becoming food and clothing, gravel becoming pearl and coral, a mountain becoming solid gold — trained specifically through a meditative sequence called the eight mastery-bases, moving from small objects to vast ones and finally to color itself.

Chinese Source

若於草葉、牛糞、泥等，起於飲食、車乘、衣服、嚴飾資具、種種塗香、華鬘勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。若於沙石、瓦礫等物，起於末尼、真珠、琉璃……勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。若於諸山、雪山王等起金勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, regarding grass, leaves, dung, or mud, the resolute conviction is formed that these are food, a carriage, clothing, ornaments, fragrant incense, or garlands, they genuinely become exactly that. If, regarding sand, gravel, or rubble, the resolute conviction is formed that these are gemstones, pearls, or lapis lazuli, they genuinely become exactly that. If, regarding any mountain, even the great snow-capped king of mountains, the resolute conviction is formed that it is gold, it genuinely becomes gold.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi connects this capacity to a specific meditative training called the eight mastery-bases (八勝處): the first two, cultivated in the first and second dhyānas, master a small range of ordinary objects and then a vast range (a single teacup, then an entire palace or river, appearing the instant it is willed); the third and fourth repeat this at a deeper level of absorption, once attachment to form itself has loosened; and the final four, cultivated in the fourth dhyāna, master pure color itself — turning everything perceived uniformly blue, yellow, red, or white at will, the meditative root of a painter's palette taken to its limit, where a practitioner need not wait for autumn wind to turn a leaf red, but simply wills it so. Mastery over these eight bases is what makes possible the treasure-transmutation just described — coarse material transmuted into refined material is simply a further application of exactly the same trained conviction. He notes this power is available not only to a fully realized bodhisattva but, to a lesser and more effortful degree, even to accomplished meditators generally, since it rests on skill in concentration rather than complete awakening alone.

12. Milestone: Transmuting a Being's Own Appearance — The Ghost Story That Dissolved a Monk's Attachment to Self

The same transmuting power extends even to a sentient being's own physical appearance — beautiful made plain, plain made beautiful, missing limbs restored — illustrated with a striking story in which two ghosts, quarreling over a corpse, unknowingly dismantle a doubting monk's entire body piece by piece and replace it with the corpse's own parts, leaving him utterly unable to say who he still is.

Chinese Source

若於好色有情起惡色勝解，於惡色有情起好色勝解，於俱非有情起好色、惡色勝解，於俱有情起俱非勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。如於好色、惡色，於具支節、不具支節，及肥瘦等，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, regarding a being of pleasing appearance, the resolute conviction is formed that they are of displeasing appearance — or the reverse — or regarding a being neither pleasing nor displeasing, that they are one or the other — or regarding a being who is both, that they are neither — it genuinely becomes exactly that. Just as with pleasing and displeasing appearance, so too with having complete or incomplete limbs, and with being stout or thin — it should be understood to hold the same way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this power to reshape appearance and even restore missing limbs belongs not only to buddhas and bodhisattvas but, in a lesser form, to accomplished arhats — illustrated by a story concerning a disciple of the arhat Upagupta who, worn down by the rigors of practice, wished to return to lay life. Upagupta simply asked him to spend one more night in his room before leaving. That night, the young monk saw a small ghost drag in a corpse, followed by a large ghost demanding to eat it; the two quarreled and, noticing the monk, demanded he settle whose corpse it rightfully was. Reasoning that a lie would get him killed as readily as the truth, he answered honestly that the small ghost had dragged it in — enraging the large ghost, who tore off the monk's right arm and ate it, whereupon the small ghost calmly reattached the corpse's own right arm in its place. This repeated, limb by limb and finally the torso and head themselves, until every part of the monk's original body had been eaten by the large ghost and replaced by the corpse's, the two ghosts finally wiping their mouths and departing.

Waking the next morning, the monk found himself seized by a single overwhelming question: "who am I?" Every part of the body he had always taken to be himself had, over the course of one night, been entirely replaced — he could no longer locate any "self" left to return home in. Reporting this to Upagupta, he received in response the teaching of no-self: the body was never truly his to begin with, and form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness alike are without a self and without anything belonging to a self. Hearing this teaching in the direct aftermath of the night's own visceral demonstration, the monk realized the first fruit of the path on the spot, and never again wished to return to lay life. Master Cháng Bǎi observes that if even a ghost's crude interference could dismantle self-clinging this thoroughly, how much more can a buddha or bodhisattva's own deliberate transformation of a being's appearance serve the identical purpose — restoring sight to the blind, hearing to the deaf, and limbs to the maimed, wherever doing so genuinely serves to loosen the same attachment. This closes transmuting, the fifth of the eighteen; coming-and-going and several further transformations follow next.

13. Coming and Going Without Obstruction, Folding Mount Sumeru Into an Atom, and Gathering Every Form Into One's Own Body

Three further transformations display mastery over space itself: moving through solid walls and mountains as freely as through open air, traveling to any world at the speed of a single thought; contracting the vastest mountain into a single mote of dust and expanding a single mote into a mountain range without either genuinely growing or shrinking; and drawing an entire assembled crowd, town, forest, and mountain range into one's own body, with everyone inside plainly seeing themselves there.

Chinese Source

往來者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，隨其所樂，於諸牆壁、山石等中縱身往來，無有滯礙。廣說乃至往於梵世，乃至上至色究竟天，還來無礙。或復傍於無量無數三千大千世界，若往、若來皆無滯礙。

Ru-Yi Translation

Coming and going: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, move their bodies freely through walls, mountains, and rock as they please, without any obstruction — extending even to journeying to the Brahmā realm and up to the highest form-realm heaven, returning without hindrance; or ranging across an immeasurable, uncountable number of great trichiliocosms, coming and going entirely without obstruction.

Chinese Source

卷、舒者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，能卷一切雪山王等如一極微，舒一極微令如一切雪山王等。是名卷、舒。眾像入身者……能以種種現前大眾，及以一切村邑、聚落、草木、叢林、諸山、大地一切色像內己身中，令諸大眾各各自知入其身內。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contracting and expanding: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, can contract even the great snow-capped king of mountains until it is like a single mote of dust, and expand a single mote of dust until it is like the great snow-capped king of mountains. This is called contracting and expanding. Gathering forms into the body: ... they are able to gather the entire assembled crowd before them, along with every village, town, plant, forest, mountain, and expanse of earth, into their own body, causing everyone to directly know that they have entered within it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains coming-and-going through the same principle already established: "a wall is not truly a wall — it is merely called a wall." An ordinary being, seeing a wall and taking it as genuinely solid, collides with it and is hurt; a buddha or bodhisattva, never mistaking the wall's imputed solidity for an independent reality, passes through as freely as through open space — reaching even the highest heaven of the form realm, or traveling to countless other world-systems, in a single instant, since the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s own commentary notes that such travel can cross immeasurable worlds "in a single moment." He explains contracting and expanding through the *Avataṃsaka*'s teaching of the mutual, unobstructed interpenetration of the one and the many — folding ten directions' worth of infinite worlds into a single mote of dust without the dust growing any larger, or expanding a single mote to contain ten directions' worth of worlds without those worlds growing any smaller, since size, like every other quality already examined in this chapter, was never a fixed, independent property to begin with. Gathering forms into the body he illustrates with the familiar image of the Monkey King unable to escape the Buddha's own palm — no matter how far he leapt, he never left it — a fitting picture of how thoroughly an ordinary being's entire experienced world remains, all along, within the compass of an awakened mind. This closes contracting-expanding and gathering-forms, the seventh and eighth of the eighteen transformations; a ninth, going forth in an audience's own form, follows next with two especially memorable stories.

14. Milestone: Going Forth in an Audience's Own Form — Two Stories of Mistaking a Bodhisattva for an Ordinary Being

The ninth transformation lets a buddha or bodhisattva appear in exactly the form, speech, and manner of whichever audience they wish to teach — a king among kings, a god among gods — teaching through four kinds of genuine reasoning before vanishing the instant the work is done, illustrated here with two well-loved stories in which Mañjuśrī tests unsuspecting monks in disguise, with very different outcomes.

Chinese Source

同類往趣者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，或能往趣剎帝利眾，同其色類，如彼形量，似彼言音……然後為其演說正法，示現、教導、讚勵、慶慰。化事既終，欻然隱沒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Going forth in the same form: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, are able to go among an assembly of kings and nobles in their same form, matching their stature and manner of speech ... and then expound the true teaching to them — showing, instructing, encouraging, and consoling. Once this work of transformation is complete, they suddenly vanish.

Chinese Source

沒後時眾迭相顧言：不知沒者天耶？人耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

After they vanish, the assembly looks at one another and asks: "Was the one who just disappeared a god, or a human being?"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the four-fold teaching method named here — showing the four genuine principles of reasoning, settling students into the precepts appropriate to them, encouraging those who falter, and warmly congratulating those who progress — drawing in passing on the treatise's own four kinds of reasoning already used to investigate all things: reasoning by mutual dependence (nothing exists on its own, everything arises relative to something else — the root insight behind mind-only itself); reasoning by function (each thing, however empty of fixed nature, still has its own working role — earth is solid, water is moist, precisely as a monastery's kitchen-duty monk and its liturgy-duty monk each have distinct tasks); reasoning by valid proof (checked against direct experience, sound inference, and reliable scriptural testimony); and reasoning by the nature of things as they simply are (impermanence is simply how conditioned things are, and pressing "but why is it impermanent?" past this point leads nowhere useful).

He then tells two stories of Mañjuśrī testing unsuspecting hosts in disguise. In the first, at a great open feast where none were to be turned away, a visibly pregnant woman arrived with a dog and asked for a meal box — then a second for the dog — then a third, explaining she was eating for the unborn child as well. The attending monk, forgetting the feast's own founding vow, scolded her for greed; she instantly rose into the sky, revealed as Mañjuśrī with the dog in tow, leaving the monk overcome with regret at having failed to recognize the very bodhisattva he had just insulted. In the second, Mañjuśrī appeared cooking in a monastery kitchen during an ordination ceremony; a monk who had been told the cook might be Mañjuśrī bowed and asked for wisdom, and was told he need only climb into the blazing stove to receive it — hesitating at the threshold, he lost his nerve, and Mañjuśrī vanished along with the chance. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the two stories together as a single, gentle challenge to the reader: recognizing genuine virtue often asks more of us than politeness toward whoever looks the part — and asks, quite directly, whether any of us, offered that same fiery threshold, would have stepped through.

15. Extending to Every Realm — The Full Ladder of Heavens

Matching-form extends without exception up through the entire cosmology the treatise recognizes: brahmins, ascetics, elders, and lay Buddhists; the six heavens of the desire realm; the eighteen heavens of the form realm across all four levels of meditative absorption, including the five pure abodes reserved for non-returners; and, though not detailed here since it has no physical form to match, the formless realm above even these.

Chinese Source

如能往趣剎帝利眾，如是往趣婆羅門眾、若沙門眾、若長者眾、若居士眾、四天王天、三十三天、夜摩天、覩史多天、樂變化天、他化自在天、梵眾天……乃至色究竟天，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as one may go forth among an assembly of kings and nobles, so too among brahmins, ascetics, elders, and lay householders; among the Four Great Kings, the Thirty-Three, the Yāma heaven, the Tuṣita heaven, the heaven of delighting in creation, and the heaven of mastery over others' creations; among the Brahmā retinue heavens ... up to the highest form-realm heaven — it should be understood to hold the same way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps out the full ladder briefly: six heavens of the desire realm, rising from the Four Great Kings and the Thirty-Three (ruled by Śakra) through increasingly refined pleasure up to the sixth heaven, home of the desire realm's own tempter; eighteen heavens of the form realm, three for each of the first three levels of meditative absorption (graded by depth of concentration) and a further group for the fourth level, including five "pure abode" heavens reserved specifically for non-returners approaching final liberation; and, above all form entirely, the four formless heavens — twenty-eight heavens in all. He notes the treatise omits detailing the formless realm here for a simple reason: since beings there possess no physical form at all, a bodhisattva teaches them mind-to-mind rather than through any matching bodily appearance, so the entire premise of "going forth in matching form" has nothing to match. This closes the ninth transformation in full; three brief transformations — appearing and disappearing, free accomplishment of any action, and restraining others' miraculous power — follow next.

16. Appearing and Disappearing at Will, Free Accomplishment, and a Hierarchy of Restraining Power

Three further transformations round out the middle of the eighteen: vanishing and reappearing before an audience hundreds or thousands of times at will; moving, staying, coming, and speaking with complete freedom while living among ordinary beings; and restraining a lesser power's display entirely — a hierarchy in which a buddha outranks every bodhisattva, and each bodhisattva ground outranks the ones below it.

Chinese Source

隱、顯者……於大眾前，百度、千度，或過於是，隱沒自身，復令顯現。所作自在者……普於一切諸有情界往、來、住等所作事中，皆自在轉。令去即去，令住即住，令來即來，令語即語。

Ru-Yi Translation

Concealing and revealing: ... before a great assembly, hundreds or thousands of times, or even more, they conceal themselves and then cause themselves to reappear. Free accomplishment: ... in every act of going, coming, staying, and so on, throughout the entire world of sentient beings, they act with complete freedom — going when they wish to go, staying when they wish to stay, coming when they wish to come, and speaking when they wish to speak.

Chinese Source

制他神通者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，能制伏他所現神通。如來神通普能制伏其餘一切具神通者所現神通，如其所欲，令事成辦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Restraining others' miraculous power: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, are able to restrain the miraculous power displayed by others. A Tathāgata's own miraculous power is universally able to restrain the power displayed by every other possessor of miraculous power, accomplishing whatever is wished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates free accomplishment with the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* commentary's own example — transforming a demon king's very appearance into a buddha's own form — and treats restraining others' power as establishing a clear hierarchy: a Buddha's power restrains everyone else's without exception, since no rival resolve can withstand it, exactly as in a contest between two bodhisattvas willing opposing outcomes, the stronger conviction always prevails. He recalls the *Vimalakīrti-nirdeśa*'s own famous illustration of this hierarchy: when Śāriputra objects to a goddess's teaching, she transforms him into her own likeness and herself into his, leaving him powerless to reverse it — proof that a bodhisattva's mastery of concentration outstrips a hearer's own; the same episode's scattered flowers cling to Śāriputra's robes no matter how hard he brushes them off, while falling cleanly away from the bodhisattvas present, since flowers only cling where some remaining attachment gives them something to stick to. This closes appearing-disappearing, free accomplishment, and the general principle behind restraining others' power; the treatise now turns to the specific rankings among bodhisattvas themselves.

17. Milestone: The Ultimate Bodhisattva, and a Distinctive Teaching on Where Buddhahood Is Actually Attained

Restraining others' power sorts bodhisattvas into a precise hierarchy, culminating in the "ultimate bodhisattva" — one bound to only one more lifetime before buddhahood, like Maitreya now residing in Tuṣita, or one already in their final existence before awakening — and the treatise offers a striking, specific claim about exactly where full buddhahood is attained: not in this ordinary human world at all, but in the highest heaven above even the form realm's own summit.

Chinese Source

究竟菩薩一生所繫，或最後有所有神通，除諸如來、等類菩薩，悉能制伏其餘一切具神通者所現神通。

Ru-Yi Translation

The miraculous power possessed by an ultimate bodhisattva — one bound to only one more rebirth, or one already in their final existence — is able to restrain the power displayed by every possessor of miraculous power other than the Tathāgatas and bodhisattvas of the same rank.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two kinds of "ultimate bodhisattva": one bound to a single further lifetime, like Maitreya presently residing in Tuṣita heaven awaiting his final descent, whose next rebirth will certainly be as a buddha; and one already in that very final lifetime, typically born into a great brahmin or royal household with the provisions for awakening already fully complete, in which full, unsurpassed enlightenment will actually be realized. He shares Elder Miàojìng's own striking observation on the scale involved: after three full incalculable eons of accumulating merit through the six perfections, it would seem strange for a buddha's culminating achievement to be nothing grander than an ordinary small human body in this Sahā world — and indeed, per the treatise's own teaching, this is not where full buddhahood is actually realized. The treatise instead locates it in the Heaven of Great Sovereignty (Maheśvara), a heaven standing even above the form realm's own highest peak, Akaniṣṭha — the single most exalted rebirth destination described anywhere in the cosmology, reached only through the purest possible supramundane karma. What is seen in this world — birth in Lumbinī, the six years of austerity, the awakening beneath the bodhi tree, and the eventual passing into final nirvāṇa — is, on this account, an emanation body's own display for the sake of beings here, appropriately scaled to what an ordinary audience can actually perceive, while the truly complete realization unfolds at a register no unassisted human eye could take in. This closes the account of restraining others' power in full; bestowing eloquence and bestowing memory, two brief and practically comforting transformations, follow next.

18. Bestowing Eloquence When Words Run Dry, and Memory When the Teaching Slips Away

Two further transformations offer quiet, practical reassurance to anyone teaching the Dharma: when a speaker's eloquence genuinely runs out mid-sentence, it can simply be restored; and when the thread of the teaching slips from memory entirely, it can simply be given back.

Chinese Source

能施辯才者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，若諸有情辯才窮盡，能與辯才。是名能施辯才。能施憶念者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，若諸有情於法失念，能與憶念。是名能施憶念。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bestowing eloquence: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, are able to give eloquence to beings whose own eloquence has run out. This is called bestowing eloquence. Bestowing memory: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, are able to give memory back to beings who have lost their recollection of the teaching. This is called bestowing memory.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi offers this as genuinely practical comfort for anyone who teaches: an ordinary person, mid-explanation, sometimes finds their mind suddenly blank, unable to continue — precisely the moment, he suggests, when quietly turning the intention toward benefiting the listener invites unseen support to fill the gap, rather than being cause for panic. He connects bestowing memory to the *Lotus Sūtra*'s own account of Samantabhadra Bodhisattva, who is said to appear and gently remind a reciter who has grown lax exactly when their attention on the text has begun to wander, restoring the thread without the reciter needing to notice anything unusual happened at all. This closes both transformations; bestowing ease — including two memorable stories about relief from illness and disaster — follows next.

19. Milestone: Bestowing Ease — Temporary Relief From the Five Hindrances, and Two Stories of Mind Transforming Circumstance

The fifteenth transformation offers listeners a real, if temporary, physical and mental ease during teaching — freeing them from the five hindrances that would otherwise pull attention away — and extends further to relieving plagues and disasters altogether, illustrated with two stories: villagers saved from blood-drinking spirits by Amitābha and Avalokiteśvara, and a monk who healed a cancer-like affliction over five years of sustained meditation on emptiness.

Chinese Source

能施安樂者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，說正法時，與聽法者饒益身心輕安之樂，令離諸蓋，專心聽法。暫時方便，而非究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bestowing ease: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, when expounding the true teaching, grant listeners a beneficial ease of body and mind, freeing them from the hindrances so they may listen with focused attention. This is a temporary expedient, not an ultimate one.

Chinese Source

又令諸界互相違變能為損害、非人所行、災癘、疾疫皆得息滅。是名能施安樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

They are also able to bring to rest the harm caused by the four elements turning against one another within the body, the doings of non-human spirits, and epidemics and plagues. This is called bestowing ease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to preserve the treatise's own honest qualification: the ease granted during teaching — freedom from craving, aversion, dullness, restlessness, and doubt for the duration of the discourse — is a genuine kindness but only ever temporary, since the underlying seeds of these hindrances are not thereby destroyed; permanently uprooting them still requires the listener's own sustained practice, exactly as an audience absorbed for hours in a film or a game shows the mind is capable of sustained, undistracted attention when sufficiently engaged — the real task is bringing that same capacity to the cultivation of concentration itself.

He tells of a village during the Buddha's own lifetime plagued by blood-drinking spirits, its people falling sick with no remedy found. When the village elders came before the Buddha for help, he directed them not to his own intervention but toward Amitābha Buddha and Avalokiteśvara in the west, instructing them to bow and pray in that direction — whereupon Amitābha and Avalokiteśvara appeared directly before the assembly, and the villagers, confessing their karmic obstructions before the two, were freed from the affliction. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the Buddha could certainly have subdued the spirits himself, but chose this occasion instead to build the villagers' own confidence in seeking refuge in Amitābha's Pure Land — a reminder that sincere, heartfelt prayer genuinely can relieve both bodily illness and karmic affliction, though its effectiveness depends on the sincerity, correctness, and undivided attention actually brought to the practice.

He pairs this with an account from Elder Miàojìng of a monk diagnosed with a carbuncle resembling what would now be called cancer, who retreated to the mountains to meditate rather than seek treatment, sustaining a rigorous practice of emptiness-contemplation through years of considerable pain until, after five years, the illness had genuinely resolved. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson directly: an ordinary being's mind is generally controlled by the body — even simply choosing to stay awake a little longer feels like an ordeal — while a mind that has genuinely transformed itself through practice can, to a real degree, transform the very circumstances the body presents; taking a buddha or bodhisattva as one's sustained object of mind is itself already a share in this same principle, since a pure object cultivated with a pure mind naturally yields a purer experienced world. This closes bestowing ease, the fifteenth transformation; radiating great light, the eighteenth and final one, opens next.

20. Radiating Great Light — Two Kinds, and Summoning Every Realm to Assembly

The final transformation is light itself: an ordinary, constant radiance every buddha and bodhisattva always possesses, and a second, deliberately purposeful radiance sent out to relieve suffering across the lower realms in all ten directions, or to summon the full array of heavenly and semi-divine beings to gather for a teaching about to begin.

Chinese Source

放大光明者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，以神通力，身放光明。或有一光往十方面無量無數諸世界中，令惡趣等一切有情息彼眾苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Radiating great light: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, radiate light from their bodies through miraculous power. A single ray of this light may travel to immeasurable, uncountable worlds throughout the ten directions, bringing all beings in the miserable realms and elsewhere to the cessation of their many sufferings.

Chinese Source

或有一光往諸天界，令大威德天、龍、藥叉、健達縛、阿素洛、揭路荼、緊捺洛、牟呼洛伽等住自宮中，蒙光覺悟，皆來集會。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or a single ray of light may travel to the heavenly realms, so that mighty gods, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kiṃnaras, and mahoragas, each dwelling in their own palace, are awakened by this light and all gather together in assembly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two kinds of light named here: an "ordinary radiance" every buddha and bodhisattva continuously possesses regardless of occasion, and a deliberately directed light summoned for a specific purpose, as described in this passage. One ray reaching the lower realms in every direction at once brings immediate relief from suffering there — not final liberation, but a genuine, if temporary, respite; another reaching the heavens rouses the eightfold assembly of divine and semi-divine beings named here (the same group already met earlier in this fascicle showing every realm to an audience) out of their own palaces and into gathered assembly, since a teaching of consequence calls for exactly this kind of announcement beforehand. This closes the account of the sixteenth transformation; the treatise now turns to the remaining transformations that complete the eighteen.

21. Radiating Light Closes All Eighteen Transformations of the Power to Transmute

A third and final use of light — summoning bodhisattvas from countless other world-systems to attend a teaching — closes out radiating light, and with it all eighteen varieties of transmuting what already exists, which the treatise now names, in one summary statement, the power to transmute an existing thing's own nature into something else entirely.

Chinese Source

或有一光往十方面無量無數諸世界中，令住他方世界菩薩蒙光覺悟，皆來集會。……以要言之，諸如來等能放無量無數品類種種光明，能作無量無數世界無量無數諸有情類無量無數利益之事。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or a single ray of light may travel to immeasurable, uncountable worlds throughout the ten directions, so that bodhisattvas dwelling in other world-systems are awakened by this light and all gather together in assembly. ... In brief, the Tathāgatas are able to radiate immeasurable, uncountable kinds of light of every variety, accomplishing immeasurable, uncountable acts of benefit for immeasurable, uncountable beings across immeasurable, uncountable worlds.

Chinese Source

當知如是一切能變神境智通品類差別，一一分別無量無數。由此神通，能轉所餘有自性物令成餘物，故名能變神境智通。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood that all such distinct varieties of the power to transmute are, if distinguished one by one, truly immeasurable and uncountable. Because this miraculous power is able to change any remaining thing possessing its own nature into another thing, it is called the power to transmute.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that bodhisattvas summoned from distant pure lands first formally ask leave of their own resident buddha — "I wish to go hear Śākyamuni Buddha teach" — and receive not only permission but active encouragement to attend, illustrating how buddhas across countless world-systems support rather than compete with one another's teaching activity. He confirms the eighteen named varieties are only a convenient summary — the treatise itself says the true count is beyond measure — and clarifies precisely why this whole category is called "transmuting": every example given, from quaking worlds to radiating light, takes some already-existing thing possessing its own working nature (earth's solidity, a body's given form) and turns it into another thing possessing a different working nature, never conjuring something from outright nothing. This closes the power to transmute (能變通) in its entirety, all eighteen varieties complete; the power to conjure (能化通), the second basic mode of miraculous ability, opens next.

22. Milestone: Conjuring Opens — Creating Body, Realm, and Speech From Nothing at All

Where transmuting changes something that already exists, conjuring brings into being what was not there at all — sorted into three basic kinds: conjuring a body where none existed, conjuring a realm or object where none existed, and conjuring speech itself, even from something as ordinarily silent as an empty forest or a bare rock.

Chinese Source

云何能化神境智通品類差別？謂若略說，無事而有，是名為化。能以化心，隨其所欲，造作種種未曾有事，故名能化神境智通。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the distinct varieties of the power to conjure? In brief: bringing into being what was not there at all is called conjuring. Being able, through a conjuring mind, to produce whatever unprecedented thing is wished for — this is called the power to conjure.

Chinese Source

此復多種。或化為身及化為境，或化為語。

Ru-Yi Translation

This, too, has many varieties: conjuring a body, conjuring a realm or object, and conjuring speech.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes conjuring cleanly from transmuting: transmuting always starts from some existing thing and redirects it, while conjuring produces an entirely new appearance where nothing at all stood before — a body appearing where there was none, treasures or provisions materializing from empty space, or speech arising from what is ordinarily voiceless, as in the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-sūtra*'s account of the bodhisattva Sadāprarudita, who, searching for the perfection of wisdom in a quiet forest, heard the teaching spoken directly from the empty air itself. He notes one deliberate omission: no fourth category, "conjuring mind," is given, since mind is always the direct fruit of one's own karma and can never itself be manufactured — what appears to be another being's presence may be conjured convincingly in body, environment, and voice, yet carries no actual mind or karmic history of its own behind it. This closes the basic three-fold division; the treatise now details conjured bodies specifically, beginning with how closely they may resemble their source.

23. Conjured Bodies — Sometimes Countless Copies of Oneself, Sometimes Utterly Unlike

A conjured body may closely resemble the one conjuring it, or not resemble it at all, and may likewise resemble some other being entirely, or not — four basic combinations illustrated vividly by Amitābha Buddha's countless emanated forms and Avalokiteśvara's traditional thirty-three distinct manifestations, none of them sharing the same genuine sense faculties as an embodied being born of actual karma.

Chinese Source

或化為身及化為境者，化似自身，或不相似；化似他身，或不相似。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding conjuring a body or a realm: a conjured body may resemble one's own form, or not resemble it; it may resemble another's form, or not resemble it.

Chinese Source

又所化身，若自、若他，或似、不似，唯能化作與根相似根所依處，而非實根。復能化作相似境界。謂飲食等，末尼、真珠、琉璃寶等，所有色、香、味、觸所攝外資生具。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, whether such a conjured body resembles oneself or another, whether similar or dissimilar, it can only be conjured with the outward appearance of the sense faculties' physical basis, never the genuine sense faculties themselves. They are also able to conjure resembling environments — food and drink, gemstones, pearls, lapis lazuli, and every other outward provision comprising form, scent, taste, and texture.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates all four combinations with familiar devotional imagery: Amitābha Buddha emanating countless identical buddha-forms from his own light is conjuring resembling oneself; those same emanations differing subtly from one another in posture — some seated, some standing to welcome the dying — is conjuring resembling oneself yet not entirely alike; Avalokiteśvara's traditional thirty-three manifestations, from the fish-basket form to the child-bestowing form, are all recognizably Avalokiteśvara yet no two identical, illustrating conjuring resembling another that is not entirely alike either; and a bodhisattva conjuring the form of an entirely different bodhisattva altogether covers the remaining combination.

He stresses one consistent limitation running through every conjured body: however convincing its outward eyes, ears, or limbs may appear, these are only the physical basis resembling genuine sense faculties, never the actual purified sense faculties themselves, which arise strictly from one's own karmic ripening — struck, a conjured body feels nothing, since no actual perceiving consciousness is behind it. Conjured environments follow the identical principle: a conjured banquet or a conjured cache of jewels appears with full convincing detail, yet remains, at root, an image without its own independent substance. This closes the basic distinction between conjured bodies and conjured environments; the treatise now details conjured bodies at far greater length, across the full range of beings a bodhisattva might take the form of.

24. Conjured Bodies Across Every Realm — From Gods to Buddhas, One Form or Countless at Once

A bodhisattva's conjured bodies span every category of being without exception — devas, nāgas, and the rest of the eightfold retinue; humans, animals, ghosts, and hell-beings; even hearers, solitary buddhas, bodhisattvas, and buddhas themselves — each conjured form graded by how closely it resembles its model, and produced singly or in countless number at once, precisely as needed to benefit however many beings a given moment calls for.

Chinese Source

又所化身與己同類，非一眾多種種差別。或作天、龍、藥叉、健達縛、阿素洛、揭路荼、緊捺洛、牟呼洛伽色像，或作人、傍生、鬼、那落迦色像，或作聲聞、獨覺、菩薩、如來色像。

Ru-Yi Translation

A conjured body of the same general kind as oneself may take countless distinct forms. One may conjure the form of a deva, nāga, yakṣa, gandharva, asura, garuḍa, kiṃnara, or mahoraga; the form of a human, animal, ghost, or hell-being; or the form of a hearer, solitary buddha, bodhisattva, or Tathāgata.

Chinese Source

云何此中化作多身？謂佛菩薩於十方面無量無數諸世界中，一時化作種種形類，能為無量無數有情作利益事。

Ru-Yi Translation

What does it mean to conjure many bodies at once? Buddhas and bodhisattvas, throughout immeasurable, uncountable worlds in the ten directions, simultaneously conjure every variety of form, thereby accomplishing acts of benefit for immeasurable, uncountable sentient beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the treatise's own long, careful sorting of "resembling self or another, similar or dissimilar" across every one of these categories as one continuous point rather than many separate rules: whatever form is conjured — deva or hell-being, hearer or buddha — it may match its model with photographic closeness or only loosely, and the same bodhisattva may conjure now one such form and now countless different ones, all at the identical moment, across as many world-systems as beings need reaching. He notes the deliberate completeness of the list itself: nothing in the entire range of possible rebirth, from the lowest hell to the highest sagehood, falls outside a bodhisattva's conjuring reach, since a form suited to whichever specific being needs teaching is always the actual criterion, never any inherent limit on what can be produced. This closes the basic account of conjured bodies' range; the treatise now turns to how these many simultaneous conjured forms actually conclude their work once it is finished.

25. Conjured Forms Persist or Cease as Needed, Matched to Each Being's Own Readiness

A conjured form may either vanish the instant its immediate task is finished, or continue functioning through a buddha's own sustaining power long after that buddha's final passing — and the treatise sorts a buddha's teaching emanations into four levels, precisely matched to how ready each audience already is: a bodhisattva's form for those not yet ripe, a buddha's own form across vast realms for those ready for liberation now, and a hearer's or solitary buddha's form for those specifically aiming at those two paths.

Chinese Source

如是所化種種形類，於中或有諸佛菩薩雖滅度後，由住持力而故隨轉；或有暫時作利益已，化事便息。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these various conjured forms, some, even after the buddhas and bodhisattvas who conjured them have passed into final nirvāṇa, continue to function through their sustaining power; others, having accomplished a temporary benefit, simply cease once that work is done.

Chinese Source

諸佛世尊有四種化，遍十方界功能無礙。一、未成熟有情令成熟故，作菩薩行化。二、已成熟有情令解脫故……同於一時方便攝受如來之化。三、即為彼所化有情作聲聞化。四、即為彼所化有情作獨覺化。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas, the world-honored ones, have four kinds of emanation, functioning without obstruction throughout the ten directions. First, in order to bring not-yet-ripened beings to ripeness, they conjure the activity of a bodhisattva. Second, in order to liberate already-ripened beings ... they simultaneously conjure, through skillful means, the presence of a Tathāgata itself. Third, for beings to be guided in this way, they conjure the activity of a hearer. Fourth, for beings to be guided in this way, they conjure the activity of a solitary buddha.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi offers two examples of persisting conjured presence: one buddha is said to have entered final nirvāṇa shortly after attaining awakening, leaving behind a single emanated form to continue teaching for a full eon in his place; and Śākyamuni's own true teaching, though he himself has passed away, is said to continue functioning in the world through this same sustaining power, letting later students still genuinely learn from it. He pairs this with well-known accounts of lingering, conditional visibility — Xuánzàng, on pilgrimage, is said to have seen the Buddha's own shadow in a stone chamber only after sincere repentance, while the bandits traveling with him saw it only partially or not at all until their own repentance deepened; a similar cave at Mount Pǔtuó is said to reveal Avalokiteśvara only to some visitors, Dr. Sun Yat-sen among those reportedly granted the vision. He explains the four-fold emanation hierarchy as matched precisely to spiritual readiness: a bodhisattva-emanation for beings whose wholesome roots are not yet mature enough for direct liberation this life; a buddha's own emanated presence, extending simultaneously across a vast range of world-systems, for beings whose roots have already ripened and need only direct teaching to attain the path; and a hearer's or solitary buddha's own emanated form for those specifically oriented toward those two more limited vehicles. This closes conjured bodies in full; conjured realms and environments follow next.

26. Conjured Realms — Sometimes Only to Be Seen, Sometimes Genuinely Usable

A conjured environment falls into two kinds: some are produced purely for an audience to see, with no real substance behind their convincing appearance, while others — food that genuinely nourishes, treasure that can genuinely be sold, a carriage that genuinely carries — are, for all practical purposes, indistinguishable from the real thing.

Chinese Source

又佛菩薩或作化事，唯令眾生覩見而已，如幻所作不堪受用。或復化作飲食、衣服、末尼、真珠、琉璃、螺貝、璧玉、珊瑚、車乘等事，與實無異。如是所作財食眾具，令諸眾生常得受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas and bodhisattvas sometimes conjure a display meant only for beings to look upon, illusion-like and incapable of genuine use. At other times they conjure food, clothing, gemstones, pearls, lapis lazuli, coral, or carriages that are no different from the genuine article — such conjured wealth, food, and provisions can be put to lasting, ordinary use by any being who receives them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts this with an ordinary stage magician's illusion — a conjured loaf of bread that vanishes the instant someone tries to actually pick it up and eat it — and stresses that a buddha or bodhisattva's genuine conjuring, resting on the same mastery of mind-only reality already established throughout this chapter, can instead produce provisions with all the same lasting function as anything karmically produced, precisely because mind's own conviction is what determined the object's function to begin with, not some independent physical process that a mere illusion could never actually replicate. This closes the distinction between conjured bodies and conjured realms; conjured speech, the third and final category, opens next with seven distinct varieties.

27. Milestone: Conjured Speech's Eight Qualities of Perfection — And Three Disciples Each Criticized for How They Taught

Conjured speech divides into seven varieties — wondrous in tone, vast in reach, spoken through oneself or another or from nowhere at all, teaching the true Dharma or admonishing negligence — and the treatise then names eight qualities that make such speech genuinely perfect, illustrated with a memorable story: three of the Buddha's own senior disciples, teaching in three completely different styles, were each criticized in turn by the very same audience.

Chinese Source

妙音相應者，謂佛菩薩所說化語，其聲深遠如雲雷音，其聲和雅如頻迦音，能感眾心，甚可愛樂。又此化語，圓上、微妙、顯了、易解、樂聞、無逆、無依、無盡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wondrous in tone: conjured speech whose sound is as deep and far-reaching as thunder in the clouds, yet as gentle and refined as the call of the kalaviṅka bird, moving the hearts of the assembly with genuine delight. This conjured speech is, moreover, supreme, wondrous, clear, easily understood, delightful to hear, never contrary to the listener, free of any self-interested motive, and inexhaustible.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi briefly names the seven basic varieties of conjured speech — wondrous-toned, vast-reaching, spoken as one's own words, spoken through another's conjured form, spoken from an empty place with no speaker at all, devoted to expounding the true teaching, and devoted to admonishing negligence — before dwelling on the eight qualities that make such speech genuinely perfect. Supreme means the speech always ultimately points toward complete liberation as its highest aim; wondrous means its very tone is as pleasing as birdsong; clear means its wording is simple enough for anyone — educated or not, young or old — to follow; easily understood means complex teachings are broken down skillfully enough that insight comes readily; delightful to hear means listeners genuinely want to keep listening, since real content, not mere eloquence, is what makes teaching worth returning to; never contrary means the speaker gauges exactly how much a listener can absorb, saying neither too much nor too little; free of self-interest means the teaching seeks nothing in return — no follower's loyalty, no personal following, no returned favor; and inexhaustible means the teaching's own wisdom never runs dry no matter how long it continues.

He illustrates "never contrary" with a well-loved story about how impossibly demanding an audience can be: three seekers, unsatisfied after visiting Śāriputra, complained he explained things far too briefly to actually understand; visiting Ānanda next, they complained he explained at such length it became tedious; visiting Mahākāśyapa last, and receiving only silence, they complained he was cold and arrogant. Brought before the Buddha with this litany of complaints — one disciple too terse, one too talkative, one too aloof — the Buddha's own reply was simply that teaching well is difficult precisely because no single approach satisfies everyone: a listener already inclined to find fault will find fault regardless of what is actually said, while a listener already favorably disposed will find a way to excuse whatever isn't said perfectly. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the reassurance out directly: only a fully awakened teacher ever manages true "never-contrary" speech in every single case — any teacher who falls short of that standard is in exactly the same company as Śāriputra, Ānanda, and Mahākāśyapa, three of the Buddha's own most accomplished disciples, and needn't take an audience's dissatisfaction as proof of genuine failure. This closes the eight qualities in detail; the treatise now turns to the vast reach a single conjured voice can achieve.

28. One Voice, Countless Worlds, Each Listener Understanding in Their Own Way

Vast-reaching conjured speech extends outward without limit — a single voice audible in full clarity to every kind of being within a great distance, and further still across entire world-systems without number — and within that single sound, each listener hears exactly the specific teaching that benefits them, in whatever manner of speech makes it comprehensible.

Chinese Source

廣音具足者，謂佛菩薩所說化語，其聲廣大，隨其所樂……以妙圓音隨類遍告。……於此聲中出種種音，為諸眾生說種種法，隨其所應，各得義利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Vast in reach: conjured speech whose sound is vast, extending as far as wished ... proclaiming the teaching to every kind of being through its wondrous, perfect tone. ... Within this single sound, various specific teachings emerge, expounding various specific dharmas to various beings, each according to what benefits them, each attaining genuine benefit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the classical formula behind this teaching: "the Buddha expounds the Dharma in a single voice, and beings understand each according to their own kind, each believing the Tathāgata speaks their own language alone, addressing them personally" — a Chinese speaker hearing Chinese, a speaker of any other tongue hearing their own, all from the identical utterance. He treats this as the natural extension of everything already established: since every listener's experience of sound, like every other object of perception, is mind-only rather than a single fixed acoustic signal delivered identically to everyone, one conjured voice can appear, without contradiction, as countless specific teachings to countless specific listeners simultaneously — the same principle behind the "one water, four minds" teaching, now applied to the ear rather than the eye. This closes the account of conjured speech's own scope and quality; the remaining varieties (spoken as one's own words, through another's form, or from an unattached source, and devoted respectively to teaching or to admonishing negligence) follow only briefly, since their basic sense was already given at this section's opening, before the treatise draws all three kinds of conjuring — body, realm, and speech — to a close.

29. Milestone: Miraculous Ability Closes; Past-Life Recall Opens With Eight Recalled Details

With both transmuting and conjuring now fully described, miraculous ability's own two functions are named — drawing beings into the Buddhist teaching, and directly relieving suffering beings' hardship — closing the first of the six superknowledges. The second, recalling past lives, then opens with eight specific details recoverable from any past existence, extendable to other beings and even passed on so they can recall their own.

Chinese Source

如是二種諸佛菩薩神境智通能辦二事。一者、示現種種神通，引諸眾生入佛聖教。二者、示現種種神通，惠施無量受苦眾生眾多品類利益安樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

These two kinds of miraculous ability accomplish two things for buddhas and bodhisattvas. First, displaying various miraculous powers draws beings into the Buddha's own teaching. Second, displaying various miraculous powers bestows many kinds of benefit and ease upon immeasurable suffering beings.

Chinese Source

謂佛菩薩以宿住智，自能隨念己之宿住，曾於如是有情類中，我如是名，如經廣說。……亦能隨念他諸有情身等一切品類差別，如自隨念己事無異。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas and bodhisattvas, through the wisdom of recalling past lives, are able to recall their own past existences — that among such and such a kind of being, they bore such and such a name — exactly as elaborated in the sūtras. ... They are likewise able to recall the same range of details for other beings, no differently than recalling their own affairs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the classical eight details recoverable from any recalled past life: one's name at the time, the type of birth (womb, egg, moisture, or transformation), one's caste or social standing, the food habitually eaten, the balance of pleasure and pain experienced, and three graded measures of lifespan — living past fifty, past eighty, or to the very outer limit of what that life could reach. He offers an everyday illustration of the food detail: a monk who spent his prior life in northern China will likely favor noodles this time around, one from the south will favor rice, one from Sichuan will crave chilies, and a Westerner reborn will gravitate toward bread and coffee — small preferences quietly tracing all the way back to a previous existence.

He explains that this power extends in three further ways: a buddha or bodhisattva can grant this same recollective capacity to another being directly — Amitābha's own vow promises that all reborn in his Pure Land possess this power in full; a being who has received it can, in turn, pass it on to others, extending in an unbroken chain; and it reaches with equal clarity into the smallest details — every subtle word, deed, or fleeting thought a being has ever had, something an ordinary person often cannot even recall from earlier that same day, remains completely available across every scale of time, from the shortest uninterrupted instant to spans of countless, uncountable eons. This closes the basic scope of past-life recall; the treatise turns next to why the Buddha actually exercises this power — not merely as a display, but to inspire genuine faith and to correct a specific, persistent wrong view.

30. Milestone: Why the Buddha Recalls Past Lives — The Nine-Colored Deer and the Self-Sacrificing Rabbit

The Buddha exercises past-life recall for a specific purpose: recounting his own former lives' most difficult, most extraordinary bodhisattva conduct, so that hearing of it inspires genuine faith, deepens reverence, and cultivates real weariness with the endless cycle of birth and death — illustrated with two of the best-loved jātaka tales, the nine-colored deer and the self-sacrificing rabbit.

Chinese Source

又由隨念宿住智故，憶念本生，為諸有情開示先世種種品類第一希有菩薩所行難行苦行。令於佛所生淨信故，起恭敬故，令於生死深厭離故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, by means of the wisdom of recalling past lives, they recollect their own former births, revealing to beings the most extraordinary and difficult bodhisattva conduct undertaken in various past existences — so that pure faith arises toward the Buddha, so that reverence arises, and so that deep weariness with birth-and-death arises.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi retells two of the Buddha's own most cherished former-life accounts. In one, the Buddha was once a deer with fur of nine brilliant colors and horns as white as snow, living peacefully beside a river. Seeing a drowning man swept along the current, the deer rescued him, carrying him to safety on its own back — asking, as its only reward, that the man never reveal where the deer lived, since hunters would certainly kill it for such a rare and valuable hide. Sometime later, the country's queen dreamed of this very deer and grew so fixated on possessing its hide and horns that she feigned illness until the king offered a great reward for it — at which point the rescued man, driven by greed, broke his promise and betrayed the deer's location. When the king's men arrived, the deer calmly recounted before the king exactly how it had once saved this very informant's life, and the king, moved and ashamed on the deer's behalf, punished the traitor and placed the deer under his own protection instead.

In the other tale, during a severe famine, a hermit sustaining his own spiritual practice found himself with nothing left to eat; a rabbit living nearby, wishing to support his practice, leapt of its own accord into the hermit's fire so that its body might become his meal, asking nothing in return. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that stories like these — the Buddha's own difficult, self-sacrificing conduct stretching across countless past lives — are precisely what "recollection of past births" (本生) means in the treatise's own terms, and explains why the Buddha shares them: hearing what genuine bodhisattva conduct actually costs, sustained patiently across lifetime after lifetime long before buddhahood was ever reached, is what turns an abstract admiration for the Buddha into the kind of faith, reverence, and real disillusionment with ordinary existence capable of actually motivating practice. This closes the account of "recollection of past births"; the treatise turns next to a second reason for exercising this same power — correcting a specific and persistent wrong view about permanence.

31. Recalling Past Lives Refutes the Wrong View of Permanence

A second reason the Buddha exercises this power is corrective: recounting the specific karmic causes behind present circumstances — why one disciple has an exceptional memory, why another must chew and re-swallow every meal — directly dismantles the various eternalist views that mistake some portion of experience for a fixed, unchanging self or world.

Chinese Source

又由此智憶念本事，為諸眾生開示種種先世相應業果異熟，為令妄計前際常論、一分常論常見眾生破常見故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, by means of this wisdom, they recollect past events, revealing to beings the various ripened karmic fruits corresponding to former lives — in order to break the wrong view of those who mistakenly hold to eternalism regarding the past, whether wholesale or partial eternalism.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi gives two concrete examples of this corrective use: Ānanda's own famously perfect memory traced back to a past life in which, as a wealthy patron, he supported a young novice's uninterrupted study of the scriptures — the novice being the Buddha's own earlier incarnation — showing that present ability is simply former generosity ripening; and a monk nicknamed "the cud-chewer," who had to bring up and re-chew every meal, whose affliction traced back to harsh speech in a past life. Recounting causes like these dismantles two families of eternalist error the treatise names: wholesale eternalism, held by meditators whose deep concentration lets them recall the universe's own cycles of formation and dissolution across twenty, forty, or eighty eons (or, without any meditation at all, simply asserted by pure philosophical conviction) and who mistake this vast but still bounded recall for proof of a truly permanent self and world; and partial eternalism, held by those who, recalling a past life spent as one of Brahmā's own attendant gods, conclude that Brahmā himself must be eternal while ordinary beings like themselves are merely impermanent — or, in a different version, that the four material elements persist permanently while only the mind itself is impermanent, since a corpse's elements simply return to earth while consciousness alone seems to move on.

He notes that arguments like these can sound superficially plausible if examined uncritically, which is exactly why the Buddha, rather than merely asserting they are wrong, uses direct recollection of specific past lives to show concretely that what looks permanent is simply a longer-than-usual causal chain — samsara is nothing more than karmic results following one another in succession, with no fixed, unchanging substance underlying any of it, however far back the recollection reaches. This closes the account of past-life recall (隨念宿住智通) in full, the second of the six superknowledges; the divine ear (天耳智通), the third, opens next.

32. Milestone: The Divine Ear — Twelve Kinds of Sound, and the Story of Two Rival Masters

The divine ear hears every conceivable kind of sound without obstruction — heavenly and human voices, noble and ordinary speech, loud and whisper-quiet sounds, easily understood and utterly foreign ones, conjured and genuine voices, distant and near — illustrated with a famous, humbling story about two great Tang-dynasty monks, one renowned for iron discipline and one for brilliant scholarship.

Chinese Source

謂佛菩薩以淨天耳，能於種種天聲、人聲、聖聲、非聖聲、大聲、小聲、辯聲、非辯聲、化聲、非化聲、遠聲、近聲皆悉得聞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas and bodhisattvas, through the purified divine ear, are able to hear every kind of sound: heavenly voices and human voices, noble speech and ordinary speech, loud sounds and soft sounds, easily comprehensible speech and difficult speech, conjured sounds and genuine sounds, and sounds distant and near.

Chinese Source

聞小聲者，下至耳語諸微細聲皆悉能聞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hearing soft sounds: down to the faintest whisper or the most subtle sound, all of it can be heard.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi runs quickly through the twelve categories: without effort, a buddha hears every voice from the desire realm up through the highest form-realm heaven; with deliberate intention, this reach extends to countless further world-systems; noble speech covers every teaching, admonition, and encouragement spoken by any buddha, hearer, solitary buddha, or bodhisattva anywhere; ordinary speech covers every lie, slander, harsh word, or idle talk any unenlightened being ever utters; sounds are heard at every scale from an entire crowd's roar down to the faintest private whisper; meaning is grasped whether the speech is immediately clear or expressed in some obscure foreign incantation, birdsong, or animal cry no ordinary listener could parse at all; and sound is heard whether genuinely spoken or conjured by another's own miraculous power, whether close at hand or immeasurably distant.

He illustrates the reach of this superknowledge — or something approaching it — with a well-loved story about two towering Tang-dynasty monks. Dàoxuān, founder of the Vinaya school, kept such rigorous discipline in his mountain retreat that a heavenly prince was said to deliver him a bowl of food by hand each day at noon. When Kuījī, the brilliant founder of the Faxiang school and a student of Xuánzàng, came to visit, Dàoxuān was secretly pleased, hoping the sight of such devoted heavenly support might humble his famously undisciplined but dazzlingly eloquent guest. Noon came and went with no food delivered at all. The next day, after Kuījī had left, the heavenly visitor returned as usual, and Dàoxuān asked why he had failed to come the previous day. "A great bodhisattva was on the mountain," the deity replied, "and the entire mountainside was filled with protective spirits — I simply couldn't get through." Dàoxuān broke into a cold sweat of shame, realizing he had badly underestimated his guest.

The same visit is said to include a second episode: sharing a room that night, Dàoxuān heard a passing carriage rumble by and noticed Kuījī sleeping soundly through it. The next morning, mentioning the noise and assuming his guest must have slept through it entirely, Dàoxuān was startled by Kuījī's reply: "Two bedbugs were quarreling all night — one shoved the other off a ledge and broke its leg, and the racket kept me awake the whole time. Did you not hear it?" Master Cháng Bǎi offers the story simply as a delightful, well-attested case of just how far genuine hearing can reach in someone of real attainment — whatever exact level Kuījī had reached, the tale has entertained and humbled listeners for over a thousand years. This closes the divine ear (天耳智通), the third of the six superknowledges; seeing death and rebirth (見死生智通), the fourth, opens next.

33. Seeing Death and Rebirth — The Divine Eye's View of the Intermediate State

The fourth superknowledge, seeing death and rebirth, watches a being's transition through its most intimate detail: the moment of dying, the intermediate state between death and the next birth — appearing dark and murky for those bound toward a lower rebirth, or bright and clear for those bound upward — and the gradual unfolding of that new life from infancy through full maturity.

Chinese Source

謂佛菩薩以超過人清淨天眼，見諸有情死時、生時、妙色、惡色、若劣、若勝，及於後際生已增長，諸根成熟，身諸所作善、惡、無記差別而轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas and bodhisattvas, through the purified divine eye surpassing that of ordinary humans, see beings at the moment of death and at the moment of rebirth — whether their appearance is radiant or murky, inferior or excellent — and, in what follows, see them growing after birth, their faculties maturing, their bodily, verbal, and mental actions unfolding as wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the divine eye as the fruit of the fourth dhyāna's own purest concentration — categorically different from an ordinary human eye, and even surpassing the innate, weaker version of it some desire-realm gods are simply born possessing. "Death" names the moment of dying itself; "rebirth" names the intermediate existence a being takes on between one life and the next — appearing, for those bound toward the lower realms, in a murky, dark form resembling a black sheep's dull coat under a moonless night sky, and for those bound toward the human or heavenly realms, in a bright, clear form resembling a clear night or the famously pure white cloth once woven near the Indian city of Vārāṇasī. A being's darker or brighter intermediate-state appearance is what "inferior" or "excellent" refers to here, corresponding directly to a lower or higher coming rebirth.

He explains the second half of the passage as a plain, sequential description of an ordinary human life once that new birth actually begins: infancy giving way to the growth of childhood, and childhood giving way, through youth and middle age, to full maturity — the same natural unfolding the treatise elsewhere associates with reaching the milestone of fifty years, considered in that era already a notable "long life." Every wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral action performed at every stage of this unfolding life remains fully visible to the one exercising this superknowledge, precisely because it watches not only the fact of death and rebirth but the entire causal thread connecting one to the other. This closes the opening description of seeing death and rebirth; the treatise turns next to the deeper purpose this superknowledge actually serves.

34. Seeing the Physical World in Full Detail, and the Divine Eye and Ear's Shared Purpose

The divine eye sees the physical world's own subtlest textures — natural luminosity, fine particulate forms, miraculously conjured appearances, and the refined purity of the higher heavens — reaching without effort from the deepest hell to the highest heaven, and, when deliberately extended, taking in countless world-systems and distant buddha-assemblies all at once, each perfectly distinct rather than blurred together.

Chinese Source

又現見知諸光明色、諸微細色、諸變化色、諸淨妙色，下至無間，上至色究竟宮，不由作意皆能見知。若作意時，能見上下無量無數餘世界色，亦能見傍無量無數諸世界中一切諸色，乃至能見彼彼佛土彼彼如來安坐彼彼異類大會，宣說正法，顯然無亂。

Ru-Yi Translation

They also directly perceive luminous forms, subtle particulate forms, miraculously transformed forms, and the pure, wondrous forms of the higher heavens, from the deepest hell up to the very highest heaven, without needing to make any effort of attention. When attention is deliberately directed, they can see the forms of immeasurable, uncountable other worlds above and below, and every form throughout immeasurable, uncountable worlds in every direction, even perceiving various buddha-lands where various Tathāgatas sit amid various distinct assemblies, expounding the true teaching with perfect clarity and no confusion whatsoever.

Chinese Source

又佛菩薩以淨天眼，普見十方無量無數諸有情類身之所作淨不淨業……以淨天耳，普聞十方無量無數諸有情類語之所作淨不淨業。既聞彼已，隨其所應、隨其所宜，施作種種利益安樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas and bodhisattvas, through the purified divine eye, universally see the pure and impure bodily deeds of beings throughout the ten directions ... and through the purified divine ear, universally hear the pure and impure verbal deeds of beings throughout the ten directions. Having seen and heard this, they bestow whatever benefit and ease is fitting and appropriate to each.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the fine particulate forms named here from genuine atomic particles, which only wisdom's own analytical eye can resolve — the divine eye sees a form's outer surface, top, bottom, and sides, light and darkness, but not what is only ever discerned through conceptual analysis. He compares seeing countless world-systems and buddha-assemblies simultaneously, each perfectly distinct, to a kaleidoscope or many television channels displaying at once without ever blurring into one another. He notes that seeing and hearing here serve a single unified purpose, plainly stated: perceiving exactly what pure or impure deed a being has performed becomes the basis for bestowing precisely the benefit that being actually needs — not indiscriminate blessing, but a response fitted exactly to what has been seen. This closes both the divine eye (見死生智通) and, jointly, the divine ear's shared function; mind-reading (知心差別智通), the fifth superknowledge, opens next.

35. Milestone: Mind-Reading — Every State a Mind Can Be In, and a Debate on How "Other-Minds" Knowledge Is Even Possible

The fifth superknowledge reads every state a mind could possibly be in — afflicted or free of affliction, genuinely liberated or merely carrying dormant seeds, wrongly or rightly oriented, inferior or refined, joyful, pained, or neutral — for one being or countless beings at once, in a single instant. A scholarly puzzle then follows: if every object is mind-only, how can "reading another's mind" mean anything beyond reading one's own?

Chinese Source

謂佛菩薩以他心智，遍知十方無量無數諸世界中他有情類，若有纏煩惱心，若離纏煩惱心，若有隨縛、有隨眠煩惱心，若離隨縛、離隨眠煩惱心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas and bodhisattvas, through the wisdom of knowing others' minds, universally know, throughout the ten directions' immeasurable, uncountable worlds, whether another being's mind is presently gripped by affliction or free of it in the moment, and whether it still carries the underlying bonds and latent seeds of affliction or is genuinely free of them.

Chinese Source

又能以一知他心智，於一有情如是所有、如是體性、如是品類、如是行相、如是分齊心起現前，於一念頃并如實知。……又佛菩薩此他心通，知諸有情諸根勝劣，知諸有情種種勝解，知諸有情種種界行，隨其所應，能正安處趣涅槃宮種種正行。

Ru-Yi Translation

With a single act of knowing others' minds, they truly know, for a single being or for countless beings alike, the exact affliction present, its wholesome or unwholesome character, its grade of intensity, how it arose with respect to its object, and whether it belongs to an unenlightened or noble mind — all within a single instant. ... Through this same knowledge of others' minds, buddhas and bodhisattvas know beings' faculties, strong or weak, their manifold degrees of understanding, and their manifold underlying dispositions, and accordingly are able to correctly guide each toward whatever practice actually leads them to nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first walks through the mental states catalogued here: affliction presently active versus merely dormant as an underlying seed; a defiled, wrongly-oriented mind (an outsider's teaching mistaking deep meditative absorption itself for liberation) versus a genuinely purified mind aimed correctly at true awakening; the graded quality of mind across the three realms (desire-realm minds, coarsest, shared even with animals; form-realm minds, subtler, still carrying dormant seeds; formless-realm minds, most refined of the three yet still short of genuine liberation); and the three basic feelings of pleasure, pain, and neutrality that color any of these states.

He then raises a genuine scholarly puzzle the *Discourse Records of the Yogācārabhūmi* records: if mind-only means there is no object outside mind, how does "knowing another's mind" differ from simply knowing an image within one's own? Two classical answers are recorded. The first holds that everyone's cognition of another's mind — ordinary being, hearer, or bodhisattva alike — works through an internal image merely resembling its target, more or less faithfully depending on how much residual discrimination distorts it, while a buddha's own image-based cognition matches its target so exactly and completely that it may properly be called genuine, direct knowledge rather than mere resemblance. The second holds that any being still short of buddhahood sees only an imperfect image because discrimination has not yet been fully exhausted, while a buddha, having exhausted discrimination entirely, perceives another's mind directly rather than through any mediating image at all — the same distinction, he notes, between an ordinary consciousness that only ever grasps a mediated image of its object and the storehouse consciousness's own direct apprehension of its object's actual basis.

He adds a comparative note on scope, drawn from the same source: an ordinary person can know only the minds of beings in their own immediate world; the great Brahmā kings know minds across an entire small-thousand world-system; hearers know across two-thousand-fold systems; solitary buddhas across three-thousand-fold systems; while buddhas and bodhisattvas know without any such limit at all, reaching immeasurable, uncountable worlds — a scale distinguishing them not merely in intensity but in kind. This closes mind-reading, the fifth superknowledge; the extinction of contaminants, the sixth and final one, opens next with one of the chapter's most distinctive Mahāyāna teachings.

36. Milestone: Why a Bodhisattva Knows How to End All Affliction, Yet Deliberately Does Not

The sixth superknowledge, knowing the extinction of contaminants, reads precisely who has genuinely exhausted affliction and who merely believes they have — and then delivers one of Mahāyāna's most distinctive teachings: a bodhisattva, though fully capable of exhausting every affliction completely, deliberately declines to do so, retaining just enough residual affliction to keep returning to ordinary existence and its beings, a restraint the treatise calls the single most excellent power of them all.

Chinese Source

謂佛菩薩如實了知煩惱盡得，如實了知若自、若他於諸漏盡已得未得，如實了知若自、若他所有能得漏盡方便……如實知他於漏盡得有增上慢，如實知他於漏盡得離增上慢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas and bodhisattvas truly know the attainment of extinguishing contaminants; they truly know, for themselves or others, whether the extinction of contaminants has been attained or not yet attained; they truly know, for themselves or others, the actual means for attaining it ... they truly know when another holds a presumptuous overestimation regarding their own attainment of this extinction, and truly know when another is genuinely free of such overestimation.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，雖能如實了知一切漏盡功德能證方便，而不作證。是故菩薩於有漏事及與諸漏不速捨離，雖行種種有漏事中，而不染汙。如是威力，於諸威力最為殊勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, bodhisattvas, although able to truly know every virtue of extinguishing contaminants and the very means to realize it, do not actually realize it. For this reason, bodhisattvas do not swiftly abandon conditioned, contaminated existence and its contaminants — and though they move through the very midst of contaminated affairs, they remain undefiled by them. This power, among all the powers described, is the most excellent of all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "presumptuous overestimation" as mistaking a lesser attainment for a greater one — believing a preliminary meditative absorption is already the first fruit of the path, or believing one has entered that absorption at all when one has not — a common trap the Buddha's own knowledge of others' minds can identify and correct with precision. He then turns to the passage's real weight: a bodhisattva capable of exhausting every affliction completely — reaching the same "forbearance regarding the birthless nature of phenomena" already described in earlier fascicles — chooses restraint instead, retaining just enough residual affliction (traditionally described as "retaining a trace of affliction to moisten future rebirth") to keep taking birth among ordinary beings rather than passing permanently beyond contact with them, the way a hearer eager for personal liberation typically does. Crucially, this retained trace never actually defiles the bodhisattva's own mind — they move through contaminated, ordinary existence untouched by its craving, hatred, or confusion, exactly as already established in the earlier account of entering phenomena's selflessness.

He connects this restraint to the classical distinction between the "easy path" and the "difficult path": the easy path, as Nāgārjuna's *Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā* describes it, entrusts a practitioner to a buddha's own sustaining power — reciting the names of Amitābha and other buddhas and bodhisattvas to reach a pure land where practice unfolds free of any opportunity for affliction to arise at all; the difficult path, as Tánluán's own commentary on Vasubandhu's rebirth treatise describes it, means remaining in this degenerate world without a buddha's direct presence, surrounded by rival teachings, tempted toward a hearer's premature self-benefit, exposed to genuinely harmful company, and relying on one's own strength alone rather than another's support — a path the treatise's own account of the bodhisattva grounds associates with choosing to be reborn repeatedly as a ruler precisely because that position reaches the greatest number of beings. Neither path is superior in principle, Master Cháng Bǎi notes; both are simply different routes suited to different practitioners' own circumstances, and this restraint from swift, total liberation — retained specifically in order to remain useful to others — is why the treatise names it the most excellent of all the powers described in this chapter.

37. All Six Superknowledges Close; the Power of the Dharma Reopens With Ten Perfections

With the extinction of contaminants explained, all six superknowledges — and with them, the power of holiness in its entirety — draw to a close, each ultimately resting on wisdom itself. The power of the Dharma then reopens with a fuller account: not six perfections alone, but ten, the final four serving as direct supports strengthening specific members of the original six.

Chinese Source

又佛菩薩由漏盡智，自無染汙；亦善為他廣分別說，壞增上慢。當知是名此所作業。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, through this wisdom of extinguishing contaminants, buddhas and bodhisattvas remain themselves undefiled, and are also able to skillfully and thoroughly explain this to others, dismantling any presumptuous overestimation. This should be understood as its function.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that every one of the six superknowledges just detailed — miraculous ability, past-life recall, the divine ear, seeing death and rebirth, mind-reading, and the extinction of contaminants — ultimately rests on wisdom as its single underlying basis, which is precisely why this entire, exhaustive chapter is classified under wisdom's own power rather than treated as some separate, wisdom-independent ability. This closes the power of holiness (聖威力) in its complete entirety.

He then turns to the *Avataṃsaka-sūtra*'s own fuller account of the power of the Dharma (法威力): where the treatise's basic classification names six perfections, ground-by-ground bodhisattva practice actually cultivates ten, the final four serving as direct reinforcements of specific members among the first six — skillful means supporting giving, moral discipline, and patience together; the vow perfection supporting diligence; the power perfection supporting concentration; and the wisdom perfection supporting wisdom's own culmination. This closes the brief transition back into the power of the Dharma; its fuller treatment follows next.

38. The Dharma's Power Has Four Faces for Every Perfection

Each of the six perfections carries the very same fourfold power: breaking the specific affliction it directly opposes, ripening the provisions toward buddhahood, benefiting oneself and others simultaneously, and yielding a specific, predictable fruit in future lives — a single template the treatise now applies to giving, moral discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom in turn.

Chinese Source

云何法威力？謂布施威力乃至般若威力。此布施等諸法威力，應知一一略有四相。一者、斷所對治相，二者、資糧成熟相，三者、饒益自他相，四者、與當來果相。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the power of the Dharma? It is the power of giving, up through the power of wisdom. It should be understood that each of these practices — giving and the rest — has, in brief, four aspects: first, breaking what it directly opposes; second, ripening the provisions toward awakening; third, benefiting oneself and others; and fourth, yielding a specific future fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the pattern about to unfold six times over: each perfection directly breaks one specific affliction that opposes it (giving breaks stinginess, discipline breaks transgression, patience breaks intolerance, diligence breaks laziness, concentration breaks scattered distraction, wisdom breaks ignorance); each ripens the provisions a bodhisattva needs toward buddhahood while simultaneously maturing other beings through one of the four means of attracting them (giving, kind speech, beneficial action, and cooperative engagement); each brings real, felt benefit to the practitioner's own present peace of mind as well as concrete relief to whoever receives it; and each ripens into a specific, appropriate future fruit — never an arbitrary reward, but the natural continuation of exactly the quality cultivated. He notes plainly why this matters practically: a bodhisattva returning to the world to help beings needs a genuinely excellent basis of their own to do so credibly — otherwise, as he puts it, they would simply be another drowning person trying to pull others out of the same water. The treatise now works through each perfection's own four aspects in turn, beginning with giving.

39. Giving's Four Powers — From Breaking Stinginess to a Future of Effortless Abundance

Giving directly dissolves stinginess, matures the practitioner's own path while drawing others toward it, brings genuine joy before, during, and after the act while relieving hunger, cold, sickness, want, and fear in the recipient, and ripens into a future life of natural abundance — high standing, great wealth, capable companions, and an accomplished circle of dependents.

Chinese Source

布施四相者，謂諸菩薩修行惠施，能斷慳吝，施所對治。……施先意悅，施時心淨，施已無悔，於三時中心常歡喜，以自饒益；亦能除他饑渴、寒熱、種種疾病、所欲匱乏、怖畏眾苦，以饒益他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Giving's four aspects: bodhisattvas who practice giving are able to break stinginess, the very thing giving directly opposes. ... Before giving, the mind delights; while giving, the mind is pure; after giving, there is no regret — across all three times, the mind remains constantly joyful, benefiting oneself; and it removes others' hunger, thirst, cold, heat, various illness, want, and fearful suffering, benefiting others.

Chinese Source

於當來世在在生處，恆常富樂，得大祿位、得大財寶、得大朋黨、得大眷屬。

Ru-Yi Translation

In whatever place they are reborn in future lives, they constantly enjoy wealth and ease, attaining high position, great riches, capable companions, and an accomplished circle of dependents.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the future-fruit aspect with an everyday observation: a physician who has generously given medicine in past lives finds their prescriptions working unusually well now, even when their technique is no better than a colleague's whose own remedies mysteriously fail to help — the difference traced to karmic affinity built through past generosity, not present skill alone. He notes the same principle explains why some people find whatever they need arriving almost the instant they mention wanting it, simply the natural ripening of habitual giving carried from earlier lives. This closes giving's four powers; moral discipline's own four powers follow next.

40. Moral Discipline's Four Powers — Sleeping Safely Under a Guardian's Watch

Moral discipline directly breaks the tendency toward transgression, matures the path while drawing others through shared practice, brings the practitioner genuine ease of both waking and sleeping life — free from fear, wrongdoing, and hostility, and protected by unseen guardians exceeding even a great king's own retinue — and ripens into rebirth among the heavens.

Chinese Source

持戒四相者，謂諸菩薩受持清淨身語律儀，能斷犯戒，戒所對治。……受持淨戒，捨離犯戒為緣所生怖畏、眾罪、怨敵等事，寢寤安樂，以自饒益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moral discipline's four aspects: bodhisattvas who uphold pure conduct of body and speech are able to break transgression, the very thing discipline directly opposes. ... Upholding pure discipline, they are freed from the fear, wrongdoing, and hostility that transgression would otherwise produce, resting easily whether waking or sleeping, benefiting themselves.

Chinese Source

由是因緣，身壞已後，生於善趣天世界中。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this cause, once the body has perished, they are reborn among the heavens, a favorable realm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the treatise's own vivid account of what genuine, harmless discipline actually earns: a bodhisattva who upholds it becomes protected by guardian spirits in a measure exceeding even a wheel-turning king's own retinue, so that no malevolent spirit, whether waking, sleeping, or even unconscious, can cause them harm. He offers a concrete example of discipline's power to draw others through shared conduct — a strict, exemplary monk whose own rigor is said to have quietly brought everyone living near him into the same disciplined practice, simply through sustained proximity and example, no explicit persuasion required. This closes moral discipline's four powers; patience's own four powers, illustrated with several of the tradition's most memorable stories, follow next.

41. Milestone: Patience's Four Powers — The Turtle Who Couldn't Stay Silent, and Rāhula's Two Lessons

Patience directly breaks intolerance, matures the path while drawing others into shared endurance, rescues both self and others from genuinely great fears, and ripens into a future free of enemies, betrayal, and grief — illustrated here with three unforgettable stories: a foolish turtle undone by its own inability to stay silent, and two formative lessons the Buddha's own son Rāhula learned about truthful speech and true patience.

Chinese Source

忍辱四相者，謂諸菩薩修行忍辱，能斷不忍，忍所對治。……由此忍辱，濟拔自他大怖畏事，饒益自他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Patience's four aspects: bodhisattvas who practice patience are able to break intolerance, the very thing patience directly opposes. ... Through this patience, they rescue both self and others from great fearful matters, benefiting both.

Chinese Source

由是因緣，能令菩薩於當來世無多怨敵、無多離隔、無多憂苦；於現法中，臨命終時心無憂悔，身壞已後生於善趣天世界中。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this cause, bodhisattvas come to have few enemies in future lives, few who would estrange them from friends, and little grief; in this very life, at the time of death their mind holds no regret, and once the body has perished they are reborn among the heavens, a favorable realm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens with the tradition's own contrast for what patience actually feels like: an ordinary person, insulted, is like a small bird struck by stones the size of raindrops — genuinely unable to bear it — while someone who has realized the forbearance of the birthless nature of phenomena is like a great elephant that a falling flower lands on without the elephant even noticing. He then tells of two geese who, seeing their close friend the turtle's pond drying up in a drought, offered to carry it by air to a new pond — the turtle biting down on the middle of a stick held at each end by a goose — on one strict condition: no matter what anyone below said, the turtle must not open its mouth to reply. Midflight, children on the ground below, delighted at the sight, shouted up "the geese are carrying a turtle!" — and the turtle, stung by what it took as mockery, opened its mouth to protest and fell to its death. The Buddha identified this turtle as a past life of his cousin Devadatta, whose habitual harsh speech carried forward, this time costing him his life through an identical inability to simply stay silent.

He then recounts two formative episodes involving Rāhula, the Buddha's own son, still a novice given to careless speech and exaggeration. In the first, the Buddha had him fetch a basin of water to wash his feet, then ordered it poured out, then kicked the empty basin rolling across the ground, asking whether Rāhula would drink from that same water now, or wash his face in that same kicked, dirtied basin — Rāhula, of course, refused both. The Buddha drew the lesson directly: careless, dishonest speech makes a person exactly like that discarded water and that kicked basin — no longer trusted or valued by others, however good their intentions, since undisciplined speech itself creates the very condition that invites such treatment. In the second episode, Rāhula, begging for alms, was struck on the head and had sand thrown into his begging bowl by a hostile passerby; rather than retaliating, he simply washed his wound and his bowl at a nearby river and returned to report the incident calmly. The Buddha praised his patience, then described at length the extraordinarily long and specific chain of suffering that awaited his attacker across many future lives — a warning less about vengeance than about how seriously the treatise takes harm done to a genuinely patient practitioner. From that point on, Rāhula became known as foremost in inconspicuous, careful practice, weighing his own speech with real caution ever after.

Master Cháng Bǎi closes with a shorter, gentler story illustrating that patience must extend even to one's own attachment, not only to others' hostility: a devout, diligent Buddhist husband died young, and might have been reborn in the heavens on the strength of his own practice — except that, unable to bear his grieving wife's tearful clinging as she wept "what will I do without you?", he was instead reborn as a small worm in her own nose. A visiting monk with a karmic connection to the family recognized what had happened, gently stopped her weeping, and spoke directly to the worm about its own wasted merit, at which point the worm — recognizing itself for what it now was — died of its own shame and was immediately reborn in the heavens after all. This closes patience's four powers, richly illustrated; diligence, concentration, and wisdom's own four powers follow next, completing all six perfections.

42. Ten Conditions for Genuine Patience, and Ten Blessings It Brings

Two scriptural lists round out the treatment of patience: ten inner conditions the *Ratnakūṭa-sūtra* names as what actually makes patience possible at all, and ten concrete blessings the *Candrapradīpa-samādhi-sūtra* promises to whoever sustains it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the ten conditions the *Ratnakūṭa* names as genuinely enabling patience: not clinging to a fixed self or what belongs to it; not dwelling on one's own caste or family standing, which easily breeds the pride that makes patience impossible; actively breaking down arrogance; never repaying wrong with wrong; contemplating impermanence, since both the one insulted and the one insulting are already different from who they were a moment ago; cultivating loving-kindness and compassion for the other person's own suffering; remaining diligent rather than negligent; releasing attachment to hunger, thirst, pain, and pleasure alike; actively working to dissolve anger through whatever method actually helps; and cultivating wisdom itself, since, as he puts it plainly, only someone with genuine insight can actually bear what needs bearing — patience without wisdom rarely lasts.

He then lists the ten blessings the *Candrapradīpa-samādhi-sūtra* promises a patient bodhisattva: immunity from death by fire, blade, poison, or drowning; protection by non-human spirits who favor patience's gentleness; a naturally dignified bodily appearance; the lower realms' door closed to them, since anger is precisely what opens that door; rebirth in the Brahmā heavens whenever wished; constant ease by day and by night, since holding no grudges leaves nothing to disturb sleep or waking life; and a body that never departs from genuine joy. This closes patience's four powers entirely, along with these two supporting lists; diligence's own four powers, illustrated with a memorable story about a bee and a sleepy monk, follow next.

43. Milestone: Diligence's Four Powers, and the Bee Who Kept a Sleepy Monk Awake

Diligence directly breaks laziness, matures the path toward buddhahood while inspiring others through simple example, brings the practitioner ever-deepening meditative joy while never harming others through careless conduct, and ripens into a future admiration for genuinely difficult, worthwhile achievement — illustrated with a charming story of the Buddha's own past life as a bee, buzzing and stinging a drowsy fellow monk (a past life of Maitreya) awake again and again until the lesson finally landed.

Chinese Source

精進四相者，謂諸菩薩住勤精進，能斷懈怠，精進所治。……勤精進故，得安樂住，不為一切惡不善法之所雜亂，後後所證轉勝於前，倍生歡喜，以自饒益；勤修善品，不以身語損惱於他，令他發生精進樂欲，以饒益他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Diligence's four aspects: bodhisattvas abiding in diligent effort are able to break laziness, the very thing diligence directly opposes. ... Through diligent effort, they attain a joyful abiding undisturbed by any unwholesome state, each attainment surpassing the one before it, redoubling their own joy, benefiting themselves; diligently cultivating wholesome practices, they never harm others through body or speech, and inspire in others the same joyful wish for diligence, benefiting others.

Chinese Source

由此因力，於當來世，愛樂殊勝士夫功業。是名第四。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the power of this cause, in future lives they come to genuinely admire the difficult, extraordinary achievements of truly excellent beings. This is the fourth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tells the story from the *Ṣaṭpāramitā-saṃgraha-sūtra*: under a past buddha, two monks practiced side by side — one, "Diligent Debater," a past life of Śākyamuni, quickly attained the path through sheer sustained effort; the other, "Joyful-in-Rest," a past life of Maitreya, meant well but simply could not stay awake, dozing off even seated at the edge of a lotus pond specifically to keep himself alert through fear of falling in. Seeing his friend's practice going nowhere, Diligent Debater transformed himself into a bee, buzzing insistently around Joyful-in-Rest's head to keep him from drifting off; each time the bee flew away to sip nectar, the monk fell straight back asleep, until the bee returned to sting him awake again. At one point the bee itself, distracted by the nectar, tumbled into the pond's mud — prompting the drowsy monk to scold it for its own greed, warning that the lotus flowers close at sunset and it would suffocate inside one if it wasn't careful. The bee turned the criticism neatly back on him: "You're no different from me, dozing off standing, sitting, walking — mired in the mud of birth-and-death. The Buddha is alive right now, like the sun still up — practice diligently while you still can, because once he is gone, who will guide you?" Struck by shame, the monk finally applied himself in earnest and went on to attain the same forbearance regarding the birthless nature of phenomena his friend already had. The Buddha, recounting this story of his own past life, added that had he not pushed his friend this way, Maitreya might still be cycling through birth-and-death even now.

Master Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson pointedly toward the present: if a practice as accessible as chanting Amitābha's name has been available for ten full eons and a practitioner still has not reached the Pure Land, despite countless past opportunities to have heard the very same teaching, laziness rather than difficulty is very often the real obstacle — the *Sūtra of Immeasurable Life* itself observes that the Pure Land stands ready and willing, yet remarkably few actually arrive. He also lists the *Ṣaṭpāramitā-sūtra*'s own four kinds of diligence said to fill a practitioner with genuine wisdom: diligence in extensive learning, diligence in sustained meditative retention, diligence in joyfully teaching what has been learned, and diligence in actual practice itself — calm-abiding and insight together. This closes diligence's four powers in full; concentration's own four powers, with the classic image of a turtle guarding its six limbs, follow next.

44. Milestone: Concentration's Four Powers, and the Turtle That Outwitted a Hungry Otter

Concentration directly breaks the coarser afflictions and the subtler hindrances that disturb absorption, matures the path while equipping the practitioner to attract others through miraculous ability, brings a joy of present ease that grows more refined and less attached at each successive depth while never harming any being, and ripens into rebirth among the pure heavens — illustrated with the classic image of a turtle who survives a hungry otter's attack simply by withdrawing every limb into its shell.

Chinese Source

靜慮四相者，謂諸菩薩入靜慮時，能斷煩惱、語言尋伺、喜、樂、色想等隨煩惱，靜慮所治。是名第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Concentration's four aspects: bodhisattvas, upon entering concentration, are able to break the afflictions, the discursive deliberation that produces speech, and the secondary hindrances of joy, pleasure, and fixation on form — all of which concentration directly opposes. This is the first.

Chinese Source

現法樂住以自饒益，其心寂靜、最極寂靜，遠離貪愛；於諸有情無損、無惱，以饒益他。……由此因緣，智得清淨，能發神通，於當來世生淨天處，得靜慮果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Abiding in present ease benefits themselves — the mind grows still, then utterly still, released from craving; and causing no harm or disturbance to any being benefits others. ... By this cause, wisdom becomes purified, miraculous ability is unlocked, and in future lives they are reborn among the pure heavens, the very fruit of concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own progression through the four dhyānas: the first breaks desire, hatred, and harmful intent; the second breaks the discursive deliberation that gives rise to inner speech; the third breaks attachment to joy's own restless excitement; and the fourth breaks even attachment to refined pleasure itself, settling into pure equanimity — each stage genuinely more stable than the one before, exactly as "each attainment surpassing the one before it" describes. He lists, more briefly, eleven classical obstacles the treatise elsewhere names as disturbing concentration — doubt about the method, keeping company with those uninterested in practice, physical restlessness from poorly guarded senses, excessive drowsiness, straining too hard, giving up too easily once strain sets in, becoming prematurely elated at the first sign of progress, panicking at frightening visions that are only the mind's own projections, wandering thoughts about ordinary sensory life, excessive talking, and a restless craving to look outward at the world once some inner brightness first appears — noting that this last one, wanting to "go for a walk" the moment practice starts feeling too still, is one Elder Miàojìng singled out as a particularly common, quietly self-sabotaging temptation.

He tells a well-loved story to illustrate the danger of leaving the senses unguarded: a hermit had meditated on a mountain for twelve years without ever attaining concentration. The Buddha, recognizing the right conditions had finally arrived, appeared disguised as a fellow ascetic and sat beside him. Before them, a turtle in a pond was suddenly threatened by a hungry otter; the turtle simply withdrew its head, tail, and four legs entirely into its shell, and the otter, unable to get past the hard casing, eventually gave up and left. When the hermit remarked with relief that the turtle had escaped, the Buddha-in-disguise offered the teaching directly: ordinary beings, reaching their six sense faculties out toward the six sense objects exactly the way the turtle's limbs once reached outward, gather in so much defiled information that discursive thought multiplies endlessly and concentration never takes hold — before reciting the verse: "withdraw the six like a turtle, guard the mind like a fortress wall; fight the demons with wisdom, and once victorious, no more suffering remains." This closes concentration's four powers, illustrated in full; wisdom's own four powers, completing all six perfections, follow next.

45. Wisdom's Four Powers Complete All Six Perfections, Closing the Dharma's Power in Full

Wisdom directly breaks ignorance itself, matures the path while drawing others through all four attracting methods at once, brings a clear, joyful comprehension of reality that flows outward as genuinely liberating teaching for others, and ripens into the ultimate future fruit of all six perfections combined: release from both the afflictive and cognitive obstructions. With wisdom's own four powers named, the power of the Dharma closes in its entirety.

Chinese Source

般若四相者，謂諸菩薩具足妙慧，能斷無明，慧所對治。……於所知事如義覺了，能引廣大清淨歡喜，以自饒益；普為有情稱理說法，令其獲得現法當來利益安樂，以饒益他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wisdom's four aspects: bodhisattvas endowed with wondrous wisdom are able to break ignorance, the very thing wisdom directly opposes. ... Comprehending the object of knowledge exactly as it truly is, they bring forth vast and pure joy, benefiting themselves; and teaching every being the Dharma in accordance with genuine principle, they grant them benefit and ease both now and in the future, benefiting others.

Chinese Source

由是因緣，攝諸善根，能正所作，於當來世能證二障離繫，謂煩惱障離繫及所知障離繫。是名第四。……是名法威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this cause, gathering every wholesome root and rightly accomplishing every task, they come in future lives to be released from both obstructions — release from the afflictive obstruction, and release from the cognitive obstruction. This is the fourth. ... This is called the power of the Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes wisdom's fourth power as the fitting capstone to the entire six-part sequence: where giving, discipline, patience, diligence, and concentration each ripen into their own specific worldly or heavenly fruit, wisdom alone ripens into the ultimate release the other five were always building toward — freedom from both the afflictive obstruction that binds a being to suffering and the cognitive obstruction that blocks complete omniscience. He notes wisdom is described here as the attracting cause behind faith, diligence, mindfulness, and concentration themselves, meaning the other four wholesome roots only mature into genuine spiritual faculties under wisdom's own guidance — without it, effort, faith, and even concentration can just as easily reinforce delusion as dissolve it. This closes the power of the Dharma (法威力) in its entirety, all six perfections detailed in full; innate power (俱生威力), the third of the five kinds of power, opens next with an extended account of the Buddha's own final human birth.

46. Milestone: Innate Power Opens — Bearing Immense Suffering Without a Second Thought, and Delighting in the Cost of Helping

Innate power begins with two qualities so deeply ingrained they require no deliberation at all: an unreflective, natural capacity to endure the most severe and prolonged suffering for as long as benefiting beings requires, and a genuine delight — not mere grim endurance — in whatever hardship actually helping others turns out to cost.

Chinese Source

云何諸佛菩薩俱生威力？謂性能憶念諸本生事，為欲利益諸有情故，不由思擇，於極長時種種猛利無間大苦悉能堪忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the innate power of buddhas and bodhisattvas? It is that, by nature, they recollect their own past births, and for the sake of benefiting sentient beings, without needing to deliberate at all, they are able to endure every kind of intense, uninterrupted, and immense suffering across an extremely long span of time.

Chinese Source

為欲利益諸有情故，欣樂領受能辦有情利益事苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

For the sake of benefiting sentient beings, they joyfully and gladly receive whatever suffering accomplishing genuine benefit for beings requires.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws a sharp contrast with ordinary experience: most beings resist even minor discomfort undertaken for their own sake, let alone hardship taken on purely to benefit someone else — yet a bodhisattva accepts exactly this kind of cost not through gritted-teeth willpower but through genuine, spontaneous delight, since eleven specific categories of beneficial activity toward beings (detailed in the treatise's own precepts chapter) are simply second nature by this stage of practice. He cites the example of a bodhisattva known as "Constantly Diligent," whose vow to work ceaselessly, day and night, across countless eons to rescue suffering beings never wavers despite having already reached the exalted rank of a tenth-ground bodhisattva — proof that this endurance is not reluctant sacrifice but settled character. This closes the first two facets of innate power; the third, the specific circumstances of dwelling in Tuṣita heaven before a final human birth, opens next with a striking excursion into cosmic time itself.

47. Milestone: Born in Tuṣita Heaven — Cosmic Lifespans, and Three Marks That Outshine Every Other God

A bodhisattva bound for buddhahood in their very next life is reborn into the fourth heaven, Tuṣita, and remains there for its full lifespan — a span the treatise's own cosmological arithmetic reveals as almost incomprehensibly vast — distinguished from every other god there by three specific marks: living out that heaven's complete lifespan without exception, an unmatched physical splendor, and a renown known throughout the entire realm.

Chinese Source

為欲利益諸有情故，上生第四覩史多天，盡覩史多壽量而住。有三勝事，映彼受生諸天子眾。一、天壽量，二、天形色，三、天名稱。

Ru-Yi Translation

For the sake of benefiting sentient beings, they ascend to the fourth heaven, Tuṣita, and dwell there for the entirety of that heaven's lifespan. Three excellent marks outshine every other god born there: first, the fullness of their heavenly lifespan; second, their heavenly form; and third, their heavenly renown.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the treatise's own careful reckoning of heavenly time, each level of the desire realm's gods experiencing time at a different scale entirely: for the Four Great Kings, lowest of the six heavens, fifty human years pass in a single day and night, and their own lifespan of five hundred such years comes to roughly nine thousand human years; for the Heaven of the Thirty-Three, ruled by Śakra, a hundred human years make one day, yielding a lifespan of some 3.6 million human years; for the Yāma heaven, two hundred human years make one day, yielding roughly 144 million human years; and for Tuṣita itself, four hundred human years make one single day and night, so that its full four-thousand-year lifespan comes to approximately 576 million human years — a span so vast that even Xuánzàng, who is said to have been reborn there over a thousand years ago, would by tradition have experienced only a few months of Tuṣita time since his passing.

He explains the three marks that set a future buddha apart from every other resident there: unlike ordinary gods, whose lives in any heaven can end early through misfortune, a bodhisattva destined for buddhahood always lives out the heaven's complete natural lifespan without exception; their physical form and stature exceed every other god's own considerable radiance; and their renown is known throughout the entire heaven, the way an unusually famous public figure is recognized by everyone rather than remaining quietly anonymous. He notes Maitreya, born in this same heaven roughly two and a half millennia ago by earthly reckoning, still has an immense span of Tuṣita time yet to complete before descending for his own final human birth. This closes the account of dwelling in Tuṣita; the treatise now turns to the elaborate details of a buddha's actual descent into human birth.

48. Milestone: A Buddha's Descent to the Human World — Fully Aware Through Birth Itself, Unlike Any Other Being

A bodhisattva's final human birth unfolds through a series of marks unlike any ordinary being's: light filling the world as the descent begins, complete awareness maintained through entering, dwelling in, and leaving the womb (a clarity the treatise says only a buddha achieves in full), walking seven steps unassisted at the very moment of birth to declare supreme awakening, and a body already marked with the thirty-two excellent signs and eighty minor marks of a great being, each limb said to carry the strength of Nārāyaṇa himself.

Chinese Source

將欲下生入母胎時，放大光明，普照世界。於降母胎入、住、出位，皆正了知。既出胎已，即於地上，不待扶侍而行七步，自稱德號。

Ru-Yi Translation

As they are about to descend and enter the mother's womb, they radiate great light, illuminating the entire world. Throughout entering, dwelling in, and leaving the womb, they remain fully and correctly aware. Once born, standing on the ground, without needing any support, they walk seven steps and proclaim their own excellent title.

Chinese Source

於初生時，有大威德天、龍、藥叉……散以種種天妙華香，持天伎樂……殊勝供具而為供養。又以無上三十二大丈夫相等，莊嚴其身。……一一支節，皆悉備足那羅延力。

Ru-Yi Translation

At the moment of birth, mighty gods, nāgas, and yakṣas ... scatter every kind of wondrous heavenly flower and incense, play heavenly music ... and make offerings of every excellent kind. They are further adorned with the unsurpassed thirty-two marks of a great being. ... Every single limb and joint is fully endowed with the strength of Nārāyaṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the treatise's own four-fold classification of how aware a being is while taking birth: a wheel-turning king, or one bound for stream-entry, is aware only while entering the womb, then loses awareness through the rest of gestation and birth; a solitary buddha remains aware through both entering and dwelling in the womb, but loses awareness at the moment of birth itself; a bodhisattva bound for buddhahood remains fully aware through all three stages without exception; and an ordinary being is aware of none of it at all, entering, dwelling, and emerging in complete confusion. He notes this is why the newborn Buddha alone is recorded walking seven steps unassisted and declaring, in the classical formulation, "in all the heavens and all below them, none is like the Buddha" — an ordinary infant could never do this, but one fully aware even at the moment of birth genuinely can.

He runs through the eightfold assembly of celestial beings said to attend this birth with offerings — gods, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kiṃnaras, and mahoragas, the same eight orders already met earlier in this fascicle, each devoted to the Buddha and frequently present at his later teaching assemblies as well. He notes the thirty-two marks and eighty accompanying signs of physical excellence, detailed individually elsewhere in the treatise, are the direct fruit of countless lifetimes of accumulated merit — adding, with some amusement, Elder Miàojìng's observation that traditional Chinese depictions of arhats as unusually strange or homely-looking sit oddly with the fact that genuine spiritual accomplishment, cultivated over many lives, should if anything produce an unusually dignified appearance, not the opposite. Finally, he explains "the strength of Nārāyaṇa" — a legendary being of immense physical power — as indicating that even the Buddha's ordinary human body, quite apart from any miraculous ability, already possesses physical strength verging on the inconceivable in every single limb. This closes the account of the Buddha's birth itself; the remaining marks of his final human life continue next.

51. Immeasurable Strength, Effortless Skill, Awakening Without a Teacher, and Brahmā's Own Request

Four further marks complete the picture of this final life: a body carrying strength beyond ordinary reckoning in every limb; childhood skill in every worldly craft with no training required; complete, unassisted awakening beneath the bodhi tree with no teacher's guidance; and the striking detail that the newly awakened Buddha initially had no wish to teach at all, until the very lord of this world-system, Great Brahmā, personally descended to beg him to do so out of compassion for suffering beings.

Chinese Source

於稚童時，不由習學，自然善巧，於諸世間工巧業處疾疾能入。無師自然獨處三千大千世界，證得無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

In childhood, without any training at all, they are naturally skillful, swiftly mastering every worldly craft. Without a teacher, dwelling alone in the great trichiliocosm, they attain unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Chinese Source

索訶界主大梵天王自然來下，殷勤勸請哀愍世間宣說正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Great Brahmā, lord of this Sahā world-system, comes down of his own accord, earnestly beseeching the Buddha, out of compassion for the world, to expound the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the *Abhidharmakośa*'s own vivid measure of the strength named in the previous section: imagine seven successive elephants, each ten times stronger than the last — an ordinary elephant, a "fragrant" elephant, and five further grades culminating in an elephant literally named Nārāyaṇa — with the Buddha's own bodily strength equal to that seventh, most powerful elephant. He draws a simple practical point from this: genuine spiritual accomplishment requires real strength too, not only good intention — a body and mind too depleted to even sit upright cannot sustain the sitting practice awakening itself requires.

He explains that this final life's childhood mastery of ordinary skills and its awakening beneath the bodhi tree both stem from the same source: three full incalculable eons of prior cultivation already completed, so that this last life simply brings it to visible fruition rather than starting the work from scratch — after briefly studying under teachers who had attained only the formless absorptions and finding these insufficient, the Buddha-to-be left them behind and reached genuine awakening entirely on his own. Master Cháng Bǎi lingers on the striking detail that immediately follows: rather than announcing this awakening at once, the newly enlightened Buddha initially considered the truth he had realized too profound and subtle for anyone to actually understand, and very nearly declined to teach at all — it took Great Brahmā's own descent and earnest plea, moved by compassion for a suffering world, to change his mind. This closes these four marks; the treatise turns next to the Buddha's own unshakeable meditative stability.

52. Milestone: Unshakeable Meditation — The Woman in Samādhi Only One Bodhisattva Could Wake

The Buddha's own concentration remains utterly undisturbed by anything external — not even violent thunder can break it — illustrated with a well-known story in which Mañjuśrī himself, despite every effort, cannot rouse a woman absorbed in deep samādhi beside the Buddha, until a far less renowned bodhisattva succeeds with a single snap of the fingers, for a reason that reveals something important about what actually creates a lasting debt of gratitude.

Chinese Source

其定寂靜，設大雲雷，曾無覺受，安然不起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Their concentration is so utterly still that, even should great thunderclouds roll in, they experience no disturbance whatsoever, remaining perfectly at peace.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tells of an occasion when Mañjuśrī Bodhisattva, visiting the Buddha, found a woman seated beside him in a profoundly deep meditative absorption, and, wishing to sit in that same place, tried every means to rouse her — snapping his fingers, taking her hand and shaking it, even causing the entire great trichiliocosm to tremble — all without success. Only when the Buddha summoned the bodhisattva Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin ("Remover of All Obstructions") up from beneath the earth, whose single snap of the fingers brought her out of absorption instantly, did the mystery resolve: when Mañjuśrī asked why his own considerable power had failed where this comparatively obscure bodhisattva succeeded so effortlessly, the Buddha explained that it was this very bodhisattva who had originally inspired her own resolve toward awakening in the first place. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson directly: whoever first moves another being to generate the aspiration for awakening becomes, from that moment on, the single greatest benefactor that being will ever have — a debt no other kindness in the world can equal or override, which is precisely why only that particular bodhisattva's touch could reach her. This closes the account of unshakeable meditative stability; the marks of the Buddha's ordinary daily life — resting, walking, and eating — follow next.

53. Perfect Stillness in Rest, Effortless Grace in Walking, and Food That Never Fails to Nourish

Even the most ordinary moments of the Buddha's daily existence carry distinctive marks: shade from a tree's own branches following wherever he sits without ever failing to cover him; six full years in which Māra, watching for any opening at all, could never find one; sleep taken in perfect stillness without the slightest shifting through the night; a walking gait that disturbs neither wind nor loose stone, smoothing the very ground beneath each step; and food that is thoroughly digested down to the last grain, none of it ever wasted.

Chinese Source

證菩提已，於六年中，魔求其便竟不能得。常俱行念每恆現前；由此念故，受想尋思生住滅等無不覺了。……菩薩如是若坐樹下，一切枝條并皆垂影，隨蔭其身，曾無虧捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having attained awakening, for six full years Māra sought any opening at all and never once found one. Constant mindfulness remains always present; through this mindfulness, every arising, abiding, and ceasing of feeling, perception, and discursive thought is fully known. ... Whenever the bodhisattva sits beneath a tree, every branch bends to cast its shade over his body, never once failing to shelter him.

Chinese Source

賢聖行住所攝俱生威力者，謂佛菩薩常右脅臥，如師子王……一切如來應正等覺，雖現睡眠而無轉側。大風卒起，不動身衣。行如師子，步若牛王。……食所食時，有粒皆碎，無口不殫。

Ru-Yi Translation

The innate power belonging to noble conduct in rest and motion: buddhas and bodhisattvas always sleep lying on their right side, like a lion king ... every Tathāgata, though appearing to sleep, never shifts position. Even a sudden strong wind does not stir their body or robes. Their walk is like a lion's, their stride like a bull-king's. ... When eating, every grain of food is thoroughly broken down; not a single mouthful goes undigested.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the tree-shade detail and the six years of Māra's failed search together as evidence of the same underlying reality: since the afflictive and cognitive obstructions have already been fully exhausted, there is simply no opening left anywhere in body, speech, or mind for either external harm or temptation to find purchase. He explains constant, unbroken mindfulness — extending to every single arising and passing thought, word, and bodily movement, without exception, across every activity and every span of time — as what the treatise elsewhere calls the Tathāgata's own "faculty of never forgetting," categorically different from an ordinary being's memory, which loses track of most of what actually crosses the mind in a single day.

He notes the significance of sleeping without ever shifting position through the night as itself a mark of a mind utterly without inner agitation — an ordinary person's restless tossing and rumpled bedding through the night are outward signs of an unsettled mind, while the Buddha's own stillness in sleep mirrors his waking stillness exactly. Walking without disturbing wind or ground, and doorways that widen of their own accord to admit him, express the same principle in motion: obstacles simply cease to be obstacles once genuine attainment removes whatever inner friction they would otherwise catch on. This closes the twelve marks of the Buddha's final human life in full, now grouped into two broader categories — benefit granted merely by the sight of him, and the noble bearing of his rest and motion just described; the account of his final passing follows next.

54. Milestone: The Marks of Final Nirvāṇa Close Innate Power in Full

Innate power's final mark accompanies the Buddha's own passing into final nirvāṇa: the earth itself trembling, stars streaming visibly across the sky before falling, an inexplicable heat sweeping every direction at once, and celestial music filling the whole of space — closing, with this, the entire account of innate power from birth to death.

Chinese Source

般涅槃時，大地震動，眾星晃耀，交流而隕，諸方一時欻然大熱，遍滿虛空奏天大樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

At the time of final nirvāṇa, the great earth quakes, the stars shine and stream across the sky before falling, every direction suddenly grows intensely hot all at once, and heavenly music resounds throughout the whole of space.

Chinese Source

如是無量甚希有事，皆是如來俱生威力，非是神通威力所作。如是名為諸佛菩薩俱生威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

All such immeasurable and truly extraordinary events as these are the Tathāgata's own innate power, not something produced by deliberately exercised miraculous ability. This is called the innate power of buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi underlines the crucial distinction the treatise draws in its own closing line: everything described across this entire, extensive account — Tuṣita's cosmic lifespan, the twelve marks of birth and life, and now these final signs at death — is innate power, the automatic ripening of countless past lifetimes' accumulated merit, categorically different from miraculous power, which requires deliberate mastery of concentration exercised in the moment. A buddha does not have to try to produce these signs; they simply occur, the way a tree bears its own fruit without deliberation, because the causes were already planted lifetimes ago. This closes innate power (俱生威力), the third of the five kinds of power, in its entirety — the treatise now turns to its final classification: exactly which powers buddhas and bodhisattvas share with hearers and solitary buddhas, and which remain entirely their own.
