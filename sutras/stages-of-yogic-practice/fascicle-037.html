Fascicle 37 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十七 · The Chapter on Power Opens

The Buddhas' and Bodhisattvas' Power, and Why Every Object Is Mind-Only

With the chapter on true meaning complete, the treatise opens 威力品, the chapter on power — the fourth of the seven fields of the factors of awakening — beginning with three kinds of power a bodhisattva possesses: the power of holiness resting on mastery of concentration, the power inherent in the Dharma's own teachings, and the power present from birth.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 37 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Chapter on Power Opens — Four Ways Buddhas Save Beings, and Elder Dàochuò's Seven Similes for a Single Name's Power

Fascicle 37 opens the chapter on power (威力品), the fourth of the seven fields structuring the factors of awakening, following directly after the chapter on true meaning just completed. Before defining a bodhisattva's own power directly, the text surveys four ways buddhas save beings — through teaching, through their radiant physical presence, through miraculous power, and through their very names — and lingers on the last of these with seven vivid similes explaining how reciting a single name can outweigh a lifetime of accumulated karma.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處威力品第五

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the Ground of the Bodhisattva, the first division on yogic practice: the fifth chapter, on power.

Chinese Source

云何諸佛菩薩威力？當知略有三種。一者、聖威力。謂佛菩薩得定自在，依定自在，隨其所欲一切事成。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the power of buddhas and bodhisattvas? It should be understood that, in brief, there are three kinds. The first is the power of holiness: buddhas and bodhisattvas attain mastery over concentration, and resting on this mastery, everything they wish for comes to pass.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by situating this new chapter within the seven fields already introduced back in fascicle 35 (self-benefit, benefiting others, true meaning, power, ripening beings, ripening one's own buddha-qualities, and unsurpassed awakening): having completed true meaning, the bodhisattva has fully transformed from ordinary being into noble one for their own benefit, and now turns outward, using post-realization wisdom to cultivate miraculous power in service of ripening other beings — which is precisely why power follows true meaning here. He frames this against the *Mahāsaṃnipāta-sūtra*'s own four-fold account of how buddhas rescue beings: through spoken teaching, dispelling ignorance and opening the eye of wisdom; through their physical radiance and marks, so that merely fixing one's attention on a buddha's form — even a mere painted image, as in the story of a wayward young Śākyamuni whose heart turned at the sight of one — plants the seed of eventual awakening; through miraculous power, this chapter's own topic; and through their names, where sincere recitation is said to dissolve karmic obstruction across countless past lives.

He recounts at length the seven similes the Tang-dynasty Pure Land teacher Dàochuò offered to answer a natural objection — how could reciting a single name possibly outweigh lifetimes of accumulated karmic wrongdoing? A mountain of firewood, stacked by a hundred people over a hundred years higher than the Himalayas, still burns to nothing from a single bean-sized flame, just as a name's merit consumes vast accumulated karma; a crippled man who can barely walk three steps unaided crosses a thousand miles in a day riding a favorable wind, just as a name carries a struggling practitioner swiftly toward the Pure Land; a poor man who unearths a thousand-year-old treasure and presents it to the king is instantly wealthy, just as an ordinary being's severe karmic burden is instantly outweighed once brought to a buddha's own field; a bedridden invalid unable even to mount a donkey rides effortlessly through the sky astride a wheel-turning king's flying wheel, just as a name carries the powerless along regardless of their own strength; a person bound many times over by rope is freed in one stroke by a child's sword, just as a name's power severs every karmic bond at once; a poisonous bird's touch kills fish in the water, yet a rhinoceros horn's touch revives the dead, illustrating that the world already contains inexplicable reversals of fortune, which a name's power resembles; and the legend of Yellow Crane Tower, where a crane calls a dead immortal's name for a thousand years until, astonishingly, he returns to life — evidence, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, that the world holds wonders beyond ordinary reckoning, and a buddha's power exceeds even these. He closes by noting the chapter's own dual purpose: to deepen confidence in the buddhas' virtues for those practicing through faith and recitation, and to establish the correct view — that every object is mind-only — for those walking the path of direct insight, the two approaches meant to reinforce rather than compete with one another.

2. Milestone: Four Kinds of Wisdom Establishing "Consciousness-Only, No Object" — Why a Bodhisattva's Mind Can Reshape What It Perceives

The power of holiness rests entirely on one insight: every object of experience is mind-only. The treatise draws on the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s own four kinds of wisdom to establish this — the same object appearing differently to different beings, cognition arising with no object actually present, sages perceiving without the distorted effort ordinary beings require, and, most strikingly, masters of mind and concentration reshaping objects at will, right up to a fully awakened one for whom no object appears at all.

Chinese Source

成就相違識相智。如餓鬼傍生及諸天人，同於一事，見彼所識有差別故。成就無所緣識現可得智。如過去未來夢影緣中有所得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Accomplishing the wisdom of contradictory cognized appearances: as with hungry ghosts, animals, and heavenly beings, who, regarding one and the same thing, perceive it with differing cognitions. Accomplishing the wisdom that cognition can arise with no object actually present: as with past events, future events, and dream-images, where cognition is nevertheless obtained regarding them.

Chinese Source

得心自在一切菩薩，得靜慮者，隨勝解力諸義顯現。得奢摩他修法觀者，纔作意時諸義顯現。已得無分別智者，無分別智現在前時，一切諸義皆不顯現。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who have attained mastery over mind, and those who have attained meditative absorption, can make any object appear simply through the power of resolute intention. Those who have attained calm-abiding and cultivate contemplation on phenomena can make any object appear the very moment attention is directed toward it. For those who have already attained non-discriminating wisdom, when that non-discriminating wisdom is manifest before them, no object whatsoever appears at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all four wisdoms as a cumulative case for "consciousness-only, no [independently real] object" (唯識無義) — not a denial that experience occurs, but a demonstration that what appears to experience never was the fixed, mind-independent thing it seemed to be. The first wisdom is the "one water, four minds" teaching already met in fascicle 36: a human sees ordinary water, a heavenly being sees lapis lazuli, a fish sees its own house, a hungry ghost sees pus and blood — the identical location yielding entirely different experienced objects depending purely on each viewer's own karma, which could never happen if the water possessed one single, fixed, independent nature everyone equally perceived. The second wisdom points to dreaming, remembering the past, and anticipating the future: in every case a vivid cognition arises with no corresponding object actually present anywhere to be found, proving cognition does not require a genuine external object in order to occur.

The third wisdom, which the text takes up separately, contrasts the ordinary being's confidence in their own perception with the sage's very different situation: an unenlightened person assumes what they see is simply how things are and feels no need to question it, while only a noble one perceives without distortion and without needing any effort to correct a mistaken view — the ordinary confidence turns out to be exactly backward, since it is the ordinary view that is habitually mistaken, not the sage's. Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates this with the plain observation that no one questions why cultivating precepts, concentration, and wisdom should be necessary at all if ordinary perception were already accurate.

The fourth wisdom, drawn out through three concrete levels, is the most vivid: a bodhisattva who has attained mastery over mind, or accomplished meditators generally, can transform an object the instant they resolve to — wishing a teacup to become a balloon, or a small thing to become large, is simply accomplished, exactly as Vimalakīrti's famously tiny room held countless visitors without any sense of crowding, impossible if the room's smallness were genuinely fixed. Someone cultivating calm-abiding can make a contemplated object — impermanence, suffering, or any other topic of meditation — appear vividly the instant attention turns to it, in stark contrast to an ordinary being's scattered mind, which cannot even sustain continuous recitation of a single Buddha's name without wandering. And most tellingly of all, one who has actually attained non-discriminating wisdom finds that when this wisdom is genuinely present, no object appears whatsoever — exactly like waking from a dream and finding the entire dreamed world simply gone, which could never happen if that world had been genuinely, independently real in the first place. Together, these four wisdoms are what the treatise means by the power of holiness resting on mastery of concentration — a bodhisattva's mind can reshape what it perceives precisely because nothing perceived was ever fixed to begin with.

3. The Power of Holiness Completed — A Mind Made Utterly Pliant Through Three Incalculable Eons

The power of holiness closes with what makes it possible in the first place: a mind rendered completely pliant — like water taking the shape of whatever vessel holds it — through the sustained cultivation of no-self and no-fixed-phenomena across three incalculable eons, free of affliction and thus free to move however compassion and wisdom together require.

Chinese Source

心調柔故，善修心故，是名聖威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because the mind has become pliant, and because the mind has been well cultivated, this is called the power of holiness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that an afflicted mind is inherently rigid — anger, craving, and confusion each pull thought, speech, and action into fixed, predictable grooves — while a mind that has genuinely subdued self-clinging and clinging to fixed phenomena becomes soft in the way water is soft: poured into a round vessel it is round, poured into a square one it is square, taking whatever shape compassion and wisdom call for without resistance, able to answer a thousand simultaneous appeals exactly where each is made. This pliancy is not a natural gift — no buddha or bodhisattva was born already awakened — but the fruit of uninterrupted cultivation across the provisions, preparation, seeing, cultivation, and final stages of the path, sustained across three full incalculable eons. He cites the *Discourse Records of the Yogācārabhūmi*'s own gloss identifying the power of holiness with the six superknowledges — the first five shared even with accomplished outsiders to the teaching, the sixth, the extinction of contaminants, belonging to noble ones alone — noting that even a bodhisattva who retains subtle habitual traces of past affliction, without the affliction itself any longer active, still counts as having attained this sixth superknowledge in its own partial, genuine sense. This closes the power of holiness (聖威力), the first of the three kinds of power; the power inherent in the Dharma itself (法威力) opens next.

4. The Power of the Dharma Opens — Vast Fruit from Supreme Practices

The second kind of power belongs not to the practitioner directly but to the teachings and practices themselves: the six perfections, cultivated from long before non-discriminating wisdom is ever attained and continuing all the way to buddhahood, carry within them the capacity to produce vast fruit and supreme benefit simply by virtue of what they are.

Chinese Source

二者、法威力。謂諸勝法有廣大果，有大勝利，是名法威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

The second is the power of the Dharma: it is that these supreme practices carry vast fruit and great benefit — this is called the power of the Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies these "supreme practices" as the six perfections — giving, moral discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom — cultivated continuously from well before a bodhisattva ever attains non-discriminating wisdom, through the entire path, and onward without interruption even after buddhahood itself is reached. What distinguishes this from the power of holiness just described is where the power is located: the power of holiness is a capacity possessed by the practitioner's own mastered mind, while the power of the Dharma is, so to speak, built into the practices themselves — vast fruit and supreme benefit follow from genuinely cultivating giving or patience or wisdom regardless of which specific individual carries it out, the way a seed of a particular kind reliably produces its own particular fruit. The treatise now turns to explaining this power's own precise nature in more detail.

5. The Third Power — Innate Power Carried Over From Accumulated Merit, Completing All Three Kinds

The third kind of power needs no present cultivation at all: it is simply carried forward, already complete, from merit accumulated across countless past lives — appearing as an inborn marvel the moment a buddha or bodhisattva is born, closing out the three basic kinds of power before the treatise expands them into a fuller fivefold account.

Chinese Source

此中法者，即是六種波羅蜜多，所謂布施乃至般若。如是諸法有大威力，名法威力。三者、俱生威力。謂佛菩薩先集廣大福德資糧，證得俱生甚希奇法，是名俱生威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

The "Dharma" spoken of here refers to the six perfections — giving, and so on up through wisdom. These practices possess vast power; this is called the power of the Dharma. The third is innate power: buddhas and bodhisattvas, having previously gathered vast provisions of merit, thereby attain innate and truly marvelous qualities — this is called innate power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates innate power with several concrete examples: certain arhats are said to have been born with a lump of gold appearing in their open palm, needing never to beg for support, a direct fruit of generosity practiced in earlier lives; the monk known as "Honey-Bowl" always found his alms bowl spontaneously filled with honey, since in a past life as a monkey he had joyfully offered honey to the Buddha; and the Buddha's own birth was marked by walking seven steps and declaring his own supremacy above all realms, among many other extraordinary signs detailed elsewhere in the treatise. He notes this differs from both prior kinds of power precisely in requiring no present mastery of concentration or present practice of the perfections — it simply ripens automatically, the way a planted seed eventually bears its own fruit without needing to be replanted. This completes the three basic kinds of power (聖威力, 法威力, 俱生威力); the treatise now expands this into a fuller five-fold classification.

6. Milestone: Five Kinds of Power, and the Six Superknowledges That Constitute Miraculous Power

The treatise refines its three-fold account into five: miraculous power, the power of the Dharma, innate power, power shared with hearers and solitary buddhas, and power unique to buddhas and bodhisattvas alone — beginning with miraculous power's own six classical constituents, from moving through solid matter to the extinction of every contaminant.

Chinese Source

又佛菩薩如是威力品類差別，復有五種。一者、神通威力，二者、法威力，三者、俱生威力，四者、共諸聲聞、獨覺威力，五者、不共聲聞、獨覺威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, the distinct kinds of power belonging to buddhas and bodhisattvas may further be classified into five: first, the power of miraculous ability; second, the power of the Dharma; third, innate power; fourth, power shared in common with hearers and solitary buddhas; and fifth, power unique to buddhas and bodhisattvas, not shared with hearers or solitary buddhas.

Chinese Source

云何諸佛菩薩神通威力？謂六神通。一者、神境智作證通，二者、隨念宿住智作證通，三者、天耳智作證通，四者、見死生智作證通，五者、知心差別智作證通，六者、漏盡智作證通。是名神通威力。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the miraculous power of buddhas and bodhisattvas? It is the six superknowledges: first, the superknowledge of miraculous ability; second, the superknowledge of recollecting past lives; third, the superknowledge of the divine ear; fourth, the superknowledge of seeing death and rebirth; fifth, the superknowledge of knowing others' minds; and sixth, the superknowledge of the extinction of contaminants. This is called miraculous power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi runs through the six briefly: the superknowledge of miraculous ability transforms one thing into many or many into one, reaches bodily even into the form realm's own first dhyāna heaven, and underlies everything the chapter is about to detail; recollecting past lives recovers one's own and others' names, food, and circumstances across countless former births; the divine ear hears speech at any distance without the ordinary ear's limitation; seeing death and rebirth observes precisely where a being goes after dying; knowing others' minds reads another's mental state with a precision ordinary familiarity only crudely approximates; and the extinction of contaminants — attained only by noble ones, unlike the first five, which even accomplished non-Buddhist practitioners can develop — is the direct fruit of having actually exhausted every affliction through the supramundane path. He notes each of these six ultimately rests on the same wisdom already established: since every object is mind-only, a mind that has genuinely mastered itself can display, recall, hear, see, and know across boundaries an ordinary unliberated mind takes to be fixed and impassable. The treatise now turns to the first and most elaborately detailed of the six — the superknowledge of miraculous ability — beginning with its two basic modes.

7. Miraculous Ability's Two Modes — Transforming What Exists, Conjuring What Did Not

The superknowledge of miraculous ability divides into two basic modes: transformation, which takes something already existing and changes it into something else, and conjuring, which brings into being something that was not there at all — together said to comprise eighteen distinct varieties, several of which the treatise now details one by one.

Chinese Source

謂佛菩薩神境智通略有二種。一者、能變通，二者、能化通。如是二種品類差別，各有多種。

Ru-Yi Translation

The superknowledge of miraculous ability possessed by buddhas and bodhisattvas is, in brief, of two kinds: first, the power of transformation; second, the power of conjuring. Each of these two kinds has, in turn, many further distinct varieties.

Chinese Source

謂十八變。一者、震動，二者、熾然，三者、流布，四者、示現，五者、轉變，六者、往來，七者、卷，八者、舒，九者、眾像入身，十者、同類往趣，十一者、顯，十二者、隱，十三者、所作自在，十四者、制他神通，十五者、能施辯才，十六者、能施憶念，十七者、能施安樂，十八者、放大光明。

Ru-Yi Translation

These are the eighteen transformations: quaking, blazing, pervading, showing, transmuting, coming and going, contracting, expanding, gathering every form into one's own body, going forth in the same form as one's audience, revealing, concealing, freely accomplishing whatever is willed, restraining another's miraculous power, bestowing eloquence, bestowing memory, bestowing ease, and radiating great light.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two modes precisely: transformation changes an already-existing thing's own reference point — a teacup becomes a washbasin — while conjuring produces something out of nothing that was not there before — a teacup appears where none existed. He notes an earlier scholastic dispute the treatise's own Mahāyāna position resolves clearly: some schools held that only transformation counts as a genuine "miraculous power" while conjuring is merely its fruit, and that a conjuring mind is karmically neutral rather than wholesome; the Mahāyāna view taken here treats conjuring as equally a form of miraculous power, and treats a buddha's own conjuring as always wholesome, since it exists solely to benefit beings, though an ordinary conjuring act might remain karmically neutral if no such intention is present. With both modes and all eighteen varieties named, the treatise proceeds to explain several of the most vivid ones individually, beginning with the first three.

8. Milestone: Quaking Worlds, Blazing Fire and Water, and Light That Fills Every Realm

The first three of the eighteen transformations announce a buddha's presence before a single word is spoken: making entire world-systems quake, producing fire and water simultaneously from different parts of the body in every color imaginable, and radiating light that fills every dwelling and world without exception.

Chinese Source

震動者，謂佛菩薩得定自在……普能震動寺館舍宅、村邑聚落、城郭國土、那落迦世界、傍生世界、祖父世界、人世界、天世界……乃至無量無數三千大千世界皆能震動。是名震動。

Ru-Yi Translation

Quaking: buddhas and bodhisattvas, having attained mastery over concentration ... are universally able to make temples, dwellings, villages, towns, walled cities and countries, the hell realms, the animal realm, the ghost realm, the human realm, and the heavenly realm quake ... up to an immeasurable, uncountable number of great trichiliocosms, all of them able to quake. This is called quaking.

Chinese Source

熾然者……從其身上發猛焰火，於其身下注清冷水；從其身下發猛焰火，於其身上注清冷水。……是名熾然。流布者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，流布光明，遍滿一切寺館舍宅，乃至無量無數世界無不充滿……是名流布。

Ru-Yi Translation

Blazing: ... fierce flames issue from the upper body while cool water pours from the lower body; fierce flames issue from the lower body while cool water pours from the upper body ... this is called blazing. Pervading: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, radiate light pervading every temple and dwelling, filling even an immeasurable, uncountable number of worlds without exception ... this is called pervading.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lingers on quaking's own cosmology, since the treatise measures its scope in specific units: a single world with four continents; a "small thousand world," containing a thousand such systems, each with its own sun, moon, and Mount Sumeru, reaching up to the first dhyāna heaven; a "medium thousand world," a thousand small-thousand worlds, reaching the second dhyāna; and a "great trichiliocosm," a thousand medium-thousand worlds, reaching the third dhyāna heaven — with the fourth dhyāna standing above even this. He notes a curious etymological aside the treatise flags: the ghost realm is called the "grandfather world" (祖父世界) because, according to the *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā*, the very first being ever to hoard resources selfishly and then steal was also the very first to fall into that realm as a consequence — everyone born there since is, in a sense, a descendant of that original ancestor. He stresses that a buddha's quaking never causes the harm an earthquake does; its entire purpose, as Elder Miàojìng noted, is to shake loose beings' own dormant ignorance and afflictions, functioning almost like an announcement before a teaching begins, drawing attention the way a temple bell calls people to assembly. Blazing similarly displays water and fire moving in perfect, uncontradictory coexistence through a single body, in the seven colors of light the treatise names, demonstrating mastery over even the four elements themselves; pervading simply fills every space touched with light, so vivid that a buddha teaching indoors needs no lamp at all — together, these three transformations announce a buddha's presence and inspire confidence in an audience before a single word of teaching is even spoken.

9. Milestone: Showing Every Realm and Every Pure Land — With a Story of a Monk Humbled by His Own Lay Disciple

The fourth transformation lets an assembled audience see, on request, exactly what samsara and liberation actually look like: every realm of rebirth from hell to the heavens, and every buddha's pure land across universes beyond counting — a vision meant to inspire both renunciation of suffering and aspiration toward genuine purity.

Chinese Source

示現者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，如其所樂，示彼一切諸來會眾……令悉現見下諸惡趣、上諸人天。復令現見諸餘佛土，及於其中諸佛菩薩……悉令現見；亦為宣說彼佛土名，及如來名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Showing: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, show to the entire assembly, as they wish, whatever they take delight in ... causing them all to directly see the miserable realms below and the human and heavenly realms above. They further cause them to directly see other buddhas' lands, and the buddhas and bodhisattvas dwelling within them ... causing them all to directly see this, and also proclaiming the name of that buddha-land, and the name of its Tathāgata.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this transformation reaches every kind of being present at a teaching assembly — monastics, learned laypeople, hearers, bodhisattvas, and the full array of celestial and semi-divine beings named in the treatise's own list — each shown, according to what genuinely benefits them, the lower realms' suffering and the higher realms' relative ease, alongside distant buddhas' pure lands, so that renunciation and aspiration both arise from direct sight rather than mere description.

He shares a story illustrating why sincerity in practice matters more than outward skill: two monks with beautiful voices for chanting nonetheless performed the morning and evening liturgy carelessly, as if merely singing a song, without genuine reverence. A lay visitor, deeply moved by their chanting despite this, practiced with real sincerity alongside it and, in time, attained the first stage of awakening. After this disciple's death and rebirth among the heavens, he encountered the same two monks — reborn not as gods but as a music-deity and a song-deity, entertaining the heavenly assembly, a direct karmic fruit of their fondness for chanting without the devotion that should have accompanied it. Greeting them with familiar warmth, he was met with their own embarrassed admission: "once you came to hear us chant — now we sing for you, and you have attained realization while we still have not." Stung into genuine effort, the two are said to have gone on to attain the fruit themselves not long after. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson plainly: a student can sometimes outpace the very teacher who first inspired them, and talent without sincerity, however impressive, only postpones — never replaces — the real work of practice.

10. Milestone: Transmuting the Four Elements — Mind Creates the World It Stands On

The fifth transformation, transmuting, takes any one of the four elements — earth, water, fire, or wind — and turns it genuinely into any other, precisely as resolved and never by accident, revealing the same truth behind every transformation in this chapter: nothing perceived was ever fixed, only ever mind's own display.

Chinese Source

轉變者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，若於其地起水勝解，即令成水如實非餘；火、風勝解亦復如是。……若於一切起餘勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Transmuting: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration — if they form the resolute conviction that earth is water, it genuinely becomes water and nothing else; the same holds for resolving it into fire or wind. ... Whatever resolute conviction is formed regarding any of these, it genuinely becomes exactly that and nothing else.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi grounds this directly in the mind-only teaching already established: ordinary beings stand on solid ground because collective karmic seeds project a shared, seemingly fixed earth-experience, yet even among ordinary beings the same ground never appears quite identically to any two perceivers. A buddha or bodhisattva, never mistaking this collectively-projected ground for something genuinely independent of mind, can simply resolve it into water — and genuinely drink it, not merely display an illusion of water the way a stage magician's trick water cannot actually be swallowed — precisely because "earth" was never more solid than mind's own conviction to begin with. He cites the *Avataṃsaka-sūtra*'s own famous verse — "if you wish to know all buddhas of past, present, and future, contemplate the nature of the Dharma-realm: everything is mind alone" — as the doctrinal heart of every transformation this chapter describes, including a mustard seed containing an entire Mount Sumeru without either one changing size, since "large" and "small" were never fixed properties to begin with.

He adds a curious clarification from elsewhere in the treatise: if two bodhisattvas of equal power simultaneously will opposing transformations onto the same object — one resolving earth into water, the other into fire — the equal, opposing convictions cancel out and neither succeeds; if their power is unequal, the stronger resolve prevails; and if their power is equal but one commits first with full attention, that one succeeds. The lesson he draws is a practical one about ordinary effort as much as miraculous power: single-minded resolve accomplishes what divided or hesitant intention cannot, in worldly practice no less than supernormal display.

11. Transmuting Ordinary Materials into Treasure, Trained Through the Eight Mastery-Bases

The same power that transmutes one element into another extends to their derivative qualities and to ordinary materials themselves — grass and dung becoming food and clothing, gravel becoming pearl and coral, a mountain becoming solid gold — trained specifically through a meditative sequence called the eight mastery-bases, moving from small objects to vast ones and finally to color itself.

Chinese Source

若於草葉、牛糞、泥等，起於飲食、車乘、衣服、嚴飾資具、種種塗香、華鬘勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。若於沙石、瓦礫等物，起於末尼、真珠、琉璃……勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。若於諸山、雪山王等起金勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, regarding grass, leaves, dung, or mud, the resolute conviction is formed that these are food, a carriage, clothing, ornaments, fragrant incense, or garlands, they genuinely become exactly that. If, regarding sand, gravel, or rubble, the resolute conviction is formed that these are gemstones, pearls, or lapis lazuli, they genuinely become exactly that. If, regarding any mountain, even the great snow-capped king of mountains, the resolute conviction is formed that it is gold, it genuinely becomes gold.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi connects this capacity to a specific meditative training called the eight mastery-bases (八勝處): the first two, cultivated in the first and second dhyānas, master a small range of ordinary objects and then a vast range (a single teacup, then an entire palace or river, appearing the instant it is willed); the third and fourth repeat this at a deeper level of absorption, once attachment to form itself has loosened; and the final four, cultivated in the fourth dhyāna, master pure color itself — turning everything perceived uniformly blue, yellow, red, or white at will, the meditative root of a painter's palette taken to its limit, where a practitioner need not wait for autumn wind to turn a leaf red, but simply wills it so. Mastery over these eight bases is what makes possible the treasure-transmutation just described — coarse material transmuted into refined material is simply a further application of exactly the same trained conviction. He notes this power is available not only to a fully realized bodhisattva but, to a lesser and more effortful degree, even to accomplished meditators generally, since it rests on skill in concentration rather than complete awakening alone.

12. Milestone: Transmuting a Being's Own Appearance — The Ghost Story That Dissolved a Monk's Attachment to Self

The same transmuting power extends even to a sentient being's own physical appearance — beautiful made plain, plain made beautiful, missing limbs restored — illustrated with a striking story in which two ghosts, quarreling over a corpse, unknowingly dismantle a doubting monk's entire body piece by piece and replace it with the corpse's own parts, leaving him utterly unable to say who he still is.

Chinese Source

若於好色有情起惡色勝解，於惡色有情起好色勝解，於俱非有情起好色、惡色勝解，於俱有情起俱非勝解，即隨勝解如實非餘。如於好色、惡色，於具支節、不具支節，及肥瘦等，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, regarding a being of pleasing appearance, the resolute conviction is formed that they are of displeasing appearance — or the reverse — or regarding a being neither pleasing nor displeasing, that they are one or the other — or regarding a being who is both, that they are neither — it genuinely becomes exactly that. Just as with pleasing and displeasing appearance, so too with having complete or incomplete limbs, and with being stout or thin — it should be understood to hold the same way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this power to reshape appearance and even restore missing limbs belongs not only to buddhas and bodhisattvas but, in a lesser form, to accomplished arhats — illustrated by a story concerning a disciple of the arhat Upagupta who, worn down by the rigors of practice, wished to return to lay life. Upagupta simply asked him to spend one more night in his room before leaving. That night, the young monk saw a small ghost drag in a corpse, followed by a large ghost demanding to eat it; the two quarreled and, noticing the monk, demanded he settle whose corpse it rightfully was. Reasoning that a lie would get him killed as readily as the truth, he answered honestly that the small ghost had dragged it in — enraging the large ghost, who tore off the monk's right arm and ate it, whereupon the small ghost calmly reattached the corpse's own right arm in its place. This repeated, limb by limb and finally the torso and head themselves, until every part of the monk's original body had been eaten by the large ghost and replaced by the corpse's, the two ghosts finally wiping their mouths and departing.

Waking the next morning, the monk found himself seized by a single overwhelming question: "who am I?" Every part of the body he had always taken to be himself had, over the course of one night, been entirely replaced — he could no longer locate any "self" left to return home in. Reporting this to Upagupta, he received in response the teaching of no-self: the body was never truly his to begin with, and form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness alike are without a self and without anything belonging to a self. Hearing this teaching in the direct aftermath of the night's own visceral demonstration, the monk realized the first fruit of the path on the spot, and never again wished to return to lay life. Master Cháng Bǎi observes that if even a ghost's crude interference could dismantle self-clinging this thoroughly, how much more can a buddha or bodhisattva's own deliberate transformation of a being's appearance serve the identical purpose — restoring sight to the blind, hearing to the deaf, and limbs to the maimed, wherever doing so genuinely serves to loosen the same attachment. This closes transmuting, the fifth of the eighteen; coming-and-going and several further transformations follow next.

13. Coming and Going Without Obstruction, Folding Mount Sumeru Into an Atom, and Gathering Every Form Into One's Own Body

Three further transformations display mastery over space itself: moving through solid walls and mountains as freely as through open air, traveling to any world at the speed of a single thought; contracting the vastest mountain into a single mote of dust and expanding a single mote into a mountain range without either genuinely growing or shrinking; and drawing an entire assembled crowd, town, forest, and mountain range into one's own body, with everyone inside plainly seeing themselves there.

Chinese Source

往來者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，隨其所樂，於諸牆壁、山石等中縱身往來，無有滯礙。廣說乃至往於梵世，乃至上至色究竟天，還來無礙。或復傍於無量無數三千大千世界，若往、若來皆無滯礙。

Ru-Yi Translation

Coming and going: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, move their bodies freely through walls, mountains, and rock as they please, without any obstruction — extending even to journeying to the Brahmā realm and up to the highest form-realm heaven, returning without hindrance; or ranging across an immeasurable, uncountable number of great trichiliocosms, coming and going entirely without obstruction.

Chinese Source

卷、舒者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，能卷一切雪山王等如一極微，舒一極微令如一切雪山王等。是名卷、舒。眾像入身者……能以種種現前大眾，及以一切村邑、聚落、草木、叢林、諸山、大地一切色像內己身中，令諸大眾各各自知入其身內。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contracting and expanding: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, can contract even the great snow-capped king of mountains until it is like a single mote of dust, and expand a single mote of dust until it is like the great snow-capped king of mountains. This is called contracting and expanding. Gathering forms into the body: ... they are able to gather the entire assembled crowd before them, along with every village, town, plant, forest, mountain, and expanse of earth, into their own body, causing everyone to directly know that they have entered within it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains coming-and-going through the same principle already established: "a wall is not truly a wall — it is merely called a wall." An ordinary being, seeing a wall and taking it as genuinely solid, collides with it and is hurt; a buddha or bodhisattva, never mistaking the wall's imputed solidity for an independent reality, passes through as freely as through open space — reaching even the highest heaven of the form realm, or traveling to countless other world-systems, in a single instant, since the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s own commentary notes that such travel can cross immeasurable worlds "in a single moment." He explains contracting and expanding through the *Avataṃsaka*'s teaching of the mutual, unobstructed interpenetration of the one and the many — folding ten directions' worth of infinite worlds into a single mote of dust without the dust growing any larger, or expanding a single mote to contain ten directions' worth of worlds without those worlds growing any smaller, since size, like every other quality already examined in this chapter, was never a fixed, independent property to begin with. Gathering forms into the body he illustrates with the familiar image of the Monkey King unable to escape the Buddha's own palm — no matter how far he leapt, he never left it — a fitting picture of how thoroughly an ordinary being's entire experienced world remains, all along, within the compass of an awakened mind. This closes contracting-expanding and gathering-forms, the seventh and eighth of the eighteen transformations; a ninth, going forth in an audience's own form, follows next with two especially memorable stories.

14. Milestone: Going Forth in an Audience's Own Form — Two Stories of Mistaking a Bodhisattva for an Ordinary Being

The ninth transformation lets a buddha or bodhisattva appear in exactly the form, speech, and manner of whichever audience they wish to teach — a king among kings, a god among gods — teaching through four kinds of genuine reasoning before vanishing the instant the work is done, illustrated here with two well-loved stories in which Mañjuśrī tests unsuspecting monks in disguise, with very different outcomes.

Chinese Source

同類往趣者，謂佛菩薩依定自在，或能往趣剎帝利眾，同其色類，如彼形量，似彼言音……然後為其演說正法，示現、教導、讚勵、慶慰。化事既終，欻然隱沒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Going forth in the same form: buddhas and bodhisattvas, resting on mastery over concentration, are able to go among an assembly of kings and nobles in their same form, matching their stature and manner of speech ... and then expound the true teaching to them — showing, instructing, encouraging, and consoling. Once this work of transformation is complete, they suddenly vanish.

Chinese Source

沒後時眾迭相顧言：不知沒者天耶？人耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

After they vanish, the assembly looks at one another and asks: "Was the one who just disappeared a god, or a human being?"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the four-fold teaching method named here — showing the four genuine principles of reasoning, settling students into the precepts appropriate to them, encouraging those who falter, and warmly congratulating those who progress — drawing in passing on the treatise's own four kinds of reasoning already used to investigate all things: reasoning by mutual dependence (nothing exists on its own, everything arises relative to something else — the root insight behind mind-only itself); reasoning by function (each thing, however empty of fixed nature, still has its own working role — earth is solid, water is moist, precisely as a monastery's kitchen-duty monk and its liturgy-duty monk each have distinct tasks); reasoning by valid proof (checked against direct experience, sound inference, and reliable scriptural testimony); and reasoning by the nature of things as they simply are (impermanence is simply how conditioned things are, and pressing "but why is it impermanent?" past this point leads nowhere useful).

He then tells two stories of Mañjuśrī testing unsuspecting hosts in disguise. In the first, at a great open feast where none were to be turned away, a visibly pregnant woman arrived with a dog and asked for a meal box — then a second for the dog — then a third, explaining she was eating for the unborn child as well. The attending monk, forgetting the feast's own founding vow, scolded her for greed; she instantly rose into the sky, revealed as Mañjuśrī with the dog in tow, leaving the monk overcome with regret at having failed to recognize the very bodhisattva he had just insulted. In the second, Mañjuśrī appeared cooking in a monastery kitchen during an ordination ceremony; a monk who had been told the cook might be Mañjuśrī bowed and asked for wisdom, and was told he need only climb into the blazing stove to receive it — hesitating at the threshold, he lost his nerve, and Mañjuśrī vanished along with the chance. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the two stories together as a single, gentle challenge to the reader: recognizing genuine virtue often asks more of us than politeness toward whoever looks the part — and asks, quite directly, whether any of us, offered that same fiery threshold, would have stepped through.
