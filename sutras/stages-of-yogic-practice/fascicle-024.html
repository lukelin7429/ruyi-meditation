Fascicle 24 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十四 · Wakeful Practice, Day and Night

Awake Through the Watches of the Night

Fascicle 24 continues the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation's long account of the fourteen path-provisions, picking up exactly where fascicle 23 left off: wakeful practice, the seventh provision, structured around the day and the four watches of the night, followed by clear awareness in conduct, the eighth.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 24 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Wakeful Practice Opens: What Counts as the First Watch and the Last Watch

Fascicle 24 opens directly onto the seventh of the fourteen provisions, wakeful practice — a discipline for structuring the entire day and night, day practice, first-watch practice, and last-watch practice, around sitting and walking meditation, with the middle watches reserved for sleep. The treatise begins by defining the night's four watches with technical precision.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地初瑜伽處出離地品第三之三……復次，初夜後夜常勤修習悎寤瑜伽者，云何初夜？云何後夜？云何悎寤瑜伽？云何常勤修習悎寤瑜伽？言初夜者，謂夜四分中，過初一分，是夜初分。言後夜者，謂夜四分中，過後一分，是夜後分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first yogic process, the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation, the third part of the third chapter … Furthermore, as for one who constantly and diligently cultivates wakeful practice in the first watch and the last watch of the night: what is the first watch? What is the last watch? What is wakeful practice? What is constant, diligent cultivation of wakeful practice? The first watch means that, of the night's four divisions, the first division passed through is the night's first part. The last watch means that, of the night's four divisions, the last division passed through is the night's last part.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating this material precisely: fascicle 24 is the third of four installments covering the "provisions for detachment" within the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation — fascicle 22 covered detachment itself and moral discipline, fascicle 23 covered restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating, and this fascicle covers wakeful practice through the first and last watches of the night, plus clear awareness in conduct; fascicle 25 will close out the remaining provisions.

He then explains the treatise's specific way of dividing a day: rather than the six-period scheme familiar from other sutras (dawn, midday, sunset, evening, midnight, cockcrow), this treatise splits both day and night into four three-hour periods each. The night's four periods run from 6 pm to 6 am; the "first watch" is 6–9 pm, and the "last watch" is 3–6 am. The two middle periods, 9 pm to 3 am, are simply sleep — six hours, which Cháng Bǎi notes is a genuinely workable amount even by modern standards, more realistic than what many retreat schedules actually allow. The structure this sets up is elegant: meditate in the evening, sleep six hours, wake and meditate again before dawn — with the daytime hours reserved for their own parallel practice, discussed next.

2. Wakeful Practice Defined: Walking and Sitting Around the Clock

Chinese Source

悎寤瑜伽者，謂如說言：於晝日分經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心；於初夜分經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心。淨修心已，出住處外洗濯其足，還入住處，右脅而臥，重累其足，住光明想、正念、正知，思惟起想，巧便而臥。至夜後分，速疾悎寤，經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wakeful practice means: it is said that, during the day, one purifies the mind of the obstructions and what accords with them through walking meditation and sitting meditation; in the first watch of the night, one purifies the mind of the obstructions and what accords with them through walking meditation and sitting meditation. Having purified the mind, one goes outside the dwelling to wash the feet, then returns inside, lies down on the right side, one leg resting upon the other, abiding in the perception of light, mindfulness, and clear comprehension, forming the intention to rise, and lying down skillfully. Upon reaching the last watch, one wakes swiftly, and again purifies the mind of the obstructions and what accords with them through walking meditation and sitting meditation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the entire twenty-four-hour cycle this defines. Throughout the day (6 am–6 pm) and the first watch (6–9 pm), the practitioner alternates walking meditation — pacing a straight line or a loop, east to west and back — with formal cross-legged sitting, using both postures to purify the mind of the five hindrances (desire, ill-will, sloth-and-torpor, restlessness-and-remorse, doubt) and of whatever sense-objects tend to provoke them. Before sleep, a precise protocol follows: washing the feet (bare feet in the ancient setting made this a practical necessity, not merely ritual), lying on the right side in the Buddha's traditional posture, legs stacked, and settling the mind on a "perception of light" — Cháng Bǎi identifies three versions: the natural luminosity of a divine body, the physical light of sun, moon, and stars, or the figurative light of Dharma teachings such as loving-kindness or the contemplation of non-self and emptiness. Any of these keeps the mind bright and wards off nightmares. Alongside this, the practitioner maintains mindfulness and clear comprehension — checking whether the mind has drifted toward sensual thoughts and pulling it back if so — and deliberately forms the intention to wake at a set hour, trusting the storehouse consciousness to register the resolve, before finally settling into sleep "skillfully," meaning with this whole structure in place rather than simply collapsing into unconsciousness. Waking in the last watch is to be swift, not lingering, immediately resuming the same walking-and-sitting purification used during the day and the first watch.

3. Constant Diligent Cultivation: Five Kinds of Energy

Chinese Source

常勤修習悎寤瑜伽者，謂如有一世尊弟子，聽聞悎寤瑜伽法已，欲樂修學，便依如是悎寤瑜伽，作如是念：我當成辦佛所聽許悎寤瑜伽。發生樂欲，精進勤劬，超越勇猛勢力發起、勇悍、剛決、不可制伏、策勵其心無間相續。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for one who constantly and diligently cultivates wakeful practice: it is like a disciple of the Blessed One who, having heard the teaching on wakeful practice, wishes to study and practice it, and, relying on this wakeful practice, thinks: I shall accomplish the wakeful practice permitted and taught by the Buddha. Such a one generates aspiration, strives with vigor, arises with a force that surpasses ordinary courage, is bold, resolute, unable to be overcome, and spurs the mind on in unbroken continuity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as a compressed portrait of genuine diligence, and maps its five phrases onto the five kinds of energetic effort taught elsewhere in the treatise (Compendium of Determinations, fascicle 89). "Arising with surpassing force" is armor-like energy — the resolve one puts on before battle, here aimed at one's own afflictions, born from intense aspiration. "Boldness" is applied energy — the courage to actually take up the practice once armored. "Resoluteness" is unyielding energy — refusing to belittle oneself or grow timid about reaching the goal. "Unable to be overcome" is unshakable energy — the capacity to bear cold, heat, hunger, and other hardships without the resolve wavering. And "spurring the mind on in unbroken continuity" is insatiable energy — never resting content with progress already made, always seeking the next, subtler stage of the path. Cháng Bǎi warns against the common failure mode: a meditator whose legs finally stop aching, who sits comfortably through one session, and mistakes that ease for arrival — genuine diligence keeps pressing forward rather than settling for a single good sitting. With this, the treatise's brief outline of wakeful practice — first watch, last watch, the practice itself, and diligent cultivation of it — is complete; a detailed exposition follows.
